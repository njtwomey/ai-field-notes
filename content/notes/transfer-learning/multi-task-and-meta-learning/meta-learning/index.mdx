<Definition>
**Meta-learning**, or learning to learn, trains a learning algorithm on a distribution of tasks $p(\Tcal)$ so that it
performs well on new tasks from the same distribution after seeing few of their examples <Cite id="thrun1998" /> <Cite
id="hospedales2021" />. Each task $\Tcal_i$ comes with a **support set** $\Dcal_i^{\text{tr}}$ for adaptation and a
**query set** $\Dcal_i^{\text{val}}$ for evaluation. The algorithm has meta-parameters $\thetavec$ shared across tasks,
and task-specific parameters $\phivec_i = \text{Adapt}(\thetavec, \Dcal_i^{\text{tr}})$. Meta-training chooses
$\thetavec$ to minimise the expected query loss after adaptation,
$$
\min_{\thetavec} \; \expect_{\Tcal_i \sim p(\Tcal)} \brack{L_i^{\text{val}}\paren{\text{Adapt}(\thetavec,
\Dcal_i^{\text{tr}})}} .
$$
</Definition>

## Intuition

Ordinary training learns one function from many examples. Meta-learning learns *how to learn* a function from few
examples, by practising on many small problems. The training data are tasks, and each task is split the way it will be
at test time: a few labelled examples to learn from and some held-out examples to be scored on. What is learnt is
whatever makes learning fast: a starting point, a metric, a learning rule or an architecture.

## Three families

Hospedales et al. classify methods by what $\text{Adapt}$ is <Cite id="hospedales2021" />.

1. **Optimisation-based.** Adaptation is <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on the support set, and $\thetavec$ is the initialisation or
   the optimiser's hyperparameters. Model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML) and Reptile are the main examples. A learnt <Gloss name="lstm" /> optimiser is another
   <Cite id="ravi2017" />.
2. **Metric-based.** Adaptation is nearest-neighbour or kernel classification in a learnt embedding, so it involves no
   <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> steps. See <NoteLink to="metric-based-few-shot-learning" />.
3. **Model-based.** A network reads the support set and outputs predictions for the query set in one forward pass, so
   $\text{Adapt}$ is a black box.

## Model-agnostic meta-learning

MAML takes $\text{Adapt}$ to be one or a few steps of gradient descent from $\thetavec$ <Cite id="finn2017maml" />. With
one step of size $\alpha$,
$$
\phivec_i = \thetavec - \alpha \nabla_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{tr}}(\thetavec),
\qquad
\min_{\thetavec} \sum_{\Tcal_i} L_i^{\text{val}}\paren{\thetavec - \alpha \nabla_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{tr}}(\thetavec)} .
$$
It applies to any model trained by gradient descent, which is what *model-agnostic* means. The meta-parameters are an
initialisation that is not good for any task by itself but is a short step from a good solution for every task.

### The meta-gradient

The outer objective depends on $\thetavec$ through $\phivec_i$, so the chain rule gives
$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i) = \paren{\frac{\partial \phivec_i}{\partial \thetavec}}\transpose
\nabla_{\phivec} L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i) .
$$
The Jacobian of the inner step is
$$
\frac{\partial \phivec_i}{\partial \thetavec} = \eye - \alpha \nabla^2_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{tr}}(\thetavec),
$$
which is symmetric because the <NoteLink to="hessian" /> is. Hence
$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i) = \paren{\eye - \alpha \nabla^2 L_i^{\text{tr}}(\thetavec)}
\nabla L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i) .
$$
The query gradient is evaluated at the adapted parameters and then corrected by the curvature of the support loss at
the initialisation. The correction accounts for how moving $\thetavec$ changes where the inner step lands. For $k$ inner
steps with iterates $\phivec^{(0)} = \thetavec, \phivec^{(1)}, \dots, \phivec^{(k)}$, the Jacobian is the product
$\prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \paren{\eye - \alpha \nabla^2 L_i^{\text{tr}}(\phivec^{(j)})}$, taken in reverse order.

The outer update over a batch of $B$ tasks is $\thetavec \leftarrow \thetavec - \beta \sum_{i=1}^{B}
\nabla_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i)$. Automatic differentiation computes the Hessian–vector product without
forming the Hessian, at the cost of keeping the inner computation graph in memory and roughly one extra backward pass
per inner step.

### First-order MAML

Dropping the second-derivative term gives **first-order MAML**:
$\nabla_{\thetavec} L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i) \approx \nabla_{\phivec} L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i)$, the query gradient
at the adapted parameters, applied to $\thetavec$. Finn et al. report that it performs nearly as well as full MAML on
their few-shot image benchmarks, and suggest that ReLU networks are locally almost linear, so their Hessians contribute
little <Cite id="finn2017maml" />.

### Reptile

**Reptile** removes the query set altogether <Cite id="nichol2018reptile" />. Sample a task, run $k$ steps of stochastic
gradient descent from $\thetavec$ to reach $\tilde\phivec$, and move the initialisation towards the result:
$$
\thetavec \leftarrow \thetavec + \epsilon \paren{\tilde\phivec - \thetavec} .
$$
With $k = 1$ this is ordinary SGD on the pooled tasks, which learns the average task. With $k > 1$ it is not.

### Why first-order methods work

Nichol et al. expand the updates to second order in the inner step size $\alpha$ <Cite id="nichol2018reptile" />. Take
two inner steps on minibatches 1 and 2 of one task, with gradients $\gvec_1, \gvec_2$ at the iterates and gradients
$\bar\gvec_j$ and Hessians $\bar\Hmat_j$ of minibatch $j$'s loss at the initialisation. By a first-order <NoteLink
to="taylor-series" /> expansion around $\thetavec$,
$$
\gvec_2 = \nabla L_2(\thetavec - \alpha \gvec_1) \approx \bar\gvec_2 - \alpha \bar\Hmat_2 \bar\gvec_1 .
$$
The update directions are then, to order $\alpha$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{MAML:} &\quad \bar\gvec_2 - \alpha \bar\Hmat_2 \bar\gvec_1 - \alpha \bar\Hmat_1 \bar\gvec_2, \\
\text{first-order MAML:} &\quad \gvec_2 \approx \bar\gvec_2 - \alpha \bar\Hmat_2 \bar\gvec_1, \\
\text{Reptile:} &\quad (\gvec_1 + \gvec_2) \approx \bar\gvec_1 + \bar\gvec_2 - \alpha \bar\Hmat_2 \bar\gvec_1 .
\end{aligned}
$$
Take expectations over tasks and minibatches. The minibatches are exchangeable, so $\expect[\bar\gvec_1] =
\expect[\bar\gvec_2]$, call it **AvgGrad**, and $\expect[\bar\Hmat_2 \bar\gvec_1] = \expect[\bar\Hmat_1 \bar\gvec_2]$,
call it **AvgGradInner**. AvgGradInner is half the gradient of $\expect[\bar\gvec_1\transpose \bar\gvec_2]$, because
$\nabla(\bar\gvec_1\transpose \bar\gvec_2) = \bar\Hmat_1 \bar\gvec_2 + \bar\Hmat_2 \bar\gvec_1$. So, in expectation,
$$
\text{MAML} \approx \text{AvgGrad} - 2\alpha\, \text{AvgGradInner}, \qquad
\text{FOMAML} \approx \text{AvgGrad} - \alpha\, \text{AvgGradInner}, \qquad
\text{Reptile} \approx 2\, \text{AvgGrad} - \alpha\, \text{AvgGradInner} .
$$
Descending along $-\text{AvgGradInner}$ increases the inner product between gradients from different minibatches of the
same task. When those gradients agree, a step on one minibatch also lowers the loss on another: the model generalises
within the task from few examples. All three methods contain this term with a positive coefficient; they differ in its
weight relative to the ordinary joint-training term. Plain pooled training has no such term.

## Other views

- **Hierarchical Bayes.** A few gradient steps from $\thetavec$ with early stopping approximate the maximum a posteriori
  estimate under a Gaussian prior centred at $\thetavec$. MAML is then empirical Bayes: it learns the prior mean of a
  hierarchical model across tasks <Cite id="grant2018" />. See <NoteLink to="hierarchical-bayesian-transfer" />.
- **Implicit gradients.** iMAML defines adaptation as the minimiser of $L_i^{\text{tr}}(\phivec) + \frac{\lambda}{2}
  \norm{\phivec - \thetavec}^2$. The implicit function theorem gives the meta-gradient $\paren{\eye + \frac{1}{\lambda}
  \nabla^2 L_i^{\text{tr}}(\phivec_i)}^{-1} \nabla L_i^{\text{val}}(\phivec_i)$, which depends only on the solution, not on
  the path, so memory does not grow with the number of inner steps <Cite id="rajeswaran2019imaml" />.
- **Feature reuse.** Raghu et al. found that on standard few-shot image benchmarks the representations of a
  MAML-trained network barely change in the inner loop; adapting only the final layer (ANIL) matched MAML
  <Cite id="raghu2020anil" />. On those benchmarks, MAML's benefit comes mostly from learning good features.
- **Bias learning.** Baxter's environment of tasks is the statistical model behind all of these: tasks are draws from
  one distribution, and what is learnt is a bias for that distribution <Cite id="baxter2000" />.
- **Algorithm selection.** An older sense of meta-learning learns which algorithm suits which dataset from meta-features
  of datasets; at the level of single examples, <NoteLink to="instance-hardness" /> and <NoteLink
  to="item-response-theory-for-machine-learning">item response theory over many models</NoteLink> describe which
  examples are hard, for which models, and which ones separate good models from poor ones.

## Assumptions

- Meta-test tasks come from the same distribution as meta-training tasks. Meta-learnt initialisations are specialised
  and can adapt worse than a generic one to tasks unlike any seen.
- The inner-loop step size and number of steps at meta-test time match those used in meta-training.

## Pitfalls

- **Weak baselines.** A network pretrained on the pooled meta-training classes and fine-tuned, or used with a linear
  classifier on its features, is a strong baseline for few-shot classification. See <NoteLink
  to="few-shot-learning-evaluation" />.
- **Instability.** Second-order meta-gradients through several steps can explode or vanish. First-order methods and
  per-step learning rates are common remedies.
- **Memory.** Full MAML stores the inner computation graph for every task in the meta-batch.
