<Definition>
**Hierarchical Bayesian transfer** treats the parameters $\wvec_t$ of each task $t$ as draws from a common prior
$p(\wvec \mid \etavec)$ with hyperparameters $\etavec$. The hyperparameters are learnt from the source tasks, either
as a point estimate (empirical Bayes) or as a posterior $p(\etavec \mid \Dcal_1, \dots, \Dcal_T)$. For a new target
task, the learnt prior replaces an uninformative one. With no target data the model predicts like an average source
task; as target data arrive, the posterior moves to the target's own estimate.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A prior says what parameter values are plausible before seeing data. In a single task it must be chosen by hand. Across
many related tasks it can be estimated: if every previous user's activity classifier put a large positive weight on a
given accelerometer feature, a new user's classifier probably does too. The hierarchy makes this precise. How much the
new task borrows is set by the spread of the source tasks around their mean, which is itself estimated from the data.
This is partial pooling, as in <NoteLink to="hierarchical-models" />, with the groups being tasks and the quantity of
interest being the prediction for a new group.

## The Gaussian linear case

Let task $t$ have inputs $\Xmat_t$, targets $\yvec_t$ and the model $\yvec_t = \Xmat_t \wvec_t +
\varepsilonvec_t$ with $\varepsilonvec_t \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2 \eye)$, and let the task
weights share the prior $\wvec_t \sim \Gauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$.

### The target posterior

For a new task with data $(\Xmat, \yvec)$, the prior $\Gauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$ and the Gaussian likelihood give a
Gaussian posterior, as in <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression" />:
$$
\Smat = \paren{\Sigmamat^{-1} + \sigma^{-2} \Xmat\transpose \Xmat}^{-1}, \qquad \mvec = \Smat \paren{\Sigmamat^{-1}
\muvec + \sigma^{-2} \Xmat\transpose \yvec} .
$$
When $\Xmat\transpose\Xmat$ is invertible, write the least-squares estimate as $\hat\wvec = (\Xmat\transpose
\Xmat)^{-1} \Xmat\transpose \yvec$, so that $\Xmat\transpose \yvec = \Xmat\transpose \Xmat \hat\wvec$. Then
$$
\mvec = \Smat \Sigmamat^{-1} \muvec + \Smat\, \sigma^{-2} \Xmat\transpose \Xmat\, \hat\wvec ,
$$
and the two matrix weights sum to $\Smat(\Sigmamat^{-1} + \sigma^{-2} \Xmat\transpose \Xmat) = \eye$. The posterior mean
is a precision-weighted average of the source mean $\muvec$ and the target's own estimate $\hat\wvec$. With no target
data it is $\muvec$. As the target data grow, $\sigma^{-2}\Xmat\transpose\Xmat$ dominates $\Sigmamat^{-1}$ and the
posterior mean tends to $\hat\wvec$. A small $\Sigmamat$, meaning the source tasks agreed with each other, makes the
target borrow for longer. This is the same bias–variance trade-off as in <NoteLink to="when-does-transfer-help" />,
with the weight chosen by the estimated spread of the tasks rather than by hand.

### Worked example

One feature, prior $w \sim \Gauss(2, 0.5^2)$ learnt from source tasks, noise $\sigma = 1$. A new task observes $n = 4$
points at $x = \pm 1$, so $\sum x^2 = 4$, with least-squares slope $\hat w = 3$. The posterior precision is $1/0.25 + 4/1
= 8$ and the posterior mean is $(4 \times 2 + 4 \times 3)/8 = 2.5$, halfway. With $n = 40$ points at $x = \pm 1$ the
precision is $4 + 40 = 44$ and the mean is $(8 + 120)/44 = 2.91$, close to the target's own slope. With a flat prior the
mean would be $3$ in both cases, and with $4$ points its standard deviation would be $0.5$ rather than the hierarchical
$1/\sqrt{8} = 0.35$.

### Learning the prior: empirical Bayes

The hyperparameters are estimated by maximising the marginal likelihood of all source tasks, with each task's weights
integrated out:
$$
p(\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_T \mid \muvec, \Sigmamat) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \Gauss\paren{\yvec_t \mid \Xmat_t \muvec,\; \sigma^2
\eye + \Xmat_t \Sigmamat \Xmat_t\transpose} .
$$
The <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm</NoteLink> treats the $\wvec_t$ as
missing data. The E-step computes each task's posterior $\Gauss(\mvec_t, \Smat_t)$ under the current prior, by the
formulas above. The M-step maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood
$\sum_t \expect\brack{\log \Gauss(\wvec_t \mid \muvec, \Sigmamat)}$, which gives
$$
\muvec = \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \mvec_t, \qquad \Sigmamat = \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \brack{(\mvec_t - \muvec)(\mvec_t -
\muvec)\transpose + \Smat_t} .
$$
The prior mean is the average task, and the prior covariance is the scatter of the tasks plus their individual
uncertainty.

Bakker and Heskes use this model for multi-task neural networks, with a hidden layer shared by all tasks and
task-specific output weights drawn from the hierarchical prior, and extend it to a mixture of Gaussian priors, which
clusters tasks, and to priors gated by task features <Cite id="bakker2003" />.

Empirical Bayes treats the estimated hyperparameters as known, which understates uncertainty when there are few source
tasks. A full Bayesian treatment puts a hyperprior on $(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$, such as a Gaussian on the mean and a Gamma
on each precision, and passes the posterior over them to the target task <Cite id="gelman2013" />.

## Other forms of the same idea

- **Regularised multi-task learning.** The penalty that pulls each task's weights towards their average is the negative
  log of the Gaussian prior above, so its solution is the <Gloss name="map-estimate">maximum a posteriori</Gloss>
  estimate <Cite id="evgeniou2004" />. See <NoteLink to="multi-task-learning" />.
- **Hierarchical and multi-task <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss>.** A shared function with task-specific
  deviations, $f_t \mid g \sim \GP(g, k_f)$, is the nonparametric version; see
  <NoteLink to="hierarchical-gaussian-process" />. Multi-task Gaussian processes couple tasks through a task covariance
  matrix <Cite id="bonilla2008" />.
- **Learning to learn.** Baxter models tasks as draws from an environment and learns the bias from them; the
  hierarchical prior is a Bayesian bias of this kind <Cite id="baxter2000" />.
- **Gradient-based meta-learning.** A few <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> steps from a learnt initialisation
  behave like maximum a posteriori inference under a prior centred at the initialisation, so model-agnostic
  meta-learning can be read as empirical Bayes in a hierarchical model <Cite id="grant2018" />. See
  <NoteLink to="meta-learning" />.
- **Personalisation.** A community of individuals shares a prior over classifier weights, and a new individual starts
  from the community posterior and is updated online as labels arrive. Diethe, Twomey and Flach use this construction
  for activity recognition across people and combine it with <Gloss name="active-learning">active learning</Gloss>
  <Cite id="diethe2016active" /> <Cite
  id="twomey2015evidence" />. See <NoteLink to="active-transfer-learning" />.

## Assumptions

- Tasks are exchangeable: before seeing data, no task is expected to differ from the others in a particular direction.
  Known task features break exchangeability and call for a gated or regression prior.
- The prior family can express how tasks vary. A single Gaussian cannot represent two clusters of tasks; a mixture can.

## Pitfalls

- **Too few source tasks.** The covariance $\Sigmamat$ has $d(d+1)/2$ free entries and is estimated from $T$ tasks.
  With $T$ small, use a diagonal or scaled-identity covariance, or a hyperprior.
- **Transferring certainty.** Source tasks with much data give a narrow prior. If the target differs from them in ways
  the source tasks did not, the narrow prior slows adaptation. Widening the transferred prior, or transferring the mean
  but not the precision, lets the target posterior move to the target's own estimate with fewer target data, at the
  cost of less borrowing when the target resembles the source tasks.
