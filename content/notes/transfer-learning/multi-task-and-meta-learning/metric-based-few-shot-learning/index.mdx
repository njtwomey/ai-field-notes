<Definition>
In **$N$-way $K$-shot classification**, a task presents $N$ classes never seen in training, each with $K$ labelled
**support** examples, and asks for the labels of unlabelled **query** examples. **Metric-based** methods learn an
embedding $f_{\phivec} : \Xcal \to \reals^M$ on many training episodes of the same form, and classify each query by its
similarity to the embedded support examples. Adapting to a new task needs no <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> steps: it is a forward pass through
the embedding and a comparison <Cite id="vinyals2016" /> <Cite id="snell2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier with thousands of parameters cannot be fitted to five examples per class. In a good embedding, in which
examples of one class sit close together and examples of different classes far apart, nearest neighbours work from a
single example. Metric-based methods learn such an embedding from classes that have plenty
of data, so that it works on classes that do not.

## Episodic training

Training mimics the test condition <Cite id="vinyals2016" />. Each training step samples $N$ classes from the training
classes, $K$ support examples and some query examples per class, classifies the queries using only the support set, and
back-propagates the query loss into the embedding. The embedding is optimised for exactly the operation it will perform
on new classes.

## Matching networks

Matching networks predict the label of a query $\hat\xvec$ as an attention-weighted sum of the support labels
<Cite id="vinyals2016" />:
$$
\hat y = \sum_{i=1}^{NK} a(\hat\xvec, \xvec_i)\, \yvec_i, \qquad a(\hat\xvec, \xvec_i) = \frac{\exp\paren{c(f(\hat\xvec),
g(\xvec_i))}}{\sum_{j} \exp\paren{c(f(\hat\xvec), g(\xvec_j))}},
$$
where $\yvec_i$ is the one-hot label of support example $i$, $c$ is <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink>, and $f$ and $g$ embed queries and
support examples. In the full version, $g$ embeds each support example in the context of the whole support set with a
bidirectional <Gloss name="lstm" />, and $f$ attends over the support set. With a single support example per class, the prediction is a
soft nearest neighbour.

## Prototypical networks

Prototypical networks summarise each class by the mean of its embedded support examples, the **prototype**
<Cite id="snell2017" />:
$$
\cvec_k = \frac{1}{\abs{S_k}} \sum_{(\xvec_i, y_i) \in S_k} f_{\phivec}(\xvec_i),
$$
and classify a query by a softmax over negative distances to the prototypes,
$$
p_{\phivec}(y = k \mid \xvec) = \frac{\exp\paren{-d\paren{f_{\phivec}(\xvec), \cvec_k}}}{\sum_{k'} \exp\paren{-d\paren{f_{\phivec}(\xvec),
\cvec_{k'}}}} .
$$
Training minimises the negative log-probability of the true class of each query, averaged over episodes.

### The mean minimises any Bregman divergence

For squared Euclidean distance, the point $\cvec$ that minimises the total distance $\sum_i \norm{\zvec_i - \cvec}^2$
to a class's embedded points $\zvec_i$ solves $\sum_i 2(\cvec - \zvec_i) = \zeros$, which is their mean. More generally,
the mean minimises the expected Bregman divergence from a set of points to a representative, and squared Euclidean
distance is a Bregman divergence. The softmax over negative Bregman divergences is the posterior class probability of a
mixture of <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential-family</NoteLink> densities with those means, so a prototypical network performs mixture density estimation
in the embedding space <Cite id="snell2017" />.

### It is a linear classifier

Expand the squared distance:
$$
-\norm{\zvec - \cvec_k}^2 = -\zvec\transpose \zvec + 2 \cvec_k\transpose \zvec - \cvec_k\transpose \cvec_k .
$$
The first term is the same for every class, so it cancels in the softmax. What remains is linear in the embedding
$\zvec = f_{\phivec}(\xvec)$:
$$
p(y = k \mid \xvec) \propto \exp\paren{\wvec_k\transpose \zvec + b_k}, \qquad \wvec_k = 2\cvec_k, \quad b_k = -\norm{\cvec_k}^2 .
$$
A prototypical network with squared Euclidean distance is therefore <NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression" /> on
the embedding, with weights read off the prototypes instead of fitted. All the nonlinearity is in the embedding. Snell
et al. found squared Euclidean distance clearly better than cosine similarity, and training with more classes per
episode than at test time better still <Cite id="snell2017" />. The same form, class means with a shared metric, gives
<NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis" /> when the embedding is the identity and the covariance is shared.

### Worked example

A 3-way 2-shot episode in a two-dimensional embedding. The support embeddings are $(1, 0)$ and $(1.4, 0.6)$ for class 1,
$(-1, 1)$ and $(-0.6, 1.4)$ for class 2, and $(0, -1.2)$ and $(0.4, -0.8)$ for class 3. The prototypes are the means,
$\cvec_1 = (1.2, 0.3)$, $\cvec_2 = (-0.8, 1.2)$ and $\cvec_3 = (0.2, -1)$. A query embedded at $(0.6, 0.2)$ has squared
distances $0.37$, $2.96$ and $1.60$. The softmax of their negatives gives probabilities $0.731$, $0.055$ and $0.214$,
so the query is assigned to class 1.

## Relation networks

Relation networks replace the fixed distance by a learnt one: a second network takes the concatenated embeddings of a
query and a class and outputs a relation score in $[0, 1]$, trained by regression to $1$ for matching pairs and $0$
otherwise <Cite id="sung2018relation" />. The comparison can then capture similarities that no fixed metric in the
embedding does.

## Cost

Adapting to a task is one forward pass per support and query example and $N$ distance computations per query. There
are no inner-loop gradients, which makes metric-based methods cheaper than <NoteLink to="meta-learning">MAML</NoteLink>
to train and to use.

## Trade-offs

- **The embedding does the work.** Chen et al. and Tian et al. showed that an embedding trained by ordinary supervised
  classification on all training classes, followed by a nearest-centroid or linear classifier, matches or beats
  episodically trained metric methods on standard benchmarks <Cite id="chen2019closer" /> <Cite
  id="tian2020rethinking" />. See <NoteLink to="few-shot-learning-evaluation" />.
- **Fixed capacity per class.** A prototype is one point. Classes with several modes, or support sets with outliers,
  are poorly summarised by their mean.
- **Distribution of classes.** The embedding generalises to new classes that resemble the training classes. For
  classes from a different domain, such as medical images after training on natural images, the benefit shrinks.
