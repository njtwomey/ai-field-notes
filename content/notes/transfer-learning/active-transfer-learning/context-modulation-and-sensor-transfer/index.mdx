<Definition>
In a smart home, activity recognition learns a map from a stream of sensor events, such as a motion sensor turning on
or a door opening, to the activity being performed. Two homes rarely have the same sensors, so their input spaces
differ and transfer between them is **heterogeneous** <Cite id="pan2010survey" />. This note covers two techniques by
Twomey and colleagues for building representations that can be shared. **Context modulation** rewrites an existing
dataset into new, user-defined contexts by deleting, merging and renaming sensors and labels <Cite id="twomey2014context" />.
**Sensor topology learning** estimates from unlabelled data which sensors are adjacent in the home, and uses the
adjacency to build features <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier that learns that sensor `m17` predicts cooking in one house has learnt nothing about a house without an
`m17`. House-to-house transfer needs a vocabulary shared by the homes. Rashidi and Cook assign each sensor a location
label, the room or functional area it is in, build activity templates in both houses and learn a mapping between them
<Cite id="rashidi2011" />. Feuz and Cook instead map the target feature space into the source's with heuristic search,
and evaluate on 18 smart apartments <Cite id="feuz2014" />. Both need to know which sensors belong together. Two
questions follow. How much accuracy does a model lose when the sensors are coarsened to such a vocabulary? And can the
grouping be learnt from the data instead of from floor plans?

## Context modulation

Twomey and Flach define **context modulation** as modifying a dataset so that it represents a new context chosen by the
analyst <Cite id="twomey2014context" />. A sensor event file lists a timestamp, a sensor identifier, its state and the
start or end of labelled activities. Their software applies four kinds of rewrite, each specified by regular expressions:

- **Sensor deletion** removes every event from matching sensors, simulating a failed sensor or a blind spot. The
  activity boundaries on deleted rows are remembered, so labels are not lost.
- **Sensor merging** renames several sensors to one identifier, such as all motion sensors in a room to `m_kitchen`.
  This simulates a sparser layout or sensors with wider fields of view, and produces the room-level vocabulary that
  transfer needs.
- **Feature engineering** rewrites derived attributes, for example mapping hours to periods such as breakfast time,
  or days to weekday and weekend.
- **Label engineering** rewrites activity labels. Removing the resident identifier from labels such as `R1_Work`
  anonymises the task, so that the model classifies activities regardless of who performs them. Harmonising names such as
  "cooking" and "meal preparation" makes labels consistent across datasets, which transfer between homes requires.
  Keeping the resident and the activity as separate labels recasts the problem as multi-task learning.

When several residents are active, an event can belong to any of the currently open activities. The software records
the set of open activities with each event, which makes the labels ambiguous in the sense of <NoteLink to="partial-label-learning" />, and
evaluation counts a prediction as correct if it is in that set.

### What coarsening costs

The experiments use the CASAS `twor.2009` dataset: two residents over about three months, 13 labelled activities of which
eight belong to a specific resident, and 71 sensors, of which 51 are motion sensors spaced about two metres apart
<Cite id="twomey2014context" />. A linear-chain <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field" /> classified each event from the
day of the week, the hour and the current and two previous sensor events. Precision is the fraction of predictions in the set of open
activities; the false-negative rate is the fraction of activities never predicted during their span.

Always predicting the most common label gives a precision of about 36%. Anonymising the labels raised precision by
about 9 points, roughly a 35% reduction in error, which the authors attribute to activities looking the same to the
sensors whoever performs them. Merging motion sensors to room level cost about 12 points of precision and raised the
false-negative rate by a factor of two to three, so that about one activity in five was never predicted: several
activities take place in one room, and a room-level sensor cannot tell them apart. Removing door and light sensors
changed precision and the false-negative rate by less than 4 points. For this home, dense motion sensing mattered and door
and light sensors did not <Cite id="twomey2014context" />.

For transfer, the table measures a price. The room-level vocabulary that makes homes comparable costs this home about
12 points of precision. A transferred model can be no better than a model trained on the target with the same
coarsened representation.

## Learning sensor topologies

Sensor events are usually treated as independent features, but complex activities show up as combinations of sensors.
Taking every combination, the power set of the active sensors, is equivalent to a polynomial kernel and learns weights
for spurious combinations: motion in a bedroom together with motion in the kitchen, when two residents happen to be in
those rooms at once. Twomey, Diethe, Craddock and Flach learn an **adjacency matrix** $\Amat$ of the sensor network from
unlabelled event streams, and allow only combinations of adjacent sensors <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.

### Weighted cross-correlation

Represent the stream as a sparse matrix $\Xmat \in \reals^{m \times n}$, one row per one-second time slice and one column
per sensor, with entries in $\set{0, 1}$ for sensor-on events or $\set{-1, 0, 1}$ for on and off events. Let $\Ymat$ be
$\Xmat$ lagged by $l$ slices. The correlation between columns needs centred data, and centring destroys sparsity. It can
be avoided. For columns $\xvec$ and $\yvec$ with means $\bar x$ and $\bar y$,
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m} (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) = \xvec\transpose \yvec - m \bar x \bar y ,
$$
so the matrix of centred cross-products is $\Xmat\transpose \Ymat - m\, \bar\xvec \bar\yvec\transpose$, where $\bar\xvec$
and $\bar\yvec$ are the column means. For entries in $\set{-1, 0, 1}$, $x_i^2 = \abs{x_i}$, so the variance of a column is
$\frac1m \sum_i \abs{x_i} - \bar x^2$, computable from the column sums of $\abs{\Xmat}$. Every quantity uses sparse
products, and the cost is governed by the number of non-zero entries. On the whole `twor.2009` dataset the computation took
under two seconds <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.

The correlations at lags $l = -L, \dots, L$ are combined with weights $w(l) = \exp(-\abs{l})$ for lags in seconds, so
that short lags, most likely caused by one person walking past both sensors, count most. Positive and negative lags are
treated alike, since the direction of travel does not matter for adjacency. The weighted sum is thresholded at the first
value that gives an average of fewer than five connections per sensor, the heuristic being that a sensor is adjacent to
itself and a few neighbours.

### Mutual information

The second method sets $A_{ij}$ to the <NoteLink to="mutual-information" /> between the on–off states of sensors $i$ and
$j$, on the reasoning that neighbouring sensors switch on and off together. Mutual information is not bounded, so a single
threshold does not fit every pair. The threshold for a pair is the mean of the two sensors' average mutual information with
all sensors, $T_{ij} = \frac12\paren{\expect[A_{i,:}] + \expect[A_{:,j}]}$, and the pair is adjacent if $A_{ij} > T_{ij}$
<Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.

### Using the adjacency

Given $\Amat$, the active sensors in each time slice are split into groups of mutually reachable sensors, which separates
parallel streams of activity by different residents without hand segmentation. Features are the conjunctions of sensors
within a group, up to a maximum cardinality $c$, rather than all conjunctions. The authors describe this as a graph-based
regularisation of the conditional random field's feature space <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.

### Results as reported

The learnt adjacencies were checked against a hand-built adjacency matrix for `twor.2009` by treating the learnt weights
as scores for the hand-built links. The area under the ROC curve was $0.879$ for weighted cross-correlation and $0.843$
for mutual information, and both thresholds gave accuracies of about $0.94$, close to the best achievable. Visually, the
learnt adjacencies respected walls on three differently laid-out testbeds: `twor.2009`, a two-storey home with two
residents; `tokyo`, an open-plan office with nine employees; and `aruba`, a single-resident home with sensors of two
different ranges <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.

scores, and the unrestricted power set overfitted.

## Why this matters for transfer

The authors argue that learnt adjacency matrices give a way to construct high-level features suitable for transfer
between smart houses <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />. The learnt graphs are dense within rooms and sparse between
them, so groups of adjacent sensors approximate functional areas, the vocabulary that house-to-house methods need,
without a floor plan or labels from the new house. Context modulation measures what such a vocabulary costs on a house
with labels <Cite id="twomey2014context" />. With the vocabulary in place, the person-to-person transfer of <NoteLink
to="active-transfer-learning" /> applies within it <Cite id="diethe2016active" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Coarser is more transferable and less accurate.** Room-level features are shared across homes but lose the
  within-room detail that separates activities in the same room.
- **Adjacency from behaviour, not geometry.** A learnt adjacency reflects how people move. Rarely visited areas, or
  sensors with unusual behaviour, can be left unconnected; the authors observed isolated sensors in all three homes.
- **Sequential dependence.** The adjacency features are used inside a conditional random field. Whether structured
  sequence models are needed at all depends on the features <Cite id="twomey2016" />; see <NoteLink
  to="when-is-structure-needed" />.
