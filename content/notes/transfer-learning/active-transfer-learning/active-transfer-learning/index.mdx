<Definition>
**Active transfer learning** combines two ways of saving target labels. **Transfer** initialises the target model with
knowledge from source domains, such as a prior over parameters learnt from other people or other houses. **<Gloss name="active-learning">Active
learning</Gloss>** then chooses which unlabelled target examples to send to an annotator, one at a time or in batches, under a
labelling budget <Cite id="settles2009" />. The transferred model gives useful predictions before any target label
arrives, and its uncertainty guides the choice of queries <Cite id="diethe2016active" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Activity recognition in smart homes has both problems at once <Cite id="diethe2015bayesian" /> <Cite
id="diethe2016active" />. First, the deployment context differs from the learning context. People perform the same
activity in different ways and with different frequencies, and houses differ in layout and in where their sensors are.
Second, labels are expensive and unreliable. Accurate labels come from experts annotating video from head-mounted or
room cameras; cheaper labels come from residents reporting what they are doing, either at the moment through a phone app
or at the end of the day, and are less precise about when an activity started and ended.

Transfer alone gives a model that fits the average person, not this one. Active learning alone starts from nothing: the
first queries are chosen by a model whose uncertainty is itself uninformed, the **cold-start** problem. Combining the
two starts active learning from a model whose uncertainty already reflects what varies between people.

Diethe, Twomey and Flach distinguish two transfer problems <Cite id="diethe2015bayesian" />:

1. **Person to person.** Different people have different activity patterns and perform activities differently. This
   calls for learning about groups of individuals, with group-level priors transferred to a new individual.
2. **House to house.** Different houses have different layouts and sensors, so there may be no correspondence between
   the sensors of two houses. This calls for a shared feature vocabulary. Rashidi and Cook label each sensor with its
   room or functional area and map activities between houses through these meta-features <Cite id="rashidi2011" />. See
   <NoteLink to="context-modulation-and-sensor-transfer" />.

## Mechanism: the community Bayes point machine

The model of Diethe, Twomey and Flach is a hierarchical, multi-class extension of the <NoteLink to="bayes-point-machine">Bayes point machine</NoteLink>, a Bayesian
linear classifier <Cite id="herbrich2001" />. It makes five assumptions <Cite id="diethe2016active" />:

1. the features $\xvec$ are fully observed;
2. the order of instances does not matter;
3. the prediction is a linear discriminant, $p(y_i \mid \xvec_i, \wvec) = p(y_i \mid s_i = \wvec\transpose \xvec_i)$, with
   score $s_i$;
4. the scores are subject to additive Gaussian noise;
5. each individual has their own weights, drawn from a communal prior.

For class $c$ of individual $r$, the weight vector $\wvec_{rc}$ has independent Gaussian components whose means
$\mu_{cd}$ and precisions $\tau_{cd}$, one per class and feature $d$, are shared by the whole community of $R$
individuals. The means have Gaussian priors and the precisions Gamma priors. Each class score $s_{rnc} =
\wvec_{rc}\transpose \xvec_{rn}$ receives Gaussian noise, and the label is the class with the largest noisy score, an
arg-max factor that acts as a probabilistic multi-class switch. Inference uses <Gloss name="ep">expectation propagation</Gloss>
<Cite id="minka2001" />, and <NoteLink to="online-learning">online learning</NoteLink> uses <Gloss name="assumed-density-filtering">assumed-density filtering</Gloss>, which updates the Gaussian posterior one
example at a time <Cite id="opper1998" />.

Assumption 2 ignores the sequential nature of activity data. The authors note that the error it introduces depends on how strongly
consecutive examples depend on each other, which in turn depends on the preprocessing: features computed over windows of
the raw signal weaken the dependence.

### Transfer to a new individual

Training on the community gives posteriors over the shared means and precisions. For a new individual, the same model is
configured with $R = 1$, and the priors over $\mu$ and $\tau$ are replaced by the community posteriors. The new person's
classifier can then predict before seeing any of their data, and it is trained online as their labels arrive. It moves
smoothly from generic predictions that fit anyone in the community to personalised predictions for this person
<Cite id="diethe2016active" />.

When source and target are different datasets, the authors transfer the posterior over the weight means but reset the
precisions to their prior, $\Gamma(1, 1)$: there is no reason for the target to be as certain about the weights as the
source was. Keeping the means keeps the source's decision boundaries as the starting point <Cite
id="diethe2016active" /> <Cite id="twomey2015evidence" />. This is the choice discussed under "transferring certainty" in
<NoteLink to="hierarchical-bayesian-transfer" />.

## Choosing what to label

Three selection rules suit the personalisation phase. Each is myopic: one example is labelled at a time from a pool.

- **Uncertainty sampling** queries the example whose predicted class probability is closest to chance, $0.5$ for two
  classes.
- **Certainty sampling**, the opposite, queries the example the classifier is most confident about. It serves as a
  sanity check <Cite id="diethe2016active" />.
- **Value of information** extends the decision-theoretic method of Kapoor, Horvitz and Basu
  <Cite id="kapoor2007" />.

### Value of information

Let $\Cmat \in \reals^{\abs{\Ycal} \times \abs{\Ycal}}$ be a cost matrix, where $C_{ij}$ is the cost of classifying a
point of class $i$ as class $j$ and $C_{ii} = 0$, and let $p_i$ be the model's predicted probability for example $i$.
The total expected misclassification cost on the labelled set is
$$
J_S = \sum_{a \in \Ycal} \paren{\sum_{i : y_i = a} C_{ai}(1 - p_i) + \sum_{i : y_i \ne a} C_{ia}\, p_i} .
$$
For an unlabelled example $\tilde\xvec_i$ the true class probability $p_i^*$ is unknown and is replaced by the model's
estimate $p_i$:
$$
J_{\tilde\xvec_i} = \sum_{a \in \Ycal} \paren{C_{ai}(1 - p_i)\, p_i^* + C_{ia}\, p_i (1 - p_i^*)} \approx \sum_{a \in
\Ycal} (C_{ai} + C_{ia})\, p_i (1 - p_i) .
$$
The value of information of labelling $\tilde\xvec_i$ adds the label costs, expressed in the same currency as the
misclassification costs, and the example with the highest value is queried. A negative value for the best candidate
means that no single label reduces the total cost, which gives a stopping rule <Cite id="diethe2016active" />.

The workshop version of the framework allows several labelling methods $j = 1, \dots, n$ with costs $L_j$ and a quality
$0 \le G_j \le 1$, the expected gain from a label of method $j$, where $G_j = 1$ is perfect labelling. The criterion
becomes
$$
\text{VOI}(\tilde\xvec_i, j) = J_S + J_{\tilde\xvec_i} + \sum_{i'=1}^{m} G_j L_j(\xvec_{i'}) - G_j L_j(\tilde\xvec_i),
$$
and the pair of example and labelling method with the highest value is chosen; labelling stops when the value is
negative for every method <Cite id="diethe2015bayesian" />. This lets the system decide between asking the resident on
their phone and paying for video annotation.

For two classes with symmetric costs and a label cost that does not
depend on the example, the approximate $J_{\tilde\xvec_i}$ is proportional to $p_i(1 - p_i)$, which is largest at $p_i = 1/2$. The value
of information then ranks the pool in the same order as uncertainty sampling. The two rules differ when costs are
asymmetric, when there are more than two classes, or when label costs vary.

## Experimental effects of transfer and selection

The two halves of the method solve different problems, and experiments separate them. Transfer fixes the start. In
transfer between two accelerometer datasets recorded by different groups with different sensors
<Cite id="anguita2013" /> <Cite id="zhang2012uschad" />, the community posterior used as a prior gave about 70%
accuracy on a new subject before any of that subject's labels <Cite id="diethe2016active" />. Without transfer the same
model started at chance.

Active selection fixes the spread. The active rules gave consistently lower variance across subjects than random
sampling, but no single rule was best, and the value of information did not beat uncertainty sampling on the real data
<Cite id="diethe2016active" />. The last result is expected from the approximation above: with two classes and uniform
costs the two rules rank the pool almost identically.

A synthetic experiment exposes a failure of uncertainty sampling. When most candidate examples are uninformative, for
instance with feature vectors set to zero by a sensor fault, the model is permanently unsure about them, and
uncertainty sampling keeps querying them <Cite id="diethe2016active" />. A zero feature vector gives the linear score
$s = 0$ whatever the weights, so its label carries no information about $\wvec$. A rule that measures what the label
would teach about $\wvec$, such as the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">information gain</NoteLink> of <NoteLink to="bayesian-active-learning" />,
avoids them. The approximate value of information above does not, because with two classes and symmetric costs it
ranks the pool by $p_i(1 - p_i)$, as uncertainty sampling does.

## Other approaches

- **Stream-based active transfer.** Kale and Liu combine labelled source data with an importance-weighted active learner
  that sees target examples as a stream and queries each with some probability. They derive a generalisation bound and
  find that the source data mitigate the cold start <Cite id="kale2013" />. See <NoteLink
  to="stream-based-active-learning" />.
- **Label complexity.** Yang, Hanneke and Carbonell bound the total number of labels needed to learn a sequence of related
  target tasks, and show that with transfer it can be much smaller than learning each task separately, including for
  self-verifying active learning <Cite id="yang2013transfer" />.
- **<Gloss name="domain-adaptation">Domain adaptation</Gloss> with queries.** Rai et al. start active learning in the target from a classifier trained on the
  source, and filter candidate queries with a classifier that separates source-like from target-like inputs
  <Cite id="rai2010" />. Chattopadhyay et al. choose source weights and a batch of target queries jointly, so that the
  reweighted source and the labelled target batch match the target distribution in <Gloss name="mmd">maximum mean discrepancy</Gloss>
  <Cite id="chattopadhyay2013" />. Su et al. weight uncertainty by a domain discriminator's estimate of how target-like
  an example is <Cite id="su2020aada" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Transferring means versus precisions.** Transferring posterior certainty along with posterior means can make the target model
  slow to move away from the source. Resetting the precisions to the prior keeps the source's direction while allowing
  fast personalisation.
- **Myopic selection.** Choosing one example at a time with a one-step criterion ignores what later labels will reveal,
  and can choose redundant examples when run in batches.
- **Selection bias.** Actively chosen examples are not a random sample of the target. Accuracy measured on them is
  biased, so evaluation needs a separate random hold-out set.
- **Uninformative candidates.** Uncertainty sampling favours examples that are uncertain because they carry no signal,
  such as corrupted or empty feature vectors. Filter them, or use a criterion that measures information about the model.
