<Definition>
**Bayesian active learning** keeps a posterior $p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal)$ over model parameters given the labelled data
$\Dcal$, and chooses the next input $\xvec$ from a pool to maximise the **expected information gain** about
$\thetavec$ from observing its label:
$$
\text{EIG}(\xvec) = \entropy\brack{\thetavec \mid \Dcal} - \expect_{y \sim p(y \mid \xvec, \Dcal)} \entropy\brack{\thetavec
\mid \Dcal \cup \set{(\xvec, y)}} .
$$
This equals the mutual information between the label and the parameters, which can be computed in the space of labels:
$\entropy\brack{y \mid \xvec, \Dcal} - \expect_{\thetavec \sim p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal)} \entropy\brack{y \mid \xvec,
\thetavec}$, the form known as **BALD** <Cite id="lindley1956" /> <Cite id="mackay1992info" /> <Cite
id="houlsby2011bald" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A label is worth asking for if the answer will change what the model believes. Two things make the answer uncertain.
The model may not know which parameters are right, so different plausible models predict differently: asking resolves
the disagreement. Or the label may be inherently random given the input, so every plausible model predicts a coin flip:
asking tells nothing about the model. Only the first kind of uncertainty is worth paying for. Predictive entropy mixes
the two; the expected information gain keeps only the first. The decomposition is the one in <NoteLink
to="aleatoric-and-epistemic-uncertainty" />.

## From parameter space to label space

The definition in parameter space needs the entropy of a posterior over many parameters for every candidate label, which
is intractable for most models. The mutual information is symmetric, $I(\thetavec; y) = I(y; \thetavec)$, which gives a
form that needs entropies of the label only. Write $\entropy$ for entropy and condition everything on $\xvec$ and
$\Dcal$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{EIG}(\xvec) &= \entropy[\thetavec] - \expect_y \entropy[\thetavec \mid y] = I(\thetavec; y) \\
&= \entropy[y] - \expect_{\thetavec} \entropy[y \mid \thetavec] \\
&= \entropy\brack{\expect_{\thetavec}\, p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)} - \expect_{\thetavec}\, \entropy\brack{p(y \mid \xvec,
\thetavec)} .
\end{aligned}
$$
The first line is the definition of <NoteLink to="mutual-information" /> as the expected reduction in entropy of one
variable from observing the other. The second writes the same quantity with the roles swapped. The third uses that the
predictive distribution is the posterior average of the model's predictions. For classification both terms are entropies
of a distribution over classes and can be computed from posterior samples of $\thetavec$ <Cite id="houlsby2011bald" />.

The first term is the **predictive entropy**; maximising it alone is uncertainty sampling <Cite id="lewis1994" />. The
second is the **expected entropy**, the uncertainty that would remain even if the parameters were known. BALD, *Bayesian
active learning by disagreement*, is their difference: it is high where individual posterior samples are confident but
disagree with each other.

### Worked example

Suppose the posterior puts probability $1/2$ on each of two parameter settings, $\thetavec_1$ and $\thetavec_2$. At input
A, both predict $p(y = 1) = 0.5$. At input B, $\thetavec_1$ predicts $0.9$ and $\thetavec_2$ predicts $0.1$. With the
binary entropy $h(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1 - p) \log_2 (1 - p)$:

- Both inputs have predictive probability $0.5$ and predictive entropy $h(0.5) = 1$ bit, so uncertainty sampling cannot
  choose between them.
- At A the expected entropy is $1$ bit, so the information gain is $0$: the label is a coin flip under both hypotheses and
  its answer changes nothing.
- At B the expected entropy is $h(0.9) = 0.469$ bits, so the information gain is $0.531$ bits: the label will favour one
  hypothesis over the other by odds of $9$ to $1$.

BALD queries B.

## Computing it

- **Gaussian process classifiers.** For a probit likelihood and a Gaussian posterior with mean $\mu_{\xvec}$ and variance
  $\sigma_{\xvec}^2$ for the latent function, Houlsby et al. approximate the expected entropy with a Gaussian-shaped curve
  and obtain, in bits,
  $$
  \text{BALD}(\xvec) \approx h\paren{\Phi\paren{\frac{\mu_{\xvec}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\xvec}^2 + 1}}}} - \frac{C}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\xvec}^2 +
  C^2}} \exp\paren{-\frac{\mu_{\xvec}^2}{2(\sigma_{\xvec}^2 + C^2)}}, \qquad C = \sqrt{\frac{\pi \ln 2}{2}},
  $$
  where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function <Cite id="houlsby2011bald" />.
- **Deep networks.** Samples from an approximate posterior, such as Monte Carlo dropout, give both terms by averaging over
  $T$ stochastic forward passes <Cite id="gal2017dbal" />.
- **Batches.** Taking the $b$ inputs with the highest individual scores picks near-duplicates, because their information
  overlaps. BatchBALD scores a batch by the joint mutual information $I(y_1, \dots, y_b; \thetavec)$, which counts shared
  information once, and builds the batch greedily <Cite id="kirsch2019batchbald" />.

Other criteria target the goal of learning rather than the parameters: expected reduction in prediction error or
variance <Cite id="cohn1996" />, or expected reduction in misclassification cost net of labelling cost, the value of
information used in <NoteLink to="active-transfer-learning" /> <Cite id="kapoor2007" />.

## Evidence-based instance selection

Twomey, Diethe and Flach propose a selection rule built on Bayesian model comparison rather than on entropy
<Cite id="twomey2015evidence" />. The **evidence** of a model $H$ is its marginal likelihood, $p(\Dcal \mid H) = \int
p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec, H)\, p(\thetavec \mid H)\, d\thetavec$, and the **Bayes factor** $p(\Dcal \mid H_1) / p(\Dcal \mid
H_2)$ measures how strongly the data favour $H_1$ over $H_2$ (see <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison" />).

For a binary problem with labelled set $L$ and a candidate unlabelled instance $\xvec_u$, the rule is:

1. Update the current model with $(\xvec_u, \oplus)$ to obtain a candidate model $M_\oplus$, and with $(\xvec_u,
   \ominus)$ to obtain $M_\ominus$. The updates are Bayesian, by expectation propagation.
2. Compute the evidence of the labelled set extended by each hypothesis under its candidate model:
   $E_\oplus = \text{Evidence}(L \cup \set{(\xvec_u, \oplus)}, M_\oplus)$ and $E_\ominus = \text{Evidence}(L \cup
   \set{(\xvec_u, \ominus)}, M_\ominus)$.
3. Score the instance by the **maximal Bayes factor**, $\text{BF}(\xvec_u) = \max\set{E_\oplus / E_\ominus, E_\ominus /
   E_\oplus}$, so that strong evidence in either direction gives a large score.
4. Query the instance with the smallest score, $u = \argmin_{u \in U} \text{BF}(\xvec_u)$: the one for which neither
   hypothesised label is strongly preferred.

Because the whole labelled set enters each evidence, the score is not the model's predictive probability at $\xvec_u$
alone, which is what distinguishes it from uncertainty sampling. For comparison: if each evidence were the exact
marginal likelihood under the original prior, then $E_\oplus = p(L)\, p(\oplus \mid \xvec_u, L)$, the ratio
$E_\oplus / E_\ominus$ would be the predictive odds, and the rule would reduce to uncertainty sampling. The difference
comes from evaluating each evidence under the model already updated with the hypothesised label. Bayes factors are read
on conventional scales of weak, moderate, strong and decisive evidence <Cite id="kass1995" />, and the authors suggest a
stopping rule based on the band into which the selected instance's Bayes factor falls, without testing it.

Learning is online and myopic: once the parameters have been updated with an instance, that instance is not used for
classification again, although it is used for model selection. The rule extends to more than two classes by comparing
the evidence of every class hypothesis.

The rule was tested on a binary transfer task between two accelerometer datasets, with the source posterior over
weight means as the target prior <Cite id="twomey2015evidence" />. It beat random selection and gave better-calibrated
predictions, measured by the Brier score, than uncertainty sampling. Its cost is much higher than that of uncertainty
sampling: each candidate needs two model updates and two evidence computations.

## Other kinds of query

The query need not be a single label. Poyiadzi, Santos-Rodriguez and Twomey let the learner ask for the label of a
**bag** of instances, answered with the bag's class proportions. The answer carries less information than the instance
labels, but they show that learning remains effective, which suits annotators who find aggregate questions easier to
answer <Cite id="poyiadzi2019" />. See <NoteLink to="learning-from-label-proportions" />.

## Assumptions

- The model is well specified enough that its posterior uncertainty means something. A misspecified model's disagreement
  can point to regions where no setting of its parameters fits.
- The pool is representative of the inputs the model will face.

## Pitfalls

- **Posterior approximations.** Information gain computed from a poor approximate posterior, such as too few samples or an
  overconfident variational fit, can be badly wrong.
- **Myopia.** Greedy one-step information gain can be far from the best sequence of queries.
- **Sampling bias.** Actively chosen labelled sets are not random samples; estimates of accuracy from them are biased.
