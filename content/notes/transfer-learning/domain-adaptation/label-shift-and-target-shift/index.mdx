<Definition>
**Label shift**, also called **target shift**, is the case in which the source and target share the class-conditional
distributions, $p_S(\xvec \mid y) = p_T(\xvec \mid y)$, and differ in the class proportions, $p_S(y) \ne p_T(y)$.
**Conditional shift** is the case in which $p(\xvec \mid y)$ changes too. In domain adaptation, labelled source data
and unlabelled target inputs are available, and the task is to estimate the target proportions and correct the
classifier for them <Cite id="zhang2013target" /> <Cite id="lipton2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Label shift is the natural shift when the label causes the input. A disease produces symptoms; a spoken word produces a
sound. How a disease presents does not depend on how common it is, so $p(\xvec \mid y)$ is stable, while prevalence
changes between hospitals, seasons and populations. Covariate shift is the natural shift in the other direction, when
the input causes the label. The two assumptions are incompatible in general: if $p(y)$ changes and $p(\xvec \mid y)$
does not, then $p(\xvec)$ and $p(y \mid \xvec)$ both change.

The single-classifier view of the problem, correcting the posterior of an already trained model at deployment, is in
<NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction" />. This note takes the domain-adaptation view: identify the shift from
unlabelled target data, and train for the target.

## The reweighted risk

With $w(y) = p_T(y) / p_S(y)$ and $p(\xvec \mid y)$ shared, the target risk is a reweighted source risk:
$$
R_T(h) = \sum_y p_T(y) \int p(\xvec \mid y)\, \ell(h(\xvec), y)\, d\xvec = \sum_y p_S(y)\, w(y) \int p(\xvec \mid y)\,
\ell(h(\xvec), y)\, d\xvec = \expect_S\brack{w(y)\, \ell(h(\xvec), y)} .
$$
This is importance weighting with weights on labels instead of inputs. There are only $K$ weights for $K$ classes, so
their variance is small unless a class is rare in the source and common in the target.

## Identifying the target proportions

The target labels are unobserved, so $w$ must be estimated from target inputs. The key identity is that the target
input distribution is a mixture of the shared class conditionals with the target proportions:
$$
p_T(\xvec) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p(\xvec \mid y = k)\, p_T(y = k) .
$$
Apply any feature map $\phivec$ and take expectations. With $\muvec_k = \expect\brack{\phivec(\xvec) \mid y = k}$ the
class-conditional mean feature, which the labelled source estimates,
$$
\expect_T\brack{\phivec(\xvec)} = \sum_{k} \muvec_k\, p_T(y = k) = \sum_k \muvec_k\, p_S(y = k)\, w_k .
$$
This is a linear system in the $K$ unknowns $w_k$. The target mean on the left is estimated from unlabelled target
inputs. The proportions are **identified** when the $K$ vectors $\muvec_k$ are linearly independent, so that different
mixtures give different means.

Two choices of $\phivec$ give the two standard estimators.

- **Black-box shift estimation** takes $\phivec(\xvec)$ to be the one-hot vector of a fixed classifier's predicted
  label. Then $\expect_T[\phivec]$ is the vector $\qvec$ of predicted-label frequencies on the target, and
  $\muvec_k\, p_S(y = k)$ is column $k$ of the joint confusion matrix $\Cmat_{jk} = p_S(\hat y = j, y = k)$, estimated on
  held-out source data. The system becomes $\qvec = \Cmat \wvec$, solved as $\hat\wvec = \hat\Cmat^{-1} \hat\qvec$
  <Cite id="lipton2018" />. The classifier need not be accurate or calibrated; its confusion matrix must be invertible.
  The derivation of the estimator and a worked example are in <NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction" />.
- **Kernel mean matching of labels** takes $\phivec$ to be the feature map of a characteristic kernel. For discrete labels, the
  method of Zhang et al. chooses $\betavec \ge \zeros$ to minimise $\norm{\sum_k \beta_k\, \hat p_S(y = k)\, \hat\muvec_k -
  \hat\muvec_T}^2$ in the reproducing-kernel Hilbert space, subject to $\sum_k \beta_k \hat p_S(y = k) = 1$ <Cite
  id="zhang2013target" />; they state it for general labels with a conditional embedding operator. This is the label analogue of kernel mean matching for covariate shift (see <NoteLink
  to="importance-weighting-for-covariate-shift" />), a quadratic programme in $K$ variables, and it uses the maximum
  mean discrepancy of <Cite id="gretton2012" />.

A third estimator maximises the likelihood of the target inputs under the mixture, by the EM algorithm of Saerens et
al. <Cite id="saerens2002" />. Garg et al. show that black-box shift estimation is roughly maximum likelihood with a
particular coarse calibration of the classifier, that maximum likelihood is consistent when the classifier is calibrated
and the confusion matrix is invertible, and that black-box shift estimation loses efficiency because of its coarse
calibration <Cite id="garg2020" />. Alexandari et al. found that maximum likelihood after
bias-corrected calibration matched or beat black-box shift estimation <Cite id="alexandari2020" />.

Once $\hat w$ is available there are two uses. Retrain with the weighted risk above, or keep the source classifier
and multiply its predicted class probabilities by $\hat w_k$ and renormalise, which is <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem" />
applied with the new prior.

## Conditional and generalised target shift

If the class conditionals also change, the mixture identity fails and the problem is not identifiable without further
assumptions. Zhang et al. assume the change in each class conditional is a **location–scale** transformation, $\xvec
\mapsto \Wmat_k \xvec + \bvec_k$ for class $k$, and estimate the transformation together with the proportions by kernel
mean matching; they call this **generalised target shift** <Cite id="zhang2013target" />. More flexible assumptions,
such as matching features learnt by a network, lose identifiability: many combinations of new proportions and new
conditionals explain the same target inputs.

## Interaction with feature alignment

Methods that align the marginal feature distributions, such as <NoteLink to="domain-adversarial-training" />, assume the
aligned domains should look alike. Under label shift they should not: the target inputs are a differently weighted
mixture of the same classes, so their marginal distribution legitimately differs. Forcing it to match the source moves
target inputs of the more common target class onto source regions of other classes. Zhao et al. prove that when the
label distributions differ, invariant representations increase the combined source and target error by an amount that
grows with the difference between label distributions <Cite id="zhao2019invariant" />. Weighting the source by $\hat
w(y)$ before aligning removes this conflict.

## Assumptions

- The class conditionals are shared, or change only in the assumed parametric way.
- Every class present in the target is present in the source.
- The features used for matching separate the classes, so that the linear system is well conditioned.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating label shift as covariate shift.** Input-space importance weights assume $p(y \mid \xvec)$ is fixed, which
  label shift violates.
- **Ill-conditioned systems.** With a weak classifier or many similar classes, $\hat\Cmat$ is close to singular and the
  estimated weights are noisy or negative. Clip to non-negative values and renormalise.
- **Rare source classes.** A class with few source examples has a poorly estimated conditional mean and, if common in
  the target, a large weight.
