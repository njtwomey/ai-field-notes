<Definition>
In **domain generalisation**, training data come from several source domains, or *environments*, $e \in \Ecal_{\text{tr}}$,
each with its own distribution $p^e(\xvec, y)$, and the environment of each example is known. The goal is a predictor
$f$ with low risk $R^e(f) = \expect_{p^e}\brack{\ell(f(\xvec), y)}$ on environments not seen during training, from which
no data, labelled or unlabelled, are available. It differs from <Gloss name="domain-adaptation">domain adaptation</Gloss>, which sees unlabelled target inputs
<Cite id="blanchard2011" /> <Cite id="muandet2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

With one source domain, nothing distinguishes a stable relation between input and label from an accident of how the data
were collected. With several, relations that hold in every domain can be told apart from relations that vary. A
classifier of cows and camels trained on photographs in which cows stand on grass and camels on sand can use the
background; if the proportion of grassy camels differs between training environments, the background's predictive power
changes across them and the animal's shape does not. Domain generalisation methods try to learn the relations that do
not change, on the assumption that they will not change in the next environment either.

## Formal views

- **A distribution over domains.** Blanchard et al. treat each domain's distribution as a draw from a
  meta-distribution, and learn a classifier that takes the domain's input distribution as a second argument. A new
  domain's unlabelled test inputs then select its classifier, and generalisation bounds hold over the meta-distribution
  <Cite id="blanchard2011" />.
- **Invariant features.** Domain-invariant component analysis finds a kernel feature map that minimises the variance
  of the domains' distributions around their mean in the feature space, while keeping the information about the label
  <Cite id="muandet2013" />.
- **Worst-case risk.** Distributionally robust optimisation minimises $\max_{e \in \Ecal_{\text{tr}}} R^e(f)$ rather than
  the average; see <NoteLink to="robustness-to-distribution-shift" /> for group DRO <Cite id="sagawa2020" />.
- **Meta-learning.** Split the training domains into meta-train and meta-test domains on each step, and update the
  parameters so that a step that lowers the meta-train loss also lowers the meta-test loss <Cite id="li2018mldg" />. See
  <NoteLink to="meta-learning" />.

## Invariant risk minimisation

**Invariant risk minimisation** (IRM) asks for a representation $\Phi : \Xcal \to \Hcal$ on which a single classifier
$w$ is optimal in every training environment at once <Cite id="arjovsky2019" />:
$$
\min_{\Phi,\, w} \sum_{e \in \Ecal_{\text{tr}}} R^e(w \circ \Phi) \quad \text{subject to} \quad w \in \argmin_{\bar w}
R^e(\bar w \circ \Phi) \text{ for all } e \in \Ecal_{\text{tr}} .
$$
The constraint is a bilevel problem, one inner optimisation per environment. Its practical version, **IRMv1**, fixes the
classifier to the scalar $w = 1$, so that $\Phi$ itself produces the prediction, and replaces the constraint by a penalty
on how far $w = 1$ is from optimal in each environment:
$$
\min_\Phi \sum_{e \in \Ecal_{\text{tr}}} \paren{R^e(\Phi) + \lambda \norm{\nabla_{w \mid w = 1} R^e(w \cdot \Phi)}^2} .
$$
If $w = 1$ is optimal for environment $e$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of $R^e(w \cdot \Phi)$ in $w$ is zero at $w = 1$, so the
penalty is zero. For the squared loss the condition reads
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w} \expect_e\brack{(w\, \Phi(\xvec) - y)^2} \Big|_{w = 1} = 2\, \expect_e\brack{\Phi(\xvec)
\paren{\Phi(\xvec) - y}} = 0 \iff \frac{\expect_e\brack{\Phi(\xvec)\, y}}{\expect_e\brack{\Phi(\xvec)^2}} = 1 :
$$
the least-squares coefficient of $y$ on the feature is the same, $1$, in every environment. IRMv1 penalises features
whose regression coefficient varies across environments.

### Worked example: a spurious feature

Arjovsky et al. use a three-variable structural model with an environment parameter $\sigma^2$ <Cite id="arjovsky2019" />:
$$
X_1 \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2), \qquad Y = X_1 + \Gauss(0, \sigma^2), \qquad X_2 = Y + \Gauss(0, 1).
$$
$X_1$ causes $Y$, and $X_2$ is an effect of $Y$. Compute the least-squares coefficients in an environment with a given
$\sigma^2$. The variances and covariances are $\var(X_1) = \sigma^2$, $\var(Y) = 2\sigma^2$, $\var(X_2) = 2\sigma^2 +
1$, $\cov(X_1, Y) = \cov(X_1, X_2) = \sigma^2$ and $\cov(X_2, Y) = 2\sigma^2$.

- **Regress on $X_1$ alone:** coefficient $\cov(X_1, Y)/\var(X_1) = 1$ in every environment.
- **Regress on $X_2$ alone:** coefficient $2\sigma^2 / (2\sigma^2 + 1)$, which depends on the environment.
- **Regress on both:** the normal equations $\sigma^2 \beta_1 + \sigma^2 \beta_2 = \sigma^2$ and $\sigma^2 \beta_1 +
  (2\sigma^2 + 1)\beta_2 = 2\sigma^2$ give $\beta_1 + \beta_2 = 1$, then $\beta_2 = \sigma^2 / (\sigma^2 + 1)$ and
  $\beta_1 = 1 / (\sigma^2 + 1)$. Both depend on the environment.

For $\sigma^2 = 1$ the regression on both features has coefficients $(0.5, 0.5)$; for $\sigma^2 = 3$ it has $(0.25,
0.75)$. The pooled least-squares fit uses $X_2$ heavily, because $X_2$ is the better predictor within each training
environment. In an environment with $\sigma^2 = 100$ it is miscalibrated, and under an intervention that sets $X_2$
directly it is wrong. Only the regression on $X_1$ has the same coefficient everywhere, and it is the one IRM selects.

## What IRM does not guarantee

Rosenfeld, Ravikumar and Risteski analyse IRM in a model with invariant and environment-specific latent features
<Cite id="rosenfeld2021" />. In the linear case, IRM recovers the invariant predictor only when the number of training
environments exceeds the number of spurious feature dimensions; with fewer, it can rely on the spurious features just as <Gloss name="erm" /> does. In the
nonlinear case they construct predictors that satisfy the IRMv1 condition nearly perfectly on the training environments
while relying on spurious features, and that fail on test environments that are not close to the training ones.

## The DomainBed finding

Methods for domain generalisation had been evaluated with inconsistent datasets, architectures and, above all, model
selection rules. Some selected hyperparameters with labelled data from the test domain, which the setting forbids.
Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz built DomainBed, a testbed of seven multi-domain datasets, nine baseline algorithms and three
model selection criteria, and reran the comparison <Cite id="gulrajani2021" />. Carefully tuned empirical risk
minimisation on the pooled training domains, with <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>, performed as well as the best alternatives on every
dataset. Their conclusion is that a domain-generalisation algorithm without a model selection rule that uses only
training domains is incomplete.

The three selection criteria are:

1. **training-domain validation:** hold out part of each training domain and pick the model with the best pooled
   held-out accuracy;
2. **leave-one-domain-out:** hold out a whole training domain in turn and average;
3. **test-domain validation:** use a small labelled sample of the test domain, an oracle that indicates an upper limit.

## Assumptions

- The test environment is related to the training environments in the way the method assumes: drawn from the same
  meta-distribution, or sharing the invariant mechanism.
- The training environments differ enough to expose the spurious relations. Environments that differ little give
  invariance penalties nothing to detect.
- The environment label of every training example is known.

## Pitfalls

- **Selecting models on the test domain.** It turns domain generalisation into domain adaptation with labelled target
  data and inflates reported gains <Cite id="gulrajani2021" />.
- **Too few environments.** Invariance-based methods need many diverse environments relative to the number of spurious
  directions <Cite id="rosenfeld2021" />.
- **Reading invariance as causality.** A relation can be invariant across the training environments by accident and
  still change in a new one.
