<Definition>
In **unsupervised domain adaptation** a learner sees labelled examples from a source distribution $\Dcal_S$ over
inputs $\Xcal$ and unlabelled inputs from a target distribution $\Dcal_T$, and must output a classifier $h \in \Hcal$
with low target error $R_T(h)$. **Domain adaptation theory** bounds $R_T(h)$ by quantities that can be estimated from
these data. The central result, due to Ben-David et al., is
$$
R_T(h) \le R_S(h) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) + \lambda
$$
for every $h \in \Hcal$, where $d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}$ is a divergence between the input distributions that depends on the
hypothesis class and $\lambda$ is the smallest combined source and target error of any single hypothesis
<Cite id="bendavid2007" /> <Cite id="bendavid2010" />.
</Definition>

## Setting and notation

Each domain is a distribution $\Dcal$ over $\Xcal$ with a labelling function $f : \Xcal \to [0, 1]$, the probability
that the label is $1$. The **error** of a hypothesis $h : \Xcal \to \set{0, 1}$ against a labelling $f$ under
$\Dcal$ is the expected disagreement
$$
R(h, f) = \expect_{\xvec \sim \Dcal} \abs{h(\xvec) - f(\xvec)} .
$$
Write $R_S(h) = R_S(h, f_S)$ and $R_T(h) = R_T(h, f_T)$ for the source and target errors, and $R_S(h, h')$ for the
source probability that two hypotheses disagree. This disagreement satisfies the triangle inequality
$R(f_1, f_2) \le R(f_1, f_3) + R(f_3, f_2)$ for any three labelling functions, because $\abs{a - b} \le \abs{a - c} +
\abs{c - b}$ holds pointwise.

## Why a naive divergence is not enough

The total variation distance $d_1(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) = 2 \sup_B \abs{\Dcal_S(B) - \Dcal_T(B)}$, taken over all measurable
sets $B$, gives a first bound: $R_T(h) \le R_S(h) + d_1(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) + \min\set{\expect_{\Dcal_S}\abs{f_S -
f_T}, \expect_{\Dcal_T}\abs{f_S - f_T}}$ <Cite id="bendavid2010" />. It has two defects. It cannot be estimated
accurately from finite samples of arbitrary distributions, and it is too strict: it counts differences on sets where
no hypothesis in $\Hcal$ can make a mistake. Ben-David et al. replace it with a divergence that looks only at the sets a
hypothesis class can carve out.

## The $\Hcal$-divergence

For a hypothesis class $\Hcal$, the **$\Hcal$-divergence** between two distributions is
$$
d_{\Hcal}(\Dcal, \Dcal') = 2 \sup_{h \in \Hcal} \abs{\pr_{\Dcal}\brack{h(\xvec) = 1} - \pr_{\Dcal'}\brack{h(\xvec) = 1}} .
$$
It is the largest difference in the mass that the two distributions give to a set of the form $\set{\xvec : h(\xvec) =
1}$ <Cite id="kifer2004" />. It is never larger than the total variation distance. For a class of finite VC dimension
it can be estimated from samples: if $U$ and $U'$ are samples of size $m$ and $d$ is the VC dimension of $\Hcal$, then
with probability at least $1 - \delta$,
$$
d_{\Hcal}(\Dcal, \Dcal') \le \hat{d}_{\Hcal}(U, U') + 4 \sqrt{\frac{d \log(2m) + \log(2/\delta)}{m}}
$$
<Cite id="bendavid2010" />.

**Estimating it with a domain classifier.** Label every source input $0$ and every target input $1$, and train a
classifier from $\Hcal$ to tell them apart. If $\Hcal$ is symmetric (it contains $1 - h$ whenever it contains $h$), the
empirical divergence is determined by the best achievable error $\epsilon$ on this balanced problem:
$$
\hat{d}_{\Hcal}(U, U') = 2\paren{1 - 2\epsilon}.
$$
A classifier that cannot beat chance, $\epsilon = 1/2$, gives divergence $0$; one that separates the domains perfectly
gives the maximum, $2$. Finding the minimum-error classifier is intractable for most classes, so in practice a linear or
kernel classifier is trained with a convex loss and its held-out error is plugged in. The result is called the **proxy
$\Acal$-distance**; it approximates the divergence from below <Cite id="bendavid2007" />.

## The symmetric difference class

The bound needs a divergence for the class of disagreement regions. The **symmetric difference hypothesis class**
$\Hcal\Delta\Hcal$ contains $g(\xvec) = h(\xvec) \oplus h'(\xvec)$ for all $h, h' \in \Hcal$, where $\oplus$ is
exclusive or: $g$ is $1$ exactly where $h$ and $h'$ disagree. Its divergence is therefore
$$
d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) = 2 \sup_{h, h' \in \Hcal} \abs{R_S(h, h') - R_T(h, h')},
$$
twice the largest change, between domains, in how often two hypotheses disagree. It follows at once that for any $h,
h' \in \Hcal$,
$$
\abs{R_S(h, h') - R_T(h, h')} \le \tfrac{1}{2}\, d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T).
$$
The VC dimension of $\Hcal\Delta\Hcal$ is at most twice that of $\Hcal$, so the finite-sample estimate above applies
with $2d$ in place of $d$ <Cite id="bendavid2010" />.

## Deriving the bound

Let $h^* = \argmin_{h \in \Hcal} \brack{R_S(h) + R_T(h)}$ be the **ideal joint hypothesis** and $\lambda = R_S(h^*) +
R_T(h^*)$ its combined error. For any $h \in \Hcal$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_T(h) &\le R_T(h^*) + R_T(h, h^*) && \text{triangle inequality through } h^* \\
&\le R_T(h^*) + R_S(h, h^*) + \abs{R_T(h, h^*) - R_S(h, h^*)} && \text{add and subtract } R_S(h, h^*) \\
&\le R_T(h^*) + R_S(h, h^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) && \text{by the inequality above} \\
&\le R_T(h^*) + R_S(h) + R_S(h^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) && \text{triangle inequality through } f_S \\
&= R_S(h) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(\Dcal_S, \Dcal_T) + \lambda .
\end{aligned}
$$
The first line compares $h$ with the target labels by way of $h^*$. The second moves the disagreement of $h$ and $h^*$
from the target to the source, where it can be measured without target labels. The third bounds the cost of that move by
the divergence. The fourth splits the source disagreement into the errors of $h$ and $h^*$ against the source labels.
Replacing the divergence by its estimate from $m$ unlabelled examples per domain gives, with probability at least
$1 - \delta$,
$$
R_T(h) \le R_S(h) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, \hat{d}_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(U_S, U_T) + 4 \sqrt{\frac{2d \log(2m) +
\log(2/\delta)}{m}} + \lambda
$$
<Cite id="bendavid2010" />.

## Reading the three terms

1. **Source error** $R_S(h)$ is what ordinary training minimises.
2. **Divergence** $\tfrac12 d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}$ is measurable from unlabelled data. It depends on the representation:
   the same two input distributions can be far apart in one feature space and indistinguishable in another. This term
   motivates methods that learn features under which a domain classifier fails, such as <NoteLink
   to="domain-adversarial-training" /> and <NoteLink to="feature-alignment-methods" /> <Cite id="ganin2016" />.
3. **The joint error** $\lambda$ cannot be estimated without target labels. It is small when one hypothesis in the class
   is good on both domains. When it is large, no method that relies on the source can succeed, whatever the
   divergence.

The bound is an argument about *adaptability*: if some hypothesis works on both domains, a hypothesis that works on the
source and cannot tell the domains apart will work on the target. It does not say that making the domains
indistinguishable keeps $\lambda$ small. Changing the representation changes $\lambda$, and aligning feature
distributions can raise it when the class proportions differ between domains <Cite id="zhao2019invariant" />. See
<NoteLink to="negative-transfer" />.

## The discrepancy distance

Mansour, Mohri and Rostamizadeh extend the argument to general losses $L$, including the squared loss for regression
<Cite id="mansour2009" />. The **discrepancy distance** between a target distribution $P$ and a source distribution $Q$ is
$$
\text{disc}_L(P, Q) = \max_{h, h' \in \Hcal} \abs{L_P(h', h) - L_Q(h', h)},
$$
where $L_P(h', h) = \expect_P\brack{L(h'(\xvec), h(\xvec))}$. For the 0–1 loss it equals half the $\Hcal\Delta\Hcal$
divergence. If $L$ is symmetric and satisfies the triangle inequality, and $h_P^*$ and $h_Q^*$ are the best hypotheses
for the target and source, then for every $h \in \Hcal$
$$
L_P(h, f_P) \le L_P(h_P^*, f_P) + L_Q(h, h_Q^*) + \text{disc}_L(P, Q) + L_Q(h_Q^*, h_P^*).
$$
The proof follows the same steps as above, with the triangle inequality taken through $h_Q^*$ and $h_P^*$ instead of
the ideal joint hypothesis. The bound compares $h$ with the best source hypothesis rather than with the source labels,
so it has only one term that involves the target labelling. Mansour et al. give cases in which it is tighter than
the Ben-David bound by a factor of three and note that in general neither bound implies the other. They also bound the discrepancy from samples with
Rademacher complexity and derive a reweighting of source examples that minimises the empirical discrepancy
<Cite id="mansour2009" />.

## Assumptions

- The class $\Hcal$ has finite VC dimension, or finite Rademacher complexity for the discrepancy bounds.
- The unlabelled samples from each domain are independent and identically distributed.
- A hypothesis that is good on both domains exists, so that $\lambda$ is small. Nothing in the data can check this.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating the bound as an estimate.** The divergence term uses a supremum over the class and is often near its
  maximum of $2$ for flexible classes, which makes the bound loose. It is a guide to what matters, not a prediction.
- **Reading a small divergence as safety.** Divergences between input distributions ignore labels. Two domains can
  have the same inputs and opposite labels.
- **Estimating the divergence with the class being adapted.** A domain classifier more flexible than $\Hcal$ reports a
  larger divergence than the one in the bound; a weaker one reports a smaller one.
