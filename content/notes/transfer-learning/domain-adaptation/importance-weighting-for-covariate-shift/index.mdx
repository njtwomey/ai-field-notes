<Definition>
**Covariate shift** is the case in which the source and target distributions share the conditional $p(y \mid \xvec)$
and differ in the input distribution, $p_S(\xvec) \ne p_T(\xvec)$. **Importance weighting** trains on labelled source
examples $(\xvec_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n_s$, with each loss multiplied by the **importance weight** $w(\xvec_i) =
p_T(\xvec_i) / p_S(\xvec_i)$. The weighted average loss is then an unbiased estimate of the target risk
<Cite id="shimodaira2000" />. The weights are estimated from the source inputs and a sample of unlabelled target inputs
$\xvec'_j$, $j = 1, \dots, n_t$.
</Definition>

## Problem

A model fitted by minimising the average training loss minimises an estimate of the source risk. Under covariate shift
the target risk weights the same pointwise losses differently, by where the target inputs fall. If the model class
contains the true regression function, the difference does not matter: the unweighted fit converges to the truth wherever
the source has support, and the truth is right for every input distribution. If the model is **misspecified**, the best
approximation depends on the input distribution, and the fit that is best for the source is not the best for the target
<Cite id="shimodaira2000" />. Linear models of curved functions, and any model regularised enough to underfit, are in
this position.

## Mechanism

### The reweighted risk

Let $\ell(h(\xvec), y)$ be the loss of hypothesis $h$ on example $(\xvec, y)$. Because the conditional is shared, the
target risk can be rewritten as an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over the source:
$$
R_T(h) = \int p_T(\xvec)\, p(y \mid \xvec)\, \ell(h(\xvec), y)\, d\xvec\, dy
= \int p_S(\xvec)\, p(y \mid \xvec)\, \frac{p_T(\xvec)}{p_S(\xvec)}\, \ell(h(\xvec), y)\, d\xvec\, dy
= \expect_{S}\brack{w(\xvec)\, \ell(h(\xvec), y)} .
$$
The middle step multiplies and divides by $p_S(\xvec)$, which requires $p_S(\xvec) > 0$ wherever $p_T(\xvec) > 0$. The
**importance-weighted empirical risk**
$$
\hat R_w(h) = \frac{1}{n_s} \sum_{i=1}^{n_s} w(\xvec_i)\, \ell(h(\xvec_i), y_i)
$$
therefore has expectation $R_T(h)$ for every fixed $h$. Minimising it is <Gloss name="erm">empirical risk minimisation</Gloss> for the target,
carried out on source data. This is <NoteLink to="importance-sampling" /> with the source as the proposal
distribution.

For a maximum-likelihood model, Shimodaira showed that the weighted log-likelihood gives a consistent estimate of the
parameter that is best for the target, and that the unweighted estimate is more efficient when the model is correctly
specified <Cite id="shimodaira2000" />. He proposed the flattened weights $w(\xvec)^\lambda$, $0 \le \lambda \le 1$,
which interpolate between the unweighted fit ($\lambda = 0$: low variance, biased) and the fully weighted fit
($\lambda = 1$: unbiased, high variance).

### Model selection needs weighting too

Ordinary cross-validation estimates the source risk, so under covariate shift it chooses hyperparameters for the wrong
distribution. **Importance-weighted cross-validation** multiplies each held-out loss by its weight, which makes the
validation estimate unbiased for the target risk. Sugiyama et al. show that it is almost unbiased under covariate shift
and use it to choose the flattening exponent $\lambda$ <Cite id="sugiyama2007iwcv" />.

## Estimating the weights

Estimating $p_T$ and $p_S$ separately and dividing is unreliable in more than a few dimensions: small errors in the
denominator's tail become large errors in the ratio. The methods below estimate the ratio directly.

### Kernel mean matching

Let $k$ be a kernel with feature map $\phivec$ into a <NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space" />
$\Fcal$. The **mean embedding** of a distribution $p$ is $\muvec_p = \expect_p\brack{\phivec(\xvec)}$. For a
characteristic kernel, such as the Gaussian kernel, the mean embedding determines the distribution
<Cite id="gretton2012" />. If $w$ is the true ratio, the reweighted source has the target's mean embedding:
$$
\expect_S\brack{w(\xvec)\, \phivec(\xvec)} = \int p_S(\xvec) \frac{p_T(\xvec)}{p_S(\xvec)} \phivec(\xvec)\, d\xvec =
\muvec_T .
$$
**Kernel mean matching** turns this identity into an estimator: choose weights $\beta_i$ on the source examples so that
the weighted source mean embedding matches the target sample's <Cite id="huang2007kmm" />:
$$
\min_{\betavec} \norm{\frac{1}{n_s} \sum_{i=1}^{n_s} \beta_i\, \phivec(\xvec_i) - \frac{1}{n_t} \sum_{j=1}^{n_t}
\phivec(\xvec'_j)}_{\Fcal}^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad 0 \le \beta_i \le B, \quad \abs{\frac{1}{n_s} \sum_i \beta_i
- 1} \le \epsilon .
$$
Expanding the squared norm with $\inner{\phivec(\xvec)}{\phivec(\xvec')} = k(\xvec, \xvec')$ gives
$$
\frac{1}{n_s^2} \betavec\transpose \Kmat \betavec - \frac{2}{n_s^2} \kappavec\transpose \betavec + \text{const},
\qquad K_{ii'} = k(\xvec_i, \xvec_{i'}), \quad \kappa_i = \frac{n_s}{n_t} \sum_{j=1}^{n_t} k(\xvec_i, \xvec'_j),
$$
a quadratic programme in $n_s$ variables. The bound $B$ caps any single weight, which limits variance; the second
constraint keeps the weights averaging to about one. The objective is the squared maximum mean discrepancy between the
weighted source and the target, the statistic of the kernel two-sample test <Cite id="gretton2012" />.

### KLIEP

The **Kullback–Leibler importance estimation procedure** models the ratio as a non-negative combination of basis
functions, $\hat w(\xvec) = \sum_{l=1}^{b} \alpha_l \psi_l(\xvec)$ with $\alpha_l \ge 0$, usually Gaussian kernels centred
on target points <Cite id="sugiyama2008kliep" />. It chooses $\alphavec$ so that $\hat p_T(\xvec) = \hat w(\xvec)\,
p_S(\xvec)$ is close to $p_T$ in <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>:
$$
\KL(p_T \,\|\, \hat w\, p_S) = \expect_T\brack{\log \frac{p_T(\xvec)}{p_S(\xvec)}} - \expect_T\brack{\log \hat
w(\xvec)} .
$$
The first term does not depend on $\alphavec$, so minimising the divergence means maximising $\expect_T\brack{\log \hat
w(\xvec)}$. For $\hat p_T$ to be a density, $\hat w$ must average to one under the source. Replacing both
expectations by sample averages gives a convex problem:
$$
\max_{\alphavec \ge \zeros} \frac{1}{n_t} \sum_{j=1}^{n_t} \log \sum_l \alpha_l \psi_l(\xvec'_j) \quad \text{subject to}
\quad \frac{1}{n_s} \sum_{i=1}^{n_s} \sum_l \alpha_l \psi_l(\xvec_i) = 1 .
$$
Because it has an objective on held-out target points, the kernel width can be chosen by likelihood cross-validation,
which kernel mean matching lacks. Least-squares importance fitting (uLSIF) fits the same model by minimising the squared
error $\expect_S\brack{(\hat w - w)^2}$, which has a closed-form solution <Cite id="kanamori2009ulsif" />.

### A domain classifier

Pool the source and target inputs, label them $d = 0$ and $d = 1$, and train a probabilistic classifier for $p(d = 1 \mid
\xvec)$. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, $p(d = 1 \mid \xvec) \propto n_t\, p_T(\xvec)$ and $p(d = 0 \mid \xvec) \propto n_s\,
p_S(\xvec)$ with the same constant, so
$$
w(\xvec) = \frac{p_T(\xvec)}{p_S(\xvec)} = \frac{n_s}{n_t} \cdot \frac{p(d = 1 \mid \xvec)}{p(d = 0 \mid \xvec)} .
$$
For <NoteLink to="logistic-regression" /> the odds are $\exp(\thetavec\transpose \xvec)$, so the weight is an exponential
in the classifier's score <Cite id="bickel2009" />. The same classifier also measures the divergence between the domains
(see <NoteLink to="domain-adaptation-theory" />). The figure below uses a logistic domain classifier with quadratic
features, which can represent the ratio of two Gaussians exactly.

## Cost

Importance weighting trades bias for variance. A weighted average of $n$ terms with normalised weights $\tilde w_i =
w_i / \sum_{i'} w_{i'}$ and a common variance $\sigma^2$ has variance $\sigma^2 \sum_i \tilde w_i^2$. An unweighted
average of $n_e$ terms has variance $\sigma^2 / n_e$, so the weighted average is worth
$$
n_e = \frac{\paren{\sum_i w_i}^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}
$$
unweighted examples, the **effective sample size**. It equals $n$ when all weights are equal and falls towards $1$ when a
single weight dominates.

The effective sample size is governed by the second moment of the weights. Its expectation is roughly $n /
\expect_S\brack{w^2}$, since $\expect_S[w] = 1$. Cortes, Mansour and Mohri show that learning guarantees for importance
weighting hold when $\expect_S\brack{w^2}$ is finite, and can fail when it is not <Cite id="cortes2010" />. The second
moment can be large or infinite for distributions that look close.

### Worked example: Gaussian shift

Let the source be $\Gauss(0, 1)$ and the target $\Gauss(\mu, 1)$. The weight is $w(x) = \exp\paren{\mu x - \mu^2/2}$,
and
$$
\expect_S\brack{w^2} = \expect\brack{e^{2\mu x - \mu^2}} = e^{-\mu^2}\, e^{(2\mu)^2/2} = e^{\mu^2},
$$
using $\expect\brack{e^{t x}} = e^{t^2/2}$ for a standard normal $x$. The effective sample size is about $n e^{-\mu^2}$.
A shift of one standard deviation leaves $37\%$ of the sample; two standard deviations leave $1.8\%$; three leave
$0.012\%$. With $n = 1000$ source examples and $\mu = 2$, the weighted estimate is worth about 18 target examples.

<Derivation title="When the second moment is infinite">
Let the source be $\Gauss(0, \sigma_S^2)$ and the target $\Gauss(0, \sigma_T^2)$. Then
$$
\expect_S\brack{w^2} = \int \frac{p_T(x)^2}{p_S(x)}\, dx
= \frac{\sigma_S}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_T^2} \int \exp\paren{-\frac{x^2}{2}\paren{\frac{2}{\sigma_T^2} -
\frac{1}{\sigma_S^2}}} dx .
$$
The integral is finite only if $2/\sigma_T^2 > 1/\sigma_S^2$, that is, $\sigma_T^2 < 2\sigma_S^2$. When it is finite
it equals $\sqrt{2\pi} / \sqrt{2/\sigma_T^2 - 1/\sigma_S^2}$, so
$$
\expect_S\brack{w^2} = \frac{\sigma_S^2}{\sigma_T \sqrt{2\sigma_S^2 - \sigma_T^2}} .
$$
A target only $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.41$ times wider than the source already has weights with infinite variance: the rare
source points in the tails, where the target has relatively more mass, receive enormous weights. A target narrower than
the source is always safe.
</Derivation>

## Trade-offs

- **Flatten or cap the weights.** The exponent $\lambda$, the cap $B$ in kernel mean matching and clipping at a
  quantile all accept bias for lower variance. Choose them by importance-weighted cross-validation.
- **Weight only where support overlaps.** Where the target has mass and the source has none, the weights are undefined
  and no reweighting can help. The effective sample size reveals this: it collapses as the overlap shrinks.
- **Weight only when misspecified.** A well-specified model gains nothing in expectation from weighting and loses
  efficiency. The more flexible the model, the closer it is to well-specified on the region the source covers, and the
  less weighting changes the fit there.
- **The ratio of marginals only.** Importance weighting corrects covariate shift. It does not correct a change in
  $p(y \mid \xvec)$. A change in class proportions needs weights on labels instead; see <NoteLink
  to="label-shift-and-target-shift" />.
