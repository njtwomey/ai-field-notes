<Definition>
**Feature alignment** learns a map $\phivec : \Xcal \to \Zcal$ under which the distribution of mapped source inputs is
close to that of mapped target inputs, and trains a classifier on the mapped labelled source data. Closeness is measured
by a statistic computable from unlabelled samples: the difference of kernel mean embeddings (maximum mean discrepancy),
the difference of covariance matrices (CORAL), or the error of a domain classifier (<NoteLink
to="domain-adversarial-training" />).
</Definition>

## Problem

The target-error bound of <NoteLink to="domain-adaptation-theory" /> has three terms: source error, a divergence between
the input distributions and the error $\lambda$ of the best joint hypothesis <Cite id="bendavid2010" />. Importance weighting leaves the inputs alone
and reweights them. Feature alignment changes the inputs so that the divergence term is small, while the classifier keeps
the source error small. The price is that the map can also change $\lambda$.

## Maximum mean discrepancy

Let $k$ be a kernel with feature map $\varphivec$ into a <NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space" /> $\Fcal$, so
that $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \inner{\varphivec(\xvec)}{\varphivec(\xvec')}_{\Fcal}$ and $f(\xvec) = \inner{f}{\varphivec(\xvec)}_{\Fcal}$
for every $f \in \Fcal$. The **maximum mean discrepancy** between distributions $p$ and $q$ is the largest difference in
the expectation of a unit-norm function <Cite id="gretton2012" />:
$$
\operatorname{MMD}(p, q) = \sup_{\norm{f}_{\Fcal} \le 1} \paren{\expect_{p}\brack{f(\xvec)} - \expect_{q}\brack{f(\yvec)}}.
$$
By the reproducing property, $\expect_p[f(\xvec)] = \inner{f}{\muvec_p}_{\Fcal}$ with mean embedding $\muvec_p =
\expect_p\brack{\varphivec(\xvec)}$. The supremum is therefore $\sup_{\norm{f} \le 1} \inner{f}{\muvec_p -
\muvec_q}$, which by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is attained at $f = (\muvec_p - \muvec_q) / \norm{\muvec_p -
\muvec_q}$:
$$
\operatorname{MMD}(p, q) = \norm{\muvec_p - \muvec_q}_{\Fcal} .
$$
Expanding the square with the kernel,
$$
\operatorname{MMD}^2(p, q) = \expect\brack{k(\xvec, \xvec')} + \expect\brack{k(\yvec, \yvec')} - 2\, \expect\brack{k(\xvec,
\yvec)},
$$
with $\xvec, \xvec' \sim p$ and $\yvec, \yvec' \sim q$ independent. Every term is an average of kernel evaluations, so
the statistic is estimated from samples without density estimation. For a characteristic kernel, such as the Gaussian
kernel, $\operatorname{MMD}(p, q) = 0$ only if $p = q$ <Cite id="gretton2012" />. The unbiased estimator and its use as a
test are covered in <NoteLink to="kernel-inception-distance" />.

With a linear kernel, $\muvec_p$ is the mean and the MMD compares only means. With a quadratic kernel it compares first
and second moments. A Gaussian kernel compares all moments, weighted by the bandwidth.

## Transfer component analysis

Transfer component analysis looks for a low-dimensional projection in the kernel feature space in which the domains have
close means and the data keep their variance <Cite id="pan2011tca" />. Stack the $n = n_s + n_t$ source and target inputs,
form the kernel matrix $\Kmat \in \reals^{n \times n}$, and project with $\Wmat \in \reals^{n \times m}$, so that the
embedded data are the rows of $\Kmat \Wmat$. The squared empirical MMD between the embedded domains is
$$
\norm{\frac{1}{n_s} \sum_{i \in S} \zvec_i - \frac{1}{n_t} \sum_{j \in T} \zvec_j}^2 = \trace\paren{\Wmat\transpose \Kmat
\Lmat \Kmat \Wmat},
\qquad
L_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1/n_s^2 & i, j \in S \\ 1/n_t^2 & i, j \in T \\ -1/(n_s n_t) & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$
The identity holds because $\Lmat = \evec \evec\transpose$ with $e_i = 1/n_s$ on source rows and $-1/n_t$ on target rows,
and $\evec\transpose \Kmat \Wmat$ is the difference of the two embedded means. The variance of the embedded data is
$\trace\paren{\Wmat\transpose \Kmat \Hmat \Kmat \Wmat}$, where $\Hmat = \eye - \frac{1}{n} \ones \ones\transpose$ centres
the data. TCA solves
$$
\min_{\Wmat} \; \trace\paren{\Wmat\transpose \Kmat \Lmat \Kmat \Wmat} + \mu \trace\paren{\Wmat\transpose \Wmat}
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \Wmat\transpose \Kmat \Hmat \Kmat \Wmat = \eye_m ,
$$
where $\mu > 0$ regularises the projection and the constraint fixes the scale. With a symmetric matrix $\Zmat$ of
<NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers" />, the Lagrangian is $\trace\paren{\Wmat\transpose (\Kmat \Lmat \Kmat + \mu
\eye) \Wmat} - \trace\paren{(\Wmat\transpose \Kmat \Hmat \Kmat \Wmat - \eye) \Zmat}$. Setting its gradient in $\Wmat$ to
zero gives
$$
(\Kmat \Lmat \Kmat + \mu \eye)\, \Wmat = \Kmat \Hmat \Kmat\, \Wmat \Zmat ,
$$
a generalised eigenproblem. The objective at a solution is $\trace(\Zmat)$, so the minimum takes the $m$ eigenvectors of
$(\Kmat \Lmat \Kmat + \mu \eye)^{-1} \Kmat \Hmat \Kmat$ with the largest eigenvalues <Cite id="pan2011tca" />. With $\Lmat =
\zeros$ this reduces to <NoteLink to="kernel-principal-component-analysis" />; the $\Lmat$ term is what makes the components transferable. A
classifier is then trained on the embedded source rows and applied to the embedded target rows.

## Correlation alignment

CORAL aligns second-order statistics with a linear map <Cite id="sun2016coral" />. Let $\Cmat_S$ and $\Cmat_T$ be the
$d \times d$ covariance matrices of the source and target features. Find a matrix $\Amat$ such that the transformed
source features $\xvec\transpose \Amat$ have the target covariance:
$$
\min_{\Amat} \norm{\Amat\transpose \Cmat_S \Amat - \Cmat_T}_F^2 .
$$
If both covariances are full rank, $\Amat = \Cmat_S^{-1/2} \Cmat_T^{1/2}$ reaches the minimum of zero, since
$$
\Amat\transpose \Cmat_S \Amat = \Cmat_T^{1/2} \Cmat_S^{-1/2} \Cmat_S \Cmat_S^{-1/2} \Cmat_T^{1/2} = \Cmat_T,
$$
using that symmetric square roots are symmetric and commute with their own matrix. The map **whitens** the source,
removing its correlations, and then **re-colours** it with the target's. Sun et al. derive the general solution when the
covariances are rank deficient, through their <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecompositions</NoteLink>; in
practice they add the identity to each covariance, which makes both full rank <Cite id="sun2016coral" />. The algorithm
has four steps: add $\eye$ to each covariance, multiply the source features by $\Cmat_S^{-1/2}$, multiply by
$\Cmat_T^{1/2}$, and train any classifier on the result. Target features are used unchanged.

CORAL needs no hyperparameters beyond the regulariser and no target labels. It aligns only the first two moments and
only linearly.

## Deep alignment losses

In a network, the alignment term becomes a loss on the activations of one or more layers, computed on each minibatch of
source and target inputs and added to the classification loss on the source.

- **Deep domain confusion** adds the squared MMD with a linear kernel at one adaptation layer <Cite id="tzeng2014ddc" />.
- **Deep adaptation networks** add a multi-kernel MMD, a combination of Gaussian kernels of several bandwidths, at
  several task-specific layers <Cite id="long2015dan" />.
- **Deep CORAL** adds $\ell_{\text{CORAL}} = \frac{1}{4d^2} \norm{\Cmat_S - \Cmat_T}_F^2$ on the last layer's
  activations, where $\Cmat_S$ and $\Cmat_T$ are minibatch covariances of $d$-dimensional features
  <Cite id="sun2016deepcoral" />. The loss is differentiable in the features and is trained jointly with the source
  classification loss, whose weight balances the two.

The MMD and CORAL losses are fixed statistics. A domain classifier is a learnt statistic that looks for whatever
difference remains; see <NoteLink to="domain-adversarial-training" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Alignment does not guarantee transfer.** Aligning the marginal distributions of features reduces the divergence term
  of the bound but can raise the joint error $\lambda$. When the class proportions differ between domains, perfectly
  aligned features force errors on one of them <Cite id="zhao2019invariant" />.
- **Kernel methods scale with $n^2$.** TCA forms and decomposes $n \times n$ matrices; CORAL works with $d \times d$
  covariances and scales to any number of examples.
- **Moment matching is partial.** CORAL and linear-kernel MMD match only low-order moments. Distributions that agree in
  mean and covariance can still differ. <NoteLink to="optimal-transport-for-domain-adaptation">Optimal transport
  domain adaptation</NoteLink> matches whole distributions and pairs individual samples.
- **The bandwidth matters.** A Gaussian-kernel MMD with too small a bandwidth sees every sample as different; with too
  large a bandwidth it sees only the means. Multi-kernel MMD averages over bandwidths to reduce this sensitivity.
