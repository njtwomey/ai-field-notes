<Definition>
Transfer from a source to a target **helps** when a learner that uses both source and target data reaches lower target
risk than the same learner given the target data alone. Whether it does depends on two quantities: how far the source
is from the target, measured by the bias its data introduce, and how much the target data already determine, measured
by the variance of a target-only estimate. Transfer helps while the variance removed exceeds the bias added.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A source is a large sample from the wrong distribution. Its data cost little variance and some bias. A target sample is
from the right distribution and is small, so it costs no bias and much variance. Every transfer method chooses, openly
or not, how much weight to give each. The right weight depends on the size of the bias compared with the variance of the
target estimate, and it moves towards the target as the target sample grows. This is the <NoteLink
to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance trade-off</NoteLink> applied to the choice of data.

## The trade-off for a mean

The simplest case shows the whole structure. A target quantity $\mu_T$ is estimated from $n_t$ target observations
with mean $\bar{x}_t$ and $n_s$ source observations with mean $\bar{x}_s$. Both samples have noise variance $\sigma^2$.
The source mean is off by $\delta$: $\expect[\bar{x}_s] = \mu_T + \delta$. Consider the combined estimate
$$
\hat\mu_\alpha = \alpha\, \bar{x}_s + (1 - \alpha)\, \bar{x}_t, \qquad 0 \le \alpha \le 1.
$$
Its bias is $\alpha\delta$, and its variance is $\alpha^2 \sigma^2 / n_s + (1 - \alpha)^2 \sigma^2 / n_t$ because the
two samples are independent. Writing $v_s = \sigma^2 / n_s$ and $v_t = \sigma^2 / n_t$, the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> is
$$
\text{MSE}(\alpha) = \alpha^2 \paren{\delta^2 + v_s} + (1 - \alpha)^2 v_t .
$$
Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $2\alpha(\delta^2 + v_s) - 2(1 - \alpha) v_t$ to zero gives the best weight on the source,
$$
\alpha^* = \frac{v_t}{\delta^2 + v_s + v_t}.
$$
The source gets weight in proportion to how uncertain the target estimate is, and loses weight as the bias grows.

**When does any weight help?** Using the source at weight $\alpha$ beats the target alone when $\text{MSE}(\alpha) <
\text{MSE}(0) = v_t$. Subtracting,
$$
\text{MSE}(\alpha) - v_t = \alpha^2 \paren{\delta^2 + v_s + v_t} - 2 \alpha v_t = \alpha \paren{\delta^2 + v_s + v_t}
\paren{\alpha - 2\alpha^*},
$$
which is negative exactly for $0 < \alpha < 2\alpha^*$. Some weight on the source always helps, however large the bias,
because $\alpha^* > 0$. Too much weight hurts: that is <NoteLink to="negative-transfer" />.

**When does pooling help?** Pooling the samples, as a learner that ignores the domain label does, uses $\alpha_p = n_s /
(n_s + n_t)$. With an unlimited source, $v_s \to 0$ and $\alpha_p \to 1$, and the condition $\alpha_p < 2\alpha^*$
becomes $1 < 2 v_t / (\delta^2 + v_t)$, that is,
$$
\delta^2 < \frac{\sigma^2}{n_t}.
$$
Pooling helps only while the source bias is smaller than the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the target-only estimate. Every target
observation added lowers that standard error, so a source that helps a small target sample hurts a large one.

### Worked example

Take $\sigma = 1$, $\delta = 0.3$, $n_t = 10$ and $n_s = 500$. Then $v_t = 0.1$ and $v_s = 0.002$. The best weight is
$\alpha^* = 0.1 / (0.09 + 0.002 + 0.1) = 0.521$, with mean squared error $0.048$, less than half the target-only $0.1$.
Pooling uses $\alpha_p = 500/510 = 0.980$ and has mean squared error $0.0885$: still better than the target alone,
because $\delta^2 = 0.09 < v_t = 0.1$. With $n_t = 20$ the target-only error drops to $0.05$, the pooled error is
$0.0851$, and pooling now hurts. The optimal combination still helps, at $0.032$.

## The same threshold in domain adaptation

Ben-David et al. analyse a classifier trained on $m$ labelled examples, a fraction $\beta$ from the target and $1 -
\beta$ from the source, by minimising $\alpha \hat{R}_T(h) + (1 - \alpha) \hat{R}_S(h)$ <Cite id="bendavid2010" />.
Their bound on the target error of the minimiser, relative to the best target hypothesis, has the shape of
$\text{MSE}(\alpha)$ above. A complexity term of order $\sqrt{d/m}$ is multiplied by $\sqrt{\alpha^2/\beta + (1 -
\alpha)^2/(1 - \beta)}$, which is smallest when every example has equal weight, and a divergence term $A$ is multiplied
by $2(1 - \alpha)$. Here $d$ is the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> of the hypothesis class, and $A$ combines the divergence between the
domains and the error $\lambda$ of the best joint hypothesis (see <NoteLink to="domain-adaptation-theory" />).

Minimising the bound over $\alpha$ gives two phase transitions <Cite id="bendavid2010" />. If the number of target
examples satisfies $m_T \ge d / A^2$, the optimal $\alpha$ is $1$ and any source data make the bound worse. Below that
threshold the source is worth using only once there is enough of it. The condition $m_T \ge d/A^2$ plays the role of
$n_t \ge \sigma^2 / \delta^2$ for the mean: the target sample is large enough that its estimation error is below the
source's bias.

## Task relatedness

The trade-off needs a measure of how far apart source and target are. Several are in use.

- **Divergence between input distributions.** The $\Hcal$-divergence and the proxy distance from a domain classifier
  measure how distinguishable the inputs are. They are blind to the labels, so they miss a change in $p(y \mid \xvec)$
  (see <NoteLink to="negative-transfer" />).
- **Error of the best joint hypothesis.** The term $\lambda$ in the <Gloss name="domain-adaptation">domain-adaptation</Gloss> bound is small when one hypothesis
  is good on both domains. It cannot be computed without target labels.
- **Relatedness through transformations.** Ben-David and Schuller call tasks related when a known family of
  transformations of the input space maps one task's distribution to another's. Under that assumption they prove bounds
  that improve with the number of related tasks <Cite id="bendavid2003relatedness" />.
- **An environment of tasks.** Baxter models tasks as draws from one distribution over tasks, so that related means
  drawn from the same environment. The number of examples needed per task then falls as the number of tasks grows
  <Cite id="baxter2000" />.
- **Empirical transferability.** Yosinski et al. trained networks on two halves of <Gloss name="imagenet" /> and found that features
  from lower layers transferred almost without loss, while transfer from higher layers dropped as the two halves became
  less similar <Cite id="yosinski2014" />. In multi-task training, which task pairs help each other is found by
  training them together <Cite id="standley2020" />.

## Assumptions

- The mean example assumes the bias $\delta$ is known. In practice it must be estimated, which is what empirical Bayes
  and <NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">hierarchical models</NoteLink> do: the James–Stein estimator shrinks each group mean towards the pooled mean by an amount
  estimated from the spread of the groups <Cite id="efron1975" />. See <NoteLink to="hierarchical-bayesian-transfer" />.
- Bounds such as Ben-David et al.'s are worst-case over a hypothesis class. They predict the shape of the trade-off,
  not the value of the best weight on a given data set.

## Pitfalls

- **Pooling by default.** Training one model on the union of source and target data is the choice $\alpha = \alpha_p$,
  which is close to $1$ whenever the source is large. It is the right choice only when the bias is below the target's
  standard error.
- **Ignoring the target sample size.** A study that finds transfer helpful at one amount of target data says little
  about another amount <Cite id="wang2019negative" />.
