<Definition>
Over a set of queries $Q$, the hit rate at $k$ is

$$
\text{HR}@k = \frac{1}{\abs{Q}}\sum_{q \in Q} \indicator\brack{\text{at least one relevant item in the top } k \text{ for } q}.
$$

For a query with $R$ relevant items, the R-precision is $P@R$, the precision of the top $R$ results
<Cite id="manning2008" />.
</Definition>

## Hit rate

Hit rate depends only on whether the top $k$ contains a relevant item. It is the standard metric for next-item
recommendation, where each query (a user's history) has a single held-out item: then a hit means that item appears in
the top $k$, and hit rate equals recall at $k$ averaged over users.

Hit rate is a step function of the first relevant rank $r$: a query scores 1 when $r \le k$ and 0 otherwise. It is
coarser than <NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-and-mean-reciprocal-rank">reciprocal rank</NoteLink>, which also uses $r$ but gives
partial credit.

**Worked example.** Four queries have their first relevant item at ranks 1, 3, 2, and beyond the list. Then
$\text{HR}@1 = 1/4$, $\text{HR}@2 = 2/4$ and $\text{HR}@3 = 3/4$, and no $k$ gives a hit for the fourth query.

## R-precision

Choosing $k$ is arbitrary, and with a fixed $k$ a query with fewer than $k$ relevant items has $P@k < 1$ even under a
perfect ranking. R-precision sets the cut-off
per query to its number of relevant items. At that cut-off precision and recall are equal, because both have the same
numerator and $k = R$ makes the denominators equal: $P@R = R@R$. A perfect ranking scores 1 for every query, whatever
$R$ is, so every query has the same maximum in the average.

**Worked example.** The ranking with relevant items at ranks 1, 3, 4 and 7, and $R = 5$, has three relevant items in
its top five, so its R-precision is $3/5 = 0.6$.

A fixed cut-off can also fail to separate two rankings that R-precision separates. List A has relevant items at ranks
1, 2 and 3 and list B at ranks 8, 9 and 10, from a collection with $R = 5$ relevant items. Both have $P@10 = 0.3$ and
$R@10 = 0.6$. Their R-precisions, $P@5$, are $3/5 = 0.6$ for A and $0$ for B.

R-precision is also where the <Gloss name="pr">precision–recall curve</Gloss> of a single query crosses the diagonal $P = R$, and it is
strongly correlated with <NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-and-mean-reciprocal-rank">average precision</NoteLink> in practice.

## Pitfalls

- **Hit rate saturates.** With a large $k$ almost every query gets a hit, and systems become indistinguishable. Report
  several values of $k$, or use reciprocal rank or <Gloss name="ndcg" />.
- **Sampled evaluation.** Recommender evaluations often rank the held-out item among a small random sample of other
  items instead of the full catalogue. Hit rates on sampled candidates are much higher than on the full catalogue, and
  they can reorder systems.
- **R-precision needs $R$.** Like recall, it depends on knowing every relevant item.
