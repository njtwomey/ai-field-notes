<Definition>
Let $g_i \ge 0$ be the relevance grade of the item at rank $i$. The discounted cumulative gain at cut-off $k$ and its
normalised form are <Cite id="jarvelin2002" />

$$
\text{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{g_i}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \qquad \text{NDCG}@k = \frac{\text{DCG}@k}{\text{IDCG}@k},
$$

where $\text{IDCG}@k$ is the DCG@k of the same items sorted by decreasing grade. A common variant replaces the gain
$g_i$ by $2^{g_i} - 1$, which weights highly relevant items more.
</Definition>

## The two ingredients

**Cumulative gain.** Graded relevance lets a ranking earn more for a highly relevant item than for a marginally
relevant one. Summing the grades of the top $k$ items gives the cumulative gain, which, like
<NoteLink to="precision-and-recall-at-k">precision at k</NoteLink>, ignores order within the prefix.

**Discount.** Dividing by $\log_2(i + 1)$ makes each position worth less than the one above it: rank 1 has weight 1,
rank 2 has $1/\log_2 3 = 0.63$, rank 4 has $0.43$, rank 10 has $0.29$. The logarithm falls slowly, so lower ranks still
count, unlike the reciprocal rank's $1/i$. It models a reader who is less and less likely to look further down.

## Normalisation

DCG grows with the number and grades of the relevant items, so raw DCG values are not comparable across queries. The
ideal ordering maximises DCG for a given multiset of grades: the discounts decrease with rank, and a sum of products is
largest when the largest gains are paired with the largest weights (the rearrangement inequality). Dividing by that
maximum gives $\text{NDCG}@k \in [0, 1]$, with 1 exactly when the top $k$ are in ideal order. NDCG therefore measures
how close a ranking is to the best possible ordering of the same items, and averages sensibly over queries.

The ideal ordering can be defined from the retrieved items only, or from all judged items including unretrieved ones.
The second is stricter, because a missing highly relevant document lowers the score. Libraries differ: scikit-learn's
`ndcg_score` uses the items it is given.

**Worked example.** Six items are ranked with grades $(3, 2, 3, 0, 1, 2)$. With linear gains,

$$
\text{DCG}@6 = \frac{3}{1} + \frac{2}{1.585} + \frac{3}{2} + \frac{0}{2.322} + \frac{1}{2.585} + \frac{2}{2.807} = 6.861 .
$$

The ideal order is $(3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 0)$ with $\text{IDCG}@6 = 7.141$, so $\text{NDCG}@6 = 0.961$, which is what
`sklearn.metrics.ndcg_score` returns. The exponential gain $2^{g} - 1$ gives $0.949$: it penalises the misplaced grade-3
item more. At $k = 3$ the ranking scores $0.978$, because its top three items are close to ideal.

## Pitfalls

- **State the gain and the cut-off.** Linear and exponential gains give different numbers for the same ranking, and
  NDCG@10 differs from NDCG over the full list. Papers that omit either are not comparable.
- **All-zero queries.** If a query has no relevant items, IDCG is 0 and NDCG is undefined. Libraries set it to 0 or
  drop the query; the choice changes the average.
- **Near-perfect scores.** Because the logarithmic discount is gentle, NDCG values bunch near 1 when most items are
  relevant, and small differences can be noise. Compare systems with paired tests over queries.
