<Definition>
For one query, let $\text{rel}_i \in \set{0, 1}$ say whether the item at rank $i$ is relevant, and let $R$ be the
number of relevant items in the whole collection. Then <Cite id="manning2008" />

$$
P@k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{rel}_i, \qquad R@k = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{rel}_i .
$$

Both take values in $[0, 1]$ and are averaged over queries to evaluate a system.
</Definition>

## From sets to rankings

Precision and recall were defined for an unordered set of retrieved items: the fraction of the set that is relevant,
and the fraction of the relevant items that the set contains. A ranking produces a nested sequence of sets, one for
each prefix. Precision and recall at $k$ evaluate the prefix of length $k$, which models a reader who looks at the
first $k$ results and stops.

The two measures pull in opposite directions as $k$ grows. $R@k$ never decreases, because a longer prefix can only
contain more relevant items, and it reaches its maximum once every retrieved relevant item is inside the prefix.
$P@k$ can rise or fall: it falls whenever a non-relevant item joins the prefix. Their numerators are the same count,
so

$$
P@k = \frac{R}{k}\, R@k .
$$

At $k = R$ the two are equal. That common value is the R-precision, described in
<NoteLink to="hit-rate-and-r-precision">hit rate and R-precision</NoteLink>.

**Worked example.** A ranking of 10 documents has relevant items at ranks 1, 3, 4 and 7, and one more relevant
document exists that was not retrieved, so $R = 5$.

| $k$ | relevant in top $k$ | $P@k$ | $R@k$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 | 1.00 | 0.20 |
| 5 | 3 | 0.60 | 0.60 |
| 10 | 4 | 0.40 | 0.80 |

Recall never reaches 1, because one relevant document is missing from the list.

## What they ignore

- **Order inside the cut-off.** Swapping the first and $k$-th items leaves both values unchanged. A relevant item at
  rank 1 counts the same as one at rank $k$.
- **Everything below the cut-off.** Items at ranks beyond $k$ do not affect either measure.
- **Grades of relevance.** Both are binary. <NoteLink to="normalised-discounted-cumulative-gain">NDCG</NoteLink> uses graded relevance and discounts
  by rank.

<NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-and-mean-reciprocal-rank">Average precision</NoteLink> summarises precision at every rank where a
relevant item appears, which removes the need to choose $k$.

## Comparing two rankings

Two lists, A and B, for the same query can be compared by $P@k$ or $R@k$. At a fixed $k$ the two metrics always agree.
Both are the count $c(k)$ of relevant items in the top $k$, divided by a constant shared by the two lists ($k$ for
precision, $R$ for recall), so $P@k_A > P@k_B$ exactly when $c_A(k) > c_B(k)$, exactly when $R@k_A > R@k_B$.

The verdict can still change with $k$. It changes wherever the cumulative counts $c_A(k)$ and $c_B(k)$ cross. A list
with relevant items at ranks 5, 7 and 11 beats a list with relevant items at ranks 11, 13, 17, 18, 19, 21, 24 and 28
at $k = 10$ ($c_A = 2$, $c_B = 0$), ties at $k = 17$ ($c_A = c_B = 3$), and loses from $k = 18$ on. Asking "which list
is better at 10?" and "which is better at 30?" gives opposite answers, and neither is wrong: they describe readers who
stop at different depths. <NoteLink to="evaluating-retrieval-systems">Evaluating retrieval systems</NoteLink> works
through the same pair under every common metric.

## Pitfalls

- **$P@k$ is capped when few items are relevant.** A query with 2 relevant documents has $P@10 \le 0.2$ even for a
  perfect ranking. Averaging $P@k$ over queries with very different numbers of relevant items mixes easy and
  impossible targets.
- **Recall needs $R$.** Computing $R@k$ requires knowing every relevant item in the collection, which large
  collections rarely provide. Evaluations pool the top results of several systems and judge only the pool, which
  underestimates $R$. When relevance labels across languages are missing entirely,
  <NoteLink to="backretrieval">Backretrieval</NoteLink> computes a recall at $K$ over images instead of texts.
- **Short result lists.** When a system returns fewer than $k$ items, $P@k$ still divides by $k$; the missing positions
  count as non-relevant.
