<Definition>
For a forecast given as a <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss> $F$ and an observation $y$, the continuous ranked
probability score is <Cite id="matheson1976" />

$$
\operatorname{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \paren{F(z) - \indicator\set{y \le z}}^2 dz.
$$

For a central $(1 - \alpha)$ prediction interval $[\ell, u]$, the interval score is

$$
\operatorname{IS}_\alpha(\ell, u; y) = (u - \ell) + \frac2\alpha(\ell - y)\,\indicator\set{y < \ell}
+ \frac2\alpha(y - u)\,\indicator\set{y > u}.
$$

Lower is better for both <Cite id="gneiting2007" />.
</Definition>

## Scoring a distribution

A point error such as MAE ignores the forecast's uncertainty, and interval coverage alone rewards infinitely wide
intervals. A **proper scoring rule** rewards a forecaster most, in <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, for reporting their true belief
<Cite id="gneiting2007" />. The CRPS is proper and scores the whole forecast distribution against the single value that
occurred.

It compares two cumulative distribution functions: the forecast $F$, and the step function that jumps from 0 to 1 at
the observation, which is the cdf of a forecast certain of $y$. The squared gap is integrated over every threshold $z$.
So the CRPS is the <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">Brier score</NoteLink> for the binary event $Y \le z$,
integrated over all thresholds.

Three properties make it easy to use.

- **Units.** It is in the units of $y$, like MAE, so it can be read and compared with point errors.
- **Point forecasts.** If $F$ puts all its mass on $x$, the gap is 1 exactly between $x$ and $y$, so
  $\operatorname{CRPS} = \abs{x - y}$: the CRPS generalises absolute error.
- **Samples.** For $X$ and $X'$ independent draws from $F$,
  $\operatorname{CRPS}(F, y) = \expect\abs{X - y} - \tfrac12\expect\abs{X - X'}$ <Cite id="gneiting2007" />. The first
  term rewards being close to $y$; the second rewards spread, so the score balances accuracy against sharpness. This
  form scores an ensemble or a set of posterior samples directly.

**Quantile form.** The CRPS also equals twice the <NoteLink to="robust-and-distributional-regression-losses">pinball loss</NoteLink> averaged over all quantile levels,
$2\int_0^1 \rho_\tau\paren{y - F^{-1}(\tau)}\,d\tau$ <Cite id="gneiting2007" />. A forecaster judged by CRPS must get
every quantile right, not only the median.

<Derivation title="From the integral to the kernel form">
Split the integral at $y$. For $z < y$ the integrand is $F(z)^2$ and for $z \ge y$ it is $(1 - F(z))^2$. With $X, X'$
independent from $F$, $F(z)^2 = P(X \le z, X' \le z)$ and $(1 - F(z))^2 = P(X > z, X' > z)$. Integrating
$P(\max(X, X') \le z)$ over $z < y$ gives $\expect[(y - \max(X, X'))^+]$, and integrating $P(\min(X, X') > z)$ over
$z \ge y$ gives $\expect[(\min(X, X') - y)^+]$. It remains to check that, for each pair of draws,
$(y - \max)^+ + (\min - y)^+ = \tfrac12\paren{\abs{X - y} + \abs{X' - y}} - \tfrac12\abs{X - X'}$. If $y$ lies below
both draws, both sides equal $\min - y$; if above both, both equal $y - \max$; if between them, both are 0. Taking
expectations gives $\expect\abs{X - y} - \tfrac12\expect\abs{X - X'}$. Numerically, for $F = \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $y = 0.8$, the integral, the closed form below and a Monte Carlo
estimate of the kernel form agree at 0.476.
</Derivation>

## Gaussian forecasts

For $F = \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $z = (y - \mu)/\sigma$, the integral has a closed form <Cite id="gneiting2007" />:

$$
\operatorname{CRPS} = \sigma\paren{z\,\paren{2\Phi(z) - 1} + 2\varphi(z) - \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}},
$$

with $\Phi$ and $\varphi$ the standard normal cdf and density.

<CrpsExplorer />

The log score, $-\log$ of the forecast density at $y$, is also proper, but behaves differently. For $y = 0.8$ and
$\mu = 0$, the log score is minimised by $\sigma = 0.8$ and grows quickly as $\sigma$ shrinks, because the density at
$y$ collapses; the CRPS is minimised by $\sigma = 0.96$ and rises gently on both sides. The log score punishes an
overconfident forecast of an unlikely outcome without limit; the CRPS stays bounded by the distance involved.

## Interval scores

The interval score has three parts: the width of the interval, and a penalty for each side the observation falls
outside, scaled by $2/\alpha$. A wider interval pays for its width; a narrow one pays for misses. Its expected value
is minimised by the true $\alpha/2$ and $1 - \alpha/2$ quantiles <Cite id="gneiting2007" />, so reporting honest
quantiles is optimal. Coverage, the fraction of observations inside the interval, should be reported alongside but is
not a score on its own.

## Worked example

The forecast is $\Gauss(0, 1)$.

- If $y = 0.8$, then $z = 0.8$ and $\operatorname{CRPS} = 0.476$. The absolute error of the mean is 0.8; the CRPS is
  smaller because the forecast placed probability near the outcome.
- If $y = 0$, $\operatorname{CRPS} = 2\varphi(0) - 1/\sqrt\pi = 0.234$, its smallest value for this forecast.
- A point forecast of 0.3 has $\operatorname{CRPS} = \abs{0.3 - 0.8} = 0.5$.
- The central 80% interval is $\pm 1.2816$, width 2.563. If $y = 0.8$, inside, the interval score is 2.563. If
  $y = 2$, the penalty is $(2/0.2)(2 - 1.2816) = 7.18$, for a total of 9.75.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale.** Like MAE, the CRPS is in the units of the data. Averaging it across series of different scales lets the
  largest dominate; scale it as <NoteLink to="scaled-errors-and-mean-absolute-scaled-error">MASE</NoteLink> does.
- **Multivariate forecasts.** The CRPS scores each variable separately and ignores dependence between them. The energy
  score, which replaces $\abs{\cdot}$ with a <NoteLink to="vector-norms">Euclidean norm</NoteLink> in the kernel form, scores joint forecasts.
- **Coverage is not calibration of the whole distribution.** A correct 80% interval says nothing about the tails;
  scoring several quantile levels, or the CRPS, does.
