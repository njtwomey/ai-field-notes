<Definition>
Let $y_1, \dots, y_T$ be the training series and $m$ its seasonal period ($m = 1$ for non-seasonal data). The scale is
the in-sample mean absolute error of the seasonal naive forecast, which predicts each value by the value one season
earlier:

$$
Q = \frac{1}{T - m}\sum_{t = m+1}^{T} \abs{y_t - y_{t-m}}.
$$

For forecasts $\hat y_t$ of held-out values $y_t$, the scaled errors are $q_t = (y_t - \hat y_t)/Q$, and the mean
absolute scaled error is $\operatorname{MASE} = \frac1h\sum_t \abs{q_t}$ over the $h$ forecast periods
<Cite id="hyndman2006" />.
</Definition>

## A benchmark in the denominator

<NoteLink to="percentage-errors">Percentage errors</NoteLink> remove scale by dividing each error by its own actual
value, which fails at zero and distorts small values. MASE removes scale by dividing every error by one constant: how
wrong the simplest reasonable forecast was on the training data. That constant is positive unless the training series
never changes, so MASE is defined for series with zeros and for intermittent demand.

The reading is direct. $\operatorname{MASE} < 1$ means the forecast's errors are smaller than the naive method's typical
one-step error in sample. $\operatorname{MASE} = 2$ means they are twice as large. Because every error is divided by the
same constant, MASE is symmetric: over- and under-forecasts of the same size cost the same, and the best forecast for
it is the median, as for MAE.

The benchmark is computed in sample and one step ahead. A multi-step forecast is compared with a one-step naive
error, so values above 1 are common for long horizons even for good methods; MASE compares methods on the same series
and horizon, not horizons with each other.

## Squared version

The root mean squared scaled error uses squared errors in both places:

$$
\operatorname{RMSSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\frac1h\sum_t (y_t - \hat y_t)^2}{\frac{1}{T - m}\sum_{t = m+1}^T (y_t - y_{t-m})^2}}.
$$

It rewards the mean rather than the median. The M5 forecasting competition ranked methods by a weighted RMSSE across
thousands of retail series, many of them intermittent <Cite id="makridakis2022" />.

## Worked example

The training series is $(10, 12, 11, 13, 15, 14)$ with $m = 1$. The naive one-step errors are $2, 1, 2, 2, 1$, so
$Q = 8/5 = 1.6$.

The next two values are $(16, 15)$. A forecast of $(15, 16)$ has absolute errors $(1, 1)$, so MAE is 1 and
$\operatorname{MASE} = 1/1.6 = 0.625$. Forecasting the last observed value, 14, for both periods has MAE 1.5 and
$\operatorname{MASE} = 0.94$: the naive method out of sample, scored against its own in-sample error. The RMSSE of the
first forecast is 0.60.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing m.** For seasonal data, the non-seasonal naive benchmark can be a poor scale, because consecutive values
  differ by the seasonal swing. Use the seasonal period.
- **Averaging across series.** MASE can be averaged across series, since each is scale-free, but series with small
  in-sample variation have small $Q$ and can dominate. Weighting by volume, as in M5, reflects business importance.
- **Constant training series.** $Q = 0$ when the training series never changes, and MASE is undefined.
