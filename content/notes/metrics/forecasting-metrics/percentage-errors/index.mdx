<Definition>
For actual values $y_t$ and forecasts $\hat y_t$, $t = 1, \dots, n$:

$$
\operatorname{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n}\sum_t \frac{\abs{y_t - \hat y_t}}{\abs{y_t}}, \qquad
\operatorname{sMAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n}\sum_t \frac{2\abs{y_t - \hat y_t}}{\abs{y_t} + \abs{\hat y_t}}, \qquad
\operatorname{WMAPE} = \frac{\sum_t \abs{y_t - \hat y_t}}{\sum_t \abs{y_t}}.
$$

MAPE and sMAPE average errors relative to each actual value; WMAPE divides total absolute error by total actual
volume <Cite id="hyndman2006" />.
</Definition>

## Why percentages

Absolute errors are in the units of the series, so they cannot compare forecasts of sales in units with forecasts of
revenue in pounds, or a small product with a large one. Dividing by the actual value removes the scale. A 10% error
means the same for every series, which makes MAPE easy to explain and common in business forecasting.

## What goes wrong

**Zeros and near-zeros.** The error is divided by $y_t$, so any zero actual makes MAPE infinite, and small actuals
make it huge. Intermittent demand, with many zero periods, cannot be scored by MAPE at all.

**Asymmetry.** For a positive actual and a non-negative forecast, a forecast that is too low has an absolute percentage
error of at most 100%, reached by forecasting 0. A forecast that is too high has no upper limit: forecasting 300 for
100 is a 200% error. The same absolute miss costs the same percentage, but the ceiling applies only to one side.

**It rewards forecasting low.** The forecast $c$ that minimises the expected absolute percentage error minimises
$\expect\brack{\abs{Y - c}/Y}$: an absolute error with weight $1/Y$ on each outcome. By the argument that makes the
median optimal for absolute error, the optimum is the median of the distribution reweighted by $1/y$. Small outcomes
get more weight, so the optimum moves down. For a log-normal target with parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$, multiplying the
density by $1/y$ gives another log-normal density with parameter $\mu - \sigma^2$, so

$$
c^\star = e^{\mu - \sigma^2} < e^{\mu} = \text{median} < e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2} = \text{mean}.
$$

With $\sigma = 0.5$ the MAPE-optimal forecast is 78% of the median. A forecaster judged by MAPE is paid to forecast
systematically low.

## The alternatives

**sMAPE** divides by the average of actual and forecast instead of the actual alone <Cite id="makridakis1993" />. It is
bounded, between 0% and 200%, and no longer infinite when $y_t = 0$ unless the forecast is also 0. But it is not
symmetric in the sense its name suggests. With an actual of 100, forecasts of 50 and 150 miss by the same amount, yet
their symmetric errors are 67% and 40%: a low forecast is penalised more. Several incompatible definitions circulate,
including one without the factor of 2 that ranges from 0% to 100%; state which is used.

**WMAPE** divides total absolute error by total actual volume, so it is MAE relative to the mean level of the series.
Large actuals dominate, zeros cause no problem unless every actual is zero, and it measures the error in the quantity
that usually matters to the business: total units mis-forecast as a share of total units. It is minimised by the
median, not a shrunken value.

## Worked example

Actuals $(100, 50, 20, 80)$ and forecasts $(110, 40, 30, 80)$ have absolute errors $(10, 10, 10, 0)$.

- The absolute percentage errors are $(10\%, 20\%, 50\%, 0\%)$, so $\operatorname{MAPE} = 20\%$. The single miss of 10
  on an actual of 20 contributes most of it.
- $\operatorname{sMAPE} = 17.9\%$.
- $\operatorname{WMAPE} = 30/250 = 12\%$: the same three misses of 10, measured against the total volume.

scikit-learn's `mean_absolute_percentage_error` returns 0.2, as a fraction rather than a percentage
<Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing across series.** Averaging MAPE over many series lets the smallest series dominate. Use WMAPE or
  <NoteLink to="scaled-errors-and-mase">scaled errors</NoteLink> for collections of series.
- **Scale versus percentage.** Percentages are meaningful only for quantities with a true zero. Temperatures in Celsius
  have no meaningful percentage error.
- **Choosing the forecast to fit the score.** Because MAPE rewards low forecasts, optimising a model for MAPE and then
  using it to plan stock leads to shortages. Match the score to the decision the forecast feeds.
