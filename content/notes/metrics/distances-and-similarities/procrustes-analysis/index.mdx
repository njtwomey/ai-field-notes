<Definition>
Given matrices $\Amat, \Bmat \in \reals^{d \times n}$ whose columns are $n$ matched points in $d$ dimensions, the
**orthogonal Procrustes problem** is

$$
\min_{\Rmat} \norm{\Rmat\Amat - \Bmat}_F^2 \quad \text{subject to } \Rmat^\top\Rmat = \eye.
$$

Its solution is $\Rmat = \Vmat\Umat^\top$, where $\Amat\Bmat^\top = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$ is a
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> <Cite id="schonemann1966" />.
</Definition>

## Deriving the rotation

Expanding the <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">Frobenius norm</NoteLink> with $\norm{\Mmat}_F^2 = \trace(\Mmat^\top\Mmat)$ and using $\Rmat^\top\Rmat = \eye$,

$$
\norm{\Rmat\Amat - \Bmat}_F^2 = \norm{\Amat}_F^2 + \norm{\Bmat}_F^2 - 2\trace\paren{\Rmat\Amat\Bmat^\top}.
$$

The first two terms do not depend on $\Rmat$, so the problem is to maximise $\trace(\Rmat\Amat\Bmat^\top)$. Substitute
the SVD and use the cyclic property of the trace:

$$
\trace\paren{\Rmat\Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top} = \trace\paren{\Vmat^\top\Rmat\Umat\,\Sigmamat} = \sum_k z_{kk}\,\sigma_k,
$$

where $\Zmat = \Vmat^\top\Rmat\Umat$ is orthogonal. Every entry of an orthogonal matrix has magnitude at most 1, and
the singular values are non-negative, so the sum is at most $\sum_k \sigma_k$. It reaches that bound at $\Zmat = \eye$,
that is at $\Rmat = \Vmat\Umat^\top$.

**Rotations only.** $\Vmat\Umat^\top$ may be a reflection, with <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> $-1$. When only proper rotations are
allowed, as for molecules or rigid bodies, the best rotation flips the direction paired with the smallest singular
value: $\Rmat = \Vmat\,\diag(1, \dots, 1, \det(\Vmat\Umat^\top))\,\Umat^\top$. This is the Kabsch algorithm
<Cite id="kabsch1976" />.

## Translation and scale

Full Procrustes analysis also removes differences in position and size. Centring both point sets makes the best
translation zero. For a scale factor $s$, minimising $\norm{s\Rmat\Amat - \Bmat}_F^2$ over $s$ after finding $\Rmat$
gives

$$
s = \frac{\trace(\Rmat\Amat\Bmat^\top)}{\norm{\Amat}_F^2} = \frac{\sum_k \sigma_k}{\norm{\Amat}_F^2}.
$$

**Disparity.** If both centred sets are also scaled to unit Frobenius norm, the minimum squared error after the best
rotation and scaling is

$$
d = 1 - \paren{\sum_k \sigma_k}^2,
$$

which lies in $[0, 1]$. It is 0 when one shape is an exact rotated, scaled and shifted copy of the other. This is the
disparity reported by SciPy's `procrustes` and TorchMetrics' `procrustes_disparity` <Cite id="detlefsen2022" />.

## Worked example

Take the four points $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(0, 2)$, and rotate them by $30°$.

- Orthogonal Procrustes recovers the rotation exactly: $30.0°$.
- A copy scaled by 2 and shifted by $(3, -1)$ has disparity $10^{-31}$: the same shape.
- Adding perturbations of about 0.1 to the rotated points gives disparity 0.0056, a fitted rotation of $31.2°$ and a
  fitted scale of 1.05.
- A mirror image has $\det(\Vmat\Umat^\top) = -1$: the unconstrained solution is a reflection, and the Kabsch
  correction is needed if reflections are not allowed.

## Uses

- **Comparing shapes.** Landmark coordinates of skulls, leaves or faces are compared after removing position, size and
  orientation. Generalised Procrustes analysis aligns many shapes to a common mean shape by alternating alignment and
  averaging <Cite id="gower1975" />.
- **Aligning embedding spaces.** Two embeddings of the same items, trained separately, are defined only up to a
  rotation. The Procrustes rotation fitted on a set of anchor items maps one space onto the other, for example <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word
  embeddings</NoteLink> of two languages matched through a small dictionary.
- **Comparing learned representations.** The disparity between two models' representations of the same inputs is a
  similarity measure that ignores rotation and scale.
- **Structural biology.** The root-mean-square deviation between two protein structures is computed after Kabsch
  alignment.
- **Unknown correspondence.** Procrustes needs paired points. When the pairing is unknown, <NoteLink
  to="optimal-transport">optimal transport</NoteLink> finds the cheapest pairing for a fixed alignment, and the two
  steps can be alternated.

## Keeping embeddings compatible across model versions

A factorisation model that scores users and items by a dot product, such as a <NoteLink
to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink>, has no fixed meaning for its embedding axes. Any orthogonal change of
basis, applied to every user and item vector together, leaves every dot product and every ranking unchanged.
Retraining the model, even on unchanged data, lands in a different arbitrary basis. Downstream consumers built
against the old basis then break: an <NoteLink to="approximate-nearest-neighbour-search">approximate
nearest-neighbour index</NoteLink> built on the old item vectors returns the wrong neighbours for a query encoded in
the new space, a cached user embedding stored as another model's input feature no longer means what that model was
trained on, and a score computed from one old vector and one new vector is meaningless. Some of this mismatch is only
a change of basis. Some of it is real drift, from new interactions or a changed model, and cannot be undone.

Orthogonal Procrustes removes the basis mismatch. Take the new run's embeddings and the old run's embeddings for a
set of items or users present in both runs, the anchors, and fit $\Rmat$ by the closed-form solution derived above.
Apply $\Rmat$ to every embedding in the new run, not only the anchors. Because $\Rmat$ is orthogonal, this step is
lossless: it preserves every dot product between transformed vectors, so it costs one small matrix multiply and does
not change what the model predicts.

Alignment can only recover a rotation, and possibly a reflection (see the Kabsch correction, above). It cannot
recover a change that is not a rotation. An anchor whose true representation moved, because the item or the user
genuinely changed, stays out of place after alignment. A single such anchor also pulls the fitted $\Rmat$ slightly
off target, the outlier <Gloss name="tpr">sensitivity</Gloss> noted in Pitfalls, below. Residual drift after alignment is measured the way the
disparity, defined above, measures shape difference: the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> or distance between each anchor's old and
aligned-new embedding, and whether an item's or a user's nearest neighbours still agree across the retraining.

<EmbeddingCompatibility />

Netflix aligns the user and item embeddings of a production recommender this way every time the base model retrains
<Cite id="zielnicki2025" />. A raw factorisation is degenerate in more ways than a rotation: any invertible change of
basis leaves the dot product it feeds unchanged, orthogonal or not. Each run's embeddings are therefore first reduced
to a canonical low-rank factorisation by SVD. This SVD is computed cheaply from the embedding matrices rather than the
full user-item score matrix. Only then are the embeddings aligned onto a fixed reference run by orthogonal Procrustes.
The anchors are the item embeddings, because items are more stable day to day than users. On production data, the same
user's embedding taken two weeks apart had a cosine similarity close to zero before alignment, since the retrained
space is an arbitrary rotation away from the old one, and 0.82 after; four weeks apart it was 0.75, the remaining gap
now measuring real drift rather than basis mismatch <Cite id="zielnicki2025" />.

Training a new model to already sit close to the old one is an alternative, called backward-compatible representation
learning <Cite id="shen2020" />: it avoids realignment, but ties every future training run to imitating its predecessor,
where Procrustes alignment is a post-hoc step that leaves training untouched.

## Pitfalls

- **Matched points only.** Procrustes needs the correspondence between points. Without it, the problem is point-set
  registration, which alternates between matching and alignment.

- **Outliers.** The Frobenius norm is a sum of squares, so one badly matched point can pull the fitted rotation.
