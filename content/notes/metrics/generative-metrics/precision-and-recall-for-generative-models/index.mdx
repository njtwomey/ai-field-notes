<Definition>
Let $\Phi_r$ and $\Phi_g$ be the feature vectors of real and generated samples. For a set $\Phi$, let
$\text{NND}_k(\phivec)$ be the distance from $\phivec \in \Phi$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbour in $\Phi$, and define
the estimated manifold $M(\Phi) = \bigcup_{\phivec \in \Phi} B\paren{\phivec, \text{NND}_k(\phivec)}$. Then
<Cite id="kynkaanniemi2019" />

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{1}{\abs{\Phi_g}}\sum_{\phivec \in \Phi_g} \indicator\brack{\phivec \in M(\Phi_r)}, \qquad
\text{recall} = \frac{1}{\abs{\Phi_r}}\sum_{\phivec \in \Phi_r} \indicator\brack{\phivec \in M(\Phi_g)} .
$$
</Definition>

## Two failure modes, two numbers

A single distance such as <NoteLink to="frechet-inception-distance">FID</NoteLink> cannot say whether a generator is
poor because its samples look wrong or because it covers only part of the data. Precision measures the first: the
share of generated samples that fall where real data live. Recall measures the second: the share of real data that the
generator can produce. A generator that produces a few perfect images has high precision and low recall; one that
produces everything including garbage has high recall and low precision. The analogy with
<NoteLink to="precision-recall-and-f-score">classification precision and recall</NoteLink> is that generated samples play
the role of predictions and real samples the role of positives.

The first formulation defined precision and recall between distributions as a whole curve, trading one against the
other <Cite id="sajjadi2018" />. The $k$-nearest-neighbour version gives one number each and is the one in common use.

**Worked example.** Real features $\set{(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)}$ and generated features $\set{(0.1,0.1),
(0.9,0.1), (3,3)}$, with $k = 1$. Each real point's ball has radius 1. The first two generated points fall inside real
balls and $(3, 3)$ does not, so precision is $2/3$. The generated balls have radii $0.8$, $0.8$ and $3.58$ (the
outlier's nearest neighbour is far away). Three of the four real points fall inside one of them, so recall is $3/4$.
Without the outlier, precision would be 1 and recall only $1/2$: the single outlier lowered precision but raised recall,
because its large ball swallowed a real point.

## Pitfalls

- **Outliers inflate the manifolds.** As the example shows, an outlier's large ball covers much of the space, raising
  recall (for generated outliers) or precision (for real outliers). Density and coverage replace the union of balls by
  counts of how many balls contain each sample, and are more robust <Cite id="naeem2020" />.
- **The choice of $k$.** Larger $k$ makes the manifolds larger and both numbers higher. Compare at the same $k$ and
  sample size (the original uses $k = 3$).
- **Feature space.** The results depend on the embedding network, as for FID.
