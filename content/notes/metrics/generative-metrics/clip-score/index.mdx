<Definition>
Let $\evec_I$ and $\evec_C$ be the <Gloss name="clip" /> embeddings of an image and a caption <Cite id="radford2021" />. The CLIPScore
is <Cite id="hessel2021" />

$$
\text{CLIP-S}(I, C) = w \cdot \max\paren{\cos(\evec_I, \evec_C),\, 0}, \qquad w = 2.5 .
$$
</Definition>

## Why it works

CLIP is trained on hundreds of millions of image–text pairs to embed matching pairs close together and mismatched
pairs far apart, by a contrastive objective. The <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> of the two embeddings therefore estimates how well a
caption describes an image. Earlier captioning metrics such as <Gloss name="bleu" /> and CIDEr compare a candidate caption with
human-written references; CLIPScore compares it with the image itself, so it needs no references. The weight $w = 2.5$
only rescales the cosine values, which rarely exceed about 0.4, toward the unit interval.

When references are available, RefCLIPScore takes the harmonic mean of CLIP-S and the largest cosine similarity between
the candidate and any reference caption, combining image grounding with agreement with people.

For text-to-image generation the roles reverse: the CLIPScore between a prompt and the generated image measures how
closely the image follows the prompt, alongside <NoteLink to="frechet-inception-distance">FID</NoteLink>, which measures
image realism.

## Pitfalls

- **Bag-of-concepts behaviour.** CLIP embeddings capture which objects and attributes appear better than how they
  relate: "a horse riding an astronaut" and "an astronaut riding a horse" can score similarly.
- **Counting and text.** Numbers of objects and rendered text in images are captured poorly.
- **Inherited biases.** The score reflects CLIP's training data, including its social biases, and can be gamed by a
  generator optimised against the same model.
- **Model version.** Scores from different CLIP backbones are not comparable.
