<Definition>
For real features $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_m$ and generated features $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n$ in $\reals^d$, with kernel
$k(\xvec, \yvec) = (\xvec^\top\yvec/d + 1)^3$, the kernel inception distance is the unbiased estimate of the squared
maximum mean discrepancy <Cite id="binkowski2018" />:

$$
\widehat{\text{KID}} = \frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i \ne j} k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) + \frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i \ne j} k(\yvec_i, \yvec_j)
- \frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i, j} k(\xvec_i, \yvec_j) .
$$
</Definition>

## Maximum mean discrepancy

For a kernel $k$ with feature map $\phivec$, the maximum mean discrepancy between distributions $P$ and $Q$ is the
distance between their mean embeddings, $\text{MMD}^2 = \norm{\expect_P \phivec(\xvec) - \expect_Q \phivec(\yvec)}^2$
<Cite id="gretton2012" />. Expanding the square with $\phivec(\xvec)^\top\phivec(\yvec) = k(\xvec, \yvec)$ gives

$$
\text{MMD}^2 = \expect\brack{k(\xvec, \xvec')} + \expect\brack{k(\yvec, \yvec')} - 2\,\expect\brack{k(\xvec, \yvec)},
$$

with $\xvec, \xvec' \sim P$ and $\yvec, \yvec' \sim Q$ independent. Replacing each expectation by an average over
distinct pairs gives the estimator in the definition. Leaving out the $i = j$ terms makes it unbiased, because
$k(\xvec_i, \xvec_i)$ is not an unbiased estimate of $\expect[k(\xvec, \xvec')]$.

The cubic polynomial kernel compares the first three moments of the feature distributions, so KID sees skewness that
<NoteLink to="frechet-inception-distance">FID</NoteLink>, which uses means and covariances only, does not.

**Worked example.** In one dimension ($d = 1$) with real features $\set{0, 1}$ and generated features $\set{1, 2}$,
$k(x, y) = (xy + 1)^3$. The within-real term averages $k(0, 1) = 1$; the within-generated term averages $k(1, 2) = 27$;
the cross terms are $k(0, 1) = 1$, $k(0, 2) = 1$, $k(1, 1) = 8$ and $k(1, 2) = 27$, averaging $9.25$. So
$\widehat{\text{KID}} = 1 + 27 - 2 \times 9.25 = 9.5$.

## Why unbiasedness matters

FID's estimate shifts with the number of samples, so FID values at different sample sizes are not comparable, and small
test sets bias it. KID's estimate is correct in expectation at any sample size, so it can be computed on a few thousand
images and averaged over random subsets, which also gives a standard error. The price is higher variance per estimate.

## Pitfalls

- **Can be negative.** As an unbiased estimate of a non-negative quantity, $\widehat{\text{KID}}$ is sometimes slightly
  below 0 when the distributions are close. Do not clip it before averaging.
- **Scale.** KID values are small (often quoted $\times 10^3$) and depend on $d$ and the feature extractor; compare only
  within one setup.
- **Cost.** The estimator is quadratic in the number of samples; subsets of about 1,000 are averaged in practice.
