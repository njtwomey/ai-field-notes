<Definition>
An internal clustering index is a function of the data $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ and their cluster assignments only. It
rewards clusters that are compact (small distances within a cluster) and separated (large distances between clusters).
</Definition>

## Scoring without labels

External scores such as the <NoteLink to="rand-index-and-adjusted-rand-index">adjusted Rand index</NoteLink> need a
reference clustering. In most clustering problems there is none. Internal indices judge a clustering by its geometry
instead. Every index encodes an assumption about what a good cluster looks like, usually compact and roughly convex. A
clustering that suits the data but violates that assumption, such as two interleaved rings, scores badly.

The four indices below use a distance $d$ between points, Euclidean unless stated. Let $C_1, \dots, C_k$ be the
clusters, $\muvec_j$ the centroid of $C_j$, and $\muvec$ the overall mean.

## Silhouette

For a point $\xvec_i$ in cluster $C$, let $a_i$ be its mean distance to the other points of $C$, and $b_i$ its mean
distance to the points of the nearest other cluster. The silhouette of the point is <Cite id="rousseeuw1987" />

$$
s_i = \frac{b_i - a_i}{\max(a_i, b_i)} \in [-1, 1].
$$

A value near 1 means the point is much closer to its own cluster than to any other. A value near 0 means it lies
between two clusters. A negative value means it is closer, on average, to another cluster. A point alone in its cluster
gets $s_i = 0$ by convention. The silhouette score is the mean of $s_i$ over all points. Per-point silhouettes, plotted
by cluster, show which clusters are poorly separated. The cost is $O(n^2)$ distances.

## Calinski–Harabasz

The Calinski–Harabasz index, or variance ratio criterion, compares the between-cluster dispersion
$B = \sum_j \abs{C_j}\,\norm{\muvec_j - \muvec}^2$ with the within-cluster dispersion
$W = \sum_j \sum_{\xvec \in C_j} \norm{\xvec - \muvec_j}^2$ <Cite id="calinski1974" />:

$$
\operatorname{CH} = \frac{B/(k - 1)}{W/(n - k)}.
$$

The degrees of freedom $k - 1$ and $n - k$ play the same role as in the $F$ statistic of a one-way analysis of variance.
Higher is better. $W$ is the quantity <NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> minimises, and $B + W$ is the fixed
total dispersion, so CH favours the same compact, spherical clusters. The cost is $O(nk)$.

## Davies–Bouldin

Let $S_j$ be the mean distance of the points of $C_j$ to $\muvec_j$, and $M_{jl} = d(\muvec_j, \muvec_l)$. For each
cluster, take its worst ratio of combined spread to separation, and average <Cite id="davies1979" />:

$$
\operatorname{DB} = \frac1k \sum_{j=1}^k \max_{l \ne j} \frac{S_j + S_l}{M_{jl}}.
$$

Lower is better; 0 is the ideal. Each term punishes a cluster that has a wide, close neighbour.

## Dunn

The Dunn index divides the smallest distance between points of different clusters by the largest cluster diameter
<Cite id="dunn1973" />:

$$
\operatorname{D} = \frac{\min_{j \ne l} \min_{\xvec \in C_j, \xvec' \in C_l} d(\xvec, \xvec')}
{\max_j \max_{\xvec, \xvec' \in C_j} d(\xvec, \xvec')}.
$$

Higher is better. A value above 1 means every cluster is narrower than the gap to its nearest neighbour. Because it
uses a single minimum and a single maximum, one outlier can change the index completely.

## Choosing the number of clusters

The main use of internal indices is to choose $k$: fit the clustering for several $k$ and pick the best score. Raw
within-cluster dispersion $W$ cannot do this, because it always falls as $k$ grows. The indices balance compactness
against separation, so they can peak. For a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture</NoteLink>,
likelihood-based criteria are an alternative.

The indices do not always agree. For 300 points drawn from four overlapping Gaussian blobs (scikit-learn's
`make_blobs` with `cluster_std=1.5` and `random_state=0`) clustered by k-means:

| $k$ | Silhouette | Calinski–Harabasz | Davies–Bouldin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | **0.416** | 284.6 | 0.905 |
| 3 | 0.375 | 269.7 | 0.953 |
| 4 | 0.383 | **286.7** | **0.848** |
| 5 | 0.357 | 257.6 | 0.909 |
| 6 | 0.327 | 249.2 | 0.941 |

Calinski–Harabasz and Davies–Bouldin pick the true $k = 4$. The silhouette prefers $k = 2$, because two pairs of blobs
overlap and merging them gives cleaner separation. Values computed with scikit-learn <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Worked example

Five points on a line: $0, 1$ in cluster 1 and $5, 6, 7$ in cluster 2. The centroids are 0.5 and 6; the overall mean
is 3.8.

- **Silhouette.** For the point 0, $a = 1$ and $b = (5 + 6 + 7)/3 = 6$, so $s = 1 - 1/6 = 0.833$. For the point 5,
  $a = (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5$ and $b = (5 + 4)/2 = 4.5$, so $s = 0.667$. The other points give 0.8, 0.818 and 0.769, and the
  mean is 0.777.
- **Calinski–Harabasz.** $B = 2 \cdot 3.3^2 + 3 \cdot 2.2^2 = 36.3$ and $W = 0.5 + 2 = 2.5$. With $k = 2$ and $n = 5$,
  $\operatorname{CH} = (36.3/1)/(2.5/3) = 43.56$.
- **Davies–Bouldin.** $S_1 = 0.5$, $S_2 = 2/3$ and $M_{12} = 5.5$, so $\operatorname{DB} = (0.5 + 0.667)/5.5 = 0.212$.
- **Dunn.** The closest points of different clusters are 1 and 5, distance 4. The widest cluster is $\set{5, 6, 7}$,
  diameter 2. $\operatorname{D} = 4/2 = 2$.

## Pitfalls

- **Shape assumptions.** All four indices prefer compact, convex clusters. They mislead for density-based clusterings
  and elongated or nested clusters.
- **Scale.** Distances depend on feature scaling. Standardise features, or choose a distance on purpose; see
  <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">pairwise distances</NoteLink>.
- **Comparing algorithms.** An index that shares the objective of one algorithm favours it: CH rewards whatever
  k-means optimises.
- **One cluster.** None of the indices is defined for $k = 1$, so none can say that the data has no cluster structure.
