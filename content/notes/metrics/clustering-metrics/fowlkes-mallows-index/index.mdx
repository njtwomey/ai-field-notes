<Definition>
Compare a predicted clustering with a reference clustering by pairs of items. Let $\text{TP}$ be the number of pairs
together in both, $\text{FP}$ the pairs together only in the prediction, and $\text{FN}$ the pairs together only in
the reference. The Fowlkes–Mallows index is <Cite id="fowlkes1983" />

$$
\operatorname{FMI} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\sqrt{(\text{TP} + \text{FP})(\text{TP} + \text{FN})}}.
$$
</Definition>

## Pairwise precision and recall

Treat "these two items belong together" as a binary prediction made for every pair. The pairwise precision
$\text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FP})$ is the fraction of pairs the prediction groups that the reference also groups. The
pairwise recall $\text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FN})$ is the fraction of reference pairs the prediction recovers. The
Fowlkes–Mallows index is the geometric mean of the two:

$$
\operatorname{FMI} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}} \cdot \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}}.
$$

The geometric mean is close to the harmonic mean, the pairwise
<NoteLink to="precision-recall-and-f-score">F-score</NoteLink>, when precision and recall are similar. Merging clusters
raises recall and lowers precision; splitting does the reverse. FMI is 1 only when the two clusterings are identical.

In terms of the contingency table $n_{ij}$ with row sums $a_i$ and column sums $b_j$,
$\text{TP} = \sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}2$, $\text{TP} + \text{FN} = \sum_i\binom{a_i}2$ and
$\text{TP} + \text{FP} = \sum_j\binom{b_j}2$, so FMI takes one pass over the table.

## Pairs kept apart do not count

Unlike the <NoteLink to="rand-index-and-adjusted-rand-index">Rand index</NoteLink>, FMI ignores <Gloss name="tn">true negatives</Gloss>, the pairs
both clusterings keep apart. With many clusters almost all pairs are true negatives, and they push the Rand index
towards 1 regardless of quality. FMI avoids that inflation.

FMI is still not corrected for chance. A random labelling with the same cluster sizes puts some pairs together by
accident. For 90 items in three equal clusters, random labels give an FMI of about 0.33; for 60 items in five clusters,
about 0.20. Fowlkes and Mallows derived the expectation and variance of the index under random labelling and used them
to judge <NoteLink to="hierarchical-clustering">hierarchical clusterings</NoteLink> level by level <Cite id="fowlkes1983" />. For a single chance-corrected number, use
the adjusted Rand index or <NoteLink to="information-theoretic-clustering-metrics">adjusted mutual information</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

The true clustering is $\set{1,2,3}, \set{4,5,6}, \set{7,8,9}$ and the prediction is $\set{1,2,9}, \set{3,4,5,6},
\set{7,8}$. The pair counts are $\text{TP} = 5$, $\text{FP} = 5$ and $\text{FN} = 4$.

- Pairwise precision is $5/10 = 0.5$.
- Pairwise recall is $5/9 = 0.556$.
- $\operatorname{FMI} = 5/\sqrt{10 \cdot 9} = 0.527$.

The adjusted Rand index of the same pair is 0.357: after subtracting chance, the agreement looks weaker. scikit-learn's
`fowlkes_mallows_score` gives 0.527 <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Singletons.** If the prediction puts every item alone, $\text{TP} + \text{FP} = 0$ and FMI is undefined; libraries
  return 0.
- **Few clusters.** One cluster containing everything has pairwise recall 1 and precision equal to the fraction of
  pairs that truly belong together, which can be large when there are few classes.
- **Not chance-corrected.** Compare FMI only between clusterings with similar numbers and sizes of clusters.
