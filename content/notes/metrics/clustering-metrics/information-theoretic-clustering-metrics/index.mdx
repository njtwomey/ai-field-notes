<Definition>
Let $C$ be the true class and $K$ the assigned cluster of a randomly chosen item, with joint distribution given by the
contingency table $n_{ij}/n$. Then:

- the mutual information is $I(C; K)$ and the entropies are $\entropy(C)$ and $\entropy(K)$;
- $\operatorname{NMI} = I(C; K)/m(\entropy(C), \entropy(K))$ for a mean $m$ (arithmetic, geometric, maximum or minimum);
- homogeneity is $h = I(C; K)/\entropy(C)$, completeness is $c = I(C; K)/\entropy(K)$, and the V-measure is their
  harmonic mean <Cite id="rosenberg2007" />;
- $\operatorname{AMI} = \paren{I - \expect[I]}/\paren{m(\entropy(C), \entropy(K)) - \expect[I]}$, with $\expect[I]$
  taken over random labellings with the same cluster sizes <Cite id="vinh2010" />.
</Definition>

## Mutual information between labels

Treat the two labels of a random item as <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink>. Their
<NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> measures how much knowing the cluster reduces
uncertainty about the class. It is 0 when the clustering is independent of the classes and reaches
$\min(\entropy(C), \entropy(K))$ when one determines the other. Like the pair-counting scores, it ignores the names of
the clusters: relabelling permutes the table without changing any probability.

Raw mutual information is not comparable across problems, because its maximum depends on the entropies. Dividing by a
mean of the two entropies gives a score in $[0, 1]$, the normalised mutual information. The four common means give
different values when the entropies differ.

## Homogeneity, completeness and V-measure

The two ways a clustering can fail get separate scores <Cite id="rosenberg2007" />.

- **Homogeneity** asks whether each cluster contains a single class: $h = 1 - \entropy(C \mid K)/\entropy(C)
  = I(C; K)/\entropy(C)$. Merging two classes into one cluster lowers it.
- **Completeness** asks whether each class sits in a single cluster: $c = 1 - \entropy(K \mid C)/\entropy(K) =
  I(C; K)/\entropy(K)$. Splitting a class across clusters lowers it.

Homogeneity is Theil's uncertainty coefficient of the classes given the clusters; see
<NoteLink to="nominal-association">nominal association</NoteLink>. Putting every item in its own cluster gives
perfect homogeneity; putting all items in one cluster gives perfect completeness. Neither alone is enough.

**V-measure is NMI.** The harmonic mean of $h$ and $c$ simplifies:

$$
V = \frac{2hc}{h + c}
= \frac{2\,I^2/\paren{\entropy(C)\entropy(K)}}{I/\entropy(C) + I/\entropy(K)}
= \frac{2I}{\entropy(C) + \entropy(K)},
$$

which is the NMI normalised by the arithmetic mean of the entropies.

## Correcting for chance

NMI and V-measure are not zero for random clusterings, and their chance value grows with the <NoteLink to="choosing-the-number-of-clusters">number of clusters</NoteLink>
relative to the number of items. For 60 items and five clusters, random labellings average about 0.10; with many small
clusters the bias is larger. A clustering with more clusters can therefore score higher without being better.

Adjusted mutual information (AMI) subtracts the expected mutual information under the permutation model, which fixes the cluster sizes and assigns
items at random <Cite id="vinh2010" />. The expectation has a closed form as a sum over the possible cell counts,
weighted by hypergeometric probabilities. AMI is then about 0 for random clusterings and 1 for identical ones, like the
<NoteLink to="rand-index-and-adjusted-rand-index">adjusted Rand index</NoteLink>.

## Variation of information

The variation of information $\operatorname{VI} = \entropy(C) + \entropy(K) - 2I(C; K) = \entropy(C \mid K) +
\entropy(K \mid C)$ is a distance between clusterings: it satisfies the triangle inequality, so it can be used where a
metric space of clusterings is needed <Cite id="meila2007" />. It is in nats and bounded by $\log n$, so values are not
comparable across data sets of different sizes.

## Worked example

The true clustering is $\set{1,2,3}, \set{4,5,6}, \set{7,8,9}$ and the prediction is $\set{1,2,9}, \set{3,4,5,6},
\set{7,8}$, as in the <NoteLink to="rand-index-and-adjusted-rand-index">Rand index</NoteLink> example. In nats:

| Score | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $I(C; K)$ | 0.637 |
| homogeneity $h$ | 0.579 |
| completeness $c$ | 0.600 |
| V-measure = NMI (arithmetic) | 0.590 |
| NMI (geometric) | 0.590 |
| AMI | 0.409 |

The predicted cluster $\set{3,4,5,6}$ mixes two classes, which costs homogeneity; class $\set{7,8,9}$ is split, which
costs completeness. AMI is well below NMI: with nine items, chance alone produces substantial mutual information. These
match scikit-learn <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **State the normalisation.** NMI with the maximum, geometric or arithmetic mean are different numbers. Libraries
  differ in their defaults.
- **Do not compare NMI across different numbers of clusters** without chance correction. Use AMI.
- **Cost.** The expected mutual information sums over every possible cell count, so AMI is much slower than NMI for
  large $n$ and many clusters.
