<Definition>
Two clusterings $U$ and $V$ of the same $n$ items induce a contingency table $n_{ij}$: the number of items in cluster
$i$ of $U$ and cluster $j$ of $V$, with row sums $a_i$ and column sums $b_j$. Of the $\binom n2$ pairs of items, let
$\text{TP}$ be the pairs together in both, $\text{TN}$ the pairs apart in both. The **Rand index** is
$\operatorname{RI} = (\text{TP} + \text{TN})/\binom n2$ <Cite id="rand1971" />. The **adjusted Rand index** is
<Cite id="hubert1985" />

$$
\operatorname{ARI} = \frac{\sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}2 - \dfrac{\sum_i\binom{a_i}2\sum_j\binom{b_j}2}{\binom n2}}
{\dfrac12\paren{\sum_i\binom{a_i}2 + \sum_j\binom{b_j}2} - \dfrac{\sum_i\binom{a_i}2\sum_j\binom{b_j}2}{\binom n2}}.
$$
</Definition>

## Comparing clusterings by pairs

Cluster labels are arbitrary. Calling the clusters $\set{1, 2}$ or $\set{2, 1}$ describes the same partition, so a
score must not depend on the names. Pairs of items avoid the problem: for any pair, a clustering either puts both in
the same cluster or not, whatever the clusters are called.

Every pair falls into one of four cells, like a <NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink> with the
true clustering as the reference:

| | Together in $V$ | Apart in $V$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Together in $U$ | <Gloss name="tp" /> | <Gloss name="fn" /> |
| Apart in $U$ | <Gloss name="fp" /> | <Gloss name="tn" /> |

The counts come from the contingency table. $\text{TP} = \sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}2$ counts pairs within a cell;
$\sum_i\binom{a_i}2 = \text{TP} + \text{FN}$ counts pairs together in $U$; and
$\sum_j\binom{b_j}2 = \text{TP} + \text{FP}$ counts pairs together in $V$. The Rand index is the accuracy of this
pairwise classification.

## Why adjust

Most pairs are apart in any clustering with several clusters, so TN is large for almost any labelling, and the Rand
index stays high even when the clustering is random. For three equal clusters of 30 points and fully random labels,
the Rand index averages 0.56.

Hubert and Arabie correct this in the usual way for chance-corrected scores: subtract the <NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink> and rescale
so that the maximum is 1 <Cite id="hubert1985" />.

$$
\operatorname{ARI} = \frac{\text{Index} - \expect[\text{Index}]}{\text{Max} - \expect[\text{Index}]}, \qquad
\text{Index} = \sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}2.
$$

**The expected index.** Under the permutation model, the cluster sizes $a_i$ and $b_j$ are fixed and labels are
assigned at random. A particular pair of items is together in $U$ with probability $\sum_i\binom{a_i}2/\binom n2$, and
independently together in $V$ with probability $\sum_j\binom{b_j}2/\binom n2$. Multiplying by the number of pairs,

$$
\expect[\text{Index}] = \frac{\sum_i\binom{a_i}2\,\sum_j\binom{b_j}2}{\binom n2}.
$$

The maximum is taken as the average of the two marginal counts, $\frac12\paren{\sum_i\binom{a_i}2 +
\sum_j\binom{b_j}2}$, which the index reaches only when the two clusterings agree. So ARI is 1 for identical
clusterings, about 0 on average for random ones, and negative for clusterings that agree less than chance.

## Worked example

The true clustering is $\set{1,2,3}, \set{4,5,6}, \set{7,8,9}$ and the prediction is $\set{1,2,9}, \set{3,4,5,6},
\set{7,8}$. The contingency table has rows $(2, 1, 0)$, $(0, 3, 0)$ and $(1, 0, 2)$.

- $\text{TP} = \binom22 + \binom32 + \binom22 = 5$.
- Pairs together in the truth: $3\binom32 = 9$, so $\text{FN} = 4$. Pairs together in the prediction:
  $\binom32 + \binom42 + \binom22 = 10$, so $\text{FP} = 5$. Of the 36 pairs, $\text{TN} = 22$.
- $\operatorname{RI} = (5 + 22)/36 = 0.75$.
- $\expect[\text{Index}] = 9 \cdot 10/36 = 2.5$ and the maximum is $(9 + 10)/2 = 9.5$, so
  $\operatorname{ARI} = (5 - 2.5)/(9.5 - 2.5) = 0.357$.

The prediction looks 75% right by the Rand index, but only a third of the way from chance to perfect by the adjusted
index. scikit-learn's `rand_score` and `adjusted_rand_score` give the same values <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Unequal cluster sizes.** The pair counts are dominated by large clusters, so ARI rewards getting the big clusters
  right and barely notices errors in small ones.
- **Different numbers of clusters.** ARI penalises splitting a true cluster and merging two true clusters, but not
  symmetrically; compare with the <NoteLink to="information-theoretic-clustering-metrics">information-theoretic
  scores</NoteLink>, which separate the two failures.
- **External scores need ground truth.** ARI compares with known labels. Without them, use
  <NoteLink to="internal-clustering-indices">internal indices</NoteLink>, which judge a clustering by its geometry
  alone.
