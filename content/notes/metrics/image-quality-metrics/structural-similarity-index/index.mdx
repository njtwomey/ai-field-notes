<Definition>
For two image patches $x$ and $y$ with means $\mu_x, \mu_y$, <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviations</NoteLink> $\sigma_x, \sigma_y$ and covariance
$\sigma_{xy}$ <Cite id="wang2004" />:

$$
\text{SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2)},
$$

with $C_1 = (K_1 L)^2$, $C_2 = (K_2 L)^2$, $K_1 = 0.01$, $K_2 = 0.03$ and $L$ the dynamic range. The image SSIM is the
mean of this quantity over local windows.
</Definition>

## Three comparisons

SSIM is the product of three terms, each a similarity in $[0, 1]$ or $[-1, 1]$:

$$
l = \frac{2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1}{\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1}, \qquad
c = \frac{2\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2}, \qquad
s = \frac{\sigma_{xy} + C_3}{\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_3}.
$$

The luminance term $l$ compares means, the contrast term $c$ compares standard deviations, and the structure term $s$
is the correlation of the patches after removing mean and contrast. The luminance and contrast terms have the form
$2ab/(a^2 + b^2)$, which is 1 exactly when $a = b$ and depends only on the ratio $a/b$, so a given absolute change
matters less in bright or high-contrast regions, as it does to the eye. The constants keep the ratios stable when the denominators are near zero.

With $C_3 = C_2/2$, the contrast and structure terms combine:

$$
c \cdot s = \frac{2\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2} \cdot
\frac{2\sigma_{xy} + C_2}{2\sigma_x\sigma_y + C_2} = \frac{2\sigma_{xy} + C_2}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2},
$$

and $l \cdot c \cdot s$ is the formula in the definition.

**Worked example.** Two 8-bit patches $x = (50, 60, 70, 80)$ and $y = (52, 58, 75, 79)$ have means $65$ and $66$,
sample variances $166.7$ and $170.0$ and covariance $163.3$. Then $l = 0.99988$, $c = 0.99996$, $s = 0.9747$ and
$\text{SSIM} = 0.9746$: the patches have nearly the same brightness and contrast, and the small loss is structural.

## Windows and scales

SSIM is computed in a sliding window and averaged. The original method uses an $11 \times 11$ Gaussian window with
standard deviation 1.5; scikit-image defaults to a $7 \times 7$ uniform window with sample covariances. The two give
different numbers, so the convention must be stated. The map of window values shows where an image is degraded.

Multi-scale SSIM (MS-SSIM) repeats the contrast and structure comparison on successively downsampled images, and the
luminance comparison at the coarsest scale only, and combines them as a weighted product <Cite id="wang2003" />. It
suits images viewed at different distances, and is the more common choice for compression research.

## Pitfalls

- **SSIM is not a distance.** $1 - \text{SSIM}$ does not satisfy the triangle inequality, and SSIM can be negative when
  local structure is anti-correlated.
- **Data range.** $C_1$ and $C_2$ depend on $L$. Floating-point images need `data_range` set explicitly; the wrong range
  changes the constants and the score.
- **Colour.** SSIM is defined on greyscale; colour images are usually converted to luminance or scored per channel and
  averaged.
- **Geometric shifts.** A one-pixel translation lowers SSIM sharply, though people cannot see it. Complex-wavelet and
  learned metrics are more tolerant (see <NoteLink to="perceptual-image-metrics">perceptual image metrics</NoteLink>).
