<Definition>
For two regions $A$ and $B$, the intersection over union is

$$
\text{IoU}(A, B) = \frac{\abs{A \cap B}}{\abs{A \cup B}} \in [0, 1].
$$

For axis-aligned boxes $B$ (prediction) and $B^{\text{gt}}$ (ground truth), with $C$ the smallest box enclosing both,
$\bvec$ and $\bvec^{\text{gt}}$ their centres, $\rho$ the <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">Euclidean distance</NoteLink> and $c$ the diagonal length of $C$
<Cite id="rezatofighi2019" /> <Cite id="zheng2020" />:

$$
\text{GIoU} = \text{IoU} - \frac{\abs{C \setminus (B \cup B^{\text{gt}})}}{\abs{C}}, \qquad
\text{DIoU} = \text{IoU} - \frac{\rho^2(\bvec, \bvec^{\text{gt}})}{c^2}, \qquad
\text{CIoU} = \text{DIoU} - \alpha v .
$$
</Definition>

## IoU

IoU is the Jaccard index of two sets, applied to regions of the plane <Cite id="jaccard1912" />. It is 1 for identical
regions and 0 for disjoint ones, and it is invariant to the scale of the scene: doubling every coordinate leaves it
unchanged, so a 5-pixel error on a small box costs more than on a large one. That scale invariance is why detection
benchmarks threshold IoU (a detection counts as correct at $\text{IoU} \ge 0.5$) rather than threshold coordinate
errors. $1 - \text{IoU}$ is a proper distance on sets: it satisfies the triangle inequality.

**Worked example.** $A = [0, 4] \times [0, 4]$ and $B = [2, 6] \times [2, 6]$ overlap in a $2 \times 2$ square. The
intersection is 4 and the union $16 + 16 - 4 = 28$, so $\text{IoU} = 4/28 = 0.143$.

## Why variants are needed

Using $1 - \text{IoU}$ as a loss for box regression fails when the boxes do not overlap: IoU is 0 for every
non-overlapping placement, so its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is 0 and the loss cannot tell a near miss from a distant one. It also
cannot tell how two boxes with the same overlap are arranged.

**GIoU** subtracts the fraction of the enclosing box $C$ that neither box covers. When the boxes separate, $C$ grows
with the gap, so GIoU keeps decreasing. It lies in $(-1, 1]$ and equals IoU when one box contains the other, because
then $C$ is the union. In the example, $C = [0, 6] \times [0, 6]$ has area 36 and the uncovered part is $36 - 28 = 8$,
so $\text{GIoU} = 0.143 - 8/36 = -0.079$.

**DIoU** subtracts the squared distance between centres, normalised by the squared diagonal of $C$. It penalises
misplacement directly, and converges faster in training because the penalty points straight at the target centre. In
the example the centres $(2, 2)$ and $(4, 4)$ are $\sqrt 8$ apart and $c^2 = 72$, so
$\text{DIoU} = 0.143 - 8/72 = 0.032$.

**CIoU** adds a term for the aspect ratio,

$$
v = \frac{4}{\pi^2}\paren{\arctan\frac{w^{\text{gt}}}{h^{\text{gt}}} - \arctan\frac{w}{h}}^2, \qquad
\alpha = \frac{v}{(1 - \text{IoU}) + v},
$$

where $w$ and $h$ are width and height. $v \in [0, 1]$ measures the difference in angle of the diagonals, and $\alpha$
gives it more weight as the overlap improves. For the two squares of the example $v = 0$ and CIoU equals DIoU. If $B$
is stretched to $[2, 6] \times [2, 8]$, then $\text{IoU} = 0.111$, $\text{DIoU} = -0.019$, and the aspect penalty
$\alpha v$ lowers CIoU slightly further, to $-0.0192$.

## As losses and as metrics

The variants were introduced as training losses, $1 - \text{GIoU}$ and so on. Evaluation still uses plain IoU, to
decide whether a detection matches a ground-truth object (see <NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-for-detection">mean
average precision for detection</NoteLink>). For segmentation masks, IoU per class is averaged into mean IoU (see
<NoteLink to="dice-and-segmentation-overlap">Dice and segmentation overlap</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Small objects.** Because IoU is scale-invariant, a fixed pixel error hurts small objects much more. A 2-pixel shift
  of a $10 \times 10$ box gives IoU 0.67; of a $100 \times 100$ box, 0.96.
- **Pixel conventions.** Some datasets count box sizes inclusively ($x_1 - x_0 + 1$), which changes IoU noticeably for
  small boxes. Match the convention of the benchmark.
- **Rotated boxes.** The formulas above assume axis-aligned boxes. Rotated boxes need polygon intersection.
