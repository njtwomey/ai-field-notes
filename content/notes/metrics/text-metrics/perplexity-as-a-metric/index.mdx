<Definition>
For a model $p$ evaluated on held-out tokens $x_1, \dots, x_T$,

$$
\mathrm{PPL} = \exp\paren{-\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p\paren{x_t \mid x_{<t}}}.
$$

The exponent is the average negative log-likelihood per token, the cross-entropy of the model on the data. PPL is at
least 1, and lower is better <Cite id="jelinek1977" />.
</Definition>

## What the number means

The derivation of perplexity from cross-entropy is in <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy and
perplexity</NoteLink>. As an evaluation metric the useful reading is the effective branching factor: a model with
perplexity $k$ is as uncertain, on average, as one choosing uniformly among $k$ tokens at every step. A uniform model
over a vocabulary of $V$ tokens has perplexity exactly $V$, and a model that assigns probability 1 to every correct
token has perplexity 1.

If a model assigns the probabilities $0.5, 0.25, 0.25, 0.125$ to the four tokens of a held-out text, the average
negative log-likelihood is $\tfrac14(\ln 2 + \ln 4 + \ln 4 + \ln 8) = 2\ln 2$ nats, which is 2 bits per token, and the
perplexity is $e^{2\ln 2} = 4$.

## The unit is the tokeniser's

The average is taken per token, so perplexity depends on what a token is. Two models with different tokenisers
spread the same total log-likelihood over different numbers of tokens. The total negative log-likelihood $L$ of a text
is the quantity that can be compared; the normalisation is a choice.

For a text of $T$ tokens and $W$ words,

$$
\mathrm{PPL}_{\text{word}} = e^{L/W} = \paren{e^{L/T}}^{T/W} = \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{token}}^{\,T/W}.
$$

A text of 8 words split into 12 tokens, with token-level perplexity 4, has word-level perplexity $4^{12/8} = 8$.
Normalising by characters or bytes instead gives bits per character or bits per byte, $L / (\text{bytes} \cdot \ln
2)$, which is comparable across tokenisers and connects the model to compression: by the
<NoteLink to="source-coding-theorem">source coding theorem</NoteLink>, an arithmetic coder driven by the model
compresses the text to about $L/\ln 2$ bits.

## How it is measured

- **Held-out data only.** Perplexity on training data measures memorisation.
- **Context length.** A model with a fixed context window must evaluate long texts in chunks. Non-overlapping chunks
  give the tokens at the start of each chunk almost no context, which inflates perplexity; a sliding window with a
  stride shorter than the context gives every token a long context at more cost.
- **Special tokens and padding.** Beginning- and end-of-sequence tokens and padding must be counted or excluded
  consistently.

## Pitfalls

- **Not comparable across vocabularies.** Compare per-token perplexity only between models with the same tokeniser;
  otherwise use bits per byte or the total log-likelihood.
- **Low perplexity is not good generation.** Perplexity measures how well the model predicts held-out text. It does
  not measure factual accuracy, instruction following or the quality of sampled text, and it can be lowered by
  predicting frequent tokens well.
- **Domain dependence.** Perplexity on one corpus says little about another. Report the evaluation corpus.
