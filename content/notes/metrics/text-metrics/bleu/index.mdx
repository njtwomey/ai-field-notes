<Definition>
For a corpus of candidate translations, each with one or more references, BLEU is

$$
\mathrm{BLEU} = \mathrm{BP} \cdot \exp\paren{\sum_{n=1}^{N} w_n \log p_n}, \qquad
\mathrm{BP} = \begin{cases} 1 & c > r, \\ e^{1 - r/c} & c \le r, \end{cases}
$$

where $p_n$ is the clipped n-gram precision pooled over the corpus, $c$ is the total candidate length, $r$ is the
total effective reference length, and the standard weights are $w_n = 1/4$ for $n = 1, \dots, 4$
<Cite id="papineni2002" />. It lies in $[0, 1]$, often reported times 100, and higher is better.
</Definition>

## Clipped n-gram precision

Plain n-gram precision, the fraction of candidate n-grams that appear in a reference, rewards repetition. The
candidate "the the the the the the the" against the reference "the cat is on the mat" has unigram precision
$7/7$. BLEU clips each candidate n-gram's count at the largest number of times it occurs in any single reference:

$$
p_n = \frac{\sum_{\text{candidates}} \sum_{g \in \text{n-grams}} \min\paren{\mathrm{count}(g),\ \max_{\text{refs}} \mathrm{count}_{\text{ref}}(g)}}
{\sum_{\text{candidates}} \sum_{g \in \text{n-grams}} \mathrm{count}(g)}.
$$

With the two references "the cat is on the mat" and "there is a cat on the mat", "the" occurs at most twice in one
reference, so the degenerate candidate scores $p_1 = 2/7$ <Cite id="papineni2002" />.

The counts are summed over the whole corpus before dividing. BLEU is not the average of sentence scores.

## Why a geometric mean and a brevity penalty

Unigram precision measures whether the right words appear; higher orders measure fluency and word order. The
geometric mean is the exponential of the average log precision, so a system must do reasonably at every order: a low
$p_4$ cannot be offset by a high $p_1$ as it could in an arithmetic mean.

Precision alone rewards short candidates. A candidate of three correct words has perfect precision whatever it omits.
Recall is awkward with several references, since a good translation should not have to contain every word of every
reference, so BLEU uses a brevity penalty instead. With $c$ the candidate length and $r$ the effective reference length
(for each sentence, the reference length closest to the candidate's), the penalty is $e^{1 - r/c}$ when $c \le r$ and 1
otherwise. Its logarithm is $1 - r/c$, which is 0 at $c = r$ and falls steeply as the candidate shrinks. Longer
candidates are not penalised directly; they already lose precision through their extra, unmatched n-grams.

## Worked example

Reference "the cat is on the mat", candidate "the cat sat on the mat", one sentence.

| order $n$ | candidate n-grams | clipped matches | $p_n$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 6 | 5 | $5/6$ |
| 2 | 5 | 3 (the cat, on the, the mat) | $3/5$ |
| 3 | 4 | 1 (on the mat) | $1/4$ |
| 4 | 3 | 0 | 0 |

Both sentences have 6 words, so $\mathrm{BP} = 1$. Because $p_4 = 0$, $\log p_4 = -\infty$ and the sentence-level
BLEU is **0**, although five of six words are right.

Add a second sentence pair, "there is a dog in the garden" against "there is a dog in the yard". Pooled over both
sentences the precisions are $11/13$, $8/11$, $5/9$ and $3/7$, and the corpus BLEU is $0.619$. A zero on one
sentence does not zero the corpus, because the counts are pooled before the logarithm.

Shortening the first candidate to "the cat is on" gives $p_1 = \dots = p_4 = 1$ but $\mathrm{BP} = e^{1 - 6/4} =
0.607$, so BLEU is $0.607$.

## Sentence-level BLEU and smoothing

BLEU was designed for corpora. On one sentence, any order without a match makes the score zero, and short sentences
have few higher-order n-grams to match. Smoothing adds a small amount to the counts. Adding one to the matched and
total counts for $n \ge 2$, one of the methods compared by Chen and Cherry, turns the example above into
$p = (5/6, 4/6, 2/5, 1/4)$ and a sentence BLEU of $0.485$ <Cite id="chen2014" />. Smoothed sentence BLEU is useful as a
per-example signal, for instance as a reward, but it is a different number from corpus BLEU and should be labelled so.

## Reporting BLEU

BLEU depends on tokenisation, lowercasing, the number of references and the n-gram weights. Scores computed with
different preprocessing differ by more than many reported improvements. SacreBLEU fixes the tokenisation and reports a
signature string that records every setting, so that scores can be compared across papers <Cite id="post2018" />.
Libraries such as TorchMetrics expose both the original computation and the SacreBLEU one
<Cite id="detlefsen2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **It is a precision metric with a brevity penalty.** BLEU never checks that the content of the reference is covered,
  only that the candidate's n-grams appear and that it is long enough.
- **No credit for synonyms or morphology.** "sat" and "sits" count as a miss. Character-level metrics such as
  <NoteLink to="chrf">chrF</NoteLink> and embedding-based ones such as <NoteLink to="bertscore">BERTScore</NoteLink>
  address this.
- **Sentence-level scores are noisy.** Differences of a point or two on small test sets are often not significant;
  <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> resampling of the test set gives a <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink>.
- **Scores are not comparable across preprocessing.** Report the SacreBLEU signature, or at least the tokenisation and
  casing.
