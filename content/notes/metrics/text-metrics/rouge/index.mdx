<Definition>
For a candidate $\hat y$ and reference $y$, ROUGE-N is the n-gram overlap measured against the reference,

$$
R_N = \frac{\sum_{g \in \text{n-grams}(y)} \min\paren{\mathrm{count}_{\hat y}(g),\ \mathrm{count}_y(g)}}{\sum_{g \in \text{n-grams}(y)} \mathrm{count}_y(g)},
$$

and ROUGE-L uses the length of the longest common subsequence, $R_L = \mathrm{LCS}(\hat y, y)/\abs{y}$ and $P_L =
\mathrm{LCS}(\hat y, y)/\abs{\hat y}$. Precision uses the candidate's counts in the denominator, and the F-score
combines the two <Cite id="lin2004" />. All lie in $[0, 1]$; higher is better.
</Definition>

## Recall first

ROUGE was built for summarisation, where the question is how much of the content of a human summary the system
summary covers. That is recall: the denominator counts the reference's n-grams. It is the mirror image of
<NoteLink to="bleu">BLEU</NoteLink>, which divides by the candidate's n-grams. A recall-only score rewards long
candidates, which cover more by saying more, so modern implementations usually report the F1 of recall and precision,
or truncate candidates to a fixed length. With several references, ROUGE-N sums the matches and the reference counts
over all of them <Cite id="lin2004" />.

## The longest common subsequence

A subsequence keeps order but not adjacency: "the gunman" is a subsequence of "the armed gunman". ROUGE-L scores the
longest sequence of words that appears in both texts in the same order. This rewards the right order without
demanding contiguous n-grams, and it needs no choice of $n$. With $l = \mathrm{LCS}(\hat y, y)$,

$$
R_L = \frac{l}{\abs{y}}, \qquad P_L = \frac{l}{\abs{\hat y}}, \qquad F_L = \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\, R_L P_L}{R_L + \beta^2 P_L}.
$$

The LCS is computed by dynamic programming in $O(\abs{y}\,\abs{\hat y})$ time: the LCS of two prefixes is one more
than that of the shorter prefixes when their last words match, and otherwise the larger of the two ways of dropping a
last word. For multi-sentence summaries, ROUGE-Lsum takes the union of the LCS matches of each reference sentence
against the whole candidate.

Lin also defined ROUGE-W, which weights consecutive matches more, and ROUGE-S, which counts skip-bigrams, pairs of
words in order with any gap. ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2 and ROUGE-L are the ones reported in practice.

## Worked example

Reference "the cat is on the mat", candidate "the cat sat on the mat".

- **ROUGE-1.** Five of the reference's six unigrams are matched and five of the candidate's six, so $R = P = F =
  5/6 = 0.833$.
- **ROUGE-2.** Three of five bigrams match in each direction (the cat, on the, the mat): $0.6$.
- **ROUGE-L.** The LCS is "the cat on the mat", five words, so $R_L = P_L = F_L = 5/6$.

Word order changes ROUGE-L but not ROUGE-1. For the reference "police killed the gunman" and the candidate "the
gunman killed police", every unigram matches, so ROUGE-1 is 1, but the LCS has only two words, "the gunman" or
"killed police", so ROUGE-L is $0.5$, and only one bigram, "the gunman", matches, so ROUGE-2 is $1/3$.

## Pitfalls

- **Lexical overlap, not meaning.** A faithful paraphrase scores low and a fluent but wrong summary that reuses the
  source's words scores high. ROUGE says nothing about factual consistency.
- **Implementation details change the numbers.** Stemming, stop-word removal, tokenisation, whether ROUGE-L or
  ROUGE-Lsum is used, and recall versus F1 all vary between toolkits. State them.
