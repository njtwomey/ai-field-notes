<Definition>
Align a hypothesis transcript to a reference of $N$ words with the fewest edits, and count $H$ matched words
(hits), $S$ substitutions, $D$ deletions and $I$ insertions, so that $N = H + S + D$. The word error rate is

$$
\mathrm{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}.
$$

The character <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink> (CER) is the same computation over characters. Both are 0 for a perfect transcript and
have no upper bound.
</Definition>

## The alignment

$S + D + I$ is the Levenshtein edit distance between the two word sequences: the fewest single-word edits that turn
one into the other. It is computed by dynamic programming. With $d_{i,j}$ the distance between the first $i$
reference words and the first $j$ hypothesis words, $d_{i,0} = i$, $d_{0,j} = j$ and

$$
d_{i,j} = \min\paren{d_{i-1,j} + 1,\ \ d_{i,j-1} + 1,\ \ d_{i-1,j-1} + [r_i \ne h_j]},
$$

whose three cases are a deletion, an insertion, and a match or substitution. Tracing back the minimising choices
recovers the alignment and hence $H$, $S$, $D$ and $I$. Several alignments can tie; the error count is the same for
all of them, but the split between substitutions and insertion–deletion pairs can differ.

## Why WER is not bounded

The denominator is the reference length, but insertions are not limited by it. The reference "yes" and the
hypothesis "no no no" need one substitution and two insertions, so $\mathrm{WER} = 3$. WER is also asymmetric: the
reference and hypothesis cannot be swapped. It is an error rate, not a percentage of words wrong, and it is not an
accuracy; $1 - \mathrm{WER}$ can be negative.

## Bounded relatives

Morris, Maier and Green proposed measures that lie in $[0, 1]$ <Cite id="morris2004" />:

- **Match error rate**, the fraction of aligned positions that are errors:
  $\mathrm{MER} = \dfrac{S + D + I}{H + S + D + I}$.
- **Word information preserved**, the product of the fraction of reference words recovered and the fraction of
  hypothesis words that are correct: $\mathrm{WIP} = \dfrac{H}{N} \cdot \dfrac{H}{P}$, with $P = H + S + I$ the
  hypothesis length.
- **Word information lost**, $\mathrm{WIL} = 1 - \mathrm{WIP}$.

WIP penalises a hypothesis both for missing words and for adding wrong ones, like the product of recall and
precision. TorchMetrics implements all of WER, CER, MER, WIL and WIP with these definitions <Cite id="detlefsen2022" />.

## Worked example

Reference "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" ($N = 9$), hypothesis "the quick brown fox jumped over a
lazy dog today" ($P = 10$). The minimum-edit alignment has $H = 7$ hits, $S = 2$ substitutions (jumps → jumped, the →
a), $D = 0$ and $I = 1$ (today). Then

$$
\mathrm{WER} = \tfrac{3}{9} = 0.333, \quad \mathrm{MER} = \tfrac{3}{10} = 0.3, \quad
\mathrm{WIP} = \tfrac{7}{9} \cdot \tfrac{7}{10} = 0.544, \quad \mathrm{WIL} = 0.456.
$$

At the character level, "jumps" → "jumped" is one substitution and one insertion, so it costs 2 characters of 5
rather than a whole word: CER gives partial credit for near misses.

## Pitfalls

- **Normalise before scoring.** Case, punctuation, numerals ("twenty" or "20") and contractions change WER by several
  points. Apply the same normaliser to both texts and report it.
- **CER for languages without spaces.** WER is undefined for languages written without word boundaries, such as
  Chinese and Japanese; CER is used instead.
- **Average over the corpus, not over sentences.** Corpus WER is total edits divided by total reference words. The
  mean of per-sentence WERs weights short sentences as heavily as long ones.
- **All errors cost the same.** Dropping "not" and dropping "the" each cost one edit.
