<Definition>
A scoring classifier predicts positive when its score $s(\xvec)$ exceeds a threshold $t$. Its receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve is the set of points $\paren{\text{FPR}(t), \text{TPR}(t)}$ as $t$ runs from $+\infty$ to
$-\infty$. The area under the curve is

$$
\text{AUROC} = \int_0^1 \text{TPR}\;d\,\text{FPR} = P\paren{s(X^+) > s(X^-)},
$$

the probability that a randomly drawn positive scores higher than a randomly drawn negative, counting ties as half
<Cite id="fawcett2006" />.
</Definition>

## The curve

At a very high threshold nothing is predicted positive, the point $(0, 0)$. As the threshold falls, positives and
negatives cross it and the point moves up (a positive crosses) or right (a negative crosses), until at a very low
threshold everything is positive, the point $(1, 1)$. A classifier whose scores carry no information moves up and right
in proportion, along the diagonal $\text{TPR} = \text{FPR}$. A perfect classifier passes through $(0, 1)$.

Both axes are rates within a true class, so the curve does not depend on the prevalence. It depends only on the two
class-conditional score distributions, and it is unchanged by any strictly increasing transformation of the scores:
only their order matters.

## AUROC as a probability

For an empirical curve with $n_+$ positives and $n_-$ negatives, sort the cases by score and walk down the list. Each
time a negative is passed, the curve moves right by $1/n_-$ at a height equal to the fraction of positives already
passed, which is the fraction of positives that outscore that negative. The area is the average of those heights:

$$
\text{AUROC} = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-}\sum_{i \in +}\sum_{j \in -}\paren{\indicator[s_i > s_j] + \tfrac12\indicator[s_i = s_j]}.
$$

The double sum counts the pairs in which the positive outscores the negative. It is the Mann–Whitney $U$ statistic
divided by $n_+ n_-$ <Cite id="mann1947" />, so AUROC is the probability that the classifier orders a random
positive–negative pair correctly <Cite id="hanley1982" />. In medical statistics it is called the $c$-statistic.

For the binormal model with negatives $\sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and positives $\sim \Gauss(d, 1)$, the difference of a
positive and a negative score is $\Gauss(d, 2)$, so $\text{AUROC} = \Phi(d/\sqrt 2)$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal
cdf. At $d = 1.5$ it is $\Phi(1.061) = 0.856$.

## Worked example

Four positives score 0.9, 0.8, 0.6 and 0.55; five negatives score 0.7, 0.5, 0.4, 0.3 and 0.2. Of the 20
positive–negative pairs, the positives win 18: the two top positives beat all five negatives, and 0.6 and 0.55 beat all
but 0.7. So $\text{AUROC} = 18/20 = 0.90$. Walking down the sorted scores traces the curve through $(0, 0.25)$,
$(0, 0.5)$, $(0.2, 0.5)$, $(0.2, 1)$ and on to $(1, 1)$, whose area is $0.2 \times 0.5 + 0.8 \times 1 = 0.90$.

## Operating points and costs

A point on the curve is a classifier with a threshold. Which point is best depends on the costs of the two errors and
the prevalence. With prevalence $\pi$ and costs $c_{\text{FP}}$ and $c_{\text{FN}}$ of a false positive and a <Gloss name="fn">false
negative</Gloss>, expected cost is constant along straight lines in ROC space whose slope is
$(c_{\text{FP}}\,(1 - \pi))/(c_{\text{FN}}\,\pi)$, and the best threshold is where such an iso-cost line touches the
curve <Cite id="flach2003" />. Only points on the convex hull of the curve can be optimal for some costs. See
<NoteLink to="operating-points-and-equal-error-rate">operating points and the equal error rate</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive learning</NoteLink>.

## Multiclass AUROC

For $K$ classes, compute one-against-rest AUROCs and average them, or average the pairwise AUROCs over all pairs of
classes (the Hand–Till measure). The first depends on class proportions through the "rest" group; the second does not.

## Pitfalls

- **AUROC can look good when precision is poor.** With rare positives, a small <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> still means many
  <Gloss name="fp">false positives</Gloss>. The <NoteLink to="precision-recall-curve-and-average-precision">precision-recall curve</NoteLink>
  shows this; the ROC curve does not.
- **AUROC summarises all thresholds equally,** including ones no one would use. Partial AUROC over a range of low
  false-positive rates, or a metric at the operating threshold, is often more relevant.
- **Crossing curves.** Two classifiers with equal AUROC can be better in different regions. Compare curves, not only
  areas; Hand's H-measure is a coherent alternative when costs are uncertain <Cite id="hand2009" />.
- **Comparing AUROCs on the same data** needs a paired method such as DeLong's <Cite id="delong1988" /> (see
  <NoteLink to="metric-confidence-intervals">confidence intervals for metrics</NoteLink>).
