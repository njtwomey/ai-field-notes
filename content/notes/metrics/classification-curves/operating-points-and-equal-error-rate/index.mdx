<Definition>
An operating point is a choice of threshold $t$ for a scoring classifier, giving one point on its <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">ROC curve</NoteLink> and one
<NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink>. Common rules for choosing it are: fix one rate and read off the other (for example the recall at a
precision of 0.9), balance the two <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rates</NoteLink> (the equal error rate, where $\text{FPR} = \text{FNR}$), or minimise the
expected cost of errors <Cite id="fawcett2006" />.
</Definition>

## Rates at a fixed constraint

Many applications have a hard constraint on one error. A screening test may need a <Gloss name="tpr">sensitivity</Gloss> of at least 0.95; a
fraud system may tolerate at most 1% false alarms; a search engine may need precision above 0.9. The metric is then
the other rate at the lowest threshold that meets the constraint: specificity at 95% sensitivity, TPR at 1% <Gloss name="fpr" />,
recall at 90% precision. These read one point off the curve, the point the system will actually run at, and ignore the
rest.

## The equal error rate

The equal error rate is the error rate at the threshold where the false-positive rate equals the <Gloss name="fn">false-negative</Gloss> rate.
On the ROC curve it is where the curve crosses the anti-diagonal $\text{TPR} = 1 - \text{FPR}$. It is standard in
biometric verification and speaker recognition, where one number must summarise a system whose threshold is set later.

For the binormal model with negatives $\sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and positives $\sim \Gauss(d, 1)$, the false-positive rate at
threshold $t$ is $1 - \Phi(t)$ and the false-negative rate is $\Phi(t - d)$. They are equal when $t = d - t$, so at
$t = d/2$, and

$$
\text{EER} = \Phi(-d/2).
$$

At $d = 1.5$ the EER is $\Phi(-0.75) = 0.227$.

## Youden's index

Youden's index picks the threshold that maximises $J = \text{TPR} - \text{FPR}$, the vertical distance above the chance
diagonal <Cite id="youden1950" />. At the maximum, $d\,\text{TPR}/d\,\text{FPR} = 1$: the curve's slope is 1, which is
where the two class densities, unweighted by prevalence, are equal. In the equal-variance binormal model this is again
$t = d/2$, with $J = 2\Phi(d/2) - 1 = 0.547$ at $d = 1.5$. Maximising $J$ is the same as minimising the expected cost
when both errors cost the same and the classes are equally likely, which is rarely the real situation.

## Minimum expected cost

With a cost $c_{\text{FP}}$ per <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss>, $c_{\text{FN}}$ per false negative and prevalence $\pi$, the expected
cost per case at a point on the ROC curve is

$$
\pi\, c_{\text{FN}}\paren{1 - \text{TPR}} + (1 - \pi)\, c_{\text{FP}}\,\text{FPR}.
$$

It is constant along straight lines of slope $\paren{(1 - \pi)\,c_{\text{FP}}}/\paren{\pi\,c_{\text{FN}}}$, and the best
operating point is where such a line touches the ROC curve's convex hull <Cite id="flach2003" />. With calibrated
probabilities the same point is a threshold on the probability itself (see
<NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive learning</NoteLink>). Equal error rate and Youden's index are the
special cases of particular cost and prevalence assumptions.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing the threshold on the test set** makes the reported rates optimistic. Choose it on validation data and
  report the test-set rates at that fixed threshold.
- **Fixed-rate metrics are unstable with few cases.** At 1% FPR with 500 negatives, the constraint is set by five cases.
- **The EER is a summary, not an operating point** anyone necessarily uses. It is useful for comparing systems, not for
  deploying one.
