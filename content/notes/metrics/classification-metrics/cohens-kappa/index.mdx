<Definition>
For two raters who each assign one of $K$ categories to the same $n$ items, let $p_o$ be the observed proportion of
items on which they agree and $p_e$ the proportion expected if they rated independently with their observed marginal
frequencies. Then

$$
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}.
$$

$\kappa = 1$ for perfect agreement and $\kappa = 0$ for agreement at the chance level <Cite id="cohen1960" />.
</Definition>

## Correcting agreement for chance

With the agreement table $\Cmat$, whose entry $C_{jk}$ counts items put in category $j$ by the first rater and $k$ by
the second, the observed agreement is the diagonal share $p_o = \frac1n\sum_k C_{kk}$. If the raters chose
independently, each with their own category frequencies $r_k = \frac1n\sum_j C_{kj}$ and $c_k = \frac1n\sum_j C_{jk}$,
they would agree on category $k$ with probability $r_k c_k$, so

$$
p_e = \sum_k r_k c_k.
$$

$\kappa$ is the observed agreement beyond chance, $p_o - p_e$, as a fraction of the largest possible agreement beyond
chance, $1 - p_e$. Negative values mean agreement below chance.

For a classifier, the two raters are the true labels and the predictions, and $p_o$ is the accuracy. Kappa then
discounts the accuracy that the classifier would get by predicting with the right marginal frequencies but no
information.

## Worked examples

**Classifier.** On the screening data (TP = 40, FN = 10, FP = 90, TN = 860), $p_o = 0.900$. The truth is positive for
5% of cases and the classifier predicts positive for 13%, so

$$
p_e = 0.05 \times 0.13 + 0.95 \times 0.87 = 0.833, \qquad \kappa = \frac{0.900 - 0.833}{1 - 0.833} = 0.401.
$$

Most of the 90% accuracy is what two uninformed raters with these marginals would achieve.

**Two raters, ordinal grades.** Two clinicians grade 70 cases as mild, moderate or severe:

| | B: mild | B: moderate | B: severe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A: mild** | 20 | 5 | 1 |
| **A: moderate** | 4 | 15 | 5 |
| **A: severe** | 1 | 4 | 15 |

They agree on $50/70 = 0.714$ of cases; chance agreement is $p_e = 0.336$, so $\kappa = 0.570$.

## Weighted kappa

On an ordinal scale, a disagreement between mild and moderate is smaller than one between mild and severe. Weighted
kappa assigns each pair of categories a disagreement weight $w_{jk}$, zero on the diagonal, and compares the observed
and chance-expected weighted disagreement <Cite id="cohen1968" />:

$$
\kappa_w = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j,k} w_{jk}\,C_{jk}/n}{\sum_{j,k} w_{jk}\, r_j c_k}.
$$

Linear weights $w_{jk} = \abs{j - k}$ and quadratic weights $w_{jk} = (j - k)^2$ are the usual choices; with 0/1
weights, $\kappa_w$ reduces to $\kappa$. For the clinicians' table, linear weights give $0.642$ and quadratic weights
$0.715$: most disagreements are between neighbouring grades. Quadratic weighted kappa is close to the intraclass
correlation of the grades treated as numbers, and is a common metric for ordinal prediction tasks.

## Interpreting the value

Landis and Koch proposed verbal bands: 0.41–0.60 moderate, 0.61–0.80 substantial, above 0.80 almost perfect
<Cite id="landis1977" />. They are conventions, not derived thresholds, and kappa values from different tasks are not
comparable.

## Pitfalls

- **High agreement, low kappa.** When one category dominates, $p_e$ is large and kappa is small even with high agreement.
  Two raters who agree on 90 of 100 items, with 90% of their ratings in one category, have $p_e = 0.82$ and
  $\kappa = 0.44$ <Cite id="feinstein1990" />. The kappa is not wrong: most of their agreement is what the base rate
  gives for free. But it is not comparable to a kappa from balanced data.
- **Kappa depends on the marginals.** Two pairs of raters with the same underlying accuracy can have different kappas if
  their category frequencies differ.
- **Two raters only.** For many raters, or different raters per item, use <NoteLink to="fleiss-kappa">Fleiss'
  kappa</NoteLink>.
- **Versus MCC.** For binary classification both correct for chance. MCC is a correlation and symmetric in the two
  directions of prediction; kappa measures agreement. They coincide when the predicted and true marginals are equal.
