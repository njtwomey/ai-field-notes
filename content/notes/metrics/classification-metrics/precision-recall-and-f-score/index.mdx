<Definition>
For a positive class with $\text{TP}$ true positives, $\text{FP}$ false positives and $\text{FN}$ false negatives,

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \qquad
\text{recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}, \qquad
F_\beta = \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\beta^2\,\text{precision} + \text{recall}}.
$$

$F_1$ is the harmonic mean of precision and recall; $\beta > 1$ weights recall more, $\beta < 1$ precision more
<Cite id="vanrijsbergen1979" />.
</Definition>

## Two questions about the positive class

Precision and recall come from information retrieval, where the positives are the relevant documents. Recall asks
what fraction of the relevant documents were retrieved. Precision asks what fraction of the retrieved documents are
relevant. Recall conditions on the truth and is the true-positive rate; precision conditions on the prediction and is
the positive predictive value.

Neither involves the true negatives. In retrieval that is deliberate: the irrelevant documents that were correctly not
returned are uncountable and uninteresting. In classification it means precision, recall and $F$ are blind to how well
the negative class is handled, and they change when the labels are swapped.

Precision depends on prevalence and recall does not. With prevalence $\pi$, true-positive rate $\text{TPR}$ and
false-positive rate $\text{FPR}$,

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\pi\,\text{TPR}}{\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1 - \pi)\,\text{FPR}}.
$$

This is Bayes' theorem: precision is the posterior probability of the positive class given a positive prediction, as in
<NoteLink to="medical-diagnosis-base-rate">medical diagnosis and the base rate</NoteLink>. At fixed TPR and FPR,
precision falls as positives become rarer.

## Why the harmonic mean

Combining precision $p$ and recall $r$ into one number, the harmonic mean
$F_1 = 2pr/(p + r) = \paren{\tfrac12(1/p + 1/r)}^{-1}$ is dominated by the smaller of the two. The arithmetic mean of
$p = 1$ and $r = 0.01$ is about 0.5; $F_1$ is about 0.02. A classifier cannot score well by being excellent on one and
useless on the other.

$F_\beta$ comes from van Rijsbergen's effectiveness measure $E = 1 - \paren{\alpha/p + (1 - \alpha)/r}^{-1}$, with
$\alpha = 1/(1 + \beta^2)$. Writing $F_\beta = 1 - E$ and substituting,

$$
F_\beta = \frac{1}{\dfrac{1}{1 + \beta^2}\cdot\dfrac1p + \dfrac{\beta^2}{1 + \beta^2}\cdot\dfrac1r}
= \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\,pr}{\beta^2 p + r}.
$$

The parameter $\beta$ is the ratio of recall to precision at which the user is indifferent to a small trade between
them. Substituting the counts gives a form without precision and recall:

$$
F_\beta = \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\,\text{TP}}{(1 + \beta^2)\,\text{TP} + \beta^2\,\text{FN} + \text{FP}}, \qquad
F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{TP}}{2\,\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}}.
$$

$F_1$ is also a monotone function of the <NoteLink to="hamming-jaccard-and-exact-match">Jaccard index</NoteLink>
$J = \text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN})$: $F_1 = 2J/(1 + J)$.

## Worked example

On the screening data (TP = 40, FN = 10, FP = 90):

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{40}{130} = 0.308, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{40}{50} = 0.800, \qquad
F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot 40}{80 + 90 + 10} = 0.444.
$$

$F_2 = 0.606$ weights the high recall more; $F_{0.5} = 0.351$ weights the low precision more. The Jaccard index is
$40/140 = 0.286$, and $2(0.286)/(1.286) = 0.444$ recovers $F_1$.

## Pitfalls

- **Precision is undefined with no positive predictions** ($\text{TP} + \text{FP} = 0$), and $F$ follows. Libraries
  substitute 0 or 1, or warn; the choice changes averages across folds or classes.
- **$F_1$ ignores true negatives.** Two classifiers with very different false-positive rates can have the same $F_1$
  when the negative class is large. The <NoteLink to="matthews-correlation-coefficient">Matthews correlation
  coefficient</NoteLink> uses all four cells <Cite id="chicco2020" />.
- **The F-score's chance level depends on prevalence.** A classifier that predicts every case positive has recall 1 and
  precision $\pi$, so $F_1 = 2\pi/(1 + \pi)$. Compare against that baseline, not against 0.
- **F-scores are not linear in the threshold's consequences.** Averaging $F_1$ across folds or classes, or
  interpolating it between points on a curve, is not the same as computing it from pooled counts. Flach and Kull's
  precision-recall-gain framework rescales precision and recall so that such averages behave linearly
  <Cite id="flach2015" />.
