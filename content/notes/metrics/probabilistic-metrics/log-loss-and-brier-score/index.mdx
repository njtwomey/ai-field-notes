<Definition>
For $n$ cases, true classes $y_i \in \set{1, \dots, K}$ and predicted probability vectors $\qvec_i$, with one-hot
outcome vectors $\evec_{y_i}$:

$$
\text{log loss} = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \log q_{i, y_i}, \qquad
\text{Brier score} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \norm{\qvec_i - \evec_{y_i}}^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K \paren{q_{ik} - \indicator[y_i = k]}^2.
$$

Both are strictly proper scoring rules: their <NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink> is minimised only by reporting the true distribution
<Cite id="gneiting2007" />.
</Definition>

## Scoring probabilities, not decisions

Threshold metrics such as accuracy discard the probability a model reports: 0.51 and 0.99 give the same decision. A
scoring rule instead charges each case according to the probability given to what happened. Log loss is the mean
negative log-likelihood of the observed labels under the model, the same quantity as the cross-entropy between the
empirical and predicted distributions (see <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy and
perplexity</NoteLink>). The Brier score is the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> of the probability vector <Cite id="brier1950" />.

For two classes, many libraries report the Brier score as $\frac1n\sum_i (p_i - y_i)^2$ with $p_i$ the probability of the
positive class; Brier's original definition sums over both classes and is exactly twice that.

## Properness

A scoring rule $S(\qvec, y)$ is proper if, when outcomes are drawn from a distribution $\pvec$, the expected score
$\expect_{y \sim \pvec} S(\qvec, y)$ is minimised by $\qvec = \pvec$, and strictly proper if $\pvec$ is the only
minimiser. A strictly proper rule cannot be gamed: reporting anything but your actual beliefs does worse on average.

For log loss, the expected score minus its value at $\qvec = \pvec$ is

$$
-\sum_k p_k \log q_k + \sum_k p_k \log p_k = \KL(\pvec \,\Vert\, \qvec) \ge 0,
$$

with equality only at $\qvec = \pvec$ (see <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>). For the
Brier score, expanding $\expect_{y}\norm{\qvec - \evec_y}^2$ with $\expect[\evec_y] = \pvec$ gives

$$
\expect_y \norm{\qvec - \evec_y}^2 = \norm{\qvec - \pvec}^2 + \expect_y\norm{\pvec - \evec_y}^2,
$$

a squared distance from the truth plus a term that does not depend on $\qvec$. Again the minimum is at $\qvec = \pvec$.

## Unbounded against bounded

The two rules punish confident mistakes very differently. Giving probability $q$ to the outcome that occurs costs
$-\ln q$ in log loss and, for two classes, $(1 - q)^2$ in the per-class Brier score:

| $q$ | log loss | Brier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.9 | 0.105 | 0.010 |
| 0.1 | 2.303 | 0.810 |
| 0.01 | 4.605 | 0.980 |

Log loss is unbounded: a single prediction of probability near 0 for something that happens can dominate the mean.
The Brier score is bounded by 1 per case for two classes, so it is more robust to occasional confident errors, and
less sensitive to the tails of the predicted distribution.

## Calibration and refinement

Murphy decomposed the Brier score of binned binary forecasts into three parts <Cite id="murphy1973" />. Group the
cases into bins $b$ of $n_b$ cases with a common forecast $f_b$, observed frequency $\bar o_b$ and overall base rate
$\bar o$:

$$
\text{Brier} = \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_b n_b\paren{f_b - \bar o_b}^2}_{\text{reliability}}
- \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_b n_b\paren{\bar o_b - \bar o}^2}_{\text{resolution}}
+ \underbrace{\bar o(1 - \bar o)}_{\text{uncertainty}}.
$$

Reliability is a calibration error (see <NoteLink to="calibration-error">calibration error</NoteLink>); resolution
rewards forecasts that separate cases with different outcome rates; uncertainty is the Brier score of always forecasting
the base rate. A model can be perfectly calibrated and uninformative (always forecasting $\bar o$ has perfect reliability and zero
resolution), which is why calibration alone is not a quality measure.

## Worked example

Ten binary predictions $0.10, 0.15, 0.25, 0.30, 0.45, 0.55, 0.70, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95$ with outcomes
$0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1$ have log loss 0.466 and Brier score 0.157 (binary form). A model that always predicts the
base rate 0.5 scores $\ln 2 = 0.693$ and $0.25$: the baselines to compare against.

## Pitfalls

- **Clip probabilities** when computing log loss. A predicted 0 for an observed class gives infinite loss; libraries
  clip to a small $\epsilon$, which changes the value.
- **Compare against the base rate,** not against 0. The entropy of the class distribution is the log loss of the
  best uninformed model.
- **Proper scores reward calibration and refinement together.** Two models with the same log loss can differ in both;
  inspect a reliability diagram as well.
