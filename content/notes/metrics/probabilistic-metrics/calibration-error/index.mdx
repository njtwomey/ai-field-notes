<Definition>
A binary classifier reporting probability $p(\xvec)$ for the positive class is calibrated if
$P\paren{Y = 1 \mid p(\xvec) = p} = p$ for every $p$. Its expected calibration error is the mean absolute gap
$\expect\abs{P(Y = 1 \mid p(\xvec)) - p(\xvec)}$, estimated by binning the predictions into $M$ bins $B_m$:

$$
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{\abs{B_m}}{n}\,\abs{\bar y(B_m) - \bar p(B_m)},
$$

where $\bar p(B_m)$ is the mean prediction and $\bar y(B_m)$ the fraction of positives in bin $m$
<Cite id="naeini2015" />.
</Definition>

## What calibration means

Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities can be taken at face value. Of the days a calibrated forecaster says
"70% chance of rain", it rains on 70%. Calibration is separate from discrimination: a model that always predicts the
base rate is perfectly calibrated and useless, and a model with a perfect ranking can be badly calibrated. Decisions
based on probabilities, such as the expected-cost thresholds of <NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive
learning</NoteLink>, are only optimal if the probabilities are calibrated.

A reliability diagram plots $\bar y(B_m)$ against $\bar p(B_m)$ for each bin <Cite id="degroot1983" />. A calibrated
model lies on the diagonal. An overconfident model, whose probabilities are too close to 0 and 1, gives a curve flatter
than the diagonal; an underconfident one gives a steeper curve.

## Worked example

Ten binary predictions and outcomes, in five equal-width bins:

| bin | predictions | mean $\bar p$ | outcomes | frequency $\bar y$ | gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $[0, 0.2)$ | 0.10, 0.15 | 0.125 | 0, 0 | 0.0 | 0.125 |
| $[0.2, 0.4)$ | 0.25, 0.30 | 0.275 | 0, 1 | 0.5 | 0.225 |
| $[0.4, 0.6)$ | 0.45, 0.55 | 0.500 | 0, 1 | 0.5 | 0.000 |
| $[0.6, 0.8)$ | 0.70, 0.75 | 0.725 | 0, 1 | 0.5 | 0.225 |
| $[0.8, 1]$ | 0.85, 0.95 | 0.900 | 1, 1 | 1.0 | 0.100 |

Every bin holds two of the ten cases, so $\text{ECE} = 0.2 \times (0.125 + 0.225 + 0 + 0.225 + 0.1) = 0.135$. The
maximum calibration error, the largest gap, is 0.225.

## Variants

- **Maximum calibration error (MCE)** takes the largest bin gap instead of the weighted mean <Cite id="naeini2015" />.
  It matters where any miscalibrated region is unacceptable.
- **Confidence ECE** for multiclass models bins only the top predicted probability and compares it with accuracy. It was
  popularised by Guo et al., who showed that modern neural networks are overconfident and that
  <NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">temperature scaling</NoteLink> largely fixes it <Cite id="guo2017" />.
- **Classwise ECE** applies the binary ECE to each class's predicted probability and averages over classes. It detects
  miscalibration in the probabilities of classes other than the predicted one, which confidence ECE ignores
  <Cite id="kull2019" />.
- **Adaptive binning** uses equal-mass bins, each holding the same number of predictions, so bins are never nearly
  empty <Cite id="nixon2019" />.

## Pitfalls

- **ECE depends on the binning.** Different numbers of bins, and equal-width against equal-mass bins, give different
  values on the same predictions. Report the scheme.
- **ECE is biased upwards with finite data.** Even a perfectly calibrated model has a positive ECE from sampling noise,
  larger with more bins and fewer cases. Compare against that noise level, for example by resampling labels from the
  predicted probabilities.
- **ECE is not a proper scoring rule.** A model can lower its ECE by predicting the base rate for everything. Report a
  proper score such as <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink> or the Brier score alongside it
  (see <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">log loss and Brier score</NoteLink>).
