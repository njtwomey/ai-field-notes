<Definition>
For true values $y_i$ with mean $\bar y$ and predictions $\hat y_i$, the coefficient of determination is

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\operatorname{SSE}}{\operatorname{SST}}
= 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}.
$$

The explained variance score replaces the sum of squared errors by the variance of the errors,
$\operatorname{EV} = 1 - \var(y - \hat y)/\var(y)$ <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Reading R²

$R^2$ compares the model with the simplest possible baseline: predicting $\bar y$ for every case. That baseline has
$\operatorname{SSE} = \operatorname{SST}$, so it scores exactly 0. Perfect predictions score 1. Dividing numerator and
denominator by $n$ gives $R^2 = 1 - \operatorname{MSE}/\var(y)$, with the population variance: $R^2$ is mean squared
error as a fraction of the target's variance, subtracted from 1.

**R² can be negative.** Nothing bounds SSE by SST. A model whose squared error exceeds that of the mean, for example
one evaluated on data unlike its training data, has $R^2 < 0$. With $y = (3, -0.5, 2, 7)$ and the predictions
$(7, 2, -0.5, 3)$, which put the right values in the wrong places, $R^2 = -0.52$.

**When R² equals a squared correlation.** For ordinary least squares with an intercept, evaluated on its own training
data, the residuals $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ are orthogonal to the fitted values and to the constant. Expanding
$y_i - \bar y = (\hat y_i - \bar y) + e_i$ and squaring, the cross term $\sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y) e_i$ vanishes, so

$$
\operatorname{SST} = \sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 + \operatorname{SSE}.
$$

$R^2$ is then the share of the total sum of squares carried by the fitted values, and it equals the squared Pearson
correlation between $y$ and $\hat y$. Out of sample, without an intercept, or for any other model, the cross term does
not vanish, and $R^2$ and $r^2$ differ. See <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">Pearson correlation</NoteLink>.

## Explained variance and bias

The explained variance score ignores a constant offset. Writing $e = y - \hat y$ with mean $\bar e$, the mean squared
error is $\var(e) + \bar e^2$, so

$$
\operatorname{EV} - R^2 = \frac{\bar e^2}{\var(y)} \ge 0.
$$

The two agree exactly when the predictions are unbiased on average. A model that is right up to a constant shift keeps
a high explained variance while its $R^2$ falls.

## Worked example

With $y = (3, -0.5, 2, 7)$ and $\hat y = (2.5, 0, 2, 8)$: $\bar y = 2.875$, $\operatorname{SST} = 29.19$ and
$\operatorname{SSE} = 1.5$, so $R^2 = 1 - 1.5/29.19 = 0.949$.

The errors $e = (0.5, -0.5, 0, -1)$ have mean $\bar e = -0.25$. With $\var(y) = 29.19/4 = 7.297$, the gap is
$0.0625/7.297 = 0.0086$, so $\operatorname{EV} = 0.957$.

Adding 1 to every prediction leaves the explained variance at 0.957, because it only shifts the errors, but drops
$R^2$ to 0.743. Predicting $\bar y$ everywhere gives $R^2 = 0$. These values match scikit-learn's `r2_score` and
`explained_variance_score` <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Adjusted R²

On training data, adding a predictor to a least-squares model can never lower $R^2$. Adjusted $R^2$ charges for each
of the $p$ predictors:

$$
R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}.
$$

It replaces SSE and SST by their unbiased variance estimates, with $n - p - 1$ and $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. It is a
crude complexity penalty; held-out $R^2$ or cross-validation measures generalisation directly.

## Pitfalls

- **R² depends on the spread of the targets.** The same errors give a higher $R^2$ on data with a wider range of $y$,
  because SST grows. $R^2$ cannot compare models across datasets.
- **A high R² is not a good fit.** A curved relationship fitted by a line can have a high $R^2$ with systematic
  residuals. Plot residuals against predictions.
- **Multi-output averaging.** For several targets, the average of per-target $R^2$ values and an average weighted by
  each target's variance can differ widely when one target dominates the variance.
- **R² is not agreement.** $R^2$ against a baseline measures predictive value, not whether predictions and truth
  coincide; see <NoteLink to="concordance-correlation-coefficient">concordance correlation</NoteLink>.
