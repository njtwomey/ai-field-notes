<Definition>
For true values $y_1, \dots, y_n$ and predictions $\hat y_1, \dots, \hat y_n$, with errors $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$,

$$
\operatorname{MSE} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2, \qquad
\operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt{\operatorname{MSE}}, \qquad
\operatorname{MAE} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \abs{e_i}.
$$

All three are 0 for perfect predictions and grow without bound. MSE is in squared units of $y$; RMSE and MAE are in
the units of $y$ <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## What each one rewards

The two scores reward different point predictions. For a random target $Y$ and a constant prediction $c$:

**MSE is minimised by the mean.** $\expect[(Y - c)^2] = \var(Y) + (\expect[Y] - c)^2$, obtained by adding and
subtracting $\expect[Y]$ inside the square; the cross term has zero expectation. The second term is the only one that
depends on $c$, and it vanishes at $c = \expect[Y]$. Conditioning on the inputs, the prediction that minimises
expected squared error is the conditional mean $\expect[Y \mid \xvec]$.

**MAE is minimised by the median.** $\frac{d}{dc}\expect\abs{Y - c} = P(Y < c) - P(Y > c)$ wherever it exists, since
moving $c$ up by a small amount increases the error of every observation below $c$ and decreases it for every
observation above. The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is zero, or changes sign, where half the probability lies on each side: at a
median. Conditioning on the inputs, MAE rewards the conditional median.

The two differ exactly when the conditional distribution is skewed. For a right-skewed target such as income or
waiting time, a model trained to minimise MSE predicts higher values than one trained to minimise MAE, and each looks
worse under the other's score.

The same split holds for the losses used in training: MSE is the Gaussian <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative log-likelihood</NoteLink> up to constants,
and MAE is the Laplace negative log-likelihood. The <NoteLink to="linear-regression">least-squares</NoteLink> fit is
the MSE-optimal linear predictor.

## Sensitivity to large errors

Squaring makes MSE dominated by the largest errors. An error of 10 adds 100 to the sum; ten errors of 1 add 10.
MAE counts every unit of error equally. So MSE and RMSE respond strongly to a single outlying prediction, and MAE
responds only in proportion to its size.

The two are ordered. By Jensen's inequality, or the power-mean inequality,
$\operatorname{MAE} = \frac1n\sum \abs{e_i} \le \paren{\frac1n\sum e_i^2}^{1/2} = \operatorname{RMSE}$, with equality
only when every $\abs{e_i}$ is the same. At the other extreme, when a single error carries all the loss,
$\operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt n\,\operatorname{MAE}$. The ratio RMSE / MAE, between 1 and $\sqrt n$, measures how
concentrated the errors are.

MSE also splits into bias and variance. Writing $\bar e$ for the mean error,
$\operatorname{MSE} = \bar e^2 + \frac1n\sum (e_i - \bar e)^2$: a systematic offset and the spread around it.

## Worked example

With $y = (3, -0.5, 2, 7)$ and $\hat y = (2.5, 0, 2, 8)$, the errors are $e = (0.5, -0.5, 0, -1)$.

- $\operatorname{MSE} = (0.25 + 0.25 + 0 + 1)/4 = 0.375$ and $\operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt{0.375} = 0.612$.
- $\operatorname{MAE} = (0.5 + 0.5 + 0 + 1)/4 = 0.5$.
- RMSE / MAE = 1.22: one error of 1 is larger than the rest.
- The median absolute error is 0.5 and the maximum error is 1.

These match scikit-learn's `mean_squared_error`, `mean_absolute_error`, `median_absolute_error` and `max_error`
<Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Related scores

- **Median absolute error** takes the median of $\abs{e_i}$ instead of the mean, so up to half the predictions can be
  arbitrarily wrong without changing it.
- **Maximum error** is the worst single miss, for settings with a hard tolerance.
- **Normalised RMSE** divides RMSE by the range, mean or standard deviation of $y$ so that datasets on different
  scales can be compared. Dividing by the standard deviation gives $\sqrt{1 - R^2}$; see
  <NoteLink to="r-squared-and-explained-variance">R²</NoteLink>.
- **Relative and scaled errors** for forecasting are in <NoteLink to="percentage-errors">percentage errors</NoteLink>
  and <NoteLink to="scaled-errors-and-mean-absolute-scaled-error">scaled errors</NoteLink>.

TorchMetrics implements all of these, including a log-scaled MSE, with streaming accumulation over batches
<Cite id="detlefsen2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale dependence.** MSE and MAE are in the units of $y$. They cannot compare models across targets with different
  scales without normalisation.
- **Choosing the wrong one.** Report the score that matches the decision. If a large miss is much worse than several
  small ones, use RMSE; if every unit of error costs the same, use MAE. Training with one and evaluating with the
  other rewards a different point prediction.
- **Averaging RMSE over groups.** The mean of per-group RMSEs is not the RMSE of the pooled data. Pool squared errors,
  then take the root.
