<Definition>
For a reference signal $\svec \in \reals^T$ and an estimate $\hat\svec$, the signal-to-noise ratio in decibels is

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\norm{\svec}^2}{\norm{\svec - \hat\svec}^2}.
$$

The scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio projects the estimate onto the reference first,

$$
\alpha = \frac{\hat\svec^\top \svec}{\norm{\svec}^2}, \qquad
\mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\norm{\alpha\svec}^2}{\norm{\alpha\svec - \hat\svec}^2}
$$

<Cite id="leroux2019" />. Both are in decibels, unbounded above; higher is better.
</Definition>

## The projection

$\alpha\svec$ is the <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> of the estimate onto the line
spanned by the reference: $\alpha$ minimises $\norm{\hat\svec - \alpha\svec}^2$, and setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>
$-2\svec^\top(\hat\svec - \alpha\svec)$ to zero gives $\alpha = \hat\svec^\top\svec / \norm{\svec}^2$. The error
$\evec = \hat\svec - \alpha\svec$ is orthogonal to $\svec$, so the estimate splits into the part that is the reference
and the part that is not, $\norm{\hat\svec}^2 = \norm{\alpha\svec}^2 + \norm{\evec}^2$, and SI-SDR is the ratio of the
two in decibels.

Scaling the estimate by any $c \ne 0$ scales $\alpha$ and $\evec$ by $c$, so the ratio, and hence SI-SDR, is unchanged.
This is what makes it scale-invariant. SNR has no such step: an estimate at half the right volume is penalised although
nothing about its content is wrong. Separation systems often recover a source up to an unknown gain, so SI-SDR measures
what matters.

Writing $\rho = \hat\svec^\top\svec / \paren{\norm{\hat\svec}\,\norm{\svec}}$ for the cosine between estimate and reference,
$\norm{\alpha\svec}^2 = \rho^2\norm{\hat\svec}^2$ and $\norm{\evec}^2 = (1 - \rho^2)\norm{\hat\svec}^2$, so

$$
\mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR} = 10 \log_{10} \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho^2}.
$$

SI-SDR is a function of the angle between the two signals alone. The literature on speech separation often calls the
same quantity SI-SNR.

## SDR, SIR and SAR

The earlier BSS eval toolkit decomposes the estimate into a target part, an interference part (other sources that
leaked in), a noise part and an artefact part, by projecting onto the reference and onto the other sources
<Cite id="vincent2006" />. Its signal-to-distortion ratio compares the target part with everything else; SIR compares
it with the interference and SAR with the artefacts. The widely used version allows the target to be a filtered
reference, with a filter of up to 512 taps, not just a scaled one. Le Roux et al. showed that this flexibility lets
trivially distorted estimates score well and makes SDR depend on implementation details, and proposed SI-SDR as the
simpler, better-behaved alternative <Cite id="leroux2019" />.

Results are often reported as an improvement, SI-SDRi: the SI-SDR of the estimate minus the SI-SDR of the unprocessed
mixture against the same reference.

## Worked example

Reference $\svec = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ and estimate $\hat\svec = (2.5, 3.5, 6.5, 7.5)$: twice the reference plus a small
error $(0.5, -0.5, 0.5, -0.5)$.

- **SNR.** $\svec - \hat\svec = (-1.5, -1.5, -3.5, -3.5)$ has squared norm 29, against $\norm{\svec}^2 = 30$, so
  $\mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}(30/29) = 0.15$ dB. The doubled gain is counted as error.
- **SI-SDR.** $\hat\svec^\top\svec = 59$, so $\alpha = 59/30 = 1.967$. The error $\hat\svec - \alpha\svec$ has squared
  norm $0.967$ against $\norm{\alpha\svec}^2 = 116.0$, so $\mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR} = 20.8$ dB.

Halving the estimate brings it close to $\svec$, and SNR jumps to 20.8 dB; SI-SDR stays at 20.8 dB throughout. The
gain, not the content, was what SNR had been measuring.

## Pitfalls

- **Not invariant to delay.** A time shift of a few samples moves the estimate away from the reference and lowers
  SI-SDR sharply. Align the signals first.
- **Can be improved without better perception.** SI-SDR is a waveform measure; it does not model hearing. Perceptual
  measures such as <NoteLink to="perceptual-speech-quality-and-intelligibility">PESQ and STOI</NoteLink> are reported alongside it for speech.
- **Needs the clean reference.** Like SNR, SI-SDR is intrusive: it cannot score a recording for which no clean
  reference exists.
- **Silent references.** When $\norm{\svec}$ is near zero the projection is ill-defined; implementations add a small
  constant, which changes the score for near-silent segments.
