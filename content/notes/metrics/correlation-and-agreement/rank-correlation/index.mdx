<Definition>
Let $R_i$ and $S_i$ be the ranks of $x_i$ among the $x$ values and of $y_i$ among the $y$ values, with tied values given
the mean of their ranks. **Spearman's** $\rho$ is the Pearson correlation of $(R_i, S_i)$ <Cite id="spearman1904" />.

A pair $i < j$ is **concordant** if $x_i - x_j$ and $y_i - y_j$ have the same sign and **discordant** if they have
opposite signs. With $C$ concordant and $D$ discordant pairs among the $\binom n2$ pairs and no ties, **Kendall's**
$\tau$ is <Cite id="kendall1938" />

$$
\tau = \frac{C - D}{\binom n2}.
$$
</Definition>

## Invariance and bounded influence

Both coefficients depend on the data only through the ranks. So they are unchanged by any strictly increasing
transformation of either variable: $\log x$, $x^3$ or $e^x$ give the same values. They measure **monotone**
association: both equal 1 exactly when larger $x$ always goes with larger $y$, however curved the relationship, and
$-1$ when it always goes with smaller $y$.

Ranks also bound the influence of any single point. A point can move at most from the lowest rank to the highest,
however extreme its value. That is why rank correlation discounts the single distant point that creates Anscombe's
fourth correlation; see <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">Pearson correlation</NoteLink>.

## Spearman's formula

Without ties, the ranks $R_i$ and $S_i$ are each a permutation of $1, \dots, n$. Both have mean $(n+1)/2$ and the same
sum of squared deviations,

$$
\sum_i \paren{R_i - \tfrac{n+1}2}^2 = \frac{n(n^2 - 1)}{12}.
$$

Write $d_i = R_i - S_i$. Expanding $\sum_i d_i^2$ around the common mean gives
$\sum d_i^2 = 2\cdot\frac{n(n^2-1)}{12} - 2\sum_i (R_i - \bar R)(S_i - \bar S)$. Dividing the covariance term by
$n(n^2-1)/12$ gives $\rho$, so

$$
\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum_i d_i^2}{n(n^2 - 1)}.
$$

With ties this shortcut is inexact; compute the Pearson correlation of the averaged ranks instead.

## Kendall's τ as a probability

Pick two observations at random. $\tau$ is the probability that they are concordant minus the probability that they
are discordant. When there are no ties, $\tau = 0.6$ means that ordering a random pair by $x$ agrees with ordering it
by $y$ 80% of the time and disagrees 20% of the time.

**Ties.** $\tau_b$ corrects for tied pairs, which count as neither concordant nor discordant:
$\tau_b = (C - D)/\sqrt{(n_0 - T_x)(n_0 - T_y)}$, with $n_0 = \binom n2$ and $T_x$, $T_y$ the numbers of pairs tied in
$x$ and in $y$. This is the version SciPy and pandas report.

Kendall's $\tau$ is usually smaller in magnitude than Spearman's $\rho$ on the same data. The two measure different
things and should not be compared on the same scale.

## Worked example

Take $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$ and $y = (2, 1, 4, 3, 5)$, which are already ranks. The rank differences are
$d = (-1, 1, -1, 1, 0)$, so $\sum d_i^2 = 4$ and $\rho = 1 - 24/120 = 0.8$.

Of the 10 pairs, only $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 4)$ are discordant, so $C = 8$, $D = 2$ and $\tau = 6/10 = 0.6$.

With ties, $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$ and $y = (2, 4, 5, 4, 5)$ give $\rho = 0.738$ and $\tau_b = 0.671$, against a
Pearson correlation of 0.775. These match SciPy's `spearmanr` and `kendalltau`.

## In machine learning

- **Ranking evaluation.** When only the order of predictions matters, as in recommendation or retrieval, rank
  correlation between predicted and true scores measures ranking quality; see <NoteLink to="normalised-discounted-cumulative-gain">NDCG</NoteLink> for a
  score that weights the top of the list.
- **The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC)** is a rank statistic of the same family: the probability that a random positive outranks a random negative;
  see <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">ROC curves</NoteLink>.
- **Metric agreement.** Comparing two evaluation metrics, or a model's predicted scores with human judgements, is
  usually done with Spearman or Kendall correlation, since only the ranking of systems matters.

## Pitfalls

- **Monotone is not linear.** $\rho = 1$ says nothing about the shape of the relationship or the size of the effect.
- **Many ties.** With heavily tied data, such as ratings on a 1–5 scale, the tie-corrected versions differ noticeably
  from the textbook formulas. State which one is reported.
- **Non-monotone dependence.** A U-shaped relationship can have rank correlation near zero, just as with Pearson.
