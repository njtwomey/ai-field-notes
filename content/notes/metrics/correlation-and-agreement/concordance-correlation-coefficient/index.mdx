<Definition>
For paired measurements $(X, Y)$ with means $\mu_x$, $\mu_y$, variances $\sigma_x^2$, $\sigma_y^2$ and covariance
$\sigma_{xy}$, the concordance <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">correlation coefficient</NoteLink> is <Cite id="lin1989" />

$$
\rho_c = \frac{2\sigma_{xy}}{\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + (\mu_x - \mu_y)^2}.
$$

The sample version plugs in sample moments with divisor $n$. $\rho_c = 1$ exactly when every pair satisfies $y = x$.
</Definition>

## Agreement, not association

Pearson correlation is 1 whenever the points lie on any rising line, $y = a + bx$. Two instruments that always disagree
by a factor of 2, or a model whose predictions are all off by 10, can have perfect correlation. When the question is
whether two measurements are interchangeable, or whether predictions equal the truth, the relevant line is $y = x$.

$\rho_c$ scores closeness to that line. Start from the expected squared difference, which is 0 only for perfect
agreement:

$$
\expect\brack{(X - Y)^2} = (\mu_x - \mu_y)^2 + \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 - 2\sigma_{xy}.
$$

If $X$ and $Y$ kept the same means and variances but were uncorrelated, the covariance term would vanish. Scaling the
actual squared difference by that uncorrelated value,

$$
\rho_c = 1 - \frac{\expect\brack{(X - Y)^2}}{(\mu_x - \mu_y)^2 + \sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2},
$$

which simplifies to the definition. So $\rho_c$ is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 when the pairs do no better than
unrelated measurements with the same moments, and $-1$ when $y = -x$ with zero means.

## Correlation times a bias correction

Let $r = \sigma_{xy}/(\sigma_x\sigma_y)$ be the Pearson correlation, $v = \sigma_x/\sigma_y$ the ratio of scales and
$u = (\mu_x - \mu_y)/\sqrt{\sigma_x\sigma_y}$ the shift in means relative to the scales. Dividing the numerator and
denominator of $\rho_c$ by $\sigma_x\sigma_y$ gives

$$
\rho_c = r \cdot C_b, \qquad C_b = \frac{2}{v + 1/v + u^2} \in (0, 1].
$$

$r$ measures **precision**, how tightly the points hug their own best line. $C_b$ measures **accuracy**, how close that
line is to $y = x$. $C_b = 1$ exactly when $v = 1$ and $u = 0$: equal scales and equal means.

## Worked example

With $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$, population moments $\mu_x = 3$ and $\sigma_x^2 = 2$:

| Second measurement | Pearson $r$ | $\rho_c$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $y = x$ | 1 | 1 |
| $y = x + 1$ | 1 | $2 \cdot 2/(2 + 2 + 1) = 0.8$ |
| $y = 2x$ | 1 | $2 \cdot 4/(2 + 8 + 9) = 0.421$ |

For $y = 2x$: $v = \sqrt 2/\sqrt 8 = 0.5$ and $u = (3 - 6)/\sqrt{\sqrt 2 \cdot \sqrt 8} = -1.5$, so
$C_b = 2/(0.5 + 2 + 2.25) = 0.421$. The correlation is perfect and the agreement is poor.

## In machine learning

$\rho_c$ is the standard evaluation score for continuous-valued prediction where calibration of level and scale
matters, such as predicting emotional arousal and valence, and in medicine for comparing a new measurement method
with a reference. Unlike $R^2$ it is symmetric in its two arguments and bounded in $[-1, 1]$. $1 - \rho_c$ is also used
directly as a training loss, which pushes predictions to match the target's mean and variance as well as its
ordering. TorchMetrics implements it as `ConcordanceCorrCoef` <Cite id="detlefsen2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **It depends on the spread of the data.** Like Pearson's $r$, $\rho_c$ rises with the between-subject variance for
  the same measurement error. Compare values only across data with similar ranges.
- **One number hides two failures.** Report $r$ and $C_b$, or a plot of differences against means, to show whether
  poor agreement comes from noise or from a systematic shift or scale error.
- **Sample size.** Lin's estimator uses divisor $n$; with small samples its sampling distribution is wide, and
  intervals should come from the asymptotic variance in Lin's paper or from a <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink>.
