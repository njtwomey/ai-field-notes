<Definition>
For paired observations $(x_i, y_i)$ with means $\bar x$ and $\bar y$, Pearson's correlation coefficient is

$$
r = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}}.
$$

It is the sample version of $\corr(X, Y) = \cov(X, Y)/\sqrt{\var(X)\var(Y)}$ <Cite id="pearson1895" />.
</Definition>

## A cosine

Let $\tilde\xvec$ and $\tilde\yvec$ be the centred data vectors, with entries $x_i - \bar x$ and $y_i - \bar y$. The
numerator of $r$ is their dot product and the denominator is the product of their lengths, so

$$
r = \frac{\tilde\xvec^\top \tilde\yvec}{\norm{\tilde\xvec}\,\norm{\tilde\yvec}} = \cos\theta,
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between them. Pearson correlation is the
<NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">cosine similarity</NoteLink> of centred data.



- **Bounds.** The <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink> gives
  $\abs{\tilde\xvec^\top\tilde\yvec} \le \norm{\tilde\xvec}\,\norm{\tilde\yvec}$, so $-1 \le r \le 1$.
- **Extremes.** Equality holds exactly when $\tilde\yvec$ is a multiple of $\tilde\xvec$: when every point lies on a
  line $y = a + bx$. The sign of $r$ is the sign of $b$.
- **Invariance.** Replacing $x$ by $a + bx$ with $b > 0$ changes neither the centred vector's direction nor $r$. So $r$
  is unchanged by shifts and positive rescaling of either variable, and flips sign under a negative rescaling.

## What it measures

$r$ measures how well a straight line describes the relationship, not how strongly the variables depend on each
other. A perfect but curved relationship such as $y = x^2$ on a symmetric range gives $r = 0$. Independent variables
have $r = 0$ in the population, but $r = 0$ does not imply <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.

Anscombe's quartet makes the point <Cite id="anscombe1973" />. Four datasets of 11 points share the same means,
variances, fitted line and $r = 0.816$. One is a noisy line, one a clean curve, one a line with one outlier, and one a
vertical stack of points plus a single distant point that alone creates the correlation. Their
<NoteLink to="rank-correlation">Spearman correlations</NoteLink> are 0.818, 0.691, 0.991 and 0.5: rank correlation
sees the monotone relationship in the third set and discounts the single point in the fourth.

For least squares with an intercept on its training data, $r^2$ between $y$ and the fitted values equals the
coefficient of determination; see <NoteLink to="r-squared-and-explained-variance">R²</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

With $x = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$ and $y = (2, 4, 5, 4, 5)$, the centred vectors are $(-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)$ and
$(-2, 0, 1, 0, 1)$. Their dot product is 6 and their lengths are $\sqrt{10}$ and $\sqrt 6$, so
$r = 6/\sqrt{60} = 0.775$.

**Testing and intervals.** Under independence and normality,
$t = r\sqrt{(n-2)/(1-r^2)}$ has a $t$ distribution with $n - 2$ degrees of freedom <Cite id="casella2002" />. Here
$t = 2.12$ with 3 degrees of freedom, a two-sided <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> of 0.124. Fisher's transform $z = \operatorname{artanh} r$
is approximately normal with standard deviation $1/\sqrt{n - 3}$, which gives the 95% interval
$\tanh(1.032 \pm 1.96/\sqrt 2) = (-0.34, 0.98)$. Five points say little about $r$. SciPy's `pearsonr` returns the same
values.

## Pitfalls

- **Outliers.** One distant point can create or destroy a correlation. Plot the data, or report a
  <NoteLink to="rank-correlation">rank correlation</NoteLink> alongside.
- **Restricted range.** Correlation computed on a narrow slice of $x$ is weaker than over the full range, because the
  variance of $x$ shrinks while the noise does not.
- **Correlation is not agreement.** $y = 2x$ has $r = 1$, but the two variables never agree. To measure agreement
  between a prediction and the truth, use the
  <NoteLink to="concordance-correlation-coefficient">concordance correlation coefficient</NoteLink> or an error score.
- **Aggregated data.** Correlations between group averages are usually much stronger than correlations between
  individuals, because averaging removes within-group noise.
