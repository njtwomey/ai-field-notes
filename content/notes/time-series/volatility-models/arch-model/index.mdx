<Definition>
A series $r_t$ follows an **ARCH($q$)** process, for autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity, when
<Cite id="engle1982" />

$$
r_t = \sigma_t\varepsilon_t, \qquad \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^q \alpha_i r_{t-i}^2, \qquad
\varepsilon_t \iid \Gauss(0, 1),
$$

with $\omega > 0$ and $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_q \ge 0$. The conditional variance $\sigma_t^2$ is known at time
$t - 1$: it is a fixed function of past returns. $r_t$ is often the residual of a mean model, such as a constant mean or
a regression.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The model says that tomorrow's variance is a baseline $\omega$ plus a weighted sum of today's and recent squared
surprises. A large move of either sign at time $t - 1$ raises $\sigma_t^2$, so large moves tend to be followed by large
moves. This is <NoteLink to="volatility-clustering">volatility clustering</NoteLink> written as a regression of the
variance on past squared returns. The constraints $\omega > 0$ and $\alpha_i \ge 0$ keep $\sigma_t^2$ positive for
every possible history.

## Conditional and unconditional moments

Write $\Fcal_{t-1}$ for the information in $r_{t-1}, r_{t-2}, \dots$. Because $\sigma_t$ is fixed given
$\Fcal_{t-1}$ and $\varepsilon_t$ is independent of it:

$$
\expect[r_t \mid \Fcal_{t-1}] = \sigma_t\expect[\varepsilon_t] = 0, \qquad
\var(r_t \mid \Fcal_{t-1}) = \sigma_t^2.
$$

The returns are uncorrelated. For $k \ge 1$, $\expect[r_t r_{t-k}] = \expect\brack{r_{t-k}\expect[r_t \mid
\Fcal_{t-1}]} = 0$ by the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">tower property</NoteLink>.

**Unconditional variance.** Suppose the process is stationary with variance $\sigma^2 = \expect[r_t^2]$. Taking
expectations of the variance equation, and using $\expect[r_t^2] = \expect[\sigma_t^2]\expect[\varepsilon_t^2] =
\expect[\sigma_t^2]$:

$$
\sigma^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^q \alpha_i \sigma^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \sum_{i=1}^q \alpha_i}.
$$

A finite positive variance requires $\sum_i \alpha_i < 1$. That condition is also sufficient for <NoteLink to="stationarity">weak stationarity</NoteLink>
<Cite id="engle1982" />.

**The squares are an autoregression.** Define $\nu_t = r_t^2 - \sigma_t^2 = \sigma_t^2(\varepsilon_t^2 - 1)$. It has
conditional mean zero, so it is serially uncorrelated. Adding $\nu_t$ to both sides of the variance equation gives

$$
r_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^q \alpha_i r_{t-i}^2 + \nu_t.
$$

The squared returns follow an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">AR($q$)</NoteLink> with innovations $\nu_t$. Their autocorrelation at lag $k$ in an ARCH(1) is
$\alpha_1^k$, when the fourth moment exists. The shocks $\nu_t$ are heteroscedastic, so this AR representation gives
the autocorrelations but not an efficient estimator.

<Derivation title="Kurtosis of ARCH(1)">
Take $q = 1$, write $\alpha = \alpha_1$ and $m_2 = \expect[r_t^2] = \omega / (1 - \alpha)$. The fourth moment is
$\expect[r_t^4] = \expect[\sigma_t^4]\expect[\varepsilon_t^4] = 3\expect[\sigma_t^4]$. Squaring the variance equation
and taking expectations:

$$
\expect[\sigma_t^4] = \omega^2 + 2\omega\alpha m_2 + \alpha^2\expect[r_{t-1}^4] = \omega^2 + 2\omega\alpha m_2 +
3\alpha^2\expect[\sigma_t^4].
$$

So $\expect[\sigma_t^4] = (\omega^2 + 2\omega\alpha m_2)/(1 - 3\alpha^2)$, finite only if $3\alpha^2 < 1$. Substituting
$m_2$ gives $\omega^2 + 2\omega\alpha m_2 = \omega^2(1 + \alpha)/(1 - \alpha)$. The kurtosis is

$$
\kappa = \frac{\expect[r_t^4]}{m_2^2} = \frac{3\omega^2(1 + \alpha)/\paren{(1 - \alpha)(1 - 3\alpha^2)}}{\omega^2/(1 -
\alpha)^2} = 3\,\frac{1 - \alpha^2}{1 - 3\alpha^2}.
$$

It equals 3 at $\alpha = 0$ and grows without bound as $\alpha \to 1/\sqrt3 \approx 0.577$.
</Derivation>

## Forecasting the variance

For ARCH(1) with $\sigma^2 = \omega/(1 - \alpha)$, write $\omega = \sigma^2(1 - \alpha)$. The variance equation
becomes $\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \sigma^2 = \alpha(r_t^2 - \sigma^2)$. Taking conditional expectations at time $t$ and
recursing, with $\expect[r_{t+j}^2 \mid \Fcal_t] = \expect[\sigma_{t+j}^2 \mid \Fcal_t]$:

$$
\expect[\sigma_{t+h}^2 \mid \Fcal_t] = \sigma^2 + \alpha^{h-1}\paren{\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \sigma^2}, \qquad h \ge 1.
$$

The forecast decays geometrically from the current conditional variance towards the unconditional one, at rate
$\alpha$ per step.

## Estimation

Conditioning on the first $q$ observations, the Gaussian log-likelihood of $\theta = (\omega, \alpha_1, \dots,
\alpha_q)$ is

$$
\ell(\theta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=q+1}^T \paren{\log 2\pi + \log\sigma_t^2(\theta) + \frac{r_t^2}{\sigma_t^2(\theta)}}.
$$

Each $\sigma_t^2(\theta)$ is computed from observed returns, so the likelihood is explicit. It is maximised
numerically under the positivity constraints. When the true shocks are not Gaussian, the Gaussian likelihood still
gives consistent estimates (quasi-maximum likelihood), with <NoteLink to="residual-diagnostics">robust standard errors</NoteLink> <Cite id="hamilton1994" />.

**Testing for ARCH effects.** Engle's <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> test regresses $\hat r_t^2$ on a constant and
$\hat r_{t-1}^2, \dots, \hat r_{t-q}^2$ by <NoteLink to="linear-regression">least squares</NoteLink>. Under the null of
no ARCH ($\alpha_1 = \dots = \alpha_q = 0$), $T R^2$ from that regression is asymptotically $\ChiSq_q$
<Cite id="engle1982" />.

## Worked example

Take ARCH(1) with $\omega = 0.5$ and $\alpha = 0.5$.

- The unconditional variance is $0.5 / (1 - 0.5) = 1$.
- The kurtosis is $3(1 - 0.25)/(1 - 0.75) = 9$, three times the Gaussian value, although every shock is Gaussian.
- After a return $r_t = 3$, a three-standard-deviation move, the next conditional variance is
  $\sigma_{t+1}^2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 9 = 5$, a standard deviation of $2.24$.
- The variance forecasts for $h = 1, 2, 3$ steps are $5$, $1 + 0.5 \times 4 = 3$ and $1 + 0.25 \times 4 = 2$. They
  halve their distance to 1 at every step.

## Pitfalls

- **Many lags.** Observed clustering decays slowly, so a pure ARCH model needs a large $q$ and many non-negative
  parameters. The <NoteLink to="garch-model">GARCH model</NoteLink> <Cite id="bollerslev1986" /> replaces the long lag
  polynomial with one lagged variance.
- **Symmetry.** $\sigma_t^2$ depends on $r_{t-1}^2$, so a fall and a rise of the same size raise volatility equally.
  Equity returns show a leverage effect that ARCH cannot capture.
- **Mean misspecification.** Unmodelled autocorrelation or outliers in the mean equation leak into $\hat r_t^2$ and can
  make the LM test reject.
