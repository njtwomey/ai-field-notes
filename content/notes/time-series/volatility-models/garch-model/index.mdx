<Definition>
A series $r_t$ follows a **GARCH($p$, $q$)** process, for generalised autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity, when
<Cite id="bollerslev1986" />

$$
r_t = \sigma_t\varepsilon_t, \qquad
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^q \alpha_i r_{t-i}^2 + \sum_{j=1}^p \beta_j\sigma_{t-j}^2, \qquad
\varepsilon_t \iid \Gauss(0, 1),
$$

with $\omega > 0$ and $\alpha_i, \beta_j \ge 0$. The conditional variance depends on past squared returns, as in the
<NoteLink to="arch-model">ARCH model</NoteLink>, and on its own past values. The case used in practice is
**GARCH(1,1)**: $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

GARCH(1,1) updates a volatility estimate. The new variance is a weighted combination of a long-run level, yesterday's
squared return and yesterday's variance. The coefficient $\alpha$ is the **reaction**: how much one surprise moves the
variance. The sum $\alpha + \beta$ is the **persistence**: how much of today's variance deviation survives to tomorrow.
Daily financial returns typically give $\alpha$ between $0.03$ and $0.15$ and $\alpha + \beta$ between $0.9$ and
$0.99$: small reactions that last a long time.

## GARCH(1,1) is ARCH with infinitely many lags

Substitute the variance equation into itself repeatedly. With $\beta < 1$,

$$
\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha r_{t-1}^2 + \beta\paren{\omega + \alpha r_{t-2}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-2}^2} = \dots =
\frac{\omega}{1 - \beta} + \alpha\sum_{j=1}^\infty \beta^{j-1} r_{t-j}^2.
$$

The conditional variance is an ARCH($\infty$) whose lag weights $\alpha\beta^{j-1}$ decay geometrically. Three
parameters replace the long, unconstrained lag polynomial that a pure ARCH model needs to match slowly decaying
clustering <Cite id="bollerslev1986" />. With $\omega = 0$ and $\alpha = 1 - \beta$, the formula is an exponentially
weighted moving average of squared returns, the RiskMetrics estimator.

## Unconditional variance and persistence

Write $\Fcal_{t-1}$ for the information in $r_{t-1}, r_{t-2}, \dots$. Since $\expect[r_t^2 \mid \Fcal_{t-1}] =
\sigma_t^2$, the tower property gives $\expect[r_t^2] = \expect[\sigma_t^2]$. Suppose the process is stationary with
$\sigma^2 = \expect[r_t^2] = \expect[\sigma_t^2]$ for all $t$. Taking expectations of the variance equation:

$$
\sigma^2 = \omega + \alpha\sigma^2 + \beta\sigma^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta}.
$$

A finite positive variance requires $\alpha + \beta < 1$, and that condition is also sufficient for weak stationarity
<Cite id="bollerslev1986" />.

**Forecasts decay at rate $\alpha + \beta$.** Replace $\omega$ by $\sigma^2(1 - \alpha - \beta)$ and subtract
$\sigma^2$ from both sides:

$$
\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \sigma^2 = \alpha\paren{r_t^2 - \sigma^2} + \beta\paren{\sigma_t^2 - \sigma^2}.
$$

For $h \ge 2$, take expectations given $\Fcal_t$. The future squared return $r_{t+h-1}^2$ has conditional expectation
$\expect[\sigma_{t+h-1}^2 \mid \Fcal_t]$, so both terms on the right become the same forecast:

$$
\expect[\sigma_{t+h}^2 \mid \Fcal_t] - \sigma^2 = (\alpha + \beta)\paren{\expect[\sigma_{t+h-1}^2 \mid \Fcal_t] -
\sigma^2} = (\alpha + \beta)^{h-1}\paren{\sigma_{t+1}^2 - \sigma^2}.
$$

A deviation of the variance from its long-run level shrinks by the factor $\alpha + \beta$ per step. Its
**half-life** is $\log(1/2) / \log(\alpha + \beta)$ steps. This is why $\alpha + \beta$ is called the persistence.

**The squares are an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-moving-average-model">ARMA(1,1)</NoteLink>.** Define $\nu_t = r_t^2 - \sigma_t^2$, which has conditional mean zero. Substituting
$\sigma_t^2 = r_t^2 - \nu_t$ and $\sigma_{t-1}^2 = r_{t-1}^2 - \nu_{t-1}$ into the variance equation gives

$$
r_t^2 = \omega + (\alpha + \beta) r_{t-1}^2 + \nu_t - \beta\nu_{t-1}.
$$

The autoregressive coefficient is the persistence, which is why the autocorrelation of $r_t^2$ decays at the geometric
rate $\alpha + \beta$ from lag 1 onward.

<Derivation title="Kurtosis of GARCH(1,1)">
Write $\rho = \alpha + \beta$ for the persistence and $m_2 = \omega / (1 - \rho)$ for the unconditional variance.
Write $\sigma_{t+1}^2 = \omega + (\alpha\varepsilon_t^2 + \beta)\sigma_t^2$. Squaring and taking expectations, with
$\varepsilon_t$ independent of $\sigma_t$, $\expect[\varepsilon_t^2] = 1$ and $\expect[\varepsilon_t^4] = 3$:

$$
\expect[\sigma^4] = \omega^2 + 2\omega\rho\,m_2 + (3\alpha^2 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2)\expect[\sigma^4]
= \omega^2 + 2\omega\rho\,m_2 + (\rho^2 + 2\alpha^2)\expect[\sigma^4].
$$

So $\expect[\sigma^4] = \omega^2(1 + \rho)/\paren{(1 - \rho)(1 - \rho^2 - 2\alpha^2)}$, finite only when
$\rho^2 + 2\alpha^2 < 1$. The kurtosis of $r_t$ is $3\expect[\sigma^4]/m_2^2$:

$$
\frac{\expect[r_t^4]}{\paren{\expect[r_t^2]}^2} = 3\,\frac{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2}{1 - (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha^2}.
$$

It exceeds 3 whenever $\alpha > 0$. At fixed persistence, a larger reaction $\alpha$ gives heavier tails.
</Derivation>

## Estimation

The parameters $\theta = (\omega, \alpha, \beta)$ are estimated by maximising the Gaussian log-likelihood

$$
\ell(\theta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=1}^T \paren{\log 2\pi + \log\sigma_t^2(\theta) + \frac{r_t^2}{\sigma_t^2(\theta)}},
$$

where $\sigma_t^2(\theta)$ is computed by running the variance recursion over the data from a starting value such as
the sample variance. The optimisation enforces $\omega > 0$, $\alpha, \beta \ge 0$ and usually $\alpha + \beta < 1$.
**Variance targeting** fixes $\omega = \hat\sigma^2(1 - \alpha - \beta)$ with $\hat\sigma^2$ the sample variance, which
leaves a two-parameter search. When the shocks are not Gaussian, the same objective gives consistent
quasi-maximum-likelihood estimates; a Student t shock distribution is the common alternative <Cite id="hamilton1994" />.

## Worked example

Take $\omega = 0.05$, $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0.85$, with returns in percent per day.

- The persistence is $0.95$ and the unconditional variance is $0.05 / (1 - 0.95) = 1$, a daily volatility of 1%.
- The half-life of a variance shock is $\log 0.5 / \log 0.95 = 13.5$ days.
- Suppose tomorrow's conditional variance is $\sigma_{t+1}^2 = 4$, a volatility of 2%. The forecasts are
  $1 + 0.95^{h-1} \times 3$: $4$, $3.85$, $3.44$, $2.89$ and $2.13$ for $h = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20$.
- The kurtosis is $3(1 - 0.9025)/(1 - 0.9025 - 0.02) = 3.77$.

## Variants

- **IGARCH.** With $\alpha + \beta = 1$, variance shocks never decay and the unconditional variance is infinite. The
  process is still strictly stationary for $\omega > 0$ <Cite id="nelson1990" />.
- **GJR-GARCH** adds $\gamma\,r_{t-1}^2\indicator\set{r_{t-1} < 0}$ to the variance equation, so negative returns raise
  volatility more than positive ones <Cite id="glosten1993" />.
- **EGARCH** models $\log\sigma_t^2$, which removes the positivity constraints and allows asymmetric responses
  <Cite id="nelson1991" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Near-unit persistence.** Estimates of $\alpha + \beta$ close to 1 are common. Structural breaks in the volatility
  level push estimates towards 1 even when the true persistence within each regime is lower.
- **Positivity constraints bind.** An estimate at $\alpha = 0$ or $\beta = 0$ is on the boundary of the parameter space,
  where the usual standard errors and likelihood-ratio tests do not apply.
- **Starting values.** $\sigma_1^2$ must be chosen. For short series and high persistence the choice affects the
  likelihood.
- **Symmetric response.** Plain GARCH ignores the sign of $r_{t-1}$. Test the residuals for a leverage effect before
  relying on it for equities.
