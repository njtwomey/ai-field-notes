<Definition>
A series of returns $r_1, \dots, r_T$ shows **volatility clustering** when the magnitudes $\abs{r_t}$ or the squares
$r_t^2$ are positively autocorrelated while the returns themselves are close to uncorrelated. Periods of large
movements alternate with calm periods. Equivalently, the conditional variance
$\sigma_t^2 = \var(r_t \mid r_{t-1}, r_{t-2}, \dots)$ is not constant but depends on recent history
<Cite id="mandelbrot1963" />.
</Definition>

## Returns and their stylised facts

A price series $P_t$ is converted to **log returns** $r_t = \log P_t - \log P_{t-1}$. Log returns add over time and are
close to the percentage change for small moves. Daily log returns of stocks, indices and exchange rates share a set of
empirical regularities called **stylised facts** <Cite id="cont2001" />:

- **Little linear autocorrelation.** The sample autocorrelation of $r_t$ is close to zero beyond the first lag. Past
  returns do not predict the sign of the next return.
- **Volatility clustering.** The sample autocorrelation of $r_t^2$ or $\abs{r_t}$ is positive and decays slowly, often
  staying significant for dozens or hundreds of lags.
- **Heavy tails.** The unconditional distribution of $r_t$ has kurtosis well above the value 3 of a Gaussian. Moves of
  five or more standard deviations occur far more often than a Gaussian predicts <Cite id="mandelbrot1963" />.
- **Leverage effect.** Negative returns are followed by larger increases in volatility than positive returns of the
  same size, for equities in particular.

The first two facts together say that $r_t$ is uncorrelated but not independent. <NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> would make every
function of $r_t$ uncorrelated with every function of $r_{t-k}$, including the squares.

## Why clustering produces heavy tails

Suppose $r_t = \sigma_t\varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t \iid \Gauss(0, 1)$ independent of the volatility $\sigma_t$.
Conditionally on $\sigma_t$, the return is Gaussian. Unconditionally, it is a scale <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">mixture of Gaussians</NoteLink>. Its second
and fourth moments are

$$
\expect[r_t^2] = \expect[\sigma_t^2]\,\expect[\varepsilon_t^2] = \expect[\sigma_t^2], \qquad
\expect[r_t^4] = \expect[\sigma_t^4]\,\expect[\varepsilon_t^4] = 3\,\expect[\sigma_t^4].
$$

The kurtosis is therefore

$$
\kappa = \frac{\expect[r_t^4]}{\paren{\expect[r_t^2]}^2} = 3\,\frac{\expect[\sigma_t^4]}{\paren{\expect[\sigma_t^2]}^2}
= 3\paren{1 + \frac{\var(\sigma_t^2)}{\paren{\expect[\sigma_t^2]}^2}} \ge 3.
$$

The kurtosis exceeds 3 whenever $\sigma_t^2$ varies at all. Any model in which volatility moves over time therefore
produces heavy-tailed returns even with Gaussian shocks. The two stylised facts are one phenomenon seen twice.

## Why squared returns are autocorrelated

With the same model and $\sigma_t$ independent of the current and future shocks, the autocovariance of the squares at
lag $k \ge 1$ is

$$
\cov(r_t^2, r_{t-k}^2) = \expect[\sigma_t^2 \sigma_{t-k}^2\varepsilon_t^2\varepsilon_{t-k}^2] - \paren{\expect[\sigma_t^2]}^2
= \cov(\sigma_t^2, \sigma_{t-k}^2\varepsilon_{t-k}^2).
$$

The step uses $\expect[\varepsilon_t^2] = 1$ and the independence of $\varepsilon_t$ from everything before it. The
covariance is positive when a large $\sigma_{t-k}$ or a large shock at $t - k$ raises the later variance
$\sigma_t^2$. The returns themselves stay uncorrelated: $\expect[r_t r_{t-k}] = \expect[\sigma_t\varepsilon_t
\sigma_{t-k}\varepsilon_{t-k}] = 0$ because $\varepsilon_t$ has mean zero and is independent of the other three
factors.

## Models of time-varying volatility

Two families specify how $\sigma_t^2$ evolves.

- **Conditionally heteroscedastic models** make $\sigma_t^2$ a deterministic function of past returns. The
  <NoteLink to="arch-model">ARCH model</NoteLink> <Cite id="engle1982" /> uses past squared returns. The
  <NoteLink to="garch-model">GARCH model</NoteLink> adds past conditional variances. The likelihood is available in
  closed form.
- **<NoteLink to="stochastic-volatility">Stochastic volatility</NoteLink> models** give $\log\sigma_t^2$ its own random
  shock. The volatility is then a latent process, and the likelihood requires filtering.

## Diagnostics

- Plot the <NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation">sample autocorrelation function</NoteLink> of $r_t$ and of $r_t^2$ side by side. Clustering shows as a flat first plot
  and a slowly decaying second plot.
- A <NoteLink to="ljung-box-test">Ljung–Box test</NoteLink> applied to $r_t^2$ tests the joint null that the first $m$ autocorrelations of the squares are zero.
  Engle's <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> test regresses $r_t^2$ on $r_{t-1}^2, \dots, r_{t-q}^2$ and compares $T R^2$ with a
  $\ChiSq_q$ distribution <Cite id="engle1982" />.
- After fitting a volatility model, the <NoteLink to="residual-diagnostics">standardised residuals</NoteLink> $r_t / \hat\sigma_t$ should show neither clustering nor
  excess kurtosis. Remaining kurtosis suggests a heavier-tailed shock distribution, such as a
  <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Uncorrelated is not independent.** Clustered returns are <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white noise</NoteLink> in the weak sense but not iid. A white-noise test on $r_t$ alone passes on clustered returns. Test the squares
  as well.
- **Sample kurtosis is unstable.** It depends on fourth powers, so one crash can dominate it. Report it with the sample
  period.
- **Structural breaks mimic persistence.** A single shift in the volatility level produces a slowly decaying
  autocorrelation in $r_t^2$ even when there is no clustering within each regime.
