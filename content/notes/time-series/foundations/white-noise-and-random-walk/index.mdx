<Definition>
A series $\set{\varepsilon_t}$ is **white noise** with variance $\sigma^2$, written $\varepsilon_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0,
\sigma^2)$, if $\expect[\varepsilon_t] = 0$, $\var(\varepsilon_t) = \sigma^2$ and $\cov(\varepsilon_s, \varepsilon_t) = 0$
for $s \ne t$. A **random walk** is the running sum of white noise: $x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ for $t \ge 1$,
from a fixed start $x_0$. A **random walk with drift** adds a constant $\delta$ at each step,
$x_t = \delta + x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ <Cite id="brockwell1991" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

White noise is the series with no memory that a linear model can use. Each value is uncorrelated with every other,
so the past gives no linear information about the future. It is the building block of every ARMA model, and it is the
target of residual diagnostics: a model has captured the linear structure of a series when its residuals look like
white noise.

A random walk has perfect memory. Each shock $\varepsilon_t$ moves the level permanently, and nothing pulls the series
back. Stock prices, exchange rates and many economic series behave approximately like random walks over short
horizons.

## White noise

The definition constrains only the first two moments. Stronger versions add distributional assumptions:

- **Independent and identically distributed (iid) noise** requires the $\varepsilon_t$ to be independent with a common
  distribution. Iid noise with finite variance is white noise. The converse fails: uncorrelated values can still be
  dependent, for example when large values cluster in time, as in ARCH processes.
- **Gaussian white noise** requires each $\varepsilon_t \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$ with the vector of any finite set of
  values jointly Gaussian. Uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are independent, so Gaussian white noise is iid.

White noise is weakly stationary. Its autocovariance is $\gamma(0) = \sigma^2$ and $\gamma(h) = 0$ for $h \ne 0$, so
its autocorrelation function is 1 at lag 0 and 0 elsewhere. Its power spectral density is the constant $\sigma^2$ at
every frequency, which is the origin of the name: all frequencies contribute equal power, as all colours do in white
light.

## The random walk

With $x_0 = 0$, unrolling the recursion gives $x_t = \sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_s$. The moments follow from the
properties of white noise:

$$
\expect[x_t] = 0, \qquad \var(x_t) = \sum_{s=1}^{t}\var(\varepsilon_s) = t\sigma^2.
$$

The variance sums because the shocks are uncorrelated. For $s \le t$, the covariance counts the shocks the two values
share:

$$
\cov(x_s, x_t) = \cov\paren{\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_i,\, \sum_{j=1}^{t}\varepsilon_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{s}\var(\varepsilon_i) = s\sigma^2,
$$

because only the pairs with $i = j$ contribute. So $\cov(x_s, x_t) = \min(s, t)\,\sigma^2$, and the correlation is

$$
\corr(x_t, x_{t+h}) = \frac{t\sigma^2}{\sqrt{t\sigma^2 \cdot (t + h)\sigma^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{t}{t + h}}.
$$

The variance depends on $t$ and the covariance depends on $t$ as well as the lag, so the random walk is not
stationary. The walk is the AR(1) recursion $x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ at the boundary $\phi = 1$. For
$\abs\phi < 1$ the variance from $x_0 = 0$ is $\sigma^2(1 - \phi^{2t})/(1 - \phi^2)$, which converges to the stationary
value $\sigma^2/(1 - \phi^2)$. At $\phi = 1$ the same sum is $t\sigma^2$. A process with $\phi = 1$ is said to have a
**unit root** (<NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">differencing and unit roots</NoteLink>).

With drift, $x_t = x_0 + \delta t + \sum_{s=1}^{t}\varepsilon_s$. The mean $x_0 + \delta t$ is a straight line, and the
variance is $t\sigma^2$ as before. The line is a deterministic trend and the sum of shocks is a **stochastic trend**.

The first difference of a random walk is $\Delta x_t = x_t - x_{t-1} = \delta + \varepsilon_t$: white noise plus a
constant. Differencing is the transformation that makes a random walk stationary.

## Worked example: correlation and forecasts

Take a random walk without drift, $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $x_0 = 0$.

- At $t = 100$ the variance is $100$ and the standard deviation is $10$. At $t = 400$ the standard deviation is $20$:
  quadrupling the time doubles the spread.
- The correlation between $x_{100}$ and $x_{110}$ is $\sqrt{100/110} = 0.953$. Values ten steps apart are nearly
  perfectly correlated, because they share 100 of their shocks.

Forecasting is direct. Given $x_1, \dots, x_T$, the future value is
$x_{T+h} = x_T + \delta h + \sum_{s=1}^{h}\varepsilon_{T+s}$, and the future shocks have mean 0 and are uncorrelated
with the past. So the minimum mean-squared-error linear forecast and its error variance are

$$
\hat x_{T+h \mid T} = x_T + \delta h, \qquad \var(x_{T+h} - \hat x_{T+h \mid T}) = h\sigma^2.
$$

Without drift the forecast is the last observed value at every horizon. This is the **naive forecast**, the usual
baseline in forecasting practice <Cite id="hyndman2021" />. The 95% prediction interval
$\hat x_{T+h \mid T} \pm 1.96\sigma\sqrt h$ widens as $\sqrt h$. With $\sigma = 1$, the interval at $h = 25$ is
$x_T \pm 9.8$.

## Cycles and trends from noise

Sums of random shocks look structured. A random walk wanders in long excursions that resemble trends, and its turning
points resemble business cycles. Slutsky showed in 1927 (published in English in 1937) that moving sums of
independent random numbers produce series with apparent regular cycles <Cite id="slutsky1937" />. The apparent
structure has no predictive value beyond the last observed value.

The same effect breaks regression. Regress one random walk on another, independent random walk. The fitted slope does
not converge to 0, the $t$-statistic diverges as the sample grows, and $R^2$ is typically large. Granger and Newbold
called this **spurious regression** <Cite id="granger1974" />. The remedy is to difference both series, or to test for
cointegration.

## Assumptions

- White noise needs finite variance. A sequence of iid Cauchy variables has no variance and is not white noise.
- The random-walk moment formulas assume a fixed start $x_0$. A random walk has no stationary distribution to start
  from.

## Pitfalls

- **Uncorrelated is not independent.** A white-noise test on residuals (such as the
  <NoteLink to="ljung-box-test">Ljung–Box test</NoteLink>) checks correlation only. Squared residuals can still be
  correlated, which is the signature of <NoteLink to="volatility-clustering">volatility clustering</NoteLink>.
- **Trend-stationary versus difference-stationary.** A deterministic trend plus stationary noise,
  $x_t = a + bt + u_t$, also drifts upward. Its deviations from the line are bounded in distribution, and removing the
  line makes it stationary. Differencing it instead gives $b + u_t - u_{t-1}$, a non-invertible moving average. The two
  models give different long-horizon forecast intervals: constant width for the trend model, width growing as
  $\sqrt h$ for the random walk.
