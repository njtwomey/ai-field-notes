<Definition>
For a weakly stationary series with autocovariance $\gamma(h) = \cov(x_t, x_{t+h})$, the **autocorrelation function
(ACF)** is $\rho(h) = \gamma(h)/\gamma(0)$. The **partial autocorrelation function (PACF)** at lag $h \ge 1$ is the
last coefficient $\phi_{hh}$ of the best linear predictor of $x_t$ from $x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-h}$. Equivalently,
$\phi_{hh}$ is the correlation between $x_t$ and $x_{t-h}$ after each has been linearly regressed on
$x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-h+1}$ <Cite id="brockwell1991" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The ACF measures total linear dependence at lag $h$, direct and indirect. In an AR(1) series,
$x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, the value $x_t$ depends directly only on $x_{t-1}$. It is still correlated with
$x_{t-2}$, with $\rho(2) = \phi^2$, because $x_{t-2}$ drives $x_{t-1}$, which drives $x_t$. The PACF removes such
chains. Once $x_{t-1}$ is accounted for, $x_{t-2}$ adds nothing, so $\phi_{22} = 0$.

The two functions together are the main tool for choosing the orders of an ARMA model
(<NoteLink to="box-jenkins-method">Box–Jenkins method</NoteLink>). A moving-average process of order $q$ has an ACF
that is exactly 0 beyond lag $q$. An <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive process</Gloss> of order $p$ has a PACF that is exactly 0 beyond lag $p$.

## The sample ACF

Given observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with mean $\bar x$, the **sample autocovariance** and **sample autocorrelation**
at lag $h = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1$ are

$$
\hat\gamma(h) = \frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n-h}(x_t - \bar x)(x_{t+h} - \bar x), \qquad r_h = \frac{\hat\gamma(h)}{\hat\gamma(0)}.
$$

The divisor is $n$, not the number of terms $n - h$. With divisor $n$, the matrix
$[\hat\gamma(i - j)]_{i,j=1}^{k}$ is non-negative definite for every $k$, as a true autocovariance matrix must be. It
equals $\frac1n\Lmat\Lmat^\top$, where $\Lmat$ is a $k \times (n + k - 1)$ matrix whose rows are shifted copies of the
centred data padded with zeros. The divisor $n - h$ gives an estimate with less bias at each lag, but the resulting
matrix can be indefinite, and at large lags the few available products make it very noisy <Cite id="brockwell1991" />.
A plot of $r_h$ against $h$ is a **correlogram**.

## Standard errors of the sample ACF

For <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">iid noise</NoteLink> with variance $\sigma^2$ and a large sample, the sample mean and $\hat\gamma(0)$ are close to $0$ and
$\sigma^2$. Then, for $h \ge 1$,

$$
r_h \approx \frac{1}{n\sigma^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n-h}x_tx_{t+h}.
$$

The products $x_tx_{t+h}$ have mean 0, because $x_t$ and $x_{t+h}$ are independent with mean 0. They have variance
$\expect[x_t^2]\expect[x_{t+h}^2] = \sigma^4$. Two different products are uncorrelated:
$\expect[x_sx_{s+h}x_tx_{t+h}] = 0$ for $s \ne t$, because at least one factor appears only once and has mean 0. So

$$
\var(r_h) \approx \frac{(n - h)\sigma^4}{n^2\sigma^4} \approx \frac1n.
$$

A <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> for the sum makes $r_h$ approximately $\Gauss(0, 1/n)$, and different lags are approximately
independent. This justifies the dashed band $\pm 1.96/\sqrt n$ drawn on correlograms: under iid noise, about 95% of
the sample autocorrelations lie inside it <Cite id="box2015" />.

For a series that is not white noise, Bartlett's formula gives the large-sample variance of $r_h$ for a linear process
with iid innovations <Cite id="bartlett1946" />:

$$
\var(r_h) \approx \frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\paren{\rho(k + h) + \rho(k - h) - 2\rho(k)\rho(h)}^2.
$$

Its main use is testing whether an <Gloss name="ma" />($q$) model fits. If $\rho(k) = 0$ for $\abs k > q$, then for every $h > q$ the
formula reduces to

$$
\var(r_h) \approx \frac1n\paren{1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{q}\rho(k)^2},
$$

and the band beyond lag $q$ widens by the square root of the factor in brackets. In practice $\rho(k)$ is replaced by
$r_k$.

<Derivation title="Reducing Bartlett's formula for an MA(q) process">
Fix $h > q$, so $\rho(h) = 0$ and the cross term vanishes. For $k \ge 1$, $k + h > q$, so $\rho(k + h) = 0$. Each
summand is therefore $\rho(k - h)^2$. As $k$ runs over $1, 2, \dots$, the index $j = k - h$ runs over
$1 - h, 2 - h, \dots$. Because $1 - h \le -q$, this range contains every $j$ with $\abs j \le q$, which are the only
non-zero terms. So the sum is $\sum_{j=-q}^{q}\rho(j)^2 = 1 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{q}\rho(j)^2$, using $\rho(0) = 1$ and
$\rho(-j) = \rho(j)$.
</Derivation>

## The PACF

The best linear predictor of $x_t$ (centred to mean 0) from the $h$ previous values is
$\hat x_t = \phi_{h1}x_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_{hh}x_{t-h}$. Its coefficients minimise the <Gloss name="mse">mean-squared error</Gloss>
$\expect[(x_t - \hat x_t)^2]$. Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> with respect to each $\phi_{hj}$ to 0 says that the error is
uncorrelated with each predictor: $\expect[(x_t - \hat x_t)x_{t-j}] = 0$ for $j = 1, \dots, h$. Dividing by
$\gamma(0)$ gives the **Yule–Walker equations** of order $h$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho(1) & \cdots & \rho(h-1) \\ \rho(1) & 1 & \cdots & \rho(h-2) \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\
\rho(h-1) & \rho(h-2) & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \phi_{h1} \\ \phi_{h2} \\ \vdots \\ \phi_{hh} \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} \rho(1) \\ \rho(2) \\ \vdots \\ \rho(h) \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The PACF at lag $h$ is the last component, $\phi_{hh}$. At lag 1 the system is $\phi_{11} = \rho(1)$. At lag 2,
Cramer's rule gives

$$
\phi_{22} = \frac{\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho(1) \\ \rho(1) & \rho(2) \end{bmatrix}}{\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho(1) \\ \rho(1) & 1 \end{bmatrix}}
= \frac{\rho(2) - \rho(1)^2}{1 - \rho(1)^2}.
$$

For the AR(1) example, $\rho(2) = \phi^2 = \rho(1)^2$, so $\phi_{22} = 0$, as the intuition required.

**Why the PACF of an AR($p$) process cuts off.** If $x_t = \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_ix_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t$ and $h \ge p$,
the predictor $\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_ix_{t-i}$ leaves the error $\varepsilon_t$, which is uncorrelated with all past values.
So it satisfies the orthogonality conditions for every $h \ge p$, and by uniqueness it is the best predictor of order
$h$. Its coefficient on $x_{t-h}$ is $\phi_p$ when $h = p$ and 0 when $h > p$.

Solving the full system at every $h$ costs $O(h^3)$. The **Durbin–Levinson recursion** obtains order $h$ from order
$h - 1$ in $O(h)$ operations, using the Toeplitz structure:

$$
\phi_{hh} = \frac{\rho(h) - \sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\phi_{h-1,j}\,\rho(h - j)}{1 - \sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\phi_{h-1,j}\,\rho(j)}, \qquad
\phi_{hj} = \phi_{h-1,j} - \phi_{hh}\,\phi_{h-1,h-j} \quad (j < h).
$$

The same recursion appears in speech coding as the Levinson–Durbin algorithm for
<NoteLink to="linear-prediction">linear prediction</NoteLink>, where the $\phi_{hh}$ are the reflection coefficients,
up to a sign convention.

The **sample PACF** runs the Durbin–Levinson recursion on $r_1, r_2, \dots$ in place of $\rho$. For an AR($p$) process
and $h > p$, the sample $\hat\phi_{hh}$ is approximately $\Gauss(0, 1/n)$ <Cite id="quenouille1949" />, so the same
$\pm 1.96/\sqrt n$ band applies.

## Worked example

Take $n = 8$ observations $x = (1, 3, 4, 3, 2, 0, 1, 2)$. The mean is $\bar x = 16/8 = 2$ and the deviations are
$d = (-1, 1, 2, 1, 0, -2, -1, 0)$.

- $\hat\gamma(0) = (1 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 1 + 0)/8 = 12/8 = 1.5$.
- $\hat\gamma(1)$ sums $d_td_{t+1}$ over the seven adjacent pairs: $-1 + 2 + 2 + 0 + 0 + 2 + 0 = 5$, so
  $\hat\gamma(1) = 5/8 = 0.625$ and $r_1 = 0.625/1.5 = 0.417$.
- $\hat\gamma(2)$ sums $d_td_{t+2}$ over six pairs: $-2 + 1 + 0 - 2 + 0 + 0 = -3$, so $\hat\gamma(2) = -0.375$ and
  $r_2 = -0.25$.

The sample PACF is $\hat\phi_{11} = r_1 = 0.417$ and

$$
\hat\phi_{22} = \frac{r_2 - r_1^2}{1 - r_1^2} = \frac{-0.25 - 0.174}{1 - 0.174} = -0.513.
$$

The white-noise band is $\pm 1.96/\sqrt8 = \pm 0.693$. Every value lies inside it: eight observations cannot
distinguish this series from white noise. Under an MA(1) hypothesis, the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of $r_2$ is
$\sqrt{(1 + 2r_1^2)/8} = \sqrt{1.347/8} = 0.410$, a little larger than $1/\sqrt8 = 0.354$.

## Reading a correlogram

| Model                  | ACF                                        | PACF                                       |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| White noise            | 0 at all lags $h \ge 1$                    | 0 at all lags $h \ge 1$                    |
| AR($p$)                | Tails off (exponentials and damped cosines) | Cuts off after lag $p$                     |
| MA($q$)                | Cuts off after lag $q$                     | Tails off                                  |
| ARMA($p$, $q$)         | Tails off after lag $q - p$                | Tails off after lag $p - q$                |
| <NoteLink to="random-walk">Random walk</NoteLink> or trend   | Decays very slowly, nearly linearly        | Large spike at lag 1 near 1                |
| Seasonal, period $m$   | Spikes at lags $m, 2m, \dots$              | Spikes near lags $m, 2m, \dots$            |

The ARMA explorer in <NoteLink to="autoregressive-moving-average-model">the ARMA model</NoteLink> note shows each pattern for chosen
coefficients.

## Assumptions

- The ACF and PACF are defined for weakly stationary series. For a non-stationary series the sample ACF is still
  computable, but it estimates nothing fixed.
- The $1/n$ standard errors assume iid noise. For uncorrelated but dependent noise, such as <Gloss name="arch" /> errors, the true
  variance of $r_h$ is larger and the band is too narrow.

## Pitfalls

- **Many lags, some false alarms.** With 20 lags plotted and a 95% band, about one lag of true white noise falls
  outside by chance. A joint test such as the <NoteLink to="ljung-box-test">Ljung–Box test</NoteLink> controls this.
- **Trends dominate.** A trend or a <NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">unit root</NoteLink> makes $r_h$ large at every lag and hides all other structure. Remove the
  trend or difference first.
- **Sample autocorrelations are correlated with each other.** A single large $r_h$ can produce a smooth ripple in
  neighbouring lags, which is easy to over-interpret.
- **Small samples bias the ACF downwards.** Because the mean is estimated, for white noise
  $\expect[r_h] \approx -1/n$, not 0.
