<Definition>
A **time series** is a sequence of <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $\set{x_t}$ indexed by integer time $t$. The series is **strictly
stationary** if, for every $k \ge 1$, every set of times $t_1, \dots, t_k$ and every shift $h$, the vectors
$(x_{t_1}, \dots, x_{t_k})$ and $(x_{t_1 + h}, \dots, x_{t_k + h})$ have the same joint distribution. The series is
**weakly stationary** if $\expect[x_t^2] < \infty$, the mean $\expect[x_t] = \mu$ does not depend on $t$, and the
covariance $\cov(x_t, x_{t+h}) = \gamma(h)$ depends on the lag $h$ only <Cite id="brockwell1991" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A stationary series has no preferred point in time. Its level, its spread and the way each value relates to the values
before it are the same in every stretch of the record. A window taken from the start of the series and a window taken
from the end are two samples of the same random object.

Stationarity is what makes a single record informative. A series is usually observed once. Averages over time can
estimate a mean, a variance or a correlation only if those quantities are the same at every time. Most of classical
time-series analysis, including autoregressive and <Gloss name="ma">moving-average models</Gloss>, assumes a weakly stationary series, or one
that becomes weakly stationary after a transformation such as differencing.

## Weak stationarity and the autocovariance function

For a weakly stationary series, the function $\gamma(h) = \cov(x_t, x_{t+h})$ is the **autocovariance function**. The
**autocorrelation function** is $\rho(h) = \gamma(h) / \gamma(0)$, where $\gamma(0) = \var(x_t)$. Three properties
follow from the definition.

- **Symmetry.** $\gamma(-h) = \cov(x_t, x_{t-h}) = \cov(x_{t-h}, x_t) = \gamma(h)$. The second step shifts both
  indices by $h$, which weak stationarity allows.
- **Bound.** $\abs{\gamma(h)} \le \gamma(0)$, by the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink>
  $\abs{\cov(x_t, x_{t+h})} \le \sqrt{\var(x_t)\var(x_{t+h})} = \gamma(0)$. So $\abs{\rho(h)} \le 1$.
- **Non-negative definiteness.** For any times $t_1, \dots, t_k$ and real weights $a_1, \dots, a_k$,
  $\sum_{i,j} a_i a_j \gamma(t_i - t_j) = \var\paren{\sum_i a_i x_{t_i}} \ge 0$.

The third property is the one that constrains which functions can be autocovariances. It is why an estimated
autocovariance must be built with care: an estimator that is not non-negative definite can imply a negative variance
for some <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">linear combination</NoteLink>. The <NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-wiener-khinchin">Wiener–Khinchin theorem</NoteLink>
states the same property in the frequency domain, as a non-negative power spectral density.

## Strict versus weak

Neither definition implies the other in general.

- **Strict does not imply weak** when the second moment is infinite. An <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss>
  sequence of Cauchy variables is strictly stationary, because every joint distribution is a product of identical
  marginals. It has no mean and no variance, so it is not weakly stationary.
- **Weak does not imply strict** when higher moments or shapes change over time. Let $x_t$ be independent, with
  $x_t \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ for odd $t$ and $x_t$ uniform on $[-\sqrt3, \sqrt3]$ for even $t$. Every $x_t$ has mean 0
  and variance 1, and distinct times are uncorrelated, so the series is weakly stationary. The marginal distribution
  changes from one time to the next, so it is not strictly stationary.
- **Gaussian processes** are the exception. A Gaussian process is fixed by its mean and <NoteLink to="covariance-functions">covariance functions</NoteLink>. If the
  mean is constant and the covariance depends only on the lag, every joint distribution is a <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> with
  shift-invariant parameters. So a weakly stationary Gaussian process is strictly stationary.

In practice "stationary" usually means weakly stationary.

## Worked example: a stationary AR(1) series

Let $x_t = c + \phi x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, where the $\varepsilon_t$ are uncorrelated with mean 0 and variance
$\sigma^2$, and $\abs{\phi} < 1$. Substituting the equation into itself $k$ times gives

$$
x_t = c\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\phi^j + \phi^k x_{t-k} + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}.
$$

As $k \to \infty$, $\phi^k \to 0$, and the series has the representation
$x_t = c/(1 - \phi) + \sum_{j \ge 0}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}$. Its moments follow directly:

$$
\expect[x_t] = \frac{c}{1 - \phi}, \qquad
\gamma(h) = \sigma^2\sum_{j \ge 0}\phi^j\phi^{j+h} = \frac{\sigma^2\phi^h}{1 - \phi^2}, \qquad
\rho(h) = \phi^h \quad (h \ge 0).
$$

None of these depends on $t$, so the series is weakly stationary. With $c = 2$, $\phi = 0.6$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$, the
mean is $2 / 0.4 = 5$, the variance is $1 / (1 - 0.36) = 1.5625$, and the autocorrelations are $0.6$, $0.36$,
$0.216, \dots$ at lags 1, 2, 3.

With $\phi = 1$ the geometric series diverge. The same recursion started at $x_0 = 0$ gives
$\var(x_t) = t\sigma^2$, which grows with $t$. That series is a <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>, and it is not stationary
(<NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white noise and random walks</NoteLink>).

## Ergodicity

Stationarity says that the moments are the same at every time. It does not say that one realisation reveals them. Let
$Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and set $x_t = Z$ for all $t$. The series is strictly stationary, but the time average
$\bar x_n = \frac1n\sum_{t=1}^n x_t$ equals $Z$ for every $n$ and never approaches the mean 0.

A stationary series is **mean-ergodic** when $\bar x_n \to \mu$ in mean square. The variance of the time average is

$$
\var(\bar x_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{s=1}^n\sum_{t=1}^n \gamma(s - t) = \frac1n\sum_{\abs{h} < n}\paren{1 - \frac{\abs h}{n}}\gamma(h).
$$

The double sum has $n - \abs h$ pairs at lag $h$, which gives the second form. If $\gamma(h) \to 0$ as
$h \to \infty$, the Cesàro average $\frac1n\sum_{\abs h < n}(1 - \abs h/n)\gamma(h)$ tends to 0, so
$\var(\bar x_n) \to 0$ and the series is mean-ergodic <Cite id="brockwell1991" />. In the constant example,
$\gamma(h) = 1$ at every lag and the condition fails. If the autocovariances are absolutely summable,
$n\var(\bar x_n) \to \sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma(h)$. For the <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">AR(1)</NoteLink> example this long-run variance is
$\sigma^2/(1 - \phi)^2$, larger than $\gamma(0)$ whenever $\phi > 0$: positively correlated data carry less information
about the mean than the same number of independent values.

## Checking stationarity in data

Stationarity is a property of a model, not of a finite record, so it cannot be verified from data. Common evidence
against it:

- A trend in the level, or a spread that grows with the level. A log transform often stabilises the spread, and
  differencing removes a stochastic trend (<NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">differencing and unit roots</NoteLink>).
- Seasonality: the mean depends on the time of year, so $\expect[x_t]$ is not constant.
- A sample autocorrelation function that decays very slowly, which is typical of a unit root
  (<NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation">ACF and PACF</NoteLink>).
- Structural breaks, where the mean or variance changes at a point in time.

Unit-root tests (Dickey–Fuller) and stationarity tests (KPSS) formalise the first check for trends
<Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

## Assumptions

- Weak stationarity requires finite second moments.
- Most estimators for stationary series also assume ergodicity, so that time averages converge to the corresponding
  expectations.

## Pitfalls

- **A constant sample mean is not stationarity.** A random walk has a constant mean of 0 but a variance that grows
  without bound.
- **Cyclic is not seasonal.** A stationary AR(2) process can show irregular cycles. Cycles of variable length are
  compatible with stationarity; a fixed calendar pattern is not <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.
- **<NoteLink to="volatility-clustering">Conditional heteroscedasticity</NoteLink>.** A series whose variance changes over time given its past, as in <NoteLink to="garch-model">GARCH models</NoteLink>,
  can still be weakly stationary, because its unconditional variance is constant <Cite id="hamilton1994" />.
