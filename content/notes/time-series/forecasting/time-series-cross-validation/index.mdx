<Definition>
**Time-series cross-validation**, also called rolling-origin evaluation or backtesting, measures forecast accuracy by
repeating a forecast from many **origins** $n_1 < n_2 < \dots < n_F$. At origin $n$, the model is fitted to
observations up to time $n$ and forecasts $y_{n+1}, \dots, y_{n+h}$. The errors at each horizon $1, \dots, h$ are
averaged over the origins <Cite id="tashman2000" />. The training data always precede the test data.
</Definition>

## Problem

Standard $k$-fold cross-validation assigns observations to folds at random. For a time series that has two
consequences.

- **Training on the future.** A fold's model is fitted on observations after the ones it is tested on. In deployment
  the future is never available, so the estimate is optimistic for any model that exploits trends, seasonality or
  slowly moving levels.
- **Dependence across the split.** Neighbouring observations are correlated. A test point whose neighbours on both
  sides are in the training set is easier to predict than a genuine future value.

A single train–test split at one date avoids both problems but gives one noisy estimate that depends on what happened
in that one test period. Rolling-origin evaluation keeps the time order and averages over many periods.

## Mechanism

Choose an initial training size $n_1$, a step $s$ between origins, a forecast horizon $h$ and a window type. The
origins are $n_1, n_1 + s, n_1 + 2s, \dots$, up to the last origin $n_F$ with $n_F + h \le T$. The number of folds is

$$
F = \left\lfloor \frac{T - n_1 - h}{s} \right\rfloor + 1.
$$

At each origin $n$:

1. Fit the model to the training window: $y_1, \dots, y_n$ for an **expanding window**, or
   $y_{n-w+1}, \dots, y_n$ for a **sliding window** of fixed width $w$.
2. Forecast $\hat y_{n+j \mid n}$ for $j = 1, \dots, h$.
3. Record the errors $e_{n,j} = y_{n+j} - \hat y_{n+j \mid n}$, or a score of the full predictive distribution.

The accuracy at horizon $j$ is the average of a loss over origins, for example $\frac1F\sum_{f=1}^F \abs{e_{n_f, j}}$.
Reporting accuracy separately for each horizon matters, because errors almost always grow with $j$
<Cite id="hyndman2021" />. Scale-free measures such as
<NoteLink to="scaled-errors-and-mean-absolute-scaled-error">MASE</NoteLink> allow averaging over several series; interval and distribution
forecasts are scored with <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">the interval score and CRPS</NoteLink>.

<CvFolds />

**Expanding or sliding.** An expanding window uses all available history, which lowers the variance of the fitted
parameters. A sliding window discards old data, which lets the model track a changing process and makes every fold's
training set the same size, so errors are comparable across folds.

**A gap between training and test.** Features built from lags or rolling statistics, and targets defined over several
periods (such as a 7-day total), can make the last training rows overlap the first test rows. Leaving a gap of $g$
observations after each origin removes that overlap, at the price of testing horizons $g + 1$ to $g + h$.

**When random folds are valid.** For a purely <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss>, in which $y_t$ is regressed on a fixed set of its
own lags and the errors are uncorrelated, ordinary $k$-fold cross-validation over the rows (target and lagged
features) gives valid estimates of the forecast error, and it uses the data more efficiently than rolling origins
<Cite id="bergmeir2018" />. The condition fails when the model is misspecified and its residuals are autocorrelated,
so the residuals should be checked.

## Sources of leakage

Leakage is any path by which information from the test period reaches the fitted model.

- **Preprocessing on the full series.** Scaling, detrending, imputation or outlier thresholds computed on the whole
  series use test-period statistics. Recompute them inside each training window.
- **Centred smoothing.** A centred moving average or a two-sided filter at time $t$ uses values after $t$. Use trailing
  windows for features.
- **Tuning on the test folds.** Choosing hyperparameters by the backtest error and then reporting that error is
  optimistic. Nest a second rolling-origin split inside each training window, or hold out the final period entirely.
- **Revised data.** Economic and business data are often revised after first release. A backtest on the final revised
  values uses information that was unavailable at the forecast origin. Use the data vintage available at each origin
  when it exists.
- **Future covariates.** An exogenous regressor that is known only after the fact, such as realised temperature, must
  be replaced by its forecast when evaluating a real forecast.

## Worked example

A series has $T = 100$ observations. With $n_1 = 60$, $h = 5$ and $s = 5$, the number of folds is
$\lfloor (100 - 60 - 5)/5 \rfloor + 1 = 8$, at origins $60, 65, \dots, 95$. The last fold trains on
$y_1, \dots, y_{95}$ and tests on $y_{96}, \dots, y_{100}$. Each horizon $j$ is scored on 8 forecasts, 40 test
forecasts in total. With $s = 1$ there would be 36 folds and 36 forecasts per horizon, at the price of 36 model fits.

## Cost

The model is refitted $F$ times. With an expanding window, the total training data processed is
$\sum_f n_f$, roughly $F\bar n$ for a mean training size $\bar n$. Three measures reduce the cost:

- a larger step $s$ between origins;
- refitting only every few origins and updating the forecasts with the new observations in between, which is cheap for
  state-space and exponential-smoothing models;
- fixing hyperparameters found on the earliest folds.

## Trade-offs

- **Few folds, high variance.** A long seasonal period or long horizon leaves few origins. Accuracy estimates from a
  handful of folds have wide uncertainty; compare models on the same folds and look at paired differences.
- **Early folds are unrepresentative.** With an expanding window, the first folds train on much less data than the
  final model will. Weight or exclude them when the model is data-hungry.
- **<NoteLink to="stationarity">Stationarity</NoteLink>.** Averaging over origins assumes that accuracy is comparable across periods. A <NoteLink to="change-point-detection">regime change</NoteLink> inside
  the backtest mixes two different error distributions.
