<Definition>
A **probabilistic forecast** of $y_{T+h}$ made at time $T$ is a predictive distribution $F_{T+h \mid T}$ for the future
value, rather than a single number <Cite id="gneiting2014" />. It can be given as a parametric distribution, as a set of
quantiles $q_{\tau_1} < \dots < q_{\tau_K}$ at levels $\tau_k \in (0, 1)$, or as sample paths drawn from a
simulation. A point forecast and a prediction interval are summaries of it.
</Definition>

## Why a distribution

Decisions depend on more than the <NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink>. A retailer sets stock at a high quantile of demand, because running
out costs more than holding a spare unit. A grid operator plans reserves for the upper tail of load. Both need
quantiles, and different decisions need different quantiles of the same forecast. A predictive distribution supplies
all of them at once, and it also states how much the forecaster knows: a wide distribution warns that the point
forecast is unreliable.

## Calibration and sharpness

Gneiting and co-authors state the goal as **maximising sharpness subject to calibration** <Cite id="gneiting2014" />.

- **Calibration** is statistical consistency between the forecasts and the outcomes. Events forecast with probability
  $p$ happen with frequency $p$; 90% intervals contain 90% of outcomes; the $\tau$-quantile is exceeded a fraction
  $1 - \tau$ of the time.
- **Sharpness** is the concentration of the forecast distributions. It is a property of the forecasts alone. Among
  calibrated forecasters, the sharper one is more useful.

The climatological forecast, the same unconditional distribution every time, is calibrated but not sharp. A forecast
that is sharp but miscalibrated is overconfident.

**The <Gloss name="probability-integral-transform">probability integral transform</Gloss> checks calibration.** If $y$ is drawn from a continuous distribution $F$, then
$F(y)$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$, because $\prob(F(y) \le u) = \prob(y \le F^{-1}(u)) = F(F^{-1}(u)) = u$. So if the
forecasts are the true <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distributions</NoteLink>, the values $u_t = F_{t \mid t-h}(y_t)$ over many forecasts are
uniform. A histogram of the $u_t$ diagnoses the failure: a U shape means the forecasts are too narrow, a hump means too
wide, and a slope means biased.

## Quantile forecasts and the pinball loss

A quantile forecast $q$ at level $\tau$ is scored by the **pinball loss**

$$
\rho_\tau(y - q) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y - q) & y \ge q, \\ (1 - \tau)(q - y) & y < q. \end{cases}
$$

An outcome above the forecast costs $\tau$ per unit and one below costs $1 - \tau$ per unit. For $\tau = 0.9$,
under-forecasting is nine times as expensive as over-forecasting, which pushes the forecast up.

**The expected pinball loss is minimised at the $\tau$-quantile.** Let $Y$ have a continuous distribution $F$ with
density $f$. The expected loss of a forecast $q$ is

$$
L(q) = \expect[\rho_\tau(Y - q)] = \tau\int_q^\infty (y - q) f(y)\,dy + (1 - \tau)\int_{-\infty}^q (q - y) f(y)\,dy.
$$

Differentiate under the integral sign. The integrands vanish at $y = q$, so the boundary terms are zero, and

$$
L'(q) = -\tau\int_q^\infty f(y)\,dy + (1 - \tau)\int_{-\infty}^q f(y)\,dy = -\tau(1 - F(q)) + (1 - \tau)F(q) =
F(q) - \tau.
$$

$L'(q)$ is negative for $F(q) < \tau$ and positive for $F(q) > \tau$, so $L$ is convex with its minimum where
$F(q) = \tau$: at the $\tau$-quantile $F^{-1}(\tau)$. The pinball loss is therefore a consistent scoring function for
quantiles, and minimising its sample average over a model's parameters estimates conditional quantiles. This is
quantile regression <Cite id="koenker1978" />. At $\tau = 0.5$ the loss is half the absolute error, and the minimiser is
the median.

A model that outputs several quantiles, such as the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles, is trained on the sum of their
pinball losses. Averaged over all levels $\tau \in (0, 1)$, twice the pinball loss equals the
<NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">continuous ranked probability score</NoteLink>, so a set of quantile forecasts
is scored as a whole by the average pinball loss over its levels <Cite id="gneiting2007" />.

## Producing probabilistic forecasts

- **Parametric models.** <Gloss name="arima" />, exponential smoothing and <Gloss name="ssm">state-space models</Gloss> give a Gaussian predictive distribution
  whose variance grows with the horizon, the basis of analytic
  <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink>. Count data use Poisson or <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink>
  predictive distributions.
- **Simulation.** Running a fitted model forward with resampled or simulated shocks gives sample paths. Paths preserve
  the dependence across horizons, which is needed for totals over several periods.
- **Direct quantile models.** Gradient-boosted trees or neural networks trained on the pinball loss output quantiles
  directly, with no distributional assumption <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

## Worked example

Suppose $Y \sim \Gauss(10, 2^2)$ and a forecaster must give the 0.9 quantile.

- The optimal forecast is $10 + 2 \times 1.2816 = 12.56$, with expected pinball loss $0.351$.
- Forecasting the median, $q = 10$, gives expected loss $0.798$, more than twice as large: at $\tau = 0.9$ the loss
  penalises the frequent outcomes above 10 heavily.
- Over-shooting to $q = 14$ gives $0.417$. The loss rises more slowly above the quantile than below it, because each
  unit of over-forecast costs only $1 - \tau = 0.1$.

For a Gaussian the expected loss has the closed form
$L(q) = \tau(\mu - q) + \sigma\paren{\phi(z) + z\,\Phi(z)}$ with $z = (q - \mu)/\sigma$, where $\phi$ and $\Phi$ are the
standard-normal density and distribution function.

## Pitfalls

- **Quantile crossing.** Separately estimated quantiles can cross, giving a 90% quantile below the 80% one. Sort them,
  or constrain the model to produce increasing quantiles.
- **Coverage is not enough.** An interval can reach its nominal coverage by being wide at calm times and narrow at
  volatile ones in the wrong proportions. Check calibration conditionally, for example by season or by forecast
  level, and score with a proper scoring rule.
- **Aggregating quantiles.** The sum of 90% quantiles of several series is not the 90% quantile of their sum unless
  the series are perfectly dependent. Aggregate sample paths instead.
- **Point summaries.** The mean of a predictive distribution minimises squared error and the median minimises
  absolute error. Report the summary that matches the loss used for evaluation.
