<Definition>
Given observations $y_1, \dots, y_T$, a **$(1 - \alpha)$ prediction interval** for the value $h$ steps ahead is a pair
of statistics $[\ell_{T+h}, u_{T+h}]$ such that

$$
\prob\paren{\ell_{T+h} \le y_{T+h} \le u_{T+h}} = 1 - \alpha.
$$

It describes the uncertainty of a future observation, which includes the noise still to come. A <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink>
describes the uncertainty of a fixed parameter and is narrower.
</Definition>

## Analytic intervals for linear models

Many forecasting models, including <Gloss name="arima" /> and linear exponential smoothing, can be written with future values as a
linear function of future shocks. If the shocks $\varepsilon_t$ are independent with variance $\sigma^2$, the $h$-step
forecast error has the form

$$
y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h \mid T} = \sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}, \qquad \psi_0 = 1,
$$

where the weights $\psi_j$ are the model's moving-average ($\psi$) weights, as in the <NoteLink to="autoregressive-moving-average-model">ARMA model</NoteLink>. The shocks after time $T$ are independent,
so the forecast error variance is the sum of their variances:

$$
\sigma_h^2 = \sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1} \psi_j^2.
$$

If the shocks are Gaussian, the forecast error is Gaussian, and the interval is

$$
\hat y_{T+h \mid T} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_h,
$$

with $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ the standard-normal quantile: $1.28$ for 80% and $1.96$ for 95% <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

Two special cases show the range of behaviour.

- **<NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">AR(1)</NoteLink>**, $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ with $\abs{\phi} < 1$. Substituting the model into itself gives
  $\psi_j = \phi^j$, so $\sigma_h^2 = \sigma^2(1 - \phi^{2h})/(1 - \phi^2)$. The interval widens with $h$ and
  approaches the stationary variance $\sigma^2/(1 - \phi^2)$.
- **<NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">Random walk</NoteLink>**, $\phi = 1$. Every $\psi_j = 1$, so $\sigma_h^2 = h\sigma^2$. The interval widens like $\sqrt h$ without
  bound.

## What the analytic interval leaves out

The formula treats the model and its parameters as known. In practice $\sigma$, the coefficients and the model form
are all estimated. Parameter uncertainty adds a term of order $1/T$ to the variance, which matters for short series
and long horizons. Model uncertainty is usually larger and is not captured by any single-model formula. Empirical
coverage of nominal 95% intervals is often well below 95% for this reason <Cite id="hyndman2021" />. The Gaussian
assumption also fails for heavy-tailed or skewed shocks, and constant $\sigma$ fails when volatility clusters, as
modelled by <NoteLink to="garch-model">GARCH</NoteLink>.

## Bootstrapped intervals

The residual <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> replaces the Gaussian assumption with the empirical distribution of the fitted residuals
$\hat\varepsilon_1, \dots, \hat\varepsilon_T$ <Cite id="thombs1990" />:

1. Simulate a future path by running the fitted model forward from time $T$, drawing each shock with replacement from
   the residuals.
2. Repeat $B$ times, for example $B = 1000$.
3. At each horizon $h$, take the $\alpha/2$ and $1 - \alpha/2$ empirical quantiles of the simulated values.

The intervals inherit the skewness and tail weight of the residuals, and they apply to nonlinear models whose forecast
distribution has no closed form. To include parameter uncertainty, each replicate also refits the model to a
bootstrap series generated from the fitted model, and simulates the future from the refitted parameters
<Cite id="thombs1990" />.

## Conformal intervals

Split conformal prediction gives a finite-sample coverage guarantee without distributional assumptions
<Cite id="vovk2005" /> <Cite id="lei2018" />. Hold out $n$ calibration points that the model did not see, with
absolute errors $s_1, \dots, s_n$, called conformity scores. Let $s_{(k)}$ be the $k$-th smallest, with
$k = \lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha) \rceil$. The interval for a new point is $\hat y \pm s_{(k)}$.

**Coverage under exchangeability.** Suppose the $n$ calibration scores and the new point's score $s_{n+1}$ are exchangeable:
their joint distribution does not change when they are permuted. Then $s_{n+1}$ is equally likely to occupy each of
the $n + 1$ rank positions, ignoring ties. The new value falls inside the interval exactly when $s_{n+1} \le s_{(k)}$,
which happens when its rank among all $n + 1$ scores is at most $k$. That has probability at least
$k/(n + 1) \ge 1 - \alpha$.

Time series are not exchangeable: errors are autocorrelated, and their scale drifts. The guarantee then holds only
approximately. Practical versions calibrate separately for each horizon on rolling-origin errors from
<NoteLink to="time-series-cross-validation">time-series cross-validation</NoteLink>, and use recent errors or
weighting to track changes in scale.

## Worked example

An AR(1) with $\phi = 0.8$, $\sigma = 1$ and mean zero is observed at $y_T = 3$. The point forecasts are
$\hat y_{T+h} = 0.8^h \times 3$ and the standard deviations are $\sigma_h = \sqrt{(1 - 0.64^h)/0.36}$.

| $h$ | Forecast | $\sigma_h$ | 95% interval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2.40 | 1.00 | $[0.44, 4.36]$ |
| 2 | 1.92 | 1.28 | $[-0.59, 4.43]$ |
| 5 | 0.98 | 1.57 | $[-2.10, 4.07]$ |
| 10 | 0.32 | 1.66 | $[-2.93, 3.57]$ |

As $h$ grows the interval approaches the unconditional interval $0 \pm 1.96 \times 1.67 = \pm 3.27$: the point
forecast $0.8^h \times 3$ tends to the mean zero.

## Pitfalls

- **Evaluating coverage.** Check the proportion of held-out values inside the intervals, separately for each horizon.
  Coverage alone rewards wide intervals, so also score width with the
  <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">interval score</NoteLink>.
- **Transformations.** An interval computed on $\log y$ and back-transformed is a valid interval for $y$, because
  quantiles are preserved by monotone maps. The back-transformed point forecast is the median, not the mean.
- **Simultaneous coverage.** A 95% interval at each of $h$ horizons does not contain the whole future path with
  probability 95%. Path-wise statements need simulated paths.
