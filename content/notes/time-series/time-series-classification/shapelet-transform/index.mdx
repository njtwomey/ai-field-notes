<Definition>
Given training series $T_1, \dots, T_N$ and a set of $k$ <NoteLink to="shapelets">shapelets</NoteLink>
$S_1, \dots, S_k$, the shapelet transform maps series $T_j$ to the vector

$$
\xvec_j = \paren{d(T_j, S_1), \dots, d(T_j, S_k)} \in \reals^k,
$$

where $d(T, S)$ is the subsequence distance, the smallest distance between $S$ and any window of $T$ of the same
length. The shapelets are the $k$ candidate subsequences of the training data that score best under a class
separation measure. The transformed data is an ordinary $N \times k$ feature table <Cite id="lines2012" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The original shapelet classifier embeds the search inside a <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink>
<Cite id="ye2009" />. Each node runs a full search for one shapelet, on the subset of data that reaches it. Three
problems follow.

- A tree uses one shapelet per node, and deep nodes see few series, so their shapelets are chosen from little data.
- Decision trees are high-variance classifiers. Replacing the tree with a stronger classifier means redesigning the
  search.
- A class may be recognised by the combination of several patterns, none of which separates the classes alone.

## Mechanism

The transform performs one search over the whole training set and keeps the best $k$ shapelets instead of the single
best one <Cite id="lines2012" />.

1. **Enumerate candidates.** Every subsequence of every training series, for lengths in a range
   $[m_{\min}, m_{\max}]$, is a candidate.
2. **Score each candidate.** Compute its distance to every training series and measure how well those $N$ distances
   separate the classes.
3. **Remove self-similar candidates.** Candidates from the same series that overlap in time are near-copies. For each
   series, only the best of a group of overlapping candidates is kept.
4. **Keep the top $k$.** Sort the remaining candidates by score and keep the $k$ best.
5. **Transform.** Compute $\xvec_j$ for every training and test series, and train any classifier on the vectors.

The quality measure need not be <NoteLink to="mutual-information">information gain</NoteLink>. Hills et al. compared
information gain with three statistics of the distances grouped by class: the one-way <Gloss name="anova" />
F-statistic, the Kruskal–Wallis statistic and Mood's median statistic <Cite id="hills2014" />. For $C$ classes, class
$c$ holding $N_c$ distances with mean $\bar d_c$, and overall mean $\bar d$, the F-statistic is the ratio of
between-class to within-class variance:

$$
F = \frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C} N_c \paren{\bar d_c - \bar d}^2 / (C - 1)}
{\sum_{c=1}^{C} \sum_{j \in c} \paren{d_j - \bar d_c}^2 / (N - C)}.
$$

The F-statistic needs no threshold search and handles any number of classes directly. It is therefore cheaper to
compute than information gain, which must try every threshold on the orderline.

## Worked example

Take six series of length 6, three per class, and two candidate shapelets $S_1 = (0, 2, 0)$ and
$S_2 = (2, 2, 2)$, with raw Euclidean distances.

| Series | Values           | Class | $d(T, S_1)$ | $d(T, S_2)$ |
| ------ | ---------------- | ----- | ----------- | ----------- |
| $T_1$  | 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0 | 1     | 0.000       | 2.828       |
| $T_2$  | 1, 0, 2, 1, 0, 0 | 1     | 1.000       | 2.236       |
| $T_3$  | 0, 0, 0, 1, 2, 0 | 1     | 1.000       | 2.236       |
| $T_4$  | 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0 | 0     | 1.414       | 1.732       |
| $T_5$  | 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2 | 0     | 2.828       | 0.000       |
| $T_6$  | 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 | 0     | 2.000       | 3.464       |

The last two columns are the transformed data, a $6 \times 2$ table. For $S_1$, the class means are $0.667$ and
$2.081$, and the F-statistic is $7.16$. For $S_2$, the class-0 distances spread from $0$ to $3.464$, and the
F-statistic is $0.47$. $S_1$ is ranked first. A threshold on the $S_1$ column alone classifies all six series
correctly.

## Cost

Discovery has the cost of exhaustive shapelet search: $O(N^2 n^4)$ for $N$ series of length $n$ over all lengths.
The transform itself costs $O(k \, n \, m)$ per series for shapelets of length $m$. Implementations reduce discovery
cost by sampling candidates at random or by searching for a fixed time budget, which in practice loses little
accuracy because many candidates are near-duplicates.

## Trade-offs

- **Flexibility.** Discovery and classification are independent. Trained with an ensemble of standard classifiers, the
  shapelet transform was among the most accurate single-representation methods in the 2017
  <NoteLink to="time-series-classification-benchmarks">bake off</NoteLink> <Cite id="bagnall2017" />. It is the shapelet
  module of HIVE-COTE <Cite id="lines2018" />.
- **Interpretability.** A tree over one or two shapelets can be read directly. A classifier over hundreds of shapelet
  features is harder to explain, although the features themselves remain pieces of real data.
- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> of features.** Each shapelet is scored alone, so two shapelets
  that are only informative together can both be missed. <NoteLink to="learned-shapelets">Learned shapelets</NoteLink>
  optimise the set jointly instead.
