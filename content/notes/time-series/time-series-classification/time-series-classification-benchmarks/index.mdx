<Definition>
A time-series classification benchmark is a fixed collection of labelled datasets, each with a train and test split,
on which classifiers are compared by accuracy. The standard collection for univariate series is the UCR time-series
archive, which holds 128 datasets since its 2018 expansion from 85 <Cite id="dau2019" />. Results over many datasets
are summarised by each classifier's average rank and tested with rank-based statistics.
</Definition>

## Intuition

No single time-series classifier is best on every problem. A method built for local shapes wins on some datasets, a
method built for frequencies on others. A claim that one method is better therefore needs many datasets, a fixed
protocol and a test that accounts for the variation across datasets. A shared archive makes results from different
papers comparable, and a shared baseline makes it clear whether a new method adds anything.

## The UCR archive

The UCR archive was started by Keogh's group at the University of California, Riverside, and is maintained with
collaborators including Bagnall's group at the University of East Anglia <Cite id="dau2019" />. Each dataset holds
univariate series from one domain: sensor readings, image outlines turned into series, spectrograms, motion capture,
electrocardiograms and simulated data. Each has a fixed train and test split, so that published accuracies can be
compared directly.

For every dataset the archive reports the error of three baselines: 1-NN with Euclidean distance, 1-NN with
unconstrained <Gloss name="dtw">dynamic time warping</Gloss>, and 1-NN with dynamic time warping in a window learned on the training data
<Cite id="dau2019" />. The 2018 expansion added 43 datasets.

## The bake off

The great time-series classification bake off re-implemented 18 recently published algorithms in one Java framework
and ran each on the 85 datasets of the archive at the time <Cite id="bagnall2017" />. Instead of the single
fixed split, every dataset was resampled into 100 random train and test splits of the original sizes. Averaging over
resamples reduces the risk that a method looks good because of one lucky split.

The two baselines were 1-NN with dynamic time warping (DTW) in a cross-validated window and a rotation forest on the raw values. Only 9 of the
18 algorithms were significantly more accurate than both baselines. The collective of transformation-based ensembles
(COTE), which combines classifiers built on several representations of the series, was significantly more accurate
than all the others <Cite id="bagnall2017" />.

The comparison across datasets used ranks. On each dataset the classifiers are ranked by mean accuracy, rank 1 being
best. The Friedman test asks whether the average ranks differ more than chance allows. The post-hoc Nemenyi test then
finds which pairs differ <Cite id="bagnall2017" />. The result is drawn as a critical difference diagram: classifiers
on an axis of average rank, with bars joining groups whose ranks are not significantly different.

## Worked example

Three classifiers, with made-up accuracies on $N = 6$ datasets, are ranked on each dataset:

| Dataset      | Accuracy A | Accuracy B | Accuracy C | Ranks (A, B, C) |
| ------------ | ---------- | ---------- | ---------- | --------------- |
| 1            | 0.80       | 0.85       | 0.90       | 3, 2, 1         |
| 2            | 0.70       | 0.72       | 0.71       | 3, 1, 2         |
| 3            | 0.95       | 0.96       | 0.99       | 3, 2, 1         |
| 4            | 0.60       | 0.58       | 0.66       | 2, 3, 1         |
| 5            | 0.88       | 0.91       | 0.93       | 3, 2, 1         |
| 6            | 0.75       | 0.80       | 0.79       | 3, 1, 2         |
| Average rank |            |            |            | 2.833, 1.833, 1.333 |

With $k = 3$ classifiers and average ranks $\bar R_1, \dots, \bar R_k$, the Friedman statistic is

$$
\chi^2_F = \frac{12 N}{k (k + 1)} \paren{\sum_{c=1}^{k} \bar R_c^2 - \frac{k (k + 1)^2}{4}}
= \frac{72}{12} \paren{8.028 + 3.361 + 1.778 - 12} = 7.00.
$$

Under the <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> of equal ranks, $\chi^2_F$ follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $k - 1 = 2$ degrees of
freedom, which gives $p = 0.030$. The Nemenyi critical difference at the 5% level is

$$
\mathrm{CD} = q_{0.05} \sqrt{\frac{k (k + 1)}{6 N}} = 2.343 \times \sqrt{\frac{12}{36}} = 1.353,
$$

where $q_{0.05} = 2.343$ is the critical value of the studentised range for $k = 3$, divided by $\sqrt{2}$. A and C
differ in average rank by $1.5 > 1.353$, so C is significantly better than A. The other two differences, $1.0$ and
$0.5$, are not significant. With so few datasets, even a consistent winner is hard to separate from the rest.

## HIVE-COTE

The hierarchical vote collective of transformation-based ensembles (HIVE-COTE) <Cite id="lines2018" /> reorganised
COTE. The flat version pooled 35 classifiers over four representations. HIVE-COTE builds one ensemble module per
representation and combines the modules by a weighted vote of their class probabilities. Each module's weight is set
from its accuracy estimated on the training data. It uses five representations.

| Module                                | Representation         | What it captures                                           |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Elastic ensemble                      | Whole series           | Similarity under DTW and other elastic distances           |
| <NoteLink to="shapelet-transform">Shapelet transform</NoteLink>                    | Shapelet distances     | Local shapes anywhere in the series                        |
| Bag of SFA symbols (BOSS)             | Dictionary of words    | Frequency of short repeated patterns                       |
| Time series forest (TSF)              | Interval statistics    | Mean, spread and slope of fixed intervals                  |
| Random interval spectral ensemble     | <NoteLink to="spectral-features">Spectral features</NoteLink>      | Frequency content of random intervals                      |

HIVE-COTE was significantly more accurate than flat COTE and than all other classifiers its authors knew of, over 100
resamples of the 85 datasets <Cite id="lines2018" />. Its cost is high: the elastic ensemble and the shapelet
transform scale badly with series length and training-set size, so HIVE-COTE is impractical for large datasets.

## ROCKET

ROCKET (random convolutional kernel transform) <Cite id="dempster2020" /> reaches accuracy comparable to HIVE-COTE at
a small fraction of the cost. It applies 10,000 random one-dimensional <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> kernels to each series and trains a
linear classifier on two summary features per kernel. Each kernel's parameters are drawn at random and never trained:

- **Length** is 7, 9 or 11 with equal probability.
- **Weights** are drawn from $\Gauss(0, 1)$ and then centred to mean 0.
- **Bias** is drawn from $\Unif(-1, 1)$.
- **Dilation** is $\lfloor 2^x \rfloor$ with $x \sim \Unif(0, A)$, where $A = \log_2 \paren{(\ell_{\text{input}} - 1) /
  (\ell_{\text{kernel}} - 1)}$ for the series length $\ell_{\text{input}}$ and the kernel length $\ell_{\text{kernel}}$.
  The dilated kernel can then cover up to the whole series, so kernels respond to patterns at many scales.
- **Padding** is used or not with equal probability.

Each kernel's output series is reduced to its maximum and to the proportion of positive values (ppv), giving 20,000
features. A <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge-regression</NoteLink> classifier with cross-validated regularisation is trained on them. Restricted to one <Gloss name="cpu" />
core, training and testing on all 85 bake-off datasets took 1 hour 50 minutes, against more than 11 days for each of
two other state-of-the-art methods. ROCKET learned from 1 million series in 1 hour 15 minutes <Cite id="dempster2020" />.

The random kernels play the role that learned features play elsewhere. A kernel with a given dilation responds to a
pattern at one scale, the bias sets which responses count as positive, and ppv measures how much of the series
contains the pattern. The linear classifier selects the few useful kernels from the many random ones.

## Pitfalls

- **Overfitting the archive.** Methods are designed and tuned while their authors look at archive results, so the
  archive is not a truly held-out test. A gain of a fraction of a rank on the archive may not transfer to new data.
- **Fixed split versus resamples.** Accuracies on the single published split vary a lot for small datasets.
  Resampling gives more stable comparisons but differs from the numbers other papers report.
- **Average rank hides magnitudes.** A method can have a better average rank while losing badly on the datasets that
  matter for an application. Pairwise win counts and per-dataset accuracy complement the rank.
- **Nemenyi is conservative.** With many classifiers the critical difference grows, and genuine differences go
  undetected. Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with a <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple-testing</NoteLink> correction are a common alternative.
- **Univariate only.** The UCR archive holds univariate series of mostly equal length. Multivariate, variable-length
  and streaming problems need other collections and can change the ranking.
