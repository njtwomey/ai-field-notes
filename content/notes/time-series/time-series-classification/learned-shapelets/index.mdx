<Definition>
Learned <NoteLink to="shapelets">shapelets</NoteLink> <Cite id="grabocka2014" /> classify a time series $T_i$ of length $n$ with a linear model on $K$
shapelet features,

$$
\hat y_i = w_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k M_{i,k},
$$

where $M_{i,k}$ is a differentiable approximation to the distance between series $T_i$ and shapelet
$S_k = (s_{k,1}, \dots, s_{k,L})$. The shapelet values $s_{k,l}$ and the weights $w_{k}$ are fitted together by
<Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> on the logistic loss. The shapelets need not be subsequences of the training data.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Search-based methods choose shapelets from a finite pool: the subsequences of the training series. The best shape for
separating the classes may lie between those candidates, for example the average of several noisy occurrences of a
pattern. Treating the shapelet values as continuous parameters lets an optimiser move each shapelet towards that
shape.

The obstacle is the minimum over positions in the subsequence distance. The minimum is piecewise differentiable, and
its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> reaches only the single best-matching window. Grabocka et al. replace it with a soft-minimum, a weighted
average of the distances at every position that puts almost all its weight on the smallest ones.

## The model

For series $T_i = (t_{i,1}, \dots, t_{i,n})$ and shapelet $S_k$ of length $L$, the distance at position $j$ is the
mean squared difference over the window,

$$
D_{i,k,j} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \paren{t_{i,j+l-1} - s_{k,l}}^2, \qquad j = 1, \dots, J = n - L + 1.
$$

The feature is the soft-minimum of these $J$ values with sharpness $\alpha < 0$:

$$
M_{i,k} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J} D_{i,k,j} \, e^{\alpha D_{i,k,j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J} e^{\alpha D_{i,k,j}}}
= \sum_{j=1}^{J} \psi_{j} D_{i,k,j}, \qquad
\psi_j = \frac{e^{\alpha D_{i,k,j}}}{\sum_{q=1}^{J} e^{\alpha D_{i,k,q}}}.
$$

The weights $\psi_j$ are a softmax of $\alpha D_{i,k,j}$. They are positive and sum to 1, so $M_{i,k}$ is a weighted
average of the distances and satisfies $\min_j D_{i,k,j} \le M_{i,k}$. As $\alpha \to -\infty$ all the weight moves to
the smallest distance and $M_{i,k} \to \min_j D_{i,k,j}$, the hard minimum.

For binary labels $y_i \in \set{0, 1}$ and the sigmoid $\sigma$, the per-series loss is the logistic loss with an
$\ell_2$ penalty on the weights:

$$
\ell_i = -y_i \log \sigma(\hat y_i) - (1 - y_i) \log\paren{1 - \sigma(\hat y_i)}
+ \frac{\lambda}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k^2,
$$

with $N$ the number of training series and $\lambda \ge 0$. Multi-class problems use one such model per class
(one-versus-all) with shared shapelets <Cite id="grabocka2014" />.

## The gradient

The gradient of $\ell_i$ with respect to one shapelet value $s_{k,l}$ passes through four factors, by the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial s_{k,l}}
= \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial \hat y_i} \,
\frac{\partial \hat y_i}{\partial M_{i,k}}
\sum_{j=1}^{J} \frac{\partial M_{i,k}}{\partial D_{i,k,j}} \,
\frac{\partial D_{i,k,j}}{\partial s_{k,l}}.
$$

Three of the factors are standard. The logistic loss gives $\partial \ell_i / \partial \hat y_i = \sigma(\hat y_i) -
y_i$. The linear model gives $\partial \hat y_i / \partial M_{i,k} = w_k$. The squared distance gives

$$
\frac{\partial D_{i,k,j}}{\partial s_{k,l}} = \frac{2}{L} \paren{s_{k,l} - t_{i,j+l-1}}.
$$

The remaining factor is the derivative of the soft-minimum. Write $D_j$ for $D_{i,k,j}$ and $M$ for $M_{i,k}$. The
softmax weights have derivative $\partial \psi_q / \partial D_j = \alpha \psi_q (\delta_{qj} - \psi_j)$, where
$\delta_{qj}$ is 1 if $q = j$ and 0 otherwise. Differentiating $M = \sum_q \psi_q D_q$ by the product rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial D_j}
= \psi_j + \sum_{q=1}^{J} D_q \, \alpha \psi_q \paren{\delta_{qj} - \psi_j}
= \psi_j + \alpha \psi_j D_j - \alpha \psi_j \sum_{q=1}^{J} \psi_q D_q
= \psi_j \paren{1 + \alpha \paren{D_j - M}}.
$$

The derivative has three properties.

- **It is local.** Each position's gradient is scaled by its weight $\psi_j$. Positions whose distance is far above the
  minimum receive almost no gradient, so the shapelet is pulled towards the windows that best match it.
- **It sums to 1.** $\sum_j \psi_j = 1$ and $\sum_j \psi_j (D_j - M) = 0$, so $\sum_j \partial M / \partial D_j = 1$.
  Adding the same constant to every distance raises $M$ by exactly that constant, as it would raise the hard minimum.
- **It can be negative.** For $D_j > M$ the factor $1 + \alpha(D_j - M)$ is below 1 and becomes negative once
  $D_j - M > 1 / \abs{\alpha}$. Raising a distance that is above the average moves weight towards the smaller
  distances, so $M$ falls.

## Worked example

Take one series and one shapelet with $J = 3$ window distances $D = (0.5, 1.0, 3.0)$. The hard minimum is $0.5$.

| $\alpha$ | $\psi$                  | $M$     | $\partial M / \partial D$     |
| -------- | ----------------------- | ------- | ----------------------------- |
| $-1$     | $(0.592, 0.359, 0.049)$ | $0.801$ | $(0.771, 0.288, -0.058)$      |
| $-4$     | $(0.881, 0.119, 0.000)$ | $0.560$ | $(1.091, -0.091, -0.000)$     |
| $-16$    | $(1.000, 0.000, 0.000)$ | $0.500$ | $(1.002, -0.002, -0.000)$     |

For $\alpha = -4$, $M = 0.5 \times 0.881 + 1.0 \times 0.119 + 3.0 \times 0.000 = 0.560$, which is $0.060$ above the
hard minimum. The derivative with respect to the second distance is $0.119 \times (1 - 4 \times (1.0 - 0.560)) =
-0.091$. In each row the three derivatives sum to 1. As $\alpha$ becomes more negative, $M$ approaches the hard
minimum and the gradient concentrates on the best-matching window.

## Details

- **Initialisation.** The loss is not convex in the shapelets. Grabocka et al. initialise the shapelets with the
  centroids of a $k$-means clustering of all training windows of length $L$, and the weights at random
  <Cite id="grabocka2014" />.
- **Several lengths.** Shapelets of $R$ lengths $L_{\min}, 2 L_{\min}, \dots, R \, L_{\min}$ are learned together,
  with $K$ shapelets per length, and all feed the same linear model.
- **Cost.** One pass over the data costs $O(N \, K \, R \, n \, L)$: every window of every series is compared with
  every shapelet. Search-based discovery is $O(N^2 n^4)$ <Cite id="yeh2016" />, so learning is much cheaper for long
  series, at the price of a local rather than exhaustive optimum.

## Assumptions

- The classes differ in local shapes whose position varies, which is the premise of every shapelet method.
- A linear model on $K$ distances separates the classes. Interactions between shapelets are not modelled.
- Distances are not z-normalised per window, so level and scale are part of the shape. Series are typically
  z-normalised as a whole beforehand.

## Pitfalls

- **The choice of $\alpha$.** A small $\abs{\alpha}$ gives a smooth objective whose feature is far from the minimum.
  A large $\abs{\alpha}$ makes the feature almost the hard minimum, and the gradient reaches only one window, so
  learning slows. $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter. In floating point, the exponentials must be
  computed after subtracting the smallest $\alpha D_j$ to avoid underflow.
- **Interpretability.** A learned shapelet may not resemble any real subsequence. Unlike a searched shapelet
  <Cite id="lines2012" />, it cannot be traced back to a training example.
- **Hyperparameters.** $K$, $L_{\min}$, $R$, $\lambda$, $\alpha$, the <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> and the number of epochs all
  interact, and the method is sensitive to them on small training sets.
