<Definition>
An **autoregressive model of order $p$**, AR($p$), is

$$
x_t = c + \phi_1x_{t-1} + \phi_2x_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_px_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t,
$$

where $c$ is a constant, $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_p$ are real coefficients with $\phi_p \ne 0$, and
$\varepsilon_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$ is white noise. With the backshift operator $B$ ($Bx_t = x_{t-1}$)
and the **autoregressive polynomial** $\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1z - \dots - \phi_pz^p$, the model is
$\phi(B)x_t = c + \varepsilon_t$ <Cite id="box2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An AR model is a linear regression of the current value on the $p$ previous values. The noise term $\varepsilon_t$ is
the part of $x_t$ that the past cannot predict, the **innovation**. Each innovation enters the series once and then
echoes through the recursion. The coefficients decide whether the echo dies away (a stationary series), persists (a
unit root) or grows (an explosive series).

Yule introduced the model in 1927 to describe the sunspot cycle as a damped oscillation driven by random shocks, and
Walker extended it to general order <Cite id="yule1927" /> <Cite id="walker1931" />.

## AR(1): stationarity by substitution

Take $p = 1$ and $c = 0$: $x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$. Substituting the equation into itself $k$ times gives
$x_t = \phi^kx_{t-k} + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}$. If $\abs\phi < 1$, the first term vanishes as
$k \to \infty$ and the sum converges in mean square, so

$$
x_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\phi^j\varepsilon_{t-j}, \qquad \var(x_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - \phi^2}, \qquad \rho(h) = \phi^{\abs h}.
$$

This representation expresses $x_t$ through present and past shocks only, which is called **causal**. It has constant
mean and an autocovariance that depends only on the lag, so the process is stationary. For $\abs\phi = 1$ the series
is a random walk and has no stationary solution. For $\abs\phi > 1$ the recursion explodes from any fixed start.

## AR(p): stationarity from the characteristic roots

The same argument works for any order after factoring the polynomial. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, with
roots $z_1, \dots, z_p$ of $\phi(z)$ (the **characteristic roots**) and $\lambda_i = 1/z_i$,

$$
\phi(z) = (1 - \lambda_1z)(1 - \lambda_2z)\cdots(1 - \lambda_pz).
$$

The model is $\phi(B)x_t = \varepsilon_t$. Each factor $(1 - \lambda_iB)$ is an AR(1) operator. Its inverse is the
geometric series $(1 - \lambda_iB)^{-1} = \sum_{j \ge 0}\lambda_i^jB^j$, which converges when $\abs{\lambda_i} < 1$,
exactly as in the AR(1) case. If every factor can be inverted,

$$
x_t = \phi(B)^{-1}\varepsilon_t = \prod_{i=1}^{p}(1 - \lambda_iB)^{-1}\varepsilon_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j},
$$

a product of convergent power series in $B$ with absolutely summable coefficients $\psi_j$. The result is a causal,
stationary process. So:

**An AR($p$) process has a causal stationary solution if and only if every root of $\phi(z)$ satisfies $\abs z > 1$**,
that is, every $\abs{\lambda_i} < 1$ <Cite id="brockwell1991" />.

The $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of the companion matrix that writes the AR($p$) recursion as a first-order
system in the state $(x_t, \dots, x_{t-p+1})$. They are also the poles of the transfer function $1/\phi(z^{-1})$ in
signal-processing terms, where the same condition reads "all poles inside the unit circle"
(<NoteLink to="poles-and-zeros">poles and zeros</NoteLink>). A root at $z = 1$ is a **unit root**, and the series is
then non-stationary but becomes stationary after differencing
(<NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">differencing and unit roots</NoteLink>).

With a constant $c$, the stationary mean follows from taking expectations of both sides:
$\mu = c + \sum_i\phi_i\mu$, so $\mu = c/(1 - \phi_1 - \dots - \phi_p) = c/\phi(1)$.

## The autocorrelation function

Centre the series so that $c = 0$. Multiply both sides of the model by $x_{t-h}$ for $h \ge 1$ and take expectations.
The innovation $\varepsilon_t$ is uncorrelated with $x_{t-h}$, because a causal $x_{t-h}$ depends only on shocks up to
time $t - h$. So

$$
\gamma(h) = \phi_1\gamma(h - 1) + \dots + \phi_p\gamma(h - p), \qquad h \ge 1.
$$

At $h = 0$ the innovation contributes $\expect[\varepsilon_tx_t] = \sigma^2$, which gives
$\gamma(0) = \phi_1\gamma(1) + \dots + \phi_p\gamma(p) + \sigma^2$.

The autocovariances satisfy the same linear recursion as the series without noise. Its general solution for distinct
roots is $\gamma(h) = \sum_iA_i\lambda_i^h$. Each real $\lambda_i$ contributes an exponential decay (alternating in
sign if $\lambda_i < 0$). A complex pair $\lambda = re^{\pm i\omega}$ contributes a damped cosine
$r^h\cos(\omega h + \theta)$ with period $2\pi/\omega$. The ACF of a stationary AR process therefore **tails off**
geometrically and never cuts off. Its PACF cuts off after lag $p$ (<NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation">ACF and PACF</NoteLink>).

## Yule–Walker estimation

Divide the recursion for $h = 1, \dots, p$ by $\gamma(0)$. The result is $p$ linear equations in $p$ unknowns:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \rho(1) & \cdots & \rho(p-1) \\ \rho(1) & 1 & \cdots & \rho(p-2) \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\
\rho(p-1) & \rho(p-2) & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \phi_1 \\ \phi_2 \\ \vdots \\ \phi_p \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} \rho(1) \\ \rho(2) \\ \vdots \\ \rho(p) \end{bmatrix}.
$$

These are the **Yule–Walker equations**. Read forwards, they give the ACF from the coefficients. Read backwards with
the sample autocorrelations $r_h$ in place of $\rho(h)$, they give the **Yule–Walker estimator** $\hat\phi$, and the
$h = 0$ equation gives $\hat\sigma^2 = \hat\gamma(0)(1 - \hat\phi_1r_1 - \dots - \hat\phi_pr_p)$. The matrix is
Toeplitz, so the Durbin–Levinson recursion solves the system in $O(p^2)$ operations, the same algorithm used in
<NoteLink to="linear-prediction">linear prediction</NoteLink>.

Built from the divisor-$n$ sample autocovariances, the Yule–Walker estimate always defines a stationary model. For
pure AR models it has the same asymptotic distribution as the maximum-likelihood estimator, but in small samples, and
when a root is near the unit circle, it is more biased than least squares or maximum likelihood
<Cite id="brockwell1991" />.

## Worked example: an AR(2) model

Take $x_t = 0.5x_{t-1} + 0.3x_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$ with $\sigma^2 = 1$.

- **Stationarity.** The polynomial $1 - 0.5z - 0.3z^2$ has roots $z = 1.174$ and $z = -2.840$. Both lie outside the
  unit circle, so the process is stationary. The root near 1 means that shocks decay slowly.
- **ACF.** The Yule–Walker equation at $h = 1$ is $\rho(1) = 0.5 + 0.3\rho(1)$, using $\rho(0) = 1$ and
  $\rho(-1) = \rho(1)$. So $\rho(1) = 0.5/0.7 = 0.714$. The recursion then gives
  $\rho(2) = 0.5 \cdot 0.714 + 0.3 = 0.657$ and $\rho(3) = 0.5 \cdot 0.657 + 0.3 \cdot 0.714 = 0.543$.
- **Variance.** $\gamma(0)(1 - 0.5\rho(1) - 0.3\rho(2)) = \sigma^2$ gives $\gamma(0) = 1/(1 - 0.357 - 0.197) = 2.244$.

Now suppose a sample gives $r_1 = 0.6$ and $r_2 = 0.4$. The Yule–Walker estimate of an AR(2) solves
$\hat\phi_1 + 0.6\hat\phi_2 = 0.6$ and $0.6\hat\phi_1 + \hat\phi_2 = 0.4$, so $\hat\phi_1 = 0.5625$ and
$\hat\phi_2 = 0.0625$. The small $\hat\phi_2$, which is also the lag-2 partial autocorrelation, suggests that an AR(1)
would suffice.

A second example has complex roots: $x_t = x_{t-1} - 0.5x_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$. The roots of $1 - z + 0.5z^2$ are
$z = 1 \pm i$, with modulus $\sqrt2 > 1$, so the process is stationary. Their argument is $\omega = \pi/4$, so the ACF
is a damped cosine with period $2\pi/\omega = 8$ steps. The series shows irregular cycles of about 8 steps.

## The AR(2) stationarity region

For $p = 2$ the root condition becomes three linear inequalities:

$$
\phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1, \qquad \phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1, \qquad \abs{\phi_2} < 1.
$$

They define a triangle with vertices $(-2, -1)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(2, -1)$ in the $(\phi_1, \phi_2)$ plane. The parabola
$\phi_1^2 + 4\phi_2 = 0$ splits it. Above the parabola the roots are real and the ACF decays as a mix of two
exponentials. Below it the roots are complex and the ACF oscillates.

<Derivation title="Deriving the triangle">
The reciprocal roots $\lambda = 1/z$ solve $\lambda^2 - \phi_1\lambda - \phi_2 = 0$. Write this as
$p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + a\lambda + b$ with $a = -\phi_1$ and $b = -\phi_2$. Stationarity requires both roots inside
the unit disc.

**Complex roots** ($a^2 < 4b$, so $b > 0$). The roots are conjugates with $\abs\lambda^2 = \lambda\bar\lambda = b$. They
lie inside the disc if and only if $b < 1$. In that case $\abs a < 2\sqrt b < 1 + b$ holds automatically, because
$(1 - \sqrt b)^2 > 0$.

**Real roots** ($a^2 \ge 4b$). The parabola $p$ opens upward, so both roots lie in $(-1, 1)$ if and only if
$p(1) > 0$, $p(-1) > 0$ and the vertex $-a/2$ lies in $(-1, 1)$. The first two conditions are $1 + a + b > 0$ and
$1 - a + b > 0$, that is $\abs a < 1 + b$. Given $\abs a < 1 + b$ and $b < 1$, $\abs a < 2$, so the vertex condition
holds.

In both cases the conditions are $\abs a < 1 + b$ and $b < 1$. The first implies $b > -1$. In terms of $\phi$:
$\abs{\phi_1} < 1 - \phi_2$ and $\abs{\phi_2} < 1$, which are the three inequalities.
</Derivation>

## Forecasting

The minimum mean-squared-error forecast replaces future innovations by 0 and future values by their forecasts:
$\hat x_{T+h \mid T} = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\hat x_{T+h-i \mid T}$, where $\hat x_{s \mid T} = x_s$ for $s \le T$. For
a stationary model the forecasts decay geometrically to the mean $\mu$, and the forecast error variance rises to
$\gamma(0)$. With the causal weights $\psi_j$, the $h$-step error variance is $\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2$.

## Assumptions

- The innovations are white noise, uncorrelated with past values of the series.
- The orders and coefficients are constant over the sample.
- Inference with the usual standard errors assumes the roots are not close to the unit circle.

## Pitfalls

- **Stationarity is not visible in the coefficients alone.** $\phi_1 = 0.6$ and $\phi_2 = 0.5$ are both below 1, but
  $\phi_1 + \phi_2 = 1.1 > 1$ and the process explodes. Check the roots.
- **Cycles do not imply seasonality.** Complex roots produce cycles of variable length. A fixed calendar period needs a
  seasonal model (<NoteLink to="seasonal-autoregressive-integrated-moving-average">seasonal ARIMA</NoteLink>).
- **Order selection by the PACF alone is noisy.** Sample partial autocorrelations beyond lag $p$ fall outside the
  $\pm 1.96/\sqrt n$ band about 5% of the time. Information criteria (AIC, BIC) are more reliable for choosing $p$.
