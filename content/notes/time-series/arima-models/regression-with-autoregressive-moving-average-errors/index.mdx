<Definition>
A **regression with ARMA errors** is

$$
y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1u_{1,t} + \dots + \beta_ku_{k,t} + \eta_t, \qquad \phi(B)\eta_t = \theta(B)\varepsilon_t,
$$

where $u_{1,t}, \dots, u_{k,t}$ are observed external regressors (covariates), $\beta_0, \dots, \beta_k$ are regression
coefficients, and the error $\eta_t$ follows an ARMA($p$, $q$) model driven by <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white noise</NoteLink>
$\varepsilon_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$. When $\eta_t$ is <Gloss name="arima" />($p$, $d$, $q$), the model is a
**regression with ARIMA errors** <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A time-series regression has two sources of structure. The regressors explain part of $y_t$: temperature explains
electricity demand, advertising explains sales, a holiday indicator explains a traffic dip. What they leave unexplained
is rarely white noise. It is autocorrelated, which an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-moving-average-model">ARMA model</NoteLink> describes. The model keeps both. The
$\beta$ coefficients have the ordinary regression meaning, the effect of a unit change in $u_{j,t}$ on $y_t$ with the
other regressors fixed, and the ARMA error supplies the dynamics and the forecast of what the regressors miss.

The name ARIMAX is used for this model and for a different one, in which lagged values of $y_t$ appear as regressors
alongside $u_t$. In that form, $y_t = \beta u_t + \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, the coefficient $\beta$ is only the
immediate effect; the total effect of a permanent unit change in $u$ is $\beta/(1 - \phi)$. The regression-with-errors
form keeps $\beta$ interpretable <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

## Effect of autocorrelated errors on OLS standard errors

Suppose the true error is <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">AR(1)</NoteLink>, $\eta_t = \phi\eta_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, and the model is fitted by <Gloss name="ols">ordinary least
squares (OLS)</Gloss> as if the errors were independent. The OLS estimator is still unbiased when the regressors are
independent of the errors. The variance that OLS reports for it is wrong.

Take one centred regressor $u_t$ and no intercept, so $\hat\beta = \sum_tu_ty_t/\sum_tu_t^2$ and
$\hat\beta - \beta = \sum_tu_t\eta_t/\sum_tu_t^2$. Conditional on the regressor,

$$
\var(\hat\beta) = \frac{\sum_s\sum_tu_su_t\gamma_\eta(s - t)}{\paren{\sum_tu_t^2}^2},
$$

where $\gamma_\eta(h) = \sigma_\eta^2\phi^{\abs h}$ is the error autocovariance. OLS software reports only the $s = t$
terms, $\sigma_\eta^2/\sum_tu_t^2$. Suppose the regressor is itself autocorrelated, with sample autocorrelation
$\rho_u(h) \approx \rho^{\abs h}$. Grouping the double sum by lag $h = s - t$ gives, for large $n$,
$\sum_s\sum_tu_su_t\phi^{\abs{s-t}} \approx \sum_tu_t^2\sum_h\rho^{\abs h}\phi^{\abs h}$. So

$$
\frac{\var(\hat\beta)}{\sigma_\eta^2/\sum_tu_t^2} \approx \sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}(\phi\rho)^{\abs h}
= 1 + \frac{2\phi\rho}{1 - \phi\rho} = \frac{1 + \phi\rho}{1 - \phi\rho}.
$$

When both the regressor and the error are positively autocorrelated, the true variance exceeds the reported one.
Trending regressors and persistent errors are the usual situation in time series, so naive $t$-tests reject far too
often. In the limit $\phi \to 1$ the error is a <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> and the regression becomes spurious
<Cite id="granger1974" />.

## Worked example

Take $\phi = \rho = 0.8$. The variance ratio is $(1 + 0.64)/(1 - 0.64) = 1.64/0.36 = 4.56$. The true <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>
is $\sqrt{4.56} = 2.13$ times the reported one. A coefficient reported with $t = 2.5$ has a correct $t$ of about
$2.5/2.13 = 1.17$, which is not significant.

Forecasting uses both parts. Suppose a fitted model is $y_t = 5 + 2u_t + \eta_t$ with $\eta_t = 0.8\eta_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$,
and the last observation is $y_T = 14$ with $u_T = 4$. The last error is $\eta_T = 14 - 5 - 8 = 1$. With planned values
$u_{T+1} = u_{T+2} = 3$, the forecasts are

$$
\hat y_{T+1} = 5 + 2 \cdot 3 + 0.8 \cdot 1 = 11.8, \qquad \hat y_{T+2} = 5 + 6 + 0.8^2 \cdot 1 = 11.64.
$$

The regression part carries the effect of the planned regressor, and the error part adds a correction, proportional
to the last error $\eta_T$, that decays at rate $\phi$ with each step ahead.

## Estimation

**<Gloss name="gls">Generalised least squares</Gloss>.** For AR(1) errors, subtract $\phi$ times the lagged equation:

$$
y_t - \phi y_{t-1} = \beta_0(1 - \phi) + \beta_1(u_t - \phi u_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_t.
$$

The transformed errors are white noise, so OLS on the **quasi-differenced** data is efficient. The Cochrane–Orcutt
procedure alternates: estimate $\beta$ by OLS, estimate $\phi$ from the residuals, quasi-difference, and repeat until
convergence <Cite id="cochrane1949" />.

**Maximum likelihood.** Modern software estimates $\beta$, $\phi$, $\theta$ and $\sigma^2$ jointly by maximising the
Gaussian likelihood, usually through a state-space form and the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> <Cite id="hamilton1994" />. This handles
any ARMA order, includes the first observations exactly, and gives standard errors for all parameters together.

**Non-stationary errors.** If $\eta_t$ has a <NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">unit root</NoteLink>, the regression is fitted in differences:
$\Delta y_t = \beta_1\Delta u_{1,t} + \dots + \beta_k\Delta u_{k,t} + \Delta\eta_t$, with $\Delta\eta_t$ an ARMA process.
The coefficients keep their meaning, because differencing both sides of a linear equation preserves $\beta$.

## Model building

A standard sequence is <Cite id="hyndman2021" />:

1. Fit the regression with a generous error model, or difference if the series or the regressors are non-stationary.
2. Choose the ARMA orders for the error from the <Gloss name="acf" /> and <Gloss name="pacf" /> of the regression residuals, or by <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information criteria</NoteLink>.
3. Refit jointly and check that the ARMA innovations (not the regression residuals $\hat\eta_t$) are white noise with a
   <NoteLink to="ljung-box-test">Ljung–Box test</NoteLink>.

**Dynamic harmonic regression** uses sine and cosine pairs as regressors to model seasonality:
$u_{2k-1,t} = \sin(2\pi kt/m)$ and $u_{2k,t} = \cos(2\pi kt/m)$ for $k = 1, \dots, K$. A few pairs describe a smooth
seasonal shape with any period, including long ones such as $m = 52$ or $m = 365.25$, where a
<NoteLink to="seasonal-autoregressive-integrated-moving-average">seasonal ARIMA</NoteLink> would need hundreds of lags.

## Assumptions

- The regressors are exogenous: the error $\eta_t$ is uncorrelated with present, past and future regressors. Feedback
  from $y$ to $u$ violates this and calls for a multivariate model such as a
  <NoteLink to="vector-autoregression">vector autoregression</NoteLink>.
- Future values of the regressors are known when forecasting. Calendar effects and planned prices are known;
  temperature and competitor behaviour must themselves be forecast, which adds uncertainty the model's intervals omit.

## Pitfalls

- **Residuals versus innovations.** The regression residuals $\hat\eta_t$ are autocorrelated by design. <NoteLink to="medical-diagnosis-base-rate">Diagnostic
  tests</NoteLink> belong on the innovations $\hat\varepsilon_t$.
- **Regressing levels on levels.** Two trending series regressed on each other give a large $R^2$ and a significant
  slope even when unrelated. Use ARIMA errors with $d = 1$, or difference both sides.
- **Lagged effects.** The effect of a regressor may be spread over time (advertising this week affects sales next
  week). Include lagged regressors $u_{t-1}, u_{t-2}, \dots$ explicitly.
