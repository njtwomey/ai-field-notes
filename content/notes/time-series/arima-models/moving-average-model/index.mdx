<Definition>
A **moving-average model of order $q$**, MA($q$), is

$$
x_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q},
$$

where $\mu$ is the mean, $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_q$ are real coefficients with $\theta_q \ne 0$, and
$\varepsilon_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$. With the backshift operator $B$ and the **moving-average
polynomial** $\theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1z + \dots + \theta_qz^q$, the model is $x_t = \mu + \theta(B)\varepsilon_t$
<Cite id="box2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Each shock $\varepsilon_t$ affects the series for exactly $q + 1$ steps, with weights $1, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_q$,
and is then forgotten. An MA model suits effects that last a fixed time: a promotion that lifts sales for two weeks, a
measurement error that contaminates two adjacent readings, or the change in a series that was differenced once too
often.

The name has nothing to do with the moving averages used for smoothing. The weights need not be positive or sum to 1.
The model is a finite impulse response filter applied to white noise (<NoteLink to="fir-filters">FIR filters</NoteLink>).

## Moments

Set $\theta_0 = 1$, so $x_t - \mu = \sum_{j=0}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j}$. The mean is $\mu$. For $h \ge 0$ the
autocovariance is

$$
\gamma(h) = \cov\paren{\sum_{j=0}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{t-j},\, \sum_{k=0}^{q}\theta_k\varepsilon_{t+h-k}}
= \sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{q-h}\theta_j\theta_{j+h}, \qquad 0 \le h \le q,
$$

and $\gamma(h) = 0$ for $h > q$. Only pairs of terms that contain the same shock contribute. A shock $\varepsilon_{t-j}$
in the first sum matches $\varepsilon_{t+h-k}$ in the second when $k = j + h$, and $k \le q$ requires $j \le q - h$.
When $h > q$ no shock appears in both sums.

The mean and autocovariances do not depend on $t$, so **every MA($q$) process is stationary**, whatever its
coefficients. Its ACF **cuts off** after lag $q$. That cut-off is the MA signature in a correlogram
(<NoteLink to="acf-and-pacf">ACF and PACF</NoteLink>).

For the MA(1) model $x_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$:

$$
\gamma(0) = \sigma^2(1 + \theta^2), \qquad \gamma(1) = \sigma^2\theta, \qquad \rho(1) = \frac{\theta}{1 + \theta^2}.
$$

The function $\theta/(1 + \theta^2)$ has maximum $1/2$ at $\theta = 1$ and minimum $-1/2$ at $\theta = -1$. An MA(1)
process cannot have a lag-1 autocorrelation larger than $0.5$ in absolute value.

## Invertibility

The ACF does not determine the MA coefficients. In the MA(1) formula, $\theta$ and $1/\theta$ give the same value:
$\frac{1/\theta}{1 + 1/\theta^2} = \frac{\theta}{\theta^2 + 1}$. Rescaling the noise variance matches $\gamma(0)$ too. So
$(\theta, \sigma^2)$ and $(1/\theta, \theta^2\sigma^2)$ define processes with identical autocovariances, and for
Gaussian noise identical distributions. The data cannot tell them apart.

The convention is to pick the **invertible** representation. Write $\theta(B) = \prod_j(1 - \kappa_jB)$, with $1/\kappa_j$
the roots of $\theta(z)$. If every $\abs{\kappa_j} < 1$, each factor has the convergent inverse
$\sum_{i \ge 0}\kappa_j^iB^i$, as in the AR case, and

$$
\varepsilon_t = \theta(B)^{-1}(x_t - \mu) = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\pi_j(x_{t-j} - \mu).
$$

The shock is then a convergent function of the present and past observations. **An MA($q$) process is invertible if
and only if every root of $\theta(z)$ lies outside the unit circle** <Cite id="brockwell1991" />. For MA(1) the
condition is $\abs\theta < 1$, and $\pi_j = (-\theta)^j$:

$$
x_t - \mu = \varepsilon_t - \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-\theta)^j(x_{t-j} - \mu).
$$

An invertible MA(1) is an AR($\infty$) with geometrically decaying coefficients. Invertibility matters for three
reasons:

- It makes the model identifiable: among the models that share one ACF, which differ by replacing roots with their reciprocals, exactly one is
  invertible
  (ignoring roots on the unit circle).
- Under invertibility, $\varepsilon_t$ is the one-step prediction error from the infinite past, so the model's
  innovations are the forecast errors. The non-invertible version has shocks that are not recoverable from the data.
- Estimation reconstructs the shocks recursively from the data. The recursion is stable only when the model is
  invertible.

## Worked example

Take $\theta = 0.5$ and $\sigma^2 = 4$. The autocovariances are $\gamma(0) = 4(1 + 0.25) = 5$ and $\gamma(1) = 4 \cdot 0.5
= 2$, so $\rho(1) = 0.4$. Now take $\theta = 2$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$: $\gamma(0) = 1 + 4 = 5$ and $\gamma(1) = 2$. The two
models have identical ACFs. The root of $1 + 0.5z$ is $z = -2$, outside the unit circle, so $\theta = 0.5$ is the
invertible choice. The root of $1 + 2z$ is $z = -0.5$, inside.

The invertible model's PACF does not cut off. For an MA(1) it is

$$
\phi_{hh} = -\frac{(-\theta)^h(1 - \theta^2)}{1 - \theta^{2(h+1)}},
$$

which for $\theta = 0.5$ gives $0.4$, $-0.190$ and $0.094$ at lags 1, 2 and 3: an alternating geometric decay. That is
the mirror image of the AR(1) pattern, where the ACF decays and the PACF cuts off.

## Forecasting

Given the infinite past, the invertible representation recovers $\varepsilon_T, \varepsilon_{T-1}, \dots$. Future shocks
have mean 0, so

$$
\hat x_{T+h \mid T} = \mu + \sum_{j=h}^{q}\theta_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}, \qquad h \le q,
$$

and $\hat x_{T+h \mid T} = \mu$ for $h > q$. An MA($q$) model has a memory of exactly $q$ steps: beyond that horizon
the forecast is the mean, and the error variance is $\gamma(0)$. With a finite sample, the shocks are estimated by
running the inverse recursion from $\varepsilon_0 = 0$, or exactly with the innovations algorithm or a Kalman filter.

## Estimation

The shocks are not observed. For MA(1), they are rebuilt as $\hat\varepsilon_t = x_t - \mu - \theta\hat\varepsilon_{t-1}$,
so each residual is a polynomial in $\theta$ and the sum of squares is a high-degree polynomial in $\theta$. The model
is linear in its parameters only through the unobserved shocks, so least squares requires numerical optimisation.
Moment estimators, which solve $\rho(1) = r_1$ for $\theta$, are inefficient and have no solution when
$\abs{r_1} > 0.5$ (<NoteLink to="box-jenkins-method">Box–Jenkins method</NoteLink>).

## Assumptions

- The innovations are white noise with constant variance.
- The model is invertible. Software usually constrains estimates to the invertible region.

## Pitfalls

- **A unit root in $\theta(z)$** signals over-differencing. Differencing white noise gives an MA(1) with $\theta = -1$
  and $\rho(1) = -0.5$ (<NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">differencing and unit roots</NoteLink>).
- **Short memory, long appearance.** Moving sums of white noise produce smooth, cycle-like paths. Slutsky used this
  effect to argue that business cycles could arise from summed random shocks <Cite id="slutsky1937" />.
- **An estimate near $\abs\theta = 1$** makes the reconstructed shocks depend strongly on the starting value
  $\varepsilon_0$, and the conditional and exact likelihoods differ sharply.
