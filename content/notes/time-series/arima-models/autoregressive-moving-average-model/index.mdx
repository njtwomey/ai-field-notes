<Definition>
An **ARMA($p$, $q$) model** is

$$
x_t = c + \phi_1x_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_px_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q},
$$

or $\phi(B)x_t = c + \theta(B)\varepsilon_t$, with the autoregressive polynomial $\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1z - \dots - \phi_pz^p$,
the moving-average polynomial $\theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1z + \dots + \theta_qz^q$, the <NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">backshift operator</NoteLink> $B$ and <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white
noise</NoteLink> $\varepsilon_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$. The polynomials are assumed to have no common root
<Cite id="box2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A pure AR model needs many lags to mimic a moving-average effect, and a pure MA model needs many lags to mimic a
decaying memory. An ARMA model has both parts and is usually more economical. An ARMA(1, 1) model with three
parameters ($\phi$, $\theta$, $\sigma^2$) can match a pure AR model with many more lags.

The reason is algebraic. Any stationary series without a deterministic part is an infinite moving average of its
innovations, $x_t = \mu + \sum_{j \ge 0}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}$ (Wold's decomposition) <Cite id="brockwell1991" />. An
ARMA model approximates the power series $\psi(z) = \sum_j\psi_jz^j$ by a ratio of polynomials, $\theta(z)/\phi(z)$. A
rational function captures geometric tails with a few coefficients, as a Padé approximant does.

## Stationarity, causality and invertibility

The two polynomials play separate roles.

- **Causal and stationary.** If every root of $\phi(z)$ lies outside the unit circle, $\phi(B)$ can be inverted as in
  the <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">AR model</NoteLink>, and
  $x_t = \mu + \frac{\theta(B)}{\phi(B)}\varepsilon_t = \mu + \sum_{j \ge 0}\psi_j\varepsilon_{t-j}$, with
  $\mu = c/\phi(1)$ and absolutely summable $\psi_j$.
- **Invertible.** If every root of $\theta(z)$ lies outside the unit circle, $\theta(B)$ can be inverted as in the
  <NoteLink to="moving-average-model">MA model</NoteLink>, and the innovations are recoverable from the past:
  $\varepsilon_t = \sum_{j \ge 0}\pi_j(x_{t-j} - \mu)$.

The **ψ weights** follow from matching coefficients of $z^j$ in $\phi(z)\psi(z) = \theta(z)$:

$$
\psi_0 = 1, \qquad \psi_j = \theta_j + \sum_{i=1}^{\min(j, p)}\phi_i\psi_{j-i}, \qquad \theta_j = 0 \text{ for } j > q.
$$

The weight $\psi_j$ is the effect of a unit shock on the series $j$ steps later, the **impulse response**. After lag
$q$ the weights obey the AR recursion alone, so they decay geometrically at the rate set by the roots of $\phi$.

**Common factors.** If $\phi(z)$ and $\theta(z)$ share a root, the factor cancels in $\theta(z)/\phi(z)$ and the
process is an ARMA of lower order. For example, $(1 - 0.5B)x_t = (1 - 0.5B)\varepsilon_t$ is white noise. Fitting a
model with a near-common factor makes both coefficients poorly determined, because many pairs give almost the same
ratio.

## The autocovariance function

Multiply the centred model by $x_{t-h}$ and take <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink>. For $h > q$, every innovation on the right is
uncorrelated with $x_{t-h}$, which depends only on shocks up to $t - h$. So

$$
\gamma(h) = \phi_1\gamma(h - 1) + \dots + \phi_p\gamma(h - p), \qquad h > q.
$$

Beyond lag $q$ the ACF obeys the AR recursion and tails off as a mix of exponentials and damped cosines. The first $q$
values carry the MA part and set the starting values of the recursion. The PACF also tails off, because the invertible
MA part makes the process an AR($\infty$). Neither function cuts off, which is what makes mixed orders hard to read
from a correlogram (<NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation">ACF and PACF</NoteLink>).

For ARMA(1, 1), $x_t = \phi x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$, the two cross-moments needed are
$\expect[x_t\varepsilon_t] = \sigma^2$ and $\expect[x_t\varepsilon_{t-1}] = \phi\sigma^2 + \theta\sigma^2$. The second
follows by substituting the model for $x_t$: $\varepsilon_{t-1}$ enters through $\phi x_{t-1}$ with coefficient
$\phi$, and directly with coefficient $\theta$. Multiplying the model by $x_t$ and by $x_{t-1}$ gives

$$
\gamma(0) = \phi\gamma(1) + \sigma^2 + \theta(\phi + \theta)\sigma^2, \qquad \gamma(1) = \phi\gamma(0) + \theta\sigma^2.
$$

Substituting the second into the first and solving:

$$
\gamma(0) = \sigma^2\frac{1 + 2\phi\theta + \theta^2}{1 - \phi^2}, \qquad
\rho(1) = \frac{(1 + \phi\theta)(\phi + \theta)}{1 + 2\phi\theta + \theta^2}, \qquad
\rho(h) = \phi\rho(h - 1) \quad (h \ge 2).
$$

## Worked example

Take $\phi = 0.7$, $\theta = 0.4$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$. The AR root is $1/0.7 = 1.43$ and the MA root is $-1/0.4 = -2.5$,
both outside the unit circle, so the model is causal and invertible.

- **ψ weights.** $\psi_1 = \theta + \phi = 1.1$, then $\psi_j = 0.7\psi_{j-1}$: $0.77$, $0.539$, $0.377$. A unit shock
  raises the next value by 1.1, more than the shock itself, and then decays by 30% per step.
- **Variance.** $\gamma(0) = (1 + 0.56 + 0.16)/(1 - 0.49) = 1.72/0.51 = 3.373$.
- **ACF.** $\rho(1) = (1.28)(1.1)/1.72 = 0.819$, then $\rho(2) = 0.7 \cdot 0.819 = 0.573$ and $\rho(3) = 0.401$.

An AR(1) with the same lag-1 autocorrelation, $\phi = 0.819$, would have $\rho(2) = 0.670$. The ARMA's ACF starts
higher and then decays faster. No AR(1) or MA(1) reproduces both features.

## The spectrum

The ψ weights are the impulse response of the filter $\theta(B)/\phi(B)$ applied to white noise. The <Gloss name="power-spectral-density">power spectral
density</Gloss> of a stationary ARMA process is therefore

$$
S(\omega) = \sigma^2\frac{\abs{\theta(e^{-i\omega})}^2}{\abs{\phi(e^{-i\omega})}^2}, \qquad -\pi \le \omega \le \pi.
$$

Roots of $\phi$ near the unit circle make peaks, and roots of $\theta$ near it make troughs. AR models are the basis of
<NoteLink to="parametric-spectral-estimation">parametric spectral estimation</NoteLink> for the same reason.

## State-space form and likelihood

An ARMA($p$, $q$) model is a linear-Gaussian state-space model with a state of dimension $\max(p, q + 1)$
(<NoteLink to="linear-gaussian-state-space-models">linear-Gaussian state-space models</NoteLink>). The <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>
then computes the one-step prediction errors and their variances, which give the exact Gaussian likelihood in $O(n)$
time. This is how most software fits ARMA models (<NoteLink to="box-jenkins-method">Box–Jenkins method</NoteLink>)
<Cite id="hamilton1994" />.

## Forecasting

Forecasts use the model recursion with future innovations set to 0 and past innovations replaced by their estimates.
For $h > q$ the forecasts obey the AR recursion and decay to the mean $\mu$. The $h$-step error is
$\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}$, with variance $\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j^2$. In the worked example,
the one-, two- and three-step error variances are $1$, $1 + 1.21 = 2.21$ and $2.21 + 0.593 = 2.803$, rising towards
$\gamma(0) = 3.373$.

## Assumptions

- <NoteLink to="stationarity">Stationarity</NoteLink> and invertibility (roots of both polynomials outside the unit circle), and no common roots.
- White-noise innovations; Gaussian innovations for exact likelihood inference and <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink>.
- Non-stationary series need differencing first (<NoteLink to="autoregressive-integrated-moving-average-model">ARIMA</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Over-parameterisation.** Increasing both $p$ and $q$ at once invites near-cancelling roots. Choose orders with an
  information criterion and check the estimated roots for near-common factors.
- **The ACF and PACF of a mixed model are ambiguous.** Several (p, q) pairs produce similar correlograms. Correlograms
  suggest candidates; likelihood and residual checks decide.
- **Forecasts revert to the mean quickly.** A stationary ARMA forecast is informative only for a horizon of the order of
  the ACF's decay time. Beyond it, the forecast is the mean and the interval is the marginal distribution.
