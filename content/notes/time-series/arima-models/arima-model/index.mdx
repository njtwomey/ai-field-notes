<Definition>
A series $x_t$ follows an **ARIMA($p$, $d$, $q$) model** if its $d$-th difference $w_t = (1 - B)^dx_t$ follows a
stationary, invertible ARMA($p$, $q$) model:

$$
\phi(B)(1 - B)^dx_t = c + \theta(B)\varepsilon_t,
$$

where $B$ is the backshift operator, $\phi(z) = 1 - \phi_1z - \dots - \phi_pz^p$ and
$\theta(z) = 1 + \theta_1z + \dots + \theta_qz^q$ have all roots outside the unit circle, $c$ is a constant, and
$\varepsilon_t \sim \operatorname{WN}(0, \sigma^2)$. The **I** stands for integrated: $x_t$ is recovered from $w_t$ by
summing $d$ times <Cite id="box2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

ARMA models describe stationary series, which fluctuate around a fixed mean. Many real series drift. ARIMA handles
drift by modelling the changes instead of the levels. The combined polynomial $\phi(z)(1 - z)^d$ has $d$ roots exactly
on the unit circle at $z = 1$, and all its other roots outside. The unit roots give the series a stochastic trend, and
the ARMA part describes the short-run dynamics of the changes.

## Special cases

| Model                          | Equation                                                    | Behaviour                                  |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| ARIMA(0, 0, 0)                 | $x_t = c + \varepsilon_t$                                   | White noise around $c$                     |
| ARIMA(0, 1, 0)                 | $x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$                             | Random walk                                |
| ARIMA(0, 1, 0) with constant   | $x_t = c + x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$                         | Random walk with drift                     |
| ARIMA(0, 1, 1)                 | $x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$   | Simple exponential smoothing               |
| ARIMA(0, 2, 2)                 | $(1 - B)^2x_t = (1 + \theta_1B + \theta_2B^2)\varepsilon_t$ | Holt's linear trend method                 |
| ARIMA($p$, 0, $q$)             | $\phi(B)x_t = c + \theta(B)\varepsilon_t$                   | Stationary ARMA                            |

The exponential-smoothing equivalences are exact for the linear methods and give those methods a likelihood and
prediction intervals (<NoteLink to="ets-models">ETS models</NoteLink>).

## The constant and long-run forecasts

The constant $c$ and the order $d$ together fix the shape of long-horizon forecasts. Taking expectations of the
stationary differenced series gives $\expect[w_t] = c/\phi(1)$, so the $d$-th difference has a non-zero mean when
$c \ne 0$. Summing $d$ times turns a constant into a polynomial of degree $d$ <Cite id="hyndman2021" />:

| $d$ | $c = 0$                        | $c \ne 0$                    |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| 0   | Forecasts go to 0              | Forecasts go to the mean     |
| 1   | Forecasts go to a constant     | Forecasts follow a line      |
| 2   | Forecasts follow a line        | Forecasts follow a quadratic |

A quadratic trend is rarely credible, so software usually omits the constant when $d = 2$.

## Forecasting

Expand the operator. For ARIMA(1, 1, 0), $(1 - \phi B)(1 - B)x_t = \varepsilon_t$ becomes
$x_t = (1 + \phi)x_{t-1} - \phi x_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$. This is an AR(2) recursion whose polynomial
$1 - (1 + \phi)z + \phi z^2 = (1 - z)(1 - \phi z)$ has a unit root. Forecasts run the recursion forward with future
shocks set to 0, exactly as for an AR model. The difference from the stationary case appears in the error variance.

The $h$-step forecast error is $\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\psi_j\varepsilon_{T+h-j}$, with $\psi_j$ the coefficients of
$\theta(z)/(\phi(z)(1 - z)^d)$. With a unit root the $\psi_j$ do not decay to 0. For $d = 1$ they approach a nonzero
constant $\psi_\infty = \theta(1)/\phi(1)$, so the error variance $\sigma^2\sum_{j<h}\psi_j^2$ grows linearly in $h$ and
prediction intervals widen as $\sqrt h$.

## Worked example: ARIMA(1, 1, 0)

Take $\phi = 0.5$, $\sigma^2 = 1$, no constant, and the last two observations $x_{T-1} = 9$ and $x_T = 10$. The last
change is $w_T = 1$.

- **Point forecasts.** The changes are forecast as an AR(1): $\hat w_{T+h} = 0.5^h$. Cumulating them gives
  $\hat x_{T+1} = 10.5$, $\hat x_{T+2} = 10.75$, $\hat x_{T+3} = 10.875$, and the forecasts converge to
  $10 + 0.5/(1 - 0.5) = 11$. The momentum in the last change carries on, decays, and leaves a permanent level shift.
- **ψ weights.** The coefficients of $1/((1 - 0.5z)(1 - z))$ are the partial sums of $0.5^j$:
  $\psi_j = (1 - 0.5^{j+1})/0.5$, giving $1$, $1.5$, $1.75$, $1.875$ and the limit $2 = 1/\phi(1)$.
- **Error variances.** $\sigma^2\sum_{j<h}\psi_j^2$ gives $1$, $3.25$, $6.3125$ and $9.828$ for $h = 1, \dots, 4$. For
  large $h$ each extra step adds about $\psi_\infty^2 = 4$.

## ARIMA(0, 1, 1) is simple exponential smoothing

The model $x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$ gives the one-step forecast
$\hat x_{t+1 \mid t} = x_t + \theta\varepsilon_t$. Under invertibility, the innovation is the one-step forecast error,
$\varepsilon_t = x_t - \hat x_{t \mid t-1}$. Substituting:

$$
\hat x_{t+1 \mid t} = x_t + \theta(x_t - \hat x_{t \mid t-1}) = (1 + \theta)x_t - \theta\hat x_{t \mid t-1}.
$$

With $\alpha = 1 + \theta$, this is $\hat x_{t+1 \mid t} = \alpha x_t + (1 - \alpha)\hat x_{t \mid t-1}$, the recursion
of <NoteLink to="simple-exponential-smoothing">simple exponential smoothing</NoteLink>. Invertibility,
$-1 < \theta < 1$, corresponds to $0 < \alpha < 2$. Muth showed that exponential smoothing is the optimal forecast for
a random walk observed with noise, which has exactly this ARIMA(0, 1, 1) form <Cite id="muth1960" />.

## Choosing $d$

Differencing is chosen before the ARMA orders. Unit-root and stationarity tests (ADF, KPSS) give a formal choice
(<NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">differencing and unit roots</NoteLink>). A sample ACF that decays slowly and
almost linearly suggests another difference. A lag-1 autocorrelation near $-0.5$ after differencing suggests one
difference too many. In practice $d$ is 0, 1 or 2. Seasonal differencing handles seasonal unit roots
(<NoteLink to="seasonal-arima">seasonal ARIMA</NoteLink>).

A log or Box–Cox transformation comes before differencing when the variance grows with the level. The differenced log
series is then approximately the growth rate.

## Assumptions

- After $d$ differences the series is stationary and follows an invertible ARMA model.
- Innovations are white noise with constant variance; Gaussian innovations for likelihood fitting and prediction
  intervals.
- The model's parameters are constant over the sample and the forecast horizon.

## Pitfalls

- **Long-horizon intervals depend on $d$.** Choosing $d = 1$ instead of $d = 0$ for a near-unit-root series turns
  bounded intervals into intervals that grow without limit. The data rarely decide this confidently.
- **A constant with $d = 1$ is a drift.** It is easy to include by default and obtain a trend in the forecasts that the
  data barely support.
- **Undoing a log transform.** The exponential of a forecast on the log scale is the median of the forecast
  distribution, not its mean.
