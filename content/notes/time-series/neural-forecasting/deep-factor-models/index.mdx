<Definition>
A **deep factor model with random effects** generates each series $z_{i,t}$, $i = 1, \dots, N$, from a latent function
that is the sum of a fixed global effect and a local random effect <Cite id="wang2019" />:

$$
f_{i,t} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{i,k}\, g_k(\xvec_{i,t}), \qquad r_{i,\cdot} \sim R_i, \qquad
u_{i,t} = f_{i,t} + r_{i,t}, \qquad z_{i,t} \sim p(z_{i,t} \mid u_{i,t}).
$$

The $K$ **global factors** $g_k$ are deterministic RNNs shared by all series, the weights $\wvec_i = (w_{i,k})_k$ are a
learnt embedding of series $i$, $R_i$ is a classical local probabilistic time-series model, and $p$ is the observation
likelihood.
</Definition>

## Global and local

Forecasting a large collection of related series pulls in two directions. A **local** model, fitted per series, captures
each series' own noise and gives calibrated uncertainty, but learns nothing across series. A **global** model, shared by
all series, learns common patterns from much more data, but its uncertainty is hard to specify for each series.

Deep factor models split the job. The global part is a small set of **deep factors** $g_1, \dots, g_K$, which act like
dynamic principal components or "eigen time series": every series' fixed effect is a linear combination of the same
$K$ curves, mixed by its own weights $\wvec_i$. The local part is a random effect $r_{i,t}$ drawn from a classical model
fitted with per-series parameters. The global factors are kept deterministic, so no inference over latent variables
that couple all $N$ series is needed.

The paper motivates this split with a characterisation of exchangeable time series: a collection whose joint
distribution is invariant to relabelling the series decomposes into a global part and conditionally independent local
parts <Cite id="wang2019" />.

## Three local models

The paper gives three instantiations, which differ only in $R_i$. With a Gaussian observation model
$z_{i,t} \sim \Gauss(u_{i,t}, \sigma_{i,t}^2)$ each has a closed-form likelihood.

| Model  | Local random effect $r_{i,t}$                                          | Likelihood of $\zvec_i$                                                          |
| ------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| DF-RNN | Independent $\Gauss(0, \sigma_{i,t}^2)$, $\sigma_{i,t}$ from a noise RNN | $\prod_t \Gauss(z_{i,t} - f_{i,t} \mid 0, \sigma_{i,t}^2)$                       |
| DF-LDS | Linear dynamical system (innovation state space model)                 | Kalman filter on $z_{i,t} - f_{i,t}$                                             |
| DF-GP  | Zero-mean Gaussian process with kernel $K_i$                           | $\Gauss(\zvec_i - \fvec_i \mid \zeros, \Kmat_i + \sigma_i^2\eye)$            |

In DF-LDS the local state evolves as $\hvec_{i,t} = \Fmat_{i,t}\hvec_{i,t-1} + \qvec_{i,t}\epsilon_{i,t}$ with
$r_{i,t} = \avec_{i,t}^\top\hvec_{i,t}$, the same structure as the state space models used by
<NoteLink to="deep-state-space-models">DeepState</NoteLink>. In DF-GP each series has its own kernel hyperparameters.

## Deriving the likelihood

Take the Gaussian case. Given the global factors, the fixed effect $\fvec_i = (f_{i,1}, \dots, f_{i,T})$ is a
known vector. The observation is $\zvec_i = \fvec_i + \rvec_i + \epsilonvec_i$, where
$\rvec_i$ is the random effect and $\epsilonvec_i$ the observation noise. Subtracting the known mean
leaves the residual $\zvec_i - \fvec_i = \rvec_i + \epsilonvec_i$, which is distributed exactly as
the local model plus white noise. The likelihood of series $i$ is therefore the local model's marginal likelihood
evaluated on the residual.

- For DF-GP, $\rvec_i \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat_i)$ and $\epsilonvec_i \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma_i^2\eye)$
  are independent, so their sum is $\Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat_i + \sigma_i^2\eye)$. This is the standard GP marginal
  likelihood with mean $\fvec_i$.
- For DF-LDS, the noise is absorbed into the observation equation of the linear dynamical system, and the Kalman filter
  computes the likelihood of the residual by the prediction error decomposition.
- For DF-RNN, the residuals are independent Gaussians, and the likelihood is a product.

The parameters $\Theta$ (the factor RNNs, the embeddings $\wvec_i$ and the local hyperparameters) are fitted by
maximum likelihood, $\Theta = \argmax \sum_i \log p(\zvec_i \mid \Theta)$, with stochastic gradients. Each series'
likelihood depends on the global factors only through $\fvec_i$, so a mini-batch of series gives an unbiased
gradient.

For non-Gaussian observations, such as counts, the marginal likelihood is intractable. The paper then fits a
variational lower bound, with an encoder network that proposes the latent function $\uvec_i$ from $\zvec_i$
<Cite id="wang2019" />.

## Forecasting

To forecast, the global RNNs are unrolled over the future covariates to get $f_{i,T+1:T+\tau}$. The local model gives
the predictive distribution of $r_{i,T+1:T+\tau}$ conditioned on the observed residuals: Kalman prediction for DF-LDS,
GP conditioning for DF-GP. The forecast is the sum. Uncertainty comes entirely from the local model and the observation
noise, so it is expressed in the familiar terms of that model.

## Why factor the global part

A shared RNN producing $K$ curves is cheaper than a full per-series recurrent model: the $K$ factor RNNs run on
covariates, and each series adds only its $K$ weights and a few local parameters. On the electricity data the paper
reports lower error and lower variance than a standard RNN forecaster of similar size trained with the same squared
loss <Cite id="wang2019" />.

## Assumptions

- The global signal of every series lies in the span of $K$ shared curves. Series with idiosyncratic structure must
  push it into the local model.
- The global factors are deterministic. Their own uncertainty is not represented.
- The local model family is chosen by hand.

## Pitfalls

- DF-GP costs $O(T^3)$ per series for the kernel inverse, which limits the history length.
- $K$ trades expressiveness for data efficiency. Too few factors force structure into the local model, whose
  uncertainty then inflates; too many lose the sharing benefit.
- The non-Gaussian variant optimises a bound, and the quality of its forecast uncertainty depends on the encoder.
