<Definition>
  A **neural forecasting model** maps the observed history of a time series, together with covariates, to a forecast of
  its next $H$ values using a neural network. The network's parameters are usually **global**: one set is fitted to a
  collection of related series, rather than one set per series as in ARIMA or exponential smoothing. The output is a
  point forecast, a set of quantiles, or the parameters of a predictive distribution for each future step.
</Definition>

## What the models share

Every model in this family answers the same five design questions. The answers, more than the backbone, separate one
model from the next.

- **Global or local parameters.** A global model shares its weights across all series, so a pattern seen in many
  series is learnt once and a series with little history borrows strength from the rest. Some models add a per-series
  component: <NoteLink to="deep-state-space-models">deep state space models</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="deep-factor-models">deep factor models</NoteLink> keep a classical local model inside a global network.
- **What is output.** A likelihood model outputs the parameters of a distribution and is trained by maximum
  likelihood, as in <NoteLink to="deepar">DeepAR</NoteLink>. A quantile model outputs chosen quantiles and is trained on
  the quantile (pinball) loss, as in <NoteLink to="multi-horizon-quantile-recurrent-network">MQ-RNN</NoteLink>. A point model outputs one value per step
  and is trained on squared or absolute error, as in <NoteLink to="n-beats">N-BEATS</NoteLink> and most long-horizon
  transformers.
- **How the horizon is produced.** A **recursive** model predicts one step, feeds the prediction back as an input, and
  repeats $H$ times. A **direct multi-horizon** model outputs all $H$ steps from one pass. Recursive models give joint
  sample paths but let errors compound over the horizon. Direct models avoid the feedback but give only marginal
  forecasts per step.
- **Which covariates are allowed.** Inputs fall into three kinds: static attributes of a series (a product category),
  inputs known in advance for the whole horizon (a calendar, a planned promotion), and inputs observed only up to the
  forecast origin (past sales of related items). Models differ in which kinds they accept and how they route them.
- **How scale is handled.** Series in one collection can differ in level by orders of magnitude. Most models divide
  each input window by a per-series scale (a mean, a mean absolute value, or a standard deviation) and multiply the
  output back.

## Backbones

- **Recurrent networks.** An <NoteLink to="long-short-term-memory">LSTM</NoteLink> reads the history one step at a time and carries a
  hidden state forward. DeepAR, DeepState, Deep Factors and MQ-RNN use one.
- **Dilated causal convolutions.** A stack of convolutions whose dilation doubles per layer sees an input window that
  grows exponentially with depth, and it trains in parallel over time. See
  <NoteLink to="temporal-convolutional-networks">temporal convolutional networks</NoteLink>.
- **Fully connected stacks.** N-BEATS and <NoteLink to="n-hits">N-HiTS</NoteLink> map a fixed look-back window to the
  horizon with residual stacks of multilayer perceptrons that emit coefficients of basis functions.
- **Attention.** The <NoteLink to="temporal-fusion-transformer">temporal fusion transformer</NoteLink> combines an LSTM
  with interpretable attention. <NoteLink to="transformer-forecasters">Informer, Autoformer and PatchTST</NoteLink>
  adapt the <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> to long look-back windows and long horizons.
- **Linear maps.** A single linear layer from the look-back window to the horizon is the baseline that
  <NoteLink to="dlinear">DLinear</NoteLink> showed to beat several long-horizon transformers.
- **Pretrained foundation models.** Transformers trained once on large, varied corpora of series and applied to new
  series without retraining. See <NoteLink to="forecasting-foundation-models">forecasting foundation models</NoteLink>.

## Data flow

A training example is a window cut from one series at a **forecast origin** $t$. The window holds the look-back values
$y_{t-L+1:t}$, the covariates over the look-back and the horizon, and the targets $y_{t+1:t+H}$. The model scales the
window, maps it to outputs for the $H$ future steps, rescales them and scores them against the targets. Windows from
all series and many origins are mixed in each minibatch. At forecast time the same pipeline runs once from the last
observed origin.

Evaluation must respect time order: models are fitted on data before a cutoff and scored on data after it, often at
several rolling origins. See <NoteLink to="time-series-cross-validation">time-series cross-validation</NoteLink>.
Probabilistic outputs are scored with proper scoring rules such as the

<NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">CRPS and the quantile loss</NoteLink>.

## Where they stand

- N-BEATS, a network with no time-series-specific components in its generic form, beat the statistical benchmarks
  and the hybrid winner of the M4 competition on the M4 data. Its authors describe it as the first pure deep-learning
  model to do so <Cite id="oreshkin2020" />.
- On the long-horizon benchmarks (electricity transformer temperature, electricity load, traffic, weather, exchange
  rates, influenza), a linear map from the look-back window outperformed the transformers of 2021–2022
  <Cite id="zeng2023" />. PatchTST and N-HiTS then beat the linear baseline on the larger of these datasets
  <Cite id="nie2023" /> <Cite id="challu2023" />.
- Pretrained foundation models forecast unseen series without training, with accuracy close to supervised models
  fitted to each dataset <Cite id="das2024" /> <Cite id="ansari2024" />.
- For a single short series, classical local models such as the
  <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">autoregressive model</NoteLink> or exponential smoothing remain strong baselines.
  Every neural model should be compared with them and with a seasonal naive forecast.
