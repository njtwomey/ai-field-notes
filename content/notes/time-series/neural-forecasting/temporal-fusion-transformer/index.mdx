<Definition>
The **temporal fusion transformer** (TFT) is a direct multi-horizon forecaster <Cite id="lim2021" />. At forecast time
$t$ it outputs <NoteLink to="probabilistic-forecasting">quantile forecasts</NoteLink>

$$
\hat y_i(q, t, \tau) = f_q\paren{\tau,\ y_{i,t-k:t},\ \zvec_{i,t-k:t},\ \xvec_{i,t-k:t+\tau},\ \svec_i}
$$

for every horizon $\tau = 1, \dots, \tau_{\max}$ and quantile level $q$. $y_{i,\cdot}$ is the target of entity $i$,
$\zvec$ are inputs observed only up to $t$, $\xvec$ are inputs known in advance over the whole horizon,
$\svec_i$ are static covariates, and $k$ is the look-back length. The network is built from gated <Gloss name="resnet">residual
networks</Gloss>, variable selection networks, static covariate encoders, an <Gloss name="lstm" /> encoder–decoder and a masked interpretable
self-attention layer, and is trained on the quantile loss.

</Definition>

## The problem

A multi-horizon forecasting problem usually mixes three kinds of input.

- **Static covariates** $\svec_i$ do not change over time: a store's location, a product's category.
- **Observed inputs** $\zvec_{i,t}$ are measured as time passes and are unknown for the future: past sales, past
  weather.
- **Known inputs** $\xvec_{i,t}$ are fixed in advance for the future too: the day of the week, a holiday, a planned
  price.

Most relevant inputs are unknown in advance, and many available inputs are noise. TFT is designed to route each kind of
input to where it is useful, to suppress inputs and layers that do not help, and to expose which inputs and which past
time steps drove a forecast.

## Gated residual network

The basic unit is the **gated residual network** (GRN). It takes a primary input $\avec$ and an optional context
vector $\cvec$:

$$
\operatorname{GRN}(\avec, \cvec) = \operatorname{LayerNorm}\paren{\avec + \operatorname{GLU}(\etavec_1)},
\qquad \etavec_1 = \Wmat_1\etavec_2 + \bvec_1, \qquad
\etavec_2 = \operatorname{ELU}(\Wmat_2\avec + \Wmat_3\cvec + \bvec_2).
$$

The **gated linear unit** <Cite id="dauphin2017" /> is

$$
\operatorname{GLU}(\gammavec) = \sigma(\Wmat_4\gammavec + \bvec_4) \odot (\Wmat_5\gammavec + \bvec_5),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ the elementwise product. LayerNorm is layer normalisation

<Cite id="ba2016" />.

**Why the gate lets the network skip a layer.** Each component of the sigmoid lies in $(0, 1)$. If training drives the
gate's pre-activation $\Wmat_4\gammavec + \bvec_4$ to large negative values, the gate tends to $\zeros$ and
$\operatorname{GLU}(\etavec_1) \to \zeros$. The GRN's output then tends to $\operatorname{LayerNorm}(\avec)$:
the nonlinear branch contributes nothing and the unit passes its input through. If the gate is open, the branch adds a
learnt correction. The network can thus choose how much nonlinear processing each place in the architecture applies,
which matters on small or noisy datasets. The <Gloss name="elu" /> adds a second degree of freedom: for large positive arguments it is
the identity and for large negative ones it is constant, so the branch itself can act as a linear layer.

## Variable selection

Each input variable is first mapped to a vector in $\reals^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$: categorical variables through an
entity embedding, continuous ones through a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink>. Write $\xivec^{(j)}_t$ for variable $j$ at time $t$
and $\Ximat_t$ for all of them concatenated. A **variable selection network** computes weights over the $m$
variables and a processed version of each:

$$
\vvec_t = \operatorname{Softmax}\paren{\operatorname{GRN}_v(\Ximat_t, \cvec_s)}, \qquad
\tilde{\xivec}^{(j)}_t = \operatorname{GRN}_j(\xivec^{(j)}_t), \qquad
\tilde{\xivec}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{m} v^{(j)}_t\,\tilde{\xivec}^{(j)}_t.
$$

The weights $\vvec_t$ are non-negative and sum to one, and they are recomputed at every time step and for every entity.
An input that carries no signal can receive weight near zero. Averaging $\vvec_t$ over a dataset gives a variable
importance score. Static, past and future inputs each have their own selection network.

## Static covariate encoders

The static variables pass through their own selection network, giving a vector $\zetavec$. Four separate GRNs
turn $\zetavec$ into four **context vectors**, each wired to a different place:

- $\cvec_s$ conditions the temporal variable selection networks;
- $\cvec_c$ and $\cvec_h$ initialise the cell state and hidden state of the LSTM encoder;
- $\cvec_e$ enriches the temporal features before attention.

A static attribute can thus change which inputs are selected, how the recent past is summarised, and how the temporal
features are weighted, rather than entering only as one more input column.

## Temporal processing

**Local processing with an LSTM.** The selected past inputs $\tilde{\xivec}_{t-k:t}$ feed an LSTM encoder

<Cite id="hochreiter1997" />, and the selected known future inputs $\tilde{\xivec}_{t+1:t+\tau_{\max}}$ feed an
LSTM decoder that continues from the encoder's state. The result is one feature vector $\phivec(t, n)$ per
position $n \in \{-k, \dots, \tau_{\max}\}$. The recurrence captures local patterns such as spikes and change points,
and replaces the <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encoding</NoteLink> of a standard <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> by giving the features an order. A gated skip
connection adds the LSTM's input back:
$\tilde{\phivec}(t, n) = \operatorname{LayerNorm}\paren{\tilde{\xivec}_{t+n} + \operatorname{GLU}(\phivec(t, n))}$.

**Static enrichment.** Each position passes through a GRN with the static context $\cvec_e$:
$\thetavec(t, n) = \operatorname{GRN}(\tilde{\phivec}(t, n), \cvec_e)$.

**Self-attention.** One interpretable <Gloss name="mha">multi-head attention</Gloss> layer (below) runs over all $k + \tau_{\max} + 1$ positions,
with a causal mask so that each position attends only to itself and earlier positions. This layer captures long-range
dependencies, such as a value one week earlier, that the LSTM may not retain. A gated skip connection and a
position-wise GRN follow, and a final gated skip connection runs around the whole attention block back to the LSTM
output. When attention does not help, the gates can close and the model reduces to the LSTM encoder–decoder.

## Interpretable multi-head attention

Standard multi-head attention <Cite id="vaswani2017" /> gives each head $h$ its own query, key and value projections
and concatenates the heads:

$$
\operatorname{MultiHead}(\Qmat, \Kmat, \Vmat) = [\Hmat_1, \dots, \Hmat_{m_H}]\,\Wmat_H, \qquad
\Hmat_h = \operatorname{Softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat\Wmat^{(h)}_Q (\Kmat\Wmat^{(h)}_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{attn}}}}}
\Vmat\Wmat^{(h)}_V.
$$

Each head attends over different value vectors, so one head's attention weights say nothing about another's, and no
single matrix shows which time steps mattered. TFT shares the value projection $\Wmat_V$ across heads and averages the
heads instead of concatenating them. Write $\Amat_h$ for head $h$'s softmax matrix. Then

$$
\tilde\Hmat = \frac{1}{m_H}\sum_{h=1}^{m_H} \Amat_h \Vmat\Wmat_V
= \paren{\frac{1}{m_H}\sum_{h=1}^{m_H} \Amat_h} \Vmat\Wmat_V
= \tilde\Amat\,\Vmat\Wmat_V,
$$

because <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix multiplication</NoteLink> distributes over the sum. The layer is exactly one attention operation with the averaged
weight matrix $\tilde\Amat$. Each row of $\tilde\Amat$ is an average of probability vectors, so it is itself a
probability distribution over positions. Row $n$ of $\tilde\Amat$ therefore states how much each earlier time step
contributed to position $n$. Different heads can still learn different temporal patterns, because their $\Amat_h$
differ.

## Quantile outputs

A linear layer per quantile level maps the final feature at each future position to a forecast,
$\hat y(q, t, \tau) = \wvec_q^\top\tilde{\psivec}(t, \tau) + b_q$. The model is trained on the quantile loss,
summed over horizons and levels $\mathcal{Q} = \{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$:

$$
\mathrm{QL}(y, \hat y, q) = q\,(y - \hat y)_+ + (1 - q)\,(\hat y - y)_+, \qquad (a)_+ = \max(0, a).
$$

For an outcome $Y$ with distribution function $F$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of $\expect[\mathrm{QL}(Y, \hat y, q)]$ with respect
to $\hat y$ is $-q\,(1 - F(\hat y)) + (1 - q)\,F(\hat y) = F(\hat y) - q$. It is zero where $F(\hat y) = q$, so each
output learns the conditional $q$-quantile <Cite id="koenker1978" />. This is the same output scheme as

<NoteLink to="multi-horizon-quantile-recurrent-network">MQ-RNN</NoteLink> <Cite id="wen2017" />. The paper evaluates with the normalised quantile loss

$$
q\text{-Risk} = \frac{2\sum_{y_t}\sum_{\tau} \mathrm{QL}\paren{y_t, \hat y(q, t - \tau, \tau), q}}
{\sum_{y_t}\sum_{\tau}\abs{y_t}},
$$

reported for $q = 0.5$ (P50) and $q = 0.9$ (P90).

## What the model exposes

- **Variable importance.** Selection weights, averaged over entities and time, rank static, past and future inputs.
  On the electricity data, hour of day ranked above past power usage among the temporal inputs.
- **Persistent temporal patterns.** Attention weights averaged over the test set show spikes at daily intervals on
  the electricity and traffic data and a weaker weekly pattern on retail sales.
- **Regimes and events.** The distance between the attention pattern at a given time and its average rises during
  unusual periods. On S&P 500 realised volatility it rose around the 2008 financial crisis.

## Results reported

The paper evaluates on four datasets: hourly electricity use of 370 customers and hourly occupancy of 440 freeways,
each with a 168-hour look-back and a 24-hour horizon; Favorita grocery sales, 90 days back and 30 ahead; and realised
volatility of 31 stock indices, 252 business days back and 5 ahead. TFT's P50 and P90 losses were on average 7% and 9%
lower than the next best model's <Cite id="lim2021" />. Removing components one at a time increased the losses. The
LSTM and attention layers mattered most, with P90 loss increases above 6% on average. Static encoders and variable
selection followed. Replacing the gates with plain layers increased P90 loss by 1.9% on average and 4.1% on the
volatility data, the smallest and noisiest dataset.

## Pitfalls

- Attention weights show where the model looked, not a causal effect of the inputs on the forecast.
- The model has many components and hyperparameters. The paper tuned them separately for each dataset.
- Quantiles are separate outputs and can cross. Their order is not enforced.
