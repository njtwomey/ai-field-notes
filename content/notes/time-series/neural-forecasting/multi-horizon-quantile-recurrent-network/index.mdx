<Definition>
The **multi-horizon quantile recurrent forecaster** (MQ-RNN) is a sequence-to-sequence network that outputs, at a
forecast creation time $t$, a $K \times Q$ matrix of quantile forecasts $\hat y^{(q)}_{t+k}$ for horizons
$k = 1, \dots, K$ and quantile levels $q \in \{q_1, \dots, q_Q\}$ <Cite id="wen2017" />:

$$
\hvec_t = \operatorname{LSTM}(\hvec_{t-1}, y_t, \xvec_t), \qquad
(\cvec_{t+1}, \dots, \cvec_{t+K}, \cvec_a) = m_G(\hvec_t, \xvec^{(f)}_{t+1:t+K}), \qquad
\hat y^{(q_1:q_Q)}_{t+k} = m_L(\cvec_{t+k}, \cvec_a, \xvec^{(f)}_{t+k}).
$$

$\hvec_t$ is the encoder state, $\xvec_t$ are the covariates at time $t$, $\xvec^{(f)}$ are covariates known in advance
for the future, $m_G$ is the **global** <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptron</Gloss>, and $m_L$ is the **local** multilayer perceptron shared
by all horizons. The network is trained by minimising the quantile loss summed over horizons, quantiles and forecast
creation times.

</Definition>

## The problem it solves

A retailer needs forecasts for every product at every week, for each of the next 52 weeks, and needs more than a
point forecast. Stock decisions depend on quantiles: an order that covers demand with probability 0.9 is the 0.9
quantile of demand. The inputs have three kinds:

- **history-only covariates** $\xvec^{(h)}_{1:t}$, known only up to the forecast creation time, such as past demand;
- **known future covariates** $\xvec^{(f)}_{t+1:t+K}$, such as calendar features, holidays and planned promotions;
- **static covariates** $\xvec^{(s)}$, attributes of the series such as its product category.

One network is fitted to all series, so a new product with little history borrows patterns from the others.

MQ-RNN combines three ideas: sequence-to-sequence networks for the temporal structure, quantile regression for the
output distribution, and the direct multi-horizon strategy for producing several steps at once.

## Quantile loss

For a quantile level $q \in (0, 1)$, a forecast $\hat y$ and an outcome $y$, the **quantile loss** is

$$
L_q(y, \hat y) = q\,(y - \hat y)_+ + (1 - q)\,(\hat y - y)_+, \qquad (a)_+ = \max(0, a).
$$

An outcome above the forecast costs $q$ per unit and an outcome below it costs $1 - q$ per unit. At $q = 0.5$ the loss
is half the absolute error.

**The expected quantile loss is minimised at the $q$-quantile.** Let $Y$ have <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss> $F$ and
density $f$. The expected loss of a forecast $\hat y$ is

$$
R(\hat y) = \expect[L_q(Y, \hat y)] = q\int_{\hat y}^{\infty} (y - \hat y) f(y)\,dy + (1 - q)\int_{-\infty}^{\hat y}
(\hat y - y) f(y)\,dy.
$$

Differentiate with respect to $\hat y$. Each integrand is zero at $y = \hat y$, so the terms from the moving limits
vanish:

$$
R'(\hat y) = -q\,(1 - F(\hat y)) + (1 - q)\,F(\hat y) = F(\hat y) - q.
$$

$R'$ is negative where $F(\hat y) < q$ and positive where $F(\hat y) > q$, so $R$ falls and then rises. Its minimum is
at $F(\hat y) = q$, which defines the $q$-quantile. A network trained to minimise the average quantile loss over
training pairs therefore estimates the conditional $q$-quantile of $y$ given its inputs. This is quantile regression

<Cite id="koenker1978" />. No distributional family is assumed. The
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-forecasting">probabilistic forecasting</NoteLink> note works through the same result with an
interactive figure.

With $K$ horizons and $Q$ levels, the network's output at creation time $t$ is the $K \times Q$ matrix
$\hat{\Ymat}_t = [\hat y^{(q)}_{t+k}]_{k,q}$. The training loss is

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{t} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{q} L_q\paren{y_{t+k}, \hat y^{(q)}_{t+k}},
$$

where $t$ runs over forecast creation times. Terms can be weighted to stress some horizons or quantiles.

## Direct multi-horizon forecasting

A **recursive** forecaster predicts $\hat y_{t+1}$, feeds it back as the next input, and repeats. At training time it
consumes true values; at forecast time it consumes its own predictions, so errors accumulate along the horizon. MQ-RNN
uses the **direct multi-horizon** strategy instead: the whole vector $(y_{t+1}, \dots, y_{t+K})$ is a target, and all
horizons are predicted from the same encoder state. Nothing is fed back.

Feeding quantiles back would also be ill-defined. The 0.9 quantile of $y_{t+2}$ is not a function of the 0.9 quantile
of $y_{t+1}$ alone, because quantiles do not add. A recursive quantile model would have to learn how to combine them.

## Architecture

**Encoder.** An <Gloss name="lstm" /> <Cite id="hochreiter1997" /> reads the history. At each step it takes the target $y_t$, the
history-only and known covariates at $t$, and an embedding of the static covariates repeated over time.

**Global MLP.** At a creation time $t$, $m_G$ takes the encoder state $\hvec_t$ and all known future covariates
$\xvec^{(f)}_{t+1:t+K}$. It outputs $K + 1$ context vectors:

- a **horizon-specific context** $\cvec_{t+k}$ for each $k$, which tells the decoder how far ahead step $t+k$ lies and
  carries what the history implies for that step;
- a **horizon-agnostic context** $\cvec_a$, which carries information common to every horizon.

Because $m_G$ sees the whole future covariate block, an event late in the horizon, such as a holiday in week 6, can
change the forecast for week 5. The paper calls this anticipation.

**Local MLP.** $m_L$ is applied to each horizon separately, with the same weights for every $k$. It takes
$\cvec_{t+k}$, $\cvec_a$ and that step's own future covariates $\xvec^{(f)}_{t+k}$, and outputs the $Q$ quantiles for
step $t+k$. Tying $\xvec^{(f)}_{t+k}$ directly to its own output lets a promotion in week $k$ produce a sharp spike in
week $k$ and not in its neighbours. When future covariates carry no information, the local MLP can be dropped and $m_G$
outputs the $K \times Q$ matrix directly.

The decoder is not recurrent. The authors argue that the horizon-specific contexts already carry the temporal
information, so a recurrent decoder would add cost without adding information <Cite id="wen2017" />.

## Forking sequences

Most sequence-to-sequence forecasters are trained by **cutting**: each training example is one series cut at one
randomly chosen creation time, with the history before it as input and the next $K$ values as target. A series of
length $T$ yields many overlapping examples, and each is encoded from scratch.

An LSTM already computes $\hvec_t$ at every $t$ as it reads the series. MQ-RNN attaches a copy of the decoder, with
shared weights, to the encoder state at **every** time step and computes the multi-horizon loss at every $t$:

$$
\hvec_t = \operatorname{encoder}(y_{1:t}, \xvec_{1:t}), \qquad
\hat{\Ymat}_t = \operatorname{decoder}(\hvec_t, \xvec^{(f)}_{t+1:t+K}) \quad \text{for all } t.
$$

One forward and one backward pass through the series then collects the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> of all creation times. This is
**forking sequences**. A whole series is one training example, so no windows need to be cut. The losses at adjacent
creation times are highly correlated, and updating them together stabilised training in the paper's experiments.

Near the end of the training data, creation times $t > T - K$ have only part of their $K$-step target. MQ-RNN masks the
missing terms of the loss rather than discarding those creation times, so the model still learns from the most recent
short-horizon targets.

## Encoder variants

Any encoder that produces a state at every step with shared weights over time is compatible with forking sequences.
The paper tried three alternatives to the plain LSTM:

- **Skip connections to past states.** A linear layer summarises $(\hvec_t, \dots, \hvec_{t-D})$ before the decoder,
  as in a nonlinear autoregressive network with exogenous inputs (NARX).
- **Lagged targets as inputs.** Feeding $(y_{t-1}, \dots, y_{t-D})$ into the LSTM at step $t$. With $D = 52$ weeks
  this gave the best recurrent variant.
- **Dilated causal convolutions.** A WaveNet-style stack <Cite id="oord2016c" /> replaces the LSTM, giving
  **MQ-CNN**. It had the highest accuracy on the Amazon data. See
  <NoteLink to="temporal-convolutional-networks">temporal convolutional networks</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Take one creation time, horizon $k = 1$ and the three quantile levels $0.1$, $0.5$ and $0.9$. The network outputs
$\hat y^{(0.1)} = 8$, $\hat y^{(0.5)} = 10$ and $\hat y^{(0.9)} = 13$, and the outcome is $y = 12$.

- $q = 0.1$: $y$ is above the forecast by 4, so the loss is $0.1 \times 4 = 0.4$.
- $q = 0.5$: $y$ is above the forecast by 2, so the loss is $0.5 \times 2 = 1.0$.
- $q = 0.9$: $y$ is below the forecast by 1, so the loss is $(1 - 0.9) \times 1 = 0.1$.

The contribution to $\mathcal{L}$ is $1.5$. The median forecast carries most of the loss because it missed by the most.
The 0.9 forecast was above the outcome, which that level expects to happen nine times in ten, so it pays little.

The minimiser property holds for samples as well as for distributions. For the five outcomes $3, 5, 6, 8, 13$ and
$q = 0.7$, the average quantile loss as a function of a constant forecast $c$ has slope equal to (fraction of outcomes
below $c$) $- 0.7$. The slope changes sign at $c = 8$, where the fraction below jumps from $0.6$ to $0.8$. The average
loss at $c = 8$ is $\big(0.7 \times 5 + 0.3 \times (5 + 3 + 2)\big)/5 = 1.3$, and $8$ is the sample 0.7-quantile.

## Results reported

- **Amazon demand.** Weekly demand for about 60,000 products, trained on data before 2016 and forecast at each of the 52
  weeks of 2016 for horizons of 1 to 52 weeks. Variants each changed one design choice: a log-Gaussian likelihood in
  place of the quantile loss, cutting in place of forking, and a recursive <Gloss name="seq2seq">sequence-to-sequence model</Gloss> with a
  log-Gaussian likelihood. Among these, the full MQ-RNN was the most accurate at every horizon. The likelihood variant's
  80% interval was on average almost twice as wide <Cite id="wen2017" />.
- **GEFCom2014.** On the electricity price task, 24 hours ahead with 99 quantiles, MQ-RNN's average quantile loss of
  2.63 was lower than the winning entry's 2.72. On the load task, a month of hourly quantiles, its loss of 7.43 would
  also have placed first.

## Assumptions

- The quantile levels are fixed before training. A level not in the set must be interpolated. The GEFCom2014 entry
  trained 5 levels and interpolated linearly to 99.
- Known future covariates must be available for the whole horizon at forecast time.
- Each output is a marginal quantile of one step. The model says nothing about the joint behaviour of several steps.

## Pitfalls

- Quantiles are separate outputs, so nothing prevents the 0.9 forecast from falling below the 0.5 forecast. Sorting the
  outputs, or predicting positive increments between levels, restores the order.
- Marginal quantiles cannot answer questions about sums over the horizon, such as the 0.9 quantile of total demand over
  four weeks. A model that produces sample paths, such as <NoteLink to="deepar">DeepAR</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="salinas2020" />, can.
- Quantile forecasts should be scored with the quantile loss or the
  <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)</NoteLink>, and checked for calibration: the 0.9 forecast should exceed
  about 90% of outcomes.
