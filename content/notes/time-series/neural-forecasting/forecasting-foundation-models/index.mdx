A **forecasting foundation model** is trained once on many series from many domains and frequencies, then forecasts a
new series from its context alone, with no <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> updates. This is **zero-shot** forecasting. The four models here
were published within months of each other and share the <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> backbone. They differ in how a series becomes
tokens, what the output head predicts, and how they cope with differences in scale, frequency and dimension across
their corpora.

## Architecture

**Chronos** reuses a <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> unchanged apart from its vocabulary <Cite id="ansari2024" />. A series is scaled
and quantised into a vocabulary of $4096$ tokens: $4094$ value bins plus padding and end-of-sequence tokens. The main
models are <Gloss name="t5" /> encoder–decoders; a <Gloss name="gpt-2" /> decoder-only variant also works. Calendar and frequency information are
deliberately ignored: the model sees only the sequence of tokens.

**TimesFM** is a decoder-only transformer over patches <Cite id="das2024" />. The context is cut into non-overlapping
input patches of 32 values. A residual <Gloss name="mlp" /> maps each patch to a token, <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encodings</NoteLink> are added, and 20 causally
masked transformer layers (model width 1280, 16 heads) process the tokens. Each output token is mapped by another
residual MLP to an **output patch** of 128 values: the forecast for the 128 steps after that input patch.

**Moirai** is a masked encoder <Cite id="woo2024" />. The context and horizon are cut into patches whose size depends on
the data's frequency, from 8 for yearly and quarterly data to 64 or 128 for minute-level data, with one input and one
output projection per patch size. Horizon patches are replaced by a learnt mask embedding, and the encoder fills in
the distribution parameters for every masked patch in one pass. Every variable of a multivariate series, including
covariates, is flattened into one token sequence.

**Lag-Llama** follows the <Gloss name="llama" /> decoder: <Gloss name="rmsnorm" /> pre-normalisation and <NoteLink to="rotary-position-embedding">rotary position encodings</NoteLink>

<Cite id="rasul2023" />. Its token at time $t$ is a vector of past values at a fixed set of lags, chosen to cover
quarterly, monthly, weekly, daily, hourly and second-level cycles, together with date-time features and summary
statistics of the window. A distribution head outputs the degrees of freedom, location and scale of a
<NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student-$t$</NoteLink> for the next value.

## Scaling and tokenisation

Pretraining corpora mix series whose levels differ by orders of magnitude, so every model normalises each context
window.

- **Chronos** divides by the mean absolute value of the context, $s = \frac1C\sum_{i=1}^{C}\abs{x_i}$, and keeps zeros
  as zeros. It then quantises each scaled value to the nearest of $B = 4094$ bin centres spaced uniformly on
  $[-15, 15]$. The bin width is $30 / 4093 \approx 0.0073$ in scaled units.
- **TimesFM** subtracts the mean and divides by the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of the first input patch of the context.
- **Moirai** and **Lag-Llama** standardise each window. Lag-Llama found a robust version, subtracting the median and
  dividing by the interquartile range, to work well, and passes the window's statistics to the model as features.

Quantisation turns forecasting into classification. Chronos predicts a probability for each bin and is trained with
cross-entropy against the bin of the true next value. A categorical output can take any shape, including several
modes. The price is that the loss ignores the order of the bins: predicting the bin next to the true one costs as much
as predicting one far away.

**The bins bound the forecast.** A value maps to one of the bins between $c_1 = -15$ and $c_B = 15$, so after
rescaling no forecast can exceed $15\,s$ in absolute value. A series whose future grows beyond fifteen times the mean
absolute level of its context cannot be forecast correctly, whatever the network learns. The Chronos authors note this
limit for series with strong trends.

## Training

- **Chronos** trains on 28 public datasets. Two augmentations add diversity. **TSMixup** takes convex combinations of up
  to three series from different datasets, with weights from a symmetric <NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution">Dirichlet distribution</NoteLink>, to make 10 million new
  series. **KernelSynth** samples 1 million series from <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss> whose kernels are random combinations of
  linear kernels for trend, radial-basis kernels for smooth variation and periodic kernels for seasonality. The context
  is 512 values and the prediction length 64.
- **TimesFM** trains on about 100 billion time points: Google Trends queries, Wikipedia page views, synthetic series
  (<Gloss name="ar">autoregressive processes</Gloss>, sinusoids, trends and steps) and public benchmarks. Batches are 80% real and 20% synthetic,
  with equal weight on hourly, daily, weekly and monthly data. The model predicts the next output patch after every
  input patch in parallel, as a language model predicts every next token. Random masking of the start of the context
  teaches it every context length from 1 to 512.
- **Moirai** trains on LOTSA, a collection of open datasets with over 27 billion observations across nine domains,
  with context and prediction lengths sampled at random.
- **Lag-Llama** trains on 27 datasets from six domains, sampling datasets in proportion to their number of series and
  applying frequency-mixing and frequency-masking augmentation.

## What is distinctive

**Chronos: a language model for numbers.** It changes nothing in the transformer and shows that tokenisation plus
cross-entropy is enough for <NoteLink to="probabilistic-forecasting">probabilistic forecasting</NoteLink>. Forecasts are sample paths drawn one token at a time, then
dequantised and rescaled; quantiles come from the samples. On datasets in its training corpus it outperformed local
and task-specific models. On 27 datasets it had not seen, its zero-shot accuracy was comparable to, and sometimes
better than, models trained on those datasets <Cite id="ansari2024" />.

**TimesFM: long output patches.** A language model generates one token per pass. Direct multi-horizon prediction is more
accurate for long horizons, but a foundation model does not know the horizon in advance. An output patch four times as
long as the input patch is the compromise. Forecasting 256 steps from a 256-step context takes two passes, one for steps
257 to 384 and one for 385 to 512, where an output patch of 32 would take eight. Zero-shot, TimesFM had the lowest
scaled error on 18 Monash datasets, within significance of <Gloss name="n-beats" />. On the Darts collection it was within significance
of the best models. On four ETT datasets it was the most accurate, with a supervised <Gloss name="patchtst" /> within significance of it

<Cite id="das2024" /> <Cite id="nie2023" />.

**Moirai: any number of variables.** Flattening all variables into one sequence needs attention that knows which
tokens share a variable but does not depend on the variables' order. Moirai's attention score between the token at
time $i$ of variable $m$ and the token at time $j$ of variable $n$ is

$$
E_{ij,mn} = (\Wmat_Q\xvec_{i,m})^\top \Rmat_{i-j} (\Wmat_K\xvec_{j,n})
+ u^{(1)}\,\indicator\{m = n\} + u^{(2)}\,\indicator\{m \ne n\},
$$

where $\Rmat_{i-j}$ is the rotary matrix for the time offset and $u^{(1)}, u^{(2)}$ are learnt scalars. The variable
term depends only on whether two tokens share a variable, never on which variable. Relabelling the variables therefore
leaves every score unchanged, and any number of variables fits. The output is a mixture
$p(y) = \sum_{c} w_c\,p_c(y)$ of a Student-$t$ for general data, a

<NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink> for counts, a log-normal for right-skewed
data and a low-variance normal for near-certain values, so one head can fit data of any type. Zero-shot, Moirai was
competitive with or better than models trained on each target dataset <Cite id="woo2024" />.

**Lag-Llama: lags as tokens.** Each token carries values from a week, a month or a year earlier, so seasonal context is
present at every step without a long attention span. The head is a

<NoteLink to="deepar">DeepAR</NoteLink>-style parametric likelihood <Cite id="salinas2020" />, and forecasts are sample
paths. It is by far the smallest of the four. Zero-shot it was competitive, and after fine-tuning on a small fraction of
a new dataset it reached state-of-the-art accuracy <Cite id="rasul2023" />.

## Worked example

Chronos receives the context $(10, 20, 30)$. The scale is $s = (10 + 20 + 30)/3 = 20$, so the scaled values are
$(0.5, 1, 1.5)$. Bin centres are $c_j = -15 + (j - 1) \times 30/4093$. The value $1$ lies $16$ units above $-15$, which
is $16 / 0.00733 \approx 2182.9$ bin widths, so it falls in bin $j = 2184$ with centre $1.0005$. The three values fall
in bins $2116$, $2184$ and $2252$. Mapped back and multiplied by $s$, they become $10.04$, $20.01$ and $29.98$: the
quantisation error is at most half a bin width, $0.0037 \times 20 \approx 0.073$ in the original units. No forecast
from this context can exceed $15 \times 20 = 300$.

## Comparison

- **Probabilistic output.** Chronos, Moirai and Lag-Llama give full predictive distributions. The TimesFM paper trains
  a point forecaster and notes that quantile heads could be added. Score the probabilistic ones with the
  <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">CRPS</NoteLink> or the quantile loss, not only with point errors.
- **Multivariate structure.** Only Moirai models several variables and covariates jointly. The others forecast each
  series alone.
- **Cost at inference.** Chronos and Lag-Llama sample one value per forward pass and need many sample paths. TimesFM
  needs one pass per 128 steps. Moirai needs one pass for the whole horizon.
- **Evaluation risk.** A zero-shot claim holds only for datasets absent from pretraining. Large corpora overlap public
  benchmarks, so the papers separate in-corpus and held-out results. Any comparison should check the overlap.
