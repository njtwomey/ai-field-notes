<Definition>
**DeepAR** models the future of each time series $i$ as a product of one-step conditional distributions
<Cite id="salinas2020" />:

$$
p(z_{i,t_0:T} \mid z_{i,1:t_0-1}, \xvec_{i,1:T}) = \prod_{t=t_0}^{T} \ell\paren{z_{i,t} \mid \thetavec(\hvec_{i,t})}, \qquad
\hvec_{i,t} = h(\hvec_{i,t-1}, z_{i,t-1}, \xvec_{i,t}; \Phi).
$$

$z_{i,t}$ is the value of series $i$ at time $t$, $\xvec_{i,t}$ are covariates known for all $t$, $h$ is a multi-layer
LSTM <Cite id="hochreiter1997" /> with parameters $\Phi$ shared by every series, $\thetavec(\hvec)$ is a small output
layer, and $\ell$ is a fixed likelihood such as a Gaussian or a negative binomial. Forecasts are joint samples of
$z_{i,t_0:T}$ drawn one step at a time.
</Definition>

## The forecasting problem

A retailer has $N$ related series, one per product, with $N$ in the thousands or millions. Many series are short, many
are intermittent counts, and their scales span orders of magnitude. A classical local model such as ARIMA or
exponential smoothing fits each series separately. A short series then has too little data to estimate its own
parameters, and patterns shared across products (a holiday spike, a weekly cycle) are learnt again for every series.

DeepAR fits one **global** model to all series. The network parameters $\Phi$ are shared, so a pattern seen in many
series is learnt once. The output is a full predictive distribution, from which any quantile or any function of the
future path (such as total demand over the next four weeks) can be computed.

The time index splits into two ranges. The **conditioning range** $[1, t_0 - 1]$ holds observed values. The
**prediction range** $[t_0, T]$ holds the values to forecast. During training both ranges lie in the past, so every
$z_{i,t}$ is observed.

## Architecture

At each step $t$ the network receives three inputs: its previous hidden state $\hvec_{i,t-1}$, the previous value
$z_{i,t-1}$, and the covariates $\xvec_{i,t}$. The model is **autoregressive** because it consumes the last observation,
and **recurrent** because the hidden state carries information forward. The paper uses the same network, with the same
weights, over both ranges. The state at the end of the conditioning range, $\hvec_{i,t_0-1}$, summarises the history.
The initial state $\hvec_{i,0}$ and $z_{i,0}$ are zero.

<DeepArDiagram />

The covariates carry calendar information (day of week, hour of day, week or month of year), an **age** feature (the
distance to the series' first observation) and any known future inputs such as a planned price or promotion. A
categorical item identifier or product category enters through a learnt embedding. All covariates are standardised to
zero mean and unit variance <Cite id="salinas2020" />.

An affine layer maps $\hvec_{i,t}$ to the parameters of $\ell$. Parameters that must be positive pass through the
softplus function $\operatorname{softplus}(a) = \log(1 + e^a)$.

- **Gaussian likelihood** for real-valued data: $\mu = \wvec_\mu^\top\hvec + b_\mu$ and
  $\sigma = \operatorname{softplus}(\wvec_\sigma^\top\hvec + b_\sigma)$.
- **Negative binomial likelihood** for non-negative counts, parameterised by its mean $\mu > 0$ and shape $\alpha > 0$:

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{NB}}(z \mid \mu, \alpha) = \frac{\Gamma(z + 1/\alpha)}{\Gamma(z + 1)\,\Gamma(1/\alpha)}
\paren{\frac{1}{1 + \alpha\mu}}^{1/\alpha} \paren{\frac{\alpha\mu}{1 + \alpha\mu}}^{z}, \qquad
\var[z] = \mu + \alpha\mu^2.
$$

Both $\mu$ and $\alpha$ come from softplus layers. The shape $\alpha$ sets how far the variance exceeds the mean. At
$\alpha \to 0$ the distribution tends to a Poisson with mean $\mu$. This is the
<NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink> in its mean and dispersion form. Any
likelihood with a cheap sampler and a differentiable log-density fits the same slot, for example a
<NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student-$t$</NoteLink> for heavy tails or a Bernoulli for binary data.

## Deriving the training objective

The joint density of a whole window factorises by the chain rule of probability, with no approximation:

$$
p(z_{i,1:T} \mid \xvec_{i,1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(z_{i,t} \mid z_{i,1:t-1}, \xvec_{i,1:T}).
$$

DeepAR models each factor as $\ell(z_{i,t} \mid \thetavec(\hvec_{i,t}))$. The hidden state $\hvec_{i,t}$ is a
deterministic function of $z_{i,1:t-1}$ and $\xvec_{i,1:t}$, computed by unrolling the recurrence. Conditioning on
$\hvec_{i,t}$ is therefore the same as conditioning on the observed history. Taking logarithms turns the product into a
sum, and summing over independent series gives the log-likelihood

$$
\mathcal{L}(\Phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=t_0}^{T} \log \ell\paren{z_{i,t} \mid \thetavec(\hvec_{i,t})}.
$$

Every quantity in $\mathcal{L}$ is observed during training. A state-space model has latent states that must be
integrated out, which requires filtering. DeepAR has no latent variables: $\hvec_{i,t}$ is computed, not inferred. The
gradient $\nabla_\Phi\mathcal{L}$ is therefore exact and is obtained by
<NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink> through time, and $\Phi$ is fitted by stochastic
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. Because the encoder and decoder share weights, the
paper also includes the terms for $t < t_0$ in the sum, which is the same as setting $t_0$ to the start of the window.

For the Gaussian likelihood, one term of $-\mathcal{L}$ is

$$
-\log \ell_G(z \mid \mu, \sigma) = \tfrac12\log(2\pi) + \log\sigma + \frac{(z - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
$$

The squared error is weighted by $1/\sigma^2$, and the $\log\sigma$ term stops the network from inflating $\sigma$ to
make every error cheap. The network learns a mean and an uncertainty jointly.

Training examples are windows cut from each series at many start points, with fixed conditioning and prediction
lengths. A window may start before the series' first observation, in which case the missing targets are padded with
zeros. This teaches the model how new series behave. Absolute time is then visible only through covariates, not
through position in the window.

<Derivation title="Teacher forcing and the train–test mismatch">
In the training objective, $\hvec_{i,t}$ is computed from the true $z_{i,t-1}$. At prediction time the true value is
unknown for $t \ge t_0$, and a sample $\tilde z_{i,t-1}$ is fed back instead. Training on true inputs while predicting
from the model's own outputs is called **teacher forcing**, and the gap between the two input distributions can
compound over the horizon. Scheduled sampling mixes model samples into training inputs to close the gap. The DeepAR
authors report that variants of scheduled sampling gave no significant accuracy gain on their forecasting data and
slowed convergence <Cite id="salinas2020" />.
</Derivation>

## Scale handling

Retail series follow a rough power law: most items sell a few units a week and a few items sell thousands. Two
problems follow.

- **Input and output scale.** The autoregressive input $z_{i,t-1}$ and the output mean $\mu$ both scale with the series,
  but the network's nonlinearities work in a limited range. DeepAR divides the autoregressive inputs by an item scale
  $\nu_i$ and multiplies the scale-dependent likelihood parameters back. The paper uses
  $\nu_i = 1 + \frac{1}{t_0}\sum_{t=1}^{t_0} z_{i,t}$, the mean over the conditioning range plus one. For the negative
  binomial with network outputs $o_\mu$ and $o_\alpha$, $\mu = \nu_i\operatorname{softplus}(o_\mu)$ and
  $\alpha = \operatorname{softplus}(o_\alpha)/\sqrt{\nu_i}$. Counts cannot simply be divided by $\nu_i$ before
  modelling, because the result would no longer be integer.
- **Sampling imbalance.** Uniform sampling of training windows rarely visits the few large series. DeepAR samples a
  window from series $i$ with probability proportional to $\nu_i$.

The $\sqrt{\nu_i}$ in the shape follows from the variance formula. With $\mu = \nu_i\tilde\mu$ and
$\alpha = \tilde\alpha/\sqrt{\nu_i}$, the variance is $\nu_i\tilde\mu + \nu_i^{3/2}\tilde\alpha\tilde\mu^2$. It grows
faster than the Poisson variance $\nu_i\tilde\mu$ and slower than the constant-coefficient-of-variation growth
$\nu_i^2$.

## Forecasting by ancestral sampling

Once trained, the model draws joint sample paths from $p(z_{i,t_0:T} \mid z_{i,1:t_0-1}, \xvec_{i,1:T})$:

1. Run the recurrence over the conditioning range with observed values to get $\hvec_{i,t_0-1}$.
2. For $t = t_0, \dots, T$: compute $\tilde\hvec_{i,t} = h(\tilde\hvec_{i,t-1}, \tilde z_{i,t-1}, \xvec_{i,t}; \Phi)$
   and draw $\tilde z_{i,t} \sim \ell(\cdot \mid \thetavec(\tilde\hvec_{i,t}))$, starting from
   $\tilde\hvec_{i,t_0-1} = \hvec_{i,t_0-1}$ and $\tilde z_{i,t_0-1} = z_{i,t_0-1}$.
3. Repeat $S$ times to get $S$ paths.

Each path is an exact draw from the model's joint distribution. The chain-rule factorisation says that drawing
$z_{t_0}$ from its marginal, then $z_{t_0+1}$ from its conditional given the drawn $z_{t_0}$, and so on, produces a
sample from the product of the factors, which is the joint. This procedure is **ancestral sampling**.

Quantiles of $z_{i,t}$ for each $t$ are the empirical quantiles across the $S$ paths. Because the paths are joint,
they also answer questions about sums or maxima over the horizon, which per-step quantiles cannot. The spread of
the paths widens with the horizon because every sampled value feeds its noise into the next step.

## Worked example: one likelihood term

Take one step of a count series with network outputs giving $\mu = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.5$, and suppose the observed
value is $z = 2$. Then $1/\alpha = 2$ and $\alpha\mu = 2$, so

$$
\ell_{\mathrm{NB}}(2 \mid 4, 0.5) = \frac{\Gamma(4)}{\Gamma(3)\,\Gamma(2)} \paren{\tfrac13}^{2} \paren{\tfrac23}^{2}
= 3 \cdot \tfrac19 \cdot \tfrac49 = \tfrac{4}{27} \approx 0.148.
$$

The term contributes $\log(4/27) \approx -1.910$ to $\mathcal{L}$. The predictive variance is
$4 + 0.5 \cdot 16 = 12$, three times the Poisson variance of the same mean.

The scale factor is computed from the conditioning range. A series with conditioning values $0, 3, 5, 4, 8$ has mean
$4$ and $\nu_i = 5$. The network sees inputs $0, 0.6, 1, 0.8, 1.6$.

## Results reported

On the paper's datasets (automotive parts demand, Amazon retail demand, electricity and traffic), DeepAR improved accuracy over the
state-of-the-art baselines by about 15% <Cite id="salinas2020" />. A full training and prediction run on 500,000
series took under ten hours on a single GPU instance.

## Assumptions

- Series are conditionally independent given their covariates and $\Phi$. DeepAR shares parameters across series but
  does not model their joint distribution at one time.
- The one-step likelihood is from a fixed parametric family. A wrong family (a Gaussian for spiky counts) gives
  miscalibrated intervals however well the mean is fitted.
- Covariates in the prediction range must be known at forecast time.

## Pitfalls

- Per-step quantiles from sample paths are noisy for small $S$. Tail quantiles need many paths.
- The recurrence is sequential in $t$. Training and sampling cost grow linearly with the window length, and sampling
  cannot be parallelised across the horizon.
- Scaling by the conditioning mean fails for series whose conditioning range is all zeros or dominated by an outlier;
  the $+1$ in $\nu_i$ only prevents division by zero.
- Probabilistic forecasts should be evaluated with proper scoring rules such as the
  <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">CRPS or quantile loss</NoteLink>, not only by the error of the median.
