<Definition>
A **deep state space model** forecasts each series $z^{(i)}_{1:T_i}$ with a <NoteLink to="linear-gaussian-state-space-models">linear-Gaussian state space model</NoteLink> whose
parameters $\Theta^{(i)}_t$ change over time and are produced by a recurrent network from the series' covariates
<Cite id="rangapuram2018" />:

$$
\Theta^{(i)}_t = \Psi(\xvec^{(i)}_{1:t}; \Phi), \qquad
p(z^{(i)}_{1:T_i} \mid \xvec^{(i)}_{1:T_i}; \Phi) = p_{\mathrm{SS}}(z^{(i)}_{1:T_i} \mid \Theta^{(i)}_{1:T_i}).
$$

$\Psi$ is a multi-layer <Gloss name="lstm" /> followed by affine maps and range-constraining transforms, $\Phi$ are its parameters
shared by all series, and $p_{\mathrm{SS}}$ is the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> of the state space model with the latent state
integrated out.
</Definition>

## Motivation

Classical state space models, such as exponential smoothing in innovations form or structural models with level,
trend and seasonal states, encode known structure. That structure makes them data-efficient and interpretable, but
each series is fitted separately, and covariates must be chosen by hand. <NoteLink to="neural-forecasting-models">Neural forecasters</NoteLink> such as
<NoteLink to="deepar">DeepAR</NoteLink> learn patterns across series from raw covariates, but need more data and give
no handle on smoothness or components.

DeepState keeps the linear-Gaussian state space model as the per-series likelihood and moves only the choice of its
parameters into a network. The network is shared, so patterns are learnt across series. The model for any single
series is still linear and Gaussian, so the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> computes its
likelihood and forecast exactly.

## The per-series model

Drop the series index $i$ for this section. The latent state $\lvec_t \in \reals^L$ encodes components such as level,
trend and seasonality. The model is

$$
\lvec_t = \Fmat_t\lvec_{t-1} + \gvec_t\varepsilon_t, \qquad
z_t = \avec_t^\top\lvec_{t-1} + b_t + \sigma_t\epsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t, \epsilon_t \sim \Gauss(0, 1),
$$

with $\lvec_0 \sim \Gauss(\muvec_0, \diag(\sigmavec_0^2))$. $\Fmat_t$ ($L \times L$) is the <NoteLink to="markov-chain">transition matrix</NoteLink>,
$\gvec_t \in \reals^L$ the innovation strength, $\avec_t \in \reals^L$ and $b_t$ the observation weights and offset,
and $\sigma_t > 0$ the observation noise. The structure of $\Fmat_t$ and $\avec_t$ fixes which components the state
holds. In the simplest case, a <NoteLink to="simple-exponential-smoothing">local level model</NoteLink>, $L = 1$, $F_t = 1$ and $a_t = 1$: the level drifts as a <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>
with step size $g_t$ and is observed with noise $\sigma_t$.

The structure of the state (which components it holds, and hence the pattern of $\Fmat_t$ and $\avec_t$) is chosen
per dataset, for example a day-of-week or hour-of-day seasonal model <Cite id="rangapuram2018" />.

The last LSTM layer's output is mapped to the time-varying parameters by affine maps followed by elementwise transforms
that keep each parameter in its valid range, such as a softplus for noise scales.

The network reads only the covariates $\xvec_t$ (for example day of week, product category, price), not the target.
All information from the observed values $z_{1:t}$ enters through the Kalman filter. This is the main structural
difference from DeepAR, whose network consumes $z_{t-1}$ directly.

## Deriving the likelihood

The marginal likelihood of one series factorises by the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> into one-step predictive densities, the
**prediction error decomposition**:

$$
\log p_{\mathrm{SS}}(z_{1:T} \mid \Theta_{1:T}) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(z_t \mid z_{1:t-1}, \Theta_{1:t}).
$$

Each factor is Gaussian and is computed by the Kalman filter. Suppose that after seeing $z_{1:t-1}$ the state that
emits $z_t$ has posterior $\lvec_{t-1} \mid z_{1:t-1} \sim \Gauss(\mvec_{t-1}, \Pmat_{t-1})$. The observation
$z_t = \avec_t^\top\lvec_{t-1} + b_t + \sigma_t\epsilon_t$ is a linear function of this Gaussian plus independent
Gaussian noise, so

$$
z_t \mid z_{1:t-1} \sim \Gauss\paren{\avec_t^\top\mvec_{t-1} + b_t,\ \avec_t^\top\Pmat_{t-1}\avec_t + \sigma_t^2}.
$$

Conditioning on $z_t$ updates the state posterior by the Kalman gain $\kvec_t = \Pmat_{t-1}\avec_t / S_t$, where
$S_t = \avec_t^\top\Pmat_{t-1}\avec_t + \sigma_t^2$ is the predictive variance:

$$
\mvec'_{t-1} = \mvec_{t-1} + \kvec_t\paren{z_t - \avec_t^\top\mvec_{t-1} - b_t}, \qquad
\Pmat'_{t-1} = \Pmat_{t-1} - \kvec_t S_t\kvec_t^\top.
$$

The transition then moves the state forward: $\mvec_t = \Fmat_t\mvec'_{t-1}$ and
$\Pmat_t = \Fmat_t\Pmat'_{t-1}\Fmat_t^\top + \gvec_t\gvec_t^\top$. Summing the Gaussian log-densities over $t$ gives the
exact log-likelihood of the series.

Every step is a sequence of <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix products</NoteLink> and one scalar division, so the whole computation is differentiable in
$\Theta_{1:T}$ and hence in $\Phi$. The training objective is

$$
\mathcal{L}(\Phi) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_{\mathrm{SS}}\paren{z^{(i)}_{1:T_i} \mid \Theta^{(i)}_{1:T_i}(\Phi)},
$$

maximised by stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> ascent with gradients from automatic differentiation through the filter
<Cite id="rangapuram2018" />.

## Forecasting

For series $i$ with history $z_{1:T}$ and covariates $\xvec_{1:T+\tau}$:

1. Unroll the <Gloss name="rnn" /> over the training range to get $\Theta_{1:T}$ and run the Kalman filter to get the posterior of the
   last state, $p(\lvec_T \mid z_{1:T})$.
2. Unroll the RNN over the prediction range to get $\Theta_{T+1:T+\tau}$ from the future covariates.
3. Sample $\lvec_T$ from its posterior and apply the transition and observation equations recursively to get sample
   paths $\hat z_{T+1:T+\tau}$.

Because the model is linear-Gaussian given $\Theta$, the forecast distribution at each step is also available in
closed form. Sampling is used when joint quantities over the horizon are needed.

## What the structure buys

- **Small data.** The state space structure constrains forecasts to smooth, interpretable components, which limits
  overfitting when series are short.
- **Interpretability.** The per-series parameters $\Theta^{(i)}_t$ can be inspected and even changed, because each
  one has a meaning in the state space model (a noise level, a prior seasonal profile).
- **Exact inference.** No sampling or variational approximation is needed in training.

## Assumptions

- Observations are Gaussian given the state. Counts and intermittent demand fit poorly unless transformed.
- Dynamics are linear given $\Theta_t$. Nonlinear effects must come through the covariates that drive $\Theta_t$.
- Series are independent given their covariates and $\Phi$.

## Pitfalls

- The Kalman recursion is sequential in $t$, so training cost is linear in series length and cannot be parallelised
  over time.
- The chosen state structure (which components, which seasonal period) is a modelling decision the network cannot
  undo. A missing seasonal component is not learnt from data.
- Numerical care is needed: covariance updates in the plain form can lose symmetry or positive definiteness in long
  series.
