<Definition>
**N-BEATS** (neural basis expansion analysis for time series) maps a look-back window $\xvec \in \reals^{L}$ of one
series to a forecast $\hat\yvec \in \reals^{H}$ of its next $H$ values <Cite id="oreshkin2020" />. It is a sequence of
blocks. Block $\ell$ reads its input $\xvec_\ell$, computes expansion coefficients $\thetavec^b_\ell$ and
$\thetavec^f_\ell$ with a fully connected network, and turns them into a **backcast** $\hat\xvec_\ell$ and a **partial
forecast** $\hat\yvec_\ell$ through basis layers. Blocks are joined by **doubly residual stacking**:

$$
\xvec_1 = \xvec, \qquad \xvec_{\ell+1} = \xvec_\ell - \hat\xvec_\ell, \qquad \hat\yvec = \sum_\ell \hat\yvec_\ell.
$$

</Definition>

## Motivation

In the 2018 M4 competition, the most accurate entry was a hybrid of an LSTM network and Holt-Winters exponential
smoothing. The competition's organisers concluded that hybrids and combinations were the way forward. N-BEATS was built
to test whether a network with no time-series-specific components could do better, and whether constraining its
outputs could make its forecasts interpretable <Cite id="oreshkin2020" />. It forecasts one univariate series at a
time, from its own past values only, with weights shared across all series of a dataset.

## The block

Block $\ell$ has two parts.

**Coefficient network.** Four fully connected layers with ReLU activations, followed by two linear heads:

$$
\hvec_{\ell} = \operatorname{FC}_{\ell,4}\big(\operatorname{FC}_{\ell,3}(\operatorname{FC}_{\ell,2}(
\operatorname{FC}_{\ell,1}(\xvec_\ell)))\big), \qquad
\thetavec^b_\ell = \Wmat^b_\ell \hvec_\ell, \qquad \thetavec^f_\ell = \Wmat^f_\ell \hvec_\ell.
$$

**Basis layers.** Each coefficient vector weights a set of basis vectors:

$$
\hat\yvec_\ell = \sum_{i} \theta^f_{\ell,i}\, \vvec^f_i, \qquad \hat\xvec_\ell = \sum_i \theta^b_{\ell,i}\, \vvec^b_i.
$$

The forecast basis vectors $\vvec^f_i \in \reals^H$ are waveforms over the horizon, and the backcast basis vectors
$\vvec^b_i \in \reals^L$ are waveforms over the look-back window. The basis is either learnt or fixed.

## Doubly residual stacking

A standard residual network adds each layer's input to its output. N-BEATS has two residual branches.

- **Backcast branch.** Each block subtracts its backcast from its input and passes the remainder on. Unrolling the
  recursion gives $\xvec_{\ell+1} = \xvec - \sum_{j \le \ell} \hat\xvec_j$. Block $\ell + 1$ sees only the part of the
  window that earlier blocks have not explained. The backcast is not a forecast target. Its job is to remove what the
  block has already accounted for.
- **Forecast branch.** Each block's partial forecast is added to the total, $\hat\yvec = \sum_\ell \hat\yvec_\ell$.
  Blocks are grouped into stacks, and the partial forecasts can be summed per stack.

Both branches are sums, so gradients reach every block directly, which lets the network be very deep. The generic
configuration has 30 stacks of one block each.

## Generic and interpretable bases

**Generic.** The basis layers are learnt linear maps, $\hat\yvec_\ell = \Vmat^f_\ell\thetavec^f_\ell + \bvec^f_\ell$
with $\Vmat^f_\ell \in \reals^{H \times \dim\thetavec^f_\ell}$. Its columns are waveforms over the horizon, but nothing
constrains their shape, and the learnt ones show no structure. This is **N-BEATS-G**.

**Interpretable.** Fixed bases force stacks to produce the components of a classical decomposition. This is
**N-BEATS-I**. Time over the horizon is $\tvec = (0, 1, \dots, H-1)/H$, which runs from $0$ to $(H-1)/H$.

- **Trend stack.** The basis is a polynomial of small degree $p$:

  $$
  \hat\yvec^{\,\mathrm{tr}}_\ell = \Tmat\,\thetavec^f_\ell, \qquad \Tmat = [\ones, \tvec, \tvec^2, \dots, \tvec^p],
  $$

  where powers are elementwise. With $p$ equal to 2 or 3 the output varies slowly across the horizon, as a trend should.

- **Seasonality stack.** The basis is a truncated Fourier series:

  $$
  \hat y^{\,\mathrm{seas}}_{\ell}(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor H/2 \rfloor - 1} \theta^f_{\ell,i} \cos(2\pi i t) +
  \theta^f_{\ell,i+\lfloor H/2 \rfloor} \sin(2\pi i t).
  $$

  Harmonic $i$ completes $i$ cycles over the horizon. The backcast uses the same functions over the look-back window.

The interpretable model has a trend stack followed by a seasonality stack, three blocks each. Within a stack, all blocks
share their weights as well as their basis.

**Why the stack outputs are a trend and a seasonal component.** Every block in the trend stack outputs a vector in the
column space of $\Tmat$. A sum of polynomials of degree at most $p$ is a polynomial of degree at most $p$, so the trend
stack's total output $\sum_{\ell \in \mathrm{trend}} \Tmat\thetavec^f_\ell = \Tmat \sum_\ell \thetavec^f_\ell$ is
again a single polynomial of degree at most $p$ in $t$. Likewise, every seasonal block's output is a combination of
$\cos(2\pi i t)$ and $\sin(2\pi i t)$, and so is their sum. Each such function has period $1/i$ in $t$, which divides
$1$, so the seasonal stack's output is a periodic function whose period divides the window length. The forecast is
therefore exactly

$$
\hat\yvec = \underbrace{\Tmat\,\alphavec}_{\text{trend}} + \underbrace{\Smat\,\betavec}_{\text{seasonality}},
$$

with coefficients $\alphavec$ and $\betavec$ chosen by the networks for each input window. The order of
the stacks matters. The trend stack's backcast is subtracted before the seasonality stack runs, so the seasonality
stack models a detrended window.

## Training and ensembling

Each model is trained on windows sampled from the most recent part of each series' training data. The loss is a
scale-free forecast error: symmetric MAPE, MASE or MAPE. The reported forecasts are the median of an ensemble of 180
models, which differ in three ways: the loss (three choices), the look-back length ($L = 2H, 3H, \dots, 7H$, six
choices) and the random initialisation

<Cite id="oreshkin2020" />. The authors found the ensemble a stronger regulariser than dropout or weight decay, which
improved single models but hurt the ensemble.

## Worked example

Take a horizon $H = 4$, so $\tvec = (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75)$, and a trend basis of degree $p = 2$:

$$
\Tmat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0.25 & 0.0625 \\ 1 & 0.5 & 0.25 \\ 1 & 0.75 & 0.5625 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

If the trend stack's coefficients sum to $\alphavec = (1, 2, -1)$, its output is $1 + 2t - t^2$ at each time:
$(1, 1.4375, 1.75, 1.9375)$. The first seasonal harmonic over the same grid is $\cos(2\pi t) = (1, 0, -1, 0)$ and
$\sin(2\pi t) = (0, 1, 0, -1)$. A seasonal coefficient of $2$ on the cosine and $0$ on the sine gives $(2, 0, -2, 0)$.
The forecast is the sum, $(3, 1.4375, -0.25, 1.9375)$, and the two stack outputs are its trend and seasonal parts.

## Results reported

On the 100,000 series of M4, the ensemble of generic and interpretable models reached a symmetric MAPE of 11.135 and an
overall weighted average (OWA) of 0.795. The competition winner scored 11.374 and 0.821. OWA averages symmetric MAPE and
MASE, each divided by that of a seasonally adjusted naive forecast, so lower is better and $1$ is naive. The paper
reports an 11% improvement over the statistical benchmark and 3% over the winner. N-BEATS was also the most accurate
model on the M3 and TOURISM datasets. The interpretable model's accuracy was close to the generic model's: 11.174
against 11.168 on M4 <Cite id="oreshkin2020" />.

## Assumptions

- The look-back length $L$ is fixed for a trained model. Series shorter than $L$ are padded with zeros and masked.
- The model is univariate and uses no covariates. Known future inputs such as holidays cannot enter.
- The seasonal basis has harmonics of the horizon length. A seasonal period that does not divide the horizon is
  represented only approximately.

## Pitfalls

- The output layer has $H$ units per block and the input $L$ units, and both grow with the horizon. For horizons of
  hundreds of steps the model becomes large and its forecasts can be jagged. <NoteLink to="n-hits">N-HiTS</NoteLink>
  addresses this with pooling and interpolation.
- Interpretability holds only for the fixed bases. The generic model's stacks are not trend and seasonality.
- The accuracy figures come from a large ensemble. A single N-BEATS model is less accurate than the reported median.
