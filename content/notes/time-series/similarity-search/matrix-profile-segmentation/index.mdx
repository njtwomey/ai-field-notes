<Definition>
Let $I_1, \dots, I_n$ be the <NoteLink to="matrix-profile">matrix profile</NoteLink> index of a series: $I_i$ is the start of the nearest neighbour of the
subsequence starting at $i$. Draw an arc from each $i$ to $I_i$. The arc curve $AC_k$ counts the arcs that pass over
position $k$, that is, arcs with $\min(i, I_i) < k < \max(i, I_i)$. FLUSS (Fast Low-cost Unipotent <NoteLink to="semantic-segmentation">Semantic
Segmentation</NoteLink>) divides it by the idealised arc curve $IAC_k = 2k(n - k)/n$, its <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> when neighbours are placed
at random, to give the corrected arc curve $CAC_k = \min(AC_k / IAC_k, 1)$. Positions where $CAC$ is close to 0 are
regime boundaries. FLOSS is the online version <Cite id="gharghabi2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Semantic segmentation divides a series into regimes that correspond to different states of the process: walking,
then running; sleep stage 2, then REM; a machine idling, then loaded. Statistical
<NoteLink to="change-point-detection">change-point detection</NoteLink> looks for shifts in a model's parameters, such
as the mean or variance. Many regime changes preserve both. A switch between two gaits can leave the mean and variance
of an accelerometer unchanged while changing the shape of every cycle. FLUSS detects changes in shape, needs only the
subsequence length $m$ and the number of regimes, and reuses the matrix profile index.

## Mechanism

### Arcs rarely cross a boundary

If a series consists of regime A followed by regime B, the nearest neighbour of a subsequence in A is almost always
another subsequence in A, because B contains different shapes. The same holds for B. Arcs therefore stay on their own
side, and the count of arcs over the boundary is close to 0. Away from boundaries, arcs from both sides of a
position pass over it, and the count is large.

The raw count cannot be read directly. Near the ends of the series few arcs can pass over any position, because there
is little room on one side. A dip near an edge is expected even with no boundary. Dividing by the expected count
removes this effect.

### The idealised arc curve

Assume the series has no regime structure, so that each $I_i$ is a uniformly random position among the $n$
positions. An arc from $i$ passes over $k$ when $i$ and $I_i$ lie on opposite sides of $k$. For $i < k$ that happens
when $I_i > k$, with probability about $(n - k)/n$. For $i > k$ it happens when $I_i < k$, with probability about
$k/n$. There are about $k$ positions before $k$ and $n - k$ after it, so the expected count is

$$
IAC_k = \expect\brack{AC_k} \approx k \cdot \frac{n - k}{n} + (n - k) \cdot \frac{k}{n} = \frac{2k(n - k)}{n} .
$$

This idealised arc curve is a parabola that is 0 at both ends and peaks at $n/2$ at the centre.

The corrected arc curve $CAC_k = \min(AC_k / IAC_k, 1)$ is near 1 where arcs cross as often as
chance predicts and near 0 where almost none cross. Within a few subsequence lengths of either end, $IAC$ is too small
for the ratio to be reliable, and $CAC$ is set to 1 there <Cite id="gharghabi2017" />.

### Extracting boundaries

The position of the minimum of $CAC$ is the first boundary. To find $r$ regimes, FLUSS takes the minimum, excludes a
zone of several subsequence lengths around it and repeats until $r - 1$ boundaries are found. The value of the minimum
also says how strong the boundary is. Values near 0 are unambiguous; values near 1 mean the series has no clear
second regime.

### FLOSS: online segmentation

In a stream the right half of each arc is unknown: a subsequence's nearest neighbour may not have arrived yet. FLOSS
(Fast Low-cost Online Semantic Segmentation) uses only arcs that point to the past, from each subsequence to its left
nearest neighbour, over a sliding window of recent data <Cite id="gharghabi2017" />. These arcs never change once
drawn (<NoteLink to="streaming-and-incremental-matrix-profile">left matrix profile</NoteLink>). One-directional arcs
have a different expected count, so FLOSS corrects the arc curve with the idealised curve for that case. The cost per
new point is that of one incremental matrix profile update.

## Worked example

In the figure's default series, $n = 600$ points form two regimes that meet at position 300, and $m = 40$ gives 561
subsequences.

- Only 24 of the 561 arcs cross position 300, and no arc joins a subsequence that starts before 260 to one that starts
  after 300.
- At position 275, $AC = 0$ while $IAC = 2 \cdot 275 \cdot 286 / 561 = 280.4$, so $CAC = 0$. That is the minimum, and it
  lies 25 positions before the true change: subsequences starting there span the boundary.
- Away from the boundary, at position 100, $AC = 70$ and $IAC = 164.3$, so $CAC = 0.43$. The value is below 1 because
  the series is periodic, and each subsequence's nearest neighbour is usually only a few beats away. Short arcs pass
  over fewer positions than random ones.

## Cost

FLUSS costs one matrix profile, $O(n^2)$, plus $O(n)$ for the arc curve. The count is computed with a difference array:
add 1 at $\min(i, I_i) + 1$, subtract 1 at $\max(i, I_i)$, and take a running sum. FLOSS costs $O(w)$ per new point for
a window of $w$ points.

## Trade-offs

- **Only shape changes.** Regimes that differ only in level or amplitude look identical after z-normalisation. A
  statistical change-point detector such as <NoteLink to="cumulative-sum-control-chart">CUSUM</NoteLink> finds those.
- **Repeated regimes.** If regime A returns after B, subsequences in the second A find neighbours in the first, and
  their arcs cross B. The dips at the boundaries of B become shallow.
- **The boundary is approximate.** The minimum can sit up to one subsequence length before the true change, where
  windows straddle the two regimes.
- **Short regimes.** A regime shorter than a few subsequence lengths contains too few subsequences to form its own
  cluster of arcs.
