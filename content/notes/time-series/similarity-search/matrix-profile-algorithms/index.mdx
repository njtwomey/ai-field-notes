<Definition>
A matrix profile algorithm computes, for a series $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and subsequence length $m$, the minimum of every
row of the $N \times N$ z-normalised distance matrix $d(i, j)$, with $N = n - m + 1$ and entries within an exclusion
zone $\abs{i - j} \le e$ ignored. The algorithms differ in the order in which they visit the $O(N^2)$ entries and in
how they compute each entry. STAMP visits whole rows in random order <Cite id="yeh2016" />; STOMP visits rows in
order, updating each from the last <Cite id="zhu2016" />; SCRIMP visits diagonals in random order <Cite id="zhu2018" />; SCAMP visits diagonals in
parallel tiles with a numerically stable update <Cite id="zimmerman2019" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Every entry of the matrix profile depends on a whole row of the distance matrix. Computing each distance from scratch
costs $O(m)$, so the brute-force profile costs $O(N^2 m)$. For $n = 10^6$ and $m = 100$ that is about $10^{14}$
multiply-adds. The algorithms below reduce the cost per entry to $O(1)$ and change the order of work so that a useful
approximation exists long before the exact answer.

All of them rest on one identity. With $Q_{ij} = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} x_{i+k} x_{j+k}$ the dot product of subsequences
$T_i$ and $T_j$, $\mu_i, \sigma_i$ their means and standard deviations, and $\rho_{ij}$ their Pearson correlation,

$$
d(i, j) = \sqrt{2m \paren{1 - \rho_{ij}}}, \qquad \rho_{ij} = \frac{Q_{ij} - m \mu_i \mu_j}{m \sigma_i \sigma_j} .
$$

The means and standard deviations cost $O(n)$ from cumulative sums. The algorithms differ in how they obtain
$Q_{ij}$.

## Mechanism

### STAMP: one row at a time, in random order

STAMP (Scalable Time series Anytime Matrix Profile) computes row $i$ of the distance matrix with MASS, the FFT-based
<NoteLink to="distance-profile-and-mass">distance profile</NoteLink>, in $O(n \log n)$ <Cite id="yeh2016" />. The
distance matrix is symmetric, so row $i$ also supplies one entry of every column. After processing row $i$, STAMP sets

$$
P_i \leftarrow \min_j d(i, j), \qquad P_j \leftarrow \min\paren{P_j, d(i, j)} \text{ for every } j .
$$

The total cost is $O(N n \log n) = O(n^2 \log n)$. Rows are processed in random order. After any number of rows,
every $P_j$ is an upper bound on its exact value, and the bound only decreases. STAMP is therefore an anytime
algorithm: it can be stopped at any moment and returns an approximate profile.

### Why the anytime approximation converges quickly

Let $r$ be the fraction of rows processed, in uniformly random order. Entry $P_i$ is exact as soon as row $i$ is
processed, or as soon as the row of its nearest neighbour $I_i$ is processed, because that row contains $d(I_i, i)$.
The probability that neither of the two rows is among the first $rN$ is
$\frac{(N - rN)(N - rN - 1)}{N(N - 1)} \approx (1 - r)^2$. The expected share of exact entries is therefore

$$
\expect\brack{\text{share exact}} \approx 1 - (1 - r)^2 = 2r - r^2 ,
$$

about twice the share of rows processed at the start. The error in the inexact entries falls faster still. Any
subsequence close to $T_i$, not only the nearest, brings $P_i$ close to its exact value. In a series with repeated
structure each subsequence has many near neighbours, and the chance that none of $k$ of them has been processed is
about $(1 - r)^k$. The figure replays STAMP on a synthetic series and shows the measured share of exact entries
following $1 - (1 - r)^2$.

### STOMP: constant-time updates along diagonals

Consecutive entries on a diagonal of the dot-product matrix share $m - 1$ products. Moving from $(i, j)$ to
$(i + 1, j + 1)$ drops the first product and adds a new last one:

$$
Q_{i+1, j+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} x_{i+1+k} x_{j+1+k} = Q_{ij} - x_i x_j + x_{i+m} x_{j+m} .
$$

STOMP (Scalable Time series Ordered-search Matrix Profile) computes the first row $Q_{1j}$ with one FFT. It then
derives each row from the one before with the update above, in $O(N)$ per row. Each entry costs $O(1)$, so the total
is $O(N^2)$ time and $O(n)$ memory, independent of $m$ <Cite id="zhu2016" />. The update for each column of a row
is independent of the others, which maps well to GPUs. Matrix Profile II used GPUs to compute the profile of a
series of 100 million points.

STOMP processes rows in order, so its partial result is exact for a prefix of the series and says nothing about the
rest. It is not an anytime algorithm.

### SCRIMP and PreSCRIMP: anytime again

SCRIMP (Scalable Column Independent Matrix Profile) processes whole diagonals in random order, each with the STOMP
update <Cite id="zhu2018" />. Its cost is the same $O(N^2)$ as STOMP, and its partial result is an upper bound on the
whole profile, as for STAMP.

PreSCRIMP provides a better starting point. It computes the distance profile of every $s$-th subsequence with MASS,
with $s$ a fraction of $m$ such as $m/4$. For each sampled subsequence $T_i$ with best match $T_j$, it also walks the
short diagonal segment through $(i, j)$ in both directions and updates the entries around it. The reasoning is that if
$T_i$ matches $T_j$, then $T_{i+1}$ probably matches $T_{j+1}$. SCRIMP++ runs PreSCRIMP first and then SCRIMP. The
approximate profile after PreSCRIMP alone is often close enough to find the top motifs <Cite id="zhu2018" />.

### SCAMP: a numerically stable update

The STOMP update accumulates rounding error along long diagonals, and the formula for $\rho_{ij}$ subtracts two large,
nearly equal numbers when the mean is large compared with the variation. SCAMP (SCAlable Matrix Profile) tracks the
centred covariance $C_{ij} = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} (x_{i+k} - \mu_i)(x_{j+k} - \mu_j)$ instead of the raw dot product
<Cite id="zimmerman2019" />. With

$$
f_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\paren{x_{i+m} - x_i}, \qquad g_i = \paren{x_{i+m} - \mu_{i+1}} + \paren{x_i - \mu_i},
$$

the diagonal update is

$$
C_{i+1, j+1} = C_{ij} + f_i g_j + f_j g_i ,
$$

and $\rho_{ij} = C_{ij} / (m \sigma_i \sigma_j)$. Every term is a difference between nearby values or a deviation from
a mean, so the arithmetic stays well conditioned in single precision. SCAMP computes $\rho$ rather than distance in
its inner loop, splits the distance matrix into tiles, and distributes the tiles across GPUs or cloud machines.

<Derivation title="Derivation of the covariance update">
Write $a = x_{i+m}$, $b = x_i$, $c = x_{j+m}$, $d = x_j$. From $C_{ij} = Q_{ij} - m \mu_i \mu_j$ and the dot-product
update,

$$
C_{i+1, j+1} - C_{ij} = (ac - bd) - m\paren{\mu_{i+1} \mu_{j+1} - \mu_i \mu_j} .
$$

The means update as $\mu_{i+1} = \mu_i + (a - b)/m$, so $a - b = m(\mu_{i+1} - \mu_i)$ and
$c - d = m(\mu_{j+1} - \mu_j)$. Expand $f_i g_j + f_j g_i$ with $f_i = (a - b)/2$, $f_j = (c - d)/2$,
$g_j = (c + d) - (\mu_{j+1} + \mu_j)$ and $g_i = (a + b) - (\mu_{i+1} + \mu_i)$. The terms without means give

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}\brack{(a - b)(c + d) + (c - d)(a + b)} = \tfrac{1}{2}(2ac - 2bd) = ac - bd .
$$

The terms with means give

$$
-\tfrac{m}{2}\brack{(\mu_{i+1} - \mu_i)(\mu_{j+1} + \mu_j) + (\mu_{j+1} - \mu_j)(\mu_{i+1} + \mu_i)}
= -m\paren{\mu_{i+1} \mu_{j+1} - \mu_i \mu_j} .
$$

The two parts sum to $C_{i+1, j+1} - C_{ij}$.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Take $x = (0, 1, 3, 2, 0, 1, 3, 2, 1, 0, 1, 4, 2, 0)$ with $m = 4$, positions counted from 1.

- **STOMP update.** $T_2 = (1, 3, 2, 0)$ and $T_6 = (1, 3, 2, 1)$ have $Q_{2,6} = 1 + 9 + 4 + 0 = 14$. The update gives
  $Q_{3,7} = 14 - x_2 x_6 + x_6 x_{10} = 14 - 1 + 0 = 13$. Directly, $T_3 = (3, 2, 0, 1)$ and $T_7 = (3, 2, 1, 0)$
  give $9 + 4 + 0 + 0 = 13$.
- **SCAMP update.** $\mu_2 = 1.5$, $\mu_6 = 1.75$, $\mu_3 = \mu_7 = 1.5$. Then $C_{2,6} = 14 - 4 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 1.75 = 3.5$.
  The update terms are $f_2 = (x_6 - x_2)/2 = 0$, $f_6 = (x_{10} - x_6)/2 = -0.5$, $g_2 = (1 - 1.5) + (1 - 1.5) = -1$
  and $g_6 = (0 - 1.5) + (1 - 1.75) = -2.25$. So $C_{3,7} = 3.5 + 0 \cdot (-2.25) + (-0.5)(-1) = 4$. Directly,
  $C_{3,7} = 13 - 4 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 1.5 = 4$.
- **Anytime bound.** The figure's series has 400 points, so $N = 369$ subsequences of length 32. Processing 10 % of
  the rows (37 rows) made 16.8 %, 18.2 % and 19.8 % of the entries exact for seeds 1, 2 and 3. The expected share is
  $1 - 0.9^2 = 0.19$.

## Cost

| Algorithm           | Time                        | Memory | Anytime | Notes                                            |
| ------------------- | --------------------------- | ------ | ------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Brute force         | $O(n^2 m)$                  | $O(n)$ | yes     | Direct distances                                 |
| STAMP               | $O(n^2 \log n)$             | $O(n)$ | yes     | MASS per row, random row order                   |
| STOMP               | $O(n^2)$                    | $O(n)$ | no      | Row-to-row dot-product update; one FFT for row 1 |
| SCRIMP              | $O(n^2)$                    | $O(n)$ | yes     | STOMP update, random diagonal order              |
| PreSCRIMP           | $O\paren{(n^2 / s) \log n}$ | $O(n)$ | yes     | MASS on every $s$-th subsequence, local refinement |
| SCAMP               | $O(n^2)$                    | $O(n)$ | no      | Covariance update; tiled across GPUs and machines |

The $O(n^2)$ bound is for the exact profile. It does not depend on $m$, because each entry costs $O(1)$ once the first
row or the first entry of each diagonal is known.

## Trade-offs

- **Exact versus anytime.** STOMP and SCAMP are the fastest route to the exact profile. STAMP and SCRIMP++ give a
  usable approximation after a small fraction of the work, which suits interactive exploration.
- **Numerical precision.** The raw dot-product update drifts over long diagonals in single precision. SCAMP's update
  avoids that drift. Double precision is safe for the STOMP update at moderate lengths.
- **Parallelism.** Diagonals are independent, so SCRIMP and SCAMP parallelise by diagonal or by tile. STOMP
  parallelises across the columns of each row, and STAMP across rows.
- **Memory.** No algorithm stores the distance matrix. The figure above stores it only to replay STAMP at different
  stopping points.
