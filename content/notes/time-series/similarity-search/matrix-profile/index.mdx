<Definition>
Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be a time series and $m$ a subsequence length. Write $T_i = (x_i, \dots, x_{i+m-1})$ for the
subsequence starting at $i$, and $d(i, j)$ for the Euclidean distance between $T_i$ and $T_j$ after each is
z-normalised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The matrix profile is the vector

$$
P_i = \min_{j \,:\, \abs{i - j} > e} d(i, j), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n - m + 1,
$$

and the matrix profile index $I_i$ records the minimising $j$. The exclusion zone $e$, typically $m/4$, rules out
trivial matches: subsequences that overlap $T_i$ and so resemble it for no interesting reason <Cite id="yeh2016" />.
</Definition>

## What it answers

Many time-series questions are questions about nearest neighbours between subsequences. Which pattern repeats? Which
stretch is unlike everything else? Where does the behaviour change? Answering them naively means comparing every
subsequence with every other one. The matrix profile computes that all-pairs comparison once, and keeps only the one
number per subsequence that most questions need: the distance to its nearest neighbour.

- **Motifs.** The smallest values of $P$ mark subsequences that have a close match elsewhere. The global minimum and
  its index give the best-matching pair, the top motif.
- **Discords.** The largest values mark subsequences whose nearest neighbour is still far away. The global maximum is
  the top discord, a strong candidate anomaly.
- **Segmentation.** The index $I$ shows where each subsequence finds its match. Arcs from $i$ to $I_i$ that rarely
  cross a point suggest that the regimes on either side of it differ.

The only parameter is $m$, the length of pattern of interest. There is no model to fit and no training set.

## Why z-normalise, and why it is cheap

Z-normalisation compares shapes, not levels or amplitudes: a heartbeat at a higher baseline, or a larger version of
the same gesture, still counts as a match. It also makes the distance a function of correlation. For subsequences of
length $m$ with means $\mu_i, \mu_j$, standard deviations $\sigma_i, \sigma_j$ and dot product
$Q_{ij} = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} x_{i+k} x_{j+k}$, the Pearson correlation is

$$
\rho_{ij} = \frac{Q_{ij} - m \mu_i \mu_j}{m \sigma_i \sigma_j}.
$$

Expanding the squared distance between the z-normalised vectors $\hat T_i$ and $\hat T_j$, each of which has squared
norm $m$, gives

$$
d(i, j)^2 = \norm{\hat T_i}^2 + \norm{\hat T_j}^2 - 2\,\hat T_i^\top \hat T_j = m + m - 2 m \rho_{ij}
= 2m\paren{1 - \rho_{ij}}.
$$

So the distance needs only the dot product and the running means and standard deviations. The means and standard
deviations of all subsequences come from cumulative sums in $O(n)$. The dot products are where the algorithms
differ.

- **One subsequence against all others.** The dot products of one query with every subsequence form a sliding dot
  product, which is a convolution. The FFT computes it in $O(n \log n)$; this is the MASS algorithm, and it gives one
  row of the distance matrix, the distance profile of that query.
- **All pairs (STOMP).** Along a diagonal of the distance matrix the dot product updates in constant time,
  $Q_{i+1, j+1} = Q_{ij} - x_i x_j + x_{i+m} x_{j+m}$. Computing the first row by FFT and then walking the diagonals
  gives the whole matrix profile in $O(n^2)$ time and $O(n)$ memory, independent of $m$ <Cite id="zhu2016" />.

The figure above uses the naive $O(n^2 m)$ loop, which is fine at a few hundred points and keeps the definition
visible. The same $O(n^2)$ structure, applied in parallel on GPUs or computed incrementally as new points arrive,
scales the method to time series of hundreds of millions of points <Cite id="zhu2016" />.

## Worked example

Take a series of 50 standard-normal values and $m = 8$. For the subsequences starting at positions 3 and 20, the
direct z-normalised distance and the formula $\sqrt{2m(1 - \rho)}$ both give $3.216$. Computing the full matrix
profile with an exclusion zone of $\lceil m/4 \rceil = 2$ positions places the top motif at position 4, at distance
$1.098$ from its match, and the top discord at position 19, at distance $2.523$. For pure noise these extremes mean
little; on real data with repeated structure, the gap between the motif distance and the typical distance is what
makes a motif meaningful.

The distance is bounded: $\rho \in [-1, 1]$ gives $0 \le d \le 2\sqrt{m}$. Dividing by $\sqrt{m}$, or comparing
against the distribution of $P$ itself, puts profiles computed with different $m$ on a common scale.

## Pitfalls

- **The choice of $m$.** A window much shorter than the pattern matches fragments that recur by chance; a much
  longer one dilutes the pattern with its surroundings. Scanning several values of $m$ is common practice.
- **Flat subsequences.** Where a subsequence is almost constant, $\sigma$ is near 0 and z-normalisation amplifies noise
  into a spurious shape. Implementations treat near-constant windows specially.
- **Discords are relative.** A discord is unusual only relative to the rest of the series. A second occurrence of the
  same anomaly makes each the other's nearest neighbour, and both disappear from the top of the profile. Variants
  that look for the $k$-th nearest neighbour address this.
- **Trivial matches.** Without the exclusion zone, every subsequence's nearest neighbour is its immediate shift, and
  the profile is uniformly close to 0.
