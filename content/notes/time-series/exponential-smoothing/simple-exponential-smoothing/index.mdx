<Definition>
**Simple exponential smoothing (SES)** forecasts a series $y_1, \dots, y_T$ that has no trend or seasonality. It
maintains a **level** $\ell_t$, updated after each observation by

$$
\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{t-1}, \qquad 0 < \alpha \le 1,
$$

from an initial level $\ell_0$. The forecast for every horizon $h \ge 1$ is the last level:
$\hat y_{T+h \mid T} = \ell_T$ <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Two naive forecasts bracket the problem. The mean of all past values ignores that the level may have moved. The last
value ignores that it contains noise. SES interpolates between them with one parameter, the **smoothing constant**
$\alpha$. At $\alpha = 1$ it is the last value (the <NoteLink to="random-walk">random-walk</NoteLink> forecast). As $\alpha \to 0$ it approaches a long-run
average.

The method was developed in operations research in the 1950s, by Brown for inventory control and by Holt, because it
needs one stored number per series and one multiplication per update <Cite id="gardner1985" />.

## Mechanism

### A weighted average with geometric weights

Substitute the recursion into itself:

$$
\ell_T = \alpha y_T + (1 - \alpha)\paren{\alpha y_{T-1} + (1 - \alpha)\ell_{T-2}} = \dots
= \sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\alpha(1 - \alpha)^jy_{T-j} + (1 - \alpha)^T\ell_0.
$$

The weight on the observation $j$ steps in the past is $\alpha(1 - \alpha)^j$. The weights decay geometrically, which
gives the method its name. They sum to 1 with the weight on $\ell_0$:
$\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\alpha(1 - \alpha)^j = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^T$. So the level is a weighted average of the data and the
initial level, and the initial level's influence decays like $(1 - \alpha)^T$.

The **mean age** of the data in the average is $\sum_{j \ge 0}j\,\alpha(1 - \alpha)^j = (1 - \alpha)/\alpha$. At
$\alpha = 0.2$ the level reflects data about 4 steps old on average; at $\alpha = 0.5$, about 1 step. A small $\alpha$
averages away more noise and reacts more slowly to real changes.

In signal-processing terms SES is a first-order recursive low-pass filter with transfer function
$\alpha/(1 - (1 - \alpha)z^{-1})$ (<NoteLink to="infinite-impulse-response-filters">IIR filters</NoteLink>).

### Error-correction form

Write the one-step forecast error as $e_t = y_t - \ell_{t-1}$. The update rearranges to

$$
\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha e_t.
$$

The level moves a fraction $\alpha$ of the way towards each new observation. This form is the basis of the
<Gloss name="ssm">state-space models</Gloss> that give SES a likelihood (<NoteLink to="error-trend-seasonal-models">ETS models</NoteLink>).

### SES is a steady-state Kalman filter

Consider the **local-level model**: a hidden level $\mu_t$ follows a random walk and is observed with noise,

$$
y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \eta_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma_\varepsilon^2),
\quad \eta_t \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma_\eta^2).
$$

This is a scalar <NoteLink to="linear-gaussian-state-space-models">linear-Gaussian state-space model</NoteLink> with $A = C = 1$, $Q = \sigma_\eta^2$ and $R = \sigma_\varepsilon^2$.
The <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> estimates the level by
$\hat\mu_t = \hat\mu_{t-1} + k_t(y_t - \hat\mu_{t-1})$, the error-correction form of SES with a time-varying gain
$k_t$. The gain converges to a constant $k_\infty$, and from then on the filter is SES with $\alpha = k_\infty$.

The relation between $\alpha$ and the **signal-to-noise ratio** $q = \sigma_\eta^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ follows from the
<NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria">steady state</NoteLink>. The predicted variance $P$ satisfies the scalar Riccati fixed point $P^2 = Q(P + R)$, and the gain is
$\alpha = P/(P + R)$. Solving the second equation for $P = \alpha R/(1 - \alpha)$ and substituting into the first:

$$
\frac{\alpha^2R^2}{(1 - \alpha)^2} = Q\,\frac{\alpha R + (1 - \alpha)R}{1 - \alpha} = \frac{QR}{1 - \alpha}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q = \frac{\alpha^2}{1 - \alpha}.
$$

A level that moves a lot relative to the noise (large $q$) calls for a large $\alpha$.

SES is the optimal forecast, among all forecasts, for a random walk observed with Gaussian noise once the filter has
settled, a result first shown by Muth <Cite id="muth1960" />. The same model is an
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-integrated-moving-average-model">ARIMA(0, 1, 1)</NoteLink> process with
$\theta = \alpha - 1$.

## Worked example

Take $y = (10, 12, 11, 13, 12)$, $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\ell_0 = 10$.

| $t$ | $y_t$ | forecast $\ell_{t-1}$ | error $e_t$ | $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + 0.5e_t$ |
| --- | ----- | --------------------- | ----------- | ------------------------------ |
| 1   | 10    | 10                    | 0           | 10                             |
| 2   | 12    | 10                    | 2           | 11                             |
| 3   | 11    | 11                    | 0           | 11                             |
| 4   | 13    | 11                    | 2           | 12                             |
| 5   | 12    | 12                    | 0           | 12                             |

The forecast for every future period is $\ell_5 = 12$. The weights on $y_5, y_4, y_3, \dots$ are
$0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.0625, 0.03125$, and $\ell_0$ keeps the remaining $0.5^5 = 0.03125$.

For the local-level model, $\alpha = 0.5$ is the steady-state Kalman gain when $q = 0.5^2/0.5 = 0.5$: the level's
step variance is half the observation noise variance. The Kalman note's example with $q = 1$ gives
$\alpha = 1/\varphi = 0.618$, where $\varphi$ is the golden ratio; checking, $0.618^2/0.382 = 1$.

## Choosing $\alpha$ and $\ell_0$

The parameters are usually chosen to minimise the sum of squared one-step errors, $\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_t^2$, by numerical
search over $0 < \alpha < 1$ and $\ell_0$. Under the Gaussian state-space form this is maximum likelihood, which also
gives <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink> <Cite id="hyndman2008" />. For the innovations model the $h$-step forecast variance is
$\sigma^2\paren{1 + (h - 1)\alpha^2}$, where $\sigma^2$ is the one-step error variance: the interval grows because
the level itself keeps moving.

## Cost

Each update costs one multiplication and two additions, and the method stores one number. Fitting $\alpha$ costs a
one-dimensional optimisation over $O(T)$ passes.

## Trade-offs

- **Responsiveness versus noise.** A large $\alpha$ tracks level shifts quickly and passes noise into the forecast; a
  small $\alpha$ smooths the noise and lags behind shifts.
- **Flat forecasts.** SES forecasts are flat at every horizon. Series with a trend need
  <NoteLink to="holt-linear-trend">Holt's method</NoteLink>, and seasonal series need
  <NoteLink to="holt-winters">Holt–Winters</NoteLink>.
- **Simplicity as a strength.** SES is a strong baseline for short, noisy series without trend, and more complex
  methods often fail to beat it <Cite id="gardner1985" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Trends make SES lag.** On a trending series the one-step errors all have the same sign, and once the
  transient has passed the one-step error settles at $b/\alpha$ for a trend of slope $b$ per step.
- **An optimised $\alpha$ near 1** indicates that the series behaves like a random walk; SES then adds nothing over the
  naive forecast.
- **Initialisation matters for short series.** With $T = 20$ and $\alpha = 0.1$, the initial level still carries weight
  $0.9^{20} = 0.12$.
