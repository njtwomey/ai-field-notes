<Definition>
The **Holt–Winters method** forecasts a series with trend and seasonality of period $m$ (for example $m = 12$ for
monthly data) <Cite id="winters1960" />. It keeps a level $\ell_t$, a slope $b_t$ and $m$ seasonal states, one for
each season. The **additive** method updates them by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t &= \alpha(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1 - \alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}), \\
b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta^*)b_{t-1}, \\
s_t &= \gamma(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1 - \gamma)s_{t-m},
\end{aligned}
$$

with smoothing constants $\alpha, \beta^*, \gamma \in [0, 1]$, and forecasts
$\hat y_{T+h \mid T} = \ell_T + hb_T + s_{T+h-m(k+1)}$ with $k = \lfloor(h - 1)/m\rfloor$, so that the seasonal state
used is the most recent one for the forecast's season.
</Definition>

## Problem

A seasonal series defeats both simpler smoothers. <NoteLink to="simple-exponential-smoothing">Simple exponential
smoothing</NoteLink> chases each seasonal peak and trough as if it were a level shift.
<NoteLink to="holt-linear-trend">Holt's method</NoteLink> reads the rising half of each season as a trend. Winters added a third smoothed component for the seasonal pattern. Each season keeps its own state, updated
only when that season recurs.

## Mechanism

### The three updates

Each equation averages a new estimate with the old state.

- **Level.** The new estimate is the observation with its seasonal effect removed, $y_t - s_{t-m}$ (the seasonally
  adjusted value). The old state is the level carried forward by one step of slope.
- **Slope.** As in Holt's method, the new estimate is the change in level.
- **Season.** The new estimate is the observation minus the predicted level, $y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}$. The old
  state is the same season's index one period ago, $s_{t-m}$.

The seasonal state for a season changes only once per period, so the effective smoothing of the seasonal pattern is
much slower than that of the level for the same constant. For monthly data with $\gamma = 0.2$, a change in the
December effect takes several years to be absorbed.

### Error-correction form

Let $e_t = y_t - (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + s_{t-m})$ be the one-step forecast error. Substituting into the three updates:

$$
\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \alpha e_t, \qquad b_t = b_{t-1} + \alpha\beta^*e_t, \qquad s_t = s_{t-m} + \gamma e_t.
$$

The seasonal line follows from $y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1} = e_t + s_{t-m}$. All three states move in proportion to
the same error. Winters' original seasonal update used the new level, $\gamma(y_t - \ell_t)$, which gives
$s_t = s_{t-m} + \gamma(1 - \alpha)e_t$; the two forms differ only in how $\gamma$ is scaled
<Cite id="hyndman2021" />. For the seasonal state not to absorb the level's share of the error, the constant is
usually restricted to $0 \le \gamma \le 1 - \alpha$.

### Multiplicative seasonality

When the seasonal swing grows with the level, the seasonal states are ratios rather than offsets. The multiplicative
method divides where the additive one subtracts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_t &= \alpha\frac{y_t}{s_{t-m}} + (1 - \alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}), \\
b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta^*)b_{t-1}, \\
s_t &= \gamma\frac{y_t}{\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}} + (1 - \gamma)s_{t-m}, \\
\hat y_{T+h \mid T} &= (\ell_T + hb_T)\,s_{T+h-m(k+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

A seasonal index of 1.2 means the season runs 20% above the level. The trend is still additive. Both methods can use
a damped trend by replacing $b_{t-1}$ with $\phi b_{t-1}$ and $hb_T$ with $(\phi + \dots + \phi^h)b_T$, as in the
damped Holt method.

### Initialisation

The level, slope and $m$ seasonal states need starting values. A common heuristic takes the level as the mean of the
first season, the slope as the difference between the means of the first two seasons divided by $m$, and the seasonal
states as the first season's deviations from (or ratios to) the level. Modern implementations estimate the initial
states along with the smoothing constants by maximum likelihood <Cite id="hyndman2008" />.

## Worked example

Quarterly data ($m = 4$). Before observing $y_t$, the states are $\ell_{t-1} = 100$, $b_{t-1} = 2$, and the seasonal
state for this quarter from a year ago is $s_{t-4} = 5$. The constants are $\alpha = 0.3$, $\beta^* = 0.1$ and
$\gamma = 0.2$. The observation is $y_t = 110$.

**Additive.** The forecast was $100 + 2 + 5 = 107$, so the error is $e_t = 3$. The updates are

$$
\ell_t = 102 + 0.3 \cdot 3 = 102.9, \qquad b_t = 2 + 0.03 \cdot 3 = 2.09, \qquad s_t = 5 + 0.2 \cdot 3 = 5.6.
$$

The same values follow from the component form: $\ell_t = 0.3(110 - 5) + 0.7(102) = 102.9$,
$b_t = 0.1(102.9 - 100) + 0.9 \cdot 2 = 2.09$ and $s_t = 0.2(110 - 102) + 0.8 \cdot 5 = 5.6$. The surprise of 3 is split: 0.9
raises the level, 0.6 raises this quarter's seasonal effect, and 0.09 raises the slope.

**Multiplicative**, with seasonal state $s_{t-4} = 1.05$ instead. The forecast was $102 \times 1.05 = 107.1$. The
updates are $\ell_t = 0.3(110/1.05) + 0.7 \cdot 102 = 102.829$, $b_t = 0.1(102.829 - 100) + 0.9 \cdot 2 = 2.083$ and
$s_t = 0.2(110/102) + 0.8 \cdot 1.05 = 1.0557$.

## Cost

Each update costs a few arithmetic operations. The method stores $m + 2$ numbers per series. Fitting optimises three
or four constants and $m + 2$ initial states, which becomes slow for long periods such as $m = 52$.

## Trade-offs

- **Additive versus multiplicative.** Additive seasonality suits a stable swing; multiplicative suits a swing that
  scales with the level. Applying the additive method to the log of the series is an alternative to the
  multiplicative method, and it makes the trend multiplicative too.
- **Adaptivity.** A large $\gamma$ lets the seasonal pattern change quickly, which suits evolving patterns but passes
  noise into the seasonal states. With few periods of data, a small $\gamma$ is safer.
- **Versus seasonal ARIMA.** Additive Holt–Winters corresponds to a restricted seasonal ARIMA model. The multiplicative
  method has no ARIMA equivalent. The state-space form gives both a likelihood and prediction intervals
  (<NoteLink to="ets-models">ETS models</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Long or multiple seasonal periods.** The method needs one state per season and one period $m$. Hourly data with
  daily and weekly patterns, or weekly data with $m = 52.18$, need other methods.
- **Seasonal states drift from normalisation.** In the additive method nothing forces the $m$ seasonal states to sum to
  zero, so part of the level can migrate into them. Renormalising the states, and adjusting the level to compensate,
  keeps the components interpretable without changing the forecasts.
- **Multiplicative seasonality with values near zero.** Dividing by a seasonal index or a level near 0 makes the
  updates unstable. The multiplicative method needs strictly positive data.
