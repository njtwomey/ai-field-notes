<Definition>
An **ETS model** is a <Gloss name="ssm">state-space model</Gloss> whose point forecasts equal those of an exponential-smoothing method
<Cite id="hyndman2002" />. The label ETS($E$, $T$, $S$) names its **error** ($E$: A additive or M multiplicative),
**trend** ($T$: N none, A additive or A$_d$ additive damped) and **seasonal** ($S$: N none, A additive or M
multiplicative) components. With a state vector $\xvec_t$ (level, slope, seasonal states) and <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white noise</NoteLink>
$\varepsilon_t \iid \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, every ETS model has the **innovations** form

$$
y_t = w(\xvec_{t-1}) + r(\xvec_{t-1})\varepsilon_t, \qquad \xvec_t = f(\xvec_{t-1}) + \gvec(\xvec_{t-1})\varepsilon_t,
$$

where $w$ gives the one-step forecast, $f$ propagates the states, and $r$ and $\gvec$ set how the single error
$\varepsilon_t$ enters the observation and each state <Cite id="hyndman2008" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The exponential-smoothing methods (<Gloss name="ses" />, Holt, Holt–Winters) are recursions that produce point forecasts. They say
nothing about uncertainty and give no principled way to choose between them. ETS supplies the missing statistical
model. Each method is rewritten so that its one-step forecast error is a random innovation with a distribution. Then
the parameters have a likelihood, models can be compared by an information criterion, and <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink> follow
from the model.

The same point forecasts arise from two models that differ only in how the error enters. With additive errors the
noise has constant size. With multiplicative errors the noise is a percentage of the level. The forecasts agree but
the prediction intervals do not.

## The taxonomy

Combining the trend and seasonal options gives nine exponential-smoothing methods <Cite id="hyndman2021" />:

| Trend \ Season         | N (none)        | A (additive)             | M (multiplicative)            |
| ---------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| N (none)               | SES             | additive seasonal        | multiplicative seasonal       |
| A (additive)           | Holt            | additive Holt–Winters    | multiplicative Holt–Winters   |
| A$_d$ (damped)         | damped Holt     | damped additive HW       | damped multiplicative HW      |

Each method has an additive-error and a multiplicative-error model, so there are 18 ETS models. Earlier versions of
the framework also included multiplicative trends <Cite id="hyndman2008" />; they tend to give explosive forecasts and
are usually omitted.

## Two examples

**ETS(A, N, N)**, simple exponential smoothing with additive errors. The state is the level $\ell_t$:

$$
y_t = \ell_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha\varepsilon_t.
$$

The first equation says that the one-step forecast error $y_t - \ell_{t-1}$ is the innovation. Substituting it into
the second gives $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - \ell_{t-1})$, the SES update. Here $w(\ell) = \ell$, $r = 1$,
$f(\ell) = \ell$ and $g = \alpha$.

**ETS(M, N, N)**, the same method with multiplicative errors. The innovation is a relative error:

$$
y_t = \ell_{t-1}(1 + \varepsilon_t), \qquad \ell_t = \ell_{t-1}(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t).
$$

Now $\varepsilon_t = (y_t - \ell_{t-1})/\ell_{t-1}$, and substituting gives
$\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - \ell_{t-1})$ again. The point forecasts are identical to ETS(A, N, N). The
one-step <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> is $\sigma\ell_{t-1}$, proportional to the level.

The general additive-error Holt–Winters model, ETS(A, A, A), writes the error-correction form of
<NoteLink to="holt-winters">Holt–Winters</NoteLink> with $e_t$ replaced by $\varepsilon_t$:

$$
y_t = \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + s_{t-m} + \varepsilon_t, \quad \ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \alpha\varepsilon_t, \quad
b_t = b_{t-1} + \beta\varepsilon_t, \quad s_t = s_{t-m} + \gamma\varepsilon_t,
$$

with $\beta = \alpha\beta^*$.

## Likelihood and model selection

Because the innovations are the one-step forecast errors, the likelihood follows from the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>. Given the
parameters $\thetavec$ (smoothing constants and initial states), each $\varepsilon_t$ is computed recursively from the
data. Conditional on the past, $y_t \sim \Gauss(w(\xvec_{t-1}), r(\xvec_{t-1})^2\sigma^2)$, so

$$
\log L(\thetavec, \sigma^2) = -\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\abs{r(\xvec_{t-1})} - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_t^2.
$$

Maximising over $\sigma^2$ gives $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_t\varepsilon_t^2$. Substituting it back, maximising the
likelihood is equivalent to minimising

$$
L^*(\thetavec) = n\log\paren{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\varepsilon_t^2} + 2\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\abs{r(\xvec_{t-1})}.
$$

For additive-error models $r = 1$, and this is least squares on the one-step errors. For multiplicative-error models
$r$ is the one-step forecast $w(\xvec_{t-1})$, and the second term penalises fits with a large level. The likelihood is
for the undifferenced data $y_t$ in every model, so the <Gloss name="aicc" /> can compare all 18 models on the same series, including
models with and without a trend or season <Cite id="hyndman2002" />. This differs from <Gloss name="arima" />, where models with
different differencing orders are not comparable by AICc.

## Prediction intervals

For ETS(A, N, N), iterate the state equation forward from $T$. The level at $T + h - 1$ is
$\ell_T + \alpha\sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\varepsilon_{T+j}$, so

$$
y_{T+h} = \ell_T + \alpha\sum_{j=1}^{h-1}\varepsilon_{T+j} + \varepsilon_{T+h}, \qquad
\var(y_{T+h} \mid y_{1:T}) = \sigma^2\paren{1 + (h - 1)\alpha^2}.
$$

The innovations are independent, so the variances add. The variance grows linearly in $h$ because the level is a
<NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>. For linear (additive-error, additive or no trend and season) models, all forecast variances have closed
forms of this kind. For multiplicative models the forecast distribution is not Gaussian, and intervals are usually
obtained by simulating future paths from the model <Cite id="hyndman2008" />.

## Worked example

Take ETS(A, N, N) with $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$. The forecast variances for $h = 1, \dots, 5$ are
$1 + 0.25(h - 1)$: $1$, $1.25$, $1.5$, $1.75$ and $2$. The 95% interval at $h = 5$ is $\ell_T \pm 1.96\sqrt2 =
\ell_T \pm 2.77$.

Take ETS(M, N, N) with the same $\alpha$, $\sigma = 0.05$ and last level $\ell_T = 200$. The one-step forecast is 200,
with standard deviation $0.05 \times 200 = 10$. A series at level 20 would have a one-step standard deviation of 1.
The additive model gives the same interval width at every level.

## Relation to ARIMA and to Kalman filtering

- **Linear ETS models are ARIMA models.** ETS(A, N, N) is ARIMA(0, 1, 1) with $\theta = \alpha - 1$. ETS(A, A, N) is
  ARIMA(0, 2, 2) with $\theta_1 = \alpha + \beta - 2$ and $\theta_2 = 1 - \alpha$
  (<NoteLink to="holt-linear-trend">Holt's method</NoteLink>). ETS(A, A$_d$, N) is ARIMA(1, 1, 2). The additive seasonal
  models are <NoteLink to="seasonal-autoregressive-integrated-moving-average">seasonal ARIMA models</NoteLink> with restricted coefficients. The multiplicative models have no ARIMA equivalent,
  and many ARIMA models have no ETS equivalent.
- **Single versus multiple sources of error.** The local-level model has two independent noises,
  $y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t$ and $\mu_t = \mu_{t-1} + \eta_t$
  (<NoteLink to="linear-gaussian-state-space-models">linear-Gaussian state-space models</NoteLink>). Its
  <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> converges to SES with a gain fixed by $\sigma_\eta^2/\sigma_\varepsilon^2$.
  The innovations model ETS(A, N, N) has one noise, perfectly shared between observation and state. The two models
  imply the same ARIMA(0, 1, 1) process for $y_t$ when $0 < \alpha < 1$, so they give the same forecasts once the
  Kalman gain has converged. The innovations form needs no filter: its states are computed directly from the data.

## Assumptions

- Innovations are independent Gaussian with constant variance (for additive errors) or constant relative variance (for
  multiplicative errors).
- The parameters lie in the region where the model is stable, so that the effect of the initial states dies out. For
  ETS(A, N, N) this is $0 < \alpha < 2$, wider than the usual $0 < \alpha < 1$.
- Multiplicative errors, trends or seasonality require strictly positive data.

## Pitfalls

- **Multiplicative errors do not change the point forecasts.** They change only the intervals and the likelihood, and
  hence which model the AICc selects.
- **Automatic selection is not a diagnosis.** Choosing among 18 models by AICc picks the best of the family. If the
  series has multiple seasonal periods, structural breaks or outliers, every member can be poor.
- **Intervals ignore parameter uncertainty.** The formulas treat $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ and the initial states as
  known, so intervals are too narrow for short series.
