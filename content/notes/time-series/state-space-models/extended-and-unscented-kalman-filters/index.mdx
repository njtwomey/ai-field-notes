<Definition>
A **nonlinear state-space model** with additive Gaussian noise is

$$
\zvec_t = f(\zvec_{t-1}) + \wvec_t, \qquad \xvec_t = h(\zvec_t) + \vvec_t, \qquad
\wvec_t \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Qmat), \quad \vvec_t \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Rmat),
$$

with differentiable $f: \reals^d \to \reals^d$ and $h: \reals^d \to \reals^p$. The filtering distribution
$p(\zvec_t \mid \xvec_{1:t})$ is then not Gaussian. The **extended Kalman filter** (EKF) and the **unscented Kalman
filter** (UKF) approximate it by a Gaussian $\Gauss(\muvec_{t \mid t}, \Pmat_{t \mid t})$, and apply the
<NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>'s predict and update steps with approximate moments. The EKF
obtains the moments by linearising $f$ and $h$. The UKF obtains them by the unscented transform
<Cite id="julier1997" />.
</Definition>

## The common problem

Both filters must answer the same question twice per step: if $\zvec \sim \Gauss(\muvec, \Pmat)$, what are the mean
and covariance of $g(\zvec)$ for a nonlinear $g$? In the predict step $g = f$. In the update step $g = h$, and the
filter also needs the cross-covariance $\cov(\zvec, h(\zvec))$. For linear $g$ the answers are exact and give the
Kalman filter. For nonlinear $g$ the transformed distribution is not Gaussian, and its moments are integrals with no
closed form in general.

Once the three moments are available, the update is the Gaussian conditioning of the Kalman filter. With predicted
mean $\muvec^-$ and covariance $\Pmat^-$, write $\hat\xvec = \expect[h(\zvec_t)]$,
$\Smat = \cov(h(\zvec_t)) + \Rmat$ and $\Pmat_{zx} = \cov(\zvec_t, h(\zvec_t))$, all under $\Gauss(\muvec^-, \Pmat^-)$.
Then

$$
\Kmat = \Pmat_{zx}\Smat^{-1}, \qquad \muvec_{t \mid t} = \muvec^- + \Kmat(\xvec_t - \hat\xvec), \qquad
\Pmat_{t \mid t} = \Pmat^- - \Kmat\Smat\Kmat^\top.
$$

The two filters differ only in how they compute $\hat\xvec$, $\Smat$, $\Pmat_{zx}$ and the predicted moments.

## Extended Kalman filter: linearise

The EKF replaces each function by its first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> about the
current mean. With the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> $\Jmat_g(\muvec)$ of $g$ at $\muvec$,

$$
g(\zvec) \approx g(\muvec) + \Jmat_g(\muvec)(\zvec - \muvec).
$$

The right side is linear in $\zvec$, so for $\zvec \sim \Gauss(\muvec, \Pmat)$ it has mean $g(\muvec)$, covariance
$\Jmat_g\Pmat\Jmat_g^\top$ and cross-covariance $\Pmat\Jmat_g^\top$ with $\zvec$. Write
$\Fmat_t = \Jmat_f(\muvec_{t-1 \mid t-1})$ and $\Hmat_t = \Jmat_h(\muvec_{t \mid t-1})$. The EKF is the Kalman filter
with $\Amat$ and $\Cmat$ replaced by these Jacobians, except that the means pass through the nonlinear functions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\muvec_{t \mid t-1} &= f(\muvec_{t-1 \mid t-1}), & \Pmat_{t \mid t-1} &= \Fmat_t\Pmat_{t-1 \mid t-1}\Fmat_t^\top + \Qmat, \\
\Smat_t &= \Hmat_t\Pmat_{t \mid t-1}\Hmat_t^\top + \Rmat, & \Kmat_t &= \Pmat_{t \mid t-1}\Hmat_t^\top\Smat_t^{-1}, \\
\muvec_{t \mid t} &= \muvec_{t \mid t-1} + \Kmat_t\paren{\xvec_t - h(\muvec_{t \mid t-1})}, &
\Pmat_{t \mid t} &= (\eye - \Kmat_t\Hmat_t)\Pmat_{t \mid t-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The error of linearisation shows in one dimension. For $z \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, take expectations of the
second-order expansion $g(z) \approx g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(z - \mu) + \tfrac12 g''(\mu)(z - \mu)^2$. The linear term has
mean zero, and $\expect[(z - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$, so

$$
\expect[g(z)] \approx g(\mu) + \tfrac12 g''(\mu)\sigma^2.
$$

The EKF keeps only $g(\mu)$. Its predicted mean is biased by about $\tfrac12 g''(\mu)\sigma^2$, which grows with the
curvature of $g$ and with the variance. The EKF is accurate when $g$ is close to linear over the region where the
state is likely to be, a few standard deviations around $\muvec$.

The **iterated EKF** reduces the update's linearisation error. It relinearises $h$ at the updated mean and repeats the
update, which is a Gauss–Newton iteration for the posterior mode.

## Unscented Kalman filter: propagate sigma points

The unscented transform chooses a small set of points, the **sigma points**, whose weighted sample mean and
covariance equal $\muvec$ and $\Pmat$. It passes each point through $g$ exactly and takes the weighted moments of
the images <Cite id="julier1997" />. It needs no <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink>, and $g$ need not even be differentiable.

**The scalar case.** Take $z \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and three points placed symmetrically: $\mu$ with weight
$w_0$, and $\mu \pm a\sigma$ with weight $w$ each. Choose $a$, $w_0$ and $w$ so that the points reproduce the first
moments of the Gaussian. By symmetry every odd central moment of the points is 0, as for the Gaussian. The even ones
give three equations:

$$
w_0 + 2w = 1, \qquad 2w\,a^2\sigma^2 = \sigma^2, \qquad 2w\,a^4\sigma^4 = 3\sigma^4.
$$

The first says the weights sum to 1. The second matches the variance. The third matches the fourth central moment of
a Gaussian, $3\sigma^4$. Dividing the third by the second gives $a^2 = 3$. Then $w = 1/6$ and $w_0 = 2/3$. So the
points $\mu$, $\mu \pm \sqrt3\,\sigma$ with weights $2/3, 1/6, 1/6$ match every central moment of the Gaussian up to
the fifth. For any polynomial $g$ of degree at most 5, $\expect[g(z)]$ is a combination of those moments, so the
weighted average of $g$ at the three points equals $\expect[g(z)]$ exactly. These points and weights are the
three-point <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">Gauss–Hermite quadrature</NoteLink> rule.

**In $d$ dimensions.** The unscented transform uses $2d + 1$ points. With a matrix square root
$\Lmat\Lmat^\top = (d + \kappa)\Pmat$, for example the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink>, and $\lvec_i$ the $i$-th column
of $\Lmat$,

$$
\chivec_0 = \muvec, \qquad \chivec_{\pm i} = \muvec \pm \lvec_i, \qquad
W_0 = \frac{\kappa}{d + \kappa}, \qquad W_{\pm i} = \frac{1}{2(d + \kappa)}.
$$

The weighted mean of the points is $\muvec$, and their weighted covariance is
$\sum_i 2W_i\lvec_i\lvec_i^\top = \Lmat\Lmat^\top/(d + \kappa) = \Pmat$. With $d = 1$ and
$\kappa = 2$ this is the scalar rule above; $\kappa = 3 - d$ is the general choice that matches the Gaussian's
fourth moments along each axis <Cite id="julier1997" />. The transformed moments are

$$
\hat\xvec = \sum_i W_i\, g(\chivec_i), \qquad
\cov(g(\zvec)) \approx \sum_i W_i\paren{g(\chivec_i) - \hat\xvec}\paren{g(\chivec_i) - \hat\xvec}^\top, \qquad
\Pmat_{zx} \approx \sum_i W_i\paren{\chivec_i - \muvec}\paren{g(\chivec_i) - \hat\xvec}^\top.
$$

The predicted mean is correct to second order in the Taylor expansion of $g$ for any nonlinearity, so the
$\tfrac12 g''\sigma^2$ term that the EKF drops is included <Cite id="julier2004" />. The **UKF** runs this transform
through $f$ in the predict step (adding $\Qmat$ to the covariance) and through $h$ in the update step (adding
$\Rmat$ to $\Smat$), then applies the Gaussian update above. A step costs $2d + 1$ evaluations of each function and
one Cholesky factorisation. Practical UKFs often use a scaled variant with parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$,
which moves the points closer to the mean for large $d$ <Cite id="sarkka2013" />.

## Worked example: a sine observation

A sensor observes the sine of a phase, $x = \sin z + v$, and the predicted phase is $z \sim \Gauss(\pi/2, 1)$. The
exact moments of $y = \sin z$ follow from $\expect[e^{iz}] = e^{i\mu - \sigma^2/2}$: the mean is
$e^{-1/2}\sin(\pi/2) = 0.607$, and the standard deviation is $0.447$.

- **Linearised.** The slope of $\sin$ at $\pi/2$ is $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$. The EKF predicts the observation mean
  $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$ with variance $0^2 \times 1 = 0$. The mean is off by 0.39, and the observation's only predicted
  uncertainty is the sensor noise $\Rmat$.
- **Unscented.** The sigma points are $\pi/2$ and $\pi/2 \pm \sqrt3$, with images $1$ and $\cos\sqrt3 = -0.161$
  twice. The mean is $\tfrac23 \times 1 + \tfrac13 \times (-0.161) = 0.613$, and the standard deviation is $0.547$.
  The mean is off by 0.007.

For $g(z) = z^2$ with $z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ the contrast is starker. The exact mean and variance are 1 and 2. The
linearisation at 0 has slope 0 and gives mean 0 and variance 0. The unscented transform gives exactly 1 and 2, because
$z^2$ and $z^4$ are polynomials of degree at most 5.

## When each fails

- **The EKF** fails when the functions curve strongly over the spread of the state, when the Jacobians are
  inaccurate or expensive, and when the state estimate is poor, because the linearisation point is then wrong too.
  Its covariance is often too small, and an overconfident filter ignores the observations that would correct it.
- **At a stationary point of $h$**, as in the sine example, the EKF's $\Hmat_t$ is zero, so its gain is zero and the
  observation changes nothing. The UKF does no better there: by symmetry its cross-covariance $\Pmat_{zx}$ is also
  zero. The observation says how far $z$ is from $\pi/2$ but not on which side, and a Gaussian update cannot use that.
- **Both** keep a single Gaussian. When the posterior is multimodal, as when a sensor measures $z^2$ and cannot tell
  $z$ from $-z$, no Gaussian approximation is adequate. Sampling methods such as the <NoteLink to="particle-filter">particle filter</NoteLink> represent such
  posteriors directly, at a higher cost.

## Assumptions

- The noise is additive and Gaussian. Non-additive noise is handled by appending the noise to the state before
  applying the unscented transform.
- The EKF needs differentiable $f$ and $h$ and their Jacobians. The UKF needs only function evaluations.
- Both assume the filtering distribution is close to Gaussian. Neither can represent skew or multiple modes.
