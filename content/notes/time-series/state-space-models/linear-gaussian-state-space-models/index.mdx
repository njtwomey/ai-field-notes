<Definition>
A **linear-Gaussian state-space model**, or **linear dynamical system** (LDS), models observations
$\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_T \in \reals^p$ through hidden states $\zvec_0, \dots, \zvec_T \in \reals^d$:

$$
\zvec_0 \sim \Gauss(\muvec_0, \Pmat_0), \qquad
\zvec_t = \Amat\zvec_{t-1} + \wvec_t, \qquad
\xvec_t = \Cmat\zvec_t + \vvec_t, \qquad
\wvec_t \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Qmat), \quad \vvec_t \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Rmat),
$$

with all noise terms independent. The parameters are $\theta = (\Amat, \Cmat, \Qmat, \Rmat, \muvec_0, \Pmat_0)$. Every
joint and conditional distribution of states and observations is Gaussian <Cite id="roweis1999" />.
</Definition>

## The model

The model makes two assumptions. The state is Markov: given $\zvec_{t-1}$, the state $\zvec_t$ does not depend on
earlier states. Each observation depends only on the current state. The joint density therefore factorises as

$$
p(\zvec_{0:T}, \xvec_{1:T}) = p(\zvec_0) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\zvec_t \mid \zvec_{t-1})\, p(\xvec_t \mid \zvec_t),
$$

with $p(\zvec_t \mid \zvec_{t-1}) = \Gauss(\zvec_t;\, \Amat\zvec_{t-1}, \Qmat)$ and
$p(\xvec_t \mid \zvec_t) = \Gauss(\xvec_t;\, \Cmat\zvec_t, \Rmat)$. This is the graph of a
<NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink>, with a continuous Gaussian state in place of a
discrete one. Because the whole vector $(\zvec_{0:T}, \xvec_{1:T})$ is a linear function of independent Gaussians, it
is jointly Gaussian. Every inference question is a Gaussian conditioning, and the chain structure lets it be answered
in $O(T d^3)$ time instead of $O(T^3 d^3)$.

The three inference problems have exact recursive solutions:

- **filtering**, $p(\zvec_t \mid \xvec_{1:t})$: the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>;
- **smoothing**, $p(\zvec_t \mid \xvec_{1:T})$: the <NoteLink to="kalman-smoother">Kalman smoother</NoteLink>;
- **prediction**, $p(\zvec_{t+k} \mid \xvec_{1:t})$: the Kalman filter's predict step applied $k$ times.

The Kalman filter's innovations also give the log-likelihood $\log p(\xvec_{1:T} \mid \theta)$ exactly.

## What the model contains

Many familiar models are linear-Gaussian state-space models with particular parameters <Cite id="roweis1999" />.

- **Factor analysis and probabilistic PCA.** With $\Amat = \zeros$, the states are independent draws from
  $\Gauss(\zeros, \Qmat)$ and each observation is $\xvec_t = \Cmat\zvec_t + \vvec_t$. That is
  <NoteLink to="factor-analysis">factor analysis</NoteLink> when $\Rmat$ is diagonal, and
  <NoteLink to="probabilistic-pca">probabilistic principal component analysis</NoteLink> when $\Rmat = \sigma^2\eye$.
  The linear dynamical system is factor analysis whose factors evolve in time.
- **Autoregressive and ARMA processes.** An autoregression of order $k$,
  $x_t = \phi_1 x_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_k x_{t-k} + \epsilon_t$, is an LDS with state
  $\zvec_t = (x_t, x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-k+1})$, a companion matrix $\Amat$ with $(\phi_1, \dots, \phi_k)$ in its first
  row and ones below the diagonal, $\Cmat = (1, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $\Rmat = 0$. Every ARMA process has a state-space
  form of this kind. Writing it that way gives the exact likelihood, and handles missing values, through the Kalman
  filter.
- **Structural time-series models.** A local level (a random walk observed with noise), a local linear trend, and
  seasonal components are each small LDSs. Stacking them block-diagonally gives a model whose states are
  interpretable components of the series.
- **Tracking models.** Constant-velocity and constant-acceleration motion models are LDSs with position, velocity and
  acceleration in the state.

## Learning the parameters by EM

When $\theta$ is unknown it can be estimated by maximising $\log p(\xvec_{1:T} \mid \theta)$. The states are missing
data, so <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation maximisation</NoteLink> applies. Shumway and Stoffer
derived it for this model <Cite id="shumway1982" />, and Ghahramani and Hinton gave the general form with $\Cmat$
learned too <Cite id="ghahramani1996" />. EM alternates two steps and never decreases the likelihood.

**E-step.** Run the Kalman smoother with the current parameters. It gives, for every $t$, the smoothed mean
$\hat\zvec_t = \expect[\zvec_t \mid \xvec_{1:T}]$, the smoothed covariance $\Pmat_{t \mid T}$, and the lag-one
covariance $\Pmat_{t, t-1 \mid T} = \cov(\zvec_t, \zvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_{1:T}) = \Gmat_{t-1}\Pmat_{t \mid T}$, where
$\Gmat_{t-1}$ is the smoother gain. From them come the expected sufficient statistics

$$
\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_t^\top] = \Pmat_{t \mid T} + \hat\zvec_t\hat\zvec_t^\top, \qquad
\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_{t-1}^\top] = \Pmat_{t, t-1 \mid T} + \hat\zvec_t\hat\zvec_{t-1}^\top.
$$

**M-step.** Maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood

$$
\Qcal(\theta) = \expect\left[\log p(\zvec_0) + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \Gauss(\zvec_t;\, \Amat\zvec_{t-1}, \Qmat)
+ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \Gauss(\xvec_t;\, \Cmat\zvec_t, \Rmat)\right].
$$

The dynamics term involves only $\Amat$ and $\Qmat$. Up to a constant it is

$$
-\frac{T}{2}\log\det\Qmat - \frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\expect\left[(\zvec_t - \Amat\zvec_{t-1})^\top\Qmat^{-1}(\zvec_t - \Amat\zvec_{t-1})\right].
$$

Its gradient with respect to $\Amat$ is $\Qmat^{-1}\sum_t\paren{\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_{t-1}^\top] -
\Amat\,\expect[\zvec_{t-1}\zvec_{t-1}^\top]}$. Setting it to zero gives a least-squares regression of each state on
the previous one, with expectations in place of the unknown states. Setting the gradient with respect to
$\Qmat^{-1}$ to zero gives the average expected residual covariance. The observation term gives $\Cmat$ and $\Rmat$ the
same way:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Amat &= \paren{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_{t-1}^\top]}\paren{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\expect[\zvec_{t-1}\zvec_{t-1}^\top]}^{-1}, &
\Qmat &= \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_t^\top] - \Amat\,\expect[\zvec_{t-1}\zvec_t^\top]}, \\
\Cmat &= \paren{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\xvec_t\hat\zvec_t^\top}\paren{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_t^\top]}^{-1}, &
\Rmat &= \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{\xvec_t\xvec_t^\top - \Cmat\hat\zvec_t\xvec_t^\top}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The $\Qmat$ and $\Rmat$ updates use the new $\Amat$ and $\Cmat$. The initial-state parameters update to
$\muvec_0 = \hat\zvec_0$ and $\Pmat_0 = \Pmat_{0 \mid T}$, although with a single sequence they are usually held fixed,
since one sample cannot estimate a covariance <Cite id="ghahramani1996" />.

<Derivation title="The Q update">
Write $\Mmat = \sum_t \expect[(\zvec_t - \Amat\zvec_{t-1})(\zvec_t - \Amat\zvec_{t-1})^\top]$. Using
$\vvec^\top\Qmat^{-1}\vvec = \trace(\Qmat^{-1}\vvec\vvec^\top)$, the dynamics term is
$\tfrac{T}{2}\log\det\Qmat^{-1} - \tfrac12\trace(\Qmat^{-1}\Mmat)$. The gradient with respect to $\Qmat^{-1}$ is
$\tfrac{T}{2}\Qmat - \tfrac12\Mmat$, using $\partial\log\det\Xmat/\partial\Xmat = \Xmat^{-1}$ for symmetric $\Xmat$.
Setting it to zero gives $\Qmat = \Mmat/T$. Expanding $\Mmat$ gives four terms. At the optimal $\Amat$,
$\Amat\sum_t\expect[\zvec_{t-1}\zvec_{t-1}^\top] = \sum_t\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_{t-1}^\top]$, so the term
$\Amat\sum_t\expect[\zvec_{t-1}\zvec_{t-1}^\top]\Amat^\top$ cancels one of the two cross terms, leaving
$\Mmat = \sum_t\paren{\expect[\zvec_t\zvec_t^\top] - \Amat\,\expect[\zvec_{t-1}\zvec_t^\top]}$.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

The figure below fits a scalar model, $z_t = a z_{t-1} + w_t$ and $x_t = z_t + v_t$, with $\Cmat = 1$ held fixed and
three parameters $a$, $q$ and $r$. The scalar M-step is

$$
a = \frac{\sum_t \expect[z_t z_{t-1}]}{\sum_t \expect[z_{t-1}^2]}, \qquad
q = \frac1T\sum_t\paren{\expect[z_t^2] - a\,\expect[z_t z_{t-1}]}, \qquad
r = \frac1T\sum_t\paren{x_t^2 - 2x_t\hat z_t + \expect[z_t^2]}.
$$

The data are 200 steps simulated with $a = 0.9$, $q = 0.5$ and $r = 1$. EM starts from $a = 0.2$, $q = 2$,
$r = 0.2$, where the log-likelihood is $-435.5$. After one iteration $a = 0.744$ and the log-likelihood is $-359.8$:
most of the gain comes from correcting the dynamics. The next 50 iterations raise the log-likelihood by only another
9, to $-350.7$, while $q$ falls from 2.04 to 0.66 and $r$ rises from 0.21 to 0.89. After 150 iterations the estimates
are $a = 0.935$, $q = 0.509$, $r = 1.011$, with log-likelihood $-350.45$.

The slow phase is typical. A larger $q$ with a smaller $r$ and a smaller $q$ with a larger $r$ explain the same data
almost equally well, because both attribute the same total variance to the observations. The likelihood surface has a
long, shallow ridge in the $(q, r)$ plane, and EM moves along it slowly.

## Identifiability

The parameters are not unique. For any invertible $d \times d$ matrix $\Mmat$, the substitution
$\zvec_t \to \Mmat\zvec_t$ with $\Amat \to \Mmat\Amat\Mmat^{-1}$, $\Cmat \to \Cmat\Mmat^{-1}$,
$\Qmat \to \Mmat\Qmat\Mmat^\top$ and $(\muvec_0, \Pmat_0)$ transformed alike gives exactly the same distribution of
the observations. So the state has no fixed coordinate system unless one is imposed. Common constraints are
$\Qmat = \eye$, or fixing $\Cmat$ as in the worked example, or giving the state a physical meaning (position,
velocity) that fixes $\Amat$ and $\Cmat$ from domain knowledge and leaves only the noise covariances to learn.

## Relations

- **Hidden Markov models** share the graph and the forward–backward structure. The HMM's state is discrete; the
  LDS's is continuous and Gaussian. Both are learned by EM with the smoother in the E-step.
- **Gaussian mixture models** relate to hidden Markov models as factor analysis relates to the LDS: the static model
  has independent latent variables, and the dynamic model links them in a Markov chain <Cite id="roweis1999" />.
- **Switching linear dynamical systems** combine the two: a discrete Markov chain selects which of several LDSs
  generates each step. Exact inference is then intractable, because the number of Gaussian components grows
  exponentially with $T$.

## Pitfalls

- **Local maxima and slow convergence.** EM converges to a local maximum, and along ridges it can take hundreds of
  iterations. Several restarts, or switching to direct optimisation of the likelihood near convergence, help.
- **Choosing the state dimension.** $d$ is not learned by EM. Compare held-out likelihood, or an information
  criterion, over several values of $d$.
- **Stability is not enforced.** The M-step can return an $\Amat$ with eigenvalues outside the unit circle, which
  makes forecasts explode. Constrained M-steps or post-hoc projection keep the system stable when that matters.
