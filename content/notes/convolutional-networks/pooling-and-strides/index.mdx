<Definition>
A pooling layer slides a $k \times k$ window over each channel of a feature map with stride $s$ and outputs one summary
per window: the maximum (max pooling) or the mean (average pooling). It has no parameters and treats channels
independently. A strided layer, pooling or convolution, computes its output only at every $s$-th position, so the output
is about $s$ times smaller along each axis.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink> with stride 1 keeps the spatial size of its input.
A network made only of such layers keeps full resolution to the end. That is expensive, and each output still sees only
a small window of the image. Downsampling fixes both problems. After a stride-2 layer, one step on the feature map
covers two pixels of the input, so the next $3 \times 3$ kernel covers a region twice as wide.

Max pooling also discards the exact position of a feature within each window. If an edge moves by one pixel inside a
$2 \times 2$ window, the window's maximum does not change. This gives a small amount of translation invariance.

## Pooling

For channel $c$, max pooling with window $k$ and stride $s$ outputs

$$
y_{c, i, j} = \max_{0 \le u, v < k} x_{c,\, si + u,\, sj + v},
$$

and average pooling replaces the maximum by the mean of the $k^2$ values. The output size follows the same formula as a
convolution: for input side length $n$ and padding $p$, it is $\lfloor (n + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1$.
The standard choice is $k = s = 2$, which halves each side and keeps one value in four.

**<NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> of max pooling.** Within one window, $y = \max_m x_m$. When the maximum is unique at position $m^\star$,
changing any other $x_m$ by a small amount leaves $y$ unchanged, and changing $x_{m^\star}$ changes $y$ one for one:

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_m} = \begin{cases} 1 & m = m^\star, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
$$

Backpropagation therefore routes the whole gradient of each output to the input $x_{m^\star}$ that attains the maximum. Average pooling
spreads it evenly, $\partial y / \partial x_m = 1/k^2$.

**Global average pooling** takes the mean of each channel over the whole feature map. A $C \times 7 \times 7$ map becomes
a vector of $C$ numbers, whatever the input size. Modern networks use it before the classifier instead of flattening.

## Strided convolution

A convolution with stride $s$ downsamples and filters in one layer. It is equivalent to a stride-1 convolution followed
by keeping every $s$-th output, but it skips the discarded work. Since <Gloss name="resnet" />, most architectures downsample with
strided convolutions and use pooling only in the stem and at the end <Cite id="he2016" />.

## Receptive field

The receptive field of a unit is the side length $r$ of the input region that can affect it. Define the **jump**
$j_l$ as the distance, in input pixels, between two adjacent units of layer $l$. The input has $r_0 = 1$ and $j_0 = 1$.

Layer $l$ has kernel size $k_l$ and stride $s_l$. Adjacent units of layer $l$ are $s_l$ units of layer $l - 1$ apart,
and those are $j_{l-1}$ pixels apart, so

$$
j_l = j_{l-1}\, s_l = \prod_{m=1}^{l} s_m.
$$

A unit of layer $l$ reads $k_l$ adjacent units of layer $l - 1$. Their centres span $(k_l - 1)\, j_{l-1}$ input pixels,
and each of them sees $r_{l-1}$ pixels around its own centre. The union of their fields therefore has side

$$
r_l = r_{l-1} + (k_l - 1)\, j_{l-1}.
$$

With stride 1 everywhere, $j_l = 1$ and $r_L = 1 + L(k - 1)$: linear growth. Each stride-2 layer doubles the jump, so
every later layer adds twice as many pixels. With a stride-2 layer in every stage, the field grows geometrically with
the number of stages <Cite id="dumoulin2016" />.

## Worked example

<NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">VGG-16</NoteLink> has five stages of $3 \times 3$ stride-1 convolutions (2, 2, 3, 3 and 3 layers), each followed by a
$2 \times 2$ stride-2 max pool. Apply the recursion:

- Stage 1: two convolutions give $r = 1 + 2 + 2 = 5$. The pool adds $(2 - 1) \cdot 1 = 1$, so $r = 6$ and $j = 2$.
- Stage 2: two convolutions add $2 \cdot 2$ each, $r = 14$. The pool adds $2$: $r = 16$, $j = 4$.
- Stage 3: three convolutions add $2 \cdot 4$ each, $r = 40$. The pool adds $4$: $r = 44$, $j = 8$.
- Stage 4: three convolutions add $16$ each, $r = 92$. The pool adds $8$: $r = 100$, $j = 16$.
- Stage 5: three convolutions add $32$ each, $r = 196$. The pool adds $16$: $r = 212$, $j = 32$.

The last convolution sees a $196 \times 196$ region of a $224 \times 224$ input. Thirteen stride-1 convolutions
without pooling would see only $1 + 13 \cdot 2 = 27$ pixels.

## Assumptions

- The exact position of a feature within a pooling window does not matter for the task. This holds for classification.
  It fails for tasks that need precise localisation, such as segmentation, which recover resolution with upsampling or
  skip connections.
- The signal is not badly aliased by subsampling. Striding without a low-pass filter can alias high frequencies, and it
  makes the output shift by different amounts depending on the phase of a shift of the input.

## Pitfalls

- **Invariance is approximate.** A $2 \times 2$ pool is invariant only to shifts that stay inside its window. Deep
  strided networks can change their prediction when the input moves by one pixel.
- **Theoretical versus effective field.** The recursion gives the region that can affect a unit. The influence of
  input pixels on a unit falls off roughly like a Gaussian from the centre, so the effective field is much smaller than
  the theoretical one <Cite id="luo2016" />.
- **Gradient sparsity.** Max pooling passes gradient to one input in each window. Early in training this can leave many
  units with zero gradient for many steps.
