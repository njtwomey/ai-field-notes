<Definition>
A residual block computes $\yvec = \xvec + F(\xvec; \theta)$, where $F$ is a small stack of layers with parameters
$\theta$ and the addition is elementwise. The path that carries $\xvec$ unchanged is the shortcut or skip connection. A
residual network is a stack of such blocks <Cite id="he2016" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Adding layers to a network should never make its training error worse: the extra layers could learn the identity and
reproduce the shallower network. In practice, plain deep networks trained with gradient descent do get worse. On
CIFAR-10, a plain 56-layer network has higher training error than a plain 20-layer one <Cite id="he2016" />. This is
the degradation problem. It is an optimisation failure, not overfitting, because the training error itself rises.

A residual block makes the identity the default. When $F = 0$ the block passes its input through, so setting the
weights of $F$ near zero recovers a shallower network. The block only has to learn the difference between the identity
and the desired map. If the desired map is close to the identity, that difference is small and easy to find.

## Why gradients survive

Differentiate one block. By the sum rule,

$$
\frac{\partial \yvec}{\partial \xvec} = \I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial \xvec}.
$$

The Jacobian of a plain layer is $\partial F / \partial \xvec$ alone. Stack $L$ plain layers and the gradient at the
input is a product of $L$ such Jacobians, which shrinks or grows exponentially unless every factor is close to
orthogonal. The identity term changes this.

Consider blocks $l, l+1, \dots, L-1$ with pure identity shortcuts and no operation after the addition, as in the
pre-activation design <Cite id="he2016b" />: $\xvec_{m+1} = \xvec_m + F_m(\xvec_m)$. Unrolling the recursion gives

$$
\xvec_L = \xvec_l + \sum_{m=l}^{L-1} F_m(\xvec_m).
$$

The output of any deeper block is the output of any shallower block plus a sum of residuals. For a loss $\ell$, the
chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \xvec_l} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \xvec_L}
\paren{\I + \frac{\partial}{\partial \xvec_l} \sum_{m=l}^{L-1} F_m(\xvec_m)}.
$$

The first term, $\partial \ell / \partial \xvec_L$, reaches block $l$ without passing through any weight layer. The
gradient at a shallow block cannot vanish just because the network is deep. For the second term to cancel the first,
the residual Jacobians would have to sum to exactly $-\I$ for every example, which is unlikely.

The original ResNet applied a ReLU after the addition, so its shortcut is not quite the identity. Moving the batch
normalisation and ReLU inside $F$ (pre-activation) makes the shortcut an exact identity. The pre-activation form trains
a 1001-layer network on CIFAR-10 <Cite id="he2016b" />.

## Block designs

**Basic block.** $F$ is two $3 \times 3$ convolutions with batch normalisation and a ReLU between them. It is used in
ResNet-18 and ResNet-34.

**Bottleneck block.** $F$ is a $1 \times 1$ convolution that reduces the channels, a $3 \times 3$ convolution at the
reduced width, and a $1 \times 1$ convolution that restores the width. It is used in ResNet-50, -101 and -152.

**Projection shortcut.** When a block changes the number of channels or downsamples with stride 2, $\xvec$ and $F(\xvec)$
have different shapes. The shortcut then applies a $1 \times 1$ convolution with the same stride,
$\yvec = \Wmat_s \xvec + F(\xvec)$. All other shortcuts are identities, which add no parameters.

## Worked example

Compare the weights of a bottleneck block on 256 channels with a basic block of two $3 \times 3$ convolutions at 256
channels, ignoring biases and normalisation.

- Bottleneck: $1 \times 1$ from 256 to 64 channels has $256 \cdot 64 = 16{,}384$ weights. The $3 \times 3$ at 64
  channels has $9 \cdot 64 \cdot 64 = 36{,}864$. The $1 \times 1$ back to 256 has $16{,}384$. The total is $69{,}632$.
- Basic: two $3 \times 3$ convolutions at 256 channels have $2 \cdot 9 \cdot 256^2 = 1{,}179{,}648$ weights.

The bottleneck block uses about 17 times fewer weights while keeping 256-channel inputs and outputs. That saving is what
makes a 152-layer network affordable. ResNet-152 needs 11.3 billion multiply-adds per $224 \times 224$ image, fewer than
the 15.3 billion of the 16-layer VGG-16 <Cite id="he2016" />.

## Assumptions

- $F(\xvec)$ and $\xvec$ have the same shape, or a projection makes them match.
- The desired mapping is close to the identity, or can be built from many small corrections to it. Deep networks whose
  layers each refine a representation fit this assumption.

## Pitfalls

- **Operations on the shortcut.** Anything placed on the identity path, such as a ReLU after the addition, dropout or a
  scaling, reintroduces a product of factors along the shortcut. He et al. found that constant scaling and gating of the
  shortcut both hurt optimisation <Cite id="he2016b" />.
- **Variance growth.** Each addition adds the variance of $F(\xvec)$ to the variance of $\xvec$. Without normalisation
  inside $F$ or a small initialisation of its last layer, activations grow with depth.
- **Effective depth.** Many paths through a residual network are short. Much of the gradient flows through paths that
  skip most blocks, so a 100-block network does not behave like a 100-layer plain network <Cite id="veit2016" />.
