<Definition>
Let $f_{\thetavec} : \reals^d \to \reals^K$ be a classifier network whose output $f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)[y]$ is the logit
of class $y$. A **joint energy-based model** (JEM) uses the same logits to define an
<NoteLink to="energy-based-models">energy-based model</NoteLink> of inputs and labels together:

$$
p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, y) = \frac{\exp\paren{f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)[y]}}{Z_{\thetavec}}, \qquad
E_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = -\log\sum_{y=1}^K \exp\paren{f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)[y]},
$$

where $E_{\thetavec}$ is the energy of the input alone, so that $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = \exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec)) /
Z_{\thetavec}$. It is trained to maximise $\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) + \log p_{\thetavec}(y \mid \xvec)$
<Cite id="grathwohl2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A softmax classifier ignores one number per input. Adding the same constant $c(\xvec)$ to every logit leaves the
predicted probabilities unchanged, so an ordinary classifier's logit scale carries no meaning. JEM gives it one: the
overall level of the logits, measured by their log-sum-exp, becomes the negative energy of the input. Inputs that
resemble the training data should get high logits for some class; inputs unlike anything seen should get uniformly low
logits. The classifier and the generative model share every parameter, and the generative objective trains the degree
of freedom that classification leaves unused.

## Details

### The classifier is unchanged

Marginalise the joint over labels:

$$
p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = \sum_y p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, y) = \frac{\sum_y \exp\paren{f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)[y]}}{Z_{\thetavec}}
= \frac{\exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec))}{Z_{\thetavec}}.
$$

Divide the joint by this marginal. The constant $Z_{\thetavec}$ cancels:

$$
p_{\thetavec}(y \mid \xvec) = \frac{p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, y)}{p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)} =
\frac{\exp\paren{f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)[y]}}{\sum_{y'} \exp\paren{f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)[y']}},
$$

which is the ordinary softmax of the logits. Any trained classifier is therefore already a JEM; standard training has
simply left $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ unconstrained. Shifting all logits of an input by $c$ multiplies its unnormalised
density $\exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec))$ by $e^c$ and leaves $p_{\thetavec}(y \mid \xvec)$ fixed.

### The training objective

The joint log-likelihood factorises as

$$
\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, y) = \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) + \log p_{\thetavec}(y \mid \xvec).
$$

The second term is the negative <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy loss</NoteLink>, computed
exactly. The first term is an energy-based-model log-likelihood, whose <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is
the positive phase minus the negative phase:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = -\nabla_{\thetavec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec) +
\expect_{\xvec' \sim p_{\thetavec}}\brack{\nabla_{\thetavec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec')}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> is estimated with samples from a Langevin sampler. Grathwohl et
al. chose this factorisation over $\log p(\xvec \mid y) + \log p(y)$ so that the sampler's bias affects only the
generative term and the classifier is trained with an unbiased loss <Cite id="grathwohl2020" />. A model trained as a
class-conditional energy-based model, with a separate unknown normaliser for each class, cannot compute
$p(y \mid \xvec)$ at all, and the paper reports that this alternative classifies much worse.

### The sampler

Negative samples come from Langevin updates on $E_{\thetavec}$, following the practice of Du and Mordatch
<Cite id="du2019" />:

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \alpha\, \nabla_{\xvec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec_k) + \sigma\, \xivec_k, \qquad \xivec_k \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \eye),
$$

with inputs scaled to $[-1, 1]$, gradient step $\alpha = 1$, noise $\sigma = 0.01$ and 20 steps per training iteration
<Cite id="grathwohl2020" />.

- **Persistent chains in a replay buffer.** Each chain starts from a buffer of 10,000 past samples, or with probability
  0.05 from uniform noise. This is <NoteLink to="energy-based-models">persistent contrastive divergence</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="tieleman2008" />, with occasional restarts.
- **Far less noise than the theory requires.** Correct <NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink>
  with gradient step $\alpha$ needs noise $\sigma = \sqrt{2\alpha} = 1.41$. With $\sigma = 0.01$ the sampler targets
  $p_{\thetavec}^{1/T}$ at temperature $T = \sigma^2/(2\alpha) = 5 \times 10^{-5}$, which is nearly noisy
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on the energy. The authors state that the sampler is
  biased and that, at step sizes large enough to be useful, correctly scaled noise swamps the gradient signal
  <Cite id="grathwohl2020" />.
- **Cost.** Each Langevin step costs about as much as one classifier training step, so JEM training costs roughly 20
  times as much per iteration as ordinary classifier training.

### What the generative term buys

- **Calibration.** Classifiers trained by cross-entropy are often overconfident <Cite id="guo2017" />. On CIFAR-100 the
  JEM trained by Grathwohl et al. had an <Gloss name="ece">expected calibration error</Gloss> of 4.9%, against 22.3% for
  the same architecture trained as a plain classifier <Cite id="grathwohl2020" />. The energy term penalises high logits
  on inputs the model considers unlikely. Cross-entropy alone rewards widening the gap between the true class's logit
  and the others, which makes the softmax overconfident.
- **Out-of-distribution detection.** Three scores are available: $\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = -E_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ up
  to a constant, the maximum softmax probability, and the **approximate mass**
  $-\norm{\nabla_{\xvec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}$. The last one rests on the observation that a point of high density
  in a tiny region has little probability mass nearby, so the log-density changes steeply around it. Tractable
  generative models such as flows can assign higher likelihood to out-of-distribution data than to their training data
  <Cite id="nalisnick2019" />; JEM's density ranks in-distribution data higher on the benchmarks reported.
- **Robustness.** <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples">Adversarial perturbations</NoteLink> exploit inputs that lie off
  the data manifold. JEM is more robust to adversarial attacks than the baseline classifier, and running a few Langevin

<JemExplorer />
  steps from the input before classifying, which moves it towards higher density, adds more robustness. It remains below
  dedicated adversarial training at many perturbation sizes.
- **Samples.** The model generates class-conditional images by Langevin sampling from $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, y)$.

The price is some accuracy. On CIFAR-10 the JEM reached 92.9% against 95.8% for a standard Wide <Gloss name="resnet" />
classifier. Part of the gap comes from removing <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> to
make the network's outputs deterministic, which alone raised the classifier's error from 4.2% to 6.4%
<Cite id="grathwohl2020" />.

## Worked example: logits, energies and the free scale

Take three classes and logits $f(\xvec) = (2, 1, 0)$ for some input.

- **Classifier.** $\sum_y e^{f[y]} = e^2 + e^1 + e^0 = 7.389 + 2.718 + 1 = 11.107$. The softmax is
  $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$.
- **Energy.** $E(\xvec) = -\log 11.107 = -2.408$.
- **Shifted logits.** For a second network with logits $(3, 2, 1)$ at the same input, the softmax is the same
  $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, but $E(\xvec) = -3.408$. The unnormalised density $e^{-E}$ is larger by the factor
  $e^1 = 2.718$.
- **An uncertain input.** Logits $(-3, -3, -3)$ give a uniform softmax $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ and energy
  $E = 3 - \log 3 = 1.901$, a density $e^{-(1.901 + 2.408)} = 0.013$ times that of the first input. JEM training
  pushes inputs unlike the data towards this regime: low logits for every class, high energy, and a hedged prediction.

## Pitfalls

- **Training is unstable.** The energy gradients can grow by orders of magnitude when a buffer sample with unusually
  high energy is drawn, and the model diverges. The authors report that runs regularly diverged, that gradient and
  energy clipping did not fix it without harming performance, and that they recovered by restarting from checkpoints,
  lowering the learning rate or raising the Langevin steps per iteration from 20 to 40 <Cite id="grathwohl2020" />.
- **Short-run chains are stabler but slower.** Chains started from noise every iteration needed about 80 steps to train
  stably, four times the persistent sampler's cost, and still diverged late in training <Cite id="grathwohl2020" />
  <Cite id="nijkamp2019" />.
- **No usable likelihood.** $Z_{\thetavec}$ remains unknown, so $\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ compares inputs under one
  model but cannot be compared across models or reported as a test likelihood.
- **Stochastic layers.** Batch normalisation makes the energy of an input depend on the rest of its batch. The reported
  models removed it to keep the energy a deterministic function of the input; the authors add that later runs trained
  with batch normalisation and dropout without trouble <Cite id="grathwohl2020" />.
- **Biased sampler.** At temperature $5 \times 10^{-5}$, the negative samples sit near energy minima rather than
  following $p_{\thetavec}$. The learned density is therefore not the maximum-likelihood fit that the objective names;
  the benefits above are empirical.
