<Definition>
These four families of **deep generative model** each fit a distribution $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ to data with neural
networks, and each makes a different part of the problem tractable. An <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss>
factorises the density by the chain rule. A normalising flow transforms noise invertibly, so the density follows from
the change of variables <Cite id="papamakarios2021" />. A generative adversarial network (GAN) never writes a density
and learns a sampler against a discriminator <Cite id="goodfellow2014" />. An energy-based model writes an unnormalised
density and never computes its normalising constant.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The families trade three things against each other: whether the likelihood can be computed, how fast samples are drawn,
and how freely the network can be designed. Autoregressive models give exact likelihoods and power
<Gloss name="llm">large language models</Gloss>, but sample one element at a time. Flows give exact likelihoods and fast
sampling, at the cost of invertible architectures. GANs sample in one pass and produced the sharpest images before
<Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss>, but give no likelihood and are hard to train. Energy-based
models allow any network, at the cost of sampling by <Gloss name="mcmc">Markov chain Monte Carlo</Gloss>. Each family
also appears inside other models: autoregressive priors over <Gloss name="vq-vae" /> codes, flows in
<Gloss name="vi">variational inference</Gloss>, adversarial losses in image decoders.

## Main ideas

**Factorise by the chain rule.** <NoteLink to="autoregressive-generative-models" /> write
$p(\xvec) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ and predict each conditional with a network masked so that it cannot see later
elements. Training on all positions is parallel and the likelihood is exact; sampling takes one network pass per
element.

**Transform noise invertibly.** A <NoteLink to="normalising-flow" /> models data as $\xvec = f(\zvec)$ for invertible,
differentiable $f$ and simple noise $\zvec$. The <NoteLink to="change-of-variables" /> formula gives the exact
log-likelihood, and coupling layers make both the inverse and the Jacobian determinant cheap.

**Train a sampler against a critic.** A <NoteLink to="generative-adversarial-network" /> trains a generator against a
discriminator that tells real from generated data. At the optimal discriminator the generator minimises a
Jensen–Shannon divergence, which gives no useful gradient when the two distributions do not overlap. The
<NoteLink to="wasserstein-gan" /> replaces it with the Wasserstein-1 distance, estimated by a 1-Lipschitz critic.

**Assign energies, sample by Langevin dynamics.** An <NoteLink to="energy-based-models">energy-based model</NoteLink>
defines $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) \propto \exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec))$ with any scalar network. The log-likelihood
gradient lowers the energy of data and raises it at model samples, which are drawn by Langevin dynamics.
<NoteLink to="joint-energy-models" /> reinterpret a classifier's logits as energies, so one network is both a
classifier and a generative model.
