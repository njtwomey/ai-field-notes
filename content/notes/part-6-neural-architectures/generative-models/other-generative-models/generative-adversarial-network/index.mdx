<Definition>
A **generative adversarial network** (GAN) consists of two networks <Cite id="goodfellow2014" />. The **generator**
$G_{\thetavec} : \reals^k \to \reals^d$ maps noise $\zvec \sim p(\zvec)$, e.g. $\Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$, to a sample
$G(\zvec)$; the distribution of $G(\zvec)$ is written $p_g$. The **discriminator** $D_{\phivec} : \reals^d \to (0, 1)$
outputs the probability that its input came from the data rather than from the generator. They play the two-player
minimax game

$$
\min_{G}\, \max_{D}\; V(D, G) = \expect_{\xvec \sim p_{\text{data}}}\brack{\log D(\xvec)} + \expect_{\zvec \sim
p(\zvec)}\brack{\log\paren{1 - D(G(\zvec))}}.
$$

$V$ is the log-likelihood of a binary classifier that labels data 1 and generated samples 0. The discriminator
maximises it; the generator minimises it.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The generator never sees the data directly. It receives only the discriminator's
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, which says how to change a generated sample to make it look more like data.
The discriminator is a learned <Gloss name="loss-function">loss function</Gloss> that keeps improving as the generator
does. The model has no explicit density: $p_g$ is defined only through the sampler $G$, so a GAN is an **implicit**
generative model. Sampling is a single forward pass.

## The optimal discriminator

Fix $G$. Write both <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> as integrals over $\xvec$, the second by the
change of variable $\xvec = G(\zvec)$:

$$
V(D, G) = \int \brack{p_{\text{data}}(\xvec) \log D(\xvec) + p_g(\xvec) \log\paren{1 - D(\xvec)}} d\xvec.
$$

The integrand can be maximised separately at each $\xvec$. For $a, b \ge 0$ not both zero, the function $y \mapsto a
\log y + b \log(1 - y)$ on $(0, 1)$ has <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $a/y - b/(1 - y)$, which is zero
at $y = a/(a + b)$, and it is concave. So

$$
D^\star_G(\xvec) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(\xvec)}{p_{\text{data}}(\xvec) + p_g(\xvec)}.
$$

## The generator minimises the Jensen–Shannon divergence

Substitute $D^\star_G$. Let $m = \tfrac12(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$, so that $D^\star_G = p_{\text{data}}/(2m)$ and
$1 - D^\star_G = p_g/(2m)$:

$$
V(D^\star_G, G) = \expect_{p_{\text{data}}}\brack{\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}}{m}} + \expect_{p_g}\brack{\log
\frac{p_g}{m}} - 2\log 2 = \KL(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, m) + \KL(p_g \,\|\, m) - \log 4.
$$

The two <Gloss name="kl" /> terms are twice the <NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon">Jensen–Shannon
divergence</NoteLink>:

$$
V(D^\star_G, G) = 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}, p_g) - \log 4.
$$

JS is non-negative and zero only when the distributions are equal. The global minimum over $G$ is therefore $-\log 4$,
reached exactly when $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$, and at that point $D^\star = \tfrac12$ everywhere
<Cite id="goodfellow2014" />. If $G$ and $D$ had unlimited capacity and $D$ were trained to optimality after every
generator step, training would minimise JS.

## Training

Training alternates stochastic gradient steps: ascend $V$ in $\phivec$ on a minibatch of data and a minibatch of
generated samples, then descend in $\thetavec$.

**The non-saturating loss.** Early in training the generator is poor, $D(G(\zvec)) \approx 0$, and $\log(1 -
D(G(\zvec)))$ is flat. Write $D = \sigma(a)$ with logit $a$. The derivative of $\log(1 - \sigma(a))$ with respect to $a$
is $-\sigma(a) = -D$, close to 0, so the generator gets almost no gradient. Goodfellow et al. instead have the generator
maximise $\log D(G(\zvec))$ <Cite id="goodfellow2014" />. Its derivative with respect to $a$ is $1 - D$, close to 1 when
$D$ is near 0. With $D = 0.01$ the two gradients with respect to the logit
are $-0.01$ and $0.99$. The fixed points of the game are unchanged.

## Worked example

The data put probability $(0.5, 0.5, 0)$ on three
points and the generator puts $(0.25, 0.25, 0.5)$.

- Optimal discriminator: $D^\star = 0.5/0.75 = 2/3$ on the first two points and $0/0.5 = 0$ on the third.
- Value: $V = \log\tfrac23 + \brack{0.5\log\tfrac13 + 0.5 \log 1} = -0.405 - 0.549 = -0.955$.
- Jensen–Shannon divergence: $(V + \log 4)/2 = (-0.955 + 1.386)/2 = 0.216$ nats. A direct computation with $m = (0.375,
  0.375, 0.25)$ gives the same value.

The third point receives $D^\star = 0$: the discriminator is certain that any sample there is fake, and the generator's
mass there costs it most.

## Failure modes

- **Mode collapse.** The generator maps many noise vectors to one or a few outputs. The alternating updates do not
  solve $\min_G \max_D$ exactly. If the generator takes several steps against a fixed discriminator, its best response
  is to put all mass at the point the discriminator currently rates most real. The discriminator then learns to reject
  that point, and the generator jumps to another. Samples look realistic but cover only part of the data
  <Cite id="salimans2016" />.
- **Vanishing gradients with disjoint supports.** Images lie near low-dimensional manifolds, and $p_g$ and
  $p_{\text{data}}$ often barely overlap early in training. If their supports are disjoint, $\mathrm{JS} = \log 2$
  whatever the distance between them, and a near-optimal discriminator gives the generator no useful gradient. The
  Wasserstein GAN replaces JS by the <NoteLink to="earth-movers-distance">earth mover's distance</NoteLink>, which
  shrinks smoothly as the distributions approach each other <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />.
- **Non-convergence.** <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on a minimax game can oscillate or
  cycle instead of converging.

## Variants

- **DCGAN** fixed an all-convolutional architecture with <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch
  normalisation</NoteLink> that made training stable enough for images <Cite id="radford2016" />.
- **f-GAN** generalises the objective to any <NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon">f-divergence</NoteLink>
  through its <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">variational lower bound</NoteLink>; the original GAN is the
  Jensen–Shannon case <Cite id="nowozin2016" />.
- **WGAN** trains a critic with a Lipschitz constraint to estimate the Wasserstein-1 distance
  <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />; see <NoteLink to="wasserstein-gan">Wasserstein GAN</NoteLink>.
- **Conditional GANs** feed a label or other condition to both networks.

## Pitfalls

- **No likelihood.** A GAN cannot score a data point, so it cannot be evaluated or compared by held-out log-likelihood.
  Sample-based metrics such as <NoteLink to="frechet-inception-distance">Fréchet Inception distance (FID)</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="precision-and-recall-for-generative-models">precision and recall</NoteLink> are used instead.
- **Training tricks matter.** Results depend strongly on architecture, normalisation,
  <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rates</Gloss> and the balance of discriminator and generator steps
  <Cite id="salimans2016" />.
- **The theory assumes an optimal discriminator.** The JS result holds only at $D^\star$. In practice the discriminator
  is far from optimal, and the generator minimises something else.
