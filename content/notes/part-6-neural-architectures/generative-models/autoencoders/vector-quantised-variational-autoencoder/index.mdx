<Definition>
A **vector-quantised variational autoencoder** (VQ-VAE) has an encoder $\zvec_e(\xvec)$, a **codebook** of $K$
vectors $\evec_1, \dots, \evec_K \in \reals^D$, and a decoder $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec_q)$
<Cite id="oord2017" />. Each encoder output vector is replaced by its nearest codebook entry,

$$
\zvec_q(\xvec) = \evec_{k^\star}, \qquad k^\star = \argmin_{j}\, \norm{\zvec_e(\xvec) - \evec_j}^2,
$$

and the model minimises

$$
L = -\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec_q) + \norm{\mathrm{sg}[\zvec_e] - \evec_{k^\star}}^2 + \beta\,
\norm{\zvec_e - \mathrm{sg}[\evec_{k^\star}]}^2,
$$

where $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ is the stop-gradient operator: identity in the forward pass, zero
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> in the backward pass. The prior over codes is trained separately, after
the <NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Why discrete latents

Many kinds of data are naturally described by discrete symbols: phonemes in speech, words in text, object categories in
images. A discrete latent also has a simple prior: a <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical
distribution</NoteLink> over $K$ indices, which an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-generative-models">autoregressive
model</NoteLink> can fit exactly by maximum likelihood. A Gaussian-latent
<NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">VAE</NoteLink> with an expressive decoder tends to suffer posterior collapse. The
VQ-VAE avoids it because its <Gloss name="kl" /> term is a constant, as shown below, so there is no pressure towards an
uninformative code <Cite id="oord2017" />.

In image models the encoder is convolutional and outputs a grid of vectors, e.g. $32 \times 32$ vectors of dimension
$D$ for a $128 \times 128$ image. Each grid position is quantised separately, so an image becomes a $32 \times 32$ grid
of integers in $\set{1, \dots, K}$.

## The VAE view and its constant KL term

Read the <NoteLink to="quantisation">quantisation</NoteLink> as a posterior that is one-hot: $q(k \mid \xvec) = 1$ for
$k = k^\star$ and 0 otherwise. During training the prior is uniform, $p(k) = 1/K$. The KL term of the
<Gloss name="elbo" /> is then

$$
\KL\paren{q(k \mid \xvec) \,\|\, p(k)} = \sum_k q(k \mid \xvec) \log \frac{q(k \mid \xvec)}{1/K} = 1 \cdot \log
\frac{1}{1/K} = \log K.
$$

It does not depend on any parameter and drops out of training. The ELBO reduces to the reconstruction term.

## The three loss terms

**Reconstruction.** $-\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec_q)$ trains the decoder and, through the straight-through
estimator, the encoder.

**Straight-through <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>.** The $\argmin$ is piecewise constant, so its derivative
is zero almost everywhere. The VQ-VAE copies the gradient of the loss with respect to the decoder input $\zvec_q$
directly to the encoder output $\zvec_e$, as if quantisation were the identity <Cite id="oord2017" />. In code, this is
$\zvec_q \leftarrow \zvec_e + \mathrm{sg}[\zvec_q - \zvec_e]$: the forward value is $\zvec_q$, and the backward pass
sees only $\zvec_e$. The approach is the straight-through estimator of Bengio et al. <Cite id="bengio2013b" />. It is
biased but works because $\zvec_q$ and $\zvec_e$ are close, which the next two terms ensure.

**Codebook loss.** $\norm{\mathrm{sg}[\zvec_e] - \evec_{k^\star}}^2$ moves the selected codebook vector towards the
encoder outputs assigned to it. The reconstruction gradient never reaches the codebook, because the straight-through
copy bypasses it, so this term is the only one that trains the codebook. It is a stochastic version of the
<NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> centroid update. Replacing it by an exponential moving average of the
assigned encoder outputs is a common alternative <Cite id="oord2017" />.

**Commitment loss.** $\beta\norm{\zvec_e - \mathrm{sg}[\evec_{k^\star}]}^2$ keeps the encoder outputs near their chosen
codes. Without it, the encoder output could drift far from every codebook vector while the codebook chases it. Van den
Oord et al. used $\beta = 0.25$ and found results robust to values from 0.1 to 2 <Cite id="oord2017" />.

## The prior, fitted afterwards

After the autoencoder is trained, every training image is encoded to its grid of indices. A separate
<Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss>, a PixelCNN for images or a WaveNet for audio, is trained by maximum
likelihood on these grids. To generate, sample a grid of indices from the prior, look up the codebook vectors and decode
<Cite id="oord2017" />. The two-stage split means the autoencoder handles local detail and the prior handles global
structure, on a grid many times smaller than the image.

## VQ-VAE-2

VQ-VAE-2 uses a hierarchy of two latent grids <Cite id="razavi2019" />. For $256 \times 256$ images, a top grid of
$32 \times 32$ codes captures global structure such as shape and layout. A bottom grid of $64 \times 64$ codes,
conditioned on the top, captures local texture. Separate PixelCNN priors with self-attention are fitted to each level,
the bottom one conditioned on the top. The model generated <Gloss name="imagenet" /> samples whose quality and diversity
were competitive with <Gloss name="gan" plural /> of the time <Cite id="razavi2019" />.

Later work kept the two-stage recipe and changed its parts. VQGAN adds an adversarial and a perceptual loss to the
autoencoder and fits a transformer as the prior <Cite id="esser2021" />. The same learned discrete codes serve as image
tokens for large autoregressive image and multimodal models.

## Worked example

A codebook has $K = 3$ entries in $D = 2$: $\evec_1 = (0, 0)$, $\evec_2 = (1, 0)$ and $\evec_3 = (0, 1)$. The encoder
outputs $\zvec_e = (0.8, 0.3)$. The squared distances are

$$
\norm{\zvec_e - \evec_1}^2 = 0.73, \qquad \norm{\zvec_e - \evec_2}^2 = 0.04 + 0.09 = 0.13, \qquad \norm{\zvec_e -
\evec_3}^2 = 0.64 + 0.49 = 1.13.
$$

The nearest entry is $\evec_2$, so $\zvec_q = (1, 0)$ and the index 2 is stored. The codebook loss is $0.13$; its
gradient moves $\evec_2$ towards $(0.8, 0.3)$. With $\beta = 0.25$ the commitment loss is $0.25 \times 0.13 = 0.0325$;
its gradient moves $\zvec_e$ towards $(1, 0)$. The decoder receives $(1, 0)$, and whatever gradient reaches $(1, 0)$ is
applied unchanged to $\zvec_e$.

## Pitfalls

- **Codebook collapse.** Only a few codebook vectors get selected, and unselected vectors receive no gradient and
  never recover. Remedies include initialising the codebook from encoder outputs with k-means, resetting unused
  entries to random encoder outputs, and lower-dimensional or normalised codes.
- **Bias of the straight-through gradient.** The encoder is updated as if quantisation were absent. The error grows
  with the distance between $\zvec_e$ and $\zvec_q$.
- **Sampling needs the second stage.** Decoding uniformly random indices gives noise. The generative quality depends
  on the prior as much as on the autoencoder.
