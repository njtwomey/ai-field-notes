<Definition>
A **residual-quantised variational autoencoder** (RQ-VAE) has an encoder $\zvec_e(\xvec)$, a decoder, and $D$ stages of
quantisation <Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />. Starting from the residual $\rvec_0 = \zvec_e$, stage $d$ picks the codeword
nearest to the residual and subtracts it:

$$
k_d = \argmin_{j}\, \norm{\rvec_{d-1} - \evec_{d,j}}^2, \qquad \rvec_d = \rvec_{d-1} - \evec_{d,k_d}.
$$

The code of $\xvec$ is the tuple $(k_1, \dots, k_D)$, and the decoder receives the sum of the chosen codewords,
$\hat\zvec = \evec_{1,k_1} + \dots + \evec_{D,k_D}$. The stages use one shared codebook or one codebook each.
</Definition>

## From one codeword to a sum of codewords

A <Gloss name="vq-vae" /> replaces the encoder output by one codeword from a codebook of $K$ <Cite id="oord2017" />. The
quantisation error is the distance to the nearest of $K$ points, and lowering it means a larger codebook. An RQ-VAE
keeps $K$ small and quantises again: the second stage quantises the error the first one left, the third quantises the
error after two stages, and so on. This is <NoteLink to="additive-and-residual-quantisation">residual
quantisation</NoteLink> <Cite id="chen2010rvq" />, applied to the latent of an
<NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink>.

The partial sum after $d$ stages is

$$
\hat\zvec^{(d)} = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \evec_{i,k_i} = \zvec_e - \rvec_d,
$$

so its error is the size of the residual, $\norm{\zvec_e - \hat\zvec^{(d)}}^2 = \norm{\rvec_d}^2$. Every prefix
$(k_1, \dots, k_d)$ of the code is a code in its own right, for the coarser latent $\hat\zvec^{(d)}$.

## Capacity

Each stage chooses one of $K$ codewords, so $D$ stages can produce $K^D$ distinct sums: residual quantisation with depth
$D$ partitions the latent space into at most $K^D$ cells <Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />. A VQ-VAE needs a codebook of $K^D$
vectors for the same number of cells. The RQ-VAE stores $DK$ codewords with per-stage codebooks, or $K$ with a shared
one, and searches $K$ of them at each stage instead of $K^D$ at once.

Lee et al. used the capacity to shorten the code grid. With a shared codebook of 16,384 codewords and $D = 4$, an
$256 \times 256$ image became an $8 \times 8 \times 4$ grid of indices, 64 positions with four indices each. VQGAN used
a $16 \times 16 \times 1$ grid, 256 positions. The two reconstructed about equally well; VQGAN with an $8 \times 8$ grid
reconstructed far worse <Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />.

## Coarse to fine

The first stage sees the whole encoder output, so its codeword captures the largest distinction. Each later stage sees
only the error left over, so it adds a smaller correction. Codes that share their first $d$ indices share the coarse
latent $\hat\zvec^{(d)}$ and differ only in the corrections after it. The set of codes therefore forms a tree: $K$
branches at stage 1, $K$ more inside each branch at stage 2, and so on, with the whole codes as leaves. Similar inputs
tend to share long prefixes.

Encoding is greedy. Each stage takes the codeword nearest to its own residual and never revisits an earlier choice.
Averaged over the data, the error cannot rise from one stage to the next when each codebook is fitted by
<NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> on the residuals it receives. Each codeword is then the mean of the residuals
assigned to it, and a mean is at least as close to its points, in total squared distance, as the zero vector, which
would leave those residuals unchanged. For a single point the error can rise, because the nearest codeword to a small
residual can be larger than the residual.

## Worked example

Two stages with two codewords each, in two dimensions. Stage 1 has $\evec_{1,1} = (0, 0)$ and $\evec_{1,2} = (2, 2)$;
stage 2 has $\evec_{2,1} = (0, 0.5)$ and $\evec_{2,2} = (0, -0.5)$. The encoder outputs $\zvec_e = (1.8, 2.4)$.

Stage 1 compares squared distances $1.8^2 + 2.4^2 = 9$ and $0.2^2 + 0.4^2 = 0.2$, chooses $k_1 = 2$, and leaves
$\rvec_1 = (-0.2, 0.4)$. The coarse latent is $\hat\zvec^{(1)} = (2, 2)$, with error $0.2$.

Stage 2 compares $0.2^2 + 0.1^2 = 0.05$ and $0.2^2 + 0.9^2 = 0.85$, chooses $k_2 = 1$, and leaves
$\rvec_2 = (-0.2, -0.1)$. The latent is $\hat\zvec^{(2)} = (2, 2.5)$, with error $0.05$, and the code is $(2, 1)$.

A second encoder output $\zvec_e = (2, 2)$ shows the greedy caveat. Stage 1 matches it exactly, with error 0. Stage 2
has no zero codeword, so it adds $(0, \pm 0.5)$ and the error rises to $0.25$.

## Training

The loss of an RQ-VAE is the VQ-VAE loss with the quantised latent $\hat\zvec = \hat\zvec^{(D)}$ in place of the single
codeword. Gradients pass the quantisation by the straight-through estimator,
$\hat\zvec \leftarrow \zvec_e + \mathrm{sg}[\hat\zvec - \zvec_e]$, where $\mathrm{sg}$ is the stop-gradient. Lee et
al.'s commitment loss sums over the partial sums of every stage <Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />:

$$
L_{\text{commit}} = \sum_{d=1}^{D} \norm{\zvec_e - \mathrm{sg}[\hat\zvec^{(d)}]}^2.
$$

Each term pulls the encoder output towards the coarse latent of one stage, so the encoder learns outputs that are
already well described by the first few codewords. The codebooks are trained by the matching codebook loss, or by moving
averages of the residuals assigned to each codeword. Starting each codebook from k-means on the residuals of a first
batch avoids codewords that are never chosen. <Gloss name="tiger" /> initialised its codebooks this way
<Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />.

**Shared or per-stage codebooks.** Lee et al. shared one codebook across all stages and reported that per-stage
codebooks did worse at their scale <Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />. A shared codebook must serve corrections of every size,
from the first stage's large ones to the last stage's small ones, which needs many codewords. With a few codewords per
stage, as in small models and in <NoteLink to="recommender-systems">recommender systems</NoteLink>, per-stage codebooks
are the usual choice. TIGER used three stages with 256 codewords each <Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F


class ResidualQuantiser(nn.Module):
    """D stages, one codebook of K codewords each, on rows of size L."""

    def __init__(self, depth=3, K=256, L=32, beta=0.25):
        super().__init__()
        self.codebooks = nn.ModuleList(
            nn.Embedding(K, L) for _ in range(depth)
        )
        self.beta = beta

    def forward(self, z_e):  # z_e: [N, L]
        residual = z_e
        z_hat = torch.zeros_like(z_e)
        codes, codebook_loss, commitment_loss = [], 0.0, 0.0
        for book in self.codebooks:
            e = book.weight  # [K, L]
            # [N, K] squared distances, then the nearest codeword
            dist = (
                residual.pow(2).sum(1, keepdim=True)
                - 2 * residual @ e.T
                + e.pow(2).sum(1)
            )
            k = dist.argmin(1)  # [N]
            chosen = book(k)  # [N, L]
            # Codebook loss: the chosen codeword moves to the residual.
            codebook_loss += F.mse_loss(chosen, residual.detach())
            z_hat = z_hat + chosen  # the partial sum after this stage
            # Commitment: z_e moves to the partial sum (Lee et al.).
            commitment_loss += F.mse_loss(z_e, z_hat.detach())
            residual = residual - chosen.detach()
            codes.append(k)
        z_q = z_e + (z_hat - z_e).detach()  # straight-through
        vq_loss = codebook_loss + self.beta * commitment_loss
        return z_q, torch.stack(codes, 1), vq_loss  # codes: [N, D]
```

The encoder and decoder are those of a VQ-VAE: convolutions for images, <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptrons</Gloss>
for item embeddings. A code can be turned back into a latent at any depth by summing the first $d$ looked-up codewords,
which is how the coarse reconstructions in the figure below are made.

## Priors over codes

A generative model over RQ-VAE codes predicts $D$ indices per position instead of one. Flattening them into one long
sequence multiplies its length by $D$. Lee et al.'s RQ-Transformer splits the work. A spatial
<NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> reads the positions in order, its input at each position being the sum
of the codewords chosen at the previous one. A depth transformer then predicts the $D$ indices at the current position
one after another, from the spatial transformer's output and the codewords already chosen there
<Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />. On their $8 \times 8 \times 4$ grids, sampling was 4.1 and 5.6 times faster than with
VQGAN's $16 \times 16$ grids at batch sizes of 100 and 200 <Cite id="lee2022rqvae" />.

## Semantic IDs

In <NoteLink to="generative-retrieval-for-recommendation">generative retrieval</NoteLink>, an RQ-VAE turns each item's
content embedding into a short code, its <Gloss name="semantic-identifier">semantic ID</Gloss>. TIGER encoded a
768-dimensional <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence embedding</NoteLink> of each item's title, price, brand and
category with three stages of 256 codewords. A sequence model then generates the next item's semantic ID index by index,
so items that share a prefix share what the model has learned about that prefix <Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />. Two items
can receive the same code. TIGER appended a fourth index to tell such items apart: $(7, 1, 4)$ becomes $(7, 1, 4, 0)$
and $(7, 1, 4, 1)$ <Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />.

The prefix structure of semantic IDs is a nesting of cells. In two dimensions, every point of the plane has a code, and
the points with a given prefix of length $d$ form one cell of the stage-$d$ partition. Each stage-1 cell is split by the
stage-2 codebook, shifted to the cell's codeword, and so on down. With $K = 2$ codewords per stage, a code is a string
of $D$ bits.

## Pitfalls

- **Greedy coding.** An early stage's choice is never revised, so a code is not the best sum of $D$ codewords.
  <Gloss name="beam-search">Beam search</Gloss> over the stages finds better codes at a multiple of the cost.
- **Unused codewords.** As in a VQ-VAE, a codeword that no residual chooses gets no gradient. Later stages see small
  residuals that change as the earlier stages learn, so their codewords are easy to strand. Initialisation from
  residual k-means and resets of unused codewords help.
- **Collisions.** Different inputs can share a whole code. Where codes serve as identifiers, a collision must be
  resolved, for example by an extra index.
- **Late stages matter little.** The share of error each stage removes shrinks with depth. Past a few stages, extra
  stages add code length and little accuracy.
