<Definition>
A **variational autoencoder** (VAE) is a latent-variable generative model together with an approximate-inference
network, both trained by maximising the <Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower bound (ELBO)</Gloss> <Cite id="kingma2014" />
<Cite id="rezende2014" />.

- The **generative model**, or **decoder**, draws a latent $\zvec \in \reals^k$ from a prior $p(\zvec) =
  \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ and then an observation $\xvec \in \reals^d$ from $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)$, a simple
  distribution whose parameters are the output of a neural network with weights $\thetavec$.
- The **encoder**, or inference network, maps $\xvec$ to an approximate posterior $q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec) =
  \Gauss\paren{\muvec_{\phivec}(\xvec), \diag \sigmavec^2_{\phivec}(\xvec)}$, where $\muvec_{\phivec}$ and
  $\sigmavec_{\phivec}$ are neural network outputs with weights $\phivec$.

Both networks maximise $\sum_i \Lcal(\thetavec, \phivec; \xvec_i)$ over the training set, where

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec, \phivec; \xvec) = \expect_{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)}
- \KL\paren{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec)} \;\le\; \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec).
$$
</Definition>

## The problem the VAE solves

A latent-variable model defines the density of an observation by integrating over the latent:

$$
p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = \int p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)\, p(\zvec)\, d\zvec.
$$

When $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)$ is a neural network, this integral has no closed form. Maximum likelihood needs
it. The posterior $p_{\thetavec}(\zvec \mid \xvec) = p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)\,p(\zvec) / p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$
is also intractable, so the <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">EM algorithm</NoteLink>, which needs the exact
posterior, does not apply. Classical <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">variational inference</NoteLink>
would fit separate variational parameters to each data point by an inner optimisation loop. The VAE makes three
changes that make the approach scale to large data sets and deep networks:

1. **<NoteLink to="amortised-inference">Amortised inference</NoteLink>.** One encoder network outputs the variational
   parameters for any $\xvec$ in a single forward pass. There is no per-point optimisation.
2. **A single objective.** The ELBO is maximised over the decoder weights $\thetavec$ and the encoder weights $\phivec$
   together by stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> ascent on minibatches.
3. **Low-variance gradients.** The <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over $q_{\phivec}$ is
   differentiated with the reparameterisation trick.

## Deriving the evidence lower bound

For any distribution $q(\zvec \mid \xvec)$ that is positive wherever the posterior is, the log evidence splits exactly
into two terms. Since $\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ does not depend on $\zvec$, it equals its own expectation under $q$.
Bayes' theorem gives $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \zvec) / p_{\thetavec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)$ for every
$\zvec$. Multiply and divide by $q$ inside the logarithm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)
&= \expect_{q}\brack{\log \frac{p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \zvec)}{p_{\thetavec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}}
 = \expect_{q}\brack{\log \frac{p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \zvec)}{q(\zvec \mid \xvec)}}
 + \expect_{q}\brack{\log \frac{q(\zvec \mid \xvec)}{p_{\thetavec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}} \\
&= \Lcal + \KL\paren{q(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\, p_{\thetavec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is non-negative, so
$\Lcal \le \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$, with equality exactly when $q$ equals the true posterior. Two conclusions follow.

- Maximising $\Lcal$ over $\phivec$ with $\thetavec$ fixed minimises the KL divergence from $q_{\phivec}$ to the true
  posterior, because the left-hand side does not depend on $\phivec$.
- Maximising $\Lcal$ over $\thetavec$ pushes up a lower bound on the log-likelihood.

The first term of the split is the ELBO. Expanding $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \zvec) = p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid
\zvec)\,p(\zvec)$ separates it into the two terms of the definition:

$$
\Lcal = \underbrace{\expect_{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid
\zvec)}}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\KL\paren{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\,
p(\zvec)}}_{\text{regulariser}}.
$$

The reconstruction term is the expected log-likelihood of $\xvec$ after encoding it to a random code and decoding the
code. With a Gaussian decoder $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec) = \Gauss(\xvec; \hat{\xvec}_{\thetavec}(\zvec),
s^2\Imat)$ it equals $-\norm{\xvec - \hat{\xvec}_{\thetavec}(\zvec)}^2 / (2s^2)$ plus a constant, the squared error of
an <NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink>. With a Bernoulli decoder it is the negative
<NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">binary cross-entropy</NoteLink>. The regulariser keeps each encoder
distribution close to the prior. It penalises codes that carry more information about $\xvec$ than the reconstruction
gain justifies, and it keeps the codes of the training data in the region the prior covers, so decoding prior samples
gives realistic outputs.

## Gradients through the sample

The reconstruction term is an expectation over $q_{\phivec}$, which depends on $\phivec$, and it is estimated by
sampling. A sampled $\zvec$ is not a differentiable function of $\phivec$. The
<NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink> writes the sample as a deterministic
function of $\phivec$ and parameter-free noise:

$$
\zvec = \muvec_{\phivec}(\xvec) + \sigmavec_{\phivec}(\xvec) \odot \epsilonvec, \qquad \epsilonvec \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \Imat),
$$

where $\odot$ is the elementwise product. Then $\expect_{q_{\phivec}}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)} =
\expect_{\epsilonvec}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \muvec_{\phivec} + \sigmavec_{\phivec} \odot \epsilonvec)}$.
The distribution of $\epsilonvec$ does not depend on $\phivec$, so the gradient moves inside the expectation. A
single draw of $\epsilonvec$ per data point gives an unbiased estimate that backpropagation computes through the
decoder and the encoder. The <NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function estimator</NoteLink> is also
unbiased but has much higher variance here <Cite id="kingma2014" />.

The KL term needs no sampling. For a diagonal Gaussian encoder and a standard Gaussian prior it has a closed form
(derivation below):

$$
\KL\paren{\Gauss(\muvec, \diag\sigmavec^2) \,\|\, \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)} = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^k \paren{\mu_j^2 +
\sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2}.
$$

<Derivation title="KL divergence between a diagonal Gaussian and the standard Gaussian">
Both distributions factorise over the $k$ coordinates, and the KL divergence of product distributions is the sum of
the coordinate-wise divergences. Take one coordinate, $q = \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \Gauss(0, 1)$. The log
densities are

$$
\log q(z) = -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, \qquad \log p(z) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi) -
\frac{z^2}{2}.
$$

Take expectations under $q$, using $\expect_q\brack{(z - \mu)^2} = \sigma^2$ and $\expect_q\brack{z^2} = \mu^2 +
\sigma^2$:

$$
\KL(q \,\|\, p) = \expect_q\brack{\log q(z) - \log p(z)} = -\tfrac12\log\sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 +
\sigma^2).
$$

Summing over coordinates gives the formula. It is zero exactly when $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$.
</Derivation>

## Training

Each step takes a minibatch, encodes it, draws one $\epsilonvec$ per example, decodes, and ascends the ELBO:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def vae_loss(x, encoder, decoder):
    mu, log_var = encoder(x)                         # each (batch, k)
    z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * log_var) * torch.randn_like(mu)   # reparameterised sample
    logits = decoder(z)                              # Bernoulli decoder for pixels in [0, 1]
    recon = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction="sum")
    kl = 0.5 * torch.sum(mu**2 + log_var.exp() - 1 - log_var)
    return (recon + kl) / x.shape[0]                # negative ELBO per example
```

The encoder outputs $\log \sigma^2$ rather than $\sigma$ so that the variance stays positive without a constraint.

**Generation.** Draw $\zvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ and pass it through the decoder. The encoder is not used.

With a bag-of-words decoder and a softmax that maps $\zvec$ to topic proportions, the same recipe gives the
<NoteLink to="neural-topic-models">neural topic models</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Use the model in the figure: $p(z) = \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $p(x \mid z) = \Gauss(wz, s^2)$ with $w = 2$ and $s = 0.5$. The
marginal is $p(x) = \Gauss(0, w^2 + s^2) = \Gauss(0, 4.25)$, so for $x = 1$ the log evidence is
$-\tfrac12\log(2\pi \times 4.25) - 1/(2 \times 4.25) = -1.760$.

The exact posterior is Gaussian with precision $1 + w^2/s^2 = 17$ and mean $(wx/s^2)/17 = 8/17 = 0.471$. Take this as
the encoder distribution $q(z \mid x) = \Gauss(0.471, 1/17)$, with $\sigma^2 = 0.0588$.

- KL term: $\tfrac12(0.471^2 + 0.0588 - 1 - \log 0.0588) = 1.057$.
- Reconstruction term: $\expect_q\brack{(x - wz)^2} = (1 - 2 \times 0.471)^2 + 4 \times 0.0588 = 0.239$, so
  $\expect_q\brack{\log p(x \mid z)} = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi \times 0.25) - 0.239/(2 \times 0.25) = -0.703$.
- ELBO: $-0.703 - 1.057 = -1.760$, equal to $\log p(x)$ because $q$ is the exact posterior.

## Assumptions

- **A factorised Gaussian posterior.** The diagonal covariance cannot represent correlations between latent
  coordinates in the true posterior. The shortfall is part of the KL gap.
- **One encoder for all inputs.** The amortised encoder may fit individual posteriors worse than per-point optimisation
  would. This extra loss is called the amortisation gap.
- **A simple decoder distribution.** A factorised Gaussian or Bernoulli decoder treats pixels as independent given
  $\zvec$.

## Pitfalls

- **Blurry samples.** A factorised Gaussian decoder with a fixed variance penalises squared error, whose optimum under
  uncertainty is an average of plausible outputs. Averages of images look blurred.
- **Posterior collapse.** With an expressive autoregressive decoder, the model can ignore $\zvec$: the encoder outputs
  the prior for every input, the KL term is zero, and the decoder models $\xvec$ alone. Bowman et al. observed this in
  text VAEs and reduced it by increasing the KL weight from 0 to 1 during training <Cite id="bowman2016" />.
- **The ELBO is not the likelihood.** Comparing models by the ELBO mixes model quality with the quality of the encoder.
  Estimate $\log p(\xvec)$ with <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink>, using $q_{\phivec}$
  as the proposal, to compare models.
- **Summing versus averaging.** The reconstruction term is a sum over $d$ pixels and the KL term a sum over $k$
  latents. Averaging the reconstruction over pixels, as some library losses do by default, silently changes their
  relative weight.
