<Definition>
A recurrent neural network (RNN) maps a sequence $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_T$ with $\xvec_t \in \reals^m$ to hidden states
$\hvec_t \in \reals^n$ by the recurrence
$$
\hvec_t = \phi\paren{\Wmat \hvec_{t-1} + \Umat \xvec_t + \bvec}, \qquad \hvec_0 = \zeros,
$$
where $\Wmat \in \reals^{n \times n}$, $\Umat \in \reals^{n \times m}$, $\bvec \in \reals^n$ and $\phi$ is an
elementwise nonlinearity, usually $\tanh$. An output $\hat\yvec_t = g(\Vmat \hvec_t + \cvec)$ can be read at any step.
The same parameters are used at every step <Cite id="elman1990" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A feed-forward network takes an input of fixed size. Text, speech and sensor readings arrive as sequences of varying
length, and their meaning depends on order. An RNN handles them with a loop. At each step it combines the new input with
a state vector that carries information from earlier steps.

Because the weights are shared across steps, the number of parameters does not depend on $T$. What the network learns
about a pattern at one position applies at every position. This is the same idea as weight sharing in a
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink>, applied along time instead of space.

## Unrolling

Writing out the recurrence for $T$ steps turns the RNN into a feed-forward network with $T$ layers. Layer $t$ takes
$\hvec_{t-1}$ and $\xvec_t$, and every layer has the same weights. This unrolled graph is a <Gloss name="dag">directed
acyclic graph</Gloss>, so ordinary <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink> applies to it. Applied to
the unrolled graph it is called <NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">backpropagation through time</NoteLink>.

The hidden state is a deterministic function of the whole prefix:

$$
\hvec_t = f_{\theta}(\hvec_{t-1}, \xvec_t) = f_{\theta}(f_{\theta}(\hvec_{t-2}, \xvec_{t-1}), \xvec_t) = \cdots,
$$

so $\hvec_t$ depends on $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_t$ and on nothing later.

## Input–output patterns

- **Many to one.** Read the whole sequence and predict from $\hvec_T$. Example: sentiment of a review.
- **Many to many, aligned.** Predict $\hat\yvec_t$ at every step. Examples: tagging each word with its part of speech,
  or predicting the next character.
- **Many to many, unaligned.** An encoder reads the input and a decoder writes an output of a different length. See
  <NoteLink to="sequence-to-sequence">sequence-to-sequence models</NoteLink>.
- **Bidirectional.** A second RNN reads the sequence backwards. Concatenating the two states at step $t$ gives a
  representation of the whole sequence centred on $t$. This needs the whole input in advance.

As a **<Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss>**, an RNN predicts
$p(x_{t+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_t) = \operatorname{softmax}(\Vmat \hvec_t + \cvec)$ and is trained to maximise the
log-likelihood of the next symbol at every step.

## Parameters

An RNN with $m$ inputs and $n$ hidden units has $n^2 + nm + n = n(n + m) + n$ recurrent parameters. With $m = n = 256$
that is $256 \cdot 512 + 256 = 131{,}328$ parameters, for any sequence length.

## Worked example

Take a scalar RNN with $n = m = 1$, $h_t = \tanh(w h_{t-1} + u x_t)$, $w = 0.5$, $u = 1$, no bias and $h_0 = 0$. Feed
the input $x = (1, 0, 0)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h_1 &= \tanh(0.5 \cdot 0 + 1) = \tanh(1) = 0.762, \\
h_2 &= \tanh(0.5 \cdot 0.762 + 0) = \tanh(0.381) = 0.363, \\
h_3 &= \tanh(0.5 \cdot 0.363 + 0) = \tanh(0.182) = 0.180.
\end{aligned}
$$

The input at step 1 is still visible at step 3, but its trace shrinks by roughly a factor $w = 0.5$ per step. Near zero,
$\tanh z \approx z$, so the state decays like $w^t$. With $\abs{w} < 1$ the network forgets geometrically. The same
factor governs how <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> shrink when they flow backwards, which is the subject of
<NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">backpropagation through time</NoteLink>.

## Relation to other sequence models

A <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink> also keeps a state that summarises the past. Its
state is a discrete <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> and inference averages over it.
An RNN's state is a deterministic real vector, so there is nothing to average over, and its update can be any
differentiable function.

Gated variants, the <NoteLink to="long-short-term-memory">long short-term memory (LSTM)</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="gated-recurrent-unit">gated recurrent unit (GRU)</NoteLink>, replace the RNN's update to keep
information for longer.

## Assumptions

- The past relevant to the future can be compressed into a fixed-size vector $\hvec_t$.
- The same update rule applies at every step: the process is stationary in the sense that position does not change the
  dynamics.

## Pitfalls

- **Short memory in practice.** A vanilla RNN can represent long dependencies, but
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> rarely finds them because gradients vanish over many steps
  <Cite id="bengio1994" />. Use an LSTM or GRU, or attention.
- **Sequential computation.** Step $t$ needs $\hvec_{t-1}$, so the steps of one sequence cannot run in parallel. This
  makes RNNs slow to train on long sequences compared with convolutions or transformers.
- **Exploding states.** With $\phi$ unbounded, such as <Gloss name="relu" />, and a recurrent matrix with spectral
  radius above 1, the state can grow without limit. $\tanh$ keeps every unit in $(-1, 1)$.
