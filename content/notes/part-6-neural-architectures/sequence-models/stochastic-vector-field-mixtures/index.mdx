<Definition>
A **stochastic vector field mixture** (SVFM) models continuous-time latent states $\hvec(t) \in \reals^D$ through a
mixture of $K$ candidate vector fields $\fvec^{(k)}(\hvec(t), t; \thetavec)$, governed by time-dependent mixing
probabilities $\pivec(t) \in \Delta^{K-1}$:

$$
\dot{\hvec}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k(t) \, \fvec^{(k)}\paren{\hvec(t), t; \thetavec},
$$

where each component $\fvec^{(k)}$ can be either a deterministic vector field (VF) or a **stochastic vector field**
(SVF) parameterized by a directional distribution on the unit sphere and a speed distribution on $\reals_{>0}$
<Cite id="twomey2020" />.
</Definition>

## Why standard neural ODEs fail on branching and crossing

A standard <NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">neural ordinary differential equation</NoteLink>
governs trajectories by $\dot{\hvec}(t) = \fvec_{\thetavec}(\hvec(t), t)$. When $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ is Lipschitz
continuous, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem guarantees that initial value problems have unique solutions:
trajectories starting at distinct points can never cross, merge, or branch in state space <Cite id="chen2018" />.

While uniqueness ensures invertibility in continuous normalising flows, it creates structural bottlenecks for real-world
processes:

1. **Splitting / Multimodal outcomes:** If a system begins at state $\xvec(0) \approx \zeros$ and can diverge towards
   distinct destinations (such as pedestrians choosing left or right hallways), a single deterministic ODE is forced to
   average the destinations, predicting a physically impossible intermediate state.
2. **Crossing:** If two trajectories must swap coordinates (for instance, $x_1(0) = -1 \to x_1(1) = 1$ and
   $x_2(0) = 1 \to x_2(1) = -1$ in 1-D), their continuous paths would need to intersect, which is impossible without
   lifting the state into unobserved dimensions (as in augmented neural ODEs <Cite id="dupont2019" />).
3. **Scaling / Heterogeneous speeds:** When instances travel along the same direction but cover widely differing
   distances, a single deterministic field requires extreme gradients or stiffness in the ODE solver.

## The SVFM formulation

To resolve these limitations, Twomey et al. <Cite id="twomey2020" /> introduce a lattice of continuous-depth models:

- **Deterministic Vector Field (VF):** An MLP predicting the derivative $\dot{\hvec} = \fvec(\hvec, t)$.
- **Stochastic Vector Field (SVF):** Separates the vector field into an **orientation** $\uvec$ on the unit sphere
  and a scalar **speed** $v > 0$:

$$
\uvec \sim \Ncal_s\paren{\muvec^{(u)}, \tau^{(u)}}, \qquad \log v \sim \Ncal\paren{\log \mu^{(v)}, \tau^{(v)}}, \qquad \dot{\hvec} = v \, \uvec.
$$

The spherical distribution $\Ncal_s$ is constructed as a projected normal distribution with tangential projection
$\Pmat = \Imat - \muvec^{(u)} {\muvec^{(u)}}^\top$, ensuring directional consistency ($\uvec^\top \muvec^{(u)} > 0$)
while capturing velocity spread.

### Component selection: pick-and-stick vs forward filtering

When $K > 1$, assigning instances to vector fields over time can follow two regimes:

1. **Pick-and-stick:** Initial routing $\pivec(t_0) = \operatorname{softmax}(\fvec_\pi(\hvec(t_0), t_0))$ is evaluated
   at the start and held constant throughout the trajectory ($\pivec(t) = \pivec(t_0)$).
2. **Forward filtering:** Dynamic gating where a transition matrix $\boldsymbol{\Psi}(t) \in \reals^{K \times K}$ and
   emission vector $\boldsymbol{\psi}(t) \in \Delta^{K-1}$ allow trajectories to transition between vector fields at
   branching points:

$$
\pivec(t_i) \propto \boldsymbol{\Psi}(t_i)^\top \paren{\boldsymbol{\psi}(t_i) \odot \pivec(t_{i-1})}.
$$

### Path regularisation

Unregularised neural ODEs often develop convoluted, high-frequency vector fields that require hundreds of numerical
solver steps (NFEs). SVFM incorporates path regularisers:

- **Transport loss ($\Lcal_T$):** Penalises kinetic energy $\int_0^T \|\dot{\hvec}(t)\|^2 \, dt$, encouraging straight,
  constant-velocity paths.
- **Variance loss ($\Lcal_V$):** Penalises spatial variation across the vector field along the trajectory, dampening
  spurious oscillations.

## Worked comparison

Consider the splitting problem with $x(0) \approx 0$ and targets $y \in \{-1, +1\}$:

- **Standard Neural ODE (VF):** The loss $\expect[(h(1) - y)^2]$ forces the flow to converge to $h(1) \approx 0$,
  completely failing to capture either target branch.
- **SVF (Single stochastic field):** Generates speed variance, spreading outputs along a Gaussian cloud around 0, but
  cannot split into two well-separated modes.
- **VFM ($K=2$):** Component 1 drives paths towards $+1$ and Component 2 drives paths towards $-1$. The mixture weights
  $\pi_1 \approx 0.5$ and $\pi_2 \approx 0.5$ correctly capture both destination clusters.
- **SVFM ($K=2$ with SVF units):** Captures both modes while accurately modelling the spread within each destination
  cluster.
