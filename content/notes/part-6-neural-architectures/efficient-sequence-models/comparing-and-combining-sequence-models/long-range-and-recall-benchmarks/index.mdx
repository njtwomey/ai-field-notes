<Definition>
**Long-range benchmarks** give a model a long input, thousands to tens of thousands of steps, and one label that depends
on elements far apart; the Long Range Arena (LRA) and its hardest task, Path-X, are the standard suite
<Cite id="tay2021lra" />. **Recall benchmarks** ask the model to reproduce a token seen earlier in the context, chosen
by its content: copying, selective copying, induction heads, and multi-query associative recall (MQAR), in which the
model reads $N$ key-value pairs and must answer every key with its value <Cite id="arora2024zoology" />. The two kinds
separate sequence layers along different axes: how far information can travel, and how much of it a fixed-size state can
hold.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A sequence layer can fail on a long input in two ways. It can lose the signal before it reaches the output, as an
<NoteLink to="recurrent-neural-network">RNN</NoteLink> with <NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">vanishing
gradients</NoteLink> does. Or it can carry information across the whole input but not enough of it, as any layer with a
state of fixed size must once the input holds more facts than the state has room for.

Long-range benchmarks test the first. A classifier of a 16,384-pixel image of two dots and a winding path needs to
integrate evidence along the path, but the answer is one bit. A <NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models">structured
state-space layer</NoteLink> with a long, smooth convolution kernel is built for this, and S4 was the first model to
solve Path-X <Cite id="gu2022s4" />.

Recall benchmarks test the second. To answer "C?" after reading "A 4 B 3 C 6 F 1", a model must have kept the pair
(C, 6) and every other pair, because it did not know which would be asked. Attention keeps every token in its cache
and looks the answer up. A recurrent layer with a state of $s$ numbers can store only so many pairs, and its accuracy
falls as the number of pairs grows against $s$ <Cite id="arora2024based" />.

## Details

### Long Range Arena

Long Range Arena has six tasks, each a classification of one long sequence (or two, for retrieval)
<Cite id="tay2021lra" />:

| Task       | Input                                                    | Length       | Classes |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------- |
| ListOps    | nested expressions with MAX, MEAN, MEDIAN and SUM <Gloss name="mod" />    | up to 2,048  | 10      |
| Text       | IMDb movie reviews as bytes                              | 4,096        | 2       |
| Retrieval  | two papers as bytes; do they cite each other?            | 4,096 each   | 2       |
| Image      | <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> images as sequences of grey-level pixels        | 1,024        | 10      |
| Pathfinder | a 32 × 32 image: are the two dots joined by a dashed path? | 1,024      | 2       |
| Path-X     | the same at 128 × 128                                    | 16,384       | 2       |

The tasks forbid what would make them easy: no pretraining, byte or pixel inputs instead of words or patches, and no
cross-attention between the two documents of the retrieval task. In the original paper a transformer averaged 54.4 over
the first five tasks, the best <NoteLink to="efficient-attention">efficient transformer</NoteLink> 55.0, and every model
was at chance (50%) on Path-X <Cite id="tay2021lra" />. S4 averaged 80.5 and reached 88% on Path-X; an updated S4
averaged 86.1 and reached 96.4% <Cite id="gu2022s4" />. The <NoteLink to="linear-recurrent-unit-and-s5">linear recurrent
unit and S5</NoteLink> reached similar scores with diagonal recurrences.

These results measure more than the layer. Amos, Berant and Gupta pretrained each model on the training inputs of the
task itself with a denoising objective before fine-tuning. Pretrained this way, a plain transformer matched S4 on LRA,
and the gaps between architectures shrank <Cite id="amos2024" />. Training from random weights on 16,000-step inputs
rewards a good initialisation of long-range memory, which S4's <Gloss name="hippo" /> matrices supply, as much as it
rewards the capacity to represent the task.

### Recall tasks

- **Copying.** Reproduce a string of $n$ random tokens after a separator. A two-layer transformer can copy strings of
  length exponential in its size, while a model with a fixed-size state cannot copy strings whose information exceeds
  the state; trained transformers learned copying faster and generalised to longer strings than
  <Gloss name="state-space-model">state-space models</Gloss> of the same size <Cite id="jelassi2024" />.
- **Selective copying.** A few content tokens at random positions among noise tokens; output the content tokens in
  order. The gaps vary, so a time-invariant kernel cannot pick the tokens out. S4 solved about 18% and Mamba's selective
  layer about 97% <Cite id="gu2023mamba" /> (see <NoteLink to="mamba" />).
- **Induction heads.** After "… Harry Potter …", predict "Potter" the next time "Harry" appears. The task names the
  attention circuit of two heads that does this in transformers <Cite id="olsson2022" /> (see
  <NoteLink to="induction-heads-and-circuits" />).
- **Associative recall.** Pairs of a key and a value, then one key; output its value. H3 solved it with 99.8% accuracy
  against 86.0% for S4D, which matched attention <Cite id="fu2023h3" />.
- **Multi-query associative recall (MQAR).** All keys are queried, at several positions, in one sequence: "A 4 B 3 C
  6 F 1 E 2 → A ? C ? F ? E ? B ?" has answers 4, 6, 1, 2, 3 <Cite id="arora2024zoology" />. Language models answer
  many recall queries per sequence, and single-query associative recall had stopped separating architectures: the
  convolutional and recurrent models that solved it still trailed attention in perplexity.

### What MQAR explains

Arora et al. measured the perplexity gap between attention and gated-convolution models (H3, Hyena, RWKV) on the Pile
<Cite id="arora2024zoology" />. Tokens that complete an earlier n-gram, which a recall step predicts, were about 6.4%
of the tokens and accounted for 82% of the gap. On synthetic MQAR, attention solved every sequence length with a model
width of 64, while two-layer gated convolutions needed a width at least as large as the sequence length.

A follow-up made the cost a tradeoff <Cite id="arora2024based" />. Across attention, Mamba, RWKV, linear attention and
others, recall accuracy on MQAR rose with the size of the recurrent state, and attention, whose state is its whole
cache, sat at the end of the curve. Their model, Based, combines linear attention with a short sliding window. Its two
sizes, the feature dimension and the window, move it along the recall-memory curve. A 1.3B Based model matched Mamba in
perplexity and beat it by 6.22 points on recall-intensive tasks. Mamba-2's larger state was measured the same way: on
MQAR, raising its state from 16 to 64 and to 256 improved accuracy each time <Cite id="dao2024" />.

### Why a fixed state limits recall

A counting argument sets the limit. Storing $N$ pairs whose values come from a vocabulary of $V$ tokens takes at least
$N\log_2 V$ bits. A state of $s$ numbers with $b$ bits each holds $sb$ bits, so no recurrent layer, however trained, can
answer every query once $N\log_2 V > sb$. Attention escapes the bound because its cache grows by one key and one value
per token.

A linear memory meets a sharper limit. The state of causal <NoteLink to="linear-attention">linear attention</NoteLink>
is $\Smat = \sum_i \vvec_i\kvec_i^\tr \in \reals^{d_v \times d_k}$, and its read for a query $\qvec$ is
$\Smat\qvec = \sum_i (\kvec_i^\tr\qvec)\,\vvec_i$. With orthonormal keys, reading key $j$ returns $\vvec_j$ exactly,
and at most $d_k$ keys can be orthonormal. Any further key overlaps some stored key and its read picks up a share of
that key's value. The <NoteLink to="delta-rule-and-test-time-training">delta rule</NoteLink> chooses which
associations to keep, but its state is the same $d_v \times d_k$ matrix and holds at most $d_k$ exact associations.

<Derivation title="A d_v × d_k matrix stores at most d_k exact associations">
Suppose a matrix $\Smat \in \reals^{d_v \times d_k}$ returns $\Smat\kvec_i = \vvec_i$ for $N$ pairs. Stack the keys as
the columns of $\Kmat \in \reals^{d_k \times N}$ and the values as the columns of $\Vmat \in \reals^{d_v \times N}$, so
$\Smat\Kmat = \Vmat$. The rank of $\Smat\Kmat$ is at most $\min(\operatorname{rank}\Smat, \operatorname{rank}\Kmat)
\le d_k$. When the values are in general position and $N \le d_v$, $\operatorname{rank}\Vmat = N$, so $N \le d_k$.
Conversely, for $N \le d_k$ linearly independent keys, $\Smat = \Vmat(\Kmat^\tr\Kmat)^{-1}\Kmat^\tr$ satisfies
$\Smat\Kmat = \Vmat$: a $d_k$-dimensional key space stores exactly $d_k$ arbitrary associations, and no more.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Take MQAR with $N = 8$ pairs and scalar values ($d_v = 1$): keys A to H with values 4, 3, 6, 1, 2, 7, 5, 9. A read is
decoded to the nearest value token in $\{1, \dots, 9\}$.

**Keys of dimension 4.** Embed the first four keys as the coordinate vectors $\evec_1, \dots, \evec_4$ of $\reals^4$
and the last four as the rows of a $4 \times 4$ Hadamard matrix divided by 2: $\tfrac12(1, 1, 1, 1)$,
$\tfrac12(1, -1, 1, -1)$, $\tfrac12(1, 1, -1, -1)$ and $\tfrac12(1, -1, -1, 1)$. Every key has unit length. Each of
the last four has dot product $\pm\tfrac12$ with each of the first four, and the last four are orthonormal among
themselves.

1. **Linear memory.** The state is the row vector $\wvec = \sum_i v_i\kvec_i$. Its first entry collects $v_1 = 4$ from
   $\evec_1$ and $\tfrac12(2 + 7 + 5 + 9) = 11.5$ from the Hadamard keys: $w_1 = 15.5$. In full,
   $\wvec = (15.5, -1.5, 3.5, 0.5)$.
2. **Reads.** Key A reads $\wvec^\tr\evec_1 = 15.5$ instead of 4, key B reads $-1.5$ instead of 3, key C 3.5 instead
   of 6, and key D 0.5 instead of 1. Key E reads $\tfrac12(15.5 - 1.5 + 3.5 + 0.5) = 9$ instead of 2; F, G and H read
   10, 5 and 7 instead of 7, 5 and 9. Decoded, only G (5) and D (0.5, nearest token 1) are right. Eight associations
   do not fit in four numbers.
3. **<NoteLink to="scaled-dot-product-attention">Softmax attention</NoteLink>.** The cache keeps all 8 keys and values.
   A query equal to key A has score 1 with A, 0 with B, C and D, and $\tfrac12$ with E to H. With a score scale of 10
   the softmax weights are proportional to $e^{10} =
   22{,}026$ for A, $e^{5} = 148.4$ for each of E to H, and $e^{0} = 1$ for B to D. Their sum is 22,623, so A gets
   weight 0.974. The read is $0.974 \times 4 + 0.00656 \times (2 + 7 + 5 + 9) + 0.00004 \times (3 + 6 + 1) = 4.05$,
   decoded as 4. Every other key reads within 0.1 of its value, and all 8 answers are right.
4. **A flat softmax.** With the default scale $1/\sqrt{d_k} = 0.5$ the weights are nearly uniform and every read lies
   between 4.26 and 4.96, the mean of the values pulled slightly towards the right one. Recall needs a sharp softmax:
   queries and keys with large norms, or a large learned scale.

Attention's memory here is $2 \times 8 \times 4 = 64$ numbers and grows with every pair; the linear memory is 4 numbers
and stays 4. The figure repeats the comparison with random keys over many sequences.

## Assumptions

- **Synthetic tasks stand for real ones.** MQAR, copying and selective copying isolate one ability. Their value rests on
  evidence such as the 82% of the Pile perplexity gap that recall accounted for <Cite id="arora2024zoology" />.
- **Training from scratch.** LRA assumes models are trained from random weights on the task alone; with
  self-pretraining the ranking changes <Cite id="amos2024" />.
- **Equal sizes.** Comparisons hold model width or parameters fixed. A recurrent layer's state size is then set by its
  width and state dimension, and an attention layer's cache by the sequence length.

## Pitfalls

- **LRA is not language.** S4 solved LRA about two years before state-space models matched transformers on language,
  because LRA rewards long smooth kernels and does not test recall of many facts.
- **Passkey retrieval is weak evidence.** Finding one random number hidden in filler text needs only one stored item;
  a recurrent state can carry it across any length. Samba retrieved passkeys at 256K tokens with 2,048-token windows
  (see <NoteLink to="hybrid-sequence-models" />). Many-item tasks such as phone-book lookup or MQAR separate the models.
- **Window lengths hide failures.** A model with <NoteLink to="sliding-window-and-sparse-attention">sliding-window
  attention</NoteLink> recalls perfectly up to its window and fails beyond it: Griffin's phone-book accuracy dropped at
  its window of 1,024 tokens <Cite id="de2024griffin" />. Evaluate past every window in the model.
- **Accuracy is not the only axis.** A recurrent model that recalls worse may still be the better choice at a fixed
  memory or throughput budget; Based and the hybrids are points on that curve, not winners or losers.
