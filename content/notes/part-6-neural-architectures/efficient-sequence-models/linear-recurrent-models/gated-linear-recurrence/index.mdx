<Definition>
A **gated linear recurrence** updates a state $\hvec_t \in \reals^D$ by

$$
\hvec_t = \avec_t \odot \hvec_{t-1} + \bvec_t,
$$

where $\odot$ is the elementwise product, the decay $\avec_t \in (0, 1)^D$ and the input $\bvec_t \in \reals^D$ are
functions of the current token $\xvec_t$ only, and neither depends on $\hvec_{t-1}$. The decay acts as a forget gate
chosen by the token. Because the update is linear in the state, every $\hvec_t$ can be computed at once by a
<NoteLink to="parallel-scan">parallel scan</NoteLink> <Cite id="martin2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two older designs sit on either side of this one. A <NoteLink to="gated-recurrent-unit">GRU</NoteLink> or an
<NoteLink to="long-short-term-memory">LSTM</NoteLink> computes its gates from the previous hidden state, so it can
decide what to keep from what it has already stored. That dependence on $\hvec_{t-1}$ makes each step wait for the one
before it, and training runs one token at a time. A time-invariant linear recurrence, as in
<NoteLink to="rwkv-and-retentive-networks">RetNet</NoteLink>, <Gloss name="s4" /> or the
<NoteLink to="linear-recurrent-unit-and-s5">linear recurrent unit</NoteLink>, applies the same decay at every step. It
trains in parallel, but it forgets a noise token and an important token at the same rate.

A gated linear recurrence keeps the gate and drops its dependence on the state. The token decides how much of the past
survives it: a large decay ($a_t \approx 1$) holds the state through filler, and a small decay overwrites the state
with the current token. The gates for all positions are computed in parallel from the inputs, and the recurrence that
remains is the linear one that a scan evaluates in $O(\log T)$ depth. What is lost is the ability to gate on memory
content within one layer; stacking layers recovers part of it, because each layer's gates read the previous layer's
states.

## Details

### Why the gates must not read the state

Write step $t$ as the affine map $f_t(\hvec) = \avec_t \odot \hvec + \bvec_t$. The scan of the
<NoteLink to="parallel-scan">parallel-scan note</NoteLink> composes these maps in any bracketing, which requires every
$f_t$ to be known before the scan starts. When $\avec_t$ and $\bvec_t$ depend on $\xvec_t$ alone, a matrix product over
the whole sequence gives all of them first. If $\avec_t$ depended on $\hvec_{t-1}$, the map $f_t$ would not exist until
$\hvec_{t-1}$ had been computed, and the computation would be sequential again. The diagonal form matters too: a
combination of two steps costs $D$ multiplications, and its result is again diagonal.

### The family

The models below differ in how they form $\avec_t$ and $\bvec_t$ and in the shape of the state.

- **minGRU and minLSTM.** Feng et al. strip the GRU and LSTM of their hidden-state inputs. minGRU uses
  $\zvec_t = \sigma(\Wmat_z\xvec_t)$, a candidate $\tilde\hvec_t = \Wmat_h\xvec_t$ and
  $\hvec_t = (1 - \zvec_t) \odot \hvec_{t-1} + \zvec_t \odot \tilde\hvec_t$, so $\avec_t = 1 - \zvec_t$
  <Cite id="feng2024minrnn" />. minLSTM normalises its forget and input gates to sum to one. Trained by a scan, minGRU
  is about 175 times faster per training step than a GRU at sequence length 512.
- **HGRN.** The forget gate has a learned lower bound that grows with depth:
  $\lambdavec_t = \gamma_k + (1 - \gamma_k)\,\sigma(\Wmat_\mu\xvec_t + \bvec_\mu)$ in layer $k$, with input weight
  $1 - \lambdavec_t$. Low layers can forget fast and model local structure; high layers are forced to remember
  <Cite id="qin2023hgrn" />.
- **RG-LRU (Griffin and Hawk).** The decay is a power of a learned base decay, set by a recurrence gate. It is described
  in full below <Cite id="de2024griffin" />.
- **Gated linear attention.** <Gloss name="gla" /> keeps a matrix state, as in <NoteLink to="linear-attention">linear
  attention</NoteLink>, and its gate decays each key dimension separately:
  $\Smat_t = \operatorname{diag}(\alphavec_t)\,\Smat_{t-1} + \kvec_t\vvec_t^\tr$ with
  $\alphavec_t = \sigma(\xvec_t\Wmat_{\alpha 1}\Wmat_{\alpha 2} + \bvec_\alpha)^{1/\tau}$ and $\tau = 16$, and the
  output is $\Smat_t^\tr\qvec_t$ <Cite id="yang2024gla" />. The low-rank gate map ($d \to 16 \to d_k$) keeps the gate
  cheap, and the temperature pushes decays towards 1.
- **Mamba.** The selective state-space layer is the same pattern with $\avec_t = e^{\Delta_t\Amat}$ for a step
  $\Delta_t$ computed from the token, and with input and output maps that also depend on it. The
  <NoteLink to="mamba">Mamba note</NoteLink> shows that its scalar case is a gated update of this form. RWKV-6 and
  <NoteLink to="mamba-2-and-state-space-duality">Mamba-2</NoteLink> also belong here; they are treated in their own
  notes.

### The RG-LRU

Griffin's recurrent block uses the real-gated linear recurrent unit. For an input $\xvec_t \in \reals^D$,

$$
\rvec_t = \sigma(\Wmat_a\xvec_t + \bvec_a), \qquad \ivec_t = \sigma(\Wmat_x\xvec_t + \bvec_x), \qquad
\avec_t = \avec^{c\,\rvec_t}, \qquad
\hvec_t = \avec_t \odot \hvec_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \avec_t^2} \odot (\ivec_t \odot \xvec_t),
$$

with the power and the square root taken elementwise <Cite id="de2024griffin" />. The **recurrence gate** $\rvec_t$
sets the decay and the **input gate** $\ivec_t$ filters the token. The base decay is $\avec = \sigma(\Lambdab)$ for a
learned $\Lambdab \in \reals^D$, so $0 < a < 1$ and the recurrence is stable. The constant is $c = 8$, and
$\Lambdab$ is initialised so that $a^c$ is uniform in $[0.9, 0.999]$. The decay is computed in log space,
$\log a_t = -c\,r_t\,\operatorname{softplus}(-\Lambda)$, which keeps decays near 1 accurate.

The recurrence gate interpolates between two behaviours. At $r_t = 1$ the decay is $a^c$, the fastest forgetting the
channel allows. As $r_t \to 0$ the decay tends to 1 and the input weight $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2}$ tends to 0, so the token is
ignored and the state is carried forward unchanged.

**Memory half-life.** A token written at step $s$ is multiplied by $a_{s+1}a_{s+2}\cdots a_t$ by step $t$. With a
constant gate $r$ the factor per step is $a^{c r}$, and the weight halves after

$$
k_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{-c\,r\ln a} = \frac{\ln 2}{-r\ln a^c}
$$

tokens. A channel with $a^c = 0.9$ has a half-life of 6.6 tokens at $r = 1$ and 66 tokens at $r = 0.1$. The gate
stretches or shrinks each channel's memory by the factor $1/r_t$ at every token.

**Why $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2}$.** The input weight keeps the state on the scale of the input whatever the decay. Take a fixed
decay $a$ and inputs $u_t$ with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$, independent across steps. In the stationary regime
$\var(h_t) = \var(h_{t-1}) = v$, so

$$
v = a^2 v + (1 - a^2)\sigma^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sigma^2.
$$

With the weight $1 - a$ of an <Gloss name="ema">exponential moving average</Gloss>, the same argument gives
$v = \sigma^2(1 - a)^2/(1 - a^2) = \sigma^2(1 - a)/(1 + a)$, which falls towards 0 as $a \to 1$. Slow channels would
then carry tiny states, and their contribution to the next layer would depend on their memory length. The LRU, the
time-invariant ancestor of the RG-LRU, introduced this normalisation <Cite id="orvieto2023" />.

### Computing it

Training has two phases. All gates, decays and inputs are computed for the whole sequence by matrix products, then the
recurrence runs as a scan over the pairs $(\avec_t, \bvec_t)$. A diagonal recurrence does little arithmetic per byte it
reads, so its speed is set by memory traffic. On <Gloss name="tpu" plural />, Griffin's authors found a fused sequential
scan almost 3 times faster than the native JAX one, and the associative scan slower than both
<Cite id="de2024griffin" />. GLA's matrix state is trained chunk by chunk: matrix products within a chunk and the
recurrence between chunks, with the cumulative gates kept in log space <Cite id="yang2024gla" />.

Inference is the recurrence itself. The state has $D$ numbers for the RG-LRU and $d_k \times d_v$ per head for GLA,
whatever the context length, and each token costs a constant amount of work.

### In practice

Hawk stacks RG-LRU blocks alone. Griffin alternates two recurrent blocks with one block of
<NoteLink to="sliding-window-and-sparse-attention">local attention</NoteLink> over a window of 1024 tokens. Hawk exceeds
the reported downstream performance of Mamba, and Griffin matches Llama-2 with more than six times fewer training tokens
<Cite id="de2024griffin" />. The <NoteLink to="hybrid-sequence-models">hybrid-models note</NoteLink> covers this mixing
of recurrent and attention layers. A vector state stores few associations: the RG-LRU keeps one number per channel,
while a GLA head keeps $d_k \times d_v$. The matrix-state layers of this family (GLA, Mamba-2, and the gated
<NoteLink to="delta-rule-and-test-time-training">delta rule</NoteLink>) trade a larger state for more recall.

## Worked example

Take one RG-LRU channel with $c = 8$ and base decay $a = 0.25^{1/8} = 0.8409$, so $a^c = 0.25$. Four tokens arrive,
$x = (2,\ 0.5,\ 0.5,\ -1)$. Suppose the gates, computed from the tokens, mark tokens 1 and 4 as important:
$r = (1,\ 0.1,\ 0.1,\ 1)$ and $i = (1,\ 0.1,\ 0.1,\ 1)$. Start from $h_0 = 0$.

1. **Decays.** $a_t = 0.25^{r_t}$ gives $0.25$ for $r_t = 1$ and $0.25^{0.1} = 0.8706$ for $r_t = 0.1$. The half-lives
   are $\ln 2 / \ln 4 = 0.5$ tokens and $\ln 2 / (0.1 \ln 4) = 5$ tokens.
2. **Input weights.** $\sqrt{1 - 0.25^2} = 0.9682$ and $\sqrt{1 - 0.8706^2} = 0.4921$.
3. **Writes.** $b_t = \sqrt{1 - a_t^2}\,i_t x_t$ gives $b = (1.9365,\ 0.0246,\ 0.0246,\ -0.9682)$.
4. **States.**
   - $h_1 = 1.9365$.
   - $h_2 = 0.8706 \times 1.9365 + 0.0246 = 1.7104$.
   - $h_3 = 0.8706 \times 1.7104 + 0.0246 = 1.5136$.
   - $h_4 = 0.25 \times 1.5136 - 0.9682 = -0.5898$.

After the two filler tokens the state still holds $0.8706^2 = 76\%$ of token 1's contribution. Token 4 then replaces
most of it.

Compare the same channel with its gates held open ($r_t = i_t = 1$ at every step), a fixed decay of 0.25. The states are
$(1.9365,\ 0.9682,\ 0.7262,\ -0.7867)$. Only $0.25^2 = 6.25\%$ of token 1 survives the filler, and the filler tokens
themselves make up most of $h_3$. The gate turned a memory of half a token into a memory of five tokens exactly where
the input called for it.

## Assumptions

- **Gates read only the input.** The decay and the write are functions of $\xvec_t$. A layer cannot condition its
  forgetting on what it holds; deeper layers can, through the states of the layers below.
- **Diagonal or scalar transitions.** Each channel, or each key dimension of a matrix state, decays independently.
  Mixing channels inside the recurrence would make each scan combination a matrix product.
- **Decays in $(0, 1)$.** Positive decays below 1 keep the recurrence stable and the gradients bounded.

## Pitfalls

- **No state tracking from positive decays.** A diagonal recurrence with decays in $(0, 1)$ cannot track the parity of
  a bit string at any fixed depth in finite precision. The
  <NoteLink to="expressiveness-of-linear-recurrences">expressiveness note</NoteLink> covers the limit and the fixes
  (negative eigenvalues, non-diagonal transitions such as the <NoteLink to="delta-rule-and-test-time-training">delta
  rule</NoteLink>).
- **Recall is bounded by the state.** A vector state of $D$ numbers holds far fewer key-value associations than an
  attention cache. Retrieval tasks expose the gap; see the
  <NoteLink to="long-range-and-recall-benchmarks">recall benchmarks</NoteLink>.
- **Underflow of long products.** The cumulative decays $a_{s+1}\cdots a_t$ underflow in low precision over long
  chunks. Implementations keep cumulative log decays and subtract them, as GLA's chunked kernel does.
- **Initialisation sets the memory.** With $a^c$ initialised far below 0.9, every channel starts with a memory of a few
  tokens, and gradients through long spans vanish before the gate learns to close.
- **The leak of $\sqrt{1 - a_t^2}$.** Near $a_t = 1$ the input weight is about $\sqrt{2(1 - a_t)}$, much larger than
  $1 - a_t$. A decay close to 1 alone does not block a token; the input gate $\ivec_t$ must also close.
