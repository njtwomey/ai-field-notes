<Definition>
**Low-rank attention** replaces the $n \times n$ attention matrix
$\Pmat = \operatorname{softmax}(\Qmat\Kmat^\tr/\sqrt d)$ by a product of thin matrices: Linformer projects the keys and
values along the sequence to $k \ll n$ rows <Cite id="wang2020linformer" />, and Nyströmformer rebuilds $\Pmat$ from $m$
landmark rows and columns <Cite id="xiong2021nystromformer" />. **Hashing attention** keeps exact softmax weights but
scores each query only against keys that land in the same bucket of a
<NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hash</NoteLink>, as in Reformer
<Cite id="kitaev2020reformer" />. Both cut the cost from $O(n^2 d)$ to $O(nkd)$ or $O(n \log n\, d)$ for $n$ tokens of
head width $d$.
</Definition>

## Problem

Full attention computes all $n^2$ entries of $\Pmat$ and, in the standard algorithm, stores them
(<NoteLink to="efficient-attention">efficient attention</NoteLink> gives the costs). Two observations about trained
attention matrices suggest cheaper forms. First, many rows look alike: tokens on the same topic attend to the same
places, so $\Pmat$ has a few large <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular values</NoteLink> and a small
tail, and a low-rank matrix can stand in for it. Second, each row puts most of its weight on the few keys most similar
to the query, so finding those keys and ignoring the rest loses little.

Low-rank methods exploit the first observation and hashing the second. They fail in opposite cases. A sharp row that
attends to one token raises the rank and hurts low-rank methods. A diffuse row that spreads its weight over many keys
hurts hashing, which keeps only a bucket.

## Mechanism

### Linformer: projecting along the sequence

Linformer multiplies the keys and values by learned matrices $\Emat, \Fmat \in \reals^{k \times n}$ that mix positions:

$$
\Omat = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat(\Emat\Kmat)^\tr}{\sqrt d}}\Fmat\Vmat.
$$

$\Emat\Kmat$ and $\Fmat\Vmat$ have $k$ rows, so the score matrix is $n \times k$ and time and memory are $O(nkd)$ and
$O(nk)$ <Cite id="wang2020linformer" />. The effective attention matrix is $\operatorname{softmax}(\cdot)\Fmat$, of rank
at most $k$. Wang et al. show, by a <NoteLink to="random-projections">random-projection</NoteLink> argument, that a $k$
independent of $n$ suffices for a given approximation error, and that the attention matrices of a pretrained model have
most of their spectrum in the first few singular values. The projections can be shared between keys and values, across
heads and across layers, and can be replaced by mean pooling or a <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">strided
convolution</NoteLink>, which is what the figure uses.

### Nyströmformer: landmarks

The <NoteLink to="kernel-approximation">Nyström method</NoteLink> approximates a kernel matrix from a few of its rows
and columns <Cite id="williams2001" />. Nyströmformer applies it to the attention matrix, with $m$ landmark queries
$\tilde\Qmat$ and keys $\tilde\Kmat$ taken as the means of $m$ consecutive segments
<Cite id="xiong2021nystromformer" />:

$$
\hat\Pmat = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat\tilde\Kmat^\tr}{\sqrt d}}
\paren{\operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\tilde\Qmat\tilde\Kmat^\tr}{\sqrt d}}}^{+}
\operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\tilde\Qmat\Kmat^\tr}{\sqrt d}}.
$$

The three factors are $n \times m$, $m \times m$ and $m \times n$, and $^{+}$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse,
which Nyströmformer approximates by a few matrix iterations. Multiplying from the right by $\Vmat$ never forms
$\hat\Pmat$, so the cost is $O(nmd)$. When the middle matrix is ill-conditioned, its inverse amplifies errors, and
$\hat\Pmat$ can have negative entries; dropping its small singular values, as the iteration does implicitly, keeps it
stable.

### Reformer: hashing into buckets

Reformer finds each query's nearest keys with angular locality-sensitive hashing <Cite id="kitaev2020reformer" />. For
a random matrix $\Rmat \in \reals^{d \times b/2}$ with standard normal entries, the hash of a vector $\xvec$ is

$$
h(\xvec) = \arg\max\,[\xvec^\tr\Rmat;\ -\xvec^\tr\Rmat] \in \set{1, \dots, b},
$$

the index of the largest of the $b$ projections onto the columns of $\Rmat$ and their negatives. Vectors at a small
angle land in the same bucket with high probability. The algorithm has four steps:

1. **Share queries and keys.** Each token's key is its query scaled to unit length, so a query and the keys it should
   find hash alike. A token does not attend to itself unless no other key is available, since its own key would
   otherwise dominate.
2. **Sort and chunk.** Tokens are sorted by bucket (and by position within a bucket) and cut into chunks of equal
   length $c$. Each query attends to the keys in its own chunk and the one before it, which hold the tokens of its
   bucket.
3. **Repeat.** Hashing is random, so two similar vectors can fall into different buckets. Reformer hashes $r$ times
   with independent $\Rmat$ and combines the rounds, which lowers the chance of missing a key.
4. **Cost.** Sorting costs $O(n \log n)$ and attention $O(ncd)$ per round.

Reformer also saves memory with reversible residual layers, which recompute activations in the backward pass instead of
storing them.

The <NoteLink to="linear-attention">Performer</NoteLink> is a third approximation of the same matrix: it estimates the
softmax kernel with random features, so attention becomes a product of $n \times m$ factors computed right to left
<Cite id="choromanski2021" />. It is covered with linear attention.

## Worked example

**Linformer with mean pooling.** Take one query $q = 1$ and four keys and values of width 1, with the scale set to 1:
keys $(2, 2, -1, -1)$ and values $(1, 3, 0, 2)$.

1. **Exact attention.** The scores are $2, 2, -1, -1$ and their exponentials $7.389, 7.389, 0.368, 0.368$, summing to
   $15.51$. The weights are $0.476, 0.476, 0.024, 0.024$, and the output is
   $0.476 \times (1 + 3) + 0.024 \times (0 + 2) = 1.953$.
2. **Pool to $k = 2$.** $\Emat = \Fmat$ averages positions 1–2 and 3–4, so the projected keys are $(2, -1)$ and the
   projected values $(2, 1)$. The weights are $e^2 / (e^2 + e^{-1}) = 0.953$ and $0.047$, and the output is
   $0.953 \times 2 + 0.047 \times 1 = 1.953$. Pooling loses almost nothing, because each segment's keys are equal.
3. **Reorder the keys** to $(2, -1, 2, -1)$ with the same values. Exact attention now weights positions 1 and 3 by
   0.476 and gives $0.476 \times 1 + 0.024 \times 3 + 0.476 \times 0 + 0.024 \times 2 = 0.595$. The pooled keys are
   both $0.5$, so pooling weights the two pooled values $(2, 1)$ equally and gives $1.5$. A projection that mixes
   dissimilar keys averages away exactly the contrast that attention needs. Learned projections reduce this, but a
   fixed rank cannot follow every pattern.

**Reformer with four buckets.** Take $d = 2$, $b = 4$ and $\Rmat = \eye$, so $h(\xvec)$ picks the largest of $x_1$,
$x_2$, $-x_1$ and $-x_2$: buckets are quarter-planes centred on the four axis directions. The query is
$\qvec = (1, 0.2)$, in bucket 1 ($+x_1$). Five keys and their buckets:

| Key          | Bucket       | Score $\qvec^\tr\kvec$ | $e^{\text{score}}$ |
| ------------ | ------------ | ---------------------- | ------------------ |
| $(0.9, -0.3)$ | 1 ($+x_1$)  | 0.84                   | 2.316              |
| $(0.3, 1)$    | 2 ($+x_2$)  | 0.50                   | 1.649              |
| $(-1, 0.1)$   | 3 ($-x_1$)  | $-0.98$                | 0.375              |
| $(0.8, 0.5)$  | 1 ($+x_1$)  | 0.90                   | 2.460              |
| $(0, -1)$     | 4 ($-x_2$)  | $-0.20$                | 0.819              |

The query scores only the two keys in bucket 1. Full attention's normaliser is 7.619, and the two kept keys hold
$(2.316 + 2.460) / 7.619 = 62.7\%$ of the weight. The key $(0.3, 1)$, at $62°$ from the query, falls in the next bucket
and its 21.6% is lost. A second round with a rotated $\Rmat$ moves the bucket boundaries and may catch it.

## Cost

| Method        | Time per head         | Score memory | Causal |
| ------------- | --------------------- | ------------ | ------ |
| Full          | $O(n^2 d)$            | $O(n^2)$     | yes    |
| Linformer     | $O(nkd)$              | $O(nk)$      | no     |
| Nyströmformer | $O(nmd + m^3)$        | $O(nm)$      | no     |
| Reformer      | $O(rn\log n + rncd)$  | $O(rnc)$     | yes    |

$k$ is the projected length, $m$ the number of landmarks, $r$ the number of hashing rounds and $c$ the chunk length.
In the figure, with four topics and $\beta = 6$, the best rank-4 matrix misplaces 17% of the weight, Linformer with
pooling at $k = 4$ 20%, and Nyström with 4 landmarks 17%. LSH with two rounds scores about 55 keys per query to
misplace 21%. Sharpening the attention to $\beta = 15$ raises the rank-4 error to 37%.

## Trade-offs

- **Low rank against sharp attention.** A matrix whose rows put most weight on one or two keys is close to a permutation
  and has no good low-rank approximation. <NoteLink to="induction-heads-and-circuits">Induction heads</NoteLink> and
  copying, which look up one specific earlier token, are this case.
- **Causality.** Linformer's $\Emat$ and Nyströmformer's segment means mix all positions, including future ones, so
  neither serves a decoder without changes. The fixed $\Emat \in \reals^{k \times n}$ also fixes the sequence length.
  Reformer's hashing is compatible with a <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink>.
- **Hashing against diffuse attention.** When a query spreads its weight over many keys, a bucket holds only a part of
  it. More rounds recover more weight at proportionally more cost.
- **Exact attention got faster.** <NoteLink to="flash-attention">FlashAttention</NoteLink> made exact attention cheap in
  memory and fast at lengths of tens of thousands of tokens <Cite id="dao2022" />. Since then, <Gloss name="llm">large
  language models</Gloss> have used exact attention, windows, learned block selection or linear-attention layers rather
  than these approximations; see <NoteLink to="trainable-sparse-attention">trainable sparse attention</NoteLink>.
- **Where the idea survives.** Low-rank compression now acts on the feature dimension rather than the sequence:
  <NoteLink to="multi-head-latent-attention">multi-head latent attention</NoteLink> caches a low-rank latent per token.
  Projecting a long input onto a fixed set of learned latent queries, the Linformer idea with learned queries in place
  of $\Emat$, is the basis of <NoteLink to="perceiver-and-latent-bottlenecks">Perceiver-style bottlenecks</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Unstable pseudo-inverse.** Nyström's middle matrix can be nearly singular when two landmarks are alike. Without a
  cutoff on its small singular values the approximation can be far worse than a plain low-rank one; in the figure the
  Nyström curve leaves the chart at high $\beta$.
- **Unbalanced buckets.** Real queries cluster, so some buckets hold many tokens and some none. Reformer's
  sort-and-chunk step bounds the work per chunk but can split a large bucket across chunks, and a query then misses keys
  in its own bucket.
- **Hashing is not differentiable.** Gradients flow through the attention within buckets, not through the bucket
  choice, so the model cannot learn to fix a bad hash.
- **Speed in practice.** Sorting, gathering and hashing are memory-bound and map poorly onto the dense matrix units of
  accelerators, so the asymptotic saving turns into a speedup over exact attention only at long lengths. Tay et al.
  survey the variants and their costs <Cite id="tay2022" />.
