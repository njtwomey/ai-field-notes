<Definition>
**Efficient attention** is the family of methods that compute, approximate or replace
<NoteLink to="scaled-dot-product-attention">scaled dot-product attention</NoteLink> with less than the $O(n^2)$ time or
memory of the standard algorithm, where $n$ is the sequence length. The methods compute exact attention without storing
the $n \times n$ score matrix, restrict which query–key pairs are scored, approximate the attention matrix by a low-rank
or hashed form, or replace the softmax by a kernel so that attention becomes a recurrence <Cite id="tay2022" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Attention compares every token with every other token. Doubling the length doubles the number of queries and doubles
the number of keys each query reads, so the work grows four times. The rest of a transformer layer acts on each token
separately and grows only twice. At short lengths the per-token parts dominate; past a length set by the layer width,
attention does.

Three different costs grow with $n$, and the methods cut different ones:

- the **arithmetic** of the $n^2$ scores, in training and in reading a long prompt;
- the **memory** of the $n \times n$ score matrix, if it is stored;
- at generation, the **cache** of past keys and values that each new token reads.

Every method keeps something and gives something up. Exact methods give up nothing in quality but keep the quadratic
arithmetic. Approximate methods cut the arithmetic and lose the ability of every query to reach every key with an exact
softmax weight.

## Details

### The cost of full attention

A self-attention head takes $\Qmat, \Kmat \in \reals^{n \times d}$ and $\Vmat \in \reals^{n \times d_v}$ and computes

$$
\Omat = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat\Kmat^\tr}{\sqrt d}}\Vmat.
$$

The score matrix $\Qmat\Kmat^\tr$ has $n^2$ entries, each a dot product of length $d$, so it costs $n^2 d$
multiply–adds. The product with $\Vmat$ costs another $n^2 d_v$. The softmax normalises each row over all $n$ keys, so
every entry is needed before any output is final. Standard implementations store the $n \times n$ matrix, and keep it
for the backward pass <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.

Everything else in a transformer layer is linear in $n$: the projections and the feed-forward network act on each token
separately. For a layer of width $d_{\text{model}}$ with a feed-forward hidden width of $4d_{\text{model}}$, the four
projections cost $4nd_{\text{model}}^2$ multiply–adds and the feed-forward network $8nd_{\text{model}}^2$. The heads'
two $n \times n$ products cost $2n^2 d_{\text{model}}$ summed over heads, since the head widths add up to
$d_{\text{model}}$. The two costs are equal when $2n^2 d_{\text{model}} = 12 n d_{\text{model}}^2$, at
$n = 6d_{\text{model}}$.

In 16-bit precision the score matrix of one head for one sequence takes 32 MiB at $n = 4096$, 2 GiB at
$n = 32{,}768$ and 32 GiB at $n = 131{,}072$. At generation, a decoder reads its whole
<NoteLink to="key-value-cache">key-value cache</NoteLink> for every new token, so producing token $t$ costs $O(td)$
per head and the cache grows by one key and one value per token per layer.

### Families of methods

**Exact attention with less memory traffic.** <NoteLink to="flash-attention">FlashAttention</NoteLink> computes exact
softmax attention in tiles held in on-chip memory and combines them with an online softmax <Cite id="dao2022" />. It
keeps the $O(n^2 d)$ arithmetic but never stores the score matrix, so the extra memory is $O(n)$. Attention on
<Gloss name="gpu" plural /> is limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic at moderate lengths, so the tiled
computation is also several times faster. Because it is exact, it is the default in current training and inference
code, and the other methods are combined with it rather than compared against the naive algorithm.

**Fixed sparse patterns.** Each query attends to a fixed subset of keys: a local window, a few global tokens, strided
or random positions. Cost is proportional to the number of scored pairs. The Sparse Transformer's strided pattern costs
$O(n\sqrt n)$ <Cite id="child2019" />; Longformer's sliding window plus global tokens and BigBird's window, global and
random keys cost $O(n)$ <Cite id="beltagy2020" /> <Cite id="zaheer2020" />. The softmax is exact over the kept keys.
Information between distant tokens flows through global tokens or through several layers, and a dependency the pattern
does not cover is lost. See <NoteLink to="sliding-window-and-sparse-attention">sliding-window and sparse
attention</NoteLink>.

<AttentionPatterns initial="bigbird" />

**Learned sparse patterns.** Each query scores cheap summaries of blocks of keys and attends in full only to the
best-scoring blocks. Native sparse attention scores compressed block keys and keeps 16 blocks of 64 tokens per query
beside a sliding window <Cite id="yuan2025nsa" />. MoBA scores the mean key of each block and keeps the top $k$
<Cite id="lu2025moba" />. DeepSeek-V3.2 selects 2048 keys per query with a small scoring network
<Cite id="deepseek2025v32" />. The pattern now depends on content, and the model is trained with it. A key in a block
that scores low is missed. See <NoteLink to="trainable-sparse-attention">trainable sparse attention</NoteLink>.

**Low-rank projection and hashing.** Linformer projects the $n$ keys and values along the sequence to $k \ll n$ rows,
so the score matrix is $n \times k$ and costs $O(nk)$ <Cite id="wang2020linformer" />. Nyströmformer rebuilds the
attention matrix from a few landmark rows and columns <Cite id="xiong2021nystromformer" />. Reformer hashes queries and
keys with <Gloss name="lsh">locality-sensitive hashing</Gloss> so that each query scores only keys in its bucket, at
$O(n \log n)$ <Cite id="kitaev2020reformer" />. Low-rank methods lose sharp attention to single tokens, which raises the
rank, and Linformer's projections mix future positions, so it cannot be used causally. See
<NoteLink to="low-rank-and-hashing-attention">low-rank and hashing attention</NoteLink>.

**Kernel <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">feature maps</NoteLink>.** <NoteLink to="linear-attention">Linear
attention</NoteLink> writes the similarity as $\phi(\qvec)^\tr\phi(\kvec)$ for a feature map $\phi$ into $\reals^m$. The
product $\phi(\Qmat)(\phi(\Kmat)^\tr\Vmat)$ is then computed right to left in $O(nmd_v)$ time
<Cite id="katharopoulos2020" />. The Performer chooses random positive features whose inner product estimates the
softmax kernel without bias <Cite id="choromanski2021" />. Causal linear attention is a recurrent network whose state
has $m \times d_v$ entries, so generation needs no growing cache. The fixed state cannot hold many tokens exactly, so
recall of one specific distant token is weaker than with softmax.

**Replacing attention.** Structured state-space layers, <NoteLink to="mamba">Mamba</NoteLink> and linear-recurrent
layers such as <NoteLink to="rwkv-and-retentive-networks">RetNet and RWKV</NoteLink> drop attention for a recurrence
with a fixed-size state. <NoteLink to="hybrid-sequence-models">Hybrid models</NoteLink> keep a few attention layers
among them.

### Comparison

Costs are per head for one sequence of $n$ tokens with head width $d$. $w$ is a window size, $s$ the number of keys a
learned pattern keeps, $k$ a projection rank, $b$ a number of hash buckets and $m$ a feature dimension.

| Method                                                | Keys per query     | Time               | Score memory      | Exact softmax     | Causal      | Generation state       |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ------------------ | ----------------- | ----------------- | ----------- | ---------------------- |
| Full attention                                        | $n$                | $O(n^2 d)$         | $O(n^2)$          | yes               | yes         | <Gloss name="kv-cache" />, $O(nd)$ |
| FlashAttention <Cite id="dao2022" />                  | $n$                | $O(n^2 d)$         | $O(n)$            | yes               | yes         | KV cache, $O(nd)$      |
| Sparse Transformer <Cite id="child2019" />            | $O(\sqrt n)$       | $O(n\sqrt n\,d)$   | $O(n\sqrt n)$     | on the pattern    | yes         | KV cache               |
| Sliding window <Cite id="beltagy2020" />              | $w$                | $O(nwd)$           | $O(nw)$           | on the pattern    | yes         | last $w$ keys, $O(wd)$ |
| BigBird <Cite id="zaheer2020" />                      | $w + g + r$        | $O(n(w+g+r)d)$     | $O(n(w+g+r))$     | on the pattern    | encoder use | —                      |
| Block selection <Cite id="lu2025moba" />              | $s$                | $O(nsd + n^2d/B)$  | $O(ns)$           | on the kept keys  | yes         | KV cache, $s$ read     |
| Linformer <Cite id="wang2020linformer" />             | $k$ projected keys | $O(nkd)$           | $O(nk)$           | no                | no          | —                      |
| Reformer <Cite id="kitaev2020reformer" />             | about $2n/b$       | $O(n \log n\, d)$  | $O(n^2/b)$        | on the buckets    | yes         | KV cache               |
| Linear attention <Cite id="katharopoulos2020" />      | all, via a summary | $O(nmd)$           | $O(md)$ causal    | no                | yes         | $m \times d$ matrix    |
| Performer <Cite id="choromanski2021" />               | all, via a summary | $O(nmd)$           | $O(md)$ causal    | unbiased estimate | yes         | $m \times d$ matrix    |

"On the pattern" means that the softmax is exact over the keys the pattern keeps, and zero elsewhere. $B$ is the block
length of block selection; scoring the $n/B$ block summaries for every query is quadratic but $B$ times cheaper than
full attention.

## Worked example

Take a layer of width $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ with heads of width $d = 128$, so 32 heads, and a feed-forward width of
$4 \times 4096$.

1. **Where attention starts to dominate.** The crossover is at $n = 6 \times 4096 = 24{,}576$ tokens. At $n = 8192$,
   attention costs $2 \times 8192^2 \times 4096 = 5.50 \times 10^{11}$ multiply–adds and the rest of the layer
   $12 \times 8192 \times 4096^2 = 1.65 \times 10^{12}$, so attention is 25% of the layer. At $n = 32{,}768$ it is 57%,
   and at $n = 131{,}072$ it is 84%.
2. **One head at $n = 32{,}768$.** Full attention costs $2 \times 32{,}768^2 \times 128 = 2.75 \times 10^{11}$
   multiply–adds. Its score matrix takes 2 GiB in 16-bit; FlashAttention keeps two statistics per query instead, 128
   KiB.
3. **A window of $w = 4096$ keys.** The cost is $2nwd = 3.44 \times 10^{10}$, eight times less, because each query
   scores $4096$ of $32{,}768$ keys.
4. **Block selection with 16 kept blocks of 64.** Each query reads its own block and 16 others, $17 \times 64 = 1088$
   keys, and scores $32{,}768 / 64 = 512$ block summaries. The cost is
   $n d\,(1 + 512 + 2 \times 1088) = 1.13 \times 10^{10}$, 24 times less than full attention.
5. **Linear attention with $m = d = 128$ features.** Building the $128 \times 128$ state and reading it cost
   $2nmd = 1.07 \times 10^{9}$, about 250 times less. The causal state per head is $128 \times 128$ numbers at every
   length, against a cache of $32{,}768$ keys and values.

## Choosing a method

- **Moderate lengths, quality first.** Exact attention with FlashAttention. There is no approximation to tune.
- **Long contexts in decoder <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss>.** Current open models mix layer types
  instead of approximating every layer. Gemma 3 uses five sliding-window layers of span 1024 per global layer
  <Cite id="gemmateam2025" />; Qwen3-Next uses three linear-attention layers of the
  <NoteLink to="delta-rule-and-test-time-training">Gated DeltaNet</NoteLink> kind per attention layer
  <Cite id="qwen2025next" />; DeepSeek-V3.2 keeps attention in every layer but lets each query select 2048 keys
  <Cite id="deepseek2025v32" />.
- **Very long documents in an encoder.** Sparse patterns with global tokens, which keep exact softmax locally and give
  every token a two-hop path to every other.
- **Long generation with bounded memory.** A sliding window caps the KV cache at $w$ tokens. Linear attention and
  recurrent layers cap it at a fixed state, and lose exact recall of distant tokens. Sharing keys and values across
  heads shrinks the cache without changing the cost in $n$; see
  <NoteLink to="multi-query-and-grouped-query-attention">multi-query and grouped-query attention</NoteLink>.
- **Extending a trained model's context.** Besides the cost, positions beyond the training length must also be handled;
  see <NoteLink to="long-context-methods">long-context methods</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Leading-order costs.** The counts ignore the softmax exponentials, hashing and sorting, and the halving that a
  <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink> allows. They compare growth with $n$, not wall-clock time.
- **Arithmetic is not time.** On accelerators, a method is fast only if it maps to dense tiles. A sparse pattern scored
  key by key, or a mask applied to a dense kernel, saves no time; block-sparse kernels make the saving real.

## Pitfalls

- **Asymptotics at short lengths.** Below $n = 6d_{\text{model}}$ the per-token parts of the layer cost more than
  attention, so an approximate attention saves little and still loses accuracy.
- **Recall.** Every approximate method weakens exact retrieval of a single distant token: a fixed pattern may not cover
  it, a learned selection may score its block low, a low-rank or kernel summary blurs it. Tasks such as copying and
  associative recall expose this first; see <NoteLink to="long-range-and-recall-benchmarks">long-range and recall
  benchmarks</NoteLink>.
- **Causality and fixed length.** Linformer's projections mix all positions and fix the sequence length, so it cannot
  serve a decoder. Hashing with buckets that change from step to step complicates caching at generation.

Tay et al. survey further variants, including memory tokens, downsampling and clustering-based routing
<Cite id="tay2022" />.
