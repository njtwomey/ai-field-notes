<Definition>
Given an associative binary operator $\oplus$ and a sequence $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the **scan** (all-prefix-sums)
returns $(x_1,\ x_1 \oplus x_2,\ \dots,\ x_1 \oplus x_2 \oplus \dots \oplus x_n)$ <Cite id="blelloch1990" />. A
**parallel scan** computes all $n$ prefixes in $O(\log n)$ steps on $O(n)$ processors, with $O(n)$ total work. The
first-order linear recurrence $h_t = a_t h_{t-1} + b_t$ is a scan over the pairs $(a_t, b_t)$, so a sequence model
whose state update is linear can be evaluated in parallel over time <Cite id="martin2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A recurrence looks inherently sequential: $h_t$ needs $h_{t-1}$, which needs $h_{t-2}$. For a sum this is false. To add
eight numbers one can add them in pairs, then the pairs in pairs, and finish in three rounds instead of seven. The
rounds work because addition is associative: brackets can be placed anywhere.

A linear recurrence has the same property once each step is seen as a function. Step $t$ is the affine map
$f_t(h) = a_t h + b_t$. The state after $t$ steps is $h_t = (f_t \circ \dots \circ f_1)(h_0)$. Function composition is
associative, and the composition of two affine maps is again affine, described by two numbers. So partial compositions
can be computed in any bracketing, in parallel, and each fits in the same space as one step.

## The operator for a linear recurrence

Represent step $t$ by the pair $e_t = (a_t, b_t)$. Applying $f_s$ first and then $f_t$ gives

$$
f_t(f_s(h)) = a_t(a_s h + b_s) + b_t = (a_t a_s)\,h + (a_t b_s + b_t).
$$

Define the combination "$e_s$ then $e_t$" as

$$
(a_s, b_s) \bullet (a_t, b_t) = (a_t a_s,\ a_t b_s + b_t).
$$

The operator is associative. For three pairs,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\paren{(a_1, b_1) \bullet (a_2, b_2)} \bullet (a_3, b_3) &= (a_2 a_1,\ a_2 b_1 + b_2) \bullet (a_3, b_3)
= (a_3 a_2 a_1,\ a_3 a_2 b_1 + a_3 b_2 + b_3), \\
(a_1, b_1) \bullet \paren{(a_2, b_2) \bullet (a_3, b_3)} &= (a_1, b_1) \bullet (a_3 a_2,\ a_3 b_2 + b_3)
= (a_3 a_2 a_1,\ a_3 a_2 b_1 + a_3 b_2 + b_3).
\end{aligned}
$$

Both equal the composition $f_3 \circ f_2 \circ f_1$. The identity element is $(1, 0)$, the map $h \mapsto h$. The
operator is not commutative: the order of steps matters.

With $h_0 = 0$, the prefix $e_1 \bullet e_2 \bullet \dots \bullet e_t = (A_t, B_t)$ describes the map
$h_0 \mapsto A_t h_0 + B_t$, so $h_t = B_t$. A nonzero initial state is absorbed by replacing $b_1$ with
$a_1 h_0 + b_1$. Every state $h_1, \dots, h_n$ is therefore the second component of a scan over $e_1, \dots, e_n$
with the operator $\bullet$.

## Two parallel algorithms

### Hillis–Steele: $\log_2 n$ steps, $O(n \log n)$ work

In round $r = 0, 1, \dots$, every position $t$ with $t > 2^r$ replaces its value $y_t$ by $y_{t - 2^r} \bullet y_t$,
all positions at once. After round $r$, position $t$ holds the combination of the $\min(t, 2^{r+1})$ elements ending at
$t$. The claim holds after round 0, when each $y_t$ covers $e_{t-1}, e_t$. If it holds after round $r - 1$, the
left operand covers the $2^r$ elements ending at $t - 2^r$ and the right operand the $2^r$ elements ending at $t$, so
their combination covers $2^{r+1}$ elements. After $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ rounds every position holds its full
prefix. Each round does up to $n$ combinations, so the total work is $O(n \log n)$.

### Blelloch: $2\log_2 n$ steps, $O(n)$ work

Blelloch's scan is **work-efficient**: it does the same $O(n)$ work as the sequential loop <Cite id="blelloch1990" />.
It uses a balanced binary tree over the $n$ elements.

1. **Up-sweep (reduce).** Combine neighbouring pairs, then pairs of pairs, level by level. Each internal node stores the
   combination of the leaves beneath it. The levels have $n/2, n/4, \dots, 1$ nodes, so there are $n - 1$ combinations
   in $\log_2 n$ rounds.
2. **Down-sweep.** Give the root the identity. Each node passes to its left child the prefix it received, and to its
   right child that prefix combined with the left child's total from the up-sweep. After $\log_2 n$ rounds each leaf
   holds the combination of everything before it (an exclusive scan). Combining it with the leaf's own element gives the
   inclusive prefix.

Both sweeps do $O(n)$ work and each has $\log_2 n$ rounds. With $p$ processors the time is $O(n/p + \log n)$. On a
<Gloss name="gpu" /> the scan is usually blocked: each thread block scans its chunk sequentially or in shared memory, a
second scan runs over the chunk totals, and a final pass adds each chunk's offset.

### Worked example

Take $n = 4$ steps with $a = (0.5, 2, 0.5, 1)$, $b = (1, 1, 2, -1)$ and $h_0 = 0$. The sequential loop gives
$h_1 = 1$, $h_2 = 2 \times 1 + 1 = 3$, $h_3 = 0.5 \times 3 + 2 = 3.5$ and $h_4 = 1 \times 3.5 - 1 = 2.5$.

The up-sweep combines neighbours:

- $e_1 \bullet e_2 = (2 \times 0.5,\ 2 \times 1 + 1) = (1, 3)$;
- $e_3 \bullet e_4 = (1 \times 0.5,\ 1 \times 2 - 1) = (0.5, 1)$;
- root: $(1, 3) \bullet (0.5, 1) = (0.5,\ 0.5 \times 3 + 1) = (0.5, 2.5)$, so $h_4 = 2.5$.

Hillis–Steele reaches the same states in two rounds. After round 0 the four positions hold
$e_1 = (0.5, 1)$, $e_1 \bullet e_2 = (1, 3)$, $e_2 \bullet e_3 = (1, 2.5)$ and $e_3 \bullet e_4 = (0.5, 1)$. In round 1,
position 3 computes $e_1 \bullet (e_2 \bullet e_3) = (0.5, 1) \bullet (1, 2.5) = (0.5, 3.5)$ and position 4 computes
$(1, 3) \bullet (0.5, 1) = (0.5, 2.5)$. The second components $(1, 3, 3.5, 2.5)$ are $h_1, \dots, h_4$.

## Vector states

For a state $\hvec_t \in \reals^N$ with $\hvec_t = \Amat_t\hvec_{t-1} + \bvec_t$, the operator becomes
$(\Amat_s, \bvec_s) \bullet (\Amat_t, \bvec_t) = (\Amat_t\Amat_s,\ \Amat_t\bvec_s + \bvec_t)$, by the same derivation.
The cost of one combination depends on the structure of $\Amat_t$:

- **Dense $\Amat_t$.** A combination multiplies two $N \times N$ matrices, $O(N^3)$ time, and each partial result
  stores $N^2$ numbers. The parallel scan then costs far more than the $O(N^2)$ sequential step.
- **Diagonal $\Amat_t$.** The <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink> is elementwise, and the
  recurrence splits into $N$ independent scalar scans. This is the case for
  <NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models">diagonal state-space models</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="mamba">Mamba</NoteLink>.
- **Scalar times a matrix state.** In <NoteLink to="linear-attention">linear attention</NoteLink> with decay, the state
  is a matrix $\Smat_t = \gamma_t \Smat_{t-1} + \kvec_t\vvec_t^\tr$ and the coefficient is a scalar, so a combination
  costs one scaled matrix addition.

This is why efficient recurrent sequence models keep their transition diagonal or scalar.

Martin and Cundy applied the scan to linear recurrent networks, including a linear surrogate of the
<Gloss name="lstm" />, and reported speed-ups in training and inference of up to 9 times <Cite id="martin2018" />.
Mamba uses a fused, work-efficient scan on the GPU for its input-dependent recurrence <Cite id="gu2023mamba" />.

## Assumptions

- **Linearity in the state.** The coefficients $a_t$ and $b_t$ may depend on the input $x_t$, but not on $h_{t-1}$. A
  nonlinear recurrence such as $h_t = \tanh(W h_{t-1} + U x_t)$ has no closed composition of fixed size and cannot be
  scanned this way.
- **All inputs known.** The scan needs the whole sequence at once. It speeds up training and prompt processing, not
  token-by-token generation, where the plain recurrence is already optimal.

## Pitfalls

- **Underflow of products.** The prefix products $a_t a_{t-1} \cdots a_1$ shrink geometrically when $\abs{a_t} < 1$ and
  underflow in low precision over long sequences. Implementations scan in chunks, or keep the log of the products as a
  cumulative sum.
- **Order of arguments.** The operator is not commutative. Writing $a_s b_t + b_s$ instead of $a_t b_s + b_t$ still
  gives an associative operator, but it computes the recurrence run backwards.
- **Work versus depth.** The Hillis–Steele scan has the shortest depth but does $O(n \log n)$ work. When the operator is
  expensive, as with matrix states, the work-efficient version is faster in practice.
