<Definition>
Let $x_{n,c,h,w}$ be an activation tensor of shape $[N, C, H, W]$: $N$ examples, $C$ channels and an $H \times W$ grid.
A normalisation layer partitions the tensor's entries into sets $\Scal$, computes each set's mean $\mu_\Scal$ and
biased variance $\sigma^2_\Scal$, and outputs

$$
y_{n,c,h,w} = \gamma_c\, \frac{x_{n,c,h,w} - \mu_\Scal}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_\Scal + \epsilon}} + \beta_c
$$

for the set $\Scal$ that contains the entry, with a learned scale $\gamma_c$ and shift $\beta_c$ per channel and a small
$\epsilon$. **Batch normalisation** takes one set per channel, over $(N, H, W)$. **Layer normalisation** takes one set
per example, over $(C, H, W)$. **<NoteLink to="series-normalisation">Instance normalisation</NoteLink>** takes one set
per example and channel, over $(H, W)$. **Group normalisation** with $G$ groups takes one set per example and group of
$C/G$ consecutive channels, over $(C/G, H, W)$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

All four layers do the same arithmetic. They differ in which numbers they average together. Picture the activations as
a box with axes $N$, $C$ and the spatial plane. Batch normalisation slices the box by channel and averages each slice
across the whole mini-batch. The other three slice it by example first, so no example's output depends on another
example, and then differ in how many channels they pool.

That choice decides what the layer removes and what it depends on.

- Statistics over the batch depend on which other examples are in it. Large batches estimate them well; batches of one
  or two do not.
- Statistics over one example's channels and positions remove that example's overall scale and offset, and leave the
  relations between its channels.
- Statistics over one channel of one example remove that channel's mean and contrast, which for a
  <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">feature map</NoteLink> of an image is much of its style.

## Details

### The four sets

<NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">Batch normalisation</NoteLink> on a convolutional layer treats each channel as one
feature and pools its values over every example and every position, $NHW$ values per set <Cite id="ioffe2015" />.
Pooling over positions keeps the layer consistent with weight sharing: the same channel is normalised the same way
everywhere in the image. At inference the batch statistics are replaced by running averages, and the layer becomes a
fixed affine map.

<NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">Layer normalisation</NoteLink> pools all $CHW$ values of one example
<Cite id="ba2016" />. It has no batch dependence and behaves the same in training and inference. It forces every
channel of an example through one mean and variance, so a channel with large activations sets the scale for the rest.

Instance normalisation pools the $HW$ values of one channel of one example <Cite id="ulyanov2016instance" />. Ulyanov,
Vedaldi and Lempitsky introduced it for fast style transfer, where the contrast of the content image should not reach
the output. Adaptive instance normalisation sets $\gamma$ and $\beta$ of each channel to the standard deviation and mean
of the same channel in a style image, and so transfers one image's feature statistics onto another
<Cite id="huang2017adain" />.

<Gloss name="group-normalisation" /> sits between them. With $G$ groups it pools $(C/G)HW$ values per set
<Cite id="wu2018groupnorm" />. $G = 1$ is layer normalisation over $(C, H, W)$ and $G = C$ is instance normalisation.
Wu and He used $G = 32$ by default. They argued that channels form groups that are naturally normalised together, as
the orientation bins of hand-designed descriptors such as histograms of oriented gradients are.

### Batch size

The batch mean of a channel is an average of $NHW$ values, but values from one image are strongly correlated, so its
noise falls mainly with $N$. With a few images per device the batch statistics change from step to step, and the running
averages used at inference no longer match what the network saw in training. Wu and He trained
<NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">ResNet-50</NoteLink> on <Gloss name="imagenet" /> with 2
images per device: group normalisation had 10.6% lower error than batch normalisation, and its error stayed nearly the
same from 32 images per device down to 2 <Cite id="wu2018groupnorm" />.

This is why the normalisation depends on the task. Classification on large batches uses batch normalisation. Detection
and segmentation, with large images and a few per device, use group normalisation or batch normalisation with statistics
frozen from pretraining. Transfer from large pretrained networks in Big Transfer used group normalisation with weight
standardisation instead of batch normalisation <Cite id="kolesnikov2020" />.
<NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-probabilistic-models">Denoising diffusion</NoteLink> models use
<Gloss name="u-net" plural /> with group normalisation throughout <Cite id="ho2020" />.

### Layer normalisation in ConvNeXt

<NoteLink to="modern-convolutional-networks">ConvNeXt</NoteLink> replaced batch normalisation by layer normalisation
<Cite id="liu2022" />. Its layer normalisation is the transformer's: it normalises the $C$ channels of each pixel
separately, one set per $(n, h, w)$. That is a fifth choice of axes, over $C$ alone. It is not group normalisation with
$G = 1$, which pools over the whole $(C, H, W)$ block of an example. Library layers named LayerNorm differ on this
point, so the axes have to be checked.

### Without normalisation

Normalisation is not required for training. Brock et al. trained normaliser-free <Gloss name="resnet" plural /> that
matched the accuracy of EfficientNet-B7 while training up to 8.7 times faster. Their residual branches are scaled to
keep activations controlled, and <NoteLink to="gradient-clipping">adaptive gradient clipping</NoteLink> limits each
layer's gradient relative to the size of its weights <Cite id="brock2021nfnet" />. The normalisation layer's main job,
keeping activations at a controlled scale through depth, can be done by initialisation and clipping instead.

## Worked example

Take $N = 2$ examples, $C = 4$ channels and $2 \times 2$ grids, with $\epsilon = 0$ and no scale or shift. Each
channel's four values are listed in row order.

| channel | example 0      | example 1        |
| ------- | -------------- | ---------------- |
| 0       | $1, 3, 5, 7$   | $9, 11, 13, 15$  |
| 1       | $2, 2, 4, 4$   | $0, 0, 2, 2$     |
| 2       | $0, 4, 0, 4$   | $3, 3, 5, 5$     |
| 3       | $6, 6, 6, 10$  | $2, 4, 6, 8$     |

Normalise the first value, $x_{0,0,0,0} = 1$, four ways.

1. **Batch.** The set is channel 0 of both examples: $1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15$. Its mean is $8$. The squared
   deviations are $49, 25, 9, 1, 1, 9, 25, 49$, which sum to $168$, so the variance is $168/8 = 21$. The output is
   $(1 - 8)/\sqrt{21} = -1.528$.
2. **Layer.** The set is all 16 values of example 0, which sum to $64$, so the mean is $4$. The squared deviations sum
   to $108$, so the variance is $108/16 = 6.75$. The output is $(1 - 4)/\sqrt{6.75} = -1.155$.
3. **Instance.** The set is channel 0 of example 0: $1, 3, 5, 7$, mean $4$, variance $(9 + 1 + 1 + 9)/4 = 5$. The
   output is $(1 - 4)/\sqrt{5} = -1.342$.
4. **Group, $G = 2$.** The set is channels 0 and 1 of example 0: $1, 3, 5, 7, 2, 2, 4, 4$, mean $28/8 = 3.5$. The
   squared deviations sum to $26$, so the variance is $3.25$. The output is $(1 - 3.5)/\sqrt{3.25} = -1.387$.

Now replace example 1 by a copy of example 0. Channel 0 across the batch becomes $1, 3, 5, 7, 1, 3, 5, 7$, with mean 4
and variance 5, and batch normalisation now gives $-1.342$ for the same value. The other three outputs do not change,
because their sets never contain example 1. The figure shows both cases.

## Assumptions

- The values in one set share a scale worth removing. Batch normalisation assumes a channel's statistics are similar
  across images; instance normalisation assumes a channel's mean and contrast in one image carry no needed information.
- For batch normalisation, the examples in a batch are drawn independently from the same distribution as at test time,
  and the batch is large enough to estimate the statistics.

## Pitfalls

- **Small batches.** Batch normalisation with one or two images per device gives noisy statistics in training and a
  mismatch at inference. Group normalisation does not have this failure <Cite id="wu2018groupnorm" />.
- **Instance normalisation removes information.** Contrast and overall brightness of each channel are gone after
  instance normalisation, which helps style transfer and hurts classification when those cues matter.
- **Too few values per set.** On a $1 \times 1$ map, instance normalisation has one value per set and outputs zero.
  Group normalisation needs $(C/G)HW$ well above 1.
- **Divisibility.** Group normalisation needs $C$ divisible by $G$; narrow layers may need fewer groups.
- **Names hide axes.** A layer called LayerNorm may normalise over $(C, H, W)$ or over $C$ at each pixel. The two give
  different networks.
