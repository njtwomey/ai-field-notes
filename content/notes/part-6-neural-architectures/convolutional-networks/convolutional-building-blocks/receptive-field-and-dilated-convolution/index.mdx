<Definition>
The **receptive field** of a unit in a convolutional network is the set of input positions whose values can change the
unit. Along one axis it is an interval of $r$ positions. A **dilated convolution** with dilation $d$ places its $k$ taps
$d$ positions apart: in one dimension, $y_i = \sum_{u=0}^{k-1} w_u\, x_{si - p + du}$ for stride $s$ and padding $p$.
Its kernel spans $d(k - 1) + 1$ input positions and keeps $k$ weights. The **effective receptive field** is the
gradient of the unit with respect to each input position, which shows how strongly each position in the receptive field
actually acts on it.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A unit can only respond to what lies inside its receptive field. A classifier that must recognise a dog needs units
whose fields cover the dog. A segmentation network that labels a pixel as road needs a field wide enough to see the
surrounding scene. A <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink> with a $3 \times 3$ kernel sees
three pixels per side, so networks grow the field by stacking layers. There are three ways to make the growth fast.

- **Depth.** Each stride-1 layer adds $k - 1$ positions. Growth is linear in the number of layers.
- **Downsampling.** A stride or a pooling layer spaces the next layer's units further apart in the input, so every
  later layer adds more input positions; see <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">pooling and strides</NoteLink>. Growth
  is geometric, and the feature map loses resolution.
- **Dilation.** A dilated kernel skips $d - 1$ positions between taps, so one layer reaches further without more
  weights. With dilation doubling per layer the growth is geometric and the feature map keeps full resolution.

The theoretical field says which inputs can matter. It does not say how much. An input at the edge of the field reaches
the unit through one chain of connections; an input at the centre reaches it through many. The effective field is
therefore concentrated in the middle of the theoretical one.

## Details

### The recursion

Work along one axis, with input positions indexed by integers. Layer $l$ has kernel size $k_l$, stride $s_l$, dilation
$d_l$ and padding $p_l$. Its dilated extent is $e_l = d_l(k_l - 1) + 1$. Track three numbers per layer:

- the **jump** $j_l$, the distance in input positions between two adjacent units of layer $l$;
- the **size** $r_l$, the number of input positions one unit can see;
- the **start** $c_l$, the input coordinate of the centre of the first unit's field.

The input has $j_0 = 1$, $r_0 = 1$ and $c_0 = 0$. Adjacent units of layer $l$ are $s_l$ units of layer $l - 1$ apart,
and those are $j_{l-1}$ positions apart, so

$$
j_l = j_{l-1}\, s_l = \prod_{m=1}^{l} s_m.
$$

A unit of layer $l$ reads $k_l$ units of layer $l - 1$ spread over $e_l$ consecutive units. Their centres span
$(e_l - 1)\, j_{l-1}$ input positions, and each of them sees $r_{l-1}$ positions around its own centre. The union has

$$
r_l = r_{l-1} + (e_l - 1)\, j_{l-1}, \qquad r_L = 1 + \sum_{l=1}^{L} (e_l - 1) \prod_{m=1}^{l-1} s_m.
$$

The first unit of layer $l$ reads its window starting $p_l$ units of layer $l - 1$ before the first one, so its centre
sits $(e_l - 1)/2 - p_l$ units of layer $l - 1$ after it, $c_l = c_{l-1} + \paren{(e_l - 1)/2 - p_l}\, j_{l-1}$
<Cite id="araujo2019" />. With same padding, $p_l = (e_l - 1)/2$, the centres line up: unit $i$ of every layer is
centred on input position $i\, j_l$. Pooling layers enter the recursion as kernels with $d = 1$. In two dimensions the
recursion runs separately along each axis.

The closed form shows where the field comes from. Each layer adds its extent minus one, scaled by the product of all
strides before it. A layer placed after three stride-2 stages counts eight times as much as the same layer at the
input.

### Dilated convolution

Dilation inserts $d - 1$ gaps between the taps of a kernel. In two dimensions a $3 \times 3$ kernel with $d = 2$
reads a $5 \times 5$ region at its nine corners, edge midpoints and centre. The layer has $k^2 C C'$ weights and costs
$k^2 C C'$ multiply-accumulates per output position, the same as the undilated layer. The output size follows the usual
formula with $k$ replaced by $e = d(k - 1) + 1$ <Cite id="dumoulin2016" />. Dilation is also called atrous convolution,
from the French à trous, "with holes", the name of a wavelet algorithm that upsamples its filters the same way.

Yu and Koltun stacked $3 \times 3$ layers with dilations $1, 2, 4, 8, \dots$ as a context module for
<NoteLink to="semantic-segmentation">semantic segmentation</NoteLink> <Cite id="yu2016" />. With dilation
$d_l = 2^{l-1}$ and stride 1 the recursion gives $r_L = 1 + \sum_{l=1}^{L} 2 \cdot 2^{l-1} = 2^{L+1} - 1$. The field
grows exponentially with depth and the number of weights grows linearly.
<NoteLink to="wavenet-and-causal-convolution">WaveNet</NoteLink> uses the same schedule with causal kernels on audio.

### Dilation against stride

A stride and a dilation can produce the same receptive field. They differ in what they do to the feature map.

- **Stride** spaces the outputs. Four stride-2 layers give a field of 31 positions per side, and the map shrinks by
  $2^4 = 16$ per side. Every later layer is $256$ times cheaper, and spatial detail is lost.
- **Dilation** spaces the taps. Four layers with dilations $1, 2, 4, 8$ also give 31 positions per side, and the map
  keeps its size. Every layer runs at full resolution and costs as much as the first.

Dense prediction needs both context and resolution. DeepLab removes the strides of a classification backbone's last
one or two stages and dilates the later layers by the factor the strides would have contributed, so the receptive field
is unchanged and the output stride falls from 32 to 16 or 8 <Cite id="chen2018deeplab" />. Its atrous spatial pyramid
runs several dilation rates in parallel; see <NoteLink to="segmentation-decoders">segmentation decoders</NoteLink>.

### Effective receptive field

Luo et al. measured how much each input position affects a unit by the gradient $g_q = \partial y / \partial x_q$
<Cite id="luo2016" />. For a stack of linear layers the gradient does not depend on the input. Take $L$ stride-1
layers whose $k$ taps all have weight $1/k$. Input position $q$ reaches the output through every chain of taps whose
offsets $a_1 + \dots + a_L$ add up to the distance from $q$ to the centre, each chain with weight $k^{-L}$. The
gradient is therefore the distribution of a sum of $L$ independent offsets, each uniform on
$\set{-(k-1)/2, \dots, (k-1)/2}$.

One offset has variance $(k^2 - 1)/12$, so the sum has standard deviation

$$
\sigma_L = \sqrt{\frac{L(k^2 - 1)}{12}}.
$$

By the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> the gradient approaches a Gaussian of
that width. The theoretical field grows as $L(k - 1) + 1$ and the effective one as $\sqrt{L}$, so the fraction of the
field that carries most of the influence shrinks as $1/\sqrt{L}$. Luo et al. found the same Gaussian shape with random
weights and nonlinearities, and found that training, subsampling and dilation enlarge it <Cite id="luo2016" />.

Dilation changes the picture because its offsets at layer $l$ are multiples of $d_l$. With dilations $1, 2, 4, 8$ the
offset sum is a number written in base 2 with digits in $\set{-1, 0, 1}$, and the gradient spreads across the whole
field instead of piling up in the centre.

### Practice

Classification backbones such as ResNet-50 reach theoretical fields wider than their input images by their last stage,
so the theoretical field rarely limits them. The effective field does. Modern convolutional networks widen it with
larger depthwise kernels: <NoteLink to="modern-convolutional-networks">ConvNeXt</NoteLink> uses $7 \times 7$, and
RepLKNet $31 \times 31$, which gave much larger effective fields than stacks of $3 \times 3$ layers
<Cite id="ding2022replknet" />. Dilation is used where resolution must be kept: segmentation decoders, audio and
time-series models. <Gloss name="vit">Vision transformers</Gloss> have a global field from the first layer; see
<NoteLink to="convolution-versus-attention-in-vision">convolution versus attention in vision</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

**VGG-16.** <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">VGG-16</NoteLink> has five stages of
$3 \times 3$ stride-1 convolutions (2, 2, 3, 3 and 3 layers), each followed by a $2 \times 2$ stride-2 max pool
<Cite id="simonyan2015" />. Each convolution adds $(3 - 1)\, j = 2j$, and each pool adds $(2 - 1)\, j = j$ and doubles
the jump.

1. Stage 1, jump 1: two convolutions give $r = 1 + 2 + 2 = 5$. The pool adds 1, so $r = 6$ and $j = 2$.
2. Stage 2, jump 2: two convolutions add $4$ each, $r = 14$. The pool adds 2: $r = 16$, $j = 4$.
3. Stage 3, jump 4: three convolutions add $8$ each, $r = 40$. The pool adds 4: $r = 44$, $j = 8$.
4. Stage 4, jump 8: three convolutions add $16$ each, $r = 92$. The pool adds 8: $r = 100$, $j = 16$.
5. Stage 5, jump 16: three convolutions add $32$ each, $r = 196$. The pool adds 16: $r = 212$, $j = 32$.

The last convolution sees a $196 \times 196$ region of the $224 \times 224$ input. Thirteen stride-1 convolutions
without pooling would see $1 + 13 \cdot 2 = 27$ pixels per side.

**Dilation against stride.** Take four layers of 3-tap kernels in one dimension.

- Plain: $e_l = 3$ and $j = 1$ throughout, so $r = 1 + 4 \cdot 2 = 9$.
- Stride 2 in every layer: the jumps before the layers are $1, 2, 4, 8$, so $r = 1 + 2(1 + 2 + 4 + 8) = 31$. The output
  has one unit per 16 inputs.
- Dilations $1, 2, 4, 8$: the extents are $3, 5, 9, 17$ and the jump stays 1, so $r = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 = 31$. The
  output has one unit per input.

Both use 12 weights per channel pair.

**Effective field.** With uniform weights $1/3$, the plain stack's gradient is the coefficient list of
$(1 + z + z^2)^4 / 81$:

$$
g = \tfrac{1}{81}\,(1,\ 4,\ 10,\ 16,\ 19,\ 16,\ 10,\ 4,\ 1).
$$

The centre position carries $19/81 = 23\%$ of the total and the two edge positions together carry $2/81 = 2.5\%$. The
standard deviation is $\sqrt{4 \cdot 8/12} = 1.63$ positions, so $\pm 2\sigma$ covers 6.5 of the 9 positions. The
dilated stack with rates $1, 2, 4, 8$ has gradient coefficients between $1/81$ and $5/81$ over all 31 positions and a
standard deviation of $7.5$, so $\pm 2\sigma$ covers 30 of them.

## Assumptions

- The network is a chain. For branched networks, such as <NoteLink to="inception-networks">Inception modules</NoteLink>
  or <NoteLink to="residual-network">residual blocks</NoteLink>, the receptive field is the largest over all paths from
  input to unit <Cite id="araujo2019" />.
- Padded positions count as positions. Units near the border have part of their field outside the image, filled with
  zeros.
- The effective-field argument assumes independent, identically distributed offsets per layer. Trained weights are
  neither uniform nor independent, and the measured field of a trained network is wider and less regular.

## Pitfalls

- **Gridding.** Stacking layers with the same dilation $d > 1$ reaches only every $d$-th input position: with dilation 2
  in every layer, half of the field is never read. Choosing rates without a common factor, such as $1, 2, 3$, fills the
  gaps <Cite id="wang2018hdc" />.
- **Rates close to the map size.** A dilated tap that falls outside the feature map reads padding. When the rate
  approaches the size of the map, a $3 \times 3$ kernel has only its centre tap inside at most positions and acts as a
  $1 \times 1$ layer <Cite id="chen2017deeplab" />.
- **Theoretical versus effective field.** A theoretical field larger than the object does not mean the unit uses the
  whole object; the effective field may be a small fraction of it.
- **Misaligned centres.** Even kernel sizes and same padding with a stride put the field's centre half a position away
  from the unit's nominal position. The start recursion gives the exact offset.
