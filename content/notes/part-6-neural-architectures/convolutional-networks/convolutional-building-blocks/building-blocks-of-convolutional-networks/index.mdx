<Definition>
The building blocks of convolutional networks are the layers from which image and signal networks are assembled. The
core is the convolutional layer, a bank of small kernels shared across the positions of a grid. The other blocks vary
one part of it: the kernel size, the channels each kernel reads, the spacing of its taps or outputs, the direction of
the map between sizes, or the statistics used to normalise its outputs. Each block is defined by its output shape, its
parameter count and its cost in multiply-accumulates.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every convolutional architecture, from <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">LeNet</NoteLink> to
ConvNeXt, and every dense-prediction model, from <Gloss name="u-net" /> to a feature pyramid detector, is a stack of a
dozen kinds of layer. A designer chooses among them by their effect on three things: the shape of the feature map, the
receptive field, and the cost. These notes give each block's definition, its shape and cost arithmetic, and a worked
example with small numbers, so that a whole network can be read layer by layer and its sizes and costs checked by hand
<Cite id="dumoulin2016" />.

## Main ideas

**One layer, three cost factors.** A <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer" /> with a $k \times k$ kernel from $C$ to $C'$
channels on an $H' \times W'$ output costs $k^2 C C' H' W'$ multiply-accumulates and holds $k^2 C C'$ weights. Its
output width is $\lfloor (n + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1$ for input width $n$, padding $p$ and stride $s$. Its backward pass
is a convolution with the flipped kernel, and its forward pass is equivariant to translation
<Cite id="goodfellow2016" />.

**Cutting one factor at a time.** Efficient blocks each reduce one factor of the cost. A
<NoteLink to="one-by-one-convolution" /> sets $k = 1$ and mixes channels at every pixel; it is the bottleneck of
Inception and ResNet. <NoteLink to="depthwise-and-grouped-convolution" /> splits the channels into $g$ groups and
divides $C C'$ by $g$; depthwise convolution followed by a $1 \times 1$ layer is the separable block of MobileNet. A
stride or a pooling layer divides $H' W'$ by $s^2$; see <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides" />.

**Seeing further.** A unit can respond only to the input positions in its receptive field. Depth grows the field
linearly, strides and pooling geometrically, and dilation geometrically without losing resolution; the effective field
is narrower and roughly Gaussian. See <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution" />.
<NoteLink to="deformable-convolution" /> goes one step further and learns, at every position, where each tap reads.

**Changing resolution.** Pooling and strides downsample, and without a low-pass filter they alias, which breaks the
shift invariance a classifier relies on. Global average pooling reduces a map to one vector per image. A
<NoteLink to="transposed-convolution" /> upsamples by the transpose of a convolution's matrix; its uneven overlaps cause
checkerboard artefacts, which resize-then-convolve avoids.

**Normalising activations.** <NoteLink to="normalisation-in-convolutional-networks" /> compares batch, layer, instance
and group normalisation. All four standardise the activations of an $[N, C, H, W]$ tensor and differ only in which axes
share one mean and variance. Batch normalisation depends on the batch size; group normalisation does not.

**Computing it fast.** The same output can be computed several ways. im2col turns the layer into one matrix product,
FFT convolution makes the cost almost independent of kernel size, and Winograd's minimal filtering cuts the
multiplications of a $3 \times 3$ layer by 2.25 to 4 times. See <NoteLink to="fast-convolution-algorithms" />.
