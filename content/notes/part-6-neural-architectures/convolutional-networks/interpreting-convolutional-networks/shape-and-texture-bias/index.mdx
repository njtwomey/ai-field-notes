<Definition>
The shape bias of an image classifier is the share of its decisions that follow an object's shape when shape and
texture point to different classes. It is measured on cue-conflict images, which carry the outline of one class and
the surface texture of another, as the number of shape-class answers divided by the number of shape-class plus
texture-class answers. A shape bias near 1 means the classifier recognises objects by their outline; near 0 means it
recognises them by their texture.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A cat is recognisable from its silhouette and from a patch of its fur. In natural photographs the two cues agree, so
accuracy on ordinary test images cannot reveal which one a network uses. A cue-conflict image separates them: the shape
of a cat filled with the skin texture of an elephant. People shown such images answer "cat". ImageNet-trained
<NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink> answer "elephant" <Cite id="geirhos2019" />.

Texture is the easier cue for a convolutional network. A texture is a local statistic: it is present in every small
patch of the object, so a few layers, each with a small <Gloss name="receptive-field" />, detect it anywhere, and
<NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">global average pooling</NoteLink> collects the votes. A shape is a long-range
arrangement of edges that only a deep unit with a <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive
field</NoteLink> the size of the object can see. When both cues predict the label equally well,
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> finds the easy one first and has no reason to learn the
other. Texture bias is a case of <NoteLink to="subpopulation-shift-and-shortcuts">shortcut learning</NoteLink>: a
solution that is right on the training distribution for a reason that fails outside it
<Cite id="geirhos2020shortcut" />.

## Details

**The cue-conflict test.** Geirhos and colleagues made 1,280 cue-conflict images, 80 for each of 16 classes, by
transferring the texture of one image onto the shape of another with neural style transfer. They mapped the 1,000
<Gloss name="imagenet" /> classes onto the 16 categories and counted, among answers that named either the shape class or
the texture class, the share that named the shape <Cite id="geirhos2019" />. Human observers chose the shape in 95.9% of
these decisions. <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">ResNet-50</NoteLink> chose it in 22.1%,
VGG-16 in 17.2%, <NoteLink to="inception-networks">GoogLeNet</NoteLink> in 31.2% and AlexNet in 42.9%
<Cite id="geirhos2019" />.

**Local features suffice on ImageNet.** BagNets classify each $33 \times 33$ patch of an image independently and
average the patch scores, ignoring all spatial arrangement beyond the patch. They reach 87.6% top-5 accuracy on
ImageNet, and $17 \times 17$ patches already match AlexNet <Cite id="brendel2019" />. So high ImageNet accuracy does not
require shape.

**Stylized-ImageNet.** Restyling every ImageNet training image with the texture of a randomly chosen painting, by
adaptive instance normalisation <Cite id="huang2017adain" />, removes texture as a reliable cue while keeping shape.
ResNet-50 trained on this set reached a shape bias of 81%, against 22% for the same architecture trained on ImageNet
<Cite id="geirhos2019" />. Training on both sets and fine-tuning on ImageNet gave higher ImageNet accuracy than ImageNet
alone and better transfer to <NoteLink to="object-detection">object detection</NoteLink>, which depends on outlines.

**Where the bias comes from.** Hermann, Chen and Kornblith varied the training of ImageNet networks one factor at a time
<Cite id="hermann2020" />. Data augmentation had the largest effect: less aggressive random cropping and natural
augmentations such as colour distortion, noise and blur raised the shape bias so far that most cue-conflict decisions
followed shape. Architecture and training objective had small effects. Shape information could be decoded from the
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">hidden layers</NoteLink> of texture-biased networks, so the bias lies in what the
classifier relies on, not in what the network represents. Their conclusion is that the difference between people and
networks comes mainly from the data the networks see.

**Scale and architecture.** ViT-22B, a
<NoteLink to="vision-transformer">vision transformer</NoteLink> of 22 billion parameters, reached 87% shape bias,
against about 20 to 30% for most earlier models and 96% for people <Cite id="dehghani2023" />. The comparison of
<NoteLink to="convolution-versus-attention-in-vision">convolution and attention in vision</NoteLink> is confounded by
training data and augmentation, which, by the result above, matter more than the architecture.

texture matches, because those images now reward a cue that the network has learned to ignore.

## Worked example

A two-feature model shows why gradient descent picks the easier cue. A <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic
regression</NoteLink> with labels $y \in \set{-1, +1}$ sees two features: a shape feature $s = 0.5\,y$ and a texture
feature $t = y$. Both predict the label perfectly. The texture feature has twice the magnitude, which stands for being
easier to extract. The model is $\hat y = \operatorname{sign}(w_s s + w_t t)$, trained from $w = (0, 0)$ by gradient
descent on the mean logistic loss $\log(1 + e^{-y(w_s s + w_t t)})$ with step size 1.

**Step 1: the gradient's direction.** The gradient of one example's loss is $-\sigma(-m)\, y\,(s, t)$, with margin
$m = y(w_s s + w_t t)$ and $\sigma$ the logistic function. Every training example has $y(s, t) = (0.5, 1)$, so every
gradient points along $-(0.5, 1)$. The weights stay on the ray $w = a\,(0.5, 1)$ with $a > 0$ growing: after one step
$w = (0.25, 0.5)$, after ten $(1.00, 2.00)$, after a hundred $(1.93, 3.86)$.

**Step 2: a cue-conflict input.** Give the model the shape of class $+1$ and the texture of class $-1$: $s = 0.5$,
$t = -1$. Its score is $a\,(0.5 \cdot 0.5 - 1) = -0.75\,a < 0$ for every $a > 0$. The model answers with the texture's
class at every step of training, so its shape bias is 0.

**Step 3: restyle a quarter of the data.** With $\rho = 0.25$, a quarter of the examples get a texture of random sign,
so one example in eight has $t = -y$. Now the texture feature is wrong on one example in eight and the shape feature
is never wrong. The first step is $w = (0.25, 0.375)$, still texture-led: the conflict score is
$0.5 \cdot 0.25 - 0.375 = -0.25$. After ten steps $w = (1.43, 1.31)$ and the score is $-0.59$. After a hundred steps
$w = (5.25, 1.09)$ and the score is $+1.54$: the model now answers by shape. Gradient descent on separable data turns
towards the direction of largest margin (see <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink>),
and only the shape feature separates every restyled example.

The two cases match the figure. With perfectly correlated cues, the solution keeps the proportions in which the cues
are easy to use, and texture wins. Restyling makes texture unreliable, and the network moves to shape, after a phase
in which it still relies on texture.

## Assumptions

- **A meaningful split into shape and texture.** The test assumes the two cues can be separated, as style transfer
  does approximately. Stylised images also carry artefacts, and a network trained on one style-transfer method can
  learn to read them; Geirhos and colleagues trained with adaptive instance normalisation and tested with iterative
  style transfer to avoid this <Cite id="geirhos2019" />.
- **Decisions that name one of the two classes.** Shape bias ignores answers that name neither class, so a model can
  have a high shape bias and a low accuracy on cue-conflict images.
- **A fixed class mapping.** The 16-category test maps ImageNet's fine classes to coarse categories. The bias of a
  model with another label set depends on that mapping.

## Pitfalls

- **Accuracy does not reveal the cue.** Two networks with the same test accuracy can have shape biases of 0.2 and 0.8.
  Test with cue-conflict, silhouette or edge images before relying on a network under texture change, such as
  sketches, paintings or new imaging devices.
- **Shape bias is not robustness.** It correlates with robustness to some distortions, but it is one number on one
  stimulus set. Report it beside <Gloss name="ood">out-of-distribution</Gloss> accuracy, not instead of it.
- **Maps show where, not what.** A <NoteLink to="class-activation-mapping">class activation map</NoteLink> on the
  object looks correct for a texture-biased network too, since the texture is on the object.
- **More shape is not always better.** Texture is the right cue for materials, fabrics and many medical images.
