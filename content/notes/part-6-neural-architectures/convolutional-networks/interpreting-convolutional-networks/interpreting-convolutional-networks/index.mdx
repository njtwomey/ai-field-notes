<Definition>
Interpreting a convolutional network means finding out what its units detect, where in an image it finds each class,
and which visual cues its decisions rest on. The methods use the structure of the network: feature maps keep a spatial
layout, units have local receptive fields, and the gradient of any unit with respect to the input or a feature map is
one backward pass away.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A trained image classifier is accurate or not on a test set, but accuracy does not show how it decides. Interpretation
finds out whether a medical classifier looks at the lesion or at a ruler beside it, whether a detector's units encode
object parts, and whether a network will survive a change of image style. The methods here are specific to
<NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink>. The general methods of the
<NoteLink to="interpretability">interpretability</NoteLink> chapter, such as
<NoteLink to="feature-attribution-methods">feature attribution</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="concept-based-explanations">concept-based explanations</NoteLink>, apply to them too.

## Main ideas

**Units can be visualised.** A first-layer unit is a dot product with its kernel, so the kernel itself is the image
that excites the unit most; trained first layers show oriented edges and colour blobs. Deeper units are shown by the
dataset patches that activate them most or by activation maximisation, gradient ascent on the input with regularisers
such as jitter and blur. Zeiler and Fergus used top patches to show a hierarchy from edges to textures, parts and
objects across layers <Cite id="zeiler2014" />. Regularisers decide whether a synthetic image looks natural
<Cite id="olah2017" />, and in a human study natural top-activating images explained units better than synthetic ones
<Cite id="borowski2021" />. <NoteLink to="feature-visualisation">Feature visualisation</NoteLink>
derives the first-layer result and works through both methods.

**The last feature maps localise a class.** When global average pooling feeds a linear classifier, the class score is
the mean over positions of a weighted sum of the last feature maps, so that sum is a map of each position's
contribution. Grad-CAM replaces the classifier's weights by spatially averaged gradients and works for any network
<Cite id="selvaraju2017" />. A map must pass the model-randomisation sanity check to describe the model rather than the
input. <NoteLink to="class-activation-mapping">Class activation mapping</NoteLink> derives CAM, shows that Grad-CAM
reduces to it, and covers Grad-CAM++, Score-CAM and HiResCAM.

**Networks can rely on texture.** On cue-conflict images with the shape of one class and the texture of another,
ImageNet-trained networks mostly choose the texture, while people choose the shape <Cite id="geirhos2019" />. The bias
comes mainly from the training data and augmentation, and training on stylised images moves decisions to the shape.
<NoteLink to="shape-and-texture-bias">Shape and texture bias</NoteLink> reproduces the effect on a small network and
explains it with a two-feature model.

## How the notes fit together

Read <NoteLink to="feature-visualisation">feature visualisation</NoteLink> first: it introduces units, receptive fields
and gradient ascent on the input. <NoteLink to="class-activation-mapping">Class activation mapping</NoteLink> builds on
it and on global average pooling from <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">pooling and strides</NoteLink>.
<NoteLink to="shape-and-texture-bias">Shape and texture bias</NoteLink> needs only the convolutional layer, and it
explains why a correct-looking class activation map can still come from a texture-biased network. All three notes use
the same small architecture, trained in the page on images of discs, squares and crosses.
