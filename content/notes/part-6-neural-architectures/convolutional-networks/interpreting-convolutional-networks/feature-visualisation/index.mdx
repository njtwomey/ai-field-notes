<Definition>
Feature visualisation shows what a unit of a trained network responds to. A unit is one value inside the network: a
position of a feature map, a whole channel, or a class score. The first layer is shown directly, since its filters are
images. Deeper units are shown by the inputs from a dataset that activate them most, or by activation maximisation:
gradient ascent on the input image, with regularisers that keep the image natural, to find an input that makes the
unit large.
</Definition>

## Problem

A trained convolutional network stores what it has learned in millions of weights. The weights of one deep unit do not
say what the unit detects, because the unit reads the outputs of earlier units, not pixels. Accuracy on a test set says
how well the network classifies, not which patterns it uses. Feature visualisation answers a narrower question: which
input patterns drive a chosen unit. The answers show the hierarchy that a convolutional network builds, from edges and
colour blobs in the first layer to textures, parts and objects in deeper layers <Cite id="zeiler2014" />.

Feature visualisation describes units. It differs from <NoteLink to="feature-attribution-methods">feature
attribution</NoteLink>, which explains one prediction by scoring the pixels of one input, and from
<NoteLink to="class-activation-mapping">class activation mapping</NoteLink>, which shows where in one input a class was
found.

## Mechanism

### First-layer filters

A first-layer unit computes $w^\top x + b$ on a $k \times k \times C$ patch $x$ of the input, with kernel $w$ of the
same shape (see the <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink>). Among patches of a fixed norm
$\norm{x} = \rho$, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $w^\top x \le \norm{w}\norm{x}$ is an equality only for
$x = \rho\, w / \norm{w}$. So the patch that excites a first-layer unit most is its kernel, rescaled, and drawing the
kernel as a small image is an exact visualisation of the unit <Cite id="erhan2009" />.
<NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">AlexNet</NoteLink>'s 96 first-layer kernels of
$11 \times 11 \times 3$ were oriented edges at several frequencies, Gabor-like patterns and colour blobs
<Cite id="krizhevsky2012" />. Most image networks trained on natural images learn similar first layers.

The argument stops at the first layer. A second-layer unit is a nonlinear function of the input, through
<Gloss name="relu" plural /> and pooling, so no single kernel describes it.

### Top-activating patches

The second method searches a dataset. Run the network on many images, record a channel's values at every position, and
keep the images where the channel is largest. A unit at one position of a feature map sees only its
<Gloss name="receptive-field" />, so the useful picture is the patch of the input inside that field. Zeiler and Fergus
collected the top nine patches for units in each layer of an <Gloss name="imagenet" /> network: second-layer units
responded to corners and colour conjunctions, third-layer units to textures and repeated patterns, and fourth- and
fifth-layer units to object parts and whole objects, such as dog faces and wheels <Cite id="zeiler2014" />. Network
dissection makes the search quantitative: it scores how well each unit's high-activation region overlaps human labels
for objects, parts, scenes, materials, textures and colours <Cite id="bau2017" />.

Top-activating patches show real inputs, so they never invent a pattern. They show only what the dataset contains, and
the top patches of one unit often share several features (a colour, a texture and an object at once) without saying
which one the unit uses.

### Activation maximisation

The third method synthesises the input. Let $f(x)$ be the value of the chosen unit as a function of the input image
$x \in \reals^{C \times H \times W}$. Activation maximisation solves

$$
x^\star = \argmax_{x} \; f(x) - \lambda\, R(x)
$$

by gradient ascent from a random or grey image <Cite id="erhan2009" />. The regulariser $R$ keeps $x$ image-like, and
$\lambda \ge 0$ sets its weight. Each step computes $\nabla_x f$ by backpropagation, with the weights held fixed, and
moves the image along it:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t + \eta \left( \nabla_x f(x_t) - \lambda\, \nabla_x R(x_t) \right),
$$

with step size $\eta > 0$. Many implementations divide the step by the root mean square of the gradient, so that
$\eta$ is in units of pixel values whatever the scale of the unit. Simonyan, Vedaldi and Zisserman applied the method to
the class scores of an ImageNet network, before the softmax, with $R(x) = \norm{x}^2$ <Cite id="simonyan2014" />. The
score before the softmax is used because the softmax probability can rise by lowering the other classes' scores.

Without a regulariser, the ascent produces high-frequency noise that excites the unit strongly and looks like nothing.
These images are close relatives of <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples">adversarial examples</NoteLink>: small,
unnatural changes to which the network is sensitive <Cite id="olah2017" />. The regularisers in common use each remove
one kind of artefact:

- **L2 decay**, $R(x) = \norm{x}^2$, keeps pixel values small, so only patterns that pay for their energy survive
  <Cite id="simonyan2014" />.
- **Frequency penalties** suppress noise between neighbouring pixels: a total-variation term
  $\sum_{i,j} \abs{x_{i+1,j} - x_{i,j}} + \abs{x_{i,j+1} - x_{i,j}}$ <Cite id="mahendran2015" />, or a Gaussian blur of
  $x$ every few steps <Cite id="yosinski2015" />.
- **Transformation robustness**, or jitter, shifts, rotates or rescales the image at random before each gradient
  step. A pattern that excites the unit only at one exact pixel offset does not survive <Cite id="olah2017" />.
- **Learned priors** search the latent space of a generative model instead of pixel space, which gives photographic
  images at the price of mixing the prior's preferences into the picture <Cite id="olah2017" />.

The unit can be a single position of a channel (a neuron), the mean of a channel over all positions, or a class score. A
neuron's image fills only its <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive field</NoteLink>. A
channel or class score that is averaged over positions rewards its pattern everywhere at once, so its image repeats a
motif across the frame. DeepDream applies the same ascent to a whole layer of a photograph's activations, amplifying
whatever patterns the layer already sees in the photograph <Cite id="mordvintsev2015" />.

A unit can respond to several distinct patterns. One ascent finds one of them. Olah, Mordvintsev and Schubert add a
diversity term that pushes a batch of images apart, measured by the Gram matrices of their activations, to show several
facets of one unit <Cite id="olah2017" />.

## Worked example

**A linear unit by gradient ascent.** Take a first-layer unit with the $3 \times 3$ vertical-edge kernel and no bias:

$$
w = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad f(x) = \sum_{u,v} w_{uv}\, x_{uv}.
$$

With L2 decay of weight $\lambda = 1$, the objective is $f(x) - \norm{x}^2$, with gradient $w - 2x$. Plain ascent with
step $\eta = 0.25$ from $x_0 = 0$ gives

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t + 0.25\,(w - 2 x_t) = 0.5\, x_t + 0.25\, w.
$$

The iterates are $x_1 = 0.25\, w$, $x_2 = 0.375\, w$ and $x_3 = 0.4375\, w$; in general
$x_t = 0.5\,(1 - 0.5^t)\, w$. Every iterate is a multiple of $w$, and the limit is $x^\star = w / (2\lambda) = 0.5\, w$:
a dark left column at $-0.5$, a zero middle column and a bright right column at $0.5$. At $x^\star$ the unit's value
is $0.5 \norm{w}^2 = 0.5 \cdot 6 = 3$ and the penalty is $\norm{x^\star}^2 = 0.25 \cdot 6 = 1.5$. The visualisation is
the kernel, as the Cauchy–Schwarz argument predicts.

**Top-activating patch.** Slide the same kernel, without padding, over the $5 \times 5$ image

$$
x = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Each output is the sum of the window's right column minus the sum of its left column. The nine outputs are

$$
y = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 2 & -2 \\ 2 & 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

For example, the window at row 1, column 0 covers rows 1 to 3 and columns 0 to 2. Its left column is $(0, 0, 0)$ and
its right column is $(1, 1, 1)$, so $y_{1,0} = 3 - 0 = 3$, the largest value. The top-activating patch is that window,

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

dark on the left and bright on the right, like $w$. Its cosine similarity with $w$ is
$w^\top x / (\norm{w}\norm{x}) = 3 / (\sqrt{6} \cdot 2) \approx 0.61$. The patch also contains a bright centre pixel
that the kernel ignores, since $w$ is zero there. A reader of the patch alone cannot tell whether the unit cares about
that pixel. The synthesised image, $0.5\, w$, answers it: the unit ignores the middle column.

## Cost

One ascent step costs one forward and one backward pass of the network up to the unit, about three
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">forward passes</NoteLink>. A visualisation takes hundreds to a few thousand steps.
A set of visualisations for every channel of a large network therefore costs as much as running the network on a few
million images. Top-activating patches need one forward pass over the dataset, with each unit's values recorded, and a
sort per unit.

## Trade-offs

- **Synthetic against natural.** Activation maximisation can isolate a single pattern that the dataset never shows
  alone. Top-activating patches show only real inputs. In a psychophysics study, people predicted which of two images
  activates a unit more often when shown natural top-activating images (92% correct) than when shown synthetic
  visualisations (82%), against 50% by chance <Cite id="borowski2021" />.
- **Regularisation strength.** Strong priors give recognisable, photograph-like images that may show the prior more
  than the unit. Weak priors give faithful but noisy images.
- **Neuron, channel or class.** A neuron shows a local pattern in one receptive field. A channel or class shows the
  same pattern tiled, because of weight sharing and pooling.

## Pitfalls

- **Visualisations can mislead.** Geirhos and colleagues built networks whose feature visualisations show arbitrary
  chosen patterns while their behaviour on natural images is unchanged. They also found that, in ordinary networks,
  synthetic visualisations are processed very differently from natural images, and they prove that feature
  visualisation can reliably describe only a narrow class of functions <Cite id="geirhos2024" />.
- **One image per unit hides polysemantic units.** A unit that responds to several unrelated patterns appears to
  detect only the one that the ascent reached. Show several restarts, a diversity term, or top patches beside the
  synthetic image.
- **Units may not be the right description.** Directions in activation space can be more interpretable than single
  units; see <NoteLink to="concept-based-explanations">concept-based explanations</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="sparse-autoencoders-for-interpretability">sparse autoencoders for interpretability</NoteLink>.
- **Maximise the score, not the probability.** Ascent on the softmax probability can succeed by suppressing the other
  classes, which shows what the class is not.
- **Clip to the input range.** Pixel values outside the range the network was trained on can excite a unit through
  patterns that never occur in data.
