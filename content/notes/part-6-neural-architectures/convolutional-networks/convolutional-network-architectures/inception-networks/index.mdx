<Definition>
An Inception module applies four branches to the same input and concatenates their outputs along the channel axis: a
$1 \times 1$ convolution, a $1 \times 1$ reduction followed by a $3 \times 3$ convolution, a $1 \times 1$ reduction
followed by a $5 \times 5$ convolution, and a $3 \times 3$ max pool followed by a $1 \times 1$ projection. Every branch
keeps the spatial size, so the outputs line up. An Inception network (GoogLeNet and its successors) is a stack of such
modules between ordinary convolution and pooling stages <Cite id="szegedy2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A plain convolutional stage applies one kernel size. The right size depends on the feature: a texture fits in a
$3 \times 3$ window, a part of an object may need $5 \times 5$ or more, and the same object appears at different sizes
in different images. An Inception module does not choose. It runs several kernel sizes side by side and lets the next
layer weigh their outputs.

The obvious version of this idea is expensive. A $5 \times 5$ convolution that reads all $C$ input channels costs 25
times $C$ multiply-adds per output value, and concatenating a pooling branch passes all $C$ input channels on, so the
width of the representation grows from module to module. GoogLeNet fixes both with
<NoteLink to="one-by-one-convolution">one-by-one convolutions</NoteLink>, the channel-mixing layers of Network in
Network <Cite id="lin2014nin" />. A $1 \times 1$ layer first shrinks the channels that the $3 \times 3$ and $5 \times 5$
convolutions read, and another $1 \times 1$ layer projects the pooled channels down. The large kernels then work on a
narrow input.

The Inception line keeps the stage-by-stage layout of <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">AlexNet
and VGG</NoteLink>, with channels growing as resolution falls. It changes the unit of design from a layer to a
multi-branch block. Residual networks changed the same unit in a different way, by adding a shortcut around it, and
later Inception versions adopted the shortcut too.

## Details

### Cost of a module

Let the input have $C$ channels on an $H \times W$ grid. Write $c_1$ for the filters of the $1 \times 1$ branch, $r_3$
and $c_3$ for the reduction and output widths of the $3 \times 3$ branch, $r_5$ and $c_5$ for those of the $5 \times 5$
branch, and $c_p$ for the pool projection. Every convolution uses stride 1 and padding that keeps the grid, so each
costs (kernel taps) $\times$ (input channels) $\times$ (output channels) multiply-adds per pixel. The module costs

$$
HW \left( C c_1 + C r_3 + 9 r_3 c_3 + C r_5 + 25 r_5 c_5 + C c_p \right)
$$

multiply-adds and outputs $c_1 + c_3 + c_5 + c_p$ channels. The naive module without reductions costs

$$
HW \left( C c_1 + 9 C c_3 + 25 C c_5 \right)
$$

and outputs $c_1 + c_3 + c_5 + C$ channels, because the pool branch passes all $C$ channels on. The terms $9 C c_3$ and
$25 C c_5$ become $C r_3 + 9 r_3 c_3$ and $C r_5 + 25 r_5 c_5$. With $r_3$ and $r_5$ well below $C$, the large kernels
cost a fraction of what they did.

### GoogLeNet

GoogLeNet (Inception v1) won the ILSVRC 2014 classification task with a top-5 error of 6.67% <Cite id="szegedy2015" />.
It is 22 weight layers deep and has nine Inception modules in three groups, at $28 \times 28$, $14 \times 14$ and
$7 \times 7$. A stem of ordinary convolutions and max pools reduces the $224 \times 224$ input to $28 \times 28$ with
192 channels first. The network ends in <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">global average pooling</NoteLink> and a
single dense layer, which removes the large dense layers of AlexNet. It was designed to a budget of 1.5 billion
multiply-adds per image <Cite id="szegedy2015" />. Counted from its layer table without the auxiliary classifiers, it
has about 7 million parameters against AlexNet's 61 million.

Two auxiliary classifiers sit on the outputs of modules 4a and 4d during training. Each is a small pooling, convolution
and dense head whose loss is added to the main loss with weight 0.3, to push gradient into the middle of the network.
They are removed at test time. Szegedy et al. later found that they do not speed up early training and act mainly as a
regulariser <Cite id="szegedy2016" />.

### Factorised convolutions (Inception v2 and v3)

The second paper on the design adds <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> and replaces large
kernels by stacks of smaller ones <Cite id="szegedy2016" />.

**Two $3 \times 3$ for one $5 \times 5$.** Two stacked $3 \times 3$ convolutions with stride 1 have a
<NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive field</NoteLink> of $1 + 2 + 2 = 5$ pixels, the same as
one $5 \times 5$ convolution. With $C$ channels in and out, the stack has $2 \cdot 9 C^2 = 18 C^2$ weights and the
single layer $25 C^2$. The stack saves 28% of the weights and multiply-adds and adds a nonlinearity between the two
layers.

**Asymmetric factorisation.** A $1 \times n$ convolution followed by an $n \times 1$ convolution also covers an
$n \times n$ window, with $2n C^2$ weights instead of $n^2 C^2$. For $n = 7$ this is 14 against 49, a saving of 71%.
Inception v3 uses $1 \times 7$ and $7 \times 1$ pairs on its $17 \times 17$ grid. The pair is a constrained
$7 \times 7$ filter, and Szegedy et al. found it works well on grids of 12 to 20 pixels and poorly in early layers.

**Grid reduction.** Pooling before a widening layer squeezes the representation through a narrow point; widening
before pooling costs more. Inception v3 runs a stride-2 convolution branch and a stride-2 pooling branch in parallel and
concatenates them, which halves the grid and widens the channels in one cheap step.

The same paper introduced <NoteLink to="label-smoothing">label smoothing</NoteLink> as a regulariser for the
classifier.

### Residual Inception and Xception

Inception-v4 and Inception-ResNet add <NoteLink to="residual-network">residual shortcuts</NoteLink> around Inception
modules; the shortcuts mainly speed up training <Cite id="szegedy2017inceptionv4" />. Xception pushes the
reduction idea to its limit: one $1 \times 1$ convolution maps the channels, and then a separate $3 \times 3$ filter
runs on every single channel. That is a <NoteLink to="depthwise-and-grouped-convolution">depthwise separable
convolution</NoteLink>, which Chollet describes as an "extreme" Inception module <Cite id="chollet2017xception" />.
Depthwise separable convolutions became the basis of
<NoteLink to="efficient-convolutional-networks">efficient convolutional networks</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Take GoogLeNet's module 3a <Cite id="szegedy2015" />. The input is $28 \times 28$ with $C = 192$ channels, so
$HW = 784$. The widths are $c_1 = 64$, $r_3 = 96$, $c_3 = 128$, $r_5 = 16$, $c_5 = 32$ and $c_p = 32$. Count
multiply-adds branch by branch, ignoring biases.

1. **$1 \times 1$ branch.** $192 \cdot 64 = 12{,}288$ weights, applied at 784 pixels: $9.6$ million.
2. **$3 \times 3$ branch.** The reduction has $192 \cdot 96 = 18{,}432$ weights. The $3 \times 3$ layer reads 96
   channels: $9 \cdot 96 \cdot 128 = 110{,}592$ weights. Together $129{,}024$ weights and $101.2$ million
   multiply-adds.
3. **$5 \times 5$ branch.** The reduction has $192 \cdot 16 = 3{,}072$ weights and the $5 \times 5$ layer
   $25 \cdot 16 \cdot 32 = 12{,}800$. Together $15{,}872$ weights and $12.4$ million multiply-adds.
4. **Pool branch.** The $3 \times 3$ max pool has no weights; the projection has $192 \cdot 32 = 6{,}144$ weights and
   $4.8$ million multiply-adds.

The module has $163{,}328$ weights, costs $128.0$ million multiply-adds and outputs $64 + 128 + 32 + 32 = 256$
channels at $28 \times 28$.

Without the reductions, the $3 \times 3$ layer reads all 192 channels: $9 \cdot 192 \cdot 128 = 221{,}184$ weights and
$173.4$ million multiply-adds. The $5 \times 5$ layer has $25 \cdot 192 \cdot 32 = 153{,}600$ weights and
$120.4$ million multiply-adds. With the $1 \times 1$ branch the naive module costs $303.5$ million, 2.4 times as much,
and outputs $64 + 128 + 32 + 192 = 416$ channels. The next module would then read 416 channels instead of 256, and the
gap would widen at every module. Summed over the nine modules of GoogLeNet, each fed its real input, the modules cost
$1.10$ billion multiply-adds with reductions and $3.88$ billion without.

## Assumptions

- Features at several scales are useful at the same depth, so parallel kernel sizes add information that one size
  would miss.
- The input channels are redundant enough that a $1 \times 1$ layer can compress them before a spatial filter without
  losing what the filter needs. Szegedy et al. state this as the principle that spatial aggregation can be done over
  lower-dimensional embeddings <Cite id="szegedy2016" />.
- Branch widths are set by hand, module by module. The design has many such numbers and no rule for choosing them.

## Pitfalls

- **Multiply-adds are not latency.** Four narrow branches run as many small kernels in sequence or compete for the
  device, so an Inception module is slower than its multiply-add count suggests. ShuffleNet V2 measures this: splitting
  a layer into parallel fragments with the same total cost slows it on a <Gloss name="gpu" />
  <Cite id="ma2018shufflenetv2" />.
- **Reductions that are too narrow.** A $1 \times 1$ reduction to very few channels is a bottleneck in the information
  sense too. Squeezing the representation early in the network hurts accuracy <Cite id="szegedy2016" />.
- **Asymmetric pairs in early layers.** $1 \times n$ and $n \times 1$ pairs worked on medium grids and poorly on the
  large grids of early layers <Cite id="szegedy2016" />.
- **Inception in current practice.** New vision backbones are residual, depthwise or attention-based; Inception
  modules are rarely chosen for new work. Inception-v3 survives as the fixed feature extractor of the
  <NoteLink to="frechet-inception-distance">Fréchet Inception distance</NoteLink>, so its quirks shape that metric.
