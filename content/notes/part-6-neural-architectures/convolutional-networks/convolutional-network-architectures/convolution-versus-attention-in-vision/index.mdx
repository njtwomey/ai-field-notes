<Definition>
A convolutional layer and a self-attention layer are two ways to mix information across the positions of an image. A
$k \times k$ convolution computes each output from a fixed $k \times k$ neighbourhood with weights that depend only on
the relative offset. Self-attention over image patches computes each output from all positions with weights that depend
on the content of the query and the keys. The comparison between them is a comparison of
<NoteLink to="inductive-bias">inductive biases</NoteLink>: built-in locality and translation equivariance against
input-dependent, global mixing learned from data <Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolution</NoteLink> assumes two things about images. Nearby pixels matter most
(locality), and a pattern means the same wherever it appears (translation equivariance through shared weights). Both are
nearly true for natural images, so a convolutional network does not need to learn them, and it learns from fewer
examples. The cost is that a unit sees only its neighbourhood, and the weights cannot adapt to the image: the same
$3 \times 3$ filter applies to a cat's eye and to the sky.

A <NoteLink to="vision-transformer">vision transformer</NoteLink> keeps almost none of this. Every patch can attend to
every other from the first layer, and how much it attends depends on what the two patches contain. Locality has to be
learned from data. With little data the transformer learns it poorly; with enough data it learns it, and its flexibility
pays off <Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />.

The two are closer than they look. A <NoteLink to="depthwise-and-grouped-convolution">depthwise convolution</NoteLink>
mixes each channel across positions with fixed weights; attention mixes all channels across positions with weights
computed from the input. A transformer's <Gloss name="mlp" /> and a $1 \times 1$ convolution are the same operation.
<NoteLink to="modern-convolutional-networks">ConvNeXt</NoteLink> exploits this mapping to build a convolutional network
shaped like a transformer <Cite id="liu2022" />.

## Details

### Two mixing rules

Index positions on the grid by $i$ and $j$, and let $\xvec_j \in \reals^d$ be the features at position $j$.

- **Convolution.** $\yvec_i = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_k(i)} \Wmat_{j - i}\, \xvec_j$, with one $d \times d$ matrix per
  offset $j - i$ in the $k \times k$ neighbourhood $\mathcal{N}_k(i)$. The weights depend on the offset only.
- **Self-attention.** $\yvec_i = \sum_{j} a_{ij}\, \Wmat_V \xvec_j$, with
  $a_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(\xvec_i^\top \Wmat_Q^\top \Wmat_K \xvec_j / \sqrt{d})$ summed over all positions.
  The weights depend on the content and sum to 1 over $j$.

CoAtNet writes the two together as <NoteLink to="relative-position-representations">relative attention</NoteLink>,
$a_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(\xvec_i^\top \xvec_j + w_{i - j})$: the content term is attention, the offset term
$w_{i - j}$ is a convolution kernel, and the layer can lean on either <Cite id="dai2021coatnet" />.

### Attention can express convolution

Cordonnier et al. prove that a multi-head self-attention layer with $N_h$ heads and a relative positional encoding can
express any convolution with a $\sqrt{N_h} \times \sqrt{N_h}$ kernel <Cite id="cordonnier2020" />. Each head attends to
one fixed offset, and the output projection gives each offset its own weights. Nine heads can therefore act as a
$3 \times 3$ convolution. They also find that trained attention layers in early layers often learn such grid-like
patterns. Attention is the more general layer; convolution is a constrained special case that does not have to be
learned.

### What vision transformers learn

Raghu et al. compare the representations of ViTs and <Gloss name="resnet" plural /> <Cite id="raghu2021vit" />. Even in
the lowest layers, ViT has a mix of heads that attend locally and heads that attend globally, whereas a convolutional
network is local there by construction. Trained with less data, the lower layers of ViT do not learn to attend locally,
and performance suffers; local information in early layers matters. ViT representations are also more uniform across
layers, carried by strong <NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink>. The data story in numbers
is in the <NoteLink to="resnet-vs-vision-transformer">ResNet and ViT case study</NoteLink>: ViT falls behind ResNets
when pretrained on <Gloss name="imagenet" /> alone and overtakes them with hundreds of millions of images
<Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />.

### Cost

On an $n \times n$ grid of $N = n^2$ tokens with $d$ channels, a $k \times k$ convolution from $d$ to $d$ channels costs
$k^2 d^2 N$ multiply-adds. Global self-attention costs $4 d^2 N$ for the query, key, value and output projections plus
$2 N^2 d$ for the scores $\Qmat \Kmat^\top$ and the weighted sum of the values. Attention is cheaper than the
convolution while

$$
4 d^2 N + 2 N^2 d < k^2 d^2 N \iff N < \frac{(k^2 - 4)\, d}{2},
$$

so for $k = 3$, below $N = 2.5 d$ tokens. Above it, the $N^2$ term dominates, and doubling the image side multiplies the
cost by about 16. Convolution cost grows with $N$ only.

<NoteLink to="sliding-window-and-sparse-attention">Windowed attention</NoteLink> restricts each token to a $w \times w$
window, which turns $2N^2 d$ into $2 N w^2 d$, linear in $N$. Swin alternates regular and shifted windows so that
information crosses window borders, and merges patches between stages to build a convolution-like pyramid
<Cite id="liu2021swin" />. It is attention with convolution's locality and cost profile put back.

### Between the two

- **Swin** (windows, shifts, a stage hierarchy) <Cite id="liu2021swin" />.
- **Hybrids.** CoAtNet stacks convolution stages first and attention stages last; among its variants, two convolutional
  stages followed by two transformer stages (C-C-T-T) gave the best balance of generalisation and capacity. Its
  convolutional stages use MBConv blocks <Cite id="dai2021coatnet" />. Convolution generalises better from limited data;
  attention has more capacity for large data.
- **MLP-Mixer** replaces attention by an MLP across the patch axis, with weights that depend on position but not on
  content, and an MLP across channels <Cite id="tolstikhin2021mixer" />; see <NoteLink to="mlp-mixer" />.
- **ConvNeXt** stays fully convolutional and adopts the transformer's block shape and training recipe
  <Cite id="liu2022" />.

## Worked example

Take a $14 \times 14$ grid of tokens, $N = 196$, with $d = 384$ channels: ViT-S/16 on a $224 \times 224$ image, or the
third stage of a hierarchical network. Count multiply-adds per image for one spatial-mixing layer.

1. **$3 \times 3$ convolution.** $9 \cdot 384^2 \cdot 196 = 260.1$ million multiply-adds, with
   $9 \cdot 384^2 = 1{,}327{,}104$ weights.
2. **Global self-attention, projections.** $4 \cdot 384^2 \cdot 196 = 115.6$ million, with $4 \cdot 384^2 = 589{,}824$
   weights.
3. **Global self-attention, scores and weighted sum.** $2 \cdot 196^2 \cdot 384 = 29.5$ million.
4. **Compare.** Attention costs $145.1$ million, 56% of the convolution, and has 44% of its weights, while every output
   sees all 196 tokens instead of 9.

The crossover sits at $N = 2.5 \cdot 384 = 960$ tokens, a grid of about $31 \times 31$. At the first stage of a
hierarchical network the grid is $56 \times 56$ ($N = 3{,}136$) with $d = 96$. There a $3 \times 3$ convolution costs
$260$ million multiply-adds, global attention $115.6 + 2 \cdot 3{,}136^2 \cdot 96 / 10^6 = 2{,}004$ million, and
$7 \times 7$ windowed attention $115.6 + 29.5 = 145.1$ million. Global attention is unaffordable at high resolution,
which is why hierarchical transformers use windows and why convolution remains the default for the first stages.

The receptive field tells the other half. After one layer, the convolution's output sees 3 tokens per side; after
$L$ stride-1 layers it sees $1 + 2L$, so 7 layers are needed to span the $14 \times 14$ grid. Attention spans it in
one.

## Assumptions

- The comparison holds the training recipe, data and compute fixed. Under equal compute at large scale,
  convolutional networks match vision transformers <Cite id="smith2023convnets" />, so many reported gaps measure
  recipe and scale more than architecture.
- The cost model counts multiply-adds of the mixing layer only. Memory for the $N \times N$ attention matrix, and the
  efficiency of attention kernels on a given device, change the picture in practice; see
  <NoteLink to="efficient-attention">efficient attention</NoteLink>.
- Images are natural images on a regular grid. For data whose relevant structure is not local, the locality prior
  helps less.

## Pitfalls

- **Small data.** Trained from scratch on ImageNet-1k, ViT-L/16 was less accurate than ResNets of similar size
  <Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />, and smaller datasets widen the gap. Use a convolutional or hybrid model, or start from
  pretrained weights.
- **High resolution.** Global attention's $N^2$ term makes detection and segmentation inputs expensive. Use windows, a
  hierarchy or convolutional early stages.
- **Equivariance is approximate on both sides.** Strides and pooling break a convolutional network's exact translation
  equivariance. A ViT with <NoteLink to="learned-absolute-position-embeddings">absolute position embeddings</NoteLink>
  is not built to be equivariant to any shift, and its patch grid cuts objects differently after a shift smaller than a
  patch; any equivariance it has is learned. See <NoteLink to="symmetry-and-equivariance">symmetry and
  equivariance</NoteLink>.
- **One axis is not the whole choice.** Deployment matters: convolutions are well supported on phones and embedded
  accelerators, while attention-heavy models depend on attention kernels being fast on the target hardware.
