<Definition>
Modern <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink> are pure convolutional image backbones
redesigned after <Gloss name="vit">vision transformers</Gloss>, chiefly ConvNeXt. A ConvNeXt block applies a
$7 \times 7$ <NoteLink to="depthwise-and-grouped-convolution">depthwise convolution</NoteLink>, layer normalisation, a
$1 \times 1$ convolution that expands the channels four times, a <Gloss name="gelu" /> and a $1 \times 1$ convolution
back, inside a residual connection. The network has a $4 \times 4$ stride-4 patchify stem, four stages separated by
$2 \times 2$ stride-2 downsampling layers, and is trained with a transformer-style recipe <Cite id="liu2022" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

By 2021 vision transformers, and the windowed Swin Transformer in particular, beat ResNets on <Gloss name="imagenet" />
and as backbones for detection and segmentation <Cite id="liu2021swin" />. The comparison mixed two things: a new
architecture, and a new way of training (longer schedules, <Gloss name="adamw" />, heavy augmentation and
regularisation). A <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">ResNet-50</NoteLink> trained with the old
recipe was compared against a transformer trained with the new one.

ConvNeXt separates the two <Cite id="liu2022" />. It starts from ResNet-50, trains it with the transformer recipe, and
then changes the architecture one decision at a time towards a Swin Transformer while staying convolutional, keeping
each change that helps. Most of a transformer's design choices turn out to have convolutional counterparts:

- a patchify stem is a convolution whose stride equals its kernel;
- the per-channel spatial mixing of attention is like a depthwise convolution;
- a transformer's <Gloss name="mlp" />, wide in the middle, is an inverted bottleneck of $1 \times 1$ convolutions;
- a large attention window corresponds to a large kernel.

The result keeps convolution's locality and translation equivariance and its simple implementation, and matched Swin at
the same cost. ConvNeXt keeps the four-stage residual layout of <NoteLink to="residual-network">ResNet</NoteLink> and
the depthwise and inverted-residual blocks of <NoteLink to="efficient-convolutional-networks">MobileNetV2</NoteLink>. It
changes the stem, the stage ratios, the kernel size and the normalisation and activation details.

## Details

### The training recipe

The new recipe trains for 300 epochs instead of 90 with the AdamW optimiser, Mixup, CutMix, RandAugment and random
erasing, stochastic depth and label smoothing. It alone raised ResNet-50's ImageNet top-1 accuracy from 76.1% to 78.8%
<Cite id="liu2022" />. Wightman et al. pushed a plain ResNet-50 to 80.4% with a tuned recipe in timm
<Cite id="wightman2021resnet" />. A large part of the gap between ResNets and transformers in 2020 was training, not
architecture.

### The modernisation steps

The figure steps through the changes in the order of the paper. Each step is a complete network whose cost is counted
layer by layer.

1. **Stage ratio.** ResNet-50 has $(3, 4, 6, 3)$ blocks per stage. Swin-T spends compute in the ratio $1:1:3:1$, so
   ConvNeXt uses $(3, 3, 9, 3)$.
2. **Patchify stem.** ResNet's stem, a $7 \times 7$ stride-2 convolution and a max pool, reduces the image four times.
   A single $4 \times 4$ convolution with stride 4 does the same on non-overlapping patches, as ViT's patch embedding
   does with $16 \times 16$.
3. **Depthwise convolution.** The $3 \times 3$ convolution in each bottleneck becomes depthwise, one filter per channel,
   as in ResNeXt with as many groups as channels. Spatial mixing and <NoteLink to="one-by-one-convolution">channel
   mixing</NoteLink> are then separate operations, as attention and the MLP are in a transformer. The cost halves, and
   widening the network from 64 to 96 channels per block buys capacity back.
4. **Inverted bottleneck.** The block becomes narrow-wide-narrow, $96 \to 384 \to 96$, like a transformer's MLP with
   ratio 4.
5. **Depthwise layer first, then $7 \times 7$.** Moving the depthwise layer to the start of the block puts it on the
   narrow 96 channels, like attention before the MLP, and makes a large kernel cheap. Kernels of 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11 were
   tried; accuracy rose up to $7 \times 7$ and then saturated <Cite id="liu2022" />.
6. **Micro design.** <Gloss name="relu" /> becomes GELU; the block keeps one activation (between the $1 \times 1$
   layers) and one normalisation (after the depthwise layer); <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch
   normalisation</NoteLink> becomes <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink> over the channels
   at each pixel.
7. **Separate downsampling.** Instead of a stride-2 block at the start of each stage, a layer normalisation and a
   $2 \times 2$ stride-2 convolution sit between stages. Training diverged until normalisation was added wherever the
   resolution changes <Cite id="liu2022" />.

The ImageNet accuracy rose from 78.8% to 82.0% over these steps, against 81.3% for Swin-T at similar cost
<Cite id="liu2022" />.

### The ConvNeXt block

For an input $\xvec$ with $C$ channels,

$$
\yvec = \xvec + \lambda \odot \Wmat_2\, \mathrm{GELU}\paren{\Wmat_1\, \mathrm{LN}(\mathrm{DW}_{7 \times 7}(\xvec))},
$$

where $\mathrm{DW}_{7 \times 7}$ is a depthwise convolution, $\mathrm{LN}$ normalises the $C$ values at each pixel,
$\Wmat_1 \in \reals^{4C \times C}$ and $\Wmat_2 \in \reals^{C \times 4C}$ act at every pixel ($1 \times 1$
convolutions), and $\lambda \in \reals^C$ is a learned per-channel scale (layer scale), initialised small. ConvNeXt-T
uses $(3, 3, 9, 3)$ blocks with $C = 96, 192, 384, 768$, and has 28.6 million parameters and 4.5 billion multiply-adds
per $224 \times 224$ image.

### Larger kernels: RepLKNet

RepLKNet goes further, to depthwise kernels of $31 \times 31$ <Cite id="ding2022replknet" />. Large kernels train poorly
on their own, so during training each large kernel has a parallel small-kernel branch; after training the two are merged
into one kernel by adding the small kernel into the centre of the large one, which is exact because convolution is
linear (structural re-parameterisation). Ding et al. report much larger
<NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">effective receptive fields</NoteLink> than small-kernel networks,
a higher <NoteLink to="shape-and-texture-bias">shape bias</NoteLink>, and accuracy matching or exceeding Swin
<Cite id="ding2022replknet" />.

### ConvNeXt V2: masked pretraining

Masked autoencoding, which drives <NoteLink to="masked-image-modelling">masked image modelling</NoteLink> for
transformers, did not transfer directly: a convolution slides over the masked regions. ConvNeXt V2 treats the visible
pixels as a sparse set and uses sparse convolutions during pretraining (a fully convolutional masked autoencoder, FCMAE)
<Cite id="woo2023convnextv2" />. It also adds global response normalisation (GRN), which scales each channel by its
spatial $L_2$ norm divided by the mean of that norm over all channels, to counter channels that collapse to similar
features during masked pretraining. The family spans 3.7 million parameters (Atto) to 650 million (Huge), which reached
88.9% top-1 on ImageNet with public data only <Cite id="woo2023convnextv2" />.

### Scale

At large scale convolutional networks keep pace with transformers when they get the same compute. For NFNets pretrained
on the JFT-4B dataset, held-out loss falls as a power law in compute, and after fine-tuning they match the reported
ImageNet accuracy of vision transformers with comparable compute budgets <Cite id="smith2023convnets" />. The choice
between the two families is then about cost, resolution and deployment more than accuracy; see
<NoteLink to="convolution-versus-attention-in-vision">convolution versus attention in vision</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Compare a stage-1 block of ResNet-50 with one of ConvNeXt-T, both on a $56 \times 56$ map ($3{,}136$ pixels). Count
parameters, including biases and normalisation, and multiply-adds for one image.

**ResNet-50 bottleneck, 256 channels.**

1. $1 \times 1$ from 256 to 64: $16{,}384$ weights, plus 128 batch-norm parameters.
2. $3 \times 3$ from 64 to 64: $9 \cdot 64^2 = 36{,}864$ weights, plus 128.
3. $1 \times 1$ from 64 to 256: $16{,}384$ weights, plus 512.

Total $70{,}400$ parameters. The convolutions cost $3{,}136 \cdot 69{,}632 = 218.4$ million multiply-adds.

**ConvNeXt-T block, 96 channels.**

1. $7 \times 7$ depthwise on 96 channels: $49 \cdot 96 = 4{,}704$ weights and 96 biases.
2. Layer normalisation: $2 \cdot 96 = 192$.
3. $1 \times 1$ from 96 to 384: $36{,}864$ weights and 384 biases.
4. $1 \times 1$ from 384 to 96: $36{,}864$ weights and 96 biases.
5. Layer scale: 96.

Total $79{,}296$ parameters. The convolutions cost $3{,}136 \cdot (4{,}704 + 2 \cdot 36{,}864) = 246.0$ million
multiply-adds.

The two blocks cost about the same, but they spend it differently. In ResNet, the $3 \times 3$ spatial filter takes 53%
of the weights and sees a $3 \times 3$ window. In ConvNeXt, the spatial filter takes 6% of the weights and sees
$7 \times 7$; the channel-mixing $1 \times 1$ layers take 94%. After the three blocks of stage 1, a ResNet-50 unit sees
35 pixels of the input and a ConvNeXt-T unit 76, as the figure's readout shows.

## Assumptions

- The training recipe is fixed across the comparison. ConvNeXt's gains are architecture gains only relative to the
  improved ResNet baseline, not to the 2015 one.
- The design steps are tuned at one size (ResNet-50 and Swin-T scale) and on ImageNet classification; the paper checks
  a larger regime and downstream detection and segmentation <Cite id="liu2022" />.
- Images are the target. The block assumes a regular grid and translation-equivariant features.

## Pitfalls

- **Depthwise convolutions are memory-bound.** A $7 \times 7$ depthwise layer has few operations per byte, so
  ConvNeXt's multiply-add count understates its run time on some accelerators.
- **Layer normalisation on channels-first tensors.** LN must normalise over the channel axis at each pixel; applying
  a framework's default LN to an $[N, C, H, W]$ tensor normalises over the wrong axes. Implementations permute to
  channels-last or use a dedicated channel norm.
- **Attributing gains.** Comparing a new architecture under a new recipe against an old architecture under an old
  recipe conflates the two; the ResNet-50 recipe alone moved accuracy by 2.7 points <Cite id="liu2022" />.
- **Large kernels without help.** Very large depthwise kernels trained alone learn poorly; RepLKNet needed the parallel
  small kernel during training <Cite id="ding2022replknet" />.
