<Definition>
A residual block computes $\yvec = \xvec + F(\xvec; \theta)$, where $F$ is a small stack of layers with parameters
$\theta$ and the addition is elementwise. The path that carries $\xvec$ unchanged is the shortcut or skip connection. A
residual network is a stack of such blocks <Cite id="he2016" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Adding layers to a network should never make its training error worse: the extra layers could learn the identity and
reproduce the shallower network. In practice, plain deep networks trained with <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> do get worse. On <Gloss name="cifar-10" />, a plain 56-layer network has higher training error than a
plain 20-layer one <Cite id="he2016" />. This is the degradation problem. It is an optimisation failure, not
overfitting, because the training error itself rises.

A residual block makes the identity the default. When $F = 0$ the block passes its input through, so setting the
weights of $F$ near zero recovers a shallower network. The block only has to learn the difference between the identity
and the desired map. If the desired map is close to the identity, that difference is small and easy to find.

## Why the identity path matters

Consider blocks $l, l+1, \dots, L-1$ with pure identity shortcuts and no operation after the addition, as in the
pre-activation design <Cite id="he2016b" />: $\xvec_{m+1} = \xvec_m + F_m(\xvec_m)$, where $\xvec_m$ is the input of
block $m$. Unrolling the recursion gives

$$
\xvec_L = \xvec_l + \sum_{m=l}^{L-1} F_m(\xvec_m).
$$

The output of any deeper block is the output of any shallower block plus a sum of residuals. Differentiating, the
gradient of a loss $\ell$ at block $l$ is $\partial \ell / \partial \xvec_L$ plus terms that pass through the residual
branches. The first term reaches block $l$ without passing through any weight layer, so the gradient at a shallow block
cannot vanish because the network is deep. <NoteLink to="residual-connections">Residual connections</NoteLink> derives
this from the per-layer Jacobian $\I + \partial F / \partial \xvec$, shows that the signal's mean square instead grows
by a factor $1 + \alpha^2$ per block for a branch scale $\alpha$, and gives the branch scalings that control the growth.

The original ResNet applied a <Gloss name="relu" /> after the addition, so its shortcut is not quite the identity.
Moving the <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> and ReLU inside $F$ (pre-activation) makes
the shortcut an exact identity. The pre-activation form trains a 1001-layer network on CIFAR-10 <Cite id="he2016b" />.

## Block designs

**Basic block.** $F$ is two $3 \times 3$ convolutions with batch normalisation and a ReLU between them. It is used in
ResNet-18 and ResNet-34.

**Bottleneck block.** $F$ is a $1 \times 1$ convolution that reduces the channels by a factor of 4, a $3 \times 3$
convolution at the reduced width, and a $1 \times 1$ convolution that restores the width. It is used in
<NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">ResNet-50</NoteLink>, -101 and -152. The
<NoteLink to="one-by-one-convolution">one-by-one convolutions</NoteLink> do the channel mixing, so the expensive
$3 \times 3$ convolution runs on a quarter of the channels.

**Stages.** A ResNet for <Gloss name="imagenet" /> has a stem (a $7 \times 7$ stride-2 convolution and a max pool), then
four stages of 64, 128, 256 and 512 channels at the bottleneck width. The first block of stages 2 to 4 halves the
resolution with stride 2. <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">Global average pooling</NoteLink> and one dense layer
classify.

**Normalisation.** Each convolution is followed by batch normalisation. Setting the scale of the last normalisation in
each branch to zero makes every block the identity at initialisation, which helps large-batch training
<Cite id="goyal2017" />. Detection and segmentation networks train with a few images per device, where batch statistics
are noisy; they freeze the batch statistics or use group normalisation instead, as
<NoteLink to="normalisation-in-convolutional-networks">normalisation in convolutional networks</NoteLink> explains.

## Identity against projection shortcuts

When a block changes the number of channels or downsamples with stride 2, $\xvec$ and $F(\xvec)$ have different shapes,
and the shortcut must change shape too. He et al. compared three choices <Cite id="he2016" />:

- **A.** Every shortcut is an identity. Where the resolution halves, the shortcut takes every second pixel; where the
  channels grow, the extra channels are padded with zeros. A adds no parameters.
- **B.** A shortcut that changes shape is a projection, a $1 \times 1$ convolution $\Wmat_s$ with the block's stride:
  $\yvec = \Wmat_s \xvec + F(\xvec)$. All other shortcuts are identities. The ImageNet ResNets use B.
- **C.** Every shortcut is a projection, including those between blocks of the same shape.

The cost of a projection from $C$ to $C'$ channels is $CC'$ weights. In ResNet-34 three blocks change shape, at the
start of stages 2, 3 and 4. Option B adds $64 \cdot 128 + 128 \cdot 256 + 256 \cdot 512 = 172{,}032$ weights, 0.8% of
the network's 21.8 million. Option C projects all 16 shortcuts and adds 1.09 million weights, 5.0% of the network. The
ImageNet top-1 errors of ResNet-34 with A, B and C were 25.03%, 24.52% and 24.19% <Cite id="he2016" />. B gains over A
because the zero-padded channels of A carry no residual. C gains a little more from its extra parameters. The
differences are small next to the gain of any of them over a plain network, so projections are not what solves the
degradation problem.

Projections cost much more in <NoteLink to="autoencoder">bottleneck networks</NoteLink>, because a bottleneck shortcut
connects the two wide ends of a block. In ResNet-50 the four shape-changing blocks are at the start of every stage,
including stage 1, whose first block widens 64 channels to 256. Option B's four projections hold
$64 \cdot 256 + 256 \cdot 512 + 512 \cdot 1024 + 1024 \cdot 2048 = 2{,}768{,}896$ weights, 10.8% of the network's 25.6
million. A projection on a 256-channel bottleneck block has $256^2 = 65{,}536$ weights, about as many as the block's
branch ($69{,}632$, counted below), so option C would nearly double the size and the multiply-adds of every block.
Identity shortcuts are what keep bottleneck networks cheap <Cite id="he2016" />.

## Worked example

Compare the weights of a bottleneck block on 256 channels with a basic block of two $3 \times 3$ convolutions at 256
channels, ignoring biases and normalisation.

1. The bottleneck's first $1 \times 1$ convolution maps 256 channels to 64. It has $256 \cdot 64 = 16{,}384$ weights.
2. Its $3 \times 3$ convolution at 64 channels has $9 \cdot 64 \cdot 64 = 36{,}864$ weights.
3. Its last $1 \times 1$ convolution maps 64 channels back to 256, with $16{,}384$ weights. The branch has
   $16{,}384 + 36{,}864 + 16{,}384 = 69{,}632$ weights.
4. The basic block's two $3 \times 3$ convolutions at 256 channels have $2 \cdot 9 \cdot 256^2 = 1{,}179{,}648$
   weights.
5. An identity shortcut adds nothing. A projection shortcut would add $256^2 = 65{,}536$ weights, 94% of the
   bottleneck branch.

The bottleneck block uses about 17 times fewer weights than the basic block while keeping 256-channel inputs and
outputs. That saving is what makes a 152-layer network affordable. ResNet-152 needs 11.3 billion multiply-adds per
$224 \times 224$ image, fewer than the 15.3 billion of the 16-layer VGG-16 <Cite id="he2016" />.

## Variants and practice

The residual block is the base of most later convolutional designs. A <NoteLink to="densely-connected-network">densely
connected network</NoteLink> concatenates each layer's output to its input instead of adding it, so later layers read
earlier features unchanged. ResNeXt splits the $3 \times 3$ convolution into groups <Cite id="xie2017resnext" /> (see
<NoteLink to="depthwise-and-grouped-convolution">grouped convolution</NoteLink>).
<NoteLink to="squeeze-and-excitation">Squeeze-and-excitation</NoteLink> rescales the branch's channels before the
addition. <NoteLink to="efficient-convolutional-networks">MobileNetV2</NoteLink> inverts the bottleneck, and
<NoteLink to="modern-convolutional-networks">ConvNeXt</NoteLink> rebuilds the ResNet block with the design of
<Gloss name="vit">vision transformers</Gloss>. The <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> wraps every
attention and feed-forward layer in the same identity shortcut.

ResNet-50 remains the standard baseline of <NoteLink to="image-classification">image classification</NoteLink> and the
default backbone of many detection and segmentation models. Its accuracy depends strongly on training. With the 2021
training recipe of the timm library (longer schedules, stronger augmentation, a <Gloss name="bce">binary
cross-entropy</Gloss> loss), an unchanged ResNet-50 reaches 80.4% top-1 on ImageNet <Cite id="wightman2021resnet" />,
against 76.1% with the original 90-epoch recipe <Cite id="liu2022" />. Both figures are single $224 \times 224$ crops.

## Assumptions

- $F(\xvec)$ and $\xvec$ have the same shape, or a projection or zero-padding makes them match.
- The desired mapping is close to the identity, or can be built from many small corrections to it. Deep networks whose
  layers each refine a representation fit this assumption.

## Pitfalls

- **Operations on the shortcut.** Anything placed on the identity path, such as a ReLU after the addition,
  <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> or a scaling, reintroduces a product of factors along the shortcut. He et
  al. found that constant scaling and gating of the shortcut both hurt optimisation <Cite id="he2016b" />.
- **Variance growth.** Each addition adds the variance of $F(\xvec)$ to the variance of $\xvec$. Without normalisation
  inside $F$ or a small initialisation of its last layer, activations grow with depth; see
  <NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink> for the rate.
- **Projections everywhere.** Projecting every shortcut (option C) nearly doubles the weights of each bottleneck block
  and takes ResNet-50 from 25.6 to 40.1 million parameters for a small gain, and it removes the exact identity path that
  the gradient argument relies on.
- **Effective depth.** Many paths through a residual network are short. Much of the gradient flows through paths that
  skip most blocks, so a 100-block network does not behave like a 100-layer plain network <Cite id="veit2016" />.
