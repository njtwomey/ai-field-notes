<Definition>
A squeeze-and-excitation (SE) block takes a <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">feature map</NoteLink> $\Umat$ with $C$
channels $u_1, \dots, u_C$ on an $H \times W$ grid and returns $\tilde u_c = s_c u_c$ for each channel. The squeeze step
averages each channel over the grid, $z_c = \frac{1}{HW} \sum_{i, j} u_c(i, j)$. The excitation step maps
$\zvec \in \reals^C$ to gates $\svec = \sigma(\Wmat_2\, \mathrm{ReLU}(\Wmat_1 \zvec)) \in (0, 1)^C$, with
$\Wmat_1 \in \reals^{(C/r) \times C}$, $\Wmat_2 \in \reals^{C \times (C/r)}$ and reduction ratio $r$
<Cite id="hu2018senet" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A convolution mixes channels with fixed weights inside a small window. Output channel $c'$ at a pixel is a weighted sum
of the input channels near that pixel, and the weights are the same for every image. The layer cannot decide, for one
particular image, that the "fur" channels matter more than the "sky" channels. It also cannot see beyond its window:
even deep in a network, a $3 \times 3$ convolution combines channels using only local evidence, so a global fact about
the image, such as "this is an indoor scene", reaches a channel only after many layers.

Squeeze-and-excitation gives every block a cheap global, input-dependent reweighting of its channels. It summarises
the whole feature map in one number per channel, decides from all $C$ numbers together how much each channel should
count, and scales the channels accordingly. Hu et al. describe this as modelling the interdependencies between
channels <Cite id="hu2018senet" />. The block keeps the convolutional backbone unchanged and adds a side branch, so it
fits into ResNet, Inception and MobileNet blocks alike.

## Mechanism

**Squeeze.** <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">Global average pooling</NoteLink> turns each $H \times W$ channel into
its mean $z_c$. The vector $\zvec$ is a descriptor of the whole image as this block sees it. Max pooling works nearly as
well; average pooling was slightly better in the ablations <Cite id="hu2018senet" />.

**Excitation.** A two-layer <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">perceptron</NoteLink> maps $\zvec$ to the gates. The
first layer reduces $C$ to $C/r$ dimensions and applies a <Gloss name="relu" />; the second restores $C$ dimensions and
applies a sigmoid,

$$
\svec = \sigma\paren{\Wmat_2 \max(0, \Wmat_1 \zvec)}.
$$

The bottleneck limits the parameters and forces the gates through a low-dimensional summary, so channels are
reweighted jointly rather than one by one. The sigmoid keeps each gate in $(0, 1)$ and lets several channels be
emphasised at once, unlike a softmax, which would make the channels compete for one unit of weight. Replacing the
sigmoid with a ReLU or tanh made SE-ResNet-50 worse <Cite id="hu2018senet" />.

**Scale.** Each channel is multiplied by its gate, $\tilde u_c(i, j) = s_c\, u_c(i, j)$. The gate is constant over
the grid, so the block changes how much a channel counts, not where it fires.

**Placement.** In a <NoteLink to="residual-network">residual network</NoteLink> the SE branch acts on the output of
the residual branch $F(\xvec)$, before the addition to the shortcut: $\yvec = \xvec + \svec \odot F(\xvec)$, where
$\odot$ scales channel $c$ of $F(\xvec)$ by $s_c$. The identity path stays unscaled. Hu et al. found that gating after
the addition gave higher error than gating the branch <Cite id="hu2018senet" />.

## Worked example

**A gate by hand.** Take $C = 4$ channels and $r = 2$, as in the figure. The four $12 \times 12$ channels have means
$\zvec = (1, 4, 0.5, 0)$. Use no biases and the weights

$$
\Wmat_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Wmat_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0 \\ -0.4 & 1 \\ 0.6 & -1 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

1. **Reduce.** $\Wmat_1 \zvec = (1 + 2 - 0.5 + 0,\; -1 + 0 + 1 + 0) = (2.5, 0)$. The ReLU leaves $(2.5, 0)$.
2. **Expand.** $\Wmat_2 (2.5, 0)^\top = (2.0, -1.0, 1.5, 0)$.
3. **Gate.** $\sigma$ of these values gives $\svec = (0.881, 0.269, 0.818, 0.500)$.
4. **Scale.** Channel 1 keeps 88% of its values, channel 2 keeps 27%, channel 3 keeps 82% and channel 4 keeps half.

Channel 2 has the largest mean and the smallest gate. Its gate is set by the hidden unit, which is a function of all
four means, not of $z_2$ alone. Raising the strength of channel 3 to 2 makes $z_3 = 1$, the hidden vector $(2, 1)$ and
the gates $(0.832, 0.550, 0.550, 0.622)$: channel 2 recovers and channel 4 changes though its own input did not.

**Parameters at realistic widths.** At $C = 64$ and $r = 16$ the bottleneck has $64/16 = 4$ units. $\Wmat_1$ and
$\Wmat_2$ have $2 \cdot 64 \cdot 4 = 512$ weights, plus $4 + 64 = 68$ biases. A $3 \times 3$ convolution at 64 channels
has $9 \cdot 64^2 = 36{,}864$ weights, so the SE block adds 1.6% of one such layer.

## Cost

An SE block with $C$ channels adds $2C^2/r$ weights (and $C/r + C$ biases) and $2C^2/r$ multiply-adds per image for the
two dense layers, plus $HWC$ additions for the pooling and $HWC$ multiplications for the scaling. The dense layers act
on one vector per image, so their cost does not grow with the image size.

In SE-ResNet-50 with $r = 16$, stage $s$ has $N_s$ blocks with $C_s$ output channels, $(N_s, C_s) = (3, 256)$,
$(4, 512)$, $(6, 1024)$ and $(3, 2048)$. The blocks add

$$
\frac{2}{r} \sum_s N_s C_s^2 = \frac{2}{16} \paren{3 \cdot 256^2 + 4 \cdot 512^2 + 6 \cdot 1024^2 + 3 \cdot 2048^2}
= 2{,}514{,}944
$$

weights, about 10% of <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">ResNet-50</NoteLink>'s 25.6 million.
The last stage alone contributes $1{,}572{,}864$, because the cost grows with $C^2$. Hu et al. report 3.87 against 3.86
billion multiply-adds per $224 \times 224$ image, a 0.26% increase, and that removing the SE blocks of the last stage
cuts the parameter increase to about 4% at a loss of less than 0.1 percentage points of top-5 accuracy
<Cite id="hu2018senet" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Accuracy for almost no arithmetic.** SE-ResNet-50 reached 6.62% single-crop top-5 error on the
  <Gloss name="imagenet" /> validation set against 7.48% for ResNet-50, close to ResNet-101's 6.52% at half the
  multiply-adds <Cite id="hu2018senet" />.
- **Parameters against $r$.** The added parameters grow as $1/r$. Accuracy did not improve steadily as $r$ fell, and
  the paper chose $r = 16$ as a balance between accuracy and size <Cite id="hu2018senet" />.
- **Latency.** The pooling is a reduction over the whole feature map that must finish before the scaling can start, so
  an SE block adds a synchronisation point. On mobile hardware the sigmoid itself costs time; MobileNetV3 replaces it by
  a piecewise-linear hard sigmoid <Cite id="howard2019mobilenetv3" />.
- **Where it went.** SE blocks are part of MobileNetV3 <Cite id="howard2019mobilenetv3" />, of the inverted residual
  blocks of <NoteLink to="efficient-convolutional-networks">EfficientNet</NoteLink> <Cite id="tan2019" /> and of the
  RegNetY design space, where Y means X plus SE <Cite id="radosavovic2020regnet" />.
  <NoteLink to="modern-convolutional-networks">ConvNeXt V2</NoteLink> uses a parameter-free global response
  normalisation of per-channel norms in a similar role <Cite id="woo2023convnextv2" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Gating the shortcut.** Placing the gate after the residual addition scales the identity path too, so the gradient
  through the shortcut is scaled by $s_c$ at every block; put the gate on the residual branch.
- **Too few hidden units.** With $C$ small, $C/r$ can fall to 1 or 2 units, which forces all gates through one or two
  numbers. Choose $r$ per stage, or set a minimum width, as EfficientNet does by tying the hidden width to the block's
  input channels.
- **Global averages hide small objects.** The squeeze averages over the whole image, so a small object barely moves
  $z_c$. The gate responds to what dominates the image, which suits classification and can mislead dense prediction,
  where a local cue matters.
- **Saturated gates.** Large pre-sigmoid values push gates towards 0 or 1, where the sigmoid's derivative
  $s_c(1 - s_c)$ is close to 0 and the excitation weights learn slowly.
