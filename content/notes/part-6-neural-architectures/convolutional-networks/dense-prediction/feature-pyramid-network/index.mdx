<Definition>
A feature pyramid network (FPN) takes the stage outputs $C_2, \dots, C_5$ of a convolutional backbone, at strides 4,
8, 16 and 32, and builds pyramid levels $P_2, \dots, P_5$ of the same sizes with a fixed number $d$ of channels (256).
It projects each $C_\ell$ by a lateral $1 \times 1$ convolution, adds the merged map of the level above upsampled by a
factor 2, and smooths the sum with a $3 \times 3$ convolution. A detector or segmenter then predicts at every level
with one shared head, assigning each object to a level by its size.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Objects in one image differ in size by a factor of 20 or more: a distant pedestrian may be 20 pixels tall and a nearby
bus 600. A detector needs features in which an object of any size covers a handful of cells, enough to recognise it and
few enough for one head to handle.

The classical answer is an image pyramid: resize the image to several scales and run the detector on each (see
<NoteLink to="image-pyramids-and-scale-space" />). A deep network multiplies its cost by the number of scales, so this
is slow. A convolutional backbone already computes a pyramid of its own, one map per stage at strides 4 to 32. Its fine
maps are good at placing small things but weak in meaning, because few layers have processed them; its coarse maps are
strong in meaning but too coarse for small things. SSD predicted from several backbone maps directly and was much weaker
on small objects than on large ones <Cite id="liu2016ssd" />.

FPN fixes the fine maps by sending semantics down. A top-down path starts from the coarsest map, upsamples it by 2 and
adds it to the next finer backbone map, and repeats. Every level then holds features as meaningful as the top's, at its
own resolution. This is the skip structure of <NoteLink to="fully-convolutional-network-and-u-net">U-Net</NoteLink>,
with a prediction at every level instead of only at the finest.

## Details

### Construction

Write $C_\ell$ for the output of the last residual block of stage $\ell$ of a <NoteLink to="residual-network" />, with
stride $2^\ell$ relative to the input. FPN uses $\ell = 2, \dots, 5$ and leaves out the stride-2 stage $C_1$, whose
map is large and shallow. With $L_\ell$ a lateral $1 \times 1$ convolution from the channels of $C_\ell$ to $d$,
$S_\ell$ a $3 \times 3$ convolution from $d$ to $d$, and $\mathrm{up}_2$ nearest-neighbour upsampling by 2, the
merged maps $M_\ell$ and pyramid levels $P_\ell$ are

$$
M_5 = L_5(C_5), \qquad M_\ell = L_\ell(C_\ell) + \mathrm{up}_2(M_{\ell + 1}) \quad (\ell = 4, 3, 2), \qquad
P_\ell = S_\ell(M_\ell).
$$

Addition needs matching shapes. The lateral <NoteLink to="one-by-one-convolution" /> brings every level to $d$
channels, and upsampling by exactly 2 matches the spatial size when each stage halves it. The $3 \times 3$ convolution
reduces the aliasing of nearest-neighbour upsampling. The paper uses $d = 256$ and no nonlinearities in these extra
layers <Cite id="lin2017fpn" />. A coarser level $P_6$ is a stride-2 subsampling of $P_5$, used only to hold the
largest anchors.

The fixed width $d$ makes the levels interchangeable: one head with the same weights runs on every level, as a
classifier slides over an image pyramid. Sharing the head across levels gave about the same accuracy as separate heads
<Cite id="lin2017fpn" />.

### Assigning objects to levels

Each object is handled on one level, chosen by its size. Proposals in a region proposal network are matched to anchors,
and FPN gives each level one anchor size: $32^2$, $64^2$, $128^2$, $256^2$ and $512^2$ pixels on $P_2$ to $P_6$, in the
three aspect ratios 1:2, 1:1 and 2:1, 15 anchors over the pyramid <Cite id="lin2017fpn" />. An anchor's side is 8 cells
of its level, whatever the level. The labelling rules are those of Faster R-CNN, an <Gloss name="iou" /> above 0.7 for a
positive and below 0.3 for a negative <Cite id="ren2015" />; see <NoteLink to="detection-heads-and-anchors" />.

A second-stage head pools features for a region of width $w$ and height $h$ from level

$$
k = \left\lfloor k_0 + \log_2\paren{\sqrt{wh} / 224} \right\rfloor, \qquad k_0 = 4,
$$

clamped to the levels that exist <Cite id="lin2017fpn" />. The number 224 is the <Gloss name="imagenet" /> training
size, and $k_0 = 4$ because the single-scale Faster R-CNN that FPN replaced pooled every region from $C_4$. Halving the
region's side lowers $k$ by 1, which halves the stride, so the region covers the same number of cells: between 14 and 28
per side. Dense one-stage detectors use the same idea with levels $P_3$ to $P_7$, where $P_6$ and $P_7$ come from
stride-2 convolutions <Cite id="lin2017" />. FCOS assigns objects to levels by the distance from a cell to the box edges
instead <Cite id="tian2019" />.

### Variants

- **Extra bottom-up path.** PANet adds a second, bottom-up path after the top-down one, so that location information
  from $P_2$ reaches the coarse levels in a few layers rather than through the whole backbone
  <Cite id="liu2018panet" />. The necks of most current YOLO detectors are built this way.
- **Weighted, repeated fusion.** EfficientDet's BiFPN removes nodes with a single input, adds edges from each backbone
  map to its output node, repeats the top-down and bottom-up block several times, and weights each input of an addition
  by a learned non-negative scalar <Cite id="tan2020efficientdet" />.
- **Pyramid from one map.** A plain <Gloss name="vit">vision transformer</Gloss> has one stride-16 map. ViTDet builds
  the pyramid from that map alone with deconvolutions and pooling, and competes with hierarchical backbones that use FPN
  <Cite id="li2022vitdet" />.
- **Segmentation.** Panoptic FPN adds a semantic branch that upsamples every level to stride 4 and sums them, beside
  an instance branch on the same pyramid <Cite id="kirillov2019fpn" />; see <NoteLink to="segmentation-decoders" />.

FPN or one of these variants is the default neck of detectors and instance segmenters with a convolutional or
hierarchical transformer backbone. The task view is in <NoteLink to="object-detection" />.

## Worked example

**Shapes for an $800 \times 1024$ image.** A
<NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures">ResNet-50</NoteLink> gives

| Level | Stride | $C_\ell$ size and channels | $P_\ell$ size and channels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 4 | $200 \times 256$, 256 | $200 \times 256$, 256 |
| 3 | 8 | $100 \times 128$, 512 | $100 \times 128$, 256 |
| 4 | 16 | $50 \times 64$, 1024 | $50 \times 64$, 256 |
| 5 | 32 | $25 \times 32$, 2048 | $25 \times 32$, 256 |
| 6 | 64 | | $13 \times 16$, 256 |

$P_6$ subsamples $P_5$ with stride 2: $\lfloor (25 - 1)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 13$ rows and $\lfloor (32 - 1)/2 \rfloor + 1 =
16$ columns.

**Top-down step at level 4.** $M_5$ has $25 \times 32$ cells. Upsampling by 2 gives $50 \times 64$, the size of
$L_4(C_4)$, so the two add cell by cell. Each cell $(i, j)$ of $\mathrm{up}_2(M_5)$ copies cell
$(\lfloor i/2 \rfloor, \lfloor j/2 \rfloor)$ of $M_5$: four fine cells share one coarse value, and the lateral term
supplies what varies between them. A stage whose input side is odd breaks this: a side of 25 halves to 13, which doubles
to 26, not 25, and real implementations then resize to the lateral map's shape instead of doubling.

**Cost.** The lateral layers have $(256 + 512 + 1024 + 2048) \cdot 256 = 983{,}040$ weights and the four $3 \times 3$
layers $4 \cdot 9 \cdot 256 \cdot 256 = 2{,}359{,}296$. With biases, FPN adds 3.34 million parameters to the 25 million
of a ResNet-50. At $800 \times 1024$ it costs 46.4 billion multiply-accumulates, most of them in the $3 \times 3$ layer
on $P_2$. The pyramid holds $51{,}200 + 12{,}800 + 3{,}200 + 800 + 208 = 68{,}208$ positions, 1.33 times the positions
of $P_2$ alone, because each level has a quarter of the cells of the one below.

**Level assignment.** For region features, a $224 \times 224$ box goes to $k = \lfloor 4 + \log_2 1 \rfloor = 4$. A
$112 \times 112$ box gives $\lfloor 4 - 1 \rfloor = 3$. A $120 \times 300$ box has $\sqrt{wh} = 189.7$, so
$k = \lfloor 4 + \log_2 0.847 \rfloor = \lfloor 3.76 \rfloor = 3$, and it covers $15 \times 37.5$ cells of $P_3$. A
$50 \times 50$ box gives $\lfloor 1.84 \rfloor = 1$, clamped to 2. For proposals, the same $120 \times 300$ box at
the centre of a cell has its best anchor on $P_5$, the $1{:}2$ anchor of area $256^2$ ($181 \times 362$) with IoU
0.55, against 0.46 for the $1{:}2$ anchor of $P_4$.

## Assumptions

- Backbone stages halve the resolution exactly, so upsampling by 2 matches the lateral map. Inputs padded to a multiple
  of 32 satisfy it.
- Features at different levels are comparable after the lateral projection, so one head serves every level and an
  object's level depends only on its size.
- Object size is a good proxy for the scale at which its features are best. Elongated objects, with a short side and a
  long side, fit any single level less well.

## Pitfalls

- **Odd sizes.** An input side that is not a multiple of 32 gives odd stage sizes, and the upsampled map is one cell
  larger than the lateral one. Pad the input, or resize to the lateral shape.
- **Dropping the finest level.** $P_2$ holds most positions and most cost. Detectors that start at $P_3$ for speed,
  as RetinaNet does, have no anchor smaller than 32 pixels, and much smaller objects match no anchor well.
- **Boundary cases of the equation.** A box of side 223 goes to $P_3$ and one of side 224 to $P_4$. Hard rules at
  level boundaries make similar boxes use different features; pooling from several levels, or anchor-free assignment
  by regression range, softens this.
- **Mixing the two assignments.** The equation for region features and the anchor sizes for proposals send the same box
  to levels one or two apart. Changing anchor sizes without changing the equation, or the reverse, shifts which level
  learns which objects.
