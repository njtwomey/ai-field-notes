<Definition>
A dense detection head is a small convolutional network that runs on every cell of a
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">feature map</NoteLink>, or of every level of a
<NoteLink to="feature-pyramid-network">feature pyramid</NoteLink>, and outputs a fixed set of candidate detections per
cell. In an anchor-based head each cell holds $A$ anchors, reference boxes of preset sizes and aspect ratios centred on
the cell. For each anchor the head outputs $K$ class scores (or an objectness score and $K$ class scores) and four
offsets $(t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h)$ that move and rescale the anchor into a predicted box. An anchor-free head predicts, at
each cell, class scores and the distances from the cell to the four edges of a box. Training assigns each ground-truth
box to some anchors or cells as positives; inference decodes every candidate, discards low scores and removes duplicates
by non-maximum suppression.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An image holds an unknown number of objects, and a network outputs a tensor of fixed size. A dense detector resolves
the mismatch by making many guesses: one candidate box per anchor per cell, tens of thousands per image. Each candidate
says how likely it is to hold an object of each class and how to adjust its box. Almost all candidates are background,
and only those that score highly are kept.

Predicting a box from nothing is hard: its corners can be anywhere and its size ranges over two orders of magnitude.
Predicting a correction to a nearby reference box is easy. An anchor near an object and of about its size needs only a
small shift and a small rescaling, numbers of order 0.1. The anchor set covers the range of sizes and shapes, and each
object is learned by the anchors that already roughly fit it.

The hard part is deciding which anchors should fit which object during training, the label assignment. The choice of
positives, more than the choice between anchors and points, separates detectors of similar architecture
<Cite id="zhang2020atss" />.

## Details

### Anchors

An anchor of size $a$ (the square root of its area) and aspect ratio $\rho = h/w$ has width $a/\sqrt{\rho}$ and height
$a\sqrt{\rho}$. On a feature map of stride $s$, cell $(i, j)$ is centred at $((j + \tfrac12)s, (i + \tfrac12)s)$ in
input pixels, and every cell carries the same $A$ anchors there. Faster R-CNN's region proposal network (RPN) uses
$A = 9$ on one map: areas $128^2$, $256^2$ and $512^2$, each in ratios $\tfrac12$, 1 and 2 <Cite id="ren2015" />. With
a feature pyramid each level takes one size, $32 \cdot 2^{\ell - 2}$ on $P_\ell$, in three ratios
<Cite id="lin2017fpn" />. RetinaNet adds the scales $2^0$, $2^{1/3}$ and $2^{2/3}$ per level, so $A = 9$ on each of
$P_3$ to $P_7$ <Cite id="lin2017" />. YOLOv2 chose its anchor shapes by $k$-means on the training boxes with the
distance $1 - \text{IoU}$, settling on $k = 5$ <Cite id="redmon2017" />.

### The head

The head is fully convolutional and shared by all cells and all pyramid levels. The RPN applies a $3 \times 3$
convolution and then two sibling $1 \times 1$ convolutions, one with $2A$ outputs (object or not) and one with $4A$
outputs (the offsets) <Cite id="ren2015" />. RetinaNet uses two subnetworks of four $3 \times 3$ convolutions with 256
channels each: the classification branch ends in $KA$ outputs with a sigmoid per class, the box branch in $4A$ linear
outputs <Cite id="lin2017" />. YOLO first predicted a few boxes and one set of class scores per cell
<Cite id="redmon2016" />; from YOLOv2 on, the head predicts per anchor an objectness score, $K$ class scores and four
box numbers, $A(5 + K)$ channels per cell <Cite id="redmon2017" />.

Almost every anchor is background, so a classifier initialised to output probability $\tfrac12$ starts with a loss
dominated by easy negatives. RetinaNet initialises the bias $b$ of the last classification layer so that every
anchor's foreground probability starts at a prior $\pi = 0.01$: $\sigma(b) = \pi$ gives
$b = \log(\pi / (1 - \pi)) = -4.6$ <Cite id="lin2017" />. The training loss for the class scores is the
<NoteLink to="focal-loss" />, which down-weights well-classified anchors.

### Box offsets

Let an anchor have centre $(x_a, y_a)$, width $w_a$ and height $h_a$, and let the target box have centre $(x, y)$,
width $w$ and height $h$. The regression targets are

$$
t_x = \frac{x - x_a}{w_a}, \qquad t_y = \frac{y - y_a}{h_a}, \qquad t_w = \log\frac{w}{w_a}, \qquad
t_h = \log\frac{h}{h_a},
$$

and decoding a prediction inverts them: $x = x_a + t_x w_a$, $y = y_a + t_y h_a$, $w = w_a e^{t_w}$,
$h = h_a e^{t_h}$ <Cite id="ren2015" />.

The form follows from what the targets should not depend on. Scale the image, the anchor and the box together by
$c > 0$. The centre offset $x - x_a$ and the width $w_a$ both scale by $c$, so $t_x$ is unchanged; the ratio $w / w_a$
is unchanged, so $t_w$ is too. A detector therefore learns one regression for small and large objects alike. The
logarithm makes any real $t_w$ decode to a positive width, and it treats doubling and halving symmetrically as
$\pm \log 2$. YOLOv2 bounds the centre instead: it predicts $x = c_x + \sigma(t_x)$ in units of cells, with $c_x$ the
cell's corner, so a prediction cannot leave its cell, and keeps $w = w_a e^{t_w}$ <Cite id="redmon2017" />.

The offsets are trained with the smooth $L_1$ loss in Faster R-CNN and RetinaNet. Many current detectors train the
decoded box against the target with an <NoteLink to="intersection-over-union">IoU</NoteLink>-based loss instead, so
that the loss measures overlap directly.

### Label assignment

Training needs, for every candidate, a label: positive (with a matched ground-truth box), negative, or ignored.

- **IoU thresholds.** Compute the IoU of every anchor with every ground-truth box. The RPN makes an anchor positive if
  its IoU with some box is above 0.7 or if it is that box's best anchor, and negative if its IoU is below 0.3 with every
  box; the rest are ignored <Cite id="ren2015" />. RetinaNet uses 0.5 and 0.4 <Cite id="lin2017" />. The best-anchor
  rule ensures that a box whose shape no anchor fits still gets one positive.
- **Adaptive thresholds.** ATSS takes, on each pyramid level, the $k = 9$ anchors whose centres are closest to the
  box's centre, computes their IoUs with the box, and sets the box's threshold to the mean plus the standard deviation
  of those IoUs; candidates above it whose centres lie inside the box are positive <Cite id="zhang2020atss" />.
- **Points instead of anchors.** FCOS makes a cell positive for a box when the cell's centre lies inside it. The target
  is the distances $(l, t, r, b)$ from the centre to the box's left, top, right and bottom edges. A level $\ell$ keeps
  only cells with $m_{\ell - 1} \le \max(l, t, r, b) \le m_\ell$, where $m_2, \dots, m_7$ are 0, 64, 128, 256, 512 and
  $\infty$ and the levels are $P_3$ to $P_7$, so each box goes to one level by size. A cell inside two boxes takes the
  smaller. A centre-ness branch predicts $\sqrt{\frac{\min(l, r)}{\max(l, r)} \cdot \frac{\min(t, b)}{\max(t, b)}}$,
  which is 1 at the box's centre and falls to 0 at its edges, and multiplies the class score at inference so that
  off-centre boxes rank lower <Cite id="tian2019" />.
- **Dynamic assignment.** YOLOX scores each candidate against each box by a cost that adds the classification loss to
  the IoU loss of the current prediction, and gives each box its lowest-cost candidates, their number set from the IoUs
  (SimOTA) <Cite id="ge2021yolox" />. The positives then change during training as the predictions improve.

### From many candidates to a set

Dense heads fire on several neighbouring anchors or cells for one object. Inference keeps candidates above a score
threshold, decodes their boxes and runs non-maximum suppression per class; see the NMS explorer in
<NoteLink to="object-detection" />. A head trained with one-to-one assignment, one positive per object, can skip NMS.
DETR predicts a fixed set of boxes matched to the objects by the Hungarian algorithm <Cite id="carion2020" />, and
YOLOv10 trains a one-to-many head and a one-to-one head together and uses only the one-to-one head at inference
<Cite id="wang2024yolov10" />.

### Practice

Current real-time detectors in the YOLO line are anchor-free, with separate ("decoupled") classification and box
branches, a PAN-style feature pyramid and dynamic label assignment, a design YOLOX established
<Cite id="ge2021yolox" />. Anchor-based heads remain in two-stage detectors, whose RPN feeds a second stage, and in many
deployed RetinaNet-style models. The evaluation of all of them is the <Gloss name="mean-average-precision">mean average
precision</Gloss> of <NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-for-detection" />.

## Worked example

**Decoding.** Take a stride-16 map and cell $(5, 7)$, row 5 and column 7. Its centre is
$(x_a, y_a) = (7.5 \cdot 16, 5.5 \cdot 16) = (120, 88)$, and its $64 \times 64$ anchor spans $[88, 152]$ in $x$ and
$[56, 120]$ in $y$. The head predicts $(t_x, t_y, t_w, t_h) = (0.1, -0.2, 0.3, 0)$. Then

$$
x = 120 + 0.1 \cdot 64 = 126.4, \quad y = 88 - 0.2 \cdot 64 = 75.2, \quad w = 64\, e^{0.3} = 86.4, \quad
h = 64\, e^{0} = 64.
$$

The predicted box has corners $(126.4 \mp 43.2,\; 75.2 \mp 32)$, that is $[83.2, 169.6]$ in $x$ and $[43.2, 107.2]$ in
$y$. The anchor moved right by 6.4 and up by 12.8 pixels and widened by 35%.

**Encoding.** A ground-truth box has corners $(90, 50)$ and $(170, 110)$: centre $(130, 80)$, size $80 \times 60$. Its
targets relative to the same anchor are

$$
t_x = \frac{130 - 120}{64} = 0.156, \quad t_y = \frac{80 - 88}{64} = -0.125, \quad t_w = \log\frac{80}{64} = 0.223,
\quad t_h = \log\frac{60}{64} = -0.065.
$$

The overlap of anchor and box spans $[90, 152]$ in $x$ and $[56, 110]$ in $y$, an area of $62 \cdot 54 = 3348$. The
union is $4800 + 4096 - 3348 = 5548$, so the IoU is $3348 / 5548 = 0.603$.

**Matching on the grid.** Give every cell of the $16 \times 16$ map nine anchors, sides 32, 64 and 128 in ratios
$\tfrac12$, 1 and 2, as in the figure's starting position. With RetinaNet's thresholds, 8 anchors are positive: the
side-64 anchors of ratio $\tfrac12$ and 1 at cells $(4, 7)$, $(4, 8)$, $(5, 7)$ and $(5, 8)$, with IoUs from 0.603 to
0.660. Ten more fall between 0.4 and 0.5 and are ignored. The best is the wide anchor (ratio $\tfrac12$, $90.5 \times
45.3$) at cell $(4, 8)$, IoU 0.660, since the box itself is wider than tall. With the RPN's 0.7 no anchor passes, and
only the best-anchor rule makes $(4, 8)$ positive; 39 anchors fall between 0.3 and 0.7 and are ignored.

**Anchor-free targets.** Under FCOS's rule the cells whose centres lie inside the box are positive: 5 columns (centres
104 to 168) by 4 rows (56 to 104), 20 points. The cell centred at $(136, 72)$ has
$(l, t, r, b) = (46, 22, 34, 38)$ and centre-ness

$$
\sqrt{\frac{34}{46} \cdot \frac{22}{38}} = \sqrt{0.739 \cdot 0.579} = 0.654,
$$

the highest of the 20, shared with the cell below it at $(136, 88)$; the corner cell at $(168, 56)$, 2 pixels from the
right edge, has 0.053.

**Head size.** RetinaNet on an $800 \times 1024$ image with $K = 80$ classes predicts on $P_3$ to $P_7$, with
$100 \times 128$, $50 \times 64$, $25 \times 32$, $13 \times 16$ and $7 \times 8$ cells: 17,064 cells and, at 9 anchors
each, 153,576 anchors. Its classification branch outputs $9 \cdot 80 = 720$ channels per cell and its box branch 36.

## Assumptions

- Every object is well described by an axis-aligned box whose centre lies on or near a cell of a level of about its
  size. Thin, rotated or heavily occluded objects fit boxes and cells poorly.
- The anchors, or the points with their regression ranges, cover the sizes and shapes of the dataset's objects.
- One positive set per object, fixed or recomputed, defines what the head should learn. Ambiguous cells that lie in two
  boxes are resolved by a rule (higher IoU, smaller box), not by the data.

## Pitfalls

- **Anchors that miss the data.** Objects whose size or shape no anchor approaches have no anchor above the positive
  threshold and are learned only through the best-anchor rule, from one positive. Check the IoU of each training box
  with its best anchor before training.
- **Imbalance.** About 150,000 anchors for a handful of objects swamp an unweighted loss with easy negatives. The focal
  loss, the prior initialisation, or sampling a fixed ratio of positives to negatives is needed.
- **Thresholds and NMS interact.** Loose assignment produces many positives per object and more duplicates for NMS to
  remove; strict assignment starves small objects of positives.
- **Inconsistent coordinates.** Implementations differ on whether a box's width is $x_1 - x_0$ or $x_1 - x_0 + 1$ and
  on where a cell's centre lies. Encoding with one convention and decoding with another shifts every box.
- **Scores that ignore localisation.** A high class score on a badly placed box ranks above a lower score on a good
  box. Centre-ness, an IoU-aware score or a one-to-one head counters it.
