<Definition>
A segmentation decoder is the part of a <NoteLink to="semantic-segmentation">semantic segmentation</NoteLink> network
between the encoder (a classification backbone) and the per-pixel class scores. It takes one or more encoder
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">feature maps</NoteLink>, at output strides such as 4, 8, 16 or 32, and returns $K$
class scores at every pixel. Decoders differ in two choices: how they gather context wider than one feature-map cell
(atrous convolution at several rates, pooling over grids of several sizes, or fusion of several encoder levels), and how
they recover resolution (bilinear upsampling, or upsampling joined with fine encoder maps).
</Definition>

## Intuition

A pixel's label depends on context far beyond the pixel. A patch of grey is road in one scene and sky in another; a
boat and a car can look alike up close, and the water around the boat tells them apart. Placing the label boundary
needs the opposite: features at high resolution.

A <NoteLink to="fully-convolutional-network-and-u-net">fully convolutional network</NoteLink> gets context by
downsampling, which costs resolution. Segmentation decoders take one of three routes out. The DeepLab family stops
downsampling early and replaces the lost strides by atrous (dilated) convolutions, which keep the receptive field
growing without shrinking the map. It then adds a context module that looks at the map at several scales at once. PSPNet
pools the final map over grids of several sizes, so each pixel also sees summaries of its region and of the whole image.
<Gloss name="seq2seq">Encoder-decoder</Gloss> designs, U-Net among them, upsample in steps and pull in fine encoder maps
on the way. DeepLabv3+ combines a context module with one such refinement step.

## Details

### Output stride by atrous convolution

The output stride $s$ of an encoder is the ratio of input size to feature-map size. A <Gloss name="resnet" /> has
$s = 32$: a $513 \times 513$ input gives a $17 \times 17$ map. DeepLab removes the stride of the last stage, or of the
last two, and multiplies the dilation of every later $3 \times 3$ convolution by 2, or by 2 and then 4
<Cite id="chen2018deeplab" />. An atrous convolution with rate $r$ spaces its $k$ taps $r$ positions apart, so it covers
$r(k - 1) + 1$ positions with $k$ weights <Cite id="yu2016" /> (see
<NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution" />). The pretrained weights are kept: each dilated layer sees the
same relative pattern as before, on a map twice as dense. The map at $s = 16$ is $33 \times 33$ and at $s = 8$ is
$65 \times 65$. The later stages then run at 4 or 16 times as many positions, which is the price.

### Atrous spatial pyramid pooling

Objects appear at many scales. Atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) runs several branches in parallel on the final map
and concatenates them. In DeepLabv3 the branches are a $1 \times 1$ convolution, three $3 \times 3$ convolutions with
rates 6, 12 and 18 at output stride 16, and an image-level branch: <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">global average
pooling</NoteLink>, a $1 \times 1$ convolution, and bilinear upsampling back to the map. Each branch has 256 output
channels with <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink>, and a final $1 \times 1$ convolution
fuses the $5 \times 256 = 1280$ concatenated channels to 256 <Cite id="chen2017deeplab" />. At output stride 8 the rates
are doubled to 12, 24 and 36, so each branch spans the same number of input pixels.

The image-level branch exists because of padding. A $3 \times 3$ kernel with rate $r$ centred on cell $i$ of an $n$-cell
axis reads cells $i - r$, $i$ and $i + r$. Taps that fall outside the map read zero padding. As $r$ approaches $n$ the
outer taps are outside almost everywhere, and the $3 \times 3$ kernel degenerates to its centre tap, a $1 \times 1$
convolution <Cite id="chen2017deeplab" />. <NoteLink to="graph-pooling-and-readout">Global pooling</NoteLink> supplies
the image-wide context the largest rate was meant to give.

### Pyramid pooling

PSPNet's pyramid pooling module average-pools the final feature map into $1 \times 1$, $2 \times 2$, $3 \times 3$
and $6 \times 6$ bins <Cite id="zhao2017pspnet" />. A $1 \times 1$ convolution reduces each pooled map to $1/N$ of the
input channels, with $N = 4$ levels. Each is bilinearly upsampled to the map's size and concatenated with the map
itself. A pixel then carries its own features, a summary of the quarter, ninth and thirty-sixth of the image around it,
and a summary of the whole image. ASPP samples context at a few spacings; pyramid pooling averages over fixed regions.

### Decoders with fine encoder features

DeepLabv3+ adds a decoder to DeepLabv3 <Cite id="chen2018deeplabv3plus" />. The ASPP output at stride 16 is bilinearly
upsampled by 4. It is concatenated with the backbone's stride-4 features, first reduced by a $1 \times 1$ convolution to
48 channels so that they do not outweigh the 256 encoder channels. Two $3 \times 3$ convolutions refine the result,
and a final bilinear upsampling by 4 gives full resolution. The decoder improves boundaries most: the context comes from
ASPP and the location from the stride-4 skip.

Multi-scale fusion decoders take every level of a feature pyramid. The semantic branch of Panoptic FPN upsamples each
pyramid level to stride 4, adds them, and predicts from the sum <Cite id="kirillov2019fpn" />; see
<NoteLink to="feature-pyramid-network" />. SegFormer's decoder projects the four levels of a transformer encoder to one
width with per-pixel linear layers, upsamples them to stride 4, concatenates and fuses them with an <Gloss name="mlp" />
<Cite id="xie2021segformer" />. Its large encoder receptive field makes a context module unnecessary.

### Practice

Three designs cover most use. DeepLabv3+ with a ResNet or
<NoteLink to="efficient-convolutional-networks">MobileNet</NoteLink> encoder at output stride 16 is the common
convolutional baseline. A pretrained transformer or <NoteLink to="modern-convolutional-networks">ConvNeXt</NoteLink>
backbone with a light multi-level decoder is the usual choice for accuracy on natural scenes. Mask-classification models
such as Mask2Former predict a set of masks, each with a class, and produce semantic, instance and panoptic maps with one
architecture <Cite id="cheng2022" />; see <NoteLink to="instance-and-panoptic-segmentation" />. In medical images the
U-Net decoder remains the default. Output stride 16 is the usual training setting: stride 8 costs about four times as
much in the last stages for a small gain.

## Worked example

**Atrous rates on a $33 \times 33$ map.** A $513 \times 513$ crop at output stride 16 gives a map of
$(513 - 1)/16 + 1 = 33$ cells per side. One cell spans 16 input pixels. A $3 \times 3$ kernel with rate $r$ spans
$2r + 1$ cells, so the three ASPP branches span

$$
(2 \cdot 6 + 1) \cdot 16 = 208, \quad (2 \cdot 12 + 1) \cdot 16 = 400, \quad (2 \cdot 18 + 1) \cdot 16 = 592
$$

input pixels. The last span is larger than the image.

Along one axis with $n = 33$ cells indexed 0 to 32, a rate-18 kernel centred on cell $i$ reads $i - 18$ and $i + 18$.
The first is inside when $i \ge 18$ and the second when $i \le 14$. No cell satisfies both, so at most two of the three
taps per axis are ever inside, and at most $2 \times 2 = 4$ of the nine taps in two dimensions. On cells 15, 16 and 17
only the centre tap is inside along that axis. Counting all $33^2 = 1089$ positions, 82.6% see exactly four taps and
none sees nine. For rate 12, a cell sees all three taps along an axis when $12 \le i \le 20$, nine cells, so
$9^2 = 81$ positions, 7.4%, see all nine taps. For rate 6 the count is $21^2 = 441$, 40.5%.

**Cost of ASPP.** On a 2048-channel ResNet map, the $1 \times 1$ branch has $2048 \cdot 256 = 524{,}288$ weights and
each $3 \times 3$ branch $9 \cdot 2048 \cdot 256 = 4{,}718{,}592$. The pooling branch has another 524,288 and the fusion
$1280 \cdot 256 = 327{,}680$, for 15.5 million weights in all. The rate does not change the count. At $33 \times 33$
the module costs 16.3 billion multiply-accumulates; at output stride 8 the same weights run on $65 \times 65 = 4225$
positions and cost 63.4 billion, 3.9 times as much.

**DeepLabv3+ decoder.** The stride-4 map of the same crop is $(513 - 1)/4 + 1 = 129$ cells per side. The ASPP output,
256 channels at $33 \times 33$, is upsampled by 4 to $129 \times 129$ and concatenated with 48 reduced channels, giving
304. Two $3 \times 3$ convolutions to 256 channels then cost 21.7 billion multiply-accumulates, more than ASPP itself,
because they run at 16 times as many positions.

**Pyramid pooling.** PSPNet's encoder runs at output stride 8 <Cite id="zhao2017pspnet" />, so a $480 \times 480$ input
gives a map of $60 \times 60$ cells with 2048 channels. Its bins average blocks of $60 \times 60$, $30 \times 30$,
$20 \times 20$ and $10 \times 10$ cells. Each pooled map is reduced to $2048/4 = 512$ channels, and concatenating the
four with the original map gives $2048 + 4 \cdot 512 = 4096$ channels.

## Assumptions

- The encoder's features are already semantic at every position, so the decoder only adds context and resolution.
- Context helps uniformly: a pixel's class depends on its surroundings in a way a fixed set of spacings or regions
  captures. Long, thin structures across the image fit poorly in square pooling bins.
- The training crop is about the size of the test images; the rates and bin sizes are tuned to the map size the crop
  gives.

## Pitfalls

- **Rates too large for the map.** A rate close to the map size wastes the outer taps on padding. Choose rates for the
  feature-map size at the training crop, and double them when halving the output stride.
- **Gridding.** Stacking atrous layers with the same rate $r > 1$ samples the input on a sparse lattice and misses the
  pixels in between. Mixed rates, or a $1 \times 1$ branch alongside, fill the gaps.
- **Memory at small output stride.** Stride 8 multiplies the activations of the last stages by 4 relative to stride 16
  and by 16 relative to 32. Batch normalisation then sees small batches, and its statistics are noisy; see
  <NoteLink to="normalisation-in-convolutional-networks" />.
- **Misaligned upsampling.** Bilinear upsampling with and without aligned corners places samples differently, and a
  $33 \to 513$ resize that does not match the encoder's own sampling shifts the prediction by half a cell.
