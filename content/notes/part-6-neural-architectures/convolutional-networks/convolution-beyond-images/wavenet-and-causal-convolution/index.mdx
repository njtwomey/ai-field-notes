<Definition>
A **causal convolution** is a one-dimensional convolution whose output at time $t$ depends only on the inputs at times
$t, t - 1, \dots$. With kernel size $k$ and dilation $d$ it computes $y_t = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} w_i\, x_{t - id}$. A stack
of $L$ such layers with dilations $d_1, \dots, d_L$ has a receptive field of $1 + (k - 1)\sum_l d_l$ steps; with $k = 2$
and $d_l = 2^{l-1}$ that is $2^L$. **WaveNet** is an <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss> of raw audio,
$p(\xvec) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$, built from stacks of dilated causal convolutions with gated
activations, residual and skip connections, and a softmax over 256 quantised amplitude levels at every step
<Cite id="oord2016c" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An autoregressive model generates a sequence one value at a time, each drawn from a distribution conditioned on the
values before it. Training such a model means fitting $p(x_t \mid x_{<t})$ at every $t$ of every training sequence. A
convolutional network can compute all those conditionals in one parallel pass if no output reads an input from its own
future. A causal convolution enforces that rule by placing every tap at or before the output's time step. It plays the
role that the <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink> plays in a transformer decoder and that masked
kernels play in <NoteLink to="pixelcnn-and-masked-convolution">PixelCNN</NoteLink>.

Raw audio makes the receptive field the central problem. Speech is sampled at 16,000 values per second or more, and a
phoneme lasts about a tenth of a second, so a model that hears one phoneme back must see over a thousand samples. A
stack of undilated causal layers with kernel size 2 adds one step per layer, so a 1,000-step field would need 1,000
layers. A <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">dilated convolution</NoteLink> spaces its taps $d$
steps apart. Doubling the dilation at every layer doubles the field with every layer, so ten layers see 1,024 steps
with ten pairs of weights per channel pair.

## Details

### Causal convolution

A library convolution with kernel size $k$, dilation $d$ and no padding has output $t$ read inputs
$t, t + d, \dots, t + (k - 1)d$. Padding the sequence with $(k - 1)d$ zeros on the left only, and none on the right,
shifts every window back so that output $t$ reads inputs $t - (k - 1)d, \dots, t$. The output then has the input's
length, and output $t$ sits at time $t$. Writing the taps in reverse order of time gives the form
$y_t = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} w_i\, x_{t - id}$, where $w_0$ multiplies the current input. A layer that predicts $x_{t+1}$
from $y_t$ reads $x_t$ and earlier; a model that predicts $x_t$ itself shifts the targets by one step, so the first
layer never sees the value it predicts.

Symmetric padding, the default "same" padding of most libraries, puts half the zeros on the right. Output $t$ then reads
inputs up to $\lceil (k - 1)d/2 \rceil$ steps ahead, which breaks causality silently: the shapes are unchanged and
the training loss falls, because the network can copy the answer from the future.

### Dilation and the receptive field

Consider a unit at time $s$ in layer $L$ of a stack of causal layers with kernel size $k$. Layer $L$ reads layer
$L - 1$ at times $s - i_L d_L$ for $i_L \in \set{0, \dots, k - 1}$. Each of those reads layer $L - 2$ at a further
offset $i_{L-1} d_{L-1}$, and so on down to the input. The unit therefore depends on the inputs at

$$
s - \sum_{l=1}^{L} i_l\, d_l, \qquad i_l \in \set{0, 1, \dots, k - 1}.
$$

The smallest offset is 0, with every $i_l = 0$. The largest is $(k - 1)\sum_l d_l$, with every $i_l = k - 1$. The span
of inputs from the earliest to the current one is the receptive field,

$$
R = 1 + (k - 1)\sum_{l=1}^{L} d_l.
$$

Without dilation, $d_l = 1$ and $R = 1 + L(k - 1)$: one step per layer for $k = 2$. With $d_l = 2^{l-1}$ the sum is a
geometric series, $\sum_{l=1}^{L} 2^{l-1} = 2^L - 1$, so $R = 1 + (k - 1)(2^L - 1)$, which is $2^L$ for $k = 2$. The
weights grow linearly in $L$ and the field exponentially.

The span does not guarantee that every input inside it is read. For $k = 2$ and doubling dilations the offsets are
$\sum_l i_l 2^{l-1}$ with each $i_l \in \set{0, 1}$: the binary representations of $0, 1, \dots, 2^L - 1$. Every input
in the span is read, through exactly one path. Dilation 2 in every layer reaches only even offsets, and dilations
$1, 4, 16$ with $k = 2$ reach offsets $\set{0, 1, 4, 5, 16, 17, 20, 21}$ and leave gaps.

<Derivation title="When a dilation schedule leaves no gaps">
Let $S_l = (k - 1)\sum_{j \le l} d_j$ be the largest offset after $l$ layers. Suppose the offsets reachable after
$l - 1$ layers are all of $\set{0, 1, \dots, S_{l-1}}$. Layer $l$ adds $i\, d_l$ for $i = 0, \dots, k - 1$, so the
reachable set after $l$ layers is the union of the intervals $[i\, d_l,\ i\, d_l + S_{l-1}]$. Consecutive intervals
touch or overlap when $d_l \le S_{l-1} + 1$, and then the union is all of $\set{0, \dots, S_l}$. The first layer reaches
$\set{0, \dots, (k - 1)d_1}$ without gaps when $d_1 = 1$. By induction, a schedule with $d_1 = 1$ and
$d_l \le 1 + (k - 1)\sum_{j < l} d_j$ for every $l$ has no gaps. Doubling satisfies it for every $k \ge 2$, because
$2^{l-1} \le 1 + (k - 1)(2^{l-1} - 1)$. Quadrupling with $k = 2$ violates it at $l = 2$, since $4 > 1 + 1$.
</Derivation>

WaveNet doubles the dilation up to a limit and then starts again: $1, 2, 4, \dots, 512, 1, 2, 4, \dots, 512, \dots$
<Cite id="oord2016c" />. One block of ten layers has $R = 1 + (1 + 2 + \dots + 512) = 1024$, the span of a single
1,024-tap filter, with ten two-tap filters and a nonlinearity after each. Each further block adds 1,023 steps, so three
blocks see $1 + 3 \times 1023 = 3070$ samples. Repeating blocks adds depth and nonlinearity without letting the
dilation outgrow the sequence. The same schedule on images, with centred kernels, is the context module of Yu and
Koltun <Cite id="yu2016" />.

### WaveNet

WaveNet models a waveform $x_1, \dots, x_T$ by the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">chain rule of
probability</NoteLink>. A stack of dilated causal convolutions maps the waveform to a
<NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical distribution</NoteLink> over the next sample at every time step, and
training maximises the log-likelihood of the observed samples <Cite id="oord2016c" />. During training the true past is
fed in, so all $T$ conditionals come from one parallel pass, as in
<NoteLink to="teacher-forcing-and-exposure-bias">teacher forcing</NoteLink>.

**Quantisation.** Audio is stored as 16-bit integers, and a softmax over 65,536 values per step is costly and
data-hungry. WaveNet first compresses the amplitude $x \in (-1, 1)$ by μ-law companding <Cite id="itu1988g711" />,

$$
f(x) = \sgn(x)\, \frac{\ln(1 + \mu \abs{x})}{\ln(1 + \mu)}, \qquad \mu = 255,
$$

and quantises $f(x) \in (-1, 1)$ to 256 equal steps. The logarithm spends most levels on quiet sounds: the levels near
0 are about $1.7 \times 10^{-4}$ apart in $x$ and those near $\pm 1$ about $0.043$ apart, a ratio of $1 + \mu = 256$.
A softmax over 256 classes makes no assumption about the shape of the distribution, which can be skewed or have
several modes.

**Gated activation.** Each layer computes two dilated convolutions of its input $\hvec$, a filter and a gate, and
multiplies them elementwise:

$$
\zvec = \tanh(\Wmat_{f} * \hvec) \odot \sigma(\Wmat_{g} * \hvec),
$$

where $*$ is the dilated causal convolution, $\odot$ the elementwise product and $\sigma$ the logistic sigmoid. The
gate, between 0 and 1, decides how much of each filter channel passes. The authors found it modelled audio better than a
<Gloss name="relu" /> <Cite id="oord2016c" />. The same gating appears in the
<NoteLink to="gated-feed-forward-layers">gated linear units</NoteLink> of later <Gloss name="language-model">language
models</Gloss>.

**Residual and skip connections.** A $1 \times 1$ convolution of $\zvec$ is added to the layer's input and passed to
the next layer, a <NoteLink to="residual-network">residual connection</NoteLink>. A second $1 \times 1$ convolution
sends a copy to a skip sum. The output head adds the skip outputs of every layer, applies a ReLU, a $1 \times 1$
convolution, a ReLU and another $1 \times 1$ convolution, and ends with the softmax.

**Conditioning.** A conditional WaveNet models $p(\xvec \mid \hvec)$. A global condition, such as a speaker embedding,
is projected and added inside the filter and the gate at every time step. A local condition, such as linguistic features
or a <NoteLink to="mel-spectrogram">mel spectrogram</NoteLink> at a lower rate, is upsampled to the audio rate and added
the same way. Tacotron 2 used a WaveNet conditioned on predicted mel spectrograms as its vocoder and reached a
<NoteLink to="learned-speech-quality-metrics">mean opinion score</NoteLink> of 4.53, against 4.58 for recorded speech
<Cite id="shen2018tacotron2" />.

### Generation

Generation is sequential. Each sample is drawn from the softmax, appended to the input, and the network runs again for
the next. One second of 16 kHz audio takes 16,000 network evaluations, one after another.

Recomputing the whole receptive field for every new sample wastes work. For one block with $k = 2$ and $L$ layers, the
output unit at time $t$ needs 2 units of layer $L - 1$, 4 of layer $L - 2$ and $2^{L-1}$ of layer 1: $2^L - 1$ unit
evaluations, 1,023 for $L = 10$. All but one unit per layer were already computed at earlier steps. Fast WaveNet keeps,
for each layer, a queue of the last $d_l$ activations of the layer below, so each new sample costs one unit per layer:
$L$ evaluations, 10 instead of 1,023 <Cite id="paine2016" />. The queues hold
$\sum_l (k - 1) d_l = R - 1$ activations per channel.

Even with caching, sample-by-sample generation is slow on parallel hardware. Parallel WaveNet trains a feed-forward
student, an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-flows">inverse autoregressive flow</NoteLink>, to match a trained WaveNet, and
generates speech more than 20 times faster than real time <Cite id="oord2017parallel" />. WaveRNN replaced the stack
with a single recurrent layer optimised for sequential sampling <Cite id="kalchbrenner2018wavernn" />. Adversarially
trained vocoders such as HiFi-GAN generate all samples in parallel from a mel spectrogram, 167.9 times faster than real
time on one V100 <Gloss name="gpu" /> in their paper <Cite id="kong2020hifigan" />. Today's speech systems mostly use
such vocoders, or neural audio codecs whose discrete tokens a language model predicts <Cite id="defossez2022encodec" />.

### Beyond audio

Dilated causal convolution is a general sequence layer. The <Gloss name="tcn" /> keeps the dilated causal stack and the
<NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink> and drops the gating and skip connections; it
matched or beat <NoteLink to="recurrent-networks">recurrent networks</NoteLink> of similar size on standard sequence
benchmarks <Cite id="bai2018" />. See <NoteLink to="temporal-convolutional-networks">temporal convolutional
networks</NoteLink> for its use in forecasting. ByteNet stacked dilated convolutions for
<Gloss name="machine-translation">machine translation</Gloss>, with a <NoteLink to="decoder-only-transformers">causal
decoder</NoteLink> over characters <Cite id="kalchbrenner2016bytenet" />. State-space blocks such as
<NoteLink to="mamba">Mamba</NoteLink> place a short causal convolution, width 4 in the reference code, before their
recurrence <Cite id="gu2023mambacode" />. <NoteLink to="long-convolution-models">Long convolution models</NoteLink> take
the opposite extreme, with one causal filter as long as the sequence.

Against attention, a causal convolution stack has a fixed receptive field and a cost linear in the sequence length. A
causal transformer has an unbounded field and a cost quadratic in the length. A convolution that must see further has
to be rebuilt with more layers or larger dilations.

## Worked example

Take four causal layers with kernel size 2 and dilations $1, 2, 4, 8$, one channel, and in every layer the taps
$w_0 = 1$ on $x_t$ and $w_1 = 0.5$ on $x_{t-d}$, with no bias and no nonlinearity. So layer $l$ computes
$h^{(l)}_t = h^{(l-1)}_t + 0.5\, h^{(l-1)}_{t - d_l}$.

1. **Receptive field.** $R = 1 + (2 - 1)(1 + 2 + 4 + 8) = 16$. The output at $t = 15$ reads inputs $x_0$ to $x_{15}$.
2. **Paths.** Input $x_{15 - o}$ reaches $y_{15}$ by taking the delayed tap in exactly the layers whose dilations add
   up to $o$. For $o = 5 = 4 + 1$, the path takes the delayed tap in the layers with dilations 1 and 4 and the direct
   tap in the layers with dilations 2 and 8. Its coefficient is $0.5 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 1 = 0.25$.
3. **All coefficients.** The coefficient of $x_{15 - o}$ is $0.5^{b(o)}$, where $b(o)$ is the number of ones in the
   binary representation of $o$. For $o = 0, 1, \dots, 15$ the coefficients are
   $1, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.25, 0.25, 0.125, 0.5, 0.25, 0.25, 0.125, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125, 0.0625$. Their sum is
   $(1 + 0.5)^4 = 5.0625$, one factor per layer.
4. **Causality check.** Raise $x_{12}$ by 1 and leave the rest. Outputs $y_{12}, y_{13}, y_{14}, y_{15}$ change by
   the coefficients at offsets $0, 1, 2, 3$: $1, 0.5, 0.5, 0.25$. Output $y_{11}$ and every earlier output are
   unchanged, because no path runs from a later input to an earlier output.
5. **Weights.** The stack holds $4 \times 2 = 8$ weights. One causal filter with the same 16-step span would hold 16,
   and an undilated stack with $k = 2$ would need 15 layers.
6. **Quantisation.** An amplitude $x = 0.01$ gives $f(x) = \ln(1 + 2.55)/\ln 256 = 1.267/5.545 = 0.2285$. Mapping
   $(-1, 1)$ to levels $0, \dots, 255$ by $(f + 1)/2 \times 255 = 156.63$ and rounding gives level 157. Decoding level
   157 gives $x = 0.0102$, within 2.3% of the input. Linear 8-bit quantisation has a step of $2/255 = 0.0078$, 78% of
   the amplitude itself.

## Assumptions

- The sequence is ordered and values depend on the past. Causality is a modelling choice for generation and
  forecasting; a classifier that sees the whole sequence gains nothing from it.
- A fixed context of $R$ steps suffices. WaveNet's speech models had receptive fields of about 240 to 300 milliseconds
  <Cite id="oord2016c" />, which limits them to the last few phonemes; their prosody needed extra conditioning.
- The sampling grid is regular. A dilation of $d$ means a lag of $d$ samples only when samples are equally spaced.

## Pitfalls

- **Symmetric padding.** A library's "same" padding reads the future. The loss looks good in training and generation
  fails. Pad on the left only, and test by perturbing one input and checking that no earlier output changes.
- **Off-by-one targets.** If output $t$ reads $x_t$, it must predict $x_{t+1}$. Predicting $x_t$ from a stack whose
  first layer reads $x_t$ lets the network copy its input.
- **Gaps in the dilation schedule.** Constant dilations above 1, or dilations that grow faster than the field, leave
  inputs that no path reaches.
- **Slow generation.** Without cached activations, each new sample costs work exponential in the number of layers.
  Even with caching, generation runs one network evaluation per sample.
- **Field versus use.** A long receptive field does not mean the model uses distant inputs. Inputs reached by one path
  through several delayed taps can carry little weight, as the coefficients in the worked example show.
