<Definition>
A one-dimensional convolutional layer maps a sequence with $C$ channels and $n$ positions, an array of shape
$[C, n]$, to a sequence with $C'$ channels. Output channel $c'$ at position $i$ is a bias $b_{c'}$ plus the sum, over
the $C$ input channels and $k$ consecutive positions starting at $si - p$, of the input values times weights
$w_{c', c, u}$. The same $k \times C$ kernel is used at every position, so the layer is a bank of $C'$ learned
finite impulse response filters. It has $kCC' + C'$ parameters whatever the length $n$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Many signals are sequences of measurements on a regular grid in one dimension: an audio waveform at 16,000 samples per
second, an <Gloss name="ecg" /> at 360 samples per second, the three axes of an accelerometer at 50 samples per second,
or the word vectors of a sentence, one per word. The two assumptions behind the
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink> carry over from images unchanged
<Cite id="kiranyaz2019" />.

- **Locality.** A useful feature, such as a heartbeat, a footstep or a three-word phrase, occupies a short stretch of
  the sequence. Each output reads a window of $k$ positions.
- **Stationarity.** A feature means the same thing wherever it occurs. A heartbeat at second 3 has the same shape as a
  heartbeat at second 30. One kernel is applied at every position.

Each kernel is a template. Its output is large where the window of the input looks like the kernel and small
elsewhere, so a layer with $C'$ kernels is a bank of $C'$ detectors swept along the sequence. In signal processing the
same operation is a <NoteLink to="finite-impulse-response-filters">finite impulse response filter</NoteLink>, and a
filter designed to detect a known shape is the <NoteLink to="matched-filter">matched filter</NoteLink>. A
one-dimensional convolutional network learns its filters from labelled data instead of designing them.

A one-dimensional input still has channels. The three accelerometer axes are three channels. Each word vector of
dimension 300 is a column of a $[300, n]$ array, so a sentence of $n$ words has 300 channels. A kernel spans all input
channels and $k$ positions, so it detects patterns across channels as well as along time.

## Details

### The layer in full

Write the input as $x_{c, j}$ for channel $c \in \set{1, \dots, C}$ and position $j \in \set{0, \dots, n - 1}$, with
zeros outside that range after padding $p$ positions at each end. With stride $s$, output channel $c'$ at position $i$
is

$$
y_{c', i} = b_{c'} + \sum_{c=1}^{C} \sum_{u=0}^{k-1} w_{c', c, u}\; x_{c,\, si - p + u}.
$$

Libraries store a mini-batch of $N$ sequences as a tensor of shape $[N, C, n]$, the weights as $[C', C, k]$ and the
biases as a vector of length $C'$. The output has shape $[N, C', m]$ with $m = \lfloor (n + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1$, the
output-size formula of the convolutional layer along one axis. As there, the sum slides the kernel without flipping
it: deep-learning libraries compute a cross-correlation and call it convolution.

The layer has $kCC'$ weights and $C'$ biases, and each output value costs $kC$ multiply-accumulates. A full output
costs $kCC'm$ multiply-accumulates. A layer from $C = 3$ accelerometer channels to $C' = 16$ channels with $k = 5$ has
$5 \cdot 3 \cdot 16 + 16 = 256$ parameters, whether the recording lasts one second or one day. A dense layer from 1,000
time steps of 3 channels to 1,000 steps of 16 channels would have $3{,}000 \times 16{,}000 = 4.8 \times 10^7$ weights.

### Stacks, pooling and the receptive field

A network stacks layers with nonlinearities between them. Each layer widens the
<Gloss name="receptive-field" />, the stretch of input that can change one output, by $k - 1$ times the product of the
strides before it; <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive field and dilated
convolution</NoteLink> derives the recursion. Strides and pooling shorten the sequence and make later layers cheaper.
Dilation widens the field without shortening the sequence, which matters when every position needs an output;
<NoteLink to="wavenet-and-causal-convolution">WaveNet and causal convolution</NoteLink> develops it.

The feature encoder of wav2vec 2.0 shows the arithmetic on raw audio at 16 kHz <Cite id="baevski2020" />. It stacks
seven layers of 512 channels with kernel sizes $10, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2$ and strides $5, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2$. The total
stride is $5 \cdot 2^6 = 320$ samples, so it emits one vector every 20 milliseconds. The receptive field is

$$
r = 1 + 9 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 10 + 2 \cdot 20 + 2 \cdot 40 + 1 \cdot 80 + 1 \cdot 160 = 400
$$

samples, or 25 milliseconds: each term is a layer's $k - 1$ times the product of the strides before it. The encoder
turns a waveform into a sequence of short-time features, the job a
<NoteLink to="mel-spectrogram">mel spectrogram</NoteLink> does by hand.

A task that needs one label per sequence ends with a global pooling layer, the maximum or the mean of each channel over
all positions. The result has $C'$ numbers whatever $n$ is, so one network classifies sequences of any length. Global
max pooling also makes the output invariant to where in the sequence a pattern occurs.

### Learned filters and matched filters

A trained kernel often looks like the event it detects. The first gradient step shows why. Take one channel whose
output $z_t = b + \sum_u w_u\, x_{t - o + u}$ is passed through a sigmoid and trained by
<NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">binary cross-entropy</NoteLink> to equal a target $y_t \in [0, 1]$ at every
position $t$, where $o$ is the offset of the window. Write $\xvec_{(t)}$ for the window of $k$ inputs that output $t$
reads. The loss is $\ell = \sum_t \paren{\log(1 + e^{z_t}) - y_t z_t}$, and its gradient in the weights is

$$
\nabla_{\wvec}\, \ell = \sum_t \paren{\sigma(z_t) - y_t}\, \xvec_{(t)}.
$$

At $\wvec = \zeros$ every $z_t$ equals $b$, and the bias that minimises the loss there gives $\sigma(b) = \bar y$, the
mean target. The steepest-descent direction is then

$$
-\nabla_{\wvec}\, \ell = \sum_t (y_t - \bar y)\, \xvec_{(t)} = \sum_t y_t\, \paren{\xvec_{(t)} - \bar\xvec},
$$

where $\bar\xvec$ is the mean window. The second form follows because $\sum_t \bar y\, \xvec_{(t)} = \bar y\, n\,
\bar\xvec = \paren{\sum_t y_t}\, \bar\xvec$. With targets of 1 at the events and 0 elsewhere, the first step sets the
kernel to the average window at an event minus the average window. That is the template of the average event, the
matched filter for it in white noise <Cite id="turin1960" />.

Training does not stop there. Later steps also push down the output at every position where the kernel responds but
the target is zero: shifted copies of the event, and other events that resemble it. The trained kernel keeps the
template's main features and adds whatever separates the event from those look-alikes. Weight decay pulls it back
towards the template.

<Derivation title="Strong weight decay gives the matched filter">
Add a penalty $\lambda \norm{\wvec}^2$ to the loss, with $\lambda > 0$, and leave the bias unpenalised. At a minimiser
$\wvec_\lambda$ the gradient of the total is zero, so
$\wvec_\lambda = -\nabla_{\wvec}\, \ell(\wvec_\lambda) / (2\lambda)$. The gradient of $\ell$ is bounded on bounded sets,
so $\wvec_\lambda \to \zeros$ as $\lambda \to \infty$. By continuity
$\nabla_{\wvec}\, \ell(\wvec_\lambda) \to \nabla_{\wvec}\, \ell(\zeros)$, with the bias at its optimum for
$\wvec = \zeros$. Hence $2 \lambda \wvec_\lambda \to -\nabla_{\wvec}\, \ell(\zeros) = \sum_t y_t \paren{\xvec_{(t)} -
\bar\xvec}$: under strong weight decay the trained kernel points along the matched filter of the average event, and only
its length shrinks.
</Derivation>

### In practice

- **Time-series classification.** A fully convolutional network of three convolutional blocks, each a convolution,
  batch normalisation and a ReLU, followed by global average pooling, was a strong baseline on the UCR archive
  <Cite id="wang2016fcn" />. InceptionTime ensembles five networks whose blocks run kernels of lengths 10, 20 and 40 in
  parallel <Cite id="ismailfawaz2020" />. ROCKET skips training of the convolutions altogether: it applies 10,000
  random kernels with random lengths and dilations, keeps two numbers per kernel, the maximum and the proportion of
  positive outputs, and fits a linear classifier on them <Cite id="dempster2020" />. See
  <NoteLink to="time-series-classification">time-series classification</NoteLink>.
- **Medical signals.** A deep one-dimensional network trained on 91,232 single-lead ECGs from 53,549 patients
  classified 12 rhythm classes with an average F1 score above that of the average cardiologist on a test set labelled
  by expert consensus <Cite id="hannun2019" />.
- **Speech and audio.** Self-supervised speech encoders start with a strided convolutional stack on the raw waveform,
  as in wav2vec 2.0 above <Cite id="baevski2020" />. Whisper passes its log-mel spectrogram, 80 channels per frame,
  through two convolutions of width 3, the second with stride 2, before its transformer <Cite id="radford2023" />.
- **Text.** Kim's sentence classifier runs kernels of widths 3, 4 and 5 words, 100 of each, over pretrained word
  vectors and takes the maximum of each channel over the sentence <Cite id="kim2014cnn" />. Transformers have replaced
  such networks for most text tasks.
- **Inside sequence models.** Mamba applies a short causal depthwise convolution, width 4 in the reference code, to
  each channel before its state-space layer <Cite id="gu2023mambacode" />; see
  <NoteLink to="mamba">Mamba</NoteLink>. Depthwise means one kernel per channel, $k$ weights per channel instead of
  $kC'$.

## Worked example

Take one input channel of length $n = 12$,

$$
\xvec = (0,\ 1,\ 0,\ 0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 2,\ 1,\ 0,\ 0,\ 1),
$$

which holds a bump centred at position 6 and two isolated spikes. Use two kernels of size $k = 5$, stride 1, no padding
and zero bias: a bump template $\wvec^{(1)} = (1, 2, 3, 2, 1)$ and a slope detector
$\wvec^{(2)} = (-1, -1, 0, 1, 1)$. The output length is $m = 12 - 5 + 1 = 8$, and output $i$ reads positions $i$ to
$i + 4$.

1. **First output of the bump channel.** The window is $(0, 1, 0, 0, 1)$. The weighted sum is
   $1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 = 3$.
2. **The peak.** At $i = 4$ the window is $(1, 2, 3, 2, 1)$, the bump itself, and the sum is
   $1 + 4 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 19$.
3. **All eight outputs.** Sliding the window one position at a time gives

   $$
   \yvec^{(1)} = (3,\ 5,\ 10,\ 16,\ 19,\ 16,\ 10,\ 5).
   $$

   The maximum sits at $i = 4$, whose window is centred on position $i + 2 = 6$, the centre of the bump. The spikes at
   positions 1 and 11 give only 3 and 5: they match one tap each.
4. **The slope channel.** The kernel subtracts the two left values of the window from the two right values. At $i = 2$
   the window $(0, 0, 1, 2, 3)$ gives $-0 - 0 + 0 + 2 + 3 = 5$; at $i = 6$ the window $(3, 2, 1, 0, 0)$ gives
   $-3 - 2 + 0 + 0 + 0 = -5$. All eight outputs are

   $$
   \yvec^{(2)} = (0,\ 2,\ 5,\ 4,\ 0,\ -4,\ -5,\ -2),
   $$

   positive on the rising side of the bump, zero at its top and negative on the falling side.
5. **Equivariance.** Shift the input two positions to the right, keeping its length by dropping the last two values and
   adding two zeros at the start. The bump's centre moves to position 8, and the peak of $\yvec^{(1)}$ moves to $i = 6$
   with the same value 19. The layer reports the same feature at the shifted place.
6. **Parameters.** The layer holds $5 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 10$ weights and 2 biases. Applied to a sequence of a million
   samples it would hold the same 12 numbers.

## Assumptions

- The positions are equally spaced. Irregularly sampled series, such as clinical measurements taken when a nurse
  visits, break the meaning of a kernel tap as a fixed time lag.
- The statistics are roughly the same at every position. A kernel shared along a recording assumes a heartbeat looks
  alike at every time; a sequence whose position carries meaning, such as the fields of a fixed-format record, does not
  satisfy this.
- The patterns that matter are short compared with the receptive field. Dependencies longer than the field are
  invisible to the network.

## Pitfalls

- **Channel and time axes swapped.** Libraries expect $[N, C, n]$. A $[N, n, C]$ array passed unchanged convolves
  across channels and treats time as channels; the shapes often still fit.
- **Symmetric padding in forecasting.** A centred window reads future values. A model that predicts the next value must
  pad only on the left; see <NoteLink to="wavenet-and-causal-convolution">causal convolution</NoteLink>.
- **Sample rate.** A kernel of 25 taps spans 0.5 seconds at 50 Hz and 0.1 seconds at 250 Hz. A network trained at one
  rate does not transfer to another without resampling.
- **Receptive field shorter than the event.** A kernel shorter than a beat or a word sees only part of it. Count the
  receptive field in samples and in seconds before training.
- **Reading kernels.** A trained kernel is a discriminative filter, not a picture of the event. Its shape depends on
  what it had to reject and on the weight decay, as the figure shows.
