<Definition>
A three-dimensional <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink> maps a clip with $C$ channels, $T$
frames and $H \times W$ pixels, an array of shape $[C, T, H, W]$, to a clip with $C'$ channels. Each output value is a
bias plus the weighted sum of a $k_t \times k \times k$ window of every input channel, spanning $k_t$ frames and
$k \times k$ pixels, with weights shared across all positions in space and time. The layer has $k_t k^2 C C'$ weights
and costs $k_t$ times as much as a two-dimensional layer applied to every frame. Video networks use full 3-D kernels
(C3D), 3-D kernels inflated from a pretrained image network (I3D), a spatial convolution followed by a temporal one
($(2{+}1)$-D), or two 2-D streams on frames and optical flow.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A video is a stack of images, and an image network applied to each frame recognises what is in the frame. Many actions
cannot be told apart from single frames. Opening a door and closing it pass through the same frames in opposite orders;
a person standing up and sitting down do the same. The difference is motion, and motion lives in how pixels change from
frame to frame.

In a space-time volume a moving edge is a slanted surface. A still vertical bar traces a vertical stripe in the slice
through one row over time, and a bar moving right by one pixel per frame traces a stripe tilted by one pixel per frame.
Detecting motion is therefore detecting orientation in space and time <Cite id="adelson1985" />, and a kernel that spans
several frames can do it. A $k_t \times k \times k$ kernel whose taps follow a tilted line responds to motion at that
speed and direction, just as a 2-D kernel whose taps follow a line responds to edges at that angle.

Three families of design follow from that. Give the network motion explicitly, as optical flow, and keep 2-D kernels:
two-stream networks. Let the kernels span time: 3-D convolution. Or split each 3-D kernel into a spatial and a temporal
part to save computation: $(2{+}1)$-D and channel-separated convolution.

## Details

### The layer in full

Write the input as $x_{c, \tau, r, q}$ for channel $c$, frame $\tau$, row $r$ and column $q$. With zero padding and
strides $s_t$ in time and $s$ in space, output channel $c'$ at frame $i$, row $j$ and column $l$ is

$$
y_{c', i, j, l} = b_{c'} + \sum_{c=1}^{C} \sum_{a=0}^{k_t - 1} \sum_{u=0}^{k-1} \sum_{v=0}^{k-1}
w_{c', c, a, u, v}\; x_{c,\, s_t i - p_t + a,\, sj - p + u,\, sl - p + v}.
$$

Libraries store a mini-batch as $[N, C, T, H, W]$ and the weights as $[C', C, k_t, k, k]$. The output size follows the
usual formula separately along each of the three axes. The layer has $k_t k^2 C C' + C'$ parameters, and an output of
shape $[C', T', H', W']$ costs

$$
k_t\, k^2\, C\, C'\, T' H' W'
$$

multiply-accumulates per clip. Relative to a $1 \times k \times k$ layer on every frame, the 3-D layer multiplies both
the weights and the computation by $k_t$. Relative to one image, the clip multiplies the computation by $T'$. Both
factors make video networks expensive: a 16-frame clip through a $3 \times 3 \times 3$ layer costs 48 times the same
layer on one frame.

### Early designs and two-stream networks

Ji et al. built one of the first 3-D <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink> for action
recognition, on short stacks of frames from surveillance video <Cite id="ji2013" />. Karpathy et al. trained networks on
a million sports videos and compared ways to fuse time: one frame, frames stacked as channels in the first layer, two
distant frames fused late, and slow fusion of neighbouring frames through several layers. Slow fusion was best, but only
slightly better than the single-frame network <Cite id="karpathy2014video" />: the networks learned little motion.

Two-stream networks gave motion to the network directly <Cite id="simonyan2014twostream" />. A spatial stream, an image
network, sees one frame. A temporal stream sees the <NoteLink to="motion-representations">optical flow</NoteLink> of ten
consecutive frames, the horizontal and vertical displacement of every pixel stacked as 20 input channels. The two
streams' class scores are combined, by averaging or by a linear classifier. The temporal stream alone was more accurate
than the spatial stream on the action datasets of the time, which showed how much the task depends on motion. The cost
is the optical flow, computed by a separate algorithm before the network runs.

### C3D: full 3-D kernels

C3D applies $3 \times 3 \times 3$ kernels in every layer to clips of 16 frames at $112 \times 112$ pixels, with eight
convolutional layers and five pooling layers <Cite id="tran2015c3d" />. Among homogeneous temporal kernel sizes, 3 was
the best. Its features, fed to a linear classifier, transferred to several video tasks. C3D was trained from scratch on
video. The authors of I3D argued that such networks were held back by the small labelled video datasets of the time,
while image networks could start from <Gloss name="imagenet" /> <Cite id="carreira2017" />.

### I3D: inflating a pretrained image network

I3D starts from a 2-D image network and inflates every $k \times k$ kernel into a $k \times k \times k$ kernel by
repeating it $k$ times along time and dividing by $k$ <Cite id="carreira2017" />. Inflation preserves the image
network's function on a still video. Let $\tilde w_{a, u, v} = w_{u, v}/k_t$ for $a = 0, \dots, k_t - 1$, and let every
frame of the clip equal one image $x$. Then

$$
\sum_{a=0}^{k_t - 1} \sum_{u, v} \tilde w_{a, u, v}\, x_{\tau + a,\, j + u,\, l + v}
= \sum_{a=0}^{k_t - 1} \frac{1}{k_t} \sum_{u, v} w_{u, v}\, x_{j + u,\, l + v}
= \sum_{u, v} w_{u, v}\, x_{j + u,\, l + v},
$$

the 2-D layer's output on the image. Pooling and nonlinearities act on each position, so the whole inflated network
reproduces the image network on such a clip. Training on video then changes the kernels along time and learns motion
from a starting point that already recognises objects. Carreira and Zisserman also introduced the Kinetics dataset; see
<NoteLink to="video-datasets">video datasets</NoteLink>. Their best model combined an inflated RGB network with an
inflated optical-flow network, two streams of 3-D convolutions.

### (2+1)-D and channel-separated convolution

A $(2{+}1)$-D block replaces a $k_t \times k \times k$ convolution from $C$ to $C'$ channels with a
$1 \times k \times k$ spatial convolution to $M$ channels, a nonlinearity, and a $k_t \times 1 \times 1$
<NoteLink to="one-dimensional-convolutional-networks">temporal convolution</NoteLink> to $C'$ channels
<Cite id="tran2018r21d" />. The block has $k^2 C M + k_t M C'$ weights. Tran et al. chose

$$
M = \left\lfloor \frac{k_t k^2 C C'}{k^2 C + k_t C'} \right\rfloor,
$$

so that the block has about as many weights as the 3-D layer it replaces. The factorisation keeps the parameter count
and doubles the number of nonlinearities. It also made training easier: R(2+1)D networks reached lower training error
than 3-D networks of the same depth and size. S3D found that 3-D or separable temporal convolutions help most in the
upper layers, and that the lower layers can stay 2-D at little loss in accuracy <Cite id="xie2018s3d" />.

A channel-separated layer goes further and factorises across channels: a depthwise $k_t \times k \times k$
convolution, one kernel per channel, followed by a $1 \times 1 \times 1$ convolution that mixes channels, as in
<NoteLink to="depthwise-and-grouped-convolution">depthwise separable convolution</NoteLink> for images
<Cite id="tran2019csn" />. X3D builds efficient video networks from such layers by expanding a small 2-D network one
axis at a time, frames, frame rate, resolution, width or depth, keeping whichever step buys the most accuracy per unit
of computation <Cite id="feichtenhofer2020x3d" />. SlowFast runs two pathways: a slow one at a low frame rate with many
channels for appearance, and a fast one at 8 times the frame rate with an eighth of the channels for motion, joined by
<NoteLink to="feature-pyramid-network">lateral connections</NoteLink> <Cite id="feichtenhofer2019" />.

### Practice

Since about 2021 the most accurate video recognition models are transformers that attend over space-time patches.
TimeSformer alternates attention over time and over space in each block <Cite id="bertasius2021timesformer" />, ViViT
factorises space and time in several ways <Cite id="arnab2021vivit" />, and VideoMAE pretrains such models by masking 90
to 95% of space-time patches and reconstructing them <Cite id="tong2022videomae" />. The tubelet embedding of ViViT and
VideoMAE, which cuts the clip into blocks of a few frames by $16 \times 16$ pixels, is itself a 3-D convolution with a
stride equal to its kernel. 3-D and $(2{+}1)$-D convolutional networks remain in use where computation is tight, such as
X3D-style models on devices, and as the backbone of many action-detection and video-segmentation systems.
<NoteLink to="video-diffusion-models">Video diffusion models</NoteLink> compress clips with
<NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoders</NoteLink> over space and time.

How frames are sampled from a long video matters as much as the layer type; see
<NoteLink to="clip-sampling">clip and frame sampling</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Take a 16-frame clip at $112 \times 112$ pixels, the input size of C3D, and count with same padding and stride 1.

1. **First layer.** C3D's first layer maps $C = 3$ colour channels to $C' = 64$ with $3 \times 3 \times 3$ kernels.
   It has $3 \cdot 9 \cdot 3 \cdot 64 = 5{,}184$ weights and 64 biases, 5,248 parameters. The output has shape
   $[64, 16, 112, 112]$, $1.28 \times 10^7$ values, and each costs $3 \cdot 9 \cdot 3 = 81$ multiply-accumulates. The
   layer costs $81 \cdot 64 \cdot 16 \cdot 112^2 = 1.04 \times 10^9$ multiply-accumulates per clip.
2. **A middle layer, 3-D.** After a $2 \times 2$ spatial pooling the clip is $16 \times 56 \times 56$. A
   $3 \times 3 \times 3$ layer from 64 to 128 channels has $27 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 = 221{,}184$ weights, 221,312
   parameters with biases, and costs $221{,}184 \cdot 16 \cdot 56^2 = 1.11 \times 10^{10}$ multiply-accumulates.
3. **The same layer per frame.** A $1 \times 3 \times 3$ kernel has $9 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 = 73{,}728$ weights and costs
   $3.70 \times 10^9$ multiply-accumulates: a third of the 3-D layer, because $k_t = 3$.
4. **(2+1)-D.** The matched width is $M = \lfloor 3 \cdot 9 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 / (9 \cdot 64 + 3 \cdot 128) \rfloor =
   \lfloor 221{,}184 / 960 \rfloor = 230$. The spatial part has $9 \cdot 64 \cdot 230 = 132{,}480$ weights and the
   temporal part $3 \cdot 230 \cdot 128 = 88{,}320$, 220,800 in all, within 0.2% of the 3-D layer. The cost is
   $220{,}800 \cdot 16 \cdot 56^2 = 1.11 \times 10^{10}$ multiply-accumulates, the same as the 3-D layer, with a
   <Gloss name="relu" /> between the two parts.
5. **Channel-separated.** The depthwise $3 \times 3 \times 3$ part has $27 \cdot 64 = 1{,}728$ weights and the
   $1 \times 1 \times 1$ part $64 \cdot 128 = 8{,}192$. Together they cost $9{,}920 \cdot 16 \cdot 56^2 = 4.98 \times
   10^8$ multiply-accumulates, 4.5% of the 3-D layer.
6. **Memory.** The middle layer's output holds $128 \cdot 16 \cdot 56^2 = 6.4 \times 10^6$ values per clip. Training
   stores such activations for every layer, so batch sizes for video are far smaller than for images.

## Assumptions

- The frames are equally spaced in time. A temporal kernel tap means a fixed lag only at a fixed frame rate; clips
  sampled at another stride see motion at another speed.
- Motion is local. A $k_t \times k \times k$ kernel sees displacements of at most about $k$ pixels over $k_t$ frames;
  faster motion needs pooling, strides or a lower frame rate to fit in the kernel.
- Statistics are the same at every position in space and time, as for any convolution.

## Pitfalls

- **Temporal kernels that are too short for the frame rate.** At 30 frames per second, a stack of $3 \times 3 \times 3$
  layers with no temporal pooling covers a fraction of a second. Long actions need temporal downsampling or sampling
  frames further apart.
- **Background bias.** Many actions are recognisable from the scene alone: a swimming pool implies swimming. A network
  can score well on such datasets with almost no motion modelling. Datasets built so that motion matters, such as
  Something-Something, test it.
- **Cost of optical flow.** Two-stream accuracy depends on flow computed by a separate, often slow, algorithm. The flow
  must be computed again at test time.
- **Memory.** Activations grow with $T$. Halving the frames or the resolution is often the only way to fit a batch.
- **Inflation at the borders.** An inflated kernel reproduces the image network only away from the first and last
  frames, where zero padding enters the temporal window.
