<Definition>
A **physics-informed neural network** (<Gloss name="pinn" />) represents the solution of a differential equation
$\mathcal{N}[u] = 0$ by a neural network $u_{\thetavec}(x, t)$ that takes the coordinates as input and returns the value
of the solution. Its parameters $\thetavec$ minimise the mean squared **residual** $\mathcal{N}[u_{\thetavec}]$ at a set
of **collocation points** inside the domain, plus the mean squared misfit to the initial values, the boundary values and
any observations. The derivatives inside the residual are derivatives of the network with respect to its inputs, taken
by <Gloss name="autodiff">automatic differentiation</Gloss> <Cite id="raissi2019" /> <Cite id="lagaris1998" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A differential equation states how a function changes, not what the function is. A classical solver turns the equation
into arithmetic on a grid and steps through it. A physics-informed network takes a different route: it picks a flexible
family of functions, a <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">multilayer perceptron</NoteLink>, and searches that family
for a member that breaks the equation as little as possible.

The search needs a measure of how badly a candidate breaks the equation. The residual provides it. For the damped
oscillator $x'' + 2\zeta\omega_0 x' + \omega_0^2 x = 0$, the residual of a candidate $x_{\thetavec}(t)$ is
$r(t) = x_{\thetavec}''(t) + 2\zeta\omega_0 x_{\thetavec}'(t) + \omega_0^2 x_{\thetavec}(t)$. The residual is zero at
every $t$ exactly when the candidate solves the equation. Squaring it and averaging over many points $t_i$ gives a loss
that a gradient method can drive down.

The equation acts as a constraint on the model family. Observations pin the network down only where they are; between
and beyond them a network trained on data alone follows whatever its architecture and initial weights favour. The
residual term is enforced wherever there are collocation points, and collocation points need no labels, so they can
cover the whole domain. Of all the functions the network can represent, the loss favours those that obey the equation
everywhere and pass through the data.

The equation alone does not single out one function. A linear homogeneous equation of order $k$ has a $k$-dimensional
family of solutions, and the residual is zero on all of them, including $u = 0$. The initial values, boundary values or
observations choose the member. The
<NoteLink to="physics-informed-network-for-a-damped-oscillator">damped oscillator example</NoteLink> shows the family,
the choice and the training side by side.

## The loss

Take an equation $\mathcal{N}[u](x, t) = 0$ for $x$ in a spatial domain $\Omega$ and $t \in (0, T]$, an initial profile
$u(x, 0) = u_0(x)$ and boundary values $u(x, t) = g(x, t)$ for $x$ on the boundary $\partial\Omega$. The loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(\thetavec) ={} & \frac{1}{N_r} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \mathcal{N}[u_{\thetavec}](x_i, t_i)^2
  + \frac{\lambda_0}{N_0} \sum_{j=1}^{N_0} \paren{u_{\thetavec}(x_j, 0) - u_0(x_j)}^2 \\
  & + \frac{\lambda_b}{N_b} \sum_{k=1}^{N_b} \paren{u_{\thetavec}(x_k, t_k) - g(x_k, t_k)}^2
  + \frac{\lambda_d}{N_d} \sum_{l=1}^{N_d} \paren{u_{\thetavec}(x_l, t_l) - y_l}^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

- $(x_i, t_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, N_r$, are the collocation points inside the domain. They are chosen by the modeller and
  carry no labels: a grid, uniform random draws, or a low-discrepancy set such as a Sobol sequence, which covers the
  domain more evenly than random draws.
- $(x_j, 0)$ are points on the initial line and $(x_k, t_k)$ points on the boundary.
- $(x_l, t_l, y_l)$ are observations, when there are any.
- $\lambda_0, \lambda_b, \lambda_d \ge 0$ weight the condition and data terms against the residual term.

A loss of zero means the network satisfies the equation at the collocation points and the conditions at their points.
It says nothing directly about the network between the points. A smooth network that obeys the equation at densely
spread points also obeys it closely between them, which is why the points must be dense relative to the scale on which
the solution varies.

The loss is the <NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">quadratic penalty method</NoteLink> applied to a constrained
problem: the equation and the conditions are constraints, and they enter the objective as squared violations rather
than being imposed exactly. The weights $\lambda$ play the part of penalty parameters.

## How the network is handled

**Input and output.** The network takes the coordinates, $t$ for an <Gloss name="ode">ordinary differential
equation</Gloss> or $(x, t)$ for a partial one, and returns $u$. One network represents the whole solution as a
continuous function: it can be evaluated at any point, not only on a grid, and it is differentiable everywhere the
activation is.

**Derivatives in the inputs.** The residual needs $u_t$, $u_x$, $u_{xx}$ and so on at every collocation point. They are
computed by automatic differentiation of the network with respect to its inputs, exact up to rounding, with no
<Gloss name="finite-difference">finite-difference</Gloss> step to choose <Cite id="baydin2018" />. A network applied row
by row to an $n \times d$ matrix of points is pointwise: row $i$ of the output depends only on row $i$ of the input. One
<NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">forward-mode</NoteLink> pass with the tangent $\evec_k$ in every row therefore
gives $\partial u / \partial x_k$ at all $n$ points at once. A second derivative nests two such passes, so a derivative
of order $p$ costs about $2^p$ evaluations of the network. Layers that mix rows, such as
<NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink>, break the pointwise property and give sums of
derivatives instead.

**Derivatives in the weights.** The residual is built from $u_{\thetavec}$ and its input derivatives, and every one of
them depends on $\thetavec$. Training differentiates the loss with respect to $\thetavec$ by reverse mode, which passes
through the input derivatives as well: a derivative of a derivative. One training step therefore costs a few times as
much as a step of plain regression with the same network.

**Scaling.** The network trains best when its inputs lie in about $[-1, 1]$ and the terms of the residual have similar
sizes. Inputs are mapped affinely onto $[-1, 1]$. The equation is divided by a characteristic scale, so that it is
written in dimensionless variables. In the oscillator, $\omega_0^2 x$ is 225 times $x$ for $\omega_0 = 15$; dividing
the residual by $\omega_0^2$ gives $x''/\omega_0^2 + (2\zeta/\omega_0) x' + x$, whose three terms are all of the size
of $x$. Without the rescaling the residual term dwarfs the data term and its gradient is dominated by the
$\omega_0^2 x$ part.

## Which networks work

**Smoothness.** The network must have as many derivatives as the equation, and they must be informative. The
hyperbolic tangent, sine, softplus, <Gloss name="gelu" /> and <Gloss name="silu" /> are infinitely differentiable. A
<Gloss name="relu" /> network is piecewise linear: its second derivative is zero everywhere except at the kinks, where
it does not exist. A residual that contains $u_{xx}$ or $x''$ sees none of a ReLU network's curvature, and the gradient
that should shape the curvature is zero. Networks with a smooth, bounded, nonconstant activation can approximate a
smooth function and its derivatives together, uniformly on compact sets <Cite id="hornik1991" />, so the family is
rich enough in principle.

**Spectral bias.** <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on a network fits low frequencies before
high ones <Cite id="rahaman2019" />. A solution with many oscillations in the domain, or with a sharp front such as a
shock, therefore converges slowly, and training may stall on a smooth approximation. Three remedies change the family
rather than the optimiser: <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">random Fourier features</NoteLink> of the input, $\paren{\cos(2\pi\bvec^\top\xvec),
\sin(2\pi\bvec^\top\xvec)}$ with random frequencies $\bvec$ <Cite id="tancik2020" />; sine activations with scaled
first-layer weights <Cite id="sitzmann2020" />; and splitting the domain into pieces, each with its own small network.

**Size.** The solution is a function of a few coordinates, so small networks suffice. Raissi et al. used fully
connected networks of 4 to 9 layers with 20 to 200 units per layer <Cite id="raissi2019" />. Each extra unit adds to
the cost of every input derivative, which is paid at every collocation point on every step.

**Hard constraints.** The conditions can be built into the family instead of penalised. For an oscillator with
$x(0) = x_0$ and $x'(0) = v_0$, the trial function

$$
x_{\thetavec}(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + t^2 \, N_{\thetavec}(t),
$$

with $N_{\thetavec}$ a network, meets both conditions for every $\thetavec$, because the $t^2$ term and its derivative
vanish at $t = 0$ <Cite id="lagaris1998" />. The condition terms and their weights then disappear from the loss, and
only the residual is trained. The construction is easy for simple domains and conditions and hard for irregular
boundaries.

## Training

**Adam, then L-BFGS.** The usual recipe runs <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> <Cite id="kingma2015" /> for a few
hundred to a few thousand steps and then switches to full-batch <Gloss name="l-bfgs" /> <Cite id="liu1989" />
<Cite id="raissi2019" />. The collocation set is fixed and the loss is deterministic and smooth, the setting in which a
<NoteLink to="quasi-newton-methods">quasi-Newton method</NoteLink> with a <NoteLink to="line-search">line
search</NoteLink> converges much faster than a first-order method near a minimum. Adam first moves the weights away from
the flat region around the initial weights, where curvature estimates are poor.

**Balancing the terms.** The residual and condition terms have different scales and different gradient magnitudes.
When the residual's gradient dominates, training finds a network that obeys the equation but not the conditions: a
different member of the solution family, often $u \approx 0$. The weights $\lambda$ can be set by hand, after
rescaling, or adapted during training from the relative sizes of the terms' gradients <Cite id="wang2021pinn" />.

**Time and causality.** In a time-dependent problem the residual at late times can be driven down before the solution
at early times is right. The network then settles on a function that obeys the equation locally but descends from the
wrong initial state. Weighting the residual at each time by how well the residual at earlier times is already met
restores the order in which a time-stepping solver would work <Cite id="wang2024causality" />. Training on a window that
grows in time has the same effect.

**Failure modes.** For some equations, such as convection with a large velocity, the loss surface becomes hard enough
that training fails even though a network close to the solution exists. Starting from an easy version of the equation
and moving gradually to the hard one, or training on successive time windows, recovers the solution
<Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" />.

## Forward and inverse problems

In a **forward problem** the equation, its coefficients and its conditions are known and the solution is wanted. In an
**inverse problem** a coefficient is unknown, such as a damping ratio $\zeta$, a viscosity $\nu$ or a diffusivity
$\kappa$, and observations of the solution are given. A physics-informed network treats the unknown coefficient as one
more trainable parameter next to $\thetavec$ and minimises the same loss <Cite id="raissi2019" />. A classical solver
needs an outer optimisation loop that solves the equation once per trial value; the network fits the solution and the
coefficient together.

## Compared with classical solvers

For a forward problem with a known equation on a simple domain, classical methods are faster and more accurate:
<NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers">Runge–Kutta</NoteLink> for ordinary equations, finite differences and finite
elements for partial ones. A 2024 comparison on Poisson problems in one to three dimensions, the Allen–Cahn equation and
a semilinear Schrödinger equation found that physics-informed networks did not beat finite elements in solution time or
accuracy; in some cases the trained networks were faster to evaluate <Cite id="grossmann2024" />. Classical solvers come
with error estimates and convergence orders; a physics-informed network's error is not controlled by any step size and
must be checked against a reference.

Physics-informed networks are most useful where classical solvers are awkward:

- inverse problems, where the coefficient and the solution are fitted together;
- scattered or partial data combined with a known or partly known equation;
- problems without a convenient mesh, since collocation points need no connectivity;
- high-dimensional problems, where a grid grows exponentially with dimension but collocation points do not have to.

Surveys of the field are Karniadakis et al. <Cite id="karniadakis2021" /> and Cuomo et al. <Cite id="cuomo2022" />.

## Assumptions

- The equation is known, up to the coefficients to be fitted.
- The solution is smooth enough to have the derivatives in the equation, and the network's activation is at least that
  smooth.
- The collocation points are dense relative to the scale on which the solution varies.
- The network is pointwise in its inputs, so that batched input derivatives are per point.

## Pitfalls

- **ReLU with a second-order equation.** The network's second derivative is zero almost everywhere, so the residual
  cannot see the network's curvature.
- **The residual alone.** Without condition or data terms, or with their weights too small, the network converges to
  the trivial or another unwanted member of the solution family.
- **Unscaled terms.** A residual in physical units can be hundreds of times larger than the data term, and training
  then serves the largest term only.
- **Too few collocation points.** The network obeys the equation at the points and does as it likes between them. For
  an oscillation, fewer than a few points per swing is too few.
- **Reading the loss as the error.** A small loss means the equation and conditions nearly hold at the training points.
  It is not the distance to the true solution, which has to be measured against a reference solution.
- **Layers that mix rows.** Batch normalisation and attention across the batch corrupt the batched input derivatives.
