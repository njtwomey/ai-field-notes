<Definition>
A **deep belief network** (DBN) is a generative model of data $\vvec$ with <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">hidden
layers</NoteLink> $\hvec^{(1)}, \dots,
\hvec^{(L)}$ of binary units. The top two layers form a <NoteLink to="restricted-boltzmann-machine">restricted Boltzmann
machine</NoteLink>, an undirected model $p(\hvec^{(L-1)}, \hvec^{(L)})$. Every lower layer is generated from the layer
above by directed sigmoid connections, $p(h^{(l)}_i = 1 \mid \hvec^{(l+1)}) = \sigma(b^{(l)}_i + \sum_j
W^{(l+1)}_{ij} h^{(l+1)}_j)$, with $\hvec^{(0)} = \vvec$. It is trained greedily, one RBM per layer, from the bottom up
<Cite id="hinton2006dbn" />.
</Definition>

## Greedy layer-wise training

Deep networks were hard to train in the early 2000s: <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> through many sigmoid
layers from small random weights were weak, and <NoteLink to="deep-generative-models">deep generative models</NoteLink>
with many hidden layers had intractable posteriors. Hinton, Osindero and Teh avoided both problems by never training
more than one layer at a time <Cite id="hinton2006dbn" />:

1. Train an RBM on the data $\vvec$ with <Gloss name="contrastive-divergence">contrastive divergence</Gloss>. Keep its
   weights $\Wmat^{(1)}$.
2. Pass each training vector up through the RBM to get the hidden probabilities $p(\hvec^{(1)} \mid \vvec)$, or
   samples from them. Treat these as data for the next layer.
3. Train a second RBM on them, and repeat for as many layers as wanted.
4. Keep the last RBM as an undirected top-level model; turn the weights of every lower RBM into directed generative
   connections pointing down, and use the same weights upwards as an approximate recognition model.

<DeepBeliefNetDiagram />

To generate a sample, run block Gibbs sampling in the top RBM for many steps, then pass the result down through the
directed layers with one sample per layer. To recognise, pass the data up with the recognition weights.

## Why adding a layer helps

Adding a layer can be justified with a variational bound. For any distribution $q(\hvec \mid \vvec)$ over the first
hidden layer, <NoteLink to="expectation">Jensen's inequality</NoteLink> applied to the concave logarithm gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(\vvec) &= \log \sum_{\hvec} q(\hvec \mid \vvec)\, \frac{p(\hvec)\, p(\vvec \mid \hvec)}{q(\hvec \mid \vvec)} \\
&\ge \sum_{\hvec} q(\hvec \mid \vvec) \brack{\log p(\hvec) + \log p(\vvec \mid \hvec) - \log q(\hvec \mid \vvec)},
\end{aligned}
$$

the <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink>. After the first RBM is trained, take
$p(\vvec \mid \hvec)$ and $q(\hvec \mid \vvec)$ to be its two conditionals, and $p(\hvec)$ its marginal on the hidden
layer. For this model $q$ is the exact posterior, so the bound equals $\log p(\vvec)$.

Now hold $p(\vvec \mid \hvec)$ and $q$ fixed and replace the prior $p(\hvec)$ with a better model of the hidden
activities. Only the term $\sum_{\hvec} q(\hvec \mid \vvec)\log p(\hvec)$ changes. Averaged over the training data it is
the log-likelihood of the new prior on the hidden vectors that $q$ produces from the data, which is exactly what the
second RBM is trained to maximise. If the second RBM starts with the transposed weights of the first,
$\Wmat^{(2)} = {\Wmat^{(1)}}^\top$, its marginal is the first RBM's $p(\hvec)$, so the bound starts at the old
log-likelihood. Any improvement in the second RBM's likelihood then raises the bound on $\log p(\vvec)$. In practice
layers differ in size and contrastive divergence is approximate, so the guarantee holds only in this idealised form, but
it explains why stacking does not undo the work of the layers below <Cite id="hinton2006dbn" />.

## Fine-tuning and the revival of deep learning

The greedy stack is a starting point. Hinton, Osindero and Teh fine-tuned it as a generative model with an up-down
variant of the wake-sleep algorithm, and reached 1.25% error on <Gloss name="mnist" /> digit classification by modelling
digits and labels jointly <Cite id="hinton2006dbn" />. More often the pretrained weights initialise a feed-forward
network that is then fine-tuned with backpropagation on a supervised task. The same year, Hinton and Salakhutdinov
pretrained a deep <NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink> layer by layer with RBMs and obtained codes far
better than <Gloss name="pca">principal component analysis</Gloss> <Cite id="hinton2006" />, and Bengio and colleagues
showed that greedy layer-wise pretraining, with RBMs or with autoencoders, helped deep networks generalise
<Cite id="bengio2007" />.

These results made depth trainable, and within a few years pretrained deep networks replaced <Gloss name="gmm">Gaussian
mixture models</Gloss> as the acoustic models of large speech recognisers <Cite id="hinton2012speech" />. Unsupervised
pretraining then faded from supervised practice. <Gloss name="relu">Rectified linear units</Gloss>, careful
<NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">weight initialisation</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> and
much larger labelled datasets let networks train from random weights directly. The <NoteLink to="boltzmann-machine">deep
Boltzmann machine</NoteLink>, which keeps every layer undirected, is the fully undirected relative of the DBN
<Cite id="salakhutdinov2009dbm" />.

## Assumptions

- **Each layer is a good RBM.** The bound argument needs each RBM to model the hidden activities of the layer below;
  poorly trained layers pass their errors up.
- **The recognition weights are approximate.** Using the generative weights upwards ignores the influence of the
  layers above on each posterior, the "explaining away" that a directed model implies.

## Pitfalls

- **Likelihood is hard to evaluate.** Neither the DBN's likelihood nor its posterior is tractable; comparisons use
  annealed <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> estimates or downstream task performance.
- **Pretraining is not required for depth today.** Modern initialisation and normalisation make it unnecessary for
  most supervised tasks; the DBN matters mainly for its history and for the idea of learning features without labels.
