<Definition>
A **feedforward network** computes its output from its input through a fixed sequence of layers, with no loops. Each
layer applies an affine map followed by an elementwise nonlinearity, $\hvec_\ell = \phi(\Wmat_\ell\hvec_{\ell-1} +
\bvec_\ell)$. The foundations cover what a single unit computes, what stacking units adds, and which functions such
networks can represent <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The multilayer perceptron is the simplest deep model and a component of nearly every larger one. The feed-forward
sublayer of a transformer, the prediction heads of a convolutional network and the update functions of a
<Gloss name="gnn">graph neural network</Gloss> are all small multilayer perceptrons. On tabular data a multilayer
perceptron is used directly for regression and classification. The ideas here, nonlinearity, depth and the role of the
activation's derivative, explain why deep networks can fit complex functions and why training them needs the care
described in <NoteLink to="training-deep-networks" />.

## Main ideas

**One unit is a linear classifier.** The <NoteLink to="perceptron" /> computes $\sgn(\wvec^\top\xvec)$ and learns by
adding misclassified examples to its weights. On linearly separable data it stops after a bounded number of mistakes.
It cannot represent XOR, which motivates hidden layers.

**Stacking layers needs a nonlinearity.** A <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron" /> alternates affine maps and
elementwise nonlinearities. Without the nonlinearity the composition of affine maps is one affine map, so depth would
add nothing. The note works XOR with two hidden units and connects the output layer to the loss: a sigmoid output with
cross-entropy is <NoteLink to="logistic-regression" /> on learned features.

**The activation shapes the gradient.** <NoteLink to="activation-functions" /> compares sigmoid, tanh, ReLU and its
variants, GELU and SiLU. During <NoteLink to="backpropagation" /> each gradient is multiplied by the activation's
derivative. Where that derivative is near zero, as in the saturated tails of the sigmoid, learning stalls. ReLU and its
smooth successors avoid saturation for positive inputs, which is why they replaced the sigmoid in deep networks.

**One hidden layer can approximate anything, in principle.** The <NoteLink to="universal-approximation-theorem" />
states that a single hidden layer with a non-polynomial activation can approximate any continuous function on a
compact set to any accuracy. It says nothing about how many units are needed or how to find the weights.

**Depth is exponentially more efficient for some functions.** <NoteLink to="depth-versus-width" /> shows that a deep
ReLU network can compute functions with exponentially many linear pieces, such as an iterated sawtooth, that a shallow
network can match only with exponentially many units. Depth multiplies the number of pieces; width only adds to it.

**The output can be a distribution.** A <NoteLink to="mixture-density-networks">mixture density network</NoteLink>
predicts the parameters of a mixture model in place of a single value. It captures conditional distributions with
several modes, such as those of inverse problems, where the conditional mean falls between the modes.
