<Definition>
During training, **dropout** replaces a layer's activations $\hvec \in \reals^n$ by

$$
\tilde\hvec = \frac{1}{1 - p}\,\mvec \odot \hvec, \qquad m_k \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \Bern(1 - p),
$$

drawing a new mask $\mvec$ for every example and every step. The **drop probability** $p \in [0, 1)$ is a
hyperparameter. At test time $\tilde\hvec = \hvec$ <Cite id="srivastava2014" />. This is **inverted dropout**. The
original formulation left the training activations unscaled and multiplied the outgoing weights by $1 - p$ at test time,
which gives the same network.
</Definition>

## Problem

A large network can fit its training set by building units that only work in combination with specific other units.
Hinton et al. called such dependencies co-adaptations: a feature is useful only in the presence of others, and the
combination fits noise in the training data <Cite id="hinton2012" />. Averaging the predictions of many separately
trained networks reduces this overfitting, but training and running many large networks is expensive.

## Mechanism

### Expected activation

Each mask entry $m_k$ is 1 with probability $1 - p$ and 0 with probability $p$, so $\expect[m_k] = 1 - p$ and

$$
\expect[\tilde h_k] = \frac{1}{1 - p}\,\expect[m_k]\,h_k = h_k.
$$

The scaling by $1/(1 - p)$ makes the dropped-out activation an unbiased estimate of the original, so the layer above
sees inputs of the same expected size in training and at test time. The variance is
$\var(\tilde h_k) = h_k^2\,\var(m_k)/(1 - p)^2 = h_k^2\,p/(1 - p)$: equal to $h_k^2$ for $p = 1/2$.

### The ensemble view

A layer of $n$ units has $2^n$ possible masks. Each mask defines a thinned subnetwork, and all subnetworks share the
same weights. Each training step trains one randomly sampled subnetwork on one example. Dropout is therefore bagging
over an exponential number of models with complete weight sharing <Cite id="srivastava2014" />.

The ensemble's prediction would average all $2^n$ subnetworks. Running the full network with unscaled weights, the
**weight scaling inference rule**, approximates this average in one pass. For some architectures it is exact.

- **Linear layer.** A linear function commutes with <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>:
  $\expect[\wvec^\top\tilde\hvec] = \wvec^\top\hvec$. The test network's output is exactly the mean output of the
  ensemble.
- **Softmax output.** Let the logits of a subnetwork be $\zvec_{\mvec} = \Wmat(\mvec \odot \hvec)$ and consider the
  geometric mean of the subnetworks' class distributions, renormalised. Its logarithm is, up to a constant,

  $$
  \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{\mvec}\log\operatorname{softmax}(\zvec_{\mvec})_c = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{\mvec} z_{\mvec,c} -
  \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{\mvec}\log\sum_{c'} e^{z_{\mvec,c'}}.
  $$

  The second term is the same for every class $c$ and disappears in the renormalisation. The first is the average
  logit, $\Wmat\,\expect[\mvec \odot \hvec] = \Wmat\,(1 - p)\hvec$ for $p = 1/2$, where every mask is equally likely.
  The renormalised geometric mean is therefore the softmax of the logits of the weight-scaled network
  <Cite id="hinton2012" />.
- **Deeper nonlinear networks.** The rule is an approximation, and the test network is not the exact ensemble average.
  Srivastava et al. compared it with averaging over sampled masks and found the two agreed closely once about 50 masks
  were averaged <Cite id="srivastava2014" />.

### Dropout as a penalty

For <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> with dropout on the inputs, the dropout noise can be
averaged out analytically, and dropout turns into a penalty on the weights.

<Derivation title="Dropout in linear regression is a scaled ridge penalty">
Let the prediction for one example be $\hat y = \sum_j w_j\tilde x_j$ with $\tilde x_j = m_jx_j/(1 - p)$. Each
$\tilde x_j$ has mean $x_j$ and variance $x_j^2\,p/(1 - p)$, and the $\tilde x_j$ are independent. So $\hat y$ has mean
$\wvec^\top\xvec$ and variance $\frac{p}{1 - p}\sum_j w_j^2x_j^2$. The expected squared error is the squared bias plus
the variance:

$$
\expect\brack{(y - \hat y)^2} = (y - \wvec^\top\xvec)^2 + \frac{p}{1 - p}\sum_j w_j^2x_j^2.
$$

Summed over the training set, the penalty on $w_j$ is weighted by $\sum_i x_{ij}^2$, the energy of feature $j$. This is
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> with a penalty scaled per feature by the feature's second
moment, and with strength $p/(1 - p)$ <Cite id="srivastava2014" />.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

A layer outputs $\hvec = (2, 0, 1, 3)$ and uses $p = 0.5$, so survivors are scaled by 2.

- The mask $\mvec = (1, 0, 0, 1)$ gives $\tilde\hvec = (4, 0, 0, 6)$.
- Averaged over all 16 masks, each entry of $\tilde\hvec$ equals the corresponding entry of $\hvec$.
- For a linear regression with weights $\wvec = (1, -2, 0.5, 1)$, input $\xvec = (2, 0, 1, 3)$ and target $y = 3$, the
  prediction without dropout is $\wvec^\top\xvec = 5.5$ and the squared error is $6.25$. The penalty is
  $\frac{0.5}{0.5}(1 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 0 + 0.25 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 9) = 13.25$. The expected squared error under dropout
  is $6.25 + 13.25 = 19.5$. Averaging the squared error over all 16 masks directly gives $19.5$.

## Cost

Sampling a mask costs one random number per unit per example, and the layer does no extra arithmetic at test time.
Training is slower in a different way: each step updates a noisy subnetwork, and dropout networks typically need two to
three times as many steps to converge <Cite id="srivastava2014" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Choice of $p$.** Srivastava et al. used $p = 0.5$ for hidden units and $p = 0.2$ for input units
  <Cite id="srivastava2014" />. They also suggested widening a layer by the factor $1/(1 - p)$ when adding dropout, so
  that the expected number of active units stays the same.
- **Train and test modes differ.** A network left in training mode at test time makes random predictions.
- **Uncertainty.** Keeping dropout on at test time and averaging $T$ stochastic passes gives a predictive mean and a
  spread. Gal and Ghahramani interpret this Monte Carlo dropout as approximate
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian inference</NoteLink> in a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss>
  <Cite id="gal2016" />.
- **Recurrent and convolutional layers.** Dropping independent entries of a feature map removes little information,
  because neighbouring positions are correlated. Variants drop whole channels, or reuse one mask across time steps in a
  recurrent network.
- **Other regularisers.** Dropout combines with <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> and <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>. Networks
  with <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> often need less of it, because batch noise
  already regularises.
