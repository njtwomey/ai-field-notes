<Definition>
A **residual connection** computes a layer's output as its input plus a learned branch:
$\hvec_\ell = \hvec_{\ell-1} + \alpha F_\ell(\hvec_{\ell-1})$, where $F_\ell$ is a small stack of layers and
$\alpha > 0$ is a fixed or learned branch scale, often 1. The path that carries $\hvec_{\ell-1}$ unchanged is the
identity shortcut. A stack of $L$ such layers has, at every depth, a path from input to output that passes through no
weights <Cite id="he2016" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A deep plain network multiplies its signal by one <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> per layer in each
direction; see <NoteLink to="vanishing-and-exploding-gradients">vanishing and exploding gradients</NoteLink>. Careful
<NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">initialisation</NoteLink> sets the average factor to 1 at the start, but only for a
matched activation, and the factor drifts as the weights change. He et al. observed that plain convolutional networks of
56 layers had higher training error than networks of 20 layers, an optimisation failure rather than overfitting
<Cite id="he2016" />. The architecture that fixed it is described in <NoteLink to="residual-network">residual
network</NoteLink>.

## Mechanism

### The identity in the Jacobian

Differentiating one residual layer gives

$$
\frac{\partial\hvec_\ell}{\partial\hvec_{\ell-1}} = \I + \alpha\,\frac{\partial F_\ell}{\partial\hvec_{\ell-1}}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the loss $\Lcal$ with respect to layer $\ell - 1$ is therefore

$$
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_{\ell-1}} = \frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_\ell} +
\alpha\paren{\frac{\partial F_\ell}{\partial\hvec_{\ell-1}}}^{\!\top}\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_\ell}.
$$

The first term copies the incoming gradient unchanged. Applied through $L$ layers, the product
$\prod_\ell(\I + \alpha\Jmat_\ell)$ expands into $2^L$ terms, one for each subset of branches, and the term with no
branches is $\I$. The gradient at the input always contains the gradient at the output, whatever the branches do. A
branch whose own Jacobian is tiny, for example because its units are saturated or its weights are small, no longer
blocks the signal below it. Veit et al. read the same expansion as an ensemble of paths of different lengths, and found
that most of the gradient in a 110-layer residual network comes from paths of 10 to 34 branches <Cite id="veit2016" />.

### Growth with depth

The identity path cannot shrink the signal, but the branches add to it. Take the pre-activation branch
$F_\ell(\hvec) = \Wmat_\ell\,\phi(\hvec)$ <Cite id="he2016b" />, with weights $W_{\ell,ij}$ drawn independently with
mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$, and width $n$. The branch output has mean zero and is uncorrelated with
$\hvec_{\ell-1}$, because each of its entries is a sum of terms $W_{\ell,ij}\,\phi(h_j)$ whose weights have mean zero.
The mean squares therefore add. Write $q_\ell = \expect[h_{\ell,i}^2]$:

$$
q_\ell = q_{\ell-1} + \alpha^2\,n\sigma^2\,\expect\brack{\phi(h)^2}.
$$

For <Gloss name="relu" /> with He initialisation, $n\sigma^2 = 2$ and $\expect[\phi(h)^2] = \tfrac12 q_{\ell-1}$ for $h$
symmetric about zero, so $q_\ell = (1 + \alpha^2)\,q_{\ell-1}$. The same holds for linear branches with Xavier
initialisation. After $L$ layers,

$$
q_L = (1 + \alpha^2)^L\,q_0.
$$

With $\alpha = 1$ the mean square doubles at every layer. The backward pass behaves the same way: the gradient's mean
square is multiplied by $1 + \alpha^2\,n\sigma^2\expect[\phi'(h)^2] = 1 + \alpha^2$ per layer. A residual stack
does not vanish, but with unit branch scale it explodes.

### Branch scaling

The growth $(1 + \alpha^2)^L$ stays bounded as $L$ grows only if $\alpha^2 L$ does. With $\alpha = 1/\sqrt L$,

$$
q_L = \paren{1 + \frac1L}^L q_0 \le e\,q_0,
$$

with the bound approached as $L \to \infty$. Every scheme that trains very deep residual networks controls this growth
in one of three ways.

- **Scale the branch at initialisation.** Fixup rescales the weights inside each branch by a power of $L$ and
  initialises the last layer of each branch to zero, and trains residual networks of up to 10 000 layers without
  normalisation <Cite id="zhang2019fixup" />.
- **Start each branch at zero.** Setting the scale of the last normalisation layer in each branch to 0 makes every block
  the identity at initialisation. Goyal et al. used this for <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">large-batch
  training</NoteLink> of ResNet-50 <Cite id="goyal2017" />.
- **Normalise inside the branch.** In a pre-normalisation block,
  $\hvec_\ell = \hvec_{\ell-1} + F_\ell(\operatorname{LN}(\hvec_{\ell-1}))$, each branch sees an input of unit scale, so
  its output has a bounded scale. The residual stream's mean square then grows linearly in $L$ rather than
  exponentially, and each branch's relative contribution shrinks with depth. Pre-LN
  <NoteLink to="transformer">transformers</NoteLink> train without warmup <Cite id="xiong2020" />; see
  <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink>.

## Worked example: 30 layers of width 128

ReLU branches, He initialisation, $L = 30$.

| Branch scale $\alpha$  | Predicted RMS growth $(1 + \alpha^2)^{L/2}$ | Simulated RMS of $\hvec_{30}$ | Simulated gradient RMS at the input |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| 1                      | $2^{15} = 32\,768$                           | $47\,000$                     | $48\,900$                           |
| 0.5                    | $1.25^{15} = 28.4$                           | $38.8$                        | $38.9$                              |
| $1/\sqrt{30} = 0.183$  | $(31/30)^{15} = 1.64$                        | $1.70$                        | $1.69$                              |

The simulated columns are the figure's first draw of the weights. The input and the top gradient both have RMS close to 1.
Over 20 draws of the weights, the mean square of $\hvec_{30}$ was $9.0 \times 10^8$ for $\alpha = 1$, against the
predicted $2^{30} = 1.07 \times 10^9$, and $2.66$ for $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{30}$, against the predicted
$(31/30)^{30} = 2.67$. A single draw deviates more for $\alpha = 1$ because the random per-layer factors compound over
30 layers.

For comparison, the plain sigmoid stack with Xavier initialisation shrinks the gradient by a factor of about
$10^{-19}$ over the same 30 layers. The residual sigmoid stack with $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{30}$ passes it with RMS $1.03$.

## Cost

The addition costs one elementwise operation per layer, and the shortcut stores no parameters. When the branch changes
the width, the shortcut needs a projection, which adds parameters and breaks the exact identity. The input of each block
must be kept until the addition, which is already required for backpropagation.

## Trade-offs

- **Depth that behaves like less depth.** A residual stack with small branches is close to the identity, and much of
  its gradient travels along short paths. Very deep residual networks can behave like ensembles of shallower ones
  <Cite id="veit2016" />; see <NoteLink to="depth-versus-width">depth versus width</NoteLink>.
- **Normalisation placement.** Normalising after the addition (post-norm) removes the exact identity path and needs a
  warmup to train. Normalising inside the branch (pre-norm) keeps it and needs a final normalisation before the output
  <Cite id="xiong2020" />.
- **The branch scale is a hyperparameter.** Learned scales initialised at zero, fixed scales of $1/\sqrt L$, and
  normalisation layers are alternatives. Which works best depends on the architecture and the optimiser.
