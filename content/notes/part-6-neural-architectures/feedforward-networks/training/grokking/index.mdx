<Definition>
**Grokking** (or delayed generalisation) is the empirical phenomenon in which a model trained on an algorithmic task quickly reaches zero empirical risk on the training set, suffers near-chance accuracy on unseen test data for a prolonged plateau, and then abruptly transitions to near-perfect generalisation after many thousands of additional gradient steps <Cite id="power2022" />.

Mathematically, let $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} \subset \mathbb{Z}_p \times \mathbb{Z}_p$ be a fraction of all pairs $(a, b)$ under a modular operation $a \circ b \pmod p$ (for prime $p$). Grokking occurs when:
$$
\lim_{t \to t_{\text{mem}}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{train}}(\thetavec_t) = 0 \quad \text{while} \quad \mathcal{R}_{\text{test}}(\thetavec_t) \approx 1 - \frac{1}{p},
$$
and for some delayed step $t_{\text{grok}} \gg t_{\text{mem}}$ under $\ell_2$ weight regularisation $\frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\thetavec}_2^2$:
$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{test}}(\thetavec_t) \to 0 \quad \text{as } t \ge t_{\text{grok}}.
$$
</Definition>

## Intuition

In classical learning theory, training past the point where training error hits zero is considered dangerous: once a model has memorised its training samples, further optimization risks severe overfitting. Early stopping is therefore widely used to halt optimization when validation loss begins to rise.

Grokking turns this conventional wisdom upside down. For clean algorithmic tasks—such as modular addition, polynomial evaluation, or permutation groups—a high-capacity network can fit the training set in two completely different ways:
1. **Memorisation circuit**: A memorising solution fits each training point independently using high-norm, unstructured parameter vectors. This circuit forms quickly because gradient descent rapidly minimizes cross-entropy on observed pairs without needing global coordination.
2. **Generalising circuit**: A structured, mathematically symmetric solution (such as computing trigonometric sums in a discrete Fourier basis) that simultaneously solves all $p^2$ pairs. Finding this global structure requires coordinating weights across all units, which takes much longer.

Because the generalising circuit requires far smaller parameter magnitudes $\norm{\thetavec}_2$, <NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay">weight decay (AdamW)</NoteLink> exerts a continuous inward pressure. As the high-norm memorisation weights slowly decay, the model abruptly "discovers" the compact, symmetrical Fourier mechanism, causing test accuracy to suddenly spike from random guessing to 100%.

## The Three Phases of Grokking

Mechanistic interpretability studies <Cite id="nanda2023" /> decompose grokking into three distinct dynamical phases:

### 1. Memorisation ($t \le t_{\text{mem}}$)
The model rapidly drives training cross-entropy to zero. Because only a fraction (e.g. 50%) of all $(a, b)$ combinations are observed, the unconstrained weights memorize specific pairs. Test accuracy remains indistinguishable from random guessing ($1/p$).

### 2. Weight norm compression ($t_{\text{mem}} < t < t_{\text{grok}}$)
Although the training loss is already near zero, training is not finished. Gradient updates continue because decoupled weight decay actively penalises parameter magnitude:
$$
\thetavec_{t+1} = (1 - \eta \lambda)\thetavec_t - \eta \nabla_{\thetavec} L_{\text{task}}(\thetavec_t).
$$
This constant penalty shrinks unnecessary weights, pruning random memorisation pathways while slowly reinforcing features that share parameters across many training examples.

### 3. Generalisation transition ($t \approx t_{\text{grok}}$)
Once the effective weight norm drops below a critical threshold, the generalising Fourier circuit cleanly out-competes the remnants of the memorisation circuit. Test loss plummets exponentially, and test accuracy transitions to 100% within a narrow window of steps.

## The Mechanistic Fourier Mechanism

Why does modular arithmetic admit such a compact neural circuit?

Consider modular addition: $(a + b) \pmod p$. In $\mathbb{Z}_p$, translation corresponds to rotation around a circle. By Euler's formula and the angle-sum trigonometric identities:
$$
\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k (a + b)}{p}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k a}{p}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k b}{p}\right) - \sin\left(\frac{2\pi k a}{p}\right)\sin\left(\frac{2\pi k b}{p}\right).
$$

A one-hidden-layer network with embedding matrix $\Emat \in \reals^{p \times d}$ can implement this by:
1. Mapping residue $a$ to its Fourier components $\left[\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k a}{p}\right), \sin\left(\frac{2\pi k a}{p}\right)\right]$ for a small set of frequencies $k \in \{1, \dots, \lfloor p/2 \rfloor\}$.
2. Concatenating $[\Emat_a, \Emat_b]$ and passing them through quadratic or $\mathrm{ReLU}$ activations to compute the cross-terms $\cos \omega a \cos \omega b$ and $\sin \omega a \sin \omega b$.
3. Linearly projecting the hidden activations into the output logits to produce the highest score for residue $(a + b) \pmod p$.

## Why Early Stopping Fails Here

If early stopping had been applied when validation loss first stalled or began rising (near step 150), training would have halted with 0% validation accuracy, concluding that neural networks are incapable of learning modular addition. 

Grokking demonstrates that in regularised over-parameterised systems, optimization dynamics can undergo late-stage structural transitions that are invisible to short-horizon evaluation metrics.
