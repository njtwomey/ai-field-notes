<Definition>
**Training a deep network** means minimising a loss over millions or billions of parameters by stochastic gradient
methods, with gradients computed by backpropagation. Three problems arise that shallow models do not have. Gradients
shrink or grow geometrically with depth; the loss surface is badly conditioned and non-convex; and a network large
enough to fit its training set can memorise it. The methods in this topic address the three in turn: signal
propagation, optimisation and regularisation <Cite id="goodfellow2016" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A deep network defined on paper is useless until its weights are found, and plain <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient
descent</Gloss> on a deep, freshly initialised network often fails. It can stall, diverge, or reach a low training loss
with a high test loss. The techniques here are the standard recipe that makes training reliable: an adaptive optimiser
with a warmup and decay schedule, scaled initialisation, normalisation layers, residual connections and some form of
regularisation. Every architecture in this Part, from <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional
networks</NoteLink> to <Gloss name="llm">large language models</Gloss>, is trained with a combination of them.

## Main ideas

**Signals must keep their scale through depth.** The gradient at a layer is a product of one Jacobian per layer above
it, so it vanishes or explodes geometrically unless each factor stays near one;
<NoteLink to="vanishing-and-exploding-gradients" /> derives this. <NoteLink to="weight-initialisation" /> chooses each
layer's weight variance, Xavier for tanh and He for <Gloss name="relu" />, so that activations and gradients keep a
constant scale at the start of training. <NoteLink to="residual-connections" /> add each layer's input to its output,
giving the gradient an identity path to every layer.

**Normalisation keeps activations well scaled during training.** <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation" /> standardises each
feature over the mini-batch, then applies a learned scale and shift, which permits larger
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rates</Gloss>. <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation" /> standardises over the features
of one example, so it does not depend on the batch; it and its variant <Gloss name="rmsnorm" /> are the normalisation of
<NoteLink to="transformers">transformers</NoteLink>.

**Optimisers adapt the step.** <NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov" /> accumulates a velocity so that consistent
gradient directions build up and oscillating ones cancel. <NoteLink to="adagrad-and-rmsprop" /> rescale each coordinate
by the root of its accumulated squared gradient. <NoteLink to="adam" /> combines the two, with bias correction, and is
the default optimiser for most deep networks.

**The step size should change over training.** <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules" /> explains why large early
steps make fast progress and small late steps lower the noise floor of stochastic gradients. Linear warmup protects the
first steps, when adaptive optimisers' statistics are unreliable, and cosine decay is a common shape for the rest.

**Regularisation trades training fit for generalisation.** <NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay" /> shrinks weights
outside the gradient step, which differs from an L2 penalty under Adam and gives AdamW.
<NoteLink to="early-stopping" /> returns the parameters with the best validation loss and acts like an L2 penalty.
<NoteLink to="dropout" /> zeroes random units during training and approximates an average over many subnetworks.
<NoteLink to="data-augmentation" /> trains on label-preserving transformations of each example, encoding known
invariances.

**Generalisation can arrive long after fitting.** <NoteLink to="grokking" /> describes networks that reach zero training
loss early, stay at chance on held-out data, and then generalise abruptly after many more steps under
<Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>.
