<Definition>
  **Attention head analysis** examines the attention weights $\alpha_{ij}$ of each head in a trained
  <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>, which say how much query position $i$ reads from key position $j$.
  It asks three questions: which patterns individual heads compute, how much the model depends on each head, and whether
  the weights explain the model's predictions.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Each head produces an $n \times n$ matrix of weights that can be drawn and inspected. That makes heads the most visible
part of a transformer. Inspection shows interpretable patterns, but a pattern is not the same as a contribution to the
output: what a head adds to the stream also depends on what it reads from each position.

## What heads attend to

Clark et al. studied the 144 heads of BERT-base <Cite id="clark2019bert" />.

- **Positional heads.** Four heads, in layers 2, 4, 7 and 8, put on average more than half their attention on the
  previous token, and five put more than half on the next token.
- **Special tokens.** Over half of <Gloss name="bert" />'s attention in layers 6 to 10 goes to `[SEP]`.
  <NoteLink to="gradient">Gradients</NoteLink> of the loss with respect to attention on `[SEP]` are small, and heads
  with a specific function attend to `[SEP]` when that function does not apply. Clark et al. interpret this attention as
  a no-op.
- **Syntax and coreference.** Taking each head's most-attended word as a prediction, particular heads find direct
  objects of verbs, determiners of nouns, objects of prepositions and objects of possessive pronouns with more than 75%
  accuracy. No single head does well on many relations. One head links coreferent mentions to their antecedents with 65%
  accuracy. A probe that combines attention maps reaches 77 unlabelled attachment score on dependency parsing.

Voita et al. classified the encoder heads of translation transformers by function <Cite id="voita2019" />:
**positional** heads attend to an adjacent token at least 90% of the time; **syntactic** heads identify a dependency
relation at least 10% more accurately than a positional baseline; and a **rare-words** head attends to the least
frequent tokens of the sentence.

## How much each head matters

Michel et al. found that a large share of heads can be removed at test time without a significant loss, and that some
layers can be reduced to a single head <Cite id="michel2019" />. Voita et al. pruned heads during training with a
stochastic gate on each head and a differentiable relaxation of the $L_0$ penalty on the number of open gates. On
English–Russian WMT, pruning 38 of 48 encoder heads reduced <Gloss name="bleu" /> by 0.15. The specialised heads,
positional, syntactic and rare-words, were the last to be pruned <Cite id="voita2019" />.

Pruning results say that the trained model has redundant heads, not that a smaller model trained from the start would do
as well. They also do not say that a pruned head did nothing: other heads may compute the same function.

## Attention weights as explanation

### The case against

Jain and Wallace examined recurrent (BiLSTM) encoders with attention on <NoteLink to="text-classification">text
classification</NoteLink>. Attention weights correlated only weakly with gradient-based and leave-one-out measures of
feature importance. For many inputs they could construct very different attention distributions, including adversarial
ones, that left the prediction almost unchanged. If other weightings give the same output, the observed weighting cannot
be taken as the reason for the output <Cite id="jain2019" />.

### The case for a narrower claim

Wiegreffe and Pinter argued that the conclusion depends on what "explanation" means and that the adversarial search had
too much freedom: it chose a new distribution for each input without a model that would produce it
<Cite id="wiegreffe2019" />. They proposed four tests: compare with uniform attention weights, to see whether attention
is needed at all; compare with the variance across random seeds; transfer the learned weights to a model without
attention; and train an adversarial model end to end. Adversarial distributions that fooled one prediction at a time did
not transfer as useful weights, so prior work did not show that attention is useless as explanation
<Cite id="wiegreffe2019" />. Both papers studied recurrent models with a single attention layer.

### What a head actually writes

In a transformer the output of one head at query position $i$ is

$$
\sum_j \alpha_{ij}\,\xvec_j\,\Wmat_{OV}, \qquad \Wmat_{OV} = \Wmat_V\Wmat_O ,
$$

where $\xvec_j$ is the residual vector at position $j$ (see <NoteLink to="residual-stream-view">the residual stream
view</NoteLink>). The contribution of key $j$ is the vector $\alpha_{ij}\,\xvec_j\Wmat_{OV}$, whose size is
$\alpha_{ij}\norm{\xvec_j\Wmat_{OV}}$. A large weight on a position whose transformed vector is short contributes
little. Kobayashi et al. measured these norms in BERT and found that, contrary to the weights, BERT "pays poor attention
to special tokens" <Cite id="kobayashi2020" />. This fits the no-op reading of attention to `[SEP]`: a head can put its
weight on a position whose value it has learned to make small. A head's output is then added to the stream, mixed by
later layers, and combined with every other head's, so even the norm-weighted contribution describes one step of a
longer computation.

## Worked example

A head attends from one query to two keys with weights $\alpha = (0.9, 0.1)$. The transformed vectors have norms
$\norm{\xvec_1\Wmat_{OV}} = 0.05$ and $\norm{\xvec_2\Wmat_{OV}} = 2.0$. The contributions have norms
$0.9 \times 0.05 = 0.045$ and $0.1 \times 2.0 = 0.2$. The key with 10% of the attention contributes more than four
times as much to the output as the key with 90%. A heat map of $\alpha$ would point at the wrong position.

## Assumptions

- A head's pattern is read on typical inputs. Heads can behave differently on other distributions.
- The most-attended position is used as the head's "prediction" in probing. Heads that spread weight across positions
  are scored poorly by this rule even if they carry information.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading weights as importance.** Weights ignore the value norms and everything downstream of the head.
- **Reading pruning as uselessness.** Redundancy means another head can take over, not that the function is absent.
- **Generalising from one model.** Results for BERT-base, translation encoders or BiLSTM classifiers do not transfer
  automatically to large decoders, whose heads include mechanisms such as
  <NoteLink to="in-context-learning">induction heads</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="attention-sinks">attention sinks</NoteLink>.
