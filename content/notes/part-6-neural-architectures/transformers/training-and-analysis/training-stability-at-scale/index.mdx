<Definition>
  A training run is **unstable** when the loss diverges, or rises sharply for a few hundred steps and then recovers (a
  **loss spike**), at hyperparameters that give smooth training for a smaller model. Instabilities in large
  <NoteLink to="transformer">transformers</NoteLink> have several distinct causes, each with a targeted fix. Wortsman et
  al. showed that the main ones also appear in small models trained at high <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning
  rates</Gloss>, which makes them cheap to study <Cite id="wortsman2023" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A training run of a large model cannot be repeated many times, so an instability late in training is expensive. The
learning rate that is optimal for a small model is often too large for a big one, and quantities that stay bounded in
a small model, such as the size of attention logits, grow with width or with training time. Each fix below removes one
route by which a quantity grows until the arithmetic or the optimiser breaks.

## Observations

<Gloss name="palm" />'s 540-billion-parameter model spiked roughly 20 times during training, despite gradient clipping,
at irregular intervals and sometimes late in training. The smaller PaLM models did not spike. The team restarted from a
checkpoint about 100 steps before each spike and skipped 200 to 500 batches, after which the spike did not recur.
Replaying the same batches from an earlier checkpoint did not cause a spike either, so the spikes came from the
combination of a model state and particular data, not from bad data alone <Cite id="chowdhery2022" />.

## Attention logit growth

The attention logits $\qvec_i \cdot \kvec_j/\sqrt{d_h}$ are bilinear in the query and key weights. If both weight
matrices grow by a factor $a$, the logits grow by $a^2$. Large logits make the softmax nearly one-hot: the attention
entropy collapses, and the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> through the softmax vanishes for all but one key
<Cite id="zhai2023entropy" />. Dehghani et al. observed this divergence when scaling <Gloss name="vit">vision
transformers</Gloss> to 22 billion parameters and fixed it by normalising queries and keys before the dot product
<Cite id="dehghani2023" />. Wortsman et al. reproduced the same failure in small models at high learning rates, found
that it came from growth in the queries and keys rather than in their alignment, and showed that QK normalisation lets a
1.2B model train at learning rate 0.3 <Cite id="wortsman2023" />. The fix and its variants are covered in
<NoteLink to="query-key-normalisation-and-logit-soft-capping">QK normalisation and logit soft-capping</NoteLink>.

## Output logit divergence and z-loss

The softmax is unchanged when a constant is added to every logit: $\operatorname{softmax}(\zvec + c\ones) =
\operatorname{softmax}(\zvec)$. The cross-entropy therefore does not constrain the mean of the logits, and the log
normaliser $\log Z = \log\sum_w e^{z_w}$ can drift far from 0. Large logits lose precision in low-precision
arithmetic.

PaLM adds an auxiliary **z-loss** $10^{-4}\,(\log Z)^2$ "to encourage the softmax normalizer $\log(Z)$ to be close to 0"
<Cite id="chowdhery2022" />. Its gradient with respect to logit $z_w$ is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_w}\,10^{-4}(\log Z)^2 = 2 \times 10^{-4}\,\log Z\;\frac{e^{z_w}}{Z} = 2 \times 10^{-4}\,\log Z\;p_w .
$$

When $\log Z > 0$ every logit is pushed down in proportion to its probability; when $\log Z < 0$ every logit is pushed
up. Write $c = 2 \times 10^{-4}\log Z$. A gradient step of size $s$ moves the logits by $-sc\,\pvec$, and since
$\partial\log Z/\partial z_w = p_w$, it changes $\log Z$ by about $-sc\norm{\pvec}^2$, which has the opposite sign to
$\log Z$. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on the z-loss therefore drives $\log Z$ towards 0.
With $\log Z = 0$ the logits are the log-probabilities themselves. Wortsman et al. found that output logit divergence
occurs in models trained without <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss> at any scale, and that z-loss with
coefficient $10^{-4}$ removes it <Cite id="wortsman2023" />.

## Adam's epsilon

Adam's update is $\Delta = \eta\,\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon)$, where $\hat m$ and $\hat v$ are the bias-corrected
averages of the gradient and its square (see <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink>). The division normalises the update
to about $\eta$ per parameter when $\sqrt{\hat v} \gg \epsilon$. The root mean square of the gradient shrinks as models
grow. When it approaches the default $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, the denominator is dominated by $\epsilon$ and the update
shrinks with the gradient. Wortsman et al. observed this in their largest models, and lowering $\epsilon$ to $10^{-15}$
improved the loss of a 4.8B model <Cite id="wortsman2023" />.

## Warmup, clipping and weight decay

- **Warmup.** Post-norm transformers have large gradients near the output at initialisation and need a learning-rate
  warmup <Cite id="xiong2020" />; see <NoteLink to="pre-norm-and-post-norm">pre-norm and post-norm</NoteLink>. Pre-norm
  models can train without it but still benefit: longer warmup reduced sensitivity to the learning rate in Wortsman et
  al.'s experiments <Cite id="wortsman2023" />. <Gloss name="llama" /> used 2,000 warmup steps and a cosine schedule
  <Cite id="touvron2023b" />.
- **Gradient clipping.** Rescaling the gradient to norm at most $c$,
  $\gvec \leftarrow \gvec \min(1, c/\norm{\gvec})$ <Cite id="pascanu2013" />, bounds the contribution of one extreme
  batch to Adam's moment estimates. PaLM and LLaMA clip at $c = 1.0$ <Cite id="chowdhery2022" />
  <Cite id="touvron2023b" />. Clipping did not prevent PaLM's spikes, which shows that it limits a symptom rather than a
  cause.
- **Weight decay.** Decay that is not multiplied by the learning rate, as in decoupled weight decay
  <Cite id="loshchilov2019" />, reduced sensitivity to the learning rate <Cite id="wortsman2023" />. Without any decay,
  output logits drifted; see <NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay">decoupled weight decay</NoteLink>.
- **Parameterisation.** Under the standard parameterisation the best learning rate falls as width grows, so a learning
  rate tuned on a small model is too high for a large one. <NoteLink to="maximal-update-parameterisation">Maximal update
  parameterisation</NoteLink> keeps it fixed. In Wortsman et al.'s setting it stabilised the optimal learning rate
  across widths without improving the loss <Cite id="wortsman2023" />.

## Other design choices made for stability

PaLM removed all biases, citing stability, and used $\beta_2 = 1 - k^{-0.8}$ at step $k$ rather than a fixed 0.99,
because rare embedding tokens had poorly estimated second moments over short windows <Cite id="chowdhery2022" />.
Pre-norm placement, bf16 rather than fp16 arithmetic (see <NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">mixed-precision
training</NoteLink>) and removing biases (see <NoteLink to="bias-terms-and-dropout-in-modern-transformers">bias terms
and dropout</NoteLink>) are other design choices made at least in part for stability.

| Cause                         | Symptom                                    | Fix                                  |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| attention logit growth        | attention entropy collapses, loss diverges | QK normalisation, logit soft-capping |
| output logit drift            | $\lvert\log Z\rvert$ grows, precision lost | z-loss, weight decay                 |
| gradient RMS near $\epsilon$  | updates shrink, loss stalls                | smaller $\epsilon$                   |
| large early updates           | divergence in the first steps              | warmup, pre-norm                     |
| rare extreme batches          | single large gradient                      | gradient clipping, skipping batches  |
| learning rate tuned too small | rate too high for the large model          | maximal update parameterisation, learning-rate sweeps at scale |

## Worked example

Take three logits $\zvec = (2, 1, 0)$. Then $\log Z = \log(e^2 + e + 1) = 2.408$, the probabilities are
$(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, and the cross-entropy for class 1 is $2.408 - 2 = 0.408$.

Shift all logits by 300. The probabilities and the cross-entropy do not change, because the softmax ignores the shift.
Now $\log Z = 302.408$. In bf16, numbers between 256 and 512 are spaced 2 apart, so the logits
$(302.41, 301.41, 300.41)$ round to $(302, 302, 300)$. The softmax of the rounded logits is $(0.468, 0.468, 0.063)$ and
the cross-entropy becomes $0.759$: the rounding has destroyed the difference between the first two classes.

The z-loss detects the drift. At $\log Z = 302.408$ it is $10^{-4} \times 302.408^2 = 9.15$, against $0.00058$ at
$\log Z = 2.408$. Its gradient is $2 \times 10^{-4} \times 302.408 \times \pvec = (0.040, 0.015, 0.005)$, which lowers
every logit. At $\log Z = 0$ the logits are $(-0.41, -1.41, -2.41)$, where bf16 spacing is at most $2^{-6} = 0.016$.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating spikes as bad data.** PaLM's spikes did not recur when the same batches were replayed from an earlier
  state <Cite id="chowdhery2022" />. Skipping batches works as a recovery, not as a diagnosis.
- **Relying on clipping.** A clipped gradient still moves the model in the direction of the offending batch.
- **Testing only at the target scale.** Instabilities that appear in large runs usually also appear in small models at
  higher learning rates, where they can be diagnosed cheaply <Cite id="wortsman2023" />.
