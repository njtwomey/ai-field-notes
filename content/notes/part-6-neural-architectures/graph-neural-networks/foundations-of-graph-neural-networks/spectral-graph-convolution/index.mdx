<Definition>
A **spectral graph convolution** filters a graph signal $\xvec \in \reals^n$ by a learned
<NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink> $g_\theta$ of the Laplacian's eigenvalues,
$\yvec = \Umat\, g_\theta(\Lambdamat)\, \Umat^\tr\xvec$, where $\Lmat = \Umat\Lambdamat\Umat^\tr$ is the
eigendecomposition of a graph Laplacian <Cite id="bruna2014" />. A **Chebyshev filter** restricts $g_\theta$ to a
polynomial of degree $K$ in the Chebyshev basis,

$$
g_\theta(\Lmat)\,\xvec = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k\, T_k(\tilde\Lmat)\,\xvec, \qquad \tilde\Lmat = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}\Lmat - \I,
$$

which needs no eigenvectors, costs $K$ sparse <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">matrix-vector products</NoteLink>,
and mixes only nodes at most $K$ hops apart (<Gloss name="chebnet" />) <Cite id="defferrard2016" />. The graph
convolutional network is the case $K = 1$ with tied coefficients.
</Definition>

## Intuition

On an image, convolution is the linear map that commutes with translation. The Fourier basis diagonalises every such
map, so a convolution is a pointwise product of Fourier coefficients: the
<NoteLink to="convolution-theorem" />. A graph has no translations, which rules out defining a "shifted copy" of a
filter. The frequency side survives. The eigenvectors of the Laplacian are the
<NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform">graph Fourier basis</NoteLink>, and a filter is a choice of
gain for each frequency.

Learning one free gain per frequency fails for three reasons. It needs the full eigendecomposition. Its parameters
number $n$ per filter. And a free set of gains acts on the whole graph at once, so nothing makes the filter local the
way a 3 × 3 image kernel is. The classical remedy is the same as on images: a smooth frequency response corresponds to a
localised filter in space. On a graph the cleanest smooth family is the polynomials, and a polynomial of degree $K$ in
$\Lmat$ is local by construction.

## Free-form spectral filters

Bruna et al. defined the first spectral graph network <Cite id="bruna2014" />. A layer with $F$ input and $F'$ output
channels computes, for output channel $j$,

$$
\yvec_j = \sigma\paren{\sum_{i=1}^{F} \Umat\, \diag(\thetavec_{ij})\, \Umat^\tr\xvec_i},
$$

with a gain vector $\thetavec_{ij} \in \reals^n$ for each pair of channels. The layer has $nFF'$ parameters, each
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">forward pass</NoteLink> multiplies by the dense $n \times n$ matrix $\Umat$, and
$\Umat$ itself costs $O(n^3)$ to compute. The gains are tied to one graph's eigenvectors, so a trained filter has no
meaning on a different graph. Bruna et al. kept the eigenvectors of only the smallest eigenvalues and interpolated the
gains smoothly to reduce the parameter count, which already pointed at smooth responses.

## Polynomial filters are local

Let the response be a polynomial, $g_\theta(\lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k \lambda^k$. Because
$\Umat\Lambdamat^k\Umat^\tr = \Lmat^k$ for an orthogonal $\Umat$,

$$
\Umat\, g_\theta(\Lambdamat)\, \Umat^\tr = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k \Lmat^k = g_\theta(\Lmat).
$$

The filter is now a matrix polynomial in $\Lmat$, with $K + 1$ parameters and no eigenvectors.

**Locality.** $(\Lmat^k)_{ij} = 0$ whenever nodes $i$ and $j$ are more than $k$ hops apart. The proof is by induction on
$k$. For $k = 0$, $\Lmat^0 = \I$ is non-zero only on the diagonal, at distance 0. For $k \ge 1$,
$(\Lmat^k)_{ij} = \sum_l (\Lmat^{k-1})_{il}L_{lj}$. The factor $L_{lj}$ is non-zero only when $l = j$ or $l$ is a
neighbour of $j$, and by induction $(\Lmat^{k-1})_{il}$ is non-zero only when $l$ is within $k - 1$ hops of $i$. A
non-zero term therefore needs a node $l$ within $k - 1$ hops of $i$ and at most one hop from $j$, so $j$ is within $k$
hops of $i$. A degree-$K$ filter thus computes each output node from its $K$-hop neighbourhood, like a kernel of
radius $K$ <Cite id="hammond2011" />.

**The basis matters.** In the monomial basis $\Lmat^k$ the entries grow like $\lambda_{\max}^k$ and the columns
$\Lmat^k\xvec$ become nearly parallel, so learning $\theta_k$ is badly conditioned. Defferrard et al. use the Chebyshev
polynomials of the first kind, $T_0(t) = 1$, $T_1(t) = t$, $T_k(t) = 2tT_{k-1}(t) - T_{k-2}(t)$, which are bounded by 1
on $[-1, 1]$ and orthogonal there <Cite id="defferrard2016" />. The Laplacian's spectrum $[0, \lambda_{\max}]$ is mapped
to $[-1, 1]$ by $\tilde\Lmat = 2\Lmat/\lambda_{\max} - \I$. Any upper bound on the largest eigenvalue works; for the
normalised Laplacian the bound 2 is standard, and for $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Amat$ the bound $2d_{\max}$ always holds.

<Derivation title="Chebyshev polynomials: boundedness and the recurrence">
For $t = \cos\phi \in [-1, 1]$, define $T_k(t) = \cos(k\phi)$. The identity
$\cos((k + 1)\phi) + \cos((k - 1)\phi) = 2\cos\phi\cos(k\phi)$ gives $T_{k+1}(t) = 2tT_k(t) - T_{k-1}(t)$, and with
$T_0 = 1$, $T_1 = t$ this shows that $T_k$ is a polynomial of degree $k$. The definition gives
$\abs{T_k(t)} \le 1$ on $[-1, 1]$. Substituting $t = \cos\phi$,
$\int_{-1}^{1} T_j(t)T_k(t)(1 - t^2)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t = \int_0^\pi \cos(j\phi)\cos(k\phi)\,\mathrm{d}\phi$, which is
zero for $j \ne k$: the polynomials are orthogonal under the weight $(1 - t^2)^{-1/2}$.
</Derivation>

**The recurrence on signals.** The filter never forms $T_k(\tilde\Lmat)$ as a matrix. With
$\bar\xvec_k = T_k(\tilde\Lmat)\xvec$,

$$
\bar\xvec_0 = \xvec, \qquad \bar\xvec_1 = \tilde\Lmat\xvec, \qquad \bar\xvec_k = 2\tilde\Lmat\bar\xvec_{k-1} - \bar\xvec_{k-2},
$$

and $\yvec = \sum_k \theta_k\bar\xvec_k$. Each step is one product with the sparse $\tilde\Lmat$, so a filter costs
$O(K\abs{\Ecal})$ operations <Cite id="hammond2011" />. The ChebNet layer applies it with weight matrices
$\Thetamat_k \in \reals^{F \times F'}$:

$$
\Hmat' = \sigma\paren{\sum_{k=0}^{K} T_k(\tilde\Lmat)\,\Hmat\,\Thetamat_k}.
$$

It has $(K + 1)FF'$ parameters, independent of $n$, and the same weights apply to any graph, because the filter is
defined through $\Lmat$ rather than through one graph's eigenvectors.

## Approximating a target response

A polynomial filter of degree $K$ can match a prescribed response $g$ only approximately. Interpolating $g$ at the
$K + 1$ Chebyshev nodes $\lambda_j = \tfrac{\lambda_{\max}}{2}\paren{\cos\frac{\pi(j + 1/2)}{K + 1} + 1}$ gives the
coefficients

$$
\theta_k = \frac{2 - [k = 0]}{K + 1}\sum_{j=0}^{K} g(\lambda_j)\cos\frac{\pi k(j + 1/2)}{K + 1},
$$

which avoid the large oscillations of interpolation at equally spaced points
(<NoteLink to="polynomial-interpolation-and-runge" />). Hammond et al. used Chebyshev expansions this way to apply
wavelets on large graphs <Cite id="hammond2011" />, and ChebNetII learns the values $g(\lambda_j)$ at the nodes rather
than the $\theta_k$ directly <Cite id="he2022chebnetii" />.

How fast the approximation improves depends on the smoothness of $g$. On the 30-node graph of the figure, the heat
kernel $e^{-\lambda/2}$ is matched at every eigenvalue to within 0.078 with $K = 2$ and 0.016 with $K = 3$. The ideal
low-pass with a cut-off at $\lambda = 2$ is still off by 0.25 at some eigenvalue with $K = 10$, because a jump cannot be
fitted by a polynomial without ringing. A sharp response needs a high degree, and a high degree means a wide
neighbourhood. That is the graph version of the time-frequency trade-off.

## From ChebNet to the graph convolutional network

Kipf and Welling simplified the Chebyshev filter to first order <Cite id="kipf2017" />. Take the symmetric normalised
Laplacian $\Lmat_{\text{sym}} = \I - \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$, $K = 1$, and $\lambda_{\max} \approx 2$, so
$\tilde\Lmat = \Lmat_{\text{sym}} - \I = -\Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$:

$$
g_\theta(\Lmat)\,\xvec \approx \theta_0\xvec + \theta_1\tilde\Lmat\xvec = \theta_0\xvec - \theta_1\Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}\xvec.
$$

Tying $\theta = \theta_0 = -\theta_1$ leaves one parameter per channel pair,
$\theta\paren{\I + \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}}\xvec$, whose response $2 - \lambda$ is a low-pass on $[0, 2]$. The
matrix $\I + \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$ has eigenvalues up to 2, so stacked layers can blow up activations. The
renormalisation $\hat\Amat = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}(\Amat + \I)\tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}$ adds self-loops before normalising and
keeps the eigenvalues in $(-1, 1]$. The result is the <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network" /> layer
$\Hmat' = \sigma(\hat\Amat\Hmat\Wmat)$, a one-hop filter whose depth replaces the polynomial degree: $L$ layers reach
$L$ hops, with a nonlinearity between hops.

## Practice

Polynomial filters are <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss>. A degree-$K$ filter is $K$ rounds of
<NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">neighbourhood aggregation</NoteLink> with fixed coefficients, which is why
the spectral and spatial views of graph networks met in the GCN. The descendants of ChebNet keep that equivalence and
change how the polynomial is parametrised:

- **<NoteLink to="neighbour-sampling-and-scalable-graph-networks">SGC</NoteLink>** fixes the filter to $\hat\Amat^K$,
  precomputes $\hat\Amat^K\Xmat$ once, and trains only a linear classifier on top. It is a fixed low-pass filter
  followed by <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> <Cite id="wu2019sgc" />.
- **GPR-GNN** learns one weight per propagation step, $\sum_k \gamma_k\hat\Amat^k\Hmat$. Learned weights can make the
  response high-pass, which suits graphs where neighbours tend to differ <Cite id="chien2021gprgnn" />.
- **BernNet** writes the response in the Bernstein basis on $[0, 2]$, whose non-negative coefficients give a
  non-negative response by construction <Cite id="he2021bernnet" />.
- **ChebNetII** returns to the Chebyshev basis with coefficients from interpolation, and attributes ChebNet's weak
  benchmark results to ill-behaved learned coefficients rather than to the basis <Cite id="he2022chebnetii" />.

These models are used mainly for node classification on a single graph, where the spectral view explains when a low-pass
filter helps (homophily) and when it hurts (<NoteLink to="graph-neural-networks-under-heterophily" />). For graph-level
tasks and large-scale use, <NoteLink to="message-passing-layers">message-passing layers</NoteLink> with learned,
nonlinear messages dominate. The spectral view also explains <NoteLink to="oversmoothing" />. Without weights and
nonlinearities, $L$ GCN layers apply $\hat\Amat^L$, whose response is $(1 - \lambda)^L$ in the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of
the symmetric Laplacian of the graph with self-loops. Every factor except the one at $\lambda = 0$ has magnitude below
1, so the output converges to a projection onto the lowest frequency.

## Worked example

Take the graph of <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs" />: a triangle on nodes 1, 2, 3, the edge $3{-}4$, and
nodes 5 and 6 attached to node 4. The degrees are $(2, 2, 3, 3, 1, 1)$, and $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Amat$ is unnormalised.
Use the bound $\lambda_{\max} \le 2d_{\max} = 6$, so $\tilde\Lmat = \Lmat/3 - \I$. Filter an impulse at node 1,
$\xvec = (1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)$, with $K = 2$.

**Order 0.** $\bar\xvec_0 = \xvec = (1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)$.

**Order 1.** $\Lmat\xvec$ is the first column of $\Lmat$, $(2, -1, -1, 0, 0, 0)$. Then

$$
\bar\xvec_1 = \tfrac13\Lmat\xvec - \xvec = \paren{-\tfrac13, -\tfrac13, -\tfrac13, 0, 0, 0}.
$$

The signal has reached nodes 2 and 3, the one-hop neighbours.

**Order 2.** First $\Lmat\bar\xvec_1 = -\tfrac13\Lmat(1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0)$. For the indicator of the triangle, row $i$ of
$\Lmat$ gives $d_i$ times the indicator minus the number of triangle neighbours: 0 at nodes 1 and 2, $3 - 2 = 1$ at node
3, and $-1$ at node 4. So $\Lmat\bar\xvec_1 = (0, 0, -\tfrac13, \tfrac13, 0, 0)$ and
$\tilde\Lmat\bar\xvec_1 = \tfrac13\Lmat\bar\xvec_1 - \bar\xvec_1 = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac29, \tfrac19, 0, 0)$. The
recurrence gives

$$
\bar\xvec_2 = 2\tilde\Lmat\bar\xvec_1 - \bar\xvec_0 = \paren{-\tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac49, \tfrac29, 0, 0}.
$$

Node 4, two hops from node 1, is now non-zero. Nodes 5 and 6 are three hops away and stay exactly zero.

**Combine.** Choose $\thetavec = (0.375, -0.5, 0.125)$. Then

$$
\yvec = 0.375\,\bar\xvec_0 - 0.5\,\bar\xvec_1 + 0.125\,\bar\xvec_2 = (0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.222,\ 0.028,\ 0,\ 0).
$$

**Check against the spectral form.** With $t = \lambda/3 - 1$, the response is
$0.375 - 0.5t + 0.125(2t^2 - 1) = \tfrac14(1 - t)^2 = (1 - \lambda/6)^2$, a low-pass that falls from 1 at $\lambda = 0$
to 0 at $\lambda = 6$. So $\yvec$ must equal $(\I - \Lmat/6)^2\xvec$. One step gives
$(\I - \Lmat/6)\xvec = (\tfrac23, \tfrac16, \tfrac16, 0, 0, 0)$, and a second step gives
$(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac29, \tfrac1{36}, 0, 0) = (0.5, 0.25, 0.222, 0.028, 0, 0)$. The two computations agree, and
neither needed an eigenvector.

## Assumptions

- **A symmetric Laplacian.** The derivation uses real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis, which require an
  undirected graph with non-negative weights.
- **A bound on the spectrum.** $\tilde\Lmat$ must have its eigenvalues in $[-1, 1]$, where the $T_k$ are bounded. An
  underestimate of $\lambda_{\max}$ lets $T_k(\tilde\Lmat)$ grow like the polynomial outside $[-1, 1]$.
- **Smooth responses.** Low degrees fit smooth responses well. Tasks that need a sharp frequency selection need high
  degrees, and with them wide <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive fields</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Free-form spectral filters do not transfer.** Gains attached to one graph's eigenvectors are meaningless on another
  graph, and even on the same graph after an edge changes. Polynomial filters transfer because they are defined through
  $\Lmat$.
- **Eigenvector ambiguity.** A free-form filter written in terms of $\Umat$ inherits the sign and rotation ambiguity of
  eigenvectors with repeated eigenvalues. Polynomial filters do not, because $g(\Lmat)$ depends only on the eigenvalues
  and eigenspaces.
- **The choice of Laplacian changes the filter.** Responses learned with $\Lmat$, $\Lmat_{\text{sym}}$ or the
  self-loop-renormalised $\hat\Amat$ differ on irregular graphs, and $\lambda_{\max}$ differs between them.
- **High degree without nonlinearity is not depth.** One degree-10 filter mixes 10 hops linearly. Ten GCN layers mix the
  same neighbourhood with nonlinearities in between, at the risk of <NoteLink to="oversmoothing" />.
- **Learned coefficients can overfit.** Unconstrained $\theta_k$ can produce responses with large oscillations between
  eigenvalues. Interpolation-based or non-negative parametrisations regularise them <Cite id="he2022chebnetii" />
  <Cite id="he2021bernnet" />.
