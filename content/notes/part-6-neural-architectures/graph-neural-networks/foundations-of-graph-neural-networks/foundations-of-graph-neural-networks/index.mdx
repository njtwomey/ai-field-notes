<Definition>
The foundations of graph neural networks are the representation of a graph as network input and the spectral theory
from which graph convolutions were first derived. The first part fixes what a model receives (node features, edges,
edge features) and the symmetry it must respect, permutation of the node labels. The second part defines frequencies on
a graph through the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, and filters as functions of its eigenvalues.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every graph network, from a two-layer <Gloss name="gcn" /> on a citation graph to a message-passing model of molecules,
makes the same two commitments. Its layers read a graph through node features and an adjacency structure, and their
outputs do not depend on how the nodes are numbered. The spectral side explains what those layers compute: a GCN layer
is a low-pass filter on the graph, deep stacks of such filters oversmooth, and Laplacian eigenvectors serve as
<NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encodings</NoteLink>. The chapter gives these facts the definitions and
derivations that later chapters assume <Cite id="hamilton2020grl" />.

## Main ideas

**A graph is a set of feature rows plus relations.** A graph enters a network as a node feature matrix, an edge list or
adjacency matrix, and optional edge and graph features. Predictions are made per node, per edge or per graph, and
batches of small graphs are stored as one block-diagonal graph. <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs" />
covers these formats and tasks.

**Layers must be permutation equivariant.** Relabelling the nodes permutes the rows of the feature matrix and the rows
and columns of the adjacency matrix. A node-level layer must permute its output in the same way, and a graph-level
readout must not change at all. Shared per-node functions combined with sums over neighbours satisfy both; a
<NoteLink to="perceptron">perceptron</NoteLink> on the flattened adjacency matrix does not <Cite id="bronstein2021" />.
The argument and its consequences are in <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs" />.

**The Laplacian defines smoothness and frequency.** The quadratic form of $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Amat$ is the sum of squared
differences across edges. Its eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis ordered from smooth to oscillating, the graph
Fourier basis, and a filter multiplies each frequency by a gain <Cite id="shuman2013" />.
<NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform" /> derives these facts and works them through on a path
graph.

**Polynomial filters are local and cheap.** A free gain per frequency needs the full eigendecomposition and acts on the
whole graph. A degree-$K$ polynomial in the Laplacian needs neither: it is $K$ sparse matrix products and reaches
exactly $K$ hops. The Chebyshev basis makes the polynomial well conditioned (ChebNet), and its first-order case with
tied coefficients is the graph convolutional network. <NoteLink to="spectral-graph-convolution" /> follows that path
and the polynomial-filter models that came after it.
