<Definition>
Oversquashing is the loss of sensitivity of a <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing</NoteLink>
network's output at a node $v$ to the input features of distant nodes $u$, because information from a
<NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive field</NoteLink> that grows quickly with depth must
pass through fixed-width vectors along few edges <Cite id="alon2021" />. It is measured by the Jacobian
$\partial\hvec_v^{(r)}/\partial\xvec_u$ of the layer-$r$ features of $v$ with respect to the input of $u$, which is
bounded by the entry $(\hat\Amat^r)_{vu}$ of a power of the normalised adjacency matrix
<Cite id="topping2022curvature" />. **Graph rewiring** changes the edges that messages travel along, by adding edges
across bottlenecks, a virtual node joined to every node, or attention between all pairs, so that distant nodes interact
through fewer and wider routes.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A node's receptive field after $r$ layers is every node within $r$ hops. In a tree where every node has two children,
that field holds $2^r$ nodes at the last level alone. A <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss> network must summarise
all of them in one vector of fixed width $F$ at the root. As $r$ grows, the vector's capacity stays the same while the
information it must carry grows exponentially. Alon and Yahav named this oversquashing and showed that popular networks
fail to fit long-range signals even on their training data <Cite id="alon2021" />.

The graph's shape makes it worse. Two dense communities joined by one edge must exchange everything through that edge.
Each layer passes one message per edge, and the message from the bridge node is one vector averaged with all its
other neighbours' messages. Distant information arrives diluted by every averaging step on the way. Adding layers does
not help: the receptive field already contains the distant node, and more layers mostly add more averaging.

Oversquashing differs from two neighbouring failures. **Under-reaching** is a network with fewer layers than the
distance between the nodes that must interact; more layers fix it.
**<NoteLink to="oversmoothing">Oversmoothing</NoteLink>** is the collapse of all features to one profile with depth.
Oversquashing happens in networks deep enough to reach the distant node and not yet oversmoothed, when the graph's
topology throttles the flow between the two.

## Details

### Sensitivity through powers of the adjacency

Write the propagation matrix with self-loops as
$\hat\Amat = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}(\Amat + \I)\tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}$, where $\tilde d_i$ is the degree of node $i$ plus one.
Consider a message passing network with scalar features whose message and update functions have derivatives bounded by
$\alpha$ and $\beta$. Topping et al. prove that for a node $u$ at distance $r + 1$ from $v$,

$$
\abs{\frac{\partial h_v^{(r+1)}}{\partial x_u}} \le (\alpha\beta)^{r+1}\, \paren{\hat\Amat^{r+1}}_{vu}
$$

<Cite id="topping2022curvature" />. The weights set the factor $(\alpha\beta)^{r+1}$; the graph sets
$(\hat\Amat^{r+1})_{vu}$. At the exact distance, only shortest paths contribute to the matrix power, so

$$
\paren{\hat\Amat^{r}}_{vu} = \sum_{\text{shortest paths } v = w_0, w_1, \dots, w_r = u}\ \prod_{t=1}^{r}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde d_{w_{t-1}}\tilde d_{w_t}}} .
$$

Every edge on the path divides by the square root of its endpoints' degrees. A path through high-degree nodes, or
through few parallel routes, gives a small entry. When the ball of radius $r + 1$ around $v$ is a binary tree, the entry
for a node at distance $r + 1$ is $2^{-1}3^{-r}$: exponential decay with distance <Cite id="topping2022curvature" />.

Width and depth change only the factor in front. Wider layers raise $\alpha\beta$ and so the bound for every pair, not
just the distant ones. More layers let longer walks contribute to $(\hat\Amat^r)_{vu}$, but the entry then tends to its
oversmoothed limit $\sqrt{\tilde d_u\tilde d_v}/\sum_i \tilde d_i$, and deep stacks bring
<NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">vanishing gradients</NoteLink>. The topology remains the decisive term:
oversquashing is worst between nodes with high commute time, the expected number of steps a
<NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> takes to go from $u$ to $v$ and back
<Cite id="digiovanni2023oversquashing" />.

### Bottlenecks: the Cheeger constant and curvature

Three graph quantities identify bottlenecks.

- **The Cheeger constant.** For a set of nodes $S$, let $\abs{\partial S}$ be the number of edges leaving it and
  $\operatorname{vol}(S) = \sum_{i \in S} d_i$ its total degree. The Cheeger constant is
  $h_G = \min_S \abs{\partial S} / \min\paren{\operatorname{vol}(S), \operatorname{vol}(\bar S)}$. A small $h_G$ means
  the graph splits into two large parts joined by few edges. The Cheeger inequality ties it to the spectral gap
  $\lambda_1$, the second-smallest eigenvalue of the
  <NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform">normalised Laplacian</NoteLink>
  $\I - \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$: $2h_G \ge \lambda_1 \ge h_G^2/2$ <Cite id="topping2022curvature" />. The
  spectral gap is cheap to compute and stands in for $h_G$.
- **Curvature.** An edge between two nodes whose neighbourhoods overlap, in triangles or 4-cycles, has positive
  curvature, as in a clique. An edge whose endpoints share nothing, so that their neighbourhoods spread apart as in a
  tree, has negative curvature. Topping et al. define the balanced Forman curvature $\operatorname{Ric}(i, j)$ from the
  degrees and the triangles and 4-cycles at the edge. For an edge with no triangles or 4-cycles it is
  $2/d_i + 2/d_j - 2$. They prove that strongly negatively curved edges cause oversquashing: they bound the average
  Jacobian between a node and the nodes two hops away across the edge <Cite id="topping2022curvature" />.
- **Effective resistance.** Treat each edge as a unit resistor. The effective resistance between $u$ and $v$ is large
  when few, long paths join them, and it is proportional to their commute time. Black et al. use the total effective
  resistance over all pairs as a measure of oversquashing in the whole graph <Cite id="black2023resistance" />.

### Rewiring

Rewiring passes messages over a graph different from the input graph. Each method adds routes across bottlenecks.

- **Virtual node.** Add one node joined to every node, the master node of Gilmer et al. <Cite id="gilmer2017" />.
  Every pair is then within two hops. The virtual node itself becomes a bottleneck of width $F$ that every global
  message shares.
- **Fully adjacent layer.** Make the last layer's graph complete, so every pair interacts once. Alon and Yahav removed
  the bottleneck of previously tuned networks this way and improved their results without new weights
  <Cite id="alon2021" />. Attention between all pairs in every layer is a <NoteLink to="graph-transformer" />.
- **Curvature-based rewiring (SDRF).** Stochastic discrete Ricci flow repeatedly finds the most negatively curved edge,
  adds an edge between the neighbourhoods of its endpoints, sampled with a preference for the edge that most raises its
  curvature, and optionally removes the most positively curved edge <Cite id="topping2022curvature" />. The changes stay
  local: each added edge closes a triangle or a 4-cycle around a bridge.
- **First-order spectral rewiring (FoSR).** Add edges one at a time to raise the spectral gap as fast as possible
  <Cite id="karhadkar2023fosr" />. Recomputing the gap for every candidate edge is expensive, so FoSR uses the
  first-order change of the second-largest eigenvalue of $\Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$. With $\xvec$ the
  corresponding eigenvector, it adds the non-edge $(i, j)$ that minimises
  $$
  \frac{x_i x_j}{\sqrt{(1 + d_i)(1 + d_j)}} .
  $$
  The eigenvector has opposite signs on the two sides of the weakest cut, so the product $x_i x_j$ is most negative
  for a pair that straddles it, at large $\abs{x_i}$ and $\abs{x_j}$ and low degree. FoSR passes messages along the
  original and the added edges with separate weights, so the network can learn how much to use the new edges.
- **Effective-resistance rewiring.** Add the edges that most reduce the total effective resistance
  <Cite id="black2023resistance" />.

Rewiring conflicts with <NoteLink to="oversmoothing" />. Oversmoothing proceeds at a rate set by the spectral gap, and
rewiring raises the gap, so a heavily rewired graph oversmooths in fewer layers. Keeping the added edges as a separate
edge type, as FoSR does, lets the network weight them down where they hurt <Cite id="karhadkar2023fosr" />.

## Worked example

Take two cliques of 5 nodes joined by a path of 3 nodes: nodes 0 to 4 form the first clique, nodes 5, 6, 7 the path
and nodes 8 to 12 the second clique, with edges 4–5, 5–6, 6–7 and 7–8. There are 13 nodes and 24 edges. Node 4 has
degree 5 (four clique neighbours and node 5), and so does node 8; the other clique nodes have degree 4 and the path
nodes degree 2.

**Receptive field.** Node 12 is at distance 6 from node 0, along the unique shortest path
$0, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 12$. A network needs 6 layers for node 12 to see node 0 at all. After 6 layers the receptive field
of node 12 is the whole graph.

**Sensitivity at the exact distance.** With self-loops the degrees along the path are $\tilde d = 5, 6, 3, 3, 3, 6, 5$.
The product formula gives

$$
\paren{\hat\Amat^6}_{12,0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5 \cdot 6}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{6 \cdot 3}} \cdot \frac13 \cdot \frac13
\cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{3 \cdot 6}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{6 \cdot 5}} = \frac{1}{30 \cdot 18 \cdot 9} = \frac{1}{4860}
\approx 2.1 \times 10^{-4} .
$$

On a plain path of 7 nodes, where the ends have $\tilde d = 2$ and the interior nodes $\tilde d = 3$, the same distance
gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt6} \cdot \paren{\frac13}^4 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt6} = \frac{1}{486} \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-3}$, ten
times more. The cliques cost a factor of 10: node 0's feature is averaged with four clique neighbours at node 4, and
the arriving message at node 8 is averaged with four more.

**Bottleneck.** The Cheeger constant is attained by $S = \set{0, \dots, 5}$, the first clique and the first path node.
One edge, 5–6, leaves $S$, and $\operatorname{vol}(S) = 4 \cdot 4 + 5 + 2 = 23$ against 25 for the rest, so
$h_G = 1/23 = 0.043$. The spectral gap of the normalised Laplacian is $\lambda_1 = 0.022$, inside the Cheeger bounds
$2h_G = 0.087$ and $h_G^2/2 = 0.0009$. The bridge edge 4–5 has no triangles or 4-cycles, so its curvature is
$2/5 + 2/2 - 2 = -0.6$. An edge inside a clique of 5 has curvature $5/4$.

**Virtual node.** Join a virtual node to all 13 nodes. Nodes 0 and 12 now have $\tilde d = 6$ and the virtual node
$\tilde d = 14$, and the shortest path has 2 edges:

$$
\paren{\hat\Amat^2}_{12,0} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6 \cdot 14}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{14 \cdot 6}} = \frac{1}{84}
\approx 1.2 \times 10^{-2},
$$

about 60 times the original value at a third of the depth. The spectral gap rises from 0.022 to 0.214.

**FoSR.** The second eigenvector of the normalised adjacency is $-0.312$ on nodes 0 to 3, $-0.318$ on node 4, $-0.103,
0, 0.103$ on the path, and the mirror image on the second clique. A pair across the cut scores
$x_i x_j / \sqrt{(1 + d_i)(1 + d_j)}$. Nodes 0 and 9 score $-0.312 \times 0.312 / \sqrt{5 \cdot 5} = -0.0195$. Nodes 4
and 8 have larger entries but degree 5, and score $-0.318 \times 0.318 / \sqrt{6 \cdot 6} = -0.0169$. So the first edge
FoSR adds joins node 0 of the first clique to node 9 of the second (ties broken by node number), and the next two join 1
to 10 and 2 to 11. After three edges the spectral gap is 0.234 and $(\hat\Amat^6)_{12,0} = 6.1 \times 10^{-2}$, about
300 times the original.

## Assumptions

- **The task needs long-range interactions.** Oversquashing matters only when the label of a node or graph depends on
  features far away. Many <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs">node classification</NoteLink> benchmarks are
  local, and rewiring them can hurt.
- **The bound is an upper bound.** $(\hat\Amat^r)_{vu}$ bounds the Jacobian for networks of the <Gloss name="gcn" />
  form with bounded derivatives. Attention and gating reweight the edges: networks that weight incoming edges unequally,
  such as <NoteLink to="graph-attention-network">graph attention</NoteLink> and gated graph networks, are less affected
  than GCN and <Gloss name="gin" /> <Cite id="alon2021" />.
- **The input graph is not sacred.** Rewiring assumes the edges are a channel for computation, not a fact the model
  must respect. Where an edge has a physical meaning, such as a chemical bond, added edges should be marked as a
  separate type.

## Pitfalls

- **Rewiring speeds up oversmoothing.** Every added edge raises the spectral gap and with it the rate at which features
  converge. A heavily rewired graph needs fewer layers or a separate edge type for the new edges.
- **Long-range benchmarks need careful baselines.** The <NoteLink to="graph-learning-benchmarks">Long Range Graph
  Benchmark</NoteLink> was built so that tasks require long-range interactions, and graph transformers first
  outperformed message passing networks on it <Cite id="dwivedi2022lrgb" />. After basic hyperparameter tuning of the
  message passing baselines the gap vanished on several of its datasets <Cite id="tonshoff2023lrgb" />. A claimed gain
  from rewiring or attention is only as good as the tuning of the baseline.
- **Cost of the measures.** Exact curvature, effective resistance and the Cheeger constant are expensive on large
  graphs: the Cheeger constant is NP-hard to compute exactly, effective resistance needs a pseudoinverse of the
  Laplacian, and curvature scales with the number of edges times a power of the maximum degree. FoSR's first-order
  rule and power iteration keep spectral rewiring cheap.
- **A virtual node is a new bottleneck.** It gives every pair a two-hop route, but every route shares one vector of
  width $F$. Several virtual nodes, or attention, widen the channel.
