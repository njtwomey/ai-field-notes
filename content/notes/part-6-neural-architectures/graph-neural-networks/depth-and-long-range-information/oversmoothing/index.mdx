<Definition>
Oversmoothing is the convergence of the node features of a deep <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message
passing</NoteLink> network to vectors that no longer depend on the input features, so that nodes become
indistinguishable as layers are added. For a <Gloss name="gcn" /> on a connected graph with propagation matrix
$\hat\Amat = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}(\Amat + \I)\tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}$, the powers $\hat\Amat^L\Xmat$ converge to a matrix
whose row $i$ is $\sqrt{\tilde d_i}$ times one common vector, and the degree-normalised Dirichlet energy of the features
falls at least as fast as $\lambda^{2L}$, where $\lambda < 1$ is the second-largest magnitude among the eigenvalues of
$\hat\Amat$ <Cite id="li2018" /> <Cite id="oono2020" /> <Cite id="cai2020oversmoothing" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A graph convolution replaces each node's features by a weighted average of its own and its neighbours' features. One
average removes noise: nodes in the same community pull towards their community mean. A second average reaches two
hops and smooths more. Every further average mixes in more of the graph, and on a connected graph the averages keep
mixing until every node holds the same blend of all the input features.

The <NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform">graph Fourier</NoteLink> view says the same thing in
frequencies. Multiplication by $\hat\Amat$ is a low-pass filter: it scales the component of the features along each
eigenvector of $\hat\Amat$ by that eigenvector's eigenvalue. The top eigenvalue is exactly 1 and every other one has
magnitude below 1. Repeated multiplication therefore keeps only the top component. The top eigenvector of $\hat\Amat$
is proportional to $\sqrt{\tilde d_i}$, the square root of each node's degree plus one, so the only information left
after many layers is the degree of each node and one vector shared by all of them.

The decay happens at different speeds for different components. High-frequency components, such as the differences
between nodes inside one community, have eigenvalues near 0 and vanish in one or two layers. The component that
separates two communities has an eigenvalue near 1 and fades slowly. A few layers therefore help classification, and
many layers hurt it <Cite id="keriven2022smoothing" />. Graph convolutional networks are usually two or three layers
deep for this reason. On Cora, the accuracy of a plain GCN falls from 81.1% with 2 layers to 28.7% with 64
<Cite id="chen2020gcnii" />.

## The limit of repeated propagation

Let the graph be undirected and connected with $N$ nodes. Write $\tilde\Amat = \Amat + \I$ for the adjacency matrix
with self-loops, $\tilde d_i = 1 + \deg(i)$ for its degrees and $\tilde\Dmat = \diag(\tilde d_1, \dots, \tilde d_N)$.
The matrix $\hat\Amat = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}\tilde\Amat\tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}$ is symmetric, so it has orthonormal
eigenvectors $\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_N$ and real eigenvalues

$$
1 = \mu_1 > \mu_2 \ge \dots \ge \mu_N > -1.
$$

The bounds come from the <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">GCN</NoteLink> note: the eigenvalues lie in
$(-1, 1]$ because every node has a self-loop. The top eigenvalue is simple on a connected graph, so $\mu_2 < 1$. Its
eigenvector is $\uvec_1 = \tilde\Dmat^{1/2}\ones / \norm{\tilde\Dmat^{1/2}\ones}$, since
$\hat\Amat\tilde\Dmat^{1/2}\ones = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}\tilde\Amat\ones = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}\tilde\dvec =
\tilde\Dmat^{1/2}\ones$, with $\tilde\dvec$ the vector of degrees.

Expand the feature matrix $\Xmat \in \reals^{N \times F}$ in the eigenvectors, $\Xmat = \sum_{k} \uvec_k \cvec_k^\tr$
with coefficients $\cvec_k = \Xmat^\tr\uvec_k \in \reals^F$. Then

$$
\hat\Amat^L\Xmat = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \mu_k^L\, \uvec_k \cvec_k^\tr \;\longrightarrow\; \uvec_1\cvec_1^\tr
\qquad (L \to \infty),
$$

because $\abs{\mu_k} < 1$ for $k \ge 2$. Row $i$ of the limit is

$$
\hvec_i^{(\infty)} = \sqrt{\tilde d_i}\; \frac{\sum_j \sqrt{\tilde d_j}\, \xvec_j}{\sum_j \tilde d_j}.
$$

Every node holds the same vector, scaled by the square root of its degree. The distance to the limit shrinks as
$\lambda^L$, with $\lambda = \max(\abs{\mu_2}, \abs{\mu_N})$. The mean-aggregation variant
$\tilde\Dmat^{-1}\tilde\Amat$ has the same eigenvalues and converges to identical rows, the degree-weighted average of
the input features.

## The Dirichlet energy decays geometrically

The Dirichlet energy measures how far the features are from that limit. With the normalised Laplacian
$\tilde\Lmat = \I - \hat\Amat$ of the graph with self-loops,

$$
E(\Hmat) = \trace\paren{\Hmat^\tr \tilde\Lmat\, \Hmat}
= \frac12 \sum_{i,j} \tilde A_{ij} \norm{\frac{\hvec_i}{\sqrt{\tilde d_i}} - \frac{\hvec_j}{\sqrt{\tilde d_j}}}^2 .
$$

The second form is the quadratic form of a normalised Laplacian, derived in the
<NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform">graph Laplacian</NoteLink> note. $E(\Hmat) \ge 0$, and
$E(\Hmat) = 0$ exactly when every row $\hvec_i / \sqrt{\tilde d_i}$ is the same vector, which is the oversmoothed
state. The central result bounds one layer.

**Claim.** $E(\hat\Amat\Hmat) \le \lambda^2 E(\Hmat)$ for every $\Hmat$.

**Step 1: the energy in the eigenbasis.** $\tilde\Lmat$ has the eigenvectors of $\hat\Amat$ with eigenvalues
$1 - \mu_k \ge 0$. Substituting $\Hmat = \sum_k \uvec_k\cvec_k^\tr$ and using orthonormality,

$$
E(\Hmat) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} (1 - \mu_k) \norm{\cvec_k}^2 .
$$

The $k = 1$ term is zero because $\mu_1 = 1$: the top component carries no energy.

**Step 2: one propagation.** $\hat\Amat\Hmat = \sum_k \mu_k\uvec_k\cvec_k^\tr$ has coefficients $\mu_k\cvec_k$, so

$$
E(\hat\Amat\Hmat) = \sum_{k=2}^{N} (1 - \mu_k)\, \mu_k^2 \norm{\cvec_k}^2 .
$$

**Step 3: bound each term.** For $k \ge 2$, $\mu_k^2 \le \lambda^2$ and $1 - \mu_k \ge 0$, so each term is at most
$\lambda^2 (1 - \mu_k)\norm{\cvec_k}^2$. Summing gives $E(\hat\Amat\Hmat) \le \lambda^2 E(\Hmat)$.

Applying the claim $L$ times gives $E(\hat\Amat^L\Xmat) \le \lambda^{2L} E(\Xmat)$. Equality holds when $\Xmat$ has
components only along the eigenvectors with $\abs{\mu_k} = \lambda$, so the rate is exact in the worst case.

**Weights and nonlinearities.** A full GCN layer is $\Hmat' = \sigma(\hat\Amat\Hmat\Wmat)$. Two further facts extend the
bound <Cite id="cai2020oversmoothing" />. Multiplying by $\Wmat$ scales the energy by at most the square of its largest
singular value $s$: $E(\Hmat\Wmat) \le s^2 E(\Hmat)$, because each $\norm{\Wmat^\tr\cvec_k} \le s\norm{\cvec_k}$. A
<Gloss name="relu" /> or <NoteLink to="activation-functions">leaky ReLU</NoteLink> does not increase the energy
(derivation below). Together,

$$
E\paren{\Hmat^{(L)}} \le (s\lambda)^{2L}\, E(\Xmat)
$$

when every weight matrix has largest singular value at most $s$. The energy vanishes exponentially whenever
$s\lambda < 1$. Oono and Suzuki prove the matching statement for the distance of $\Hmat^{(L)}$ to the subspace of
features that carry only degree and connected-component information <Cite id="oono2020" />.

<Derivation title="A ReLU does not increase the Dirichlet energy">
Let $\sigma(a) = \max(a, 0)$ act elementwise. For $c > 0$, $\sigma(a)/c = \sigma(a/c)$. So for each feature $f$ and
each pair $(i, j)$,
$$
\abs{\frac{\sigma(h_{if})}{\sqrt{\tilde d_i}} - \frac{\sigma(h_{jf})}{\sqrt{\tilde d_j}}}
= \abs{\sigma\paren{\frac{h_{if}}{\sqrt{\tilde d_i}}} - \sigma\paren{\frac{h_{jf}}{\sqrt{\tilde d_j}}}}
\le \abs{\frac{h_{if}}{\sqrt{\tilde d_i}} - \frac{h_{jf}}{\sqrt{\tilde d_j}}},
$$
because $\sigma$ is 1-Lipschitz. Squaring, weighting by $\tilde A_{ij} \ge 0$ and summing gives
$E(\sigma(\Hmat)) \le E(\Hmat)$. The same argument holds for a leaky ReLU, which is also positively homogeneous and
1-Lipschitz.
</Derivation>

## PairNorm

PairNorm is a normalisation layer that keeps the features of a deep graph network from collapsing to one point
<Cite id="zhao2020pairnorm" />. After each propagation it centres the features and rescales them to a fixed total size:

$$
\tilde\xvec_i = \xvec_i - \frac1N \sum_{j} \xvec_j, \qquad
\dot\xvec_i = s\, \frac{\tilde\xvec_i}{\sqrt{\tfrac1N \sum_j \norm{\tilde\xvec_j}^2}},
$$

with a scale $s > 0$ chosen as a hyperparameter. After centring, the total squared distance between all pairs of
rows is $\sum_{i,j}\norm{\tilde\xvec_i - \tilde\xvec_j}^2 = 2N\sum_i\norm{\tilde\xvec_i}^2$. The rescaling fixes it at
$2N^2 s^2$ whatever the depth. Propagation still pulls neighbours together, but the nodes can no longer all
approach one point. PairNorm adds no parameters and works with any <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss> layer.

PairNorm removes the collapse of scale, not the collapse of direction. Repeated propagation followed by
renormalisation is a power iteration: the features converge to the dominant eigenvectors of $\hat\Amat$ after the top
one, so their rank still falls towards 1 <Cite id="roth2023rankcollapse" />. On a graph with two communities that
direction separates the communities. On graphs with several classes, or classes that do not follow the graph's
coarsest cut, it does not.

## Worked example

Take two triangles joined by one edge: nodes 1, 2, 3 form a triangle, nodes 4, 5, 6 form another, and an edge joins
3 and 4. With self-loops, nodes 1, 2, 5 and 6 have $\tilde d = 3$ and the two bridge nodes have $\tilde d = 4$. The
entries of $\hat\Amat$ are $1/3$ between two nodes of degree 3, $1/\sqrt{12} = 0.289$ between degrees 3 and 4, and
$1/4$ between the two bridge nodes and on their diagonal.

**Spectrum.** The eigenvalues of $\hat\Amat$ are $1,\ 0.860,\ 0.167,\ 0,\ 0,\ -0.194$. The second eigenvector is
$(-0.452, -0.452, -0.303, 0.303, 0.452, 0.452)$, negative on one triangle and positive on the other: it is the
community indicator. So $\lambda = 0.860$ and the energy shrinks by at least $\lambda^2 = 0.740$ per layer.

**One layer.** Start from the scalar feature $\xvec = (3, 1, 2, 0, 1, -1)$, larger on the first triangle, with noise
inside each triangle. Node 1 averages itself, node 2 and node 3: $h_1 = \tfrac13 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + 0.289
\cdot 2 = 1.911$. Node 4 averages node 3, itself and nodes 5 and 6: $h_4 = \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac14 \cdot 0 +
0.289\,(1 - 1) = 0.5$. All six values are in the table.

**Many layers.** The table repeats the propagation. The gap is the mean of the first triangle minus the mean of the
second.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 3 | 1 | 2 | 0 | 1 | −1 | 5.048 | 2.000 |
| 1 | 1.911 | 1.911 | 1.655 | 0.500 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.610 | 1.659 |
| 2 | 1.751 | 1.751 | 1.642 | 0.539 | 0.144 | 0.144 | 0.446 | 1.439 |
| 4 | 1.544 | 1.544 | 1.494 | 0.692 | 0.349 | 0.349 | 0.244 | 1.064 |
| 8 | 1.274 | 1.274 | 1.312 | 0.873 | 0.619 | 0.619 | 0.073 | 0.583 |
| 16 | 1.045 | 1.045 | 1.159 | 1.027 | 0.848 | 0.848 | 0.0066 | 0.175 |
| $\infty$ | 0.946 | 0.946 | 1.093 | 1.093 | 0.946 | 0.946 | 0 | 0 |

Three phases are visible.

- **Layer 1 denoises.** Nodes 1 and 2 become equal, and so do nodes 5 and 6. The energy falls by a factor of 8, far
  more than $\lambda^2$, because the noise sat on the eigenvectors with eigenvalues $0.167$, $0$ and $-0.194$, which
  one multiplication nearly removes. A threshold at 1 now classifies every node correctly.
- **Layers 2 to 16 erode the communities.** Only the components along $\uvec_1$ and $\uvec_2$ remain. The energy
  ratio per layer settles at $0.446/0.610 = 0.73$, close to $\lambda^2 = 0.740$, and the gap shrinks by about 0.86 per
  layer. By layer 32, node 4 of the second triangle holds a larger value than node 1 of the first (1.087 against
  0.955).
- **The limit keeps only degrees.** The last row follows from the formula for $\hvec^{(\infty)}$:
  $\sum_j \sqrt{\tilde d_j} x_j = \sqrt3 (3 + 1 + 1 - 1) + 2 (2 + 0) = 10.93$ and $\sum_j \tilde d_j = 20$, so
  $h_i^{(\infty)} = \sqrt{\tilde d_i} \times 0.546$. That is $0.946$ for degree 3 and $1.093$ for degree 4. The bridge
  nodes end largest because they have the highest degree, whatever their community.

## Remedies

Several families of fixes trade some smoothing for depth.

- **<NoteLink to="residual-network">Skip connections</NoteLink>.**
  <NoteLink to="residual-and-jumping-knowledge-graph-networks">Residual, initial-residual and jumping-knowledge
  connections</NoteLink> keep a path from the input features to every layer. With an initial residual of weight
  $\alpha$, the propagation $\Hmat \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\hat\Amat\Hmat + \alpha\Xmat$ converges to personalised PageRank
  features instead of the degree profile.
- **Normalisation.** PairNorm, above, keeps the spread of the features fixed.
- **Fewer multiplications.** DropEdge removes a random fraction of edges at each training step, which slows the
  convergence without changing its limit <Cite id="rong2020dropedge" /> <Cite id="chen2020gcnii" />. Decoupled models
  apply a fixed number of propagation steps once, outside the network; see <NoteLink to="untrained-graph-propagation" />
  and <NoteLink to="label-spreading" />.
- **Weight scale.** The bound $(s\lambda)^{2L}$ says nothing when the weights have singular values above $1/\lambda$.
  Oono and Suzuki propose scaling the weights with this in mind <Cite id="oono2020" />.

Preventing oversmoothing is necessary but not sufficient for a useful deep graph network: a model that keeps the
energy high can still be no more accurate than a shallow one <Cite id="rusch2023oversmoothing" />.

## Assumptions

- **Connected, undirected graph with self-loops.** On a graph with several components the features converge
  separately on each component, to the same degree profile scaled by a different vector. Without self-loops a
  bipartite component has the eigenvalue $-1$, and the features oscillate rather than converge.
- **No bias and bounded weights.** The energy bound uses $\Hmat' = \sigma(\hat\Amat\Hmat\Wmat)$ with
  $s\lambda < 1$. A bias adds the same vector to every row, which has nonzero energy when degrees differ, so the energy
  need not tend to zero.
- **Low-pass is the right prior.** Smoothing helps only while connected nodes tend to share labels. On heterophilous
  graphs even one layer can remove the useful signal; see
  <NoteLink to="graph-neural-networks-under-heterophily">graph neural networks under heterophily</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Energy decay is not loss of information in exact arithmetic.** At depth 30 the community component in the worked
  example is about $0.86^{30} \approx 0.01$ of its starting size, but it still points the right way, and an
  unregularised linear readout could rescale it. <Gloss name="weight-decay">Weight decay</Gloss>, noise and finite
  precision lose it. The figure's readout uses a small ridge penalty, as weight decay does in training.
- **Oversmoothing is not the only reason deep graph networks fail.** Deep stacks also suffer from
  <NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">vanishing gradients</NoteLink> and from
  <NoteLink to="oversquashing-and-graph-rewiring">oversquashing</NoteLink>, the compression of information from many
  distant nodes. Adding edges to fight oversquashing tends to raise the spectral gap $1 - \mu_2$ and so to speed up
  oversmoothing.
- **Depth is often not needed.** A node classifier on a homophilous graph rarely needs more than two or three hops. A
  deeper <NoteLink to="receptive-field-and-dilated-convolution">receptive field</NoteLink> is better bought with
  decoupled propagation than with more layers of weights.
- **Measuring with the wrong quantity.** The raw distance between node features shrinks when the features shrink in
  scale, without any loss of contrast between nodes. Scale-invariant measures, such as the energy divided by
  $\norm{\Hmat}^2$, separate the two effects.
