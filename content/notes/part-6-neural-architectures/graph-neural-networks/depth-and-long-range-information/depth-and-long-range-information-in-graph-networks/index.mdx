<Definition>
This chapter covers what happens to a <Gloss name="gnn">graph neural network</Gloss> as it gets deeper and as the
nodes that must interact get further apart. A message passing network with $L$ layers lets each node read its $L$-hop
neighbourhood, so long-range tasks seem to need deep networks. Two effects stop that from working: features converge
to one profile with depth, and information from distant nodes is compressed through the graph's bottlenecks. The notes
derive both effects and the architectures that counter them.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Many <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs">graph tasks</NoteLink> depend on nodes far apart: whether two atoms
at opposite ends of a protein fragment are in contact, which function a peptide of a hundred residues has, or how a
signal spreads across a road network. A <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolutional
network</NoteLink> with two or three layers sees only two or three hops. Stacking more layers of the same kind usually
makes accuracy worse, not better. On the Cora citation graph the accuracy of a plain GCN falls from 81.1% at 2 layers to
28.7% at 64 <Cite id="chen2020gcnii" />.

The chapter explains that failure in terms a practitioner can measure, the spectrum of the propagation matrix and the
sensitivity of one node's output to another node's input, and it compares the fixes by what they change and what they
cost.

## Main ideas

**Repeated averaging converges.** A graph convolution multiplies the features by a normalised adjacency matrix whose
top eigenvalue is 1 and whose others have magnitude below 1. Repeated multiplication keeps only the top eigenvector, so
every node's features approach one shared vector scaled by its degree. The Dirichlet energy, the degree-normalised
squared difference across edges, falls at least geometrically, at a rate set by the second-largest eigenvalue
magnitude. <NoteLink to="oversmoothing" /> derives the rate, shows that a few layers help before many layers hurt, and
defines PairNorm, which holds the spread of the features fixed.

**Skip paths change the limit.** A plain residual connection only slows the convergence. A connection back to the
input changes where the features converge: with restart weight $\alpha$, propagation converges to personalised
PageRank features, which still depend on the input. GCNII adds this initial residual and an identity mapping to every
layer and trains at 64 layers. Jumping knowledge lets each node combine the outputs of all its layers.
<NoteLink to="residual-and-jumping-knowledge-graph-networks" /> covers these designs, DropEdge, and their costs.

**Bottlenecks throttle long-range signal.** The influence of node $u$'s input on node $v$'s output after $r$ layers is
bounded by the $(v, u)$ entry of the $r$-th power of the normalised adjacency <Cite id="topping2022curvature" />. That
entry is a sum over paths of products of inverse square-root degrees, small when few paths join the two nodes. The
Cheeger constant, the spectral gap, negative edge curvature and effective resistance all measure such bottlenecks.
<NoteLink to="oversquashing-and-graph-rewiring" /> derives the bound and computes it on a barbell graph.

**Rewiring adds routes.** Adding edges across bottlenecks, by curvature (SDRF), by the first-order change of the
spectral gap (FoSR) or by a virtual node joined to every node, shortens the paths between distant nodes. Rewiring also
raises the spectral gap and so speeds up oversmoothing: the two effects pull in opposite directions, and a good design
balances them.

**Global attention is the extreme rewiring.** A <NoteLink to="graph-transformer" /> lets every node attend to every
other node, so every pair is one hop apart, and supplies the graph through <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional
encodings</NoteLink>, a learned bias per shortest-path distance or a message passing branch beside the attention. It
costs $O(N^2)$ per layer in the number of nodes $N$, so large graphs need sparse or
<NoteLink to="linear-attention">linear attention</NoteLink> <Cite id="muller2024attending" />.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="oversmoothing" />. The spectral argument there underlies every other note in the chapter.
- <NoteLink to="residual-and-jumping-knowledge-graph-networks" /> uses the same example graph and asks which
  connections stop the collapse.
- <NoteLink to="oversquashing-and-graph-rewiring" /> moves from depth to distance: networks deep enough to reach a node
  that still cannot hear it.
- <NoteLink to="graph-transformer" /> takes rewiring to the complete graph and covers how the structure is put back.

All four notes measure a network by quantities of the weight-free propagation matrix, so their figures can be compared:
the energy decay of oversmoothing, the matrix powers of oversquashing and the distance bias of attention. Preventing
oversmoothing alone does not make a deep network useful <Cite id="rusch2023oversmoothing" />; the notes give the
evidence for and against depth in each case.
