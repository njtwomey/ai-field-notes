<Definition>
A **graph neural network** computes a vector for every node of a graph, and optionally for every edge or for the
whole graph, by layers that combine each node's vector with those of its neighbours. The layers are shared across nodes
and do not depend on how the nodes are numbered: relabelling the nodes relabels the outputs in the same way. Most such
networks are instances of message passing <Cite id="gilmer2017" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Much data is relational: molecules are atoms joined by bonds, social networks are people joined by friendships, a
recommender's data is users joined to items they interacted with, and a road network is junctions joined by roads. A
multilayer perceptron needs a fixed-length input in a fixed order, and a graph has neither. Graph neural networks take
the graph as given and learn from both node features and connectivity.

Typical tasks are node classification, such as labelling papers in a citation network; link prediction, such as
recommending items; and graph-level prediction, such as a molecule's properties. In recommendation,
<NoteLink to="lightgcn" /> propagates user and item embeddings over the interaction graph. The same symmetry argument
that motivates graph networks also explains convolutional networks and transformers: each respects a symmetry of its
input domain, translations of a grid or permutations of a set or graph <Cite id="bronstein2021" />.

## Main ideas

**Messages from neighbours, aggregated without order.** A
<NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network" /> updates each node from its own vector and a sum, mean or maximum of
messages from its neighbours. Because the aggregate ignores order, the layer commutes with any relabelling of the
nodes. Stacking $K$ rounds lets information travel $K$ hops. A readout turns the node vectors into a graph-level
output.

**Graph convolution as spectral filtering.** The <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network" /> updates node features by
a degree-normalised average over each node and its neighbours, followed by a shared linear map and a nonlinearity. It
is derived as a first-order approximation of a filter defined through the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, the
graph analogue of Fourier modes. The note applies it to semi-supervised node classification.

**Learned neighbour weights.** The <NoteLink to="graph-attention-network" /> replaces the fixed degree-based weights
with attention weights computed from both endpoints of each edge and normalised by a softmax over the neighbourhood.
It is the graph form of the attention used in transformers, restricted to the edges of the graph.

**Depth and expressiveness are limited.** Many rounds of averaging make all node vectors in a connected graph similar,
the oversmoothing problem, so graph networks are usually shallow. A message passing network can distinguish two graphs
only if the Weisfeiler–Lehman colour refinement test does, so it cannot count triangles or detect some cycles. Both
limits are derived in <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network" /> and
<NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network" />.
