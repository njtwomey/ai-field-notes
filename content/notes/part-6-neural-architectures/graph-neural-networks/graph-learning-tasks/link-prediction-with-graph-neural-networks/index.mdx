<Definition>
**Link prediction** scores unobserved node pairs $(u, v)$ of a graph by how likely an edge between them is. A
<Gloss name="gnn">graph neural network</Gloss> encoder maps every node to an embedding $\zvec_v \in \reals^k$ by message
passing over the observed edges, and a decoder turns the two endpoint embeddings into a score, such as
$\zvec_u^\tr \zvec_v$ or an <Gloss name="mlp" /> of $\zvec_u \odot \zvec_v$. Training treats observed edges as positives
and sampled non-edges as negatives under a <Gloss name="bce">binary cross-entropy</Gloss> loss. Pair-aware models (SEAL,
neural common neighbours) also feed the structure around the pair, which per-node embeddings cannot represent.
</Definition>

## Problem

Many tasks ask which edges are missing or will appear: which products a user will buy, which proteins interact, which
papers a new paper will cite, which drug binds which target. The observed graph is a sample of the true one, and the
task is to rank the absent pairs. <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs">Node classification</NoteLink> asks a
question about one node; link prediction asks a question about two, and the answer depends on how they sit relative to
each other, not only on what each is.

Classical heuristics score a pair from its neighbourhoods <Cite id="libennowell2007" />. The common-neighbour count
$\abs{\Ncal(u) \cap \Ncal(v)}$ says that two people with many friends in common are likely to be friends. The
Adamic–Adar index $\sum_{w \in \Ncal(u) \cap \Ncal(v)} 1 / \log \deg(w)$ counts shared neighbours with weight falling
in their degree, since sharing a hub says less than sharing a rarely connected node. These heuristics need no training
and remain strong baselines. They use structure only, cannot use node features, and each encodes one fixed assumption
about why edges form. A learned model can use features and learn which structural patterns predict edges in a given
graph.

## Mechanism

**Encoder.** Any message passing network maps the node features $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times f}$ and the observed
adjacency $\Amat$ to embeddings $\Zmat \in \reals^{n \times k}$. The graph
<NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink> (<Gloss name="gae" />) of Kipf and Welling uses two
<NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolution</NoteLink> layers <Cite id="kipf2016vgae" />:

$$
\Zmat = \hat\Amat \,\mathrm{ReLU}\paren{\hat\Amat \Xmat \Wmat_1} \Wmat_2,
$$

with $\hat\Amat$ the normalised adjacency with self-loops of the observed graph.

**Decoder.** The inner product $s_{uv} = \zvec_u^\tr \zvec_v$ gives the probability $\sigma(s_{uv})$ of an edge, with
$\sigma$ the logistic function. The decoder reconstructs the whole adjacency as $\sigma(\Zmat\Zmat^\tr)$, which is the
"autoencoder" in the name. An MLP of $\zvec_u \odot \zvec_v$, with $\odot$ the elementwise product, is the usual
alternative. It stays symmetric in $u$ and $v$ and can express relations an inner product cannot, such as "a pair is
likely when both nodes are hubs".

**Training with negatives.** A sparse graph has $O(n)$ edges among $O(n^2)$ pairs, so training on every non-edge
would be dominated by negatives and cost quadratic time. Each step instead draws a set $\Ecal^-$ of non-edges, often one
per positive edge, and minimises

$$
\ell = -\frac{1}{\abs{\Ecal^+} + \abs{\Ecal^-}}
\paren{\sum_{(u, v) \in \Ecal^+} \log \sigma(s_{uv}) + \sum_{(u, v) \in \Ecal^-} \log\paren{1 - \sigma(s_{uv})}}.
$$

This is the graph form of <NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">negative sampling</NoteLink>. The
variational form (VGAE) makes each $\zvec_v$ a Gaussian with mean and log-variance from two encoder heads and adds the
KL divergence to a standard normal prior, as in a <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational
autoencoder</NoteLink> <Cite id="kipf2016vgae" />.

**Evaluation split.** The edges to be predicted must be absent from the graph the encoder sees. A split holds out a
share of the edges as positive test pairs, draws as many non-edges as negative test pairs, and trains on the graph of
the remaining edges. If a test edge stays in the message passing graph, its two ends aggregate each other's features
and the task becomes trivial (<NoteLink to="graph-splits-and-sampling">splitting and sampling graphs</NoteLink>).
Scores are compared by the <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">AUC</NoteLink>, the
probability that a random positive pair outscores a random negative one, or by ranking metrics: Hits@$K$, the share of
positives ranked above the $K$-th best negative, and the <Gloss name="mrr">mean reciprocal rank</Gloss>.

### What node embeddings cannot see

A decoder of two <NoteLink to="node-embeddings">node embeddings</NoteLink> gives the same score to any two pairs whose
endpoints have the same embeddings. Node embeddings from message passing are equal for nodes that are structurally
interchangeable, so such a model can confuse a pair of adjacent nodes with a pair of distant ones. Take the path
1–2–3–4–5 with the same feature on every node. The reflection $1 \leftrightarrow 5$, $2 \leftrightarrow 4$ maps the
graph onto itself, so every equivariant encoder gives $\zvec_2 = \zvec_4$. Any decoder then scores the pair $(1, 2)$,
one hop apart, the same as the pair $(1, 4)$, three hops apart. Zhang et al. prove the general form: aggregating
independently computed node representations cannot capture the dependence between the nodes of a set
<Cite id="zhang2021labeling" />.

**SEAL** extracts the $h$-hop subgraph enclosing each target pair, labels every node by its distances to the two
targets, and classifies the subgraph with a GNN <Cite id="zhang2018seal" />. The labels make $(1, 2)$ and $(1, 4)$ look
different: in the first subgraph a node at distance 0 from one target sits at distance 1 from the other, in the second
at distance 3. Zhang et al. call any such target-aware labelling the **labeling trick** and show it lets a
sufficiently expressive GNN learn the most expressive pair representations <Cite id="zhang2021labeling" />. SEAL's
weakness is cost: one subgraph and one GNN pass per scored pair. **Neural common neighbours** (NCN) keep a single
encoder pass and add the sum of the embeddings of the pair's common neighbours to the decoder's input, so the
common-neighbour heuristic becomes a learned feature <Cite id="wang2024ncn" />.

## Worked example

**One loss term of each kind.** Let four nodes have embeddings $\zvec_1 = (1, 0)$, $\zvec_2 = (0.8, 0.6)$,
$\zvec_3 = (-0.6, 0.8)$ and $\zvec_4 = (0, -1)$, with $(1, 2)$ an edge and $(1, 3)$ a sampled non-edge.

- Positive pair: $s_{12} = 1 \cdot 0.8 + 0 \cdot 0.6 = 0.8$, $\sigma(0.8) = 0.690$, loss $-\log 0.690 = 0.371$.
- Negative pair: $s_{13} = -0.6$, $\sigma(-0.6) = 0.354$, loss $-\log(1 - 0.354) = 0.437$.
- The mean loss over the two pairs is $0.404$. Its gradient raises $s_{12}$ and lowers $s_{13}$, pulling $\zvec_2$
  towards $\zvec_1$ and pushing $\zvec_3$ away.

**AUC by counting.** Three held-out edges score $(2.1, 0.9, 0.4)$ and three non-edges score $(1.2, 0.3, -0.5)$. Of the
nine positive–negative pairs, $2.1$ beats all three negatives, $0.9$ beats two and $0.4$ beats two, so
$\text{AUC} = 7/9 = 0.78$.

**Heuristics.** In the triangle 1–2–3 with a tail 3–4, the non-edge $(1, 4)$ has one common neighbour, node 3, of degree
3, so its common-neighbour score is 1 and its Adamic–Adar score is $1/\ln 3 = 0.91$. The non-edge $(2, 4)$ has the same
scores, and so it should: nodes 1 and 2 are interchangeable in this graph.

With one-hot node identities the encoder learns an embedding per node and passes both heuristics within 200 steps.
With the same feature on every node, nodes differ only in degree, so their embeddings nearly coincide, the loss stays
near $\log 2$, and the AUC stays near 0.5. The heuristics work here because communities are dense: two nodes in the
same community share neighbours far more often than two nodes in different communities. Held-out pairs with no common
neighbour all tie at a score of 0, which caps the heuristics' AUC on a graph this sparse.

## Cost

- **Encoder:** one forward pass over the graph, $O(\abs{\Ecal} k + n k^2)$ per layer, shared by every scored pair.
- **Training:** each step scores $\abs{\Ecal^+} + \abs{\Ecal^-}$ pairs at $O(k)$ each.
- **Inference:** scoring all pairs costs $O(n^2 k)$. Recommenders instead retrieve the top candidates for a node by
  <Gloss name="ann-search">approximate nearest-neighbour search</Gloss> over the embeddings, which an inner-product
  decoder allows.
- **SEAL:** one subgraph extraction and one GNN pass per pair, so inference over many candidates is orders of magnitude
  slower than an embedding lookup.

## Trade-offs

| model | uses features | pair structure | cost per scored pair |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| common neighbours, Adamic–Adar | no | fixed heuristic | neighbourhood intersection |
| GAE with inner-product decoder | yes | none beyond the embeddings | $O(k)$ |
| GAE with MLP decoder | yes | none beyond the embeddings | $O(k^2)$ |
| NCN | yes | learned common-neighbour feature | $O(k \cdot \abs{\Ncal(u) \cap \Ncal(v)})$ |
| SEAL | yes | full labelled subgraph | one GNN pass on a subgraph |

Node-embedding decoders suit retrieval over millions of candidates. Pair-aware models lead on benchmarks where local
structure decides, such as the collaboration and protein-association graphs of the
<NoteLink to="graph-learning-benchmarks">Open Graph Benchmark</NoteLink> <Cite id="hu2020ogb" />, at higher cost per
pair. <NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-embeddings">Knowledge graph embeddings</NoteLink> are the multi-relational case,
with one learned relation operator per edge type, and <NoteLink to="graph-collaborative-filtering">graph collaborative
filtering</NoteLink> is the case of a bipartite user–item graph.

## Pitfalls

- **Test edges in the message passing graph.** The encoder then sees the answer. Remove held-out edges before
  computing any embedding, heuristic or feature.
- **Easy negatives.** Uniformly random non-edges are mostly between distant nodes and are trivial to reject. Li et al.
  found that results under random negatives overstate how models rank realistic candidates, that several baselines
  had been under-tuned, and that hard negatives chosen by heuristics change the ranking of methods
  <Cite id="li2023linkpitfalls" />.
- **AUC on a balanced test set.** A real candidate set holds far more non-edges than edges. AUC on one negative per
  positive hides precision problems that Hits@$K$ or mean reciprocal rank expose.
- **Symmetric nodes.** With uninformative features, interchangeable nodes get equal embeddings, and the decoder cannot
  separate pairs that differ only in distance. Distinctive features, <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional
  encodings</NoteLink> or a labeling trick are needed.
- **Disconnected nodes after the split.** Holding out all edges of a low-degree node leaves it with no messages. Splits
  that keep a spanning tree of every component in the training graph avoid it.
