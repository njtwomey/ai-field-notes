<Definition>
Predict a real-valued property $y$ of a molecule from its structure. Each molecule is a carbon scaffold, a six-ring, a
five-ring or a chain of four carbons, carrying one to four substituents drawn from methyl ($\text{–CH}_3$), ethyl
($\text{–CH}_2\text{CH}_3$), hydroxyl ($\text{–OH}$), methoxy ($\text{–OCH}_3$), amine ($\text{–NH}_2$) and carbonyl
($\text{=O}$). The property is a sum of per-atom contributions set by each atom's element, hydrogen count and double
bond, plus $0.5$ for every pair of nitrogen or oxygen atoms exactly three bonds apart, plus Gaussian noise of standard
deviation $0.05$. There are 180 molecules. The test set is every five-ring molecule (55 of them), so the test scaffold
never appears in training. Compare a <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss> network with a sum readout against ridge
regression on count fingerprints, by the <Gloss name="rmse">root mean squared error</Gloss> on the test molecules.
</Definition>

## Setup

**The molecular graph.** Atoms are nodes and bonds are edges. Hydrogens are left implicit, as in most molecular graphs:
an atom of element $e$ with valence $\nu_e$ ($\nu_{\mathrm C} = 4$, $\nu_{\mathrm N} = 3$, $\nu_{\mathrm O} = 2$) and
bond orders $b_1, \dots$ carries $\nu_e - \sum_j b_j$ hydrogens. Each atom $v$ has a feature vector
$\xvec_v \in \set{0, 1}^9$: its element (3 entries, one-hot), its hydrogen count from 0 to 3 (4 entries, one-hot),
whether it lies on the ring, and whether it carries a double bond. Each edge carries its bond order, 1 or 2.

**The property.** Let $c(e, k, \delta)$ be the contribution of an atom of element $e$ with $k$ hydrogens and double-bond
flag $\delta$. The values used here are

| atom | contribution |
| --- | --- |
| C with 3, 2, 1, 0 hydrogens | 0.55, 0.40, 0.25, 0.10 |
| N with 2, 1, 0 hydrogens | −1.00, −0.75, −0.50 |
| O with 1 hydrogen (hydroxyl) | −0.90 |
| O with no hydrogen, single bonds (ether) | −0.35 |
| O with a double bond (carbonyl) | −0.60 |

The property of a molecule with atoms $\Vcal$ and topological distances $d(u, v)$ (the number of bonds on a shortest
path) is

$$
y = \sum_{v \in \Vcal} c(e_v, k_v, \delta_v) + 0.5 \cdot \abs{\set{\set{u, v} : e_u, e_v \in \set{\mathrm N, \mathrm O},\ d(u, v) = 3}} + \varepsilon,
\qquad \varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 0.05^2).
$$

The first term is an atom-additive model of the kind used for partition coefficients, where carbon raises the value and
polar atoms lower it <Cite id="wildman1999crippen" />. The values here are invented for the example. The second term
stands for an interaction between two polar groups, as an internal hydrogen bond would be, and is the part no single
atom's own environment reveals.

**The two models.**

- **Fingerprint and ridge.** An extended-connectivity (Morgan) fingerprint names every atom by its element, hydrogen
  count, ring membership and double bond at round 0, and at round $r$ by its round-$(r-1)$ name together with the
  sorted multiset of its neighbours' round-$(r-1)$ names and bond orders <Cite id="rogers2010" />. The count
  fingerprint of radius $R$ counts how many atoms carry each name at rounds $0, \dots, R$, over the names seen in the
  training molecules. Ridge regression with penalty $0.1$ on these counts is the baseline
  (<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>).
- **Message passing network.** Three layers update each atom's vector $\hvec_v \in \reals^{12}$ by
  $\hvec_v' = \mathrm{ReLU}\paren{\Wmat_0^\tr \hvec_v + \Wmat_1^\tr \sum_{u \in \Ncal_1(v)} \hvec_u + \Wmat_2^\tr \sum_{u \in \Ncal_2(v)} \hvec_u + \bvec}$,
  where $\Ncal_1(v)$ and $\Ncal_2(v)$ are the neighbours of $v$ joined by single and by double bonds. One weight matrix
  per bond order is the simplest edge-conditioned message
  (<NoteLink to="gated-and-edge-conditioned-message-passing">gated and edge-conditioned message passing</NoteLink>). A
  linear map of the three layers' vectors, concatenated, gives each atom one number $o_v$, and the prediction is the sum
  readout $\hat y = \sum_v o_v$ (<NoteLink to="graph-pooling-and-readout">graph pooling and readout</NoteLink>).
  Full-batch <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> minimises the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> on the
  training molecules.

## Solution

**Two molecules by hand.** Put hydroxyl groups on two neighbouring carbons of the six-ring (a 1,2-diol). The ring
carbons that carry a group have one hydrogen each, the other four have two, and each oxygen has one hydrogen:

- ring carbons: $2 \cdot 0.25 + 4 \cdot 0.40 = 2.10$;
- oxygens: $2 \cdot (-0.90) = -1.80$;
- the two oxygens are three bonds apart, O–C–C–O, which adds $0.5$;
- so $y = 2.10 - 1.80 + 0.5 = 0.80$, before noise.

Move the second hydroxyl one carbon further round the ring (a 1,3-diol). The atom counts and types are unchanged, so the
atom terms still sum to $0.30$, but the oxygens are now four bonds apart and the interaction term is 0: $y = 0.30$.

**What each model can represent.** The atom terms depend only on round-0 names, so a fingerprint of radius 0 with a
linear model represents them exactly, with one weight per atom type. It cannot separate the two diols, which have the
same atom types and differ by $0.5$. The interaction needs both polar atoms in one atom's environment. The middle
carbons of O–C–C–O see one oxygen at distance 1 and the other at distance 2, so a radius-2 fingerprint has a name for
that pattern, and a linear model on radius-2 counts can represent the whole noise-free property. The price is the
number of names: 499 different radius-2 environments occur in the training molecules, for 125 training molecules, so
ridge regression must choose among many nearly collinear counts.

The message passing network represents the property with no names to choose. After three rounds, atom $v$'s vector can
depend on every atom within three bonds, so $o_v$ can equal $c(e_v, k_v, \delta_v)$ plus $0.25$ for every polar
partner at distance 3. The sum readout then adds the per-atom values, as the property does. The sum matters: with a
mean readout, a molecule's prediction would not grow with the number of atoms, while the property does
(<NoteLink to="graph-pooling-and-readout">graph pooling and readout</NoteLink>).

## Simulation

<MoleculeRegression />

At the defaults (scaffold split, 150 Adam steps at rate 0.02, seed 1) the network's test error falls to about 0.17,
against a standard deviation of the property of 0.99 across molecules and a noise floor of 0.05. The radius-2
fingerprint model reaches about 0.20, and the radius-0 model, which sees only atom types, about 0.28, roughly the spread
of the interaction term it cannot see. The per-atom outputs of the trained network correlate with the true contributions
(a correlation of 0.77 over the test atoms at the defaults, between 0.4 and 0.6 for seeds 2 and 3). The loss constrains
only their sum, so training can move value between neighbouring atoms without changing any prediction, and a per-atom
attribution read from a sum readout is not identified by the data. Switching to a random split of the same size changes
the ranking of the two models from one seed to another: at this data size neither model wins reliably, which matches
comparisons on real molecular benchmarks <Cite id="jiang2021descriptors" />.

## What it illustrates

- **Molecules as graphs.** Atoms with element and hydrogen features, bonds with their orders, and permutation-invariant
  readout give a model that needs no hand-chosen descriptors (<NoteLink to="molecular-representations">molecular
  representations</NoteLink>). Duvenaud et al. introduced the learned analogue of the circular fingerprint
  <Cite id="duvenaud2015fingerprints" />, and Gilmer et al. framed it as message passing <Cite id="gilmer2017" />.
- **Readout matches the target.** An extensive property, one that adds over atoms, calls for a sum readout of per-atom
  outputs, and those outputs give a per-atom attribution for free.
- **Fingerprints are a strong baseline.** A fixed fingerprint with a linear model or a tree ensemble matches graph
  networks on many property benchmarks, especially with a few hundred to a few thousand molecules
  <Cite id="jiang2021descriptors" />. A graph network's advantage is that it learns which environments matter instead of
  counting all of them.
- **Scaffold splits test extrapolation.** Holding out a whole scaffold tests whether a model generalises to new
  chemistry, which a random split does not (<NoteLink to="scaffold-and-similarity-splits">scaffold and similarity
  splits</NoteLink>) <Cite id="bemis1996" />.

## Variations

- **Directed bond messages.** Chemprop's directed message passing network passes messages along directed bonds rather
  than atoms, which stops a message from returning at once to the atom it came from, and pairs the learned
  representation with a few computed descriptors <Cite id="yang2019chemprop" />. It is a common default for property
  prediction.
- **More data, less additivity.** Larger training sets and properties with long-range or three-dimensional effects
  favour graph networks; the MoleculeNet and <NoteLink to="graph-learning-benchmarks">OGB</NoteLink> molecular tasks are
  the usual tests, with scaffold splits <Cite id="wu2018moleculenet" /> <Cite id="hu2020ogb" />.
- **Three-dimensional geometry.** Energies and forces depend on atomic positions. Equivariant networks on the 3D point
  cloud handle them (<NoteLink to="euclidean-equivariant-graph-network">E(n)-equivariant graph network</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="machine-learned-interatomic-potentials">machine-learned interatomic potentials</NoteLink>).
- **A mean readout.** Replacing the sum with a mean leaves the network unable to represent the size dependence of the
  property; a target such as a concentration that does not grow with size would favour the mean.
- **A larger radius.** A radius-3 fingerprint adds more environments again; with this training set its test error
  rises rather than falls, because the extra names are rare.
