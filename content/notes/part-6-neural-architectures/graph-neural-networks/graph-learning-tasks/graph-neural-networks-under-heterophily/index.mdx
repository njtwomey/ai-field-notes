<Definition>
A labelled graph is **homophilous** when its edges mostly join nodes of the same class and **heterophilous** when they
mostly join nodes of different classes. The **edge homophily** $h \in [0, 1]$ is the share of edges whose two ends
share a class. A graph convolution that averages a node with its neighbours mixes the node's evidence with evidence
for the classes its neighbours belong to, which helps when $h$ is high and destroys the node's own signal at
intermediate $h$. Models for heterophilous graphs keep the node's own representation apart from its neighbours'
(H2GCN), read several neighbourhood radii, or learn signed propagation weights that can act as high-pass filters
(GPR-GNN).
</Definition>

## Intuition

Citation graphs are homophilous: a paper on <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss> cites mostly papers on
reinforcement learning. A <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolutional network</NoteLink> exploits
that. Averaging a node's features with its neighbours' cancels noise that differs from node to node and keeps the signal
the neighbours share. Many graphs work the other way. In a dating network edges join people of different genders, in a
transaction network fraudsters trade with ordinary accounts, and in a protein contact graph amino acids of different
types bind. There the neighbours carry evidence for the other class, and an average that includes them pulls each node
towards the wrong answer.

The neighbours are not useless on such graphs. "My neighbours are mostly of class B" is informative when class A nodes
tend to link to class B nodes. The failure is in how a graph convolution uses that information: it adds the
neighbours' features to the node's own with a fixed positive weight, so it cannot reverse their sign or keep the two
apart.

## Measuring homophily

Let a graph have node set $\Vcal$, edge set $\Ecal$ and labels $y_v \in \set{1, \dots, C}$. Three measures are in use.

- **Edge homophily**, the share of edges inside a class <Cite id="zhu2020h2gcn" />:
  $h_{\text{edge}} = \abs{\set{(u, v) \in \Ecal : y_u = y_v}} / \abs{\Ecal}$.
- **Node homophily**, the mean over nodes of the share of a node's neighbours in its own class
  <Cite id="pei2020geomgcn" />:
  $h_{\text{node}} = \frac{1}{\abs{\Vcal}} \sum_v \abs{\set{u \in \Ncal(v) : y_u = y_v}} / \abs{\Ncal(v)}$.
- **Adjusted homophily** <Cite id="platonov2023characterizing" />, which subtracts what edges that ignore the labels
  would give:

$$
h_{\text{adj}} = \frac{h_{\text{edge}} - \sum_{k=1}^{C} \bar p_k^2}{1 - \sum_{k=1}^{C} \bar p_k^2}, \qquad
\bar p_k = \frac{1}{2\abs{\Ecal}} \sum_{v : y_v = k} \deg(v).
$$

Here $\bar p_k$ is the share of all edge endpoints that sit in class $k$. If edges ignored labels, an endpoint's partner
would be in class $k$ with probability $\bar p_k$, and the expected edge homophily would be $\sum_k \bar p_k^2$. The
adjusted measure is therefore 0 for label-blind edges, 1 for perfect homophily and negative for heterophily. The raw
measures lack that reference point: a graph with one class holding 90% of the nodes has $h_{\text{edge}} \approx 0.82$
even when its edges are random.

## Why averaging fails at intermediate homophily

A two-class contextual stochastic block model (<Gloss name="sbm" />) shows the mechanism with exact numbers
<Cite id="deshpande2018csbm" />. Each node $v$ has a class $s_v \in \set{-1, +1}$ and a scalar feature
$x_v = \mu s_v + \varepsilon_v$ with $\varepsilon_v \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ independent across nodes, so $\mu > 0$ is the
signal in noise units. Suppose every node has $d$ neighbours, of which a share $h$ are in its own class.

A linear graph convolution with self-loops and mean normalisation computes
$z_v = \frac{1}{d + 1}\paren{x_v + \sum_{u \in \Ncal(v)} x_u}$. Its mean, given $s_v$, adds the node's $\mu s_v$, the
$hd$ same-class neighbours' $\mu s_v$ each, and the $(1 - h)d$ other neighbours' $-\mu s_v$ each:

$$
\expect[z_v \mid s_v] = \frac{\mu s_v \paren{1 + hd - (1 - h)d}}{d + 1} = \mu s_v \, \frac{1 + d(2h - 1)}{d + 1}.
$$

The $d + 1$ noise terms are independent with variance 1, so $\operatorname{Var}(z_v) = 1/(d + 1)$. The two class means
of $z_v$ lie at $\pm \expect[z_v \mid s_v = 1]$, and the best threshold is 0. Dividing the mean by the standard
deviation gives the separation in noise units:

$$
\delta_{\text{GCN}} = \mu \, \frac{\abs{1 + d(2h - 1)}}{\sqrt{d + 1}}, \qquad
\text{accuracy} = \Phi(\delta_{\text{GCN}}),
$$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function. A classifier that ignores the edges has $\delta = \mu$. The graph
convolution beats it only when $\abs{1 + d(2h - 1)} > \sqrt{d + 1}$, which holds near $h = 1$ and, by a sign flip, near
$h = 0$. Between the two it is worse, and it fails completely at

$$
h^\star = \frac{d - 1}{2d},
$$

where the node's own contribution and the net contribution of its neighbours cancel. For $d = 6$, $h^\star = 5/12$ and
the convolution beats the edge-blind classifier only for $h > 0.64$ or $h < 0.20$. Ma et al. make the same point from
the other side: a graph convolution can do well on a heterophilous graph when nodes of each class have distinctive
neighbour distributions, as at $h = 0$ <Cite id="ma2022homophily" />.

**Keeping the node apart.** Give the classifier the pair $(x_v, m_v)$, where
$m_v = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{u \in \Ncal(v)} x_u$ is the neighbours' mean. Given $s_v$, $x_v$ has mean $\mu s_v$ and variance
1, and $m_v$ has mean $\mu(2h - 1)s_v$ and variance $1/d$; the two are independent. For independent Gaussian features
with the same variance in both classes, the best linear rule weights each feature by its mean difference over its
variance, and the squared separations add:

$$
\delta_{\text{ego}}^2 = \mu^2 + \frac{\mu^2 (2h - 1)^2}{1/d} = \mu^2\paren{1 + d(2h - 1)^2}.
$$

This is never smaller than $\mu^2$, and it equals $\mu^2$ only at $h = 1/2$, where the neighbours say nothing about the
class. The weight on $m_v$ is $\mu(2h - 1)d$, which is negative under heterophily: the classifier reads "neighbours
look like class B" as evidence for class A.

## Designs for heterophily

**Ego and neighbour embeddings apart.** H2GCN concatenates a node's own embedding with the aggregated embeddings of its
1-hop and 2-hop neighbourhoods, with no self-loops in the aggregation, and feeds the concatenation forward
<Cite id="zhu2020h2gcn" />. The <NoteLink to="graphsage">GraphSAGE</NoteLink> update,
$\sigma(\Wmat [\hvec_v \Vert \mvec_v])$, has the same separation built in <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />, and so
does any layer with a residual connection around the aggregation.

**Higher-order neighbourhoods.** Under heterophily, a node's 2-hop neighbours can be more homophilous than its 1-hop
neighbours: in a bipartite-like graph the neighbours of neighbours are back in the node's own class. H2GCN aggregates
the 1-hop and the strict 2-hop neighbourhoods separately, and combines the representations of every layer at the end
<Cite id="zhu2020h2gcn" />.

**Signed polynomial filters.** GPR-GNN first maps features through an <Gloss name="mlp" />,
$\Hmat^{(0)} = f_\theta(\Xmat)$, then propagates with learned scalar weights $\gamma_k$:

$$
\Zmat = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \gamma_k \hat\Amat^k \Hmat^{(0)}
$$

with $\hat\Amat$ the normalised adjacency with self-loops <Cite id="chien2021gprgnn" />. In the spectral view of
<NoteLink to="spectral-graph-convolution">spectral graph convolution</NoteLink>, $\hat\Amat$ has eigenvalues
$1 - \lambda$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda \in [0, 2)$ of the
<NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform">normalised Laplacian</NoteLink> of the graph with self-loops,
so $\Zmat$ applies the polynomial filter $g(\lambda) = \sum_k \gamma_k (1 - \lambda)^k$. With all $\gamma_k \ge 0$ the
filter is low-pass and smooths labels across edges. Negative weights can make it high-pass. $\gamma = (1, -1)$ gives
$\Hmat - \hat\Amat\Hmat$, whose response $g(\lambda) = \lambda$ amplifies exactly the components that change sign across
edges, which is what a heterophilous label signal does.

**Using the adjacency as features.** LINKX embeds each node's row of the adjacency matrix with one MLP and its features
with another, and combines the two <Cite id="lim2021linkx" />. A node's set of neighbours, not their features, then
carries the structural signal, which works at any homophily and scales by minibatches of nodes.

## Worked example

**Homophily of a small graph.** Take a 6-cycle with labels $A, A, B, B, A, B$ around it. Its edges are 1–2 ($AA$),
2–3 ($AB$), 3–4 ($BB$), 4–5 ($BA$), 5–6 ($AB$) and 6–1 ($BA$).

- Edge homophily: 2 of the 6 edges join equal labels, so $h_{\text{edge}} = 1/3$.
- Node homophily: nodes 1 to 4 each have one neighbour of their own class out of two, and nodes 5 and 6 have none, so
  $h_{\text{node}} = (4 \cdot \tfrac12 + 2 \cdot 0)/6 = 1/3$.
- Adjusted homophily: each class holds three nodes of degree 2, so $\bar p_A = \bar p_B = 6/12 = 1/2$ and
  $\sum_k \bar p_k^2 = 1/2$. Then $h_{\text{adj}} = (1/3 - 1/2)/(1 - 1/2) = -1/3$: fewer same-class edges than random
  wiring would give.

**Cancellation at one node.** Node $v$ is in class $A$ with feature $x_v = +1$. It has $d = 3$ neighbours: one in class
$A$ with feature $+1$ and two in class $B$ with features $-1$ each, so its own share of same-class neighbours is $1/3$.

- The graph convolution's mean with a self-loop is $z_v = (1 + 1 - 1 - 1)/4 = 0$. The node's evidence is gone. The value
  $h^\star = (d - 1)/(2d) = 2/6 = 1/3$ of the formula is exactly this node's share.
- The separated model sees $(x_v, m_v) = (1, (1 - 1 - 1)/3) = (1, -1/3)$. With $\mu = 1$, $h = 1/3$ and $d = 3$, the
  optimal weights are $\mu = 1$ on $x_v$ and $\mu(2h - 1)d = -1$ on $m_v$. The score is
  $1 \cdot 1 + (-1)(-1/3) = 4/3 > 0$, which assigns class $A$. A class-$B$ node in the mirror position,
  $(x_v, m_v) = (-1, 1/3)$, scores $-4/3$.

**A high-pass filter on a bipartite cycle.** On a 6-cycle with alternating classes, $h = 0$, and features $\pm 1$ that
follow the classes, the normalised adjacency with self-loops averages each node with its two neighbours:
$(\hat\Amat\xvec)_v = (-1 + 1 - 1)/3 = -1/3$ for a node with $x_v = 1$. The low-pass output is weak and has the wrong
sign. The GPR-GNN filter with $\gamma = (1, -1)$ gives $x_v - (\hat\Amat\xvec)_v = 1 + 1/3 = 4/3$, larger than the
input.

**At the figure's defaults**, $d = 6$, $h = 0.2$ and a feature signal of 1, which along the class-difference
direction is $\mu = 1/\sqrt 2 = 0.707$. Then $\delta = 0.707$ without edges, $\delta_{\text{GCN}} =
0.707 \cdot \abs{1 - 3.6}/\sqrt 7 = 0.695$, and $\delta_{\text{ego}} = 0.707\sqrt{1 + 6 \cdot 0.36} = 1.257$. The
predicted accuracies are $\Phi(0.707) = 0.760$, $\Phi(0.695) = 0.756$ and $\Phi(1.257) = 0.896$.

## Practice

Heterophily benchmarks went through a correction. The six small graphs used from 2020, Texas, Wisconsin, Cornell,
Chameleon, Squirrel and Actor, have a few hundred to a few thousand nodes and fixed splits. Platonov et al. found that
Chameleon and Squirrel contain many duplicate nodes, which leak between training and <NoteLink to="test-sets">test
sets</NoteLink> <Cite id="platonov2023heterophily" />. On five new heterophilous datasets they proposed, standard
<Gloss name="gnn" plural /> with <NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink> between the ego and
neighbour representations almost always outperformed models built for heterophily. The working practice is therefore:

- report adjusted homophily rather than edge homophily alone;
- compare against an MLP on node features, which ignores edges, and against a GNN with residual or concatenated ego
  embeddings, such as GraphSAGE;
- use the newer heterophilous benchmarks, or the large non-homophilous graphs of Lim et al.
  <Cite id="lim2021linkx" />, instead of Chameleon and Squirrel as distributed before 2023.

## Assumptions

- The labels relate to the edges consistently across the graph. Mixed graphs, homophilous in some regions and
  heterophilous in others, need node-specific propagation weights.
- The formulas assume independent Gaussian features, a fixed degree and a fixed share $h$ at every node. Real graphs
  vary in all three; the measured curves in the figure show the effect of varying degrees.
- Homophily is measured with labels, which are known only for training nodes at prediction time. An estimate from the
  training nodes is noisy when they are few.

## Pitfalls

- **Edge homophily with unbalanced classes.** A graph where one class holds most nodes looks homophilous under
  $h_{\text{edge}}$ even with random edges. Adjusted homophily corrects for this.
- **Treating low homophily as "edges are useless".** At $h = 0$ a graph convolution can work well through a sign flip;
  the hard regime is intermediate $h$, where neighbours carry no consistent signal for an average to use.
- **Leaky heterophily benchmarks.** Duplicate nodes in Chameleon and Squirrel let models memorise test nodes through
  their training copies <Cite id="platonov2023heterophily" />.
- **Untuned baselines.** Many reported gains of heterophily-specific models disappear against a tuned GNN with
  residual connections.
