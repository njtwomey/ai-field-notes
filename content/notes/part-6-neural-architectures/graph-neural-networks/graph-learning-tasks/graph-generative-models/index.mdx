<Definition>
A **graph generative model** learns a distribution over graphs from a set of example graphs and samples new graphs from
it. A graph with $n$ nodes is stored as an adjacency matrix $\Amat \in \set{0, 1}^{n \times n}$ and node features
$\Xmat$, but the same graph has up to $n!$ such representations, one per node ordering. <Gloss name="ar">Autoregressive
models</Gloss> such as GraphRNN fix an ordering and generate nodes and their edges one at a time; one-shot models such
as GraphVAE decode a whole adjacency matrix from a latent vector; <NoteLink to="diffusion-models">diffusion
models</NoteLink> such as DiGress corrupt node and edge types step by step and learn to reverse the corruption.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Molecule design, circuit layout, road networks and synthetic social networks all need new graphs that look like existing
ones. Classical random graph models, Erdős–Rényi, <Gloss name="sbm">stochastic block models</Gloss> and preferential
attachment, fix a few statistics by hand: an edge probability, block sizes, a degree exponent. A learned model instead
captures whatever structure the training graphs share, such as the ring sizes and valence rules of molecules or the
community structure of social graphs.

Two properties separate graphs from images and text. A graph has no canonical order: an image has a first pixel and a
sentence a first word, but a graph's nodes can be listed in any order, and every order describes the same graph. And
graphs are discrete and sparse: an edge is present or absent, and most pairs are absent. Each family of generators deals
with these two facts in its own way.

## Details

**The likelihood of a graph.** Write $\Amat^\pi$ for the adjacency matrix of graph $G$ under node ordering $\pi$. A
model that defines probabilities $p(\Amat^\pi)$ of ordered adjacency matrices assigns the unordered graph

$$
p(G) = \sum_{\Amat \in \set{\Amat^\pi : \pi \in \Pi_n}} p(\Amat),
$$

the sum over the distinct matrices that represent $G$. Their number is $n! / \abs{\operatorname{Aut}(G)}$, where
$\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ is the group of relabellings that map $G$ onto itself. For most graphs it grows like $n!$, so
the sum cannot be computed and maximum likelihood is replaced by a bound or by a canonical choice of ordering.

**Autoregressive generation.** GraphRNN writes $\Amat^\pi$ as a sequence: for $i = 1, \dots, n - 1$ the vector
$S_i \in \set{0, 1}^{i}$ records which of the $i$ earlier nodes the $i$-th node joins <Cite id="you2018graphrnn" />. A
graph-level recurrent network carries a state from row to row, and an edge-level network emits the entries of each row,
so

$$
p(S^\pi) = \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} \prod_{j=0}^{i-1} p\paren{S_{i,j} \mid S_{<i}, S_{i,<j}}.
$$

Training uses breadth-first orderings from random roots. Under a breadth-first ordering, each node's neighbours among
the earlier nodes lie in the current or the previous breadth-first layer, so every edge reaches back at most $M$
positions, and each row needs only its last $M$ entries. This cuts the work per graph from $O(n^2)$ to $O(nM)$ and
reduces the number of orderings the model must assign probability to. Generation stops when the model emits an empty
row. Li et al. use a graph network instead of a recurrent state: at each step it runs <Gloss name="gnn">message
passing</Gloss> on the partial graph and decides whether to add a node and which edges to give it
<Cite id="li2018graphgen" />.

**One-shot generation.** GraphVAE decodes a latent vector $\zvec$ into a full probabilistic graph of a fixed maximum
size $k$: an edge probability for every pair and type distributions for every node and edge
<Cite id="simonovsky2018graphvae" />. The encoder is a graph network, as in the
<NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational autoencoder</NoteLink>, and the decoder is an <Gloss name="mlp" />.
The decoder's nodes come in a fixed order and the target's in an arbitrary one, so the reconstruction loss needs a
matching between them, an instance of graph matching that is solved approximately; that matching limits the method to
small graphs. The variational graph autoencoder of Kipf and Welling is different: it decodes edges from
<NoteLink to="node-embeddings">node embeddings</NoteLink> of a known graph and suits
<NoteLink to="link-prediction-with-graph-neural-networks">link prediction</NoteLink>, not generation of new graphs
<Cite id="kipf2016vgae" />.

**Diffusion.** DiGress treats every node type and every edge type, including "no edge", as a categorical variable
<Cite id="vignac2023digress" />. A forward Markov chain resamples each one towards the marginal distribution of types in
the training data, so after many steps a graph is noise with realistic sparsity. A
<NoteLink to="graph-transformer">graph transformer</NoteLink> is trained to predict the clean node and edge types from a
noisy graph, and sampling runs the chain backwards from noise. The denoiser is permutation equivariant, so the learned
distribution is permutation invariant and no ordering is needed. The <NoteLink to="discrete-diffusion">discrete
diffusion</NoteLink> note covers the corruption process in general.

**Molecules.** Valence rules make most random graphs invalid molecules. The junction-tree VAE generates a tree of
chemical substructures (rings, bonds) first and then assembles atoms within it, so every output is a valid molecule
<Cite id="jin2018jtvae" />. Diffusion and autoregressive models instead check valence during sampling or are
conditioned on it. A molecular generator is usually paired with a property predictor
(<NoteLink to="molecular-property-prediction-with-graph-networks">molecular property prediction</NoteLink>) to steer
sampling towards molecules with wanted properties.

**Evaluation.** Two sets of graphs are compared by the <NoteLink to="maximum-mean-discrepancy">maximum mean
discrepancy</NoteLink> between distributions of graph statistics: degrees, clustering coefficients and counts of small
subgraph orbits <Cite id="you2018graphrnn" />. Molecular generators also report the share of valid, unique and novel
molecules. O'Bray et al. showed that MMD-based comparisons depend on the choice of kernel and its parameters, which
papers often set differently, so rankings can change with these choices <Cite id="obray2022graphgen" />.

## Worked example

**Orderings of a 4-cycle.** The 4-cycle 0–1–2–3–0 has $4! = 24$ node orderings. Its automorphism group has 8 elements,
the four rotations and four reflections of a square, so the orderings give $24 / 8 = 3$ distinct adjacency matrices.
Under the breadth-first ordering from node 0, the nodes are placed as 0, 1, 3, 2: node 0's neighbours 1 and 3 first,
then node 2. The rows are

$$
S_1 = (1), \qquad S_2 = (1, 0), \qquad S_3 = (0, 1, 1).
$$

Node 1 joins node 0. Node 3 joins node 0 and not node 1. Node 2 joins nodes 1 and 3 and not node 0. The longest step
back is from node 2, at position 3, to node 1, at position 1, so the bandwidth is $M = 2$. Every one of the 8
breadth-first orderings, four roots times two orders of the root's neighbours, gives this same sequence, so a
breadth-first model must learn one sequence for this graph instead of three.

**A likelihood by counting.** Under the Erdős–Rényi model $G(4, 1/2)$ every edge is present independently with
probability $1/2$, so each ordered adjacency matrix on 4 nodes has probability $(1/2)^6 = 1/64$. The unordered 4-cycle
has 3 distinct matrices, so $p(\text{4-cycle}) = 3/64$. A model that scores only one ordering, the breadth-first one,
would report $1/64$: the gap is the sum over orderings that exact likelihoods need.

**Growth of the sum.** A 10-cycle has $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$ orderings and 20 automorphisms, so 181,440 distinct
adjacency matrices represent it.

On the $3 \times 4$ grid, a breadth-first ordering from a corner keeps every edge within 4 positions, about one row of
the grid. A random ordering spreads edges across the whole triangle: with seed 1 its longest edge reaches back 9 of
the 11 possible positions.

## Assumptions

- The training graphs come from one distribution and are small enough for the method: hundreds of nodes for
  autoregressive models with a band, tens of nodes for one-shot models with matching.
- For autoregressive models, the chosen family of orderings, breadth-first from a random root, covers the graphs well;
  a model trained on one family assigns low probability to sequences from other orderings.
- For molecules, validity rules are either built into the generator or enforced at sampling time.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting a likelihood under one ordering.** It is a lower bound on $p(G)$, not $p(G)$, and the bound's looseness
  depends on the number of automorphisms, so it differs between graphs.
- **Comparing MMD values across papers.** Different statistics, kernels and kernel parameters give different numbers
  and rankings <Cite id="obray2022graphgen" />.
- **Novelty without validity, or validity without novelty.** A molecular generator can score perfect validity by
  copying training molecules. Report validity, uniqueness and novelty together.
- **One-shot decoders at large size.** Dense $k \times k$ outputs and graph matching make the cost grow at least
  quadratically in the maximum size $k$.
