<Definition>
A heterogeneous graph has a node-type map $\tau : \Vcal \to \Tcal_V$ and an edge-type map $\phi : \Ecal \to \Rcal$, with
more than one type in total. A heterogeneous graph network is a <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss> network whose
message, attention or update functions depend on these types. The relational graph convolutional network (R-GCN) gives
each relation $r \in \Rcal$ its own weight matrix. HAN aggregates along typed paths called meta-paths. The heterogeneous
<NoteLink to="graph-transformer">graph transformer</NoteLink> (HGT) attends with projections chosen by the types of the
two endpoints and of the edge.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A bibliographic graph has authors, papers and venues. A paper is written by authors, cites papers and appears in a
venue. A <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing</NoteLink> layer that ignores the types applies
one weight matrix to every neighbour. It must then turn an author's vector, a cited paper's vector and a venue's vector
into messages with the same map, and it averages all of them together. The three kinds of neighbour carry different
information, and their vectors may not even live in the same space: an author can be described by a publication
record, a venue by a topic distribution.

A heterogeneous network keeps one function per type. The simplest version groups a node's neighbours by relation,
transforms each group with that relation's weights, and adds the groups. Relations then contribute separately, and a
relation with many edges does not drown one with few. The <NoteLink to="heterogeneous-and-temporal-graphs">heterogeneous
and temporal graphs</NoteLink> note describes the data: type maps, schemas and meta-paths.

## Details

### Relational graph convolution

Write $\Ncal_i^r$ for the neighbours of node $i$ under relation $r$ and $\hvec_j^{(l)} \in \reals^{d_l}$ for node $j$'s
vector at layer $l$. The R-GCN layer is

$$
\hvec_i^{(l+1)} = \sigma\paren{\Wmat_0^{(l)} \hvec_i^{(l)} + \sum_{r \in \Rcal} \sum_{j \in \Ncal_i^r}
\frac{1}{c_{i,r}} \Wmat_r^{(l)} \hvec_j^{(l)}},
$$

with one weight matrix $\Wmat_r^{(l)} \in \reals^{d_{l+1} \times d_l}$ per relation, a self-connection $\Wmat_0^{(l)}$,
an elementwise nonlinearity $\sigma$ and a normalising constant $c_{i,r}$ <Cite id="schlichtkrull2018rgcn" />. The
paper's entity classification uses $c_{i,r} = \abs{\Ncal_i^r}$, a mean per relation. Its
<NoteLink to="link-prediction-with-graph-neural-networks">link prediction</NoteLink> found
$c_{i,r} = \sum_r \abs{\Ncal_i^r}$, one mean across all relations, to work best <Cite id="schlichtkrull2018rgcn" />. The
relation set holds every relation in both directions ("cites" and "cited by"), so information flows both ways along an
edge.

The layer is a message passing layer whose message is $\Wmat_{\phi(j,i)} \hvec_j$: the edge type picks the weight
matrix. With one relation and $c_{i,r}$ the degree, it reduces to a mean-aggregation
<NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolution</NoteLink> with a separate self weight.

### Basis and block-diagonal decomposition

A knowledge graph can have hundreds of relations, and $\abs{\Rcal}$ full weight matrices overfit the rare ones. The
**basis decomposition** writes every relation's weight as a combination of $B$ shared bases,

$$
\Wmat_r^{(l)} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} a_{rb}^{(l)} \Vmat_b^{(l)},
$$

so only the $B$ coefficients $a_{rb}$ are specific to relation $r$ <Cite id="schlichtkrull2018rgcn" />. A rare relation
then shares most of its parameters with frequent ones. The **block-diagonal decomposition** instead makes each
$\Wmat_r$ block-diagonal, with $B$ blocks of size $(d_{l+1}/B) \times (d_l/B)$, which is a sparsity constraint
rather than sharing <Cite id="schlichtkrull2018rgcn" />.

### Meta-paths and the heterogeneous graph attention network

A **meta-path** is a sequence of node and relation types, such as author–paper–author (APA, co-authorship) or
author–paper–venue–paper–author (APVPA, same venue). Two nodes are meta-path neighbours if a path of that type joins
them. The meta-path adjacency is the product of the relation adjacencies along it, $\Amat_{\text{APA}} =
\Amat_{\text{AP}}\Amat_{\text{AP}}^{\tr}$, whose $(i, j)$ entry counts the paths.

HAN works in two levels <Cite id="wang2019han" />. First, a type-specific matrix projects every node's features into a
shared space, $\hvec'_i = \Mmat_{\tau(i)} \xvec_i$. **Node-level attention** then runs a
<NoteLink to="graph-attention-network">graph attention</NoteLink> layer on each meta-path's neighbour graph $\Phi$:

$$
\alpha_{ij}^{\Phi} = \mathrm{softmax}_{j \in \Ncal_i^{\Phi}}\paren{\sigma\paren{\avec_\Phi^{\tr}[\hvec'_i \Vert \hvec'_j]}},
\qquad \zvec_i^{\Phi} = \sigma\paren{\sum_{j \in \Ncal_i^{\Phi}} \alpha_{ij}^{\Phi} \hvec'_j},
$$

with one attention vector $\avec_\Phi$ per meta-path. **Semantic-level attention** weighs the meta-paths. It scores each
by $w_\Phi = \frac{1}{\abs{\Vcal}} \sum_i \qvec^{\tr}\tanh(\Wmat \zvec_i^{\Phi} + \bvec)$, takes
$\beta_\Phi = \mathrm{softmax}_\Phi(w_\Phi)$, and outputs $\zvec_i = \sum_\Phi \beta_\Phi \zvec_i^{\Phi}$
<Cite id="wang2019han" />. The meta-paths are chosen by hand, which needs knowledge of the schema.

### Heterogeneous graph transformer

HGT drops hand-chosen meta-paths and parameterises attention by the **meta-relation**
$\langle \tau(s), \phi(e), \tau(t) \rangle$ of each edge $e$ from source $s$ to target $t$ <Cite id="hu2020hgt" />. For
<NoteLink to="multi-head-attention">attention head</NoteLink> $i$, the key is
$\mathrm{K}^i(s) = \text{K-Linear}^i_{\tau(s)}(\hvec_s)$ and the query
$\mathrm{Q}^i(t) = \text{Q-Linear}^i_{\tau(t)}(\hvec_t)$, each a linear map chosen by node type. The score is

$$
\text{ATT-head}^i(s, e, t) = \paren{\mathrm{K}^i(s)\, \Wmat^{\text{ATT}}_{\phi(e)}\, \mathrm{Q}^i(t)^{\tr}}
\cdot \frac{\mu_{\langle \tau(s), \phi(e), \tau(t) \rangle}}{\sqrt d},
$$

with a matrix $\Wmat^{\text{ATT}}_{\phi(e)}$ per edge type and a learned prior $\mu$ per meta-relation, followed by a
softmax over the target's neighbours <Cite id="hu2020hgt" />. Messages are built the same way with type-specific value
projections. Node types and edge types are parameterised separately, so the number of weight matrices grows with
$\abs{\Tcal_V} + \abs{\Rcal}$; only the scalar prior $\mu$ is kept per meta-relation. HGT was trained on the Open
Academic Graph of 179 million nodes and 2 billion edges, with a type-aware minibatch sampler <Cite id="hu2020hgt" />.

### Practice

Current libraries build heterogeneous layers by wrapping a homogeneous layer once per edge type and summing the outputs
at each destination, which is the R-GCN pattern with any message function. Type-specific input projections map
features of different widths to one hidden width first. The same machinery applies to relational databases, with
tables as node types and foreign keys as relations <Cite id="fey2024" />. Strong baselines matter: a benchmark of
heterogeneous models found that a homogeneous graph attention network given proper inputs matched or beat most
specialised heterogeneous models <Cite id="lv2021hgb" />. For link prediction on knowledge graphs, an R-GCN encoder is
paired with a triple-scoring decoder such as DistMult <Cite id="schlichtkrull2018rgcn" />. The
<NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-embeddings">knowledge graph embeddings</NoteLink> note covers those decoders.

## Worked example

Paper P1 has feature vector $(1, 0)$. It is written by two authors with vectors $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$, cites two papers
with vectors $(2, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$, and appears in one venue with vector $(1, -1)$. Take $\Wmat_0 = \I$ and

$$
\Wmat_{\text{written by}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\Wmat_{\text{cites}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\Wmat_{\text{published in}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which swap the two features, halve them, and keep the first, respectively. Use $c_{i,r} = \abs{\Ncal_i^r}$.

1. **Written by.** The authors' mean is $(0.5, 1)$; the swap gives $(1, 0.5)$.
2. **Cites.** The cited papers' mean is $(1, 1)$; halving gives $(0.5, 0.5)$.
3. **Published in.** The single venue gives $(1, -1)$; keeping the first feature gives $(1, 0)$.
4. **Self.** $\Wmat_0 (1, 0) = (1, 0)$.
5. **Sum.** $(1 + 0.5 + 1 + 1,\; 0.5 + 0.5 + 0 + 0) = (3.5, 1)$, unchanged by a <Gloss name="relu" />.

**Normalising across relations.** With $c_{i,r} = 5$ for every relation, the relation sums $(2, 1)$, $(1, 1)$ and
$(1, 0)$ are each divided by 5. The result is $(1, 0) + (0.8, 0.4) = (1.8, 0.4)$. The venue's single edge now counts a
fifth as much as before, and the written-by and cites relations count in proportion to their number of edges.

**One shared weight.** A homogeneous layer with the identity weight averages all five neighbours to $(0.8, 0.6)$ and
adds the self term, giving $(1.8, 0.6)$. The venue's $-1$ cancels part of the others' second feature. No single weight
matrix can treat the venue differently from the authors, because they are averaged before the weight is applied.

**Parameter count.** A knowledge graph with $\abs{\Rcal} = 100$ relations and hidden width $d = 64$ needs
$100 \cdot 64^2 = 409{,}600$ weights per layer for the relation matrices. With $B = 10$ bases it needs
$10 \cdot 64^2 + 100 \cdot 10 = 41{,}960$, about a tenth.

**Meta-path adjacency.** Three authors and two papers: P1 by A1 and A2, P2 by A2 and A3. Then

$$
\Amat_{\text{AP}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\Amat_{\text{APA}} = \Amat_{\text{AP}}\Amat_{\text{AP}}^{\tr} =
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The diagonal counts each author's papers. The off-diagonal entries count shared papers: A1 and A3 share none, so they
are not APA neighbours, although both are two hops from A2.

## Assumptions

- **Types carry information.** The gain over a homogeneous model comes from treating relations differently. If the
  types are arbitrary, separate weights only divide the data.
- **The schema is fixed and known.** R-GCN and HGT need the relation set in advance; a relation that first appears at
  test time has no weights. HAN also needs meta-paths chosen by hand.
- **Enough edges per relation.** A relation with a handful of edges cannot train a full weight matrix; the basis
  decomposition and HGT's factorisation by node and edge type exist for this case.

## Pitfalls

- **Weak baselines.** Reported gains of specialised heterogeneous models over a well-tuned homogeneous attention
  network often vanish on a common benchmark <Cite id="lv2021hgb" />. Compare against one.
- **Inverse-relation leakage.** In link prediction on knowledge graphs, a test triple $(h, r, t)$ can be predicted from
  its inverse $(t, r^{-1}, h)$ in the training set. The FB15k-237 split removes such pairs for this reason
  <Cite id="schlichtkrull2018rgcn" />.
- **Meta-path explosion.** The number of candidate meta-paths grows exponentially with their length, and products of
  adjacency matrices along long meta-paths become dense.
- **Unbalanced relations.** With normalisation across relations, a relation with thousands of edges per node
  dominates one with a single edge, as the worked example shows on a small scale. Per-relation normalisation, or
  attention over relations, keeps each relation's contribution separate.
