<Definition>
The expressiveness of a <Gloss name="gnn">graph neural network</Gloss> architecture is the set of graphs, nodes or pairs
of nodes it can map to different outputs for some choice of weights, and so the set of functions on graphs it can
represent. For message passing networks this set is bounded by colour refinement, the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman
test (<Gloss name="wl" form="short" />). The notes of this chapter state the bound, the architecture that attains it,
and the methods that go beyond it with features, extra aggregators, tuples of nodes or bags of subgraphs.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A model cannot learn a distinction it cannot represent. A message passing network gives a 6-cycle and two separate
triangles the same output for every setting of its weights, so no amount of training teaches it to count triangles or to
decide whether a graph is connected. Molecules make the limit practical: decalin and bicyclopentyl, with rings of six
and of five atoms, look identical to such a network unless extra features are supplied.

The theory answers three practical questions. Which aggregator should a layer use? When do node features remove the
limit? Which more expensive architecture is worth its cost for a given task? It also explains why synthetic
benchmarks built from regular and strongly regular graphs defeat plain message passing.

## Main ideas

**Colour refinement is the ceiling.** The <NoteLink to="weisfeiler-leman-test" /> recolours every node by its own
colour and the multiset of its neighbours' colours. A message passing layer computes a node's vector from the same two
inputs, so by induction it never separates two nodes or graphs that colour refinement keeps together
<Cite id="xu2019" /> <Cite id="morris2019weisfeiler" />. The test fails on regular graphs of equal degree and on
symmetric ring systems, and succeeds on almost every random graph and on all trees <Cite id="morris2023wl" />.

**Sum aggregation reaches the ceiling.** The <NoteLink to="graph-isomorphism-network" />
(<Gloss name="gin" form="short" />) applies an <Gloss name="mlp" /> to the node's own vector, weighted by
$1 + \epsilon$, plus the sum of its neighbours' vectors. A sum identifies a multiset of countable features, a mean only
its proportions, and a maximum only its set of values. With injective updates, GIN separates exactly what colour
refinement separates.

**Real-valued features need several aggregators.** With continuous features no single continuous aggregator identifies
every multiset. <NoteLink to="principal-neighbourhood-aggregation" /> (<Gloss name="pna" form="short" />) combines mean,
standard deviation, maximum and minimum with logarithmic degree scalers, which recovers more of the separations colour
refinement allows.

**The limits are concrete.** <NoteLink to="expressive-power-of-message-passing" /> lists what lies past the bound:
cycles and connectivity, counts of triangles and other induced subgraphs, ring sizes in molecules, strongly regular
graphs, and the distinction between pairs of nodes that <NoteLink to="link-prediction-with-graph-neural-networks">link
prediction</NoteLink> needs.

**Features move the bound.** The bound is relative to the input features. Random-walk return probabilities, cycle counts
and Laplacian eigenvectors, the subject of <NoteLink to="positional-and-structural-encodings" />, start refinement from
richer colours. They cost one preprocessing pass, and eigenvector encodings need care because their signs and bases are
arbitrary.

**Tuples and subgraphs pass the bound at a price.**
<NoteLink to="higher-order-and-subgraph-graph-neural-networks" /> covers networks that keep vectors for pairs or triples
of nodes, matching the higher-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman tests at cubic cost, and networks that run message passing on
bags of node-deleted, edge-deleted or ego-network subgraphs. Subgraph methods with one subgraph per node are bounded by
3-WL.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="weisfeiler-leman-test" />. The rest of the chapter measures architectures against it.
- <NoteLink to="graph-isomorphism-network" /> proves the bound and gives the architecture that attains it.
  <NoteLink to="principal-neighbourhood-aggregation" /> refines the aggregation for continuous features.
- <NoteLink to="expressive-power-of-message-passing" /> surveys what the bound rules out and the four ways past it.
- <NoteLink to="positional-and-structural-encodings" /> and
  <NoteLink to="higher-order-and-subgraph-graph-neural-networks" /> develop the cheap and the expensive way past it.

Every note uses the same small graphs, the path and the star, the 6-cycle and two triangles, decalin and bicyclopentyl,
and the rook's and Shrikhande graphs, so results can be compared across notes and figures.
