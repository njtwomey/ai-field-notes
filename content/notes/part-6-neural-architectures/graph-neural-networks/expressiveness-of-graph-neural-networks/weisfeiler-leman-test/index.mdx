<Definition>
Colour refinement, the 1-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman test (1-WL), gives every node $v$ of a graph a colour
$c^{(0)}(v)$, all equal or taken from node labels, and then repeats one rule. In round $t + 1$, node $v$ receives a new
colour that is a one-to-one function of the pair formed by its old colour and the multiset of its neighbours' old
colours:

$$
c^{(t+1)}(v) = \operatorname{relabel}\paren{c^{(t)}(v),\ \mset{c^{(t)}(u) : u \in \Ncal(v)}}.
$$

The rounds stop when no colour class splits. Two graphs refined with one shared relabelling are distinguished when
their colour histograms differ in some round, and then they are certainly not isomorphic
<Cite id="weisfeiler1968" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two graphs are **isomorphic** when some relabelling of the nodes of one turns it into the other. Checking all $n!$
relabellings of $n$ nodes is hopeless beyond a dozen nodes. A cheaper approach computes an **invariant**, a summary that
does not depend on the labelling, and compares the summaries. Unequal summaries prove the graphs differ. Equal summaries
leave the question open.

The degree sequence is the simplest invariant. Colour refinement extends it. After one round, a node's colour records
its degree. After two rounds, it records its degree and the degrees of its neighbours. After $t$ rounds, it records the
whole tree of walks of length $t$ that start at the node, with repeated visits drawn as separate branches. This tree is
the node's **unfolding tree** of depth $t$. Two nodes have the same colour after $t$ rounds exactly when their unfolding
trees of depth $t$ are isomorphic <Cite id="shervashidze2011" /> <Cite id="xu2019" />.

The test therefore sees a graph the way a walker sees it from inside: how many neighbours each node has, how many
neighbours they have, and so on. It never sees which branches of the unfolding tree are the same node, so it cannot tell
whether a walk has closed into a cycle. A hexagon and two separate triangles look the same from inside every node: two
neighbours, each with two neighbours, forever.

The same local view is what a <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing network</NoteLink> has. This
is why colour refinement became the yardstick for <Gloss name="gnn">graph neural networks</Gloss> <Cite id="xu2019" />
<Cite id="morris2019weisfeiler" />.

## Details

### The algorithm

Take an undirected graph $G = (\Vcal, \Ecal)$ with $n = \abs{\Vcal}$ nodes, $m = \abs{\Ecal}$ edges and neighbourhoods
$\Ncal(v) = \set{u : (u, v) \in \Ecal}$. A colour is a non-negative integer.

1. **Initialise.** Set $c^{(0)}(v) = 0$ for every node, or set it to the node's discrete label (an atom type, a node
   category).
2. **Collect signatures.** For every node, form the signature
   $\sigma^{(t+1)}(v) = \paren{c^{(t)}(v), \mset{c^{(t)}(u) : u \in \Ncal(v)}}$. The double braces $\mset{\cdot}$ denote
   a **multiset**: a collection in which order does not matter and repeats count. Writing the multiset as a sorted list
   makes it a concrete key.
3. **Relabel.** Give each distinct signature a fresh colour, the same colour for equal signatures and different colours
   for different ones. Sorting the distinct signatures and numbering them $0, 1, 2, \dots$ is one injective choice.
4. **Stop** when the number of colour classes did not grow. Otherwise go to step 2.

The signature contains the node's own old colour. Two nodes that differed in round $t$ therefore still differ in round
$t + 1$, so each round only splits classes. The number of classes grows by at least one in every round except the last,
and it cannot exceed $n$. Refinement therefore stabilises within $n$ rounds. Each round reads every edge twice and sorts
the signatures, so it costs $O((n + m)\log n)$ operations.

The colouring that comes out is the coarsest **equitable partition** compatible with the initial colours: every node of
class $i$ has the same number of neighbours in class $j$, for every pair of classes $i$ and $j$. A partition that fails
this condition contains a node whose neighbour counts differ from those of another node in its class, and the next
round splits them.

### Comparing two graphs

To compare graphs $G$ and $H$, refine both with **one** relabelling table, so that equal colours in the two graphs mean
equal signatures. Running refinement on the disjoint union $G \cup H$ does this automatically. In each round, compare
the **colour histograms**, the number of nodes of each colour in each graph.

- Histograms that differ in any round prove $G$ and $H$ are not isomorphic. An isomorphism maps each node to a node with
  the same signature in every round, so it preserves every histogram.
- Histograms that agree at the stable round prove nothing. The test is **sound** but not **complete**.

### What colour refinement detects

- **Degrees and their nesting.** Round 1 compares degree sequences. Round 2 compares, for each node, the multiset of its
  neighbours' degrees.
- **Almost every graph.** For a graph drawn uniformly at random from all graphs on $n$ nodes, colour refinement gives
  every node its own colour with probability tending to 1 as $n \to \infty$. A graph in which every node has its own
  colour is identified up to isomorphism by its colouring <Cite id="babai1980random" />.
- **Trees.** Colour refinement distinguishes any two non-isomorphic trees <Cite id="morris2023wl" />.

### What it misses

- **Regular graphs of one degree.** In a $d$-regular graph every node has $d$ neighbours. Round 1 gives every node the
  same colour, round 2 changes nothing, and the test stops. Any two $d$-regular graphs on $n$ nodes tie. The 6-cycle
  and two disjoint triangles are the smallest pair.
- **Cycles and connectivity.** Ties occur also in graphs that are not regular, such as decalin and bicyclopentyl in the
  figure, with six-membered rings in one and five-membered rings in the other. The 6-cycle against two triangles shows
  that colour refinement cannot decide whether a graph is connected or count its triangles. Which subgraph counts it
  does determine is worked out by Arvind et al. <Cite id="arvind2020wl" />.
- **Hard families for every fixed dimension.** The $k$-dimensional test colours $k$-tuples of nodes instead of single
  nodes and is strictly stronger for larger $k$. For every $k$ there are pairs of non-isomorphic graphs with $O(k)$
  nodes that the $k$-dimensional test cannot separate <Cite id="cai1992" />. The hierarchy is the subject of
  <NoteLink to="higher-order-and-subgraph-graph-neural-networks" />.

### The Weisfeiler-Lehman kernel

The colour histograms are also features. The Weisfeiler-Lehman subtree kernel stacks the histograms of rounds
$0, \dots, T$ of a graph into one count vector and takes the inner product of two graphs' vectors as their similarity
<Cite id="shervashidze2011" />. The kernel costs $O(Tm)$ per graph. A <Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss>
on it was a strong baseline for <NoteLink to="graphs-as-neural-network-inputs">graph classification</NoteLink> before
graph neural networks, and it remains one. Other hand-built structural features of graphs are in
<NoteLink to="graph-features" />.

### Variants

- **Edge labels.** The signature uses the multiset of pairs $\paren{c^{(t)}(u), \ell(u, v)}$ with edge label
  $\ell(u, v)$, such as a bond type.
- **Directed graphs.** The signature holds the multisets of in-neighbour and out-neighbour colours separately.
- **Hashing.** Implementations often replace the injective relabelling by a hash of the signature. A hash collision
  merges two classes, and the test can then report a tie between graphs it should separate.

## Worked example

**A path and a star.** Both graphs have four nodes and three edges. The path has edges $1{-}2$, $2{-}3$, $3{-}4$. The
star has a centre joined to three leaves.

- **Round 0.** Every node has colour 1. Both histograms are $\set{4 \times 1}$.
- **Round 1.** The signatures are: a node of degree 1 has $(1, \mset{1})$; a node of degree 2 has $(1, \mset{1, 1})$;
  a node of degree 3 has $(1, \mset{1, 1, 1})$. Sorting the three distinct signatures numbers them 1, 2 and 3.
  - Path: nodes 1 and 4 have degree 1, nodes 2 and 3 have degree 2. Histogram $\set{2 \times 1, 2 \times 2}$.
  - Star: three leaves of degree 1, a centre of degree 3. Histogram $\set{3 \times 1, 1 \times 3}$.

The histograms differ, so the graphs are not isomorphic. The test stops here because one difference is enough.

**A 6-cycle and two triangles.** Every node in both graphs has two neighbours.

- **Round 0.** All six nodes in each graph have colour 1.
- **Round 1.** Every node has signature $(1, \mset{1, 1})$, which becomes colour 1 again. No class split, so the
  colouring is stable. Both histograms are $\set{6 \times 1}$.

The test ties, although one graph is connected and the other is not, and one has two triangles and the other none.

**Decalin and bicyclopentyl** (choose the pair in the figure). Both skeletons have ten nodes and eleven edges, with two
adjacent nodes of degree 3 and eight of degree 2.

- **Round 1** splits the two degree-3 nodes (colour 2) from the eight degree-2 nodes (colour 1) in both graphs.
- **Round 2** splits the degree-2 nodes by whether they touch a degree-3 node. In both graphs four of them do and four
  do not. The histogram is $\set{4, 4, 2}$ in both.
- **Round 3** splits nothing. Each degree-3 node sees one degree-3 node and two nodes next to the junction; each node
  next to the junction sees one junction node and one other node; each remaining node sees one node next to the
  junction and one other node. These counts are the same in both graphs.

The stable histograms agree, so colour refinement cannot tell a pair of six-membered rings from a pair of five-membered
rings. Both colourings are equitable partitions with the same class sizes and the same neighbour counts between
classes, which is exactly what makes them indistinguishable.

## Assumptions

- **Discrete initial colours.** The test compares colours for equality. Real-valued node features must be discretised,
  or each distinct value treated as its own colour.
- **One relabelling for both graphs.** Colours computed in separate runs, each numbered from scratch, cannot be
  compared.
- **Isomorphism as the question.** The test answers "certainly different" or "maybe the same". It gives no measure of
  how different two graphs are; the kernel adds that.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading a tie as isomorphism.** Equal stable histograms are common for regular graphs, strongly regular graphs and
  molecules with similar ring systems. Only a complete algorithm, such as canonical labelling with search, confirms
  isomorphism.
- **Stopping before the stable round.** A tie at round $t$ can turn into a difference at round $t + 1$. The test
  reports a tie only at the stable round of the union of both graphs.
- **Comparing graphs of different sizes.** Different node counts give different histograms at round 0, which is a
  trivial difference. A statement such as "1-WL cannot distinguish these graphs" is interesting only for graphs that
  agree on simple statistics.
- **Ignoring features.** In machine-learning data, nodes usually carry features. Distinct features break symmetry from
  round 0, and the regular-graph failures above disappear. The failures matter when features repeat, as in molecules
  with many identical carbon atoms; see <NoteLink to="expressive-power-of-message-passing" />.
- **Spelling.** The second author is A. A. Leman. The spelling "Lehman" is common in the machine-learning literature
  and refers to the same test.
