<Definition>
A positional or structural encoding is a vector $\pvec_v \in \reals^{k}$ computed from the graph alone for every node
$v$ and appended to its input features, $\hvec_v^{(0)} = [\xvec_v \Vert \pvec_v]$, before <Gloss name="gnn">message
passing</Gloss> or attention. A **structural** encoding describes the node's neighbourhood and is equal on nodes that an
automorphism maps onto each other: degrees, cycle counts, random-walk return probabilities. A **positional** encoding
describes where the node sits in the graph, as coordinates: entries of Laplacian eigenvectors. Both supply information
that a <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing network</NoteLink> cannot compute from equal
features.
</Definition>

## Problem

A message passing network can separate only what colour refinement (<Gloss name="wl" form="short" />) separates
<Cite id="xu2019" />. With equal node features it gives every node of a 6-cycle and of two disjoint triangles the same
vector, it cannot count triangles, and it sees decalin and bicyclopentyl as the same molecule; see
<NoteLink to="expressive-power-of-message-passing" />. A graph transformer has the opposite gap: attention between all
pairs of nodes ignores the edges entirely unless structure is supplied.

Encodings close both gaps cheaply. They are computed once per graph, before training, and the network itself stays a
standard message passing network or transformer. Refinement started from the encodings as colours is at least as strong
as refinement started from constant colours, so an encoding can only add separating power.

Sequences have an obvious encoding: the position index, as in a
<NoteLink to="positional-encoding">transformer</NoteLink>. Graphs have no canonical order of nodes, so an index would
break permutation equivariance. Graph encodings must come from quantities that relabelling the nodes only permutes.

## Mechanism

### Random-walk structural encoding

Let $\Amat$ be the adjacency matrix and $\Dmat$ the diagonal degree matrix. The random-walk matrix
$\Pmat = \Dmat^{-1}\Amat$ moves a walker from a node to a uniformly chosen neighbour, so $(\Pmat^k)_{vv}$ is the
probability that a walk of $k$ steps from $v$ ends back at $v$. The random-walk structural encoding (RWSE) is

$$
\pvec_v = \paren{(\Pmat)_{vv},\ (\Pmat^2)_{vv},\ \dots,\ (\Pmat^K)_{vv}} \in [0, 1]^K.
$$

<Cite id="dwivedi2022" /> introduced it as a positional encoding (RWPE) and found it beat Laplacian encodings on
molecules. Relabelling the nodes permutes the rows and columns of $\Pmat^k$ together, so the diagonal entry of a node
moves with the node: the encoding is equivariant, with no arbitrary choices. It is structural: nodes that an
automorphism exchanges get the same vector.

Return probabilities see cycles. A closed walk of odd length must use an odd cycle, so $(\Pmat^3)_{vv} > 0$ exactly when
$v$ lies on a triangle, and odd entries vanish on bipartite graphs. Entry $k$ depends on the $\lfloor k/2 \rfloor$-hop
neighbourhood, so $K$ sets the radius of the structure the encoding describes.

### Laplacian positional encoding

Take the symmetric <NoteLink to="graph-laplacian-and-graph-fourier-transform">normalised Laplacian</NoteLink>
$\Lmat_{\text{sym}} = \I - \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$ with eigenvalues
$0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_n \le 2$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_n$. The
Laplacian positional encoding (LapPE) gives node $v$ the entries of the eigenvectors for the $k$ smallest non-zero
eigenvalues:

$$
\pvec_v = \paren{u_{2}(v),\ u_{3}(v),\ \dots,\ u_{k+1}(v)} \in \reals^{k}.
$$

These are the coordinates of a spectral embedding: the eigenvectors of small eigenvalues vary slowly across edges, so
nearby nodes get nearby encodings, as in <NoteLink to="laplacian-eigenmaps" />. On a cycle the eigenvectors are sines
and cosines of the position, the functions of the sinusoidal encoding of sequences, so LapPE generalises that encoding
to graphs <Cite id="dwivedi2020benchmarking" />. The encoding is positional: on a path, the two ends are exchanged by an
automorphism but receive opposite values.

### The ambiguity of eigenvectors

An eigenvector is defined only up to its sign: if $\Lmat_{\text{sym}}\uvec = \lambda\uvec$ then
$\Lmat_{\text{sym}}(-\uvec) = \lambda(-\uvec)$. With $k$ eigenvectors there are $2^k$ equally valid encodings of one
graph. When an eigenvalue is repeated with multiplicity $m$, any orthonormal basis of its $m$-dimensional eigenspace is
valid, a continuum of choices. A network fed one choice in training and another at test time sees two different inputs
for the same graph. Three remedies are used.

- **Random sign flips.** Flip each eigenvector's sign at random in every training batch, so the network learns to ignore
  signs <Cite id="dwivedi2020benchmarking" />. This handles signs, not bases, and the network only approximately learns
  the invariance.
- **Sign- and basis-invariant encoders.** SignNet maps each eigenvector by $\rho\paren{\phi(\uvec_i) + \phi(-\uvec_i)}$,
  which is unchanged by $\uvec_i \to -\uvec_i$ by construction. BasisNet processes the projector $\Umat_\lambda
  \Umat_\lambda^\tr$ onto each eigenspace, which does not depend on the basis <Cite id="lim2022signnet" />.
- **Spectral attention.** The spectral attention network treats each (eigenvalue, eigenvector entry) pair as a token and
  attends over the spectrum, learning which frequencies to use <Cite id="kreuzer2021san" />.

### Other encodings

- **Degree** and centralities add little to a message passing network, which already sees degrees after one round, but
  they help a transformer.
- **Substructure counts.** The number of triangles, cycles of length 4 to 6 or cliques through each node or edge,
  computed by subgraph matching, gives the graph substructure network its power beyond 1-WL
  <Cite id="bouritsas2023gsn" />.
- **Relative encodings.** Shortest-path distances or random-walk probabilities $(\Pmat^k)_{uv}$ between pairs of nodes
  become attention biases in a <NoteLink to="graph-transformer" />.

## Worked example

**Return probabilities on the 6-cycle and two triangles.** In both graphs every node has degree 2, so each step goes to
either neighbour with probability $1/2$, and a walk of $k$ steps has probability $(1/2)^k$.

- **Step 1.** Neither graph has self-loops, so $(\Pmat)_{vv} = 0$.
- **Step 2.** The walk returns by going out and straight back, to either neighbour: 2 walks, $2 \cdot (1/2)^2 = 1/2$ in
  both graphs.
- **Step 3.** In a triangle, the walks $v \to a \to b \to v$ and $v \to b \to a \to v$ return: $2 \cdot (1/2)^3 = 1/4$.
  The 6-cycle is bipartite, so no walk of odd length returns: 0.

The encodings $(0, \tfrac12, 0)$ and $(0, \tfrac12, \tfrac14)$ differ at $K = 3$, and a <Gloss name="gin" /> given them
separates the two graphs that it cannot separate without them.

**Decalin and bicyclopentyl.** Decalin has only six-membered rings and is bipartite, so its odd return probabilities
are 0. Bicyclopentyl has two five-membered rings. A node on a pentagon returns after five steps by going round the ring
in either direction, passing four degree-2 nodes and one degree-3 node, so its probability is
$2 \cdot (1/2)^4 \cdot (1/3) = 1/24 \approx 0.042$. The encodings first differ at $K = 5$, as Dwivedi et al. report
<Cite id="dwivedi2022" />.

**Where it stops.** The 4-by-4 rook's graph and the Shrikhande graph are both strongly regular with parameters
$(16, 6, 2, 2)$, have the same eigenvalues, and look the same from every node. All their return probabilities agree, so
RWSE leaves them tied.

**A Laplacian encoding on a path.** The path on 4 nodes, $1{-}2{-}3{-}4$, has degrees $(1, 2, 2, 1)$. The eigenvalues
of $\Lmat_{\text{sym}}$ are $1 - \cos(\pi j / 3)$ for $j = 0, 1, 2, 3$, that is $0, \tfrac12, \tfrac32, 2$, all simple.
The eigenvector of $\lambda_2 = \tfrac12$ is

$$
\uvec_2 = \paren{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3},\ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6},\ -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt6},\ -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}}
\approx (0.577,\ 0.408,\ -0.408,\ -0.577).
$$

Check one row: $(\Lmat_{\text{sym}}\uvec_2)_1 = u_1 - u_2/\sqrt{1 \cdot 2} = 0.577 - 0.289 = 0.289 = \tfrac12 \cdot
0.577$. With $k = 1$, node 1 gets $p_1 = 0.577$ and node 4 gets $-0.577$, although the reflection of the path exchanges
them. The sign of $\uvec_2$ is arbitrary: $-\uvec_2$ gives node 1 the value $-0.577$. The absolute value $0.577$ is the
same under both signs, which is what a sign-invariant encoder computes.

On the 6-cycle, $\lambda = \tfrac12$ has multiplicity 2, and every rotation of a basis of its eigenspace gives another
valid pair of eigenvectors. The diagonal of the projector onto the eigenspace is $\tfrac13$ at every node for every
choice of basis, which shows that the basis-invariant information in that eigenspace does not separate the nodes of a
cycle.

## Cost

- **RWSE.** Computing the diagonals of $\Pmat, \dots, \Pmat^K$ by repeated sparse-dense products costs $O(K\,n\,m)$
  time and $O(n^2)$ memory for a graph with $n$ nodes and $m$ edges. For molecules with tens of nodes this is negligible
  and done once in preprocessing. For large graphs, estimating the diagonal with random probe vectors or sampled walks
  avoids the dense matrix.
- **LapPE.** A full eigendecomposition costs $O(n^3)$, acceptable for small graphs. For large graphs, the $k$ smallest
  eigenpairs come from a Lanczos iteration, each step of which costs one sparse product, $O(m)$.
- **Model.** The encodings add $k$ input dimensions, and SignNet adds a small network per eigenvector. Message passing
  costs are unchanged.

## Trade-offs

| | RWSE | LapPE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kind | structural | positional |
| arbitrary choices | none | sign and basis |
| automorphic nodes | equal encodings | can differ |
| sees | cycles and local density within $K/2$ hops | global position, clusters, long-range distance |
| fails on | strongly regular and other cospectral, vertex-transitive pairs | depends on handling of ambiguity |

GPS, a framework for graph transformers, tested both inside one architecture. Random-walk encodings helped most on
molecular data, Laplacian encodings on image superpixel graphs, and Laplacian eigenvectors processed by SignNet worked
well across tasks <Cite id="rampasek2022gps" />. The GPS configurations use RWSE with 16 or 20 steps on molecular
benchmarks <Cite id="rampasek2022gps" />, and in 2024 to 2026 that remains the usual default: RWSE appended to the input
of a message passing network or graph transformer, with LapPE through a sign-invariant encoder added where global
position matters.

## Pitfalls

- **Feeding raw eigenvectors.** Without sign flipping or an invariant encoder, the model learns a sign convention that
  the eigensolver does not guarantee. A different solver, or a relabelled graph, can flip signs and change predictions.
- **Repeated eigenvalues.** Sign flips do not fix basis ambiguity. Symmetric graphs such as cycles, grids and many
  molecules have repeated eigenvalues, and basis choices there are arbitrary.
- **Disconnected graphs.** Each connected component adds an eigenvalue 0. Skipping only the first eigenvector then
  leaves indicator-like vectors of components in the encoding.
- **Too few steps.** RWSE with $K$ steps sees only cycles up to length $K$. Decalin and bicyclopentyl need $K \ge 5$.
- **Encodings are not a complete invariant.** Neither encoding separates all non-isomorphic graphs. The rook's and
  Shrikhande graphs defeat both.
