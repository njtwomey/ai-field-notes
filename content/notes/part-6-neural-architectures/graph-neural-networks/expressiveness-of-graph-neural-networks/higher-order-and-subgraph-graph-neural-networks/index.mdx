<Definition>
Higher-order and subgraph graph networks are graph architectures whose separating power exceeds colour refinement
(<Gloss name="wl" form="short" />). A **higher-order** network keeps a vector for every $k$-tuple or $k$-subset of nodes
and passes messages between tuples that differ in one node, matching the $k$-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman test at
$O(n^k)$ memory for $n$ nodes <Cite id="morris2019weisfeiler" /> <Cite id="maron2019ppgn" />. A **subgraph** network
turns a graph into a bag of subgraphs by a fixed policy, such as deleting one node or keeping one node's neighbourhood,
runs an ordinary <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss> network on each, and pools the results
<Cite id="bevilacqua2022esan" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A message passing network fails on the 6-cycle and two triangles because every node sees the same thing: two neighbours,
each with two neighbours. The failure is one of symmetry. No node is special, so nothing breaks the tie.

Two ways to break it follow from that diagnosis.

- **Look at pairs or triples.** A pair of nodes knows whether its two members are adjacent, at distance two, or in
  different components. In the 6-cycle some non-adjacent pairs share a neighbour; in two triangles none do. Colouring
  pairs instead of nodes exposes the difference.
- **Make one node special.** Delete a node, or mark it, and run message passing again. Deleting a node from the 6-cycle
  leaves a path of five nodes; deleting one from two triangles leaves an edge and a triangle. Plain message passing
  tells those apart.

Both cost more than message passing. The first stores $n^2$ or $n^3$ vectors; the second runs message passing $n$
times. The rest of this note makes the two ideas precise and compares their power and cost.

## Details

### The Weisfeiler-Leman hierarchy

The $k$-dimensional **folklore** test ($k$-FWL) colours every ordered $k$-tuple $\vvec = (v_1, \dots, v_k)$. The initial
colour is the tuple's **atomic type**: which entries are equal, which pairs are adjacent, and the node labels. For $k =
2$, a pair $(u, v)$ is refined by the multiset, over all nodes $w$, of the colours of the two pairs that route through
$w$:

$$
c^{(t+1)}(u, v) = \operatorname{relabel}\paren{c^{(t)}(u, v),\ \mset{\paren{c^{(t)}(u, w),\ c^{(t)}(w, v)} : w \in
\Vcal}}.
$$

The **oblivious** test $k$-WL refines a tuple by the $k$ separate multisets obtained by replacing one position at a
time. The two conventions are offset by one: $k$-FWL is as strong as $(k + 1)$-WL, and 2-WL is no stronger than 1-WL.
Each level is strictly stronger than the one below <Cite id="morris2023wl" />, and no fixed level separates all graphs
<Cite id="cai1992" />. Strongly regular graphs with equal parameters, such as the 4-by-4 rook's graph and the Shrikhande
graph, tie under 2-FWL <Cite id="arvind2020wl" />.

The 2-FWL update has the shape of a matrix product: entry $(u, v)$ combines row $u$ with column $v$ over the middle
index $w$. One round costs $O(n^3)$ time and the colours take $O(n^2)$ memory.

### Higher-order networks

- **$k$-GNNs.** Morris et al. learn a vector for every $k$-element subset of nodes, initialised by the isomorphism type
  of the subgraph it induces, and pass messages between subsets that share $k - 1$ nodes
  <Cite id="morris2019weisfeiler" />. To save memory they keep only local neighbours, subsets whose differing nodes are
  adjacent. Their hierarchical 1-2-3-GNN feeds node vectors into pair vectors and pair vectors into triple vectors.
- **Provably powerful graph networks** (PPGN) keep an $n \times n \times F$ tensor of pair features and update it by
  applying <Gloss name="mlp" plural /> to the feature dimension and multiplying the resulting $n \times n$ matrices,
  which is the 2-FWL update with learned functions. They match 3-WL in separating power with $O(n^2)$ memory and
  $O(n^3)$ time per layer <Cite id="maron2019ppgn" />.

Cubic cost limits these models to graphs with tens to a few hundred nodes, such as molecules.

### Subgraph networks

A **subgraph selection policy** $\pi$ maps a graph $G$ to a bag $\pi(G) = \mset{G_1, \dots, G_b}$ of subgraphs on the
same nodes <Cite id="bevilacqua2022esan" />.

- **Node-deleted:** one subgraph per node $v$, with $v$'s edges removed ($b = n$).
- **Edge-deleted:** one subgraph per edge, with that edge removed ($b = m$).
- **Ego network:** one subgraph per root $v$, keeping the edges within $r$ hops of $v$; in the marked variant (EGO+)
  the root carries an extra feature ($b = n$).

DS-GNN encodes each subgraph independently with a shared message passing network $E$ and pools the subgraph vectors with
a set network, $f(G) = \rho\paren{\sum_{i} E(G_i)}$. DSS-GNN also passes information between corresponding nodes of
different subgraphs inside every layer. The DS-WL test, colour refinement on every subgraph followed by comparison of
the bags of colour histograms, bounds DS-GNN with a message passing encoder <Cite id="bevilacqua2022esan" />.

Related designs mark a root node instead of deleting it. Identity-aware networks give the root its own message
functions <Cite id="you2021idgnn" />, and nested networks encode the rooted subgraph around every node and pool them
<Cite id="zhang2021nested" />. All of these use one subgraph per node, a **node-based** policy, and every node-based
subgraph network is bounded by 3-WL <Cite id="frasca2022subgraph" />.

### Cost

A node-based policy runs the encoder on $n$ subgraphs, so a layer costs $O(n(n + m))$ instead of $O(n + m)$, and an
edge-based policy $O(m(n + m))$. For molecules with 20 to 50 atoms the factor is affordable. For large graphs, subgraph
networks sample a few subgraphs per graph during training, or restrict ego networks to small radii
<Cite id="bevilacqua2022esan" />.

## Worked example

**2-FWL on the 6-cycle and two triangles.** Both graphs have 6 nodes, so 36 ordered pairs.

1. **Atomic types.** Each graph has 6 pairs $(v, v)$, 12 ordered adjacent pairs and 18 ordered non-adjacent pairs. The
   round-0 histograms are equal.
2. **Round 1, adjacent pairs.** For an adjacent pair $(u, v)$, count the nodes $w$ for which both $(u, w)$ and
   $(w, v)$ are adjacent: the common neighbours. In the 6-cycle an edge has none. In a triangle an edge has one, the
   third corner.
3. **Round 1, non-adjacent pairs.** In the 6-cycle, the 12 ordered pairs at distance 2 have one common neighbour, and
   the 6 ordered pairs at distance 3 have none. In two triangles, all 18 non-adjacent pairs lie in different triangles
   and have none.
4. **Compare.** The 6-cycle has 12 adjacent pairs with no common neighbour; two triangles have 12 adjacent pairs with
   one. The histograms differ at round 1, so 2-FWL separates the graphs that 1-WL ties.

**Node deletion on the same pair.** Delete node 1 from each graph.

1. The 6-cycle becomes the path $2{-}3{-}4{-}5{-}6$ plus the isolated node 1. Two triangles become the edge $2{-}3$,
   the triangle $4{-}5{-}6$ and the isolated node 1.
2. **Round 1** colours by degree. Both subgraphs have degrees $\set{0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2}$, so the histograms tie.
3. **Round 2** colours by neighbours' degrees. The path's ends have a neighbour of degree 2. The edge's ends have a
   neighbour of degree 1. The histograms differ.

Every node of either graph gives the same subgraph up to isomorphism, so the two bags hold six copies each of two
different subgraphs, and DS-WL separates the graphs.

**Where node deletion stops.** Deleting a node of the rook's graph or of the Shrikhande graph leaves two 15-node graphs
that colour refinement ties, so the node-deleted bags tie. This agrees with the 3-WL bound on node-based policies, since
2-FWL already ties these graphs. Edge deletion is not node-based, and the figure shows that its bags separate them
<Cite id="bevilacqua2022esan" />.

## Assumptions

- **Small graphs.** Both families pay a factor of $n$ or more over message passing. They suit molecules and other
  graphs of up to a few hundred nodes.
- **Symmetry is the bottleneck.** The extra power helps when graphs are symmetric and features repeat. On graphs with
  distinct node features, message passing already separates almost everything.
- **Fixed policy.** A subgraph network's power depends on its policy. Depth-1 ego networks of decalin and bicyclopentyl
  are stars with the same shapes, and those bags tie.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing the two conventions.** "3-WL" in one paper is "2-FWL" in another. Check which test a bound refers to
  before comparing models.
- **Memory before time.** A $k$-tuple model with $k = 3$ stores $n^3$ vectors. At $n = 100$ and 64 features in 32-bit
  floats this is 256 MB per layer for one graph.
- **Bag pooling must be invariant.** The subgraphs come in no natural order. Pooling them by concatenation would break
  <NoteLink to="deep-sets">permutation invariance</NoteLink>; a sum or mean over the bag keeps it.
- **More power is not more accuracy.** Synthetic expressiveness benchmarks reward separating power directly. On real
  data the gain must be weighed against a cost of $n$ times or more, and cheaper
  <NoteLink to="positional-and-structural-encodings" /> are the usual first step.
