<Definition>
Principal neighbourhood aggregation (<Gloss name="pna" form="short" />) is a <Gloss name="gnn">message passing</Gloss>
layer that summarises the multiset of messages arriving at node $v$ by four statistics, the mean $\mu$, the standard
deviation $\sigma$, the maximum and the minimum, and multiplies each by three functions of the node's degree $d_v$,

$$
S(d_v, \alpha) = \paren{\frac{\log(d_v + 1)}{\delta}}^{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha \in \set{0, 1, -1},
$$

the identity, amplification and attenuation scalers, where $\delta$ is the mean of $\log(d + 1)$ over the training
graphs' nodes. The twelve results are concatenated and mapped back to the hidden width by a learned update
<Cite id="corso2020pna" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="graph-isomorphism-network">graph isomorphism network</NoteLink> shows that a sum followed by an
<Gloss name="mlp" /> can identify any multiset of neighbour features, provided the features come from a countable set
such as atom types. The proof places each possible feature in its own "digit" of a sum. Hidden vectors inside a network
are real-valued, and the neighbourhoods a layer must tell apart differ by small amounts. A single sum of real numbers
cannot keep every multiset apart: $\mset{-1, 1}$ and $\mset{-2, 2}$ both sum to 0, and so do infinitely many other
pairs.

A distribution of real numbers is described by several statistics, not one. Mean and spread, together with the extremes,
already tell apart many neighbourhoods that any single statistic confuses. PNA applies this idea to message passing: it
keeps several aggregators side by side and lets the update learn which to use.

The mean and the standard deviation do not change when every neighbour is duplicated, so they lose the number of
neighbours. Sum keeps it, because a sum is a mean times the degree. PNA restores the degree with a separate factor, and
uses the logarithm of the degree, so that a node with 1,000 neighbours is not weighted 1,000 times more than a node with
one.

PNA keeps the message passing template of GIN and changes the aggregation: four statistics instead of one sum, and an
explicit, logarithmic dependence on degree instead of the implicit, linear one of the sum.

## Details

### Why one aggregator is not enough

Corso et al. prove that separating all multisets of $n$ real numbers needs at least $n$ continuous aggregators
<Cite id="corso2020pna" />. The reason is dimension. Multisets of $n$ reals correspond to sorted tuples $x_1 \le \dots
\le x_n$, a region of $\reals^n$ with $n$ free coordinates. A continuous map from that region into $\reals^{k}$ with
$k < n$ cannot be one-to-one. The $n$ moments of the multiset are $n$ aggregators that do separate all such multisets.

Neighbourhoods in real graphs have many more than four members, so four aggregators do not achieve injectivity either.
They cover the most common differences: location (mean), spread (standard deviation) and range (maximum and minimum).
Higher normalised moments $\sqrt[k]{\expect[(X - \mu)^k]}$ can be added for graphs with large degrees.

### The aggregators

For node $v$ with neighbour messages $x_u$, $u \in \Ncal(v)$, and $d_v = \abs{\Ncal(v)}$:

$$
\mu_v = \frac{1}{d_v}\sum_{u \in \Ncal(v)} x_u, \quad
\sigma_v = \sqrt{\mathrm{ReLU}\paren{\frac{1}{d_v}\sum_{u \in \Ncal(v)} x_u^2 - \mu_v^2} + \varepsilon}, \quad
\max_{u \in \Ncal(v)} x_u, \quad \min_{u \in \Ncal(v)} x_u.
$$

Each is applied to every feature separately. The <Gloss name="relu" /> removes small negative values that rounding
produces, and the constant $\varepsilon > 0$ keeps the square root differentiable at zero variance
<Cite id="corso2020pna" />.

### The degree scalers

The sum aggregator equals the mean times the degree: $\sum_u x_u = d_v\,\mu_v$. Any scaler that is an injective
function of $d_v$ restores the information about multiset size that the mean drops, so a mean times an injective scaler
is as discriminative as a sum on countable features <Cite id="corso2020pna" />. The linear scaler $d_v$ has a cost: a
layer multiplies its input by the degree, and $L$ layers by roughly $d^L$, which makes trained networks sensitive to
graphs with larger degrees than the training set. The logarithm grows slowly, and $\delta$ normalises it so that a node
of typical degree gets a factor near 1.

Amplification ($\alpha = 1$) emphasises high-degree nodes, attenuation ($\alpha = -1$) emphasises low-degree nodes, and
the identity ($\alpha = 0$) leaves the aggregate as it is. The combined aggregator is the tensor product of the three
scalers with the four aggregators, giving twelve blocks of $F$ features each.

### The layer

PNA sits inside the general message passing network <Cite id="corso2020pna" />:

$$
\hvec_v' = U\paren{\hvec_v,\ \bigoplus_{u \in \Ncal(v)} M(\hvec_v, \evec_{uv}, \hvec_u)},
$$

with $\bigoplus$ the twelve scaled aggregates, $M$ a message network and $U$ an update that maps the concatenation of
size $13F$ (the node's own vector and twelve aggregates) back to $F$. Corso et al. found a linear $M$ and $U$ sufficient
on their benchmarks, and split the features into towers, each with its own aggregation, to keep the cost down.

PNA is still a message passing network, so it is still bounded by colour refinement (<Gloss name="wl" form="short" />).
Its gain is within the bound: it reaches more of the separations that colour refinement allows when features are
continuous.

### Practice

PNA is a standard strong baseline for molecular graphs. In the GPS study of <NoteLink to="graph-transformer">graph
transformers</NoteLink>, a plain PNA generally outperformed GINE and
<NoteLink to="gated-and-edge-conditioned-message-passing">GatedGCN</NoteLink> as the local message passing module,
before positional and structural encodings were added <Cite id="rampasek2022gps" />.

## Worked example

**Equal mean, different spread.** Node $a$ has neighbour features $\mset{-1, 1}$ and node $b$ has $\mset{-2, 2}$.

- Mean: $(-1 + 1)/2 = 0$ and $(-2 + 2)/2 = 0$. Equal.
- Sum: 0 and 0. Equal.
- Maximum: 1 and 2. Different.
- Minimum: $-1$ and $-2$. Different.
- Standard deviation: $\sqrt{(1 + 1)/2 - 0} = 1$ and $\sqrt{(4 + 4)/2 - 0} = 2$. Different.

A GIN layer on real-valued hidden vectors would rely on its MLP to have made these sums differ; PNA separates them
directly with three of its four statistics.

**Equal statistics, different degree.** Node $a$ has $\mset{1, 1}$ and node $b$ has $\mset{1}$.

- Mean 1 and 1, maximum 1 and 1, minimum 1 and 1, standard deviation 0 and 0. All four aggregators tie.
- The amplification scaler with $\delta = 1$ multiplies the mean by $\log 3 = 1.099$ for $a$ and by $\log 2 = 0.693$ for
  $b$. The scaled means differ.
- The attenuation scaler gives $1/\log 3 = 0.910$ and $1/\log 2 = 1.443$.

**Computing $\delta$.** A training set whose nodes have degrees $1, 1, 2, 2, 3$ has
$\delta = (\log 2 + \log 2 + \log 3 + \log 3 + \log 4)/5 = (0.693 + 0.693 + 1.099 + 1.099 + 1.386)/5 = 0.994$. A node of
degree 2 then gets an amplification factor $1.099/0.994 = 1.105$, close to 1.

## Assumptions

- **Continuous features.** The motivation is real-valued hidden vectors. With few, discrete features, a sum with an MLP
  already separates the multisets.
- **Representative degrees.** $\delta$ comes from the training graphs. Test graphs with very different degree
  distributions shift every scaled aggregate.
- **Neighbourhood statistics matter.** The extra aggregators help where neighbourhoods differ in spread or range, as
  with atom features in molecules or signal values on meshes.

## Pitfalls

- **Width and cost.** Twelve aggregates per feature make the update's input 13 times wider than the hidden width. The
  layer costs several times a GIN layer in memory and parameters; towers reduce it.
- **Zero variance.** Without $\varepsilon$, the gradient of $\sigma$ is infinite where all neighbours agree, which is
  common for leaf nodes and for constant input features.
- **Degree zero.** The scalers are defined for $d > 0$. Isolated nodes need a convention, such as zero aggregates.
- **No escape from the bound.** Multiple aggregators do not separate the 6-cycle from two triangles, or any other pair
  that colour refinement ties; see <NoteLink to="expressive-power-of-message-passing" />.
