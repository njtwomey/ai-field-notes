<Definition>
<Gloss name="lora" /> adapts a pretrained network by freezing each chosen weight matrix
$\Wmat_0 \in \reals^{d \times k}$ and learning an additive update of rank at most $r$. For a layer input
$\xvec \in \reals^k$, the adapted layer computes

$$
\hvec = \Wmat_0 \xvec + \frac{\alpha}{r} \Bmat \Amat \xvec, \qquad \Bmat \in \reals^{d \times r},\; \Amat \in \reals^{r \times k},\; r \ll \min(d, k),
$$

where $\hvec \in \reals^d$ is the layer output and $\alpha > 0$ is a fixed scale. $\Bmat$ starts at zero and $\Amat$ at
random values, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model. Only $\Amat$ and $\Bmat$ are trained. After training,
the update $(\alpha / r) \Bmat \Amat$ can be added into $\Wmat_0$, which leaves a model of the original shape and speed
<Cite id="hu2022" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Full fine-tuning updates every parameter of a pretrained model. With <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> in
<NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">mixed precision</NoteLink>, each trained parameter costs about 16 bytes of
accelerator memory: a 16-bit weight, a 16-bit gradient, a 32-bit master copy and two 32-bit moment estimates
<Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />. An 8-billion-parameter model therefore needs about 128 GB for its training state before
any activations are stored, more than one 80 GB accelerator holds. The
<NoteLink to="quantised-low-rank-adaptation">memory of fine-tuning</NoteLink> note derives this accounting in full.

Full fine-tuning also produces a full copy of the model for every task. A 16-bit checkpoint of an 8-billion-parameter
model takes 16 GB. A hundred task-specific variants take 1.6 TB on disk and cannot share accelerator memory at serving
time.

LoRA addresses both costs. Gradients and optimiser states exist only for the small factors $\Amat$ and $\Bmat$, and a
task is stored as those factors alone. On <Gloss name="gpt-3" /> 175B, Hu et al. reduced training memory from 1.2 TB to
350 GB and the per-task checkpoint from 350 GB to 35 MB <Cite id="hu2022" />.

## Mechanism

### The low-rank update and its parameter count

Each column of $\Bmat \Amat$ is a linear combination of the $r$ columns of $\Bmat$, so
<NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">$\rank(\Bmat \Amat) \le r$</NoteLink>. The update has

$$
\underbrace{d r}_{\Bmat} + \underbrace{r k}_{\Amat} = r(d + k)
$$

trainable parameters, against $dk$ for an unconstrained update of the same matrix. The saving factor is

$$
\frac{dk}{r(d + k)}, \qquad \text{which for a square matrix } (d = k) \text{ is } \frac{d}{2r}.
$$

A $4096 \times 4096$ attention projection has $4096^2 = 16{,}777{,}216$ weights. A rank-16 update of it has
$16 \times (4096 + 4096) = 131{,}072$ parameters, $4096 / 32 = 128$ times fewer.

The update is a bottleneck. The input $\xvec$ is first projected to $r$ numbers, $\Amat \xvec$, and then expanded back
to $d$ outputs by $\Bmat$. Computing $\Bmat (\Amat \xvec)$ in that order costs $r(d + k)$ multiply-adds per token,
against $dk$ for $\Wmat_0 \xvec$. The product $\Bmat \Amat$ is never formed during training.

### Why a low-rank update can be enough

The **intrinsic dimension** of a training problem is the smallest number of free parameters, in a random subspace
$\thetavec = \thetavec_0 + \Pmat \zvec$ with $\zvec \in \reals^{D}$, at which training reaches 90% of the performance of
training all parameters <Cite id="li2018intrinsic" />. Aghajanyan et al. measured it for fine-tuning pretrained language
models. Optimising 200 parameters, projected randomly into the full parameter space, brought <Gloss name="roberta" /> to
90% of its full fine-tuning performance on the MRPC paraphrase task. Longer pretraining lowered the intrinsic dimension,
and larger models had lower intrinsic dimension after the same number of pretraining updates
<Cite id="aghajanyan2021" />. A pretrained model already holds most of what a downstream task needs, and fine-tuning has
to move it only a short way in a few directions.

LoRA makes the analogous assumption per weight matrix: the change $\Delta\Wmat$ that adapts the model has low rank. Hu
et al. found that ranks as low as 1 to 8 on the query and value projections of GPT-3 matched full fine-tuning on the
tasks they tested. The learned update was not a copy of the dominant directions of $\Wmat_0$. It amplified directions
that $\Wmat_0$ represented only weakly, by a factor of about 21.5 at $r = 4$ <Cite id="hu2022" />.

The assumption has limits. Biderman et al. took the difference between fully fine-tuned and pretrained weights of
Llama 2 7B on code and mathematics and found that it needed a rank 10 to 100 times larger than typical LoRA ranks to
explain 90% of its variance <Cite id="biderman2024" />. Low rank suits tasks that reuse the model's knowledge, such as
a new output format, a style or a narrow classification. It suits less well tasks that add much new knowledge, such as a
new programming language or domain.

### Initialisation: zero $\Bmat$, random $\Amat$

Write $s = \alpha / r$ and $\zvec = \Amat \xvec \in \reals^r$, so that $\hvec = \Wmat_0 \xvec + s \Bmat \zvec$. Let
$\gvec = \partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \hvec$ be the gradient of the loss with respect to the layer output, supplied
by <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>. For one input, the gradients of the factors are

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \Bmat} = s\, \gvec \zvec^{\tr} = s\, \gvec (\Amat \xvec)^{\tr}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \Amat} = s\, \Bmat^{\tr} \gvec \xvec^{\tr}.
$$

The first follows because $\hvec$ depends on $B_{ij}$ only through $h_i$, with $\partial h_i / \partial B_{ij} = s z_j$.
The second follows by applying the chain rule through $\zvec$: $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \zvec = s \Bmat^{\tr}
\gvec$, and $\zvec = \Amat \xvec$ is linear in $\Amat$.

Three initialisations are possible, and only one works.

- **Both zero.** Then $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \Bmat = s \gvec (\zeros)^{\tr} = \zeros$ and
  $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \Amat = \zeros$. The factors are at a stationary point and never move.
- **Both random.** Then $s \Bmat \Amat \neq \zeros$, and the model starts at a randomly perturbed copy of the pretrained
  one. Training first has to undo the damage.
- **$\Bmat = \zeros$, $\Amat$ random.** Then $s \Bmat \Amat = \zeros$, so the adapted model computes exactly the
  pretrained function. $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \Amat = \zeros$, but $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \Bmat
  = s \gvec (\Amat \xvec)^{\tr}$ is non-zero whenever $\Amat \xvec \neq \zeros$. $\Bmat$ moves first, in the random
  $r$-dimensional subspace of inputs that $\Amat$ selects, and $\Amat$ starts to move once $\Bmat$ is non-zero.

Hu et al. draw $\Amat$ from a Gaussian <Cite id="hu2022" />. The <Gloss name="peft" form="short" /> library draws it
from a Kaiming-uniform distribution by default <Cite id="mangrulkar2022" />.

### The scale $\alpha / r$, and why rsLoRA uses $\alpha / \sqrt{r}$

The scale $s$ multiplies every update. Hu et al. chose $s = \alpha / r$, set $\alpha$ to the first rank they tried and
did not tune it. With Adam, they note, tuning $\alpha$ is roughly the same as tuning the
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> <Cite id="hu2022" />. Whether $1/r$ is the right dependence on rank
depends on the optimiser, which a one-step calculation shows.

Start from $\Bmat = \zeros$ and take one step on a single example. Let the entries of $\Amat$ be independent with mean
zero and variance $\sigma^2$, so that $\expect \norm{\Amat \xvec}^2 = r \sigma^2 \norm{\xvec}^2$: a sum of $r$ squared
projections.

**Plain <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>.** With learning rate $\eta$,
$\Bmat_1 = -\eta s \gvec (\Amat \xvec)^{\tr}$. The adapter's new output on the same input is

$$
s \Bmat_1 \Amat \xvec = -\eta s^2 \norm{\Amat \xvec}^2 \gvec \approx -\eta s^2 r \sigma^2 \norm{\xvec}^2 \gvec.
$$

With $s = \alpha / r$ the change is proportional to $\eta \alpha^2 / r$: doubling the rank halves the initial speed of
learning, and a large rank learns too slowly to use its extra capacity. With $s = \alpha / \sqrt r$ the change is
proportional to $\eta \alpha^2$, whatever the rank. Kalajdzievski proved the general form of this result. The adapter's
activations and gradients stay of constant size as $r$ grows, without collapsing or exploding, if and only if
$s \propto 1 / \sqrt r$. He called the choice $s = \alpha / \sqrt r$ <Gloss name="rslora" form="full" />.
Fine-tuning Llama 2 7B at ranks from 4 to 2048 with the $1/r$ scale gave about the same loss at every rank. With the
$1/\sqrt r$ scale, higher ranks reached lower loss <Cite id="kalajdzievski2023" />.

**Adam.** Adam's first step, after bias correction, moves each coordinate by exactly $\eta$ in the direction of the
negative gradient sign. So $(\Bmat_1)_{ij} = -\eta \sgn(g_i z_j)$, whatever the value of $s$. Entry $i$ of the
adapter's new output is

$$
s \sum_{j=1}^{r} (\Bmat_1)_{ij} z_j = -\eta s \sgn(g_i) \sum_{j=1}^{r} \abs{z_j}.
$$

All $r$ terms have the same sign, so the sum grows in proportion to $r$, and $s = \alpha / r$ makes the first step's
effect independent of the rank. This is consistent with the observation by Schulman et al. that, with the $1/r$ scale
and Adam, the best learning rate is almost independent of the rank <Cite id="schulman2025lora" />. Later steps are not
pure sign steps, and the high-rank gains that Kalajdzievski measured with <Gloss name="adamw" /> show that $1/r$ still
under-uses large ranks.

In practice, $s = \alpha / r$ is the default. When the rank changes by a large factor, retune the learning rate, or use
$s = \alpha / \sqrt r$ (`use_rslora=True` in PEFT <Cite id="mangrulkar2022" />). Biderman et al. recommend
$\alpha = 2r$ <Cite id="biderman2024" />.

### Which matrices to adapt

A <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> block of a modern decoder has seven linear layers: the query, key,
value and output projections of attention ($\Wmat_q, \Wmat_k, \Wmat_v, \Wmat_o$), and the gate, up and down
projections of the <NoteLink to="gated-feed-forward-layers">gated feed-forward layer</NoteLink>. In Llama 3 8B the
feed-forward layers hold 81% of the block's weights.

Hu et al. adapted only $\Wmat_q$ and $\Wmat_v$. Under a fixed budget of 18 million parameters on GPT-3, spreading rank
over $\Wmat_q$ and $\Wmat_v$ beat putting all of it into one matrix type <Cite id="hu2022" />. Later work found that
attention-only adapters underfit:

- <Gloss name="qlora" /> could not match full fine-tuning of LLaMA 7B with adapters on the query and value projections.
  With adapters on every linear layer it could, and the rank then made little difference <Cite id="dettmers2023" />.
- Schulman et al. found that adapters on the feed-forward layers alone, at rank 128, beat attention-only adapters at
  rank 256 with a similar parameter count. Adding attention adapters to feed-forward adapters gave no further gain
  <Cite id="schulman2025lora" />.

The default today is to adapt every linear layer of every block (`target_modules="all-linear"` in PEFT
<Cite id="mangrulkar2022" />). The embedding table and the output layer are usually left frozen.

### Choosing the rank

The rank sets the capacity of the update: the number of independent directions per matrix in which the model can
change. Ranks of 8 to 64 are common. The right rank depends on how much the task asks the model to learn.

For a single matrix the capacity limit is exact. Let $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots$ be the singular values of the
change $\Delta\Wmat$ that the task needs. No update of rank $r$ brings $\tfrac12 \norm{s \Bmat \Amat - \Delta\Wmat}_F^2$
below $\tfrac12 \sum_{i > r} \sigma_i^2$, the energy in the singular values past the $r$th. The best rank-$r$ update
keeps the top $r$ singular triplets of $\Delta\Wmat$ <Cite id="eckart1936" />.

- Narrow tasks that reuse the model's knowledge, such as classification, extraction or a fixed output format, are
  usually served by ranks of 8 to 16.
- Tasks that add knowledge need more. For code <NoteLink to="instruction-tuning-and-alignment">instruction
  tuning</NoteLink> of Llama 2 7B, rank 16 reached a <NoteLink to="language-model-evaluation">HumanEval</NoteLink> pass
  rate of 0.358 against 0.497 for full fine-tuning; rank 256 came close to full fine-tuning <Cite id="biderman2024" />.
- <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards">Reinforcement learning</NoteLink> with a scalar reward
  conveys little information per episode. Schulman et al. found that rank 1 matched full fine-tuning in policy-gradient
  training <Cite id="schulman2025lora" />.

A practical test: if the LoRA training loss plateaus above the loss of a short full fine-tuning run on the same data,
the update is capacity-limited. Raise the rank or adapt more matrices.

### Reading a trained adapter

A trained adapter is a set of explicit matrices, so it can be inspected. For each adapted matrix, form
$\Delta \Wmat = s \Bmat \Amat$ and compute its <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value
decomposition</NoteLink>, which costs only $O(r^2 (d + k))$ from the factors.

- **Spectrum.** If the singular values of $\Delta\Wmat$ fall off quickly, the update uses fewer directions than $r$
  allows and a smaller rank would do. If all $r$ are of similar size, the rank may be binding.
- **Relative size.** The ratio $\norm{\Delta \Wmat}_F / \norm{\Wmat_0}_F$ per layer shows where the model changed most.
- **Stability across ranks.** Hu et al. trained adapters of rank 8 and rank 64 and compared their column spaces. The top
  few directions agreed closely, and the rest of the rank-64 directions looked like noise <Cite id="hu2022" />.

### Merging, and serving many adapters

After training, the update can be folded into the frozen weight once:

$$
\Wmat = \Wmat_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r} \Bmat \Amat.
$$

The merged model has the original shape, so inference costs exactly what the pretrained model costs. Adapter layers
that insert small networks between existing layers add depth, and therefore latency, at every token. LoRA does not.
Subtracting the same product unmerges it, up to rounding.

Leaving the adapter unmerged allows one base model to serve many tasks. A batch can then mix requests for different
adapters. The base product $\Wmat_0 \Xmat$ is computed once for the whole batch, and each request adds its own
$s \Bmat \Amat \xvec$ through small batched products. Punica's batched kernel for mixed adapters gave 12 times the
throughput of serving systems without it, at 2 ms extra latency per token <Cite id="chen2024punica" />. S-LoRA keeps
all adapters in host memory, pages the active ones into accelerator memory alongside the
<NoteLink to="key-value-cache">key-value cache</NoteLink>, and serves thousands of adapters on one base model
<Cite id="sheng2024slora" />. vLLM accepts an adapter per request, with limits on the number of adapters resident at
once and on their largest rank <Cite id="vllm2023" />.

### Variants

- **LoRA+** gives $\Bmat$ a larger learning rate than $\Amat$. Hayou et al. show that equal learning rates make feature
  learning inefficient when the model is wide. The best ratio $\eta_B / \eta_A$ depended on the model: about $2^4$ for
  RoBERTa and $2^1$ to $2^2$ for Llama. They report gains of 1 to 2 percentage points and up to twice the training speed
  <Cite id="hayou2024" />.
- **<Gloss name="dora" />** writes each weight column as a magnitude times a unit direction. It trains the magnitudes
  $\mvec$ directly and the direction through LoRA:
  $\Wmat = \mvec \odot (\Wmat_0 + \Bmat\Amat) / \norm{\Wmat_0 + \Bmat\Amat}_c$, where $\norm{\cdot}_c$ is the norm of
  each column. In full fine-tuning, the changes in magnitude and in direction were negatively correlated ($-0.62$); in
  LoRA they were positively correlated ($0.83$). DoRA moved the correlation to $-0.31$ and improved on LoRA at the same
  rank <Cite id="liu2024dora" />. It costs more per training step and is merged for inference.
- **<Gloss name="pissa" />** initialises the adapter from the top $r$ singular triplets of $\Wmat_0$,
  $\Bmat \Amat = \Umat_r \Sigmamat_r \Vmat_r^{\tr}$, and freezes the residual
  $\Wmat_0 - \Umat_r \Sigmamat_r \Vmat_r^{\tr}$. The model still starts exactly at the pretrained function, but training
  moves the principal directions of the weight instead of a random subspace. On GSM8K, Mistral 7B reached 72.86%
  accuracy with PiSSA against 67.7% with LoRA <Cite id="meng2024pissa" />.
- **<Gloss name="vera" />** shares one pair of frozen random matrices across all layers and trains only two scaling
  vectors per layer, $\Delta \Wmat = \diag(\bvec) \Bmat \diag(\dvec) \Amat$. Each layer then trains $d + r$
  parameters instead of $r(d + k)$ <Cite id="kopiczko2024" />.

The <NoteLink to="quantised-low-rank-adaptation">QLoRA</NoteLink> note covers LoRA on a quantised base model, and the
<NoteLink to="parameter-efficient-fine-tuning">parameter-efficient fine-tuning</NoteLink> note covers methods that are
not low-rank.

## Cost

### Worked example: Llama 3 8B

<NoteLink to="llama-family">Llama 3 8B</NoteLink> has 32 blocks with model width 4096. Attention has 32 query heads and
8 key-value heads of width 128, so the key and value projections map 4096 inputs to $8 \times 128 = 1024$ outputs. The
feed-forward width is 14,336 <Cite id="grattafiori2024" />. LoRA at $r = 16$ on every linear layer of one block trains:

| Matrix            | Shape $d \times k$       | Full update $dk$ | LoRA $r(d + k)$ |
| ----------------- | ------------------------ | ---------------- | --------------- |
| $\Wmat_q$         | $4096 \times 4096$       | 16,777,216       | 131,072         |
| $\Wmat_k$         | $1024 \times 4096$       | 4,194,304        | 81,920          |
| $\Wmat_v$         | $1024 \times 4096$       | 4,194,304        | 81,920          |
| $\Wmat_o$         | $4096 \times 4096$       | 16,777,216       | 131,072         |
| gate, up, each    | $14336 \times 4096$      | 58,720,256       | 294,912         |
| down              | $4096 \times 14336$      | 58,720,256       | 294,912         |
| **Block total**   |                          | 218,103,808      | 1,310,720       |

Over 32 blocks, LoRA trains $32 \times 1{,}310{,}720 = 41{,}943{,}040$ parameters. That is 0.6% of the
$6.98 \times 10^9$ weights it adapts and 0.52% of the model's $8.03 \times 10^9$ parameters.

- **Training state.** At 16 bytes per trained parameter, the adapters' weights, gradients and Adam state take
  $16 \times 41.9 \times 10^6 \approx 0.67$ GB, against $16 \times 8.03 \times 10^9 \approx 128$ GB for full
  fine-tuning. The frozen 16-bit base still takes $2 \times 8.03 \times 10^9 \approx 16$ GB.
- **Storage.** The adapter in 16 bits takes $2 \times 41.9 \times 10^6 \approx 84$ MB, against 16 GB for a full
  checkpoint.
- **Compute.** A training step costs about one forward pass $F$ plus a backward pass of about $2F$: one $F$ for the
  gradients with respect to activations and one for the gradients with respect to weights. LoRA skips the weight
  gradients of the frozen matrices, so a step costs about $2F$ instead of $3F$, plus the adapters' 0.6% extra. Hu et al.
  measured a 25% speed-up on GPT-3 <Cite id="hu2022" />.

LoRA does not reduce activation memory. Gradients must still flow back through every frozen layer to reach the adapters
in earlier blocks, so every block's activations are kept or recomputed as in full fine-tuning.

## Trade-offs

### LoRA learns less and forgets less

Biderman et al. compared LoRA with full fine-tuning of Llama 2 7B on code and mathematics, both for instruction tuning
(about 100,000 prompt–response pairs) and for continued pretraining (up to 20 billion tokens)
<Cite id="biderman2024" />.

- **Learns less.** LoRA underperformed full fine-tuning on the target domain at typical ranks. The gap was larger for
  code than for mathematics, and larger for continued pretraining than for instruction tuning. On mathematics
  instruction tuning, rank 64 reached 62.4% on GSM8K against 64.2% for full fine-tuning.
- **Forgets less.** LoRA kept more of the base model's performance on tasks outside the target domain, measured on
  HellaSwag, WinoGrande and ARC-Challenge. It limited forgetting more than <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight
  decay</Gloss> or <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> did, and kept generations more diverse.

Both effects follow from the constraint. In each adapted matrix, LoRA can change the model only within an
$r$-dimensional subspace of outputs and of inputs, so it moves the weights a shorter distance from the pretrained point.
That limits what the model can absorb, and it also limits how much of its prior behaviour it can overwrite. Biderman et
al. recommend LoRA for instruction tuning rather than continued pretraining. Schulman et al. reach a compatible
conclusion: LoRA matches full fine-tuning when its capacity exceeds what the dataset contains and it adapts every
layer, and falls behind once the dataset outgrows it <Cite id="schulman2025lora" />.

| Choice                  | Gains                                           | Costs                                             |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| LoRA over full tuning   | about an eighth of the model-state memory        | lower ceiling on large or knowledge-heavy tasks   |
| Higher rank             | capacity for harder tasks                        | more memory; drifts further from the base         |
| All linear layers       | matches full tuning where attention-only fails   | several times the adapter parameters              |
| Merged adapter          | zero inference overhead                          | one task per model copy                           |
| Unmerged adapters       | many tasks on one base model                     | small per-token cost; batching kernels needed     |

## Pitfalls

- **Learning rate copied from full fine-tuning.** The best LoRA learning rate is about 10 times the best full
  fine-tuning rate <Cite id="schulman2025lora" />. QLoRA used $2 \times 10^{-4}$ for 7B and 13B models and $10^{-4}$ for
  33B and 65B models <Cite id="dettmers2023" />. A rate tuned for full fine-tuning leaves LoRA undertrained.
- **Attention-only targets.** Adapting only $\Wmat_q$ and $\Wmat_v$, the original default, underfits instruction
  tuning. Adapt every linear layer.
- **Changing the rank without retuning.** Under $s = \alpha / r$ with plain gradient descent, a larger rank learns
  more slowly. Retune the learning rate or $\alpha$, or use rsLoRA.
- **Large batches.** LoRA pays a larger loss penalty than full fine-tuning as the batch grows past some size, whatever
  the rank <Cite id="schulman2025lora" />.
- **Dropout.** LoRA dropout zeroes parts of the adapter's input only, $\hvec = \Wmat_0 \xvec + s \Bmat \Amat
  \operatorname{dropout}(\xvec)$. QLoRA found a rate of 0.05 useful for 7B and 13B models and not for 33B and 65B
  models <Cite id="dettmers2023" />.
- **Merging into a quantised base.** An adapter trained on a quantised base learned to correct the quantised weights,
  not the originals. Merging it into 16-bit original weights, or re-quantising the merged weights, changes the model.
  The <NoteLink to="quantised-low-rank-adaptation">QLoRA</NoteLink> note derives when the change matters.
- **Rounding at merge.** Adding $\Delta\Wmat$ to a 16-bit $\Wmat_0$ rounds each sum. In bfloat16 an increment smaller
  than about $\abs{w} / 256$ of a weight $w$ can be lost entirely (see
  <NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">mixed-precision training</NoteLink>). Merge in 32 bits and evaluate the merged
  model, not only the unmerged one.
- **New tokens.** A token added to the vocabulary, such as a chat-template marker, gets a new embedding row. If the
  embedding table is frozen, that row stays at its random initial value. Train the new rows, or the whole embedding and
  output layers, alongside the adapters.
- **Wrong base.** An adapter is a correction to one specific set of weights. Applied to another checkpoint, even
  another release of the same model, it adds an update that was never trained for it.
