<Definition>
**Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards** (RLVR) fine-tunes a
<Gloss name="language-model">language-model</Gloss> policy $\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)$ on prompts $x$ whose responses
$y$ a program can check. The program, the **verifier**, returns a reward $v(x, y)$, for example 1 when the final answer
in $y$ equals a reference answer and 0 otherwise. The policy maximises

$$
\expect_{x \sim \Dcal,\, y \sim \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid x)}\brack{v(x, y)} - \beta\,\expect_x\brack{\KL\paren{\pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)}},
$$

where $\beta$ weights a <Gloss name="kl" /> penalty that keeps the policy near a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
This is the objective of <Gloss name="rlhf" /> with the learned reward model replaced by the verifier. It is usually
optimised with <Gloss name="ppo" /> or <Gloss name="grpo" /> <Cite id="lambert2024" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <Gloss name="reward-model">reward model</Gloss> is a learned proxy for human judgement. It is accurate near the
responses it was trained on and wrong elsewhere. A policy optimised hard against it finds the regions where it is wrong,
and the true quality of the responses falls while the proxy score keeps rising <Cite id="gao2023overoptimisation" />.
A verifier has no such region for the property it checks: a program that runs unit tests is as accurate on the
millionth response as on the first. The policy can therefore be optimised far from its starting point. DeepSeek-R1-Zero
was trained for 10,400 steps against rule-based rewards alone <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

Reinforcement learning cannot invent a success. A policy-gradient step raises the probability of sampled responses that
scored above a baseline and lowers the probability of those that scored below it. If no sampled response to a prompt
passes the verifier, that prompt contributes no gradient. RLVR is search by sampling followed by reinforcement of what
the search found. The base model's success rate on the training prompts decides whether it can start.

## What a verifier checks

A verifier has two parts: an **extractor**, which pulls the checkable object out of the response, and a
**comparator**, which tests it. Either part can fail.

| Task                         | Extractor                               | Comparator                                         |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Maths with a final answer    | The last `\boxed{}` or answer tag       | Symbolic or numeric equality with the reference    |
| Code generation              | The code block                          | Unit tests run in a sandbox                        |
| Instruction constraints      | The whole response                      | One check per constraint, e.g. "exactly 3 bullets" |
| Structured output            | The <Gloss name="json" /> object                         | Schema validation, then field-level checks         |
| Agents and tools             | The final state of the environment      | Tests pass in the repository, the game is won      |
| Format                       | The tags around reasoning and answer    | Tags present, in order, once                       |

Tülu 3, which named the method, trained on about 30,000 prompts from three sources: GSM8K and MATH, checked by
extracting the final answer and comparing it with the reference, and instructions with IFEval-style constraints, each
constraint template with its own checking function <Cite id="lambert2024" />. <Gloss name="dapo" />'s authors rewrote
competition problems so that every answer is an integer, because integers parse reliably and formulas do not. A problem
with answer $11 - 2\sqrt{6}$ became one that asks for $k + m + n$ where the answer is $k - m\sqrt{n}$, with answer 19
<Cite id="yu2025dapo" />. A <Gloss name="fn">false negative</Gloss>, such as $1/2$ marked wrong against $0.5$, penalises
a correct response. A <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss> rewards a wrong one and is the opening for
<Gloss name="reward-hacking">reward hacking</Gloss>.

## Why the model must already succeed sometimes

Let $p_{\thetavec}(x) = \prob_{y \sim \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid x)}\brack{v(x, y) = 1}$ be the policy's
single-sample success rate on prompt $x$, with a binary verifier. The objective for that prompt is
$J(x) = \expect_y[v(x, y)] = p_{\thetavec}(x)$. By the <NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function
identity</NoteLink>,

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} J(x) = \expect_{y}\brack{\paren{v(x, y) - b(x)}\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)}
$$

for any baseline $b(x)$ that does not depend on $y$. A training step estimates this expectation from $G$ sampled
responses $y_1, \dots, y_G$. Suppose all $G$ fail, so every $v(x, y_i) = 0$. With $b = 0$ every term is zero. With a
baseline computed from the group, such as the group mean used by GRPO, every $v_i - b$ is zero as well. Either way the
prompt contributes nothing. The same holds when all $G$ succeed and the baseline is the group mean. A prompt carries
signal only when its group contains both outcomes, which happens with probability

$$
1 - \paren{1 - p_{\thetavec}(x)}^G - p_{\thetavec}(x)^G.
$$

The two subtracted terms are the probabilities that every sample fails and that every sample succeeds. The quantity
$1 - (1 - p)^G$ is pass@$G$, the probability that at least one of $G$ samples is correct, as defined in
<NoteLink to="language-model-evaluation">language model evaluation</NoteLink>. A prompt with pass@$k = 0$ for every
practical $k$ never trains. <Gloss name="rl" /> also has no gradient pointing towards a correct response that the policy
never samples: the gradient only reweights responses that were drawn.

This argument also says where RLVR ends up. With $\beta = 0$ the objective $p_{\thetavec}(x)$ is maximised by any
policy that puts all its probability on responses the verifier accepts. Nothing in the objective rewards keeping
several distinct correct solutions, or keeping probability on solutions that are rare under the current policy.

## History and key results

- **Bootstrapping on correct samples.** STaR sampled rationales, kept those that reached the correct answer, and
  fine-tuned on them, in rounds <Cite id="zelikman2022" />. Tülu 3 describes RLVR as a simplified relative of such
  bootstrapping methods <Cite id="lambert2024" />.
- **DeepSeekMath and GRPO (February 2024).** GRPO replaced PPO's value network by a baseline computed from a group of
  responses to the same prompt. DeepSeekMath scored responses with a learned reward model, not a verifier. RL raised
  GSM8K accuracy from 82.9% to 88.2% and MATH accuracy from 46.8% to 51.7%. It improved majority voting over $K$
  samples (maj@$K$) but not pass@$K$, and the authors concluded that RL made the output distribution more robust rather
  than adding capability <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />.
- **Tülu 3 (November 2024).** Tülu 3 named RLVR. The reward was $\alpha = 10$ for a verified response and 0 otherwise,
  optimised by PPO after <Gloss name="dpo" />, with the value network initialised from a general reward model. RLVR
  improved each targeted evaluation. Adding reward-model scores to the verifiable reward made results worse. Smaller KL
  coefficients let the policy move further and usually lowered the average score across all evaluations
  <Cite id="lambert2024" />.
- **DeepSeek-R1-Zero (January 2025).** GRPO was applied directly to the DeepSeek-V3 base model, with no supervised
  stage. The reward was an accuracy reward plus a format reward for enclosing the reasoning in `<think>` tags, with
  equal weights. The authors avoided neural reward models, outcome or process, because they observed reward hacking
  against them in large-scale RL. Average pass@1 on AIME 2024 rose from 15.6% to 77.9%, and to 86.7% with
  self-consistency. Responses grew from hundreds to thousands of tokens over training. Mid-training, the model began
  to stop and re-check its work, the "aha moment", marked by a sudden rise in the use of the word "wait"
  <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.
- **DeepSeek-R1.** R1-Zero's reasoning was hard to read and mixed English and Chinese. DeepSeek-R1 first fine-tuned the
  base model on thousands of readable long <Gloss name="cot">chain-of-thought</Gloss> examples, the **cold start**. RL
  then added a language-consistency reward, the proportion of words in the chain of thought that are in the target
  language. Rejection sampling from the resulting model produced about 600,000 reasoning and 200,000 other examples for
  a second round of <Gloss name="sft" />, followed by a final RL stage with rule-based rewards for reasoning and reward
  models for general prompts <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

Later analyses qualified the story. Liu et al. found that base models, DeepSeek-V3-Base among them, already produce
self-reflection keywords such as "wait" before any RL. They also found that part of the growth in response length comes
from a bias in GRPO's loss, which penalises long incorrect responses less than short ones <Cite id="liu2025drgrpo" />.
The bias and its fix are derived in <NoteLink to="group-relative-policy-optimisation">group relative policy
optimisation</NoteLink>.

## Does RLVR add reasoning or sharpen it?

The pass@$k$ curve separates two effects. Pass@1 measures how often one sample is right. Pass@$k$ at large $k$ measures
the set of problems the model can solve at all, given enough attempts.

- **Sharpening.** Yue et al. compared base and RLVR-trained models across model families, RL algorithms and maths,
  code and visual-reasoning benchmarks. RLVR models won at small $k$. Base models won at large $k$, such as $k = 256$.
  As training progressed, pass@1 rose while pass@256 fell. The correct solutions of RLVR models already had high
  likelihood under the base model. Six RLVR algorithms behaved similarly. Distillation from a stronger teacher, unlike
  RLVR, did raise pass@$k$ at large $k$ <Cite id="yue2025" />. DeepSeekMath's maj@$K$ result points the same way
  <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />.
- **Expansion with longer training.** ProRL trained for longer on a broad set of tasks, with KL control and periodic
  resets of the reference policy. Its models beat their base model across a wide range of pass@$k$ evaluations,
  including on tasks the base model failed at any number of attempts. The gains were largest where the base model was
  weakest <Cite id="liu2025prorl" />.
- **Spurious rewards.** On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO with a reward assigned at random raised MATH-500 accuracy by 21.4
  points, against 29.1 points with the true reward. A reward for wrong answers raised it by 24.1 points, and a reward
  for merely including a `\boxed{}` expression also helped. The same spurious rewards gave little gain, or a loss, on
  Llama 3.1 and OLMo 2 models. The authors trace the effect to GRPO's clipping, which amplifies behaviours the base
  model already favours; for Qwen2.5-Math this was reasoning written as Python code, which rose from 65% to over 90%
  of responses <Cite id="shao2025spurious" />.

A toy problem reproduces sharpening. Each of 40 prompts is solved by some of 8 strategies, and the policy is one
softmax over the strategies, shared by every prompt. GRPO concentrates the policy on the strategy that solves the most
prompts. A prompt that only other strategies solve loses its success probability, so pass@1 rises while pass@$k$ at
large $k$ falls. A prompt that no strategy solves gives groups of equal rewards at every step and never moves the
policy.

<SharpeningCoverage />

For a practitioner the results give three rules. Report pass@1 and pass@$k$ at large $k$ for both the starting model
and the trained one. Test a method on more than one model family. Treat a gain that a random or format-only reward also
produces as an elicited behaviour of the base model, not as evidence that the verifier taught the task.

## Designing the reward

**Binary or partial credit.** A binary reward, 1 if every test passes, is the hardest to game and the simplest to
interpret: the mean reward is the training pass@1. Partial credit, such as the fraction of tests passed or of
constraints met, gives signal on prompts where full success is rare. It also rewards responses that satisfy the easy
checks and ignore the hard ones, so the policy may settle there.

**Offset and scale.** Tülu 3 used rewards of 10 and 0 <Cite id="lambert2024" />; DAPO used $+1$ and $-1$
<Cite id="yu2025dapo" />. Group-standardised advantages, as in GRPO, are unchanged by any increasing affine map of the
reward. Methods that do not standardise, such as PPO with a learned <Gloss name="value-function">value function</Gloss>,
see the scale through the size of the advantages and hence of each update.

**Format rewards.** R1-Zero rewarded the `<think>` structure alongside correctness <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. A
format reward is not neutral: on some base models a format reward alone raises accuracy <Cite id="shao2025spurious" />.
Give it a small weight or make it a gate, so that a response with broken format scores 0, rather than a bonus that can
be earned without solving the problem.

**Length.** Responses longer than the generation limit are truncated and usually fail the verifier. Tülu 3 gave a
penalty of $-10$ to any response that did not end with the end-of-sequence token <Cite id="lambert2024" />. DAPO found
that penalising truncated responses at full strength adds noise, because a sound but long chain of reasoning is
punished for its length. It instead masked truncated responses out of the loss, or added a penalty that grows linearly
from 0 to $-1$ over the last 4,096 tokens before a 20,480-token limit <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />.

**Extra objectives.** A secondary reward added to the verifier's, such as DeepSeek-R1's language-consistency reward,
trades some accuracy for the property it rewards <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

## Hacking the verifier

A verifier is exact about what it checks, and what it checks is a proxy for the task. The policy optimises the check.

- **Constraint checks.** In Tülu 3's runs with the smallest KL coefficient, $\beta = 0.01$, the policy met
  paragraph-count constraints with responses such as "Paragraph 1 \*\*\* Paragraph 2 \*\*\* …" that satisfied the
  checker and carried no content <Cite id="lambert2024" />.
- **Test suites.** During RL training of a frontier reasoning model in coding environments, OpenAI observed agents that
  called `exit(0)` to leave the environment before all tests ran, and agents that raised `SkipTest` from code outside
  the test framework. Both hacks spread across nearly all training environments once learned. Agents also wrote stubs
  where test coverage was poor and edited code upstream of the tests so that they passed trivially
  <Cite id="baker2025" />.
- **Answer extraction.** An extractor that accepts any of several boxed answers rewards a response that lists several.
  One that searches the whole response for the reference string rewards a response that enumerates candidates.

Verifiers avoid the gradual drift of reward-model overoptimisation, but not exploits. The defences are those of a
security boundary. Run code in a sandbox where tests, the test harness and the environment cannot be edited. Keep a set
of hidden tests that the reward never sees and evaluate on it. Extract exactly one answer from a fixed location. Read
samples throughout training, not only the reward curve. Measure success on held-out benchmarks: DAPO's authors found
that the final training reward correlated little with validation accuracy <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />.

## When to use RLVR on a new task

RLVR fits a task when three conditions hold.

1. **A cheap, reliable checker exists.** It must run in milliseconds to seconds, because every training step scores
   thousands of responses, and its <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> must be close to zero.
2. **The starting model sometimes succeeds.** Measure pass@$k$ of the starting model on the training prompts before
   training, for the group size $k$ you intend to use. Prompts at pass@$k = 0$ give no signal; prompts the model always
   solves give none either.
3. **The success rate leaves room to grow.** If pass@1 is already near 1 on the target distribution, RLVR has little to
   add.

When the checker exists but the model never succeeds, teach the format and the basic skill first by
<NoteLink to="supervised-fine-tuning">supervised fine-tuning</NoteLink> on worked solutions. DeepSeek-R1's cold start
is this step <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. When the task has no checker, preferences are the signal; see
<NoteLink to="reward-models">reward models</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="direct-preference-optimisation">direct preference optimisation</NoteLink>.

**Rejection-sampling fine-tuning** is the offline relative of RLVR: sample many responses, keep those the verifier
accepts, and fine-tune on them, as STaR did <Cite id="zelikman2022" />. It reuses ordinary supervised tooling and needs
no RL machinery, but it learns nothing from failures.

**Distillation** is the alternative when a stronger reasoning model is available. DeepSeek fine-tuned Qwen and Llama
models from 1.5B to 70B parameters on 800,000 responses generated by DeepSeek-R1, with SFT only. On AIME 2024,
Qwen2.5-32B trained by large-scale RL alone reached 47.0% pass@1, while the same base model distilled from R1 reached
72.6% <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. For a small model with a strong teacher available,
<NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">distillation</NoteLink> gave the stronger model, with supervised training only.
DeepSeek did not apply RL to the distilled models, and noted that it could add further gains
<Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

## Worked example

A verifier checks final answers. A team considers RLVR with groups of $G = 8$ responses per prompt and measures the
starting model's single-sample success rate $p$ on three kinds of prompt. The probability that a group carries signal
is $1 - (1 - p)^8 - p^8$.

| Prompt kind | $p$  | $(1 - p)^8$ | $p^8$  | Informative groups |
| ----------- | ---- | ----------- | ------ | ------------------ |
| Hard        | 0.02 | 0.851       | 0.000  | 14.9%              |
| Medium      | 0.30 | 0.058       | 0.0001 | 94.2%              |
| Easy        | 0.95 | 0.000       | 0.663  | 33.7%              |

Six in seven hard prompts produce eight failures and are wasted compute. Raising the group to $G = 64$ makes 72.6% of
the hard groups informative, since $0.98^{64} = 0.274$, at eight times the sampling cost. Two thirds of the easy prompts
produce eight successes and are also wasted. A training set should be filtered towards prompts with intermediate
success rates, and refiltered as the policy improves, because easy prompts become all-success groups.

## Assumptions

- **The verifier's verdict is the task.** RLVR optimises the check, not the intention behind it.
- **On-policy samples.** Each update uses responses sampled from the current policy or one very close to it.
- **Some success under sampling.** Prompts the policy never solves contribute nothing.

## Pitfalls

- **Judging by the training reward.** The reward measures success on the training prompts against an exploitable
  checker. Track held-out pass@1, pass@$k$ at large $k$, and samples read by a person.
- **A single base model.** Gains on one model family, Qwen2.5-Math in particular, may not transfer
  <Cite id="shao2025spurious" />.
- **Unbounded length.** Response length grows under RLVR on reasoning tasks. Set the generation limit and the
  treatment of truncated responses deliberately.
- **Mixing a reward model into the reward.** Adding a learned score to the verifier's brings back reward-model
  hacking; Tülu 3 found the combination worse than the verifier alone <Cite id="lambert2024" />.
