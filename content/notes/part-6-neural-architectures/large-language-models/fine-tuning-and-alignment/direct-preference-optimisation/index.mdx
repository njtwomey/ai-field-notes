<Definition>
**Direct preference optimisation** (DPO) fine-tunes a <Gloss name="language-model">language-model</Gloss> policy
$\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)$ on preference pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$, in which response $y_w$ was preferred to response $y_l$
for prompt $x$. It minimises

$$
L_{\text{DPO}}(\thetavec) = -\expect_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\brack{\log\sigma\paren{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}}},
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a frozen reference policy (usually the supervised
fine-tuned model) and $\beta > 0$ sets how far $\pi_{\thetavec}$ may move from it. DPO optimises the same
KL-regularised objective as reinforcement learning from human feedback, without training a separate reward model and
without sampling from the policy during training <Cite id="rafailov2023" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

DPO turns preference learning into classification on a fixed dataset. For each pair it asks one question: relative to
the reference model, has the policy raised the probability of the chosen response more than that of the rejected one?
The quantity

$$
\hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}
$$

plays the part of a reward and is called the **implicit reward**. The DPO loss is the
<Gloss name="bradley-terry">Bradley–Terry</Gloss> loss of a <NoteLink to="reward-models">reward model</NoteLink>,
applied to $\hat r_{\thetavec}$. The language model is its own reward model.

A training step costs about as much as a supervised step on two sequences, plus forward passes of the frozen reference
on the same two sequences. The reference log-probabilities can be computed once before training, after which the
reference model need not be kept in memory <Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />. Nothing is generated during training. This is why
DPO is usually the first preference method tried after <NoteLink to="supervised-fine-tuning">supervised
fine-tuning</NoteLink>.

## Derivation

**The objective.** <Gloss name="rlhf" /> seeks, for each prompt $x$, a distribution $\pi$ over responses that earns a
high reward $r(x, y)$ without moving far from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:

$$
\max_{\pi}\ \expect_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\brack{r(x, y)} - \beta\,\KL\paren{\pi(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)}.
$$

**The optimal policy.** Define the normaliser $Z(x) = \sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp(r(x, y)/\beta)$ and the
distribution $\pi^*(y \mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp(r(x, y)/\beta)/Z(x)$. Taking logarithms of the
definition gives $r(x, y) = \beta\log(\pi^*(y \mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)) + \beta\log Z(x)$. Substituting
this into the objective, for any $\pi$,

$$
\expect_{\pi}\brack{r - \beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}} = \expect_{\pi}\brack{\beta\log\frac{\pi^*}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} + \beta\log Z(x) - \beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}} = \beta\log Z(x) - \beta\,\KL\paren{\pi \,\|\, \pi^*}.
$$

$Z(x)$ does not depend on $\pi$, and the <Gloss name="kl" /> divergence is non-negative and zero only at $\pi = \pi^*$.
So $\pi^*$ is the unique maximiser: the optimal policy reweights the reference by $\exp(r/\beta)$.

**From policy to reward.** The same identity read backwards says that any policy $\pi$ with the support of
$\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is optimal for the reward $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z(x)$. Every reward
defines one optimal policy, and every policy defines its reward up to a function of $x$ alone.

**Bradley–Terry cancels the normaliser.** Under the Bradley–Terry model, the probability that $y_w$ is preferred is
$\sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$. Writing both rewards through the policy, the term $\beta\log Z(x)$ appears in each and
cancels in the difference:

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\paren{\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}}.
$$

$Z(x)$ sums over every possible response and cannot be computed. Its cancellation is what makes DPO tractable.
Parameterising $\pi$ as $\pi_{\thetavec}$ and maximising the likelihood of the observed preferences gives
$L_{\text{DPO}}$. Fitting the policy is fitting a reward model whose optimal policy is the policy itself
<Cite id="rafailov2023" />. A fuller treatment of the RLHF side is in
<NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback">reinforcement learning from human feedback</NoteLink>.

**Sequences.** For a response of $T$ tokens, $\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^T\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_t \mid x,
y_{<t})$, the same sum as in the supervised loss. The implicit reward is therefore a sum over tokens of per-token
log-ratios, and its magnitude grows with response length.

## The gradient

Write the margin $z = \hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y_w) - \hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y_l)$, so that the loss of one pair is
$-\log\sigma(z)$. Since $\tfrac{d}{dz}\brack{-\log\sigma(z)} = -\sigma(-z)$ and
$\nabla_{\thetavec}z = \beta\paren{\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)}$,

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}L_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\,\expect\brack{\sigma\paren{\hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y_l) - \hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y_w)}\paren{\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)}}.
$$

A gradient step raises the log-probability of $y_w$ and lowers that of $y_l$. Each pair is weighted by
$\sigma(-z)$, the probability that the implicit reward model assigns to the wrong order. A misordered pair has weight
near 1. A pair already ordered by a wide margin has weight near 0 and stops pulling. Rafailov et al. found that a
version without this weight, which pushes every pair equally, can make the language model degenerate
<Cite id="rafailov2023" />.

The gradient fixes only the difference between the two log-probabilities. Nothing in it requires
$\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)$ to rise. This gap is the source of the failure described under
[likelihood displacement](#likelihood-displacement).

## Choosing the hyperparameters

**$\beta$.** The loss depends on the log-ratios only through $\beta$ times their difference. To reach a given margin
$z$, the policy must move the difference of log-ratios by $z/\beta$. A small $\beta$ lets the policy move far from the
reference; a large $\beta$ holds it close. The usual range is 0.01 to 0.5.
<NoteLink to="adapting-pretrained-language-models">TRL</NoteLink>'s default is 0.1 <Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />, the value
used for Zephyr-7B <Cite id="tunstall2023" />.

**Learning rate.** DPO uses learning rates one to two orders of magnitude below
supervised fine-tuning. Zephyr-7B used a peak learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ for <Gloss name="sft" /> and
$5 \times 10^{-7}$ for DPO, 40 times smaller <Cite id="tunstall2023" />. Meng et al. searched DPO learning rates in
$\{3, 5, 6, 10\} \times 10^{-7}$ and $\beta$ in $\{0.01, 0.05, 0.1\}$, and found that the best learning rate differed
between methods and strongly affected benchmark scores <Cite id="meng2024simpo" />. TRL's default is $10^{-6}$ for full
fine-tuning. Training a <Gloss name="lora" /> adapter tolerates larger rates, about $10^{-5}$
<Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />.

**Epochs.** Training accuracy on the preference pairs often reaches 1 within an epoch. Zephyr-7B overfitted the training
pairs in this sense after one epoch, yet its benchmark scores still improved with three epochs of DPO. When the SFT
model had been trained for more than one epoch, longer DPO training made the model worse <Cite id="tunstall2023" />.
Rafailov et al. found that direct alignment methods overoptimise as RLHF does: past some KL divergence from the
reference, quality falls, often before one epoch is complete <Cite id="rafailov2024overoptimisation" />. Select
checkpoints by evaluating generations, not by the training loss.

**The reference model.** The derivation assumes $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the policy whose KL divergence is penalised, and
the preference data are best drawn from it. When the pairs come from other models, Rafailov et al. first fine-tuned the
reference on the preferred responses, by maximum likelihood, to reduce the mismatch <Cite id="rafailov2023" />.

## Reading the training metrics

TRL's `DPOTrainer` logs the following per interval, averaged over pairs <Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />:

| Metric | Meaning | At the start |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `loss` | the DPO loss | $\log 2 = 0.693$ |
| `rewards/chosen` | mean $\hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y_w)$ | 0 |
| `rewards/rejected` | mean $\hat r_{\thetavec}(x, y_l)$ | 0 |
| `rewards/margins` | mean of chosen minus rejected | 0 |
| `rewards/accuracies` | fraction of pairs with chosen above rejected | 0, as no margin is yet positive |
| `logps/chosen`, `logps/rejected` | log-probability of the responses under the policy | the reference's values |

At the start the policy equals the reference, so every implicit reward is 0 and the loss is
$-\log\sigma(0) = \log 2$ whatever $\beta$ is. The loss alone says little: it depends only on the margins. The
patterns below combine the metrics.

- **Healthy.** `rewards/margins` grows steadily and evaluation `rewards/accuracies` rises above one half and levels off.
  `rewards/rejected` falls, and `rewards/chosen` stays near 0 or rises. Ethayarajh et al. describe the same target for
  their related method: ideally only the rejected rewards fall <Cite id="ethayarajh2024" />.
- **Both falling.** `rewards/chosen` and `rewards/rejected` both fall, with the rejected falling faster, so the margin
  still grows. This is the most common pattern in practice and is the subject of the next section. A learning rate that
  is too high makes it worse <Cite id="ethayarajh2024" />.
- **Overfitting.** Training accuracy approaches 1 and training margins keep growing while evaluation accuracy and
  margins stall. More epochs will not help the held-out pairs.
- **Runaway.** Margins grow very large, `logps/chosen` collapses, and sampled responses become long, repetitive or
  malformed. $\beta$ is too small or the learning rate too high.
- **Nothing learned.** Margins stay near 0. Check that chosen and rejected are not swapped and that the learning rate is
  not far too small.

Offline DPO does not log the KL divergence from the reference, because it samples nothing. Estimate it on held-out
prompts by sampling $y \sim \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid x)$ and averaging
$\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)$.

## Likelihood displacement

<Gloss name="likelihood-displacement" /> is the fall of $\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)$ during DPO training. Pal et
al. showed that the DPO loss can decrease while the probability of the preferred response falls, provided the
probability of the rejected response falls faster. In practice it happened most when the two responses of a pair
differed in few tokens <Cite id="pal2024smaug" />. Razin et al. showed that the probability mass released can move to
responses with the opposite meaning. Training a model to prefer "No" over "Never" sharply raised the probability of
"Yes". Training Llama-3-8B-Instruct to prefer refusals over harmful completions lowered its refusal rate on unsafe
prompts from 74.4% to 33.4%. The displacement was driven by pairs whose chosen and rejected responses have similar
hidden representations, and removing the pairs with the most similar representations mitigated it
<Cite id="razin2025" />.

Remedies act on the missing pressure on $y_w$:

- **Add the supervised loss on the chosen response.** Pang et al. added the negative log-likelihood of $y_w$ to the
  DPO loss and found the term crucial for reasoning tasks <Cite id="pang2024irpo" />. In TRL, pass
  `loss_type=["sigmoid", "sft"]` with weights in `loss_weights` <Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />.
- **Penalise any fall.** DPO-Positive subtracts $\lambda\max\paren{0, \log(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)/\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w
  \mid x))}$ from the margin inside the sigmoid, which is active only when the policy has lowered the chosen response
  below the reference <Cite id="pal2024smaug" />.
- **Curate pairs.** Remove pairs whose responses are near-duplicates, and lower the learning rate.

## Variants

Each variant changes one assumption of DPO. All are available in TRL <Cite id="vonwerra2020" />.

| Method | Data | Reference model | Main change | TRL |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DPO | pairs | yes | the baseline | `DPOTrainer` |
| <Gloss name="ipo" /> | pairs | yes | squared loss to a finite target margin | `DPOTrainer`, `loss_type="ipo"` |
| <Gloss name="kto" /> | single responses labelled good or bad | yes | prospect-theory value of each response | `KTOTrainer` |
| <Gloss name="orpo" /> | pairs | no | odds-ratio term added to the SFT loss | `trl.experimental.orpo` |
| <Gloss name="simpo" /> | pairs | no | mean per-token log-probability, target margin | `trl.experimental.cpo`, `loss_type="simpo"` |
| Online DPO | prompts and a judge | yes | pairs sampled from the current policy | `trl.experimental.online_dpo` |

### Identity preference optimisation

**Why.** Bradley–Terry turns a preference probability $p$ into a reward difference $\log(p/(1 - p))$, which is
infinite when $p = 1$. A pair preferred every time it was labelled, which is the usual case when each pair has one
label, therefore drives the optimal policy to $\pi(y_l \mid x) = 0$ whatever $\beta$ is. The KL penalty stops
regularising exactly when the data are most deterministic, and a small sample makes an empirical $p = 1$ likely even
when the true $p$ is 0.8 <Cite id="azar2024" />.

**Objective.** With
$h_{\thetavec} = \log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)}$,
the difference of log-ratios, IPO minimises

$$
L_{\text{IPO}}(\thetavec) = \expect\brack{\paren{h_{\thetavec} - \frac{1}{2\tau}}^2}.
$$

The loss regresses the gap onto the finite target $1/(2\tau)$, so a perfectly separated pair stops being pushed once
its gap reaches the target <Cite id="azar2024" />. In TRL, `beta` plays the role of $\tau$ <Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />.

### Kahneman–Tversky optimisation

**Why.** Pairs are expensive. Thumbs-up and thumbs-down labels on single responses are cheap and plentiful. Prospect
theory describes how people value an outcome relative to a reference point, with losses weighing more than gains.
Ethayarajh et al. built a loss on that value function <Cite id="ethayarajh2024" />.

**Objective.** Each example is a prompt, a response and a label. With $r_{\thetavec}(x, y) = \log(\pi_{\thetavec}(y
\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x))$ and a reference point $z_0$, an estimate of
$\KL(\pi_{\thetavec} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ from mismatched prompt and response pairs in the batch that is not
differentiated,

$$
v(x, y) = \begin{cases}\lambda_D\,\sigma\paren{\beta\,(r_{\thetavec}(x, y) - z_0)} & y \text{ desirable,} \\ \lambda_U\,\sigma\paren{\beta\,(z_0 - r_{\thetavec}(x, y))} & y \text{ undesirable,}\end{cases}
\qquad L_{\text{KTO}} = \expect\brack{\lambda_y - v(x, y)},
$$

where $\lambda_y$ is $\lambda_D$ or $\lambda_U$ according to the label <Cite id="ethayarajh2024" />. A desirable
response gains value when its log-ratio exceeds the policy's average divergence, and an undesirable one when its
log-ratio falls below it.

**Practice.** KTO matched or exceeded pairwise methods such as DPO at scales from 1 to 30 billion parameters. It matched
DPO with up to 90% fewer desirable examples <Cite id="ethayarajh2024" />. With $n_D$ desirable and $n_U$ undesirable
examples, set the weights so that $\lambda_D n_D/(\lambda_U n_U)$ lies between 1 and 4/3. The KL estimate needs at least
4 examples per device step, and for $\beta = 0.1$ the learning rate should not exceed $10^{-6}$
<Cite id="hf2026trlkto" />.

### Odds ratio preference optimisation

**Why.** Supervised fine-tuning on chosen responses also raises the probability of similar rejected responses, because
it has no term that pushes them down. Hong et al. observed rejected responses reaching higher log-probabilities than
chosen ones during SFT. ORPO adds a small penalty for the rejected style to the SFT loss, in one stage and without a
reference model <Cite id="hong2024orpo" />.

**Objective.** Let $P_{\thetavec}(y \mid x) = \exp\paren{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_t \mid x,
y_{<t})}$ be the geometric-mean token probability and
$\operatorname{odds}_{\thetavec}(y \mid x) = P_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)/(1 - P_{\thetavec}(y \mid x))$. Then

$$
L_{\text{ORPO}} = \expect\brack{L_{\text{SFT}}(x, y_w) - \lambda\log\sigma\paren{\log\frac{\operatorname{odds}_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)}{\operatorname{odds}_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)}}},
$$

with $L_{\text{SFT}}$ the negative mean log-likelihood of $y_w$ and $\lambda$ between 0.1 and 0.25 in the paper's
experiments <Cite id="hong2024orpo" />. In TRL's `ORPOTrainer`, the argument `beta` is $\lambda$
<Cite id="vonwerra2020" />.

### Simple preference optimisation

**Why.** DPO's implicit reward is a sum of log-ratios over tokens, while generation favours responses with a high
average log-probability per token. A response can have the higher DPO reward and the lower average likelihood, and the
summed reward can be raised by length. SimPO uses the average log-probability itself as the reward. It needs no
reference model, which saves memory and compute <Cite id="meng2024simpo" />.

**Objective.**

$$
L_{\text{SimPO}} = -\expect\brack{\log\sigma\paren{\frac{\beta}{\abs{y_w}}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x) - \frac{\beta}{\abs{y_l}}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x) - \gamma}},
$$

where $\abs{y}$ is the number of tokens and $\gamma > 0$ is a target reward margin. The authors recommend $\beta$
between 2.0 and 2.5 and $\gamma$ between 0.5 and 1.5. Removing the length normalisation biased the model towards long,
lower-quality responses. SimPO outperformed DPO by up to 6.4 points on AlpacaEval 2 and 7.5 points on Arena-Hard.
Without a KL term, it relies on a small learning rate and diverse preference data to stay close to the starting model
<Cite id="meng2024simpo" />.

### Online and iterative DPO

**Why.** Offline pairs are usually generated by other models, and the policy drifts from them as it trains. The data
are off-policy from the first step <Cite id="guo2024oaif" />.

**Method.** Sample two or more responses per prompt from the current policy, label them with a reward model or a
language-model judge, and take a DPO step on the fresh pairs. Guo et al. sampled two responses from the current model at
every iteration and asked a language model which was better. In human evaluation this beat both offline direct alignment
and RLHF <Cite id="guo2024oaif" />. Xiong et al. analysed the KL-regularised problem and derived an iterative DPO that
alternates sampling and DPO training, with finite-sample guarantees <Cite id="xiong2024iterative" />. Pang et al. ran
iterations of DPO with the added negative log-likelihood term on pairs of correct and incorrect chains of thought, and
raised Llama-2-70B-Chat's GSM8K accuracy from 55.6% to 81.6% <Cite id="pang2024irpo" />. TRL's `OnlineDPOTrainer` takes
prompts and one or more reward functions or reward models <Cite id="vonwerra2020" />.

## Evidence: DPO compared with PPO

- Rafailov et al. found DPO better than <Gloss name="ppo" />-based RLHF at controlling sentiment, and as good or better
  on summarisation and single-turn dialogue <Cite id="rafailov2023" />.
- Xu et al. argued that DPO can find solutions that exploit responses outside the preference data, and that its results
  depend on the distribution shift between the model's outputs and the preference data. With advantage normalisation,
  large batches and an exponential moving average of the reference model, PPO surpassed DPO on every testbed they ran,
  dialogue and competitive programming included <Cite id="xu2024dpoppo" />.
- Ivison et al. varied data, algorithm, reward model and prompts. Better preference data gave the largest gains, up to
  8% on instruction following and truthfulness. PPO outperformed DPO by up to 2.5% in maths and 1.2% in general domains
  <Cite id="ivison2024" />.
- Tajwar et al. found that methods that sample from the current policy, or that push down the likelihood of specific
  responses as DPO does, outperform offline maximum-likelihood objectives <Cite id="tajwar2024" />.

DPO is the cheaper and more stable first step. Pairs sampled from the model being trained close much of the gap. When a
reward can be computed, such as the correctness of a maths answer, on-policy <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement
learning</Gloss> such as <NoteLink to="group-relative-policy-optimisation">group relative policy optimisation</NoteLink>
optimises it directly.

## Training one in practice

```python
from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import DPOConfig, DPOTrainer

# Each row: {"prompt": ..., "chosen": ..., "rejected": ...}, as text or as chat messages.
dataset = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized")

trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model="my-org/my-sft-model",  # the SFT checkpoint; it also becomes the frozen reference
    args=DPOConfig(output_dir="dpo", beta=0.1, learning_rate=5e-7, num_train_epochs=1),
    train_dataset=dataset["train"],
    eval_dataset=dataset["test"],
)
trainer.train()
```

With `ref_model` left unset, the trainer uses the initial state of `model` as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Setting
`precompute_ref_log_probs=True` computes the reference log-probabilities once, so the reference model does not stay in
memory <Cite id="hf2026trldpo" />.

## Worked example

Take $\beta = 0.1$.

**A healthy pair.** The policy has raised $\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)$ by 3 nats relative to the reference and
lowered $\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)$ by 7 nats. The implicit rewards are $0.1 \times 3 = 0.3$ and
$0.1 \times (-7) = -0.7$, the margin is 1.0, the loss is $-\log\sigma(1) = 0.313$ and the gradient weight is
$\sigma(-1) = 0.269$.

**A displaced pair.** The policy has lowered $\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)$ by 2 nats and
$\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)$ by 12 nats. The implicit rewards are $-0.2$ and $-1.2$, the margin is again 1.0 and
the loss is again 0.313. The loss cannot tell the two pairs apart. Only `logps/chosen` and `rewards/chosen` show that
the second policy is less likely than the reference to produce the preferred response.

**Saturation.** At margins of 0.2, 1, 2 and 4 the loss is 0.598, 0.313, 0.127 and 0.018 and the gradient weight is
0.450, 0.269, 0.119 and 0.018. With $\beta = 0.1$, a margin of 4 needs a 40-nat difference of log-ratios, after which
the pair has almost no effect.

**Length.** A 400-token response whose per-token log-ratio averages 0.01 nats has a sequence log-ratio of 4 nats and
an implicit reward of 0.4. A 40-token response needs a per-token log-ratio of 0.1 nats, ten times larger, to earn the
same reward.

**IPO target.** With $\tau = 0.1$, IPO drives the difference of log-ratios of every pair towards $1/(2 \times 0.1) = 5$
nats and no further.

## Assumptions

- **Bradley–Terry preferences.** The derivation assumes preferences come from one scalar reward through the logistic
  link. Deterministic preferences break the regularisation, which IPO repairs.
- **A shared reference.** The KL penalty is measured against $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and the data should come from it or
  near it. Off-policy pairs make DPO an approximation to the objective it was derived from.
- **Full support.** Every response the policy could produce must have non-zero probability under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, or
  the log-ratio is undefined.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating rising margins as progress.** Margins can grow while the chosen responses become less likely and while
  generations get worse. Watch `logps/chosen` and sample outputs.
- **Copying SFT <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rates</Gloss>.** Rates suited to supervised fine-tuning are far too
  large for DPO and quickly collapse the policy.
- **Comparing $\beta$ across losses.** $\beta$ means different things in DPO, IPO, ORPO and SimPO, and in TRL the same
  argument name carries each meaning. Retune when switching.
- **Length exploitation.** The summed implicit reward rewards length. Compare models with length-controlled metrics and
  consider a length-normalised loss.
- **Stale reference.** Running DPO from a model other than the one the reference represents breaks the derivation's
  assumptions and inflates the initial KL divergence.
