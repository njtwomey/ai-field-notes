<Definition>
**Group relative policy optimisation** (GRPO) is a policy-gradient method for <Gloss name="language-model">language
models</Gloss>. For each prompt $x$ it samples a group of $G$ responses $y_1, \dots, y_G$ from the current policy
$\pi_{\text{old}}$ and scores them with rewards $r_1, \dots, r_G$. Every token of response $i$ receives the same
advantage, the reward standardised within the group, $\hat A_i = (r_i - \bar r)/s_r$, where $\bar r$ and $s_r$ are the
group's mean and standard deviation. The parameters $\thetavec$ maximise

$$
J(\thetavec) = \expect\brack{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{\abs{y_i}}\sum_{t=1}^{\abs{y_i}}\paren{\min\paren{\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}\paren{\rho_{i,t}, 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon}\hat A_i} - \beta\,\hat k_{i,t}}},
$$

where $\rho_{i,t} = \pi_{\thetavec}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$ is the
probability ratio of token $t$, $\abs{y_i}$ is the length of response $i$ in tokens, and $\hat k_{i,t}$ estimates the
<Gloss name="kl" /> from a frozen reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<Gloss name="ppo" />, as used in <Gloss name="rlhf" />, needs four networks of similar size: the policy, a frozen
reference, a reward model and a value network. The value network is the critic of an
<NoteLink to="actor-critic">actor-critic</NoteLink> method: it predicts, at every token, the reward the response will
finally receive, and <Gloss name="gae" /> turns those predictions into per-token advantages. For a language model the
critic is costly and hard to train. It is usually as large as the policy, so it doubles the trained weights and
optimiser state. The reward arrives only at the last token, so the critic must predict a final verdict from a partial
response <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />. In long reasoning, later text often revises or contradicts earlier text, which
makes that prediction harder still <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

With a programmatic reward, as in <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards">reinforcement learning
with verifiable rewards</NoteLink>, the reward model is not needed either. GRPO removes the critic. Only the policy is
trained, and the reference is kept only if the KL term is used.

## Mechanism

### From the policy gradient to a group baseline

Treat a whole response as one action. For one prompt the objective is
$J(x) = \expect_{y \sim \pi_{\thetavec}}[r(x, y)]$, and the <NoteLink to="policy-gradient-theorem">policy
gradient</NoteLink> with a baseline $b$ is

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} J(x) = \expect_{y}\brack{\paren{r(x, y) - b}\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)}.
$$

The baseline leaves the gradient unbiased if it does not depend on $y$, because
$\expect_y[\nabla\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)] = \nabla\sum_y\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x) = \nabla 1 = 0$. The best simple
choice is close to the expected reward $V(x) = \expect_y[r(x, y)]$, which a critic would estimate. With $G$ samples of
the same prompt, the other samples estimate it directly. The **leave-one-out** baseline for response $i$ is
$b_i = \frac{1}{G - 1}\sum_{j \ne i} r_j$. The responses are drawn independently, so $b_i$ does not depend on $y_i$ and
the estimate stays unbiased. This is REINFORCE leave-one-out (<Gloss name="rloo" />) <Cite id="kool2019" />
<Cite id="ahmadian2024" />.

GRPO subtracts the group mean $\bar r = \frac1G\sum_j r_j$ instead, which includes $r_i$ itself. Writing
$\bar r = \frac{1}{G}\paren{r_i + (G - 1)b_i}$ gives

$$
r_i - \bar r = \frac{G - 1}{G}\paren{r_i - b_i}.
$$

The mean-centred advantage is the leave-one-out advantage scaled by $(G - 1)/G$. It is therefore an unbiased gradient
estimate up to that constant factor, which the learning rate absorbs.

GRPO then divides by the group's standard deviation $s_r$. With a binary reward and a fraction $\hat p$ of correct
responses in the group, $\bar r = \hat p$ and $s_r = \sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}$, so

$$
\hat A_{\text{correct}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \hat p}{\hat p}}, \qquad \hat A_{\text{wrong}} = -\sqrt{\frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p}}.
$$

A rare success gets a large positive advantage. The division multiplies the whole group's contribution by $1/s_r$, which
depends on the sampled rewards, so the estimate is no longer an unbiased estimate of $\nabla J$. Implementations differ
in whether $s_r$ divides by $G$ or $G - 1$, and add a small constant to avoid division by zero.

**Every token gets the same advantage.** The log-probability of a response is the sum over its tokens,
$\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_i \mid x) = \sum_t\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$, so
$\hat A_i\nabla\log\pi_{\thetavec}(y_i \mid x)$ is a sum of per-token terms that share $\hat A_i$. A critic would
give each token its own advantage. GRPO does no credit assignment within a response. A variant with a process reward
model, which scores each reasoning step, gives each token the sum of the standardised step rewards from its step onwards
<Cite id="shao2024grpo" />.

### The clipped objective

Sampling $G$ long responses per prompt is the expensive part of a step. The clipped ratio of
<NoteLink to="trust-region-and-proximal-policy-optimisation">PPO</NoteLink> lets one batch of samples support several
gradient steps: once a token's probability has moved by a factor $1 \pm \varepsilon$ in the direction its advantage
favours, the clip stops that token from pushing further. On the first gradient step after sampling,
$\pi_{\thetavec} = \pi_{\text{old}}$, every $\rho_{i,t} = 1$, the clip is inactive, and the gradient of $J$ is the
policy gradient above, with each response's terms divided by $\abs{y_i}$. In DeepSeek-R1's first <Gloss name="rl" />
stage, each rollout of 8,192 responses was split into 16 minibatches for a single pass, with $\varepsilon = 10$, so the
clip almost never acted <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

### The KL term and the k3 estimator

GRPO puts the KL penalty in the loss. PPO-based RLHF instead subtracts a per-token KL penalty from the reward, which
then enters the advantages. DeepSeek notes that the reward-side penalty accumulates over the response and so also
penalises length <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. The exact per-token KL divergence needs the full next-token distributions
of both models. GRPO estimates it from the sampled token alone, with
$q = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i,t} \mid \cdot)/\pi_{\thetavec}(y_{i,t} \mid \cdot)$:

$$
\hat k_{i,t} = q - \log q - 1,
$$

the estimator called $k_3$ by Schulman <Cite id="schulman2020kl" /> <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />.

**It is unbiased.** For a token $a$ sampled from $\pi_{\thetavec}$,
$\expect[q] = \sum_a\pi_{\thetavec}(a)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(a)/\pi_{\thetavec}(a) = \sum_a\pi_{\text{ref}}(a) = 1$, and
$\expect[-\log q] = \sum_a\pi_{\thetavec}(a)\log(\pi_{\thetavec}(a)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(a)) = \KL(\pi_{\thetavec} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$.
So $\expect[\hat k] = 1 + \KL - 1 = \KL(\pi_{\thetavec} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$. The first sum needs
$\pi_{\thetavec}(a) > 0$ wherever $\pi_{\text{ref}}(a) > 0$, which a softmax policy satisfies.

**It is non-negative.** The tangent line of $\log$ at 1 lies above it, $\log q \le q - 1$, so $q - 1 - \log q \ge 0$,
with equality only at $q = 1$. The simpler unbiased estimate $-\log q$ is negative whenever the sampled token is more
probable under the reference, which makes it noisy. $k_3$ adds $q - 1$, a term with mean zero that is negatively
correlated with $-\log q$. In Schulman's Gaussian example with a true KL divergence of 0.005, the standard deviation of
$-\log q$ was 20 times the true value and that of $k_3$ was 1.42 times <Cite id="schulman2020kl" />.

**Its gradient is not the KL gradient.** GRPO differentiates $\hat k$ as a loss. For one token,
$\nabla_{\thetavec}\hat k = (1 - q)\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(a)$, and its expectation is

$$
\sum_a\pi_{\thetavec}(a)\paren{1 - \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a)}{\pi_{\thetavec}(a)}}\nabla\log\pi_{\thetavec}(a) = -\sum_a\pi_{\text{ref}}(a)\nabla\log\pi_{\thetavec}(a) = \nabla_{\thetavec}\KL(\pi_{\text{ref}} \,\|\, \pi_{\thetavec}).
$$

An unbiased estimate of $\KL(\pi_{\thetavec} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ has the gradient, in expectation, of the
divergence in the other direction. Over a sequence the per-token losses also ignore how the policy's change at one token
alters the distribution of later tokens. Tang and Munos show that differentiating KL estimates as losses generally does
not give the KL gradient <Cite id="tang2025kl" />. Both directions are zero only at
$\pi_{\thetavec} = \pi_{\text{ref}}$, so the term still pulls towards the reference. Differentiating
$k_2 = \frac12(\log q)^2$ instead gives $\log(\pi_{\thetavec}(a)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(a))\nabla\log\pi_{\thetavec}(a)$,
whose expectation is the gradient of $\KL(\pi_{\thetavec} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$; REINFORCE++ uses it for this reason
<Cite id="hu2025reinforcepp" />.

**Often switched off.** In reasoning training the policy is meant to move far from its starting point. DAPO removes the
KL term for that reason <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />, Open-Reasoner-Zero trains without it <Cite id="hu2025orz" />, and
TRL's GRPO trainer defaults to $\beta = 0$, which also means the reference model is never loaded
<Cite id="hf2026trlgrpo" />. DeepSeek-R1-Zero used $\beta = 0.001$ and replaced the reference with the current policy
every 400 steps <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. DeepSeekMath used $\beta = 0.04$ <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />.

## Biases and fixes

**Dr. GRPO.** Liu et al. identify two biases in GRPO's objective <Cite id="liu2025drgrpo" />.

- **Length.** Dividing response $i$'s terms by $\abs{y_i}$ gives each token of a short response more weight than each
  token of a long one. A correct short response is reinforced more per token than a correct long one, which favours
  brevity among correct answers. A wrong long response is penalised less per token than a wrong short one, which
  favours length among wrong answers. Response length keeps growing even after the reward stops improving.
- **Difficulty.** Dividing by $s_r$ gives more weight to prompts whose groups are nearly all correct or nearly all
  wrong, because their $s_r$ is small.

<Gloss name="dr-grpo" /> ("GRPO done right") removes both divisions: the advantage is $r_i - \bar r$ and the token sum
is divided by a constant, such as the generation limit, instead of $\abs{y_i}$. This recovers the
policy gradient with a group-mean baseline. The authors found the same length normalisation in open-source PPO
implementations for language models, which average the loss over each response's tokens. With Dr. GRPO the length of
incorrect responses fell while accuracy stayed similar <Cite id="liu2025drgrpo" />.

**DAPO.** <Gloss name="dapo" /> adds four changes to GRPO, without a KL term and with rewards of $+1$ and $-1$
<Cite id="yu2025dapo" />.

- **Clip-higher.** With $\varepsilon = 0.2$ and a positive advantage, a token at probability 0.01 can rise only to 0.012
  within the clip, while a token at 0.9 has no effective limit. The upper clip therefore holds back unlikely tokens, the
  ones that explore. DAPO decouples the bounds, with $\varepsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$ and
  $\varepsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$, which kept the policy's entropy from collapsing. Raising
  $\varepsilon_{\text{low}}$ instead would let unlikely tokens be pushed to zero.
- **Dynamic sampling.** A group whose rewards are all equal has zero advantages and gives no gradient. As training
  progresses more prompts are solved every time, and the effective batch shrinks. DAPO over-samples prompts and drops
  every group with all responses correct or all wrong, until the batch is full.
- **Token-level loss.** DAPO divides the sum over all tokens of the group by the total number of tokens,
  $\sum_i\abs{y_i}$, instead of averaging within each response first. Every token then has equal weight, so repetitive
  or meaningless text in long responses is penalised in full.
- **Overlong reward shaping.** Truncated responses are either masked out of the loss or given a penalty that rises
  linearly from 0 to $-1$ over the last $L_{\text{cache}} = 4{,}096$ tokens before the limit $L_{\max} = 20{,}480$.

On Qwen2.5-32B base, AIME 2024 accuracy averaged over 32 samples rose from 30% with naive GRPO to 36% with overlong
filtering, 38% with clip-higher, 41% with soft overlong punishment, 42% with the token-level loss and 50% with dynamic
sampling. DeepSeek-R1-Zero's recipe reached 47% on the same base model, with twice as many training steps
<Cite id="yu2025dapo" />.

**Normalising over the batch.** REINFORCE++ standardises advantages over the whole batch instead of within each group,
which its authors argue removes the bias of per-prompt normalisation <Cite id="hu2025reinforcepp" />.

**Keeping a critic.** A critic is not inherently worse. DeepSeek found PPO far behind GRPO with the usual GAE setting
$\lambda = 0.95$, and close to it with $\lambda = 1$ <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. Open-Reasoner-Zero used PPO with
$\lambda = 1$, $\gamma = 1$ and no KL term, and reported that the learned critic gave low values to repetitive text
<Cite id="hu2025orz" />.

| Method      | Baseline                | Advantage scale | Token weights          | Critic | KL term                     |
| ----------- | ----------------------- | --------------- | ---------------------- | ------ | --------------------------- |
| PPO (RLHF)  | Learned value, with GAE | Batch std       | $1/\abs{y_i}$, usually | Yes    | In the reward               |
| RLOO        | Mean of the other $G-1$ | None            | 1 (sequence sum)       | No     | In the reward               |
| GRPO        | Group mean              | Group std       | $1/\abs{y_i}$          | No     | $k_3$ in the loss           |
| Dr. GRPO    | Group mean              | None            | Constant               | No     | Optional                    |
| DAPO        | Group mean              | Group std       | $1/\sum_j\abs{y_j}$    | No     | None                        |
| REINFORCE++ | Group mean or none      | Batch std       | Token-level            | No     | $k_1$ in the reward, or $k_2$ loss |

## Worked example

A maths prompt gets $G = 8$ responses. The verifier marks responses 1, 4 and 7 correct, so the rewards are
$(1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0)$ and $\hat p = 3/8 = 0.375$.

- **GRPO.** $\bar r = 0.375$ and $s_r = \sqrt{0.375 \times 0.625} = 0.484$. A correct response has
  $\hat A = 0.625/0.484 = 1.29$ and a wrong one $\hat A = -0.375/0.484 = -0.77$. The advantages sum to zero:
  $3 \times 1.29 = 5 \times 0.77 = 3.87$. With the $G - 1$ convention, $s_r = 0.518$ and the advantages are $1.21$ and
  $-0.72$.
- **RLOO.** A correct response's baseline is the mean of the other seven, $2/7$, so $\hat A = 1 - 0.286 = 0.71$. A
  wrong response's baseline is $3/7$, so $\hat A = -0.43$. These are the mean-centred values $0.625$ and $-0.375$
  times $8/7$.
- **Dr. GRPO.** $\hat A = 0.625$ and $-0.375$.

**A harder prompt.** With one correct response in eight, $\hat p = 0.125$ and $s_r = 0.331$. GRPO gives the correct
response $0.875/0.331 = 2.65$ and each wrong one $-0.38$. Dr. GRPO gives $0.875$ and $-0.125$. Standardisation has
multiplied this group's weight by $1/0.331 = 3.0$, against $1/0.484 = 2.1$ for the first group, so the harder prompt
counts about 1.5 times as much relative to Dr. GRPO.

**An easy prompt.** All eight correct: $\bar r = 1$, every $r_i - \bar r = 0$, and the prompt contributes no gradient.
DAPO's dynamic sampling would drop it and sample another prompt.

**Length.** Suppose the first group's correct responses have 200 and 800 tokens and two of its wrong responses have 300
and 1,200. GRPO weights each token by $\hat A_i/\abs{y_i}$: $1.29/200 = 0.0065$ against $1.29/800 = 0.0016$ for the
correct ones, and $-0.77/300 = -0.0026$ against $-0.77/1200 = -0.0006$ for the wrong ones. Each token of the long
wrong response is penalised four times less than each token of the short one. Under DAPO's token-level loss, every
token's weight is $\hat A_i$ divided by the same total, so the four responses' tokens are weighted by $1.29$ and
$-0.77$ alone.

## Settings in practice

|                     | DeepSeekMath                 | DeepSeek-R1-Zero                | DAPO                                  | TRL default |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------- |
| Group size $G$      | 64                           | 16                              | 16                                    | 8           |
| Batch per step      | 1,024                        | 32 prompts, 512 responses       | 512 prompts                           |             |
| Learning rate       | $10^{-6}$                    | $3 \times 10^{-6}$              | $10^{-6}$, constant after warm-up     |             |
| KL coefficient      | 0.04                         | 0.001                           | 0                                     | 0           |
| Clip                | not stated                   | not stated; 10 in R1's RL stage | $0.2$ low, $0.28$ high                | 0.2         |
| Response limit      | 1,024 tokens                 | 32,768, then 65,536             | 20,480 tokens                         |             |

Sources: DeepSeekMath <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />, DeepSeek-R1 <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />, DAPO
<Cite id="yu2025dapo" />, TRL documentation <Cite id="hf2026trlgrpo" />. Sampling temperature is usually 1 during
rollouts, so that the samples are drawn from the policy that the gradient assumes; DeepSeek-R1-Zero used 1
<Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />.

**Rollouts dominate the cost.** Each step generates $G$ responses for every prompt, often thousands of tokens each.
Training frameworks generate with a dedicated inference engine, usually vLLM, whose paged
attention cache serves large batches efficiently <Cite id="kwon2023" />. After each update the new weights are copied
into the inference engine. The engine's log-probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's, so TRL offers an
importance-sampling correction for the mismatch <Cite id="hf2026trlgrpo" />.

## Reading the training curves

TRL logs each of these under the name in brackets <Cite id="hf2026trlgrpo" />.

| Metric                                         | Healthy                                         | Warning                                                          |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mean reward (`reward`)                         | Rises, then flattens                            | Rises while held-out accuracy does not                           |
| Zero-variance groups (`frac_reward_zero_std`)  | A minority of groups                            | Approaches 1: prompts are all solved or all failed               |
| Response length (`completions/mean_length`)    | Grows with reward on reasoning tasks            | Keeps growing after the reward flattens; repetition              |
| Truncated fraction (`completions/clipped_ratio`) | Small                                         | Rising: responses hit the limit and fail                         |
| Entropy (`entropy`)                            | Flat or slowly rising                           | Falls fast: samples in a group become identical                  |
| KL to reference (`kl`)                         | Grows smoothly                                  | Jumps: unstable updates                                          |
| Clipped fraction (`clip_ratio/region_mean`)    | Small                                           | Large: updates leave the trust region                            |

- **Reward.** DAPO found that the final training reward correlated little with validation accuracy
  <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />. Evaluate pass@1 on held-out prompts at fixed intervals.
- **Zero-variance groups.** Each such group is wasted compute. A rising fraction of all-correct groups means the
  prompts are too easy for the current policy; a high fraction of all-wrong groups means they are too hard. Refilter the
  prompts, raise $G$, or use dynamic sampling.
- **Length.** DAPO treats a rise in length as room for exploration, and also saw it stall or fall for long periods; it
  judges a run by length together with validation accuracy <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />. Length that keeps growing while
  the reward is flat is the signature of GRPO's length bias <Cite id="liu2025drgrpo" />.
- **Entropy.** <Gloss name="entropy-collapse">Entropy collapse</Gloss> ends exploration: the group's responses become
  near-copies, their rewards become equal, and the advantages vanish. DAPO observed it with naive GRPO and PPO and found
  that a slow upward trend in entropy went with improving performance; very high entropy came with gibberish and
  repetition <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />. Cui et al. fitted downstream performance as $R = -a e^{H} + b$ in the policy
  entropy $H$, so performance saturates as entropy runs out <Cite id="cui2025" />.
- **Clipping.** DeepSeek reports that too small a clip range truncates the gradients of many tokens and degrades
  performance, while too large a range makes training unstable <Cite id="deepseek2025r1" />. With clip-higher, watch the
  upper and lower clipped fractions separately (`clip_ratio/high_mean`, `clip_ratio/low_mean`).

## Tools

- **TRL** provides `GRPOTrainer`. A reward function receives the completions and any dataset columns as keyword
  arguments and returns one float per completion. Options select the loss aggregation (`loss_type`: `"grpo"`,
  `"dr_grpo"`, `"dapo"` and others), the reward scaling (`scale_rewards`: per group, per batch or none), the upper clip
  (`epsilon_high`) and generation with vLLM (`use_vllm`, in the training process or as a separate server)
  <Cite id="hf2026trlgrpo" />.
- **verl** is the open-source implementation of HybridFlow, a framework for reinforcement learning on language models
  <Cite id="sheng2025" />. It implements PPO, GRPO, DAPO, RLOO, <Gloss name="reinforce" />++ and Dr. GRPO, with vLLM or
  SGLang for rollouts. DAPO was built on it <Cite id="yu2025dapo" />.
- **OpenRLHF** schedules the policy, reference, reward and critic models across <Gloss name="gpu" plural /> with Ray and
  generates with vLLM. It implements PPO, REINFORCE++, RLOO, GRPO and Dr. GRPO <Cite id="hu2024openrlhf" />.

A minimal TRL run with a verifier reward, Dr. GRPO's loss and DAPO's upper clip, in the API documented at the time of
writing:

```python
from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import GRPOConfig, GRPOTrainer

def correct(completions, answer, **kwargs):
    # 1.0 when the text after the last "Answer:" equals the reference answer, else 0.0.
    return [1.0 if c.rsplit("Answer:", 1)[-1].strip() == a else 0.0 for c, a in zip(completions, answer)]

dataset = load_dataset("json", data_files="train.jsonl", split="train")  # columns: prompt, answer
config = GRPOConfig(
    output_dir="grpo-run",
    num_generations=8,         # G
    beta=0.0,                  # no KL term, no reference model
    loss_type="dr_grpo",
    scale_rewards=False,       # advantage r_i - mean(r)
    epsilon_high=0.28,
    max_completion_length=1024,
    use_vllm=True,
)
trainer = GRPOTrainer(model="Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct", reward_funcs=correct, args=config, train_dataset=dataset)
trainer.train()
```

## Cost

- **Memory.** One trained model with its optimiser state. A reference model for inference only if $\beta > 0$. No
  critic, and no <NoteLink to="reward-models">reward model</NoteLink> when the reward is a program. PPO trains a critic
  of the policy's size as well.
- **Compute.** $G$ responses per prompt. Generation usually takes most of the time of a step, so the cost grows with
  $G$ and with response length.
- **Samples per useful prompt.** Groups with equal rewards are discarded or wasted, so the cost of each informative
  prompt rises as the policy saturates the training set.

## Trade-offs

- **Against PPO.** GRPO needs no critic and no GAE tuning. It needs several samples per prompt, and it gives every token
  of a response the same advantage, so it cannot tell which step of a long response helped.
- **Against RLOO.** The baselines differ only by the factor $(G - 1)/G$ and by GRPO's division by $s_r$. RLOO keeps the
  estimate unbiased; GRPO reweights prompts by their difficulty.
- **Group size.** A larger $G$ makes more groups informative on hard prompts and gives a better baseline, at a
  proportional cost in sampling.
- **Against direct preference optimisation.** <NoteLink to="direct-preference-optimisation">DPO</NoteLink> learns
  offline from fixed preference pairs. GRPO learns online from the policy's own samples, which lets it find and
  reinforce responses that no dataset contains, at the cost of generation during training.
