<Definition>
A **reward model** is a function $r_\phi(x, y) \in \reals$, with parameters $\phi$, that scores a response $y$ to a
prompt $x$. It is trained to agree with judgements that no program computes, usually human comparisons of two responses
to the same prompt. A policy is then trained to raise the score, by <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss> or
by selecting the best-scored of several samples <Cite id="stiennon2020" /> <Cite id="ouyang2022" />. The reward model is
a learned proxy for the judge, and it is accurate only near the responses it was trained on.
</Definition>

## Intuition

People find it hard to give a response an absolute score, but easier to say which of two responses is better. A reward
model compresses many such comparisons into a function that scores responses no person has read. Once trained, it
replaces the person in the loop and scores the policy's samples at every step of training.

When an existing <Gloss name="llm" /> is adapted to a new task, a reward model has four uses:

- **Training signal.** Policy-gradient methods such as <Gloss name="ppo" /> and <Gloss name="grpo" /> maximise its
  score on the policy's own samples.
- **Selection.** <Gloss name="best-of-n">Best-of-$n$ sampling</Gloss> draws $n$ responses and keeps the
  highest-scoring one, at inference time or to build <Gloss name="sft">supervised fine-tuning</Gloss> data.
- **Labelling.** It ranks pairs of fresh samples to create preference pairs for
  <NoteLink to="direct-preference-optimisation">direct preference optimisation</NoteLink>.
- **Filtering and monitoring.** It screens data and tracks progress, with the caveats below.

Every use pushes the scored responses away from the data the model was fitted on. A reward model is a classifier
evaluated off its training distribution, and its errors there are systematic. Most practical advice about reward models
follows from that fact.

## From comparisons to a loss

**Data.** A comparison is a triple $(x, y_w, y_l)$: for prompt $x$, a rater preferred response $y_w$ to response $y_l$.
The responses are usually samples from the model that will be trained, so that the reward model learns to separate the
outputs it will later score. A rater who ranks $K$ responses to one prompt yields $\binom{K}{2}$ ordered pairs.
<NoteLink to="instruction-tuning-and-alignment">InstructGPT</NoteLink>'s labellers ranked between 4 and 9 responses per
prompt <Cite id="ouyang2022" />.

**The Bradley–Terry model.** Suppose the rater perceives response $y$ as having utility $u(y) = r(x, y) + \epsilon_y$,
where $r$ is a true reward and the noise terms $\epsilon_y$ are independent standard Gumbel variables, and prefers the
response with the larger utility. The difference of two independent standard Gumbel variables follows the standard
logistic distribution, whose <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss> is the logistic sigmoid
$\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$. Therefore

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = P\paren{\epsilon_{y_l} - \epsilon_{y_w} < r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)} = \sigma\paren{r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)}.
$$

This is the <Gloss name="bradley-terry">Bradley–Terry model</Gloss> of paired comparisons <Cite id="bradley1952" />.
The probability depends only on the difference of the two rewards. Its fitting and relatives are covered in
<NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">paired-comparison models</NoteLink>.

**The loss.** Treat the comparisons in a dataset $\Dcal$ as independent. Their likelihood under a reward model $r_\phi$
is $\prod_{(x, y_w, y_l) \in \Dcal}\sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))$. The negative mean log-likelihood is

$$
L(\phi) = -\frac{1}{\abs{\Dcal}}\sum_{(x, y_w, y_l) \in \Dcal}\log\sigma\paren{r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)}.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> with the label "first response wins" and
the margin $\Delta = r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)$ as the logit. Since $\tfrac{d}{d\Delta}\brack{-\log\sigma(\Delta)}
= -\sigma(-\Delta)$, the gradient for one pair is

$$
\nabla_\phi L = -\sigma(-\Delta)\paren{\nabla_\phi r_\phi(x, y_w) - \nabla_\phi r_\phi(x, y_l)}.
$$

The weight $\sigma(-\Delta)$ is the probability the model assigns to the wrong order. Misordered pairs get weights near
1, and pairs already separated by a wide margin get weights near 0.

<Derivation title="The difference of two Gumbel variables is logistic">
Let $G_1$ and $G_2$ be independent standard Gumbel variables, with distribution function $F(g) = \exp(-e^{-g})$ and
density $f(g) = e^{-g}\exp(-e^{-g})$. For any $z$,

$$
P(G_2 - G_1 < z) = \int f(g)\,F(g + z)\,dg = \int e^{-g}\exp\paren{-e^{-g}}\exp\paren{-e^{-g}e^{-z}}\,dg.
$$

Substitute $t = e^{-g}$, so that $dt = -e^{-g}\,dg$ and $t$ runs over $(0, \infty)$:

$$
P(G_2 - G_1 < z) = \int_0^\infty \exp\paren{-t\,(1 + e^{-z})}\,dt = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z).
$$

With $z = r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$, $G_1 = \epsilon_{y_w}$ and $G_2 = \epsilon_{y_l}$, this is the Bradley–Terry
probability that $y_w$ has the larger utility.
</Derivation>

**The offset is not identified.** Adding any function $c(x)$ of the prompt to every reward leaves every margin, and so
the loss, unchanged. The scale of the reward is set by the noise model, but its zero is arbitrary. Practitioners fix the
zero after training: InstructGPT added a bias so that the labellers' demonstrations scored 0 on average
<Cite id="ouyang2022" />. The offset matters when rewards from different models are averaged or compared. Eisenstein et
al. add the penalty $\eta\,\expect\brack{(r_\phi(x, y_w) + r_\phi(x, y_l))^2}$ with a small $\eta$, which pulls the
rewards of each pair towards zero <Cite id="eisenstein2024" />.

**Graded comparisons.** When raters also say how much better one response is, the grade can set a margin. Llama 2
trained with $-\log\sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l) - m)$, where the margin $m$ is larger for "significantly
better" than for "slightly better" <Cite id="touvron2023" />.

**Correlated pairs.** The $\binom{K}{2}$ pairs from one ranking share responses: each response appears in $K - 1$ pairs.
InstructGPT found that shuffling these pairs into one dataset made the reward model overfit within one pass. It put all
pairs from a prompt into one batch element instead, which needs one forward pass per response and no longer overfit. It
trained for a single epoch <Cite id="ouyang2022" />.

## Architecture

The standard reward model is a pretrained <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>
<Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> whose output layer is replaced by a linear head that returns one
number. The prompt and response are concatenated, formatted with the chat template, and passed through the network. The
head reads the final hidden state $\hvec_T \in \reals^d$ at the last token:

$$
r_\phi(x, y) = \wvec^\top\hvec_T(x, y) + b.
$$

The last token is used because, in a causal transformer, it is the only position that has attended to the whole prompt
and response. In Hugging Face Transformers this is a sequence-classification model with one output label.

**Which model to start from.** The reward model is usually initialised from the same family as the policy, often the
supervised fine-tuned checkpoint. Llama 2 initialised its reward models from its chat model checkpoints so that the
reward model knows what the chat model knows. Touvron et al. argue that an information mismatch between the two could
make the reward model favour hallucinations <Cite id="touvron2023" />. Malik et al. found that a reward model from a
different lineage than the policy can make the policy trained with <Gloss name="rlhf" /> much worse, so the reward model
that scores highest on a benchmark does not guarantee a good policy <Cite id="malik2025rewardbench2" />.

**Size.** The reward model need not be as large as the policy. InstructGPT used a 6-billion-parameter reward model to
train a 175-billion-parameter policy. Its 175-billion-parameter reward models trained unstably <Cite id="ouyang2022" />.

## What reward models get wrong

### Overoptimisation

Optimising a policy against a reward model moves its outputs into regions the reward model never saw. The proxy score
keeps rising there, while the quality the proxy was meant to measure first rises and then falls. This is
<Gloss name="reward-hacking">reward overoptimisation</Gloss>, an instance of Goodhart's law
<Cite id="gao2023overoptimisation" />.

Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured the effect without human raters. A 6-billion-parameter "gold" reward model played
the human: it labelled 100,000 pairs of samples, on which proxy reward models from 3 million to 3 billion parameters
were trained. A 1.2-billion-parameter policy was then optimised against each proxy, by best-of-$n$ sampling or by PPO,
and its outputs were scored by the gold model <Cite id="gao2023overoptimisation" />. Distance from the initial policy
$\pi_{\text{init}}$ was measured by $d = \sqrt{\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{init}})}$. The gold score, with the initial
policy's score set to 0, fitted

$$
R_{\text{bon}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{bon}} - \beta_{\text{bon}}\,d), \qquad R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}}\log d),
$$

with coefficients $\alpha, \beta > 0$ that depend on the proxy's size and training data
<Cite id="gao2023overoptimisation" />. Both curves rise, peak and fall:

- **Best-of-$n$.** $R_{\text{bon}}'(d) = \alpha_{\text{bon}} - 2\beta_{\text{bon}}d$ vanishes at
  $d^* = \alpha_{\text{bon}}/(2\beta_{\text{bon}})$, where the gold score is
  $\alpha_{\text{bon}}^2/(4\beta_{\text{bon}})$.
- **Reinforcement learning.** $R_{\text{RL}}'(d) = \alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}}\log d - \beta_{\text{RL}}$
  vanishes at $d^* = \exp(\alpha_{\text{RL}}/\beta_{\text{RL}} - 1)$. Substituting gives
  $R_{\text{RL}}(d^*) = \beta_{\text{RL}}\,d^*$.

Past $d^*$, more optimisation makes the policy worse by the measure that matters.

The study also found the following <Cite id="gao2023overoptimisation" />:

- The coefficients vary smoothly, roughly logarithmically, with the number of reward-model parameters, so the peak
  gold score of a larger reward model can be predicted. Larger reward models reach higher gold scores.
- With fewer than about 2,000 comparisons, the proxies were barely better than chance.
- Larger policies started higher and gained less from optimisation, but overoptimised by about as much: the gap between
  proxy and gold scores and the distance at the peak were similar.
- Adding a <Gloss name="kl" /> penalty to the reinforcement-learning reward raised the proxy score reached at a given KL
  divergence, but not the gold score. In this setting the penalty acted like <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early
  stopping</NoteLink>.

For best-of-$n$, the KL divergence from the sampling policy is at most $\log n - (n - 1)/n$ nats
<Cite id="beirami2025" />. It grows like $\log n$, so best-of-$n$ explores only near the initial policy.

<Derivation title="The KL divergence of best-of-n sampling">
Assume every response has a distinct reward and let $F$ be the distribution function of $R = r(x, Y)$ for
$Y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)$, taken continuous. Best-of-$n$ returns $y$ when $y$ is drawn and the other
$n - 1$ draws score lower, in any of $n$ positions, so its distribution is

$$
\pi_{\text{bon}}(y \mid x) = n\,F\paren{r(x, y)}^{n - 1}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x).
$$

The KL divergence is the expected log-ratio under $\pi_{\text{bon}}$:
$\KL(\pi_{\text{bon}} \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \log n + (n - 1)\,\expect\brack{\log V}$, where $V = F(R_{\max})$ is
the largest of $n$ independent uniform variables. $V$ has density $n v^{n-1}$ on $[0, 1]$, so

$$
\expect\brack{\log V} = \int_0^1 n v^{n - 1}\log v\,dv = -\frac{1}{n},
$$

by <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink>. Hence $\KL = \log n - (n - 1)/n$. When distinct
responses can share a reward, or the same response can be drawn twice, the true divergence is smaller, and the formula
is an upper bound <Cite id="beirami2025" />.
</Derivation>

### Length bias

Raters often prefer longer responses, and reward models learn that preference. Singhal et al. found that, on three
datasets, most of the reward gained by RLHF came from making responses longer. A reward equal to response length alone
reproduced most of the downstream improvement of RLHF over the supervised model. The reward models were the main source
of the bias and were easily swayed by length biases in the preference data <Cite id="singhal2024" />.

### Sycophancy

Human preference data reward agreement with the user. Sharma et al. found that a response that matched the user's
stated views was more likely to be preferred. Both people and preference models preferred convincingly written
sycophantic responses to correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time. Optimising against a preference model
sometimes traded truthfulness for <Gloss name="sycophancy" /> <Cite id="sharma2024sycophancy" />.

### Underspecification

Reward models that agree on held-out preference data can disagree on the policy's outputs. Eisenstein et al. trained
reward models that differed only in their random seeds and had similar in-distribution accuracy. They gave very
different rewards under distribution shift, and a policy aligned to one of them did not score higher under another
trained on the same data <Cite id="eisenstein2024" />.

## Mitigations

**KL penalty and early stopping.** A penalty $\beta\,\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ keeps the policy near the
outputs the reward model was trained on. It does not raise the peak of the gold curve; it limits how far past the peak
training can run <Cite id="gao2023overoptimisation" />. In practice, log the KL divergence of the policy from the
reference, evaluate checkpoints with a held-out judge, and keep the checkpoint where that judge peaks.

**Ensembles.** Train $M$ reward models $r_1, \dots, r_M$ and optimise a conservative combination. Coste et al. compared
the worst case $\min_m r_m(x, y)$ with the mean minus a multiple $\lambda$ of the variance across the ensemble. With
best-of-$n$, conservative optimisation practically eliminated overoptimisation and improved the gold score by up to
70%. With PPO, it reduced overoptimisation in every setting, and together with a small KL penalty it prevented it
<Cite id="coste2024" />. Eisenstein et al. found that ensembles whose members differ in their pretraining seeds
generalise better than ensembles that differ only in fine-tuning seeds. Ensembles still did not remove reward hacking
that all members shared, such as systematic changes in response length <Cite id="eisenstein2024" />.

**Refresh the data.** Collect new comparisons on the current policy's samples and retrain the reward model. The reward
model then sees the region the policy has moved to. Llama 2 trained five successive RLHF models, each with reward
models retrained on newly collected preference data <Cite id="touvron2023" />.

**Control known biases.** Compare responses of matched length in evaluation, report the correlation between reward and
length, and add penalties for length or format when they are not part of the task.

## Evaluating a reward model

**Held-out pairwise accuracy** is the fraction of held-out pairs with $r_\phi(x, y_w) > r_\phi(x, y_l)$. It is bounded
by how often raters agree: InstructGPT's training labellers agreed with each other on 72.6% of comparisons
<Cite id="ouyang2022" />. A reward model at 72% on such data is near its ceiling, not mediocre. The predicted
probabilities $\sigma(\Delta)$ should also be calibrated against the observed win rates.

**Benchmarks.** RewardBench scores reward models on prompt, chosen and rejected triples in four sections: chat, hard
chat (trick questions and subtly different answers), safety (refusing harmful requests without refusing harmless ones)
and reasoning (correct against buggy code, correct against flawed maths). It evaluates classifier reward models and
the implicit rewards of DPO-trained models alike <Cite id="lambert2024rewardbench" />. RewardBench 2 asks the reward
model to pick one correct response from four, so random guessing scores 25%. It uses mostly new human-written prompts
in six domains: factuality, precise instruction following, maths, safety, focus and ties. Leading models scored 20 or
more points lower than on RewardBench, and the scores correlated with the gains of best-of-$n$ sampling
<Cite id="malik2025rewardbench2" />.

**Downstream.** The test that matters is the policy the reward model produces. Run best-of-$n$ or a short RL run, then
judge the outputs with people or a separate judge. Never use the reward model being optimised as the final metric: it
is the quantity being overfitted.

## Outcome and process reward models

An <Gloss name="orm" /> scores a whole response or its final answer. Cobbe et al. trained verifiers of this kind for
grade-school maths: sample many candidate solutions, keep the one the verifier ranks highest. Verification scaled
better with more data than fine-tuning the generator did <Cite id="cobbe2021" />.

A <Gloss name="prm" /> scores each step of a multi-step solution, such as each line of a
<NoteLink to="chain-of-thought">chain of thought</NoteLink>. Lightman et al. collected PRM800K, 800,000 human labels
on individual steps of solutions to MATH problems. They scored a solution by the product of the PRM's probabilities that
each step is correct. Choosing the best of 1,860 samples on a representative subset of the MATH test set, the PRM
solved 78.2% of problems, an ORM 72.4% and majority voting 69.6% <Cite id="lightman2023" />. Uesato et al. found on
GSM8K that outcome supervision reached similar final-answer error rates with less labelling. Process supervision, or a
reward model trained to imitate it, was needed for the reasoning steps themselves to be correct. Their best system cut
the rate of flawed reasoning among solutions with a correct final answer from 14.0%, the previous best, to 3.4%
<Cite id="uesato2022" />.

Step labels are expensive. Math-Shepherd labels them automatically: from each step, a model completes the solution
several times, and the step is labelled good if a completion reaches the correct final answer
<Cite id="wang2024mathshepherd" />.

## Generative reward models and judges

A reward model need not have a scalar head. A <NoteLink to="language-model-as-a-judge">language-model judge</NoteLink>
is prompted with the question and one or two responses and asked for a score or a preference. Zheng et al. introduced
MT-Bench, 80 multi-turn questions in 8 categories, and found that GPT-4 as a judge agreed with human preferences more
than 80% of the time, the same level at which humans agreed with each other <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />. They also
documented the judges' biases: they favoured the response in one position, favoured longer responses, and favoured their
own outputs. Asking twice with the order swapped, and declaring a tie when the verdicts disagree, controls for the
<Gloss name="position-bias">position bias</Gloss> <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />. Panickssery et al. linked
self-preference to self-recognition: the better a model recognised its own outputs, the more it preferred them
<Cite id="panickssery2024" />.

A **generative reward model** is a language model fine-tuned to produce the verdict as text. Zhang et al. trained a
model to answer whether a solution is correct and used the probability of "Yes" as the reward. The model could write a
chain of thought before its verdict, and several sampled verdicts could be averaged. A 9-billion-parameter verifier of
this kind, choosing among GSM8K solutions sampled from another model, raised the fraction of problems solved from 73%
to 93.4% <Cite id="zhang2025genrm" />. Mahan et al. found
that prompting a language model as a judge without training trailed Bradley–Terry reward models in distribution by
9–36%. Training it on its own reasoning about preferences matched Bradley–Terry models in distribution and beat them
out of distribution by 10–45% <Cite id="mahan2024" />.

## Rewards that are computed

When correctness can be checked by a program, the reward need not be learned. A unit-test suite, an
<Gloss name="exact-match">exact match</Gloss> on a numeric answer or a format check returns a reward with no error of
the kind a learned model makes off its training distribution. This is the limiting case of a reward model, and it is the
basis of <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards">reinforcement learning with verifiable
rewards</NoteLink>. Such a reward can still be gamed when it checks less than the task requires: a policy rewarded by
three unit tests can pass them without solving the problem.

## Training one in practice

Hugging Face's <NoteLink to="adapting-pretrained-language-models">TRL</NoteLink> library trains Bradley–Terry reward
models with `RewardTrainer` <Cite id="hf2026trlreward" />:

```python
from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import RewardConfig, RewardTrainer

# Each row: {"prompt": ..., "chosen": ..., "rejected": ...}, as text or as chat messages.
dataset = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized")

trainer = RewardTrainer(
    model="Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B",  # loaded as a sequence classifier with one output
    args=RewardConfig(output_dir="rm", num_train_epochs=1, center_rewards_coefficient=0.01),
    train_dataset=dataset["train"],
    eval_dataset=dataset["test"],
)
trainer.train()
```

`center_rewards_coefficient` is the $\eta$ of the centring penalty above. When the model is trained through a
<Gloss name="lora" /> adapter, the new scalar head (named `score`) must be listed among the modules to train, or it
keeps its random initialisation <Cite id="hf2026trlreward" />. Pairs whose chosen or rejected sequence exceeds
`max_length` tokens (1,024 by default) are dropped, not truncated, so check how many pairs survive
<Cite id="hf2026trlreward" />.

The trainer logs, per interval, `accuracy` (the fraction of pairs ordered correctly), `margin` (the mean of $\Delta$),
and `mean_reward`, `min_reward` and `max_reward` <Cite id="hf2026trlreward" />. Read them as follows:

- **Healthy.** Evaluation accuracy rises and levels off near the rater agreement of the data. Training and evaluation
  accuracy stay within a few points of each other.
- **Overfitting.** Training accuracy climbs towards 1 and the training margin keeps growing while evaluation accuracy
  stays flat or falls. Stop at one epoch, add data, or regularise.
- **Drifting offset.** `mean_reward` wanders far from 0 while accuracy is unchanged. The offset is unidentified; centre
  the rewards.
- **Nothing learned.** Accuracy stays near 0.5. Check that chosen and rejected were not swapped, that the head is
  trained, and that the prompt is not truncated away.

## Worked example

**One pair.** A reward model scores the chosen response 1.2 and the rejected one 0.4. The margin is $\Delta = 0.8$,
the modelled probability that the chosen response wins is $\sigma(0.8) = 0.690$, and the loss is
$-\log 0.690 = 0.371$. The gradient weight $\sigma(-0.8) = 0.310$ is the modelled probability of the wrong order.

**Best-of-$n$ budget.** For $n = 16$, the KL divergence of best-of-$n$ from the sampling policy is at most
$\log 16 - 15/16 = 2.773 - 0.938 = 1.835$ nats, so $d \le 1.355$. For $n = 256$ it is at most 4.549 nats.

**Where the peak lies.** Take illustrative best-of-$n$ coefficients $\alpha_{\text{bon}} = 1$ and
$\beta_{\text{bon}} = 0.25$. The gold score peaks at $d^* = 1/(2 \times 0.25) = 2$, a KL divergence of $d^{*2} = 4$
nats, with gold score $1^2/(4 \times 0.25) = 1$. Solving $\log n - (n - 1)/n = 4$ gives $n \approx 148$. Selecting the
best of more than about 148 samples makes the outputs worse by the gold measure, although the proxy score keeps rising.

## Assumptions

- **One shared reward.** Bradley–Terry assumes every rater judges by the same $r$ plus independent noise. Raters with
  different values are averaged into one reward that none of them holds.
- **Comparisons depend on the reward difference only.** The model cannot represent intransitive preferences, where
  $y_1 \succ y_2 \succ y_3 \succ y_1$.
- **The scored responses resemble the training responses.** Accuracy measured on held-out pairs transfers to the
  policy's samples only while those samples stay close to the data.

## Pitfalls

- **Optimising the metric you report.** A rising reward-model score during training says nothing about quality past the
  overoptimisation peak. Report a separate judge or human evaluation.
- **Choosing the benchmark leader.** The reward model that tops a benchmark is not necessarily the best for a given
  policy; one from the policy's own family can work better <Cite id="malik2025rewardbench2" />.
- **Template mismatch.** A reward model scores text formatted the way it was trained. A different chat template or
  tokenizer at scoring time changes its inputs and its scores.
- **Length.** Without a check, policies learn that longer is better because the reward model learned it from the raters
  <Cite id="singhal2024" />.
- **Ties and low-agreement pairs.** Pairs on which raters disagree add noise and bound accuracy. Remove ties or give
  confident pairs a larger margin.
