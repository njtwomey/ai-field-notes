<Callout variant="warning" title="An exploratory note">
TypeSafe has not published RLCD's reward, algorithm or data. This note states what TypeSafe has said, derives which
rewards *could* produce calibrated decisions, and describes OpenJev-RLCD, an open implementation by Gao and Wang that
reaches the same rewards and reports results <Cite id="gao2026openjev" />. OpenJev-RLCD is a reconstruction: its
authors claim nothing about <NoteLink to="jev">Jev</NoteLink>'s internal algorithm.
</Callout>

<Definition>
**<Gloss name="rlcd" />** is the post-training method TypeSafe AI names for its decision model Jev, presented as a third
way to post-train a pretrained <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> beside <Gloss name="rlhf" /> and
<Gloss name="rlvr" /> <Cite id="typesafe2026primer" />. Its target is an *output contract*: the model returns a choice
among fixed options with a probability for each, and "higher probability should correspond to a greater chance that the
answer is correct" <Cite id="typesafe2026primer" />. In decision-theoretic terms the target is a calibrated predictive
distribution $\pivec(\cdot \mid \xvec)$ over the options, as sharp as the evidence allows.
</Definition>

## What is published

- The method's name, its output contract and its contrast with RLHF and RLVR <Cite id="typesafe2026primer" />.
- That Jev's launch combined a new architecture, a "parallel sampler" and RLCD <Cite id="typesafe2026launch" />.
- That Jev is not fine-tuned on customer data <Cite id="typesafe2026models" />.

TypeSafe has not published the reward, the policy-gradient algorithm, what counts as an action, the training data or
the base model. A paper titled after RLCD uses Jev as a black box and does not describe the method
<Cite id="guo2026justaskjev" />. Gao and Wang published a working RLCD for reasoning models, with code, run logs and
an API-compatible server <Cite id="gao2026openjev" /> <Cite id="gao2026openjevcode" />; it is described below.

## Intuition

A decision model maps a prompt $\xvec$ to a distribution $\pivec = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$ over $K$ options (see
<NoteLink to="answer-token-scoring" />). Let $\pvec = (p_1, \dots, p_K)$ be the true distribution of the correct option
given $\xvec$. The output contract asks for $\pivec = \pvec$.

<Gloss name="rl">Reinforcement learning</Gloss> optimises the expected reward. The reward decides which $\pivec$
maximises it. Two familiar rewards fail the contract:

- **A correctness reward**, $R = \indicator[a = y]$ for a sampled answer $a \sim \pivec$ and the correct option $y$,
  has expectation $\sum_k \pi_k p_k$. That is linear in $\pivec$, so its maximum over the probability simplex is a
  vertex: all probability on $\argmax_k p_k$. RLVR-style training of a decision therefore pushes it towards
  certainty, whatever the evidence.
- **A preference reward**, as in RLHF, rewards the answer people prefer. TypeSafe's primer argues that preference
  training concentrates probability on a favoured style and drops other outputs <Cite id="typesafe2026primer" />.
  Neither target is the probability of being right.

A reward meets the contract when its expected value is a <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule" /> of $\pivec$: a score
$S(\pivec, y)$ whose expectation $\expect_{y \sim \pvec}[S(\pivec, y)]$ is maximised at $\pivec = \pvec$
<Cite id="gneiting2007" />. The log score $\log \pi_y$ and the <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">Brier
score</NoteLink> $-\norm{\pivec - \evec_y}^2$ <Cite id="brier1950" /> are the standard examples.

## Details

### With the label, the reward is supervised learning

For a one-token decision model the policy *is* the distribution $\pivec$, read in one pass. When the correct option $y$
is known, a reward $S(\pivec, y)$ that scores the whole distribution needs no sampling: the expected gradient is the
exact gradient $\nabla_{\thetavec} S(\pivec_{\thetavec}, y)$. With the log score it is the gradient of cross-entropy, so
reinforcement learning with a log-score reward and full labels *is* supervised training on those labels (see
<NoteLink to="training-decision-models" />). Reinforcement learning adds something only when training sees less than the
label: a check on a sampled answer, a judge's verdict, or the outcome of acting on the decision.

### A proper reward from a verifier

Suppose training can sample an answer $a \sim \pivec$ and learn only whether it was correct, $c = \indicator[a = y]$.
Consider the reward

$$
R(a, c) = 2c - \norm{\pivec}^2 .
$$

The penalty $\norm{\pivec}^2 = \sum_k \pi_k^2$ needs no label; it is computed from the policy. Since
$\expect_{a \sim \pivec}[\indicator[a = y]] = \pi_y$,

$$
\expect_{a \sim \pivec}[R] = 2\pi_y - \norm{\pivec}^2 = 1 - \norm{\pivec - \evec_y}^2 ,
$$

which is one minus the Brier score. Taking the expectation over $y \sim \pvec$ as well,

$$
\expect[R] = 2\langle \pvec, \pivec \rangle - \norm{\pivec}^2 = \norm{\pvec}^2 - \norm{\pivec - \pvec}^2 ,
$$

which is maximised exactly at $\pivec = \pvec$. The correctness term rewards putting probability on the right answer;
the penalty charges for sharpness that the evidence does not support. Without the penalty the optimum is the vertex
found above.

<Derivation title="Every proper score has a verifier-only reward">
A proper score can be written as $S(\pivec, y) = G(\pivec) + \partial_y G(\pivec) - \langle \nabla G(\pivec), \pivec
\rangle$ for a convex $G$ (the Savage representation) <Cite id="gneiting2007" />. Only $\partial_y G$ depends on the
label. Since $\expect_{a \sim \pivec}[c\, \partial_a G(\pivec) / \pi_a] = \partial_y G(\pivec)$, the reward

$$
R(a, c) = G(\pivec) - \langle \nabla G(\pivec), \pivec \rangle + c\, \frac{\partial_a G(\pivec)}{\pi_a}
$$

has expectation $S(\pivec, y)$ over the sampled answer. For the Brier score, $G(\pivec) = \norm{\pivec}^2$ and
$\partial_a G / \pi_a = 2$, which gives $R = 2c - \norm{\pivec}^2$: the importance weight $1/\pi_a$ cancels. For the
log score, $G(\pivec) = \sum_k \pi_k \log \pi_k$ and the weight $(\log \pi_a + 1) / \pi_a$ does not cancel, so the
reward has high variance when the sampled answer was unlikely. The Brier score is the natural choice when the feedback
is a check on one sampled answer.
</Derivation>

Gao and Wang reach the same reward from the other direction <Cite id="gao2026openjev" />. Its expectation splits as

$$
\expect[2c - \norm{\pivec}^2] = \underbrace{2\langle \pvec, \pivec \rangle - 1}_{2 P(\text{correct}) - 1}
\; + \; \underbrace{1 - \norm{\pivec}^2}_{\text{Gini impurity of } \pivec} ,
$$

so the calibrated reward is the RLVR objective plus a bonus for spread whose coefficient must be exactly 1. Any
smaller coefficient moves the optimum away from $\pvec$; a coefficient of 0, plain RLVR, puts it at the mode.

### Confidence that is also the policy

Damani and co-authors train a reasoning model to state a confidence $\hat c$ alongside its answer, with the reward
$\indicator[a = y] - (\hat c - \indicator[a = y])^2$ <Cite id="damani2025" />. Because $\hat c$ is a separate output,
the Brier term is maximised by the true probability that the answer is correct. Bani-Harouni and co-authors use the log
score of a stated confidence in the same way <Cite id="baniharouni2025" />.

In a one-token decision model there is no separate confidence: the probability of the sampled answer, $\pi_a$, is both
how the answer was chosen and how confident the model is. For a yes-or-no decision with $\pi = \pi_{\text{yes}}$ and
$p = p_{\text{yes}}$, the same reward with $\hat c = \pi_a$ has expected value

$$
\expect[R] = \pi p + (1 - \pi)(1 - p) - \bigl[p(1 - \pi)^2 + (1 - p)\pi^2\bigr],
$$

Setting $\partial \expect[R] / \partial \pi = (2p - 1) + 2(p - \pi)$ to zero gives

$$
\pi^\star = 2p - \tfrac12 , \quad \text{clipped to } [0, 1].
$$

At $p = 0.7$ the optimum is $\pi^\star = 0.9$: the correctness term still pays for sharpness. Coupling the confidence
to the policy breaks the calibration guarantee; the reward $2c - \norm{\pivec}^2$ restores it.

### A construction for verifier feedback

Combining the pieces gives one concrete reading of RLCD for a single-pass decision model trained from checks on
sampled answers. It is a construction, not TypeSafe's method.

```text
input: base model θ₀; tasks (state, question, options, checker);
       group size G; KL weight β
θ ← θ₀
repeat:
    sample a batch of (state, question) pairs
    for each pair, one forward pass:
        π ← softmax(code logits)             # all K probabilities
        sample G answers a₁ … a_G ~ π
        cᵢ ← checker(aᵢ)                     # correct or not
        Rᵢ ← 2 cᵢ − ‖π‖²                     # proper in expectation
        Aᵢ ← Rᵢ − mean of the other R's     # group baseline
    gradient ← mean of  Aᵢ ∇ log π(aᵢ)  +  ∇(−‖π‖²)  −  β ∇ KL(π ‖ π_θ₀)
    θ ← θ + η · gradient
```

- The group baseline is the device of <Gloss name="grpo" /> <Cite id="shao2024grpo" />, which removes the need for a
  value network; the score-function gradient is <Gloss name="reinforce" /> <Cite id="williams1992" />.
- The penalty's gradient is exact, because $\norm{\pivec}^2$ is known.
- The <Gloss name="kl" /> term to the starting model is the RLHF safeguard against drifting from the pretrained language
  prior.
- Sampling is only needed when checking is expensive. A one-pass model already has all $K$ probabilities; if a
  checker could judge all $K$ options cheaply, the label would be known and supervised training on it would do.

What the "checker" can be:

| feedback | what is observed | reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rules in code over the state | the correct option | log or Brier score; supervised |
| a checker of one proposed answer | correct or not, for the sampled answer | $2c - \norm{\pivec}^2$ |
| a judge model with probabilities | a soft target $\bar\pvec$ | $-\norm{\pivec - \bar\pvec}^2$ or cross-entropy to $\bar\pvec$ |
| an environment (an agent acted on the decision) | success, possibly delayed | $2c - \norm{\pivec}^2$ with $c$ the outcome |

### An open implementation for reasoning models

Gao and Wang's OpenJev-RLCD applies the same idea to a model that reasons before it decides
<Cite id="gao2026openjev" />. A question $\xvec$ has $K$ options and an outcome $Y \sim \qvec(\cdot \mid \xvec)$. The
model samples a rationale $r \sim \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid \xvec)$ that ends in the marker `Answer:`, and then the
answer distribution it commits to is *read*, not sampled: $\uvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, r)$, the softmax of the option
tokens' logits at that point, as in <NoteLink to="answer-token-scoring" />. With an empty rationale this is a
single-pass decision model, and training it with the Brier score is supervised training, as derived above.

**Score each rationale, not the mixture.** The model's overall decision is the mixture
$\pvec = \expect_r \uvec(r)$. For the Brier score $J(\uvec, \qvec) = 2\uvec^\top\qvec - \norm{\uvec}^2$,

$$
J(\pvec, \qvec) = \expect_r J\bigl(\uvec(r), \qvec\bigr) + \expect_r \norm{\uvec(r) - \pvec}^2 ,
$$

because $J$ is affine in $\uvec$ apart from $-\norm{\uvec}^2$. Scoring the mixture of several samples therefore pays a
bonus for rationales that disagree, whether or not the disagreement is grounded; a policy can collect it by randomising
its conclusions. Scoring each rationale's own distribution removes the bonus. When the outcome does not depend on the
model's rationale, the optimum is then $\uvec(\xvec, r) = \qvec(\xvec)$ for every rationale, so a single query is
calibrated.

**Two failure modes.** Trained end to end, this per-rationale objective failed in two ways.

- *Reasoning switched off.* Hedging after an empty rationale is learned faster than hedging after reasoning, so the
  policy gradient moved all rationales to the bare `Answer:` marker within about 100 steps on MMLU-Pro. A KL anchor
  to the starting model prevented this.
- *The readout drowned.* The score-function term's gradient was 7 to 12 times larger than the pathwise term's, and it
  dominated the optimiser; more weight on it made every metric worse.

**Calibrate, then reinforce.** The recipe separates the two roles:

```text
each step: sample rationales r₁ … r_M ~ π_θ(· | x) up to "Answer:"
           read uᵢ = u_θ(x, rᵢ); observe Y
           Rᵢ ← J(uᵢ, Y)          # a strictly proper score of uᵢ
stage 1:   L ← −(1/M) Σᵢ Rᵢ        # pathwise only: rationales not reinforced
stage 2:   from the stage-1 model θ₁:
           Aᵢ ← (1/M)(Rᵢ − β log π_θ(rᵢ)/π_θ₁(rᵢ)) − leave-one-out mean
           L ← −(1/M) Σᵢ Rᵢ − c Σᵢ stopgrad(Aᵢ) log π_θ(rᵢ | x)
           # c = 0.3, β = 0.04
```

Stage 1 trains the decision the model reports after its own reasoning; stage 2 reinforces the reasoning with the same
proper score as reward, anchored at the calibrated model.

**Results.** With Qwen3-1.7B, 400 steps per stage and three seeds, each method's single-query decision after
<NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">temperature scaling</NoteLink> compared as follows <Cite id="gao2026openjev" />:

| task | method | accuracy | Brier | decided at the error budget | fitted $T$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GSM8K answer verification (budget 5%) | <Gloss name="sft" /> | 70.4% | 0.382 | 6% | 2.1 |
| | GRPO | 91.8% | 0.150 | 19% | 7.2 |
| | RLCD, two stages | 92.2% | 0.135 | 81% | 1.4 |
| MMLU-Pro (budget 20%) | SFT | 42.3% | 0.711 | 16% | 1.6 |
| | GRPO | 42.4% | 0.753 | 0% | 11.1 |
| | RLCD, two stages | 47.7% | 0.658 | 28% | 1.6 |

GRPO matched RLCD's accuracy on answer verification, but its confidence separated right from wrong answers so poorly
that temperature scaling could not repair it; a fitted $T$ near 1 means calibrated as trained. Stage 1 carried most of
the gain on both tasks, and the Brier and log scores worked about equally well. Stage 2 helped where reasoning changes
the answer (verification) and was neutral where it does not (MMLU-Pro). Continuing from the calibrated checkpoint with
GRPO's correctness reward re-inflated confidence on MMLU-Pro, raising the fitted $T$ from 1.4 to 3.8.

**Where reinforcement learning cannot help.** On ChaosNLI the target is the spread of 100 annotators' labels, so the
uncertainty is aleatoric and no rationale can reduce it. The per-rationale objective switched reasoning off and matched
cross-entropy on direct answers (<Gloss name="js">Jensen–Shannon divergence</Gloss> 0.075 against 0.074), as the
analysis predicts: with an explicit readout and labels from the world, the RLCD gradient equals the Brier gradient in
expectation. When uncertainty is disagreement rather than missing computation, a fast single-pass scorer trained with
cross-entropy is the right model.

### Ordinal answers: rewards on a rating scale

A score question, such as a rating from 1 to 5, has ordered answers. The rewards above treat its levels as unordered
options. They are still proper, but they are blind to order: with the truth at 2, favouring 3 costs exactly as much as
favouring 5, so a near miss earns no credit and training gets no signal pulling probability towards the right part of
the scale. The overview's section on ordinal ratings describes the readout side of this problem (see
<NoteLink to="decision-models" />); this section is about the reward.

**Reduce the rating to its cut-points.** A rating $y \in \set{1, \dots, L}$ is the same information as $L - 1$ yes-or-no
answers, one per cut-point: "is the rating above $\ell$?" for $\ell = 1, \dots, L - 1$. This is the reduction behind
binary decomposition <Cite id="frank2001" />, behind the up-to-$k$ codes of structured
<NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink>, in which the number of bits on which two ratings differ
is exactly their distance <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />, and behind the <Gloss name="clm">cumulative link
model</Gloss>, which gives each cut-point the probability $P(y \le \ell) = \sigma(\theta_\ell - s)$ from one score $s$
<Cite id="mccullagh1980" />. A model's level probabilities $\pivec$ give each cut-point a probability through the
cumulative sum $F(\ell) = \sum_{k \le \ell} \pi_k$.

**Sum a proper reward over the cut-points.** Scoring each cut-point's yes-or-no answer with the Brier score and adding
them gives the ranked probability score <Cite id="epstein1969" />:

$$
\mathrm{RPS}(\pivec, y) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1} \bigl(F(\ell) - \indicator[y \le \ell]\bigr)^2 .
$$

Each term is a proper score for its own event, and the true distribution makes every term's expectation best at once,
so the sum is proper: a reward of $-\mathrm{RPS}$ is maximised in expectation by the true level probabilities. It is
also order-aware. Moving probability from the truth to a level $d$ steps away changes $F$ at $d$ cut-points, so the
penalty grows with the distance of the mistake.

**The same score as an expectation over sampled answers.** For independent draws $X, X'$ from $\pivec$ on the levels
$1, \dots, L$,

$$
\mathrm{RPS}(\pivec, y) = \expect\abs{X - y} - \tfrac12 \expect\abs{X - X'} ,
$$

the kernel form of the <Gloss name="crps">continuous ranked probability score</Gloss> restricted to integer levels
<Cite id="gneiting2007" />. That gives a reward for reinforcement learning that needs neither the labels' full
distribution nor the cumulative sums: sample two answers $a, a'$ from the model, ask a checker how far $a$ is from the
truth, and reward

$$
R = -\abs{a - y} + \tfrac12 \abs{a - a'} .
$$

Its expectation is $-\mathrm{RPS}$. The first term rewards answers close to the truth. The second rewards spread, and
plays the part that $-\norm{\pivec}^2$ plays in $2c - \norm{\pivec}^2$: without it, the best policy puts all its
probability on the median level, which is the ordinal version of a correctness reward making a model overconfident.

<OrdinalReward />

**Rewarding the expected level is not enough.** Clients often read a rating as its expected level
$\sum_\ell \ell\, \pi_\ell$, a Likert-style index. A reward of $-(\sum_\ell \ell \pi_\ell - y)^2$ is proper for the
mean only: every distribution with the right mean scores equally well, so the spread of $\pivec$ is left unconstrained
and its probabilities are not calibrated. The ranked probability score calibrates the whole distribution, and the
expected level read from it is then the right mean as well.

**Where the order is enforced.** The reward makes training order-aware; the readout decides whether the model can
express the order. The level codes of an ordinary score question can put probability on 1 and 5 with nothing between.
Reading the $L - 1$ cut-point questions from one shared score, as the cumulative link model and CORAL do
<Cite id="cao2020coral" />, guarantees that $P(y > \ell)$ falls as $\ell$ rises, and the cut-point questions share one
read of the state like any other questions (see <NoteLink to="shared-prefix-decision-batching" />).

| feedback available | reward | proper | sensitive to order |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| the true level | $-\log \pi_y$ or the Brier score | yes | no |
| the true level | $-\mathrm{RPS}(\pivec, y)$ | yes | yes |
| right or wrong for a sampled level | $2c - \norm{\pivec}^2$ | yes | no |
| distance $\abs{a - y}$ for sampled levels $a, a'$ | $-\abs{a - y} + \tfrac12 \abs{a - a'}$ | yes, in expectation | yes |
| distance for one sampled level | $-\abs{a - y}$ | no; rewards a point mass at the median | yes |
| the true level, scored on the expected level | $-(\sum_\ell \ell \pi_\ell - y)^2$ | for the mean only | yes |

## Assumptions

- **Calibration is in expectation over the training distribution.** A proper reward makes $\pivec = \pvec$ optimal
  on the prompts it is trained on. On new tasks the probabilities are calibrated only as far as the model generalises.
- **The model can represent $\pvec$.** A restricted model settles on the proper score's best approximation, which is
  calibrated only on average.
- **The checker is right.** A checker that errs at rate $\varepsilon$ calibrates $\pivec$ to the checker's verdicts,
  not to the truth.

## Pitfalls

- **Correctness-only rewards.** Training a decision model with an RLVR-style reward makes it overconfident by design.
- **Scoring the mixture of samples.** Rewarding a group of samples by a proper score of their average pays the samples
  for disagreeing; score each sample's own distribution instead <Cite id="gao2026openjev" />.
- **Per-type calibration.** One reward over a mix of question types can leave each type miscalibrated in a different
  direction. Porcedda found Jev's choice answers less calibrated than its yes-or-no and score answers on items whose
  true probability is known <Cite id="porcedda2026" />, and Deußer and co-authors found Jev's yes-or-no probabilities
  ranked examples well but sat poorly against a 0.5 threshold <Cite id="deusser2026" />.
- **Calling a decoding trick RLCD.** A public checkpoint named "Qwen-2.5-1B-RLCD" demonstrates answer-token scoring
  and describes no reinforcement learning <Cite id="rogge2026" />. The name says nothing about training.
