<Definition>
A **failure mode** of a <NoteLink to="physics-informed-neural-networks">physics-informed network</NoteLink> is a
mechanism by which training stops at a network that is far from the solution although a network close to it exists.
Three are well documented: **spectral bias**, the tendency of <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> to fit low frequencies first, which stalls on oscillatory, sharp or multiscale solutions
<Cite id="rahaman2019" />; an **ill-conditioned loss**, because the differential operator in the residual amplifies high
frequencies <Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" /> <Cite id="rathore2024" />; and **causality violation**, the fitting of the
residual at late times before the solution at early times is right <Cite id="wang2024causality" />. Fourier-feature
inputs, curriculum training and causal weights in time are the matching remedies.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A classical time-stepping solver cannot get the late solution right before the early one: it computes $u(t + \Delta t)$
from $u(t)$. A physics-informed network has no such order. It fits a function on the whole space-time domain at once,
and the loss is an average over collocation points that is indifferent to where the error sits. Nothing in the loss
stops the network from satisfying the equation at late times by a smooth function unrelated to the initial profile,
and a smooth function is exactly what gradient descent finds first.

The convection equation $u_t + \beta u_x = 0$ shows all three mechanisms at once. Its solution is the initial wave
moved at speed $\beta$, so with $u(x, 0) = \sin x$ on $[0, 2\pi] \times [0, 1]$ it is $\sin(x - \beta t)$, which
oscillates $\beta / 2\pi$ times in time across the domain. The function $u \equiv 0$, or any function constant along
the characteristics of a slower wave, makes the residual small in large parts of the domain. As $\beta$ grows the
target becomes higher in frequency, the residual's derivatives grow in proportion to $\beta$, and training fails:
Krishnapriyan et al. report a relative error of almost 100% for $\beta > 10$ with a standard setup
<Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" />. A network that can represent the solution exists; the optimiser does not reach it.

## Details

**Spectral bias.** Gradient descent on a network learns a target's Fourier components at rates that fall with frequency
<Cite id="rahaman2019" />. In the <NoteLink to="neural-tangent-kernel">neural tangent kernel</NoteLink> picture, the
kernel's eigenvalues set the rate of each eigendirection, and the eigenfunctions with large eigenvalues are smooth
<Cite id="wang2022ntk" />. For a physics-informed network the bias acts twice: once on the solution, and once on the
residual, whose operator multiplies high-frequency components by large factors.
<NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">Random Fourier features</NoteLink> feed the network
$\gamma(\xvec) = \paren{\cos \Bmat\xvec, \sin \Bmat\xvec}$ with entries of $\Bmat$ drawn from
$\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, which shifts the kernel's spectrum towards the frequency $\sigma$ <Cite id="tancik2020" />
<Cite id="wang2021fourier" />. The scale $\sigma$ must roughly match the solution: too small gives blurred fits, too
large gives noisy ones, and $\sigma$ between 1 and 10 is the current recommendation for inputs scaled to unit range
<Cite id="wang2023expert" />. The note on <NoteLink to="implicit-neural-representation">implicit neural
representations</NoteLink> treats the same bias in signal fitting.

**Ill-conditioning.** The residual applies a differential operator to the network. An operator such as
$-\mathrm{d}^2/\mathrm{d}x^2$ multiplies the mode $\sin k\pi x$ by $(k\pi)^2$, so its
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> over the first $K$ modes is $K^2$, and a least-squares loss
squares it. Gradient descent needs a number of steps that grows with the condition number of the loss's Hessian, so the
residual term converges slowly along its smallest-curvature directions while it races along the largest. Loss landscapes
of the convection problem are smooth at $\beta = 1$ and rugged at large $\beta$, with the optimiser stuck at a high loss
<Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" />. A 2024 analysis traced the difficulty to the operator's conditioning and found that
<NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> followed by <Gloss name="l-bfgs" /> outperforms either alone, the practice of the
original paper <Cite id="rathore2024" /> <Cite id="raissi2019" />.

**Causality violation.** Write the residual loss as an average over $M$ consecutive time bins,
$L_r = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M L_r^i$. Standard training lowers every $L_r^i$ at once, and often the late ones first,
because a smooth function of $(x, t)$ satisfies the equation easily where it has not yet been forced to match the
initial profile <Cite id="wang2024causality" />. Causal training weights the bins by how well the earlier ones are
already fitted,

$$
L_r = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} w_i L_r^i, \qquad w_i = \exp\paren{-\epsilon \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} L_r^k},
$$

with the weights held constant in the gradient and $\epsilon > 0$ a slope parameter. The bin $i$ is trained only once
the earlier bins' residuals are small, which restores the order in which a time-stepping solver works. A too small
$\epsilon$ leaves training unchanged; a too large one stalls it at the first bins, and $\epsilon$ is chosen so that all
weights reach 1 by the end of training <Cite id="wang2023expert" />.

**Curriculum and time-marching.** Curriculum regularisation trains first on an easy version of the equation, such as
$\beta = 1$, and warm-starts each harder version from the previous network. On the convection equation it lowered the
error from 96% to 5.3% at $\beta = 30$ and from 75% to 1.0% at $\beta = 20$ <Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" />.
Sequence-to-sequence training, or time-marching, splits $[0, T]$ into windows and trains one window at a time, each
starting from the previous window's final profile <Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" />. Both reduce the problem the optimiser
faces at any moment. The two last remedies are about the optimisation, not the model: the same network represents the
solution in all cases.

## Worked example

**Causal weights.** Take $M = 4$ bins and $\epsilon = 10$. Early in training the bins' residual losses are
$L_r = (0.3, 0.2, 0.01, 0.01)$: the late bins already have small residuals, the typical sign of causality violation.
The cumulative sums before each bin are $0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.51$, so

$$
w = \paren{e^{0}, e^{-3}, e^{-5}, e^{-5.1}} = (1, 0.050, 0.0067, 0.0061).
$$

The weighted residual is almost entirely the first bin's, so the gradient works on the early times and ignores the
late ones however small their residual. Later, with $L_r = (0.01, 0.01, 0.01, 0.01)$, the cumulative sums are
$0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03$ and the weights are $(1, 0.905, 0.819, 0.741)$: every bin trains.

**Conditioning.** Restrict $-u''$ on $[0, 1]$ to the modes $\sin k\pi x$ for $k = 1, \dots, 10$. The operator's
eigenvalues run from $\pi^2 \approx 9.9$ to $(10\pi)^2 \approx 987$, a condition number of 100. The squared residual
has Hessian proportional to the operator's square, with condition number $10^4$. Gradient descent with a step size
safe for the stiffest mode shrinks the error in the smoothest mode by a factor of only about $1 - 10^{-4}$ per step,
so that mode needs on the order of $10^4$ steps to converge.

## Simulation

The figure trains a network on the convection equation with the exact end values, $\sin(-\beta t)$ at both ends, in
place of the periodic condition of the original study. Its defaults are cheap: two
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">hidden layers</NoteLink> of 16 hyperbolic tangent units, 128 collocation points,
300 Adam steps at learning rate 0.005 and 100 L-BFGS steps, about two seconds of training.

<ConvectionFailure />

With one seed and the defaults, the plain network ends as follows.

| $\beta$ | 1 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relative error | 3.3% | 0.8% | 3.5% | 19% | 81% |

The failure is not a gradual loss of accuracy: between $\beta = 5$ and $\beta = 20$ the error goes from a few percent to
nearly the whole solution. In this short budget the remedies help only partly. At $\beta = 10$, Fourier features with
$\sigma = 2$ bring the error to 10% and causal weights with $\epsilon = 10$ to 12%, while a curriculum from $\beta = 1$
over 70% of the Adam steps leaves it at 31%.

A larger budget moves the threshold and lets the remedies work. With the wide network (32 units), 256 points, 1000 Adam
and 300 L-BFGS steps, about twenty seconds per run, the plain network ends as follows.

| $\beta$ | 1 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 30 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relative error | 0.89% | 0.39% | 1.5% | 5.9% | 47% | 84% |

At $\beta = 20$ the three remedies, each alone, end at 3.7% (Fourier features), 3.4% (causal weights) and 5.0% (a
curriculum). At $\beta = 30$ none of them succeeds within this budget: 95%, 57% and 77%. The published fixes train for
tens of thousands of steps and combine the remedies <Cite id="wang2023expert" />.

The longer plain run at $\beta = 20$ shows causality violation directly. After 200 steps its mean squared residual is
$7.6 \times 10^{-2}$ at $t = 0$ and $1.7 \times 10^{-4}$ at $t = 0.75$, while its error is about 100% at every time.
At the end its error grows along time, from 4% at $t = 0$ to 57% at $t = 0.75$ and 104% at $t = 1$, while its residual
at $t = 1$ is $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, the smallest anywhere: the network obeys the equation at late times for the wrong
wave. With causal weights the late bins' weights are near zero after 200 steps (0.24, 0.05, then 0.00 from the fourth
bin) and all reach about 1 by step 1000; the final error is at most 6% at every time.

## Assumptions

- The failures are failures of optimisation. They are diagnosed against a reference solution, since the loss can be
  small while the error is large.
- The remedies assume structure in the problem: Fourier features assume a known range of frequencies, causal weights a
  time direction, a curriculum a family of easier problems connected to the target one.
- The numbers above come from short runs on one seed. The threshold $\beta$ at which training fails moves with the
  network, the number of points and the training budget.

## Pitfalls

- **Taking a small loss as success.** A network that has fitted the residual at late times for the wrong wave has a
  small loss and a large error. The residual by time, and a reference solution where one exists, show it.
- **A Fourier scale that does not match.** Features with $\sigma$ far above the solution's frequencies add noise and can
  make training worse; in the longer runs, $\sigma = 2$ fixes $\beta = 20$ and $\sigma = 5$ does not fix $\beta = 30$.
- **Causal weights that never open.** With a large $\epsilon$ and a network that cannot drive the early residual low,
  the late bins keep weight near zero for the whole run. The weights should all be near 1 at the end.
- **Blaming the architecture.** Krishnapriyan et al. showed that the same network solves the convection problem at
  large $\beta$ when trained by a curriculum <Cite id="krishnapriyan2021" />. Widening or deepening the network does not
  address an optimisation failure.
- **Optimising with Adam alone.** On an ill-conditioned residual, <NoteLink to="first-order-methods">first-order
  methods</NoteLink> stall long before a quasi-Newton phase would converge <Cite id="rathore2024" />.
