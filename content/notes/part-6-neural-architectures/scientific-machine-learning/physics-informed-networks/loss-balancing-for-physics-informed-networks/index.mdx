<Definition>
**Loss balancing** sets the weights of the terms of a
<NoteLink to="physics-informed-neural-networks">physics-informed loss</NoteLink> during training, so that the
equation's residual, the initial and boundary conditions and the data all shape the network. The common schemes weight
each term inversely to the norm of its gradient <Cite id="wang2021pinn" /> <Cite id="wang2023expert" />, inversely to
the trace of its <Gloss name="ntk">neural tangent kernel</Gloss> <Cite id="wang2022ntk" />, or by trainable weights
<Cite id="mcclenny2023" />. **Residual-based adaptive sampling** is the companion fix inside the residual term: it
moves the collocation points to where the residual is large <Cite id="wu2023rad" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A physics-informed loss is a sum of terms,

$$
L(\thetavec) = \lambda_r L_r(\thetavec) + \lambda_b L_b(\thetavec) + \lambda_0 L_0(\thetavec) + \lambda_d L_d(\thetavec),
$$

the mean squared residual $L_r$ at the collocation points, the mean squared misfits $L_b$ and $L_0$ at the boundary and
initial points, and the misfit $L_d$ to observations, with weights $\lambda \ge 0$. A gradient step moves the
parameters $\thetavec$ along $\sum_i \lambda_i \nabla_{\thetavec} L_i$. When one gradient is a thousand times longer
than another, the step serves the long one and the short one barely moves.

The residual's gradient is usually the long one. The residual contains derivatives of the network, and each derivative
of a solution with a length scale $\ell$ brings a factor of about $1/\ell$. A solution $\sin(k\pi x)$ has a second
derivative of size $(k\pi)^2$, so for $k = 4$ the residual of Poisson's equation is about 160 times the solution and its
squared mean about $2.5 \times 10^4$ times. Training then finds a network that obeys the equation and ignores the
conditions: a different member of the equation's solution family. Wang, Teng and Perdikaris traced this failure to the
imbalance of the back-propagated gradients <Cite id="wang2021pinn" />.

Non-dimensionalising the equation, as the physics-informed network note does for the oscillator, removes the imbalance
that comes from units. It cannot remove the imbalance that comes from the solution's own scales, which are unknown
before the solution is. A second, separate failure lives inside the residual term: a fixed set of collocation points
can miss a narrow region where the solution changes fast, so the residual is small at every point and large between
them.

## Mechanism

**Gradient-norm weights.** Every $f$ steps, compute the gradient norm of each term, $g_i = \norm{\nabla_{\thetavec}
L_i(\thetavec)}_2$, and set the target weights

$$
\hat\lambda_i = \frac{\sum_j g_j}{g_i},
$$

so that every weighted term has the same gradient norm, $\hat\lambda_i g_i = \sum_j g_j$. The weights follow the
targets by a moving average,

$$
\lambda_i \leftarrow \alpha \lambda_i + (1 - \alpha)\hat\lambda_i ,
$$

with $\alpha = 0.9$, and the weights are constants in the gradient: no derivative flows through them. The recommended
defaults are an update every $f = 1000$ steps of a run of tens of thousands <Cite id="wang2023expert" />. The 2021
version of the scheme used the largest entry of the residual's gradient over the mean absolute entry of each other
term's gradient as the target <Cite id="wang2021pinn" />. Each update costs one extra backward pass per term.

**Neural tangent kernel weights.** The neural tangent kernel of a term, $K_i = \Jmat_i \Jmat_i^\top$ with $\Jmat_i$ the
Jacobian of the term's pointwise values (residuals or misfits) in $\thetavec$, governs how fast
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> shrinks that term: in the linearised regime each eigenvalue
is a convergence rate <Cite id="wang2022ntk" />. The weights
$\hat\lambda_i = \sum_j \operatorname{tr} K_j / \operatorname{tr} K_i$ equalise the terms' average rates. The trace
needs only the diagonal, $\operatorname{tr} K_i = \sum_k \norm{\nabla_{\thetavec} r_k}^2$ over the term's points, which
is one gradient per point. The weights are steadier than the gradient-norm ones and more expensive
<Cite id="wang2023expert" />.

**Learned weights.** Self-adaptive networks give every training point its own weight and train the weights by gradient
ascent while the network trains by descent, so points with large residuals gain weight <Cite id="mcclenny2023" />.
Uncertainty weighting, borrowed from <NoteLink to="multi-task-learning">multi-task learning</NoteLink>, writes each
term as $L_i / (2\sigma_i^2) + \log \sigma_i$ and learns $\sigma_i$ <Cite id="kendall2018" />.

**Residual-based adaptive sampling.** Every few hundred steps, evaluate the residual on a large pool of candidate
points and draw the new collocation set with probability

$$
p(\xvec) \propto \frac{\abs{r(\xvec)}^k}{\mathbb{E}\abs{r}^k} + c ,
$$

with the expectation estimated by the pool's mean. The exponent $k$ sharpens the preference for large residuals, and
the offset $c$ keeps a uniform floor so the rest of the domain stays covered; $k = 1$, $c = 1$ is the recommended
default <Cite id="wu2023rad" />. The draw is a form of <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink>
of the residual integral.

## Worked example

**Weights.** At the first step of the figure's plain run, the residual's gradient has norm $g_r = 2450$ and the boundary
term's has norm $g_b = 6.8$. The targets are

$$
\hat\lambda_r = \frac{2450 + 6.8}{2450} = 1.003, \qquad \hat\lambda_b = \frac{2450 + 6.8}{6.8} = 361.3 .
$$

Starting from $\lambda_r = \lambda_b = 1$, the first moving-average update gives
$\lambda_b = 0.9 \times 1 + 0.1 \times 361.3 = 37.0$ and leaves $\lambda_r \approx 1.000$. The weighted boundary
gradient now has norm $37.0 \times 6.8 = 252$, a tenth of the residual's rather than a three-hundred-and-sixtieth. If
the norms stayed put, after $m$ updates the weight would be $361.3 - 360.3 \times 0.9^m$, and after 10 updates 236.

**Sampling.** A pool of four candidates has residuals $\abs{r} = 0.1, 0.1, 0.2, 1.6$, with mean 0.5. With $k = 1$ and
$c = 1$ the unnormalised probabilities are $0.1/0.5 + 1 = 1.2$, then 1.2, 1.4 and $1.6/0.5 + 1 = 4.2$. They sum to 8,
so the probabilities are 0.15, 0.15, 0.175 and 0.525. The point with the largest residual is drawn three and a half
times as often as each of the two smallest, and no candidate has probability zero. With $c = 0$ the last candidate's
probability would be 0.8 and the two smallest 0.05 each.

## Simulation

The figure solves $-u'' = f$ on $[0, 1]$ whose solution $u = \tanh((x - 1/2)/\varepsilon)$ has a layer of width about
$\varepsilon$ at the centre, with the two end values as boundary conditions. The forcing is
$f = 2u(1 - u^2)/\varepsilon^2$, so for $\varepsilon = 0.01$ the residual's scale is $2/\varepsilon^2 = 2 \times 10^4$:
the problem has the gradient imbalance and a feature narrower than the spacing of 24 collocation points. A plain
network trains next to one with either fix or both.

<BalancingAndResampling />

With the defaults ($\varepsilon = 0.01$, 24 points, a network of two <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">hidden
layers</NoteLink> of 20 units, 800 <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> steps at <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning
rate</Gloss> 0.005 with weight updates every 50 steps and redraws every 100, then 100 <Gloss name="l-bfgs" /> steps,
about a second per network), the four combinations end as follows.

| Weights | Points | Relative error | Boundary misfit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| fixed, $\lambda_b = 1$ | fixed | 42% | 0.42 |
| fixed, $\lambda_b = 1$ | resampled | 18% | 0.17 |
| gradient norm | fixed | 39% | $4 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| gradient norm | resampled | 4.0% | $1 \times 10^{-7}$ |

The two fixes repair different things. The weights make the boundary hold, but with fixed points the network still
cannot see the layer. Resampling moves points into the layer, but without the weights the network solves the equation
for the wrong end values. Only together do they come near the solution. A hand-set boundary weight does no better than
the plain run: $\lambda_b = 100$ gives 49% and $\lambda_b = 10^4$ gives 39%, because the layer is still unresolved. A
longer run (2000 Adam steps with updates every 100 and redraws every 200, then 300 L-BFGS steps) takes the combined
fix to 0.43% and leaves the other three between 33% and 45%.

## Cost

Gradient-norm weights add one backward pass per term at each update, which is negligible when updates are every 100 to
1000 steps. NTK weights need one gradient per point at each update, the cost of a full Jacobian's diagonal. Learned
weights add one parameter per term or per point and an ascent step. Adaptive sampling evaluates the residual on the
pool at each redraw, a forward pass with derivatives but no backward pass, typically on ten times the collocation set.

## Trade-offs

- **Automatic against tuned.** Adaptive weights replace a <NoteLink to="grid-search">grid search</NoteLink> over
  $\lambda$, which has no validation set when the solution is unknown <Cite id="wang2023expert" />. They also change the
  objective during training, so a quasi-Newton phase needs the weights frozen first, as the figure does before L-BFGS.
- **Gradient norms against kernel traces.** The gradient-norm weights are cheap and noisy under random collocation
  batches; the NTK weights are smoother and cost a gradient per point <Cite id="wang2023expert" />.
- **Concentration against coverage.** A large $k$ or a small $c$ puts nearly every point where the residual is large
  now and leaves the rest of the domain unconstrained. Which side wins depends on the problem and the budget: with
  balanced weights on the figure's single layer, $k = 2$ and $c = 0$ ended at 2.7% against 4.0% for $k = 1$, $c = 1$ in
  the default run, and at 1.3% against 0.43% in the longer run.

## Pitfalls

- **Exploding weights.** A satisfied term has a vanishing gradient, so its target $\sum_j g_j / g_i$ grows without
  bound. In the figure the boundary weight climbs past $2 \times 10^7$. Large weights are harmless while the term stays
  satisfied and make the loss ill-conditioned for a second-order optimiser; capping the weights or freezing them is
  common.
- **Balancing an unscaled equation.** Weights repair imbalance between terms, not within one. A residual whose own
  parts differ by orders of magnitude, such as $\omega_0^2 x$ against $x''$, still needs non-dimensionalisation.
- **Reading the loss across runs.** With adaptive weights the loss is a different function at every update, so its
  value cannot be compared between runs or between steps far apart. The unweighted terms, and the error against a
  reference, can.
- **Resampling the boundary away.** Residual-based sampling moves the collocation points only. The condition points
  keep their own fixed sets.
