<Definition>
A **hard constraint** builds the initial and boundary conditions of a differential equation into the
<NoteLink to="physics-informed-neural-networks">physics-informed network</NoteLink> itself. The network's output is
replaced by a **trial function**

$$
u_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = g(\xvec) + d(\xvec)\, N_{\thetavec}(\xvec),
$$

where $N_{\thetavec}$ is a neural network with parameters $\thetavec$, the **lift** $g$ is a fixed function that meets
every condition, and the **distance function** $d$ is a fixed function that is zero where the conditions are imposed
and positive elsewhere. The trial function meets the conditions for every $\thetavec$, so the loss keeps only the
residual of the equation and any data terms <Cite id="lagaris1998" /> <Cite id="sukumar2022" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The standard physics-informed loss imposes the conditions softly. It adds the mean squared misfit to the initial values
and to the boundary values, each multiplied by a weight $\lambda$, to the mean squared residual of the equation. The
minimiser of that sum is a compromise. Lowering the residual and lowering the misfit pull the parameters in different
directions, and the minimiser misses the conditions by an amount that shrinks only as $\lambda$ grows. The misfit is
not merely a small inaccuracy at the edge of the domain. A linear equation with homogeneous conditions has a family of
solutions, and the conditions choose the member. A network that misses the conditions solves the equation for a
different member, so the error spreads through the whole domain.

The weights are also new hyperparameters. Raising $\lambda$ makes the misfit smaller and the loss harder to optimise:
the residual and condition terms then differ in scale, and the gradient of the larger term dominates the update. The
<NoteLink to="loss-balancing-for-physics-informed-networks">loss-balancing</NoteLink> note treats that difficulty. A
hard constraint removes the condition terms, and with them both the compromise and the weights.

## Mechanism

**The trial function.** At a point $\xvec$ where a condition $u = h$ is imposed, $d(\xvec) = 0$ and
$g(\xvec) = h(\xvec)$, so $u_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = h(\xvec)$ whatever the network outputs. Inside the domain $d > 0$, and
the network supplies the correction $d \, N_{\thetavec}$ that the residual asks for. The lift and the distance are
chosen once, by hand, and are not trained.

**An interval in space and time.** For an equation in $u(x, t)$ on $[a, b] \times [t_0, t_1]$ with initial profile
$u(x, t_0) = u_0(x)$ and end values $u(a, t) = g_a(t)$, $u(b, t) = g_b(t)$, one choice is

$$
g(x, t) = u_0(x) + \frac{b - x}{b - a}\,\paren{g_a(t) - g_a(t_0)} + \frac{x - a}{b - a}\,\paren{g_b(t) - g_b(t_0)},
\qquad d(x, t) = \frac{4\,(x - a)(b - x)\,(t - t_0)}{(b - a)^2\,(t_1 - t_0)}.
$$

At $t = t_0$ the two brackets vanish and $g = u_0$. At $x = a$ the second fraction vanishes and
$g = u_0(a) + g_a(t) - g_a(t_0) = g_a(t)$, provided the data are compatible at the corner, $u_0(a) = g_a(t_0)$. The
distance $d$ vanishes on the three sides where conditions hold and is open on the final time $t = t_1$, where nothing is
imposed. The factor in front of $d$ scales it to a largest value of 1.

**Other conditions.** A second-order equation in time with $u(t_0) = u_0$ and $u'(t_0) = v_0$ uses
$g = u_0 + v_0 (t - t_0)$ and $d = (t - t_0)^2$: the squared factor makes $d$ and its derivative vanish at $t_0$, so
both conditions hold. The physics-informed network note uses this trial function for the oscillator
<Cite id="lagaris1998" />. A periodic condition with period $P$ is met exactly by feeding the network
$\paren{\cos(2\pi x / P), \sin(2\pi x / P)}$ in place of $x$, since every function of those two features is periodic
<Cite id="wang2023expert" />. On an irregular domain, $d$ is an approximate distance function built from the boundary's
pieces, and inhomogeneous, Neumann and Robin conditions are handled by transfinite interpolation of the boundary data
<Cite id="sukumar2022" />.

**The residual.** The derivatives in the residual are taken of the whole trial function, by
<NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation">automatic differentiation</NoteLink>, exactly as for an unconstrained network.
For a second derivative in $x$ the product rule gives

$$
\partial_x^2 u_{\thetavec} = \partial_x^2 g + (\partial_x^2 d)\, N_{\thetavec} + 2\, (\partial_x d)\, \partial_x N_{\thetavec}
  + d\, \partial_x^2 N_{\thetavec}.
$$

The network's own derivatives are damped by $d$ near the boundary. The loss is

$$
L(\thetavec) = \frac{1}{N_r} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \mathcal{N}[u_{\thetavec}](\xvec_i)^2
  + \frac{\lambda_d}{N_d} \sum_{l=1}^{N_d} \paren{u_{\thetavec}(\xvec_l) - y_l}^2 ,
$$

the residual at $N_r$ collocation points $\xvec_i$ plus, when there are observations $(\xvec_l, y_l)$, the data term.
Without observations only the residual remains.

## Worked example

Take $u'(x) = u(x)$ on $[0, 1]$ with $u(0) = 1$, whose solution is $e^x$. Use the residual $r = u' - u$ at the three
points $x = 0, \tfrac12, 1$ and, to keep the arithmetic by hand, the smallest trial families: straight lines. The loss
is a sum of squares rather than a mean, which only rescales it.

**Hard.** The trial function $u = 1 + a x$ has lift $g = 1$ and distance $d = x$, and meets $u(0) = 1$ for every slope
$a$. Its residual is $r(x) = a - (1 + a x) = a(1 - x) - 1$:

| $x$ | $r(x)$ |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $a - 1$ |
| 1/2 | $a/2 - 1$ |
| 1 | $-1$ |

The loss is $L(a) = (a - 1)^2 + (a/2 - 1)^2 + 1$. Setting $L'(a) = 2(a - 1) + (a/2 - 1) = 2.5a - 3$ to zero gives
$a = 1.2$. The fit is $u = 1 + 1.2x$: exact at $x = 0$, and $u(1) = 2.2$ against $e \approx 2.718$, the best a straight
line through $(0, 1)$ does for this residual.

**Soft.** The trial function $u = c_0 + c_1 x$ leaves both coefficients free, and the condition becomes the penalty
$\lambda (c_0 - 1)^2$. The residual is $r(x) = c_1 (1 - x) - c_0$, so

$$
L(c_0, c_1) = (c_1 - c_0)^2 + (c_1/2 - c_0)^2 + c_0^2 + \lambda (c_0 - 1)^2 .
$$

Setting $\partial L / \partial c_1 = 2.5 c_1 - 3 c_0$ to zero gives $c_1 = 1.2 c_0$. Setting $\partial L / \partial c_0$
to zero gives $3c_0 + \lambda c_0 - 1.5 c_1 = \lambda$, and substituting $c_1 = 1.2 c_0$ gives

$$
c_0 = \frac{\lambda}{1.2 + \lambda}.
$$

| $\lambda$ | $c_0 = u(0)$ | misfit $1 - c_0$ | $u(1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 0.455 | 0.545 | 1.00 |
| 10 | 0.893 | 0.107 | 1.96 |
| 100 | 0.988 | 0.012 | 2.17 |

With $\lambda = 0$ the minimiser is $u = 0$, the trivial solution of the equation. With $\lambda = 1$ the line misses
the condition by more than half its value. The soft fit approaches the hard one only as $\lambda \to \infty$, which is
the behaviour of every quadratic <NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">penalty method</NoteLink>. The hard trial
function reaches the limit at every $\lambda$ because the constraint is no longer in the loss.

## Simulation

The figure solves the <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink> $u_t = \kappa u_{xx}$ on
$[-1, 1] \times [0, 1]$ from $u(x, 0) = \sin \pi x$ with $u(\pm 1, t) = 0$, whose solution is
$e^{-\kappa \pi^2 t} \sin \pi x$. The soft network penalises the initial and boundary misfits with weight 1. The hard
network is $u = \sin \pi x + t(1 - x^2) N_{\thetavec}(x, t)$: the lift $\sin \pi x$ meets the initial profile and both
end values, and the distance $t(1 - x^2)$ vanishes on the initial line and at both ends and peaks at 1. Both networks
have two <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">hidden layers</NoteLink> of 12 hyperbolic tangent units and start from the
same weights.

With the defaults ($\kappa = 0.1$, 64 collocation points, 300 <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> steps at
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> 0.005, then 100 <Gloss name="l-bfgs" /> steps, about a second per
network), the run ends as follows.

| Network | Error after 100 steps | Error at the end | Largest condition misfit at the end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soft conditions | 68% | 1.5% | $3.7 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| Hard conditions | 6.0% | 0.20% | $1.2 \times 10^{-16}$ |

The error is the relative $L_2$ error on a grid of $41 \times 21$ points. The soft network's error map is largest near
$t = 0$ and the two ends, where it trades the conditions against the residual. The hard network's misfit stays at
rounding level from the first step, and its error is about seven times smaller at the end. A longer run, with two
hidden layers of 20 units, 128 points, 800 Adam and 200 L-BFGS steps, ends at 0.38% for the soft network and 0.067%
for the hard one, with a soft misfit of $1.0 \times 10^{-2}$: the hard network stays about six times more accurate.

## Cost

A hard constraint makes each step slightly cheaper: the condition terms and their points disappear, and evaluating $g$
and $d$ is negligible next to the network. The residual's derivatives include those of $g$ and $d$, which automatic
differentiation supplies with no extra code. The real cost is human: $g$ and $d$ must be designed for each geometry and
each kind of condition, and a poor design slows training.

## Trade-offs

- **Exactness against flexibility.** A hard constraint is exact and removes the condition weights. A soft penalty
  works for any geometry and any condition without design work, and it is the right choice when the conditions are
  themselves measured with noise and should be fitted rather than imposed.
- **The scale of $d$.** The network has to supply $(u - g)/d$, which is large where $d$ is small. On Poisson's
  equation $-u'' = (4\pi)^2 \sin 4\pi x$ on $[0, 1]$ with $u = \sin 4\pi x + x$, the same network trained with
  $d = x(1 - x)$, which peaks at 0.25, ended with a 530% error, and with $d = 4x(1 - x)$, which peaks at 1, with 0.1%
  (64 collocation points, 2000 Adam and 300 L-BFGS steps). Normalising $d$ to a largest value near 1 keeps the
  network's target of order one.
- **Simple domains only, in practice.** Intervals, rectangles, discs and periodic boxes have closed-form $g$ and $d$.
  Irregular geometries need approximate distance functions <Cite id="sukumar2022" />, and many practitioners fall back
  on soft penalties there. Current practice imposes periodic conditions exactly by the Fourier embedding and keeps the
  remaining conditions soft with adaptive weights <Cite id="wang2023expert" />.

## Pitfalls

- **A distance with zero slope on the boundary.** With $d = (x - a)^2$ the derivative of $d \, N_{\thetavec}$ also
  vanishes at $x = a$, so the trial function satisfies $u_x(a) = g_x(a)$ as well: a second condition that the problem
  never asked for. A first-order zero, such as $x - a$, imposes the value only.
- **Incompatible data at a corner.** The lift exists only if the initial profile and the end values agree where they
  meet, $u_0(a) = g_a(t_0)$. Incompatible data need a smoothed corner or a soft penalty.
- **Reading a zero misfit as a correct solution.** The conditions hold by construction, so their misfit carries no
  information. Training progress shows in the residual, and accuracy shows only against a reference solution.
- **Forgetting the derivatives of $g$ and $d$.** A hand-written residual that differentiates $N_{\thetavec}$ instead of
  the whole trial function solves a different equation. Differentiating the trial function as a whole avoids it.
