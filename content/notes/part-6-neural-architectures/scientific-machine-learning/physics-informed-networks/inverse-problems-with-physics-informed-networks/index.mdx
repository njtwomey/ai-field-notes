<Definition>
In an **inverse problem** a differential equation $\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}[u] = 0$ is known up to a vector of
coefficients $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, and observations $y_l \approx u(\xvec_l)$ of its solution are given. A
<NoteLink to="physics-informed-neural-networks">physics-informed network</NoteLink> solves it by making
$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ trainable next to the network's parameters $\thetavec$ and minimising one loss: the mean squared
residual $\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}[u_{\thetavec}]$ at collocation points plus the mean squared misfit to the
observations and to any known conditions <Cite id="raissi2019" />. The fit returns an estimate of the coefficients and
a solution consistent with them.
</Definition>

## Problem

A coefficient of a physical law is often what an experiment is for: the viscosity of a fluid, the damping of a
structure, the diffusivity of a material. The solution is observed at a few places and times, with noise, and the
coefficient is not observed at all. The classical approach nests two loops. An outer optimiser proposes a value of the
coefficient; an inner solver computes the solution for that value; the misfit to the data scores the proposal. Every
outer step costs a full forward solve, and the gradient of the misfit with respect to the coefficient needs either
<Gloss name="finite-difference">finite differences</Gloss> over solves or an adjoint equation derived for the problem.

A physics-informed network removes the inner loop. The network represents the solution, the coefficient is one more
parameter, and one gradient method fits both together. The same code solves the forward problem when the coefficient is
fixed and the inverse problem when it is free.

## Mechanism

Take an equation with coefficients $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, collocation points $\xvec_i$, $i = 1, \dots, N_r$, and
observations $(\xvec_l, y_l)$, $l = 1, \dots, N_d$. The loss is

$$
L(\thetavec, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}[u_{\thetavec}](\xvec_i)^2
  + \frac{\lambda_d}{N_d}\sum_{l=1}^{N_d} \paren{u_{\thetavec}(\xvec_l) - y_l}^2 + L_{\text{conditions}}(\thetavec),
$$

where $L_{\text{conditions}}$ holds the misfits to the initial and boundary values that are known, and $\lambda_d$
weights the data. The data term pins the network to the observations. The residual term forces the network, between
the observations, to be a solution of the equation for the current $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$. The two are compatible only
near the true coefficients, so minimising both moves $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ there.

**The coefficient's gradient.** For a residual linear in a coefficient, $r = u_t - \kappa u_{xx}$ for the
<NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink>, the derivative of the residual term is

$$
\frac{\partial L_r}{\partial \kappa} = -\frac{2}{N_r} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} r_i \, u_{xx}(\xvec_i).
$$

It is the correlation of the residual with the coefficient's own term: $\kappa$ grows when the residual is positive
where $u_{xx}$ is positive, the signature of too little diffusion. For a fixed network the residual term is a
least-squares problem in $\kappa$ with minimiser

$$
\kappa^\star = \frac{\sum_i u_t(\xvec_i)\, u_{xx}(\xvec_i)}{\sum_i u_{xx}(\xvec_i)^2}.
$$

**Positivity and scale.** A diffusivity or a viscosity must be positive and may be orders of magnitude from 1. Writing
$\kappa = e^{s}$ and training $s$ keeps it positive and makes a step in $s$ a relative change in $\kappa$. Raissi et al.
fitted $\nu$ in <NoteLink to="physics-informed-network-for-burgers-equation">Burgers' equation</NoteLink> this way
<Cite id="raissi2017discovery" />.

**Identifiability.** A coefficient is identifiable from the data when changing it changes the observable solution. The
sensitivity $\partial u / \partial \kappa$ at the observation points measures it. With Gaussian noise of standard
deviation $\sigma$ and the rest of the problem known, the best possible <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
error</NoteLink> of $\kappa$ is $\sigma / \sqrt{\sum_l (\partial u(\xvec_l) / \partial \kappa)^2}$, the Cramér-Rao
bound. For the heat equation's solution $u = e^{-\kappa \pi^2 t} \sin \pi x$, the sensitivity is $-\pi^2 t \, u$: zero
at $t = 0$ and growing with time. Observations confined to early times identify $\kappa$ poorly, however many there are.

**Unknown conditions.** When the initial profile or the boundary values are unknown too, their terms leave the loss and
the observations must determine them. The equation then constrains the network only as far as the data reach.

## Worked example

Suppose the network has been trained for a while and has, at three collocation points, the input derivatives

| point | $u_{xx}$ | $u_t$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $-6.0$ | $-0.62$ |
| 2 | $-8.0$ | $-0.78$ |
| 3 | $-3.0$ | $-0.31$ |

The best coefficient for these derivatives is

$$
\kappa^\star = \frac{(-0.62)(-6.0) + (-0.78)(-8.0) + (-0.31)(-3.0)}{6.0^2 + 8.0^2 + 3.0^2}
  = \frac{3.72 + 6.24 + 0.93}{36 + 64 + 9} = \frac{10.89}{109} = 0.0999 .
$$

A current estimate of $\kappa = 0.5$ gives residuals $r = u_t - 0.5\, u_{xx} = 2.38, 3.22, 1.19$, all positive where
$u_{xx}$ is negative. The gradient $\partial L_r / \partial \kappa = -\tfrac{2}{3}(2.38 \times (-6.0) + 3.22 \times
(-8.0)
+ 1.19 \times (-3.0)) = 29.1$ is positive, so a gradient step lowers $\kappa$ towards $\kappa^\star$.

The denominator $\sum u_{xx}^2 = 109$ is the coefficient's leverage. If the three points sat where the solution is
nearly straight, with $u_{xx} \approx 0$, the denominator would vanish and every value of $\kappa$ would fit the
residual equally well: the coefficient would not be identifiable from those points.

With data across the whole time interval the estimate is within 9% of the truth even at 30% noise, because the
equation averages the noise over the whole solution. With data only up to $t = 0.05$ the estimate is a quarter of the
truth in the short run and half in the longer one. The noise allows a standard error of 0.0086 there, so even the
longer run's estimate is almost six standard errors off: the failure is not noise but leverage. Over the first 0.05 time
units the solution decays by $1 - e^{-0.1 \pi^2 \times 0.05} \approx 5\%$, and small errors of the network's fit move
$\kappa$ far. The window $t \le 0.3$ is enough with the longer budget and not with the short one: the less leverage
the data give, the more accurately the network must fit before the coefficient settles. A wrong coefficient gives a
wrong solution everywhere past the data; the longer run's error against the exact solution is 24% with $t \le 0.05$.

For Burgers' equation $u_t + \lambda_1 u u_x - \lambda_2 u_{xx} = 0$, with 2000 observations scattered over the whole
domain and a network of 9 layers of 20 units, Raissi et al. recovered $\lambda_1 = 0.99915$ and
$\lambda_2 = 0.0031794$ against the true $1$ and $0.0031831$ from clean data, and $\lambda_2 = 0.0032098$ with 1%
noise, an error of 0.8% <Cite id="raissi2017discovery" />.

## Cost

An inverse problem costs about what the forward problem costs: the coefficients add a handful of parameters to
thousands of weights, and their gradients come from the same backward pass. Observations add a data term, which needs
no input derivatives. The nested classical approach costs one forward solve per outer iteration and, for gradients,
one adjoint solve per iteration as well; it is faster when the forward solver is fast and the coefficients few, and
slower when each solve is expensive.

## Trade-offs

- **One loop against two.** The joint fit avoids nested solves and adjoint derivations. It gives a point estimate with
  no error bar, where a Bayesian treatment of the classical problem gives a posterior. Bayesian physics-informed
  networks and ensembles add uncertainty at extra cost <Cite id="karniadakis2021" />.
- **Weight of the data.** A large $\lambda_d$ fits the noise and lets the residual absorb it into
  $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$; a small one lets the network solve the equation for a wrong coefficient. The
  <NoteLink to="loss-balancing-for-physics-informed-networks">loss-balancing</NoteLink> schemes apply.
- **Fixed form against discovered form.** Fitting coefficients assumes the form of the equation. When the form is
  unknown, <NoteLink to="sparse-identification-of-nonlinear-dynamics">sparse identification</NoteLink> chooses terms
  from a library and <NoteLink to="universal-differential-equations">universal differential equations</NoteLink>
  learn a missing term as a network.

## Pitfalls

- **Unidentifiable coefficients.** Two coefficients that enter the solution only through their product, or a
  coefficient whose term is near zero where the data are, cannot be separated by any amount of training. The
  sensitivity of the observations to each coefficient, checked before training, shows it.
- **Data only where the coefficient does not act.** Early-time data for a decay rate, or smooth-region data for a
  viscosity that only shapes a front, give the estimate no leverage, as the table's $t \le 0.05$ rows show.
- **A wrong equation fitted well.** The fit returns the coefficient that best explains the data with the assumed
  equation. A missing term is absorbed into the coefficients that exist, and the estimate is then biased however low the
  loss.
- **Reading the loss as confidence.** A small loss means the network, the equation and the data agree. It does not
  bound the coefficient's error; the sensitivity and the noise do.
