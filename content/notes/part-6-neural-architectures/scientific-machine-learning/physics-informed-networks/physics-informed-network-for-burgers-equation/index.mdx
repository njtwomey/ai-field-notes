<Definition>
Solve the viscous Burgers equation

$$
u_t + u\,u_x = \nu\,u_{xx}, \qquad x \in [-1, 1], \quad t \in [0, 1],
$$

with initial profile $u(x, 0) = -\sin \pi x$, end values $u(-1, t) = u(1, t) = 0$ and viscosity
$\nu = 0.01/\pi \approx 0.00318$, by a <NoteLink to="physics-informed-neural-networks">physics-informed
network</NoteLink> trained without any observations inside the domain. Compare the network with the exact solution given
by the Cole-Hopf transform, and find where its error lies. This is the first benchmark of Raissi, Perdikaris and
Karniadakis <Cite id="raissi2019" />.
</Definition>

## Setup

- $u(x, t)$ is a velocity: positive moves material to the right. The term $u\,u_x$ carries each value of $u$ at its own
  speed $u$. The term $\nu u_{xx}$ is viscous diffusion with viscosity $\nu > 0$.
- The initial profile $-\sin \pi x$ is positive on $[-1, 0)$ and negative on $(0, 1]$, so material on both sides moves
  towards $x = 0$.
- The network $u_{\thetavec}(x, t)$ is a <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptron</Gloss> with inputs $(x, t)$ scaled to
  $[-1, 1]^2$, hyperbolic tangent hidden units and one output.
- The loss is the mean squared residual $r = u_t + u u_x - \nu u_{xx}$ at $N_r$ collocation points, a scrambled Sobol
  set in the domain, plus $w$ times the mean squared misfits at evenly spaced points on the initial line and on each end
  (40 and 20 in the figure, 100 and 50 in the longer runs quoted below). The derivatives are the network's own, by
  <NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation">automatic differentiation</NoteLink> in $x$ and $t$.
- Training is <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> at <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> 0.005, then
  full-batch <Gloss name="l-bfgs" />.

## Solution

**Steepening.** Without viscosity, $u_t + u u_x = 0$ keeps $u$ constant along the characteristics
$\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = u$ (<NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">transport equations</NoteLink>). The
characteristic from $x_0$ is the straight line $x = x_0 - t \sin \pi x_0$. Two neighbouring characteristics meet when
$1 + t\, u_0'(x_0) = 0$, which first happens where the initial slope is most negative. The slope $u_0'(x) = -\pi \cos
\pi x$ is $-\pi$ at $x = 0$, so the inviscid profile breaks at

$$
t_b = \frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.318 .
$$

**The viscous front.** Viscosity stops the breaking and leaves a steep front at $x = 0$. Near the front the solution
is close to the steady profile that balances the two terms: $u\,u_x = \nu u_{xx}$ with $u \to \pm U$ on either side
integrates to $\nu u_x = (u^2 - U^2)/2$, whose solution is

$$
u(x) = -U \tanh\paren{\frac{U x}{2\nu}}, \qquad u_x(0) = -\frac{U^2}{2\nu}.
$$

The front's width is about $2\nu / U$. At $t = 0.5$ the exact solution has $U \approx 0.98$ just beside the front, so
the predicted slope is $-0.98^2 / (2 \times 0.00318) \approx -151$; the exact slope there is $-152$, against $-\pi$ at
$t = 0$. The front is about $0.0065$ wide.

**Cole-Hopf.** The substitution $u = -2\nu\, \phi_x / \phi$ turns Burgers' equation into the
<NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink> $\phi_t = \nu \phi_{xx}$ <Cite id="hopf1950" />
<Cite id="cole1951" />. The initial profile becomes $\phi(x, 0) = \exp\paren{-\cos(\pi x)/(2\pi\nu)}$ up to a constant
factor, and convolving it with the heat kernel gives

$$
u(x, t) = -\frac{\displaystyle\int \sin \pi(x - \eta)\, f(x - \eta)\, e^{-\eta^2 / 4\nu t}\, \mathrm{d}\eta}
  {\displaystyle\int f(x - \eta)\, e^{-\eta^2 / 4\nu t}\, \mathrm{d}\eta}, \qquad f(y) = e^{-\cos(\pi y) / 2\pi\nu}.
$$

The solution is odd in $x$, so it is zero at $x = 0$ and, by periodicity, at $x = \pm 1$ for all $t$. The figure
evaluates the integrals by the trapezoid rule.

**What the network must resolve.** The front is 0.0065 wide and lives at $x = 0$ for $t \gtrsim 0.4$. With 1024
collocation points spread evenly over an area of 2, the expected number inside the strip $\abs{x} < 0.0065$,
$t > 0.4$ is $1024 \times (0.013 \times 0.6) / 2 \approx 4$. Almost every collocation point lies where the solution is
smooth.

## Simulation

The figure trains the network in the compute worker and streams snapshots every 50 steps. Its defaults are cheap,
about two seconds: two hidden layers of 16 units (337 parameters), 256 collocation points, $w = 1$, 200 Adam steps and
100 L-BFGS steps, with the error measured on a $61 \times 31$ grid.

<BurgersPinn />

The default run ends with a relative $L_2$ error of 48%, a rough fit. Its largest pointwise error, 0.96, sits at
$x = -0.03$, $t = 0.47$, on the front, and the region $\abs{x} \le 0.1$, $t \ge 0.4$, about 7% of the grid, holds 34% of
the squared error. With 256 points the strip of the front holds on average one collocation point.

Longer runs show how the error falls with budget. With three hidden layers of 20 units (921 parameters), 1024
collocation points, 1000 Adam steps and 500 L-BFGS steps, about 100 seconds on a laptop processor, the error is 24%
on a $101 \times 51$ grid. The largest pointwise error, 0.91, is again on the front, at $x = 0.02$, $t = 0.48$: the
network places the front but not its steepness. The region $\abs{x} < 0.1$, $t \ge 0.4$ is 6% of the domain and holds
37% of the squared error.

| Change from the longer run | Relative error |
| --- | --- |
| none | 24% |
| 2048 collocation points | 10% |
| 2000 L-BFGS steps | 16% |
| $\nu = 0.01$ | 7.6% |
| $\nu = 0.05$ | 0.39% |

The viscosity sets the difficulty. At $\nu = 0.05$ the front is about 0.1 wide and the same network reaches 0.39%; at
$\nu = 0.01$ it is about 0.02 wide, the error is 7.6% and 86% of the squared error lies in the front region. Raissi et
al. reached a relative error of $6.7 \times 10^{-4}$ with eight hidden layers of 20 units (3021 parameters), 10,000
collocation points from a Latin hypercube and L-BFGS run to convergence on a <Gloss name="gpu" />
<Cite id="raissi2019" />. The gap is training budget and point density, not a different method.

## What it illustrates

- **Collocation points must be dense where the solution changes fast.** Doubling the points cuts the error from 24% to
  10% here, because the front is narrower than their spacing. Residual-based adaptive sampling puts the points there
  instead of everywhere <Cite id="wu2023rad" />; the
  <NoteLink to="loss-balancing-for-physics-informed-networks">loss-balancing</NoteLink> note shows it on a steep layer.
- **<NoteLink to="implicit-neural-representation">Spectral bias</NoteLink>.** A network fits the smooth parts of the
  solution first and the front last, which is one of the
  <NoteLink to="failure-modes-of-physics-informed-networks">failure modes</NoteLink> of physics-informed networks.
- **A loss that is small is not an error that is small.** The residual is large only in the thin front, so its mean
  over the collocation points can be small while the pointwise error there is of the size of the solution.
- **Classical structure helps check the network.** The breaking time, the front's slope $-U^2/2\nu$ and the Cole-Hopf
  solution give three independent checks that need no network at all.

## Variations

- **Smaller viscosity.** The front's width scales with $\nu$, so halving $\nu$ halves the width and doubles the
  number of points needed across it. In the inviscid limit the front becomes a shock, and the strong-form residual is
  undefined there.
- **Unknown viscosity.** Making $\nu$ trainable and adding observations turns the example into an
  <NoteLink to="inverse-problems-with-physics-informed-networks">inverse problem</NoteLink>. The front's width carries
  most of the information about $\nu$, so observations must sample it.
- **Hard conditions.** The trial function $u = -\sin \pi x + t(1 - x^2) N_{\thetavec}(x, t)$ meets the initial profile
  and both end values exactly (<NoteLink to="hard-constraints-for-physics-informed-networks">hard
  constraints</NoteLink>).
- **Many initial profiles.** A physics-informed network solves one initial profile per training run. A
  <NoteLink to="fourier-neural-operator">Fourier neural operator</NoteLink> learns the map from initial profile to
  solution and answers a new profile in one forward pass.
