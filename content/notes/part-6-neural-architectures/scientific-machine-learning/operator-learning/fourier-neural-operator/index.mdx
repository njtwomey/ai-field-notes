<Definition>
A **Fourier neural operator** (<Gloss name="fno" />) maps an input function $a(x)$ on a periodic domain to an output
function $u(x)$. A pointwise linear map lifts $a(x)$ to a vector $v_0(x) \in \reals^c$ of $c$ channels at each point,
$L$ **Fourier layers** follow, and a pointwise network projects $v_L(x)$ to $u(x)$. A Fourier layer is

$$
v_{\ell+1}(x) = \sigma\paren{W v_\ell(x) + \Fcal^{-1}\brack{R_k\, (\Fcal v_\ell)_k \text{ for } \abs{k} < k_{\max}}(x) + b},
$$

where $\Fcal$ is the <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> of each channel,
$R_k \in \complex^{c \times c}$ is a learned matrix for each of the lowest $k_{\max}$ frequencies, the other frequencies
are set to zero, $W \in \reals^{c \times c}$ acts at every point, $b \in \reals^c$ is a bias and $\sigma$ is an
activation. On a uniform grid $\Fcal$ is computed by the <Gloss name="fft" /> <Cite id="li2021fno" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink> mixes each point with its few neighbours, and it
takes many layers before information crosses the domain. The solution operators of many <Gloss name="pde" plural /> are
global: in the <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink>, a change of the initial temperature anywhere is
felt everywhere at once. A Fourier layer mixes every point with every other point in one step, by doing a convolution
with a kernel as wide as the domain. The <NoteLink to="convolution-theorem">convolution theorem</NoteLink> makes such a
convolution cheap: it is a product of Fourier coefficients.

The Fourier coefficients also give the layer its resolution independence. The coefficient of $e^{i2\pi kx}$ is a
property of the function, not of the grid it is sampled on. A weight attached to frequency $k$ means the same thing on a
grid of 32 points and on a grid of 1,024 points, so a trained model can be run on either. A convolution kernel stored
as $3 \times 3$ grid weights covers a different physical width at every resolution.

The lowest frequencies carry most of the energy of a smooth solution. Keeping only $k_{\max}$ of them makes the layer
cheap and acts as a smoothness prior. The pointwise path $W v(x)$ and the activation restore what the truncation
discards: products of low-frequency fields contain higher frequencies, so a stack of layers can represent outputs with
detail beyond $k_{\max}$.

## Details

**The spectral convolution.** The general layer of a neural operator contains the integral
$\int_D \kappa(x, y)\, v(y)\, dy$ with a learned kernel $\kappa$
(<NoteLink to="operator-learning">operator learning</NoteLink>). The Fourier neural operator assumes the kernel depends
only on the difference, $\kappa(x, y) = \kappa(x - y)$, so that the integral is a convolution. On a periodic domain the
convolution theorem gives

$$
\paren{\kappa * v}(x) = \Fcal^{-1}\brack{\paren{\Fcal\kappa}_k \paren{\Fcal v}_k}(x).
$$

Instead of learning $\kappa$ and transforming it, the layer learns its transform directly: $R_k = (\Fcal\kappa)_k$, a
$c \times c$ complex matrix that mixes the channels at frequency $k$. The kernel then never appears. Truncating to
$\abs{k} < k_{\max}$ limits the parameters of one spectral convolution to $k_{\max} c^2$ complex numbers, whatever the
grid. For a real field the coefficients at $-k$ are the conjugates of those at $k$, so only non-negative frequencies are
stored, and the real FFT (rfft) computes them.

**The pointwise path.** The term $W v_\ell(x)$ is a $1 \times 1$ convolution. It carries the frequencies above
$k_{\max}$ through the layer, which the spectral path would set to zero, and with the bias it handles the
non-periodic part of the output. Without it, every layer would output a band-limited function.

**Resolution independence.** On a grid of $n$ points, the real FFT gives $n/2 + 1$ coefficients, the layer multiplies
the lowest $k_{\max}$ and pads the rest with zeros, and the inverse FFT returns $n$ values. With the convention that
the forward transform is a sum and the inverse divides by $n$, a band-limited input sampled on any grid with
$n \ge 2(k_{\max} - 1)$ gives the same output function, sampled on the same grid. The lift, $W$ and the projection act
pointwise and do not depend on $n$ at all. Li et al. trained on one resolution and evaluated on finer ones with the
same error, and called this **zero-shot super-resolution** <Cite id="li2021fno" />. The output on the finer grid has no
detail above what the trained layers can produce: the finer grid samples the same function more densely.

**Cost.** The FFT and its inverse cost $O(c\, n \log n)$ per layer and the <NoteLink to="one-by-one-convolution">channel
mixing</NoteLink> costs $O(k_{\max} c^2)$ per input. On a $256 \times 256$ grid Li et al. measured 0.005 s for one
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">forward pass</NoteLink> of a trained Fourier neural operator, against 2.2 s for the
pseudo-spectral solver that produced the Navier–Stokes training data <Cite id="li2021fno" />.

**Universality.** Kovachki, Lanthaler and Mishra proved that Fourier neural operators approximate any continuous
operator between Sobolev spaces on the torus, and that for the operators of some elliptic and fluid problems the size
of the network grows only slowly with the inverse of the error <Cite id="kovachki2021fnouniversal" />.

**Several dimensions.** For a field on a $d$-dimensional grid the transform is the $d$-dimensional FFT and the kept
frequencies are the vectors $\kvec$ with every $\abs{k_j} < k_{\max}$. Li et al. used $k_{\max} = 16$ and $c = 64$ for
one-dimensional problems and $k_{\max} = 12$, $c = 32$ for two-dimensional ones, with four Fourier layers
<Cite id="li2021fno" />.

## Worked example

One spectral convolution with one channel, $k_{\max} = 2$ and weights $R_0 = 0.5$ and $R_1 = 1 - i$, applied to the
input $v(x) = 1 + \cos 2\pi x$.

1. **Sample on $n = 4$ points**, $x_j = j/4$: $v = (2, 1, 0, 1)$.
2. **Transform.** $V_k = \sum_{j=0}^{3} v_j e^{-i 2\pi jk/4}$. For $k = 0$: $V_0 = 2 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 4$. For $k = 1$:
   $V_1 = 2 + 1 \cdot (-i) + 0 + 1 \cdot i = 2$. For $k = 2$: $V_2 = 2 - 1 + 0 - 1 = 0$.
3. **Multiply and truncate.** $Y_0 = 0.5 \times 4 = 2$, $Y_1 = (1 - i) \times 2 = 2 - 2i$, and $Y_2 = 0$ because
   $k = 2 \ge k_{\max}$.
4. **Transform back.** For a real signal $y_j = \tfrac14\brack{Y_0 + 2\operatorname{Re}\paren{Y_1 e^{i2\pi j/4}}}$. With
   $e^{i2\pi j/4} = 1, i, -1, -i$: $\operatorname{Re}(Y_1 e^{i2\pi j/4}) = 2, 2, -2, -2$, so
   $y = (1.5, 1.5, -0.5, -0.5)$.
5. **Read the weights.** $R_0 = 0.5$ halves the mean, from 1 to 0.5. $R_1 = 1 - i = \sqrt2\, e^{-i\pi/4}$ scales the
   first harmonic by $\sqrt2$ and delays it by an eighth of a period: $\cos 2\pi x$ becomes
   $\sqrt2\cos(2\pi x - \pi/4) = \cos 2\pi x + \sin 2\pi x$. The output function is
   $y(x) = 0.5 + \cos 2\pi x + \sin 2\pi x$, and its values at $x = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75$ are those of step 4.
6. **Same weights, $n = 8$.** Now $V_0 = 8$ and $V_1 = 4$; the products are $Y_0 = 4$ and $Y_1 = 4 - 4i$, and the
   inverse transform divides by 8 instead of 4. The output is again $0.5 + \cos 2\pi x + \sin 2\pi x$, sampled at eight
   points: $(1.5, 1.91, 1.5, 0.5, -0.5, -0.91, -0.5, 0.5)$. Every second value repeats the four-point output. The
   weights were never tied to the grid.

A full layer adds $W v(x) + b$ at every point and applies $\sigma$; with $W = 0.2$, $b = 0$ and the identity in place of
$\sigma$, the first output value on the four-point grid is $1.5 + 0.2 \times 2 = 1.9$.

## Practice

Current implementations follow Li et al. with small changes: <Gloss name="gelu" /> activations, four Fourier layers, 12
to 32 modes per dimension, 32 to 128 channels, the grid coordinates appended as input channels, Adam with a decaying
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> and the relative $L^2$ loss. Non-periodic inputs are padded with zeros
before the FFT so that the periodic convolution does not wrap the two ends together. Variants change the geometry:
geo-FNO learns a deformation that maps an irregular domain onto a uniform grid, where the FFT applies
<Cite id="li2023geofno" />. In a comparison on 16 benchmarks the Fourier neural operator matched
<Gloss name="deeponet" /> on simple problems and degraded on complex geometries and on inputs with noise
<Cite id="lu2022fairoperators" />. A review of neural operators in science and engineering is Azizzadenesheli et al.
<Cite id="azizzadenesheli2024neuraloperators" />.

## Assumptions

- The domain is a periodic box sampled on a uniform grid, or can be mapped onto one.
- The operator is close to translation-equivariant in its global part, so that a convolution with a learned kernel and
  pointwise corrections can represent it.
- The relevant information in the input and output is carried by a modest number of low frequencies per layer.

## Pitfalls

- **Irregular domains.** The FFT needs a uniform grid. Interpolating mesh data onto a grid adds error at boundaries,
  and graph or deformation-based operators are the alternatives.
- **Wrap-around.** Without padding, a non-periodic input is treated as periodic, and the jump between its two ends
  produces ringing that the network has to learn to cancel.
- **Aliasing from the nonlinearity.** The activation of a band-limited field creates frequencies above the grid's
  Nyquist frequency, which fold back onto lower ones. The aliased part depends on the grid, so resolution independence
  holds only approximately.
- **Super-resolution read as accuracy.** Evaluating on a finer grid gives a denser sample of the same output, not detail
  the training data did not contain.
- **Too many modes for the training grid.** $k_{\max}$ above $n/2 + 1$ cannot be used at the training resolution, and
  modes near the Nyquist frequency of coarse data are learned from aliased values.
