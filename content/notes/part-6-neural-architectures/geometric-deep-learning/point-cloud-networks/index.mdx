<Definition>
A point cloud is a set of $n$ points $\xvec_i \in \reals^3$, often with extra features per point such as colour or a
surface normal. PointNet maps it to a global feature

$$
\gvec = \max_{i = 1, \dots, n} h(\xvec_i) \in \reals^K,
$$

where $h$ is one <Gloss name="mlp" /> shared by every point and the maximum is taken separately in each of the $K$
channels; a second network $\gamma$ maps $\gvec$ to class scores <Cite id="qi2017pointnet" />. The output does not
depend on the order of the points. PointNet++ and the dynamic graph <Gloss name="cnn" /> (DGCNN) apply the same
construction to local neighbourhoods.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A LiDAR scan, a depth camera or a photogrammetry pipeline returns a list of 3-D points. The list has no grid and no
order, the number of points varies from scan to scan, and the density varies across one scan. Voxelising the points
into a 3-D grid makes a <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolution</NoteLink> possible, but the grid is mostly empty
and its memory grows with the cube of the resolution. Rendering the cloud to images from several viewpoints works for
classification but loses the 3-D geometry.

PointNet treats the cloud as what it is, a set, and uses the recipe of <NoteLink to="deep-sets" />: process each point
alone with a shared network, then pool. It pools with a maximum. Each of the $K$ channels of $h$ learns to respond to
some region or direction of space, and the maximum keeps, for each channel, the strongest response anywhere in the
cloud. The global feature summarises the shape by its extreme responses, and a small number of points carry them.

## PointNet

The per-point network $h$ lifts each point from 3 to $K$ dimensions; PointNet uses layers of widths 64, 64, 64, 128 and
1024, so $K = 1024$. The max pool gives $\gvec \in \reals^{1024}$, and a classifier with layers of 512 and 256 units
maps it to class scores. For segmentation, the network concatenates $\gvec$ to every point's own feature and applies a
second shared MLP, so each point's label depends on both its local feature and the whole shape.

Two results explain why the maximum works <Cite id="qi2017pointnet" />:

- **Universal approximation.** Any continuous function of point sets, continuous in the Hausdorff distance, can be
  approximated to any accuracy by $\gamma(\max_i h(\xvec_i))$ with $K$ large enough. The construction partitions space
  into small cells and lets each channel of $h$ detect whether some point falls in its cell.
- **Critical point set.** For every cloud $S$ there is a subset $\Ccal_S \subseteq S$ with $\abs{\Ccal_S} \le K$ such
  that the output is the same for every cloud $T$ with $\Ccal_S \subseteq T \subseteq \Ncal_S$, where $\Ncal_S$ is the
  largest set with the same channel maxima. The critical points are the winners of the $K$ maxima. Removing any other
  point, or adding points whose responses stay below the maxima, changes nothing.

The second result is a direct reading of the maximum. Channel $c$ of $\gvec$ is $\max_i h_c(\xvec_i)$, attained at some
point $i_c$. Removing a point that is not one of $i_1, \dots, i_K$ leaves every maximum, and so $\gvec$ and the output,
unchanged. There are at most $K$ distinct winners. The critical points of trained networks sit on the outline of the
object, a skeleton of its shape.

PointNet also predicts a $3 \times 3$ matrix from the cloud with a small PointNet of its own (the T-net) and multiplies
the points by it, to bring them to a canonical pose; a second T-net aligns the 64-dimensional features. On ModelNet40,
classification of 40 object categories from 1,024 points per object, max pooling alone gave 87.1% accuracy and the full
model with both transforms 89.2% <Cite id="qi2017pointnet" />. With half the points removed at random, accuracy fell by
3.8 points.

## Local structure: PointNet++ and DGCNN

PointNet sees each point alone until the global maximum. It has no notion of a neighbourhood, so it cannot build up
local features such as corners or edges the way stacked convolutions do. Two families add locality.

**PointNet++** builds a hierarchy <Cite id="qi2017pointnetplusplus" />. A set abstraction layer picks $n'$ centres by
farthest point sampling, which spreads them evenly over the cloud; gathers the points within a radius $r$ of each centre
(a ball query); and runs a small PointNet on each group, with coordinates taken relative to the centre. The output is a
smaller cloud of $n'$ points, each with a feature describing its neighbourhood. Repeating with larger radii gives
features of growing scale, as pooling layers do in a CNN. Grouping at several radii per layer handles varying density.

**DGCNN** builds a graph <Cite id="wang2019dgcnn" />. Each point is joined to its $k$ nearest neighbours, and an
EdgeConv layer updates it by

$$
\hvec_i' = \max_{j \in \Ncal_k(i)} \text{MLP}\paren{\hvec_i, \hvec_j - \hvec_i},
$$

a <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing</NoteLink> step whose message sees the point and its
offset to a neighbour. The graph is rebuilt after every layer from the current features, not the coordinates, so points
far apart in space but similar in feature space become neighbours.

Large outdoor and indoor scenes, with $10^5$ to $10^6$ points, are handled today by sparse voxel convolutions and by
point transformers that attend within local windows. Point Transformer V3 orders the points along space-filling curves
and attends within fixed-size windows of that order, which avoids neighbour search and scales to large scenes
<Cite id="wu2024ptv3" />.

## Worked example

Take four points in the plane, $A = (1, 0)$, $B = (0, 2)$, $C = (-1, -1)$ and $D = (0.2, 0.3)$, and a per-point network
with $K = 4$ channels,

$$
h(x, y) = \paren{\max(0, x),\ \max(0, y),\ \max(0, -x),\ \max(0, -y)}.
$$

**Per-point features.** $h(A) = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, $h(B) = (0, 2, 0, 0)$, $h(C) = (0, 0, 1, 1)$ and
$h(D) = (0.2, 0.3, 0, 0)$.

**Max pool.** Channel by channel,

$$
\gvec = \paren{\max(1, 0, 0, 0.2),\ \max(0, 2, 0, 0.3),\ \max(0, 0, 1, 0),\ \max(0, 0, 1, 0)} = (1, 2, 1, 1).
$$

The four channels are the rightmost, topmost, leftmost and bottommost extents of the cloud, measured
from the origin. A classifier $\gamma$ can read the width $g_1 + g_3 = 2$ and the height $g_2 + g_4 = 3$ of the bounding
box from them.

**Critical set.** Channel 1 is won by $A$, channel 2 by $B$, and channels 3 and 4 by $C$. The critical set is
$\set{A, B, C}$, three points, within the bound $\abs{\Ccal_S} \le K = 4$.

**Remove a non-critical point.** Without $D$, the maxima are still $(1, 2, 1, 1)$. So is the output. Moving $D$ anywhere
inside the box $[-1, 1] \times [-1, 2]$, or adding any number of points inside it, changes nothing either.

**Remove a critical point.** Without $B$, channel 2 falls to $\max(0, 0, 0.3) = 0.3$, the next-highest response, which
is $D$'s. $D$ becomes critical, and the global feature is $(1, 0.3, 1, 1)$.

**Order.** Listing the points as $D, C, B, A$ gives the same four maxima.

## Assumptions

- **The cloud samples a surface well enough.** The critical points must survive in the input. Very sparse or partial
  scans can miss the extremes the network relies on.
- **Coordinates are in a consistent frame and scale.** PointNet is not invariant to rotation, translation or scale.
  Inputs are centred and scaled to the unit sphere, and rotations are handled by augmentation, by the T-net, or by an
  <NoteLink to="euclidean-equivariant-graph-network">equivariant network</NoteLink>.
- **Local geometry matters less than global shape** for plain PointNet. Tasks that depend on fine local structure need
  PointNet++, DGCNN or a point transformer.

## Pitfalls

- **Rotation sensitivity.** A PointNet trained on upright objects fails on rotated ones; the figure shows the drop when
  the training shapes are rotated and few. Augmenting with rotations teaches approximate invariance at the cost of
  capacity; <NoteLink to="canonicalisation-and-frame-averaging" /> gives exact alternatives.
- **Outliers can become critical.** A stray point far from the object wins the channels that respond in its direction,
  and the global feature then describes the outlier.
- **Non-uniform density.** Ball queries with one radius find too few neighbours in sparse regions and too many in dense
  ones; PointNet++ uses several radii for this reason.
- **Neighbour search cost.** $k$-nearest-neighbour graphs over $n$ points cost $O(n^2)$ by brute force, which dominates
  DGCNN on large clouds; spatial trees, voxel hashing or serialisation reduce it.
