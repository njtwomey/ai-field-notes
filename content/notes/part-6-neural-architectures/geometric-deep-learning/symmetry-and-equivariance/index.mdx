<Definition>
A group $G$ acts on an input space $\Xcal$ and an output space $\Ycal$, written $g \cdot x$ and $g \cdot y$. A map
$f : \Xcal \to \Ycal$ is **equivariant** to $G$ if $f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x)$ for every $g \in G$ and every $x$:
transforming the input transforms the output in the matching way. It is **invariant** if $f(g \cdot x) = f(x)$, the
case where $G$ leaves the outputs unchanged. Equivariant layers followed by an invariant pooling give an invariant
network.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A photograph of a cat shifted ten pixels to the right is still a photograph of a cat. A classifier should give it the
same label: the label is invariant to shifts. An edge detector run on the shifted photograph should find the same edges,
ten pixels to the right: the edge map is equivariant to shifts. A list of the atoms of a molecule describes the same
molecule in any order, and a rotated molecule has the same energy and forces that rotate with it.

Each of these statements names a set of transformations that leave the meaning of the input alone and says how the
output should respond. A network can obtain the property in three ways:

- **Learn it from data.** <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">Data augmentation</NoteLink> shows the network transformed
  copies of each example with the same label. The network becomes approximately invariant near the training data, and
  pays for it in data and compute.
- **Remove it from the input.** Map each input to a standard pose before the network sees it, or average the network
  over the transformations. <NoteLink to="canonicalisation-and-frame-averaging" /> describes both.
- **Build it into the layers.** Restrict every layer to maps that commute with the transformations. The network is
  then equivariant exactly, for every input and every weight setting, and spends no parameters on learning the
  symmetry.

The third way is the subject of <NoteLink to="geometric-deep-learning" /> <Cite id="bronstein2021" />. A
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink> is its oldest example.

## Groups and actions

The transformations of interest form a **group**: a set $G$ with a composition $gh$ that is associative, an identity
$e$ with $eg = ge = g$, and an inverse $g^{-1}$ for every element. Composing two symmetries gives a symmetry, doing
nothing is a symmetry, and every symmetry can be undone.

A group **acts** on a set $\Xcal$ when each $g$ maps $\Xcal$ to itself with $e \cdot x = x$ and
$g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x$. When $\Xcal = \reals^n$ and each $g$ acts by a matrix $\rho(g)$ with
$\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\rho(h)$, the map $\rho$ is a **representation** of $G$. The same group can act on inputs and
outputs through different representations. The groups used in networks are:

| Group                      | Acts on                       | Network family                                                                         |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| cyclic shifts $\integers_n$ | signals and images on a torus | convolutional layer                                                                    |
| permutations $S_n$         | sets, graph nodes             | <NoteLink to="deep-sets" />, <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing</NoteLink> |
| $p4$, $p4m$                | images                        | <NoteLink to="group-equivariant-convolutional-network">group-equivariant CNN</NoteLink>       |
| $SO(2)$, $O(2)$            | images, planar fields         | <NoteLink to="steerable-and-spherical-networks">steerable CNN</NoteLink>               |
| $E(n)$, $SE(3)$            | point clouds, molecules       | <NoteLink to="euclidean-equivariant-graph-network">EGNN</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="se3-equivariant-networks">SE(3) networks</NoteLink> |

Here $p4$ is the group of translations and rotations by multiples of $90°$, $p4m$ adds reflections, $SO(n)$ is the
group of rotations of $\reals^n$, $O(n)$ adds reflections, $E(n)$ adds translations to $O(n)$, and $SE(3)$ is the
group of rotations and translations of 3-D space.

## Building equivariant networks

Three facts make equivariance a property of the architecture rather than of individual weights.

**Composition.** If $f_1$ and $f_2$ are equivariant, so is $f_2 \circ f_1$:

$$
f_2(f_1(g \cdot x)) = f_2(g \cdot f_1(x)) = g \cdot f_2(f_1(x)).
$$

A stack of equivariant layers is equivariant, however deep. One layer that breaks the symmetry breaks it for the whole
stack.

**Pointwise nonlinearities.** An elementwise function $\sigma$, such as $\max(0, z)$, commutes with every permutation
matrix $\Pmat$: $\sigma(\Pmat \xvec) = \Pmat\, \sigma(\xvec)$, because it treats each entry alike. Shifts and
reflections of a signal, permutations of a set and $90°$ rotations of a pixel grid all act by permuting entries, so
$\sigma$ is equivariant to them. It is not equivariant to a rotation matrix acting on a vector. Let $\Rmat$ rotate the
plane by $90°$, $\Rmat(a, b) = (-b, a)$, and take $\vvec = (1, -1)$. Then $\sigma(\Rmat\vvec) = \sigma(1, 1) = (1, 1)$,
while $\Rmat\, \sigma(\vvec) = \Rmat(1, 0) = (0, 1)$. Networks whose features are vectors therefore use nonlinearities
that act on vector lengths only, as in <NoteLink to="se3-equivariant-networks" />.

**Invariant pooling.** Summing over the orbit of the group turns equivariant features into invariant ones. For cyclic
shifts $S$ of a signal, $\sum_i (S\xvec)_i = \sum_i x_i$, because a shift only reorders the terms. A global sum, mean or
maximum over positions, over set elements or over graph nodes is invariant for the same reason.

A fourth construction makes any map equivariant when $G$ is finite and acts linearly on the outputs. The **group
average** of $f$ is

$$
\bar f(x) = \frac{1}{\abs{G}} \sum_{g \in G} g^{-1} \cdot f(g \cdot x).
$$

For $h \in G$, substitute $g' = gh$, so that $g^{-1} = h g'^{-1}$ and $g'$ runs over $G$ as $g$ does:
$\bar f(h \cdot x) = h \cdot \frac{1}{\abs{G}} \sum_{g'} g'^{-1} \cdot f(g' \cdot x) = h \cdot \bar f(x)$. The cost is
$\abs{G}$ evaluations of $f$ per input, which is affordable for the 8 elements of a square's symmetry group and not for
the $n!$ permutations of a set.

## Convolution is the linear shift-equivariant map

The central result says which linear layers commute with shifts. Take signals $\xvec \in \reals^n$ on a cycle of $n$
positions, indexed $0, \dots, n - 1$ modulo $n$. The shift $S$ moves every entry one place on: $(S\xvec)_i = x_{i-1}$.
A linear layer is a matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$. It is equivariant to all shifts exactly when
$\Amat S = S \Amat$, since every shift is a power of $S$.

Let $\evec_j$ be the $j$-th standard basis vector, a single 1 at position $j$. Then $S \evec_j = \evec_{j+1}$, so
$\evec_j = S^j \evec_0$. Column $j$ of $\Amat$ is

$$
\Amat \evec_j = \Amat S^j \evec_0 = S^j \Amat \evec_0,
$$

using $\Amat S = S \Amat$ $j$ times. So every column is the first column shifted down by $j$ places. Write the first
column as $\avec = (a_0, \dots, a_{n-1})$. Then $A_{ij} = a_{i - j}$, and

$$
y_i = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} a_{i-j}\, x_j,
$$

which is the circular convolution of $\xvec$ with the kernel $\avec$. Such a matrix is called circulant. Conversely,
every circulant matrix commutes with $S$, by the same index shift. A general linear layer has $n^2$ parameters; a
shift-equivariant one has $n$. Locality, the second assumption of the convolutional layer, cuts them to the $k$ taps of
a short kernel.

The result generalises: for a compact group acting on the input and output, a linear layer is equivariant exactly when
it is a convolution over the group, suitably defined <Cite id="kondor2018generalization" />. Group-equivariant,
steerable and spherical CNNs are instances.

<Derivation title="Linear maps that commute with every permutation">
Let $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ satisfy $\Pmat \Amat \Pmat^\tr = \Amat$ for every permutation matrix $\Pmat$, which
is $\Amat \Pmat = \Pmat \Amat$ because $\Pmat^\tr = \Pmat^{-1}$. Entry $(i, j)$ of $\Pmat \Amat \Pmat^\tr$ is
$A_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}$, where $\pi$ is the permutation. For any two diagonal positions $i, k$, some $\pi$ maps $i$ to $k$,
so $A_{ii} = A_{kk}$: all diagonal entries are equal. For any two off-diagonal pairs $(i, j)$ and $(k, l)$ with
$i \neq j$ and $k \neq l$, some $\pi$ maps $i$ to $k$ and $j$ to $l$, so all off-diagonal entries are equal. Hence
$\Amat = a\I + b\ones\ones^\tr$ for two numbers $a, b$, and

$$
\Amat\xvec = a\xvec + b\,\paren{\textstyle\sum_j x_j}\ones.
$$

Each element is updated from itself and from the sum over the set. Two parameters remain of $n^2$, whatever $n$ is.
This is the linear layer of <NoteLink to="deep-sets" /> <Cite id="zaheer2017" />.
</Derivation>

## Invariant or equivariant

The output's type decides which property a layer should have. A classifier needs an invariant output. A segmentation
map, a force on each atom or an updated vector per node needs an equivariant one. Invariance should come late. A
network that pools over the group in its first layers throws away the relative pose of parts: a face and a scramble of
its eyes, nose and mouth give the same pooled features. Cohen and Welling measured this on rotated handwritten digits: a
network that pooled over rotations after every layer had 3.21% test error, and one that kept rotation channels until
the last layer had 2.28% <Cite id="cohen2016gcnn" />. The blueprint of geometric deep learning is therefore a stack of
equivariant layers, optional local pooling to coarsen the domain, and one invariant pooling at the end
<Cite id="bronstein2021" />.

## Worked example

Take a signal of length $n = 6$ on a cycle, $\xvec = (1, 3, 0, 2, 0, 0)$, and the difference kernel $a_0 = 1$,
$a_1 = -1$, so that $y_i = x_i - x_{i-1}$ with indices modulo 6.

**Convolve.** Position 0 reads $x_0 - x_5 = 1 - 0 = 1$. Position 1 reads $x_1 - x_0 = 3 - 1 = 2$. Position 2 reads
$0 - 3 = -3$, position 3 reads $2 - 0 = 2$, position 4 reads $0 - 2 = -2$ and position 5 reads $0 - 0 = 0$. So
$\yvec = (1, 2, -3, 2, -2, 0)$.

**Shift, then convolve.** The shifted input is $S\xvec = (0, 1, 3, 0, 2, 0)$: every entry moves one place on, and the
last entry wraps to the front. Its convolution is $(0 - 0, 1 - 0, 3 - 1, 0 - 3, 2 - 0, 0 - 2) = (0, 1, 2, -3, 2, -2)$,
which is $S\yvec$. The two orders agree, as the derivation requires.

**A position-dependent layer fails.** Let $\Dmat = \diag(0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$ weight each position by its index. Then
$\Dmat\xvec = (0, 3, 0, 6, 0, 0)$ and $S\Dmat\xvec = (0, 0, 3, 0, 6, 0)$, but $\Dmat S\xvec = (0, 1, 6, 0, 8, 0)$.
Shifting the input does not shift the output, because the weights belong to positions, not to the pattern.

**<Gloss name="relu" /> and pooling.** Rectifying gives $\max(0, \yvec) = (1, 2, 0, 2, 0, 0)$, and rectifying $S\yvec$
gives its shift. The sum over positions is $1 + 2 + 2 = 5$ for both: the composition "convolve, rectify, sum" is
invariant to shifts.

**Reflection.** Let $(F\xvec)_i = x_{-i}$, so $F\xvec = (1, 0, 0, 2, 0, 3)$. Its convolution is
$(1 - 3, 0 - 1, 0 - 0, 2 - 0, 0 - 2, 3 - 0) = (-2, -1, 0, 2, -2, 3)$, while $F\yvec = (1, 0, -2, 2, -3, 2)$. The
difference kernel is not symmetric, so the layer is not equivariant to reflections. A symmetric kernel, such as
$y_i = x_{i-1} + 2x_i + x_{i+1}$, is.

**Count.** A dense layer on 6 positions has 36 weights. A circular convolution has 6. The difference kernel uses 2. A
layer equivariant to all $6! = 720$ permutations has 2, the $a$ and $b$ of the derivation above.

## Assumptions

- **The symmetry holds for the task.** Upright photographs are not rotation-invariant: gravity fixes "up", and a
  rotated 6 is a 9. Building in a symmetry the labels do not have removes information the model needs.
- **The group acts in a known way.** Equivariance needs the action on the input and the output written down: a shift of
  pixels, a rotation of coordinates and of force vectors, a relabelling of nodes.
- **The domain supports the action.** Shifts are exact on a cycle. A finite image with borders, a pixel grid under
  non-right-angle rotations, or a mesh with irregular sampling supports the action only approximately.

## Pitfalls

- **Borders, strides and pooling break shift equivariance.** Zero padding makes outputs near the border depend on
  absolute position, and a stride $s$ keeps equivariance only to shifts by multiples of $s$. Standard image classifiers
  change their output under one-pixel shifts for this reason <Cite id="azulay2019" />; blurring before subsampling
  restores most of the lost equivariance <Cite id="zhang2019antialiased" />.
- **One non-equivariant layer spoils the stack.** A <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional embedding</NoteLink>,
  a flatten followed by a dense layer, or a learned per-position bias makes the network depend on absolute position.
- **Pointwise nonlinearities on vectors.** A ReLU applied to the coordinates of a vector feature breaks rotation
  equivariance, as the example under "Building equivariant networks" shows.
- **Exact symmetry is not always worth its cost.** Equivariant layers are more expensive per parameter and narrower in
  what they can express. With enough data and augmentation, unconstrained models can match them:
  <NoteLink to="protein-structure-prediction">AlphaFold 3</NoteLink> predicts atom coordinates with a non-equivariant
  network trained on randomly rotated and translated structures <Cite id="abramson2024alphafold3" />. Equivariance pays
  most when data are scarce and the symmetry is exact, as for
  <NoteLink to="machine-learned-interatomic-potentials">interatomic potentials</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="batzner2022nequip" />.
