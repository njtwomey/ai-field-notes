<Definition>
A **novelty effect** is a treatment effect that is large when users first meet a change, because it is new, and
declines as they become used to it. A **primacy effect**, or change aversion, is the reverse: users who are used to the
old experience first respond worse to the new one, and the effect improves as they learn it. In both cases the effect
depends on the time since a user's first exposure, and an experiment that ends before the transient has passed
estimates a mixture of transient and long-run effects <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A new button colour draws clicks because it is unfamiliar. After a week, users stop noticing it. A redesigned menu
slows down users who knew where everything was. After a week, they are as fast as before, or faster. The experiment
measures what users do during the experiment, and the launch decision is about what they will do for the following
year. When the two differ, the experiment's length decides which one it reports.

## Details

**A model.** Let the effect on a user $t$ days after first exposure be

$$
\tau(t) = \tau_\infty + (\tau_0 - \tau_\infty)\,e^{-t/\lambda},
$$

where $\tau_0$ is the initial effect, $\tau_\infty$ the long-run effect and $\lambda$ the time constant in days.
$\tau_0 > \tau_\infty$ describes novelty and $\tau_0 < \tau_\infty$ primacy.

**The estimate.** Suppose every user is first exposed on day 0 and the experiment weights each of its $T$ days
equally. Its estimate is the average of $\tau(t)$ over $[0, T]$:

$$
\bar\tau(T) = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \tau(t)\,dt = \tau_\infty + (\tau_0 - \tau_\infty)\,\frac{\lambda}{T}\paren{1 - e^{-T/\lambda}},
$$

using $\int_0^T e^{-t/\lambda}\,dt = \lambda\paren{1 - e^{-T/\lambda}}$. For $T$ much larger than $\lambda$ the bias is
approximately $(\tau_0 - \tau_\infty)\lambda/T$. It decays like $1/T$, not exponentially, because the early days
remain in the average: doubling the experiment's length roughly halves the bias.

**Worked example.** A change has an initial lift of $\tau_0 = 5\%$ that decays to $\tau_\infty = 1\%$ with
$\lambda = 3$ days. After $T = 14$ days,
$\bar\tau = 1 + 4 \times \tfrac{3}{14}\paren{1 - e^{-14/3}} = 1.85\%$, which overstates the long-run lift by 85%.
After 28 days the estimate is 1.43%. The daily effect is within 0.04 points of $\tau_\infty$ by day 14, but the
estimate still carries the first days.

**Staggered entry.** In practice users enter throughout the experiment, the heaviest users first. On a given calendar
day the experiment mixes users at different times since exposure, and the users who arrive late have had little time
to get past the transient. The calendar-time estimate therefore approaches $\tau_\infty$ even more slowly than the
model above.

## Detection

- **Effect by time since exposure.** Plotting the treatment effect against days since each user's first exposure,
  rather than against calendar days, separates the transient from changes in who is in the experiment.
- **New against existing users.** Users who never saw the old experience cannot experience change aversion or novelty
  relative to it. A large difference between the effect on new users and on existing users points to a transient.
- **Long-term holdouts.** Keeping a small share of users on the old experience for weeks or months after launch
  measures the long-run effect directly.
- **Learning experiments.** Hohnhold, O'Brien and Tang expose one group of users to a treatment for a long period, then
  give it and a control group the same experience and measure the difference that remains. That difference is what
  users learned <Cite id="hohnhold2015" />.

## Assumptions

- The model assumes one exponential transient and a stable long-run effect. Real effects can have several time scales,
  and seasonal changes can look like decay.

## Pitfalls

- **Declining effects that are not novelty.** An effect estimated by calendar day can fall because heavy users
  entered first and lighter users later, even when each user's effect is constant. The plot by time since exposure
  tells these apart. See <NoteLink to="reweighting-biased-samples">reweighting biased samples</NoteLink>.
- **Stopping at the first significant result.** A novelty effect is largest at the start, so an experiment stopped
  as soon as it becomes significant is especially likely to report an inflated effect; see
  <NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping">peeking and optional stopping</NoteLink>.
- **Reading a declining cumulative estimate as a trend.** The cumulative estimate and its <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> also
  change as data accumulate by chance. A trend should be judged from the daily or per-cohort effects and their
  intervals.
