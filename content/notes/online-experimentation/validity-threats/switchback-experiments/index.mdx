<Definition>
A **switchback experiment** divides time into periods, optionally within each of several regions, and randomly assigns
each period to treatment or control. Every user and every transaction in a period experiences the same variant. The
treatment effect is estimated by comparing outcomes in treated and control periods. **Carryover** is the part of a
period's outcome caused by the previous period's variant. A **burn-in** discards the start of each period, where
carryover is concentrated <Cite id="bojinov2023" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In a ride-hailing market, a new pricing rule shown to half the riders changes which drivers are free for the other
half. Riders and drivers share one pool, so user-level randomisation is contaminated by
<NoteLink to="interference-and-cluster-randomisation">interference</NoteLink>, and a city can be too tightly connected
to split into clusters. Switching the whole market between variants over time makes the comparison one between
market states, each with a single variant. The price is that consecutive periods are not independent: drivers
positioned by the previous period's rule are still where it put them.

## Details

**The estimator.** Let $n$ periods of length $L$ each be assigned treatment independently with probability $1/2$, and
let $\bar Y_k$ be the mean outcome in period $k$. The estimate is the mean of $\bar Y_k$ over treated periods minus the
mean over control periods. The independent units are the periods, so the standard error is roughly
$\sigma_p\sqrt{4/n}$, where $\sigma_p$ is the standard deviation of a period mean. Periods at the same time of day
or day of week differ systematically, so randomising within blocks of comparable periods, such as each hour of a
week, removes that variation from the comparison.

**Carryover bias.** Suppose the treatment raises the outcome by $\tau$, but the system responds with a lag: for the
first $c$ minutes of each period the outcome still reflects the previous period's variant, and afterwards it
reflects the current one. With $T_k \in \set{0, 1}$ the assignment of period $k$, the expected effect contained in
period $k$ is

$$
\tau\paren{\frac{L - c}{L}\,T_k + \frac{c}{L}\,T_{k-1}}.
$$

Assignments are independent, so $\expect[T_{k-1} \mid T_k] = 1/2$. Treated periods contain on average
$\tau\paren{(L - c)/L + c/(2L)}$ and control periods $\tau c/(2L)$. The difference is

$$
\tau\paren{1 - \frac{c}{L}}.
$$

The carryover appears equally, on average, in both kinds of period, so it cancels, and what remains is the
treatment's effect on the uncontaminated share of each period. The estimate is biased towards zero by the factor
$1 - c/L$.

**Burn-in.** Discarding the first $b \ge c$ minutes of every period removes all contaminated data, and the estimate on
the remaining minutes is unbiased for $\tau$. It keeps a share $(L - b)/L$ of the data.

**Worked example.** A pricing change has a carryover of $c = 10$ minutes.

| Period length $L$ | Estimate without burn-in | Data kept with a 10-minute burn-in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 minutes | $0.83\,\tau$ | 83% |
| 30 minutes | $0.67\,\tau$ | 67% |
| 20 minutes | $0.50\,\tau$ | 50% |

Short periods give many periods and a small standard error, but they lose a large share of each period to carryover
or to the burn-in. Long periods waste little of each period but give few periods. The period length balances the two,
and it should exceed the carryover by a wide margin.

**Optimal designs.** Bojinov, Simchi-Levi and Zhao derive switchback designs that are minimax optimal when the
carryover lasts at most a known number of periods, give randomisation-based inference for them, and propose a
data-driven procedure to identify the length of the carryover <Cite id="bojinov2023" />.

## Assumptions

- **Carryover shorter than the burn-in.** If the system takes longer to settle than the burn-in, the estimate is still
  biased towards zero.
- **No anticipation.** Outcomes in a period do not depend on future assignments. This holds when users and algorithms
  cannot know the schedule.
- **Stable effect over the experiment.** The periods sample different times, so the estimate averages the effect over
  the times sampled. Blocking by time of day makes that average explicit.

## Pitfalls

- **Users as the unit of analysis.** A switchback experiment has as many independent units as periods, often a few
  hundred. A standard error computed over users or rides is far too small <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
- **Users who span periods.** A user whose session crosses a switch sees both variants. Metrics that depend on user
  learning, such as retention, cannot be measured by a switchback.
- **Regular schedules.** Alternating variants on a fixed pattern, such as every other hour, can align with periodic
  demand and bias the comparison. Assignments must be random.
