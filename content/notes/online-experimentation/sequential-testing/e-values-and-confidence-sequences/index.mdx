<Definition>
An **e-value** for a <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> $H_0$ is a non-negative statistic $E$ with $\expect_P[E] \le 1$ for every
distribution $P$ in $H_0$. Rejecting when $E \ge 1/\alpha$ is a level-$\alpha$ test. A **test martingale** is a
non-negative process $M_0 = 1, M_1, M_2, \dots$ that is a martingale, or supermartingale, under $H_0$; its value at any
stopping time is an e-value. A **confidence sequence** for a parameter $\theta$ is a sequence of sets $C_1, C_2, \dots$
with $P\paren{\theta \in C_n \text{ for all } n} \ge 1 - \alpha$. Inverting a family of test martingales, one for each
candidate value of $\theta$, gives a confidence sequence <Cite id="ramdas2023" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An e-value is the payoff of a bet. The analyst buys a ticket for 1 that pays $E$, priced so that the bet does not make
money on average if $H_0$ is true. Turning 1 into 20 is then evidence against $H_0$: by Markov's inequality, the chance
of doing so under $H_0$ is at most $1/20$ <Cite id="shafer2021" />. The winnings of one bet can be staked on the next,
so evidence from a sequence of observations multiplies. The gambler can stop whenever they like, because no stopping
rule turns a fair game into a profitable one.

A confidence sequence is the interval version. A 95% <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> computed at one planned sample size covers
the truth with probability 0.95. Computed after every observation, the pointwise intervals miss the truth at some point
with probability that grows toward 1. A confidence sequence is wider at each sample size and covers the truth at all of
them together.

## Details

**E-values are tests.** For any non-negative $E$ and $a > 0$, Markov's inequality gives $P(E \ge a) \le \expect[E]/a$.
Under $H_0$, $\expect[E] \le 1$, so $P_{H_0}(E \ge 1/\alpha) \le \alpha$. See
<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's inequality</NoteLink>. $p = \min(1, 1/E)$ is then a valid
<NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink>, though usually a conservative one.

**Examples.**

- **Likelihood ratios.** For a simple null $f_0$ and any fixed density $f_1$, $E = f_1(X)/f_0(X)$ has
  $\expect_{f_0}[E] = \int f_1 = 1$. Averages of likelihood ratios over a prior are also e-values, which is how the
  <NoteLink to="mixture-sequential-probability-ratio-test">mixture sequential probability ratio test (mSPRT)</NoteLink> is built.
- **Calibrated p-values.** If $p$ is a valid p-value and $0 < \kappa < 1$, then $E = \kappa\,p^{\kappa - 1}$ is an
  e-value, because $\int_0^1 \kappa p^{\kappa - 1}\,dp = 1$ and the function decreases in $p$ <Cite id="vovk2021" />.
  With $\kappa = 1/2$, $p = 0.01$ becomes $E = 5$. The calibrated e-value is smaller than $1/p$.

**Combining e-values.** The average of any e-values for the same null is an e-value, whatever their dependence, by
<NoteLink to="expectation">linearity of expectation</NoteLink>. The product of e-values is an e-value when each has <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">conditional expectation</NoteLink> at most 1 given
the previous ones, as in successive experiments where each e-value is computed from new data. If two independent
experiments give $E_1 = 4$ and $E_2 = 6$, their product 24 exceeds 20 and rejects at $\alpha = 0.05$. Whether to run the
second experiment may depend on the first result; this is called optional continuation <Cite id="grunwald2024" />.

**Test martingales and anytime validity.** Let $E_1, E_2, \dots$ each have conditional expectation at most 1 given the
past, and let $M_n = E_1 E_2 \cdots E_n$. Then $M_n$ is a non-negative supermartingale with $M_0 = 1$. Ville's
inequality, proved by <NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping">optional stopping</NoteLink> in the <NoteLink to="mixture-sequential-probability-ratio-test">mSPRT note</NoteLink>,
states

$$
P_{H_0}\paren{M_n \ge 1/\alpha \text{ for some } n} \le \alpha.
$$

So the analyst may monitor $M_n$ continuously and reject the first time it reaches $1/\alpha$.

**From test martingales to confidence sequences.** Suppose that for each candidate value $\theta_0$ there is a test
martingale $M_n(\theta_0)$ for $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$. Define

$$
C_n = \set{\theta_0 : M_n(\theta_0) < 1/\alpha}.
$$

The true $\theta$ is excluded from some $C_n$ only if $M_n(\theta)$ reaches $1/\alpha$ at some $n$, which by Ville's
inequality has probability at most $\alpha$. Hence $P(\theta \in C_n \text{ for all } n) \ge 1 - \alpha$.

**The normal-mixture confidence sequence.** For observations $X_i \iid \Gauss(\theta, 1)$ and a mixing distribution
$\Gauss(0, \rho^2)$ on the shift, the mixture likelihood ratio of the mSPRT with $V_n = 1/n$ and $\tau = \rho$ gives the
confidence sequence <Cite id="robbins1970" />

$$
\bar X_n \pm \sqrt{\frac{1 + n\rho^2}{n^2\rho^2}\paren{2\ln\frac{1}{\alpha} + \ln\paren{1 + n\rho^2}}}.
$$

For large $n$ the half-width behaves like $\sqrt{\ln n / n}$. The pointwise interval $\bar X_n \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}/\sqrt n$,
with $z_q$ the standard normal $q$-quantile, shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$. The law of the iterated logarithm shows that no
confidence sequence can shrink faster than $\sqrt{\ln\ln n / n}$ as $n$ grows. Stitched boundaries attain that rate, and similar
constructions give confidence sequences without the normality assumption <Cite id="howard2021" />. For bounded
observations, confidence sequences can also be built from betting strategies <Cite id="waudby2024" />.

## Worked example

With $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\rho = 1$, the half-widths for unit-variance data are:

| Observations $n$ | Pointwise interval | Confidence sequence | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 0.620 | 0.961 | 1.55 |
| 100 | 0.196 | 0.327 | 1.67 |
| 1,000 | 0.062 | 0.114 | 1.83 |
| 10,000 | 0.020 | 0.039 | 1.99 |

The confidence sequence is 1.5 to 2 times wider. In return, in 20,000 simulated streams of 2,000 observations each,
checked after every observation, the pointwise interval excluded the true mean at some point in 57% of streams. The
confidence sequence did so in 2.9% with $\rho = 1$ and 3.4% with $\rho = 0.5$, below the 5% guaranteed.

## Assumptions

- **Correct validity conditions.** A test martingale must be a supermartingale under every distribution in $H_0$. For
  the normal mixture this means independent observations with known variance; with an estimated variance the
  guarantee holds only approximately.
- **Fixed choices.** The mixing scale $\rho$, like the mSPRT's $\tau$, must be chosen before the data are seen. It
  sets the sample size at which the sequence is tightest and does not affect validity.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing widths at one sample size.** A confidence sequence is wider than a confidence interval at every $n$.
  The comparison that matters is with an interval that remains valid under the monitoring actually done.
- **Multiplying dependent e-values.** A product is valid only when each factor has conditional expectation at most 1
  given the previous ones. E-values from overlapping data must be averaged, not multiplied.
- **Converting p-values and back.** An e-value from a calibrated p-value is smaller than $1/p$, and $\min(1, 1/E)$ is
  larger than $p$. E-values are useful for monitoring and combining evidence, not for making a single fixed-sample
  test more powerful.
