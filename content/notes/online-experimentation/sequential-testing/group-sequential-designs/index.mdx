<Definition>
A **group-sequential design** plans up to $K$ analyses of an accumulating experiment, at information fractions
$0 < t_1 < \dots < t_K = 1$ of a maximum sample size. At look $k$ it computes the usual z-statistic $Z_k$ from all data
so far and stops, rejecting $H_0$, if $\abs{Z_k} \ge c_k$. The boundaries $c_1, \dots, c_K$ are chosen so that
$P_{H_0}\paren{\abs{Z_k} \ge c_k \text{ for some } k} = \alpha$. An **alpha-spending function** $\alpha(t)$, increasing
from $\alpha(0) = 0$ to $\alpha(1) = \alpha$, sets the boundaries look by look: the probability under $H_0$ of stopping
at or before look $k$ is $\alpha(t_k)$ <Cite id="lan1983" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Checking an experiment repeatedly with the fixed-sample threshold of 1.96 gives the z-statistic under $H_0$ many
opportunities to cross it, and the <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> grows with every look, as shown in
<NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping">peeking and optional stopping</NoteLink>. A group-sequential design keeps
the looks but raises the threshold. The total false-positive rate is a budget $\alpha$, and the design decides in
advance how much of it each look may use. Spending little early keeps the final analysis almost as powerful as a
fixed-sample test. Spending more early makes <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> more likely when the effect is large.

## Details

**The joint distribution of the looks.** Let $S_k$ be the sum of the standardised data up to look $k$, with variance
$I_k$, the information, proportional to the sample size. Then $Z_k = S_k/\sqrt{I_k}$ and $t_k = I_k/I_K$. For
$j \le k$, $S_k = S_j + (S_k - S_j)$, and the increment is independent of $S_j$, so

$$
\cov(Z_j, Z_k) = \frac{\var(S_j)}{\sqrt{I_j I_k}} = \sqrt{\frac{I_j}{I_k}} = \sqrt{\frac{t_j}{t_k}}.
$$

Under $H_0$, $(Z_1, \dots, Z_K)$ is <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> with mean zero, unit variances and these correlations. Under an
effect $\theta$ per unit of information, $Z_k$ has mean $\theta\sqrt{I_k}$. Every probability a design needs is a
rectangle probability of this distribution. They are computed by propagating the density of $S_k$ from look to look,
discarding the paths that have crossed, as a convolution with the density of the independent increment
<Cite id="armitage1969" />.

**Classical boundaries.** Pocock's design uses a constant $c_k = c$ <Cite id="pocock1977" />. O'Brien and Fleming's
uses $c_k = c\sqrt{K/k}$, very strict early and close to the fixed-sample value at the end <Cite id="obrien1979" />.
Both require $K$ equally spaced looks fixed in advance. For $K = 5$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, Pocock's $c = 2.413$ and
O'Brien and Fleming's $c = 2.040$, giving boundaries 4.56, 3.23, 2.63, 2.28 and 2.04.

**Alpha spending.** Lan and DeMets replaced the fixed boundary shape by a spending function. At look $k$, with
$c_1, \dots, c_{k-1}$ already fixed, $c_k$ solves

$$
P_{H_0}\paren{\abs{Z_j} < c_j \text{ for } j < k,\ \abs{Z_k} \ge c_k} = \alpha(t_k) - \alpha(t_{k-1}).
$$

The equation uses only the information fractions reached so far. The number and timing of looks therefore need not be
fixed in advance, provided they are not chosen in response to the observed results <Cite id="demets1994" />. Two
functions approximate the classical designs:

$$
\alpha_{\text{OF}}(t) = 2\paren{2 - 2\Phi\paren{\frac{z_{1-\alpha/4}}{\sqrt t}}}, \qquad
\alpha_{\text{P}}(t) = \alpha\ln\paren{1 + (e - 1)t},
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">normal distribution</NoteLink> function and $z_q$ its $q$-quantile. The O'Brien–Fleming-type
function spends $2 - 2\Phi(z_{1-\alpha/4}/\sqrt t)$ in each tail. It is the one-sided function
$2 - 2\Phi(z_{1-\alpha'/2}/\sqrt t)$ at $\alpha' = \alpha/2$ per tail, the convention of the R packages ldbounds and
gsDesign. At $t = 1$ both functions equal $\alpha$.

**Worked example.** Five equally spaced looks at $\alpha = 0.05$:

| Look | $t$ | O'Brien–Fleming-type: cumulative $\alpha$ | $c_k$ | Nominal $p$ | Pocock-type: cumulative $\alpha$ | $c_k$ | Nominal $p$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.2 | 0.000001 | 4.88 | 0.000001 | 0.0148 | 2.44 | 0.0148 |
| 2 | 0.4 | 0.0008 | 3.36 | 0.0008 | 0.0262 | 2.43 | 0.0152 |
| 3 | 0.6 | 0.0076 | 2.68 | 0.0074 | 0.0354 | 2.41 | 0.0159 |
| 4 | 0.8 | 0.0244 | 2.29 | 0.0220 | 0.0432 | 2.40 | 0.0165 |
| 5 | 1.0 | 0.0500 | 2.03 | 0.0423 | 0.0500 | 2.39 | 0.0170 |

The nominal $p$ is the two-sided <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> at which each look stops, $2\paren{1 - \Phi(c_k)}$. A look stops if the
ordinary p-value computed at that look is below it. From look 2 onward the nominal values exceed the
increments of $\alpha(t)$: some of the paths beyond $c_k$ at look $k$ crossed at an earlier look, stopped there, and
use no new $\alpha$.

**What sequential looks cost and save.** A design that must keep power 0.8 against an effect $\delta$ needs a larger
maximum sample than the fixed-sample test, because its final boundary is above 1.96. It also stops early when the
effect is real. For the designs in the table, with power 0.8 at $\alpha = 0.05$:

| Design | Maximum sample, relative to fixed | Expected sample when the effect is $\delta$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| O'Brien–Fleming-type | 1.025 | 0.824 |
| Pocock-type | 1.212 | 0.796 |

The O'Brien–Fleming-type design costs 2.5% more users at most and saves 18% on average when the treatment works. The
Pocock-type design stops earlier on average but needs 21% more users if it runs to the end.

## Assumptions

- **Information fractions not chosen from the data.** The looks may be irregular, but their timing must not depend
  on the observed effect.
- **A fixed maximum.** The spending function is defined relative to the maximum information $I_K$. Extending an
  experiment beyond it requires a new design.
- **Normal statistics with independent increments.** This holds for differences in means of independent randomisation
  units once samples are large. Metrics whose per-user values keep changing, such as cumulative <NoteLink to="revenue-and-heavy-tailed-metrics">revenue per user</NoteLink>,
  need care, because a user's contribution at one look is not independent of their contribution at the next.

## Pitfalls

- **Biased estimates at stopping.** An experiment that stops early because $\abs{Z_k}$ crossed a high boundary
  selects estimates that are too large, as in the <NoteLink to="winners-curse">winner's curse</NoteLink>. The naive
  <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> at the stopping look does not have its nominal coverage; adjusted intervals order the possible
  outcomes by stopping look and value <Cite id="jennison2000" />.
- **Looking without a boundary.** Glancing at a dashboard is harmless only if no decision follows from it. Any look
  that could stop the experiment must be one of the design's looks.
- **Futility stopping without planning.** Stopping for lack of effect lowers power and must also be planned, with its
  own spending of the type II error. Stopping for futility never raises the false-positive rate.
