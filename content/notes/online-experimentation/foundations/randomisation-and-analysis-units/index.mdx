<Definition>
The **randomisation unit** of an online experiment is the entity that is assigned to a variant, such as a user, a
device, a session, a page view or a whole city. The **analysis unit** is the entity over which a metric is computed, for
example page views for a <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rate</Gloss>. Standard variance formulas treat analysis units as independent. That
holds when the analysis unit is the randomisation unit. When each randomisation unit contributes $m$ correlated
analysis units, the naive variance is too small by the **design effect** $1 + (m - 1)\rho$, where $\rho$ is the
correlation between analysis units of the same randomisation unit <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Randomisation makes the variants comparable at the level of the unit that was randomised. Two users in the same arm
are independent draws, so their contributions to a metric are independent. Two page views of the same user are not:
a user who clicks a lot on one page tends to click a lot on the next. A metric computed over page views therefore
contains fewer independent pieces of information than it has page views. A variance formula that counts page views as
independent overstates the information and understates the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>.

## Choosing the randomisation unit

- **Consistency of experience.** A user should see the same variant on every visit. Randomising by user, through a
  stable identifier hashed together with the experiment identifier, does this. Randomising by page view shows the
  same user both variants, which is noticeable for visible changes and lets one variant's effect leak into the other.
- **Granularity and power.** Finer units, such as sessions or page views, give more independent units and smaller
  standard errors. They are acceptable only when the treatment cannot affect later units, for example a change in
  server latency that users do not learn about.
- **Interference.** The unit must be coarse enough that treated and control units do not affect each other. Markets
  with shared supply, and social features, often need clusters such as cities or friend groups; see
  <NoteLink to="interference-and-cluster-randomisation">interference and cluster randomisation</NoteLink>.
- **Identifier quality.** Anonymous cookies are cleared and devices are shared, so one person can appear as several
  users and see several variants. Logged-in identifiers are more stable but cover only part of the traffic.

## Unit mismatch and the design effect

Let each arm contain $U$ randomisation units, each contributing $m$ analysis units with outcomes $Y_{uj}$,
$j = 1, \dots, m$. Assume every outcome has variance $\sigma^2$, outcomes of the same unit have correlation $\rho$,
and different units are independent. The arm mean is $\bar Y = \frac{1}{Um}\sum_{u}\sum_{j} Y_{uj}$. Its variance is
the sum of all variances and covariances, divided by $(Um)^2$:

$$
\var(\bar Y) = \frac{1}{(Um)^2} \sum_{u=1}^{U} \paren{m\sigma^2 + m(m - 1)\rho\sigma^2}
= \frac{\sigma^2}{Um}\,\paren{1 + (m - 1)\rho}.
$$

Each unit contributes $m$ variance terms and $m(m - 1)$ covariance terms, each equal to $\rho\sigma^2$. The naive
formula $\sigma^2/(Um)$ keeps only the variance terms. The ratio of the true variance to the naive one is the design
effect <Cite id="lohr2021" />

$$
D = 1 + (m - 1)\rho.
$$

$D = 1$ when $m = 1$ or $\rho = 0$. The effective number of independent observations is $Um/D$. For
$0 \le \rho \le 1$ it lies between $U$, reached at $\rho = 1$, and $Um$, reached at $\rho = 0$.

**The effect on an A/A test.** Under the <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> the naive statistic $Z_{\text{naive}} = \hat\Delta /
\widehat{\text{se}}_{\text{naive}}$ equals the correctly standardised statistic multiplied by $\sqrt D$, so
$Z_{\text{naive}} \sim \Gauss(0, D)$ approximately. The naive test rejects when $\abs{Z_{\text{naive}}} \ge
z_{1-\alpha/2}$, where $z_q$ is the standard normal $q$-quantile. Its <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> is

$$
P\paren{\abs{\Gauss(0, D)} \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}} = 2\paren{1 - \Phi\paren{\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt D}}},
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function. At $\alpha = 0.05$:

| Views per user $m$ | Correlation $\rho$ | Design effect $D$ | A/A false-positive rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 0.05 | 1.2 | 0.074 |
| 10 | 0.1 | 1.9 | 0.155 |
| 20 | 0.1 | 2.9 | 0.250 |

With 10 page views per user and a modest correlation of 0.1, an experiment with no effect is declared significant
15.5% of the time instead of 5%. An <NoteLink to="a-a-test">A/A test</NoteLink> analysed this way shows the problem as
too many small <NoteLink to="p-value">p-values</NoteLink>.

## Correct analyses

- **Aggregate to the randomisation unit.** Compute one value per user, such as clicks per user, and compare the
  per-user means. The users are independent, so the usual standard error is valid.
- **Ratio metrics.** A page-level rate such as total clicks over total page views is a ratio of two per-user sums. Its
  variance comes from the <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">delta method</NoteLink> applied to per-user
  totals <Cite id="deng2018" />. The delta method handles unequal numbers of views per user, which the design-effect
  formula with a single $m$ does not.
- **Resampling by unit.** A <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> must resample users, with all their page views, not individual page views.
- **Cluster-robust standard errors.** A regression on page-level data gives valid standard errors when they are
  clustered by user.

## Assumptions

- Randomisation units are independent of one another. Interference between users breaks this, and no analysis at the
  user level repairs it.
- Assignment is by a fixed unit identifier. If the identifier changes during the experiment, as with cleared cookies,
  one person becomes several units, and those units are correlated.

## Pitfalls

- **Binomial variance on page views.** Treating a click-through rate as a binomial proportion over page views assumes
  $\rho = 0$. The variance is too small whenever users differ in their propensity to click <Cite id="deng2017" />.
- **Power calculations at the wrong unit.** A power calculation that treats page views as independent understates
  the required sample size by the factor $D$. The variance must be the variance of the per-user metric; see
  <NoteLink to="a-b-test-power">power for A/B tests</NoteLink>.
