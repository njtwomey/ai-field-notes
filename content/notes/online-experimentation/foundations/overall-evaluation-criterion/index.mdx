<Definition>
The **overall evaluation criterion** (OEC) of an online experiment is the quantitative measure that the experiment is
designed to improve and on which the launch decision rests. It is a single metric or a weighted combination of
metrics, measured per randomisation unit during the experiment, and chosen so that a short-term improvement predicts
long-term value <Cite id="kohavi2009" />. **Guardrail metrics** are metrics that must not get worse. Each guardrail is
checked with a **non-inferiority test**: the treatment passes when the data rule out a degradation larger than a
margin set before the experiment.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An experiment measures dozens of metrics, and some move in opposite directions. Without an agreed criterion, the
decision drifts toward whichever metric looks best after the fact, which is the multiple-comparisons problem described
in <NoteLink to="many-metrics-and-variants">many metrics and variants</NoteLink>. Fixing the OEC before the experiment
turns the launch decision into a test of one pre-specified hypothesis. The guardrails bound how much other metrics may
degrade while the OEC improves.

## Kinds of metrics

- **Goal metrics** describe what the organisation ultimately wants, such as long-term revenue or retained users. They
  often move too slowly or too noisily to measure in a two-week experiment.
- **Driver metrics**, also called surrogate or signpost metrics, move within an experiment and are believed to cause
  the goal metrics. The OEC is built from driver metrics.
- **Guardrail metrics** protect the business and the validity of the experiment. Organisational guardrails include
  page load time, error and crash rates, and revenue. Trust-related guardrails check the experiment itself, such as
  the <NoteLink to="sample-ratio-mismatch">sample ratio</NoteLink> between arms.
- **Debug metrics** break the OEC and guardrails into components, so that a surprising movement can be explained.

This taxonomy follows <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.

## Properties of a good OEC

1. **Measurable in the experiment.** It is computed from the experiment's own data within its duration.
2. **Attributable.** It is defined per randomisation unit, so that it can be compared between arms with a valid
   variance.
3. **Sensitive.** It moves detectably for changes that matter. A metric with a large variance needs a large sample;
   see <NoteLink to="a-b-test-power">power for A/B tests</NoteLink>.
4. **Directionally aligned with long-term value.** A short-term gain in the OEC must not come from a change that hurts
   users. Metric development should check this against experiments with known outcomes <Cite id="deng2016" />.

A short-term gain in the OEC can easily come from a change that hurts users. Kohavi and colleagues report a
search-engine experiment in which a ranking bug that showed worse results raised queries per user by over 10% and
<NoteLink to="revenue-and-heavy-tailed-metrics">revenue per user</NoteLink> by over 30% <Cite id="kohavi2012" />. Users
searched more because they could not find what they wanted. Monthly queries factor as users, times sessions per user,
times queries per session. A better search engine should raise sessions per user and lower queries per session, so the
authors argue that sessions per user belongs in the OEC and that queries per user should not be maximised.

## Combining metrics

When several driver metrics matter, an OEC can be a weighted sum of their relative changes,

$$
\text{OEC} = \sum_{k} w_k \frac{\Delta_k}{\mu_k},
$$

where $\Delta_k$ is the treatment-minus-control difference in metric $k$, $\mu_k$ is its control mean and $w_k$ is a
weight that states how much a 1% change in metric $k$ is worth. The weights make the trade-offs explicit before the
data arrive. The variance of the combination includes the covariances between the metrics, which are estimated from
per-unit data in the same way as each metric's own variance.

## Guardrails as non-inferiority tests

Let $\Delta$ be the true relative change in a guardrail metric for which larger values are worse, such as page load
time. A **non-inferiority margin** $\delta_{\text{NI}} > 0$ is the largest degradation the organisation accepts. The
hypotheses are

$$
H_0: \Delta \ge \delta_{\text{NI}} \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_1: \Delta < \delta_{\text{NI}}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> is that the treatment is unacceptably
worse. With estimate $\hat\Delta$ and <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> $\widehat{\text{se}}$, the
level-$\alpha$ test rejects $H_0$, and the guardrail passes, when the one-sided <Gloss name="ucb">upper confidence
bound</Gloss> lies below the margin:

$$
\hat\Delta + z_{1-\alpha}\,\widehat{\text{se}} < \delta_{\text{NI}},
$$

where $z_q$ is the standard normal $q$-quantile. This is the <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink> of
$H_0: \Delta = \delta_{\text{NI}}$ with a one-sided alternative, rewritten as a confidence bound: the statistic
$(\hat\Delta - \delta_{\text{NI}})/\widehat{\text{se}}$ is below $-z_{1-\alpha}$ exactly when the inequality holds.

**Worked example.** A new page layout is tested with page load time as a guardrail, a margin of $+2\%$ and
$\alpha = 0.05$, so $z_{0.95} = 1.645$.

| Estimate $\hat\Delta$ | Standard error | Upper bound $\hat\Delta + 1.645\,\widehat{\text{se}}$ | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $+0.8\%$ | $0.5\%$ | $+1.62\%$ | passes |
| $+0.8\%$ | $1.0\%$ | $+2.45\%$ | not shown to pass |

In the second row the <NoteLink to="one-and-two-sided-tests">two-sided test</NoteLink> of $\Delta = 0$ gives $z = 0.8$
and $p = 0.42$, so the slowdown is not significant. A rule that passes a guardrail whenever the degradation is not
significant would pass it. The data are nevertheless compatible with a slowdown of 2.45%, beyond the margin. The
non-inferiority test puts the burden of proof on the treatment, so an underpowered experiment cannot pass a guardrail by
default.

**Sample size for a guardrail.** For power $1 - \beta$ to pass when the true change is zero, the standard error must
satisfy $(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta})\,\text{se} \le \delta_{\text{NI}}$. The derivation is the one for any one-sided
z-test: under $\Delta = 0$ the upper bound is normal with mean $z_{1-\alpha}\,\text{se}$, and it must fall below
$\delta_{\text{NI}}$ with probability $1 - \beta$. With $\alpha = 0.05$, power 0.8 and a 2% margin, the standard error
must be at most $2\% / (1.645 + 0.842) = 0.80\%$.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing the OEC after seeing the data.** Any metric chosen because it moved is a biased estimate of that
  movement, and its <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is not valid.
- **An OEC that rewards harm.** Metrics such as clicks or queries can rise because users struggle. Every candidate
  OEC should be checked for changes that raise it while making the product worse.
- **Absence of a significant degradation.** A non-significant change in a guardrail is not evidence that it held.
  Only a confidence bound inside the margin is.
- **Many guardrails.** A harmless treatment must pass every non-inferiority test, so its chance of failing at least
  one grows with the number of guardrails, unless each test has high power. A rule that alarms on any significant
  degradation has the opposite problem: its chance of a false alarm grows with the number of guardrails. The decision
  rule must be fixed in advance; see <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>.
- **Click metrics in recommender tests.** A recommender that surfaces already popular items can win on
  <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rate</Gloss> while reducing discovery and diversity. Guardrails on
  catalogue coverage, diversity and long-term retention catch this. See
  <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in recommendation</NoteLink>.
