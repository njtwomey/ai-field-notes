<Definition>
The **power** of an A/B test is the probability that it rejects the null hypothesis of no difference when the true
difference between arms is $\delta$. For a two-sided test at level $\alpha$ comparing two means, each estimated from
$n$ independent randomisation units with per-unit standard deviation $\sigma$, the power is $1 - \beta$ when

$$
n = \frac{2\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2}
$$

units per arm, where $z_q$ is the standard normal $q$-quantile. The smallest $\delta$ detectable for a given $n$ is
the minimum detectable effect (MDE). The required duration is the time the experiment takes to enrol the required
number of distinct units.
</Definition>

## Problem

A team must decide, before an experiment starts, how many users to enrol and for how long. Too few users give an
experiment that cannot detect the lifts the team cares about, and whose significant results overstate the effect, as
in the <NoteLink to="winners-curse">winner's curse</NoteLink>. Too many users waste traffic that other experiments
could use and delay the decision.

## Mechanism

**The sample size.** The estimated difference $\hat\Delta = \bar Y_T - \bar Y_C$ of two arm means with $n$ units each
has standard error $\text{se} = \sigma\sqrt{2/n}$, assuming a common per-unit variance $\sigma^2$. The two-sided test
rejects when $\abs{\hat\Delta}/\text{se} \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$. If the true difference is $\delta > 0$, then
$\hat\Delta/\text{se} \sim \Gauss(\delta/\text{se}, 1)$ approximately. Ignoring the negligible chance of rejecting in
the wrong direction, the power is

$$
1 - \beta = P\paren{\frac{\hat\Delta}{\text{se}} \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}} = 1 - \Phi\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} - \frac{\delta}{\text{se}}},
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function. Solving gives $\delta = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})\,
\text{se}$: the true difference must lie $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ standard errors beyond zero to reach the critical value, and
a further $z_{1-\beta}$ standard errors beyond that so that a fraction $1 - \beta$ of the estimates fall past it.
Substituting $\text{se} = \sigma\sqrt{2/n}$ and solving for $n$ gives the formula in the definition. The general form of
this argument is in <NoteLink to="sample-size-planning">sample-size planning</NoteLink>.

For $\alpha = 0.05$ and power 0.8, $z_{0.975} + z_{0.8} = 1.960 + 0.842 = 2.802$ and $2 \times 2.802^2 = 15.7$. Rounding
up gives the **rule of 16**, $n \approx 16\sigma^2/\delta^2$ per arm <Cite id="kohavi2009" />.

**Relative lifts.** Experiments are usually planned for a relative lift $r$, so $\delta = r\mu$ for a control mean
$\mu$. Then

$$
n = \frac{2\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}^2}{r^2}\paren{\frac{\sigma}{\mu}}^2.
$$

Only the coefficient of variation $\sigma/\mu$ of the per-unit metric matters. For a conversion rate $p$, each unit
converts or not, $\sigma^2 = p(1 - p)$ and $(\sigma/\mu)^2 = (1 - p)/p$. Rare events have a large coefficient of
variation and need large samples. Revenue per user has a larger one still; see
<NoteLink to="revenue-and-heavy-tailed-metrics">revenue and heavy-tailed metrics</NoteLink>.

**Unequal allocation.** Let a fraction $q$ of $N$ units go to treatment and $1 - q$ to control. Then

$$
\text{se}^2 = \sigma^2\paren{\frac{1}{qN} + \frac{1}{(1 - q)N}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{N q (1 - q)},
\qquad
N = \frac{\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}^2 \sigma^2}{\delta^2\, q(1 - q)}.
$$

$q(1 - q)$ is largest at $q = 1/2$, so an equal split needs the fewest units in total. Any other split multiplies $N$
by $1/\paren{4q(1 - q)}$: 1.33 for $q = 0.25$ and 2.78 for $q = 0.1$.

**Duration.** If $U(d)$ distinct eligible units arrive in the first $d$ days, the experiment needs the smallest $d$
with $U(d) \ge N$. When most visitors on a given day are returning users, $U(d)$ grows more slowly than the traffic, and
the MDE falls more slowly than $1/\sqrt d$. Suppose each of $P$ users visits on any day with probability $\pi$,
independently. The chance that a user has not visited in $d$ days is $(1 - \pi)^d$, so

$$
U(d) = P\paren{1 - (1 - \pi)^d}.
$$

The daily traffic is $P\pi$, but $U(d)$ approaches $P$ and stops growing.

## Worked example

A shop converts $p = 5\%$ of users and wants to detect a relative lift of $r = 2\%$, from 5.0% to 5.1%, at
$\alpha = 0.05$ with power 0.8. The absolute difference is $\delta = 0.001$ and $\sigma^2 = 0.05 \times 0.95 =
0.0475$.

1. **Per arm.** $n = 15.698 \times 0.0475 / 0.001^2 = 745{,}644$ users per arm, 1.49 million in total. The rule of 16
   gives 760,000 per arm.
2. **A 10% treatment.** With $q = 0.1$ the total is $1.49\text{ million} \times 2.78 = 4.14$ million users. A 10/10
   split that leaves the other 80% of traffic out of the experiment needs 1.49 million users in the experiment, and
   therefore 7.46 million users of traffic. When traffic, not risk, is the constraint, giving control the other 90%
   uses it better than a small equal split.
3. **Duration.** If 100,000 new eligible users arrive each day and the split is 50/50, the experiment needs
   $1.49\text{ million}/100{,}000 = 14.9$ days. Rounding up to whole weeks, so that each day of the week is equally
   represented, gives three weeks.

## Cost

The sample size scales as $1/\delta^2$. Halving the detectable lift quadruples the users needed. It scales linearly in
$\sigma^2$, so any reduction in the per-unit variance acts as extra traffic:
<NoteLink to="cuped">CUPED</NoteLink> with a pre-experiment covariate of correlation $\rho$ multiplies $\sigma^2$ by
$1 - \rho^2$, and <NoteLink to="triggering-and-dilution">triggering</NoteLink> removes units the change cannot affect.

## Trade-offs

- **Allocation against risk.** A small treatment share limits the harm of a bad change but needs more units. Many
  teams ramp up: a few percent of traffic first to catch crashes, then an equal split for the measurement.
- **Duration against novelty.** A longer run detects smaller lifts and averages over weekly cycles and short-lived
  <NoteLink to="novelty-and-primacy-effects">novelty effects</NoteLink>. It also delays the decision and exposes more
  users to a possibly worse variant.
- **Power against false discoveries.** Lowering $\alpha$ from 0.05 to 0.01 raises $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ from 1.96 to 2.58
  and the sample size by the factor $\paren{(2.576 + 0.842)/(1.960 + 0.842)}^2 = 1.49$.

## Pitfalls

- **Variance at the wrong unit.** $\sigma^2$ must be the variance of the metric per randomisation unit. Using
  page-level variance for a user-randomised test understates the required sample; see
  <NoteLink to="randomisation-and-analysis-units">randomisation and analysis units</NoteLink>.
- **Planning for an optimistic lift.** The MDE should be the smallest lift worth acting on, not the lift the team
  hopes for. Planning for a large lift gives an underpowered test for realistic ones.
- **Stopping when significant.** The calculation assumes one analysis at the planned sample size. Checking repeatedly
  and stopping at the first significant result inflates the false-positive rate, as shown in
  <NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping">peeking and optional stopping</NoteLink>.
- **Post-hoc power.** Power computed from the observed effect after the experiment adds nothing to its p-value.
