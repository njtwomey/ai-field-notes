<Definition>
An <Gloss name="aa-test" /> splits users between two identical versions of a product, so every
<NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> is true. It is planned for 10,000 users per
arm and a two-sided $z$-test at $\alpha = 0.05$. Instead of waiting, the analyst looks at the p-value after every 1,000
users per arm and stops as soon as $p < 0.05$. What is the probability of declaring a difference?
</Definition>

## Setup

Split the experiment into $K = 10$ equal batches. Let $X_i$ be the standardised difference in means computed from
batch $i$ alone. Under the null hypothesis the batches are independent and $X_i \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. After $j$
batches, the $z$-statistic for all the data so far is

$$
Z_j = \frac{S_j}{\sqrt j}, \qquad S_j = X_1 + \dots + X_j,
$$

because $S_j$ has variance $j$. Each $Z_j$ on its own is standard normal, so each single look is a valid test at level
$\alpha$. The procedure rejects if $\abs{Z_j} \ge z$ at any look $j \le K$, with $z = z_{1 - \alpha/2} = 1.96$. The
question asks for

$$
P\paren{\max_{j \le K} \abs{Z_j} \ge z}.
$$

## Solution

The looks are not independent: they share data. For $i \le j$,

$$
\cov(Z_i, Z_j) = \frac{\cov(S_i, S_j)}{\sqrt{ij}} = \frac{\var(S_i)}{\sqrt{ij}} = \frac{i}{\sqrt{ij}} = \sqrt{\frac{i}{j}},
$$

since $S_j = S_i + (X_{i+1} + \dots + X_j)$ and the added batches are independent of $S_i$. Consecutive looks late in
the experiment are highly correlated; early looks are nearly independent of late ones.

**Two looks.** $(Z_1, Z_2)$ is bivariate normal with correlation $\sqrt{1/2} = 0.707$. The probability that neither
crosses is the bivariate normal probability of the square $\abs{z_1} < 1.96$, $\abs{z_2} < 1.96$, which is 0.917. The
<Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> is therefore $1 - 0.917 = 0.083$, not 0.05. The second look adds 0.033:
the chance that the statistic crosses at the second look after missing at the first.

**K looks.** Each extra look adds a smaller increment, because it is more correlated with the looks before it, but the
increments never stop. Propagating the distribution of $S_j$ look by look, discarding the paths that have crossed,
gives the exact rates <Cite id="armitage1969" />:

| Looks $K$ | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 50 | 100 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $P(\text{false positive})$ | 0.050 | 0.083 | 0.142 | 0.193 | 0.248 | 0.320 | 0.373 |

With 10 looks the analyst declares a difference in 19% of A/A tests, almost four times the nominal rate.

**Unlimited looks.** If the experiment can run indefinitely and is checked after every user, the rate tends to 1. The
law of the iterated logarithm states that $\limsup_{n \to \infty} S_n / \sqrt{2n \log\log n} = 1$ with probability 1. So
$Z_n = S_n / \sqrt n$ exceeds $(1 - \epsilon)\sqrt{2\log\log n}$ infinitely often for every $\epsilon > 0$. That bound
grows without limit, so $\abs{Z_n}$ eventually passes 1.96, or any fixed threshold, with certainty. An analyst who
keeps collecting data until the test is significant is guaranteed to get there. This is sampling to a foregone
conclusion.

## What it illustrates

- **A p-value is valid for one planned analysis.** Its guarantee assumes the sample size was fixed in advance.
  Choosing when to stop by looking at the <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> changes the procedure, and the
  false-positive rate is that of the whole procedure, not of any single look.
- **Peeking is multiple testing through time.** Each look is another test of the same hypothesis. It inflates the error
  rate less than independent tests would, because the looks are correlated, but still to 0.193 with 10 looks and 0.373
  with 100. See <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="many-metrics-and-variants">many metrics and variants</NoteLink>.
- **Fixing the horizon is the simplest remedy.** Choose the sample size with
  <NoteLink to="sample-size-planning">sample-size planning</NoteLink>, run to that size, and test once. Monitoring the
  dashboard is harmless as long as the stopping decision ignores it.

## Variations

- **Pocock's boundary.** Use the same raised threshold $c$ at every look, chosen so that
  $P(\max_{j \le K} \abs{Z_j} \ge c) = \alpha$ <Cite id="pocock1977" />. For $K = 5$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, $c = 2.413$;
  for $K = 10$, $c = 2.555$. The overall rate is exactly 0.05, but the final analysis needs $\abs{Z} \ge 2.41$ rather
  than 1.96, so a test that runs to the end has less power than a fixed-sample test of the same size.
- **O'Brien–Fleming's boundary.** Use $c\sqrt{K/j}$ at look $j$, which is very strict early and close to 1.96 at the end
  <Cite id="obrien1979" />. For $K = 5$ and $\alpha = 0.05$, $c = 2.040$, giving thresholds 4.56, 3.23, 2.63, 2.28 and
  2.04. <NoteLink to="early-stopping">Early stopping</NoteLink> needs overwhelming evidence, and the final test loses
  almost nothing to the interim looks. Most clinical trials with interim analyses use boundaries of this shape, often
  through an alpha-spending function that allows unequal or unplanned looks <Cite id="jennison2000" />. See
  <NoteLink to="group-sequential-designs">group-sequential designs and alpha spending</NoteLink>.
- **Always-valid p-values.** Sequential tests such as the mixture sequential probability ratio test produce p-values
  that remain valid however often they are checked and whenever the experiment stops <Cite id="johari2017" />. They
  suit online experimentation, where continuous monitoring is the norm, at some cost in power relative to a
  well-planned fixed-horizon test. See <NoteLink to="mixture-sequential-probability-ratio-test">mSPRT and always-valid
  p-values</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="e-values-and-confidence-sequences">e-values and confidence
  sequences</NoteLink>.
- **Peeking under a real effect.** When the treatment works, early stopping on a crossing selects experiments whose
  noise pushed the estimate up. The estimate at stopping is exaggerated, as in the
  <NoteLink to="winners-curse">winner's curse</NoteLink>.
