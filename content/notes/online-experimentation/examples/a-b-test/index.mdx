<Definition>
The current checkout page converts 10% of visitors. A new page is worth launching if it raises conversion by at least
1 percentage point, to 11%. The team wants a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$ with 80% power to detect that lift.
How many users does each arm need, and what does the team conclude if arm A converts 1,475 users and arm B 1,652?
</Definition>

## Setup

Arm A (the old page) and arm B (the new page) each get $n$ users, assigned at random. Their conversion rates are $p_A$
and $p_B$, and the observed rates are $\hat p_A = X_A / n$ and $\hat p_B = X_B / n$ with $X_A \sim \Binom(n, p_A)$ and
$X_B \sim \Binom(n, p_B)$. The hypotheses are

$$
H_0: p_B = p_A \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_1: p_B \ne p_A.
$$

The smallest lift worth detecting, the minimum detectable effect, is $\delta = p_B - p_A = 0.01$ on a baseline of
$p_A = 0.10$. It is a business decision, made before the data arrive: a smaller lift would not pay for the change.

## Solution

**Step 1: the sample size.** The observed difference $D = \hat p_B - \hat p_A$ is approximately normal. Under $H_0$
both arms share one rate, estimated by the pooled rate $\bar p$, so

$$
\sigma_0^2 = \var(D \mid H_0) = \frac{2\bar p(1 - \bar p)}{n}, \qquad
\sigma_1^2 = \var(D \mid H_1) = \frac{p_A(1 - p_A) + p_B(1 - p_B)}{n}.
$$

The test rejects when $\abs{D} > z_{1 - \alpha/2}\,\sigma_0$, where $z_q$ is the $q$ quantile of $\Gauss(0, 1)$. If the
true difference is $\delta > 0$, the chance of landing in the lower rejection region is negligible, so

$$
\text{power} \approx P(D > z_{1 - \alpha/2}\,\sigma_0) = \Phi\paren{\frac{\delta - z_{1 - \alpha/2}\,\sigma_0}{\sigma_1}}.
$$

Setting the power to $1 - \beta$ requires $(\delta - z_{1 - \alpha/2}\sigma_0)/\sigma_1 = z_{1 - \beta}$, that is
$\delta = z_{1 - \alpha/2}\,\sigma_0 + z_{1 - \beta}\,\sigma_1$. Both standard deviations scale as $1/\sqrt n$, so
multiplying by $\sqrt n$ and squaring gives <Cite id="fleiss2003" />

$$
n = \frac{\paren{z_{1 - \alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1 - \bar p)} + z_{1 - \beta}\sqrt{p_A(1 - p_A) + p_B(1 - p_B)}}^2}{\delta^2}.
$$

With $p_A = 0.10$, $p_B = 0.11$, $\bar p = 0.105$, $z_{0.975} = 1.960$ and $z_{0.8} = 0.842$:

$$
n = \frac{\paren{1.960 \times 0.4335 + 0.842 \times 0.4335}^2}{0.01^2} = \frac{1.2145^2}{0.0001} \approx 14{,}751
$$

users per arm, about 29,500 in total. The general version of this calculation is in
<NoteLink to="sample-size-planning">sample-size planning</NoteLink>.

**Step 2: the test.** The experiment runs until each arm has $n = 14{,}751$ users. Arm A converts 1,475 users, a rate
of 10.00%, and arm B converts 1,652, a rate of 11.20%. The observed difference is $D = 0.0120$. The pooled rate is
$\hat p = (1{,}475 + 1{,}652)/29{,}502 = 0.1060$, so

$$
\hat\sigma_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.1060 \times 0.8940}{14{,}751}} = 0.00358, \qquad
Z = \frac{0.0120}{0.00358} = 3.35, \qquad p = 2\paren{1 - \Phi(3.35)} = 0.0008.
$$

The <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink> rejects $H_0$ at $\alpha = 0.05$.

**Step 3: the confidence interval.** The interval for $p_B - p_A$ uses each arm's own variance, since it does not
assume the arms are equal:

$$
D \pm z_{0.975} \sqrt{\frac{\hat p_A(1 - \hat p_A)}{n} + \frac{\hat p_B(1 - \hat p_B)}{n}}
= 0.0120 \pm 1.96 \times 0.00358 = [0.0050,\ 0.0190].
$$

The new page raises conversion by between 0.5 and 1.9 percentage points, with 95% confidence.

**Step 4: the decision.** The result is statistically significant, and the point estimate, 1.2 points, exceeds the
1-point threshold. But the interval includes lifts as small as 0.5 points, below the threshold that makes the change
worthwhile. Statistical significance says the lift is probably not zero. Whether it is large enough is answered by
the <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> against the business threshold, not by the
p-value. A cautious team launches, or extends the experiment to narrow the interval.

## What it illustrates

- **Power is chosen, not observed.** The sample size follows from four inputs fixed in advance: $\alpha$, the power,
  the baseline and the minimum detectable effect. With the true lift set to the minimum detectable effect and
  $n = 14{,}751$, the simulated power is close to 80%. See
  <NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors">Type I and Type II errors</NoteLink>.
- **Small lifts need large samples.** $n$ scales as $1/\delta^2$. Halving the minimum detectable effect to 0.5 points
  multiplies the sample size by about four, to roughly 58,000 users per arm.
- **Statistical against practical significance.** With enough users any nonzero lift becomes significant. The
  <NoteLink to="effect-size">effect size</NoteLink> and its interval, not the p-value, say whether the lift matters.
- **Relative lifts.** A lift of 1.2 points on a 10% baseline is a relative lift of 12%. Reports often quote the
  relative figure, which sounds larger; both describe the same data. Its confidence interval must account for the
  noise in the baseline; see <NoteLink to="relative-lift">relative lift</NoteLink>.

## Variations

- **Stopping early.** Checking the result daily and stopping at the first significant value inflates the Type I error
  rate well above $\alpha$. The planned $n$ is only valid if the analysis happens once, at the end, unless the looks
  are planned as in <NoteLink to="group-sequential-designs">group-sequential designs</NoteLink>.
- **An unequal split.** If the arms end up with noticeably different numbers of users when the plan was 50/50, the
  assignment or logging is broken, and the comparison may be biased. This is a sample ratio mismatch.
- **Underpowered experiments.** If the true lift is 0.3 points and $n$ is sized for 1 point, most experiments are not
  significant, and the few that are overstate the lift. Set the true lift low in the simulation and rerun until the
  result is significant to see it.
- **One-sided tests.** A one-sided test for $p_B > p_A$ replaces $z_{0.975}$ with $z_{0.95} = 1.645$ and needs about
  21% fewer users, but it cannot detect a new page that is worse.
