<Definition>
An online shop runs an experiment in which both arms show the same page. Each arm gets $n = 10{,}000$ users, and each
user converts with probability $p = 0.05$. The difference in conversion rate is tested with a two-sided two-proportion
z-test at $\alpha = 0.05$. How often is the result significant, and how large a difference can chance alone produce?
</Definition>

## Setup

Let $X_A$ and $X_B$ be the numbers of converting users in arms A and B, so $X_A, X_B \iid \Binom(n, p)$. The observed
rates are $\hat p_A = X_A / n$ and $\hat p_B = X_B / n$, and the observed difference is $D = \hat p_B - \hat p_A$. The
test statistic is

$$
Z = \frac{D}{\sqrt{\hat p (1 - \hat p)\, (2/n)}}, \qquad \hat p = \frac{X_A + X_B}{2n},
$$

the <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink> for two proportions with the pooled rate $\hat p$. Both arms are identical,
so the null hypothesis $H_0: p_A = p_B$ is true by construction. An A/A test asks whether the analysis behaves as the
theory says it should when there is nothing to find <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.

## Solution

**The spread of the difference.** Each rate has variance $p(1 - p)/n$, and the arms are independent, so

$$
\var(D) = \frac{p(1 - p)}{n} + \frac{p(1 - p)}{n} = \frac{2p(1 - p)}{n}, \qquad
\operatorname{sd}(D) = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 0.05 \times 0.95}{10{,}000}} = 0.00308.
$$

By the central limit theorem $D$ is approximately $\Gauss(0, 0.00308^2)$. So 95% of A/A experiments show a difference
within $\pm 1.96 \times 0.00308 = \pm 0.0060$, that is up to 0.6 percentage points. On a 5% baseline this is a
relative "lift" of $\pm 12\%$, produced by chance alone.

**The p-values are uniform.** Under $H_0$, $Z$ is approximately standard normal. The two-sided p-value is
$P = 2(1 - \Phi(\abs{Z}))$. For any $u \in (0, 1)$,

$$
P(P \le u) = P\paren{\abs{Z} \ge z_{1 - u/2}} = 2\paren{1 - \Phi(z_{1 - u/2})} = u,
$$

where $z_q$ is the $q$ quantile of $\Gauss(0, 1)$. So $P$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$, and a test at level $\alpha$
rejects with probability exactly $\alpha$. This is the definition of the
<NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink> at work: in an A/A test, every rejection is a
<NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors">Type I error</NoteLink>.

**How many of many A/A tests are significant.** Each of $N$ independent A/A tests is significant with probability
$\alpha$, so the count $S$ of significant tests satisfies $S \sim \Binom(N, \alpha)$. For $N = 1{,}000$ and
$\alpha = 0.05$, $\expect S = 50$ and $\operatorname{sd}(S) = \sqrt{1{,}000 \times 0.05 \times 0.95} = 6.9$. The
central 95% of the distribution runs from 37 to 64. A count outside that range is evidence that the test does not
hold its level.

**Confidence intervals cover zero.** The 95% interval $D \pm 1.96\, \widehat{\operatorname{sd}}(D)$ contains the true
difference, 0, in about 95% of A/A experiments. This is the duality between tests and
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>: the interval excludes 0 exactly when the test
rejects, up to the small difference between the pooled and unpooled standard errors.

## What it illustrates

- **What "significant" means under the null.** A p-value below 0.05 happens in 5% of experiments where nothing
  differs. A single significant result is weak evidence on its own; see <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink>.
- **The noise floor of a metric.** The A/A spread $\sqrt{2p(1 - p)/n}$ is the smallest difference an experiment of this
  size can hope to separate from chance. Plans for real experiments start from it; see
  <NoteLink to="sample-size-planning">sample-size planning</NoteLink>.
- **A calibration check for the whole pipeline.** Companies run A/A tests continuously <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
  Too many significant results, p-values piled near 0 or 1, or an observed spread larger than the formula all point to
  a bug. Common causes are users assigned to both arms, events counted twice, a variance formula that assumes
  independent observations when one user contributes many, and an unequal split between arms (a sample ratio
  mismatch).

## Variations

- **Several metrics.** An A/A test that checks 20 independent metrics at $\alpha = 0.05$ finds at least one significant
  difference with probability $1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64$. See <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>.
- **Peeking.** Checking the p-value every day and stopping at the first value below $\alpha$ raises the false-positive
  rate of an A/A test far above $\alpha$, because each look is another chance to cross the threshold.
- **Correlated observations.** If the analysis unit is a page view but randomisation is by user, a user's views are
  correlated. The formula $2p(1 - p)/n$ then understates the variance, and more than $\alpha$ of A/A tests are
  significant. Here the simulation's observed spread would exceed the formula.
- **Rare events.** When $np$ is small, $X_A$ and $X_B$ are far from normal and the p-values are discrete. The share of
  significant A/A tests then drifts from $\alpha$; set $n$ and $p$ low in the simulation to see it.
