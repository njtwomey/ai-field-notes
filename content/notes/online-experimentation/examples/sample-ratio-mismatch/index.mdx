<Definition>
An experiment assigns users to arms A and B with equal probability. The analysis logs 100,000 users in A and 94,000 in
B. Is the imbalance chance? If not, can the conversion rates of the two arms still be compared?
</Definition>

## Setup

Each of $N$ logged users would land in B with probability $\tfrac12$ if assignment and logging worked. So the count
$N_B$ in B would satisfy $N_B \sim \Binom(N, \tfrac12)$. A sample ratio mismatch (SRM) is an observed split too
uneven for this model <Cite id="fabijan2019" />. It is tested with Pearson's χ² goodness-of-fit test, with
observed counts $O_A = N_A$, $O_B = N_B$ and expected counts $E_A = E_B = N/2$.

## Solution

**The test statistic.** Pearson's statistic is

$$
\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{arms}} \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} = \frac{(N_A - N/2)^2 + (N_B - N/2)^2}{N/2} = \frac{(N_A - N_B)^2}{N_A + N_B},
$$

because $N_A - N/2 = (N_A - N_B)/2$ and $N_B - N/2 = -(N_A - N_B)/2$. The statistic is the square of the normal
approximation to the <NoteLink to="binomial-test">binomial test</NoteLink>:
$Z = (N_B - N/2)/\sqrt{N/4}$ gives $Z^2 = (N_B - N_A)^2 / N$, the same expression. The square of a standard normal is
$\ChiSq_1$, so under the model $\chi^2$ follows the <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared
distribution</NoteLink> with one degree of freedom.

**The numbers.** With $N_A = 100{,}000$ and $N_B = 94{,}000$,

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{6{,}000^2}{194{,}000} = 185.6, \qquad p = P(\ChiSq_1 \ge 185.6) \approx 3 \times 10^{-42}.
$$

The imbalance is not chance. A B share of 48.45% looks close to 50%, but with 194,000 users the standard deviation of
the share is $0.5/\sqrt{194{,}000} = 0.11\%$, so 48.45% is 13.6 standard deviations from 50%. SRM checks usually use a
strict threshold, such as $p < 0.001$, because they run on every experiment and a false alarm stops a valid one
<Cite id="fabijan2019" />.

**Why the comparison is broken.** Randomisation makes the arms comparable because every kind of user is equally
likely to be in either. An SRM means some users were lost, or added, unequally, and the lost users are rarely a
random sample. Suppose the new page in B loads slowly. Of the users with slow connections, 30% of traffic, 20% leave
before being logged in B; none leave in A. Slow users convert at 4% and fast users at 12%, in both arms: the new page
has no effect on conversion. Then

$$
N_B = 100{,}000\,(1 - 0.3 \times 0.2) = 94{,}000,
$$

which is the imbalance above. Arm A converts at $0.3 \times 0.04 + 0.7 \times 0.12 = 9.60\%$. In B the slow users
are $0.24 / 0.94 = 25.5\%$ of those logged rather than 30%, so

$$
\hat p_B = 0.255 \times 0.04 + 0.745 \times 0.12 = 9.96\%.
$$

The experiment reports a lift of 0.36 percentage points, a relative lift of 3.7%. The two-proportion
<NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink> gives $z = 2.65$ and $p = 0.008$: a significant win for a page that changes
nothing. The lift comes entirely from which users were counted. This is the mechanism of
<NoteLink to="simpsons-paradox">Simpson's paradox</NoteLink>: the arms mix two groups in different proportions.

## What it illustrates

- **The SRM test is a validity check, not a result.** A significant SRM does not say which arm is better. It says the
  arms are no longer comparable, so no other result of the experiment can be trusted until the cause is found
  <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
- **Detection comes before visible bias.** With 100,000 users per arm, losing 5% of slow users in B gives an SRM
  p-value of 0.0008, below the 0.001 threshold. The bias it causes, 0.09 points, is not significant on its own
  ($p = 0.52$). The count of users is a far more sensitive signal than the metric.
- **Goodness-of-fit testing.** Pearson's χ² compares observed counts with the counts a model predicts. With two
  categories it reduces to the binomial test.

## Variations

- **Common causes.** Bot filtering that treats the arms differently, redirects that lose users in one arm, a variant
  that crashes on some devices, logging that depends on the variant, and users who switch arms. Fabijan et al.
  classify these by the stage of the experiment where they occur <Cite id="fabijan2019" />.
- **Unequal planned splits.** For a planned split of $q$ to B, the expected counts are $E_A = (1 - q)N$ and
  $E_B = qN$, and the same χ² test applies.
- **Many arms.** With $k$ arms the statistic sums over all arms and has $k - 1$ degrees of freedom.
- **Small imbalances in small samples.** 49,400 against 49,100 users gives $\chi^2 = 0.91$ and $p = 0.34$: consistent
  with chance. An SRM check that passes does not prove the split is perfect. It rules out losses large enough to
  detect at this sample size.
