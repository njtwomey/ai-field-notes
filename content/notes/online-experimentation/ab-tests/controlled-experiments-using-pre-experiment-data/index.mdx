<Definition>
**CUPED** (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) estimates a treatment effect from adjusted outcomes
$Y_i - \theta(X_i - \bar X)$, where $Y_i$ is user $i$'s metric during the experiment, $X_i$ is a covariate measured
before assignment, $\bar X$ is its mean over all users and $\theta = \cov(X, Y)/\var(X)$. The estimate is

$$
\hat\Delta_{\text{cv}} = (\bar Y_T - \bar Y_C) - \theta(\bar X_T - \bar X_C).
$$

It is unbiased for the same effect as the raw difference in means, and its variance is $1 - \rho^2$ times the raw
variance, where $\rho$ is the correlation between $X$ and $Y$ <Cite id="deng2013" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Much of the variance of a user's metric during an experiment reflects who the user is, not what the experiment did.
Heavy users are heavy users in both arms. When one arm happens to receive more heavy users, the raw difference in
means moves, and without adjustment its variance falls only as the sample grows. A covariate that measures how heavy
each user was before the experiment predicts that part of the variance, and it cannot have been affected by the
treatment.

## Mechanism

**Unbiasedness.** $X$ is measured before assignment, so it has the same distribution in both arms and
$\expect[\bar X_T - \bar X_C] = 0$. For any constant $\theta$,
$\expect[\hat\Delta_{\text{cv}}] = \expect[\bar Y_T - \bar Y_C] - \theta \cdot 0 = \Delta$.

**The optimal coefficient.** For users in one arm, $\var(Y - \theta X) = \sigma_Y^2 - 2\theta\sigma_{XY} +
\theta^2\sigma_X^2$. This is a quadratic in $\theta$ with minimum where its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $-2\sigma_{XY} +
2\theta\sigma_X^2$ is zero:

$$
\theta^* = \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X^2}, \qquad
\var(Y - \theta^* X) = \sigma_Y^2 - \frac{\sigma_{XY}^2}{\sigma_X^2} = \sigma_Y^2\paren{1 - \rho^2},
$$

with $\rho = \sigma_{XY}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$. The arms are independent, so the variance of $\hat\Delta_{\text{cv}}$ is
$\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)(1/n_T + 1/n_C)$, against $\sigma_Y^2(1/n_T + 1/n_C)$ for the raw difference. $\theta^*$ is the
slope of the least-squares regression of $Y$ on $X$.

**Estimating $\theta$.** $\theta$ is estimated from both arms pooled <Cite id="deng2013" />. The estimation error adds
a term of relative order $1/n$ to the variance and a bias of the same order, both negligible at the sample sizes of
online experiments. Separate slopes per arm, with $X$ centred at its overall mean, are also valid; that is the
interacted regression below.

**Relation to regression.** $\hat\Delta_{\text{cv}}$ is, up to how $\theta$ is estimated, the coefficient on the
treatment indicator in the least-squares regression of $Y$ on the indicator and $X$. Regression adjustment with
treatment-by-covariate interactions is never less precise asymptotically than the unadjusted difference in means
<Cite id="lin2013" />. See <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Both arms have $n = 5{,}000$ users, and the metric is standardised to $\sigma_Y = 1$. The raw <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> is
$\sqrt{2/5{,}000} = 0.0200$. The same metric measured in the two weeks before the experiment has correlation
$\rho = 0.7$ with the in-experiment value.

1. **Variance factor.** $1 - \rho^2 = 1 - 0.49 = 0.51$.
2. **Standard error.** $0.0200 \times \sqrt{0.51} = 0.0143$.
3. **Equivalent traffic.** The raw analysis would need $1/0.51 = 1.96$ times as many users to reach the same
   standard error.

In 2,000 simulated experiments of this design, the variance of the CUPED estimate was 0.517 times that of the raw
estimate, against 0.51 in theory.

## Cost

CUPED needs only per-user covariates from before the experiment and one pooled regression slope. The computation is
negligible. Its benefit depends on $\rho^2$: a covariate with $\rho = 0.3$ removes 9% of the variance, while
$\rho = 0.9$ removes 81%.

## Trade-offs

- **Choice of covariate.** The same metric over a pre-period is usually the strongest single covariate. Longer
  pre-periods raise $\rho$ up to a point. Several covariates can be used together, as in multiple regression. Any
  covariate is allowed if it cannot be affected by the treatment.
- **Users without history.** New users have no pre-period value. Setting $X = 0$ for them and adding an indicator of
  missing history as a second covariate lets the adjustment apply to returning users while leaving new users
  unadjusted. The benefit shrinks as the share of new users grows.
- **Ratio metrics.** A ratio metric is first linearised into a per-user variable, as in
  <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">the delta method</NoteLink>, and then adjusted like any per-user mean.

## Pitfalls

- **Covariates measured after assignment.** A covariate that the treatment can affect, such as in-experiment
  sessions, absorbs part of the effect and biases the estimate. Only data from before a user's first exposure
  qualifies.
- **Adjusting the headline number but not the power calculation.** A power calculation for a CUPED analysis should
  use $\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$, estimated on historical data. Using $\sigma_Y^2$ oversizes the experiment.
- **Expecting a change in the point estimate's meaning.** CUPED estimates the same <NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">average treatment effect</NoteLink> as the raw
  difference. When the two estimates disagree in one experiment, the CUPED estimate is the more precise one, not a
  different quantity.
