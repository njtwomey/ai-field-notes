<Definition>
A **heavy-tailed metric** in an online experiment is a per-user metric whose distribution has a long right tail, so
that a small share of users contributes a large share of the total. Revenue per user is the standard case: most users
spend nothing and a few spend a great deal. Such metrics have a large coefficient of variation, which makes
experiments on them need many users, and a large skewness, which slows the convergence of the sample mean to the
<NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">normal distribution</NoteLink> that tests assume <Cite id="kohavi2014" />. **Capping**, also called winsorising, replaces each
value above a threshold $c$ by $c$. It reduces the variance but tests a different quantity, the mean of the capped
metric.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A difference in means estimated from 50,000 users can move by more than the effect being measured when one user
places a single very large order. The variance of the mean is driven by the rare large values, and whether one of them
lands in treatment or control is luck. Capping removes that luck. It also removes whatever part of the treatment
effect lives in the tail, so whether it helps depends on where the effect is.

## A model of revenue per user

Let each user buy with probability $p$, and let a buyer's spend be log-normal. Revenue per user is $Y = BR$ with
$B \sim \Bern(p)$ and $R \sim \LogNorm(\mu, \sigma^2)$ independent. Because $B^k = B$, the moments are
$\expect[Y^k] = p\,\expect[R^k] = p\,e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}$.

Take $p = 0.05$, $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma = 1.2$, so the median purchase is $e^{3.5} = 33.1$.

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Mean $\expect[Y] = p\,e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ | 3.40 |
| Standard deviation | 31.07 |
| Coefficient of variation | 9.13 |
| Skewness | 39.1 |

**Sample size.** From <NoteLink to="a-b-test-power">power for A/B tests</NoteLink>, the users per arm needed to detect a
relative lift $r$ are $2(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2(\sigma_Y/\mu_Y)^2/r^2$, where $z_q$ is the standard normal
$q$-quantile. At $\alpha = 0.05$, power 0.8 and $r = 5\%$, revenue per user needs 523,765 users per arm. Conversion
alone, with coefficient of variation $\sqrt{(1 - p)/p} = 4.36$, needs 119,303. The ratio is
$9.13^2 / 4.36^2 = 4.39$: revenue needs 4.4 times as many users as conversion for the same relative lift.

**Normality.** The skewness of a mean of $n$ independent values is the skewness of one value divided by $\sqrt n$, so
the sampling distribution approaches the normal slowly when the skewness $\gamma$ is large. A rule of thumb for the
normal approximation to be adequate is $n > 355\gamma^2$ per arm <Cite id="kohavi2014" />. Here that is
$355 \times 39.1^2 = 544{,}065$ users, about the same as the sample needed for power. For a difference between two arms of equal size
and the same distribution, the third cumulants of the two means cancel, so the difference is closer to normal than
either mean. The rule matters most for <NoteLink to="one-and-two-sided-tests">one-sided tests</NoteLink>, for unequal arms and when the treatment changes the tail.

<Derivation title="Skewness of a mean">
Let $Y_1, \dots, Y_n$ be independent with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$ and third central moment $\kappa_3$.
Cumulants of independent variables add, so $\bar Y - \mu$ has variance $\sigma^2/n$ and third central moment
$n\kappa_3/n^3 = \kappa_3/n^2$. Its skewness is $(\kappa_3/n^2)/(\sigma^2/n)^{3/2} = (\kappa_3/\sigma^3)/\sqrt n$.
</Derivation>

## Capping

Let $Y_c = \min(Y, c)$. For the log-normal model the capped moments have a closed form, with $\Phi$ the standard normal
distribution function:

$$
\expect\brack{\min(R, c)^k} = e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}\,\Phi\paren{\frac{\ln c - \mu}{\sigma} - k\sigma}
+ c^k \paren{1 - \Phi\paren{\frac{\ln c - \mu}{\sigma}}}.
$$

The first term integrates $r^k$ over $r < c$ and the second counts every larger value as $c$.

The sample size needed for a fixed power is proportional to $\var(Y_c)/\Delta_c^2$, where $\Delta_c$ is the treatment
effect on the capped mean. Capping helps when it lowers the variance by more than it lowers the squared effect. The
table uses the model above with a 5% lift applied in two ways: to every purchase value, which shifts $\mu$ by
$\ln 1.05$, or to the purchase rate $p$.

| Cap at percentile of all users | $c$ | Buyers capped | Variance ratio | Value lift: effect kept | Value lift: users needed | Rate lift: effect kept | Rate lift: users needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | 90.9 | 20% | 0.141 | 0.352 | 1.14 | 0.627 | 0.36 |
| 99.5% | 154.1 | 10% | 0.249 | 0.524 | 0.91 | 0.759 | 0.43 |
| 99.9% | 389.4 | 2% | 0.516 | 0.798 | 0.81 | 0.918 | 0.61 |

Users needed are relative to the uncapped metric. When the treatment scales purchase values, a tight cap removes most
of the effect along with the variance, and at the 99th percentile the test needs more users than without a cap. When
the treatment raises the purchase rate, the effect scales with the capped mean and capping is a large gain. Capping at
the 99.9th percentile also lowers the skewness from 39.1 to 11.4, and the rule of thumb for normality from 544,065 to
46,058 users.

**The estimand changes.** A capped metric answers a different question: the change in mean revenue after limiting
each user's contribution to $c$. It estimates the change in true mean revenue with a bias that depends on the tail.
The cap $c$ must be fixed before the experiment, from pre-experiment data, and applied identically in both arms. A cap
computed from the pooled experiment data depends on the treatment.

## Other remedies

- **Log transformation.** Comparing means of log revenue tests a change in the geometric mean, which describes the
  typical buyer, not total revenue. The two can have opposite signs. If buyers' log-spend moves from
  $\Gauss(3.5, 1.0^2)$ to $\Gauss(3.4, 1.2^2)$, mean log-spend falls by 0.10 while mean spend rises by
  $e^{3.4 + 0.72 - 3.5 - 0.5} - 1 = 12.7\%$.
- **Rank tests.** The Mann–Whitney test detects a shift in the whole distribution. It is not a test of equal means.
- **<NoteLink to="variance-reduction">Variance reduction</NoteLink>.** <NoteLink to="controlled-experiments-using-pre-experiment-data">CUPED</NoteLink> with pre-experiment revenue as the covariate reduces
  variance without changing the estimand.
- **Decomposition.** Revenue per user is conversion times revenue per buyer. Conversion is a well-behaved
  <NoteLink to="conversion-rate-metrics">conversion-rate metric</NoteLink> and is often the more sensitive signal.

## Pitfalls

- **Revenue per buyer as a test metric.** Revenue per buyer conditions on buying, which the treatment can change. If
  the treatment converts extra buyers who spend less than average, revenue per buyer falls while revenue per user
  rises. Its denominator is also random, so it is a <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">ratio metric</NoteLink>.
- **Removing outliers after seeing the data.** Deleting the largest orders because they look anomalous, after the
  results are known, makes the test invalid. Bots and fraud should be filtered by rules fixed in advance and applied
  to both arms.
- **Trusting normality for a single mean.** With skewness near 40, an interval for one arm's mean from 50,000 users
  is far from the $355\gamma^2$ rule, and its two tails have unequal error rates. A cap, a larger sample or a
  <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> check is needed.
