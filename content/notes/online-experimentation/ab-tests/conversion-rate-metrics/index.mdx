<Definition>
A **conversion-rate metric** is the proportion of randomisation units that complete a target action, such as a
purchase or a sign-up, during the experiment. With $n$ users in an arm and $X$ of them converting, the estimate is
$\hat p = X/n$. Each user's outcome is a Bernoulli variable with mean $p$ and variance $p(1 - p)$, and users are
independent, so $\var(\hat p) = p(1 - p)/n$. Two arms are compared with the two-proportion
<NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink> <Cite id="fleiss2003" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A conversion rate reduces each user to one bit: converted or not. The bit's variance is fixed by its mean, $p(1 - p)$,
so no separate variance estimate is needed and no user can dominate the average. This makes conversion rates the most
stable metrics in online experiments. The price is sensitivity: a rate near zero has a large coefficient of variation,
$\sqrt{(1 - p)/p}$, and detecting small relative changes needs many users.

## Details

**Which denominator.** The unit in the denominator must be the randomisation unit.

- **Per user.** The share of users who converted at least once. Users are independent, so the Bernoulli variance
  applies.
- **Per session or per visit.** Conversions divided by sessions. Sessions of one user are correlated, and the number
  of sessions per user is random, so this is a ratio of two per-user sums. Its variance comes from the
  <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">delta method</NoteLink>, not from $p(1 - p)/n_{\text{sessions}}$
  <Cite id="deng2018" />.
- **Conversions per user.** A count, such as orders per user, is a mean of a non-negative integer variable. Its
  variance must be estimated from the data, as in the <NoteLink to="welch-t-test">Welch t-test</NoteLink>.

**The test.** Let arm A have $x_A$ conversions from $n_A$ users and arm B $x_B$ from $n_B$. Under
$H_0: p_A = p_B$ both arms share one rate, estimated by the pooled rate $\bar p = (x_A + x_B)/(n_A + n_B)$. The
statistic

$$
Z = \frac{\hat p_B - \hat p_A}{\sqrt{\bar p(1 - \bar p)\paren{\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}}}}
$$

is approximately standard normal under $H_0$. The confidence interval for $p_B - p_A$ does not assume $H_0$, so it
uses each arm's own variance:

$$
\hat p_B - \hat p_A \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_A(1 - \hat p_A)}{n_A} + \frac{\hat p_B(1 - \hat p_B)}{n_B}},
$$

where $z_q$ is the standard normal $q$-quantile.

**Three tests, one answer.** The pooled z-test, Pearson's $\chi^2$ test of independence on the 2×2 table of arm
against outcome, and the Welch t-test on the 0/1 outcomes agree closely in large samples. The first two are exactly
equivalent: $Z^2$ equals the $\chi^2$ statistic, as shown below. Welch's test uses the unpooled variance, which differs
from the pooled one only when the rates differ.

<Derivation title="The z-test and the chi-squared test agree">
Write the table with rows for arms and columns for converted and not converted: $a = x_A$, $b = n_A - x_A$,
$c = x_B$, $d = n_B - x_B$. Let $C_1 = a + c$ and $C_2 = b + d$ be the column totals and $N = n_A + n_B$.

**The $\chi^2$ statistic.** The expected count in cell $(i, j)$ is $E_{ij} = R_i C_j / N$, with row totals
$R_1 = n_A$ and $R_2 = n_B$. For cell $(1, 1)$, $a - n_A C_1/N = \paren{a(a + b + c + d) - (a + b)(a + c)}/N =
(ad - bc)/N$. The margins are fixed, so the other three cells have the same deviation up to sign. Hence

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(ad - bc)^2}{N^2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{E_{ij}} = \frac{(ad - bc)^2}{N^2} \cdot \frac{N (R_1 + R_2)(C_1 + C_2)}{R_1R_2C_1C_2}
= \frac{N (ad - bc)^2}{n_A n_B C_1 C_2}.
$$

**The z statistic.** The numerator is $\hat p_B - \hat p_A = (c\,n_A - a\,n_B)/(n_A n_B)$, and
$c\,n_A - a\,n_B = c(a + b) - a(c + d) = bc - ad$. The pooled rate is $\bar p = C_1/N$, so the denominator squared is
$(C_1 C_2/N^2) \cdot N/(n_A n_B) = C_1 C_2/(N n_A n_B)$. Therefore

$$
Z^2 = \frac{(ad - bc)^2}{(n_A n_B)^2} \cdot \frac{N n_A n_B}{C_1 C_2} = \frac{N (ad - bc)^2}{n_A n_B C_1 C_2} = \chi^2.
$$

A $\chi^2$ variable with one degree of freedom is the square of a standard normal, so the two tests give the same
two-sided p-value.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Arm A converts 2,000 of 50,000 users and arm B 2,150 of 50,000.

1. **Rates.** $\hat p_A = 4.00\%$, $\hat p_B = 4.30\%$, and the difference is 0.30 percentage points.
2. **Test.** The pooled rate is $\bar p = 4{,}150/100{,}000 = 0.0415$. The standard error under $H_0$ is
   $\sqrt{0.0415 \times 0.9585 \times (2/50{,}000)} = 0.001261$, so $Z = 0.0030/0.001261 = 2.378$ and the two-sided
   p-value is 0.0174.
3. **Equivalent tests.** Pearson's $\chi^2$ on the table is $5.656 = 2.378^2$, with the same p-value. Welch's
   t-test on the 100,000 zeros and ones gives $t = 2.378$ and $p = 0.0174$.
4. **Interval.** The unpooled standard error is also 0.001261 to four significant figures, so the 95% interval is
   $0.0030 \pm 1.96 \times 0.001261 = [0.053, 0.547]$ percentage points.

The relative lift is $0.30/4.00 = 7.5\%$. Its interval is not the absolute interval divided by 4%, because the
denominator is also estimated; see <NoteLink to="relative-lift">relative lift</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Independent units.** Each user converts or not independently of other users. The denominator is the
  randomisation unit.
- **Large counts.** The normal approximation needs several conversions and non-conversions in each arm. For very
  small counts, Fisher's exact test or the <NoteLink to="binomial-test">binomial test</NoteLink> applies.
- **A fixed window.** Every user has the same opportunity to convert. Users who joined on the last day of the
  experiment have had less time, so the rate depends on when users arrived, equally in both arms.

## Pitfalls

- **Binomial variance for per-session rates.** Sessions per user vary and are correlated within users, so
  $p(1 - p)/n_{\text{sessions}}$ underestimates the variance of a per-session rate.
- **Pooled variance in the interval.** The pooled variance is valid only under $H_0$. The interval must not assume the
  hypothesis it is used to assess.
- **Many conversion events.** Tracking several conversion events and reporting the one that moved is the
  multiple-comparisons problem of <NoteLink to="many-metrics-and-variants">many metrics and variants</NoteLink>.
