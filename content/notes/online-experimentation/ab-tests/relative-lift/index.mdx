<Definition>
The **relative lift** of a treatment is $L = \mu_T/\mu_C - 1$, the change in a metric's mean as a fraction of the
control mean. Its estimate is $\hat L = \bar Y_T/\bar Y_C - 1$. Because the denominator $\bar Y_C$ is itself estimated,
a confidence interval for $L$ must account for the noise in both means. The delta method, a log-ratio transformation
and Fieller's method each give such an interval <Cite id="deng2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A difference of 0.3 percentage points means more on a 4% baseline than on a 40% one, so experiments report lifts in
percent. Dividing the absolute confidence interval by the observed control mean treats that mean as known. If the
control mean was underestimated by chance, the lift is overestimated, and the interval ignores this. The ratio of two
noisy quantities is also skewed: the denominator appears in the reciprocal, so an equally large error below the true
control mean moves the ratio more than one above it.

## Details

Let the arms be independent with $\var(\bar Y_T) = v_T$ and $\var(\bar Y_C) = v_C$.

**Delta method.** Expanding $g(y_T, y_C) = y_T/y_C$ to first order around $(\mu_T, \mu_C)$,

$$
\hat L - L \approx \frac{1}{\mu_C}(\bar Y_T - \mu_T) - \frac{\mu_T}{\mu_C^2}(\bar Y_C - \mu_C),
\qquad
\var(\hat L) \approx \frac{v_T}{\mu_C^2} + \frac{\mu_T^2\, v_C}{\mu_C^4}.
$$

The interval is $\hat L \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat\var(\hat L)}$ with the means and variances replaced by
estimates, where $z_q$ is the standard normal $q$-quantile. The second term is the one the naive interval omits. For
a ratio metric in each arm, $v_T$ and $v_C$ are themselves delta-method variances; see
<NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">ratio metrics and the delta method</NoteLink>.

**Log ratio.** The logarithm makes the ratio a difference, $\ln(\bar Y_T/\bar Y_C) = \ln\bar Y_T - \ln\bar Y_C$. The
delta method applied to each logarithm gives

$$
\var\paren{\ln\frac{\bar Y_T}{\bar Y_C}} \approx \frac{v_T}{\mu_T^2} + \frac{v_C}{\mu_C^2}.
$$

A symmetric interval on the log scale, transformed back by $\exp(\cdot) - 1$, is asymmetric for $L$ and never
extends below $-1$.

**Fieller's method.** If $\bar Y_T$ and $\bar Y_C$ are normal, then for the true ratio $\rho = \mu_T/\mu_C$ the
quantity $\bar Y_T - \rho\bar Y_C$ has mean zero and variance $v_T + \rho^2 v_C$. The set of $\rho$ not rejected at
level $\alpha$ is

$$
\set{\rho : \paren{\bar Y_T - \rho\bar Y_C}^2 \le z_{1-\alpha/2}^2\paren{v_T + \rho^2 v_C}}.
$$

Expanding gives the quadratic inequality
$\paren{\bar Y_C^2 - z^2 v_C}\rho^2 - 2\bar Y_T\bar Y_C\,\rho + \paren{\bar Y_T^2 - z^2 v_T} \le 0$, with
$z = z_{1-\alpha/2}$. When $\bar Y_C^2 > z^2 v_C$, the control mean is significantly different from zero, the
leading coefficient is positive, and the set is the interval between the two roots <Cite id="fieller1954" />. Its
coverage does not rely on a linearisation, only on the normality of the two means.

## Worked example

Arm C converts 2,000 of 50,000 users (4.00%) and arm T 2,150 of 50,000 (4.30%), as in
<NoteLink to="conversion-rate-metrics">conversion-rate metrics</NoteLink>. The estimated lift is
$\hat L = 0.043/0.040 - 1 = 7.5\%$. The variances are $v_C = 0.04 \times 0.96/50{,}000$ and
$v_T = 0.043 \times 0.957/50{,}000$.

| Method | 95% interval for the lift |
| --- | --- |
| Absolute interval divided by $\bar Y_C$ | $[1.32\%,\ 13.68\%]$ |
| Delta method, standard error 3.27 points | $[1.09\%,\ 13.91\%]$ |
| Log ratio, standard error 0.0304 on the log scale | $[1.28\%,\ 14.10\%]$ |
| Fieller | $[1.28\%,\ 14.12\%]$ |

The naive interval is the narrowest because it omits $\mu_T^2 v_C/\mu_C^4$. Its standard error is 3.15 points against
3.27 for the delta method. The log-ratio and Fieller intervals agree closely and are shifted upward, reflecting the
skew of a ratio. At this sample size all four exclude zero. With smaller samples or rarer events the differences grow,
and the naive interval under-covers.

## Assumptions

- **Independent arms.** The formulas above omit a covariance term between $\bar Y_T$ and $\bar Y_C$, which is zero
  when different units are randomised to each arm.
- **A control mean far from zero.** All three methods need $\bar Y_C$ to be many standard errors from zero. For
  metrics such as profit that can be zero or negative, a relative lift is not meaningful.
- **Approximate normality of the means.** For heavy-tailed metrics this needs large samples; see
  <NoteLink to="revenue-and-heavy-tailed-metrics">revenue and heavy-tailed metrics</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Testing the lift and the difference separately.** $L = 0$ exactly when $\mu_T = \mu_C$. The test of no effect
  should be the test of the absolute difference. A delta-method z-statistic for $L$ gives a slightly different
  p-value, here $0.075/0.0327 = 2.29$ against 2.38, because it uses a different standard error.
- **Averaging lifts across experiments.** Relative lifts from experiments with different baselines are not on a
  common scale. Combining them needs weights, and their sum is not the lift of the combined changes.
- **Planning in relative terms, analysing in absolute terms.** A relative MDE must be converted with the control mean
  before sizing; see <NoteLink to="a-b-test-power">power for A/B tests</NoteLink>.
