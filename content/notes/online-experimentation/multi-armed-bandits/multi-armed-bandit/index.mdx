<Definition>
A **stochastic $K$-armed bandit** is a set of $K$ reward distributions $\nu_1, \dots, \nu_K$ on $\reals$ with means
$\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K$. In each round $t = 1, \dots, T$ a learner chooses an arm $A_t \in \set{1, \dots, K}$, using
only the arms and rewards of rounds $1$ to $t - 1$, and receives a reward $X_t$ drawn from $\nu_{A_t}$, independent of
everything else given $A_t$. The learner's **regret** after $T$ rounds is

$$
R_T = T\mu^* - \expect\brack{\sum_{t=1}^{T} X_t}, \qquad \mu^* = \max_i \mu_i,
$$

the expected reward lost to not pulling an optimal arm in every round <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
</Definition>

## The problem

The name comes from a row of slot machines, each a "one-armed bandit", with unknown payout rates. A gambler with $T$
coins wants to win as much as possible. Each coin spent on an arm returns a reward from that arm only, so the gambler
learns about an arm only by playing it. Robbins posed the problem in this form in 1952, as a model of the sequential
design of experiments <Cite id="robbins1952" />.

The same structure appears whenever choices are made repeatedly and each choice reveals information about itself only.

- A website shows one of $K$ page layouts to each visitor and observes whether that visitor converts.
- A clinical trial assigns each patient one of $K$ treatments and observes that patient's outcome.
- A recommender chooses one item to show and observes a click or no click on that item.

The learner faces the **exploration-exploitation trade-off**. Exploiting means pulling the arm with the highest
estimated mean, which earns reward now. Exploring means pulling an arm whose estimate is uncertain, which may earn
less now but improves later choices. An algorithm that never explores can lock onto a suboptimal arm forever after an
unlucky start. An algorithm that explores at a constant rate keeps paying for information it no longer needs.

## Policies and feedback

A **policy** maps the history $H_{t-1} = (A_1, X_1, \dots, A_{t-1}, X_{t-1})$ to a distribution over arms, from which
$A_t$ is drawn. The feedback is **bandit feedback**: the learner sees $X_t$ but not the rewards the other arms would
have paid. This is what separates a bandit from supervised learning, where every label is revealed, and from the
full-information "experts" setting, where the rewards of all arms are revealed after each round.

Two counts appear throughout the theory. $N_i(t) = \sum_{s=1}^{t} \indicator\set{A_s = i}$ is the number of pulls of
arm $i$ in the first $t$ rounds. $\hat\mu_i(t)$ is the average of the rewards from those pulls, the **empirical mean**
of arm $i$.

## Regret and the gaps

The **gap** of arm $i$ is $\Delta_i = \mu^* - \mu_i \ge 0$. An arm with $\Delta_i = 0$ is optimal, and pulling it costs
nothing in expectation.

The **pseudo-regret** is the random quantity $\bar R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Delta_{A_t} = T\mu^* - \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{A_t}$.
It charges each round the expected shortfall of the arm chosen, not the shortfall of the reward actually drawn. The
expected regret equals the expected pseudo-regret. Given the history, the reward $X_t$ has mean $\mu_{A_t}$, so by the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total expectation</NoteLink>
$\expect[X_t] = \expect[\expect[X_t \mid H_{t-1}, A_t]] = \expect[\mu_{A_t}]$, and therefore

$$
R_T = T\mu^* - \sum_{t=1}^{T}\expect[\mu_{A_t}] = \expect\brack{\bar R_T}.
$$

Grouping the rounds by the arm pulled gives the **regret decomposition**. The pseudo-regret is a sum over rounds of
$\Delta_{A_t} = \sum_i \Delta_i \indicator\set{A_t = i}$. Swapping the two sums,

$$
\bar R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_i\indicator\set{A_t = i} = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_i N_i(T), \qquad
R_T = \sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_i\,\expect[N_i(T)].
$$

Regret is small exactly when every suboptimal arm is pulled rarely. The whole analysis of a bandit algorithm reduces
to bounding $\expect[N_i(T)]$ for each suboptimal arm $i$. An arm with a large gap costs a lot per pull but is easy
to rule out. An arm with a small gap is hard to rule out but costs little per pull.

<Callout variant="note">
Regret is not the same as the error of the final recommendation. A learner that must name the best arm after an
exploration budget, and is scored only on that choice, solves a different problem:
<NoteLink to="best-arm-identification">best-arm identification</NoteLink>. Regret charges every round of learning.
</Callout>

## Linear, logarithmic and square-root regret

Regret is at most $T\Delta_{\max}$, where $\Delta_{\max}$ is the largest gap, and every algorithm that ignores the
data achieves order $T$. Uniform random play, the design of a classical
<NoteLink to="a-b-test">A/B test</NoteLink>, has regret $T \cdot \frac1K\sum_i \Delta_i$. The question is how much
better an adaptive policy can do. Two kinds of bound answer it.

- An **instance-dependent** bound depends on the gaps of the particular problem. The best achievable order is
  $\sum_{i:\Delta_i > 0} \log T / \Delta_i$ up to constants, which grows only logarithmically in $T$.
- A **worst-case** or **minimax** bound holds for every problem with rewards in $[0, 1]$. The best achievable order is
  $\sqrt{KT}$. The worst case has gaps of order $\sqrt{K/T}$, small enough that the arms are hard to tell apart in $T$
  rounds but large enough that confusing them costs of order $\sqrt{KT}$.

Both bounds are sublinear, so the average regret per round $R_T / T$ tends to zero: the learner eventually plays as
well as someone who knew the best arm.

## The Lai–Robbins lower bound

Lai and Robbins found how slowly regret can grow on a fixed problem <Cite id="lai1985" />. Call a policy **consistent**
on a family of reward distributions if, on every problem in the family, $\expect[N_i(T)] = o(T^a)$ for every
suboptimal arm $i$ and every $a > 0$. Consistency rules out policies that ignore the data to do well on one problem.
For a family of distributions indexed by their mean, such as Bernoulli or Gaussian with known variance, every
consistent policy satisfies, for every suboptimal arm $i$,

$$
\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{\expect[N_i(T)]}{\log T} \ge \frac{1}{\KL(\nu_i \,\|\, \nu^*)},
$$

where $\nu^*$ is the distribution of an optimal arm and $\KL$ is the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>. With the regret decomposition this gives

$$
\liminf_{T \to \infty} \frac{R_T}{\log T} \ge \sum_{i:\Delta_i > 0} \frac{\Delta_i}{\KL(\nu_i \,\|\, \nu^*)}.
$$

For Bernoulli arms $\KL(\nu_i \,\|\, \nu^*) = \mu_i \log\frac{\mu_i}{\mu^*} + (1 - \mu_i)\log\frac{1 - \mu_i}{1 - \mu^*}$.
For unit-variance Gaussian arms it is $\Delta_i^2 / 2$, and the constant becomes $\sum_i 2/\Delta_i$. An algorithm
whose regret matches this constant is **asymptotically optimal**. <Gloss name="kl-ucb" /> and <Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> are; <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink> matches the
$\log T$ rate with a larger constant.

The bound is asymptotic. For small $T$ and close arms the curve $\sum_i \Delta_i \log T / \KL(\nu_i \,\|\, \nu^*)$ can
lie above the regret of a good algorithm, because the $o(1)$ terms have not yet vanished. The figure shows this for
three arms with means 0.6, 0.5 and 0.45.

<Derivation title="Why about log T / KL pulls are needed">
The argument is a change of measure <Cite id="lattimore2020" />. Fix a suboptimal arm $i$. Build a second problem
$\nu'$ that equals $\nu$ except that arm $i$'s distribution is replaced by $\nu_i'$ with mean slightly above $\mu^*$,
so that in $\nu'$ arm $i$ is the unique best arm. The two problems differ only in arm $i$. The KL divergence between
the laws of the whole history under $\nu$ and $\nu'$ is therefore
$\expect_\nu[N_i(T)]\,\KL(\nu_i \,\|\, \nu_i')$: each pull of arm $i$ contributes one term, and pulls of the other
arms contribute nothing.

A consistent policy must behave very differently on the two problems. On $\nu$ it pulls arm $i$ only $o(T^a)$ times.
On $\nu'$ it must pull arm $i$ in all but $o(T^a)$ rounds, so it pulls it more than $T/2$ times. Take the event
$E = \set{N_i(T) > T/2}$. Its probability is small under $\nu$ and close to one under $\nu'$. The Bretagnolle–Huber
inequality states that for any event $E$ and laws $P, Q$,
$P(E) + Q(E^c) \ge \tfrac12\exp(-\KL(P \,\|\, Q))$. Both probabilities on the left are $O(T^{a-1})$ by consistency
and <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's inequality</NoteLink>, so

$$
\expect_\nu[N_i(T)]\,\KL(\nu_i \,\|\, \nu_i') \ge (1 - a)\log T - O(1).
$$

Dividing by $\log T$, letting $T \to \infty$, then $a \to 0$ and $\nu_i' \to \nu^*$ gives the bound. The intuition:
unless arm $i$ has been sampled about $\log T / \KL$ times, the data cannot rule out with confidence $1 - 1/T$ that
arm $i$ is the best, and being wrong about that on the alternative costs of order $T$.
</Derivation>

## The minimax lower bound

The worst-case counterpart does not need consistency. For every policy and every $T \ge K - 1$, there is a $K$-armed
problem with unit-variance Gaussian rewards on which $R_T \ge \frac{1}{27}\sqrt{(K - 1)T}$ <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
The hard problem gives one arm a gap of about $\sqrt{K/T}$ over the others. Distinguishing the arms then needs about
$T/K$ pulls of each, and each of those pulls costs $\sqrt{K/T}$, for a total of order $\sqrt{KT}$.
UCB1 achieves $O(\sqrt{KT\log T})$ and MOSS achieves
$O(\sqrt{KT})$ <Cite id="bubeck2012" />.

## Variants

The stochastic $K$-armed bandit is the base of a family of problems. Each changes one assumption.

- **Structured arms.** In a <NoteLink to="linucb">linear bandit</NoteLink> each arm is a feature vector and its mean
  is linear in the features. The learner shares information across arms, so the number of arms can be large or
  infinite.
- **Contexts.** In a <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandit</NoteLink> the learner sees a feature vector
  before each choice, and the best arm depends on it.
- **Changing rewards.** In a <NoteLink to="non-stationary-bandits">non-stationary bandit</NoteLink> the means drift or
  jump, and the learner must forget old data.
- **Adversarial rewards.** In an <NoteLink to="exp3">adversarial bandit</NoteLink> an opponent chooses the rewards,
  and regret is measured against the best fixed arm in hindsight.
- **Pure exploration.** In <NoteLink to="best-arm-identification">best-arm identification</NoteLink> only the final
  recommendation is scored.
- **Bayesian bandits.** With a prior over the means, the problem of maximising expected discounted reward has an exact
  solution, the Gittins index.
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-and-frequentist-bandits">Bayesian and frequentist bandits</NoteLink> compares the two views.

## Assumptions

- The reward distributions do not change over time, and rewards are independent across rounds given the arms.
- The learner observes the reward of the chosen arm immediately, before the next choice. Delayed or batched feedback
  changes the algorithms but not the lower bounds.
- Rewards are bounded, often in $[0, 1]$, or sub-Gaussian. Heavy-tailed rewards need robust mean estimators.
- The choice of arm does not change the future state of the world. When it does, the problem is a <Gloss name="mdp">Markov decision
  process</Gloss>, the subject of <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss>.

## Pitfalls

- **Regret is relative to the best single arm.** If the best arm differs between user segments, a context-free bandit
  converges to the best arm on average and leaves the segment differences unexploited.
- **<NoteLink to="bandits-vs-a-b-tests">Adaptive allocation</NoteLink> biases naive estimates.** The empirical means at the end of a bandit run are biased downwards
  for arms that were abandoned after an unlucky start, because the algorithm stopped sampling them while they looked
  bad <Cite id="nie2018" />. Standard <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink> and $t$-tests do not hold for adaptively collected data.
- **Some algorithms need the horizon in advance.** An algorithm tuned for $T$ rounds, such as <NoteLink to="explore-then-commit">explore-then-commit</NoteLink>, can do badly when run for a
  different number of rounds. Anytime algorithms such as UCB1 and Thompson sampling do not need $T$.
