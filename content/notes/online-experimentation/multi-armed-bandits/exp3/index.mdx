<Definition>
In an **adversarial bandit** with $K$ arms and horizon $T$, an adversary fixes rewards $x_{t,i} \in [0, 1]$ for every
round $t$ and arm $i$ before play begins. The learner sees only $x_{t,A_t}$, and its regret is measured against the
best fixed arm in hindsight,
$R_T = \max_i\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t,i} - \expect\brack{\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t,A_t}}$. **EXP3** (exponential weights for
exploration and exploitation) samples $A_t$ from

$$
P_{t,i} = \frac{\exp(-\eta\hat L_{t-1,i})}{\sum_{j}\exp(-\eta\hat L_{t-1,j})}, \qquad
\hat L_{t,i} = \sum_{s=1}^{t}\frac{(1 - x_{s,i})\indicator\set{A_s = i}}{P_{s,i}},
$$

with a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> $\eta > 0$ <Cite id="auer2002b" />.
</Definition>

## Deterministic policies have linear regret against an adversary

The <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">stochastic bandit</NoteLink> assumes each arm's rewards are independent draws from a fixed distribution. The adversarial
bandit drops that assumption: the rewards are an arbitrary sequence. The only restriction is that the sequence is fixed
in advance, before the learner's random choices are made (an **oblivious** adversary).

Every deterministic policy, <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink> included, has linear regret in this setting. A
deterministic policy's choice at round $t$ is a function of the rewards it has seen. The adversary can simulate the
policy in advance and set $x_{t,i} = 0$ for the arm it will pull and $x_{t,i} = 1$ for all others. The policy then earns
0. The other arms' totals sum to $T(K - 1)$, so the best of them has at least $T(K - 1)/K \ge T/2$. The regret is at
least $T/2$. A randomised policy escapes because the adversary, having fixed the sequence, cannot know which arm the
learner will draw.

## The algorithm

EXP3 keeps a weight per arm that decays exponentially with the arm's estimated cumulative loss, where the loss of
reward $x$ is $y = 1 - x$. Arms that have lost little get most of the probability. Only the
pulled arm's loss is seen. EXP3 fills in the rest with an **importance-weighted estimate**:

$$
\hat y_{t,i} = \frac{y_{t,i}\indicator\set{A_t = i}}{P_{t,i}}.
$$

The estimate is zero for arms not pulled and $y_{t,i}/P_{t,i}$ for the pulled arm. It is unbiased. Given the history,
arm $i$ is pulled with probability $P_{t,i}$, so

$$
\expect_t[\hat y_{t,i}] = P_{t,i}\cdot\frac{y_{t,i}}{P_{t,i}} + (1 - P_{t,i})\cdot 0 = y_{t,i},
$$

where $\expect_t$ is the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over $A_t$ given the history. Dividing by $P_{t,i}$ corrects for how rarely the
arm is observed. An arm pulled with probability $0.01$ that shows a loss has that loss counted a hundred times, standing
in for the ninety-nine rounds in which it would not have been seen. The same device underlies
<NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink>.

Each round costs $O(K)$: compute the probabilities, draw an arm, and add $\hat y_{t,A_t}$ to its cumulative loss
estimate.

## The regret bound

The analysis follows the sum of weights $W_t = \sum_{i}\exp(-\eta\hat L_{t,i})$, with $W_0 = K$ <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.

**Upper bound on the growth of $W_t$.** The ratio of successive sums is the expected exponential of the new estimate
under the current probabilities, because $P_{t,i} = \exp(-\eta\hat L_{t-1,i})/W_{t-1}$:

$$
\frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}} = \sum_{i}P_{t,i}\exp(-\eta\hat y_{t,i})
\le \sum_{i}P_{t,i}\paren{1 - \eta\hat y_{t,i} + \tfrac12\eta^2\hat y_{t,i}^2}
= 1 - \eta\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i} + \tfrac12\eta^2\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i}^2.
$$

The inequality is $e^{-z} \le 1 - z + z^2/2$, valid for $z \ge 0$; the estimates are never negative. Taking logarithms
with $\log(1 + u) \le u$ and summing over rounds,

$$
\log\frac{W_T}{W_0} \le -\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i} + \frac{\eta^2}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i}^2.
$$

**Lower bound.** For any fixed arm $j$, $W_T \ge \exp(-\eta\hat L_{T,j})$, so $\log(W_T/W_0) \ge -\eta\hat L_{T,j} - \log K$.

**Combine and take expectations.** Joining the two bounds and dividing by $\eta$,

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i} - \hat L_{T,j} \le \frac{\log K}{\eta} + \frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i}^2.
$$

Three expectations finish the proof. By unbiasedness, $\expect_t[\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i}] = \sum_i P_{t,i}y_{t,i}$,
the expected loss of the learner in round $t$, and $\expect[\hat L_{T,j}] = \sum_t y_{t,j}$, the loss of arm $j$. For
the second moment, $\hat y_{t,i}^2 = y_{t,i}^2\indicator\set{A_t = i}/P_{t,i}^2$, so

$$
\expect_t\brack{\sum_i P_{t,i}\hat y_{t,i}^2} = \sum_i P_{t,i}\cdot\frac{y_{t,i}^2}{P_{t,i}} = \sum_i y_{t,i}^2 \le K.
$$

Therefore the expected loss of EXP3 exceeds the loss of any arm $j$ by at most $\log K/\eta + \eta KT/2$. Losses and
rewards differ by a sign and a constant, so the same bound holds for the regret. The right side is smallest at
$\eta = \sqrt{2\log K / (KT)}$, where

$$
R_T \le \sqrt{2KT\log K}.
$$

The $1/P_{t,i}$ in the estimate makes its variance large when an arm is rarely pulled, but the variance enters the
bound weighted by $P_{t,i}$, which cancels it.

For $K = 3$ and $T = 3000$, the bound is $\sqrt{2 \cdot 3000 \cdot 3 \cdot \log 3} = 141$,
while the sequence built against UCB1 in the figure forces a regret of at least $T(K - 1)/K = 2000$ on it.

The bound is nearly optimal. For every algorithm there is a reward sequence on which the regret is of order
$\sqrt{KT}$ <Cite id="auer2002b" />. The same bound holds against a stochastic environment, so EXP3 is safe everywhere,
but on an easy stochastic problem it stays at order $\sqrt{T}$ where UCB1 achieves $\log T$ <Cite id="bubeck2012" />.

## Variants

- **Original EXP3.** Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Freund and Schapire mix the exponential weights with a uniform distribution,
  $P_{t,i} = (1 - \gamma)\,w_{t,i}/\sum_j w_{t,j} + \gamma/K$, and estimate rewards rather than losses. The mixing keeps
  every probability at least $\gamma/K$, which bounds the reward estimates. The loss-based estimate above needs no
  mixing because losses estimated this way are never negative <Cite id="auer2002b" /> <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
- **EXP3.P and EXP3-IX.** The regret of EXP3 is small in expectation, but its variance can be large. EXP3.P adds a
  confidence bonus to the estimates, and EXP3-IX adds a constant to the denominator, $P_{t,i} + \gamma$; both give
  bounds that hold with high probability <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
- **EXP4.** With $N$ experts, each recommending a distribution over arms every round, EXP4 runs exponential weights
  over the experts and has regret $O(\sqrt{KT\log N})$ against the best expert <Cite id="auer2002b" />. With a finite
  class of policies as experts this solves the <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandit</NoteLink>.
- **EXP3.S.** Mixing the weights towards uniform in every round lets the algorithm track the best sequence of arms with
  a bounded number of switches, for <NoteLink to="non-stationary-bandits">non-stationary</NoteLink> rewards
  <Cite id="auer2002b" />.
- **Full information.** When all $K$ rewards are revealed each round, the same exponential weights without importance
  weighting is the Hedge algorithm, with regret $O(\sqrt{T\log K})$. Observing one reward instead of $K$ multiplies
  the regret bound by a factor $\sqrt{K}$.

## Assumptions

- Rewards lie in $[0, 1]$ and are fixed by an oblivious adversary. Against an adaptive adversary that reacts to the
  learner's past choices, the regret against the best fixed arm can be linear, and other notions of regret are used.
- The tuned learning rate needs $T$. The doubling trick, restarting with $T$ doubled each time the horizon is reached,
  or a decreasing rate $\eta_t = \sqrt{\log K / (Kt)}$ removes this requirement at a constant-factor cost.

## Pitfalls

- **Numerical overflow.** Weights $\exp(-\eta\hat L)$ underflow when the estimated losses grow. Subtract the smallest
  $\hat L$ before exponentiating, as in the <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">log-sum-exp trick</NoteLink>.
- **Linear regret against the best sequence.** The guarantee compares with the best fixed arm. If the best arm changes
  over time, the best fixed arm can be poor and EXP3's low regret can still mean low reward.
- **Slow on easy problems.** On stationary stochastic rewards EXP3 keeps exploring at a rate that suits the worst case.
  When rewards are known to be stochastic, UCB or <Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> are far better.
