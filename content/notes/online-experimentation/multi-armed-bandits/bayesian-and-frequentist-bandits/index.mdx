<Definition>
In a $K$-armed bandit with arm means $\muvec = (\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K)$, the **frequentist regret** of a policy is the
function $R_T(\muvec) = T\mu^* - \expect_{\muvec}[\sum_t X_t]$, with the expectation over rewards and the policy's
randomness for fixed $\muvec$. Given a prior $\Pi$ on $\muvec$, the **Bayesian regret** is its average,
$\mathrm{BR}_T = \expect_{\muvec \sim \Pi}[R_T(\muvec)]$. A **Bayes-optimal** policy minimises the Bayesian regret, or
maximises expected discounted reward under the prior.
</Definition>

## Frequentist and Bayesian guarantees

Both views study the same problem: choose arms, observe rewards, lose reward while learning. They differ in what the
guarantee promises.

A frequentist bound holds for every $\muvec$. It can depend on $\muvec$ through the gaps, as in the
$\sum_i \log T / \Delta_i$ bounds of <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink>, or be a worst case
over all $\muvec$, as in the $\sqrt{KT}$ minimax rate. It protects against the least favourable problem, whatever its
prior probability.

A Bayesian bound holds on average over problems drawn from the prior. It is a statement about a population of
problems: the next thousand pages to test, or the next thousand ad creatives. A policy that is bad on rare problems but
good on typical ones can have small Bayesian regret. When the prior is a fair description of where problems come from,
the Bayesian regret is the average regret over the population of problems the policy will meet.

Every frequentist bound implies a Bayesian bound: if $R_T(\muvec) \le B$ for all $\muvec$, then the average is at most
$B$. The converse fails, because an average can hide bad cases.

## The Bayes-optimal policy

With a prior, the bandit becomes a fully specified decision problem. The state is the posterior over $\muvec$, a pull
changes the posterior by <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, and the expected reward of a pull is
the posterior mean of the pulled arm. The Bayes-optimal policy is the solution of this Markov decision problem over
posteriors. For Bernoulli arms with Beta priors the state is the success and failure counts of every arm. The number of
states reachable in $T$ rounds grows like $T^{2K}$, so exact dynamic programming is out of reach beyond small problems.

### The Gittins index

For an infinite horizon with discount factor $\gamma \in (0, 1)$ and independent arms, the problem decomposes. Gittins
proved that the Bayes-optimal policy pulls, in each round, the arm with the highest **Gittins index**, a number computed
from that arm's posterior alone <Cite id="gittins1979" />. A $K$-armed problem becomes $K$ one-armed problems.

The index of an arm is defined by calibration against a known alternative. Suppose the only other option is a
**retirement arm** that pays a known constant $\lambda$ per round. Pulling the retirement arm reveals nothing, so the
posterior of the unknown arm stays the same, and a decision-maker who retires once retires for good. The one-armed
problem is therefore an optimal stopping problem: pull the unknown arm until some <NoteLink to="martingale">stopping
time</NoteLink> $\tau$, then collect $\lambda$ per round. Its value is

$$
V(\lambda) = \max_{\tau \ge 0}\ \expect\brack{\sum_{t=0}^{\tau - 1}\gamma^t X_t + \sum_{t=\tau}^{\infty}\gamma^t\lambda}.
$$

Retiring immediately, $\tau = 0$, earns $\lambda/(1 - \gamma)$. For small $\lambda$ it is better to pull the arm; for
$\lambda$ above every plausible mean it is better to retire. The **Gittins index** is the $\lambda$ at which the two are
equally good: the largest $\lambda$ with $V(\lambda) > \lambda/(1 - \gamma)$. It is the fair price of the arm.
Rearranging the indifference condition gives the equivalent ratio form,

$$
\nu = \sup_{\tau > 0}\frac{\expect\brack{\sum_{t=0}^{\tau - 1}\gamma^t X_t}}{\expect\brack{\sum_{t=0}^{\tau - 1}\gamma^t}},
$$

the best discounted reward per unit of discounted time the arm can deliver before stopping.

The index exceeds the posterior mean. Pulling reveals information, and the option to retire later means bad news costs
little while good news is exploited for ever. For $\gamma = 0.9$, an arm with a uniform $\Beta(1, 1)$ prior has
posterior mean $0.5$ and Gittins index $0.703$. A Bayes-optimal player prefers it to a known arm that pays $0.65$. After
eight observations with half successes, $\Beta(5, 5)$, the same mean has index $0.568$: the posterior is sharper and
the information is worth less.

### Limits of the index

The index theorem needs geometric discounting, independent arms and arms whose state changes only when pulled. It
fails for a fixed finite horizon, where the value of information falls as the end approaches, and for correlated arms,
where pulling one arm teaches something about another. It also depends on the prior: with a prior far from the truth,
the Bayes-optimal policy for that prior can have large frequentist regret. And the index must be computed per arm
state, which is cheap for Beta–Bernoulli arms and expensive for richer models.

## Algorithms with guarantees in both senses

The most used algorithms are good in both senses.

**Bayesian algorithms with frequentist guarantees.** <NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</NoteLink> is
defined through a prior and a posterior, but its regret bounds for Bernoulli arms hold for every fixed $\muvec$ and
match the Lai–Robbins constant <Cite id="agrawal2012" /> <Cite id="kaufmann2012" />. The prior acts as a device for
randomising sensibly, not as an assumption the guarantee rests on.

**Frequentist analysis gives Bayesian guarantees.** Russo and Van Roy showed that any upper confidence bound analysis
transfers to Thompson sampling's Bayesian regret, even though Thompson sampling never computes a confidence bound
<Cite id="russo2018" />. Let $A^* = \argmax_i \mu_i$ be the optimal arm, random under the prior,
and let $U_t(i)$ be any upper confidence bound computed from the history $H_{t-1}$. Add and subtract:

$$
\mu_{A^*} - \mu_{A_t} = \paren{\mu_{A^*} - U_t(A^*)} + \paren{U_t(A^*) - U_t(A_t)} + \paren{U_t(A_t) - \mu_{A_t}}.
$$

Thompson sampling matches probabilities: given $H_{t-1}$, the pulled arm $A_t$ has the same distribution as $A^*$. $U_t$
is fixed given $H_{t-1}$, so $\expect[U_t(A^*) \mid H_{t-1}] = \expect[U_t(A_t) \mid H_{t-1}]$, and the middle term has
expectation zero. Summing over rounds,

$$
\mathrm{BR}_T = \expect\brack{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{U_t(A_t) - \mu_{A_t}}} + \expect\brack{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{\mu_{A^*} - U_t(A^*)}}.
$$

For a UCB policy, $A_t$ maximises $U_t$, so the middle term is at most zero and the same two sums bound its regret. The
first sum is the total width of the confidence intervals at the pulled arms, the second the cost of the intervals
failing to cover the best arm. Both are exactly what a UCB analysis bounds. Every such bound, for $K$-armed, linear or
other structured bandits, is therefore also a bound on the Bayesian regret of Thompson sampling. For $K$ arms this gives
$O(\sqrt{KT\log T})$ for every prior.

## Comparison

| | Frequentist view | Bayesian view |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unknowns | fixed means $\muvec$ | means drawn from a prior $\Pi$ |
| Criterion | $R_T(\muvec)$ for every $\muvec$, or its maximum | $\expect_{\Pi}[R_T(\muvec)]$ |
| Optimal policy | none uniformly; asymptotically optimal ones reach the Lai–Robbins constant | the Bayes-optimal policy for $\Pi$; Gittins index for discounted independent arms |
| Typical algorithms | UCB1, <Gloss name="kl-ucb" />, <NoteLink to="best-arm-identification">successive elimination</NoteLink> | Gittins index, Thompson sampling, Bayes-optimal lookahead |
| Main risk | over-exploring on problems that are easy in practice | a prior that misdescribes the problems met |

## Assumptions

- The Gittins index theorem assumes independent arms, geometric discounting and arms that change only when pulled
  <Cite id="gittins1979" />.
- Bayesian regret bounds assume that the means are drawn from the stated prior. A frequentist bound needs no prior.

## Pitfalls

- **Discounting is a modelling choice.** The discount $\gamma$ sets an effective horizon of $1/(1 - \gamma)$ rounds. A
  Gittins policy tuned for $\gamma = 0.9$ explores as if the game lasted ten rounds, which is far too little for a
  campaign of a million visitors.
- **Bayes-optimal is not robust.** Optimality holds under the stated prior. A tight prior centred on the wrong value
  can make the Bayes-optimal policy commit to a bad arm, with regret linear in $T$ on that problem.
- **Bayesian regret is not a posterior statement.** A bound on $\mathrm{BR}_T$ says nothing about the regret on the
  particular problem at hand, only about the average over problems like it.
