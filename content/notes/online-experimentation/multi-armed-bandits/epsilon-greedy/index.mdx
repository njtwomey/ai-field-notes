<Definition>
**ε-greedy** is a policy for a $K$-armed bandit with an exploration rate $\varepsilon_t \in [0, 1]$. In round $t$ it
draws $U_t \sim \Unif(0, 1)$. If $U_t < \varepsilon_t$ it pulls an arm chosen uniformly at random from all $K$ arms;
otherwise it pulls the arm with the highest empirical mean $\hat\mu_i(t-1)$, breaking ties arbitrarily. **Constant
ε-greedy** uses $\varepsilon_t = \varepsilon$. **Decaying ε-greedy** uses a schedule that tends to zero, such as
$\varepsilon_t = \min(1, cK/t)$ <Cite id="auer2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The greedy policy, $\varepsilon = 0$, always plays the arm that looks best. It can fail forever. If the best arm's
first few rewards are unlucky, its empirical mean falls below another arm's, the greedy policy stops pulling it, and
its estimate never gets the chance to recover. The random pulls of ε-greedy guarantee that every arm keeps being
sampled, so every estimate converges to its true mean and the greedy choice eventually becomes right.

The random pulls are undirected. They are spread uniformly over all arms, including arms that are already known to be
bad and the arm that is already known to be best. This is the weakness that UCB and Thompson sampling fix: they direct
exploration towards arms that might still be the best.

## Constant ε has linear regret

With constant $\varepsilon$, each round pulls each arm $i$ with probability at least $\varepsilon/K$, whatever the
history. So $\expect[N_i(T)] \ge \varepsilon T/K$ for every arm. The regret decomposition of the
<NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">multi-armed bandit</NoteLink>, $R_T = \sum_i \Delta_i\expect[N_i(T)]$, then gives

$$
R_T \ge \frac{\varepsilon T}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_i,
$$

where $\Delta_i = \mu^* - \mu_i$ is the gap of arm $i$. This lower bound is linear in $T$. Once the estimates have
converged, the greedy rounds cost nothing and the regret grows at exactly this rate. Constant ε-greedy trades a fast
start for a permanent tax. It remains popular when rewards drift, because the tax buys continued tracking.

## Decaying ε

To reach sublinear regret the exploration rate must fall. With $\varepsilon_t = \min(1, cK/t)$, the expected number of
exploration pulls that arm $i$ receives up to round $T$ is

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\varepsilon_t}{K} \approx \sum_{t = cK}^{T}\frac{c}{t} \approx c\log\frac{T}{cK}.
$$

Each arm is therefore explored about $c\log T$ times, and the exploration part of the regret is of order
$c\log T\sum_i \Delta_i$. The greedy rounds are safe once every arm has enough samples for its empirical mean to be
within half the smallest gap of its true mean. By a sub-Gaussian tail bound, $n$ samples give a deviation larger than
$\Delta/2$ with probability at most $2\exp(-n\Delta^2/8)$ for rewards in $[0, 1]$. With $n \approx c\log t$ samples at
round $t$, this is at most $2t^{-c\Delta^2/8}$, which is summable over $t$ when $c\Delta^2 > 8$. So the schedule works
when $c$ is of order $1/\Delta_{\min}^2$.

Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer made this precise. With $\varepsilon_t = \min(1, cK/(d^2 t))$, $c > 5$ and
$0 < d \le \Delta_{\min}$, the probability of pulling any given suboptimal arm in round $t \ge cK/d^2$ is at most
$c/(d^2 t)$ plus a term of order $1/t$ that vanishes faster, so the regret is $O(K\log T/d^2)$ <Cite id="auer2002" />.
The guarantee needs $d$, a lower bound on the smallest gap, which is unknown in practice. A schedule tuned for a large
gap under-explores when the gap is small, and one tuned for a small gap over-explores otherwise. The figure's $c$
slider shows both failures.

## Variants

- **Greedy with optimistic initial values.** Initialise every estimate to a value above any plausible mean, as if each
  arm had already paid a high reward. The greedy policy then tries every arm until its estimate falls below the
  others. This explores early and never afterwards, so it has the weakness of explore-then-commit with an implicit
  $m$ <Cite id="sutton2018" />.
- **Boltzmann (softmax) exploration.** Pull arm $i$ with probability proportional to $\exp(\hat\mu_i/\tau)$, where
  $\tau > 0$ is a temperature. Better-looking arms are explored more, which is less wasteful than uniform exploration.
  With a fixed or naively decaying temperature it can still have linear or polynomial regret on some problems
  <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
- **ε-first.** All exploration happens at the start: this is
  <NoteLink to="explore-then-commit">explore-then-commit</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- Rewards are stationary and bounded, as in the stochastic bandit. Constant ε-greedy tolerates slow drift, because it
  never stops sampling.
- The decaying schedule's guarantee needs a lower bound on the smallest gap.

## Pitfalls

- **Pure greedy is not a safe default.** With Bernoulli rewards and one initial pull per arm, the greedy policy
  abandons an arm whose first reward is 0 if another arm's first reward is 1. For two arms with means 0.6 and 0.5,
  this happens with probability $0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.2$. The policy then pulls the worse arm forever, because that
  arm's running mean, at least $1/n$ after $n$ pulls, never falls to the better arm's estimate of 0.
- **Uniform exploration does not scale with many arms.** With $K$ arms of which most are clearly bad, ε-greedy keeps
  spending $\varepsilon(K-1)/K$ of its rounds on them.
- **ε is not a confidence level.** The value $\varepsilon = 0.1$ is common in practice by convention, not because it
  balances anything for a given problem.
