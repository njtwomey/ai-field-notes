<Definition>
**Best-arm identification** is a bandit problem in which a learner samples $K$ arms with unknown means
$\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K$ and then recommends one arm $\hat\imath$. Only the recommendation is scored. In the
**fixed-confidence** setting the learner chooses when to stop and must satisfy
$\prob(\hat\imath \ne \argmax_i \mu_i) \le \delta$ for a given $\delta \in (0, 1)$, which makes it **$\delta$-PAC**;
the cost is the number of samples $\tau$. In the **fixed-budget** setting the number of samples $n$ is given and the
cost is the error probability <Cite id="kaufmann2016" />.
</Definition>

## Pure exploration

The <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">regret</NoteLink> of a bandit charges every pull of a suboptimal arm. Best-arm
identification charges nothing during sampling and everything for a wrong final answer. It models a test phase
followed by a long deployment: choosing which of $K$ page designs to launch, which drug dose to take to the next trial
phase, or which hyperparameter setting to train at full scale.

The two goals need different allocations. A regret-minimising algorithm such as
<NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink> pulls each suboptimal arm only about $\log T$ times over
a horizon of $T$ pulls, and nearly all its pulls go to the leader. The leader is then measured precisely, but the
runner-up is not, and it is the runner-up's measurement that decides whether the leader can be declared best with
confidence. An identification algorithm spends its samples where the comparison is close: on the best arm and its
nearest competitors, roughly in proportion to $1/\Delta_i^2$, where $\Delta_i$ is arm $i$'s gap to the best mean.

## Successive elimination

Successive elimination samples all surviving arms equally and drops an arm as soon as the data show it is not the best
<Cite id="evendar2006" />. For rewards in $[0, 1]$:

1. Start with every arm active. In round $s = 1, 2, \dots$, pull every active arm once. Each active arm now has $s$
   samples and a sample mean $\hat\mu_{i,s}$.
2. Set the radius $r_s = \sqrt{\log(4Ks^2/\delta) / (2s)}$.
3. Remove every active arm $i$ with $\hat\mu_{i,s} + r_s < \max_{j \text{ active}}(\hat\mu_{j,s} - r_s)$: its upper
   bound is below some arm's lower bound.
4. Stop when one arm remains and recommend it.

<SuccessiveElimination />

**Correctness.** Call the run *good* if every sample mean stays within $r_s$ of its true mean, for every arm and every
round. By <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>, arm $i$ at round $s$ violates this
with probability at most $2e^{-2sr_s^2} = 2\delta/(4Ks^2) = \delta/(2Ks^2)$ <Cite id="hoeffding1963" />. A union bound
over the $K$ arms and all rounds gives

$$
\prob(\text{not good}) \le \sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\delta}{2Ks^2} = \frac{\delta}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{6}
< \delta.
$$

On a good run the best arm is never removed. Its upper bound satisfies $\hat\mu^* + r_s \ge \mu^*$, and every other
arm's lower bound satisfies $\hat\mu_j - r_s \le \mu_j \le \mu^*$. So the recommendation is correct with probability at
least $1 - \delta$.

**Sample complexity.** On a good run, a suboptimal arm $i$ is removed once its upper bound falls below the best arm's
lower bound. That is guaranteed when $\mu_i + 2r_s < \mu^* - 2r_s$, that is when $r_s < \Delta_i/4$. Squaring,
$\log(4Ks^2/\delta)/(2s) < \Delta_i^2/16$, which holds once

$$
s \ge s_i \approx \frac{8}{\Delta_i^2}\log\frac{4Ks_i^2}{\delta} = O\paren{\frac{1}{\Delta_i^2}\log\frac{K}{\delta\Delta_i}}.
$$

Arm $i$ is sampled $s_i$ times. The best arm survives until the last competitor is removed, so it is sampled as often
as the arm with the smallest gap; set its $\Delta$ to that smallest gap. The total is
$\sum_i s_i = O\paren{\sum_i \Delta_i^{-2}\log(K/(\delta\Delta_i))}$. Uniform sampling with the same intervals must
sample every arm until the closest pair separates, $K$ times the largest $s_i$. The saving is large when the gaps
differ.

The quantity $H = \sum_i \Delta_i^{-2}$ is the **complexity** of the problem. Kaufmann, Cappé and Garivier showed that
every $\delta$-PAC algorithm needs of order $H\log(1/\delta)$ samples in expectation, with exact constants given by KL
divergences between the arms' distributions <Cite id="kaufmann2016" />. Successive elimination is optimal up to the
logarithmic factor inside the sum.

## Top-two Thompson sampling

<NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</NoteLink> pulls each arm with its posterior probability of being
best. For identification this starves the challengers: once the leader is fairly certain, almost every pull goes to it,
and the runner-up's estimate improves slowly. Russo's **top-two Thompson sampling** fixes this with a parameter
$\beta \in (0, 1)$, usually $1/2$ <Cite id="russo2016" />:

1. Draw $\tilde\thetavec$ from the posterior and let $I = \argmax_i \tilde\theta_i$.
2. With probability $\beta$, pull $I$.
3. Otherwise, draw again from the posterior until the argmax $J$ differs from $I$, and pull $J$.

The leader gets a fraction $\beta$ of the pulls, and the rest go to the arms most likely to beat it. Russo proved that
the posterior probability of a wrong answer then decays at the fastest exponential rate possible for any allocation that
gives the best arm a fraction $\beta$ of the samples. Stopping when the posterior probability of the leader being best
exceeds $1 - \delta$ gives a practical fixed-confidence procedure.

## Fixed budget

With a budget of $n$ samples and no stopping decision, **successive rejects** divides the budget into $K - 1$ phases
of increasing length. After each phase it discards the arm with the lowest sample mean, so the last phases compare only
the best few arms with many samples each. Its error probability is at most about
$\exp\paren{-n/(H_2\log K)}$, where $H_2 = \max_i i\,\Delta_{(i)}^{-2}$ over the gaps sorted in increasing order
<Cite id="audibert2010" />. Unlike fixed-confidence methods, it needs no knowledge of $\delta$ or the gaps.

## Relation to A/B testing

A classical <NoteLink to="a-b-test">A/B test</NoteLink> is fixed-budget identification with uniform allocation and a
significance test at the end. With two arms, uniform allocation is optimal for equal variances. With many arms,
elimination saves samples by dropping clear losers early, and a sequential stopping rule saves samples when the best
arm is clearly better.

Comparing $K$ arms with pairwise tests at level $\alpha$ each inflates the error rate, a problem of
<NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>; the union bound in the elimination radius, the
$\log(4Ks^2/\delta)$ term, keeps the error probability below $\delta$ over all $K$ arms and all rounds at once.

## Assumptions

- There is a unique best arm. With ties, a fixed-confidence algorithm may never stop; an $\varepsilon$-good version
  accepts any arm within $\varepsilon$ of the best.
- Rewards are bounded or sub-Gaussian and independent, and the arms are stationary during sampling.

## Pitfalls

- **Hoeffding intervals are conservative.** For <NoteLink to="conversion-rate-metrics">conversion rates</NoteLink> near
  0 the elimination radius above is several times wider than an exact binomial interval. KL-based intervals stop much
  earlier with the same guarantee.
- **Sample means at the <NoteLink to="martingale">stopping time</NoteLink> are biased.** The winner's estimate
  is too high, because arms were kept or dropped by comparing the same noisy estimates.
- **Close runners-up dominate the cost.** Two arms with gap $0.01$ need about $8 \times 10^4\log(4Ks^2/\delta)$ samples
  each under successive elimination. If any arm within $\varepsilon$ would do, set $\varepsilon$ and stop far earlier.
