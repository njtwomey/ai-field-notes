<Definition>
**UCB1** is a policy for a $K$-armed bandit with rewards in $[0, 1]$. It pulls each arm once. In every later round $t$
it pulls

$$
A_t = \argmax_{i} \paren{\hat\mu_i(t-1) + \sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{N_i(t-1)}}},
$$

where $N_i(t-1)$ is the number of pulls of arm $i$ in the first $t - 1$ rounds and $\hat\mu_i(t-1)$ is the mean of
their rewards. The bracket is arm $i$'s **index**, an upper confidence bound on its mean $\mu_i$
<Cite id="auer2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

UCB1 follows the principle of **optimism in the face of uncertainty**: act as if each arm were as good as the data
plausibly allow. The index of an arm is its sample mean plus a bonus that shrinks as the arm is pulled. An arm can have
the highest index for two reasons. Either its sample mean is high, and pulling it exploits. Or it has few pulls, its
bonus is large, and pulling it explores.

Optimism corrects itself. If an arm is pulled because its index is high but its true mean is low, its rewards pull
the sample mean down and its bonus shrinks, so its index falls below the best arm's. If the optimal arm has an unluckily
low sample mean, its bonus keeps growing with $\log t$ while it is not pulled, so it is eventually tried again. A
suboptimal arm stops being pulled once its interval is narrow enough to lie below the optimal mean, and it is pulled
again only rarely, as $\log t$ grows.

The exploration is directed. Unlike <NoteLink to="epsilon-greedy">ε-greedy</NoteLink>, UCB1 never spends a round on an
arm that is confidently worse than another. And unlike
<NoteLink to="explore-then-commit">explore-then-commit</NoteLink>, it needs neither the horizon $T$ nor the gaps.

## Where the bonus comes from

The bonus is the width of a one-sided <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> from
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> <Cite id="hoeffding1963" />. Let
$X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent rewards in $[0, 1]$ with mean $\mu$, and let $\hat\mu_n$ be their average. Then for
every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$
\prob\paren{\mu \ge \hat\mu_n + \varepsilon} \le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\prob\paren{\mu \le \hat\mu_n - \varepsilon} \le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.
$$

The proof is the Chernoff method. For any $\lambda > 0$, <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's
inequality</NoteLink> applied to $e^{\lambda(\mu - \hat\mu_n)n}$ gives

$$
\prob\paren{\mu - \hat\mu_n \ge \varepsilon} \le e^{-\lambda n\varepsilon}\,\expect\brack{e^{\lambda\sum_{j}(\mu - X_j)}}
= e^{-\lambda n\varepsilon}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\expect\brack{e^{\lambda(\mu - X_j)}}.
$$

**Hoeffding's lemma** bounds each factor: a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> with mean zero on
an interval of length 1 has $\expect[e^{\lambda Y}] \le e^{\lambda^2/8}$. The bound becomes
$\exp(-\lambda n\varepsilon + n\lambda^2/8)$. The exponent is smallest at $\lambda = 4\varepsilon$, where it equals
$-2n\varepsilon^2$.

To turn this into an interval, fix a failure probability $\delta$ and solve $e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} = \delta$ for
$\varepsilon$:

$$
\varepsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n}}.
$$

With probability at least $1 - \delta$, $\mu < \hat\mu_n + \sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/(2n)}$. UCB1 takes $\delta = t^{-4}$,
which makes the bonus $\sqrt{4\log t/(2n)} = \sqrt{2\log t/n}$. The confidence level rises with $t$: the longer the
game, the more costly it is to write off the best arm by mistake, so the intervals must fail less often.

<Derivation title="Hoeffding's lemma">
Let $Y$ take values in $[a, b]$ with $\expect[Y] = 0$, and let $\psi(\lambda) = \log\expect[e^{\lambda Y}]$. Then
$\psi(0) = 0$ and $\psi'(0) = \expect[Y] = 0$. The second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is the
variance of $Y$ under the tilted law with density proportional to $e^{\lambda y}$. That law still lives on $[a, b]$, and
any random variable on an interval of length $b - a$ has variance at most $(b - a)^2/4$, the variance of a fair coin on
the two endpoints. So $\psi''(\lambda) \le (b - a)^2/4$ for every $\lambda$. Taylor's theorem with the remainder in
Lagrange form gives $\psi(\lambda) = \psi(0) + \lambda\psi'(0) + \tfrac12\lambda^2\psi''(\xi) \le \lambda^2(b - a)^2/8$
for some $\xi$ between $0$ and $\lambda$. For rewards in $[0, 1]$, $Y = \mu - X$ lies in an interval of length 1, which
gives $\expect[e^{\lambda Y}] \le e^{\lambda^2/8}$.
</Derivation>

## Regret

Write $\mu^*$ for the optimal mean, $\Delta_i = \mu^* - \mu_i$ for the gap of arm $i$, and
$c_{t,s} = \sqrt{2\log t / s}$ for the bonus of an arm with $s$ pulls at round $t$. By the regret decomposition of the
<NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">multi-armed bandit</NoteLink>, $R_T = \sum_i \Delta_i\expect[N_i(T)]$, so it
suffices to bound the pulls of each suboptimal arm <Cite id="auer2002" />.

**Step 1: a suboptimal pull needs one of three events.** Suppose arm $i$ is pulled at round $t$ when it has $s_i$
pulls and the optimal arm has $s$ pulls, with sample means $\hat\mu_{i,s_i}$ and $\hat\mu^*_s$. Its index is at least
the optimal arm's:

$$
\hat\mu^*_s + c_{t,s} \le \hat\mu_{i,s_i} + c_{t,s_i}.
$$

This inequality implies at least one of the following.

1. The optimal arm's index is below its mean: $\hat\mu^*_s + c_{t,s} \le \mu^*$.
2. Arm $i$'s sample mean is too high: $\hat\mu_{i,s_i} \ge \mu_i + c_{t,s_i}$.
3. Arm $i$'s interval is still wide: $\mu^* < \mu_i + 2c_{t,s_i}$.

If all three failed, then $\hat\mu^*_s + c_{t,s} > \mu^* \ge \mu_i + 2c_{t,s_i} > \hat\mu_{i,s_i} + c_{t,s_i}$,
which contradicts the inequality.

**Step 2: event 3 stops after enough pulls.** Event 3 says $c_{t,s_i} > \Delta_i/2$, that is
$2\log t / s_i > \Delta_i^2/4$, that is $s_i < 8\log t / \Delta_i^2$. Once arm $i$ has
$\ell = \lceil 8\log T / \Delta_i^2\rceil$ pulls, event 3 is impossible for every $t \le T$.

**Step 3: events 1 and 2 are rare.** By Hoeffding's inequality with $\varepsilon = c_{t,s}$, each of events 1 and 2
has probability at most $e^{-2s \cdot 2\log t / s} = t^{-4}$ for any fixed pull counts. At round $t$ the counts $s$ and
$s_i$ are unknown, so take a union bound over all $t^2$ pairs of values. The probability that arm $i$ is pulled at
round $t$ with at least $\ell$ pulls is at most $2t^2 \cdot t^{-4} = 2t^{-2}$.

**Step 4: add up.** Arm $i$ is pulled at most $\ell$ times before it has $\ell$ pulls, and afterwards only on rounds
where event 1 or 2 occurs:

$$
\expect[N_i(T)] \le \frac{8\log T}{\Delta_i^2} + 1 + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{t^2}
= \frac{8\log T}{\Delta_i^2} + 1 + \frac{\pi^2}{3}.
$$

Multiplying by $\Delta_i$ and summing gives the regret bound of Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer:

$$
R_T \le \sum_{i:\Delta_i > 0}\frac{8\log T}{\Delta_i} + \paren{1 + \frac{\pi^2}{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\Delta_i.
$$

The bound is logarithmic in $T$ and holds at every $T$, so UCB1 is an anytime algorithm. The constant 8 is loose. A
finer analysis of the same index shows $\expect[N_i(T)] \le (2 + o(1))\log T / \Delta_i^2$ <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
The figure shows this rate: a suboptimal arm's count follows $c\log t / \Delta_i^2$ with $c = 2$, the count at which
its bonus equals its gap.

<Derivation title="A bound that does not depend on the gaps">
The gap-dependent bound is large when some $\Delta_i$ is tiny, but an arm with a tiny gap costs little per pull. Split
the arms at a threshold $\delta > 0$. Arms with $\Delta_i < \delta$ cost less than $\delta$ per round, so at most
$T\delta$ in total. Each arm with $\Delta_i \ge \delta$ costs at most
$\Delta_i\expect[N_i(T)] \le 8\log T/\Delta_i + O(1) \le 8\log T/\delta + O(1)$. So

$$
R_T \le T\delta + \frac{8K\log T}{\delta} + O(K).
$$

The right side is smallest at $\delta = \sqrt{8K\log T / T}$, which gives $R_T \le 2\sqrt{8KT\log T} + O(K)$. This is
within a factor $\sqrt{\log T}$ of the minimax lower bound of order $\sqrt{KT}$ <Cite id="bubeck2012" />.
</Derivation>

## Comparison with the lower bound

For Bernoulli arms the Lai–Robbins bound says every consistent policy pulls arm $i$ at least
$(1 + o(1))\log T / \KL(\mu_i \,\|\, \mu^*)$ times, where $\KL(p \,\|\, q)$ is the KL divergence between Bernoulli
distributions with means $p$ and $q$ <Cite id="lai1985" />. Pinsker's inequality gives
$\KL(\mu_i \,\|\, \mu^*) \ge 2\Delta_i^2$, so the lower bound is at most $\log T / (2\Delta_i^2)$. UCB1 pulls about
$2\log T / \Delta_i^2$ times, at least four times the lower bound. The gap is largest when the means are near 0 or 1,
where Bernoulli rewards have small variance and Hoeffding's inequality, which assumes the worst variance, is loose. For
means $0.05$ and $0.1$, $\KL = 0.0167$ while $2\Delta^2 = 0.005$. <Gloss name="kl-ucb" /> replaces the Hoeffding
interval with a KL interval and attains the Lai–Robbins constant.

## Variants

- **General confidence level.** The index $\hat\mu_i + \sqrt{2\log f(t) / N_i}$ with $f(t) = 1 + t\log^2 t$ has the
  asymptotic constant $2/\Delta_i^2$ exactly <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
- **Sub-Gaussian rewards.** For rewards that are $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, not bounded, the bonus becomes
  $\sigma\sqrt{2\log f(t) / N_i}$ with the same analysis.
- **Variance-aware bonuses.** Replacing the worst-case variance $1/4$ by an empirical variance gives tighter intervals
  for low-variance arms.
- **MOSS.** A bonus of $\sqrt{\max(0, \log(T/(KN_i))) / N_i}$ removes the $\sqrt{\log T}$ factor from the worst-case
  bound, at the cost of needing $T$ <Cite id="bubeck2012" />.
- **KL-UCB.** The Hoeffding interval is replaced by the set of means within a KL-divergence budget of the sample mean.

## Assumptions

- Rewards lie in $[0, 1]$ or another known bounded range; the bonus must be rescaled by the range width otherwise.
- The rewards of each arm are <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss> over time.
- Feedback is immediate. With delayed feedback the index is computed from stale counts, and several rounds may pull
  the same arm before its bonus shrinks.

## Pitfalls

- **The bonus assumes worst-case variance.** For <NoteLink to="conversion-rate-metrics">conversion rates</NoteLink> of a
  few percent the Hoeffding interval is several times wider than an exact binomial interval, and UCB1 explores far more
  than needed.
- **Deterministic choices.** Given the same history, UCB1 always pulls the same arm. In a batched deployment, where
  thousands of users are served between updates, every user in the batch gets the same arm. Randomised policies such
  as <Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> spread a batch across arms.
- **The rewards must be scaled.** Using revenue in euros with the bonus $\sqrt{2\log t / N_i}$ makes the bonus
  negligible, and UCB1 becomes nearly greedy.
