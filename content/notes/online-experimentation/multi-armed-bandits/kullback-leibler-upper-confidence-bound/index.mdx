<Definition>
**KL-UCB** is an index policy for a $K$-armed bandit with rewards in $[0, 1]$. Write
$\operatorname{kl}(p, q) = p\log\frac{p}{q} + (1 - p)\log\frac{1 - p}{1 - q}$ for the <Gloss name="kl">KL divergence</Gloss> between <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli
distributions</NoteLink> with means $p$ and $q$. After pulling each arm once, KL-UCB pulls in round $t$ the arm with the largest
index

$$
U_i(t) = \max\set{q \in [\hat\mu_i, 1] : N_i \operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu_i, q) \le \log t + c\log\log t},
$$

where $N_i$ and $\hat\mu_i$ are the pull count and sample mean of arm $i$ after $t - 1$ rounds and $c \ge 0$ is a
constant: $c = 3$ in the analysis and $c = 0$ in practice <Cite id="garivier2011" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Like <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink>, KL-UCB is optimistic: it plays each arm as if its mean were the largest
value the data do not rule out. The difference is how "rule out" is measured. UCB1 uses <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>, which
holds for every distribution on $[0, 1]$ and therefore assumes the worst variance, $1/4$. A Bernoulli arm with mean
near 0 or 1 has much smaller variance, $\mu(1 - \mu)$, and its sample mean concentrates faster. The KL divergence
measures distance between Bernoulli means in a way that accounts for this. Near the boundary the same distance
$\abs{q - \hat\mu}$ is a much larger divergence, so the interval is narrower.

A candidate mean $q$ is kept when data with mean $\hat\mu_i$ would not be surprising under $q$. The level $\log t$
sets the surprise threshold: $q$ is dropped when the observed data have probability below about $1/t$ under $q$.

## The Chernoff bound

The index is built from the exact large-deviation rate of a Bernoulli sample mean. Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be
independent Bernoulli($q$) rewards with average $\hat\mu_n$, and let $x < q$. For every $\lambda > 0$, <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's
inequality</NoteLink> applied to $e^{-\lambda\sum_j X_j}$ gives

$$
\prob(\hat\mu_n \le x) = \prob\paren{e^{-\lambda\sum_j X_j} \ge e^{-\lambda nx}}
\le e^{\lambda nx}\paren{1 - q + qe^{-\lambda}}^n = \exp\paren{n f(\lambda)},
$$

with $f(\lambda) = \lambda x + \log(1 - q + qe^{-\lambda})$. Setting $f'(\lambda) = 0$ gives
$x(1 - q + qe^{-\lambda}) = qe^{-\lambda}$, so $e^{-\lambda} = \frac{x(1 - q)}{q(1 - x)}$, which is below 1 because
$x < q$. At this $\lambda$, $1 - q + qe^{-\lambda} = \frac{1 - q}{1 - x}$ and $\lambda x = x\log\frac{q(1 - x)}{x(1 - q)}$.
Adding the two logarithms,

$$
f(\lambda) = x\log\frac{q}{x} + x\log\frac{1 - x}{1 - q} + \log\frac{1 - q}{1 - x}
= -\operatorname{kl}(x, q),
\qquad
\prob(\hat\mu_n \le x) \le e^{-n\operatorname{kl}(x, q)}.
$$

This is the **Chernoff bound**. Its exponent is the best possible: <NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds">Cramér's theorem</NoteLink> shows the probability decays at
exactly this rate as $n$ grows.

Now reverse the roles. After observing $\hat\mu_n = \hat\mu$, a candidate mean $q > \hat\mu$ explains the data with
probability at most $e^{-n\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu, q)}$ of seeing a sample mean this low. Requiring that probability
to be at least $e^{-\ell}$ keeps the set $\set{q : n\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu, q) \le \ell}$, an upper confidence bound
at level $\ell$. KL-UCB takes $\ell = \log t + c\log\log t$. The bound also holds for any rewards in $[0, 1]$, not only
Bernoulli ones, because for a fixed mean the Bernoulli distribution has the largest <Gloss name="mgf">moment generating function</Gloss> among
distributions on $[0, 1]$ <Cite id="garivier2011" />.

**Comparison with Hoeffding.** Pinsker's inequality states $\operatorname{kl}(p, q) \ge 2(p - q)^2$. Every $q$ with
$n\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu, q) \le \ell$ therefore satisfies $2n(q - \hat\mu)^2 \le \ell$, so the KL interval lies
inside the Hoeffding interval $\hat\mu + \sqrt{\ell/(2n)}$ at the same level. The two agree near $\hat\mu = 1/2$, where
Pinsker's inequality is nearly tight. They differ most near the boundary. With $\hat\mu = 0.1$, $n = 20$ and
$\ell = \log 1000 = 6.91$, the Hoeffding index is $0.516$ and the KL-UCB index is $0.486$; with $\hat\mu = 0.5$ they
are $0.916$ and $0.853$.

## Regret

Garivier and Cappé proved that for rewards in $[0, 1]$, KL-UCB with $c = 3$ satisfies, for every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$
\expect[N_i(T)] \le \frac{(1 + \varepsilon)\log T}{\operatorname{kl}(\mu_i, \mu^*)} + O(\log\log T)
$$

for every suboptimal arm $i$ <Cite id="garivier2011" />. For Bernoulli rewards this matches the <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">Lai–Robbins lower bound</NoteLink>
$\log T / \operatorname{kl}(\mu_i, \mu^*)$ <Cite id="lai1985" />, so KL-UCB is asymptotically optimal.

The constant follows from the index. With high probability the optimal arm's index is at least $\mu^*$, because the
confidence set contains the true mean. Arm $i$ is then pulled only while its own index is at least $\mu^*$, which by
the definition of the index means $N_i\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu_i, \mu^*) \le \log t$. Once the sample mean
$\hat\mu_i$ has settled near $\mu_i$, this allows at most about $\log t / \operatorname{kl}(\mu_i, \mu^*)$ pulls. The
$\log\log t$ term in the level pays for the rounds in which the optimal arm's index falls below $\mu^*$. Its sample
mean is a <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>, and the $c\log\log t$ margin keeps the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">total probability</NoteLink> of those rounds summable.

UCB1 pulls a suboptimal arm about $2\log T / \Delta_i^2$ times. Since $\operatorname{kl}(\mu_i, \mu^*) \ge 2\Delta_i^2$,
KL-UCB pulls it at most a quarter as often, and far less near the boundary. For means $0.05$ and $0.1$,
$\operatorname{kl}(0.05, 0.1) = 0.0167$ against $2\Delta^2 = 0.005$.

## Computing the index

The index has no closed form. For fixed $\hat\mu$, $q \mapsto \operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu, q)$ is convex and increasing on
$[\hat\mu, 1)$, from 0 to $+\infty$, so the index is the unique root of $N\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu, q) = \ell$ there.
Bisection finds it to $10^{-6}$ in about 20 steps. <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink>, started at the Hoeffding bound
$\hat\mu + \sqrt{\ell/(2N)}$, which lies to the right of the root, converges faster and decreases monotonically because
the function is convex. When $\hat\mu = 1$ the index is 1.

## Variants

- **Other <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential families</NoteLink>.** Replacing the Bernoulli divergence by the KL divergence of another one-parameter
  exponential family gives an optimal index for that family. For Gaussian rewards with known variance $\sigma^2$ the
  divergence is $(p - q)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ and the index reduces to a UCB1-style bonus <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
- **KL-UCB+ and horizon-aware levels.** Levels such as $\log(t/N_i)$ shrink the bonus of well-sampled arms and improve
  the finite-time behaviour.
- **Empirical KL-UCB.** For general bounded rewards, a divergence computed from the empirical distribution adapts to the
  arm's actual variance and shape.

## Assumptions

- Rewards lie in $[0, 1]$. For other bounded rewards, rescale them first.
- The arms are stationary and independent, as in the stochastic bandit.

## Pitfalls

- **Numerical care at the boundary.** $\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu, q)$ has logarithms of $\hat\mu$, $1 - \hat\mu$, $q$
  and $1 - q$. Clip them away from 0 and 1, or handle $\hat\mu \in \set{0, 1}$ separately.
- **Asymptotic optimality is not finite-time dominance.** The $O(\log\log T)$ term and the requirement $c = 3$ for the
  proof make the guarantee asymptotic. In practice $c = 0$ works better, and <Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> often matches KL-UCB.
