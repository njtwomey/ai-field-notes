<Definition>
A log $\Dcal = \set{(x_i, a_i, r_i, p_i)}_{i=1}^{n}$ records, for each round, a context $x_i \sim D$, the arm
$a_i \sim \pi_0(\cdot \mid x_i)$ chosen by a **logging policy** $\pi_0$, the reward $r_i$ with mean $\mu(x_i, a_i)$, and
the **propensity** $p_i = \pi_0(a_i \mid x_i)$. **Off-policy evaluation** estimates the value of a different **target
policy** $\pi$,

$$
V(\pi) = \expect_{x \sim D}\brack{\sum_a \pi(a \mid x)\,\mu(x, a)},
$$

from $\Dcal$ alone, without deploying $\pi$ <Cite id="dudik2011" />.
</Definition>

## Why it is hard

A log answers questions about the policy that produced it. Its average reward estimates $V(\pi_0)$, not $V(\pi)$. For
rounds in which $\pi$ would have chosen a different arm, the log holds no reward for that arm: the outcome is
counterfactual. And the rounds in which $\pi$ agrees with $\pi_0$ are not a random sample of all rounds, because
$\pi_0$ chose its arms depending on the context.

Off-policy evaluation makes a log reusable. A team can compare many candidate policies offline and deploy only the
promising ones, instead of running an <NoteLink to="a-b-test">A/B test</NoteLink> for each. It needs the logging policy
to have been randomised, and its probabilities recorded, as described in
<NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities">logging policies and propensities</NoteLink>. The same estimators,
with clipping and the practice of obtaining propensities from a production recommender, are applied to recommenders in
<NoteLink to="counterfactual-evaluation-of-recommenders">counterfactual evaluation of recommenders</NoteLink>.

## Inverse propensity scoring

The **inverse propensity scoring** (IPS) estimator reweights each logged reward by how much more, or less, often the
target policy would have chosen the logged arm:

$$
\hat V_{\text{IPS}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i r_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{\pi(a_i \mid x_i)}{p_i}.
$$

It is the <NoteLink to="reweighting-biased-samples">Horvitz–Thompson estimator</NoteLink> of survey sampling applied to actions <Cite id="horvitz1952" />.

**Unbiasedness.** Assume **overlap**: $\pi_0(a \mid x) > 0$ whenever $\pi(a \mid x) > 0$. Condition on the context $x$
and average over the logged arm and reward. The arm $a$ is drawn from $\pi_0(\cdot \mid x)$ and the reward has mean
$\mu(x, a)$, so

$$
\expect\brack{\frac{\pi(a \mid x)}{\pi_0(a \mid x)}r \,\Big|\, x}
= \sum_a \pi_0(a \mid x)\,\frac{\pi(a \mid x)}{\pi_0(a \mid x)}\,\mu(x, a)
= \sum_a \pi(a \mid x)\,\mu(x, a).
$$

The logging probabilities cancel. Averaging over $x \sim D$ by the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total expectation</NoteLink> gives $\expect[w r] = V(\pi)$, and the mean
of $n$ independent terms has the same expectation. Without overlap, the sum over $a$ skips the arms that $\pi_0$ never
plays, and the estimator is biased by exactly the value $\pi$ gets from those arms.

**Variance.** The terms are independent, so $\var(\hat V_{\text{IPS}}) = \var(w r)/n$. For a deterministic target
policy the weight is $1/\pi_0(\pi(x) \mid x)$ when the logged arm matches and 0 otherwise, and the second moment is

$$
\expect[w^2r^2] = \expect_x\brack{\frac{\expect[r^2 \mid x, \pi(x)]}{\pi_0(\pi(x) \mid x)}}.
$$

It grows like the inverse of the logging probability of the target's arm. When the logging policy rarely does what the
target would do, a few rows with huge weights dominate the estimate.

## Self-normalised IPS

The weights have expectation 1: given $x$, $\expect[w \mid x] = \sum_a \pi_0(a \mid x)\,\pi(a \mid x)/\pi_0(a \mid x) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid x) = 1$.
In a finite sample their average $\bar w = \frac1n\sum_i w_i$ is not 1, and IPS inherits its fluctuations. The
**self-normalised** estimator divides by it <Cite id="swaminathan2015b" />:

$$
\hat V_{\text{SNIPS}} = \frac{\sum_i w_i r_i}{\sum_i w_i}.
$$

SNIPS is a weighted average of the observed rewards, so it always lies within their range; IPS can exceed the largest
possible reward. It is biased, by an amount of order $1/n$, because it is a ratio of two random sums, but it is
consistent, and its variance is usually much smaller. The average weight $\bar w$ is also a diagnostic: far from 1, it
signals too little overlap or wrong propensities.

## The direct method

The **direct method** (DM) fits a reward model $\hat\mu(x, a)$ on the log, by regression of $r_i$ on $(x_i, a_i)$, and
averages its predictions for the target's actions:

$$
\hat V_{\text{DM}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_a \pi(a \mid x_i)\,\hat\mu(x_i, a).
$$

It uses no propensities and has low variance. Its bias is the error of the model on the target's actions,
$\expect_x[\sum_a\pi(a \mid x)(\hat\mu(x, a) - \mu(x, a))]$. The model is fitted mostly on the logging policy's
actions, so it is least accurate exactly where the target differs from the log, and that bias does not shrink as $n$
grows.

## Doubly robust

The **doubly robust** (DR) estimator starts from the direct method and corrects it with the importance-weighted
residuals of the model <Cite id="dudik2011" />:

$$
\hat V_{\text{DR}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\brack{\sum_a \pi(a \mid x_i)\,\hat\mu(x_i, a) + w_i\paren{r_i - \hat\mu(x_i, a_i)}}.
$$

It is unbiased if either the propensities or the reward model is correct.

**Correct propensities, any model.** The correction term is IPS applied to the residual $r - \hat\mu(x, a)$. By the
same calculation as for IPS, its conditional expectation given $x$ is
$\sum_a\pi(a \mid x)(\mu(x, a) - \hat\mu(x, a))$. Adding the model term $\sum_a\pi(a \mid x)\hat\mu(x, a)$ leaves
$\sum_a\pi(a \mid x)\mu(x, a)$, whose expectation over $x$ is $V(\pi)$. The model's errors cancel.

**Correct model, any weights.** If $\hat\mu = \mu$, then given $x$ and $a$ the residual $r - \mu(x, a)$ has mean zero,
whatever weight multiplies it. The correction has expectation zero, and the model term is exactly the direct method
with a correct model, which is unbiased.

The variance of DR is that of IPS with the reward replaced by the residual. A good model makes the residuals small, and
DR's variance far below IPS's; a useless model, $\hat\mu = 0$, reduces DR to IPS.

## Worked example

A site shows one of two layouts, arm 1 or arm 2, to two types of user, A and B. The logging policy shows arm 1 to type
A users with probability 0.8 and to type B users with probability 0.5. The target policy shows arm 2 to type A and arm
1 to type B. Six rows were logged, with reward 1 for a click:

| row | type $x$ | arm $a$ | propensity $p$ | reward $r$ | target arm $\pi(x)$ | weight $w$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A | 1 | 0.8 | 1 | 2 | 0 |
| 2 | A | 2 | 0.2 | 1 | 2 | 5 |
| 3 | A | 1 | 0.8 | 0 | 2 | 0 |
| 4 | B | 1 | 0.5 | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| 5 | B | 2 | 0.5 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 6 | B | 1 | 0.5 | 0 | 1 | 2 |

The weight is $1/p$ where the logged arm matches the target's arm and 0 elsewhere.

- **IPS.** $\hat V_{\text{IPS}} = (5 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0)/6 = 7/6 = 1.167$. The estimate of a click rate
  exceeds 1, because row 2, a rare action that happened to succeed, carries weight 5.
- **SNIPS.** The weights sum to 9, so $\hat V_{\text{SNIPS}} = 7/9 = 0.778$. The average weight is $9/6 = 1.5$, far
  from its expectation of 1, which warns that six rows are too few.
- **Direct method.** Suppose a reward model, fitted on other data, predicts
  $\hat\mu(A, 1) = 0.5$, $\hat\mu(A, 2) = 0.6$, $\hat\mu(B, 1) = 0.5$ and $\hat\mu(B, 2) = 0.4$. The target plays
  arm 2 for the three type A rows and arm 1 for the three type B rows, so
  $\hat V_{\text{DM}} = (3 \cdot 0.6 + 3 \cdot 0.5)/6 = 0.55$.
- **Doubly robust.** The corrections $w_i(r_i - \hat\mu(x_i, a_i))$ are nonzero only on the matched rows:
  $5(1 - 0.6) = 2$ for row 2, $2(1 - 0.5) = 1$ for row 4 and $2(0 - 0.5) = -1$ for row 6. Their average is
  $2/6 = 0.333$, so $\hat V_{\text{DR}} = 0.55 + 0.333 = 0.883$.

The four estimates range from 0.55 to 1.17. The spread shows how the estimators treat the heavily weighted row 2: IPS
takes it at face value; SNIPS dilutes it by the total weight; DM ignores it; DR adds its importance-weighted residual
relative to the model. With six rows, none of the estimates is reliable.

## Variants

- **Replay.** With a uniformly random logging policy and a deterministic target, keep only the rows where the target
  agrees with the log and average their rewards. This is SNIPS with equal weights. Li et al. used it to evaluate news
  recommendation policies offline <Cite id="li2011" />.
- **Clipping.** Replace $w_i$ by $\min(w_i, M)$. The variance falls and a downward bias appears, of the size of the
  value the target gets from rows with weights above $M$ <Cite id="bottou2013" />.
- **<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">Confidence intervals</NoteLink>.** The terms of IPS and DR are independent, so a normal interval
  $\hat V \pm 1.96\,\hat s/\sqrt{n}$, with $\hat s$ the sample standard deviation of the terms, is standard. With heavy
  weights it undercovers; the <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss> $(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ should be checked first.

## Assumptions

- **Overlap.** Every action the target can take has positive logging probability in every context where the target
  takes it.
- **Correct propensities.** IPS, SNIPS and the propensity part of DR need the true logging probabilities, recorded at
  decision time.
- **No unrecorded confounders.** The logging policy's choice depends only on the recorded context. If it also used
  information not in $x$, the propensities computed from $x$ are wrong.
- **Stationarity.** The reward distribution when the target is deployed is the one seen in the log.

## Pitfalls

- **Deterministic logging.** A logging policy that always picks its favourite arm gives weight 0 or $\infty$. Nothing
  can be learned about other arms, whatever the estimator.
- **Reusing the log to fit the model.** Fitting $\hat\mu$ on the same rows used for the DR correction adds a small
  bias. Cross-fitting, where each row's residual uses a model fitted on the other rows, removes it.
- **Reporting IPS without diagnostics.** A single row with weight 1,000 can move the estimate by more than the
  differences between the policies being compared. Report the average weight, the effective sample size and the
  largest weights.
