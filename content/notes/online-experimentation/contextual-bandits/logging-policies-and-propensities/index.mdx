<Definition>
A **logging policy** $\pi_0$ is the rule that chose the action $a$ in each logged round, as a probability distribution
$\pi_0(\cdot \mid x)$ over the $K$ actions given the context $x$. The **propensity** of a logged round is
$p = \pi_0(a \mid x)$, the probability that the logging policy gave the action it actually took. A log has
**overlap** with a target policy $\pi$ if $\pi_0(a \mid x) > 0$ whenever $\pi(a \mid x) > 0$
<Cite id="bottou2013" />.
</Definition>

## Propensities in off-policy evaluation

Every log of a deployed system is shaped by the policy that produced it. A recommender that shows popular items gets
clicks on popular items, and the log says little about unpopular ones. To ask "what would another policy have
earned?", the analysis must undo that shaping. <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">Off-policy evaluation</NoteLink>
does so by weighting each logged reward by $w = \pi(a \mid x) / \pi_0(a \mid x)$. The weight needs the propensity,
and it exists only where the propensity is positive.

A logging policy that is useful for later analysis has three properties.

1. **Randomised.** Each action it might take has a known probability given the context. A deterministic policy has
   propensities of 0 and 1, and a log from it can evaluate only policies that agree with it everywhere.
2. **Overlapping.** The probabilities are positive for every action the policies to be evaluated might take.
3. **Recorded.** The propensity is written to the log at decision time, together with the context exactly as the
   policy saw it.

For a production recommender, where propensities come from sampled scores or impression counts, see
<NoteLink to="counterfactual-evaluation-of-recommenders">counterfactual evaluation of recommenders</NoteLink>.

## Common logging policies

- **ε-greedy.** Play the currently best action with probability $1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K$ and each other
  action with probability $\varepsilon/K$. The propensities are known in closed form and bounded below by
  $\varepsilon/K$. See <NoteLink to="epsilon-greedy">ε-greedy</NoteLink>.
- **Softmax.** Play action $a$ with probability $\exp(s_a/\tau) / \sum_b\exp(s_b/\tau)$ for model scores $s_a$ and a
  temperature $\tau > 0$. Propensities are in closed form but can be tiny for low-scoring actions; a floor, by mixing
  with the uniform distribution, keeps them bounded.
- **Uniform exploration bucket.** Serve a small random share of traffic, say 1%, with uniformly random actions. That
  share has overlap with every target policy: every propensity is $1/K$. Li et al. used such a bucket to evaluate news
  recommendation policies offline without bias <Cite id="li2011" />.
- **<Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss>.** The propensity of an action is the posterior
  probability that it is the best, which has no closed form. It must be estimated at decision time by drawing many
  posterior samples and counting how often each action wins. With $M$ draws, an action with true probability $10^{-3}$
  is seen about $M/1000$ times, so small propensities are estimated poorly unless $M$ is large.

## Variance from small propensities

An importance weight is large where the logging policy rarely did what the target does. Large weights make the
estimates noisy. Two quantities describe how much a log is worth for evaluating a given target.

**<Gloss name="ess">Effective sample size</Gloss>.** For a weighted average $\sum_i w_iy_i / \sum_i w_i$ of independent
values $y_i$ with variance $\sigma^2$, the variance is

$$
\var\paren{\frac{\sum_i w_iy_i}{\sum_i w_i}} = \frac{\sigma^2\sum_i w_i^2}{\paren{\sum_i w_i}^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n_{\text{eff}}},
\qquad n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\paren{\sum_i w_i}^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}.
$$

An unweighted average of $n_{\text{eff}}$ values would have the same variance. For large $n$, the weights have mean 1
and $n_{\text{eff}} \approx n / \expect[w^2]$.

**The second moment of the weights.** Take an ε-greedy logging policy with greedy action $g(x)$ and a deterministic
target $\pi$ that agrees with $g$ on a fraction $1 - \rho$ of contexts and picks another action on the rest. Where they
agree, the weight is $1/(1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K)$, earned with probability $1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K$.
Where they disagree, the weight is $K/\varepsilon$, earned with probability $\varepsilon/K$. Otherwise the weight is 0.
So

$$
\expect[w^2] = (1 - \rho)\cdot\frac{1}{1 - \varepsilon + \varepsilon/K} + \rho\cdot\frac{K}{\varepsilon}.
$$

The second term dominates for small $\varepsilon$. For rewards in $[0, 1]$, the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
error</NoteLink> of the <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">IPS estimate</NoteLink> from $n$ rows is at most
$\sqrt{\expect[w^2]/n}$.

**Example.** With $K = 4$ actions, $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and a target that disagrees on $\rho = 20\%$ of contexts, the
weights are $1/0.925 = 1.08$ on agreeing rows and $4/0.1 = 40$ on disagreeing ones. Then
$\expect[w^2] = 0.8/0.925 + 0.2 \cdot 40 = 8.86$, and 10,000 logged rows are worth about $10{,}000/8.86 = 1{,}128$ rows
of an on-policy experiment. Raising $\varepsilon$ to $0.5$ lowers $\expect[w^2]$ to $0.8/0.625 + 0.2 \cdot 8 = 2.88$,
worth about 3,470 rows, at the price of acting randomly half the time.

## What to log

- The context exactly as the policy saw it, including feature values computed at decision time.
- The action taken and its propensity. When cheap, the whole distribution $\pi_0(\cdot \mid x)$, which lets later
  analyses evaluate stochastic targets exactly.
- The version of the policy and its random seed, so that propensities can be recomputed or audited.
- The reward, with its timestamp, and whether it was observed at all. Missing rewards that depend on the action bias
  every estimator.

## Estimated propensities

When propensities were not logged, they are sometimes estimated from the log by regressing the chosen action on the
context, for example with <NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression">multinomial logistic regression</NoteLink>.
This is the propensity-score method of observational studies. It works only if the logging policy depended on nothing
beyond the recorded context. Errors in the estimated propensities bias IPS; the doubly robust estimator remains unbiased
if the reward model is correct <Cite id="dudik2011" />.

## Assumptions

- The logging policy's choice depends only on the recorded context and on randomness independent of the rewards.
- The logged propensities are the probabilities actually used. A system that logs the policy's intended probabilities
  but then applies business rules that override some actions logs the wrong numbers.

## Pitfalls

- **Rounding propensities.** Logging $p = 0$ for an action that had probability $10^{-6}$ makes its weight infinite.
  Store probabilities at full precision and set a floor in the policy itself.
- **Overlap only on average.** A policy that explores a lot overall can still never explore in some contexts, such as
  a segment always served by a rule. Estimates for targets that act differently in that segment are biased. Check
  overlap per segment, not only globally.
- **Clipping weights.** Capping weights at $M$ reduces variance but silently drops the value of the rows it
  clips <Cite id="bottou2013" />. Report how much weight was clipped.
