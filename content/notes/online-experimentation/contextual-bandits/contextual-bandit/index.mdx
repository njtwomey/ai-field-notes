<Definition>
A **contextual bandit** with $K$ arms is played over rounds $t = 1, \dots, T$. In each round a context $x_t \in \Xcal$
is drawn from a distribution $D$ and shown to the learner. The learner chooses an arm $a_t \in \set{1, \dots, K}$ and
observes a reward $r_t$ with mean $\mu(x_t, a_t)$, and nothing about the other arms. A **policy** is a map
$\pi : \Xcal \to \set{1, \dots, K}$, or a distribution over arms given $x$, and its **value** is
$V(\pi) = \expect_{x \sim D}[\mu(x, \pi(x))]$. For a class $\Pi$ of policies, the regret is
$R_T = T\max_{\pi \in \Pi}V(\pi) - \expect[\sum_t r_t]$ <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">multi-armed bandit</NoteLink> looks for one arm that is best on average. Often
the best arm depends on who is asking: the headline that one reader clicks is not the one another reader clicks. A
contextual bandit sees a description of the situation first, such as the user's features, the time or the device, and
learns which arm is best for each description.

The feedback is still bandit feedback: the learner sees the reward of the arm it chose, for the context it saw. It
does not learn what the other arms would have paid for this user. This separates a contextual bandit from supervised
learning, where the correct label is revealed, and from reinforcement learning, where actions also change future
contexts.

## What the context is worth

The best policy $\pi^*(x) = \argmax_a \mu(x, a)$ picks the best arm separately for each context. Its value is at least
that of the best single arm. For any arm $a$ and any context $x$, $\max_b\mu(x, b) \ge \mu(x, a)$. Taking expectations
over $x$ gives $\expect[\max_b\mu(x, b)] \ge \expect[\mu(x, a)]$ for every $a$, and so

$$
V(\pi^*) = \expect_x\brack{\max_a \mu(x, a)} \ge \max_a \expect_x[\mu(x, a)].
$$

The difference is lost in every round by a learner that ignores the context, however long it runs. The two sides are
equal exactly when one arm is best for almost every context.

**Example.** Take $x \sim \Unif(0, 1)$ and three arms with $\mu(x, 1) = 0.7 - 0.5x$, $\mu(x, 2) = 0.35 + 0.3x$ and
$\mu(x, 3) = 0.15 + 0.4x$. The averages are $0.45$, $0.5$ and $0.35$, so the best single arm is arm 2, with value
$0.5$. Arms 1 and 2 cross at $x = 0.4375$, and arm 3 is never the best. The best policy plays arm 1 below the crossing
and arm 2 above it, with value

$$
\int_0^{0.4375}(0.7 - 0.5x)\,dx + \int_{0.4375}^{1}(0.35 + 0.3x)\,dx = 0.5766.
$$

A context-free bandit forgoes $0.0766$ per round, 15% of the best single arm's value.

## Approaches

**One bandit per context.** With finitely many contexts, run a separate $K$-armed bandit for each. If context $x$
occurs $T_x$ times and each bandit has regret at most $c\sqrt{KT_x}$, the total regret is at most, by the
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

$$
\sum_{x \in \Xcal}c\sqrt{KT_x} \le c\sqrt{K}\sqrt{\abs{\Xcal}\sum_x T_x} = c\sqrt{K\abs{\Xcal}T}.
$$

The bound grows with the number of contexts, because nothing learned about one context transfers to another. With
continuous or high-dimensional contexts this is useless.

**Reward models.** Assume $\mu(x, a)$ belongs to a model class and share statistical strength across contexts. The
linear model $\mu(x, a) = \xvec_{a}\transpose\thetavec$, where $\xvec_a$ is a feature vector for the pair of context
and arm, gives <NoteLink to="linucb">LinUCB</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="linear-thompson-sampling">linear Thompson
sampling</NoteLink>, with regret of order $d\sqrt{T}$ up to logarithms in the feature dimension $d$, independent of
the number of contexts <Cite id="chu2011" />. Generalised linear models such as
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> and neural networks extend the same idea with
approximate confidence sets or posteriors.

**Policy classes.** Without a reward model, compete with the best policy in a class $\Pi$. EXP4 runs exponential weights
over the policies and has regret $O(\sqrt{KT\log\abs{\Pi}})$ even against adversarial rewards
<Cite id="auer2002b" />. It needs a weight per policy, so for large classes practical algorithms instead call a
supervised learner, trained on importance-weighted data as in
<NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive classification</NoteLink>, to find good policies.

**ε-greedy with a regression model.** Fit a model of $\mu(x, a)$ on the data so far, play its best arm, and explore
uniformly with probability $\varepsilon$. It has no strong guarantee but is simple, and its known exploration
probabilities make the logged data easy to reuse.

## News recommendation

Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire used a contextual bandit to choose which news article to feature on the Yahoo! front
page. The context was the user's features and the articles' features; the reward was a click. LinUCB raised the click
rate by 12.5% over a context-free bandit <Cite id="li2010" />. The same group showed how to evaluate such policies
offline without bias, by replaying logged data collected with uniformly random choices and keeping only the rounds where
the evaluated policy agrees with the log <Cite id="li2011" />. Logs and their reuse are the subject of
<NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities">logging policies</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink>.

## Where contextual bandits fit in recommendation

A full recommender picks from a catalogue of millions of items and learns mostly from logged data. A contextual bandit
suits a different class of personalisation problem: a small, well-defined set of actions, say 10 to 100 banners, layouts
or article slots, that must be chosen per user in real time, often for new users or new actions with little history. Its
performance depends on the context features. Too many or irrelevant features make the data too sparse to learn from
quickly, the curse of dimensionality in another form. When the choice also changes the user's future state, for example
by shaping their tastes over weeks, the problem is no longer a bandit; see
<NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-for-recommendation">reinforcement learning for recommendation</NoteLink>. A naive
bandit over items also tends to lock onto popular items; see
<NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- Contexts are drawn independently of the learner's past actions. If showing an arm changes who arrives next, the
  problem is reinforcement learning.
- The reward depends on the context and the chosen arm only. Interference between users, such as a recommendation that
  changes what others see, breaks this.
- Model-based methods assume the model class contains the true mean reward function, or nearly so. A misspecified
  linear model can have regret linear in $T$.

## Pitfalls

- **Features that depend on the action.** A context feature recorded after the choice, or computed from it, leaks the
  outcome of the choice into the policy and biases every estimate.
- **Unlogged probabilities.** The data a contextual bandit collects are shaped by its own choices. Without the
  probability of each chosen arm, later analysis cannot correct for this.
- **Too many features, too little data.** Exploration cost grows with the feature dimension. A linear bandit with
  1,000 sparse features explores for a long time before its confidence sets are useful.
