<Definition>
For a linear bandit with rewards $r = \xvec\transpose\thetavec^* + \eta$, **linear Thompson sampling** keeps
$\Vmat_t = \lambda\I + \sum_{s \le t}\xvec_s\xvec_s\transpose$, $\bvec_t = \sum_{s \le t}r_s\xvec_s$ and
$\hat\thetavec_t = \Vmat_t^{-1}\bvec_t$ over the pulled arms' features. In round $t + 1$ it draws

$$
\tilde\thetavec \sim \Gauss\paren{\hat\thetavec_t,\ v^2\Vmat_t^{-1}}
$$

and pulls the arm whose features $\xvec_{t+1,a}$ maximise $\xvec_{t+1,a}\transpose\tilde\thetavec$. The scale $v > 0$
sets how widely it explores <Cite id="agrawal2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</NoteLink> for independent arms draws a mean for each arm from its
posterior and pulls the largest. With a linear model the unknown is the single vector $\thetavec^*$, so one draw of
$\tilde\thetavec$ scores every arm at once. Arms that are best for many plausible parameters are pulled often; arms
that are best only for implausible parameters are pulled rarely.

The draw spreads along the directions where the posterior is wide, the directions that past pulls have not explored.
An arm pointing along such a direction sometimes gets a high score from a draw and is tried. Once its direction has
been measured, the posterior narrows there, and the arm wins only if it really is better.

## The Gaussian posterior

With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise the posterior is exact and has the ridge estimate as its mean. Take the prior
$\thetavec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, (\sigma^2/\lambda)\I)$ and noise $\eta \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. The log posterior is
the log prior plus the log likelihood, up to a constant in $\thetavec$:

$$
-\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\thetavec}^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{s \le t}(r_s - \xvec_s\transpose\thetavec)^2
= -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\paren{\thetavec\transpose\Vmat_t\thetavec - 2\thetavec\transpose\bvec_t} + \text{const}.
$$

Expanding the square gives $\thetavec\transpose(\sum_s\xvec_s\xvec_s\transpose)\thetavec - 2\thetavec\transpose\sum_s r_s\xvec_s$
plus terms without $\thetavec$, and adding the prior's $\lambda\thetavec\transpose\thetavec$ turns the first matrix into
$\Vmat_t$. Completing the square,
$\thetavec\transpose\Vmat_t\thetavec - 2\thetavec\transpose\bvec_t = (\thetavec - \Vmat_t^{-1}\bvec_t)\transpose\Vmat_t(\thetavec - \Vmat_t^{-1}\bvec_t) - \bvec_t\transpose\Vmat_t^{-1}\bvec_t$.
This is the log density of a multivariate Gaussian, so

$$
\thetavec \mid \text{data} \sim \Gauss\paren{\hat\thetavec_t,\ \sigma^2\Vmat_t^{-1}}.
$$

Linear Thompson sampling with $v = \sigma$ is exact posterior sampling under this model. Other values of $v$ widen or
narrow the posterior deliberately. See <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>.

## A randomised LinUCB

For a single arm with features $\xvec$, the sampled score is a linear function of a Gaussian vector, so it is Gaussian:

$$
\xvec\transpose\tilde\thetavec \sim \Gauss\paren{\xvec\transpose\hat\thetavec_t,\ v^2\norm{\xvec}^2_{\Vmat_t^{-1}}},
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
\xvec\transpose\tilde\thetavec = \xvec\transpose\hat\thetavec_t + v\norm{\xvec}_{\Vmat_t^{-1}}Z_{\xvec},
\quad Z_{\xvec} \sim \Gauss(0, 1).
$$

<NoteLink to="linucb">LinUCB</NoteLink> scores the same arm by $\xvec\transpose\hat\thetavec_t + \alpha\norm{\xvec}_{\Vmat_t^{-1}}$.
Linear Thompson sampling replaces the fixed multiplier $\alpha$ by a random one, $vZ_{\xvec}$. The exploration bonus
has the same shape, the width of the ellipsoid in the arm's direction, but it is sometimes negative, and it is
correlated across arms because all scores come from one draw $\tilde\thetavec$. Two arms pointing the same way get
similar random bonuses.

## Regret

Agrawal and Goyal proved a frequentist bound for linear Thompson sampling. With the posterior scale widened to
$v = \sigma\sqrt{c\,d\log(t/\delta)}$ for a constant $c$, the regret is $\tilde O(d^{3/2}\sqrt{T})$ with probability
$1 - \delta$, or $\tilde O(d\sqrt{T\log K})$ with $K$ arms per round <Cite id="agrawal2013" />. This is a factor
$\sqrt{d}$ worse than LinUCB's $\tilde O(d\sqrt{T})$ <Cite id="abbasi2011" />. The extra factor comes from the
widening: the proof needs each draw to be optimistic, above the true best score, with constant probability, and a
Gaussian in $d$ dimensions puts little mass in any one optimistic direction unless it is inflated by about $\sqrt{d}$.

The Bayesian regret, averaged over a Gaussian prior on $\thetavec^*$, of exact posterior sampling ($v = \sigma$) is
$\tilde O(d\sqrt{T})$, matching LinUCB <Cite id="russo2018" />. In practice $v = \sigma$ or smaller works well, and the
widened version explores too much.

## Computation

A draw needs a matrix square root of $\Vmat_t^{-1}$. With the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky
factor</NoteLink> $\Lmat$ of $\Vmat_t^{-1} = \Lmat\Lmat\transpose$, set $\tilde\thetavec = \hat\thetavec_t + v\Lmat\zvec$ with
$\zvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \I)$. A fresh factorisation costs $O(d^3)$ per round; a rank-one update of the factor
after each pull costs $O(d^2)$. Scoring $K$ arms then costs $O(Kd)$, less than LinUCB's $O(Kd^2)$, because the bonus
comes from the draw rather than from one quadratic form per arm.

## Beyond linear rewards

The same recipe applies to any model with an approximate posterior. For click-through rates, a logistic model
$\prob(r = 1 \mid \xvec) = \sigma(\xvec\transpose\thetavec)$ has no conjugate prior. A
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> replaces the posterior by a Gaussian centred at
the maximum a posteriori estimate, with precision given by the Hessian of the negative log posterior, and Thompson
sampling draws from that Gaussian <Cite id="russo2018" />. Neural reward models use a Gaussian over the last layer's
weights, bootstrapped ensembles or dropout as approximate posteriors.

## Assumptions

- Rewards are linear in the features with sub-Gaussian noise, as for LinUCB. The exact-posterior interpretation needs
  Gaussian noise and a Gaussian prior; the algorithm and its frequentist bound do not.
- The scale $v$ matches the noise level. A $v$ far below $\sigma$ makes the draws too confident and can lock onto a
  wrong arm.

## Pitfalls

- **Stale noise scale.** $v$ is often fixed at a guessed $\sigma$. If the reward noise is larger, for example revenue
  with heavy tails, the posterior is overconfident and exploration stops early.
- **Propensities are not free.** The probability that linear Thompson sampling picks arm $a$ for context $\xvec$ has no
  closed form. Off-policy evaluation of its logs needs these probabilities, estimated by repeated draws at logging
  time.
- **Correlated exploration.** Because one draw scores all arms, many similar arms are explored together or not at all.
  With near-duplicate arms this is efficient; with a misspecified model it can hide a good arm behind similar bad ones.
