<Definition>
Let $y = f(\xvec) + \varepsilon$ with $\expect[\varepsilon] = 0$ and $\var(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$, and let
$\hat f_{\Dcal}$ be a predictor learned from a random training set $\Dcal$. At a fixed input $\xvec$, the expected
squared error on a new target, averaged over training sets and noise, decomposes as

$$
\expect\brack{(y - \hat f_{\Dcal}(\xvec))^2} = \sigma^2 + \paren{f(\xvec) - \expect_{\Dcal}[\hat f_{\Dcal}(\xvec)]}^2 + \var_{\Dcal}\paren{\hat f_{\Dcal}(\xvec)}.
$$

The three terms are the **irreducible noise**, the **squared bias** and the **variance** <Cite id="geman1992" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A learning procedure maps a training set to a function. Train it on many independent training
sets of the same size and it produces many different functions. The **bias** is how far their average is from the
truth: a systematic error that more data of the same kind would not remove. The **variance** is how much they
scatter around their own average: an error caused by the particular sample. The noise $\sigma^2$ is the part of $y$
that no function of $\xvec$ can predict.

A rigid procedure, such as fitting a straight line, gives nearly the same answer on every training set, so its variance
is small. If the truth is curved, every one of those answers is wrong in the same way, so its bias is large. A
flexible procedure, such as a high-degree polynomial, can follow the curve, so its bias is small. It also follows the
noise, so its answers disagree from one training set to the next and its variance is large. The best test error sits
between the two extremes. Underfitting is the high-bias side of this trade-off; overfitting is the high-variance side.

## Derivation

Fix $\xvec$ and write $\hat f = \hat f_{\Dcal}(\xvec)$ and $\bar f = \expect_{\Dcal}[\hat f]$. The new target
$y = f(\xvec) + \varepsilon$ has noise $\varepsilon$ that is independent of $\Dcal$, because the test point is drawn
separately from the training set. Split the error into three pieces:

$$
y - \hat f = \varepsilon + (f(\xvec) - \bar f) + (\bar f - \hat f).
$$

Square and take the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over $\varepsilon$ and $\Dcal$. The square of each
piece gives

$$
\expect[\varepsilon^2] = \sigma^2, \qquad (f(\xvec) - \bar f)^2, \qquad \expect[(\bar f - \hat f)^2] = \var_{\Dcal}(\hat f).
$$

The middle term is a constant, so it passes through the expectation unchanged. Each cross term vanishes:

- $2\expect[\varepsilon](f(\xvec) - \bar f) = 0$, because $\expect[\varepsilon] = 0$.
- $2\expect[\varepsilon(\bar f - \hat f)] = 2\expect[\varepsilon]\,\expect[\bar f - \hat f] = 0$, because $\varepsilon$
  is independent of $\Dcal$.
- $2(f(\xvec) - \bar f)\,\expect[\bar f - \hat f] = 0$, because $\expect[\hat f] = \bar f$ by definition.

Adding the three squares gives the decomposition. Averaging it over the distribution of $\xvec$ gives the same split
of the overall expected test error, with integrated squared bias and integrated variance
<Cite id="hastie2009" />.

The same algebra with $\sigma^2 = 0$ is the familiar statement that the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> of
an estimator is its squared bias plus its variance (see <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">variance and
covariance</NoteLink>). Here the "estimator" is the whole fitted function, and the target it estimates is $f(\xvec)$.

<Derivation title="Variance of a least-squares fit on a fixed design">
Let the training inputs $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ be fixed and fit $\hat f(\xvec) = \phivec(\xvec)^\top\hat\wvec$ by
least squares on $p$ <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis functions</NoteLink>, with design matrix
$\Phimat \in \reals^{n \times p}$. Then $\hat\wvec = (\Phimat^\top\Phimat)^{-1}\Phimat^\top\yvec$, and since
$\cov(\yvec) = \sigma^2\eye$,

$$
\var \hat f(\xvec) = \sigma^2\,\phivec(\xvec)^\top(\Phimat^\top\Phimat)^{-1}\phivec(\xvec).
$$

At the training inputs, $\phivec(\xvec_i)^\top(\Phimat^\top\Phimat)^{-1}\phivec(\xvec_i)$ is the $i$-th diagonal entry
of the <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">hat matrix</NoteLink>
$\Hmat = \Phimat(\Phimat^\top\Phimat)^{-1}\Phimat^\top$. Its trace is
$\trace\Hmat = \trace\paren{(\Phimat^\top\Phimat)^{-1}\Phimat^\top\Phimat} = p$. So the variance averaged over the
training inputs is exactly

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \var \hat f(\xvec_i) = \frac{\sigma^2 p}{n}.
$$

Each extra coefficient adds $\sigma^2/n$ of variance, whatever the basis. The bias falls only while the extra basis
function captures structure that the others missed. The trace of $\Hmat$ is the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> of the fit.
</Derivation>

## Worked example: polynomial degree

Let $f(x) = e^{-x}\sin 5x$ on $n = 25$ equally spaced inputs in $[-1, 1]$, with noise $\sigma = 0.4$, so
$\sigma^2 = 0.16$. Fit polynomials of degree $d$ by least squares. Because the design is fixed and the fit is linear
in $\yvec$, the bias and variance can be computed exactly. Averaged over $x \in [-1, 1]$:

| Degree $d$ | Squared bias | Variance | Bias² + variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.937 | 0.012 | 0.950 |
| 3 | 0.189 | 0.023 | 0.212 |
| 6 | 0.0030 | 0.040 | 0.043 |
| 10 | $< 10^{-4}$ | 0.066 | 0.066 |
| 14 | $< 10^{-4}$ | 0.129 | 0.129 |

The squared bias falls by more than two orders of magnitude between degree 1 and degree 6, then stops mattering. The
variance grows steadily with $d$, at first by about $\sigma^2/n = 0.0064$ per coefficient and faster near degree 14,
where the fit swings at the ends of the interval. The sum is smallest at $d = 6$. The expected test error adds
$\sigma^2 = 0.16$ to every row.

## Controlling the trade-off

Every capacity control acts on this trade-off:

- **Model size.** Fewer basis functions, smaller trees and larger $k$ in
  <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours">k-nearest neighbours</NoteLink> lower variance and raise bias.
- **Penalties.** <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge regression</NoteLink> and other forms of
  <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> shrink the fit towards a simple default, buying a large cut
  in variance with a small increase in bias.
- **Averaging.** <NoteLink to="bagging">Bagging</NoteLink> averages predictors trained on
  <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> resamples. The average has roughly the same bias as each member and a
  lower variance.
- **More data.** Variance typically falls like $1/n$, while bias does not depend on $n$ for a fixed model. A larger
  training set therefore moves the optimum towards more flexible models.

The decomposition explains the trade-off but cannot choose the model on its own, because the bias depends on the
unknown $f$. In practice the complexity is chosen with <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or an
<NoteLink to="information-criteria">information criterion</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Squared loss.** The additive split holds exactly for squared error. For the 0–1 loss, bias and variance interact:
  variance can even reduce the error at points where the average prediction is on the wrong side of the boundary.
  Several incompatible definitions exist for classification <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
- **Independent test noise.** The cross terms vanish because the new target's noise is independent of the training
  set. This fails if test points overlap the training set.
- **A distribution over training sets.** Bias and variance are properties of the procedure over repeated sampling, not
  of one fitted model. They cannot be read off a single fit.

## Pitfalls

- **The U-shape is not a law.** The classical picture of test error falling and then rising with model size fails for
  heavily over-parameterised models. Past the point where the model interpolates the training data, test error can fall
  again. See <NoteLink to="double-descent">double descent</NoteLink>.
- **Training error measures neither term.** Training error keeps falling as complexity grows, even after the variance
  dominates the test error.
- **Parameter count is a poor proxy for complexity.** Penalised and averaged fits have far fewer effective degrees of
  freedom than parameters. Compare models by their variance or effective degrees of freedom, not by raw size.
