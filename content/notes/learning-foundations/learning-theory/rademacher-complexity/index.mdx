<Definition>
Let $\Fcal$ be a class of real-valued functions on $\Zcal$ and $S = (z_1, \dots, z_m)$ a sample. Let
$\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m$ be independent **Rademacher** variables, each $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac12$. The
**empirical Rademacher complexity** of $\Fcal$ on $S$ is

$$
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\Fcal) = \expect_{\sigmavec}\brack{\sup_{f \in \Fcal}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m \sigma_i f(z_i)},
$$

and the **Rademacher complexity** is its average over samples of size $m$ from a distribution $P$,
$\mathfrak{R}_m(\Fcal) = \expect_S[\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\Fcal)]$ <Cite id="bartlett2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Assign each training point a random label $\sigma_i$ by a fair coin. The sum $\frac1m\sum_i\sigma_i f(z_i)$ is large
when $f$ agrees with those random labels. The supremum picks the member of the class that fits the noise best, and
the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> averages over the <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flips</NoteLink>. A class that can match any pattern of signs has complexity near its
maximum; a class that cannot fit noise has complexity near zero.

This is the same question that makes the training error unreliable. If a class can fit random labels, then fitting
the real labels is no evidence that it has found structure. Deep networks can fit random labels on standard image
datasets, so their Rademacher complexity over the full parameter space is large <Cite id="zhang2017" />.

Unlike the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>, Rademacher complexity applies to real-valued
functions, depends on the data distribution, and can be estimated from the sample itself.

## The generalisation bound

Let $\Fcal$ be a class of <Gloss name="loss-function">loss functions</Gloss> $f(z) = \ell(h(\xvec), y)$ taking values in $[0, 1]$, one for each hypothesis
$h$. Write $\expect f$ for the risk and $\hat\expect_S f = \frac1m\sum_i f(z_i)$ for the <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk</NoteLink>. Then, with
probability at least $1 - \delta$, every $f \in \Fcal$ satisfies <Cite id="bartlett2002" />

$$
\expect f \le \hat\expect_S f + 2\mathfrak{R}_m(\Fcal) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

The proof has two steps.

**Step 1: concentration of the worst gap.** Let $\Phi(S) = \sup_{f \in \Fcal}(\expect f - \hat\expect_S f)$ be the
largest gap over the class. Replacing one point $z_i$ changes each $\hat\expect_S f$ by at most $1/m$, so it changes
$\Phi$ by at most $1/m$. McDiarmid's <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">bounded-differences inequality</NoteLink> then gives, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,
$\Phi(S) \le \expect_S[\Phi(S)] + \sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$.

**Step 2: symmetrisation.** Draw an independent ghost sample $S' = (z'_1, \dots, z'_m)$. Since
$\expect f = \expect_{S'}[\hat\expect_{S'} f]$, and the supremum of an expectation is at most the expectation of the
supremum,

$$
\expect_S[\Phi(S)] \le \expect_{S, S'}\brack{\sup_{f}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\paren{f(z'_i) - f(z_i)}}.
$$

Swapping $z_i$ with $z'_i$ leaves the joint distribution unchanged and flips the sign of the $i$-th term. So
multiplying each term by an independent random sign $\sigma_i$ does not change the expectation:

$$
\expect_S[\Phi(S)] \le \expect_{S, S', \sigmavec}\brack{\sup_{f}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sigma_i\paren{f(z'_i) - f(z_i)}}
\le 2\mathfrak{R}_m(\Fcal).
$$

The last step splits the supremum of a difference into two suprema, each of which is a Rademacher complexity because
$-\sigma_i$ has the same distribution as $\sigma_i$. Combining the two steps gives the bound. Applying McDiarmid once
more to $\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S$ gives a version that can be computed from the data: $2\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\Fcal) + 3\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$
in place of the last two terms <Cite id="mohri2018" />.

## Linear functions of bounded norm

Let $\Fcal = \set{\xvec \mapsto \wvec^\top\xvec : \norm{\wvec} \le B}$ and let every sample point satisfy
$\norm{\xvec_i} \le X$. For fixed signs, the supremum over the ball is attained at $\wvec$ parallel to
$\sum_i\sigma_i\xvec_i$ (Cauchy–Schwarz):

$$
\sup_{\norm{\wvec} \le B}\frac1m\wvec^\top\sum_i\sigma_i\xvec_i = \frac{B}{m}\norm{\sum_i\sigma_i\xvec_i}.
$$

By Jensen's inequality, $\expect\norm{\vvec} \le \sqrt{\expect\norm{\vvec}^2}$. Expanding the square, the cross terms
have $\expect[\sigma_i\sigma_j] = 0$ for $i \ne j$, so

$$
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\Fcal) \le \frac{B}{m}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^m\norm{\xvec_i}^2} \le \frac{BX}{\sqrt m}.
$$

The bound does not depend on the dimension of $\xvec$. It holds in an infinite-dimensional feature space, which is why
norm constraints, not parameter counts, control the capacity of <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">kernel methods</NoteLink> such as the
<NoteLink to="kernel-support-vector-machine">kernel SVM</NoteLink>.

### Two tools for other classes

- **Contraction.** If $\phi$ is $L$-Lipschitz, composing every function in the class with $\phi$ multiplies the
  Rademacher complexity by at most $L$ (Talagrand's lemma). A Lipschitz loss therefore inherits the bound of the
  predictors it scores <Cite id="mohri2018" />.
- **Finite classes.** Massart's lemma gives $\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\Fcal) \le r\sqrt{2\ln\abs{\Fcal}}/m$, where
  $r = \max_f\sqrt{\sum_i f(z_i)^2}$. Combined with the Sauer–Shelah lemma it gives
  $\mathfrak{R}_m \le \sqrt{2d\ln(em/d)/m}$ for a binary class of VC dimension $d$, recovering the VC bound up to
  constants <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.

## Worked example

**Checking the linear bound.** Draw $m = 100$ points uniformly on the unit sphere in $\reals^5$, so $X = 1$, and take
$B = 1$. The bound gives $\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S \le 1/\sqrt{100} = 0.1$. A Monte Carlo estimate over 20,000 draws of the
signs gives $\expect\norm{\sum_i\sigma_i\xvec_i}/m = 0.0956$. The only slack is Jensen's inequality.

**A margin bound.** Classify with $\sgn(\wvec^\top\xvec)$, $\norm{\wvec} \le 1$, $\norm{\xvec} \le 1$. Use the ramp
loss with margin $\gamma$: 1 for margin $y\wvec^\top\xvec \le 0$, 0 for margin at least $\gamma$, linear in between. It
is at least the 0–1 loss and $1/\gamma$-Lipschitz in the margin. Contraction and the linear bound give, for
$\gamma = 0.1$, $m = 10{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$, a gap of

$$
\frac{2}{\gamma\sqrt m} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln 20}{2m}} = 0.200 + 0.012 = 0.212
$$

between the test error and the fraction of training points with margin below $\gamma$. The bound depends on the
margin and the sample size, not on the dimension.

## Assumptions

- **Independent, identically distributed data**, as in all uniform-convergence bounds.
- **Bounded losses.** McDiarmid's inequality needs each point to have bounded influence. Unbounded losses such as the
  squared error on unbounded targets need truncation or other tools.

## Pitfalls

- **The class must be fixed in advance.** Bounding the complexity of the norm ball of the final weights, chosen after
  training, is not valid without a union bound over a family of balls.
- **The bound is on the whole class.** It holds uniformly, so it cannot exploit the particular solution an algorithm
  finds. Algorithm-dependent analyses (stability, compression, PAC-Bayes) address that.
