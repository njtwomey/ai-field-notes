<Definition>
Let $\Xcal$ be a finite input set, let the target $f: \Xcal \to \set{0, 1}$ be unknown, and let a learner see the
labels of a training set $D \subset \Xcal$. The **off-training-set error** of the learner's prediction $\hat f$ is its
error rate on the points of $\Xcal \setminus D$. The **no free lunch theorem** states that, averaged uniformly over all
$2^{\abs{\Xcal}}$ target functions, every learner has the same expected off-training-set error, equal to $\frac12$
<Cite id="wolpert1996" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The training labels say what the target does on the training points. They say nothing about any other point unless
the learner assumes a link between points: that nearby inputs have similar labels, that the target is linear, that
it is simple. Averaged over every possible target, each such assumption is right exactly as often as it is wrong. A
learner that does well on smooth targets must do badly on rough ones, by the same amount.

The theorem does not say that learning is impossible. It says that every successful learner carries an
**inductive bias**, an assumption about which targets are likely, and that its success depends on the assumption
matching the world.

## Derivation

Let the target $f$ be uniform over all functions from $\Xcal$ to $\set{0, 1}$. Equivalently, the labels $f(\xvec)$ of the
different points are independent fair <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flips</NoteLink>.

Fix a training set $D$ and a point $\xvec \notin D$. The learner's prediction $\hat f(\xvec)$ depends only on $D$ and on
the training labels $\set{f(\xvec') : \xvec' \in D}$. The label $f(\xvec)$ is independent of those training labels,
because the labels of distinct points are independent. So, conditional on everything the learner saw, $f(\xvec)$ is
still a fair coin, and

$$
\prob\paren{\hat f(\xvec) \ne f(\xvec) \mid D, f|_D} = \tfrac12,
$$

whatever rule produced $\hat f(\xvec)$, deterministic or random. Averaging over the points of $\Xcal \setminus D$ gives an
expected off-training-set error of $\frac12$ for every learner. The difference in expected off-training-set error
between any two learners is therefore zero <Cite id="wolpert1996" />.

The same argument works for any loss and any number of classes: under a uniform prior on targets, off-training-set
labels are independent of training labels, so the expected loss of a prediction does not depend on how it was
made.

## A version for a single distribution

Averaging over all targets can look like an artificial worst case. A sharper statement shows that every learner fails
badly on some specific task that another learner solves perfectly.

**Theorem.** Let $A$ be any learner for binary classification over a domain $\Xcal$, and let $m < \abs{\Xcal}/2$. There
is a distribution $P$ over $\Xcal \times \set{0, 1}$ such that some function $f$ has zero risk under $P$, yet with
probability at least $\frac17$ over a sample of size $m$, $A$ returns a predictor with risk at least $\frac18$
<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.

The proof chooses $P$ uniform on $2m$ points and considers all $2^{2m}$ labellings of them. For each labelling, the
learner sees at most half the points. Averaged over the labellings, its error on the unseen half is $\frac12$, so its
risk averages at least $\frac14$, and some labelling makes it that bad for this learner.

A consequence is that the class of all functions on an infinite domain is not PAC learnable. Restricting the
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">hypothesis class</NoteLink>, as in <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC learning</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>, is exactly the prior knowledge the theorem says is needed.

## Worked example

Let $\Xcal$ have 8 points and let the learner see the labels of 3 of them. There are $2^5 = 32$ targets consistent
with those labels, one for each labelling of the 5 unseen points. For each unseen point, exactly 16 of the 32 targets
label it 1.

- A learner that predicts the majority training label everywhere is right on a given unseen point for 16 of the 32
  targets.
- A learner that predicts the minority training label is also right for 16 of the 32.

Both have expected off-training-set error $\frac12$. Neither can be preferred without saying which of the 32 targets
are more plausible.

## Assumptions

- **A uniform prior over targets.** The equality of learners holds on average over all targets. Real targets are not
  drawn uniformly: images, text and physical measurements have strong regularities. Learners that exploit them can,
  and do, beat chance on those targets.
- **Off-training-set error.** When test inputs can repeat training inputs, memorising the training set helps, and
  learners differ. The theorem is about generalisation to genuinely new points.

## Pitfalls

- **"No algorithm is better than another" is a misreading.** Algorithms differ greatly on the problems that occur in
  practice. The theorem says only that the difference comes from assumptions about the problem, not from the
  algorithm alone.
- **Benchmarks carry an implicit prior.** A method that wins on a benchmark suite wins on targets like those in the
  suite. Its advantage transfers only to problems that share their structure.
