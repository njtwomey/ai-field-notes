<Definition>
Let a model with $k$ free parameters be fitted by maximum likelihood to $n$ observations, with maximised
log-likelihood $\ell(\hat\thetavec)$. The **Akaike** and **Bayesian** information criteria are

$$
\AIC = -2\ell(\hat\thetavec) + 2k, \qquad \BIC = -2\ell(\hat\thetavec) + k\ln n,
$$

and the small-sample **corrected AIC** is $\operatorname{AICc} = \AIC + \dfrac{2k(k + 1)}{n - k - 1}$. Among candidate
models fitted to the same data, the one with the smallest criterion is preferred <Cite id="akaike1974" />
<Cite id="schwarz1978" /> <Cite id="hurvich1989" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The maximised log-likelihood always improves when parameters are added, because a larger model contains the smaller
one. It measures fit to the data at hand, not the quality of predictions for new data. Each criterion adds a penalty
that grows with $k$, and the two main criteria answer different questions.

- **AIC** asks which model will predict new data best, measured by the log-likelihood it assigns to them. Its penalty
  corrects the optimism of the in-sample log-likelihood.
- **BIC** asks which model is most probable given the data, under a Bayesian view with equal prior odds. Its penalty
  comes from integrating over the parameters rather than maximising.

## AIC from the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence

Let the data come from a true density $g$ and let the model be $f(y \mid \thetavec)$. A good model for prediction has
small <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>
$\KL(g \,\|\, f_{\hat\thetavec}) = \expect_g[\log g(Y)] - \expect_g[\log f(Y \mid \hat\thetavec)]$. The first term is the
same for every model, so comparing models means comparing the **expected predictive log-likelihood**
$Q(\hat\thetavec) = n\,\expect_g[\log f(Y \mid \hat\thetavec)]$, where $Y$ is a new observation independent of the data.
The natural estimate is the maximised log-likelihood $\ell(\hat\thetavec)$. It is biased upward, because
$\hat\thetavec$ was chosen to make it large. The bias is about $k$.

Let $\thetavec_0$ maximise $\expect_g[\log f(Y \mid \thetavec)]$, the best parameter in the model, and let
$\Jmat = -\expect_g[\nabla^2\log f(Y \mid \thetavec_0)]$ be the per-observation curvature there. Split the bias into
three parts:

$$
\ell(\hat\thetavec) - Q(\hat\thetavec) = \underbrace{[\ell(\hat\thetavec) - \ell(\thetavec_0)]}_{(a)} + \underbrace{[\ell(\thetavec_0) - Q(\thetavec_0)]}_{(b)} + \underbrace{[Q(\thetavec_0) - Q(\hat\thetavec)]}_{(c)}.
$$

- **(b)** has expectation zero: at the fixed $\thetavec_0$, the sample log-likelihood is an unbiased estimate of its
  expectation.
- **(a)**: expand $\ell(\thetavec_0)$ in a <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> around its maximum
  $\hat\thetavec$, where the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is zero. For large $n$ the Hessian is close to $-n\Jmat$, so
  $(a) \approx \frac{n}{2}(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)^\top\Jmat(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)$.
- **(c)**: expand $Q(\hat\thetavec)$ around its maximum $\thetavec_0$, where its gradient is zero and its Hessian is
  $-n\Jmat$. The same <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> appears: $(c) \approx \frac{n}{2}(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)^\top\Jmat(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)$.

When the model contains $g$, the maximum-likelihood estimate is asymptotically Gaussian with covariance
$\Jmat^{-1}/n$. Then $\sqrt{n}\,\Jmat^{1/2}(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)$ is approximately standard Gaussian in $k$
dimensions, and $n(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)^\top\Jmat(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)$ is approximately $\ChiSq_k$ with
mean $k$. So (a) and (c) each have expectation about $k/2$, and

$$
\expect[\ell(\hat\thetavec) - Q(\hat\thetavec)] \approx k.
$$

$\ell(\hat\thetavec) - k$ is therefore an approximately unbiased estimate of the expected predictive log-likelihood.
Multiplying by $-2$ gives AIC <Cite id="akaike1974" />. When the model is misspecified, the covariance of
$\hat\thetavec$ is $\Jmat^{-1}\Kmat\Jmat^{-1}/n$, with $\Kmat$ the variance of the score, and the penalty becomes
$2\trace(\Kmat\Jmat^{-1})$: the Takeuchi information criterion <Cite id="burnham2002" />.

AIC and leave-one-out <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> of the log-likelihood estimate the
same quantity, and they are asymptotically equivalent <Cite id="stone1977aic" />.

## BIC from the marginal likelihood

In Bayesian model comparison with equal prior probabilities, the preferred model has the largest **<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal
likelihood</NoteLink>** $p(\Dcal \mid M) = \int p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)\,p(\thetavec)\,d\thetavec$. For large $n$ the integrand
is sharply peaked near $\hat\thetavec$. The <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> fits a
Gaussian at the peak: with $\Amat = -\nabla^2\ell(\hat\thetavec) \approx n\hat\Jmat$,

$$
p(\Dcal \mid M) \approx p(\Dcal \mid \hat\thetavec)\,p(\hat\thetavec)\,(2\pi)^{k/2}\abs{\Amat}^{-1/2}.
$$

Since $\abs{n\hat\Jmat} = n^k\abs{\hat\Jmat}$,

$$
\ln p(\Dcal \mid M) \approx \ell(\hat\thetavec) - \frac{k}{2}\ln n + \underbrace{\ln p(\hat\thetavec) + \frac{k}{2}\ln 2\pi - \frac12\ln\abs{\hat\Jmat}}_{O(1)}.
$$

The bracketed terms do not grow with $n$. Dropping them and multiplying by $-2$ gives BIC <Cite id="schwarz1978" />.
Because the dropped terms are $O(1)$, the approximation to the log marginal
likelihood has an error that does not vanish, although it becomes negligible relative to the terms that grow with $n$.
Differences in BIC approximate $-2$ times log <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factors</NoteLink>, so $e^{-\Delta\BIC/2}$ approximates the posterior odds
of two models <Cite id="kass1995" />.

## AICc for small samples

For <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> with Gaussian errors, Hurvich and Tsai computed the bias of the log-likelihood exactly and
obtained the extra term $2k(k + 1)/(n - k - 1)$ <Cite id="hurvich1989" />. It vanishes as $n \to \infty$ but is large
when $k$ is a sizeable fraction of $n$, where plain AIC chooses models that are too big. A common rule is to use AICc
whenever $n/k < 40$ <Cite id="burnham2002" />.

## Worked example: polynomial degree

Fit polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 3 to $n = 30$ points by least squares, with Gaussian errors. The parameters are
the $d + 1$ coefficients of a polynomial of degree $d$ and the noise variance, so $k = d + 2$.
With residual sum of squares $\operatorname{RSS}$, the
maximised log-likelihood is $\ell = -\frac{n}{2}\paren{\ln(2\pi\operatorname{RSS}/n) + 1}$. Suppose the residual sums
of squares are 12.4, 6.1 and 5.9.

| Degree | $k$ | $\ell$ | AIC | AICc | BIC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 | $-29.32$ | 64.63 | 65.55 | 68.84 |
| 2 | 4 | $-18.68$ | 45.35 | 46.95 | 50.95 |
| 3 | 5 | $-18.17$ | 46.35 | 48.85 | 53.36 |

All three criteria choose degree 2. The cubic improves the log-likelihood by 0.50, less than the 1 unit that AIC
charges per parameter and the $\frac12\ln 30 = 1.70$ that BIC charges. The margin grows from AIC (1.0) to AICc (1.9) to
BIC (2.4).

## Assumptions

- **Maximum-likelihood fits on the same data.** Criteria are compared only across models fitted to exactly the same
  observations and the same response. A log-transformed response changes the likelihood scale.
- **Large samples and regular models.** Both derivations use Taylor expansions around a unique interior optimum.
  Mixture models, change points and other models with boundary or unidentified parameters violate this.
- **A count of free parameters.** Penalised and smoothed fits need the
  <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> in place of $k$.

## Pitfalls

- **AIC and BIC answer different questions.** BIC's penalty exceeds AIC's once $\ln n > 2$, that is $n \ge 8$. BIC is
  consistent: if one candidate is the true model, BIC picks it with probability tending to 1. AIC is efficient for
  prediction and tends to keep extra parameters that improve predictions slightly. Neither is uniformly better.
- **Only differences matter.** An AIC value on its own has no meaning. Differences below about 2 are weak evidence
  <Cite id="burnham2002" />.
- **Criteria do not account for the search.** Comparing thousands of models by AIC, as in <NoteLink to="feature-selection">stepwise selection</NoteLink>,
  overfits in the same way as any other selection over many candidates.
