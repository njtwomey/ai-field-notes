<Definition>
**Feature scaling** applies a per-feature affine map $x_j \mapsto (x_j - a_j)/b_j$ with constants estimated from the
training data. **Standardisation** uses the training mean and standard deviation, giving features with mean 0 and
variance 1. **Min–max scaling** uses the minimum and the range, mapping the training values onto $[0, 1]$. **Robust
scaling** uses the median and the interquartile range. The same constants are then applied to validation, test and
production data.
</Definition>

## Problem

Raw features come in arbitrary units. Income in dollars varies over tens of thousands, age in years over tens, and a
ratio over fractions of one. Many learning methods treat all coordinates of $\xvec$ alike. For them, a change of units,
which carries no information, changes the fitted model, the speed of training, or both.

## Mechanism

### Gradient descent and conditioning

For least squares with centred features, the loss $L(\wvec) = \frac{1}{2n}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ has constant
Hessian $\Hmat = \frac1n\Xmat^\top\Xmat$, the covariance matrix of the features. Its diagonal entries are the feature
variances. Measuring feature $j$ in units $c$ times smaller multiplies column $j$ of $\Xmat$ by $c$, and so multiplies
row $j$ and column $j$ of $\Hmat$ by $c$ and the entry $H_{jj}$ by $c^2$.

Gradient descent with step size $\eta$ updates $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta\Hmat(\wvec - \wvec^*)$, where $\wvec^*$ is
the minimum. In the eigenbasis of $\Hmat$, the error along the eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda_k$ is multiplied by
$1 - \eta\lambda_k$ at every step. The iteration is stable only if $\abs{1 - \eta\lambda_k} < 1$ for all $k$, which
requires $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$. The error along the flattest direction is then multiplied by
$1 - \eta\lambda_{\min} > 1 - 2\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}$ per step, so it can never fall faster than that. With
the common choice $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$ the factor is $1 - 1/\kappa$, where $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink>.
Reducing the error by a factor $\epsilon$ takes about $\kappa\ln(1/\epsilon)$ steps.

Two uncorrelated features with standard deviations 1 and $s$ give $\Hmat = \diag(1, s^2)$ and $\kappa = s^2$. A ratio of
100 in units is a factor of 10,000 in conditioning. Standardising makes every diagonal entry 1. For uncorrelated
features that makes $\Hmat = \eye$ and $\kappa = 1$. Correlated features keep a condition number of
$(1 + \rho)/(1 - \rho)$ for two features with correlation $\rho$; only whitening, such as rotating onto principal
components and rescaling, removes that. The same reasoning applies to neural networks, where normalised inputs are a
standard recommendation <Cite id="lecun1998" /> and <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink>
extends it to hidden layers.

### Distances

<NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours">k-nearest neighbours</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> and
kernel methods with a Gaussian kernel depend on Euclidean distances $\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2 = \sum_j (x_j - x'_j)^2$. Each
feature contributes in proportion to its squared units. With age in years (standard deviation 15) and income in dollars
(standard deviation 20,000), a typical squared income difference exceeds a typical squared age difference by a factor
of about $(20{,}000/15)^2 \approx 1.8$ million. Neighbours are then chosen by income alone. After standardising, each
feature contributes about equally on average.

### Penalties

A penalty on the coefficient vector, as in <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink>, depends on the units of each feature. If $x_j$ is replaced by $c\,x_j$, the same
predictions are obtained with coefficient $w_j/c$. The ridge penalty on that coefficient becomes $\lambda w_j^2/c^2$, so
rescaling a feature by $c$ is the same as penalising it with strength $\lambda/c^2$. A feature in small units, with
large coefficients, is penalised heavily; a feature in large units is nearly free. Standardising first makes one
$\lambda$ mean the same for every feature <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

### Methods that do not need scaling

- **Trees and tree ensembles** split on thresholds of one feature at a time. Any increasing transformation of a feature
  leaves the possible partitions unchanged.
- **Unpenalised least squares and logistic regression**, solved exactly, give the same predictions in any units: the
  coefficients absorb the scale. Scaling still helps their iterative solvers converge.
- **Gaussian naive Bayes and LDA** estimate a mean and variance per feature, which rescale with the feature.

## Worked example

The figure uses two features, with the loss minimised by gradient descent at step size $1/\lambda_{\max}$ until the loss
falls by a factor of $10^6$, from the same start.

| Setting | $\kappa$ | Steps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uncorrelated, scale ratio $s = 5$ | 25 | 140 |
| Uncorrelated, $s = 10$ | 100 | 493 |
| Uncorrelated, standardised | 1 | 1 |
| Correlation $\rho = 0.5$, standardised | 3 | 17 |
| Correlation $\rho = 0.9$, standardised | 19 | 109 |

Doubling the scale ratio multiplies $\kappa$ by 4 and the number of steps by about 3.5. Standardising uncorrelated
features removes the problem entirely. Strong correlation leaves a condition number of 19 even after standardising.

## Cost

Standardisation and min–max scaling cost one pass over the training data to compute two numbers per feature, and one
multiply-add per feature per prediction. The constants must be stored with the model.

## Trade-offs

- **Standardisation** keeps outliers as outliers and does not bound the range. It is the default for linear models,
  kernels and neural networks.
- **Min–max scaling** bounds the training values but is set by the two most extreme values, so one outlier compresses
  every other value. Test values can fall outside $[0, 1]$.
- **Robust scaling** by the median and interquartile range resists outliers. For heavy-tailed features a log or
  quantile transform before scaling often helps more than any affine map.
- **Sparse data.** Centring turns zeros into nonzeros and destroys sparsity. For sparse inputs, scale by the maximum
  absolute value or the standard deviation without centring <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Fitting the scaler on all the data.** Means and standard deviations computed on the full dataset, including test
  rows, leak information about the test set. Fit the scaler inside each training fold. See
  <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>.
- **Forgetting to apply the same constants at prediction time.** Recomputing the constants on a batch of new data
  changes what every feature means.
- **Scaling one-hot or binary features.** Standardising a rare binary feature gives it a large value when it is 1, which
  increases its effective weight under a penalty. Whether that is wanted is a modelling choice, not a default.
