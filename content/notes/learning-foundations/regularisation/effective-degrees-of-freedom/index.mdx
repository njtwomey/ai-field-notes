<Definition>
Let $y_i = f(\xvec_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with independent noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and fixed inputs, and let
$\hat y_i$ be the fitted value at $\xvec_i$. The **effective degrees of freedom** of the fitting procedure are

$$
\operatorname{df} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \cov(\hat y_i, y_i).
$$

For a **linear smoother**, whose fitted values are $\hat\yvec = \Smat\yvec$ for a matrix $\Smat$ that does not depend
on $\yvec$, this equals $\trace\Smat$ <Cite id="efron1986" /> <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Complexity measured by covariance

<Gloss name="ols">Ordinary least squares</Gloss> with $p$ coefficients uses $p$ degrees of freedom. Penalised, smoothed
and averaged fits do not have a meaningful parameter count: a ridge fit has $p$ coefficients but all are shrunk, and a
<NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing spline</NoteLink> has a coefficient per data point. The definition above
measures complexity by behaviour instead. A flexible fit follows its own targets closely, so each $\hat y_i$ covaries
strongly with $y_i$. A rigid fit barely responds to any single target, so the covariances are small. The sum of
covariances, in units of the noise variance, counts how many targets the fit is effectively reproducing.

## Linear smoothers: the trace

If $\hat\yvec = \Smat\yvec$ then $\hat y_i = \sum_j S_{ij}y_j$. The targets are independent with variance $\sigma^2$,
so only the $j = i$ term covaries with $y_i$:

$$
\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \sum_j S_{ij}\cov(y_j, y_i) = S_{ii}\sigma^2.
$$

Summing over $i$ and dividing by $\sigma^2$ gives $\operatorname{df} = \sum_i S_{ii} = \trace\Smat$. The diagonal
entry $S_{ii}$ is the weight a point's own target receives in its fitted value.

- **Least squares** with a full-rank $n \times p$ design $\Xmat$ has
  $\Smat = \Hmat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$. By the cyclic property of the trace,
  $\trace\Hmat = \trace\paren{(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Xmat} = \trace\eye_p = p$. The definition recovers the
  parameter count.
- **$k$-nearest-neighbour regression** at a training point averages the targets of its $k$ nearest training points,
  which include the point itself. So $S_{ii} = 1/k$ and $\operatorname{df} = n/k$.


## Ridge regression through the SVD

The ridge fit is $\hat\yvec = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$. Write the
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> $\Xmat = \Umat\Dmat\Vmat^\top$, with
$\Umat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ and $\Vmat \in \reals^{p \times p}$ having orthonormal columns and
$\Dmat = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_p)$. Since $\Vmat^\top\Vmat = \Vmat\Vmat^\top = \eye$,

$$
\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye = \Vmat(\Dmat^2 + \lambda\eye)\Vmat^\top,
\qquad
(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)^{-1} = \Vmat(\Dmat^2 + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\Vmat^\top.
$$

Substituting,

$$
\Smat_\lambda = \Umat\Dmat\Vmat^\top\Vmat(\Dmat^2 + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\Vmat^\top\Vmat\Dmat\Umat^\top
= \Umat\,\diag\paren{\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}}\Umat^\top.
$$

The trace of $\Umat\Amat\Umat^\top$ with $\Umat^\top\Umat = \eye$ is $\trace\Amat$, so

$$
\operatorname{df}(\lambda) = \sum_{j=1}^p \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}.
$$

Each direction of the data contributes between 0 and 1. A direction with a large singular value, where the data
determine the coefficient well, counts almost fully. A direction with a small singular value counts little. The total
falls smoothly from $p$ at $\lambda = 0$ to 0 as $\lambda \to \infty$.

## Degrees of freedom and the optimism of training error

The training error of a fit is lower than its error on new targets at the same inputs. The gap is set by the degrees
of freedom. Let $y_i^{\text{new}}$ be a fresh target at $\xvec_i$, independent of the training targets. Then

$$
\expect\brack{\frac1n\sum_i (y_i^{\text{new}} - \hat y_i)^2} - \expect\brack{\frac1n\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}
= \frac{2}{n}\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \frac{2\sigma^2}{n}\operatorname{df}.
$$

To see this, expand both squares around $f_i = f(\xvec_i)$. For the new target,
$\expect(y_i^{\text{new}} - \hat y_i)^2 = \sigma^2 + \expect(f_i - \hat y_i)^2$, because the new noise is independent of
$\hat y_i$. For the training target,
$\expect(y_i - \hat y_i)^2 = \sigma^2 + \expect(f_i - \hat y_i)^2 - 2\expect[\varepsilon_i(\hat y_i - f_i)]$, and
$\expect[\varepsilon_i(\hat y_i - f_i)] = \cov(y_i, \hat y_i)$ because $f_i$ is fixed. The difference is
$2\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$ per point <Cite id="efron1986" />.

This gives **Mallows' $C_p$**, an estimate of the in-sample prediction error <Cite id="mallows1973" />:

$$
C_p = \frac1n\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 + \frac{2\hat\sigma^2}{n}\operatorname{df}.
$$

For Gaussian noise with known variance this is equivalent to <NoteLink to="information-criteria">AIC</NoteLink>.
Generalised cross-validation uses $\trace\Smat$ in the same role
(see <NoteLink to="additive-model-smoothing-parameter-selection">GAM smoothing parameter selection</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

**Ridge.** Let $\Xmat$ have singular values $(3, 1, 0.3)$ and take $\lambda = 1$. The three directions contribute
$9/10 = 0.900$, $1/2 = 0.500$ and $0.09/1.09 = 0.083$, so $\operatorname{df} = 1.483$. The fit has three coefficients
but behaves like one with about one and a half. The weakest direction is almost entirely suppressed.

**$C_p$.** Suppose $n = 50$, the training <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> is $0.80$,
$\operatorname{df} = 6$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = 1$. Then $C_p = 0.80 + 2 \times 6/50 = 1.04$. A rival fit with training
error $0.70$ and $\operatorname{df} = 12$ has $C_p = 0.70 + 0.48 = 1.18$ and is predicted to do worse on new targets.

**$k$-NN.** With $n = 100$ points and $k = 5$, $\operatorname{df} = 20$. Halving $k$ doubles the degrees of freedom.

## Assumptions

- **Fixed inputs, independent homoscedastic noise.** The covariance definition and the optimism identity treat
  $\xvec_i$ as fixed and the $\varepsilon_i$ as independent with a common variance.
- **Linearity for the trace formula.** $\operatorname{df} = \trace\Smat$ needs $\Smat$ not to depend on $\yvec$. For
  adaptive procedures the covariance definition still applies, but the answer can differ from any count. For the lasso,
  the number of nonzero coefficients is an unbiased estimate of the degrees of freedom <Cite id="zou2007" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Selection adds degrees of freedom.** Choosing the best 5 of 100 features and then fitting least squares uses more
  than 5 degrees of freedom, because the choice was made using $\yvec$. Counting only the final coefficients makes
  $C_p$ and AIC too optimistic.
- **$\sigma^2$ must come from elsewhere.** Estimating $\sigma^2$ from the same flexible fit understates it. Use a
  low-bias model or replicate measurements.
