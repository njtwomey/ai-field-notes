<Definition>
Let $\cvec(u) = (x(u), y(u))$ be a plane curve with $\cvec' \ne 0$, arc length $s$ and unit tangent
$\tvec = \cvec'/\norm{\cvec'}$. Its **signed curvature** $\kappa$ is defined by $d\tvec/ds = \kappa\,\nvec$, where
$\nvec$ is $\tvec$ rotated by $+90°$. It equals

$$
\kappa = \frac{x'y'' - y'x''}{\paren{x'^2 + y'^2}^{3/2}}
$$

in any regular parameterisation. $\abs{\kappa}$ is the reciprocal of the radius of the **osculating circle**, the
circle that best fits the curve at the point <Cite id="farin2002" />.
</Definition>

## Deriving the formula

Write $\sigma = ds/du = \norm{\cvec'}$ for the speed, so that $\cvec' = \sigma\,\tvec$. Differentiate with respect to
$u$ and use the chain rule $d\tvec/du = \sigma\,d\tvec/ds = \sigma\kappa\,\nvec$:

$$
\cvec'' = \sigma'\,\tvec + \sigma^2\kappa\,\nvec.
$$

The second derivative splits into a part along the tangent, which changes the speed, and a part along the normal,
which bends the path. Take the two-dimensional cross product $\avec \times \bvec = a_1b_2 - a_2b_1$ with $\cvec'$.
Since $\tvec \times \tvec = 0$ and $\tvec \times \nvec = 1$,

$$
\cvec' \times \cvec'' = \sigma\,\tvec \times (\sigma'\,\tvec + \sigma^2\kappa\,\nvec) = \sigma^3\kappa,
\qquad \kappa = \frac{x'y'' - y'x''}{\sigma^3}.
$$

The formula does not depend on the parameterisation. Reparameterising multiplies $\sigma$ by $\phi'$ and adds a
tangential term to $\cvec''$, which the cross product ignores. For a graph $y = f(x)$, take $u = x$:
$x' = 1$, $x'' = 0$, and

$$
\kappa = \frac{f''}{\paren{1 + f'^2}^{3/2}}.
$$

For small slopes $\kappa \approx f''$. That is the link between geometric curvature and the $\int f''^2$ penalties of
<NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">interpolating</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">smoothing</NoteLink> splines.

**The osculating circle.** The circle with centre $\cvec + \nvec/\kappa$ and radius $1/\abs{\kappa}$ passes through
the point with the same tangent and the same curvature. It agrees with the curve to second order, which no other
circle does.

**Worked example.** For the circle $\cvec(u) = (R\cos u, R\sin u)$, $x'y'' - y'x'' = R^2\sin^2u + R^2\cos^2u = R^2$
and $\sigma = R$, so $\kappa = 1/R$. For the parabola $y = x^2$, $f' = 2x$ and $f'' = 2$: $\kappa(0) = 2$ and
$\kappa(1) = 2/5^{3/2} = 0.179$. The osculating circle at the vertex has radius $1/2$ and centre $(0, 1/2)$.

## Curvature combs

A **curvature comb** draws, at many points along the curve, a line segment along the normal with length proportional
to $\kappa$. The tips form a second curve. The comb shows at a glance what the eye struggles to see in the curve
itself:

- **Jumps** in the comb are discontinuities of curvature: the curve is $G^1$ but not $G^2$ there. Cubic Hermite and
  PCHIP curves jump at every data point; cubic B-splines with simple knots do not.
- **Sign changes** are inflection points, where the curve changes from turning left to turning right.
- **Bumps** in the comb envelope are places where curvature rises and falls again. Each one is a place a reflection
  on a glossy surface will visibly wobble.

## Fairness

A curve is **fair** if it looks smooth to a trained eye. The working definitions in design translate the eye's
judgement into curvature <Cite id="farin1989" />:

- the curvature plot is continuous ($G^2$),
- it consists of as few monotone pieces as possible, with no unintended bumps,
- the curvature is small where the designer intends the curve to be nearly straight.

**Energy.** A thin elastic rod that passes through fixed points takes the shape that minimises its bending energy

$$
E = \int \kappa^2\,ds.
$$

Its minimisers are Euler's **elastica**. The problem is non-linear, because both $\kappa$ and $ds$ depend on the
curve, and it needs the <NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation">Euler–Lagrange equation</NoteLink> for a solution
<Cite id="levien2009" />. For a graph with small slope, $\kappa \approx f''$ and $ds \approx dx$, so
$E \approx \int f''^2\,dx$. The natural cubic spline minimises that linearised energy exactly, by Holladay's theorem
<Cite id="holladay1957" />, which is why it approximates a draughtsman's elastic spline when the slopes are small. For
steep or closed curves it does not, and fairing methods minimise $\int\kappa^2\,ds$ or $\int (d\kappa/ds)^2\,ds$
directly. The second energy favours curves whose curvature changes linearly, such as clothoids, used for road and
rail transitions <Cite id="levien2009" /> <Cite id="moreton1992" />.

**Fairing** improves a given spline. Common methods remove a knot and reinsert it where the comb shows a bump, move
the single control point that most reduces a fairness energy, or add an energy term to a least-squares fit. Each step
checks the curvature plot.

## Pitfalls

- **Parametric continuity is not fairness.** A $C^2$ curve in its parameter can still have an unfair comb with
  bumps, and a $G^2$ curve with a poor parameterisation can be perfectly fair.
- **Curvature amplifies noise.** $\kappa$ involves second derivatives. A control point moved by a small amount can
  change the comb a lot, so fairing works on the comb, not on positions.
- **Scaling.** Curvature has units of inverse length. Scaling a shape by $a$ divides $\kappa$ by $a$ and $E$ by $a$,
  so compare energies only at the same scale.
