<Definition>
In machine learning a spline is a **learnable function of one variable**, $\phi(x) = \sum_{i} c_i B_i(x)$, whose
coefficients $c_i$ are fitted to data by least squares, maximum likelihood or <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. The fixed B-spline
basis $B_i$ on a grid of knots makes $\phi$ smooth, local and linear in its parameters <Cite id="unser1999" />.
Models differ in where they put these functions and in the constraints and penalties on the coefficients.
</Definition>

## Why splines suit learning

Three properties of <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-splines</NoteLink> carry over directly.

- **Linear in the parameters.** With the knots fixed, $\phi$ is a linear model in the coefficients. Least squares has
  a closed form, <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> are the basis values, and penalties on coefficients are quadratic.
- **Local.** For B-splines of degree $k$, a data point at $x$ affects only the $k + 1$ coefficients whose basis functions are non-zero at $x$. Each
  gradient step changes the function only near the data that produced it, and the design matrix is banded.
- **Shape constraints are linear.** A B-spline function is non-decreasing if its coefficients are, because
  $\phi' = \sum_i k\,(c_i - c_{i-1})/(t_{i+k} - t_i)\,B_{i,k-1}$ and the $B_{i,k-1}$ are non-negative. Monotonicity
  and convexity become inequality constraints on coefficient differences.

## Generalised additive models

A <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive model</NoteLink> writes the linear predictor as a sum
of smooth functions of single features, $g(\expect[y]) = \beta_0 + \sum_j f_j(x_j)$. Each $f_j$ is a
<NoteLink to="p-splines">P-spline</NoteLink> or another penalised spline <Cite id="eilers1996" />
<Cite id="wood2017" />. The additive structure keeps the model interpretable: every $f_j$ is a curve that can be
plotted. The <NoteLink to="smoothing-and-penalised-splines">smoothing note</NoteLink> covers the penalties, and
<NoteLink to="shape-constrained-additive-models">shape-constrained generalised additive models</NoteLink> the monotone and convex variants.

## Kolmogorov–Arnold networks

The Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem states that every continuous $f : [0, 1]^n \to \reals$ can be written
as

$$
f(\xvec) = \sum_{q=1}^{2n+1}\Phi_q\paren{\sum_{p=1}^n \phi_{q,p}(x_p)}
$$

with continuous functions of one variable $\Phi_q$ and $\phi_{q,p}$. Addition is the only multivariate operation.
**Kolmogorov–Arnold networks** (KANs) turn the theorem's two-layer form into a deep architecture
<Cite id="liu2024kan" />. A KAN layer with $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs and $n_{\text{out}}$ outputs has a learnable
function on every edge, and each output sums its incoming edges:

$$
x_{l+1,j} = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}}\phi_{l,j,i}(x_{l,i}), \qquad
\phi(x) = w_b\,\operatorname{silu}(x) + w_s\sum_{i=0}^{G+k-1}c_i\,B_i(x).
$$

Here $B_i$ are the B-splines of degree $k$ (usually 3) on a uniform grid of $G$ intervals, extended by $k$ knots
beyond each end, and $\operatorname{silu}(x) = x/(1 + e^{-x})$ is a fixed residual term. Where a <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptron</Gloss>
has learnable weights on edges and fixed <NoteLink to="activation-functions">activations</NoteLink> on nodes, a KAN
has learnable activations on edges and plain sums on nodes.

**Grid extension.** A trained KAN can be refined by fitting each edge's spline on a finer grid, by least squares on
samples of the coarse spline, and continuing training. Each refinement adds capacity without discarding what was
learned <Cite id="liu2024kan" />.

**Worked example.** A KAN with widths $[2, 5, 1]$, grid $G = 5$ and cubic splines has $2 \times 5 + 5 \times 1 = 15$
edges. Each edge has $G + k = 8$ spline coefficients and two scalars $w_b, w_s$, so the network has
$15 \times 10 = 150$ parameters. A multilayer perceptron with the same widths has $2 \times 5 + 5 + 5 \times 1 + 1 = 21$. KANs spend
parameters on flexible one-dimensional functions, which can be plotted and in some cases replaced by symbolic
formulas; they are slower per parameter to evaluate than <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix multiplications</NoteLink>.

## Spline activation functions

A network can keep weights on edges and still learn its activations. **Deep spline networks** give each neuron a
learnable linear spline activation. A total-variation penalty on its second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> favours activations with few
knots, and the optimal activations under this penalty are provably linear splines <Cite id="bohra2020" />. A <Gloss name="relu" /> is
the special case of one knot. The <NoteLink to="universal-approximation-theorem">universal approximation
theorem</NoteLink> holds for any non-polynomial activation, so the gain is in efficiency and <NoteLink to="interpretability">interpretability</NoteLink> rather
than expressiveness.

## Calibration maps

A classifier's scores can be mapped to calibrated probabilities by a monotone one-dimensional function. <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">Isotonic
regression</NoteLink> gives a step function (<NoteLink to="isotonic-calibration">isotonic calibration</NoteLink>). A spline
gives a smooth map with few parameters: Lucena fits a spline to the log-odds by penalised logistic regression
<Cite id="lucena2018" />, and Gupta and co-authors fit a cubic spline to the cumulative distribution of scores and
recover calibrated probabilities from its derivative <Cite id="gupta2021" />.

## Monotone splines and flows

**I-splines** are integrals of normalised B-splines (M-splines). Each is monotone, so any non-negative combination is
monotone. Ramsay used them for monotone regression <Cite id="ramsay1988" />. The same idea powers **neural spline
flows**: a <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flow</NoteLink> needs invertible one-dimensional transforms with
cheap Jacobians, and a monotone rational-quadratic spline with bins and derivatives output by a network is
invertible in closed form <Cite id="durkan2019" />. Monotonicity in both uses comes from the
<NoteLink to="monotone-and-shape-preserving-interpolation">Hermite slope conditions</NoteLink> or from non-negative
coefficient differences.

## Quantile smoothing splines

Replacing squared error by the check loss $\rho_\tau(r) = r(\tau - \indicator[r < 0])$ turns a smoothing spline into
an estimate of the conditional $\tau$-quantile. With an $L^1$ penalty on the total variation of $f'$, the solution is
a linear spline, computable by <Gloss name="lp">linear programming</Gloss> <Cite id="koenker1994" />. Asymmetric squared losses give expectile
splines (<NoteLink to="expectile-generalised-additive-models">expectile generalised additive models</NoteLink>).

## Interpolation inside networks

Splines also appear as fixed layers. Image resampling in spatial transformers and many upsampling layers use linear
or cubic interpolation kernels. <NoteLink to="n-hits">N-HiTS</NoteLink> forecasts a few coefficients per block and
interpolates them to the full horizon.

## Pitfalls

- **Grid range.** B-splines are zero outside their knots. Inputs that drift beyond the grid fall on the silu term
  alone in a KAN, or on nothing in a plain spline layer. Normalise inputs or update the grid.
- **Too many knots without a penalty.** An unpenalised spline with many coefficients overfits, exactly as a
  <NoteLink to="regression-splines">regression spline</NoteLink> with too many knots does.
- **Evaluation cost.** Each spline evaluation finds a knot span and runs a short recursion; on <Gloss name="gpu" plural /> this is slower
  than a dense matrix product with the same number of parameters.
