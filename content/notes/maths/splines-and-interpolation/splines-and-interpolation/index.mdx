<Definition>
A **spline** of degree $p$ is a function or curve made of polynomial pieces of degree at most $p$ that meet at
parameter values called **knots**, with a prescribed number of continuous <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> at each knot. An
**interpolating** spline passes through given data points. An **approximating** spline is steered by control points
that it does not in general pass through. Splines replace one polynomial of high degree, which oscillates, by many
polynomials of low degree, which do not <Cite id="deboor1978" />.
</Definition>

## Why piecewise polynomials

One polynomial through $n + 1$ points has degree $n$. Its coefficients depend on every data value, and on equally
spaced points its error grows without bound near the ends as $n$ grows. This is
<NoteLink to="polynomial-interpolation-and-runge">Runge's phenomenon</NoteLink>. A spline keeps the degree fixed,
usually at 3, and adds pieces instead. Each piece is cheap to evaluate, the error falls like a power of the knot
spacing, and the smoothness conditions at the knots make the joins invisible to the eye.

## The spline families

"Local" means that moving one point changes the curve only on
a bounded stretch of parameter. "Global" means it changes the curve everywhere, however slightly. Continuity is
parametric continuity $C^k$ at the joins for the usual (cubic) member of the family.

| Family                                                                                          | Continuity      | Control      | Passes through points  | Typical use                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- | ------------ | ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Piecewise linear                                                                                | $C^0$           | local        | yes                    | tables, plotting, look-up                          |
| <NoteLink to="polynomial-interpolation-and-runge">Interpolating polynomial</NoteLink>           | $C^\infty$      | global       | yes                    | <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">quadrature rules</NoteLink>, spectral methods                 |
| <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">Cubic interpolating spline</NoteLink>                 | $C^2$           | global       | yes                    | tabulated functions, resampling, ship lofting      |
| <NoteLink to="hermite-and-catmull-rom-splines">Hermite and Catmull–Rom</NoteLink>               | $C^1$           | local        | yes                    | animation paths, camera motion, keyframes          |
| <NoteLink to="monotone-and-shape-preserving-interpolation">PCHIP and Akima</NoteLink>           | $C^1$           | local        | yes                    | monotone data, <Gloss name="cdf" plural />, physical tables               |
| <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-spline</NoteLink>                                                    | $C^{p-m}$       | local        | no (ends only if open) | CAD, fonts, regression bases, Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (KANs) |
| <NoteLink to="bezier-curves">Bézier</NoteLink>                                                  | $C^\infty$      | global       | end points only        | fonts, vector graphics, UI easing curves           |
| <NoteLink to="non-uniform-rational-b-splines">NURBS</NoteLink>                                                           | $C^{p-m}$       | local        | no                     | CAD exchange formats, exact conics, car bodies     |
| <NoteLink to="smoothing-and-penalised-splines">Smoothing spline and P-spline</NoteLink>         | $C^2$           | global       | no                     | noisy data, <Gloss name="gam" /> smooths                            |

For B-splines and NURBS, $p$ is the degree and $m$ the multiplicity of a knot. A cubic B-spline with simple knots is
$C^2$. The <NoteLink to="piecewise-polynomials-and-continuity">note on continuity</NoteLink> counts the degrees of
freedom behind each entry and separates parametric continuity from geometric continuity.

Interpolants of data $y = f(x)$ (linear, polynomial, cubic, PCHIP, Akima,
smoothing) treat the points as data sorted by $x$. Curves in the plane (Catmull–Rom, B-spline, Bézier, NURBS) treat
them as an ordered control polygon. Every curve in the playground agrees with scipy's `scipy.interpolate` to about $10^{-13}$,
and Catmull–Rom curves agree to the same accuracy with an independent implementation of the Barry–Goldman pyramid.

## Lineage

- **1901.** Runge shows that interpolating polynomials on equally spaced points can diverge
  <Cite id="runge1901" />.
- **1946.** Schoenberg names splines, after the flexible strips of wood that draughtsmen bent through pegs, and
  introduces the B-spline as a basis for them <Cite id="schoenberg1946" />. Curry and Schoenberg prove that
  B-splines span every spline space with given knots <Cite id="curry1966" />.
- **1957–1967.** Holladay proves that the natural cubic spline is the smoothest interpolant
  <Cite id="holladay1957" />. Reinsch turns the same variational idea into the smoothing spline for noisy data
  <Cite id="reinsch1967" />.
- **1959–1966.** De Casteljau at Citroën and Bézier at Renault develop polynomial curves steered by control points, in
  the Bernstein basis <Cite id="farin2002" /> <Cite id="farouki2012" />.
- **1972.** Cox and de Boor publish the stable recursion that evaluates B-splines <Cite id="cox1972bspline" />
  <Cite id="deboor1972" />. De Boor's book follows in 1978 <Cite id="deboor1978" />.
- **1974.** Catmull and Rom introduce local interpolating splines for computer animation
  <Cite id="catmull1974" />. Chaikin's corner cutting turns out to generate B-splines by subdivision
  <Cite id="chaikin1974" />.
- **1980.** Fritsch and Carlson give conditions for monotone cubic interpolation <Cite id="fritsch1980" />, and Boehm
  gives knot insertion <Cite id="boehm1980" />.
- **1980s–1990s.** Rational B-splines (NURBS) become the standard representation in CAD because they represent conics
  exactly <Cite id="piegl1997" />.
- **1996.** Eilers and Marx combine a rich B-spline basis with a difference penalty: P-splines
  <Cite id="eilers1996" />.
- **2024.** Kolmogorov–Arnold networks place a learnable B-spline on every edge of a neural network
  <Cite id="liu2024kan" />. See <NoteLink to="splines-in-machine-learning">splines in machine learning</NoteLink>.

## Choosing a spline

- **Data to pass through exactly, smooth result.** A <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">cubic
  spline</NoteLink>. Use not-a-knot ends when nothing is known about the ends.
- **Data that must stay monotone or within its range.** PCHIP.
- **A path through keyframes, edited one point at a time.** A centripetal Catmull–Rom spline.
- **A shape designed by hand.** A B-spline or Bézier curve; a NURBS curve when arcs of circles must be exact.
- **Noisy data.** A smoothing spline or a P-spline, not an interpolant.
