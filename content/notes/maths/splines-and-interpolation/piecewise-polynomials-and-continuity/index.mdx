<Definition>
A **piecewise polynomial** of degree $p$ with breakpoints $\xi_0 < \xi_1 < \dots < \xi_m$ is a function whose
restriction to each interval $[\xi_{i-1}, \xi_i]$ is a polynomial of degree at most $p$. It is **$C^k$ at $\xi_i$** if
its value and first $k$ derivatives agree from both sides. A <NoteLink to="splines-for-curves-and-surfaces">parametric curve</NoteLink> $\cvec$ with regular pieces ($\cvec' \ne \zeros$ at the join) is **$G^k$** (geometrically continuous of
order $k$) at the join if some regular reparameterisation of one piece makes it $C^k$ there: $G^1$ means a common unit
tangent, $G^2$ a common unit tangent and curvature vector <Cite id="barsky1989" />.
</Definition>

## Counting degrees of freedom

Each of the $m$ pieces has $p + 1$ polynomial coefficients, $m(p + 1)$ in total. Requiring $C^k$ at the $m - 1$
interior breakpoints imposes $k + 1$ linear equations at each: equal value, first derivative, ..., $k$-th derivative.
These equations are independent. On the interval to the right of $\xi_i$, write the piece as the piece on the left
plus a correction $\sum_{r=0}^p c_r (x - \xi_i)^r$. $C^k$ at $\xi_i$ says exactly $c_0 = \dots = c_k = 0$, which
leaves $p - k$ free coefficients per join. So the space of <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">piecewise polynomials</NoteLink> of degree $p$ that are $C^k$ at every
interior breakpoint has dimension

$$
(p + 1) + (m - 1)(p - k).
$$

The first term is the free polynomial on the first interval. Two special cases are splines and Hermite cubics.

- **Splines**, $k = p - 1$: dimension $p + 1 + (m - 1) = m + p$. A cubic spline with $K = m - 1$ interior knots has
  $K + 4$ degrees of freedom. The corrections are multiples of $(x - \xi_i)^p$, which is the truncated power basis of
  <NoteLink to="regression-splines">regression splines</NoteLink>.
- **Hermite cubics**, $p = 3$, $k = 1$: dimension $4 + 2(m - 1) = 2(m + 1)$, one value and one slope per breakpoint.
  This is the space used by <NoteLink to="hermite-and-catmull-rom-splines">Hermite</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="monotone-and-shape-preserving-interpolation">PCHIP</NoteLink> interpolation.

If the smoothness differs from knot to knot, say $C^{k_i}$ at $\xi_i$, the dimension is
$(p + 1) + \sum_i (p - k_i)$. In the language of <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-splines</NoteLink>, a knot repeated $r$
times allows smoothness $C^{p-r}$ there and contributes $r$ to the dimension <Cite id="curry1966" />.

**Worked example.** Cubic pieces on four intervals have $4 \times 4 = 16$ coefficients. $C^2$ at the three interior
breakpoints imposes $3 \times 3 = 9$ conditions, leaving $16 - 9 = 7 = 3 + 4$. Interpolating five values leaves two
free parameters, which the end conditions of a <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">cubic spline</NoteLink> fix.
If the middle breakpoint is only required to be $C^1$ (a double knot), the dimension rises to 8.

## Parametric curves: two kinds of continuity

A plane curve $\cvec(u) = (x(u), y(u))$ is built from pieces $\cvec_1$ on $[u_0, u_1]$ and $\cvec_2$ on
$[u_1, u_2]$. Parametric continuity $C^k$ compares derivatives with respect to $u$. That is a statement about the
parameterisation as much as about the shape. A curve is a set of points traced in some order. Tracing the second
piece twice as fast doubles $\cvec_2'$ without changing a single point.

**$C^1$ without $G^1$.** $\cvec(u) = (u^3, u^2)$ is a polynomial, so it is $C^\infty$ in $u$. At $u = 0$ its
derivative $(3u^2, 2u)$ is zero, and the curve has a cusp: both branches, $u < 0$ and $u > 0$, reach the origin from
above, tangent to the $y$-axis. A zero derivative lets a smooth parameterisation draw a corner.

**$G^1$ without $C^1$.** Let $\cvec_1(u) = (u, u^2)$ on $[0, 1]$, ending at $(1, 1)$ with $\cvec_1'(1) = (1, 2)$. Let
$\cvec_2(u) = (1 + 2(u - 1), 1 + 4(u - 1))$ on $[1, 2]$, a straight line leaving $(1, 1)$ with
$\cvec_2'(1) = (2, 4)$. The tangent directions agree and the magnitudes differ by a factor 2. The join is $G^1$ and not
$C^1$. Reparameterising $\cvec_2$ at half speed makes it $C^1$.

## Deriving the $G^k$ conditions

Let $u = \phi(v)$ be a regular reparameterisation of $\cvec_2$, with $\phi' > 0$. By the chain rule,

$$
\frac{d}{dv}\cvec_2(\phi(v)) = \phi'\,\cvec_2', \qquad
\frac{d^2}{dv^2}\cvec_2(\phi(v)) = \phi'^2\,\cvec_2'' + \phi''\,\cvec_2'.
$$

$C^1$ after reparameterisation means $\phi'\,\cvec_2' = \cvec_1'$ for some $\phi' > 0$: the derivatives are parallel
and point the same way. With $\beta_1 = 1/\phi'$,

$$
G^1: \quad \cvec_2' = \beta_1\,\cvec_1', \quad \beta_1 > 0.
$$

$C^2$ after reparameterisation adds $\phi'^2\,\cvec_2'' + \phi''\,\cvec_2' = \cvec_1''$. Solving for $\cvec_2''$ and
writing $\beta_2$ for the free multiple of the tangent that $\phi''$ introduces,

$$
G^2: \quad \cvec_2' = \beta_1\,\cvec_1', \qquad \cvec_2'' = \beta_1^2\,\cvec_1'' + \beta_2\,\cvec_1'.
$$

These are the **beta-constraints** <Cite id="barsky1989" />. The term $\beta_2\,\cvec_1'$ lies along the tangent, so
it changes the speed and not the bending. The curvature is
$\kappa = (x'y'' - y'x'')/\norm{\cvec'}^3$ (derived in <NoteLink to="curvature-and-fairness">curvature and
fairness</NoteLink>). Its numerator is the cross product $\cvec' \times \cvec''$. Under the beta-constraints,
$\cvec_2' \times \cvec_2'' = \beta_1^3\,\cvec_1' \times \cvec_1''$, because $\cvec_1' \times \cvec_1' = 0$, and
$\norm{\cvec_2'}^3 = \beta_1^3\norm{\cvec_1'}^3$. The factors cancel, so both pieces have the same curvature at the
join. Conversely, equal unit tangents and equal curvature let one solve for $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$. $G^2$ is exactly
equal unit tangent and equal curvature at the join.

## Consequences of each order of continuity

- **$C^0$ / $G^0$.** The pieces meet. Corners are allowed.
- **$G^1$.** No corners. Light reflected from a surface with a $G^1$ but not $G^2$ seam shows a visible kink in the
  highlight, because the reflection direction depends on the normal, and the normal's <NoteLink to="derivative">rate of change</NoteLink> jumps.
- **$G^2$.** Curvature is continuous. A car driving along a $G^2$ road turns its steering wheel continuously. Designers
  of car bodies ask for $G^2$ or $G^3$ <Cite id="farin2002" />.
- **$C^2$ functions.** For a graph $y = f(x)$ the parameter is $x$ itself, so $C^k$ and $G^k$ coincide for smooth
  pieces. A cubic spline is $C^2$: its second derivative is continuous and its third jumps at each knot.

## Pitfalls

- **$C^1$ in the parameter is not smoothness of the shape.** A zero derivative permits a cusp. Check $\cvec' \ne 0$.
- **Matching magnitudes is not required for a smooth look.** $G^1$ joins look smooth; forcing $C^1$ in a design tool
  constrains the designer for no visual gain.
