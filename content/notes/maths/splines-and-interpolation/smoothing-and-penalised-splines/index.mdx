<Definition>
A **smoothing spline** with parameter $\lambda \ge 0$ is the minimiser over twice-differentiable $f$ of

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \paren{y_i - f(x_i)}^2 + \lambda\int f''(x)^2\,dx.
$$

The minimiser is a natural cubic spline with knots at the data <Cite id="reinsch1967" />. A **penalised spline** is
the same idea on a fixed B-spline basis, with a quadratic penalty on the coefficients. Both trade closeness to the data
against roughness, with $\lambda$ setting the exchange rate <Cite id="green1994" />.
</Definition>

## From interpolation to fitting

An interpolating spline, such as the <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">cubic spline</NoteLink>, passes
through every data point. When the $y_i$ carry measurement noise, passing through them reproduces the noise, and the
curve wiggles at the scale of the data spacing. There are three standard ways to stop interpolating.

1. **Fewer basis functions.** A <NoteLink to="regression-splines">regression spline</NoteLink> uses $K$ knots with
   $K$ much smaller than $n$, and fits the $K + 4$ B-spline coefficients by least squares. The number and placement of
   knots control smoothness.
2. **A roughness penalty on a function.** A <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing spline</NoteLink> keeps a knot
   at every data point and adds $\lambda\int f''^2$. There is no knot choice; $\lambda$ controls smoothness.
3. **A roughness penalty on coefficients.** A <NoteLink to="p-splines">P-spline</NoteLink> uses a generous B-spline
   basis, say 20 to 40 functions, and penalises squared second differences of neighbouring coefficients,
   $\lambda\sum_j (\theta_j - 2\theta_{j-1} + \theta_{j-2})^2$ <Cite id="eilers1996" />. Because B-splines are local
   and sum to one, differences of coefficients approximate derivatives of the curve.

All three are linear smoothers: the fitted values are $\hat\yvec = \Smat_\lambda\yvec$ for a matrix
$\Smat_\lambda$ that does not depend on $\yvec$. The <NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">penalty
note</NoteLink> works out the matrices.

## Why the minimiser is a natural cubic spline

Let $g$ be any candidate and $s$ the natural cubic spline that interpolates $g$ at the distinct $x_i$. The residual
sum of squares depends only on the values at the $x_i$, so it is the same for $g$ and $s$. By Holladay's theorem,
proved in the <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">cubic spline note</NoteLink>, $\int s''^2 \le \int g''^2$ with
equality only if $g = s$. So replacing $g$ by $s$ never increases the objective. The search over all functions reduces
to a search over the $n$ values of a natural spline, a finite-dimensional ridge problem solved by Reinsch's algorithm
in $O(n)$ <Cite id="reinsch1967" />.

## The $\lambda$ continuum

**As $\lambda \to 0$** the penalty vanishes, and the objective is minimised by any interpolant. Among interpolants the
limit picks the one with least $\int f''^2$, the natural cubic interpolating spline.

**As $\lambda \to \infty$** any curvature is infinitely costly, so the limit must have $\int f''^2 = 0$, that is
$f'' = 0$: a straight line. Among straight lines the penalty is zero, and the residual sum of squares is minimised by
the ordinary least-squares line. The limit is the <NoteLink to="linear-regression">least-squares line</NoteLink>.

The lines are exactly the functions the penalty does not see, its **null space**. Between the two ends, the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> $\trace\Smat_\lambda$ fall
continuously from $n$ to 2.

**Worked example.** Take $x = 0, 1, \dots, 5$ and $y = 1, 2.2, 1.8, 3.5, 3.1, 4.6$. scipy's
`make_smoothing_spline(x, y, lam)` gives the fitted values

| $\lambda$     | fitted values at $x = 0, \dots, 5$           | $\trace\Smat_\lambda$ |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| $10^{-8}$     | 1.000, 2.200, 1.800, 3.500, 3.100, 4.600     | 6.00                  |
| $10^{-2}$     | 1.029, 2.102, 1.951, 3.344, 3.207, 4.567     | 5.59                  |
| $1$           | 1.137, 1.768, 2.333, 2.967, 3.611, 4.385     | 3.03                  |
| $10^{2}$      | 1.102, 1.740, 2.378, 3.018, 3.659, 4.302     | 2.03                  |
| $10^{8}$      | 1.100, 1.740, 2.380, 3.020, 3.660, 4.300     | 2.00                  |

At $\lambda = 10^{-8}$ the fit reproduces the data, and at $x = 2.5$ it equals the natural cubic interpolant's value,
2.6559, to eight digits. At $\lambda = 10^8$ it is the least-squares line $1.1 + 0.64x$.

## Choosing $\lambda$

$\lambda$ is a smoothing parameter, chosen from the data. Generalised cross-validation <Cite id="craven1979" /> and
restricted maximum likelihood are standard; see <NoteLink to="additive-model-smoothing-parameter-selection">smoothing parameter
selection</NoteLink>. Without the data's help, $\lambda$ has no natural scale: it depends on the units of $x$ and
$y$. Report the effective degrees of freedom instead of $\lambda$ itself.

## Where these lead

- **Bayesian view.** The smoothing spline is the posterior mean of a Gaussian process with an integrated Wiener
  process prior <Cite id="wahba1990" />.
- **Additive models.** Each smooth term of a <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive
  model</NoteLink> is a penalised spline.
- **Two and more dimensions.** <NoteLink to="thin-plate-regression-splines">Thin-plate splines</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="tensor-product-smooths">tensor-product smooths</NoteLink> generalise the penalty.
