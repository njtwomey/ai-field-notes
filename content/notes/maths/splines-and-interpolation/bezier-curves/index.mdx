<Definition>
The **Bernstein polynomials** of degree $n$ are $b_{i,n}(t) = \binom{n}{i}t^i(1 - t)^{n-i}$, $i = 0, \dots, n$. A
**Bézier curve** with control points $\pvec_0, \dots, \pvec_n$ is $\cvec(t) = \sum_{i=0}^n \pvec_i\,b_{i,n}(t)$ for
$t \in [0, 1]$. De Casteljau at Citroën and Bézier at Renault developed these curves for car body design around 1960
<Cite id="farin2002" /> <Cite id="farouki2012" />.
</Definition>

## The Bernstein basis

The binomial theorem gives $\sum_i b_{i,n}(t) = ((1 - t) + t)^n = 1$, and every $b_{i,n}$ is non-negative on
$[0, 1]$. So each curve point is a convex combination of the control points:

- **Convex hull.** $\cvec(t)$ lies in the convex hull of $\pvec_0, \dots, \pvec_n$.
- **Affine invariance.** Applying an affine map (rotation, scaling, shear, translation) to the control points applies
  it to the curve.
- **End points.** $b_{i,n}(0) = \indicator[i = 0]$ and $b_{i,n}(1) = \indicator[i = n]$, so $\cvec(0) = \pvec_0$ and
  $\cvec(1) = \pvec_n$.

$b_{i,n}$ peaks at $t = i/n$, so each control point pulls the curve most near that parameter. Every $b_{i,n}$ is
non-zero on the open interval $(0, 1)$: moving one control point moves the whole curve. Control is global.

## De Casteljau's algorithm

Pascal's rule $\binom{n}{i} = \binom{n-1}{i} + \binom{n-1}{i-1}$ gives the Bernstein recursion

$$
b_{i,n}(t) = (1 - t)\,b_{i,n-1}(t) + t\,b_{i-1,n-1}(t).
$$

Substitute into the curve and collect the terms in each $b_{i,n-1}$:

$$
\cvec(t) = \sum_{i=0}^n \pvec_i b_{i,n}(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \brack{(1 - t)\,\pvec_i + t\,\pvec_{i+1}}b_{i,n-1}(t).
$$

The degree-$n$ curve equals a degree-$(n - 1)$ curve whose control points are the points at fraction $t$ along each
leg of the control polygon. Repeating $n$ times leaves one point:

$$
\pvec_i^{[r]} = (1 - t)\,\pvec_i^{[r-1]} + t\,\pvec_{i+1}^{[r-1]}, \qquad \cvec(t) = \pvec_0^{[n]}.
$$

This is **de Casteljau's algorithm**. It uses only convex combinations, costs $O(n^2)$ and is numerically stable. It
is the special case of <NoteLink to="b-splines">de Boor's algorithm</NoteLink> for the knot vector with $n + 1$ zeros
followed by $n + 1$ ones: a Bézier curve is a clamped B-spline with a single span.

**Worked example.** Take the quadratic curve with $\pvec_0 = (0, 0)$, $\pvec_1 = (1, 2)$, $\pvec_2 = (2, 0)$ at
$t = 1/2$. The first level gives $(0.5, 1)$ and $(1.5, 1)$. The second gives $\cvec(1/2) = (1, 1)$. The Bernstein
form agrees: $\tfrac14(0, 0) + \tfrac12(1, 2) + \tfrac14(2, 0) = (1, 1)$. The segment from $(0.5, 1)$ to $(1.5, 1)$ is
tangent to the curve there, and the curve's peak height 1 is half the height of the middle control point.

## Derivatives

Differentiating the Bernstein polynomial gives $b_{i,n}' = n\,(b_{i-1,n-1} - b_{i,n-1})$. Summing by parts,

$$
\cvec'(t) = n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(\pvec_{i+1} - \pvec_i)\,b_{i,n-1}(t).
$$

The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is a Bézier curve of degree $n - 1$ whose control points are the scaled legs of the polygon. At the
ends, $\cvec'(0) = n(\pvec_1 - \pvec_0)$ and $\cvec'(1) = n(\pvec_n - \pvec_{n-1})$: the curve leaves along the first
leg and arrives along the last. The last level of de Casteljau's algorithm also gives the tangent:
$\cvec'(t) = n(\pvec_1^{[n-1]} - \pvec_0^{[n-1]})$. In the example, $\cvec'(1/2) = 2((1.5, 1) - (0.5, 1)) = (2, 0)$.

Joining Bézier pieces uses these formulas. Two cubic pieces on parameter intervals of equal length meet with $C^1$ continuity
when the shared point is the midpoint of the two adjacent control points, and with $G^1$ continuity when the three points are collinear with the
shared point between them (see <NoteLink to="piecewise-polynomials-and-continuity">continuity</NoteLink>).

## Subdivision

The triangle of de Casteljau's algorithm at $t$ also splits the curve. The points down its left edge,
$\pvec_0^{[0]}, \pvec_0^{[1]}, \dots, \pvec_0^{[n]}$, are the control points of the piece of the curve on $[0, t]$,
reparameterised to $[0, 1]$. The points up its right edge,
$\pvec_0^{[n]}, \pvec_1^{[n-1]}, \dots, \pvec_n^{[0]}$, are the control points of the piece on $[t, 1]$
<Cite id="farin2002" />. Under repeated subdivision at $t = 1/2$ the control polygons converge to the curve, and
the distance between them falls by a factor of about 4 at each level, which gives fast rendering, intersection tests and flatness checks.

## Degree elevation

A curve of degree $n$ is also a curve of degree $n + 1$. Multiply by $1 = (1 - t) + t$ and use
$(1 - t)\,b_{i,n} = \frac{n + 1 - i}{n + 1}b_{i,n+1}$ and $t\,b_{i,n} = \frac{i + 1}{n + 1}b_{i+1,n+1}$. Collecting the
terms in each $b_{i,n+1}$ gives the new control points

$$
\qvec_i = \frac{i}{n + 1}\,\pvec_{i-1} + \paren{1 - \frac{i}{n + 1}}\pvec_i, \qquad i = 0, \dots, n + 1.
$$

Each new point lies on a leg of the old polygon. The curve is unchanged, and the new polygon is closer to it.
Repeated elevation makes the polygons converge to the curve, but slowly.

## Variation diminishing

A straight line crosses a Bézier curve no more often than it crosses the control polygon <Cite id="farin2002" />. The
curve therefore wiggles no more than its polygon does. The same holds for B-spline curves. It is the geometric
counterpart of the fact that Bernstein coefficients bound the number of sign changes of a polynomial.

## Where Bézier curves are used

- **Fonts.** TrueType outlines are quadratic Bézier pieces; PostScript Type 1 and CFF outlines are cubic.
- **Vector graphics.** SVG paths and <Gloss name="pdf" /> use quadratic and cubic Bézier segments.
- **Easing curves.** CSS `cubic-bezier(x1, y1, x2, y2)` is a cubic Bézier from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$ that maps time to
  progress.
- **CAD.** Composite Bézier pieces are the building block of B-spline and <NoteLink to="non-uniform-rational-b-splines">non-uniform rational B-spline (NURBS)</NoteLink>
  geometry. Knot insertion converts any B-spline to Bézier pieces.

## Pitfalls

- **High degree.** Global control and slow convergence of the polygon make high-degree curves hard to shape. Use
  piecewise cubics.
- **No circles.** A polynomial curve cannot trace an exact circle. Rational curves (<NoteLink to="non-uniform-rational-b-splines">NURBS</NoteLink>)
  can.
- **Parameter is not arc length.** Equal steps in $t$ bunch up where control points are close.
