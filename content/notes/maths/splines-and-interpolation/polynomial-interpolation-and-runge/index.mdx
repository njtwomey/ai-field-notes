<Definition>
Given $n + 1$ nodes $x_0, \dots, x_n$ that are pairwise distinct and values $y_0, \dots, y_n$, the **interpolating
polynomial** is the unique polynomial $p_n$ of degree at most $n$ with $p_n(x_j) = y_j$ for every $j$. When
$y_j = f(x_j)$ for a function $f$ with $n + 1$ continuous derivatives, the error at $x$ is
$f(x) - p_n(x) = f^{(n+1)}(\xi)\,\omega(x)/(n + 1)!$ for some $\xi$ in the smallest interval holding $x$ and the
nodes, where $\omega(x) = \prod_{j=0}^n (x - x_j)$ is the **node polynomial** <Cite id="suli2003" />.
</Definition>

## Existence and uniqueness

Write $p_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_k x^k$. The $n + 1$ conditions $p_n(x_j) = y_j$ are a linear system
$\Vmat\cvec = \yvec$ with the Vandermonde matrix $V_{jk} = x_j^k$. Uniqueness follows without computing
$\det\Vmat$. If $p$ and $q$ both interpolate, then $p - q$ has degree at most $n$ and $n + 1$ roots $x_0, \dots, x_n$.
A nonzero polynomial of degree at most $n$ has at most $n$ roots, so $p - q = 0$. Uniqueness of the solution of a
square system implies $\Vmat$ is invertible, so a solution exists for every $\yvec$.

Solving the Vandermonde system is a poor way to compute $p_n$. The monomials $x^k$ are nearly parallel on an interval,
so $\Vmat$ is badly conditioned. Two other forms are better.

## Lagrange form

The **Lagrange basis polynomial** for node $j$ is

$$
\ell_j(x) = \prod_{k \ne j} \frac{x - x_k}{x_j - x_k}.
$$

$\ell_j$ has degree $n$, equals 1 at $x_j$ and 0 at every other node. Hence

$$
p_n(x) = \sum_{j=0}^n y_j\,\ell_j(x)
$$

takes the value $y_j$ at $x_j$. The interpolant is linear in the data: $\ell_j$ is the interpolant of the unit vector
that is 1 at node $j$.

**Barycentric form.** Divide $p_n$ by the interpolant of the constant 1, which is $\sum_j \ell_j(x) = 1$. With
weights $w_j = 1/\prod_{k \ne j}(x_j - x_k)$, the factor $\omega(x)$ cancels and

$$
p_n(x) = \frac{\sum_j \frac{w_j}{x - x_j} y_j}{\sum_j \frac{w_j}{x - x_j}}.
$$

Evaluation costs $O(n)$ per point once the weights are known, and the formula is numerically stable for any nodes
<Cite id="berrut2004" />.

## Newton form and divided differences

The **Newton form** builds $p_n$ one node at a time:

$$
p_n(x) = a_0 + a_1(x - x_0) + a_2(x - x_0)(x - x_1) + \dots + a_n\prod_{j<n}(x - x_j).
$$

Adding node $x_{n+1}$ adds one term and leaves $a_0, \dots, a_n$ unchanged, because the new term vanishes at the old
nodes. The coefficient $a_k$ is the **divided difference** $f[x_0, \dots, x_k]$, defined by $f[x_j] = y_j$ and

$$
f[x_j, \dots, x_{j+k}] = \frac{f[x_{j+1}, \dots, x_{j+k}] - f[x_j, \dots, x_{j+k-1}]}{x_{j+k} - x_j}.
$$

The recursion holds because $a_k$ is the leading coefficient of the degree-$k$ interpolant on $x_0, \dots, x_k$. That
interpolant can be written as a blend of the two degree-$(k - 1)$ interpolants $q$ on $x_1, \dots, x_k$ and $r$ on
$x_0, \dots, x_{k-1}$:

$$
p(x) = \frac{(x - x_0)\,q(x) - (x - x_k)\,r(x)}{x_k - x_0}.
$$

At $x_0$ this gives $r(x_0)$, at $x_k$ it gives $q(x_k)$, and at an interior node both $q$ and $r$ equal $y_j$, so
$p$ interpolates. Its leading coefficient is the difference of the leading coefficients of $q$ and $r$ divided by
$x_k - x_0$, which is the recursion.

**Worked example.** Take nodes $0, 1, 2$ with values $1, 3, 2$. The first divided differences are
$f[0, 1] = (3 - 1)/1 = 2$ and $f[1, 2] = (2 - 3)/1 = -1$. The second is $f[0, 1, 2] = (-1 - 2)/(2 - 0) = -1.5$. So

$$
p_2(x) = 1 + 2x - 1.5\,x(x - 1), \qquad p_2(1.5) = 1 + 3 - 1.5 \cdot 0.75 = 2.875.
$$

The Lagrange form gives the same value: $\ell_0(1.5) = -0.125$, $\ell_1(1.5) = 0.75$, $\ell_2(1.5) = 0.375$, and
$1 \cdot (-0.125) + 3 \cdot 0.75 + 2 \cdot 0.375 = 2.875$.

## The error formula

Fix $x$ distinct from the nodes and define, for $t$ in an interval $I$ holding $x$ and the nodes,

$$
g(t) = f(t) - p_n(t) - \frac{f(x) - p_n(x)}{\omega(x)}\,\omega(t).
$$

$g$ vanishes at the $n + 1$ nodes, where $f = p_n$ and $\omega = 0$, and at $t = x$ by construction: $n + 2$ zeros.
By Rolle's theorem, between each pair of consecutive zeros $g'$ has a zero, so $g'$ has at least $n + 1$ zeros.
Repeating, $g^{(n+1)}$ has at least one zero $\xi \in I$. Now $p_n^{(n+1)} = 0$, because $p_n$ has degree at most $n$,
and $\omega^{(n+1)} = (n + 1)!$, because $\omega$ is monic of degree $n + 1$. So

$$
0 = g^{(n+1)}(\xi) = f^{(n+1)}(\xi) - \frac{f(x) - p_n(x)}{\omega(x)}(n + 1)!,
\qquad
f(x) - p_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n + 1)!}\,\omega(x).
$$

The error has two factors. One depends on $f$: how large its high derivatives are. The other depends only on the
nodes: the node polynomial $\omega$.

## Runge's phenomenon

Runge interpolated $f(x) = 1/(1 + 25x^2)$ on $[-1, 1]$ at equally spaced nodes <Cite id="runge1901" />. The function
is smooth, yet the maximum error grows without bound as $n$ grows. The interpolant converges in the middle of the
interval and oscillates with growing amplitude near $\pm 1$.

Both factors of the error formula explain it. For equispaced nodes $\abs{\omega(x)}$ is much larger near the ends
than in the middle, because a point near an end is far from most nodes. At degree 10 the ratio of its maximum near
$\pm 1$ to its maximum on $[-0.1, 0.1]$ is about 87, and the ratio grows exponentially with $n$. And the derivatives of $f$ grow
like $(n + 1)!\,5^{n+1}$: $f$ has poles at $x = \pm i/5$, close to the real interval, and the Taylor series of $f$ has
radius of convergence $1/5$. The product outruns $(n + 1)!$ near the ends <Cite id="trefethen2019" />.

The figure's numbers, checked with scipy's `BarycentricInterpolator`: at degree 10 the maximum error is 1.92 with
equispaced nodes and 0.109 with Chebyshev nodes; at degree 20 it is 59.8 and 0.0153.

## Chebyshev nodes minimise the node polynomial

The only part of the error bound we control is $\max_{[-1,1]}\abs{\omega}$. The **Chebyshev polynomial**
$T_m(x) = \cos(m\arccos x)$ is a polynomial of degree $m$: from $\cos((m + 1)\theta) + \cos((m - 1)\theta) =
2\cos\theta\cos(m\theta)$ follows $T_{m+1} = 2xT_m - T_{m-1}$ with $T_0 = 1$, $T_1 = x$, so its leading
coefficient is $2^{m-1}$. Choose the nodes as the roots of $T_{n+1}$,

$$
x_j = \cos\frac{(2j + 1)\pi}{2n + 2}, \qquad j = 0, \dots, n.
$$

Then $\omega = T_{n+1}/2^n$, and $\max\abs{\omega} = 2^{-n}$, since $\abs{T_{n+1}} \le 1$.

**No monic polynomial of degree $n + 1$ does better.** $T_{n+1}$ reaches $\pm 1$ with alternating signs at the
$n + 2$ points $z_k = \cos(k\pi/(n + 1))$. Suppose a monic $q$ of degree $n + 1$ had $\max\abs{q} < 2^{-n}$. Then
$d = T_{n+1}/2^n - q$ has degree at most $n$, since the leading terms cancel. At each $z_k$, $d(z_k)$ has the sign of
$T_{n+1}(z_k)$, because $\abs{q(z_k)} < 2^{-n} = \abs{T_{n+1}(z_k)}/2^n$. So $d$ changes sign $n + 1$ times and has
$n + 1$ roots, which forces $d = 0$ and $q = T_{n+1}/2^n$, a contradiction.

For equispaced nodes at degree 10 the maximum of $\abs{\omega}$ is $8.5 \times 10^{-3}$, against
$2^{-10} = 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ for Chebyshev nodes. On an interval $[a, b]$ the nodes map linearly and the bound
scales by $((b - a)/2)^{n+1}$.

## The Lebesgue constant

The error formula needs $f^{(n+1)}$. A bound that needs no derivatives compares $p_n$ with the best polynomial
approximation $p^*$ of degree $n$ in the maximum norm. Interpolation is linear and reproduces polynomials of degree
at most $n$, so $p_n - p^*$ is the interpolant of $f - p^*$:

$$
\abs{p_n(x) - p^*(x)} = \abs{\sum_j (f - p^*)(x_j)\,\ell_j(x)} \le \norm{f - p^*}_\infty \sum_j \abs{\ell_j(x)}.
$$

With the **Lebesgue constant** $\Lambda_n = \max_x \sum_j \abs{\ell_j(x)}$ and the triangle inequality,

$$
\norm{f - p_n}_\infty \le (1 + \Lambda_n)\,\norm{f - p^*}_\infty.
$$

$\Lambda_n$ also bounds how much errors in the data $y_j$ are amplified. For Chebyshev nodes
$\Lambda_n \le \frac{2}{\pi}\log(n + 1) + 1$; for equispaced nodes $\Lambda_n$ grows like
$2^{n+1}/(e\,n\log n)$ <Cite id="trefethen2019" />. The measured values are 2.49 and 29.9 at degree 10, and 2.90 and
$1.1 \times 10^4$ at degree 20.

## Pitfalls

- **High degree on equispaced data.** Tabulated or sampled data usually comes on a uniform grid. Use a
  <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">spline</NoteLink> or local polynomials of low degree instead of one global
  polynomial.
- **The Vandermonde system.** Solving for monomial coefficients loses digits as $n$ grows. Evaluate through the
  barycentric formula or the Newton form.
- **Extrapolation.** Outside the nodes $\abs{\omega}$ grows like $\abs{x}^{n+1}$, and so does the error.
