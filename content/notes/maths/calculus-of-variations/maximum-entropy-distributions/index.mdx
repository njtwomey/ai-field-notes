<Definition>
The differential entropy of a density $p$ on a support $\Xcal$ is $\entropy[p] = -\int_\Xcal p(x)\log p(x)\, dx$. Among
all densities on $\Xcal$ with prescribed expectations $\expect[T_i(X)] = c_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, k$, the one with the
largest entropy, when it exists, is

$$
p^*(x) = \frac{1}{Z(\lambdavec)} \exp\paren{\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i T_i(x)}, \qquad
Z(\lambdavec) = \int_\Xcal \exp\paren{\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i T_i(x)} dx,
$$

with the multipliers $\lambda_i$ chosen so that the constraints hold <Cite id="jaynes1957" />.
</Definition>

## Why maximise entropy

Entropy measures how spread out, or uncommitted, a distribution is. Constraints encode what is known, for example a
measured mean. The maximum-entropy distribution satisfies the constraints and assumes nothing else. Jaynes proposed it
as the principle for assigning probabilities from partial information <Cite id="jaynes1957" />.

## Derivation of the general form

Maximise $\entropy[p]$ subject to $\int p\, dx = 1$ and $\int T_i\, p\, dx = c_i$. This is a
<NoteLink to="constrained-variational-problems">constrained variational problem</NoteLink> with no derivative of $p$ in
the integrands, so the stationarity condition is pointwise. The functional derivative of the entropy is
$-\log p(x) - 1$, and each constraint contributes its multiplier times its integrand:

$$
-\log p(x) - 1 + \lambda_0 + \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i T_i(x) = 0.
$$

Solving for $p$ gives $p(x) = \exp(\lambda_0 - 1) \exp\paren{\sum_i \lambda_i T_i(x)}$. The factor
$\exp(\lambda_0 - 1)$ is whatever makes $p$ integrate to 1, namely $1/Z(\lambdavec)$. This is an
<NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> with sufficient statistics $T_i$ and natural parameters
$\lambda_i$.

### The stationary point is the maximum

The derivation finds a stationary density. A short argument shows it is the global maximum. Let $p$ be any density
satisfying the same constraints. Because $\log p^* = \sum_i \lambda_i T_i - \log Z$ and both $p$ and $p^*$ give $T_i$
the same expectation $c_i$,

$$
-\int p \log p^*\, dx = \log Z - \sum_i \lambda_i c_i = -\int p^* \log p^*\, dx = \entropy[p^*].
$$

Therefore

$$
\entropy[p] = -\int p \log p\, dx = -\int p\log p^*\, dx - \int p \log\frac{p}{p^*}\, dx
= \entropy[p^*] - \KL(p \,\|\, p^*).
$$

The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is non-negative and zero only when $p = p^*$, so
$\entropy[p] \le \entropy[p^*]$, with equality only for $p^*$ <Cite id="cover2006" />.

## Three standard cases

Each case chooses a support and constraints, applies the general form, and then solves for the multipliers.

### Bounded interval, no constraint: uniform

On $[a, b]$ with only normalisation, $k = 0$ and $p^*$ is constant. Normalisation gives $p^*(x) = 1/(b - a)$, with
entropy $\log(b - a)$.

### Fixed mean on $[0, \infty)$: exponential

With $T_1(x) = x$ and $\expect[X] = m$, the form is $p^*(x) \propto e^{\lambda_1 x}$. It is integrable on
$[0, \infty)$ only if $\lambda_1 < 0$. Write $\lambda_1 = -\beta$; then $Z = 1/\beta$ and $p^*(x) = \beta e^{-\beta x}$.
Its mean is $1/\beta$, so the constraint gives $\beta = 1/m$:

$$
p^*(x) = \frac{1}{m} e^{-x/m}, \qquad \entropy[p^*] = 1 + \log m.
$$

This is the <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>.

### Fixed mean and variance on $\reals$: Gaussian

With $T_1(x) = x$ and $T_2(x) = x^2$, the form is $p^*(x) \propto \exp(\lambda_1 x + \lambda_2 x^2)$. It is integrable
only if $\lambda_2 < 0$. Completing the square writes the exponent as $-(x - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2)$ plus a constant,
with $\sigma^2 = -1/(2\lambda_2)$ and $\mu = \lambda_1\sigma^2$. The density is therefore Gaussian, with normalising constant
$\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$ from the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>, and the constraints make its
mean and variance the prescribed $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$:

$$
p^*(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\paren{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad
\entropy[p^*] = \tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e \sigma^2).
$$

This is the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian distribution</NoteLink>. The same argument with
$T(\xvec) = (\xvec, \xvec\xvec^\top)$ shows that the multivariate normal has the largest entropy for a given mean and
covariance.

### Worked example

With standard deviation 1, the entropies in nats are: Gaussian $\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e) = 1.419$, logistic
$2 + \log(\sqrt3/\pi) = 1.405$, Laplace $1 + \log\sqrt 2 = 1.347$, uniform $\log\sqrt{12} = 1.242$. The Gaussian is
largest, as the theorem requires.

## In machine learning

- **Softmax and logistic regression.** The conditional distribution of maximum entropy subject to matching the
  empirical expectations of features is a softmax, or log-linear, model. Logistic regression is also called the
  maximum-entropy classifier.
- **Default Gaussian assumptions.** When only a mean and a covariance are known, assuming a Gaussian adds the least
  extra information. This is one justification for Gaussian noise models.
- **Entropy regularisation.** Adding an entropy bonus to an objective, as in maximum-entropy reinforcement learning,
  pushes solutions towards the exponential-family form derived here.

## Pitfalls

- **The support is part of the problem.** The same mean constraint gives an exponential on $[0, \infty)$ and no
  solution at all on $\reals$, where $\lambda_1 x$ cannot be normalised.
- **Existence.** Some constraint sets have no maximiser. On $\reals$ with a fixed mean and nothing else, entropy is
  unbounded: spreading the density further always raises it.
- **Differential entropy is not invariant.** It changes under a change of variables, and it can be negative. The
  maximum-entropy answer therefore depends on the parameterisation of $x$.
