<Definition>
Mean-field variational inference restricts the approximating density to the factorised family

$$
q(\zvec) = \prod_{j=1}^m q_j(\zvec_j),
$$

where $\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_m$ partition the latent variables, and maximises the
<NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink> over this family. Holding every factor but $q_j$
fixed, the optimal $q_j$ satisfies

$$
\log q_j^*(\zvec_j) = \expect_{-j}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec)} + \text{const},
$$

where $\expect_{-j}$ averages over all factors except $q_j$ <Cite id="blei2017" />.
</Definition>

## Why factorise

The unrestricted maximiser of the ELBO is the posterior itself, which is what could not be computed. The factorised
family removes all dependence between groups of latent variables. In exchange, each factor can often be found in closed
form, and each expectation involves only one group at a time. The name comes from statistical physics, where each
particle feels only the average field of the others.

## Derivation of the optimal factor

Fix $q_i$ for all $i \ne j$ and write the ELBO as a functional of $q_j$ alone. Because $q$ factorises, the entropy of
$q$ is the sum of the factors' entropies, and only one of them involves $q_j$:

$$
\operatorname{ELBO}(q_j) = \int q_j(\zvec_j)\, \expect_{-j}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec)}\, d\zvec_j
- \int q_j(\zvec_j) \log q_j(\zvec_j)\, d\zvec_j + \text{const}.
$$

Here $\expect_{-j}[\log p(\xvec, \zvec)] = \int \prod_{i \ne j} q_i(\zvec_i)\, \log p(\xvec, \zvec)\, d\zvec_{-j}$ is a
function of $\zvec_j$ only. Maximise this functional subject to $\int q_j\, d\zvec_j = 1$, a
<NoteLink to="constrained-variational-problems">constrained variational problem</NoteLink>. Neither term contains a
derivative of $q_j$, so the stationarity condition is pointwise. The functional derivative of the first term is
$\expect_{-j}[\log p(\xvec, \zvec)]$, that of the entropy term is $-\log q_j - 1$, and the normalisation constraint adds
a multiplier $\lambda$:

$$
\expect_{-j}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec)} - \log q_j(\zvec_j) - 1 + \lambda = 0.
$$

Solving for $\log q_j$ gives the optimal factor, with every constant absorbed by normalisation:

$$
q_j^*(\zvec_j) = \frac{\exp\paren{\expect_{-j}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec)}}}
{\int \exp\paren{\expect_{-j}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec)}}\, d\zvec_j}.
$$

<Derivation title="The same result without functional derivatives">
Define the density $\tilde p_j(\zvec_j) \propto \exp\paren{\expect_{-j}[\log p(\xvec, \zvec)]}$ with normalising
constant $C$. Then $\expect_{-j}[\log p(\xvec, \zvec)] = \log \tilde p_j(\zvec_j) + \log C$, and the ELBO as a function
of $q_j$ becomes

$$
\int q_j \log\tilde p_j\, d\zvec_j - \int q_j \log q_j\, d\zvec_j + \text{const}
= -\KL\paren{q_j \,\|\, \tilde p_j} + \text{const}.
$$

The KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when $q_j = \tilde p_j$, so this choice is the unique maximiser over
$q_j$. The argument also shows the stationary point is a maximum, not just a stationary point.
</Derivation>

Only the terms of $\log p(\xvec, \zvec)$ that involve $\zvec_j$ matter; the rest are absorbed in the constant. In
conditionally conjugate models, those terms are linear in the sufficient statistics of $\zvec_j$, so $q_j^*$ belongs to
the same exponential family as the prior on $\zvec_j$, and the update only changes its parameters.

## Coordinate ascent (CAVI)

Coordinate ascent variational inference initialises every factor and then cycles through $j = 1, \dots, m$, replacing
$q_j$ by $q_j^*$ computed from the current other factors <Cite id="bishop2006" />.

**Monotonicity.** Each update maximises the ELBO exactly over one factor with the others fixed, so it cannot lower the
ELBO. The ELBO is bounded above by $\log p(\xvec)$, so the sequence of values converges. The limit is a stationary point
of the ELBO over the factorised family. It is often only a local optimum, so CAVI is typically run from several
initialisations.

The full update equations for a non-trivial model, a Gaussian with unknown mean and precision, are derived in the
worked example <NoteLink to="cavi-gaussian-unknown-mean-and-precision">CAVI for a Gaussian with unknown mean and
precision</NoteLink>.

## Worked example: a correlated Gaussian

Let the target be $\Gauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat)$ in two dimensions with unit variances and correlation $\rho$, whose
precision matrix is $\Lambdamat = \Sigmamat^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - \rho^2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1
\end{bmatrix}$. The terms of $\log p(\zvec) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\zvec^\top\Lambdamat\zvec + \text{const}$ that involve $z_1$
are $-\tfrac{1}{2}\Lambda_{11} z_1^2 - \Lambda_{12} z_1 z_2$. Taking the expectation over $q_2$ replaces $z_2$ by its mean
$m_2$:

$$
\log q_1^*(z_1) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\Lambda_{11} z_1^2 - \Lambda_{12} z_1 m_2 + \text{const}.
$$

This is a Gaussian with precision $\Lambda_{11}$ and mean $-\Lambda_{12} m_2 / \Lambda_{11} = \rho\, m_2$. By symmetry
$q_2^*$ has mean $\rho\, m_1$. Coordinate ascent therefore alternates $m_1 \leftarrow \rho m_2$ and
$m_2 \leftarrow \rho m_1$, which shrinks the means towards 0 by a factor of $\rho^2$ per sweep. At convergence the means
are exact, but each factor has variance $1/\Lambda_{11} = 1 - \rho^2$, while the true marginal variance is 1. With
$\rho = 0.8$ the mean-field variance is 0.36, and the remaining divergence is
$\KL(q \,\|\, p) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1 - \rho^2) = 0.51$.

## Properties

- **Variance is underestimated.** Maximising the ELBO minimises $\KL(q \,\|\, p)$, which heavily penalises $q$ for
  putting mass where $p$ is small. A factorised $q$ cannot follow a diagonal ridge, so it shrinks to fit inside it. The
  example shows the factor variance is the conditional variance $1/\Lambda_{jj}$, never more than the marginal
  variance. See <NoteLink to="kl-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> for the forward and reverse directions.
- **Mode seeking.** For a multimodal posterior, $q$ tends to lock onto one mode, chosen by the initialisation.
- **Means can be right.** For a Gaussian target, mean-field means are exact at convergence, as in the example.
- **Scale.** Each update involves one group of variables. With stochastic optimisation over data subsets, the method
  scales to very large models, such as topic models over millions of documents <Cite id="blei2017" />.

## Relation to EM

If one group of variables is a point estimate rather than a distribution, its "update" becomes a maximisation. EM is the
special case with an unrestricted $q$ over the latent variables and point estimates for the parameters; see
<NoteLink to="em-as-variational-inference">EM as variational inference</NoteLink>. The
<NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> can be fitted either way.

## Pitfalls

- **Non-conjugate terms.** When $\expect_{-j}[\log p]$ has no closed form, the optimal factor cannot be computed
  exactly. Gradient-based variational inference with a parametric family is then used instead.
- **Reporting uncertainty.** Credible intervals from a mean-field posterior are too narrow when the latent variables are
  correlated in the true posterior.
- **Convergence is not correctness.** A converged ELBO certifies a stationary point of a restricted problem, not a good
  approximation. The gap to the log evidence is unknown in general.
