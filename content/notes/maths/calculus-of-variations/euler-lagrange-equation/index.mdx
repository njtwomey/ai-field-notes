<Definition>
Let $J[y] = \int_a^b L\paren{x, y(x), y'(x)}\, dx$ over twice-differentiable functions $y$ with fixed end values
$y(a) = y_a$ and $y(b) = y_b$. If $y$ makes $J$ stationary, then $y$ satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx} \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = 0 \qquad \text{for all } x \in [a, b].
$$

The integrand $L$ is called the Lagrangian <Cite id="gelfand1963" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation

Perturb a stationary $y$ by $\epsilon\eta$. The end values are fixed, so the perturbation must vanish there:
$\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$. Differentiate $J[y + \epsilon\eta]$ at $\epsilon = 0$. The chain rule applies to both the
second and third arguments of $L$, because $(y + \epsilon\eta)' = y' + \epsilon\eta'$:

$$
\frac{d}{d\epsilon} J[y + \epsilon\eta] \Big|_{\epsilon=0}
= \int_a^b \paren{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\, \eta + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\, \eta'}\, dx.
$$

The second term contains $\eta'$, not $\eta$. Integrate it by parts:

$$
\int_a^b \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\, \eta'\, dx
= \brack{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\, \eta}_a^b - \int_a^b \frac{d}{dx}\paren{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}}\eta\, dx.
$$

The boundary term is zero because $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$. Stationarity therefore requires

$$
\int_a^b \paren{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}}\eta\, dx = 0
$$

for every such $\eta$. By the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations (see
<NoteLink to="functionals-and-functional-derivatives">functionals and functional derivatives</NoteLink>), the bracket is
zero at every $x$. That is the Euler–Lagrange equation. The bracket is also the functional derivative $\delta J / \delta y$.

## Worked example: the shortest path

The length of the curve $y(x)$ from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$ is

$$
J[y] = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 + y'(x)^2}\, dx.
$$

Here $L = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ does not depend on $y$, so $\partial L / \partial y = 0$, and
$\partial L / \partial y' = y' / \sqrt{1 + y'^2}$. The Euler–Lagrange equation becomes

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \frac{y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = c.
$$

The map $s \mapsto s / \sqrt{1 + s^2}$ is strictly increasing, so a constant value forces a constant slope $y' = m$. The
curve is a straight line, and the end points fix it as $y = x$, with length $\sqrt 2$.

The integrand $\sqrt{1 + s^2}$ is convex in $s$, so $J$ is a convex functional, and its stationary point is the global
minimum. The figure shows this: along every admissible perturbation, $J$ has zero slope and a minimum at $\epsilon = 0$.

## The Beltrami identity

When $L$ does not depend on $x$ explicitly, the Euler–Lagrange equation has a first integral:

$$
L - y' \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = C.
$$

**Derivation.** Differentiate the left side along the curve, using $\partial L / \partial x = 0$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\paren{L - y'\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}}
= \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\, y' + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\, y'' - y''\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}
- y'\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}
= y'\paren{\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}}.
$$

The bracket is zero by the Euler–Lagrange equation, so the quantity is constant. The identity replaces a second-order
equation by a first-order one. In mechanics, where $x$ is time, it is conservation of energy.

## The brachistochrone

The problem that started the subject asks which curve carries a bead, sliding without friction under gravity, from
rest to a lower point in the least time. The time is the functional $T[y] = \int \sqrt{1 + y'^2} / \sqrt{2gy}\,dx$. Its
integrand does not depend on $x$, so the Beltrami identity gives $y\paren{1 + y'^2} = 2r$, whose solution is a cycloid.
The <NoteLink to="brachistochrone-problem">brachistochrone problem</NoteLink> works this through, with its history.

## Several functions and higher derivatives

For $J = \int L(x, y_1, \dots, y_k, y_1', \dots, y_k')\, dx$, perturbing one function at a time gives one equation per
function: $\partial L / \partial y_i - \frac{d}{dx}\, \partial L / \partial y_i' = 0$. For an integrand that depends on
$y''$, integrating by parts twice gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} + \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y''} = 0.
$$

## In machine learning

- **Smoothing splines.** Minimising $\sum_i (y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda \int f''(x)^2\, dx$ over functions gives a
  natural cubic spline. Between data points the higher-order equation reads $f'''' = 0$, so $f$ is cubic there.
- **Variational inference.** Optimising a divergence over densities is a variational problem whose integrand has no
  derivative term. The Euler–Lagrange equation then reduces to $\partial L / \partial q = 0$ plus a constraint; see
  <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>.
- **Continuous-time models.** The adjoint equations used to train neural ordinary differential equations come from the
  same first-variation argument applied to a loss constrained by a differential equation.

## Pitfalls

- **Necessary, not sufficient.** A solution of the equation is stationary. It can be a minimum, a maximum or neither.
  Convexity of $L$ in $(y, y')$ makes it a minimum.
- **Boundary conditions.** With a free end value, $\eta$ need not vanish there, and the boundary term must vanish on its
  own: $\partial L / \partial y' = 0$ at that end. This is the natural boundary condition. The figure's
  "moves the end" perturbation shows the nonzero slope that an unaccounted boundary term produces.
- **Smoothness.** The derivation assumes $y$ is twice differentiable. Some problems have minimisers with corners, which
  satisfy weaker conditions.
