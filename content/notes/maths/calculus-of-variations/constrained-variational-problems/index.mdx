<Definition>
Let $J[y] = \int_a^b L(x, y, y')\, dx$ and $K[y] = \int_a^b M(x, y, y')\, dx$, with fixed end values. If $y$ makes $J$
stationary among functions with $K[y] = c$, and $y$ is not itself a stationary function of $K$, then there is a
constant $\lambda$ such that $y$ satisfies the <NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation">Euler–Lagrange equation</NoteLink> for $L - \lambda M$:

$$
\frac{\partial (L - \lambda M)}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial (L - \lambda M)}{\partial y'} = 0.
$$

The constant $\lambda$ is a <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink>. It is fixed afterwards by the constraint $K[y] = c$
<Cite id="gelfand1963" />. Several constraints $K_i[y] = c_i$ each get their own multiplier.
</Definition>

## Derivation

A one-parameter perturbation $y + \epsilon\eta$ generally violates the constraint, so the unconstrained argument does
not apply. Use two perturbations instead: $y + \epsilon_1\eta_1 + \epsilon_2\eta_2$, with $\eta_1$ and $\eta_2$
vanishing at the end points. Define the ordinary functions of two variables

$$
\Phi(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2) = J[y + \epsilon_1\eta_1 + \epsilon_2\eta_2], \qquad
\Psi(\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2) = K[y + \epsilon_1\eta_1 + \epsilon_2\eta_2].
$$

The point $(0, 0)$ makes $\Phi$ stationary subject to $\Psi = c$. By the ordinary Lagrange multiplier theorem in two
variables, $\nabla\Phi(0, 0) = \lambda \nabla\Psi(0, 0)$ for some $\lambda$, provided $\nabla\Psi(0, 0) \ne \zeros$.
Each <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivative</NoteLink> is a <NoteLink to="functionals-and-functional-derivatives">first variation</NoteLink>:

$$
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\epsilon_i}\Big|_{0} = \int_a^b \frac{\delta J}{\delta y}\, \eta_i\, dx, \qquad
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\epsilon_i}\Big|_{0} = \int_a^b \frac{\delta K}{\delta y}\, \eta_i\, dx.
$$

Because $y$ is not stationary for $K$, some $\eta_2$ has $\int (\delta K / \delta y)\, \eta_2\, dx \ne 0$. Fix that
$\eta_2$; it determines $\lambda$. The equation for the first component then reads

$$
\int_a^b \paren{\frac{\delta J}{\delta y} - \lambda\frac{\delta K}{\delta y}} \eta_1\, dx = 0
$$

for every admissible $\eta_1$, with the same $\lambda$. The fundamental lemma gives
$\delta J / \delta y - \lambda\, \delta K / \delta y = 0$ pointwise, which is the Euler–Lagrange equation for
$L - \lambda M$.

## Worked example

Find the function on $[0, 1]$ with $y(0) = y(1) = 0$ and $\int_0^1 y\, dx = 1$ that minimises $\int_0^1 y'^2\, dx$.

The combined integrand is $y'^2 - \lambda y$. Its Euler–Lagrange equation is $-\lambda - 2y'' = 0$, so
$y'' = -\lambda/2$ and $y = -\tfrac{\lambda}{4}x^2 + bx + c$. The end values give $c = 0$ and $b = \lambda/4$, so
$y = \tfrac{\lambda}{4}\, x(1 - x)$. The constraint fixes the multiplier:

$$
\int_0^1 \frac{\lambda}{4}\, x(1 - x)\, dx = \frac{\lambda}{24} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = 24,
\qquad y = 6x(1 - x).
$$

The minimum value is $\int_0^1 (6 - 12x)^2\, dx = 12$. The multiplier $\lambda$ is the rate at which the optimal value
changes with the constraint level. Requiring area $c$ instead of 1 scales $y$ by $c$ and the value by
$c^2$, giving $12c^2$, whose <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> at $c = 1$ is $24 = \lambda$.

## Constraints on densities

Optimising over probability densities always carries the constraint $\int p(x)\, dx = 1$, and often moment constraints
$\int T_i(x)\, p(x)\, dx = c_i$. These integrands contain no $p'$, so the Euler–Lagrange equation reduces to a pointwise
condition on the functional derivative:

$$
\frac{\delta F}{\delta p}(x) + \lambda_0 + \sum_i \lambda_i T_i(x) = 0.
$$

The normalisation multiplier $\lambda_0$ absorbs every additive constant, which is why variational solutions are often
written as "$\log p(x) = \dots + \text{const}$". Two results follow directly from this condition:

- <NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-distributions">Maximum-entropy distributions</NoteLink>: with $F$ the <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink>, the
  solution is an <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">Mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>: with $F$ the <Gloss name="elbo">evidence
  lower bound</Gloss> restricted to one factor, the solution is the optimal factor update.

## The isoperimetric problem

Among curves $y(x)$ of fixed length $\ell$ joining two points on the $x$-axis, find the one that encloses the largest
area $\int y\, dx$ between itself and the axis.

<Derivation title="The solution is a circular arc">
The combined integrand is $y + \lambda\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$. It does not depend on $y'$ except through the length term, and
$\partial/\partial y = 1$. The Euler–Lagrange equation is

$$
1 - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = x - x_0.
$$

Write $s = (x - x_0)/\lambda$. Solving for the slope gives $y' = s / \sqrt{1 - s^2}$, which integrates to
$y = y_0 - \lambda\sqrt{1 - s^2}$. Rearranged, $(x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 = \lambda^2$: a circle of radius $\lambda$.
The multiplier is the radius, and it is fixed by the length $\ell$.
</Derivation>

The answer is an arc of a circle. The problem is sometimes called Dido's problem, after the legend of a queen allowed as
much land as an ox-hide could enclose.

## Pitfalls

- **The regularity condition.** The theorem needs $y$ not to be stationary for the constraint functional. Otherwise
  every multiplier may fail, as with the ordinary Lagrange condition when the constraint <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is zero.
- **Sign of $\lambda$.** The sign convention ($L - \lambda M$ or $L + \lambda M$) changes the sign of
  $\lambda$, not the solution.
- **Inequality constraints.** A constraint such as $p(x) \ge 0$ is not an integral equality. It is usually inactive at
  the solution, which must then be checked, or it is handled with <Gloss name="kkt">Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions</Gloss>.
