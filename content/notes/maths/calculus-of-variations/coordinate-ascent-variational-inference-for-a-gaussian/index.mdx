<Definition>
Observations $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are independent draws from $\Gauss(\mu, \tau^{-1})$, where both the mean $\mu$ and the
precision $\tau$ are unknown. The prior is $\mu \mid \tau \sim \Gauss\paren{\mu_0, (\lambda_0\tau)^{-1}}$ and
$\tau \sim \GammaD(a_0, b_0)$, with shape $a_0$ and rate $b_0$. Approximate the posterior $p(\mu, \tau \mid \xvec)$ by a
factorised $q(\mu)\, q(\tau)$ that maximises the evidence lower bound <Cite id="bishop2006" />.
</Definition>

## Setup

The exact posterior of this model is known in closed form, so the example shows exactly what the mean-field
approximation keeps and what it loses. The log joint density, keeping every term that involves $\mu$ or $\tau$, is

$$
\log p(\xvec, \mu, \tau) = \frac{N}{2}\log\tau - \frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2
+ \frac{1}{2}\log\tau - \frac{\lambda_0\tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + (a_0 - 1)\log\tau - b_0\tau + \text{const}.
$$

The three groups of terms come from the likelihood, the prior on $\mu$ given $\tau$, and the prior on $\tau$. Write
$\bar x$ for the sample mean.

## Solution

By <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>, each optimal factor is
$\log q^*(\cdot) = \expect_{\text{other}}[\log p(\xvec, \mu, \tau)] + \text{const}$.

### The factor $q(\mu)$

Take the expectation over $q(\tau)$ and keep the terms in $\mu$. Only $\expect[\tau]$ appears:

$$
\log q^*(\mu) = -\frac{\expect[\tau]}{2}\brack{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2} + \text{const}.
$$

This is a quadratic in $\mu$, so $q^*(\mu)$ is Gaussian. Collecting the coefficient of $\mu^2$ gives the precision, and
the coefficient of $\mu$ gives the mean:

$$
q^*(\mu) = \Gauss\paren{\mu_N, \lambda_N^{-1}}, \qquad
\mu_N = \frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + N\bar x}{\lambda_0 + N}, \qquad \lambda_N = (\lambda_0 + N)\,\expect[\tau].
$$

### The factor $q(\tau)$

Take the expectation over $q(\mu)$ and keep the terms in $\tau$:

$$
\log q^*(\tau) = \paren{a_0 + \frac{N + 1}{2} - 1}\log\tau
- \tau\brack{b_0 + \frac{1}{2}\expect_\mu\brack{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2}} + \text{const}.
$$

This is the log of a <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma density</NoteLink>. Using
$\expect_\mu[(c - \mu)^2] = (c - \mu_N)^2 + 1/\lambda_N$ for each squared term,

$$
q^*(\tau) = \GammaD(a_N, b_N), \qquad a_N = a_0 + \frac{N + 1}{2}, \qquad
b_N = b_0 + \frac{1}{2}\brack{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu_N)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu_N - \mu_0)^2 + \frac{N + \lambda_0}{\lambda_N}}.
$$

### The coordinate ascent

The mean $\mu_N$ and the shape $a_N$ do not depend on the other factor, so they are fixed after one update. Only
$\lambda_N$ and $b_N$ interact, through $\expect[\tau] = a_N / b_N$. CAVI alternates: set $b_N$ from the current
$\lambda_N$, then set $\lambda_N = (\lambda_0 + N)\, a_N / b_N$. Each update maximises the ELBO in one factor, so the
ELBO never decreases.

### Comparison with the exact posterior

The exact posterior is Normal-Gamma: $\mu \mid \tau \sim \Gauss\paren{\mu_N, ((\lambda_0 + N)\tau)^{-1}}$ with the same
$\mu_N$, and $\tau \sim \GammaD(a', b')$ with

$$
a' = a_0 + \frac{N}{2}, \qquad
b' = b_0 + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar x)^2 + \frac{\lambda_0 N (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2(\lambda_0 + N)}.
$$

<Derivation title="At convergence, E_q[τ] equals the exact posterior mean a′/b′">
The sum in $b_N$ decomposes around $\bar x$: $\sum_i (x_i - \mu_N)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu_N - \mu_0)^2 =
\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 + \lambda_0 N(\bar x - \mu_0)^2/(\lambda_0 + N)$, because $\mu_N$ is the precision-weighted
average of $\bar x$ and $\mu_0$. Therefore $b_N = b' + (N + \lambda_0)/(2\lambda_N)$. At the fixed point
$\lambda_N = (\lambda_0 + N)\, \expect_q[\tau]$, so $b_N = b' + 1/(2\expect_q[\tau])$. Substituting into
$\expect_q[\tau] = a_N / b_N$ with $a_N = a' + \tfrac{1}{2}$:

$$
\expect_q[\tau]\, b' + \tfrac{1}{2} = a' + \tfrac{1}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \expect_q[\tau] = \frac{a'}{b'},
$$

which is the exact posterior mean of $\tau$.
</Derivation>

Three consequences follow. The mean of $\mu$ is exact: both are $\mu_N$. The mean of $\tau$ is exact at convergence,
as the derivation shows. The variance of $\mu$ is too small. Under $q$ it is
$1/\lambda_N = b'/\paren{a'(\lambda_0 + N)}$, while the exact posterior variance of $\mu$, a Student $t$ marginal, is
$b'/\paren{(a' - 1)(\lambda_0 + N)}$. The ratio is $1 - 1/a'$, so the mean-field approximation understates the
uncertainty in $\mu$ most for small samples. It cannot represent the posterior's dependence between $\mu$ and $\tau$:
the exact posterior is wider in $\mu$ where $\tau$ is small, and the factorised $q$ averages that away.

### Numbers

Take the ten observations $0.53, 1.8, 0.2, 1.1, 0.9, 1.4, 0.75, 1.9, 0.1, 1.2$, with $\bar x = 0.988$, and the prior
$\mu_0 = 0$, $\lambda_0 = 1$, $a_0 = b_0 = 1$. Then $\mu_N = 9.88/11 = 0.898$, $a_N = 6.5$, $a' = 6$ and
$b' = 3.145$. Starting from $\expect[\tau] = 0.1$, the ELBO after successive half-updates is $-24.52, -16.71, -14.26,
-12.74, -12.55, \dots$ and converges to $-12.516$. The log evidence is $-12.475$, so the remaining gap
$\KL(q \,\|\, p) = 0.041$. At convergence $\expect_q[\tau] = 1.908 = a'/b'$, and the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of $\mu$ is
$0.218$ under $q$ against $0.239$ exactly, a ratio of $\sqrt{5/6} = 0.913$.

## What it illustrates

- **Optimal factors from the model's structure.** No family was assumed for $q(\mu)$ or $q(\tau)$. The Gaussian and
  gamma forms fall out of the mean-field update because the model is conditionally conjugate; see
  <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink>.
- **Monotone coordinate ascent.** The ELBO rises at every half-update and stops just short of the log evidence, as
  <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink> requires.
- **Mean-field underestimates variance.** The means are right; the spread in $\mu$ is too small by the factor
  $\sqrt{1 - 1/a'}$, a concrete instance of the reverse KL divergence preferring narrow approximations.

## Variations

- **More data.** As $N$ grows, $a'$ grows and the variance ratio $1 - 1/a'$ tends to 1. The posterior dependence
  between $\mu$ and $\tau$ weakens and the mean-field approximation becomes accurate.
- **A strong prior.** A large $\lambda_0$ pulls $\mu_N$ towards $\mu_0$ in both the exact and the approximate posterior.
  The variance ratio $1 - 1/a'$ does not change, because $a'$ does not depend on $\lambda_0$: a stronger prior on $\mu$
  narrows both posteriors without closing the gap between them.
- **An independent prior.** With $\mu \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ independent of $\tau$, the exact posterior has no
  closed form, but the mean-field updates keep the same Gaussian and gamma forms. This is the usual situation in which
  <Gloss name="vi">variational inference</Gloss> is used.
