<Definition>
Let $p(\xvec, \zvec)$ be a joint model of observed data $\xvec$ and latent variables $\zvec$, and let $q(\zvec)$ be any
density whose support contains that of the posterior. The evidence lower bound is

$$
\operatorname{ELBO}(q) = \expect_{q}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec) - \log q(\zvec)}.
$$

It satisfies $\log p(\xvec) = \operatorname{ELBO}(q) + \KL\paren{q(\zvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}$, so
$\operatorname{ELBO}(q) \le \log p(\xvec)$, with equality exactly when $q$ is the posterior <Cite id="blei2017" />.
</Definition>

## The problem it solves

By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, the posterior is
$p(\zvec \mid \xvec) = p(\xvec, \zvec) / p(\xvec)$, with evidence $p(\xvec) = \int p(\xvec, \zvec)\, d\zvec$. The joint
is usually easy to evaluate. The evidence is an integral over every configuration of the latent variables, which is
intractable for most models. <Gloss name="vi">Variational inference</Gloss> replaces the integral by an optimisation: choose $q$ from a
tractable family to be close to the posterior <Cite id="jordan1999" />. The ELBO is the objective that makes this
possible without knowing $p(\xvec)$. The main alternatives are sampling methods and the
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink>, which fits a Gaussian at the posterior mode.

## Derivation 1: Jensen's inequality

Multiply and divide by $q$ inside the evidence, then use the concavity of the logarithm,
$\log \expect[Y] \ge \expect[\log Y]$:

$$
\log p(\xvec) = \log \int q(\zvec)\, \frac{p(\xvec, \zvec)}{q(\zvec)}\, d\zvec
\ge \int q(\zvec) \log\frac{p(\xvec, \zvec)}{q(\zvec)}\, d\zvec = \operatorname{ELBO}(q).
$$

This shows the ELBO is a lower bound, but not how loose it is.

## Derivation 2: the exact decomposition

Substitute $p(\xvec, \zvec) = p(\zvec \mid \xvec)\, p(\xvec)$ into the definition. The term $\log p(\xvec)$ does not
depend on $\zvec$, and $q$ integrates to 1:

$$
\operatorname{ELBO}(q)
= \expect_q\brack{\log p(\zvec \mid \xvec) + \log p(\xvec) - \log q(\zvec)}
= \log p(\xvec) - \expect_q\brack{\log\frac{q(\zvec)}{p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}}.
$$

The last <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> is $\KL\paren{q \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}$. Rearranged,

$$
\log p(\xvec) = \operatorname{ELBO}(q) + \KL\paren{q(\zvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}.
$$

The gap in Jensen's inequality is exactly the KL divergence from $q$ to the posterior.

### Maximising the ELBO minimises the KL divergence

The left side does not depend on $q$. Any increase in the ELBO is therefore an equal decrease in the KL divergence to
the posterior. Maximising the ELBO, which needs only the joint $p(\xvec, \zvec)$, minimises
$\KL\paren{q \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}$, which needs the unknown posterior. Because the KL divergence is non-negative
(see <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink>), the ELBO never exceeds the log evidence.

## Other forms

Two rearrangements of the definition are used in practice.

**Likelihood minus complexity.** Write $p(\xvec, \zvec) = p(\xvec \mid \zvec)\, p(\zvec)$:

$$
\operatorname{ELBO}(q) = \expect_q\brack{\log p(\xvec \mid \zvec)} - \KL\paren{q(\zvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec)}.
$$

The first term rewards latent values that explain the data. The second penalises departing from the prior. This is the
form optimised by <Gloss name="vae">variational autoencoders</Gloss> <Cite id="kingma2014" />.

**Energy plus <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink>.** Split the logarithm of the quotient:

$$
\operatorname{ELBO}(q) = \expect_q\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec)} + \entropy[q].
$$

The first term pulls $q$ towards high-probability configurations; the entropy keeps it spread out. In statistical
physics the negative of this quantity is the variational free energy.

## The unrestricted optimum is the posterior

Treat the ELBO as a functional of $q$ and optimise it subject to $\int q\, d\zvec = 1$. Its functional derivative is
$\log p(\xvec, \zvec) - \log q(\zvec) - 1$ (see
<NoteLink to="functionals-and-functional-derivatives">functional derivatives</NoteLink>). Adding a multiplier
$\lambda$ for normalisation and setting the result to zero gives

$$
\log q(\zvec) = \log p(\xvec, \zvec) + \lambda - 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(\zvec) \propto p(\xvec, \zvec),
$$

which is the posterior. Over all densities, maximising the ELBO recovers exact inference. It is useful because $q$ is
restricted to a tractable family, such as the factorised family of
<NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink> or a parametric family
$q_{\phivec}$.

## Worked example

Take the prior $z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, one observation $x \mid z \sim \Gauss(z, 1)$, and a Gaussian
$q(z) = \Gauss(m, s^2)$. Both terms of the likelihood-minus-complexity form are Gaussian expectations:

$$
\expect_q\brack{\log p(x \mid z)} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log 2\pi - \tfrac{1}{2}\brack{(x - m)^2 + s^2}, \qquad
\KL\paren{q \,\|\, p(z)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\paren{s^2 + m^2 - 1 - \log s^2}.
$$

Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of their difference with respect to $m$ to zero gives
$(x - m) - m = 0$. The derivative with respect to $s^2$ is $-\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} + 1/(2s^2)$, which is zero
when $s^2 = 1/2$. So $m = x/2$ and $s^2 = 1/2$. This is the exact posterior $\Gauss(x/2, 1/2)$, because the Gaussian
family contains it. Substituting back gives $\operatorname{ELBO} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log 4\pi - x^2/4$, which is the log
evidence $\log \Gauss(x \mid 0, 2)$: the bound is tight.

For $x = 1.5$, $m = -1$ and $s = 1.2$, the ELBO is $-5.302$, the log evidence is $-1.828$, and the KL divergence to
the posterior is $3.474$; the ELBO and the KL divergence sum to the log evidence.

## Optimising the ELBO in practice

- **Coordinate ascent.** For conditionally conjugate models, each factor of a mean-field $q$ has a closed-form update;
  see <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>.
- **Stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>.** For a parametric $q_{\phivec}$, the gradient
  $\nabla_{\phivec}\operatorname{ELBO}$ is an expectation under $q_{\phivec}$ and is estimated by sampling, either with
  the <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink> or with the
  <NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function estimator</NoteLink>.
- **<Gloss name="em-algorithm">Expectation–maximisation</Gloss>.** EM maximises the same bound, alternating between $q$ and the model parameters; see
  <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation-as-variational-inference">EM as variational inference</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Direction of the divergence.** Maximising the ELBO minimises $\KL(q \,\|\, p)$, not $\KL(p \,\|\, q)$. This reverse
  divergence is mode-seeking: $q$ avoids regions where the posterior is small and tends to underestimate its spread.
- **The bound is not the evidence.** Comparing models by their ELBOs compares lower bounds whose gaps can differ.
- **Support.** If $q$ puts mass where the posterior has none, the ELBO is $-\infty$. The family must respect the
  posterior's support, for example positive variances.
