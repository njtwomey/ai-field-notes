<Definition>
A bead slides without friction under gravity along a wire from rest at a point $A$ to a lower point $B$ that is not
directly below it. Which shape of wire makes the journey take the least time? The name is Greek for "shortest time".
</Definition>

## History

Galileo considered the question in his *Two New Sciences* of 1638. He showed that sliding along two chords of a
circle is faster than along the single chord they replace, and suggested that the arc of a circle is the fastest path.
It is not <Cite id="sussmann1997" />.

In June 1696 Johann Bernoulli posed the problem as a public challenge to the mathematicians of Europe in the journal
*Acta Eruditorum*. Solutions were published in 1697, by Johann himself, his brother Jakob, Leibniz, L'Hôpital and
Newton. Newton's arrived anonymously, and the traditional story has Johann recognising its author at once, "as the
lion by its claw" <Cite id="sussmann1997" />.

The solutions differ in method, and two of them mattered for what followed. Johann's was an analogy with
light, set out below. Jakob's was clumsier but general: it varied the curve over small pieces and asked when no
variation could shorten the time. That idea, developed by Euler in 1744 and put into its modern form by Lagrange from
1755, became the calculus of variations and the <NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation">Euler–Lagrange
equation</NoteLink> <Cite id="goldstine1980" />.

The answer, the cycloid, was already famous. Huygens had shown in 1673 that a pendulum bob moving on a cycloid takes
the same time to reach the bottom whatever height it starts from, the tautochrone property derived below, and used it
to design a more accurate pendulum clock.

## Setup

Put $A$ at the origin and $B$ at $(L, H)$, with $y$ measured downwards and $H > 0$. Describe the wire as a curve
$y(x)$ with $y(0) = 0$ and $y(L) = H$.

The bead starts at rest and there is no friction, so energy is conserved: kinetic energy gained equals potential
energy lost, $\tfrac12 m v^2 = m g y$, and the speed after dropping a height $y$ is

$$
v = \sqrt{2 g y}.
$$

The speed depends only on the depth, not on the path taken to reach it. Along an element of arc length
$ds = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$ the bead spends time $ds / v$, so the total time is the functional

$$
T[y] = \int_0^L \frac{\sqrt{1 + y'(x)^2}}{\sqrt{2 g\, y(x)}}\,dx.
$$

The problem is to minimise $T[y]$ over curves joining the two points. The two terms pull in opposite directions. A
straight line is the shortest path, which keeps $\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ small. A curve that drops steeply at first reaches a
high speed early, which keeps $1/\sqrt{y}$ small for the rest of the trip. The optimum balances the two.

## Solution by optics

Johann Bernoulli's solution treats the bead as a ray of light <Cite id="erlichson1999" />. By Fermat's principle,
light between two points follows the path of least time, and at the boundary between two layers with speeds $v_1$ and
$v_2$ it bends according to Snell's law, $\sin\alpha_1 / v_1 = \sin\alpha_2 / v_2$, with $\alpha$ the angle from the
normal to the layers.

Slice the plane into thin horizontal layers and give each layer the bead's speed at that depth, $v = \sqrt{2gy}$. A
least-time path through the layers must obey Snell's law at every boundary, so $\sin\alpha / v$ is the same constant
all along it. Here the layers are horizontal, so $\alpha$ is the angle between the curve and the vertical, and
$\sin\alpha = dx/ds = 1/\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$. The condition becomes

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,\sqrt{2 g y}} = \text{constant}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
y\paren{1 + y'^2} = 2r
$$

for some constant $2r$. As the bead goes deeper it speeds up, so the ray bends away from the vertical, towards the
horizontal, exactly as light bends entering a faster medium.

## Solution by the Euler–Lagrange equation

The integrand $F(y, y') = \sqrt{1 + y'^2} / \sqrt{2 g y}$ does not depend on $x$ explicitly. For such integrands the
Euler–Lagrange equation has a first integral, the Beltrami identity $F - y' \,\partial F/\partial y' = C$. With
$\partial F / \partial y' = y' / \paren{\sqrt{2gy}\,\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}$,

$$
F - y'\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = \frac{(1 + y'^2) - y'^2}{\sqrt{2gy}\,\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2gy}\,\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = C,
$$

which is the same equation, $y(1 + y'^2) = 2r$, that Johann reached through optics.

**The cycloid solves it.** A cycloid is the curve traced by a point on the rim of a wheel of radius $r$ rolling along
the underside of the line $y = 0$:

$$
x = r(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y = r(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad \theta \ge 0.
$$

Then $dx/d\theta = r(1 - \cos\theta)$ and $dy/d\theta = r\sin\theta$, so $y' = \sin\theta / (1 - \cos\theta)$ and

$$
1 + y'^2 = \frac{(1 - \cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta}{(1 - \cos\theta)^2} = \frac{2}{1 - \cos\theta},
\qquad
y\paren{1 + y'^2} = r(1 - \cos\theta)\cdot\frac{2}{1 - \cos\theta} = 2r.
$$

The cycloid starts at the origin with a vertical tangent: the bead first falls straight down, the fastest way to gain
speed.

**Fitting the end point.** The cycloid must pass through $(L, H)$ at some parameter $\theta_e$, so
$L = r(\theta_e - \sin\theta_e)$ and $H = r(1 - \cos\theta_e)$. Dividing eliminates $r$:

$$
\frac{\theta_e - \sin\theta_e}{1 - \cos\theta_e} = \frac{L}{H}.
$$

The left-hand side increases from $0$ to $\infty$ as $\theta_e$ runs over $(0, 2\pi)$, so this has exactly one
solution, found numerically, for example by bisection. Then $r = H / (1 - \cos\theta_e)$. When $L/H > \pi/2$, the
solution has $\theta_e > \pi$: the fastest path dips below $B$ and climbs back up to it, because the extra speed gained
lower down more than repays the climb.

**The descent time.** Along the cycloid, $ds = r\sqrt{2 - 2\cos\theta}\,d\theta$ and $v = \sqrt{2 g r(1 - \cos\theta)}$,
so $ds / v = \sqrt{r/g}\,d\theta$, a constant rate. The total time is

$$
T = \theta_e \sqrt{\frac{r}{g}}.
$$

## Worked example

Take $L = 1$ m, $H = 0.5$ m and $g = 9.81$ m/s².

- **Straight line.** The incline has length $s = \sqrt{L^2 + H^2} = 1.118$ m and constant acceleration
  $g H / s$ along it, so $s = \tfrac12 (gH/s) T^2$ and $T = s\sqrt{2/(gH)} = 0.714$ s.
- **Circular arc.** The circle through both points that leaves the origin vertically has centre $(R, 0)$ with
  $R = (L^2 + H^2)/(2L) = 0.625$ m. Integrating $ds / v$ along it numerically gives $T = 0.587$ s, which is Galileo's
  improvement on the line.
- **Cycloid.** Solving $(\theta_e - \sin\theta_e)/(1 - \cos\theta_e) = 2$ gives $\theta_e = 3.508$, just past $\pi$, and
  $r = 0.2586$ m. The time is $T = 3.508\sqrt{0.2586/9.81} = 0.570$ s, the fastest of the three. The curve reaches a
  depth of $2r = 0.517$ m before rising to $0.5$ m.

The cycloid beats the straight line by 20% and the circle by 3%. A power curve $y = H(x/L)^p$ with $p = 0.4$, which
also starts steeply, takes $0.580$ s: close, but not the minimum.

## What it illustrates

- **Optimising over functions.** The unknown is a whole curve, and the objective is an integral over it: a
  <NoteLink to="functionals-and-functional-derivatives">functional</NoteLink>. Setting its first variation to zero
  gives a differential equation, the <NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation">Euler–Lagrange equation</NoteLink>.
- **Conservation laws from symmetry.** The integrand does not depend on $x$, and that symmetry gives a conserved
  quantity, the Beltrami identity, which reduces a second-order equation to a first-order one.
- **The same mathematics in different physics.** Least time for a falling bead and least time for light obey the same
  equation. Fermat's principle is itself a variational principle, as is the principle of least action in mechanics.
- **Greedy is not optimal.** The shortest path is not the fastest. Accepting a longer route to gain speed early is
  better overall, a trade-off that reappears in optimal control <Cite id="sussmann1997" />.

## Variations

- **The tautochrone.** Release the bead from rest at any point $\theta_0$ on a cycloid, and it reaches the lowest point
  $\theta = \pi$ after the same time, $\pi\sqrt{r/g}$. Starting from $\theta_0$, the speed is
  $v = \sqrt{2g(y - y_0)} = 2\sqrt{gr}\,\sqrt{\cos^2(\theta_0/2) - \cos^2(\theta/2)}$, using
  $1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2(\theta/2)$, and $ds = 2r\sin(\theta/2)\,d\theta$. Substituting
  $u = \cos(\theta/2)/\cos(\theta_0/2)$, which runs from 1 to 0,
  $$
  T = \sqrt{\frac{r}{g}}\int_{\theta_0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin(\theta/2)\,d\theta}{\sqrt{\cos^2(\theta_0/2) - \cos^2(\theta/2)}}
  = 2\sqrt{\frac{r}{g}}\int_0^1 \frac{du}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{r}{g}},
  $$
  independent of $\theta_0$. For the worked example's cycloid this is $0.510$ s from every starting point. Huygens used
  this property to make a pendulum whose period does not depend on its swing.
- **A free end point.** If $B$ may be anywhere on a vertical line $x = L$, the time is least when the cycloid meets
  that line horizontally, at $\theta_e = \pi$. The fixed-end condition is replaced by a natural boundary condition,
  $\partial F/\partial y' = 0$ at $x = L$ <Cite id="gelfand1963" />.
- **Friction.** With friction the speed depends on the path, not only on the depth, and the optimal curve is no longer
  a cycloid. The problem then needs the more general methods of optimal control.
