<Definition>
A functional $F$ assigns a real number $F[f]$ to each function $f$ in some class, for example
$F[f] = \int_a^b f(x)^2\, dx$. The first variation of $F$ at $f$ in the direction $\eta$ is

$$
\delta F[f; \eta] = \frac{d}{d\epsilon} F[f + \epsilon\eta] \Big|_{\epsilon = 0}.
$$

The functional derivative $\delta F / \delta f$ is the function that represents the first variation as an inner
product: $\delta F[f; \eta] = \int (\delta F / \delta f)(x)\, \eta(x)\, dx$ for every admissible $\eta$
<Cite id="gelfand1963" />.
</Definition>

## From gradients to functional derivatives

For a function of $n$ variables, the directional derivative along $\vvec$ is
$\frac{d}{d\epsilon} g(\xvec + \epsilon\vvec)|_{\epsilon=0} = \sum_i (\partial g / \partial x_i)\, v_i$. The
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is the vector whose inner product with the direction gives this rate.

A function $f$ on $[a, b]$ can be seen as a vector with one coordinate $f(x)$ for each point $x$. The sum over
coordinates becomes an integral over $x$, the direction $\vvec$ becomes a perturbation function $\eta$, and the
gradient becomes the functional derivative. The value $(\delta F / \delta f)(x)$ is the sensitivity of $F$ to a change
in $f$ near the point $x$.

## Local functionals

Most functionals in practice integrate a function of $x$ and $f(x)$:

$$
F[f] = \int_a^b L\paren{x, f(x)}\, dx.
$$

Perturb $f$ by $\epsilon\eta$ and differentiate under the integral sign. The chain rule gives

$$
\frac{d}{d\epsilon} F[f + \epsilon\eta] \Big|_{\epsilon = 0}
= \int_a^b \frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\paren{x, f(x)}\, \eta(x)\, dx,
$$

so the functional derivative is the ordinary partial derivative of the integrand with respect to its second argument:

$$
\frac{\delta F}{\delta f}(x) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\paren{x, f(x)}.
$$

When the integrand also depends on $f'(x)$, the derivative picks up a second term. That case gives the
<NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation">Euler–Lagrange equation</NoteLink>.

## Examples

Each example applies the rule above with a different integrand.

- **Squared norm.** $F[f] = \int f^2\, dx$ has $L = f^2$, so $\delta F / \delta f = 2f$. This is the analogue of
  $\nabla \norm{\xvec}^2 = 2\xvec$.
- **Linear functional.** $F[f] = \int g f\, dx$ for a fixed $g$ has $\delta F / \delta f = g$, the analogue of
  $\nabla(\gvec^\top\xvec) = \gvec$.
- **Entropy.** For a density $p$, $\entropy[p] = -\int p \log p\, dx$ has $L = -p \log p$. Differentiating in $p$ gives
  $$
  \frac{\delta \entropy}{\delta p}(x) = -\log p(x) - 1.
  $$
- **Kullback–Leibler divergence.** For a fixed target $p$, $\KL(q \,\|\, p) = \int q \log(q/p)\, dx$ has
  $L = q \log q - q \log p$. Differentiating in $q$ gives
  $$
  \frac{\delta \KL}{\delta q}(x) = \log q(x) - \log p(x) + 1.
  $$
  Every variational method in Bayesian inference starts from this derivative; see
  <NoteLink to="kl-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink>.

### Worked example

Take $F[f] = \int_0^1 f(x)^2\, dx$ at $f(x) = x$, perturbed in the direction $\eta(x) = 1$. Directly,
$F[f + \epsilon\eta] = \int_0^1 (x + \epsilon)^2\, dx = 1/3 + \epsilon + \epsilon^2$, whose derivative at $\epsilon = 0$
is 1. The functional derivative gives the same number: $\int_0^1 2f\eta\, dx = \int_0^1 2x\, dx = 1$.

## Stationary functions

A function $f^*$ that minimises or maximises $F$ has zero first variation in every admissible direction:
$\delta F[f^*; \eta] = 0$. For an unconstrained problem this forces $\delta F / \delta f = 0$ at every point, by the
following lemma.

**Fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations.** If $g$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $\int_a^b g\,\eta\, dx = 0$
for every smooth $\eta$ that vanishes at $a$ and $b$, then $g = 0$ on $[a, b]$.

**Proof.** Suppose $g(x_0) > 0$ at some interior point. By continuity, $g > 0$ on an interval
$(x_0 - \delta, x_0 + \delta)$. Choose a smooth bump $\eta$ that is positive on that interval and zero elsewhere, for
example $\eta(x) = \exp\paren{-1/(\delta^2 - (x - x_0)^2)}$ inside the interval. Then $\int g\,\eta\, dx > 0$, a
contradiction. The case $g(x_0) < 0$ is the same with $-\eta$. Continuity extends $g = 0$ to the endpoints.

The lemma turns a condition on integrals into a condition at every point, which is what makes the Euler–Lagrange
equation and the optimal factors of <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational
inference</NoteLink> pointwise equations.

## Constraints

Densities must integrate to 1, so perturbations of a density must satisfy $\int \eta\, dx = 0$. The lemma then only
gives $\delta F / \delta p = $ constant. A Lagrange multiplier accounts for the constraint; see
<NoteLink to="constrained-variational-problems">constrained variational problems</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Admissible perturbations.** The class of $\eta$ matters. Fixed boundary values require $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$;
  densities require $\int \eta = 0$ and $p + \epsilon\eta \ge 0$. Too small a class gives too weak a condition.
- **Stationary is not optimal.** Zero first variation holds at minima, maxima and saddle functions alike. The second
  variation, or convexity of $F$, decides which.
- **Existence.** A functional bounded below need not attain its infimum. Minimising sequences can converge to a
  function outside the admissible class.
