<Definition>
For a model $p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)$ with latent variables $\zvec$ and parameters $\thetab$, define the bound

$$
\Lcal(q, \thetab) = \expect_q\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)} + \entropy[q].
$$

Expectation–maximisation (EM) starts from $\thetab^{(0)}$ and repeats two steps <Cite id="dempster1977" />:

- **E-step:** $q^{(t)}(\zvec) = p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab^{(t)})$;
- **M-step:** $\thetab^{(t+1)} = \argmax_{\thetab}\ \expect_{q^{(t)}}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)}$.

Each step maximises $\Lcal$ over one of its two arguments, so EM is coordinate ascent on the evidence lower bound
<Cite id="neal1998" />.
</Definition>

This note develops EM from the variational side. The
<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation–maximisation</NoteLink> note covers the algorithm itself: worked
examples, the bound drawn step by step, its limit, the rate of convergence and the classic variants.

## The decomposition behind EM

The <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink> decomposition holds for every $\thetab$:

$$
\log p(\xvec \mid \thetab) = \Lcal(q, \thetab) + \KL\paren{q(\zvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab)}.
$$

The log-likelihood that EM maximises is the bound plus a non-negative gap. The gap depends on both $q$ and $\thetab$.

## The E-step closes the gap

With $\thetab = \thetab^{(t)}$ fixed, $\log p(\xvec \mid \thetab^{(t)})$ does not depend on $q$. Maximising $\Lcal$ over
$q$ is therefore the same as minimising the <Gloss name="kl" /> term, which is zero when $q$ is the posterior under the current
parameters. After the E-step,

$$
\Lcal\paren{q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t)}} = \log p\paren{\xvec \mid \thetab^{(t)}}.
$$

The bound touches the log-likelihood at the current parameters.

## The M-step raises the bound

With $q = q^{(t)}$ fixed, the <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> $\entropy[q^{(t)}]$ does not depend on $\thetab$. Maximising $\Lcal$ over
$\thetab$ is therefore maximising the expected complete-data log-likelihood
$Q(\thetab) = \expect_{q^{(t)}}[\log p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)]$, which is the M-step. For models whose
complete-data likelihood is an <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink>, this has a closed form: the usual maximum-likelihood estimates with
counts replaced by expected counts.

## Why the likelihood never decreases

Chain the two facts with the decomposition at the new parameters:

$$
\log p\paren{\xvec \mid \thetab^{(t+1)}}
= \Lcal\paren{q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t+1)}} + \KL\paren{q^{(t)} \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab^{(t+1)})}
\ge \Lcal\paren{q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t+1)}}
\ge \Lcal\paren{q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t)}}
= \log p\paren{\xvec \mid \thetab^{(t)}}.
$$

The first inequality holds because the KL divergence is non-negative. The second holds because the M-step maximises
$\Lcal$ over $\thetab$, so it does at least as well as the old value. The last equality is the E-step's tight bound.
The argument needs only $\Lcal(q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t+1)}) \ge \Lcal(q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t)})$, so any M-step that
merely increases $Q$ also works. That variant is generalised EM.

## Worked example

Fit the means $\mu_1, \mu_2$ of a two-component mixture with unit variances and equal weights to the data
$x = (-2, -1, 1, 3)$, starting from $\mu = (-0.5, 0.5)$. The log-likelihood at the start is $-10.146$.

**E-step.** Each responsibility is the posterior probability of a component,
$r_{i1} = \Gauss(x_i \mid \mu_1, 1) / \brack{\Gauss(x_i \mid \mu_1, 1) + \Gauss(x_i \mid \mu_2, 1)}$. For the four points
$r_{i1} = (0.881, 0.731, 0.269, 0.047)$.

**M-step.** The expected complete-data log-likelihood is
$\sum_i \sum_k r_{ik}\brack{-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i - \mu_k)^2} + \text{const}$. Setting its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> in $\mu_k$ to zero gives
the responsibility-weighted mean, $\mu_k = \sum_i r_{ik} x_i / \sum_i r_{ik}$, so $\mu = (-1.080, 1.487)$.

**Check.** At the new means the bound with the old responsibilities is $\Lcal = -8.813$ and the log-likelihood is
$-7.967$. The ordering $-10.146 \le -8.813 \le -7.967$ is the chain of inequalities above. Iterating to convergence gives
$\mu = (-1.397, 2.028)$ and a log-likelihood of $-7.607$. The
<NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> note steps through the same algorithm with full
covariances.

## Variants

The full catalogue of variants is in <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation–maximisation</NoteLink>. Three
follow directly from the variational view.

- **Partial E-steps.** Any $q$ that increases $\Lcal$ keeps the monotonicity argument, so the E-step can update the
  responsibilities of one data point at a time. This is incremental EM <Cite id="neal1998" />.
- **Variational EM.** When the exact posterior is intractable, restrict $q$ to a family such as the factorised one of
  <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>. The E-step no longer
  closes the gap, and the algorithm maximises the bound rather than the likelihood.
- **Variational Bayes.** Treating $\thetab$ as a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> with a prior, and giving it its own factor in $q$,
  replaces the M-step point estimate by a posterior factor. Every step is then a mean-field update.

## Pitfalls

- **Local optima.** Monotonicity guarantees convergence of the likelihood values, not a global maximum. Mixture models
  have many local optima and are usually fitted from several starts.
- **Degenerate solutions.** For mixtures with free variances, the likelihood is unbounded: a component can shrink onto a
  single point. EM can follow such a path.
- **Slow convergence.** When the posterior over $\zvec$ is very uncertain, each step moves $\thetab$ only a little. The
  rate is linear, set by the fraction of missing information.
