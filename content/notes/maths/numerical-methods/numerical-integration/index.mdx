<Definition>
A **quadrature rule** with nodes $x_1, \dots, x_n \in [a, b]$ and weights $w_1, \dots, w_n$ approximates an integral by

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx Q_n(f) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_if(x_i).
$$

Its **degree of exactness** is the largest $d$ such that $Q_n(p) = \int_a^b p$ for every polynomial $p$ of degree at
most $d$. A **composite rule** splits $[a, b]$ into $m$ panels of width $h = (b - a)/m$ and applies a simple rule on
each <Cite id="suli2003" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most integrals have no closed form. A quadrature rule samples $f$ at a few points and integrates a simple function
that matches those samples: a piecewise-constant, piecewise-linear or piecewise-quadratic interpolant, or a polynomial
through well-chosen points. The error is the integral of the interpolation error. Smooth functions are well
approximated by polynomials, so their integrals converge fast. Kinks, singularities and oscillation slow every rule
down.

Two levers control accuracy: more points, and better-placed points. Newton–Cotes rules fix equally spaced points and
raise the number of panels. Gaussian rules choose the points themselves, and gain twice the degree of exactness for the
same number of evaluations.

## The trapezoid rule

On one panel $[x_0, x_0 + h]$, replace $f$ by the straight line through its endpoint values. The integral of the line
is the area of a trapezoid:

$$
\int_{x_0}^{x_0 + h}f(x)\,dx \approx \frac h2\paren{f(x_0) + f(x_0 + h)}.
$$

**Error of one panel.** Shift so that $x_0 = 0$. The linear interpolant $p$ satisfies, for $f$ twice continuously
differentiable, $f(x) - p(x) = \frac12 f''(\xi_x)\,x(x - h)$ for some $\xi_x \in (0, h)$. This is the standard
<NoteLink to="polynomial-interpolation-and-runge">interpolation error formula</NoteLink>. The factor $x(x - h)$ does not change sign on $[0, h]$, so the <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean value theorem</NoteLink> for
integrals gives a single $\xi$ with

$$
\int_0^h (f - p)\,dx = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}\int_0^h x(x - h)\,dx = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}\paren{-\frac{h^3}{6}} =
-\frac{h^3}{12}f''(\xi).
$$

**Composite rule.** With $m$ panels, nodes $x_j = a + jh$ and $f_j = f(x_j)$,

$$
T_h = h\paren{\tfrac12 f_0 + f_1 + \dots + f_{m-1} + \tfrac12 f_m}.
$$

Summing the $m$ panel errors, and replacing the average of the $f''(\xi_j)$ by one value $f''(\xi)$ (possible because
$f''$ is continuous),

$$
\int_a^b f\,dx - T_h = -\frac{(b - a)h^2}{12}f''(\xi), \qquad \xi \in (a, b).
$$

The error is $O(h^2)$: doubling the number of panels divides it by 4. The rule is exact for straight lines, so its
degree of exactness is 1.

## Simpson's rule

Simpson's rule fits a parabola through three equally spaced points. On $[-h, h]$,

$$
\int_{-h}^h f(x)\,dx \approx \frac h3\paren{f(-h) + 4f(0) + f(h)}.
$$

**Error.** Expand $f$ in its <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> about 0. Odd powers integrate to zero over $[-h, h]$ and cancel in the
symmetric rule, so only even terms matter. The exact integral is

$$
\int_{-h}^h f = 2hf(0) + \frac{h^3}{3}f''(0) + \frac{h^5}{60}f^{(4)}(0) + O(h^7),
$$

and the rule gives

$$
\frac h3\paren{6f(0) + h^2f''(0) + \frac{h^4}{12}f^{(4)}(0) + O(h^6)} = 2hf(0) + \frac{h^3}{3}f''(0) +
\frac{h^5}{36}f^{(4)}(0) + O(h^7).
$$

The difference is $h^5(1/60 - 1/36)f^{(4)}(0) = -\frac{h^5}{90}f^{(4)}(0)$. The quadratic interpolant happens to
integrate cubics exactly, by symmetry, so the degree of exactness is 3 rather than 2. The composite rule on an even
number $m$ of panels has error

$$
\int_a^b f\,dx - S_h = -\frac{(b - a)h^4}{180}f^{(4)}(\xi).
$$

Doubling the number of panels divides the error by 16.

**Richardson extrapolation.** The trapezoid error has the form $c_2h^2 + c_4h^4 + \dots$. Combining two step sizes to
cancel the $h^2$ term gives $(4T_{h/2} - T_h)/3$, which is exactly Simpson's rule. Repeating the cancellation for
$h^4$, $h^6$ and so on is **Romberg integration**.

<Derivation title="Euler–Maclaurin: why the trapezoid rule excels for periodic functions">
The Euler–Maclaurin formula expands the trapezoid error of a smooth $f$ as

$$
T_h - \int_a^b f\,dx = \sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{B_{2j}h^{2j}}{(2j)!}\paren{f^{(2j-1)}(b) - f^{(2j-1)}(a)} + O(h^{2r+2}),
$$

where $B_{2j}$ are Bernoulli numbers ($B_2 = 1/6$, which recovers the leading term $\frac{h^2}{12}(f'(b) - f'(a))$).
Every term involves only the difference of odd <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> at the two ends. If $f$ is smooth and periodic with period
$b - a$, all those differences vanish, and the error is smaller than any power of $h$. For analytic periodic functions
it decays geometrically in the number of points <Cite id="trefethen2008" />.
</Derivation>

## Gaussian quadrature

An $n$-point rule has $2n$ free parameters, $n$ nodes and $n$ weights. Matching the integrals of $1, x, \dots,
x^{2n-1}$ gives $2n$ equations, so one can hope for degree of exactness $2n - 1$. Gauss–Legendre quadrature achieves
it on $[-1, 1]$.

**Construction.** Let $P_n$ be the Legendre polynomial of degree $n$: the polynomials $P_0, P_1, \dots$ are orthogonal
on $[-1, 1]$, $\int_{-1}^1 P_jP_k\,dx = 0$ for $j \ne k$. Take the nodes $x_i$ to be the $n$ roots of $P_n$, which are
real, distinct and inside $(-1, 1)$. Take the weights so that the rule integrates every polynomial of degree at most
$n - 1$ exactly (the rule is interpolatory).

**Exactness to degree $2n - 1$.** Let $p$ have degree at most $2n - 1$. Divide by $P_n$: $p = qP_n + r$, with $q$ and
$r$ of degree at most $n - 1$. Then

$$
\int_{-1}^1 p\,dx = \int_{-1}^1 qP_n\,dx + \int_{-1}^1 r\,dx = 0 + \sum_i w_ir(x_i) = \sum_i w_ip(x_i).
$$

The first integral vanishes because $P_n$ is orthogonal to every polynomial of lower degree. The rule is exact for
$r$ by construction. At the nodes $P_n(x_i) = 0$, so $p(x_i) = r(x_i)$. No $n$-point rule can do better: the
polynomial $\prod_i(x - x_i)^2$ has degree $2n$ and positive integral, but vanishes at every node.

The weights of Gauss rules are all positive, which makes them numerically stable. Nodes and weights can be computed
as the eigenvalues and first eigenvector components of a symmetric tridiagonal matrix <Cite id="golub1969" />.

Changing the weight function gives other families of Gauss rules. Gauss–Hermite quadrature approximates
$\int e^{-x^2}f(x)\,dx$ and therefore <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> under a Gaussian.
Gauss–Laguerre handles $\int_0^\infty e^{-x}f(x)\,dx$.

## Worked example: $\int_0^1 e^x\,dx$

The exact value is $e - 1 = 1.718281828$. With $m = 4$ panels ($h = 1/4$, five evaluations):

- **Trapezoid:** $T = 1.727222$, error $8.94 \times 10^{-3}$. The leading-order prediction is
  $\frac{h^2}{12}(f'(1) - f'(0)) = \frac{1}{192}(e - 1) = 8.95 \times 10^{-3}$.
- **Simpson:** $S = 1.718319$, error $3.70 \times 10^{-5}$. The prediction is
  $\frac{h^4}{180}(f'''(1) - f'''(0)) = 3.73 \times 10^{-5}$.
- **Gauss–Legendre** with 3 points: error $8.2 \times 10^{-7}$, from only three evaluations. With 5 points the error is
  $6.5 \times 10^{-13}$.

Doubling to $m = 8$ divides the trapezoid error by $4.0$ and the Simpson error by $15.9$, as the $h^2$ and $h^4$ laws
predict.

## Many dimensions

A product rule with $n$ points per axis needs $n^d$ evaluations in $d$ dimensions. With $N$ total points, a rule of
order $h^r$ has error $O(N^{-r/d})$, which deteriorates quickly as $d$ grows.
<NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo integration</NoteLink> has error $O(N^{-1/2})$ in any dimension. It
beats product rules when $d > 2r$, roughly beyond a handful of dimensions. Sparse grids and quasi-Monte Carlo sit in
between.

## In machine learning

- **Expectations under Gaussians.** <Gloss name="vi">Variational inference</Gloss> and <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification">Gaussian-process classification</NoteLink> need
  $\expect_{\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)}[g(x)]$ for one-dimensional non-linear $g$, such as a log-sigmoid likelihood.
  Gauss–Hermite quadrature with 10 to 20 points computes these to high accuracy.
- **Normalising constants.** One- and two-dimensional posteriors can be normalised by quadrature on a grid. It is
  exact enough to check a <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> or an <Gloss name="mcmc" /> run.
- **Adaptive integrators.** Library routines such as SciPy's `quad` (QUADPACK) subdivide panels where an error
  estimate is large. They handle kinks and integrable singularities automatically.

## Pitfalls

- **Smoothness assumptions.** The $O(h^2)$ and $O(h^4)$ laws need bounded second and fourth derivatives. For $\sqrt x$
  on $[0, 1]$ both rules converge only as $h^{1.5}$.
- **Equally spaced high-order rules.** Newton–Cotes rules of high degree have negative weights and can diverge as the
  degree grows, as for Runge's function $1/(1 + 25x^2)$. Raise the number of panels, not the degree.
- **Infinite intervals.** Truncating $\int_0^\infty$ at an arbitrary point, or applying a rule for finite intervals
  after a careless substitution, loses the tail. Use a <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink> or a Gauss rule with the matching weight.
