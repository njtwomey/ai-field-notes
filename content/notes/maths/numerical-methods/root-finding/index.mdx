<Definition>
Given a continuous function $f : \reals \to \reals$, **root finding** computes an $x^*$ with $f(x^*) = 0$ by
generating iterates $x_0, x_1, \dots$ that converge to $x^*$. A method has **order of convergence** $p \ge 1$ if the
errors $e_k = x_k - x^*$ satisfy

$$
\lim_{k \to \infty}\frac{\abs{e_{k+1}}}{\abs{e_k}^p} = C
$$

for a constant $0 < C < \infty$ (with $C < 1$ required when $p = 1$). Order 1 is **linear** convergence and order 2
**quadratic** convergence <Cite id="suli2003" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most equations have no closed-form solution. Iterative methods trade exactness for a sequence that approaches the
root. Two ideas dominate. Bracketing traps the root in an interval and shrinks the interval; it is slow but cannot
fail. Local models replace $f$ near the current guess by something solvable, such as its <NoteLink to="derivative">tangent line</NoteLink>, and jump to
that model's root; they are fast but need a good start.

The order of convergence measures speed in digits. <NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">Linear convergence</NoteLink> adds a fixed number of correct digits per
step. Quadratic convergence doubles the number of correct digits per step.

## Bisection

If $f$ is continuous and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, the intermediate value theorem guarantees a root in
$[a, b]$. Bisection evaluates $f$ at the midpoint $m = (a + b)/2$ and keeps the half on which the sign still changes.

After $k$ steps the bracket has width $(b - a)/2^k$, and its midpoint is within $(b - a)/2^{k+1}$ of a root. The error
bound halves each step: linear convergence with rate $1/2$, about $0.3$ decimal digits per step. Reaching a tolerance
$\tau$ takes

$$
k = \left\lceil\log_2\frac{b - a}{\tau}\right\rceil
$$

steps, whatever $f$ is. From $[1, 2]$ with $\tau = 10^{-10}$, finding $\sqrt 2$ as the root of $x^2 - 2$ takes 34
steps. Bisection uses only the sign of $f$, so it cannot find a root where $f$ touches zero without crossing, such as
the double root of $(x - 1)^2$.

## Newton's method

<NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> replaces $f$ by its tangent line at $x_k$, $f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x - x_k)$, and takes the tangent's root
as the next iterate:

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}.
$$

**Quadratic convergence.** Suppose $f$ is twice continuously differentiable near a simple root $x^*$, meaning
$f'(x^*) \ne 0$. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder expands $f(x^*)$ around $x_k$:

$$
0 = f(x^*) = f(x_k) + f'(x_k)(x^* - x_k) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi_k)(x^* - x_k)^2
$$

for some $\xi_k$ between $x_k$ and $x^*$. Divide by $f'(x_k)$ and rearrange, using the Newton update
$x_k - f(x_k)/f'(x_k) = x_{k+1}$:

$$
x_{k+1} - x^* = \frac{f''(\xi_k)}{2f'(x_k)}(x_k - x^*)^2.
$$

So $e_{k+1} \approx C e_k^2$ with $C = f''(x^*)/(2f'(x^*))$. Once $\abs{Ce_k} < 1$, the error squares at every step.
The result is local. It holds once $x_k$ is close enough that $f'$ stays away from 0 and $\abs{Ce_k}$ is small.

**Worked example: $\sqrt 2$.** For $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ the update is $x_{k+1} = (x_k + 2/x_k)/2$, the Babylonian method.
From $x_0 = 1$:

| $k$ | $x_k$              | $\abs{x_k - \sqrt 2}$   |
| --- | ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| 1   | 1.5                | $8.6 \times 10^{-2}$    |
| 2   | 1.416666666666667  | $2.5 \times 10^{-3}$    |
| 3   | 1.414215686274510  | $2.1 \times 10^{-6}$    |
| 4   | 1.414213562374690  | $1.6 \times 10^{-12}$   |
| 5   | 1.414213562373095  | $0$ (to <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">double precision</NoteLink>) |

Here $C = 2/(2 \cdot 2\sqrt 2) = 0.354$. The error ratios $e_{k+1}/e_k^2$ are $0.33$, $0.35$ and $0.35$, as predicted.

**Multiple roots.** At a root of multiplicity $m > 1$, $f'(x^*) = 0$ and the argument above fails. For
$f(x) = (x - x^*)^m g(x)$ with $g(x^*) \ne 0$, the Newton step gives $e_{k+1} \approx (1 - 1/m)e_k$: linear
convergence. For $(x - 1)^2$ from $x_0 = 2$ the iterates are $1.5, 1.25, 1.125, \dots$, halving the error each step.

**Failure modes.** Far from a root the tangent can point anywhere.

- For $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$ from $x_0 = 0$ the iterates cycle $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$ forever.
- For $f(x) = \arctan x$ from $x_0 = 1.5$ the iterates are $-1.69, 2.32, -5.11, 32.3, \dots$ and diverge. From
  $x_0 = 1.3$ they converge to 0.
- If $f'(x_k) = 0$ the step is undefined.

**Several variables.** For $\fvec : \reals^n \to \reals^n$, the tangent line becomes the
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> $\Jmat(\xvec_k)$, and each step solves the linear system
$\Jmat(\xvec_k)\Delta\xvec = -\fvec(\xvec_k)$ and sets $\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k + \Delta\xvec$. Minimising a smooth
function by Newton's method is root finding on its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, with the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> as the
Jacobian <Cite id="nocedal2006" />. Convergence is again quadratic near a root with a non-singular Jacobian.

## The secant method

The secant method replaces $f'(x_k)$ by the slope through the last two iterates:

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - f(x_k)\frac{x_k - x_{k-1}}{f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})}.
$$

It needs no derivative and one new evaluation of $f$ per step.

**Order $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2$.** A <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> of the same kind as for Newton gives, near a simple root,

$$
e_{k+1} \approx C e_ke_{k-1}, \qquad C = \frac{f''(x^*)}{2f'(x^*)}.
$$

Suppose $\abs{e_{k+1}} \approx A\abs{e_k}^p$ for some order $p$. Then $\abs{e_{k-1}} \approx (\abs{e_k}/A)^{1/p}$.
Substituting into the recurrence, $A\abs{e_k}^p \approx \abs C\abs{e_k}\,(\abs{e_k}/A)^{1/p}$. Matching the powers of
$\abs{e_k}$ requires $p = 1 + 1/p$, that is $p^2 = p + 1$. The positive root is $p = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2 \approx 1.618$,
the golden ratio.

From $x_0 = 1$ and $x_1 = 2$ for $x^2 - 2$, the errors are $8.1 \times 10^{-2}$, $1.4 \times 10^{-2}$,
$4.2 \times 10^{-4}$, $2.1 \times 10^{-6}$, $3.2 \times 10^{-10}$ and then $2 \times 10^{-16}$. Each exponent is roughly the
sum of the previous two, as $e_{k+1} \approx Ce_ke_{k-1}$ predicts.

**Cost per evaluation.** Newton needs $f$ and $f'$ each step; the secant method needs only $f$. If a derivative costs
as much as a function value, two secant steps cost the same as one Newton step and improve the order to
$\varphi^2 = 2.618 > 2$. Per function evaluation, the secant method is faster.

## Safeguarded methods

Practical solvers combine the reliability of bracketing with the speed of local models. **Brent's method** keeps a
sign-changing bracket and tries a secant or inverse quadratic interpolation step. It falls back to bisection whenever
the fast step leaves the bracket or does not shrink it enough <Cite id="press2007" />. It converges whenever bisection
does, and superlinearly near a simple root. It is the default scalar root finder in most libraries, for example
SciPy's `brentq`.

## In machine learning

- **Newton's method for logistic regression.** Fitting <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>
  by Newton's method on the gradient of the log-likelihood (<Gloss name="irls">iteratively reweighted least squares</Gloss>) converges in a
  handful of iterations.
- **Quantiles.** <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">Inverse transform sampling</NoteLink> needs $F^{-1}(u)$.
  When the distribution function $F$ has no closed-form inverse, $F(x) - u = 0$ is solved by bisection or Newton's
  method.
- **Confidence bounds.** The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-upper-confidence-bound">KL-UCB</NoteLink> index is the root of a monotone equation in the
  arm's mean, found by bisection or Newton's method.
- **Implicit layers.** Deep equilibrium models define a layer's output as a root of $\zvec - g(\zvec, \xvec) = 0$ and
  solve it with Newton-type methods.

## Pitfalls

- **Stopping on $\abs{f(x_k)}$ alone.** A flat function has small values far from its root. Stop when both the step
  $\abs{x_{k+1} - x_k}$ and $\abs{f(x_k)}$ are small.
- **Attainable accuracy.** Near a root, $f(x)$ is computed with an absolute error of about $u$ times the size of its
  terms. No method can locate the root more accurately than about that error divided by $\abs{f'(x^*)}$. At a double
  root the attainable accuracy is only about $\sqrt u$.
- **Unsafeguarded Newton.** Newton's method from an arbitrary start can cycle or diverge. Use a bracket, a <NoteLink to="line-search">line
  search</NoteLink> or a <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods">trust region</NoteLink> when the start is not known to be close.
