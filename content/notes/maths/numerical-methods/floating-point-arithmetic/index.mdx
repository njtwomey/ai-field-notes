<Definition>
A binary **floating-point number** with precision $p$ is

$$
x = (-1)^s \times (1.b_1b_2\dots b_{p-1})_2 \times 2^e,
$$

with sign bit $s$, significand bits $b_i$ and an integer exponent $e$ in a fixed range $[e_{\min}, e_{\max}]$. The
**IEEE 754** standard fixes the formats and requires every basic operation ($+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$, $\sqrt{\ }$)
to return the exact result rounded to a representable number, by default to the nearest <Cite id="ieee2019" />. The
**machine epsilon** $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} = 2^{1-p}$ is the gap between 1 and the next larger number, and the **unit
roundoff** is $u = \epsilon_{\text{mach}}/2 = 2^{-p}$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A floating-point number is scientific notation in base 2 with a fixed number of significant digits. Double precision
keeps 53 significant bits, about 16 decimal digits, whether the number is $10^{-300}$ or $10^{300}$. The absolute
spacing between neighbouring numbers therefore grows with their size, while the relative spacing stays near
$10^{-16}$.

Every operation rounds its result to the nearest representable number. One rounding is harmless. Trouble comes from
steps that turn small absolute errors into large relative ones. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers is the main
culprit.

## The formats

| Format                | Precision $p$ | $\epsilon_{\text{mach}}$           | Largest                | Smallest normal         |
| --------------------- | ------------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ----------------------- |
| binary64 (double)     | 53            | $2^{-52} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ | $1.8 \times 10^{308}$ | $2.2 \times 10^{-308}$ |
| binary32 (single)     | 24            | $2^{-23} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$  | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$  | $1.2 \times 10^{-38}$  |
| binary16 (half)       | 11            | $2^{-10} \approx 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$  | $65\,504$             | $6.1 \times 10^{-5}$   |
| bfloat16              | 8             | $2^{-7} \approx 7.8 \times 10^{-3}$   | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$  | $1.2 \times 10^{-38}$  |

The leading bit of a normal significand is always 1, so it is not stored: double precision stores 52 fraction bits, an
11-bit exponent and a sign. bfloat16 keeps the 8-bit exponent of single precision and cuts the significand to 8 bits,
trading precision for range. Below the smallest normal number, **subnormal** numbers drop the leading 1 and fill the
gap down to 0 with evenly spaced values, at reduced precision. Overflow gives $\pm\infty$, and invalid operations such
as $0/0$ or $\infty - \infty$ give NaN ("not a number").

## Rounding error

**Representation.** Take a real $x$ with $2^e \le \abs x < 2^{e+1}$, inside the normal range. The representable numbers
in that interval are spaced $2^{e-p+1}$ apart. Rounding to the nearest moves $x$ by at most half a spacing,
$2^{e-p}$. Relative to $\abs x \ge 2^e$, the error is at most $2^{-p} = u$. So the rounded value is

$$
\operatorname{fl}(x) = x(1 + \delta), \qquad \abs\delta \le u.
$$

**Operations.** Because IEEE 754 rounds the exact result of each operation, the same bound holds for every basic
operation. This is the **standard model** of floating-point arithmetic <Cite id="higham2002" />:

$$
\operatorname{fl}(x \mathbin{\text{op}} y) = (x \mathbin{\text{op}} y)(1 + \delta), \qquad \abs\delta \le u,
\qquad \text{op} \in \set{+, -, \times, \div}.
$$

Error analysis of an algorithm chains these factors. A product of $n$ numbers carries at most $n - 1$ factors
$(1 + \delta_i)$, so its relative error is at most about $(n - 1)u$. Rounding errors of a long computation usually
partly cancel, so the typical error grows more like $\sqrt n\,u$.

**Decimal fractions are not exact.** The number $0.1$ has the infinite binary expansion $0.000110011001\dots_2$. The
nearest double is $0.1000000000000000055511\dots$, and $0.1 + 0.2$ evaluates to $0.30000000000000004$, which is not the
double nearest to $0.3$. Floating-point results should be compared with a tolerance, never with $=$.

**Arithmetic is not associative.** In double precision $(0.1 + 0.2) + 0.3 = 0.6000000000000001$ while
$0.1 + (0.2 + 0.3) = 0.6$. The order of a sum changes its value. Parallel reductions on <Gloss name="gpu" plural /> add in an order that can
vary from run to run, which makes results non-deterministic in the last bits.

## Catastrophic cancellation

Subtraction of exact numbers is exact up to one rounding. The danger is subtracting numbers that already carry errors.
Suppose $\hat a = a(1 + \delta_a)$ and $\hat b = b(1 + \delta_b)$ with $\abs{\delta_a}, \abs{\delta_b} \le \delta$.
Then

$$
\frac{\abs{(\hat a - \hat b) - (a - b)}}{\abs{a - b}} = \frac{\abs{a\delta_a - b\delta_b}}{\abs{a - b}} \le
\delta\,\frac{\abs a + \abs b}{\abs{a - b}}.
$$

The factor $(\abs a + \abs b)/\abs{a - b}$ is the relative condition number of subtraction. When $a \approx b$ it is
huge: the leading digits cancel, and what remains is dominated by the earlier errors. The subtraction itself did
nothing wrong. It exposed errors that were already there.

The remedy is to rearrange the formula so that the nearly equal quantities are never subtracted.

## Worked examples

**A small root of a quadratic.** The roots of $x^2 - 10^8x + 1 = 0$ are about $10^8$ and $10^{-8}$. The textbook
formula for the small root, $(10^8 - \sqrt{10^{16} - 4})/2$, subtracts two numbers that agree to 16 digits. In double
precision it returns $7.45 \times 10^{-9}$, with a relative error of 25%. The product of the roots equals the constant
term, $x_1x_2 = 1$. Computing the large root with the formula, which adds rather than subtracts, and then setting
$x_2 = 1/x_1$ gives $10^{-8}$ to full precision.

**$(1 - \cos x)/x^2$ near 0.** The true value tends to $1/2$. At $x = 10^{-8}$, $\cos x = 1 - 5 \times 10^{-17}$ rounds
to exactly 1, and the naive formula returns 0. The identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$ removes the subtraction and
gives $0.5$.

**Sample variance in one pass.** The formula $s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\paren{\sum_i x_i^2 - \frac1n\paren{\sum_i x_i}^2}$
subtracts two large, nearly equal sums. For the four values $10^8 + \set{4, 7, 13, 16}$, whose variance is exactly 30,
it returns $29.33$ in double precision. The two-pass formula $\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(x_i - \bar x)^2$ subtracts the mean
first and returns $30.0$. Welford's one-pass update achieves the same accuracy without storing the data
<Cite id="chan1983" />.

**Summing many numbers.** Adding $0.1$ to a single-precision accumulator ten million times gives $1\,087\,937$
instead of $10^6$. Once the running total is large, each addition of $0.1$ is rounded to a multiple of the total's
spacing, and the errors accumulate in one direction. Summing pairwise (as NumPy does) gives $1\,000\,000.1$. Kahan's
compensated summation carries the rounding error of each addition in a second variable and adds it back, which makes
the error independent of $n$ to first order <Cite id="kahan1965" />.

## Overflow and underflow

$e^x$ overflows in double precision for $x > 709.78$. A softmax over logits $(1000, 1001, 1002)$ computed directly
evaluates $e^{1000} = \infty$ and returns NaN. Subtracting the largest logit first leaves the result unchanged
mathematically and keeps every exponent at most 0. The same shift makes $\log\sum_i e^{z_i}$ safe; see
<NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax and log-sum-exp</NoteLink>. For these logits it gives $1002.408$.

Probabilities of long sequences underflow: a product of 1000 probabilities around $0.1$ is $10^{-1000}$, below the
smallest double. Working with log-probabilities turns the product into a sum and avoids underflow. Library functions
`log1p(x)` and `expm1(x)` compute $\log(1 + x)$ and $e^x - 1$ accurately for small $x$, where the direct formulas
cancel.

## In machine learning

- **Mixed precision.** <NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">Mixed-precision training</NoteLink> in 16-bit formats
  halves memory and speeds up <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix multiplication</NoteLink>. Weights are kept in single precision, because an update smaller
  than $\epsilon_{\text{mach}}/2$ times a weight rounds away entirely in half precision.
- **Loss scaling.** Small <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> underflow to zero in binary16. Multiplying the loss by a large factor before
  back-propagation, and dividing the gradients afterwards, keeps them in range. bfloat16 has the range of single
  precision and needs no loss scaling.
- **Stable primitives.** Log-softmax, log-sum-exp, cross-entropy from logits and `logaddexp` are provided as fused,
  stable operations because their naive forms overflow or cancel.

## Pitfalls

- **Testing equality.** Replace `a == b` with $\abs{a - b} \le \tau\max(\abs a, \abs b)$ for a tolerance $\tau$ chosen
  from the expected error.
- **Blaming the last operation.** Cancellation reveals earlier errors; the fix is usually to reformulate an earlier
  step.
- **Assuming more digits fix everything.** An ill-conditioned problem loses the same number of digits at any precision.
  Higher precision only postpones the loss; see
  <NoteLink to="numerical-stability-and-conditioning">numerical stability and conditioning</NoteLink>.
