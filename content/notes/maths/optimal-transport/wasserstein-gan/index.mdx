<Definition>
Let $p_{\text{data}}$ be the data distribution and $p_{\thetavec}$ the distribution of $G_{\thetavec}(\zvec)$ for noise
$\zvec \sim p(\zvec)$. A **Wasserstein GAN** trains a **critic** $f_{\wvec} : \reals^d \to \reals$, constrained to be
1-Lipschitz, and a generator $G_{\thetavec}$ on

$$
\min_{\thetavec}\ \max_{\wvec :\, \norm{f_{\wvec}}_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1}\
\expect_{\xvec \sim p_{\text{data}}}\brack{f_{\wvec}(\xvec)} - \expect_{\zvec \sim p(\zvec)}\brack{f_{\wvec}\paren{G_{\thetavec}(\zvec)}}
$$

<Cite id="arjovsky2017" />. By Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality the inner maximum over all 1-Lipschitz functions is
$W_1(p_{\text{data}}, p_{\thetavec})$, so the generator minimises an estimate of the Wasserstein-1 distance.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The discriminator of a standard <NoteLink to="generative-adversarial-network">GAN</NoteLink> is a classifier. Once real
and generated samples are separable, a good classifier outputs 1 on the data and 0 on the samples, and it is flat
around both. Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> tells the generator nothing about where the data are. The WGAN critic is not a classifier. It
is a potential whose slope is bounded by 1, and its best value separates the two sample sets by as much as a slope-1
function can: it rises steadily from the generated samples towards the data. The generator follows that slope. The
critic's objective is also an estimate of a distance, so it falls as the samples improve, which the standard GAN loss
does not do.

## Continuity of $W_1$ versus Jensen–Shannon

**Parallel lines.** Let $Z \sim \Unif(0, 1)$, let the data be the vertical segment $(0, Z)$ in the plane, and let the
generator produce $(\theta, Z)$ <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />. For $\theta \ne 0$ the two segments are disjoint.

- Coupling each point $(0, z)$ with $(\theta, z)$ moves every point by $\abs{\theta}$, and no coupling moves mass less
  on average, since the first coordinates differ by $\theta$ for every pair. So $W_1 = \abs{\theta}$.
- The supports do not overlap, so the <Gloss name="js">Jensen–Shannon divergence</Gloss> is $\log 2$, the Kullback–Leibler divergence is $\infty$ and the
  total variation is 1, for every $\theta \ne 0$.

$W_1$ gives the gradient $\operatorname{sgn}\theta$, which points the generator towards the data. The divergences are
constant and give no gradient. The standard GAN, which minimises Jensen–Shannon at the optimal discriminator, is in the
second situation whenever generated and real samples lie on disjoint low-dimensional sets, as images often do early in
training.

**Continuity in general.** If $G_{\thetavec}$ is continuous in $\thetavec$, then so is $W_1(p_{\text{data}},
p_{\thetavec})$. Couple $p_{\thetavec}$ and $p_{\thetavec'}$ through the same noise, $(G_{\thetavec}(Z),
G_{\thetavec'}(Z))$. Then

$$
\abs{W_1(p_{\text{data}}, p_{\thetavec}) - W_1(p_{\text{data}}, p_{\thetavec'})} \le W_1(p_{\thetavec}, p_{\thetavec'})
\le \expect\norm{G_{\thetavec}(Z) - G_{\thetavec'}(Z)},
$$

by the triangle inequality and the definition of $W_1$ as a minimum over couplings. The right-hand side tends to 0 as
$\thetavec' \to \thetavec$ (by dominated convergence, when the generator is bounded or Lipschitz in $\zvec$). If
$G_{\thetavec}$ is locally Lipschitz in $\thetavec$ with integrable constants, the same bound shows that $W_1$ is
Lipschitz in $\thetavec$ and hence differentiable almost everywhere <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />.

## The critic and the generator gradient

Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality states $W_1(p, q) = \sup_{\norm{f}_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1} \expect_p f - \expect_q f$ (see
<NoteLink to="wasserstein-distances">Wasserstein distances</NoteLink>). The critic network parametrises a family of
Lipschitz functions and approximates the supremum by gradient ascent on minibatches. Let $f^\star$ be an optimal critic.
By the envelope theorem, the generator's gradient is

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} W_1\paren{p_{\text{data}}, p_{\thetavec}} = -\expect_{\zvec}\brack{\nabla_{\thetavec} f^\star\paren{G_{\thetavec}(\zvec)}},
$$

obtained by backpropagating the critic's output through the generator <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />. The critic is trained
for several steps (five in the original recipe) for each generator step, so that it stays close to optimal.

## Enforcing the Lipschitz constraint

**Weight clipping.** The original WGAN clips every critic weight to $[-c, c]$ after each update, with $c = 0.01$
<Cite id="arjovsky2017" />. A network with bounded weights is $K$-Lipschitz for some $K$ that depends on the
architecture, so the critic estimates $K \cdot W_1$, which has the same minimiser. A critic with
clipped weights tends to learn very simple functions, and training is sensitive to $c$: large values make optimisation
slow, and small values cause vanishing gradients in deep critics <Cite id="gulrajani2017" />.

**Gradient penalty (WGAN-GP).** Gulrajani et al. replace clipping by a soft constraint on the gradient norm
<Cite id="gulrajani2017" />. The penalty rests on a property of the optimal critic. By <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">complementary slackness</NoteLink> in the
duality, an optimal coupling $\pi^\star$ and an optimal critic satisfy $f^\star(\xvec) - f^\star(\yvec) = \norm{\xvec -
\yvec}$ for every pair $(\xvec, \yvec)$ that $\pi^\star$ matches. Along the segment $\xvec_t = t\xvec + (1 - t)\yvec$, a
1-Lipschitz function can increase by at most $\norm{\xvec - \yvec}$ from $\yvec$ to $\xvec$, and $f^\star$ increases by
exactly that. So $f^\star$ increases at rate 1 along the segment, in the direction of $\xvec - \yvec$. Since the gradient
norm is at most 1, the gradient must equal the unit vector $(\xvec - \yvec)/\norm{\xvec - \yvec}$ wherever it exists on
the segment. The optimal critic has gradient norm 1 on straight lines between matched real and generated points.

WGAN-GP adds to the critic loss

$$
\lambda\, \expect_{\hat\xvec}\brack{\paren{\norm{\nabla_{\hat\xvec} f_{\wvec}(\hat\xvec)} - 1}^2},
\qquad \hat\xvec = u\,\xvec + (1 - u)\,G_{\thetavec}(\zvec),\ \ u \sim \Unif(0, 1),
$$

with $\xvec$ a real sample and $\lambda = 10$. The random interpolates $\hat\xvec$ stand in for the segments between
matched pairs, since the optimal coupling is unknown. The penalty is per sample, so the critic uses <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink>
or none, not <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink>, which would couple the gradients of different samples <Cite id="gulrajani2017" />.

**Spectral normalisation** divides each weight matrix by its largest singular value, estimated by <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">power iteration</NoteLink>.
Each linear layer is then 1-Lipschitz, and with 1-Lipschitz activations so is the network <Cite id="miyato2018" />. It
is cheaper than the gradient penalty and is also used with the standard GAN loss.

## Worked example

Take the parallel lines with $\theta = 2$: data on $\set{0} \times [0, 1]$, samples on $\set{2} \times [0, 1]$. The
function $f(x_1, x_2) = -x_1$ is 1-Lipschitz and gives $\expect_{\text{data}} f - \expect_{\text{gen}} f = 0 - (-2) = 2
= W_1$, so it is an optimal critic. Its gradient is $(-1, 0)$, of norm 1 everywhere, consistent with the gradient
penalty's target. The generator's loss is $-\expect f(G_\theta(\zvec)) = \theta$, with <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> 1, so <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> moves $\theta$ towards 0 at unit speed. The optimal standard GAN discriminator outputs $1$ on the data line and
$0$ on the generated line, and its loss is constant in $\theta$.

## Pitfalls

- **The critic is not the supremum.** A finite network trained for a few steps gives a lower bound on $W_1$ of unknown
  tightness. The WGAN loss tracks sample quality in practice but is not an accurate distance estimate.
- **Scale is arbitrary with clipping.** With weight clipping the critic estimates $K \cdot W_1$ for an unknown $K$, so
  loss values are comparable only within one run.
- **Batch normalisation in the critic** breaks the per-sample gradient penalty.
- **$W_1$ in pixel space.** The Euclidean distance between images is a poor ground metric. The critic's features,
  not the pixel metric, carry most of what makes WGAN samples realistic.
