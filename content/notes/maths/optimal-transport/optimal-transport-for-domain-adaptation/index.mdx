<Definition>
Let $\xvec^s_1, \dots, \xvec^s_n$ be labelled source samples with labels $y_1, \dots, y_n$ and weights $\avec$, and
$\xvec^t_1, \dots, \xvec^t_m$ unlabelled target samples with weights $\bvec$. **Optimal transport domain adaptation**
computes a transport plan $\Pmat$ between the two samples with cost $C_{ij} = \norm{\xvec^s_i - \xvec^t_j}^2$, usually
entropically regularised, maps each source sample to

$$
\hat\xvec^s_i = \frac{1}{a_i}\sum_{j} P_{ij}\,\xvec^t_j ,
$$

and trains a classifier on the pairs $(\hat\xvec^s_i, y_i)$ <Cite id="courty2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier trained on a source domain loses accuracy on a target domain whose input distribution differs, for
example images from another camera or sensor data from another subject. Target labels are unavailable. The methods of
<NoteLink to="feature-alignment-methods">feature alignment</NoteLink> match statistics of the two domains, such as kernel
mean embeddings or covariances, and <NoteLink to="domain-adversarial-training">domain-adversarial training</NoteLink>
learns features a domain classifier cannot separate. Neither says which source sample corresponds to which target
sample. When the shift is a smooth deformation of the input space (a translation, a rotation, a change of scale),
optimal transport recovers that correspondence directly.

## Mechanism

### Barycentric mapping

The plan $\Pmat$ says how much of each source sample travels to each target sample. Source sample $i$ sends mass $a_i$
in total, split over targets in proportions $P_{ij}/a_i$. The **barycentric mapping** replaces $\xvec^s_i$ by the
weighted mean of its destinations. With uniform weights $a_i = 1/n$, it is $\hat\xvec^s_i = n\sum_j P_{ij}\xvec^t_j$.
The mapped source samples lie in the target domain and keep their labels, so a classifier trained on them applies to
target data. For an exact plan between equal-size samples with uniform weights, the plan is a matching, and each
source sample is moved onto one target sample.

The barycentric mapping is also the minimiser, for each $i$, of $\sum_j P_{ij}\norm{\zvec - \xvec^t_j}^2$ over
$\zvec$: setting the gradient $2\sum_j P_{ij}(\zvec - \xvec^t_j)$ to zero gives $\zvec = \sum_j P_{ij}\xvec^t_j /
\sum_j P_{ij}$, and $\sum_j P_{ij} = a_i$.

### Class-based regularisation

An unregularised plan can send source samples of different classes to the same target sample, which then receives
conflicting labels. Courty et al. add a group-sparsity penalty <Cite id="courty2017" />:

$$
\min_{\Pmat \in \Pi(\avec, \bvec)} \inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat} - \varepsilon H(\Pmat) + \eta\sum_j\sum_{c}\norm{\Pmat_{\Ical_c, j}}_q^p ,
$$

where $\Ical_c$ is the set of source samples of class $c$ and $\Pmat_{\Ical_c, j}$ is the part of column $j$ from class
$c$. With $p < 1$ (or the group lasso, $p = 1$, $q = 2$), the penalty is cheaper when a column's mass comes from a
single class, so each target sample tends to receive mass from one class. The problem is solved by majorisation:
linearising the penalty at the current plan gives a modified cost matrix, and each step is a Sinkhorn problem.

### Out-of-sample points

The barycentric mapping is defined only on the training source samples. New source samples are mapped by
interpolation, for example by moving each by the displacement of its nearest training neighbour, or by fitting a
smooth map (linear or kernel) to the pairs $(\xvec^s_i, \hat\xvec^s_i)$ <Cite id="courty2017" />.

### Joint distribution optimal transport

JDOT transports the joint distribution of inputs and labels <Cite id="courty2017jdot" />. It learns the target
classifier $h$ and the plan together, with a cost that compares both features and labels:

$$
\min_{h,\ \Pmat \in \Pi(\avec, \bvec)}\ \sum_{i,j} P_{ij}\paren{\alpha\norm{\xvec^s_i - \xvec^t_j}^2 + \Lcal\paren{y_i, h(\xvec^t_j)}} .
$$

The label cost $\Lcal$ is a classification loss. Minimisation alternates between the two blocks. For fixed $h$, the
cost matrix is fixed and the plan is a transport problem. For fixed $\Pmat$, the objective is a weighted empirical risk
of $h$ on target inputs, where target sample $j$ carries the label $y_i$ with weight $P_{ij}$. Transport therefore
assigns soft pseudo-labels to the target, and the classifier in turn changes which matches are cheap. DeepJDOT applies
the same idea to minibatches with a deep feature extractor <Cite id="damodaran2018" />.

## Cost

- **Plan.** An $n \times m$ transport problem, solved by Sinkhorn's algorithm in $O(nm)$ per iteration
  <Cite id="cuturi2013" />. Class regularisation multiplies this by the number of majorisation steps.
- **Memory.** The cost matrix has $nm$ entries, which limits full-batch methods to moderate sample sizes.
  Minibatch variants such as DeepJDOT trade exactness of the plan for scale.
- **Software.** POT implements the regularised mappings and their out-of-sample extensions <Cite id="flamary2021" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Assumes a transport-like shift.** The method works when the target is a deformation of the source that moves
  nearby points to nearby points. If classes swap places, the cheapest plan matches the wrong classes.
- **Label proportions.** Balanced transport requires the plan to move all source mass onto all target mass. If class
  proportions differ between domains (label shift), mass of an over-represented source class is forced onto another
  class. <NoteLink to="unbalanced-and-partial-transport">Unbalanced and partial transport</NoteLink> relax this.
- **Compared with moment matching.** Matching covariances or kernel mean embeddings is cheaper and scales to
  minibatches of deep features. Transport uses the whole distribution and a per-sample correspondence.

## Worked example

**Translation in one dimension.** Source samples at $0, 1, 3, 4$ with labels $0, 0, 1, 1$; target samples at $2.5, 3.5,
5.5, 6.5$, the source shifted by $2.5$. A threshold classifier trained on the source puts the boundary at $2$, between
the classes, and labels every target sample as class 1: half the target is misclassified. With uniform weights and
squared cost, the optimal plan is the monotone matching $0 \to 2.5$, $1 \to 3.5$, $3 \to 5.5$, $4 \to 6.5$. The mapped
source samples are the target samples with labels $0, 0, 1, 1$, and a threshold trained on them lies between $3.5$ and
$5.5$, classifying the target without error.

**Transport map against CORAL.** For Gaussian domains, both CORAL <Cite id="sun2016coral" /> and optimal transport map
the source with a linear transformation. Let the source covariance be $\Sigmamat_s = \diag(4, 1)$ and the target
covariance $\Sigmamat_t = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, both centred. CORAL uses $\Amat_c =
\Sigmamat_t^{1/2}\Sigmamat_s^{-1/2}$. The transport map uses the symmetric matrix $\Amat = \Sigmamat_s^{-1/2}
\paren{\Sigmamat_s^{1/2}\Sigmamat_t\Sigmamat_s^{1/2}}^{1/2}\Sigmamat_s^{-1/2}$. Both send $\Gauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat_s)$
to $\Gauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat_t)$. The mean squared displacement $\expect\norm{\xvec - \Amat\xvec}^2$ is $0.771$ for the
transport map, equal to $W_2^2$ between the two Gaussians, and $0.804$ for CORAL. CORAL moves samples further than
necessary, and differently: $\Amat_c$ is not symmetric, so it includes a rotation that the transport map does not.
