<Definition>
For histograms $\avec \in \reals_{>0}^n$ and $\bvec \in \reals_{>0}^m$ of total mass 1, a cost matrix $\Cmat$ and
$\varepsilon > 0$, **entropic optimal transport** solves

$$
\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\avec, \bvec) = \min_{\Pmat \ge 0,\ \Pmat\ones = \avec,\ \Pmat^\top\ones = \bvec}
\inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat} + \varepsilon\KL\paren{\Pmat \,\|\, \avec\bvec^\top} .
$$

The solution is unique and has the form $\Pmat = \diag(\uvec)\,\Kmat\,\diag(\vvec)$ with the **Gibbs kernel**
$K_{ij} = a_i b_j e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}$. **Sinkhorn's algorithm** finds the scalings by alternating
$\uvec \leftarrow \avec \oslash \Kmat\vvec$ and $\vvec \leftarrow \bvec \oslash \Kmat^\top\uvec$, where $\oslash$ is
elementwise division <Cite id="cuturi2013" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The <NoteLink to="earth-movers-distance">transport linear program</NoteLink> has $nm$ variables. Exact solvers such as
the network simplex cost roughly $O(n^3\log n)$ for $n = m$, run sequentially, and return a sparse plan that jumps from
one vertex to another when the inputs change slightly. The optimal cost is then not differentiable everywhere in the
point positions. Machine learning needs transport costs between many pairs of batches, on GPUs, inside a loss that is
differentiated. The entropic problem trades exactness for all three.

## Mechanism

### The regulariser

For a plan with marginals $\avec$ and $\bvec$, $\KL(\Pmat \,\|\, \avec\bvec^\top) = \sum_{ij} P_{ij}
\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_i b_j}$ is the mutual information between source and destination under $\Pmat$. It is zero for the
independent plan $\avec\bvec^\top$ and grows as the plan becomes more deterministic. Since $\KL$ is strictly convex in
$\Pmat$, the regularised problem is strictly convex and has one solution. Small $\varepsilon$ favours cheap plans; large
$\varepsilon$ favours diffuse plans.

The KL term equals $-H(\Pmat) + H(\avec) + H(\bvec)$ for every feasible plan, where $H(\Pmat) = -\sum_{ij} P_{ij}\log
P_{ij}$ is the Shannon entropy. Regularising with $-\varepsilon H(\Pmat)$, as Cuturi did, therefore gives the same
plan, and the optimal values differ by the constant $\varepsilon\paren{H(\avec) + H(\bvec)}$ <Cite id="cuturi2013" />.

### The form of the solution

Attach multipliers $f_i$ to the row constraints and $g_j$ to the column constraints. The Lagrangian is

$$
\Lcal = \sum_{ij} C_{ij}P_{ij} + \varepsilon\sum_{ij} P_{ij}\paren{\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_i b_j} - 1}
+ \sum_i f_i\paren{a_i - \sum_j P_{ij}} + \sum_j g_j\paren{b_j - \sum_i P_{ij}} .
$$

The $-1$ inside the bracket adds $-\varepsilon$ to the objective for any feasible plan, which changes nothing, and
simplifies the derivative. Setting the derivative
$\partial\Lcal / \partial P_{ij} = C_{ij} + \varepsilon\log\frac{P_{ij}}{a_i b_j} - f_i - g_j$ to zero gives

$$
P_{ij} = e^{f_i/\varepsilon}\; a_i b_j e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}\; e^{g_j/\varepsilon} = u_i K_{ij} v_j ,
\qquad u_i = e^{f_i/\varepsilon},\ v_j = e^{g_j/\varepsilon} .
$$

The logarithm forces $P_{ij} > 0$, so the constraint $\Pmat \ge 0$ is inactive and needs no multiplier. The optimal plan
is the kernel $\Kmat$ with its rows scaled by $\uvec$ and its columns by $\vvec$. Only $n + m$ numbers remain unknown.

### Sinkhorn's iterations

The scalings must satisfy the two marginal constraints:

$$
\uvec \odot (\Kmat\vvec) = \avec, \qquad \vvec \odot (\Kmat^\top\uvec) = \bvec .
$$

Each equation is solved exactly for one vector when the other is fixed. Sinkhorn's algorithm alternates:

$$
\uvec^{(k+1)} = \avec \oslash \Kmat\vvec^{(k)}, \qquad \vvec^{(k+1)} = \bvec \oslash \Kmat^\top\uvec^{(k+1)} .
$$

After a $\uvec$-update the row sums are exact; after a $\vvec$-update the column sums are. Each iteration is two
matrix-vector products. Sinkhorn and Knopp proved that this alternating normalisation converges for any matrix with
positive entries <Cite id="sinkhorn1967" />.

The iterations are also block coordinate ascent on the dual. Substituting the optimal plan back into $\Lcal$ gives the
concave dual function

$$
D(\fvec, \gvec) = \avec^\top\fvec + \bvec^\top\gvec - \varepsilon\sum_{ij} a_i b_j\, e^{(f_i + g_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon}
+ \varepsilon .
$$

Setting $\partial D / \partial f_i = 0$ for fixed $\gvec$ gives

$$
f_i = -\varepsilon\log\sum_j b_j\, e^{(g_j - C_{ij})/\varepsilon} ,
$$

which is the $\uvec$-update in logarithms. As $\varepsilon \to 0$ the right-hand side tends to $\min_j (C_{ij} - g_j)$,
the $c$-transform of <NoteLink to="kantorovich-duality">Kantorovich duality</NoteLink>. Sinkhorn's algorithm is
alternating soft $c$-transforms.

### Log-domain stabilisation

For small $\varepsilon$, $e^{-C_{ij}/\varepsilon}$ underflows to zero in floating point: with $C_{ij} = 10$ and
$\varepsilon = 0.01$ it is $e^{-1000}$, below the smallest double. The scalings $\uvec$, $\vvec$ then overflow. The fix
is to iterate on the potentials $\fvec$, $\gvec$ with the update above, computing each log-sum-exp by subtracting its
largest term first. This costs one exponential per entry per iteration instead of one multiplication. Faster schemes
iterate in the scaling domain and move large factors into the potentials only when they grow, and they drop
negligible kernel entries to make the problem sparse <Cite id="schmitzer2019" />.

## Cost

- **Per iteration.** Two products with an $n \times m$ matrix, $O(nm)$ operations. They are dense linear algebra and
  run well on GPUs. Many problems of the same size are solved in parallel as a batch.
- **Structured kernels.** On a regular grid with the squared Euclidean cost, $\Kmat$ is a Gaussian blur, applied by
  separable convolution without forming the matrix <Cite id="peyre2019" />.
- **Number of iterations.** The iterations contract at a linear rate in Hilbert's projective metric
  <Cite id="franklin1989" />. The contraction factor depends on the ratios $K_{ij}K_{kl} / (K_{il}K_{kj})$, which grow
  like $e^{2\max C/\varepsilon}$. The factor approaches 1, and convergence slows sharply, as $\varepsilon \to 0$.
  Sinkhorn's algorithm approximates the unregularised cost to additive accuracy $\delta$ in time near-linear in the
  input size $nm$ for fixed $\delta$ <Cite id="altschuler2017" />.
- **Memory.** Storing $\Cmat$ costs $O(nm)$. Implementations that recompute cost entries on the fly scale to hundreds
  of thousands of points.

## Trade-offs

### The bias of $\varepsilon$

Let $\Pmat_\varepsilon$ be the entropic plan and $\Pmat^\star$ an exact optimal plan. Because $\Pmat_\varepsilon$
minimises $\inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat} - \varepsilon H(\Pmat)$,

$$
\inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat_\varepsilon} - \varepsilon H(\Pmat_\varepsilon) \le \inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat^\star} - \varepsilon
H(\Pmat^\star)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
0 \le \inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat_\varepsilon} - \inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat^\star} \le \varepsilon\paren{H(\Pmat_\varepsilon) -
H(\Pmat^\star)} .
$$

The entropy of a joint distribution lies between the larger and the sum of the entropies of its marginals, so
$H(\Pmat_\varepsilon) - H(\Pmat^\star) \le \min\paren{H(\avec), H(\bvec)} \le \log\min(n, m)$. The transport cost of
the entropic plan exceeds the optimum by at most $\varepsilon\log\min(n, m)$.

- **$\varepsilon \to 0$**: the entropic plan converges to the exact optimal plan of maximal entropy, but the iterations
  slow down and need the log domain.
- **$\varepsilon \to \infty$**: the kernel tends to $\avec\bvec^\top$ and the plan to the independent coupling. The
  cost tends to $\sum_{ij} a_i b_j C_{ij}$, which ignores the matching altogether.

A common schedule is **$\varepsilon$-scaling**: start with a large $\varepsilon$, run a few iterations, and use the
potentials to warm-start a smaller $\varepsilon$.

### Other consequences

- **Differentiability.** $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon$ is a smooth function of $\avec$, $\bvec$ and $\Cmat$. By the
  envelope theorem its gradient with respect to $\avec$ is the potential $\fvec$ (up to an additive constant), and its
  gradient with respect to $C_{ij}$ is $P_{ij}$. Gradients with respect to point positions follow through $\Cmat$.
- **Dense plans.** Every $P_{ij}$ is positive, so the plan is never a matching. Applications that need a map, such as
  mapping source points to target points, take the barycentric projection $\sum_j P_{ij}\yvec_j / a_i$.
- **Bias at equal inputs.** $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\avec, \avec) > 0$. The
  <NoteLink to="sinkhorn-divergences">Sinkhorn divergence</NoteLink> subtracts the self-transport terms to remove this
  bias.

## Worked example

Two points at $0$ and $1$ with masses $\avec = \bvec = (0.5, 0.5)$ and cost $C_{ij} = \abs{x_i - x_j}$, so
$\Cmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The exact plan keeps every point in place, at cost 0. By
symmetry the entropic plan is $\Pmat = s\begin{bmatrix} 1 & e^{-1/\varepsilon} \\ e^{-1/\varepsilon} & 1
\end{bmatrix}$, and the row sums fix $s = 0.5 / (1 + e^{-1/\varepsilon})$. The mass that moves is

$$
2P_{12} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{1/\varepsilon}} .
$$

At $\varepsilon = 0.5$ this is $1/(1 + e^2) = 0.119$, and the transport cost is $0.119$ instead of 0. At $\varepsilon
= 0.1$ it is $4.5 \times 10^{-5}$. As $\varepsilon \to \infty$ it tends to $0.5$, the cost of the independent plan.
The bias bound above gives $\varepsilon\log 2 = 0.347$ at $\varepsilon = 0.5$, which holds. Sinkhorn's iterations
started from $\vvec = \ones$ reach this plan in one step, because the kernel is already symmetric; `ot.sinkhorn` from
POT <Cite id="flamary2021" /> and a log-domain implementation give the same plan.
