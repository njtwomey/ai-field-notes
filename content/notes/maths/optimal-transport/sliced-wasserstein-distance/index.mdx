<Definition>
Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be distributions on $\reals^d$ with finite $p$-th moments. For a unit vector $\thetavec$, let
$\thetavec_\#\mu$ be the distribution of $\thetavec^\top X$ with $X \sim \mu$. The **sliced Wasserstein distance** is
<Cite id="rabin2011" />

$$
SW_p(\mu, \nu) = \paren{\int_{\Scal^{d-1}} W_p^p\paren{\thetavec_\#\mu, \thetavec_\#\nu}\, d\sigma(\thetavec)}^{1/p},
$$

where $\sigma$ is the uniform distribution on the unit sphere $\Scal^{d-1}$. Each $W_p$ inside the integral is between
distributions on the real line.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Transport in one dimension is cheap: the optimal plan pairs quantiles, and between two samples of equal size it pairs
the sorted values. Transport in $d$ dimensions is a linear program. Slicing replaces one hard problem by many easy ones.
Each direction $\thetavec$ gives a one-dimensional shadow of each distribution, and the distance compares the shadows.
Two distributions that differ at all differ in the shadow along some direction, so averaging over all directions loses
no information about whether they are equal. It does lose the joint geometry: the matchings found on different slices
need not agree with each other or with the $d$-dimensional optimal plan.

## Computation

Draw $L$ directions $\thetavec_1, \dots, \thetavec_L$ uniformly on the sphere, by normalising standard Gaussian vectors.
For samples $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ and $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n$ with equal weights, each slice costs one projection
and two sorts:

$$
\widehat{SW}_p^p = \frac1L \sum_{l=1}^{L} \frac1n \sum_{k=1}^{n} \abs{\paren{\thetavec_l^\top\xvec}_{(k)} -
\paren{\thetavec_l^\top\yvec}_{(k)}}^p ,
$$

where $(k)$ denotes the $k$-th smallest value. The total cost is $O(Ldn + Ln\log n)$. Exact $W_p$ between the same
samples is an assignment problem of cost about $O(n^3)$. The estimate is differentiable almost everywhere in the sample
positions: for fixed sort orders it is a sum of $p$-th powers of differences. That makes it usable as a training loss
<Cite id="deshpande2018" />.

## Properties

**$SW_p \le W_p$.** The projection $x \mapsto \thetavec^\top x$ is 1-Lipschitz: $\abs{\thetavec^\top(x - y)} \le
\norm{x - y}$. Take an optimal coupling $(X, Y)$ for $W_p(\mu, \nu)$. Then $(\thetavec^\top X, \thetavec^\top Y)$
couples the two shadows, so $W_p^p(\thetavec_\#\mu, \thetavec_\#\nu) \le \expect\abs{\thetavec^\top(X - Y)}^p \le
\expect\norm{X - Y}^p = W_p^p(\mu, \nu)$. Averaging over $\thetavec$ keeps the inequality.

**$SW_p$ is a metric** <Cite id="bonneel2015" />.

- Symmetry and $SW_p(\mu, \mu) = 0$ follow from the same properties of each one-dimensional $W_p$.
- Triangle inequality: each slice satisfies $W_p(\thetavec_\#\mu, \thetavec_\#\rho) \le W_p(\thetavec_\#\mu,
  \thetavec_\#\nu) + W_p(\thetavec_\#\nu, \thetavec_\#\rho)$. Taking the $L^p(\sigma)$ norm over $\thetavec$ of both sides
  and applying Minkowski's inequality gives $SW_p(\mu, \rho) \le SW_p(\mu, \nu) + SW_p(\nu, \rho)$.
- $SW_p(\mu, \nu) = 0$ implies $\mu = \nu$. If $SW_p = 0$, then $\thetavec_\#\mu = \thetavec_\#\nu$ for almost every
  $\thetavec$, and by continuity of characteristic functions for every $\thetavec$. The characteristic function of
  $\mu$ at $t\thetavec$ is $\expect e^{\mathrm{i}t\thetavec^\top X}$, the characteristic function of $\thetavec_\#\mu$ at
  $t$. So $\mu$ and $\nu$ have the same characteristic function everywhere and are equal (the Cramér–Wold device).

**Estimation.** Each slice is a one-dimensional problem, for which the empirical $W_p$ does not suffer the $n^{-1/d}$
rate of the full distance <Cite id="fournier2015" />. This is one reason sliced distances are used as losses in high
dimension.

## Worked example: a shift in $d$ dimensions

Let $\nu$ be $\mu$ translated by a vector $\deltavec \in \reals^d$. Every shadow is shifted by $\thetavec^\top\deltavec$,
so $W_2^2(\thetavec_\#\mu, \thetavec_\#\nu) = \paren{\thetavec^\top\deltavec}^2$ and

$$
SW_2^2(\mu, \nu) = \expect_{\thetavec}\paren{\thetavec^\top\deltavec}^2 = \deltavec^\top\expect\brack{\thetavec\thetavec^\top}\deltavec .
$$

By symmetry of the sphere, $\expect\brack{\thetavec\thetavec^\top}$ is a multiple of the identity. Its trace is
$\expect\norm{\thetavec}^2 = 1$, so it equals $\eye / d$. Hence $SW_2 = \norm{\deltavec} / \sqrt{d}$, while $W_2 =
\norm{\deltavec}$. For $\deltavec = (3, 0, 0, 0, 0)$ in $d = 5$, $SW_2^2 = 9/5 = 1.8$; a Monte Carlo estimate with
$2 \times 10^5$ directions gives $1.806$. The sliced distance shrinks relative to the full one by $\sqrt d$ even for the
simplest difference, because most random directions are nearly orthogonal to $\deltavec$ in high dimension.

## Uses

- **Generative models.** Deshpande et al. train a generator by minimising the sliced distance between generated and
  real samples, a single objective with no adversary. Because most random directions are uninformative on images,
  they project onto directions learnt by a discriminator-like network <Cite id="deshpande2018" />.
- **Barycentres and texture mixing.** Averaging distributions by moving samples along the gradients of sliced costs
  gives fast approximate <NoteLink to="wasserstein-barycentres">Wasserstein barycentres</NoteLink>, used for texture
  mixing and colour transfer <Cite id="rabin2011" /> <Cite id="bonneel2015" />.
- **Kernels on distributions.** Because $SW_2$ is built from one-dimensional distances, it is Hilbertian in a way
  $W_2$ in $d \ge 2$ is not, and a Gaussian of $SW_2^2$ gives a positive definite kernel between distributions
  <Cite id="kolouri2016" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Uninformative directions.** In high dimension a random direction is nearly orthogonal to the directions in which
  the distributions differ. The estimate then needs many projections, or directions chosen to be informative (the
  max-sliced distance takes the worst direction).
- **Not the same geometry as $W_p$.** $SW_p \le W_p$, and the inequality can be loose by a factor growing with $d$.
  Rankings by the two distances need not agree.
- **No transport plan.** Each slice has its own matching. Their average is not a coupling of $\mu$ and $\nu$.
