<Definition>
For histograms $\avec \in \reals_{\ge 0}^n$, $\bvec \in \reals_{\ge 0}^m$ of equal mass and a cost matrix $\Cmat$, the
**Kantorovich dual** of the transport problem is

$$
\max_{\fvec \in \reals^n,\, \gvec \in \reals^m}\ \avec^\top\fvec + \bvec^\top\gvec
\quad \text{subject to} \quad f_i + g_j \le C_{ij} \ \text{ for all } i, j .
$$

Its optimal value equals the optimal transport cost $\min_{\Pmat} \inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat}$ over plans with marginals
$\avec$ and $\bvec$ <Cite id="kantorovich1942" />. The maximisers $(\fvec, \gvec)$ are the **Kantorovich potentials**.
For general distributions the same statement holds with functions $\varphi(x)$, $\psi(y)$ and expectations in place of
vectors and sums <Cite id="villani2009" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A shipping company offers to take over the transport. It charges $f_i$ per unit to collect from source $i$ and $g_j$
per unit to deliver to destination $j$. Its prices are competitive if collecting at $i$ and delivering to $j$ never
costs more than doing the job oneself: $f_i + g_j \le C_{ij}$. Under competitive prices, the company's revenue
$\sum_i a_i f_i + \sum_j b_j g_j$ can never exceed the cost of any plan the owner could carry out. Duality says that a
company that sets its prices well earns exactly the optimal transport cost <Cite id="villani2009" />. Prices may be
negative; a negative $g_j$ is a rebate.

## Derivation from the Lagrangian

The primal problem is: minimise $\inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat}$ subject to $\Pmat\ones = \avec$, $\Pmat^\top\ones = \bvec$ and
$\Pmat \ge 0$. Attach a multiplier $f_i$ to each row constraint and $g_j$ to each column constraint. The Lagrangian is

$$
\Lcal(\Pmat, \fvec, \gvec) = \sum_{ij} C_{ij}P_{ij} + \sum_i f_i\paren{a_i - \sum_j P_{ij}} + \sum_j g_j\paren{b_j - \sum_i P_{ij}}
= \avec^\top\fvec + \bvec^\top\gvec + \sum_{ij} P_{ij}\paren{C_{ij} - f_i - g_j} .
$$

The dual function minimises $\Lcal$ over $\Pmat \ge 0$. If some $C_{ij} - f_i - g_j < 0$, letting $P_{ij} \to \infty$
sends $\Lcal$ to $-\infty$. Otherwise the minimum is at $\Pmat = 0$ and equals $\avec^\top\fvec + \bvec^\top\gvec$. The
dual problem, maximising the dual function, is therefore the problem in the definition.

**Weak duality** is one line. For any feasible plan and any feasible potentials,

$$
\avec^\top\fvec + \bvec^\top\gvec = \sum_{ij} P_{ij}\paren{f_i + g_j} \le \sum_{ij} P_{ij}C_{ij},
$$

using $\sum_j P_{ij} = a_i$, $\sum_i P_{ij} = b_j$ and $P_{ij} \ge 0$. **Strong duality**, equality of the optima, holds
because both are linear programs with finite optima (see <NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear programming</NoteLink>).

## Complementary slackness

At optimal $\Pmat^\star$ and $(\fvec^\star, \gvec^\star)$ the inequality above is an equality, so every term
$P^\star_{ij}\paren{C_{ij} - f^\star_i - g^\star_j}$ vanishes:

$$
P^\star_{ij} > 0 \implies f^\star_i + g^\star_j = C_{ij} .
$$

Mass travels only along routes where the company's prices are exactly the true cost. This gives a certificate: a plan
and a pair of potentials that are both feasible and satisfy complementary slackness are both optimal.

The potentials are not unique. Adding a constant $t$ to every $f_i$ and subtracting it from every $g_j$ keeps the
constraints and the objective, because $\sum_i a_i = \sum_j b_j$.

## The $c$-transform

For fixed $\fvec$, the best $\gvec$ is the largest one allowed by the constraints:

$$
g_j = \fvec^c_j \eqdef \min_i \paren{C_{ij} - f_i} .
$$

This is the **$c$-transform** of $\fvec$. Increasing any $g_j$ above $\fvec^c_j$ violates a constraint, and any smaller
value lowers the objective because $b_j \ge 0$. The dual therefore reduces to an unconstrained problem in $\fvec$
alone, the **semi-dual**:

$$
\max_{\fvec}\ \avec^\top\fvec + \sum_j b_j \min_i\paren{C_{ij} - f_i} .
$$

The objective is concave and piecewise linear in $\fvec$. The same transform in the other direction, $\gvec^{\bar c}_i
= \min_j (C_{ij} - g_j)$, improves $\fvec$ for fixed $\gvec$. A function that equals the $c$-transform of some function
is **$c$-concave**, and optimal potentials can always be taken $c$-concave. Alternating the two transforms is a
coordinate ascent on the dual. Replacing the hard minimum by the soft minimum $-\varepsilon\log\sum_i
e^{-(C_{ij} - f_i)/\varepsilon}$ gives exactly the updates of <NoteLink
to="entropic-regularisation-and-sinkhorn">Sinkhorn's algorithm</NoteLink>.

When $c$ is a metric, the $c$-transform of a 1-Lipschitz function $f$ is $-f$, and the dual collapses to a single
Lipschitz function. This is the Kantorovich–Rubinstein form of the <NoteLink to="wasserstein-distances">Wasserstein-1
distance</NoteLink>.

## Continuous form and transport maps

For distributions $\mu$ on $\Xcal$ and $\nu$ on $\Ycal$ with a lower semicontinuous cost bounded below,

$$
\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int c\, d\pi = \sup_{\varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x, y)} \int \varphi\, d\mu + \int \psi\, d\nu,
$$

and the supremum is attained by a pair $(\varphi, \varphi^c)$ with $\varphi$ $c$-concave <Cite id="villani2009" />. The
optimal plan is concentrated on the set where $\varphi(x) + \varphi^c(y) = c(x, y)$.

This set determines the plan. Take $c(x, y) = \tfrac12\norm{x - y}^2$ on $\reals^d$. If $(x, y)$ carries mass, then
$\varphi(x') + \varphi^c(y) \le c(x', y)$ for every $x'$, with equality at $x' = x$. So $x' \mapsto c(x', y) -
\varphi(x')$ is minimised at $x' = x$, and where $\varphi$ is differentiable its gradient vanishes:
$x - y - \nabla\varphi(x) = 0$. Hence

$$
y = T(x) = x - \nabla\varphi(x) = \nabla\paren{\tfrac12\norm{x}^2 - \varphi(x)} .
$$

Each $x$ is sent to a single $y$, so the optimal plan is a map. The function $\tfrac12\norm{x}^2 - \varphi(x)$ is
convex when $\varphi$ is $c$-concave for this cost. This is Brenier's theorem: for the quadratic cost and a source with a
density, the optimal plan is the gradient of a convex function <Cite id="brenier1991" />.

## Worked example

Sources at $x = (0, 1, 3)$ with masses $\avec = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$; destinations at $y = (1, 2, 4)$ with masses
$\bvec = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3)$; cost $C_{ij} = (x_i - y_j)^2$:

$$
\Cmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 16 \\ 0 & 1 & 9 \\ 4 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Pmat^\star = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat^\star} = 2.7 .
$$

The potentials follow from complementary slackness. Fix $g_3 = 0$, which removes the free constant. The five routes
that carry mass give five equations: $f_3 + g_3 = 1$, $f_2 + g_3 = 9$, $f_2 + g_2 = 1$, $f_1 + g_2 = 4$, $f_1 + g_1 = 1$.
They solve to $\fvec = (12, 9, 1)$ and $\gvec = (-11, -8, 0)$. The four unused routes satisfy the constraints:
$f_1 + g_3 = 12 \le 16$, $f_2 + g_1 = -2 \le 0$, $f_3 + g_1 = -10 \le 4$ and $f_3 + g_2 = -7 \le 1$. So the potentials
are feasible, and both solutions are optimal. The dual value is

$$
\avec^\top\fvec + \bvec^\top\gvec = 6 + 2.7 + 0.2 - 2.2 - 4 + 0 = 2.7,
$$

equal to the primal cost. $\gvec$ is the $c$-transform of $\fvec$: for example $g_1 = \min(1 - 12,\ 0 - 9,\ 4 - 1) =
-11$. The dual multipliers returned by `scipy.optimize.linprog` (HiGHS) are the same vectors.

## Sensitivity

The potentials are the derivatives of the optimal cost with respect to the masses. Moving a small amount of mass from
source $k$ to source $l$ changes the optimal cost at rate $f_l - f_k$, as long as the optimal plan keeps its routes
(the shadow prices of <NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear programming</NoteLink>). In the example, moving mass
from the source at $0$ to the source at $3$ lowers the cost at rate $12 - 1 = 11$ per unit. This is how the gradient of
a transport cost with respect to histogram weights is computed when the cost is used as a loss <Cite id="peyre2019" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Only differences are determined.** Potentials are defined up to $(\fvec + t, \gvec - t)$, and entries of $\fvec$
  at sources with zero mass are arbitrary. Fix one entry before comparing solutions.
- **Degenerate problems.** When the optimal plan has fewer than $n + m - 1$ nonzero entries, complementary slackness
  does not pin the potentials down, and several dual solutions exist. The gradient with respect to the masses is then
  not unique.
