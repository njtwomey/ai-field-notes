<Definition>
Let $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi(\alpha, \beta)} \int c\, d\pi +
\varepsilon\KL\paren{\pi \,\|\, \alpha \otimes \beta}$ be the entropic transport cost between distributions $\alpha$ and
$\beta$. The **Sinkhorn divergence** is <Cite id="genevay2018" />

$$
S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = \operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) - \tfrac12\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha)
- \tfrac12\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\beta, \beta) .
$$

For costs such as $c(x, y) = \norm{x - y}$ or $\norm{x - y}^2$ on $\reals^d$, $S_\varepsilon$ is nonnegative, zero only
when $\alpha = \beta$, and convex in each argument <Cite id="feydy2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The entropic plan blurs every point's mass over its neighbours. Transporting a distribution onto itself then costs
something, because the blurred plan moves mass by a typical distance of order $\sqrt\varepsilon$ for the squared
cost. So $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha) > 0$, and $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \cdot)$ is
not minimised at $\alpha$. Subtracting the two self-transport costs, the price of blurring each distribution onto
itself, leaves a quantity that is zero at $\alpha = \beta$. The construction resembles the maximum mean discrepancy,
$\operatorname{MMD}^2 = \expect k(X, X') + \expect k(Y, Y') - 2\expect k(X, Y)$, which also subtracts within-sample
terms from a cross term.

## Why the entropic cost is biased

The bias is easiest to see at large $\varepsilon$. There the optimal plan tends to the independent coupling
$\alpha \otimes \beta$, because the KL term dominates, and

$$
\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) \to \iint c(x, y)\, d\alpha(x)\, d\beta(y) = \expect\, c(X, Y),
\qquad X \sim \alpha,\ Y \sim \beta \text{ independent}.
$$

For the squared Euclidean cost, $\expect\norm{X - Y}^2 = \norm{\mvec_\alpha - \mvec_\beta}^2 + \trace\Sigmamat_\alpha +
\trace\Sigmamat_\beta$, where $\mvec$ and $\Sigmamat$ are means and covariances. For fixed $\alpha$ this is minimised by
a $\beta$ with the mean of $\alpha$ and zero spread: a point mass at $\mvec_\alpha$. Minimising
$\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta)$ over $\beta$, as when fitting a generative model, therefore shrinks the
model towards the mean. For finite $\varepsilon$ the effect is weaker but has the same direction.

## The two limits

**Small $\varepsilon$.** $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) \to \operatorname{OT}_0(\alpha, \beta)$, the
unregularised cost. For a cost with $c(x, x) = 0$, $\operatorname{OT}_0(\alpha, \alpha) = 0$ through the plan that keeps
every point in place. So $S_\varepsilon \to \operatorname{OT}_0$, the optimal transport cost; with $c = \norm{x - y}^p$
that is $W_p^p$.

**Large $\varepsilon$.** Each term tends to its independent-coupling cost, so

$$
S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) \to \expect\, c(X, Y) - \tfrac12\expect\, c(X, X') - \tfrac12\expect\, c(Y, Y'),
$$

with $X, X' \sim \alpha$ and $Y, Y' \sim \beta$ all independent. Writing $k = -c$, this is
$\tfrac12\paren{\expect k(X, X') + \expect k(Y, Y') - 2\expect k(X, Y)}$, half the squared MMD with kernel $-c$
<Cite id="feydy2019" />. Two cases:

- **$c(x, y) = \norm{x - y}$.** The limit is half the **energy distance**, a proper metric between distributions. The
  kernel $-\norm{x - y}$ is conditionally positive definite, which is what an MMD needs.
- **$c(x, y) = \norm{x - y}^2$.** Expanding, $\expect\norm{X - Y}^2 - \tfrac12\expect\norm{X - X'}^2 -
  \tfrac12\expect\norm{Y - Y'}^2 = \norm{\mvec_\alpha - \mvec_\beta}^2$: the covariance terms cancel. The limit sees only
  the means. With the squared cost, very large $\varepsilon$ gives a weak divergence.

$\varepsilon$ thus moves $S_\varepsilon$ between optimal transport, which follows the geometry of the space, and a
kernel distance, which is cheap to estimate. Its square root behaves like a length scale: structures finer than about
$\sqrt\varepsilon$ (squared cost) or $\varepsilon$ (Euclidean cost) are blurred, and coarser ones are transported.

## Statistical and computational cost

- **Sample complexity.** The plug-in estimate of $W_p$ from $n$ samples in $d$ dimensions converges like $n^{-1/d}$
  <Cite id="fournier2015" />. For fixed $\varepsilon > 0$, the entropic cost converges at the parametric rate
  $n^{-1/2}$, with a constant that grows as $\varepsilon$ decreases <Cite id="genevay2019" />. MMD also converges at
  $n^{-1/2}$ <Cite id="gretton2012" />.
- **Three Sinkhorn problems.** $S_\varepsilon$ needs $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta)$ and the two
  symmetric problems. The symmetric problems have a single potential, $f = g$, and converge quickly with the averaged
  update $f \leftarrow \tfrac12\paren{f + \text{soft-}c\text{-transform}(f)}$ <Cite id="feydy2019" />.
- **Gradients.** Each term is differentiable in the sample positions and weights, with gradients given by the
  potentials, so $S_\varepsilon$ is used directly as a training loss for generative models <Cite id="genevay2018" />
  and for fitting shapes and point clouds.

## Worked example

Let $\alpha = \tfrac12\delta_0 + \tfrac12\delta_1$ on the real line, cost $c(x, y) = (x - y)^2$, and $\varepsilon =
0.5$. The entropic plan of $\alpha$ onto itself moves mass $0.0596$ each way (see
<NoteLink to="entropic-regularisation-and-sinkhorn">Sinkhorn's algorithm</NoteLink>), so its transport cost is $0.119$.
Adding $\varepsilon$ times its KL divergence from $\alpha \otimes \alpha$ gives $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha,
\alpha) = 0.283$.

Now let $\beta = \delta_{0.5}$, all the mass at the midpoint. The only plan is the product, so
$\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = 0.25$ with no entropy penalty, and $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\beta,
\beta) = 0$. The entropic cost rates the collapsed $\beta$ closer to $\alpha$ ($0.25$) than $\alpha$ itself ($0.283$).
The Sinkhorn divergence does not:

$$
S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \alpha) = 0, \qquad S_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta) = 0.25 - \tfrac12(0.283) - \tfrac12(0) = 0.108 > 0 .
$$

These values match a log-domain Sinkhorn solver.

## Pitfalls

- **Only $S_\varepsilon$, not $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon$, as a loss.** Minimising $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon$
  over a model shrinks the model. Use the debiased form when the cost is a training objective.
- **The debiased plan does not exist.** $S_\varepsilon$ is a number, not a transport plan. Tasks that need a matching
  still use the plan of $\operatorname{OT}_\varepsilon(\alpha, \beta)$, which is blurred.
- **Choice of $\varepsilon$.** $\varepsilon$ is a length scale in the units of the cost. Rescaling the data without
  rescaling $\varepsilon$ changes where $S_\varepsilon$ sits between transport and MMD.
