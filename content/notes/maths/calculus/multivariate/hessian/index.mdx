<Definition>
For a twice differentiable $f : \reals^n \to \reals$, the Hessian at $\xvec$ is the $n \times n$ matrix of second
partial derivatives

$$
\Hmat(\xvec)_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}(\xvec).
$$

It is the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> of the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. When the second partials are continuous, it is symmetric
<Cite id="nocedal2006" />.
</Definition>

Symmetry follows from the Clairaut–Schwarz theorem in <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink>:
mixed partials of a function with continuous second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> do not depend on the order of differentiation. A real
symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors.

## The second-order Taylor expansion

Fix a point $\xvec$ and a direction $\hvec$, and study $f$ along the line through them with $\phi(t) = f(\xvec +
t\hvec)$. The chain rule gives the derivatives of this one-variable function:

$$
\phi'(t) = \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,h_i = \nabla f(\xvec + t\hvec)^\top\hvec, \qquad
\phi''(t) = \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\,h_i h_j = \hvec^\top\Hmat(\xvec + t\hvec)\,\hvec.
$$

The second follows from differentiating each term of the first again, with the chain rule. The one-variable
<NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> $\phi(1) \approx \phi(0) + \phi'(0) + \tfrac12\phi''(0)$ then
reads

$$
f(\xvec + \hvec) \approx f(\xvec) + \nabla f(\xvec)^\top\hvec + \frac{1}{2}\,\hvec^\top\Hmat(\xvec)\,\hvec.
$$

The gradient gives the slope in every direction, and the Hessian gives the curvature: along a unit direction $\uvec$ the
second derivative of $f$ is $\uvec^\top\Hmat\uvec$.

## Classifying stationary points

At a stationary point, $\nabla f(\xvec) = \zeros$ and the expansion is $f(\xvec + \hvec) - f(\xvec) \approx
\tfrac12\hvec^\top\Hmat\hvec$. Write the eigendecomposition $\Hmat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top$ with eigenvalues
$\lambda_i$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $\qvec_i$. In the eigenvector coordinates $c_i = \qvec_i^\top\hvec$,

$$
\hvec^\top\Hmat\hvec = \sum_i \lambda_i\,c_i^2.
$$

The sign of this sum for small $\hvec$ decides the shape:

- **All $\lambda_i > 0$** ($\Hmat$ positive definite): every direction curves up, so $\xvec$ is a strict local minimum.
- **All $\lambda_i < 0$** (negative definite): every direction curves down, a strict local maximum.
- **Some positive, some negative** (indefinite): $f$ rises along some eigenvectors and falls along others, a saddle
  point.
- **Some $\lambda_i = 0$, the rest of one sign** (semidefinite): the test is inconclusive. $f = x^4 + y^2$ and
  $f = x^3 + y^2$ both have Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(0, 2)$ at the origin; the first has a minimum there and the
  second does not.

The eigenvalues are the curvatures along the eigenvectors, the principal directions of the surface. The
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive-definite matrices</NoteLink> note gives tests for definiteness that
avoid computing eigenvalues.

## Worked example

Take $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$. The gradient and Hessian are

$$
\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} 3x^2 - 3 \\ 2y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Hmat = \begin{bmatrix} 6x & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The gradient vanishes at $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$.

- At $(1, 0)$, $\Hmat = \operatorname{diag}(6, 2)$ is positive definite: a local minimum, with $f(1, 0) = -2$.
- At $(-1, 0)$, $\Hmat = \operatorname{diag}(-6, 2)$ is indefinite: a saddle, with $f(-1, 0) = 2$. Moving along $x$
  lowers $f$ and moving along $y$ raises it.

The minimum is only local: $f \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$.

## Newton's step

Minimise the quadratic model instead of $f$ itself. Setting the gradient of
$m(\hvec) = f + \nabla f^\top\hvec + \tfrac12\hvec^\top\Hmat\hvec$ to zero gives $\nabla f + \Hmat\hvec = \zeros$, so

$$
\hvec_{\text{Newton}} = -\Hmat^{-1}\nabla f(\xvec).
$$

When $\Hmat$ is positive definite this is a descent direction, and for a quadratic $f$ one step lands exactly on the
minimum. Near a minimum of a smooth $f$, <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> converges quadratically: the number of correct digits roughly
doubles each step <Cite id="nocedal2006" />. Each step forms and solves with an $n \times n$ matrix, which is why
large models use gradient methods or approximations of $\Hmat$.

## Curvature and gradient descent

Near a minimum $f$ is close to $\tfrac12\hvec^\top\Hmat\hvec$, and a <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient-descent</NoteLink>
step with rate $\eta$ is $\hvec \leftarrow \hvec - \eta\Hmat\hvec = (\eye - \eta\Hmat)\,\hvec$. In the eigenvector
coordinates each component evolves on its own:

$$
c_i \leftarrow (1 - \eta\lambda_i)\,c_i.
$$

Every component shrinks only if $\abs{1 - \eta\lambda_i} < 1$ for all $i$, that is $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$. With the
safe choice $\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max}$, the slowest component shrinks by the factor $1 - \lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max} =
1 - 1/\kappa$ per step, where $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ is the
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> of $\Hmat$. Reducing the error by a factor of $10^6$ takes
about 131 steps when $\kappa = 10$ and about 1,375 when $\kappa = 100$. Newton's step removes the dependence on $\kappa$
by rescaling each direction by $1/\lambda_i$.

## In machine learning

- **Loss landscapes.** In high dimensions most stationary points of a neural-network loss are saddles, not local
  minima; the Hessian's negative eigenvalues are the directions of escape.
- **Second-order optimisers** such as <Gloss name="l-bfgs" />, natural gradient and K-FAC approximate $\Hmat^{-1}\nabla f$ without
  forming $\Hmat$.
- **Hessian-vector products** $\Hmat\vvec$ cost about as much as two gradient evaluations with automatic
  differentiation, which is enough for <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">power iteration</NoteLink> on the top eigenvalue.
- **The Laplace approximation** of a posterior is a Gaussian whose precision matrix is the Hessian of the negative
  log-posterior at its mode.

## Pitfalls

- **Semidefinite Hessians.** A zero eigenvalue makes the second-derivative test inconclusive; higher-order terms decide.
- **Saddles look like minima to first order.** Gradient descent slows down near saddles because the gradient is small
  there, even though the point is not a minimum.
- **Cost and memory.** A model with $n$ parameters has $n^2$ Hessian entries: $10^{18}$ for a billion parameters.
