<Definition>
Let $\gvec : \reals^n \to \reals^m$ be differentiable at $\xvec$ and $\fvec : \reals^m \to \reals^k$ differentiable at
$\gvec(\xvec)$. Then $\fvec \circ \gvec$ is differentiable at $\xvec$, and its $k \times n$ Jacobian is the product

$$
\Jmat_{\fvec \circ \gvec}(\xvec) = \Jmat_{\fvec}\bigl(\gvec(\xvec)\bigr)\,\Jmat_{\gvec}(\xvec)
$$

of a $k \times m$ and an $m \times n$ matrix <Cite id="magnus2019" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation

The argument is the one-variable chain rule with matrices in place of numbers. Differentiability of $\gvec$ means
$\gvec(\xvec + \hvec) = \gvec(\xvec) + \Jmat_{\gvec}\hvec + o(\norm{\hvec})$. Let $\kvec = \gvec(\xvec + \hvec) -
\gvec(\xvec) = \Jmat_{\gvec}\hvec + o(\norm{\hvec})$. Differentiability of $\fvec$ at $\gvec(\xvec)$ gives

$$
\fvec\bigl(\gvec(\xvec) + \kvec\bigr) = \fvec\bigl(\gvec(\xvec)\bigr) + \Jmat_{\fvec}\,\kvec + o(\norm{\kvec}).
$$

Since $\norm{\kvec} \le \norm{\Jmat_{\gvec}}\,\norm{\hvec} + o(\norm{\hvec})$, a term that is $o(\norm{\kvec})$ is also
$o(\norm{\hvec})$. Substituting $\kvec$:

$$
\fvec\bigl(\gvec(\xvec + \hvec)\bigr) = \fvec\bigl(\gvec(\xvec)\bigr) + \Jmat_{\fvec}\Jmat_{\gvec}\,\hvec +
o(\norm{\hvec}).
$$

The linear map in this expansion is the Jacobian of the composition, so it equals $\Jmat_{\fvec}\Jmat_{\gvec}$. The
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> note covers what each matrix means on its own.

## Entry by entry

Row $i$, column $j$ of the product is

$$
\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{m} \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial g_\ell}\,\frac{\partial g_\ell}{\partial x_j}.
$$

Each intermediate variable $g_\ell$ is a path from $x_j$ to $f_i$, and the rate along the path is the product of the
two local rates. The total rate is the sum over paths. Two scalar cases come up constantly.

- **A path through space.** For $z = f(x_1(t), \dots, x_m(t))$,
  $\dfrac{dz}{dt} = \sum_\ell \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_\ell}\,\dfrac{dx_\ell}{dt} = \nabla f^\top\dot{\xvec}(t)$.
- **Explicit and implicit dependence.** For $F(t) = f(\xvec(t), t)$, the variable $t$ reaches $f$ directly and through
  $\xvec$, so $\dfrac{dF}{dt} = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \nabla_{\xvec} f^\top\dot{\xvec}(t)$. The left side
  is the total derivative; $\partial f/\partial t$ is only the direct part.

## Worked examples

**Two intermediate variables.** Let $z = x^2 y$ with $x = st$ and $y = s + t$. The partials of $z$ are $z_x = 2xy$ and
$z_y = x^2$, and $x_s = t$, $y_s = 1$, $x_t = s$, $y_t = 1$. So

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = 2xy \cdot t + x^2 \cdot 1, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = 2xy \cdot s + x^2
\cdot 1.
$$

At $(s, t) = (1, 2)$, $x = 2$ and $y = 3$, giving $\partial z / \partial s = 24 + 4 = 28$ and $\partial z / \partial t
= 12 + 4 = 16$. Expanding first, $z = s^3t^2 + s^2t^3$, gives $3s^2t^2 + 2st^3 = 28$ and $2s^3t + 3s^2t^2 = 16$, the same
values.

**Polar coordinates.** With $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$, the Jacobian of the coordinate change is

$$
\frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(r, \theta)} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta
\end{bmatrix},
$$

so for any $f(x, y)$,

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial r} = f_x\cos\theta + f_y\sin\theta, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta} =
-f_x\,r\sin\theta + f_y\,r\cos\theta.
$$

For $f = x^2 + y^2$ this gives $\partial f / \partial r = 2r\cos^2\theta + 2r\sin^2\theta = 2r$ and $\partial f /
\partial\theta = 0$, as expected for a function of the radius alone.

## Long chains and the order of multiplication

A network, or any program, is a long composition $\fvec = \fvec_L \circ \dots \circ \fvec_1$, and its Jacobian is a long
product:

$$
\Jmat = \Jmat_L\,\Jmat_{L-1} \cdots \Jmat_1.
$$

Matrix multiplication is associative, so the product can be evaluated in any order, but the cost depends on the order.
Suppose the output is a scalar loss, so $\Jmat_L$ is a single row, and every intermediate layer has width $d$.

- **Right to left**, starting from $\Jmat_1$, multiplies $d \times d$ matrices at each step: about $L d^3$ operations,
  and it yields the derivative of every intermediate quantity with respect to every input. This order follows the
  computation forwards. <NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">Forward-mode automatic differentiation</NoteLink> computes
  it one input direction at a time, as Jacobian-vector products.
- **Left to right**, starting from the row $\Jmat_L$, keeps a single row vector and multiplies it by one matrix at a
  time: about $L d^2$ operations. This is <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>, reverse-mode
  differentiation, and the row vector holds the adjoints.

For one scalar output and many inputs, left to right is cheaper by a factor of the width. For one input and many
outputs, right to left is cheaper.

## Pitfalls

- **Evaluate each factor at the right point.** $\Jmat_{\fvec}$ is evaluated at $\gvec(\xvec)$, not at $\xvec$. Mixing
  them up is the most common error in hand derivations.
- **Partial versus total derivatives.** When a variable appears both directly and through others, the partial
  derivative with the others held fixed is not the total derivative. Write out every path.
- **Shapes.** A Jacobian of a map $\reals^n \to \reals^m$ is $m \times n$. Checking that the inner dimensions of each
  product agree catches most mistakes.
