<Definition>
For $f : \reals^n \to \reals$, the partial derivative with respect to $x_i$ at $\xvec$ is

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\xvec) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\xvec + h\evec_i) - f(\xvec)}{h},
$$

where $\evec_i$ is the $i$-th standard basis vector. It is the ordinary <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of
the one-variable function obtained by fixing every coordinate except $x_i$ <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## Slices

Fixing $y = y_0$ in $f(x, y)$ leaves a function of $x$ alone, whose graph is the curve where the vertical plane
$y = y_0$ cuts the surface. Its slope at $x_0$ is $\partial f / \partial x$. Fixing $x$ instead gives
$\partial f / \partial y$. Other notations are $f_x$, $\partial_x f$ and $D_1 f$.

## Computing partials

Treat every other variable as a constant and apply the one-variable rules. For $f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin(xy)$:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2xy + y\cos(xy), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x^2 + x\cos(xy).
$$

In the first, $y$ is a constant, so $x^2 y$ differentiates to $2xy$ and the chain rule on $\sin(xy)$ gives the factor
$y$. The second holds $x$ fixed.

## Partials are not enough for differentiability

A function of several variables is differentiable at $\xvec$ if there is a vector $\gvec$ with

$$
f(\xvec + \hvec) = f(\xvec) + \gvec^\top\hvec + o(\norm{\hvec})
$$

as $\hvec \to \zeros$ from every direction. This is the total derivative: a single linear approximation valid in all
directions at once. When it exists, choosing $\hvec = h\evec_i$ shows $g_i = \partial f / \partial x_i$, so $\gvec$ is
the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>.

Partial derivatives look only along the coordinate axes, so they can exist when the total derivative does not.
**Counterexample.** Let

$$
f(x, y) = \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2} \text{ for } (x, y) \neq (0, 0), \qquad f(0, 0) = 0.
$$

On both axes $f = 0$, so $\partial f/\partial x (0, 0) = \partial f/\partial y (0, 0) = 0$. Along the diagonal $x = y =
t$, however, $f(t, t) = t^2/(2t^2) = 1/2$ for every $t \neq 0$. So $f$ is not even continuous at the origin, and it
cannot be differentiable there: a linear approximation with $\gvec = \zeros$ would predict $f \approx 0$ near the
origin.

The standard sufficient condition is continuity of the partials.

<Derivation title="Continuous partials imply differentiability">
Take $n = 2$; the general case repeats the argument one coordinate at a time. Write the change in $f$ as two steps,
first along $x$ and then along $y$:

$$
f(x + h, y + k) - f(x, y) = \bigl[f(x + h, y + k) - f(x, y + k)\bigr] + \bigl[f(x, y + k) - f(x, y)\bigr].
$$

By the one-variable <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean value theorem</NoteLink>, the first bracket is $h\,f_x(x + \theta h, y + k)$ and the second is
$k\,f_y(x, y + \phi k)$, for some $\theta, \phi \in (0, 1)$. Since $f_x$ and $f_y$ are continuous at $(x, y)$, these
equal $h\,f_x(x, y) + h\,\epsilon_1$ and $k\,f_y(x, y) + k\,\epsilon_2$ with $\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2 \to 0$ as
$(h, k) \to \zeros$. The remainder $h\epsilon_1 + k\epsilon_2$ is at most $(\abs{\epsilon_1} + \abs{\epsilon_2})
\norm{(h, k)}$, which is $o(\norm{(h, k)})$. So $f$ is differentiable with gradient $(f_x, f_y)$.
</Derivation>

## Second partials and their symmetry

Differentiating twice gives second partials such as $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$ and the mixed partials
$f_{xy} = \partial_y(\partial_x f)$ and $f_{yx} = \partial_x(\partial_y f)$. For the example above,

$$
f_{xy} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigl(2xy + y\cos(xy)\bigr) = 2x + \cos(xy) - xy\sin(xy),
$$

and differentiating $f_y = x^2 + x\cos(xy)$ with respect to $x$ gives the same expression. This is no coincidence.

**Clairaut–Schwarz theorem.** If the mixed partials $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ exist and are continuous near a point, they
are equal there. The matrix of second partials, the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>, is therefore symmetric
for every twice continuously differentiable function.

<Derivation title="Why mixed partials commute">
Consider the mixed difference

$$
\Delta(h, k) = f(x + h, y + k) - f(x + h, y) - f(x, y + k) + f(x, y).
$$

Grouped as $\bigl[u(x + h) - u(x)\bigr]$ with $u(s) = f(s, y + k) - f(s, y)$, the mean value theorem in $s$ and then in
the second argument gives $\Delta = hk\,f_{xy}(\xi, \eta)$ for some point $(\xi, \eta)$ in the rectangle. Grouped the
other way, as a difference in $y$ of differences in $x$, the same steps give $\Delta = hk\,f_{yx}(\xi', \eta')$ for
another point. So $f_{xy}(\xi, \eta) = f_{yx}(\xi', \eta')$. Letting $h, k \to 0$, both points approach $(x, y)$, and
continuity gives $f_{xy}(x, y) = f_{yx}(x, y)$.
</Derivation>

Continuity matters. For $f(x, y) = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)$ with $f(0, 0) = 0$, the mixed partials exist at the
origin but $f_{xy}(0, 0) = -1$ while $f_{yx}(0, 0) = 1$; the mixed partials are discontinuous there.

## In machine learning

- **Gradients** are vectors of partial derivatives, one per parameter. Automatic differentiation computes all of them
  together rather than one slice at a time.
- **Coordinate descent** updates one parameter at a time using only its partial derivative, as in the fitting of
  lasso regression.
- **Symmetric Hessians** are what make second-order methods and curvature analysis work with real eigenvalues.

## Pitfalls

- **Partials along axes only.** A zero partial derivative in every coordinate does not mean the function is flat in
  every direction, as the counterexample shows. Use the gradient only where $f$ is differentiable.
- **Holding the right things fixed.** When variables depend on each other, as in $f(x, y(x))$, the partial
  $\partial f / \partial x$ differs from the total derivative $df / dx = f_x + f_y\,y'(x)$. The
  <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">multivariable chain rule</NoteLink> tracks both.
