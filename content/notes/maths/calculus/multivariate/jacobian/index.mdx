<Definition>
For a differentiable $\fvec : \reals^n \to \reals^m$, the Jacobian at $\xvec$ is the $m \times n$ matrix

$$
\Jmat_{\fvec}(\xvec) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row $i$ is the transposed <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the output $f_i$ <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.
</Definition>

## Local linear approximation

Near $\xvec$, the function behaves like its Jacobian:

$$
\fvec(\xvec + \hvec) \approx \fvec(\xvec) + \Jmat_{\fvec}(\xvec)\, \hvec \quad \text{for small } \hvec.
$$

A smooth map may bend the plane globally, but a small enough neighbourhood is mapped almost linearly. The Jacobian is
that <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink>. For $m = 1$ it reduces to the gradient, as a row vector.

<Warp />

## The determinant

When $m = n$, the Jacobian is square. The absolute value of its <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is the factor by which $\fvec$ scales small
areas or volumes near $\xvec$. A negative determinant means the map reverses orientation there. A zero determinant
means it collapses some direction, and $\fvec$ is not locally invertible.

## Change of variables

If $\yvec = \fvec(\xvec)$ is invertible and differentiable and $\xvec$ has density $p_X$, then

$$
p_Y(\yvec) = p_X(\xvec)\, \abs{\det \Jmat_{\fvec}(\xvec)}^{-1}, \qquad \xvec = \fvec^{-1}(\yvec).
$$

Probability mass is conserved, so where the map stretches volume, density falls by the same factor. This formula
underlies sampling by transformation, the density of a Gaussian after a linear map, and <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flows</NoteLink>.

## The chain rule in matrix form

For a composition $\gvec \circ \fvec$, the Jacobians multiply:

$$
\Jmat_{\gvec \circ \fvec}(\xvec) = \Jmat_{\gvec}(\fvec(\xvec))\, \Jmat_{\fvec}(\xvec).
$$

Backpropagation evaluates this product from the output end, one layer at a time. The
<NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">multivariable chain rule</NoteLink> derives the product and compares the costs
of evaluating it from either end.

## Examples

| Map | Jacobian | $\det$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\fvec(\xvec) = \Amat\xvec$ | $\Amat$ | $\det \Amat$ |
| Polar to Cartesian, $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ | $\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$ | $r$ |
| Elementwise $\sigma$, $\fvec(\xvec) = (\sigma(x_1), \dots, \sigma(x_n))$ | $\diag(\sigma'(x_1), \dots, \sigma'(x_n))$ | $\prod_i \sigma'(x_i)$ |

The polar example explains why a uniform draw in the disc needs radius $\sqrt{U}$: the area element is $r\,dr\,d\theta$,
so the density in $(r, \theta)$ is proportional to $r$.

## Pitfalls

- **Layout conventions differ.** Some texts define the Jacobian as the transpose of the matrix above. Check which one a
  formula assumes before multiplying.
- **Cost.** A full Jacobian of $\fvec : \reals^n \to \reals^m$ has $mn$ entries. Machine learning rarely forms it.
  Automatic differentiation computes Jacobian–vector or <Gloss name="vjp">vector–Jacobian products</Gloss> instead.
