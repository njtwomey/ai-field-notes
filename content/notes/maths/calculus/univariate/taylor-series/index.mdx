<Definition>
The Taylor polynomial of order $n$ of a function $f$ about a point $a$ is

$$
T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x - a)^k,
$$

where $f^{(k)}$ is the $k$-th derivative. It is the unique polynomial of degree at most $n$ whose value and first $n$
derivatives at $a$ equal those of $f$. The Taylor series is the limit as $n \to \infty$ <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.
</Definition>

## Where the coefficients come from

The polynomial is built by matching derivatives. Near $a$, a function is described by its value, its slope, its
curvature and so on. A polynomial that agrees with $f$ in value and in its first $n$ derivatives should track $f$ closely near $a$.

Write a candidate polynomial of degree $n$ in powers of $(x - a)$, with unknown coefficients:

$$
p(x) = c_0 + c_1 (x - a) + c_2 (x - a)^2 + \dots + c_n (x - a)^n.
$$

Differentiate $j$ times and set $x = a$. Terms with $k < j$ have vanished, since differentiating $(x - a)^k$ more than
$k$ times gives zero. Terms with $k > j$ still contain a factor $(x - a)$, so they are zero at $x = a$. Only the $k = j$
term survives, and differentiating $(x - a)^j$ exactly $j$ times leaves $j!$:

$$
p^{(j)}(a) = j!\, c_j.
$$

Each derivative at $a$ therefore fixes exactly one coefficient. Requiring $p^{(j)}(a) = f^{(j)}(a)$ for
$j = 0, \dots, n$ gives

$$
c_j = \frac{f^{(j)}(a)}{j!},
$$

which is the Taylor polynomial. The same argument shows it is the only polynomial of degree at most $n$ that matches
those $n + 1$ derivatives.

For example, every derivative of $e^x$ is $e^x$, which equals 1 at $a = 0$. So $c_j = 1/j!$, and
$T_n(x) = 1 + x + x^2/2 + \dots + x^n/n!$. For $\sin x$ about 0 the derivatives cycle through
$0, 1, 0, -1$, so only odd powers appear, with alternating signs: $x - x^3/3! + x^5/5! - \dots$.

<Derivation title="The same result from the fundamental theorem of calculus">
Start from $f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t)\, dt$ and integrate by parts, using $-(x - t)$ as the antiderivative of 1:

$$
\int_a^x f'(t)\, dt = \Big[-(x - t) f'(t)\Big]_a^x + \int_a^x (x - t) f''(t)\, dt = (x - a) f'(a) + \int_a^x (x - t) f''(t)\, dt.
$$

Integrating by parts again, now with $-(x - t)^2/2$ as the antiderivative of $(x - t)$, produces the
$f''(a)(x - a)^2/2$ term and leaves an integral of $f'''$. After $n$ steps,

$$
f(x) = T_n(x) + \int_a^x \frac{f^{(n+1)}(t)}{n!} (x - t)^n\, dt.
$$

This is Taylor's theorem with the integral form of the remainder. The weight $(x - t)^n$ does not change sign on the
interval, so the mean value theorem for integrals replaces $f^{(n+1)}(t)$ by $f^{(n+1)}(\xi)$ for some $\xi$ between $a$
and $x$. What remains is $\int_a^x (x - t)^n\, dt = (x - a)^{n+1}/(n + 1)$, and with the $1/n!$ in front this gives
the Lagrange form below, with $(n + 1)!$ in the denominator.
</Derivation>

## Approximation error

For $f$ with $n + 1$ continuous derivatives, the error of the order-$n$ polynomial has the Lagrange form

$$
f(x) - T_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n + 1)!}(x - a)^{n+1}
$$

for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$. Near $a$, the error shrinks like $\abs{x - a}^{n+1}$. Each extra order buys one more
power of accuracy close to the expansion point.

## Radius of convergence

The series need not converge everywhere. For $1/(1 - x)$ about 0 the series is $1 + x + x^2 + \dots$, which converges
only for $\abs{x} < 1$. For $\log(1 + x)$ about 0 it converges only for $-1 < x \le 1$. In both cases the distance to
the nearest singularity bounds the interval. Functions such as $e^x$ and $\sin x$ have no singularities, and their
series converge for every $x$.

## Several variables

For $f : \reals^n \to \reals$ about $\xvec$, the second-order expansion is

$$
f(\xvec + \hvec) \approx f(\xvec) + \nabla f(\xvec)^\top \hvec + \tfrac{1}{2} \hvec^\top \Hmat(\xvec)\, \hvec,
$$

where $\Hmat$ is the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>, the matrix of second partial derivatives. Its
eigenvalues decide whether a stationary point is a minimum, a maximum or a saddle.

## In machine learning

- **Gradient descent** minimises the first-order expansion within a small step: the best direction is $-\nabla f$.
  The stability limit on the step size comes from the second-order term; see
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>.
- **Newton's method** minimises the second-order expansion exactly, stepping by $-\Hmat^{-1}\nabla f$.
- **The Laplace approximation** fits a Gaussian to a posterior using the second-order expansion of its log density at
  the mode.
- **Linearisation.** The delta method, extended Kalman filters and first-order influence estimates all replace a
  function by its first-order expansion.

## Pitfalls

- **Far from the expansion point** a high-order polynomial can be worse than a low-order one, and outside the radius
  of convergence adding terms makes it diverge.
- **Smooth is not enough.** $e^{-1/x^2}$ (with value 0 at 0) has every derivative zero at the origin. Its Taylor series
  is identically zero, yet the function is not.
