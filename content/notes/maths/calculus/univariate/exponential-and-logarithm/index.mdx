<Definition>
The exponential function $\exp : \reals \to (0, \infty)$ is the unique differentiable function with

$$
\exp'(x) = \exp(x), \qquad \exp(0) = 1.
$$

Euler's number is $e = \exp(1) \approx 2.71828$, and $\exp(x) = e^x$. The natural logarithm $\log : (0, \infty) \to
\reals$ is the inverse of $\exp$: $\log(e^x) = x$ and $e^{\log y} = y$ <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## The series and the limit

The <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> about 0 of a function equal to its own derivative has every
derivative equal to $\exp(0) = 1$, so

$$
e^x = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k!} = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{6} + \dots
$$

The series converges for every $x$, and differentiating it term by term shifts every term down by one place, which
returns the same series. So the series satisfies both defining conditions.

The same number appears as a limit of compounding. Interest of 100% a year paid in $n$ instalments multiplies savings by
$(1 + 1/n)^n$: 2 for $n = 1$, 2.25 for $n = 2$, 2.7048 for $n = 100$, approaching $e$ as $n \to \infty$. More generally,

$$
e^x = \lim_{n \to \infty} \paren{1 + \frac{x}{n}}^n.
$$

<Derivation title="Why the limit equals the series">
The binomial theorem gives

$$
\paren{1 + \frac{x}{n}}^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} \frac{x^k}{n^k}
= \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{x^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{n}{n} \cdot \frac{n-1}{n} \cdots \frac{n-k+1}{n}.
$$

For each fixed $k$ the product of fractions tends to 1 as $n \to \infty$, so each term tends to $x^k/k!$. The terms
are bounded in absolute value by $\abs{x}^k/k!$, whose sum converges, so the limit of the sum is the sum of the limits
(dominated convergence for series).
</Derivation>

## The derivative of $e^x$ from first principles

The difference quotient of $e^x$ factors:

$$
\frac{e^{x + h} - e^x}{h} = e^x \cdot \frac{e^h - 1}{h}.
$$

From the series, $e^h - 1 = h + h^2/2 + \dots$, so $(e^h - 1)/h = 1 + h/2 + \dots \to 1$. The derivative is $e^x$. This
limit is what the table of common derivatives in the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> note relies on.

## The exponential law

$e^{a + b} = e^a e^b$ follows from the defining property alone. Fix $a$ and let $g(x) = e^{a + x} e^{-x}$. The product
and chain rules give

$$
g'(x) = e^{a + x} e^{-x} - e^{a + x} e^{-x} = 0,
$$

so $g$ is constant (by the <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean value theorem</NoteLink>) and equals
$g(0) = e^a$. Hence $e^{a + x} e^{-x} = e^a$ for every $a$ and $x$. With $a = 0$ this reads $e^x e^{-x} = 1$, so
$e^{-x} = 1/e^x$. Substituting back gives $e^{a + x} = e^a e^x$, and the law follows with $x = b$.

## The logarithm

Because $e^x$ is positive and increasing, it has an inverse on $(0, \infty)$. Differentiating $e^{\log y} = y$ with
the chain rule gives $e^{\log y} \cdot (\log y)' = 1$, so

$$
\frac{d}{dy} \log y = \frac{1}{y}.
$$

The exponential law turns into the logarithm's rules. With $a = \log u$ and $b = \log v$, $e^{a + b} = e^a e^b = uv$,
so $\log(uv) = \log u + \log v$. Similarly $\log(u/v) = \log u - \log v$ and $\log(u^r) = r \log u$.

## Why machine learning works in log space

A likelihood is a product of many probabilities. The logarithm turns it into a sum:

$$
\log \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

- **Underflow.** A product of $n$ probabilities of about 0.01 each is $10^{-2n}$. In
  <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">double precision</NoteLink> that is 0 once $n \ge 162$, and every
  <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of it is 0 too. The sum of logs is about $-4.6\,n$, which is perfectly
  representable.
- **Same optimum.** $\log$ is increasing, so the parameters that maximise the log-likelihood maximise the likelihood.
- **Easier derivatives.** The derivative of a sum is a sum of derivatives. The derivative of a product needs the product
  rule for every factor.

## The log-sum-exp trick

Normalising constants and mixture likelihoods need $\log \sum_i e^{x_i}$ where the $x_i$ are themselves logs. Computing
$e^{x_i}$ directly overflows when some $x_i$ is large and underflows when all are very negative. Factor out the largest
term, $m = \max_i x_i$:

$$
\log \sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{x_i} = \log \paren{e^m \sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{x_i - m}} = m + \log \sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{x_i - m}.
$$

Every exponent $x_i - m$ is at most 0, so no term overflows, and the largest term is exactly $e^0 = 1$, so the sum is at
least 1 and its logarithm is finite <Cite id="blanchard2021" />.

**Worked example.** For $\xvec = (1000, 1001, 1002)$, computing $e^{1000}$ overflows to infinity. With $m = 1002$,

$$
1002 + \log\paren{e^{-2} + e^{-1} + 1} = 1002 + 0.4076 = 1002.4076.
$$

The same identity bounds the result: since the sum lies between 1 and $n$,
$\max_i x_i \le \log \sum_i e^{x_i} \le \max_i x_i + \log n$. Log-sum-exp is a smooth version of the maximum; its
gradient is the softmax, as derived in <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax and log-sum-exp</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **$\log(1 + x)$ for small $x$.** Computing $1 + x$ first rounds away most of $x$'s digits when $\abs{x}$ is tiny.
  Use `log1p(x)`, and `expm1(x)` for $e^x - 1$.
- **Log of zero.** A model that assigns probability exactly 0 to an observed event has log-likelihood $-\infty$.
  Probabilities computed in log space from the start, for example with log-softmax, avoid forming the zero.
- **Other bases.** $\log_2 x = \log x / \log 2$. Entropies in bits use base 2; derivatives are simplest in base $e$,
  which is why $e$ is the default in calculus and in most libraries.
