<Definition>
If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, there is a point $c \in (a, b)$ with

$$
f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.
$$

The right side is the average <NoteLink to="derivative">rate of change</NoteLink> of $f$ over the interval, the slope of
the chord from $(a, f(a))$ to $(b, f(b))$. At some interior point the instantaneous rate equals it
<Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## Rolle's theorem

The special case $f(a) = f(b)$ is Rolle's theorem: some $c \in (a, b)$ has $f'(c) = 0$.

**Proof.** A continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum and a minimum (the extreme value theorem). If
both are attained only at the endpoints, then the maximum and the minimum both equal $f(a) = f(b)$, so $f$ is constant
and $f'(c) = 0$ everywhere. Otherwise an extremum occurs at an interior point $c$. Suppose it is a maximum. Then
$f(c + h) - f(c) \le 0$ for every small $h$. The difference quotient is $\le 0$ for $h > 0$ and $\ge 0$ for $h < 0$, so
its limit $f'(c)$ is both $\le 0$ and $\ge 0$, hence 0. A minimum is the same with the signs reversed.

## Proof of the theorem

Subtract the chord from $f$:

$$
g(x) = f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}\,(x - a).
$$

Then $g(a) = g(b) = 0$, and $g$ is continuous and differentiable wherever $f$ is. Rolle's theorem gives a $c$ with
$0 = g'(c) = f'(c) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$, which is the statement.

## Consequences

Each consequence converts a statement about $f'$ into one about $f$, by writing $f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)\,(y - x)$.

- **Zero derivative means constant.** If $f' = 0$ on an interval, then $f(y) - f(x) = 0$ for every $x, y$ in it.
- **Positive derivative means increasing.** If $f' > 0$, then $f(y) - f(x) > 0$ whenever $y > x$.
- **Bounded derivative means Lipschitz.** If $\abs{f'} \le L$, then

$$
\abs{f(y) - f(x)} = \abs{f'(c)}\,\abs{y - x} \le L\,\abs{y - x}.
$$

The same holds in several variables. For differentiable $f : \reals^n \to \reals$, apply the theorem to
$\phi(t) = f(\xvec + t(\yvec - \xvec))$ on $[0, 1]$. The chain rule gives $\phi'(t) = \nabla f(\zvec)^\top(\yvec -
\xvec)$ at $\zvec = \xvec + t(\yvec - \xvec)$, and Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$\abs{f(\yvec) - f(\xvec)} \le \sup_{\zvec} \norm{\nabla f(\zvec)}\,\norm{\yvec - \xvec}$.

## Smoothness and the gradient-descent step size

A differentiable $f$ is $L$-smooth if its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is $L$-Lipschitz: $\norm{\nabla f(\yvec) - \nabla f(\xvec)} \le
L\,\norm{\yvec - \xvec}$. Smoothness bounds how far $f$ can rise above its tangent plane. Along the segment from $\xvec$
to $\yvec$, the <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink> applied to
$\phi$ above gives

$$
f(\yvec) - f(\xvec) - \nabla f(\xvec)^\top(\yvec - \xvec)
= \int_0^1 \bigl(\nabla f(\xvec + t(\yvec - \xvec)) - \nabla f(\xvec)\bigr)^\top (\yvec - \xvec)\,dt
\le \int_0^1 L t\,\norm{\yvec - \xvec}^2\,dt = \frac{L}{2}\norm{\yvec - \xvec}^2.
$$

This is the descent lemma <Cite id="boyd2004" />. Take a <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient-descent</NoteLink>
step $\yvec = \xvec - \eta\nabla f(\xvec)$:

$$
f(\yvec) \le f(\xvec) - \eta\paren{1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}}\norm{\nabla f(\xvec)}^2.
$$

For any $0 < \eta < 2/L$ the bracket is positive, so every step decreases $f$ unless the gradient is zero. The choice
$\eta = 1/L$ maximises the guaranteed decrease, giving $f(\yvec) \le f(\xvec) - \norm{\nabla f(\xvec)}^2/(2L)$. The
bound $2/L$ is tight: for $f(x) = \tfrac{L}{2}x^2$ a step gives $x \leftarrow (1 - \eta L)\,x$, which diverges once
$\eta > 2/L$.

## Worked examples

**Estimating a square root.** Apply the theorem to $\sqrt{x}$ on $[100, 101]$:
$\sqrt{101} - 10 = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{c}}$ for some $c \in (100, 101)$. Since $100 < c < 101 < 10.05^2$, we have
$10 < \sqrt{c} < 10.05$, and the
difference lies between $\frac{1}{2 \times 10.05} \approx 0.04975$ and $\frac{1}{20} = 0.05$. So
$10.04975 < \sqrt{101} < 10.05$; the true value is $10.04988$.

**A Lipschitz bound.** $\abs{\sin' x} = \abs{\cos x} \le 1$, so $\abs{\sin y - \sin x} \le \abs{y - x}$ for all $x, y$.
A network layer $\xvec \mapsto \sigma(\Wmat\xvec)$ with an activation whose derivative is at most 1 inherits a Lipschitz
constant of at most $\norm{\Wmat}_2$, which is what spectral normalisation controls.

## Pitfalls

- **Differentiability is needed everywhere inside.** $\abs{x}$ on $[-1, 1]$ has equal endpoint values but no point with
  zero derivative; the kink at 0 breaks Rolle's theorem.
- **The theorem says nothing about where $c$ is.** It guarantees existence only. Bounds come from bounding $f'$ over
  the whole interval.
- **No vector-valued version.** For $\fvec : \reals \to \reals^2$ with $\fvec(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$ on $[0, 2\pi]$ the
  chord is zero but $\fvec'$ never vanishes. Use the inequality $\norm{\fvec(b) - \fvec(a)} \le \sup \norm{\fvec'}\,(b -
  a)$ instead.
