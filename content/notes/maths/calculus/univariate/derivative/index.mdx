<Definition>
The derivative of $f : \reals \to \reals$ at $a$ is

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h},
$$

when the limit exists. The fraction is the difference quotient: the slope of the secant line through $(a, f(a))$ and
$(a + h, f(a + h))$. A function whose derivative exists at $a$ is differentiable at $a$ <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## Secants and tangents

As $h$ shrinks, the secant's second point slides along the curve towards $(a, f(a))$ and the secant turns into the
tangent line. The derivative is the tangent's slope. The limit must be the same whether $h$ approaches 0 from above or
from below.

## The best linear approximation

Write the error of the tangent line as $r(h) = f(a + h) - f(a) - f'(a)\,h$. Dividing by $h$ gives

$$
\frac{r(h)}{h} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} - f'(a) \to 0 \quad \text{as } h \to 0.
$$

So $f(a + h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + o(h)$, where $o(h)$ is a term that vanishes faster than $h$. Conversely, if some number
$m$ makes $f(a + h) - f(a) - m h = o(h)$, dividing by $h$ shows the difference quotient tends to $m$, so $m = f'(a)$.
The derivative is therefore the unique slope for which the line $f(a) + m h$ matches $f$ to first order. This form is
the one that generalises to several variables, where the slope becomes the
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, and to higher orders, in the <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor
series</NoteLink>.

A differentiable function is continuous, because $f(a + h) - f(a) = h \cdot \tfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \to 0 \cdot f'(a)
= 0$. The converse fails, as the pitfalls below show.

## The rules of differentiation

Each rule follows from the definition.

**Sums and multiples.** Limits are linear, so $(\alpha f + \beta g)' = \alpha f' + \beta g'$.

**Product rule.** Add and subtract $f(a + h)\,g(a)$ in the numerator:

$$
\frac{f(a+h)g(a+h) - f(a)g(a)}{h} = f(a + h)\,\frac{g(a + h) - g(a)}{h} + g(a)\,\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}.
$$

As $h \to 0$, $f(a + h) \to f(a)$ because $f$ is continuous, and the two quotients tend to $g'(a)$ and $f'(a)$. Hence

$$
(fg)'(a) = f(a)\,g'(a) + f'(a)\,g(a).
$$

**Chain rule.** Use the linear approximation twice. Let $k = g(a + h) - g(a) = g'(a)\,h + o(h)$. Then

$$
f(g(a + h)) = f(g(a) + k) = f(g(a)) + f'(g(a))\,k + o(k).
$$

Since $k$ is proportional to $h$ to first order, $o(k)$ is also $o(h)$. Substituting $k$ gives
$f(g(a + h)) = f(g(a)) + f'(g(a))\,g'(a)\,h + o(h)$, and the uniqueness of the linear approximation gives

$$
(f \circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a))\,g'(a).
$$

**Quotient rule.** First, for $r(t) = 1/t$,

$$
\frac{r(t + h) - r(t)}{h} = \frac{1}{h}\paren{\frac{1}{t + h} - \frac{1}{t}} = \frac{-1}{t\,(t + h)} \to -\frac{1}{t^2}.
$$

The chain rule then gives $(1/g)' = -g'/g^2$, and the product rule applied to $f \cdot (1/g)$ gives

$$
\paren{\frac{f}{g}}' = \frac{f'}{g} - \frac{f\,g'}{g^2} = \frac{f'g - f\,g'}{g^2}.
$$

## Common derivatives

| $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ | Derivation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $x^n$, integer $n \ge 1$ | $n x^{n-1}$ | Expand $(x + h)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1} h + h^2(\dots)$; the quotient is $n x^{n-1} + h(\dots)$. |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ | $\tfrac{e^{x+h} - e^x}{h} = e^x\,\tfrac{e^h - 1}{h}$, and $\tfrac{e^h - 1}{h} \to 1$. |
| $\log x$, $x > 0$ | $1/x$ | Differentiate $e^{\log x} = x$ with the chain rule: $e^{\log x}(\log x)' = 1$. |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | $\sin(x + h) - \sin x = \sin x\,(\cos h - 1) + \cos x \sin h$; use $\tfrac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$, $\tfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$. |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | The same expansion of $\cos(x + h)$. |

The limit $(e^h - 1)/h \to 1$ is the defining property of $e$; the
<NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">exponential and logarithm</NoteLink> note derives it. The rule for $x^n$ extends
to every real exponent: $x^r = e^{r \log x}$, so the chain rule gives $(x^r)' = e^{r \log x} \cdot r/x = r x^{r-1}$.

## Worked examples

**A product.** For $f(x) = x^2 e^{-x}$, the product and chain rules give

$$
f'(x) = 2x\,e^{-x} + x^2 \cdot (-e^{-x}) = x\,e^{-x}(2 - x).
$$

The derivative is zero at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. It is negative for $x < 0$, positive between 0 and 2, and negative after
2, so $f$ has a minimum at 0 and a maximum at 2, where $f(2) = 4e^{-2} \approx 0.541$.

**The sigmoid.** For $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$, the rule $(1/g)' = -g'/g^2$ with $g = 1 + e^{-x}$ gives

$$
\sigma'(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr).
$$

The derivative is largest at $x = 0$, where it equals $1/4$, and it decays towards 0 in both tails. That decay is why
saturated sigmoid units pass back almost no gradient in
<NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>.

## In machine learning

- **Training** needs the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter. The rules above, applied
  mechanically along a computation, are what automatic differentiation implements.
- **Activation functions** are chosen partly for their derivatives: ReLU has derivative 1 on its active side and does
  not saturate there, unlike the sigmoid.
- **Finite differences** approximate $f'(a)$ with a small $h$. The central quotient $\tfrac{f(a + h) - f(a - h)}{2h}$
  has error of order $h^2$ rather than $h$, and is the standard check on hand-written gradients.

## Pitfalls

- **Continuity is not enough.** $\abs{x}$ is continuous at 0, but its difference quotient is $+1$ for $h > 0$ and $-1$
  for $h < 0$, so the limit does not exist. ReLU, $\max(0, x)$, has the same kink. Frameworks return a fixed value
  there (usually 0), which is a valid subgradient.
- **Step size in finite differences.** Too large an $h$ gives truncation error; too small an $h$ subtracts nearly equal
  floating-point numbers and loses precision. In double precision $h \approx 10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ is a reasonable
  choice for the central quotient.
