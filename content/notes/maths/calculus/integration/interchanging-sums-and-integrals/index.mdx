<Definition>
Let $f_1, f_2, \dots$ be integrable functions. Then

$$
\int \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(x)\,dx = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \int f_n(x)\,dx
$$

holds in either of two cases <Cite id="folland1999" />:

- **Tonelli.** Every $f_n \ge 0$. The two sides are then equal, possibly both $+\infty$.
- **Fubini.** $\sum_n \int \abs{f_n(x)}\,dx < \infty$. The series $\sum_n f_n(x)$ then converges for almost every $x$,
  and both sides are finite and equal.
</Definition>

## Why a condition is needed

A sum is a limit, $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n = \lim_N S_N$ with partial sums $S_N = \sum_{n \le N} f_n$. For a finite sum,
the integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals. So the question is whether
$\int \lim_N S_N = \lim_N \int S_N$: whether a limit can be taken inside an integral. In general it cannot.

**Counterexample.** On $[0, 1]$, let $g_N = N$ on the interval $(0, 1/N)$ and $0$ elsewhere, a spike of area 1. Set
$f_1 = g_1$ and $f_n = g_n - g_{n-1}$ for $n \ge 2$. The partial sums telescope to $S_N = g_N$.

- Each $\int f_n = \int g_n - \int g_{n-1} = 1 - 1 = 0$ for $n \ge 2$, and $\int f_1 = 1$, so
  $\sum_n \int f_n = 1$.
- For each fixed $x > 0$, $g_N(x) = 0$ once $N > 1/x$, so $\sum_n f_n(x) = \lim_N g_N(x) = 0$ and
  $\int \sum_n f_n = 0$.

The two sides are $0$ and $1$. The mass of the spikes escapes into a single point as $N$ grows, where no fixed
integrable function can contain it.

<VanishingSpike />

The theorem's conditions rule this out. The terms change sign, so Tonelli does not apply. And
$\int \abs{f_n} = 2/n$ for $n \ge 2$: the spike $g_n$ and the negative part of $f_n$ each contribute $1/n$. So
$\sum_n \int\abs{f_n}$ is the harmonic series, which diverges, and Fubini does not apply either.

## The two theorems behind it

Both statements are consequences of the two basic convergence theorems of integration.

**Monotone convergence.** If $0 \le h_1 \le h_2 \le \dots$ and $h_N \to h$ pointwise, then $\int h_N \to \int h$.
An increasing sequence of non-negative functions cannot lose mass in the limit, because nothing can escape
downwards.

**Dominated convergence.** If $h_N \to h$ pointwise and $\abs{h_N} \le G$ for all $N$ with $\int G < \infty$, then
$\int h_N \to \int h$. A single integrable function $G$ fences in every $h_N$, so no mass can escape. The spikes above
have no such fence: $\sup_N g_N(x) \approx 1/x$ near 0, which is not integrable.

**Tonelli from monotone convergence.** If every $f_n \ge 0$, the partial sums $S_N$ increase with $N$ and converge to
$\sum_n f_n$. Monotone convergence gives $\int \sum_n f_n = \lim_N \int S_N = \lim_N \sum_{n \le N} \int f_n =
\sum_n \int f_n$. The middle step is linearity of the integral for a finite sum.

**Fubini from Tonelli and dominated convergence.** Apply Tonelli to the non-negative functions $\abs{f_n}$: the
function $G = \sum_n \abs{f_n}$ has $\int G = \sum_n \int \abs{f_n} < \infty$. So $G$ is finite almost everywhere, and
$\sum_n f_n(x)$ converges absolutely there. Every partial sum satisfies $\abs{S_N} \le \sum_{n \le N}\abs{f_n} \le G$,
so dominated convergence applies to $S_N$ and gives $\int \sum_n f_n = \lim_N \int S_N = \sum_n \int f_n$.

The same argument covers the other common swaps, since each is a limit taken inside an integral: the limit of a
sequence of integrals, and a derivative taken inside an integral, which is a limit of <NoteLink to="derivative">difference quotients</NoteLink>.

## Sums of sums

A sum is an integral with respect to counting measure, so the theorem covers double series as well. For numbers
$a_{mn}$,

$$
\sum_m \sum_n a_{mn} = \sum_n \sum_m a_{mn} \qquad \text{if every } a_{mn} \ge 0 \text{ or } \sum_{m,n} \abs{a_{mn}} < \infty.
$$

**Worked example.** Let $a_{mm} = 1$, $a_{m,m+1} = -1$, and every other entry 0. Each row sums to $1 - 1 = 0$, so
summing rows first gives $0$. Column 1 contains only $a_{11} = 1$; every later column contains a $1$ and a $-1$. So
summing columns first gives $1$. The entries are not absolutely summable, since $\sum \abs{a_{mn}} = \infty$, and the
condition fails.

## In machine learning

- **Expectation of a series.** $\expect\brack{\sum_n X_n} = \sum_n \expect[X_n]$ holds for non-negative $X_n$, and
  whenever $\sum_n \expect\abs{X_n} < \infty$. This justifies, for example, computing the mean of a count as a sum of
  indicator expectations over infinitely many possible events <Cite id="billingsley2012" />.
- **Moments from a generating function.** If $\expect[e^{t\abs{X}}] < \infty$ for some $t > 0$, the series
  $e^{tX} = \sum_k t^k X^k / k!$ is dominated by $e^{\abs{t X}}$, so $\expect[e^{tX}] = \sum_k t^k \expect[X^k]/k!$ and the
  moments are the Taylor coefficients of the <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">moment-generating
  function</NoteLink>.
- **<NoteLink to="gradient">Gradients</NoteLink> of expectations.** $\nabla_\theta \expect[f_\theta(X)] = \expect[\nabla_\theta f_\theta(X)]$ needs the
  same kind of domination, now on the difference quotients. It is the step behind every stochastic-gradient estimator
  that differentiates inside an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Pointwise convergence is not enough.** The counterexample converges to 0 at every point and still fails.
- **Uniform convergence on an infinite domain is not enough either.** $f_N = \tfrac1N$ on $[0, N]$ converges
  uniformly to 0 on $[0, \infty)$, yet every $\int f_N = 1$. On a bounded interval, uniform convergence does suffice.
- **Check absolute summability, not just convergence.** A series of integrals can converge while
  $\sum \int\abs{f_n}$ diverges, and then Fubini gives no guarantee.
