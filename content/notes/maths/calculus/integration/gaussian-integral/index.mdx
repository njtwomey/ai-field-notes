<Definition>
The Gaussian integral is

$$
I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx = \sqrt{\pi}.
$$

The function $e^{-x^2}$ has no antiderivative expressible in elementary functions, so the
<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem</NoteLink> cannot evaluate it directly
<Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation

Square the integral and write the square as a double integral over the plane, using a second variable $y$:

$$
I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2}\, dy
= \iint_{\reals^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy.
$$

The integrand depends only on the distance $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, so polar coordinates suit it. With
$dx\, dy = r\, dr\, d\theta$ (see <NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">multiple integrals</NoteLink>),

$$
I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta = 2\pi \Big[-\tfrac{1}{2} e^{-r^2}\Big]_0^\infty
= 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \pi.
$$

Since $I > 0$, $I = \sqrt{\pi}$. The factor $r$ from the <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">change of coordinates</NoteLink> is exactly what makes $e^{-r^2}$ integrable in closed form: $r e^{-r^2}$
is the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of $-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-r^2}$. Swapping to polar coordinates is
justified by Tonelli's theorem, because the integrand is positive.

## The one-dimensional Gaussian

The substitution $u = (x - \mu)/(\sigma\sqrt{2})$, with $dx = \sigma\sqrt{2}\, du$, gives

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\paren{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\, dx = \sigma\sqrt{2}\, \sqrt{\pi} =
  \sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}.
$$

Dividing by this constant makes the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian density</NoteLink> integrate to 1:
$p(x) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2} \exp\paren{-(x - \mu)^2 / (2\sigma^2)}$.

## The multivariate Gaussian

For $\xvec \in \reals^d$ and a symmetric positive definite $\Sigmamat$,

$$
\int_{\reals^d} \exp\paren{-\tfrac{1}{2}(\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec)}\, d\xvec
= (2\pi)^{d/2} \abs{\Sigmamat}^{1/2}.
$$

Derivation: write $\Sigmamat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top$ with $\Qmat$ orthogonal and
$\Lambdamat = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$, all $\lambda_i > 0$. Substitute $\yvec = \Qmat^\top(\xvec - \muvec)$.
The map is a rotation followed by a shift, so its <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian determinant</NoteLink> has absolute value 1 and $d\xvec = d\yvec$. The
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> becomes $\yvec^\top\Lambdamat^{-1}\yvec = \sum_i y_i^2/\lambda_i$, so the integrand factorises into $d$
one-dimensional Gaussians:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{d} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y_i^2/(2\lambda_i)}\, dy_i = \prod_{i=1}^{d} \sqrt{2\pi\lambda_i}
= (2\pi)^{d/2} \paren{\prod_i \lambda_i}^{1/2} = (2\pi)^{d/2}\abs{\Sigmamat}^{1/2}.
$$

The last step uses that the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is the product of the eigenvalues. This is the normalising constant of the
<NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal distribution</NoteLink> <Cite id="bishop2006" />.

## Moments by differentiating under the integral

For $a > 0$, the substitution $u = \sqrt{a}\, x$ gives $J(a) = \int e^{-a x^2}\, dx = \sqrt{\pi}\, a^{-1/2}$.
Differentiating both sides with respect to $a$ brings down a factor $-x^2$ inside the integral:

$$
\int x^2 e^{-a x^2}\, dx = -J'(a) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\, a^{-3/2}, \qquad
\int x^4 e^{-a x^2}\, dx = J''(a) = \frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\, a^{-5/2}.
$$

With $a = 1/2$ and dividing by $\sqrt{2\pi}$, these give the standard normal moments $\expect[X^2] = 1$ and
$\expect[X^4] = 3$. Differentiating under the integral sign is valid here because the differentiated integrands are
dominated by an integrable function uniformly for $a$ in a neighbourhood.

## Pitfalls

- **The finite-interval integral** $\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\, dt$ has no closed form. It defines the error function,
  $\operatorname{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^x e^{-t^2} dt$, which is computed numerically, and in terms of
  which the normal cdf is $\Phi(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\paren{1 + \operatorname{erf}(z/\sqrt{2})}$.
- **Positive definiteness.** The multivariate formula needs every eigenvalue of $\Sigmamat$ to be positive. With a zero
  eigenvalue the integral diverges along that direction; with a negative one it is not a Gaussian at all.
