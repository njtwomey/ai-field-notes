<Definition>
For $\alpha > 0$, the gamma function is

$$
\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t}\, dt.
$$

It satisfies $\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha\,\Gamma(\alpha)$ and $\Gamma(1) = 1$, so $\Gamma(n + 1) = n!$ for every
non-negative integer $n$ <Cite id="apostol1967" />.
</Definition>

## The recursion and the factorial

<NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">Integration by parts</NoteLink> with $u = t^\alpha$ and $dv = e^{-t}\, dt$ gives

$$
\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \Big[-t^\alpha e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty + \alpha\int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t}\, dt =
  \alpha\,\Gamma(\alpha).
$$

The boundary term is zero: $t^\alpha \to 0$ as $t \to 0$ for $\alpha > 0$, and $e^{-t}$ beats any power as
$t \to \infty$. Directly, $\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\, dt = 1$. Applying the recursion $n$ times,
$\Gamma(n + 1) = n(n - 1)\cdots 1 \cdot \Gamma(1) = n!$.

## The value at one half

Substitute $t = x^2$, so $dt = 2x\, dx$ and $t^{-1/2} = 1/x$:

$$
\Gamma\paren{\tfrac{1}{2}} = \int_0^\infty t^{-1/2} e^{-t}\, dt = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{x}\, e^{-x^2}\, 2x\, dx
= 2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\, dx = \sqrt{\pi},
$$

by the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>. The recursion then gives every half-integer, e.g.
$\Gamma(5/2) = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi} \approx 1.329$.

## The beta function

The beta function $B(a, b) = \int_0^1 u^{a - 1}(1 - u)^{b - 1}\, du$ normalises the
<NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta distribution</NoteLink>. It is a ratio of gamma functions:

$$
B(a, b) = \frac{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a + b)}.
$$

Derivation: write the product $\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)$ as a <NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">double integral</NoteLink> over $s, t > 0$,

$$
\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b) = \int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty s^{a - 1} t^{b - 1} e^{-(s + t)}\, ds\, dt,
$$

and change variables to the total $r = s + t$ and the fraction $u = s/(s + t)$, so $s = ru$ and $t = r(1 - u)$. The
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> of $(r, u) \mapsto (s, t)$ is

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} u & r \\ 1 - u & -r \end{bmatrix} = -ru - r(1 - u) = -r,
$$

so $ds\, dt = r\, dr\, du$. The integrand becomes $(ru)^{a - 1}\paren{r(1 - u)}^{b - 1} e^{-r}\, r$, which separates:

$$
\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b) = \int_0^\infty r^{a + b - 1} e^{-r}\, dr \int_0^1 u^{a - 1}(1 - u)^{b - 1}\, du = \Gamma(a + b)\,
  B(a, b).
$$

For example, $B(3, 4) = 2!\,3!/6! = 12/720 = 1/60$. The same <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink> shows that normalising two
independent gamma variables gives a beta variable, and normalising $K$ of them gives a Dirichlet.

## Stirling's approximation

For large $n$,

$$
n! \approx \sqrt{2\pi n}\, \paren{\frac{n}{e}}^n.
$$

Derivation by Laplace's method: write $n! = \Gamma(n + 1) = \int_0^\infty e^{\,n\log t - t}\, dt$ and substitute
$t = ns$:

$$
n! = n^{n + 1} \int_0^\infty e^{\,n(\log s - s)}\, ds.
$$

The exponent $f(s) = \log s - s$ has its maximum at $s = 1$, where $f(1) = -1$ and $f''(1) = -1$. For large $n$ the
integrand is sharply peaked there, so a <NoteLink to="hessian">second-order Taylor expansion</NoteLink> $f(s) \approx -1 - \tfrac{1}{2}(s - 1)^2$
suffices, and the integral becomes a Gaussian:

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{\,n(\log s - s)}\, ds \approx e^{-n} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-n(s - 1)^2/2}\, ds =
  e^{-n}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}}.
$$

Multiplying by $n^{n + 1}$ gives Stirling's formula. The general method is the
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink>. The relative error is about $1/(12n)$: for
$n = 10$ the formula gives 3,598,696 against $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$, a ratio of 0.9917.

In logarithms, $\log n! \approx n\log n - n + \tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi n)$. This is how factorials in likelihoods, such as
binomial coefficients, are handled for large counts.

## In machine learning

- **Normalising constants.** The gamma, <NoteLink to="inverse-gamma-distribution">inverse gamma</NoteLink>, beta, Dirichlet, Student t and $\chi^2$ densities all contain
  gamma functions. Software evaluates $\log\Gamma$ directly, because $\Gamma$ overflows <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">double precision</NoteLink> above
  $\alpha \approx 171$.
- **The digamma function** $\psi(\alpha) = \frac{d}{d\alpha}\log\Gamma(\alpha)$ appears in the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink>
  $\expect[\log\theta_k] = \psi(\alpha_k) - \psi(\sum_j \alpha_j)$ under a Dirichlet, which <Gloss name="vi">variational inference</Gloss> for
  topic models needs <Cite id="bishop2006" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Off-by-one.** $\Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!$, not $n!$.
- **Poles.** Extended to the whole real line through the recursion, $\Gamma$ is infinite at $0, -1, -2, \dots$ and
  negative on some intervals between them. Only $\alpha > 0$ appears in probability densities.
