<Definition>
The double integral $\iint_R f(x, y)\, dx\, dy$ over a region $R$ is the limit of sums $\sum f(x_i, y_i)\, \Delta A_i$
over ever finer partitions of $R$ into small cells of area $\Delta A_i$. An iterated integral integrates one variable
at a time, e.g. $\int \paren{\int f(x, y)\, dy} dx$.
</Definition>

## Fubini and Tonelli

**Tonelli's theorem.** If $f \ge 0$, the double integral equals both iterated integrals, in either order, whether or not
they are finite.

**Fubini's theorem.** If $\iint \abs{f}\, dx\, dy < \infty$, the double integral equals both iterated integrals, in
either order <Cite id="folland1999" />.

The idea behind both is that the sum over a grid of cells can be added up row by row or column by column. For finite
sums the order never matters. For limits it matters only when positive and negative parts are each infinite, so that
the total depends on the order in which they cancel. Tonelli rules this out by making every term non-negative, and
Fubini by requiring the absolute values to sum to something finite.

On a rectangle with $f(x, y) = g(x)\, h(y)$, the double integral factorises:
$\int_a^b \int_c^d g(x) h(y)\, dy\, dx = \paren{\int_a^b g}\paren{\int_c^d h}$. This is why <NoteLink to="independence">independent random variables</NoteLink>
have $\expect[g(X) h(Y)] = \expect[g(X)]\, \expect[h(Y)]$.

## When the order matters

Take $f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2$ on the unit square. Since
$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2} = f$,

$$
\int_0^1 \int_0^1 f\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \frac{\pi}{4}, \qquad
\int_0^1 \int_0^1 f\, dx\, dy = -\frac{\pi}{4},
$$

where the second follows by the symmetry $f(x, y) = -f(y, x)$. The two orders disagree because
$\iint \abs{f} = \infty$: near the origin $\abs{f}$ behaves like $1/r^2$, and in polar coordinates
$\int_0 (1/r^2)\, r\, dr$ diverges. Fubini's hypothesis fails.

## Polar coordinates

With $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$,

$$
\iint f(x, y)\, dx\, dy = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\infty f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\, r\, dr\, d\theta.
$$

The factor $r$ is the area of the small cell swept out by steps $dr$ and $d\theta$. The cell is nearly a rectangle with
sides $dr$ (radial) and $r\, d\theta$ (an arc of radius $r$), so its area is $r\, dr\, d\theta$. Formally, $r$ is the
absolute <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> of the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> of the map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (x, y)$:

$$
\det \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = r\cos^2\theta +
  r\sin^2\theta = r.
$$

The general rule, $dx\, dy = \abs{\det \Jmat}\, du\, dv$ for any smooth invertible change of variables, is derived in
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

The area of the unit disc is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r\, dr\, d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \pi$. The integral
$\iint e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy$ over the whole plane becomes $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r\, dr\, d\theta
= 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \pi$, which gives the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Checking absolute integrability.** Swapping the order of integration, or of an integral and an infinite sum, is
  safe for non-negative integrands and for absolutely integrable ones. For signed integrands without that check, the
  answer can change, as in the example above.
- **Limits of the inner integral.** On a non-rectangular region the inner limits depend on the outer variable. Sketch
  the region before swapping the order.
