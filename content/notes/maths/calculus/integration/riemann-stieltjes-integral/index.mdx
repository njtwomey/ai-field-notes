<Definition>
Let $f$ and $g$ be real functions on $[a, b]$. For a partition $a = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = b$ and points
$\tau_j \in [t_j, t_{j+1}]$, form the sum $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} f(\tau_j)\,\paren{g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j)}$. If these sums
approach one limit as the largest step shrinks to 0, whatever the choice of the points $\tau_j$, that limit is the
**Riemann–Stieltjes integral** $\int_a^b f\,dg$ <Cite id="stieltjes1894" />. The function $f$ is the integrand and $g$
the integrator.
</Definition>

## Intuition: weighting by how much $g$ moves

The ordinary <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">Riemann integral</NoteLink> $\int_a^b f(t)\,dt$ weights $f$ by the length of each step, $t_{j+1} - t_j$. The
Riemann–Stieltjes integral weights $f$ by how much $g$ changes over each step, $g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j)$. Where $g$ is flat,
$f$ counts for nothing. Where $g$ rises steeply, $f$ counts heavily. Where $g$ jumps, the value of $f$ at the jump is
counted with the size of the jump as its weight.

Three integrators show the range:

- $g(t) = t$ gives the ordinary Riemann integral.
- A smooth $g$ stretches the time axis: $\int f\,dg = \int f(t)\,g'(t)\,dt$.
- A step function $g$ turns the integral into a weighted sum of values of $f$ at the jumps.

A single definition therefore covers sums, integrals and mixtures of the two. In particular it expresses <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink>: for a random variable with <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss> $F$,
$\expect[f(X)] = \int f(x)\,dF(x)$, whether $X$ is discrete, continuous or a mixture.

## When the integrator is smooth

If $g$ has a continuous <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>, the integral reduces to an ordinary one. By the <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean value theorem</NoteLink>, on each step
there is a point $s_j$ with $g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j) = g'(s_j)\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)$. The Riemann–Stieltjes sum becomes

$$
\sum_j f(\tau_j)\,g'(s_j)\,(t_{j+1} - t_j),
$$

which is a Riemann sum for $\int_a^b f(t)\,g'(t)\,dt$, apart from the evaluation points $\tau_j$ and $s_j$ being
different points of the same small step. For continuous $f$ and $g'$ that difference vanishes as the steps shrink, so

$$
\int_a^b f\,dg = \int_a^b f(t)\,g'(t)\,dt.
$$

**Worked example.** $\int_0^2 t\,d(t^2) = \int_0^2 t \cdot 2t\,dt = \brack{\tfrac23 t^3}_0^2 = \tfrac{16}{3} \approx
5.333$.

## When the integrator jumps

Let $g(t) = \lfloor t \rfloor$, the floor of $t$, which is flat except for jumps of 1 at $t = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. On
any step that contains no jump, $g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j) = 0$ and the term vanishes. A step containing the jump at $k$
contributes $f(\tau_j) \times 1$, and $f(\tau_j) \to f(k)$ as the step shrinks, if $f$ is continuous at $k$.

**Worked example.** On $[0, 3]$ the jumps are at 1, 2 and 3, so
$\int_0^3 t^2\,d\lfloor t \rfloor = 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 = 14$.

**Worked example: an expectation.** Let $X$ be 0 with probability $0.3$ and otherwise uniform on $[0, 1]$. Its
distribution function is $F(x) = 0.3 + 0.7x$ on $[0, 1]$, with a jump of $0.3$ at 0 and slope $0.7$ after it. Then
$\expect[X] = \int x\,dF(x) = 0 \times 0.3 + \int_0^1 x \times 0.7\,dx = 0.35$. The jump contributes the discrete
part and the slope the continuous part, in one integral.

## When it exists, and why the evaluation point stops mattering

The integral exists if $f$ is continuous and $g$ has **bounded variation**: the total up-and-down movement

$$
V(g) = \sup_{\text{partitions}} \sum_j \abs{g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j)}
$$

is finite <Cite id="apostol1967" />. Every monotone function has bounded variation (then $V(g) = \abs{g(b) - g(a)}$),
and so does every function with a bounded derivative.

Take two choices of evaluation points on the same partition, $\tau_j$ and $\tau_j'$.
Their sums differ by

$$
\abs{\sum_j \paren{f(\tau_j) - f(\tau_j')}\paren{g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j)}} \le \max_j \abs{f(\tau_j) - f(\tau_j')}
\cdot \sum_j \abs{g(t_{j+1}) - g(t_j)} \le \omega_f(\delta) \cdot V(g),
$$

where $\omega_f(\delta)$ is the largest change of $f$ over a step of length $\delta$. As the steps shrink,
$\omega_f(\delta) \to 0$ because $f$ is continuous, and $V(g)$ stays finite by assumption. So every choice of
evaluation point gives the same limit. Both factors are needed: continuity of $f$ makes the first small, and bounded
variation of $g$ stops the second from growing.

## Integration by parts

Summing $f(t_{j+1})g(t_{j+1}) - f(t_j)g(t_j) = f(t_j)\,\Delta g_j + g(t_{j+1})\,\Delta f_j$ over the partition gives the
exact identity $\sum_j f(t_j)\,\Delta g_j + \sum_j g(t_{j+1})\,\Delta f_j = f(b)g(b) - f(a)g(a)$. In the limit, when both
integrals exist,

$$
\int_a^b f\,dg + \int_a^b g\,df = f(b)g(b) - f(a)g(a).
$$

This is the <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink> formula, with no assumption that
either function is differentiable. It also shows that $\int_a^b g\,df$ exists whenever $\int_a^b f\,dg$ does.

## Where it breaks: integrating a function against itself

The evaluation point matters when $f$ and $g$ move together. Take $f = g$ and compare the left-point and right-point
sums of $\int g\,dg$. Their difference is

$$
\sum_j g(t_{j+1})\,\Delta g_j - \sum_j g(t_j)\,\Delta g_j = \sum_j (\Delta g_j)^2,
$$

the sum of squared increments. Three integrators give three behaviours:

- **Smooth**, $g(t) = t^2$ on $[0, 1]$: each $(\Delta g_j)^2$ is of order $1/n^2$, so the sum is of order $1/n$ and
  vanishes. With $n = 10$ steps it is $0.133$. The integral exists, and $\int_0^1 g\,dg = \tfrac12 g(1)^2 = \tfrac12$.
- **A jump shared by $f$ and $g$**: with $g = 0$ before $t = 0.5$ and $1$ after, one step contains the jump and
  contributes $(\Delta g)^2 = 1$ however fine the partition. The left sum gives 0 and the right sum 1, so the integral
  does not exist. Riemann–Stieltjes integration needs $f$ and $g$ not to jump at the same point.
- **Brownian motion**: the sum of squared increments of a Brownian path converges to the elapsed time $T$, not to 0.
  The left and right sums of $\int_0^T W\,dW$ differ by about $T$ at every resolution.

A Brownian path also has unbounded variation: with $n$ steps on $[0, 1]$,
$\expect\sum_j\abs{\Delta W_j} = n\sqrt{2/(\pi n)} = \sqrt{2n/\pi}$, which is $8.0$ at $n = 100$ and $79.8$ at
$n = 10{,}000$ and grows without bound. So the existence theorem above does not apply. For an integrand
$H$ that moves with the path, such as $H = W$, the sums $\sum_j H(\tau_j)\,\Delta W_j$ depend on the evaluation
points $\tau_j$. <NoteLink to="stochastic-calculus">Stochastic calculus</NoteLink> resolves this by fixing the point:
the <NoteLink to="ito-integral">Itô integral</NoteLink> always takes the left end, and the Stratonovich integral the
midpoint <Cite id="oksendal2003" />.

## Beyond bounded variation

Young showed that $\int f\,dg$ still exists as a limit of Riemann–Stieltjes sums when $f$ and $g$ are rough, provided
their roughness exponents add to more than 1: if $f$ is Hölder continuous of order $\alpha$ and $g$ of order $\beta$,
the integral exists when $\alpha + \beta > 1$ <Cite id="young1936" />. Brownian paths are Hölder of every order below
$\tfrac12$, so $\int W\,dW$ has $\alpha + \beta < 1$ and falls just outside. A smooth deterministic integrand against a
Brownian path, such as $\int_0^1 t\,dW_t$, falls inside, and there the Riemann–Stieltjes, Itô and Stratonovich answers
agree.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming the evaluation point is harmless.** It is harmless only under the existence conditions. Against a rough or
  jumping integrator, left, right and midpoint sums can converge to different answers.
- **Forgetting shared jumps.** A step-function integrand and a step-function integrator that jump at the same point
  have no Riemann–Stieltjes integral. The Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral, defined through the measure that $g$ induces,
  handles such cases by fixing what counts at a jump.
- **Reading $dg$ as $g'(t)\,dt$ when $g$ is not differentiable.** For step functions and Brownian paths there is no
  derivative; the integral is defined through the increments of $g$ directly.
