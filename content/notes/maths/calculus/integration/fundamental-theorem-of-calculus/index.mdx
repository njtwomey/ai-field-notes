<Definition>
For a bounded function $f$ on $[a, b]$, split the interval into $n$ pieces of width $h = (b - a)/n$ and pick a point
$t_i$ in the $i$-th piece. The Riemann sum $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(t_i)\, h$ adds the areas of the rectangles. If $S_n$
tends to the same limit for every choice of points as $n \to \infty$, that limit is the definite integral
$\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$. Every continuous function on $[a, b]$ is integrable <Cite id="spivak2008" />.

The fundamental theorem has two parts. If $f$ is continuous, the accumulated area $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt$ has
derivative $F'(x) = f(x)$. If $G$ is any function with $G' = f$, then $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = G(b) - G(a)$.
</Definition>

## Notation

$f$ is the integrand and $[a, b]$ the interval of integration. An antiderivative of $f$ is any $G$ with $G' = f$. Two
antiderivatives differ by a constant, which is why an indefinite integral is written $\int f(x)\, dx = G(x) + C$.

## Riemann sums

The left, right and midpoint rules choose $t_i$ at the left end, the right end or the middle of each piece. For an
increasing $f$ the left sum underestimates the area and the right sum overestimates it, so the integral lies between
them. The two differ by $\paren{f(b) - f(a)}h$, which tends to zero, so both converge to the same limit.

For $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ with $n = 4$, the left sum is $(0 + 1 + 4 + 9)/64 = 0.219$ and the right sum is
$(1 + 4 + 9 + 16)/64 = 0.469$. With $n = 100$ they are $0.328$ and $0.338$. The midpoint sum is $0.33333$, already
within $10^{-5}$ of the exact value $1/3$. The midpoint rule's error shrinks like $h^2$ rather than $h$, because the
first-order errors on the two halves of each piece cancel.

## The first part: accumulation has derivative $f$

Let $f$ be continuous and $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt$. For a small step $\delta > 0$, the change in $F$ is the area of a
thin strip:

$$
F(x + \delta) - F(x) = \int_x^{x + \delta} f(t)\, dt.
$$

On $[x, x + \delta]$ the integrand lies between its minimum $m_\delta$ and its maximum $M_\delta$, so the strip's area
lies between $m_\delta \delta$ and $M_\delta \delta$. Dividing by $\delta$,

$$
m_\delta \le \frac{F(x + \delta) - F(x)}{\delta} \le M_\delta.
$$

As $\delta \to 0$, continuity forces both $m_\delta$ and $M_\delta$ to $f(x)$. The difference quotient is squeezed
between them, so $F'(x) = f(x)$. The same argument works for $\delta < 0$.

In words: the rate at which area accumulates equals the height of the curve at the moving edge.

## The second part: evaluating integrals

Let $G$ be any antiderivative of $f$. By the first part, $F$ is also an antiderivative, so $(F - G)' = 0$ on the
interval. A function with zero derivative is constant: by the mean value theorem,
$(F - G)(x) - (F - G)(a) = (F - G)'(\xi)(x - a) = 0$ for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$. Hence $F(x) = G(x) + C$.
Setting $x = a$ gives $0 = F(a) = G(a) + C$, so $C = -G(a)$, and setting $x = b$,

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\, dx = F(b) = G(b) - G(a).
$$

The limit of sums never has to be computed. For $f(x) = x^2$, $G(x) = x^3/3$ gives $\int_0^1 x^2\, dx = 1/3$.

## Worked example

The area under one arch of $\sin x$ is $\int_0^\pi \sin x\, dx$. An antiderivative is $G(x) = -\cos x$, so the area is
$-\cos\pi - (-\cos 0) = 1 + 1 = 2$.

## In machine learning

- **Probability.** A cdf is the accumulated area of a density, $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} p(t)\, dt$, so by the first
  part the density is the derivative of the cdf.
- **Expectations and normalising constants** are integrals. Most have no closed-form antiderivative and are
  approximated by sampling, quadrature or the
  <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink>.
- **Continuous-time models.** Neural ordinary differential equations and diffusion models integrate a learned
  derivative over time. Their solvers are refined Riemann sums.

## Pitfalls

- **Discontinuous integrands.** The first part needs continuity at $x$. At a jump of $f$, the accumulated area has a
  corner and no derivative.
- **Improper integrals.** Integrals over infinite ranges or with unbounded integrands are limits of proper integrals.
  They may diverge, as $\int_1^\infty dx/x$ does.
- **The constant of integration.** Antiderivatives are defined only up to a constant. It cancels in $G(b) - G(a)$ but
  matters when solving differential equations.
