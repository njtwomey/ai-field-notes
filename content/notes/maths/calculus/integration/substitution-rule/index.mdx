<Definition>
If $g$ has a continuous <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> on $[a, b]$ and $f$ is continuous on the range
of $g$, then

$$
\int_a^b f\paren{g(x)}\, g'(x)\, dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\, du
$$

<Cite id="spivak2008" />. In practice one writes $u = g(x)$ and $du = g'(x)\, dx$.
</Definition>

## Derivation

Let $F$ be an antiderivative of $f$. By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dx} F\paren{g(x)} = f\paren{g(x)}\, g'(x)$, so
$F \circ g$ is an antiderivative of the left-hand integrand. The
<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink> applied to both sides gives

$$
\int_a^b f\paren{g(x)}\, g'(x)\, dx = F\paren{g(b)} - F\paren{g(a)} = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\, du.
$$

The factor $g'(x)$ is the local stretch of the substitution: a small interval of length $dx$ maps to one of length
$\abs{g'(x)}\, dx$. When $g$ is decreasing, the limits swap order, which supplies the sign.

## Worked examples

- **$\int_0^1 2x\, e^{x^2}\, dx$.** With $u = x^2$, $du = 2x\, dx$ and the limits become 0 and 1:
  $\int_0^1 e^u\, du = e - 1$.
- **$\int \tan x\, dx$.** Write $\tan x = \sin x / \cos x$ and set $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\, dx$:
  $\int \tan x\, dx = -\int du/u = -\log\abs{\cos x} + C$.
- **Scaling.** With $u = x/\sigma$, $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}\, dx = \sigma
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2/2}\, du$. This is how the normalising constant of every Gaussian follows from the
  standard one; see the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>.

## Densities

A density transforms with the same factor. If $X$ has density $p_X$ and $Y = g(X)$ with $g$ increasing, then
$P(Y \le y) = P\paren{X \le g^{-1}(y)}$, and differentiating with the chain rule gives
$p_Y(y) = p_X\paren{g^{-1}(y)}\, \frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y)$. The several-variable version, with a
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian determinant</NoteLink> in place of the derivative, is in
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Forgetting to change the limits.** After substituting, either change the limits to $g(a)$ and $g(b)$ or return to
  $x$ before evaluating. Mixing the two gives wrong answers.
- **Non-monotone substitutions.** The formula holds as written for any continuously differentiable $g$, but inverting
  $u = g(x)$ to express $dx$ in terms of $du$ needs $g$ to be one-to-one on the interval. For densities, a map that is
  not one-to-one needs a sum over the branches.
