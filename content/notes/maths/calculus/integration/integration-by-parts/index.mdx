<Definition>
For functions $u$ and $v$ with continuous derivatives on $[a, b]$,

$$
\int_a^b u(x)\, v'(x)\, dx = \Big[u(x)\, v(x)\Big]_a^b - \int_a^b u'(x)\, v(x)\, dx,
$$

or in short form $\int u\, dv = uv - \int v\, du$ <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation

The product rule gives $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$. Integrate both sides over $[a, b]$. By the
<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink>, the integral of a derivative
is the change in the function, so

$$
u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) = \int_a^b u'v\, dx + \int_a^b uv'\, dx.
$$

Rearranging gives the formula. The rule trades one integral for another. It helps when differentiating $u$ simplifies
it and integrating $v'$ does not make it worse.

## Worked examples

- **$\int x e^x\, dx$.** Take $u = x$ and $dv = e^x dx$, so $du = dx$ and $v = e^x$. Then
  $\int x e^x\, dx = x e^x - \int e^x\, dx = (x - 1)e^x + C$. Over $[0, 1]$ this gives $0 - (-1) = 1$.
- **$\int \log x\, dx$.** There is no visible product, so take $u = \log x$ and $dv = dx$. Then $du = dx/x$ and $v = x$,
  and $\int \log x\, dx = x\log x - \int x \cdot \tfrac{1}{x}\, dx = x \log x - x + C$.
- **The gamma recursion.** For $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t}\, dt$, take $u = t^\alpha$ and
  $dv = e^{-t} dt$:

  $$
  \Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \Big[-t^\alpha e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty + \alpha \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t}\, dt
  = \alpha\, \Gamma(\alpha).
  $$

  The boundary term vanishes at both ends for $\alpha > 0$. With $\Gamma(1) = 1$ this gives $\Gamma(n + 1) = n!$; see
  the <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma function</NoteLink>.

## The DI method

Some integrals need integration by parts several times in a row. $\int x^3 e^{2x}\,dx$ needs it three times, once for
each power of $x$. The DI method, also called tabular integration, does the repeated steps in a table instead of
nested formulas.

**The table.** Make two columns. In the D column, write the factor to be differentiated and its successive
derivatives. In the I column, write the other factor and its successive antiderivatives. Give the rows alternating
signs, $+, -, +, -, \dots$, starting with $+$. The integral is the sum of the diagonal products: each D entry times the
I entry one row below it, with the sign of the D entry's row.

**Why it works.** Write $V_1, V_2, \dots$ for successive antiderivatives of $v$, so $V_k' = V_{k-1}$ with $V_0 = v$.
Integration by parts once gives $\int u v\,dx = u V_1 - \int u' V_1\,dx$. The remaining integral has the same form,
one derivative further down $u$ and one antiderivative further along $v$, so applying the rule again gives
$\int u' V_1\,dx = u' V_2 - \int u'' V_2\,dx$. After $n$ steps,

$$
\int u v\,dx = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (-1)^k\, u^{(k)} V_{k+1} \;+\; (-1)^n \int u^{(n)} V_n\,dx.
$$

This follows by induction: if it holds for $n$, apply integration by parts to the last integral,
$\int u^{(n)} V_n\,dx = u^{(n)} V_{n+1} - \int u^{(n+1)} V_{n+1}\,dx$, and the formula holds for $n + 1$. Each term of
the sum is exactly one diagonal of the table: row $k$ of the D column, $u^{(k)}$, times row $k + 1$ of the I column,
$V_{k+1}$, with sign $(-1)^k$. The last term is the product across row $n$, still under an integral sign.

The table works well because it separates the two jobs. Integration by parts is mechanical once $u$ and $v$ are
chosen, but written as nested formulas the signs and the growing chain of antiderivatives are easy to get wrong. The
table computes every derivative and antiderivative first, then reads off the answer; the alternating signs follow the
rows automatically.

**When to stop.** The formula holds at every $n$, so the table can stop at any row. Three stopping rules cover almost
every case:

1. **The D column reaches 0.** The leftover integral vanishes and the sum is the answer. This happens when $u$ is a
   polynomial of degree $n - 1$: it takes $n$ derivatives to reach 0.
2. **A row repeats the original integrand, up to a constant.** The leftover integral is a multiple of the original
   one. Move it to the left-hand side and solve.
3. **The product across a row is easy to integrate.** Integrate it directly and stop.

**Example 1: the D column reaches 0.** For $\int x^3 e^{2x}\,dx$, put $x^3$ in D, since differentiating a polynomial
eventually gives 0, and $e^{2x}$ in I, since integrating it only rescales it.

| sign | D | I |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $+$ | $x^3$ | $e^{2x}$ |
| $-$ | $3x^2$ | $\tfrac12 e^{2x}$ |
| $+$ | $6x$ | $\tfrac14 e^{2x}$ |
| $-$ | $6$ | $\tfrac18 e^{2x}$ |
| $+$ | $0$ | $\tfrac1{16} e^{2x}$ |

The diagonals give

$$
\int x^3 e^{2x}\,dx = \tfrac12 x^3 e^{2x} - \tfrac34 x^2 e^{2x} + \tfrac34 x e^{2x} - \tfrac38 e^{2x} + C
= e^{2x}\paren{\tfrac12 x^3 - \tfrac34 x^2 + \tfrac34 x - \tfrac38} + C.
$$

Differentiating the right-hand side returns $x^3 e^{2x}$. Done by hand as nested formulas, this is three rounds of
integration by parts with a sign to track in each.

**Example 2: a row repeats.** For $I = \int e^x \sin x\,dx$, neither factor reaches 0 when differentiated.

| sign | D | I |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $+$ | $\sin x$ | $e^x$ |
| $-$ | $\cos x$ | $e^x$ |
| $+$ | $-\sin x$ | $e^x$ |

Stop at the third row, whose product $-\sin x \cdot e^x$ is minus the original integrand. The diagonals and the
leftover integral across the last row give

$$
I = \sin x\, e^x - \cos x\, e^x + \int (-\sin x)\, e^x\,dx = e^x(\sin x - \cos x) - I,
$$

so $2I = e^x(\sin x - \cos x)$ and $I = \tfrac12 e^x(\sin x - \cos x) + C$.

**Example 3: a row becomes easy.** For $\int x^2 \ln x\,dx$, put $\ln x$ in D, because its derivative $1/x$ is
simpler, while its antiderivative is not.

| sign | D | I |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $+$ | $\ln x$ | $x^2$ |
| $-$ | $1/x$ | $\tfrac13 x^3$ |

The product across the second row is $\tfrac1x \cdot \tfrac13 x^3 = \tfrac13 x^2$, which integrates directly:

$$
\int x^2 \ln x\,dx = \tfrac13 x^3 \ln x - \int \tfrac13 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13 x^3 \ln x - \tfrac19 x^3 + C.
$$

**Choosing the columns.** Put in D the factor that gets simpler when differentiated (polynomials, logarithms, inverse
trigonometric functions), and in I the factor that integrates without getting worse (exponentials, sines and
cosines). A wrong choice shows at once: the D column grows more complicated instead of shrinking toward 0.

## Expectation as the area above the cdf

For a non-negative random variable $X$ with density $p$ and cdf $F$,

$$
\expect[X] = \int_0^\infty \paren{1 - F(x)}\, dx.
$$

Derivation: write $\expect[X] = \int_0^\infty x\, p(x)\, dx$ and integrate by parts with $u = x$ and
$v = -(1 - F(x))$, so that $v' = p(x)$:

$$
\expect[X] = \Big[-x\paren{1 - F(x)}\Big]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty \paren{1 - F(x)}\, dx.
$$

The boundary term is zero at 0, and it tends to zero at infinity whenever $\expect[X]$ is finite, because
$x\paren{1 - F(x)} \le \int_x^\infty t\, p(t)\, dt \to 0$. For an exponential variable with rate 2,
$1 - F(x) = e^{-2x}$ and the formula gives $\expect[X] = 1/2$.

## Stein's identity for Gaussians

If $X \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $g$ is differentiable with $\expect\abs{g'(X)} < \infty$, then

$$
\expect\brack{X\, g(X)} = \expect\brack{g'(X)}.
$$

Derivation: the standard normal density $\varphi(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ satisfies $\varphi'(x) = -x\varphi(x)$.
So $\expect[X g(X)] = \int g(x)\, x\varphi(x)\, dx = -\int g(x)\, \varphi'(x)\, dx$. Integrate by parts with $u = g$ and
$v = \varphi$:

$$
-\int g\, \varphi'\, dx = -\Big[g\,\varphi\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int g'(x)\, \varphi(x)\, dx =
  \expect\brack{g'(X)},
$$

since $\varphi$ decays faster than $g$ grows. For $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ the identity becomes
$\expect[(X - \mu)\, g(X)] = \sigma^2 \expect[g'(X)]$ <Cite id="stein1981" />.

Example: with $g(x) = \sin x$, $\expect[X\sin X] = \expect[\cos X] = e^{-1/2} \approx 0.607$. A simulation with two
million draws gives $0.606$.

Stein's identity characterises the Gaussian: a distribution satisfying it for every such $g$ is standard normal. It
underlies Stein's unbiased risk estimate and several gradient estimators for Gaussian variational families.

## Pitfalls

- **Boundary terms.** On infinite ranges the term $[uv]$ must be checked to vanish. It does not always: for a
  distribution without a finite mean, the tail-area formula gives $\infty$, as it should.
- **Choosing $u$.** A poor choice makes the new integral harder. A common heuristic is to take for $u$ the factor that
  simplifies when differentiated: logarithms and polynomials before exponentials and trigonometric functions.
