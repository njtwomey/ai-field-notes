<Definition>
A **first-order linear ordinary differential equation** for an unknown function $x(t)$ has the form

$$
\dot x(t) + a(t)\,x(t) = b(t),
$$

where $\dot x = dx/dt$, and $a$ and $b$ are given continuous functions. With an initial value $x(t_0) = x_0$ it has
exactly one solution,

$$
x(t) = e^{-A(t)}\paren{x_0 + \int_{t_0}^t e^{A(s)}\,b(s)\,ds}, \qquad A(t) = \int_{t_0}^t a(s)\,ds.
$$

The equation is **homogeneous** when $b = 0$. A **separable** equation $\dot x = g(t)\,h(x)$ need not be linear, and is
solved by integrating $dx/h(x) = g(t)\,dt$ <Cite id="teschl2012" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A cup of tea at 90 °C sits in a room at 20 °C. The tea loses heat faster when it is much hotter than the room and more
slowly as the gap closes. The rule "the rate of cooling is proportional to the temperature gap" is a first-order linear
ODE, and its solution is an exponential approach to room temperature.

Many processes follow the same pattern. A drug is cleared from the blood at a rate proportional to its concentration.
A population with plenty of food grows at a rate proportional to its size. An exponential moving average pulls its
state towards each new input by a fixed fraction. Each is the equation $\dot x = -k(x - x_\infty)$: the state moves
towards a target $x_\infty$ at a speed proportional to the distance.

## Exponential growth and decay

The simplest case is $\dot x = a\,x$ with constant $a$. The <NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">exponential
function</NoteLink> is defined as the function equal to its own derivative, so $x(t) = x_0e^{at}$ satisfies
$\dot x = a\,x_0e^{at} = a\,x$ and $x(0) = x_0$.

No other solution exists. If $y$ is any solution, then $\frac{d}{dt}\paren{e^{-at}y} = e^{-at}(\dot y - a\,y) = 0$, so
$e^{-at}y(t)$ is constant and equals $y(0) = x_0$. That one-line argument is the integrating factor, used below in
general.

The sign of $a$ decides the behaviour:

- $a > 0$: growth. The solution doubles every $\log 2/a$ time units.
- $a < 0$: decay. The solution halves every $\log 2/\abs{a}$ time units, the **half-life**.
- $a = 0$: $x$ is constant.

**Worked example (drug clearance).** A 100 mg dose is cleared at rate $k = 0.2$ per hour, so $\dot x = -0.2\,x$ with
$x(0) = 100$. After 5 hours $x(5) = 100\,e^{-1} = 36.8$ mg. The half-life is $\log 2/0.2 = 3.47$ hours.

## Newton's law of cooling

Let $T(t)$ be the temperature of the tea and $T_\infty$ the room temperature. Newton's law of cooling is

$$
\dot T = -k\,(T - T_\infty), \qquad k > 0.
$$

Substituting $y = T - T_\infty$ gives $\dot y = -k\,y$, because $T_\infty$ is constant. So $y(t) = y_0e^{-kt}$ and

$$
T(t) = T_\infty + (T_0 - T_\infty)\,e^{-kt}.
$$

**Worked example.** With $T_0 = 90$ °C, $T_\infty = 20$ °C and $k = 0.05$ per minute, the tea is at
$20 + 70\,e^{-0.5} = 62.5$ °C after 10 minutes. It reaches 60 °C when $70\,e^{-0.05t} = 40$, that is at
$t = \log(70/40)/0.05 = 11.2$ minutes. The gap to room temperature shrinks by the same factor, $e^{-0.05}$, every minute,
whatever the temperature.

## Separable equations

An equation is **separable** when its right side factors into a function of $t$ times a function of $x$:
$\dot x = g(t)\,h(x)$. Where $h(x) \ne 0$, divide by $h(x)$ and integrate both sides with respect to $t$. The
<NoteLink to="substitution-rule">substitution rule</NoteLink> turns the left side into an integral over $x$:

$$
\int \frac{\dot x(t)}{h(x(t))}\,dt = \int \frac{dx}{h(x)} = \int g(t)\,dt.
$$

Writing $dx/h(x) = g(t)\,dt$ and integrating each side is shorthand for this step.

**Worked example ($\dot x = t\,x$).** Here $g(t) = t$ and $h(x) = x$. Integrating $dx/x = t\,dt$ gives
$\log\abs{x} = t^2/2 + c$, so $x(t) = x_0e^{t^2/2}$. With $x_0 = 1$, $x(1) = e^{0.5} = 1.649$. The growth rate $t$
increases with time, so the solution grows faster than any fixed exponential.

**Worked example (logistic growth).** A population $x$, measured as a fraction of the capacity of its environment,
follows $\dot x = x(1 - x)$. Growth is nearly exponential while $x$ is small and stops as $x \to 1$. Partial fractions
give $\frac{1}{x(1 - x)} = \frac1x + \frac{1}{1 - x}$, so integrating $dx/(x(1 - x)) = dt$ gives

$$
\log\frac{x}{1 - x} = t + c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(t) = \frac{1}{1 + \paren{1/x_0 - 1}e^{-t}}.
$$

With $x_0 = 0.1$, $x(2) = 1/(1 + 9e^{-2}) = 0.451$. The logistic equation is nonlinear, but it is still separable. Its
solution is the logistic sigmoid, shifted in time.

Separation divides by $h(x)$, so it loses any constant solution with $h(x^\ast) = 0$. For the logistic equation these
are $x = 0$ and $x = 1$. They must be added back by hand.

## The integrating factor

The general linear equation $\dot x + a(t)\,x = b(t)$ is not separable when $b \ne 0$. The trick is to find a function
$\mu(t) > 0$ such that multiplying the equation by $\mu$ makes the left side the derivative of a single product. The
product rule gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\paren{\mu x} = \mu\,\dot x + \dot\mu\,x.
$$

This matches $\mu\,\dot x + \mu\,a\,x$ exactly when $\dot\mu = a\,\mu$. That is a homogeneous linear equation for
$\mu$, with solution $\mu(t) = e^{A(t)}$ where $A(t) = \int_{t_0}^t a(s)\,ds$. Multiplying the ODE by this
**integrating factor** gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\paren{e^{A(t)}x(t)} = e^{A(t)}\,b(t).
$$

The <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink> integrates both sides
from $t_0$ to $t$. Since $A(t_0) = 0$,

$$
e^{A(t)}x(t) - x_0 = \int_{t_0}^t e^{A(s)}\,b(s)\,ds,
$$

which is the formula in the definition. Every step is reversible, so the solution exists and is unique.

For constant $a$ and $t_0 = 0$ the formula reads

$$
x(t) = e^{-at}x_0 + \int_0^t e^{-a(t - s)}\,b(s)\,ds.
$$

The first term is the initial value, forgotten at rate $a$. The second is a weighted sum of past inputs $b(s)$, with
weight $e^{-a(t - s)}$ that decays with their age $t - s$. An exponential moving average has the same structure in
discrete time.

**Worked example ($\dot x + x = t$, $x(0) = 1$).** Here $a = 1$ and $\mu = e^t$. Then
$\frac{d}{dt}\paren{e^tx} = t\,e^t$. <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">Integration by parts</NoteLink> gives
$\int_0^t s\,e^s\,ds = (t - 1)e^t + 1$, so

$$
e^tx(t) - 1 = (t - 1)e^t + 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(t) = t - 1 + 2e^{-t}.
$$

Check: $\dot x = 1 - 2e^{-t}$ and $\dot x + x = t$. At $t = 2$, $x = 1 + 2e^{-2} = 1.271$. After the transient
$2e^{-t}$ dies away, the solution trails the input $t$ by exactly 1 time unit, the time constant $1/a$.

**Worked example (sinusoidal forcing).** Take $\dot x + x = \sin t$ with $x(0) = 0$. Try a particular solution
$x_p = \alpha\sin t + \beta\cos t$. Substituting and matching the coefficients of $\sin t$ and $\cos t$ gives
$\alpha - \beta = 1$ and $\alpha + \beta = 0$, so $x_p = \tfrac12(\sin t - \cos t)$. Adding the homogeneous solution $Ce^{-t}$ and imposing $x(0) = 0$ gives
$C = \tfrac12$:

$$
x(t) = \tfrac12(\sin t - \cos t) + \tfrac12e^{-t}.
$$

At $t = 3$, $x = 0.590$. The steady oscillation $\tfrac12(\sin t - \cos t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\sin(t - \pi/4)$ has
amplitude $0.707$ and lags the input by $\pi/4$. The equation acts as a low-pass filter.

<Derivation title="Solving for the coefficients of the particular solution">
With $x_p = \alpha\sin t + \beta\cos t$, $\dot x_p = \alpha\cos t - \beta\sin t$. Then

$$
\dot x_p + x_p = (\alpha - \beta)\sin t + (\alpha + \beta)\cos t.
$$

Matching this with $\sin t$ requires $\alpha - \beta = 1$ and $\alpha + \beta = 0$, so $\alpha = \tfrac12$ and
$\beta = -\tfrac12$. The integrating-factor formula, with two integrations by parts, gives the same answer:
$\int_0^t e^{-(t - s)}\sin s\,ds = \tfrac12(\sin t - \cos t + e^{-t})$.
</Derivation>

## In machine learning

- **Exponential moving averages.** The update $m_k = \beta\,m_{k - 1} + (1 - \beta)\,g_k$ used by momentum and Adam is
  one Euler step of $\dot m = (g - m)/\tau$ with step $1$ and $\tau = 1/(1 - \beta)$. The weight on an input $j$ steps
  old is $(1 - \beta)\beta^j$, the discrete counterpart of $e^{-a(t - s)}$.
- **Gradient flow on a quadratic.** For the loss $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}(w - w^\ast)^2$, gradient flow
  $\dot w = -\lambda(w - w^\ast)$ is Newton cooling towards $w^\ast$ at rate $\lambda$. Directions of high curvature
  converge first.
- **Diffusion models.** The mean of the variance-preserving forward process obeys $\dot m = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,m$. The
  integrating factor gives $m(t) = m(0)\exp\paren{-\tfrac12\int_0^t\beta(s)\,ds}$, the $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ factor of
  <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-probabilistic-models">DDPM</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- $a$ and $b$ are continuous on an interval containing $t_0$. The solution then exists on that whole interval.
- For separable equations, $h$ is continuous and nonzero along the solution. Where $h$ vanishes, uniqueness can fail;
  see the counter-example in <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">ordinary differential equations</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Forgetting the constant solutions.** Dividing by $h(x)$ discards solutions with $h(x^\ast) = 0$.
- **Sign of the exponent.** The integrating factor for $\dot x + a\,x = b$ is $e^{+\int a}$. Writing the equation as
  $\dot x = a\,x + b$ flips the sign.
- **Linear in $x$, not in $t$.** $\dot x + t^2x = \sin t$ is linear; $\dot x = x^2$ is not. Linearity refers to the
  unknown function and its derivative only.
