<Definition>
A **linear system** of <Gloss name="ode" plural /> with constant coefficients is

$$
\dot{\xvec}(t) = \Amat\,\xvec(t), \qquad \xvec(0) = \xvec_0,
$$

with state $\xvec \in \reals^d$ and a fixed $d \times d$ matrix $\Amat$. Its unique solution is
$\xvec(t) = e^{\Amat t}\xvec_0$, where the **matrix exponential** is the convergent series

$$
e^{\Amat t} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\Amat t)^k}{k!} = \Imat + \Amat t + \frac{\Amat^2t^2}{2} + \dots
$$

<Cite id="hirsch2013" />. The eigenvalues of $\Amat$ determine whether solutions grow, decay or rotate.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two tanks of water are joined by a pipe, and each also drains to the outside. The level in each tank changes at a rate
that depends on both levels. Each rate is a fixed <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">linear
combination</NoteLink> of the levels, so the state $\xvec = (x_1, x_2)$ obeys $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec$.

In one dimension, $\dot x = a\,x$ has solution $e^{at}x_0$. In several dimensions the coupling between coordinates mixes
them, and a single rate no longer describes the motion. The eigenvectors of $\Amat$ are the directions in which there is
no mixing. Along an eigenvector $\vvec$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, $\Amat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$, so the system behaves
like the scalar equation $\dot x = \lambda x$. When $\Amat$ has a full set of independent eigenvectors (a basis), every
solution is a combination of these independent motions, called **modes**.

## The matrix exponential

The scalar solution $e^{at}x_0$ suggests trying the same series with a matrix in place of $a$. Differentiating the
series term by term gives

$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^{\Amat t} = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k\,\Amat^kt^{k-1}}{k!} = \Amat\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\frac{\Amat^{k-1}t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \Amat\,e^{\Amat t}.
$$

So $\xvec(t) = e^{\Amat t}\xvec_0$ satisfies $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec$, and at $t = 0$ the series is $\Imat$, so
$\xvec(0) = \xvec_0$. The series converges for every matrix, because $\norm{\Amat^kt^k/k!} \le
(\norm{\Amat}\abs{t})^k/k!$, whose sum is $e^{\norm{\Amat}\abs{t}}$. The right side $\Amat\xvec$ is Lipschitz with
constant $\norm{\Amat}$, so this is the only solution.

Three properties follow from the series:

- $e^{\Amat(t + s)} = e^{\Amat t}e^{\Amat s}$: evolving for $s$ and then for $t$ is evolving for $t + s$.
- $e^{\Amat t}$ is invertible with inverse $e^{-\Amat t}$: the system can be run backwards.
- $e^{\Amat + \Bmat} = e^{\Amat}e^{\Bmat}$ only when $\Amat\Bmat = \Bmat\Amat$. The scalar rule fails in general
  because the terms of $(\Amat + \Bmat)^2$ include both $\Amat\Bmat$ and $\Bmat\Amat$.

## Solving by diagonalisation

Suppose $\Amat$ has $d$ independent eigenvectors $\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_d$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots,
\lambda_d$, so that $\Amat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1}$ with $\Vmat = [\vvec_1 \cdots \vvec_d]$ and
$\Lambdamat = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$. Powers telescope, $\Amat^k = \Vmat\Lambdamat^k\Vmat^{-1}$, so
substituting into the series gives

$$
e^{\Amat t} = \Vmat\,e^{\Lambdamat t}\,\Vmat^{-1}, \qquad e^{\Lambdamat t} = \diag\paren{e^{\lambda_1t}, \dots,
e^{\lambda_dt}}.
$$

Write the initial state in the eigenvector basis, $\xvec_0 = \sum_i c_i\vvec_i$ with $\cvec = \Vmat^{-1}\xvec_0$. Then

$$
\xvec(t) = \sum_{i=1}^d c_i\,e^{\lambda_it}\,\vvec_i.
$$

Each mode grows or decays at its own rate and never mixes with the others. For large $t$, the mode whose eigenvalue
has the largest real part dominates.

**Worked example (a saddle).** Let

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \xvec_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic polynomial is $(1 - \lambda)^2 - 4 = 0$, so $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$. Solving
$(\Amat - \lambda\Imat)\vvec = \zeros$ gives $\vvec_1 = (1, 2)$ and $\vvec_2 = (1, -2)$. The start is
$\xvec_0 = \tfrac12\vvec_1 + \tfrac12\vvec_2$, so

$$
\xvec(t) = \tfrac12e^{3t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac12e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

At $t = 0.5$, $\xvec = (2.544, 3.875)$. The $\vvec_2$ component shrinks and the $\vvec_1$ component grows, so every
start not exactly on the line through $\vvec_2$ is eventually carried out along $\vvec_1$.

## Complex eigenvalues: rotation

**Worked example (a rotation).** Let $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the spring
$\ddot x = -x$ written as a system. Its powers cycle: $\Amat^2 = -\Imat$, $\Amat^3 = -\Amat$, $\Amat^4 = \Imat$.
Splitting the series into even and odd powers gives

$$
e^{\Amat t} = \paren{1 - \frac{t^2}{2!} + \frac{t^4}{4!} - \dots}\Imat + \paren{t - \frac{t^3}{3!} + \dots}\Amat =
\begin{bmatrix} \cos t & \sin t \\ -\sin t & \cos t \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The solution rotates clockwise at one radian per unit time and never grows or shrinks. The eigenvalues are
$\pm i$: purely imaginary eigenvalues give rotation.

In general a real $2 \times 2$ matrix with eigenvalues $\alpha \pm i\beta$ has solutions of the form
$e^{\alpha t}$ times a rotation at angular speed $\beta$, in coordinates set by the eigenvectors. The real part
$\alpha$ decides whether the spiral winds in ($\alpha < 0$) or out ($\alpha > 0$).

**Worked example (a damped spring).** The spring with friction $\ddot x + 0.5\,\dot x + x = 0$ becomes
$\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $-0.25 \pm 0.968i$. The amplitude decays
like $e^{-0.25t}$ and the oscillation has period $2\pi/0.968 = 6.49$.

## Repeated eigenvalues

A matrix without a full set of eigenvectors cannot be diagonalised, and a new term appears. Take
$\Amat = \lambda\Imat + \Nmat$ with $\Nmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, so $\Nmat^2 = \zeros$.
Since $\lambda\Imat$ commutes with $\Nmat$, $e^{\Amat t} = e^{\lambda t}e^{\Nmat t}$, and the series for $e^{\Nmat t}$
stops after two terms:

$$
e^{\Amat t} = e^{\lambda t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

With $\lambda = -1$ and $t = 2$ the entries are $e^{-2} = 0.135$ and $2e^{-2} = 0.271$. The factor $t\,e^{\lambda t}$
first grows and then decays: a stable system can amplify a state for a while before it settles.

## Phase portraits in two dimensions

For $2 \times 2$ matrices the eigenvalues depend only on the trace $\tau = a_{11} + a_{22}$ and the
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> $\Delta = \det\Amat$, through $\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0$.
So $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \tau$, $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \Delta$, and
$\lambda = \tfrac12\paren{\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}$. The picture of all trajectories, the **phase portrait**,
has one of a few shapes <Cite id="strogatz2015" />:

| Condition                        | Eigenvalues                 | Portrait                                   |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| $\Delta < 0$                     | real, opposite signs        | saddle: in along one line, out along other |
| $\Delta > 0$, $\tau^2 > 4\Delta$ | real, same sign             | node: all paths enter or leave the origin      |
| $\Delta > 0$, $\tau^2 < 4\Delta$ | complex, real part $\tau/2$ | spiral: winds in or out                    |
| $\Delta > 0$, $\tau = 0$         | purely imaginary            | centre: closed orbits                      |

The origin is **asymptotically stable**, with every solution tending to $\zeros$, exactly when both eigenvalues have
negative real part, that is when $\tau < 0$ and $\Delta > 0$.

## Computing $e^{\Amat t}$

Summing the series in floating point is inaccurate when $\norm{\Amat t}$ is large. For the scalar $e^{-20}$ the terms
reach $20^{20}/20! \approx 4 \times 10^7$ before they cancel down to $2 \times 10^{-9}$, and
<NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding errors</NoteLink> in the large terms swamp the answer: the float64
series gives $6.1 \times 10^{-9}$, three times too large. Libraries use scaling and squaring: compute $e^{\Amat t/2^s}$
for a small matrix with a Padé approximant, then square it $s$ times <Cite id="moler2003" />. `scipy.linalg.expm` does
this. Diagonalisation is accurate only when $\Vmat$ is well conditioned.

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria">Gradient flow</NoteLink> on a quadratic.** For the loss
  $\tfrac12(\wvec - \wvec^\ast)^\top\Hmat(\wvec - \wvec^\ast)$ with positive definite
  <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> $\Hmat$, gradient flow $\dot{\wvec} = -\Hmat(\wvec - \wvec^\ast)$ is a
  linear system in $\wvec - \wvec^\ast$. The error decays like $e^{-h_it}$ along each eigenvector of $\Hmat$, with $h_i$
  its eigenvalue. The smallest eigenvalue sets the time to converge, which is why ill-conditioned losses train slowly.
- **State-space sequence models.** <NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models">S4</NoteLink> and its successors
  discretise $\dot{\hvec} = \Amat\hvec + \Bmat u$ with $\bar{\Amat} = e^{\Amat\Delta}$. Eigenvalues with negative real
  part make the recurrence forget at controlled rates.
- **Control and filtering.** The <NoteLink to="state-space-representation">state-space representation</NoteLink> adds an
  input, $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec$, and the continuous-time <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman
  filter</NoteLink> propagates means with $e^{\Amat t}$.
- **Linear <Gloss name="sde" plural />.** The mean of the <NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
  process</NoteLink> $d\xvec = \Amat\xvec\,dt + \Sigmamat^{1/2}d\wvec$ evolves as $e^{\Amat t}\muvec_0$.

## Assumptions

- $\Amat$ is constant. For a time-varying $\Amat(t)$, $\exp\paren{\int_0^t\Amat(s)\,ds}$ solves the system only when the
  matrices $\Amat(s)$ commute for all $s$.
- Diagonalisation needs a full set of eigenvectors. The Jordan form handles the rest, adding polynomial factors
  $t^k e^{\lambda t}$.

## Pitfalls

- **Entrywise exponential.** $e^{\Amat t}$ is not the matrix of $e^{a_{ij}t}$. The two agree only for diagonal
  $\Amat$.
- **Eigenvalues do not tell the whole short-term story.** Non-normal matrices, such as the repeated-eigenvalue example,
  can amplify states by large factors before the eventual decay.
- **Stability is about real parts.** A centre has $\tau = 0$ and is stable but not asymptotically stable; an arbitrarily
  small perturbation of $\Amat$ can turn it into a spiral either way.
