<Definition>
An **equilibrium** (fixed point) of the autonomous system $\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(\xvec)$ is a state $\xvec^\ast$ with
$\fvec(\xvec^\ast) = \zeros$. A solution started there stays there. The equilibrium is **stable** if solutions started
near it stay near it, and **asymptotically stable** if they also converge to it. Its **linearisation** is the linear
system $\dot{\yvec} = \Jmat\yvec$ for the deviation $\yvec = \xvec - \xvec^\ast$, where $\Jmat = \partial\fvec/\partial
\xvec$ at $\xvec^\ast$ <Cite id="strogatz2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A marble in a bowl rests at the bottom. Nudged, it rolls back. A marble balanced on an upturned bowl also rests, but
the smallest nudge sends it away. Both are equilibria: the net force is zero. They differ in what happens nearby.

The same question applies to any ODE. Where does the vector field vanish, and do nearby arrows point towards that
state or away from it? Close to an equilibrium, $\fvec$ is small and nearly linear, so the answer comes from the
eigenvalues of a matrix.

## One dimension: the phase line

For $\dot x = f(x)$ with scalar $x$, the sign of $f$ says which way the state moves. Where $f > 0$ it moves right;
where $f < 0$ it moves left. An equilibrium $x^\ast$ with $f'(x^\ast) < 0$ has $f > 0$ just to its left and $f < 0$ just
to its right, so nearby states move towards it. With $f'(x^\ast) > 0$ they move away.

**Worked example (logistic growth).** For $\dot x = x(1 - x)$ the equilibria are $x^\ast = 0$ and $x^\ast = 1$. The
derivative is $f'(x) = 1 - 2x$, so $f'(0) = 1$ and $f'(1) = -1$. Extinction is unstable: a small population grows
away from 0 at rate 1. Full capacity is stable: a small deviation from 1 decays like $e^{-t}$.

A one-dimensional autonomous system cannot oscillate. The state moves monotonically along the line, because to turn
round it would have to pass through a point where $f = 0$, and it cannot reach an equilibrium in finite time when $f$ is
Lipschitz.

## Linearisation

Near an equilibrium, the first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> of $\fvec$ is

$$
\fvec(\xvec^\ast + \yvec) = \fvec(\xvec^\ast) + \Jmat\yvec + O(\norm{\yvec}^2) = \Jmat\yvec + O(\norm{\yvec}^2).
$$

Dropping the quadratic term gives $\dot{\yvec} = \Jmat\yvec$, whose solutions are
<NoteLink to="linear-systems-and-the-matrix-exponential">$e^{\Jmat t}\yvec_0$</NoteLink>. The eigenvalues of $\Jmat$
then classify the equilibrium:

- every eigenvalue has negative real part: asymptotically stable (a stable node or spiral);
- some eigenvalue has positive real part: unstable (a saddle, unstable node or unstable spiral);
- some eigenvalue has zero real part and none positive: the linearisation does not decide.

An equilibrium with no eigenvalue on the imaginary axis is **hyperbolic**. The **Hartman–Grobman theorem** says that
near a hyperbolic equilibrium the nonlinear flow is a continuous deformation of the linear one: the same saddle, node or
spiral shape, bent <Cite id="teschl2012" />. At a non-hyperbolic equilibrium the neglected quadratic terms decide.

**Worked example (the damped pendulum).** With angle $\theta$, angular velocity $\omega$ and friction coefficient
$\gamma$,

$$
\dot\theta = \omega, \qquad \dot\omega = -\sin\theta - \gamma\,\omega.
$$

The equilibria have $\omega = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 0$: hanging down ($\theta = 0$) and balanced upright
($\theta = \pi$). The Jacobian is

$$
\Jmat(\theta, \omega) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos\theta & -\gamma \end{bmatrix}.
$$

With $\gamma = 0.5$:

- At $\theta = 0$, $\Jmat$ has trace $-0.5$ and determinant $1$. The eigenvalues are $-0.25 \pm 0.968i$: a stable
  spiral. The pendulum swings with decaying amplitude.
- At $\theta = \pi$, $\cos\pi = -1$, so the determinant is $-1$ and the eigenvalues are
  $\tfrac12\paren{-0.5 \pm \sqrt{0.25 + 4}} = 0.781$ and $-1.281$: a saddle. Only starts on one curve, the stable
  manifold, reach the upright position; all others fall away.

With $\gamma = 0$ the eigenvalues at $\theta = 0$ are $\pm i$. The linearisation predicts a centre, but it is not
hyperbolic, so the linearisation alone cannot say whether the nonlinear pendulum keeps swinging. Conservation of energy
settles it: without friction the energy $E = \tfrac12\omega^2 + 1 - \cos\theta$ is constant, so the orbits are closed.

**Worked example (predator and prey).** The Lotka–Volterra equations, scaled so all rates are 1, are

$$
\dot x = x(1 - y), \qquad \dot y = y(x - 1),
$$

for prey $x$ and predators $y$. The equilibria are $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$. At $(0, 0)$ the Jacobian is
$\diag(1, -1)$: a saddle, so a few prey with no predators grow. At $(1, 1)$ it is
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $\pm i$, again non-hyperbolic. The quantity
$V = x - \log x + y - \log y$ is constant along solutions, since $\dot V = (1 - 1/x)\dot x + (1 - 1/y)\dot y =
(x - 1)(1 - y) + (y - 1)(x - 1) = 0$. Solutions follow the closed level curves of $V$, and the populations cycle.

## Lyapunov functions

Energy settled both non-hyperbolic cases. The general tool is a **Lyapunov function**: a function $V(\xvec)$ with a
strict minimum at $\xvec^\ast$ that does not increase along solutions. By the chain rule,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}V(\xvec(t)) = \nabla V(\xvec)^\top\fvec(\xvec).
$$

If this is $\le 0$ near $\xvec^\ast$, a solution cannot climb out of any level set of $V$, so it stays near
$\xvec^\ast$: the equilibrium is stable. If it is $< 0$ except at $\xvec^\ast$, $V$ keeps falling and the solution
converges: asymptotic stability. No solution of the ODE is needed. The
<NoteLink to="lyapunov-stability">Lyapunov stability</NoteLink> note develops the theory.

**Worked example (friction).** For the damped pendulum, $E = \tfrac12\omega^2 + 1 - \cos\theta$ gives
$\dot E = \omega\dot\omega + \sin\theta\,\dot\theta = \omega(-\sin\theta - \gamma\omega) + \omega\sin\theta =
-\gamma\omega^2 \le 0$. Friction removes energy whenever the pendulum moves.

## Gradient flow

The ODE $\dot{\xvec} = -\nabla L(\xvec)$ follows the steepest descent of a function $L$. Its equilibria are the
critical points of $L$, and its Jacobian is $-\Hmat$, minus the Hessian of $L$. Since $\Hmat$ is symmetric, its
eigenvalues are real:

- at a strict local minimum, $\Hmat$ is positive definite, so every eigenvalue of $-\Hmat$ is negative: a stable node;
- at a saddle point of $L$, $-\Hmat$ has eigenvalues of both signs: a saddle of the flow;
- at a local maximum, all are positive: an unstable node.

Gradient flows never spiral or cycle, because $L$ itself is a Lyapunov function:
$\frac{d}{dt}L(\xvec(t)) = -\norm{\nabla L}^2 \le 0$.

**Worked example (a double well).** Let $L(x, y) = \tfrac14(x^2 - 1)^2 + \tfrac12y^2$. Then
$\dot x = x - x^3$ and $\dot y = -y$. The equilibria are $(\pm1, 0)$, with Jacobian $\diag(-2, -1)$, both stable
nodes, and $(0, 0)$, with Jacobian $\diag(1, -1)$, a saddle. Starts with $x > 0$ converge to $(1, 0)$ and starts with
$x < 0$ to $(-1, 0)$. The line $x = 0$ is the boundary between the two **basins of attraction**.

<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> is the Euler discretisation of gradient flow. A step size
$\eta$ keeps the discrete iteration stable at a minimum only when $\eta < 2/h_{\max}$, with $h_{\max}$ the largest
Hessian eigenvalue; the <NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers">numerical ODE solvers</NoteLink> note derives this
condition.

## Assumptions

- The system is autonomous and $\fvec$ is continuously differentiable near $\xvec^\ast$, so the Jacobian exists and the
  Taylor remainder is quadratic.
- Linearisation is local. It says nothing about how large the basin of attraction is.

## Pitfalls

- **Centres of the linearisation.** Purely imaginary eigenvalues prove nothing about the nonlinear system. The
  equilibrium may be a centre, a slow stable spiral or a slow unstable spiral.
- **Stable is not attracting.** A frictionless pendulum near the bottom is stable but never settles.
- **Local stability, global failure.** Both minima of the double well are asymptotically stable, but which one a
  solution reaches depends on its start.
