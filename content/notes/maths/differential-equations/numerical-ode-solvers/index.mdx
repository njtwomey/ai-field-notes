<Definition>
A **one-step solver** for the <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">initial value problem</NoteLink> $\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(t, \xvec)$, $\xvec(t_0) = \xvec_0$ produces
approximations $\xvec_n \approx \xvec(t_n)$ on a grid $t_n = t_0 + nh$ by a rule $\xvec_{n+1} = \xvec_n +
h\,\Phi(t_n, \xvec_n, h)$. The simplest is **Euler's method**, $\Phi = \fvec(t_n, \xvec_n)$. A solver has **order**
$p$ if its error at a fixed final time is $O(h^p)$. An **explicit** solver evaluates $\fvec$ only at known states; an
**implicit** solver's rule involves $\xvec_{n+1}$ on both sides and needs an equation solve each step
<Cite id="hairer1993" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A hiker crosses fog with a compass that shows the slope of the ground underfoot. The hiker walks a fixed distance in
the direction the compass shows, stops, reads it again, and repeats. Each leg is straight, so the route drifts from the
true curved path wherever the direction changes during a leg. Shorter legs drift less but take more readings. That is
Euler's method, and every other solver is a smarter way to choose the direction of each leg.

## Euler's method from the Taylor series

The <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> of the exact solution over one step is

$$
\xvec(t + h) = \xvec(t) + h\,\dot{\xvec}(t) + \frac{h^2}{2}\ddot{\xvec}(\xi) = \xvec(t) + h\,\fvec(t, \xvec(t)) +
O(h^2),
$$

for some $\xi$ between $t$ and $t + h$. Dropping the $O(h^2)$ term gives Euler's method:

$$
\xvec_{n+1} = \xvec_n + h\,\fvec(t_n, \xvec_n).
$$

The dropped term is the **local truncation error**, the error made in one step from an exact starting value. It is
$\tfrac{h^2}{2}\ddot{\xvec}$, so it is $O(h^2)$.

**Worked example.** For $\dot x = -x$, $x(0) = 1$, Euler's step is $x_{n+1} = (1 - h)\,x_n$. With $h = 0.25$ the
iterates are $1, 0.75, 0.5625, 0.4219, 0.3164$, so $x_4 = 0.3164$ approximates $x(1) = e^{-1} = 0.3679$ with error
$0.0515$. With $h = 0.1$, $x_{10} = 0.9^{10} = 0.3487$ and the error is $0.0192$. With $h = 0.05$ it is $0.0094$.
Halving $h$ roughly halves the error.

### From local to global error

Reaching a fixed time $T$ takes $N = T/h$ steps. Each step adds a local error of order $h^2$, and $N$ of them add up to
order $N h^2 = T h$. So Euler's method has **global error** $O(h)$ and is a first-order method.

The argument must also account for errors made early being carried forward and possibly amplified. Let
$e_n = \norm{\xvec_n - \xvec(t_n)}$ and let $L$ be the Lipschitz constant of $\fvec$. Subtracting the exact Taylor step
from the Euler step gives

$$
e_{n+1} \le (1 + hL)\,e_n + Ch^2,
$$

where $C$ bounds $\tfrac12\norm{\ddot{\xvec}}$. The first term is the old error, stretched by at most $1 + hL$ because
$\fvec$ differs by at most $L e_n$ between the two states. Unrolling from $e_0 = 0$ gives a geometric sum:

$$
e_N \le Ch^2\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(1 + hL)^k = Ch^2\,\frac{(1 + hL)^N - 1}{hL} \le \frac{Ch}{L}\paren{e^{LT} - 1},
$$

using $(1 + hL)^N \le e^{NhL} = e^{LT}$. The error is proportional to $h$, with a constant that can grow exponentially
in the time horizon <Cite id="suli2003" />.

## Runge–Kutta methods

Euler uses the slope at the start of the step. A better step uses the slope at the middle. The **midpoint method**
takes a half Euler step to estimate the midpoint, then uses the slope there for the full step:

$$
\kvec_1 = \fvec(t_n, \xvec_n), \qquad \kvec_2 = \fvec\paren{t_n + \tfrac h2, \xvec_n + \tfrac h2\kvec_1}, \qquad
\xvec_{n+1} = \xvec_n + h\,\kvec_2.
$$

Expanding $\kvec_2$ in a Taylor series shows that the step matches the exact solution through the $h^2$ term, so the
local error is $O(h^3)$ and the method has order 2.

<Derivation title="Why the midpoint method has order 2">
Write $\fvec$ for $\fvec(t_n, \xvec_n)$ and use subscripts for its <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink> there. Then
$\kvec_2 = \fvec + \tfrac h2\paren{\fvec_t + \fvec_{\xvec}\fvec} + O(h^2)$. The exact solution has
$\ddot{\xvec} = \fvec_t + \fvec_{\xvec}\fvec$ by the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>, so

$$
\xvec(t_n + h) = \xvec_n + h\fvec + \tfrac{h^2}{2}\paren{\fvec_t + \fvec_{\xvec}\fvec} + O(h^3) = \xvec_n + h\kvec_2 +
O(h^3).
$$

The two agree up to $h^2$. Euler's step $\xvec_n + h\fvec$ misses the $h^2$ term.
</Derivation>

For $\dot x = -x$ with $h = 0.5$, one midpoint step gives $1 - 0.5 + 0.125 = 0.625$ against the exact $e^{-0.5} =
0.607$. Euler gives $0.5$.

The **classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method** (RK4) samples four slopes per step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kvec_1 &= \fvec(t_n, \xvec_n), & \kvec_2 &= \fvec\paren{t_n + \tfrac h2, \xvec_n + \tfrac h2\kvec_1}, \\
\kvec_3 &= \fvec\paren{t_n + \tfrac h2, \xvec_n + \tfrac h2\kvec_2}, & \kvec_4 &= \fvec(t_n + h, \xvec_n + h\kvec_3),
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\xvec_{n+1} = \xvec_n + \tfrac h6\paren{\kvec_1 + 2\kvec_2 + 2\kvec_3 + \kvec_4}.
$$

For $\fvec$ independent of $\xvec$ this is Simpson's rule from <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical
integration</NoteLink>. Its global error is $O(h^4)$: halving $h$ divides the error by about 16.

**Worked example (the three methods on $\dot x = -x$ to $T = 1$).** On a linear equation each method multiplies the
state by a polynomial in $h$ per step: $1 - h$ for Euler, $1 - h + h^2/2$ for midpoint, and the Taylor polynomial of
$e^{-h}$ to degree 4 for RK4.

| $h$  | Euler error | midpoint error         | RK4 error              |
| ---- | ----------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| 0.25 | 0.0515      | $4.6 \times 10^{-3}$   | $1.5 \times 10^{-5}$   |
| 0.1  | 0.0192      | $6.6 \times 10^{-4}$   | $3.3 \times 10^{-7}$   |
| 0.05 | 0.0094      | $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$   | $2.0 \times 10^{-8}$   |

From $h = 0.1$ to $0.05$ the errors fall by factors of 2.0, 4.2 and 16.7, the orders 1, 2 and 4. RK4 uses four
evaluations of $\fvec$ per step against Euler's one, but at equal cost it is still far more accurate.

## Stability and stiffness

Accuracy is about small $h$. Stability is about what happens at a given $h$ over many steps. Apply a solver to the
**test equation** $\dot x = \lambda x$ with $\lambda < 0$, whose exact solution decays. Euler gives
$x_{n+1} = (1 + h\lambda)\,x_n$, which decays only if $\abs{1 + h\lambda} < 1$, that is if

$$
h < \frac{2}{\abs\lambda}.
$$

Beyond that step size the iterates oscillate with growing amplitude, although the true solution decays. RK4's limit on
the negative real axis is $h < 2.785/\abs\lambda$. For complex $\lambda$ the condition is that $h\lambda$ lies in the
method's **stability region**, the set of $z$ with $\abs{R(z)} \le 1$ where $R$ is the per-step polynomial.

**Worked example (Euler on an oscillator).** For $\ddot x = -x$ written as a system, $\lambda = \pm i$ and Euler
multiplies the amplitude by $\abs{1 \pm ih} = \sqrt{1 + h^2} > 1$ every step. With $h = 0.1$ over $t \in [0, 10]$ the
amplitude grows by $1.01^{50} = 1.64$, although the true oscillation has constant amplitude. The imaginary axis lies
outside Euler's stability region for every $h > 0$.

An equation is **stiff** when it has a fast decaying component that is not needed for accuracy but still limits the
step size through stability. The equation

$$
\dot x = -50\,(x - \cos t)
$$

has a solution that tracks $\cos t$ closely after a transient lasting about $1/50$. Accuracy would allow steps of 0.1.
Euler is stable only for $h < 2/50 = 0.04$. At $h = 0.05$ the multiplier is $1 - 2.5 = -1.5$ and the iterates explode:
starting from $x(0) = 0$, Euler at $t = 1$ gives $-3325$ where the exact value is $0.557$.

**Implicit Euler** evaluates the slope at the end of the step:

$$
\xvec_{n+1} = \xvec_n + h\,\fvec(t_{n+1}, \xvec_{n+1}).
$$

On the test equation $x_{n+1} = x_n/(1 - h\lambda)$, and $\abs{1/(1 - h\lambda)} < 1$ for every $h > 0$ when
$\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda < 0$. The method is **A-stable**: stable for any step size on any decaying linear mode. On the
stiff equation above it gives $0.5566$ at $t = 1$ with $h = 0.05$, and $0.5563$ even with $h = 0.1$. The price is an
equation solve per step, usually by <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> with the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> of $\fvec$ <Cite id="hairer1996" />. Stiff solvers
such as `Radau`, `BDF` and Crank–Nicolson for <Gloss name="pde" plural /> are implicit.

## Adaptive step size

A fixed $h$ wastes effort where the solution is smooth and loses accuracy where it changes quickly. An **adaptive**
solver estimates its local error each step and adjusts $h$. **Embedded Runge–Kutta** pairs compute two estimates of
different orders from the same slope evaluations, for example orders 5 and 4 in the **Dormand–Prince** method
(RK45) <Cite id="dormand1980" />. Their difference $\hat e$ estimates the local error of the lower-order one. If
$\norm{\hat e}$ exceeds a tolerance the step is rejected and retried with a smaller $h$; otherwise it is accepted. In
both cases the next step size is

$$
h_{\text{new}} = h \cdot s \cdot \paren{\frac{\text{tol}}{\norm{\hat e}}}^{1/5},
$$

with a safety factor $s \approx 0.9$. The exponent is $1/(q + 1)$ for a lower order $q = 4$, since the local error of
that estimate scales as $h^5$.

**Worked example.** SciPy's `solve_ivp` uses RK45 by default. On the oscillator over $[0, 10]$ with relative tolerance
$10^{-6}$ it takes 52 steps and 368 evaluations of $\fvec$, and the final error is $1.3 \times 10^{-6}$. On the stiff
equation over $[0, 200]$ it needs 9438 steps, because stability rather than accuracy caps its step size. The implicit
`Radau` solver needs 1249.

## In machine learning

- **Gradient descent is Euler's method** on <NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria">gradient flow</NoteLink> $\dot{\wvec} = -\nabla L(\wvec)$, with the learning rate $\eta$ as step
  size. Near a minimum with Hessian eigenvalues $h_i$, the stability condition $\eta < 2/\max_i h_i$ is the classic
  learning-rate limit, and a loss with widely spread curvatures is a stiff problem.
- **<Gloss name="diffusion-model">Diffusion model</Gloss> samplers.** <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-implicit-models">DDIM</NoteLink> is an Euler-type
  step on the <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability flow ODE</NoteLink>, and higher-order solvers such as Heun's method (a second-order Runge–Kutta method)
  reduce the number of network evaluations needed per sample.
- **Stochastic equations.** Adding a Gaussian increment with variance proportional to $h$ to each Euler step gives the
  <NoteLink to="euler-maruyama-method">Euler–Maruyama method</NoteLink> for <Gloss name="sde">stochastic differential equations</Gloss>. Its strong order is $\tfrac12$, not 1, because the noise
  over a step has size $\sqrt h$.
- **Neural ODEs.** A <NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">neural ODE</NoteLink> is evaluated with an
  adaptive solver, so the number of network evaluations depends on the input and the tolerance.
- **<Gloss name="resnet">Residual networks</Gloss>.** A residual block $\xvec + \fvec(\xvec)$ is one Euler step with $h = 1$.

## Assumptions

- The error bounds assume $\fvec$ is smooth enough: order $p$ needs $p$ continuous derivatives of the solution.
- <NoteLink to="adaptive-control">Adaptive step-size control</NoteLink> keeps the local error per step near the tolerance. It does not bound the global error, which can
  still grow with the time horizon.

## Pitfalls

- **Stability is not accuracy.** Implicit Euler is stable at any step but only first-order accurate. A large stable
  step can be badly wrong.
- **Round-off at tiny steps.** Below a certain $h$ the error stops falling and rises, because each step adds <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding
  error</NoteLink> and the number of steps grows as $1/h$.
- **Energy drift.** Standard Runge–Kutta methods do not conserve energy. Long simulations of Hamiltonian systems use
  symplectic methods such as leapfrog, as in <NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo">Hamiltonian Monte Carlo</NoteLink>.
