<Definition>
The **heat equation** in one dimension, with diffusion coefficient $D > 0$, is

$$
\partial_tu(x, t) = D\,\partial_x^2u(x, t).
$$

In $d$ dimensions the second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> becomes the Laplacian,
$\partial_tu = D\,\Delta u$ with $\Delta u = \sum_i \partial^2u/\partial x_i^2$. On the whole line with initial
condition $u(x, 0) = u_0(x)$, the solution is the convolution $u(\cdot, t) = G_t * u_0$ with the **heat kernel**

$$
G_t(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\paren{-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}},
$$

the density of $\Gauss(0, 2Dt)$ <Cite id="evans2010" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Put a drop of ink in a glass of still water. The ink spreads into a cloud that grows and fades. No current carries it:
each ink molecule is knocked about at random by water molecules, and the cloud is the histogram of where all the
molecules have wandered. The heat equation is the rule that this histogram obeys. Heat spreads through a metal rod the
same way, carried by random molecular motion, which is where the name comes from.

The equation says the density at a point rises when the point is lower than the average of its neighbours
($\partial_x^2u > 0$) and falls when it is higher. Peaks sink, troughs fill, and the profile smooths.

## From a random walk to the heat equation

Let many independent particles move on a grid of spacing $\Delta x$. Every $\Delta t$, each particle steps left or right
with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Let $p(x, t)$ be the fraction of particles at $x$ at time $t$. To be at $x$ at time
$t + \Delta t$, a particle was at $x - \Delta x$ and stepped right, or at $x + \Delta x$ and stepped left:

$$
p(x, t + \Delta t) = \tfrac12\,p(x - \Delta x, t) + \tfrac12\,p(x + \Delta x, t).
$$

Expand both sides in a <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink>. On the left,
$p(x, t + \Delta t) = p + \Delta t\,\partial_tp + O(\Delta t^2)$. On the right the first-derivative terms cancel:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\tfrac12\brack{p - \Delta x\,\partial_xp + \tfrac12\Delta x^2\,\partial_x^2p} + \tfrac12\brack{p + \Delta x\,\partial_xp +
\tfrac12\Delta x^2\,\partial_x^2p} + O(\Delta x^4) \\
&\qquad = p + \tfrac12\Delta x^2\,\partial_x^2p + O(\Delta x^4).
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtract $p$ from both sides and divide by $\Delta t$:

$$
\partial_tp = \frac{\Delta x^2}{2\Delta t}\,\partial_x^2p + O(\Delta t) + O\paren{\frac{\Delta x^4}{\Delta t}}.
$$

Now let $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$ shrink together with $D = \Delta x^2/(2\Delta t)$ held fixed. The error terms vanish
and the heat equation remains. Space steps must shrink like the square root of time
steps. That is the scaling of <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink>, whose displacement over time
$h$ is of size $\sqrt h$. Einstein used this argument in 1905 to connect the diffusion coefficient of pollen grains to
molecular motion <Cite id="einstein1905" />.

## The fundamental solution is a Gaussian

A single particle starting at 0 has, after $n = t/\Delta t$ steps, a position that is a sum of $n$ independent
$\pm\Delta x$ steps. Its mean is 0 and its variance is $n\,\Delta x^2 = t\,\Delta x^2/\Delta t = 2Dt$. By the
<NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> its distribution tends to $\Gauss(0, 2Dt)$. So
the density that starts as a spike at 0 should be

$$
G_t(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\paren{-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}}.
$$

This can be checked directly. Write $G$ for $G_t(x)$. Differentiating,

$$
\partial_tG = G\paren{-\frac{1}{2t} + \frac{x^2}{4Dt^2}}, \qquad \partial_xG = -\frac{x}{2Dt}\,G, \qquad
\partial_x^2G = G\paren{-\frac{1}{2Dt} + \frac{x^2}{4D^2t^2}}.
$$

Multiplying the last by $D$ gives exactly $\partial_tG$, so $G$ solves the heat equation. As $t \to 0$ it concentrates
all its unit mass at $x = 0$. The variance $2Dt$ grows linearly in time, so the width grows like $\sqrt t$. With
$D = \tfrac12$ the kernel is $\Gauss(0, t)$, the density of standard Brownian motion at time $t$.

**Worked example (why tea is stirred).** Sugar molecules in water have $D \approx 5 \times 10^{-10}$ m²/s. The typical
distance diffused, the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sqrt{2Dt}$, is $\sqrt{2 \times 5 \times 10^{-10} \times 3600} =
1.9$ mm after an hour and $9.3$ mm after a day. Diffusion alone carries sugar less than a centimetre in a day, far short
of the width of a cup. Doubling the distance takes four times as long.

<Derivation title="The Gaussian from the Fourier transform">
Take the <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> in $x$,
$\hat u(k, t) = \int u(x, t)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx$. Each $x$-derivative becomes a factor $ik$, so $\partial_x^2$ becomes
$-k^2$ and the <Gloss name="pde" /> becomes an <Gloss name="ode" /> for each frequency:

$$
\partial_t\hat u(k, t) = -Dk^2\,\hat u(k, t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat u(k, t) = e^{-Dk^2t}\,\hat u_0(k).
$$

A product of transforms is the transform of a convolution, so $u = G_t * u_0$ where $\hat G_t(k) = e^{-Dk^2t}$. That is
the <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">characteristic function</NoteLink> of $\Gauss(0, 2Dt)$, since a Gaussian
with variance $\sigma^2$ has $\expect\brack{e^{-ikX}} = e^{-\sigma^2k^2/2}$.
</Derivation>

## Solutions as Gaussian convolution

The heat equation is linear, so solutions add. Any initial density $u_0$ is a superposition of point sources, a source
of strength $u_0(y)\,dy$ at each $y$. Each spreads into $u_0(y)\,G_t(x - y)$, and adding them gives

$$
u(x, t) = \int u_0(y)\,G_t(x - y)\,dy = \paren{G_t * u_0}(x).
$$

The probabilistic meaning is direct. If $X_0$ has density $u_0$ and $B_t$ is an independent Brownian motion, then
$X_t = X_0 + \sqrt{2D}\,B_t$ adds independent $\Gauss(0, 2Dt)$ noise, and the density of a sum of independent
variables is the <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> of their densities. **Running the heat equation for
time $t$ is adding Gaussian noise of variance $2Dt$.**

**Worked example (two sources).** Start with half the mass at $-1$ and half at $+1$, with $D = \tfrac12$. At time $t$
the density is $\tfrac12\Gauss(x; -1, t) + \tfrac12\Gauss(x; 1, t)$. At $t = 1$ the density at 0 is
$\tfrac12\varphi(1) + \tfrac12\varphi(1) = 0.242$, where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density. For $t < 1$ the
density has two peaks. At $t = 1$ they merge, and for $t \ge 1$ it has a single peak at 0. An equal mixture of
$\Gauss(\pm\mu, \sigma^2)$ is unimodal exactly when $\mu \le \sigma$.

That example is a <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss>'s forward process in miniature. A
**variance-exploding** diffusion model noises data by $\xvec_t = \xvec_0 + \sigma(t)\,\zvec$, so the density of
$\xvec_t$ is the data distribution run through the heat equation to time $t$ with $2Dt = \sigma(t)^2$
<Cite id="song2021" />. At large noise levels the modes of the data merge into one Gaussian, which can be sampled
directly; the generative model learns to undo the smoothing.

## Smoothing in frequency

The Fourier derivation shows what the heat equation does to each frequency $k$: it multiplies it by $e^{-Dk^2t}$.
Frequency $2k$ decays four times faster than frequency $k$. Sharp edges, which are made of high frequencies, blur
first; broad shapes last. This is **Gaussian blur** in image processing, where the blur radius $\sigma$ corresponds to
$\sqrt{2Dt}$. Blurring twice with widths $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ equals blurring once with width
$\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}$, because running the equation for $t_1$ and then $t_2$ runs it for $t_1 + t_2$.

## Properties

- **Mass is conserved.** $\frac{d}{dt}\int u\,dx = D\int\partial_x^2u\,dx = D\brack{\partial_xu}_{-\infty}^{\infty} =
  0$ for densities that decay at infinity. Particles are neither created nor destroyed.
- **Positivity and the maximum principle.** A convolution with a positive kernel keeps densities positive, and the
  solution never exceeds the maximum of its initial values.
- **Infinite speed.** For any $t > 0$, $G_t(x) > 0$ for every $x$: a disturbance is felt everywhere at once, though
  far away it is exponentially small.
- **Irreversibility.** Running the equation backwards multiplies frequency $k$ by $e^{+Dk^2t}$, which amplifies small
  high-frequency errors without bound. The backward heat equation is ill posed. Reversing diffusion needs extra
  information about the density, which is what the score $\nabla\log p_t$ provides in the
  <NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">reverse-time stochastic differential equation</NoteLink>
  of <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score-based generative models</NoteLink>.

## The heat equation as a continuity equation

The heat equation can be written as the <NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">continuity equation</NoteLink>
of a velocity field. Since $\partial_xu = u\,\partial_x\log u$,

$$
\partial_tu = D\,\partial_x^2u = \partial_x\paren{D\,u\,\partial_x\log u} = -\partial_x\paren{u\,v}, \qquad v(x, t) =
-D\,\partial_x\log u(x, t).
$$

So the density evolves exactly as if each particle moved deterministically with velocity $v = -D\,\partial_x\log u$,
down the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the log-density. Random particles and these deterministic
particles have the same density at every time, although individual paths differ. With a drift added, the heat equation
becomes the <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink>, and the same rewriting turns it
into the <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability flow ODE</NoteLink> of a diffusion model.

## Finite differences

To solve the heat equation on a computer, put $u$ on a grid $x_j = j\,\Delta x$, $t_n = n\,\Delta t$, and replace
derivatives by differences. The forward difference in time and the centred second difference in space give the
**explicit scheme**

$$
u_j^{n+1} = u_j^n + r\paren{u_{j+1}^n - 2u_j^n + u_{j-1}^n}, \qquad r = \frac{D\,\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}.
$$

It is Euler's method applied to the system of ODEs obtained by discretising space. Its error is $O(\Delta t) +
O(\Delta x^2)$ <Cite id="leveque2007" />.

**Stability.** Rewrite the update as $u_j^{n+1} = r\,u_{j-1}^n + (1 - 2r)\,u_j^n + r\,u_{j+1}^n$. When $r \le \tfrac12$
all three weights are non-negative and sum to 1: each new value is an average of old values, so the scheme cannot create
new maxima. It is the <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> from the start of this note, with probability
$1 - 2r$ of staying put.

When $r > \tfrac12$ the centre weight is negative. To see what goes wrong, try a single Fourier mode
$u_j^n = \xi^n e^{ikj\Delta x}$. Substituting gives the amplification factor per step:

$$
\xi = 1 + r\paren{e^{ik\Delta x} - 2 + e^{-ik\Delta x}} = 1 - 2r\paren{1 - \cos k\Delta x} = 1 - 4r\sin^2\frac{k
\Delta x}{2}.
$$

The worst mode is the saw-tooth $k\Delta x = \pi$, alternating signs from point to point, with $\xi = 1 - 4r$. Stability
needs $\abs{\xi} \le 1$ for every $k$, which holds exactly when $r \le \tfrac12$:

$$
\Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x^2}{2D}.
$$

**Worked example.** With $D = 1$ and $\Delta x = 0.1$, the time step must satisfy $\Delta t \le 0.005$. Halving
$\Delta x$ to get a finer picture forces $\Delta t$ down by a factor of 4, so the cost of reaching a fixed time rises
by a factor of 8.

The **Crank–Nicolson** scheme averages the explicit and implicit updates, is stable for every $\Delta t$ and has
error $O(\Delta t^2) + O(\Delta x^2)$, at the price of a tridiagonal solve per step <Cite id="crank1947" />.

## Assumptions

- The medium is uniform: $D$ does not depend on $x$, $t$ or $u$. With variable $D(x)$ the equation becomes
  $\partial_tu = \partial_x\paren{D(x)\,\partial_xu}$.
- The solution formula $G_t * u_0$ is for the whole line. On a bounded interval,
  <NoteLink to="partial-differential-equations">boundary conditions</NoteLink> change the solution, and separation of
  variables or images replaces the single Gaussian.

## Pitfalls

- **Variance $2Dt$, not $Dt$.** Physics writes $\partial_tu = D\,\partial_x^2u$ with variance $2Dt$. Probability often
  writes $\partial_tp = \tfrac12\,\partial_x^2p$ for Brownian motion, which is $D = \tfrac12$ and variance $t$.
- **Explicit schemes on fine grids.** The stability limit $\Delta t \le \Delta x^2/(2D)$ makes explicit schemes very
  slow on fine grids. The heat equation is stiff once discretised.
- **Running it backwards.** Deblurring by solving the heat equation in reverse amplifies noise exponentially in
  frequency. It needs regularisation or a prior.
