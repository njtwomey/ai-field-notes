<Definition>
A **partial differential equation** (PDE) is an equation for an unknown function $u$ of two or more variables that
involves its partial derivatives, for example $u(x, t)$ with $\partial_tu = D\,\partial_x^2u$. Subscripts denote
partial derivatives: $u_t = \partial u/\partial t$, $u_{xx} = \partial^2u/\partial x^2$. A PDE problem also specifies
the domain of $u$, **initial conditions** at $t = 0$ and **boundary conditions** on the edge of the spatial domain. It
is **well posed** when a solution exists, is unique and depends continuously on these data <Cite id="evans2010" />.
</Definition>

## What changes with several variables

An <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">ordinary differential equation</NoteLink> tracks one state through
time: a temperature, a position. A PDE tracks a whole field: the temperature at every point of a rod, the height of a
string at every point along it, the density of a probability distribution at every point of space. The unknown is a
function of position as well as time, and the equation links how it changes in time to how it varies in space.

Two consequences follow.

- **The state is infinite-dimensional.** The initial condition is a whole function $u(x, 0)$, not a vector.
  Discretising space on $m$ points turns a PDE into a system of $m$ ODEs, which is how most PDEs are solved
  numerically.
- **Boundaries matter.** An ODE needs one initial value per state variable. A PDE on a bounded region also needs to be
  told what happens at the edges, for all time.

## The classic three

Three linear second-order equations model almost every basic phenomenon. Each has a one-line picture.

- **Heat equation, $u_t = D\,u_{xx}$.** Heat flows from hot to cold. Where the temperature profile is curved upwards
  ($u_{xx} > 0$), the point is colder than the average of its neighbours and warms up. Bumps flatten and the profile
  smooths. The same equation describes dye spreading in still water and the density of a particle doing a random walk;
  see the <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink>.
- **Wave equation, $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$.** The acceleration of a taut string is proportional to its curvature. A
  disturbance splits into two copies that travel in opposite directions at speed $c$ without changing shape.
- **Laplace equation, $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$.** There is no time. The equation describes an equilibrium, such as the
  steady temperature of a plate whose edges are held at fixed temperatures. Its solutions, **harmonic functions**, have
  the mean value property: the value at any point equals the average over any circle around it.

<ClassicThree />

The three behave differently because of how information moves. The heat equation is **parabolic**: a change anywhere is
felt immediately everywhere, but high frequencies are damped. The wave equation is **hyperbolic**: information travels
at a finite speed $c$ and nothing is damped. The Laplace equation is **elliptic**: the solution at each point depends on
the whole boundary at once. For a general second-order equation $a\,u_{xx} + 2b\,u_{xy} + c\,u_{yy} + \dots = 0$, the
type is set by the sign of $b^2 - ac$: negative for elliptic, zero for parabolic, positive for hyperbolic.

**Worked example (checking a heat solution).** Let $u(x, t) = e^{-D\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)$. Then
$u_t = -D\pi^2e^{-D\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)$ and $u_{xx} = -\pi^2e^{-D\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)$, so $u_t = D\,u_{xx}$. It also
satisfies $u(0, t) = u(1, t) = 0$. The shape stays a sine and its amplitude decays at rate $D\pi^2$.

**Worked example (d'Alembert's solution of the wave equation).** For any twice-differentiable $g$,
$u(x, t) = \tfrac12\brack{g(x - ct) + g(x + ct)}$ solves $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$: each term, differentiated twice in $t$,
gives $c^2$ times its second derivative in $x$. At $t = 0$, $u = g$ and $u_t = \tfrac12\brack{-cg' + cg'} = 0$. The
string starts at rest in the shape $g$, and two half-height copies of $g$ move apart at speed $c$.

**Worked example (a harmonic function).** $u(x, y) = \sinh(\pi y)\sin(\pi x)/\sinh(\pi)$ satisfies
$u_{xx} = -\pi^2u$ and $u_{yy} = \pi^2u$, which cancel. On the unit square it is 0 on three edges and equals
$\sin(\pi x)$ on the top edge $y = 1$. At the centre, $u(0.5, 0.5) = \sinh(\pi/2)/\sinh(\pi) = 0.199$.

## Initial and boundary conditions

The heat equation on a rod $0 \le x \le 1$ needs:

- an **initial condition** $u(x, 0) = g(x)$, the starting temperature profile;
- a **boundary condition** at each end for all $t > 0$. A **Dirichlet** condition fixes the value, $u(0, t) = 0$: the
  end is held in ice. A **Neumann** condition fixes the derivative, $u_x(0, t) = 0$: the end is insulated and no heat
  flows through it. A **periodic** condition joins the ends into a ring.

The wave equation is second order in time, so it needs both an initial shape $u(x, 0)$ and an initial velocity
$u_t(x, 0)$. The Laplace equation needs boundary values only. Giving the wrong kind of data makes a problem ill posed:
the heat equation run backwards in time, from a final profile to an earlier one, has solutions that change by
arbitrarily large amounts when the data change slightly.

## Linearity and superposition

A PDE is **linear** when $u$ and its derivatives appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together. For a
linear homogeneous equation, any sum of solutions is again a solution. This **superposition principle** is how the
classic equations are solved: find simple solutions, then add them up to match the initial condition.

**Separation of variables** finds the simple solutions. For the heat equation on $[0, 1]$ with zero ends, try
$u = X(x)\,T(t)$. Substituting gives $X\,T' = D\,X''\,T$, so $T'/(DT) = X''/X$. The left side depends only on $t$ and the
right only on $x$, so both equal a constant $-\lambda$. The zero boundary values force $X = \sin(n\pi x)$ with
$\lambda = n^2\pi^2$, and then $T = e^{-Dn^2\pi^2t}$. Expanding the initial condition as a
<NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier sine series</NoteLink>, $g(x) = \sum_n b_n\sin(n\pi x)$, gives

$$
u(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} b_n\,e^{-Dn^2\pi^2t}\sin(n\pi x).
$$

Mode $n$ decays at rate $Dn^2\pi^2$, so fine detail disappears first.

**Worked example.** With $D = 0.1$ and $g(x) = \sin(\pi x) + \tfrac12\sin(3\pi x)$, mode 1 decays by $e^{-0.987} =
0.373$ in one time unit and mode 3 by $e^{-8.88} = 0.00014$. At $t = 1$, $u(0.5, 1) = 0.373 - \tfrac12 \times 0.00014 =
0.373$: the third harmonic has vanished.

Nonlinear PDEs, such as the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation or fluid equations, do not obey superposition. Their
solutions can form shocks and are usually found numerically.

## Components

The notes in this section follow the question of how a density of particles moves:

- <NoteLink to="heat-equation">Heat equation</NoteLink>: derived from a random walk; its solution is a Gaussian whose
  variance grows linearly in time.
- <NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">Transport and continuity equations</NoteLink>: a density carried by
  a velocity field, solved by following particles along characteristics.

## In machine learning

- **Diffusion models.** The density of a noised data point obeys a
  <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink>, a heat equation with drift.
  The probability flow ODE rewrites it as a continuity equation.
- **Optimal control and reinforcement learning.** The <NoteLink to="hamilton-jacobi-bellman-equation">Hamilton–Jacobi–
  Bellman equation</NoteLink> is a nonlinear PDE for the value function.
- **Physics-informed neural networks** fit a network $u_{\thetavec}(x, t)$ by penalising the PDE residual at sampled
  points <Cite id="raissi2019" />.
- **Graph neural networks.** Message passing that averages neighbours is a discrete heat equation on a graph, which is
  why deep stacks of such layers over-smooth node features.
