<Definition>
To minimise $S(\xvec) = \tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^m r_i(\xvec)^2 = \tfrac12\norm{\rvec(\xvec)}^2$ with residuals
$\rvec : \reals^n \to \reals^m$ and Jacobian $\Jmat = \partial\rvec/\partial\xvec \in \reals^{m \times n}$,
**Gauss–Newton** takes the step $\pvec$ that solves $\Jmat^\top\Jmat\,\pvec = -\Jmat^\top\rvec$.
**Levenberg–Marquardt** solves $(\Jmat^\top\Jmat + \lambda\Imat)\pvec = -\Jmat^\top\rvec$ with a damping parameter
$\lambda \ge 0$ that is decreased after successful steps and increased after failed ones
<Cite id="levenberg1944" /> <Cite id="marquardt1963" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Fitting a model $y \approx h(t; \xvec)$ that is nonlinear in its parameters, such as an exponential growth curve, a
camera projection or a chemical rate law, leads to a sum of squared residuals $r_i = h(t_i; \xvec) - y_i$.
<NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> needs the Hessian of $S$, which involves second derivatives of
every residual. Gradient descent ignores curvature and is slow. The least-squares structure gives most of the Hessian
for free.

## Mechanism

### The Gauss–Newton approximation

Differentiate $S = \tfrac12\sum_i r_i^2$. The gradient and Hessian are

$$
\nabla S = \sum_i r_i\nabla r_i = \Jmat^\top\rvec, \qquad
\nabla^2 S = \sum_i \nabla r_i\nabla r_i^\top + \sum_i r_i\nabla^2 r_i = \Jmat^\top\Jmat + \sum_i r_i\nabla^2 r_i.
$$

The first term of the Hessian needs only the Jacobian, which the gradient already uses. The second term needs every
residual's Hessian. Gauss–Newton drops it. The approximation is good in two cases: when the residuals $r_i$ are small
at the solution (the model fits well), and when the residuals are nearly linear ($\nabla^2 r_i$ small).

With $\Jmat^\top\Jmat$ in place of $\nabla^2 S$, the Newton equation becomes $\Jmat^\top\Jmat\pvec = -\Jmat^\top\rvec$.
These are the normal equations of the linear least-squares problem

$$
\min_{\pvec}\ \tfrac12\norm{\rvec + \Jmat\pvec}^2.
$$

This is the same step derived differently: linearise the residuals, $\rvec(\xvec + \pvec) \approx \rvec + \Jmat\pvec$,
and solve the resulting <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>. It is solved stably with a QR
factorisation of $\Jmat$, without forming $\Jmat^\top\Jmat$.

The matrix $\Jmat^\top\Jmat$ is always positive semidefinite, so the Gauss–Newton direction is a descent direction
whenever $\Jmat$ has full column rank. Near a solution with zero residual, Gauss–Newton converges quadratically, like
Newton. With large residuals it converges only linearly, or not at all.

### Levenberg–Marquardt damping

Adding $\lambda\Imat$ to $\Jmat^\top\Jmat$ does three things.

- The matrix becomes positive definite for any $\lambda > 0$, so the step exists even when $\Jmat$ is rank deficient.
- As $\lambda \to 0$ the step becomes the Gauss–Newton step.
- As $\lambda \to \infty$ the step becomes $-\Jmat^\top\rvec/\lambda$, a short gradient descent step.

The damping adapts to how well the linearisation predicts. A common rule: after a step that reduces $S$, accept it and
divide $\lambda$ by 10; after a step that increases $S$, reject it, multiply $\lambda$ by 10 and solve again. The step
with damping $\lambda$ is the solution of the Gauss–Newton model restricted to a ball whose radius shrinks as $\lambda$
grows, so Levenberg–Marquardt is a <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods">trust-region method</NoteLink> that controls
the multiplier rather than the radius <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.

Marquardt replaced $\lambda\Imat$ with $\lambda\diag(\Jmat^\top\Jmat)$ <Cite id="marquardt1963" />. The damping then
scales with each parameter's curvature, and the step no longer depends on the units of the parameters.

## Worked example

Fit $y \approx a e^{bt}$ to the four points $t = 0, 1, 2, 3$, $y = 2.0, 2.7, 3.7, 4.9$, from $(a, b) = (1, 0)$. The
residuals are $r_i = a e^{bt_i} - y_i$, and the Jacobian has rows $(e^{bt_i},\ a t_i e^{bt_i})$. The starting loss is
$S = 13.2$.

| iteration | Gauss–Newton $(a, b)$ | $S$ | Levenberg–Marquardt $(a, b)$ | $S$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $(1.870, 0.970)$ | $478.8$ | $(1.080, 0.161)$ | $8.85$ |
| 2 | $(1.279, 0.788)$ | $41.4$ | $(1.349, 0.601)$ | $5.95$ |
| 3 | $(1.580, 0.494)$ | $2.38$ | $(1.717, 0.377)$ | $0.149$ |
| 4 | $(1.943, 0.318)$ | $0.0136$ | $(1.992, 0.298)$ | $0.0038$ |
| 5 | $(2.015, 0.297)$ | $0.00148$ | $(2.015, 0.298)$ | $0.00147$ |

Both reach $(a, b) = (2.0154, 0.2975)$ with $S = 0.001472$. The first Gauss–Newton step overshoots badly: the
linearisation at $b = 0$ cannot see the exponential growth, and the loss rises from 13.2 to 479. Levenberg–Marquardt,
starting at $\lambda = 0.01$, rejects steps until $\lambda = 100$, takes a short cautious step, and then decreases
$\lambda$ tenfold per iteration. Its loss decreases at every step. Near the solution both methods take nearly the same
steps, and both converge rapidly because the residuals there are small.

## Cost

Each iteration evaluates the $m \times n$ Jacobian and solves an $n \times n$ system (or an $m \times n$ least-squares
problem), at $O(mn^2)$. No second derivatives are needed. Levenberg–Marquardt may solve several systems per iteration
when steps are rejected. In <NoteLink to="structure-from-motion">bundle adjustment</NoteLink>, $\Jmat$ is sparse and
the normal equations are solved with a Schur complement that exploits the structure.

## Trade-offs

- **Large residuals.** When the model fits poorly, the dropped term matters and Gauss–Newton slows down. Quasi-Newton
  methods that approximate only the dropped term can help.
- **Other losses.** For a loss that is not a sum of squares, the **generalised Gauss–Newton** matrix
  $\Jmat^\top\nabla^2\ell\,\Jmat$ keeps the curvature of the loss $\ell$ and drops that of the model. For a GLM with
  canonical link it equals the Fisher information, and the method is <NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">iteratively
  reweighted least squares</NoteLink>.
- **Robustness.** Squared residuals give outliers large influence. Robust losses such as Huber are handled by
  reweighting the residuals at each iteration.
