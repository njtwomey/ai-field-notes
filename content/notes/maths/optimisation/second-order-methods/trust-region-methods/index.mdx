<Definition>
At iterate $\xvec_k$ with <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $\gvec_k$ and a symmetric matrix $\Bmat_k$ (the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> or an approximation), a
**trust-region method** solves the subproblem

$$
\min_{\pvec}\ m_k(\pvec) = f(\xvec_k) + \gvec_k^\top\pvec + \tfrac12\pvec^\top\Bmat_k\pvec \quad \text{subject to} \quad \norm{\pvec} \le \Delta_k,
$$

then computes $\rho_k = \dfrac{f(\xvec_k) - f(\xvec_k + \pvec_k)}{m_k(\zeros) - m_k(\pvec_k)}$. It accepts the step if
$\rho_k$ exceeds a threshold, and adjusts the radius $\Delta_k$ according to $\rho_k$ <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The quadratic model behind <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> is accurate only near the point
where it was built. Far from that point, its minimiser can lie far outside the region where the model is accurate, and if $\Bmat_k$ is indefinite the model
has no minimiser at all. A <NoteLink to="line-search">line search</NoteLink> keeps the Newton direction and shortens the
step, but the Newton direction itself may be poor or uphill. A trust region instead restricts the model to the region
where it is believed, and finds the best step inside it. The direction and the length are chosen together.

## Mechanism

### Radius update

The ratio $\rho_k$ compares actual reduction with predicted reduction. A standard rule <Cite id="nocedal2006" />:

- If $\rho_k < \tfrac14$, the model was poor: set $\Delta_{k+1} = \tfrac14\Delta_k$.
- If $\rho_k > \tfrac34$ and the step reached the boundary, the model was good and the radius was binding: set
  $\Delta_{k+1} = 2\Delta_k$, up to a maximum.
- Otherwise keep $\Delta_{k+1} = \Delta_k$.
- Accept the step, $\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k + \pvec_k$, only if $\rho_k > \eta$ for a small $\eta$ such as $0.1$.
  Otherwise stay at $\xvec_k$ and solve again with the smaller radius.

Near a minimum with positive definite Hessian, the Newton step is inside the region and $\rho_k \approx 1$, so the method
becomes pure Newton and inherits <NoteLink to="root-finding">quadratic convergence</NoteLink>.

### The Cauchy point

The subproblem need not be solved exactly. Global convergence only needs a step that does at least as well as the
**Cauchy point**, the model minimiser along the <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">steepest descent</NoteLink> direction inside the region. Write
$\pvec = -\tau\Delta\,\gvec/\norm{\gvec}$ with $\tau \in [0, 1]$. The model along this ray is

$$
m(\tau) = f - \tau\Delta\norm{\gvec} + \tfrac12\tau^2\Delta^2\frac{\gvec^\top\Bmat\gvec}{\norm{\gvec}^2}.
$$

If $\gvec^\top\Bmat\gvec \le 0$, the model decreases all the way to the boundary and $\tau = 1$. Otherwise the model is
a convex parabola in $\tau$ with minimum at $\tau = \norm{\gvec}^3/(\Delta\,\gvec^\top\Bmat\gvec)$, capped at 1. The
decrease at the Cauchy point is at least $\tfrac12\norm{\gvec}\min(\Delta, \norm{\gvec}/\norm{\Bmat})$. Any step with a
fixed fraction of this decrease makes the method converge to a stationary point from any start
<Cite id="conn2000" />.

### The exact solution

A vector $\pvec^*$ solves the subproblem if and only if there is $\lambda \ge 0$ with

$$
(\Bmat + \lambda\Imat)\pvec^* = -\gvec, \qquad \lambda(\Delta - \norm{\pvec^*}) = 0, \qquad \Bmat + \lambda\Imat \succeq 0.
$$

These are the <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions</NoteLink> of the subproblem, plus a second-order condition
that makes the result a global minimum even when $\Bmat$ is indefinite. If $\Bmat \succ 0$ and the Newton step
$-\Bmat^{-1}\gvec$ is inside the region, $\lambda = 0$. Otherwise $\lambda > 0$ is found by a one-dimensional root search on $\norm{(\Bmat + \lambda\Imat)^{-1}\gvec}
= \Delta$. The shift $\lambda\Imat$ is the same damping that <NoteLink to="gauss-newton-and-levenberg-marquardt">Levenberg–Marquardt</NoteLink>
adds, and $\lambda$ plays the role of a <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> for the radius.

### Approximate solutions

- **Dogleg.** When $\Bmat \succ 0$, follow the path from $\zeros$ to the unconstrained steepest-descent minimiser
  $\pvec^U = -(\gvec^\top\gvec/\gvec^\top\Bmat\gvec)\gvec$, then on to the Newton step $\pvec^N = -\Bmat^{-1}\gvec$, and
  stop where the path leaves the region.
- **Steihaug <Gloss name="cg">conjugate gradient</Gloss>.** Run conjugate gradients on $\Bmat\pvec = -\gvec$ from $\zeros$, and stop at the
  boundary or on meeting a direction of negative curvature. It needs only products $\Bmat\vvec$, so it scales to large
  problems.

## Worked example

Take $\gvec = (2, 1)$ and $\Bmat = \diag(4, 1)$. The Newton step is $\pvec^N = (-0.5, -1)$, with length $1.118$ and
model decrease $m(\zeros) - m(\pvec^N) = 1$.

- With $\Delta = 2$, the Newton step is inside the region and is the solution.
- With $\Delta = 1$, the Newton step is outside. The Cauchy point has $\tau = 5^{3/2}/(1 \cdot 17) = 0.658$ and is
  $(-0.588, -0.294)$, with model decrease $0.735$. The dogleg path crosses the boundary at $(-0.518, -0.855)$, with
  decrease $0.989$. The exact solution has $\lambda = 0.142$ and is $(-0.483, -0.876)$, with decrease $0.992$. The
  dogleg step captures almost all of the achievable decrease.
- With $\Delta = 0.5$, the steepest-descent minimiser $\pvec^U$ has length $0.658 > 0.5$, so the Cauchy point is on the
  boundary: $\tau = 1$ and $\pvec = (-0.447, -0.224)$, with decrease $0.693$.

The exact and dogleg steps bend from the gradient direction towards the Newton direction as the radius grows.

## Cost

The subproblem solution costs about the same as a Newton step: one or a few factorisations of $\Bmat + \lambda\Imat$
for the exact method, one factorisation for the dogleg, and a number of <NoteLink to="jacobian-vector-products-and-higher-order-derivatives">Hessian-vector products</NoteLink> for Steihaug CG. A
rejected step costs one function evaluation and a new subproblem.

## Trade-offs

- **Indefinite Hessians.** Trust regions use negative curvature instead of discarding it. On nonconvex problems they
  escape saddle points that a line search along a modified Newton direction can approach.
- **Scale.** The region is a ball in the <NoteLink to="vector-norms">Euclidean norm</NoteLink>, so badly scaled variables shrink the useful radius. Scaled
  versions use an ellipsoidal region $\norm{\Dmat\pvec} \le \Delta$.
- **In reinforcement learning,** <NoteLink to="trust-region-and-proximal-policy-optimisation">TRPO</NoteLink> applies the same idea with a KL
  divergence between policies in place of the Euclidean norm.
