<Definition>
To minimise $F(\xvec) = \expect_\xi[f(\xvec, \xi)]$, **<Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>** (SGD)
iterates $\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \eta_k\gvec_k$, where $\gvec_k = \nabla f(\xvec_k, \xi_k)$ is computed from a fresh
sample $\xi_k$ and is **unbiased**: $\expect[\gvec_k \mid \xvec_k] = \nabla F(\xvec_k)$. Its convergence depends on the
step sizes $\eta_k$ and on the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> noise, measured by the variance $\sigma^2 \ge \expect\norm{\gvec_k -
\nabla F(\xvec_k)}^2$ or the second moment $G^2 \ge \expect\norm{\gvec_k}^2$ <Cite id="robbins1951" />
<Cite id="bottou2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In machine learning $F$ is an average loss over $n$ training examples, and $\xi$ picks one example or a minibatch. A
full gradient costs $n$ example-gradients. A stochastic gradient costs one and points in the right direction on average.
Far from the optimum, the true gradient is large and the noise is a small perturbation, so SGD makes almost the same
progress per step as <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> at a fraction of the cost. Near the
optimum, the true gradient shrinks to zero but the noise does not. With a constant step the iterate does not converge:
its expected squared distance to the optimum settles at a level proportional to the step. Reaching the optimum itself
needs the steps to shrink.

## Convex functions: the O(1/√K) rate

Assume $F$ is convex, $\expect\norm{\gvec_k}^2 \le G^2$ and $\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*} \le R$. Expand the squared
distance after one step and take the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> conditional on $\xvec_k$:

$$
\expect\LB\norm{\xvec_{k+1} - \xvec^*}^2 \mid \xvec_k\RB = \norm{\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2 - 2\eta_k\nabla F(\xvec_k)^\top(\xvec_k - \xvec^*) + \eta_k^2\expect\LB\norm{\gvec_k}^2 \mid \xvec_k\RB.
$$

Unbiasedness replaced $\gvec_k$ by $\nabla F(\xvec_k)$ in the middle term. Convexity gives
$\nabla F(\xvec_k)^\top(\xvec_k - \xvec^*) \ge F(\xvec_k) - F^*$. Write $D_k = \expect\norm{\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2$.
Taking full expectations,

$$
2\eta_k\,\expect\LB F(\xvec_k) - F^*\RB \le D_k - D_{k+1} + \eta_k^2 G^2.
$$

Sum over $k = 0, \dots, K - 1$ with a constant step $\eta$. The distances telescope and $D_K \ge 0$, so
$\frac1K\sum_k \expect[F(\xvec_k) - F^*] \le R^2/(2\eta K) + \eta G^2/2$. By convexity, the averaged iterate
$\bar\xvec_K = \frac1K\sum_{k<K}\xvec_k$ does at least as well as the average of the values. Choosing
$\eta = R/(G\sqrt K)$ balances the two terms:

$$
\expect\LB F(\bar\xvec_K)\RB - F^* \le \frac{RG}{\sqrt K}.
$$

This is the same argument as for the deterministic <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradient method</NoteLink>, with an
expectation. No stochastic first-order method can do better than $O(1/\sqrt K)$ on this class, so the rate is optimal
<Cite id="nemirovski2009" />. Smoothness does not help here: the noise, not the curvature, limits the rate.

## Strongly convex functions: the noise floor

Now let $F$ be $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth, and let the noise have variance at most $\sigma^2$. Split the
update into a deterministic gradient step and noise:

$$
\xvec_{k+1} - \xvec^* = \underbrace{\LP\xvec_k - \eta\nabla F(\xvec_k) - \xvec^*\RP}_{\text{gradient step}} - \eta\underbrace{\LP\gvec_k - \nabla F(\xvec_k)\RP}_{\text{noise}}.
$$

The noise has mean zero given $\xvec_k$, so the cross term vanishes in expectation and the squared norms add. For
$\eta \le 1/L$ the gradient step is a contraction, $\norm{\xvec - \eta\nabla F(\xvec) - \xvec^*} \le (1 - \eta\mu)
\norm{\xvec - \xvec^*}$. On a quadratic this is visible directly: the step multiplies each eigen-direction of the
Hessian by $1 - \eta\lambda_i \in [0, 1 - \eta\mu]$. Hence

$$
D_{k+1} \le (1 - \eta\mu)^2 D_k + \eta^2\sigma^2.
$$

Unrolling the recursion gives a geometric decay towards a fixed point:

$$
D_k \le (1 - \eta\mu)^{2k}D_0 + \frac{\eta^2\sigma^2}{1 - (1 - \eta\mu)^2} = (1 - \eta\mu)^{2k}D_0 + \frac{\eta\sigma^2}{\mu(2 - \eta\mu)}.
$$

The first term is the deterministic linear rate of <NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink>. The second is the **noise floor**, approximately $\eta\sigma^2/(2\mu)$. On a quadratic with
isotropic noise the bound is exact, coordinate by coordinate. A constant step converges linearly to a neighbourhood of
radius $O(\sqrt{\eta})$ and then stops improving. Halving the step halves the floor but also halves the linear rate.

## Decreasing steps

### Robbins–Monro conditions

Unroll the recursion with varying steps, taking $(1 - \eta_k\mu)^2 \le 1 - \eta_k\mu$:

$$
D_K \le \prod_{k<K}(1 - \eta_k\mu)\,D_0 + \sigma^2\sum_{k<K}\eta_k^2\prod_{j=k+1}^{K-1}(1 - \eta_j\mu).
$$

The first term forgets the starting point only if $\prod_k(1 - \eta_k\mu) \to 0$, which holds if and only if
$\sum_k \eta_k = \infty$. The second term, the accumulated noise, vanishes only if the steps shrink: it is controlled
when $\sum_k \eta_k^2 < \infty$. Together these are the **Robbins–Monro conditions**

$$
\sum_{k=0}^\infty \eta_k = \infty, \qquad \sum_{k=0}^\infty \eta_k^2 < \infty,
$$

under which stochastic approximation converges to the root of $\nabla F = \zeros$ <Cite id="robbins1951" />. The
schedule $\eta_k = c/k$ satisfies both. The schedule $c/\sqrt k$ fails the second, and a constant step fails it too.

### The O(1/k) rate

For strongly convex $F$, the schedule $\eta_k = 2/(\mu(k + k_0))$ with $k_0 \ge 2L/\mu$ (so that $\eta_k \le 1/L$)
gives

$$
D_k \le \frac{\nu}{k + k_0}, \qquad \nu = \max\LP k_0 D_0,\ \frac{4\sigma^2}{\mu^2}\RP.
$$

The proof is by induction. Write $m = k + k_0$ and assume $D_k \le \nu/m$. Then
$D_{k+1} \le (1 - 2/m)\nu/m + 4\sigma^2/(\mu^2 m^2) \le \nu(m - 1)/m^2 \le \nu/(m + 1)$, using $4\sigma^2/\mu^2 \le \nu$
and $(m - 1)(m + 1) \le m^2$ <Cite id="bottou2018" />. The rate $O(\sigma^2/(\mu^2 k))$ is optimal for stochastic
first-order methods on strongly convex problems <Cite id="nemirovski2009" />.

A step $c/k$ with $c$ below $1/(2\mu)$ can slow convergence to $O(k^{-2c\mu})$, far
slower than $1/k$. Underestimating $\mu$ is safe; overestimating it is not.

### Iterate averaging

**Polyak–Ruppert averaging** runs SGD with steps that decay more slowly than $1/k$, such as $\eta_k \propto k^{-\alpha}$
with $\tfrac12 < \alpha < 1$, and returns the running average $\bar\xvec_K = \frac1K\sum_{k<K}\xvec_k$. Larger steps
make fast progress and forget the start quickly; averaging cancels the fluctuations. The average attains the optimal
asymptotic covariance, the one achieved by the best possible estimator from the same samples, without knowing $\mu$ or
the curvature <Cite id="polyak1992" />. On quadratics even a constant step with averaging converges, as the figure
shows.

## Nonconvex functions

For $L$-smooth nonconvex $F$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$, SGD with a constant step $\eta \le 1/L$ satisfies

$$
\frac1K\sum_{k<K}\expect\norm{\nabla F(\xvec_k)}^2 \le \frac{2(F(\xvec_0) - F^*)}{\eta K} + L\eta\sigma^2,
$$

from the <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">descent lemma</NoteLink> and unbiasedness <Cite id="bottou2018" />. With
$\eta \propto 1/\sqrt K$ the average squared gradient norm falls like $1/\sqrt K$. This guarantees approximate
stationarity, not a minimum. The theory of neural network training rests on this bound and on empirical findings.

## Worked example

Take $F(x) = \tfrac{\mu}{2}x^2$ with $\mu = 1$, and gradients $\mu x + \sigma\epsilon$ with $\epsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$
and $\sigma = 1$. With $\eta = 0.1$ the recursion is exact: $D_{k+1} = 0.81 D_k + 0.01$. The fixed point is
$D_\infty = 0.01/0.19 = 0.0526$, matching $\eta\sigma^2/(\mu(2 - \eta\mu))$. A simulation of 20000 runs for 2000 steps
gives $0.0529$. The approximation $\eta\sigma^2/(2\mu) = 0.05$ is close. With $\eta = 0.01$, the floor drops to
$0.0050$, but the contraction per step weakens from $0.9$ to $0.99$, so reaching the floor from $D_0 = 9$ takes about
ten times as many steps.

## Assumptions

- **Unbiased gradients from independent samples.** <NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">Sampling without
  replacement</NoteLink> (epochs over shuffled data) violates <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> within
  an epoch; it usually converges faster, but the analysis differs.
- **Bounded variance.** The results use a bound on $\sigma^2$ or $G^2$ over the path. For least squares the variance
  grows with $\norm{\xvec}$, and the bounds hold only in a bounded region or under a growth condition.

## Pitfalls

- **A flat loss curve is not convergence.** With a constant step, the training loss stops improving at the noise floor.
  Decaying the step, or averaging, often lowers it further. See <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate
  schedules</NoteLink>.
- **Reducing the noise instead of the step.** Larger <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">minibatches</NoteLink>
  divide $\sigma^2$ by the batch size, and <NoteLink to="variance-reduction">variance reduction</NoteLink> removes the
  floor for finite sums while keeping a constant step.
- **Rates count samples, not time.** SGD's $O(1/\sqrt K)$ is worse than gradient descent's $O(1/K)$ per iteration, but
  each SGD iteration is $n$ times cheaper. For large $n$ and modest accuracy, SGD reaches the target with less total
  computation than gradient descent <Cite id="bottou2010" />.
