<Definition>
For a loss $F(\xvec) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f_i(\xvec)$, a **minibatch gradient** averages the gradients of a random
subset $\Bcal$ of $B$ examples: $\gvec_\Bcal = \frac1B\sum_{i \in \Bcal}\nabla f_i(\xvec)$. It is an unbiased estimate
of $\nabla F(\xvec)$. If the examples are drawn independently, with per-example gradient covariance $\Sigmamat$, its
covariance is $\Sigmamat/B$ <Cite id="bottou2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A single-example gradient is cheap and noisy. A full-batch gradient is exact and costs $n$ example-gradients.
A minibatch sits between them. Averaging $B$ independent gradients leaves the mean unchanged and divides the variance
by $B$, so the noise, measured by its <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>, falls like $1/\sqrt B$.

On parallel hardware, the $B$ gradients in a batch are computed at the same time. Up to the size that fills the
hardware, a batch of 256 costs about the same wall-clock time as a batch of 1. The question is then not the cost per
step but how much progress each step makes, and how that progress grows with $B$.

## Noise and the step size

In the analysis of <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">SGD</NoteLink>, the noise enters through the
variance $\sigma^2$. With batch size $B$ it becomes $\sigma^2/B$. For a strongly convex problem the constant-step noise
floor becomes approximately

$$
\expect\norm{\xvec - \xvec^*}^2 \approx \frac{\eta\sigma^2}{2\mu B}.
$$

The floor depends on $\eta/B$. Doubling the batch and doubling the step keeps the floor fixed while halving the number
of steps needed to cover the same distance. This is the **linear scaling rule**: multiply the <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> by $k$ when
the batch is multiplied by $k$ <Cite id="goyal2017" />. It fails once $\eta$ approaches the curvature limit $2/L$ of
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. Past that point the step cannot grow, and extra batch size
only lowers noise that is no longer the bottleneck.

Changing the batch size during training is equivalent, to first order, to changing the step. Increasing the batch
instead of decaying the learning rate gives similar results with fewer, larger steps <Cite id="smith2018batch" />.

## The critical batch size

How much does a larger batch help per step? Model the loss near the current point by a quadratic with gradient
$\bar\gvec = \nabla F$ and Hessian $\Hmat$. A step $-\epsilon\gvec_\Bcal$ changes the expected loss by

$$
\expect\LB F(\xvec - \epsilon\gvec_\Bcal)\RB - F(\xvec) \approx -\epsilon\norm{\bar\gvec}^2 + \frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\LP\bar\gvec^\top\Hmat\bar\gvec + \frac{\trace(\Hmat\Sigmamat)}{B}\RP.
$$

The second-order term uses $\expect[\gvec^\top\Hmat\gvec] = \bar\gvec^\top\Hmat\bar\gvec + \trace(\Hmat\cov(\gvec))$ with
$\cov(\gvec_\Bcal) = \Sigmamat/B$. Minimising over $\epsilon$ gives the best step and the best expected decrease:

$$
\epsilon_{\text{opt}}(B) = \frac{\epsilon_{\max}}{1 + B_{\text{noise}}/B}, \qquad
\Delta F_{\text{opt}}(B) = \frac{\Delta F_{\max}}{1 + B_{\text{noise}}/B}, \qquad
B_{\text{noise}} = \frac{\trace(\Hmat\Sigmamat)}{\bar\gvec^\top\Hmat\bar\gvec},
$$

where $\epsilon_{\max} = \norm{\bar\gvec}^2/\bar\gvec^\top\Hmat\bar\gvec$ and $\Delta F_{\max} = \norm{\bar\gvec}^4/(2\bar\gvec^\top\Hmat\bar\gvec)$
are the values for an exact gradient <Cite id="mccandlish2018" />.

The **gradient noise scale** $B_{\text{noise}}$ separates two regimes.

- For $B \ll B_{\text{noise}}$, $\Delta F_{\text{opt}} \approx \Delta F_{\max} B/B_{\text{noise}}$: progress per step
  grows linearly with $B$, and the optimal step grows with it. Doubling the batch halves the number of steps at no extra
  cost in examples. This is the regime of the linear scaling rule.
- For $B \gg B_{\text{noise}}$, progress per step saturates at $\Delta F_{\max}$. Doubling the batch doubles the
  examples per step and barely reduces the number of steps.

When the Hessian is a multiple of the identity, the noise scale simplifies to $B_{\text{simple}} = \trace(\Sigmamat)/
\norm{\bar\gvec}^2$: total gradient variance divided by squared gradient norm. It can be estimated during training from
gradient norms at two batch sizes. It grows as training proceeds, because $\norm{\bar\gvec}$ shrinks, which is why larger
batches become useful later in training.

## Worked example

**Progress per step and per example.** Suppose $B_{\text{noise}} = 100$. The fraction of the exact-gradient progress
that one step achieves is $1/(1 + 100/B)$:

| $B$ | progress per step | progress per example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | $0.091\,\Delta F_{\max}$ | $0.0091\,\Delta F_{\max}$ |
| 100 | $0.5\,\Delta F_{\max}$ | $0.005\,\Delta F_{\max}$ |
| 1000 | $0.909\,\Delta F_{\max}$ | $0.00091\,\Delta F_{\max}$ |

Going from 10 to 100 multiplies progress per step by 5.5 at a cost of 10 times the examples. Going from 100 to 1000
multiplies it by only 1.8. Near $B_{\text{noise}}$ the trade-off between steps and examples is balanced.

**<NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">Sampling without replacement</NoteLink>.** Drawing a batch without replacement from a finite dataset reduces the variance by the
finite-population factor $(n - B)/(n - 1)$. For $n = 1000$ and $B = 100$ the variance is $0.00901\,\sigma^2$ instead of
$0.01\,\sigma^2$: a 10% reduction. For $B = n$ it is zero, since the batch is the full dataset.

<Derivation title="Variance of a minibatch drawn without replacement">
Let $\vvec_i = \nabla f_i(\xvec) - \nabla F(\xvec)$, so $\sum_i\vvec_i = \zeros$, and let
$\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i\norm{\vvec_i}^2$. The batch error is $\bar\vvec = \frac1B\sum_{i \in \Bcal}\vvec_i$. Each $i$
is in $\Bcal$ with probability $B/n$, and each pair $i \ne j$ with probability $B(B - 1)/(n(n - 1))$. Then

$$
\expect\norm{\bar\vvec}^2 = \frac{1}{B^2}\LP\frac{B}{n}\sum_i\norm{\vvec_i}^2 + \frac{B(B - 1)}{n(n - 1)}\sum_{i \ne j}\vvec_i^\top\vvec_j\RP.
$$

Since $\sum_{i \ne j}\vvec_i^\top\vvec_j = \norm{\sum_i\vvec_i}^2 - \sum_i\norm{\vvec_i}^2 = -n\sigma^2$, this is
$\frac{\sigma^2}{B}\LP 1 - \frac{B - 1}{n - 1}\RP = \frac{\sigma^2}{B}\cdot\frac{n - B}{n - 1}$.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

- **Local quadratic model.** The critical batch size comes from a second-order expansion around the current point. It
  describes the next step, and it changes as training proceeds.
- **Independent examples.** Correlated examples in a batch, such as consecutive frames of a video, carry less
  information than $B$ independent ones, and the variance falls more slowly than $1/B$.

## Pitfalls

- **Scaling the batch without the step.** A larger batch with an unchanged learning rate makes the same progress per
  step with more computation. Adjust the step, typically with a warm-up period at the start
  <Cite id="goyal2017" />.
- **Generalisation.** Very large batches have been reported to find sharper minima that generalise worse
  <Cite id="keskar2017" />. Much of the gap closes with tuned step sizes and longer training, and the effect is still
  debated.
- **Batch-dependent layers.** <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">Batch normalisation</NoteLink> computes statistics over
  the batch. Changing the batch size changes the model's behaviour, not only the optimiser's noise.
- **Throughput is not progress.** A batch that saturates the hardware maximises examples per second, not loss decrease
  per second. Beyond the critical batch size, the extra examples are mostly wasted.
