<Definition>
The **convex conjugate** (Fenchel conjugate) of a function $f : \reals^n \to \reals \cup \set{+\infty}$ is

$$
f^*(\yvec) = \sup_{\xvec}\,\LP \yvec^\top\xvec - f(\xvec) \RP.
$$

It maps a slope $\yvec$ to a number. It is convex whether or not $f$ is. For every $\xvec$ and $\yvec$ it satisfies
the **Fenchel–Young inequality** $f(\xvec) + f^*(\yvec) \ge \yvec^\top\xvec$ <Cite id="fenchel1949" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Fix a slope $\yvec$. The linear function $\yvec^\top\xvec$ and the graph of $f$ differ by $\yvec^\top\xvec - f(\xvec)$.
The conjugate $f^*(\yvec)$ is the largest such difference. Equivalently, $-f^*(\yvec)$ is the intercept of the
highest line (hyperplane) of slope $\yvec$ that stays below the graph of $f$. A <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> is the upper envelope
of the lines that lie below it. The conjugate records, for each slope, where that line sits. It describes $f$ by its
supporting lines instead of by its points.

For differentiable strictly convex $f$ and a slope $\yvec$ in the range of $\nabla f$, the supremum is attained where the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> equals the slope:
$\nabla f(\xvec) = \yvec$. Writing $\xvec(\yvec)$ for that point,
$f^*(\yvec) = \yvec^\top\xvec(\yvec) - f(\xvec(\yvec))$. This is the classical **Legendre transform**.

## Properties

**Convexity.** For each fixed $\xvec$, the map $\yvec \mapsto \yvec^\top\xvec - f(\xvec)$ is affine. The conjugate is
their pointwise supremum, and a pointwise supremum of affine functions is convex and closed.

**Fenchel–Young inequality.** For any $\xvec$ and $\yvec$, the value at $\xvec$ is one of the candidates in the
supremum, so $f^*(\yvec) \ge \yvec^\top\xvec - f(\xvec)$. Rearranged, $f(\xvec) + f^*(\yvec) \ge \yvec^\top\xvec$.
For convex $f$, equality holds exactly when $\yvec \in \partial f(\xvec)$: the supremum is attained at $\xvec$ precisely
when $\yvec$ is a <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradient</NoteLink> there. With $f(\xvec) = \tfrac12\norm{\xvec}^2$,
which is its own conjugate, the inequality becomes $\tfrac12\norm{\xvec}^2 + \tfrac12\norm{\yvec}^2 \ge \yvec^\top\xvec$.

**Inverse gradients.** For closed convex $f$, $\yvec \in \partial f(\xvec)$ if and only if $\xvec \in \partial f^*(\yvec)$.
When both are differentiable, $\nabla f^* = (\nabla f)^{-1}$. <NoteLink to="mirror-descent">Mirror descent</NoteLink> uses
$\nabla f$ and $\nabla f^*$ to move between a space and its dual.

**Biconjugate.** The conjugate of the conjugate is $f^{**}$. By Fenchel–Young, $f^{**}(\xvec) = \sup_{\yvec}(\yvec^\top
\xvec - f^*(\yvec)) \le f(\xvec)$. The biconjugate is the largest closed convex function below $f$, its convex
envelope. When $f$ is closed and convex, $f^{**} = f$ <Cite id="rockafellar1970" />. The transform is then an
involution: no information is lost.

**Scaling rules.** For $a > 0$: the conjugate of $a f(\xvec)$ is $a f^*(\yvec/a)$; the conjugate of $f(\xvec) + \bvec^\top
\xvec$ is $f^*(\yvec - \bvec)$; and for separable $f(\xvec) = \sum_i f_i(x_i)$ the conjugate is $\sum_i f_i^*(y_i)$.

## Examples

| $f(x)$ | $f^*(y)$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\tfrac12 x^2$ | $\tfrac12 y^2$ |
| $\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Qmat\xvec$, $\Qmat \succ 0$ | $\tfrac12\yvec^\top\Qmat^{-1}\yvec$ |
| $\abs{x}$ | $0$ if $\abs{y} \le 1$, $+\infty$ otherwise |
| $\norm{\xvec}$ (any norm) | $0$ if the dual norm $\norm{\yvec}_* \le 1$, $+\infty$ otherwise |
| $e^x$ | $y\log y - y$ for $y > 0$; $0$ at $y = 0$; $+\infty$ for $y < 0$ |
| $-\log x$, $x > 0$ | $-1 - \log(-y)$ for $y < 0$ |
| $\log\sum_i e^{x_i}$ | $\sum_i y_i\log y_i$ on the probability simplex, $+\infty$ elsewhere |

The quadratic case follows from the Legendre transform: $\nabla f(\xvec) = \Qmat\xvec = \yvec$ gives $\xvec =
\Qmat^{-1}\yvec$ and $f^* = \yvec^\top\Qmat^{-1}\yvec - \tfrac12\yvec^\top\Qmat^{-1}\yvec$.

The absolute value case shows that the conjugate of a norm is the indicator of the unit ball of the dual norm. For
$\abs{y} \le 1$, $yx - \abs{x} \le 0$ with equality at $x = 0$. For $\abs{y} > 1$, the difference grows without bound
as $x \to \sgn(y)\cdot\infty$.

The last row pairs <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">log-sum-exp</NoteLink> with negative <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink>. The gradient
of log-sum-exp is the softmax, and the inverse-gradient property says the softmax output is the point on the simplex
where the entropy-regularised linear objective is maximised. The same pairing links the log-partition function of an
<NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> to the negative entropy of its mean parameters.

## Worked example: the exponential

Take $f(x) = e^x$. For $y > 0$, maximise $yx - e^x$: the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $y - e^x$ vanishes at $x = \log y$, giving
$f^*(y) = y\log y - y$. For $y = 0$, $-e^x$ has supremum $0$ as $x \to -\infty$. For $y < 0$, $yx - e^x \to +\infty$ as
$x \to -\infty$, so $f^*(y) = +\infty$.

Check Fenchel–Young at $x = 0$, $y = 1$. Here $f(0) = 1$ and $f^*(1) = 1\cdot 0 - 1 = -1$. The sum is $0$, and
$yx = 0$. Equality holds, as it must: $y = 1 = f'(0)$. At $x = 0$, $y = 2$ instead, $f^*(2) = 2\log 2 - 2 = -0.614$
and $f(0) + f^*(2) = 0.386 > 0 = yx$: the inequality is strict because $2 \ne f'(0)$.

## Fenchel duality

Conjugates give the dual of a composite problem without writing a Lagrangian by hand. Consider
$p^* = \min_{\xvec} f(\xvec) + g(\Amat\xvec)$. Introduce $\zvec = \Amat\xvec$ with multiplier $\yvec$:

$$
L(\xvec, \zvec, \yvec) = f(\xvec) + g(\zvec) + \yvec^\top(\Amat\xvec - \zvec).
$$

Minimising over $\xvec$ gives $-\sup_{\xvec}((-\Amat^\top\yvec)^\top\xvec - f(\xvec)) = -f^*(-\Amat^\top\yvec)$.
Minimising over $\zvec$ gives $-g^*(\yvec)$. The dual problem is

$$
d^* = \max_{\yvec}\ -f^*(-\Amat^\top\yvec) - g^*(\yvec),
$$

with $d^* \le p^*$ by <NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">weak duality</NoteLink>, and equality under mild conditions.
For the lasso, $f = \lambda\norm{\cdot}_1$ has conjugate the indicator of $\norm{\yvec}_\infty \le \lambda$, so the
dual of the lasso is a quadratic problem over a box. Duality gaps computed this way are the stopping rule of lasso
solvers.

## Pitfalls

- **Extended values are essential.** Conjugates routinely take the value $+\infty$. Treating them as real-valued
  functions loses the implicit constraints (such as $\abs{y} \le 1$ above) that carry the information.
- **Nonconvex $f$.** The conjugate of a nonconvex function is still convex, but $f^{**}$ is its convex envelope, not
  $f$. Dualising a nonconvex problem through conjugates silently convexifies it.
- **Legendre transform needs strict convexity.** The formula $f^*(\yvec) = \yvec^\top\xvec - f(\xvec)$ at
  $\nabla f(\xvec) = \yvec$ assumes the gradient is invertible. For functions with flat pieces or kinks, use the
  supremum definition.
