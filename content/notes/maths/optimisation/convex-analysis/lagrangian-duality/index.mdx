<Definition>
For the problem of minimising $f_0(\xvec)$ subject to $f_i(\xvec) \le 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$ and
$h_j(\xvec) = 0$ for $j = 1, \dots, p$, the **Lagrangian** is

$$
L(\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec) = f_0(\xvec) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i f_i(\xvec) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j h_j(\xvec).
$$

The **dual function** is $g(\lambdavec, \nuvec) = \inf_{\xvec} L(\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec)$. The **dual problem**
maximises $g$ subject to $\lambdavec \ge \zeros$. Its optimal value $d^*$ never exceeds the primal optimal value
$p^*$ <Cite id="boyd2004" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A hard constraint $f_i(\xvec) \le 0$ is an infinite wall: any violation costs $+\infty$. The Lagrangian replaces the
wall with a linear price $\lambda_i f_i(\xvec)$. A nonnegative price never rewards a violation, and it rewards slack
($f_i < 0$) by lowering the cost. Minimising the priced objective over all $\xvec$, with no constraints, can only
reach lower than the constrained optimum. So every price vector gives a lower bound on $p^*$. The dual problem searches
for the prices that make the lower bound as high as possible.

The dual function is **concave** whatever the primal problem looks like. For each fixed $\xvec$, the Lagrangian is an
affine function of $(\lambdavec, \nuvec)$. The dual function is the pointwise infimum of a family of affine functions,
and a pointwise infimum of affine functions is concave. The dual problem is therefore a convex optimisation problem even
when the primal is not.

## Weak duality

**Claim.** For any $\lambdavec \ge \zeros$ and any $\nuvec$, $g(\lambdavec, \nuvec) \le p^*$.

**Proof.** Let $\tilde\xvec$ be any feasible point, so $f_i(\tilde\xvec) \le 0$ and $h_j(\tilde\xvec) = 0$. Then
each $\lambda_i f_i(\tilde\xvec) \le 0$ and each $\nu_j h_j(\tilde\xvec) = 0$, so

$$
g(\lambdavec, \nuvec) = \inf_{\xvec} L(\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec) \le L(\tilde\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec)
= f_0(\tilde\xvec) + \sum_i \lambda_i f_i(\tilde\xvec) \le f_0(\tilde\xvec).
$$

The bound holds for every feasible $\tilde\xvec$, so it holds for the infimum over them, which is $p^*$. Taking the
supremum over dual-feasible $(\lambdavec, \nuvec)$ gives $d^* \le p^*$.

The difference $p^* - d^*$ is the **duality gap**. For any primal-feasible $\xvec$ and dual-feasible
$(\lambdavec, \nuvec)$, the gap $f_0(\xvec) - g(\lambdavec, \nuvec)$ bounds how far $\xvec$ is from optimal. Solvers
use it as a stopping criterion that needs no knowledge of $p^*$.

## Strong duality and Slater's condition

**Strong duality** means $d^* = p^*$. It does not hold in general. For convex problems (convex $f_0, f_i$ and
affine $h_j$) it holds under a **constraint qualification**. The most common is **Slater's condition**: there is a
strictly feasible point, one with $f_i(\xvec) < 0$ for every $i$ and $h_j(\xvec) = 0$ for every $j$. Affine
inequality constraints need not be strict <Cite id="boyd2004" />.

The proof separates two <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex sets</NoteLink> with a hyperplane. Consider the
set of achievable (constraint, objective) values,
$\Acal = \set{(\uvec, t) : \exists\,\xvec,\ f_i(\xvec) \le u_i,\ h_j(\xvec) = 0,\ f_0(\xvec) \le t}$. For a convex
problem $\Acal$ is convex. The point $(\zeros, p^*)$ lies on its boundary, and a supporting hyperplane at that point has
normal $(\lambdavec, 1)$ with $\lambdavec \ge \zeros$. The hyperplane's offset is $g(\lambdavec)$, which equals $p^*$.
Slater's point guarantees the hyperplane is not vertical, so the normal's last component can be scaled to 1.

<Derivation title="The hyperplane argument in one inequality constraint">
Take one constraint $f_1(\xvec) \le 0$ and no equalities. Plot the set
$\Gcal = \set{(f_1(\xvec), f_0(\xvec)) : \xvec \in \reals^n}$ in the plane, with constraint value $u$ on the horizontal
axis and objective $t$ on the vertical axis. For a fixed $\lambda \ge 0$, the value $g(\lambda) = \inf_{(u, t) \in
\Gcal} (t + \lambda u)$ is the intercept on the $t$ axis of the lowest line of slope $-\lambda$ that touches $\Gcal$.
Every point with $u \le 0$ is feasible, and every such point lies above that line, so its height $t$ is at least the
intercept. This is weak duality in a picture.

The primal value $p^*$ is the lowest $t$ among points of $\Gcal$ with $u \le 0$. Strong duality asks for a line that
touches the region above and to the right of $\Gcal$ exactly at height $p^*$ on the $t$ axis. When that region is
convex such a supporting line exists. It fails only if the supporting line is vertical, which happens when the only
feasible points have $u = 0$ exactly. A Slater point with $u < 0$ rules that out.
</Derivation>

Linear programs have strong duality whenever either the primal or the dual is feasible, with no Slater point needed.
Nonconvex problems usually have a positive gap. Their duals still give certified lower bounds, which is how
relaxations of combinatorial problems are used.

## Saddle-point form

The primal problem is $p^* = \inf_{\xvec}\sup_{\lambdavec \ge \zeros, \nuvec} L(\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec)$: the
inner supremum is $f_0(\xvec)$ when $\xvec$ is feasible and $+\infty$ otherwise. The dual is the same expression with
the order swapped, $d^* = \sup_{\lambdavec \ge \zeros, \nuvec}\inf_{\xvec} L$. Weak duality is the general inequality
$\sup\inf \le \inf\sup$. Strong duality with attained optima means $(\xvec^*, \lambdavec^*, \nuvec^*)$ is a saddle
point of $L$: a minimum in $\xvec$ and a maximum in the multipliers. The
<NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">KKT conditions</NoteLink> characterise such saddle points.

## Dual of a linear program

Take the standard-form LP: minimise $\cvec^\top\xvec$ subject to $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ and $\xvec \ge \zeros$. Write
the constraint $\xvec \ge \zeros$ as $-\xvec \le \zeros$ with multiplier $\lambdavec \ge \zeros$, and
$\Amat\xvec - \bvec = \zeros$ with multiplier $\nuvec$:

$$
L = \cvec^\top\xvec - \lambdavec^\top\xvec + \nuvec^\top(\Amat\xvec - \bvec)
= -\bvec^\top\nuvec + (\cvec + \Amat^\top\nuvec - \lambdavec)^\top\xvec.
$$

A linear function of $\xvec$ is bounded below only if its coefficient is zero. So $g(\lambdavec, \nuvec) =
-\bvec^\top\nuvec$ when $\cvec + \Amat^\top\nuvec = \lambdavec \ge \zeros$, and $-\infty$ otherwise. Substituting
$\yvec = -\nuvec$, the dual is: maximise $\bvec^\top\yvec$ subject to $\Amat^\top\yvec \le \cvec$. The dual of an LP is
another LP, with one variable per primal constraint and one constraint per primal variable. See
<NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear programming</NoteLink>. The dual of the
<NoteLink to="optimal-transport">optimal transport</NoteLink> problem is <NoteLink to="kantorovich-duality">Kantorovich
duality</NoteLink>.

## Dual of a quadratic program

Take minimise $\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec + \qvec^\top\xvec$ subject to $\Amat\xvec \le \bvec$, with $\Pmat$ positive
definite. The Lagrangian is $\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec + (\qvec + \Amat^\top\lambdavec)^\top\xvec -
\bvec^\top\lambdavec$. It is a strictly convex quadratic in $\xvec$, minimised where its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> vanishes: $\xvec = -\Pmat^{-1}(\qvec + \Amat^\top\lambdavec)$. Substituting,

$$
g(\lambdavec) = -\tfrac12(\qvec + \Amat^\top\lambdavec)^\top\Pmat^{-1}(\qvec + \Amat^\top\lambdavec) - \bvec^\top\lambdavec.
$$

The dual maximises this concave quadratic over $\lambdavec \ge \zeros$. The only constraints left are simple bounds,
which is why the dual is often easier to solve. The <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector
machine</NoteLink> dual has this form, and its dual variables are nonzero only for the support vectors.

## Worked example

Minimise $x^2$ subject to $x \ge 1$. The optimum is $x^* = 1$ with $p^* = 1$. Write the constraint as $1 - x \le 0$.
The Lagrangian is $L(x, \lambda) = x^2 + \lambda(1 - x)$. Setting $\partial L/\partial x = 2x - \lambda = 0$ gives
$x = \lambda/2$, so

$$
g(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{4} + \lambda\LP 1 - \frac{\lambda}{2}\RP = \lambda - \frac{\lambda^2}{4}.
$$

For $\lambda = 1$ the bound is $g(1) = 0.75 \le 1$. The dual maximum is at $g'(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda/2 = 0$, so
$\lambda^* = 2$ and $d^* = g(2) = 1 = p^*$. Strong duality holds, as Slater's condition predicts: $x = 2$ is strictly
feasible. The minimiser of the Lagrangian at $\lambda^*$ is $x = \lambda^*/2 = 1 = x^*$. The multiplier
$\lambda^* = 2$ is the rate at which $p^*$ grows as the constraint tightens: for $x \ge c$ the optimum is $c^2$, with
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $2c = 2$ at $c = 1$.

## Assumptions

- Weak duality needs nothing: no convexity, no differentiability.
- Strong duality needs convexity and a constraint qualification, or special structure such as linearity.
- The dual function can be $-\infty$ for many multipliers. Those multipliers are excluded implicitly; the effective
  dual constraints come from requiring the infimum to be finite, as in the LP example.

## Pitfalls

- **A dual optimum does not always give a primal optimum.** If the Lagrangian at $\lambdavec^*$ has several
  minimisers, not all of them are feasible. For LPs the Lagrangian is linear in $\xvec$ and every $\xvec$ minimises it
  at the dual optimum; the primal solution must be recovered another way, for example from complementary slackness.
- **Sign conventions vary.** Writing constraints as $f_i \ge 0$ or maximising the primal flips signs throughout. Fix
  one convention and derive the dual from it.
- **Reformulations change the dual.** Two primal problems with the same solution can have different duals. Adding a
  redundant constraint, or introducing a new variable with an equality constraint, can make the dual easier or harder.
