<Definition>
Let $f : \reals^n \to \reals$ be convex. A vector $\gvec$ is a **subgradient** of $f$ at $\xvec$ if

$$
f(\yvec) \ge f(\xvec) + \gvec^\top(\yvec - \xvec) \quad \text{for all } \yvec.
$$

The set of all subgradients at $\xvec$ is the **subdifferential** $\partial f(\xvec)$. It is a nonempty, closed,
convex set at every point in the interior of the domain. It is the single point $\set{\nabla f(\xvec)}$ where $f$ is
differentiable <Cite id="rockafellar1970" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

For a differentiable convex function, the tangent plane at $\xvec$ lies below the graph everywhere (see
<NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex sets and functions</NoteLink>). At a kink there is no tangent plane,
but there are many planes that touch the graph at $\xvec$ and stay below it. Each such supporting plane has a slope.
The subdifferential collects those slopes. A smooth point has exactly one supporting plane. A corner has a fan of them.

## Examples

**Absolute value.** For $f(x) = \abs{x}$:

- If $x > 0$, $f$ is differentiable with slope 1, so $\partial f(x) = \set{1}$.
- If $x < 0$, $\partial f(x) = \set{-1}$.
- At $x = 0$, $g$ is a subgradient if $\abs{y} \ge g y$ for all $y$. Taking $y = 1$ gives $g \le 1$ and $y = -1$
  gives $g \ge -1$. Every $g \in [-1, 1]$ satisfies the inequality, so $\partial f(0) = [-1, 1]$.

For the $\ell_1$ norm $\norm{\xvec}_1 = \sum_i \abs{x_i}$, the subdifferential is the product of the coordinate-wise
sets: $g_i = \sgn(x_i)$ where $x_i \ne 0$ and $g_i \in [-1, 1]$ where $x_i = 0$.

**Pointwise maximum.** Let $f(\xvec) = \max_i f_i(\xvec)$ with each $f_i$ convex and differentiable. Call $f_i$
active at $\xvec$ if $f_i(\xvec) = f(\xvec)$. The subdifferential is the convex hull of the active <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>:

$$
\partial f(\xvec) = \operatorname{conv}\set{\nabla f_i(\xvec) : f_i \text{ active at } \xvec}.
$$

Each active gradient is a subgradient, because $f(\yvec) \ge f_i(\yvec) \ge f_i(\xvec) + \nabla f_i(\xvec)^\top(\yvec
- \xvec)$ and $f_i(\xvec) = f(\xvec)$. The subdifferential is convex, so it contains their convex hull. The reverse
inclusion is proved in <Cite id="rockafellar1970" />.

**<Gloss name="hinge-loss">Hinge loss</Gloss>.** The hinge loss $\ell(z) = \max(0, 1 - z)$ is the maximum of $0$ and $1 - z$, with slopes $0$ and
$-1$. For $z < 1$ only $1 - z$ is active and $\partial\ell(z) = \set{-1}$. For $z > 1$ only $0$ is active and
$\partial\ell(z) = \set{0}$. At $z = 1$ both are active and $\partial\ell(1) = [-1, 0]$. For a linear classifier
with score $z = y\,\wvec^\top\xvec$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> for affine maps gives $\partial_{\wvec}\ell = y\xvec\,\partial\ell(z)$.
This is the subgradient used to train the <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> in the
primal.

## Calculus rules

Three rules cover most cases <Cite id="boyd2004" />.

- **Scaling.** $\partial(\alpha f)(\xvec) = \alpha\,\partial f(\xvec)$ for $\alpha > 0$.
- **Sum.** $\partial(f_1 + f_2)(\xvec) = \partial f_1(\xvec) + \partial f_2(\xvec)$, the set of all sums
  $\gvec_1 + \gvec_2$. The inclusion $\supseteq$ follows by adding the two subgradient inequalities. Equality needs a
  mild condition, such as both functions being finite everywhere.
- **Affine composition.** For $h(\xvec) = f(\Amat\xvec + \bvec)$,
  $\partial h(\xvec) = \Amat^\top\partial f(\Amat\xvec + \bvec)$.

## Optimality: 0 is a subgradient

**Claim.** A convex $f$ attains its minimum at $\xvec^*$ if and only if $\zeros \in \partial f(\xvec^*)$.

**Proof.** Substitute $\gvec = \zeros$ in the definition. The statement $\zeros \in \partial f(\xvec^*)$ reads
$f(\yvec) \ge f(\xvec^*)$ for all $\yvec$, which is the definition of a global minimum.

The condition extends $\nabla f = \zeros$ to functions with kinks.
The <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> optimality conditions and the
soft-thresholding operator follow from it directly (see <NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">proximal gradient
methods</NoteLink>).

## Worked example: the median

Let $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \abs{x - a_i}$ for data $a_1, \dots, a_n$. By the sum rule, the subdifferential is

$$
\partial f(x) = \#\set{i : a_i < x} - \#\set{i : a_i > x} + [-m, m], \qquad m = \#\set{i : a_i = x}.
$$

For data $\set{1, 2, 7}$:

- At $x = 4$: one point lies above and two below, so $\partial f(4) = \set{2 - 1} = \set{1}$. The minimum is to the
  left.
- At $x = 2$: one point lies below, one above, one equal, so $\partial f(2) = 1 - 1 + [-1, 1] = [-1, 1]$. This contains
  $0$, so $x = 2$ is the minimiser.

The minimiser is the median. The condition $0 \in \partial f$ says that at most half the data lie strictly on either
side. The outlier 7 has no more influence than any other point above the median, which is why the $\ell_1$ loss is
robust.

## The subgradient method

The **subgradient method** replaces the gradient in <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> with any subgradient:
$\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \eta_k\gvec_k$ with $\gvec_k \in \partial f(\xvec_k)$. A negative subgradient need not be a
descent direction. Take $f(x, y) = \abs{x} + 2\abs{y}$ at the point $(1, 0)$, where $f = 1$. By the sum rule,
$\partial f(1, 0) = \set{(1, g) : g \in [-2, 2]}$, so $(1, 2)$ is a subgradient. A step of length $\eta$ against it
reaches $(1 - \eta, -2\eta)$, where $f = 1 - \eta + 4\eta = 1 + 3\eta$. The objective rises. The method still makes
progress in distance to the minimiser, and that is what its analysis tracks.

Assume $\norm{\gvec} \le G$ for all subgradients and $\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*} \le R$. Expand the squared distance:

$$
\norm{\xvec_{k+1} - \xvec^*}^2 = \norm{\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2 - 2\eta_k\gvec_k^\top(\xvec_k - \xvec^*) + \eta_k^2\norm{\gvec_k}^2.
$$

The subgradient inequality at $\yvec = \xvec^*$ gives $\gvec_k^\top(\xvec_k - \xvec^*) \ge f(\xvec_k) - f^*$. Hence
$2\eta_k(f(\xvec_k) - f^*) \le \norm{\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2 - \norm{\xvec_{k+1} - \xvec^*}^2 + \eta_k^2 G^2$. Summing
from $k = 0$ to $K - 1$, the distances telescope:

$$
\min_{k < K} f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le \frac{R^2 + G^2\sum_k \eta_k^2}{2\sum_k \eta_k}.
$$

With a constant step $\eta = R/(G\sqrt{K})$, the bound is $RG/\sqrt{K}$ <Cite id="nesterov2004" />. Reaching accuracy
$\epsilon$ takes $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ iterations, against $O(1/\epsilon)$ for gradient descent on smooth functions. No
first-order method can beat $O(1/\sqrt{K})$ on the class of Lipschitz convex functions in high dimension, so the
subgradient method is optimal for that class. The same argument, with <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink>, gives the rate of
<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Not a descent method.** The objective can rise between iterations. Track the best iterate, or an average of
  iterates, rather than the last one.
- **Small steps are required.** A constant step size converges only to within $G^2\eta/2$ of the optimum. Exact
  convergence needs $\eta_k \to 0$ with $\sum\eta_k = \infty$.
- **Subgradients of nonconvex functions.** For nonconvex functions the definition above is usually empty at many points.
  Automatic differentiation of $\abs{x}$ or <Gloss name="relu" /> at 0 returns one fixed value, which is a valid subgradient of the
  convex case only.
- **Exploit structure when possible.** When the nonsmooth part is simple, such as an $\ell_1$ penalty,
  <NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">proximal methods</NoteLink> recover the $O(1/k)$ rate of smooth problems.
