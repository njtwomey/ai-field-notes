<Definition>
**Nesterov's accelerated gradient method** minimises an $L$-smooth convex $f$ from $\xvec_0$ by setting
$\xvec_1 = \xvec_0 - \tfrac1L\nabla f(\xvec_0)$, $t_1 = 1$, and for $k \ge 1$

$$
t_{k+1} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 4t_k^2}}{2}, \qquad
\yvec_k = \xvec_k + \frac{t_k - 1}{t_{k+1}}(\xvec_k - \xvec_{k-1}), \qquad
\xvec_{k+1} = \yvec_k - \frac1L\nabla f(\yvec_k).
$$

It guarantees $f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le 2L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2/(k+1)^2$ <Cite id="nesterov1983" />
<Cite id="beck2009" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> with step $1/L$ uses only the current
gradient. On a long, shallow valley it takes many short steps in nearly the same direction. An accelerated method
remembers the previous displacement $\xvec_k - \xvec_{k-1}$ and extrapolates along it before taking the gradient step.
The extrapolation weight $(t_k - 1)/t_{k+1}$ starts at 0 and rises towards 1 as $1 - 3/k$ for large $k$. Consistent
directions build speed. The gradient at the extrapolated point, rather than at $\xvec_k$, corrects the step before it
overshoots.

The gain is in the rate, not the constant. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> needs $O(1/\epsilon)$ iterations for accuracy $\epsilon$.
The accelerated method needs $O(1/\sqrt\epsilon)$. For $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ that is the difference between about a
million iterations and about a thousand.

The deep-learning view of the same update, with a fixed momentum coefficient and stochastic gradients, is in
<NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">momentum and Nesterov momentum</NoteLink>. This note derives the guarantees.

## The O(1/k²) rate

The proof builds a quantity that never increases and that bounds $f(\xvec_k) - f^*$ <Cite id="beck2009" />.

**Step 1: a one-step inequality.** Let $\xvec^+ = \yvec - \tfrac1L\nabla f(\yvec)$ be a gradient step from any
$\yvec$. The <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">descent lemma</NoteLink> gives $f(\xvec^+) \le f(\yvec) - \tfrac{1}{2L}\norm{\nabla f(\yvec)}^2$. Convexity gives
$f(\yvec) \le f(\uvec) + \nabla f(\yvec)^\top(\yvec - \uvec)$ for any $\uvec$. Adding them and writing
$\nabla f(\yvec) = L(\yvec - \xvec^+)$,

$$
f(\xvec^+) \le f(\uvec) + L(\yvec - \xvec^+)^\top(\yvec - \uvec) - \frac{L}{2}\norm{\yvec - \xvec^+}^2
= f(\uvec) + \frac{L}{2}\LP\norm{\yvec - \uvec}^2 - \norm{\xvec^+ - \uvec}^2\RP.
$$

The equality uses $2\avec^\top\bvec - \norm{\avec}^2 = \norm{\bvec}^2 - \norm{\bvec - \avec}^2$ with
$\avec = \yvec - \xvec^+$ and $\bvec = \yvec - \uvec$.

**Step 2: choose $\uvec$ between the iterate and the optimum.** Apply step 1 at $\yvec = \yvec_k$, so
$\xvec^+ = \xvec_{k+1}$, with $\uvec = (1 - 1/t)\xvec_k + (1/t)\xvec^*$ and $t = t_{k+1} \ge 1$. By convexity
$f(\uvec) \le (1 - 1/t)f(\xvec_k) + (1/t)f^*$. Write $\delta_k = f(\xvec_k) - f^*$ and multiply by $t^2$:

$$
t^2\delta_{k+1} \le (t^2 - t)\,\delta_k + \frac{L}{2}\LP\norm{t\yvec_k - (t-1)\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2 - \norm{t\xvec_{k+1} - (t-1)\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2\RP.
$$

**Step 3: the choices of $t_k$ and $\yvec_k$ make it telescope.** The recursion for $t_{k+1}$ is the positive root of
$t_{k+1}^2 - t_{k+1} = t_k^2$, so the first term is $t_k^2\delta_k$. The extrapolation rule is equivalent to
$t_{k+1}\yvec_k - (t_{k+1} - 1)\xvec_k = t_k\xvec_k - (t_k - 1)\xvec_{k-1}$. Define
$\wvec_k = t_k\xvec_k - (t_k - 1)\xvec_{k-1} - \xvec^*$. The inequality becomes

$$
t_{k+1}^2\delta_{k+1} + \frac{L}{2}\norm{\wvec_{k+1}}^2 \le t_k^2\delta_k + \frac{L}{2}\norm{\wvec_k}^2.
$$

The quantity $E_k = t_k^2\delta_k + \tfrac{L}{2}\norm{\wvec_k}^2$ never increases.

**Step 4: start and finish.** At $k = 1$, $t_1 = 1$ and $\wvec_1 = \xvec_1 - \xvec^*$. Step 1 with $\yvec = \xvec_0$
and $\uvec = \xvec^*$ gives $E_1 \le \tfrac{L}{2}\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2$. So
$t_k^2\delta_k \le E_k \le \tfrac{L}{2}\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2$. Finally, $\sqrt{1 + 4t^2} \ge 2t$ gives
$t_{k+1} \ge t_k + \tfrac12$, so $t_k \ge (k+1)/2$ and

$$
f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le \frac{L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2}{2t_k^2} \le \frac{2L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2}{(k+1)^2}.
$$

Unlike gradient descent, the function values need not decrease at every step. The guarantee is on $E_k$, which mixes
function value and distance.

## The lower bound

Nesterov constructed a quadratic, a tridiagonal "chain" function, on which any method whose iterates lie in the span
of the previous gradients has $f(\xvec_k) - f^* \ge 3L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2/(32(k+1)^2)$ for $k$ up to half the
dimension <Cite id="nesterov2004" />. Each gradient reveals only one more coordinate of the chain. The accelerated method
matches this bound up to a constant factor, so it is an **optimal** first-order method for the class. Gradient descent
is a factor of $k$ slower.

## Strongly convex functions

If $f$ is also $\mu$-strongly convex, with $\kappa = L/\mu$, a constant momentum weight

$$
\beta = \frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}, \qquad \yvec_k = \xvec_k + \beta(\xvec_k - \xvec_{k-1}), \qquad
\xvec_{k+1} = \yvec_k - \frac1L\nabla f(\yvec_k),
$$

gives $f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le (1 - 1/\sqrt\kappa)^k\,(f(\xvec_0) - f^* + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2)$
<Cite id="nesterov2004" />. The number of iterations for accuracy $\epsilon$ drops from $\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)$ for
gradient descent to $\sqrt\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)$. For $\kappa = 100$ and a reduction by $10^6$, that is about 1380
against 140. The lower bound for this class is $((\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1))^k$, so this rate is optimal up to
constants.

The constant weight needs $\mu$. The $t_k$ schedule does not, and it still converges on strongly convex problems, but
only at the $O(1/k^2)$ rate, with visible ripples. Restarting the schedule whenever $f$ increases recovers a linear
rate in practice without knowing $\mu$.

## Heavy ball on quadratics

Polyak's **heavy-ball method** evaluates the gradient at $\xvec_k$ instead of at an extrapolated point:
$\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \alpha\nabla f(\xvec_k) + \beta(\xvec_k - \xvec_{k-1})$ <Cite id="polyak1964" />. On a
quadratic with <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> eigenvalues in $[\mu, L]$, the choice

$$
\alpha = \frac{4}{(\sqrt L + \sqrt\mu)^2}, \qquad \beta = \LP\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\RP^2
$$

makes every eigen-direction contract by $(\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)$ per step. That is faster than Nesterov's
$1 - 1/\sqrt\kappa$. The derivation, by eigen-decomposing a two-step linear recursion, is in
<NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">momentum and Nesterov momentum</NoteLink>.

The heavy-ball guarantee does not extend beyond quadratics. There are smooth strongly convex functions on which heavy
ball with these parameters cycles and never converges <Cite id="lessard2016" />. Nesterov's method, by evaluating the
gradient after extrapolating, keeps its guarantee on the whole class.

At $\kappa = 100$ and the default start, reducing $f$ by a factor $10^6$ takes 480 iterations of gradient descent, 65
of Nesterov's method with known $\mu$ and 58 of heavy ball. The step $1/L$ removes the steep component in one
iteration, so gradient descent then moves straight along the valley floor, and its slow progress there accounts for the whole
iteration count. Heavy ball's larger step overshoots the floor and spirals in, so its curve is not monotone. The convex schedule
starts fast, but its curve ripples: each ripple is a stretch in which the momentum is too large for the flat direction.
Raising $\kappa$ widens the gap between gradient descent and the accelerated methods.

## Pitfalls

- **Acceleration is fragile to noise.** With stochastic gradients the extrapolation amplifies errors, and the
  accelerated rate is lost to the noise term. In deep learning, momentum helps mostly through smoothing, not through
  the rate derived here.
- **Nonmonotone progress.** The function value rises at times. A stopping rule based on a single increase will stop too
  early.
- **The step must be $1/L$ or smaller.** Overestimating the step breaks the proof. A backtracking version increases
  $L$ whenever the descent lemma fails at the tried step.
- **Proximal version.** The same scheme with a proximal step in place of the gradient step is FISTA, which carries the
  $O(1/k^2)$ rate to composite problems such as the lasso (see <NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">proximal
  gradient methods</NoteLink>).
