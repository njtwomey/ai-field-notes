<Definition>
<NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> descent minimises a differentiable loss $L(\thetavec)$ by repeating the update
$\thetavec \leftarrow \thetavec - \eta \nabla L(\thetavec)$. The step size $\eta > 0$
is also called the learning rate. The negative gradient is the direction of steepest local decrease.
</Definition>

## The update

The gradient $\nabla L(\thetavec)$ points in the direction of steepest increase. Its length is the rate of
increase in that direction. Each step moves a distance proportional to the gradient's length. Steps therefore shrink
as the gradient approaches zero near a minimum.

The step size is the only hyperparameter of plain gradient descent with a constant step. It controls both speed and stability.

## Step size

The examples below fit a line $y \approx wx + b$ by minimising the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error (MSE)</Gloss> over the two parameters
$(w, b)$. The MSE is a quadratic function of $(w, b)$. Its contours are ellipses.

Four regimes appear as $\eta$ grows:

- **Too small.** The path is smooth but slow. Most of the budget is spent crossing the valley.
- **Well chosen.** The path reaches the minimum in few steps.
- **Overshooting.** The path zig-zags across the valley floor. The loss still falls at every step.
- **Too large.** Each step overshoots by more than the last. The loss grows without bound.

## The stability limit

Near a minimum, any smooth loss is approximately quadratic. Let $H$ be its <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>, with eigenvalues
$\lambda_{\min} \le \dots \le \lambda_{\max}$. Along the eigenvector of $\lambda_i$, each step multiplies the
distance to the minimum by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$ <Cite id="boyd2004" />.

- If $\eta\lambda_i < 1$, the distance shrinks without changing sign.
- If $1 < \eta\lambda_i < 2$, the distance shrinks but changes sign each step. This is the zig-zag.
- If $\eta\lambda_i > 2$, the distance grows. The iteration diverges.

Gradient descent therefore converges on a quadratic if and only if $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$. The eigenvector of
$\lambda_{\max}$, the direction of largest curvature, sets the limit.

<Derivation title="Why each direction scales by 1 − ηλ">
Write the quadratic as $L(\thetavec) = L^* + \tfrac{1}{2}(\thetavec - \thetavec^*)^\top
H (\thetavec - \thetavec^*)$. Its gradient is $H(\thetavec - \thetavec^*)$.

Let $\evec_t = \thetavec_t - \thetavec^*$ be the error. The update gives
$\evec_{t+1} = \evec_t - \eta H \evec_t = (I - \eta H)\evec_t$.

$H$ is symmetric, so it has orthogonal eigenvectors. In that basis, $I - \eta H$ is diagonal with entries
$1 - \eta\lambda_i$. Each component of the error evolves independently: $e_{t,i} = (1 - \eta\lambda_i)^t e_{0,i}$.

The excess loss is $L - L^* = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i \lambda_i e_{t,i}^2$. Every term shrinks while
$|1 - \eta\lambda_i| < 1$. The loss therefore decreases monotonically on a quadratic, even while the parameters
zig-zag.
</Derivation>

## Conditioning

The <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ measures how elongated the loss contours are. The
stability limit is set by $\lambda_{\max}$. Progress along the flattest direction is set by $\lambda_{\min}$. A large
$\kappa$ forces a step small enough for the steep direction and too small for the flat one.

The best constant step is $\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda_{\max})$. The error then shrinks by at least a factor
$(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step <Cite id="boyd2004" />. In the example above, $\kappa \approx 32$. Each step
removes only about 6% of the remaining error.

The feature $x$ in the example is not centred. That couples $w$ and $b$ and tilts the ellipses. Centring and scaling
features reduces $\kappa$.

## Momentum

The heavy-ball method adds a velocity term <Cite id="polyak1964" />:

$$
\vvec \leftarrow \beta\vvec - \eta\nabla L(\thetavec), \qquad
\thetavec \leftarrow \thetavec + \vvec
$$

The coefficient $\beta \in [0, 1)$ sets how much of the previous step carries over. With $\beta = 0$ the method is
plain gradient descent.

Momentum helps because the two directions of a narrow valley behave differently. Across the valley, the gradient
changes sign each step, and the accumulated velocity cancels. Along the valley, the gradient keeps the same sign,
and the velocity accumulates. The effective step along the floor grows toward $\eta/(1 - \beta)$
<Cite id="goh2017" />.

much narrower than the one above.

On a quadratic with well-tuned $\eta$ and $\beta$, the error shrinks by a factor $(\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)$
per step <Cite id="polyak1964" />. This replaces $\kappa$ with $\sqrt\kappa$ in the rate. For $\kappa = 100$, plain
gradient descent needs about 115 steps to reduce the error tenfold. Tuned momentum needs about 12.

<Callout variant="warning">
Momentum does not decrease the loss at every step. With $\beta$ close to 1, the iterate overshoots the valley floor
and returns. The loss rises and falls. A loss curve that oscillates is not by itself a sign of a bad step size.
</Callout>

Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point $\thetavec + \beta\vvec$ instead of at
$\thetavec$. It is the variant most used for training neural networks <Cite id="sutskever2013" />.

## Choosing a step size

- Start from a stable value and increase it until the loss stops falling or starts to oscillate. Then back off.
- A schedule that decreases $\eta$ over training allows large early steps and precise late ones.
- A <NoteLink to="line-search">line search</NoteLink> chooses $\eta$ at each step by testing the loss along the gradient direction <Cite id="boyd2004" />.
- Adaptive methods such as Adam scale the step separately for each parameter <Cite id="ruder2016" />.

## Pitfalls

- A loss that is falling slowly may be ill-conditioned rather than near convergence. Plot the excess loss on a log
  scale. A straight line means steady <NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">linear convergence</NoteLink>.
- Raw loss values hide the difference between two slow runs. Compare distance to the optimum, or compare on a log
  scale.
- The stability limit depends on the data scale. Rescaling a feature by 10 multiplies the corresponding curvature by
  100.
