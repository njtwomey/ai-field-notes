<Definition>
Let $\psi$ be a differentiable, strictly convex function, the **mirror map**. Its **Bregman divergence** is
$D_\psi(\xvec, \yvec) = \psi(\xvec) - \psi(\yvec) - \nabla\psi(\yvec)^\top(\xvec - \yvec)$. **Mirror descent** for
minimising a convex $f$ over a convex set $\Ccal$ updates

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = \argmin_{\xvec \in \Ccal}\ \eta\,\gvec_k^\top\xvec + D_\psi(\xvec, \xvec_k), \qquad \gvec_k \in \partial f(\xvec_k).
$$

With $\psi = \tfrac12\norm{\xvec}^2$, $D_\psi$ is half the squared Euclidean distance and the update is projected
(sub)gradient descent <Cite id="beck2003" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradient method</NoteLink> measures step length in the Euclidean norm. Its rate is
$RG/\sqrt{K}$, where $R$ is the Euclidean radius of the feasible set and $G$ bounds the Euclidean norm of
subgradients. For some sets the Euclidean norm is a poor fit. On the probability simplex in $\reals^n$, gradients are
often bounded coordinate-wise, so their Euclidean norm can be $\sqrt n$ times larger than their largest entry.

Mirror descent measures distance with a divergence adapted to the set. The Bregman divergence $D_\psi(\xvec, \yvec)$
is the gap at $\xvec$ between $\psi$ and its tangent plane at $\yvec$. It is nonnegative by convexity and zero only when
$\xvec = \yvec$. It is not symmetric. The KL divergence and the squared Euclidean distance are both Bregman divergences; their
other properties are covered in <NoteLink to="bregman-divergences-and-scoring-rules">Bregman divergences and scoring
rules</NoteLink>.

The name comes from the unconstrained update. Setting the gradient of the objective to zero gives
$\eta\gvec_k + \nabla\psi(\xvec_{k+1}) - \nabla\psi(\xvec_k) = \zeros$, that is

$$
\nabla\psi(\xvec_{k+1}) = \nabla\psi(\xvec_k) - \eta\gvec_k.
$$

The gradient step is taken in the "mirror" (dual) space of $\nabla\psi$ values, and mapped back through the inverse
of $\nabla\psi$, which is $\nabla\psi^*$ by the inverse-gradient property of <NoteLink to="conjugate-functions">conjugate
functions</NoteLink>.

## Exponentiated gradient

On the simplex $\Delta_n = \set{\xvec \ge \zeros : \ones^\top\xvec = 1}$, take the negative entropy
$\psi(\xvec) = \sum_i x_i\log x_i$. Its Bregman divergence between points of the simplex is the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> $\sum_i x_i\log(x_i/y_i)$. The update minimises
$\eta\gvec^\top\xvec + \sum_i x_i\log(x_i/x_{k,i})$ subject to $\ones^\top\xvec = 1$. With multiplier $\nu$ for the
constraint, the stationarity condition in $x_i$ is $\eta g_i + \log(x_i/x_{k,i}) + 1 + \nu = 0$, so
$x_i = x_{k,i}e^{-\eta g_i}e^{-1-\nu}$. The factor $e^{-1-\nu}$ is fixed by normalisation:

$$
x_{k+1,i} = \frac{x_{k,i}\,e^{-\eta g_{k,i}}}{\sum_j x_{k,j}\,e^{-\eta g_{k,j}}}.
$$

This is the **exponentiated gradient** update <Cite id="kivinen1997" />, also called multiplicative weights. It stays
on the simplex without any projection. Coordinates with large gradients lose weight multiplicatively.

## Rate

The analysis of the subgradient method carries over with $D_\psi$ in place of the squared distance. If $\psi$ is
1-strongly convex with respect to a norm $\norm{\cdot}$ and $\norm{\gvec_k}_* \le G$ in the dual norm, then with a
constant step

$$
\min_{k < K} f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le \frac{D_\psi(\xvec^*, \xvec_0)}{\eta K} + \frac{\eta G^2}{2}
$$

<Cite id="beck2003" />. Choosing $\eta$ to balance the terms gives $G\sqrt{2D_\psi(\xvec^*, \xvec_0)/K}$.

For the simplex with negative entropy, $\psi$ is 1-strongly convex with respect to $\norm{\cdot}_1$ (this is Pinsker's
inequality), the dual norm is $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$, and starting from the uniform point gives
$D_\psi(\xvec^*, \xvec_0) \le \log n$. The rate is $G_\infty\sqrt{2\log n/K}$. Projected subgradient descent on the
same problem has a bound of order $G_\infty\sqrt{n/K}$, because $G_2$ can be $\sqrt n\,G_\infty$. In high dimension the
difference between $\log n$ and $n$ is large.

## Worked example

Start at the uniform point $\xvec_0 = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ with gradient $\gvec = (1, 0, -1)$ and $\eta = 1$. The
unnormalised weights are $\tfrac13(e^{-1}, 1, e^{1}) = (0.123, 0.333, 0.906)$. Normalising gives
$\xvec_1 = (0.090, 0.245, 0.665)$. The coordinate with negative gradient gains weight, the one with positive gradient
loses it, and the point stays on the simplex. A Euclidean step of the same size, $\xvec_0 - \gvec = (-0.667, 0.333,
1.333)$, leaves the simplex and must be projected back.

## Pitfalls

- **The divergence must match the set.** The gradient of negative entropy, $\log x_i + 1$, tends to $-\infty$ at the
  edge of the simplex, which keeps iterates strictly inside. On an unbounded set negative entropy is not a sensible
  choice.
- **Zero weights are permanent.** Multiplicative updates never revive a coordinate that reaches exactly zero. Start from
  a point with all coordinates positive.
- **Online learning.** The same update, applied to a sequence of loss vectors, is the Hedge algorithm for prediction
  with expert advice. Its regret after $K$ rounds is of order $\sqrt{K\log n}$: the rate above multiplied by $K$.
- **Adaptive methods.** AdaGrad is mirror descent with a quadratic mirror map $\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Hmat_k\xvec$ that
  adapts to the observed gradients (see <NoteLink to="adagrad-and-rmsprop">AdaGrad and RMSProp</NoteLink>).
