<Definition>
A differentiable function $f$ is **$L$-smooth** if its gradient is $L$-Lipschitz:
$\norm{\nabla f(\xvec) - \nabla f(\yvec)} \le L\norm{\xvec - \yvec}$. For $L$-smooth convex $f$ with minimiser
$\xvec^*$, <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> $\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \tfrac1L\nabla
f(\xvec_k)$ satisfies

$$
f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le \frac{L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2}{2k}.
$$

If $f$ is also $\mu$-strongly convex, $f(\xvec_k) - f^* \le (1 - \mu/L)^k (f(\xvec_0) - f^*)$
<Cite id="nesterov2004" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two numbers describe a smooth <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> for the purposes of
gradient descent. The smoothness constant $L$ is an upper bound on curvature: the largest eigenvalue of the
<NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>, when it exists. The strong convexity constant $\mu$ is a lower bound on
curvature. Upper curvature limits the step size, because a step that is long compared with $1/L$ can overshoot. Lower
curvature guarantees that a small gradient means being close to the minimum. Without it, the function can be nearly flat
far from the minimiser, and progress slows to a sublinear rate.

The ratio $\kappa = L/\mu$ is the <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> of the problem. It sets
the number of steps needed per digit of accuracy.

## The descent lemma

$L$-smoothness gives a quadratic upper bound on $f$ around any point:

$$
f(\yvec) \le f(\xvec) + \nabla f(\xvec)^\top(\yvec - \xvec) + \frac{L}{2}\norm{\yvec - \xvec}^2.
$$

**Proof.** Write $f(\yvec) - f(\xvec)$ as the integral of the <NoteLink to="gradient">directional derivative</NoteLink>
along the segment: $f(\yvec) - f(\xvec) = \int_0^1 \nabla f(\xvec + t(\yvec - \xvec))^\top(\yvec - \xvec)\,dt$. Subtract
$\nabla f(\xvec)^\top(\yvec - \xvec)$ and bound the rest with Cauchy–Schwarz and the
<NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">Lipschitz condition</NoteLink>:

$$
\int_0^1 \LP\nabla f(\xvec + t(\yvec - \xvec)) - \nabla f(\xvec)\RP^\top(\yvec - \xvec)\,dt
\le \int_0^1 L t\norm{\yvec - \xvec}^2\,dt = \frac{L}{2}\norm{\yvec - \xvec}^2.
$$

**Sufficient decrease.** Put $\yvec = \xvec - \eta\nabla f(\xvec)$ into the bound:

$$
f(\xvec - \eta\nabla f(\xvec)) \le f(\xvec) - \eta\LP 1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\RP\norm{\nabla f(\xvec)}^2.
$$

The factor $\eta(1 - L\eta/2)$ is positive for $0 < \eta < 2/L$ and largest at $\eta = 1/L$. With $\eta = 1/L$, each
step decreases $f$ by at least $\norm{\nabla f(\xvec)}^2/(2L)$. This holds for any $L$-smooth function, convex or not.

## Nonconvex functions: stationarity

Sum the sufficient decrease inequality over $k = 0, \dots, K - 1$. The left side telescopes:

$$
\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\norm{\nabla f(\xvec_k)}^2 \le f(\xvec_0) - f(\xvec_K) \le f(\xvec_0) - f^*.
$$

The smallest gradient seen therefore satisfies $\min_{k<K}\norm{\nabla f(\xvec_k)}^2 \le 2L(f(\xvec_0) - f^*)/K$.
Gradient descent finds an approximately stationary point at rate $O(1/\sqrt{K})$ in gradient norm. For nonconvex
functions this is all that can be said: a stationary point can be a saddle or a local minimum.

## Convex functions: the O(1/k) rate

Convexity adds a lower bound, $f^* \ge f(\xvec) + \nabla f(\xvec)^\top(\xvec^* - \xvec)$. Write $\gvec =
\nabla f(\xvec_k)$ and $\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \gvec/L$. Combine the sufficient decrease with convexity:

$$
f(\xvec_{k+1}) - f^* \le \gvec^\top(\xvec_k - \xvec^*) - \frac{1}{2L}\norm{\gvec}^2.
$$

The right-hand side is a difference of squared distances. Expanding
$\norm{\xvec_{k+1} - \xvec^*}^2 = \norm{\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2 - \tfrac{2}{L}\gvec^\top(\xvec_k - \xvec^*) +
\tfrac{1}{L^2}\norm{\gvec}^2$ shows that

$$
f(\xvec_{k+1}) - f^* \le \frac{L}{2}\LP\norm{\xvec_k - \xvec^*}^2 - \norm{\xvec_{k+1} - \xvec^*}^2\RP.
$$

Two consequences follow. The distance to $\xvec^*$ never increases, since the left side is nonnegative. And summing
over $k = 0, \dots, K - 1$ telescopes the right side:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{K}(f(\xvec_k) - f^*) \le \frac{L}{2}\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2.
$$

The function values decrease monotonically, so the last term is the smallest of the $K$ terms, and
$f(\xvec_K) - f^* \le L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2/(2K)$. Halving the error takes twice as many steps. This is
**sublinear** convergence: $O(1/\epsilon)$ steps for accuracy $\epsilon$.

## Strongly convex functions: the linear rate

Strong convexity with constant $\mu$ implies the **Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) inequality**
$\norm{\nabla f(\xvec)}^2 \ge 2\mu(f(\xvec) - f^*)$. To see it, minimise both sides of the strong convexity bound
$f(\yvec) \ge f(\xvec) + \nabla f(\xvec)^\top(\yvec - \xvec) + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\norm{\yvec - \xvec}^2$ over $\yvec$. The
left side becomes $f^*$. The right side is a quadratic in $\yvec$ minimised at $\yvec = \xvec - \nabla f(\xvec)/\mu$,
where it equals $f(\xvec) - \norm{\nabla f(\xvec)}^2/(2\mu)$.

Substitute PL into the sufficient decrease with $\eta = 1/L$:

$$
f(\xvec_{k+1}) - f^* \le f(\xvec_k) - f^* - \frac{1}{2L}\norm{\nabla f(\xvec_k)}^2 \le \LP 1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\RP(f(\xvec_k) - f^*).
$$

The error shrinks by the constant factor $1 - 1/\kappa$ at each step. This is **linear** convergence: a straight line
on a log scale. The number of steps for accuracy $\epsilon$ is about $\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)$. The argument needs only
PL, not strong convexity. The linear rate therefore also holds for least squares with a rank-deficient design, which
is convex but not strongly convex, and for some nonconvex functions <Cite id="bottou2018" />.

The step $2/(L + \mu)$ improves the rate in distance to $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step
<Cite id="nesterov2004" />. On a quadratic this is the best constant step, as derived in
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. Both rates scale with $\kappa$.

## Worked example

Take $f(\xvec) = \tfrac12(x_1^2 + x_2^2/100)$, so $L = 1$, $\mu = 0.01$ and $\kappa = 100$. Start at
$\xvec_0 = (1, 1)$, where $f(\xvec_0) = 0.505$ and $\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2 = 2$.

- The step $1/L = 1$ solves the steep coordinate $x_1$ in one step. The flat coordinate shrinks by $1 - 0.01 = 0.99$
  per step, and $f$ by $0.99^2$.
- After 10 steps $f = 0.0041$. After 100 steps $f = 6.7\times10^{-4}$. After 1000 steps $f = 9.3\times10^{-12}$.
- The linear bound predicts that reducing the error by $10^6$ takes $\log(10^6)/(-\log 0.99) \approx 1375$ steps.
  The approximation $\kappa\log(1/\epsilon) = 100 \times 13.8 \approx 1382$ agrees.
- The sublinear bound $L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2/(2k) = 1/k$ is loose here: it gives $10^{-3}$ at $k = 1000$,
  because it ignores strong convexity.

<NoteLink to="accelerated-gradient-methods">Accelerated methods</NoteLink> reduce the dependence to $\sqrt\kappa$: on
the same problem they need about 140 steps for the same factor $10^6$.

## Lower bounds

No method that only combines past gradients can beat $O(L\norm{\xvec_0 - \xvec^*}^2/k^2)$ on $L$-smooth convex
functions, or a rate of $((\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1))^k$ on strongly convex ones, for $k$ smaller than the
dimension <Cite id="nesterov2004" />. Gradient descent is a factor of $k$ (convex) or $\sqrt\kappa$ (strongly convex)
away from these limits. Accelerated gradient methods attain them.

## Assumptions

- **Global smoothness.** $L$ must bound the curvature everywhere along the path. Functions such as $x^4$ or the
  logistic loss with unbounded features have no global $L$ on $\reals^n$. A <NoteLink to="line-search">line
  search</NoteLink> adapts the step to the local curvature instead.
- **Exact gradients.** With noisy gradients the rates change; see
  <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">stochastic gradient descent theory</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Rates are worst-case bounds.** The observed rate can be much better, for example when the start point has no
  component along the flattest direction.
- **The step must match $L$.** With $\eta > 2/L$ the sufficient decrease guarantee is lost and the iteration can
  diverge. Estimating $L$ from a Hessian at one point can underestimate it elsewhere.
- **Suboptimality is not distance.** Without strong convexity, a small $f(\xvec_k) - f^*$ does not imply that
  $\xvec_k$ is near a minimiser: the function can be flat over a long valley.
