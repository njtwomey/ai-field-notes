<Definition>
A **linear program** (LP) minimises a linear function subject to linear equality and inequality constraints. In
**standard form** it is

$$
\min_{\xvec \in \reals^n}\ \cvec^\top\xvec \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Amat\xvec = \bvec,\ \ \xvec \ge \zeros,
$$

with $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ of full row rank $m < n$. Its **dual** is $\max_{\yvec}\ \bvec^\top\yvec$
subject to $\Amat^\top\yvec \le \cvec$. When either problem has a finite optimum, both do, and the optimal values are
equal <Cite id="dantzig1963" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Each linear inequality $\avec_i^\top\xvec \le b_i$ keeps one side of a hyperplane. Their intersection, the **feasible
region**, is a convex polyhedron with flat faces and corners. The objective $\cvec^\top\xvec$ has parallel hyperplanes
as level sets. Minimising it means sliding a level hyperplane in the direction $-\cvec$ until it is about to leave the
polyhedron. The last contact is a corner, an edge or a whole face. In every case it includes a corner. So a finite
optimum, when it exists, is attained at a **vertex**, and the search can be restricted to vertices.

Any LP can be put in standard form. An inequality $\avec^\top\xvec \le b$ becomes $\avec^\top\xvec + s = b$ with a
**slack** variable $s \ge 0$. A free variable $x$ is written as $x^+ - x^-$ with both parts nonnegative. Maximisation
becomes minimisation of $-\cvec^\top\xvec$.

## Why the optimum is at a vertex

A **vertex** of the feasible set $\Pcal$ is a point that is not the midpoint of two other points of $\Pcal$. In
standard form, vertices are the **basic feasible solutions**: choose $m$ columns of $\Amat$ that form an invertible
matrix $\Bmat$, set the other $n - m$ variables to zero, and solve $\Bmat\xvec_B = \bvec$. If the result is nonnegative,
it is a vertex. There are at most $\binom{n}{m}$ of them.

Suppose $\xvec$ is optimal but not a vertex. Then $\xvec = \tfrac12(\xvec + \dvec) + \tfrac12(\xvec - \dvec)$ for
some $\dvec \ne \zeros$ with both $\xvec \pm \dvec$ feasible. Since $\xvec$ is optimal, neither point can be better, so
$\cvec^\top\dvec = 0$ and the whole segment is optimal. Move along $\pm\dvec$ until a new coordinate of $\xvec$ reaches
zero. The new point is still optimal and has one more zero coordinate. Repeating reaches a point with at most $m$
nonzero coordinates whose columns are independent: a vertex. If the feasible set contains no line, which holds in
standard form, this process terminates <Cite id="boyd2004" />.

## The simplex method

The **simplex method** moves from vertex to adjacent vertex, improving the objective at each move <Cite id="dantzig1963" />.
Two vertices are adjacent when their sets of basic columns differ in one column.

1. At a vertex with basis $\Bmat$, compute the **reduced costs** of the nonbasic variables, the rate at which the
   objective changes if one of them is increased from zero while the basic variables adjust to keep $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$.
2. If no reduced cost is negative, the vertex is optimal. Stop.
3. Otherwise choose a nonbasic variable with negative reduced cost to enter the basis. Increase it until a basic
   variable reaches zero (the **ratio test**). That variable leaves the basis.
4. If no basic variable ever reaches zero, the LP is unbounded. Otherwise repeat from the new vertex.

The number of vertices can grow exponentially, and there are LPs (the Klee–Minty cubes) on which the simplex method
with the classic pivot rule visits all $2^n$ vertices <Cite id="nocedal2006" />. On practical problems it typically
needs a small multiple of $m$ pivots.

**Interior-point methods** instead follow a smooth path through the interior of the polyhedron towards the optimum.
Karmarkar's method was the first practical one with a polynomial bound on the number of iterations
<Cite id="karmarkar1984" />. Modern solvers use primal-dual interior-point methods based on log barriers (see
<NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">penalty and barrier methods</NoteLink>). They take 20 to 60 iterations almost
independently of problem size, each iteration being a sparse linear solve.

## Duality

Take an LP in inequality form: maximise $\cvec^\top\xvec$ subject to $\Amat\xvec \le \bvec$, $\xvec \ge \zeros$. Multiply
the $i$-th constraint by a weight $y_i \ge 0$ and add them: $\yvec^\top\Amat\xvec \le \yvec^\top\bvec$. If the weights
also satisfy $\Amat^\top\yvec \ge \cvec$, then for every feasible $\xvec \ge \zeros$,

$$
\cvec^\top\xvec \le (\Amat^\top\yvec)^\top\xvec = \yvec^\top\Amat\xvec \le \bvec^\top\yvec.
$$

Every such $\yvec$ gives an upper bound on the primal objective. The dual LP looks for the smallest bound: minimise
$\bvec^\top\yvec$ subject to $\Amat^\top\yvec \ge \cvec$, $\yvec \ge \zeros$. This is **weak duality**. The same dual
follows from the Lagrangian (see <NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">Lagrangian duality</NoteLink>).

**Strong duality** holds for every LP with a finite optimum: the best bound is tight. No Slater condition is needed.
The inequalities in the chain above are then equalities, which gives **complementary slackness**:

- if a primal constraint is slack ($\avec_i^\top\xvec < b_i$), its dual variable is zero;
- if a primal variable is positive, its dual constraint is tight.

Each dual variable $y_i$ is the **shadow price** of resource $i$: the increase in the optimal objective per unit
increase of $b_i$.

The transportation problem, moving goods from supplies to demands at least cost, is a classic LP. Its value is the
<NoteLink to="earth-movers-distance">earth mover's distance</NoteLink>, and its dual variables are the potentials of
<NoteLink to="kantorovich-duality">Kantorovich duality</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Maximise $3x_1 + 2x_2$ subject to $x_1 + x_2 \le 4$, $x_1 + 3x_2 \le 6$, $x_1 \le 3.5$ and $x_1, x_2 \ge 0$.

The feasible region is a pentagon with vertices $(0, 0)$, $(3.5, 0)$, $(3.5, 0.5)$, $(3, 1)$ and $(0, 2)$. The objective
values there are $0$, $10.5$, $11.5$, $11$ and $4$. The optimum is $(3.5, 0.5)$ with value $11.5$. Starting at the
origin, the simplex method increases $x_1$ first (its objective coefficient is larger) and stops at $(3.5, 0)$ when the
constraint $x_1 \le 3.5$ binds. It then increases $x_2$ along the edge $x_1 = 3.5$ until $x_1 + x_2 \le 4$ binds, at
$(3.5, 0.5)$. No adjacent vertex improves the objective, so it stops after two pivots.

The dual is: minimise $4y_1 + 6y_2 + 3.5y_3$ subject to $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 \ge 3$, $y_1 + 3y_2 \ge 2$, $\yvec \ge \zeros$.
Complementary slackness finds its solution. The second primal constraint is slack ($3.5 + 1.5 = 5 < 6$), so $y_2 = 0$.
Both primal variables are positive, so both dual constraints are tight: $y_1 + y_3 = 3$ and $y_1 = 2$. Hence
$\yvec = (2, 0, 1)$ with dual value $8 + 3.5 = 11.5$, equal to the primal value. Raising the right-hand side of the first
constraint from 4 raises the optimum at rate $y_1 = 2$. The rate holds until that right-hand side reaches $13/3$,
where the point $(3.5,\ 5/6)$ makes the second constraint active and the basis changes.

## Pitfalls

- **Infeasible and unbounded are different failures.** An infeasible LP has no feasible point; an unbounded one has
  feasible points with objective tending to $-\infty$. Solvers report which. If the primal is unbounded the dual is
  infeasible.
- **Degeneracy.** When more than $m$ constraints pass through a vertex, simplex pivots can make no progress and, with a
  poor rule, cycle. Bland's rule or perturbation prevents cycling.
- **Integer variables change everything.** Requiring $\xvec$ to be integer makes the problem NP-hard. The LP relaxation
  gives a bound, used by branch-and-bound solvers.
- **Numerical scaling.** Coefficients spanning many orders of magnitude cause solvers to misjudge feasibility.
  Rescale rows and columns before solving.
