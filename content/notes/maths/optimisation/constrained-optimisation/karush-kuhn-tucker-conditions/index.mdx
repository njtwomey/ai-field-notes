<Definition>
For the problem of minimising $f_0(\xvec)$ subject to $f_i(\xvec) \le 0$ ($i = 1, \dots, m$) and $h_j(\xvec) = 0$
($j = 1, \dots, p$), with all functions differentiable, the **KKT conditions** at $(\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec)$ are:

1. **Stationarity:** $\nabla f_0(\xvec) + \sum_i \lambda_i\nabla f_i(\xvec) + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j(\xvec) = \zeros$.
2. **Primal feasibility:** $f_i(\xvec) \le 0$ and $h_j(\xvec) = 0$.
3. **Dual feasibility:** $\lambda_i \ge 0$.
4. **Complementary slackness:** $\lambda_i f_i(\xvec) = 0$ for each $i$.

They were stated by Karush in 1939 and by Kuhn and Tucker in 1951 <Cite id="kuhn1951" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An inequality constraint is either **active** at the optimum ($f_i(\xvec^*) = 0$, the point is on the boundary) or
**inactive** ($f_i(\xvec^*) < 0$, the point is strictly inside). An inactive constraint could be removed without
changing the local solution, so it should have no multiplier: $\lambda_i = 0$. An active constraint behaves like an
equality constraint, with one extra requirement. The constraint only pushes one way. At a minimum on the boundary of
$f_i \le 0$, the objective must be trying to leave the feasible region, not enter it. Otherwise moving inward would
decrease $f_0$. So $-\nabla f_0$ must point outward, along $+\nabla f_i$, which means $\lambda_i \ge 0$.

Complementary slackness combines both cases into one equation: for each $i$, either the constraint is active or its
multiplier is zero (or both).

## Derivation from duality

Suppose strong duality holds (see <NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">Lagrangian duality</NoteLink>) and that $\xvec^*$ is
primal optimal and $(\lambdavec^*, \nuvec^*)$ dual optimal. Write
$L(\xvec, \lambdavec, \nuvec) = f_0(\xvec) + \sum_i\lambda_i f_i(\xvec) + \sum_j\nu_j h_j(\xvec)$. Then

$$
f_0(\xvec^*) = g(\lambdavec^*, \nuvec^*) = \inf_{\xvec} L(\xvec, \lambdavec^*, \nuvec^*) \le L(\xvec^*, \lambdavec^*, \nuvec^*)
= f_0(\xvec^*) + \sum_i \lambda_i^* f_i(\xvec^*) + \sum_j \nu_j^* h_j(\xvec^*) \le f_0(\xvec^*).
$$

The first equality is strong duality. The last inequality holds because $h_j(\xvec^*) = 0$ and each
$\lambda_i^* f_i(\xvec^*) \le 0$. The chain starts and ends at the same number, so both inequalities are equalities.

- The first equality says that $\xvec^*$ minimises $L(\cdot, \lambdavec^*, \nuvec^*)$. Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> vanishes there,
  which is **stationarity**.
- The second says that $\sum_i \lambda_i^* f_i(\xvec^*) = 0$. Each term is $\le 0$, so each term is zero, which is
  **complementary slackness**.

Feasibility of $\xvec^*$ and of $\lambdavec^*$ hold by assumption. So under strong duality, every primal-dual optimal
pair satisfies KKT.

**For convex problems, KKT is also sufficient.** Let $f_0, f_i$ be convex and $h_j$ affine, and let
$(\tilde\xvec, \tilde\lambdavec, \tilde\nuvec)$ satisfy KKT. With $\tilde\lambdavec \ge \zeros$ the Lagrangian is convex
in $\xvec$, and stationarity makes $\tilde\xvec$ its global minimiser. Then
$g(\tilde\lambdavec, \tilde\nuvec) = L(\tilde\xvec, \tilde\lambdavec, \tilde\nuvec) = f_0(\tilde\xvec)$, using
complementary slackness and $h_j(\tilde\xvec) = 0$. A feasible point and a dual point with zero gap are both optimal by
weak duality.

For convex problems with a Slater point, KKT is therefore a complete characterisation: a point is optimal if and only
if there are multipliers making KKT hold <Cite id="boyd2004" />.

## Constraint qualifications

For nonconvex problems, KKT is necessary at a local minimum only under a **constraint qualification**, a condition on
the constraints that rules out degenerate geometry. The most common is **LICQ**: the gradients of the active
inequality constraints and of all equality constraints are linearly independent at $\xvec^*$
<Cite id="nocedal2006" />. Without a qualification, a minimum can exist where no multipliers satisfy stationarity, as in
the cusp example of <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multipliers</NoteLink>. KKT is then never sufficient
on its own; second-order conditions on the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> of the Lagrangian are needed, restricted to directions tangent to
the active constraints.

## Worked example

Minimise $f_0(x, y) = (x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2$ subject to $f_1(x, y) = x + y - 2 \le 0$. The unconstrained minimiser
$(2, 1)$ has $x + y = 3 > 2$, so it is infeasible.

Case analysis on complementary slackness:

- **$\lambda = 0$.** Stationarity gives $(2(x - 2), 2(y - 1)) = \zeros$, so $(x, y) = (2, 1)$. This violates the
  constraint. Rejected.
- **Constraint active, $x + y = 2$.** Stationarity is $(2(x - 2), 2(y - 1)) + \lambda(1, 1) = \zeros$, so
  $x = 2 - \lambda/2$ and $y = 1 - \lambda/2$. The active constraint gives $3 - \lambda = 2$, so $\lambda = 1 \ge 0$.
  Accepted.

The solution is $(1.5, 0.5)$ with $f_0^* = 0.5$ and $\lambda = 1$. The point is the projection of $(2, 1)$ onto the
halfplane. The multiplier is a sensitivity, as for equality constraints: for the constraint $x + y \le c$ with $c < 3$,
$f_0^*(c) = (3 - c)^2/2$, and $df_0^*/dc = -(3 - c) = -1$ at $c = 2$. Relaxing the constraint by one unit lowers the
optimum at rate $\lambda = 1$. For $c \ge 3$ the constraint is inactive, $\lambda = 0$ and $f_0^* = 0$.

The <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> is the classic case in machine learning.
Its KKT conditions say that each training point either lies strictly outside the margin, with multiplier zero, or lies
on or inside it, with a positive multiplier. Only the second group, the support vectors, determines the classifier.
In <NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="ordering-constraints-on-ordinal-thresholds">ordering constraints on the
thresholds</NoteLink> have one multiplier per neighbouring pair, which is positive only when the two thresholds coincide, so the constraint on that pair is active.

## Assumptions

- **Differentiability.** For nonsmooth convex problems, replace gradients by <NoteLink to="subgradients">subdifferentials</NoteLink>:
  stationarity becomes $\zeros \in \partial f_0(\xvec) + \sum_i\lambda_i\partial f_i(\xvec) + \sum_j\nu_j\nabla h_j(\xvec)$.
- **Strong duality or a constraint qualification** for necessity; convexity for sufficiency.

## Pitfalls

- **Case analysis grows exponentially.** With $m$ inequality constraints there are $2^m$ active sets. Hand solution by
  enumeration only works for tiny problems. Active-set and interior-point algorithms search the active sets
  efficiently (see <NoteLink to="quadratic-programming">quadratic programming</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">penalty and barrier methods</NoteLink>).
- **A negative multiplier marks a constraint to drop from the active set.** If the solution with a constraint forced active has $\lambda_i < 0$,
  moving into the interior decreases $f_0$.
- **Degenerate constraints.** An active constraint with $\lambda_i = 0$ is called weakly active. Small perturbations can
  switch it on or off, which makes solutions sensitive and some algorithms slow.
