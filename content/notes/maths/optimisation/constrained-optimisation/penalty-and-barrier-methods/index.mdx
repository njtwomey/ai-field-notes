<Definition>
For minimising $f_0(\xvec)$ subject to constraints, a **penalty method** minimises $f_0(\xvec) + \mu P(\xvec)$ for an
increasing sequence of weights $\mu$, where $P \ge 0$ vanishes exactly on the feasible set. A **barrier method**, for
inequality constraints $f_i(\xvec) \le 0$, minimises $t f_0(\xvec) - \sum_i \log(-f_i(\xvec))$ for an increasing
sequence of $t$, starting from a strictly feasible point. Each unconstrained minimiser is the starting point of the next
<Cite id="fiacco1990" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Unconstrained minimisation is well understood: Newton's method, quasi-Newton methods and gradient descent all apply.
Constraints break these methods, because a step can leave the feasible set. Penalty and barrier methods move the
constraints into the objective and reuse unconstrained solvers. The price is a sequence of problems instead of one,
and conditioning that deteriorates as the sequence approaches the constrained solution.

## Mechanism

### Quadratic penalty

For equality constraints $h_j(\xvec) = 0$ the quadratic penalty is $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_j h_j(\xvec)^2$. For
inequalities it is $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\sum_i \max(0, f_i(\xvec))^2$. The penalised minimiser $\xvec(\mu)$ is generally
infeasible, and approaches the constrained solution only as $\mu \to \infty$. At $\xvec(\mu)$, stationarity reads
$\nabla f_0 + \sum_j \mu h_j\nabla h_j = \zeros$. Comparing with the Lagrange condition, $\mu h_j(\xvec(\mu))$ estimates
the multiplier $\nu_j$. So the violation $h_j \approx \nu_j/\mu$ shrinks only like $1/\mu$.

Large $\mu$ makes the Hessian of the penalised function ill-conditioned: its curvature across the constraint grows like
$\mu$ while the curvature along it stays fixed. Unconstrained solvers slow down, and the sequence must increase $\mu$
gradually with warm starts.

An **exact penalty** $\mu\sum_j\abs{h_j(\xvec)}$ has the constrained solution as its exact minimiser for every $\mu$
larger than the largest $\abs{\nu_j}$. It is nonsmooth, which complicates the inner solver <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.

### Log barrier and the central path

For inequality constraints, the barrier $-\sum_i\log(-f_i(\xvec))$ is finite only strictly inside the feasible set and
grows to $+\infty$ at its boundary. Minimising $t f_0 - \sum_i \log(-f_i)$ therefore never leaves the interior. As $t$
grows, the objective dominates and the minimiser $\xvec^*(t)$ moves towards the constrained optimum along a smooth
curve, the **central path**.

The central path comes with a certificate of accuracy. Stationarity at $\xvec^*(t)$ is

$$
t\nabla f_0(\xvec^*) + \sum_i \frac{1}{-f_i(\xvec^*)}\nabla f_i(\xvec^*) = \zeros.
$$

Divide by $t$ and define $\lambda_i = 1/(-t f_i(\xvec^*)) > 0$. The equation says that $\xvec^*(t)$ minimises the
Lagrangian $L(\xvec, \lambdavec) = f_0 + \sum_i\lambda_i f_i$ for a convex problem, so the dual function at $\lambdavec$
is

$$
g(\lambdavec) = f_0(\xvec^*) + \sum_i \lambda_i f_i(\xvec^*) = f_0(\xvec^*) - \frac{m}{t}.
$$

By <NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">weak duality</NoteLink>, $f_0(\xvec^*(t)) - p^* \le m/t$, where $m$ is the number
of inequality constraints <Cite id="boyd2004" />. The central path point for weight $t$ is within $m/t$ of optimal,
with a dual point that proves it. The condition $\lambda_i(-f_i) = 1/t$ is a relaxed complementary slackness that
becomes exact as $t \to \infty$.

**Interior-point methods** follow the central path approximately: increase $t$ by a factor such as 10, take a few
damped Newton steps, and repeat. Primal-dual versions update $\xvec$ and $\lambdavec$ together by Newton steps on the
relaxed KKT equations. They solve linear programs, quadratic programs and many other convex problems in 20 to 60 Newton
steps. Karmarkar's algorithm started this line of work for linear programming <Cite id="karmarkar1984" />.

### Augmented Lagrangian

The **augmented Lagrangian** for equality constraints adds a quadratic penalty to the Lagrangian:

$$
\Lcal_\rho(\xvec, \nuvec) = f_0(\xvec) + \nuvec^\top\hvec(\xvec) + \frac{\rho}{2}\norm{\hvec(\xvec)}^2.
$$

The **method of multipliers** alternates $\xvec_{k+1} = \argmin_{\xvec}\Lcal_\rho(\xvec, \nuvec_k)$ with
$\nuvec_{k+1} = \nuvec_k + \rho\hvec(\xvec_{k+1})$ <Cite id="hestenes1969" />. The update comes from comparing
stationarity of $\Lcal_\rho$, $\nabla f_0 + \sum_j(\nu_j + \rho h_j)\nabla h_j = \zeros$, with the Lagrange
condition: $\nu_j + \rho h_j$ is the improved multiplier estimate. Because the multiplier absorbs the constraint force,
the penalty weight $\rho$ need not grow without bound, and the subproblems stay well conditioned.

### ADMM

The **alternating direction method of multipliers** applies the augmented Lagrangian to problems that split into two
parts, $\min f(\xvec) + g(\zvec)$ subject to $\Amat\xvec + \Bmat\zvec = \cvec$. It minimises over $\xvec$ and $\zvec$
one at a time instead of jointly, then updates the multiplier <Cite id="boyd2011" />:

$$
\xvec \leftarrow \argmin_{\xvec}\Lcal_\rho(\xvec, \zvec, \yvec), \qquad
\zvec \leftarrow \argmin_{\zvec}\Lcal_\rho(\xvec, \zvec, \yvec), \qquad
\yvec \leftarrow \yvec + \rho(\Amat\xvec + \Bmat\zvec - \cvec).
$$

For the lasso, $f = \tfrac12\norm{\Dmat\xvec - \bvec}^2$ and $g = \lambda\norm{\zvec}_1$ with the constraint
$\xvec - \zvec = \zeros$. The $\xvec$-update is a ridge regression and the $\zvec$-update is soft-thresholding (see
<NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">proximal gradient methods</NoteLink>). ADMM converges for convex problems
under mild conditions, often slowly to high accuracy but quickly to modest accuracy, and it distributes naturally across
machines when $f$ is a sum over data shards.

## Worked example

**Penalty and barrier.** Minimise $x$ subject to $x \ge 1$, so $p^* = 1$ with multiplier $\lambda^* = 1$.

- Quadratic penalty: $x + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\max(0, 1 - x)^2$ is minimised where $1 - \mu(1 - x) = 0$, at
  $x = 1 - 1/\mu$. For $\mu = 1, 10, 100$ the iterates are $0, 0.9, 0.99$: infeasible, approaching from outside, with
  $\mu(1 - x) = 1 = \lambda^*$.
- Log barrier: $tx - \log(x - 1)$ is minimised where $t - 1/(x - 1) = 0$, at $x = 1 + 1/t$. For $t = 1, 10, 100$ the
  iterates are $2, 1.1, 1.01$: strictly feasible, approaching from inside. The gap $f_0 - p^* = 1/t$ equals $m/t$ with
  $m = 1$, and $\lambda = 1/(t(x - 1)) = 1 = \lambda^*$ exactly.

**Augmented Lagrangian.** Minimise $\tfrac12 x^2$ subject to $x = 1$. The multiplier is $\nu^* = -1$. With $\rho = 1$
and $\nu_0 = 0$, the $x$-update is $x = (\rho - \nu)/(1 + \rho)$. The iterates are $x = 0.5, 0.75, 0.875, \dots$ with
$\nu = -0.5, -0.75, -0.875, \dots$: the error halves each iteration, with $\rho$ fixed at 1. The error factor is
$1/(1 + \rho)$, so $\rho = 10$ divides the error by 11 per iteration. The quadratic penalty with the same weight
$\mu = 1$ stops at $x = 0.5$ and needs $\mu \to \infty$ to converge.

## Cost

Each outer iteration is an unconstrained minimisation, usually a few Newton steps from a warm start. Interior-point
methods need 20 to 60 Newton steps in total; each Newton step solves a linear system of the size of the KKT system. ADMM
iterations are cheap when the two subproblems have closed forms, but can take hundreds of iterations.

## Trade-offs

- **Penalty:** simple and works from infeasible starts, but ill-conditioned for large $\mu$ and never exactly feasible.
- **Barrier:** always feasible and gives a duality gap bound, but needs a strictly feasible start, and cannot handle
  equality constraints by the barrier itself.
- **Augmented Lagrangian:** keeps $\rho$ bounded and converges to exact feasibility; the multiplier update adds an outer
  loop.
- **ADMM:** splits hard problems into easy pieces, at the cost of slow convergence to high accuracy and sensitivity to
  $\rho$.
