<Definition>
A **quadratic program** (QP) is

$$
\min_{\xvec \in \reals^n}\ \tfrac12\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec + \qvec^\top\xvec \quad \text{subject to} \quad
\Amat\xvec = \bvec,\ \ \Gmat\xvec \le \hvec,
$$

with $\Pmat$ symmetric. It is a **convex QP** when $\Pmat$ is positive semidefinite. A convex QP has a global minimum
characterised exactly by the <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">KKT conditions</NoteLink>. A QP with indefinite $\Pmat$ is
NP-hard in general <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A QP is the simplest step beyond a <NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear program</NoteLink>: a quadratic bowl
instead of a tilted plane, over the same kind of polyhedron. Unlike an LP, the minimum need not be at a vertex. It can
be in the interior (where the bowl's bottom is feasible), on a face or edge, or at a vertex. Which constraints are
active at the solution is the unknown that makes QPs harder than linear systems.

QPs appear throughout machine learning and control:

- the <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> dual, a QP with box constraints and one
  equality;
- the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink>, which becomes a QP when $\wvec$ is split into positive and negative parts;
- <NoteLink to="model-predictive-control">model predictive control</NoteLink> with linear dynamics, quadratic cost and
  input limits, which solves one QP per time step;
- Markowitz portfolio selection, which minimises the variance $\xvec^\top\Sigmamat\xvec$ of a portfolio subject to
  budget and return constraints.

## Equality constraints: one linear system

With only $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$, the Lagrangian is $\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec + \qvec^\top\xvec +
\nuvec^\top(\Amat\xvec - \bvec)$. Setting its gradient in $\xvec$ to zero and adding the constraint gives the
**KKT system**

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \Pmat & \Amat^\top \\ \Amat & \zeros \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \xvec \\ \nuvec \end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix} -\qvec \\ \bvec \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The KKT matrix is nonsingular when $\Amat$ has full row rank and $\Pmat$ is positive definite on the null space of
$\Amat$ ($\vvec^\top\Pmat\vvec > 0$ whenever $\Amat\vvec = \zeros$, $\vvec \ne \zeros$). Then the QP has a unique
solution, found by one linear solve. The matrix is symmetric but indefinite, so it is factorised with an $\Lmat\Dmat\Lmat^\top$
factorisation, or reduced by eliminating $\xvec$ when $\Pmat$ is invertible: $\xvec = -\Pmat^{-1}(\qvec +
\Amat^\top\nuvec)$ and $\Amat\Pmat^{-1}\Amat^\top\nuvec = -\bvec - \Amat\Pmat^{-1}\qvec$.

## Inequality constraints

### Active-set methods

If the set $\Wcal$ of constraints active at the solution were known, the QP would reduce to an equality-constrained QP
with the constraints in $\Wcal$ treated as equalities. An **active-set method** guesses $\Wcal$ and corrects the guess
<Cite id="nocedal2006" />:

1. Solve the equality-constrained QP with the current working set $\Wcal$.
2. If the solution violates a constraint outside $\Wcal$, move towards it only as far as feasibility allows and add
   the blocking constraint to $\Wcal$.
3. If the solution is feasible, check the multipliers of the inequality constraints in $\Wcal$. If all are
   nonnegative, KKT holds and the point is optimal. If one is negative, the objective improves by leaving that
   constraint, so remove it from $\Wcal$.

Each step changes $\Wcal$ by one constraint and updates a factorisation cheaply. Active-set methods are fast when the
active set changes little between related problems, as in model predictive control, where the previous solution is a
good warm start.

### Interior-point methods

Interior-point methods replace the inequalities by a logarithmic barrier and follow the minimiser as the barrier
weight shrinks (see <NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">penalty and barrier methods</NoteLink>). Each iteration
solves a KKT system like the one above with an extra diagonal term. They take a few tens of iterations regardless of
the number of constraints and scale to large sparse problems.

### Dual methods

For $\Pmat \succ 0$ the dual of a QP with inequality constraints $\Gmat\xvec \le \hvec$ has only the bounds
$\lambdavec \ge \zeros$ (derived in <NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">Lagrangian duality</NoteLink>). Bound-constrained
problems are easy for projected gradient and coordinate methods, which is how SVM solvers such as SMO work on the dual.

## Worked example

Minimise the variance of a portfolio of three independent assets with variances 1, 2 and 4, fully invested:
$\min \tfrac12\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec$ with $\Pmat = \diag(1, 2, 4)$ subject to $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1$.

Stationarity gives $p_i x_i + \nu = 0$, so $x_i = -\nu/p_i$: each weight is proportional to the inverse variance.
The constraint gives $-\nu(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14) = 1$, so $\nu = -4/7$ and $\xvec = (4/7, 2/7, 1/7)$. The objective
is $2/7$, so the portfolio variance $\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec$ is $4/7$, lower than that of any single asset.

Now add the inequality $x_1 \le 0.5$. The previous solution has $x_1 = 0.571$, which violates it, so the active-set
method adds it to the working set. With $x_1 = 0.5$ fixed, the remaining budget $0.5$ is again split in inverse
proportion to the variances 2 and 4: $x_2 = 1/3$ and $x_3 = 1/6$. Check the signs. The gradient $\Pmat\xvec =
(0.5, 2/3, 2/3)$ must equal $-\nu\ones - \mu\evec_1$ with $\mu \ge 0$ the multiplier of $x_1 - 0.5 \le 0$. The second
and third components give $\nu = -2/3$, and the first gives $\mu = 2/3 - 0.5 = 1/6 \ge 0$. KKT holds, so
$\xvec = (0.5, 1/3, 1/6)$ is optimal, with objective $0.292$. The positive multiplier says that loosening the cap would
lower the variance further.

## Pitfalls

- **Check convexity.** If $\Pmat$ has a negative eigenvalue on the feasible directions, KKT points can be saddle points
  or maxima, and local solvers return local solutions only.
- **Nearly singular $\Pmat$.** A positive semidefinite but singular $\Pmat$ can make the solution nonunique and the KKT
  matrix singular. A small ridge term $\epsilon\Imat$ restores uniqueness at a small bias.
- **Degenerate active sets.** Constraints that are active with zero multiplier make active-set methods cycle or stall.
- **Do not solve a QP with a general nonlinear solver** when a QP solver is available. QP solvers exploit the structure
  and certify optimality through the KKT residual.
