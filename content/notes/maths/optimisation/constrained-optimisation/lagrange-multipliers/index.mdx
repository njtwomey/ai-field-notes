<Definition>
Let $f, g_1, \dots, g_p : \reals^n \to \reals$ be continuously differentiable. If $\xvec^*$ is a local minimum or
maximum of $f$ subject to $g_j(\xvec) = 0$ for all $j$, and the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> $\nabla g_j(\xvec^*)$ are linearly
independent, then there are unique **Lagrange multipliers** $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p$ with

$$
\nabla f(\xvec^*) = \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j\nabla g_j(\xvec^*).
$$

Equivalently, $(\xvec^*, \lambdavec)$ is a stationary point of the **Lagrangian**
$\Lcal(\xvec, \lambdavec) = f(\xvec) - \sum_j \lambda_j g_j(\xvec)$ <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Picture the constraint $g(\xvec) = 0$ as a curve in the plane and the contours of $f$ as a family of curves. Moving
along the constraint curve crosses contours of $f$, and each crossing changes the value of $f$. At a point where the
constraint curve crosses a contour at an angle, moving one way along the constraint increases $f$ and the other way
decreases it, so the point is not optimal. At a constrained optimum the constraint curve must touch a contour of $f$
without crossing it. Two curves that touch share a <NoteLink to="derivative">tangent line</NoteLink>, so their normals are parallel. The normal of a contour
of $f$ is $\nabla f$, and the normal of the constraint curve is $\nabla g$. So $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$.

## Derivation

Let $\xvec^*$ be a local minimum of $f$ on the set $\Mcal = \set{\xvec : g_j(\xvec) = 0 \text{ for all } j}$, with
$\nabla g_1(\xvec^*), \dots, \nabla g_p(\xvec^*)$ linearly independent.

**Step 1: tangent directions.** Let $\xvec(t)$ be a differentiable curve in $\Mcal$ with $\xvec(0) = \xvec^*$ and
velocity $\vvec = \xvec'(0)$. Each $g_j(\xvec(t))$ is identically zero, so its derivative is zero:
$\nabla g_j(\xvec^*)^\top\vvec = 0$. Conversely, by the implicit function theorem (which needs the linear <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>
of the $\nabla g_j$), every $\vvec$ with $\nabla g_j^\top\vvec = 0$ for all $j$ is the velocity of such a curve. The
tangent space of $\Mcal$ at $\xvec^*$ is therefore $\Tcal = \set{\vvec : \nabla g_j(\xvec^*)^\top\vvec = 0 \text{ for all } j}$.

**Step 2: $\nabla f$ is orthogonal to the tangent space.** Along any such curve, $t = 0$ is a local minimum of
$f(\xvec(t))$. Its derivative at $t = 0$ vanishes: $\nabla f(\xvec^*)^\top\vvec = 0$ for every $\vvec \in \Tcal$.

**Step 3: orthogonal to $\Tcal$ means in the span of the constraint gradients.** $\Tcal$ is the orthogonal complement of
$\Span\set{\nabla g_1, \dots, \nabla g_p}$. The orthogonal complement of $\Tcal$ is therefore that span. So
$\nabla f(\xvec^*) = \sum_j \lambda_j\nabla g_j(\xvec^*)$ for some $\lambda_j$, which are unique because the
$\nabla g_j$ are linearly independent.

The Lagrangian packages the result. Its gradient in $\xvec$ is $\nabla f - \sum_j \lambda_j\nabla g_j$, and its
<NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivative</NoteLink> in $\lambda_j$ is $-g_j(\xvec)$. Setting all of them to zero gives the multiplier condition and the
constraints together: $n + p$ equations in $n + p$ unknowns.

## The multiplier is a sensitivity

Replace the constraint by $g(\xvec) = c$ and let $\xvec^*(c)$ be the solution, with optimal value
$f^*(c) = f(\xvec^*(c))$. Differentiate $g(\xvec^*(c)) = c$ in $c$: $\nabla g^\top\frac{d\xvec^*}{dc} = 1$. Then

$$
\frac{df^*}{dc} = \nabla f^\top\frac{d\xvec^*}{dc} = \lambda\nabla g^\top\frac{d\xvec^*}{dc} = \lambda.
$$

The multiplier is the marginal change in the optimal value per unit relaxation of the constraint. In economics it is
the **shadow price** of the constrained resource. With several constraints $g_j(\xvec) = c_j$, the same argument gives
$\partial f^*/\partial c_j = \lambda_j$.

## Worked examples

In the figures with a point to drag, the point stays on the constraint. At a point that is not stationary, the readout
$\lambda = \nabla f^\top\nabla g / \norm{\nabla g}^2$ is the least-squares fit of $\nabla f$ by a multiple of
$\nabla g$, and the residual $\norm{\nabla f - \lambda\nabla g}$ is the part of $\nabla f$ along the constraint. The
residual is zero exactly at the stationary points.

### Closest point on a line

Minimise $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + 2y = c$. This asks for the point on a line closest to the origin. The
Lagrangian is $\Lcal = x^2 + y^2 - \lambda(x + 2y - c)$. Its partial derivatives in $x$ and $y$ give $2x = \lambda$
and $2y = 2\lambda$, so $x = \lambda/2$ and $y = \lambda$. Substituting into the constraint gives
$\lambda/2 + 2\lambda = c$, so $\lambda = 2c/5$. The solution is $(x^*, y^*) = (c/5, 2c/5)$ with $f^*(c) = c^2/5$. At
$c = 5$ the solution is $(1, 2)$, with $f^* = 5$ and $\lambda = 2$. The solution is a multiple of the normal $(1, 2)$
of the line: the closest point lies on the perpendicular from the origin.

The sensitivity result holds here: $df^*/dc = 2c/5 = \lambda$. The multiplier is positive, because moving the line
away from the origin ($c$ larger) makes its closest point farther away. The problem is a minimum because $f$ is
convex and the constraint is linear, so $f$ restricted to the line is a <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> of one variable.

<ClosestPointOnLine />

The same steps solve the general **least-norm problem**: minimise $\norm{\xvec}^2$ subject to $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$,
where $\Amat$ is $p \times n$ with linearly independent rows. Stationarity of
$\norm{\xvec}^2 - \lambdavec^\top(\Amat\xvec - \bvec)$ gives $2\xvec = \Amat^\top\lambdavec$. The constraint then gives
$\Amat\Amat^\top\lambdavec / 2 = \bvec$, so $\lambdavec = 2(\Amat\Amat^\top)^{-1}\bvec$ and

$$
\xvec^* = \Amat^\top(\Amat\Amat^\top)^{-1}\bvec.
$$

This is the minimum-norm solution of an underdetermined linear system, the one that the
<NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink> returns. With $\Amat = (1 \;\; 2)$ and $\bvec = c$,
$\Amat\Amat^\top = 5$ and $\xvec^* = c(1, 2)/5$, as above.

### Quadratic objective on a circle

Minimise and maximise $f(x, y) = (x - 2)^2 + 2(y - 1)^2$ subject to $g(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0$. The explorer in
the Intuition section draws this problem. The stationarity equations are $2(x - 2) = 2\lambda x$ and
$4(y - 1) = 2\lambda y$. The first has no solution when $\lambda = 1$ and the second none when $\lambda = 2$. Otherwise
they give $x = 2/(1 - \lambda)$ and $y = 2/(2 - \lambda)$. Substituting into the constraint gives

$$
\frac{4}{(1 - \lambda)^2} + \frac{4}{(2 - \lambda)^2} = 1,
$$

a quartic in $\lambda$ after clearing denominators. It has two real roots:

| $\lambda$ | $(x, y)$           | $f$    | type    |
| --------- | ------------------ | ------ | ------- |
| $-1.453$  | $(0.815, 0.579)$   | $1.758$  | minimum |
| $4.453$   | $(-0.579, -0.815)$ | $13.242$ | maximum |

The circle is closed and bounded, so $f$ attains a minimum and a maximum on it, and both must be among the solutions.
Comparing the values of $f$ identifies them. The negative multiplier at the minimum says that enlarging the circle
(raising $c$ in $x^2 + y^2 - 1 = c$) lowers the minimum, because the circle moves towards the unconstrained minimum at
$(2, 1)$. The positive multiplier at the maximum says that enlarging the circle raises the maximum.

### Maximise a product under a sum constraint

Maximise $f(x, y) = xy$ subject to $x + y = c$. For a rectangle with sides $x$ and $y$ and perimeter $2c$, $f$ is the
area. The Lagrangian is $\Lcal = xy - \lambda(x + y - c)$. Stationarity gives $y = \lambda$ and $x = \lambda$, so
$x = y$. The constraint then gives $x^* = y^* = c/2$ and $\lambda = c/2$, with $f^*(c) = c^2/4$. The largest rectangle
of a given perimeter is a square. The point is a maximum: along the line, with $x = c/2 + s$ and $y = c/2 - s$,
$f = c^2/4 - s^2$.

The multiplier is the **shadow price** of the perimeter: $df^*/dc = c/2 = \lambda$. Raising the half-perimeter by one unit
raises the maximum area by approximately $c/2$.

The same problem appears in economics as a consumer maximising the utility $u(x, y) = xy$ of two goods with prices
$p_x$ and $p_y$ and a budget $m$, so that $p_x x + p_y y = m$. Stationarity gives $y = \lambda p_x$ and
$x = \lambda p_y$. The budget then gives $\lambda = m/(2p_xp_y)$, $x^* = m/(2p_x)$ and $y^* = m/(2p_y)$. The optimal
utility is $u^*(m) = m^2/(4p_xp_y)$, and $du^*/dm = m/(2p_xp_y) = \lambda$. The multiplier is the marginal utility of
money.

### Can with least surface area

A closed cylindrical can with radius $r$ and height $h$ has surface area $A(r, h) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh$ and volume
$\pi r^2h$. Minimise $A$ subject to $\pi r^2h = V$. The Lagrangian is
$\Lcal = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh - \lambda(\pi r^2h - V)$. Stationarity gives

$$
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial r} = 4\pi r + 2\pi h - 2\lambda\pi rh = 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial h} = 2\pi r - \lambda\pi r^2 = 0.
$$

The second equation gives $\lambda = 2/r$. Substituting into the first gives $4\pi r + 2\pi h = 4\pi h$, so $h = 2r$:
the height equals the diameter. The volume constraint $2\pi r^3 = V$ then gives $r^* = (V/2\pi)^{1/3}$ and
$A^* = 6\pi r^{*2}$. Along the constraint, $h = V/(\pi r^2)$ and $A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2V/r$, whose second derivative
$4\pi + 4V/r^3$ is positive, so the point is a minimum.

The multiplier $\lambda = 2/r^*$ is positive: a larger volume needs more material. As a check,
$A^*(V) = 6\pi r^{*2}$ with $V = 2\pi r^{*3}$ has derivative $12\pi r^* \cdot dr^*/dV = 12\pi r^*/(6\pi r^{*2}) = 2/r^*$. For
$V = 2\pi$ the optimal can has $r^* = 1$, $h^* = 2$, $A^* = 6\pi \approx 18.85$ and $\lambda = 2$.

### Rayleigh quotient and eigenvectors

Let $\Amat$ be a symmetric $n \times n$ matrix. Maximise $f(\xvec) = \xvec^\top\Amat\xvec$ subject to
$\xvec^\top\xvec = 1$. The constraint gradient $2\xvec$ is nonzero on the unit sphere, so every point is regular. The
Lagrangian is $\Lcal = \xvec^\top\Amat\xvec - \lambda(\xvec^\top\xvec - 1)$, and its gradient in $\xvec$ is
$2\Amat\xvec - 2\lambda\xvec$. Stationarity is therefore

$$
\Amat\xvec = \lambda\xvec.
$$

The stationary points are the unit eigenvectors of $\Amat$, and the multiplier at each one is its eigenvalue. The value
there is $f = \xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \lambda\xvec^\top\xvec = \lambda$. The sphere is closed and bounded, so the maximum
and minimum exist and are attained at stationary points. The maximum of $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec$ on the unit sphere is
therefore the largest eigenvalue, and the minimum is the smallest <Cite id="horn2012" />. The argument also proves that
every real symmetric matrix has a real eigenvalue with a real eigenvector. The ratio
$\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec / \xvec^\top\xvec$ is the **Rayleigh quotient**; it equals $f$ on the sphere.

In $n$ dimensions with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \dots > \lambda_n$ there are $2n$ stationary points
$\pm\vvec_i$. Only $\pm\vvec_1$ are maxima and only $\pm\vvec_n$ are minima. At $\vvec_i$ with $1 < i < n$, moving
along the sphere towards $\vvec_1$ increases $f$ and moving towards $\vvec_n$ decreases it, so the point is a saddle of
$f$ on the sphere. The second-order test below confirms this: the Hessian of the Lagrangian is
$2(\Amat - \lambda_i\eye)$, and on the tangent space (vectors orthogonal to $\vvec_i$) its eigenvalues are
$2(\lambda_j - \lambda_i)$ for $j \ne i$, which take both signs. In two dimensions only the maximum and the minimum
exist.

The same problem defines <NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">principal component analysis</NoteLink>. The
variance of data projected onto a unit vector $\wvec$ is $\wvec^\top\Smat\wvec$, where $\Smat$ is the sample
covariance matrix. The maximising direction is the top eigenvector of $\Smat$, and the multiplier is the variance
captured.

### Minimum-variance portfolio

An investor splits one unit of wealth across $n$ assets with weights $\wvec$, so $\ones^\top\wvec = 1$. The asset
returns have covariance matrix $\Sigmamat$, assumed positive definite. The portfolio's variance is
$\wvec^\top\Sigmamat\wvec$ <Cite id="markowitz1952" />. Minimise it subject to the budget. The Lagrangian is
$\Lcal = \wvec^\top\Sigmamat\wvec - \lambda(\ones^\top\wvec - 1)$, and stationarity gives
$2\Sigmamat\wvec = \lambda\ones$, so $\wvec = (\lambda/2)\Sigmamat^{-1}\ones$. The budget gives
$(\lambda/2)\,\ones^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\ones = 1$, so

$$
\wvec^* = \frac{\Sigmamat^{-1}\ones}{\ones^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\ones}, \qquad
\sigma^{*2} = \wvec^{*\top}\Sigmamat\wvec^* = \frac{1}{\ones^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\ones}, \qquad
\lambda = 2\sigma^{*2}.
$$

The objective is strictly convex and the constraint is linear, so this stationary point is the global minimum. For
a budget $c$ instead of 1, the solution scales to $c\wvec^*$ with variance $c^2\sigma^{*2}$, whose derivative at
$c = 1$ is $2\sigma^{*2} = \lambda$.

For two assets with volatilities $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and correlation $\rho$, the first weight is

$$
w_1^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}.
$$

With $\sigma_1 = 0.2$, $\sigma_2 = 0.3$ and $\rho = 0.2$, $\wvec^* = (0.736, 0.264)$ and $\sigma^* = 0.181$, below
either asset's volatility. When $\sigma_1 < \sigma_2$ and $\rho > \sigma_1/\sigma_2$, $w_2^* < 0$: the minimum-variance
portfolio sells the riskier asset short. A ban on short positions adds the inequalities $\wvec \ge \zeros$,
which need the <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions</NoteLink>.

### Maximum likelihood of a categorical distribution

Observe counts $n_1, \dots, n_K$ of $K$ categories, with $N = \sum_i n_i$. The log-likelihood of a
<NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical distribution</NoteLink> with probabilities $\pvec$ is
$\ell(\pvec) = \sum_i n_i\log p_i$. Maximise it subject to $\sum_i p_i = 1$. The Lagrangian is
$\Lcal = \sum_i n_i\log p_i - \lambda(\sum_i p_i - 1)$. Its partial derivative in $p_i$ is $n_i/p_i - \lambda$, so
$p_i = n_i/\lambda$. Summing over $i$ gives $N/\lambda = 1$, so

$$
\lambda = N, \qquad \hat p_i = \frac{n_i}{N}.
$$

The maximum-likelihood estimate is the vector of observed frequencies. $\ell$ is concave and the constraint is linear,
so the stationary point is the global maximum. The multiplier $N$ is a sensitivity: under $\sum_i p_i = c$ the
solution is $p_i = cn_i/N$ with $\ell^*(c) = \sum_i n_i\log(n_i/N) + N\log c$, whose derivative at $c = 1$ is $N$.

The derivation ignores the constraints $p_i \ge 0$. They are inactive when every $n_i > 0$, because $\ell \to -\infty$
as any $p_i \to 0$. A category with $n_i = 0$ has $\hat p_i = 0$, a point on the boundary of the simplex where the
inequality is active.

### Maximum entropy with a known mean

A die has faces $k = 1, \dots, 6$. Of all distributions $\pvec$ on the faces with mean $\mu$, find the one with the
largest <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> $H(\pvec) = -\sum_k p_k\log p_k$ <Cite id="jaynes1957" />. There are two constraints,
$\sum_k p_k = 1$ and $\sum_k kp_k = \mu$, and so two multipliers. The Lagrangian is

$$
\Lcal = -\sum_k p_k\log p_k - \lambda_0\Big(\sum_k p_k - 1\Big) - \lambda_1\Big(\sum_k kp_k - \mu\Big).
$$

Its partial derivative in $p_k$ is $-\log p_k - 1 - \lambda_0 - \lambda_1k$. Setting it to zero gives
$p_k = \exp(-1 - \lambda_0 - \lambda_1k)$. Normalisation fixes $\lambda_0$: with
$Z(\lambda_1) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda_1k}$, $e^{1 + \lambda_0} = Z$ and

$$
p_k = \frac{e^{-\lambda_1k}}{Z(\lambda_1)}.
$$

This is the Gibbs form, a member of the <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> with
<NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink> $k$ <Cite id="cover2006" />. The mean constraint fixes $\lambda_1$ through
$-d\log Z/d\lambda_1 = \sum_k kp_k = \mu$. The mean falls as $\lambda_1$ rises, because its derivative in $\lambda_1$ is
minus the variance of $k$. The equation therefore has one root, found by a one-dimensional search; there is no closed
form for six faces. Every $p_k$ is positive, so the constraints $p_k \ge 0$ are inactive. $H$ is strictly concave and
the constraints are linear, so the stationary point is the unique maximum.

The maximum entropy is $H^* = \sum_k p_k(\lambda_1k + \log Z) = \lambda_1\mu + \log Z$. Its derivative in $\mu$ is
$(\mu + d\log Z/d\lambda_1)\,d\lambda_1/d\mu + \lambda_1 = \lambda_1$, the multiplier of the mean constraint. At
$\mu = 3.5$, $\lambda_1 = 0$, the distribution is uniform and $H^* = \log 6 = 1.792$ nats. For $\mu = 4.5$,
$\lambda_1 = -0.371$, $\pvec = (0.054, 0.079, 0.114, 0.165, 0.240, 0.347)$ and $H^* = 1.614$ nats. The same
construction gives the other <NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-distributions">maximum entropy distributions</NoteLink>, and
with densities in place of vectors it becomes a
<NoteLink to="constrained-variational-problems">constrained variational problem</NoteLink>.

### Two constraints: closest point on a circle in space

The plane $\ones^\top\xvec = 1$ meets the unit sphere $\xvec^\top\xvec = 1$ in $\reals^3$ in a circle. Find the point of
that circle closest to a given point $\avec$: minimise $f(\xvec) = \norm{\xvec - \avec}^2$ subject to
$g_1(\xvec) = \ones^\top\xvec - 1 = 0$ and $g_2(\xvec) = \xvec^\top\xvec - 1 = 0$. The constraint gradients $\ones$
and $2\xvec$ are linearly independent unless $\xvec$ is a multiple of $\ones$. The only multiple of $\ones$ in the
plane is $\tfrac13\ones$, whose norm is $1/\sqrt3 \ne 1$, so every point of the circle is regular.

The multiplier condition is $2(\xvec - \avec) = \lambda_1\ones + 2\lambda_2\xvec$: $\nabla f$ lies in the plane
spanned by the two constraint normals. Rearranging gives

$$
(1 - \lambda_2)\,\xvec = \avec + \frac{\lambda_1}{2}\ones,
$$

so the solution lies in the span of $\avec$ and $\ones$. Geometrically, the circle has centre $\cvec = \tfrac13\ones$ and
radius $\rho = \sqrt{2/3}$, because $\norm{\xvec}^2 = \norm{\cvec}^2 + \norm{\xvec - \cvec}^2$ for $\xvec$ in the
plane and $\norm{\cvec}^2 = 1/3$. Let $\avec_\parallel = \avec - \frac{\ones^\top\avec - 1}{3}\ones$ be the projection
of $\avec$ onto the plane. The stationary points are

$$
\xvec_\pm = \cvec \pm \rho\,\frac{\avec_\parallel - \cvec}{\norm{\avec_\parallel - \cvec}},
$$

the closest ($+$) and the farthest ($-$). They are defined when $\avec_\parallel \ne \cvec$; otherwise every point of
the circle is equally far from $\avec$.

For $\avec = (2, 1, 0)$: $\ones^\top\avec = 3$, $\avec_\parallel = (4/3, 1/3, -2/3)$ and
$\avec_\parallel - \cvec = (1, 0, -1)$. The closest point is
$\xvec_+ = (1/3 + 1/\sqrt3,\ 1/3,\ 1/3 - 1/\sqrt3) \approx (0.911, 0.333, -0.244)$ with $f^* = 1.691$. Its second
coordinate gives $-4/3 = \lambda_1 + 2\lambda_2/3$, and its first gives
$2(1/\sqrt3 - 5/3) = \lambda_1 + 2\lambda_2(1/3 + 1/\sqrt3)$. Subtracting, $\lambda_2 = 1 - \sqrt3 \approx -0.732$
and $\lambda_1 = -2 + 2/\sqrt3 \approx -0.845$. Both are negative. Raising the plane's level towards $\avec$
($\ones^\top\avec = 3 > 1$) or enlarging the sphere towards $\avec$ ($\norm{\avec}^2 = 5 > 1$) reduces the distance.

The figure shows the plane face on, in coordinates $(s_1, s_2)$ along two orthonormal vectors of the plane. Within the
plane, the sphere constraint is the circle $s_1^2 + s_2^2 = 2/3$. The part of $\nabla f$ along the plane's normal
$\ones$ is always absorbed by $\lambda_1$, so the in-plane picture is again a single constraint, and its multiplier is
$\lambda_2$.

### Several solutions of the Lagrange equations

Find the extremes of $f(x, y) = x^2y$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 3$. Stationarity of
$\Lcal = x^2y - \lambda(x^2 + y^2 - 3)$ gives

$$
2xy = 2\lambda x, \qquad x^2 = 2\lambda y.
$$

The first equation holds when $x = 0$ or when $y = \lambda$. Dividing it by $x$ would lose the first case.

- **$x = 0$.** The constraint gives $y = \pm\sqrt3$, and the second equation gives $\lambda = 0$. Here $f = 0$.
- **$y = \lambda$.** The second equation gives $x^2 = 2y^2$, and the constraint gives $3y^2 = 3$, so $y = \pm1$ and
  $x = \pm\sqrt2$. Here $f = 2y$: $f = 2$ at $(\pm\sqrt2, 1)$ with $\lambda = 1$, and $f = -2$ at $(\pm\sqrt2, -1)$
  with $\lambda = -1$.

The equations have six solutions. The circle is closed and bounded, so the global maximum and minimum are among them,
and comparing values gives the maximum $2$ and the minimum $-2$. The points $(0, \pm\sqrt3)$ are local extrema of $f$
along the circle but not global ones. Near $(0, \sqrt3)$, $y > 0$, so $f = x^2y \ge 0$ and the point is a local
minimum with value $0$. At both of these points $\lambda = 0$ because $\nabla f = \zeros$ there: they are
unconstrained stationary points of $f$ that happen to lie on the circle.

### Second-order test: when a convex objective has a constrained maximum

Find the extremes of $f(x, y) = x^2 + ay^2$ on the unit circle, for a constant $a \ne 1$. Stationarity of
$\Lcal = x^2 + ay^2 - \lambda(x^2 + y^2 - 1)$ gives $2x = 2\lambda x$ and $2ay = 2\lambda y$. The solutions are
$(\pm1, 0)$ with $\lambda = 1$ and $f = 1$, and $(0, \pm1)$ with $\lambda = a$ and $f = a$. The Hessian of the
Lagrangian is $\nabla^2\Lcal = \mathrm{diag}(2 - 2\lambda,\ 2a - 2\lambda)$. At $(\pm1, 0)$ the tangent direction is
$\vvec = (0, 1)$, and $\vvec^\top\nabla^2\Lcal\,\vvec = 2(a - 1)$. At $(0, \pm1)$ the tangent direction is
$\vvec = (1, 0)$, and $\vvec^\top\nabla^2\Lcal\,\vvec = 2(1 - a)$.

For $0 < a < 1$, $f$ is convex (its Hessian $\mathrm{diag}(2, 2a)$ is positive definite), yet $(\pm1, 0)$ is a
constrained maximum, because $2(a - 1) < 0$. The Hessian of $f$ alone would call it a minimum. The term
$-\lambda\nabla^2 g = -2\lambda\eye$ subtracts the curvature of the circle. At $(\pm1, 0)$ the circle has curvature
$1$ and the level set $x^2 + ay^2 = 1$ has curvature $a < 1$, so the circle stays inside that level set, where $f < 1$.
For $a > 1$ the roles swap. At $a = 1$, $f = 1$ everywhere on the circle, every point is
stationary, and the second-order quantity is zero.

A stationary point can be neither a minimum nor a maximum. Minimise $f(x, y) = y$ subject to $y = x^3$. At the origin
$\nabla f = (0, 1) = \nabla g$ for $g = y - x^3$, so $\lambda = 1$. The Hessian of the Lagrangian is
$\nabla^2\Lcal = -\lambda\nabla^2 g = \mathrm{diag}(6x, 0)$, which vanishes at the origin, so the test is
inconclusive. Along the curve $f = x^3$, which has an inflection at $0$: the point is not an extremum.

### Regularity fails at a cusp

Minimise $f(x, y) = x$ subject to $g(x, y) = x^3 - y^2 = 0$. The constraint forces $x = y^{2/3} \ge 0$, so the minimum
is $x = 0$, at the origin. The Lagrange equations are $1 = 3\lambda x^2$ and $0 = -2\lambda y$. The first needs
$\lambda \ne 0$, so the second gives $y = 0$, the constraint gives $x = 0$, and the first becomes $1 = 0$. The
equations have no solution, and the method misses the minimum.

The theorem does not apply because $\nabla g = (3x^2, -2y)$ is zero at the origin: the curve has a cusp there and no
normal line. Along the curve $(t^2, t^3)$ near the cusp, $\nabla g = (3t^4, -2t^3)$ and the best-fitting multiplier
$\nabla f^\top\nabla g / \norm{\nabla g}^2 = 3/(t^2(9t^2 + 4))$ grows without bound as $t \to 0$. A complete search
therefore adds every feasible point where the constraint gradients are linearly dependent to the solutions of the
Lagrange equations.

## Second-order conditions

The multiplier condition is necessary, not sufficient. It holds at constrained minima, maxima and saddle points alike.
The second derivative of $f$ along the constraint decides between them. Let $\xvec(t)$ be a curve in $\Mcal$ with
$\xvec(0) = \xvec^*$ and $\xvec'(0) = \vvec$, for one constraint $g$. Then

$$
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}f(\xvec(t))\Big|_{t=0} = \vvec^\top\nabla^2 f\,\vvec + \nabla f^\top\xvec''(0).
$$

Differentiating $g(\xvec(t)) = 0$ twice gives $\vvec^\top\nabla^2 g\,\vvec + \nabla g^\top\xvec''(0) = 0$. With
$\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$, the last term is $\lambda\nabla g^\top\xvec''(0) = -\lambda\vvec^\top\nabla^2 g\,\vvec$,
so

$$
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}f(\xvec(t))\Big|_{t=0} = \vvec^\top\big(\nabla^2 f - \lambda\nabla^2 g\big)\vvec
= \vvec^\top\nabla^2_{\xvec\xvec}\Lcal\,\vvec.
$$

The <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> of the Lagrangian, restricted to the tangent space, is the curvature of
$f$ along the constraint. If $\vvec^\top\nabla^2_{\xvec\xvec}\Lcal(\xvec^*, \lambdavec)\vvec > 0$ for every nonzero
$\vvec \in \Tcal$, then $\xvec^*$ is a strict local minimum; with several constraints the curvature term is
$-\sum_j\lambda_j\nabla^2 g_j$ <Cite id="nocedal2006" />. The Hessian of $f$ alone is not the right matrix.

In small problems the test is often stated through the **bordered Hessian**, the Hessian of $\Lcal$ in
$(\lambda, \xvec)$ jointly. Its border holds $-\nabla g$; flipping the border's sign multiplies one row and one column
by $-1$ and leaves the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> unchanged. For two variables and one constraint,

$$
\bar\Hmat = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & g_x & g_y \\ g_x & \Lcal_{xx} & \Lcal_{xy} \\ g_y & \Lcal_{xy} & \Lcal_{yy} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det\bar\Hmat = -\vvec^\top\nabla^2_{\xvec\xvec}\Lcal\,\vvec \text{ with } \vvec = (-g_y, g_x).
$$

The identity follows by expanding the determinant along its first row. The vector $\vvec$ is tangent to the
constraint, so $\det\bar\Hmat < 0$ means a strict local minimum and $\det\bar\Hmat > 0$ a strict local maximum.

## Assumptions

- **Regularity.** The constraint gradients must be linearly independent at $\xvec^*$. Without it the theorem can fail,
  as at the cusp of $x^3 = y^2$ in the worked examples, where $\nabla g = \zeros$ at the minimum and no multiplier
  exists.
- **Equality constraints only.** Inequality constraints need signs on the multipliers and complementary slackness; see
  <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">KKT conditions</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Sign conventions.** Writing $\Lcal = f + \lambda g$ instead of $f - \lambda g$ flips the sign of $\lambda$. The
  sensitivity interpretation changes sign with it.
- **Solving the equations finds all stationary points.** Compare the value of $f$ at every solution, and check the
  boundary if the constraint set is not closed and bounded. Do not divide an equation by a variable that can be zero:
  in $2xy = 2\lambda x$, dividing by $x$ loses the solutions with $x = 0$.
- **Points where regularity fails.** Add the feasible points where the constraint gradients are linearly dependent to
  the candidates; the Lagrange equations never find them.
- **Functions as unknowns.** The same argument works when the unknown is a function, with a constraint such as
  normalisation of a density. See <NoteLink to="constrained-variational-problems">constrained variational problems</NoteLink>
  and <NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-distributions">maximum entropy distributions</NoteLink>.
