<Definition>
The **Nelder–Mead method** minimises $f : \reals^n \to \reals$ by maintaining a simplex of $n + 1$ points
$\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_{n+1}$ ordered so that $f(\xvec_1) \le \dots \le f(\xvec_{n+1})$. Each iteration computes the
centroid $\bar\xvec$ of the best $n$ points and tries points on the line from the worst point $\xvec_{n+1}$ through
$\bar\xvec$. It replaces $\xvec_{n+1}$ with a better point on that line, or else shrinks the whole simplex towards
$\xvec_1$ <Cite id="nelder1965" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Some objectives have no usable <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. The function may be a simulation, a physical experiment or a black-box
program; it may be noisy or have kinks. Finite-difference gradients cost $n$ extra evaluations each and fail on noisy or
nonsmooth functions. Nelder–Mead needs only comparisons between function values, and usually one or two evaluations
per iteration.

The name "simplex" refers to the geometric shape (a triangle in two dimensions, a tetrahedron in three). The method is
unrelated to the simplex method of <NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear programming</NoteLink>.

## Mechanism

With the standard coefficients (reflection 1, expansion 2, contraction $\tfrac12$, shrink $\tfrac12$), one iteration
is <Cite id="nocedal2006" />:

1. **Order** the vertices by $f$ and compute the centroid $\bar\xvec = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\xvec_i$ of all but the
   worst.
2. **Reflect**: $\xvec_r = \bar\xvec + (\bar\xvec - \xvec_{n+1})$. If $f(\xvec_1) \le f(\xvec_r) < f(\xvec_n)$,
   replace the worst point with $\xvec_r$ and stop the iteration.
3. **Expand**: if $f(\xvec_r) < f(\xvec_1)$, the direction is promising. Try $\xvec_e = \bar\xvec + 2(\bar\xvec -
   \xvec_{n+1})$ and keep the better of $\xvec_e$ and $\xvec_r$.
4. **Contract**: if $f(\xvec_r) \ge f(\xvec_n)$, the reflected point is poor. Try a point halfway between $\bar\xvec$
   and the better of $\xvec_r$ and $\xvec_{n+1}$. Accept it if it improves on that point.
5. **Shrink**: if contraction fails, move every vertex halfway towards the best, $\xvec_i \leftarrow \xvec_1 +
   \tfrac12(\xvec_i - \xvec_1)$.

The reflection moves the simplex away from high values, like a crude gradient step. Expansion lets the simplex grow and
accelerate along a descending valley. Contraction and shrinking make it smaller, so it can settle into a minimum. The
simplex adapts its shape to the local level sets: long along a valley, narrow across it.

The method stops when the simplex is small or the spread of function values across its vertices falls below a
tolerance.

## Worked example

Minimise $f(x, y) = (x - 1)^2 + 4(y - 2)^2$ from the simplex $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, with values 17, 16 and 5.

**Iteration 1.** The worst vertex is $(0, 0)$. The centroid of the other two is $(0.5, 0.5)$. The reflected point is
$(1, 1)$ with $f = 4$, better than the best vertex (5), so try expanding: $(1.5, 1.5)$ with $f = 0.25 + 1 = 1.25$. The
expansion is better still and replaces $(0, 0)$. The simplex is now $(1.5, 1.5)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 0)$ with values
1.25, 5 and 16.

**Iteration 2.** The worst vertex is $(1, 0)$. The centroid of the other two is $(0.75, 1.25)$. The reflected point is
$(0.5, 2.5)$ with $f = 0.25 + 1 = 1.25$. It ties with the best vertex and is better than the second worst (5), so it is
accepted without trying an expansion. The simplex is now $(1.5, 1.5)$, $(0.5, 2.5)$, $(0, 1)$.

Two iterations reduced the worst value in the simplex from 17 to 5 with three function evaluations. On the Rosenbrock
function in the figure, from $(-1.2, 1)$, the best vertex reaches $f < 10^{-12}$ in about 100 iterations.

## Cost

One iteration evaluates $f$ once or twice in the usual case (reflection, possibly expansion or contraction) and $n$
times after a shrink. Memory is $n + 1$ points. There is no linear algebra beyond vector averages.

## Trade-offs

- **No convergence theory in general.** In one dimension the method converges for strictly <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex functions</NoteLink>, and in two
  dimensions it has some guarantees <Cite id="lagarias1998" />. McKinnon constructed a smooth strictly convex function
  in two dimensions on which the simplex collapses and converges to a point that is not a minimiser
  <Cite id="mckinnon1998" />. Restarting from the returned point with a fresh simplex usually recovers.
- **Poor in high dimension.** Performance degrades quickly beyond about 10 variables. The simplex tends to degenerate
  into a lower-dimensional shape, and progress per evaluation falls.
- **Robust to kinks and mild noise.** Using only rankings of function values makes it insensitive to monotone
  transformations of $f$ and to small discontinuities.
- **Alternatives.** For expensive evaluations in a few dimensions, Bayesian optimisation (see
  <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink>) makes better use of each evaluation. For
  moderate dimensions and noisy or rugged objectives, <NoteLink to="evolution-strategies-and-covariance-matrix-adaptation">CMA-ES</NoteLink>
  is more reliable.
