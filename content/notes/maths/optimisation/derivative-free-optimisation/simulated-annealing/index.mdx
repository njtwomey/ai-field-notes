<Definition>
**Simulated annealing** minimises $f$ over a set $\Xcal$, discrete or continuous, using a proposal distribution
$q(\xvec' \mid \xvec)$ and a decreasing sequence of temperatures $T_k > 0$. At step $k$ it proposes $\xvec' \sim
q(\cdot \mid \xvec_k)$, computes $\Delta = f(\xvec') - f(\xvec_k)$, and moves to $\xvec'$ with probability
$\min(1, e^{-\Delta/T_k})$; otherwise it stays <Cite id="kirkpatrick1983" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Local search that only accepts improvements stops at the first local minimum. Many objectives, especially combinatorial
ones such as the travelling salesman problem, graph partitioning or circuit placement, have enormous numbers of local
minima and no <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>. Random restarts help little when the number of basins is exponential. Simulated annealing
lets the search climb out of basins, often early and rarely late.

The name comes from metallurgy. Cooling a metal slowly lets its atoms settle into a low-energy crystal. Cooling it
quickly freezes defects in place. The algorithm treats $f$ as energy and $T$ as temperature. Kirkpatrick drew
the method from the physics of <NoteLink to="spin-glass">spin glasses</NoteLink>, whose rugged energies have many local
minima, and the <NoteLink to="boltzmann-machine">Boltzmann machine</NoteLink> used it to bring its units to
equilibrium.

## Mechanism

### The Metropolis rule samples the Boltzmann distribution

At a fixed temperature $T$, the acceptance rule is the <NoteLink to="metropolis-hastings">Metropolis</NoteLink> rule for
the target density

$$
\pi_T(\xvec) \propto e^{-f(\xvec)/T},
$$

the **Boltzmann distribution** <Cite id="metropolis1953" />. To check this, take a symmetric proposal,
$q(\xvec' \mid \xvec) = q(\xvec \mid \xvec')$, and a pair with $f(\xvec') > f(\xvec)$. The probability flow from
$\xvec$ to $\xvec'$ is $\pi_T(\xvec)\,q\,e^{-(f(\xvec') - f(\xvec))/T}$, and from $\xvec'$ back to $\xvec$ it is
$\pi_T(\xvec')\,q \cdot 1$. Both equal $q\,e^{-f(\xvec')/T}$ up to the same normalising constant. This **<NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">detailed
balance</NoteLink>** makes $\pi_T$ a stationary distribution of the chain.

### Low temperature concentrates on the global minima

The ratio of the Boltzmann probabilities of any point $\xvec$ and a global minimiser $\xvec^*$ is

$$
\frac{\pi_T(\xvec)}{\pi_T(\xvec^*)} = e^{-(f(\xvec) - f^*)/T}.
$$

For any $\xvec$ with $f(\xvec) > f^*$, the ratio tends to zero as $T \to 0$. On a finite set, $\pi_T$ therefore converges
to the uniform distribution over the global minimisers. At high temperature $\pi_T$ is nearly uniform and the walk
explores freely. At low temperature it is concentrated but the walk moves slowly, because escaping a basin of depth $d$
requires an uphill run whose probability scales like $e^{-d/T}$. Annealing starts hot, to explore, and cools, to
concentrate, hoping to stay close to $\pi_T$ throughout.

### Cooling schedules

The schedule decides whether the chain can track $\pi_T$ as it changes.

- **Logarithmic:** $T_k = c/\log(k + 2)$. For $c$ large enough the probability of being at a global minimiser tends to
  1 <Cite id="geman1984" />. On a finite set, the exact condition is that $c$ is at least the depth of the deepest local
  minimum that is not global, the height of the barrier that must be climbed to leave it <Cite id="hajek1988" />. The
  schedule is so slow that it is a theoretical guarantee rather than a practical recipe.
- **Geometric:** $T_{k+1} = \alpha T_k$ with $\alpha$ between 0.8 and 0.99, holding each temperature for a number of
  proposals proportional to the neighbourhood size <Cite id="kirkpatrick1983" />. This is the common practical choice.
  It carries no guarantee of reaching the global minimum.

The initial temperature is set so that most uphill proposals are accepted, for example $T_0$ comparable to a typical
$\Delta$. The final temperature is set so that almost none are.

## Worked example

**Acceptance probabilities.** An uphill move with $\Delta = 1$ is accepted with probability $e^{-1} = 0.368$ at
$T = 1$, $e^{-2} = 0.135$ at $T = 0.5$ and $e^{-10} = 4.5\times10^{-5}$ at $T = 0.1$. Lowering the temperature tenfold
makes the same uphill move about 8000 times less likely.

**Two minima.** Let $\Xcal$ have two local minima with $f = 0$ (global) and $f = 0.5$ (local), and ignore the other
states. Their Boltzmann probability ratio is $e^{-0.5/T}$: $0.61$ at $T = 1$ and $e^{-5} = 0.0067$ at $T = 0.1$. At high
temperature both are visited almost equally. At low temperature the global minimum dominates, provided the chain
has had time to cross between them. If the barrier between the basins has height $d = 2$, the rate of crossing at
$T = 0.1$ scales like $e^{-20} \approx 2\times10^{-9}$ per attempt. A chain that cooled to $0.1$ while in the wrong
basin will not leave it.

## Cost

Each step costs one proposal and one evaluation of $f$, often only of the change $\Delta$, which for local moves in
combinatorial problems can be computed in constant time. Runs need many steps, typically $10^5$ to $10^8$.

## Trade-offs

- **Generality.** It needs only a proposal and a way to compute $\Delta$, and applies to discrete, continuous and mixed
  spaces.
- **Tuning.** The initial temperature, cooling rate, time per temperature and proposal size all matter, and poor
  choices either waste time or freeze too early.
- **Guarantees are asymptotic.** The global convergence result holds only for the logarithmic schedule and infinite
  time. In practice simulated annealing is a heuristic that usually finds good, not optimal, solutions.
- **Relatives.** Parallel tempering runs chains at several temperatures and swaps states between them, keeping exploration
  alive without a schedule. For continuous problems with gradients, <NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin
  dynamics</NoteLink> adds noise of temperature $T$ to <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> and has the same Boltzmann stationary
  distribution; as $T \to 0$ it becomes plain gradient descent.
