<Definition>
Every matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ can be written as

$$
\Amat = \Umat \Sigmamat \Vmat^\top,
$$

where $\Umat \in \reals^{m \times m}$ and $\Vmat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ are orthogonal and
$\Sigmamat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ is zero except for the diagonal entries
$\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$, the singular values <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
</Definition>

## Terms

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\vvec_i$ | right singular vector | Column $i$ of $\Vmat$: an orthonormal direction in the input space. |
| $\sigma_i$ | singular value | How much $\Amat$ stretches $\vvec_i$. |
| $\uvec_i$ | left singular vector | Column $i$ of $\Umat$: the output direction, with $\Amat\vvec_i = \sigma_i \uvec_i$. |
| $r$ | rank | The number of nonzero singular values. |

## Geometry

Read $\Amat\xvec = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top\xvec$ from right to left. $\Vmat^\top$ rotates (or reflects) the input,
$\Sigmamat$ stretches each axis by its singular value, and $\Umat$ rotates the result. A matrix therefore maps the unit
sphere to an ellipsoid whose semi-axes are $\sigma_i\uvec_i$.

## Why every matrix has one

The construction starts from the symmetric matrix $\Amat^\top\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$. By the
<NoteLink to="spectral-theorem">spectral theorem</NoteLink> it has orthonormal eigenvectors $\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_n$
with real eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$. The eigenvalues are never negative, because

$$
\lambda_i = \vvec_i^\top\Amat^\top\Amat\vvec_i = \norm{\Amat\vvec_i}^2 \ge 0.
$$

Set $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$, so $\norm{\Amat\vvec_i} = \sigma_i$. Let $r$ be the number of nonzero $\sigma_i$, and
for $i \le r$ define the output directions

$$
\uvec_i = \frac{\Amat\vvec_i}{\sigma_i}.
$$

These are orthonormal:

$$
\uvec_i^\top\uvec_j = \frac{\vvec_i^\top\Amat^\top\Amat\vvec_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j} = \frac{\lambda_j\,\vvec_i^\top\vvec_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j},
$$

which is 1 when $i = j$ and 0 otherwise. Extend $\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_r$ to an orthonormal basis
$\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_m$ of $\reals^m$, for example with <NoteLink to="gram-schmidt">Gram–Schmidt</NoteLink>. For
$i > r$, $\Amat\vvec_i = \zeros$ because its length $\sigma_i$ is 0. So $\Amat\vvec_i = \sigma_i\uvec_i$ for every $i$,
which in matrix form is $\Amat\Vmat = \Umat\Sigmamat$. Multiplying on the right by $\Vmat^\top = \Vmat^{-1}$ gives
$\Amat = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$.

## Relation to eigendecomposition

The construction shows that the singular vectors are eigenvectors of two symmetric matrices. Substituting the SVD and
using $\Umat^\top\Umat = \eye$ and $\Vmat^\top\Vmat = \eye$ gives

$$
\Amat^\top\Amat = \Vmat\,\Sigmamat^\top\Sigmamat\,\Vmat^\top, \qquad \Amat\Amat^\top = \Umat\,\Sigmamat\Sigmamat^\top\,\Umat^\top.
$$

So $\sigma_i^2$ are the eigenvalues of $\Amat^\top\Amat$. Unlike an eigendecomposition, the SVD exists for every matrix,
square or not, and its bases are always orthonormal. For a symmetric <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss> matrix the two coincide.

Singular values are never negative, so they cannot show whether a symmetric matrix is positive definite. That needs
the signs of its eigenvalues.

## Low-rank approximation

Write the SVD as a sum of rank-one matrices, $\Amat = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i \uvec_i \vvec_i^\top$, and keep the first
$k$ terms:

$$
\Amat_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \uvec_i \vvec_i^\top.
$$

**Eckart–Young theorem.** No matrix of rank at most $k$ is closer to $\Amat$ in the spectral or Frobenius norm
<Cite id="eckart1936" />. The errors are exactly the discarded singular values:

$$
\norm{\Amat - \Amat_k}_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \qquad \norm{\Amat - \Amat_k}_F = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i > k} \sigma_i^2}.
$$

The error formulas hold because $\Amat - \Amat_k = \sum_{i > k}\sigma_i\uvec_i\vvec_i^\top$ is itself an SVD, with
singular values $\sigma_{k+1}, \sigma_{k+2}, \dots$. Its spectral norm is the largest of these and its Frobenius norm is
the root of their sum of squares (see <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">matrix norms</NoteLink>).

<Derivation title="Why no rank-k matrix does better in the spectral norm">
Let $\Bmat$ have rank at most $k$. By rank–nullity its null space has dimension at least $n - k$. The span of
$\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_{k+1}$ has dimension $k + 1$. Two subspaces of $\reals^n$ whose dimensions add up to more than
$n$ share a nonzero vector, so there is a unit vector $\wvec = \sum_{i \le k+1} c_i\vvec_i$ with $\Bmat\wvec = \zeros$
and $\sum_i c_i^2 = 1$. Then

$$
\norm{(\Amat - \Bmat)\wvec}^2 = \norm{\Amat\wvec}^2 = \norm{\textstyle\sum_{i \le k+1} c_i\sigma_i\uvec_i}^2 = \sum_{i \le k+1} c_i^2\sigma_i^2
\ge \sigma_{k+1}^2.
$$

So $\norm{\Amat - \Bmat}_2 \ge \sigma_{k+1} = \norm{\Amat - \Amat_k}_2$. The Frobenius statement follows from a similar
argument applied to each singular value in turn <Cite id="eckart1936" />.
</Derivation>

Storing $\Amat_k$ takes $k(m + n + 1)$ numbers instead of $mn$. When the singular values decay fast, the discarded
singular values are small, so $\Amat_k$ is close to $\Amat$ for small $k$.

## In machine learning

- **Principal component analysis** is the SVD of the centred data matrix. The right singular vectors are the principal
  directions, and $\sigma_i^2/(n - 1)$ are the variances along them.
- **<NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">Pseudoinverse</NoteLink> and least squares.** $\Amat^+ = \Vmat\Sigmamat^+\Umat^\top$
  inverts the nonzero singular values. It gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution and projects onto the column
  space; see <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink>.
- **Conditioning.** The <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> $\sigma_1/\sigma_r$ measures how
  much errors in the data can be amplified when solving $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$.
- **Compression.** <Gloss name="lora">Low-rank adaptation</Gloss> of large models, <Gloss name="lsa">latent semantic analysis</Gloss> and <Gloss name="collaborative-filtering">collaborative filtering</Gloss> all rest
  on low-rank structure.

## Pitfalls

- **Signs are arbitrary.** Flipping the signs of both $\uvec_i$ and $\vvec_i$ gives another valid SVD. Different
  libraries can return different signs.
- **The pseudoinverse is discontinuous.** When a singular value crosses zero, the rank changes and $\Amat^+$ jumps.
  Small singular values should be truncated or regularised, not inverted.
- **Cost.** A full SVD of an $m \times n$ matrix costs $O(mn\min(m, n))$. For large matrices, compute only the leading
  singular vectors with a randomised or iterative method.
