<Definition>
A nonzero vector $\vvec$ is an eigenvector of a square matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$
if $\Amat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$. If $\Amat$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, collected as the columns of
$\Vmat$, then

$$
\Amat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1}, \qquad \Lambdamat = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n),
$$

and $\Amat$ is called diagonalisable <Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Why the factorisation holds

The factorisation is the eigenvector equation written for all $n$ eigenvectors at once. Column $i$ of $\Amat\Vmat$ is
$\Amat\vvec_i = \lambda_i\vvec_i$. Column $i$ of $\Vmat\Lambdamat$ is also $\lambda_i\vvec_i$, because multiplying by a
diagonal matrix on the right scales each column. So

$$
\Amat\Vmat = \Vmat\Lambdamat.
$$

The columns of $\Vmat$ are independent, so $\Vmat$ is invertible, and multiplying on the right by $\Vmat^{-1}$ gives
$\Amat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1}$. Conversely, any factorisation $\Amat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1}$ with diagonal
$\Lambdamat$ gives $\Amat\Vmat = \Vmat\Lambdamat$, so the columns of $\Vmat$ are eigenvectors.

## Finding eigenvalues

$\Amat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$ with $\vvec \ne \zeros$ means $(\Amat - \lambda\eye)\vvec = \zeros$ has a nonzero
solution, so $\Amat - \lambda\eye$ is singular. The eigenvalues are therefore the roots of the characteristic
polynomial $\det(\Amat - \lambda\eye) = 0$, which has degree $n$. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix,

$$
\lambda^2 - \trace(\Amat)\,\lambda + \det\Amat = 0, \qquad
\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2}\trace(\Amat) \pm \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}\trace(\Amat)^2 - \det\Amat}.
$$

In general, the sum of the eigenvalues is $\trace(\Amat)$ and their product is $\det\Amat$. The characteristic
polynomial factors as $\det(\Amat - \lambda\eye) = \prod_i(\lambda_i - \lambda)$ over the complex numbers. Setting
$\lambda = 0$ gives the product. The coefficient of $\lambda^{n-1}$ is $(-1)^{n-1}\sum_i\lambda_i$ in the factored form,
and $(-1)^{n-1}\sum_i a_{ii}$ in the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink>, since only the product of the
diagonal entries contributes $\lambda^{n-1}$. So the sum is the trace.

A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues, in conjugate pairs. A rotation of the plane by an angle other than 0 or
$\pi$ has none that are real, because it leaves no direction unchanged.

Numerical software does not solve the characteristic polynomial, whose roots are very sensitive to its coefficients. It
uses the <Gloss name="qr" /> algorithm, which reduces $\Amat$ to triangular form by orthogonal similarity
transformations <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.

## The eigenbasis as coordinates

Write $\xvec = \Vmat\cvec$, so $\cvec$ holds the coordinates of $\xvec$ along the eigenvectors. Then
$\Amat\xvec = \Vmat\Lambdamat\cvec$: in the eigenbasis, $\Amat$ multiplies each coordinate by its own eigenvalue.
Repeated application gives

$$
\Amat^k\xvec = \Vmat\Lambdamat^k\cvec = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\lambda_i^k\,\vvec_i.
$$

The middle step uses
$\Amat^k = (\Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1})(\Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1})\cdots = \Vmat\Lambdamat^k\Vmat^{-1}$: every inner
$\Vmat^{-1}\Vmat$ cancels. Components with $\abs{\lambda_i} > 1$ grow, those with $\abs{\lambda_i} < 1$ decay, and the
component with the largest $\abs{\lambda_i}$ eventually dominates. Dividing by $\lambda_1^k$ shows how fast:

$$
\frac{\Amat^k\xvec}{\lambda_1^k} = c_1\vvec_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} c_i \paren{\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}}^k \vvec_i,
$$

and the other components shrink like $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k$ when $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \dots$
and $c_1 \neq 0$.

## Worked example

Let $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $\trace\Amat = 4$ and $\det\Amat = 3$, so
$\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0$ and $\lambda \in \{3, 1\}$. For $\lambda = 3$, $(\Amat - 3\eye)\vvec = \zeros$ gives
$\vvec_1 = (1, 1)$. For $\lambda = 1$ it gives $\vvec_2 = (1, -1)$. Hence

$$
\Amat^{10} = \Vmat \begin{bmatrix} 3^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \Vmat^{-1}
= \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 3^{10} + 1 & 3^{10} - 1 \\ 3^{10} - 1 & 3^{10} + 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with no <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix multiplications</NoteLink> beyond the change of basis.

## When a matrix is diagonalisable

- **Distinct eigenvalues suffice.** Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are linearly independent, so $n$ distinct
  eigenvalues give $n$ independent eigenvectors.
- **Repeated eigenvalues may not.** $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has the double eigenvalue 1 but only
  one eigenvector direction, $(1, 0)$. Such a matrix is called defective. The Jordan form or the Schur form
  $\Amat = \Qmat\Tmat\Qmat^\top$, with $\Tmat$ triangular, replaces the diagonal form <Cite id="golub2013" />.
- **Symmetric matrices always are**, with real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors. This is the
  <NoteLink to="spectral-theorem">spectral theorem</NoteLink>.

<Derivation title="Why eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent">
Suppose not, and take the shortest dependent list $\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_k$ of eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues:
$\sum_{i=1}^k c_i\vvec_i = \zeros$ with every $c_i \neq 0$, and $k \ge 2$ since an eigenvector is nonzero. Apply
$\Amat$, and separately multiply by $\lambda_k$:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^k c_i\lambda_i\vvec_i = \zeros, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^k c_i\lambda_k\vvec_i = \zeros.
$$

Subtracting gives $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k)\vvec_i = \zeros$. Every coefficient is nonzero, because
the eigenvalues are distinct. That is a shorter dependent list, a contradiction.
</Derivation>

## In machine learning

- **<Gloss name="pca">Principal component analysis</Gloss>** diagonalises the
  <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">covariance matrix</NoteLink>. Its eigenvectors are the principal directions and
  its eigenvalues are the variances along them.
- **Power iteration.** Repeating $\xvec \leftarrow \Amat\xvec / \norm{\Amat\xvec}$ converges to the dominant eigenvector
  when $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2}$, at a rate set by $\abs{\lambda_2 / \lambda_1}$. PageRank is power iteration
  on a link matrix.
- **Stability of linear dynamics.** $\xvec_{t+1} = \Amat\xvec_t$ converges to zero from every start exactly when the
  spectral radius $\max_i \abs{\lambda_i}$ is below 1. The same quantity governs exploding and vanishing gradients in
  linear recurrent networks.
- **Optimisation.** The eigenvalues of the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> set the curvature in each
  direction. Their ratio, the <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink>, limits the step size and
  speed of <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>.
- **<NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">Spectral clustering</NoteLink>** uses the eigenvectors of a graph Laplacian with
  the smallest eigenvalues.

## Relation to the SVD

The <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> exists for every matrix, uses
two orthonormal bases, and has non-negative singular values. The eigendecomposition needs a square, diagonalisable
matrix, uses one basis that need not be orthogonal, and its eigenvalues can be negative or complex. For a symmetric
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive semidefinite matrix</NoteLink> the two coincide.

## Pitfalls

- **Ill-conditioned eigenvectors.** For non-symmetric matrices, eigenvectors can be nearly parallel. Then $\Vmat$ is
  close to singular, and $\Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1}$ amplifies <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding
  errors</NoteLink>. Transient growth can occur even when every $\abs{\lambda_i} < 1$.
- **Scaling and sign.** Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector. Libraries normalise to unit length,
  but signs and, for repeated eigenvalues, the basis of the eigenspace are arbitrary.
- **Eigenvalues are not singular values.** For a non-symmetric matrix, $\abs{\lambda_i}$ and $\sigma_i$ differ. The
  largest $\abs{\lambda_i}$ can be much smaller than $\sigma_1$.
