<Definition>
An LU decomposition of a square matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is a factorisation

$$
\Pmat\Amat = \Lmat\Umat,
$$

where $\Pmat$ is a permutation matrix, $\Lmat$ is lower triangular with ones on its diagonal, and $\Umat$ is upper
triangular. Every square matrix has one. When no row exchanges are needed, $\Pmat = \eye$ and $\Amat = \Lmat\Umat$
<Cite id="golub2013" />.
</Definition>

## From Gaussian elimination

Gaussian elimination subtracts multiples of each pivot row from the rows below it until the matrix is upper
triangular. The triangular result is $\Umat$. The multiplier $\ell_{ij}$ used to clear entry $(i, j)$ becomes entry
$(i, j)$ of $\Lmat$. The factorisation is therefore a record of elimination, not a new algorithm.

The multipliers land in $\Lmat$ unchanged because each elimination step is a <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink>. Step $k$ clears column $k$
below the pivot by subtracting $\ell_{ik}$ times row $k$ from each row $i > k$. That is multiplication on the left by

$$
\Emat_k = \eye - \lvec_k\evec_k^\top,
$$

where $\lvec_k$ holds the multipliers $\ell_{k+1,k}, \dots, \ell_{nk}$ in positions $k+1$ to $n$ and zeros above. After
$n - 1$ steps, $\Emat_{n-1}\cdots\Emat_2\Emat_1\Amat = \Umat$, so

$$
\Amat = \Emat_1^{-1}\Emat_2^{-1}\cdots\Emat_{n-1}^{-1}\Umat.
$$

Each inverse is easy. Since $\lvec_k$ is zero in position $k$, $\evec_k^\top\lvec_k = 0$, and

$$
(\eye - \lvec_k\evec_k^\top)(\eye + \lvec_k\evec_k^\top) = \eye - \lvec_k(\evec_k^\top\lvec_k)\evec_k^\top = \eye,
$$

so $\Emat_k^{-1} = \eye + \lvec_k\evec_k^\top$: undoing a step adds the rows back. In the product of the inverses every
cross term $\lvec_j\evec_j^\top\lvec_k\evec_k^\top$ with $j < k$ vanishes, because $\lvec_k$ is zero in position $j$.
What remains is

$$
\Lmat = \eye + \lvec_1\evec_1^\top + \lvec_2\evec_2^\top + \dots + \lvec_{n-1}\evec_{n-1}^\top,
$$

the identity with multiplier $\ell_{ik}$ in position $(i, k)$. Hence $\Amat = \Lmat\Umat$.

Row exchanges, recorded in $\Pmat$, are needed when a pivot is zero. **Partial pivoting** exchanges rows at every step
so that the pivot is the largest entry in its column. This keeps every $\abs{\ell_{ij}} \le 1$ and is the default in
numerical libraries <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.

## Solving linear systems

To solve $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ with $\Pmat\Amat = \Lmat\Umat$, write $\Lmat\Umat\xvec = \Pmat\bvec$ and set
$\yvec = \Umat\xvec$:

1. **Forward substitution.** Solve $\Lmat\yvec = \Pmat\bvec$ from the first row down. Row $i$ involves only
   $y_1, \dots, y_i$.
2. **Back substitution.** Solve $\Umat\xvec = \yvec$ from the last row up. Row $i$ involves only $x_i, \dots, x_n$.

$\Lmat$ has ones on its diagonal and is always invertible. Back substitution divides by the diagonal entries of
$\Umat$, so the solution exists and is unique exactly when every $u_{ii}$ is nonzero, that is when $\Amat$ is
invertible.

## Cost

Factorising a dense $n \times n$ matrix costs about $\tfrac{2}{3}n^3$ <Gloss name="flop">floating-point operations</Gloss>. Step $k$ updates the
$(n - k)^2$ entries below and to the right of the pivot with one multiplication and one subtraction each, and
$\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} 2(n - k)^2 \approx \tfrac{2}{3}n^3$. Each triangular solve
costs about $n^2$. A system with $k$ right-hand sides therefore costs $\tfrac{2}{3}n^3 + 2kn^2$ with one factorisation,
against $k \cdot \tfrac{2}{3}n^3$ if elimination is repeated for each. Forming $\Amat^{-1}$ explicitly is slower and
less accurate than solving with the factors <Cite id="higham2002" />.

The factors also give the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink>:
$\det\Amat = \det(\Pmat)^{-1}\prod_i u_{ii}$, because $\det\Lmat = 1$ and determinants multiply. $\det\Pmat = \pm 1$
counts the parity of the row exchanges. Solving with the factors is also why forming the
<NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">matrix inverse</NoteLink> is never needed.

## Worked example

Take

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\bvec = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 8 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Eliminate.** The multiplier for row 2 is $\ell_{21} = 3/4$, which leaves row 2 as $(0, \tfrac{7}{4}, -1)$. Row 3
already starts with zero, so $\ell_{31} = 0$. The next pivot is $\tfrac{7}{4}$, and $\ell_{32} = -1 / \tfrac{7}{4} =
-\tfrac{4}{7}$ leaves row 3 as $(0, 0, \tfrac{24}{7})$. No row exchange is needed, so

$$
\Lmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \tfrac{3}{4} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -\tfrac{4}{7} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Umat = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac{7}{4} & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac{24}{7} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Forward substitution** on $\Lmat\yvec = \bvec$ gives $y_1 = 10$, then
$y_2 = 8 - \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 10 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, then $y_3 = 10 + \tfrac{4}{7} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{72}{7}$.

**Back substitution** on $\Umat\xvec = \yvec$ gives $x_3 = \tfrac{72}{7} / \tfrac{24}{7} = 3$, then
$\tfrac{7}{4}x_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} + 3$ so $x_2 = 2$, then $4x_1 = 10 - 3 \cdot 2$ so $x_1 = 1$. The solution is
$\xvec = (1, 2, 3)$.

The determinant is $4 \cdot \tfrac{7}{4} \cdot \tfrac{24}{7} = 24$.

## Relation to other factorisations

- For a symmetric positive definite matrix, the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky decomposition</NoteLink>
  $\Amat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$ is the symmetric form of LU. It needs no pivoting and costs half as much.
- The <NoteLink to="qr-decomposition">QR decomposition</NoteLink> also solves square systems and handles rectangular
  least-squares problems. It costs about twice as much as LU for square matrices and is more robust when $\Amat$ is
  close to singular.

## Pitfalls

- **No pivoting.** Without row exchanges, a tiny pivot creates huge multipliers and destroys accuracy. The matrix
  $\begin{bmatrix} \epsilon & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ with small $\epsilon$ factors exactly, but in floating point the
  computed solution is badly wrong unless the rows are exchanged <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
- **Ill-conditioning.** Pivoting controls the growth of <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding errors</NoteLink> in the factors. It cannot fix a large
  <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink>, which amplifies errors in $\bvec$ whatever method is
  used.
- **Fill-in.** For sparse matrices, $\Lmat$ and $\Umat$ can be much denser than $\Amat$. Sparse solvers reorder rows
  and columns to limit this.
