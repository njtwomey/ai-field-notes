<Definition>
The condition number of an <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">invertible matrix</NoteLink> $\Amat$ in a given matrix norm is

$$
\kappa(\Amat) = \norm{\Amat}\,\norm{\Amat^{-1}}.
$$

In the spectral norm, $\kappa_2(\Amat) = \sigma_1 / \sigma_n$, the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value.
It satisfies $\kappa(\Amat) \ge 1$, and a singular matrix is assigned $\kappa = \infty$ <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
</Definition>

Both facts follow from the definition. For an induced norm,
$1 = \norm{\eye} = \norm{\Amat\Amat^{-1}} \le \norm{\Amat}\,\norm{\Amat^{-1}}$ by submultiplicativity, so
$\kappa \ge 1$. In the spectral norm, $\norm{\Amat}_2 = \sigma_1$ (see <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">matrix
norms</NoteLink>). If $\Amat = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$ is the <Gloss name="svd">singular value decomposition</Gloss>, then
$\Amat^{-1} = \Vmat\Sigmamat^{-1}\Umat^\top$, whose singular values are $1/\sigma_i$. The largest of these is
$1/\sigma_n$, so $\norm{\Amat^{-1}}_2 = 1/\sigma_n$ and $\kappa_2(\Amat) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$.

## What it bounds

Let $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ and perturb the right-hand side to $\bvec + \delta\bvec$. The solution moves to
$\xvec + \delta\xvec$ with $\Amat\,\delta\xvec = \delta\bvec$, and

$$
\frac{\norm{\delta\xvec}}{\norm{\xvec}} \le \kappa(\Amat)\,\frac{\norm{\delta\bvec}}{\norm{\bvec}}.
$$

The bound combines two inequalities, one for the numerator and one for the denominator. The error solves
$\delta\xvec = \Amat^{-1}\delta\bvec$, so

$$
\norm{\delta\xvec} \le \norm{\Amat^{-1}}\,\norm{\delta\bvec}.
$$

The solution itself satisfies $\bvec = \Amat\xvec$, so $\norm{\bvec} \le \norm{\Amat}\,\norm{\xvec}$, which rearranges
to

$$
\frac{1}{\norm{\xvec}} \le \frac{\norm{\Amat}}{\norm{\bvec}}.
$$

Multiplying the two inequalities gives the bound, with $\kappa(\Amat) = \norm{\Amat}\,\norm{\Amat^{-1}}$. Each
inequality is an equality for a suitable vector, so the bound is tight. In the spectral norm, equality holds when
$\bvec$ lies along $\uvec_1$, the output direction $\Amat$ stretches most, and $\delta\bvec$ along $\uvec_n$, the
direction it stretches least. The solution is then as small as possible for its $\bvec$, and the error as large as
possible for its $\delta\bvec$.

A small relative change in the data can therefore cause a relative change $\kappa$ times larger in the solution.
Perturbations of $\Amat$ itself obey a similar bound, to first order <Cite id="higham2002" />.

## Geometry

$\Amat^{-1}$ maps a ball of perturbations around $\bvec$ to an ellipse around $\xvec$. The semi-axes of the ellipse are
$\norm{\delta\bvec}/\sigma_i$, so the ellipse is long in the directions that $\Amat$ squashes. The condition number is
the ratio of its longest to its shortest semi-axis. For a $2 \times 2$ system, each equation is a line and the solution
is their intersection. Nearly parallel lines give an ill-conditioned system: shifting one line slightly slides the
intersection a long way.

## Digits lost

In floating point with machine precision $\epsilon$ (about $10^{-16}$ in <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">double precision</NoteLink>), a backward-stable solver
returns the exact solution of a nearby system. Its relative error is roughly $\kappa(\Amat)\,\epsilon$. With
$\kappa = 10^{k}$, about $k$ of the 16 significant digits are unreliable <Cite id="higham2002" />.

## Worked example

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.001 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \bvec = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.001 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The solution is $\xvec = (1, 1)$. Changing $b_2$ to $2.002$, a relative change in $\bvec$ of about $3.5 \times 10^{-4}$,
moves the solution to $(0, 2)$, a relative change of 1. The singular values of $\Amat$ are about $2.0005$ and
$0.0005$, so $\kappa_2(\Amat) \approx 4000$, and the observed amplification of about 2800 is within the bound.

## Well- and ill-conditioned matrices

- Orthogonal matrices have $\kappa_2 = 1$, the smallest possible value. They preserve lengths, so every singular value
  is 1 and they never amplify errors. Stable algorithms (<NoteLink to="qr-decomposition">QR</NoteLink>, SVD) are built
  from them.
- $\kappa(\alpha\Amat) = \norm{\alpha\Amat}\,\norm{\alpha^{-1}\Amat^{-1}} = \kappa(\Amat)$: scaling the whole matrix
  changes the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> but not the conditioning.
- $\kappa_2(\Amat^\top\Amat) = \kappa_2(\Amat)^2$, because $\Amat^\top\Amat = \Vmat\Sigmamat^2\Vmat^\top$ has singular
  values $\sigma_i^2$. Forming the normal equations of least squares squares the condition number. QR-based least
  squares avoids this <Cite id="golub2013" />. For rank-deficient problems the
  <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink> drops the zero singular values, and its conditioning is governed
  by the smallest nonzero one.

## In machine learning

- **Optimisation speed.** On a quadratic with <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> $\Hmat$, gradient descent needs a number of iterations roughly
  proportional to $\kappa(\Hmat)$ to reach a fixed accuracy. Elongated loss valleys are ill-conditioned Hessians; see
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. Feature standardisation, preconditioning and adaptive
  optimisers all aim to reduce the effective condition number.
- **Collinear features.** Nearly dependent feature columns make $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ ill-conditioned, so the fitted weights
  swing wildly with small changes in the data. <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge regression</NoteLink> replaces $\sigma_i^2$ with $\sigma_i^2 + \lambda$ and
  bounds the condition number by $(\sigma_1^2 + \lambda)/\lambda$.

## Pitfalls

- **The determinant is not a conditioning measure.** $10^{-3}\,\eye$ has determinant $10^{-3n}$ and condition number 1.
- **Condition is a property of the problem.** A large $\kappa$ limits every algorithm. An unstable algorithm can lose
  accuracy even on a well-conditioned problem; the two issues are separate.
- **Estimate, do not compute.** Computing $\sigma_n$ exactly costs as much as an SVD. Libraries estimate $\kappa$
  cheaply from an existing <NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">LU factorisation</NoteLink>.
