<Definition>
Let $\Scal$ be a subspace of $\reals^n$. The orthogonal projection of $\xvec$ onto $\Scal$ is the unique
$\pvec \in \Scal$ such that the residual $\xvec - \pvec$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\Scal$. It is
also the point of $\Scal$ closest to $\xvec$ in Euclidean distance <Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Onto a line

For the line spanned by a nonzero vector $\uvec$:

$$
\pvec = \frac{\uvec^\top \xvec}{\uvec^\top \uvec}\, \uvec.
$$

If $\uvec$ has unit length, this simplifies to $\pvec = (\uvec^\top \xvec)\, \uvec$. The scalar $\uvec^\top \xvec$ is
the coordinate of $\xvec$ along $\uvec$.

The formula comes from minimising the distance. Write the candidate point as $c\uvec$ and minimise

$$
g(c) = \norm{\xvec - c\uvec}^2 = \xvec^\top\xvec - 2c\, \uvec^\top\xvec + c^2\, \uvec^\top\uvec
$$

over $c$. This is a parabola that opens upwards. Setting $g'(c) = -2\,\uvec^\top\xvec + 2c\,\uvec^\top\uvec = 0$ gives
$c = \uvec^\top\xvec / \uvec^\top\uvec$. At this $c$ the residual is orthogonal to the line:
$\uvec^\top(\xvec - c\uvec) = \uvec^\top\xvec - c\, \uvec^\top\uvec = 0$.

## Onto a subspace

### Orthogonal residual means closest point

The two characterisations in the definition agree for any subspace. Suppose $\pvec \in \Scal$ has
$\xvec - \pvec$ orthogonal to $\Scal$. Any other point $\svec \in \Scal$ differs from $\pvec$ by a vector
$\pvec - \svec$ in $\Scal$, which is orthogonal to the residual. Pythagoras gives

$$
\norm{\xvec - \svec}^2 = \norm{(\xvec - \pvec) + (\pvec - \svec)}^2 = \norm{\xvec - \pvec}^2 + \norm{\pvec - \svec}^2 \ge \norm{\xvec - \pvec}^2,
$$

with equality only when $\svec = \pvec$. So $\pvec$ is the unique closest point.

### The projection formula

Let the columns of $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times k}$ be a basis of $\Scal$. Write $\pvec = \Amat\cvec$. Requiring the
residual to be orthogonal to every column gives the normal equations $\Amat^\top(\xvec - \Amat\cvec) = \zeros$, that is
$\Amat^\top\Amat\,\cvec = \Amat^\top\xvec$. The matrix $\Amat^\top\Amat$ is invertible: if
$\Amat^\top\Amat\cvec = \zeros$ then $\norm{\Amat\cvec}^2 = \cvec^\top\Amat^\top\Amat\cvec = 0$, so
$\Amat\cvec = \zeros$, and independent columns force $\cvec = \zeros$. Therefore

$$
\pvec = \Pmat\xvec, \qquad \Pmat = \Amat(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}\Amat^\top.
$$

If the columns are orthonormal, collected in $\Qmat$, then $\Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$ and $\Pmat = \Qmat\Qmat^\top$.

## Properties of a projection matrix

- **Idempotent:** $\Pmat^2 = \Pmat$. Projecting twice changes nothing.
- **Symmetric:** $\Pmat^\top = \Pmat$. Symmetry distinguishes orthogonal projections from oblique ones.
- **Eigenvalues are 0 or 1.** Vectors in $\Scal$ are kept; vectors orthogonal to $\Scal$ are removed.
- **Rank** equals $\dim \Scal$, which also equals $\trace(\Pmat)$.
- **Complement:** $\eye - \Pmat$ projects onto the orthogonal complement $\Scal^\perp$.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the properties">
**Idempotent.** The middle $\Amat^\top\Amat$ cancels one inverse:

$$
\Pmat^2 = \Amat(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}\Amat^\top\Amat(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}\Amat^\top = \Amat(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}\Amat^\top = \Pmat.
$$

**Symmetric.** $(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}$ is symmetric, because $\Amat^\top\Amat$ is, so transposing $\Pmat$ returns it.

**Eigenvalues.** If $\Pmat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$ with $\vvec \neq \zeros$, then
$\lambda\vvec = \Pmat\vvec = \Pmat^2\vvec = \lambda^2\vvec$, so $\lambda^2 = \lambda$ and $\lambda \in \set{0, 1}$.
Vectors in $\Scal$ are fixed, $\Pmat\Amat\cvec = \Amat\cvec$, and vectors orthogonal to every column are sent to
$\zeros$, since $\Amat^\top\vvec = \zeros$.

**Rank and trace.** The eigenvalue 1 has the $k$-dimensional eigenspace $\Scal$, so $\rank\Pmat = k$. The trace is the
sum of the eigenvalues, also $k$. Directly,
$\trace\Pmat = \trace\paren{(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}\Amat^\top\Amat} = \trace(\eye_k) = k$, using
$\trace(\Bmat\Cmat) = \trace(\Cmat\Bmat)$.

**Complement.** $(\eye - \Pmat)\xvec = \xvec - \pvec$ is the residual, which lies in $\Scal^\perp$, and
$\xvec - (\xvec - \pvec) = \pvec$ is orthogonal to $\Scal^\perp$. So $\eye - \Pmat$ satisfies the definition for
$\Scal^\perp$.
</Derivation>

## Least squares is a projection

In <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>, the fitted values
$\hat\yvec = \Xmat\hat\wvec$ lie in the <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">column space</NoteLink> of $\Xmat$. Least squares chooses the point of that space closest
to $\yvec$, so $\hat\yvec$ is the orthogonal projection of $\yvec$:

$$
\hat\yvec = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top \yvec.
$$

This $\Pmat$ is called the hat matrix, because it puts the hat on $\yvec$. The residuals $\yvec - \hat\yvec$ are
orthogonal to every column of $\Xmat$. In particular, when $\Xmat$ has a column of ones, the residuals sum to zero.

## Pitfalls

- **Forming the inverse.** Computing $(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}$ squares the <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink>. Use a
  <NoteLink to="qr-decomposition">QR factorisation</NoteLink>, $\Amat = \Qmat\Rmat$, and project with $\Qmat\Qmat^\top$
  instead. For columns of $\Amat$ that are not independent, the <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink> gives
  $\Pmat = \Amat\Amat^+$.
- **Non-orthonormal bases.** $\Amat\Amat^\top$ is a projection only when the columns of $\Amat$ are orthonormal.
