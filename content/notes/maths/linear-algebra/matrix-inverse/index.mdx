<Definition>
A square matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is invertible (nonsingular) if there is a matrix $\Amat^{-1}$ with

$$
\Amat^{-1}\Amat = \Amat\Amat^{-1} = \eye.
$$

The inverse is unique when it exists. A matrix without an inverse is singular <Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

The inverse is unique because two inverses $\Bmat$ and $\Cmat$ of $\Amat$ must agree:
$\Bmat = \Bmat\eye = \Bmat(\Amat\Cmat) = (\Bmat\Amat)\Cmat = \eye\Cmat = \Cmat$.

## Undoing a map

The inverse undoes the map: $\Amat^{-1}(\Amat\xvec) = \xvec$ for every $\xvec$.
A singular $\Amat$ sends two different points to the same
image, so no map can undo it: in two dimensions it flattens the plane onto a line, and the position of each point
along the lost direction is gone. The best that can be done is the
<NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink>, which recovers the component of each point that survived.

A nearly singular matrix is invertible, but it squeezes one direction almost flat. Its inverse must stretch that
direction back by the same large factor, and it stretches any error in $\Amat\xvec$ by that factor too. The
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> measures this amplification.

## When an inverse exists

For $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ the following statements are equivalent. Each says that $\Amat$ loses no
information.

- $\Amat$ is invertible.
- $\det\Amat \neq 0$.
- $\rank\Amat = n$: the columns are linearly independent and form a basis of $\reals^n$.
- $\Null(\Amat) = \set{\zeros}$: only $\zeros$ maps to $\zeros$.
- $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ has exactly one solution for every $\bvec \in \reals^n$.
- 0 is not an eigenvalue of $\Amat$.



- **Invertible implies trivial null space.** If $\Amat\xvec = \zeros$, then
  $\xvec = \Amat^{-1}\Amat\xvec = \Amat^{-1}\zeros = \zeros$.
- **Trivial null space is <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">full rank</NoteLink>.** By the rank–nullity theorem, $\rank\Amat = n - \dim\Null(\Amat) = n$. The null
  space is trivial exactly when 0 is not an eigenvalue, since an eigenvector for 0 is a nonzero null-space vector.
- **Full rank gives unique solutions.** $n$ independent columns in $\reals^n$ form a basis, so every $\bvec$ is a
  combination of them, with unique coefficients. Those coefficients are the unique $\xvec$ with $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$.
- **Unique solutions give an inverse.** Solve $\Amat\xvec_j = \evec_j$ for each standard basis vector and put the
  solutions into the columns of $\Xmat$. Then $\Amat\Xmat = \eye$. For the other side,
  $\Amat(\Xmat\Amat - \eye) = (\Amat\Xmat)\Amat - \Amat = \zeros$, so every column of $\Xmat\Amat - \eye$ lies in the
  null space, which is trivial. Hence $\Xmat\Amat = \eye$ too, and $\Xmat = \Amat^{-1}$.
- **Determinant.** $\det\Amat \neq 0$ exactly when the columns are independent, as shown in the
  <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> note.

A non-square matrix has no two-sided inverse. A tall matrix with independent columns has left inverses, and a wide
matrix with independent rows has right inverses. The Moore–Penrose <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink>
generalises the inverse to every matrix; it is built from the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value
decomposition</NoteLink>.

## The $2 \times 2$ case

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal and divide by the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink>.
Multiplying out checks it:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{bmatrix} = (ad - bc)\,\eye.
$$

Dividing by $ad - bc$ gives the identity. The formula breaks down exactly when $ad - bc = 0$.

## Rules

- $(\Amat\Bmat)^{-1} = \Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}$. To undo "apply $\Bmat$, then $\Amat$", undo $\Amat$ first.
- $(\Amat^\top)^{-1} = (\Amat^{-1})^\top$.
- $(\alpha\Amat)^{-1} = \alpha^{-1}\Amat^{-1}$ for $\alpha \neq 0$, and $\det\Amat^{-1} = 1/\det\Amat$.
- An orthogonal matrix $\Qmat$ has $\Qmat^{-1} = \Qmat^\top$. A diagonal matrix with nonzero entries inverts entry by
  entry.

<Derivation title="Why the product rule reverses the order">
Multiply $\Amat\Bmat$ by the candidate $\Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}$:

$$
(\Amat\Bmat)(\Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}) = \Amat(\Bmat\Bmat^{-1})\Amat^{-1} = \Amat\Amat^{-1} = \eye,
$$

and in the other order $(\Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1})(\Amat\Bmat) = \Bmat^{-1}\Bmat = \eye$. Since the inverse is unique, it is
$\Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}$. For the transpose, $\Amat^\top(\Amat^{-1})^\top = (\Amat^{-1}\Amat)^\top = \eye^\top = \eye$,
using $(\Cmat\Dmat)^\top = \Dmat^\top\Cmat^\top$. For an orthogonal matrix, $\Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$ says that the
columns are orthonormal, and that is exactly the statement that $\Qmat^\top$ is the inverse.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det\Amat = 24 - 14 = 10, \qquad
\Amat^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check the first column of $\Amat\Amat^{-1}$: $4 \cdot 0.6 + 7 \cdot (-0.2) = 1$ and $2 \cdot 0.6 + 6 \cdot (-0.2) = 0$.
The solution of $\Amat\xvec = (1, 2)$ is $\xvec = \Amat^{-1}(1, 2) = (-0.8, 0.6)$.

## Solve, do not invert

$\xvec = \Amat^{-1}\bvec$ is notation, not an algorithm. To solve $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$, factor $\Amat = \Pmat\Lmat\Umat$
by Gaussian elimination (<NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">LU decomposition</NoteLink>) and solve two triangular systems
<Cite id="golub2013" />. Forming $\Amat^{-1}$ first costs about three times as much and is less accurate: the computed
product $\Amat^{-1}\bvec$ carries larger errors than the solution from the factorisation <Cite id="higham2002" />. The
factorisation can be reused for many right-hand sides, so there is no speed advantage to the inverse either. NumPy's
`np.linalg.solve(A, b)` does this; `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b` does not.

## Updating an inverse

When a known inverse needs to change by a rank-one term, the Sherman–Morrison formula avoids starting again:

$$
(\Amat + \uvec\vvec^\top)^{-1} = \Amat^{-1} - \frac{\Amat^{-1}\uvec\,\vvec^\top\Amat^{-1}}{1 + \vvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\uvec},
$$

valid when the denominator is nonzero. It costs $O(n^2)$ instead of $O(n^3)$, and underlies <NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter">recursive least squares</NoteLink> and
quasi-Newton updates.

<Derivation title="Checking the Sherman–Morrison formula">
Write $\Bmat = \Amat^{-1}$ and $\beta = 1 + \vvec^\top\Bmat\uvec$. Multiply $\Amat + \uvec\vvec^\top$ by the claimed
inverse $\Bmat - \Bmat\uvec\vvec^\top\Bmat/\beta$:

$$
(\Amat + \uvec\vvec^\top)\paren{\Bmat - \frac{\Bmat\uvec\vvec^\top\Bmat}{\beta}}
= \eye + \uvec\vvec^\top\Bmat - \frac{\uvec\vvec^\top\Bmat + \uvec\,(\vvec^\top\Bmat\uvec)\,\vvec^\top\Bmat}{\beta}.
$$

The scalar $\vvec^\top\Bmat\uvec$ equals $\beta - 1$, so the fraction is $\uvec\,\beta\,\vvec^\top\Bmat/\beta =
\uvec\vvec^\top\Bmat$. It cancels the middle term and leaves $\eye$.
</Derivation>

## In machine learning

- **Normal equations.** Least squares gives $\hat\wvec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$ when $\Xmat$ has full
  column rank. See <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>. Solvers use a <Gloss name="qr" /> or
  <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky</NoteLink> factorisation instead of the inverse.
- **Ridge regression** replaces $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ with $\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye$. For $\lambda > 0$ this matrix is
  always invertible, whatever the rank of $\Xmat$. For any $\xvec \neq \zeros$,
  $\xvec^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)\xvec = \norm{\Xmat\xvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\xvec}^2 > 0$, so no nonzero
  vector is sent to $\zeros$.
- **Gaussian models.** The inverse covariance, the precision matrix $\Sigmamat^{-1}$, appears in every Gaussian density.
  A zero in entry $(i, j)$ of the precision matrix means variables $i$ and $j$ are conditionally independent given the
  rest.

## Pitfalls

- **Invertible does not mean well-conditioned.** A matrix can be invertible and still amplify <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding errors</NoteLink> by a huge factor. The
  <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> measures this, not the determinant.
- **Inverses of sparse matrices are dense.** A banded or sparse $\Amat$ usually has a dense inverse. Keeping the sparse
  factorisation saves both memory and time.
