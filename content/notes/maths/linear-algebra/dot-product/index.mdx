<Definition>
The dot product of $\xvec, \yvec \in \reals^n$ is

$$
\xvec^\top\yvec = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i.
$$

For nonzero vectors it equals $\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\cos\theta$, where $\norm{\xvec} = \sqrt{\xvec^\top\xvec}$ is
the Euclidean length and $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle between the vectors. The cosine similarity is

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\xvec^\top\yvec}{\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}}
$$

<Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

The dot product is also written $\xvec \cdot \yvec$ or $\inner{\xvec}{\yvec}$. It is symmetric, linear in each argument,
and $\xvec^\top\xvec = \norm{\xvec}^2 \ge 0$ with equality only for $\xvec = \zeros$.

## Geometry

The sign of the dot product gives the angle's range:

- $\xvec^\top\yvec > 0$: the angle is acute, and the vectors point broadly the same way.
- $\xvec^\top\yvec = 0$: the vectors are orthogonal.
- $\xvec^\top\yvec < 0$: the angle is obtuse, and the vectors point broadly in opposite directions.

For a unit vector $\uvec$, the dot product $\uvec^\top\yvec$ is the signed length of the shadow of $\yvec$ on the line
through $\uvec$, the scalar projection. For a general nonzero $\xvec$ the scalar projection of $\yvec$ onto $\xvec$ is
$\xvec^\top\yvec / \norm{\xvec} = \norm{\yvec}\cos\theta$. The full construction is covered in
<NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink>.

## Why the algebraic and geometric forms agree

The algebraic form $\sum_i x_i y_i$ and the geometric form $\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\cos\theta$ are the same number.
The argument compares two expressions for the squared length of the third side of a triangle.

Place the triangle with vertices $\zeros$, $\xvec$ and $\yvec$. Its sides have lengths $\norm{\xvec}$, $\norm{\yvec}$
and $\norm{\xvec - \yvec}$, and the angle between the first two sides is $\theta$. The three points lie in a plane, so
plane geometry applies in any dimension. The law of cosines states

$$
\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 = \norm{\xvec}^2 + \norm{\yvec}^2 - 2\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\cos\theta.
$$

Expanding the left side with the algebraic dot product, using its symmetry and linearity, gives

$$
\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 = (\xvec - \yvec)^\top(\xvec - \yvec) = \xvec^\top\xvec - 2\,\xvec^\top\yvec + \yvec^\top\yvec
= \norm{\xvec}^2 + \norm{\yvec}^2 - 2\,\xvec^\top\yvec.
$$

The two right sides are equal. Cancelling $\norm{\xvec}^2 + \norm{\yvec}^2$ and dividing by $-2$ gives

$$
\xvec^\top\yvec = \norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\cos\theta.
$$

<Derivation title="The law of cosines from Pythagoras">
Drop a perpendicular from $\yvec$ to the line through $\xvec$. Its foot is at distance $a = \norm{\yvec}\cos\theta$ from
the origin along that line, and the perpendicular has length $h = \norm{\yvec}\sin\theta$. The foot is at distance
$\norm{\xvec} - a$ from $\xvec$. Pythagoras on the right triangle with vertices $\xvec$, the foot and $\yvec$ gives

$$
\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 = (\norm{\xvec} - \norm{\yvec}\cos\theta)^2 + \norm{\yvec}^2\sin^2\theta
= \norm{\xvec}^2 - 2\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\cos\theta + \norm{\yvec}^2,
$$

using $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. The same algebra holds when $\theta$ is obtuse, where $a$ is negative.
</Derivation>

In $\reals^2$ and $\reals^3$ the angle is a geometric fact, and the identity is a theorem. In $\reals^n$ for $n > 3$ and
in general inner-product spaces the angle has no prior meaning. There the identity is turned round and used as the
definition, $\cos\theta = \xvec^\top\yvec / (\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec})$. That definition makes sense only if the
right side lies in $[-1, 1]$, which is what the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality guarantees.

## Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

For all $\xvec, \yvec \in \reals^n$,

$$
\abs{\xvec^\top\yvec} \le \norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec},
$$

with equality exactly when one vector is a multiple of the other. The proof uses only the algebraic dot product, so it
does not assume anything about angles.

If $\yvec = \zeros$, both sides are 0. Otherwise, consider the squared length of $\xvec - t\yvec$ as a function of the
real number $t$:

$$
0 \le \norm{\xvec - t\yvec}^2 = \norm{\xvec}^2 - 2t\,\xvec^\top\yvec + t^2 \norm{\yvec}^2.
$$

The right side is a quadratic in $t$ that is never negative. It is smallest at
$t^\star = \xvec^\top\yvec / \norm{\yvec}^2$, the coefficient of the projection of $\xvec$ onto $\yvec$. Substituting
$t^\star$ gives

$$
0 \le \norm{\xvec}^2 - \frac{(\xvec^\top\yvec)^2}{\norm{\yvec}^2}.
$$

Multiplying by $\norm{\yvec}^2$ and taking square roots gives the inequality. Equality holds exactly when
$\norm{\xvec - t^\star\yvec} = 0$, that is, when $\xvec = t^\star \yvec$. The same proof works for any inner product,
because it uses only symmetry, linearity and positivity.

The inequality guarantees that cosine similarity lies in $[-1, 1]$, and so that the angle $\theta$ is well defined in
every dimension.

## Worked example

For $\xvec = (3, 4)$ and $\yvec = (4, -3)$: $\xvec^\top\yvec = 12 - 12 = 0$, so the vectors are orthogonal. For
$\xvec = (1, 2, 2)$ and $\yvec = (2, 0, 1)$: $\xvec^\top\yvec = 2 + 0 + 2 = 4$, $\norm{\xvec} = 3$ and
$\norm{\yvec} = \sqrt 5$. The cosine similarity is $4 / (3\sqrt 5) \approx 0.596$, an angle of about $53.4°$.

## Cosine similarity and cosine distance

Cosine similarity ignores length. Two documents represented by word counts have the same cosine similarity whether one
is ten times longer or not, which is why it became the standard similarity in the vector-space model of information
retrieval <Cite id="salton1975" />. For unit vectors, cosine similarity equals the dot product. Setting
$\norm{\xvec} = \norm{\yvec} = 1$ in the expansion of $\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2$ above relates it to Euclidean distance:

$$
\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta.
$$

Ranking unit vectors by cosine similarity and ranking them by Euclidean distance therefore give the same order. The
quantity $1 - \cos\theta$ is often called cosine distance, although it does not satisfy the triangle inequality.

## In machine learning

- **Linear models.** A linear model scores an input by $\wvec^\top\xvec + b$: the dot product measures how well the
  input aligns with the weight vector. See <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>.
- **Attention.** <NoteLink to="transformer">Transformer</NoteLink> attention scores are scaled dot products between
  queries and keys, $\qvec^\top\kvec / \sqrt{d}$ <Cite id="vaswani2017" />. If the entries are independent with mean
  0 and variance 1, each product $q_i k_i$ has variance 1, so $\var(\qvec^\top\kvec) = d$. Dividing by $\sqrt d$
  brings the variance back to 1.
- **Embedding search.** Retrieval over text or image embeddings ranks candidates by cosine similarity, usually after
  normalising every embedding to unit length so that a dot product suffices.

## Pitfalls

- **Zero vectors.** Cosine similarity is undefined when either vector is $\zeros$. Implementations add a small constant
  to the denominator, which silently returns 0.
- **High dimensions.** Independent random vectors in high dimension are nearly orthogonal. For vectors with independent
  standard Gaussian entries, $\xvec^\top\yvec$ has variance $n$ and each norm is close to $\sqrt n$, so the cosine
  similarity concentrates around 0 with spread about $1/\sqrt{n}$. A cosine similarity of 0.1 can be meaningful in
  $\reals^{1000}$.
- **Mean offsets.** When all vectors share a large common component, every pair looks similar. Centring the vectors
  first removes it.
