<Definition>
The determinant $\det\Amat$ of $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the
columns of $\Amat$, the image of the unit cube. Its absolute value is the factor by which $\Amat$ scales every volume.
It is positive when $\Amat$ keeps orientation and negative when it reverses it. For $n = 2$,

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc
$$

<Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Signed area in two dimensions

The columns $\xvec = (a, c)$ and $\yvec = (b, d)$ span a parallelogram with area $\abs{ad - bc}$. The area is base times
height, $\norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the columns. Squaring it and using
$\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$ with $\xvec^\top\yvec = \norm{\xvec}\,\norm{\yvec}\cos\theta$ (see
<NoteLink to="dot-product">dot product</NoteLink>) gives

$$
\text{area}^2 = \norm{\xvec}^2\norm{\yvec}^2 - (\xvec^\top\yvec)^2 = (a^2 + c^2)(b^2 + d^2) - (ab + cd)^2 = (ad - bc)^2.
$$

The middle expansion has the terms $a^2b^2$ and $c^2d^2$ cancel, leaving $a^2d^2 + b^2c^2 - 2abcd$. So the area is
$\abs{ad - bc}$. The sign is positive when the second
column lies counterclockwise from the first, as $\evec_2$ does from $\evec_1$, and negative when it lies clockwise. A
negative determinant means the map includes a reflection: the image of a letter reads mirrored.

## Properties

These rules determine the determinant uniquely <Cite id="axler2015" />:

- **Multilinear:** it is linear in each column when the other columns are held fixed.
- **Alternating:** swapping two columns flips the sign. Two equal columns therefore give 0.
- **Normalised:** $\det\eye = 1$.

Everything else follows:

- $\det(\Amat\Bmat) = \det\Amat\,\det\Bmat$. Volume factors multiply under composition.
- $\det\Amat^\top = \det\Amat$, so the rules hold for rows as well as columns.
- $\det\Amat^{-1} = 1/\det\Amat$.
- $\det(\alpha\Amat) = \alpha^n\det\Amat$ for $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$.
- The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries.
- The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity (see
  <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>).
- $\det\Amat = 0$ if and only if the columns are <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">linearly
  dependent</NoteLink>, that is, $\rank\Amat < n$.

The last rule is the one used most, and it follows from the three defining rules directly. If the columns are dependent,
some column equals a combination $\sum_{k \neq j} c_k \avec_k$ of the others. Linearity in column $j$ splits the
determinant into a sum of determinants, each with column $\avec_k$ appearing twice. Each of those is 0 by the
alternating rule, so $\det\Amat = 0$. If the columns are independent, $\Amat$ is invertible, and the product rule gives
$\det\Amat\,\det\Amat^{-1} = \det\eye = 1$, so $\det\Amat \neq 0$.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the other properties">
**Product rule.** Fix $\Amat$ and consider $D(\Bmat) = \det(\Amat\Bmat)$ as a function of the columns $\bvec_j$ of
$\Bmat$. Column $j$ of $\Amat\Bmat$ is $\Amat\bvec_j$, which is linear in $\bvec_j$, so $D$ is multilinear. Swapping two
columns of $\Bmat$ swaps two columns of $\Amat\Bmat$, so $D$ is alternating. And $D(\eye) = \det\Amat$. The function
$\det\Amat\cdot\det\Bmat$ has the same three properties. The three rules fix a function up to its value at $\eye$, so
$D(\Bmat) = \det\Amat\,\det\Bmat$. The inverse rule follows by taking $\Bmat = \Amat^{-1}$.

**Scaling.** $\alpha\Amat$ multiplies each of the $n$ columns by $\alpha$. Linearity in each column contributes one
factor of $\alpha$, so $\det(\alpha\Amat) = \alpha^n\det\Amat$.

**Triangular matrices.** For an upper triangular $\Umat$, expand along the first column, whose only possibly nonzero
entry is $u_{11}$. The minor is again upper triangular, so induction gives $\det\Umat = \prod_i u_{ii}$. Lower
triangular matrices work the same way along the first row.

**Transpose.** Writing each column as a combination of standard basis vectors and expanding by multilinearity gives the
Leibniz formula $\det\Amat = \sum_{\sigma} \sgn(\sigma)\prod_{j} a_{\sigma(j) j}$, a sum over all permutations $\sigma$
of $\set{1, \dots, n}$. The terms with a repeated basis vector vanish by the alternating rule, and reordering the rest
to $\eye$ costs the sign of the permutation. Each term of $\det\Amat^\top$ is the term of $\det\Amat$ for $\sigma^{-1}$,
which has the same sign, so the sums are equal.

**Eigenvalues.** The characteristic polynomial $\det(\Amat - \lambda\eye)$ has the eigenvalues as roots and leading term
$(-\lambda)^n$, so it factors as $\prod_i (\lambda_i - \lambda)$ over the complex numbers. Setting $\lambda = 0$ gives
$\det\Amat = \prod_i \lambda_i$.
</Derivation>

## Computing it

Laplace (cofactor) expansion along the first row defines the determinant recursively,

$$
\det\Amat = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j} a_{1j} \det\Amat_{1j},
$$

where $\Amat_{1j}$ deletes row 1 and column $j$. For $n = 3$ this gives

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{bmatrix} = a(ei - fh) - b(di - fg) + c(dh - eg).
$$

Expansion costs on the order of $n!$ operations, which limits it to small $n$. Libraries factor
$\Amat = \Pmat\Lmat\Umat$ by Gaussian elimination in $O(n^3)$ operations (see
<NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">LU decomposition</NoteLink>). Then $\det\Amat = \pm\prod_i u_{ii}$ by the
product and triangular rules, with the sign set by the row permutation $\Pmat$ <Cite id="golub2013" />.

## Worked example

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
= 2(3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1) - 1(1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 0) + 0 = 10 - 2 = 8.
$$

The matrix scales volumes by 8 and keeps orientation. Replacing the last row by the sum of the first two makes the rows
dependent, and the determinant becomes 0.

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">Change of variables</NoteLink>.** If $\yvec = g(\xvec)$ for an invertible,
  differentiable $g$, densities transform as $p_Y(\yvec) = p_X(\xvec)\,\abs{\det \Jmat_g(\xvec)}^{-1}$, where $\Jmat_g$
  is the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>. The determinant is the local volume factor.
  <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">Normalising flows</NoteLink> are built from layers whose Jacobian determinant is
  cheap, usually because the Jacobian is triangular, so the determinant is the product of its diagonal entries.
- **Gaussian densities.** The <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> density
  contains the factor $(\det\Sigmamat)^{-1/2}$. The log-density needs $\log\det\Sigmamat = 2\sum_i \log l_{ii}$,
  computed from the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink> $\Sigmamat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$
  rather than as the logarithm of a determinant.

## Pitfalls

- **Small determinant does not mean nearly singular.** $\det(0.1\,\eye_{100}) = 10^{-100}$, yet $0.1\,\eye$ is perfectly
  conditioned. Scale changes the determinant enormously. Closeness to singularity is measured by the
  <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink>.
- **Overflow and underflow.** Products of many diagonal entries leave floating-point range quickly. Work with
  $\log\abs{\det\Amat} = \sum_i \log\abs{u_{ii}}$ and the sign separately, as `slogdet` does.
