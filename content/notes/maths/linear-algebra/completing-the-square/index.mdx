<Definition>
For $a \neq 0$,

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = a\paren{x + \frac{b}{2a}}^2 + c - \frac{b^2}{4a}.
$$

For a symmetric <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">invertible matrix</NoteLink> $\Amat$, a vector $\bvec$ and a scalar $c$,

$$
\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec - 2\bvec^\top\xvec + c = \paren{\xvec - \Amat^{-1}\bvec}^\top \Amat \paren{\xvec - \Amat^{-1}\bvec} + c -
\bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\bvec.
$$

Both rewrite a quadratic as a square centred at a shifted point plus a constant that does not depend on the variable.
</Definition>

## The geometric idea

The method is older than algebraic notation. Greek mathematics treated quantities as lengths and areas: a product of
two numbers was the area of a rectangle, and a square number was the area of a square. Euclid's *Elements*, Book II,
states the identity behind the method as a fact about areas: the square on a line cut into two parts equals the
squares on the parts plus twice the rectangle they contain, $(y + z)^2 = y^2 + 2yz + z^2$ <Cite id="euclid" />.
Al-Khwārizmī, writing in Baghdad around 820, used that picture to solve equations, and his example is still the
standard one <Cite id="khwarizmi1831" />.

**The problem.** Find $x$ such that

$$
x^2 + 10x = 39.
$$

The unknown terms stay on the left and the constant goes on the right, because the right-hand side is then a known
area and the left-hand side is a shape built from the unknown side $x$.

**The shape.** $x^2$ is a square of side $x$. $10x$ is a rectangle of sides $10$ and $x$. Cut the rectangle into two
strips of $5 \times x$ and lay one along the right side of the square and one along the top. The three pieces form an
L-shaped figure, called a gnomon, whose area is $39$.

**The missing corner.** The gnomon is a square of side $x + 5$ with its top-right corner missing. That corner is a
$5 \times 5$ square of area $25$. Adding it to the figure, and therefore to both sides of the equation, completes the
square:

$$
x^2 + 10x + 25 = 39 + 25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (x + 5)^2 = 64.
$$

**The answer.** A square of area $64$ has side $8$, so $x + 5 = 8$ and $x = 3$. Check: $9 + 30 = 39$.

The equation also has the root $x = -13$, since $(-13 + 5)^2 = 64$ as well. In the geometric reading a side cannot be
negative, so this root does not appear; the algebraic reading below recovers it.

**The general case.** The same steps solve $x^2 + bx = c$ for any $b$ and $c$. Split $bx$ into two strips of width
$b/2$, and add the missing corner $(b/2)^2$ to both sides:

$$
x^2 + bx + \paren{\frac{b}{2}}^2 = c + \paren{\frac{b}{2}}^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\paren{x + \frac{b}{2}}^2 = c + \frac{b^2}{4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x = -\frac{b}{2} \pm \sqrt{c + \frac{b^2}{4}}.
$$

Only the $+$ root is a length. Algebra takes both signs, and allows negative $b$ and $c$, which no area can
represent. For $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, divide by $a$ and move the constant across: $x^2 + (b/a)x = -c/a$. The general
case with $b/a$ and $-c/a$ in place of $b$ and $c$ gives $x = \paren{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}/2a$, the quadratic
formula.

## The identity

Algebra turns the construction into an identity that holds for every $x$, with no equation to solve. Factor out $a$,
then add and subtract the missing corner, the square of half the linear coefficient:

$$
ax^2 + bx + c = a\paren{x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x} + c = a\paren{x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2}} - \frac{b^2}{4a} + c =
a\paren{x + \frac{b}{2a}}^2 + c - \frac{b^2}{4a}.
$$

The corner $b^2/4a^2$ is what makes the bracket exactly $(x + b/2a)^2$, by Euclid's identity with $y = x$ and
$z = b/2a$. It is added inside and subtracted outside, so the value is unchanged.

The square is never negative, so for $a > 0$ the minimum is
at $x = -b/2a$, with value $c - b^2/4a$: the vertex of the parabola.

**Worked example.** $2x^2 - 8x + 3 = 2(x^2 - 4x) + 3 = 2(x^2 - 4x + 4) - 8 + 3 = 2(x - 2)^2 - 5$. The minimum is $-5$,
at $x = 2$.

## The matrix case

Expand the right-hand side of the matrix identity. With $\mvec = \Amat^{-1}\bvec$,

$$
(\xvec - \mvec)^\top\Amat(\xvec - \mvec) = \xvec^\top\Amat\xvec - \xvec^\top\Amat\mvec - \mvec^\top\Amat\xvec +
\mvec^\top\Amat\mvec.
$$

$\Amat$ is symmetric, so $\mvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \xvec^\top\Amat\mvec = \xvec^\top\bvec$, and the two middle terms
together are $-2\bvec^\top\xvec$. The last term is $\bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\Amat\Amat^{-1}\bvec = \bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\bvec$,
which the identity subtracts again. The scalar case is the $1 \times 1$ version, with $\Amat = a$ and $\bvec = -b/2$.

When $\Amat$ is positive definite, the square term is positive except at $\xvec = \Amat^{-1}\bvec$. That point is the
unique minimiser, and the minimum value is $c - \bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\bvec$. No <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is needed.

**Worked example.** Take $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\bvec = (1, 1)$. Then
$\Amat^{-1}\bvec = (1/3, 1/3)$ and $\bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\bvec = 2/3$, so

$$
\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec - 2\bvec^\top\xvec = \paren{\xvec - \tfrac{1}{3}\ones}^\top \Amat \paren{\xvec - \tfrac{1}{3}\ones} -
\tfrac{2}{3}.
$$

The minimum is $-2/3$, at $\xvec = (1/3, 1/3)$.

## Where it is used

- **Gaussian integrals.** The exponent $-\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec + \bvec^\top\xvec$ completes to
  $-\tfrac12(\xvec - \Amat^{-1}\bvec)^\top\Amat(\xvec - \Amat^{-1}\bvec) + \tfrac12\bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\bvec$. The shift does
  not change the integral, so
  $\int \exp(-\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec + \bvec^\top\xvec)\,d\xvec = (2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\Amat)^{-1/2}\exp(\tfrac12\bvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\bvec)$.
  See the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>.
- **Gaussian posteriors.** A product of Gaussian densities in $\xvec$ has an exponent that is quadratic in $\xvec$.
  Collecting the quadratic term gives $\Amat$, the posterior precision, and collecting the linear term gives $\bvec$.
  Completing the square then reads off the posterior mean $\Amat^{-1}\bvec$ and covariance $\Amat^{-1}$ without
  computing any normalising constant <Cite id="bishop2006" />.
- **Least squares.** $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 = \wvec^\top\Xmat^\top\Xmat\wvec - 2(\Xmat^\top\yvec)^\top\wvec +
  \yvec^\top\yvec$. With $\Amat = \Xmat^\top\Xmat$ and $\bvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec$, the minimiser is
  $(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$, the normal equations of <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear
  regression</NoteLink>.
- **Block matrices.** Completing the square in one block of variables of a joint <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> produces the
  <NoteLink to="schur-complement">Schur complement</NoteLink>. This is how the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distributions</NoteLink> of a
  <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> are derived.

## Pitfalls

- **Symmetry.** The matrix identity uses $\Amat = \Amat^\top$. For a nonsymmetric $\Amat$, replace it by
  $(\Amat + \Amat^\top)/2$ first; the quadratic form $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec$ is unchanged by this.
- **Sign conventions.** Some texts write the linear term as $+\bvec^\top\xvec$ or $-\bvec^\top\xvec$ rather than
  $-2\bvec^\top\xvec$. The centre is then $\pm\tfrac12\Amat^{-1}\bvec$. Check which convention is in use before reading
  off a mean.
- **Indefinite $\Amat$.** The identity holds for any symmetric invertible $\Amat$, but only a positive definite
  $\Amat$ gives a minimum. An indefinite one gives a saddle at $\Amat^{-1}\bvec$.
