<Definition>
A symmetric matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is **positive definite** (PD) if $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec > 0$ for every
nonzero $\xvec \in \reals^n$. It is **positive semi-definite** (PSD) if $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec \ge 0$ for every
$\xvec$. The notation is $\Amat \succ 0$ for PD and $\Amat \succeq 0$ for PSD <Cite id="horn2012" />.
</Definition>

Every PD matrix is PSD. The converse fails: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ is PSD but not PD, since
$\xvec = (0, 1)$ gives $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = 0$.

## The eigenvalue test

The sign of the quadratic form is decided by the eigenvalues. The <NoteLink to="spectral-theorem">spectral
theorem</NoteLink> writes a symmetric matrix as $\Amat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top$, with $\Qmat$ orthogonal and
$\Lambdamat = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. In the rotated coordinates $\yvec = \Qmat^\top\xvec$,

$$
\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \xvec^\top\Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top\xvec = \yvec^\top\Lambdamat\yvec = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i y_i^2.
$$

$\Qmat$ is invertible, so $\yvec$ runs over every nonzero vector as $\xvec$ does. If every $\lambda_i > 0$, the sum is
positive for every nonzero $\yvec$, so $\Amat \succ 0$. If some $\lambda_k \le 0$, the eigenvector $\yvec = \evec_k$
gives $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \lambda_k \le 0$, so $\Amat$ is not PD. The same argument with $\ge$ in place of $>$ gives
the PSD test.

With all $\lambda_i > 0$ the form is a bowl, and its level sets $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = 1$ are ellipsoids with semi-axes
$1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ along the eigenvectors. A zero eigenvalue flattens the bowl into a trough. Eigenvalues of both
signs make a saddle, and the matrix is called **indefinite**.

## Equivalent tests

For a symmetric $\Amat$, each of the following is equivalent to $\Amat \succ 0$ <Cite id="horn2012" />:

1. **Eigenvalues.** Every eigenvalue of $\Amat$ is positive. For PSD, every eigenvalue is non-negative.
2. **Leading principal minors** (Sylvester's criterion). The determinants of the top-left $1 \times 1$, $2 \times 2$,
   …, $n \times n$ submatrices are all positive.
3. **Cholesky factor.** $\Amat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$ (the
   <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky decomposition</NoteLink>) for a lower-triangular $\Lmat$ with a
   positive diagonal.
4. **Gram matrix.** $\Amat = \Bmat^\top\Bmat$ for some $\Bmat$ with linearly independent columns. For PSD, any $\Bmat$.

Test 1 is proved above. Test 4 follows from $\xvec^\top\Bmat^\top\Bmat\xvec = \norm{\Bmat\xvec}^2$, which is never
negative and is zero only when $\Bmat\xvec = \zeros$. With independent columns that forces $\xvec = \zeros$. Conversely,
a PD matrix is $\Bmat^\top\Bmat$ with $\Bmat = \Lambdamat^{1/2}\Qmat^\top$, whose columns are independent. Test 3 is the
special case of test 4 with $\Bmat = \Lmat^\top$.

<Derivation title="Why Sylvester's criterion works">
**Necessity.** Let $\Amat_k$ be the top-left $k \times k$ block. For $\xvec$ whose last $n - k$ entries are zero,
$\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \xvec_k^\top\Amat_k\xvec_k$, where $\xvec_k$ holds the first $k$ entries. So $\Amat_k$ is PD
too. Its determinant is the product of its eigenvalues, which are all positive, so $\det\Amat_k > 0$.

**Sufficiency.** Gaussian elimination without row swaps factors a symmetric matrix as $\Amat = \Lmat\Dmat\Lmat^\top$,
with $\Lmat$ unit lower triangular and $\Dmat$ diagonal, provided every pivot is nonzero. Elimination on the first $k$
rows only involves $\Amat_k$, so $\det\Amat_k = d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$. Positive leading minors therefore give positive
pivots, $d_k = \det\Amat_k / \det\Amat_{k-1} > 0$. With $\zvec = \Lmat^\top\xvec$, which is nonzero whenever $\xvec$ is,
$\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \zvec^\top\Dmat\zvec = \sum_k d_k z_k^2 > 0$.
</Derivation>

The minor test does not carry over to PSD directly. $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ has leading minors
$0$ and $0$, yet it is not PSD. Semi-definiteness needs every principal minor, not only the leading ones, to be
non-negative.

Singular values do not reveal definiteness. They are never negative, so
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ has singular values $1, 1$ although it is indefinite. The signs of the
eigenvalues decide.

## Worked example

For $2 \times 2$ matrices $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$, Sylvester's criterion reads $a > 0$ and
$ac - b^2 > 0$. For example, $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has minors $2$ and $3$ and eigenvalues $3$
and $1$, so it is PD. $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has minors $1$ and $-3$ and eigenvalues $3$ and
$-1$, so it is indefinite.

## Where they appear

- **<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">Covariance matrices</NoteLink>** are PSD:
  $\wvec^\top\Sigmamat\wvec = \var(\wvec^\top\xvec) \ge 0$. They are PD unless some
  linear combination of the variables is constant.
- **Gram and kernel matrices** $K_{ij} = k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$ are PSD for every valid kernel.
- **Hessians and convexity.** A twice-differentiable function is convex exactly when its Hessian is PSD everywhere
  <Cite id="boyd2004" />. A PD Hessian everywhere is sufficient for strict convexity but not necessary: $f(x) = x^4$ is
  strictly convex with $f''(0) = 0$. At a critical point, a PD Hessian certifies a strict local minimum and an
  indefinite Hessian certifies a saddle.
- **Regularisation.** $\Amat + \lambda\eye$ with $\lambda > 0$ is PD whenever $\Amat$ is PSD, since every eigenvalue
  shifts up by $\lambda$. Ridge regression and jitter added to kernel matrices use this.

## Properties

- A PD matrix is invertible, and its inverse is PD.
- Sums of PSD matrices are PSD. If one term is PD, the sum is PD.
- $\Bmat^\top\Amat\Bmat$ is PSD whenever $\Amat$ is. It is PD when $\Amat$ is PD and $\Bmat$ has independent columns.
- The diagonal entries of a PD matrix are positive, and $\abs{a_{ij}} < \sqrt{a_{ii}a_{jj}}$ for $i \ne j$.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the properties">
**Inverse.** A PD matrix has positive eigenvalues, so none is zero and it is invertible. $\Amat^{-1} =
\Qmat\Lambdamat^{-1}\Qmat^\top$ has eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i > 0$.

**Sums.** $\xvec^\top(\Amat + \Bmat)\xvec = \xvec^\top\Amat\xvec + \xvec^\top\Bmat\xvec$, a sum of nonnegative terms,
and positive when one term is positive.

**Congruence.** $\xvec^\top\Bmat^\top\Amat\Bmat\xvec = (\Bmat\xvec)^\top\Amat(\Bmat\xvec) \ge 0$. When $\Amat \succ 0$
and $\Bmat$ has independent columns, $\Bmat\xvec \neq \zeros$ for $\xvec \neq \zeros$, so the form is positive.

**Entries.** $\evec_i^\top\Amat\evec_i = a_{ii} > 0$. The $2 \times 2$ principal submatrix on rows and columns $i$ and
$j$ is PD by the necessity argument above, so its determinant $a_{ii}a_{jj} - a_{ij}^2$ is positive.

**Regularisation.** $\Amat + \lambda\eye = \Qmat(\Lambdamat + \lambda\eye)\Qmat^\top$ has eigenvalues
$\lambda_i + \lambda$.
</Derivation>

## Pitfalls

- **Symmetry is part of the definition.** For a non-symmetric $\Mmat$, only its symmetric part affects the form:
  $\xvec^\top\Mmat\xvec = \xvec^\top\tfrac{1}{2}(\Mmat + \Mmat^\top)\xvec$. Test that part, not the eigenvalues of
  $\Mmat$.
- **Numerical PSD.** Round-off can make a PSD matrix show tiny negative eigenvalues. Adding a small multiple of the
  identity, often called jitter, restores positive definiteness before a Cholesky factorisation.
- **Positive entries are neither necessary nor sufficient.** $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has
  positive entries and is indefinite; $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has a negative entry and is PD.
