<Definition>
Given linearly independent vectors $\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_k \in \reals^n$, the Gram–Schmidt process computes, for
$j = 1, \dots, k$,

$$
\wvec_j = \vvec_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} (\qvec_i^\top\vvec_j)\, \qvec_i, \qquad \qvec_j = \frac{\wvec_j}{\norm{\wvec_j}}.
$$

The vectors $\qvec_1, \dots, \qvec_k$ are orthonormal, and for every $j$ the first $j$ of them span the same subspace as
$\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_j$ <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
</Definition>

## Mechanism

Each step removes from $\vvec_j$ its <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> onto the
span of the earlier $\qvec_i$. Because those $\qvec_i$ are orthonormal, the projection is the sum of the
one-dimensional projections $(\qvec_i^\top\vvec_j)\qvec_i$. Scaling what remains to unit length gives $\qvec_j$.

## Why the result is orthonormal

The proof is by induction on $j$. Suppose $\qvec_1, \dots, \qvec_{j-1}$ are orthonormal, so that
$\qvec_l^\top\qvec_i = 1$ if $l = i$ and 0 otherwise. Take the <NoteLink to="dot-product">dot product</NoteLink> of
$\wvec_j$ with any earlier $\qvec_l$:

$$
\qvec_l^\top\wvec_j = \qvec_l^\top\vvec_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} (\qvec_i^\top\vvec_j)\, \qvec_l^\top\qvec_i
= \qvec_l^\top\vvec_j - \qvec_l^\top\vvec_j = 0.
$$

Only the $i = l$ term of the sum survives, and it cancels the first term exactly. So $\wvec_j$ is orthogonal to every
earlier $\qvec_l$, and so is $\qvec_j = \wvec_j/\norm{\wvec_j}$, which has unit length by construction.

$\wvec_j$ is never zero, so the division is safe. If it were zero, $\vvec_j$ would equal
$\sum_{i<j}(\qvec_i^\top\vvec_j)\qvec_i$, a combination of $\qvec_1, \dots, \qvec_{j-1}$, and so of
$\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_{j-1}$. That contradicts independence.

## Why the span is preserved

Also by induction, $\Span\set{\qvec_1, \dots, \qvec_j} = \Span\set{\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_j}$. Each $\qvec_j$ is
$\vvec_j$ minus a combination of earlier $\qvec_i$, which are combinations of earlier $\vvec_i$, all divided by a
nonzero number. So $\qvec_j$ lies in the span of $\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_j$. Conversely, the definition rearranges to
$\vvec_j = \norm{\wvec_j}\qvec_j + \sum_{i<j} (\qvec_i^\top\vvec_j)\qvec_i$, so $\vvec_j$ lies in the span of
$\qvec_1, \dots, \qvec_j$. The two spans contain each other, so they are equal.

## Worked example

Take $\vvec_1 = (3, 4)$ and $\vvec_2 = (1, 2)$ in $\reals^2$.

1. $\norm{\vvec_1} = 5$, so $\qvec_1 = (0.6, 0.8)$.
2. $\qvec_1^\top\vvec_2 = 0.6 + 1.6 = 2.2$, so $\wvec_2 = (1, 2) - 2.2\,(0.6, 0.8) = (-0.32, 0.24)$.
3. $\norm{\wvec_2} = 0.4$, so $\qvec_2 = (-0.8, 0.6)$.

The check $\qvec_1^\top\qvec_2 = -0.48 + 0.48 = 0$ confirms orthogonality.

## Relation to QR

Collect the coefficients as $r_{ij} = \qvec_i^\top\vvec_j$ for $i < j$ and $r_{jj} = \norm{\wvec_j}$. Rearranging the
definition gives $\vvec_j = \sum_{i \le j} r_{ij}\qvec_i$. In matrix form this is $\Amat = \Qmat\Rmat$, with
$\Amat = [\vvec_1 \cdots \vvec_k]$, $\Qmat = [\qvec_1 \cdots \qvec_k]$ and $\Rmat$ upper triangular with a positive
diagonal. This is the <NoteLink to="qr-decomposition">QR decomposition</NoteLink>. In the example above,
$\Rmat = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2.2 \\ 0 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix}$.

## Classical and modified variants

The formula above is **classical** Gram–Schmidt: every projection coefficient uses the original $\vvec_j$.
**Modified** Gram–Schmidt subtracts the projections one at a time, and computes each coefficient from the partially
reduced vector:

$$
\wvec \leftarrow \vvec_j, \qquad
\text{for } i = 1, \dots, j - 1: \quad \wvec \leftarrow \wvec - (\qvec_i^\top\wvec)\,\qvec_i.
$$

In exact arithmetic the two are identical. In floating point, classical Gram–Schmidt can lose orthogonality badly when
the input vectors are nearly dependent. The loss in modified Gram–Schmidt is proportional to the condition number of
$\Amat$ <Cite id="bjorck1967" />. Householder reflections give orthogonality to machine precision and are the standard
method for computing QR in libraries <Cite id="golub2013" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Nearly dependent inputs.** A small $\norm{\wvec_j}$ means $\vvec_j$ is almost in the span of the earlier vectors.
  Dividing by it amplifies rounding errors. Re-orthogonalising, that is running the projection loop twice, restores
  orthogonality in practice.
- **Order matters.** The first vector fixes $\qvec_1$. Reordering the inputs gives a different orthonormal basis of the
  same subspace.
- **Dependent inputs.** If the vectors are dependent, some $\wvec_j$ is zero and the division fails. Skipping such
  vectors yields an orthonormal basis of the span, with fewer vectors than inputs.
