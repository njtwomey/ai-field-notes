<Definition>
If $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is symmetric, $\Amat^\top = \Amat$, then there is an orthogonal matrix $\Qmat$,
$\Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$, and a real diagonal matrix $\Lambdamat$ such that

$$
\Amat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \qvec_i\qvec_i^\top.
$$

The columns $\qvec_i$ of $\Qmat$ are orthonormal eigenvectors and the $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues
<Cite id="horn2012" />.
</Definition>

## What it says

A general square matrix may have complex eigenvalues, too few eigenvectors, or eigenvectors at awkward angles. A
symmetric matrix has none of these problems:

1. **Real eigenvalues.** If $\Amat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$ with complex $\vvec$, then
   $\bar\vvec^\top\Amat\vvec = \lambda\,\bar\vvec^\top\vvec = \lambda\norm{\vvec}^2$, where
   $\norm{\vvec}^2 = \sum_i \abs{v_i}^2 > 0$. The left side is its own complex conjugate: conjugating gives
   $\vvec^\top\Amat\bar\vvec$, a scalar, which equals its transpose
   $\bar\vvec^\top\Amat^\top\vvec = \bar\vvec^\top\Amat\vvec$ because $\Amat$ is real and symmetric. So
   $\lambda\norm{\vvec}^2$ is real, and so is $\lambda$.
2. **Orthogonal eigenvectors.** For $\Amat\vvec_i = \lambda_i\vvec_i$ and $\Amat\vvec_j = \lambda_j\vvec_j$ with
   $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_j$, symmetry gives $\lambda_j\vvec_i^\top\vvec_j = \vvec_i^\top\Amat\vvec_j =
   (\Amat\vvec_i)^\top\vvec_j = \lambda_i\vvec_i^\top\vvec_j$, so $\vvec_i^\top\vvec_j = 0$.
3. **A complete basis.** Even with repeated eigenvalues there are $n$ orthonormal eigenvectors. This is the part that
   needs a real proof, given below. It implies that each eigenspace has dimension equal to the eigenvalue's
   multiplicity.

The sum form writes $\Amat$ as a weighted sum of projections $\qvec_i\qvec_i^\top$ onto perpendicular lines. $\Amat$
therefore stretches space by $\lambda_i$ along each axis $\qvec_i$ and does nothing else.

## Proof of existence

The proof builds the orthonormal eigenvectors one at a time, each as the maximiser of a quadratic form.

Define the Rayleigh quotient $R(\xvec) = \xvec^\top\Amat\xvec / \xvec^\top\xvec$ for $\xvec \ne \zeros$. It is
continuous on the unit sphere, which is closed and bounded, so it attains a maximum at some unit vector $\qvec_1$. At a
maximum of $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec$ subject to $\xvec^\top\xvec = 1$, the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the objective is parallel to the
gradient of the constraint. The gradient of $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec$ is $(\Amat + \Amat^\top)\xvec = 2\Amat\xvec$, which
uses the symmetry, and the gradient of $\xvec^\top\xvec$ is $2\xvec$. So there is a <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> $\lambda_1$ with

$$
\Amat\qvec_1 = \lambda_1\qvec_1, \qquad \lambda_1 = \qvec_1^\top\Amat\qvec_1 = R(\qvec_1),
$$

and $\qvec_1$ is a unit eigenvector with a real eigenvalue.

Next, the subspace $\qvec_1^\perp$ of vectors orthogonal to $\qvec_1$ is mapped into itself by $\Amat$. If
$\qvec_1^\top\xvec = 0$, then, again by symmetry,

$$
\qvec_1^\top\Amat\xvec = (\Amat\qvec_1)^\top\xvec = \lambda_1\qvec_1^\top\xvec = 0.
$$

So $\Amat$ restricted to $\qvec_1^\perp$ is a symmetric map on an $(n-1)$-dimensional space. Maximising $R$ over unit
vectors in $\qvec_1^\perp$ gives a second unit eigenvector $\qvec_2$, orthogonal to $\qvec_1$. Repeating $n$ times gives
orthonormal eigenvectors $\qvec_1, \dots, \qvec_n$. Collected as the columns of $\Qmat$, they satisfy
$\Amat\Qmat = \Qmat\Lambdamat$, and $\Qmat^{-1} = \Qmat^\top$ turns this into $\Amat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top$.

Multiplying out the columns gives the sum form: $\Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top = \sum_i \lambda_i\qvec_i\qvec_i^\top$.

## Variational characterisation

The proof gives more than existence. With eigenvalues ordered $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$,

$$
\lambda_1 = \max_{\xvec \ne \zeros} \frac{\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec}{\xvec^\top\xvec}, \qquad
\lambda_n = \min_{\xvec \ne \zeros} \frac{\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec}{\xvec^\top\xvec},
$$

attained at $\qvec_1$ and $\qvec_n$. In the eigenvector coordinates $\yvec = \Qmat^\top\xvec$ the quotient is a weighted
average of the eigenvalues,

$$
R(\xvec) = \frac{\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2}{\sum_i y_i^2},
$$

with weights $y_i^2/\sum_j y_j^2$ that are nonnegative and sum to 1. A weighted average lies between the smallest and
largest values, and puts all its weight on $\lambda_1$ exactly when $\yvec$ points along $\evec_1$. The Courant–Fischer
theorem extends this to every $\lambda_k$ as a min–max over subspaces <Cite id="horn2012" />. <Gloss name="pca">Principal component
analysis</Gloss> uses the first statement directly: the unit direction that maximises the variance $\wvec^\top\Sigmamat\wvec$ is
the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix $\Sigmamat$.

## Worked example

$\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 3 and 1 with unit eigenvectors
$\qvec_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, 1)$ and $\qvec_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1, -1)$. The sum form is

$$
\Amat = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1
\\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The Rayleigh quotient at $\xvec = (1, 0)$ is $2$, which lies between 1 and 3, as it must.

## Consequences

- **Functions of a matrix.** $f(\Amat) = \Qmat f(\Lambdamat)\Qmat^\top$ for any function $f$ applied to the
  eigenvalues. Examples are $\Amat^{-1}$, $\Amat^{1/2}$ for PSD $\Amat$, and $\exp(\Amat)$.
- **Definiteness.** $\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec = \sum_i \lambda_i (\qvec_i^\top\xvec)^2$. The signs of the eigenvalues
  decide whether $\Amat$ is <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite</NoteLink>, semi-definite or
  indefinite.
- **SVD of a symmetric matrix.** Its singular values are $\abs{\lambda_i}$. For a PSD matrix the eigendecomposition
  and the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">SVD</NoteLink> are the same factorisation.
- **Stable computation.** Symmetric eigensolvers, such as the symmetric <Gloss name="qr" /> algorithm and Lanczos iteration, keep
  orthogonality and compute eigenvalues to high relative accuracy <Cite id="golub2013" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Repeated eigenvalues.** The eigenvectors for a repeated eigenvalue are not unique. Any orthonormal basis of the
  eigenspace works, so two libraries can return different vectors for the same matrix.
- **Near-symmetric input.** Round-off can make a matrix that should be symmetric slightly asymmetric. Symmetrise it as
  $\tfrac{1}{2}(\Amat + \Amat^\top)$ before calling a symmetric solver, which assumes exact symmetry.
- **Complex matrices.** The theorem holds for Hermitian matrices, $\Amat^* = \Amat$, with a unitary $\Qmat$. A
  complex symmetric matrix, $\Amat^\top = \Amat$ but not Hermitian, need not be diagonalisable by a unitary matrix.
