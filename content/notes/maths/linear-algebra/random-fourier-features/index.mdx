<Definition>
For a shift-invariant kernel $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \kappa(\xvec - \xvec')$ on $\reals^n$, draw frequencies
$\omegavec_1, \dots, \omegavec_D$ from the kernel's spectral density and phases $b_1, \dots, b_D$ uniformly from
$[0, 2\pi)$. The random Fourier feature map is

$$
\zvec(\xvec) = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{D}}\,\paren{\cos(\omegavec_1^\top\xvec + b_1), \dots, \cos(\omegavec_D^\top\xvec + b_D)},
$$

and $\zvec(\xvec)^\top\zvec(\xvec')$ is an unbiased estimate of $k(\xvec, \xvec')$ <Cite id="rahimi2007" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">kernel trick</NoteLink> works with the $N \times N$ Gram matrix, which costs $O(N^2)$
memory and $O(N^3)$ time to solve with. For large $N$ this is prohibitive. A finite feature map whose <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink>
approximate the kernel lets a linear model be fitted instead, at cost $O(ND^2)$ for a least-squares fit with $D$
features.

## Mechanism

Bochner's theorem states that a continuous shift-invariant kernel with $\kappa(\zeros) = 1$ is positive definite
exactly when $\kappa$ is the <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> of a probability distribution $p(\omegavec)$:

$$
\kappa(\xvec - \xvec') = \int p(\omegavec)\, e^{i\omegavec^\top(\xvec - \xvec')}\, d\omegavec
= \expect_{\omegavec}\brack{\cos\paren{\omegavec^\top(\xvec - \xvec')}}.
$$

The second form holds because a real kernel is symmetric, $\kappa(\deltab) = \kappa(-\deltab)$, so the imaginary parts
$\sin(\omegavec^\top\deltab)$ average to zero. A kernel is therefore an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, and a Monte Carlo average over $D$
sampled frequencies estimates it.

## Why the estimate is unbiased

Fix one frequency $\omegavec$ and phase $b$, and write $u = \omegavec^\top\xvec + b$ and $v = \omegavec^\top\xvec' + b$.
The product-to-sum identity gives

$$
2\cos u \cos v = \cos(u - v) + \cos(u + v) = \cos\paren{\omegavec^\top(\xvec - \xvec')} + \cos\paren{\omegavec^\top(\xvec + \xvec') + 2b}.
$$

The phase has cancelled from the first term. In the second, $2b$ is uniform over a whole number of periods, so the
cosine averages to zero over $b$. Hence

$$
\expect_{b}\brack{2\cos(\omegavec^\top\xvec + b)\cos(\omegavec^\top\xvec' + b)} = \cos\paren{\omegavec^\top(\xvec - \xvec')},
$$

and averaging over $\omegavec$ as well gives $\kappa(\xvec - \xvec')$ by Bochner's theorem. The feature inner product is

$$
\zvec(\xvec)^\top\zvec(\xvec') = \frac{1}{D}\sum_{j=1}^{D} 2\cos(\omegavec_j^\top\xvec + b_j)\cos(\omegavec_j^\top\xvec' + b_j),
$$

an average of $D$ independent copies of the same quantity. Its expectation is therefore $k(\xvec, \xvec')$. Each term
lies in $[-2, 2]$, so its variance is at most 4 and the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of the average is at most $2/\sqrt D$.

For the <Gloss name="rbf-kernel">Gaussian kernel</Gloss> $\exp(-\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2 / 2\ell^2)$, the spectral density is itself Gaussian,
$\omegavec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \ell^{-2}\eye)$. This is the <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">characteristic function</NoteLink> of a Gaussian: for
$\omegavec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \ell^{-2}\eye)$, $\omegavec^\top\deltab$ is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance
$\norm{\deltab}^2/\ell^2$, and $\expect[\cos t] = e^{-\sigma^2/2}$ for $t \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. Narrow kernels,
with small $\ell$, need high frequencies.

## Cost

Each estimate averages $D$ bounded terms, so its standard deviation falls like $1/\sqrt D$, as shown above. The uniform
error over a compact set also shrinks at this rate, up to logarithmic factors <Cite id="rahimi2007" />. Computing the
features costs $O(NDn)$, and a linear model on them costs $O(ND^2)$ to fit and $O(Dn)$ per prediction. The approximation
pays off when $D$ can be much smaller than $N$.

## Trade-offs

- **Accuracy against size.** Halving the error needs four times as many features. Methods that adapt to the data, such
  as the Nyström approximation, are often more accurate for the same $D$ <Cite id="hofmann2008" />.
- **Shift-invariant kernels only.** The construction needs $k$ to depend on $\xvec - \xvec'$. Polynomial kernels need
  other random feature schemes.
- **Randomness.** Different draws give different models. Fix the seed for reproducibility.
- **Uses.** Scalable <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge regression</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="kernel-support-vector-machine">kernel support vector machines</NoteLink> and <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss>, each fitted as a
  linear model on $\zvec(\xvec)$.
