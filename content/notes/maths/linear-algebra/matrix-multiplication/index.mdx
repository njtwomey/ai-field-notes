<Definition>
For $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ and $\Bmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$, the product
$\Amat\Bmat \in \reals^{m \times p}$ has entries

$$
(\Amat\Bmat)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}.
$$

It is the unique matrix with $(\Amat\Bmat)\xvec = \Amat(\Bmat\xvec)$ for every $\xvec \in \reals^p$: the matrix of the
map that applies $\Bmat$ first and $\Amat$ second <Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Where the formula comes from

The entry formula is forced by the requirement that the product represent composition. Apply $\Bmat$ to $\xvec$, then
$\Amat$ to the result, and expand both matrix–vector products:

$$
\paren{\Amat(\Bmat\xvec)}_i = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}\,(\Bmat\xvec)_k = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik} \sum_{j=1}^p b_{kj} x_j
= \sum_{j=1}^p \paren{\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik} b_{kj}} x_j.
$$

The last expression is a matrix–vector product with the matrix whose entry $(i, j)$ is $\sum_k a_{ik}b_{kj}$. That
matrix is $\Amat\Bmat$. It is the only matrix that works: a matrix is determined by what it does to the standard basis
vectors, and taking $\xvec = \evec_j$ above picks out column $j$ of this one.

## Four ways to read the product

Write $\avec_i^\top$ for the rows of $\Amat$ and $\bvec_j$ for the columns of $\Bmat$.

| View           | Formula                                          | Reading                                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| entries        | $(\Amat\Bmat)_{ij} = \avec_i^\top\bvec_j$        | one dot product per entry                              |
| columns        | column $j$ of $\Amat\Bmat$ is $\Amat\bvec_j$     | $\Amat$ applied to each column of $\Bmat$              |
| rows           | row $i$ of $\Amat\Bmat$ is $\avec_i^\top\Bmat$   | each row of $\Amat$ combines the rows of $\Bmat$       |
| outer products | $\Amat\Bmat = \sum_{k=1}^n \cvec_k \rvec_k^\top$ | column $k$ of $\Amat$ times row $k$ of $\Bmat$, summed |

In the last row, $\cvec_k$ is column $k$ of $\Amat$ and $\rvec_k^\top$ is row $k$ of $\Bmat$. Each term
$\cvec_k\rvec_k^\top$ is an $m \times p$ matrix of rank at most one. All four views are the same sum
$\sum_k a_{ik}b_{kj}$ grouped differently. The outer product $\cvec_k\rvec_k^\top$ has entry $(i, j)$ equal to
$a_{ik}b_{kj}$, so summing the $n$ outer products adds up exactly the terms of the entry formula. The outer-product view
explains the rank bound below and the low-rank sums in the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value
decomposition</NoteLink>.

## Composition

The inner dimensions must agree because the output of $\Bmat$, a vector in $\reals^n$, is the input of $\Amat$. The
product reads right to left, like function composition: $\Amat\Bmat\xvec$ means apply $\Bmat$, then $\Amat$.

## Algebraic rules

- **Associative:** $(\Amat\Bmat)\Cmat = \Amat(\Bmat\Cmat)$. Composition of maps is associative, so the product is too.
- **Distributive:** $\Amat(\Bmat + \Cmat) = \Amat\Bmat + \Amat\Cmat$ and
  $(\Amat + \Bmat)\Cmat = \Amat\Cmat + \Bmat\Cmat$.
- **Not commutative:** $\Amat\Bmat \neq \Bmat\Amat$ in general, even when both products are defined. Rotating then
  stretching is not stretching then rotating.
- **Transpose reverses order:** $(\Amat\Bmat)^\top = \Bmat^\top\Amat^\top$. The same reversal holds for inverses,
  $(\Amat\Bmat)^{-1} = \Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}$.
- **Identity:** $\eye\Amat = \Amat\eye = \Amat$ with identities of the matching sizes.
- **Rank:** $\rank(\Amat\Bmat) \le \min(\rank\Amat, \rank\Bmat)$. A product can never recover dimensions that a factor
  has lost.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the rules">
**Associativity.** $\paren{(\Amat\Bmat)\Cmat}\xvec = (\Amat\Bmat)(\Cmat\xvec) = \Amat\paren{\Bmat(\Cmat\xvec)}$ by the
defining property applied twice, and
$\paren{\Amat(\Bmat\Cmat)}\xvec = \Amat\paren{(\Bmat\Cmat)\xvec} = \Amat\paren{\Bmat(\Cmat\xvec)}$ in the same way. The
two matrices agree on every $\xvec$, so they are equal.

**Distributivity.** Entry $(i, j)$ of $\Amat(\Bmat + \Cmat)$ is $\sum_k a_{ik}(b_{kj} + c_{kj})$, which splits into the
entries of $\Amat\Bmat$ and $\Amat\Cmat$. The other side is the same computation.

**Transpose.** Entry $(i, j)$ of $(\Amat\Bmat)^\top$ is entry $(j, i)$ of $\Amat\Bmat$, which is $\sum_k a_{jk}b_{ki}$.
Entry $(i, j)$ of $\Bmat^\top\Amat^\top$ is $\sum_k (\Bmat^\top)_{ik}(\Amat^\top)_{kj} = \sum_k b_{ki}a_{jk}$. The sums
are equal.

**Inverse.** $(\Amat\Bmat)(\Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}) = \Amat(\Bmat\Bmat^{-1})\Amat^{-1} = \Amat\Amat^{-1} = \eye$ by
associativity, so $\Bmat^{-1}\Amat^{-1}$ is the inverse of $\Amat\Bmat$. Undoing a composition undoes the last step
first.

**Rank.** Every column $\Amat\bvec_j$ of $\Amat\Bmat$ lies in the column space of $\Amat$, so
$\rank(\Amat\Bmat) \le \rank\Amat$. Every row $\avec_i^\top\Bmat$ is a combination of the rows of $\Bmat$, so
$\rank(\Amat\Bmat) \le \rank\Bmat$, using row rank equals column rank.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Bmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

$\Amat$ doubles the first coordinate and $\Bmat$ rotates by $90°$ counterclockwise. Then

$$
\Amat\Bmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Bmat\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

$\Amat\Bmat$ rotates first, so the doubling acts on the rotated first coordinate and $\Amat\Bmat\evec_2 = (-2, 0)$.
$\Bmat\Amat$ doubles first and then rotates, giving $\Bmat\Amat\evec_2 = (-1, 0)$.

## Cost

The definition takes $mnp$ multiplications, or $n^3$ for two $n \times n$ matrices. Strassen-type algorithms lower the
exponent below 3, but blocked implementations of the definition are faster for the sizes used in practice. Their speed
comes from reusing each block of the inputs while it sits in cache <Cite id="golub2013" />.

The order of a chain matters. For $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$, $\Bmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ and a vector
$\xvec \in \reals^p$, computing $\Amat(\Bmat\xvec)$ costs $np + mn$ operations, while $(\Amat\Bmat)\xvec$ costs
$mnp + mp$.

## In machine learning

- **Stacked linear layers collapse.** Two linear layers without a nonlinearity between them compute
  $\Wmat_2\Wmat_1\xvec$, which is one linear layer with weights $\Wmat_2\Wmat_1$. Nonlinearities are what make depth add
  expressive power.
- **Batches.** Applying a layer to a batch of inputs stacked as the columns of $\Xmat$ is one product $\Wmat\Xmat$: the
  column view. Hardware accelerators are built around this operation.
- **Attention.** Self-attention computes all query–key scores at once as $\Qmat\Kmat^\top$: the entry view, one dot
  product per pair.

## Pitfalls

- **Element-wise versus matrix product.** In NumPy and PyTorch, `A * B` is the element-wise (Hadamard) product and
  `A @ B` is the matrix product.
- **Chain order.** Multiplying a long chain left to right can create large intermediate matrices. Evaluate products
  with a vector from the right.
