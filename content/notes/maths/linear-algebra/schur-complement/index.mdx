<Definition>
Partition a square matrix as

$$
\Mmat = \begin{bmatrix} \Amat & \Bmat \\ \Cmat & \Dmat \end{bmatrix}
$$

with $\Amat$ square and invertible. The Schur complement of $\Amat$ in $\Mmat$ is

$$
\Mmat / \Amat = \Dmat - \Cmat\Amat^{-1}\Bmat.
$$

When $\Dmat$ is invertible, the Schur complement of $\Dmat$ is $\Mmat/\Dmat = \Amat - \Bmat\Dmat^{-1}\Cmat$
<Cite id="zhang2005" />.
</Definition>

## Block elimination

Solve the block system

$$
\Amat\xvec + \Bmat\yvec = \fvec, \qquad \Cmat\xvec + \Dmat\yvec = \gvec.
$$

The first equation gives $\xvec = \Amat^{-1}(\fvec - \Bmat\yvec)$. Substituting it into the second eliminates $\xvec$:

$$
(\Dmat - \Cmat\Amat^{-1}\Bmat)\,\yvec = \gvec - \Cmat\Amat^{-1}\fvec.
$$

The matrix on the left is $\Mmat/\Amat$. The Schur complement is the matrix of the reduced system for $\yvec$ once
$\xvec$ has been solved out. Gaussian elimination computes it one pivot at a time: after the first elimination step,
the remaining submatrix is the Schur complement of the first pivot.

## Factorisation, inverse and determinant

Block elimination is the factorisation

$$
\Mmat = \begin{bmatrix} \eye & \zeros \\ \Cmat\Amat^{-1} & \eye \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \Amat & \zeros \\ \zeros & \Mmat/\Amat \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \eye & \Amat^{-1}\Bmat \\ \zeros & \eye \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Multiplying the three factors out checks it. The last two give
$\begin{bmatrix} \Amat & \Bmat \\ \zeros & \Mmat/\Amat \end{bmatrix}$, and the first factor then adds $\Cmat\Amat^{-1}$
times the top block row to the bottom one, giving $\Cmat$ and
$\Cmat\Amat^{-1}\Bmat + \Dmat - \Cmat\Amat^{-1}\Bmat = \Dmat$.

The outer factors are block triangular with identity blocks on the diagonal. They have determinant 1, and each is
inverted by negating its off-diagonal block. It follows that <Cite id="horn2012" />:

- $\det\Mmat = \det\Amat \cdot \det(\Mmat/\Amat)$.
- $\Mmat$ is invertible exactly when $\Mmat/\Amat$ is, and $\rank\Mmat = \rank\Amat + \rank(\Mmat/\Amat)$.
- With $\Smat = \Mmat/\Amat$,

$$
\Mmat^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \Amat^{-1} + \Amat^{-1}\Bmat\Smat^{-1}\Cmat\Amat^{-1} & -\Amat^{-1}\Bmat\Smat^{-1} \\
-\Smat^{-1}\Cmat\Amat^{-1} & \Smat^{-1} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The inverse is the product of the three inverted factors in reverse order,

$$
\Mmat^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \eye & -\Amat^{-1}\Bmat \\ \zeros & \eye \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \Amat^{-1} & \zeros \\ \zeros & \Smat^{-1} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \eye & \zeros \\ -\Cmat\Amat^{-1} & \eye \end{bmatrix},
$$

and multiplying out gives the four blocks above. The determinant is the product of the three determinants, and the rank
of the block-diagonal middle factor is $\rank\Amat + \rank\Smat$. The bottom-right block of $\Mmat^{-1}$ is the inverse
of the Schur complement. Comparing this formula with the one
obtained by eliminating $\yvec$ first gives the Woodbury matrix identity.

## Positive definiteness

For a symmetric matrix with $\Cmat = \Bmat^\top$ and $\Amat \succ 0$,

$$
\Mmat \succ 0 \iff \Mmat/\Amat \succ 0,
$$

and the same holds with $\succeq$ <Cite id="zhang2005" />. With $\Cmat = \Bmat^\top$, the factorisation above reads
$\Mmat = \Pmat^\top\diag(\Amat, \Mmat/\Amat)\,\Pmat$ with the invertible matrix
$\Pmat = \begin{bmatrix} \eye & \Amat^{-1}\Bmat \\ \zeros & \eye \end{bmatrix}$. Substituting $\zvec = \Pmat\xvec$,
which runs over every nonzero vector as $\xvec$ does, gives

$$
\xvec^\top\Mmat\xvec = \zvec^\top\diag(\Amat, \Mmat/\Amat)\,\zvec = \zvec_1^\top\Amat\zvec_1 + \zvec_2^\top(\Mmat/\Amat)\zvec_2.
$$

With $\Amat \succ 0$, the sum is positive for every nonzero $\zvec$ exactly when $\Mmat/\Amat \succ 0$. This is the step
that makes the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factorisation</NoteLink> succeed on every positive
definite matrix, and it turns many convex constraints into linear matrix inequalities.

## Conditioning a Gaussian

Split a Gaussian vector into two parts,

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \xvec_a \\ \xvec_b \end{bmatrix} \sim
\Gauss\paren{\begin{bmatrix} \muvec_a \\ \muvec_b \end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix} \Sigmamat_{aa} & \Sigmamat_{ab} \\ \Sigmamat_{ba} & \Sigmamat_{bb} \end{bmatrix}}.
$$

Given $\xvec_b$, the distribution of $\xvec_a$ is Gaussian with <Cite id="bishop2006" />

$$
\muvec_{a \mid b} = \muvec_a + \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}(\xvec_b - \muvec_b), \qquad
\Sigmamat_{a \mid b} = \Sigmamat_{aa} - \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{ba} = \Sigmamat / \Sigmamat_{bb}.
$$

<Derivation title="Deriving the conditional distribution">
The conditional density $p(\xvec_a \mid \xvec_b)$ is proportional to the joint density as a function of $\xvec_a$, so
only the exponent $-\tfrac12 \dvec^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\dvec$ matters, with
$\dvec = (\xvec_a - \muvec_a, \xvec_b - \muvec_b)$. Apply the factorisation with the observed block $\Sigmamat_{bb}$ as
the pivot, eliminating $\xvec_b$ from $\xvec_a$. Write $\Smat = \Sigmamat/\Sigmamat_{bb}$ and
$\rvec = (\xvec_a - \muvec_a) - \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}(\xvec_b - \muvec_b)$. The same change of variables as
in the positive-definiteness argument splits the quadratic form:

$$
\dvec^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\dvec = \rvec^\top\Smat^{-1}\rvec + (\xvec_b - \muvec_b)^\top\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}(\xvec_b - \muvec_b).
$$

The second term does not involve $\xvec_a$. The first is the exponent of a Gaussian in $\xvec_a$ with mean
$\muvec_a + \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}(\xvec_b - \muvec_b)$ and covariance $\Smat$. The second term, read on its
own, also shows that the marginal of $\xvec_b$ is $\Gauss(\muvec_b, \Sigmamat_{bb})$.
</Derivation>

The conditional covariance is the Schur complement of the observed block. It does not depend on the observed value
$\xvec_b$, and it is never larger than $\Sigmamat_{aa}$: the subtracted term
$\Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{ba}$ is PSD, so observing $\xvec_b$ removes the variance that $\xvec_a$
shares with it. See also the <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal
distribution</NoteLink>. The block-inverse formula also shows that the precision matrix $\Sigmamat^{-1}$ has top-left
block $(\Sigmamat/\Sigmamat_{bb})^{-1}$, the conditional precision. Gaussian process regression, the Kalman filter and
Gaussian graphical models all rest on this identity.

## Worked example

For two variables with standard deviations $\sigma_a, \sigma_b$ and correlation $\rho$, the covariance is
$\begin{bmatrix} \sigma_a^2 & \rho\sigma_a\sigma_b \\ \rho\sigma_a\sigma_b & \sigma_b^2 \end{bmatrix}$. The Schur
complement of $\sigma_b^2$ is

$$
\sigma_a^2 - \frac{(\rho\sigma_a\sigma_b)^2}{\sigma_b^2} = \sigma_a^2(1 - \rho^2).
$$

Observing $x_b$ shrinks the variance of $x_a$ by the factor $1 - \rho^2$. With $\rho = 0.8$, the conditional standard
deviation is $0.6\,\sigma_a$. The conditional mean moves by $\rho\,(\sigma_a/\sigma_b)(x_b - \mu_b)$, the regression
of $x_a$ on $x_b$.

## Pitfalls

- **Which block.** $\Mmat/\Amat$ and $\Mmat/\Dmat$ are different matrices of different sizes. Notation varies between
  texts; check which block is eliminated.
- **Invertibility of the pivot block.** The definition needs $\Amat$ invertible. When it is singular, a generalised
  Schur complement uses the <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink> $\Amat^+$, and the identities hold
  only under extra range conditions.
- **Numerical use.** Forming $\Amat^{-1}$ explicitly is unnecessary. Compute $\Amat^{-1}\Bmat$ by solving with a
  factorisation of $\Amat$, for example its Cholesky factor.
