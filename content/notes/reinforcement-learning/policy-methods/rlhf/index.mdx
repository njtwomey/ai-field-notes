<Definition>
**Reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) trains a policy $\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)$ on a task whose
reward cannot be written down, using human comparisons instead. People compare pairs of behaviours $(y_w, y_l)$ for
the same input $x$ and say which is better. A reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ is fitted to these comparisons, and the
policy is optimised against it with a penalty for moving away from a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:

$$
\max_{\thetavec}\ \expect_{x \sim \Dcal,\, y \sim \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid x)}\brack{r_\phi(x, y)} - \beta\,\expect_x\brack{\KL\paren{\pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)}}
$$

<Cite id="christiano2017" /> <Cite id="ouyang2022" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Humans find it hard to score behaviour on an absolute scale but easy to say which of two attempts is better.
Comparisons are therefore cheaper and more consistent than ratings. Christiano et al. used them to teach simulated
robots and Atari agents tasks, such as a backflip, from under an hour of human time, by querying people on short clips
where the reward model was most uncertain <Cite id="christiano2017" />. Applied to language models, with $x$ a prompt
and $y$ a response, the same recipe produced InstructGPT <Cite id="ouyang2022" />. The language-model side of the
pipeline, including supervised fine-tuning, is in <NoteLink to="instruction-tuning-and-alignment">instruction tuning
and alignment</NoteLink>. This note covers the reinforcement-learning side.

## Reward models from preferences

**The Bradley–Terry model.** Suppose a rater's judgement of response $y$ is a latent utility $u(y) = r(x, y) + \epsilon_y$,
where $r$ is the true reward and the noise terms $\epsilon_y$ are independent standard Gumbel variables. The rater
prefers the response with the larger utility. By the <NoteLink to="gumbel-max-trick">Gumbel-max trick</NoteLink>, the
index of the largest of $r_i + \epsilon_i$ is distributed as $\operatorname{softmax}(r)$. For two responses this gives

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{e^{r(x, y_w)}}{e^{r(x, y_w)} + e^{r(x, y_l)}} = \sigma\paren{r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)},
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. This is the **Bradley–Terry** model of paired comparisons
<Cite id="bradley1952" />. Only reward differences matter, so the reward is identified up to an additive constant per
prompt.

**Fitting.** Maximum likelihood over a dataset of comparisons $(x, y_w, y_l)$ minimises

$$
L(\phi) = -\expect_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\brack{\log\sigma\paren{r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)}},
$$

which is <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on the difference of two network outputs.
For language models, $r_\phi$ is usually the pretrained model with its output layer replaced by a scalar head
<Cite id="stiennon2020" />.

## Policy optimisation with a KL penalty

**Why the penalty.** The reward model is accurate only near the responses it was trained on. A policy that maximises
$r_\phi$ without restraint finds inputs on which the model is wrong: text that scores highly but that people dislike.
This is **reward overoptimisation**, an instance of Goodhart's law: past a point, true preference falls while the
proxy reward keeps rising. The KL term keeps the policy near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the supervised model on whose outputs
the preferences were collected <Cite id="stiennon2020" />.

**As an RL problem.** A response is a sequence of tokens $y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$, each an action in a state made of the
prompt and the tokens so far. The KL divergence of sequence distributions is the expected sum of per-token
log-ratios, so the objective is an ordinary MDP return with reward

$$
R_t = -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})} + \indicator[t = T]\,r_\phi(x, y),
$$

a KL penalty at every token and the reward model's score at the last <Cite id="ziegler2019" />. PPO optimises it,
with a value head on the policy network estimating the return for <NoteLink to="actor-critic">GAE</NoteLink>
advantages, and clipped updates as in <NoteLink to="trust-region-and-ppo">PPO</NoteLink>. The episode is one response,
the horizon is its length, and $\gamma = 1$.

## The optimal policy in closed form

For one prompt, the objective over distributions $\pi$ on responses is
$\sum_y\pi(y)r(y) - \beta\sum_y\pi(y)\log(\pi(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y))$. Define
$Z = \sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp(r(y)/\beta)$ and $\pi^*(y) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp(r(y)/\beta)/Z$. Since
$r(y) = \beta\log(\pi^*(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)) + \beta\log Z$, the objective equals

$$
\sum_y\pi(y)\brack{\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} + \beta\log Z - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}}
= \beta\log Z - \beta\,\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*).
$$

The KL divergence is non-negative and zero only at $\pi = \pi^*$, so $\pi^*$ is the unique maximiser. The optimal
policy reweights the reference by the exponentiated reward. $\beta$ sets the trade-off: small $\beta$ concentrates on
the highest-reward responses, large $\beta$ stays near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

## Direct preference optimisation

The closed form can be inverted: any policy $\pi$ is optimal for the reward $r(x, y) = \beta\log(\pi(y \mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)) + \beta\log Z(x)$.
Substituting this into the Bradley–Terry likelihood, the intractable $\beta\log Z(x)$ appears in both rewards and
cancels in their difference:

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\paren{\beta\log\frac{\pi(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}}.
$$

**Direct preference optimisation** (DPO) fits the policy by maximising this likelihood, with the loss

$$
L_{\text{DPO}}(\thetavec) = -\expect\brack{\log\sigma\paren{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}}}
$$

<Cite id="rafailov2023" />. The policy is its own reward model. DPO needs no separate reward network, no sampling from
the policy during training and no value function; it is a classification loss on the preference data. Its gradient
raises the log-probability of $y_w$ and lowers that of $y_l$, weighted by $\sigma$ of the implied reward margin in the
wrong direction, so pairs the policy already ranks correctly contribute little.

**Trade-off.** DPO optimises on a fixed dataset, so it is an offline method. PPO-based RLHF samples fresh responses
from the current policy and scores them with the reward model, which lets it explore responses not in the data and
lets the reward model generalise to them. Which is better depends on the task and the data.

## Worked example

**Reward model.** A reward model scores the preferred response 1.0 and the other 0.2. The Bradley–Terry probability
that the preferred one wins is $\sigma(0.8) = 0.690$, and the loss for this pair is $-\log 0.690 = 0.371$. Training
pushes the margin up.

**DPO.** With $\beta = 0.1$, the policy has raised the log-probability of $y_w$ by 1.5 nats relative to the reference
and lowered that of $y_l$ by 0.5 nats. The implied reward margin is $0.1\,(1.5 - (-0.5)) = 0.2$, the modelled
preference probability is $\sigma(0.2) = 0.550$ and the loss is $0.598$. The gradient weight $\sigma(-0.2) = 0.450$ is
still large: this pair keeps pushing until the margin grows.

## Pitfalls

- **Reward hacking.** Policies exploit reward-model errors, for example by producing longer responses when raters
  tended to prefer longer ones. Monitor the KL divergence and evaluate with held-out human judgements, not the reward
  model.
- **Rater disagreement.** Bradley–Terry assumes one latent reward. Preferences that differ systematically between
  raters are averaged into a reward nobody holds.
- **Choosing $\beta$.** Too small overoptimises; too large learns nothing. The KL budget actually spent is a more
  interpretable control than $\beta$ itself.
