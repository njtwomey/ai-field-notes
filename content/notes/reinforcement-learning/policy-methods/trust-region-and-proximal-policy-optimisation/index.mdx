<Definition>
Given data collected by a policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ and advantage estimates $\hat A_t$, both methods update the
parameters $\thetavec$ of a new policy through the **probability ratio**
$r_t(\thetavec) = \pi_{\thetavec}(a_t \mid s_t)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)$.
**Trust region policy optimisation** (TRPO) maximises $\expect_t[r_t(\thetavec)\hat A_t]$ subject to an average
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> $\expect_t[\KL(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid s_t))] \le \delta$
<Cite id="schulman2015" />. **Proximal policy optimisation** (PPO) instead maximises the clipped objective

$$
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\thetavec) = \expect_t\brack{\min\paren{r_t(\thetavec)\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}\paren{r_t(\thetavec), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon}\hat A_t}}
$$

by several epochs of minibatch gradient ascent <Cite id="schulman2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A policy-gradient step uses data from the current policy, so each batch supports one small step and is then
discarded. A larger step, or several steps on the same batch, can change the policy so much that the gradient no
longer describes it. The policy may collapse onto a poor action, and the data it then collects cannot reveal the
mistake. A step size in parameter space does not control this: the same change in $\thetavec$ can change the action
probabilities a lot in one region of parameter space and very little in another. Trust-region methods bound the change
in the policy itself.

## The surrogate objective

**Performance difference.** For any two policies $\pi$ and $\pi'$,

$$
J(\pi') - J(\pi) = \expect_{\tau \sim \pi'}\brack{\sum_{t \ge 0}\gamma^t A_\pi(S_t, A_t)},
$$

where $A_\pi = q_\pi - v_\pi$ is the old policy's advantage and the trajectory is generated by the new policy
<Cite id="kakade2002" />. To derive it, note that
$A_\pi(s, a) = \expect[R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1}) - v_\pi(S_t) \mid S_t = s, A_t = a]$ under the shared dynamics,
so the right-hand side equals $\expect_{\tau \sim \pi'}\brack{\sum_t\gamma^t(R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1}) - v_\pi(S_t))}$.
The value terms telescope to $-v_\pi(S_0)$, leaving $\expect_{\pi'}[G_0] - \expect[v_\pi(S_0)] = J(\pi') - J(\pi)$.

**Surrogate.** The difference requires states visited by $\pi'$, which has not been run. The surrogate keeps the old
policy's states and corrects only the action distribution by
<NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink>:

$$
L_{\pi}(\pi') = \expect_{s_t, a_t \sim \pi}\brack{\frac{\pi'(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi(a_t \mid s_t)}A_\pi(s_t, a_t)}.
$$

$L_\pi(\pi)= 0$, and $L_\pi$ and $J(\cdot) - J(\pi)$ have the same gradient at $\pi' = \pi$: differentiating the ratio at
$\thetavec_{\text{old}}$ gives $\nabla\log\pi_{\thetavec}$, which recovers the
<NoteLink to="policy-gradient-theorem">policy gradient</NoteLink>. Away from $\pi$ the two drift apart, by an amount
that grows with how different the state distributions are. Schulman et al. bound the gap by a constant times the
largest KL divergence between the policies over states, so maximising the surrogate minus a KL penalty improves $J$
monotonically <Cite id="schulman2015" />.

## TRPO

TRPO turns the penalty into a constraint on the average KL divergence, which permits larger steps:

$$
\max_{\thetavec}\ \expect_t\brack{r_t(\thetavec)\hat A_t}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\expect_t\brack{\KL\paren{\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid s_t)}} \le \delta.
$$

Near $\thetavec_{\text{old}}$ the objective is linear, $\gvec\transpose\Delta$ with $\gvec$ the policy gradient and
$\Delta = \thetavec - \thetavec_{\text{old}}$. The KL divergence is quadratic, $\frac12\Delta\transpose\Fmat\Delta$,
where $\Fmat$ is the Fisher information matrix of the policy, because the KL divergence and its gradient vanish at
$\Delta = 0$. Setting the gradient of the Lagrangian $\gvec\transpose\Delta - \mu(\frac12\Delta\transpose\Fmat\Delta - \delta)$
to zero gives $\Delta \propto \Fmat^{-1}\gvec$. Scaling it so the constraint holds with equality gives

$$
\Delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{\gvec\transpose\Fmat^{-1}\gvec}}\,\Fmat^{-1}\gvec.
$$

$\Fmat^{-1}\gvec$ is the **natural policy gradient**, the steepest-ascent direction when distance is measured by KL
divergence between policies rather than Euclidean distance between parameters. TRPO computes it by conjugate gradient
using Fisher-vector products, never forming $\Fmat$, and then backtracks along $\Delta$ until the true KL constraint
holds and the surrogate improves <Cite id="schulman2015" />.

## PPO and why clipping works

TRPO's second-order machinery is complex and does not fit architectures that share parameters between policy and value
function. PPO keeps the idea, stay close to the old policy, with a first-order objective <Cite id="schulman2017" />.

Consider one sample and the clipped term $\min(r\hat A, \operatorname{clip}(r, 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)\hat A)$.

- **Positive advantage.** The action was better than average, and the objective rewards making it more likely. For
  $r \le 1 + \varepsilon$ the two terms are equal or the unclipped one is smaller, and the objective is $r\hat A$. For
  $r > 1 + \varepsilon$ the clipped term $(1 + \varepsilon)\hat A$ is smaller, so the objective is constant in $r$ and
  its gradient is zero. Once the action's probability has risen by a factor $1 + \varepsilon$, this sample stops
  pushing.
- **Negative advantage.** Symmetrically, the objective is flat for $r < 1 - \varepsilon$: once the action's probability
  has fallen by a factor $1 - \varepsilon$, the sample stops pushing it down.
- **Moves in the wrong direction are not clipped.** If an update made a good action less likely ($r < 1$ with
  $\hat A > 0$), the minimum picks the unclipped term $r\hat A$, and the full gradient pulls it back.

Because it takes a minimum with the unclipped term, $L^{\text{CLIP}} \le \expect_t[r_t\hat A_t]$ always: it is a
pessimistic lower bound on the surrogate that agrees with it to first order at $r = 1$. The incentive to move each
ratio outside $[1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon]$ is removed, so running several epochs of minibatch updates on the
same batch does not drive the policy far from the one that collected it. Clipping does not enforce a hard bound; ratios
can still leave the interval through the effect of other samples on shared parameters. Typical settings are
$\varepsilon = 0.2$, advantages from <NoteLink to="actor-critic">GAE</NoteLink> with $\lambda = 0.95$, and 3 to 10
epochs per batch <Cite id="schulman2017" />.

<ClippedObjective />

The full PPO loss adds a value-function error term and an entropy bonus, as in A2C. A variant replaces clipping by a
KL penalty $\beta\,\KL(\pi_{\text{old}} \,\|\, \pi_{\thetavec})$ whose weight $\beta$ is doubled when the measured KL
divergence exceeds a target and halved when it falls below; clipping performed better in the original experiments
<Cite id="schulman2017" />.

## Worked example

**Clipped objective**, with $\varepsilon = 0.2$:

- $\hat A = 2$, $r = 1.3$: $\min(2.6,\ 1.2 \cdot 2) = 2.4$. The clipped term is active, and the gradient in $r$ is 0.
- $\hat A = 2$, $r = 0.7$: $\min(1.4,\ 0.8 \cdot 2) = 1.4$. The unclipped term is active: the update will raise $r$.
- $\hat A = -1$, $r = 0.7$: $\min(-0.7,\ -0.8) = -0.8$. The clipped term is active, and the gradient is 0.

**Natural gradient step.** Suppose $\gvec = (1, 0)$, $\Fmat = \diag(4, 1)$ and $\delta = 0.02$. Then
$\Fmat^{-1}\gvec = (0.25, 0)$, $\gvec\transpose\Fmat^{-1}\gvec = 0.25$, the scale is $\sqrt{0.04/0.25} = 0.4$, and
$\Delta = (0.1, 0)$. The quadratic KL is $\frac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 0.1^2 = 0.02 = \delta$. The first parameter has high
Fisher information, so the policy is sensitive to it, and the step along it is short.

## Assumptions

- **Good advantage estimates.** Both methods improve the surrogate; the surrogate is only as good as $\hat A_t$.
- **Small policy change per batch.** The surrogate approximates $J$ only near $\pi_{\text{old}}$.

## Pitfalls

- **Implementation details matter.** Advantage normalisation, value clipping, learning-rate annealing, observation
  normalisation and gradient clipping all change PPO's results substantially.
- **Too many epochs.** Clipping weakens but does not remove the drift; monitor the average KL divergence and stop the
  epochs early if it exceeds a threshold.
