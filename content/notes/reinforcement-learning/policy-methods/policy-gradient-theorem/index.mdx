<Definition>
Let $\pi_{\thetavec}(a \mid s)$ be a policy differentiable in its parameters $\thetavec$, and let
$J(\thetavec) = \expect_{\pi_{\thetavec}}\brack{\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t R_{t+1}}$ be its expected discounted return
from a start-state distribution. The **policy gradient theorem** states

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}J(\thetavec) = \expect_{\pi_{\thetavec}}\brack{\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t\,q_\pi(S_t, A_t)\,\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t)},
$$

where $q_\pi$ is the policy's action-value function and the expectation is over trajectories generated by
$\pi_{\thetavec}$ <Cite id="sutton2000" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Changing $\thetavec$ changes which actions are taken, which changes which states are visited, which changes the
rewards. The state distribution depends on the unknown dynamics, so its derivative looks impossible to compute. The
theorem says it is not needed: the gradient only involves the derivative of the policy's own log-probabilities,
weighted by how good each action turned out to be. Actions with high $q_\pi$ have their probability pushed up; actions
with low $q_\pi$ are pushed down.

Policy methods optimise the policy directly, instead of deriving it from a value function. They handle continuous
actions, learn stochastic policies when those are optimal (for example when the state is partially observed), and
change the policy smoothly, whereas a greedy policy can flip under a small change in $Q$.

## Derivation

**Trajectory form.** A trajectory $\tau = (S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, A_1, \dots)$ has probability

$$
p_{\thetavec}(\tau) = d_0(S_0)\prod_{t \ge 0}\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t)\,p(S_{t+1}, R_{t+1} \mid S_t, A_t).
$$

Only the policy factors depend on $\thetavec$, so the log-derivative of the dynamics vanishes:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}\log p_{\thetavec}(\tau) = \sum_{t \ge 0}\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t).
$$

The <NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function identity</NoteLink>
$\nabla_{\thetavec}\expect_{p_{\thetavec}}[f(\tau)] = \expect_{p_{\thetavec}}[f(\tau)\nabla_{\thetavec}\log p_{\thetavec}(\tau)]$,
applied to the return $f(\tau) = G_0 = \sum_k\gamma^k R_{k+1}$, gives

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}J = \expect\brack{G_0\sum_{t \ge 0}\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t)}.
$$

**Causality.** The score has mean zero under the policy: for any state $s$,

$$
\expect_{A \sim \pi_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid s)}\brack{\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A \mid s)}
= \sum_a\pi_{\thetavec}(a \mid s)\frac{\nabla_{\thetavec}\pi_{\thetavec}(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\thetavec}(a \mid s)}
= \nabla_{\thetavec}\sum_a\pi_{\thetavec}(a \mid s) = \nabla_{\thetavec}1 = 0.
$$

A reward $R_{k+1}$ with $k < t$ is determined before $A_t$ is drawn. Conditioning on the history up to $S_t$, the
factor $R_{k+1}$ is fixed and the score of $A_t$ averages to zero, so $\expect[R_{k+1}\nabla\log\pi(A_t \mid S_t)] = 0$.
Past rewards drop out, and the reward-to-go $\sum_{k \ge t}\gamma^k R_{k+1} = \gamma^t G_t$ remains:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}J = \expect\brack{\sum_{t \ge 0}\gamma^t G_t\,\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t)}.
$$

**Action values.** Condition each term on $(S_t, A_t)$. The score is then fixed, and by the Markov property
$\expect[G_t \mid S_t, A_t] = q_\pi(S_t, A_t)$. Replacing $G_t$ by its conditional mean gives the theorem.

Written with states, the theorem reads $\nabla J = \sum_s\eta_\gamma(s)\sum_a q_\pi(s, a)\nabla\pi_{\thetavec}(a \mid s)$,
where $\eta_\gamma(s) = \sum_t\gamma^t\pr(S_t = s)$ is the discounted visitation of $s$. The derivative of $\eta_\gamma$
never appears <Cite id="sutton2000" />.

## REINFORCE

Sampling a trajectory and using the observed $G_t$ for $q_\pi(S_t, A_t)$ gives an unbiased gradient estimate. The
**REINFORCE** algorithm follows it by stochastic gradient ascent <Cite id="williams1992" />:

$$
\thetavec \leftarrow \thetavec + \alpha\sum_{t}\gamma^t G_t\,\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t).
$$

Most implementations drop the factor $\gamma^t$. The result is no longer the gradient of $J$ but of an objective that
weights all visited states equally; it works well in practice and is standard <Cite id="sutton2018" />.

REINFORCE is unbiased but has high variance. $G_t$ sums many random rewards, and the score's magnitude varies from
action to action. Adding the same constant to every reward leaves the true gradient unchanged, yet it changes every
sample, and the variance of the estimate grows with the square of the constant.

## Baselines

Subtract any function $b(s)$ of the state from the return:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}J = \expect\brack{\sum_t\gamma^t\paren{G_t - b(S_t)}\nabla_{\thetavec}\log\pi_{\thetavec}(A_t \mid S_t)}.
$$

**No bias.** Given $S_t$, the baseline is a constant and the score has mean zero, so
$\expect[b(S_t)\nabla\log\pi(A_t \mid S_t) \mid S_t] = b(S_t) \cdot 0 = 0$. The baseline term vanishes in expectation
for any $b$ that does not depend on $A_t$.

**Less variance.** Take one step with scalar score $g = \partial_\theta\log\pi(A \mid s)$ and return $G$. The estimate
$(G - b)g$ has mean $\expect[Gg]$ for every $b$, and variance

$$
\var\brack{(G - b)g} = \expect\brack{(G - b)^2g^2} - \paren{\expect[Gg]}^2.
$$

Setting the derivative in $b$ to zero, $-2\expect[(G - b)g^2] = 0$, gives the variance-minimising constant

$$
b^* = \frac{\expect\brack{G\,g^2}}{\expect\brack{g^2}},
$$

an average of the returns weighted by the squared score. When the return and the score's magnitude are roughly
uncorrelated, $b^* \approx \expect[G \mid s] = v_\pi(s)$. The state value is therefore the standard baseline, and
$G_t - v_\pi(S_t)$ estimates the **advantage** $q_\pi(S_t, A_t) - v_\pi(S_t)$: how much better the action taken was
than the policy's average. Learning $v_\pi$ alongside the policy is the idea behind
<NoteLink to="actor-critic">actor-critic methods</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

A one-step problem has two actions. A softmax policy with logits $(\theta_1, \theta_2) = (0, 0)$ picks each with
probability 0.5. Action 1 pays 1 and action 2 pays 3, so $J = 2$. For a softmax policy,
$\partial\log\pi(a)/\partial\theta_1 = \indicator[a = 1] - \pi(1)$, which is $0.5$ for action 1 and $-0.5$ for action 2.

- **True gradient.** $\partial J/\partial\theta_1 = \pi(1)(r_1 - J) = 0.5\,(1 - 2) = -0.5$: raising $\theta_1$ makes
  the worse action more likely.
- **REINFORCE without baseline.** The sample is $1 \cdot 0.5 = 0.5$ if action 1 is drawn and $3 \cdot (-0.5) = -1.5$ if
  action 2 is drawn. Its mean is $0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot (-1.5) = -0.5$, correct, and its variance is
  $0.5 \cdot 0.5^2 + 0.5 \cdot 1.5^2 - 0.5^2 = 1$. Half the samples point the wrong way.
- **With baseline $b = 2$.** The samples are $(1 - 2) \cdot 0.5 = -0.5$ and $(3 - 2)(-0.5) = -0.5$. Every sample equals
  the true gradient, and the variance is 0.

## Assumptions

- **Differentiable policy with full support where needed.** $\log\pi_{\thetavec}$ must be differentiable, and actions
  with zero probability are never tried, so their gradients are never estimated.
- **On-policy samples.** The expectation is under the current policy. Reusing data from older policies requires
  importance weights, as in <NoteLink to="trust-region-and-ppo">PPO</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Step size.** A gradient step in parameter space can change the policy a lot in some directions and hardly at all
  in others. Too large a step collapses the policy onto a poor action, and the data it then collects cannot undo the
  mistake. Trust-region methods address this.
- **Baselines that depend on the action.** A baseline $b(s, a)$ introduces bias unless its effect is corrected.
