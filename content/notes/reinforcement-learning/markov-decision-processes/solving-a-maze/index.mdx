<Definition>
An agent moves through a rectangular grid of cells. Some cells are walls, one is the start, one is the goal, and some
are traps. In each open cell the agent chooses one of four moves: up, right, down or left. A move goes in the chosen
direction with probability $1 - \rho$ and in each of the two perpendicular directions with probability $\rho/2$, where
$\rho \in [0, 1)$ is the **slip probability**. A move into a wall or off the grid leaves the agent where it is. Every
move pays $-1$. A move that enters the goal pays $+10$ instead and ends the episode. A move that enters a trap pays
$-20$ instead and puts the agent back on the start. Future rewards are discounted by $\gamma \in (0, 1)$. Find the
policy that maximises the expected discounted return from the start, first with the transition probabilities known,
then without them.
</Definition>

## Setup

The maze is a <NoteLink to="markov-decision-process">Markov decision process</NoteLink> $(\Scal, \Acal, p, r, \gamma)$.

- **States.** $\Scal$ is the set of open cells: every cell that is not a wall, not a trap and not the goal. The goal is
  a terminal state with value 0. A trap is not a state, because the agent never stays in it.
- **Actions.** $\Acal = \set{\text{up}, \text{right}, \text{down}, \text{left}}$ in every state.
- **Transitions and rewards.** $p(s', r \mid s, a)$ is the probability of landing in $s'$ with reward $r$. With $\rho =
  0$
  every move is deterministic. Entering the goal gives $(s', r) = (\text{goal}, 10)$. Entering a trap gives
  $(s', r) = (\text{start}, -20)$. Any other landing cell $c$, including the current cell after a bump, gives
  $(s', r) = (c, -1)$.
- **Return.** From time $t$ the return is $G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \dots$, up to the step
  that enters the goal.

The step cost makes short routes better than long ones. The discount makes the goal reward worth less the further away
it is. With $\rho = 0$ and $\gamma = 0.9$, a cell $d$ moves from the goal along the shortest route has optimal value

$$
v_*(d) = -\sum_{k=0}^{d-2}\gamma^k + \gamma^{d-1} \cdot 10 = -\frac{1 - \gamma^{d-1}}{1 - \gamma} + 10\gamma^{d-1},
$$

because the first $d - 1$ moves each pay $-1$ and the last pays $+10$. This gives $v_*(1) = 10$, $v_*(2) = 8$,
$v_*(3) = 6.2$, and a negative value from $d = 8$ on: $v_*(7) = 0.63$ and $v_*(8) = -0.43$.

## Solution

### Planning: value iteration with the model known

The **Bellman optimality equation** for action values follows from splitting the return after one step,
$G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}$. An agent that takes $a$ in $s$ and acts optimally afterwards collects
$R_{t+1}$ and then, from $S_{t+1}$, at best $\max_{a'}q_*(S_{t+1}, a')$ in <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>. Averaging over the outcome of
the move gives

$$
q_*(s, a) = \sum_{s', r}p(s', r \mid s, a)\brack{r + \gamma\max_{a'}q_*(s', a')},
$$

with $\max_{a'}q_*(\text{goal}, a') = 0$ <Cite id="bellman1957" />. The right-hand side defines an operator $\Hcal$ on
action-value tables, and $q_*$ is its fixed point. <NoteLink to="value-iteration">Value iteration</NoteLink> applies
$\Hcal$ repeatedly from $Q_0 = 0$:

$$
Q_{k+1}(s, a) = \sum_{s', r}p(s', r \mid s, a)\brack{r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q_k(s', a')}.
$$

$\Hcal$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm, so $Q_k \to q_*$ from any start. The state value is
$V_k(s) = \max_a Q_k(s, a)$, and the greedy policy picks $\argmax_a Q_k(s, a)$.

**Two sweeps by hand.** Take the open-room maze, an $8 \times 8$ grid without walls, with the start at $(0, 0)$ and the
goal at $(7, 7)$. Let $\gamma = 0.9$ and $\rho = 0$. Cell $A = (7, 6)$ is directly below the goal and cell
$B = (7, 5)$ is below $A$.

- Sweep 1. $Q_1(A, \text{up}) = 10 + 0 = 10$, because the move enters the goal. Every move that does not enter the goal
  pays $-1$ and lands in a cell with $V_0 = 0$, so it gets $-1$. So $V_1(A) = 10$ and $V_1(s) = -1$ in every cell
  that does not touch the goal.
- Sweep 2. $Q_2(B, \text{up}) = -1 + 0.9 \cdot V_1(A) = -1 + 9 = 8$. A cell far from the goal gets
  $-1 + 0.9 \cdot (-1) = -1.9$.

The values match $v_*(1) = 10$ and $v_*(2) = 8$. Sweep $k$ fixes the cells within $k$ moves of the goal, because an
optimal route of $d \le k$ moves is already inside the $k$-step lookahead. A cell further away holds
$-(1 - \gamma^k)/(1 - \gamma)$, the return of $k$ moves that never reach the goal. The start is 14 moves from the goal,
so the values are exact after 14 sweeps, and $V(0, 0) = v_*(14) = -4.92$.

With slip, the backup averages over the three outcomes. At $\rho = 0.2$ the first sweep gives
$Q_1(A, \text{up}) = 0.8 \cdot 10 + 0.1 \cdot (-1) + 0.1 \cdot (-1) = 7.8$: a slip right bumps into the edge of the
grid, and a slip left lands in $(6, 6)$.

### Learning: Q-learning without the model

Without $p$, the agent cannot compute the sum in the backup. It can sample it. Each step from $(S_t, A_t)$ produces one
outcome $(R_{t+1}, S_{t+1})$ drawn from $p(\cdot \mid S_t, A_t)$, and the **target**

$$
Y_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1}, a')
$$

satisfies $\expect[Y_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a] = (\Hcal Q)(s, a)$. Each target is an unbiased sample of one
value-iteration
backup. **Q-learning** moves the entry towards the target by a step size $\alpha \in (0, 1]$
<Cite id="watkins1989" />:

$$
Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\paren{Y_t - Q(S_t, A_t)}.
$$

The update is an incremental average. Write $Q_n$ for the entry after its $n$-th update and $Y_n$ for that update's
target. With $\alpha_n = 1/n$, if $Q_{n-1} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i<n}Y_i$ then

$$
Q_n = Q_{n-1} + \frac{1}{n}\paren{Y_n - Q_{n-1}} = \frac{n-1}{n}Q_{n-1} + \frac{1}{n}Y_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i,
$$

so by induction $Q_n$ is the mean of the targets seen so far. A constant $\alpha$ gives an exponentially weighted mean
instead, which tracks targets that change as $Q$ improves. Tabular Q-learning converges to $q_*$ with probability 1 when
every state-action pair is updated infinitely often and the step sizes satisfy $\sum_n\alpha_n = \infty$ and
$\sum_n\alpha_n^2 < \infty$ <Cite id="watkins1992" />.

The agent must choose actions while it learns. **$\varepsilon$-greedy** behaviour takes a uniformly random action with
probability $\varepsilon$ and the greedy action otherwise, with ties broken at random. The table starts at $Q = 0$.
With $\gamma = 0.9$ that is above the true value of every cell 8 or more moves from the goal. An action that has been
tried and has paid
$-1$ therefore looks worse than an untried one, so the agent systematically tries untried actions. This is <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">optimism in
the face of uncertainty</NoteLink>, and it comes for free from the initial table.

**Three updates by hand.** In the open room with $\gamma = 0.9$, $\alpha = 0.5$ and $Q = 0$:

1. The agent moves up from $B$ to $A$. The target is $-1 + 0.9 \cdot \max_{a'}Q(A, a') = -1$, so
   $Q(B, \text{up}) \leftarrow 0 + 0.5\,(-1 - 0) = -0.5$.
2. It moves up from $A$ into the goal. The target is $10$, because the goal is terminal, so
   $Q(A, \text{up}) \leftarrow 0 + 0.5 \cdot 10 = 5$.
3. In a later episode it moves up from $B$ to $A$ again. The target is now $-1 + 0.9 \cdot 5 = 3.5$, so
   $Q(B, \text{up}) \leftarrow -0.5 + 0.5\,(3.5 - (-0.5)) = 1.5$, and up becomes the greedy action in $B$.

Value iteration moves information about the goal one cell per sweep over every cell. Q-learning moves it one cell per
visit, and only along the paths the agent happens to take. Early episodes are therefore long <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walks</NoteLink>, and the
arrows fill in backwards from the goal as episodes accumulate.

### Learning on-policy: SARSA

**SARSA** replaces the maximum in the target by the value of the action $A_{t+1}$ that the agent will actually take
next, chosen $\varepsilon$-greedily <Cite id="rummery1994" />:

$$
Y_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}).
$$

Its expected target is the Bellman expectation backup for the $\varepsilon$-greedy policy $\pi$, so SARSA learns
$q_\pi$ rather than $q_*$. In update 3 above, suppose the agent in $A$ next draws the exploratory action left, with
$Q(A, \text{left}) = -0.5$. Q-learning's target for $(B, \text{up})$ is still $-1 + 0.9 \cdot 5 = 3.5$. SARSA's is
$-1 + 0.9 \cdot (-0.5) = -1.45$.

The difference decides the route in the two-routes maze. The start and the goal are joined by a bridge of 8 moves and
by a detour of 16 moves around a block of walls. Five of the bridge cells have a trap directly above and directly
below. An $\varepsilon$-greedy agent takes a random action with probability $\varepsilon$, and half of the random
actions are up or down. Each of those five moves therefore enters a trap with probability $\varepsilon/2$. At
$\varepsilon = 0.1$ the probability of crossing without a fall is $0.95^5 = 0.774$, so 22.6% of crossings end in
at least one fall, each costing $-20$ and a restart.

- Q-learning's targets assume greedy behaviour from the next state on, and a greedy agent never falls. Q-learning
  learns $q_*$ and takes the bridge.
- SARSA's targets include the falls, so the bridge looks worse than the detour. With $\alpha = 0.5$, $\gamma = 0.95$
  and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, SARSA's greedy route after 300 episodes is the detour in 10 of 10 seeds, and Q-learning's
  is the bridge in 10 of 10.

Slip has the same effect on the planner. A slip on the bridge enters a trap with probability $\rho$, since both
perpendicular cells are traps. Value iteration keeps the bridge for $\rho \le 0.065$ and switches to the detour from
$\rho = 0.07$, at both $\gamma = 0.9$ and $\gamma = 0.95$.

The cliff-edge maze repeats the comparison of <Cite id="sutton2018" />: a row of traps lies between the start and the
goal, Q-learning learns the route along the edge, and SARSA learns a route one or more rows away from it.

## Simulation

Useful things to try:

- **Travel back.** After 50 to 100 episodes, pause and drag the chart's handle to episode 1, then step forward with the
  arrows. After episode 1 most visited cells point at an action they have not tried yet, and only the last cell before
  the goal points at the goal. Over later episodes, arrows that lead to the goal spread back from it one visited cell
  at a time, and the path of each episode shortens.
- **Q-learning against SARSA.** On the two-routes maze, run both with the same seed. The final greedy routes differ as
  derived above. Switch on the decay of $\varepsilon$: SARSA's behaviour then approaches greedy, its values approach
  $q_*$, and in some seeds its route moves back to the bridge (2 of 10 seeds within 300 episodes).
- **Planning.** Choose value iteration and step through sweeps. The value of the start stays at
  $-(1 - \gamma^k)/(1 - \gamma)$ until sweep $k$ reaches the start's distance from the goal. Raise the slip
  probability past 0.07 and the greedy route leaves the bridge.
- **Return and reference.** With $\rho = 0$, the dashed line is the return of the shortest route. The moving average of
  the learning agents stays below it while $\varepsilon > 0$, because exploratory moves cost steps and falls.

## What it illustrates

- A <NoteLink to="markov-decision-process">Markov decision process</NoteLink> models the maze exactly: the next cell
  depends only on the current cell and the move.
- The <NoteLink to="bellman-equations">Bellman optimality equation</NoteLink> is the one equation both planners and
  learners solve. <NoteLink to="value-iteration">Value iteration</NoteLink> applies it with the model;
  <NoteLink to="q-learning">Q-learning</NoteLink> applies it to one sampled transition at a time.
- Reward information propagates backwards from the goal: one cell per sweep for value iteration, one cell per visit
  for <NoteLink to="temporal-difference-learning">temporal-difference</NoteLink> methods.
- Exploration is necessary and costly. <NoteLink to="epsilon-greedy">$\varepsilon$-greedy</NoteLink> behaviour and
  optimistic initial values make every action get tried; see <NoteLink to="exploration-in-reinforcement-learning">exploration in
  RL</NoteLink>.
- On-policy and <NoteLink to="off-policy-learning">off-policy learning</NoteLink> differ in what they learn. <NoteLink to="sarsa">SARSA</NoteLink> values the policy
  it follows, exploration included; Q-learning values the greedy policy.

## Variations

- **Goal reward only.** With no step cost and $\gamma < 1$, the discount alone favours short routes, because
  $\gamma^{d-1} \cdot 10$ falls with the distance $d$. With $\gamma = 1$ and no step cost every route that reaches the
  goal is optimal.
- **Terminal traps.** If a trap ended the episode with reward $-20$, a long enough maze would make jumping into the
  nearest trap better than walking to the goal. Returning the agent to the start instead makes a trap strictly worse
  than any move.
- **Model-based learning.** An agent can estimate $p$ from its transitions and run value iteration on the estimate,
  which reuses each experience many times. See <NoteLink to="model-based-reinforcement-learning">model-based reinforcement learning</NoteLink>.
- **Large mazes.** A table has one entry per state-action pair. Mazes seen as images, or with many cells, need a
  function approximator such as a <NoteLink to="deep-q-network">deep Q-network</NoteLink>.
