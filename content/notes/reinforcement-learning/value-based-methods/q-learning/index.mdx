<Definition>
**Q-learning** learns the optimal <Gloss name="value-function">action-value function</Gloss> $q_*$ of a <Gloss name="mdp">Markov decision process</Gloss> from sampled transitions
$(S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1})$, with the update

$$
Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\brack{R_{t+1} + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1}, a') - Q(S_t, A_t)}.
$$

The transitions may come from any **behaviour policy**, for example $\varepsilon$-greedy with respect to $Q$. The
learned **target policy** is greedy, $\pi(s) = \argmax_a Q(s, a)$ <Cite id="watkins1989" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Q-learning is a sampled version of Q-value iteration. The <NoteLink to="bellman-equations">Bellman optimality
equation</NoteLink> for action values,

$$
q_*(s, a) = \expect\brack{R_{t+1} + \gamma\max_{a'}q_*(S_{t+1}, a') \mid S_t = s, A_t = a},
$$

has the maximum inside the expectation. A single observed transition from $(s, a)$ is therefore an unbiased sample of
the right-hand side, with $Q$ standing in for $q_*$. Which action the agent takes next does not enter the target, and
neither does the policy that chose $A_t$: the equation holds for each pair $(s, a)$ separately. That is why Q-learning
is **off-policy**. It can learn the optimal policy while behaving randomly, from another agent's logs, or from replayed
old experience.

## Convergence

Let $\Hcal$ be the Q-value optimality operator,
$(\Hcal Q)(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma\sum_{s'}p(s' \mid s, a)\max_{a'}Q(s', a')$. Given $S_t = s$ and $A_t = a$, the
expected Q-learning target is $(\Hcal Q)(s, a)$, so the expected update is $\alpha(\Hcal Q - Q)(s, a)$. $\Hcal$ is a
$\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm, by the same argument as for the state-value operator: the difference of two
maxima is at most the maximum difference, and averaging over $s'$ does not increase it. Q-learning is therefore
asynchronous <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">stochastic approximation</NoteLink> of a contraction's fixed point.

**Theorem.** In a finite MDP with bounded rewards and $\gamma < 1$, tabular Q-learning converges to $q_*$ with
probability 1 if <Cite id="watkins1992" /> <Cite id="tsitsiklis1994" /> <Cite id="jaakkola1994" />:

- every state-action pair is updated infinitely often, and
- the step sizes $\alpha_n(s, a)$ at the $n$-th update of each pair satisfy $\sum_n\alpha_n(s, a) = \infty$ and
  $\sum_n\alpha_n(s, a)^2 < \infty$.

The behaviour policy is otherwise arbitrary. The first condition is the only demand on exploration, and it is why a
purely greedy behaviour policy can fail: pairs it never tries keep their initial values.

<CliffWalking />

On the cliff-walking task, learning off-policy while exploring lowers the reward collected during learning. Q-learning learns the optimal path
along the cliff edge, because its targets assume greedy behaviour. The agent does not behave greedily: one step in ten
is random, and a random step off the edge costs 100. <NoteLink to="sarsa">SARSA</NoteLink> learns the values of the
exploring policy and takes a longer, safer path, so it earns more reward while exploration continues. Averaged over 10
runs, the last 100 of 500 episodes pay about $-49$ per episode for Q-learning and $-27$ for SARSA at $\varepsilon = 0.1$
and $\alpha = 0.5$.

## Maximisation bias

The target uses $\max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1}, a')$, the maximum of noisy estimates. For any random estimates $\hat Q(a)$ with
means $q(a)$,

$$
\expect\brack{\max_a\hat Q(a)} \ge \max_a\expect\brack{\hat Q(a)} = \max_a q(a),
$$

because the maximum is a <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="expectation">Jensen's inequality</NoteLink> applies. More
directly: $\max_a\hat Q(a) \ge \hat Q(a^\dagger)$ for the truly best action $a^\dagger$, whose expectation is
$\max_a q(a)$. The bias is largest when many actions have similar values and the estimates are noisy. It is then fed
back through bootstrapping, and states with many actions look better than they are <Cite id="hasselt2010" />.

**Double Q-learning** removes the bias by using two tables, $Q^A$ and $Q^B$, trained on different samples. At each step
one of them, say $Q^A$, is chosen at random and updated with the other's estimate of its own best action:

$$
Q^A(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q^A(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\brack{R_{t+1} + \gamma Q^B\paren{S_{t+1}, \argmax_{a'}Q^A(S_{t+1}, a')} - Q^A(S_t, A_t)}.
$$

If $Q^B$ is independent of $Q^A$, then given the selected action $\hat a = \argmax_{a'}Q^A(S_{t+1}, a')$, the estimate
$Q^B(S_{t+1}, \hat a)$ has mean $q(S_{t+1}, \hat a) \le \max_{a'}q(S_{t+1}, a')$. The selection noise no longer inflates
the value. The estimator can underestimate, but it does not compound an upward bias <Cite id="hasselt2010" />.

## Worked example

**One update.** An agent in $S$ takes $A$ with $Q(S, A) = 0$, receives $R = 1$ and lands in $S'$ with
$Q(S', \cdot) = (0.2, 0.5, -0.1)$. With $\gamma = 0.9$ and $\alpha = 0.1$, the target is $1 + 0.9 \cdot 0.5 = 1.45$ and
$Q(S, A) \leftarrow 0 + 0.1 \cdot 1.45 = 0.145$. The update is the same whichever action the agent takes in $S'$.

**Maximisation bias.** A state has 10 actions, each with true value 0. Each action's estimate is its true value plus
independent standard normal noise. The single estimator reports $\max_a\hat Q(a)$, whose expectation is the expected
maximum of 10 standard normals, $1.54$, although every action is worth 0. The double estimator selects
$\hat a = \argmax_a\hat Q^A(a)$ and reports $\hat Q^B(\hat a)$, an independent estimate with mean 0: it is unbiased here.

**A maze.** The <NoteLink to="solving-a-maze">solving a maze</NoteLink> example runs Q-learning live on several mazes,
with a time slider that shows the values and greedy policy after any past episode.

## Variants

- **Deep Q-learning.** A neural network replaces the table; experience replay and a target network stabilise training.
  See <NoteLink to="deep-q-network">deep Q-network</NoteLink>.
- **Expected SARSA** with a greedy target policy is Q-learning; with an $\varepsilon$-greedy target it interpolates
  between Q-learning and SARSA.
- **$n$-step Q-learning.** Multi-step targets propagate reward faster but are off-policy for $n > 1$ unless corrected
  by importance weights or truncated at exploratory actions.

## Assumptions

- **Tabular representation.** The convergence theorem is for tables. With function approximation, off-policy
  bootstrapping can diverge.
- **Markov states and stationary environment.**

## Pitfalls

- **Online performance versus learned policy.** Q-learning's greedy policy can be optimal while the exploring agent
  performs poorly, as on the cliff. Report the greedy policy's return separately from the reward collected while
  learning.
- **Insufficient exploration.** Greedy behaviour with zero-initialised values can lock onto the first action that pays
  anything. Keep $\varepsilon > 0$ or initialise optimistically; see <NoteLink to="exploration-in-reinforcement-learning">exploration in
  RL</NoteLink>.
