<Definition>
**SARSA** learns an action-value table $Q(s, a)$ while acting with a policy derived from it, usually
<NoteLink to="epsilon-greedy">$\varepsilon$-greedy</NoteLink>. After each step it has the quintuple
$(S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$, which gives the method its name, and updates

$$
Q(S_t, A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t, A_t) + \alpha\brack{R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) - Q(S_t, A_t)},
$$

with $Q(s, \cdot) = 0$ at terminal states <Cite id="rummery1994" /> <Cite id="sutton2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

SARSA is <NoteLink to="temporal-difference-learning">TD(0)</NoteLink> applied to action values, combined with a policy
that keeps changing to follow them. The TD part evaluates the current policy $\pi$: the target
$R_{t+1} + \gamma Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$ is a sample of the <NoteLink to="bellman-equations">Bellman expectation equation</NoteLink>

$$
q_\pi(s, a) = \expect\brack{R_{t+1} + \gamma\,q_\pi(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s, A_t = a},
$$

because $A_{t+1}$ is drawn from $\pi$. The control part improves the policy, because an $\varepsilon$-greedy policy is
greedy with respect to $Q$ most of the time. Evaluation and improvement are interleaved at every step. This is
generalised <NoteLink to="policy-iteration">policy iteration</NoteLink> with both steps done approximately.

Action values, not state values, are what make this model-free. Improving a policy from $v_\pi$ requires the one-step
look-ahead $\sum_{s'}p(s' \mid s, a)v_\pi(s')$, which needs $p$. Improving it from $q_\pi$ needs only
$\argmax_a Q(s, a)$.

## On-policy learning

SARSA learns the value of the policy that generates its data, exploration included. With $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the
values it learns account for the random action taken one step in ten. A state next to a hazard gets a low value under
that policy, even if a perfectly greedy agent could pass it safely. SARSA's learned policy therefore avoids states
where exploration is dangerous. <NoteLink to="q-learning">Q-learning</NoteLink>, which <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstraps</NoteLink> from the greedy
action, learns the values of the greedy policy instead and ignores the risk that exploration creates.

## Convergence

Two conditions make tabular SARSA converge to $q_*$ with probability 1 in a <NoteLink to="markov-decision-process">finite MDP</NoteLink> <Cite id="singh2000" />:

- **<NoteLink to="stochastic-variational-inference">Robbins–Monro step sizes</NoteLink>.** For each pair $(s, a)$, the step sizes sum to infinity and their squares sum to a finite
  value.
- **GLIE: greedy in the limit with infinite exploration.** Every state-action pair is visited infinitely often, and the
  policy becomes greedy with respect to $Q$ in the limit. An $\varepsilon$-greedy policy with $\varepsilon_t = 1/t$
  satisfies both.

With a fixed $\varepsilon$, SARSA instead settles near the best $\varepsilon$-greedy policy, which is what the
cliff-walking figure shows.

## Worked example

An agent in state $S$ takes action $A$ with $Q(S, A) = -3$, receives $R = -1$ and lands in $S'$, where
$Q(S', \text{left}) = -2$ and $Q(S', \text{right}) = -5$. The $\varepsilon$-greedy policy explores and picks
$A' = \text{right}$. With $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\gamma = 1$:

- **SARSA** uses the action it will take: target $-1 + (-5) = -6$, and
  $Q(S, A) \leftarrow -3 + 0.5\,(-6 - (-3)) = -4.5$.
- **Q-learning** would use the best action: target $-1 + (-2) = -3$, and $Q(S, A)$ stays at $-3$.
- **Expected SARSA** (below) with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ over two actions weights left by $0.95$ and right by $0.05$:
  target $-1 + (0.95 \cdot (-2) + 0.05 \cdot (-5)) = -3.15$, and $Q(S, A) \leftarrow -3.075$.

SARSA's single sample punishes $(S, A)$ for an exploratory choice made afterwards. Averaged over many visits, that
punishment is the true cost of exploring from $S'$.

The <NoteLink to="solving-a-maze">solving a maze</NoteLink> example shows the consequence on a maze with two routes:
SARSA learns the long detour and Q-learning the short bridge between traps.

## Variants

- **Expected SARSA.** Replace $Q(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1})$ by its <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> under the policy,
  $\sum_{a'}\pi(a' \mid S_{t+1})\,Q(S_{t+1}, a')$. This removes the variance due to sampling $A_{t+1}$ at the cost of a
  sum over actions. With a greedy target policy it is Q-learning <Cite id="sutton2018" />.
- **$n$-step SARSA and SARSA($\lambda$).** Use $n$-step returns or eligibility traces over state-action pairs, as in
  <NoteLink to="temporal-difference-learning">TD($\lambda$)</NoteLink>. Traces speed up credit assignment when rewards
  are sparse.
- **Function approximation.** Semi-gradient SARSA with linear features is more stable than off-policy Q-learning with
  the same features, because it learns on-policy.

## Pitfalls

- **Freezing $\varepsilon$ and reading the greedy policy.** With constant exploration, $Q$ estimates the exploring
  policy's values. The greedy policy read off it is safe but can be longer than optimal, as the cliff figure shows.
- **Step size too large with stochastic rewards.** A large $\alpha$ lets single unlucky samples dominate $Q$.
