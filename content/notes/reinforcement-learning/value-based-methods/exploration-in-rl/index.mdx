<Definition>
**Exploration** is the choice of actions for the information they yield about the environment, rather than for their
estimated reward. An agent that only **exploits**, choosing $\argmax_a Q(s, a)$, never corrects an underestimated
action. In a <NoteLink to="markov-decision-process">Markov decision process</NoteLink>, exploration must reach states
as well as try actions, and the value of information depends on what the agent can do with it later.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">multi-armed bandit</NoteLink> is exploration in a single state. There,
<NoteLink to="epsilon-greedy">$\varepsilon$-greedy</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="ucb1">upper confidence bounds</NoteLink>
and <NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</NoteLink> balance trying arms against playing the best one.
MDPs add a new difficulty: to learn about a distant state, the agent must first get there. A sequence of individually
unpromising actions may be needed to reach the one region where reward is found. Exploration that decides afresh at
each step whether to act randomly cannot plan such a sequence.

## Dithering and its cost

**$\varepsilon$-greedy** takes a uniformly random action with probability $\varepsilon$ and the greedy action
otherwise. **Boltzmann** (softmax) exploration takes action $a$ with probability proportional to $\exp(Q(s, a)/\tau)$.
Both perturb the greedy policy independently at every step; they are called dithering strategies.

Consider a chain of $N$ states. From each state, action "right" moves one state right and action "left" returns to
the start. Only the right end pays a reward. A uniformly random agent must choose "right" $N$ times in a row, which
happens with probability $2^{-N}$ per attempt, so it needs about $2^N$ attempts to see the reward once. With $N = 20$
that is a million attempts. Every value is zero until then, so $\varepsilon$-greedy on zero-initialised values behaves
like the random agent. The number of steps needed grows exponentially with the depth of the reward.

## Optimism

An optimistic agent treats what it does not know as good. It then goes where it is uncertain because it expects reward
there, and either finds reward or learns that the region is poor.

- **Optimistic initial values.** Initialise $Q(s, a) = R_{\max}/(1 - \gamma)$, the largest possible value. Every
  untried action looks best until tried. Unlike dithering, this makes the agent travel to unvisited states on purpose
  <Cite id="sutton2018" />.
- **R-max.** Learn a model of the MDP. Treat any state-action pair tried fewer than $m$ times as leading to a fictitious
  state that pays $R_{\max}$ for ever, and act optimally in that optimistic model. Either the optimistic policy
  collects near-optimal reward, or it visits an unknown pair and learns about it. R-max finds a near-optimal policy
  with high probability within a number of steps polynomial in the numbers of states and actions, $1/(1 - \gamma)$
  and the accuracy <Cite id="brafman2002" />.
- **Count-based bonuses.** Add $\beta/\sqrt{N(s, a)}$ to the reward, where $N(s, a)$ counts visits. The bonus is the
  width of a confidence interval on the mean reward, as in UCB. MBIE-EB plans in the learned model with this bonus
  and has the same kind of polynomial guarantee <Cite id="strehl2008" />.

## Pseudo-counts and intrinsic rewards

In large state spaces each exact state is seen once, and counts are useless. Three approaches generalise them.

**Pseudo-counts.** Let $\rho_n(s)$ be the probability a density model trained on $n$ states assigns to $s$, and
$\rho'_n(s)$ the probability after one more update on $s$. If the model behaved like an empirical frequency with a
pseudo-count $\hat N$ out of a pseudo-total $\hat n$, then $\rho = \hat N/\hat n$ and $\rho' = (\hat N + 1)/(\hat n + 1)$.
Solving the two equations gives

$$
\hat N(s) = \frac{\rho_n(s)\,(1 - \rho'_n(s))}{\rho'_n(s) - \rho_n(s)}.
$$

A state the model has learned well barely changes its probability after one more update, so $\hat N$ is large. A
novel state changes it a lot, so $\hat N$ is small. The bonus $\beta/\sqrt{\hat N + 0.01}$ then generalises
count-based exploration to images. It made progress on Montezuma's Revenge, an Atari game whose first reward requires
a long sequence of precise moves <Cite id="bellemare2016" />.

**Prediction-error curiosity.** Train a model to predict something about the next state, and pay the agent its
prediction error. The intrinsic curiosity module predicts learned features of $s'$ from $s$ and $a$
<Cite id="pathak2017" />. **Random network distillation** trains a network to match the outputs of a fixed, randomly
initialised network on visited states; the error is small on familiar states and large on novel ones
<Cite id="burda2019" />.

A random-network target is deterministic, which avoids the **noisy-TV problem**: a predictor of genuinely random
observations, such as a television showing static, never stops being surprised, and a curious agent watches it for
ever.

## Posterior sampling

Thompson sampling extends to MDPs. Sample a plausible MDP, or a plausible $Q$-function, from a posterior; act
greedily with respect to the sample for a whole episode. Committing to one sample for many steps gives deep
exploration, because a sample that is optimistic about a distant region makes the agent go there directly.
Bootstrapped DQN approximates the posterior with an ensemble of Q-network heads trained on bootstrap resamples of the
data, and follows one randomly chosen head per episode <Cite id="osband2016" />.

## Worked example

**Count bonus.** With $\beta = 0.1$, a pair tried 4 times earns a bonus $0.1/\sqrt 4 = 0.05$, and one tried 100 times
earns $0.01$. The bonus fades as $1/\sqrt{N}$, as fast as the standard error of a mean reward.

**Pseudo-count.** A density model assigns a frame probability $\rho = 0.01$; after one update on it, $\rho' = 0.0102$.
Then $\hat N = 0.01 \cdot 0.9898/0.0002 = 49.5$: the model treats the frame as seen about 50 times out of about 4,950
frames.

## Pitfalls

- **Bonuses change the objective.** An agent maximising reward plus bonus is not maximising reward. The bonus must
  decay, or be removed at evaluation.
- **Detachment.** An agent can leave a promising frontier, forget how to return to it, and have exhausted the bonus on
  the way.
- **Stochastic distractions.** Prediction-error rewards are attracted to unpredictable noise; see the noisy-TV problem
  above.
