<Definition>
**Temporal-difference (TD) learning** estimates the value function $v_\pi$ of a policy $\pi$ from sampled transitions,
without a model of the environment. After each transition $(S_t, R_{t+1}, S_{t+1})$ generated by $\pi$, **TD(0)**
updates the estimate at the state just left:

$$
V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\,\delta_t, \qquad \delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1}) - V(S_t),
$$

with step size $\alpha \in (0, 1]$. $\delta_t$ is the **TD error**. The value of a terminal state is 0
<Cite id="sutton1988" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="bellman-equations">Bellman expectation equation</NoteLink> says $v_\pi(s)$ equals the expected value
of $R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1})$ given $S_t = s$. TD learning uses one sample of that expression, with the current
estimate $V$ in place of the unknown $v_\pi$, as a regression target. It learns a guess from a guess. This is called
**bootstrapping**.

The alternative, **Monte Carlo** evaluation, waits until the episode ends and moves $V(S_t)$ towards the observed
return $G_t$:

$$
V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\paren{G_t - V(S_t)}.
$$

Monte Carlo needs complete episodes. TD updates after every step, so it works in continuing tasks and learns during an
episode.

## TD(0) as stochastic approximation

Stochastic approximation solves an equation $\expect[Y \mid \theta] = \theta$ from noisy samples $Y$ by the update
$\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha(Y - \theta)$ <Cite id="robbins1951" />. For state $s$, the unknown is $v_\pi(s)$
and the equation is the Bellman equation,

$$
v_\pi(s) = \expect_\pi\brack{R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s} = (T_\pi v_\pi)(s).
$$

Replacing $v_\pi$ on the right by the current estimate $V$ gives the sample $Y = R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1})$ and the
TD(0) update. The expected update at state $s$, averaging over the next reward and state, is

$$
\expect\brack{\alpha\,\delta_t \mid S_t = s} = \alpha\paren{(T_\pi V)(s) - V(s)}.
$$

On average, TD(0) moves $V$ a fraction $\alpha$ of the way towards $T_\pi V$. The operator $T_\pi$ is a
$\gamma$-contraction with fixed point $v_\pi$, so the averaged dynamics are a damped version of iterating $T_\pi$, and
the noise averages out when the step sizes shrink.

**Convergence.** In a finite MDP, tabular TD(0) converges to $v_\pi$ with probability 1 if every state is visited
infinitely often and the step sizes $\alpha_n(s)$ used at the $n$-th visit to $s$ satisfy the Robbins–Monro conditions

$$
\sum_{n}\alpha_n(s) = \infty, \qquad \sum_{n}\alpha_n(s)^2 < \infty,
$$

for example $\alpha_n = 1/n$ <Cite id="jaakkola1994" />. The first condition lets the estimate travel any distance; the
second makes the accumulated noise finite. With a constant $\alpha$, the estimate does not converge but fluctuates
around $v_\pi$ with a spread that shrinks with $\alpha$.

## Bias and variance

The Monte Carlo target $G_t$ is an unbiased sample of $v_\pi(S_t)$. The TD target $R_{t+1} + \gamma V(S_{t+1})$ is
biased whenever $V(S_{t+1}) \ne v_\pi(S_{t+1})$. In exchange, the TD target has much lower variance.

To see how much lower, write the Monte Carlo error as a sum of TD errors computed with the true values,
$\delta^\pi_k = R_{k+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{k+1}) - v_\pi(S_k)$. The sum telescopes:

$$
\sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\gamma^{k-t}\delta^\pi_k = \sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\gamma^{k-t}R_{k+1} + \sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\paren{\gamma^{k-t+1}v_\pi(S_{k+1}) - \gamma^{k-t}v_\pi(S_k)} = G_t - v_\pi(S_t).
$$

By the Bellman equation each $\delta^\pi_k$ has mean zero given the history up to $S_k$. Terms with mean zero given
everything before them are uncorrelated, so the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances:

$$
\var\paren{G_t \mid S_t} = \sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\gamma^{2(k-t)}\,\expect\brack{(\delta^\pi_k)^2 \mid S_t}.
$$

The TD target with the true values, $R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1})$, has conditional variance
$\expect[(\delta^\pi_t)^2 \mid S_t]$, the first term alone. The Monte Carlo target adds the randomness of every later
step. In long episodes with noisy rewards or transitions, the difference is large.

## Batch learning and certainty equivalence

If a fixed batch of episodes is replayed until the updates converge, Monte Carlo and TD(0) reach different answers
<Cite id="sutton1988" />. Batch Monte Carlo gives each state the average of its observed returns, the least-squares fit
to the data. Batch TD(0) gives the exact value of the Markov chain whose transition probabilities and rewards are the
empirical frequencies in the batch: the **certainty-equivalence** estimate. When the process is Markov, the second
answer generalises better, because it pools what is known about a state's successors across every episode that
passes through them.

## $n$-step returns and TD($\lambda$)

Between the one-step TD target and the full return lie the **$n$-step returns**,

$$
G_t^{(n)} = R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n} + \gamma^n V(S_{t+n}).
$$

$n = 1$ is TD(0) and $n = \infty$ is Monte Carlo. The **$\lambda$-return** averages all of them with geometric weights,
$G_t^\lambda = (1 - \lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}G_t^{(n)}$, for $\lambda \in [0, 1]$. TD($\lambda$) moves
$V(S_t)$ towards $G_t^\lambda$, trading bias ($\lambda$ near 0) for variance ($\lambda$ near 1) <Cite id="sutton1988" />.

The $\lambda$-return needs future rewards. **Eligibility traces** implement the same update online. Each state carries
a trace, decayed every step and incremented on a visit:

$$
e_t(s) = \gamma\lambda\,e_{t-1}(s) + \indicator[S_t = s], \qquad V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\,\delta_t\,e_t(s) \quad\text{for all } s.
$$

Each TD error is broadcast backwards to recently visited states in proportion to how recently and how often they were
visited. With $V$ held fixed during an episode, the summed trace updates equal the summed $\lambda$-return updates.

<Derivation title="Why traces reproduce the λ-return">
With $V$ fixed, the same telescoping as above, weighted by $(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}$, gives
$G_t^\lambda - V(S_t) = \sum_{k \ge t}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\delta_k$. The forward view updates state $s$ by
$\alpha\sum_{t: S_t = s}\sum_{k \ge t}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\delta_k$ over an episode. Exchanging the order of the sums,
the TD error $\delta_k$ contributes $\alpha\,\delta_k\sum_{t \le k: S_t = s}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}$ to state $s$. That
inner sum is exactly the trace $e_k(s)$ defined by the recursion above, so the two views make the same total update.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

A walker is in state $A$ with estimate $V(A) = 0.5$. It moves to state $B$, with $V(B) = 0.6$, and receives reward 1.
With $\gamma = 0.9$ and $\alpha = 0.1$, the TD error is $\delta = 1 + 0.9 \cdot 0.6 - 0.5 = 1.04$ and the update is
$V(A) \leftarrow 0.5 + 0.1 \cdot 1.04 = 0.604$. The outcome was better than $V(A)$ predicted, so the prediction rises.
A Monte Carlo learner would change nothing until the episode ended.

## Function approximation

With a parametric estimate $V_{\wvec}(s)$, **semi-gradient TD(0)** updates
$\wvec \leftarrow \wvec + \alpha\,\delta_t\nabla_{\wvec}V_{\wvec}(S_t)$. It is called semi-gradient because it
differentiates the prediction but not the target, which also depends on $\wvec$. For linear
$V_{\wvec}(s) = \wvec\transpose\phivec(s)$ and on-policy sampling, it converges to a fixed point whose error is within
a factor $1/(1 - \gamma)$ of the best linear fit's <Cite id="tsitsiklis1997" />. Combining bootstrapping, function
approximation and off-policy data can diverge, as the <NoteLink to="deep-q-network">deep Q-network</NoteLink> note
discusses.

## Assumptions

- **Markov states.** TD's advantage over Monte Carlo relies on the Bellman equation holding. With non-Markov
  observations, bootstrapping from $V(S_{t+1})$ propagates errors, and Monte Carlo can be better.
- **On-policy data.** The transitions are generated by the policy being evaluated.

## Pitfalls

- **Initial values matter.** Early TD targets are mostly the initial estimates. A poor initialisation biases learning
  until rewards have propagated back.
- **Step size too large.** A constant $\alpha$ near 1 makes $V(s)$ track the latest sample, with an error floor that
  never falls; the figure shows the floor rising with $\alpha$.
