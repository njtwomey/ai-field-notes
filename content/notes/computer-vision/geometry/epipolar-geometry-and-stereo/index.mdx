<Definition>
Two cameras with centres $\cvec_1 \neq \cvec_2$ view the same scene. The **epipolar constraint** states that
corresponding image points $\xvec_1 \leftrightarrow \xvec_2$, in homogeneous pixel coordinates, satisfy
$\xvec_2^\top \Fmat \xvec_1 = 0$ for a $3 \times 3$ **fundamental matrix** $\Fmat$ of rank 2. In normalised coordinates
$\hat\xvec_i = \Kmat_i^{-1}\xvec_i$ the same constraint reads $\hat\xvec_2^\top \Emat \hat\xvec_1 = 0$, with the
**essential matrix** $\Emat = [\tvec]_\times \Rmat$ built from the relative rotation $\Rmat$ and translation $\tvec$
between the cameras.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A pixel in one image fixes a ray into the scene, not a point: the scene point can be anywhere along it. Seen from a
second camera, that ray is a line in the second image. So the match for a pixel must lie on a known line, the epipolar
line, and a search for correspondences becomes one-dimensional.

All epipolar lines in an image pass through one point, the epipole: the image of the other camera's centre. When the
two cameras are side by side and parallel, the epipoles are at infinity and the epipolar lines are the image rows. The
horizontal shift of a point between the two images, its disparity, is then inversely proportional to its depth, which
is how stereo cameras and human eyes judge distance.

## Details

### The essential matrix

Place camera 1 at the origin of its own frame. A scene point with coordinates $\Xmat$ in camera 1 has coordinates
$\Xmat_2 = \Rmat\Xmat + \tvec$ in camera 2. Its normalised image points are $\hat\xvec_1 \sim \Xmat$ and
$\hat\xvec_2 \sim \Xmat_2$.

The two camera centres and the scene point span a plane, the epipolar plane. In camera 2's frame, camera 1's centre is at
$\tvec$, so the vectors $\tvec$, $\Rmat\Xmat = \Xmat_2 - \tvec$ and $\Xmat_2$ all lie in it. To turn coplanarity into an
equation, take the cross product of $\tvec$ with both sides of $\Xmat_2 = \Rmat\Xmat + \tvec$:

$$
\tvec \times \Xmat_2 = \tvec \times \Rmat\Xmat.
$$

The left side is perpendicular to $\Xmat_2$, so its dot product with $\Xmat_2$ is zero:

$$
\Xmat_2^\top (\tvec \times \Rmat\Xmat) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \Xmat_2^\top [\tvec]_\times \Rmat \Xmat = 0,
$$

where $[\tvec]_\times$ is the skew-symmetric matrix with $[\tvec]_\times \avec = \tvec \times \avec$:

$$
[\tvec]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -t_3 & t_2 \\ t_3 & 0 & -t_1 \\ -t_2 & t_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Dividing by the two depths replaces $\Xmat$ and $\Xmat_2$ by $\hat\xvec_1$ and $\hat\xvec_2$, giving
$\hat\xvec_2^\top \Emat \hat\xvec_1 = 0$ with $\Emat = [\tvec]_\times\Rmat$ <Cite id="longuethiggins1981" />. The
equation is homogeneous in $\tvec$, so the scale of the translation cannot be recovered from images alone. $\Emat$ has
5 degrees of freedom: 3 for rotation and 2 for the direction of translation.

<Derivation title="Derivation: the singular values of E are (s, s, 0)">
$\Emat\Emat^\top = [\tvec]_\times \Rmat\Rmat^\top [\tvec]_\times^\top = [\tvec]_\times [\tvec]_\times^\top$, since
$\Rmat$ is orthogonal. For any vector $\avec$, $[\tvec]_\times^\top\avec = -\tvec \times \avec$, so
$[\tvec]_\times[\tvec]_\times^\top\avec = -\tvec \times (\tvec \times \avec) = \norm{\tvec}^2\avec -
(\tvec^\top\avec)\tvec$ by the vector triple product. So $\Emat\Emat^\top = \norm{\tvec}^2\I - \tvec\tvec^\top$. Its
eigenvalues are 0 on $\tvec$ and $\norm{\tvec}^2$ on the two directions orthogonal to $\tvec$. The singular values of
$\Emat$ are the square roots: $(\norm{\tvec}, \norm{\tvec}, 0)$. A $3 \times 3$ matrix is an essential matrix exactly
when it has two equal singular values and a zero one <Cite id="hartley2004" />.
</Derivation>

### The fundamental matrix

In pixels, $\hat\xvec_i = \Kmat_i^{-1}\xvec_i$. Substituting gives $\xvec_2^\top \Kmat_2^{-\top}\Emat\Kmat_1^{-1}
\xvec_1 = 0$, so

$$
\Fmat = \Kmat_2^{-\top}\, [\tvec]_\times \Rmat\, \Kmat_1^{-1}.
$$

$\Fmat$ relates uncalibrated images. Its properties follow from the constraint:

- **Epipolar lines.** For fixed $\xvec_1$, the points $\xvec_2$ with $\xvec_2^\top(\Fmat\xvec_1) = 0$ form the line
  $\lvec_2 = \Fmat\xvec_1$ in the second image. Likewise $\lvec_1 = \Fmat^\top\xvec_2$.
- **Epipoles.** Every epipolar line in image 2 passes through the epipole $\evec_2$, so $\evec_2^\top\Fmat\xvec_1 = 0$
  for every $\xvec_1$, that is $\Fmat^\top\evec_2 = \zeros$. Likewise $\Fmat\evec_1 = \zeros$.
- **Rank 2.** A skew-symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix is singular, since $[\tvec]_\times\tvec = \zeros$. So
  $\det\Fmat = 0$, and $\Fmat$ has $9 - 1 - 1 = 7$ degrees of freedom: nine entries, less one for scale and one for
  the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> constraint.

### The eight-point algorithm

Each correspondence $(u_1, v_1) \leftrightarrow (u_2, v_2)$ gives one equation linear in the nine entries of $\Fmat$:

$$
\xvec_2^\top \Fmat \xvec_1 = \sum_{i, j} x_{2i}\, x_{1j}\, F_{ij} =
(u_2u_1, u_2v_1, u_2, v_2u_1, v_2v_1, v_2, u_1, v_1, 1) \cdot \fvec = 0.
$$

Eight or more correspondences stack into $\Amat\fvec = \zeros$. As in the homography DLT, the least-squares solution
with $\norm{\fvec} = 1$ is the right singular vector of $\Amat$ for its smallest singular value. With noise the result
has <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">full rank</NoteLink>, so its epipolar lines do not meet in a point. Replace it by the nearest rank-2 matrix in the <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">Frobenius
norm</NoteLink>: take its <Gloss name="svd" /> $\Umat\diag(s_1, s_2, s_3)\Vmat^\top$ and set $s_3 = 0$.

The coordinates must be normalised first, as for the homography: translate each image's points to zero mean and scale
them to a mean distance of $\sqrt{2}$, estimate, then undo the transforms. Without this the products $u_2u_1$ are of
order $10^6$ and the linear system is badly conditioned. With it, the eight-point algorithm is competitive with
iterative methods <Cite id="hartley1997" />.

Seven points suffice if $\det\Fmat = 0$ is imposed during the solve, which gives a cubic with one or three real
solutions. For calibrated cameras, five points determine $\Emat$ <Cite id="nister2004" />. All are run inside RANSAC
to reject false matches (see <NoteLink to="homography" />).

Given $\Emat$, the relative pose follows from its SVD. There are four $(\Rmat, \tvec)$ pairs consistent with it,
differing by the sign of $\tvec$ and a rotation of 180 degrees about the baseline. Only one places the scene points in
front of both cameras; triangulating one point selects it.

### Rectified stereo and disparity

In a rectified stereo pair both cameras have the same intrinsics, their image planes are coplanar and their $x$ axes
lie along the baseline, the line joining the centres. Let the right camera sit a distance $B$ to the right of the left
one. A point $(X, Y, Z)$ in the left camera's frame is at $(X - B, Y, Z)$ in the right camera's frame. With focal
length $f$ in pixels and principal point $c_x$,

$$
u_\ell = f\frac{X}{Z} + c_x, \qquad u_r = f\frac{X - B}{Z} + c_x, \qquad v_\ell = v_r.
$$

The rows agree, so each epipolar line is an image row. The disparity is

$$
d = u_\ell - u_r = \frac{fB}{Z} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad Z = \frac{fB}{d}.
$$

Depth is inversely proportional to disparity. A point at infinity has zero disparity. Differentiating gives the depth
resolution:

$$
\abs{\frac{\partial Z}{\partial d}} = \frac{fB}{d^2} = \frac{Z^2}{fB}, \qquad \delta Z \approx \frac{Z^2}{fB}\,\delta d.
$$

A fixed disparity error $\delta d$ gives a depth error that grows with the square of the distance. A longer baseline
or focal length reduces it, at the cost of more occlusion and a smaller overlap between the views. Cameras that are not
rectified are made so by warping each image with a homography that sends its epipole to infinity along the $x$ axis.

### Stereo matching

A dense stereo algorithm computes a disparity for every pixel. Scharstein and Szeliski divided the task into four
steps: a matching cost per pixel and disparity, aggregation of the cost over a support window, optimisation of the
disparity, and refinement <Cite id="scharstein2002" />. Block matching compares a window around each left pixel with
windows along the same row of the right image using the sum of squared differences or normalised cross-correlation,
and picks the disparity with the lowest cost. It fails in textureless regions and at occlusions. Semi-global matching
adds a penalty for disparity changes between neighbours and minimises it along 8 or 16 directions by dynamic
programming <Cite id="hirschmuller2008" />. Learned matchers now build and filter the cost volume with neural networks.

## Worked example

**A pure sideways translation.** Let $\Rmat = \I$ and $\tvec = (1, 0, 0)$. Then

$$
\Emat = [\tvec]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\hat\xvec_2^\top\Emat\hat\xvec_1 = \hat\xvec_2^\top (0, -1, \hat y_1)^\top = \hat y_1 - \hat y_2 = 0
$$

for points $\hat\xvec_i = (\hat x_i, \hat y_i, 1)$. Matches share a row: this is the rectified case. The epipole solves
$\Emat\evec_1 = \zeros$, giving $\evec_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, a point at infinity along the rows. The singular values of
$\Emat$ are $(1, 1, 0)$, as they must be for $\norm{\tvec} = 1$.

**Depth from disparity.** A stereo camera has $f = 700$ pixels and a baseline $B = 0.12$ m, so $fB = 84$ pixel-metres.
A point with disparity $d = 20$ pixels is at $Z = 84/20 = 4.2$ m. An error of one pixel in disparity changes the depth
by about $Z^2/(fB) = 4.2^2/84 = 0.21$ m. At $Z = 1$ m the disparity is 84 pixels and the same one-pixel error moves the
depth by only 1.2 cm.

## Assumptions

- Both images follow the pinhole model: <NoteLink to="pinhole-camera-model">lens distortion</NoteLink> is removed, and a point has one image in each view.
- The scene is static between the two exposures, or both images are taken at the same instant.
- The camera centres differ. For a pure rotation the constraint degenerates: $\tvec = \zeros$ gives $\Emat = \zeros$,
  and the views are related by a homography instead.

## Pitfalls

- **Planar scenes.** If all correspondences lie on one plane, $\Fmat$ is not determined: a whole family of fundamental
  matrices fits them. Check for a dominant homography first.
- **Skipping normalisation or rank enforcement.** An unnormalised eight-point estimate can be badly wrong, and a
  full-rank $\Fmat$ gives epipolar lines that do not meet.
- **The epipolar constraint is necessary, not sufficient.** A false match that happens to lie on the epipolar line
  passes the test. RANSAC with a tight threshold and a third view reduce such errors.
- **Depth far away.** Depth error grows as $Z^2$. A small-baseline stereo camera is useless beyond a few tens of
  metres whatever its matching accuracy.
