<Definition>
A homography, or projective transformation of the plane, maps a point with <NoteLink to="pinhole-camera-model">homogeneous coordinates</NoteLink>
$\xvec = (x, y, 1)^\top$ to $\xvec' \sim \Hmat\xvec$, where $\Hmat$ is an invertible $3 \times 3$ matrix and $\sim$
means equal up to a nonzero scale. In pixel coordinates,
$x' = (h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}) / (h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33})$ and likewise for $y'$ with the second row. $\Hmat$
and $c\Hmat$ define the same map, so a homography has 8 degrees of freedom <Cite id="hartley2004" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Photograph a flat poster from two different places. Each pixel of the first photograph shows one point of the poster,
and that point appears at some pixel of the second. The correspondence is a homography. Straight lines stay straight,
but lengths, angles and parallelism are not kept: the rectangle of the poster becomes a general quadrilateral, and its
parallel edges converge to a vanishing point. Four corners fix the whole map.

The same map relates two photographs taken from one position with the camera turned. That is why panorama stitching
works for any scene, flat or not, as long as the camera rotates about its centre.

## Details

### Where homographies come from

**A plane seen by a camera.** Choose world coordinates so that the plane is $Z = 0$. With the camera matrix
$\Pmat = \Kmat\brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \rvec_3 \mid \tvec}$, where $\rvec_i$ are the columns of $\Rmat$, a point
$(X, Y, 0)$ on the plane projects to

$$
\xvec \sim \Kmat \brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \rvec_3 \mid \tvec} \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} =
\Kmat \brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \tvec} \begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The zero coordinate removes the third column, so plane-to-image is the $3 \times 3$ matrix
$\Hmat = \Kmat\brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \tvec}$. Two cameras viewing the plane give $\xvec_1 \sim \Hmat_1 \pvec$ and
$\xvec_2 \sim \Hmat_2 \pvec$ for plane coordinates $\pvec$, so $\xvec_2 \sim \Hmat_2 \Hmat_1^{-1} \xvec_1$.

**A rotating camera.** Let a camera with intrinsics $\Kmat$ rotate by $\Rmat$ about its centre. A point with camera
coordinates $\Xmat$ images at $\xvec_1 \sim \Kmat\Xmat$ before and $\xvec_2 \sim \Kmat\Rmat\Xmat$ after. Eliminating
$\Xmat = \Kmat^{-1}\xvec_1$ up to scale gives $\xvec_2 \sim \Kmat\Rmat\Kmat^{-1}\xvec_1$. The depth of the point has
cancelled, so the map holds for every point in the scene.

If the camera both moves and the scene is not planar, points at different depths shift by different amounts
(parallax), and no single homography relates the views. The relation is then the epipolar constraint (see
<NoteLink to="epipolar-geometry-and-stereo" />).

### Special cases

Homographies contain a hierarchy of simpler maps. With $h_{31} = h_{32} = 0$ the denominator is constant and the map is
affine (6 degrees of freedom): parallel lines stay parallel. Restricting the upper-left block to a scaled rotation
gives a similarity (4), and to a rotation a Euclidean motion (3). The bottom row $(h_{31}, h_{32})$ carries the
perspective effect: the line $h_{31}x + h_{32}y + h_{33} = 0$ maps to infinity, and the images of parallel lines meet
on the image of the line at infinity. The vanishing point of lines with direction $(d_x, d_y)$ is
$\Hmat(d_x, d_y, 0)^\top$.

### Direct linear transform

Given $n \ge 4$ correspondences $\xvec_i \leftrightarrow \xvec'_i$, find $\Hmat$. The relation $\xvec' \sim \Hmat\xvec$
holds up to scale, so it is not a linear equation as written. Two vectors are parallel exactly when their cross product
is zero, so it is equivalent to

$$
\xvec'_i \times \Hmat\xvec_i = \zeros.
$$

Write $\hvec_j^\top$ for row $j$ of $\Hmat$, so $\Hmat\xvec_i = (\hvec_1^\top\xvec_i, \hvec_2^\top\xvec_i,
\hvec_3^\top\xvec_i)$, and $\xvec'_i = (u_i, v_i, 1)$. The cross product is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
v_i\, \hvec_3^\top\xvec_i - \hvec_2^\top\xvec_i \\
\hvec_1^\top\xvec_i - u_i\, \hvec_3^\top\xvec_i \\
u_i\, \hvec_2^\top\xvec_i - v_i\, \hvec_1^\top\xvec_i
\end{bmatrix} = \zeros.
$$

The third row equals $-u_i$ times the first minus $v_i$ times the second, so only two rows are independent. Each is
linear in the nine unknowns $\hvec = (\hvec_1, \hvec_2, \hvec_3) \in \reals^9$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\zeros^\top & -\xvec_i^\top & v_i\,\xvec_i^\top \\
\xvec_i^\top & \zeros^\top & -u_i\,\xvec_i^\top
\end{bmatrix} \hvec = \zeros.
$$

Stacking all $n$ correspondences gives $\Amat\hvec = \zeros$ with $\Amat \in \reals^{2n \times 9}$. Four points in
general position, no three collinear, give 8 independent equations and determine $\hvec$ up to scale.

With more points and noise, $\Amat\hvec = \zeros$ has no exact solution other than zero. Fix the scale by
$\norm{\hvec} = 1$ and minimise $\norm{\Amat\hvec}$. Take the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition" />
$\Amat = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$ with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_9 \ge 0$. Since $\Umat$ has
orthonormal columns and $\Vmat$ is orthogonal, substituting $\yvec = \Vmat^\top\hvec$ gives

$$
\norm{\Amat\hvec}^2 = \norm{\Sigmamat \Vmat^\top\hvec}^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{9} \sigma_k^2 y_k^2, \qquad
\norm{\yvec} = \norm{\hvec} = 1.
$$

The sum weights the $\sigma_k^2$ by the $y_k^2$, which sum to one, so it is smallest when all the weight is on the
smallest $\sigma_k^2$.
So $\yvec = \evec_9$ and $\hvec = \Vmat\evec_9 = \vvec_9$, the right singular vector for the smallest singular value.
The minimum is $\sigma_9^2$, which is zero for exact data.

### Normalisation

Pixel coordinates are of order $10^2$ to $10^3$. The rows of $\Amat$ then mix entries of 1, of order $10^3$ and, in
the products $u_i x_i$, of order $10^6$, and the solution is badly conditioned. Hartley showed that normalising first
is essential <Cite id="hartley1997" />: translate each image's points so that their centroid is at the origin, and
scale them so that their mean distance from it is $\sqrt{2}$. With these similarity transforms $\Tmat$ and $\Tmat'$,
solve for $\tilde\Hmat$ from $\tilde\xvec_i = \Tmat\xvec_i$ and $\tilde\xvec'_i = \Tmat'\xvec'_i$, then undo it:
$\Hmat = \Tmat'^{-1}\tilde\Hmat\Tmat$.

The DLT minimises an algebraic error with no geometric meaning. The estimate is usually refined by minimising the
reprojection error $\sum_i d(\xvec'_i, \Hmat\xvec_i)^2$ in pixels, a nonlinear least-squares problem started from the
DLT solution.

### RANSAC

Correspondences from <NoteLink to="feature-detection-and-descriptors">feature matching</NoteLink> contain outliers,
often a large fraction. One wrong match with an error of hundreds of pixels ruins a least-squares fit. Random sample
consensus (RANSAC) fits the model to many small random subsets and keeps the one most of the data agrees with
<Cite id="fischler1981" />:

1. Draw a minimal sample of $s$ correspondences at random: $s = 4$ for a homography.
2. Fit the model to the sample by the DLT.
3. Count the inliers: correspondences whose <NoteLink to="structure-from-motion">reprojection error</NoteLink> is below a threshold $\epsilon$, such as 3 pixels.
4. Repeat $N$ times and keep the model with the most inliers. Refit it to all its inliers.

The number of samples follows from asking for at least one sample free of outliers. Let a fraction $w$ of the
correspondences be inliers. A sample of $s$ points drawn independently is all inliers with probability $w^s$ and
contains an outlier with probability $1 - w^s$. All $N$ independent samples contain an outlier with probability
$(1 - w^s)^N$. To make that failure probability at most $1 - p$:

$$
(1 - w^s)^N \le 1 - p \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad N \ge \frac{\log(1 - p)}{\log(1 - w^s)}.
$$

The direction of the inequality flips because both logarithms are negative. $N$ does not depend on the number of
correspondences, only on the inlier ratio and the sample size. The inlier ratio is usually unknown; adaptive RANSAC
starts with a pessimistic $w$ and lowers $N$ each time a model with more inliers is found.

## Worked example

**A perspective homography.** Map the unit square with corners $(0, 0), (1, 0), (1, 1), (0, 1)$ to the trapezoid
$(0, 0), (1, 0), (0.75, 0.75), (0.25, 0.75)$, a square floor tile seen from above and in front. Solving the DLT gives,
scaled so that $h_{33} = 1$,

$$
\Hmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check one corner: $\Hmat(1, 1, 1)^\top = (1.5, 1.5, 2)^\top \sim (0.75, 0.75)$. The square's centre maps to
$\Hmat(0.5, 0.5, 1)^\top = (0.75, 0.75, 1.5)^\top \sim (0.5, 0.5)$. The trapezoid is 0.75 high, so the centre lands
two thirds of the way up, not halfway: the far half of the tile is foreshortened. The vertical edges have direction
$(0, 1, 0)$ and vanish at $\Hmat(0, 1, 0)^\top = (0.5, 1.5, 1)^\top \sim (0.5, 1.5)$. The horizontal edges have
direction $(1, 0, 0)$, which maps to $(1, 0, 0)$, a point at infinity, so they stay parallel.

**RANSAC iterations.** With half the matches correct, $w = 0.5$, and success probability $p = 0.99$, a homography needs

$$
N = \frac{\log 0.01}{\log(1 - 0.5^4)} = \frac{-4.605}{-0.0645} = 71.4,
$$

so 72 samples. A fundamental matrix from 8 points needs $\log 0.01 / \log(1 - 0.5^8) = 1177$ samples at the same $w$.

## Assumptions

- The scene is planar, or the camera only rotates about its centre. Otherwise a homography holds only approximately,
  for distant scenes where parallax is small.
- Lens distortion has been removed. A distorted image does not map straight lines to straight lines.
- Inliers have small, roughly isotropic errors, so one pixel threshold separates them from outliers.

## Pitfalls

- **Unnormalised DLT.** Solving in raw pixel coordinates gives visibly worse homographies for the same data.
- **Degenerate samples.** Three collinear points in a minimal sample leave $\Amat$ with rank below 8. Reject such
  samples before fitting.
- **Threshold choice.** A threshold $\epsilon$ that is too small rejects good matches; one that is too large accepts
  outliers and biases the fit. Set it from the expected localisation error of the features.
- **Too few iterations.** $N$ grows very fast as $w$ falls. With $w = 0.2$ and $s = 4$, $p = 0.99$ needs 2876 samples.
  Filter matches with the ratio test before RANSAC.
