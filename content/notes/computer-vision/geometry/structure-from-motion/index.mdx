<Definition>
Structure from motion (SfM) takes $m$ images of a static scene and correspondences between them, and estimates the
camera matrices $\Pmat_i = \Kmat_i\brack{\Rmat_i \mid \tvec_i}$ and the 3D points $\Xmat_j$ that best explain the
observed image points $\xvec_{ij}$. "Best" means minimising the total reprojection error
$\sum_{(i, j)} \norm{\xvec_{ij} - \pi(\Pmat_i, \Xmat_j)}^2$, where $\pi$ projects a point with a camera and the sum runs
over the observed pairs. This final optimisation is bundle adjustment <Cite id="triggs2000" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A single image loses depth. Two images from known positions recover it by intersecting rays. SfM solves the harder
problem where neither the positions nor the scene are known: each image constrains the others through the points they
share. Estimating a few cameras and points from their pairwise geometry gives a rough reconstruction. Adding more
images one at a time, and repeatedly adjusting all cameras and points together so that every projected point lands
where it was observed, turns thousands of photographs into a consistent 3D model.

The name "bundle" refers to the bundle of rays leaving each camera centre towards the points it sees. Bundle adjustment
moves cameras and points until every bundle passes through its image points.

## Details

### Triangulation

Given cameras $\Pmat_1, \Pmat_2$ and a match $\xvec_1 \leftrightarrow \xvec_2$, find $\Xmat$. Each view gives
$\xvec_i \sim \Pmat_i\Xmat$. As for the homography, write this as $\xvec_i \times \Pmat_i\Xmat = \zeros$. With
$\pvec_k^{\top}$ the rows of $\Pmat_i$ and $\xvec_i = (u_i, v_i, 1)$, two independent rows of the cross product give

$$
u_i\, \pvec_3^\top\Xmat - \pvec_1^\top\Xmat = 0, \qquad v_i\, \pvec_3^\top\Xmat - \pvec_2^\top\Xmat = 0.
$$

Two views give four equations $\Amat\Xmat = \zeros$ in the four <NoteLink to="pinhole-camera-model">homogeneous coordinates</NoteLink> of $\Xmat$. The solution with
$\norm{\Xmat} = 1$ is the right singular vector of $\Amat$ for its smallest singular value. With more views the same
construction adds two rows per view.

This linear method minimises an algebraic quantity. The statistically optimal triangulation minimises the reprojection
error in both images, which for two views reduces to finding the roots of a degree-6 polynomial
<Cite id="hartley1997b" />. Accuracy depends on the angle between the rays: nearly parallel rays, from cameras close
together relative to the point's distance, give large depth errors.

### Ambiguity of the reconstruction

Images alone cannot fix the coordinate frame. For any invertible $4 \times 4$ matrix $\Gmat$, the cameras
$\Pmat_i\Gmat^{-1}$ and points $\Gmat\Xmat_j$ produce the same images, since $(\Pmat_i\Gmat^{-1})(\Gmat\Xmat_j) =
\Pmat_i\Xmat_j$. With uncalibrated cameras the reconstruction is therefore determined only up to a projective
transformation, 15 degrees of freedom. With known intrinsics, $\Gmat$ must keep every $\Pmat_i\Gmat^{-1}$ of the form
$\Kmat_i\brack{\Rmat \mid \tvec}$, which leaves a similarity: a rotation, a translation and a scale, 7 degrees of
freedom <Cite id="hartley2004" />. The overall scale of a monocular reconstruction is unknown without a measured
distance or another sensor.

### Incremental reconstruction

Incremental SfM, as in Bundler and COLMAP, builds the model one image at a time
<Cite id="snavely2006" /> <Cite id="schonberger2016" />:

1. **Correspondences.** Detect and describe features in every image, match them between pairs, and keep pairs whose
   matches fit a fundamental matrix, essential matrix or homography under <Gloss name="ransac" />.
2. **Initialisation.** Choose a pair with many matches and a wide baseline. Estimate its essential matrix, decompose
   it into $\Rmat, \tvec$ (see <NoteLink to="epipolar-geometry-and-stereo" />) and triangulate the matches.
3. **Registration.** Pick an image that sees many already-reconstructed points. Its 2D–3D correspondences determine
   its pose: the perspective-$n$-point (PnP) problem, solved from three or more points with RANSAC, for example by
   EPnP <Cite id="lepetit2009" />.
4. **Triangulation.** Triangulate the new image's matches with registered images that are not yet in the model.
5. **Bundle adjustment.** Refine the new cameras and points locally, and periodically everything, then remove points
   with large reprojection errors.
6. Repeat from step 3 until no image can be added.

Errors accumulate as images are added, so the repeated bundle adjustment is what keeps a long chain of images
consistent. Global SfM instead estimates all rotations at once from the pairwise ones, then all translations, then runs
one bundle adjustment. It is faster but more sensitive to wrong pairs. City-scale reconstructions from internet photo
collections combine these steps with large-scale image retrieval <Cite id="agarwal2011" />.

### Bundle adjustment as nonlinear least squares

Collect all camera parameters (rotation, translation and possibly intrinsics and distortion) and all point coordinates
into one vector $\thetavec$. Stack the reprojection errors $\xvec_{ij} - \pi(\Pmat_i, \Xmat_j)$ into a residual vector
$\rvec(\thetavec)$. Bundle adjustment minimises $E(\thetavec) = \norm{\rvec(\thetavec)}^2$. Under independent Gaussian
image noise of equal variance, this is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

Linearise the residual about the current estimate: $\rvec(\thetavec + \deltavec) \approx \rvec + \Jmat\deltavec$, with
$\Jmat = \partial\rvec/\partial\thetavec$ the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>. The linearised error $\norm{\rvec + \Jmat\deltavec}^2$ has
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $2\Jmat^\top(\rvec + \Jmat\deltavec)$ with respect to $\deltavec$. Setting it to zero gives the Gauss–Newton
normal equations

$$
\Jmat^\top\Jmat\, \deltavec = -\Jmat^\top\rvec.
$$

Levenberg–Marquardt adds a damping term, $(\Jmat^\top\Jmat + \mu\,\diag(\Jmat^\top\Jmat))\deltavec = -\Jmat^\top\rvec$,
increasing $\mu$ when a step fails to reduce $E$ and decreasing it when a step succeeds. Large $\mu$ gives short
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient-descent</NoteLink> steps; small $\mu$ gives Gauss–Newton steps, which converge quickly near the solution
<Cite id="nocedal2006" />. A robust loss such as Huber's replaces the square for each residual to limit the influence
of remaining outliers.

### Sparsity and the Schur complement

Each residual depends on one camera and one point. Order $\thetavec$ as all camera parameters $\thetavec_c$, then all
point parameters $\thetavec_p$. The normal equations take the block form

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \Umat & \Wmat \\ \Wmat^\top & \Vmat \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \deltavec_c \\ \deltavec_p \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} \epsilonvec_c \\ \epsilonvec_p \end{bmatrix},
$$

where $\Umat$ couples cameras, $\Vmat$ couples points and $\Wmat$ couples cameras with the points they see. Two
different points never share a residual, so $\Vmat$ is block diagonal with one $3 \times 3$ block per point, and
inverting it costs $O(n)$ for $n$ points. Multiply the second block row by $\Wmat\Vmat^{-1}$ and subtract it from the
first to eliminate $\deltavec_p$:

$$
\paren{\Umat - \Wmat\Vmat^{-1}\Wmat^\top}\deltavec_c = \epsilonvec_c - \Wmat\Vmat^{-1}\epsilonvec_p, \qquad
\deltavec_p = \Vmat^{-1}\paren{\epsilonvec_p - \Wmat^\top\deltavec_c}.
$$

The matrix $\Smat = \Umat - \Wmat\Vmat^{-1}\Wmat^\top$ is the <NoteLink to="schur-complement" /> of $\Vmat$. Its size
is set by the number of camera parameters only. The reduced camera system is solved by <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factorisation</NoteLink>, or by
preconditioned <Gloss name="cg">conjugate gradients</Gloss> for very large problems, and the points are recovered one at a time by
back-substitution <Cite id="triggs2000" />.

$\Jmat^\top\Jmat$ is singular along the 7 directions of the similarity ambiguity: moving every camera and point by the
same similarity leaves the residuals unchanged. Damping, or fixing one camera and the distance to a second, removes
this gauge freedom.

## Worked example

**Triangulation.** Take normalised cameras $\Pmat_1 = \brack{\I \mid \zeros}$ and $\Pmat_2 = \brack{\I \mid (-1, 0, 0)^\top}$,
a unit baseline along $x$. The point $\Xmat = (0.5, 0.2, 4)$ projects to $\xvec_1 = (0.125, 0.05)$ and
$\xvec_2 = (-0.125, 0.05)$. The four equations are

$$
\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0.125 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.05 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & -0.125 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.05 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\Xmat = \zeros.
$$

The smallest singular value is 0 and its right singular vector, scaled to a last coordinate of 1, is
$(0.5, 0.2, 4, 1)$: the point is recovered exactly. Shift $\xvec_2$ by $-0.01$ in $x$, a disparity error of 4%. The
solution becomes $(0.481, 0.192, 3.846)$: the depth $1/0.26 = 3.846$ is 4% short, as the rectified formula $Z = fB/d$
predicts.

**Problem size.** A reconstruction with $m = 100$ cameras of 6 parameters each, $n = 50{,}000$ points and
$300{,}000$ observations has $600 + 150{,}000 = 150{,}600$ unknowns and $600{,}000$ residuals. The full normal matrix
is $150{,}600 \times 150{,}600$. After eliminating the points, the reduced camera system is $600 \times 600$, and the
elimination needs $50{,}000$ inversions of $3 \times 3$ blocks.

## Assumptions

- The scene is static and rigid. Moving objects produce matches that fit no single camera motion and must be rejected
  as outliers.
- Image errors are independent, small and roughly Gaussian after outlier removal, so least squares is the right
  criterion.
- Images overlap enough, and with enough baseline, for every camera to share well-triangulated points with others.

## Pitfalls

- **Bad initial pair.** A pair with a small baseline gives poorly triangulated points, and errors propagate to every
  camera registered later. Choose the initial pair for both many matches and a wide angle between rays.
- **Drift.** Long sequences accumulate scale and rotation errors. Loop closure, recognising a place seen before, and a
  global bundle adjustment correct it.
- **Repetitive structure.** Symmetric buildings and repeated facades produce consistent but wrong matches that fold the
  reconstruction onto itself.
- **Local minima.** Bundle adjustment is a local method. It refines a good initial estimate; it does not find one.
