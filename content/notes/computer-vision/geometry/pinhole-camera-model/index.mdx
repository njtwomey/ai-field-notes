<Definition>
A pinhole camera maps a point $\Xmat = (X, Y, Z)$ in world coordinates to a pixel $(u, v)$ by
$\lambda\, (u, v, 1)^\top = \Pmat\, (X, Y, Z, 1)^\top$ with $\Pmat = \Kmat \brack{\Rmat \mid \tvec}$, a $3 \times 4$
camera matrix. The rotation $\Rmat$ and translation $\tvec$ (extrinsics) move the point into camera coordinates. The
upper-triangular $\Kmat$ (intrinsics) holds the focal lengths $f_x, f_y$ in pixels, the principal point $(c_x, c_y)$
and a skew $s$. The scalar $\lambda$ is the point's depth.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Light from a scene passes through a single point, the pinhole or centre of projection, and lands on an image plane
behind it. Each image point receives light from exactly one direction, so the image records directions, not
distances. Every point along one ray through the centre lands on the same pixel. Far objects look small because the
same physical size subtends a smaller angle.

A real camera has a lens rather than a pinhole, but for a focused lens the geometry is the same, up to distortion. The
model separates what belongs to the camera (focal length, sensor layout) from where the camera is (its position and
orientation).

## Details

### Projection by similar triangles

Put the centre of projection at the origin, looking along the $Z$ axis, with an image plane at distance $f$ in front of
it (a mirror image of the physical plane behind the pinhole, which avoids an inverted picture). A point $(X, Y, Z)$ with
$Z > 0$ and its image $(x, y)$ on the plane lie on the same ray through the origin. The triangles formed with the $Z$
axis are similar, so

$$
\frac{x}{f} = \frac{X}{Z}, \qquad \frac{y}{f} = \frac{Y}{Z} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = f\frac{X}{Z}, \quad
y = f\frac{Y}{Z}.
$$

Division by $Z$ makes projection nonlinear. Doubling the distance halves the image size.

### Homogeneous coordinates

Represent an image point $(x, y)$ by any vector $(wx, wy, w)$ with $w \neq 0$, and a 3D point by $(X, Y, Z, 1)$.
Vectors that differ by a nonzero factor represent the same point; write $\avec \sim \bvec$ for this. Projection then
becomes linear:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} fX \\ fY \\ Z \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} f & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & f & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} fX/Z \\ fY/Z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Dividing by the third coordinate recovers the image point. The division is postponed to the end, so chains of
projections, rotations and translations compose as <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix products</NoteLink> <Cite id="hartley2004" />.

### Intrinsics

Pixels are indexed from a corner, not from the point where the optical axis meets the sensor, and pixels need not be
square. Let the sensor have $m_x$ pixels per unit length horizontally and $m_y$ vertically, and let the optical axis hit
pixel $(c_x, c_y)$, the principal point. Then $u = m_x x + c_x$ and $v = m_y y + c_y$. Folding these into the projection
gives

$$
\Kmat = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & s & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad f_x = f m_x, \quad
f_y = f m_y,
$$

and $\lambda (u, v, 1)^\top = \Kmat\, (X_c, Y_c, Z_c)^\top$ for a point in camera coordinates, with $\lambda = Z_c$. The
focal lengths are in pixels. The skew $s$ is zero for modern sensors. $\Kmat$ has five parameters.

The horizontal field of view $\theta$ of an image $W$ pixels wide with the principal point at its centre satisfies
$\tan(\theta/2) = (W/2)/f_x$. A longer focal length gives a narrower view.

### Extrinsics and the camera matrix

World points are expressed in a frame of their own. A rotation $\Rmat$ (orthogonal, $\det \Rmat = 1$) and translation
$\tvec$ take world coordinates to camera coordinates, $\Xmat_c = \Rmat \Xmat + \tvec$. Combining with the intrinsics:

$$
\lambda \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \Kmat \brack{\Rmat \mid \tvec}
\begin{bmatrix} \Xmat \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \Pmat \begin{bmatrix} \Xmat \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

$\Pmat$ has 12 entries but is defined only up to scale, so it has 11 degrees of freedom: 5 intrinsic and 6 extrinsic (3
for rotation, 3 for translation).

The camera centre $\cvec$ in world coordinates is the point that maps to the origin of the camera frame:
$\Rmat\cvec + \tvec = \zeros$, so $\cvec = -\Rmat^\top \tvec$. Then $\Pmat (\cvec, 1)^\top = \Kmat(\Rmat\cvec + \tvec) =
\zeros$: the centre is the null vector of $\Pmat$, the one point with no image.

### Back-projection and depth

Inverting the projection gives a ray, not a point. The pixel $(u, v)$ is the image of every point

$$
\Xmat(\lambda) = \cvec + \lambda\, \Rmat^\top \Kmat^{-1} (u, v, 1)^\top, \qquad \lambda > 0.
$$

Recovering $\lambda$ needs more information: a second view (see <NoteLink to="epipolar-geometry-and-stereo" />), a
known plane (see <NoteLink to="homography" />), or a known object size.

### Vanishing points

Parallel lines in 3D meet in the image. Take a line $\Xmat(\mu) = \avec + \mu \dvec$ with direction $\dvec$. Its image
is $\Kmat(\Rmat\avec + \tvec + \mu\Rmat\dvec)$ in homogeneous coordinates. Divide by $\mu$ and let $\mu \to \infty$: the
image tends to $\Kmat \Rmat \dvec$. The limit does not depend on $\avec$, so every line with direction $\dvec$ converges
to the same vanishing point $\vvec \sim \Kmat\Rmat\dvec$. Lines parallel to the image plane have $(\Rmat\dvec)_3 = 0$,
so their vanishing point is at infinity and they stay parallel in the image.

### Lens distortion

Real lenses bend rays so that straight lines image as curves, most visibly near the edges of wide-angle images. The
standard model acts on normalised coordinates $(x_n, y_n) = (X_c/Z_c, Y_c/Z_c)$ before $\Kmat$ is applied. With
$r^2 = x_n^2 + y_n^2$,

$$
x_d = x_n (1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6) + 2p_1 x_n y_n + p_2 (r^2 + 2x_n^2),
$$

and symmetrically for $y_d$ with the roles of $p_1$ and $p_2$ exchanged. The $k_i$ model radial distortion: $k_1 < 0$
pulls points inwards (barrel) and $k_1 > 0$ pushes them outwards (pincushion). The $p_i$ model tangential distortion
from a lens not parallel to the sensor. The parameters are estimated with the intrinsics during
<NoteLink to="camera-calibration" /> <Cite id="tsai1987" />. Fisheye lenses with fields of view near 180 degrees need
different models, because $x_n = \tan\theta$ diverges <Cite id="szeliski2022" />.

## Worked example

A camera has $f_x = f_y = 800$ pixels, principal point $(320, 240)$, zero skew and a $640 \times 480$ image. Its
horizontal field of view is $2\arctan(320/800) = 43.6°$ and its vertical field of view $2\arctan(240/800) = 33.4°$.

Place the camera with $\Rmat = \I$ and $\tvec = (0, 0, 1)$ metres, and take the world point $\Xmat = (0.5, -0.2, 4)$.
In camera coordinates $\Xmat_c = (0.5, -0.2, 5)$. Then

$$
\Kmat \Xmat_c = \begin{bmatrix} 800 \cdot 0.5 + 320 \cdot 5 \\ 800 \cdot (-0.2) + 240 \cdot 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} 2000 \\ 1040 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 400 \\ 208 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The point images at pixel $(400, 208)$. The camera centre is $\cvec = -\Rmat^\top\tvec = (0, 0, -1)$ in world
coordinates.

Now add barrel distortion with $k_1 = -0.2$ and all other coefficients zero. The image corner at pixel $(640, 480)$
has normalised coordinates $(0.4, 0.3)$ and $r^2 = 0.25$. The distortion factor is $1 - 0.2 \times 0.25 = 0.95$, so the
point is imaged at $(0.38, 0.285)$, pixel $(624, 468)$: 16 pixels inwards horizontally and 12 vertically. At the point
above, with $r^2 = 0.0116$, the shift is under a quarter of a pixel.

## Assumptions

- All rays pass through one point. Thick lenses, zoom lenses at changing settings and some catadioptric cameras
  violate this.
- The sensor is a flat, rigid grid read out at one instant. Rolling-shutter cameras expose rows at different times, so
  a moving camera distorts the image in ways the model does not capture.

## Pitfalls

- **Conventions.** Libraries differ in whether $\Rmat, \tvec$ map world to camera or camera to world, in the direction
  of the $y$ axis, and in whether the camera looks along $+Z$ or $-Z$. Mixing conventions silently mirrors or inverts
  results.
- **Focal length units.** $f_x$ in pixels depends on the resolution. Resizing an image by a factor scales $f_x$, $f_y$,
  $c_x$ and $c_y$ by the same factor.
- **Points behind the camera.** The homogeneous division maps a point with $Z_c < 0$ to a valid-looking pixel. Check
  the sign of the depth.
- **Distorted coordinates.** Projective formulas such as homographies and epipolar constraints hold only after
  distortion has been removed.
