<Definition>
Camera calibration estimates the parameters of a camera model from images of a target with known geometry. The
intrinsic parameters are the matrix $\Kmat$ (focal lengths $f_x, f_y$, principal point $(c_x, c_y)$, skew $s$) and the
lens distortion coefficients. The extrinsic parameters, a rotation and translation, place the target relative to the
camera in each image. Estimation minimises the <NoteLink to="structure-from-motion">reprojection error</NoteLink>: the distance in pixels between the detected target
points and their projections under the model.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A camera turns directions into pixels. Measuring anything metric from an image, such as the angle between two rays,
the size of an object or the depth from a stereo pair, requires knowing that map. Calibration measures it by showing
the camera points whose positions are known and comparing where they land with where the model says they should.

A flat checkerboard is the usual target because its corners are easy to detect to a fraction of a pixel and its
geometry is known exactly. Photographing it at several orientations provides enough independent constraints to fix
the focal lengths and principal point. A board seen straight on tells little: its image changes the same way whether
the camera has a long focal length or the board is far away. Tilted views break that ambiguity.

## Details

### One view of a plane gives two constraints

Put the target in the plane $Z = 0$ of its own frame. A target point $(X, Y, 0)$ images at
$\xvec \sim \Kmat\brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \rvec_3 \mid \tvec}(X, Y, 0, 1)^\top$. The third rotation column multiplies
zero and drops out, so the map from target to image is a homography (see <NoteLink to="homography" />):

$$
\Hmat = \brack{\hvec_1\ \hvec_2\ \hvec_3} = \lambda\, \Kmat \brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \tvec}
$$

for some scale $\lambda \neq 0$. $\Hmat$ is estimated from the detected corners by the DLT. Solving for the rotation
columns gives $\rvec_1 = \lambda^{-1}\Kmat^{-1}\hvec_1$ and $\rvec_2 = \lambda^{-1}\Kmat^{-1}\hvec_2$. The columns of a
rotation matrix are orthonormal, so $\rvec_1^\top\rvec_2 = 0$ and $\rvec_1^\top\rvec_1 = \rvec_2^\top\rvec_2$.
Substituting, and cancelling $\lambda^{-2}$:

$$
\hvec_1^\top \Bmat\, \hvec_2 = 0, \qquad \hvec_1^\top \Bmat\, \hvec_1 = \hvec_2^\top \Bmat\, \hvec_2, \qquad
\Bmat = \Kmat^{-\top}\Kmat^{-1}.
$$

The unknown rotation and translation have been eliminated. What remains is two equations per view in the symmetric
matrix $\Bmat$ alone <Cite id="zhang2000" />. $\Bmat$ is known in projective geometry as the image of the absolute
conic <Cite id="hartley2004" />.

### Solving linearly for the intrinsics

$\Bmat$ is symmetric with six distinct entries, collected as $\bvec = (B_{11}, B_{12}, B_{22}, B_{13}, B_{23},
B_{33})$. Each <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> $\hvec_i^\top\Bmat\hvec_j$ is linear in $\bvec$: $\hvec_i^\top\Bmat\hvec_j =
\vvec_{ij}^\top\bvec$ with

$$
\vvec_{ij} = \paren{h_{i1}h_{j1},\ h_{i1}h_{j2} + h_{i2}h_{j1},\ h_{i2}h_{j2},\ h_{i3}h_{j1} + h_{i1}h_{j3},\
h_{i3}h_{j2} + h_{i2}h_{j3},\ h_{i3}h_{j3}},
$$

where $h_{ik}$ is entry $k$ of column $\hvec_i$. Each view contributes the two rows $\vvec_{12}^\top$ and
$(\vvec_{11} - \vvec_{22})^\top$. Stacking $n$ views gives $\Vmat\bvec = \zeros$ with $\Vmat \in \reals^{2n \times 6}$.
$\bvec$ is defined up to scale, so it has 5 unknowns and needs $n \ge 3$ views in general position. If the skew is
known to be zero, adding the row $(0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0)$ lets 2 views suffice. The solution is the right singular vector
of $\Vmat$ for the smallest singular value, as in the DLT.

$\Kmat^{-1}$ is upper triangular, so $\Bmat = (\Kmat^{-1})^\top\Kmat^{-1}$ is its Cholesky factorisation up to scale.
Flip the sign of $\bvec$ if needed to make $\Bmat$ positive definite, compute the Cholesky factor $\Bmat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$
with $\Lmat$ lower triangular, and take $\Kmat = (\Lmat^\top)^{-1}$ scaled so that $K_{33} = 1$ (see
<NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition" />). Zhang also gives the entries of $\Kmat$ in closed form.

### Extrinsics

With $\Kmat$ known, each view's pose follows from its homography: $\lambda = 1/\norm{\Kmat^{-1}\hvec_1}$,
$\rvec_1 = \lambda\Kmat^{-1}\hvec_1$, $\rvec_2 = \lambda\Kmat^{-1}\hvec_2$, $\rvec_3 = \rvec_1 \times \rvec_2$ and
$\tvec = \lambda\Kmat^{-1}\hvec_3$. With noise, the matrix $\brack{\rvec_1\ \rvec_2\ \rvec_3}$ is not exactly a
rotation. The nearest rotation in the <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">Frobenius norm</NoteLink> is $\Umat\Vmat^\top$, from its <Gloss name="svd" /> $\Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$.

### Nonlinear refinement with distortion

The linear solution minimises algebraic errors and ignores lens distortion. It initialises a maximum-likelihood
refinement over all parameters: with $\mvec_{ij}$ the detected position of corner $j$ in image $i$ and $\Mmat_j$ its
known position on the target, minimise

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j} \norm{\mvec_{ij} - \hat\mvec(\Kmat, k_1, k_2, \Rmat_i, \tvec_i, \Mmat_j)}^2
$$

by Levenberg–Marquardt, where $\hat\mvec$ projects with the full camera model including radial distortion
$k_1, k_2$ (see <NoteLink to="pinhole-camera-model" />). The distortion coefficients start at zero. The root mean
square reprojection error at the optimum measures the fit; a value well below one pixel is typical for a good lens and
careful <NoteLink to="feature-detection-and-descriptors">corner detection</NoteLink> <Cite id="zhang2000" />.

### Other targets and methods

With a non-planar target of known 3D points, the full $3 \times 4$ camera matrix can be estimated directly from 6 or
more points by a DLT, and split into $\Kmat$ and $\Rmat$ by an RQ decomposition of its left $3 \times 3$ block
<Cite id="hartley2004" />. Tsai's method used a planar or non-planar target with a separate model for radial
distortion and was the photogrammetric standard before Zhang's <Cite id="tsai1987" />. Self-calibration recovers
$\Kmat$ from the constraints between views of an unknown scene, without any target.

## Worked example

**Why tilted views are needed.** Let the board face the camera squarely at distance $d$: $\Rmat = \I$ and
$\tvec = (0, 0, d)$. With $\Kmat$ of zero skew,

$$
\Hmat = \Kmat\brack{\evec_1\ \evec_2\ \tvec} = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x d \\ 0 & f_y & c_y d \\ 0 & 0 & d
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

So $\hvec_1 = (f_x, 0, 0)$ and $\hvec_2 = (0, f_y, 0)$, and the two constraints read $f_xf_y B_{12} = 0$ and
$f_x^2 B_{11} = f_y^2 B_{22}$. They fix $B_{12} = 0$ and the ratio $B_{11}/B_{22}$: the skew and the aspect ratio
$f_x/f_y$. They do not involve $B_{13}$, $B_{23}$ or $B_{33}$, so they say nothing about the size of the focal length or
the principal point. Moving the board away while lengthening the focal length changes its image identically.

**Recovering $\Kmat$ from three views.** A simulated camera has $f_x = f_y = 800$ pixels and principal point
$(320, 240)$. It views a $7 \times 9$ grid of corners with 3 cm spacing from 0.6 to 0.7 m, tilted by 10 to 30 degrees
about different axes in three views. Estimating each homography, stacking the six rows into $\Vmat$ and factorising
recovers $\Kmat$ exactly from noiseless corners. Adding Gaussian noise of 0.2 pixels to every corner gives
$f_x = 806$, $f_y = 808$ and principal point $(315, 239)$: errors of about 1% in focal length and 5 pixels in the
principal point. More views and the nonlinear refinement reduce them.

## Assumptions

- The target is flat and its corner positions are known exactly. A warped printout or a board that bends violates
  this and biases every parameter.
- The camera's intrinsics do not change during calibration: fixed focus, fixed zoom and no image stabilisation.
- The distortion model has enough terms for the lens. Wide-angle and fisheye lenses need higher-order or different
  models.

## Pitfalls

- **Degenerate views.** Views that differ only by translating the board, or boards that are all parallel, add no new
  constraints on $\Bmat$. Tilt the board by different amounts about different axes.
- **Uncovered image regions.** Distortion is largest near the edges and corners of the image. If the target never
  appears there, the distortion estimate is extrapolated and wrong.
- **Low reprojection error is not accuracy.** A model can fit the calibration images well and still be wrong where
  there were no points, or be biased by a warped target. Check on held-out images.
- **Resolution changes.** Calibrating at one resolution and using another requires scaling $\Kmat$. Cropping shifts the
  principal point.
