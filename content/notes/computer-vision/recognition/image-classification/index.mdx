<Definition>
Image classification is the task of assigning an image $\xvec \in \reals^{H \times W \times 3}$ one label from a fixed
set of $K$ classes. A classifier computes a vector of $K$ logits $\zvec = f_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ and class probabilities
$p_k = \exp(z_k) / \sum_j \exp(z_j)$. It is trained on labelled images by minimising the cross-entropy
$-\log p_y$ of the true class $y$, and predicts $\argmax_k p_k$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The same object can produce very different pixel arrays. A cat photographed from the side and from above, in daylight
and under a lamp, partly hidden behind a chair, shares almost no pixel values across the photographs. Two images of
different classes, a husky and a wolf on snow, can be close in pixel space. A classifier must be insensitive to
viewpoint, lighting, deformation, occlusion, background and variation within a class, while staying sensitive to the
small differences between classes.

Every approach builds a representation in which images of the same class are close and a simple classifier, usually
linear, separates the classes. The history of the field is a history of how that representation is made: designed by
hand, learned by a convolutional network, or learned by a transformer from very large datasets.

## Details

### The classification head

Most modern classifiers share one structure. A backbone maps the image to a feature map $\Fmat \in \reals^{h \times w
\times d}$. <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">Global average pooling</NoteLink> averages it over positions to a vector $\bar\fvec = \frac{1}{hw} \sum_{i, j}
\fvec_{ij} \in \reals^d$. A linear layer gives logits $\zvec = \Wmat \bar\fvec + \bvec$, and a softmax gives
probabilities.

Averaging over positions discards where a feature occurred. A convolutional backbone is equivariant to translation: an
object shifted by a multiple of the network's total stride produces the same features, shifted (see
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer" />). The average over positions is the same for a shifted feature map, apart from
border effects. So the logits are invariant to such shifts, which is the invariance a whole-image label requires.

### The training loss and its gradient

For one image with true class $y$ and one-hot label vector $\yvec$, the cross-entropy loss is

$$
\ell(\zvec) = -\log p_y = -z_y + \log \sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(z_j).
$$

Differentiate with respect to logit $z_k$. The first term contributes $-1$ if $k = y$ and 0 otherwise, that is
$-y_k$. The second contributes $\exp(z_k) / \sum_j \exp(z_j) = p_k$. So

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z_k} = p_k - y_k, \qquad \nabla_\zvec \ell = \pvec - \yvec.
$$

The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> pushes the true class's logit up by $1 - p_y$ and every other logit down by its probability. When the
model is confident and right, $\pvec \approx \yvec$ and the example contributes almost nothing. Backpropagation carries
$\pvec - \yvec$ through $\Wmat$ into the backbone. This is <NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression" /> on learned
features.

Images with several labels at once, such as "person" and "bicycle", use $K$ independent sigmoid outputs and a binary
cross-entropy per class instead of a softmax.

### Three generations of representation

- **Hand-crafted features.** Before 2012, pipelines computed local descriptors such as <Gloss name="sift" /> or histograms of oriented
  gradients (HOG) <Cite id="dalal2005" />. The bag-of-visual-words model clusters descriptors from training images with
  $k$-means into a vocabulary of "visual words". Each image becomes a histogram of how many of its descriptors fall
  in each cluster, and a linear or kernel SVM classifies the histograms <Cite id="sivic2003" />.
- **Convolutional networks.** In the 2012 <Gloss name="imagenet" /> challenge, a <Gloss name="cnn" /> trained end to end on raw pixels with <Gloss name="gpu" plural />, <Gloss name="relu" />
  and dropout reached 15.3% top-5 error against 26.2% for the best hand-crafted entry <Cite id="krizhevsky2012" />.
  Deeper networks followed. <Gloss name="resnet">Residual networks</Gloss> reached 3.57% top-5 error in 2015 <Cite id="he2016" />. The
  representation is learned: early layers find edges and colour blobs, later layers find parts and objects (see
  <NoteLink to="classic-convolutional-network-architectures" />).
- **Transformers and scale.** The <NoteLink to="vision-transformer" /> splits an image into patches and processes them
  as a sequence with self-attention. It has weaker built-in assumptions about images than a CNN and outperforms CNNs
  when pretrained on hundreds of millions of images <Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />. Contrastive image–text pretraining,
  as in <NoteLink to="clip" />, classifies new classes from their names with no labelled examples.

### Benchmarks and metrics

ImageNet has over 14 million images labelled with WordNet categories <Cite id="deng2009" />. Its 2012 challenge subset,
often called ImageNet-1k, has 1000 classes, about 1.28 million training images and 50,000 validation images
<Cite id="russakovsky2015" />. Performance is reported as **top-1 accuracy**, the fraction of images whose most probable
class is correct, and **top-5 error**, the fraction whose correct class is not among the five most probable. Top-5
exists because many ImageNet images contain several objects and some classes are hard to tell apart, such as dozens of
dog breeds.

### Training practice

A modern recipe combines a pretrained backbone, <NoteLink to="data-augmentation" /> (random crops, flips, colour jitter
and mixing of images), label smoothing, <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss> and a learning-rate schedule. With few labelled images, starting
from a network pretrained on a large dataset and fine-tuning it is far more accurate than training from scratch (see
<NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning" />).

## Worked example

A three-class classifier outputs logits $\zvec = (2.0, 1.0, 0.1)$ for classes cat, dog and fox. The softmax gives

$$
\sum_j e^{z_j} = e^{2.0} + e^{1.0} + e^{0.1} = 7.389 + 2.718 + 1.105 = 11.212,
$$

$$
\pvec = (7.389, 2.718, 1.105) / 11.212 = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099).
$$

The prediction is cat. If the true class is dog, the loss is $-\log 0.242 = 1.417$ and the gradient with respect to
the logits is

$$
\pvec - \yvec = (0.659, 0.242 - 1, 0.099) = (0.659, -0.758, 0.099).
$$

A gradient step lowers the cat logit most, raises the dog logit, and lowers the fox logit slightly. The components sum
to zero, because adding a constant to every logit leaves the softmax unchanged.

## Assumptions

- Each image has one dominant object or scene that defines its label. Cluttered images with several objects fit
  detection or multi-label classification better.
- Training and test images come from the same distribution. Classifiers trained on ImageNet photographs lose accuracy
  on sketches, unusual viewpoints or new camera types.

## Pitfalls

- **Shortcut features.** A network uses whatever separates the training classes, including backgrounds, watermarks or
  texture rather than shape. High test accuracy on the same distribution does not rule this out.
- **Label noise and ambiguity.** Some ImageNet validation labels are wrong, and some images fit several classes, so small
  differences in top-1 accuracy between models can reflect label errors rather than skill.
- **Overconfident probabilities.** Softmax outputs of deep networks are often poorly calibrated. Recalibrate on
  held-out data before treating them as probabilities.
- **Resolution mismatch.** Training at one image size and testing at another shifts the apparent size of objects and
  changes accuracy.
