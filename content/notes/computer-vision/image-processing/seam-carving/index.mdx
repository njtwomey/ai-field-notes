<Definition>
Seam carving changes the width or height of an image one pixel at a time. A vertical seam is a path of one pixel per
row, from the top row to the bottom row, whose column changes by at most one between neighbouring rows. Each pixel has
an energy that measures its importance, usually the image gradient magnitude. Removing the vertical seam of least
total energy narrows the image by one column; dynamic programming finds that seam exactly in time proportional to the
number of pixels <Cite id="avidan2007" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

An image of $H$ rows and $W$ columns must be shown at a different aspect ratio, for example a landscape photograph on a
portrait phone screen. Two standard operations do this. Uniform scaling resamples every row to the new width, so every
object becomes narrower by the same factor and circles become ellipses. Cropping keeps a window of the target size, so
everything outside the window is lost. Both operations ignore what the image contains.

Most photographs contain regions that carry little information, such as sky, water, grass or a plain wall. Deleting
pixels from those regions changes the image less than deleting pixels from a face or a building. Seam carving removes
pixels where the image is smooth and leaves pixels where it has structure <Cite id="avidan2007" />. The operation is
called content-aware resizing, retargeting, or liquid rescaling.

## Mechanism

### Energy

The energy $e(i, j) \ge 0$ assigns an importance to the pixel in row $i$ and column $j$, with $1 \le i \le H$ and
$1 \le j \le W$. The standard choice is the $L_1$ norm of the intensity <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>:

$$
e(i, j) = \abs{\frac{\partial I}{\partial x}(i, j)} + \abs{\frac{\partial I}{\partial y}(i, j)},
$$

where $I(i, j)$ is the image intensity, $x$ runs along columns and $y$ along rows. The
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> are estimated with finite differences or derivative kernels such as
Sobel, as in <NoteLink to="edge-detection" />. For a colour image the energy is summed over the red, green and blue
channels. The energy is near zero in flat regions and large on edges and texture.

Avidan and Shamir also tested the $L_2$ gradient norm, local <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink>, and the
gradient divided by the largest bin of a local histogram of oriented gradients. They found that no single energy suits
every image, and that the $L_1$ gradient and the histogram-weighted gradient worked well on most images
<Cite id="avidan2007" />.

### Seams

A vertical seam is a sequence of column indices $s = (s_1, s_2, \dots, s_H)$, one per row, with

$$
\abs{s_i - s_{i-1}} \le 1 \quad \text{for } i = 2, \dots, H.
$$

The seam is 8-connected: each of its pixels touches the next one along an edge or a corner. The cost of the seam is
the total energy of its pixels,

$$
E(s) = \sum_{i=1}^{H} e(i, s_i).
$$

Removing a vertical seam deletes exactly one pixel from every row, and each row shifts left by one pixel from the seam
onwards. The result is a rectangular image of width $W - 1$. The connectivity constraint keeps the shift local: two
pixels that were vertical neighbours before the removal are at most one column apart afterwards. Deleting the
lowest-energy pixel in each row independently would also narrow the image, but the deleted pixels could sit in unrelated
columns, and the rows would then shear against each other. A horizontal seam is defined in the same way with rows and
columns exchanged, and is found by running the vertical algorithm on the transposed image.

### Finding the cheapest seam by dynamic programming

A seam starts at any of $W$ columns and has up to three choices in each of the next $H - 1$ rows, so there are up to
$W \cdot 3^{H - 1}$ seams. Enumeration is impossible for any real image. The cheapest seam has optimal substructure,
which reduces the search to one pass over the pixels.

Define $M(i, j)$ as the least cost of a partial seam that runs from row 1 to row $i$ and ends at pixel $(i, j)$. In the
first row the partial seam is the pixel itself, so $M(1, j) = e(1, j)$. Take any partial seam that ends at $(i, j)$ with
$i \ge 2$. The seam's pixel in row $i - 1$ is at a column $j' \in \set{j - 1, j, j + 1}$, because of the connectivity
constraint. The cost of the seam is $e(i, j)$ plus the cost of its first $i - 1$ pixels. Those $i - 1$ pixels form a
partial seam ending at $(i - 1, j')$, so their cost is at least $M(i - 1, j')$. Every partial seam ending at $(i, j)$
therefore costs at least

$$
e(i, j) + \min_{j' \in \set{j - 1, j, j + 1}} M(i - 1, j').
$$

The bound is attained: take a cheapest partial seam ending at the minimising $(i - 1, j')$ and append $(i, j)$. The
result is a valid partial seam, because $\abs{j - j'} \le 1$, and its cost equals the bound. So the least cost is the
bound itself:

$$
M(i, j) = e(i, j) + \min\set{M(i-1, j-1),\ M(i-1, j),\ M(i-1, j+1)}.
$$

Terms with a column outside $1, \dots, W$ are left out of the minimum. The argument is the <NoteLink to="bellman-equations">principle of optimality</NoteLink>
behind every dynamic program: the part of an optimal path before its last step is itself an optimal path to where that
step starts. The same recursion over a chain of rows is the <Gloss name="viterbi">Viterbi algorithm</Gloss> for the most probable state
sequence of a <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model" />, with columns as states and energy as negative log-probability.

Every seam ends in row $H$. The cost of the cheapest complete seam is therefore $\min_j M(H, j)$. The seam itself is
recovered by backtracking. Set $s_H = \argmin_j M(H, j)$. For $i = H, H - 1, \dots, 2$, set $s_{i-1}$ to the column
among $s_i - 1, s_i, s_i + 1$ that attained the minimum in $M(i, s_i)$. Storing that choice for each pixel while $M$ is
filled makes backtracking a walk of $H$ steps. Ties can be broken by any fixed rule; the figure below prefers the
pixel straight above, then the one to the left.

### Removing seams

Shrinking the width by $k$ columns repeats three steps $k$ times: compute the energy of the current image, find the
cheapest seam, and remove it. The energy must be recomputed after each removal. Pixels on either side of the removed
seam become neighbours, so the gradient changes along the seam. The cumulative cost $M$ must be recomputed as well,
because $M(i, j)$ depends on every pixel above row $i$ within the cone of columns that can reach $j$. The sequence is
greedy: each seam is optimal for the current image, but the set of $k$ seams is not guaranteed to have the least total
energy among all sets of $k$ disjoint seams.

### Inserting seams

Enlarging the width by $k$ columns inserts seams. A new pixel is placed next to each pixel of a seam, with the mean
colour of its left and right neighbours. The obvious method, to find the cheapest seam, duplicate it, and repeat,
fails. The duplicated seam and its copy are smooth by construction, because each new pixel is an average of its
neighbours. The next search therefore finds the same seam or its copy again, and $k$ repetitions stretch one column
into a band of $k$ copies.

Avidan and Shamir instead run the removal sequence for $k$ steps on a copy of the image and record which original
pixels the $k$ seams removed <Cite id="avidan2007" />. The $k$ seams are distinct and together cover the $k$ cheapest
columns' worth of pixels. All $k$ are then duplicated in the original image at once. Each row gains exactly $k$ pixels,
because each seam holds one pixel per row. Enlarging by more than about half the width duplicates nearly every
low-energy pixel, so large enlargements are done in several steps of this kind.

### Resizing in both dimensions

Changing an $H \times W$ image to $H' \times W'$ with $H' < H$ and $W' < W$ removes $r = H - H'$ horizontal seams and
$c = W - W'$ vertical seams. The order matters, because each removal changes the energy seen by later seams. Avidan and
Shamir find the best order with a second dynamic program over the table $T(r, c)$, the least total energy of reaching
an image with $r$ rows and $c$ columns removed. Each entry takes the cheaper of removing a horizontal seam from the
image at $T(r - 1, c)$ or a vertical seam from the image at $T(r, c - 1)$ <Cite id="avidan2007" />. The table has
$(r + 1)(c + 1)$ entries and each needs a seam search, so the cost is high. Alternating vertical and horizontal seams is
a cheap approximation that often gives similar results.

### Forward energy

The energy $e$ above measures the pixels that a seam removes. Rubinstein, Shamir and Avidan call it backward energy,
because it ignores what the removal creates <Cite id="rubinstein2008" />. A seam through a flat region next to a sharp
edge can join pixels of very different colour and create a new edge. Forward energy charges a seam for the new
differences between pixels that become neighbours.

When pixel $(i, j)$ is removed, pixels $(i, j - 1)$ and $(i, j + 1)$ become horizontal neighbours in every case. The
vertical neighbours of row $i$ depend on where the seam was in row $i - 1$. If the seam steps straight down from
$(i - 1, j)$, both rows shift by the same amount and every vertical pair is preserved. If it comes from $(i - 1, j - 1)$,
pixel $(i - 1, j)$ moves left into column $j - 1$, above pixel $(i, j - 1)$. If it comes from $(i - 1, j + 1)$, pixel
$(i, j + 1)$ moves left into column $j$, below pixel $(i - 1, j)$. The three cases give three costs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_U(i, j) &= \abs{I(i, j+1) - I(i, j-1)}, \\
C_L(i, j) &= C_U(i, j) + \abs{I(i-1, j) - I(i, j-1)}, \\
C_R(i, j) &= C_U(i, j) + \abs{I(i-1, j) - I(i, j+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The cost now depends on the step into $(i, j)$ as well as on the pixel, so it enters the recursion inside the minimum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(i, j) = P(i, j) + \min\{\, & M(i-1, j-1) + C_L(i, j), \\
& M(i-1, j) + C_U(i, j), \\
& M(i-1, j+1) + C_R(i, j) \,\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $P(i, j)$ is an optional per-pixel term, such as a mask. The optimal-substructure argument goes through
unchanged, because the added costs depend only on the last step. Forward energy removes fewer seams across object
boundaries and leaves fewer jagged edges. It also treats the flat interior of an object like background, because
removing pixels there creates no new edge, so a uniform object can become narrower. The same paper replaces the dynamic
program with a graph cut for video, where a seam becomes a surface through space and time <Cite id="rubinstein2008" />.

### Object removal and protection

A mask changes the energy of chosen pixels. Adding a large positive value to every pixel of a region protects it: any
seam through the region costs more than a seam around it, so the region survives until no other seam remains. Adding a
large negative value marks a region for removal. The cheapest seams then pass through the region, and each one deletes
one pixel from each of its rows. Removing seams until no marked pixel remains deletes the object. Inserting the same
number of seams afterwards restores the original width, with the background filled in around the gap
<Cite id="avidan2007" />.

## Worked example

Take an image with $H = 4$ rows and $W = 5$ columns and the energy table below.

| $e(i, j)$ | $j = 1$ | $j = 2$ | $j = 3$ | $j = 4$ | $j = 5$ |
| --------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| $i = 1$   | 4       | 3       | 5       | 1       | 6       |
| $i = 2$   | 2       | 6       | 5       | 7       | 4       |
| $i = 3$   | 1       | 5       | 3       | 8       | 5       |
| $i = 4$   | 3       | 2       | 6       | 4       | 7       |

Row 1 of $M$ copies row 1 of $e$. Each later entry adds $e(i, j)$ to the smallest of the up to three entries above it.
For example, $M(2, 1) = 2 + \min\set{4, 3} = 5$, $M(2, 2) = 6 + \min\set{4, 3, 5} = 9$ and
$M(3, 2) = 5 + \min\set{5, 9, 6} = 10$. The full table is

| $M(i, j)$ | $j = 1$ | $j = 2$ | $j = 3$ | $j = 4$ | $j = 5$ |
| --------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| $i = 1$   | 4       | **3**   | 5       | 1       | 6       |
| $i = 2$   | **5**   | 9       | 6       | 8       | 5       |
| $i = 3$   | **6**   | 10      | 9       | 13      | 10      |
| $i = 4$   | 9       | **8**   | 15      | 13      | 17      |

The smallest entry in the last row is $M(4, 2) = 8$, so the cheapest seam costs 8 and ends at $s_4 = 2$. Backtracking:

- $M(4, 2) = 2 + \min\set{M(3, 1), M(3, 2), M(3, 3)} = 2 + \min\set{6, 10, 9}$, attained at column 1, so $s_3 = 1$.
- $M(3, 1) = 1 + \min\set{M(2, 1), M(2, 2)} = 1 + \min\set{5, 9}$, attained at column 1, so $s_2 = 1$.
- $M(2, 1) = 2 + \min\set{M(1, 1), M(1, 2)} = 2 + \min\set{4, 3}$, attained at column 2, so $s_1 = 2$.

The seam is $s = (2, 1, 1, 2)$, shown in bold, with cost $e(1, 2) + e(2, 1) + e(3, 1) + e(4, 2) = 3 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 8$.
Every column changes by at most one between rows, so the seam is valid. Removing it leaves a $4 \times 4$ image whose
rows are $(4, 5, 1, 6)$, $(6, 5, 7, 4)$, $(5, 3, 8, 5)$ and $(3, 6, 4, 7)$ in energy terms, before the energy is
recomputed.

A greedy search that starts at the cheapest pixel of the first row and always steps to the cheapest of the three pixels
below does worse. It starts at $e(1, 4) = 1$, then takes $e(2, 5) = 4$, $e(3, 5) = 5$ and $e(4, 4) = 4$, for a total
of 14. The cheap first pixel leads into expensive rows, which only the full table of $M$ reveals.

## Cost

- **One seam.** Filling $M$ takes three comparisons and one addition per pixel, and the energy takes a constant number
  of operations per pixel. Finding one seam costs $O(HW)$ time. Storing $M$ and the backtracking choices costs $O(HW)$
  memory.
- **Shrinking by $k$ columns.** Each of the $k$ seams needs a new energy and a new $M$, so the cost is $O(kHW)$. The
  energy changes only near the removed seam and can be updated locally, but $M$ must be recomputed below the first
  changed row.
- **Enlarging by $k$ columns.** The $k$ seams come from one removal sequence of $k$ steps, $O(kHW)$, plus one pass to
  insert them.
- **Both dimensions.** The optimal order needs a seam search for every entry of an $(r + 1) \times (c + 1)$ table.
  Alternation costs the same as $r + c$ separate removals.
- **Interactive resizing.** Avidan and Shamir precompute the removal order of every pixel once and store it as an index
  map. Any target width is then a lookup: keep the pixels whose removal index exceeds the number of seams to remove
  <Cite id="avidan2007" />. The figure above works this way.

## Trade-offs

- **Images without low-energy regions.** Seam carving needs somewhere cheap to remove pixels. A texture, a page of
  text or a crowd has high energy everywhere, so every seam cuts through structure. Uniform scaling then distorts
  less.
- **Structure that energy does not see.** Energy is local. A face has smooth cheeks and forehead, so seams pass through
  them and change its proportions, which viewers notice at once. Avidan and Shamir protect faces with a mask from a face
  detector <Cite id="avidan2007" />. Symmetric objects, regular patterns and the spacing between objects are also lost.
- **Straight lines bend.** Each row loses one pixel per seam, at a column that varies from row to row. A straight line
  that is not vertical crosses the seams at different rows, so different parts of it shift by different amounts, and
  the line becomes a broken or curved one.
- **Backward energy creates edges.** Joining the two sides of a removed seam can place very different pixels next to
  each other. Forward energy reduces these artefacts at the cost of treating flat object interiors as removable
  <Cite id="rubinstein2008" />.
- **Greedy sequence.** Each seam is optimal for the current image. The whole sequence of $k$ seams is not optimal, and
  a seam that looked cheap early can force costly seams later.
- **Mixing with scaling and cropping.** Seam carving is one of several retargeting operators. Rubinstein, Shamir and
  Avidan combine it with uniform scaling and cropping, and search for the sequence of operators that best preserves
  the image under an image-similarity measure <Cite id="rubinstein2009" />.
