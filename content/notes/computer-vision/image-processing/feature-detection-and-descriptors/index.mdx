<Definition>
A **feature detector** finds keypoints: image locations, often with a scale and an orientation, that can be found again
in another image of the same scene. A **descriptor** summarises the image patch around each keypoint as a vector, so
that patches showing the same surface point have nearby vectors. Matching pairs keypoints across images by the distance
between their descriptors.
</Definition>

## Intuition

To align two photographs, or to reconstruct a scene from them, one needs pairs of pixels that show the same physical
point. A patch in a flat region looks like every other patch near it, so its position is undefined. A patch on a
straight edge can slide along the edge without changing, so its position is undefined along one direction. A patch at
a corner changes whichever way it moves, so its position is fixed in both directions. Corners and blobs are the points
worth matching.

A match also needs a comparison that survives the change of view. Raw pixels change with rotation, scale and lighting.
A descriptor built from histograms of <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> directions, measured relative to the keypoint's own scale and
orientation, changes much less.

## Details

### The structure tensor

Let $I$ be the image and $w$ a window function around a point, such as a Gaussian of width $\sigma_i$. Shift the window
by $(u, v)$ and measure the change as a weighted sum of squared differences:

$$
E(u, v) = \sum_{x, y} w(x, y) \brack{I(x + u, y + v) - I(x, y)}^2.
$$

For a small shift, the first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> $I(x + u, y + v) \approx I(x, y) + I_x u + I_y v$, with $I_x$ and
$I_y$ the <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink>, gives

$$
E(u, v) \approx \sum_{x, y} w(x, y)\, (I_x u + I_y v)^2 =
\begin{bmatrix} u & v \end{bmatrix} \Mmat \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Mmat = \sum_{x, y} w(x, y) \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

$\Mmat$ is the structure tensor, or second-moment matrix. It is symmetric and <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss>, so it has real
eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge 0$ with orthogonal eigenvectors. The change $E$ is $\lambda_1$ times the
squared shift along the first eigenvector and $\lambda_2$ times it along the second. The eigenvalues classify the
patch:

- **Flat:** $\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2 \approx 0$. No shift changes the patch.
- **Edge:** $\lambda_1 \gg \lambda_2 \approx 0$. Shifts along the edge (the second eigenvector) change nothing.
- **Corner:** both eigenvalues large. Every shift changes the patch.

### Harris and Shi–Tomasi responses

Harris and Stephens avoided computing eigenvalues at every pixel <Cite id="harris1988" />. The <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> and trace of
$\Mmat$ are $\det\Mmat = \lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\trace\Mmat = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$, both cheap. Their response is

$$
R = \det\Mmat - k\,(\trace\Mmat)^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2 - k(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)^2,
$$

with $k$ typically between 0.04 and 0.06. On an edge, $\lambda_2 = 0$ gives $R = -k\lambda_1^2 < 0$. At an isotropic
corner, $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda$ gives $R = \lambda^2(1 - 4k) > 0$ for $k < 1/4$. In general, write
$\rho = \lambda_1/\lambda_2 \ge 1$. Then $R > 0$ exactly when

$$
\frac{\rho}{(1 + \rho)^2} > k.
$$

The left side falls as $\rho$ grows, so $k$ sets the largest eigenvalue ratio accepted as a corner: 23.0 for
$k = 0.04$ and 14.6 for $k = 0.06$. Corners are the local maxima of $R$ above a threshold. Shi and Tomasi used
$\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2) = \lambda_2$ instead, which directly measures how well the patch can be tracked in its
weakest direction <Cite id="shi1994" />.

The response depends only on the eigenvalues, so it is unchanged by rotating the image. It is not invariant to scale:
a corner seen at a larger scale looks like an edge inside a small window.

### SIFT: scale-invariant keypoints and descriptors

Lowe's scale-invariant feature transform has four stages <Cite id="lowe2004" />:

1. **Scale-space extrema.** Build a difference-of-Gaussians stack, which approximates the scale-normalised Laplacian
   (see <NoteLink to="image-pyramids-and-scale-space" />). A sample is a candidate keypoint if it is larger or smaller
   than all 26 neighbours: 8 at its own scale and 9 at each adjacent scale.
2. **Localisation and filtering.** Fit a quadratic to the DoG function $D$ around the sample. Setting the gradient of
   $D(\xvec_0 + \deltavec) \approx D + \nabla D^\top \deltavec + \tfrac12 \deltavec^\top \Hmat \deltavec$ to zero gives
   the sub-pixel offset $\hat\deltavec = -\Hmat^{-1} \nabla D$. Candidates with $\abs{D(\hat\deltavec)} < 0.03$ (for
   intensities in $[0, 1]$) are dropped as low contrast. Candidates on edges are dropped with the $2 \times 2$ spatial
   <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> of $D$: if its eigenvalues are $\alpha = r\beta$, then $(\trace \Hmat)^2 / \det \Hmat = (r + 1)^2/r$, which
   grows with $r$. Lowe rejects points with $r > 10$, that is $(\trace \Hmat)^2/\det\Hmat > 12.1$.
3. **Orientation.** Build a 36-bin histogram of gradient directions around the keypoint, weighted by gradient
   magnitude and a Gaussian window. Its peak sets the keypoint's orientation; any other peak within 80% of it creates
   another keypoint at the same place.
4. **Descriptor.** Rotate a $16 \times 16$ sample grid at the keypoint's scale to its orientation. Split it into
   $4 \times 4$ cells and build an 8-bin orientation histogram in each: $4 \cdot 4 \cdot 8 = 128$ numbers. Normalise
   to unit length, clip entries at 0.2 and renormalise, which limits the effect of non-linear lighting changes.

Because every measurement is taken relative to the keypoint's scale and orientation, the descriptor is invariant to
image scaling and rotation. Normalisation removes changes in contrast. Histograms over cells tolerate small shifts and
distortions. SURF approximates the same pipeline with <NoteLink to="image-filtering">box filters</NoteLink> on an integral image <Cite id="bay2008" />.

### ORB: binary features

ORB replaces floating-point gradients with intensity comparisons <Cite id="rublee2011" />:

- **FAST detection.** A pixel is a corner if at least 9 contiguous pixels on a circle of 16 around it are all brighter
  or all darker than it by a threshold <Cite id="rosten2006" />. ORB ranks FAST points by the Harris response and
  detects them on an image pyramid for scale.
- **Orientation from the intensity centroid.** With patch moments $m_{pq} = \sum_{x, y} x^p y^q I(x, y)$ taken about
  the keypoint, the angle is $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(m_{01}, m_{10})$, the direction from the keypoint to the
  patch's centre of brightness.
- **Steered BRIEF descriptor.** BRIEF compares the smoothed intensities at 256 pairs of points in the patch and records
  each comparison as one bit <Cite id="calonder2010" />. ORB rotates the point pairs by $\theta$ and chooses pairs that
  are uncorrelated and have high variance.

The descriptor is 32 bytes. Two descriptors are compared by Hamming distance, the number of differing bits, which is an
XOR and a population count. ORB is about two orders of magnitude faster than SIFT, at some cost in robustness to large
scale changes.

### Matching

Each descriptor in one image is matched to its nearest neighbour in the other: Euclidean distance for SIFT, Hamming
distance for ORB. Many keypoints have no true match, because they were occluded or fell outside the other view. Lowe's
ratio test accepts a match only if the nearest neighbour is clearly closer than the second nearest, with
$d_1 / d_2 < 0.8$. In his experiments this removed 90% of false matches and less than 5% of correct ones
<Cite id="lowe2004" />. Large databases use approximate <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search" />. Surviving matches
still contain outliers, so geometric fitting uses a robust method such as RANSAC (see <NoteLink to="homography" />).

## Worked example

Suppose the structure tensor at one pixel is $\Mmat = \diag(10, 2)$, in squared intensity units. Then
$\det\Mmat = 20$, $\trace\Mmat = 12$ and, with $k = 0.04$,

$$
R = 20 - 0.04 \times 144 = 14.24 > 0.
$$

The eigenvalue ratio is 5, below the limit of 23.0, so the point is a corner. Rotate the image by $30°$. The gradients
rotate and the tensor becomes

$$
\Mmat' = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 3.46 \\ 3.46 & 4 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with $\det\Mmat' = 32 - 12 = 20$ and $\trace\Mmat' = 12$, so $R$ is again 14.24. The response ignores rotation.

Now take an edge, $\Mmat = \diag(10, 0.1)$. Then $\det\Mmat = 1$, $\trace\Mmat = 10.1$ and
$R = 1 - 0.04 \times 102.01 = -3.08$. The eigenvalue ratio is 100, well above 23, and $R$ is negative. The
Shi–Tomasi score is $\min(\lambda_1, \lambda_2) = 2$ for the corner and $0.1$ for the edge.

## Assumptions

- The scene is locally planar and rigid, so a small patch changes by roughly a similarity or affine transformation
  between views. Strong perspective, non-rigid motion and specular reflections break this.
- The scene is textured. Uniform walls, sky and repetitive patterns such as windows or tiles give few keypoints or
  ambiguous matches.

## Pitfalls

- **Threshold on an absolute scale.** $R$ grows with the fourth power of image contrast. Thresholds relative to the
  maximum response, or a fixed number of strongest keypoints, transfer better between images.
- **Clustered keypoints.** Strong responses concentrate on textured regions, leaving other parts of the image without
  matches. Adaptive non-maximal suppression or a grid spreads them out.
- **Repeated structure.** The nearest neighbour of a window corner is often another, identical window corner. The ratio
  test rejects many of these, but geometric verification is still needed.
- **Mixing distances.** Binary descriptors must be compared with Hamming distance, not Euclidean distance on the bytes.
