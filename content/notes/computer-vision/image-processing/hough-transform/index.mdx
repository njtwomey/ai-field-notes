<Definition>
The Hough transform detects straight lines in an image by voting in a space of line parameters. Every line in the
plane is written $x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta = \rho$, with $\theta \in [0, \pi)$ the direction of its unit normal and
$\rho$ its signed distance from the origin. Each edge point $(x_i, y_i)$ adds one vote to every discretised
$(\theta, \rho)$ cell whose line passes through it. Cells with many votes, the peaks of the accumulator, are the lines
supported by many edge points.
</Definition>

## Problem

An <NoteLink to="edge-detection">edge detector</NoteLink> returns a set of edge pixels, not lines. The edge pixels of
a straight boundary are scattered along it with gaps where contrast is low, displaced by noise, and mixed with edge
pixels from texture and other objects. Fitting one line by least squares fails when most points belong to other
structures, and the number of lines is not known in advance. Testing every pair of edge points as a candidate line and
counting its support costs $O(N^2)$ candidates for $N$ edge points, each checked against all $N$ points.

The Hough transform turns detection into counting. Each edge point contributes evidence for all lines that pass
through it. A line is detected where the evidence from many points agrees, however the points are spread along it and
whatever else is in the image. Hough introduced the method in a 1962 patent for finding particle tracks in
bubble-chamber photographs <Cite id="hough1962" />. Duda and Hart replaced his slope–intercept parameters with the
normal parameters $(\theta, \rho)$ used today <Cite id="duda1972" />.

## Mechanism

### Why not slope and intercept

A non-vertical line is $y = mx + c$. Fix an edge point $(x_i, y_i)$. The lines through it satisfy
$c = -x_i m + y_i$, which is itself a straight line in the $(m, c)$ plane. Two image points give two lines in the
$(m, c)$ plane, and those meet at the $(m, c)$ of the line through both points. Points and lines swap roles: this is a
point–line duality. Two points with the same $x$ give parallel lines in the $(m, c)$ plane, which never meet, because
the line through them is vertical.

The slope is unbounded. A line at angle $\alpha$ to the $x$-axis has $m = \tan\alpha$, which tends to infinity as
$\alpha \to 90°$, and a vertical line $x = x_0$ has no slope–intercept form at all. The intercept is unbounded too,
because $c = y_i - m x_i$ grows with $m$. No finite array over $(m, c)$ covers every line that crosses an image, and
equal steps in $m$ are unequal steps in angle.

### The normal parametrisation

Describe a line by its unit normal $\nvec = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and the foot of the perpendicular from the
origin, $\rho\,\nvec$. A point $\pvec = (x, y)$ lies on the line exactly when $\pvec - \rho\,\nvec$ is perpendicular to
$\nvec$:

$$
(\pvec - \rho\,\nvec) \cdot \nvec = 0 \iff \pvec \cdot \nvec = \rho\, \nvec \cdot \nvec = \rho
\iff x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta = \rho,
$$

using $\nvec \cdot \nvec = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. Every line, vertical ones included, has this form.

The pair $(\theta, \rho)$ and the pair $(\theta + \pi, -\rho)$ describe the same line, because replacing $\nvec$ by
$-\nvec$ and $\rho$ by $-\rho$ leaves the equation unchanged. Restricting $\theta$ to $[0, \pi)$ and letting $\rho$
take either sign removes the duplicate, so each line has exactly one pair. The equivalent convention
$\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$, $\rho \ge 0$ is also common; it is unique except for lines through the origin.

Both ranges are bounded for lines that meet the image. Place the origin at the image centre. For an image $W$ pixels
wide and $H$ pixels high, every image point satisfies $\norm{\pvec} \le R = \tfrac12\sqrt{W^2 + H^2}$, the distance to
a corner. If a line meets the image at a point $\pvec$, the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink> gives
$\abs{\rho} = \abs{\pvec \cdot \nvec} \le \norm{\pvec}\norm{\nvec} \le R$. The parameter space is the rectangle
$[0, \pi) \times [-R, R]$. For a $128 \times 128$ image, $R = 64\sqrt2 \approx 90.5$ pixels.

### Each point traces a sinusoid

Fix an edge point $\pvec_i = (x_i, y_i)$ and let $\theta$ vary. The lines through $\pvec_i$ have

$$
\rho_i(\theta) = x_i\cos\theta + y_i\sin\theta.
$$

Write the point in polar form, $x_i = r_i\cos\phi_i$ and $y_i = r_i\sin\phi_i$, with $r_i = \norm{\pvec_i}$. The
angle-difference identity $\cos(\theta - \phi_i) = \cos\theta\cos\phi_i + \sin\theta\sin\phi_i$ gives

$$
\rho_i(\theta) = r_i\cos\phi_i\cos\theta + r_i\sin\phi_i\sin\theta = r_i\cos(\theta - \phi_i).
$$

The curve is a sinusoid in $\theta$ with amplitude $r_i$, the point's distance from the origin, and phase $\phi_i$, the
point's direction. It peaks at $\theta = \phi_i$ with $\rho = r_i$: the line through $\pvec_i$ perpendicular to the ray
from the origin to $\pvec_i$ is the farthest line from the origin through that point. A point at the origin gives
$\rho_i(\theta) = 0$ for every $\theta$, since every line through the origin has $\rho = 0$.

### Collinear points meet at one cell

Suppose points $\pvec_1, \dots, \pvec_n$ lie on the line $(\theta^\ast, \rho^\ast)$. Each satisfies
$\pvec_i \cdot \nvec(\theta^\ast) = \rho^\ast$, so each sinusoid has $\rho_i(\theta^\ast) = \rho^\ast$. All $n$
sinusoids pass through the single point $(\theta^\ast, \rho^\ast)$.

The converse holds too. Two distinct points have equal $\rho$ at angle $\theta$ when

$$
\rho_1(\theta) = \rho_2(\theta) \iff (\pvec_1 - \pvec_2) \cdot \nvec(\theta) = 0,
$$

that is, when $\nvec(\theta)$ is perpendicular to $\pvec_1 - \pvec_2$. That fixes $\nvec$ up to sign, which is one
value of $\theta$ in $[0, \pi)$. Two sinusoids therefore cross exactly once in $[0, \pi)$, at the unique line through
the two points. If $n$ sinusoids share a point $(\theta^\ast, \rho^\ast)$, all $n$ points satisfy
$\pvec_i \cdot \nvec(\theta^\ast) = \rho^\ast$ and lie on that line. A point of the $(\theta, \rho)$ plane crossed by
many sinusoids is a line through many edge points.

### The algorithm

1. **Detect edges.** Run an edge detector, for example Canny, and collect the $N$ edge points $\pvec_i$, measured from
   the image centre.
2. **Discretise.** Choose a bin width $\Delta\theta$ and a bin width $\Delta\rho$. The columns are
   $\theta_j = j\,\Delta\theta$ for $j = 0, \dots, N_\theta - 1$ with $N_\theta = \pi / \Delta\theta$. The rows split
   $[-R, R]$ into $N_\rho = \lceil 2R / \Delta\rho \rceil$ bins. Set the accumulator $A$, an
   $N_\rho \times N_\theta$ array of counts, to zero.
3. **Vote.** For every edge point and every column $j$, compute $\rho = x_i\cos\theta_j + y_i\sin\theta_j$, find its
   row $k = \lfloor (\rho + R)/\Delta\rho \rfloor$, and add one to $A[k, j]$. Precomputed tables of
   $\cos\theta_j$ and $\sin\theta_j$ make each vote two multiplications and an addition.
4. **Find peaks.** A long line gives a peak spread over neighbouring cells, and noise gives many small local maxima.
   Keep cells whose count exceeds a threshold and that are the maximum within a window of neighbours:
   <Gloss name="nms">non-maximum suppression</Gloss>. A greedy version takes the highest cell, discards every cell
   within the window around it, and repeats. The window wraps at $\theta = \pi$, where $(\theta, \rho)$ meets
   $(\theta - \pi, -\rho)$.
5. **Convert back.** Each peak $(\theta_j, \rho_k)$ is the line $x\cos\theta_j + y\sin\theta_j = \rho_k$, with $\rho_k$
   the centre of row $k$. The line is infinite. Segments are recovered by walking along it in the edge map and
   splitting where the gap between edge points exceeds a set length.

## Worked example

Take four edge points: $\pvec_1 = (0, 2)$, $\pvec_2 = (1, 1)$, $\pvec_3 = (2, 0)$ and $\pvec_4 = (2, 2)$. The first
three lie on the line $x + y = 2$; $\pvec_4$ does not. Their sinusoids are

$$
\rho_1 = 2\sin\theta, \quad \rho_2 = \cos\theta + \sin\theta, \quad \rho_3 = 2\cos\theta, \quad
\rho_4 = 2\cos\theta + 2\sin\theta.
$$

The values at four angles, with $\sqrt2 \approx 1.414$:

| $\theta$     | $\rho_1$ | $\rho_2$ | $\rho_3$ | $\rho_4$ | shared value                    |
| ------------ | -------- | -------- | -------- | -------- | ------------------------------- |
| $0°$         | 0        | 1        | 2        | 2        | $\rho = 2$: $\pvec_3, \pvec_4$  |
| $45°$        | 1.414    | 1.414    | 1.414    | 2.828    | $\rho = \sqrt2$: $\pvec_1, \pvec_2, \pvec_3$ |
| $90°$        | 2        | 1        | 0        | 2        | $\rho = 2$: $\pvec_1, \pvec_4$  |
| $135°$       | 1.414    | 0        | $-1.414$ | 0        | $\rho = 0$: $\pvec_2, \pvec_4$  |

The crossing of the first three sinusoids follows from the perpendicularity condition. $\pvec_1 - \pvec_3 = (-2, 2)$,
and the unit normal perpendicular to it with $\theta \in [0, \pi)$ is $\nvec = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$, so $\theta = 45°$.
Then $\rho = \pvec_1 \cdot \nvec = 2/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2$. The second point gives
$\rho_2(45°) = (1 + 1)/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2$ as well. With bins of $\Delta\theta = 45°$ and $\Delta\rho = 1$, the cell
holding $(45°, \sqrt2)$ receives three votes. Every other column has at most two equal values, and each of those
pairs is the line through $\pvec_4$ and one other point: $x = 2$ at $\theta = 0°$, $y = 2$ at $\theta = 90°$ and
$y = x$ at $\theta = 135°$. The peak converts back to $x\cos 45° + y\sin 45° = \sqrt2$, which is $x + y = 2$.

## Cost

Voting costs $O(N_{\text{edge}} \cdot N_\theta)$ time for $N_{\text{edge}}$ edge points: each point casts one vote per
column. The accumulator takes $O(N_\theta N_\rho)$ memory, and a scan for peaks with a window of $w \times w$ cells
costs $O(N_\theta N_\rho w^2)$. For $2{,}000$ edge points and $1°$ columns, voting is $360{,}000$ updates. The
time is linear in the number of edge points. The pairwise search generates $O(N_{\text{edge}}^2)$ candidate lines
before it counts the support of any.

## Trade-offs

### Bin sizes

Bin widths set a trade between precision and robustness to noise, and the two widths depend on each other.

A point displaced by $e$ pixels along the normal of its line changes its $\rho$ by exactly $e$, because
$(\pvec + e\,\nvec) \cdot \nvec = \pvec \cdot \nvec + e$. With edge noise of <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma$ pixels, a
line's votes spread over about $\pm 2\sigma$ in $\rho$. Bins much narrower than $\sigma$ split the peak over several
rows and lower it below the clutter. Bins much wider than $\sigma$ merge parallel lines closer than $\Delta\rho$ and
locate each line only to within $\Delta\rho$.

The $\theta$ width must match the length of the line. Differentiating $\rho_i(\theta)$ gives

$$
\frac{d\rho_i}{d\theta} = -x_i\sin\theta + y_i\cos\theta = \pvec_i \cdot \nvec_\perp(\theta),
$$

where $\nvec_\perp = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ is the direction along the line. The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is $t_i$, the
position of $\pvec_i$ along the line. At a column $\delta$ away from the true angle, the votes of a segment of length
$L$ spread over a $\rho$ interval of width about $L\abs{\delta}$. The nearest column is at most $\Delta\theta/2$ away,
so the whole segment stays in one row when $L\,\Delta\theta / 2 \le \Delta\rho$, that is
$\Delta\theta \le 2\Delta\rho / L$ in radians. For a line crossing a $128$-pixel image and $\Delta\rho = 1$ pixel,
this asks for $\Delta\theta \le 2/128$ rad, about $0.9°$.

### Voting along the gradient direction

The image gradient at an edge point is perpendicular to the edge, so it estimates the normal of the line through the
point. With the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> angle $\phi_i = \operatorname{atan2}(g_y, g_x)$ reduced to $[0, \pi)$, the point needs to
vote only in the column $\theta = \phi_i$, or in a few columns around it to absorb error in the gradient direction
<Cite id="ogorman1976" />. Voting then costs $O(N_{\text{edge}})$ rather than $O(N_{\text{edge}} N_\theta)$, and the
accumulator holds far fewer stray votes, so peaks stand out more. The method depends on reliable gradient directions,
which noise and corners degrade.

### Probabilistic Hough transforms

A peak is found long before every edge point has voted. The probabilistic Hough transform votes with a random subset of
the edge points and scales the threshold to match <Cite id="kiryati1991" />. The progressive probabilistic Hough
transform takes points in random order and votes with each <Cite id="matas2000" />. When a cell's count becomes
unlikely to have arisen from noise, it follows the corresponding line through the edge map, extracts the segment,
removes that segment's points from the pool, and withdraws their votes. Its cost adapts to the image, and it returns
segments with end points rather than infinite lines. OpenCV's `HoughLinesP` implements it.

### Spurious peaks

Votes from unrelated points pile up. Uniform clutter of density $\lambda$ points per pixel adds on average
$\lambda\,\ell(\theta, \rho)\,\Delta\rho$ votes to a cell, where $\ell(\theta, \rho)$ is the length of that line's chord
inside the image. Chords are longest near $\rho = 0$, so clutter raises a ridge through the middle of the accumulator
and biases detection towards lines near the centre. Dividing counts by the chord length, or setting the threshold from
the expected clutter count, removes the bias. Textured regions such as foliage or brickwork give dense edge points
whose accidental alignments form peaks with no line behind them. The count of a true line is proportional to its
length in edge points, so a short real line can score below a long accidental one. A peak is evidence of many
collinear edge points, not of an object boundary.

## Generalisations

### Circles

A circle is $(x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2$, with centre $(a, b)$ and radius $r$. An edge point $(x_i, y_i)$ votes for
every circle through it. For each radius $r$, those centres form the circle of radius $r$ around $(x_i, y_i)$, so the
votes fill a cone in the three-dimensional $(a, b, r)$ accumulator. Duda and Hart described this extension
<Cite id="duda1972" />. The gradient direction $\phi_i$ points towards or away from the centre, which gives
$(a, b) = (x_i, y_i) \mp r(\cos\phi_i, \sin\phi_i)$: two votes per radius instead of a whole circle
<Cite id="kimme1975" />.

### Arbitrary shapes

The generalised Hough transform detects a shape with no analytic form <Cite id="ballard1981" />. Pick a reference point
inside a template of the shape. For each boundary point of the template, record the vector $\rvec$ from the
boundary point to the reference point, indexed by the boundary's gradient direction $\phi$. The table of vectors per
$\phi$ is the R-table. At detection, each edge point with gradient $\phi$ looks up the vectors stored under $\phi$ and
votes for the reference point at $\pvec_i + \rvec$ for each. A peak in the two-dimensional accumulator is the
location of the shape. Unknown rotation and scale add a dimension each, with the stored vectors rotated and scaled
before voting.

## Relation to the Radon transform

The Radon transform of an image $f(x, y)$ integrates $f$ along every line:

$$
\mathcal{R}f(\theta, \rho) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f\paren{\rho\,\nvec(\theta) + t\,\nvec_\perp(\theta)}\,dt,
$$

where $\nvec_\perp(\theta) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ runs along the line and $t$ is arc length on it
<Cite id="radon1917" />.

Take $f$ to be the edge image as a sum of point masses, $f = \sum_i \delta_{\pvec_i}$. The line $(\theta, \rho)$ picks
up one unit for each point with $\pvec_i \cdot \nvec(\theta) = \rho$, so $\mathcal{R}f(\theta, \rho) =
\sum_i \delta(\rho - \pvec_i \cdot \nvec(\theta))$. Integrating over a $\rho$ bin at $\theta_j$ counts the points whose
$\rho_i(\theta_j)$ falls in the bin, which is the accumulator entry $A[k, j]$. The Hough accumulator is the Radon
transform of the edge image, binned in $\rho$ <Cite id="deans1981" />. Computed tomography measures the Radon transform
of the X-ray attenuation in a slice of the body and inverts it, usually by filtered back projection, to reconstruct the
slice. A plot of $\mathcal{R}f$ over $(\theta, \rho)$ is called a sinogram because a single point traces the sinusoid
$\rho_i(\theta)$ derived above.

## Vanishing points

Lines that are parallel in the scene converge in a perspective image at a vanishing point $\vvec$ (see
<NoteLink to="pinhole-camera-model" />). Every image line through $\vvec$ satisfies $\rho = \vvec \cdot \nvec(\theta)$,
so the Hough peaks $(\theta_m, \rho_m)$ of lines that share a vanishing point lie on the sinusoid of $\vvec$. Finding
$\vvec$ is a second Hough transform: each detected line votes for every candidate $\vvec$ on it, and the peak of that
vote is the point most lines pass through. Equivalently, $\vvec$ minimises $\sum_m (\vvec \cdot \nvec(\theta_m) -
\rho_m)^2$, a linear least-squares problem in the two coordinates of $\vvec$. Vanishing points often lie outside the
image, or at infinity for lines parallel to the image plane. Barnard therefore voted on the Gaussian sphere, the unit
sphere of viewing directions around the camera centre: each image line and the camera centre span a plane, which cuts
the sphere in a great circle, and the great circles of parallel scene lines meet at the direction of those lines
<Cite id="barnard1983" />. The sphere is bounded, so vanishing points at infinity fall on its equator rather than
outside the accumulator.

## Pitfalls

- **Origin and axis conventions.** $\rho$ depends on where the origin is. With the origin at the top-left corner and
  $y$ pointing down, as in many libraries, $\rho$ ranges over $[-\sqrt{W^2 + H^2}, \sqrt{W^2 + H^2}]$ and the sign of
  $\theta$ flips relative to a $y$-up frame.
- **Peaks are infinite lines.** A peak says nothing about where a segment starts and ends, and collinear segments on
  either side of a gap merge into one peak.
- **Thick edges.** Edges several pixels wide vote as several parallel lines and smear each peak across rows. Thinning
  by non-maximum suppression in the edge detector keeps peaks sharp.
- **Threshold choice.** Peak counts scale with line length and image size, so an absolute threshold that works on one
  image fails on another <Cite id="szeliski2022" />.
