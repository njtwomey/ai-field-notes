<Definition>
A measurement scale defines the mathematical and relational properties preserved when real-world empirical phenomena are
mapped to numbers. Formalized by Stanley Smith Stevens, measurement scales dictate which mathematical transformations
leave the empirical meaning of the numbers unchanged, and consequently which statistical operations, encodings, and loss
functions are mathematically valid.
</Definition>

In machine learning pipelines, data types are frequently confused with storage formats: a floating-point column might
represent a physical ratio (mass in kilograms), an arbitrary interval scale (calendar year), an encoded ordinal rating
(star rating 1.0 to 5.0), or a hashed nominal identifier <Cite id="stevens1946" />. Treating numbers according to their
programming data type (`float64`) rather than their underlying measurement scale injects spurious inductive biases,
distorts distance metrics, and corrupts model optimization.

## The four scales

Stevens' classical taxonomy categorizes measurements into a four-level hierarchy based on the mathematical
transformations that preserve their empirical relational structure <Cite id="stevens1946" />:

### Nominal

On a nominal scale, numbers or symbols serve exclusively as distinct labels or category tags. The only meaningful
empirical relation between two observations is equivalence ($=$ or $\neq$).

- **Permissible Transformations:** Any one-to-one mapping (permutations): $f(x)$ where
  $x_1 \neq x_2 \implies f(x_1) \neq f(x_2)$.
- **Examples:** Postal codes, telephone area codes, IP addresses, blood types (`A`, `B`, `AB`, `O`), device operating
  systems.
- **Valid Operations:** Mode, frequencies, contingency tables, entropy, mutual information. Computing the arithmetic
  mean of postal codes (`mean([94103, 10001]) = 52052`) is mathematically meaningless.

### Ordinal

An ordinal scale introduces a strict transitive order relation ($<$ or $>$). Numbers reflect ranking, but the numerical
distance between adjacent ranks is undefined and non-uniform.

- **Permissible Transformations:** Any strictly monotonic increasing transformation: $f(x)$ where
  $x_1 < x_2 \iff f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.
- **Examples:** Likert agreement scales (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree), education levels (High School <
  Bachelors < Masters < PhD), stage of cancer progression (Stage I through IV), search result ranks.
- **Valid Operations:** Median, percentiles, rank-order correlations (Spearman's $\rho$, Kendall's $\tau$), ordinal
  logistic regression. Subtracting ordinal values ($4 - 3 = 1$ and $2 - 1 = 1$) assumes equal subjective spacing that is
  not supported by the scale.

### Interval

On an interval scale, differences between values are quantitatively meaningful and equal across the entire scale.
However, the origin (zero point) is arbitrary and defined by convention rather than the absolute absence of the measured
attribute.

- **Permissible Transformations:** Positive affine transformations: $f(x) = a x + b$ where $a > 0$.
- **Examples:** Temperature in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit ($0^\circ\text{C}$ is arbitrarily defined as the freezing
  point of water, not the total absence of thermodynamic heat), calendar years (AD/BC), credit scores.
- **Valid Operations:** Arithmetic mean, standard deviation, variance, Pearson correlation, Euclidean distance, linear
  regression. Ratios are invalid: $40^\circ\text{C}$ is not "twice as hot" as $20^\circ\text{C}$ (in absolute Kelvin,
  $313.15\,\text{K}$ versus $293.15\,\text{K}$ represents an increase of only $6.8\%$).

### Ratio

A ratio scale possesses all properties of an interval scale, plus an absolute, non-arbitrary zero point denoting the
complete absence of the measured property.

- **Permissible Transformations:** Similarity / positive scalar multiplications: $f(x) = a x$ where $a > 0$.
- **Examples:** Mass, length, duration, monetary amounts, Kelvin temperature, pixel luminance, network latency.
- **Valid Operations:** Geometric mean, harmonic mean, coefficient of variation ($CV = \sigma / \mu$), percent changes,
  logarithmic transformations, power-law modeling. Ratios are fully meaningful: 20 kilograms is identically twice as
  heavy as 10 kilograms.

## Typed values beyond the four scales

Modern machine learning regularly encounters complex mathematical structures that do not fit neatly into Stevens' four
classical scales:

### Counts and rates

Counts represent discrete, non-negative integers ($\mathbb{N}_0 = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$) with a natural zero. They are
bounded below and often exhibit variance proportional to the mean. Standard Gaussian regression assumes symmetric
continuous errors; count data requires Poisson, quasi-Poisson, or Negative Binomial generalized linear models with
log-link functions and exposure offsets (e.g., clicks per impression, crimes per capita).

### Proportions and compositions

Compositional data represents parts of a whole constrained to sum to a constant (e.g., budget allocations, chemical
element concentrations, topic distribution mixtures). The sample space is the simplex:

$$
\mathcal{S}^D = \left\{ (x_1, \dots, x_D) \in \mathbb{R}^D : x_i > 0, \sum_{i=1}^D x_i = 1 \right\}
$$

Applying standard Euclidean covariance to raw proportions induces spurious negative correlations because increasing one
component forces others to decrease <Cite id="aitchison1982" />. Valid modeling requires log-ratio transformations, such
as the Centered Log-Ratio (CLR) or Isometric Log-Ratio (ILR) mapped onto Aitchison geometry.

### Durations and times

Temporal variables encompass two distinct scales:
1. **Wall-clock Timestamps (Interval):** Point-in-time references (`2026-10-12T09:30:00Z`).
2. **Elapsed Durations (Ratio):** Positive continuous time intervals ($\Delta t \ge 0$). In survival analysis, durations
   are frequently right-censored, necessitating Cox proportional hazards or Weibull parametric formulations.

### Circular quantities

Directional and periodic quantities—such as compass heading ($\theta \in [0, 360^\circ)$), wind direction, and cyclical
time (hour of day $\in [0, 24)$, day of week $\in [0, 7)$)—wrap around at boundary limits. Linear metrics suffer
catastrophic boundary discontinuities: the linear distance between 23:00 and 01:00 is 22 hours, whereas the true
circular distance is 2 hours. Circular features require directional statistics (von Mises distributions) or 2D harmonic
coordinate embedding:

$$
x_{\text{sin}} = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right), \quad x_{\text{cos}} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right)
$$

### Sets, lists and free text

Modern inputs include permutation-invariant sets (tags, installed apps), ordered sequences (click paths, audio samples),
and natural language text. These require permutation-equivariant neural architectures (DeepSets), sequence models
(Transformers), or dense multi-dimensional vector embeddings.

## What each scale permits

| Scale | Relation Preserved | Permissible Transformation | Central Tendency | Dispersion | Admissible Models |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nominal** | Equality ($=, \neq$) | Permutation: $\pi(x)$ | Mode | Information Entropy | Tree splits, One-hot + linear, Embeddings |
| **Ordinal** | Order ($<, >$) | Monotonic: $f'(x) > 0$ | Median | Interquartile Range (IQR) | Decision trees, Ordinal regression, RankNet |
| **Interval** | Difference ($x_i - x_j$) | Positive Affine: $ax + b$ | Arithmetic Mean | Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) | Linear regression, Ridge/Lasso, PCA, Neural nets |
| **Ratio** | Ratio ($x_i / x_j$) | Positive Scale: $ax$ | Geometric Mean | Coeff. of Variation ($\sigma / \mu$) | Log-linear models, Gamma GLM, Elasticity models |
| **Circular** | Angle on circle | Rotation: $\theta + \alpha \pmod{2\pi}$ | Mean Direction | Circular Variance | Harmonic embedding $[\sin, \cos]$, Von Mises |

## Consequences for encoding and models

1. **Distance-Based Algorithms (k-NN, SVM, k-Means):** Computing Euclidean distance $\|x - y\|_2$ across raw nominal
   integers (treating postal codes as numbers) or ordinal ranks implicitly assumes a uniform interval metric. Points
   with numerical distance 10 are treated as 10 times further apart than points with distance 1, corrupting
   nearest-neighbor graphs.
2. **Gradient Descent and Feature Scaling:** Applying standard Z-score standardization ($z = (x - \mu) / \sigma$) to
   ordinal scales assumes that the mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ are meaningful, altering the subjective
   psychological spacing between rank steps.
3. **Tree-Based Ensembles:** Decision trees (Random Forests, LightGBM, XGBoost) split on threshold inequalities
   ($x_j \le c$). Because monotonic transformations preserve inequalities identically, tree models are invariant to
   monotonic transformations of ordinal and continuous features, making them naturally robust to ordinal scaling
   distortions.

## Pitfalls

- **The Likert-as-Interval Fallacy:** Averaging 5-point customer satisfaction surveys and computing standard deviations
  assumes the subjective gap between "Neutral" (3) and "Satisfied" (4) is identical to the gap between "Very
  Dissatisfied" (1) and "Dissatisfied" (2). In reality, the psychological step to extreme sentiment is non-linear.
- **Encoding Hour-of-Day as an Integer:** Feeding raw integers $0 \dots 23$ into a linear model forces the model to
  learn that midnight ($0$) and 23:00 are polar opposites, destroying diurnal rhythm patterns.
- **Log-Transforming Non-Ratio Variables:** Applying $\log(x)$ or $\log(x + 1)$ to interval variables (such as
  temperatures in Celsius or calendar years) produces arbitrary outputs that change completely if Celsius is converted
  to Fahrenheit. Log transforms are admissible only on ratio scales where zero represents an absolute physical floor.
- **One-Hot Encoding High-Cardinality Ordinal Variables:** Converting a 10-level ordinal severity score into 10
  independent binary columns discards natural monotonic ordering, forcing downstream models to learn 10 independent
  parameters and inviting overfitting.
