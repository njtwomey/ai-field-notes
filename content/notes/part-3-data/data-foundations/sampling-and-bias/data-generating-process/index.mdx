<Definition>
The data-generating process (DGP) is the physical, algorithmic, or social mechanism that produces real-world states and
dictates how specific units are measured, filtered, and recorded into a dataset. Selection bias occurs when inclusion in
the observed dataset is conditionally dependent on the features or target outcomes being analyzed, breaking the
representative link between the sample and the target population.
</Definition>

Machine learning models optimize loss functions over empirical sample matrices $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$.
However, algorithms cannot observe the underlying universe; they observe only the survivors of the data collection
pipeline <Cite id="pearl2009, heckman1979" />. When the probability of observation depends on the variables under study,
correlations learned by the model reflect the selection filter rather than true environmental relationships.

## The data-generating process

A complete specification of a data-generating process encompasses three distinct operational stages:
1. **The Latent World Distribution ($P(X^*, Y^*)$):** The underlying joint distribution of features and outcomes across
   all eligible entities in the universe, regardless of whether they interact with our measurement instruments.
2. **The Sampling / Inclusion Mechanism ($S \in \{0, 1\}$):** A Bernoulli indicator denoting whether entity $i$ appears
   in the recorded dataset:

$$
P(S = 1 \mid X^*, Y^*)
$$

3. **The Measurement and Recording Channel ($P(X, Y \mid X^*, Y^*, S=1)$):** The physical sensors, UI forms, or human
   annotators that map latent states $(X^*, Y^*)$ into recorded columns $(X, Y)$, potentially injecting noise,
   truncation, or rounding.

A dataset is an unbiased draw from the target distribution if and only if inclusion is conditionally independent of
features and targets: $S \perp (X^*, Y^*)$ (Missing Completely at Random), or conditionally independent given fully
observed covariates: $S \perp Y^* \mid X^*$ (Missing at Random). When inclusion depends directly on the outcome $Y^*$
without conditioning ($S \not\perp Y^*$), selection bias is severe.

## Populations, samples and target populations

Clarity of inference requires distinguishing three nested populations:

- **Target Population ($\mathcal{U}_{\text{target}}$):** The universe of entities to which the deployed model will be
  applied at inference time (e.g., all future applicants for credit next year).
- **Sampling Frame ($\mathcal{U}_{\text{frame}}$):** The operational subset of the population that has a non-zero
  probability of being recorded (e.g., individuals who have existing bank accounts and applied via the web portal). The
  difference between the frame and the target is *coverage error*.
- **Observed Sample ($\mathcal{S}$):** The finite subset of units drawn from the frame that actually completed the
  recording process.

## Sampling designs

The probabilistic architecture used to draw sample $\mathcal{S}$ determines how weights must be assigned:

### Simple random sampling

Every subset of $n$ units from population $N$ has an identical inclusion probability $\pi_i = n / N$. In commercial
machine learning, true simple random sampling is virtually non-existent; web logs and transactional tables are
convenience samples generated by self-selected user traffic.

### Stratified sampling

The population is partitioned into mutually exclusive, exhaustive strata $\{H_1, \dots, H_K\}$ based on baseline
covariates (e.g., age bracket, geographic region). Samples are drawn independently within each stratum. Stratification
guarantees representation for rare subgroups and reduces sample estimator variance when within-stratum variance is lower
than between-stratum variance.

### Cluster sampling

The population is divided into clusters (e.g., hospitals, school districts). Instead of sampling individuals, entire
clusters are sampled at random, and all individuals within chosen clusters are recorded. Cluster sampling reduces
administrative collection costs but increases estimator variance due to intra-cluster correlation.

### Case-control sampling

In domains with extreme class imbalance (e.g., rare diseases or credit defaults occurring at $0.01\%$), case-control
designs oversample positive cases ($Y=1$) and sample a small matching subset of controls ($Y=0$). Case-control datasets
allow consistent estimation of relative odds ratios via logistic regression, but distort baseline intercept
calibrations: predicted probabilities must be recalibrated to reflect the true population base rate.

## Selection bias

Selection bias manifests across classical observational failure modes:

### Survivorship bias

Units drop out of the observation pool before measurement occurs. The famous historical example is Abraham Wald's World
War II aircraft armor analysis: returning bombers exhibited bullet holes concentrated on the wings and fuselage, with
none on the engines. Analyzing only surviving planes led naive observers to recommend armoring the wings; Wald correctly
recognized that planes shot in the engines crashed and never returned to the dataset. Similarly, evaluating hedge fund
performance using currently active funds overestimates historical returns by ignoring collapsed funds.

### Self-selection and non-response

When inclusion depends on voluntary action (e.g., online product reviews, optional survey forms, customer feedback
tickets), participants exhibit extreme sentiment distributions. A customer rarely leaves a 3-star review for an adequate
meal; online ratings form bimodal J-curves dominated by ecstatic advocates and furious detractors.

### Collider bias

In a causal directed acyclic graph (DAG), a variable $S$ is a *collider* along a path between $X$ and $Y$ if both arrows
point into it: $X \to S \leftarrow Y$ <Cite id="pearl2009" />. While $X$ and $Y$ may be marginally independent in the
population, **conditioning on a collider induces a spurious correlation between them**.

A celebrated manifestation is **Berkson's Fallacy** <Cite id="berkson1946" />: in a hospital-based study of two
completely independent diseases (e.g., diabetes and cholecystitis), patients are admitted ($S=1$) if they have *either*
disease. Consequently, among hospitalized patients, having diabetes is negatively correlated with having cholecystitis:
if an admitted patient does not have diabetes, they almost certainly have cholecystitis to account for their
hospitalization.

In machine learning, filtering datasets by quality thresholds (e.g., analyzing only hired software engineers, approved
loan borrowers, or highly rated films) conditions on a collider, creating artificial negative trade-offs between
desirable qualities (e.g., coding ability vs social skills).

## Simpson's paradox

Simpson's paradox occurs when an apparent statistical association between two variables reverses direction when data is
disaggregated across confounding subpopulations <Cite id="simpson1951, bickel1975" />.

In the landmark UC Berkeley graduate admissions study <Cite id="bickel1975" />, aggregate admissions data indicated that
male applicants had a significantly higher overall admission rate ($44\%$) than female applicants ($35\%$). However,
when disaggregated by department, individual departments showed a slight bias *in favor* of women. The paradox arose
because women applied disproportionately to highly competitive humanities departments with low overall admission rates
($<10\%$), while men applied heavily to engineering departments with abundant capacity ($>60\%$). Department choice
acted as a confounder that inverted the marginal correlation.

$$
P(Y=1 \mid X) = \sum_z P(Y=1 \mid X, Z=z) P(Z=z \mid X)
$$

Failing to condition on the confounder $Z$ leads to completely inverted predictive coefficients.

## Consequences for learning

1. **Covariate Shift vs Selection on the Residual:** If selection depends strictly on features ($S \perp Y \mid X$), the
   relationship $P(Y \mid X)$ is invariant, and supervised models can still converge to the true conditional mean given
   sufficient model capacity. If selection depends on unobserved residuals ($S \not\perp \epsilon$), the observed
   conditional expectation is biased:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X, S=1] = X \beta + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid X, S=1] \neq X \beta
$$

2. **Heckman Correction:** James Heckman formulated a two-step procedure to correct selection on residuals
   <Cite id="heckman1979" />. A probit selection model first predicts inclusion probability $\Phi(Z \gamma)$. The
   inverse Mills ratio $\lambda(Z \gamma) = \frac{\phi(Z \gamma)}{\Phi(Z \gamma)}$ is computed and added as an explicit
   control covariate in the primary regression, absorbing the selection error.

## Pitfalls

- **Evaluating Deployed Models on Historical Approvals:** Evaluating a credit scoring model solely on loans that human
  underwriters previously approved measures default rates on a pre-screened population. The model will perform poorly on
  the full distribution of unfiltered applicants.
- **Treating Missingness as Random (MCAR Fallacy):** Assuming missing survey answers or missing medical tests occur
  uniformly at random. Doctors order cardiac troponin tests precisely when patients exhibit severe chest pain; the
  presence of the measurement is itself a strong prognostic signal.
- **Conditioning on Post-Treatment Variables:** Including variables in feature sets that were caused by the treatment or
  the outcome creates synthetic collider paths and blocks legitimate causal transmission channels.
- **Naive AB Test Splitting on Pre-Filtered Audiences:** Running experiments only on active, retained users blinds teams
  to treatment effects that induce early churn among new sign-ups.
