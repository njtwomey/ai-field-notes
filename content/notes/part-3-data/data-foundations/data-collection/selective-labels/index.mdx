<Definition>
The selective labels problem occurs when ground-truth outcome labels $Y$ are observed only for instances that
successfully passed a historical human or algorithmic decision gate ($D = 1$). For rejected instances ($D = 0$), the
counterfactual outcome remains completely unobserved, restricting the supervised training set to a non-random, heavily
pre-screened subpopulation.
</Definition>

High-stakes machine learning applications in lending, hiring, judicial bail, and fraud detection operate under selective
labels <Cite id="lakkaraju2017, kleinberg2018" />. In credit underwriting, a bank observes whether a borrower repays
($Y = 0$) or defaults ($Y = 1$) only for applicants granted credit. What rejected applicants would have done had they
been approved remains unrecorded. Training models directly on approved cases without accounting for the selection filter
produces severe evaluation blindness and perpetuates historical gatekeeper biases <Cite id="heckman1979" />.

## How selective labels arise

Selective labeling is structural in any operational workflow where downstream actions depend on an initial triage or
screening policy:

1. **Consumer Lending & Credit Risk:** Applicants apply for mortgages or credit cards ($X$). An underwriting policy
   approves candidates with risk scores above a threshold ($D = 1$). Repayment outcomes $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ are generated
   over the subsequent 24 months. Rejected applicants ($D = 0$) receive no capital, yielding no repayment data.
2. **Judicial Pretrial Bail:** Judges decide whether to release an arrested defendant awaiting trial ($D = 1$) or detain
   them in jail ($D = 0$) <Cite id="kleinberg2018" />. The outcome of interest—whether the defendant commits a new crime
   or fails to appear in court ($Y \in \{0, 1\}$)—can be observed only for released individuals. Jailed defendants
   cannot commit pretrial street crimes.
3. **Payment Fraud Detection:** An anti-fraud system blocks suspicious e-commerce payments ($D = 0$). True chargeback
   fraud labels ($Y = 1$) materialize only for authorized payments ($D = 1$) that settle through card payment networks.
4. **Employment Recruiting:** Recruiters interview candidate software engineers ($X$) and extend job offers ($D = 1$).
   Subsequent job performance metrics ($Y$) are observed only for hired employees.

In all four cases, the observed dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{obs}} = \{(x_i, y_i) : D_i = 1\}$ reflects the joint
distribution $P(X, Y \mid D = 1)$, which differs fundamentally from the true applicant population distribution
$P(X, Y)$.

## Reject inference

In banking and credit analytics, statistical methodologies developed to impute or account for missing labels on rejected
candidates are termed **Reject Inference**:

- **Hard Cutoff Imputation (Worst-Case Fallacy):** Naively labeling all rejected applicants as defaults ($Y = 1$). While
  conservative, this heuristic assumes zero creditworthy applicants exist in the rejected pool, entrenching historical
  thresholds and generating severe label noise.
- **Proportional Parceling:** Partitioning the applicant pool into score bands based on credit bureaus. The default rate
  observed among accepted applicants in score band $k$ is imputed proportionally onto rejected applicants in the same
  band.
- **Reweighting / Augmentation (Inverse Probability of Selection):** Treating selection as an observable propensity
  score $e(x) = P(D = 1 \mid X = x)$. Under the unconfoundedness assumption that default outcomes are independent of the
  decision given observed features ($Y \perp D \mid X$), each approved applicant is reweighted by $1 / e(x)$ during
  model training.
- **Heckman Selection Models (Bivariate Probit):** Modeling the selection decision $D^* = Z\gamma + u_1$ and outcome
  $Y^* = X\beta + u_2$ jointly with correlated error terms $\operatorname{Corr}(u_1, u_2) = \rho$
  <Cite id="heckman1979" />. Estimating $\rho$ directly corrects for unobserved selection factors.

## Exploration slices

When the historical screening policy is deterministic ($D = \mathbb{I}(X \ge \tau)$), unconfoundedness and common
support are completely violated: $P(D = 1 \mid X < \tau) = 0$. In this regime, observational reject inference cannot
extrapolate without extreme, untestable parametric assumptions <Cite id="lakkaraju2017" />.

The definitive engineering solution is to implement **Exploration Slices (Rand-Passes)**:
1. **Randomized Approval Budget:** The operational policy reserves a small fraction of capital (e.g., $1\%$ to $3\%$ of
   total originations) for controlled randomized exploration.
2. **Epsilon-Greedy Passes:** Applicants who would normally be rejected ($X < \tau$) are randomly approved with
   probability $\epsilon > 0$.
3. **Ground-Truth Counterfactual Labels:** Although the lending institution incurs a controlled financial loss from
   defaults in this exploration bucket, the resulting observations provide gold-standard, unselected ground truth across
   the entire score range, satisfying the positivity condition ($P(D=1 \mid X) > 0$) and enabling unbiased model
   recalibration.

## Evaluating under selective labels

Evaluating a candidate model $M_B$ against an incumbent production system $M_A$ under selective labels is notoriously
fraught <Cite id="lakkaraju2017, kleinberg2018" />:

- **The Evaluation Contraction Trap:** Calculating AUC or accuracy strictly on historical test cases where $D_A = 1$
  measures model performance only on the subset of applicants that incumbent system $M_A$ found acceptable. If $M_B$
  disagrees with $M_A$—for example, by wishing to approve a subpopulation that $M_A$ rejected—evaluating on $D_A = 1$
  cannot verify whether $M_B$'s novel approvals are creditworthy.
- **Contraction Mapping Bounds:** Lakkaraju et al. formulate evaluation bounds using contraction techniques and
  counterfactual bounding <Cite id="lakkaraju2017" />. By decomposing performance into the consensus region
  ($D_A = 1 \wedge D_B = 1$) and the disagreement region ($D_A = 0 \wedge D_B = 1$), practitioners can compute minimax
  performance guarantees without making ungrounded point imputations.

## Pitfalls

- **Evaluating Algorithmic Fairness Solely on Approvals:** Auditing credit scoring models for disparate impact using
  only approved borrowers produces severe survivor bias. An underwriting policy that systematically rejects qualified
  minority applicants will appear "fair" on the approved slice because default rates among the few approved minority
  applicants match white borrowers.
- **Assuming Unconfoundedness with Human Decision-Makers:** When human underwriters or judges make selection decisions
  ($D$), they observe unrecorded visual, verbal, and contextual cues (e.g., body language, unlogged references) not
  captured in tabular features $X$. Selection is confounded by unobservables ($Y \not\perp D \mid X$), invalidating
  naive propensity score reweighting.
- **Feedback Loops of Shrinking Portfolios:** A conservative model approves only ultra-low-risk applicants
  ($X \ge 750$). Retraining the model on this ultra-safe slice causes it to learn that risk variance is high below 750,
  prompting it to raise the threshold to 780 in the next iteration, steadily choking off business volume.
- **Adverse Selection in Exploration Buckets:** Announcing or leaking that an institution runs randomized exploration
  passes attracts opportunistic fraudsters who submit fraudulent applications to exploit random approvals.
