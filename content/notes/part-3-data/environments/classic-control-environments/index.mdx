<Definition>
A **classic control environment** is a simulated physical benchmark formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
$\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$ with a low-dimensional
continuous state space $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^D$ ($D \le 6$) and transitions
$\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{s}_{t+1} \mid \mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t)$ governed by deterministic, discretized Newtonian
equations of motion <Cite id="sutton2018" /> <Cite id="brockman2016" />:

$$
\mathbf{s}_{t+1} = \mathbf{s}_t + \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} f(\mathbf{s}(\tau), \mathbf{a}_t) \, d\tau.
$$

These environments provide standardized testbeds for rapid algorithmic verification, function approximation sanity
checks, and sample-efficiency analysis without GPU infrastructure.
</Definition>

## What these environments test

Classic control tasks isolate specific algorithmic capabilities in reinforcement learning:

1. **Continuous state representation**: Algorithms must generalize over uncountably infinite state vectors using linear
   tile coding, radial basis functions, or neural policy networks.
2. **Unstable non-linear equilibria**: Systems like the inverted pendulum have an open-loop unstable saddle point at the
   upright position, requiring tight closed-loop feedback to prevent divergent oscillations.
3. **Underactuation and energy accumulation**: In environments like Mountain Car and Acrobot, the actuator cannot
   overcome gravitational potential energy through brute force; the agent must discover non-greedy strategies that move
   away from the goal to accumulate momentum.
4. **Rapid turnaround debugging**: Because physics updates require only a few floating-point operations per step,
   algorithms can execute millions of interactions in seconds on a single CPU core.

## Catalogue

The canonical benchmark suite, originally popularized by Sutton and Barto <Cite id="sutton2018" /> and standardized in
OpenAI Gym <Cite id="brockman2016" /> and Gymnasium, includes four foundational systems:

### Cart-pole (`CartPole-v1`)
A pole is attached by an un-actuated joint to a cart moving along a frictionless horizontal track:

- **State space**: $\mathbf{s} = [x, \dot{x}, \theta, \dot{\theta}] \in \mathbb{R}^4$, capturing cart position, cart
  velocity, pole angle, and pole angular velocity.
- **Action space**: $\mathcal{A} = \{0, 1\}$, applying an instantaneous horizontal force of $-10\text{ N}$ (left) or
  $+10\text{ N}$ (right).
- **Reward and termination**: Reward is $+1$ for every step the pole remains upright. An episode terminates if the angle
  $|\theta| > 12^\circ$ ($0.209\text{ rad}$) or the cart leaves boundaries $|x| > 2.4$. The environment is considered
  solved when average return exceeds $475$ over $100$ consecutive episodes.

### Mountain car (`MountainCar-v0`)
An underpowered vehicle sits at the bottom of a sinusoidal valley described by $y = \sin(3x)$:

- **State space**: $\mathbf{s} = [x, \dot{x}] \in [-1.2, 0.6] \times [-0.07, 0.07]$.
- **Action space**: $\mathcal{A} = \{0, 1, 2\}$, representing left push, zero throttle, and right push.
- **The underactuation constraint**: Motor force ($0.001$) is strictly less than gravitational force
  ($\approx 0.0025 \cos(3x)$). The car cannot drive straight up the right hill to the goal ($x \ge 0.5$).
- **Reward structure**: A step cost of $-1$ is assessed at every time step until the goal is achieved. This sparse
  penalty tests an algorithm's capacity for directed exploration without reward shaping.

### Acrobot (`Acrobot-v1`)
A planar two-link, two-joint robot arm operating in a vertical plane under gravity, with only the second joint (elbow)
actuated:

- **State space**:
  $\mathbf{s} = [\cos\theta_1, \sin\theta_1, \cos\theta_2, \sin\theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2] \in \mathbb{R}^6$.
- **Action space**: $\mathcal{A} = \{-1, 0, +1\}$, applying torque to the actuated second joint.
- **Goal**: Swing the free tip of the second link above a horizontal line at height $y = 1.0$.

### Pendulum (`Pendulum-v1`)
A frictionless inverted pendulum with continuous actuation:

- **State space**: $\mathbf{s} = [\cos\theta, \sin\theta, \dot{\theta}] \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
- **Action space**: Continuous torque $u \in [-2.0, 2.0]$.
- **Reward function**: Penalizes angular deviation, angular velocity, and control effort:

  $$
  r_t = - \left( \theta_t^2 + 0.1 \dot{\theta}_t^2 + 0.001 u_t^2 \right), \quad \text{where } \theta_t \in [-\pi, \pi].
  $$

  Because starting states are initialized near the hanging bottom ($\theta \approx \pi$), the agent must execute
  non-linear swing-up maneuvers before balancing at the apex.

## Interfaces and versions

Modern benchmarks follow the standardized Gymnasium API:

```python
import gymnasium as gym

env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
obs, info = env.reset(seed=42)

for _ in range(500):
    action = env.action_space.sample()  # Policy action
    obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(action)
    if terminated or truncated:
        obs, info = env.reset()
```

- **Terminated versus truncated**:
  - `terminated=True`: The agent reached a natural absorbing MDP state (e.g. pole fell past threshold angle or goal
    reached).
  - `truncated=True`: An artificial external horizon limit was hit (e.g. $t = 500$ steps). Truncation must not be
    treated as a zero-value terminal state during bootstrap target calculation ($V(\mathbf{s}) \neq 0$).

## Known problems and limitations

1. **Trivial solution spaces**: Classical linear-quadratic regulators (LQR), PID loops, or direct policy search with 10
   random seeds can solve CartPole in milliseconds. Success on classic control provides negligible evidence that an
   algorithm will scale to high-dimensional problems.
2. **Deterministic integration overfitting**: Standard implementations use first-order semi-implicit Euler integration
   with fixed $\Delta t$. Learned policies often exploit integration inaccuracies or resonance frequencies of the
   numerical integrator rather than learning robust physical control.
3. **No perception bottleneck**: Because the true state vector $\mathbf{s}$ is directly exposed without sensor noise,
   partial observability, or visual occlusion, classic control does not test representation learning or state
   estimation.
