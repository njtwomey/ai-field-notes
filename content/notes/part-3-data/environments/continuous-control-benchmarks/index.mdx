<Definition>
A **continuous control benchmark** is a simulated physical system formulated as a Markov Decision Process
$\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma \rangle$ defined over continuous
vector spaces:

$$
\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^D, \quad \mathcal{A} = [-1, 1]^M,
$$

where transitions are governed by rigid-body articulated dynamics with contact friction solved by a physics engine such
as MuJoCo <Cite id="todorov2012" />. Control actions represent normalized motor torques applied to joint actuators.
</Definition>

## What these environments test

Continuous control tasks represent the primary testbed for deep actor-critic architectures (such as PPO
<Cite id="schulman2017" />, SAC <Cite id="haarnoja2018" />, and DDPG <Cite id="lillicrap2016" />):

1. **Continuous action spaces**: Discretizing an $M=8$ dimensional action space into $10$ bins per joint yields $10^8$
   discrete combinations, causing a catastrophic combinatorial explosion. Continuous algorithms must optimize
   parameterised probability distributions
   $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{a} \mid \mathbf{s}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{s}), \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{s}))$
   via policy gradient theorems.
2. **Contact discontinuities**: Rigid-body collisions, stick-slip friction, and impact forces produce non-smooth,
   non-differentiable dynamics, challenging gradient-based trajectory optimization and model-based RL.
3. **Multi-objective reward trade-offs**: Locomotion tasks require maximizing forward velocity while penalizing actuator
   energy expenditure and joint stress:

   $$
   r_t = v_{\text{forward}} - c_{\text{ctrl}} \|\mathbf{a}_t\|_2^2 - c_{\text{impact}} \|\mathbf{f}_{\text{contact}}\|_2^2 + r_{\text{alive}}.
   $$

   If the control penalty $c_{\text{ctrl}}$ is tuned too low, policies learn violently vibrating gaits; if set too high,
   the agent freezes to avoid actuation penalties.

## Catalogue

### MuJoCo locomotion tasks
Standardized originally by OpenAI Gym and maintained in Gymnasium, these articulated kinematic models test locomotion:

- **HalfCheetah-v4 ($D=17, M=6$)**: A 2D planar biped with eight rigid bodies and six actuated rotational joints.
  Because the cheetah cannot fall over (episodes never terminate prematurely), HalfCheetah evaluates pure optimization
  speed and policy stability without early termination traps.
- **Hopper-v4 ($D=11, M=3$)**: A single-legged planar monopod with foot, shin, and thigh segments. An episode terminates
  if the torso tilts ($|\theta| > 0.2\text{ rad}$) or height drops ($z < 0.7\text{ m}$). To survive, algorithms must
  learn rhythmic hopping with dynamic balance.
- **Walker2d-v4 ($D=17, M=6$)**: A planar biped with two articulated legs and feet. Evaluates coordinated alternating
  heel-strike gaits and avoidance of falling forward or backward.
- **Ant-v4 ($D=27, M=8$)**: A 3D quadruped with a central spherical torso and four two-segment legs. Involves 3D contact
  dynamics, yaw/pitch stabilization, and coordinated multi-leg crawl gaits.
- **Humanoid-v4 ($D=376, M=17$)**: A full 3D bipedal humanoid with 17 actuated joints. Highly underactuated and
  unstable; sample-efficient mastery requires millions of environment interactions.

### DeepMind Control Suite (DMC)
Developed by Tassa et al., the DeepMind Control Suite provides a standardized, modular alternative based on MuJoCo:
- Features unit-scaled rewards ($r_t \in [0, 1]$) with smooth bell-shaped reward profiles that eliminate heuristic
  reward clipping.
- Provides consistent observation formats, allowing benchmarks to run directly from physical states or high-dimensional
  camera pixel arrays ($64 \times 64$ RGB images) for visual reinforcement learning.

### Robotic manipulation suites
- **Meta-World**: 50 distinct robotic manipulation tasks using a simulated Sawyer arm (pushing, drawer opening, button
  pressing, peg insertion) designed to evaluate multi-task and meta-reinforcement learning.
- **Robomimic and Panda-Gym**: Franka Emika Panda robotic arms executing precision pick-and-place, insertion, and
  assembly tasks, often paired with human demonstration datasets.

## Interfaces and versions

In modern Gymnasium installations, continuous environments provide bounded box action spaces:

```python
import gymnasium as gym

env = gym.make("HalfCheetah-v4")
obs, info = env.reset(seed=42)

# Action space is continuous vector in [-1.0, 1.0]^6
assert isinstance(env.action_space, gym.spaces.Box)

for _ in range(1000):
    action = env.action_space.sample()  # Random torque vector
    obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(action)
    if terminated or truncated:
        obs, info = env.reset()
```

- **Frame skipping**: To simulate realistic physical latency, simulators apply each chosen torque vector across multiple
  internal physics steps (typically `frame_skip=5`, so an MDP step represents
  $5 \times 0.002\text{ s} = 0.01\text{ s}$).

## Known problems and limitations

1. **Reward hacking and unnatural gaits**: Without careful biomechanical regularization, policies exploit contact
   physics to discover bizarre gaits—such as dragging bodies upside-down or executing uncontrolled front flips—that
   achieve high forward velocity at the cost of physical plausibility.
2. **The sim-to-real gap**: Policies trained in pristine MuJoCo simulations routinely fail on physical robots due to
   unmodeled joint backlash, motor friction hysteresis, latency jitter, and uncalibrated mass distributions.
   Transferring policies requires extensive domain randomization of physical parameters.
3. **Observation dimension asymmetry**: Models that excel at state-based continuous control ($D \le 30$) degrade
   significantly when trained end-to-end from raw pixels, conflating control failure with representation learning
   bottlenecks.
