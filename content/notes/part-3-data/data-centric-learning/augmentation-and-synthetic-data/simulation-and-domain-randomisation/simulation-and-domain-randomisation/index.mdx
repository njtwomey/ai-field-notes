<Definition>
**Simulation and domain randomisation** encompass the methodologies for training machine learning models on synthetic
data generated by physics engines and graphic renderers. Because simulated data suffer from the **reality gap**
(systematic perceptual and physical discrepancies with physical environments), **domain randomisation** varies rendering
parameters, textures, lighting, and physical dynamics across wide distributions so that the real world appears to the
model as merely another variation of the simulation.
</Definition>

Collecting and manually annotating real-world data for robotics, autonomous driving, and surgical automation is
prohibitively slow, expensive, and dangerous. Physics engines generate virtually infinite, perfectly annotated training
datasets at zero marginal cost—producing pixel-perfect segmentation masks, dense 3D bounding boxes, depth maps, and
contact forces. However, models trained exclusively in pristine simulated environments routinely fail when deployed on
physical hardware. Bridging this simulation-to-reality (sim-to-real) gap requires engineering structured variance into
the data-generation pipeline <Cite id="tobin2017domain, ganin2016" />.

## Learning from simulation

Modern simulation platforms—such as MuJoCo, NVIDIA Isaac Sim, PyBullet, and CARLA—act as programmatic data factories:

1. **Perfect, zero-cost ground truth**: The simulator maintains the exact mathematical state of every mesh and rigid
   body. It emits dense 3D bounding boxes, surface normals, optical flow, joint torques, and semantic masks without
   human annotation error.
2. **Catastrophic safety testing**: Autonomous vehicles can be evaluated across millions of highway collisions,
   pedestrian near-misses, and sudden black-ice loss-of-traction events without endangering human life or destroying
   expensive hardware prototypes.
3. **Massive parallel throughput**: Distributed GPU-accelerated simulation environments (such as Isaac Gym) simulate
   tens of thousands of robot instances concurrently, collecting decades of interaction experience in a few hours of
   wall-clock time.

## The reality gap

Despite visual fidelity, models trained purely in simulation exhibit catastrophic performance degradation when evaluated
on physical hardware—a failure mode termed the **reality gap**:

| Reality Gap Dimension | Synthetic Simulation Abstraction | Physical Real-World Complexity | Diagnostic Hardware Symptom |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Visual Reality Gap** | Ray-traced shaders, simplified BRDFs, pristine sensors | Non-Lambertian reflectance, lens flare, rolling shutter noise | Visual detectors fail under unmodeled natural illumination |
| **Dynamical Reality Gap** | Rigid-body contact solvers, static Coulomb friction laws | Compliant soft materials, Stribeck friction, actuator delay | Robotic grippers drop objects or trigger high-frequency oscillations |

### The photorealism fallacy

A common engineering trap is attempting to bridge the reality gap by pouring resources into extreme photorealistic
rendering (e.g. real-time path tracing and complex CAD geometry). While photorealism improves qualitative human appeal,
it rarely solves sim-to-real transfer: deep neural networks exploit subtle, imperceptible rendering artifacts (such as
predictable shadow anti-aliasing or repeating texture procedurals) as shortcuts. When exposed to a real scene with
unexpected dust, sun glare, or scuffed paint, the photorealistic model collapses.

## Domain randomisation

Josh Tobin et al. introduced **Domain Randomisation (DR)** as a radical departure from photorealism
<Cite id="tobin2017domain" />. Instead of striving to make the simulator look exactly like the real world, domain
randomisation perturbs simulation parameters so broadly that the real world falls well within the support of the
training distribution:

$$
\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi} \sim P(\boldsymbol{\xi})} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi})} \left[ \ell(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}), y) \right] \right]
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is a vector of simulation parameters drawn from a wide prior distribution
$P(\boldsymbol{\xi})$, and $\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{\xi})$ denotes the data-generation pipeline conditioned on
$\boldsymbol{\xi}$.

| Randomisation Axis | Perturbed Simulation Parameters | Sampling Range / Distribution | Invariant Feature Enforced |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Visual Appearance** | Surface textures, albedo, specular material reflectances | Procedural stripes, checkerboards, random RGB color palettes | Decouples object identity from texture; forces reliance on 3D geometry |
| **Scene Illumination** | Ambient intensity, point source coordinates, chromatic tints | Uniform directional spheres, random specular intensity | Eliminates shadow and contrast shortcuts |
| **Sensor & Optics** | Camera pose, field of view, focal length, lens distortion, noise | Gaussian pose jitter, FOV scaling $\pm 15\%$, additive sensor noise | Inoculates policy against optical calibration drift |
| **Distractor Clutter** | Procedural 3D primitives (cubes, cylinders, spheres) | Random shapes scattered throughout the workspace | Enforces spatial attention on target objects amidst visual clutter |
| **Inertial Dynamics** | Link masses, center-of-mass offsets, moments of inertia | Multiplicative uniform scaling $m \sim \mathcal{U}(0.5 m_0, 1.5 m_0)$ | Prevents policy reliance on exact inertial matrices |
| **Contact Mechanics** | Coulomb friction coefficients, restitution (bounciness) | Friction $\mu \in [0.1, 1.5]$, restitution $e \in [0.0, 0.8]$ | Generalizes gripping and locomotion across varied contact surfaces |
| **Actuator & Latency** | Motor torque limits, PID gains, communication latency | Gain variation $\pm 20\%$, control latency $\Delta t \in [10\text{ ms}, 60\text{ ms}]$ | Prevents closed-loop instability caused by real-world communication lag |


By forcing the neural network to accomplish the task across hundreds of thousands of bizarre, visually implausible
synthetic variations, the network is unable to rely on background textures, lighting cues, or exact friction values. It
is forced to extract the **invariant physical and geometric structure** of the scene (e.g. object 3D geometry and
relative coordinate vectors), enabling zero-shot transfer to physical reality <Cite id="tobin2017domain" />.

## Simulation-to-real adaptation

While domain randomisation enables zero-shot transfer, combining simulated data with a small sample of unlabelled
real-world observations bridges remaining distributional residuals:

### 1. Domain-adversarial neural networks (DANN)

Yaroslav Ganin et al. formulated domain adaptation as a minimax game between a task predictor and a domain discriminator
<Cite id="ganin2016" />:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DANN}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_f, \boldsymbol{\theta}_y, \boldsymbol{\theta}_d) &= \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \ell_y(G_y(G_f(\mathbf{x}_i^s)), y_i^s) \\
&\quad - \lambda \left[ \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} \ell_d(G_d(G_f(\mathbf{x}_i^s)), 0) + \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{j=1}^{N_t} \ell_d(G_d(G_f(\mathbf{x}_j^t)), 1) \right]
\end{aligned}
$$

A **Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL)** placed between the feature extractor $G_f$ and the domain discriminator $G_d$
reverses the sign of backpropagated gradients ($-\lambda$). This forces the feature extractor to learn representations
that maximize task performance while rendering the source (synthetic) and target (real) domains indistinguishable.

### 2. System identification and meta-learning

In physical control, policies observe a temporal history of recent states and actions
$\mathbf{h}_t = (\mathbf{s}_{t-k}, \mathbf{a}_{t-k}, \dots, \mathbf{s}_t)$. A recurrent network or online Bayesian
module infers latent physical properties $\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_t$ (such as payload mass or floor friction),
dynamically adapting motor control commands to physical hardware variations in real time.

## Pitfalls

1. **Over-randomisation and policy conservatism**: Setting dynamics randomisation ranges so extreme (e.g. mass
   variations spanning three orders of magnitude) that physical control becomes impossible; policies collapse into
   overly cautious, frozen behaviors.
2. **Control latency omission**: Failing to simulate communication latency and discrete sensor sampling jitter; a policy
   trained with instantaneous control updates will violently oscillate on real actuators due to phase lag.
3. **Simulation engine physical bugs**: Exploiting numerical instability in contact solvers; policies in reinforcement
   learning easily discover unphysical artifacts (e.g. object interpenetration or infinite normal forces) that generate
   free energy in simulation but fail instantly in reality.
4. **Under-randomised static invariants**: Leaving a single incidental rendering variable constant across all runs (such
   as fixed table height, static shadow direction, or constant camera focal length); the network latches onto that
   invariant as a primary anchor, failing when the real environment deviates.
5. **Evaluating real performance exclusively on synthetic test splits**: Validating sim-to-real models on randomized
   simulated benchmarks; randomized validation suites measure robustness to the simulator's generative prior, not
   fidelity to physical real-world distributions.
