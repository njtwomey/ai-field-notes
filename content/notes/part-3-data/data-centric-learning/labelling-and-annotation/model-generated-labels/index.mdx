<Definition>
**Model-generated labels** are target annotations produced by machine learning models rather than human domain experts.
They encompass pseudo-labels derived from model confidence thresholds in semi-supervised learning, soft probability
distributions transferred via knowledge distillation, and zero- or few-shot annotations emitted by large language models
functioning as automated judges.
</Definition>

Human annotation is expensive, operationally bottlenecked, and slow. Substituting human annotators with foundation
models or high-capacity teacher networks reduces per-label costs by two to three orders of magnitude and accelerates
dataset expansion. However, model-generated labels do not represent independent ground truth: they reflect the teacher
model's inductive biases, hallucination modes, and uncalibrated confidence intervals. If deployed uncritically,
model-generated supervision risks circular error amplification, human auditor anchoring, and catastrophic autophagous
collapse <Cite id="zheng2023judge, hinton2015, lee2013pseudo" />.

## Annotation by large models

Large language models (LLMs) and multimodal foundation models are increasingly deployed as automated annotators—a
paradigm known as **LLM-as-a-judge** <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />. In tasks such as sentiment analysis, conversational
quality scoring, toxic content classification, and structured extraction, prompting a frontier model with detailed
guidelines can match crowdworker agreement rates at a fraction of the cost.

Despite high surface agreement, LLM annotators exhibit systematic, non-random error distributions that differ
fundamentally from human disagreement:

1. **Verbosity and fluency bias**: When evaluating open-ended text or comparing candidate outputs, LLMs systematically
   assign higher scores to longer, structurally elaborate, and polite responses, regardless of factual correctness or
   concise utility <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />.
2. **Self-enhancement bias**: Frontier models exhibit a measurable statistical preference for completions generated by
   their own model family or training architecture over alternative models of equivalent quality.
3. **Position bias**: In pairwise or multi-choice annotation prompts, LLMs favor items presented in the first position
   ($A$ over $B$) or items positioned near the prompt instructions, reflecting attention dispersion over long contexts.
4. **Tail edge-case hallucination**: In specialized domains (e.g., medical pathology, legal contracts, or obscure code
   APIs), LLM confidence does not track accuracy. The model produces grammatically polished, authoritative rationales
   for factually inverted labels.

## Distillation labels

Rather than generating discrete categorical tags, teacher models can supply rich, continuous target distributions to
train smaller, specialized student networks through **knowledge distillation** <Cite id="hinton2015" />.

### Soft targets and dark knowledge

Given a trained teacher network with logits $\mathbf{z}$, standard one-hot cross-entropy discards all information about
how the teacher views competing non-ground-truth classes. Geoffrey Hinton et al. introduced temperature scaling to
expose the teacher's latent semantic geometry—the **dark knowledge** <Cite id="hinton2015" />:

$$
q_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}
$$

where $T > 1$ is the distillation temperature. Higher temperatures smooth the probability distribution across
competitive alternatives (for instance, revealing that a misclassified image of an Alaskan Malamute shares high affinity
with a Siberian Husky, but zero affinity with a sports car). 

The student network is trained using a composite loss balancing hard ground-truth supervision
$\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{y}, \sigma(\mathbf{s}))$ and soft teacher distillation:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}} = (1 - \alpha) \, \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{y}, \sigma(\mathbf{s})) + \alpha T^2 \, D_{\text{KL}}(q_{\text{teacher}} \parallel q_{\text{student}})
$$

The factor $T^2$ compensates for the fact that the gradients produced by the soft cross-entropy scale as $1/T^2$ when
logits are divided by $T$, maintaining balanced gradient magnitudes across temperature settings.

### Pseudo-labelling and confirmation bias

In semi-supervised classification, **pseudo-labelling** converts high-confidence model predictions on unlabelled data
into hard training targets <Cite id="lee2013pseudo" />:

$$
\hat{y}_i = \arg\max_k p(y=k \mid \mathbf{x}_i), \quad \text{retained if } \max_k p(y=k \mid \mathbf{x}_i) \ge \tau
$$

While computationally straightforward, naive pseudo-labelling is vulnerable to **confirmation bias**
<Cite id="scudder1965" />. When a model makes a confident yet incorrect prediction on an unlabelled instance and that
instance is fed back into training, the network updates its weights to reinforce its own erroneous boundary. Over
successive training epochs, errors snowball, causing decision boundaries to drift permanently away from true class
manifolds. Mitigating this pathology requires strict dynamic thresholding, consistency regularization (e.g., FixMatch),
or model ensembles with cross-validation splits.

## Anchoring of human reviewers

To accelerate human throughput, modern annotation platforms frequently deploy **model-in-the-loop** workflows: a model
generates a pre-filled label draft, and a human annotator is tasked solely with reviewing, approving, or correcting the
draft.

While pre-annotation reduces annotation time by $30\text{--}60\%$, it introduces severe **cognitive anchoring bias**
<Cite id="tversky1974" />. Human decision-makers systematically anchor on initial reference values. When presented with
a pre-selected category or pre-drawn bounding box:

$$
\text{Reviewer Decision} \sim f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{input}}, \hat{y}_{\text{model}})
$$

Annotators switch cognitive modes from active evaluation to passive verification:

| Annotation Modality | Inputs Presented to Annotator | Cognitive Decision Process | Error Profile & Failure Mode |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Blank-Slate Annotation** | Raw instance $\mathbf{x}$ exclusively | Active cognitive synthesis and comprehensive inspection | High mental effort; uncorrelated independent human error distribution |
| **Pre-Filled Verification** | Raw instance $\mathbf{x}$ + candidate draft $\hat{y}_{\text{model}}$ | Passive heuristic verification ("rubber-stamping") | Vulnerable to cognitive anchoring; $25\text{--}45\%$ of subtle model errors accepted without correction |

Empirical audits reveal that when a drafting model makes a subtle error, human reviewers under pre-filled workflows fail
to detect the error in $25\text{--}45\%$ of cases, rubber-stamping incorrect annotations. Consequently, datasets
constructed via pre-filled review inherit the drafting model's systematic error fingerprints while masquerading as
"human-verified" gold standards.

## Audits against human samples

Deploying model-generated labels in production training corpora requires rigorous, statistically bounded audit
frameworks:

### 1. Blank-slate shadow audits

To measure true teacher accuracy, a randomly sampled evaluation cohort must be reviewed under **blank-slate
conditions**: human auditors must annotate raw examples with zero visibility into the model's suggested labels or
confidence scores <Cite id="tversky1974" />. Comparing blank-slate human annotations $y_{\text{human}}$ against model
predictions $\hat{y}_{\text{model}}$ reveals the true error rate $\epsilon_{\text{model}}$ free from anchoring
contamination.

### 2. Stratified acceptance testing

Frontier models perform unevenly across data subgroups. Audits must be stratified across critical operational slices:
- Common, high-density query heads vs rare long-tail queries.
- High-confidence predictions ($p > 0.95$) vs boundary instances ($0.60 < p < 0.80$).
- Sensitive demographic, linguistic, or regional sub-corpora.

If slice-level error exceeds an operational acceptance threshold $\epsilon_{\max}$, model-generated labels for that
entire slice must be quarantined and redirected to expert human queues.

### 3. Sequential drift monitoring

Foundation models hosted behind third-party APIs undergo periodic backend updates, weight retunings, and system prompt
changes. An automated pipeline that relies on an external LLM-as-a-judge must execute continuous Wald sequential
probability ratio tests (SPRT) on invariant benchmark test sets to detect subtle quality regressions or behavioral drift
before newly labelled batches contaminate the training lake.

## Pitfalls

1. **Autophagous model collapse**: Training successive generations of models on datasets dominated by model-generated
   outputs without human grounding degrades linguistic diversity, amplifies tail hallucinations, and eventually triggers
   mathematical variance collapse (the "model cannibalism" trap).
2. **Auditing with model-assisted tools**: Conducting quality assurance of model-generated labels using automated LLM
   evaluators creates a circular feedback loop where the judge shares the exact blind spots of the annotator.
3. **Overconfidence in tail distributions**: Assuming high softmax probabilities from deep neural networks indicate
   genuine epistemic certainty. Standard networks are notoriously miscalibrated on out-of-distribution inputs, assigning
   $>99\%$ confidence to nonsensical predictions.
4. **Unbalanced pseudo-label class quotas**: Fixed confidence thresholds $\tau$ disproportionately select instances from
   dominant head classes with high certainty, starving minority classes and severely exacerbating class imbalance in the
   pseudo-labelled set.
5. **Masking subtle human review fatigue**: Measuring annotator agreement only against pre-filled suggestions; as
   annotator fatigue increases over a shift, agreement with the model approaches $100\%$ not because the model is
   flawless, but because the reviewer has stopped scrutinizing predictions.
