<Definition>
**Coresets, data pruning, and dataset distillation** are subset-selection and data-compression methodologies that
condense large training corpora into compact subsets while bounding the loss in downstream model performance.
**Coresets** select weighted subsets that mathematically approximate the full-dataset loss or gradient; **data pruning**
discards redundant or non-informative examples without weighting; and **dataset distillation** synthesizes a small set
of artificial prototypes that optimize the training trajectory of neural networks.
</Definition>

Modern machine learning assumes that more data monotonically improves performance. However, training on web-scale
datasets incurs massive compute costs, carbon emissions, and latency. Furthermore, large datasets contain extreme
redundancy: millions of examples contribute near-zero gradient signal because the model learns them in early epochs.
Pruning or distilling datasets concentrates gradient energy on informative boundary points, accelerates hyperparameter
searches, and can even beat empirical neural scaling laws
<Cite id="mirzasoleiman2020craig, sorscher2022pruning, wang2018distillation" />.

## Coresets

A **coreset** is a weighted subset of a dataset that provably guarantees that any model trained on the subset
approximates the performance of a model trained on the complete dataset.

### Theoretical formulation

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{z}_1, \dots, \mathbf{z}_N\}$ denote a training dataset of $N$ instances, where
$\mathbf{z}_i = (\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$. Let $\ell(\mathbf{w}; \mathbf{z})$ be a loss function parameterized by weights
$\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}$. A subset $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{D}$ with non-negative sample weights
$\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \{\gamma_j \ge 0\}_{j \in \mathcal{S}}$ is an **$\epsilon$-coreset** if:

$$
\forall \mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{W}, \quad \left| \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} \ell(\mathbf{w}; \mathbf{z}_i) - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \gamma_j \ell(\mathbf{w}; \mathbf{z}_j) \right| \le \epsilon \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} \ell(\mathbf{w}; \mathbf{z}_i)
$$

Minimizing empirical risk on the weighted coreset $\mathcal{S}$ guarantees that the resulting parameters
$\mathbf{w}^*_{\mathcal{S}}$ achieve an $\epsilon$-close approximation to the full-dataset minimizer
$\mathbf{w}^*_{\mathcal{D}}$.

### Gradient-matching coresets (CRAIG)

For deep neural networks where global loss bounds across non-convex parameter spaces are intractable, Baharan
Mirzasoleiman, Jeff Bilmes, and Jure Leskovec introduced **Coresets for Accelerating Incremental Gradient Descent
(CRAIG)** <Cite id="mirzasoleiman2020craig" />.

Rather than approximating the scalar loss, CRAIG selects a weighted subset whose aggregated gradient approximates the
full-batch gradient at every step:

$$
\min_{\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{D}, \, \boldsymbol{\gamma} \ge 0} \left\| \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ell_i(\mathbf{w}) - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}} \gamma_j \nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ell_j(\mathbf{w}) \right\| \quad \text{s.t. } |\mathcal{S}| \le B
$$

CRAIG upper-bounds this gradient discrepancy by formulating it as a **submodular facility location problem**:
- Each training instance $\mathbf{z}_i$ is assigned to its nearest representative $\mathbf{z}_j \in \mathcal{S}$ in
  gradient space.
- The sample weight $\gamma_j$ is set to the cardinality of the cluster assigned to representative $j$.
- Because facility location is monotone submodular, a greedy algorithm achieves a $(1 - 1/e)$-approximation in
  near-linear time $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$.

CRAIG allows training deep convolutional and transformer models on $10\text{--}30\%$ of the data while preserving
$> 98\%$ of full-dataset accuracy and delivering up to $3\times\text{--}5\times$ end-to-end wall-clock speedups.

## Data pruning

Unlike coresets, which assign compensatory weights $\gamma_j$ to retained examples, **data pruning** discards
uninformative or redundant examples permanently without reweighting.

### Beating neural scaling laws

Standard neural scaling laws state that test error $\epsilon$ scales as a power law with dataset size $N$:

$$
\epsilon(N) \propto N^{-\alpha}
$$

Ben Sorscher et al. demonstrated that this power-law bottleneck is an artifact of uniform random sampling
<Cite id="sorscher2022pruning" />. In an unpruned corpus, as dataset size increases, the probability of sampling novel
informative examples decays exponentially: the model is repeatedly bombarded with easy, redundant instances.

| Sampling Strategy | Scaling Regime | Functional Behavior | Mechanism & Theoretical Bottleneck |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Uniform Random Sampling** | Power-law scaling | $\epsilon(N) \propto N^{-\alpha}$ | High-density redundant examples dominate large $N$; novel informative sampling probability decays rapidly |
| **Optimal Pruning Strategy** <Cite id="sorscher2022pruning" /> | Exponential scaling | $\epsilon(N) \propto \exp(-\beta N)$ | Discards uninformative points, concentrating capacity on decision boundaries and informative exemplars |

By developing an analytical statistical mechanics theory of perceptron learning, Sorscher et al. proved that under
optimal easy/hard data pruning, test error scales **exponentially** with dataset size:

$$
\epsilon(N) \propto \exp(-\beta N)
$$

### Pruning metrics

Identifying which examples to prune depends on the data regime:
1. **Error L2-Norm (EL2N)**: Mansheej Paul et al. established that the expected norm of the error vector
   $\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{y}\|_2]$ measured after just $t = 5\text{--}10$ epochs of early
   training predicts which examples are essential. Examples with lowest EL2N can be pruned aggressively.
2. **Self-supervised margin**: Computing the distance to the $k$-nearest cluster centroid in pre-trained foundation
   embedding space (e.g. CLIP or DINO). High-density points inside clusters are pruned as redundant.
3. **Data abundance regime vs data scarcity regime**:
   - In **data-abundant regimes**, the model has sufficient capacity to learn class manifolds; pruning easy,
     high-density examples accelerates convergence and prevents memorization of redundant features.
   - In **data-scarce regimes**, pruning hard outliers and boundary instances prevents overfitting on noise and
     stabilizes optimization.

## Dataset distillation

Tongzhou Wang et al. proposed **Dataset Distillation**: synthesizing a tiny set of synthetic data points
$\mathcal{S} = \{(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k, \tilde{y}_k)\}_{k=1}^K$ ($K \ll N$, e.g. 1, 10, or 50 images per class) such
that a network trained on $\mathcal{S}$ achieves performance comparable to training on the full corpus $\mathcal{D}$
<Cite id="wang2018distillation" />.

| Optimization Level | Target Variables | Objective Formulation | Operational Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Outer Loop (Meta)** | Synthetic dataset $\mathcal{S} = \{(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k, \tilde{y}_k)\}$ | $\min_{\mathcal{S}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^*(\mathcal{S}); \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}})$ | Optimizes synthetic pixel and feature representations by differentiating through the inner trajectory |
| **Inner Loop (Base)** | Network parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ | $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*(\mathcal{S}) = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}; \mathcal{S})$ | Fits model parameters directly on the condensed synthetic proxy set $\mathcal{S}$ |

### Algorithmic paradigms

1. **Gradient matching**: Matching the parameter gradients produced by synthetic samples to the gradients produced by
   real minibatches:
   $$
   \min_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta} \sim P(\boldsymbol{\theta})} \left[ 1 - \cos\left( \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}; \mathcal{S}), \, \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}; \mathcal{D}) \right) \right]
   $$
2. **Trajectory matching (MTT)**: Matching multi-step training trajectories of network weights initialized from
   identical checkpoints, ensuring synthetic gradients navigate parameter space along real-data trajectories.
3. **Distribution matching (DM)**: Matching intermediate feature representations between real batches and synthetic
   batches across hidden layers, avoiding expensive unrolled gradient loops.

Dataset distillation compresses complex visual datasets by factors of $100\times\text{--}1000\times$. For instance,
50,000 CIFAR-10 training images are condensed into just 100 synthetic prototypes (10 per class) while preserving
$> 65\%$ top-1 classification accuracy <Cite id="wang2018distillation" />.

## Pitfalls

1. **Coreset representation drift**: Coreset weights and selection sets computed on early random representations become
   stale as deep layers develop specialized abstract features, requiring periodic re-computation of facility location
   centers.
2. **Disproportionate minority slice purging**: Pruning data based solely on cluster density or gradient norms
   disproportionately eliminates small, high-stakes demographic or clinical sub-slices whose natural prevalence is low.
3. **Architectural lock-in of distilled prototypes**: Synthetic distillation exemplars optimized for a ResNet
   architecture often fail to generalize when used to train a Vision Transformer or Multi-Layer Perceptron.
4. **Metric calculation overhead**: Calculating expensive pruning scores (e.g. running multiple ensemble models for EL2N
   or full pairwise kernel matrices for facility location) can consume more FLOPs than standard full-dataset training if
   not amortized across repeated hyperparameter searches.
5. **Overfitting on distilled pixels**: Distilled synthetic images often contain high-frequency adversarial patterns
   that trick the specific loss function into rapid convergence without imparting robust, invariant semantic features.
