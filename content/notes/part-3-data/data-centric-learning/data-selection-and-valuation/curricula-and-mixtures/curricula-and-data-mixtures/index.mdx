<Definition>
**Curricula and data mixtures** govern the sequence, relative proportions, and volume of training data presented to a
model throughout optimization. **Curriculum learning** orders examples from simple to complex; **self-paced learning**
automates this progression using the model's instantaneous loss; and **data mixtures** establish sampling distributions
across heterogeneous domains under compute-optimal scaling laws.
</Definition>

Stochastic gradient descent is inherently path-dependent: the order in which data is processed, the relative exposure to
different knowledge domains, and the repetition frequency of high-quality sources fundamentally shape the topology of
the learned representations. Treating training datasets as homogeneous bags of tokens overlooks massive opportunities to
stabilize non-convex optimization, break out of poor local minima, and maximize the efficiency of finite compute budgets
<Cite id="bengio2009curriculum, kumar2010selfpaced, hoffmann2022" />.

## Curriculum learning

Inspired by biological pedagogy, Yoshua Bengio et al. formalized **Curriculum Learning** as training a model on
progressively more challenging distributions <Cite id="bengio2009curriculum" />.

Mathematically, a curriculum is a sequence of training distributions $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_T = P_{\text{target}}$ over
inputs $\mathbf{x}$, defined by reweighting functions $W_t(\mathbf{x})$:

$$
P_t(\mathbf{x}) \propto W_t(\mathbf{x}) \, P_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x}), \quad 0 \le W_t(\mathbf{x}) \le W_{t+1}(\mathbf{x}) \le 1
$$

Bengio et al. established that curriculum learning acts as a **continuation method** for non-convex optimization:
1. Early in training, $P_1$ smooths the objective landscape, replacing chaotic non-convex valleys with broader,
   well-behaved convex approximations centered on canonical features.
2. As $t$ advances, the distribution smoothly morphs into the true target landscape $P_{\text{target}}$, guiding
   parameter weights into superior basins of attraction that random initialization rarely reaches.

### Pacing functions

A **pacing function** $g(t) \in [\lambda_0, 1]$ dictates the proportion of the easiest training data available to the
model at training step $t$:

| Pacing Schedule | Mathematical Formulation $g(t)$ | Growth Profile | Optimization Dynamics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Root / Concave** | $\sqrt{\frac{t}{T_{\text{pacing}}}}$ | Rapid initial admission, slowing near threshold | Rapidly admits intermediate examples while moderating late-stage exposure to extreme boundaries |
| **Linear** | $\lambda_0 + (1 - \lambda_0) \frac{t}{T_{\text{pacing}}}$ | Constant expansion rate $\frac{1 - \lambda_0}{T_{\text{pacing}}}$ | Uniform rate of data introduction, maintaining predictable batch diversity |
| **Step-Wise** | $\lambda_k \cdot \mathbb{I}(t_k \le t < t_{k+1})$ | Piecewise discrete steps | Holds data complexity fixed until loss plateaus, preventing churn during critical convergence |

## Self-paced learning

Pre-defined curricula rely on human heuristics to define "easiness"—such as sentence length in NLP or bounding box size
in computer vision. These proxies frequently fail because superficial simplicity does not guarantee low optimization
difficulty.

M. Pawan Kumar, Benjamin Packer, and Daphne Koller introduced **Self-Paced Learning (SPL)**, where the model dynamically
selects its own curriculum using its instantaneous loss <Cite id="kumar2010selfpaced" />.

SPL augments the empirical risk objective with binary selection variables
$\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \dots, v_N)^\top \in \{0, 1\}^N$ and a pacing threshold $\lambda$:

$$
\min_{\mathbf{w}, \, \mathbf{v} \in \{0, 1\}^N} \sum_{i=1}^N v_i \, \ell(\mathbf{w}; \mathbf{x}_i, y_i) - \lambda \sum_{i=1}^N v_i
$$

For fixed parameters $\mathbf{w}$, the optimal sample weight $v_i^*$ has a closed-form threshold solution:

$$
v_i^* = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \ell(\mathbf{w}; \mathbf{x}_i, y_i) < \lambda \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

When $\lambda$ is small, only instances with loss below $\lambda$ are incorporated ($v_i = 1$), ignoring hard instances
($v_i = 0$). By gradually increasing $\lambda$ throughout optimization, the model autonomously incorporates
progressively harder instances at a pace determined by its current representational maturity.

## Data mixtures

In modern foundation model pre-training, datasets are composed of diverse, heterogeneous domains: broad web crawls (e.g.
Common Crawl), programming code repositories, peer-reviewed scientific literature, mathematical texts, and structured
encyclopedias.

The **data mixture** is defined by a probability simplex $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$ such that
$\sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k = 1$, governing the sampling probability of each domain during batch construction.

### Temperature scaling of domain sizes

If a training pipeline samples domains strictly proportional to their raw token counts, massive web scrapes
($80\text{--}90\%$ of the corpus) completely overwhelm high-value, low-volume sources such as mathematical proofs
($1\text{--}2\%$).

To balance exposure without discarding natural volume differences, mixtures apply **temperature scaling** to raw domain
token sizes $D_k$:

$$
\alpha_k = \frac{D_k^{1 / T}}{\sum_{j=1}^K D_j^{1 / T}}
$$

| Temperature Setting ($T$) | Sampling Behavior | Operational Fit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$T = 1.0$ (Proportional)** | Proportional to raw token count ($D_k$) | Natural crawl; completely swamps math, reasoning, and code |
| **$T = 2.0\text{--}3.0$ (Balanced)** | Smoothly boosts minority high-utility domains | Standard industry practice for frontier LLMs (LLaMA, Gemma) |
| **$T \to \infty$ (Uniform)** | Equal sampling probability $\alpha_k = 1/K$ across all domains | Causes catastrophic over-repetition of tiny specialized datasets |

## Repetition and epochs

When high-quality tokens (such as formal mathematics or verified code) are exhausted, should training stop, or should
high-quality domains be repeated over multiple epochs?

Niklas Muennighoff et al. demonstrated in extensive empirical studies that training over multi-epoch repetitions remains
highly effective up to a critical saturation threshold:

$$
\text{Effective Token Efficiency} = f(\text{Repetition Count } R)
$$

- **$1\text{--}4$ Repetitions**: Near-optimal data efficiency. Re-ingesting verified reasoning data up to 4 times
  delivers $> 90\%$ of the downstream performance gains achievable with completely novel tokens.
- **$> 5\text{--}8$ Repetitions**: Diminishing returns accelerate into severe capacity saturation. The model memorizes
  exact token orderings, triggering sharp spikes in validation perplexity and degrading out-of-distribution transfer.

## Data scaling laws

The relationship between compute, dataset size, and parameter count is codified by neural scaling laws
<Cite id="kaplan2020, hoffmann2022" />.

Jordan Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind Chinchilla) resolved early architectural misallocations by proving that for a
compute-optimal pre-training run with budget $C \approx 6ND$ FLOPs, model parameters $N$ and training tokens $D$ should
scale in **equal proportion** <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />:

$$
N \propto C^{a}, \quad D \propto C^{b}, \quad a \approx 0.5, \, b \approx 0.5
$$

The resulting compute-optimal allocation dictates approximately **20 tokens per model parameter** (e.g. a 70-billion
parameter model is compute-optimal at 1.4 trillion tokens).

| Scaling Regime | Optimization Goal | Token-to-Parameter Ratio | Representative Architectures |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Compute-Optimal (Chinchilla)** <Cite id="hoffmann2022" /> | Minimizes *training compute* for a given pre-training loss | $\approx 20$ tokens per parameter | Chinchilla 70B trained on 1.4T tokens |
| **Inference-Optimal / Over-Trained** <Cite id="touvron2023b" /> | Minimizes *lifetime serving cost* by incurring extra pre-training FLOPs | $100\text{--}300+$ tokens per parameter | LLaMA-3 8B trained on 15T tokens |

In customer-facing production systems, the total inference FLOPs over the lifetime of a deployed model dwarfs initial
training FLOPs by orders of magnitude. Consequently, modern architectures deliberately over-train compact models far
past the Chinchilla boundary to amortize downstream latency and memory footprints.

## Pitfalls

1. **Abrupt curriculum cutoffs causing catastrophic forgetting**: Removing simple canonical examples completely once
   complex boundary examples are unlocked; neural networks rapidly forget basic syntax and low-level perceptual
   invariants unless anchored by continuous easy replay.
2. **Excessive mixture temperature causing repetition collapse**: Setting $T > 4$ on small specialized datasets ($< 1$
   billion tokens) forces the model to repeat identical documents hundreds of times, causing severe memorization and
   gradient collapse.
3. **Superficial difficulty proxies**: Designing curricula using heuristic metrics (e.g. sentence length or token count)
   that correlate with document format rather than cognitive or semantic reasoning complexity.
4. **Late-stage mixture destabilization**: Altering domain mixture ratios during the final $10\%$ of pre-training
   without cosine learning rate annealing, causing destructive representation shock.
5. **Evaluating scaling laws on mismatched tokenizers**: Comparing token scaling laws across models with differing
   byte-pair encoding (BPE) vocabulary sizes; a model with a 128k vocabulary encodes text in $30\%$ fewer tokens than a
   32k vocabulary, skewing raw token efficiency comparisons.
