<Definition>
**Data valuation and influence** quantify the marginal contribution of individual training instances to a model's
downstream generalization performance. While **leave-one-out (LOO)** measures the performance delta when an instance is
removed, **Data Shapley** computes the unique axiomatic cooperative contribution across all possible training subsets,
and **influence functions** compute first-order Taylor approximations via the inverse empirical Hessian without
retraining.
</Definition>

In large-scale machine learning, data points are not created equal. A small fraction of clean, representative instances
anchor decision boundaries; a massive middle cohort of canonical points provides redundant confirmation; and a toxic
tail of mislabeled instances, sensor artifacts, or adversarial poisoning actively degrades generalization. Assigning
principled mathematical valuations to training records transforms data management from indiscriminate ingestion into
quantitative auditing, automated noise cleaning, and equitable data monetization
<Cite id="ghorbani2019shapley, koh2017, cook1977" />.

## Leave-one-out values

The most intuitive measure of an example's worth is its **Leave-One-Out (LOO)** score: the difference in validation
utility when instance $i$ is removed from the training set.

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{z_1, \dots, z_N\}$ denote the training dataset, where $z_i = (\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$. Let $U(S)$ be a
utility function (such as validation accuracy or negative validation loss) evaluated on a model trained on subset
$S \subseteq \mathcal{D}$. The LOO score of instance $i$ is:

$$
\Delta_{\text{LOO}}(i) = U(\mathcal{D}) - U(\mathcal{D} \setminus \{i\})
$$

While conceptually straightforward, LOO suffers from two crippling engineering deficiencies:

1. **Computational impossibility**: Computing exact LOO scores across a corpus of $N$ instances requires retraining $N$
   separate models from scratch. For deep neural networks where a single training run requires hours or days on GPU
   clusters, computing $\Delta_{\text{LOO}}$ for $N = 10^5\text{--}10^8$ instances is completely intractable.
2. **The redundancy blind spot**: In real-world datasets, information is frequently duplicated. If a training set
   contains two identical copies of an essential training example ($z_A$ and $z_B$), removing $z_A$ leaves $z_B$ in the
   training set, so the model learns identical weights:
   $$
   U(\mathcal{D} \setminus \{z_A\}) = U(\mathcal{D}) \implies \Delta_{\text{LOO}}(z_A) = 0
   $$
   LOO assigns zero value to both instances, completely obscuring the fact that the underlying information is vital to
   model performance.

## Data Shapley

To resolve the redundancy failure of LOO, Amirata Ghorbani and James Zou adapted cooperative game theory to machine
learning through **Data Shapley** <Cite id="ghorbani2019shapley" />.

In cooperative game theory (Lloyd Shapley, 1953), players form coalitions to generate total payout $U(\mathcal{D})$. The
Shapley value is the **unique** payout assignment satisfying four fundamental fairness axioms:
- **Efficiency**: $\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}} \phi_i = U(\mathcal{D}) - U(\emptyset)$.
- **Symmetry**: If $U(S \cup \{i\}) = U(S \cup \{j\})$ for all $S \subseteq \mathcal{D} \setminus \{i, j\}$, then
  $\phi_i = \phi_j$.
- **Null player**: If $U(S \cup \{i\}) = U(S)$ for all $S \subseteq \mathcal{D} \setminus \{i\}$, then $\phi_i = 0$.
- **Additivity**: For independent utilities $U_1$ and $U_2$, $\phi_i(U_1 + U_2) = \phi_i(U_1) + \phi_i(U_2)$.

### The Shapley formulation

The Data Shapley value $\phi_i$ measures the average marginal contribution of instance $i$ across all possible subsets:

$$
\phi_i = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{S \subseteq \mathcal{D} \setminus \{i\}} \frac{1}{\binom{N-1}{|S|}} \left[ U(S \cup \{i\}) - U(S) \right]
$$

When two identical copies of an essential sample exist, Data Shapley splits the credit equally between them
($\phi_{z_A} = \phi_{z_B} = \frac{1}{2} U_{\text{unique}}$), avoiding the LOO collapse.

| Valuation Tier | Shapley Signature ($\phi_i$) | Characteristic Data Profile | Prescribed Operational Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **High-Value Anchors** | $\phi_i \gg 0$ | Rare class exemplars, subtle decision boundary prototypes, clean signals | Protect from deduplication; prioritize for human verification |
| **Redundant Core** | $\phi_i \approx 0$ | Standard, easily classified examples with high overlap | Prune or subsample safely without degrading test accuracy |
| **Toxic Tail** | $\phi_i \ll 0$ | Severe label noise, corrupted inputs, adversarial artifacts | Intercept and quarantine; inspect for label flipping or pipeline errors |

### Truncated Monte Carlo Shapley (TMC-Shapley)

Evaluating $2^N$ subsets is exponential. Ghorbani and Zou introduced **TMC-Shapley**, which samples random permutations
$\pi \in \Pi(N)$ of the dataset:
1. Training begins with an empty set and adds examples in permutation order $\pi = (\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots)$.
2. At each step, record the marginal utility change $\Delta U_i = U(S \cup \{i\}) - U(S)$.
3. **Truncation**: Once the performance of coalition $S$ reaches within $\epsilon$ of the full dataset utility
   $U(\mathcal{D})$, further marginal additions contribute zero, and evaluation for that permutation halts.

Averaging marginal contributions across several thousand truncated permutations provides an unbiased, low-variance
estimate of $\phi_i$.

## Influence functions

Pang Wei Koh and Percy Liang introduced **influence functions** to deep learning as an efficient, calculus-based
alternative to retraining <Cite id="koh2017" />, drawing on classical robust statistics <Cite id="cook1977" />.

Rather than removing example $z_i$, consider upweighting its loss by an infinitesimal perturbation $\epsilon$:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\epsilon, z_i} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \ell(z_j; \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \epsilon \, \ell(z_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

Using a second-order Taylor expansion around the empirical risk minimizer $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, the parameter
derivative with respect to $\epsilon$ is given by:

$$
\left. \frac{d \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\epsilon, z_i}}{d \epsilon} \right|_{\epsilon=0} = -H_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{-1} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell(z_i; \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
$$

where
$H_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^2 \ell(z_j; \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$
is the empirical Hessian matrix.

### Test-point influence attribution

Setting $\epsilon = -1/N$ approximates the effect of removing example $z_i$ entirely. Using the chain rule, the
predicted change in loss on a specific test point $z_{\text{test}} = (\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}, y_{\text{test}})$ is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{I}_{\text{up, loss}}(z_i, z_{\text{test}}) &= \left. \frac{d \ell(z_{\text{test}}; \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\epsilon, z_i})}{d \epsilon} \right|_{\epsilon=0} \\
&= -\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell(z_{\text{test}}; \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^\top H_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{-1} \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell(z_i; \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
\end{aligned}
$$

- **Negative influence ($\mathcal{I} < 0$)**: Training sample $z_i$ *helped* the model predict correctly on
  $z_{\text{test}}$ (removing $z_i$ increases test loss).
- **Positive influence ($\mathcal{I} > 0$)**: Training sample $z_i$ *hurt* the model on $z_{\text{test}}$ (it acted as a
  confusing outlier or label error).

Computing $H^{-1}$ directly for a network with $P$ parameters requires inverting a $P \times P$ matrix
($\mathcal{O}(P^3)$). Koh and Liang bypass this using the **Linear Time Stochastic Second-Order Algorithm (LiSSA)**,
which computes Inverse Hessian-Vector Products (iHVP) $H^{-1} \mathbf{v}$ via stochastic Taylor series sampling in
$\mathcal{O}(N P)$ time.

## Uses of data values

1. **Automated label noise cleaning**: Sorting training records by Data Shapley $\phi_i$ concentrates label errors in
   the bottom $5\text{--}10\%$ negative tail. Pruning or relabeling these instances monotonically increases validation
   accuracy <Cite id="ghorbani2019shapley" />.
2. **Data marketplaces and fair revenue sharing**: When commercial models are trained on federated or user-contributed
   datasets (e.g. medical imaging consortia), Shapley efficiency guarantees an mathematically unique, ungameable revenue
   dividend proportional to each institution's true contribution.
3. **Debugging model predictions and hallucinations**: When an LLM or classifier outputs a catastrophic mistake on a
   test query $z_{\text{test}}$, querying the top harmful training examples
   ($\arg\max_i \mathcal{I}_{\text{loss}}(z_i, z_{\text{test}})$) identifies the exact training documents responsible
   for the blunder.

## Pitfalls

1. **Hessian non-convexity in deep networks**: Deep neural network loss landscapes are non-convex, with numerous
   negative or near-zero eigenvalues. Naive Hessian inversion leads to numerical explosion; influence functions require
   regularized damping $(H + \lambda I)^{-1}$.
2. **Stochastic permutation variance in Shapley estimation**: Estimating Data Shapley via Monte Carlo sampling produces
   noisy valuations for boundary samples unless averaged over thousands of permutations.
3. **Path-dependent SGD violations**: Influence functions assume optimization reaches a stationary point of a convex
   surrogate. In deep models trained with SGD, learning rate annealing, and dropout, parameter trajectories depend
   heavily on batch order, introducing discrepancies between predicted influence and actual LOO retraining.
4. **Adversarial data valuation gaming**: In commercial data sharing, participants who understand the valuation
   algorithm can craft synthetic points with inflated gradient norms to capture excessive Shapley payout without
   providing genuine semantic value.
