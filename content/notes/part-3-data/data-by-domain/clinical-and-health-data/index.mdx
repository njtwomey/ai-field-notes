<Definition>
**Clinical and health data** comprises the longitudinal observational records generated during medical care
delivery—including Electronic Health Records (EHR), clinical trial registries, laboratory tests, prescription orders,
medical imaging (DICOM), and high-frequency physiological telemetry <Cite id="johnson2016mimic" />. Rather than being
designed for scientific experimentation, clinical datasets are artifacts of billing codes, clinical workflows, and
administrative encounters, requiring careful management of irregular sampling, phenotyping uncertainty, and
epidemiological biases <Cite id="rajkomar2018" />.
</Definition>

Machine learning models applied to clinical data promise early diagnosis, personalized treatment planning, and hospital
resource optimization. However, observational health records are fundamentally shaped by the healthcare delivery system
itself: the timing, presence, and absence of clinical measurements convey diagnostic signals independent of the measured
physiological values.

## Records and coding systems

Clinical encounters are captured across structured transactional databases and unstructured clinician prose, organized
through standardized medical terminologies:

- **Diagnostic codes (ICD-9 / ICD-10 / ICD-11):** The International Classification of Diseases provides hierarchical
  alphanumeric codes for patient diagnoses, injuries, and causes of death. Originally created for epidemiological
  tracking and insurance reimbursement, ICD codes represent billing justifications rather than complete clinical
  pathology.
- **Procedural codes (CPT / HCPCS):** Current Procedural Terminology standardizes billable medical services, surgical
  procedures, and diagnostic scans performed during an encounter.
- **Clinical ontologies (SNOMED-CT):** Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine provides a comprehensive, poly-hierarchical
  concept network linking anatomy, symptoms, etiologies, and surgical findings across international healthcare systems.
- **Laboratory tests (LOINC):** Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes standardizes laboratory measurements
  (e.g., serum potassium, arterial pH, hemoglobin A1c) and clinical observations.
- **Medications (RxNorm / ATC):** Normalizes drug brand names, active chemical ingredients, dosage forms, and routes of
  administration into unified concept unique identifiers (CUIs).
- **Unstructured text:** Nursing progress notes, radiology reports, and physician discharge summaries contain dense
  clinical context, negated findings (e.g., "patient denies chest pain"), and family medical history requiring
  specialized clinical natural language processing.

## Irregular visits and measurements

Unlike standard time-series data sampled at fixed intervals, clinical data arrives at highly irregular, asynchronous
timestamps driven by patient healthcare-seeking behavior and clinical urgency:

### The informative observation process

In health informatics, the sampling process itself is not independent of patient health status:

- **Non-random missingness:** In clinical testing, data is rarely Missing Completely at Random (MCAR). Clinicians do not
  order arterial blood gas, cardiac troponin, or head CT scans randomly; tests are ordered precisely when a clinician
  suspects deterioration.
- **Presence as a feature:** The mere fact that a specific laboratory test was ordered—and the frequency with which it
  was repeated over the past 24 hours—is frequently a stronger statistical predictor of acute inpatient mortality or ICU
  transfer than the actual numerical test result <Cite id="rajkomar2018" />.
- **Masked stability:** Healthy patients may have zero laboratory tests over years. Treating the absence of a test as a
  missing value to be imputed with global population means corrupts the clean baseline distribution of healthy cohorts.

## Cohort definition

Translating raw electronic health records into an analysis-ready dataset requires defining a strict study cohort:

| Cohort Phase | Relative Window | Primary Operational Objective |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Lookback / Baseline** | $[-365, 0]$ days | Ascertain incident status and compute baseline risk profiles (e.g., Charlson Comorbidity Index) |
| **Index Date ($t_0$)** | Anchor $t = 0$ | Adjudicate qualifying clinical event or exposure (e.g., initial drug prescription, ICU admission) |
| **Prediction Horizon** | $[0, +30]$ days | Monitor forward clinical endpoint without leakage (e.g., 30-day all-cause readmission, mortality) |

1. **Index date ($t_0$):** The definitive temporal origin anchoring the patient's timeline (e.g., the date of initial
   type 2 diabetes diagnosis or emergency department presentation).
2. **Lookback / Wash-out window:** A mandatory historical observation window prior to $t_0$ (e.g., 365 days of
   continuous healthcare enrollment) used to ascertain that an exposure or disease onset is genuinely incident rather
   than prevalent, and to compute baseline risk scores.
3. **Prediction horizon:** The forward evaluation window (e.g., 30-day all-cause hospital readmission or 1-year
   cardiovascular event) across which the clinical endpoint is monitored.

## Labels from records

In clinical informatics, clean binary outcome labels rarely exist as direct database columns. Instead, researchers
engineer **computable phenotypes**:

- **Phenotype definition:** A computable phenotype is a programmatic rule set or statistical classifier that identifies
  patients with a specific disease condition by combining diagnosis codes, laboratory thresholds, prescription claims,
  and clinical note keywords. For example, identifying type 2 diabetes may require:
  $$
  \ge 2 \text{ outpatient ICD codes} \quad \text{OR} \quad \text{HbA1c} \ge 6.5\% \quad \text{OR} \quad \text{active Metformin script}
  $$
- **Upcoding and billing bias:** Because hospital reimbursements scale with patient illness severity (via Diagnosis
  Related Groups, DRGs), billing records frequently exhibit upcoding—recording borderline secondary complications to
  maximize insurance reimbursement. Treating billing diagnoses as unassailable ground truth introduces diagnostic
  inflation into machine learning training targets.

## Immortal time and other biases

Observational health studies are vulnerable to systematic structural biases that can invert clinical conclusions:

- **Immortal time bias:** Occurs when cohort membership or treatment exposure requires surviving or remaining event-free
  for a period following the index date $t_0$. For example, defining the "treated group" as patients who received
  coronary revascularization surgery within 90 days of a heart attack creates an **immortal window**: any patient who
  died within the first 14 days is automatically excluded from the treated group and allocated to the control group.
  This artifactually deflates the apparent mortality hazard ratio of the surgical procedure. Correcting immortal time
  bias requires **landmark analysis** (evaluating only patients surviving to a fixed milestone) or time-dependent Cox
  proportional hazard modeling.
- **Confounding by indication:** In clinical practice, physicians prescribe aggressive therapies specifically to the
  sickest patients. Naive supervised models will correlate the medication with elevated mortality, mistaking the
  underlying disease severity for drug toxicity.
- **Surveillance / Berkson's bias:** Patients who interact with the hospital system frequently undergo more routine
  screening, increasing their probability of being diagnosed with subclinical conditions compared to equally sick
  patients who lack regular healthcare access.

## Privacy and access

Healthcare data is governed by stringent legal protections:

- **Regulatory frameworks:** The US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and EU General Data
  Protection Regulation (GDPR) govern patient records.
- **De-identification mechanisms:**
  - **HIPAA Safe Harbor:** Mandates the removal of 18 specific identifiers—including patient names, geographic divisions
    smaller than a state, all dates directly related to an individual (except year), telephone numbers, and ages over 89.
  - **Expert determination:** Statistical methodologies demonstrating that the risk of re-identification via linkage
    attacks is vanishingly small.
- **Open critical care benchmarks:** Benchmark databases—most prominently the Medical Information Mart for Intensive
  Care (MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV <Cite id="johnson2016mimic" />) developed by MIT and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical
  Center—provide freely accessible, de-identified clinical telemetry, nursing notes, and vital signs for reproducible
  critical care machine learning research.

## Pitfalls

- **Naive numerical forward-fill across distant encounters:** Propagating an outpatient laboratory value (e.g. serum
  sodium) forward by months, ignoring physiological half-lives and temporal decay.
- **Immortal time lookahead contamination:** Defining patient exposures using information that post-dates the index date
  $t_0$, inadvertently injecting future survival knowledge into baseline predictors.
- **Hospital site and device shortcuts:** Allowing computer vision or telemetry models to latch onto hospital-specific
  artifacts—such as radiographic marker typography, ICU bed monitor manufacturer protocols, or radiologist signature
  styles—as predictive shortcuts for patient acuity.
- **Ignoring differential access barriers:** Training diagnostic risk scores on populations with disparities in
  insurance coverage, where lower diagnostic rates reflect socioeconomic access barriers rather than true physiological
  health.
