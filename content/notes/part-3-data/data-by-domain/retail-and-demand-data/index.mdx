<Definition>
Retail data captures point-of-sale transactions, inventory positions, and promotional calendars across complex product
hierarchies. Unlike pure time series with direct observation of target variables, retail transaction logs record *sales
volume* rather than *consumer demand*, leading to systematic observation censoring during stock-outs, distortion across
promotional cycles, and extreme sparsity across product catalogs.
</Definition>

Retail forecasting underpins inventory replenishment, pricing optimization, and logistics across brick-and-mortar stores
and fulfillment networks <Cite id="makridakis2022" />. However, treating historical checkout scans as ground-truth
consumer demand introduces fatal feedback loops: when inventory drops to zero, recorded sales fall to zero regardless of
how many customers intended to purchase the item.

## Censored demand

The central statistical challenge in retail telemetry is the wedge between latent demand $D_t$ and observed sales $Y_t$.
At any point in time $t$ for item $i$, observed sales are bounded by available on-hand inventory $I_t$:

$$
Y_{it} = \min(D_{it}, I_{it})
$$

When inventory is strictly positive ($I_{it} > D_{it}$), observed sales reflect true demand: $Y_{it} = D_{it}$. But when
stock is exhausted ($I_{it} = 0$ or $I_{it} < D_{it}$), the observation is right-censored.

Treating stock-out periods as days of "zero demand" creates a self-fulfilling death spiral:
1. An item runs out of stock, recording 0 sales for five consecutive days.
2. A standard supervised model (e.g., gradient boosted trees or autoregressive neural networks) incorporates these
   zero-valued labels into training.
3. The model forecasts reduced demand for the subsequent period.
4. Supply chain planning allocates fewer replenishment units, inducing earlier stock-outs in the next cycle.

To recover unbiased demand estimates, industrial pipelines apply censoring-aware formulations:
- **Tobit Models and Censored Regression:** Modeling latent demand $D_t^* \sim \mathcal{N}(X_t \beta, \sigma^2)$ where
  only $Y_t = \min(D_t^*, I_t)$ is observed. The likelihood explicitly integrates over the tail probability
  $P(D_t^* \ge I_t)$ whenever stock-outs occur.
- **Expectation-Maximization (EM) Imputation:** Iteratively imputing unobserved demand during stock-out intervals
  conditioned on store traffic, day-of-week seasonality, and peer-item velocities.
- **Intermittent Demand Models:** For slow-moving SKUs where demand occurs sporadically with long zero-demand gaps,
  Croston's decomposition models the inter-arrival time $p$ between non-zero demand events separately from the
  transaction size $z$ <Cite id="croston1972" />.

## Promotions

Promotions (temporary price reductions, multi-buy discounts, feature advertisements, end-cap displays) cause massive
non-linear shocks to retail sales patterns:

1. **Price Elasticity and Promotional Lift:** The percentage change in unit volume relative to price changes:

$$
\epsilon = \frac{\% \Delta Q}{\% \Delta P}
$$

Promotional lift is rarely symmetric. Steep temporary discounts create sudden demand spikes that exceed baseline volume
by orders of magnitude. 2. **Pantry Loading (Forward Buying):** For non-perishable consumer packaged goods (detergent,
canned foods), promotional spikes are followed by a post-promotional demand dip. Consumers purchase multiple units
during the sale to stockpile, depressing organic purchase rates in subsequent weeks. 3. **Cannibalization and Halo
Effects:** Discounting Brand A cereal steals demand from competing Brand B cereal within the same subcategory
(cross-price elasticity / cannibalization). Simultaneously, discounting taco shells increases organic sales of salsa and
shredded cheese (complementary halo effect). Demand models must ingest cross-SKU promotional flags to disentangle true
organic baseline shifts from intra-category substitution.

## Product hierarchies

Retail catalogs are structured into strict nested geographic and merchandising hierarchies
<Cite id="wickramasuriya2019, hyndman2021" />:

$$
\text{Total Company} \to \text{Division} \to \text{Department} \to \text{Category} \to \text{Subcategory} \to \text{SKU} \times \text{Store}
$$

At the top levels (e.g., total store revenue), aggregate demand is smooth, Gaussian, and exhibits strong weekly and
annual seasonality. At the bottom level (e.g., a specific flavor of yogurt in a single rural store), demand is
intermittent, discrete, and dominated by Poisson or zero-inflated negative binomial noise <Cite id="makridakis2022" />.

To ensure managerial coherence—where sum-of-store forecasts identically equal regional totals—hierarchical
reconciliation techniques are mandatory:

- **Bottom-Up:** Forecast each SKU-store independently and sum upward. Suffers from high variance and noisy estimators
  at granular leaves.
- **Top-Down:** Forecast total department volume and disaggregate downward using historical proportions. Destroys
  localized store-level stockout signals.
- **Optimal MinT Reconciliation:** Wickramasuriya et al. formulate reconciliation as a linear projection that minimizes
  the trace of the aggregate forecast error covariance matrix <Cite id="wickramasuriya2019" />:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_t = \mathbf{S} (\mathbf{S}^\top \mathbf{W}^{-1} \mathbf{S})^{-1} \mathbf{S}^\top \mathbf{W}^{-1} \hat{\mathbf{y}}_t
$$

where $\mathbf{S}$ is the summing structural matrix encoding the hierarchy, $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_t$ are unconstrained base
forecasts across all levels, and $\mathbf{W}$ is the base forecast error covariance matrix.

## New items

A substantial fraction of retail revenue stems from newly launched products (seasonal fashion, reformulated snacks,
hardware revisions) with zero sales history (the cold-start problem).

Because autoregressive lags are undefined for new SKUs, retail machine learning relies on:
- **Attribute-Based Matching:** Forecasting purely from static metadata: brand, subcategory, package size, launch price,
  organic/conventional status, and target demographic.
- **Embedding Space Nearest Neighbors:** Mapping item text descriptions and imagery into pretrained multi-modal vector
  embeddings, identifying top-$k$ historical reference SKUs with similar launch profiles, and transferring their
  early-lifecycle demand curves.
- **Hierarchical Prior Shrinkage:** Initializing Bayesian forecast distributions at the subcategory mean, allowing
  store-level observations to gradually update the posterior as early sell-through rates materialize.

## Pitfalls

- **Phantom / Ghost Inventory:** ERP systems often record positive on-hand inventory when physical shelves are
  completely empty due to shoplifting, checkout scanning errors, or warehouse misplacement. Because the system believes
  inventory is available, zero sales are misclassified as true zero demand rather than a stock-out, preventing automated
  replenishment triggers.
- **Return Lag Distortion:** In e-commerce apparel, return rates frequently exceed 30% to 40%. Transaction logs record
  gross orders immediately, while physical returns arrive 14 to 30 days later. Models trained on gross orders severely
  over-predict net retained revenue if return probabilities are not modeled separately.
- **Holiday Calendar Alignment:** Fixed-date calendar features fail across floating holidays (Easter, Ramadan, Lunar New
  Year, Thanksgiving). Shifting holiday dates distort year-over-year lag comparisons unless aligned using
  holiday-relative offset features.
- **Wholesale Bullwhip Effect:** Retail demand volatility amplifies as orders travel upstream to distributors and
  manufacturers. Distortions caused by batch ordering and safety-stock buffers should not be mistaken for sudden
  end-consumer demand swings.
