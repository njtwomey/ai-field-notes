<Definition>
Scientific and simulation data encompasses empirical observations gathered from physical instrumentation (telescopes,
particle detectors, cryo-electron microscopes, weather stations) and computational outputs generated by numerical
solvers (computational fluid dynamics, finite element analysis, molecular dynamics). Unlike consumer tabular data,
scientific datasets are governed by dimensional units, structured measurement uncertainties, coordinate symmetries, and
strict conservation laws.
</Definition>

Machine learning models deployed in scientific discovery—from neural PDE surrogates to molecular property
predictors—differ fundamentally from commercial applications <Cite id="karniadakis2021, cuomo2022" />. In conventional
supervised learning, functions interpolate statistical correlations within high-density training clusters. In scientific
domains, unconstrained function approximators produce catastrophic non-physical predictions, violating energy
conservation, generating negative mass densities, or exhibiting coordinate-dependent artifacts.

## Units and dimensions

Physical quantities are never dimensionless scalars; they are bound to dimensional units governed by the International
System of Units (SI: mass $[M]$, length $[L]$, time $[T]$, electric current $[I]$, temperature $[\Theta]$, amount of
substance $[N]$, luminous intensity $[J]$):

1. **Dimensional Homogeneity:** Any physically valid equation $A = B + C$ requires that terms $A$, $B$, and $C$ share
   identical physical dimensions. Feeding raw heterogeneous numerical columns (e.g., mixing temperatures in Celsius with
   pressures in kilopascals) into a standard multi-layer perceptron strips dimensional meaning.
2. **Nondimensionalization:** Before model ingestion, physical datasets should be transformed into dimensionless numbers
   via Buckingham's $\pi$-theorem. Dimensionless quantities—such as the Reynolds number
   $\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho v L}{\mu}$, Mach number $\mathrm{Ma} = \frac{v}{c}$, or Rayleigh number
   $\mathrm{Ra}$—condense multiple dimensional parameters into scale-invariant ratios that govern system regimes.
   Nondimensionalized models generalize across micro-fluidic channels and macro-scale atmospheric simulations without
   retraining.
3. **Unit Systems and Commensurability:** In multi-institutional datasets, silent unit mismatches (e.g., standard
   atmospheres vs bar, imperial feet vs metric meters) remain a notorious source of silent corruption. Modern scientific
   data loaders enforce schema-level unit tagging (e.g., via Astropy Units or Pint libraries).

## Measurement uncertainty

Empirical scientific measurements never report a single isolated point value; they report an estimate paired with
structured uncertainty:

$$
y = \bar{y} \pm \sigma_{\text{stat}} \pm \sigma_{\text{sys}}
$$

- **Statistical Uncertainty ($\sigma_{\text{stat}}$):** Uncorrelated noise arising from Poisson photon counting, thermal
  fluctuations, or detector shot noise. Statistical uncertainty shrinks as the sample count increases
  ($\propto 1/\sqrt{N}$).
- **Systematic Uncertainty ($\sigma_{\text{sys}}$):** Correlated bias introduced by instrument calibration drift,
  thermal expansion of detector mounts, or zero-point offsets. Systematic errors do not average away with more data;
  they skew the entire distribution.
- **Heteroskedasticity and Covariance:** Measurement error varies drastically across the observation space (e.g.,
  signal-to-noise ratio drops near detector boundaries). Training loss functions must incorporate heteroskedastic
  precision weights:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{2 \sigma_i^2} \left(y_i - f_\theta(x_i)\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2} \log \sigma_i^2
$$

Treating all observations with uniform squared error causes models to overfit highly noisy outliers in low-precision
detector regimes.

## Simulation outputs

A substantial proportion of modern scientific ML datasets are generated by high-performance computational solvers (CFD,
climate models, lattice QCD, density functional theory):

- **Grid Resolution and Discretization Artifacts:** Solvers discretize continuous partial differential equations (PDEs)
  onto spatial meshes (structured Cartesian grids, unstructured tetrahedral meshes, or AMR adaptive octrees).
  High-frequency numerical dispersion, numerical diffusion, and boundary-layer clipping are artifacts of the
  discretization scheme, not real physics. Training naive CNNs directly on fixed-resolution pixelated grid slices locks
  models to specific grid spacings <Cite id="li2021fno" />.
- **Neural Operators:** Fourier Neural Operators (FNO) and DeepONets map between infinite-dimensional function spaces
  rather than discrete tensors <Cite id="li2021fno" />. By parameterizing the integral kernel in Fourier space:

$$
\mathcal{K}(v)(x) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(R \cdot (\mathcal{F} v)\right)(x)
$$

neural operators achieve zero-shot super-resolution, evaluating continuous fields on arbitrary fine meshes regardless of
training grid resolution.
- **Simulation-to-Reality (Sim2Real) Gap:** Numerical simulations frequently approximate complex sub-grid physical
  phenomena (such as cloud microphysics or turbulent dissipation) using empirical parameterizations. Training purely on
  synthetic solver trajectories causes models to fail on real experimental apparatus.

## Physical constraints

Physics-informed machine learning embeds domain invariants directly into models and training workflows
<Cite id="raissi2019, karniadakis2021" />:

1. **Conservation Laws via Residual Regularization:** For a governing PDE $\mathcal{N}[u](x, t) = 0$ (such as
   Navier-Stokes or Burgers' equation), automatic differentiation computes exact spatial and temporal derivatives of the
   neural network $u_\theta(x, t)$ <Cite id="raissi2019" />:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}}(\theta) + \lambda \frac{1}{N_f} \sum_{j=1}^{N_f} \left\| \mathcal{N}[u_\theta](x_j, t_j) \right\|^2
$$

where $\{x_j, t_j\}_{j=1}^{N_f}$ are collocation points sampled across the interior domain. 2. **Hard Geometric and
Equivariance Invariants:** Physical laws are invariant under Galilean transformations, spatial rotations
$\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and translations $\mathrm{SE}(3)$. In molecular and atomic modeling, forces and dipole moments are
equivariant vectors: rotating the input molecular coordinates must rotate the predicted force vectors identically
without changing predicted scalar potential energy. Architectures such as EGNNs (Equivariant Graph Neural Networks)
enforce exact geometric equivariance by construction. 3. **Hamiltonian and Symplectic Mechanics:** In conservative
physical systems, phase space volume is strictly preserved (Liouville's theorem). Hamiltonian Neural Networks (HNNs)
parameterize the scalar Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}(q, p)$ directly, obtaining equations of motion via symplectic gradients
$\dot{q} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial p}$ and $\dot{p} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{H}}{\partial q}$, ensuring
zero energy drift across thousands of integration steps.

## Pitfalls

- **Silent Extrapolation Failure:** Standard neural networks do not "know" when they have left the domain of valid
  physical regimes. An atmospheric model trained on contemporary historical observations can predict negative
  atmospheric moisture or sub-absolute-zero temperatures when exposed to extreme climate tipping events.
- **Coordinate-System Artifacts:** Training spatial models on raw spherical coordinates $(\phi, \theta)$ introduces
  coordinate singularities at the poles and distortion at high latitudes. Models must be parameterized on intrinsic
  Riemannian manifolds, HEALPix spherical tessellations, or stereographic projections.
- **Synthetic Data Overconfidence:** High-fidelity simulation datasets often omit sensor noise, thermal fluctuations,
  optical aberrations, and instrument dead-time. Validating solely on synthetic benchmarks creates false confidence
  before physical deployment.
- **Loss Scale Imbalance:** When training physics-informed networks with composite objectives
  ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{pde}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{bc}}$), gradients of
  PDE residuals frequently outscale boundary and initial condition gradients by orders of magnitude, causing models to
  predict trivial zero-energy solutions that satisfy the differential equation while completely violating boundary
  conditions. Adaptive loss weighting algorithms (such as gradient pathology balancing) are required to maintain
  equilibrium.
