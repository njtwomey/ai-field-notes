<Definition>
**Industrial sensor and maintenance data** pairs continuous physical telemetry streams—including vibration spectra,
thermal sensors, hydraulic pressures, and rotational speeds captured by SCADA and IoT historians—with discrete
maintenance event logs from Computerized Maintenance Management Systems (CMMS) <Cite id="jardine2006" />. In Prognostic
Health Management (PHM), this data trains models to predict an asset's **Remaining Useful Life (RUL)** before
irreversible physical failure occurs <Cite id="saxena2008cmapss" />.
</Definition>

Unlike software event streams, industrial sensor data originates from mechanical equipment governed by materials
science, wear physics, and operational fatigue. Sensor streams capture continuous physical degradation, while
maintenance databases record human interventions. Effectively processing industrial data requires harmonizing continuous
telemetry with sparse, unstructured maintenance logs while disentangling sensor recalibration artifacts from actual
mechanical wear.

## Run-to-failure records

The gold standard for predictive maintenance research is a **run-to-failure record**, which tracks continuous sensor
measurements on an asset from initial healthy commissioning ($t = 0$) through gradual degradation until catastrophic
failure ($t = T$):

- **The NASA C-MAPSS benchmark:** The Commercial Modular Aero-Propulsion System Simulation (C-MAPSS) dataset
  <Cite id="saxena2008cmapss" /> simulates fleet degradation in turbofan aircraft engines across varying flight
  altitudes, Mach numbers, and throttle settings, providing the foundational benchmark for industrial prognostic
  algorithms.
- **Degradation kinetics:** Mechanical components (such as turbine blades, ball bearings, and gearboxes) do not degrade
  linearly from day one. Instead, they exhibit a prolonged healthy baseline where no measurable wear exists, followed by
  an inflection point where micro-fractures propagate exponentially into macroscopic failure.

$$
RUL(t) = \begin{cases}
RUL_{\max} & \text{if } t < t_{\text{inflection}} \quad (\text{healthy baseline plateau}) \\
T - t & \text{if } t \ge t_{\text{inflection}} \quad (\text{active degradation countdown})
\end{cases}
$$

| Operational Phase | Cycle Interval | Physical Component State | Target Formulation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Healthy Baseline** | $t < t_{\text{inflection}}$ | Pristine alloy; zero measurable fatigue | Constant $RUL_{\max}$ (prevents fitting noise) |
| **Active Degradation** | $t_{\text{inflection}} \le t < T$ | Micro-fracture growth and vibration wear | Linear countdown $T - t$ to failure |
| **Terminal Failure** | $t = T$ | Operational threshold breach ($RUL = 0$) | Asset emergency trip or scheduled overhaul |

### Piecewise linear RUL targets

If an algorithm is trained to predict exact linear countdown $RUL(t) = T - t$ on a machine with 300 cycles of life, it
is forced to distinguish between $RUL = 280$ and $RUL = 260$ on identical, pristine components exhibiting zero physical
damage. This forces models to overfit to uninformative baseline sensor noise.

To prevent this, prognostic pipelines employ **piecewise linear target clipping** <Cite id="saxena2008cmapss" />:

$$
RUL_{\text{target}}(t) = \min(RUL_{\text{max}}, T - t)
$$

Capping maximum target RUL (e.g. $RUL_{\text{max}} = 125$ cycles) forces model capacity to concentrate on the active
damage propagation phase where genuine degradation signatures emerge.

## Labels from maintenance logs

In operating factories, refineries, and power grids, ground-truth failure timestamps are not recorded by automated
sensors. Instead, failure annotations are extracted from unstructured technician logs stored in CMMS databases:

- **Preventive vs. corrective maintenance:**
  - **Corrective maintenance:** The component experienced functional failure in service, causing an unplanned shutdown.
    This represents a true failure event ($Y = 1$, uncensored).
  - **Preventive maintenance:** Technicians preemptively replace an operational component according to a scheduled
    calendar interval (e.g. replacing bearings every 2,000 hours). The component did *not* fail. Treating scheduled
    preventative swaps as failure events introduces severe right-censoring bias, training models on artificial
    operational schedules rather than physical wear limits <Cite id="jardine2006" />.
- **Technician text phenotyping:** CMMS records contain brief, non-standardized technician notes (e.g., "re-torqued
  flange", "vibration alarm cleared", "replaced worn seal"). Extracting clean labels requires natural language
  processing to differentiate between routine inspections, sensor swaps, and genuine component breakdowns.

## Sensor replacement and recalibration

Industrial sensors (such as thermocouples, strain gauges, and piezoelectric accelerometers) operate in harsh physical
environments subject to thermal cycling, vibration, and chemical corrosion:

- **Sensor drift vs. asset wear:** Over months of continuous operation, sensor transducers suffer from calibration drift
  (gradual baseline voltage shifts). Data pipelines must distinguish between slow sensor drift and true mechanical wear.
- **Recalibration step jumps:** When an uncalibrated or failing sensor is replaced during routine maintenance, the
  incoming signal displays an instantaneous step-function jump:
  $$
  x_{\text{sensor}}(t) \longrightarrow x_{\text{sensor}}(t) + \Delta
  $$
  A model that operates on absolute signal levels interprets the recalibration offset as an acute mechanical breakdown,
  generating spurious critical alerts.
- **Differential feature engineering:** Robust maintenance pipelines compute differential metrics—such as rate-of-change
  $\frac{dx}{dt}$, vibration spectral kurtosis, frequency-band energy ratios, or deviations from fleet peer
  medians—rather than relying on raw absolute scalar values.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing scheduled replacements with component failures:** Failing to account for right-censoring in maintenance
  logs, treating healthy parts replaced under preventive schedules as failures.
- **Neglecting operating regime normalization:** Evaluating raw sensor values across variable ambient conditions (e.g.
  varying ambient temperatures or compressor speeds); operating regime shifts produce signal changes that completely
  mask subtle degradation signatures.
- **Unclipped RUL targets on pristine assets:** Training regression models to predict exact lifetimes on newly installed
  equipment where no physical damage signature has manifested.
- **Ignoring step jumps from sensor maintenance:** Triggering automated shutdown alerts following routine sensor
  replacements because the model misinterpreted an instrument recalibration offset as mechanical bearing seizure.
