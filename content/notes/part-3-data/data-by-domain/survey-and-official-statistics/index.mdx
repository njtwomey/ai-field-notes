<Definition>
Survey and official statistics are structured microdata and macro-aggregations published by national statistical
agencies (such as the US Census Bureau, Eurostat, or the UK Office for National Statistics). Unlike convenience samples
or web telemetry, survey datasets are collected under complex, probability-based sampling designs featuring explicit
stratification, multi-stage clustering, disproportionate selection probabilities, and non-response calibration
adjustments.
</Definition>

Machine learning models trained on survey microdata (such as the American Community Survey, Current Population Survey,
or National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) cannot treat dataset rows as independent and identically
distributed (IID) draws <Cite id="horvitz1952, rubin1987" />. Every individual record represents a varying number of
non-sampled population members, and ignoring survey weights, cluster correlations, or statistical disclosure
perturbances leads to severely biased parameter estimates and artificially narrow confidence intervals.

## Design weights

In complex survey sampling, individuals do not have equal probabilities of selection. Statistical agencies routinely
oversample small demographic groups, remote geographic territories, or high-variance industries to guarantee sufficient
sample sizes for reliable subpopulation inference <Cite id="horvitz1952" />:

1. **Base Design Weights:** For each sampled unit $i$, the base design weight is the inverse of its inclusion
   probability under the sample design:

$$
w_i^{\text{base}} = \frac{1}{\pi_i} = \frac{1}{P(i \in S)}
$$

A respondent from an oversampled minority stratum with $\pi_i = 0.05$ receives a base weight of $w_i = 20$, whereas a
respondent from a large stratum with $\pi_j = 0.001$ receives $w_j = 1000$. 2. **Horvitz-Thompson Estimator:** To
estimate a total population quantity $Y = \sum_{k \in U} y_k$ or a population mean $\bar{Y}$, the Horvitz-Thompson
estimator weights each observed value:

$$
\hat{Y}_{\text{HT}} = \sum_{i \in S} w_i y_i, \quad \hat{\bar{Y}}_{\text{HT}} = \frac{\sum_{i \in S} w_i y_i}{\sum_{i \in S} w_i}
$$

3. **Calibration and Raking (Post-Stratification):** Even with rigorous sampling, realised sample totals often diverge
   from known demographic benchmarks due to random sampling fluctuation. Agencies apply iterative proportional fitting
   (raking) or generalized regression estimation (GREG) to adjust weights such that weighted sample totals match exact
   census control totals along known marginals (age brackets, sex, geographic divisions).

When fitting machine learning loss functions $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, models must optimize the survey-weighted empirical
risk:

$$
\hat{\theta} = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i \in S} w_i \, \ell\left(f_\theta(x_i), y_i\right)
$$

## Non-response

No survey achieves $100\%$ participation. Survey data faces two distinct non-response regimes
<Cite id="rubin1987, little1988" />:

- **Unit Non-Response:** An entire sampled household or person cannot be contacted or refuses participation entirely.
  Agencies model the response propensity $\hat{p}_i = P(R_i = 1 \mid X_i)$ using logistic regression conditioned on
  auxiliary sampling frame covariates, multiplying base weights by $1 / \hat{p}_i$ within response-homogeneity classes.
- **Item Non-Response:** A participating respondent completes most of the questionnaire but skips sensitive questions,
  such as annual household income, wealth assets, or medical history.

Treating missing items with naive complete-case deletion discards substantial sample volume and injects severe selection
bias if data is Missing At Random (MAR) or Missing Not At Random (MNAR) <Cite id="little1988" />. Official statistical
releases employ sophisticated imputation procedures:
- **Hot-Deck Imputation:** Replacing a missing value with the response from a randomly selected "donor" record that
  matches the recipient across fine demographic strata.
- **Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE):** Generating $M$ distinct completed datasets where missing values
  are drawn from predictive posterior distributions, allowing downstream analysts to pool point estimates and calculate
  valid imputation variance via Rubin's combination rules <Cite id="rubin1987, vanbuuren2011" />.

## Mode effects

Modern surveys employ mixed-mode collection strategies to balance administrative cost and response rates:
- **CAPI (Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing):** Face-to-face in-person interview.
- **CATI (Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing):** Phone calls conducted by trained enumerators.
- **CAWI (Computer-Assisted Web Interviewing):** Self-administered online digital surveys.
- **PAPI (Paper and Pencil Interviewing):** Mailed paper forms.

The mode of administration systematically alters respondent answers—a phenomenon known as *mode effects*:
1. **Social Desirability Bias:** In interviewer-administered modes (CAPI, CATI), respondents systematically under-report
   socially frowned-upon behaviors (illicit substance use, binge drinking, tax evasion) and over-report prosocial
   activities (voter turnout, charitable giving, book reading) relative to self-administered anonymous online forms
   (CAWI).
2. **Primacy vs Recency Effects:** In visual modes (web, paper), respondents disproportionately select options presented
   near the top of long multiple-choice lists (primacy effect). In auditory modes (telephone), respondents
   disproportionately remember and select options heard last (recency effect).

Models predicting behavioral outcomes across longitudinal survey waves must include interview mode indicator variables
to avoid confounding survey administration transitions with real secular trends.

## Disclosure control

To fulfill statutory confidentiality mandates (such as Title 13 in the United States or GDPR in Europe), agencies apply
Statistical Disclosure Control (SDC) before distributing Public Use Microdata Samples (PUMS):

- **Top-Coding and Bottom-Coding:** Extreme continuous values are truncated. For example, all annual incomes exceeding
  250,000 USD are replaced with a fixed constant or the state-level mean of the top percentile. Naive regression models
  that treat top-coded entries as literal numerical quantities suffer severe right-tail distortion.
- **Geographic Aggregation Thresholds:** Geographic identifiers are suppressed for jurisdictions with populations below
  strict thresholds (typically 100,000 residents, defining Public Use Microdata Areas, or PUMAs).
- **Differential Privacy (Formal Privacy):** Modern releases (such as the 2020 US Decennial Census) inject calibrated
  discrete Laplace or Gaussian noise into tabular query counts via centralized algorithmic mechanisms (such as the
  TopDown algorithm) to mathematically bound re-identification risk against database reconstruction attacks.

## Pitfalls

- **Ignoring Complex Clustering in Standard Errors:** Sample designs cluster respondents within primary sampling units
  (PSUs, such as census tracts or city blocks). Observations within the same PSU are correlated due to shared local
  environments. Computing naive OLS or standard library logistic regression standard errors assumes IID sampling,
  severely understating standard errors and generating false-positive $p$-values. Analysts must utilize survey design
  objects with Taylor series linearization or replicate weights (Balanced Repeated Replication / Jackknife).
- **Misinterpreting Imputation Flags:** Public use files provide allocation flags (e.g., `INCOME_ALLOC = 1`) marking
  whether a value was directly reported or imputed by the agency. Training machine learning models on imputed targets
  trains the model to emulate the agency's imputation model rather than genuine empirical phenomena.
- **Conflating Weight Scaling:** In some survey releases, weights sum to the sample size $n$; in others, they sum to the
  total national population $N \approx 330{,}000{,}000$. Feeding unnormalized population weights into machine learning
  frameworks can cause gradient explosions or miscalibrated Bayesian posteriors.
- **Frame Undercoverage:** Telephone surveys relying on landline directories omit mobile-only households; web surveys
  systematically omit older and low-income populations without internet connectivity. Relying solely on survey data
  without post-stratification coverage adjustments biases societal predictions.
