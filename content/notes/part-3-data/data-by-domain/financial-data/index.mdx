<Definition>
**Financial data** encompasses the time-series observations, order event feeds, trade executions, and fundamental
accounting reports generated across global capital markets. Spanning microsecond-level limit order books to quarterly
corporate balance sheets, financial data is characterized by low signal-to-noise ratios, non-stationary price walks,
heavy-tailed return distributions <Cite id="cont2001" />, corporate action discontinuities, and pervasive backtest
biases <Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" />.
</Definition>

Unlike physical sensor feeds where signals follow stationary physical laws, financial data is generated by strategic,
competitive market participants. Arbitrage quickly erodes simple predictive patterns, leaving behind subtle statistical
dependencies. Applying machine learning to financial data requires rigorous feature transformations and structural
protections against backtest leakage.

## Prices and returns

Raw financial asset prices $P_t$ follow non-stationary stochastic processes with time-varying drift and volatility.
Feeding unadjusted raw prices directly into supervised learning models violates basic stationarity assumptions,
producing spurious correlations and failing to generalize across different market regimes.

### Return formulations

To achieve stationarity, quantitative pipelines transform prices into returns:

- **Simple net return:**
  $$
  R_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1
  $$
- **Log (continuously compounded) return:**
  $$
  r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) = \ln(P_t) - \ln(P_{t-1})
  $$
  Log returns are time-additive across multi-period horizons: the return over $T$ steps is simply the sum of single-step
  returns: $r_{0, T} = \sum_{t=1}^T r_t$.

### Stylized facts of returns

Across equities, currencies, and commodities, asset returns exhibit universal empirical properties
<Cite id="cont2001" />:
1. **Heavy tails (Leptokurtosis):** Return distributions exhibit excess kurtosis ($K \gg 3$) and power-law tail decay.
   Extreme price shocks occur orders of magnitude more frequently than predicted by Gaussian distributions.
2. **Absence of linear autocorrelation:** Serial correlation of raw returns $\text{Corr}(r_t, r_{t-k})$ decays to near
   zero within minutes, reflecting informational market efficiency.
3. **Volatility clustering:** Non-linear functions of returns (such as absolute returns $|r_t|$ or squared returns
   $r_t^2$) exhibit persistent positive autocorrelation spanning weeks and months. Volatile days cluster together, as do
   calm periods.

### Information-driven sampling bars

Standard financial time-series are sampled at uniform chronological intervals (e.g., 1-minute, 5-minute, or 1-day **time
bars**). Marcos López de Prado <Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" /> demonstrated that fixed time bars oversample quiet
overnight hours and severely undersample high-volume market openings, introducing severe heteroskedasticity and
non-Gaussian returns.

Alternative **information bars** sample transactions dynamically:
- **Volume bars:** Sample an observation whenever a fixed quantity of shares $V_{\text{target}}$ has transacted.
- **Dollar bars:** Sample an observation whenever a fixed monetary value $D_{\text{target}} = \sum_i p_i v_i$ has
  traded. Dollar bars sample at the physical speed of information arrival, stabilizing return variance and recovering
  near-normal statistical properties.

## Corporate actions and adjustments

Raw exchange price feeds contain artificial step-function discontinuities caused by corporate capital structure
alterations:

- **Stock splits:** In a 2-for-1 split, a 100 USD stock becomes two 50 USD shares overnight. Unadjusted historical price
  series show a sudden $50\%$ crash that models misinterpret as a massive negative return.
- **Cash dividends:** On the ex-dividend date, a stock's market price drops by approximately the cash dividend payout
  per share.
- **Adjustment methodologies:**
  - **Backward adjustment:** Multiplies all historical prices prior to the corporate action by an adjustment factor:
    $$
    \text{Factor} = \frac{P_{\text{ex-action}}}{P_{\text{pre-action}}}
    $$
    This smooths historical return curves and matches current nominal pricing, but distorts historical dollar volume
    accounting.
  - **Total return index:** Synthesizes an uninterrupted equity curve assuming all dividends are continuously reinvested
    into the underlying asset.

## Order books and trades

At high frequencies (milliseconds to microseconds), markets operate via a continuous double auction managed by a **Limit
Order Book (LOB)**:

| Bid Size (Shares) | Bid Price (USD) | Order Book Tier | Ask Price (USD) | Ask Size (Shares) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| — | — | **L3 Ask** | $100.06$ | $1{,}500$ |
| — | — | **L2 Ask** | $100.05$ | $800$ |
| — | — | **L1 (Best Ask)** | $100.04$ | $300$ |
| **Total Bid: 4,100 shares** | **Spread $\Delta P = 0.03\text{ USD}$** | **Midpoint: $100.025\text{ USD}$** | **Spread $\Delta P = 0.03\text{ USD}$** | **Total Ask: 2,600 shares** |
| $1{,}200$ | $100.01$ | **L1 (Best Bid)** | — | — |
| $2{,}500$ | $100.00$ | **L2 Bid** | — | — |
| $400$ | $99.98$ | **L3 Bid** | — | — |

- **Market depth (Level 2):** Aggregates volume queues across multiple price levels away from the best bid and ask.
  Features derived from order book imbalance:
  $$
  \text{OBI} = \frac{V_{\text{bid}} - V_{\text{ask}}}{V_{\text{bid}} + V_{\text{ask}}}
  $$
  predict short-term order flow direction.
- **Tick-level feeds (Level 3):** Event logs capturing individual order placements, cancellations, and trade executions.
- **Execution slippage:** Backtests that assume infinite liquidity or assume execution exactly at the mid-price
  $(P_{\text{bid}} + P_{\text{ask}})/2$ produce illusory paper alpha; realistic pipelines model spread crossing costs
  and market impact.

## Fundamentals and reporting dates

Corporate financial statements (income statements, balance sheets, cash flow reports) are published in periodic filings
(such as US SEC Form 10-Q and 10-K):

- **Fiscal period vs. publication lag:** A fiscal quarter may conclude on March 31. However, the audited 10-Q report is
  not submitted to regulatory authorities until May 10.
- **The lookahead trap:** Sourcing fundamental metrics using fiscal period end dates introduces fatal lookahead bias: a
  backtest trading in April with knowledge of the March 31 balance sheet uses information unknown to real-world market
  participants until May.
- **Point-in-Time (PIT) data:** Quantitative pipelines must strictly utilize Point-in-Time fundamental databases that
  record both the fiscal period date and the exact regulatory publication timestamp.

## Look-ahead and survivorship bias

Backtesting investment strategies is fraught with systematic statistical biases that artificially inflate performance:

- **Survivorship bias:**
  - Filtering historical data using the *current* constituent list of an equity index (such as testing on today's S&P
    500 members over the past 20 years).
  - Companies that went bankrupt (e.g. Enron, Lehman Brothers), failed, or were delisted due to distressed price
    declines are omitted from the historical universe.
  - The model trains exclusively on long-term corporate winners, artificially inflating annualized backtest returns by
    300 to 500 basis points per year.
- **Look-ahead bias:** Feeding features that incorporate information generated after the decision timestamp—such as
  normalized variables scaled across the entire multi-year dataset (global min-max scaling) or unlagged macro releases.

## Pitfalls

- **Training directly on non-stationary price levels:** Predicting raw closing prices rather than returns, causing
  models to learn arbitrary historical level regimes that fail in out-of-sample periods.
- **Ignoring point-in-time filing lags:** Joining earnings and accounting fundamentals by fiscal quarter end rather than
  the actual public filing date.
- **Backtesting on survivor-only universes:** Neglecting to purchase survivorship-free historical databases that retain
  delisted and bankrupt tickers.
- **Assuming zero-cost execution:** Simulating quantitative strategies without accounting for bid-ask spread crossing,
  exchange fees, borrowing fees for short positions, and market impact on large orders.
