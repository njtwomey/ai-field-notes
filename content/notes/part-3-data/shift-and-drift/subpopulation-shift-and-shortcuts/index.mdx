<Definition>
**Subpopulation shift** occurs when the population is composed of discrete subgroups
$g \in \mathcal{G} = \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{A}$ defined by the joint cross-product of the target label
$y \in \mathcal{Y}$ and a spurious attribute $a \in \mathcal{A}$, such that group proportions shift between training and
deployment while the within-group data-generating distributions remain identical:

$$
P_{\text{train}}(g) \neq P_{\text{test}}(g), \quad \text{subject to } P_{\text{train}}(\mathbf{x} \mid g) = P_{\text{test}}(\mathbf{x} \mid g).
$$

When a model relies on the spurious attribute $a$ as a **shortcut**, shifts in the proportions of counterexamples expose
dramatic performance drops that average accuracy metrics conceal.
</Definition>

## Spurious correlations and ERM

Standard Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) optimizes average loss across the empirical training set:

$$
\hat{f}_{\text{ERM}} = \arg\min_f \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(f(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i) = \arg\min_f \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} P_{\text{train}}(g) \cdot \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim g}[\ell(f(\mathbf{x}), y)].
$$

Because ERM weights each subgroup proportionally to its training frequency $P_{\text{train}}(g)$, standard training
exhibits severe failure modes in the presence of spurious correlations:

- **Correlation alignment**: Suppose a spurious background attribute $a \in \{\text{land}, \text{water}\}$ strongly
  correlates with bird species $y \in \{\text{landbird}, \text{waterbird}\}$ in the training corpus (e.g., $95\%$ of
  landbirds appear on land, and $95\%$ of waterbirds appear on water).
- **Simplicity bias**: Deep neural networks and gradient boosters exhibit an inductive bias toward learning simple,
  low-frequency, or linearly separable features first. Identifying the color of a background patch requires far fewer
  parameters and training steps than parsing complex avian plumage and beak morphology.
- **The shortcut solution**: ERM satisfies its objective by adopting the shortcut decision rule $\hat{y} = a$, achieving
  $95\%$ average training accuracy with minimal optimization effort while completely ignoring the causal, invariant
  anatomical features.

## Shortcut features and classic case studies

Shortcut learning is pervasive across machine learning applications:

1. **The Snow Husky dilemma**: Ribeiro et al. <Cite id="ribeiro2016" /> demonstrated a deep neural network classifying
   wolves versus huskies that achieved high validation accuracy by detecting whether the background contained snow,
   rather than examining facial features.
2. **Clinical radiology artifacts**: Deep models trained to detect pneumothorax or COVID-19 from chest X-rays learned to
   detect the presence of chest drains, hospital radiological markers, or patient positioning pads rather than pulmonary
   pathology. When deployed in clinical wards with different treatment protocols, diagnostic performance collapsed.
3. **The Waterbirds benchmark**: Sagawa et al. <Cite id="sagawa2020" /> formalized this failure mode using the
   Waterbirds benchmark, showing that standard ERM models trained on ImageNet backbones attained $97\%$ average accuracy
   but collapsed to under $60\%$ accuracy on minority groups (waterbirds on land or landbirds on water).

## Worst-group error and Group DRO

Evaluating models purely by average test accuracy masks vulnerabilities because minority groups contribute minimally to
the aggregate sum. Robustness under subpopulation shift requires evaluating **worst-group error**:

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{worst}}(f) = \max_{g \in \mathcal{G}} \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim g} [\ell(f(\mathbf{x}), y)].
$$

A model with $95\%$ average accuracy and $10\%$ worst-group accuracy has learned a spurious shortcut that fails
catastrophically on minority slices.

### Group Distributionally Robust Optimization (Group DRO)
To force the model to rely on invariant features, Sagawa et al. <Cite id="sagawa2020" /> proposed Group DRO, which
optimizes a minimax objective over the worst-case group distribution:

$$
\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \max_{\mathbf{q} \in \Delta_{|\mathcal{G}|}} \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} q_g \, \hat{\mathcal{R}}_g(f),
$$

where $\Delta_{|\mathcal{G}|}$ represents the probability simplex over groups. In practice, group weights $q_g$ are
updated online using exponential gradient ascent:

$$
q_g^{(t+1)} \propto q_g^{(t)} \exp\left( \eta_q \, \hat{\mathcal{R}}_g(f^{(t)}) \right).
$$

When the model underperforms on minority counterexamples, their group weight $q_g$ surges, forcing backpropagation to
penalize shortcut features and prioritize the invariant core signals.

## Collecting counterexamples and dataset interventions

Algorithm-level interventions like Group DRO are only effective when counterexamples exist in the data:

1. **Strategic oversampling and balanced batches**: Ensure every mini-batch contains an equal distribution across all
   label-attribute pairs $(y, a)$.
2. **Collecting targeted counterexamples**: Actively acquire annotations for unrepresented subgroups (e.g.,
   intentionally sourcing photographs of waterbirds in forest canopies or camels in snowy terrain).
3. **Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM)**: Formulated by Arjovsky et al. <Cite id="arjovsky2019" />, IRM seeks a data
   representation $\Phi(\mathbf{x})$ such that the optimal linear classifier $\mathbf{w}$ is simultaneously optimal
   across distinct training environments $\mathcal{E}$:

   $$
   \min_{\Phi} \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}} \mathcal{R}^e(\mathbf{w} \circ \Phi) \quad \text{subject to } \mathbf{w} \in \arg\min_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}} \mathcal{R}^e(\bar{\mathbf{w}} \circ \Phi) \quad \forall e \in \mathcal{E}.
   $$

   If a feature is a spurious shortcut that varies across environments, IRM penalizes its representation.

## Pitfalls and limitations

1. **Unobserved group annotations**: Group DRO and importance reweighting require knowing the group label $g = (y, a)$
   for every training sample. In large-scale web scrapes and unstructured logs, spurious attributes $a$ are rarely
   labeled.
2. **Severe minority overfitting**: When minority groups have very few samples (e.g., $N_g < 20$), optimizing
   worst-group loss can lead models to memorize individual minority instances rather than learning true invariant
   representations.
3. **Average-accuracy trade-offs**: Eliminating reliance on shortcuts can slightly reduce average accuracy on
   distributions where the shortcut holds true, requiring practitioners to evaluate the operational trade-off between
   peak average performance and safety guarantees.
