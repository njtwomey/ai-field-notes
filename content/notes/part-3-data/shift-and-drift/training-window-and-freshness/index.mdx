<Definition>
The **training window** $\mathcal{W}(W) = \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t)\}_{t=T-W+1}^T$ defines the contiguous historical
temporal span of duration $W$ selected to fit an estimator at deployment time $T$:

$$
\hat{f}_W = \arg\min_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \sum_{t=T-W+1}^T w_t \, \ell(f(\mathbf{x}_t), y_t).
$$

In non-stationary environments, window selection balances **data freshness** (reducing obsolete concept bias) against
**sample sufficiency** (reducing parameter estimation variance).
</Definition>

## Window length as a bias-variance choice

In stationary environments, estimation variance decreases monotonically with sample size, making an expanding window
($W = T$) asymptotically optimal. In real-world production environments where data generating distributions drift over
time ($P_t \neq P_{T+1}$), the generalization error on the upcoming deployment interval $T+1$ follows a temporal
bias-variance trade-off <Cite id="widmer1996" />:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[ (\hat{f}_W(\mathbf{x}) - y_{T+1})^2 \right] = \text{Bias}^2(W) + \text{Var}(W) + \sigma^2.
$$

- **Short window ($W \ll W^*$)**: The training distribution reflects the immediate current state of the world
  ($P_t \approx P_{T+1}$), minimizing historical drift bias. However, the limited number of training rows inflates
  parameter variance ($\text{Var} \propto \frac{1}{W}$), rendering the model volatile and sensitive to transient noise.
- **Long window ($W \gg W^*$)**: Abundant data suppresses estimation variance, but training includes obsolete
  operational regimes, superseded pricing policies, or past consumer preferences, introducing severe **stale-data
  bias**.
- **Optimal window $W^*$**: Achieved at the inflection point where marginal reductions in parameter estimation variance
  equal the marginal increase in drift bias.

## Recency weights

A sharp rectangular sliding window treats an observation from $T-W+1$ as fully informative while assigning zero weight
to $T-W$. **Recency weighting** softens this boundary by assigning an exponentially decaying weight to each historical
observation:

$$
w_t = \gamma^{T - t}, \quad \gamma \in (0, 1] \quad \text{or} \quad w_t = \exp(-\lambda (T - t)).
$$

- $\gamma = 1$ recovers the unweighted expanding window.
- Smaller values of $\gamma$ (or larger half-life parameters $\tau_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$) discount historical
  data smoothly.
- **Effective sample size**: Under sample weighting, the statistical degrees of freedom correspond to:

  $$
  N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\left( \sum_{t=1}^T w_t \right)^2}{\sum_{t=1}^T w_t^2}.
  $$

  Monitoring $N_{\text{eff}}$ ensures the exponential decay parameter does not shrink effective training support below
  the minimum required for statistical convergence.

## Seasonality and cyclic alignment

When underlying data streams exhibit cyclical patterns—such as diurnal demand, weekly foot-traffic cycles, or annual
holiday retail peaks—arbitrary window lengths introduce severe distortion:

- **Phase distortion**: If a model is trained on a 10-day sliding window, the training sample contains two weekends and
  six weekdays (or three weekends and four weekdays depending on the start date). The empirical class priors become
  unbalanced, biasing probability calibrations.
- **Integer period alignment**: To maintain unbiased representation across cyclic modes, the training window length must
  equal an integer multiple of the dominant seasonal cycle:

  $$
  W = k \cdot S, \quad k \in \mathbb{N}^+,
  $$

  where $S$ is the fundamental seasonal period (e.g., exactly 7, 14, or 28 days for weekly seasonality, or exactly 52
  weeks for annual seasonality).

## Regime changes and structural breaks

While gradual drift allows smooth adaptation via sliding windows or recency weights, **abrupt regime shifts** (e.g.,
macroeconomic disruptions, regulatory policy shifts, or API schema alterations) render pre-shift data actively harmful
<Cite id="gama2014" />:

1. **Change-point detection**: Statistical process control algorithms—such as the **Page-Hinkley test** or Cumulative
   Sum (**CUSUM**)—monitor the cumulative sum of model residuals $e_t = |y_t - \hat{y}_t|$:

   $$
   m_t = \sum_{i=1}^t (e_i - \bar{e} - \delta), \quad M_t = \min_{1 \le i \le t} m_i.
   $$

   When the discrepancy $m_t - M_t > \lambda$, a change-point alarm triggers.
2. **Window reset**: Upon detecting a structural break, the pipeline resets the training window to the change-point
   timestamp $t_{\text{break}}$, discarding all pre-break observations to eliminate catastrophic bias.

## Retraining cadence

Production systems deploy two complementary retraining architectures:

- **Scheduled retraining (cron cadence)**: Retraining models at fixed calendar intervals (e.g., every 6 hours, daily, or
  weekly). Simpler to operationalize, but risks running stale models during sudden shifts or wasting compute retraining
  when data distributions are stationary.
- **Event-driven drift-triggered retraining**: The retraining pipeline executes only when distribution monitoring (such
  as PSI or MMD) or residual tracking (Page-Hinkley) exceeds pre-set alert thresholds.
- **The delayed feedback bottleneck**: In click-through rate (CTR) prediction, ground truth labels arrive within seconds
  or minutes, permitting hourly retraining. In credit default risk or insurance fraud, true labels arrive months or
  years after the initial prediction, preventing immediate retraining on recent production queries.

## Pitfalls and practical considerations

1. **Cold-start instability**: Truncating the training window too aggressively after a regime shift reduces
   $N_{\text{eff}}$ to near zero, causing complex models (such as deep neural networks or high-depth trees) to severely
   overfit or fail to converge. Regularize aggressively or fall back to linear baselines immediately following window
   resets.
2. **Hidden cyclic leakage**: When training on a short window that captured an abnormal holiday surge (e.g., Black
   Friday), the model will treat holiday volume as baseline reality for subsequent ordinary weeks unless explicit
   seasonal indicators are engineered.
3. **Training compute escalation**: Retraining large ensemble models frequently across massive rolling windows incurs
   substantial GPU/CPU infrastructure expenses and carbon footprints. Balance retraining frequency against marginal
   accuracy gains using offline freshness ablation curves.
