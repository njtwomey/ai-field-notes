<Definition>
**Symbolic music data** describe musical compositions and performances as discrete, structured events—specifying
parameters such as pitch, onset time, duration, velocity, meter, and instrumentation—rather than continuous acoustic
waveforms <Cite id="muller2015" />. By decoupling musical structure from physical acoustic rendering, room acoustics,
and recording timbres, symbolic representations provide lightweight, interpretable, and computationally efficient inputs
for music generation, algorithmic composition, and automatic music transcription (AMT).
</Definition>

In music information retrieval (MIR) and generative audio, music exists in two complementary domains: the continuous
acoustic domain (audio waveforms and spectrograms) and the discrete symbolic domain (sheet music, MIDI, and piano
rolls). 

While an audio recording captures every microsecond of sound pressure generated by instruments and reverberation, a
symbolic file encodes the composer's intent and the performer's keystrokes. Developing machine learning architectures
for music requires understanding standard event protocols like MIDI, two-dimensional piano rolls, semantic score formats
(MusicXML), and the algorithms that align symbolic scores to expressive human audio recordings.

## MIDI

The Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) is the standard digital protocol for communicating musical performance
data between electronic instruments and computers:

- **Asynchronous event stream:** MIDI encodes music not as continuous sound, but as discrete performance commands with
  timestamp offsets:
  - `Note-On`: Triggered when a key is depressed. Contains channel index, pitch $p \in [0, 127]$, and velocity
    $v \in [1, 127]$.
  - `Note-Off`: Triggered when a key is released. Alternatively encoded as a `Note-On` message with velocity $v=0$.
  - `Control Change (CC)`: Encodes continuous expressive parameters, such as sustain pedal engagement (CC64), pitch
    bend, and modulation wheel depth.
- **Pitch scale:** Standardized on 7-bit integers from 0 to 127, where pitch 60 represents middle C (C4,
  $261.63\text{ Hz}$). Each integer corresponds to an equal-tempered semitone.
- **Timing representation:** In Standard MIDI Files (SMF), timing is recorded as **delta-times** between consecutive
  events, measured in pulses per quarter note (PPQ). Converting PPQ ticks into physical seconds requires combining tick
  counts with dynamic tempo change events (microseconds per quarter note).

## Piano rolls

A **piano roll** is a discrete two-dimensional representation that translates an asynchronous MIDI stream into a
uniform, regularly sampled matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 128}$ or $\{0, 1\}^{T \times 128}$
<Cite id="muller2015" />:

- **Horizontal axis (Time $T$):** Quantized into uniform time steps, either metric subdivisions (e.g., 16th notes or
  32nd notes) or absolute physical time frames (e.g., $10\text{ ms}$ or $20\text{ ms}$ bins).
- **Vertical axis (Pitches $P=128$):** The 128 possible MIDI pitch numbers.
- **Cell values:** Binary active/inactive indicators ($x_{t, p} \in \{0, 1\}$) or continuous key strike velocities
  ($x_{t, p} \in [0, 127]$).

Piano rolls format symbolic music as two-dimensional images, enabling standard convolutional neural networks, U-Nets,
and diffusion models to process polyphonic arrangements. 

However, piano rolls introduce two significant challenges:
1. **Extreme matrix sparsity:** In typical solo piano or string quartet music, only 3 to 6 notes sound simultaneously,
   leaving $>96\%$ of the matrix populated by zeros.
2. **Note boundary ambiguity:** A sequence of active ones across consecutive time bins ($[1, 1, 1, 1]$) cannot
   distinguish a single held whole note from four rapid staccato quarter notes of the same pitch. Resolving this
   requires dual representations tracking separate onset and frame activations <Cite id="hawthorne2018" />.

## Score formats

While MIDI records mechanical performance actions, sheet music scores contain rich semantic and compositional metadata:

- **Structural score formats:** Formats such as **MusicXML**, **MEI (Music Encoding Initiative)**, and **LilyPond**
  explicitly capture musical grammar: clefs, key signatures, accidentals (e.g., distinguishing $C\sharp$ from $D\flat$,
  which share identical MIDI pitch 61), voice leading, measure bars, phrase slurs, lyrics, and tempo marks.
- **Tokenized event sequences for Transformers:** Generative language models (such as Music Transformer and Midi-BERT)
  serialize music into discrete token vocabularies:
  - **REMI (Revamped MIDI):** Introduces explicit metrical tokens (`Bar`, `Beat_Position`, `Pitch`, `Velocity`,
    `Duration`, `Chord_Symbol`).
  - **Compound Word (CP) representation:** Bundles correlated attributes (pitch, duration, velocity) into unified token
    tuples, reducing sequence length by $4\times$ and expanding attention context.

## Alignment to audio

**Audio-to-score alignment** (also known as music synchronization) establishes a continuous mapping between the physical
timeline of an acoustic recording and the musical timeline of a symbolic score <Cite id="muller2015" />:

Expressive human musicians do not play on rigid metronomic grids. They introduce expressive timing (**rubato**),
accelerating during tense passages and decelerating at cadences. Aligning a performance to a score requires **Dynamic
Time Warping (DTW)**:

1. **Feature extraction:** Both the acoustic audio recording and a synthesized reference version of the symbolic score
   are converted into 12-dimensional **chromagrams** (pitch class profiles folding all octaves into
   $C, C\sharp, \dots, B$).
2. **Cost matrix:** A pairwise distance matrix
   $\mathbf{D}(i, j) = 1 - \text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{c}_{\text{audio}}(i), \mathbf{c}_{\text{score}}(j))$ measures
   local harmonic discrepancy between audio frame $i$ and score frame $j$.
3. **Warping path optimization:** Dynamic programming computes the optimal monotonically non-decreasing path
   $\mathcal{W} = (w_1, \dots, w_K)$ that minimizes cumulative alignment cost.

### Dual-objective transcription (Onsets and Frames)

In reverse—transcribing audio into symbolic notes—Hawthorne et al. demonstrated that training separate neural heads for
note onsets and note sustain frames overcomes polyphonic smearing <Cite id="hawthorne2018" />. The onset network learns
sharp transient percussive strikes, and its predicted onset probabilities condition the frame-level pitch activation
network, dramatically boosting piano transcription F1-scores.

## Pitfalls

- **Timing jitter from coarse grid quantization:** Discretizing expressive performances into coarse piano roll grids
  ($>40\text{ ms}$) erases subtle human swing, rubato, and microtiming ornamentation.
- **Polyphonic sustain ambiguity:** Training piano roll models without dedicated onset targets leads to fragmented,
  flickering note predictions during long sustained chords.
- **Loss of harmonic spelling in MIDI:** Collapsing enharmonic pairs ($F\sharp$ and $G\flat$) to identical integers
  erases musical key context, confusing models trained on music theory and harmonic function.
- **Variable tempo tracking failures:** Slicing MIDI files into fixed-second windows without properly parsing internal
  tempo change events desynchronizes bar and measure alignments.
