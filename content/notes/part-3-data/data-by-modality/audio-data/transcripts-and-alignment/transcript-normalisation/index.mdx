<Definition>
**Transcript normalisation** is the deterministic text-processing pipeline applied to reference transcripts and model
hypothesis texts prior to acoustic model training and performance scoring <Cite id="radford2023, sproat2001" />. It
standardizes non-standard written symbols (such as digits, dates, and currencies) into spoken words, harmonizes
punctuation and letter casing, expands contractions, reconciles regional spelling variants, and filters conversational
disfluencies.
</Definition>

In speech recognition research and deployment, evaluating an automatic speech recognition (ASR) system appears
straightforward: compute the Levenshtein distance between the model's output and the human ground-truth transcript. In
practice, however, raw text evaluation is plagued by formatting discrepancies.

If an ASR model hears acoustic speech and outputs `"I paid $25 on May 3rd"`, while the reference human annotator wrote
`"I paid twenty-five dollars on May third"`, the acoustic transcription is flawless. Yet a naive Word Error Rate (WER)
calculation scores three substitutions and one insertion, severely penalizing the system. As demonstrated by Radford et
al. (2023), inconsistent transcript normalization can alter reported WER by $2\text{ to }8\text{ percentage points}$—a
discrepancy exceeding the performance margin between successive model architectures.

## Numerals

Numbers, dates, currencies, and mathematical units are written symbolically as **Non-Standard Words (NSWs)** rather than
phonetically <Cite id="sproat2001" />. Evaluating speech models requires systematically expanding NSWs into their fully
articulated spoken forms:

- **Cardinal numbers:**
  - `42` $\to$ `"forty-two"`
  - `1500` $\to$ `"fifteen hundred"` or `"one thousand five hundred"` (depending on domain conventions).
- **Ordinal numbers:**
  - `1st` $\to$ `"first"`, `2nd` $\to$ `"second"`, `23rd` $\to$ `"twenty-third"`.
- **Currency values:**
  - `$5.50` $\to$ `"five dollars and fifty cents"`
  - `£20` $\to$ `"twenty pounds"`
- **Dates, times, and years:**
  - `2026` $\to$ `"twenty twenty-six"`
  - `4:30 pm` $\to$ `"four thirty p m"`
- **Physical units:**
  - `10 kg` $\to$ `"ten kilograms"`, `60 mph` $\to$ `"sixty miles per hour"`.

Without automated numeral expansion, a single written token (`"$100"`) compared against its spoken counterpart
(`"one hundred dollars"`) creates multiple artificial substitution and insertion errors.

## Punctuation and casing

Human transcribers frequently disagree on stylistic punctuation and capitalization:

### Case folding

Letter case conveys orthographic convention rather than acoustic difference. Comparing `"Berlin"` against `"berlin"` or
`"NASA"` against `"nasa"` without case-folding introduces artificial vocabulary fragmentation. Evaluation pipelines fold
all text to lowercase prior to computing Levenshtein alignment.

### Punctuation removal

Commas, periods, semicolons, em-dashes, and quotation marks represent prosodic pauses and syntactic structure rather
than discrete acoustic phonemes. A speaker taking a breath may cause one human annotator to insert a comma and another
to omit it. Stripping all non-alphanumeric punctuation prevents punctuation differences from penalizing acoustic word
recognition.

### Contraction and spelling harmonization

English exhibits substantial variation in spelling and contractions:
- **Contraction expansion:** Standardizing `"can't"` to `"cannot"`, `"it's"` to `"it is"`, and `"they'd"` to
  `"they would"`.
- **Dialectal spelling reconciliation:** Harmonizing British and American English spellings (e.g., mapping `"colour"` to
  `"color"`, `"theatre"` to `"theater"`, and `"organise"` to `"organize"`).

## Disfluencies

Conversational speech is dense with speech disfluencies:
- **Filled pauses:** `"um"`, `"uh"`, `"er"`, `"ah"`, `"hmm"`.
- **Word repetitions:** `"the the question"`
- **False starts:** `"I was— I wanted to ask"`

In many speech datasets (such as Switchboard or CallHome), human transcribers annotate every vocal hesitation. However,
modern end-to-end ASR models trained on mixed web audio often learn to omit filler words, outputting clean grammatical
prose.

If the reference transcript contains `"um I think uh yes"` and the model predicts `"I think yes"`, naive WER penalizes
the model with two deletion errors. Standardized evaluation normalizers—such as the Whisper text normalizer
<Cite id="radford2023" />—explicitly strip common disfluencies from both reference and hypothesis before scoring to
measure true lexical accuracy.

## Effect on word error rate

Word Error Rate is computed as the normalized edit distance:

$$
\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N}
$$

where $S$ is the number of substitutions, $D$ is deletions, $I$ is insertions, and $N$ is the total number of words in
the reference transcript.

In the Whisper study, Radford et al. demonstrated that transcript normalization is as consequential to reported
benchmark scores as core model architecture choices:
1. On noisy datasets like Common Voice, unnormalized raw text evaluation frequently yields WERs exceeding $18\%$.
2. Applying lowercasing and punctuation stripping drops the measured WER to $\sim 12\%$.
3. Applying comprehensive numeral verbalization, contraction standardization, and British-American harmonization further
   lowers the measured WER to $7.8\%$.

Over half of the apparent errors in raw evaluations stem from orthographic formatting choices rather than incorrect
speech recognition.

## Pitfalls

- **Asymmetric evaluation normalization:** Normalizing the model hypothesis while leaving the ground-truth reference
  unnormalized (or vice-versa) guarantees severe formatting mismatches, wildly inflating reported error rates.
- **Accidental meaning corruption during regex normalization:** Naively applying global regex replacements (e.g.,
  replacing all periods with spaces) can corrupt decimal numbers (`"3.14"` becoming `"3 14"`) or acronyms (`"U.S.A."`
  becoming `"U S A"`).
- **Evaluating multilingual speech with English normalization rules:** Applying English-specific contraction and number
  expansion scripts to languages with complex morphological inflection (such as German compound nouns, Arabic clitics,
  or French elisions like `"l'homme"`) breaks valid words and corrupts character boundaries.
- **Discarding punctuation needed for downstream tasks:** If an ASR system feeds a downstream Large Language Model for
  summarization or machine translation, completely stripping punctuation in the production serving pipeline degrades LLM
  comprehension. In production, models should output truecased, punctuated text, with normalization applied strictly
  inside offline evaluation benchmarks.
