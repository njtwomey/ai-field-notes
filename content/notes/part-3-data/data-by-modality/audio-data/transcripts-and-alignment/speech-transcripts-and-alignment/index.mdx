<Definition>
**Speech transcripts and alignment** establish the structural and temporal mapping between continuous acoustic waveforms
$\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \dots, x_N]$ and discrete written linguistic sequences $\mathbf{y} = [y_1, \dots, y_U]$
<Cite id="graves2006, mcauliffe2017" />. In speech engineering, transcripts require phonetic and textual normalization
to resolve mismatches between written symbols and spoken acoustics, while forced alignment computes frame-level boundary
timestamps $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ for individual phones, words, and speaker turns.
</Definition>

In supervised automatic speech recognition (ASR), text-to-speech (TTS), and speech translation, high-quality audio is
useless without properly aligned textual annotations. A raw audio recording contains continuous acoustic acoustics; a
raw written transcript contains standard orthography.

Bridging the acoustic-textual gap requires navigating transcription conventions (verbatim vs cleaned prose), verbalizing
non-standard written tokens via text normalization, employing Viterbi or CTC forced alignment to generate timestamped
phonetic targets, and evaluating multi-speaker diarization boundaries under standardized evaluation collars.

## Transcription conventions

The level of detail captured by human transcribers fundamentally affects model behavior:

### Verbatim transcription

Verbatim transcription records every acoustic event exactly as uttered:
- **Disfluencies and hesitations:** Transcribes false starts (`"I want to— I need to go"`), word repetitions
  (`"the the problem"`), and filled pauses (`"um"`, `"uh"`, `"er"`, `"ah"`).
- **Non-verbal vocalizations:** Tags coughs, laughter, sighs, and throat-clearing (e.g., `[laughter]`, `[applause]`).
- **Necessity for ASR:** Essential for conversational speech recognition, voicebot turn-taking, and clinical speech
  assessment. Training an acoustic model on audio containing `"um"` where the transcript omits it forces the model to
  penalize acoustic truth.

### Non-verbatim (Cleaned) transcription

Non-verbatim transcription cleans spoken utterances into polished, grammatical written prose, removing stutters, filler
words, and self-corrections. While desirable for reading meeting minutes or court records, non-verbatim transcripts
introduce severe label noise into speech models.

### Punctuation and capitalization

- **Normalized unpunctuated:** Traditional acoustic modeling targets clean phonetic sequences without punctuation or
  casing (`the quick brown fox jumps`).
- **Orthographic truecasing:** Modern end-to-end models (such as Whisper) train directly on punctuated, truecased text
  (`The quick brown fox jumps.`), requiring the neural network to jointly learn acoustic transcription and semantic
  punctuation prediction.

## Text normalisation for speech

Written language uses compact symbolic tokens termed **Non-Standard Words (NSWs)**—including numbers, dates, currency
signs, abbreviations, and chemical formulas—that do not have a 1:1 letter-to-sound mapping:

- **Text Normalization (TN / Verbalization):** Converts written NSWs into fully articulated spoken words for ASR
  training and TTS synthesis:
  - `$50.25` $\to$ `"fifty dollars and twenty-five cents"`
  - `May 3, 2026` $\to$ `"may third twenty twenty-six"`
  - `Dr. Smith lives on Main St.` $\to$ `"doctor smith lives on main street"`
- **Inverse Text Normalization (ITN):** The reverse process performed during ASR inference: converting raw phonetic
  outputs (`"three hundred kilograms"`) into concise written display text (`"300 kg"`).

Contextual disambiguation presents a major challenge in text normalization: `"2024"` must verbalize as
`"twenty twenty-four"` when representing a calendar year, but as `"two thousand twenty-four"` when representing a
numeric count of physical items.

## Forced alignment

**Forced alignment** is the computational process of aligning an audio recording with its known reference transcript to
determine the exact start and end timestamps of each word and phonetic unit <Cite id="mcauliffe2017" />:

### HMM-based forced alignment (Montreal Forced Aligner / Kaldi)

1. **Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P):** A pronunciation lexicon (such as CMUDict) expands orthographic words into phoneme
   state sequences (e.g., `"speech"` $\to$ `/s/ /p/ /iː/ /tʃ/`).
2. **Hidden Markov Model (HMM) lattice:** Each phoneme is modeled as a 3-state HMM (beginning, middle, end states).
3. **Viterbi trellis search:** An acoustic model computes state likelihoods $P(\mathbf{x}_t \mid s_j)$ across acoustic
   frames $t$. The Viterbi algorithm searches for the optimal global state alignment constrained strictly to the
   sequential word order of the transcript.

### Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) alignment

Modern neural architectures utilize pretrained CTC models (such as Wav2Vec 2.0 or Conformer) <Cite id="graves2006" />.
The network outputs frame-level token emission probabilities $P(\pi_t \mid \mathbf{X})$. A constrained forward-backward
search matches the reference label sequence against the emission matrix, identifying frame timestamps where token
probabilities peak.

### Applications of forced alignment

- **Dataset quality screening:** Utterances where forced alignment fails or yields negative average frame likelihoods
  indicate mismatched audio and transcripts, allowing automated pruning of corrupted training data.
- **Duration modeling in TTS:** Provides ground-truth phone durations $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ required to
  supervise duration predictors in non-autoregressive neural vocoders (FastSpeech 2, VITS).
- **Automated subtitling:** Generates word-level karaoke-style closed captions synchronized with video speech.

## Diarisation labels

In multi-party conversations (podcasts, meetings, medical consultations), annotations must specify not only *what* was
said, but *who spoke when*:

$$
\mathcal{D} = \{ (\text{Speaker}_k, t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}) \}
$$

### Diarization Error Rate (DER)

Speaker diarization accuracy is evaluated via **Diarization Error Rate (DER)**:

$$
\text{DER} = \frac{\text{False Alarm Time} + \text{Missed Speech Time} + \text{Speaker Confusion Time}}{\text{Total Reference Speech Time}}
$$

- **False Alarm:** Detecting speech when the reference transcript indicates silence.
- **Missed Speech:** Labeling silence when a speaker was actively speaking.
- **Speaker Confusion:** Detecting speech correctly but assigning it to the wrong speaker identifier.

### The NIST evaluation collar

In natural conversation, turn-taking boundaries are fluid: human annotators differ by tens of milliseconds when marking
the exact breath onset of a word. 

The standard NIST RT evaluation protocol enforces a forgiveness **collar** (typically $\pm 250\text{ ms}$) around
reference speaker change boundaries. Any discrepancies occurring within this 250 ms window are forgiven, ensuring that
DER measures true speaker identity attribution rather than microsecond boundary jitter.

## Pitfalls

- **Training ASR on non-verbatim transcripts:** Training models on transcripts that omit filler words ("um", "uh")
  forces acoustic losses to suppress genuine speech sounds, elevating substitution and insertion error rates.
- **Lexicon Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) alignment crashes:** In HMM forced alignment, a single unrecognized word (e.g., a
  foreign surname or technical acronym) lacking a G2P entry causes the alignment search to abort across the entire file.
  Robust pipelines employ neural G2P fallbacks.
- **Unsegmented long-form forced alignment drift:** Attempting to force-align a 2-hour podcast in a single pass causes
  cumulative drift and out-of-memory errors. Audio must be pre-segmented via VAD into 10–30 second chunks prior to
  alignment.
- **Neglecting overlapping speech in diarization:** In conversational meetings, $10\text{--}20\%$ of speech occurs
  during overlapping interruptions. Traditional single-label diarizers force an exclusive speaker label, artificially
  inflating missed speech errors on overlapping segments.
