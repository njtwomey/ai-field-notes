<Definition>
**Digital audio** is the discrete-time, discrete-amplitude representation of continuous acoustic pressure waves $p(t)$,
converted into digital data streams through periodic time sampling at rate $f_s$ and amplitude quantization at bit depth
$B$ across one or more spatial channels <Cite id="shannon1949, oppenheim2010" />. In audio machine learning, the choice
of sampling rate, bit depth, channel configuration, and compression codec defines the physical sensory limits of the
pipeline—governing the highest resolvable frequencies, acoustic dynamic range, and fidelity available to downstream
neural architectures.
</Definition>

Unlike image representations—where pixel grids exhibit natural spatial continuity and translation invariance—raw digital
audio signals are high-frequency, one-dimensional time series. A single second of CD-quality audio comprises $44{,}100$
discrete 16-bit voltage samples.

For machine learning models operating on raw waveforms (e.g., WaveNet, SoundStream) or spectral representations (e.g.,
Whisper, AST), understanding digital audio fundamentals—including the Nyquist-Shannon sampling limit, quantization
dynamic range, lossy codec artifacts, and anti-aliasing resampling—is essential to prevent acoustic corruption.

## Sample rate

A continuous acoustic pressure wave $x(t)$ is converted to a discrete-time sequence $x[n] = x(n T_s)$ at fixed sampling
intervals $T_s = 1 / f_s$.

### The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem

The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem states that a continuous, bandlimited signal containing no frequency components
higher than $f_{\max}$ can be uniquely and completely reconstructed from its discrete samples if and only if the
sampling rate satisfies <Cite id="nyquist1928, shannon1949" />:

$$
f_s > 2 f_{\max}
$$

The **Nyquist frequency** $f_N = f_s / 2$ represents the theoretical ceiling of uncorrupted spectral representation. Any
acoustic frequency present in the continuous signal that exceeds $f_N$ folds back into the lower frequency band,
manifesting as **aliasing distortion**:

$$
f_{\text{alias}} = |f_{\text{in}} - k f_s|
$$

If an analog signal containing a $14\text{ kHz}$ harmonic is sampled at $16\text{ kHz}$ ($f_N = 8\text{ kHz}$) without
an analog anti-aliasing filter, the $14\text{ kHz}$ tone aliases into the audible speech spectrum at
$|14 - 16| = 2\text{ kHz}$, corrupting vowel and formant representations.

### Common sampling rate conventions

- **8 kHz (Narrowband Telephony):** Nyquist ceiling at $4\text{ kHz}$. Adequate for speech intelligibility, but cuts off
  unvoiced fricatives ($/s/, /f/, /\theta/$) that reside in the $5\text{--}8\text{ kHz}$ band.
- **16 kHz (Standard Speech ML):** Nyquist ceiling at $8\text{ kHz}$. Captures virtually all human vocal tract formants
  and fricative energy; standard input for models like Whisper, Wav2Vec 2.0, and Conformer.
- **44.1 kHz / 48 kHz (Full-Band Music and Studio Audio):** Nyquist ceiling at $22.05\text{ kHz}$ and $24\text{ kHz}$,
  fully covering the nominal human auditory range ($20\text{ Hz}\text{--}20\text{ kHz}$).

## Bit depth

While sample rate discretizes time, **bit depth** $B$ discretizes amplitude through uniform pulse-code modulation (PCM)
quantization. The continuous voltage range $[-V_{\max}, +V_{\max}]$ is divided into $2^B$ discrete quantization
intervals of width:

$$
\Delta = \frac{2 V_{\max}}{2^B}
$$

### Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio (SQNR)

Assuming quantization error $e[n]$ is uniformly distributed on $[-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]$ and uncorrelated with the input
signal, the theoretical maximum Signal-to-Quantization-Noise Ratio is given by <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />:

$$
\text{SQNR} = 10 \log_{10}\left( \frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{P_{\text{noise}}} \right) \approx 6.02 B + 1.76 \text{ dB}
$$

- **8-bit integer:** Yields $\approx 50\text{ dB}$ of dynamic range, introducing noticeable broadband quantization hiss.
- **16-bit signed integer (PCM16):** Yields $\approx 96\text{ dB}$ of dynamic range, sufficient for clinical and
  consumer applications.
- **24-bit / 32-bit floating point:** Yields $>144\text{ dB}$ (and virtually boundless dynamic headroom with IEEE 754
  32-bit floats), preventing digital clipping during intermediate audio processing and spectrogram computation.

## Channels

The channel dimension $C$ represents the spatial recording configuration:

- **Monaural (Mono, $C=1$):** Single audio track without spatial differentiation. Standard for automatic speech
  recognition (ASR), voice cloning, and bioacoustic classification.
- **Stereophonic (Stereo, $C=2$):** Left and right channels capturing Interaural Time Differences (ITD) and Interaural
  Level Differences (ILD), enabling human perception of lateral soundstage width.
- **Multichannel microphone arrays ($C \ge 4$):** Circular or planar arrays used in smart speakers and teleconferencing
  for spatial beamforming, acoustic source localization, and blind source separation.

When converting stereo audio to mono for speech models, naive averaging ($x_{\text{mono}} = \frac{L + R}{2}$) is
hazardous: if the two channels are $180^\circ$ out of phase due to microphone placement or stereo widening effects,
summing them results in complete destructive **phase cancellation**, rendering the audio silent.

## Codecs and containers

Audio files in machine learning datasets are stored across diverse containers and compression formats:

### Uncompressed and lossless formats

- **WAV (Waveform Audio File Format):** Uncompressed RIFF container encapsulating raw linear PCM samples. Fast,
  zero-overhead disk loading, but large storage footprint.
- **FLAC (Free Lossless Audio Codec):** Lossless compression utilizing linear predictive coding (LPC) and Golomb-Rice
  entropy coding. Reduces file size by $40\text{--}60\%$ with bit-for-bit mathematical reconstruction.

### Lossy perceptual codecs (MP3, AAC, Ogg Vorbis, Opus)

Lossy codecs discard acoustic information deemed psychoacoustically inaudible to human listeners based on simultaneous
frequency masking and temporal masking. 

However, lossy codecs introduce specific artifacts that impair machine learning:
- **High-frequency brickwall truncation:** To meet low bitrates (e.g., 64 kbps), encoders sharply low-pass filter
  frequencies above $12\text{--}16\text{ kHz}$.
- **Pre-echo artifacts:** In MDCT (Modified Discrete Cosine Transform) filterbanks, sharp percussive transients (e.g.,
  castanets, drum rimshots) smear backward in time, creating ringing artifacts prior to acoustic onsets.
- **Phase distortion:** Non-linear phase shifts and spectral quantization noise alter high-resolution acoustic
  embeddings.

## Resampling

In heterogeneous datasets, audio files originate from mixed sources (e.g., 44.1 kHz podcast audio mixed with 8 kHz call
center recordings). Training deep neural models requires standardizing all audio to a uniform sample rate (typically 16
kHz or 24 kHz).

### Sample rate conversion (SRC)

Converting between non-integer sample rate ratios (e.g., $44{,}100\text{ Hz} \to 16{,}000\text{ Hz}$, a ratio of
$160 / 441$) involves:
1. **Interpolation (Upsampling):** Inserting $L-1$ zeros between samples, scaling the rate to $L \cdot f_{s, 1}$.
2. **Anti-aliasing / Anti-imaging low-pass filtering:** Applying a linear-phase polyphase FIR filter with cutoff
   frequency $\min(\pi/L, \pi/M)$.
3. **Decimation (Downsampling):** Retaining every $M$-th sample.

Naive downsampling (subsampling without an anti-aliasing low-pass filter) causes all spectral energy above the new
Nyquist frequency to fold back into the speech band, causing severe degradation in speech recognition models.

## Pitfalls

- **Silent DC offset:** Electrical bias in analog-to-digital converters introduces a non-zero mean ($\bar{x} \ne 0$)
  into the waveform. DC bias distorts RMS energy calculations, skews zero-crossing rates, and causes sharp audible
  clicks when slicing audio segments. DC offsets must be removed via high-pass filtering (cutoff at 10–20 Hz).
- **Destructive stereo downmixing:** Averaging out-of-phase stereo channels destroys audio content through destructive
  interference. Channels must be checked for cross-correlation before mixing, or one channel isolated.
- **Resampling on the fly without anti-aliasing:** Implementing naive indexing (`audio[::step]`) inside PyTorch dataset
  workers causes severe spectral aliasing. Always utilize high-quality polyphase resamplers (e.g.,
  `torchaudio.transforms.Resample`, `librosa.resample` with Kaiser window).
- **Digital clipping from improper normalization:** Boosting waveform gain without peak limiting causes samples to
  exceed the $[-1.0, 1.0]$ range, clipping the peaks into flat square waves and generating severe harmonic distortion.
