<Definition>
**Image preprocessing** is the canonical sequence of spatial resampling, geometric transformation, photometric
normalization, and tiling operations applied to raw digital images to conform them to the input dimensions, aspect
ratios, and numerical distributions expected by computer vision models <Cite id="gonzalez2018" />. Proper preprocessing
prepares arbitrary camera inputs for neural tensor operations while preventing spatial aliasing, aspect ratio
distortion, and numerical gradient instability.
</Definition>

In deep computer vision, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) require rectangular batched
tensor inputs of fixed spatial dimensions (typically $224 \times 224$, $384 \times 384$, or $640 \times 640$). Yet
real-world datasets arrive with heterogeneous resolutions, arbitrary aspect ratios, and raw 8-bit integer ranges
($[0, 255]$).

Standardizing these inputs requires choosing appropriate interpolation filters to prevent downsampling aliasing,
selecting an aspect ratio strategy (cropping, stretching, or letterboxing), standardizing per-channel intensity
distributions, and tiling gigapixel images into overlapping patches.

## Resizing and interpolation

Resizing maps continuous pixel coordinates $(x, y)$ from a source grid of size $H_{\text{src}} \times W_{\text{src}}$ to
a target grid $H_{\text{dst}} \times W_{\text{dst}}$ via mathematical interpolation:

### Interpolation algorithms

- **Nearest neighbor:** Assigns the intensity of the closest discrete source pixel. Extremely fast, but creates jagged,
  pixelated staircasing during upsampling and severe high-frequency aliasing during downsampling.
- **Bilinear interpolation:** Computes a weighted average of the $2 \times 2$ pixel neighborhood enclosing the
  fractional coordinate. Standard choice for general resizing.
- **Bicubic interpolation:** Fits cubic polynomials over a $4 \times 4$ pixel neighborhood. Produces smoother curves and
  sharper edges than bilinear interpolation at modest compute cost.
- **Area (Box) interpolation:** Computes the exact pixel area overlap between destination and source pixels. **Mandatory
  when downsampling by large factors ($K > 2$)**, as it integrates all source pixels within the destination footprint,
  preventing fine lines from vanishing.

### The aliased resizing problem

Parmar, Zhang, and Zhu demonstrated that standard computer vision frameworks (such as OpenCV and older versions of
PyTorch `torch.nn.functional.interpolate`) historically implemented bilinear downsampling without proper anti-aliasing
low-pass filters <Cite id="parmar2022" />.

Without anti-aliasing, downsampling an image by $4\times$ or $8\times$ samples isolated spatial points, folding
high-frequency texture energy back into low-frequency feature maps. This creates severe **moiré interference patterns**
and causes evaluation discrepancies across libraries (e.g., models trained on Pillow failing on OpenCV). Modern
frameworks resolve this by enabling explicit anti-aliasing filters (`antialias=True` in torchvision).

## Cropping, padding and aspect ratio

Real-world images span diverse aspect ratios—from $16:9$ horizontal panoramas to $9:16$ vertical smartphone captures.
Because neural networks typically require fixed-size square tensors, practitioners employ three distinct aspect ratio
strategies:

### 1. Anisotropic squishing (Direct stretching)

The image is resized directly to the target square ($H_{\text{dst}} \times W_{\text{dst}}$) without preserving aspect
ratio.
- **Consequence:** Geometric distortion. Circular objects (such as traffic signs or cell nuclei) deform into vertical or
  horizontal ellipses, altering spatial aspect-ratio priors.

### 2. Center cropping (Rescale and crop)

The image is rescaled uniformly until its shorter edge matches the target size, and the center
$H_{\text{dst}} \times W_{\text{dst}}$ patch is cropped.
- **Consequence:** Preserves natural geometry and object proportions, but **discards peripheral image content**. In a
  $16:9$ landscape image, center-cropping to a square discards $43.7\%$ of the pixels, potentially removing objects
  located near the frame borders.

### 3. Letterbox padding

The image is rescaled uniformly until its longer edge matches the target dimension, and the remaining canvas borders are
filled with a neutral constant value (e.g., gray or black padding):
- **Consequence:** Preserves both geometry and the complete field of view. Standard practice in object detection
  pipelines (such as YOLO) where preserving all peripheral bounding boxes is critical.

## Per-channel normalisation

Raw pixel values are stored as unsigned 8-bit integers ($[0, 255]$). Feeding raw integers into neural networks causes
large activation magnitudes, leading to vanishing or exploding gradients.

### Scaling and standardization

1. **Floating-point scaling:** Divide by $255.0$ to map values to the continuous interval $[0.0, 1.0]$.
2. **Channel-wise standardization:** Subtract channel means $\mu_c$ and divide by standard deviations $\sigma_c$:
   $$
   x_{\text{norm}}[c] = \frac{x[c] - \mu_c}{\sigma_c}
   $$

### ImageNet standard statistics

Models pretrained on ImageNet (Deng et al. 2009 <Cite id="deng2009" />) utilize standardized channel statistics computed
across millions of natural images:

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], \quad \boldsymbol{\sigma} = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]
$$

Standardization centers feature activations around zero, ensuring that input tensors match the weight initialization
distributions assumed by deep neural layers.

## Tiling large images

In specialized domains—such as histopathological Whole Slide Images (WSI, $100{,}000 \times 100{,}000$ pixels) and
satellite orthomosaics ($20{,}000 \times 20{,}000$ pixels)—images cannot be loaded into GPU memory or downsampled whole
without destroying microscopic cellular or architectural details.

Processing gigapixel images requires **patch tiling**:

1. **Tiling grid with overlap:** Slicing the large canvas into discrete tiles (e.g., $512 \times 512$ or
   $1024 \times 1024$ pixels) with an **overlap stride** (typically $20\text{--}50\%$ overlap). Overlap ensures that
   objects split across tile boundaries are captured intact within an adjacent tile.
2. **Padding outer boundaries:** Right and bottom border tiles that exceed the image perimeter are padded with white or
   edge reflection.
3. **Inference and spatial reassembly:**
   - For classification: Tile-level feature vectors are pooled via Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) or attention
     aggregation to predict slide-level diagnoses.
   - For detection: Predicted bounding box coordinates are mapped back to global canvas coordinates, followed by global
     **Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)** across tile seams to merge duplicate detections.

## Pitfalls

- **Downsampling without anti-aliasing:** Resampling fine textures (such as brick walls, fabric weaves, or wire fences)
  using naive bilinear interpolation induces severe moiré patterns that confuse feature extractors. Always enable
  anti-aliasing (`antialias=True`).
- **Asymmetric training-inference normalization:** Training a model with ImageNet mean/std normalization but omitting
  normalization during production serving causes catastrophic inference failure due to distribution shift.
- **Bounding box desynchronization:** Applying cropping, padding, or resizing to training images without simultaneously
  applying the exact geometric affine transformation to annotation coordinates (bounding boxes, segmentation polygons,
  keypoints).
- **Neglecting overlap when tiling:** Extracting non-overlapping tiles from gigapixel images splits cells, pedestrians,
  and vehicles directly along cut lines, creating false false-negative detection errors.
