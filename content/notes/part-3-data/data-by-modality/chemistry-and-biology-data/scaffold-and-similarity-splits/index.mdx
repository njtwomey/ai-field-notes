<Definition>
A **scaffold split** (or **similarity split**) is a non-random dataset partitioning scheme that groups molecules into
disjoint subsets based on their core two-dimensional molecular frameworks (such as Bemis-Murcko scaffolds) or pairwise
fingerprint distance clusters <Cite id="bemis1996" />. Entire structural families are assigned exclusively to either
training, validation, or test partitions, ensuring that test molecules share zero scaffold identity or bounded Tanimoto
similarity with training molecules <Cite id="wu2018moleculenet" />.
</Definition>

In drug discovery, screening libraries are synthesized around medicinal chemistry series (congeneric series) that share
identical core ring architectures with minor peripheral side-chain variations. Standard random splits place close
structural analogues in both train and test partitions, yielding deceptive evaluation metrics that reward memorization
over physical generalization <Cite id="wu2018moleculenet" />.

## Why Random Splits are Optimistic

When training machine learning models on molecular datasets (e.g. ChEMBL, PubChem, Tox21), evaluating performance via
uniform random splitting creates severe **analogue leakage** <Cite id="wu2018moleculenet" />:
- **Congeneric Series Memorization**: If a dataset contains 50 analogues of an active kinase inhibitor bearing a shared
  pyrimidine-imidazole core, a random $80/20$ split places approximately 40 in the training set and 10 in the test set.
  The model merely needs to recognize the core scaffold to predict binding affinity accurately.
- **The Optimism Gap**: Benchmark studies across MoleculeNet <Cite id="wu2018moleculenet" /> show that models achieving
  $> 92\%$ ROC-AUC under random splitting often drop below $65\text{--}72\%$ ROC-AUC under scaffold splits. In
  real-world prospective drug discovery, medicinal chemists seek novel chemotypes (scaffold hopping) rather than trivial
  side-chain modifications of known patents; random splits completely fail to evaluate this prospective capability.

## Bemis-Murcko Scaffold Splits

Bemis and Murcko <Cite id="bemis1996" /> proposed decomposing molecular graphs into structural primitives:

$$
\mathcal{M} = \text{Rings} \cup \text{Linkers} \cup \text{SideChains}
$$

### Scaffold Extraction

1. **Pruning Side Chains**: All acyclic terminal atoms and non-ring bonds connected to a single ring atom are
   iteratively pruned.
2. **Murcko Framework**: The remaining connected subgraph consisting of all ring systems and the direct linkers
   connecting them defines the **Bemis-Murcko scaffold** $S(\mathcal{M})$:
   - *Example*: Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) decomposes into an empty linker with an ester side chain and a benzene
     ring, yielding a pure benzene scaffold $c1ccccc1$.
   - *Example*: Imatinib retains its central multi-ring core of pyrimidine, pyridine, benzene, and piperazine rings,
     stripping terminal methyl groups.
3. **Generic Scaffolds**: To test topological abstraction invariant to heteroatoms, generic (Bemis-Murcko skeleton)
   frameworks convert all heteroatoms to carbon and all bond orders to single bonds.

### Greedy Partitioning Algorithm

To construct train ($80\%$), validation ($10\%$), and test ($10\%$) splits:
1. Extract scaffold $s_i = S(m_i)$ for every molecule in the dataset $\mathcal{D}$.
2. Group molecules into scaffold clusters $\mathcal{C}_s = \{m \in \mathcal{D} \mid S(m) = s\}$.
3. Sort clusters by size in descending order:
   $|\mathcal{C}_{s_{(1)}}| \ge |\mathcal{C}_{s_{(2)}}| \ge \dots \ge |\mathcal{C}_{s_{(K)}}|$.
4. Greedily allocate the largest scaffold clusters to the training set until its target quota (e.g. $80\%$ of
   $|\mathcal{D}|$) is satisfied.
5. Allocate remaining clusters to validation and test partitions.

Sorting in descending order ensures that common core chemotypes are well-represented in the training set, while
validation and test sets evaluate generalization across rarer, structurally distinct chemotypes
<Cite id="wu2018moleculenet" />.

## Similarity Clustering (Sphere Exclusion and Butina)

While Bemis-Murcko frameworks operate on discrete 2D ring graphs, **similarity clustering** partitions molecules based
on pairwise distance in continuous or hashed chemical fingerprint space <Cite id="bemis1996, wu2018moleculenet" />.

### Butina Clustering

Using Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP4) and Tanimoto distance
$d_T(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = 1 - T(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})$:
1. Compute pairwise distance matrix $\mathbf{D} \in [0, 1]^{N \times N}$.
2. For a chosen distance threshold $r_{\text{cutoff}}$ (typically $r_{\text{cutoff}} \in [0.3, 0.5]$), compute the
   neighborhood size for each molecule:
   $$
   N_i = \left| \left\{ j \;\middle|\; d_T(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \le r_{\text{cutoff}} \right\} \right|
   $$
3. Sort molecules by decreasing neighborhood size. The molecule with the most neighbors becomes the centroid of cluster
   1, absorbing all its neighbors within radius $r_{\text{cutoff}}$.
4. Remove clustered molecules and repeat until all molecules are assigned to clusters or singletons.
5. Entire clusters are assigned to either train, validation, or test sets.

This guarantees that:

$$
\max_{m_{\text{test}} \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}, \, m_{\text{train}} \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} T(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\text{test}}}, \mathbf{x}_{m_{\text{train}}}) < 1 - r_{\text{cutoff}}
$$

strictly bounding maximum training-test chemical similarity.

## Pitfalls

1. **The Super-Scaffold Problem**: In many commercial chemical libraries, a single dominant core (such as benzene or
   phenyl-linked scaffolds) accounts for $30\text{--}50\%$ of the entire library. A single giant cluster cannot fit into
   validation ($10\%$) or test ($10\%$) partitions without severely violating the desired split ratios.
2. **Out of Applicability Domain (Overly Pessimistic Test Sets)**: Holding out drastically different scaffolds can
   result in test sets where test molecules lie completely outside the training applicability domain (e.g. zero overlap
   in essential pharmacophores), causing test loss to reflect impossible out-of-distribution extrapolation rather than
   useful generalization.
3. **Acyclic Molecule Degeneracy**: Acyclic aliphatic molecules (such as linear alkanes, polyethylene glycols, or fatty
   acids) contain zero ring systems. Under Bemis-Murcko decomposition, their scaffold is the empty graph $\emptyset$.
   All acyclic compounds collapse into a single monolithic cluster unless handled by separate open-chain decomposition
   rules.
