<Definition>
**Time-series augmentation** enlarges a training set of series $\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$ with labels $y$ by adding
transformed copies $(g(\xvec), y)$, where each transform $g$ is drawn at random from a family chosen so that the label
of $g(\xvec)$ is still $y$. It is <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink> for series: it encodes
the invariances a model should have, such as to small noise, to the amplitude of a movement or to a slight change of
speed.
</Definition>

## When to use it

Labelled time-series datasets are often small: a few hundred recordings of patients, a few subjects in an
activity-recognition study, the hundreds of series in many benchmark classification datasets. Deep networks trained on
so few examples overfit. Augmentation helps most when the training set is small and when the variation it simulates is
real: different people move at different speeds and strengths, sensors sit at different angles, recordings carry
different noise.

Augmentation is mostly used for classification. For forecasting, a transform must keep the relation between the input
window and the target, so it is applied to the window and its target together, and transforms that change the dynamics
are avoided.

## Transforms of values

Each transform below acts on a series $x_t$, $t = 1, \dots, T$, with random parameters drawn afresh for each copy.

- **Jitter.** Add independent noise: $x'_t = x_t + \varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. It
  simulates sensor noise. Too large a $\sigma$ hides the pattern that defines the class.
- **Scaling.** Multiply by one random factor: $x'_t = a\,x_t$ with $a \sim \Gauss(1, \sigma^2)$. It simulates
  stronger or weaker versions of the same movement or event.
- **Magnitude warping.** Multiply by a smooth random curve: $x'_t = c(t)\,x_t$, where $c$ is a cubic spline through a
  few knots whose values are drawn around 1 <Cite id="um2017" />. Different parts of the series are scaled
  differently.
- **Rotation.** For a three-axis sensor with readings $\xvec_t \in \reals^3$, apply one random rotation
  $\xvec'_t = \Rmat\,\xvec_t$ to every reading. It simulates the same device worn at a different angle
  <Cite id="um2017" />; see <NoteLink to="wearable-sensor-data">wearable sensor data</NoteLink>.

## Transforms of time

- **Time warping.** Resample the series along a smooth, increasing random map of time:
  $x'_t = x(\tau(t))$, with $\tau$ a smooth curve close to the identity and values between samples found by
  interpolation <Cite id="um2017" />. Parts of the series play faster and others slower, as when one person walks with
  a slightly different rhythm.
- **Window slicing.** Crop a random window covering a fixed share of the series, such as 90%, and stretch it back to
  the full length <Cite id="leguennec2016" />. At test time, the prediction can be the vote over several slices.
- **Window warping.** Speed up or slow down one random window, for example by a factor of 2 or of 1/2, and keep the
  rest <Cite id="leguennec2016" />.
- **Permutation.** Cut the series into a few segments and shuffle them <Cite id="um2017" />. It suits labels that
  depend on what happens, not on the order: a motor state of a patient, a type of machine fault. It destroys the label
  of anything defined by order.

## Mixing and generating series

- **Mixing.** Combine two series of one class, or interpolate two series and their labels as mixup does for images;
  see <NoteLink to="image-augmentation">image augmentation</NoteLink>. Averaging series after aligning them with
  <NoteLink to="dynamic-time-warping">dynamic time warping</NoteLink> keeps their shared shape where a plain average
  would blur it.
- **Frequency-domain changes.** Perturb the amplitudes or phases of the series' Fourier components, or mask frequency
  bands; for audio, see <NoteLink to="spectrogram-augmentation">spectrogram augmentation</NoteLink>.
- **Decomposition.** Split a series into trend, season and remainder, perturb or bootstrap the remainder, and
  recombine.
- **Generative models.** Sample new series from a model fitted to the training data. The samples are only as varied
  and as correct as the model.

Surveys group the methods into these families: transformations, mixing, decomposition and generative models
<Cite id="iwana2021" />, <Cite id="wen2021augmentation" />.

## What the evidence says

- Augmentation designed around the variation in the data can help a lot. On wearable-sensor recordings of patients
  with Parkinson's disease, suitable augmentation raised a convolutional network's accuracy for classifying motor state
  from 77.5% to 86.9% <Cite id="um2017" />.
- No method helps everywhere. An evaluation of 12 methods on 128 classification datasets with six network types found
  dramatic differences between architectures <Cite id="iwana2021" />. Window slicing and window warping tended to help,
  and window warping ranked best on average for three of the networks. Rotation, permutation and time warping reduced
  accuracy on many of these general-purpose datasets, whose labels often depend on orientation, order or timing. Gains
  were larger for smaller training sets.

## Choosing transforms

1. List what the label depends on: amplitude, timing, order, frequency, orientation.
2. Keep only transforms that leave those properties alone. Scaling is safe for an activity and unsafe for a fall
   detector that thresholds the impact; permutation is safe for a fault type and unsafe for a gesture.
3. Set each transform's strength so that a person looking at the result would still give it the same label.
4. Tune the choice and strength on validation data, as with any hyperparameter.

## Pitfalls

- **Augmenting before splitting.** Copies of a test series in the training set leak it; augment the training part
  only.
- **Augmenting validation or test data.** Scores then describe the augmented distribution, not the real one; test-time
  augmentation, where predictions are averaged over copies, is a separate and deliberate choice.
- **Transforms that change the label.** A rotation applied to data whose orientation defines the class, or a time warp
  applied to a heart rhythm whose rate defines it, teaches the model the wrong invariance.
- **Overlapping windows.** Augmenting windows that overlap multiplies near-duplicates; see
  <NoteLink to="time-series-windowing">windowing</NoteLink>.
