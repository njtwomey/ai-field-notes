<Definition>
**Normalising** a time series maps its values $y_t$ to $\tilde y_t = (y_t - m)/s$ with a location $m$ and a scale
$s > 0$. The statistics can be computed once for the whole dataset (**global**), once for each series (**per series**)
or afresh for each input window (**per window**, or **instance normalisation**). Forecasts made on the normalised
scale are mapped back by $\hat y = m + s\,\hat{\tilde y}$, so the transform must be invertible and its statistics known
at prediction time.
</Definition>

## When to use it

Normalising matters most for **global models**: one model trained on many series, such as a recurrent or transformer
forecaster across thousands of products; see <NoteLink to="forecasting-setups">forecasting setups</NoteLink>. Without
it, two things go wrong.

- **The loss is dominated by the large series.** A squared error of 10 units is negligible for a store selling 1000
  items a week and is everything for one selling 12. Training on raw values fits the large series and ignores the
  rest.
- **Inputs leave the range the network handles well.** <NoteLink to="activation-functions">Activation
  functions</NoteLink> saturate and initialisations assume inputs of order 1.

A **local model**, fitted to one series at a time, such as an
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-integrated-moving-average-model">ARIMA model</NoteLink>, needs no normalisation across
series, but it still benefits from the variance-stabilising transforms below.

## Global scaling

Global scaling pools all series and computes one mean and one standard deviation. It keeps the series' relative sizes,
which is right when size is information the model should use, and useless when sizes differ by orders of magnitude:
the small series stay flat at one end of the range.

## Per-series scaling

Per-series scaling gives each series $i$ its own location $m_i$ and scale $s_i$, computed from its training stretch.
Every series then has values of order 1 and the loss weighs them evenly.

Choices of $s_i$ include the standard deviation, the mean absolute value and the mean. DeepAR divides each series'
inputs by $\nu_i = 1 + \frac{1}{t_0}\sum_{t=1}^{t_0} z_{i,t}$, one plus the average of its first $t_0$ values, and
multiplies the predicted distribution's parameters back <Cite id="salinas2020" />; see
<NoteLink to="deepar">DeepAR</NoteLink>. The added 1 keeps the scale positive for a series that is all zeros.

## Per-window scaling and reversible instance normalisation

Per-window scaling computes the statistics from the input window alone: the last $L$ values before the forecast
origin. Each window then starts at the same level and scale, whatever the trend has done since training. **Reversible
instance normalisation** (RevIN) applies this inside a forecasting network <Cite id="kim2022revin" />.

1. Normalise the input window $\xvec \in \reals^L$ by its own mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$:
   $\tilde x_t = \gamma (x_t - \mu)/\sigma + \beta$, with a learned scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$ per variable.
2. Run the network on $\tilde\xvec$ to produce $\tilde\yvec$.
3. Map the output back with the same statistics: $\hat y_t = \mu + \sigma(\tilde y_t - \beta)/\gamma$.

The network then models the shape of the series and leaves its level and scale to the statistics of the window. This
removes much of the shift in distribution between training and test periods that trends and level changes cause.
Long-horizon forecasters such as <Gloss name="patchtst" /> use it <Cite id="nie2023" />. Deep adaptive input
normalisation learns the shift and scale as functions of the window instead of using its mean and standard deviation
<Cite id="passalis2020" />.

The price is that the model cannot use the level itself. A series whose behaviour depends on its absolute size, such
as demand that saturates at a capacity, loses that information unless the window's statistics are also given to the
model as inputs.

## Detrending and deseasonalising

Normalisation by $m$ and $s$ removes a level and a scale. Trends and seasons are removed by other transforms, applied
before the model and inverted after it.

- **Differencing** replaces $y_t$ by $y_t - y_{t-1}$, or by $y_t - y_{t-P}$ for a season of period $P$; see
  <NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">differencing and unit roots</NoteLink>.
- **Decomposition** subtracts an estimated trend and seasonal component and models the remainder; see
  <NoteLink to="time-series-decomposition">time-series decomposition</NoteLink>.

Whether to deseasonalise depends on the dataset. In an empirical study of recurrent forecasters, the networks modelled
seasonality directly when all series had similar seasonal patterns, and did better after deseasonalisation when the
patterns differed <Cite id="hewamalage2021" />.

## Variance-stabilising transforms

Many series have a variance that grows with their level: sales, traffic, counts. A logarithm, or a Box-Cox transform,
makes the variance roughly constant before scaling; see <NoteLink to="power-transforms">power transforms</NoteLink>.
For counts that include zeros, $\log(1 + y)$ is the usual choice.

A forecast of the mean on the log scale, mapped back by $\exp$, is a forecast of the median, not of the mean, when
the errors on the log scale are symmetric; see
<NoteLink to="encoding-targets">encoding targets</NoteLink>.

## Statistics from the past only

Every statistic used to normalise an input must be available when the forecast is made.

- Per-series statistics come from the training stretch, never from the whole series; the test stretch would otherwise
  shape its own inputs.
- Per-window statistics come from the input window, which ends at the forecast origin. Including the values to be
  forecast in the window's statistics is a <NoteLink to="data-leakage">leak</NoteLink>.
- Normalising before splitting into training and test stretches leaks the test period's level and spread into
  training.

## Pitfalls

- **Constant or all-zero windows.** A window with $\sigma = 0$ cannot be divided by its standard deviation; add a small
  constant, or use a scale such as DeepAR's $1 + $ mean.
- **Intermittent series.** Series that are mostly zeros have unstable per-window statistics; a scale from a longer
  history is steadier.
- **Metrics on the wrong scale.** Errors reported on normalised values are not errors in the original units; report
  both, or use a scale-free metric such as the
  <NoteLink to="scaled-errors-and-mean-absolute-scaled-error">mean absolute scaled error</NoteLink>.
- **Forgetting to invert.** Every transform applied to the target, in order, must be inverted on the forecasts in the
  reverse order.
