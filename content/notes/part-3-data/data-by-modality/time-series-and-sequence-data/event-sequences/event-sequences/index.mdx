<Definition>
An **event sequence** is a list of events $(t_1, m_1), (t_2, m_2), \dots, (t_n, m_n)$ observed over a period
$[0, T]$, with times $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n$ and **marks** $m_i$ that say what happened: an event type, a user, an
amount, a line of a log. Unlike a sampled time series, the times themselves are data: when and how often events occur
is part of what a model must describe.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A sensor is read on a clock; events happen when they happen. A web session is a burst of clicks followed by silence; a
server writes a flood of log lines during an incident and few otherwise; a customer buys on a rhythm of their own.
Turning such records into model inputs means deciding what to keep of the timing. Three representations cover most
practice: counts per interval, which give up the exact times; sequences of tokens with the gaps between them, which
keep them as features; and point processes, which model the times directly.

## Counts per interval

Binning counts the events of each type in intervals of width $\Delta$, which gives an ordinary regularly sampled
series: clicks per minute, transactions per day, errors per hour. Every time-series method then applies.

The width $\Delta$ trades detail against noise. Narrow bins are mostly zeros and the counts are dominated by chance;
wide bins merge separate bursts and hide their order. Counts of rare events in narrow bins are better modelled as
counts, with a <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution">Poisson</NoteLink> or negative binomial likelihood, than as
continuous values.

## Tokens and gaps

A sequence model can read the events one by one. Each event becomes a token for its mark, an embedding of its type, and
the **inter-event time** $\tau_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$ enters as a feature, often as $\log \tau_i$ because gaps range over
orders of magnitude. The model predicts the next mark, the next gap, or a label for the whole sequence.

This is how sessions of clicks feed <NoteLink to="sequential-recommendation-models">sequential recommenders</NoteLink>,
how log lines feed anomaly detectors once each line is reduced to a template, and how patient records feed clinical
models.

## Point processes

A **temporal point process** models the times themselves. Its **conditional intensity**

$$
\lambda^*(t) = \lim_{\delta \to 0} \frac{\Pr(\text{an event in } [t, t + \delta) \mid \mathcal{H}_t)}{\delta}
$$

is the instantaneous rate of events given the history $\mathcal{H}_t$ of events before $t$. The log-likelihood of a
sequence observed on $[0, T]$ is

$$
\log p(t_1, \dots, t_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \lambda^*(t_i) - \int_0^T \lambda^*(t)\,dt .
$$

The first term rewards a high rate where events happened; the second penalises a high rate everywhere else.

- A **<NoteLink to="poisson-process">Poisson process</NoteLink>** has a rate that does not depend on the history; with
  constant rate $\lambda$ the gaps are independent <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential</NoteLink>
  variables with mean $1/\lambda$.
- A **Hawkes process** is self-exciting: each event raises the rate for a while,
  $\lambda^*(t) = \mu + \sum_{t_i < t} \alpha e^{-\beta (t - t_i)}$, with background rate $\mu > 0$, jump $\alpha \ge 0$
  and decay $\beta > 0$ <Cite id="hawkes1971" />. Events arrive in clusters, as aftershocks, retweets and cascading
  failures do. The process stays stable while $\alpha / \beta < 1$, the expected number of events each event triggers.
- **Neural point processes** compute the intensity from a learned summary of the history. A
  <NoteLink to="recurrent-neural-network">recurrent network</NoteLink> reads each event's mark and gap and outputs the
  intensity of the next event and the distribution of its mark <Cite id="du2016" />; the neural Hawkes process lets the
  network's state decay continuously between events <Cite id="mei2017" />. A review compares the designs
  <Cite id="shchur2021" />.

## Features from events

For a tabular model, an entity's events up to the prediction time become features.

- **Counts and rates** of each event type over several <NoteLink to="forecasting-setups">look-back windows</NoteLink>:
  the last hour, day and month.
- **Recency**: the time since the last event of each type.
- **Regularity**: the mean and spread of the gaps, or the share of events at each hour of the day.
- **Sequence features**: the last few event types, or counts of short patterns of types.

These are the event versions of the rolling statistics in
<NoteLink to="time-series-feature-extraction">feature extraction</NoteLink> and the calendar features in
<NoteLink to="date-and-time-features">date and time features</NoteLink>.

## The observation window

Every sequence is seen through a window $[0, T]$. Events before the start, and the next event after the end, are not
seen. The gap after the last observed event is **censored**: it is at least $T - t_n$, not equal to it. Treating it as
complete makes entities look more active than they are; it is the setting of
<NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival analysis</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Ties and clock resolution.** Events logged to the second, or by different machines, arrive with equal or
  misordered times. Decide an order rule, and do not read zero gaps as simultaneity.
- **Events after the prediction time.** Features of an entity must be computed from events before the time of each
  prediction, not from the whole log.
- **Logging changes.** A new logging library, a new event type or a change in what is recorded shifts every count;
  check the counts over time before modelling them.
- **Activity bias.** Highly active entities contribute most events; per-event metrics describe them, and per-entity
  metrics are needed to describe a typical one.
