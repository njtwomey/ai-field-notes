<Definition>
**Time-series feature extraction** maps a series, or a window $x_1, \dots, x_L$ of one, to a vector
$\phivec(\xvec) \in \reals^d$ of summary statistics. The vector replaces the raw values as input to a model that
expects fixed-length inputs, such as a gradient-boosted tree ensemble or a linear classifier. The features are chosen
by hand, taken from a library, or generated at random.
</Definition>

## When to use it

- **Classifying or clustering series** of different lengths, or windows of a stream, with a tabular model.
- **Forecasting with a tabular model**, where each row holds the recent past of a series as features and the value to
  forecast as the target; see <NoteLink to="forecasting-setups">forecasting setups</NoteLink>.
- **Describing many series** at once, to group them, to find unusual ones or to choose a forecasting method per series
  <Cite id="monteromanso2020" />.

Learned representations from deep networks compete with extracted features on large datasets. On small and medium
datasets, and where each feature must be explained, extracted features remain strong.

## Lags

A **lag feature** is a past value: $x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \dots, x_{t-p}$ as inputs for predicting at time $t$. Seasonal
lags $x_{t-P}, x_{t-2P}$ for a season of period $P$ carry the same point of the previous cycles. A model given $p$ lags
is a nonlinear autoregression of order $p$.

For a forecast $h$ steps ahead, only values up to the forecast origin are known. A lag feature for target
$x_{t+h}$ must be at least $h$ steps old; using $x_{t+h-1}$ when forecasting $h > 1$ steps ahead is a
<NoteLink to="data-leakage">leak</NoteLink> that shows up only at prediction time.

## Rolling and expanding statistics

A **rolling statistic** summarises the last $m$ values: the rolling mean $\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1} x_{t-j}$, the
rolling standard deviation, minimum, maximum, quantiles and counts of events. An **expanding statistic** summarises
every value since the start. Several <NoteLink to="time-series-windowing">window lengths</NoteLink> at once, such as a
week, a month and a year, give the model several time scales.

The window must end at the present. A centred window, the default of some libraries' smoothing functions, uses future
values.

## Autocorrelation features

The <NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation">autocorrelation</NoteLink> at lag $k$ measures how much
the series resembles itself $k$ steps earlier. The first few autocorrelations, the lag of the first zero crossing, and
the height and lag of the largest seasonal peak describe how persistent and how periodic a series is. Partial
autocorrelations add how many past values matter directly.

## Spectral features

The <NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink> distributes the series' variance over frequencies. Features from
it include the dominant frequency, the share of power in chosen bands, the spectral centroid and the spectral entropy,
which is low for a series dominated by one frequency and high for noise. Audio uses the same family, computed per frame;
see <NoteLink to="spectral-features">spectral features</NoteLink>.

## Shape and symbolic features

Some classes differ by a local shape rather than by a statistic of the whole series: a notch in a heartbeat, a spike
in a vibration.

- **Shapelets** are short subsequences whose distance to the nearest match in a series separates classes; the
  <NoteLink to="shapelet-transform">shapelet transform</NoteLink> turns those distances into features.
- **Symbolic representations** such as <NoteLink to="symbolic-aggregate-approximation">SAX</NoteLink> turn a series
  into a word, whose counts of subwords become features.
- **Motif and discord distances** from the <NoteLink to="matrix-profile">matrix profile</NoteLink> describe how
  repetitive or unusual each part of a series is.

## Feature libraries

Libraries compute hundreds or thousands of features at once.

- **hctsa** collects thousands of features from across the time-series literature, covering correlation structure,
  distributions, entropy, stationarity and fits of time-series models, and selects among them per task
  <Cite id="fulcher2014" />.
- **catch22** is a set of 22 features chosen from a filtered set of 4791 hctsa features to classify well across 93
  datasets with little redundancy. It cuts computation about 1000-fold for an average loss of 7% in accuracy
  <Cite id="lubba2019" />.
- **tsfresh** computes several hundred features and keeps those whose relation to the target passes a hypothesis test,
  with the <Gloss name="fdr">false discovery rate</Gloss> controlled across features <Cite id="christ2018" />.

A large library needs <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink> or a model that tolerates many
redundant inputs.

## Random convolutional kernels

ROCKET convolves each series with 10 000 random kernels, of random length, weights, bias, dilation and padding, and
keeps two features per kernel: the maximum response and the proportion of positive responses. A linear classifier on
these 20 000 features matched the most accurate time-series classifiers of its time on the UCR archive at a small
fraction of their training cost <Cite id="dempster2020" />. The kernels are not trained; the dilations let them respond
to patterns at many time scales.

## Pitfalls

- **Future values in features.** Centred windows, statistics over the whole series, and lags shorter than the horizon
  all use values unknown at prediction time.
- **Statistics across the split.** Features normalised or ranked over all series, training and test together, carry
  test information into training.
- **Windows of different lengths.** Many features depend on the number of values; compare features computed over
  windows of the same length, or use length-normalised versions.
- **Selecting features on the test set.** Feature selection is part of the model and belongs inside cross-validation.
