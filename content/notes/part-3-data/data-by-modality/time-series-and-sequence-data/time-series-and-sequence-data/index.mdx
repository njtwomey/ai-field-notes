<Definition>
A **time series** is a sequence of measurements indexed by time, at regular or irregular intervals, on one or more
channels. An **event sequence** records discrete events at irregular times. In both, order carries information,
neighbouring values are correlated, and the future must never inform a prediction about the past. These three facts
set every preparation step in the chapter.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Sensors, markets, servers, patients and shoppers all produce series. Forecasting demand, recognising an activity from
an accelerometer, detecting a seizure in an electroencephalogram and predicting the next event in a log all start from
the same raw material: timestamped values with their own clocks, rates, gaps and scales. Before a model sees them, the
values are put on a common time grid, cut into examples, scaled and summarised. Each step can leak future information if
done carelessly, and a leak in a series is easy to make and hard to see.

## Main ideas

**Fix the time axis first.** <NoteLink to="time-series-sampling-and-alignment" /> fixes timestamps and time zones,
resamples irregular series to a grid by aggregation or interpolation, and aligns series recorded on different clocks.
<NoteLink to="time-series-gaps" /> fills gaps forward, interpolates or marks them, and bounds how stale a filled value
may be. A backward fill or a centred interpolation uses future values.

**Cut long series into examples.** <NoteLink to="time-series-windowing" /> sets a window length and stride, assigns a
label to each window and decides what to do with windows that straddle a change of label. Overlapping windows must not
straddle the <NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">train-test split</NoteLink>.

**Scale per series, from the past.** <NoteLink to="series-normalisation" /> normalises globally, per series or per
window, using statistics from the past only and a transform that forecasts can be mapped back through.

**Summarise or set up for forecasting.** <NoteLink to="time-series-feature-extraction" /> turns a window into lags,
rolling statistics, autocorrelations, spectral and shape features, feature sets such as catch22 and tsfresh
<Cite id="christ2018" />, or random convolutional kernels. <NoteLink to="forecasting-setups" /> fixes the look-back,
horizon, multi-step strategy, local or global model and known covariates <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.
<NoteLink to="look-ahead-bias" /> and <NoteLink to="intermittent-and-censored-series" /> treat the two commonest traps
in forecasting data.

**Augment within the label's invariances.** <NoteLink to="time-series-augmentation" /> adds noise, scales, warps,
slices, rotates sensor axes and mixes series, keeping only changes the label is invariant to.

**Events and bodies have their own conventions.** <NoteLink to="event-sequences" /> treats irregular events as tokens,
counts or point processes. <NoteLink to="wearable-sensor-data" /> covers calibration, orientation, gravity removal,
fusion and the activity-recognition chain, tested on new people <Cite id="bulling2014" />.
<NoteLink to="physiological-signals" /> covers filtering, artefact rejection, lead and montage conventions and
subject-wise splits.

## How the notes fit together

The eight sections follow a pipeline: sampling and alignment, windowing, normalisation, feature extraction, forecasting
setups and augmentation. Start with <NoteLink to="time-series-sampling-and-alignment" />, then
<NoteLink to="time-series-windowing" />. Normalisation and feature extraction can be read in either order. The
forecasting section assumes normalisation. The last two sections, event sequences and wearable, sensor and physiological
data, are applications that draw on the earlier ones.
