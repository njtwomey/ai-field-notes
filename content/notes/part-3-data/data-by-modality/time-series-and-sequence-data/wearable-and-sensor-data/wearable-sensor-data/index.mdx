<Definition>
**Wearable sensors** are worn on the body: accelerometers, gyroscopes and magnetometers, together an inertial
measurement unit (IMU), and physiological sensors such as optical heart-rate sensors. **Ambient sensors** are fixed in
the environment: motion detectors, door contacts, power meters, environmental sensors and radio receivers that locate
a wearable. Both produce multichannel streams that must be calibrated, oriented, filtered, aligned and fused before a
model can learn from them, most often to recognise what a person is doing <Cite id="bulling2014" />,
<Cite id="lara2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A wrist accelerometer does not record "walking". It records three numbers fifty or a hundred times a second: gravity,
projected onto axes that turn with the wrist, plus the acceleration of the arm, plus noise, in units that drift with
temperature. Every property of the person that a model should ignore, such as how the device sits on the wrist, which
wrist it is on, how strongly the person moves, is mixed into the same numbers as the activity. Preparation removes as
much of that nuisance variation as can be removed safely, and evaluation checks that the model learned the activity
and not the person.

## Sensors and channels

- **Accelerometers** measure specific force along three axes, in units of $g \approx 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. At
  rest they read gravity: a vector of length 1 $g$ pointing up in the device's frame. They saturate at their range,
  often $\pm 8\,g$, which hard impacts such as falls can exceed.
- **Gyroscopes** measure angular velocity in degrees or radians per second, and drift when integrated.
- **Magnetometers** measure the local magnetic field, which gives a heading but is disturbed by metal and electronics
  indoors.
- **Physiological sensors** measure heart rate, skin temperature or electrodermal activity, with artefacts whenever the
  wearer moves.
- **Ambient sensors** produce events rather than samples: a motion sensor fires, a door opens. They are
  <NoteLink to="event-sequences">event sequences</NoteLink> and are often binned or turned into
  <NoteLink to="date-and-time-features">recency features</NoteLink>.

Human movement is slow by signal-processing standards. One widely used smartphone dataset recorded at 50 Hz, on the
grounds that 99% of the energy of body motion lies below 15 Hz <Cite id="anguita2013" />.

## Calibration

Low-cost accelerometers have per-axis offsets and gains that differ from device to device and change with
temperature. At rest, the magnitude of a calibrated accelerometer's reading is 1 $g$ in any orientation. Free-living
recordings contain many still periods, and fitting an offset and a gain per axis so that their magnitudes are 1 $g$
calibrates a device from its own data, without a laboratory procedure <Cite id="vanhees2014" />.

## Orientation and placement

The same movement gives different readings when the device is rotated: worn on the other wrist, upside down, or in a
different pocket. Three responses are common.

- **Orientation-invariant features.** The magnitude $\norm{\avec_t}$ of the acceleration vector $\avec_t \in \reals^3$
  does not depend on orientation. It loses the direction of movement and the posture that gravity's direction reveals.
- **Rotation into a common frame.** Combining accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer readings in a complementary or
  <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> estimates the device's orientation, and rotating the readings
  into an Earth-fixed frame removes it.
- **Rotation as augmentation.** Training on randomly rotated copies teaches a model invariance to orientation; see
  <NoteLink to="time-series-augmentation">time-series augmentation</NoteLink>.

Placement matters more than orientation. A model trained on a hip-worn device rarely works on a wrist-worn one, because
the wrist moves in ways the hip does not.

## Gravity removal and filtering

An accelerometer reading is the sum of gravity, which changes only as the device turns, and the acceleration of the
body. A low-pass filter with a cut-off well below the frequencies of movement estimates the gravity component, and the
remainder is the body acceleration. In the smartphone dataset above, a median filter and a low-pass filter at 20 Hz
first removed noise, and a Butterworth low-pass filter at 0.3 Hz then separated gravity from body acceleration
<Cite id="anguita2013" />; see <NoteLink to="median-filter">median filter</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="infinite-impulse-response-filters">IIR filters</NoteLink>. The gravity component is kept as its own
channel, because its direction gives the posture: lying, sitting or standing.

Offline, filters can run forwards and backwards to avoid shifting the signal in time; see
<NoteLink to="zero-phase-filtering">zero-phase filtering</NoteLink>. A model that will run in real time must be trained
on causally filtered signals, as it will see them.

## Sensor fusion

Several sensors on one person, or a person in an instrumented home, must first share one timeline; see
<NoteLink to="time-series-sampling-and-alignment">sampling and alignment</NoteLink>. They can then be combined at three
levels.

- **<NoteLink to="multimodal-data-layouts">Early fusion</NoteLink>** concatenates the aligned channels into one input.
- **Feature fusion** computes features per sensor and concatenates them, which tolerates different sampling rates.
- **Decision fusion** trains a model per sensor and combines their predictions, which tolerates a sensor that is
  missing or broken.

The SPHERE challenge asked for <NoteLink to="smart-home-activity-discovery">activities of daily living</NoteLink> in a
home to be recognised from a wrist-worn accelerometer, video and environmental sensors together
<Cite id="twomey2016sphere" />. Ambient sensors in homes differ from home to home, which makes transfer between homes
its own problem; see <NoteLink to="context-modulation-and-sensor-transfer">context modulation and sensor
topologies</NoteLink> <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" />.

## The activity-recognition chain

1. **Windows.** Cut the aligned streams into windows of 1 to 2 seconds for short activities such as walking or
   climbing stairs <Cite id="banos2014" />, longer for activities such as cooking; see
   <NoteLink to="time-series-windowing">windowing</NoteLink>.
2. **Features or an encoder.** Compute statistics and <NoteLink to="spectral-features">spectral features</NoteLink> per
   window and channel, or let a convolutional or recurrent network learn them <Cite id="hammerla2016" />; see
   <NoteLink to="time-series-feature-extraction">feature extraction</NoteLink>.
3. **Classification.** Predict an activity per window.
4. **Smoothing.** Activities last longer than a window, so neighbouring predictions should mostly agree. A
   <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov model</Gloss> or a <Gloss name="crf">conditional random field</Gloss> over the window
   predictions enforces this; whether such structure modelling helps depends on how much context the features already
   carry <Cite id="twomey2016" />; see <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed">when is structure needed</NoteLink>.

## Subjects, settings and splits

People differ in how they move far more than windows of one person differ. Two consequences follow.

- **Hold out people.** The <NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">test set</NoteLink> must contain people
  absent from training whenever the model will be used on new people. Splitting windows at random, or by session within
  each person, measures how well the model recognises people it has seen, and with overlapping windows it also leaks
  near-copies of test windows <Cite id="dehghani2019" />.
- **Laboratory versus free living.** Activities performed on instruction in a laboratory are cleaner and less varied
  than those of daily life, and models trained on them may not generalise to it <Cite id="chan2024capture24" />.
  Free-living datasets label daily life with wearable cameras and diaries; CAPTURE-24 holds 3883 hours of wrist-worn
  accelerometer data from 151 people, 2562 of them annotated <Cite id="chan2024capture24" />. See
  <NoteLink to="activity-recognition-datasets">activity recognition datasets</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Units and ranges.** Mixing devices that report in $g$ with ones that report in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, or with
  different ranges, gives channels that disagree by a factor of ten or saturate differently.
- **Clocks.** A few seconds of offset between a sensor and its labels moves every activity boundary; label alignment
  matters as much as signal alignment.
- **Non-wear.** Periods when the device lies on a table look like perfect stillness and must be detected and removed,
  not labelled as sleep or sitting.
- **<NoteLink to="class-imbalance">Class imbalance</NoteLink>.** Daily life is mostly sitting, lying and sleeping.
  Accuracy is dominated by those classes; report per-class or balanced metrics.
- **Device and placement shift.** A new device model, firmware or wearing position is a new data distribution; test
  on it before deploying.
