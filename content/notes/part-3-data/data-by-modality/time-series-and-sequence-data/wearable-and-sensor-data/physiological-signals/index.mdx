<Definition>
**Physiological signals** are continuous, multi-channel bioelectrical, optical, and mechanical time series—including
electrocardiograms (ECG), electroencephalograms (EEG), electromyograms (EMG), and photoplethysmograms (PPG)—recorded
from living organisms to monitor cardiovascular, neurological, and neuromuscular state <Cite id="goldberger2000" />.
Physiological signals are non-stationary, contaminated by high-amplitude biological and motion artefacts, and subject to
profound inter-individual anatomical and physiological variability.
</Definition>

In clinical informatics and wearable health computing, machine learning models analyze physiological streams to detect
cardiac arrhythmias, classify sleep stages, predict epileptic seizures, and estimate hemodynamic parameters. 

Unlike industrial telemetry from rigid machines, biological signals originate from living, moving subjects. Raw
recordings contain respiratory baseline wander, 50/60 Hz electrical mains hum, ocular blinks, and motion artefacts that
frequently dwarf the microvolt-level bioelectrical potentials of interest. Preparing physiological data for machine
learning requires frequency-domain filtering, artefact rejection, lead montage standardization, and strict subject-wise
partitioning.

## Filtering

Electrophysiological signals occupy specific spectral bands, outside of which recorded voltages represent noise:

### Baseline wander (0.05–0.5 Hz)

Induced by patient respiration, thoracic volume changes, perspiration, and slow shifts in electrode-skin contact
impedance. Baseline wander causes the isoelectric line to drift across millivolt ranges. 

In diagnostic ECG, low-frequency wander must be removed without phase distortion: non-linear phase high-pass filters
shift the ST segment relative to the PR segment, falsely creating artificial ST-segment elevations or depressions that
mimic acute myocardial infarction. Filtering requires either zero-phase Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filters or cubic
spline baseline subtraction through PR segments.

### Powerline interference (50 Hz / 60 Hz)

Electromagnetic induction from alternating current power lines and medical machinery introduces continuous sinusoidal
hum into high-impedance leads. This is attenuated using narrow notch (band-stop) filters or adaptive notch cancellation.

### High-frequency muscle tremor (>40–100 Hz)

Electromyographic (EMG) noise from shivering, movement, or postural muscle tension produces high-frequency noise across
surface leads, attenuated via low-pass filtering (typically cutoff at 40 Hz for routine telemetry or 150 Hz for
diagnostic 12-lead ECG).

### The Pan-Tompkins pipeline

The classical standard for real-time QRS complex detection is the **Pan-Tompkins algorithm** <Cite id="pan1985" />:
1. **Bandpass filtering (5–15 Hz):** Attenuates baseline wander and high-frequency noise while passing the dominant
   spectral energy of ventricular depolarization.
2. **Derivative filter:** Highlights the steep slope of the QRS complex
   ($y[n] = \frac{1}{8}(2x[n] + x[n-1] - x[n-3] - 2x[n-4])$).
3. **Squaring function ($y[n] = x[n]^2$):** Rectifies the signal and non-linearly amplifies large QRS slopes over P and
   T waves.
4. **Moving-window integration:** Averages energy over a window matching typical QRS width ($\sim 150\text{ ms}$) prior
   to dual-threshold peak detection.

## Artefact rejection

Electrophysiological recordings are frequently corrupted by high-amplitude biological artefacts that cannot be removed
by simple linear frequency filtering:

- **Ocular artefacts (EOG):** Eyeblinks and saccades generate electro-oculographic potentials ($>100\,\mu\text{V}$) that
  propagate across the frontal scalp, dominating frontal EEG electrodes (Fp1, Fp2).
- **Cardiac artefacts in EEG/EMG:** Pulsatile blood flow under scalp electrodes or cardiac dipoles produce rhythmic ECG
  R-peak spikes in EEG channels.
- **Motion artefacts in optical PPG:** In photoplethysmography, physical motion shifts the photodiode relative to tissue
  capillary beds, causing light leakage that swamps optical microvascular pulse waveforms.

### Independent Component Analysis (ICA)

For multi-channel EEG, **Independent Component Analysis (ICA)** decomposes observed sensor voltages
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times T}$ into statistically independent spatial source components
$\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times T}$:

$$
\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{S}
$$

Because eyeblinks and cardiac spikes originate from distinct localized dipole generators, they isolate into individual
independent components. Algorithms identify and zero-out artefact components ($\mathbf{s}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$)
before reconstructing clean scalp voltages via $\mathbf{X}_{\text{clean}} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{S}_{\text{cleaned}}$.

### Signal Quality Index (SQI)

Rather than attempting to clean hopelessly corrupted segments, automated pipelines compute a **Signal Quality Index
(SQI)** across sliding epochs (e.g., 5-second windows). SQI metrics evaluate:
- Kurtosis and skewness deviations from Gaussian baselines.
- Relative power in physiological vs non-physiological spectral bands.
- Correlation between redundant sensor channels (e.g., optical PPG paired with tri-axial accelerometer streams).

Epochs failing SQI thresholds ($\text{SQI} < \tau$) are flagged as invalid and excluded from model training to prevent
gradient corruption.

## Leads and montages

The coordinate geometry of physiological recording defines the anatomical meaning of feature channels:

### 12-Lead ECG

The standard clinical electrocardiogram records cardiac electrical activity from twelve standard leads:
- **Eindhoven limb leads (I, II, III):** Bipolar frontal-plane potential differences between right arm, left arm, and
  left leg.
- **Goldberger augmented leads (aVR, aVL, aVF):** Unipolar frontal-plane recordings referenced against the average of
  the other two limbs.
- **Precordial leads (V1–V6):** Unipolar horizontal-plane electrodes positioned across the anterior chest wall,
  observing cardiac electrical propagation from right ventricle (V1) to lateral left ventricle (V6).

Pretrained convolutional networks and transformers expect specific lead orderings; permuting lead channels or
substituting a 3-lead ambulatory patch for a 12-lead array breaks spatial feature representations.

### EEG referencing montages

In electroencephalography, voltage is inherently relative: an electrode measures the potential difference between two
scalp points:
- **Common Reference montage:** Every electrode is referenced to a single shared physical electrode (e.g., earlobe
  A1/A2, mastoid, or nose). If the reference electrode picks up muscle or cardiac noise, that noise is injected into
  every channel.
- **Average Reference montage:** Each electrode potential is referenced against the instantaneous arithmetic mean of all
  scalp electrodes ($\Phi_i - \frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^M \Phi_k$).
- **Bipolar (Longitudinal / "Double Banana") montage:** Channels record potential differences between adjacent spatial
  pairs along anterior-posterior chains ($Fp1-F3, F3-C3, C3-P3, P3-O1$). Bipolar referencing cancels widespread ambient
  noise, accentuating sharp focal cortical discharges (such as epileptic spikes).

## Subject-wise splits

The most common methodological flaw in biomedical machine learning is **record-level (random window) splitting**
<Cite id="saeb2017" />:

Consider an arrhythmia detection study with 20 patients, each contributing 24 hours of Holter ECG. Slicing these
recordings into 5-second windows yields hundreds of thousands of training examples. If a researcher shuffles these
windows uniformly at random into 80% train and 20% test splits:
1. Every individual patient's unique resting heart rate, baseline PR interval, QRS morphology, and electrode contact
   impedance appear in both train and test partitions.
2. The deep neural network learns patient-specific biometric fingerprints rather than generalizable pathophysiological
   disease patterns.
3. The resulting cross-validation accuracy often reaches $98\text{--}99\%$.
4. When the model is evaluated on a prospective cohort of completely new patients, accuracy collapses to
   $75\text{--}80\%$.

Valid evaluation requires **subject-wise (patient-level) cross-validation**:

$$
\mathcal{P}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{P}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset
$$

All recordings from patient $i$ must reside exclusively in either the training set or the test set.

## Pitfalls

- **Bidirectional zero-phase filtering in streaming inference:** Using `scipy.signal.filtfilt` during offline training
  runs filters both forward and backward, eliminating phase lag by peeking into future samples. Because production
  bed-side monitors process data causally, models trained on zero-phase filtered data encounter unexpected phase
  distortion when deployed.
- **Lead cable polarity inversions:** Inverting physical lead cables (e.g., swapping right arm and left arm electrodes)
  flips the electrical axis, turning upward R-peaks into deep S-waves and causing automated arrhythmia classifiers to
  falsely classify normal sinus beats as ectopic beats.
- **Resampling without anti-aliasing filters:** Downsampling ECG from 1,000 Hz to 250 Hz using naive decimation
  (skipping samples) aliases high-frequency noise into the physiological band. Resampling must always employ low-pass
  anti-aliasing filters.
- **PPG skin-tone optical attenuation:** Optical photoplethysmography measures light absorption by hemoglobin. Increased
  epidermal melanin absorbs green optical wavelengths ($525\text{ nm}$), reducing signal-to-noise ratio in individuals
  with dark skin tones unless sensor hardware dynamically modulates LED drive current.
