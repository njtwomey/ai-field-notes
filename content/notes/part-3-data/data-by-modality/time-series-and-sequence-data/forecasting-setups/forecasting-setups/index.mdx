<Definition>
A **forecasting setup** turns series $y_{i,1}, \dots, y_{i,T}$, $i = 1, \dots, N$, into a supervised learning problem.
At a **forecast origin** $t$, the input is the **look-back window** $y_{i,t-L+1}, \dots, y_{i,t}$ of length $L$, with
any covariates, and the target is the next $H$ values $y_{i,t+1}, \dots, y_{i,t+H}$, where $H$ is the **horizon**. The
setup also fixes how the $H$ values are predicted, whether one model serves all $N$ series, and which covariates are
available at the origin.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Classical forecasting methods fit one model to one series and extrapolate it. Machine-learning forecasters instead
learn a function from recent history to the future, trained on many (history, future) pairs cut from the data. Every
choice in cutting those pairs changes what the model can learn and what it can be asked at prediction time. The same
network can be a good or a useless forecaster depending on the setup it is trained in.

## Look-back and horizon

The horizon $H$ comes from the decision the forecast serves: a week of daily values to plan staffing, a quarter of
weekly values to plan stock. The look-back $L$ is a modelling choice. It must cover the longest pattern that matters,
such as one or two seasonal cycles. A longer look-back gives more context and fewer training examples per series, and
more weights for the model to learn.

Sliding the origin along a series of length $T$ in steps of $s$ gives about $(T - L - H)/s + 1$ examples.

## Multi-step strategies

A forecast of $H > 1$ values can be produced in three ways <Cite id="bentaieb2012" />.

- **Recursive** (iterated). Train one model $f$ to predict one step ahead, $\hat y_{t+1} = f(y_{t-L+1}, \dots, y_t)$.
  To go further, append $\hat y_{t+1}$ to the window and predict again, $H$ times. One model serves every horizon, but
  each step feeds the model its own errors, which accumulate with the horizon. Training on true past values and
  predicting from predicted ones is a mismatch between training and use.
- **Direct**. Train $H$ separate models, $\hat y_{t+h} = f_h(y_{t-L+1}, \dots, y_t)$ for $h = 1, \dots, H$. No errors
  are fed back, but $H$ models cost $H$ times as much, and their forecasts need not agree with each other.
- **Multi-output** (multiple-input multiple-output, MIMO). Train one model that outputs the whole vector
  $(\hat y_{t+1}, \dots, \hat y_{t+H})$. It shares what it learns across horizons and keeps the forecasts coherent.
  <NoteLink to="n-beats">N-BEATS</NoteLink> and the temporal fusion transformer work this way; DeepAR instead
  samples recursively.

In a large comparison on the 111 series of the NN5 competition, multi-output strategies forecast best
<Cite id="bentaieb2012" />. The same study found that removing the season before modelling improved accuracy for every
strategy; see <NoteLink to="series-normalisation">normalising time series</NoteLink>.

## Local and global models

A **local** model is fitted to each series separately: $N$ series, $N$ models. A **global** model is one function
fitted to all series at once, so every series contributes training examples to every other.

Global models are not more restrictive than local ones. For any set of series, a global model exists that produces the
same forecasts as a set of local models, with no assumption that the series are similar
<Cite id="monteromanso2021" />. The complexity a local method needs grows with the number of series, while a global
model's stays fixed, so with many series a global model can afford much more capacity, such as a long memory. Global
linear models matched local methods' accuracy with two orders of magnitude fewer parameters in the same study.

A global model needs the series on a common scale; see
<NoteLink to="series-normalisation">normalising time series</NoteLink>. It can also take an identifier or static
attributes of each series as inputs, so that it can still treat series differently.

## Covariates

Covariates are inputs other than the series' own past. Their availability at the forecast origin sorts them into three
kinds, the split the <NoteLink to="temporal-fusion-transformer">temporal fusion transformer</NoteLink> builds its
architecture around <Cite id="lim2021" />.

| Kind                | Known for                        | Examples                                          |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Static              | the whole series, never changing | store size, product category, location            |
| Known-future        | the past and the whole horizon   | calendar, holidays, planned prices and promotions |
| Observed-only (past) | the past only                    | weather actuals, other products' sales, web traffic |

A known-future covariate enters the model at every step of the horizon. An observed-only covariate enters only in the
look-back window; using its future values at training time, when they are in the data, and then having to forecast
them at prediction time is a <NoteLink to="data-leakage">leak</NoteLink>. A weather forecast is a known-future covariate
only if the forecast as issued at the origin is used, not the weather that followed.

## Backtesting and forecast origins

A setup is evaluated by repeating the forecast from many origins, training only on data before each one; see
<NoteLink to="time-series-cross-validation">time-series cross-validation</NoteLink>. Errors are reported per horizon
$h = 1, \dots, H$, because they grow with $h$ <Cite id="hyndman2021" />. Examples whose targets lie after an origin must
not appear in that origin's training set; with overlapping examples this removes the last $H$ origins before the test
period from training.

## Point and probabilistic targets

The target can be a point forecast, a set of quantiles or a full predictive distribution. A distribution lets the
decision that uses the forecast trade off over- and under-forecasting; see
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-forecasting">probabilistic forecasting</NoteLink>. Global probabilistic models such as
<NoteLink to="deepar">DeepAR</NoteLink> combine a multi-step setup with a likelihood per step <Cite id="salinas2020" />.
Series organised in a hierarchy, such as products within stores within regions, add the constraint that forecasts add
up; see <NoteLink to="hierarchical-forecast-reconciliation">hierarchical forecast reconciliation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Random splits of examples.** Overlapping examples put the test period's values into training as inputs and
  targets of neighbouring examples.
- **Covariates unavailable at the origin.** Every input must be known when the forecast is made: actual weather, final
  prices and revised figures often are not.
- **Lags shorter than the horizon.** In a direct or tabular setup, a feature such as "yesterday's value" is unknown when
  forecasting a week ahead; see <NoteLink to="time-series-feature-extraction">feature extraction</NoteLink>.
- **One origin.** A single test period measures one stretch of luck; average over many origins.
- **Horizon mismatch.** A model trained for $H = 7$ and used for $H = 28$ by recursion meets inputs it never saw in
  training.
