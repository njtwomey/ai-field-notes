<Definition>
**Look-ahead bias** (also termed **peeking bias** or **temporal leakage**) is the systemic corruption of an offline
predictive pipeline in which information, statistical parameters, universe memberships, or restated revisions
originating chronologically after the forecast origin $t_0$ are utilized to engineer features, train models, or evaluate
performance <Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" />.
</Definition>

In supervised machine learning with independent and identically distributed ($\text{i.i.d.}$) tabular data, standard
preprocessing practices—such as computing global feature scaling parameters or performing cross-validation folds—are
well-understood. In temporal and sequential data, however, the fundamental constraint is the physical arrow of time. At
forecast origin $t_0$, any information from $t > t_0$ is physically unobserved.

Look-ahead bias is particularly insidious because it rarely throws runtime exceptions. Instead, it artificially inflates
backtest metrics and cross-validation scores: models learn to exploit subtle future indicators that cannot exist during
live production deployment, leading to catastrophic performance collapse upon launch.

## Full-series statistics

A frequent source of look-ahead bias is computing normalization or preprocessing parameters across the full historical
time series rather than strictly causal expanding windows:

### Global feature scaling

Computing the sample mean $\hat{\mu}$ and standard deviation $\hat{\sigma}$ across the entire dataset duration $[0, T]$:

$$
z_t = \frac{x_t - \hat{\mu}_{[0, T]}}{\hat{\sigma}_{[0, T]}}
$$

If a massive economic shock or structural shift occurs in year 5, the global mean $\hat{\mu}_{[0, T]}$ is pulled
downward, artificially informing the model at year 1 that future values will be depressed. All feature scalers
(StandardScaler, MinMaxScaler, QuantileTransformer) must be fitted strictly on historical training periods
$[0, t_{\text{train}}]$ or computed as rolling causal statistics.

### Global outlier clipping and winsorization

Determining quantile clipping thresholds using future data leaks the historical distribution's eventual extremes. If a
time series experiences a once-in-a-decade spike at $t=500$, a global 99th percentile calculated on all 1,000 steps
inflates the upper threshold, altering the scaling of normal observations at $t=50$.

### Global target encoding

Computing the conditional label mean for categorical entities (such as merchant category IDs or user cohorts) across the
entire dataset incorporates post-event outcomes into historical feature representations. In time series setups, target
encoding must be calculated as an expanding point-in-time historical average.

## Centred windows

When filtering, smoothing, or engineering technical indicators, practitioners frequently choose between **trailing**
(causal) and **centred** (bilateral) rolling windows:

### Trailing (Causal) window

A trailing window computes rolling statistics exclusively over the historical interval $[t - W + 1, t]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{x}_t^{\text{trailing}} &= \frac{1}{W} \sum_{k=0}^{W-1} x_{t - k} \\
&= \frac{1}{W} \Big( x_t + x_{t-1} + x_{t-2} + \dots + x_{t - W + 1} \Big)
\end{aligned}
$$

Every index $\tau = t - k$ satisfies $\tau \le t$. At prediction time $t$, all evaluated values reside strictly in the
historical filtration $\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(x_1, \dots, x_t)$. Trailing filters introduce a phase lag of approximately
$\frac{W - 1}{2}$ steps, delaying the detection of turning points.

### Centred (Bilateral) window

To eliminate phase lag and obtain zero-phase trend curves, practitioners often compute centred rolling averages with
span $W = 2M + 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{x}_t^{\text{centred}} &= \frac{1}{2M + 1} \sum_{k=-M}^{M} x_{t + k} \\
&= \frac{1}{2M + 1} \Bigg( \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{M} x_{t - k}}_{\text{Past observations } (\tau < t)} + x_t + \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{M} x_{t + k}}_{\textbf{Look-ahead leakage! } (\tau > t)} \Bigg)
\end{aligned}
$$

The forward summation $\sum_{k=1}^M x_{t+k}$ evaluates measurements up to $M = \lfloor W/2 \rfloor$ steps into the
unobserved future. At decision point $t_0$, a centred moving average already knows whether the series will trend upward
or crash in subsequent steps. Feeding centred rolling statistics into forecasting models constitutes direct look-ahead
leakage.

## Revised data and vintages

In macroeconomic forecasting, public health tracking, and financial analytics, initial data releases are systematically
revised over subsequent months and years <Cite id="croushore2001" />:

- **Preliminary releases:** Published immediately following the reporting period, based on partial survey responses and
  rapid estimates.
- **Subsequent revisions and restatements:** Updated weeks or months later as comprehensive administrative registries
  arrive, often followed by multi-year benchmark revisions (e.g., annual seasonal adjustment updates).

If a machine learning practitioner trains a model using current economic time series downloaded from a modern data
repository, the database serves the **latest revised vintage** for historical timestamps. 

A trading model backtested on revised GDP or corporate earnings figures appears remarkably accurate because it trains on
data that was literally unknown to market participants at the time the trades had to be placed. Eliminating vintage bias
requires maintaining **point-in-time databases** that preserve historical data releases exactly as they appeared at each
historical timestamp $t_0$.

## Survivorship among series

When forecasting across a dynamic panel of entities (e.g., predicting stock returns, corporate credit default, or
machinery failure), filtering the historical universe based on present-day survival induces **survivorship bias**
<Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" />:

$$
\mathcal{U}_{\text{survivor}} = \{i : \text{entity } i \text{ is operational at final timestamp } T\}
$$

Filtering out entities that failed, were liquidated, or were decommissioned prior to $T$ produces severe distortions:
- The historical training population contains only successful, resilient, or long-lived units.
- Distressed units that collapsed are expunged from the historical record, biasing baseline return distributions upward
  and underestimating catastrophic failure probabilities.

Point-in-time universe curation requires that at any historical timestamp $t_0$, the eligible entity set
$\mathcal{U}(t_0)$ comprises exactly those entities active and observable at $t_0$, regardless of their eventual fate.

## Pitfalls

- **Neglecting purging and embargoing in cross-validation:** In financial and economic forecasting where labels span
  multi-step forward horizons ($y_t = x_{t+H} - x_t$), adjacent observations overlap. Standard K-fold cross-validation
  mixes overlapping label horizons across splits. **Purging** removes training samples whose forward label spans overlap
  the test evaluation window, while **embargoing** adds a buffer period following the test set to account for
  auto-regressive memory <Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" />.
- **Publication lag neglect:** Recording quarterly financial statements as effective on the quarter-end calendar date
  (e.g., December 31) when the SEC 10-K filing was not made public until mid-February.
- **Global feature selection:** Executing recursive feature elimination, mutual information screening, or tree-based
  feature selection across the combined historical dataset prior to temporal splitting leaks target dependencies from
  future evaluation periods into model architecture.
- **Leakage via backward-filled exogenous series:** Forward-filling missing gaps in exogenous variables is causal;
  backward-filling propagates future values backward into decision intervals.
