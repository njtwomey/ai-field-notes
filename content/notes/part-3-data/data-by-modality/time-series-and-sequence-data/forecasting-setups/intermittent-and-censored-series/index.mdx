<Definition>
**Intermittent and censored time series** are temporal sequences characterized by high frequencies of zero observations
punctuated by sporadic non-zero events, or whose observed magnitudes are artificially truncated from above by
operational capacity constraints (such as warehouse stock-outs or server throttling)
<Cite id="croston1972, tobin1958" />. Standard forecasting loss functions (such as Mean Squared Error or Mean Absolute
Percentage Error) fail on these series, outputting uninformative fractional trickles or underestimating unfulfilled
latent demand.
</Definition>

In retail inventory management, aircraft spare parts logistics, maintenance engineering, and cloud compute capacity
planning, the vast majority of stock keeping units (SKUs) exhibit intermittent demand: an item may sell zero units for
eight consecutive weeks before a sudden customer order consumes twenty units. 

Furthermore, historical transaction databases record *observed sales*, not *true customer demand*. When inventory drops
to zero, sales drop to zero regardless of customer intent. Disentangling true latent demand from supply constraints
requires dedicated intermittent modeling, zero-inflated distributions, and censored demand unconstraining.

## Zero-heavy demand

Standard statistical and machine learning loss functions assume continuous, bell-shaped error distributions. When
applied to zero-heavy time series, these assumptions fail:

- **MSE degradation:** Minimizing Mean Squared Error on a series with $90\%$ zero periods and sporadic orders of
  magnitude 50 forces the model to predict an unconditional mean of 5 units every single day. A retailer stocking 5
  units per day ties up working capital daily, while still facing a 15-unit stock-out whenever a real 20-unit order
  arrives.
- **MAPE collapse:** Mean Absolute Percentage Error ($\text{MAPE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum |(y - \hat{y}) / y|$) divides by
  zero on every null period, rendering it mathematically invalid.
- **Classification of demand types:** Syntetos et al. formalize four demand regimes based on the squared coefficient of
  variation of demand sizes ($CV^2$) and the average inter-demand interval ($p$) <Cite id="syntetos2005" />:
  1. **Smooth:** Low interval ($p < 1.32$), low size variance ($CV^2 < 0.49$).
  2. **Intermittent:** High interval ($p \ge 1.32$), low size variance ($CV^2 < 0.49$).
  3. **Erratic:** Low interval ($p < 1.32$), high size variance ($CV^2 \ge 0.49$).
  4. **Lumpy:** High interval ($p \ge 1.32$), high size variance ($CV^2 \ge 0.49$).

### Croston's decomposition

Croston decoupled intermittent demand into two separate processes <Cite id="croston1972" />:

1. **Non-zero demand size ($z_t$):** Updated via exponential smoothing strictly when a transaction occurs ($y_t > 0$):
   $$
   \hat{z}_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha) \hat{z}_{t-1}
   $$
2. **Inter-arrival interval ($p_t$):** The number of time periods elapsed since the preceding transaction, updated when
   demand occurs:
   $$
   \hat{p}_t = \alpha q_t + (1 - \alpha) \hat{p}_{t-1}
   $$

The forecast demand rate per period is the ratio of smoothed size to smoothed interval:

$$
\hat{d}_t = \frac{\hat{z}_t}{\hat{p}_t}
$$

### Syntetos-Boylan Approximation (SBA)

Because the expected value of a ratio exceeds the ratio of expected values
($\mathbb{E}[Z/P] > \mathbb{E}[Z]/\mathbb{E}[P]$ for independent random variables), Croston's estimator suffers from
systematic positive bias. Syntetos and Boylan introduced the **SBA correction factor** <Cite id="syntetos2005" />:

$$
\hat{d}_t^{\text{SBA}} = \left( 1 - \frac{\alpha}{2} \right) \frac{\hat{z}_t}{\hat{p}_t}
$$

SBA eliminates the asymptotic over-forecasting bias, substantially improving inventory safety stock allocations.

### Hurdle and Tweedie models

Modern machine learning approaches model zero-heavy series via two-stage hurdle architectures or compound Poisson-Gamma
(Tweedie) distributions:
- **Hurdle models:** A binary classification classifier (e.g., LightGBM with binary log-loss) first predicts transaction
  occurrence probability $P(Y_t > 0)$. A separate regressor models conditional magnitude $P(Y_t \mid Y_t > 0)$.
- **Tweedie loss:** Parameterized by variance power $p \in (1, 2)$, Tweedie objective functions place an explicit
  probability mass at exact zero while fitting a right-skewed continuous Gamma distribution over positive values.

## Censoring by stock-outs

A critical trap in demand forecasting is confusing observed sales with true customer demand <Cite id="tobin1958" />:

$$
y_t = \min(D_t, I_t)
$$

where $D_t$ represents latent customer demand and $I_t$ is on-hand inventory at the start of period $t$.

When inventory is abundant ($I_t > D_t$), sales equal demand ($y_t = D_t$). However, when a **stock-out** occurs
($I_t = 0$), sales drop to zero regardless of how many customers attempted to purchase the item. Observed sales are
**right-censored** at available inventory $I_t$.

### The vicious stock-out spiral

If forecasting models train naively on raw point-of-sale data without accounting for inventory availability:
1. A stock-out occurs, causing sales to drop to zero.
2. The forecasting model treats the zero as a genuine decline in customer demand.
3. Downstream replenishment software orders less inventory for the next cycle based on the lower forecast.
4. The SKU stocks out even faster, driving historical sales lower in a self-reinforcing death spiral.

### Demand unconstraining (Tobit models)

To break the stock-out spiral, data engineers flag periods where inventory was constrained ($I_t = 0$) and apply
**demand unconstraining** <Cite id="tobin1958" />:

- **Censored Tobit regression:** Models latent demand $D_t = \mathbf{x}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + \epsilon_t$, with
  likelihood contributions partitioned into uncensored points and right-censored probability survival integrals:

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \log \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \sigma) &= \sum_{y_t < I_t} \left[ -\log \sigma + \log \phi\left( \frac{y_t - \mathbf{x}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}}{\sigma} \right) \right] \\
  &\quad + \sum_{y_t = I_t} \log \left[ 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{I_t - \mathbf{x}_t^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}}{\sigma} \right) \right]
  \end{aligned}
  $$
- **Expectation-Maximization (EM) imputation:** Replaces censored stock-out periods with their conditional expectations
  $\mathbb{E}[D_t \mid D_t \ge I_t]$ before training downstream tree-based models.

## Promotions and events

Promotional discounts, digital coupon campaigns, and marketing events drastically amplify transaction probability on
intermittent SKUs. However, promotions interact dangerously with stock-out censoring:

- **Peak censoring vulnerability:** Promotional uplift accelerates inventory turnover. Consequently, stock-outs are most
  likely to occur precisely during promotional spikes, causing naive sales models to severely underestimate promotional
  elasticity.
- **Event feature encoding:** Models must incorporate forward-looking promotion calendars (discount depth, feature ad
  placements, competitor price gaps) alongside historical stock-out flags to estimate the true promotional lift curve.

## Pitfalls

- **Evaluating intermittent models using MAPE or sMAPE:** Because zero appears frequently in the denominator, MAPE
  produces numerical singularities or wildly distorted percentages. Proper evaluation requires Mean Absolute Scaled
  Error (MASE), Root Mean Squared Scaled Error (RMSSE), or Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS).
- **Ignoring on-hand stock indicators:** Training models on raw sales transactions without joining inventory
  availability tables directly injects stock-out censoring bias into feature engineering.
- **Aggregating to monthly buckets to eliminate zeros:** While summing weekly or daily data into monthly totals reduces
  zero frequency, it obscures replenishment lead times and erases peak daily demand spikes needed for sizing logistics
  buffers.
- **Assuming Poisson arrivals when demand is bursty:** Standard Poisson count distributions assume mean equals variance
  ($\lambda = \sigma^2$). Intermittent demand frequently exhibits extreme overdispersion ($\sigma^2 \gg \mu$), requiring
  Negative Binomial or Tweedie formulations.
