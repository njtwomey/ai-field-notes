<Definition>
A raw time series is a list of timestamped values $(t_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, with $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n$.
Preparing it for a model means three things: making the timestamps mean the same thing everywhere, **resampling** the
values onto a chosen grid by aggregating or interpolating them, and **aligning** series recorded by different devices
onto one timeline.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most models for time series expect a regular grid: one value per channel every $\Delta$ seconds, with every channel on
the same grid. Real recordings rarely arrive that way. A phone samples its accelerometer at a rate that drifts with
load; a hospital records a blood test when a nurse takes one; a smart meter reports only when consumption changes; two
devices each keep their own clock. Resampling and alignment turn such records into the grid a model expects. Every
step of that conversion makes an assumption about the values between samples, and those assumptions should be chosen,
not inherited from a library default.

## Timestamps and time zones

A timestamp needs a reference and a meaning before it can be compared with another.

- **One reference.** Store times in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), and keep the local time zone as a separate field.
  Local clocks jump at daylight-saving changes: one local hour occurs twice a year and another never occurs. A series
  indexed by local time has a duplicated hour and a missing hour.
- **Local time as a feature.** Human behaviour follows local time: a commute happens at 8:00 local time in every time
  zone. Convert to local time when building <NoteLink to="date-and-time-features">calendar features</NoteLink>, after
  the series has been ordered in UTC.
- **Which event the time records.** A value can carry the time it was measured, the time it was recorded or the time
  it reached the database. Laboratory results, for example, are often stored with the time of the report rather than
  of the sample. Only the measurement time places the value correctly; using the arrival time in a model that predicts
  from the past is a <NoteLink to="data-leakage">leak</NoteLink> when arrival is late, and a needless delay when it is
  early.

## Regular and irregular sampling

A series is **regularly sampled** when $t_i = t_0 + i\Delta$ for a fixed sampling interval $\Delta$, so that the
sampling rate is $f_s = 1/\Delta$. Signal processing assumes this; the
<NoteLink to="sampling-theorem">sampling theorem</NoteLink> then says which frequencies the samples can represent.

A series is **irregularly sampled** when the gaps $t_{i+1} - t_i$ vary. Irregularity has different sources, and each
calls for different handling <Cite id="shukla2020" />.

- **Jitter.** The intended grid is regular and the actual times scatter around it by a small fraction of $\Delta$.
  Rounding each time to the nearest grid point is usually enough.
- **Event-driven sampling.** A value is recorded when something happens: a change of state, a threshold crossing, a
  clinician's decision. The time of a sample carries information about the process. A model that sees only the
  resampled values loses it; a model that also sees the sampling pattern can use it.
- **Gaps.** Long stretches with no samples: a device was off, a patient left the ward, a sensor lost its connection.
  Gaps are <NoteLink to="missing-data-and-imputation">missing data</NoteLink>, and why they are missing matters as it
  does for any missing value.

## Resampling

Resampling maps the samples onto a new grid $u_k = u_0 + k\Delta'$. Going to a coarser grid ($\Delta' > \Delta$) is
**downsampling**; going to a finer grid, or onto any grid from irregular times, is **upsampling** or
**interpolation**.

### Downsampling by aggregation

Downsampling groups the samples into bins $[u_k, u_k + \Delta')$ and summarises each bin by one value. The summary must
match what the values measure.

| Values measure                        | Summary of a bin           | Example                                  |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| a level or a rate                     | mean                       | temperature, heart rate, power           |
| an amount that accumulates            | sum                        | step counts, rainfall, sales             |
| a state that persists until it changes | last value                 | price at the close, device on or off     |
| an extreme that matters               | maximum or minimum         | peak load, lowest blood oxygen           |
| events                                | count                      | clicks, transactions                     |

Averaging over a bin is a crude low-pass filter. It suppresses most of the content above the new
<NoteLink to="aliasing">Nyquist frequency</NoteLink> $1/(2\Delta')$, but not all of it. When the high-frequency content
of a signal matters, low-pass filter it properly and then keep every $M$-th sample, as in
<NoteLink to="decimation-and-interpolation">decimation</NoteLink>. Taking every $M$-th sample without filtering folds
the high frequencies onto low ones.

### Upsampling by interpolation

Upsampling must invent values between samples. The choice of interpolant is an assumption about the signal between
them.

- **Previous value** (sample-and-hold) repeats the last sample until the next one. It suits states that persist, such
  as a price or a setpoint, and it uses only the past.
- **Linear interpolation** joins neighbouring samples by straight lines.
- **Monotone cubic interpolation** (PCHIP) fits a smooth curve that never overshoots the neighbouring samples
  <Cite id="fritsch1980" />; see <NoteLink to="monotone-and-shape-preserving-interpolation">shape-preserving
  interpolation</NoteLink>.
- **Cubic splines** fit the smoothest twice-differentiable curve through the samples; see
  <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">cubic spline interpolation</NoteLink>. They can swing beyond the samples.
- **Band-limited (sinc) interpolation** is exact for a regularly sampled signal with no content above the Nyquist
  frequency, and is what <NoteLink to="sample-rate-conversion">sample-rate conversion</NoteLink> approximates.

Three practices limit the damage of interpolating across gaps.

1. **Interpolate only short gaps.** Fill gaps up to a stated length and leave longer ones missing.
2. **Mark what was filled.** Add an indicator that a grid value was interpolated and the time since the last real
   sample. Recurrent models given these two inputs can learn how much to trust a filled value <Cite id="che2018" />.
3. **Avoid the grid.** Models for irregular series take the pairs $(t_i, y_i)$ directly: time-aware recurrent networks,
   <NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">neural ordinary differential equations</NoteLink> and
   <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss> <Cite id="shukla2020" />. The
   <NoteLink to="lomb-scargle-periodogram">Lomb–Scargle periodogram</NoteLink> does the same for spectra.

### Interpolation and the future

Linear, cubic and sinc interpolation at time $u$ use samples after $u$. A forecasting or online model trained on such
values sees a little of the future at every filled point. For data that a model will receive in real time, fill
forward with the previous value or with a model fitted to the past only.

## Synchronising several series

Two devices that record the same scene keep separate clocks. Device B's clock reads

$$
t_B = a + b \, t_A
$$

when device A's reads $t_A$, where $a$ is the **offset** and $b$ the **rate**. Cheap quartz clocks differ in rate by
tens of parts per million, so $b - 1 \approx 10^{-5}$: a drift of about a second a day. Offsets can be seconds or
minutes when devices are started by hand.

### Alignment by cross-correlation

When two streams record related signals, the offset is the lag at which they agree best. The **cross-correlation**
of two series $x_t$ and $y_t$ of length $n$, the two-series analogue of the
<NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation">autocorrelation</NoteLink>,

$$
r(k) = \frac{1}{n\,s_x s_y} \sum_t (x_t - \bar x)(y_{t+k} - \bar y)
$$

with sample means $\bar x, \bar y$ and standard deviations $s_x, s_y$, peaks at the lag $k^\star$ by which $y$ follows
$x$. Moving $y$ back by $k^\star$ samples aligns the two. Its frequency-domain counterpart, the
<NoteLink to="coherence-and-cross-spectra">cross-spectrum</NoteLink>, gives the delay as the slope of its phase.

Rate differences turn a single offset into one that grows with time. Estimating $k^\star$ in several windows along the
recording and fitting a straight line to the window offsets against time estimates both $a$ and $b$.

### Alignment by shared events

When the streams do not record related signals, alignment needs an event that every device sees. A deliberate tap, a
jump or a clap at the start and end of a recording gives a spike in every accelerometer and microphone; a light flash
appears in every camera. Two such events, at the start and the end, give the offset and the rate. Data-collection
protocols for wearable and multi-camera recordings often build such events in.

### Aligning labels to signals

Labels are a stream too. Annotations made from video, by a person pressing a key or from a diary arrive with their own
offset and a human reaction time of a few hundred milliseconds or more. Labels at boundaries between activities are
therefore least reliable. Common responses are to drop a margin around each boundary from training, or to use windows
long enough that the error is a small fraction of each; see <NoteLink to="time-series-windowing">windowing</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- Interpolation assumes the signal is smooth, or constant, between samples; it cannot recover content faster than the
  sampling.
- Aggregation assumes that the chosen summary keeps what the model needs; a mean hides peaks and a last value hides
  everything before it.
- Cross-correlation alignment assumes a constant offset over the window used, and signals related by roughly a delay
  and a gain.

## Pitfalls

- **Library defaults.** Resampling functions default to a summary (often the mean) and an interpolant (often linear or
  none) that may not suit the data. A sum resampled with a mean, or a state resampled with a linear fill, is wrong
  without any error being raised.
- **Silent gaps.** A filled gap looks like data. Without an indicator, a model learns from invented values as if they
  were measured.
- **Interpolating across the split.** Interpolation that uses samples after a training–test boundary leaks test data
  into training, and filling across a gap after the boundary leaks the future into a forecast.
- **Time-zone and daylight-saving errors.** Merging series kept in different time zones, or in local time, shifts them
  by whole hours; the shift is easy to miss when signals are daily.
- **Drift over long recordings.** An offset estimated at the start of a week-long recording can be seconds wrong at the
  end.
