<Definition>
**Gaps in time series** are intervals of unobserved, missing, or corrupted measurements
$[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{end}}]$ occurring within an otherwise continuous temporal sequence
<Cite id="little2019" />. Unlike cross-sectional tabular missingness, temporal gaps are fundamentally constrained by the
arrow of time: online real-time inference requires causal imputation using strictly historical information ($s \le t$),
whereas acausal backward filling or centred interpolation incorporates future information, introducing fatal look-ahead
leakage.
</Definition>

In real-world telemetry, time series rarely arrive on pristine, uninterrupted sampling grids. Hardware sensors lose
wireless connectivity, IoT devices experience intermittent battery outages, clinical monitors detach from patients
during transit, and financial exchanges close for weekends and holidays.

How practitioners handle these gaps directly shapes model behavior. Filling missing values forward assumes state
persistence; interpolating assumes continuous trajectories; and decaying toward global baselines accounts for
information entropy. Enforcing temporal causality and bounding staleness are mandatory to prevent offline models from
exploiting future data that will not exist during production serving.

## Forward fill

The simplest and most pervasive causal imputation method is **forward fill**, also termed **Last Observation Carried
Forward (LOCF)**:

$$
x_t^* = x_{\tau} \quad \text{where } \tau = \max \{s \le t : x_s \text{ is observed}\}
$$

- **Inductive bias:** Forward fill assumes that the underlying physical or economic state remains constant until a new
  observation is registered. This step-function behavior aligns naturally with discrete, event-driven variables—such as
  active pricing policies, software configuration flags, central bank interest rates, or user account tiers.
- **Limitations:** When applied to continuous, mean-reverting, or volatile signals (such as blood glucose levels, stock
  market returns, or seismic vibrations), forward fill produces artificial flatlines. It artificially depresses sample
  variance, eliminates high-frequency spectral power, and inflates autocorrelation estimates.

## Interpolation

When signals are known to govern smooth, continuous physical dynamical systems (such as atmospheric temperature or
vehicle velocity), practitioners frequently deploy mathematical interpolation across observed boundary points
$(t_0, x_0)$ and $(t_1, x_1)$:

- **Linear interpolation:** Connects endpoints via a straight line:
  $$
  x_t^* = x_0 + \frac{t - t_0}{t_1 - t_0} (x_1 - x_0)
  $$
- **Spline interpolation:** Fits piecewise cubic polynomials that match first and second derivatives at knots, creating
  smooth trajectories.

While interpolation is mathematically rigorous for retrospective, offline scientific studies (e.g., historical climate
analysis or paleontology), **it cannot be deployed in online real-time forecasting**. At any evaluation time
$t \in (t_0, t_1)$, the future boundary point $(t_1, x_1)$ has not yet occurred.

## Look-ahead from backward fills

A catastrophic failure mode in time series engineering is **backward fill** (Next Observation Carried Backward, or NOCB)
and centred interpolation:

$$
x_t^* = x_{\tau} \quad \text{where } \tau = \min \{s \ge t : x_s \text{ is observed}\}
$$

Carrying future values backward in time violates temporal causality:

1. **Information leakage:** If a machine sensor experiences a failure alert at $t=100$, backward-filling that alert
   across an unobserved gap from $t=80$ to $t=99$ informs the model at $t=85$ that a breakdown is imminent.
2. **Artificial predictive accuracy:** A predictive maintenance model trained on backward-filled data achieves
   near-perfect offline accuracy on historical backtests. When deployed into live production, the model completely fails
   because future observations are unavailable.
3. **Centred rolling window contamination:** Applying bilateral filters, centred moving averages, or zero-phase digital
   filtering (`scipy.signal.filtfilt`) across missing gaps injects post-gap target signals into historical features.

## Maximum staleness

Carrying the last observation forward indefinitely is dangerous. If a heart-rate monitor disconnects for six hours,
carrying forward the last known pulse rate of 72 bpm falsely presents a stable patient, masking potential cardiac
arrest.

Production data pipelines resolve this by enforcing a **maximum staleness threshold** $\tau_{\max}$:

$$
\text{Elapsed Staleness } \delta_t = t - \tau
$$

$$
\hat{x}_t = \begin{cases} x_\tau & \text{if } \delta_t \le \tau_{\max} \\ \text{Expired / Null} & \text{if } \delta_t > \tau_{\max} \end{cases}
$$

When staleness exceeds $\tau_{\max}$:
- Tabular GBDT models receive an explicit `NaN` or a sentinel value alongside a staleness feature.
- Production systems route the signal to fallback heuristic controllers or trigger hardware telemetry alarms.

## Marking gaps

In many domains, **missingness is informative** rather than missing completely at random (MCAR)
<Cite id="little2019" />. A patient who does not receive arterial blood gas tests is likely breathing comfortably; a
financial asset that is not traded for hours is suffering an illiquidity crisis.

To allow models to exploit the informational value of gaps, feature pipelines explicitly mark missingness:

### Binary missing indicators

For each channel $j$, append an explicit binary indicator:

$$
m_{t, j} = \mathbb{I}(x_{t, j} \text{ is observed}) \in \{0, 1\}
$$

### Elapsed duration features

Track the continuous time delta elapsed since the last valid measurement:

$$
\delta_{t, j} = \begin{cases} t - \tau_j & \text{if prior observation exists} \\ \Delta t_{\text{init}} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

### Continuous neural decay (GRU-D)

Che et al. formalized learned continuous decay for multivariate time series with missing values in **GRU-D**
<Cite id="che2018" />. Instead of carrying values forward rigidly, the network computes a continuous exponential decay
vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_t \in (0, 1)^D$:

$$
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_t = \exp\left( -\max\left(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{W}_\gamma \boldsymbol{\delta}_t + \mathbf{b}_\gamma\right) \right)
$$

The effective input vector $\mathbf{x}_t^*$ decays smoothly from the last observed measurement $x_\tau$ toward the
empirical historical mean $\tilde{x}$:

$$
x_{t, j}^* = m_{t, j} x_{t, j} + (1 - m_{t, j}) \left( \gamma_{t, j} x_{\tau, j} + (1 - \gamma_{t, j}) \tilde{x}_j \right)
$$

This allows neural networks to retain recent observations over short gaps while gracefully reverting to population
baselines over prolonged outages.

## Pitfalls

- **Applying global interpolation across split boundaries:** Executing `df.interpolate()` or spline smoothing on the
  entire time series prior to partitioning into train, validation, and test splits leaks future test observations into
  training folds.
- **Confounding irregular sampling with missingness:** Assuming that all gaps represent failures. Event-driven IoT
  architectures transmit data only when sensor deltas exceed deadband thresholds; assuming constant missingness
  misinterprets intentional bandwidth optimization.
- **Silent flatlining in safety-critical loops:** Forward-filling sensor values indefinitely without staleness
  expiration masks sensor hardware disconnections, leading to silent failures in autonomous control loops.
- **Discontinuous step jumps at gap boundaries:** When a long gap ends with an observation far removed from the
  forward-filled level, the sudden step change introduces high-frequency transient spikes into velocity and derivative
  features.
