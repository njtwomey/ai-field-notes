<Definition>
**Areal aggregation** is the process of partitioning continuous point-level or individual-level observations into
discrete contiguous spatial zones (such as census tracts, administrative districts, or postal codes) and computing
regional summary statistics (sums, counts, means). It gives rise to two fundamental statistical paradoxes: the
**Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP)**, where observed correlations depend on arbitrary boundary scales and shapes
<Cite id="openshaw1984" />, and the **Ecological Fallacy**, where population-level correlations contradict individual
behavior <Cite id="robinson1950" />.
</Definition>

In spatial machine learning and epidemiology, training models on aggregated areal data without accounting for boundary
arbitrariness leads to false causal attribution, geographic gerrymandering susceptibility, and failed out-of-domain
transfer <Cite id="openshaw1984" />.

## The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP)

Identified formally by Openshaw <Cite id="openshaw1984" />, the MAUP demonstrates that statistical summaries of spatial
phenomena are artifacts of the geometric boundaries used to aggregate them. The MAUP manifests in two distinct
components:

### The Scale Effect

The scale effect occurs when data are aggregated into progressively coarser geographic units (e.g. moving from census
blocks $\to$ block groups $\to$ tracts $\to$ counties $\to$ states):
- **Variance Smoothing**: Aggregation averages out local high-frequency idiosyncratic variation. If individual random
  variable $X_i = \mu + \epsilon_i$ has variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean over a zone of $M$ independent individuals
  has variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{M}$.
- **Correlation Inflation**: Because spatial autocorrelation causes neighboring individuals to share similar latent
  regional confounders, averaging over larger spatial units progressively eliminates independent error terms. The
  bivariate Pearson correlation coefficient $r(X, Y)$ systematically inflates toward $\pm 1.0$ as spatial scale becomes
  coarser <Cite id="openshaw1984" />:
  $$
  |r_{\text{coarse}}| \gg |r_{\text{fine}}| \gg |r_{\text{individual}}|
  $$
  A regression model fit on county-level summaries may report $R^2 = 0.85$, while the exact same relationship measured
  on individual households yields $R^2 = 0.08$.

### The Zoning Effect (Shape Effect)

The zoning effect occurs when the spatial scale (number of units $K$) is held constant, but the boundary configurations
are altered or rotated:
- Shifting grid boundaries by half a cell or realigning political districts (gerrymandering) redistributes high-value
  outliers between adjacent partitions.
- Openshaw <Cite id="openshaw1984" /> demonstrated that by reconfiguring boundaries across 99 spatial zones at fixed
  scale, the observed correlation between two socio-economic variables could be engineered to span anywhere from $-0.80$
  to $+0.80$.

## The Ecological Fallacy

The **ecological fallacy** occurs when statistical relationships observed across aggregate areal units are invalidly
attributed to individuals <Cite id="robinson1950" />.

### Robinson's Paradox

In his landmark 1950 paper, Robinson analyzed the correlation between literacy rates and immigrant populations across
the United States <Cite id="robinson1950" />:
- **State-Level Aggregate Correlation**: $r = +0.53$ (states with higher foreign-born proportions exhibited higher
  literacy rates).
- **Individual-Level Micro Correlation**: $r = -0.11$ (individual immigrants had lower English literacy rates than
  native-born citizens).

The paradox arose because immigrants settled disproportionately in industrial northern states with well-funded public
education systems. The areal aggregation captured state-level educational funding rather than individual immigrant
literacy.

### Covariance Decomposition (Simpson's Paradox in Space)

Mathematically, the total covariance between variables $X$ and $Y$ across individuals grouped into areal zones $Z$
decomposes via the law of total covariance:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_Z \left[ \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y \mid Z) \right]}_{\text{Within-Zone Micro Covariance}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}_Z \left( \mathbb{E}[X \mid Z], \mathbb{E}[Y \mid Z] \right)}_{\text{Between-Zone Ecological Covariance}}
$$

If the between-zone covariance is positive while within-zone covariances are negative, the aggregate slope reverses
sign:

$$
\beta_{\text{ecological}} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\bar{X}_Z, \bar{Y}_Z)}{\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_Z)} > 0 \quad \text{while} \quad \beta_{\text{individual}} < 0
$$

Using aggregate areal attributes as proxy inputs for individual prediction (e.g. credit scoring or clinical diagnosis
based on ZIP code) is prone to ecological errors.

## Changing Boundaries and Dasymetric Disaggregation

In real-world workflows, spatial datasets are aggregated to mismatched or evolving geographic partitions:
- School districts vs municipal boundaries vs hospital catchment zones.
- Decennial census redistricting altering tract shapes over time.

### Areal Weighting Interpolation

The simplest disaggregation transfers count data $Y_s$ from source zones $s \in \mathcal{S}$ to target zones
$t \in \mathcal{T}$ assuming uniform density:

$$
\hat{Y}_t = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} Y_s \frac{\operatorname{Area}(s \cap t)}{\operatorname{Area}(s)}
$$

When land use is heterogeneous (e.g. a source tract containing dense urban housing alongside an unpopulated lake or
mountain range), uniform areal weighting assigns population into uninhabited zones.

### Dasymetric Mapping

Dasymetric mapping leverages high-resolution ancillary raster data (such as impervious surface masks, satellite
nighttime lights, or building footprints) to constrain population disaggregation:

$$
\hat{Y}_t = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} Y_s \frac{\sum_{i \in s \cap t} w(L_i)}{\sum_{j \in s} w(L_j)}
$$

where $w(L_i)$ is the relative settlement density weight assigned to land-cover category $L_i$. Restricting allocation
to built-up pixels eliminates uninhabited-zone allocation error.

## Pitfalls

1. **Directly Applying Macro Models to Micro Inference**: Using county-level disease risk models to classify individual
   patient risk ignores within-county variance, committing an ecological fallacy.
2. **Ignoring Census Boundary Churn in Longitudinal Time Series**: Comparing census tract metrics between 2010 and 2020
   directly without standardizing to crosswalk boundary files (e.g. NHGIS or IPUMS crosswalks) treats artificial
   boundary rezoning as genuine demographic shift.
3. **Modelling Spatial Counts Without Population Offsets**: In Poisson or negative binomial regression on areal disease
   or crime counts $Y_i$, omitting the log-transformed population exposure $\log(\text{Pop}_i)$ as an offset leads the
   model to merely learn spatial population density rather than disease incidence rates.
