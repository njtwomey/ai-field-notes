<Definition>
**Spatial features** quantify geographic context for tabular and machine learning models, derived from hierarchical
Discrete Global Grid Systems (DGGS), continuous proximity fields to points of interest (POIs), and spatial lag operators
$\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}$ over neighboring observations. Because spatial phenomena obey Tobler's First Law—exhibiting
non-zero **spatial autocorrelation** ($I \ne 0$)—spatial features violate the independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d.) assumption, requiring spatial block and buffered cross-validation <Cite id="tobler1970, moran1950" />.
</Definition>

Directly passing raw latitude and longitude to tree-based models (such as XGBoost or LightGBM) restricts models to
axis-aligned rectangular splits in angular degrees. Constructing rotation-invariant distance metrics, hierarchical
hexagonal cell indices, and spatial lag convolutions enables models to learn genuine spatial relationships without
geographic overfitting <Cite id="tobler1970" />.

## Hierarchical Grids (Discrete Global Grid Systems)

Discrete Global Grid Systems (DGGS) tessellate the Earth's curved surface into a multi-resolution hierarchy of uniform
equal-area cells, assigning each cell a 64-bit integer identifier:
- **Uber H3 (Hexagonal DGGS)**: Based on an icosahedron projected onto the sphere with aperture-7 hexagonal
  decomposition across 16 hierarchical resolution levels (from resolution 0 with mean area $4.3 \times 10^6\text{ km}^2$
  down to resolution 15 with mean area $0.89\text{ m}^2$):
  - *Hexagonal Uniformity*: Every hexagon has exactly 6 neighbors at identical centroid-to-centroid Euclidean distances
    $\sqrt{3} r$. Unlike square grids (where orthogonal neighbors are distance 1 but diagonal neighbors are distance
    $\sqrt{2}$), hexagons have no diagonal anisotropy.
  - *Icosahedral Pentagons*: By Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$, any spherical hexagonal tessellation requires exactly
    12 pentagons across the globe. H3 positions these 12 pentagons in oceans to avoid populated landmasses.
- **Google S2 (Spherical Quadtree)**: Projects the sphere onto the 6 faces of an inscribed cube, recursively dividing
  each face into a quadtree indexed via the 1D Hilbert space-filling curve. S2 preserves spatial locality in 64-bit
  integer ranges, allowing fast B-tree range queries.
- **Geohash**: Hierarchical string encoding derived by interleaving bits of latitude and longitude in base-32. While
  simple, Geohash suffers from severe boundary discontinuities where two physically adjacent locations across a prime
  meridian or equator share zero common string prefixes.

## Proximity and Neighbourhood Features

Rather than treating coordinates as raw features, spatial feature engineering constructs metric descriptors of the
surrounding built and natural environment:

### Point-of-Interest (POI) Distance and Density

- **Nearest Neighbor Distance**:
  $d_{\min}(\mathbf{x}) = \min_{\mathbf{p} \in \mathcal{P}} d_{\text{geodesic}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p})$ (e.g. distance
  to nearest transit hub, hospital, or coastline).
- **Radial Count**:
  $N_r(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\mathbf{p} \in \mathcal{P}} \mathbb{I}[d_{\text{geodesic}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}) \le r]$.
- **Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)**:
  $$
  \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{N h^2} \sum_{i=1}^N K\left( \frac{\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}_i}{h} \right)
  $$
  where $h$ is the bandwidth parameter and $K(\cdot)$ is a radial basis kernel (Gaussian, Epanechnikov).

### Spatial Lag and Distance Decay

The **spatial lag** of variable $y$ at location $i$ is the weighted average of $y$ across all neighboring locations,
defined by the spatial weight matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ <Cite id="tobler1970" />:

$$
[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{y}]_i = \sum_{j=1}^N W_{ij} y_j, \quad W_{ij} = \frac{w_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^N w_{ik}}
$$

where $w_{ij}$ follows a distance decay function:
- **Inverse Distance Power Law**: $w_{ij} = d_{ij}^{-\alpha}$ ($\alpha \ge 1$), emphasizing immediate
  micro-neighborhoods.
- **Negative Exponential**: $w_{ij} = \exp(-\gamma d_{ij})$, decaying smoothly over regional distances.

## Spatial Autocorrelation (Moran's I)

Tobler's First Law of Geography states: *"Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related
than distant things"* <Cite id="tobler1970" />.

### Global Moran's I

Global Moran's I <Cite id="moran1950" /> measures spatial autocorrelation across continuous variables:

$$
I = \frac{N}{\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N w_{ij}} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N w_{ij} (y_i - \bar{y})(y_j - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \bar{y})^2}
$$

- **$I > \mathbb{E}[I]$ (Positive Spatial Autocorrelation)**: Similar values cluster together in space (high values near
  high values, low near low).
- **$I < \mathbb{E}[I]$ (Negative Spatial Autocorrelation / Dispersion)**: Spatial checkerboard pattern, where high
  values are surrounded by low values.
- **Expected Value under Spatial Randomness**: $\mathbb{E}[I] = -\frac{1}{N - 1}$.

### The Semivariogram

In geostatistics, spatial dependence is mapped by the **semivariogram** $\gamma(h)$ as a function of separation distance
$h$:

$$
\gamma(h) = \frac{1}{2 |N(h)|} \sum_{(i, j) \in N(h)} (y_i - y_j)^2
$$

A typical variogram displays:
1. **Nugget ($c_0$)**: Microscale measurement noise and short-range variation at $h \to 0$.
2. **Sill ($c$)**: The asymptotic variance plateau where observations become spatially independent.
3. **Range ($a$)**: The separation distance beyond which spatial correlation ceases ($\gamma(h) = c$).

## Spatial Cross-Validation

Standard random $k$-fold cross-validation assigns instances to train and test folds uniformly at random. When data
possess spatial autocorrelation ($I \gg 0$), test observations are spatially adjacent to training observations:

$$
y_{\text{test}} = f(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}) + \epsilon_{\text{test}}, \quad \operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_{\text{test}}, \epsilon_{\text{train}}) > 0
$$

The model exploits spatially correlated residuals (e.g. unobserved local microclimate or regional wealth) to "cheat",
producing overly optimistic cross-validation scores that collapse when the model is deployed in a new city or
unmonitored geographic region <Cite id="tobler1970" />.

```
Random k-fold:           Spatial Block k-fold:       Buffered Leave-One-Out:
[T] [x] [T] [T] [x]      [T] [T] [T] | [x] [x] [x]   [T] [T] [Buffer Zone] [x]
[x] [T] [x] [T] [T]      [T] [T] [T] | [x] [x] [x]   [T] [T] [  (Purged) ] [x]
(Heavy Spatial Leakage)  (Disjoint Block Clusters)   (Guaranteed Zero Leakage)
```

### Spatial Partitioning Strategies

1. **Spatial Block Cross-Validation**: Divides the bounding domain into contiguous geographic blocks (or administrative
   regions), holding out entire contiguous tiles as test folds.
2. **Buffered Cross-Validation**: When testing on a geographic zone, an exclusion buffer of radius
   $r_{\text{buffer}} \ge a$ (where $a$ is the variogram range) is purged from the training set. Eliminating training
   points within range $a$ guarantees that training and test errors are statistically independent.

## Pitfalls

1. **Random Splitting on Autocorrelated Coordinates**: Reporting $R^2 = 0.90$ under standard 5-fold CV on housing prices
   or species distribution models that collapses to $R^2 = 0.55$ when tested on an unseen neighboring county.
2. **Boundary Edge Effects**: Computing spatial neighbor counts or KDE without edge corrections causes points near the
   periphery of the study area to exhibit artificially depressed neighbor statistics simply because observations beyond
   the administrative boundary were not collected.
3. **Target Encoding on High-Resolution Spatial Grids**: Applying target encoding to fine-grained cell IDs (e.g. H3
   resolution 10) without strong regularization leaks label identity directly into training features, overfitting to
   single households.
