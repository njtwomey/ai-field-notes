<Definition>
A **modality** is a kind of data defined by its form: tables, series, sound, images,
video, text, documents, graphs, molecules, locations, 3D shapes, or combinations of these. Each modality has its own
storage formats, its own preprocessing and augmentation, its own annotation types and its own way of making examples
dependent. This subject collects, for each modality, what differs from the general methods of data preparation.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The general techniques of Part III, such as scaling, encoding and
<NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">grouped splits</NoteLink>, are written for tables. They carry over to
other modalities only in part. Images are normalised per channel, not per column. Series are normalised per series, from
the past. Molecules are split by scaffold, graphs by what is hidden, locations by spatial blocks. Each modality also has
failure modes of its own: decoding cost for video, contamination for text corpora,
<NoteLink to="temporal-synchronisation">clock drift</NoteLink> for sensors. A modality note links back to the general
note and adds only what changes.

## Main ideas

**Format fixes what a model can see.** Sample rate and codec for audio, bit depth and colour space for images,
keyframes for video, encodings and scripts for text, coordinate systems for maps. The first note of most chapters is
about the raw format.

**Preprocessing brings examples to a common form.** Resampling, resizing, cropping, normalisation, segmentation and
windowing make examples comparable. Each is fitted on training data or uses statistics from the past only.

**Augmentation encodes the invariances of the modality.** Images tolerate flips and crops, audio tolerates noise and
reverberation, series tolerate small warps, tables tolerate little. Only changes that keep the label are safe.

**Dependence decides the split.** Patients, subjects, scaffolds, protein families, graph neighbourhoods, spatial blocks
and time all create groups of related examples. Each chapter states the split that respects them, following
<NoteLink to="matching-the-split-to-deployment" />.

**Modalities combine.** Real systems pair images with text, audio with video and sensors with labels. Pairing,
synchronisation and fusion are treated once, in <NoteLink to="multimodal-and-paired-data" />.

The chapters can be read in any order after <NoteLink to="data-preparation" />:

- <NoteLink to="tabular-and-relational-data" />: layout, multi-table schemas, panels and table representations;

  <NoteLink to="forecasting-setups">forecasting setups</NoteLink>, events and sensors;

  augmentation and transcripts;

  <NoteLink to="specialised-imaging-data">specialised imaging</NoteLink>;
- <NoteLink to="video-data" />: codecs, clip sampling, motion and annotation, building on images;
- <NoteLink to="text-data-and-corpora" />: cleaning, corpora, contamination and instruction data;
- <NoteLink to="document-data" />: parsing, OCR and logs;
- <NoteLink to="graph-and-network-data" />: construction, features and splits;

  <NoteLink to="scaffold-and-similarity-splits">scaffold splits</NoteLink>;
- <NoteLink to="geospatial-data" />: coordinates, <NoteLink to="vector-and-raster-data">vector and raster
  data</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="spatial-features">spatial features</NoteLink> and imagery;

  modalities</NoteLink>, curation and fusion.

Tables come first because the rest of Part III assumes them. Video assumes images, documents assume text, and molecules
assume graphs. The multimodal chapter is best read last.
