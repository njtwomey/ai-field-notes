<Definition>
A multimodal dataset has missing modalities when some examples lack one or more of their inputs, during training, at
prediction or both. Its modalities are unequal when they differ in how informative, noisy, plentiful or quickly learned
they are. Both break the assumption that every example offers every modality on equal terms, and both are handled in how
the data are prepared and presented as much as in the model.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A video has no audio track. A patient has blood tests but no scan. A wearable's battery dies halfway through a day. A
social-media user declines to share their location. Missing modalities arise from sensor limits, cost, privacy and data
loss, and are the normal state of multimodal data collected outside the laboratory <Cite id="wu2024missing" />.

A model trained only on complete examples learns to use its inputs jointly. When one input is absent at prediction, the
model receives something it never saw in training, such as a vector of zeros, and its output can be worse than that of a
model that never had the second modality. Unequal modalities cause a related failure during training: when one modality
predicts the label easily, a jointly trained model leans on it and under-uses the others.

## Patterns of missingness

Missingness of a whole modality follows the same mechanisms as missing values in a table (see
<NoteLink to="missing-data-and-imputation">missing data and imputation</NoteLink>). It is missing completely at random
when a file is lost by accident; at random given other inputs when a camera fails more often at night, which the
timestamp records; and not at random when a doctor orders a scan because the patient looks ill. In the last case the
absence of the scan is informative about the label.

A dataset's missingness is described by its patterns, the sets of modalities present, and their frequencies. Three
modalities give up to seven non-empty patterns. Recording, for each example, a presence indicator per modality keeps
the pattern available to the model and to the analysis (see <NoteLink to="encoding-missingness">encoding
missingness</NoteLink>), and a missing input is then represented by a learned placeholder rather than a value that looks
real, such as a black image or an empty string.

## Missing modalities at training time

There are four main ways to use incomplete training data.

- **Drop incomplete examples.** Simple, wasteful, and biased unless the modality is missing completely at random. When
  most examples are incomplete, little is left.
- **Train a model per modality and combine those available.** Late fusion (see
  <NoteLink to="multimodal-data-layouts">data layouts for fusion</NoteLink>) handles any pattern at prediction by
  combining the predictions of the present modalities, at the cost of learning no interactions between them.
- **Impute the missing modality.** Fill it with a learned placeholder or a mean embedding, or generate it from the
  modalities present, for example with an autoencoder trained to reconstruct one modality from the others
  <Cite id="tran2017" />. Generated inputs carry the generator's errors, and the model cannot tell them from observed
  ones unless an indicator says so.
- **Train for the missing case.** SMIL uses Bayesian meta-learning to train with up to 90% of examples missing a
  modality <Cite id="ma2021smil" />. Missing-aware prompts add a small learned prompt for each missingness pattern to a
  pretrained multimodal transformer, with under 1% of the full model's parameters <Cite id="lee2023missing" />.

## Missing modalities at prediction time

A model's robustness to missing inputs is measured by evaluating it under each pattern it will meet. ViLT, a transformer
trained on images and text of the MM-IMDb movie dataset, reached a macro-F1 of 55.3 with all modalities. When only 30%
of test examples had text, it fell to 31.2, below the 35.0 of a model trained on images alone. The best way to fuse the
modalities for robustness differed between datasets, even for the same model <Cite id="ma2022missing" />.

The practical rule follows: the <NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">test set</NoteLink> reproduces the
missingness patterns expected in use, results are reported per pattern, and every multimodal model is compared with the
best single-modality model under the same patterns.

## Modality dropout

Modality dropout removes whole modalities at random during training, so that the model learns to predict from every
subset. ModDrop dropped individual input channels of a gesture recogniser at random while training their fusion, which
made the classifier robust to missing signals in one or more channels <Cite id="neverova2016" />. It is
<NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> applied to inputs grouped by modality.

As a data operation, modality dropout replaces a modality's input by the same placeholder and indicator used for truly
missing data. The drop probability for each modality, and whether all modalities may be dropped together, are choices:
dropping each modality with the frequency it is missing in deployment trains the model on the patterns it will meet.

## Modality imbalance

A multimodal network can do worse than its best single-modality part. On the Kinetics action-recognition benchmark,
late-fusion networks trained jointly were below the best single-modality network in every combination: audio with RGB
frames reached 71.4% top-1 accuracy against 72.6% for RGB alone, and all three of audio, RGB and optical flow reached
70.0% <Cite id="wang2020gradientblending" />. The joint networks had higher training accuracy and lower validation
accuracy. Two causes were identified: more capacity overfits more, and modalities overfit and generalise at different
rates, so one training schedule suits none of them. Gradient-Blending weights each modality's loss by its estimated
overfitting <Cite id="wang2020gradientblending" />.

When one modality reduces the loss quickly, the gradients that train the others shrink, and their encoders stay
under-optimised. On-the-fly gradient modulation tracks each modality's contribution to the objective and slows the
dominant modality's updates <Cite id="peng2022ogm" />. On the data side, the same imbalance is checked by training and
evaluating a model per modality alongside the joint model, and by adding a loss on each modality's own prediction, so
that each encoder learns even when another modality suffices for the training labels.

Modalities are also unequal in quantity. Text and images alone are plentiful while
<NoteLink to="cross-modal-pairing">paired data</NoteLink> are scarce, so encoders are commonly pretrained on each
modality separately and then joined on the smaller paired set.

## Assumptions

- The missingness patterns and their frequencies at prediction time are known or can be estimated.
- A placeholder for a missing modality is distinguishable from any real input.
- Modalities missing for reasons tied to the label are identified, so that the model's use of their absence is a
  deliberate choice.

## Pitfalls

- **Missing inputs that look real.** Zeros, black frames and silence are real values of some inputs. Without an
  indicator, the model confuses absence with content.
- **A complete test set for an incomplete world.** Evaluating only complete examples hides the drop that missing inputs
  cause in use.
- **Absence as a shortcut.** When a modality is missing more often for one class, the model can learn the class from
  the presence indicator. This is legitimate only if the same relation holds in deployment.
- **Imputed data evaluated as observed.** Scores on imputed inputs measure the imputer as much as the model.
- **No single-modality baseline.** A multimodal model that loses to its best single input is only visible when that
  baseline is reported.
