<Definition>
A paired multimodal dataset links each example in one modality to one or more counterparts in another: an image to its
captions, a recording to its transcript, a video segment to its narration. The pairing is itself data. It can be written
for the example or found next to it, correct or wrong, one-to-one or many-to-many, and it can link whole examples or
parts of them, such as a phrase to an image region or a word to a time interval.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In a paired dataset the label of an image is its caption. Everything said about labels applies to pairs: they can be
missing, wrong, ambiguous or noisier in some parts of the data than in others. Contrastive training, the usual way to
learn from pairs (see <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive learning</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="clip">CLIP</NoteLink>), adds a second assumption: every partner in the batch other than an example's own
is a negative. A wrong pair teaches the model to pull unrelated things together; a missing pair, a second caption that
also fits, teaches it to push related things apart.

## Kinds of pairs

Pairs differ by how the link between the two halves was made.

- **Written for the example.** Annotators describe each image. <Gloss name="coco" /> Captions provides five independent
  human captions for each training and validation image, over one and a half million captions for over 330,000 images
  <Cite id="chen2015cococaptions" />. Such pairs are accurate and expensive, and they describe what annotators were
  asked to describe.
- **Found next to the example.** Web pages give images alt-text. Conceptual Captions filtered about 3.3 million such
  pairs and replaced proper names with general terms, such as "actor" for a named person <Cite id="sharma2018" />.
  ALIGN used over a billion alt-text pairs with almost no filtering and found that the scale of the corpus made up for
  its noise <Cite id="jia2021" />.
- **Recorded at the same time.** A video's narration, a meeting's speech and its slides, a sensor stream and the
  activity it measures. HowTo100M cut 136 million clips with their narrations from 1.22 million instructional videos; in
  a manual check of 400 clip–narration pairs, only 51% mentioned at least one object or action visible in the clip
  <Cite id="miech2019" />. Co-occurrence in time makes these pairs depend on
  <NoteLink to="temporal-synchronisation">temporal synchronisation</NoteLink>.
- **Produced by a model.** Transcripts from a speech recogniser, captions from a captioning model. They carry the
  model's error rate and style (see <NoteLink to="paired-data-curation">curating paired data</NoteLink> on rewriting).
- **Paired by construction.** Rendered scenes with their scene descriptions, or two views of one object. The pairing is
  exact, and the data differ from real data.

## Weak and noisy pairs

A pair can fail in several ways: the text describes something else on the page; it describes only part of the image; it
describes the right event at the wrong moment; or it is boilerplate, such as a file name. Web-collected Conceptual
Captions is estimated to contain 3% to 20% incorrect pairs <Cite id="huang2021ncr" />.

Wrong pairs are label noise, and the methods of <NoteLink to="learning-with-noisy-labels">learning with noisy
labels</NoteLink> carry over. The Noisy Correspondence Rectifier uses the observation that networks fit clean pairs
before noisy ones: it splits training pairs into clean and noisy sets by their loss and then corrects the
correspondence labels of the noisy set <Cite id="huang2021ncr" />.

Pairs that are right but misaligned in time call for a different treatment. A narrator often describes an action before
or after it happens. MIL-NCE treats the narrations near a clip as a bag of candidate positives, of which at least one is
expected to match, and scores the bag as a whole; this is
<NoteLink to="multiple-instance-learning">multiple-instance learning</NoteLink> applied to pairing
<Cite id="miech2020" />.

## Grounding regions and segments

A pair-level link says that a caption describes an image; a part-level link says which words describe which pixels.
Flickr30k Entities adds to Flickr30k's 158,000 captions 244,000 chains linking mentions of the same entity across an
image's captions, and associates them with 276,000 hand-drawn bounding boxes <Cite id="plummer2015" />. Visual Genome
annotates over 100,000 images with, on average, 21 objects, 18 attributes and 18 relationships between objects each,
together with region descriptions <Cite id="krishna2017" />. The time-based equivalents link words to intervals of
audio (see <NoteLink to="speech-transcripts-and-alignment">speech transcripts and alignment</NoteLink>) and sentences to
moments in a video.

Part-level links are stored as coordinates in one half of the pair and spans in the other (see
<NoteLink to="image-annotation-formats">image annotation formats</NoteLink>). Every transform of the image must update
the coordinates, and a transform can break the text: a crop can remove the region a phrase refers to.

## Many-to-many correspondences

Most pairings are many-to-many in truth and one-to-one in the data. An image fits many captions, and a caption such as
"a man riding a wave on a surfboard" fits many images. A dataset records only the pairs that were collected; every other
fitting pair is unrecorded and, in contrastive training and retrieval evaluation, counted as a mismatch.

The effect on evaluation is measurable. ECCV Caption added machine-proposed, human-verified correspondences to COCO's
validation images and found 3.6 times as many positive image-to-caption associations and 8.5 times as many
caption-to-image associations as the original annotations record; rankings of 25 models changed when evaluated against
the extended correspondences <Cite id="chun2022" />. Probabilistic embeddings represent each image and caption as a
distribution rather than a point, so that one caption can sit close to several images <Cite id="chun2021" />.

## Assumptions

- Each recorded pair is a true correspondence, or its errors can be modelled as label noise.
- Pairs not recorded are mismatches; this holds only approximately and fails most for generic captions.
- Part-level coordinates and spans refer to the same version of each half: the same image resolution, crop and text
  tokenisation.

## Pitfalls

- **Splitting by pair instead of by item.** An image with five captions split across training and test sets leaks the
  image into the test set. Splits are made by image, by video or by source document (see
  <NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">train, validation and test splits</NoteLink>).
- **Augmentations that change the text's truth.** A horizontal flip makes "on the left" wrong; colour jitter makes "a
  red car" wrong; a crop can remove the object a caption names. Augmentation of paired data must preserve the pairing or
  edit the text with it.
- **<Gloss name="fn">False negatives</Gloss> in retrieval metrics.** Recall at $K$ against one recorded partner
  understates a model that retrieves an unrecorded but correct partner, and penalises models unequally.
- **Model-made pairs as ground truth.** Transcripts and captions from models contain the models' errors; evaluating a
  model against them measures agreement with another model.
- **Duplicates across splits.** The same image, or the same caption template, can appear many times in web data and in
  both training and test sets.
