<Definition>
**Panel data** (also known as **longitudinal data**) consist of repeated observations of the same cross-sectional units
$i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ (individuals, patients, firms, or devices) recorded across multiple time periods
$t \in \{1, \dots, T_i\}$ <Cite id="wooldridge2010" />. Because repeated measurements from the same unit share
unobserved entity-specific characteristics and exhibit temporal autocorrelation, observations violate the standard
independent and identically distributed ($\text{i.i.d.}$) assumption of supervised learning.
</Definition>

In machine learning applications, longitudinal records appear ubiquitously: electronic health records (EHR) track
patient vitals over clinic visits, SaaS analytics monitor monthly subscriber engagement, and credit bureaus evaluate
customer repayment trajectories over billing cycles. 

Unlike pure cross-sectional data—which record a single snapshot per entity—and pure univariate time series—which record
a single long trajectory ($N=1, T \gg 1$)—panel data typically exhibit the "micro-panel" structure: a massive number of
entities observed over relatively short time horizons ($N \gg T$). Effectively modeling panel data requires decomposing
within-unit from between-unit variance, accounting for informative panel attrition, engineering historical entity
baselines, and enforcing group-aware evaluation splits.

## Wide and long layouts

The physical organization of panel data governs how machine learning algorithms access historical measurements:

### Wide layout

In wide format, each row represents exactly one entity $i$, and repeated observations across time periods
$t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$ are arranged horizontally across distinct columns:

$$
\mathbf{x}_i = [x_{i, 1}, x_{i, 2}, \dots, x_{i, T}] \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times K}
$$

- **Advantages:** Fits directly into standard rectangular tabular algorithms (GBDTs, MLPs) without duplicating
  entity-level features or creating row dependencies.
- **Limitations:** Requires a fixed, balanced observation grid where all units share identical observation timestamps.
  If observation intervals vary or units suffer irregular missing periods, the wide matrix becomes heavily sparse with
  structural missing values.

### Long layout

In long format, each row represents a single entity-period observation $(i, t)$, identified by a composite key of entity
ID and timestamp:

$$
(i, t, \mathbf{x}_{it}, y_{it})
$$

- **Advantages:** Naturally accommodates unbalanced panels, irregular observation cadences, and dynamic covariates. It
  forms the standard input representation for sequential architectures (RNNs, Temporal Fusion Transformers) and
  mixed-effects regression models.
- **Limitations:** Duplicates static entity attributes across all $T_i$ rows and introduces severe autocorrelation
  across rows, requiring explicit group-aware grouping during cross-validation.

## Within-unit and between-unit variation

A defining characteristic of panel data is the decomposition of total variance into two orthogonal sources
<Cite id="wooldridge2010" />:

$$
\text{SST} = \text{SS}_{\text{between}} + \text{SS}_{\text{within}}
$$

$$
\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{T} (x_{it} - \bar{x})^2 = T \sum_{i=1}^N (\bar{x}_i - \bar{x})^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T (x_{it} - \bar{x}_i)^2
$$

1. **Between-unit variation:** The variance of entity-specific historical means
   $\bar{x}_i = \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} x_{it}$ around the grand population mean $\bar{x}$. This captures
   cross-sectional differences (e.g., patient $A$ has a naturally higher resting heart rate than patient $B$).
2. **Within-unit variation:** The variance of temporal deviations $(x_{it} - \bar{x}_i)$ around the entity's own
   baseline. This captures longitudinal dynamics and state changes over time (e.g., patient $A$'s heart rate spiking
   during an acute infection relative to their personal baseline).

In econometric modeling, **fixed effects** estimators eliminate time-invariant unobserved confounders $\alpha_i$ by
applying the within-transformation (subtracting entity means $\bar{x}_i$). 

In contrast, standard tree-based machine learning models (XGBoost, LightGBM) trained naively on long-format tables
cannot internally decouple within-unit dynamics from between-unit cross-sectional bias. If an entity's baseline is
strongly predictive of the label, the model often overfits to entity identity rather than learning generalizable
temporal transition rules.

## Unbalanced panels and dropout

A panel is **balanced** if all units are observed across the exact same set of $T$ periods ($T_i = T \ \forall i$). In
production data, panels are almost always **unbalanced**: units enter the study late (cohort staggered entry),
experience intermittent missed appointments, or exit the panel permanently (**attrition** or **dropout**).

The validity of predictive modeling on unbalanced panels depends critically on the missingness mechanism:

- **Missing Completely at Random (MCAR):** Attrition is completely independent of both observed features and the
  underlying target outcome (e.g., random network packet drops on a sensor). Unbalanced panels under MCAR can be
  analyzed without selection bias corrections.
- **Missing at Random (MAR):** Missingness depends systematically on observed historical features (e.g., younger users
  exhibiting higher application churn rates). MAR can be addressed via inverse probability weighting or conditioned
  sequential modeling.
- **Missing Not at Random (MNAR / Informative Attrition):** Dropout depends directly on unobserved latent variables or
  the target outcome itself (e.g., severely declining clinical patients dropping out due to mortality or intensive care
  transfer). Analyzing only surviving units introduces severe **survivorship bias**, causing models to underestimate
  failure rates.

## Features from a unit's history

To provide tabular learning models with longitudinal context from long-format panels without violating temporal
causality, feature engineers construct historical summary features relative to prediction timestamp $t$:

- **Lagged attributes:** Historical values from prior periods ($x_{i, t-1}, x_{i, t-2}$).
- **Delta and velocity features:** First-order changes ($\Delta x_{it} = x_{it} - x_{i, t-1}$) and second-order
  acceleration ($\Delta^2 x_{it} = \Delta x_{it} - \Delta x_{i, t-1}$).
- **Rolling window statistics:** Moving averages, rolling standard deviations, and exponential moving averages over
  backward windows $[t-k, t-1]$.
- **Deviation from entity baseline:** Measuring how extreme the current observation is relative to the unit's known
  personal history:
  $$
  z_{it} = \frac{x_{it} - \bar{x}_{i, <t}}{\sigma_{i, <t} + \epsilon}
  $$
  where $\bar{x}_{i, <t}$ and $\sigma_{i, <t}$ are computed strictly over past observations $s < t$ to prevent
  look-ahead target leakage.

## Splitting panels

Splitting panel data into training, validation, and test partitions requires aligning the validation strategy with the
operational deployment scenario:

1. **Group entity split (Out-of-unit generalization):** All observations corresponding to entity $i$ are assigned
   exclusively to either the training set or the test set. This evaluates the model's ability to generalize to
   completely novel, unseen individuals (e.g., deploying a clinical diagnostic tool on new patients admitted to a
   hospital).
2. **Temporal cutoff split (Out-of-time forecasting):** Observations for all entities up to timestamp $t_{\text{split}}$
   are placed in the training set, while all observations after $t_{\text{split}}$ form the evaluation set. This
   evaluates whether the model can forecast future states of existing known entities.
3. **Grouped-temporal split:** Enforces both axes simultaneously: the test set contains only future observations
   ($t > t_{\text{split}}$) from entities never seen during training ($i \notin \mathcal{I}_{\text{train}}$),
   replicating real-world production deployment on future incoming cohorts.

Naive random row-level splitting on long-format panel tables causes catastrophic cross-contamination: because repeated
measurements of entity $i$ appear across both train and test splits, the model exploits entity autocorrelation to
achieve artificially high validation scores that fail in production.

## Pitfalls

- **Nickell dynamic panel bias:** In autoregressive longitudinal models, including lagged dependent variables
  $y_{i, t-1}$ alongside unit-level fixed effects introduces asymptotic bias of order $O(1/T)$ due to correlation
  between the de-meaned lagged regressor and the de-meaned error term <Cite id="arellano1991" />. Correct estimation
  requires generalized method of moments (GMM) instrumental variable estimators.
- **Survivorship bias from filtering incomplete trajectories:** Filtering out units with fewer than $k$ observations
  removes early-failing or high-attrition entities, biasing training data toward resilient or long-lived units.
- **Look-ahead leakage in historical baselines:** Calculating entity summary statistics (such as entity lifetime mean
  $\bar{x}_i$) across the full duration of the dataset rather than strictly expanding historical windows prior to $t$
  leaks future information into past predictions.
- **Confounding within-unit trends with cohort entry bias (Simpson's paradox):** When different cohorts enter the panel
  at different baseline values, cross-sectional comparisons can show a positive association while individual temporal
  trajectories show a negative association.
