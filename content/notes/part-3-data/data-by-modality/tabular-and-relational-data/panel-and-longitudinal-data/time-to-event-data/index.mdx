<Definition>
**Time-to-event data** (also known as **survival data** or **duration data**) measure the elapsed time
$T \in \mathbb{R}^+$ from a well-defined temporal origin $t=0$ until the occurrence of a discrete target event (such as
clinical mortality, customer churn, hardware failure, or credit default) <Cite id="kaplan1958, cox1972" />. The
distinguishing characteristic of time-to-event data is **censoring**: for a substantial fraction of observations, the
follow-up window terminates before the event occurs, yielding only an inequality constraint on the true duration
($T > C$).
</Definition>

In classical supervised learning, targets are either real-valued scalar regression quantities $y \in \mathbb{R}$ or
categorical class labels $y \in \{0, 1\}$. Time-to-event data break both paradigms. Treating duration as an ordinary
regression target ignores the fact that censored cases have not yet experienced the event; conversely, converting the
task into a binary classification label over a fixed time horizon discards duration granularity and biases against cases
lost to follow-up.

Proper tabular curation of time-to-event records requires formatting durations and censoring indicators, managing
dynamic time-varying covariates via counting-process tables, dynamic risk evaluation via landmarking, and accounting for
competing risks.

## Durations and censoring indicators

Each observation in a standard right-censored survival table is represented as a triplet
$(T_i^*, \delta_i, \mathbf{x}_i)$:

$$
T_i^* = \min(T_i, C_i)
$$

$$
\delta_i = \mathbb{I}(T_i \le C_i) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if event observed (uncensored)} \\ 0 & \text{if right-censored} \end{cases}
$$

where $T_i$ represents the latent true event time, $C_i$ represents the latent censoring time, and
$\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the baseline covariate vector.

### Types of censoring

- **Right censoring:** The most prevalent form in industrial machine learning. The observation period ends at time $C_i$
  without observing the event. The true event time is known only to satisfy $T_i > C_i$.
- **Left censoring:** The event occurred at some unknown point prior to observation entry ($T_i < C_i$). For example, a
  patient tests positive for antibodies at study entry, but the exact infection date is unobserved.
- **Interval censoring:** The event is known only to have occurred within a bounded time interval $[L_i, R_i]$. This
  arises frequently in routine medical screenings or periodic machinery inspections.

### Non-informative censoring

Valid statistical modeling of survival data requires the **non-informative (independent) censoring assumption**:

$$
T_i \perp C_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i
$$

Conditional on observed features $\mathbf{x}_i$, an individual censored at time $c$ must face the same subsequent risk
of experiencing the event as individuals who remain under observation past time $c$. If censoring is informative (e.g.,
severely ill patients systematically drop out of a clinical study due to declining functional status), standard survival
estimators become severely biased unless explicitly corrected.

### Nonparametric survival estimation (Kaplan-Meier)

The empirical survival function $S(t) = P(T > t)$ is estimated nonparametrically via the product-limit estimator
<Cite id="kaplan1958" />:

$$
\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)
$$

where $t_i$ denotes unique event timestamps, $d_i$ is the number of events occurring at $t_i$, and $n_i$ is the size of
the active **risk set** immediately prior to $t_i$ (the number of individuals alive and uncensored). As individuals are
censored, they exit the risk set $n_i$ without triggering an event $d_i$, properly adjusting downstream survival
fractions.

## Time-varying covariates

In many applications, features evolve dynamically throughout the follow-up window. A patient's blood pressure fluctuates
across weekly visits, and a subscriber's platform usage shifts across billing periods.

### Counting-process (Start-Stop) format

To incorporate time-varying covariates into tabular survival models (such as extended Cox proportional hazards models
<Cite id="cox1972" />), the data must be reshaped from a single row per entity into multiple non-overlapping interval
rows in **start-stop format**:

$$
\text{Row}_{i, k} = (i, t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{stop}}, \delta_{i, k}, \mathbf{z}_i(t_{\text{start}}))
$$

where:
- Interval $[t_{\text{start}}, t_{\text{stop}})$ defines the window during which the covariate vector
  $\mathbf{z}_i(t_{\text{start}})$ remained constant.
- The event indicator $\delta_{i, k} = 0$ for all intermediate intervals, and $\delta_{i, k} = 1$ only in the final
  interval if the event occurred at $t_{\text{stop}}$.
- At any event time $t$, an individual enters the risk set $n(t)$ if and only if
  $t_{\text{start}} \le t < t_{\text{stop}}$.

### Internal vs external covariates

- **External covariates:** Factors whose future path does not depend on the individual's survival status (e.g., seasonal
  temperature, macro-economic interest rates).
- **Internal covariates:** Biological or behavioral measurements generated directly by the individual (e.g., organ
  biomarker levels, user engagement scores). Internal covariates exist only while the entity remains alive and active,
  requiring careful interpretation when estimating causal interventions.

## Landmarking

While counting-process formats support semi-parametric hazard modeling, training modern tree-based ensembles (such as
XGBoost, LightGBM, or Random Survival Forests) directly on longitudinal event sequences is challenging due to interval
dependencies and varying sequence lengths.

**Landmarking** provides a robust tabular framework for dynamic risk prediction <Cite id="vanhouwelingen2007" />:

1. **Select a landmark time $s > 0$ and prediction horizon $\Delta t$:** For instance, predicting 2-year mortality
   ($\Delta t = 24\text{ mo}$) dynamically for patients who have already survived to year 1 ($s = 12\text{ mo}$).
2. **Filter the landmark cohort:** Retain only entities who are event-free and under active observation at time $s$
   ($T_i^* > s$). Individuals who suffered the event or were censored prior to $s$ are excluded.
3. **Freeze historical covariates up to $s$:** Aggregate and summarize all dynamic covariates measured over the window
   $[0, s]$ (e.g., trajectory slope, maximum biomarker value, cumulative visits).
4. **Define the target over the horizon window:** For the landmark cohort, the target outcome is evaluated over
   $[s, s + \Delta t]$:
   - Event observed in $(s, s + \Delta t] \implies Y = 1$
   - Survives past $s + \Delta t \implies Y = 0$
   - Censored before $s + \Delta t \implies$ Handled via inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW).

Landmarking eliminates immortal time bias, avoids future information leakage, and converts complex longitudinal history
into fixed-width tabular matrices suitable for standard ML pipelines.

## Competing risks

In many practical survival settings, an individual can experience one of several mutually exclusive event types, such
that the occurrence of one event precludes the occurrence of all others. Examples include:
- A clinical trial where patients can experience disease recurrence or death from unrelated causes.
- Customer retention where a subscriber can voluntarily churn, face involuntary fraudulent termination, or upgrade to an
  enterprise tier.

### The fallacy of 1 - KM

A common pitfall is to isolate the primary event of interest (e.g., voluntary churn) and treat all competing events
(e.g., fraudulent termination) as ordinary right-censoring. 

Treating competing events as censoring violates the independent censoring assumption: an individual whose account is
terminated for fraud is structurally prevented from ever voluntarily churning. The naive Kaplan-Meier complement
$1 - \hat{S}_{\text{naive}}(t)$ assumes that "censored" competing cases remain capable of experiencing voluntary churn
at the same rate as active accounts, severely overestimating the true event probability.

### Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF)

Under competing risks with $K$ causes, the proper quantity of interest is the **Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF)**:

$$
\text{CIF}_k(t) = P(T \le t, \text{Cause} = k) = \int_0^t S(u^-) h_k(u) \, du
$$

where $h_k(u)$ is the cause-specific hazard of event $k$, and $S(u^-)$ is the overall event-free survival probability
across all causes. The subdistribution hazard approach formalized by Fine and Gray directly models covariates against
the CIF rather than the cause-specific hazard.

## Pitfalls

- **Converting survival into naive binary classification:** Labeling entities as $Y=1$ if an event occurred within 12
  months and $Y=0$ otherwise discards censoring timing. An entity censored at month 2 without an event is mislabeled as
  $Y=0$, introducing severe label contamination.
- **Immortal time bias:** Conditioned analyses that assign a treatment label based on events occurring after the start
  of follow-up (e.g., comparing patients who received a heart transplant against those who did not, where transplant
  recipients had to survive long enough to find a donor) induce artificial survival advantages.
- **Naive dropping of right-censored cases:** Removing all instances where $\delta_i = 0$ leaves only early-failing
  units, heavily skewing duration distributions and causing severe underestimation of true survival times.
- **Look-ahead leakage in landmarking:** Including diagnostic tests, prescriptions, or log-in events that occurred after
  the landmark cutoff $s$ when assembling feature vectors for the landmark cohort leaks future knowledge into historical
  predictors.
