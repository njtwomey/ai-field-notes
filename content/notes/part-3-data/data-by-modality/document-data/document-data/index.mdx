<Definition>
**Document data** are texts that carry structure beyond a sequence of characters: the pages, columns, headings, tables
and forms of <Gloss name="pdf" plural /> and web pages, the pixels of a scanned page, or the fixed templates of
machine-written log lines. Preparing them means recovering both the text and its structure, and the errors made in that
recovery become noise in every later feature.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Contracts, invoices, scientific papers, medical letters and web pages hold much of the text that models are asked to
read. Server and application logs hold much of the evidence for diagnosing systems. Neither arrives as clean prose. A
PDF stores glyphs at coordinates with no reading order. A scan stores only pixels. A log line mixes a constant template
with variable values. Document understanding, retrieval over company documents and log anomaly detection all depend on
how well this structure is recovered.

## Main ideas

**Parsing recovers order and structure.** <NoteLink to="document-parsing" /> extracts text from PDFs and HTML with its
reading order, headings, tables and figures. Multi-column pages, running headers and tables split across pages are the
common failures. Layout datasets such as PubLayNet <Cite id="zhong2019publaynet" /> provide labelled regions for
training layout detectors.

**OCR adds positions to text.** <NoteLink to="ocr-and-layout" /> turns images of text into words with bounding boxes.
Recognition errors follow patterns such as confusions between similar characters. Layout-aware models such as
LayoutLM take the word positions as input alongside the words <Cite id="xu2020layoutlm" />, which helps most on forms
with key-value pairs.

**Logs are templates plus variables.** <NoteLink to="log-parsing" /> separates each log line into a constant template
and its variable fields, as the Drain parser does with a fixed-depth tree <Cite id="he2017drain" />. Lines are then
grouped into sessions, which turns free-text logs into event sequences.
