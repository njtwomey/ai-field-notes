<Definition>
**Multilingual text** encompasses corpora composed of multiple human natural languages and orthographic writing systems.
Unlike monolingual text in Latin scripts, multilingual data incorporates diverse Unicode blocks, writing directions
(left-to-right and right-to-left), unsegmented writing systems without whitespace boundaries, romanised
**transliteration**, and intra-sentential **code-switching**. Preprocessing multilingual text requires script-aware
normalisation, morphological segmentation, and balanced subword vocabularies to avoid computational penalties across
languages.
</Definition>

## Scripts and orthographies

Natural human languages are expressed across dozens of distinct orthographic systems codified by the Unicode Consortium:

- **Alphabetic scripts (Latin, Cyrillic, Greek):** Consist of discrete consonant and vowel glyphs arranged
  left-to-right, separated by whitespace.
- **Abjads (Arabic, Hebrew):** Consonant-primary scripts written right-to-left (RTL). Short vowels are marked with
  optional diacritics (harakat in Arabic, niqqud in Hebrew) that are frequently omitted in informal web text, producing
  homographic ambiguities.
- **Abugidas (Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil, Thai):** Syllabic alphabets where consonants carry an inherent vowel, modified
  by vowel diacritics (matras) and consonant conjuncts (ligatures) formed with the Unicode virama (halant, `U+094D`).
- **Logographic and ideographic scripts (Chinese Hanzi, Japanese Kanji):** Morphosyllabic characters where each glyph
  represents a morpheme or meaning rather than an individual phoneme.

In web crawls, mixed scripts create homoglyphs and script confusables: visually identical characters that possess
different Unicode code points (such as Latin `a` `U+0061` versus Cyrillic `а` `U+0430`). Optical character recognition
models and adversarial spam injections frequently introduce confusable glyphs, which break
<NoteLink to="tokenisation">subword tokenisation</NoteLink> unless normalised via Unicode security skeletons (UTS #39).

## Word segmentation and scriptio continua

Many world writing systems do not employ spaces between words (*scriptio continua*). Chinese and Japanese Kanji write
uninterrupted strings of ideographs; Thai, Lao, and Khmer write unbroken sequences of syllabic glyphs without word
delimiters:

- **Rule-based and maximal matching:** Early segmenters used greedy longest-dictionary-match lookups. These fail on
  out-of-vocabulary compounds, proper nouns, and ambiguous segmentation boundaries.
- **Statistical and morphological models:** Tools like Jieba (Chinese), MeCab/Sudachi (Japanese), and PyThaiNLP (Thai)
  apply <Gloss name="crf">conditional random fields</Gloss> or lattice-based <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov
  models</Gloss> to find optimal word boundaries.
- **Language-independent subword tokenisation:** SentencePiece processes text directly as raw Unicode sequences without
  requiring pre-tokenisation whitespace splitting <Cite id="kudo2018" />. While SentencePiece eliminates hard
  language-specific segmentation pipelines, subword vocabularies trained predominantly on English segment unsegmented
  scripts into fragmented byte fragments or single characters.

## Transliteration and script mixing

**Transliteration** maps text from its native writing system into an alternative script, most commonly the Latin
alphabet (romanisation). Examples include Pinyin for Mandarin Chinese, Romaji for Japanese, and Arabizi (Franco-Arab
chat alphabet) for Arabic dialects.

Arabizi substitutes Latin digits for Arabic consonants that lack direct European phonetic equivalents (for example,
using `3` for the pharyngeal fricative *`ayn* /ع/ and `7` for the voiceless pharyngeal fricative *haa* /ح/). Informal
digital communications in South Asia heavily employ Hinglish or Romanised Bengali.

Transliteration presents severe challenges for natural language pipelines:
1. **Lack of standardised spelling:** Romanised Indic and Arabic dialects follow non-standard phonetic transcriptions;
   the same word may be spelled a dozen different ways across users.
2. **Loss of phonemic distinction:** Diacritics, vowel lengths, and tonal contours present in native scripts are
   stripped during romanisation, producing severe lexical ambiguity.

## Code-switching

**Code-switching** occurs when a speaker alternates between two or more languages within a single communicative
exchange, or even within a single sentence:

> *“I was going to the market lekin wahan bahut traffic tha.”*

Here, English (*I was going to the market*) switches directly into Hindi (*lekin wahan bahut traffic tha*).
Code-switching is prevalent in multilingual communities and social media across South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin
America, and North Africa.

Standard text processing pipelines break on code-switched text:
- **Language identification failure:** Monolingual language classifiers (such as fastText or CLD3) force a single
  categorical decision per document, misclassifying code-switched text or reporting near-zero confidence
  <Cite id="wenzek2020" />.
- **Vocabulary fragmentation:** Subwords optimized for monolingual benchmarks fragment romanised loanwords into isolated
  subword fragments, sharply increasing sequence length.

## Token fertility and fairness

A central disparity in multilingual <Gloss name="nlp">natural language processing</Gloss> is **token fertility**: the
average number of subword tokens required to represent a single semantic word or concept <Cite id="rust2021" />.

In the standard UTF-8 encoding:
- ASCII characters (Latin alphabet) require exactly 1 byte per character.
- Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic, and Hebrew characters require 2 bytes per character.
- Devanagari, Indic scripts, and CJK ideographs require 3 bytes per character.
- Rare historic scripts and emoji require 4 bytes per character.

When tokenisers are trained with a fixed vocabulary budget (such as 32,000 or 128,000 subword tokens) on corpora
dominated by English and European web dumps, rare scripts receive few dedicated token slots. Non-Latin words are chopped
into multiple subword pieces or falling back to raw UTF-8 bytes <Cite id="conneau2020xlmr" />:

$$
\text{Fertility} = \frac{\#\,\text{Subword Tokens}}{\#\,\text{Semantic Words}}.
$$

While English typically achieves a fertility of $1.1$ to $1.3$ tokens per word, languages like Hindi, Amharic, or
Burmese frequently exhibit fertilies of $2.5$ to $4.0$. This disparity creates severe computational inequity: models
process fewer words per context window in high-fertility languages, and inference cost is proportionally inflated.

## Pitfalls

- **Naive ASCII stripping:** Legacy cleaning scripts that filter out characters outside `[a-zA-Z0-9]` inadvertently wipe
  out valid text in all non-Latin languages.
- **Bidirectional (BiDi) text corruption:** Arabic and Hebrew render right-to-left, but embedded numbers, Latin
  acronyms, and URLs render left-to-right. Concatenating strings or stripping Unicode directionality markers (`U+200E`
  LRM, `U+200F` RLM) scrambles word order and destroys syntactic validity.
- **Document-level language filtering:** Filtering Common Crawl dumps by a strict language probability threshold (for
  example, $P(\text{lang}) \ge 0.8$) discards code-switched dialogues, multilingual dictionaries, and parallel corpora,
  underrepresenting linguistically diverse communities.
