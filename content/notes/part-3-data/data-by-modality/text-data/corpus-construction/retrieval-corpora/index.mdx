<Definition>
A **retrieval corpus** is a curated collection of unstructured or semi-structured text documents partitioned into
searchable passage units $\mathcal{D} = \{d_1, \dots, d_N\}$, accompanied by queries $q \in \mathcal{Q}$ and relevance
assessments $r(q, d) \in \{0, 1\}$. Training modern dense bi-encoders and cross-encoders requires segmenting
heterogeneous documents into bounded **passages**, pairing queries with relevant documents, and mining informative
**hard negatives** from lexical or dense candidate pools while navigating incomplete ground-truth annotations.
</Definition>

## Document chunking and passage boundaries

Raw source documents (such as clinical notes, financial reports, legal contracts, and encyclopedias) vary in length from
single sentences to hundreds of pages. Because transformer encoders impose strict sequence length budgets (typically 256
to 512 tokens for bi-encoder embedding models), documents must be partitioned into passages:

- **Fixed-window chunking with overlap:** A sliding window of $W$ tokens advances with stride
  $S = W(1 - \text{overlap})$. An overlap of $15\%$ to $30\%$ ensures that semantic propositions spanning chunk
  boundaries are not arbitrarily severed.
- **Syntactic and sentence-boundary chunking:** Using sentence segmenters to avoid splitting in the middle of sentences
  or lists, buffering complete sentences until the token budget is reached.
- **Hierarchical parent-child chunking:** Small child passages (e.g., 128 tokens) are indexed in the vector database for
  granular similarity search, but the retrieval pipeline returns the surrounding parent section (e.g., 512 tokens) or
  prepends the document title and section hierarchy (`Document > Section > Subsection`) to preserve surrounding context.

## Query-document pairs

Supervised retrieval training requires triples $(q, d^+, d^-)$ comprising a query $q$, a known relevant positive
document $d^+$, and one or more non-relevant negative documents $d^-$:

- **Search click logs:** Organic search engine query logs (such as the MS MARCO dataset <Cite id="bajaj2016" />) pair
  anonymised user queries with clicked web snippets. Click logs reflect real user intent and vocabulary mismatches, but
  introduce <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink> and <Gloss name="position-bias">position
  bias</Gloss> (users clicking top-ranked links regardless of quality).
- **Synthetic query generation:** When human search logs are unavailable (such as domain-specific internal
  documentation), <Gloss name="llm">large language models</Gloss> are prompted to generate candidate search queries that
  a given passage answers (e.g., InPars, Promptagator). Filtering queries by synthetic cycle-consistency (retaining
  queries where an auxiliary retriever ranks the source passage first) prevents hallucinated question-passage pairs.

## Hard negative mining

Dense bi-encoders compute the cosine similarity
$s(q, d) = \mathbf{e}_q^\top \mathbf{e}_d / (\|\mathbf{e}_q\| \|\mathbf{e}_d\|)$ between independent query and passage
vector representations. Models are trained using the <Gloss name="infonce" /> contrastive ranking loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}(q, d^+, \{d_j^-\}) = -\log \frac{\exp(s(q, d^+) / \tau)}{\exp(s(q, d^+) / \tau) + \sum_{j=1}^M \exp(s(q, d_j^-) / \tau)},
$$

where $\tau > 0$ is a temperature hyperparameter. The choice of negative candidate set $\{d_j^-\}$ dictates the
sharpness of the learned representation:

- **Random in-batch negatives:** Passages drawn at random from other rows in the training mini-batch. Random negatives
  are semantically distant from $q$ (e.g., a query about backpropagation paired with a passage about oceanography).
  While necessary during early warm-up, random negatives provide negligible gradient once the model learns basic topic
  separation.
- **<Gloss name="bm25" /> lexical hard negatives:** Passages that achieve high lexical ranking scores under BM25
  <Cite id="robertson2009" /> by matching keywords in $q$, but do not actually satisfy the informational need. Lexical
  hard negatives force bi-encoders to learn semantic comprehension beyond keyword matching <Cite id="karpukhin2020" />.
- **Dense hard negatives (cross-encoder / ANCE mining):** Passages retrieved by an earlier dense retriever checkpoint
  that score high cosine similarity with $q$ but are unannotated as positives.
- **<Gloss name="fn">False negative</Gloss> risk:** In large corpora, top-ranked negatives mined by BM25 or dense models
  often contain unlabelled <Gloss name="tp">true positives</Gloss>. Forcing the contrastive loss to push down
  representations of actually relevant passages corrupts embedding spaces.

## Incomplete relevance judgements and pooling

A fundamental limitation of retrieval evaluation is that corpora are too vast for human annotators to exhaustively
assess every passage for every query. In a corpus of 10 million passages, assessing all pairs requires prohibitive
effort:

- **TREC depth-$k$ pooling:** The top $k$ documents returned by multiple diverse retrieval systems are merged into a
  candidate pool and judged by human assessors. Documents outside the pool are assumed non-relevant ($r = 0$).
- **Evaluation bias:** Incomplete pooling introduces systemic bias against novel retrieval architectures
  <Cite id="thakur2021" />. When an advanced dense retriever surfaces highly relevant passages that were never retrieved
  by legacy BM25 systems, these passages are treated as non-relevant <Gloss name="fp">false positives</Gloss>, falsely
  penalising the new model.
- **BEIR zero-shot benchmarking:** The BEIR benchmark standardises evaluation across 17 heterogeneous retrieval tasks
  (biomedical, legal, scientific, financial) to measure out-of-distribution robustness under varying levels of
  annotation completeness <Cite id="thakur2021" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Context fragmentation and orphaned pronouns:** Splitting a document midway through a paragraph leaves child passages
  with unresolved pronouns (*“It was founded in 1998 in Menlo Park...”* without mentioning *Google*), causing dense
  retrievers to fail on entity queries. Prepending document titles mitigates this drift.
- **Passage length distortion:** Long passages that pack multiple topics dilute their dense vector representations;
  conversely, short single-sentence chunks lack sufficient lexical context for BM25 <Gloss name="idf">inverse document
  frequency</Gloss> scoring.
- **Train-test query leakage:** Queries in retrieval benchmarks often derive from identical underlying articles as test
  passages. Splitting retrieval splits randomly across query-document pairs rather than by document cluster causes
  severe test-set memorisation.
