<Definition>
**Text augmentation** synthesises label-preserving training variations of text sequences to regularise supervised
models, expand low-resource datasets, and improve out-of-distribution robustness. Unlike continuous
<NoteLink to="image-augmentation">image augmentations</NoteLink>, text consists of discrete categorical tokens where
perturbations risk corrupting grammatical syntax or reversing semantic meaning <Cite id="wei2019" />.
</Definition>

In computer vision, affine transforms and color jitter perturb continuous pixel values without disrupting object class
semantics. In <Gloss name="nlp">natural language processing</Gloss>, words form discrete symbolic structures where
altering a single token (such as inserting "not" or flipping a sentiment adjective) can invert the target label
entirely. Effective text augmentation preserves the underlying classification label or intent while introducing
syntactic and lexical diversity.

## Rule-based edits

Rule-based techniques perturb surface strings without requiring auxiliary neural models. The **Easy Data Augmentation
(EDA)** framework <Cite id="wei2019" /> defines four stochastic operations parametrised by mutation rate
$\alpha \in (0, 1)$:

1. **Synonym Replacement (SR):** Randomly selects $n = \lfloor \alpha \cdot L \rfloor$ non-stop words from a sentence of
   length $L$ and replaces each with a synonym drawn from WordNet.
2. **Random Insertion (RI):** Finds a random non-stop word, selects one of its synonyms, and inserts that synonym at an
   arbitrary position in the sentence, repeated $n$ times.
3. **Random Swap (RS):** Randomly chooses two words in the sentence and swaps their positions, repeated $n$ times. This
   simulates word-order variations and minor disfluencies.
4. **Random Deletion (RD):** Independently deletes each word in the sentence with probability $p = \alpha$.

Empirical studies show that setting $\alpha \approx 0.1$ delivers significant accuracy gains on small training sets
(under 5,000 examples) by preventing overfitting on specific lexical cues.

## Back-translation

**Back-translation** translates a source text into an intermediate pivot language using a
<Gloss name="machine-translation">machine translation</Gloss> system, then translates the result back into the original
language <Cite id="sennrich2016improving" />:

$$
x \xrightarrow{\text{translate}} y_{\text{pivot}} \xrightarrow{\text{translate}} \hat{x}
$$

Because translation models select phrasing based on <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> priors and
<Gloss name="beam-search">beam search</Gloss> decoding, the reconstituted sentence $\hat{x}$ frequently adopts
alternative idioms, varied clause structures, and synonymous vocabulary while retaining the exact semantic message.
Combining multiple diverse pivot languages (such as German, French, Chinese, and Russian) synthesises diverse paraphrase
pools for each source example.

## Masked language model substitution

Contextualised embeddings from masked language models (such as <Gloss name="bert" /> or <Gloss name="roberta" />) enable
semantically aware token substitution:

1. A subset of non-stop words in source sentence $x$ are replaced by the special mask token `[MASK]`.
2. The masked language model computes conditional log-probabilities $P(w \mid x_{\backslash i})$ over the vocabulary for
   each masked position.
3. A substitute word is sampled from the top-$k$ predictions, excluding the original word itself.

Because substitutions condition on bidirectional sentence context, words with multiple senses (such as "bank" or
"crane") receive contextually appropriate replacements that preserve local syntax and grammatical agreement.

## Generative paraphrasing

Instruction-tuned and sequence-to-sequence language models (such as <Gloss name="t5" /> or <Gloss name="gpt" /> models)
generate high-level semantic paraphrases via prompt conditioning:

- **Style and formality transfer:** Prompts condition the generator to rewrite sentences in colloquial, formal, or
  simplified phrasing.
- **Controlled complexity:** Generative models lengthen, shorten, or invert active/passive clauses while preserving core
  factual assertions.
- **<Gloss name="temperature">Sampling temperature</Gloss>:** Temperature parameter $T \in [0.4, 0.8]$ balances lexical
  novelty against hallucination risk.

## Validation and filtering

Synthesised text must be filtered to discard ungrammatical outputs or semantic label drift:

- **Semantic similarity thresholding:** Pretrained sentence encoders (such as Sentence-BERT) compute cosine similarity
  between original sentence embedding $\mathbf{z}_x$ and augmented embedding $\mathbf{z}_{\hat{x}}$. Augmented examples
  falling below a similarity threshold (such as $\cos(\mathbf{z}_x, \mathbf{z}_{\hat{x}}) < 0.80$) are pruned.
- **Round-trip classifier validation:** A baseline classifier trained on original data evaluates augmented samples. If
  the classifier's predicted probability for the ground-truth label drops sharply, the augmentation has likely flipped
  the underlying class and is discarded.
- **Perplexity screening:** Language model perplexity checks discard ungrammatical sentences generated by aggressive
  random swapping or deletion.

## Pitfalls

- **Negation inversion:** Blind synonym replacement or deletion can alter or eliminate negation particles ("not bad"
  degraded to "bad"), completely reversing sentiment labels.
- **Excessive mutation rate:** Setting EDA mutation rate $\alpha > 0.3$ destroys syntactic dependencies, producing word
  salad that degrades model representation learning.
- **Distribution shift and shortcut learning:** Augmenting solely through a single translation model introduces systemic
  translationese artifacts that downstream classifiers learn as spurious predictive shortcuts.
