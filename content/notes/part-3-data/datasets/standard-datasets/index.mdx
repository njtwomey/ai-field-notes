<Definition>
A **dataset** in this subject is a published, named collection of examples with a fixed purpose: a benchmark with
splits and a metric, a pretraining corpus, or an archive of records. Each note covers one area. It lists the standard
datasets, what each measures and at what scale, how to choose among them, and the known problems that limit what results
on them mean.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Methods are compared on shared datasets, and models are pretrained on them, so the choice of dataset decides what a
reported number means. A score depends on how the data were collected, which classes and
populations they cover, how the labels were made, how the split was drawn and whether test items leaked into training.
Standard datasets also age: benchmarks saturate, labels turn out wrong <Cite id="northcutt2021pervasive" />, licences
change and some collections are withdrawn. A datasheet records most of these facts <Cite id="gebru2021" />. The notes
here collect them for the datasets that recur across the field.

## Main ideas

**Every dataset answers a narrower question than its name.** <Gloss name="imagenet" /> measures classification of a
fixed set of web photographs; WILDS measures a set of chosen shifts <Cite id="koh2021" />. Each note says what its
datasets measure and what they leave out.

**Known problems are part of the dataset.** Label errors, near-duplicates across splits, contamination of
<NoteLink to="language-model-corpora">web corpora</NoteLink>, trivial anomalies, leaking splits and outdated populations
recur across areas. Each note ends with them.

**Splits carry the deployment assumption.** A random split of patients, molecules, scenes or windows overstates
generalisation. The notes say which split each area needs, linking to the modality chapters for the reasons.

**Some datasets exist to study data problems.** Shift, label-noise and anomaly benchmarks reproduce a defect of real
data on purpose, so that methods for it can be measured.

## How the notes fit together

The subject has eleven chapters. Seven have their own overview:

- <NoteLink to="datasets-for-data-problems" />: shift, noisy labels, anomalies and known defects;
- <NoteLink to="tabular-and-social-datasets" />: general tables, fairness, causal, finance and
  <NoteLink to="time-to-event-data">survival data</NoteLink>;
- <NoteLink to="vision-and-multimodal-datasets" />: images, detection, faces, video, 3D and driving, image-text and
  robots;
- <NoteLink to="language-and-code-datasets" />: <NoteLink to="nlp-benchmark-datasets">NLP benchmarks</NoteLink>,
  pretraining corpora, language-model benchmarks, code, documents, instructions and
  <NoteLink to="multilingual-text">multilingual data</NoteLink>;
- <NoteLink to="time-series-and-sensor-datasets" />: classification, forecasting, anomalies and activity;
- <NoteLink to="health-and-life-science-datasets" />: signals, records, medical images, proteins and genomes;
- <NoteLink to="earth-and-physical-science-datasets" />: remote sensing, weather, chemistry, physics and mobility.

Four chapters have two notes each: classic and toy data (<NoteLink to="classic-statistical-datasets" />,
<NoteLink to="toy-and-synthetic-datasets" />), audio (<NoteLink to="speech-datasets" />,
<NoteLink to="sound-and-music-datasets" />), graphs (<NoteLink to="graph-benchmark-datasets" />,
<NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-datasets" />), and recommendation and retrieval (<NoteLink to="recommender-datasets" />,
<NoteLink to="retrieval-datasets" />).

The chapters are independent. Classic and toy data come first because other notes use them as running examples.
