<Definition>
**Health and life-science datasets** are the public archives of physiological signals, clinical records, medical images
and biological sequences and structures used to develop and compare models in medicine and biology. Most require a
data use agreement, and all carry the coding practices, populations and measurement
artefacts of the institutions that produced them.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Medical and biological data are expensive to collect and hard to share, so a few public archives carry much of the
field's research. PhysioNet hosts many physiological recordings <Cite id="goldberger2000" />. MIMIC made intensive-care
records of one hospital system available to researchers <Cite id="johnson2016mimic" />. CheXpert released over 200,000
chest radiographs with labels extracted from reports <Cite id="irvin2019chexpert" />. Results on these archives are
easily overstated: recordings from the same patient fall on both sides of a random split, labels come from automated
text extraction, and hospital or scanner signatures predict the label.

## Main ideas

**Signals are split by patient.** <NoteLink to="physiological-signal-datasets" /> covers MIT-BIH Arrhythmia, PTB-XL, the
PhysioNet Challenges, Sleep-EDF, CHB-MIT and the TUH EEG corpus. Each patient's signals are distinctive, so splits by
record leak; rare arrhythmias and seizures make classes highly imbalanced.

**Records reflect care, not health.** <NoteLink to="clinical-records-datasets" /> covers MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, eICU,
UK Biobank and the synthetic Synthea. Observations are made when clinicians are worried, so missingness is informative.
Practice and coding differ between sites.

**Image labels are often extracted.** <NoteLink to="medical-imaging-datasets" /> covers ChestX-ray14, CheXpert,
MIMIC-CXR, BraTS, LIDC-IDRI, CAMELYON and the ISIC archive. Labels mined from radiology reports are noisy, and models
exploit hospital signatures.

**Biology shares ancestry.** <NoteLink to="protein-and-genomics-datasets" /> covers the Protein Data Bank, CASP,
UniProt, the 1000 Genomes Project, TCGA and single-cell atlases. Homologous sequences leak across random splits, and
single-cell data carry strong <NoteLink to="omics-tables">batch effects</NoteLink>.
