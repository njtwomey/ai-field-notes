<Definition>
**Classic statistical datasets** are canonical, low-dimensional historical datasets widely used in pedagogy and
benchmark verification to illustrate core statistical phenomena: linear versus non-linear separability, regression to
the mean, bimodal mixture distributions, severe leverage outliers, and non-stationary seasonality
<Cite id="fisher1936, anscombe1973" />.
</Definition>

Before modern deep learning on multi-terabyte web crawls, methodological breakthroughs in discriminant analysis,
exploratory data analysis (EDA), and time-series forecasting were established and battle-tested on small, curated
tabular measurements containing between 11 and 2,000 instances <Cite id="anscombe1973" />.

## What These Datasets Measure

Classic datasets prioritize interpretability and pedagogical clarity over scale:
- **Small sample regimes ($N \in [11, 2{,}224]$)**: Models can be computed analytically or fit in milliseconds, exposing
  model behavior, coefficient stability, and residual violations directly.
- **Low dimensionalities ($P \in [1, 8]$)**: Every feature corresponds to a concrete, physically measurable attribute
  (millimeters of sepal length, minutes of geyser eruption duration, passenger ticket class).
- **Pathological diagnostic edge cases**: Specifically constructed or collected to demonstrate where naive summary
  statistics (mean, variance, correlation) mislead analysts.

## Detailed Dataset Analysis

### Fisher's Iris (1936)

Collected by Edgar Anderson and published by R. A. Fisher <Cite id="fisher1936" /> to introduce Linear Discriminant
Analysis (LDA), Iris contains 150 botanical measurements (50 specimens each of *Iris setosa*, *Iris versicolor*, and
*Iris virginica*):
- Four continuous features: sepal length, sepal width, petal length, petal width (in cm).
- *Setosa* is linearly separable from the other two species with $100\%$ margin using petal dimensions alone.
- *Versicolor* and *Virginica* have overlapping convex hulls, serving as the benchmark for nonlinear support vector
  machines and decision trees.

### Anscombe's Quartet (1973)

Constructed by Francis Anscombe <Cite id="anscombe1973" /> to counter the belief among early computer statisticians that
"numerical calculations are exact, but graphs are rough":
- Four distinct datasets of 11 $(x, y)$ coordinate pairs each.
- **Identical Summary Statistics**:
  - Sample mean of $x$: $\bar{x} = 9.0$
  - Sample variance of $x$: $s_x^2 = 11.0$
  - Sample mean of $y$: $\bar{y} = 7.50$
  - Sample variance of $y$: $s_y^2 = 4.125$
  - Linear regression line: $\hat{y} = 3.00 + 0.500x$ ($R^2 = 0.67$)
  - Pearson correlation: $r = 0.816$
- **True Distributions**:
  - **Dataset I**: Standard linear relationship with homoskedastic Gaussian errors.
  - **Dataset II**: Perfect quadratic parabola ($y = -0.125x^2 + 2.75x - 5.0$); the linear model is completely
    misspecified.
  - **Dataset III**: Strict collinear line with a single high-residual vertical outlier corrupting the slope.
  - **Dataset IV**: 10 points share identical $x=8$, with a single high-leverage point at $x=19$ that entirely dictates
    the regression line.

### Old Faithful Geyser

Records 272 consecutive eruptions of the Old Faithful geyser in Yellowstone National Park:
- Measures eruption duration (minutes) and waiting time to the next eruption (minutes).
- Forms two clearly separated bivariate clusters: short eruptions ($\approx 2.0\text{ min}$) preceded by short intervals
  ($\approx 54\text{ min}$), and long eruptions ($\approx 4.4\text{ min}$) preceded by long intervals
  ($\approx 80\text{ min}$).
- The canonical benchmark for fitting two-component Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) via Expectation-Maximization (EM).

### Galton's Heights (1886)

Francis Galton's study of 928 adult children across 205 pairs of parents:
- Measured mid-parent height against adult child height (adjusted for sex).
- Demonstrated that exceptionally tall parents tend to have children who are tall, but closer to the population average
  than their parents (and vice versa for short parents).
- Coined the term **regression toward mediocrity** (modern regression).

### Titanic Passengers (1912)

Contains demographic and ticket records for passengers aboard the RMS Titanic:
- Target: binary survival ($y \in \{0, 1\}$).
- Key features: passenger class (1st, 2nd, 3rd), sex, age, siblings/spouses aboard (`SibSp`), parents/children aboard
  (`Parch`), fare, and port of embarkation.
- Highlights stark socio-demographic survival disparities: female survival was $74.2\%$ versus $18.9\%$ for males; 1st
  class survival was $62.0\%$ versus $24.2\%$ for 3rd class.
- Standard benchmark for handling missing tabular data (age is missing for $20\%$ of records) and categorical feature
  encoding.

### Airline Passengers (Box-Jenkins Series G)

Monthly totals of international airline passengers from January 1949 to December 1960 (144 observations) popularized by
Box and Jenkins:
- Exhibits an upward linear/exponential secular trend combined with a 12-month annual seasonal cycle.
- **Multiplicative Seasonality**: The seasonal oscillation amplitude grows proportionally with the series mean,
  requiring logarithmic transformation $\log(Y_t)$ before linear ARIMA fitting.

## Known Problems and Limitations

1. **Saturated Metric Ceilings**: Datasets like Iris and Wine are computationally solved; modern classifiers achieve
   $98\text{--}100\%$ accuracy, making them useless for comparing modern algorithms.
2. **Extreme Overfitting Risks from Tiny Samples**: Evaluating cross-validation performance on $N=150$ samples yields
   large standard errors ($\pm 3\text{--}5\%$), where changing a random seed changes which model appears superior.
3. **Historical Data Provenance Issues**: Datasets collected in early 20th-century contexts reflect historical social
   circumstances that must not be generalized to modern operational machine learning deployments.
