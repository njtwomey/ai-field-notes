<Definition>
**Toy and synthetic datasets** are mathematically parameterized data generators designed to produce synthetic
observations $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ with analytically known ground-truth properties: exact
functional relationships $y = f^*(\mathbf{x})$, known Bayes optimal error rates, precise manifold intrinsic dimensions
$d \ll D$, or explicit noise models $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ <Cite id="tenenbaum2000, friedman1991" />.
</Definition>

While empirical real-world datasets conflate model misspecification with measurement noise, missing confounders, and
collection artifacts, synthetic benchmarks isolate specific mathematical capabilities: can an algorithm unfold a curved
2D manifold in 3D, separate non-convex interlaced clusters, or discard independent nuisance noise features
<Cite id="tenenbaum2000" />?

## What These Datasets Measure

Synthetic datasets test targeted algorithmic failure modes under controlled conditions:
- **Known Bayes Optimal Error**: By specifying the true generating densities $p(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$, the theoretical
  minimum classification error $P^*(E) = \int \min(p(\mathbf{x}, y=0), p(\mathbf{x}, y=1)) \, d\mathbf{x}$ is known,
  allowing researchers to measure exact generalization gaps.
- **Controlled Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)**: Adding Gaussian noise $\sigma$ or flipping labels at rate
  $p_{\text{flip}}$ tests regularization strength without changing the underlying manifold geometry.
- **Scalability and Dimensionality Stress Tests**: Generating arbitrary sample sizes $N \in [10^2, 10^7]$ and appending
  $P_{\text{noise}}$ uninformative nuisance dimensions directly tests vulnerability to the curse of dimensionality.

## Detailed Manifold Formulations

### Two Moons

Two interleaved half-circles in $\mathbb{R}^2$ parameterized by angle $t \in [0, \pi]$ with added Gaussian noise
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\text{moon 1}} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{pmatrix} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \mathbf{x}_{\text{moon 2}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \cos t \\ 0.5 - \sin t \end{pmatrix} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}
$$

A linear hyperplane achieves at most $\approx 78\text{--}83\%$ accuracy. Achieving $> 98\%$ requires either an
infinite-dimensional feature space (such as a Gaussian RBF kernel
$K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|_2^2)$) or graph-based spectral diffusion that
follows the connected manifold.

### Concentric Circles

Two concentric rings of radii $r_1 < r_2$:

$$
\mathbf{x} = r_y \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta \\ \sin \theta \end{pmatrix} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \theta \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 2\pi), \quad y \in \{1, 2\}
$$

While non-separable by any linear classifier in $(x_1, x_2)$, applying a quadratic basis expansion
$\phi(\mathbf{x}) = (x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ or transforming to polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ collapses the problem to a
trivial 1D threshold $\hat{y} = \mathbb{I}[r > \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}]$.

### The Swiss Roll (Tenenbaum 2000)

Introduced by Tenenbaum et al. <Cite id="tenenbaum2000" /> to evaluate Isomap, the Swiss roll embeds a two-dimensional
rectangular strip $[1.5\pi, 4.5\pi] \times [0, 21]$ into $\mathbb{R}^3$ via an Archimedean spiral:

$$
\mathbf{x}(t, y) = \begin{pmatrix} t \cos t \\ y \\ t \sin t \end{pmatrix} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad t \in [1.5\pi, 4.5\pi], \quad y \in [0, 21]
$$

Euclidean distance in ambient $\mathbb{R}^3$ cuts directly across empty space between adjacent layers of the roll (e.g.
between $t=1.5\pi$ and $t=3.5\pi$), creating false neighbor shortcuts. Manifold learning algorithms must construct a
$k$-nearest-neighbor graph and compute shortest path graph distances to approximate the true intrinsic geodesic metric:

$$
d_{\text{geodesic}}(t_1, t_2) = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sqrt{1 + u^2} \, du = \frac{1}{2} \left[ u \sqrt{1 + u^2} + \operatorname{arsinh}(u) \right]_{t_1}^{t_2}
$$

### Friedman Regression Benchmark #1 (1991)

Designed by Jerome Friedman <Cite id="friedman1991" /> to evaluate Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS):
- Inputs: 10 independent uniform features $\mathbf{x} \in [0, 1]^{10}$.
- Generating Equation:
  $$
  y = 10 \sin(\pi x_1 x_2) + 20 (x_3 - 0.5)^2 + 10 x_4 + 5 x_5 + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
  $$
- **Nuisance Features**: Features $x_6, x_7, x_8, x_9, x_{10}$ enter neither the mean function nor the noise term.
  Models must perform feature selection while simultaneously estimating non-linear interaction $\sin(\pi x_1 x_2)$ and
  quadratic term $(x_3 - 0.5)^2$.

## Recipes and Modifiers

Synthetic generators are standardly adapted via parametric modifiers:
1. **Nuisance Dimensions (Noise Padding)**: Embedding a 2D moon dataset into $\mathbb{R}^{50}$ by concatenating 48
   Gaussian noise features $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ tests whether distance-based models
   ($k$-NN, kernel machines) degrade as noise overwhelms metric distances.
2. **Label Flipping Noise**: With probability $p_{\text{flip}} \in [0, 0.3]$, the true label is inverted
   ($y \leftarrow 1 - y$), benchmarking robust loss functions and clean-label filtering.
3. **Distribution Shift**: Generating training data with $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ and test data with
   $\theta \in [\pi, 2\pi]$ directly evaluates out-of-distribution generalization.

## Known Problems and Limitations

1. **Synthetic Optimism**: Real-world data rarely reside on smooth, infinitely differentiable low-dimensional manifolds
   with uniform noise. Algorithms that excel on the Swiss roll often struggle on tabular business data with discrete
   categorical codes and missingness.
2. **Hyperparameter Over-Tuning on Toy Manifolds**: Benchmarking algorithms exclusively on toy datasets encourages
   tuning hyperparameters (such as kernel bandwidth $\gamma$ or neighborhood $k$) to specific geometric radii rather
   than generalizable principles.
