<Definition>
**Fairness datasets** are benchmark collections that pair predictive targets $Y$ with explicitly designated protected
(sensitive) attributes $A \in \mathcal{A}$—such as race, sex, age, disability, or marital status—alongside observational
features $X$. They evaluate whether machine learning models exhibit demographic disparity, disparate impact, or unequal
error rates across demographic subgroups, and benchmark algorithmic bias mitigation techniques
<Cite id="hardt2016, barocas2023" />.
</Definition>

In high-stakes social domains (criminal justice recidivism, mortgage lending, credit scoring, employment hiring, and
university admissions), automated decision systems can perpetuate or exacerbate historical systemic inequalities.
Fairness benchmarks provide structured testbeds to quantify mathematical trade-offs between predictive utility and group
fairness criteria <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />.

---

## What These Datasets Measure

Fairness benchmarks evaluate whether decision rules satisfy specific mathematical criteria across sensitive partitions
$A = a$:
1. **Demographic Parity**: Positive prediction rates are independent of the sensitive attribute:
   $$
   P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a') \quad \forall a, a'
   $$
2. **Equalized Odds**: Predictor $\hat{Y}$ is conditionally independent of $A$ given the true outcome $Y$, matching true
   positive rates and false positive rates across groups <Cite id="hardt2016" />:
   $$
   P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = y, A = a) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = y, A = a') \quad \forall y \in \{0, 1\}
   $$
3. **Equal Opportunity**: True positive rates match across groups given $Y = 1$:
   $$
   P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a')
   $$
4. **Predictive Parity (Calibration within Groups)**: Positive predictive value is equal across groups:
   $$
   P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = a) = P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = a')
   $$
   By the Chouldechova-Kleinberg impossibility theorem <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />, when base rates
   $P(Y = 1 \mid A = a)$ differ across groups, equalized odds and predictive parity are mathematically mutually
   exclusive.

## Detailed Benchmark Profiles

### COMPAS Recidivism (2016)

Investigated by ProPublica in a landmark report <Cite id="angwin2016" />, COMPAS (Correctional Offender Management
Profiling for Alternative Sanctions) is a risk assessment tool deployed across US jurisdictions:
- **Cohort**: 7,214 pre-trial defendants in Broward County, Florida, tracked over two years.
- **The Disparity**: The tool exhibited roughly equal positive predictive value across racial groups (predictive
  parity), but demonstrated glaring disparities in false positive rates: Black defendants who did not reoffend were
  nearly twice as likely to be misclassified as high risk ($44.9\%$) compared to white defendants ($23.5\%$).
- **Fairness Impact**: COMPAS became the central case study demonstrating the irreconcilable mathematical tension
  between calibration and equalized error rates <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />.

### Folktables: Modern American Community Survey Suites (2021)

Developed by Ding, Hardt, Miller, and Schmidt <Cite id="ding2021retiring" /> to retire outdated datasets like Adult:
- **Data Source**: Microdata from the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) covering all 50 states from
  2014 to 2018.
- **Five Standard Tasks**:
  - *ACSIncome*: Predict personal income $> 50{,}000\text{ USD}$.
  - *ACSPublicCoverage*: Predict whether low-income individuals have public health insurance.
  - *ACSMobility*: Predict whether an individual moved residential locations within the past year.
  - *ACSEmployment*: Predict employment status for working-age adults.
  - *ACSTravelTime*: Predict commute time to work $> 20$ minutes.
- **Advantages**: Modern data collection, transparent state-by-state sampling weights, documented variable provenance,
  and large sample sizes preventing statistical noise.

### German Credit (1994)

A historical financial scoring dataset widely used in algorithmic fairness literature:
- **Structure**: 1,000 loan applicants evaluated across 20 financial and personal features.
- **Protected Attributes**: Age ($< 25$ vs $\ge 25$) and sex (encoded jointly with marital status).
- **Limitations**: Tiny sample size ($N = 1{,}000$) causes extreme variance when computing subgroup metrics; disparate
  impact findings on this dataset often fail to reach statistical significance.

## Choosing a Fairness Benchmark

- **Modern Social Policy & Large-Scale Evaluation**:
  - Folktables ACS (ACSIncome, ACSEmployment, state-level splits)
- **Recidivism & Error Rate Trade-off Studies**:
  - COMPAS (Disparate false positive rates, calibration conflict)
- **Legal Admissions & Education Access**:
  - Law School Admissions Council (LSAC)
- **Historical Literature Reproducibility**:
  - Adult Census / German Credit (Use with documented caveats)

## Known Problems and Benchmark Pitfalls

1. **Proxy Confounding and Measurement Bias**: Target variables in social datasets are administrative records rather
   than ground truth <Cite id="barocas2023" />. In COMPAS, the target is *arrest*, which reflects policing intensity and
   racial profiling rather than actual reoffending behavior.
2. **Over-Reliance on Toy Datasets**: For decades, hundreds of fairness papers evaluated solely on Adult and German
   Credit. Ding et al. <Cite id="ding2021retiring" /> showed that conclusions drawn from Adult often do not hold on
   modern ACS data due to arbitrary income thresholds and outdated family structures.
3. **Intersectionality Erasure**: Binarizing sensitive attributes (e.g. comparing male vs female or white vs non-white)
   completely ignores compounding discrimination at demographic intersections (e.g. Black women or elderly minority
   groups).
