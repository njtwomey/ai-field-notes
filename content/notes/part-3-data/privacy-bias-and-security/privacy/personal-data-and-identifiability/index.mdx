<Definition>
**Personal data** encompasses any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (*data subject*).
An individual is identifiable if they can be distinguished from others in a population, directly or indirectly, through
unique identifiers or combinations of non-unique auxiliary attributes (*quasi-identifiers*) <Cite id="sweeney2002k" />.
</Definition>

## Direct identifiers

A **direct identifier** is an explicit data attribute that unambiguously references an individual without requiring
auxiliary information:
- Government identification numbers (Social Security Number, national passport, driving license).
- Contact endpoints (personal email address, telephone number).
- Biometric signatures (facial scans, fingerprint hashes, DNA sequences).
- Full legal names combined with residential street addresses.

In standard data sanitization pipelines, removing direct identifiers is a mandatory first step. However, treating the
removal of direct identifiers as sufficient for anonymization is a dangerous failure mode.

## Quasi-identifiers and the uniqueness curse

A **quasi-identifier** is an attribute that does not identify a person in isolation, but whose combination with other
attributes partitions a population into uniquely identifiable equivalence classes:

$$
\mathbf{q} = \left[ \text{postal code}, \text{gender}, \text{date of birth} \right]^\top.
$$

### Sweeney's 87% landmark finding
In a foundational demographic study of the 1990 US Census, Latanya Sweeney <Cite id="sweeney2002k" /> proved that:
- Gender partitions a population into $2$ coarse bins.
- 5-digit ZIP code partitions the US into $\approx 40{,}000$ geographic sectors.
- Full date of birth provides $\approx 365.25 \times 80 \approx 29{,}220$ discrete calendar dates.

When crossed, the Cartesian product provides $2 \times 40{,}000 \times 29{,}220 \approx 2.3 \times 10^9$ discrete cells.
Across the US population, **87% of individuals** inhabit an equivalence cell containing exactly one person:

$$
P\left( \big| \{ i : \mathbf{q}_i = \mathbf{q}_j \} \big| = 1 \right) \approx 0.87.
$$

Consequently, any de-identified dataset containing only these three ordinary fields allows immediate, deterministic
re-identification of nearly nine out of ten citizens.

## Pseudonymised versus anonymised data

Under modern legal frameworks (such as GDPR Recital 26 and CCPA), there is a strict operational distinction:

| Attribute | Pseudonymised Data | Anonymised Data |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mechanism** | Direct identifiers replaced by tokens/hashes; mapping key stored separately. | Data transformed so records cannot be linked to individuals by any reasonable means. |
| **Reversibility** | Reversible via the secret lookup table or side-channel joins. | Irreversible even in the presence of auxiliary background information. |
| **Legal Status** | **Remains personal data**. Full GDPR compliance, consent, and rights apply. | **Exempt from data protection law**. Free to share, analyze, and retain indefinitely. |

Hashing an email with SHA-256 (`sha256("user@example.com")`) does not anonymize data; because the hash is deterministic,
dictionary precomputation attacks (rainbow tables) immediately invert common email addresses.

## Linkage attacks

A **linkage attack** occurs when an adversary joins two independently published datasets on their shared
quasi-identifiers to re-identify supposedly anonymous subjects:

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\text{confidential}} \bowtie_{\mathbf{q}} \mathcal{D}_{\text{public}} \implies \text{Identity} \longleftrightarrow \text{Sensitive Attribute}.
$$

### Classic case studies
1. **The Massachusetts GIC Medical Registry (1997)**: The Group Insurance Commission (GIC) published de-identified
   health records for state employees, containing diagnoses, medications, and procedures, stripping names and SSNs while
   retaining ZIP, gender, and birth date. Sweeney purchased the public voter registration list for Cambridge,
   Massachusetts ($20) containing names and identical demographics, joined the datasets on $\mathbf{q} = (\text{ZIP},
   \text{Gender}, \text{DOB})$, and successfully mailed the medical records of Massachusetts Governor William Weld to
   his office.
2. **The Netflix Prize De-anonymization (2008)**: Netflix released 100 million movie ratings from 500,000 subscribers,
   replacing usernames with random IDs. Narayanan and Shmatikov <Cite id="narayanan2008robust" /> showed that by linking
   ratings with public IMDb reviews, knowing just six movie ratings and approximate dates uniquely identified 84% of
   subscribers, exposing private viewing histories and political/sexual preferences.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **The "strip and release" fallacy**: Organizations frequently scrub names, emails, and phone numbers and assume the
   resulting dataset is anonymous. In high dimensions, sparsity makes almost every individual record unique.
2. **High-dimensional behavioral exhaust**: Credit card transaction sequences, mobile phone GPS traces, and web browsing
   histories have astronomical entropy. Studies show that four spatio-temporal points $(x, y, t)$ are sufficient to
   uniquely identify 95% of individuals in a mobile telecom dataset.
3. **Temporal auxiliary data accumulation**: Anonymity is non-monotonic: a dataset that is safe against
   re-identification today may become compromised tomorrow when a new commercial broker dataset or public breach
   provides the missing linkage key.
