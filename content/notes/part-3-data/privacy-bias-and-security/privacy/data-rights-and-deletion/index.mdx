<Definition>
**Data rights** govern the legal authorities, contractual licences, and individual prerogatives under which training
data can be collected, processed, and retained. When individuals invoke rights of erasure—such as Article 17 of the
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) or algorithmic disgorgement orders—organizations must remove target data
points from databases and eliminate their statistical influence from deployed machine learning model weights $\theta$.
</Definition>

Machine learning models act as lossy, parametric representations of their training corpora. Consequently, removing a
record from an operational datastore, feature store, or data lakehouse does not expunge the learned correlations,
gradient updates, or memorized tokens encoded within the model weights. Fulfilling modern privacy rights and regulatory
obligations necessitates managing data lifecycles from lawful ingestion to algorithmic erasure.

## Lawful basis and licences

Training machine learning models on human-generated or proprietary data requires a valid legal basis under data
protection statutes (such as GDPR Art. 6 or the California Consumer Privacy Act / CPRA) and compliance with underlying
intellectual property licences:

1. **Lawful bases for processing:**
   - **Consent (Art. 6(1)(a)):** Explicit, informed authorization from the data subject. Crucially, consent may be
     revoked at any time, instantly extinguishing the legal basis for retaining or training on that data.
   - **Contractual necessity (Art. 6(1)(b)):** Processing essential to deliver a requested service (e.g., routing
     optimization for ride-hailing).
   - **Legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f)):** A balancing test weighing the commercial utility of model training against
     individual privacy expectations and fundamental rights.
2. **Data licensing frameworks:**
   - **Open licenses:** Creative Commons (CC-BY, CC0), Open Data Commons (ODbL), and specialized AI agreements such as
     OpenRAIL impose varying obligations regarding commercial attribution, downstream distribution, and derivative model
     redistribution.
   - **Copyleft implications:** Training on data or code governed by reciprocal licences (e.g., GPL, AGPL) raises
     contested legal questions regarding whether trained weights constitute a "derivative work."
   - **Terms of Service (ToS) and web scraping:** Scraping public internet data often contravenes platform terms. While
     scraping public facts may fall under copyright exceptions (such as fair use or text and data mining exceptions
     under EU Directive 2019/790), violating access restrictions can trigger breach-of-contract or computer fraud
     claims.

## Retention

The principle of storage limitation (GDPR Art. 5(1)(e)) dictates that personal data must not be stored in identifiable
form longer than necessary for the explicit purposes for which it was collected. In machine learning infrastructure,
retention requires continuous policy enforcement across multiple storage tiers:

| Storage Tier | Target Artifacts | Typical Retention TTL | Policy Enforcement Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Raw Event Streams** | Telemetry ingestion buffers (Kafka, Kinesis) | $7\text{--}30$ days | Automated queue TTL eviction and hard drop |
| **Curated Feature Store** | Online entity stores (Redis, DynamoDB) | $90\text{--}180$ days | Sliding window TTL and tombstone propagation |
| **Analytical Lakehouse** | Parquet training snapshots and offline tables | $1\text{--}2$ years | Lifecycle archival policies and pseudonymization |
| **Trained Model Weights** | Checkpoint parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ | Model lifecycle | Selective unlearning or scheduled retraining |

- **Feature store time-to-live (TTL):** Modern feature stores (such as Feast or Hopsworks) associate sliding-window TTLs
  with entity features. Stale user attributes automatically age out and are purged from online key-value tables and
  offline parquet lakehouse stores.
- **Lineage and reproducibility:** Retention purges create a tension with model reproducibility: if historical training
  snapshots are deleted to satisfy retention policies, exact weight replication from raw data becomes impossible unless
  intermediate deterministic hashes and cryptographic provenance logs are preserved.

## Erasure requests

Under the "Right to be Forgotten" (GDPR Art. 17) and Data Subject Access Requests (DSAR), individuals can demand
complete deletion of their personal records. In machine learning, this creates two distinct obligations:

1. **Storage-level deletion:** Hard deletion of all raw records, identity graphs, cached feature values, and metadata
   from operational databases and storage buckets.
2. **Model-level disgorgement:** Removing the influence of the erased records from trained model parameters $\theta$.
   Regulatory bodies (notably the US Federal Trade Commission, FTC) have issued **algorithmic disgorgement** orders
   against companies (e.g., Cambridge Analytica, Everalbum, Kurbo/Weight Watchers), compelling them to destroy not only
   illicitly gathered data, but also all machine learning models and algorithms trained on that data.
3. **Membership leakage:** If a model memorizes sensitive points, an adversary can execute Membership Inference Attacks
   (MIAs) to verify whether target record $(x_i, y_i)$ was included in the training set $D_{\text{train}}$. Merely
   deleting the record from the database while keeping $\theta$ intact leaves the subject vulnerable to membership
   extraction, failing substantive privacy protection.

## Machine unlearning

**Machine unlearning** seeks to remove the statistical footprint of a specific subset of training data
$D_{\text{delete}} \subset D$ from a trained model $\theta$, producing an updated model $\theta^*$ that behaves as if it
had never encountered $D_{\text{delete}}$, while preserving predictive performance on the retained dataset
$D_{\text{retain}} = D \setminus D_{\text{delete}}$.

### Exact vs. approximate unlearning

Formally, let $\mathcal{A}$ be a training algorithm mapping a dataset to a distribution over model weights. 
- **Exact unlearning:** Guarantees that the distribution of parameters $P(\theta^*)$ is statistically indistinguishable
  from training from scratch on $D_{\text{retain}}$ alone:
  $$
  D_{\text{KL}}\!\left(P(\theta^*) \,\parallel\, P(\mathcal{A}(D_{\text{retain}}))\right) = 0
  $$
- **Approximate $(\epsilon, \delta)$-unlearning:** Bounds the divergence between $\theta^*$ and
  $\mathcal{A}(D_{\text{retain}})$ by privacy parameters $\epsilon$ and $\delta$, mirroring differential privacy
  definitions.

### SISA: Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, Aggregated

Full retraining from scratch guarantees exact unlearning but is computationally prohibitive for deep architectures
trained on millions of samples. The **SISA** architecture <Cite id="bourtoule2021" /> structures training to make exact
retraining efficient:

1. **Sharding:** Partition the training set $D$ into $S$ disjoint, non-overlapping shards $D_1, D_2, \dots, D_S$. Train
   an independent sub-model $f_s$ on each shard.
2. **Slicing:** Divide each shard $D_s$ into $R$ sequential slices. Train sub-model $f_s$ incrementally across slices,
   saving model checkpoints after each slice.
3. **Aggregation:** Combine sub-model outputs at inference time via voting or ensembling:
   $$
   F(x) = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S f_s(x)
   $$
4. **Targeted retraining:** When an erasure request arrives for sample $z \in D_{\text{delete}}$, identify the specific
   shard $s$ and slice $r$ containing $z$. Only shard $s$ is retrained, beginning from checkpoint $r-1$ with $z$
   excluded. The remaining $S - 1$ shards remain untouched, reducing unlearning compute overhead by approximately a
   factor of $S$.

### Influence scrubbing and gradient approximations

For smooth loss functions $\ell(z; \theta)$, second-order Taylor approximations and influence functions
<Cite id="koh2017" /> estimate parameter displacement without retraining. The parameter shift induced by upweighting
point $z$ by small $\epsilon$ is:

$$
\frac{d\theta^*}{d\epsilon}\Big|_{\epsilon=0} = - H_{\theta}^{-1} \nabla_\theta \ell(z; \theta^*)
$$

where $H_\theta = \frac{1}{|D_{\text{retain}}|} \sum_{i \in D_{\text{retain}}} \nabla_\theta^2 \ell(z_i; \theta^*)$ is
the empirical Hessian matrix. To unlearn point $z$, an approximate Newton update subtracts the influence:

$$
\theta^* \approx \theta + \frac{1}{|D_{\text{retain}}|} H_\theta^{-1} \nabla_\theta \ell(z; \theta)
$$

While influence scrubbing runs in seconds, computing or inverting large Hessians requires stochastic approximations
(e.g., LiSSA or Fisher information diagonals), and non-convex neural landscapes cause approximation errors to accumulate
over sequential deletion requests.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing database deletes with model unlearning:** Scrubbing records from PostgreSQL or S3 satisfies traditional IT
  compliance but leaves parametric memory intact, violating algorithmic disgorgement decrees and exposing users to
  membership inference.
- **Unlearning drift and catastrophic forgetting:** Sequentially applying approximate gradient-reversal or fine-tuning
  updates without retraining on retained data causes model weights to drift away from the optimal loss surface,
  degrading global accuracy.
- **Verification paradox:** Proving that a data point has been deleted from a deep neural network without disclosing
  proprietary model weights or internal gradients is challenging; empirical membership inference tests provide
  probabilistic evidence, not definitive guarantees.
- **Poisoning via unlearning APIs:** Malicious users can identify adversarial points that, when strategically submitted
  for unlearning, tilt the remaining decision boundary to degrade fairness or misclassify competitor data.
