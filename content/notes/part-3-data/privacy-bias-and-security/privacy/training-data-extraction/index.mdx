<Definition>
**Training-data extraction** is an adversarial privacy attack where an unauthorized user queries a trained model
$f_\theta$ to reconstruct verbatim training sequences $\mathbf{x} = (w_1, \dots, w_T) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$
without having direct access to the training dataset <Cite id="carlini2021extracting" />:

$$
f_\theta(w_{1:k}) \longrightarrow (w_{k+1}, \dots, w_T) \quad \text{such that } (w_1, \dots, w_T) \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}.
$$

Extraction exploits the model's capacity for **memorisation**, converting parametric neural weights into an accessible
search index over private training documents.
</Definition>

## Formalising memorisation

In deep learning, models do not merely generalize over broad statistical distributions; they also memorize specific
idiosyncratic training examples <Cite id="feldman2020" />:

### 1. Counterfactual memorisation
Feldman <Cite id="feldman2020" /> formalized memorisation as the marginal causal influence of a training record
$z = (\mathbf{x}, y)$ on the model's prediction:

$$
\text{Mem}(z) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D} \sim \mu} \left[ f_{\mathcal{D} \cup \{z\}}(\mathbf{x}) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D} \sim \mu} \left[ f_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{x}) \right].
$$

- If $\text{Mem}(z) \approx 0$, the model learns to predict $z$ through general patterns shared across many other data
  points.
- If $\text{Mem}(z) \approx 1$, the model can only predict $z$ correctly if $z$ was explicitly present in the training
  set, indicating that the instance was memorized in isolation.

### 2. $k$-eidetic memorisation
Carlini et al. <Cite id="carlini2021extracting" /> defined **$k$-eidetic memorisation** to quantify the threshold of
memory formation: a sequence $\mathbf{x}$ is $k$-eidetic memorised if it appears at most $k$ times in the entire
training corpus $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$:

$$
C(\mathbf{x}) \le k,
$$

and can nonetheless be generated verbatim by the model. When $k=1$, the model reproduces a sequence observed exactly
once across billions of training tokens.

## Extraction attacks

Adversaries extract private training sequences through black-box querying:

### 1. Targeted prefix completion
If an attacker possesses auxiliary context (such as an individual's name, employer, and residential city), they provide
this prefix to the model:
```text
Prompt:   "Dr. Marcus Vance, living at 742 Evergreen Terrace, private SSN is"
Output:   " ***-**-****"
```
If the model memorized the private record during pretraining, greedily sampling the next tokens reconstructs the
confidential suffix.

### 2. Untargeted generation and perplexity ratio filtering
Carlini et al. <Cite id="carlini2021extracting" /> demonstrated scalable untargeted extraction:
1. **Diverse generation**: Generate millions of completions by prompting the model with empty strings, random tokens, or
   internet common phrases sampled at high temperature ($T \ge 1.0$).
2. **Perplexity ratio filtering**: To distinguish between common language idioms (which have low perplexity but are
   public) and memorized private records, the attacker computes the ratio between the target model's loss
   $\mathcal{L}_{\text{target}}$ and a reference model's loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ref}}$ (or compression ratio
   $\mathcal{L}_{\text{zlib}}$):

   $$
   \text{Score}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{zlib}}(\mathbf{x})} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\mathcal{L}_{\text{target}}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{reference}}(\mathbf{x})}.
   $$

   Sequences that exhibit unusually low perplexity under the target model but high perplexity under the reference
   compressor are flagged as memorized training text, extracting thousands of verbatim personal phone numbers, emails,
   and proprietary code snippets.

### 3. Divergence attacks
Nasr et al. showed that prompting models with repetitive tokens (e.g., repeating the word *"poem"* hundreds of times)
causes the output distribution to diverge from conversational alignment, triggering raw memorized training buffers that
spew verbatim Wikipedia pages, personal blog posts, and private chat logs.

## The effect of duplicates and deduplication

The single most influential factor governing training data extraction risk is **sequence duplication multiplicity** $C$:

- **Super-linear scaling**: The probability of a model memorizing a sequence verbatim scales super-linearly with its
  duplication count <Cite id="carlini2021extracting" /> <Cite id="lee2022dedup" />:

  $$
  P(\text{extraction} \mid C) \propto C^\alpha, \quad \text{where } \alpha \approx 1.2\text{--}1.5.
  $$

- **Web crawl duplication**: Raw web scrapes contain immense duplication: open-source software license boilerplate,
  Terms of Service notices, corporate contact footers, and error logs repeat tens of thousands of times.
- **The deduplication remedy**: Katherine Lee et al. <Cite id="lee2022dedup" /> demonstrated that applying exact
  substring deduplication (suffix arrays) and near-duplicate clustering (MinHash LSH) caps sequence multiplicity to
  $C = 1$. Deduplication reduces verbatim training data extraction by **over $80\%$** while simultaneously reducing
  pretraining compute and improving downstream validation perplexity.

## Pitfalls and limitations

1. **Parameter scaling amplifies memorization**: Larger parameter architectures (e.g. 70B+ parameters) memorize
   substantially more training data than smaller architectures (7B) given the same training token budget.
2. **RLHF alignment does not erase memory**: Safety fine-tuning (RLHF, DPO) trains models to refuse to answer extraction
   prompts, but the underlying memorized strings remain embedded in the model weights. Adversaries easily bypass
   alignment using adversarial suffixes, prompt injection, or direct logit probing.
3. **Synthetic data memorization leakage**: Using LLMs to generate synthetic datasets can inadvertently leak memorized
   real-world personal data into the synthetic corpus, transferring extraction vulnerabilities into downstream models.
