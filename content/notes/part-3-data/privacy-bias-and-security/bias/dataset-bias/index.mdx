<Definition>
**Dataset bias** is any systematic discrepancy between the empirical distribution of training data
$P_{\text{data}}(X, Y)$ and the actual target population or normative distribution it aims to represent. Rather than a
singular statistical defect, dataset bias encompasses distinct failure modes arising at different stages of data
generation: historical inequities in the world, sampling imbalances, distorted proxy measurements, and invalid
aggregation across heterogeneous groups <Cite id="suresh2021" />.
</Definition>

Machine learning models do not learn abstract truths; they minimize empirical risk over datasets collected, labeled, and
processed through human systems. When practitioners treat datasets as unvarnished reflections of reality, models inherit
and amplify systemic distortions. Effectively mitigating bias requires diagnosing the exact stage of the data lifecycle
at which the distortion was introduced.

## Historical bias

**Historical bias** exists in the real world prior to data collection. Even if sampling is perfectly representative
($P_{\text{sample}}(X) = P_{\text{world}}(X)$) and measurement is error-free ($Y = Y^*$), the collected data faithfully
reflects existing social prejudices, structural inequities, and historical disparities:

- **Past hiring records:** A model trained on decades of corporate promotions will find that executive titles correlate
  strongly with male candidates, reflecting historical glass ceilings rather than intrinsic qualification differences.
- **Criminal justice data:** Arrest and conviction records reflect historical differences in police patrol density and
  discretionary stops across neighborhoods rather than actual underlying rates of illicit conduct.
- **The learning failure:** Standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) optimizes for fidelity to historical labels.
  Consequently, optimizing a model for test accuracy on historical data directly incentivizes the model to replicate
  past discrimination.

## Representation bias

**Representation bias** enters during data collection and sampling when the sampling distribution $P_{\text{sample}}(X)$
diverges from the target deployment distribution $P_{\text{target}}(X)$, systematically under-representing specific
demographic cohorts, geographical regions, or operational conditions:

- **Sample size starvation:** Deep neural networks and complex models require vast sample sizes to learn robust
  representations. When a minority subgroup $g$ comprises only a tiny fraction of training samples ($N_g \ll N$), the
  global objective function prioritizes the majority cohort, leading to elevated error rates on under-represented
  populations.
- **The Gender Shades benchmark:** In their landmark study, Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru <Cite id="buolamwini2018" />
  evaluated commercial facial analysis APIs and discovered extreme intersectional disparities. Because prominent
  training datasets (such as IJB-A and Adience) were overwhelmingly composed of lighter-skinned individuals (>79%),
  commercial classifiers achieved error rates under 0.8% on lighter-skinned males, but failed with error rates of up to
  34.7% on darker-skinned females.
- **Convenience and web sampling:** Datasets gathered by scraping English-dominated internet forums, social media, or
  university student subject pools severely under-represent non-Western populations, older demographics, and non-English
  linguistic varieties.

## Measurement bias

**Measurement bias** enters during feature engineering and label operationalization, occurring when the observable
feature or label proxy $Y$ systematically deviates from the unobservable latent construct $Y^*$ across demographic
groups ($P(Y \mid Y^*, A) \neq P(Y \mid Y^*)$):

- **Faulty proxies in healthcare:** Commercial predictive algorithms widely used in US hospitals relied on historical
  healthcare spending as a proxy for healthcare need. Because less money was historically spent on Black patients due to
  poverty and unequal insurance access, the algorithm mistakenly concluded that Black patients were healthier than
  equally sick white patients, cutting remedial care enrollments in half.
- **Differential sensor measurement:** Physical measurement instruments exhibit demographic disparities. Optical pulse
  oximeters, digital thermometers, and computer vision sensors calibrated primarily on light skin pigmentation exhibit
  higher noise and measurement drift on darker skin tones.
- **Annotator subjectivity:** Subjective classification tasks (such as content moderation, toxic language detection, or
  sentiment analysis) reflect the cultural priors, sociolects, and implicit biases of crowd-workers, systematically
  tagging African American Vernacular English (AAVE) as toxic at disproportionately high rates.

## Aggregation bias

**Aggregation bias** arises when a single global model $f_\theta(X)$ is fitted across a heterogeneous population
containing distinct subgroups with differing conditional relationships $P(Y \mid X, A)$:

- **Simpson's paradox:** A feature $X_j$ that correlates positively with outcome $Y$ within subgroup $A=0$ and within
  subgroup $A=1$ may correlate negatively when aggregated across the combined population.
- **Clinical biomarker heterogeneity:** In cardiovascular medicine and endocrinology, physiological biomarkers (such as
  HbA1c for glycemic control or body mass index for metabolic risk) exhibit different clinical risk trajectories across
  genetic ancestries and sexes. A homogeneous model fitted to aggregate data optimizes for the majority population and
  misestimates risk for all constituent groups.

## Which fix addresses which

Applying the wrong mitigation to a bias mechanism is ineffective or actively harmful. The table below maps bias types to
their appropriate engineering remedies:

| Bias Type | Pipeline Entry Stage | Ineffective Remedy | Targeted Technical Remedy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Historical** | Pre-collection world state | Gathering more data; stratified sampling | Target reweighting; causal counterfactual invariance; policy-based acceptance thresholds |
| **Representation** | Data collection & cohort sampling | Loss function tweaks without new samples | Targeted stratified sampling; active learning for minority coverage; curated diverse benchmarks <Cite id="buolamwini2018" /> |
| **Measurement** | Feature & label operationalization | Oversampling biased labels | Construct validity redesign; differential calibration; multi-annotator disagreement modeling |
| **Aggregation** | Model pooling & architecture | Uniform global training | Mixture-of-experts; group-conditioned model heads; multi-task learning across sub-cohorts |

## Pitfalls

- **The "more data" fallacy:** Assuming that expanding dataset size automatically cures bias. Scaling up web scraping
  magnifies historical stereotypes and measurement noise while entrenching majority representation.
- **Equating demographic balance with fairness:** Believing that balancing cohort counts ($N_{A=0} = N_{A=1}$)
  guarantees equitable performance; if the underlying labels carry measurement or historical bias, the balanced model
  simply becomes more confident in its biased predictions.
- **Ignoring intersectionality:** Evaluating representation along single demographic axes (e.g. evaluating sex and race
  independently) obscures severe representation starvation at intersectional boundaries (such as darker-skinned women).
- **Static assumptions under feedback loops:** Deploying a model affected by dataset bias creates self-reinforcing
  feedback loops: biased predictions dictate real-world interventions, which generate future training data that cements
  the original bias into ground truth.
