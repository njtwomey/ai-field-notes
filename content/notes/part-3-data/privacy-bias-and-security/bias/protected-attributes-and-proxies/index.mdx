<Definition>
A **protected attribute** (or sensitive feature) $A$ is a demographic or personal characteristic—such as race, sex, age,
religion, or disability status—legally shielded against discrimination in consequential domains like lending,
employment, and housing. A **proxy feature** $X_{\text{proxy}}$ is an unconstrained, seemingly neutral covariate that
statistically correlates with or reconstructs $A$, allowing models to perpetuate disparate impact even when $A$ is
explicitly omitted from training.
</Definition>

Excluding protected attributes from training datasets is often mandated by law or adopted as an intuitive anti-bias
heuristic. However, in high-dimensional datasets, mutual information between ostensibly benign inputs and sensitive
attributes allows machine learning models to recover protected class membership. Paradoxically, measuring or preventing
algorithmic bias requires access to the very protected attributes that privacy laws and corporate policies restrict
organizations from collecting.

## Removing the attribute

The naive approach to fair machine learning is **fairness through unawareness** (or "fairness through blindness")
<Cite id="dwork2012" />: training a predictive model $f(X)$ on input features $X$ from which the protected attribute $A$
has been excised:

$$
\hat{Y} = f(X \setminus \{A\})
$$

While this policy guarantees individual-level procedural blindness—preventing the model from conditioning decisions
directly on an explicit demographic flag—it fails to guarantee demographic parity or equal treatment:

- **Statistical dependency:** In real-world data, the remaining features $X$ are rarely statistically independent of
  $A$; that is, $P(X \mid A = 0) \neq P(X \mid A = 1)$.
- **Reconstruction capacity:** High-capacity non-linear models (such as gradient-boosted decision trees and neural
  networks) can learn complex non-linear combinations of correlated features to reconstruct $A$ with high fidelity
  ($R^2 \approx 1$).
- **Disparate impact:** If the ground-truth target variable $Y$ or its proxy features correlate with historical
  disadvantages, the blind model $\hat{Y} = f(X \setminus \{A\})$ will reproduce the identical discriminatory outcome
  distribution as an unconstrained model trained with explicit knowledge of $A$.

## Proxies that remain

Proxy discrimination arises when non-sensitive features carry redundant encodings of protected group identity:

1. **Geographic redlining:** Postal codes, census tracts, and municipal jurisdictions frequently serve as
   near-deterministic proxies for race and socioeconomic status due to decades of residential segregation. In lending
   and insurance, conditioning decisions on ZIP codes routinely reconstructs racial disparities without referencing race
   directly.
2. **Behavioral and digital footprints:** Web browsing logs, streaming music genres, university affiliations,
   professional association memberships, and language syntax in text resumes carry strong mutual information with
   gender, age, and cultural background.
3. **Compound proxy reconstruction:** Even if no single feature exhibits strong correlation with $A$
   ($\rho(X_j, A) < 0.2$), a linear or non-linear combination across hundreds of weak signals enables near-perfect
   reconstruction of $A$. In high dimensions, orthogonality to $A$ across all basis vectors is virtually impossible
   without deliberate decorrelation or adversarial representation learning.
4. **Legal disparate impact:** Under US legal doctrine (such as Title VII of the Civil Rights Act and the Equal Credit
   Opportunity Act), the absence of discriminatory intent or explicit protected features does not protect an
   organization from liability. The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) **four-fifths rule** defines an
   adverse impact threshold: if the selection rate for any protected group is less than 80% ($0.80$) of the rate for the
   highest group, the model faces a prima facie finding of discrimination:
   $$
   \text{Disparate Impact Ratio} = \frac{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0)}{P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)} < 0.80
   $$

## Needing the attribute to audit

The central operational paradox of algorithmic fairness is the **fairness audit dilemma**: an organization cannot
evaluate whether its models discriminate without collecting and retaining the protected attributes of its users.

### Group fairness criteria require $A$

Auditing a model against standard mathematical definitions of fairness requires conditioning model outputs on the
sensitive attribute $A$:

- **Demographic parity:** Requires acceptance rates to be independent of protected status:
  $$
  P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 0) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = 1)
  $$
- **Equalized odds <Cite id="hardt2016" />:** Requires equal true positive rates (TPR) and false positive rates (FPR)
  across groups:
  $$
  P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = y, A = 0) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid Y = y, A = 1) \quad \forall y \in \{0, 1\}
  $$
- **Predictive parity <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />:** Requires equal positive predictive value (precision) across
  groups:
  $$
  P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 0) = P(Y = 1 \mid \hat{Y} = 1, A = 1)
  $$

Without access to $A$, none of these metrics can be computed, monitored, or mitigated during model lifecycle governance.

### Legal restrictions on collecting $A$

Data protection regimes often prohibit or penalize the collection of sensitive attributes:
- **GDPR Article 9:** Classifies biometric data, ethnic origin, political opinions, and health status as "special
  categories of personal data," forbidding processing unless strict, narrowly defined exceptions (such as explicit
  consent or substantial public interest) are satisfied.
- **Fair housing and credit laws:** The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) historically barred lenders from inquiring
  about applicants' race, color, religion, national origin, or sex, creating a legal hurdle to auditing the algorithms
  used to evaluate those same applicants.

### Technical solutions to the audit dilemma

To audit models without maintaining centralized, identifiable demographic databases, engineers employ privacy-preserving
measurement techniques:

- **Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (BISG):** Uses public census distributions to compute probabilistic race and
  ethnicity estimates $P(A \mid \text{Surname}, \text{ZIP})$ from non-sensitive inputs for aggregated post-hoc auditing.
- **Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC):** Allows a third-party auditor holding protected attributes and a model owner
  holding predictions $\hat{Y}$ to jointly compute group disparity metrics without either party exposing raw inputs to
  the other.
- **Differential privacy and randomized response:** Collects noisy demographic declarations directly from users,
  bounding individual privacy risk while providing asymptotically unbiased group-level auditing statistics.

## Pitfalls

- **Equating omission with compliance:** Believing that dropping sensitive columns from feature stores insulates a model
  from regulatory scrutiny or discriminatory behavior.
- **Naive feature pruning:** Removing individual correlated features without evaluating multivariate combinations;
  remaining non-linear interactions still reconstruct the sensitive target.
- **Audit imputation bias:** Using noisy demographic proxies (such as BISG) to compute audit metrics without accounting
  for imputation variance or systematically higher error rates on multiracial and immigrant populations.
- **Single-attribute myopia:** Auditing protected categories independently while ignoring **intersectionality** (e.g.,
  assessing gender and race separately, which fails to capture severe compounded disparities experienced by women of
  color).
