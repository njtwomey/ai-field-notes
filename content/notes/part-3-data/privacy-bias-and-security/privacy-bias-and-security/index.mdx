<Definition>
**Privacy, bias and security** are three ways training data can cause harm beyond poor
accuracy. A privacy failure exposes the people the data describe. A bias makes a model serve some groups worse than
others. A security failure lets an attacker shape the model through the data. All three originate in how data are
collected, filtered and documented, and are cheapest to address there.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A model inherits more than its data's signal. It can memorise and repeat personal records
<Cite id="carlini2021extracting" />. It can reproduce historical inequities, underperform on groups that were
under-sampled, or rely on a proxy for a protected attribute that was deliberately removed. If part of the data comes
from the open web or from users, an attacker can plant examples that degrade the model or install a hidden trigger
<Cite id="gu2019badnets" />. These failures are rarely visible in an aggregate validation score. The notes here describe
how each arises in the data and which data-side measures reduce it.

## Main ideas

**Privacy starts with identifiability.** <NoteLink to="data-privacy" /> covers direct and quasi-identifiers,
<NoteLink to="pii-detection-and-redaction">PII redaction</NoteLink>, k-anonymity and its limits, memorisation and
extraction of training records, and the rights to erasure that reach trained models.

**Bias enters at different stages.** <NoteLink to="dataset-bias" /> separates historical bias in the world being
recorded, representation bias in who is sampled, measurement bias in how features and labels are recorded, and
aggregation bias in pooling groups that differ <Cite id="suresh2021" />. Each stage needs a different fix.
<NoteLink to="protected-attributes-and-proxies" /> explains why dropping a protected attribute does not remove its
influence, and why auditing needs the attribute. <NoteLink to="content-filtering" /> shows that toxicity filters remove
text unevenly by dialect and topic, changing who the data represent.

**Data are an attack surface.** <NoteLink to="data-poisoning-and-backdoors" /> covers label flipping, backdoor triggers
and poisoning of web-scale collections. Provenance checks on sources are a main defence.
