<Definition>
A **positional encoding** maps a location $p$ to a feature vector $\gamma(p)$. The location can be an index in a
sequence, a pixel's row and column, a point in space, a node in a graph or a place on the Earth. A **Fourier
encoding** of a coordinate vector $\vvec$ is $\gamma(\vvec) = (\cos 2\pi\Bmat\vvec, \sin 2\pi\Bmat\vvec)$ for a matrix
of frequencies $\Bmat$, fixed or random. **Learned** encodings give each position its own trained vector. **Relative**
encodings describe the offset between two positions rather than each position. **Structural** encodings derive a
node's position from the graph, and **cell** encodings replace a place by the index of the grid cell that contains it.
</Definition>

## Problem

Positions need encoding for two different reasons.

**Some models cannot see position at all.** Self-attention, set models and graph message passing treat their inputs
as an unordered collection: permuting the inputs permutes the outputs and changes nothing else. A sentence and the
same words shuffled look alike, as do two image patches swapped. A positional encoding added to each input tells the
model where it is <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.

**Some models see position but cannot use it finely.** A <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptron</Gloss> given raw
coordinates, such as $(x, y)$ of a pixel, learns the low-frequency parts of a function first and the high-frequency
parts very slowly <Cite id="rahaman2019" />. Asked to map coordinates to colours, it produces a blurred image. Mapping
the coordinates through sines and cosines of many frequencies first lets the same network fit sharp detail
<Cite id="tancik2020" />.

The geometry of the positions adds a third concern. Sequence indices lie on a line, angles on a circle, nodes on a
graph with no coordinates, and places on a sphere. An encoding that ignores the geometry puts neighbours far apart.

## Mechanism

### Positions in sequences

The sinusoidal encoding of the original transformer gives position $t$ the vector with components $\sin(t/10000^{2i/d})$
and $\cos(t/10000^{2i/d})$ for $i = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1$: a ladder of wavelengths from $2\pi$ to about $2\pi \times 10000$
<Cite id="vaswani2017" />. Learned absolute embeddings, relative encodings, attention biases and
<NoteLink to="rotary-position-embedding">rotary embeddings</NoteLink> are alternatives; the
<NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encoding</NoteLink> chapter of the transformer notes compares them.

The data side raises two questions. First, the encoding must match how sequences are cut. When several documents are
packed into one training sequence, positions can restart at each document boundary; if they do not, the model sees the
start of one document at the middle of a sequence. Second, learned absolute embeddings exist only for positions seen in
training, so a model trained on sequences of 512 tokens has no embedding for position 600. Sinusoidal, relative and
rotary encodings are defined at every position, which is necessary but not enough for a model to generalise to longer
inputs.

### Positions in images and volumes

An image patch or a voxel has two or three coordinates. The usual encodings treat each axis separately and concatenate
the results, or learn one vector per patch position, as the <NoteLink to="vision-transformer">vision
transformer</NoteLink> does. Learned patch embeddings are tied to one grid size; a model applied at a higher resolution
has its embeddings interpolated to the new grid <Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />.

### Fourier features of coordinates

For a coordinate $\vvec \in \reals^k$, the Fourier feature mapping is

$$
\gamma(\vvec) = \paren{\cos(2\pi\Bmat\vvec), \sin(2\pi\Bmat\vvec)}, \qquad \Bmat \in \reals^{m \times k},
$$

applied elementwise to the $m$ rows of $\Bmat\vvec$. Two choices of $\Bmat$ are common.

- **A ladder of frequencies.** Each coordinate $v$ gets the sines and cosines of $2^0\pi v, 2^1\pi v, \dots,
  2^{L-1}\pi v$. Neural radiance fields encode each of the three coordinates of a point this way with $L = 10$ before
  the network that predicts colour and density <Cite id="mildenhall2020" />.
- **Random frequencies.** The entries of $\Bmat$ are drawn from $\Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. In the neural tangent kernel view
  of a wide network trained on $\gamma(\vvec)$, the network then behaves like a kernel method with a stationary kernel
  whose width is set by $\sigma$: small $\sigma$ gives a smooth fit, large $\sigma$ a detailed one, and too large
  $\sigma$ overfits <Cite id="tancik2020" />.

The random version is the <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">random Fourier features</NoteLink> construction of
Rahimi and Recht, which approximates a Gaussian kernel of lengthscale $\ell$ when the frequencies have standard
deviation $1/\ell$ <Cite id="rahimi2007" />. Two coordinates $\Delta$ apart then have encodings whose inner product is
about $\exp(-\Delta^2/2\ell^2)$: 0.61 at $\Delta = \ell$ and 0.01 at $\Delta = 3\ell$. Points more than a few
lengthscales apart look unrelated to a linear model on the features, and points much closer than $\ell$ look the
same. The lengthscale is the resolution of the encoding.

With the defaults of the figure, a least-squares line on the raw coordinate has a test error of 0.29. A
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> on 200 Fourier features has a test error of 0.19 at

Sine activations are an alternative to a fixed encoding: a network whose layers apply $\sin$ instead of a
<Gloss name="relu" /> fits fine detail from raw coordinates when its weights are initialised at a suitable scale
<Cite id="sitzmann2020" />.

### Positions in graphs

A graph has no natural coordinates and no canonical order of its nodes. Structural encodings compute positions from
the graph itself.

- **Laplacian eigenvectors.** The eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Amat$ with the smallest nonzero
  eigenvalues vary slowly across the graph, so nearby nodes get similar values. The first $k$ of them give each node $k$
  coordinates, the same coordinates used by <NoteLink to="laplacian-eigenmaps">Laplacian eigenmaps</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">spectral clustering</NoteLink>. Each eigenvector is defined only up to its sign,
  and up to a rotation when eigenvalues repeat, so a model must be trained to ignore the choice, for example by flipping
  signs at random <Cite id="dwivedi2022" />.
- **Random-walk encodings.** Node $i$ gets the probabilities that a random walk starting at $i$ is back at $i$ after
  $1, 2, \dots, k$ steps. These depend only on the graph around the node and have no sign ambiguity
  <Cite id="dwivedi2022" />.

Both are computed once per graph before training. For a graph that changes, they must be recomputed or updated.

### Geographic coordinates

Latitude $\varphi$ and longitude $\lambda$ are angles on a sphere, not coordinates on a plane. Longitude wraps at
$\pm 180°$, and a degree of longitude spans 111 km at the equator and nothing at the poles. Raw latitude and longitude
put two points either side of the date line 360 degrees apart.

- **Unit vectors.** The point $(\cos\varphi\cos\lambda, \cos\varphi\sin\lambda, \sin\varphi)$ on the unit sphere is
  continuous everywhere, including the poles and the date line, and chord distances between such points grow with the
  distance along the surface.
- **Multi-scale sinusoids.** Sines and cosines of latitude and longitude at many frequencies give fine spatial
  detail, as Fourier features do, but they treat the globe as a rectangle and produce artefacts near the poles.
  Spherical harmonics are defined on the sphere itself and avoid them <Cite id="russwurm2024" />.
- **Cells.** A hierarchical grid assigns each point the index of the cell containing it. H3 covers the Earth with
  hexagonal cells at 16 resolutions, from cells with edges of about 1,281 km to edges of about 0.6 m; every resolution
  also has 12 pentagons <Cite id="h3docs" />. A cell index is a category, encoded like any other (see
  <NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">categorical encoding</NoteLink>), and indices at several resolutions give
  features at several scales.

Which coordinate system the data are in, and how to compute distances, is covered in
<NoteLink to="coordinate-reference-systems">coordinate reference systems</NoteLink>; features built from places, such
as distances to points of interest, in <NoteLink to="spatial-features">spatial features</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Two weather stations sit on the equator at longitudes $179.9°$ and $-179.9°$.

- **Raw coordinates.** Their longitudes differ by $359.8°$, so a model on raw longitude treats them as being on
  opposite sides of the world.
- **Unit vectors.** The angle between them is $0.2°$, and the chord between their unit vectors is
  $2\sin(0.1°) = 0.0035$. Multiplied by the Earth's radius of 6,371 km, that is 22 km, their true separation.
- **H3 cells.** At a resolution whose cells are larger than 22 km, the two stations usually share a cell, or fall in
  neighbouring cells.

## Cost

Fourier and sinusoidal encodings cost $2m$ features for $m$ frequencies and need no fitting beyond drawing or fixing
the frequencies. Learned embeddings cost one vector per position and exist only for positions seen in training.
Laplacian encodings need the $k$ smallest eigenvectors of a sparse matrix; each iteration of an iterative eigensolver
costs time proportional to the number of edges. Random-walk encodings need $k$ sparse matrix products.

## Trade-offs

- **Resolution against generalisation.** Higher frequencies resolve finer detail and also fit noise; the lengthscale
  of a Fourier encoding plays the role of a kernel bandwidth.
- **Fixed against learned.** Fixed encodings are defined everywhere and carry an explicit notion of distance. Learned
  encodings adapt to the task but only cover the positions seen in training.
- **Absolute against relative.** Absolute encodings say where something is; relative encodings say how far apart two
  things are. Tasks that depend on offsets, such as language, favour relative encodings.

## Pitfalls

- **Raw latitude and longitude near the date line or the poles.** Neighbours look far apart. Use unit vectors, cells or
  spherical encodings.
- **A Fourier lengthscale chosen without validation.** Too short and the model memorises the training points; too long
  and it cannot resolve the detail it needs.
- **Ignoring the sign of Laplacian eigenvectors.** A model trained with one sign choice fails when the solver returns
  the other.
- **Cells too fine for the data.** At a fine resolution most cells hold one or two rows, and a cell index then acts as
  an identifier: it memorises the rows and, if target-encoded, leaks their labels.
- **Positions that restart silently.** Packing, cropping and tiling change what a position means; the encoding must
  follow the same convention in training and in use.
