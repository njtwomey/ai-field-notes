<Definition>
A **numeric encoding** maps a real value $x$ to a vector $\phi(x) = (\phi_1(x), \dots, \phi_m(x)) \in \reals^m$ of fixed
functions of $x$, chosen or fitted before the model is trained. **Binning** uses indicator functions of intervals,
$\phi_j(x) = \indicator[t_{j} \le x < t_{j+1}]$. A **piecewise-linear encoding** uses functions that are linear between
breakpoints $t_1 < \dots < t_K$. A **spline encoding** uses the <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-splines</NoteLink> of degree
$p$ on those knots. A **periodic encoding** uses $\sin(2\pi c_k x)$ and $\cos(2\pi c_k x)$ for frequencies $c_k$. A
model that is linear in $\phi(x)$ is a weighted sum of the functions, so it can bend wherever the functions bend.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear model</NoteLink> given a number $x$ as one input fits a straight line in $x$:
one slope, the same everywhere. Many effects are not lines. Risk can rise with age and then level off, demand can peak
at a middle price, and a threshold can switch an outcome on. A linear model can only fit such effects if the input
carries them in a form that is linear in the parameters.

<NoteLink to="decision-tree">Decision trees</NoteLink> and their ensembles do not have this problem. A tree splits $x$
at learned thresholds, so it fits a step function in $x$ and is unchanged by any increasing transformation of $x$.
Neural networks can learn any smooth function of $x$ in principle, but a <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptron</Gloss>
given the raw scalar learns sharp changes slowly; encoding the scalar first improves tabular networks
<Cite id="gorishniy2022" />. Numeric encodings therefore matter most for linear models, generalised linear and additive
models, and neural networks.

## Mechanism

### Raw values

The simplest encoding is $\phi(x) = x$, usually after <NoteLink to="feature-scaling">scaling</NoteLink>. A monotone
transform, such as a logarithm or a <NoteLink to="power-transforms">power transform</NoteLink>, changes which straight
line fits best but still gives a monotone effect. Raw values extrapolate linearly, which is right for some quantities
and badly wrong for others.

### Discretisation and binning

Binning cuts the range of $x$ at $K$ edges $t_1 < \dots < t_K$ into $K - 1$ intervals and encodes $x$ by the
<NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">one-hot</NoteLink> indicator of its interval. A linear model on the indicators fits
a step function: one constant per bin, equal to the mean target of the training rows in the bin. Binning turns a
number into a category; everything about categorical encoding then applies.

The edges can be placed in three ways.

- **Equal width.** The edges divide $[\min x, \max x]$ into intervals of equal length. Outliers stretch the range, so
  most rows can end up in one or two bins.
- **Equal frequency.** The edges are quantiles of the training values, so each bin holds about the same number of
  rows. Bins are narrow where data are dense. A value repeated in many rows makes neighbouring quantiles coincide, which
  gives empty or merged bins.
- **Supervised.** The edges are chosen to make the target nearly constant within each bin. Fayyad and Irani's method
  splits an interval at the cut point that minimises the class entropy of the two halves, recurses on each half, and
  stops when a minimum description length criterion says that a further cut does not pay for itself
  <Cite id="fayyad1993" />. A shallow decision tree on $x$ alone gives the same kind of edges.

Bin indicators are the B-splines of degree 0, so binning is the first member of the spline family below.

### Piecewise-linear encodings

A step function jumps at every edge. A piecewise-linear encoding gives a continuous function with a change of slope at
each breakpoint instead. One basis is the **hat functions**: $\phi_j(x)$ is 1 at $t_j$, falls linearly to 0 at
$t_{j-1}$ and $t_{j+1}$, and is 0 elsewhere. These are the B-splines of degree 1. Any continuous function that is linear
between the breakpoints is a weighted sum of them.

Gorishniy, Rubachev and Babenko use a cumulative version for neural networks <Cite id="gorishniy2022" />. For bins
$[t_{j-1}, t_j)$ the $j$-th component is 0 when $x$ is left of the bin, 1 when $x$ is right of it, and
$(x - t_{j-1})/(t_j - t_{j-1})$ inside it. The vector fills up like a thermometer as $x$ grows. It spans the same
functions as the hat functions, plus a constant, but it keeps the order of the bins visible in the encoding. The bins
can come from quantiles or from a decision tree fitted to the target.

### Spline encodings

The B-splines of degree $p$ on $K$ knots give $K + p - 1$ functions <Cite id="deboor1978" />. Each is a piecewise
polynomial of degree $p$ that is nonzero on only $p + 1$ neighbouring intervals, and together they sum to 1 at every
$x$ in the training range. A linear model on them is a
<NoteLink to="regression-splines">regression spline</NoteLink>: a curve with $p - 1$ continuous derivatives that can
change shape between any two knots <Cite id="hastie2009" />. Cubic splines, $p = 3$, are the usual choice. With many
knots and a penalty on the differences of neighbouring coefficients, the fit becomes a
<NoteLink to="p-splines">P-spline</NoteLink>, which is smooth whatever the number of knots
<Cite id="eilers1996" />.

When the model has an intercept, one B-spline per feature is dropped. The functions sum to 1, the same as the
intercept's column, so keeping all of them would make the design rank-deficient, as one-hot encoding does.

#### Behaviour outside the training range

The knots cover the training range. A value beyond it needs a rule, and the rule decides the prediction there.

- **Constant.** Every feature keeps its value at the nearest end of the range, so the prediction is flat beyond the
  data. This is the safe default.
- **Continue.** The end polynomials are extended. A cubic grows like $x^3$, so predictions can run off quickly.
- **Linear.** The fit is extended along its tangent at the boundary. Natural cubic splines build this in by requiring
  the fit to be linear beyond the outermost knots <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
- **Error.** The encoder refuses values it has not seen, which forces the question to be answered upstream.

Raw values extrapolate along the fitted line and bins hold the edge bin's constant. No encoding knows what happens
outside the data; each one only fixes what the model will say there.

### Periodic encodings

A periodic encoding uses sines and cosines of $x$:

$$
\phi(x) = \paren{\sin(2\pi c_1 x), \cos(2\pi c_1 x), \dots, \sin(2\pi c_k x), \cos(2\pi c_k x)}.
$$

For a cyclical quantity, such as an hour or an angle, the frequencies are multiples of one over the period and the
encoding wraps around; the <NoteLink to="cyclical-encoding">cyclical encoding</NoteLink> note covers that case. For an
ordinary number, the frequencies are a spread of scales. Gorishniy and colleagues draw them from a normal distribution
and train them with the network; a linear layer and a <Gloss name="relu" /> follow <Cite id="gorishniy2022" />. Fixed
random frequencies give <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">random Fourier features</NoteLink>, and the spread of the
frequencies sets how fine a detail the model can fit <Cite id="tancik2020" />.

### Learned numeric embeddings

A neural network can map each scalar feature $x_j$ to a vector $x_j \wvec_j + \bvec_j$ with learned $\wvec_j$ and
$\bvec_j$, so that every feature becomes a token for a transformer <Cite id="gorishniy2021" />. That embedding is linear
in $x_j$. Applying it after a piecewise-linear or periodic encoding makes it non-linear, and in Gorishniy and
colleagues' experiments those combinations improved multilayer perceptrons and transformers on
<NoteLink to="tabular-benchmark-datasets">tabular benchmarks</NoteLink> <Cite id="gorishniy2022" />.

## Worked example

Take the step function $y = \operatorname{sgn} x$ on $[-2, 2]$ with noise of standard deviation 0.2, as in the figure
with the step function, degree 0 and uniform knots.

- **Four knots.** The edges fall near $-2, -0.67, 0.67, 2$, so the middle bin $[-0.67, 0.67)$ straddles the jump. About
  a third of the rows are in it, half with $y = -1$ and half with $y = 1$, and the bin's fitted constant is near 0. Each
  of those rows is about 1 away from its target, so the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> is near
  $\frac13 \times 1 + 0.2^2 \approx 0.37$ and the <Gloss name="rmse">root mean squared error</Gloss> near 0.6. The
  figure reports 0.68.
- **Five knots.** An edge now falls at 0, every bin lies on one side of the jump, and the error drops to 0.25, close to
  the noise level of 0.2.
- **Cubic splines on five knots.** The fit must be smooth, so it cannot jump. It overshoots on both sides of 0 and the
  error is 0.45, worse than the five bins.

The encoding that matches the shape of the true function wins. Bins suit thresholds; splines suit smooth curves.

## Cost

| Encoding           | Features per input   | Nonzero per row |
| ------------------ | -------------------- | --------------- |
| Raw                | 1                    | 1               |
| Bins on $K$ edges  | $K - 1$              | 1               |
| Hat functions      | $K$                  | 2               |
| B-splines degree $p$ | $K + p - 1$        | $p + 1$         |
| Periodic, $k$ frequencies | $2k$          | $2k$            |

Fitting the encoder needs one pass over the training column, for its range or quantiles. Supervised bins need a sort
and a search for each cut. The encoded design is sparse for bins and splines, which keeps linear models cheap even with
many knots.

## Trade-offs

- **Flexibility against variance.** More bins or knots fit finer shapes and estimate each coefficient from fewer rows.
  A penalty on neighbouring coefficients, or a number of knots chosen by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>, controls this.
- **Steps against smoothness.** Bins fit thresholds and jumps exactly when an edge is in the right place, and nothing
  else well. Splines fit smooth curves and blur jumps.
- **Interactions.** An encoding of one feature lets the model bend in that feature only. Effects that depend on two
  features together need product features (see <NoteLink to="feature-construction">feature construction</NoteLink>)
  or a model that builds them, such as a tree ensemble.
- **Interpretability.** Bins and splines give a readable curve per feature, which is the basis of
  <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive models</NoteLink>. Periodic and learned embeddings do
  not.

## Pitfalls

- **Fitting the encoder on all the data.** Quantile edges and knots computed on the test rows leak a little; supervised
  bins computed with the test labels leak a lot. Fit the encoder inside each training fold, as for any
  data-dependent step (see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>).
- **Ignoring the extrapolation rule.** A cubic spline continued beyond the data can predict values far outside anything
  seen. Check what the encoder does beyond the training range before deploying it on data that drift.
- **Equal-width bins with outliers.** One extreme value stretches the range and empties most bins.
- **Binning before a flexible model.** A tree or boosted ensemble finds its own thresholds. Binning first only throws
  away the order within each bin.
- **Too many knots without a penalty.** Each spline coefficient is then fitted from a handful of rows and the curve
  wiggles between them.
