<Definition>
**Structured target encodings** are the mathematical transformations that convert complex geometric or linguistic
annotations—such as bounding boxes, polygon masks, human joint coordinates, and entity text spans—into standardized
numerical tensors compatible with standard neural network loss functions (cross-entropy, mean squared error, smooth
$L_1$).
</Definition>

In standard supervised learning, targets are simple scalar class indices $y \in \{0, \dots, C-1\}$ or real numbers
$y \in \mathbb{R}$. In computer vision and natural language processing, however, predictions are complex structured
entities. Directly regressing raw coordinates (e.g. predicting $[x, y]$ pixels) suffers from scale variance,
multi-modality, and discontinuous optimization surfaces.

Models resolve this by re-encoding annotations into relative offsets, spatial heatmaps, or aligned sequence tags.

## Boxes and anchors in object detection

In object detection (Faster R-CNN, SSD, RetinaNet, YOLO), ground-truth bounding boxes are specified by pixel
coordinates: center coordinates $(x_c, y_c)$ and dimensions $(w, h)$.

Directly predicting absolute pixel values fails because errors on small objects (e.g. a 5-pixel error on a 10-pixel
icon) carry far greater relative consequence than errors on large objects (a 5-pixel error on an 800-pixel car).
Furthermore, standard linear heads can predict negative widths or heights.

Instead, targets are parameterized as **scale-invariant relative offsets against predefined anchor boxes**
$(x_a, y_a, w_a, h_a)$:

$$
t_x = \frac{x_c - x_a}{w_a}, \qquad t_y = \frac{y_c - y_a}{h_a}
$$

$$
t_w = \ln\left(\frac{w}{w_a}\right), \qquad t_h = \ln\left(\frac{h}{h_a}\right)
$$

During inference, predicted offsets $[\hat{t}_x, \hat{t}_y, \hat{t}_w, \hat{t}_h]$ are inverted to recover physical
pixel boxes:

$$
\hat{x}_c = x_a + \hat{t}_x w_a, \qquad \hat{w} = w_a \exp(\hat{t}_w)
$$

The exponential mapping $\exp(\cdot)$ mathematically guarantees that predicted widths and heights are strictly positive.

## Masks in semantic and instance segmentation

In semantic segmentation (U-Net, DeepLab, Mask2Former), annotations are encoded as **dense spatial label volumes**:

1. **Per-pixel class indices:** The ground-truth mask is an integer tensor $M \in \{0, \dots, C-1\}^{H \times W}$, where
   each coordinate $(i, j)$ holds the categorical class ID of that pixel.
2. **One-hot target volume:** For $C$ classes, $M$ expands to a binary tensor $Y \in \{0, 1\}^{C \times H \times W}$.
3. **Loss formulation:** Models output per-pixel logits $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ evaluated via per-pixel
   cross-entropy combined with **Soft Dice loss** to address extreme foreground/background area imbalance:
   $$
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2 \sum_{i} p_i y_i + \epsilon}{\sum_i p_i^2 + \sum_i y_i^2 + \epsilon}
   $$

In instance segmentation (Mask R-CNN), binary masks are encoded within cropped, RoI-aligned bounding box feature grids
(e.g. $28 \times 28$ binary resolution) rather than full image resolutions.

## Keypoint heatmaps in pose estimation

In human pose estimation and facial landmark tracking (OpenPose, HRNet, CenterNet), ground truth is specified by
discrete Cartesian joint coordinates $(x_k, y_k) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ for each joint $k \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ (e.g. nose,
left wrist, right ankle).

Direct regression of $(x_k, y_k)$ via mean squared error struggles with non-convex loss surfaces and multi-person
occlusions. Modern architectures encode keypoints as **continuous 2D Gaussian heatmaps**:

$$
H_k(x, y) = \exp\left( -\frac{(x - x_k)^2 + (y - y_k)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) \in [0, 1]
$$

where $\sigma$ (typically 2 to 4 pixels) controls spatial tolerance.

- The network outputs $K$ spatial feature maps of resolution $H' \times W'$.
- Trained via mean squared error or focal loss against ground-truth heatmaps.
- At inference time, exact joint coordinates are decoded by finding the spatial $\arg\max$, refined via sub-pixel Taylor
  expansion or centroid averaging.

## Span tags in natural language processing

In information extraction and Named Entity Recognition (NER), targets consist of variable-length character spans
$[s_{\text{start}}, s_{\text{end}}]$ labeled with entity types (e.g. `PERSON`, `ORGANIZATION`, `DATE`).

Because language models operate on discrete subword tokens, continuous character spans are aligned to tokens using
sequential tagging schemes:

### 1. The BIO scheme (IOB2)
- `B-<TYPE>`: The **Beginning** token of an entity span.
- `I-<TYPE>`: A token **Inside** the entity span.
- `O`: **Outside** any entity.

### 2. The BILOU / BIOES scheme
Provides finer boundary discriminability by differentiating single-token entities:
- `B-<TYPE>`: Beginning of a multi-token entity.
- `I-<TYPE>`: Inside a multi-token entity.
- `L-<TYPE>` (or `E`): **Last** (End) token of a multi-token entity.
- `U-<TYPE>` (or `S`): **Unit** (Single) token entity span.
- `O`: Outside.

Subword tokenizers (e.g. Byte-Pair Encoding or WordPiece) frequently split words across multiple tokens (e.g.
`"Bletchley"` $\to$ `["Blet", "##ch", "##ley"]`). Standard alignment assigns the label to the first subword token and
masks subsequent wordpieces, or assigns `I-<TYPE>` across all subword pieces.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Subword token misalignment in NER:** If annotation character offsets do not align cleanly with subword tokenizer
   boundaries (e.g. punctuation attached to tokens), spans are truncated or split, corrupting entity evaluation.
2. **Anchor box scale mismatch in detection:** If predefined anchor box aspect ratios do not match dataset object scales
   (e.g. looking for long, thin power lines using square $1:1$ anchors), target offsets become excessively large,
   destabilizing gradient descent.
3. **Vanishing keypoint heatmaps on downsampled feature maps:** Stride-32 convolutions can shrink a tiny joint heatmap
   to zero pixels. Always maintain high-resolution representations (via feature pyramid networks or HRNet) for keypoint
   and mask targets.
