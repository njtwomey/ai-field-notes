<Definition>
A **target encoding**, in the sense of this note, is the representation of the quantity a model is trained to predict:
the vector or number its output is compared with by the loss, and the map from the model's output back to a
prediction of the original quantity. It covers class indices and one-hot vectors, smoothed labels, ordinal,
hierarchical and multi-label codes, transformed numeric targets, and structured targets such as bounding boxes, masks
and keypoint heatmaps. It is distinct from **target encoding** of an input feature, which replaces a category by a
statistic of the target and is covered in <NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">categorical encoding</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Problem

The representation of the target decides what the model can say and how its errors are counted.

- Classes coded as the integers $0, \dots, K - 1$ and fitted by squared error impose an order and a spacing that the
  classes do not have.
- A one-hot target asks for a probability of exactly 1 on the true class, which a softmax reaches only with infinite
  logits.
- Labels with structure, such as an order, a hierarchy or several labels at once, lose that structure if coded as
  independent classes.
- A skewed numeric target, such as income or a medical cost, lets a few large values dominate a squared error, and a
  model fitted on its logarithm predicts a different quantity from the one asked for unless the back-transformation is
  done with care.

## Mechanism

### Class indices and one-hot targets

For $K$ classes, the target is either the index $y \in \set{0, \dots, K - 1}$ or the one-hot vector $\evec_y$. With
a softmax output $\pvec$ the cross-entropy is $-\sum_k (\evec_y)_k \log p_k = -\log p_y$ (see
<NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">log loss</NoteLink>), so the two forms give the same loss; the index form saves
memory. Binary targets are coded as $\set{0, 1}$ for logistic losses and as $\set{-1, +1}$ for margin losses such as the
hinge.

### Label smoothing

Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target by

$$
\qvec = (1 - \varepsilon)\,\evec_y + \frac{\varepsilon}{K}\ones,
$$

a mixture with the uniform distribution. The cross-entropy with $\qvec$ is $(1 - \varepsilon)$ times the usual loss plus
$\varepsilon$ times the cross-entropy between the uniform distribution and $\pvec$, which penalises predictions that
are too confident. Szegedy and colleagues introduced it for ImageNet classification with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and
$K = 1000$, and found an improvement of about 0.2 percentage points in both top-1 and top-5 error
<Cite id="szegedy2016" />.

Label smoothing also changes what the network learns. It makes predictions better
<NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">calibrated</NoteLink> and pulls the penultimate-layer representations of each
class into tight, equally spaced clusters. That erases information about which wrong classes are similar to the true
one, so a teacher trained with label smoothing makes a worse teacher for
<NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">knowledge distillation</NoteLink> <Cite id="muller2019" />.

### Ordinal targets

Ordered classes, such as ratings from 1 to 5 or disease stages, sit between classification and regression. One
encoding writes class $y$ as $K - 1$ binary targets, $\indicator[y > k]$ for $k = 1, \dots, K - 1$: class 3 of 5 becomes
$(1, 1, 0, 0)$. A binary classifier is trained for each threshold, and the probability of each class is recovered from
the differences of neighbouring threshold probabilities <Cite id="frank2001" />. The code fills up like a thermometer as
the class rises, so errors between neighbouring classes cost less than errors between distant ones. See
<NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="binary-decomposition-for-ordinal-regression">binary decomposition</NoteLink>.

### Hierarchical labels

When labels form a tree or a <Gloss name="dag">directed acyclic graph</Gloss>, such as animal, dog, terrier, a label can
be encoded by its leaf, by its path, or as a multi-hot vector over all its ancestors. Hierarchical classifiers are flat
(one classifier over the leaves, ignoring the hierarchy), local (one classifier per node, per parent or per level) or
global (one model over the whole hierarchy) <Cite id="silla2011" />. Encoding the path lets the model be partly right: a
terrier predicted as another dog is a smaller error than a terrier predicted as a car. Predictions must be kept
consistent, so that a node is predicted only when its parent is.

### Multi-label targets

When an example can carry several labels at once, such as the topics of an article, the target is a multi-hot vector
$\yvec \in \set{0, 1}^L$ over $L$ labels, the output-side version of a
<NoteLink to="multi-valued-field-encoding">multi-valued field</NoteLink>. The two basic reductions are **binary
relevance**, one independent binary classifier per label, and **label powerset**, one class per distinct set of labels
seen in training <Cite id="tsoumakas2007" />. Binary relevance ignores correlations between labels; label powerset keeps
them but has as many classes as label combinations, most of them rare. A network with $L$ sigmoid outputs and a
<Gloss name="bce">binary cross-entropy</Gloss> per label is binary relevance with a shared representation. Errors are
counted per label or per set; see <NoteLink to="hamming-jaccard-and-exact-match">Hamming, Jaccard and exact
match</NoteLink>.

### Transformed regression targets

A positive, right-skewed target is often modelled on a transformed scale, $z = g(y)$, with $g$ a logarithm,
$\log(1 + y)$ for counts with zeros, or a <NoteLink to="power-transforms">power transform</NoteLink>. The model fits
$z$, and a prediction must be mapped back to $y$.

The naive back-transformation $g^{-1}(\hat z)$ does not give the mean of $y$. If $\log y$ given $\xvec$ is Gaussian with
mean $\mu(\xvec)$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then

$$
\exp(\mu(\xvec)) = \operatorname{median}(y \mid \xvec), \qquad \expect[y \mid \xvec] = \exp\paren{\mu(\xvec) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}}.
$$

The naive prediction is the median, below the mean by the factor $e^{\sigma^2/2}$: 12% below for $\sigma = 0.5$ and 39%
below for $\sigma = 1$. This gap is the
<NoteLink to="retransformation-bias">retransformation bias</NoteLink>. Three corrections are common.

- **Parametric.** Multiply by $e^{\hat\sigma^2/2}$, which assumes Gaussian errors on the log scale.
- **Smearing.** Multiply by the mean of the exponentiated residuals, $\frac1n\sum_i e^{\hat\varepsilon_i}$, which
  needs no distributional assumption. This is Duan's smearing estimate <Cite id="duan1983" />.
- **Pushing the distribution forward.** If the model predicts a distribution for $z$, map the whole distribution through
  $g^{-1}$ and take its mean. For a Gaussian on $\log y$ this gives a <NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal
  distribution</NoteLink> on $y$.

Which prediction to report depends on the loss that judges it. The median minimises expected absolute error, so the
naive back-transformation is right for absolute error; the mean minimises expected squared error and is right for
totals, such as a budget summed over many patients.

Standardising a numeric target to zero mean and unit variance, common for neural networks, is an affine map, so its
back-transformation has no such bias.

### Structured targets

Some targets are not single numbers or classes, and their encoding is part of the method.

- **Bounding boxes.** A detector predicts a box relative to a reference box, an anchor of centre $(x_a, y_a)$, width
  $w_a$ and height $h_a$, as the offsets $((x - x_a)/w_a, (y - y_a)/h_a, \log(w/w_a), \log(h/h_a))$. The encoding
  makes the targets comparable across box sizes <Cite id="ren2015" />. See
  <NoteLink to="object-detection">object detection</NoteLink>.
- **Masks.** A segmentation target is a class per pixel, or one binary mask per object instance (see
  <NoteLink to="semantic-segmentation">semantic segmentation</NoteLink>).
- **Keypoints.** A keypoint is encoded as a heatmap: an image-sized map with a small Gaussian centred on the
  keypoint's location. The stacked hourglass network regresses one such map per joint with a squared error, using a
  Gaussian of standard deviation 1 pixel <Cite id="newell2016" />. The predicted location is the heatmap's maximum.
- **Spans.** A span of text, such as a named entity, is encoded as a tag per token: B for the first token of a span, I
  for the following tokens and O for tokens outside any span, so that span extraction becomes
  <NoteLink to="sequence-labelling">sequence labelling</NoteLink> <Cite id="ramshaw1995" />.

## Worked example

**Label smoothing.** With $K = 4$ classes and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the target for class 1 is
$(0.925, 0.025, 0.025, 0.025)$. The cross-entropy is smallest when the softmax output equals this target, so the
logit of the true class need only exceed each other logit by $\log(0.925/0.025) = \log 37 \approx 3.6$. With a one-hot
target the loss keeps falling as that gap grows, without limit.

**Retransformation.** In the figure, with noise of coefficient of variation 0.6 and a log target, the true mean
averages 3.04 over the test points. The naive back-transformation averages 2.61, 14% too low, because it estimates the
median. The pushed-forward mean averages 3.28: closer, and slightly high, because the model assumes log-normal noise
where the data are gamma-distributed.

## Cost

Index targets cost one integer per example; one-hot, smoothed, ordinal and multi-label codes cost a vector of length
$K$, $K - 1$ or $L$. Heatmap targets cost an image-sized map per keypoint and dominate memory in pose estimation.
Back-transformation corrections cost one pass over the training residuals.

## Trade-offs

- **Hard against smoothed labels.** Smoothing regularises and calibrates, at the cost of less informative output
  probabilities for distillation and ranking.
- **Flat against structured codes.** Ordinal, hierarchical and multi-label codes use the structure of the labels and
  give partial credit; flat classes are simpler and work with any classifier.
- **Transformed against direct targets.** A transformed target suits the skew of the data and keeps predictions in the
  valid range, but predicts a different summary of $y$ unless corrected. A model with a suitable likelihood, such as a
  gamma or Poisson <Gloss name="glm">generalised linear model</Gloss>, predicts the mean on the original scale directly.

## Pitfalls

- **Integer class codes as regression targets.** The model then assumes class 3 lies between classes 2 and 4.
- **Reporting the median as the mean.** Back-transforming a log-scale prediction without correction underestimates
  totals; summed over many predictions, the shortfall adds up.
- **Inconsistent hierarchical predictions.** A model that predicts "terrier" but not "dog" contradicts the hierarchy;
  enforce consistency when decoding.
- **Label powerset with rare combinations.** Combinations seen once or twice give classes that cannot be learned, and
  combinations never seen in training cannot be predicted at all.
- **Label smoothing before distillation.** A smoothed teacher passes on less of what it knows.
