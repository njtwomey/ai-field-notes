<Definition>
A **cyclical value** $v$ lives on a circle of **period** $P$: $v$ and $v + P$ mean the same thing. The hour of the day
has $P = 24$, the day of the week $P = 7$, the month $P = 12$ and a compass bearing $P = 360°$. Its **phase** is
$\theta = 2\pi v / P$. The **sine–cosine encoding** maps $v$ to the point $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ on the unit circle.
The **Fourier encoding** with $K$ harmonics adds $\sin k\theta$ and $\cos k\theta$ for $k = 1, \dots, K$. A **periodic
spline encoding** uses B-splines whose knots wrap around the circle.
</Definition>

## Problem

Stored as a number, a cyclical value has a false jump. Hour 23 and hour 0 are one hour apart but 23 units apart as
numbers. A <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear model</NoteLink> on the raw hour fits one slope across the day, so
it predicts a large step at midnight that is not in the data. A nearest-neighbour model or a kernel treats 23:00 and
00:00 as far apart.

<NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">One-hot encoding</NoteLink> removes the jump by treating each hour as a separate
level. It also removes the order: hour 7 and hour 8 become as unrelated as hour 7 and hour 19, and each level's effect
is estimated only from its own rows.

An encoding of a cyclical value should keep the order, close the circle, and let the model fit the shape of the effect
across the cycle with few parameters.

## Mechanism

### Sine–cosine encoding

Two coordinates are needed. With the sine alone, hours 3 and 9 both map to $\sin(\pi/4) \approx 0.71$; with the cosine
alone, hours 3 and 21 coincide. Together they fix the point on the circle and hence the hour.

The Euclidean distance between two encodings depends only on the circular difference $\Delta$ between the values:

$$
\norm{(\sin\theta_1, \cos\theta_1) - (\sin\theta_2, \cos\theta_2)} = 2\abs{\sin\paren{\frac{\pi\Delta}{P}}}.
$$

This is the chord between the two points. It is 0 for equal values, grows with the gap, and is largest, 2, for values
half a period apart. Distance-based methods then see the cycle correctly.

A linear model on the two coordinates fits

$$
f(v) = c + a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta = c + R\cos(\theta - \theta^\ast),
$$

with $R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and $\theta^\ast = \operatorname{atan2}(a, b)$. That is one smooth wave per period, with one
peak at phase $\theta^\ast$ and one trough exactly half a period later. Nothing else can be fitted.

### Several harmonics

Adding the harmonics $k = 2, \dots, K$ gives a truncated <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink> in the
phase:

$$
f(v) = c + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \paren{a_k \sin k\theta + b_k \cos k\theta}.
$$

With $K$ harmonics the effect can have up to $K$ peaks per cycle, at any positions, with $2K + 1$ parameters. Fourier
terms of this kind are the standard way to model long seasonal periods in regression-based forecasting, where a seasonal
dummy per period would need hundreds of parameters <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

For a value that takes only the $P$ integer levels $0, \dots, P - 1$, harmonics up to $K = P/2$ (for even $P$) give
exactly $P$ functions including the constant; the sine of the last harmonic is zero at every level and drops out. They
span every function of the $P$ levels, as the <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">discrete Fourier
transform</NoteLink> does, so the model is then as flexible as one-hot encoding. Fewer harmonics give a smoother, more
constrained effect between the two extremes.

### Periodic splines

A periodic spline encoding places B-spline knots around the circle and lets the splines at the end wrap onto the
start, so that every basis function is a smooth local bump on the circle. Unlike the global sine waves, each bump
affects only part of the cycle. A short peak, such as a morning rush hour, can then be fitted without disturbing the
rest of the day.

In scikit-learn's bike-sharing example, a <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> on hourly demand
had a <Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean absolute error</Gloss> of 0.142 with the raw hour, month and weekday, 0.125
with one sine–cosine pair for each, 0.099 with one-hot encodings and 0.097 with periodic splines (12 for the hour), all
as fractions of the maximum demand <Cite id="sklearncyclical" />. Demand has two peaks on working days, at the morning
and evening commutes, and a single sine–cosine pair can fit only one. The splines matched one-hot encoding with fewer
columns.

### Ordinal and one-hot alternatives

- **Ordinal value.** Fine for tree models, which split hours into ranges. A range that wraps around, such as 22:00 to
  02:00, needs two splits, which deeper trees find.
- **One-hot.** Most flexible: any shape, including sharp spikes, at the cost of $P$ columns and no sharing between
  neighbouring levels. It suits short periods with plenty of data per level, and fails for fine resolutions such as
  minute of the day ($P = 1440$).
- **Sine–cosine or Fourier.** Smooth, few columns, and correct distances; it underfits sharp or multi-peaked effects
  unless harmonics are added.
- **Periodic splines.** Smooth and local; the number of splines sets the trade-off between the two.

### Timestamps and durations

A timestamp is not cyclical as a whole. It is a position on a line, the elapsed time, carrying several cycles at once:
the time of day, the day of the week, the day of the year. A model gets a timestamp as several features: elapsed time
for trends, encoded as an ordinary number (see <NoteLink to="numeric-encoding">numeric encoding</NoteLink>), and one
phase per cycle, each encoded as above. Which calendar parts to derive, and in which time zone, is covered in
<NoteLink to="date-and-time-features">date and time features</NoteLink>.

Two cycles can interact: the hourly pattern on a Sunday differs from that on a Monday. One encoding per cycle cannot
express that. Encoding the hour of the week ($P = 168$) or adding products of the hour encoding with a working-day
indicator can. In the bike-sharing example, adding products of the hour splines with a working-day indicator lowered
the error from 0.097 to 0.078 <Cite id="sklearncyclical" />.

Durations, such as the time since the last purchase, are ordinary non-negative numbers, not cycles.

Time2Vec learns the encoding instead: one linear component $\omega_0\tau + \varphi_0$ and $k$ periodic components
$\sin(\omega_i\tau + \varphi_i)$, whose frequencies $\omega_i$ and phases $\varphi_i$ are trained with the rest of the
model <Cite id="kazemi2019" />. The model then finds the periods in the data rather than being told them.

### Calendar values with unequal periods

Some calendar cycles do not have a fixed length. A month has 28 to 31 days, a year 365 or 366, and a year has 52 or 53
ISO weeks. Dividing by the actual length of the current cycle keeps the phase in $[0, 1)$: the day of the month $d$ in
a month of $L$ days has phase $2\pi(d - 1)/L$, and the day of the year uses the length of that year. Moving holidays,
such as Easter, fall on different days of the year from year to year and are better given as indicator features.

### Angles and directions

Bearings, wind directions and joint angles are cyclical with $P = 360°$ or $2\pi$. When a direction comes with a
magnitude, such as wind speed, the products $s\sin\theta$ and $s\cos\theta$ are the velocity components. They are
continuous through calm conditions, where the direction is undefined, and are often the better pair of features.

## Worked example

Encode hours with $P = 24$.

- **23:00 and 01:00.** $\Delta = 2$ hours, so the distance is $2\sin(2\pi/24) = 2\sin 15° = 0.52$. The raw values are
  22 apart.
- **11:00 and 13:00.** Also $\Delta = 2$ and distance 0.52: the encoding treats both pairs alike, as it should.
- **00:00 and 12:00.** $\Delta = 12$, half the period, and the distance is 2, the largest possible.

Suppose demand peaks at 08:00 and again at 17:00. A linear model on one sine–cosine pair fits
$c + R\cos(2\pi(h - h^\ast)/24)$: one peak at $h^\ast$ and a trough at $h^\ast + 12$. With $h^\ast = 8$ the trough falls
at 20:00, three hours after the evening peak, so the evening peak cannot be fitted. Two harmonics allow two peaks, and
$K = 12$ harmonics, 24 functions with the constant once the vanishing $\sin 12\theta$ is dropped, fit any hourly profile
exactly.

## Cost

| Encoding                     | Columns per cyclical value |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Raw value                    | 1                          |
| Sine–cosine                  | 2                          |
| Fourier, $K$ harmonics       | $2K$                       |
| Periodic splines             | number of splines          |
| One-hot, $P$ levels          | $P$ (or $P - 1$)           |

None of the fixed encodings needs fitting beyond knowing the period. Time2Vec adds $2k$ trainable parameters per
time input.

## Trade-offs

- **Smoothness against sharpness.** Few harmonics or splines share strength between neighbouring values and smooth the
  effect; one-hot fits any spike but estimates each level alone.
- **Linear models against flexible ones.** The encoding matters most for linear and additive models. A gradient-boosted
  ensemble on the ordinal value already fits non-monotone effects, and in scikit-learn's example it beat every linear
  model on raw features <Cite id="sklearncyclical" />.
- **Fixed against learned periods.** A known period, such as a day, is best given explicitly. Learned frequencies help
  when the periods are unknown or not calendar-aligned.

## Pitfalls

- **Dividing by the largest value instead of the period.** Hours run from 0 to 23 but the period is 24. Using
  $\theta = 2\pi v/23$ puts 23:00 on top of 00:00.
- **One encoding for a multi-peaked effect.** A single sine–cosine pair forces one peak and one trough half a cycle
  apart. Check the shape of the effect, or add harmonics.
- **Splitting sine and cosine in a tree.** Each coordinate alone is ambiguous, so a tree needs splits on both to
  isolate a range of hours. For trees, the ordinal value is usually simpler.
- **Mixing time zones.** The phase of the hour depends on whether the timestamp is in UTC or local time. Behaviour
  follows local time; see <NoteLink to="date-and-time-features">date and time features</NoteLink>.
- **Encoding a duration as a cycle.** Elapsed time grows without bound and has no period. Encoding it with a period
  makes distant times look alike.
