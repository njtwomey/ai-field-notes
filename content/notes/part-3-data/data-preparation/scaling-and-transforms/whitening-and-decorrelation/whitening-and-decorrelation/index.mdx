<Definition>
**Whitening** maps a random vector $\xvec \in \reals^d$ with mean $\muvec$ and covariance $\Sigmamat$ to
$\zvec = \Wmat^\top(\xvec - \muvec)$, with the $d \times d$ matrix $\Wmat$ chosen so that $\cov(\zvec) = \eye$: the
whitened features have mean 0, variance 1 and no correlation. Many matrices do this. **PCA whitening** takes
$\Wmat = \Vmat\Lambdamat^{-1/2}$ from the <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>
$\Sigmamat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^\top$; **ZCA whitening** takes
$\Wmat = \Vmat\Lambdamat^{-1/2}\Vmat^\top = \Sigmamat^{-1/2}$, which gives the whitened vector closest to the original
<Cite id="kessy2018" />. In practice $\muvec$ and $\Sigmamat$ are the sample mean and covariance of the training data.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="feature-scaling">Feature scaling</NoteLink> gives every feature unit variance, but it treats the features
one at a time and leaves their correlations in place. Two standardised features with correlation $\rho$ still have
covariance matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $1 + \rho$ and $1 - \rho$. Its
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> $(1 + \rho)/(1 - \rho)$ is 19 for $\rho = 0.9$ and 199 for
$\rho = 0.99$. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on a least-squares loss over such features is
slow along the direction of small variance, distances are dominated by the direction of large variance, and two features
measuring nearly the same thing count twice.

Whitening removes the correlations as well as the scales. After whitening, the covariance is the identity, the
condition number is 1, and Euclidean distance between whitened vectors equals
<NoteLink to="mahalanobis-distance">Mahalanobis distance</NoteLink> between the original ones.

## Mechanism

### Centring and decorrelation

Subtracting the mean gives $\xvec - \muvec$ with covariance $\Sigmamat$. For any matrix $\Wmat$, the covariance of
$\zvec = \Wmat^\top(\xvec - \muvec)$ is $\Wmat^\top\Sigmamat\Wmat$. Whitening asks for

$$
\Wmat^\top \Sigmamat \Wmat = \eye.
$$

The covariance matrix is symmetric and <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss>, so by the
<NoteLink to="spectral-theorem">spectral theorem</NoteLink> it factors as $\Sigmamat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^\top$,
with orthonormal eigenvectors in the columns of $\Vmat$ and the variances along them, $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge
\lambda_d \ge 0$, on the diagonal of $\Lambdamat$. When all $\lambda_j > 0$, $\Wmat = \Vmat\Lambdamat^{-1/2}$
satisfies the condition:
$\Wmat^\top\Sigmamat\Wmat = \Lambdamat^{-1/2}\Vmat^\top\Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^\top\Vmat\Lambdamat^{-1/2} = \eye$.

The solution is not unique. If $\Wmat$ whitens, so does $\Wmat\Qmat$ for any orthogonal $\Qmat$, because
$\Qmat^\top\Wmat^\top\Sigmamat\Wmat\Qmat = \Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$. Conversely, any two whitening matrices differ by an
orthogonal factor. A rotation, or a reflection, of a whitened cloud is still white. The whitening methods differ only in
which rotation they pick <Cite id="kessy2018" />.

### PCA whitening

PCA whitening uses $\Wmat_{\text{PCA}} = \Vmat\Lambdamat^{-1/2}$. Coordinate $j$ of $\zvec$ is the projection of
$\xvec - \muvec$ onto the $j$-th principal axis, divided by the standard deviation $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$ along it: the
<NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">principal component</NoteLink> scores, rescaled to unit variance. Keeping
only the first $k$ columns of $\Vmat$ whitens and reduces the dimension in one step.

The output coordinates are the principal axes, not the original features. Coordinate 1 of $\zvec$ mixes all of the
original features, and the sign of each eigenvector is arbitrary, so a small change in the data can flip or reorder the
whitened coordinates.

### ZCA whitening

ZCA whitening, also called Mahalanobis whitening, rotates back to the original axes after rescaling:

$$
\Wmat_{\text{ZCA}} = \Vmat\Lambdamat^{-1/2}\Vmat^\top = \Sigmamat^{-1/2}.
$$

The matrix is symmetric, and it is the unique symmetric whitening matrix. Coordinate $j$ of $\zvec$ stays associated
with feature $j$. Among all whitening transforms, ZCA moves the data least: it minimises the expected squared distance
$\expect\norm{\zvec - (\xvec - \muvec)}^2$ between the whitened and the centred vectors <Cite id="kessy2018" />. A
variant, ZCA-cor, applies the same construction to the correlation matrix of the standardised features, and so stays
closest to the standardised data.

<Derivation title="ZCA whitening is the whitening closest to the input">
Write $\tilde\xvec = \xvec - \muvec$ and $\zvec = \Wmat^\top\tilde\xvec$. Then

$$
\expect\norm{\zvec - \tilde\xvec}^2 = \expect\norm{\zvec}^2 + \expect\norm{\tilde\xvec}^2 - 2\,\expect[\zvec^\top\tilde\xvec]
= d + \trace(\Sigmamat) - 2\trace(\Wmat^\top\Sigmamat).
$$

The first two terms do not depend on which whitening is used, because $\cov(\zvec) = \eye$. Every whitening matrix has
the form $\Wmat = \Sigmamat^{-1/2}\Qmat$ with $\Qmat$ orthogonal, so the last trace is
$\trace(\Qmat^\top\Sigmamat^{-1/2}\Sigmamat) = \trace(\Qmat^\top\Sigmamat^{1/2})$. For a positive semi-definite matrix
$\Amat$ and orthogonal $\Qmat$, $\trace(\Qmat^\top\Amat) \le \trace(\Amat)$, with equality at $\Qmat = \eye$: in the
eigenbasis of $\Amat$ the trace is $\sum_j a_j q_{jj}$ with $a_j \ge 0$ and $\abs{q_{jj}} \le 1$. The distance is
therefore smallest for $\Qmat = \eye$, that is $\Wmat = \Sigmamat^{-1/2}$.
</Derivation>

Other choices exist. **Cholesky whitening** uses the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink>
$\Sigmamat^{-1} = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$ and $\Wmat = \Lmat$, which is triangular: whitened coordinate $j$ depends only on
features $j$ to $d$, so the transform depends on the order of the features.

### Regularising small eigenvalues

Dividing by $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$ amplifies the directions of smallest variance most. In real data those directions often
hold measurement noise, rounding error or a near-exact linear dependence between features, and whitening scales them up
to the same size as the main structure. When $n \le d$ the sample covariance is singular and $\Lambdamat^{-1/2}$ does
not exist.

The usual remedy adds a constant $\epsilon > 0$ to every eigenvalue before the inverse square root,
$\Wmat = \Vmat(\Lambdamat + \epsilon\eye)^{-1/2}\Vmat^\top$. The whitened covariance is then
$\Lambdamat(\Lambdamat + \epsilon\eye)^{-1}$: directions with $\lambda_j \gg \epsilon$ are whitened as before, and
directions with $\lambda_j \ll \epsilon$ are left nearly unscaled. A <NoteLink to="shrinkage-covariance-estimation">
shrinkage estimate</NoteLink> of $\Sigmamat$ has a similar effect. In a comparison of single-layer feature learners on
image patches, ZCA whitening with a small $\epsilon$ raised classification accuracy for every learner tested
<Cite id="coates2011" />. Dropping the smallest directions altogether, by keeping $k < d$ axes in PCA whitening, is the
other remedy.

### Whitening in networks

LeCun and colleagues recommended inputs that are centred, scaled and decorrelated, because the Hessian of a linear
layer's squared loss is the input covariance, and an identity covariance makes gradient descent equally fast in every
direction <Cite id="lecun1998" />. Whitening is the full version of that advice; standardisation and
<NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> do only the scaling part. Decorrelated batch
normalisation applies ZCA whitening to mini-batches inside a network <Cite id="huang2018dbn" />.

For natural images, the pixel covariance has large eigenvalues for smooth, low-frequency patterns and small ones for
fine detail. ZCA whitening of image patches flattens that spectrum, emphasising edges, and its filters are localised
centre-surround patterns <Cite id="bell1997" />. ZCA whitening of CIFAR images was a standard preprocessing step for
convolutional networks and unsupervised feature learning <Cite id="krizhevsky2009" /> <Cite id="coates2011" />.
Whitening is also the first step of <NoteLink to="independent-component-analysis">independent component
analysis</NoteLink>, which then searches over the remaining rotation for statistically independent components
<Cite id="hyvarinen2000" />.

## Cost

Fitting costs $O(nd^2)$ for the sample covariance and $O(d^3)$ for its eigendecomposition. Applying the transform costs
$O(d^2)$ per row for full whitening and $O(dk)$ for PCA whitening to $k$ axes. For $d$ in the tens of thousands, as for
raw image pixels, the covariance and the whitening matrix no longer fit comfortably in memory, and whitening is applied
to small patches instead.

## Trade-offs

- **PCA or ZCA.** PCA whitening reduces dimension and orders the coordinates by variance, at the cost of mixing every
  feature into every coordinate. ZCA keeps each coordinate tied to one feature and changes the data least, so it suits
  images and any setting where the output should still look like the input.
- **Whitening or standardisation.** Standardisation costs $O(d)$ per row and keeps features interpretable. Whitening
  also removes correlations, which matters for methods that rely on Euclidean geometry or on isotropic noise, and for
  gradient descent on strongly correlated inputs.
- **Whitening or a model that handles covariance.** <NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">Linear discriminant
  analysis</NoteLink>, <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss> with learned length scales and Mahalanobis-distance
  methods build the covariance into the model, and do not need whitened inputs.

## Pitfalls

- **Fitting on all the data.** The mean and covariance are fitted statistics. Estimating them before splitting leaks
  information about the test rows into every feature; see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>.
- **Amplified noise.** Without regularisation, whitening scales the noisiest directions up to unit variance. A model
  trained on whitened data then gives noise the same weight as signal.
- **Too few rows for the dimension.** With $n$ rows the sample covariance has rank at most $n - 1$. When $d$ approaches
  $n$, its smallest eigenvalues are badly estimated, even when they are not zero.
- **Trees.** Tree ensembles split one feature at a time. Whitening mixes features, which removes axis-aligned structure
  that trees could have used, and gains them nothing.
- **Sparse inputs.** Centring and mixing features turn sparse vectors dense.
