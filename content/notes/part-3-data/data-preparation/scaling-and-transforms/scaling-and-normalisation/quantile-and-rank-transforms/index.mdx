<Definition>
A **quantile transform** is a non-linear, rank-based feature transformation that maps empirical continuous values to
their corresponding cumulative probability quantiles under the training distribution, followed by an optional mapping
through an inverse cumulative distribution function to enforce an exact target distribution such as $\mathcal{U}(0, 1)$
or $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.
</Definition>

Standard linear transformations such as standardisation ($z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$) or min-max scaling
($x_{\text{scaled}} = (x - x_{\min})/(x_{\max} - x_{\min})$) preserve the underlying shape of the feature distribution.
When a feature exhibits extreme skew, heavy multimodal clusters, or severe outliers, standardisation leaves the data
heavily non-normal, compressing the bulk of the observations into a narrow cluster near zero while outliers remain far
in the margin.

Quantile transformations resolve this by non-linearly warping the feature space based strictly on empirical ranks rather
than metric distances.

## Ranks and empirical quantiles

The transformation begins by evaluating the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) $\hat{F}_X(x)$ estimated
from $N$ sorted training instances $x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \dots \le x_{(N)}$:

$$
\hat{F}_X(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(x_i \le x)
$$

To avoid boundary degeneracies at $0$ and $1$, practical implementations (such as scikit-learn's `QuantileTransformer`)
fit $M$ quantile knots (by default $M = 1{,}000$) using linear interpolation across percentiles:

$$
u_i = \hat{F}_X(x_i) \in (0, 1)
$$

Because the mapping relies exclusively on monotonic ranks:
- Monotonicity is strictly preserved: $x_a < x_b \implies \hat{F}(x_a) \le \hat{F}(x_b)$.
- Bimodal or multimodal distributions are flattened into smooth targets.
- Extreme outliers are seamlessly folded into the boundary quantiles: a value of $x = 10^7$ simply receives
  $u \approx 0.999$, eliminating its leverage on downstream gradient updates.

## Uniform output

When configured with `output_distribution='uniform'`, the transformation stops directly at the empirical quantile $u$:

$$
y = \hat{F}_X(x) \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)
$$

By the probability integral transform, applying the true continuous cumulative distribution function $F_X(X)$ to random
variable $X$ yields a uniform distribution on $[0, 1]$. In sample data, the empirical quantile transform spreads
observations uniformly across the interval, guaranteeing equal density across all feature percentiles.

## Gaussian output

When configured with `output_distribution='normal'`, the empirical uniform quantiles are passed through the inverse
standard normal cumulative distribution function (also called the **probit function** $\Phi^{-1}$):

$$
y = \Phi^{-1}(\hat{F}_X(x)) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)
$$

where $\Phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^z e^{-t^2/2} dt$.

This mapping guarantees that the transformed feature follows an exact standard Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$
with zero mean, unit variance, and symmetric bell-curve density. This transformation is exceptionally potent for deep
neural networks and linear models that struggle with heavy power laws or asymmetric tails.

## Values outside the training range

A critical aspect of deploying quantile transformers is handling unseen validation and inference instances that fall
outside the minimum $x_{\min}$ or maximum $x_{\max}$ observed during training:

1. **Boundary clipping:** If an unseen test instance satisfies $x_{\text{test}} > x_{(N)}$, its empirical quantile
   evaluates to $u = 1.0$ (or $1 - \frac{1}{2N}$ with smoothing), mapping to the ceiling value.
2. **Linear extrapolation:** Some implementations extrapolate beyond the boundary knots by extending the slope of the
   extreme knot intervals:
   $$
   y(x) = y(x_{\max}) + \frac{y(x_{\max}) - y(x_{M-1})}{x_{\max} - x_{M-1}} (x - x_{\max})
   $$
   However, extrapolation risks producing outputs far outside the expected target distribution.

Because quantile transforms cannot model tail probabilities beyond the observed sample range without strong parametric
assumptions, severe covariate shifts can cause heavy accumulation of predictions at the boundary saturation caps.

## Metric distortion trade-off

While quantile transformations immunize models against outliers and skew, they achieve this by **discarding Euclidean
metric geometry**:

- **Tail compression:** Consider two rare observations at $x = 1{,}000$ and $x = 10{,}000$. Despite their massive
  physical separation ($\Delta x = 9{,}000$), both fall in the 99th percentile and may map to transformed values of
  $z = 2.32$ and $z = 2.57$ ($\Delta z = 0.25$).
- **Bulk expansion:** Conversely, observations in the dense central mode with minuscule physical differences
  ($\Delta x = 0.01$) may span the 40th to the 60th percentiles, resulting in a large transformed gap
  ($\Delta z = 0.51$).

For applications where the raw physical distance between points conveys fundamental domain semantics (e.g., physical
coordinates, temperature differentials, financial market spreads), quantile transformations can distort meaningful
physical laws.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Information loss on sparse categorical/discrete variables:** Applying a quantile transform to features with high
   repeated counts (e.g. discrete counts or sparse zeros) results in tied ranks. All zero instances receive the same
   quantile knot, creating unnatural discrete jumps in the output distribution.
2. **Knot overfitting on small sample sizes:** Fitting $M = 1{,}000$ quantiles on a dataset with only $N = 200$ rows
   causes the transformer to memorize sample noise. Constrain `n_quantiles` to be substantially smaller than $N$ (e.g.
   $N/5$ or $\le 50$).
3. **Severe saturation on heavy-tailed test data:** In non-stationary environments where test distributions exhibit
   larger extremes than training data, test points saturate at $\pm 3.5\sigma$, truncating predictive fidelity.
4. **Fitting globally before cross-validation:** Because quantile thresholds depend directly on sample rank percentiles,
   fitting outside CV folds leaks rank information across splits.
