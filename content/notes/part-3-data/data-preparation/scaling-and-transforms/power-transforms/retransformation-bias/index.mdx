<Definition>
**Retransformation bias** is the systematic divergence between the true conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ and the
naive inverse prediction $g^{-1}(\hat{z})$ obtained by back-transforming predictions from a model trained on a
transformed target $Z = g(Y)$, caused by Jensen's inequality for non-linear functions.
</Definition>

In econometric, financial, and actuarial modeling, highly skewed positive outcomes (such as healthcare expenditures,
insurance claims, wages, and property valuations) are routinely modeled by taking the natural logarithm:

$$
\ln(Y) = X \beta + \epsilon, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\epsilon \mid X] = 0
$$

An ordinary least squares model estimates the conditional mean of the transformed response:

$$
\widehat{\ln(Y)} = X \hat{\beta} = \mathbb{E}[\ln(Y) \mid X]
$$

When analysts convert predictions back to original dollar units by applying the exponential function
$\hat{y}_{\text{naive}} = \exp(X \hat{\beta})$, the resulting predictions systematically underestimate total costs and
group averages by substantial margins—often $20\%\text{--}50\%$ in high-variance regimes.

## Median versus mean after back-transformation

The mathematical origin of the bias is **Jensen's inequality**. For any strictly convex function $\phi$ (such as
$\phi(z) = \exp(z)$, where $\phi''(z) > 0$):

$$
\mathbb{E}[\exp(Z)] > \exp(\mathbb{E}[Z])
$$

Because the exponential function is strictly monotonic, quantiles are invariant under monotonic transformations:

$$
\operatorname{Median}(Y \mid X) = \exp(\operatorname{Median}(\ln Y \mid X))
$$

Under symmetric Gaussian errors $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, the mean and median on the log scale coincide:
$\mathbb{E}[\ln Y \mid X] = \operatorname{Median}(\ln Y \mid X) = X \beta$. Consequently, the naive exponentiated
prediction estimates the **conditional median**, not the conditional mean:

$$
\hat{y}_{\text{naive}} = \exp(X \hat{\beta}) = \widehat{\operatorname{Median}}(Y \mid X)
$$

In skewed financial and count distributions, the mean is substantially larger than the median because the heavy right
tail pulls the expectation upward. Summing naive predictions across an insurance portfolio $\sum \exp(X_i \hat{\beta})$
will drastically under-fund expected total claims.

## Parametric log-normal adjustments

If the error terms $\epsilon_i$ are normally distributed with constant homoscedastic variance
$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, then $Y \mid X$ follows a log-normal distribution
$\mathcal{LN}(X\beta, \sigma^2)$.

The moment-generating function of the normal distribution yields the analytical conditional mean:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = \mathbb{E}[\exp(X \beta + \epsilon) \mid X] = \exp(X \beta) \cdot \mathbb{E}[\exp(\epsilon)] = \exp\left(X \beta + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)
$$

The parametric correction factor is:

$$
\hat{y}_{\text{lognormal}} = \exp(X \hat{\beta}) \cdot \exp\left(\frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{2}\right)
$$

where $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N - p} \sum_{i=1}^N e_i^2$ is the mean squared error of the regression on the log
scale.

For example, with a typical residual standard error of $\sigma = 0.8$, the multiplier is
$\exp(0.8^2 / 2) = \exp(0.32) \approx 1.377$. The naive prediction underestimates the true expectation by nearly $28\%$.

## The smearing correction

In empirical machine learning applications, residuals $\epsilon$ on the log scale rarely conform to exact Gaussian
distributions. They frequently exhibit residual kurtosis, multi-modality, or asymmetric skew.

Naihua Duan <Cite id="duan1983" /> formulated the **smearing estimate**, a completely non-parametric correction that
requires no parametric distributional assumptions for $\epsilon$:

$$
\hat{s} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \exp(e_i)
$$

where $e_i = \ln(y_i) - X_i \hat{\beta}$ are the sample OLS residuals.

For any query instance $x_0$, Duan's smearing prediction is:

$$
\hat{y}_{\text{smear}}(x_0) = \exp(x_0 \hat{\beta}) \cdot \hat{s} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \exp(x_0 \hat{\beta} + e_i)
$$

Duan proved that $\hat{y}_{\text{smear}}$ is a strongly consistent estimator of $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x_0]$ provided
errors are independent and identically distributed.

## Predictive distributions through the inverse transform

Modern probabilistic modeling handles retransformation by generating predictions across the full predictive density:

1. **Quantile and interval forecasts:** Prediction intervals transform without bias because quantiles are invariant
   under monotonic transformations:
   $$
   [y_{\text{lower}}, y_{\text{upper}}] = [\exp(z_{\alpha/2}), \exp(z_{1-\alpha/2})]
   $$
2. **Monte Carlo draws:** For complex non-linear transforms (Box-Cox, Yeo-Johnson), draw samples
   $z^{(s)} \sim p(Z \mid X)$ from the predictive posterior, map each draw back through $y^{(s)} = g^{-1}(z^{(s)})$, and
   compute sample averages:
   $$
   \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid X] \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S g^{-1}(z^{(s)})
   $$

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Heteroscedastic smearing failure:** Duan's smearing factor $\hat{s}$ assumes that the residual distribution
   $\epsilon$ is identical across all values of $X$. If the variance on the log scale varies with $X$
   (heteroscedasticity $\sigma^2(X)$), a single scalar multiplier $\hat{s}$ over-corrects low-variance regions and
   under-corrects high-variance regions.
2. **Extreme residual leverage:** If the dataset contains a single severe positive outlier on the log scale ($e_i = 6$),
   $\exp(e_i) = \exp(6) \approx 403.4$. In small sample sizes, this single residual inflates $\hat{s}$ artificially.
3. **Evaluating models on the wrong metric:** Optimizing RMSE on $\ln(Y)$ minimizes relative percentage errors, whereas
   optimizing RMSE on $Y$ minimizes squared absolute errors. If the deployment objective is minimizing total dollar
   error, fitting directly on original dollars with Generalized Linear Models (e.g. Gamma or Tweedie regression with log
   link) avoids retransformation bias altogether.
