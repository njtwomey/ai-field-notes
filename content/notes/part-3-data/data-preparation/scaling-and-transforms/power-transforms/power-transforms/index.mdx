<Definition>
A **power transform** replaces each value $x$ of a feature or target by $x^\lambda$, suitably shifted and scaled, or
by $\log x$ when $\lambda = 0$. The **Box–Cox** family is defined for $x > 0$ and the **Yeo–Johnson** family for all
real $x$; in both, $\lambda$ is usually chosen by maximum likelihood so that the transformed values are as close to
normal as the family allows <Cite id="box1964" /> <Cite id="yeo2000" />. A **variance-stabilising transform** is a
fixed transform, such as the square root of counts, chosen so that the variance of the transformed value no longer
depends on its mean <Cite id="bartlett1947" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Many measured quantities are positive and right-skewed: incomes, prices, durations, concentrations, counts. Their
histograms have a long right tail, and their spread grows with their level. A household income of 200,000 is a
plausible value, and so is one of 20,000, and a difference of 10,000 means much more at the second than at the first.

Three kinds of method suffer from this.

- **Linear models with Gaussian noise** assume errors of constant variance. When the variance grows with the mean, least
  squares gives the noisy large values most of the influence and its <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
  errors</NoteLink> are wrong.
- **Methods built on distances and penalties**, such as <Gloss name="knn">k-nearest neighbours</Gloss>, k-means and
  <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>, let the few values in the tail dominate even after
  <NoteLink to="feature-scaling">feature scaling</NoteLink>, because standardisation is affine and keeps the tail as
  long as it was.
- **Tests and intervals derived under normality** lose their nominal coverage on strongly skewed data.

A transform that compresses large values more than small ones shortens the tail, brings the spread at different levels
closer together and often makes a relation between variables closer to linear.

## Mechanism

### Logarithms

The logarithm turns ratios into differences: $\log(2x) - \log x = \log 2$ at every $x$. A change from 20,000 to 40,000
and a change from 200,000 to 400,000 become steps of the same size. If $x$ is
<NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal</NoteLink>, $\log x$ is exactly normal. If the standard deviation
of $x$ is proportional to its mean, $\log x$ has approximately constant variance (derived below).

The logarithm needs $x > 0$. Data with zeros are often transformed as $\log(x + c)$ for a small constant $c$, most
commonly $c = 1$. The result depends on $c$, which is arbitrary, and for counts with many zeros no choice of $c$ makes
the result behave like a normal variable <Cite id="ohara2010" />.

### Box–Cox

Box and Cox embedded the logarithm in a one-parameter family <Cite id="box1964" />:

$$
x^{(\lambda)} =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^\lambda - 1}{\lambda}, & \lambda \ne 0, \\[1ex]
\log x, & \lambda = 0,
\end{cases}
\qquad x > 0.
$$

Subtracting 1 and dividing by $\lambda$ makes the family continuous in $\lambda$: as $\lambda \to 0$,
$(x^\lambda - 1)/\lambda \to \log x$. Each member is increasing in $x$, so the order of the values is kept. The
parameter sets how strongly large values are compressed:

| $\lambda$ | Transform, up to shift and scale | Effect on a right tail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | square | lengthens it |
| 1 | identity | none |
| 0.5 | square root | shortens it |
| 0 | logarithm | shortens it more |
| −1 | reciprocal | shortens it most |

Values of $\lambda$ below 1 pull in a right tail; values above 1 pull in a left tail.

### Yeo–Johnson

Box–Cox is undefined for $x \le 0$. Yeo and Johnson extended it to the whole real line by applying the Box–Cox form to
$x + 1$ for non-negative values and a mirrored form to negative values <Cite id="yeo2000" />:

$$
\psi(x, \lambda) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(x + 1)^\lambda - 1}{\lambda}, & x \ge 0,\ \lambda \ne 0, \\[1ex]
\log(x + 1), & x \ge 0,\ \lambda = 0, \\[1ex]
-\dfrac{(1 - x)^{2 - \lambda} - 1}{2 - \lambda}, & x < 0,\ \lambda \ne 2, \\[1ex]
-\log(1 - x), & x < 0,\ \lambda = 2.
\end{cases}
$$

The transform is increasing and smooth through $x = 0$, where it is 0 with slope 1. With $\lambda = 1$ it is the
identity. For non-negative data it is the Box–Cox transform of $x + 1$, which differs from the Box–Cox transform of $x$
for values near zero.

### Choosing λ by maximum likelihood

Box and Cox chose $\lambda$ by assuming that, for the right $\lambda$, the transformed values are independent draws
from a normal distribution with some mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ <Cite id="box1964" />. The likelihood of the
original values then includes the Jacobian of the transform, $\prod_i \abs{\mathrm{d}x_i^{(\lambda)}/\mathrm{d}x_i}
= \prod_i x_i^{\lambda - 1}$. Maximising over $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ for fixed $\lambda$ gives the sample mean and the
population variance $\hat\sigma^2(\lambda)$ of the transformed values, and leaves the **profile log-likelihood**

$$
\ell(\lambda) = -\frac{n}{2} \log \hat\sigma^2(\lambda) + (\lambda - 1) \sum_{i=1}^n \log x_i
$$

up to a constant. The first term rewards transforms that make the values tightly spread; the second corrects for the
fact that a transform can shrink every value and so shrink $\hat\sigma^2$ trivially. The estimate $\hat\lambda$
maximises $\ell$, a one-dimensional problem solved by a bracketing search such as Brent's method. Yeo–Johnson uses the
same profile with Jacobian term $(\lambda - 1) \sum_i \sgn(x_i) \log(1 + \abs{x_i})$.

In software, each column of a feature matrix gets its own $\hat\lambda$, and the transformed column is usually
standardised afterwards <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />. Like any fitted preprocessing step, $\hat\lambda$ is estimated
on the training data and reused unchanged on validation, test and production data.

Normality is only the criterion that picks $\lambda$. Most learning methods do not need normal inputs. What they gain
from the transform is a shorter tail, a more even spread and a more linear relation, which usually come with it.

### Variance-stabilising transforms

For some data the variance is a known function of the mean, $\var(X) = v(\mu)$: counts from a
<NoteLink to="poisson-distribution">Poisson distribution</NoteLink> have $v(\mu) = \mu$, and a proportion of $m$ trials
has $v(p) = p(1 - p)/m$. A transform $g$ can then be chosen to make the variance of $g(X)$ constant
<Cite id="bartlett1947" />; see <NoteLink to="variance-stabilising-transforms">variance-stabilising
transforms</NoteLink>.

A first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> of $g$ about $\mu$ gives
$g(X) \approx g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(X - \mu)$, so

$$
\var(g(X)) \approx g'(\mu)^2 \, v(\mu).
$$

Setting this to a constant $c^2$ gives $g'(\mu) = c / \sqrt{v(\mu)}$, and integrating:

| Mean–variance relation | $g$ | Name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $v(\mu) = \mu$ (Poisson) | $2\sqrt{x}$, variance about 1 | square root |
| $v(\mu) \propto \mu^2$ (constant coefficient of variation) | $\log x$ | logarithm |
| $v(p) = p(1 - p)/m$ (binomial proportion) | $\arcsin\sqrt{p}$, variance about $1/(4m)$ | arcsine |

The approximation is accurate when the spread of $X$ is small relative to its mean. For small Poisson means Anscombe
showed that $2\sqrt{x + 3/8}$ keeps the variance closer to 1 <Cite id="anscombe1948" />. Counts with extra variance,
such as <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink> counts with $v(\mu) = \mu + \mu^2/r$,
behave like Poisson counts at small means and like the constant-coefficient case at large ones, so neither the square
root nor the logarithm stabilises them everywhere.

<VarianceStabilising />

For proportions, the **logit** $\log(p/(1 - p))$ maps $(0, 1)$ onto the whole real line and is the link of
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>. The arcsine transform was long the default for
proportions; for binomial data a <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink> with a
logit link models the data directly and is usually more powerful <Cite id="warton2011" />. The same argument applies to
counts: a Poisson or negative binomial model avoids transforming them at all <Cite id="ohara2010" />.

## Back-transforming predictions

A model fitted to a transformed target $z = g(y)$ predicts on the transformed scale. Mapping a prediction back with
$g^{-1}$ does not give the mean of $y$, because $g^{-1}$ is non-linear. For the logarithm with normal errors of variance
$\sigma^2$ on the log scale, $\log y \mid \xvec \sim \Gauss(\mu(\xvec), \sigma^2)$, so

$$
\expect[y \mid \xvec] = e^{\mu(\xvec) + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad \operatorname{median}(y \mid \xvec) = e^{\mu(\xvec)}.
$$

The naive back-transform $e^{\hat\mu}$ estimates the median and underestimates the mean by the factor $e^{\sigma^2/2}$.
With $\sigma = 0.5$ the factor is $e^{0.125} \approx 1.13$: predictions of total cost would be 13% too low on average.
When the errors are not normal, Duan's **smearing estimate** multiplies $e^{\hat\mu}$ by the average of
$e^{r_i}$ over the training residuals $r_i$ on the log scale, without assuming a distribution <Cite id="duan1983" />.

Which quantity to report depends on the loss. Under squared error the mean is optimal; under absolute error the median
is, and the naive back-transform is then correct. Quantiles transform exactly: the $q$ quantile of $y$ is $g^{-1}$ of
the $q$ quantile of $z$, because $g$ is increasing.

## Cost

Applying a fitted transform costs one elementwise operation per value. Fitting $\lambda$ costs a one-dimensional
search, each step of which passes once over the column, so the fit is linear in the number of rows per feature.

## Trade-offs

- **Box–Cox or Yeo–Johnson.** Box–Cox needs strictly positive data and treats a variable with a natural zero, such as
  a price, on its own scale. Yeo–Johnson accepts zeros and negative values, at the price of an implicit shift by 1
  that makes its result depend on the units of $x$.
- **Fitted λ or fixed transform.** A fixed log or square root is interpretable and needs no fitting; a fitted
  $\lambda$ adapts to the data but changes when the data change, which changes what the feature means.
- **Transform or model.** A transform changes the scale on which the model's errors and effects are additive. For a
  target, a <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink> with a suitable link and
  variance function keeps predictions on the original scale and needs no back-transform.
- **Monotone transforms and trees.** Trees and tree ensembles split on thresholds, so any increasing transform of a
  feature leaves their fitted partitions unchanged. Power transforms of features matter for linear, distance-based and
  neural models, not for trees; a transform of the target matters for all of them.

## Pitfalls

- **Fitting λ on all the data.** $\hat\lambda$ is a statistic of the data like a mean. Estimated before splitting, it
  sees the test rows; see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>.
- **Shifting to make values positive.** Adding a constant before Box–Cox changes the result for the smallest values,
  and a constant chosen from the data is another fitted parameter.
- **Test values outside the training range.** A Box–Cox transform fitted on positive data fails on a zero or negative
  value at prediction time.
- **Reporting effects on the wrong scale.** A coefficient on $\log y$ is a relative effect: a change of $b$ in $\log y$
  multiplies $y$ by $e^b$. Read as an additive effect on $y$, it is wrong.
- **Expecting normality.** Maximum likelihood finds the $\lambda$ that makes the data as normal as the family allows.
  Bimodal or heavy-tailed data stay non-normal after any power transform.
