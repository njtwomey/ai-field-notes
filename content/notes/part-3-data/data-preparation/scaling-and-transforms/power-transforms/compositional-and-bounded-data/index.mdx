<Definition>
**Compositional data** consists of non-negative vector observations $x = [x_1, \dots, x_D]^\top$ whose components
represent relative parts of a whole, subject to a constant-sum constraint $\sum_{j=1}^D x_j = c$ (such as $1.0$ or
$100\%$), restricting the sample space to the $(D-1)$-dimensional **simplex** $\mathcal{S}^D$.
</Definition>

Standard multivariate statistical methods—such as ordinary least squares, principal component analysis, covariance
estimation, and clustering—implicitly assume that features reside in unconstrained Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^D$. When
applied directly to percentages, portfolio weights, chemical mixtures, microbiome fractions, or survey shares, standard
Euclidean methods produce spurious correlations, singular covariance matrices, and predictions that violate natural
physical bounds.

## Bounded values and the logit transform

When a univariate feature or target is strictly bounded within a fixed interval $y \in (a, b)$ (such as probabilities
$p \in (0, 1)$ or exam scores):

1. **The unit interval:** Normalize $y$ to $p = \frac{y - a}{b - a} \in (0, 1)$.
2. **The logit transformation:** Map the bounded probability onto the unbounded real line $(-\infty, \infty)$ using the
   log-odds:
   $$
   \operatorname{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right)
   $$

Fitting linear models or neural networks on the logit scale guarantees that back-transforming via the sigmoid function
$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ yields predictions strictly confined within the valid interval $(a, b)$, avoiding
nonsensical probabilities like $-0.08$ or $1.15$.

## Compositions on the simplex

The sample space for $D$-part compositional data is the unit simplex:

$$
\mathcal{S}^D = \left\{ x = [x_1, \dots, x_D]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^D : x_j > 0 \text{ for } j = 1, \dots, D, \text{ and } \sum_{j=1}^D x_j = 1 \right\}
$$

Because the elements must sum to unity, a $D$-part composition possesses only $D-1$ degrees of freedom. The components
are fundamentally relative: doubling all physical quantities leaves the composition unchanged.

### Karl Pearson's spurious correlation (1897)
In 1897, Karl Pearson proved that standard Pearson correlation between raw proportions is mathematically distorted.
Because the components sum to a constant:

$$
\operatorname{Cov}\left(x_i, \sum_{j=1}^D x_j\right) = \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, 1) = 0 \implies \sum_{j \ne i} \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j) = -\operatorname{Var}(x_i)
$$

Every component must have negative covariance with at least one other component, regardless of whether the underlying
generative processes are completely independent. Running standard correlation or linear regression on raw percentages
generates artifactual negative relationships that do not exist in reality.

## John Aitchison's log-ratio transforms

In 1982, John Aitchison <Cite id="aitchison1982" /> established that the natural geometry of the simplex is defined by
log-ratios between components, mapping $\mathcal{S}^D$ to Euclidean space:

### 1. Additive Log-Ratio (ALR)
Choose a reference component (typically the last component $x_D$):

$$
\operatorname{alr}(x) = \left[ \ln\left(\frac{x_1}{x_D}\right), \ln\left(\frac{x_2}{x_D}\right), \dots, \ln\left(\frac{x_{D-1}}{x_D}\right) \right]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{D-1}
$$

- **Dimension:** Perfectly reduces $D$ parts to $D-1$ unconstrained coordinates.
- **Limitation:** Asymmetric; depends on which part is selected as the arbitrary denominator.

### 2. Centered Log-Ratio (CLR)
Normalize each part by the geometric mean of all components $g(x) = \left(\prod_{j=1}^D x_j\right)^{1/D}$:

$$
\operatorname{clr}(x) = \left[ \ln\left(\frac{x_1}{g(x)}\right), \dots, \ln\left(\frac{x_D}{g(x)}\right) \right]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^D
$$

- **Symmetry:** Treats all parts symmetrically; ideal for clustering and PCA biplots.
- **Singularity:** The resulting coordinates sum to zero ($\sum_{j=1}^D \operatorname{clr}(x)_j = 0$), causing the
  covariance matrix to be singular (rank $D-1$).

### 3. Isometric Log-Ratio (ILR)
Constructs an orthonormal basis $\Psi \in \mathbb{R}^{(D-1) \times D}$ on the simplex (via sequential binary
partitioning):

$$
\operatorname{ilr}(x) = \Psi \operatorname{clr}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{D-1}
$$

The ILR is an isometric isomorphism that preserves Aitchison metric distances exactly:

$$
d_A(x, y) = \|\operatorname{ilr}(x) - \operatorname{ilr}(y)\|_2
$$

Standard multivariate algorithms (Ridge regression, Gaussian processes, PCA, $k$-NN) can be applied directly to ILR
coordinates without mathematical distortion. Predictions are mapped back to $\mathcal{S}^D$ via the inverse ILR (softmax
over $\Psi^\top z$).

## Handling zero values

Because the logarithm $\ln(0)$ is undefined, true zeros (e.g. absent chemical traces or zero spend in a budget category)
represent a fundamental hurdle for log-ratio transformations:

1. **Rounded zeros (below detection limit):** When zeros arise from instrument sensitivity limits, apply
   **multiplicative replacement**:
   $$
   x_j^* = \begin{cases} \delta & \text{if } x_j = 0 \\ x_j \left(1 - \frac{\sum_{k: x_k=0} \delta}{\sum_{k: x_k > 0} x_k}\right) & \text{if } x_j > 0 \end{cases}
   $$
   where $\delta \approx \frac{1}{2} \times \text{detection limit}$.
2. **Structural zeros (true absence):** If an entity inherently cannot contain a category (e.g. an electric vehicle
   having 0 fuel tank capacity), the zero is structural and must be modeled via a two-stage hurdle model rather than
   naive continuous replacement.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Running OLS directly on raw percentages:** Fitting unconstrained linear models directly on compositions yields
   predictions that sum to values other than 1 and produce negative component values.
2. **Ignoring Aitchison geometry in distance calculations:** Computing Euclidean distance on raw fractions $\|x - y\|_2$
   treats a difference between $0.001$ and $0.01$ (a 10-fold relative change) as negligible compared to a difference
   between $0.40$ and $0.45$, severely distorting rare-component sensitivity.
3. **Arbitrary pseudo-count addition:** Adding a constant $+1$ indiscriminately shifts relative ratios drastically when
   component values vary across multiple orders of magnitude.
