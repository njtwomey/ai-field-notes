<Definition>
A **variance-stabilising transform** (VST) is a monotonic non-linear mapping $g(Y)$ designed such that the conditional
variance $\operatorname{Var}(g(Y) \mid X)$ of the transformed response is approximately constant across all values of
the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$, satisfying the homoscedasticity assumption required by ordinary least
squares and standard Gaussian likelihood models.
</Definition>

Standard linear regression and ordinary least squares (OLS) assume homoscedastic residual errors:
$\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2$ for all observations. In count processes, biological sequencing, insurance
claim frequencies, or trial proportions, the variance is intrinsically coupled to the mean
$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = v(\mu)$. When variance expands with the mean, OLS estimates remain unbiased but suffer severe
loss of statistical efficiency, while standard confidence intervals and hypothesis tests become systematically
misleading.

## Deriving a stabilising transform via the Delta Method

The mathematical foundation of variance stabilization rests on the **Delta Method** (first-order Taylor series
approximation).

Let $Y$ be a random variable with mean $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mu$ and variance function $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = v(\mu)$. For
a smooth transformation $g(y)$, expanding $g(Y)$ around $\mu$:

$$
g(Y) \approx g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(Y - \mu)
$$

Taking the variance of both sides:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(g(Y)) \approx [g'(\mu)]^2 \operatorname{Var}(Y) = [g'(\mu)]^2 v(\mu)
$$

To make the transformed variance independent of $\mu$ and equal to a chosen constant $C^2$:

$$
[g'(\mu)]^2 v(\mu) = C^2 \implies g'(\mu) = \frac{C}{\sqrt{v(\mu)}}
$$

Integrating both sides yields the general formula for any variance-stabilising transform:

$$
g(\mu) = C \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{v(\mu)}} d\mu
$$

## Square root and Anscombe for count data

For Poisson-distributed count data, the variance equals the mean:

$$
v(\mu) = \mu
$$

Applying the derivation:

$$
g(\mu) = \int \mu^{-1/2} d\mu = 2 \sqrt{\mu}
$$

Taking $g(y) = \sqrt{y}$ stabilizes the variance to $\operatorname{Var}(\sqrt{Y}) \approx 1/4$.

### Anscombe's transform
While $g(y) = \sqrt{y}$ stabilizes variance asymptotically as $\mu \to \infty$, it degrades for small counts
($\mu < 10$). Francis Anscombe <Cite id="anscombe1948" /> introduced second-order corrections:

$$
g_{\text{Anscombe}}(y) = 2 \sqrt{y + \frac{3}{8}}
$$

For Poisson variables with mean $\mu \ge 2$, Anscombe's transform stabilizes the variance to almost exactly $1.0$ while
accelerating convergence to normality.

### Negative Binomial overdispersed counts
When counts exhibit overdispersion with variance $v(\mu) = \mu + \alpha \mu^2$, integrating yields the hyperbolic
arcsine:

$$
g(y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}} \operatorname{arsinh}\left(\sqrt{\alpha y}\right)
$$

This transform is widely employed in high-throughput RNA sequencing (e.g., DESeq2 and Seurat) to stabilize gene count
matrices prior to PCA and clustering.

## Arcsine transform for binomial proportions

For proportions $p = K / n$ arising from $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, the variance function is:

$$
v(p) = \frac{p(1 - p)}{n}
$$

Applying the integral formula:

$$
g(p) = \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(1 - p)}} dp = 2 \arcsin(\sqrt{p})
$$

After transformation:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(2 \arcsin\left(\sqrt{Y}\right)\right) \approx \frac{1}{n}
$$

The variance depends strictly on the fixed sample size $n$ and is completely independent of the underlying success
probability $p \in [0, 1]$.

## Relation to GLM link functions

Variance-stabilising transforms and Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) address the exact same underlying statistical
reality, but operate through fundamentally different mechanisms:

| Dimension | Variance-Stabilising Transform (VST) | Generalized Linear Model (GLM) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Object Transformed** | Transforms the observed data $y \mapsto g(y)$ | Transforms the expected value $\eta = g(\mu)$ |
| **Likelihood** | Gaussian OLS on transformed scale | True exponential family likelihood (Poisson, Binomial) |
| **Back-Transformation** | Suffer retransformation bias on $\hat{y}$ | Directly estimates $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ without bias |
| **Downstream Compatibility** | Compatible with standard linear/kernel/tree pipelines | Requires GLM / Quasi-Poisson solvers |

In modern predictive machine learning, when using standard tree ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM), fitting with a native
Poisson or Tweedie objective is generally preferred over applying a VST to the raw target. However, in preprocessing
pipelines where features feed into PCA, $k$-Means, or regularized linear models, VST remains an indispensable
transformation.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Retransformation bias on back-conversion:** Predicting $\hat{z}$ on the transformed scale and naively computing
   $\hat{y} = g^{-1}(\hat{z})$ (e.g. squaring the prediction) yields the median, not the conditional mean.
2. **Zero counts and negative values:** Naive square root or log transforms fail on negative numbers or zeros
   ($v(0) = 0$). Always use offset formulations like Anscombe ($y + 3/8$) or Freeman-Tukey ($\sqrt{y} + \sqrt{y+1}$).
3. **Unequal trial sizes in proportions:** The arcsine transform stabilizes variance to $1/n$. If observations represent
   proportions computed over wildly varying trial sizes $n_i$ (e.g. click-through rates calculated on 10 impressions vs
   10,000 impressions), the transformed variance remains heteroscedastic across observations.
