<Definition>
A **near-duplicate pair** consists of two distinct records $x_i$ and $x_j$ whose distance under a domain-specific metric
$d(x_i, x_j)$ falls below a threshold $\epsilon$, or whose similarity $s(x_i, x_j)$ exceeds a threshold
$\tau \in [0, 1]$. In text, similarity is commonly the Jaccard similarity of $k$-shingle sets or the cosine similarity
of term vectors. In images, it is the Hamming distance between perceptual hash bits.
</Definition>

Exact duplicates share an identical byte representation and collapse into buckets via standard cryptographic hashing
such as SHA-256. Near-duplicates differ by small mutations: minor typographical edits, reformatted templates, reordered
sentences, watermarks or slight image crops. Comparing every record pair directly requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$
comparisons, which becomes impossible on web-scale collections of millions or billions of documents. Scalable
near-duplicate detection maps high-dimensional records into compact sketches where similar items collide with high
probability.

## MinHash

MinHash maps an arbitrary set of tokens or $k$-shingles to an integer signature of fixed length $K$
<Cite id="broder1997" />. Let $S(A)$ and $S(B)$ denote the sets of $k$-shingles extracted from documents $A$ and $B$.
The resemblance between the two documents is the Jaccard index:

$$
J(A, B) = \frac{\abs{S(A) \cap S(B)}}{\abs{S(A) \cup S(B)}}
$$

Let $\pi$ be a random permutation of the universe of all possible shingles. The MinHash function $h_\pi(S)$ returns the
smallest shingle index under $\pi$:

$$
h_\pi(S) = \min_{s \in S} \pi(s)
$$

Because every shingle in the union $S(A) \cup S(B)$ is equally likely to achieve the minimum value under $\pi$, the
probability that the two minima agree equals the exact Jaccard similarity:

$$
P\bigl[h_\pi(S(A)) = h_\pi(S(B))\bigr] = J(A, B)
$$

Evaluating $K$ independent hash permutations produces a signature vector $\mathbf{h}(S) \in \mathbb{N}^K$. The fraction
of matching components provides an unbiased estimator $\hat{J}(A, B)$ of the true Jaccard similarity, with variance
bounded by $J(1 - J)/K$.

To avoid all-pairs comparison of signatures, <Gloss name="lsh">locality-sensitive hashing</Gloss> partitions each
$K$-element signature into $b$ bands of $r$ rows, where $K = b \times r$. Two documents become candidate duplicates if
their signatures match identically in at least one band. The probability that two documents with similarity $s$ become
candidates follows an S-curve:

$$
P\bigl[\text{candidate}\bigr] = 1 - \bigl(1 - s^r\bigr)^b
$$

Tuning the band parameters $b$ and $r$ sets the transition threshold $\tau \approx (1/b)^{1/r}$ where candidate
probability rises steeply from zero to one. Web-scale <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> corpora such
as RefinedWeb <Cite id="penedo2023" /> and FineWeb <Cite id="penedo2024" /> apply MinHash with $k=5$ word shingles to
remove duplicate pages across Common Crawl snapshots.

## SimHash

SimHash constructs a compact $B$-bit fingerprint for a document such that the Hamming distance between fingerprints is
proportional to the cosine angle between their weighted token vectors <Cite id="charikar2002" />.

Given a document represented by feature tokens $t_1, \dots, t_m$ with positive weights $w_1, \dots, w_m$:

1. Initialise an accumulator vector $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^B$ with zeros.
2. For each token $t_i$, compute a standard $B$-bit hash $h(t_i) \in \{0, 1\}^B$.
3. For each bit position $j \in \{1, \dots, B\}$, add $w_i$ to $v_j$ if bit $j$ of $h(t_i)$ is 1, and subtract $w_i$ if
   bit $j$ is 0.
4. Set the final fingerprint bit $f_j = 1$ if $v_j > 0$, and $f_j = 0$ otherwise.

For two documents with angle $\theta$ between their term vectors, the probability that any single fingerprint bit
matches is:

$$
P\bigl[f_j(A) = f_j(B)\bigr] = 1 - \frac{\theta}{\pi}
$$

The expected fraction of matching bits directly reflects cosine similarity. Two documents are classified as
near-duplicates if their Hamming distance falls below a small threshold, typically 3 bits for a 64-bit fingerprint.
Searching for matching fingerprints within Hamming radius $d$ runs in constant time by partitioning the 64 bits into
$d + 1$ blocks and indexing each block in a hash table.

## Perceptual hashes

In computer vision, cryptographic hashes fail because altering a single pixel flips roughly half the output bits.
Perceptual image hashes preserve visual similarity across lossy JPEG compression, resizing, brightness adjustments and
slight color shifts.

- **Average hash (aHash):** Resizes the image to an $8 \times 8$ grayscale thumbnail, computes the mean luminance, and
  assigns a 1 or 0 to each of the 64 pixels depending on whether it exceeds the mean.
- **Difference hash (dHash):** Compares adjacent horizontal pixels in an $8 \times 9$ thumbnail, setting each bit based
  on whether pixel $p(x, y) < p(x+1, y)$.
- **<Gloss name="dct" /> hash (pHash):** Applies a two-dimensional Discrete Cosine Transform to a $32 \times 32$ image,
  retains the low-frequency $8 \times 8$ coefficient block, and sets bits relative to the median AC coefficient.

Two images with perceptual hash Hamming distance below a small threshold (such as $\le 10$ out of 64 bits) represent
near-duplicate graphics, rescaled thumbnails or re-encoded uploads.

## Embedding neighbours

For complex semantics, multimodal data and graph nodes, near-duplicates are defined as nearest neighbours in a dense
learned embedding space. A frozen encoder maps each record $x$ to a unit vector $\mathbf{z} = f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

Candidate near-duplicates satisfy cosine similarity $\mathbf{z}_i^\top \mathbf{z}_j \ge \tau$. Approximate nearest
neighbour indexes, such as <Gloss name="hnsw">Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW)</Gloss> graphs or Inverted File
with <Gloss name="product-quantisation">Product Quantisation</Gloss> (IVF-PQ), retrieve matching candidates across
billions of records in sub-linear time.

## Duplicates across splits

Near-duplicates between training and evaluation splits represent a severe form of <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data
leakage</NoteLink>. When a benchmark contains questions, passages or images identical or nearly identical to items in
the training corpus:

1. **Inflated test metrics:** The model reproduces memorised training examples rather than generalising to unseen
   patterns <Cite id="lee2022dedup" />.
2. **False scaling laws:** Evaluated models appear to solve complex reasoning tasks while simply retrieving memorised
   substrings.
3. **Contamination audits:** Production pipelines run cross-split near-duplicate audits by building a MinHash or
   embedding index on the <NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">validation set</NoteLink> and probing the
   entire pretraining corpus.

## Pitfalls

- **Choice of shingle length $k$:** Setting $k$ too small (such as $k=1$ word) causes unrelated documents sharing common
  vocabulary to collide. Setting $k$ too large (such as $k=20$) allows a single inserted sentence or changed typo to
  break all spanning shingles.
- **Boilerplate dominance:** Common headers, navigation bars, cookie banners and legal disclaimers create high shingle
  overlap between otherwise unrelated web pages. Text cleaning and boilerplate removal must precede deduplication.
- **Transitivity failures:** Near-duplication is not transitive: document $A$ can be similar to $B$, and $B$ to $C$,
  while $A$ and $C$ fall below the threshold. Greedy connected-component clustering can merge distinct documents into
  oversized equivalence classes.
