<Definition>
**Value standardisation** (or canonicalisation) maps heterogeneous syntactic representations of equivalent domain
concepts to a single, consistent canonical representation:

$$
c : \mathcal{X} \longrightarrow \mathcal{X}_{\text{canonical}} \quad \text{such that } x \sim x' \implies c(x) = c(x'),
$$

where $\sim$ denotes an equivalence relation defined over physical units, date-time formats, numeric notations, or
categorical spellings.

Standardisation ensures that identical real-world entities evaluate as equal in relational joins, hash groupings,
distance metrics, and machine learning feature matrices.
</Definition>

## Physical units and dimensional consistency

In multi-source data aggregation (e.g. merging data from international subsidiaries, mobile apps, and IoT sensors),
measurements often arrive in disparate unit systems.

### The artificial multimodal mixture hazard
When physical units are mixed within a single column without explicit unit tags, the resulting distribution becomes an
artificial multimodal mixture:
- If an e-commerce platform records shipping weights where American vendors submit pounds (lbs) and European vendors
  submit kilograms (kg), an identical product ($1\text{ kg} \approx 2.205\text{ lbs}$) appears as two distinct modes
  separated by a factor of $2.205$.
- Distance metrics ($L_2$, cosine) and linear regression models assign distorted importance to observations recorded in
  smaller units (e.g., grams versus kilograms).

All quantitative features must be normalized to a standard system (typically SI units: kilograms, meters, seconds,
kelvin/celsius).

### Currency conversion and temporal point-in-time joins
Converting monetary values across currencies requires joining each transaction with the foreign exchange rate valid on
that exact transaction date:

$$
\text{Price}_{\text{USD}}(t) = \text{Price}_{\text{Local}}(t) \times \operatorname{FX}_{\text{Local}\to\text{USD}}(t).
$$

Using a static, modern exchange rate to standardize historical multi-year financial data introduces point-in-time data
leakage and distorts historical macroeconomic trends.

## Date, time, and number format unification

### Date representations
Dates written in free-form text exhibit regional ambiguities:
- `"04/05/2026"` represents **April 5, 2026** in the United States (`MM/DD/YYYY`), but **May 4, 2026** in the United
  Kingdom and Europe (`DD/MM/YYYY`).
- Ingestion pipelines must enforce canonical **ISO 8601** format:
  
  $$
  \text{YYYY-MM-DD} \quad \text{(e.g., 2026-04-05)}.
  $$

ISO 8601 is lexicographically sortable: sorting ISO strings alphabetically preserves correct chronological ordering
without requiring datetime parsing.

### Numeric formatting
Global numeric representations vary in punctuation:
- **Anglo-American notation:** Decimal point `.`, thousands comma `,` (e.g. `1,250,000.50`).
- **European notation:** Decimal comma `,`, thousands period or space `.` (e.g. `1.250.000,50` or `1 250 000,50`).
- **Currency symbols and percentage characters:** Ingestion scripts must strip currency markers (`$`, `€`, `£`, `¥`),
  parse negative accounting representations `(500.00)` to `-500.00`, and divide percentages `25.5%` by 100.

## Time zones and temporal alignment

A datetime without an explicit timezone is an incomplete, ambiguous measurement:
- Comparing a local timestamp from London (`2026-10-11 20:00:00`) directly with Tokyo (`2026-10-11 20:00:00`) introduces
  an undetected 8-hour offset error.
- All timestamps must be ingested and converted immediately to **Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)**:
  
  $$
  \text{YYYY-MM-DDTHH:MM:SSZ} \quad \text{or Unix epoch milliseconds}.
  $$

### Daylight Saving Time (DST) non-monotonicity
Local clocks jumping back by one hour during autumn transitions create non-monotonic timestamps: two distinct physical
events occurring 60 minutes apart receive the identical local clock timestamp (e.g., `01:30 AM`). Storing timestamps in
UTC preserves strict monotonicity, ensuring causal sequence order in event logs and recurrent neural models.

## Spelling variants and category harmonization

Categorical features collected from user input or federated databases exhibit combinatorial spelling and typographical
fragmentation.

### Normalisation pipeline
1. **Unicode normalization:** Decompose and compose accented characters to canonical form (Unicode NFC), converting
   full-width characters and ligature glyphs (`œ` $\to$ `oe`).
2. **Case folding and whitespace sanitization:** Lowercase strings (`s.lower()`), strip leading/trailing whitespace
   (`s.strip()`), and collapse internal multi-spaces (`re.sub(r'\s+', ' ', s)`).
3. **Punctuation and symbol stripping:** Removing hyphens and underscores (`"smart-phone"` $\to$ `"smart phone"`).
4. **Controlled taxonomy mapping:** Mapping known synonyms and acronyms to canonical master dictionary keys:
   
   $$
   \{\text{"NYC"}, \text{"New York City"}, \text{"New York, NY"}, \text{"Big Apple"}\} \longrightarrow \text{"New York"}.
   $$

5. **Fuzzy string clustering:** For high-cardinality long-tail inputs, string metric algorithms (such as Jaro-Winkler or
   Levenshtein distance) cluster candidate misspellings around high-frequency head categories.

## Pitfalls

- **Converting currency with modern exchange rates:** Applying today's exchange rates across historical transactions
  leaks future exchange movements into training data, generating spurious arbitrage signals.
- **Converting timestamps to naive datetimes:** Dropping timezone metadata while converting timestamps leaves downstream
  feature engineering pipelines unable to detect whether an event occurred during daytime or night in the user's local
  geographical region.
- **Over-aggressive fuzzy matching:** Clustering short categorical codes (such as airport IATA codes `JFK`, `LGA`,
  `EWR`) using edit distance will erroneously merge distinct facilities that differ by only one or two characters.
