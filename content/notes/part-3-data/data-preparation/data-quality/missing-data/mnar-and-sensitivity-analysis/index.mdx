<Definition>
When missingness is **missing not at random** (MNAR), the probability of missingness depends directly on the unobserved
values themselves:

$$
p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat, \psivec) = p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec) \neq p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \psivec).
$$

Because unobserved values $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$ are never seen, MNAR mechanisms are observationally unidentifiable from
observed data alone <Cite id="little2019" />. **Sensitivity analysis** formalizes untestable assumptions about
$\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$ via sensitivity parameters $\delta$, mapping how estimated quantities $\hat{Q}(\delta)$ and
decision boundaries shift as departures from MAR intensify.
</Definition>

## The two factorizations of joint incomplete data

To model data subject to missingness, statistical models express the joint distribution of data $\Ymat$ and missingness
indicators $\Rmat$ using one of two mathematically complementary factorizations:

1. **Selection models:** Decompose the joint distribution into the substantive data process and the conditional response
   mechanism:
   
   $$
   p(\Ymat, \Rmat \mid \thetavec, \psivec) = p(\Ymat \mid \thetavec)\, p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat, \psivec).
   $$

2. **Pattern-mixture models:** Decompose the joint distribution in reverse, modeling the marginal distribution of
   missingness patterns and the conditional data distribution within each pattern:
   
   $$
   p(\Ymat, \Rmat \mid \phivec, \alphavec) = p(\Rmat \mid \phivec)\, p(\Ymat \mid \Rmat, \alphavec).
   $$

## Selection models and Heckman's correction

Selection models directly represent the physical intuition of data collection: a complete target variable $Y$ exists in
nature, but an imperfect observation gate filters whether it is recorded.

### Heckman's two-step selection model
In econometrics, Heckman (1979) formulated selection bias as an omitted variable specification error
<Cite id="heckman1979" />. Let $Y_i$ denote a continuous outcome (such as wage) and $Z_i^*$ denote a latent propensity
to participate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y_i &= \xvec_i^\top \betavec + \epsilon_{1i}, \\
Z_i^* &= \wvec_i^\top \gammavec + \epsilon_{2i}, \qquad R_i = \mathbb{I}(Z_i^* > 0),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $Y_i$ is observed if and only if $R_i = 1$. The error terms follow a bivariate normal distribution:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \epsilon_{1i} \\ \epsilon_{2i} \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho \sigma_1 \\ \rho \sigma_1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right).
$$

When $\rho \neq 0$, the errors are correlated, meaning unobserved factors influencing participation also influence the
outcome (an MNAR mechanism). Conditioning on observed cases ($R_i = 1$) introduces a non-zero conditional expectation:

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y_i \mid \xvec_i, \wvec_i, R_i = 1] = \xvec_i^\top \betavec + \rho \sigma_1 \lambda(\wvec_i^\top \gammavec),
$$

where $\lambda(u) = \frac{\phi(u)}{\Phi(u)}$ is the **inverse Mills ratio** (the hazard rate of the standard normal
distribution).

Heckman's two-step procedure:
1. Estimate $\hat{\gammavec}$ via a probit regression of $R_i$ on $\wvec_i$, computing
   $\hat{\lambda}_i = \lambda(\wvec_i^\top \hat{\gammavec})$.
2. Fit OLS regression of $Y_i$ on $\xvec_i$ and the generated regressor $\hat{\lambda}_i$:
   
   $$
   Y_i = \xvec_i^\top \betavec + \beta_\lambda \hat{\lambda}_i + \nu_i.
   $$

The coefficient $\hat{\beta}_\lambda = \rho \sigma_1$ directly tests for non-ignorable selection
($H_0: \beta_\lambda = 0$).

### Fragility of selection models
Selection models rely heavily on bivariate normality. If the error distribution is misspecified (e.g., heavy tails or
skewness), the Heckman estimator becomes severely biased. Moreover, without an **exclusion restriction**—a valid
instrument variable that belongs in the selection equation $\wvec_i$ but is excluded from the outcome equation
$\xvec_i$—the inverse Mills ratio $\lambda(\wvec_i^\top \hat{\gammavec})$ is nearly linear across much of its support,
causing severe collinearity and exploding standard errors.

## Pattern-mixture models

Pattern-mixture models structure the problem transparently by acknowledging which parameters are identified by data and
which are inherently under-identified <Cite id="little2019" />.

For a binary indicator $R \in \{0, 1\}$:
- For respondents ($R = 1$), the distribution $p(Y \mid R = 1)$ is completely identified from observed data.
- For non-respondents ($R = 0$), the distribution $p(Y \mid R = 0)$ is **completely unidentified**.

To identify the overall marginal mean $\mathbb{E}[Y] = P(R=1)\mathbb{E}[Y \mid R=1] + P(R=0)\mathbb{E}[Y \mid R=0]$, the
analyst must supply an explicit identifying restriction. Common restrictions include:
- **Complete-case missing value (CCMV) restriction:** Assumes non-respondents share the conditional distribution of
  respondents ($p(Y \mid X, R=0) = p(Y \mid X, R=1)$). This reduces directly to an MAR assumption.
- **Neighboring case missing value (NCMV) restriction:** In longitudinal data, dropouts at time $t$ are assumed to match
  subjects who dropped out at adjacent time steps.

## Delta ($\delta$) adjustment and tipping-point analysis

Because fully parametric MNAR models are structurally fragile, modern statistical guidance—including recommendations
from the National Research Council (2010) for clinical trials—favors **tipping-point sensitivity analysis via
$\delta$-adjustment** <Cite id="vanbuuren2011" /> <Cite id="little2019" />.

### The $\delta$-adjustment formulation
1. Impute missing values under a credible baseline MAR model (such as MICE with predictive mean matching) to obtain
   baseline completed values $\tilde{Y}_{i, \text{mis}}^{\text{MAR}}$.
2. Apply a systematic shift parameter $\delta$ to all unobserved entries:
   
   $$
   \tilde{Y}_{i, \text{mis}}(\delta) = \tilde{Y}_{i, \text{mis}}^{\text{MAR}} + \delta.
   $$

   (For binary or log-transformed metrics, $\delta$ represents a log-odds shift or multiplicative ratio).
3. Re-estimate the substantive quantity of interest $\hat{\Delta}(\delta)$ (e.g. treatment effect) across a continuous
   sweep of $\delta \in [\delta_{\min}, \delta_{\max}]$.

In a two-arm randomized trial where proportion $p_{\text{mis}}$ of the treatment group drops out, the adjusted treatment
effect scales linearly with $\delta$:

$$
\hat{\Delta}(\delta) = \hat{\Delta}_{\text{MAR}} + p_{\text{mis}} \cdot \delta.
$$

### The tipping point ($\delta^*$)
The **tipping point** $\delta^*$ is the threshold value of $\delta$ at which the scientific or operational conclusion
overturns:
- **Inferential tipping point:** The value of $\delta$ where the $95\%$ confidence interval encompasses zero, causing
  the finding to lose statistical significance ($p \ge 0.05$).
- **Point-estimate tipping point:** The value of $\delta$ where the point estimate itself crosses zero
  ($\hat{\Delta}(\delta) = 0$).

Once $\delta^*$ is calculated, domain experts evaluate whether a discrepancy of magnitude $\delta^*$ is clinically or
economically plausible. For example:
- If a drug shows a treatment benefit of $+1.6$ units, and the tipping point requires dropouts to experience a deficit
  of $\delta^* = -4.5$ units (an implausibly catastrophic deterioration), the finding is highly robust against MNAR
  departures.
- If $\delta^* = -0.2$ units (a minor, routine fluctuation), the conclusion is fragile and cannot be trusted without
  empirical follow-up data.

## Reporting sensitivity in production and research

Rigorous reporting of incomplete data analysis follows a multi-tiered protocol:
1. **Primary analysis:** Pre-specified primary estimation conducted under an explicit, justifiable MAR model (e.g., MICE
   with all relevant covariates).
2. **Tipping-point curve:** A plot displaying the point estimate and confidence interval band across a spectrum of
   non-ignorable shifts $\delta$.
3. **Plausibility boundary:** Explicit domain justification comparing the tipping point $\delta^*$ to historical control
   variances and known clinical bounds.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming machine learning models "solve" MNAR:** Tree ensembles (such as XGBoost with native NaN splits) learn to
  route missingness based on observed correlations. If missingness is driven by unobserved values, tree algorithms
  reproduce the truncation bias of the training sample without correcting the underlying selection effect.
- **Applying Heckman corrections without exclusion restrictions:** Including the exact same feature vector $\xvec_i$ in
  both the probit selection equation and the outcome equation relies entirely on the non-linearity of the normal CDF
  tail. This produces severe multicollinearity, unstable parameter estimates, and massively inflated standard errors.
- **Treating sensitivity analysis as a formal hypothesis test:** Sensitivity analysis does not test whether data are
  MNAR; it assesses how robust a decision is to the assumption that data are MAR.
