<Definition>
Let $\Ymat \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ denote a complete dataset, partitioned into observed entries
$\Ymat_{\text{obs}}$ and unobserved entries $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$. Let $\Rmat \in \{0, 1\}^{N \times D}$ denote the
binary response indicator matrix where $R_{ij} = 1$ if $Y_{ij}$ is observed and $R_{ij} = 0$ if $Y_{ij}$ is missing. The
**missingness mechanism** is the conditional probability distribution of the missingness indicators given the complete
data and nuisance parameter vector $\psivec$:

$$
p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat, \psivec) = p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec).
$$

Rubin's taxonomy classifies this conditional distribution into three distinct regimes depending on whether missingness
depends on $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$, only on $\Ymat_{\text{obs}}$, or neither <Cite id="rubin1976" />.
</Definition>

## The three missingness regimes

Understanding which regime generates incomplete data dictates whether statistical estimators and machine learning models
are consistent, whether dropped rows induce selection bias, and whether imputation models require explicit modeling of
the missingness process.

### Missing completely at random (MCAR)

Data are **missing completely at random** if the probability of missingness is independent of both observed and
unobserved data values:

$$
p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec) = p(\Rmat \mid \psivec) \quad \text{for all } \Ymat, \psivec.
$$

Under MCAR, the missingness pattern acts as an exogenous Bernoulli trial across records or features. The observed cases
$\Ymat_{\text{obs}}$ represent a uniform, unweighted random subsample of the underlying population. Consequently:
- Sample means, variances, covariances, and ordinary least squares (OLS) regression coefficients computed on complete
  cases remain asymptotically unbiased.
- The penalty of missingness under MCAR is exclusively a loss of sample size and statistical power, not systematic
  estimation bias.

### Missing at random (MAR)

Data are **missing at random** if the probability of missingness depends on the observed data $\Ymat_{\text{obs}}$, but
is conditionally independent of the unobserved missing values $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$:

$$
p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec) = p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \psivec) \quad \text{for all } \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec.
$$

Despite its confusing historical name, MAR does not mean values drop out randomly. Instead, missingness is
systematically driven by observable variables. For example, if older survey respondents ($X$) are substantially less
likely to disclose their annual salary ($Y$), but conditional on age $X$, the non-response probability does not depend
on actual income $Y$, the data generation process is MAR.

Under MAR:
- Complete-case analysis (listwise deletion) is generally **biased**, because rows retained in the training set exhibit
  distorted covariate distributions.
- Marginal statistics (such as the unadjusted sample mean of $Y$) are shifted. However, conditional models $p(Y \mid X)$
  and properly specified multiple imputation or full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) methods remain consistent.

### Missing not at random (MNAR)

Data are **missing not at random** (also termed *non-ignorable missingness*) whenever the probability of missingness
depends directly on the unobserved values themselves, even after conditioning on all available observed variables:

$$
p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat, \psivec) \text{ depends on } \Ymat_{\text{mis}}.
$$

Prototypical examples include clinical trial patients dropping out because unmeasured adverse drug symptoms worsened, or
ultra-high-net-worth individuals systematically declining to report net worth. Under MNAR, the observed subset suffers
from survivorship and truncation biases that cannot be corrected by standard conditioning or standard imputation
algorithms. Consistent inference requires jointly specifying the outcome model and the missingness selection mechanism
<Cite id="little2019" />.

## Ignorability and likelihood factorisation

In parametric estimation, let the data-generating model be $p(\Ymat \mid \thetavec)$ parameterised by
$\thetavec \in \Omega_\theta$, while the missingness mechanism is parameterised by $\psivec \in \Omega_\psi$. The joint
marginal likelihood of the observed data $(\Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Rmat)$ integrates over all possible realizations of the
unobserved entries:

$$
p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Rmat \mid \thetavec, \psivec) = \int p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}} \mid \thetavec)\, p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec)\, d\Ymat_{\text{mis}}.
$$

When the mechanism is MAR,
$p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}}, \psivec) = p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \psivec)$. Because
this term does not involve $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$, it factors cleanly out of the integral:

$$
p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Rmat \mid \thetavec, \psivec) = p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \psivec) \int p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Ymat_{\text{mis}} \mid \thetavec)\, d\Ymat_{\text{mis}} = p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \psivec)\, p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}} \mid \thetavec).
$$

If $\thetavec$ and $\psivec$ are parameter-distinct (in the likelihood sense that their parameter space factorises as
$\Omega_\theta \times \Omega_\psi$, or in the Bayesian sense that their priors are independent), maximum likelihood and
Bayesian posterior inference for the target parameters $\thetavec$ depend solely on the observed data likelihood
$p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}} \mid \thetavec)$. Under these two conditions, the missingness mechanism is mathematically
**ignorable** <Cite id="rubin1976" />.

Under MNAR, the mechanism term cannot be factored outside the integral over $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$, making the missingness
non-ignorable and coupling $\thetavec$ directly to $\psivec$.

## Testing for MCAR

Because MCAR asserts that missingness is statistically independent of observed features, empirical tests can evaluate
the null hypothesis $H_0: \text{data are MCAR}$ against the alternative $H_1: \text{data are MAR}$.

### Little's multivariate MCAR test

Little (1988) introduced a global likelihood ratio test comparing observed subgroup means across distinct missingness
patterns <Cite id="little1988" />. Suppose the $N$ observations partition into $J$ distinct missingness patterns, where
pattern $j$ contains $n_j$ records and observes a subset of variables $D_j \subseteq \{1, \dots, D\}$. 

Assuming complete data follow a multivariate Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$, Little's test
statistic evaluates the squared Mahalanobis distances between each pattern's sample mean vector
$\bar{\yvec}_{\text{obs}, j}$ and the maximum likelihood population mean estimate restricted to pattern $j$'s observed
variables $\hat{\muvec}_{\text{obs}, j}$:

$$
d^2 = \sum_{j=1}^J n_j \left(\bar{\yvec}_{\text{obs}, j} - \hat{\muvec}_{\text{obs}, j}\right)^\top \hat{\Sigmamat}_{\text{obs}, j}^{-1} \left(\bar{\yvec}_{\text{obs}, j} - \hat{\muvec}_{\text{obs}, j}\right).
$$

Under the null hypothesis of multivariate normality and MCAR, $d^2$ asymptotically follows a central chi-squared
distribution:

$$
d^2 \sim \chi^2(\text{df}), \quad \text{df} = \sum_{j=1}^J D_j - D.
$$

Rejection of $H_0$ ($p < 0.05$) provides evidence that missingness depends on observed features, ruling out MCAR in
favour of MAR (or MNAR).

## The observational unidentifiability boundary

A fundamental theorem in incomplete data analysis states that **observed data alone cannot empirically distinguish MAR
from MNAR without untestable assumptions** <Cite id="little2019" />.

Consider testing whether unobserved values $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$ follow the same distribution as observed values within a
stratum of $\Ymat_{\text{obs}}$. By definition, $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}$ is never observed. For any non-ignorable MNAR joint
distribution $p(\Ymat, \Rmat)$, there exists an ignorable MAR model that produces the exact same observed data marginal
distribution $p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \Rmat)$.

Consequently:
- Little's test can reject MCAR in favor of MAR, but it cannot rule out MNAR.
- Verifying whether missingness is MAR versus MNAR requires domain knowledge, external auxiliary datasets (such as
  intensive follow-up audits on a random subset of dropouts), or formal sensitivity analysis.

## Practical guidance and machine learning pipelines

| Missingness Mechanism | Impact on Complete Cases (Listwise Deletion) | Valid Remediation Strategies |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **MCAR** | Loss of sample size and efficiency; parameter estimates remain unbiased. | Complete case deletion (if data volume is large); mean / median imputation; multiple imputation. |
| **MAR** | Substantial selection bias; covariate distributions are distorted. | Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE); Full-Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML); Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW). |
| **MNAR** | Severe structural bias and truncation; conditional relationships are distorted. | Heckman selection models; pattern-mixture models; tipping-point sensitivity analysis. |

In tree-based algorithms (such as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost), default handling routes missing entries down the
branch that maximizes split gain. If missingness carries non-ignorable signal (MNAR), this native routing can extract
predictive power from the missingness pattern itself. However, if the missingness mechanism shifts between training and
production environments, the model's split decisions can degrade abruptly.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing ignorability with listwise deletion safety:** Ignorability proves that one can maximize
  $p(\Ymat_{\text{obs}} \mid \thetavec)$ without modeling $p(\Rmat \mid \Ymat, \psivec)$. It does not imply that one can
  drop incomplete rows from the sample. Listwise deletion is only unbiased under MCAR or when missingness is strictly
  independent of the target variable conditional on the predictors.
- **Treating a non-significant Little's test as proof of MCAR:** Failure to reject $H_0$ in Little's test can result
  from low statistical power in moderate sample sizes, non-linear dependencies not captured by Gaussian covariance
  matrices, or the presence of MNAR mechanisms that do not distort first-order marginals.
- **Assuming imputers resolve MNAR:** Iterative imputation algorithms like MICE explicitly assume MAR. When applied to
  MNAR data, they impute values drawn from the observed conditional distribution, failing to correct the underlying
  truncation bias.
