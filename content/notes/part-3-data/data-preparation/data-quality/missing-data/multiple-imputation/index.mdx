<Definition>
**Multiple imputation** (MI) replaces each missing value with a vector of $M \ge 2$ stochastic draws from the posterior
predictive distribution of the missing values given the observed data $p(\Ymat_{\text{mis}} \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}})$
<Cite id="rubin1987" />.

The procedure operates in three distinct phases:
1. **Imputation:** Generate $M$ distinct completed datasets $\tilde{\Ymat}^{(1)}, \dots, \tilde{\Ymat}^{(M)}$.
2. **Analysis:** Fit the substantive scientific model independently to each completed dataset $m \in \{1, \dots, M\}$,
   obtaining point estimate $\hat{Q}^{(m)}$ and within-imputation variance $\hat{U}^{(m)}$.
3. **Pooling:** Combine the $M$ estimates using **Rubin's rules** to produce a pooled point estimate $\bar{Q}$ and a
   total variance $T$ that incorporates both sampling variance and missing-data uncertainty.
</Definition>

## The three phases of multiple imputation

Single imputation fails because it invents certainty, treating synthetic imputations as observed data and producing
deflated standard errors. Multiple imputation resolves this defect by decomposing total inferential uncertainty into two
orthogonal components:
- **Within-imputation variance ($\bar{U}$):** The ordinary sampling uncertainty arising from estimating a population
  parameter from a finite sample of size $N$.
- **Between-imputation variance ($B$):** The additional uncertainty stemming directly from the fact that certain entries
  were never observed and had to be estimated.

### Proper versus improper imputation
To produce valid, nominal-coverage confidence intervals, the imputation procedure must be **proper** in Rubin's sense
<Cite id="rubin1987" />. A proper imputer reflects both:
1. **Residual uncertainty:** Drawing unobserved values
   $\Ymat_{\text{mis}}^{*(m)} \sim p(\Ymat_{\text{mis}} \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}}, \thetavec^{*(m)})$.
2. **Parameter uncertainty:** Drawing the imputer's parameters
   $\thetavec^{*(m)} \sim p(\thetavec \mid \Ymat_{\text{obs}})$ from their Bayesian posterior distribution.

An *improper* imputer fixes $\thetavec$ at its maximum likelihood estimate $\hat{\thetavec}_{\text{MLE}}$ and only
injects residual noise. This fails to reflect uncertainty in the imputer's own coefficients, underestimating
between-imputation variance $B$ and yielding anti-conservative confidence intervals in small to moderate samples.

## Rubin's pooling rules

Let $Q$ denote the scalar quantity of interest (such as a regression slope $\beta$, odds ratio, or population mean).
Fitting the substantive model on dataset $m$ yields point estimate $\hat{Q}^{(m)}$ and estimated variance
$\hat{U}^{(m)} = \widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{Q}^{(m)})$.

### 1. Pooled point estimate
The overall point estimate is the arithmetic average across the $M$ analyses:

$$
\bar{Q} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{Q}^{(m)}.
$$

### 2. Within-imputation variance
The average sampling variance across the $M$ datasets is:

$$
\bar{U} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \hat{U}^{(m)}.
$$

### 3. Between-imputation variance
The sample variance among the $M$ point estimates quantifies the variability induced by the missing values:

$$
B = \frac{1}{M - 1} \sum_{m=1}^M \left(\hat{Q}^{(m)} - \bar{Q}\right)^2.
$$

### 4. Total pooled variance
Rubin's key theorem establishes that the total variance $T = \widehat{\text{Var}}(\bar{Q})$ is the sum of
within-imputation variance and between-imputation variance, augmented by a finite-sample simulation correction factor
$(1 + 1/M)$:

$$
T = \bar{U} + \left(1 + \frac{1}{M}\right) B.
$$

The factor $(1 + 1/M)$ represents the penalty for estimating the infinite-imputation conditional expectation using a
finite number $M$ of stochastic draws.

### Diagnostic metrics
- **Relative increase in variance:**
  
  $$
  r_M = \frac{(1 + 1/M) B}{\bar{U}}.
  $$

- **Fraction of Missing Information (FMI):** Quantifies the proportion of inferential uncertainty attributable to
  missing values:
  
  $$
  \lambda = \frac{(1 + 1/M)B + 2/(\nu + 3)}{T} \approx \frac{(1 + 1/M)B}{T}.
  $$

Hypothesis tests and confidence intervals use Student's $t$-distribution:

$$
\frac{\bar{Q} - Q}{\sqrt{T}} \sim t_\nu,
$$

where degrees of freedom $\nu$ are calculated using the Barnard-Rubin (1999) small-sample adjustment to prevent
overstating precision when sample size $N$ is moderate.

## Chained equations (MICE / FCS)

Specifying a joint multivariate distribution $p(Y_1, \dots, Y_D \mid \thetavec)$ is intractable when variables have
mixed types (continuous, binary, nominal, and skewed count variables). 

**Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations** (MICE), also known as *Fully Conditional Specification* (FCS),
circumvents joint distributions by defining a univariate conditional regression model for each incomplete feature given
all other features <Cite id="vanbuuren2011" />:

$$
p(Y_j \mid \Ymat_{-j}, \thetavec_j).
$$

MICE implements a Gibbs-like sampler:
1. Initialize missing values with random draws from observed marginals.
2. For cycle $t = 1, \dots, T_{\text{burn}}$:
   - For each feature $j \in \{1, \dots, D\}$:
     - Fit $p(Y_j \mid \Ymat_{-j}^{(t)}, \thetavec_j)$ on rows where $Y_j$ is observed.
     - Draw parameters $\thetavec_j^{*(t)}$ from their posterior distribution.
     - Draw replacement values for missing entries:
       $Y_{\text{mis}, j}^{*(t)} \sim p(Y_j \mid \Ymat_{-j}^{(t)}, \thetavec_j^{*(t)})$.
3. Retain the state after $T_{\text{burn}}$ iterations as imputation $\tilde{\Ymat}^{(m)}$.

### Predictive Mean Matching (PMM)
For continuous features that exhibit non-linearities, heteroskedasticity, or strict physical boundaries, MICE frequently
uses **Predictive Mean Matching** (PMM) <Cite id="vanbuuren2011" /> <Cite id="little2019" />:
1. Fit a Bayesian linear regression predicting $Y_j$ from $\Ymat_{-j}$, drawing parameters $\betavec^*$.
2. Calculate predicted values $\hat{y}_i = \xvec_i^\top \betavec^*$ for all observations.
3. For each missing recipient $i$, find the $k$ observed donor rows whose predictions $\hat{y}_d$ are closest to
   $\hat{y}_i$ in absolute distance.
4. Randomly draw one donor from the candidate pool and copy that donor's *actual observed value* $y_d^{\text{obs}}$ into
   the recipient's missing cell.

Because imputed values are copied directly from genuine observations, PMM guarantees that imputations obey natural range
bounds (e.g., non-negative values, integer counts, valid scales) and prevents out-of-bounds extrapolation.

## Congruence and congeniality

A foundational condition for multiple imputation validity is **congeniality** between the imputation model and the
substantive analysis model <Cite id="meng1994" />.

The imputation model is congenial with the analysis model if the imputation model makes assumptions that are at least as
rich and general as the analysis model. Crucial practical implications include:
- **Include the outcome variable:** When imputing predictors $\Xmat$ for a downstream supervised model predicting $Y$,
  the target $Y$ **must be included as a predictor in the imputation model**. Omitting $Y$ forces the imputer to assume
  conditional independence $X \perp Y \mid X_{\text{other}}$, severely attenuating downstream regression coefficients
  toward zero.
- **Include interaction and non-linear terms:** If the substantive analysis tests an interaction term $X_1 \cdot X_2$,
  the imputer must incorporate the interaction term directly. Standard additive linear imputers assume zero interaction,
  diluting the estimated interaction effect in the pooled analysis.

## How many imputations ($M$)?

Historically, Rubin (1987) demonstrated that $M = 3$ to $5$ imputations achieve high point-estimation efficiency,
because relative efficiency compared to an infinite number of imputations is:

$$
\text{RE} = \left(1 + \frac{\lambda}{M}\right)^{-1}.
$$

With $\lambda = 30\%$ missing information, $M = 5$ achieves $\text{RE} = (1 + 0.3/5)^{-1} \approx 94.3\%$.

However, modern statistical methodology demonstrates that while $M=5$ yields stable point estimates, it produces noisy
estimates of between-imputation variance $B$, leading to unstable $p$-values and loss of statistical power in hypothesis
tests. Modern practice adopts the rule of thumb:

$$
M \ge 100 \times \lambda,
$$

typically setting $M \in [20, 50]$ for robust inferential standard errors.

## Pitfalls

- **Averaging datasets before analysis:** Collapsing the $M$ completed matrices into a single averaged dataset
  $\bar{\Ymat} = \frac{1}{M}\sum \tilde{\Ymat}^{(m)}$ before model fitting destroys the benefits of multiple imputation.
  It eliminates between-imputation variance and collapses the procedure back into flawed single mean imputation.
- **Imputing derived features after creation:** Creating non-linear transformations (e.g. $X^2$ or $X_1 \cdot X_2$) by
  transforming imputed values can bias relationships. Variables should either be imputed using specialized
  polynomial/interaction formulas or through "just another variable" (JAV) formulations.
- **Applying MI to streaming online predictions:** Multiple imputation is an inferential methodology designed for
  parameter estimation, clinical trials, and offline research. Generating $M$ parallel stochastic completions for
  single-instance real-time scoring is computationally impractical and conceptually mismatched to point prediction.
