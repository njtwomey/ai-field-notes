<Definition>
In data quality and statistical learning:
- A **gross error** (or invalid datum) is an observation that fails to reflect physical ground truth due to sensor
  failure, pipeline corruption, typographical error, transmission noise, or unit confusion:

  $$
  x_i \notin \operatorname{supp}(X) \quad \text{or} \quad x_i = f_{\text{corrupt}}(x_i^*).
  $$

- An **outlier** is an observation that deviates markedly from the main distribution of the data. An outlier may be a
  gross error, or it may be a genuine, uncorrupted realization from a heavy-tailed, skewed, or multimodal
  data-generating process.
</Definition>

## Conceptual taxonomy: errors versus genuine extremes

A central failure mode in automated data cleaning is conflating anomalous geometry with data falsity:

| Category | Real-world Example | Underlying Mechanism | Appropriate Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Gross Error** | `Age = -1` or `Age = 999` | Sentinel encoding or keyboard error | Discard, set to null, or correct via business rules |
| **Gross Error** | Price in cents recorded as dollars | Unit conversion failure ($100\times$ shift) | Rescale by known constant factor |
| **Genuine Extreme** | Billionaire's net worth in wealth census | Pareto / power-law distributed wealth | Retain; use log transforms or robust estimators |
| **Genuine Extreme** | Network intrusion burst / credit fraud | Rare anomalous behavior of primary interest | Retain; serves as positive training signal |

Purging genuine extremes from training data creates artificial truncation: the model learns an overly narrow
distribution and fails catastrophically when encountering heavy-tailed events in production.

## Rule-based checks and domain validation

Gross errors are most reliably detected not by statistical estimators, but by deterministic domain validation rules:

1. **Domain boundary constraints:** Enforcing strict physical ranges (e.g., $0 \le \text{heart\_rate} \le 300$,
   $0 \le \text{humidity} \le 100\%$, $\text{start\_time} \le \text{end\_time}$).
2. **Syntactic and regex assertions:** Checking that identifiers, phone numbers, and codes match canonical grammars
   (e.g. ISO 8601 timestamps, UUID4 hex patterns).
3. **Relational referential integrity:** Verifying that entity foreign keys exist in master reference tables.

Observations that violate hard physical laws represent gross errors and must be prevented from entering numerical
estimators.

## Univariate statistical detection

When physical boundaries are unknown, univariate statistical dispersion metrics flag candidate anomalies.

### Tukey's boxplot fences
Tukey (1977) formulated non-parametric fences based on empirical quartiles $Q_1$ and $Q_3$, defining the interquartile
range $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$ <Cite id="tukey1977" />:

$$
\text{Inner fences} = [Q_1 - 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR},\; Q_3 + 1.5 \cdot \text{IQR}],
$$

$$
\text{Outer fences} = [Q_1 - 3.0 \cdot \text{IQR},\; Q_3 + 3.0 \cdot \text{IQR}].
$$

Values outside the inner fences are designated candidate outliers; values beyond the outer fences are classified as
extreme outliers.

### Median Absolute Deviation (MAD)
Standard sample mean $\bar{x}$ and sample standard deviation $s$ have a breakdown point of $\epsilon^* = 0$: a single
corrupted point $x \to \infty$ can inflate $\bar{x}$ and $s$ arbitrarily.

The **Median Absolute Deviation** provides a robust measure of scale with a $50\%$ breakdown point:

$$
\text{MAD} = \operatorname{median}\left(\left|x_i - \operatorname{median}(\xvec)\right|\right).
$$

For normally distributed data, the consistent scale estimator is $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}} = 1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}$.
The robust $z$-score is defined as:

$$
z_{\text{MAD}, i} = \frac{x_i - \operatorname{median}(\xvec)}{1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}}.
$$

Observations with $|z_{\text{MAD}, i}| > 3.0$ or $3.5$ are flagged as anomalous.

## Multivariate detection and leverage

In multivariate spaces $\mathbb{R}^D$, an observation can appear completely normal across every individual univariate
marginal while being an extreme multivariate outlier (e.g., a person with `Height = 195 cm` and `Weight = 40 kg`).

### Mahalanobis distance and robust covariance
The Mahalanobis distance accounts for feature covariance $\Sigmamat$:

$$
D_M(\xvec_i) = \sqrt{(\xvec_i - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec_i - \muvec)}.
$$

Because sample covariance $\hat{\Sigmamat}$ is vulnerable to extreme points, robust multivariate detection computes
distance using the **Minimum Covariance Determinant** (MCD) estimator (Rousseeuw 1984) <Cite id="rousseeuw1984" />,
which finds the subset of $h < N$ observations whose sample covariance matrix has the minimal determinant.

### Leverage versus residual outliers in regression
In linear models, outliers partition into two distinct geometrical types:
- **High leverage points:** Observations whose predictor values $\xvec_i$ are extreme in feature space, quantified by
  diagonal entries of the hat matrix $h_{ii} = \xvec_i^\top (\Xmat^\top \Xmat)^{-1} \xvec_i$.
- **High residual points:** Observations whose outcome $y_i$ deviates strongly from the model prediction $\hat{y}_i$.

A high leverage point with a large residual exerts massive influence on parameter estimation, capable of rotating the
entire regression hyperplane.

## Treatment strategies

Once detected, anomalies must be addressed through one of four operational strategies:

### 1. Correction
If the error source is known and invertible (e.g. sensor recording negative temperature due to inverted sign bit, or
prices recorded in pence instead of pounds), deterministic transformation restores physical ground truth.

### 2. Removal (Trimming)
Discarding rows that violate domain rules or exceed extreme thresholds.
- **When appropriate:** Unambiguous gross errors, corrupted sensor data, or malformed logs.
- **Hazard:** Removing genuine tail observations truncates the support of the training distribution, introducing
  survivorship bias and producing models that fail under extreme production conditions.

### 3. Winsorising and clipping
Winsorisation clamps extreme values to specified empirical percentiles (e.g., 1st and 99th percentiles) rather than
discarding the observation:

$$
\tilde{x}_i = \begin{cases} q_\alpha & \text{if } x_i < q_\alpha, \\ x_i & \text{if } q_\alpha \le x_i \le q_{1-\alpha}, \\ q_{1-\alpha} & \text{if } x_i > q_{1-\alpha}. \end{cases}
$$

Winsorising retains the full sample size $N$ and preserves relative monotonicity while bounding the influence of tail
values. The clipping boundaries $[q_\alpha, q_{1-\alpha}]$ must be computed strictly on the training partition and
frozen for inference.

### 4. Robust model estimation
Rather than altering the underlying data, the estimator itself is made resistant to outliers:
- **Huber M-estimation:** Replaces squared $L_2$ error with an absolute $L_1$ penalty for residuals exceeding threshold
  $\delta$ <Cite id="huber1964" />:
  
  $$
  \rho_\delta(e) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} e^2 & \text{if } |e| \le \delta, \\ \delta (|e| - \frac{1}{2} \delta) & \text{if } |e| > \delta. \end{cases}
  $$

- **Tree ensembles:** Decision trees split on rank orderings rather than absolute metric distances, making split
  locations naturally invariant to monotonic shifts of extreme values.

## Pitfalls

- **Data peeling (iterative masking):** Applying a 3-sigma rule iteratively without recalculating robust scale causes
  clean points to be progressively peeled away as the sample variance artificially collapses.
- **Deleting outliers to artificially boost model metrics:** Pruning extreme residuals from training and evaluation sets
  inflates $R^2$ and deflates test RMSE, creating an illusion of high predictive precision while blinding the model to
  real-world edge cases.
- **Applying symmetric clipping to asymmetric distributions:** Applying symmetric clipping (e.g. $\pm 3\sigma$) to
  log-normal or Pareto distributions (such as transaction amounts or income) truncates the genuine right tail while
  leaving the left tail untouched, introducing severe estimation bias.
