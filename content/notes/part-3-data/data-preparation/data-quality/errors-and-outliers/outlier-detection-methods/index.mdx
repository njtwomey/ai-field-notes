<Definition>
An **outlier detection method** maps an observation $\xvec \in \mathbb{R}^D$ to a continuous anomaly score
$s(\xvec) \in \mathbb{R}$, paired with a decision threshold $\tau \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$
\operatorname{status}(\xvec) = \begin{cases} \text{outlier} & \text{if } s(\xvec) \ge \tau, \\ \text{inlier} & \text{if } s(\xvec) < \tau. \end{cases}
$$

Detection algorithms evaluate observations across four distinct geometrical criteria: robust parametric scale, metric
distance, local neighbourhood density, and random tree isolation.
</Definition>

## Robust univariate z-scores and the Hampel filter

The classical sample $z$-score evaluates the standardized deviation from the arithmetic mean:

$$
z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}, \quad \text{where } \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum x_i, \quad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}.
$$

The classical $z$-score possesses a breakdown point of zero ($\epsilon^* = 0$). As an extreme outlier $x_k \to \infty$,
both the mean $\bar{x}$ and sample standard deviation $s$ inflate proportionally, imposing the strict mathematical bound
$|z_i| \le \frac{N - 1}{\sqrt{N}}$ for all $i$. Consequently, a cluster of massive outliers inflates $s$ so drastically
that their own $z$-scores collapse below standard $3\sigma$ thresholds.

### Robust z-scores via MAD
Replacing non-robust location and scale estimators with the median and Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) yields a scale
estimator with a $50\%$ breakdown point:

$$
z_{\text{MAD}, i} = \frac{x_i - \operatorname{median}(\xvec)}{1.4826 \cdot \operatorname{MAD}(\xvec)}.
$$

For Gaussian data, the scaling factor $1.4826 = 1 / \Phi^{-1}(0.75)$ ensures that $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{MAD}}$
asymptotically estimates standard deviation. Observations satisfying $|z_{\text{MAD}, i}| \ge 3.0$ or $3.5$ are flagged.

### The Hampel filter
In sequential or time-series data, the **Hampel filter** computes moving-window medians and MAD estimates over a
symmetric window $W_t = [t - k, t + k]$:

$$
\tilde{x}_t = \operatorname{median}(x_{t-k}, \dots, x_{t+k}), \qquad S_t = 1.4826 \cdot \operatorname{median}\left(|x_{t+j} - \tilde{x}_t|\right)_{j=-k}^k.
$$

If $|x_t - \tilde{x}_t| > 3 S_t$, the central point $x_t$ is flagged and replaced by the local median $\tilde{x}_t$.

## Distance-based detection and robust covariance

### $k$-nearest neighbour distance
In metric spaces, distance-based detectors measure isolation without positing a global parametric family. For each
observation $\xvec_i$, the anomaly score is defined as the distance to its $k$-th nearest neighbour $d_k(\xvec_i)$ or
the average distance across its $k$ nearest neighbours:

$$
s_{k\text{-NN}}(\xvec_i) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^k d(\xvec_i, \xvec_{(j)}).
$$

$k$-NN distance naturally detects isolated points regardless of manifold shape. However, in datasets featuring clusters
with differing internal densities, a point in a sparse cluster receives a high $k$-NN distance even when it is a
perfectly legitimate inlier for its local region.

### Minimum Covariance Determinant (FastMCD)
The Mahalanobis distance $D_M(\xvec) = \sqrt{(\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec)}$ accounts for linear
correlations among features. However, when $\muvec$ and $\Sigmamat$ are estimated using sample means and sample
covariances, outliers distort the covariance ellipse.

Rousseeuw & Van Driessen (1999) resolved this vulnerability via the **FastMCD** algorithm <Cite id="rousseeuw1999" />
<Cite id="rousseeuw1984" />:
1. Search for the subset of $h$ observations (where $\lfloor (N + D + 1) / 2 \rfloor \le h < N$) whose sample covariance
   matrix $\Sigmamat_h$ has the minimal determinant:
   
   $$
   H_{\text{MCD}} = \arg\min_{|H| = h} \det\left(\Sigmamat_H\right).
   $$
2. Compute robust center $\hat{\muvec}_{\text{MCD}}$ and scatter matrix $\hat{\Sigmamat}_{\text{MCD}}$ strictly from
   this optimal $h$-subset.
3. Evaluate robust Mahalanobis distances:
   
   $$
   D_{\text{MCD}}(\xvec_i) = \sqrt{\left(\xvec_i - \hat{\muvec}_{\text{MCD}}\right)^\top \hat{\Sigmamat}_{\text{MCD}}^{-1} \left(\xvec_i - \hat{\muvec}_{\text{MCD}}\right)}.
   $$

FastMCD achieves a breakdown point of up to $50\%$, preventing up to half the dataset from corrupting the estimated
center and covariance orientation.

## Density and isolation scores

### Local Outlier Factor (LOF)
Breunig et al. (2000) introduced LOF to overcome the limitation of distance-based methods in multi-density datasets
<Cite id="breunig2000" />:
1. **$k$-distance and reachability distance:** For points $p$ and $o$, the reachability distance is:
   
   $$
   \operatorname{reach-dist}_k(p, o) = \max\left(d_k(o), d(p, o)\right).
   $$
2. **Local reachability density (lrd):** The inverse of the average reachability distance from its $k$-nearest
   neighbours:
   
   $$
   \operatorname{lrd}_k(p) = \left(\frac{1}{k} \sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \operatorname{reach-dist}_k(p, o)\right)^{-1}.
   $$
3. **LOF score:** The average ratio of the local reachability densities of $p$'s neighbours to that of $p$:
   
   $$
   \operatorname{LOF}_k(p) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \frac{\operatorname{lrd}_k(o)}{\operatorname{lrd}_k(p)}.
   $$

If $p$ lies inside a cluster (dense or sparse), $\operatorname{lrd}(p) \approx \operatorname{lrd}(o)$ and
$\operatorname{LOF}(p) \approx 1$. If $p$ is an outlier located outside a dense cluster,
$\operatorname{lrd}(p) \ll \operatorname{lrd}(o)$ and $\operatorname{LOF}(p) \gg 1$.

### Isolation Forest (iForest)
Liu, Ting & Zhou (2008) observed that anomalies are "few and different", making them fundamentally easier to isolate
than normal observations <Cite id="liu2008" />:
1. Build an ensemble of $T$ completely randomized binary trees (Isolation Trees). At each step, randomly select an axis
   $j$ and split value $s \sim \mathcal{U}(\min X_j, \max X_j)$.
2. Compute the average tree path length $h(\xvec)$ required to isolate observation $\xvec$ into an external leaf.
3. The anomaly score is normalized by the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree of size
   $n$, $c(n) = 2 \ln(n - 1) + 0.5772 - 2(n - 1)/n$:
   
   $$
   s(\xvec, n) = 2^{-\frac{\mathbb{E}[h(\xvec)]}{c(n)}}.
   $$

Outliers separate near the root ($h \ll c(n)$, $s \to 1$); inliers require deep partitioning ($h \gg c(n)$, $s \to 0$).

## Masking and swamping

When multiple anomalies contaminate a dataset, classical estimators suffer from two severe interaction pathologies:

### Masking (False Negatives)
**Masking** occurs when the presence of multiple outliers hides their own anomalous nature. For example, if a cluster of
8 extreme observations shares a similar off-manifold location, they pull the sample mean $\bar{\xvec}$ toward themselves
and inflate the covariance matrix $\hat{\Sigmamat}$. Under standard Mahalanobis distance or classical $z$-scores, their
standardized distances shrink below the detection threshold, rendering them invisible.

### Swamping (False Positives)
**Swamping** occurs when outliers distort the estimated location and scale so severely that uncorrupted, clean inliers
appear anomalous. When the sample mean shifts away from the true population center and the covariance axes rotate,
inlying points in low-density peripheral clusters receive spuriously large distance scores, triggering false alarms.

High-breakdown estimators (such as FastMCD, LOF, and Isolation Forest) resist both masking and swamping by preventing
corrupted points from dominating the reference baseline.

## Outliers as errors versus signal

The strategic purpose of outlier detection dictates how flagged points must be handled:

- **Data cleaning objective:** When the goal is fitting a supervised regression model, flagged outliers are inspected to
  remove gross measurement errors and prevent leverage distortions.
- **Discovery objective:** In cybersecurity, financial fraud detection, and rare disease diagnostics, the outliers **are
  the target phenomenon**. Filtering them out destroys the primary value of the dataset.

In practice, high-performing production pipelines treat unsupervised anomaly scores as continuous risk features rather
than performing irreversible thresholding and dropping rows.

## Pitfalls

- **The curse of dimensionality in distance methods:** In high-dimensional spaces ($D > 50$), distance concentration
  causes the ratio $(d_{\max} - d_{\min})/d_{\min} \to 0$. All pairwise Euclidean distances become equidistant,
  rendering raw $k$-NN and LOF scores uninformative unless preceded by dimensionality reduction.
- **Assuming uniform cluster density:** Applying a global distance threshold across a dataset with multi-density
  clusters swamps all points in sparse clusters while missing subtle anomalies buried inside dense clusters. Local
  density estimators (LOF) must be preferred.
- **Using non-robust detectors to clean training data:** Using empirical 3-sigma rules or standard Mahalanobis distance
  to filter outliers creates severe masking, leaving clustered anomalies untouched while discarding clean tail points.
