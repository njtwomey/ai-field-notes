<Definition>
**Imbalanced regression** occurs when the continuous target variable $y \in \mathbb{R}$ exhibits a non-uniform
probability density such that values in rare regions (often the extreme tails or isolated modes) carry disproportionate
operational significance but suffer severe under-prediction under standard mean-squared-error objectives.
</Definition>

In standard supervised regression, empirical risk minimization minimizes the unweighted squared loss:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - f_\theta(x_i))^2
$$

Because the overwhelming majority of training instances occupy the high-density bulk of the target distribution,
minimizing global mean squared error forces the model to hedge toward the global conditional expectation
$\mathbb{E}[y \mid x]$. Consequently, predictions for extreme, rare target values are pulled sharply toward the central
mass, resulting in catastrophic underestimation of extreme events like severe insurance claims, catastrophic sensor
spikes, extreme climate events, or rare financial losses.

## Rare target ranges and relevance functions

Unlike classification where classes are discrete and cardinality counts directly quantify imbalance, continuous targets
require defining what constitutes a "rare" or "critical" value. Luis Torgo and Rita Ribeiro formalised this via a
**continuous relevance function** $\phi(y): \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$ <Cite id="torgo2013" />.

The relevance function maps the continuous target space into an importance metric:
- $\phi(y) = 1$ denotes maximum domain interest (typically extreme tails or low-frequency boundary regions).
- $\phi(y) = 0$ denotes common, standard baseline values where standard prediction errors are acceptable.

$\phi(y)$ is typically constructed from domain-specified control points using shape-preserving piecewise cubic Hermite
interpolation (PCHIP) or inverted density estimates. The rare target subspace $\mathcal{D}_R$ is defined by a relevance
cutoff $t_R \in (0, 1]$:

$$
\mathcal{D}_R = \{ y \in \mathcal{Y} : \phi(y) \ge t_R \}
$$

## Density-based sample weights

When retaining the original training dataset without row alteration, sample weighting adjusts the gradient contribution
of each instance inversely proportional to the local density of its target value $\hat{f}(y)$.

Target density $\hat{f}(y)$ is estimated via univariate kernel density estimation (KDE) with a Gaussian kernel:

$$
\hat{f}(y) = \frac{1}{N h} \sum_{i=1}^N K\left(\frac{y - y_i}{h}\right)
$$

where $h$ is the bandwidth chosen via Silverman's rule or cross-validation. The sample weight $w_i$ assigned to training
example $(x_i, y_i)$ combines relevance and inverse density:

$$
w_i = \left(\frac{1}{\hat{f}(y_i) + \epsilon}\right)^\alpha \cdot \phi(y_i)
$$

where $\alpha \in [0.2, 1.0]$ tempers extreme weight dispersion and $\epsilon > 0$ prevents numerical divergence in
zero-density regions. Training under weighted least squares or gradient boosting with weights $w_i$ amplifies the
gradient penalty for errors committed on rare target extremes:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_w(\theta) = -2 \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (y_i - f_\theta(x_i)) \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x_i)
$$

This prevents the thousands of central-bulk errors from drowning out the rare-tail gradients.

## Resampling continuous targets: SMOTER and SMOGN

An alternative or complementary strategy to sample reweighting is continuous resampling:

### 1. Continuous random undersampling
Instances falling in the common region ($\phi(y_i) < t_R$) are randomly subsampled according to an inverse frequency
schedule, reducing the overwhelming volume of bulk points.

### 2. Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique for Regression (SMOTER)
Developed by Torgo et al. <Cite id="torgo2013" />, SMOTER adapts the classification SMOTE algorithm to continuous
spaces:
1. Identify all rare training points $\mathcal{S}_R = \{(x_i, y_i) : \phi(y_i) \ge t_R\}$.
2. For each rare seed $(x_i, y_i)$, compute its $k$-nearest neighbours within $\mathcal{S}_R$ using Euclidean or
   Mahalanobis distance in feature space.
3. Randomly select a neighbor $(x_{nn}, y_{nn})$ and generate a synthetic feature vector via convex linear
   interpolation:
   $$
   x_{new} = x_i + \lambda (x_{nn} - x_i), \quad \lambda \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)
   $$
4. Assign the synthetic target $y_{new}$ via inverse distance weighting:
   $$
   y_{new} = y_i + \lambda (y_{nn} - y_i)
   $$

### 3. SMOGN (SMOTE and Gaussian Noise)
When candidate neighbours in feature space are distant (measured by a distance threshold based on the local standard
deviation), linear interpolation risks generating synthetic examples in invalid or unpopulated feature regions. SMOGN
introduces a safeguard: if $d(x_i, x_{nn})$ is smaller than a half-average threshold, SMOTER interpolation is executed;
otherwise, perturbation via Gaussian noise centered at $x_i$ is applied.

## Stratifying on a binned target

Standard cross-validation and random train/validation/test splits frequently introduce subtle evaluation leakage or
catastrophic split instability in imbalanced regression. If the rare tail contains only 15 instances across 10,000 rows,
a naive random 5-fold split may allocate 8 rare instances to fold 1 and zero rare instances to fold 3, leading to
massive fold-to-fold variance in validation scores.

Continuous target stratification solves this:
1. **Target binning:** Discretize continuous target $y$ into $B$ ordinal strata (e.g. 10 deciles, or custom bins
   separated at the 90th, 95th, and 99th percentiles).
2. **Stratified allocation:** Perform stratified $K$-fold partitioning using the discrete stratum IDs as class labels.
3. **Guaranteed tail representation:** Ensures that every training, validation, and test partition maintains an
   identical fraction of rare tail events.

## Evaluating by range and utility-based metrics

A critical trap of imbalanced regression is evaluating models using global metrics like Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)
or $R^2$:

$$
RMSE_{global} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}
$$

Because rare tail instances comprise only 1% to 5% of $N$, a naive constant model that always predicts the bulk median
can achieve an impressive $R^2 \approx 0.90$ and low global RMSE while predicting zero extreme events correctly.

Rigorous evaluation demands sliced and utility-based diagnostics:

### Sliced tail metrics
Partition test data into bulk and rare subsets based on $t_R$:

$$
RMSE_{rare} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_R|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_R} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}, \qquad MAE_{rare} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_R|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_R} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
$$

### Utility-based regression metrics
Ribeiro and Torgo define continuous precision, recall, and $F_1^\phi$ by pairing the relevance function $\phi(y)$ with
an asymmetric loss utility surface $U(\hat{y}, y)$. Over-predicting an extreme event when none occurs carries different
economic cost than failing to predict an extreme catastrophic event, which symmetric squared error cannot capture.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Extreme weight explosion:** Inverse density weights $1 / \hat{f}(y)$ on extreme outliers can reach factors of
   $10^4$ or higher. A single mislabeled noisy outlier in the tail will monopolize tree-split impurity or gradient
   steps. Always clamp weights with Winsorization or exponent tempering ($\alpha \le 0.5$).
2. **Synthetic feature manifold violation:** SMOTER generates points by linear interpolation in raw feature space. For
   tabular data containing categorical encodings, binary flags, or non-convex manifold structures (e.g. valid geospatial
   boundaries), synthetic points may represent physically impossible feature combinations.
3. **Evaluating on resampled test sets:** Never apply SMOTER or undersampling to validation or test sets. Test data must
   reflect the natural real-world operational target density.
