<Definition>
**Sample weights** (or observation weights) are non-negative scalar values $w_i \ge 0$ assigned to individual training
instances $(x_i, y_i)$, modifying their relative influence during empirical loss minimization. Sample weighting aligns
training objectives with target distributions under covariate shift, corrects for unequal survey sampling probabilities,
removes confounding in observational causal inference, or prioritizes recent temporal regimes
<Cite id="kish1965, shimodaira2000" />.
</Definition>

Standard Empirical Risk Minimization treats every training row as an equally representative draw from a fixed
population. When data collection involves stratified oversampling, non-random treatment assignment, or non-stationary
environments, unweighted optimization yields biased parameter estimates and misleading validation metrics.

## Weighted empirical risk minimization

In supervised learning, standard empirical risk averages the unweighted loss over $n$ training instances. Weighted
Empirical Risk Minimization scales each instance loss by weight $w_i$:

$$
\min_\theta \mathcal{R}_w(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \ell\big(y_i, f_\theta(x_i)\big)
$$

### Algorithmic implementation

- **Gradient-based learning**: In neural networks and generalized linear models, instance gradients are directly scaled
  by weights:
  $$
  \nabla_\theta \mathcal{R}_w = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \nabla_\theta \ell\big(y_i, f_\theta(x_i)\big)
  $$
- **Decision trees and tree ensembles**: In CART and gradient boosted decision trees, sample weights modify effective
  observation counts at internal nodes:
  $$
  N_t = \sum_{i \in \text{node } t} w_i, \qquad \bar{y}_t = \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i \in \text{node } t} w_i y_i
  $$
  Splits are chosen to maximize the reduction in weighted variance or weighted Gini impurity.

## Survey design weights (Horvitz-Thompson)

In survey sampling and official statistics, samples are frequently drawn with unequal selection probabilities
$\pi_i = \mathbb{P}(\text{unit } i \text{ included in sample})$—for example, intentionally oversampling rare rural
postcodes or high-income households to guarantee statistical power for subgroup analysis.

To produce an unbiased estimator of the true population risk, the Horvitz-Thompson theorem assigns design weights
inversely proportional to inclusion probability:

$$
w_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i}
$$

A respondent sampled with $\pi_i = 0.001$ represents $1,000$ individuals in the target population ($w_i = 1000$),
whereas a respondent sampled with $\pi_i = 0.05$ represents only $20$ individuals ($w_i = 20$).

## Importance weights for covariate shift

When the training distribution $p_{\text{train}}(x)$ differs from the target deployment distribution
$p_{\text{test}}(x)$ (covariate shift), minimizing unweighted training loss fails to minimize test risk.

Hidetoshi Shimodaira <Cite id="shimodaira2000" /> proved that weighting training instances by the Radon-Nikodym density
ratio:

$$
w_i = w(x_i) = \frac{p_{\text{test}}(x_i)}{p_{\text{train}}(x_i)}
$$

yields an asymptotically unbiased estimator of the expected risk on the target test domain:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{X \sim p_{\text{train}}}\big[ w(X) \ell(Y, f(X)) \big] = \int p_{\text{train}}(x) \frac{p_{\text{test}}(x)}{p_{\text{train}}(x)} \ell(y, f(x)) \, dx = \mathbb{E}_{X \sim p_{\text{test}}}\big[ \ell(Y, f(X)) \big]
$$

## Inverse propensity score weighting (IPW)

In observational causal inference and off-policy reinforcement learning, treatment assignment $T_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is not
randomized; it is confounded by background covariates $X$.

Paul Rosenbaum and Donald Rubin <Cite id="rosenbaum1983" /> introduced the **propensity score**
$e(x) = \mathbb{P}(T = 1 \mid X = x)$. To estimate the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) without confounding bias, Inverse
Propensity Weighting (IPW) applies weights:

$$
w_i = \frac{T_i}{e(x_i)} + \frac{1 - T_i}{1 - e(x_i)}
$$

Treated units that were highly unlikely to receive treatment ($e(x_i) \to 0$) receive large weights ($w_i \gg 1$),
effectively creating a synthetic pseudo-population where treatment assignment is independent of observed covariates $X$.

## Recency weights for temporal non-stationarity

In time series and streaming prediction, the true data-generating process drifts continuously. Rather than abruptly
truncating history with a hard cutoff window, **recency weighting** applies smooth exponential decay backwards from
current time $T$:

$$
w_t = \exp\big(-\lambda (T - t)\big) = \gamma^{T - t}, \quad \gamma \in (0, 1]
$$

Observations from yesterday receive weight near $1.0$, while events from months ago decay toward zero, allowing models
to learn long-term structure while prioritizing current operational dynamics.

## The cost of weighting: Kish's effective sample size

While sample weights eliminate structural bias, they incur a severe statistical penalty: **variance inflation**.

When weights vary significantly across instances, a small fraction of heavily weighted points dominates the objective
function, rendering the remaining points statistically negligible. Leslie Kish <Cite id="kish1965" /> derived the
classic formula for **Effective Sample Size (ESS)**:

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\left( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \right)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2} = \frac{n}{1 + \text{CV}^2(w)}
$$

where $\text{CV}(w) = \frac{\sigma_w}{\bar{w}}$ is the coefficient of variation of the sample weights.

### The Design Effect (Deff)

The ratio between nominal sample size $n$ and effective sample size $n_{\text{eff}}$ defines the **Design Effect**:

$$
\text{Deff} = \frac{n}{n_{\text{eff}}} = 1 + \text{CV}^2(w) \implies \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_w) \approx \text{Deff} \cdot \text{Var}_{\text{unweighted}}(\hat{\theta})
$$

If a dataset contains $n = 10,000$ rows, but a few extreme propensity scores produce weights where $\text{CV}(w) = 3$,
the effective sample size collapses to:

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{10000}{1 + 3^2} = \frac{10000}{10} = 1,000 \text{ rows}
$$

The parameter estimator has the statistical variance of a tiny 1,000-row sample, yielding wide confidence intervals and
unstable predictions.

## Weight regularization: Clipping and normalisation

To balance the bias-variance trade-off, practical machine learning systems regularize extreme sample weights:

1. **Weight clipping (Winsorization)**: Truncate weights exceeding a designated threshold $M$:
   $$
   w_i^{\text{clipped}} = \min(w_i, M)
   $$
   Typically, $M$ is set to the 95th or 99th percentile of weights, or capped at $10 \times \bar{w}$ or
   $20 \times \bar{w}$. Clipping deliberately accepts a small amount of residual bias in exchange for preventing
   effective sample size collapse.
2. **Weight normalisation**: Rescale weights so their sum equals the nominal dataset size:
   $$
   \tilde{w}_i = n \cdot \frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^n w_j} \implies \sum_{i=1}^n \tilde{w}_i = n
   $$
   This prevents sample weighting from altering effective learning rates in gradient-based optimizers.
