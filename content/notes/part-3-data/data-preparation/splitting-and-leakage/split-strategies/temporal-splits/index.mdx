<Definition>
A **temporal split** is a non-random dataset partitioning scheme that enforces strict chronological causality by
training models on historical observations ($t < T_{\text{split}}$) and evaluating them on subsequent future periods
($t \ge T_{\text{split}}$). In the presence of multi-step forward labels and auto-regressive feature dependencies,
temporal boundaries must incorporate **label horizon gaps**, **purging**, and **embargo** buffers to eliminate lookahead
leakage <Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" />.
</Definition>

In time series, quantitative finance, and event-driven forecasting, random cross-validation yields catastrophically
optimistic results: random splitting allows the model to interpolate between past and future observations, whereas
deployment requires out-of-time extrapolation into the unknown future.

## Training on the past and testing on the future

When data points possess temporal sequence, observations are linked through serial correlation, momentum, macro trends,
and auto-regressive state dynamics.

Applying standard random $K$-fold cross-validation destroys the arrow of time:
1. The training fold contains observations from day $t - 1$ and day $t + 1$.
2. The validation fold is asked to predict day $t$.
3. The learning algorithm solves an easy **interpolation** task by averaging adjacent time points rather than learning
   predictive signals that forecast genuine future state transitions.

A valid temporal partition enforces strict chronological precedence:

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{ (x_t, y_t) : t < T_{\text{split}} \}, \qquad \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}} = \{ (x_t, y_t) : t \ge T_{\text{split}} \}
$$

## Rolling origins and walk-forward evaluation

A single static train/test split evaluates the model on only a single historical test regime (e.g., Q4 of a single
year), leaving it vulnerable to regime-specific market anomalies. **Walk-forward evaluation** (or rolling origin
validation) repeats the temporal split across rolling temporal horizons:

### Expanding window (cumulative)

At each step $k$, the training window expands to incorporate all historical data from inception up to time $T_k$,
forecasting the subsequent evaluation window $[T_k, T_k + H)$:

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}^{(k)} = [0, T_k), \qquad \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}^{(k)} = [T_k, T_k + H)
$$

This maximizes available training volume and mirrors systems where models are retrained periodically on accumulated
historical logs.

### Rolling window (sliding fixed width)

At each step $k$, the training window maintains a constant historical length $W$, discarding observations older than
$T_k - W$:

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}^{(k)} = [T_k - W, T_k), \qquad \mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}^{(k)} = [T_k, T_k + H)
$$

Rolling windows prevent stale, obsolete patterns from distant history from dominating the model, providing greater
responsiveness during non-stationary regime shifts.

## Gaps for forward label horizons

A frequent but subtle form of lookahead leakage arises when the target variable $y_t$ is defined across a multi-step
forward window:

$$
y_t = f(z_t, z_{t+1}, \dots, z_{t+H})
$$

Common examples include:
- Predicting 7-day cumulative asset returns: $y_t = \frac{p_{t+7} - p_t}{p_t}$.
- Predicting 30-day customer churn: $y_t = \mathbb{I}(\text{cancelled between } t \text{ and } t + 30)$.
- Predicting 48-hour hospital readmission: $y_t = \mathbb{I}(\text{readmitted within } 48\text{h})$.

If the training set ends at $T_{\text{split}}$, a training observation recorded at $T_{\text{split}} - 2$ computes its
target $y_t$ using real-world events that occur up to $T_{\text{split}} + 5$—which falls **inside the test period**.

To prevent target leakage across the partition boundary, a **label horizon gap** of at least $H$ periods must separate
training and test sets:

$$
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{ (x_t, y_t) : t \le T_{\text{split}} - H \}, \qquad \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}} = \{ (x_t, y_t) : t \ge T_{\text{split}} \}
$$

## Purging and embargo in financial machine learning

In financial machine learning, Marcos López de Prado <Cite id="lopezdeprado2018" /> formalized two essential safeguards
when performing cross-validation across overlapping time series:

### Purging

Purging removes from the training set any observation whose label evaluation span $[t, t + H]$ overlaps with the time
interval of the test partition. In Purged $K$-Fold cross-validation, training samples immediately preceding any test
block are purged to prevent label leakage.

### Embargo

Even when labels do not overlap, features often exhibit long auto-regressive memory (such as 30-day exponential moving
averages, rolling volatility, or customer interaction counts). When training on data that occurs *after* a test fold,
training features can incorporate memory traces of test-period events.

An **embargo** buffer $\Delta_{\text{embargo}}$ discards training instances immediately following the test partition:

$$
\text{Post-test training allowed only for } t > T_{\text{test\_end}} + \Delta_{\text{embargo}}
$$

Together, purging before the test fold and embargoing after the test fold guarantee complete informational isolation.

## Pitfalls and structural hazards

1. **Survivorship bias in historical filters**: Constructing historical datasets by filtering for entities that are
   currently active in the database eliminates failed companies, churned users, or bankrupt accounts, creating
   unrealistic training data.
2. **Data starvation in early walk-forward splits**: Early walk-forward folds possess small training windows, leading to
   high estimation variance. Ensure minimum initial training lengths before reporting metrics.
3. **Seasonal boundary misalignment**: Placing split boundaries across volatile calendar events (e.g. Black Friday or
   quarter-end tax deadlines) distorts validation metrics. Align split boundaries with natural operational business
   cycles.
