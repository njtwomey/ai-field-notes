<Definition>
**Feature selection stability** quantifies the reproducibility and robustness of a selected feature subset under
sampling perturbations of the training data. Because standard feature selection heuristics (such as Lasso, stepwise
wrappers, or tree splits) can yield drastically different subsets when fit on slightly different subsamples, stability
techniques—including stability selection, Boruta shadow features, and Model-X knockoffs—control false discovery rates
and ensure scientific reproducibility.
</Definition>

In high-dimensional regimes ($p \gg n$) or environments with collinearity, small perturbations in data rows can cause
selection algorithms to flip-flop between competing collinear variables. Quantifying and enforcing stability transforms
feature selection from an opportunistic empirical heuristic into a statistically rigorous inference procedure.

## Instability in feature selection

Feature selection algorithms can be mathematically viewed as mapping a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ to a binary indicator
vector $s \in \{0, 1\}^p$, where $s_j = 1$ denotes feature inclusion. Instability arises when two independent random
samples $\mathcal{D}_a, \mathcal{D}_b \sim \mathbb{P}^n$ yield subsets with low overlap.

### Measuring subset stability

Historically, stability was evaluated using pairwise Jaccard similarity between two selected sets $S_a, S_b$:

$$
J(S_a, S_b) = \frac{|S_a \cap S_b|}{|S_a \cup S_b|}
$$

However, raw Jaccard index is mathematically confounded by subset size: selecting 90 out of 100 features at random
yields high Jaccard similarity by pure chance.

To resolve this, Sarah Nogueira et al. <Cite id="nogueira2018" /> established a mathematically grounded, Pearson-like
stability index $\hat{\Phi}$ across $M$ subsample runs:

$$
\hat{\Phi} = 1 - \frac{\frac{1}{p} \sum_{j=1}^p s_j^2}{\frac{\bar{k}}{p} \left( 1 - \frac{\bar{k}}{p} \right)}
$$

where $s_j^2 = \frac{M}{M-1} \hat{p}_j (1 - \hat{p}_j)$ is the sample variance of the selection indicator for feature
$j$, $\hat{p}_j$ is its empirical selection frequency, and $\bar{k} = \sum_{j=1}^p \hat{p}_j$ is the average subset
size. $\hat{\Phi}$ is bounded in $[-1, 1]$, equals zero under random feature assignment regardless of subset
cardinality, and reaches $1.0$ if and only if all subsamples select the exact same features.

## Stability selection

Developed by Nicolai Meinshausen and Peter Bühlmann <Cite id="meinshausen2010" />, **stability selection** wraps
subsampling around existing feature selectors (such as Lasso, Elastic Net, or Orthogonal Matching Pursuit) to convert
unstable point estimators into reliable selections with provable error bounds.

### The subsampling protocol

1. Given dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of $n$ samples, draw $B$ random subsamples $\mathcal{D}_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}_B$, each of
   size $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ without replacement.
2. Fit the base feature selector on each subsample $b \in \{1, \dots, B\}$, obtaining active subset $\hat{S}_b$.
3. Compute the empirical selection probability for each feature $j \in \{1, \dots, p\}$:
   $$
   \hat{\Pi}_j = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \mathbb{I}(j \in \hat{S}_b)
   $$
4. Retain the stable subset:
   $$
   \hat{S}_{\text{stable}} = \left\{ j : \hat{\Pi}_j \ge \pi_{\text{thr}} \right\}
   $$
   where $\pi_{\text{thr}} \in (0.5, 1.0)$ is a user-chosen stability threshold (typically $0.75$ or $0.80$).

### Finite-sample false discovery bound

Meinshausen and Bühlmann proved that under exchangeability of noise features, the expected number of falsely selected
noise variables $\mathbb{E}[V]$ is bounded by:

$$
\mathbb{E}[V] \le \frac{1}{2\pi_{\text{thr}} - 1} \frac{q^2}{p}
$$

where $q = \mathbb{E}[|\hat{S}|]$ is the average number of features selected per subsample, and $p$ is the total
candidate feature count. This gives practitioners a direct mathematical dial to control false discoveries without
relying on asymptotic assumptions.

## Shadow feature benchmarking: The Boruta algorithm

The Boruta algorithm <Cite id="kursa2010" /> is an all-relevant feature selection method built on Random Forests that
identifies all features statistically associated with the target, rather than seeking a minimal non-redundant subset.

### The shadow permutation mechanism

1. **Shadow creation**: For every feature $X_j$, construct a synthetic **shadow feature** $\tilde{X}_j$ by permuting its
   values across rows. This preserves the marginal distribution, mean, and variance of the feature while destroying any
   relationship with target $Y$.
2. **Joint model fit**: Concatenate original features with shadow features into an expanded matrix
   $[X, \tilde{X}] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 2p}$.
3. **Importance scoring**: Train a Random Forest and compute Z-scores of permutation importance for all $2p$ attributes.
4. **Noise ceiling**: Determine the maximum Z-score achieved across all shadow attributes:
   $$
   Z_{\max}^{\text{shadow}} = \max_{j=1,\dots,p} Z(\tilde{X}_j)
   $$
5. **Statistical comparison**: In each iteration, test whether an original feature scores significantly higher than
   $Z_{\max}^{\text{shadow}}$.
6. Features that consistently outperform the shadow ceiling across successive iterations (evaluated via a two-sided
   binomial test with Bonferroni correction) are tagged as **Confirmed**; features consistently underperforming are
   eliminated as **Rejected**.

## Model-X Knockoffs and exact FDR control

Developed by Rina Foygel Barber and Emmanuel Candès <Cite id="barber2015" />, the knockoff framework achieves exact,
finite-sample False Discovery Rate (FDR) control for arbitrary non-linear and high-dimensional models without requiring
data splitting.

### Constructing knockoff variables

For feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, a knockoff matrix $\tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ is
constructed such that:
1. Knockoffs mimic the exact covariance structure $\Sigma$ of the original features:
   $$
   [X, \tilde{X}]^T [X, \tilde{X}] \approx \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma & \Sigma - \text{diag}(s) \\ \Sigma - \text{diag}(s) & \Sigma \end{bmatrix}
   $$
2. Knockoffs are constructed without ever looking at the target $Y$, ensuring conditional independence
   $Y \perp \tilde{X} \mid X$.

### The knockoff statistic and threshold

A feature statistic $W_j = f(X_j, \tilde{X}_j)$ compares the predictive power of original feature $X_j$ against its
synthetic knockoff $\tilde{X}_j$:

$$
W_j = |Z_j| - |\tilde{Z}_j|
$$

Under the null hypothesis that feature $j$ has no true effect on $Y$, original feature $X_j$ and knockoff $\tilde{X}_j$
are exchangeable, meaning $W_j$ is symmetric around zero. A positive $W_j \gg 0$ indicates genuine predictive power.

The knockoff filter chooses a data-dependent cutoff threshold $\tau$:

$$
\tau = \min \left\{ t > 0 : \frac{1 + |\{j : W_j \le -t\}|}{|\{j : W_j \ge t\}| \vee 1} \le q \right\}
$$

Selecting all features with $W_j \ge \tau$ mathematically guarantees that the False Discovery Rate
$\mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{V}{|S| \vee 1} \right] \le q$ in finite samples.

## Selective inference: The post-selection $p$-value crisis

A ubiquitous pitfall in empirical modeling is computing standard classical $p$-values, $t$-tests, or Wald confidence
intervals on a feature subset **after** performing data-driven feature selection (such as Lasso or stepwise regression).

Classical inferential theory assumes the hypothesis $H_0: \beta_j = 0$ was formulated *a priori* before inspecting the
data. When the subset $\hat{S}$ is chosen because its sample coefficients were large, the sampling distribution of
$\hat{\beta}_j$ is conditioned on the selection event:

$$
\mathbb{P}\left( \hat{\beta}_j \mid j \in \hat{S} \right) \neq \mathbb{P}\left( \hat{\beta}_j \right)
$$

The selection event truncates the null distribution, producing catastrophically optimistic $p$-values ($p < 10^{-6}$ on
pure noise).

Modern **selective inference** (or post-selection inference) resolves this by conditioning the test statistic explicitly
on the geometric polyhedron representing the Lasso selection event $A y \le b$, deriving exact truncated Gaussian
distributions that restore rigorous confidence intervals and valid Type I error control.
