<Definition>
**Feature construction** computes new input columns as functions of existing ones and adds them to, or substitutes
them for, the original inputs. Common constructions are products of two inputs, ratios and differences, powers, and
statistics of a group the row belongs to. A constructed feature makes a relation explicit, so that a model with a
restricted form can use it directly instead of approximating it from the raw inputs.
</Definition>

## Problem

A model can only use a relation it can represent. A linear model $\hat y = \wvec^\top \xvec + b$ fits a weighted sum
of its inputs. It cannot represent the product $x_1 x_2$, a ratio $x_1 / x_2$ or a curve in one input. A tree ensemble
splits one input at a time, so a ratio of two inputs costs it many splits to approximate. A network can approximate
any of these relations in principle, but needs data and training to do so. Domingos names the choice of features as
the factor that most often separates projects that succeed from those that fail <Cite id="domingos2012" />.

## Mechanism

Each construction encodes a guess about the structure of the target.

### Interactions

An **interaction** is a product $x_j x_k$. With it, the effect of $x_j$ on the prediction changes with $x_k$:
$\partial \hat y / \partial x_j = w_j + w_{jk} x_k$. A price elasticity that differs by region, or a drug effect that
depends on dose and weight together, is an interaction. A product of a numeric input and a one-hot indicator gives the
numeric input a separate slope in each category.

### Ratios and differences

A **ratio** $x_j / x_k$ expresses one quantity per unit of another: debt per unit of income, clicks per impression,
weight per square metre of height. A **difference** $x_j - x_k$ measures a change or a gap: today's value minus
yesterday's, a price minus a reference price. Ratios need a guard against a zero or near-zero denominator, for example
$x_j / (x_k + c)$ with a small constant $c$ in the units of $x_k$.

### Polynomial terms

A **polynomial expansion** adds all monomials of the inputs up to a degree $p$, such as $x_1^2$, $x_1 x_2$ and
$x_1^2 x_3$ for $p = 3$. It is the systematic form of interactions and powers; see
<NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis expansions</NoteLink>. With $d$ inputs there are $\binom{d + p}{p} - 1$
monomials, so the expansion grows quickly: $d = 10$ and $p = 3$ give 285 columns.

### Group aggregates

A **group aggregate** describes a row by a statistic of the rows that share one of its values: the mean price in the
row's postcode, the number of earlier orders by the same customer, a row's value minus its group's mean. Aggregates
over related tables and time windows are treated in
<NoteLink to="relational-feature-aggregation">relational and aggregate features</NoteLink>. An aggregate that
includes the target, such as a group's mean outcome, is target encoding and must be computed out of fold; see
<NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">categorical encoding</NoteLink>.

### Domain features

A **domain feature** comes from knowledge of how the data arise. Body-mass index is weight divided by height squared;
a loan's monthly payment follows from its amount, rate and term; a physical law gives the expected value of a
measurement. A domain feature compresses a relation that would otherwise need several constructed terms, and it
carries an interpretation.

## Constructing features for a known target

The figure fits <Gloss name="ols">ordinary least squares</Gloss> to a target with a curve in $x_1$, a curve in $x_2$, a
linear effect of $x_3$ and an interaction $\gamma x_1 x_2$:

$$
y = \sin(\pi x_1) + x_2^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} x_3 + \gamma x_1 x_2 + \varepsilon, \qquad x_j \sim \mathcal{U}(-1, 1), \quad
\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2).
$$

Five feature sets are built from the same three inputs: the raw inputs; the raw inputs with their pairwise products;
all monomials of degree at most 2; all monomials of degree at most 3; and four constructed features
$\sin(\pi x_1)$, $x_2^2$, $x_3$ and $x_1 x_2$. Each set is scored by 5-fold cross-validated
<Gloss name="r-squared" />.

The raw inputs reach an $R^2$ of about 0.33 with $\gamma = 1.5$; the residuals against $x_1$ trace a sine curve, and the
residuals against $x_1 x_2$ slope upwards. Adding the products removes the slope. The degree-3 expansion reaches an
$R^2$ near the noise ceiling with 19 columns, because $\sin(\pi x_1)$ is close to a cubic on $[-1, 1]$. The four
constructed features reach the same score with four columns, each with a readable coefficient. A residual plot against
a candidate feature is the usual way to find what a model is missing: a trend in the residuals is structure that the
current features do not express.

## Which models need which features

Models differ in which constructions they can synthesise from raw inputs. Heaton trained neural networks,
<Gloss name="svm">support vector machines</Gloss>, <NoteLink to="random-forest">random forests</NoteLink> and
gradient-boosted trees on targets that were each one engineered function of the inputs <Cite id="heaton2016" />. Every
model learned transforms of a single input, such as a logarithm, a power or a square root. No model learned a ratio of
differences, $(x_1 - x_2)/(x_3 - x_4)$. The tree ensembles also failed on a sum and on a standard deviation across
inputs. The support vector machine failed on ratios, polynomial ratios, a maximum and a quadratic form. The network
learned everything except the ratio of differences. The practical reading is:

- **Linear and generalised linear models** need every non-linearity and interaction constructed, or supplied by a
  basis expansion.
- **Tree ensembles** split one input at a time. They gain from sums, differences and ratios of several inputs, which
  their axis-aligned splits approximate only with many steps.
- **Neural networks** synthesise sums, products and smooth curves, but still gain from ratios and from domain features,
  especially when data are limited.

## Cost

- **Columns.** A full expansion of degree $p$ in $d$ inputs has $\binom{d + p}{p} - 1$ columns. Fitting cost and the
  risk of overfitting grow with them; a constructed set chosen with knowledge grows by a handful.
- **Search.** Each candidate feature must be evaluated, and evaluating many candidates on the same validation data
  overfits that data. Candidates should be judged by <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> inside
  the training data, as in <NoteLink to="automated-feature-engineering">automated feature discovery</NoteLink>.
- **Maintenance.** Every constructed feature must be computed identically at training and prediction time. A feature
  computed one way in the training pipeline and another way in production is a common source of silent error; see
  <NoteLink to="feature-stores">feature stores</NoteLink>.

## Trade-offs

- **Construction against expansion.** Domain knowledge gives few, interpretable features. A blind expansion needs no
  knowledge but many columns, regularisation and <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink>.
- **Construction against flexible models.** A flexible model, such as gradient boosting or a network, can learn some
  constructions itself given enough data <Cite id="kuhn2019" />. With few rows, a constructed feature substitutes for
  data the model would otherwise need.
- **Interpretability.** A coefficient on a domain feature, such as a ratio with a meaning, is easier to explain than
  a coefficient spread over many monomials.

## Pitfalls

- **Leaking the target through a construction.** A feature computed from the target, or from data recorded after the
  prediction moment, inflates validation scores; see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>. Group means
  of the target must be fitted out of fold.
- **Fitting construction parameters on all the data.** A constructed feature with fitted parts, such as a group mean
  or a binned threshold, is part of the model and must be fitted inside each training fold.
- **Unscaled products.** Products of inputs on different scales have very different magnitudes. Penalised models then
  shrink them unequally; standardise inputs before forming products or after; see
  <NoteLink to="feature-scaling">feature scaling</NoteLink>.
- **Division by values near zero.** A ratio with a denominator near zero produces extreme values that dominate a
  linear fit. Clip or offset the denominator, or use a difference of logarithms.
