<Definition>
A **domain-knowledge feature** is an engineered transformation $\phi(\mathbf{x}): \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^M$ that
embeds explicit scientific mechanisms, physical conservation laws, dimensional invariance, or functional inductive
biases (such as piecewise polynomial basis expansions) directly into the feature space:

$$
\mathbf{z} = \phi(\mathbf{x}) = \left[\pi_1(\mathbf{x}), \pi_2(\mathbf{x}), \dots, B_1(x_j), \dots, B_K(x_j)\right]^\top.
$$

Rather than forcing an estimator to rediscover known physical or structural relationships from sparse, noisy
observations, mechanistic transformations restrict the hypothesis space to physically plausible functions.
</Definition>

## Features from mechanisms

In many applied domains—such as aerodynamics, pharmacokinetics, structural mechanics, and financial risk—underlying
physical or regulatory mechanisms dictate the governing equations:

- **Conservation laws**: In chemical engineering and energy systems, mass and energy balances enforce invariant sums
  ($\sum_i \dot{m}_i = 0$). Providing residual imbalances $\Delta E = E_{\text{in}} - E_{\text{out}}$ as explicit
  features isolates sensor drift or system leakage without requiring a multi-layer neural network to learn addition and
  subtraction across noisy telemetry channels.
- **Kinetic and saturation curves**: Biochemical reactions frequently obey Michaelis-Menten enzyme kinetics:

  $$
  v = \frac{V_{\max} [S]}{K_m + [S]}.
  $$

  A linear model cannot fit this hyperbolic saturation directly. However, transforming the concentration $[S]$ into its
  reciprocal $1/[S]$ (or using a log transform $\ln [S]$) linearises the response into the Lineweaver-Burk formulation:

  $$
  \frac{1}{v} = \frac{K_m}{V_{\max}} \left(\frac{1}{[S]}\right) + \frac{1}{V_{\max}},
  $$

  converting a non-linear parameter estimation problem into an exact ordinary least-squares problem.

- **Geometric and structural relationships**: In spatial navigation, computing explicit Euclidean distances
  $d = \|\mathbf{p}_1 - \mathbf{p}_2\|_2$ or Manhattan norms $\|\mathbf{p}_1 - \mathbf{p}_2\|_1$ injects metric topology
  that tree-based models (which make axis-aligned cuts) struggle to approximate without deep partitioning.

## Ratios with physical meaning

Raw numerical measurements often scale with total system size, obscuring the underlying state of the system. Dividing
one variable by another cancels extensive scaling and isolates intensive state properties:

1. **Aspect ratio and relative dimensions**: In aerodynamic drag prediction, chord length $c$ and wingspan $b$ scale
   with aircraft size; the aspect ratio $\text{AR} = b^2 / S$ (where $S$ is wing area) directly determines induced drag
   efficiency regardless of absolute scale.
2. **Financial liquidity and solvency ratios**: In credit risk, a company's total debt or current assets scale with
   enterprise size. Standard ratios—such as the debt-to-equity ratio or current ratio
   $\frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$—quantify solvency across both micro-enterprises and
   multinational corporations.
3. **Biometric indices**: Body mass index ($\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{height}^2}$) constructs an
   approximately scale-invariant measure of body volume from two correlated physical measurements.

## Dimensional analysis and dimensionless groups

The most systematic framework for mechanistic feature construction is **dimensional analysis**, governed by the
Buckingham $\pi$ theorem <Cite id="buckingham1914" />.

### The Buckingham $\pi$ theorem

Let a physical relationship involve $n$ dimensional physical variables $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n$:

$$
f(q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n) = 0.
$$

If these $n$ variables can be expressed in terms of $k$ independent fundamental physical dimensions (such as mass $[M]$,
length $[L]$, time $[T]$, temperature $[\Theta]$), then the system can be equivalently expressed in terms of $p = n - k$
independent **dimensionless groups** $\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots, \pi_p$:

$$
F(\pi_1, \pi_2, \dots, \pi_p) = 0.
$$

### Impact on sample complexity

Transforming raw physical features into dimensionless $\pi$-groups reduces the input dimension from $n$ to $n - k$.
Because statistical sample complexity scales exponentially with input dimension in non-parametric estimation, this
dimensional reduction collapses the volume of feature space that training data must cover:

- **Reynolds number ($\text{Re}$)**: Combines fluid density $\rho$, velocity $v$, characteristic length $L$, and dynamic
  viscosity $\mu$:

  $$
  \text{Re} = \frac{\rho v L}{\mu} = \frac{\text{inertial forces}}{\text{viscous forces}}.
  $$

  Flow regime transitions (from laminar to turbulent) occur at critical values of $\text{Re}$ independently of whether
  the fluid is air or water, or whether the scale is a model wind tunnel or full-scale aircraft.
- **Froude number ($\text{Fr}$)**: Governs free-surface wave resistance:

  $$
  \text{Fr} = \frac{v}{\sqrt{g L}}.
  $$

- **Mach number ($\text{Ma}$)**: Governs compressibility effects:

  $$
  \text{Ma} = \frac{v}{a}.
  $$

By computing dimensionless numbers as input features, models inherently satisfy scale invariance and dimensional
consistency without having to infer the laws of physics from data.

## Basis expansions and spline features

When an input variable exhibits a smooth, non-linear relationship with the target, standard linear models fail to
capture curvature, while high-degree global polynomials $\phi(x) = [1, x, x^2, \dots, x^d]$ suffer from severe boundary
oscillations (**Runge's phenomenon**).

### B-spline basis expansion

A **spline** is a piecewise polynomial of degree $k$ (typically cubic, $k=3$) connected smoothly at predefined break
points called **knots** $\tau_1 < \tau_2 < \dots < \tau_m$ <Cite id="deboor1978" /> <Cite id="hastie2009" />. A spline
of degree $k$ maintains continuity in derivatives up to order $k-1$ at every interior knot.

Any spline function can be represented as a linear combination of B-spline basis functions:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{m + k} \beta_j B_{j, k}(x),
$$

where the basis functions $B_{j, k}(x)$ are computed via the Cox-de Boor recurrence relation:

$$
B_{j, 0}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \tau_j \le x < \tau_{j+1} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

$$
B_{j, k}(x) = \frac{x - \tau_j}{\tau_{j+k} - \tau_j} B_{j, k-1}(x) + \frac{\tau_{j+k+1} - x}{\tau_{j+k+1} - \tau_{j+1}} B_{j+1, k-1}(x).
$$

Each basis function $B_{j, k}(x)$ has **compact local support**: it is strictly zero outside the interval
$[\tau_j, \tau_{j+k+1}]$. Consequently, modifying a knot coefficient locally leaves distant predictions unaffected,
avoiding the global instability of polynomials.

### Natural cubic splines and boundary extrapolation

Standard polynomials oscillate and diverge toward $\pm \infty$ outside the range of training data. **Natural cubic
splines** enforce a boundary constraint: the second and third derivatives must equal zero beyond the boundary knots
($\tau_1$ and $\tau_m$):

$$
f''(x) = 0, \quad f'''(x) = 0 \quad \text{for } x < \tau_1 \text{ or } x > \tau_m.
$$

This forces the spline to extrapolate **linearly** outside the observed data support, providing stable, bounded
predictions rather than catastrophic boundary explosions.

## Pitfalls and failure modes

1. **Singularities and division by zero**: Ratios such as $x_1 / x_2$ diverge to $\pm \infty$ when the denominator
   approaches zero. To avoid numerical instability and extreme outlier creation, add an empirical regulariser or
   shrinkage constant:

   $$
   z = \frac{x_1}{x_2 + \epsilon \cdot \text{IQR}(x_2)}.
   $$

2. **Multicollinearity with raw variables**: Adding both raw inputs $[x_1, x_2]$ and their ratio $x_1 / x_2$ or product
   $x_1 x_2$ introduces strong multicollinearity into linear models, inflating variance of parameter estimates. Ridge or
   elastic net regularisation is necessary to stabilize coefficients.
3. **Knot placement sensitivity**: In spline basis expansions, placing knots uniformly across a heavily skewed feature
   leaves dense data regions under-parameterised while placing too many degrees of freedom in sparse tails. Place knots
   at **empirical quantiles** of the training distribution to ensure equal observation density per spline segment.
4. **Invalid physical assumptions**: Mechanistic features derived from laminar flow equations or idealized market
   equilibrium fail catastrophically when operating conditions shift into turbulent or distressed regimes. Validate
   domain features against empirical residuals to ensure the assumed physics hold.
