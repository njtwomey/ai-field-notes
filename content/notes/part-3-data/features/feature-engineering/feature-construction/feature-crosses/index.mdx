<Definition>
A **feature cross** (or Cartesian product feature) constructs a synthetic feature by taking the logical conjunction
(tensor product) of two or more categorical features or discretized continuous variables:

$$
\phi_{\text{cross}}\left(x_1, x_2\right) = x_1 \otimes x_2.
$$

For one-hot encoded categorical features $\mathbf{u} \in \{0, 1\}^{C_1}$ and $\mathbf{v} \in \{0, 1\}^{C_2}$, the cross
produces a sparse binary indicator vector of dimension $C_1 \times C_2$:

$$
\phi_{(c_1, c_2)}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{I}\left(x_1 = c_1 \wedge x_2 = c_2\right).
$$
</Definition>

## Crossing categorical features

Standard linear models compute predictions as additive linear combinations of individual inputs:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(w_0 + \sum_{j=1}^D w_j x_j\right).
$$

A strictly additive model cannot capture non-linear feature interactions without manual transformation. A classical
failure is the non-linear XOR interaction:
- Suppose $x_1 \in \{\text{Mobile}, \text{Desktop}\}$ and $x_2 \in \{\text{Day}, \text{Night}\}$.
- If conversion rates are high during `[Mobile, Night]` and `[Desktop, Day]`, but low during `[Mobile, Day]` and
  `[Desktop, Night]`, an additive model assigns independent marginal weights $w_{\text{Mobile}}$ and $w_{\text{Night}}$,
  failing to isolate the specific interaction.

By introducing the feature cross $x_1 \otimes x_2$, the model learns a dedicated weight
$w_{\text{Mobile}\times\text{Night}}$ for that specific conjunction:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma\left(w_0 + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_{12} (x_1 \otimes x_2)\right).
$$

This allows linear estimators and logistic regression models to learn non-linear decision boundaries while retaining
linear inference latency.

### Crossing binned continuous features
Continuous variables can be crossed after discretization:
- Consider geographic coordinates `Latitude` and `Longitude`.
- Discretizing both into $K$ spatial bins (e.g., $100$ bins each) and crossing them
  ($\text{bin}(\text{Lat}) \times \text{bin}(\text{Long})$) partitions continuous space into a 2D spatial grid of
  $10{,}000$ cells.
- A linear model learns an independent weight for each spatial cell, allowing it to fit non-linear pricing heatmaps
  (such as localized real-estate values) without requiring deep non-linear architectures.

## Crosses in linear models at scale

In web-scale click-through rate (CTR) prediction and recommendation systems, linear models trained over billions of
feature crosses serve as foundational infrastructure <Cite id="cheng2016wide" /> <Cite id="mcmahan2013adclick" />:

### Google's Wide & Deep Learning
Cheng et al. (2016) introduced Wide & Deep learning to balance **memorization** and **generalization**
<Cite id="cheng2016wide" />:
- **Wide component:** A generalized linear model defined over cross-product feature transformations:
  
  $$
  \phi_k(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{j=1}^D x_j^{c_{kj}}, \quad c_{kj} \in \{0, 1\}.
  $$

  The wide model excels at *memorization*: capturing high-precision historical co-occurrence rules directly from data
  (e.g. `User_Installed_App = 'Netflix'` AND `Impression_App = 'Hulu'`).
- **Deep component:** A deep feedforward neural network operating on continuous dense embeddings, providing
  *generalization* to previously unseen item combinations.

In production ad serving, wide linear models with billions of cross terms are optimized online using algorithms such as
FTRL-Proximal (Follow-The-Regularized-Leader), which enforces aggressive $L_1$ sparsity to prune inactive cross weights
<Cite id="mcmahan2013adclick" />.

## Growth in the number of terms

While feature crosses grant linear models high expressive capacity, their parameter space explodes combinatorially:

$$
\text{Dimension of } k\text{-way cross} = \prod_{m=1}^k C_m.
$$

If a feature cross combines:
- `user_id` ($10^7$ unique categories)
- `ad_id` ($10^6$ unique categories)
- `device_type` ($10^1$ categories)

The full Cartesian product contains $10^{14}$ potential terms. Even with extreme sparsity where 99.999% of combinations
never occur, storing vocabulary lookup tables across distributed training clusters becomes impossible.

## Hashed crosses

To bound parameter storage in high-cardinality production systems, industrial pipelines combine feature crossing with
the **hashing trick** <Cite id="weinberger2009" />:

Instead of maintaining a growing string vocabulary dictionary, the algorithm concatenates category tokens with a
delimiter and hashes the composite string directly into a fixed-size index table of size $B$ (e.g.
$B = 2^{22} \approx 4.19 \times 10^6$ buckets):

$$
h(x_1, x_2) = \operatorname{hash}\left(x_1 \circ \text{"::"} \circ x_2\right) \pmod B.
$$

Combined with a secondary signed hash $\xi(x_1, x_2) \in \{-1, +1\}$, the hashed representation preserves inner product
expectations:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\langle \tilde{\mathbf{u}}, \tilde{\mathbf{v}} \rangle\right] = \langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle.
$$

### Trade-offs of hashed crosses
- **Pros:** Zero dictionary synchronization across distributed worker clusters, strictly bounded parameter memory, and
  instant support for out-of-vocabulary combinations at inference time.
- **Cons:** Hash collisions can map unrelated pairs (e.g. `[US, Shoes]` and `[JP, Electronics]`) to the same weight
  bucket, adding residual gradient noise that slightly degrades calibration.

## Pitfalls

- **Unregularized cross expansion:** Crossing high-cardinality features without $L_1$ regularization (Lasso or FTRL)
  results in massive overfitting, as millions of singleton pairs memorize sample noise.
- **Crossing features with low support:** Generating crosses where 95% of cells contain fewer than 5 observations
  produces noisy gradient updates. Frequency filtering must discard rare cross combinations before model fitting.
- **Crossing collinear or redundant features:** Crossing features that share strong hierarchical relationships (e.g.
  `City × Country` or `ZipCode × State`) multiplies parameter counts without providing new interaction signal.
