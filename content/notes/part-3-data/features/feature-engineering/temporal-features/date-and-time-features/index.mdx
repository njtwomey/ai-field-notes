<Definition>
**Date and time features** are inputs derived from the timestamp of a record. Calendar parts, such as the hour,
weekday, month and whether a day is a working day, capture effects that recur on the calendar. Holiday and event
indicators mark days with known special behaviour. Elapsed times measure the gap since or until another event. Each is
derived in the time zone where the behaviour happens.
</Definition>

## Problem

A timestamp stored as a single number, such as seconds since 1970, grows steadily and never repeats. Sales on
Mondays, traffic at 8 am and electricity demand in January are effects of where a moment falls on the calendar, not of
how large its timestamp is. A model given only the raw number can fit a trend through it, but it cannot see that two
Mondays a week apart resemble each other. Date and time features expose the recurring parts of a timestamp and the
gaps between events, so that a model can learn effects attached to them.

## Mechanism

### Calendar parts

A timestamp splits into parts at several periods:

| Part                     | Values         | Captures                                        |
| ------------------------ | -------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Hour of day              | 0–23           | daily cycles: commuting, sleep, opening hours   |
| Weekday                  | Monday–Sunday  | weekly cycles: working days and weekends        |
| Day of month             | 1–31           | pay days, billing cycles, month-end effects     |
| Month or week of year    | 1–12 or 1–53   | annual cycles: weather, school terms, seasons   |
| Quarter, year            | 1–4, 2019, …   | reporting cycles; a coarse trend                |
| Working day, weekend     | 0 or 1         | the split that most weekly effects follow       |
| Days in month            | 28–31          | monthly totals that scale with the month length |

Derived flags are often more useful than the parts themselves: the last working day of a month, the first weekend
after pay day, or the number of each weekday in a month. Monthly totals depend on how many trading days a month has,
and counting the Mondays, Tuesdays and so on in each month captures this <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

How a part is encoded decides what shape its effect can take. Indicator columns, one per value after a reference value,
let every weekday have its own level; see <NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">categorical encoding</NoteLink>. A sine
and cosine of the phase within the period give a smooth effect in a few columns and place December next to January;
see <NoteLink to="cyclical-encoding">cyclical encoding</NoteLink>. A part entered as a single number, such as the
weekday as 0 to 6, forces a straight-line effect from the first value to the last.

### Holidays and events

A **holiday indicator** is 1 on the days of a holiday and 0 otherwise. Holiday effects often spread over several
days: shopping rises before Christmas and falls after it, and travel peaks on the days around a long weekend. A window
of indicators, one for each day from $a$ days before to $b$ days after the holiday, lets each of those days have its
own effect, as in the Prophet forecasting model <Cite id="taylor2018" />. Moving holidays such as Easter fall on
different dates each year and must come from a calendar, not from a fixed month and day. With monthly data, a holiday
that spans two months is split between them in proportion to its days <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

Other known events are encoded the same way: promotions, product launches, sporting finals and school terms. A
**spike** indicator marks a single period; a **step** indicator is 0 before an event and 1 from it onwards and models a
permanent change of level <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

### Elapsed time

An **elapsed-time feature** measures the gap between the record's time and another event:

- **Time since** an earlier event: days since the customer's last purchase, hours since a machine's last service,
  the age of an account. Recency is often the strongest single predictor of whether an event repeats.
- **Time until** a known future event: days until the next holiday, hours until a scheduled departure. Such a feature
  is legitimate only when the future event is known at the moment of prediction.
- **Time since the start** of the data: a trend term $t$, in days or months. A model extrapolates a trend term beyond
  the training range, so its coefficient must be trusted only as far as the trend itself.

Elapsed times are often skewed, with a few very long gaps. A logarithm or a cap keeps them from dominating a linear
fit; aggregates of event histories over windows are in
<NoteLink to="relational-feature-aggregation">relational and aggregate features</NoteLink>.

### Time zones and local time

Behaviour follows local time: people commute at 8 am where they live, not at 8 am in the time zone of the server.
Timestamps should be stored in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) and converted to the local time of the place where the
event happened before calendar parts are extracted. The conversion uses a time zone database such as the IANA database,
which records each region's offsets and daylight-saving rules <Cite id="ianatz" />. Daylight-saving changes create a
day with 23 hours and a day with 25; hourly counts on those days need care, and an hour of day computed in UTC shifts by
one hour against local behaviour twice a year.

The elapsed days alone fit the trend and leave the cycle in the residuals. The weekday as a single number 0 to 6 fits a
straight line across the week, which cannot follow a cycle that rises and falls. Six weekday indicators give each day
its own level; with the default settings the held-out <Gloss name="rmse" /> falls from about 0.87 with the elapsed days
alone, and about 0.51 with the weekday as a number, to about 0.32, close to the noise standard deviation of 0.3. The
weekly shape here has two harmonics; a single sine-cosine pair would capture only the first of them.

## Cost

- **Columns.** Indicators for hour of day and weekday add 23 and 6 columns; their interaction adds another 138. A smooth
  encoding needs two columns per harmonic.
- **Calendars.** Holiday and event features need a maintained calendar for every region the data cover, including
  future dates for forecasting.
- **Time zones.** Converting each record to local time needs the location of the event, which is not always recorded.

## Trade-offs

- **Indicators against smooth encodings.** Indicators fit any shape across the values of a part but need one column
  per value and enough data per value. Sine-cosine pairs share strength between neighbouring values and need few
  columns, but a sharp effect, such as a single holiday or a step at the weekend, needs many harmonics.
- **Calendar features against lag features.** A model that sees last week's value at the same time already carries much
  of the weekly pattern; see <NoteLink to="time-series-feature-extraction">time-series feature extraction</NoteLink>.
  Calendar features still carry effects that lags miss, such as holidays that fall on different weekdays each year.
- **Year as a category against a trend.** The year as indicators fits each past year exactly and cannot predict an
  unseen year. A trend term extrapolates, at the risk of extrapolating too far.

## Pitfalls

- **Future information in a feature.** "Days until the customer cancels" is known only after the fact. Every
  time-until feature must refer to an event scheduled before the prediction time; see
  <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>.
- **Mixed time zones.** Records from several regions with timestamps in UTC give an hour-of-day feature that means
  different things in different regions.
- **Unseen values at prediction time.** A month or year indicator that never appeared in training has no fitted
  coefficient; the encoder must decide what to do with it.
- **Regional calendars.** Holidays, weekends and working weeks differ between countries; a single calendar applied to
  global data mislabels days.
- **Random splits of time-ordered data.** Calendar features evaluated on a random split let the model learn a holiday
  from the same holiday's other days; evaluate forward in time, as in
  <NoteLink to="forecasting-setups">forecasting setups</NoteLink>.
