<Definition>
**Corpus statistics** are the empirical quantitative distributions and scaling laws characterizing a text collection
$\mathcal{D} = \{d_1, \dots, d_N\}$. They encompass document length distributions, sublinear vocabulary growth rates,
power-law term frequencies, repetitive n-gram redundancies, semantic topic mixtures, and language proportions across
sources.
</Definition>

Before pre-training or fine-tuning language models, constructing retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) vector stores, or
fitting NLP classifiers, practitioners must audit the macroscopic statistical properties of their text corpus. Raw web
crawls and enterprise document dumps contain structural pathologies: massive context window truncation loss, web
boilerplate repetition, skewed token frequencies, and low-resource language contamination that silently degrades
downstream generative quality and retrieval accuracy <Cite id="raffel2020, brown2020" />.

## Sequence lengths and context window economics

Document lengths in natural language corpora (measured in characters, words, or subword tokens) follow heavy-tailed,
right-skewed lognormal or Pareto-like distributions.

### Transformer context trade-offs

In transformer models, the maximum input context length $L_{\max}$ represents a hard engineering boundary:
1. **Truncation loss**: If $L_{\max} = 1024$ and $18\%$ of documents exceed 1024 tokens, standard truncation discards
   the trailing portions of texts—frequently cutting off conclusions, references, and critical downstream context.
2. **Padding waste**: Setting $L_{\max} = 8192$ when $90\%$ of documents contain fewer than $300$ tokens leads to
   extreme computation and memory waste in attention operations ($O(L^2)$ in standard attention) unless efficient
   packed-sequence batching or flash attention masking is used.

### Subword tokenizer fertility

Sequence length depends heavily on the subword tokenizer (e.g. Byte-Pair Encoding <Cite id="sennrich2016" /> or
WordPiece). **Fertility** is defined as the average number of subword tokens produced per source whitespace word:

$$
\text{Fertility} = \frac{|\text{tokens}|}{|\text{words}|}
$$

While English typically achieves a compact fertility ratio of $1.2$ to $1.4$ tokens per word, languages with
agglutinative morphology or non-Latin scripts (such as Hindi, Arabic, or Korean) frequently experience fertilities
exceeding $3.5$ tokens per word in English-centric vocabularies. Corpus profiling measures fertility across languages to
identify tokenization inefficiency and inflated inference costs.

## Vocabulary growth and Zipfian scaling laws

Natural language vocabulary statistics are governed by fundamental empirical power laws.

### Zipf's law

**Zipf's law** states that the frequency $f(r)$ of a word is inversely proportional to its frequency rank $r$
<Cite id="zipf1949" />:

$$
f(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^s}, \quad s \approx 1.0
$$

| Vocabulary Tier | Rank Range | Cumulative Token Mass | Typical Constituents | Preprocessing Considerations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **High-Frequency Head** | $r \le 100$ | $40\%\text{--}50\%$ | Function words (`the`, `of`, `and`) | Subsampled or masked during embedding training |
| **Mid-Frequency Core** | $100 < r \le 10^4$ | $35\%\text{--}45\%$ | Domain nouns, verbs, descriptive predicates | Forms stable representations across architectures |
| **Long-Tail Tail** | $r > 10^4$ | $5\%\text{--}15\%$ | Rare names, jargon, *hapax legomena* (singletons) | Addressed via subword tokenizers (BPE, WordPiece) |

- **The head**: A handful of grammatical function words (stopwords) account for over $30\%$ to $50\%$ of all observed
  token occurrences.
- **The tail (*hapax legomena*)**: Approximately $40\%$ to $60\%$ of all unique vocabulary words appear exactly once in
  the entire corpus. In word-level tokenizers, this tail creates prohibitive out-of-vocabulary (OOV) rates.

### Heaps' law

**Heaps' law** describes the growth of unique vocabulary size $V(N)$ (distinct types) as a function of total running
token count $N$ <Cite id="heaps1978" />:

$$
V(N) = k N^\beta
$$

Where typical empirical parameters are $k \in [10, 100]$ and $\beta \in [0.4, 0.6]$. Because $\beta > 0$, the vocabulary
never saturates—no matter how many billions of tokens are ingested, new entities, neologisms, and misspellings continue
to appear. This sublinear growth necessitates fixed subword vocabularies (e.g., 32,000 to 128,000 BPE merge operations)
that guarantee finite vocabulary size while preserving open-vocabulary coverage <Cite id="sennrich2016" />.

## Frequent n-grams, repetition, and boilerplate

Text crawled from the public web (such as Common Crawl) is heavily polluted with duplicate boilerplate: cookie banners,
navigation sidebars, copyright notices, terms-of-service footers, and SEO link farms <Cite id="raffel2020" />.

### Higher-order n-gram redundancy

Corpus profiling extracts frequent $n$-grams ($n \in \{3, 4, 5, 8\}$) across documents:
- If an 8-gram such as `"all rights reserved click here to accept cookies"` appears across $500,000$ distinct documents,
  it represents non-informative web boilerplate that should be filtered.
- **Line and paragraph exact deduplication**: Studies in language model pre-training show that removing duplicated
  paragraphs drastically reduces model memorization and improves validation perplexity <Cite id="brown2020" />.

### Repetition loops and n-gram entropy

To detect degenerate text (such as crawler infinite loops or OCR scanning artifacts), compute Shannon entropy over local
$n$-gram distributions:

$$
H_n = -\sum_{\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{V}^n} p(\mathbf{w}) \log_2 p(\mathbf{w})
$$

Text segments with abnormally low $n$-gram entropy ($H_n \ll 2.0$) or high compression ratios indicate repetitive
synthetic spam or corrupted machine output that must be quarantined.

## Topic distributions and semantic diversity

A robust corpus must maintain diverse conceptual coverage across its target domains.

| Data Source Domain | Typical Allocation | Linguistic & Knowledge Role | Key Filtering Gate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Common Crawl Web** | $60\%$ | Broad general knowledge, informal syntax | Quality classifier and MinHash dedup |
| **Source Code Repos** | $15\%$ | Algorithmic logic, formal code syntax | Permissive licenses and syntax validation |
| **Academic Papers (arXiv)** | $10\%$ | Technical depth, rigorous math prose | TeX conversion and formula sanity |
| **Encyclopedias (Wikipedia)** | $5\%$ | Fact-grounded encyclopedic consensus | Structured text extract and vandalism checks |
| **Curated Books** | $5\%$ | Long-horizon narrative coherence | OCR repair and boilerplate stripping |
| **Dialogue & QA** | $5\%$ | Conversational interaction and instruction | Safety filters and toxic turn suppression |

Using unsupervised topic models like Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) <Cite id="blei2003" /> or semantic embedding
clustering, profiling tracks the Dirichlet topic proportions
$\boldsymbol{\theta}_d \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ across documents. This verifies whether the corpus is
dominated by specific narrow categories (e.g., $70\%$ commercial product advertisements) rather than balanced technical,
conversational, and factual prose <Cite id="brown2020" />.

## Language identification and source mix

Multilingual corpus profiling uses fast identification models (such as FastText or CLD3) to classify the primary
language $\ell \in \mathcal{L}$ of each document:

1. **Low-resource contamination**: Web crawls often misclassify gibberish, code snippets, or machine-translated junk as
   low-resource languages. Filtering by identification confidence score ($p(\ell) > 0.85$) prevents toxic synthetic
   noise from corrupting multilingual models.
2. **Code-switching and mixed scripts**: Technical forums and conversational chats frequently mix English technical
   terms with other languages, requiring multilingual tokenizers that handle script shifts gracefully.
3. **Source provenance**: Profiling maintains clear accounting of data provenance (e.g., percentage web crawl vs
   peer-reviewed papers vs curated encyclopedias), tracking domain shifts and licensing constraints
   <Cite id="raffel2020" />.

## Diagnostic checklist

To profile a text collection before model training or retrieval indexing:

1. **Measure length distributions**: Plot token sequence length histograms; quantify truncation loss at target context
   boundaries $L_{\max}$ <Cite id="raffel2020" />.
2. **Compute tokenizer fertility**: Calculate tokens-per-word across languages; check for script inefficiencies
   <Cite id="sennrich2016" />.
3. **Verify Zipfian slope**: Graph log-frequency vs log-rank; verify $s \approx 1.0$ and measure hapax legomena volume
   <Cite id="zipf1949" />.
4. **Fit Heaps' scaling law**: Track vocabulary growth against total ingested tokens; confirm sublinear scaling exponent
   $\beta \in [0.4, 0.6]$ <Cite id="heaps1978" />.
5. **Screen top n-grams for boilerplate**: Identify repetitive navigation headers, legal notices, and web disclaimers
   for automated removal.
6. **Audit language classification**: Run language ID filters; flag low-confidence documents and assess source domain
   balance <Cite id="brown2020" />.
