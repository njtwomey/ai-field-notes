<Definition>
**Routine data checks** are a short list of inspections run on every dataset before a model is chosen. Each check is
cheap, takes minutes, and targets a failure that summary statistics hide: corrupted records, a degenerate or leaking
target, a pipeline outage, a subgroup with different behaviour, or a trivial solution. Their results shape the split,
the cleaning and the baseline.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Many failures of deployed models trace back to something visible in the data at the start. A column of identical
timestamps, a label that is constant for a month, a feature that alone predicts the target perfectly, a subgroup with
twice the error rate: each shows up within minutes of looking and can take weeks to find from model metrics alone.
Aggregates hide them because very different datasets share the same means, variances and correlations
<Cite id="anscombe1973" />. The checks also have a cost. Looking at the test rows, or trying many analyses and reporting
the best, biases every later estimate <Cite id="gelman2013forking" />.

## Main ideas

**Look at individual rows.** <NoteLink to="reading-raw-examples" /> reads examples at random, at the extremes of each
feature and, once a model exists, in order of loss. Encoding errors, templated junk, mislabelled cases and impossible
values are found this way.

**Profile the target first.** <NoteLink to="target-exploration" /> measures class balance or tail shape, zero-inflation,
the target rate over time and by group, and the predictive power of each feature on its own. A single feature with
near-perfect power usually signals leakage.

**Look along time and order.** <NoteLink to="temporal-profiling" /> plots row counts, feature summaries and target
rates by period and by position in the file. Outages, late backfills, calibration jumps and rows sorted by label all
appear as steps or trends.

**Compare slices.** <NoteLink to="slice-analysis" /> breaks distributions and errors down by subgroup. A pooled average
can hide a failing subgroup, or reverse a relation as in <NoteLink to="simpsons-paradox">Simpson's paradox</NoteLink>.
Thin slices give noisy estimates that need shrinkage.

**Use a simple model as an instrument.** <NoteLink to="baseline-probes" /> fits a quick baseline. Its residuals point at
errors and structure. A version trained on shuffled labels should score at chance, and single-feature versions expose
trivial solutions.

**Explore without contaminating evaluation.** <NoteLink to="exploration-hygiene" /> keeps test rows out of exploration
and fixes the key checks in advance, which limits the <NoteLink to="many-metrics-and-variants">garden of forking
paths</NoteLink>.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="reading-raw-examples" />, the cheapest check. <NoteLink to="target-exploration" />,
<NoteLink to="temporal-profiling" /> and <NoteLink to="slice-analysis" /> each examine one axis of the data and can be
read in any order. <NoteLink to="baseline-probes" /> uses a model and comes after them.
<NoteLink to="exploration-hygiene" /> applies to all the others and is best read before exploring real data.

The section opens <NoteLink to="exploration-and-discovery" />. Systematic column summaries are in
<NoteLink to="data-profiling" />, and plots and projections in <NoteLink to="visual-exploration" />.
