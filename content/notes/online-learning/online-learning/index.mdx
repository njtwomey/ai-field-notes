<Definition>
**Online learning** is a repeated game between a learner and an environment. In each round $t = 1, \dots, T$ the learner
chooses a decision $\wvec_t$ from a set $\Wcal$, the environment reveals a <Gloss name="loss-function">loss
function</Gloss> $f_t : \Wcal \to \reals$, and the learner pays $f_t(\wvec_t)$. The learner is judged by its **regret**
against a comparator $\uvec \in \Wcal$,

$$
R_T(\uvec) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_t(\wvec_t) - \sum_{t=1}^{T} f_t(\uvec),
$$

and by $R_T = \sup_{\uvec \in \Wcal} R_T(\uvec)$. An algorithm is **no-regret** (or **Hannan consistent**) if
$R_T / T \to 0$ for every loss sequence the environment is allowed to choose <Cite id="cesabianchi2006" />.
</Definition>

## The protocol

Each round has the same three steps.

1. The learner picks $\wvec_t \in \Wcal$, possibly at random, using only $f_1, \dots, f_{t-1}$.
2. The environment picks $f_t$.
3. The learner pays $f_t(\wvec_t)$ and observes feedback about $f_t$.

The setting fixes four things: the decision set $\Wcal$, the class of losses $f_t$ may come from, how the environment
chooses them, and what the learner observes. A supervised learner fits the protocol with $\wvec_t$ its parameters and
$f_t(\wvec) = \ell(\wvec; \xvec_t, y_t)$ the loss on the example that arrives at round $t$. The example is revealed
after the prediction is made, so every round is also a test on an unseen point.

Online learning makes no statistical assumption about the sequence. A batch learner assumes the examples are drawn
independently from one distribution and bounds the risk on that distribution. An online learner bounds its cumulative
loss relative to a comparator, on the sequence that actually occurs. That guarantee survives drift, dependence and
deliberate manipulation. The price is that it is relative: the regret is small, but the comparator itself may have
large loss.

## Adversarial and stochastic environments

- **Stochastic.** The $f_t$ are drawn independently from a fixed distribution. Regret then relates directly to excess
  risk, and <NoteLink to="online-to-batch-conversion">online-to-batch conversion</NoteLink> turns a regret bound into
  a generalisation bound.
- **Oblivious adversary.** The whole sequence $f_1, \dots, f_T$ is fixed before play begins, and may be any sequence in
  the class. Randomised learners are analysed in <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over their own random
  choices.
- **Adaptive adversary.** $f_t$ may depend on the learner's past decisions $\wvec_1, \dots, \wvec_{t-1}$. Against a
  deterministic learner an adaptive adversary gains nothing over an oblivious one, because it can simulate the learner.

A deterministic learner that must pick one of finitely many actions cannot have sublinear regret. For two experts with
0/1 losses, the adversary gives loss 1 to whichever expert the learner is about to follow. The learner loses every
round, while the better of the two experts loses at most $T/2$, so the regret is at least $T/2$. Two remedies exist.
Convex losses over a <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex set</NoteLink> let the learner play a weighted
average, which is deterministic and still no-regret. Otherwise the learner randomises, and the adversary, having fixed
$f_t$ in advance, cannot know the draw. This observation goes back to Hannan <Cite id="hannan1957" />.

## Full information and bandit feedback

With **full information** the learner sees the whole function $f_t$, or at least its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> at $\wvec_t$. Every note in this topic assumes full information unless it
says otherwise. With **bandit feedback** the learner sees only the number $f_t(\wvec_t)$. Bandit problems need
exploration and pay for it in the rate: with $N$ actions the minimax regret is of order $\sqrt{TN}$ under bandit
feedback and $\sqrt{T\log N}$ under full information. The bandit setting has its own chapter, starting at the
<NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit" /> and the adversarial algorithm <NoteLink to="exp3">EXP3</NoteLink>. Partial
monitoring and label-efficient prediction lie between the two, and <NoteLink to="selective-sampling" /> is one instance.

## Notions of regret

Regret against a fixed comparator is the default, but the comparator class decides what the guarantee is worth.

- **Static (external) regret** compares with the best fixed decision in hindsight, $\min_{\uvec}\sum_t f_t(\uvec)$.
  It is the notion in <NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent" />, <NoteLink to="hedge-and-exponential-weights" /> and
  most of this topic.
- **Dynamic regret** compares with a sequence $\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_T$:
  $\sum_t f_t(\wvec_t) - \sum_t f_t(\uvec_t)$. No algorithm has small dynamic regret against every sequence, so the
  bound depends on how much the sequence moves, measured by the number of switches $m$ or the path length
  $\sum_t \norm{\uvec_{t+1} - \uvec_t}$. <NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" /> derives such a bound, of order
  $\sqrt{T m \log(NT)}$ for $N$ experts and $m$ switches <Cite id="herbster1998" />.
- **Adaptive regret** is the largest static regret over any contiguous interval,
  $\max_{[r, s]}\paren{\sum_{t=r}^{s} f_t(\wvec_t) - \min_{\uvec}\sum_{t=r}^{s} f_t(\uvec)}$. A learner with small
  adaptive regret does well on every stretch of the sequence, so it recovers quickly after a change
  <Cite id="hazan2009" />.
- **Mistake bounds** count errors of a classifier directly, without subtracting a comparator, when some expert or
  linear separator makes no mistakes. The <NoteLink to="perceptron" /> and the halving algorithm in
  <NoteLink to="weighted-majority" /> have mistake bounds <Cite id="littlestone1988" />.

Regret against the best fixed decision is weak when the best decision changes; dynamic and adaptive regret address
that. Regret against a very rich comparator class, such as all functions, cannot be sublinear; the comparator class
plays the role of the hypothesis class in batch learning.

## Components



- **<NoteLink to="online-convex-optimisation" />.** Convex losses over a convex set. <NoteLink
  to="online-gradient-descent" /> has regret $O(DG\sqrt{T})$, and $O(\log T)$ on strongly convex losses.
  <NoteLink to="follow-the-regularised-leader" /> is the unifying algorithm, <NoteLink
  to="online-newton-step-and-exp-concavity">the online Newton step</NoteLink> exploits exp-concavity, and <NoteLink
  to="adaptive-online-methods" /> tune their geometry to the data. <NoteLink to="online-to-batch-conversion" /> links
  regret to statistical risk.
- **Prediction with expert advice.** Combine $N$ forecasters. <NoteLink to="weighted-majority" /> has a mistake
  bound, <NoteLink to="hedge-and-exponential-weights">Hedge</NoteLink> has regret $\sqrt{(T\ln N)/2}$, and <NoteLink
  to="lower-bounds-for-online-learning" /> shows that rate cannot be improved. Mixable losses such as log loss give
  constant regret $\ln N$ with the <NoteLink to="aggregating-algorithm-and-mixability">aggregating
  algorithm</NoteLink>. <NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" /> handles a best expert that changes.
- **Streaming and incremental learning.** Practical learners that see each example once: <NoteLink
  to="online-learning-for-linear-models" />, <NoteLink to="sequential-bayesian-updating" />, <NoteLink
  to="concept-drift-adaptation" /> and <NoteLink to="streaming-algorithms-for-learning" /> such as reservoir sampling
  and Hoeffding trees.
- **Online active learning.** The label of each example costs something, and the learner decides whether to ask for
  it: <NoteLink to="selective-sampling" /> and <NoteLink to="stream-based-active-learning" />.
- **Continual learning.** A neural network trained on a sequence of tasks forgets earlier ones. <NoteLink
  to="continual-learning" /> sets out the scenarios, and <NoteLink to="regularisation-based-continual-learning" />,
  <NoteLink to="replay-and-architectural-methods" /> and <NoteLink to="evaluating-continual-learners" /> cover methods
  and metrics.

## Relation to neighbouring topics

- **Stochastic optimisation.** <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">Stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink>
  on an expected loss is online gradient descent on the sequence of sampled losses. Regret bounds, averaged over the
  sampling, give the SGD convergence rates.
- **<NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian inference</NoteLink>.** Under
  <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink>, the Bayesian predictive distribution is the
  aggregating algorithm, and its regret against the best model in the prior's support is at most minus the log prior
  mass of that model. <NoteLink
  to="sequential-bayesian-updating" /> treats the streaming form, and the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter" /> is its
  linear-Gaussian case with a moving state.
- **Game theory.** Two no-regret learners playing a zero-sum game against each other have average strategies that
  converge to a minimax equilibrium. Boosting is one application: <NoteLink to="adaboost" /> was derived from Hedge
  <Cite id="freund1997" />.
- **<NoteLink to="transfer-learning">Transfer learning</NoteLink>.** When a source model exists and target data arrive
  as a stream, the learner can combine the two with expert-advice methods: see
  <NoteLink to="online-transfer-learning" /> and <NoteLink
  to="test-time-adaptation" />. <NoteLink to="lifelong-and-continual-transfer" /> treats a sequence of tasks from the
  transfer side, where <NoteLink to="continual-learning" /> treats it from the side of forgetting.
- **Deployment.** <NoteLink to="dataset-shift" /> and <NoteLink to="drift-detection" /> describe the same
  non-stationarity from the monitoring side. <NoteLink to="concept-drift-adaptation" /> is the learning side.
