<Definition>
In **prediction with expert advice** for binary outcomes, each round $t$ every expert $i = 1, \dots, N$ predicts
$x_{t,i} \in \set{0, 1}$, the learner predicts $\hat y_t$, and then the outcome $y_t \in \set{0, 1}$ is revealed. The
**weighted majority algorithm** keeps weights $w_{t,i} > 0$, starting at $w_{1,i} = 1$. It predicts $1$ if the experts
predicting $1$ hold at least half the total weight, and $0$ otherwise. After $y_t$ is revealed, each expert that
predicted wrongly has its weight multiplied by a fixed $\beta \in [0, 1)$ <Cite id="littlestone1994" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

$M$ is the number of mistakes the learner makes over $T$ rounds, and $m_i$ the number made by expert $i$.
$m = \min_i m_i$ is the best expert's count. $W_t = \sum_i w_{t,i}$ is the total weight at the start of round $t$.
No assumption is made about how the outcomes or the experts' predictions are generated.

## The halving algorithm

Suppose one expert never errs ($m = 0$). The **halving algorithm** is the case $\beta = 0$: an expert that errs is
discarded, and the learner follows the majority of the experts still consistent with every outcome so far.

Each time the learner errs, the majority it followed was wrong, so at least half of the surviving experts are
discarded. The perfect expert is never discarded. After $M$ mistakes at most $N/2^M$ experts survive, and at least one
does, so $N/2^M \ge 1$:

$$
M \le \log_2 N.
$$

Twenty mistakes suffice to find the perfect expert among a million. The same argument, applied to a finite hypothesis
class, gives the mistake-bound model of learning <Cite id="littlestone1988" />.

## The mistake bound

When no expert is perfect, discarding is too harsh; weighted majority shrinks weights instead. The analysis tracks
the total weight $W_t$.

**Each mistake removes a fixed fraction of the weight.** When the learner errs, the experts that voted for the wrong
answer held at least half of $W_t$. Each of their weights is multiplied by $\beta$. So at least $W_t/2$ of the weight
shrinks by the factor $\beta$, and

$$
W_{t+1} \le \frac{W_t}{2} + \beta\frac{W_t}{2} = \frac{1 + \beta}{2}W_t.
$$

In rounds where the learner is right, $W_{t+1} \le W_t$. Starting from $W_1 = N$, after $M$ mistakes
$W_{T+1} \le N\paren{\tfrac{1 + \beta}{2}}^M$.

**The best expert keeps some weight.** Expert $i$ has been penalised $m_i$ times, so
$W_{T+1} \ge w_{T+1,i} = \beta^{m_i}$ for every $i$.

**Combine.** $\beta^{m_i} \le N\paren{\tfrac{1 + \beta}{2}}^M$. Taking logarithms and solving for $M$,

$$
M \le \frac{\ln N + m_i\ln(1/\beta)}{\ln\paren{2/(1 + \beta)}} \quad\text{for every expert } i.
$$

At $\beta = \tfrac12$, the denominator is $\ln(4/3) = 0.288$, and $\ln 2/\ln(4/3) = 2.41$, so
$M \le 2.41(m + \log_2 N)$ <Cite id="littlestone1994" />.

## The factor of two cannot be avoided deterministically

As $\beta \to 1$, $\ln(1/\beta)/\ln(2/(1 + \beta)) \to 2$, so the coefficient of $m$ approaches 2 but the $\ln N$ term
grows. No deterministic algorithm does better than $2m$. Take two experts that always disagree, one predicting 0 and
one predicting 1. The adversary knows the learner's deterministic prediction and sets the outcome to the opposite, so
$M = T$. Every round exactly one expert is wrong, so the two experts' mistakes sum to $T$, and the better one has
$m \le T/2$. Therefore $M \ge 2m$.

## Randomised weighted majority

Predicting with an expert drawn at random with probability $w_{t,i}/W_t$ removes the factor of two in expectation. Let
$F_t$ be the fraction of weight on experts that err in round $t$; it is the probability that the learner errs. The
weight update gives

$$
W_{t+1} = W_t\paren{1 - (1 - \beta)F_t} \le W_t\exp\paren{-(1 - \beta)F_t},
$$

so $\ln W_{T+1} \le \ln N - (1 - \beta)\sum_t F_t$. Combined with $\ln W_{T+1} \ge m\ln\beta$, the expected number of
mistakes is

$$
\expect[M] = \sum_{t=1}^{T}F_t \le \frac{m\ln(1/\beta) + \ln N}{1 - \beta}.
$$

With $\beta$ close to 1, $\ln(1/\beta)/(1 - \beta) \approx 1 + (1 - \beta)/2$, so the coefficient of $m$ approaches 1.
Tuning $\beta$ with knowledge of $m$ gives $\expect[M] \le m + O(\sqrt{m\ln N} + \ln N)$
<Cite id="littlestone1994" /> <Cite id="cesabianchi2006" />. The expected mistakes exceed the best expert's by a term
sublinear in $m$, which is a regret bound. The general form of this algorithm for losses in $[0, 1]$ is
<NoteLink to="hedge-and-exponential-weights">Hedge</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Take $N = 1024$ experts, the best of which makes $m = 20$ mistakes, and $\beta = \tfrac12$.

- Deterministic weighted majority: $M \le (\ln 1024 + 20\ln 2)/\ln(4/3) = (6.93 + 13.86)/0.288 = 72.3$.
- Randomised weighted majority: $\expect[M] \le (20\ln 2 + \ln 1024)/(1 - \tfrac12) = 41.6$.
- If some expert were perfect, halving would make at most $\log_2 1024 = 10$ mistakes.

## Assumptions

- The outcome is revealed after every prediction.
- The comparison is with the best single expert over the whole sequence. If different experts are best on different
  stretches, see <NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" />.
- The bound holds for every sequence of outcomes and expert predictions, including adversarial ones.

## Pitfalls

- **Tiny weights.** After many mistakes weights underflow in floating point. Store $\log w_{t,i}$, or renormalise every
  round.
- **Losing good experts for good.** A small $\beta$ drives a temporarily poor expert's weight so low that it cannot
  recover when it becomes the best. Fixed share, in <NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" />, bounds weights away
  from zero.
- **Mistake counts, not probabilities.** Weighted majority treats every error alike. When experts give probabilities
  and the loss is log loss, the <NoteLink to="aggregating-algorithm-and-mixability">aggregating algorithm</NoteLink>
  has a much smaller regret.
