<Definition>
In the **experts problem** a learner chooses, each round $t = 1, \dots, T$, a probability vector $\pvec_t$ over $N$
experts. The environment then reveals losses $\lvec_t = (\ell_{t,1}, \dots, \ell_{t,N}) \in [0, 1]^N$, and the learner
pays $\inner{\pvec_t}{\lvec_t}$, the expected loss of an expert drawn from $\pvec_t$. **Hedge** plays

$$
p_{t,i} = \frac{\exp(-\eta L_{t-1,i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(-\eta L_{t-1,j})}, \qquad L_{t,i} = \sum_{s=1}^{t}\ell_{s,i},
$$

with a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> $\eta > 0$ and $L_{0,i} = 0$ <Cite id="freund1997" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

$\hat L_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\inner{\pvec_t}{\lvec_t}$ is the learner's cumulative loss and $L_T^\star = \min_i L_{T,i}$ the
best expert's. The regret is $R_T = \hat L_T - L_T^\star$. The losses may be chosen by an adversary that sees
$\pvec_t$ before choosing $\lvec_t$; because the learner's loss is the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over $\pvec_t$, no randomness
enters the analysis.

## Intuition

Hedge is a softmax of the negated cumulative losses, $\pvec_t = \operatorname{softmax}(-\eta L_{t-1,1}, \dots, -\eta L_{t-1,N})$. The learning
rate sets how sharply it concentrates. As $\eta \to \infty$ it puts all mass on the current leader, which is
follow-the-leader and can be exploited (see <NoteLink to="follow-the-regularised-leader" />). As $\eta \to 0$ it stays
uniform and never learns. The analysis finds the $\eta$ that balances the two failures. Hedge generalises the
randomised <NoteLink to="weighted-majority" /> algorithm from mistakes to any loss in $[0, 1]$, with $\beta = e^{-\eta}$.

## The regret bound

Track the potential $W_t = \sum_{i}\exp(-\eta L_{t,i})$, with $W_0 = N$.

**Upper bound.** The ratio of successive potentials is an expectation under $\pvec_t$:

$$
\frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}} = \sum_{i}\frac{\exp(-\eta L_{t-1,i})}{W_{t-1}}\exp(-\eta\ell_{t,i}) = \sum_i p_{t,i}\exp(-\eta\ell_{t,i}).
$$

Let $X$ be the loss of an expert drawn from $\pvec_t$, a random variable in $[0, 1]$ with mean $\inner{\pvec_t}{\lvec_t}$.
**Hoeffding's lemma** states that $\ln\expect e^{sX} \le s\expect X + s^2/8$ for any real $s$ and any $X$ with values in
an interval of length 1 (see <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality" />). With $s = -\eta$,

$$
\ln\frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}} \le -\eta\inner{\pvec_t}{\lvec_t} + \frac{\eta^2}{8}.
$$

Summing over $t$: $\ln W_T - \ln N \le -\eta\hat L_T + T\eta^2/8$.

**Lower bound.** Every term of $W_T$ is positive, so $W_T \ge \exp(-\eta L_{T,i})$ for each expert $i$, and
$\ln W_T \ge -\eta L_T^\star$.

**Combine.** $-\eta L_T^\star - \ln N \le -\eta\hat L_T + T\eta^2/8$. Dividing by $\eta$,

$$
R_T = \hat L_T - L_T^\star \le \frac{\ln N}{\eta} + \frac{\eta T}{8}.
$$

The first term is the cost of starting uniform over $N$ experts; the second is the cost of reacting to each round's
losses. Their sum is smallest at $\eta = \sqrt{8\ln N/T}$, where

$$
R_T \le \sqrt{\frac{T\ln N}{2}}
$$

<Cite id="cesabianchi2006" />. The average regret per round falls as $\sqrt{\ln N/T}$. The number of experts enters
only logarithmically, so a million experts cost less than twice as much as forty. The constant is exactly right:
<NoteLink to="lower-bounds-for-online-learning" /> shows that random losses force regret
$(1 - o(1))\sqrt{(T\ln N)/2}$ on every algorithm.

## Not knowing the horizon

The tuned $\eta$ needs $T$. Two fixes exist.

**The doubling trick.** Split time into epochs $m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ of length $2^m$, and run Hedge afresh in each epoch
with $\eta$ tuned to its length. If the game ends during epoch $M$, then $2^M \le T$ and the regret is at most the sum
of the epoch bounds:

$$
\sum_{m=0}^{M}\sqrt{\frac{2^m\ln N}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{\ln N}{2}}\cdot\frac{2^{(M+1)/2} - 1}{\sqrt2 - 1}
\le \frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2 - 1}\sqrt{\frac{T\ln N}{2}}.
$$

The regret against the best expert overall is at most the sum of the per-epoch regrets, because the best expert
overall is no better within each epoch than that epoch's best. The price is a factor $\sqrt2/(\sqrt2 - 1) = 3.41$
<Cite id="cesabianchi1997" />.

**An anytime rate.** Use $\eta_t = \sqrt{8\ln N/t}$ in round $t$, without restarts. The regret is then at most
$2\sqrt{(T\ln N)/2} + \sqrt{\ln N/8}$ for every $T$, a factor of 2 over the tuned bound <Cite id="cesabianchi2006" />.

## Worked example

Take $N = 100$ experts and $T = 10\,000$ rounds. The tuned rate is $\eta = \sqrt{8\ln 100/10\,000} = 0.061$. The regret
bound is $\sqrt{10\,000 \cdot 4.605/2} = 151.7$, so the average loss per round exceeds the best expert's by at most
$0.015$. The doubling trick raises the bound to $3.41 \times 151.7 = 518$ and the anytime rate to
$2 \times 151.7 + 0.76 = 304$.

## Solving zero-sum games

Let $\Amat$ be an $N \times K$ matrix of losses in $[0, 1]$ for a two-player game: the row player picks a
distribution $\pvec$, the column player picks $\qvec$, and the row player pays $\pvec^\top\Amat\qvec$. Let the row
player run Hedge on the loss vectors $\Amat\qvec_t$, and the column player run Hedge on the gains
$\Amat^\top\pvec_t$, each with regret at most $R_T$. Write $\bar\pvec$ and $\bar\qvec$ for the average strategies and
$\bar v = \tfrac1T\sum_t\pvec_t^\top\Amat\qvec_t$ for the average payment. The two regret bounds are

$$
\bar v \le \min_{\pvec}\pvec^\top\Amat\bar\qvec + \frac{R_T}{T}, \qquad
\bar v \ge \max_{\qvec}\bar\pvec^\top\Amat\qvec - \frac{R_T}{T}.
$$

Hence $\max_{\qvec}\bar\pvec^\top\Amat\qvec \le \min_{\pvec}\pvec^\top\Amat\bar\qvec + 2R_T/T$. Since
$\min_{\pvec}\max_{\qvec} \ge \max_{\qvec}\min_{\pvec}$ always holds, and $R_T/T \to 0$, this proves von Neumann's
minimax theorem, and $\bar\pvec$ is a $2R_T/T$-optimal strategy <Cite id="freund1997" />. <NoteLink
to="adaboost" /> is this construction with examples as rows and weak hypotheses as columns.

## Relation to other algorithms

- **Follow the regularised leader** with the negative entropy on the simplex gives the same weights.
  <NoteLink to="follow-the-regularised-leader" /> derives the bound $2\sqrt{T\ln N}$ from that view; the direct analysis
  above is four times tighter.
- **Mirror descent** with the entropy mirror map, the exponentiated gradient update, is Hedge with the loss vector
  replaced by a gradient (see <NoteLink to="mirror-descent" />).
- **Bandit feedback.** When only the chosen expert's loss is seen, <NoteLink to="exp3">EXP3</NoteLink> runs Hedge on
  importance-weighted loss estimates and pays an extra $\sqrt{N}$ in the regret.

## Assumptions

- Losses lie in $[0, 1]$. For losses in $[a, b]$, rescale; the bound scales by $b - a$.
- The loss is linear in $\pvec_t$: the learner either randomises over experts and is judged in expectation, or
  combines the experts' predictions with a loss that is convex in the combination. In the convex case, predicting the
  weighted average of the experts' predictions satisfies the same bound, by Jensen's inequality.
- The comparator is the best single expert for the whole sequence.

## Pitfalls

- **Overflow.** $\exp(-\eta L_{t,i})$ underflows for large cumulative losses. Subtract $\min_j L_{t,j}$ before
  exponentiating (<NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp" />).
- **Stuck weights.** An expert that was poor early keeps a weight near zero long after it becomes the best. Hedge's
  regret against a sequence of experts can be linear; use fixed share (<NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" />).
- **Worst-case tuning on easy data.** When the best expert has small loss $L_T^\star$, a larger $\eta$ does better; the
  bound in the derivation below adapts to $L_T^\star$.

<Derivation title="A bound that improves when the best expert is good">
For $X \in [0, 1]$, convexity of $x \mapsto e^{-\eta x}$ gives $e^{-\eta X} \le 1 - (1 - e^{-\eta})X$, so
$\ln\expect e^{-\eta X} \le \ln\paren{1 - (1 - e^{-\eta})\expect X} \le -(1 - e^{-\eta})\expect X$. Using this in place
of Hoeffding's lemma in the upper bound, and the same lower bound,

$$
-\eta L_T^\star - \ln N \le -(1 - e^{-\eta})\hat L_T, \qquad \hat L_T \le \frac{\eta L_T^\star + \ln N}{1 - e^{-\eta}}.
$$

With $\eta$ tuned to $L_T^\star$ this gives $\hat L_T \le L_T^\star + \sqrt{2L_T^\star\ln N} + \ln N$
<Cite id="cesabianchi2006" />. When some expert is nearly perfect, $L_T^\star \approx 0$, the regret is $O(\ln N)$
instead of $O(\sqrt{T\ln N})$, as for the halving algorithm.
</Derivation>
