<Definition>
A **regularisation-based** continual learner trains on task $k$ by minimising the task's loss plus a penalty
$\Omega_k(\thetavec)$ that is small when $\thetavec$ still serves earlier tasks. **Elastic weight consolidation** (EWC)
uses, after task A and while training on task B,

$$
\Lcal_B(\thetavec) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_iF_{A,i}\paren{\theta_i - \theta_{A,i}^\star}^2,
$$

where $\thetavec_A^\star$ are the weights learnt on task A, $F_{A,i}$ is the $i$-th diagonal entry of the Fisher information
of task A's model at $\thetavec_A^\star$, and $\lambda \ge 0$ sets the strength <Cite id="kirkpatrick2017" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

$\Dcal_A$ and $\Dcal_B$ are the training sets of two consecutive tasks, of sizes $N_A$ and $N_B$. The model defines
$p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)$, and $\Lcal_B(\thetavec) = -\sum_{(\xvec, y) \in \Dcal_B}\log p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)$ is the
negative log-likelihood of task B. The data of task A are not available while task B is trained.

## Elastic weight consolidation as approximate Bayes

**Exact sequential inference.** If both tasks were modelled by one posterior, <NoteLink to="sequential-bayesian-updating" />
would give

$$
\log p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal_A, \Dcal_B) = \log p(\Dcal_B \mid \thetavec) + \log p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal_A) - \log p(\Dcal_B \mid \Dcal_A).
$$

The first term is $-\Lcal_B$. The last does not depend on $\thetavec$. All that task A contributes is its posterior
$p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal_A)$, which acts as the prior for task B. The data of task A are not needed, only this function.

**The Laplace approximation.** The posterior of a neural network is intractable. The
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation" /> replaces it by a Gaussian at its mode $\thetavec_A^\star$, with precision equal to
the Hessian of the negative log posterior there:
$\log p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal_A) \approx \text{const} - \tfrac12(\thetavec - \thetavec_A^\star)^\top\Hmat_A(\thetavec - \thetavec_A^\star)$.
Maximising the approximate posterior of both tasks is then minimising

$$
\Lcal_B(\thetavec) + \frac12(\thetavec - \thetavec_A^\star)^\top\Hmat_A(\thetavec - \thetavec_A^\star).
$$

**Hessian to Fisher.** For a model $p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)$, the expected score is zero:
$\expect_{y \sim p}[\nabla\log p] = \int\nabla p\,dy = \nabla\int p\,dy = \zeros$. Differentiating once more under the integral,

$$
\zeros = \int\paren{\nabla p\,\nabla\log p^\top + p\,\nabla^2\log p}dy
= \expect_{y \sim p}\brack{\nabla\log p\,\nabla\log p^\top} + \expect_{y \sim p}\brack{\nabla^2\log p}.
$$

So the expected Hessian of the negative log-likelihood equals the **Fisher information**
$\Fmat(\thetavec) = \expect\brack{\nabla\log p\,\nabla\log p^\top}$. Summed over task A's inputs, $\Hmat_A \approx N_A\bar\Fmat_A$,
plus the prior's precision, where $\bar\Fmat_A$ is the average Fisher per example. The Fisher needs only first
derivatives and is positive semidefinite, whereas the Hessian of a network can be indefinite.

**Diagonal.** A network has millions of weights, so $\Fmat$ is replaced by its diagonal. The penalty becomes the EWC
term, with $\lambda$ absorbing $N_A$ and tuned on validation data. Each weight is anchored by a spring of stiffness
$\lambda F_{A,i}$: weights to which task A's predictions are sensitive are held close, and the others are free
<Cite id="kirkpatrick2017" />.

## Worked example

Take quadratic tasks in two weights: task A's loss $\tfrac12(\thetavec - \avec)^\top\Hmat_A(\thetavec - \avec)$ with
$\avec = (1, 1)$ and $\Hmat_A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 2\end{pmatrix}$, and task B's
$\tfrac12(\thetavec - \bvec)^\top\Hmat_B(\thetavec - \bvec)$ with $\bvec = (-1, 0.5)$ and $\Hmat_B = \diag(1, 0.2)$. The EWC
minimiser solves $(\Hmat_B + \lambda\Fmat)\thetavec = \Hmat_B\bvec + \lambda\Fmat\avec$.

- Without the penalty ($\lambda = 0$), training on B moves the weights to $\bvec$, where task A's loss is $5.75$.
- With the exact curvature $\Fmat = \Hmat_A$ and $\lambda = 1$, the weights are $(0.023, 1.621)$, with losses $0.430$ on A
  and $0.649$ on B. This is the joint minimiser of the two losses: for quadratic losses the Laplace approximation is
  exact.
- With the diagonal $\Fmat = \diag(2, 2)$ and $\lambda = 1$, the weights are $(0.333, 0.955)$, with losses $0.492$ and
  $0.910$. The summed loss is $1.402$ against $1.079$: ignoring the correlation between the weights costs about 30%.
- With the exact curvature and $\lambda = 10$, task A's loss is $0.017$ but task B's is $1.669$: the model barely learns B.

## Several tasks

Kirkpatrick and co-authors add one penalty per earlier task, each centred on that task's solution with its own
Fisher. Huszár points out that the recursive Bayesian argument gives something different: after task B, the posterior
of A and B together is again approximated at its mode $\thetavec_{AB}^\star$, and its precision is the sum of the two
tasks' Fishers. A single quadratic penalty, centred on the latest solution with accumulated Fisher, is the consistent
approximation <Cite id="huszar2018" />. It also costs constant memory, where one penalty per task grows linearly. This
form is usually called online EWC.

## Synaptic intelligence

EWC measures importance at the end of a task, from local curvature. **Synaptic intelligence** (SI) measures it along
the whole training path <Cite id="zenke2017" />. During training the loss changes by

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec(t_{\text{end}})) - \Lcal(\thetavec(t_0)) = \int\nabla\Lcal\cdot d\thetavec = \sum_i\int g_i\,d\theta_i,
$$

so each weight has a share $\omega_i = -\sum_tg_i(t)\,\Delta\theta_i(t)$ of the loss decrease, accumulated over the
optimiser's steps at no extra cost. At the end of task $k$ the importance of weight $i$ is
$\Omega_i \leftarrow \Omega_i + \omega_i/(\Delta_i^2 + \xi)$, where $\Delta_i$ is how far the weight moved during the task and
$\xi > 0$ avoids division by zero. The penalty is $c\sum_i\Omega_i(\theta_i - \tilde\theta_i)^2$, with $\tilde\theta_i$ the
weight at the end of the previous task. A weight that moved little but reduced the loss much is important.

## Learning without forgetting

EWC and SI constrain weights. **Learning without forgetting** (LwF) constrains outputs instead <Cite id="li2018b" />.
Before training on the new task, record the old model's output probabilities $\hat\yvec_o$ for its old heads on the new
task's inputs. Then train on

$$
\Lcal_{\text{new}}(\thetavec) + \lambda_o\,\Lcal_{\text{distill}}(\hat\yvec_o, \yvec_o(\thetavec)),
$$

where $\yvec_o(\thetavec)$ are the current outputs of the old heads and $\Lcal_{\text{distill}}$ is the cross-entropy between
temperature-softened distributions, as in <NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation" /> <Cite id="hinton2015" />. No old data are
stored: the new inputs serve as probes of the old function. The protection is only as good as the overlap between the
new inputs and the old input distribution.

## Assumptions

- EWC and SI assume that a quadratic around the old solution describes the old loss well enough over the distance the
  weights will move. Large moves leave the region where the approximation holds.
- The diagonal approximation assumes weight importances are independent; correlated weights, as in the example, are
  handled poorly.
- Task boundaries are known, so importances can be computed and anchors reset at the end of each task.

## Pitfalls

- **Empirical Fisher.** Computing the Fisher with the observed labels, $\nabla\log p(y_{\text{obs}} \mid \xvec)$, instead of
  labels sampled from the model gives a different matrix, which is not the expected Hessian. It is common in code and
  changes which weights are protected.
- **Class-incremental failure.** In class-incremental scenarios, EWC, SI and LwF retain little: the penalties keep
  features, but nothing stops the output layer from favouring the newest classes. Replay does much better there
  <Cite id="vandeven2019" />.
- **Tuning $\lambda$ with future tasks.** Choosing $\lambda$ by the final accuracy over all tasks uses data from tasks the
  learner was not supposed to see. Tune on a separate sequence of tasks.
