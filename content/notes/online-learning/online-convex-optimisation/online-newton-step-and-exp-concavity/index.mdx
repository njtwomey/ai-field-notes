<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> $f : \Wcal \to \reals$ is **$\alpha$-exp-concave**
for $\alpha > 0$ if $\wvec \mapsto e^{-\alpha f(\wvec)}$ is concave on $\Wcal$. The **online Newton step** (ONS) plays

$$
\Amat_t = \epsilon\I + \sum_{s=1}^{t}\gvec_s\gvec_s^\top, \qquad
\wvec_{t+1} = \Pi_\Wcal^{\Amat_t}\paren{\wvec_t - \tfrac{1}{\gamma}\Amat_t^{-1}\gvec_t},
$$

where $\gvec_t = \nabla f_t(\wvec_t)$, $\gamma, \epsilon > 0$ are parameters, and
$\Pi_\Wcal^{\Amat}(\vvec) = \argmin_{\wvec \in \Wcal}(\wvec - \vvec)^\top\Amat(\wvec - \vvec)$ is the projection in the
norm induced by $\Amat$ <Cite id="hazan2007" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

$\Wcal \subseteq \reals^d$ is closed and convex with diameter $D$, and $\norm{\nabla f_t(\wvec)} \le G$ on $\Wcal$.
$R_T(\uvec) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{f_t(\wvec_t) - f_t(\uvec)}$ is the regret against $\uvec \in \Wcal$.

## Exp-concave losses

Exp-concavity is a <NoteLink to="line-search">curvature condition</NoteLink> that is weaker than strong convexity. It
asks for curvature only in the direction of the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>.

- **<NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">Log loss</NoteLink> of a linear model.**
  $f(\wvec) = -\log\inner{\wvec}{\rvec}$ with $\inner{\wvec}{\rvec} > 0$ has $e^{-f(\wvec)} = \inner{\wvec}{\rvec}$,
  which is linear and hence concave. So $f$ is $1$-exp-concave. It is not strongly convex: its Hessian
  $\rvec\rvec^\top/\inner{\wvec}{\rvec}^2$ has rank one. This is the loss of
  <NoteLink to="online-convex-optimisation">portfolio selection</NoteLink>, where $\rvec$ holds price ratios
  <Cite id="cover1991" />.
- **Squared loss on a bounded range.** For $h(p) = e^{-\alpha(p - y)^2}$,
  $h''(p) = e^{-\alpha s^2}(4\alpha^2 s^2 - 2\alpha)$ with $s = p - y$. This is non-positive when $s^2 \le 1/(2\alpha)$.
  So $(p - y)^2$ is $\alpha$-exp-concave in $p$ with $\alpha = 1/(2B^2)$ whenever $\abs{p - y} \le B$, and so is
  $(\inner{\wvec}{\xvec} - y)^2$ in $\wvec$, since composing with a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear
  map</NoteLink> preserves concavity.
- **Strongly convex losses.** A $\lambda$-strongly convex $f$ with gradients bounded by $G$ is
  $(\lambda/G^2)$-exp-concave.

The regret rate on exp-concave losses is $O(d\log T)$, logarithmic like strongly convex losses but with a factor of
the dimension. The factor $d$ arises because the curvature exists only along the observed gradients, so the algorithm
must learn which directions are curved.

## The key inequality

Exp-concavity gives a quadratic lower bound built from the gradient alone.

**Lemma.** Let $f$ be $\alpha$-exp-concave on $\Wcal$ and $\gamma \le \tfrac12\min(1/(4GD), \alpha)$. Then for all
$\wvec, \uvec \in \Wcal$, with $\gvec = \nabla f(\wvec)$,

$$
f(\uvec) \ge f(\wvec) + \inner{\gvec}{\uvec - \wvec} + \frac{\gamma}{2}\inner{\gvec}{\uvec - \wvec}^2.
$$

*Proof.* Since $2\gamma \le \alpha$, $h = e^{-2\gamma f}$ is concave (a smaller exponent keeps concavity). A concave
function lies below its tangent: $h(\uvec) \le h(\wvec) + \inner{\nabla h(\wvec)}{\uvec - \wvec}$, with
$\nabla h(\wvec) = -2\gamma e^{-2\gamma f(\wvec)}\gvec$. Dividing by $e^{-2\gamma f(\wvec)}$ and taking logarithms,

$$
f(\uvec) \ge f(\wvec) - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\log\paren{1 - 2\gamma\inner{\gvec}{\uvec - \wvec}}.
$$

Let $z = 2\gamma\inner{\gvec}{\uvec - \wvec}$. Then $\abs{z} \le 2\gamma GD \le \tfrac14$, and on that range
$-\log(1 - z) \ge z + z^2/4$. Substituting gives the lemma. $\square$

## The regret bound

**One step.** Write $\vvec_{t+1} = \wvec_t - \tfrac1\gamma\Amat_t^{-1}\gvec_t$ for the point before projection. Then

$$
(\vvec_{t+1} - \uvec)^\top\Amat_t(\vvec_{t+1} - \uvec) = (\wvec_t - \uvec)^\top\Amat_t(\wvec_t - \uvec)
- \frac{2}{\gamma}\inner{\gvec_t}{\wvec_t - \uvec} + \frac{1}{\gamma^2}\gvec_t^\top\Amat_t^{-1}\gvec_t.
$$

Projection in the $\Amat_t$-norm does not increase the $\Amat_t$-distance to $\uvec \in \Wcal$, by the same argument as
for Euclidean projection in <NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent" />. Replacing $\vvec_{t+1}$ by $\wvec_{t+1}$ on the
left and rearranging,

$$
\inner{\gvec_t}{\wvec_t - \uvec} \le \frac{1}{2\gamma}\gvec_t^\top\Amat_t^{-1}\gvec_t
+ \frac{\gamma}{2}(\wvec_t - \uvec)^\top\Amat_t(\wvec_t - \uvec) - \frac{\gamma}{2}(\wvec_{t+1} - \uvec)^\top\Amat_t(\wvec_{t+1} - \uvec).
$$

**Sum.** The distance terms nearly telescope. Since $\Amat_t - \Amat_{t-1} = \gvec_t\gvec_t^\top$ and
$\Amat_0 = \epsilon\I$, collecting them leaves
$\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\sum_t\inner{\gvec_t}{\wvec_t - \uvec}^2 + \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\epsilon D^2$, after dropping the final
negative term. So

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{\inner{\gvec_t}{\wvec_t - \uvec} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\inner{\gvec_t}{\wvec_t - \uvec}^2}
\le \frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gvec_t^\top\Amat_t^{-1}\gvec_t + \frac{\gamma\epsilon D^2}{2}.
$$

By the lemma, the left side is at least $R_T(\uvec)$. The quadratic term that the exp-concavity lemma supplies is
exactly the one that the preconditioner creates; this is why $\Amat_t$ accumulates $\gvec_t\gvec_t^\top$.

**The elliptical potential.** For $\Amat = \Bmat + \gvec\gvec^\top$ with $\Bmat$ positive definite, the matrix
determinant lemma gives $\det\Bmat = \det\Amat\,(1 - \gvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\gvec)$. Hence

$$
\gvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\gvec = 1 - \frac{\det\Bmat}{\det\Amat} \le \log\frac{\det\Amat}{\det\Bmat},
$$

using $1 - 1/x \le \log x$. Summing over rounds telescopes:
$\sum_t\gvec_t^\top\Amat_t^{-1}\gvec_t \le \log(\det\Amat_T/\det\Amat_0)$. The eigenvalues of $\Amat_T$ sum to at most
$d\epsilon + TG^2$, and the determinant of a positive definite matrix with a given trace is largest when the
eigenvalues are equal, so $\log(\det\Amat_T/\epsilon^d) \le d\log(1 + TG^2/(d\epsilon)) \le d\log(1 + TG^2/\epsilon)$.

**Choose the parameters.** With $\epsilon = 1/(\gamma^2D^2)$ and $\gamma = \tfrac12\min(1/(4GD), \alpha)$,

$$
R_T \le \frac{1}{2\gamma}\paren{d\log\paren{1 + TG^2\gamma^2D^2} + 1} \le 4\paren{GD + \frac{1}{\alpha}}\paren{d\log\paren{1 + \frac{T}{64}} + 1},
$$

using $1/(2\gamma) = \max(4GD, 1/\alpha) \le 4(GD + 1/\alpha)$ and $\gamma GD \le 1/8$. This is
$O(d(1/\alpha + GD)\log T)$ <Cite id="hazan2007" /> <Cite id="hazan2016" />.

## Worked example

Take $d = 10$, $GD = 1$ and $\alpha = 1$. Then $\gamma = 1/8$, $1/(2\gamma) = 4$, and the first form of the bound is
$4(10\log(1 + T/64) + 1)$, which is
$206$ at $T = 10^4$, $298$ at $T = 10^5$ and $390$ at $T = 10^6$. The <NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent" /> bound
$DG\sqrt{T}$ is $100$, $316$ and $1000$ at the same horizons. The logarithmic bound wins only after about $10^5$
rounds here. The constants in these bounds are loose, but the comparison shows that $d\log T$ beats $\sqrt{T}$ only
when $T$ is large compared with $d^2$.

## Cost

Each step needs $\Amat_t^{-1}$. The Sherman–Morrison formula updates it in $O(d^2)$ from $\Amat_{t-1}^{-1}$, because
$\Amat_t$ differs by a rank-one term (see <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse" />). The projection in the $\Amat_t$-norm is a
quadratic program and is the expensive part when $\Wcal$ is not the whole space. Memory is $O(d^2)$. These costs keep
ONS to moderate dimensions; <NoteLink to="adaptive-online-methods">diagonal AdaGrad</NoteLink> keeps only the diagonal
of $\Amat_t$ and costs $O(d)$.

## Assumptions

- $\Wcal$ is bounded. The rate depends on $GD$, and the exp-concavity constant of squared or log loss depends on the
  range of predictions over $\Wcal$.
- The parameters $\gamma$ and $\epsilon$ need $\alpha$, $G$ and $D$.
- For experts with mixable losses, the <NoteLink to="aggregating-algorithm-and-mixability">aggregating
  algorithm</NoteLink> does better than ONS on the simplex: its regret $(\ln N)/\eta$ does not grow with $T$.

## Pitfalls

- **Exp-concavity constants can be tiny.** Squared loss has $\alpha = 1/(2B^2)$, so a range $B = 10$ gives
  $1/\alpha = 200$, and the bound grows accordingly.
- **Skipping the projection.** Without the generalised projection the iterates can leave $\Wcal$, where the losses
  may not be exp-concave and the lemma fails.
- **Newton on non-curved directions.** $\Amat_t$ is nearly singular along directions no gradient has visited; the
  $\epsilon\I$ term prevents enormous steps there. Setting $\epsilon$ too small makes early steps unstable.
