<Definition>
Let $\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_T$ be drawn independently from a distribution $P$, let $\ell(\wvec; \zvec)$ be a loss and
$L(\wvec) = \expect_{\zvec \sim P}\ell(\wvec; \zvec)$ its **risk**. **Online-to-batch conversion** feeds the losses
$f_t(\wvec) = \ell(\wvec; \zvec_t)$ in order to an online learner, which plays $\wvec_1, \dots, \wvec_T$, and returns a
single hypothesis built from the iterates: the average $\bar\wvec_T = \tfrac1T\sum_t\wvec_t$ for convex losses, or one
iterate chosen at random or by a validation rule otherwise. Its risk is bounded by the learner's regret divided by $T$
<Cite id="cesabianchi2004" />.
</Definition>

## Conditional unbiasedness of the online loss

The online learner chooses $\wvec_t$ from $\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_{t-1}$ only. The example $\zvec_t$ is independent
of those, so it is a fresh test point for $\wvec_t$:

$$
\expect\brack{f_t(\wvec_t) \mid \zvec_{1:t-1}} = \expect_{\zvec \sim P}\brack{\ell(\wvec_t; \zvec)} = L(\wvec_t).
$$

Each online loss $f_t(\wvec_t)$ is an unbiased estimate of the risk of the hypothesis that incurred it. Summed over
rounds, the online loss is a validation estimate that uses every example for training and for testing, without
reusing any example for both at once. This is the **prequential** view: evaluate on each example before learning from
it.

## Expected excess risk

Fix any comparator $\uvec$ that does not depend on the sample, such as the risk minimiser
$\uvec^\star = \argmin_{\uvec \in \Wcal}L(\uvec)$. Taking <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> of the fact above and summing,
$\expect\brack{\sum_t L(\wvec_t)} = \expect\brack{\sum_t f_t(\wvec_t)}$. For the comparator, independence gives
$\expect\brack{\sum_t f_t(\uvec)} = TL(\uvec)$. Subtracting,

$$
\expect\brack{\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}L(\wvec_t)} - L(\uvec) = \frac{\expect[R_T(\uvec)]}{T},
$$

where $R_T(\uvec) = \sum_t f_t(\wvec_t) - \sum_t f_t(\uvec)$ is the regret on the sample. If $\ell$ is convex in
$\wvec$, so is $L$, and Jensen's inequality gives $L(\bar\wvec_T) \le \tfrac1T\sum_t L(\wvec_t)$. Therefore

$$
\expect\brack{L(\bar\wvec_T)} - L(\uvec) \le \frac{\expect[R_T(\uvec)]}{T}.
$$

Any regret bound that holds for every sequence holds in particular for the random sample, so a bound
$R_T \le B_T$ gives excess risk at most $B_T/T$. For <NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent" /> with
$B_T = DG\sqrt{T}$, the excess risk is at most $DG/\sqrt{T}$, the standard rate for
<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink> with averaging.

Without convexity, pick an index $\tau$ uniformly from $\set{1, \dots, T}$ and output $\wvec_\tau$. Then
$\expect L(\wvec_\tau) = \tfrac1T\sum_t\expect L(\wvec_t)$, and the same bound holds with no Jensen step.

## A bound that holds with high probability

Let the loss take values in $[0, 1]$. Define $V_t = L(\wvec_t) - f_t(\wvec_t)$. Given $\zvec_{1:t-1}$, $V_t$ has mean
zero, and it lies in the interval $[L(\wvec_t) - 1, L(\wvec_t)]$ of length 1. So $V_1, V_2, \dots$ is a
<NoteLink to="martingale">martingale difference sequence</NoteLink> with bounded increments. The Azuma–Hoeffding
inequality (see <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality" />) gives

$$
\prob\paren{\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_t \ge \varepsilon} \le \exp\paren{-\frac{2\varepsilon^2}{T}}.
$$

Setting the right side to $\delta$ and solving for $\varepsilon$: with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}L(\wvec_t) \le \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_t(\wvec_t) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2T}}.
$$

With a convex loss, the left side bounds $L(\bar\wvec_T)$. The right side is computed from the online run itself, so
the bound certifies the averaged model with no held-out data <Cite id="cesabianchi2004" />. To compare with the best
hypothesis, bound the online loss by $\tfrac1T\sum_t f_t(\uvec) + R_T/T$ and apply
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality" /> to the fixed $\uvec$: with probability at least $1 - 2\delta$,
$L(\bar\wvec_T) \le L(\uvec) + R_T/T + 2\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2T)}$.

## Worked example

Run online gradient descent with $D = 2$ and $G = 1$ on $T = 10\,000$ <Gloss name="iid" /> examples of a convex loss with values in
$[0, 1]$.

- Expected excess risk of $\bar\wvec_T$: at most $DG/\sqrt{T} = 0.02$.
- Suppose the average online loss is $0.130$. With probability at least $0.95$, the risk of $\bar\wvec_T$ is at most
  $0.130 + \sqrt{\ln 20/20\,000} = 0.130 + 0.012 = 0.142$.

The deviation term $0.012$ does not depend on $D$, $G$ or the dimension. No uniform convergence over the hypothesis
class was needed.

## Relation to uniform convergence

Batch generalisation bounds, such as those in <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning" /> and <NoteLink
to="rademacher-complexity" />, control the gap between empirical and true risk uniformly over a class, because the
learned hypothesis depends on every example. The online-to-batch argument avoids uniform convergence. Each iterate is
evaluated on an example it has not seen, so a <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">concentration inequality</NoteLink> for a single martingale suffices. The
complexity of the class enters only through the regret. For a finite class of $N$ experts with regret
$\sqrt{(T\ln N)/2}$, the excess risk is $\sqrt{\ln N/(2T)}$, the same order as the finite-class PAC bound.

## Assumptions

- The examples are independent and identically distributed. Under drift the fact
  $\expect[f_t(\wvec_t) \mid \zvec_{1:t-1}] = L(\wvec_t)$ fails, because there is no single $L$.
- The comparator $\uvec$ does not depend on the sample. The regret bound holds for any $\uvec$, but the step
  $\expect\sum_t f_t(\uvec) = TL(\uvec)$ needs $\uvec$ fixed in advance.
- The loss is convex in $\wvec$ for averaging, and bounded for the high-probability version.

## Pitfalls

- **Outputting the last iterate.** The bound is for the average, or for a random iterate. The argument says nothing
  about the last iterate, whose risk needs a separate analysis.
- **Several passes.** The argument holds for one pass. On a second pass $\wvec_t$ has seen $\zvec_t$ before, the
  online loss is no longer a fresh estimate, and the high-probability bound does not apply.
- **Strongly convex rates.** Plugging the $O((G^2/\lambda)\log T)$ regret into the conversion gives excess risk
  $O(G^2\log T/(\lambda T))$. The extra $\log T$ can be removed with weighted averaging of later iterates, which the
  plain conversion does not do.
