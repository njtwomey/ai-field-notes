<Definition>
A stream $(\xvec_1, y_1), (\xvec_2, y_2), \dots$ has **concept drift** if the examples at time $t$ are drawn from a
distribution $p_t(\xvec, y)$ that changes with $t$. **Real drift** changes the posterior $p_t(y \mid \xvec)$, so the
optimal predictor changes. **Virtual drift** changes only $p_t(\xvec)$. **Drift adaptation** is the part of a streaming
learner that keeps its model close to the current concept: it decides how much of the past to trust
<Cite id="gama2014" />.
</Definition>

## Kinds of drift

Drift is described by how the concept moves from an old distribution to a new one <Cite id="gama2014" />.

- **Abrupt.** The concept switches at one time, for example after a policy change or a sensor replacement.
- **Gradual.** Examples come from the old and new concepts alternately, with the new one increasingly frequent.
- **Incremental.** The concept moves continuously through intermediate states, as a mean that slides.
- **Recurring.** An earlier concept returns, as with seasons or weekday and weekend behaviour.

Real drift makes a fixed model wrong. Virtual drift can too, if the model was poor in regions of $\xvec$ that were
rare before the drift and common after. From the monitoring side the same changes are called
<NoteLink to="dataset-shift" />, and <NoteLink to="drift-detection" /> tests for them in a deployed model. This note is
about the learner's side.

## Forgetting: the bias–variance trade-off

A learner that weights all past examples equally averages over every concept it has seen. On the figure's abrupt
drift, its slope estimate settles between the two concepts. Forgetting down-weights old examples. The two standard
forms are a **sliding window** of the last $W$ examples, and **exponential forgetting**, which gives the example $k$
rounds old the weight $\lambda^k$ with $\lambda \in (0, 1)$.

The memory length trades variance against lag. Consider tracking a mean $\mu_t$ from observations
$x_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$, by the exponentially weighted average

$$
m_t = \lambda m_{t-1} + (1 - \lambda)x_t = (1 - \lambda)\sum_{k \ge 0}\lambda^kx_{t-k}.
$$

- **Variance.** The weights $(1 - \lambda)\lambda^k$ sum to 1, and the noise terms are independent, so
  $\var(m_t) = (1 - \lambda)^2\sigma^2\sum_k\lambda^{2k} = \sigma^2\frac{1 - \lambda}{1 + \lambda}$. A long memory
  ($\lambda \to 1$) averages the noise away.
- **Lag.** If the mean drifts linearly, $\mu_{t-k} = \mu_t - \delta k$, then
  $\expect[m_t] - \mu_t = -(1 - \lambda)\delta\sum_kk\lambda^k = -\delta\frac{\lambda}{1 - \lambda}$. A long memory lags behind.

The mean squared error is the sum of the squared lag and the variance,

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\lambda) = \delta^2\frac{\lambda^2}{(1 - \lambda)^2} + \sigma^2\frac{1 - \lambda}{1 + \lambda}.
$$

A window of $W$ examples has variance $\sigma^2/W$ and lag $\delta(W - 1)/2$, and matches the variance of the exponential
average when $W = (1 + \lambda)/(1 - \lambda)$.

**Worked example.** With $\sigma = 1$ and drift $\delta = 0.01$ per round, the MSE is smallest at $\lambda = 0.930$,
an effective memory of $1/(1 - \lambda) = 14$ rounds, with MSE $0.054$. A simulation over 200 000 rounds gives
$0.055$. The best window is $W = 27$, close to $(1 + \lambda)/(1 - \lambda) = 27.5$, with the same MSE. Faster drift or
less noise shortens the optimal memory. No single $\lambda$ is right for a stream whose drift rate changes, which
motivates adaptive memory.

The same trade-off governs learning rates. <NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent" /> with a constant step size is a
form of exponential forgetting; steps that decay as $1/\sqrt{t}$ or $1/t$ give an ever-longer memory and stop
tracking. For least squares, the <NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter">RLS filter</NoteLink> applies the factor $\lambda$ to the
normal equations.

## Detect, then adapt: ADWIN

An adaptive learner keeps a long memory while the stream is stable and cuts it when the stream changes. **ADWIN**
(adaptive windowing) keeps a window $\Wcal$ of recent values of a bounded statistic in $[0, 1]$, such as the 0/1 error
of the model <Cite id="bifet2007" />. After each new value, it checks every split of $\Wcal$ into an older part
$\Wcal_0$ and a newer part $\Wcal_1$, of sizes $n_0$ and $n_1$ and means $\hat\mu_0$ and $\hat\mu_1$. It drops $\Wcal_0$
whenever

$$
\abs{\hat\mu_0 - \hat\mu_1} \ge \varepsilon_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\ln\frac{4}{\delta'}}, \qquad
m = \frac{1}{1/n_0 + 1/n_1}, \quad \delta' = \frac{\delta}{n},
$$

where $n = n_0 + n_1$ and $\delta \in (0, 1)$ is a confidence parameter. It repeats until no split qualifies. The window
then contains only data consistent with a single mean, and the model is retrained, or its statistics recomputed, on it.

**No false alarms, with probability $1 - \delta$.** Suppose the mean is constant over $\Wcal$. The difference
$\hat\mu_0 - \hat\mu_1$ is a sum of $n$ independent terms: each of the $n_0$ old values enters with weight $1/n_0$ and
range $1/n_0$, each new value with range $1/n_1$. The squared ranges sum to $1/n_0 + 1/n_1 = 1/m$. By
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality" />,

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{\hat\mu_0 - \hat\mu_1} \ge \varepsilon} \le 2\exp\paren{-2m\varepsilon^2}.
$$

At $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_{\text{cut}}$ the right side is $2 \cdot \delta'/4 = \delta'/2$. A union bound over the at
most $n$ splits gives a probability of any cut of at most $n\delta'/2 = \delta/2$.

**Real changes are found.** Suppose the true means of the two parts differ by more than $2\varepsilon_{\text{cut}}$.
The same inequality shows that the estimated difference is within $\varepsilon_{\text{cut}}$ of the true difference
with probability at least $1 - \delta'/2$, so it exceeds $\varepsilon_{\text{cut}}$ and ADWIN cuts.

**Worked example.** With $n_0 = n_1 = 500$ and $\delta = 0.01$, $m = 250$, $\delta' = 10^{-5}$ and
$\varepsilon_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{\ln(4 \times 10^5)/500} = 0.161$. An observed error rate of $0.10$ in the older half
and $0.30$ in the newer half differs by $0.20$ and triggers a cut. The guarantee above promises detection whenever the
true rates differ by more than $2\varepsilon_{\text{cut}} = 0.32$. With 100 values on each side, the threshold is
$0.336$.

ADWIN's window grows without bound on a stationary stream, so it keeps all the <NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors">statistical power</NoteLink> the data allow, and
shrinks only on evidence. Checking every split costs $O(n)$ per step; the published algorithm stores the window in
exponential histograms, with $O(\log n)$ memory and time <Cite id="bifet2007" />. Other detectors, such as the
<NoteLink to="cumulative-sum-control-chart" /> and Page–Hinkley tests on the error stream, trade the guarantee for a fixed detection delay.
<NoteLink to="change-point-detection" /> and <NoteLink to="bayesian-online-changepoint-detection" /> treat detection as
inference.

## Ensembles

An ensemble keeps several models trained on different stretches of the stream and weights them by recent performance.
**Dynamic weighted majority** multiplies a member's weight by $\beta < 1$ when it errs, removes members whose weight
falls below a threshold, and adds a new member, trained from that point, whenever the ensemble errs
<Cite id="kolter2007" />. The weighting is the <NoteLink to="weighted-majority" /> rule; adding fresh members plays the role
of fixed share in <NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" />, whose regret bound against a switching sequence is the
theory behind this design. Ensembles handle recurring drift well, because an old member that fits a returning concept
can regain weight.

Early work with windows whose size adapts to the observed accuracy, and with stored concepts for recurring contexts,
is the FLORA family <Cite id="widmer1996" />. For <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision trees</NoteLink>, CVFDT grows alternative subtrees when the split
statistics in a sliding window change, and swaps them in when they become more accurate <Cite id="hulten2001" />.

## Assumptions

- The recent past is more like the near future than the distant past is. Forgetting helps only if drift is persistent
  on the time scale of the memory; it hurts on stationary data.
- Labels arrive promptly. With delayed labels, error-based detectors see the drift late; monitoring $p_t(\xvec)$ detects
  only virtual drift.
- ADWIN's guarantees need a bounded statistic and independent values within a stable period.

## Pitfalls

- **Memory set for the wrong drift rate.** The optimal $\lambda$ depends on the ratio of drift to noise, which usually
  changes over time. Adaptive windows, or an ensemble over several memory lengths, remove the single choice.
- **Resetting on false alarms.** Each reset throws away data. With many streams or features monitored at once, correct
  $\delta$ for <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink> or the model will be reset constantly.
- **Evaluating with a random split.** A shuffled test set hides drift. Evaluate prequentially: predict each example
  before training on it, and report the error over a sliding window or with a fading factor <Cite id="gama2014" />.
