<Definition>
A **streaming algorithm** reads a sequence of items $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ once, in order, and keeps a state whose size
is much smaller than $n$, typically constant or logarithmic in $n$. After any prefix it can answer a query about the
items seen so far, exactly or with a stated error and failure probability. **Streaming learners** use such states as
their only memory of the data: running <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistics</NoteLink>, uniform samples, sketches of counts, or the leaf
statistics of an incrementally grown tree.
</Definition>

## Exact running statistics

Many estimates depend on the data only through sums, which update in constant time. The mean and variance are the
common case. Adding sums of $x$ and $x^2$ loses precision when the mean is large compared with the spread, because
$\sum x^2 - n\bar x^2$ subtracts two nearly equal numbers. **Welford's algorithm** updates the mean $\mu_n$ and the sum
of squared deviations $M_n$ directly:

$$
\mu_n = \mu_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \mu_{n-1}}{n}, \qquad M_n = M_{n-1} + (x_n - \mu_{n-1})(x_n - \mu_n).
$$

The second update follows from $M_n = \sum_{i \le n}(x_i - \mu_n)^2$: expanding around $\mu_{n-1}$, the old deviations
shift by $\mu_{n-1} - \mu_n$, which adds $(n - 1)(\mu_n - \mu_{n-1})^2$, and the new item adds $(x_n - \mu_n)^2$. Since
$\mu_n - \mu_{n-1} = (x_n - \mu_{n-1})/n$, the total increase is $(x_n - \mu_{n-1})(x_n - \mu_n)$. The sample variance is
$M_n/(n - 1)$. <NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter">Recursive least squares</NoteLink>, in <NoteLink to="online-learning-for-linear-models" />, is the same idea for the
normal equations.

## Reservoir sampling

Many learning procedures need a uniform sample of the stream: a validation set, a replay buffer for
<NoteLink to="replay-and-architectural-methods">continual learning</NoteLink>, or a sample for estimating quantiles.
The stream length $n$ is unknown in advance.

**Algorithm R.** Keep the first $k$ items in a reservoir. For item $i > k$, draw $j$ uniformly from $\set{1, \dots, i}$;
if $j \le k$, replace the reservoir's $j$-th item with item $i$ <Cite id="vitter1985" />.

**Claim.** After $n \ge k$ items, each item is in the reservoir with probability $k/n$.

*Proof by induction on $n$.* At $n = k$ every item is in the reservoir. Suppose the claim holds after $n$ items. Item
$n + 1$ enters with probability $k/(n + 1)$. An item already in the reservoir is evicted only if item $n + 1$ enters and
lands on its slot, which has probability $\frac{k}{n+1}\cdot\frac1k = \frac{1}{n+1}$. So it is still there with
probability $\frac{k}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1} = \frac{k}{n+1}$. $\square$

The same argument, applied to sets, shows every subset of size $k$ is equally likely, so the reservoir is a uniform
sample without replacement. A simulation with $k = 3$ and $n = 10$ over 100 000 runs gives inclusion frequencies
between $0.298$ and $0.302$ for all ten items, against $k/n = 0.3$. Algorithm R draws one random number per item.
Vitter's skip-based variants draw the number of items to skip before the next replacement, and need
$O(k(1 + \log(n/k)))$ random numbers in expectation <Cite id="vitter1985" />.

## The count-min sketch

Counting how often each of $U$ possible items has occurred needs $U$ counters, which is too many for identifiers
such as user–item pairs or hashed n-grams. Let $\fvec \in \reals^U$ be the vector of true counts, with
$\norm{\fvec}_1 = n$. The **count-min sketch** keeps a $d \times w$ array of counters $\Cmat$ and $d$ hash functions
$h_1, \dots, h_d$, each mapping items to $\set{1, \dots, w}$, drawn from a pairwise-independent family
<Cite id="cormode2005" />.

- **Update** for item $i$: $C_{r, h_r(i)} \leftarrow C_{r, h_r(i)} + 1$ for each row $r$.
- **Query** for item $i$: $\hat f_i = \min_rC_{r, h_r(i)}$.

**Guarantee.** With $w = \lceil e/\epsilon\rceil$ and $d = \lceil\ln(1/\delta)\rceil$, $\hat f_i \ge f_i$ always, and
$\hat f_i \le f_i + \epsilon\norm{\fvec}_1$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$.

*Proof.* Every counter includes the item's own count, so $\hat f_i \ge f_i$. In row $r$, the excess
$X_r = C_{r, h_r(i)} - f_i$ is the total count of other items that collide with $i$. <NoteLink to="independence">Pairwise independence</NoteLink> gives
collision probability $1/w$, so $\expect X_r \le \norm{\fvec}_1/w \le \epsilon\norm{\fvec}_1/e$. By Markov's inequality
(<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities" />), $\prob(X_r \ge \epsilon\norm{\fvec}_1) \le 1/e$. The rows use
independent hash functions, so the probability that every row exceeds the bound, which is what the minimum needs to
fail, is at most $e^{-d} \le \delta$. $\square$

**Worked example.** For error $\epsilon = 0.001$ of the stream length and failure probability $\delta = 0.01$, the
sketch has $w = 2719$ columns and $d = 5$ rows: 13 595 counters, whatever the number of distinct items. After 10 million
events, every count estimate is within 10 000 of the truth with probability 0.99. Heavy hitters, the items with
$f_i \ge \phi n$, are therefore identified reliably when $\phi \gg \epsilon$. Count features for click-through models and
frequency filters for vocabularies are typical uses; <NoteLink to="feature-hashing-for-text" /> uses a single hash for a
different purpose, to index features rather than count them.

## Hoeffding trees

A <NoteLink to="decision-tree" /> chooses each split by comparing a heuristic such as <NoteLink to="mutual-information">information gain</NoteLink> across
attributes. On a stream, a leaf sees examples one at a time and must decide when it has seen enough to split. The
**Hoeffding tree**, or very fast decision tree (VFDT), answers with a concentration bound <Cite id="domingos2000" />.

**The Hoeffding bound.** For $n$ independent observations of a variable with range $R$, the sample mean is within

$$
\varepsilon = \sqrt{\frac{R^2\ln(1/\delta)}{2n}}
$$

of the true mean with probability at least $1 - \delta$ (see <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality" />).

**The split rule.** Each leaf keeps counts $n_{ajk}$ of examples with attribute $a$ equal to value $j$ and class $k$. From
them it computes the gain $\bar G(a)$ of every attribute. Let $a_1$ and $a_2$ be the best and second-best attributes
and $\Delta\bar G = \bar G(a_1) - \bar G(a_2) \ge 0$. If $\Delta\bar G > \varepsilon$, the true difference is positive with
probability at least $1 - \delta$, so $a_1$ is the attribute a learner with infinite data would choose at this leaf. The
leaf splits on $a_1$ and its children start collecting counts. If two attributes are nearly equal, the rule would
wait forever, so the leaf also splits once $\varepsilon$ falls below a tie threshold $\tau$.

Information gain with $c$ classes lies in $[0, \log_2c]$, so $R = \log_2c$. With a guarantee at every leaf, the
expected disagreement between the Hoeffding tree and the tree grown by the same procedure with infinite data is at
most $\delta/p$, where $p$ is the probability that an example falls into a leaf at a given level <Cite id="domingos2000" />.

**Worked example.** For two classes, $R = 1$. With $\delta = 10^{-7}$, a leaf whose two best attributes differ in gain
by $0.05$ needs $n = \ln(10^7)/(2 \cdot 0.05^2) = 3224$ examples. After $1000$ examples, $\varepsilon = 0.090$.

The memory per leaf is $O(\text{attributes} \times \text{values} \times \text{classes})$, independent of $n$, and each example
costs time proportional to the tree's depth. CVFDT extends the method to drifting streams by keeping the counts over a
sliding window and growing alternative subtrees when a split's statistics change <Cite id="hulten2001" />.

## Assumptions

- Items arrive once and cannot be revisited. Algorithms that allow a few passes, or random access, are outside this
  model.
- The guarantees of reservoir sampling and the count-min sketch hold for any stream. The Hoeffding tree's guarantee
  assumes independent, identically distributed examples.

## Pitfalls

- **Reservoir sampling under drift.** A uniform sample of the whole stream is dominated by old data once the stream
  is long. For recent data use a sliding window, or a biased reservoir that replaces with a fixed probability.
- **Count-min overestimates rare items.** The error bound is relative to the total $\norm{\fvec}_1$, so counts of rare items
  can be mostly collision noise. Use it for frequent items, or widen the sketch until $\epsilon\norm{\fvec}_1$ is small
  compared with the counts that matter.
- **The Hoeffding bound is applied to a gain, not a mean.** Information gain is a non-linear function of the counts,
  not an average of independent terms, so the bound as used is a heuristic. Hoeffding trees work well in practice, but
  the per-split guarantee is weaker than stated for sample means.
