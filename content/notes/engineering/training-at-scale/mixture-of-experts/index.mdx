<Definition>
A sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer replaces one feed-forward network with $N$ expert networks $E_1, \dots, E_N$
and a router. For an input token $\xvec$, the router computes a weight $g_i(\xvec)$ for each expert, keeps the $k$
largest, and the layer returns

$$
\yvec = \sum_{i \in \operatorname{TopK}(\xvec)} g_i(\xvec) \, E_i(\xvec).
$$

Only the $k$ selected experts run, so the number of parameters grows with $N$ while the computation per token grows
with $k$ <Cite id="shazeer2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The accuracy of a <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> improves with its number of
parameters, but in a dense model every parameter is used for every token, so compute per token grows in proportion.
Conditional computation decouples the two: different tokens use different parts of the network. Mixtures of experts
were introduced as ensembles of networks with a learned gating network that weights all of them
<Cite id="jacobs1991" />. The sparse version evaluates only the few experts the gate selects, which is what makes it
cheaper.

The difficulty is that the router is trained along with the experts. Experts that are chosen more often are trained
more, become better, and are chosen even more often. Without a counterweight, a few experts receive most tokens, the
rest are wasted, and the devices holding the popular experts become a bottleneck.

## Mechanism

### Routing

In a transformer, the MoE layer replaces the feed-forward block of some or all layers; attention is unchanged. The
router is a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink> $\Wmat_r \in \reals^{N \times h}$ from the token's hidden state to one logit per expert.

**Top-$k$ gating.** Shazeer et al. keep the $k$ largest logits, set the others to $-\infty$ and apply a softmax, so the
$k$ selected weights sum to 1 <Cite id="shazeer2017" />:

$$
\gvec(\xvec) = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\operatorname{KeepTopK}\paren{\Wmat_r \xvec, k}}.
$$

They add Gaussian noise with a learned, input-dependent scale to the logits during training, which spreads tokens over
more experts. The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> reaches the router through the weights $g_i$ of the
selected experts.

The Switch Transformer uses $k = 1$, which halves the router's computation and communication relative to $k = 2$ and
simplifies the implementation <Cite id="fedus2022" />.

**Fine-grained and shared experts.** DeepSeekMoE splits each expert into several smaller ones and routes to
proportionally more of them, which allows more combinations of experts per token, and adds **shared experts** that
every token uses to hold common knowledge <Cite id="dai2024" />. DeepSeek-V3 has one shared expert and 256 routed experts
per MoE layer and routes each token to 8 of them. It has 671 billion parameters, of which 37 billion, 5.5%, are active
for each token <Cite id="deepseek2024" />.

### The load-balancing loss

The Switch Transformer adds an auxiliary loss to the training objective. For a batch of $T$ tokens, let

$$
f_i = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \indicator\set{\text{token } t \text{ is routed to expert } i}, \qquad
P_i = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} p_i(\xvec_t),
$$

where $p_i(\xvec_t)$ is the router's softmax probability for expert $i$. $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens expert $i$
actually receives and $P_i$ the average probability the router gives it. The loss is

$$
\Lcal_{\text{aux}} = \alpha N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i P_i,
$$

with $\alpha = 10^{-2}$ in the Switch Transformer <Cite id="fedus2022" />.

**Why the product.** $f_i$ counts discrete choices and has no gradient. $P_i$ is differentiable. The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the
loss with respect to $P_i$ is $\alpha N f_i$, so <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> lowers the router probability of each expert in
proportion to the load it already carries: overloaded experts are pushed down hardest.

**Why its minimum is uniform routing.** If routing follows the probabilities, $f_i \approx P_i$, and the loss is
$\alpha N \sum_i P_i^2$. By the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink>,

$$
1 = \paren{\sum_{i=1}^{N} 1 \cdot P_i}^2 \le N \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_i^2,
$$

with equality exactly when all $P_i$ are equal. The loss is therefore at least $\alpha$, and equals $\alpha$ exactly
when every expert receives $1/N$ of the tokens and probability. Multiplying by $N$ makes this minimum independent of
the number of experts.

**Bias-based balancing.** A balancing loss competes with the language-modelling loss, and a large weight hurts quality.
DeepSeek-V3 instead adds a bias $b_i$ to expert $i$'s affinity score when choosing the top $k$, but not when computing
the gate weights. After each training step it decreases $b_i$ by a fixed amount $\gamma$ if expert $i$ was overloaded
and increases it by $\gamma$ if underloaded. The router's choices move towards balance without any gradient through the
language-modelling objective. A sequence-level balance loss with a very small weight remains as a safeguard
<Cite id="deepseek2024" />.

### Capacity and dropped tokens

For efficient batched computation, each expert processes a fixed-size buffer. Its **expert capacity** is

$$
C = \text{capacity factor} \times \frac{k T}{N}
$$

tokens per batch <Cite id="fedus2022" />. A capacity factor above 1 leaves slack for imbalance. Tokens routed to a full
expert are dropped from that layer: their representation passes on unchanged through the residual connection. A larger
capacity factor drops fewer tokens but pads more slots with nothing, wasting computation and memory. DeepSeek-V3 balances
its load well enough that it drops no tokens during training <Cite id="deepseek2024" />.

### Expert parallelism

The experts of a layer are placed on different devices, $N/D$ experts on each of $D$ devices, while attention and the
router are replicated or split in the usual ways. After routing, an **all-to-all** exchange sends each token's hidden
state to the devices holding its selected experts; after the experts run, a second all-to-all returns the outputs to the
token's original device. The all-to-all traffic is proportional to $k$ times the number of tokens, which is why
DeepSeek-V3 limits each token to experts on at most 4 nodes <Cite id="deepseek2024" />. GShard scaled expert parallelism
to a 600-billion-parameter translation model on 2048 <Gloss name="tpu" /> v3 accelerators <Cite id="lepikhin2021" />.

## Cost

### Worked example

**Auxiliary loss.** Take $N = 4$ experts with $\alpha = 0.01$. If routing is skewed, with
$(f_1, \dots, f_4) = (0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1)$ and $(P_1, \dots, P_4) = (0.6, 0.15, 0.15, 0.1)$, then

$$
N \sum_i f_i P_i = 4 \times (0.42 + 0.015 + 0.015 + 0.01) = 1.84,
$$

and the loss is $0.0184$. Balanced routing gives $4 \times 4 \times \tfrac14 \times \tfrac14 = 1$, a loss of $0.01$. The
gradient with respect to $P_1$ is $\alpha N f_1 = 0.028$, seven times that for each of the other experts.

**Capacity.** A batch of $T = 4096$ tokens routed top-1 to $N = 64$ experts with capacity factor 1.25 gives each
expert room for $1.25 \times 4096 / 64 = 80$ tokens. An expert chosen by 100 tokens drops 20 of them.

**Parameters and compute.** A dense model with DeepSeek-V3's 671 billion parameters would use about $2 \times 671$
GFLOP per token in the forward pass. DeepSeek-V3 uses about $2 \times 37 = 74$ GFLOP, 5.5% of that
<Cite id="deepseek2024" />. All 671 billion parameters must still be stored, so memory, not arithmetic, sets the number
of devices needed to serve it.

### Results

The sparsely gated MoE layer increased model capacity more than 1000-fold with minor losses in computational
efficiency, with up to 137 billion parameters <Cite id="shazeer2017" />. The Switch Transformer reached the same quality
as T5-Base up to 7 times faster in pre-training with the same computation per token <Cite id="fedus2022" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Parameters against memory.** An MoE model has the compute of a small dense model and the memory of a large one.
  It is cheapest where memory is plentiful and arithmetic is the bottleneck, as in <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">large-batch training</NoteLink> and serving.
- **Balance against quality.** Strong balancing forces tokens away from the experts that suit them best. Weak balancing
  wastes experts and devices.
- **Communication.** Expert parallelism adds two all-to-all exchanges per MoE layer, which dominate when devices are
  connected by slow links.
- **$k$.** Larger $k$ improves quality per layer and costs proportionally more computation and communication.

## Pitfalls

- **Router collapse.** Without balancing, most tokens go to a few experts early in training and the rest never learn.
- **Training instability.** Large router logits make the softmax saturate. The Switch Transformer computes the router in
  fp32 for stability <Cite id="fedus2022" />.
- **Token dropping at inference.** Capacity limits suited to training batches can drop tokens when serving batches are
  small or unusual, changing the model's outputs.
- **Counting parameters.** Quoting total parameters overstates compute; quoting active parameters understates memory.
