<Definition>
ZeRO (Zero Redundancy Optimizer) is a form of data parallelism in which each of $N$ devices stores only a $1/N$ shard of
the model state instead of a full copy. Stage 1 shards the optimiser states, stage 2 also the
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>, and stage 3 also the parameters. Collective communication gathers each
piece where and when it is needed, and the result of every step is the same as with ordinary data parallelism
<Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

In <NoteLink to="data-parallelism">data parallelism</NoteLink> every device holds identical copies of the weights, the
gradients and the optimiser state, and all $N$ devices update all of them identically. Only one copy is needed.

<NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">Mixed-precision training</NoteLink> with <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink>
keeps, for $\Psi$ parameters <Cite id="micikevicius2018" />:

- 16-bit weights for the forward and backward passes: $2\Psi$ bytes;
- 16-bit gradients: $2\Psi$ bytes;
- the optimiser state in 32-bit precision: a master copy of the weights, the first moment and the second moment,
  $4\Psi + 4\Psi + 4\Psi = 12\Psi$ bytes.

The total is $16\Psi$ bytes per device, whatever $N$ is. Writing $K = 12$ for the optimiser's bytes per parameter, the
optimiser state alone is three quarters of it. A model with $7.5 \times 10^9$ parameters needs 120 GB per device before
any activations, more than an 80 GB accelerator holds, even with 64 devices <Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />.

## Mechanism

### Stage 1: shard the optimiser states

Partition the parameters into $N$ equal shards and make device $i$ responsible for shard $i$. Device $i$ keeps the
32-bit master weights and Adam moments only for its shard. After the backward pass, it needs only the averaged
gradients of its shard, so the all-reduce is replaced by its first half, a **reduce-scatter**, which leaves device $i$
with the summed gradients of shard $i$. Device $i$ updates its shard in 32 bits and casts it to 16 bits, and an
**all-gather** sends every updated shard to every device, which rebuilds the full 16-bit weights for the next step.

Per device, the 16-bit weights and gradients stay full size and the optimiser state shrinks by $N$:

$$
M_1 = 2\Psi + 2\Psi + \frac{K\Psi}{N} = 4\Psi + \frac{12\Psi}{N}.
$$

### Stage 2: also shard the gradients

A device needs the gradients only of its own shard. As soon as a bucket of gradients has been reduced to its owner, the
other devices free it. Only the owner keeps a 16-bit gradient for each parameter:

$$
M_2 = 2\Psi + \frac{(2 + K)\Psi}{N} = 2\Psi + \frac{14\Psi}{N}.
$$

### Stage 3: also shard the parameters

Each device stores only its shard of the 16-bit weights too. Before a layer's forward pass, an all-gather assembles the
layer's full weights; after the layer runs, the non-owned parts are freed. The backward pass gathers them again. The
whole model state is divided by $N$:

$$
M_3 = \frac{(2 + 2 + K)\Psi}{N} = \frac{16\Psi}{N}.
$$

Only one or two layers' full weights exist on a device at any moment, so stage 3 fits models whose state is far larger
than one device's memory, provided that $N$ is large enough. PyTorch's fully sharded data parallel (FSDP) implements
the same scheme, gathering the weights of one wrapped group of layers at a time and prefetching the next group during
computation <Cite id="zhao2023" />.

### Communication volume

Measure communication by the data each device sends, in units of $\Psi$ numbers. A ring
<NoteLink to="data-parallelism">all-reduce</NoteLink> is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather, and each half moves
about $\Psi$ per device, since $(N-1)/N \approx 1$. Standard data parallelism therefore moves $2\Psi$ per step.

- **Stages 1 and 2** reduce-scatter the gradients ($\Psi$) and all-gather the updated weights ($\Psi$): $2\Psi$, the
  same as standard data parallelism <Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />.
- **Stage 3** all-gathers the weights before the forward pass ($\Psi$), again before the backward pass ($\Psi$), and
  reduce-scatters the gradients ($\Psi$): $3\Psi$, 1.5 times standard data parallelism <Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />.

The extra gathers can overlap with computation by fetching the next layer's weights while the current one runs.

## Cost

### Worked example

Take $\Psi = 7.5 \times 10^9$ parameters on $N = 64$ devices, the example of Rajbhandari et al.
<Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />:

| Setting          | Formula              | Per device |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | ---------- |
| Data parallelism | $16\Psi$             | 120 GB     |
| Stage 1          | $4\Psi + 12\Psi/N$   | 31.4 GB    |
| Stage 2          | $2\Psi + 14\Psi/N$   | 16.6 GB    |
| Stage 3          | $16\Psi/N$           | 1.9 GB     |

For stage 1, $4 \times 7.5 = 30$ GB of 16-bit weights and gradients plus $12 \times 7.5/64 = 1.4$ GB of optimiser state.
Stage 1 is bounded below by the full 16-bit weights and gradients, $4\Psi$, and stage 2 by the full 16-bit weights,
$2\Psi$, however many devices are used. Stage 3 has no such floor: with 1024 devices, each holds 117 MB of model state.

The table leaves out activations, temporary buffers and memory fragmentation, which ZeRO treats separately. For long
sequences, activations can exceed the sharded model state.

## Trade-offs

- **Memory against communication.** Stages 1 and 2 save memory at no extra communication and are almost always worth
  using. Stage 3 saves the most but moves 50% more data and adds latency to every layer.
- **Compared with model parallelism.** <NoteLink to="model-parallelism">Tensor and pipeline parallelism</NoteLink>
  also divide the weights, and additionally divide activations and computation within a layer, but require changes to
  the model and a bubble or per-layer all-reduces. ZeRO keeps the simplicity of data parallelism: each device runs the
  whole model on its own data.
- **Scale of the data-parallel group.** ZeRO's savings grow with $N$, so it pairs naturally with large data-parallel
  groups. At small $N$, stage 3 cannot fit a model that tensor parallelism could.
- **Offloading.** Optimiser states and even parameters can be moved further, to <Gloss name="cpu" /> memory or disk,
  trading PCIe bandwidth for accelerator memory.

## Pitfalls

- **Global operations on sharded state.** Gradient-norm clipping needs the norm over all shards, so each device's local
  sum of squares must be all-reduced before clipping.
- **Checkpoints.** Each device saves only its shard. Loading into a different number of devices requires re-sharding.
- **Small wrapped units in stage 3.** Gathering weights layer by layer in tiny pieces makes each collective
  latency-bound; units must be large enough to use the bandwidth.
