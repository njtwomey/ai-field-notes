<Definition>
The memory needed to train a neural network on one device is the sum of the **model state** (weights,
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> and optimiser state), the **activations** saved during the forward pass for
use in the backward pass, and **temporary buffers** for intermediate results and communication. Model state scales with
the number of parameters; activations scale with the <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink>,
the sequence length and the model's width and depth.
</Definition>

## Model state

Backpropagation needs the weights and produces one gradient per weight. The optimiser adds its own state. For $\Psi$
parameters, <NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">mixed-precision training</NoteLink> with Adam stores

| Item                          | Precision | Bytes per parameter |
| ----------------------------- | --------- | ------------------- |
| weights for forward/backward  | 16-bit    | 2                   |
| gradients                     | 16-bit    | 2                   |
| master weights                | 32-bit    | 4                   |
| Adam first moment             | 32-bit    | 4                   |
| Adam second moment            | 32-bit    | 4                   |
| **total**                     |           | **16**              |

so model state takes $16\Psi$ bytes <Cite id="rajbhandari2020" />. Training in fp32 with Adam also needs 16 bytes per
parameter: 4 each for weights, gradients and the two moments. <NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">SGD with
momentum</NoteLink> needs one moment instead of two. A model with $7 \times 10^9$ parameters needs 112 GB of model
state, before any activations. Inference needs only the 2 bytes of 16-bit weights, which is why a model can often be
served on hardware where it cannot be trained.

<NoteLink to="zero-and-sharded-optimisers">ZeRO</NoteLink> shards this state across data-parallel devices, and
<NoteLink to="model-parallelism">tensor and pipeline parallelism</NoteLink> split it across the devices that share a
model.

## Activations

### Why activations are stored

The backward pass of a layer $\yvec = f(\xvec; \wvec)$ needs $\partial \yvec / \partial \wvec$ and
$\partial \yvec / \partial \xvec$, which depend on the layer's input and sometimes its output. For a linear layer
$\yvec = \Wmat \xvec$, the weight gradient is $\bar{\yvec} \xvec^\tr$, where $\bar{\yvec}$ is the gradient arriving from
above, so the input $\xvec$ must be kept. Every such tensor saved during the forward pass stays in memory until the
backward pass reaches its layer. Activation memory therefore grows with depth, batch size and sequence length.

### Activation memory of a transformer layer

Korthikanti et al. count the bytes stored by one layer of a <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> with $b$
sequences of $s$ tokens, hidden width $h$ and $a$ attention heads, with activations in 16-bit precision (2 bytes) and
dropout masks in 1 byte <Cite id="korthikanti2022" />. Each $s \times b \times h$ tensor of 16-bit numbers costs $2sbh$
bytes.

**Attention block.**

- The query, key and value projections share one input: $2sbh$.
- The product $\Qmat\Kmat^\tr$ needs $\Qmat$ and $\Kmat$: $4sbh$.
- The softmax output, $a$ matrices of size $s \times s$ per sequence: $2as^2b$.
- The dropout mask on the attention probabilities: $as^2b$.
- The product with $\Vmat$ needs the dropped-out probabilities and $\Vmat$: $2as^2b + 2sbh$.
- The output projection needs its input: $2sbh$. Its dropout mask: $sbh$.

The total is $11sbh + 5as^2b$ bytes.

**<Gloss name="mlp" /> block.** The two linear layers store their inputs, of widths $h$ and $4h$: $2sbh + 8sbh$. The
GeLU needs its input: $8sbh$. The dropout mask: $sbh$. The total is $19sbh$ bytes.

**<NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">Layer normalisation</NoteLink>.** Each of the two stores its input: $4sbh$.

Summing,

$$
\text{activation bytes per layer} = 34 sbh + 5 a s^2 b = sbh\paren{34 + \frac{5as}{h}}.
$$

The first term is linear in sequence length; the second, from the attention matrices, is quadratic. The ratio $5as/h$
decides which dominates. For <Gloss name="gpt-3" />, with $a = 96$, $s = 2048$ and $h = 12288$, it is 80, more than
twice the linear term <Cite id="korthikanti2022" />.

### Reducing activation memory

- **Full recomputation.** Store only each layer's input, $2sbh$ bytes, and recompute the layer's internals during the
  backward pass. This is <NoteLink to="gradient-checkpointing">gradient checkpointing</NoteLink> at layer granularity
  <Cite id="chen2016" />. It costs about one extra forward pass, roughly a third more computation.
- **Selective recomputation.** The quadratic $5as^2b$ term comes from operations that take little arithmetic to
  recompute: the softmax, its dropout and the attention-weighted sum. Recomputing only these leaves $34sbh$ bytes per
  layer at a small extra cost <Cite id="korthikanti2022" />. Kernels that never store the attention matrix, such as
  <NoteLink to="flash-attention">FlashAttention</NoteLink>, remove this term as well.
- **Tensor and sequence parallelism.** With tensor parallelism of degree $t$, the attention and MLP internals are split
  across devices, but layer normalisation and dropout remain replicated. Sequence parallelism splits those along the
  token dimension, and the per-device activation memory becomes $sbh(34 + 5as/h)/t$ <Cite id="korthikanti2022" />.
- **Smaller micro-batches.** Activations are linear in $b$. Gradient accumulation over several micro-batches keeps the
  effective batch size.

### The output layer

The final projection to a vocabulary of $V$ tokens produces $s \times b \times V$ logits, and the loss needs them. For
$V = 50{,}257$ and $s = 2048$, the logits of one sequence take 412 MB in 32-bit precision, as much as a few transformer
layers. Computing the loss in chunks over tokens avoids holding them all at once.

## Worked example

GPT-3 175B has $L = 96$ layers, $h = 12288$ and $a = 96$, and was trained on sequences of $s = 2048$ tokens. With a
micro-batch of $b = 1$:

- Activations per layer: $2048 \times 12288 \times (34 + 80) = 2.87$ GB. Over 96 layers: 275 GB.
- With selective recomputation: $2048 \times 12288 \times 34 \times 96 = 82$ GB.
- With full recomputation: $2 \times 2048 \times 12288 \times 96 = 4.8$ GB.
- Model state: $16 \times 175 \times 10^9 = 2.8$ TB.

Both must be divided across many devices. With tensor and sequence parallelism of degree 8, the stored activations fall
to 34 GB per device and selective recomputation to 10 GB. The model state must additionally be split by pipeline
parallelism or ZeRO.

For a 7-billion-parameter model ($L = 32$, $h = 4096$, $a = 32$) at $s = 4096$, the ratio $5as/h$ is 160, and the
attention term makes stored activations 104 GB per sequence, against 18 GB with selective recomputation.

## Other contributions

- **Temporary buffers.** Communication buckets for gradient all-reduces, workspace for matrix kernels and the fused
  gradient of a large layer can each take gigabytes.
- **Fragmentation.** Tensors of different lifetimes freed and allocated in different orders leave unusable gaps, so the
  peak usable memory is below the device's capacity.
- **Peak, not total.** Memory is limited by the peak during a step. For activations, the peak is at the start of the
  backward pass, when every layer's activations are held.

## Pitfalls

- **Counting parameters, not state.** "The model is 14 GB in 16-bit" describes inference, not training, which needs
  eight times as much for model state alone.
- **Ignoring sequence length.** Activation memory is quadratic in $s$ unless attention matrices are recomputed or never
  stored; doubling the context can more than double memory.
- **Ignoring the vocabulary.** Large vocabularies make the logits and their gradient a large share of activation
  memory.
