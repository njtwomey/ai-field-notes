<Definition>
Mixed-precision training stores activations, weights for the forward and backward passes, and
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> in a 16-bit floating-point format, and runs
<NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix products</NoteLink> in that format with 32-bit accumulation. It keeps a
32-bit **master copy** of the weights to which updates are applied, and computes numerically sensitive reductions in 32
bits. With the fp16 format it also multiplies the loss by a **loss scale** before backpropagation and divides the
gradients by it before the update <Cite id="micikevicius2018" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A 16-bit number takes half the memory and half the memory bandwidth of a 32-bit number, and accelerators run 16-bit
matrix products many times faster than 32-bit ones. Training entirely in 16 bits, however, often diverges or loses
accuracy. The two causes are the limited precision and the limited range of 16-bit formats.

## Mechanism

### Floating-point formats

A binary floating-point number with $e$ exponent bits and $p$ fraction bits represents
$\pm (1.f_1 f_2 \dots f_p)_2 \times 2^{E}$. Between $2^E$ and $2^{E+1}$ there are $2^p$ equally spaced numbers, so the
spacing is $2^{E - p}$ and the relative precision is about $2^{-p}$. The exponent bits set the range. Below the smallest
normal number, **subnormal** numbers fill the gap to zero with fewer significant bits.
The <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">floating-point arithmetic</NoteLink> note covers the format in general.

| Format | Exponent / fraction bits | Largest finite       | Smallest normal         | Smallest subnormal     | Spacing at 1                   |
| ------ | ------------------------ | -------------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| fp32   | 8 / 23                   | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | $2^{-126}$              | $2^{-149}$             | $2^{-23} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$ |
| fp16   | 5 / 10                   | 65,504               | $2^{-14} \approx 6.1 \times 10^{-5}$ | $2^{-24} \approx 6.0 \times 10^{-8}$ | $2^{-10} \approx 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| bf16   | 8 / 7                    | $3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | $2^{-126}$              | $2^{-133}$             | $2^{-7} \approx 7.8 \times 10^{-3}$  |

fp16 has more precision and much less range. bf16 keeps fp32's 8 exponent bits, so it has the same range, and gives up
precision <Cite id="kalamkar2019" />.

### Why updates need a 32-bit master copy

<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> updates $w \leftarrow w - \eta g$. Suppose $\abs{w}$ lies in
$[2^E, 2^{E+1})$. In a format with $p$ fraction bits, neighbouring representable numbers near $w$ are $2^{E-p}$ apart.
Round-to-nearest maps $w + u$ back to $w$ whenever $\abs{u}$ is less than half of that spacing:

$$
\abs{u} < 2^{E - p - 1} \le \abs{w} \, 2^{-p - 1}.
$$

In fp16, $p = 10$, so an update smaller than about $\abs{w} / 2048$ can be lost entirely <Cite id="micikevicius2018" />.
Late in training, <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rates</Gloss> are small and $\abs{\eta g}$ is often thousands of
times smaller than $\abs{w}$. Every such step is silently discarded, and the discarded amounts do not accumulate. In
bf16, $p = 7$ and the threshold is $\abs{w}/256$, so the problem is worse.

The fix is to keep the weights that receive updates in fp32, where the threshold is $\abs{w}/2^{24}$, and to cast them
to 16 bits for each forward and backward pass. The optimiser state, such as Adam's moments, is kept in fp32 as well.
Micikevicius et al. also found that about 5% of the weight gradients in a speech model had magnitudes below $2^{-24}$
and would become zero in fp16 before even being multiplied by the learning rate <Cite id="micikevicius2018" />.

### Why fp16 gradients need loss scaling

Backpropagated gradients of activations are often tiny. In fp16, magnitudes below $2^{-25}$ round to zero and those just
above $2^{-24}$ keep only one significant bit. In a Multibox <Gloss name="single-shot-detector" /> detector trained in
fp32, 67% of the activation gradient values were zero, and many of the non-zero values lay below the fp16 range
<Cite id="micikevicius2018" />.

Backpropagation is linear in the gradient of the loss. Multiplying the loss by a constant $S$ multiplies every
intermediate gradient by $S$, by the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>:

$$
\frac{\partial (S L)}{\partial \theta} = S \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta}
$$

for every parameter and activation $\theta$. Choosing $S = 2^k$ shifts every gradient's exponent up by $k$ without
changing any fraction bits, so values below the fp16 range move into it. Before the optimiser step, the fp32 gradients
are divided by $S$, which restores their true values exactly. The only constraint is overflow: the largest scaled
gradient must stay below 65,504 <Cite id="micikevicius2018" />.

**Dynamic loss scaling** chooses $S$ automatically. Start with a large $S$. If any gradient is infinite or NaN after
backpropagation, skip the update and halve $S$. After a fixed number of steps without overflow, double $S$. The scale
then hovers just below the overflow threshold.

<LossScaling />

bf16's exponent range equals fp32's, so gradients neither underflow nor overflow any more often than in fp32, and bf16
training usually needs no loss scaling <Cite id="kalamkar2019" />.

### Which operations stay in 32 bits

- **Accumulation in matrix products.** Tensor cores multiply 16-bit inputs and accumulate the sum in fp32, then round
  the result to 16 bits. Accumulating a long dot product in fp16 would lose the small terms.
- **Reductions.** Sums over many elements, such as the mean and variance in normalisation layers and the softmax
  denominator, are computed in fp32.
- **The loss and the update.** The loss value, the unscaled gradients and the optimiser step use fp32.

### FP8

8-bit floating-point formats extend the idea. The E4M3 format (4 exponent bits, 3 fraction bits, largest value 448)
is used for weights and activations, and E5M2 (largest value 57,344) for gradients, which need more range. Each tensor
gets its own scale factor, a per-tensor version of loss scaling <Cite id="micikevicius2022" />.

## Cost

### Worked example

A weight $w = 1$ receives an update $u = -\eta g$ with $\eta = 10^{-3}$ and $g = 0.05$, so $u = -5 \times 10^{-5}$.

- In fp16 the spacing near 1 is $2^{-10} = 9.8 \times 10^{-4}$. Half of it, $4.9 \times 10^{-4}$, is ten times larger
  than $\abs{u}$, so $1 + u$ rounds to $1$ and the step is lost.
- In bf16 the spacing near 1 is $2^{-7} = 7.8 \times 10^{-3}$. Any update smaller than $3.9 \times 10^{-3}$ is lost.
- In fp32 the spacing near 1 is $1.2 \times 10^{-7}$, and $1 + u = 0.99995$ is stored almost exactly.

A gradient of $3 \times 10^{-9}$ is zero in fp16. With a loss scale of $2^{16}$ it becomes $1.97 \times 10^{-4}$, a
normal fp16 number with full 11-bit precision. Dividing by $2^{16}$ in fp32 afterwards returns $3 \times 10^{-9}$.

### Memory

Per parameter, mixed-precision Adam stores 2 bytes of 16-bit weights, 2 bytes of gradients, and 12 bytes of fp32 master
weights and moments: 16 bytes, against 16 bytes for fp32 Adam with fp32 weights, gradients and moments. The saving is
not in model state but in activations, which are stored in 16 bits, and in memory bandwidth and arithmetic speed. See
<NoteLink to="zero-and-sharded-optimisers">ZeRO</NoteLink> for sharding these 16 bytes.

## Trade-offs

- **fp16 against bf16.** fp16 has three more bits of precision but needs loss scaling and careful handling of overflow.
  bf16 needs no scaling and is the usual choice on hardware that supports it.
- **Precision against speed.** Lower precision runs faster and stores more activations per gigabyte. Operations whose
  results depend on small differences, such as reductions and updates, must stay in fp32.
- **Static against dynamic scaling.** A fixed scale is simpler but must be tuned per model. Dynamic scaling adapts but
  skips occasional steps.

## Pitfalls

- **Clipping scaled gradients.** Gradient-norm clipping must happen after dividing by the loss scale, or the threshold
  is effectively multiplied by $S$.
- **Silent skipped steps.** With dynamic scaling, a model that overflows every step never updates. Log the scale and the
  number of skipped steps.
- **Casting the optimiser state.** Storing Adam's second moment in 16 bits can underflow for small gradients and
  produce huge steps.
- **Comparing fp16 and fp32 runs bitwise.** Mixed-precision results differ in low-order bits and can diverge over long
  runs; compare metrics, not weights.
