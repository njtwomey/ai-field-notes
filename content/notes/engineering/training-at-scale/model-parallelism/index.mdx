<Definition>
Model parallelism places different parts of one model on different devices. **Tensor parallelism** splits the weight
matrices inside each layer across $t$ devices, which compute each layer together. **Pipeline parallelism** assigns a
contiguous run of layers, a **stage**, to each of $p$ devices, and passes activations forward and
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> backward between neighbouring stages. The two are combined with
<NoteLink to="data-parallelism">data parallelism</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="data-parallelism">Data parallelism</NoteLink> keeps a full copy of the model on every device. A model with
$6.7 \times 10^9$ parameters already needs about 107 GB for its weights, gradients and Adam state in
<NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">mixed precision</NoteLink>, more than one 80 GB accelerator holds. Even when the
state is sharded, one layer's activations and weights can exceed a device. The model itself must be divided, and the
division must keep every device busy and the communication small.

## Mechanism

### Tensor parallelism of a transformer MLP

The feed-forward block of a <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> computes

$$
\Ymat = \operatorname{GeLU}(\Xmat \Amat), \qquad \Zmat = \Ymat \Bmat,
$$

with input $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times h}$ for $n$ tokens of width $h$, $\Amat \in \reals^{h \times 4h}$ and
$\Bmat \in \reals^{4h \times h}$. Megatron-LM splits it over $t$ devices as follows <Cite id="shoeybi2019" />.

**Split $\Amat$ by columns.** Write $\Amat = [\Amat_1, \dots, \Amat_t]$ with $\Amat_i \in \reals^{h \times 4h/t}$ on
device $i$. Then $\Xmat \Amat = [\Xmat \Amat_1, \dots, \Xmat \Amat_t]$, and because GeLU acts element by element,

$$
\operatorname{GeLU}(\Xmat \Amat) = \brack{\operatorname{GeLU}(\Xmat \Amat_1), \dots, \operatorname{GeLU}(\Xmat \Amat_t)}.
$$

Device $i$ computes its slice $\Ymat_i = \operatorname{GeLU}(\Xmat \Amat_i)$ with no communication, given a copy of
$\Xmat$.

Splitting $\Amat$ by rows would fail here. With $\Amat$ split by rows and $\Xmat$ by columns, each device holds a
partial product $\Xmat_i \Amat_i$ with $\Xmat \Amat = \sum_i \Xmat_i \Amat_i$, but
$\operatorname{GeLU}\paren{\sum_i \Xmat_i \Amat_i} \ne \sum_i \operatorname{GeLU}(\Xmat_i \Amat_i)$, so the partial
products would have to be summed across devices before the non-linearity.

**Split $\Bmat$ by rows.** Write $\Bmat$ as the stack of blocks $\Bmat_i \in \reals^{4h/t \times h}$. Block
<NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix multiplication</NoteLink> gives

$$
\Zmat = [\Ymat_1, \dots, \Ymat_t] \begin{bmatrix} \Bmat_1 \\ \vdots \\ \Bmat_t \end{bmatrix} = \sum_{i=1}^{t} \Ymat_i \Bmat_i.
$$

Each device computes $\Ymat_i \Bmat_i$ from what it already holds, and one **all-reduce** sums the $t$ partial results.
The whole block needs one all-reduce in the forward pass. In the backward pass the gradient with respect to $\Xmat$ is
likewise a sum of per-device terms, needing one more.

**Attention** splits the same way. The heads of <NoteLink to="multi-head-attention">multi-head attention</NoteLink> are
independent, so each device computes the queries, keys and values of $h_{\text{heads}}/t$ heads (a column split of the
projection matrices) and its heads' attention, and the output projection is split by rows, followed by one all-reduce.
A transformer layer thus needs two all-reduces of an $n \times h$ activation in the forward pass and two in the backward
pass <Cite id="shoeybi2019" />.

These all-reduces sit on the critical path of every layer, so tensor parallelism is used within a server, where devices
share fast links, typically with $t \le 8$. Megatron-LM trained an 8.3-billion-parameter model on 512
<Gloss name="gpu" plural /> at 15.1 PetaFLOP/s, 76% scaling efficiency relative to one GPU, combining 8-way tensor
parallelism with data parallelism <Cite id="shoeybi2019" />.

### Pipeline parallelism

Split the $L$ layers into $p$ stages of $L/p$ layers on $p$ devices. A single batch would occupy one stage at a time
and leave the other $p - 1$ idle. GPipe splits each mini-batch into $m$ **micro-batches** and streams them through the
stages, so different stages work on different micro-batches at once, and accumulates gradients over all $m$ before one
synchronous update <Cite id="huang2019" />.

**The bubble.** Let one micro-batch take time $t_f$ forward and $t_b$ backward on one stage. Micro-batch $j$
($j = 1, \dots, m$) starts on stage $s$ ($s = 1, \dots, p$) at time $(s - 1 + j - 1) t_f$, because it must wait for
stage $s - 1$ to finish it and for stage $s$ to finish micro-batch $j - 1$. The last micro-batch leaves the last stage
at $(m + p - 1) t_f$. The backward pass runs through the stages in reverse and by the same argument takes
$(m + p - 1) t_b$. The step takes

$$
T_{\text{pipe}} = (m + p - 1)(t_f + t_b),
$$

while the useful work on each stage is $m (t_f + t_b)$. Each stage is idle for $(p - 1)(t_f + t_b)$, a fraction

$$
\frac{(p - 1)(t_f + t_b)}{(m + p - 1)(t_f + t_b)} = \frac{p - 1}{m + p - 1}
$$

of the step. Relative to the ideal time, the overhead is $(p - 1)/m$ <Cite id="narayanan2021" />. In this schedule,
the bubble is small only when there are many more micro-batches than stages, $m \gg p$.

**<NoteLink to="memory-footprint-of-training">Activation memory</NoteLink> and 1F1B.** GPipe runs all $m$ forward passes
before any backward pass, so each stage stores the activations of all $m$ micro-batches. The **1F1B** schedule starts
the backward pass of a micro-batch as soon as it has left the last stage. After a warm-up of at most $p - 1$ forward
passes, each stage alternates one forward and one backward pass, so it holds the activations of at most $p$
micro-batches. The step time and bubble are unchanged <Cite id="narayanan2021" />.

<PipelineSchedule />

**Interleaving.** If each device holds $v$ non-adjacent chunks of layers instead of one contiguous stage, a micro-batch
passes through each device $v$ times, each pass $v$ times shorter. The bubble falls to $(p - 1)/(v m)$ of the ideal
time, at the cost of $v$ times more point-to-point communication <Cite id="narayanan2021" />.

Pipeline parallelism sends only the activations at stage boundaries, one $n \times h$ tensor per micro-batch per
boundary, between two devices. That is much less than tensor parallelism's all-reduces, so pipeline stages can span
servers.

### Combining the three: 3D parallelism

Large models combine all three: tensor parallelism of degree $t$ within a server, pipeline parallelism of degree $p$
across servers, and data parallelism of degree $d$ over replicas of the whole pipeline, on $t \cdot p \cdot d$ devices.
Narayanan et al. trained a model with one trillion parameters on 3072 GPUs at 502 PetaFLOP/s, 52% of the GPUs' peak
throughput, with this combination and the interleaved schedule <Cite id="narayanan2021" />.

Tensor parallelism leaves some activations replicated: <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink>
and dropout operate on the full $n \times h$ tensor on every device. **Sequence parallelism** splits those operations
along the token dimension, which replaces each all-reduce with a reduce-scatter and an all-gather of the same total
volume and divides their activation memory by $t$ <Cite id="korthikanti2022" />.

## Cost

### Worked example

**Bubble.** With $p = 8$ stages and $m = 32$ micro-batches, the bubble is $7/39 = 18\%$ of the step. Reducing it to 5%
requires $(p - 1)/(m + p - 1) \le 0.05$, that is $m \ge 19 (p - 1) = 133$ micro-batches. With micro-batches of one
sequence, that fixes a global batch of at least 133 sequences per pipeline replica.

**Tensor-parallel <Gloss name="mlp" />.** <Gloss name="gpt-3" /> has width $h = 12288$ <Cite id="korthikanti2022" />.
One MLP holds $2 \times 12288 \times 49152 = 1.21 \times 10^9$ weights, 2.4 GB in 16-bit precision. With $t = 8$, each
device holds 302 MB of it. For a micro-batch of one sequence of 2048 tokens, each all-reduce carries a
$2048 \times 12288$ activation, 50 MB in 16-bit precision, and a layer needs two in the forward pass and two in the
backward pass.

## Trade-offs

| Scheme            | Splits              | Communication                                 | Main cost                            |
| ----------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Data parallel     | the batch           | all-reduce of all gradients once per step     | replicated weights and optimiser state |
| Tensor parallel   | each weight matrix  | all-reduce of activations, several per layer  | needs fast links; small matrices per device |
| Pipeline parallel | the layers          | point-to-point activations at stage boundaries | the bubble; balancing stages          |

- **Tensor-parallel degree.** Larger $t$ fits bigger layers but shrinks each device's matrices, lowering arithmetic
  efficiency, and multiplies communication. Beyond one server, the links are too slow.
- **Pipeline depth.** More stages cut memory per device but enlarge the bubble unless the number of micro-batches grows
  with them, which raises the global <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink>.
- **Stage balance.** The slowest stage sets the pace. The embedding and output layers are often much larger or smaller
  than a transformer block and must be balanced by hand.

## Pitfalls

- **Weight-tied layers across stages.** A shared input embedding and output projection on different stages need their
  gradients summed across those stages.
- **Random state.** Dropout inside a tensor-parallel region must use the same random numbers on every device for
  replicated tensors and different ones for split tensors.
- **Too few micro-batches.** A pipeline with $m < p$ is idle most of the time; with $m = 1$ it is no faster than one
  device.
- **Pipeline flushes with non-synchronous schedules.** Schedules that update weights without a flush use stale weights
  for some micro-batches, which changes the optimiser's behaviour.
