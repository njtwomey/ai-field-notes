<Definition>
Let a model be trained on examples drawn from a **source** distribution $p_s(x, y)$ over inputs $x$ and targets $y$, and
used on examples from a **target** distribution $p_t(x, y)$. **Dataset shift** is the case $p_s(x, y) \ne p_t(x, y)$
<Cite id="quinonero2009" />. Writing the joint distribution as $p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$ or as $p(y)\,p(x \mid y)$ gives three
named cases:

- **covariate shift**: $p_s(x) \ne p_t(x)$ and $p_s(y \mid x) = p_t(y \mid x)$;
- **label shift**, or prior probability shift: $p_s(y) \ne p_t(y)$ and $p_s(x \mid y) = p_t(x \mid y)$;
- **concept shift**: the relation between inputs and targets changes, $p_s(y \mid x) \ne p_t(y \mid x)$ with $p(x)$
  unchanged, or $p_s(x \mid y) \ne p_t(x \mid y)$ with $p(y)$ unchanged.
</Definition>

## Why it matters

Supervised learning minimises the average loss over training examples, which estimates the **source risk**
$\expect_{p_s}\brack{\ell(f(x), y)}$. Held-out test sets drawn from the same data estimate the same quantity. A model
is judged in use by the **target risk** $\expect_{p_t}\brack{\ell(f(x), y)}$. The two agree only if $p_s = p_t$. Every
guarantee derived from a test set assumes that the test data come from the distribution the model will meet.

Shift is common in deployed systems. Users, sensors, prices and policies change; a model trained on one hospital is used
in another; a fraud model changes the behaviour of the fraudsters it catches. Shift that develops over time in a data
stream is usually called **drift**, and is described by its speed: sudden, gradual, incremental or recurring
<Cite id="gama2014" />.

## The three kinds

### Which factorisation to use

The three definitions differ in which factor of the joint distribution stays fixed, and the natural factorisation
follows the causal direction. In an $X \to Y$ problem the inputs cause the target, as when symptoms are recorded and an
outcome follows; $p(y \mid x)$ is the stable mechanism, and $p(x)$ may change. In a $Y \to X$ problem the target
causes the inputs, as when a disease produces symptoms or a spoken word produces a sound; $p(x \mid y)$ is stable and
$p(y)$ may change. Moreno-Torres et al. define covariate shift only for $X \to Y$ problems and prior probability shift
only for $Y \to X$ problems for this reason <Cite id="morenotorres2012" />.

### Covariate shift

Under covariate shift, $p(y \mid x)$ is unchanged, so the ideal prediction for each input is unchanged too. What changes
is how much each input contributes to the risk. The target risk can be rewritten as an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over the source distribution:

$$
\expect_{p_t}\brack{\ell(f(x), y)}
= \int p_t(x) \, p(y \mid x) \, \ell(f(x), y) \, dx\, dy
= \int p_s(x) \, p(y \mid x) \, \frac{p_t(x)}{p_s(x)} \, \ell(f(x), y) \, dx\, dy
= \expect_{p_s}\brack{w(x) \, \ell(f(x), y)},
$$

with **importance weight** $w(x) = p_t(x) / p_s(x)$, defined where $p_s(x) > 0$. The second step uses the unchanged
conditional. Minimising the weighted training loss $\frac1n \sum_i w(x_i)\,\ell(f(x_i), y_i)$ therefore minimises an
unbiased estimate of the target risk <Cite id="shimodaira2000" />. This is
<NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> applied to the risk.

Covariate shift harms a model only if the model is **misspecified**. If the model class contains the true regression
function, the unweighted fit converges to it wherever $p_s(x) > 0$, and the prediction is right for any input
distribution on that support. If the class is too simple, the best approximation depends on which inputs are weighted
most, and the fit to the source is not the best fit to the target <Cite id="shimodaira2000" />.

Importance weighting increases the variance of the risk estimate. When the distributions differ strongly, a few training points get very large weights and
the weighted estimate is noisy. Shimodaira proposed weights $w(x)^\lambda$ with $0 \le \lambda \le 1$, trading bias
($\lambda = 0$) against variance ($\lambda = 1$) <Cite id="shimodaira2000" />. A common diagnostic is the <Gloss name="ess">effective
sample size</Gloss> $\paren{\sum_i w_i}^2 / \sum_i w_i^2$.

<CovariateShift />

No weighting corrects covariate shift outside the source support: where $p_t(x) > 0$ but $p_s(x) = 0$, the training data say
nothing, and $w$ is undefined.

### Label shift

Under label shift, $p(x \mid y)$ is unchanged and the class proportions change, for example a disease becoming more
common during an epidemic. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, with <NoteLink to="class-weights">class weights</NoteLink>
$w(y) = p_t(y)/p_s(y)$,

$$
p_t(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid y)\,p_t(y)}{\sum_{y'} p(x \mid y')\,p_t(y')}
= \frac{p_s(y \mid x)\, w(y)}{\sum_{y'} p_s(y' \mid x)\, w(y')},
$$

where the second form divides numerator and denominator by $p_s(x)$ and uses $p(x \mid y) p_s(y) / p_s(x) = p_s(y \mid x)$.
A calibrated source classifier can therefore be corrected by reweighting its outputs and renormalising, without
retraining. The target proportions $p_t(y)$ are usually unknown, but they can be estimated from unlabelled target data
by inverting the classifier's <NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink>, as in black-box shift estimation <Cite id="lipton2018" />. The
<NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction">prior-shift correction</NoteLink> note covers the estimators.

### Concept shift

Concept shift changes the mapping itself: the same input now has a different correct output. A spam filter meets new
kinds of spam; a credit model meets a new economic regime; the definition of a label changes. Unlabelled target data
cannot reveal concept shift in an $X \to Y$ problem, because $p(x)$ may not change at all. Only new labels show it, and
the remedy is to retrain or adapt on labelled data from the new regime.

## Worked example

A classifier is trained on data where 10% of cases are positive, $p_s(y = 1) = 0.1$, and is well calibrated there. It is
deployed where 30% are positive, and the class-conditional input distributions are unchanged. The class weights are
$w(1) = 0.3/0.1 = 3$ and $w(0) = 0.7/0.9 = 0.778$.

For an input where the source classifier outputs $p_s(y = 1 \mid x) = 0.5$, the corrected probability is

$$
p_t(y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{0.5 \times 3}{0.5 \times 3 + 0.5 \times 0.778} = \frac{1.5}{1.889} = 0.794.
$$

The uncorrected output places this input exactly at a threshold of 0.5; after correction it is positive with
probability 0.79. The ranking of inputs by score is unchanged, because the correction is monotone in the source
probability, so ranking metrics such as AUC are unaffected by label shift while calibration and thresholded accuracy are
not.

## Detection and response

- **Detect.** Compare recent inputs, and the model's predictions, with a reference sample from training using
  <NoteLink to="drift-detection">two-sample tests</NoteLink>.
- **Diagnose.** Check whether the shift is in inputs, in label proportions or in the relation between them; the three
  call for different remedies.
- **Respond.** Reweight or recalibrate for covariate and label shift, and retrain on recent labelled data for concept
  shift.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming <Gloss name="iid" /> test sets predict deployment.** A random split of historical data estimates the source risk only.
  A split by time, site or user group estimates performance under the shifts those splits contain.
- **Estimating weights in high dimensions.** Density ratios of images or text are hard to estimate directly. A
  classifier trained to distinguish source from target inputs gives the ratio through its odds, and fails when the two
  are almost perfectly separable.
- **Confusing the kinds.** Correcting for label shift when the real change is in $p(y \mid x)$ makes predictions worse.
