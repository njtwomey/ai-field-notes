<Definition>
Quantisation maps each real-valued weight or activation $x$ to an integer $q$ with $b$ bits, using a real **scale**
$s > 0$ and an integer **zero-point** $z$:

$$
q = \operatorname{clamp}\paren{\operatorname{round}(x / s) + z,\; 0,\; 2^b - 1}, \qquad \hat x = s\,(q - z).
$$

The quantised model stores $q$, $s$ and $z$ instead of $x$, and computes <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix products</NoteLink> in integer arithmetic. The
approximation $\hat x$ differs from $x$ by a <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding error</NoteLink> inside the representable range and by a clipping error
outside it.
</Definition>

## Problem

A trained network stores its weights as 32-bit or 16-bit floating-point numbers. A model with $P$ parameters in 16-bit
precision needs $2P$ bytes: 14 GB for $P = 7 \times 10^9$. During autoregressive decoding every weight is read from
memory once per generated token, so the time per token is set mostly by the number of bytes read, not by arithmetic
(see <NoteLink to="key-value-cache">key-value caching</NoteLink>). Integer arithmetic units are also smaller and use less energy than
floating-point units of the same width <Cite id="nagel2021" />.

Storing each weight in $b < 16$ bits cuts memory and memory traffic by a factor of $16 / b$. The same 7-billion
parameter model needs 7 GB at 8 bits and 3.5 GB at 4 bits, before the small overhead of the scales. The question is how
to choose the integer grid so that the network's outputs barely change.

## Mechanism

### Uniform affine quantisation

Let the values to be quantised lie in a chosen **clipping range** $[\alpha, \beta]$. A $b$-bit unsigned integer takes
$2^b$ values, $0, \dots, 2^b - 1$, so $2^b - 1$ equal steps span the range. The scale is the width of one step,

$$
s = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{2^b - 1}.
$$

The zero-point is the integer that represents the real number 0:

$$
z = \operatorname{round}\paren{-\alpha / s}.
$$

Dividing by $s$ converts $x$ to units of steps. Adding $z$ shifts the grid so that $\alpha$ maps near 0 and $\beta$
near $2^b - 1$. Rounding picks the nearest grid point, and clamping maps values outside the range to the end points.
Dequantisation inverts the affine map: $\hat x = s(q - z)$.

The zero-point is an integer, not the real number $-\alpha/s$, so that $x = 0$ is represented exactly: $q = z$ gives
$\hat x = 0$. Zero is the value of padding, of <Gloss name="relu" /> outputs below threshold, and of pruned weights, so an error there
would bias every sum that contains it <Cite id="jacob2018" />.

**Symmetric quantisation** fixes $z = 0$ and uses signed integers $-(2^{b-1} - 1), \dots, 2^{b-1} - 1$ with
$s = c / (2^{b-1} - 1)$ for a range $[-c, c]$. Weights are roughly symmetric about zero, so symmetric quantisation
wastes little range on them. Activations after a ReLU are non-negative, and an asymmetric range doubles their
resolution.

### Rounding error has variance $s^2 / 12$

Take a value $x$ inside the clipping range. Rounding moves $x/s$ to the nearest integer, so the error
$e = \hat x - x$ lies in $[-s/2, s/2]$. When the values are spread over many steps and the distribution is smooth at the
scale of one step, the fractional part of $x/s$ is close to uniform, so $e$ is close to uniform on $[-s/2, s/2]$. Its
mean is zero and its variance is

$$
\expect\brack{e^2} = \frac{1}{s} \int_{-s/2}^{s/2} e^2 \, de = \frac{1}{s} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \paren{\frac{s}{2}}^3 = \frac{s^2}{12}.
$$

For a fixed range, one more bit roughly halves $s$, so the error variance falls by a factor of 4. On a decibel scale
that is $10 \log_{10} 4 \approx 6.02$ dB per bit, the same rule as for
<NoteLink to="quantisation">quantisation of signals</NoteLink>.

### Clipping trades range for resolution

Values outside $[\alpha, \beta]$ are clamped to the end points and incur an error equal to their distance from the
range. The total <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> for a symmetric range $[-c, c]$ is

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(c) = \underbrace{\frac{s(c)^2}{12} \pr\paren{\abs{x} \le c}}_{\text{rounding}} + \underbrace{\expect\brack{(\abs{x} - c)^2 \, \indicator\set{\abs{x} > c}}}_{\text{clipping}}, \qquad s(c) = \frac{c}{2^{b-1} - 1}.
$$

The rounding term grows as $c^2$ and the clipping term falls as $c$ grows, so there is an interior minimum. Setting $c$
to the largest absolute value, the simplest choice, makes the clipping term zero but lets a single outlier widen every
step. For two million standard Gaussian weights at 4 bits, the largest value is $5.35$ <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviations</NoteLink> and gives an
MSE of $0.049$. The minimising range is $c = 2.45$ standard deviations and gives $0.013$, about a quarter. Range-setting
methods therefore search over $c$, by grid search or golden-section search, for the minimum of the MSE on the tensor
itself or on a calibration batch <Cite id="nagel2021" />.

### Integer matrix products

A linear layer computes $y_i = \sum_k W_{ik} x_k$. Quantise the weights with scale $s_w$ and zero-point $z_w$, and the
input with $s_x$ and $z_x$. Substituting $W_{ik} \approx s_w(q^w_{ik} - z_w)$ and $x_k \approx s_x(q^x_k - z_x)$ gives

$$
y_i \approx s_w s_x \sum_k \paren{q^w_{ik} - z_w}\paren{q^x_k - z_x}
= s_w s_x \paren{\sum_k q^w_{ik} q^x_k - z_x \sum_k q^w_{ik} - z_w \sum_k q^x_k + K z_w z_x},
$$

where $K$ is the length of the sum. The first sum is an integer dot product, accumulated in 32 bits to avoid overflow.
The second term depends only on the weights and is precomputed. The third costs one sum over the input per product.
With symmetric weights ($z_w = 0$) the third and fourth terms vanish, which is why weights are usually quantised
symmetrically <Cite id="jacob2018" />. The single multiplication by the real factor $s_w s_x$, often folded into the
next layer's scale, is the only floating-point step.

### Granularity: per tensor, per channel, per group

One scale per tensor is cheapest but lets the output channel with the widest range set the step for all the others.
**Per-channel** quantisation gives each output row $i$ of $\Wmat$ its own scale $s_{w,i}$. The factor $s_{w,i} s_x$ in
the expansion above still multiplies whole sums, so the integer kernel is unchanged, and per-channel weight scales are
the standard choice at 8 bits <Cite id="nagel2021" />. At 4 bits, weights are split into **groups** of 64 or 128
consecutive inputs, each with its own scale. A 16-bit scale per group of 128 adds $16/128 = 0.125$ bits per weight.

Activations cannot have per-input-channel scales, because a per-$k$ scale sits inside the sum over $k$ and cannot be
factored out. This matters for <Gloss name="llm">large language models</Gloss>: beyond about 6.7 billion parameters, a few hidden dimensions carry
activations up to about 20 times larger than the rest in almost every layer, and per-tensor activation quantisation
then fails <Cite id="dettmers2022" />. LLM.int8() keeps those outlier dimensions in 16-bit arithmetic and quantises the
other 99.9% of values to 8 bits.

### Post-training quantisation

Post-training quantisation (PTQ) quantises a trained model using at most a small calibration set of unlabelled inputs
to set activation ranges. Simple rounding to nearest works well at 8 bits for most networks. At 4 bits and below, the
errors of many weights add up in each output, and methods that choose the rounding to minimise the error of each
layer's output, not of each weight, do much better <Cite id="nagel2021" />.

- **GPTQ** quantises a linear layer's weights one input column at a time to minimise
  $\norm{\Wmat \Xmat - \hat\Wmat \Xmat}_F^2$ on calibration inputs $\Xmat$. After a column is rounded, the remaining
  unquantised columns are adjusted to cancel its error, using the inverse of the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> $\Hmat = 2 \Xmat \Xmat^\tr$ of
  that squared error. GPTQ quantises a 175-billion-parameter model to 3 or 4 bits in about four <Gloss name="gpu" /> hours with a small
  loss in perplexity <Cite id="frantar2023" />.
- **AWQ** observes that about 1% of weight channels, those that multiply large activations, cause most of the output
  error. It multiplies each such input channel of $\Wmat$ by a factor $\sigma > 1$ and divides the matching activation by
  $\sigma$, which leaves the product unchanged. The group scale barely moves, so the relative rounding error of the
  salient weights falls by about $\sigma$ <Cite id="lin2024" />. AWQ needs no backpropagation and no weight updates.

### Quantisation-aware training

Quantisation-aware training (QAT) inserts the quantise–dequantise operation $x \mapsto \hat x$ into the <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">forward pass</NoteLink>
during training or fine-tuning, so that the network learns weights that survive rounding <Cite id="jacob2018" />. The
rounding function has zero <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> almost everywhere. Training therefore uses the straight-through estimator: the
backward pass treats $\partial \hat x / \partial x$ as 1 inside the clipping range and 0 outside it. The scales can be
learned as parameters too. QAT recovers more accuracy than PTQ at 4 bits and below, at the cost of a training run and
access to training data <Cite id="nagel2021" />.

## Worked example

Quantise $\xvec = (-1.2, -0.3, 0, 0.45, 2.1)$ to $b = 4$ unsigned bits with range $[\alpha, \beta] = [-1.2, 2.1]$.

- Scale: $s = 3.3 / 15 = 0.22$.
- Zero-point: $-\alpha / s = 5.45$, so $z = 5$.
- Codes: $x / s = (-5.45, -1.36, 0, 2.05, 9.55)$ rounds to $(-5, -1, 0, 2, 10)$. Adding $z$ gives
  $q = (0, 4, 5, 7, 15)$.
- Reconstruction: $\hat x = 0.22 \, (q - 5) = (-1.10, -0.22, 0, 0.44, 2.20)$.

The errors are $(0.10, 0.08, 0, -0.01, 0.10)$. Zero is exact, as designed. Rounding $z$ from $5.45$ to $5$ shifted the
grid by $0.45$ steps, so the end points are no longer exactly representable. The largest possible rounding error is
$s/2 = 0.11$, and the uniform model predicts an error variance of $s^2 / 12 = 0.0040$; the five errors have mean square
$0.0053$.

## Cost

| Format                       | Bits per weight | 7B model | Typical accuracy effect                     |
| ---------------------------- | --------------- | -------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| bf16                         | 16              | 14 GB    | reference                                   |
| int8, per channel            | 8               | 7 GB     | negligible for most models                  |
| int4, groups of 128 (+scale) | 4.125           | 3.6 GB   | small with GPTQ or AWQ, larger with rounding |

Weight-only quantisation speeds up memory-bound decoding roughly in proportion to the bytes saved, because the weights
are dequantised on the fly inside the matrix kernel. It does not speed up compute-bound prefill unless the activations
are quantised too, so that the product runs on integer or 8-bit floating-point units.

## Trade-offs

- **Bits against accuracy.** Eight bits usually has a negligible effect on accuracy; four bits needs
  finer granularity or error-aware rounding; below three bits most models lose noticeable accuracy without QAT.
- **Granularity against overhead.** Smaller groups track local ranges better but store more scales and complicate the
  kernel.
- **PTQ against QAT.** PTQ needs minutes to hours and no labels. QAT needs a training pipeline and data but reaches
  lower bit widths.
- **Weights against activations.** Weight ranges are known in advance. Activation ranges depend on the input and have
  outliers, so activation quantisation needs calibration data and often per-token scales or outlier handling.

## Pitfalls

- **Calibration data must match deployment.** Activation ranges set on unrepresentative inputs clip real inputs.
- **Accumulators must be wide.** Each product of two signed 8-bit integers needs up to 15 bits, and a sum of 4096 of them
  needs up to 27 bits; accumulating in 16 bits overflows.
- **<NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">Batch normalisation</NoteLink> must be folded first.** Folding the normalisation's scale into the weights after quantisation
  changes the weight ranges and invalidates the scales <Cite id="jacob2018" />.
- **Perplexity hides failures.** A quantised <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> can keep its average perplexity while failing on long
  reasoning chains or rare languages; evaluate the downstream task.
