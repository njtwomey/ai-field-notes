<Definition>
Knowledge distillation trains a **student** model on the outputs of a trained **teacher** model. Both models' logits
are divided by a temperature $T \ge 1$ before the softmax. The student minimises a weighted sum of the cross-entropy
between the teacher's and its own softened distributions, scaled by $T^2$, and the ordinary cross-entropy with the true
labels at $T = 1$. At deployment the student runs at $T = 1$ <Cite id="hinton2015" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The most accurate model for a task is often too large or too slow to serve: an ensemble of many networks, or one very
large network. A smaller network trained on the same labelled data from scratch is usually much less accurate than the
large model, even when it has enough capacity to represent a similar function.

The trained large model knows more than its top-1 predictions. For an image of a handwritten 7, it assigns small but
very different probabilities to the wrong classes: perhaps $10^{-3}$ to 2 and $10^{-7}$ to 6. These ratios say which
classes resemble each other, and a one-hot label carries none of this information. Buciluǎ, Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil
showed that a small network trained to mimic a large ensemble on a large unlabelled transfer set could match the
ensemble's accuracy <Cite id="bucilua2006" />. Distillation turns this idea into a loss function.

## Mechanism

### Softened distributions

For logits $\zvec \in \reals^N$ over $N$ classes, the softmax at temperature $T$ is

$$
p_i(T) = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(z_j / T)}.
$$

At $T = 1$ it is the ordinary softmax. As $T$ grows, the logits are compressed towards each other and the distribution
flattens towards uniform, while the ranking of the classes stays the same. Small probabilities such as $10^{-7}$ and
$10^{-3}$, whose logarithms differ by about 9, become large enough to affect a cross-entropy loss.

### The loss

Let $\zvec$ be the teacher's logits and $\vvec$ the student's, and let $\pvec(T)$ and $\qvec(T)$ be their softmaxes at
temperature $T$. For a labelled example $(x, y)$, the distillation loss is

$$
\Lcal = (1 - \lambda)\paren{-\log q_y(1)} + \lambda \, T^2 \paren{-\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i(T) \log q_i(T)},
$$

with a weight $\lambda \in [0, 1]$. The soft term is the cross-entropy from the teacher's softened distribution to the
student's. It differs from $\KL\paren{\pvec(T) \,\|\, \qvec(T)}$ only by the teacher's entropy, which does not depend on
the student, so both give the same gradients. The soft term needs no labels, so it can use extra unlabelled transfer
data. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean found that a considerably lower weight on the hard-label term worked best
<Cite id="hinton2015" />.

### Why the soft loss is multiplied by $T^2$

Write the soft cross-entropy as $C = -\sum_i p_i \log q_i$ with $q_i = \exp(v_i/T) / \sum_j \exp(v_j/T)$. The
derivative of $\log q_i$ with respect to $v_k$ is $\paren{\indicator\set{i = k} - q_k} / T$. Hence

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial v_k} = -\sum_i p_i \frac{\indicator\set{i = k} - q_k}{T} = \frac{1}{T}\paren{q_k - p_k},
$$

using $\sum_i p_i = 1$. There is one explicit factor $1/T$. A second factor comes from $q_k - p_k$ itself. For $T$ large
compared with the logits, $\exp(v/T) \approx 1 + v/T$, so

$$
q_k \approx \frac{1 + v_k / T}{N + \sum_j v_j / T}.
$$

If both sets of logits have mean zero, which can be arranged by subtracting the mean because the softmax is unchanged by
it, the denominators equal $N$ and

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial v_k} \approx \frac{1}{T} \cdot \frac{v_k - z_k}{N T} = \frac{1}{N T^2}\paren{v_k - z_k}.
$$

The soft-target gradient therefore shrinks as $1/T^2$. Multiplying the soft loss by $T^2$ keeps its gradients on the
same scale as the hard-label term's whichever temperature is chosen, so $\lambda$ and the learning rate need not be
retuned when $T$ changes <Cite id="hinton2015" />.

The same expansion shows what distillation becomes at high temperature. The gradient $(v_k - z_k)/N$ of the scaled loss
is the gradient of $\frac{1}{2N} \sum_k (v_k - z_k)^2$, so the student matches the teacher's mean-centred logits by
squared error, which is the logit-matching method of Buciluǎ et al. At intermediate temperatures, distillation pays less
attention to logits far below the mean. These logits are poorly constrained by the teacher's training, so ignoring them
can help <Cite id="hinton2015" />.

### Worked example

Take $N = 4$ classes, teacher logits $\zvec = (5, 2, 1, -1)$ and student logits $\vvec = (4, 3, 0, -2)$.

- At $T = 1$ the teacher's distribution is $(0.934, 0.047, 0.017, 0.002)$. At $T = 4$ it is
  $(0.485, 0.229, 0.178, 0.108)$: the same order, but the second and third classes are now visible.
- The soft-loss gradient at $T = 4$ is $(\qvec - \pvec)/T = (-0.0157, 0.0249, -0.0058, -0.0035)$. Multiplied by
  $T^2 = 16$ it becomes $(-0.25, 0.40, -0.09, -0.06)$.
- At $T = 100$ the raw gradient has entries near $10^{-5}$, but $T^2$ times it is $(-0.13, 0.38, -0.13, -0.12)$. The
  high-temperature limit $(v_k - z_k)/N$ on mean-centred logits is $(-0.125, 0.375, -0.125, -0.125)$.

Without the $T^2$ factor, the soft term's gradients would fall by a factor of about $10^4$ between $T = 1$ and
$T = 100$, and the hard-label term would dominate.

## Cost

Distillation costs one teacher forward pass per training example, or one pass over the transfer set if the teacher's
logits are stored. Storing the full distribution over a vocabulary of $V$ tokens for every position of a language
model's training set is expensive, so teacher outputs are either computed on the fly or truncated to the top few
classes.

The results show what the teacher adds. On MNIST, a large regularised network made 67 test errors. A smaller network
with two hidden layers of 800 units and no regularisation made 146 errors when trained on the labels, and 74 errors when
trained to match the large network's soft targets at $T = 20$ <Cite id="hinton2015" />. DistilBERT, a BERT model with
half the layers trained with a distillation loss during pre-training, is 40% smaller and 60% faster than BERT and keeps
97% of its language-understanding performance <Cite id="sanh2019" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Temperature.** Low $T$ transfers only the top class and approaches training on the teacher's hard predictions.
  Very high $T$ matches all logits, including the unreliable very negative ones. Values between about 2 and 20 are
  common; the best value is found by validation.
- **Student capacity.** Hinton et al. found that when the student is very small, intermediate temperatures work best,
  because it cannot match every logit <Cite id="hinton2015" />.
- **Transfer data.** The soft loss needs no labels, so any unlabelled data from the input distribution can be used. Data
  far from that distribution teaches the student the teacher's behaviour where the teacher itself is unreliable.
- **Compared with other compression.** <NoteLink to="model-quantisation">Quantisation</NoteLink> keeps the architecture
  and stores fewer bits per weight. Distillation changes the architecture and needs a training run. The two combine: a
  distilled student can then be quantised.

## Pitfalls

- **The student inherits the teacher's errors.** Soft targets transfer systematic mistakes and biases along with the
  useful similarity structure.
- **Mismatched temperatures.** The teacher's and the student's logits must be divided by the same $T$ during training,
  and the student must be evaluated at $T = 1$.
- **Forgetting the $T^2$ factor.** Without it, changing $T$ silently changes the relative weight of the two loss terms.
- **Calibration.** A student trained at high temperature can be over- or under-confident at $T = 1$.
  <NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">Temperature scaling</NoteLink> on held-out data fixes this after training.
