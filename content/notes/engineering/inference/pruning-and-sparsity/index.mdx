<Definition>
Pruning removes parameters from a trained neural network by fixing them at zero with a binary mask $\mvec$, so that the
network computes with $\mvec \odot \wvec$ instead of $\wvec$. The **sparsity** is the fraction of zeros. Unstructured
pruning chooses individual weights; structured pruning removes whole rows, columns, channels, attention heads or
layers. The remaining weights are usually fine-tuned to recover accuracy.
</Definition>

## Problem

Trained networks are over-parameterised. Han et al. removed 89% of the weights of AlexNet, from 61 million to 6.7
million, and 92% of VGG-16's, from 138 million to 10.3 million, with no loss of ImageNet accuracy
<Cite id="han2015" />. The zero weights need not be stored or multiplied, so a sparse network can be smaller, faster and
cheaper to run. Pruning must decide which weights to remove, how to recover the accuracy lost, and how to arrange the
zeros so that hardware can exploit them.

## Mechanism

### Which weights to remove: the second-order saliency

Let $L(\wvec)$ be the training loss at the trained weights $\wvec$. A small change $\deltavec$ changes the loss by

$$
L(\wvec + \deltavec) - L(\wvec) = \gvec^\tr \deltavec + \tfrac12 \deltavec^\tr \Hmat \deltavec + O\paren{\norm{\deltavec}^3},
$$

where $\gvec = \nabla L(\wvec)$ is the gradient and $\Hmat = \nabla^2 L(\wvec)$ the Hessian. Training has converged to a
local minimum, so $\gvec \approx \zeros$. Removing weight $q$ alone sets $\deltavec = -w_q \evec_q$, where $\evec_q$ is
the $q$-th unit vector. The first-order term vanishes and the quadratic term gives the **saliency**

$$
\Delta L_q \approx \tfrac12 w_q^2 H_{qq}.
$$

Optimal Brain Damage prunes the weights of smallest saliency, computing the diagonal of $\Hmat$ by a backpropagation-like
pass <Cite id="lecun1990" />. If the diagonal entries $H_{qq}$ are similar across a layer, the saliency ranks weights by
$w_q^2$, which is **magnitude pruning**: remove the weights with the smallest $\abs{w_q}$. Magnitude pruning needs no
second derivatives, and is the standard baseline.

The saliency ignores two things. Other weights can move to compensate for the removed one, and weights are removed many
at a time, so their interactions through off-diagonal entries of $\Hmat$ matter.

<Derivation title="Optimal Brain Surgeon: compensating with the other weights">

Remove weight $q$ and let every other weight move by the best amount. The change $\deltavec$ must satisfy
$\evec_q^\tr \deltavec = -w_q$ and should minimise $\tfrac12 \deltavec^\tr \Hmat \deltavec$. The Lagrangian is
$\tfrac12 \deltavec^\tr \Hmat \deltavec + \lambda\paren{\evec_q^\tr \deltavec + w_q}$. Setting its gradient in
$\deltavec$ to zero gives $\deltavec = -\lambda \Hmat^{-1} \evec_q$. The constraint gives
$-\lambda \brack{\Hmat^{-1}}_{qq} = -w_q$, so $\lambda = w_q / \brack{\Hmat^{-1}}_{qq}$. Substituting,

$$
\deltavec = -\frac{w_q}{\brack{\Hmat^{-1}}_{qq}} \Hmat^{-1} \evec_q, \qquad
\Delta L_q = \tfrac12 \deltavec^\tr \Hmat \deltavec = \frac{w_q^2}{2 \brack{\Hmat^{-1}}_{qq}}.
$$

Optimal Brain Surgeon removes the weight with smallest $\Delta L_q$ and applies $\deltavec$ to the others
<Cite id="hassibi1993" />. When $\Hmat$ is diagonal, $\brack{\Hmat^{-1}}_{qq} = 1 / H_{qq}$, the compensation is zero,
and the result reduces to Optimal Brain Damage. SparseGPT applies this update layer by layer, with the Hessian
$2\Xmat\Xmat^\tr$ of a linear layer's squared reconstruction error on calibration inputs $\Xmat$
<Cite id="frantar2023b" />.

</Derivation>

### Recovering accuracy: prune, then fine-tune

Removing many weights at once moves the network far from the minimum where the quadratic model holds. Han et al. train
the dense network, prune the smallest-magnitude weights, and retrain the survivors with the mask fixed; repeating prune
and retrain in several rounds reaches higher sparsity than one round <Cite id="han2015" />.

Gradual pruning removes weights during training on a schedule. Zhu and Gupta raise the sparsity from $s_i$ to a target
$s_f$ over $n$ pruning steps spaced $\Delta t$ apart, starting at step $t_0$:

$$
s_t = s_f + (s_i - s_f)\paren{1 - \frac{t - t_0}{n \Delta t}}^3.
$$

The cubic prunes quickly while many redundant weights remain and slowly near the target. They found that a large model
pruned to a given number of non-zero weights consistently beat a small dense model with the same number
<Cite id="zhu2017b" />.

For large language models, retraining is expensive. SparseGPT prunes 175-billion-parameter models to 50% unstructured
sparsity in one shot, without retraining and with little loss in perplexity, and reaches 60% with a negligible increase
<Cite id="frantar2023b" />.

### The lottery ticket hypothesis

Frankle and Carbin asked whether the sparse sub-network found by pruning could have been trained from the start. Their
procedure trains a network from initial weights $\wvec_0$, prunes a fraction of the smallest-magnitude weights, and
resets the survivors to their values in $\wvec_0$, not to new random values; iterating this finds sub-networks of 10–20%
of the original size or less that train to the same accuracy as the full network on MNIST and CIFAR-10. Reinitialised
randomly, the same sparse architectures train worse <Cite id="frankle2019" />. The result suggests that dense networks
train well partly because they contain many candidate sub-networks with lucky initial weights.

### Structure of the zeros

- **Unstructured** sparsity allows any pattern. It reaches the highest sparsity for a given accuracy, but general
  hardware multiplies dense blocks, so irregular zeros save little time unless the sparsity is very high.
- **Structured** pruning removes whole output channels, attention heads or layers. The result is a smaller dense
  network that runs faster on any hardware, but removing whole units costs more accuracy per removed weight
  <Cite id="mishra2021" />.
- **N:M** sparsity keeps at most $N$ non-zeros in every group of $M$ consecutive weights. NVIDIA Ampere GPUs execute
  2:4 sparse matrix products at twice the throughput of dense ones <Cite id="mishra2021" />. The pattern is regular
  enough for hardware and fine-grained enough to keep accuracy close to the dense model after fine-tuning.

## Cost

### Worked example: when does sparsity save memory?

A $4096 \times 4096$ matrix in 16-bit precision holds $16{,}777{,}216$ weights in 32 MiB.

- **Compressed sparse row** storage keeps each non-zero value (2 bytes) and its column index. The column index needs
  $\log_2 4096 = 12$ bits, so it is stored in 2 bytes. Each non-zero costs 4 bytes, twice a dense entry, plus about
  16 KiB of row pointers. At 50% sparsity the matrix still needs 32 MiB, no saving. At 90% sparsity it needs
  $1{,}677{,}722 \times 4 \text{ bytes} = 6.4$ MiB, 20% of dense.
- **2:4 sparsity** stores the 8,388,608 kept values in 16 MiB and a 2-bit position for each, another 2 MiB. The total is
  18 MiB, or $(0.5 \times 16 + 0.5 \times 2)/16 = 56.25\%$ of dense, with a 2× speed-up in the matrix product.

Unstructured sparsity must therefore be high to pay for its indices, and speed-ups on GPUs need specialised kernels.
2:4 sparsity saves less memory but runs on existing tensor cores.

## Trade-offs

- **Sparsity against accuracy.** Moderate unstructured sparsity is often free after fine-tuning; beyond a model-specific
  level, accuracy falls quickly.
- **Granularity against speed.** Finer patterns keep more accuracy; coarser patterns run faster on standard hardware.
- **One-shot against iterative.** One-shot methods with second-order compensation avoid retraining. Iterative prune and
  fine-tune cycles reach higher sparsity at the cost of training time.
- **Compared with other compression.** <NoteLink to="model-quantisation">Quantisation</NoteLink> reduces bits per weight;
  pruning reduces the number of weights; <NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">distillation</NoteLink> trains a smaller
  dense model. The three combine: a distilled model can be pruned and then quantised.

## Pitfalls

- **Sparsity is not speed.** An unstructured 50% sparse model runs no faster, and needs no less memory, on a GPU with
  dense kernels.
- **Uniform per-layer sparsity.** Layers differ in redundancy. The first and last layers are usually most sensitive, and
  a single global magnitude threshold prunes layers unevenly.
- **Pruning after quantisation.** Pruning changes weight ranges; quantisation scales must be set after the final mask.
- **Evaluating on the wrong metric.** Average accuracy can hide large drops on rare classes or long-tail inputs.
