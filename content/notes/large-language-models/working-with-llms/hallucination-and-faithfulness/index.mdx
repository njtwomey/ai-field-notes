<Definition>
A hallucination is content in generated text that is not supported by the source the text should be based on, or not
true of the world, presented as fluently as supported content <Cite id="ji2023" />. **Faithfulness** is consistency with
a given source, such as the document being summarised. **Factuality** is consistency with world knowledge. An
**intrinsic** hallucination contradicts the source; an **extrinsic** hallucination adds information that the source
neither supports nor contradicts <Cite id="maynez2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> is trained to produce likely text, not true text. When the training data make a continuation likely,
it is usually also true. When the model lacks the relevant knowledge, a likely-sounding continuation is still available:
a plausible name, a plausible date, a plausible citation. Nothing in the generated text marks the difference, because
the same process produces both.

## Types

The source and the task decide what counts as a hallucination <Cite id="ji2023" />.

- **Source-grounded tasks** (summarisation, translation, retrieval-augmented answering) are judged by faithfulness. An
  extrinsic addition is unfaithful even when it happens to be true. Maynez et al. found hallucinated content in the
  output of every neural summariser they evaluated, and some extrinsic hallucinations were factually correct
  <Cite id="maynez2020" />.
- **Open-ended tasks** (<NoteLink to="question-answering">closed-book question answering</NoteLink>, dialogue, writing) have no source, and are judged by
  factuality. Invented citations, biographical details and quotations are typical.

## Causes

- **Data.** Training pairs whose target contains information absent from the input teach the model to add information.
  Reference summaries that go beyond their articles are an example <Cite id="maynez2020" />.
- **The objective.** Maximum likelihood rewards putting probability on plausible continuations. For arbitrary facts that
  cannot be inferred from other data, such as a person's birthday, Kalai and Vempala proved that a model calibrated in a
  suitable sense must hallucinate at a rate close to the fraction of such facts that appear exactly once in the training
  data <Cite id="kalai2024" />. The intuition is the Good–Turing estimate: the fraction of facts seen once estimates the
  probability that a new fact of that kind is one never seen. A calibrated model must generate unseen facts of that kind
  about that often, and it cannot know which of them are true. Facts that appear many times, and facts that follow
  from rules, carry no such bound.
- **Decoding.** Sampling at a high temperature draws from the low-probability tail, where errors are more likely. Once
  an error is generated, later tokens condition on it and elaborate it. See
  <NoteLink to="decoding-strategies">decoding strategies</NoteLink>.

## Detection

### Against a source

When a source exists, check that it supports each generated claim. A <NoteLink to="natural-language-inference">natural
language inference</NoteLink> model can score whether the source entails each output sentence. Maynez et al. found that
an entailment score correlated better with human faithfulness judgments than <Gloss name="rouge" /> did <Cite id="maynez2020" />.

### Agreement between samples

Without a source, sample several answers to the same prompt. A model that knows a fact tends to state it consistently; a
model that is guessing produces different guesses. SelfCheckGPT samples several responses and scores each sentence of
the main response by how well the samples support it <Cite id="manakul2023" />.

**Semantic entropy** makes the idea quantitative <Cite id="farquhar2024" />. The entropy of the answer strings is a poor
measure of uncertainty, because one answer can be phrased many ways: "Paris", "It's Paris" and "The capital is Paris"
are three strings with one meaning. Semantic entropy groups sampled answers into clusters $C$ of equal meaning, with
two answers in the same cluster when each entails the other, and computes the entropy over meanings:

$$
\operatorname{SE}(x) = -\sum_{C} p(C \mid x) \log p(C \mid x), \qquad p(C \mid x) = \sum_{s \in C} p(s \mid x).
$$

With $M$ samples, $p(C \mid x)$ can be estimated by the fraction of samples in $C$. High semantic entropy flags
questions on which the model's answers disagree in meaning, which are likely to be confabulations.

### Worked example

Ten sampled answers to one question fall into three meaning clusters of sizes 6, 3 and 1. The semantic entropy is
$-(0.6 \ln 0.6 + 0.3 \ln 0.3 + 0.1 \ln 0.1) = 0.898$ nats. For a second question, the ten answers are ten different
phrasings of one fact. The entropy over strings is $\ln 10 = 2.303$ nats, the maximum for ten samples, but the semantic
entropy is 0. The string entropy would flag the confident answer as the uncertain one.

### Asking the model

Kadavath et al. studied whether models know what they know <Cite id="kadavath2022" />. Larger models were well
calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions presented in a suitable format, with lettered options, meaning that among
answers given probability 0.7, about 70% were correct. For open-ended answers, the model can propose an answer and then
be asked whether it is true. The probability it assigns to "True", called P(True), was informative and fairly well
calibrated, and improved when the model saw several of its own samples before judging one. Models could also be trained
to predict P(IK), the probability that they know the answer, before answering; P(IK) rose appropriately when relevant
source material was placed in the context, but its calibration degraded on new tasks <Cite id="kadavath2022" />.

Calibration is measured as for any classifier, with reliability diagrams and the expected calibration error; see
<NoteLink to="calibration-error">calibration error</NoteLink>. A calibrated confidence lets a system abstain or defer
below a threshold. Recalibration methods such as <NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">temperature scaling</NoteLink> apply
to a model's answer probabilities when a labelled validation set is available.

## Mitigation

- **Ground the answer.** <NoteLink to="retrieval-augmented-generation">Retrieval-augmented generation</NoteLink> supplies
  evidence in the context and makes claims traceable to it.
- **Abstain.** Answer only when a confidence score, such as low semantic entropy or high P(True), passes a threshold.
- **Decode conservatively.** Lower temperatures reduce tail errors in factual answers.
- **Verify.** Check claims against sources or tools after generation, and cite the evidence.

## Pitfalls

- **Fluency is not evidence.** Hallucinated text is as fluent and confident in tone as correct text.
- **Overlap metrics do not measure faithfulness.** ROUGE and <Gloss name="bleu" /> reward shared words; a summary with one wrong number or
  name can score almost perfectly.
- **Consistency is not correctness.** A model can repeat the same wrong answer in every sample. Sample agreement detects
  uncertainty, not systematic errors.
- **Labels depend on the source.** The same sentence can be a hallucination in a summary, because the article does not
  support it, and correct as a general statement.
