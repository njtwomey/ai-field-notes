<Definition>
In-context learning (ICL) is the ability of a trained <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> to perform a task after conditioning on a prompt
that contains $k$ demonstrations $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_k, y_k)$ followed by a query $x$, predicting $y$ from
$p_{\thetavec}(y \mid x_1, y_1, \dots, x_k, y_k, x)$ with the weights $\thetavec$ fixed. The model is not trained on the
task. Whatever it learns from the demonstrations is computed in the forward pass <Cite id="brown2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A language model is trained to predict the next token of documents. Many documents contain repeated structure: lists of
questions and answers, tables, code with a consistent style. Predicting the next item well requires inferring the
pattern from the items so far. A prompt of demonstrations is a short document of that kind. The model continues the
pattern because continuing patterns is what <NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">next-token prediction</NoteLink> on such documents rewards.

## Observations

<Gloss name="gpt-3" />, with 175 billion parameters, was evaluated zero-shot (instruction only), one-shot and few-shot, with $k$ up to
the number of demonstrations that fit in a 2048-token context. Few-shot accuracy rose with $k$, and the gap between
zero-shot and few-shot grew with model size <Cite id="brown2020" />.

ICL is sensitive to details of the prompt.

- **Order.** The same demonstrations in different orders can give accuracy from near the state of the art to near
  chance, and a good order for one model need not be good for another <Cite id="lu2022b" />.
- **Label balance and position.** Predictions lean toward labels that are frequent among the demonstrations and toward
  the labels of the last demonstrations <Cite id="zhao2021" />. Contextual calibration, described in
  <NoteLink to="prompting">prompting</NoteLink>, corrects part of this.
- **Random labels barely lower accuracy.** Replacing the demonstrations' labels with random labels from the label
  set barely lowered accuracy on a range of classification and multiple-choice tasks, across 12 models including GPT-3.
  What mattered was that the demonstrations showed the label space, the distribution of input texts and the format of
  the sequence <Cite id="min2022" />.

The last result says that, for these tasks, demonstrations mainly tell the model *which* task is being asked rather than
teaching the input-output mapping.

## ICL as implicit Bayesian inference

Xie et al. model pretraining text as generated by a mixture: each document first draws a latent **concept**
$\phi$ from a prior $p(\phi)$, then draws its tokens from $p(o_1, \dots, o_T \mid \phi)$ <Cite id="xie2022b" />. A concept
fixes the document's topic, format and style. A model that predicts the next token optimally on such text must compute
the posterior predictive

$$
p(o_t \mid o_{<t}) = \int p(o_t \mid o_{<t}, \phi)\, p(\phi \mid o_{<t})\, d\phi,
$$

which averages each concept's prediction by how well that concept explains the text so far. Next-token training
therefore teaches the model to infer the concept of a document from its beginning.

A prompt $S_k = (x_1, y_1, \dots, x_k, y_k)$ is a document whose concept is the task $\phi^\star$. Treat the
demonstrations as independent given the concept. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>,

$$
p(\phi \mid S_k) \propto p(\phi) \prod_{i=1}^{k} p(x_i, y_i \mid \phi).
$$

Compare the task concept with any other concept $\phi$. The log posterior odds are

$$
\log \frac{p(\phi^\star \mid S_k)}{p(\phi \mid S_k)} = \log \frac{p(\phi^\star)}{p(\phi)} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \log
\frac{p(x_i, y_i \mid \phi^\star)}{p(x_i, y_i \mid \phi)}.
$$

When the demonstrations are drawn from $\phi^\star$, each term of the sum has <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>
$\KL\big(p(\cdot \mid \phi^\star) \,\|\, p(\cdot \mid \phi)\big) \ge 0$. By the <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss> the sum grows like
$k$ times this divergence, so the odds against every distinguishable concept grow exponentially in $k$. The posterior
concentrates on $\phi^\star$, and the prediction for the query becomes $p(y \mid x, \phi^\star)$: the model performs the
task.

The divergence is over the whole demonstration, input and label together. If labels are randomised, the input texts and
format still distinguish the task from other concepts, and the posterior still concentrates. This is consistent with the
finding that random labels barely hurt <Cite id="min2022" />. The account does not require the model to learn the
labelling rule from the demonstrations; the rule must already be available from pretraining.

Demonstrations joined by delimiters are unlike any pretraining document: the transitions between examples are
improbable. Xie et al. proved, for pretraining data drawn from a mixture of <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov models</Gloss>, that ICL still
succeeds when the evidence from the examples outweighs this mismatch <Cite id="xie2022b" />. On synthetic data from that
setting, transformers and <Gloss name="lstm" plural /> both learned in context, and they reproduced sensitivity to example order and cases
where zero-shot beat few-shot.

### Worked example

A prompt must select the task concept $\phi^\star$ against one alternative $\phi$. The prior gives $\phi^\star$
probability 0.2. Each demonstration has likelihood 0.9 under $\phi^\star$ and 0.5 under $\phi$, a likelihood ratio of
1.8. After $k$ demonstrations the posterior of $\phi^\star$ is $0.2 \cdot 1.8^k / (0.2 \cdot 1.8^k + 0.8)$:

| $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p(\phi^\star \mid S_k)$ | 0.200 | 0.310 | 0.448 | 0.724 | 0.965 |

Four demonstrations turn a prior of 1 in 5 into odds better than 2 to 1. A weaker prior needs more.

## Induction heads

A mechanism in the transformer implements a basic form of ICL. An **induction head** is an <NoteLink to="multi-head-attention">attention head</NoteLink> that completes
the pattern $[A][B] \dots [A] \to [B]$: at a token $A$, it attends to the token that followed an earlier occurrence of
$A$ and raises the logit of that token <Cite id="olsson2022" />.

It needs two heads in different layers, built from <NoteLink to="scaled-dot-product-attention">scaled dot-product
attention</NoteLink>.

1. A **previous-token head** in an earlier layer attends from each position $t$ to position $t - 1$ and writes the
   identity of token $o_{t-1}$ into the residual stream at $t$. The position holding $B$ now also records "preceded by
   $A$".
2. The **induction head** in a later layer, at the current position holding $A$, forms a query from $A$. Its keys are
   read from what the previous-token head wrote, so the key at the $B$ position matches. The head attends there, and its
   value and output projections copy $B$ into the prediction.

Because the query matches on content, the rule works for token pairs never seen in training. Induction heads in large
models also perform fuzzy versions of the pattern, such as $[A^*][B^*] \dots [A] \to [B]$ where $A^*$ and $B^*$ are
similar to $A$ and $B$, which is closer to completing a task from demonstrations.

Olsson et al. measured ICL as the loss on the 500th token of a context minus the loss on the 50th. In every model they
trained with more than one layer, induction heads formed during a short window early in training. The ICL score improved
abruptly in the same window, visible as a bump in the training loss curve. For small attention-only models, ablating
the induction heads gave causal evidence that they produce most of the ICL; for large models with
<Gloss name="mlp" /> layers the evidence was correlational <Cite id="olsson2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **ICL is not training.** Nothing persists after the forward pass. Every query must carry its demonstrations, which
  costs context length and compute each time.
- **Evaluate across orders and samples.** A single order of a single set of demonstrations can give a misleading
  accuracy in either direction <Cite id="lu2022b" />.
- **The task must already be learnable from pretraining.** Under the Bayesian account, demonstrations select a concept
  the model already has. A task unlike anything in pretraining text gets little help from a few examples.
