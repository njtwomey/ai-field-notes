<Definition>
Chain-of-thought (CoT) <NoteLink to="prompting">prompting</NoteLink> elicits a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps, the **rationale** $r$, before the
final answer $a$. Few-shot CoT puts worked examples with written rationales in the prompt <Cite id="wei2022b" />.
Zero-shot CoT appends an instruction such as "Let's think step by step" <Cite id="kojima2022" />. **Self-consistency**
samples several rationales and returns the answer that the most samples reach <Cite id="wang2023" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> that answers a multi-step problem immediately must compute the answer within the forward passes that
produce the answer tokens. The number of layers bounds the serial computation available per token. Arithmetic word
problems, symbolic manipulation and multi-hop questions need several dependent steps. Standard prompting that maps
question directly to answer does poorly on them, and the gap does not close quickly with model size
<Cite id="wei2022b" />.

## Mechanism

**Few-shot CoT.** Each exemplar in the prompt is a triple (question, rationale, answer) instead of a pair (question,
answer). The model imitates the format and writes a rationale for the test question before its answer. Each rationale
token is generated by a full forward pass and is then available as input to every later token. Writing out the steps
therefore spends more computation on the problem and stores intermediate results in the context.

Wei et al. prompted the 540-billion-parameter <Gloss name="palm" /> model with eight CoT exemplars. Accuracy on the GSM8K
grade-school maths benchmark rose from 17.9% with standard <Gloss name="icl">few-shot prompting</Gloss> to 56.9% <Cite id="wei2022b" />. The gains
appeared only in large models. Smaller models wrote fluent but illogical rationales and often did worse than with
standard prompting.

**Zero-shot CoT.** Kojima et al. used two prompts. The first appends "Let's think step by step." to the question and
generates a rationale. The second appends the rationale and a phrase such as "Therefore, the answer (arabic numerals)
is" to extract the answer. With <NoteLink to="instruction-tuning-and-alignment">InstructGPT</NoteLink> (text-davinci-002), accuracy on MultiArith rose from 17.7% to 78.7% and on
GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%, with no exemplars <Cite id="kojima2022" />.

### Self-consistency as marginalisation

Greedy decoding returns one rationale and the answer it reaches. The quantity of interest is the answer, not the
rationale. A model defines a joint distribution $p(r, a \mid x)$ over rationales $r$ and answers $a$ for question $x$.
The answer distribution is the marginal

$$
p(a \mid x) = \sum_{r} p(r, a \mid x),
$$

and the best single guess under this distribution is its mode, $\argmax_a p(a \mid x)$. The sum over all rationales is
intractable. Sampling $k$ pairs $(r_i, a_i) \sim p(r, a \mid x)$ with temperature or <Gloss name="nucleus-sampling">nucleus sampling</Gloss>
(see <NoteLink to="decoding-strategies">decoding strategies</NoteLink>) and counting gives an unbiased estimate of each
answer's marginal probability:

$$
\hat p(a \mid x) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \indicator[a_i = a], \qquad \hat a = \argmax_a \hat p(a \mid x).
$$

The estimated mode $\hat a$ is the majority vote. Different rationales that reach the same answer pool their
probability, so an answer reached by many distinct routes beats one reached by a single high-probability route.
Wang et al. also weighted each vote by the sequence probability of its sample; unweighted voting performed about as
well <Cite id="wang2023" />. Self-consistency improved PaLM 540B with CoT by 17.9 percentage points on GSM8K
<Cite id="wang2023" />.

### When the vote is right

A simple model shows when voting helps. Each sampled chain is independently correct with probability $p$. Suppose first
that every wrong chain gives the same wrong answer, the worst case for voting. The number of correct chains $C$ follows
a <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial distribution</NoteLink>, $C \sim \Binom(k, p)$. The vote is correct
when $C > k/2$, and a tie at $C = k/2$ is broken by a fair coin. The majority-vote accuracy is therefore

$$
A_k(p) = \sum_{c > k/2} \binom{k}{c} p^c (1-p)^{k-c} + \frac{1}{2} \binom{k}{k/2} \big(p(1-p)\big)^{k/2}
\indicator[k \text{ even}].
$$

By the <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss> $C/k \to p$, so $A_k(p) \to 1$ when $p > 1/2$ and $A_k(p) \to 0$ when $p < 1/2$. Voting
amplifies whichever answer the single chain favours.

Wrong answers are usually spread over many values. With $m$ distinct wrong answers, each with probability $(1-p)/m$, the
vote converges to the correct answer when $p > (1-p)/m$: the correct answer only has to be the most likely single
answer, not a majority. For $m = 3$ this holds for any $p > 1/4$.

<Derivation title="Plurality accuracy with m wrong answers">
Let $C$ be the number of correct chains and $W_1, \dots, W_m$ the counts of the wrong answers, so
$(C, W_1, \dots, W_m) \sim \Mult\big(k; p, \tfrac{1-p}{m}, \dots, \tfrac{1-p}{m}\big)$. Given $C = c$, the $w = k - c$
wrong chains fall into the $m$ wrong answers uniformly: $(W_1, \dots, W_m) \sim \Mult(w; 1/m, \dots, 1/m)$. The correct
answer wins when every $W_j \le c$. If $T$ of the $W_j$ equal $c$, the tie among $T + 1$ answers is broken uniformly,
so the correct answer wins with probability $1/(T + 1)$. Hence

$$
A_k = \sum_{c=1}^{k} \binom{k}{c} p^c (1-p)^{w} \; \expect\Big[\frac{\indicator[\max_j W_j \le c]}{1 + T}
\,\Big|\, C = c\Big].
$$

A uniform multinomial has $\pr(W_1 = n_1, \dots, W_m = n_m) = \frac{w!}{m^w} \prod_j \frac{1}{n_j!}$. Summing
$\prod_j 1/n_j!$ over all count vectors with total $w$ and each $n_j < c$ is the coefficient of $x^w$ in
$E_c(x)^m$, where $E_c(x) = \sum_{j < c} x^j / j!$ is the truncated exponential series. Choosing which $t$ cells equal
$c$ exactly contributes $\binom{m}{t} (x^c / c!)^t$. The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">conditional expectation</NoteLink> is therefore

$$
\frac{w!}{m^w} \sum_{t=0}^{m} \frac{1}{1+t} \binom{m}{t} \frac{1}{(c!)^t} \,\big[x^{w - tc}\big] E_c(x)^{m-t},
$$

where $[x^n] f$ is the coefficient of $x^n$ in $f$ and terms with $tc > w$ vanish. For $m = 1$ this reduces to the
binomial formula above. As $k \to \infty$ the proportions $C/k$ and $W_j/k$ converge to $p$ and $(1-p)/m$, so the vote
is eventually correct if and only if $p > (1-p)/m$.
</Derivation>

### Worked example

- **Two chains add nothing when wrong answers agree.** With $m = 1$ and $k = 2$,
  $A_2 = p^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 2p(1-p) = p$. A tie is a <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flip</NoteLink>, so the second chain is wasted.
- **Voting amplifies a good chain.** With $p = 0.6$ and $m = 1$, the accuracy is 0.600, 0.683, 0.814 and 0.898 for
  $k = 1, 5, 20, 40$.
- **Voting amplifies a bad chain.** With $p = 0.4$ and $m = 1$, it is 0.400, 0.317, 0.186 and 0.102 for the same $k$.
- **Spread-out errors rescue a weak chain.** With $p = 0.4$ and $m = 3$, each wrong answer has probability 0.2. The
  accuracy is 0.400, 0.509, 0.753 and 0.890 for $k = 1, 5, 20, 40$.

## Cost

A rationale adds tens to hundreds of generated tokens per question, and generation is serial. Self-consistency
multiplies the generation cost by $k$. The $k$ samples share the prompt, so the prompt is encoded once and the samples
decode in parallel as a batch. Gains usually flatten after 10 to 40 samples <Cite id="wang2023" />, which the simple
model reproduces: once $A_k$ is close to its limit, more samples change little.

## Trade-offs

- **Scale dependence.** CoT helped only sufficiently large models in the original experiments <Cite id="wei2022b" />.
- **Rationales are not explanations.** A rationale is generated text. A correct answer can follow a flawed rationale,
  and the stated steps need not be the computation that produced the answer.
- **Voting needs a canonical answer.** Majority voting requires answers that can be compared exactly, such as numbers
  or options. Free-form answers need normalisation or a semantic equivalence test before counting.
- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> is optimistic.** Samples from one model share its systematic errors. Correlated errors behave like
  $m = 1$ in the simple model, where more samples reinforce a wrong answer.
