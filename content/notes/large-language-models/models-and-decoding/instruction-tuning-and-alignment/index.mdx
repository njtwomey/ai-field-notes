<Definition>
Instruction tuning and alignment are the fine-tuning stages that follow pretraining. **Supervised fine-tuning** (SFT)
trains a pretrained model on prompts $x$ paired with desired responses $y$, by maximum likelihood of $y$ given $x$.
**Preference optimisation** then adjusts the resulting policy $\pi_{\thetavec}(y \mid x)$ toward responses that people
prefer, while a KL penalty keeps it close to a reference policy. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and
direct preference optimisation (DPO) are the two standard ways to do this <Cite id="ouyang2022" />
<Cite id="rafailov2023" />.
</Definition>

## Why pretraining is not enough

A model trained to predict web text continues a prompt the way web text would. Given a question, a plausible continuation
may be more questions of the same kind, because lists of questions are common documents. The model can also produce
untruthful or toxic text, because such text is in its training data. Making the model larger does not by itself fix
this <Cite id="ouyang2022" />. What the model is asked to do (continue documents) differs from what users want (answers
to requests), and the fine-tuning stages close that gap.

## Supervised fine-tuning

Collect pairs $(x, y)$ of instructions and good responses. SFT minimises the negative log-likelihood of each response
given its prompt:

$$
\Lcal_{\text{SFT}}(\thetavec) = -\sum_{(x, y)} \sum_{t=1}^{\abs{y}} \log \pi_{\thetavec}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

This is the pretraining objective of the <NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">autoregressive
language model</NoteLink>, computed on the concatenation of prompt and response, with the loss of every prompt token
masked out. The prompt tokens are inputs only, so no capacity is spent on learning to predict prompts.

**Instruction tuning on many tasks.** FLAN rewrote more than 60 existing NLP datasets as natural-language instructions
through templates and fine-tuned a 137-billion-parameter pretrained model on them. On task types held out of
fine-tuning, FLAN surpassed zero-shot GPT-3 (175 billion parameters) on 20 of 25 tasks. Its ablations found that the
benefit depended on model scale, the number of fine-tuning datasets and the use of natural-language instructions
<Cite id="wei2022" />.

**What SFT cannot do.** SFT imitates demonstrations. It cannot tell the model which of two plausible answers is better,
and good demonstrations are expensive to write. Comparing two answers is easier for a person than writing a good one.

## Preference optimisation

The data are comparisons $(x, y_w, y_l)$: for prompt $x$, a person preferred response $y_w$ to $y_l$. The
Bradley–Terry model links preferences to a scalar reward $r(x, y)$:

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big),
$$

with $\sigma$ the logistic function. The policy should earn a high reward without moving far from a reference policy
$\pi_{\text{ref}}$, usually the SFT model:

$$
\max_{\pi} \ \expect_{x}\Big[ \expect_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \KL\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)
\,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big) \Big].
$$

The KL term matters because the reward is learned from a limited set of comparisons. Far from the responses it was
fitted on, it is unreliable, and a policy that maximises it without restraint finds responses that score well but
that people do not prefer. The coefficient $\beta > 0$ sets the trade-off.

**RLHF.** InstructGPT fitted a reward model $r_{\phivec}$ to labeller rankings by maximum likelihood under the
Bradley–Terry model, then maximised the objective above with the policy-gradient method
<NoteLink to="trust-region-and-proximal-policy-optimisation">PPO</NoteLink>, sampling fresh responses from the policy and scoring them with
$r_{\phivec}$ <Cite id="ouyang2022" />. Learning a reward from pairwise human preferences and optimising a policy
against it was introduced for control tasks by Christiano et al. <Cite id="christiano2017" />. The reward model, the
optimisation and its failure modes are covered in <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback">reinforcement learning from human
feedback</NoteLink>.

**DPO.** The KL-regularised objective has the closed-form optimum $\pi^\star(y \mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,
e^{r(x, y)/\beta}$. Solving for the reward gives $r(x, y) = \beta \log\big(\pi^\star(y \mid x) / \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid
x)\big)$ plus a term that depends on $x$ only, and that term cancels in the Bradley–Terry difference. Writing the reward
through the trainable policy therefore turns preference data directly into a loss for the policy
<Cite id="rafailov2023" />:

$$
\Lcal_{\text{DPO}}(\thetavec) = -\expect_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\Big(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_w \mid
x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\thetavec}(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\Big)\Big].
$$

DPO needs only the log-probabilities of fixed responses under two models: no separate reward model and no sampling
during training.

<Derivation title="The optimal policy of the KL-regularised objective">
For one prompt $x$, write $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\, e^{r(x, y)/\beta}$ and $\pi^\star = \pi_{\text{ref}}\,
e^{r/\beta} / Z$. Then

$$
\expect_{\pi}\Big[r - \beta \log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\Big] = -\beta\, \expect_{\pi}\Big[\log
\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}\, e^{r/\beta}}\Big] = -\beta\, \expect_{\pi}\Big[\log \frac{\pi}{\pi^\star}\Big] + \beta \log
Z(x) = -\beta\, \KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi^\star) + \beta \log Z(x).
$$

$Z(x)$ does not depend on $\pi$, and the KL divergence is non-negative and zero only at $\pi = \pi^\star$, so $\pi^\star$
maximises the objective. Taking logarithms of its definition gives $r = \beta \log(\pi^\star / \pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta
\log Z(x)$.
</Derivation>

## The InstructGPT pipeline

| Stage | Data | Trains | Loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pretraining | web-scale text | $\pi_0$ | next-token cross-entropy |
| SFT | prompts with labeller-written responses | $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$ from $\pi_0$ | cross-entropy on response tokens |
| Reward model | prompts with ranked responses | $r_{\phivec}$ | Bradley–Terry log-loss |
| RL | prompts only; responses are sampled | $\pi_{\thetavec}$ from $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$ | reward minus KL penalty, by PPO |

Labellers preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3,
which has 100 times more parameters <Cite id="ouyang2022" />. The fine-tuning stages changed behaviour more than a
hundredfold increase in scale did.

## Worked example

- **SFT loss.** A prompt of 5 tokens is followed by a response of 3 tokens, to which the model assigns probabilities
  0.5, 0.25 and 0.8. The prompt tokens are masked, so the loss is $-(\ln 0.5 + \ln 0.25 + \ln 0.8) = \ln 10 = 2.303$
  nats, or 0.768 nats per response token.
- **Comparisons from rankings.** A labeller who ranks $K$ responses to one prompt yields $\binom{K}{2}$ ordered pairs:
  6 pairs for $K = 4$ and 36 for $K = 9$. InstructGPT's labellers ranked between 4 and 9 responses per prompt
  <Cite id="ouyang2022" />.
- **DPO at initialisation.** With $\pi_{\thetavec} = \pi_{\text{ref}}$, both log-ratios are zero, so the loss is
  $-\log \sigma(0) = \log 2 = 0.693$ for every pair, whatever $\beta$ is.

## Pitfalls

- **The reward is a proxy.** Preference optimisation fits what labellers prefer, which can favour length, confident tone
  or agreement with the user over correctness.
- **Alignment tax.** Fine-tuning toward preferences can lower scores on standard NLP benchmarks. InstructGPT reduced this
  by mixing pretraining updates into the RL stage <Cite id="ouyang2022" />.
- **SFT data sets the ceiling of imitation.** Demonstrations that are wrong or inconsistent are learned as faithfully
  as good ones.
- **Evaluation needs held-out prompts and people.** Reward-model scores and training-set preferences overstate the gain;
  see <NoteLink to="language-model-evaluation">language model evaluation</NoteLink>.
