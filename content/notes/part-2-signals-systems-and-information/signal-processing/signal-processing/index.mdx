<Definition>
**Signal processing** studies how to represent, analyse and transform signals, quantities that vary over time or space.
Digital signal processing works with sampled sequences $x[n]$ and the linear systems that act on them. Its central tool
is the representation of a signal by frequency, through the <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier
transform</NoteLink> and its relatives, which turns <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> into
multiplication <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every sensor produces a signal: a microphone, an accelerometer, an electrode on the scalp, a radio receiver, a camera.
Signal processing turns such recordings into usable data. It removes noise and interference, changes the sampling
rate, separates a signal into frequency bands, measures its spectrum, detects known patterns in it and compresses it.

Machine learning uses signal processing in two ways. As preprocessing, it produces the inputs that models are trained
on: the <NoteLink to="mel-spectrogram">mel spectrogram</NoteLink> for audio, band powers for EEG, filtered and resampled
sensor streams for activity recognition. As a source of model structure, it supplies the operations inside networks: the
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer" /> is a bank of learned FIR filters, pooling and striding are downsampling,
<NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models" /> are IIR filters computed with the <Gloss name="fft" />, and sparse
coding with learned dictionaries is an early form of representation learning <Cite id="mallat2009" />.

## Main ideas

**Sampling and <Gloss name="lti">linear time-invariant systems</Gloss>.** A <NoteLink to="sampling-theorem">band-limited
signal</NoteLink> is captured exactly by samples at more than twice its bandwidth; anything faster aliases. A linear
time-invariant system is described by its impulse response and acts by convolution.
<NoteLink to="signals-and-systems" /> develops these foundations, with the
<NoteLink to="z-transform">z-transform</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="poles-and-zeros">poles and zeros</NoteLink>.

**Frequency as the natural coordinate.** Sinusoids pass through linear time-invariant systems unchanged in shape, so
describing signals by frequency simplifies both analysis and design. <NoteLink to="fourier-analysis" /> covers the
<NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink> and transforms, the <Gloss name="dft" /> and the FFT, and the
leakage and resolution limits of finite records.

**Filters and rates.** <NoteLink to="digital-filters" /> keep some frequencies and suppress others, with
<Gloss name="fir" /> and <Gloss name="iir" /> designs that trade length against phase.
<NoteLink to="multirate-processing-and-filter-banks" /> changes sampling rates without aliasing and splits signals into
subbands that can be reconstructed exactly.

**Representations that adapt to the signal.** A single spectrum hides change over time.
<NoteLink to="time-frequency-analysis" /> tracks spectra over time with the <Gloss name="stft">short-time Fourier
transform</Gloss>, quadratic distributions and adaptive decompositions. <NoteLink to="wavelets" /> vary the window with
scale, giving sparse representations of signals with edges and transients. <NoteLink to="sparse-approximation" />
generalises this to overcomplete and learned dictionaries, and to <NoteLink to="compressed-sensing">compressed
sensing</NoteLink>.

**Statistics of random signals.** <NoteLink to="spectral-estimation" /> estimates the
power spectrum of a random process from a finite record, trading resolution
against variance. <NoteLink to="adaptive-and-statistical-signal-processing" /> designs filters that are optimal for
given statistics, the matched and <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filters</NoteLink>, and learns them online with
<Gloss name="lms" /> and <Gloss name="rls" />.

**Hearing as a design constraint.** <NoteLink to="audio-signal-processing" /> applies these tools to sound, with
perceptual frequency scales, mel spectrograms and <Gloss name="mfcc" plural />, pitch and beat analysis, denoising,
source separation and perceptual coding.
