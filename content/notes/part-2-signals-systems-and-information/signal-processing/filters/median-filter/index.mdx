<Definition>
The median filter of half-width $m$ maps a signal $x[n]$ to

$$
y[n] = \operatorname{median}\set{x[n - m], \dots, x[n], \dots, x[n + m]},
$$

the middle value of the $L = 2m + 1$ samples in the window once they are sorted. Tukey introduced running medians for
smoothing data <Cite id="tukey1977" />.
</Definition>

## Nonlinear, so no frequency response

The median is not linear: $\operatorname{median}(\xvec + \uvec) \neq \operatorname{median}(\xvec) +
\operatorname{median}(\uvec)$ in general. For the windows $(0, 0, 1)$ and $(1, 0, 0)$ each median is 0, but the median
of their sum $(1, 0, 1)$ is 1. The median filter therefore has no impulse response and no
<NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink>, and it cannot be analysed by the tools for
<Gloss name="lti" /> filters. It does commute with scaling by a positive constant, with adding a constant, and with any
monotone increasing transformation of the amplitude, since those preserve the order of the samples.

## What it removes and what it keeps

- **Impulses.** An outlier is replaced unless it is the middle value of the sorted window. If a window contains at most
  $m$ outliers among $2m + 1$ samples, the median is one of the good samples or lies between two of them. A burst of
  up to $m$ consecutive impulses is therefore removed completely, whatever the impulses' amplitude. A linear filter
  instead spreads each impulse over its whole impulse response, in proportion to its amplitude.
- **Edges.** An ideal step passes unchanged: at every position the window holds more samples from one side of the step
  than the other, and the median is that side's value. A monotone ramp also passes unchanged. A moving average turns
  the same step into a ramp $L$ samples wide.
- **Thin features.** A pulse lasting $m$ samples or fewer is treated like an impulse and removed. Corners are rounded.

Signals that a median filter leaves unchanged are its **root signals**. Gallagher and Wise showed that a signal is a
root of the filter with window $2m + 1$ exactly when it is built from constant runs of at least $m + 1$ samples joined
by monotone segments, and that repeated filtering of any finite signal reaches a root after a finite number of passes
<Cite id="gallagher1981" />.

## Efficiency on Gaussian noise

For independent Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$, the mean of $L$ samples has variance $\sigma^2/L$ and the median
has variance approximately $\pi\sigma^2/(2L)$ for large $L$. The median is the less efficient estimator by a factor
$2/\pi \approx 0.64$. Simulation gives $1.35\,\sigma^2/L$ for $L = 3$, $1.49\,\sigma^2/L$ for $L = 9$ and
$1.53\,\sigma^2/L$ for $L = 21$, approaching $\pi/2 = 1.57$. The median filter is the right choice when the noise has
heavy tails or contains outliers, and the moving average is the better one for Gaussian noise on smooth signals
<Cite id="arce2005" />.

## Computation

Sorting each window costs $O(L \log L)$ per sample. A running median keeps the window's samples in a sorted structure
and, per new sample, removes the oldest and inserts the newest, for $O(\log L)$ per sample with two heaps or a balanced
tree. For 8-bit images, a histogram of the window updated column by column gives constant time per pixel. The
two-dimensional median filter over a square or cross-shaped window is the standard remedy for salt-and-pepper noise in
images.

## Worked example

With $m = 1$ ($L = 3$), filter $\xvec = (1, 1, 9, 1, 1, 5, 5, 5)$, padding each end with one zero as SciPy's `medfilt`
does:

| $n$           | 0   | 1   | 2   | 3   | 4   | 5   | 6   | 7   |
| ------------- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x[n]$        | 1   | 1   | 9   | 1   | 1   | 5   | 5   | 5   |
| window at $n$ | 0,1,1 | 1,1,9 | 1,9,1 | 9,1,1 | 1,1,5 | 1,5,5 | 5,5,5 | 5,5,0 |
| median $y[n]$ | 1   | 1   | 1   | 1   | 1   | 5   | 5   | 5   |

The impulse at $n = 2$ is removed and the step at $n = 5$ stays exactly where it was. The 3-point moving average of the
same input gives $3.67$ at $n = 1, 2, 3$, spreading the impulse over three samples, and $2.33$, $3.67$, $5$ at
$n = 4, 5, 6$, blurring the step. SciPy's `medfilt(x, 3)` gives the same output as the table.

## Pitfalls

- **Window too short for the bursts.** Impulses in clusters longer than $m$ survive; widen the window, at the cost of
  removing thin genuine features.
- **Not a low-pass filter.** The median filter does not attenuate a band of frequencies. A sinusoid with period longer
  than about $2L$ passes almost unchanged; a faster one is distorted into a clipped, flat-topped wave rather than
  reduced in amplitude.
- **Edges of the record.** <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">Zero padding</NoteLink>, as in `medfilt`, pulls
  the output toward 0 at the ends when the signal is far from 0. Repeating the end samples, as in the figure, avoids
  this.
- **Combine with a linear filter.** A common pipeline removes outliers with a short median filter, then smooths the
  remaining Gaussian noise with a linear low-pass filter.
