<Definition>
A **digital filter** is a system that maps an input sequence $x[n]$ to an output sequence $y[n]$ so as to keep some
frequency components and suppress others. Most filters are linear and time-invariant and are computed by a difference
equation $y[n] = \sum_k b_k x[n-k] - \sum_{k \ge 1} a_k y[n-k]$. A filter with no feedback ($a_k = 0$) has a finite
<NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> (FIR); one with feedback has an infinite
impulse response (IIR) <Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Filtering removes what is not wanted from a signal: noise above the band of interest, mains hum at 50 or 60 Hz, a slow
drift, or isolated spikes. It also separates a signal into bands for analysis, and limits bandwidth before sampling or
downsampling to prevent <NoteLink to="aliasing">aliasing</NoteLink>. Sensor pipelines, audio processing and
physiological recordings filter their data before any model sees it.

Filtering choices matter for machine learning in three ways. A filter's delay shifts features in time relative to the
labels. A non-causal filter, such as forward-backward <NoteLink to="zero-phase-filtering" />, uses future samples, so
features built with it leak the future into a forecasting model, a case of <NoteLink to="data-leakage" />. Several
learning methods are filters in disguise: <NoteLink to="simple-exponential-smoothing" /> and the moving averages in
<NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov" /> are one-pole IIR low-pass filters.

## Main ideas

**A filter is specified by its frequency response.** A sinusoid of frequency $\omega$ leaves a linear time-invariant
filter scaled by $\abs{H(e^{i\omega})}$ and shifted by $\angle H(e^{i\omega})$. The
<NoteLink to="frequency-response" /> note shows how to read pass band, stop band and transition band from it, and how
it follows from the coefficients of a rational filter.

**FIR or IIR is a trade between length and phase.** <NoteLink to="finite-impulse-response-filters">FIR
filters</NoteLink> are always stable and can have exactly linear phase, but a sharp transition needs many coefficients.
<NoteLink to="infinite-impulse-response-filters">IIR filters</NoteLink> reach the same sharpness at low order through
poles, at the cost of possible instability and a phase that cannot be exactly linear.

**Phase determines delay and shape.** <NoteLink to="linear-phase-and-group-delay" /> defines group delay as the delay
of a narrowband envelope; linear phase delays every frequency equally and preserves waveform shape.
<NoteLink to="minimum-phase-filters" /> have the least delay for a given magnitude response and a stable inverse.
<NoteLink to="zero-phase-filtering" /> removes the phase altogether by filtering forwards and backwards, which is
possible only offline.

**Design turns a specification into coefficients.**
<NoteLink to="finite-impulse-response-filter-design">FIR design</NoteLink> truncates and tapers the ideal sinc
response with a window, or finds the shortest equiripple filter with the Parks–McClellan algorithm.
<NoteLink to="infinite-impulse-response-filter-design">IIR design</NoteLink> starts from an analog prototype
(Butterworth, Chebyshev or elliptic) and maps it to discrete time with the bilinear transform.

**Simple filters for common jobs.** <NoteLink to="moving-average-and-smoothing-filters" /> covers the moving average
and the Savitzky–Golay filter, which preserves peaks better. The <NoteLink to="median-filter" /> is nonlinear: it
removes short impulses while keeping step edges sharp. <NoteLink to="notch-and-comb-filters" /> remove one frequency,
or a frequency and all its harmonics, with a pole-zero pair placed near the unit circle.
