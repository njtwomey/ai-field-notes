<Definition>
A wavelet family is a rule that produces wavelets of increasing order. The main properties that distinguish them are
the **support** (the length of the interval where $\psi$ is nonzero), the number of **vanishing moments**
($\int t^p \psi(t)\, dt = 0$ for $p = 0, \dots, N - 1$), **smoothness**, **symmetry**, and whether the associated
transform is **orthogonal** <Cite id="daubechies1992" />.
</Definition>

## Vanishing moments

A wavelet with $N$ vanishing moments is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree less than $N$. Wherever a signal is
locally close to such a polynomial, its wavelet coefficients are small: with the normalisation
$\psi_{j,k}(t) = 2^{-j/2}\psi(2^{-j}t - k)$, of order $2^{j(N + 1/2)}$ times the size of the signal's $N$-th
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> near $k2^j$. Smooth regions therefore produce near-zero details, and
large coefficients cluster at singularities: this is the source of wavelets' sparsity on piecewise-smooth signals.

In terms of the filters, $N$ vanishing moments of $\psi$ are equivalent to $\sum_n n^p g[n] = 0$ for $p < N$. Those
discrete moments are, up to constants, the derivatives of $G(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_n g[n] e^{-i\omega n}$ at
$\omega = 0$, so they vanish exactly when $G$ has a zero of order $N$ at $\omega = 0$. Because
$g[n] = (-1)^n h[L - 1 - n]$, $\abs{G(e^{i\omega})} = \abs{H(e^{i(\omega + \pi)})}$, and that is a zero of order $N$ of
the lowpass response $H$ at $\omega = \pi$. So $H$ must factor as

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = \sqrt2 \paren{\frac{1 + e^{-i\omega}}{2}}^N Q(e^{i\omega}).
$$

## The families

- **Haar** (db1). $h = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$. One vanishing moment, support length 1, discontinuous, symmetric. The only
  orthogonal, symmetric, compactly supported real wavelet.
- **Daubechies** (dbN). The orthogonal wavelets with $N$ vanishing moments and the shortest possible support, a
  filter of $2N$ taps and support $[0, 2N - 1]$ <Cite id="daubechies1988" />. They are found by choosing the factor $Q$
  above so that $\abs{H}^2$ satisfies the halfband condition, then taking a spectral factor. The choice of
  minimum-phase roots gives the standard dbN; they are asymmetric, and their smoothness grows roughly as $0.2N$
  derivatives for large $N$.
- **Symlets.** The same moments and support as dbN, with the roots of the spectral factor chosen to make the filter
  as close to linear phase as possible.
- **Coiflets.** Vanishing moments for both the wavelet and the <NoteLink to="multiresolution-analysis">scaling
  function</NoteLink>, so the scaling coefficients of a smooth signal approximate its samples. Longer support than dbN
  for the same number of wavelet moments.
- **Biorthogonal** (for example <Gloss name="cdf" /> 9/7 and 5/3). Separate analysis and synthesis filters; this allows
  symmetric, linear-phase filters with compact support, which orthogonality forbids apart from Haar. JPEG 2000 uses the
  9/7 pair for lossy and the 5/3 pair for lossless coding <Cite id="vetterli1995" />.
- **Morlet and Mexican hat.** Wavelets for the continuous transform, with no associated filter bank or orthogonal
  basis. See <NoteLink to="continuous-wavelet-transform">continuous wavelet transform</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

The Daubechies filters follow from the spectral factorisation described above. Computed that way, with the
minimum-phase roots and the normalisation $\sum h = \sqrt2$:

| Wavelet | $h[0], h[1], \dots$ |
| --- | --- |
| db1 (Haar) | 0.7071, 0.7071 |
| db2 | 0.4830, 0.8365, 0.2241, −0.1294 |
| db4 | 0.2304, 0.7148, 0.6309, −0.0280, −0.1870, 0.0308, 0.0329, −0.0106 |

Each satisfies $\sum_n h[n] h[n - 2k] = \delta[k]$. The highpass filters $g$ have exactly 1, 2 and 4 zero moments
respectively, and their next moment is nonzero. Applied to a quadratic, the db4 details are zero in the interior to
within rounding, while the db2 details are not; applied to a straight line, both are zero.

## Choosing a wavelet

More vanishing moments give sparser representations of smooth signals, at the cost of longer filters, which spread each
singularity over more coefficients and cost more to compute. Haar suits piecewise-constant signals and fast computation;
db4 to db8 are common defaults for denoising and compression of smooth signals with occasional edges; symmetric
biorthogonal wavelets suit images, where the <NoteLink to="linear-phase-and-group-delay">phase distortion</NoteLink> of
asymmetric filters is visible.

## Pitfalls

- **Naming conventions differ.** Some software calls the 4-tap Daubechies filter db2 (two vanishing moments) and some
  calls it D4 (four taps). Check the filter length.
- **Normalisation.** Filters are normalised to $\sum h = \sqrt2$ in orthogonal wavelet theory and to $\sum h = 1$ in
  some filter-design contexts; mixing them scales every coefficient by powers of $\sqrt2$.
