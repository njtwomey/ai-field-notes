<Definition>
**Spectral estimation** infers the power spectral density $S(\omega)$ of a stationary random process from a finite,
noisy record of it. The power spectral density is the <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> of
the process's autocovariance and gives the power per unit frequency. Methods are either nonparametric, built from the
<Gloss name="dft">discrete Fourier transform</Gloss> of the data, or parametric, fitting a model whose spectrum has a
closed form <Cite id="stoica2005" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The spectrum of a random signal is a property of the process that generated it, not of the particular record. Two
recordings of the same engine noise differ sample by sample but share a spectrum. Spectral estimation recovers that
shared description. It finds periodicities in noisy data, measures the bandwidth of a channel, characterises a sensor's
noise floor, and summarises the rhythms of an EEG.

In machine learning and time series, the periodogram reveals the seasonal periods to model in a forecast, and an
estimated spectrum is a standard feature of a signal for classification. Fitting an
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-model" /> and fitting a parametric spectrum are the same computation. The spectral density
of a stationary Gaussian process is the Fourier transform of its covariance function, the link between this topic and
the <NoteLink to="covariance-functions" /> of Gaussian processes.

## Main ideas

**The spectrum is the transform of the autocovariance.** For a wide-sense stationary process, the Wiener–Khinchin
theorem states that $S(\omega) = \sum_m r[m]\,e^{-i\omega m}$, where $r[m]$ is the autocovariance at lag $m$.
<NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-wiener-khinchin" /> proves this and shows why the biased sample autocovariance is the
estimate whose transform is non-negative.

**The raw periodogram is noisy however long the record.** The <NoteLink to="periodogram" />,
$\hat S(\omega) = \frac{1}{N}\abs{\sum_n x[n]e^{-i\omega n}}^2$, is asymptotically unbiased, but its variance does not
fall as $N$ grows. A longer record gives finer frequency spacing, not a smoother estimate. Its bias comes from the
window implied by a finite record, the leakage of <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows" />.

**Averaging trades resolution for variance.** <NoteLink to="welch-method">Welch's method</NoteLink> averages the
periodograms of overlapping windowed segments; $K$ segments divide the variance by about $K$, and the shorter segments
coarsen the resolution. The <NoteLink to="multitaper-spectral-estimation">multitaper method</NoteLink> instead
multiplies the whole record by $K$ orthogonal Slepian tapers and averages, which keeps leakage low at a stated
resolution.

**Irregular sampling needs a least-squares view.** The <NoteLink to="lomb-scargle-periodogram" /> fits a sinusoid at
each frequency by least squares, so it works on unevenly spaced observations without interpolating them to a grid.

**Models resolve what windows blur.** <NoteLink to="parametric-spectral-estimation" /> fits an autoregressive model by
the Yule–Walker equations, the Levinson–Durbin recursion or Burg's method, and reports the model's spectrum. It
resolves sharp peaks from short records. <NoteLink to="subspace-frequency-estimation" /> (MUSIC and ESPRIT) separates
the eigenvectors of the sample correlation matrix into signal and noise subspaces and locates sinusoids closer together
than the Fourier limit $2\pi/N$ when the <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratio</Gloss> is high.

**Two signals have a joint spectrum.** <NoteLink to="coherence-and-cross-spectra" /> defines the cross-spectral density,
whose phase measures delay, and the coherence, the fraction of power at each frequency that is linearly related between
the two signals.
