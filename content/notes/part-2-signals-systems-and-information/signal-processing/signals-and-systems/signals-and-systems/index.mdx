<Definition>
**Signals and systems** is the foundation of <NoteLink to="signal-processing">digital signal processing</NoteLink>. A
*signal* is a sequence $x[n]$ of numbers indexed by an integer time $n$, usually obtained by sampling a continuous
quantity. A *system* maps an input signal to an output signal. The theory concentrates on linear time-invariant systems,
which are described completely by one signal, their impulse response <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every recorded signal in machine learning, such as audio, sensor streams, physiological recordings or the rows of a
time series, was produced by sampling and quantising a continuous quantity. The notes here state what survives that
step and what is lost. They also define the class of systems that later topics design and analyse: digital filters,
<NoteLink to="filter-banks">filter banks</NoteLink> and spectral estimators all act as linear time-invariant systems.

The same operations appear inside neural networks. A <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer" /> applies a learned finite
impulse response to its input, written as a correlation. Downsampling by striding or pooling aliases high frequencies
unless the signal is low-pass filtered first, the effect explained in <NoteLink to="aliasing" />.
<NoteLink to="model-quantisation" /> rounds weights to few bits, and the noise model in
<NoteLink to="quantisation" /> describes the resulting error.

## Main ideas

**Sampling is exact for band-limited signals.** A signal with no frequency content at or above $f_s/2$ is determined
by its samples at rate $f_s$ and is recovered by sinc interpolation. The <NoteLink to="sampling-theorem" /> proves
this. Components above $f_s/2$ fold to lower frequencies, an error that no processing after sampling can undo;
<NoteLink to="aliasing" /> derives the folded frequency and the anti-aliasing filter that prevents it.

**Amplitude is rounded as well as time.** <NoteLink to="quantisation" /> rounds each sample to one of $2^b$ levels.
Modelled as white noise, the rounding gives a signal-to-noise ratio of about $6.02b + 1.76$ dB for a full-scale sine,
six decibels for every bit.

**An impulse response describes a linear time-invariant system.** A system that is linear and does not change over
time is fixed by its response $h[n]$ to a single impulse. Its output is the <NoteLink to="convolution" />
$y = x * h$. Causality and stability are properties of $h$, as shown in
<NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems" />. Complex exponentials pass through such a system with only a change of
amplitude and phase, which is why frequency analysis suits it.

**The z-transform turns convolution into multiplication.** The <NoteLink to="z-transform" /> maps a sequence to a
function of a complex variable $z$. Convolution becomes a product, and a recursive
<NoteLink to="difference-equations">difference equation</NoteLink> becomes a rational transfer function
$H(z) = B(z)/A(z)$. Feedback terms make the filter infinite impulse response (<Gloss name="iir" />); without them it is
finite impulse response (<Gloss name="fir" />).

**Poles and zeros are the geometry of a filter.** The roots of $B$ and $A$ fix $H$ up to a gain. A causal system is
stable exactly when every pole lies inside the unit circle, and the frequency response is read from distances to the
poles and zeros. <NoteLink to="poles-and-zeros" /> develops this picture and uses it to design simple filters.

## How the notes fit together

The topic has two threads. The sampling thread is <NoteLink to="sampling-theorem" />, then
<NoteLink to="aliasing" /> and <NoteLink to="quantisation" />. The systems thread is
<NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems" />, <NoteLink to="convolution" />, the
<NoteLink to="z-transform" />, then <NoteLink to="difference-equations" /> and <NoteLink to="poles-and-zeros" />.

to Fourier transforms can read <NoteLink to="fourier-transform" /> alongside it.
