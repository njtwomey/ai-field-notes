<Definition>
The **constant-Q transform** of $x[n]$ at bin $k$ is a windowed <Gloss name="dft" /> evaluated at the single frequency
$f_k$ with a window whose length $N_k$ depends on $k$:

$$
X^{\mathrm{CQ}}[k] = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{n=0}^{N_k - 1} w_k[n]\, x[n]\, e^{-i 2\pi Q n / N_k},
\qquad f_k = f_{\min}\, 2^{k/b}, \qquad N_k = \frac{Q f_s}{f_k},
$$

with $b$ bins per octave and a quality factor $Q = 1/(2^{1/b} - 1)$ that is the same for every bin
<Cite id="brown1991" />.
</Definition>

## Why the quality factor is constant

With geometrically spaced centres $f_k = f_{\min} 2^{k/b}$, the spacing between neighbouring bins is
$\Delta f_k = f_{k+1} - f_k = f_k (2^{1/b} - 1)$, proportional to $f_k$. Requiring each bin's bandwidth to match its
spacing gives $\Delta f_k = f_k / Q$ with $Q = 1/(2^{1/b} - 1)$. A window of $N_k$ samples has a bandwidth of order
$f_s / N_k$, so matching needs $N_k = Q f_s / f_k$: every window spans exactly $Q$ cycles of its own centre frequency,
which is why the exponent in the definition is $2\pi Q n / N_k = 2\pi f_k n / f_s$.

The result is a log-frequency analysis with fine <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">frequency
resolution</NoteLink> at low frequencies and fine time resolution at high frequencies. It resembles the ear's frequency
resolution above a few hundred hertz and matches the geometric spacing of the musical scale. It is a sampled
<NoteLink to="continuous-wavelet-transform">continuous wavelet transform</NoteLink> with a fixed number of cycles per
wavelet.

## Worked example

With $b = 12$ bins per octave the bins are equal-tempered semitones and

$$
Q = \frac{1}{2^{1/12} - 1} \approx 16.82.
$$

At $f_s = 22{,}050$ Hz, the lowest bin at $f_{\min} = 55$ Hz (A1) needs a window of
$N_0 = 16.82 \times 22050 / 55 \approx 6742$ samples, 306 ms. Five octaves up, at 1760 Hz (A6, $k = 60$), the window is
about 211 samples, 9.6 ms. A <Gloss name="stft">short-time Fourier transform</Gloss> with one window length cannot give
both: 306 ms is far too long to follow notes at 1760 Hz, and 9.6 ms is far too short to separate semitones at 55 Hz,
where adjacent notes are only 3.3 Hz apart.

## Computing it efficiently

Direct evaluation of every bin with its own long window is expensive. Brown and Puckette computed all bins from one
<Gloss name="fft" /> per frame by transforming each bin's windowed kernel to the frequency domain, where it is sparse
<Cite id="brown1992" />. Later implementations process one octave at a time, downsampling by two between octaves so
that every octave uses the same short kernels, and design the kernels so the transform can be inverted.

## Uses

- **Music analysis:** pitch and chord recognition, where each note occupies one bin and transposition is a shift
  along the frequency axis. Folding octaves together gives <NoteLink to="chroma-features">chroma features</NoteLink>.
- **Input to neural networks** for music, where the shift structure suits
  <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> along frequency.

## Pitfalls

- **Latency and length.** The lowest bins need windows of hundreds of milliseconds, so low-frequency coefficients are
  smeared in time and unavailable near the signal's edges.
- **No constant time grid.** Bins have different natural hop sizes; sampling every bin on one common grid oversamples
  the high bins or undersamples the low ones.
- **Not directly invertible** in Brown's original form; invertible designs choose windows and hops so that the frames
  tile the time-frequency plane.
