<Definition>
The **Wigner–Ville distribution** of a signal $x(t)$ is

$$
W_x(t, f) = \int x\paren{t + \tfrac{\tau}{2}}\, x^*\paren{t - \tfrac{\tau}{2}}\, e^{-i 2\pi f \tau}\, d\tau,
$$

the <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink>, in the lag $\tau$, of the instantaneous
autocorrelation $x(t + \tau/2)\, x^*(t - \tau/2)$. It is usually computed from the
<NoteLink to="hilbert-transform">analytic signal</NoteLink>, whose spectrum has no negative frequencies
<Cite id="cohen1989" />.
</Definition>

## Properties

- **Real-valued.** The kernel $x(t + \tau/2) x^*(t - \tau/2)$ is conjugate-symmetric in $\tau$, so its transform is
  real. It is not always non-negative.
- **Correct marginals.** Integrating over frequency gives the instantaneous power, and over time the energy spectrum:

  $$
  \int W_x(t, f)\, df = \abs{x(t)}^2, \qquad \int W_x(t, f)\, dt = \abs{X(f)}^2.
  $$

  The first follows because integrating $e^{-i2\pi f \tau}$ over $f$ gives $\delta(\tau)$, leaving $x(t) x^*(t)$.
- **Covariance.** Shifting the signal in time or frequency shifts the distribution by the same amount.
- **Instantaneous frequency.** The first moment in frequency at time $t$ is the instantaneous frequency of the analytic
  signal. See <NoteLink to="instantaneous-frequency">instantaneous frequency</NoteLink>.

## Perfect localisation of a chirp

For a linear chirp $x(t) = e^{i 2\pi (f_0 t + \alpha t^2 / 2)}$, the phase terms in the kernel combine as

$$
2\pi\brack{f_0\paren{t + \tfrac\tau2} + \tfrac\alpha2\paren{t + \tfrac\tau2}^2 - f_0\paren{t - \tfrac\tau2} -
\tfrac\alpha2\paren{t - \tfrac\tau2}^2} = 2\pi\paren{f_0 + \alpha t}\tau.
$$

The quadratic terms cancel, the kernel is a pure tone in $\tau$ at frequency $f_0 + \alpha t$, and its transform is

$$
W_x(t, f) = \delta\paren{f - f_0 - \alpha t}.
$$

The distribution lies exactly on the instantaneous frequency line, with no spread at all. No spectrogram can do this:
its window gives every component a width of order $1/\sigma_t$ in frequency.

## Cross-terms

The distribution is quadratic, so for a sum of two signals

$$
W_{x_1 + x_2} = W_{x_1} + W_{x_2} + 2 \operatorname{Re} W_{x_1, x_2},
$$

where $W_{x_1, x_2}$ is the cross-distribution with $x_1$ and $x_2^*$ in the kernel. The cross-term lies midway between
the two components in the time-frequency plane and oscillates, with a frequency proportional to their separation.
For two tones at $f_1$ and $f_2$ it is a ridge at $(f_1 + f_2)/2$ modulated as $\cos(2\pi (f_2 - f_1) t)$. Cross-terms
between real-signal components and their negative-frequency images are why the analytic signal is used.

## Cohen's class and the spectrogram

Smoothing $W_x$ with a two-dimensional kernel $\Phi$ damps the oscillating cross-terms, at the price of spreading the
auto-terms. The resulting family, $C_x = W_x * \Phi$, is **Cohen's class** of time-frequency distributions
<Cite id="cohen1989" />. The spectrogram belongs to it: the squared magnitude of an <Gloss name="stft" /> with window
$w$ equals the Wigner–Ville distribution of the signal smoothed by that of the window,

$$
\abs{X_w(t, f)}^2 = \iint W_x(s, \nu)\, W_w(s - t, \nu - f)\, ds\, d\nu.
$$

The pseudo and smoothed-pseudo Wigner–Ville distributions use separable kernels that smooth less in one direction
than a spectrogram does, keeping more of the sharpness while suppressing cross-terms.

## Pitfalls

- **Negative values.** The distribution is not an energy density in the strict sense; values can be negative, and no
  bilinear distribution is both non-negative and has the correct marginals for all signals.
- **Discrete <NoteLink to="aliasing">aliasing</NoteLink>.** The lag $\tau/2$ needs samples at half-integer times.
  Discrete implementations evaluate $x[n + m]\, x^*[n - m]$, which doubles the frequency, so real signals must be
  oversampled by two or converted to analytic signals to avoid aliasing.
- **Multicomponent signals.** With several components the cross-terms can dominate the picture. Use a smoothed
  version, or read only the auto-terms.
