<Definition>
**Statistical signal processing** treats signals and noise as random processes and designs filters that are optimal for
a stated criterion, such as the largest <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratio</Gloss> or the smallest
<Gloss name="mse">mean-squared error</Gloss>. **Adaptive signal processing** estimates such a filter from data as the
data arrive, adjusting its coefficients sample by sample so that it can track statistics that change
<Cite id="sayed2008" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A frequency specification is the wrong starting point when the signal and the noise overlap in frequency. Detecting a
known pulse in noise, removing an echo from a phone line, cancelling engine noise in a headset and equalising a
communication channel all need filters fitted to the statistics of the signals involved. When those statistics are
unknown or drift, an adaptive filter learns them online.

These methods are early instances of machine learning. The least-mean-squares filter, also called the Widrow–Hoff rule,
is <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> on a linear model's squared error, the same update as the delta
rule for a linear unit <Cite id="widrow1976" />. The Wiener filter is linear least-squares estimation, the population
version of <NoteLink to="linear-regression" />. The recursive least-squares filter is a special case of the
<NoteLink to="kalman-filter" />. A matched filter correlates its input with a template, which is what each channel of a
<NoteLink to="convolutional-layer" /> computes.

## Main ideas

**Correlate with what you are looking for.** The <NoteLink to="matched-filter" /> for a known signal in white noise
is the time-reversed signal. Among all linear filters it maximises the output signal-to-noise ratio, which makes it the
basis of radar detection and pulse compression.

**Minimise the mean-squared error.** The <NoteLink to="wiener-filter" /> is the linear filter whose output is closest,
in mean square, to a desired signal. Its coefficients solve the Wiener–Hopf equations $\Rmat\wvec = \pvec$, built from
the input's autocorrelation matrix $\Rmat$ and the cross-correlation $\pvec$ with the desired signal. The orthogonality
principle, that the error is uncorrelated with the input, characterises the solution.

**Predict the next sample from the past.** <NoteLink to="linear-prediction" /> is the Wiener filter with the signal's
own next sample as the target. Its normal equations have Toeplitz structure, solved in $O(p^2)$ by the Levinson–Durbin
recursion, and the inverse of the prediction-error filter models the spectral envelope. Speech coders are built on it.

**Learn the filter online.** The <NoteLink to="least-mean-squares-filter" /> takes one stochastic-gradient step on the
Wiener cost per sample, $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec + \mu\,e[n]\,\xvec[n]$, at a cost of $O(M)$ for $M$ taps. The step
size $\mu$ trades tracking speed against excess error. The <NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter" /> solves the
exponentially weighted least-squares problem exactly at each step, at $O(M^2)$ per sample, and converges much faster
on correlated input.

**Separate convolved components with a logarithm.** The <NoteLink to="cepstrum" /> is the inverse transform of the log
magnitude spectrum. The logarithm turns the product of a source spectrum and a filter response into a sum, so the two
separate in the cepstral domain. It finds pitch periods and echo delays, and its low-order coefficients describe the
spectral envelope.

## How the notes fit together

The <NoteLink to="matched-filter" /> and the <NoteLink to="cepstrum" /> stand alone. The other four depend on the
<NoteLink to="wiener-filter" />, which comes first. <NoteLink to="linear-prediction" /> applies it to prediction, and
the two adaptive filters, <NoteLink to="least-mean-squares-filter" /> then
<NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter" />, estimate it online.
