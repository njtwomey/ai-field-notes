<Definition>
A mel filter bank with $M$ filters over $[f_{\min}, f_{\max}]$ places $M + 2$ edge frequencies $b_0 < b_1 < \dots <
b_{M+1}$ equally spaced on the <NoteLink to="auditory-scales">mel scale</NoteLink>. Filter $m = 1, \dots, M$ is the
triangle that rises linearly from 0 at $b_{m-1}$ to 1 at $b_m$ and falls back to 0 at $b_{m+1}$:

$$
H_m(f) = \max\paren{0,\ \min\paren{\frac{f - b_{m-1}}{b_m - b_{m-1}},\ \frac{b_{m+1} - f}{b_{m+1} - b_m}}}.
$$

Applied to a <NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">power spectrum</NoteLink> $P[k]$ sampled at bin frequencies $f_k$, it gives
the band energies $E_m = \sum_k H_m(f_k)\,P[k]$ <Cite id="davis1980" />.
</Definition>

## Construction, step by step

1. **Choose the range and count.** Typical speech settings are $f_{\min} = 0$ or 20 Hz, $f_{\max} = f_s/2$, and
   $M = 20$ to $40$ filters for 16 kHz audio. Music and neural-network front ends often use 64 to 128.
2. **Space the edges in mel.** Convert the range with $m(f) = 2595\log_{10}(1 + f/700)$, take $M + 2$ equally spaced
   points between $m(f_{\min})$ and $m(f_{\max})$, and convert back to hertz. Equal spacing in mel means narrow,
   closely spaced filters at low frequencies and wide ones at high frequencies, matching the ear's resolution.
3. **Build the triangles.** Each filter's centre is the next edge, and its feet are the neighbouring edges. Adjacent
   filters therefore overlap by half: at every frequency between the first and last centres, exactly two filters are
   active and their peak-normalised weights sum to 1.
4. **Sample on the <Gloss name="fft" /> grid.** Evaluate each triangle at the bin frequencies
   $f_k = k f_s/N_{\mathrm{FFT}}$, $k = 0, \dots, N_{\mathrm{FFT}}/2$, giving an $M \times (N_{\mathrm{FFT}}/2 + 1)$
   weight matrix $\Wmat$.
5. **Apply.** For each frame's power spectrum $\pvec$, the band energies are $\evec = \Wmat\pvec$.

## Normalisation

Two conventions differ in the triangles' heights:

- **Peak normalisation** (HTK): every triangle peaks at 1. Wide high-frequency filters then sum more bins, so for white
  noise their energies grow with bandwidth.
- **Area normalisation** (Slaney): each triangle is scaled by $2/(b_{m+1} - b_{m-1})$ so that its area in hertz is 1
  <Cite id="slaney1998" />. For white noise every band then has the same energy, and the band energies approximate a
  <Gloss name="power-spectral-density">power spectral density</Gloss> on the mel axis.

The choice rescales each band by a constant. After a logarithm it becomes an additive offset per band, which
downstream normalisation often removes, but the two do not produce identical features.

## Worked example

Take $M = 10$ filters from 0 to 8000 Hz. The top edge is $m(8000) = 2595\log_{10}(1 + 8000/700) = 2840.0$ mel, so the
12 edges are spaced $2840.0/11 = 258.2$ mel apart. Converting back to hertz, the edges are 0, 180, 407, 692, 1050,
1501, 2067, 2780, 3676, 4802, 6219 and 8000 Hz. Filter 1 spans 0 to 407 Hz, centred at 180 Hz. Filter 10 spans 4802 to
8000 Hz, centred at 6219 Hz, and is almost 8 times wider.

## Pitfalls

- **Empty filters.** With many filters and a short FFT, the lowest triangles can be narrower than the
  <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">bin spacing</NoteLink> $f_s/N_{\mathrm{FFT}}$ and catch no bins at all.
  Their energies are then zero and their logarithm undefined. Use a longer FFT, fewer filters or a higher $f_{\min}$.
- **Mel formula and normalisation vary between libraries.** Fix both, and check against a reference implementation,
  before comparing features across tools.
- **Power or magnitude.** <NoteLink to="filter-banks">Filter banks</NoteLink> are usually applied to the power spectrum
  $\abs{X}^2$; some pipelines apply them to the magnitude. The resulting log energies differ by a factor of 2 and a
  band-dependent offset.
