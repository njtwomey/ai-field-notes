<Definition>
Let $\Ymat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ hold $n$ training signals $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n \in \reals^m$ as columns.
Dictionary learning seeks a dictionary $\Dmat \in \reals^{m \times k}$ whose $k$ columns $\dvec_1, \dots, \dvec_k$ (the
atoms) have unit norm, and codes $\Xmat \in \reals^{k \times n}$, that solve

$$
\min_{\Dmat, \Xmat} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\Ymat - \Dmat\Xmat}_F^2
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \norm{\xvec_i}_0 \le s \text{ for } i = 1, \dots, n, \quad \norm{\dvec_j} = 1 \text{ for } j = 1, \dots, k.
$$

Here $\xvec_i$ is column $i$ of $\Xmat$, the code of $\yvec_i$, and $\norm{\xvec_i}_0$ counts its non-zero entries. The
sparsity $s$ is fixed in advance and is much smaller than $m$. The dictionary is overcomplete when $k > m$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A fixed dictionary, such as the cosines of the <Gloss name="dct" /> or a wavelet basis, is chosen before the data are
seen. It represents a class of signals sparsely only if the class happens to suit it: cosines suit smooth signals and
spread a sharp edge over many coefficients. Dictionary learning chooses the atoms from the data, so that each training
signal is close to a combination of a few of them.

A code with $s$ non-zeros places $\Dmat\xvec_i$ in the span of $s$ atoms. The model therefore says that the signals lie
near a union of $\binom{k}{s}$ subspaces of dimension $s$, one for each choice of atoms. With $m = 2$, $k = 3$ and
$s = 1$, the subspaces are three lines through the origin, and learning the dictionary means finding the three lines
that the points lie near.

<LearningDirections />

The fit alternates two stages, as k-means alternates assignment and averaging. With the dictionary fixed, each signal is
coded by a <NoteLink to="sparse-approximation">sparse approximation</NoteLink> method, usually <Gloss name="omp" />.
With the supports of the codes fixed, the atoms are moved to fit the signals that use them. Each stage lowers the
objective or leaves it unchanged, so the objective decreases until it settles. The objective is not convex, so where it
settles depends on the starting dictionary.

## Details

### The two stages

Write the objective as $f(\Dmat, \Xmat) = \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\Ymat - \Dmat\Xmat}_F^2$. Minimising it over both factors at
once is hard: for a fixed $\Dmat$, finding the best code with $s$ non-zeros is itself NP-hard in general
<Cite id="natarajan1995" />. The alternating scheme <Cite id="aharon2006" /> splits the problem into two easier ones.

1. **Sparse coding.** With $\Dmat$ fixed, $f$ is a sum over signals,
   $\sum_i \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\yvec_i - \Dmat\xvec_i}^2$, so each code can be found separately. Orthogonal matching
   pursuit with $s$ steps gives an approximate minimiser for each signal.
2. **Dictionary update.** With the codes, or at least their supports, fixed, $f$ is minimised over $\Dmat$. The method
   of optimal directions and <Gloss name="k-svd" /> differ only in this stage.

Orthogonal matching pursuit is greedy and does not always find the best code. A new code can even fit worse than the
code from the previous round. Keeping the better of the two makes the coding stage non-increasing. Both dictionary
updates below are also non-increasing, so the objective decreases monotonically. It is bounded below by zero, so the
sequence of objective values converges.

### The method of optimal directions

The <Gloss name="mod" /> <Cite id="engan1999" /> updates the whole dictionary at once, holding $\Xmat$ fixed. The
objective is a convex quadratic in $\Dmat$. Its gradient is

$$
\nabla_\Dmat f = -(\Ymat - \Dmat\Xmat)\Xmat^\top.
$$

Setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations $\Dmat\Xmat\Xmat^\top = \Ymat\Xmat^\top$. When
$\Xmat\Xmat^\top$ is invertible, which needs every atom to be used,

$$
\Dmat = \Ymat\Xmat^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-1}.
$$

This is the least-squares fit of the signals by the codes, row by row: row $r$ of $\Dmat$ regresses row $r$ of $\Ymat$
on the $k$ rows of $\Xmat$. The new atoms need not have unit norm. Scaling atom $j$ by $1/\norm{\dvec_j}$ and row $j$ of
$\Xmat$ by $\norm{\dvec_j}$ leaves every product $\dvec_j x_{ji}$, and therefore $\Dmat\Xmat$ and $f$, unchanged. The
update keeps the supports, because it changes no entry of $\Xmat$ from zero to non-zero.

### K-SVD

K-SVD <Cite id="aharon2006" /> is a dictionary-learning algorithm, not a matrix factorisation. It is distinct from the
<Gloss name="svd" /> of the data matrix $\Ymat$,
whose singular vectors are orthogonal and are not sparse codes. K-SVD uses a rank-one SVD of a small residual matrix as
one step of each atom update. Aharon et al. named it after k-means: k-means updates its $k$ centroids by computing $k$
means, and K-SVD updates its $k$ atoms by computing $k$ singular value decompositions, one per atom.

K-SVD updates one atom at a time, together with the non-zero coefficients that multiply it. Let
$\xvec^j$ denote row $j$ of $\Xmat$: the coefficients of atom $j$ in every code. The product $\Dmat\Xmat$ is a sum of
$k$ rank-one matrices, $\Dmat\Xmat = \sum_l \dvec_l \xvec^l$. Separating atom $j$,

$$
\Ymat - \Dmat\Xmat = \Big(\Ymat - \sum_{l \ne j} \dvec_l \xvec^l\Big) - \dvec_j \xvec^j = \Emat_j - \dvec_j\xvec^j.
$$

The matrix $\Emat_j$ is the error that remains when atom $j$ is removed. Minimising
$\norm{\Emat_j - \dvec_j\xvec^j}_F^2$ over $\dvec_j$ and $\xvec^j$ is a best rank-one approximation of $\Emat_j$. By
the Eckart–Young theorem, the best rank-one approximation of a matrix is $\sigma_1\uvec_1\vvec_1^\top$, built from its
largest singular value and singular vectors. This rank-one SVD is the step that gives K-SVD its name.

The unrestricted rank-one fit would fill in every entry of $\xvec^j$, so every signal would use atom $j$ and the codes
would no longer be sparse. K-SVD therefore restricts the fit to $\omega_j = \{i : x_{ji} \ne 0\}$, the signals that
already use atom $j$. Let $\Emat_j^R$ hold the columns of $\Emat_j$ in $\omega_j$ and $\xvec^j_R$ the matching entries
of $\xvec^j$. With the singular value decomposition $\Emat_j^R = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$, the update is

$$
\dvec_j = \uvec_1, \qquad \xvec^j_R = \sigma_1 \vvec_1^\top.
$$

The atom has unit norm because $\uvec_1$ does. Entries of $\xvec^j$ outside $\omega_j$ stay zero, so every code keeps
its support and its sparsity. The objective cannot increase: the old pair $(\dvec_j, \xvec^j_R)$ is one rank-one
candidate, and the update is the best one. K-SVD sweeps $j = 1, \dots, k$, and each atom's update uses the coefficients
already updated for the atoms before it.

Updating coefficients with the atom is the difference from the method of optimal directions, which holds $\Xmat$ fixed
while it moves $\Dmat$. K-SVD usually reaches a lower objective in the same number of rounds <Cite id="aharon2006" />.
Each round costs one singular value decomposition per atom, of an $m \times \abs{\omega_j}$ matrix.

### Unused atoms

An atom that no signal uses has $\omega_j$ empty and receives no update, and $\Xmat\Xmat^\top$ is then singular. The
common remedy <Cite id="aharon2006" /> replaces the atom with the training signal that the current dictionary represents
worst, scaled to unit norm. The replacement points the dictionary at the part of the data it fits least.

### Local minima

The objective is not jointly convex in $\Dmat$ and $\Xmat$. Any solution has equivalent copies: flipping the sign of
atom $j$ and of row $j$ of $\Xmat$ leaves $\Dmat\Xmat$ unchanged, and so does permuting the atoms together with the rows
of $\Xmat$. Take a solution $(\Dmat, \Xmat)$ with objective below $\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\Ymat}_F^2$ and its copy
$(-\Dmat, -\Xmat)$. If the problem were convex, the midpoint of two minimisers would be a minimiser too. The midpoint is
$(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{0})$, whose objective is $\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\Ymat}_F^2$, larger than at either end. The problem
also has genuinely different local minima. In the first figure, some starts leave two atoms on one line and a line
without an atom; no single update moves an atom across, because each step only refits the signals an atom already
serves.

The usual starting dictionary is $k$ training signals drawn at random and scaled to unit norm. Running from several
starts and keeping the lowest objective is the standard guard. Training on many more signals than atoms ($n \gg k$)
makes the objective smoother and the minima more alike.

### Relation to k-means and principal component analysis

With $s = 1$, each signal uses one atom. Orthogonal matching pursuit then picks the atom with the largest
$\abs{\dvec_j^\top\yvec_i}$, the line through the origin closest in angle to $\yvec_i$, and sets
$x_{ji} = \dvec_j^\top\yvec_i$. The K-SVD update for atom $j$ becomes the top left singular vector of the signals
assigned to it: the direction of largest second moment of that cluster. This is
<NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> with lines through the origin in place of centres. If the coefficients are
further fixed at 1 and the unit norm is dropped, so that each signal is approximated by an atom itself, the update
becomes the mean of the assigned signals and the algorithm is exactly k-means; Aharon et al. derive K-SVD as this
generalisation <Cite id="aharon2006" />.

At the other extreme, with $s = k \le m$ there is no sparsity constraint. Every code may use every atom, the model is a
single $k$-dimensional subspace, and the best dictionary spans the top $k$ left singular vectors of $\Ymat$: uncentred
<Gloss name="pca" />. Dictionary learning sits between the two. It uses more atoms than any one signal needs, and
different signals use different subsets.

### Image patches

Small image patches are the classic training set. Olshausen and Field learnt a dictionary from patches of natural images
under a sparsity penalty and found localised, oriented, band-pass atoms that resemble the receptive fields of simple
cells in the primary visual cortex <Cite id="olshausen1996" />. Elad and Aharon denoised images by coding every
overlapping patch over a K-SVD dictionary, learnt from the noisy image itself, and averaging the overlapping estimates
<Cite id="elad2006" />. The figure below learns a dictionary from $6 \times 6$ patches of a synthetic image with
straight edges, a curved edge and stripes. Each patch has its mean removed first, so the atoms model texture and edges
and the constant level is coded separately.

<PatchDictionary />

The learnt atoms become edges at the angles that occur in the image, stripes with the stripes' period and orientation,
and corners. With the same number $s$ of atoms per tile, the learnt dictionary rebuilds the image with a smaller error
than the 35 non-constant cosine atoms, even with fewer atoms. Elad's book develops the theory and the image applications
in full <Cite id="elad2010" />.

### Windows of a one-dimensional signal

A one-dimensional signal gives training signals in the same way. Cut it into overlapping windows of $L$ samples, remove
each window's mean, and use the windows as the columns of $\Ymat$. To rebuild the signal, code every window, synthesise
it, and average the overlapping estimates at each sample, as for image patches. An electrocardiogram suits this. Every
heartbeat repeats a few waves: the small P wave, the sharp QRS complex and the broad T wave. A few atoms per window
describe a beat, and white noise, which is spread over all directions, is mostly left out of the rebuild.

The figure offers a synthetic trace and a recorded one. The recording is five minutes of lead MLII of record 208 of the
MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database <Cite id="moody2001" />, distributed by PhysioNet <Cite id="goldberger2000" /> under the
Open Data Commons Attribution License. It is decimated from 360 Hz to 90 Hz, so that a beat spans about 53 samples, and
about one beat in five is a premature ventricular beat, wide and with no P wave.

<HeartbeatDictionary />

The windows start at every eighth sample, so the same wave appears at many positions within a window. A dictionary for
windows has no notion of shift: it spends several atoms on one wave at different offsets. Convolutional sparse coding
removes this redundancy by coding the whole signal as a sum of short atoms placed at any position, so one atom serves
every shift.

## Assumptions

- The signals have sparse codes over some dictionary of $k$ atoms: each lies near the span of $s$ atoms, with $s$ small.
- The sparsity $s$ and the number of atoms $k$ are chosen in advance. Neither is learnt by the objective.
- There are many more training signals than atoms, $n \gg k$, so the atoms are fitted to structure rather than to
  individual signals.
- The scale of an atom is fixed by the unit-norm constraint. Without it, scaling an atom up and its coefficients down
  would change nothing, and $\Dmat$ would not be identifiable even up to sign and order.

## Pitfalls

- **Memorising the training set.** With $k \ge n$, the dictionary can contain every training signal, and $s = 1$ fits
  them exactly with objective zero. Such a dictionary describes the training signals rather than the class they come
  from, and represents new signals poorly. Judge a dictionary on held-out signals.
- **Comparing dictionaries.** Two runs that find the same atoms list them in different orders and with different signs.
  Match atoms by the absolute value of their inner products before comparing them.
- **Local minima.** A single run can settle with duplicated atoms or a cluster without an atom. Restart from several
  initial dictionaries and keep the best objective.
- **Unused and duplicated atoms.** Without replacement, unused atoms stay where they started. Two nearly equal atoms
  raise the dictionary's <Gloss name="mutual-coherence">mutual coherence</Gloss>, which weakens the guarantees of the
  pursuit methods used to code with it.
- **Cost.** Every round codes all $n$ signals. For large training sets, online and mini-batch variants update the
  dictionary from a few signals at a time.
- **The constant level of patches.** Without mean removal, most patches of an image are dominated by their brightness,
  and several atoms are spent on nearly constant patterns.
