<Definition>
A signal $\yvec \in \reals^N$ has a **convolutional sparse representation** over $K$ filters
$\dvec_1, \dots, \dvec_K \in \reals^L$ when

$$
\yvec \approx \sum_{k=1}^K \zvec_k * \dvec_k,
$$

where each **activation map** $\zvec_k \in \reals^M$, $M = N - L + 1$, has few non-zero entries and $*$ is the full
<NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink>, $(\zvec * \dvec)[n] = \sum_m z[m]\, d[n - m]$, of length
$M + L - 1 = N$. A non-zero $z_k[m]$ places a copy of filter $k$, scaled by $z_k[m]$, on samples $m$ to $m + L - 1$.
**Convolutional sparse coding** finds the maps for given filters, here by the convolutional lasso

$$
\min_{\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_K} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\yvec - \sum_k \zvec_k * \dvec_k}^2 + \lambda \sum_k \norm{\zvec_k}_1,
$$

and **convolutional dictionary learning** also fits the filters, each constrained to $\norm{\dvec_k} \le 1$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="dictionary-learning">Dictionary learning</NoteLink> on a long signal cuts it into windows and learns
atoms for the windows. The windows start wherever the cutting puts them, so one wave of the signal appears at every
offset within a window. An atom has a fixed offset, so the dictionary spends atoms on the same wave at different
offsets. On a synthetic electrocardiogram of 15 beats, a dictionary of 24 atoms of 32 samples, trained on windows that
start every 8 samples and coded with 3 atoms per window, uses about 20 non-zero coefficients per beat.

Convolutional sparse coding removes the offset from the atom. A filter is placed by an activation, and an activation can
sit at any sample, so a wave needs one filter however it falls. On the same trace, with a quarter of its beats ectopic,
3 filters of 48 samples and $\lambda = 0.2$ code the signal with 4 to 5 non-zero activations per beat, and the rebuild
is closer to the clean trace than the window dictionary's: an RMS error of 0.049 to 0.053 against 0.054 to 0.059, over
three random traces whose noise has RMS 0.079. With $\lambda = 0.4$ the code falls to about 2 activations per beat, at
an RMS error of about 0.07. The filters are whole beats rather than copies of one wave at many offsets.

## Details

### Sparse coding over every shift

Let $\Tmat_k \in \reals^{N \times M}$ be the matrix whose column $m$ is $\dvec_k$ shifted down by $m$ samples, so that
$\Tmat_k\zvec_k = \zvec_k * \dvec_k$. Each $\Tmat_k$ is a banded Toeplitz matrix. Stacking them,
$\Dmat = [\Tmat_1, \dots, \Tmat_K] \in \reals^{N \times KM}$ and $\zvec = (\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_K)$, gives

$$
\sum_k \zvec_k * \dvec_k = \Dmat\zvec.
$$

Convolutional sparse coding is therefore ordinary <NoteLink to="sparse-approximation">sparse approximation</NoteLink>
over a structured dictionary of $KM$ atoms, every shift of every filter. Every method for sparse approximation applies.
The structure changes two things.

- **Cost.** $\Dmat$ has $NKM$ entries, but it is never formed. Applying it is $K$ convolutions, $O(KNL)$ operations
  directly or $O(KN \log N)$ with the <Gloss name="fft">fast Fourier transform</Gloss>.
- **Coherence.** Neighbouring shifts of one smooth filter are nearly parallel, so the
  <Gloss name="mutual-coherence" /> of $\Dmat$ is close to 1 and the global uniqueness guarantee covers almost no
  signals. Papyan, Sulam and Elad replace the global count of non-zeros by a local one, the largest number in any
  window of $2L - 1$ samples, and prove uniqueness and recovery when that local count is small
  <Cite id="papyan2017" />. A signal with sparse events, such as one beat per stretch of the trace, meets the local
  condition even when its total count of non-zeros is large.

### Coding by proximal gradient descent

Write $f(\zvec) = \frac{1}{2}\norm{\yvec - \Dmat\zvec}^2$ with residual $\rvec = \yvec - \Dmat\zvec$. Its gradient is
$\nabla f = -\Dmat^\top\rvec$, whose block for map $k$ is $-\Tmat_k^\top\rvec$. Entry $m$ of $\Tmat_k^\top\rvec$ is the
inner product of $\rvec$ with column $m$ of $\Tmat_k$:

$$
(\Tmat_k^\top\rvec)[m] = \sum_n r[n]\, d_k[n - m] = \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} r[m + l]\, d_k[l] = (\rvec \star \dvec_k)[m],
$$

the cross-correlation of the residual with the filter over the $M$ lags at which the filter lies inside the signal.
Correlation is the adjoint of convolution. The gradient therefore costs $K$ correlations, and each map's gradient is
large where the residual looks like the filter, as in a <NoteLink to="matched-filter">matched filter</NoteLink>.

The $\ell_1$ penalty is separable, and its <NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">proximal operator</NoteLink> is soft
thresholding, $S_\tau(v) = \operatorname{sign}(v)\max(\abs{v} - \tau, 0)$. A proximal gradient step with step size
$\eta$ is

$$
\zvec_k \leftarrow S_{\lambda\eta}\big(\zvec_k + \eta\, (\rvec \star \dvec_k)\big), \qquad k = 1, \dots, K,
$$

and <Gloss name="fista" /> adds an extrapolation between steps <Cite id="beck2009" />. The step must satisfy
$\eta \le 1/L_f$, where $L_f = \lambda_{\max}(\Dmat^\top\Dmat)$ is the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla f$. Young's
inequality, $\norm{\zvec * \dvec} \le \norm{\dvec}_1 \norm{\zvec}$, bounds it by $\sum_k \norm{\dvec_k}_1^2$, but that
bound is loose for smooth filters. The figure estimates $L_f$ by power iteration on $\Dmat^\top\Dmat$, which needs only
convolutions and correlations, and uses $\eta = 1/(1.1\,\hat{L}_f)$ because power iteration approaches the eigenvalue
from below.

### Updating the filters

With the maps fixed, the model is linear in the $KL$ filter taps. Filter tap $l$ of filter $k$ multiplies the map
$\zvec_k$ shifted by $l$, so the design matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{N \times KL}$ has column $(k, l)$ equal to that
shifted map. The least-squares filters solve the normal equations $\Amat^\top\Amat\,\dvec = \Amat^\top\yvec$, and both
sides are correlations:

$$
(\Amat^\top\Amat)_{(a, l), (b, l')} = \sum_m z_a[m + l' - l]\, z_b[m], \qquad (\Amat^\top\yvec)_{(k, l)} = \sum_m z_k[m]\, y[m + l].
$$

The system has $KL$ unknowns, 144 for three filters of 48 taps, whatever the length of the signal. This is the
convolutional form of the <Gloss name="mod" /> of dictionary learning.

The filters need a constraint. Replacing $(\dvec_k, \zvec_k)$ by $(c\,\dvec_k, \zvec_k/c)$ leaves the synthesis
unchanged and divides the penalty $\lambda\norm{\zvec_k}_1$ by $c$, so without a bound the filters grow and the maps
shrink towards zero. Constraining $\norm{\dvec_k} \le 1$ removes this freedom <Cite id="garciacardona2018" />. The
constrained least squares is solved by projected FISTA on the normal equations: a gradient step, then any filter longer
than 1 is scaled back to unit norm, the projection onto the unit ball. The step $1/L_G$ uses the Gershgorin bound
$L_G = \max_i \sum_j \abs{(\Amat^\top\Amat)_{ij}}$ on the largest eigenvalue.

Learning alternates the two stages. Each round updates the filters for the current maps, then recodes from the current
maps for a fixed number of FISTA steps. The filter update starts from the current filters and the coding from the
current maps, so neither stage increases the objective beyond what a finite number of steps leaves. The objective is
not convex in filters and maps together, and the learnt filters depend on the start.

### Worked example

Take one filter $\dvec = (1, 2, 1)$ and the signal $\yvec = (0, 0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 0, 0, -1, -2, -1, 0)$, so $N = 12$,
$L = 3$ and $M = 10$. The signal is one copy of $\dvec$ starting at sample 2 and one copy of $-\dvec$ starting at sample
8. The two copies do not overlap. Restricted to activations $z[2] = a$ and $z[8] = b$, the objective separates:

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\dvec}^2 (a - 1)^2 + \lambda\abs{a} + \tfrac{1}{2}\norm{\dvec}^2 (b + 1)^2 + \lambda\abs{b},
$$

with $\norm{\dvec}^2 = 6$. Setting the derivative of the first term to zero for $a > 0$ gives
$6(a - 1) + \lambda = 0$, so $a = 1 - \lambda/6$, and by symmetry $b = -(1 - \lambda/6)$. With $\lambda = 0.1$, the
activations are $\pm 0.9833$, and FISTA from zero reaches them, with the other eight entries of the map at exactly
zero. The penalty shrinks each activation by $\lambda/\norm{\dvec}^2$: a larger $\lambda$ gives smaller activations, and
from $\lambda = 6$ upwards both are zero.

### A heartbeat

The figure learns filters from a synthetic electrocardiogram of 900 samples, about 15 beats of 54 to 66 samples. Each
beat is a small P wave, a sharp QRS complex and a broad T wave, and a fraction of the beats are ectopic: wide, with no P
wave. Slow baseline wander is not sparse over short filters, so it is removed first by subtracting a moving average
over 121 samples. The initial filters are random stretches of the trace.

The figure also offers a recorded trace, chosen by its start time; from 170 s the stretch holds seven premature
ventricular beats among sixteen. The recording is five minutes of lead MLII of record 208 of the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia
Database <Cite id="moody2001" />, distributed by PhysioNet <Cite id="goldberger2000" /> under the Open Data Commons
Attribution License. It is decimated from 360 Hz to 90 Hz, so that a beat spans about 53 samples, and about one beat in
five is a premature ventricular beat, wide and with no P wave.

After a few rounds the activation maps show the structure of the trace. With the defaults, each beat is placed by
about one activation, and the stems line up with the beats. One filter becomes the wide ectopic beat and fires only at
the ectopic beats: over three random traces it put 98 to 100 percent of its activation there. The other two become
variants of the normal beat, a QRS complex followed by its T wave, and share the normal beats between them. The zoom
shows how a beat is rebuilt from a few scaled copies of the filters. Lowering $\lambda$ adds small activations that fit
finer detail and some of the noise; raising it leaves one activation per beat or fewer.

### Solvers and history

The direct convolutions used here suit short filters and signals of a few thousand samples. For long signals and images,
solvers work in the Fourier domain, where convolution is a product and the coding subproblem of the
<Gloss name="admm">alternating direction method of multipliers</Gloss> has a closed form at each frequency
<Cite id="bristow2013" /> <Cite id="wohlberg2016" />. Garcia-Cardona and Wohlberg compare these solvers and the filter
updates <Cite id="garciacardona2018" />.

Grosse and colleagues introduced shift-invariant sparse coding to learn features of audio for classification
<Cite id="grosse2007" />. Zeiler and colleagues stacked convolutional sparse coding layers into deconvolutional networks
for images <Cite id="zeiler2010" />. A dictionary of windows learnt by <Gloss name="k-svd" /> <Cite id="aharon2006" />
can only approximate this structure, with a separate atom for each offset of a shape that it meets.

## Assumptions

- The signal is a sum of a few shapes, each repeated at arbitrary positions with arbitrary amplitudes. A shape that
  stretches or compresses, such as a beat at a different heart rate, is only approximated by a fixed filter.
- The noise is spread over all positions and frequencies, so the penalty drops it with the small activations.
- Components that are not sparse over short filters, such as baseline wander, are removed beforehand or modelled
  separately.

## Pitfalls

- **The penalty sets the sparsity.** Too small a $\lambda$ fits the noise with many small activations; too large a
  $\lambda$ removes small waves first. The figure's default, $\lambda = 0.4$, suits noise of standard deviation about
  0.08 on beats of height 1.
- **The filter length decides what a filter is.** A filter much shorter than a beat learns parts of a beat; a filter
  longer than the gap between beats can only be placed once per gap and absorbs pieces of the neighbouring beats.
- **Filters are defined up to a shift within their support.** A wave can sit anywhere inside its filter's $L$ taps,
  with the activation shifted to match, so two runs can learn the same wave at different offsets. Compare filters up
  to a shift.
- **Boundaries.** An activation places a whole filter inside the signal, so a wave cut by either end of the signal is
  fitted only partly, by a filter placed further in, or is left in the residual.
- **Local minima.** Filters and maps are fitted jointly by a non-convex problem; different seeds can split the beat
  differently. Rerun with several starts and keep the lowest objective.
