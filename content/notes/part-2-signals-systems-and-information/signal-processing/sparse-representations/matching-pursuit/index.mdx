<Definition>
Let $\Dmat = [\dvec_1, \dots, \dvec_k]$ be an $m \times k$ dictionary of unit-norm atoms $\dvec_j \in \reals^m$, usually
with $k > m$, and let $\yvec \in \reals^m$ be a signal. Both pursuits start from $\xvec_0 = \zeros$ and the residual
$\rvec_0 = \yvec$, and at step $t = 0, 1, \dots$ pick the atom most correlated with the residual,

$$
j_t = \argmax_j \abs{\dvec_j^\top \rvec_t}.
$$

- **Matching pursuit** <Cite id="mallat1993" /> adds the residual's projection onto that atom alone:
  $x_{j_t} \leftarrow x_{j_t} + \dvec_{j_t}^\top \rvec_t$ and
  $\rvec_{t+1} = \rvec_t - (\dvec_{j_t}^\top \rvec_t)\,\dvec_{j_t}$.
- **Orthogonal matching pursuit** <Cite id="pati1993" /> adds $j_t$ to a support $S_{t+1} = S_t \cup \{j_t\}$ and refits
  every coefficient on it by least squares,
  $\xvec_{S_{t+1}} = \argmin_{\mathbf{z}} \norm{\yvec - \Dmat_{S_{t+1}}\mathbf{z}}$, with
  $\rvec_{t+1} = \yvec - \Dmat_{S_{t+1}}\xvec_{S_{t+1}}$.

Both stop after a set number of atoms $s$ or when $\norm{\rvec_t}$ falls below a tolerance.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A sparse code of $\yvec$ uses a few atoms. Finding the best few atoms exactly means searching over subsets, which is
NP-hard in general <Cite id="natarajan1995" />. A pursuit builds the subset one atom at a time instead. At each step it
asks which single atom explains most of what is still unexplained, the residual, and takes it.

The two pursuits differ in what they do with an atom once it is chosen. Matching pursuit keeps every earlier coefficient
fixed and only adds the new atom's share. The residual is then orthogonal to the last atom but not to the earlier ones,
so an atom can be picked again later. <Gloss name="omp" /> recomputes all coefficients on the chosen atoms. Its residual
is orthogonal to every chosen atom, so no atom is picked twice, and after $s$ steps $\xvec$ has exactly $s$ non-zeros.

## The greedy choice

Fix an atom $\dvec$ with $\norm{\dvec} = 1$ and a residual $\rvec$. The coefficient $\alpha$ that best explains
$\rvec$ by $\dvec$ alone minimises

$$
\norm{\rvec - \alpha\dvec}^2 = \norm{\rvec}^2 - 2\alpha\,\dvec^\top\rvec + \alpha^2.
$$

Setting the derivative $-2\dvec^\top\rvec + 2\alpha$ to zero gives $\alpha = \dvec^\top\rvec$, the
<NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> of $\rvec$ onto the line spanned by $\dvec$.
Substituting back,

$$
\norm{\rvec - (\dvec^\top\rvec)\,\dvec}^2 = \norm{\rvec}^2 - (\dvec^\top\rvec)^2.
$$

The atom that removes the most energy from the residual is therefore the one with the largest $\abs{\dvec^\top\rvec}$.
This is the selection rule of both pursuits. For atoms that are not unit-norm the same argument gives the normalised
score $\abs{\dvec_j^\top\rvec} / \norm{\dvec_j}$.

## Matching pursuit: energy and convergence

Applying the identity above at every step and summing from $t = 0$ to $T - 1$ gives an energy balance:

$$
\norm{\yvec}^2 = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (\dvec_{j_t}^\top \rvec_t)^2 + \norm{\rvec_T}^2.
$$

The residual norm never increases, and each step removes the squared correlation it found. When the atoms span
$\reals^m$, the quantity $\beta = \min_{\norm{\uvec} = 1} \max_j \abs{\dvec_j^\top\uvec}$ is positive: no unit vector is
orthogonal to every atom, and the minimum over the unit sphere is attained. Every step then removes at least a fraction
$\beta^2$ of the residual's energy,

$$
\norm{\rvec_{t+1}}^2 \le (1 - \beta^2)\,\norm{\rvec_t}^2,
$$

so $\norm{\rvec_t}$ falls geometrically to zero <Cite id="mallat1993" />. It does not reach zero in finitely many steps
in general. In the plane with two atoms at angle $\theta$, each step leaves the residual orthogonal to the atom just
used, and the angle between that residual and the other atom is $90° - \theta$. The next step keeps the fraction
$\abs{\cos\theta}$ of the residual's norm. Atoms close in angle give a slow zigzag.

## Orthogonal matching pursuit: refitting

Orthogonal matching pursuit solves a least-squares problem on the current support $S$. Its normal equations are

$$
\Dmat_S^\top(\yvec - \Dmat_S\xvec_S) = \Dmat_S^\top\rvec = \zeros.
$$

The residual is orthogonal to every chosen atom, so every chosen atom has correlation zero and cannot be picked again.
The support grows by one atom per step, and the approximation $\Dmat_S\xvec_S$ is the orthogonal projection of $\yvec$
onto the span of the chosen atoms. Once the chosen atoms span $\reals^m$ the residual is zero, so the method stops after
at most $m$ steps.

Each least-squares refit can reuse the previous one. Adding an atom adds one column to $\Dmat_S$, and its QR
factorisation grows by one step of the <NoteLink to="gram-schmidt">Gram–Schmidt process</NoteLink>. A step costs
$O(mk)$ for the correlations and $O(m\,\abs{S})$ for the update <Cite id="elad2010" />.

## Worked example in the plane

Take three unit atoms in $\reals^2$ at angles $0°$, $70°$ and $125°$,
$\dvec_1 = (1, 0)$, $\dvec_2 = (0.342, 0.940)$ and $\dvec_3 = (-0.574, 0.819)$, and the signal $\yvec = (1.3, 1.1)$ with
$\norm{\yvec} = 1.703$. This is the opening state of the first figure.

1. **Step 1, both pursuits.** The correlations are $\dvec_1^\top\yvec = 1.300$, $\dvec_2^\top\yvec = 1.478$ and
   $\dvec_3^\top\yvec = 0.155$. Both pick $\dvec_2$ with coefficient $1.478$. The residual is
   $\rvec_1 = \yvec - 1.478\,\dvec_2 = (0.794, -0.289)$, and $\norm{\rvec_1}^2 = 2.900 - 1.478^2 = 0.715$, so
   $\norm{\rvec_1} = 0.845$.
2. **Step 2, matching pursuit.** The correlations of $\rvec_1$ are $0.794$, $0$ and $-0.692$. Matching pursuit picks
   $\dvec_1$ with coefficient $0.794$, and $\norm{\rvec_2}^2 = 0.715 - 0.794^2 = 0.084$, so $\norm{\rvec_2} = 0.289$.
   The new residual $(0, -0.289)$ is orthogonal to $\dvec_1$ but not to $\dvec_2$, so step 3 picks $\dvec_2$ again. From
   here matching pursuit alternates between $\dvec_1$ and $\dvec_2$ and keeps the fraction $\cos 70° = 0.342$ of the
   norm per step.
3. **Step 2, orthogonal matching pursuit.** It also picks $\dvec_1$, then solves
   $x_1\dvec_1 + x_2\dvec_2 = \yvec$ on the support $\{1, 2\}$. The second coordinate gives
   $x_2 = 1.1 / 0.940 = 1.171$ and the first gives $x_1 = 1.3 - 0.342 \times 1.171 = 0.900$. The residual is zero after
   two steps, and the coefficient of $\dvec_2$ has dropped from $1.478$ to $1.171$.

The two pursuits choose the same atoms here. Matching pursuit needs 22 steps to bring the residual below
$10^{-10}\norm{\yvec}$ because it never revises the first coefficient. The spare atom $\dvec_3$ is never used.

## Pursuit on a signal

The second figure codes a signal of $m = 64$ samples over $k = 128$ atoms: the 64 cosines of the orthonormal
<Gloss name="dct" /> and the 64 spikes (unit impulses). A cosine spreads its energy over all samples and a spike
concentrates it in one, so a signal made of a few of each has no sparse code in either half alone. Over the union it is
sparse.

The correlation bars show what each step sees. At step 0 the largest bars usually stand at the atoms the signal was
built from, because their contributions add up while the other atoms meet the signal only through small
cross-correlations. After each pick of orthogonal matching pursuit the chosen bar drops to zero and the next one stands
out. Matching pursuit leaves small correlations on the atoms it has already used and returns to them.

## When the greedy choice is right

The pursuits pick the right atoms when the atoms are nearly orthogonal to each other. The
<Gloss name="mutual-coherence">mutual coherence</Gloss> of a dictionary of unit atoms is the largest absolute inner
product between two different atoms,

$$
\mu = \max_{i \ne j} \abs{\dvec_i^\top\dvec_j}.
$$

**Exact recovery.** Suppose $\yvec = \Dmat\xvec^\star$ where $\xvec^\star$ has $s$ non-zeros on a support $S^\star$ and

$$
s < \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{1}{\mu}\right).
$$

Then $\xvec^\star$ is the unique sparsest representation of $\yvec$ <Cite id="donoho2003" />, and orthogonal matching
pursuit picks an atom of $S^\star$ at every step and returns $\xvec^\star$ after $s$ steps <Cite id="tropp2004" />.

The proof compares the correlations of right and wrong atoms. Suppose the steps so far chose only atoms of $S^\star$.
Then $\yvec$ and the current approximation both lie in the span of $\{\dvec_i : i \in S^\star\}$, so the residual is
$\rvec = \sum_{i \in S^\star} b_i \dvec_i$ for some coefficients $b_i$. Let $b_{\max} = \max_i \abs{b_i} > 0$, attained
at $i^\star$. For the atom $i^\star$,

$$
\abs{\dvec_{i^\star}^\top\rvec} \ge \abs{b_{i^\star}} - \sum_{i \ne i^\star} \abs{b_i}\,\abs{\dvec_{i^\star}^\top\dvec_i}
\ge b_{\max}\bigl(1 - (s - 1)\mu\bigr).
$$

For an atom $j \notin S^\star$, every term is a cross-correlation,

$$
\abs{\dvec_j^\top\rvec} \le \sum_{i \in S^\star} \abs{b_i}\,\abs{\dvec_j^\top\dvec_i} \le s\mu\,b_{\max}.
$$

The atom of $S^\star$ wins whenever $1 - (s - 1)\mu > s\mu$, which rearranges to $s < \frac{1}{2}(1 + 1/\mu)$.
Orthogonal matching pursuit never picks an atom twice, so after $s$ steps its support is $S^\star$. The least-squares
fit on $S^\star$ is exact because $\yvec$ lies in that span, and it is unique because the same bound makes the atoms of
$S^\star$ linearly independent. The argument for matching pursuit is the same, except that it may revisit the atoms of
$S^\star$ and converges to $\xvec^\star$ only in the limit.

<Derivation title="Why the atoms of the support are linearly independent">
Let $\Gmat = \Dmat_{S^\star}^\top\Dmat_{S^\star}$ be the $s \times s$ Gram matrix of the atoms in $S^\star$. Its
diagonal entries are $1$ and each off-diagonal entry has magnitude at most $\mu$. Each row's off-diagonal entries sum in
magnitude to at most $(s - 1)\mu$. The condition $s < \frac{1}{2}(1 + 1/\mu)$ gives $(2s - 1)\mu < 1$, so
$(s - 1)\mu < 1$, and $\Gmat$ is strictly diagonally dominant. By the Gershgorin circle theorem every eigenvalue of
$\Gmat$ lies within $(s - 1)\mu$ of $1$, so every eigenvalue is positive and $\Gmat$ is invertible. Then
$\Dmat_{S^\star}\mathbf{z} = \zeros$ implies $\mathbf{z}^\top\Gmat\mathbf{z} = 0$ and $\mathbf{z} = \zeros$: the atoms
are linearly independent.
</Derivation>

The condition is sufficient, not necessary. The cosines and spikes of the second figure have
$\mu = \sqrt{2/64} = 0.177$, so the guarantee covers $s \le 3$. In the figure, orthogonal matching pursuit recovers
signals of 8 cosines and 8 spikes in 96 of 99 seeds, and signals of 12 and 12 in 41 of 99. The coherence bound describes
the worst arrangement of atoms, and a signal with randomly placed atoms rarely meets it.

## Variants and uses

Matching pursuit was introduced with dictionaries of Gabor atoms, windowed sinusoids at many positions, scales and
frequencies. The atoms it picks form an adaptive time–frequency decomposition of the signal
<Cite id="mallat1993" />. With such structured dictionaries, the correlations at each step can be updated by fast
transforms rather than computed from scratch <Cite id="mallat2009" />.

Orthogonal least squares, also called least-squares orthogonal matching pursuit, picks the atom whose addition minimises
the residual after refitting, not the atom with the largest correlation. It chooses better atoms at a higher cost per
step <Cite id="elad2010" />. In regression, where $\Dmat$ is a design matrix and the atoms are features, orthogonal
matching pursuit is forward stepwise <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink> by correlation with
the residual. <NoteLink to="least-angle-regression">Least angle regression</NoteLink> follows the same correlations but
moves the coefficients continuously, and with one modification it traces the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> path.
<NoteLink to="basis-pursuit">Basis pursuit</NoteLink> replaces the greedy search by a convex problem, the representation
of least $\ell_1$ norm, which finds the sparsest code under the same coherence condition <Cite id="donoho2003" />.

## Assumptions

- The signal has a sparse or compressible code in the dictionary: a few coefficients carry most of its energy.
- The atoms are scaled to unit norm, or the selection uses the normalised correlation
  $\abs{\dvec_j^\top\rvec} / \norm{\dvec_j}$.
- Guaranteed recovery needs low coherence: $s < \frac{1}{2}(1 + 1/\mu)$ for $s$ non-zeros.
- Convergence of matching pursuit to $\yvec$ needs atoms that span $\reals^m$.

## Pitfalls

- **A greedy choice is final.** Orthogonal matching pursuit never removes an atom. When a wrong atom correlates with
  $\yvec$ better than any right atom, as happens with coherent atoms or dense signals, the wrong atom stays and more
  atoms are needed to cancel it.
- **Matching pursuit's coefficients are not a least-squares fit.** After $t$ steps the coefficients on the atoms used do
  not minimise the residual for those atoms. A final least-squares refit on the chosen atoms improves the approximation
  at no cost in sparsity.
- **Unnormalised atoms bias the choice.** Scoring by $\abs{\dvec_j^\top\rvec}$ without dividing by $\norm{\dvec_j}$
  favours long atoms regardless of direction.
- **With noise, the stopping rule sets the sparsity.** Run to a small tolerance, either pursuit keeps picking atoms to
  fit the noise. For noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ per sample, stopping when $\norm{\rvec} \le \sigma\sqrt{m}$
  stops near the noise level.
- **The coherence bound is pessimistic.** Failing it does not mean the pursuit fails; passing it guarantees success only
  for exactly sparse, noise-free signals.
