<Definition>
A **dictionary** is a matrix $\Dmat \in \reals^{m \times k}$ whose columns $\dvec_1, \dots, \dvec_k$ are **atoms**,
usually of unit norm. A **sparse approximation** of a signal $\yvec \in \reals^m$ is a coefficient vector
$\xvec \in \reals^k$ with few non-zero entries such that $\Dmat\xvec$ is close to $\yvec$. The number of non-zeros is
$\norm{\xvec}_0 = \abs{\{j : x_j \ne 0\}}$, and the set of their indices is the **support** of $\xvec$. The exact
problem is

$$
\min_{\xvec} \norm{\xvec}_0 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Dmat\xvec = \yvec,
$$

and the approximate problems bound the error, $\norm{\yvec - \Dmat\xvec} \le \varepsilon$, or the sparsity,
$\norm{\xvec}_0 \le s$. A vector with at most $s$ non-zeros is **$s$-sparse**.
</Definition>

## Sparsity depends on the dictionary

A signal is sparse only relative to a dictionary. A sum of two sinusoids sampled at 64 points has 64 non-zero samples,
but only a few large coefficients in a basis of cosines. A piecewise-constant signal has few non-zero coefficients in
the <NoteLink to="wavelet-families">Haar wavelet</NoteLink> basis, whose atoms are steps. A train of isolated pulses is
already sparse in the basis of spikes, the columns of the identity.

For an orthonormal basis $\Bmat = [\bvec_1, \dots, \bvec_m]$, the best $s$-term approximation has a closed form. The
coefficients are $c_j = \bvec_j^\top\yvec$, and $\yvec = \sum_j c_j \bvec_j$. Keeping the terms with indices in a set
$S$ of size $s$ leaves the error

$$
\norm{\yvec - \sum_{j \in S} c_j \bvec_j}^2 = \norm{\sum_{j \notin S} c_j \bvec_j}^2 = \sum_{j \notin S} c_j^2,
$$

where the second equality holds because the $\bvec_j$ are orthonormal (Parseval's identity). The error is smallest when
the dropped coefficients are the smallest, so the best $s$-term approximation keeps the $s$ coefficients of largest
magnitude. This operation is **hard thresholding**, $H_s(\cvec)$. The relative error after $s$ terms,
$\sqrt{\sum_{j \notin S} c_j^2} \big/ \norm{\yvec}$, measures how compressible the signal is in that basis
<Cite id="mallat2009" />.

## Overcomplete dictionaries

A dictionary with $k > m$ atoms is **overcomplete**. The union of two orthonormal bases, $\Dmat = [\Bmat_1, \Bmat_2]$
with $k = 2m$, is the simplest case. Overcompleteness lets one dictionary serve signals of several kinds, and lets a
signal that mixes kinds have a short code. Its cost is that $\Dmat\xvec = \yvec$ has infinitely many solutions: the
null space of $\Dmat$ has dimension at least $k - m$, and adding any null-space vector to a solution gives another. The
coefficients are no longer inner products with the atoms, and choosing the sparsest solution is a combinatorial search.
Finding the sparsest $\xvec$ is NP-hard for a general $\Dmat$ <Cite id="natarajan1995" />.

The figure below builds a signal from a few cosines and a few spikes and codes it with orthogonal matching pursuit over
three dictionaries: the spikes, the cosines, and their union. Over either basis the error falls slowly with the number
of atoms. Over the union it falls to zero once the pursuit has picked as many atoms as the signal holds, and the code
splits the signal into a cosine part and a spike part. The union of two bases is coded more sparsely than either basis
because each part is sparse in one of them.

**Worked example.** Take $m = 3$ and four unit atoms: the spikes $\evec_1, \evec_2, \evec_3$ and the diagonal
$\dvec_4 = (\evec_1 + \evec_2)/\sqrt{2}$. The signal $\yvec = (1, 1, 0)$ has the representation $\xvec = (1, 1, 0, 0)$,
with two non-zeros and $\norm{\xvec}_1 = 2$, and the representation $\xvec' = (0, 0, 0, \sqrt{2})$, with one non-zero
and $\norm{\xvec'}_1 = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. Every combination $t\xvec + (1 - t)\xvec'$ is also a representation. The
sparsest one, $\xvec'$, also has the smallest $\ell_1$ norm. It is also the first atom that a greedy search picks,
because the correlations of $\yvec$ with the four atoms are $1, 1, 0$ and $2/\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.

## When the sparsest representation is unique

The **spark** of $\Dmat$ is the smallest number of columns that are linearly dependent. Suppose $\yvec = \Dmat\xvec =
\Dmat\xvec'$ with $\xvec \ne \xvec'$ both $s$-sparse. Then $\Dmat(\xvec - \xvec') = \zeros$ and $\xvec - \xvec'$ has at
most $2s$ non-zeros, so some $2s$ columns are dependent and $2s \ge \operatorname{spark}(\Dmat)$. Conversely, if
$s < \operatorname{spark}(\Dmat)/2$, no two different $s$-sparse vectors have the same image, and an $s$-sparse
representation is the unique sparsest one <Cite id="donoho2003" />.

The spark is itself expensive to compute. The **<Gloss name="mutual-coherence" />** bounds it with a quantity that is
cheap:

$$
\mu(\Dmat) = \max_{i \ne j} \frac{\abs{\dvec_i^\top\dvec_j}}{\norm{\dvec_i}\norm{\dvec_j}},
$$

the largest absolute cosine between two atoms. Normalise the atoms and take any $p$ of them as the columns of
$\Dmat_S$. Their Gram matrix $\Gmat = \Dmat_S^\top\Dmat_S$ has unit diagonal and off-diagonal entries of magnitude at
most $\mu$. By the Gershgorin circle theorem every eigenvalue of $\Gmat$ lies within $(p - 1)\mu$ of 1, so
$\lambda_{\min}(\Gmat) \ge 1 - (p - 1)\mu$. When $p < 1 + 1/\mu$ this is positive, $\Gmat$ is invertible and the $p$
columns are independent. Hence $\operatorname{spark}(\Dmat) \ge 1 + 1/\mu$, and

$$
\norm{\xvec}_0 < \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{1}{\mu(\Dmat)}\right)
$$

guarantees that $\xvec$ is the unique sparsest representation of $\Dmat\xvec$ <Cite id="donoho2003" />. The same
condition guarantees that orthogonal matching pursuit and basis pursuit both find it <Cite id="tropp2004" />.

Coherence cannot be made arbitrarily small. For $k$ unit atoms in $\reals^m$ the Welch bound gives
$\mu \ge \sqrt{(k - m)/(m(k - 1))}$, about $1/\sqrt{m}$ when $k = 2m$ <Cite id="welch1974" />. The spikes and the
orthonormal <Gloss name="dct" /> cosines in $\reals^{64}$ have $\mu = \sqrt{2/64} \approx 0.18$, so the guarantee covers
signals of at most 3 atoms. The bound is a worst case. In the solver figure below, with the default seed, orthogonal
matching pursuit and basis pursuit both recover 12 cosines plus 12 spikes exactly, 24 atoms, and both fail at 16 plus 16.

## Components

The methods split by how they search for the support.

| Approach                     | Method                                                                 | Problem solved                                                             | Typical cost per signal                |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Greedy                       | <NoteLink to="matching-pursuit">matching pursuit</NoteLink>             | picks one atom per step, subtracts its projection                          | one correlation $\Dmat^\top\rvec$ per step |
| Greedy                       | orthogonal matching pursuit                                            | picks one atom per step, refits all chosen atoms by least squares          | a least-squares solve per step          |
| Convex relaxation            | <NoteLink to="basis-pursuit">basis pursuit</NoteLink>                   | $\min \norm{\xvec}_1$ subject to $\Dmat\xvec = \yvec$, a linear program    | a linear program                        |
| Convex relaxation            | basis pursuit denoising (the lasso)                                    | $\min \frac{1}{2}\norm{\yvec - \Dmat\xvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\xvec}_1$      | proximal gradient steps                 |
| Thresholding                 | iterative hard thresholding                                            | $\min \norm{\yvec - \Dmat\xvec}^2$ subject to $\norm{\xvec}_0 \le s$       | gradient steps                          |
| Learning the dictionary      | <NoteLink to="dictionary-learning">dictionary learning</NoteLink>       | $\Dmat$ and the codes of many signals jointly                              | sparse coding of every signal per round |
| Sensing with few measurements | <NoteLink to="compressed-sensing">compressed sensing</NoteLink>         | a sparse $\xvec$ from $m \ll k$ random measurements                        | any of the solvers above                |

**Greedy pursuits** build the support one atom at a time. Each step picks the atom most correlated with the current
residual $\rvec = \yvec - \Dmat\xvec$. <Gloss name="matching-pursuit">Matching pursuit</Gloss> moves the residual's
projection onto that atom into its coefficient <Cite id="mallat1993" />. <Gloss name="omp" /> refits every chosen atom
by least squares, so the residual is orthogonal to all of them.

**Convex relaxation** replaces $\norm{\xvec}_0$ by $\norm{\xvec}_1 = \sum_j \abs{x_j}$, the largest convex function that
lies below it on the cube $\norm{\xvec}_\infty \le 1$. <Gloss name="basis-pursuit">Basis pursuit</Gloss> is the exact
form, a linear program; <Gloss name="bpdn" /> trades the fit against the $\ell_1$ norm with a weight $\lambda$
<Cite id="chen1998" />. The worked example above shows why the $\ell_1$ norm favours sparse solutions: the sparse
representation $\xvec'$ has the smaller $\ell_1$ norm.

**Thresholding** methods alternate a gradient step on $\frac{1}{2}\norm{\yvec - \Dmat\xvec}^2$ with a projection onto
sparse vectors. Soft thresholding gives the <Gloss name="ista" /> for the lasso; hard thresholding
to $s$ entries gives <Gloss name="iht" />.

**The dictionary** is fixed in all of these. A fixed dictionary comes from analysis: cosines for smooth signals,
wavelets for piecewise-smooth ones, a union for mixtures. Dictionary learning fits the atoms to a set of training
signals instead <Cite id="aharon2006" />.

**Compressed sensing** uses the same solvers on a different matrix. The measurements are $m$ random linear combinations
of a signal that is sparse in a known basis, and the solvers recover the signal from far fewer measurements than its
length <Cite id="donoho2006" />.

## Data flow

A sparse-approximation pipeline has three stages:

1. Choose a dictionary $\Dmat$: a fixed basis, a union of bases, or a learned dictionary. The atoms are normalised so
   that correlations compare shapes rather than scales.
2. Code each signal: a pursuit or a solver returns $\xvec$, given a sparsity $s$, a tolerance $\varepsilon$ or a
   penalty $\lambda$.
3. Use the code. The support and coefficients compress the signal; the synthesis $\Dmat\xvec$ is a denoised signal,
   because noise spreads over all atoms and is dropped with the small coefficients; the parts $\Dmat_{S_1}\xvec_{S_1}$
   and $\Dmat_{S_2}\xvec_{S_2}$ over the atoms of two sub-dictionaries separate a signal into its components.

The figure below runs every solver on one signal. Over the spikes alone or the cosines alone, the code is dense. Over
the union, the code separates the cosine part from the spike part, and each solver fails in its own way when the
signal has too many atoms or when its parameter is set badly. Matching pursuit with a small step budget leaves a
residual; the lasso with a large $\lambda$ shrinks every coefficient towards zero; iterative hard thresholding with $s$
below the true number of atoms settles on a wrong support.

## Variants

- **Error-constrained and sparsity-constrained forms.** The approximate problem either bounds the error and minimises
  $\norm{\xvec}_0$, or bounds $\norm{\xvec}_0 \le s$ and minimises the error. Greedy pursuits handle both by their
  stopping rule.
- **Analysis sparsity.** The synthesis model above builds $\yvec$ from few atoms. The analysis model asks that
  $\Omegamat\yvec$ be sparse for an analysis operator $\Omegamat$, such as finite differences for
  piecewise-constant signals. The two models coincide for an orthonormal basis and differ for overcomplete ones
  <Cite id="elad2010" />.
- **Structured sparsity.** Group sparsity selects predefined groups of atoms together, and tree sparsity follows the
  parent-child structure of wavelet coefficients.
- **Many signals.** Coding the columns of a matrix $\Ymat$ independently gives a code matrix $\Xmat$ with
  $\Ymat \approx \Dmat\Xmat$, the step that dictionary learning alternates with its dictionary update.

## Assumptions

- The signal is close to a combination of few atoms of $\Dmat$. Recovery guarantees need the atoms to be incoherent,
  or, for compressed sensing, the <Gloss name="rip" />.
- The atoms are normalised, or the pursuits normalise the correlations, so that a large atom is not preferred for its
  scale.
- Noise is spread over all atoms. Noise that is itself sparse in $\Dmat$, such as impulses over a dictionary that
  contains spikes, is coded as signal.

## Pitfalls

- A sparse code over a fixed basis is only as good as the basis. Signals that mix kinds need a union of bases or a
  learned dictionary.
- The coherence bound is a worst case. A method can recover far more atoms than $\frac{1}{2}(1 + 1/\mu)$ on typical
  signals, and can also fail below that count when the signal has noise.
- The $\ell_1$ penalty shrinks every kept coefficient by $\lambda$. Refitting the selected atoms by least squares
  removes that bias.
- The tolerance and the sparsity are linked to the noise level. A tolerance below the noise norm makes a pursuit fit
  the noise with extra atoms.
