<Definition>
**Fourier analysis** represents a signal as a sum, or integral, of complex sinusoids $e^{i 2\pi f t}$, each with an
amplitude and a phase. The set of amplitudes and phases against frequency is the signal's spectrum. Four transforms
cover the four combinations of continuous or discrete time with periodic or aperiodic signals, and the discrete
Fourier transform is the one computed in practice <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Sinusoids are the eigenfunctions of <Gloss name="lti">linear time-invariant systems</Gloss>: a sinusoid passes through
such a system with only its amplitude and phase changed. Describing a signal by frequency therefore turns filtering into
multiplication and makes the effect of a system visible at a glance. The spectrum also exposes what the time series
hides, such as a hum at 50 Hz, the pitch of a voice or the period of a seasonal pattern.

In machine learning, spectrograms built from the DFT are the standard input representation for audio, through the
<NoteLink to="mel-spectrogram" />. The fast Fourier transform computes the long
<NoteLink to="convolution">convolutions</NoteLink> of <NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models" />.
<NoteLink to="random-fourier-features" /> approximate a shift-invariant kernel by sampling frequencies from its Fourier
transform. The <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network" /> is derived from a Fourier transform on graphs, and
<NoteLink to="sinusoidal-positional-encoding" /> encodes positions with sinusoids of geometrically spaced frequencies.

## Main ideas

**Every signal is a sum of sinusoids.** A periodic signal is a sum of harmonics, its
<NoteLink to="fourier-series" />, with coefficients found by orthogonality. Letting the period grow gives the
<NoteLink to="fourier-transform" />, a continuum of frequencies. At a jump the partial sums of a series overshoot by
about 9% however many terms are kept, the Gibbs phenomenon. <NoteLink to="fourier-epicycles" /> applies the series to
a closed curve in the plane and draws it with chained rotating arrows.

**Sampling in one domain makes the other periodic.** A sampled sequence has a spectrum that repeats every $2\pi$ in
angular frequency, the <NoteLink to="discrete-time-fourier-transform" />. A finite sequence evaluated at $N$
equally spaced frequencies is the <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform" />, which treats its input as one period
of a periodic sequence. The <NoteLink to="fast-fourier-transform" /> computes it in $O(N \log N)$ operations by
splitting the sum recursively into even and odd samples.

**Convolution becomes multiplication, and energy is preserved.** The <NoteLink to="convolution-theorem" /> states
that the transform of $x * h$ is $XH$. With the DFT the convolution is circular, so fast linear convolution needs
zero-padding. <NoteLink to="parseval-theorem" /> states that the transform preserves energy, so power can be measured
in either domain.

**A finite record blurs the spectrum.** Analysing $N$ samples multiplies the signal by a window, which convolves its
spectrum with the window's transform. A tone between DFT bins leaks into all of them; tapered windows trade a wider
main lobe for much lower sidelobes, as shown in <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows" />. Two tones closer than
about $f_s/N$ cannot be separated, and <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution" /> explains why zero-padding
interpolates the spectrum without improving this resolution.

**Removing negative frequencies gives envelope and phase.** The <NoteLink to="hilbert-transform" /> shifts every
positive frequency by $-90°$. Adding $i$ times it to a real signal gives the analytic signal, whose magnitude is the
envelope and whose phase gives the <NoteLink to="instantaneous-frequency">instantaneous frequency</NoteLink>.

## How the notes fit together

Read <NoteLink to="fourier-series" /> and <NoteLink to="fourier-transform" /> first, then the discrete transforms in
order: <NoteLink to="discrete-time-fourier-transform" />, <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform" />,
<NoteLink to="fast-fourier-transform" />. The convolution and Parseval theorems are properties of all four transforms.
The notes on leakage, windows and zero-padding are the practical consequences of analysing finite records and lead
into <NoteLink to="spectral-estimation" />. The Hilbert transform leads into
<NoteLink to="time-frequency-analysis" />. <NoteLink to="fourier-epicycles" /> is a worked application that can be
read once the DFT is familiar.
