<Definition>
A closed curve in the plane, traced once as $t$ runs over $[0, 1)$, is a periodic complex function
$z(t) = x(t) + i\,y(t)$ with period 1. Its complex Fourier series is

$$
z(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k\, e^{i 2\pi k t}, \qquad c_k = \int_0^1 z(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k t}\,dt,
\qquad c_k \in \complex.
$$

Each term $c_k e^{i 2\pi k t}$ is an arrow of length $\abs{c_k}$ that starts at angle $\arg c_k$ and turns $k$ times per
cycle. Placed tip to tail, the arrows form a chain of circles riding on circles, epicycles, and the last tip traces the
curve.
</Definition>

## Coefficients by orthogonality

The exponentials $e^{i 2\pi k t}$ for integer $k$ are orthonormal on $[0, 1)$:

$$
\int_0^1 e^{i 2\pi k t}\, \overline{e^{i 2\pi m t}}\,dt = \int_0^1 e^{i 2\pi (k - m) t}\,dt =
\begin{cases} 1 & k = m, \\ 0 & k \ne m. \end{cases}
$$

For $k \ne m$ the integrand turns $k - m$ whole times around the unit circle, and its integral over whole turns is
zero. Multiply the series for $z(t)$ by $e^{-i 2\pi m t}$ and integrate over one cycle. Every term with $k \ne m$
integrates to zero, and the term with $k = m$ integrates to $c_m$:

$$
\int_0^1 z(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi m t}\,dt = \sum_{k} c_k \int_0^1 e^{i 2\pi (k - m) t}\,dt = c_m.
$$

The coefficient $c_m$ is the projection of the curve onto the $m$-th exponential. The derivation is the one for a real
<NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink>; nothing in it requires $z$ to be real.

The constant term $c_0 = \int_0^1 z(t)\,dt$ is the mean position of the curve, its centroid when the curve is traced at
constant speed. The chain of arrows starts there.

## From samples to coefficients: the DFT

A curve is stored as $N$ points $z_n = z(n/N)$, for $n = 0, \dots, N-1$. Replacing the integral by the Riemann sum
over these points gives

$$
c_k \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} z_n\, e^{-i 2\pi k n / N} = \frac{1}{N}\, Z[k],
$$

where $Z[k]$ is the <Gloss name="dft" /> of the complex sequence $z_0, \dots, z_{N-1}$. The approximation is exact when
$z$ has no frequencies with $\abs{k} \ge N/2$. The <Gloss name="fft" /> computes all $N$
coefficients in $O(N \log N)$ operations.

The DFT cannot tell frequency $k$ from $k + N$: both give the same samples, an instance of
<NoteLink to="aliasing">aliasing</NoteLink>. Bin $k$ of the DFT is therefore assigned to
the frequency of smallest magnitude, $k \in \set{-N/2, \dots, N/2 - 1}$, so that the arrows turn as slowly as the
samples allow. Bins $N/2 + 1, \dots, N - 1$ are the negative frequencies $-N/2 + 1, \dots, -1$.

## Arrows that turn

Write $c_k = \abs{c_k} e^{i \varphi_k}$ with phase $\varphi_k = \arg c_k$. Then

$$
c_k\, e^{i 2\pi k t} = \abs{c_k}\, e^{i (\varphi_k + 2\pi k t)}.
$$

The term is a point on a circle of radius $\abs{c_k}$. At $t = 0$ it sits at angle $\varphi_k$. As $t$ runs from 0
to 1 its angle grows by $2\pi k$: it makes $\abs{k}$ full turns, anticlockwise for $k > 0$ and clockwise for $k < 0$.
The partial sum $c_0 + c_{k_1} e^{i 2\pi k_1 t} + c_{k_2} e^{i 2\pi k_2 t} + \cdots$ is reached by walking along the
arrows in turn: each arrow is attached to the tip of the previous one, so the circle it sweeps is centred on a point
that is itself moving. The name comes from Ptolemaic astronomy, where a planet moves on a small circle, the epicycle,
whose centre moves on a larger circle, the deferent.

### Why negative frequencies are needed

For a real signal $x(t)$ the coefficients satisfy $c_{-k} = \overline{c_k}$, so the arrows for $k$ and $-k$ are
mirror images and their sum $2 \abs{c_k} \cos(2\pi k t + \varphi_k)$ stays on the real line. A closed curve is a
complex signal and has no such symmetry. The arrows for $k$ and $-k$ are independent, and both are needed: the
ellipse below has a coefficient at $k = -1$ that no combination of positive frequencies can replace.

## Worked example: an ellipse

Take the ellipse $z(t) = a \cos(2\pi t) + i\, b \sin(2\pi t)$ with semi-axes $a, b > 0$. Substitute
$\cos\theta = (e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta})/2$ and $\sin\theta = (e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta})/(2i)$, with
$\theta = 2\pi t$:

$$
z(t) = \frac{a}{2}\paren{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}} + \frac{b}{2}\paren{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}
= \frac{a + b}{2}\, e^{i 2\pi t} + \frac{a - b}{2}\, e^{-i 2\pi t}.
$$

The ellipse has exactly two nonzero coefficients, $c_1 = (a + b)/2$ and $c_{-1} = (a - b)/2$. With $a = 2$ and $b = 1$,
an arrow of length $3/2$ turns once anticlockwise and an arrow of length $1/2$ on its tip turns once clockwise. At
$t = 0$ both point along the real axis and the tip is at $3/2 + 1/2 = 2$, the end of the major axis. At $t = 1/4$ they
point in opposite directions, $i\,3/2$ and $-i\,1/2$, and the tip is at $i$, the end of the minor axis.

Two limits follow from the same formula. A circle ($a = b$) has $c_{-1} = 0$: one arrow. A segment from $-a$ to $a$
($b = 0$) has $c_1 = c_{-1} = a/2$: two equal arrows turning in opposite directions, whose sum stays on the real axis
and runs back and forth along it. Without the clockwise arrow neither the ellipse nor the segment can be drawn.

## The order of the arrows

The tip of the chain is the sum of all the arrows, and addition of complex numbers commutes. Chaining the same arrows
in any order puts the tip at the same point at every $t$, so every order traces the same curve. Only the joints in
between move differently. Sorting by magnitude, largest first, puts the large slow circles at the base and the small
circles near the tip; sorting by frequency $0, 1, -1, 2, -2, \dots$ makes each circle turn faster than the one it rides
on. The figure offers both, a third order by signed frequency, and a random order.

## Truncation and energy

A drawing uses finitely many arrows. Let $S$ be a set of frequencies and $z_S(t) = \sum_{k \in S} c_k e^{i 2\pi k t}$
the partial sum over $S$. The error $z - z_S$ is the sum of the dropped terms. By orthonormality its mean squared size
is

$$
\int_0^1 \abs{z(t) - z_S(t)}^2\,dt
= \int_0^1 \abs{\sum_{k \notin S} c_k e^{i 2\pi k t}}^2 dt
= \sum_{k \notin S} \sum_{m \notin S} c_k \overline{c_m} \int_0^1 e^{i 2\pi (k - m) t}\,dt
= \sum_{k \notin S} \abs{c_k}^2.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">Parseval's theorem</NoteLink> applied to the error. The squared error is the
sum of $\abs{c_k}^2$ over the dropped terms, whichever terms are kept. Among all sets $S$ with $K$ frequencies, the
error is therefore smallest when $S$ holds the $K$ coefficients of largest magnitude. Truncating by magnitude is the
best $K$-term approximation in mean square; truncating at the lowest frequencies $\abs{k} \le K/2$ is not, unless the
largest coefficients happen to be the slowest.

The energy of the curve about its centroid is $E = \sum_{k \ne 0} \abs{c_k}^2$, the mean squared distance of the
curve from $c_0$. The share of energy kept by $S$ is $\sum_{k \in S, k \ne 0} \abs{c_k}^2 / E$. The root mean square
error, as a fraction of the root mean square radius $\sqrt{E}$, is the square root of the share dropped. Keeping 99% of
the energy leaves an RMS error of 10% of the RMS radius; keeping 99.99% leaves 1%.

<Derivation title="The coefficients are the best weights for a fixed set of frequencies">
For a fixed set $S$ and any weights $a_k$, write $a_k = c_k + d_k$. The error of $\sum_{k \in S} a_k e^{i 2\pi k t}$ is
$\sum_{k \in S} (-d_k) e^{i 2\pi k t} + \sum_{k \notin S} c_k e^{i 2\pi k t}$, a sum of distinct exponentials, so by
the same orthonormality argument its mean squared size is

$$
\sum_{k \in S} \abs{d_k}^2 + \sum_{k \notin S} \abs{c_k}^2.
$$

The first sum is zero only when every $d_k = 0$. The Fourier coefficients are the least-squares weights for any set of
exponentials, so a truncated series never benefits from re-fitting its kept coefficients.
</Derivation>

## Corners, smooth curves and convergence

How fast $\abs{c_k}$ decays with $\abs{k}$ depends on how smooth $z(t)$ is. A curve with a corner is continuous but its
tangent jumps, and its coefficients decay like $1/k^2$. A curve whose derivatives of every order are continuous has
coefficients that decay faster than any power of $1/k$, and a curve that is analytic in $t$ has coefficients that
decay geometrically. A smooth shape such as a heart is drawn well by a dozen arrows. A letter with sharp corners needs
hundreds.

At a corner the partial sums round the point off, and small ripples run along the straight edges next to it. The
ripples are the <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Gibbs phenomenon</NoteLink> of the derivative: the velocity $z'(t)$ jumps
at the corner, so the partial sums of $z'$ overshoot there, and the curve wobbles. The curve itself converges
uniformly, because $\sum_k \abs{c_k}$ is finite when $\abs{c_k}$ decays like $1/k^2$.

## Parameterising by arc length

The same shape can be traced at different speeds, and each speed gives a different function $z(t)$ and different
coefficients. A pointer moving across a screen is sampled at equal times, so its samples bunch where the hand slowed
down. A font outline flattened from <NoteLink to="bezier-curves">Bézier curves</NoteLink> has many vertices on curves
and two on each straight edge. Using such points directly as $z_n$ makes the coefficients describe the drawing speed as
well as the shape, and changes in speed add high-frequency content.

Resampling the curve at equal arc length before the DFT fixes the speed: $\abs{z'(t)} = L$ for a curve of length $L$.
The coefficients then depend only on the shape, its starting point and its direction. Starting at $t_0$ instead of 0
multiplies $c_k$ by $e^{i 2\pi k t_0}$ and leaves every $\abs{c_k}$ unchanged; reversing the direction swaps $c_k$ and
$c_{-k}$; rotating by $\theta$ and scaling by $s$ multiplies every $c_k$ by $s e^{i\theta}$; translating changes $c_0$
alone. Coefficients normalised for these effects are shape features, called Fourier descriptors, and have been used
to recognise hand-printed characters <Cite id="granlund1972" />.

## Several contours in one path

A Fourier series traces one closed path. A letter such as B has three contours: the outline and two holes. A
reader's drawing may also lift the pen. The contours are joined into one path by bridges: from the nearest point of the
path built so far, a straight segment runs to the nearest point of the next contour, the path goes once around that
contour, and returns along the same segment. Each bridge is traversed twice in opposite directions, so it encloses no
area, and with enough terms it is drawn as a thin hairline. With few terms the bridges are blurred along with
everything else. The alternative is one series per contour, each with its own chain of arrows, drawn at the same time.

## Assumptions

- The curve is closed. An open stroke is closed by a straight segment from its end back to its start, or traced out
  and back, which adds corners at the joins.
- The samples are dense enough: frequencies with $\abs{k} \ge N/2$ alias onto lower ones.
- The figure centres every shape, a drawing included, on its centroid and scales it so that its largest radius is 1,
  so $c_0 = 0$ and the chain starts at the origin.

## Pitfalls

- **Bins are not frequencies.** DFT bin $m$ with $m > N/2$ is frequency $m - N$. Drawing bin $m$ as $m$ turns per
  cycle gives the right curve at the sample times $n/N$ and wild loops between them.
- **Truncating by frequency and by magnitude differ.** Keeping $\abs{k} \le K$ is a low-pass filter; keeping the $K$
  largest $\abs{c_k}$ is the best $K$-term fit. For a curve with corners the two differ.
- **Equal-time samples carry speed.** Without arc-length resampling, the same shape drawn quickly and slowly gives
  different coefficients.
- **Bridges show.** Joining contours adds doubled segments, which appear in the drawing. Choosing short bridges keeps
  them unobtrusive.
