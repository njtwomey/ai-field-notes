<Definition>
**Information theory** quantifies uncertainty, and the information that observing one quantity gives about another,
for random variables with known distributions. Its basic measure is entropy, the expected surprisal
$-\log p(x)$ of an outcome. Shannon introduced it to find the limits of compressing data and of communicating reliably
over a noisy channel, and both limits turn out to be expressed by entropy and mutual information
<Cite id="cover2006" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Information theory answers two engineering questions exactly: how few bits are needed to store data from a source, and
how many bits per use can be sent through a noisy channel without error. The answers are the entropy of the source
and the capacity of the channel. Between them lies rate-distortion theory, which states how far lossy compression can
go for a given tolerated error.

Machine learning uses the same quantities as losses and as tools of analysis <Cite id="mackay2003" />.

- The cross-entropy loss of a classifier or <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> is the average code
  length of the data under the model, and perplexity is its exponential; see
  <NoteLink to="language-model-evaluation" />.
- Maximum likelihood fitting minimises the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the data distribution to the model.
- <NoteLink to="variational-inference" /> and the <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder" /> minimise a KL divergence
  between an approximate and a true posterior.
- The original <NoteLink to="generative-adversarial-network" /> minimises a Jensen–Shannon divergence.
- A <NoteLink to="decision-tree" /> chooses splits by information gain, a mutual information.
- <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning" /> objectives are bounds on mutual information between views of the same input.
- <NoteLink to="minimum-description-length" /> selects models by the length of a code for data and model together.

## Main ideas

**Entropy measures uncertainty.** The <NoteLink to="entropy" /> $\entropy(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)$ of a discrete
variable is zero for a certain outcome and at most $\log K$ over $K$ outcomes. For pairs of variables,
<NoteLink to="joint-and-conditional-entropy" /> are linked by the chain rule
$\entropy(X, Y) = \entropy(X) + \entropy(Y \mid X)$, and conditioning never increases entropy on average.

**Divergences compare distributions.** The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence" /> $\KL(p \,\|\, q)$ is
non-negative, zero only when $q = p$, and asymmetric. Choosing the forward or reverse direction changes what an
approximation gets right. <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity" /> decomposes cross-entropy into entropy plus a KL
divergence, which is why minimising cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood.
<NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon" /> places KL in a family with total variation, Hellinger and $\chi^2$,
and introduces the bounded, symmetric Jensen–Shannon divergence.

**Mutual information measures dependence.** <NoteLink to="mutual-information" />
$I(X; Y) = \entropy(X) - \entropy(X \mid Y)$ is the reduction in uncertainty about one variable from observing the
other. It is symmetric, equals the KL divergence from the joint distribution to the product of the marginals, and is
zero exactly under independence. The data-processing inequality states that no processing of $Y$ can increase its
information about $X$.

**Continuous variables need care.** <NoteLink to="differential-entropy" /> extends entropy to densities, but it can be
negative and changes under rescaling. KL divergence and mutual information remain invariant under invertible changes of
variable, so they carry over to the continuous case unchanged.

**Entropy and capacity are operational limits.** The <NoteLink to="source-coding-theorem" /> shows that no lossless
code averages fewer than $\entropy(X)$ bits per symbol, and that coding long blocks approaches that bound.
<NoteLink to="channel-capacity" /> $C = \max_{p(x)} I(X; Y)$ is the highest rate at which a noisy channel transmits
with vanishing error. <NoteLink to="rate-distortion-theory" /> gives the minimum rate $R(D)$ for reconstruction within
an expected distortion $D$.
