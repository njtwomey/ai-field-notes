<Definition>
Given a discrete memoryless channel with transition matrix $W(y \mid x) = p(Y = y \mid X = x)$, the **channel capacity** $C$ is the maximum <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between the channel input $X$ and channel output $Y$ over all possible input distributions $p(x)$:

$$
C = \max_{p(x)} I(X; Y) = \max_{p(x)} \sum_{x \in \Xcal} \sum_{y \in \Ycal} p(x) W(y \mid x) \log \frac{W(y \mid x)}{\sum_{x'} p(x') W(y \mid x')}.
$$

The **Shannon noisy channel coding theorem** states that for any transmission rate $R < C$, there exist channel codes with blocklength $n$ whose probability of decoding error decays exponentially to zero as $n \to \infty$ <Cite id="shannon1948" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A communication channel introduces noise that corrupts transmitted symbols. If we transmit raw symbols across a binary symmetric channel with bit flip probability $e = 0.1$, one in ten bits arrives corrupted. Repetition codes (e.g. sending every bit 3 or 5 times and taking a majority vote) reduce the error rate, but the transmission rate shrinks to $1/3$ or $1/5$ bits per channel use, tending towards zero as we demand zero error.

Shannon's landmark insight in 1948 was that we do not need the rate to vanish to achieve arbitrarily reliable communication. Instead, every channel has a strictly positive capacity $C$. As long as our code transmits at a rate $R < C$, long block codes can protect the message almost perfectly against the channel noise.

## Canonical Discrete Channels

### Binary Symmetric Channel (BSC)

The binary symmetric channel flips binary inputs $x \in \{0, 1\}$ with crossover probability $e \in [0, 1]$:

$$
W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - e & e \\ e & 1 - e \end{bmatrix}.
$$

By symmetry, the optimal input distribution is uniform $p^*(0) = p^*(1) = 1/2$. The capacity is:

$$
C_{\mathrm{BSC}} = 1 - H_2(e) \quad \text{bits},
$$

where $H_2(e) = -e \log_2 e - (1 - e) \log_2 (1 - e)$ is the <NoteLink to="entropy">binary entropy function</NoteLink>. When $e = 0$ (clean channel), $C = 1$ bit. When $e = 1/2$ (pure noise), $H_2(1/2) = 1$, so $C = 0$.

### Binary Erasure Channel (BEC)

In the binary erasure channel, a transmitted bit is either received correctly with probability $1 - e$, or erased into an erasure token '?' with probability $e$:

$$
C_{\mathrm{BEC}} = 1 - e \quad \text{bits}.
$$

Because the receiver knows exactly which bits were lost, the capacity is linear in the retention rate $1 - e$.

### The Z Channel

The Z channel transmits 0 without distortion, but corrupts input 1 into 0 with probability $e$:

$$
W = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ e & 1 - e \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The asymmetry breaks the uniform prior. The capacity has the exact closed form:

$$
C_{\mathrm{Z}} = \log_2\left(1 + (1 - e) e^{e / (1 - e)}\right) \quad \text{bits}.
$$

## The Blahut–Arimoto Algorithm

When the transition matrix $W$ is arbitrary and lacks special symmetries, finding $p^*(x) = \argmax_p I(X; Y)$ is a constrained concave maximization problem over the probability simplex.

The **Blahut–Arimoto algorithm** alternates between computing the reverse channel transitions $Q(x \mid y)$ and updating the input distribution $p(x)$ <Cite id="cover2006" />:

$$
p^{(k+1)}(x) \propto p^{(k)}(x) \exp\left( D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(W(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, q^{(k)}\right) \right),
$$

where $q(y) = \sum_x p(x) W(y \mid x)$ is the induced marginal output distribution. At each iteration $k$, the algorithm evaluates:

1. A **lower bound**: $\sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{p^{(k)}(x)} \le C$,
2. An **upper bound**: $\max_x D_{\mathrm{KL}}(W(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, q(y)) \ge C$.

The upper and lower bounds sandwich $C$ from above and below, monotonically converging to the global capacity and optimal prior $p^*(x)$.
