<Definition>
**System modelling** in <NoteLink to="control-theory">control theory</NoteLink> is the step that turns a physical or
engineered system, the *plant*, into equations relating its inputs to its outputs. The standard forms are the linear
state-space model, a first-order differential equation in a state vector, and the
<NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams">transfer function</NoteLink>, a ratio of polynomials in the Laplace
variable $s$. Nonlinear plants are brought into these forms by linearising about an operating point
<Cite id="astrom2021" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every design method in control starts from a model. Stability tests, <NoteLink to="pole-placement">pole
placement</NoteLink>, the <Gloss name="lqr">linear-quadratic regulator</Gloss> and frequency-domain design each take a
model in one of the two standard forms as input. The model decides what the controller can know about the plant and how
well a design on paper carries over to the hardware.

The same forms appear in machine learning. A <NoteLink to="linear-gaussian-state-space-models">linear-Gaussian
state-space model</NoteLink> is the generative model behind the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter" />.
<NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models" /> in sequence modelling start from the continuous-time model
$\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec$ and discretise it for sampled inputs, as a digital controller does.
<NoteLink to="model-based-reinforcement-learning" /> learns a model of the dynamics from data instead of deriving it.

## Main ideas

**The state summarises the past.** A state-space model carries an input $\uvec(t) \in \reals^m$ to an output
$\yvec(t) \in \reals^p$ through a state $\xvec(t) \in \reals^n$. The state at one time and the input from then on
determine all later outputs. Any linear differential equation of order $n$ becomes a first-order system in $n$ state
variables. <NoteLink to="state-space-representation" /> derives the solution through the matrix exponential
$e^{\Amat t}$ and the exact discrete-time model obtained by holding the input constant between samples.

**Eigenvalues are modes.** The eigenvalues of the dynamics matrix $\Amat$ set the natural motions of the system: decay,
growth or oscillation. A linear system is stable when every eigenvalue has negative real part in continuous time. This
link between linear algebra and behaviour runs through the whole of control theory.

**Nonlinear models are linear near an equilibrium.** Most plants obey a nonlinear equation
$\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(\xvec, \uvec)$. Near an equilibrium the deviations obey a linear model whose matrices are the
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobians</NoteLink> of $\fvec$. <NoteLink to="jacobian-linearisation" /> derives this model,
states when its eigenvalues decide local stability, and works through the pendulum.

**Transfer functions turn dynamics into algebra.** The Laplace transform maps differentiation to multiplication by
$s$, so a <Gloss name="lti">linear time-invariant system</Gloss> becomes a matrix of rational functions $\Gmat(s)$.
<NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams" /> converts a state-space model to its transfer function, reduces
interconnected blocks to one transfer function, and introduces the sensitivity $S = 1/(1 + L)$ and complementary
sensitivity $T = L/(1 + L)$ of a feedback loop with loop transfer function $L$.
