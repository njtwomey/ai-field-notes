<Definition>
**Classical control** designs a feedback controller for a plant with one input and one output, working from the plant's
<NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams">transfer function</NoteLink> $G(s)$ and its frequency response
$G(i\omega)$. The designer chooses a controller $C(s)$ so that the loop transfer function $L = CG$ has the right shape:
high gain at low frequency for accuracy, a crossover frequency that sets the speed, and enough distance from $-1$ for
robustness <Cite id="franklin2019" />. The methods date from the 1930s to the 1950s and remain the most used in industry
<Cite id="astrom2021" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A feedback loop must track a reference, reject disturbances and stay stable when the plant differs from its model.
Classical control turns these requirements into conditions on the loop gain $\abs{L(i\omega)}$ and phase
$\angle L(i\omega)$ at different frequencies. The resulting controllers are low-order, easy to tune by experiment and
easy to reason about. Temperature loops, motor drives, flight-control inner loops and process plants are typically
controlled this way.

In machine learning, a PID loop is the standard baseline against which learned controllers, such as policies from
<NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning" />, are compared on physical systems. The frequency-domain view also explains
the behaviour of simple iterative updates: an integrator removes steady-state error, and adding phase lag to a loop,
such as a delay, erodes its stability margin.

## Main ideas

**Three terms cover most loops.** A <NoteLink to="proportional-integral-derivative-control">PID controller</NoteLink>
sets the input from the present error, its integral and its derivative. The integral term removes steady-state error
for a constant reference, and the derivative term adds damping. The note covers steady-state error through the final
value theorem, integrator windup and the practical forms used in real controllers.

**Logarithmic plots turn products into sums.** A loop transfer function is a product of simple factors. On a
<NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins">Bode plot</NoteLink> the log-magnitude and phase of a product are sums,
so the plot is drawn factor by factor from straight-line asymptotes.

**Margins measure robustness.** The gain margin is the factor by which the loop gain can rise before the loop becomes
unstable. The phase margin is the extra phase lag the loop can absorb at the crossover frequency. Both are read from
the Bode plot and both measure the distance of the Nyquist plot from $-1$, as derived in
<NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins" />. A phase margin also predicts the damping and overshoot of the
closed-loop step response.

**Compensators reshape the loop locally.** A single gain cannot always meet both an accuracy and a stability target.
<NoteLink to="lead-lag-compensation" /> adds a zero and a pole: a lead network adds phase near crossover to raise the
phase margin, and a lag network raises low-frequency gain to cut steady-state error.

**Gain and phase are linked.** For a minimum-phase loop, Bode's gain-phase relation ties the slope of the magnitude
curve to the phase. A loop cannot roll off steeply near crossover without losing phase margin, which limits how much
any compensator can achieve.
