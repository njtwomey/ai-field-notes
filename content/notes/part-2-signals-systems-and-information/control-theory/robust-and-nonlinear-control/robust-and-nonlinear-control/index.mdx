<Definition>
**Robust control** designs feedback that keeps a loop stable and performing well for every plant in a stated set
around the nominal model <Cite id="zhou1996" />. **Nonlinear control** designs feedback for plants whose nonlinearity
matters over the range of operation, where a linearisation about one equilibrium is not enough
<Cite id="khalil2002" />. Both answer the same weakness of linear design: the model it starts from is only
approximately true.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A controller designed on a linear model fails in three ways. The real plant differs from the model by unmodelled
dynamics, delays or parameter error. The plant leaves the neighbourhood where the linearisation holds, as a pendulum
does when swung up from hanging. The plant changes while running, as an aircraft does with speed and altitude. The four
methods in this topic each answer one of these failures. They are used in flight control, robotics, power electronics
and process plants.

The questions carry over to learned systems. The small-gain theorem bounds the effect of a perturbation by the product
of two gains. The same product of gains, taken layer by layer, bounds how far a neural network's output can move under a
small input perturbation, the quantity exploited by <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples" />. The MIT rule of adaptive
control is <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on a squared error, run online, the setting of
<NoteLink to="online-learning" />.

## Main ideas

**Worst-case gain as the design objective.** The $H_\infty$ norm of a
<NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams">transfer function</NoteLink> is its peak gain over frequency, the
largest factor by which it can amplify the energy of a signal. <NoteLink to="h-infinity-control" /> designs controllers
that minimise this norm for chosen closed-loop maps. By the small-gain theorem, a loop with closed-loop map $M$ stays
stable for every stable perturbation $\Delta$ with $\norm{\Delta}_\infty < 1/\norm{M}_\infty$. The waterbed effect
limits what any design can achieve: reducing sensitivity in one frequency band raises it in another.

**Cancel the nonlinearity through the input.** When the model's nonlinearity is known, the input can be chosen to
cancel it. <NoteLink to="feedback-linearisation" /> turns the output dynamics into a chain of integrators, which a
linear controller then stabilises over the whole operating range. The method needs an accurate model and fails where
the cancelling input becomes unbounded.

**Force the state onto a surface.** <NoteLink to="sliding-mode-control" /> chooses a surface on which the tracking
error decays, then switches the input so that the state reaches the surface in finite time and stays there. On the
surface the loop ignores any bounded disturbance that enters through the input channel. The switching causes
chattering, which a boundary layer trades against precision.

**Estimate what is unknown while running.** <NoteLink to="adaptive-control" /> adjusts controller parameters online so
that the plant tracks a reference model. Gradient adaptation by the MIT rule can go unstable; choosing the update law
from a Lyapunov function guarantees stability.

**Lyapunov functions tie the nonlinear methods together.** Sliding-mode and adaptive designs both choose the control
or the update law so that a Lyapunov function decreases. The proofs rely on the direct method of
<NoteLink to="lyapunov-stability" />.
