<Definition>
The **$H_\infty$ norm** of a stable transfer matrix $G(s)$ is its peak gain over frequency,

$$
\norm{G}_\infty = \sup_{\omega} \bar{\sigma}\paren{G(i\omega)},
$$

where $\bar{\sigma}$ is the largest singular value; for a scalar $G$ it is $\max_\omega\abs{G(i\omega)}$. **$H_\infty$
control** chooses a stabilising controller $K$ that minimises, or bounds by a given $\gamma$, the $H_\infty$ norm of a
<NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams">closed-loop transfer function</NoteLink> from disturbances to errors
<Cite id="zames1981" />.
</Definition>

## The norm is a worst-case energy gain

For a stable scalar system $Y(i\omega) = G(i\omega)U(i\omega)$ and an input of finite energy,
<NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">Parseval's theorem</NoteLink> gives

$$
\norm{y}_2^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\abs{G(i\omega)}^2\abs{U(i\omega)}^2\,d\omega
\le \norm{G}_\infty^2\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\abs{U(i\omega)}^2\,d\omega = \norm{G}_\infty^2\norm{u}_2^2.
$$

Inputs concentrated near the peak frequency approach equality, so $\norm{G}_\infty$ is exactly the largest ratio of
output energy to input energy. The matrix case uses the same argument with $\bar{\sigma}$ bounding $\norm{G\uvec}$.
Minimising $\norm{G}_\infty$ guards against the worst disturbance, not the average one. That is the contrast with
<NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-gaussian-control">linear-quadratic-Gaussian (LQG) control</NoteLink>, which minimises an
average over Gaussian noise and, as Doyle showed, can be fragile.

## The small-gain theorem

Connect two stable systems in a loop: $M$ maps $w$ to $z$, and $\Delta$ maps $z$ back to $w$. The loop gain is
$M\Delta$. If

$$
\norm{M}_\infty\,\norm{\Delta}_\infty < 1,
$$

the closed loop is stable. For <Gloss name="lti">linear time-invariant systems</Gloss> the reason is the
<NoteLink to="nyquist-stability-criterion">Nyquist criterion</NoteLink>: $\abs{M(i\omega)\Delta(i\omega)} < 1$ at every
frequency, so the Nyquist curve of the loop stays inside the unit disc, never reaches $-1$ and cannot encircle it.
Both systems are stable, so no encirclement is needed. Zames proved the theorem for general, including nonlinear and
time-varying, operators with finite gains <Cite id="zames1966" />. With $\gamma_M$ and $\gamma_\Delta$ the gains and $r$
an external input added at $z$, the loop signal satisfies $\norm{z} \le \norm{r} + \gamma_M\gamma_\Delta\norm{z}$, so
$\norm{z} \le \norm{r}/(1 - \gamma_M\gamma_\Delta)$ is bounded whenever $\gamma_M\gamma_\Delta < 1$.

The theorem is conservative: it ignores phase. A loop with gain above 1 can be stable. Its value is that it holds for
every $\Delta$ in a set, which is what robustness to model error requires.

## Robust stability

A model $P$ is never exact. A common description of the error is **multiplicative uncertainty**,

$$
P_{\text{true}} = P\,(1 + W\Delta), \qquad \norm{\Delta}_\infty \le 1,
$$

where the weight $W(s)$ gives the relative model error at each frequency: small at low frequency, where the model is
good, and larger than 1 at high frequency, where unmodelled dynamics dominate. With controller $C$, the perturbation
$\Delta$ sees the rest of the loop as the transfer function from its output back to its input. Tracing the loop, that
is $-WT$, where $T = PC/(1 + PC)$ is the complementary sensitivity. By the small-gain theorem the loop is stable for
every admissible $\Delta$ if

$$
\norm{WT}_\infty < 1,
$$

and the condition is also necessary when $\Delta$ ranges over all stable transfer functions of norm at most 1.

Robust stability limits $\abs{T}$ wherever the model is uncertain. The **sensitivity** $S = 1/(1 + PC)$ is the
transfer function from an output disturbance to the output, and $S + T = 1$. Performance asks for $\abs{S}$ to be
small where disturbances act. The **mixed-sensitivity** problem combines both:

$$
\min_{C \text{ stabilising}} \norm{\begin{bmatrix} W_1 S \\ W_2 T \end{bmatrix}}_\infty.
$$

State-space formulas solve the standard $H_\infty$ problem with two <NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator">Riccati
equations</NoteLink>, one for control and one for estimation, much like LQG, together with a coupling condition
<Cite id="doyle1989" />. The minimum achievable $\gamma$ is found by bisection.

## Worked example

Take $P(s) = 1/(s + 1)$ and $C = K$. Then $T(s) = K/(s + 1 + K)$, whose magnitude
$K/\sqrt{\omega^2 + (1 + K)^2}$ is largest at $\omega = 0$: $\norm{T}_\infty = K/(1 + K)$. With a constant uncertainty
weight $W = w$, robust stability holds when $wK/(1 + K) < 1$. For $K = 4$, $\norm{T}_\infty = 0.8$ and the loop
tolerates any stable multiplicative perturbation of relative size up to $w = 1.25$ at every frequency.

A pure gain error shows the conservatism. A real gain factor $(1 + \delta)$ on the plant gives the closed-loop pole
$-(1 + K(1 + \delta))$, stable for every $\delta > -1 - 1/K$, including gain errors of 1000 %. The small-gain bound
$\abs{\delta} < 1.25$ is much smaller, because it must also cover perturbations with arbitrary phase, such as
unmodelled lags, for which it is tight.

## Limits: the waterbed effect

$H_\infty$ design cannot make $\abs{S}$ small everywhere. For a stable open loop with at least two more poles than
zeros, Bode's **sensitivity integral** says

$$
\int_0^\infty \ln\abs{S(i\omega)}\,d\omega = 0
$$

<Cite id="bode1945" />. Pushing $\abs{S}$ below 1 over one band forces it above 1 somewhere else, like pressing on a
waterbed. With unstable open-loop poles $p_i$, the right side becomes $\pi\sum_i\operatorname{Re}p_i$, which is
positive, so instability makes the trade-off worse. $H_\infty$ design decides where the unavoidable peak goes.

## Assumptions

- The uncertainty is described by norm-bounded, stable perturbations with known frequency weights. The quality of the
  design depends on how well the weights reflect the real model error.
- The unstructured small-gain test treats $\Delta$ as a full matrix. When the uncertainty has structure, such as
  several independent scalar perturbations, the structured singular value $\mu$ gives a less conservative test.

## Pitfalls

- **Over-conservatism.** Covering a real parameter error with a complex, dynamic $\Delta$ can make the design needlessly
  cautious.
- **Controller order.** The $H_\infty$ controller has the order of the plant plus all weights, often high. Model
  reduction afterwards needs a robustness check.
- **Weight tuning is the design.** The optimiser meets the weighted objective exactly as specified. Bad weights give a
  controller that is optimal for the wrong problem.
