<Definition>
**Stability analysis** decides whether the trajectories of a dynamical system stay near an equilibrium and whether they
return to it. For a linear model the question reduces to where the poles lie: in the open left half-plane in
continuous time. For a nonlinear model it is answered with a function of the state that never increases along
trajectories <Cite id="khalil2002" />. In feedback design the system analysed is the closed loop, and the analysis is
done from a model of the open loop <Cite id="astrom2021" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Feedback can stabilise an unstable plant, and it can destabilise a stable one. A loop gain set too high turns a
well-behaved servo into an oscillator. Stability is therefore the first requirement a controller must meet, before any
measure of performance. The tests in this topic check a design, and several of them also show how far the design is
from instability, which is what makes them design tools.

The same questions arise in machine learning. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on a quadratic
loss is a linear iteration, and its step size is stable exactly when the iteration's eigenvalues lie inside the unit
circle. The energy of a <NoteLink to="hopfield-network" /> is a Lyapunov function for its update dynamics. Exploding
activations in a <NoteLink to="recurrent-neural-network" /> are the instability of a linear recurrence whose matrix has
an eigenvalue outside the unit circle.

## Main ideas

**Stability has several strengths.** An equilibrium is stable if nearby trajectories stay nearby, asymptotically stable
if they also converge, and exponentially stable if they converge at an exponential rate. For a linear system
$\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec$ the three coincide, and hold exactly when every eigenvalue of $\Amat$ has negative real
part. <NoteLink to="lyapunov-stability" /> gives the definitions and explains why they differ for nonlinear systems.

**An energy-like function proves stability without solving the equation.** Lyapunov's direct method looks for a
positive definite function $V(\xvec)$ that never increases along trajectories. Finding one proves stability.
LaSalle's invariance principle extends the conclusion to convergence when $V$ only stops decreasing on a set the
system cannot stay in. For linear systems the search becomes a matrix equation,
$\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat = -\Qmat$ with $\Pmat \succ 0$, developed in <NoteLink to="lyapunov-stability" />.

**Pole locations can be tested without computing the poles.** A closed loop is stable when every root of its
characteristic polynomial has negative real part. The <NoteLink to="routh-hurwitz-criterion" /> counts the roots in the
right half-plane from sign changes in a table built from the coefficients. Its main use is finding the range of a
design parameter, such as a gain, for which the loop is stable.

**Pole paths show the effect of a gain.** The <NoteLink to="root-locus" /> traces the closed-loop poles, the roots of
$1 + KG(s) = 0$, as the gain $K$ grows from 0. A point lies on the locus exactly when the angle of $G(s)$ is an odd
multiple of $180°$, so a sketch follows from simple rules.

**The open-loop frequency response decides closed-loop stability.** The
<NoteLink to="nyquist-stability-criterion" /> counts how often the plot of $L(i\omega)$ encircles $-1$. It needs only
the measured frequency response, handles time delays, and shows the distance to instability. That distance becomes
the gain and phase margins of <NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins" />.

## How the notes fit together

The notes divide into a state-space approach and a transfer-function approach.

- <NoteLink to="lyapunov-stability" /> is the state-space approach. It applies to nonlinear systems and underlies the
  nonlinear designs in <NoteLink to="robust-and-nonlinear-control" />, such as sliding-mode and adaptive control.
- <NoteLink to="routh-hurwitz-criterion" />, <NoteLink to="root-locus" /> and
  <NoteLink to="nyquist-stability-criterion" /> are the transfer-function approach, for linear loops. They can be read
  in this order. Each needs only <NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams" />.

The Nyquist criterion leads directly into <NoteLink to="classical-control" />, where its distance to $-1$ becomes the
basis of loop shaping. Lyapunov's equation reappears in <NoteLink to="optimal-control" />, where the cost-to-go of the
linear-quadratic regulator is a Lyapunov function, and in the stability proofs of
<NoteLink to="model-predictive-control-stability-and-feasibility">model predictive control</NoteLink>.
