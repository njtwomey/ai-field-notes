<Definition>
**Control theory** studies how to choose the inputs of a dynamical system so that its outputs behave as required:
following a reference, rejecting disturbances and staying stable. Its central tool is feedback, which computes the
input from measurements of the output. The theory supplies models of the system, tests of stability and methods for
designing the feedback law <Cite id="astrom2021" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Feedback makes a system insensitive to what is not modelled. A thermostat holds a room's temperature whatever the
weather; a cruise controller holds a car's speed on any slope. Feedback can also make an unstable system stable, as in
balancing an inverted pendulum or flying an unstable aircraft. The price is that a badly designed loop can oscillate
or diverge, so every design must be checked for stability and robustness <Cite id="franklin2019" />.

Control theory and machine learning meet in several places.

- <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning" /> solves the optimal control problem without a known model. Its value
  functions and <NoteLink to="bellman-equations" /> are the discrete counterparts of dynamic programming in control.
- The <NoteLink to="kalman-filter" /> is the observer of optimal control, applied to time series.
- <NoteLink to="structured-state-space-models" /> build sequence models from the continuous-time linear state-space
  model.
- Pontryagin's costate equation is the continuous-time form of <NoteLink to="backpropagation" />.
- The benchmark tasks of reinforcement learning, from the cart-pole to legged robots, are control problems; see
  <NoteLink to="classic-control-environments" />.

## Main ideas

**Models first.** Design starts from a model, either a state-space model $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec$ or a
transfer function $G(s)$. Nonlinear plants are linearised about an operating point.
<NoteLink to="system-modelling" /> develops both forms and the conversion between them. When a model cannot be derived
from physics, system identification fits one to data, by least squares on
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-exogenous-models-and-prediction-error-methods">ARX models</NoteLink> or by
<NoteLink to="subspace-identification" />.

**Stability is the first requirement.** A linear system is stable when its poles lie in the left half-plane.
<NoteLink to="stability-analysis" /> tests this without computing the poles, and for nonlinear systems through
Lyapunov functions that never increase along trajectories.

**Two design traditions.** Classical control shapes the frequency response of a single loop: a PID controller, Bode
plots, gain and phase margins and lead-lag compensators, in <NoteLink to="classical-control" />. State-space control
places the closed-loop poles directly by state feedback and reconstructs unmeasured states with an observer, in
<NoteLink to="state-space-control" />. The first is intuitive and suits one loop at a time; the second handles many
inputs and outputs at once.

**Optimality and constraints.** <NoteLink to="optimal-control" /> chooses the controller that minimises a cost, through
dynamic programming or Pontryagin's principle; for linear dynamics and quadratic cost the answer is the
<NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator" />. <NoteLink to="model-predictive-control" /> re-solves a finite-horizon
optimal control problem at every sample, which lets it respect limits on inputs and states.

**Robustness to a wrong model.** Every model is approximate. Gain and phase margins measure tolerance to error in one
loop. <NoteLink to="robust-and-nonlinear-control" /> designs for worst-case model error with $H_\infty$ methods, and
handles strong nonlinearity and changing plants with feedback linearisation, sliding-mode and adaptive control.
