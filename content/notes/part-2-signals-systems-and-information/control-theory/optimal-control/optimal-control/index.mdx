<Definition>
**Optimal control** chooses the input $\uvec(t)$ to a dynamical system $\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(\xvec, \uvec)$ so as to
minimise a cost $\phi(\xvec(T)) + \int_0^T \ell(\xvec, \uvec)\,dt$, made of a running cost $\ell$ and a
terminal cost $\phi$
<Cite id="kirk2004" />. Two theories characterise the solution: dynamic programming, which describes the optimal cost
from every state, and Pontryagin's principle, which describes one optimal trajectory. For linear dynamics and quadratic
costs both give a linear feedback law in closed form <Cite id="bertsekas2017" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

<NoteLink to="pole-placement">Pole placement</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="classical-control">loop shaping</NoteLink>
produce a stable loop but leave its exact behaviour to the designer's judgement. Optimal control replaces that judgement
by a cost that weighs tracking error against input effort. The answer is a controller that is best for that cost, and in
the linear-quadratic case it also comes with robustness guarantees. Trajectory planning for rockets and robots, process
control and the controllers inside <NoteLink to="model-predictive-control" /> are solved this way.

Optimal control and <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning" /> study the same problem. Reinforcement learning drops the
known model and learns from interaction; the discrete-time <NoteLink to="bellman-equations" /> it rests on are the
counterpart of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. Pontryagin's costate, discretised, is the gradient computed by
<NoteLink to="backpropagation" />, and the adjoint method that trains
<NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations" /> is the same backward equation.

## Main ideas

**Dynamic programming works backwards from the end.** Bellman's principle of optimality says the tail of an optimal
trajectory is optimal for the problem that starts where the tail starts. Applied over an infinitesimal step, it gives
the <NoteLink to="hamilton-jacobi-bellman-equation" />, a <Gloss name="pde">partial differential equation</Gloss> for
the <Gloss name="value-function">value function</Gloss> $V(\xvec, t)$, the optimal cost-to-go. The minimiser in the
equation is the optimal feedback law. The note also proves the verification theorem and treats value functions that are
not smooth.

**Necessary conditions along one trajectory.** <NoteLink to="pontryagin-maximum-principle" /> introduces the Hamiltonian
$H = \ell + \lambdavec^\top\fvec$ and a costate $\lambdavec(t)$ that runs backwards from the terminal cost's gradient.
An optimal input minimises $H$ at each instant. The principle handles bounded inputs, where the optimal control is often
bang-bang, and it contains the Euler–Lagrange equation of the <NoteLink to="calculus-of-variations">calculus of
variations</NoteLink> as a special case.

**Linear dynamics and quadratic costs give linear feedback.** For $\xvec_{k+1} = \Amat\xvec_k + \Bmat\uvec_k$ and a cost
$\sum_k \xvec_k^\top\Qmat\xvec_k + \uvec_k^\top\Rmat\uvec_k$, the cost-to-go is a quadratic form $\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec$
and the optimal input is $\uvec = -\Kmat\xvec$. The <NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator" /> derives $\Pmat$ from a
backward Riccati recursion and shows that continuous-time LQR with one input has a guaranteed
<NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins">phase margin</NoteLink> of at least $60°$.

**With noise, estimate then control.** When the state is measured through Gaussian noise,
<NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-gaussian-control" /> applies the LQR gain to the Kalman-filter estimate. This
certainty-equivalent controller is optimal for the linear-Gaussian problem. Its loop has no guaranteed margins, which
the note demonstrates and shows how to recover.

## How the notes fit together

The four notes form two pairs. The general theory is the <NoteLink to="hamilton-jacobi-bellman-equation" /> and
<NoteLink to="pontryagin-maximum-principle" />, which can be read in either order. The linear-quadratic theory is the
<NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator" /> followed by <NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-gaussian-control" />. A reader
interested in practical design can start with the LQR, which derives its result by dynamic programming without needing
the continuous-time theory.

The neighbouring topics extend these results. <NoteLink to="model-predictive-control" /> solves a finite-horizon optimal
control problem online at every sample, so it can respect constraints. <NoteLink to="h-infinity-control" /> replaces the
quadratic cost by a worst-case gain. <NoteLink to="state-space-control" /> supplies the
<NoteLink to="controllability-and-observability">controllability and observability</NoteLink> conditions under which the
LQR and the Kalman filter exist.

The notes assume <NoteLink to="state-space-representation" />, the <NoteLink to="gradient" /> and
