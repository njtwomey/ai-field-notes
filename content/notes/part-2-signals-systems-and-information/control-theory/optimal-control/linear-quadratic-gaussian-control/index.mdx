<Definition>
The **linear-quadratic-Gaussian** (LQG) problem is to control

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = \Amat\xvec_k + \Bmat\uvec_k + \wvec_k, \qquad \yvec_k = \Cmat\xvec_k + \vvec_k,
$$

with independent Gaussian noise $\wvec_k \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Wmat)$, $\vvec_k \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Vmat)$ and a
Gaussian initial state, using only past and present measurements, so as to minimise

$$
J = \expect\brack{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\paren{\xvec_k^\top\Qmat\xvec_k + \uvec_k^\top\Rmat\uvec_k} + \xvec_N^\top\Qmat_f\xvec_N}.
$$

The optimal controller is $\uvec_k = -\Kmat_k\hat{\xvec}_{k \mid k}$: the gain of the deterministic
<NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator">LQR</NoteLink> problem applied to the estimate from the
<NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>. The two designs are independent. This is the **separation
theorem**.
</Definition>

## Two problems, solved separately

The LQG controller is two blocks in series. The Kalman filter turns measurements into a Gaussian belief
$\xvec_k \mid \yvec_{0:k} \sim \Gauss(\hat{\xvec}_{k \mid k}, \Sigmamat_{k \mid k})$ about the state. The regulator
treats the mean of that belief as if it were the true state. Acting on an estimate as if it were exact is called
**certainty equivalence**. For general nonlinear or non-Gaussian problems it is only a heuristic. For LQG it is exactly
optimal.

The filter gain depends only on $\Amat$, $\Cmat$, $\Wmat$ and $\Vmat$. The regulator gain depends only on $\Amat$,
$\Bmat$, $\Qmat$ and $\Rmat$. Neither design needs to know anything about the other. The two Riccati equations are
dual: the filter's is the regulator's with $\Amat \to \Amat^\top$, $\Bmat \to \Cmat^\top$ and time reversed.

## Why certainty equivalence is optimal here

Let $\Ical_k = (\yvec_{0:k}, \uvec_{0:k-1})$ be the information available at time $k$. Write
$\hat{\xvec} = \hat{\xvec}_{k \mid k}$ and $\Sigmamat_k = \Sigmamat_{k \mid k}$. Two facts about the linear-Gaussian
model do all the work.

- The estimation error $\xvec_k - \hat{\xvec}$ has covariance $\Sigmamat_k$, which does not depend on the measured
  values or on the inputs. Inputs are known, so their effect is subtracted exactly.
- For any fixed matrix $\Mmat$, the conditional mean of the <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic
  form</NoteLink> $\xvec_k^\top\Mmat\xvec_k$ given $\Ical_k$ is
  $\hat{\xvec}^\top\Mmat\hat{\xvec} + \trace(\Mmat\Sigmamat_k)$.

**Claim:** the optimal cost-to-go is $V_k(\Ical_k) = \expect[\xvec_k^\top\Pmat_k\xvec_k \mid \Ical_k] + c_k$, with
$\Pmat_k$ from the LQR Riccati recursion and $c_k$ a constant that does not depend on the data. It holds at $k = N$ with
$c_N = 0$. Assume it at $k + 1$ and write $\Pmat = \Pmat_{k+1}$. The Bellman equation, with the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">tower property</NoteLink> of conditional expectation, asks for

$$
\min_{\uvec}\;\expect\brack{\xvec_k^\top\Qmat\xvec_k + \uvec^\top\Rmat\uvec
+ (\Amat\xvec_k + \Bmat\uvec + \wvec_k)^\top\Pmat(\Amat\xvec_k + \Bmat\uvec + \wvec_k) \Bigm| \Ical_k} + c_{k+1}.
$$

The input $\uvec$ is a function of $\Ical_k$, so it comes out of the expectation. By the second fact the expression
equals

$$
\hat{\xvec}^\top\Qmat\hat{\xvec} + \uvec^\top\Rmat\uvec + (\Amat\hat{\xvec} + \Bmat\uvec)^\top\Pmat(\Amat\hat{\xvec} + \Bmat\uvec)
+ \trace\paren{(\Qmat + \Amat^\top\Pmat\Amat)\Sigmamat_k} + \trace(\Pmat\Wmat) + c_{k+1}.
$$

The terms that involve $\uvec$ are exactly the deterministic LQR problem with $\hat{\xvec}$ in place of $\xvec$. So the
minimiser is $\uvec = -\Kmat_k\hat{\xvec}$ with the LQR gain, and the minimum is $\hat{\xvec}^\top\Pmat_k\hat{\xvec}$
plus trace terms that do not depend on the data. Writing $\hat{\xvec}^\top\Pmat_k\hat{\xvec} =
\expect[\xvec_k^\top\Pmat_k\xvec_k \mid \Ical_k] - \trace(\Pmat_k\Sigmamat_k)$ completes the induction.

The trace terms show where the cost goes. Process noise costs $\trace(\Pmat\Wmat)$ per step even with perfect
measurements. Estimation error costs extra through $\Sigmamat_k$. The controller cannot reduce either by acting
differently, which is why its action is the deterministic one.

## No guaranteed margins

State-feedback LQR in continuous time tolerates any increase of loop gain, a reduction to one half, and $60°$ of
<NoteLink to="lead-lag-compensation">phase lag</NoteLink> at the plant input. Doyle showed that an LQG controller,
though optimal in a broader sense, does not inherit these margins. His paper's abstract reads, in full, "There are none"
<Cite id="doyle1978" />.

The reason is that the margins of LQR come from a property of the loop $\Kmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat$, which is the
loop seen at the plant input only when the true state is fed back. With a filter in the loop, the loop broken at the
plant input is the plant followed by the whole controller, a different transfer function that the LQR inequality says
nothing about.

Doyle's example uses

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \Bmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\Cmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \Qmat = q\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
\Wmat = \sigma\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with $\Rmat = \Vmat = 1$, in continuous time. Multiply the plant input by a factor $\mu$, as an actuator with an
uncertain gain would, and ask for which $\mu$ the closed loop stays stable. With $q = \sigma = 100$, the LQG loop is
stable only for $0.938 < \mu < 1.007$: a gain error of less than 1 % destabilises it. With $q = \sigma = 10^4$ the upper
limit is $1.000$ to three decimal places. The margin shrinks to zero as the weights grow, while the state-feedback LQR
for the same $\Qmat$ keeps its margin of $[1/2, \infty)$.

## Recovering robustness

**Loop transfer recovery** (LTR): for a minimum-phase plant, adding fictitious process noise $\rho\Bmat\Bmat^\top$
at the plant input and letting $\rho \to \infty$ makes the LQG loop at the plant input converge to the LQR loop,
recovering its margins at the cost of a fast, noise-sensitive filter. The deeper response to Doyle's result was to
design for robustness directly, which led to
<NoteLink to="h-infinity-control">$H_\infty$ control</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- The model is linear, the noises are Gaussian, white and independent, and all matrices are known. With non-Gaussian
  noise the Kalman filter is still the best linear estimator and the controller is the best linear controller, but a
  nonlinear one may do better.
- The cost is quadratic. With other costs, or with constraints, certainty equivalence is no longer optimal in
  general. The controller still cannot act to improve future estimates, because the error covariance does not depend on
  the inputs.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming optimality implies robustness.** LQG is optimal for the model it was designed for. It says nothing about
  a slightly different plant, and its margins must be checked.
- **Tuning noise covariances as knobs.** $\Wmat$ and $\Vmat$ are often unknown and chosen to shape the filter. That is
  legitimate, but the resulting controller is then optimal for a fictitious noise model, and robustness needs checking
  in the frequency domain.
