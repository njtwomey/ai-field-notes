<Definition>
A **coordinate network**, or implicit neural representation, is a network $f_\theta$ fitted to one signal: it takes a
coordinate and returns the signal's value there, so the weights $\theta$ store the signal <Cite id="xie2022fields" />.
Here the signal is what a camera sees in a grid world: $f_\theta(x, y, \varphi) \approx d(x, y, \varphi)$, the distance
from the point $(x, y)$ to the first wall along the ray at angle $\varphi$. One query per screen column renders a view,
as the <NoteLink to="ray-casting">ray caster</NoteLink> does with one traced ray per column.
</Definition>

## Problem

An image-fitting network maps a pixel's coordinates $(u, v)$ to its colour and is trained on every pixel of one image
<Cite id="stanley2007" /> <Cite id="sitzmann2020" />. The aim is a faithful fit, not generalisation to other images. The
same construction applies to any function of a few coordinates. The depth function $d(x, y, \varphi)$ of a world is
known exactly, because the ray caster computes it, so the training set is unlimited: poses are drawn uniformly over the
free floor and angles uniformly over $[-\pi, \pi)$, and the ray caster labels each one.

The function is hard to fit. As $\varphi$ sweeps past the edge of a column, $d$ jumps from the column's distance to the
distance of the wall behind it. These jumps lie along curves in $(x, y, \varphi)$ space, and every column and corner
adds more.

## Mechanism

**Two forms.** The *ray* form, $(x, y, \varphi) \mapsto d$, has one output and renders a view of $R$ columns with $R$
queries at angles $\varphi_i = \theta + \alpha_i$, where $\theta$ is the heading and $\alpha_i$ the column's angle from
it. The *view* form, $(x, y, \theta) \mapsto (d_1, \dots, d_R)$, has $R$ outputs and renders a view with one query. Both
fit the same function. The view form must learn the dependence on $\alpha_i$ separately in each output, while the ray
form shares it across columns.

**Encoding.** A <Gloss name="mlp" /> with smooth or piecewise-linear activations fits the low-frequency content of its
target first and its high-frequency content much later: the spectral bias of neural networks <Cite id="rahaman2019" />.
Jumps in distance are high-frequency content. The fix is to feed the network fixed
<NoteLink to="sinusoidal-positional-encoding">sinusoids</NoteLink> of its inputs at octave frequencies
<Cite id="mildenhall2020" /> <Cite id="tancik2020" />. With $u = x/w$ and $v = y/h$ the position scaled to $[0, 1]$ by
the map's width $w$ and height $h$, the encoding with $L$ octaves is

$$
\gamma(u, v, \varphi) = \big(\sin 2^k \pi u,\ \cos 2^k \pi u,\ \sin 2^k \pi v,\ \cos 2^k \pi v,\ \sin 2^k \varphi,\
\cos 2^k \varphi\big)_{k = 0}^{L - 1},
$$

$6L$ features. The angle's frequencies are integers, so every feature is periodic in $\varphi$ with period $2\pi$. A
linear function of $\gamma$ is a <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink> truncated at frequency
$2^{L-1}$, so the first layer can already express detail at the scale of $1/2^{L-1}$ of the map, about one cell for
$L = 6$ on a 16-cell map.

**Target and loss.** The network outputs $\log d$ and is trained by <Gloss name="adam" /> on the <Gloss name="mse">mean
squared error</Gloss> of $\log d$ over minibatches of 1024 rays <Cite id="kingma2015" />. The logarithm weighs a
relative error the same at every distance. That suits the rendering, since a wall slice's height is proportional to
$1/d$, so a near wall's error moves many pixels and a far wall's few.

## Classifying the wall

A network that regresses $d$ smooths every occlusion edge. Near an edge, nearby inputs have targets on both sides of
the jump, and the squared error is smallest for a prediction between them, a distance at which there is no wall.

Every wall face of a grid world lies on a grid line: a vertical line $x = c$ or a horizontal line $y = c$, with $c$ an
integer. The distance along a ray from $(x, y)$ at angle $\varphi$ to a given line follows from the ray's equation,
$(x + d\cos\varphi,\ y + d\sin\varphi)$:

$$
d = \frac{c - x}{\cos\varphi} \quad \text{for } x = c, \qquad d = \frac{c - y}{\sin\varphi} \quad \text{for } y = c.
$$

A **wall-line classifier** therefore predicts only which line the ray stops on, one class for each of the $w + 1$
vertical and $h + 1$ horizontal lines, and computes $d$ from that line. Lines at $d \le 0$ lie behind the camera, and
their scores are masked before the <Gloss name="softmax">softmax</Gloss>. The label of a training ray is the line its
hit lies on, so training is classification with the <Gloss name="cross-entropy">cross-entropy</Gloss> loss. Within one
wall the distance is exact, so walls are straight. At an occlusion edge the most probable class changes, so the
predicted distance jumps rather than passing through the values between. The remaining errors are rays assigned to the
wrong line near an edge. On the 9 × 9 maze, a network with two hidden layers of 64 units puts 99 % of held-out rays
within 10 % of the true distance, against 78 % for the same network regressing $d$ with the same training budget.

## The trained models

The figure's default mode and the table use models trained offline in PyTorch, with the code in the Code tab, and
exported to ONNX. Every exported model takes rays $(x, y, \varphi)$ and returns distances, so the page runs each the
same way with ONNX Runtime Web. Each model is fitted to one map. The training set is 200 000 rays at uniform positions
on the free floor and uniform angles, labelled by the ray caster; training runs 3000 steps of
<NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> <Cite id="kingma2015" />. The score is the share of rays within 10 % of the true
distance, over 300 held-out views of 48 rays. A wall slice's height is proportional to $1/d$, so a ray within 10 % of
its distance draws a slice within about 10 % of the right height.

**Distance regression.** The network outputs $\log d$ and is trained on the absolute error of $\log d$. The absolute
error is minimised by the median of the targets near an input rather than their mean, which blurs an occlusion edge
less than the squared error does, but a continuous network still cannot jump. The variants are the Fourier-feature MLP
of the figure's own training mode, at 64 × 2 and 256 × 4 units; SIREN, whose sine activations represent high
frequencies directly <Cite id="sitzmann2020" />; and a <NoteLink to="mixture-density-networks">mixture</NoteLink> of
four candidate distances, trained by its likelihood <Cite id="bishop1994" />. The mixture predicts the most probable
candidate, so near an edge it picks the near wall or the far wall instead of averaging them.

**Wall-line classifier.** The classifier of the previous section, at 64 × 2 and 256 × 4 units. Its distances are exact
whenever it names the right line, and its errors are near edges, where it names a neighbouring line. A larger map has
more lines and more edges, which is where it falls behind the density field.

**Density field and volume rendering.** A network learns the map itself as a density $\sigma(x, y) \ge 0$, and the
distance is rendered along the ray as in NeRF <Cite id="mildenhall2020" />. The ray is sampled at $K$ points $t_k$,
eight per unit of length, with spacing $\delta$. The probability of passing a sample is $e^{-\sigma_k \delta}$, so the
ray stops at sample $k$ with probability $T_k \alpha_k$, where $\alpha_k = 1 - e^{-\sigma_k \delta}$ and
$T_k = \prod_{j<k} (1 - \alpha_j)$ is the probability of reaching it; the distance is the expected stopping point
$\sum_k T_k \alpha_k t_k$. The density comes from feature grids at several resolutions, up to 32, 64 or 128 cells
across the map, read by bilinear interpolation and passed through a small MLP. Occlusion is the renderer's work, so
edges are sharp; the errors that remain come from the sample spacing and from the interpolation, which round corners
and blur walls by a fraction of a cell.

**Learned map with exact ray casting.** The map is one number per cell, the probability $o_c$ that cell $c$ is wall, and
there is no network. Which cells a ray enters, and the distance $t_k$ at which it enters each, depend only on the ray,
so they are computed once by the grid traversal. The ray stops in the $k$-th cell it enters with probability
$p_k = o_k \prod_{j<k} (1 - o_j)$, and the expected distance $\sum_k p_k t_k$ is differentiable in every $o_c$, so the
map is fitted by <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on the same loss as the other models. A
ray's distance is evidence that each cell before the hit is empty and the hit cell is wall, the rule of occupancy-grid
mapping from range sensors <Cite id="elfes1989" />; a grid of free values in place of a network is also the approach of
Plenoxels <Cite id="fridovichkeil2022" />. Prediction rounds the map to walls and empty cells and ray-casts it exactly,
so a correct map gives exact views. The cells align with the walls and the distances are exact cell-face distances, so
neither the smoothing of regression nor the blur of sampling arises.

**<Gloss name="rl">Reinforcement learning</Gloss>.** Two models learn from a reward instead of from gradients of the
loss. The *map by <Gloss name="reinforce" />* is the learned map trained as a policy: each step samples 32 maps, each
cell an independent <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli</NoteLink> draw with $P(\text{wall}) = o_c$,
ray-casts each exactly, which no gradient passes through, and rewards each with $R = -\operatorname{mean} \abs{\log
\hat d - \log d}$ over a batch of rays, plus a small bonus for wall cells joined side by side (straight runs and tight
corners) and a penalty for wall cells that touch only at a corner. The
<NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function estimate</NoteLink> of the
<NoteLink to="policy-gradient-theorem">policy gradient</NoteLink>,
$\nabla \expect[R] = \expect[(R - b) \nabla \log p(\text{map})]$, moves each $o_c$ towards the maps that scored above
the baseline $b$, the mean reward of the other samples <Cite id="williams1992" />. The *wall-line policy* is the
wall-line classifier trained as a <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandit</NoteLink>: it samples a line for
each ray and is rewarded by $-\abs{\log \hat d - \log d}$, without being told the correct line. On the 9 × 9 maze the
map by REINFORCE reaches 100 % in 14 seconds, as the map fitted by gradient does, while the bandit reaches 87 % against
the classifier's 99 %: a reward of one number per guess carries less information than the label.

## Cost

The default network has 36 inputs, two hidden layers of 64 units and one output: 6593 parameters, against 256 bytes for
the 16 × 16 map it represents. Each column costs a forward pass of about 6600 multiply-adds against the ray caster's few
dozen cell steps. The network is therefore a far larger and slower description of this world than the grid. Coordinate
networks pay off where no compact explicit description exists, as for real scenes known only through photographs
<Cite id="mildenhall2020" />.
