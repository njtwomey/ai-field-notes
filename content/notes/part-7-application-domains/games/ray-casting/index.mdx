<Definition>
A **ray caster** renders a world of square wall cells on a grid, seen by a camera at position $(x, y)$ with heading
$\theta$ and horizontal field of view $\phi$. For each of the $W$ screen columns it traces one ray in the floor plane to
the first wall it meets, and draws that column as a vertical wall slice of height $f / p$, where $p$ is the hit's
distance perpendicular to the projection plane and $f = (W/2) / \tan(\phi/2)$ is the focal length in pixels.
</Definition>

## Mechanism

**Rays.** Column $i \in \{0, \dots, W-1\}$ looks through the point at
$c_i = 2(i + \tfrac12)/W - 1 \in (-1, 1)$ on a flat projection plane, so its ray leaves at angle
$\theta + \arctan(c_i \tan(\phi/2))$. Equal steps across the screen are unequal steps in angle.

**Traversal.** The ray is followed cell by cell with the digital differential analyser of Amanatides and Woo
<Cite id="amanatides1987" />. A ray with direction $(d_x, d_y)$ crosses consecutive vertical grid lines every
$\abs{1/d_x}$ units of length and consecutive horizontal ones every $\abs{1/d_y}$. The analyser keeps the distance to
the next line of each kind and steps across whichever is nearer. It visits exactly the cells the ray passes through, so
the cost of a ray is proportional to the number of cells it crosses, and it stops at the first wall cell. The face it
hit (across a vertical or a horizontal line) and the hit's position along that face give the shading and the texture
column.

**Projection.** A wall of height 1 at perpendicular distance $p$ spans $f/p$ pixels by similar triangles. The
perpendicular distance of a hit at Euclidean distance $d$ along a ray at angle $\alpha$ from the heading is
$p = d \cos\alpha$. Using $d$ instead makes a flat wall look bowed, because rays towards the edges of the view travel
farther to reach the same wall: the fish-eye effect. With $p$, the top edge of a straight wall projects to a straight
line, as it does in a pinhole camera.

## Worked example

**Setup.** The map is the 8 × 8 one-pillar map of the game above: a ring of wall cells around the border and a
2 × 2 pillar in columns 3 and 4 of rows 3 and 4. Cell $(i, j)$ covers $[i, i+1) \times [j, j+1)$. The $y$ axis grows
downwards, as the map is stored, and angles are measured from the $+x$ axis towards $+y$. The camera stands at
$(x, y) = (1.5, 1.5)$ with heading $\theta = 45^\circ$. The ray traced is the one at $\alpha = -15^\circ$ from the
heading, so it leaves at angle $\beta = \theta + \alpha = 30^\circ$ with unit direction
$(d_x, d_y) = (\cos\beta, \sin\beta) = (0.866, 0.5)$.

**Spacing of crossings.** The point at distance $t$ along the ray is $(x + t d_x, y + t d_y)$. Its $x$ coordinate
changes by $d_x$ per unit of distance. Consecutive vertical grid lines $x = k$ and $x = k + 1$ are 1 apart in $x$, so
the ray travels $\delta_x = 1 / \abs{d_x}$ between them. The same argument in $y$ gives $\delta_y = 1 / \abs{d_y}$.
Here $\delta_x = 1 / 0.866 = 1.155$ and $\delta_y = 1 / 0.5 = 2$. The ray moves right and down, so the first vertical
line is $x = 2$ and the first horizontal line is $y = 2$. Each is 0.5 away in its coordinate, so the distances along
the ray to the first crossings are $t_x = 0.5 \, \delta_x = 0.577$ and $t_y = 0.5 \, \delta_y = 1$.

**Steps.** Each step compares $t_x$ with $t_y$. The smaller one is the next point where the ray leaves the current
cell. The analyser moves one cell across that line and adds $\delta_x$ to $t_x$ or $\delta_y$ to $t_y$. Every cell
boundary the ray crosses is a vertical or a horizontal grid line, and the crossings are taken in increasing order of
$t$, so the cells are visited in the order the ray enters them.

| Step | Comparison        | Line crossed | $t$   | Cell entered | $t_x$ after | $t_y$ after |
| ---- | ----------------- | ------------ | ----- | ------------ | ----------- | ----------- |
| 1    | $0.577 < 1$       | $x = 2$      | 0.577 | $(2, 1)$     | 1.732       | 1           |
| 2    | $1 < 1.732$       | $y = 2$      | 1     | $(2, 2)$     | 1.732       | 3           |
| 3    | $1.732 < 3$       | $x = 3$      | 1.732 | $(3, 2)$     | 2.887       | 3           |
| 4    | $2.887 < 3$       | $x = 4$      | 2.887 | $(4, 2)$     | 4.041       | 3           |
| 5    | $3 < 4.041$       | $y = 3$      | 3     | $(4, 3)$     | 4.041       | 5           |

Cell $(4, 3)$ is part of the pillar, so the ray stops at step 5. The hit is at distance $d = 3$, at the point
$(1.5 + 3 \times 0.866, \; 1.5 + 3 \times 0.5) = (4.098, 3)$ on the pillar's face along the horizontal line $y = 3$.

**Projection.** The hit lies $d (\cos\beta, \sin\beta)$ from the camera. Its component along the heading
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is $d (\cos\beta \cos\theta + \sin\beta \sin\theta) = d \cos(\beta - \theta) = d \cos\alpha$,
which is the perpendicular distance $p$. Here $p = 3 \cos 15^\circ = 3 \times 0.966 = 2.898$. On a screen $W = 320$
pixels wide with field of view $\phi = 60^\circ$, the focal length is $f = 160 / \tan 30^\circ = 160 / 0.577 = 277.1$
pixels. The slice is $f / p = 277.1 / 2.898 = 95.6$ pixels tall, centred on the horizon. The ray meets the projection
plane at $c = \tan\alpha / \tan(\phi / 2) = -0.268 / 0.577 = -0.464$, which is column
$i = (c + 1) W / 2 - \tfrac12 = 85$, left of the screen's centre.

**The whole image.** The renderer repeats the trace for all $W$ columns: a fan of rays, one per column, each giving one
slice. The fraction of the hit's coordinate along the face, here $u = 4.098 - 4 = 0.098$, selects the column of the
wall texture to stretch over the slice; on some faces it is mirrored to $1 - u$ so that every texture reads left to
right. Faces met across a horizontal grid line, like this one, are drawn darker than faces met across a vertical line,
so two walls meeting at a corner differ in brightness <Cite id="vandevenne2004" />.

## Cost

Each frame costs $W$ ray traversals of at most a few dozen cell steps each, independent of the number of walls. This is
why the method ran in real time on 1992 PCs <Cite id="sanglard2018" />. The price is a restricted world: walls are
axis-aligned and of one height, floors are flat, and the camera cannot look up or down
<Cite id="vandevenne2004" />.
