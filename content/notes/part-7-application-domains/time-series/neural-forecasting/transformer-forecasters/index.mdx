All three target **long-sequence forecasting**: horizons of hundreds of steps on benchmarks such as electricity
transformer temperature (ETT), electricity load, traffic occupancy and weather, with a look-back window of $L$ steps and
a horizon of $H$ steps. All three produce the whole horizon in one forward pass rather than one step at a time.

## The problem with a plain transformer

A <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> layer computes attention between every pair of its $L$ tokens, so
time and memory per layer are $O(L^2)$ <Cite id="vaswani2017" />. With one token per time step, $L$ is the
number of past steps. Seeing a week of 15-minute data means $L = 672$ tokens and $672^2 \approx 450{,}000$ attention
scores per head per layer. The transformer's autoregressive decoder generates one step per
pass, so a horizon of $H$ steps takes $H$ passes and accumulates errors.

## Informer

Informer attacks all three costs: attention, depth and decoding <Cite id="zhou2021" />.

**ProbSparse self-attention.** In trained transformers, most queries attend almost uniformly over the keys, and only
a few have sharply peaked attention. A uniform query adds little beyond an average of the values. Informer scores each
query $\qvec_i$ by how far its attention is from uniform:

$$
M(\qvec_i, \Kmat) = \ln\sum_{j=1}^{L_K} e^{\qvec_i\kvec_j^\top/\sqrt d}
- \frac{1}{L_K}\sum_{j=1}^{L_K}\frac{\qvec_i\kvec_j^\top}{\sqrt d}.
$$

The first term is the log-sum-exp of the query's scores and the second is their mean. By Jensen's inequality the
difference is at least $\ln L_K$, with equality exactly when all scores are equal. Computing $M$ needs all scores, so
Informer replaces the log-sum-exp by the maximum, $\bar M(\qvec_i, \Kmat) = \max_j \qvec_i\kvec_j^\top/\sqrt d -
\text{mean}$, and estimates it from about $L \ln L$ randomly sampled query–key pairs. Only the top
$u = c \ln L$ queries by $\bar M$ enter the attention product; the rest are left out of it. With $c$ fixed
(the paper uses $c = 5$), each of the $u$ active queries costs $O(L)$, so the layer costs $O(L \ln L)$.

**Self-attention distilling.** Between encoder layers, a width-3 convolution, an <Gloss name="elu" /> and a max pool
with stride 2 halve the sequence length. Layer $j$ then holds $L / 2^{j-1}$ tokens, and total memory across layers is at
most $L(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \dots) < 2L$ tokens. The encoder combines a 3-layer stack on the full input with a
1-layer stack on a quarter of it and concatenates their outputs.

**Generative decoder.** The decoder input is a start token of $L_{\mathrm{token}}$ known values just before the
horizon, followed by $H$ zero placeholders that carry only time stamps. Masked attention and one forward pass fill in
all $H$ outputs at once. To forecast 168 hours, for example, the start token is the preceding 5 days.

## Autoformer

Autoformer builds decomposition and periodicity into the architecture <Cite id="wu2021b" />.

**Series decomposition block.** A moving average with padding splits any length-$L$ sequence $\Xmat$ into a
trend-cyclical part and a seasonal remainder:

$$
\Xmat_t = \operatorname{AvgPool}(\operatorname{Padding}(\Xmat)), \qquad \Xmat_s = \Xmat - \Xmat_t.
$$

The block appears after every attention and feed-forward layer, so the network decomposes its hidden states
progressively rather than decomposing the raw input once. The encoder keeps only seasonal parts. The decoder carries
two streams: a seasonal stream refined by attention, and a trend stream that accumulates the trend parts extracted at
each layer. The final forecast is their sum. For the decoder's input, the latter half of the look-back window is
decomposed and followed by placeholders: zeros for the seasonal part and the window's mean for the trend. See

<NoteLink to="time-series-decomposition">time-series decomposition</NoteLink> for the moving-average method.

**Auto-correlation instead of attention.** For a stationary process, the autocorrelation
$R_{XX}(\tau) = \lim_{L \to \infty} \frac1L\sum_t X_t X_{t-\tau}$ is large at lags $\tau$ near a period. Autoformer
computes $R_{\Qmat,\Kmat}(\tau)$ between queries and keys for every lag $\tau = 1, \dots, L$, keeps the
$k = \lfloor c \ln L \rfloor$ lags with the largest values, and aggregates whole shifted copies of the values:

$$
\operatorname{AutoCorrelation}(\Qmat, \Kmat, \Vmat) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \operatorname{Roll}(\Vmat, \tau_i)\,\hat R(\tau_i),
\qquad \hat R(\tau_1), \dots, \hat R(\tau_k) = \operatorname{Softmax}\paren{R(\tau_1), \dots, R(\tau_k)}.
$$

$\operatorname{Roll}(\Vmat, \tau)$ shifts the sequence by $\tau$ steps and wraps the overflow to the front. Attention
links individual time points. Auto-correlation links whole sub-series that sit at the same phase of a period, such as
the same hour on earlier days.

All $L$ lags cost $O(L \log L)$ together, because the autocorrelation is the inverse
<NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> of the power spectrum:
$R_{XX}(\tau) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\mathcal{F}(X)\,\overline{\mathcal{F}(X)}\big)(\tau)$, computed with the
<Gloss name="fft">fast Fourier transform</Gloss>.

<Derivation title="Why the autocorrelation is the inverse transform of the power spectrum">
Take the circular autocorrelation of a length-$L$ sequence, $R(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{L-1} x_t\, x_{(t-\tau) \bmod L}$, and
its <Gloss name="dft">discrete Fourier transform</Gloss> $X_f = \sum_t x_t e^{-2\pi \mathrm{i} f t / L}$. The transform
of $R$ is

$$
\sum_\tau R(\tau) e^{-2\pi \mathrm{i} f\tau/L}
= \sum_t x_t \sum_\tau x_{(t-\tau) \bmod L}\, e^{-2\pi \mathrm{i} f\tau/L}.
$$

Substitute $s = (t - \tau) \bmod L$, so $\tau \equiv t - s$ and $e^{-2\pi \mathrm{i} f\tau/L} = e^{-2\pi \mathrm{i} f
t/L} e^{2\pi \mathrm{i} f s/L}$ because the exponential has period $L$ in $\tau$. The double sum factorises:

$$
\sum_t x_t e^{-2\pi \mathrm{i} f t/L}\sum_s x_s e^{2\pi \mathrm{i} f s/L} = X_f\,\overline{X_f} = \abs{X_f}^2
$$

for real $x$. Inverting the transform gives $R = \mathcal{F}^{-1}(\abs{X}^2)$. This is the Wiener–Khinchin relation in
discrete, circular form. Two fast Fourier transforms of cost $O(L \log L)$ replace $L$ dot products of cost $O(L)$.

</Derivation>

## PatchTST

PatchTST keeps a standard transformer encoder and changes what a token is <Cite id="nie2023" />.

**Patching.** Each univariate series of length $L$ is cut into patches of length $P$ with stride $S$. After padding the
end with $S$ copies of the last value, the number of patches is

$$
N = \left\lfloor \frac{L - P}{S} \right\rfloor + 2.
$$

Each patch is mapped to a $D$-dimensional token by a shared linear layer, and a learnt position encoding is added. The
number of tokens falls from $L$ to about $L/S$, so the attention map shrinks by a factor of about $S^2$. A token now
carries a local shape, such as a rise over 16 steps, instead of a single value whose meaning depends on its
neighbours.

**Channel <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.** A multivariate series with $M$ variables is split into
$M$ univariate series. Each passes through the same encoder and head, with shared weights, and none sees the others.
Every series is normalised to zero mean and unit variance over its look-back window before patching, and the statistics
are added back to the output.

**Head.** The encoder's $N$ output vectors are flattened and a linear layer maps them to the $H$ forecast values.

**Self-supervised pretraining.** With non-overlapping patches, a random subset of patches is zeroed and the encoder is
trained to reconstruct them. Fine-tuning the pretrained encoder for forecasting beat supervised training from scratch
on the larger datasets, and representations pretrained on one dataset transferred to others.

## Worked example

Take $L = 336$ hourly values, patch length $P = 16$ and stride $S = 8$, the default PatchTST/42 setting. The number of
patches is $\lfloor (336 - 16)/8 \rfloor + 2 = 40 + 2 = 42$. A transformer with one token per step computes
$336^2 = 112{,}896$ attention scores per head per layer. PatchTST computes $42^2 = 1{,}764$, which is $64 = 8^2$ times
fewer. With $L = 512$ the same patching gives $\lfloor 496/8 \rfloor + 2 = 64$ tokens, the PatchTST/64 setting.

For Informer with $c = 5$ and $L = 96$, the number of active queries is $u = 5 \ln 96 \approx 22.8$, so about 23 of the
96 queries compute full attention. At $L = 720$, $u = 5 \ln 720 \approx 32.9$: the active queries grow by fewer than 11
while the sequence grows by 624.

## Training

All three are trained with <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> on normalised data, with Adam, on the same
public benchmarks. Informer and Autoformer embed each time step's values together with time-stamp features such as hour
and day. Autoformer used a look-back of 96 steps (36 for influenza-like illness), 2 encoder layers and 1 decoder layer.
PatchTST used a look-back of 336 or 512 steps.

## What is distinctive

- **Informer** makes attention sparse by keeping only the queries whose attention is furthest from uniform, and makes
  decoding a single pass. It introduced the ETT datasets that later long-horizon models were compared on.
- **Autoformer** replaces attention between points with aggregation of lagged sub-series, and builds trend extraction
  into every block. Its authors report a 38% average reduction in MSE over the previous best model across six
  benchmarks.
- **PatchTST** changes the tokens rather than the attention. It reported 21.0% lower MSE and 16.7% lower MAE than the
  best transformer baselines with $L = 512$, and still beat the linear baseline DLinear, particularly on the large
  weather, traffic and electricity datasets.

## Comparison

Zeng et al., whose preprint appeared between Autoformer and PatchTST, showed that a single linear layer from the
look-back window to the horizon outperformed Informer, Autoformer and their successors on these benchmarks, often by 20%
to 50% <Cite id="zeng2023" />. Shuffling the input order barely changed Informer's error on exchange-rate data, which
suggests it used little of the temporal order. Informer and Autoformer also did not improve as the look-back window
grew. See

<NoteLink to="dlinear">DLinear</NoteLink>.

PatchTST responds to both findings. A patch preserves the order within it, so shuffling tokens no longer destroys all
temporal information. Fewer tokens make long look-back windows affordable, and PatchTST's error falls as $L$ grows.
Channel independence also cut overfitting relative to mixing all variables in one token. Informer's sparse attention
and Autoformer's auto-correlation made each token cheaper. PatchTST made the tokens fewer and more informative, which
proved the more effective of the two directions on these benchmarks <Cite id="nie2023" />.
