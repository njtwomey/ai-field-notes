<Definition>
A **time series** is a sequence of observations $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ indexed by time, in which nearby values are
usually dependent. The foundations of time-series analysis are the properties that make such dependence learnable from a
single observed sequence: a distribution that does not drift, a correlation that depends only on the time gap, and
transformations that turn a drifting series into one that does not.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Most statistical tools assume independent samples. A time series offers one sample path, in which each value depends on
the values before it. Estimating anything from that path needs an assumption that the dependence structure repeats over
time. The notes here state that assumption, give the tools that measure the dependence, and show how to transform a
series until the assumption holds. Every model in the later chapters, from <Gloss name="arima" /> to
<NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filters</NoteLink> to <NoteLink to="neural-forecasting-models">neural
forecasters</NoteLink>, uses these ideas to choose its form and to check its residuals <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

## Main ideas

**Stationarity makes one path informative.** <NoteLink to="stationarity" /> defines a weakly stationary series as one
with constant mean and an autocovariance $\gamma(h) = \cov(x_t, x_{t+h})$ that depends only on the lag $h$
<Cite id="brockwell1991" />. Averages over time then estimate averages over the distribution, so a single long series
can be used to fit a model.

**White noise and the <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> are the two reference series.**
<NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk" /> defines white noise as uncorrelated shocks of mean 0 and variance
$\sigma^2$, the building block of every linear model in the chapter. A random walk adds those shocks up. Its variance
$t\sigma^2$ grows with time, so it is the simplest non-stationary series.

**Dependence is read from correlation functions.**
<NoteLink to="autocorrelation-and-partial-autocorrelation" /> defines the autocorrelation function (ACF), the
correlation between values $h$ steps apart, and the partial autocorrelation function (PACF), the same correlation after
removing the values in between. The shapes of the two functions suggest the orders of autoregressive and
<Gloss name="ma">moving-average models</Gloss>, and their sampling bands give a first test for white noise.

**A series can be split into interpretable parts.** <NoteLink to="time-series-decomposition" /> writes a series as
trend-cycle plus seasonal plus remainder, or as their product. Classical decomposition uses moving averages; STL uses
iterated <NoteLink to="local-regression">local regression</NoteLink> and lets the seasonal pattern change.

**Differencing removes a stochastic trend.** <NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots" /> shows that taking
differences $x_t - x_{t-1}$ turns a random walk into white noise. A series that needs $d$ differences is integrated of
order $d$. The Dickey–Fuller and KPSS tests decide whether a unit root is present, with opposite null hypotheses.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="stationarity" />, which every other note requires. White noise and the random walk come next,
since they are the examples the later notes test against. The ACF and PACF note gives the main diagnostic. The
decomposition and differencing notes give the two ways to remove non-stationarity: estimate the trend and season
explicitly, or difference them away.
