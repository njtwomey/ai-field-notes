<Definition>
For series $\avec = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and $\bvec = (b_1, \dots, b_m)$, a warping path is a sequence of
index pairs $(i_1, j_1), \dots, (i_K, j_K)$ that starts at $(1, 1)$, ends at $(n, m)$, and at each step increases $i$,
$j$ or both by exactly 1. The dynamic time warping (DTW) distance is the smallest total cost over all warping paths,

$$
\mathrm{DTW}(\avec, \bvec) = \min_{\text{paths}} \sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K} \paren{a_{i_k} - b_{j_k}}^2}.
$$

A Sakoe–Chiba window of half-width $r$ admits only pairs with $\abs{i - j} \le r$ <Cite id="sakoe1978" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">Euclidean distance</NoteLink> compares $a_i$ with $b_i$ for
every $i$. Two recordings of the same gesture, heartbeat or spoken word rarely proceed at the same speed throughout, so
the Euclidean distance compares a peak in one with a slope in the other and reports a large distance for similar shapes.
DTW lets one sample of $\avec$ match several consecutive samples of $\bvec$ and the reverse, which stretches and
compresses the time axis locally until the shapes line up.

The conditions on the path keep the alignment meaningful. Starting at $(1, 1)$ and ending at $(n, m)$ matches the
ends of the series. Steps of at most 1 in each index mean that no sample is skipped. Non-decreasing indices mean
that the order of events is preserved.

## The recursion

Let $D(i, j)$ be the smallest cumulative squared cost of a path from $(1, 1)$ to $(i, j)$. The last step into
$(i, j)$ comes from $(i-1, j)$, $(i, j-1)$ or $(i-1, j-1)$, and the cheapest path to $(i, j)$ extends the cheapest
path to one of them. With $D(0, 0) = 0$ and $D(i, 0) = D(0, j) = \infty$ for $i, j \ge 1$,

$$
D(i, j) = \paren{a_i - b_j}^2 + \min\set{D(i-1, j),\ D(i, j-1),\ D(i-1, j-1)},
$$

and $\mathrm{DTW}(\avec, \bvec) = \sqrt{D(n, m)}$. Filling the $n \times m$ table row by row costs $O(nm)$ time.
The path itself is recovered by walking back from $(n, m)$ to the predecessor with the smallest $D$ at each step.

**The Sakoe–Chiba window.** Setting $D(i, j) = \infty$ whenever $\abs{i - j} > r$ restricts the path to a diagonal
band <Cite id="sakoe1978" />. The table then has $O(n r)$ finite entries, and the cost falls from $O(n^2)$ to
$O(n r)$ for $n = m$. The window also prevents pathological alignments, in which one sample matches a long stretch of
the other series. With $r = 0$ the only admissible path is the diagonal, and DTW equals the Euclidean distance. With
$r \ge n - 1$ the window has no effect.

## Worked example

Take $\avec = (0, 1, 3, 1, 0, 0)$ and $\bvec = (0, 0, 1, 2, 1, 0)$. $\bvec$ has a lower peak one step later. The
Euclidean distance is $\sqrt{0 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 1 + 0} = \sqrt{7} = 2.646$. The cumulative cost table $D(i, j)$, with
rows for $a_i$ and columns for $b_j$, is:

|             | $b_1 = 0$ | $b_2 = 0$ | $b_3 = 1$ | $b_4 = 2$ | $b_5 = 1$ | $b_6 = 0$ |
| ----------- | --------- | --------- | --------- | --------- | --------- | --------- |
| $a_1 = 0$   | 0         | 0         | 1         | 5         | 6         | 6         |
| $a_2 = 1$   | 1         | 1         | 0         | 1         | 1         | 2         |
| $a_3 = 3$   | 10        | 10        | 4         | 1         | 5         | 10        |
| $a_4 = 1$   | 11        | 11        | 4         | 2         | 1         | 2         |
| $a_5 = 0$   | 11        | 11        | 5         | 6         | 2         | 1         |
| $a_6 = 0$   | 11        | 11        | 6         | 9         | 3         | 1         |

For example, $D(3, 4) = (3 - 2)^2 + \min\set{D(2, 4), D(3, 3), D(2, 3)} = 1 + \min\set{1, 4, 0} = 1$. The final
entry is $D(6, 6) = 1$, so $\mathrm{DTW}(\avec, \bvec) = 1$. The optimal path matches each point of $\avec$ with
the point of $\bvec$ one step later, and the only cost is the difference between the peaks, $(3 - 2)^2 = 1$.

With $r = 1$ the same path fits inside the window, and the windowed distance is also 1. With $r = 0$ the distance is
the Euclidean $\sqrt{7}$.

## Lower bounds and LB_Keogh

<NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">Nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> under DTW compares a query with many
candidates. Each full DTW computation costs $O(n r)$. A lower bound that costs $O(n)$ can rule out most candidates
without computing DTW: if the bound already exceeds the best distance found so far, the candidate cannot be the nearest
neighbour.

LB_Keogh <Cite id="keogh2002" /> wraps the candidate $\bvec$ in an envelope of the same window half-width $r$:

$$
U_i = \max_{\abs{j - i} \le r} b_j, \qquad L_i = \min_{\abs{j - i} \le r} b_j,
$$

and charges the query $\avec$ only where it leaves the envelope:

$$
\mathrm{LB\_Keogh}(\avec, \bvec) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\begin{cases}
(a_i - U_i)^2 & \text{if } a_i > U_i, \\
(a_i - L_i)^2 & \text{if } a_i < L_i, \\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}}
$$

**Proof sketch** <Cite id="keogh2005" />. Take any warping path inside the window, for series of equal length $n$.

1. Every index $i$ of $\avec$ appears in at least one pair $(i, j)$ of the path, because the path starts at $i = 1$,
   ends at $i = n$ and increases $i$ by at most 1 per step.
2. For any such pair, $\abs{i - j} \le r$, so $b_j$ lies between $L_i$ and $U_i$ by the definition of the envelope.
3. If $a_i > U_i$, then $a_i - b_j \ge a_i - U_i > 0$, so $(a_i - b_j)^2 \ge (a_i - U_i)^2$. The case $a_i < L_i$ is
   symmetric, and the charge is 0 otherwise.
4. Keeping one pair per index $i$ and dropping the others can only lower the sum, because every term is
   non-negative.

So the squared cost of every admissible path is at least the squared LB_Keogh, and so is their minimum, the squared
windowed DTW. In the worked example with $r = 1$, the envelope of $\bvec$ is $U = (0, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1)$ and
$L = (0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0)$. Only $a_3 = 3$ leaves it, by 1, so LB_Keogh $= 1$, equal to the windowed DTW distance here.

## The UCR suite

The <Gloss name="ucr-archive" /> suite <Cite id="rakthanmanon2012" /> searches a long series for the subsequence closest
to a query under windowed DTW. It combines four ideas, each of which removes work without changing the answer.

- **Early-abandoning z-normalisation.** Each subsequence is z-normalised as it is compared, with running sums for its
  mean and <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>, instead of normalising all subsequences
  in advance.
- **Reordering.** Distance terms are accumulated in decreasing order of the query's absolute z-normalised values.
  Those terms tend to be largest, so the running sum crosses the best-so-far sooner and the computation stops earlier.
- **Reversing the roles.** LB_Keogh is computed with the envelope around the query and, when that fails to prune,
  with the envelope around the candidate. The two bounds differ, and the larger one prunes more.
- **Cascading bounds.** A constant-time bound from the first and last points is tried first, then the two LB_Keogh
  variants in $O(n)$, and full DTW only for the candidates that survive. The DTW computation itself abandons early,
  adding the remaining LB_Keogh terms to its partial cost.

With these ideas the authors searched a trillion subsequences, and reported exact DTW search faster than earlier
approximate methods <Cite id="rakthanmanon2012" />.

## Why 1-NN DTW is the baseline

The one-nearest-neighbour classifier with DTW (1-NN DTW) labels a test series with the class of its closest training
series. Its only parameter is the window half-width $r$, usually set by <Gloss name="loo">leave-one-out
cross-validation</Gloss> on the training data. It has no training phase beyond that choice, needs no feature design, and
makes the classification decision traceable to one training example.

Across the UCR archive, 1-NN DTW with a cross-validated window is more accurate than 1-NN Euclidean on most datasets,
and it was hard to beat for many years. The archive reports its error rate for every dataset alongside those of
Euclidean distance and unconstrained DTW <Cite id="dau2019" />. The 2017 bake off used 1-NN DTW as one of its two
benchmarks, and found that only 9 of 18 recent algorithms were significantly more accurate than both benchmarks
<Cite id="bagnall2017" />. A new time-series classifier is expected to report its accuracy against 1-NN DTW.

## Assumptions

- The series are comparable after local stretching. DTW does not account for patterns that differ in amplitude or
  offset, so series are usually z-normalised first.
- Corresponding events occur in the same order in both series. DTW cannot align events that are swapped.

## Pitfalls

- **DTW is not a metric.** It violates the triangle inequality, so metric-tree indexes do not apply. Lower bounds
  such as LB_Keogh are the standard route to fast search instead.
- **Unconstrained warping.** Without a window, a flat stretch can absorb a long feature of the other series at little
  cost, and accuracy often drops. A window of about 10% of the series length is a common default, but the best value
  varies by dataset and is best chosen by cross-validation.
- **Endpoint effects.** The path must match the first and last points. Series cut at arbitrary points, with a pattern
  partly outside the segment, are penalised for the unmatched part.
- **Cost.** Even with a window, DTW costs $O(n r)$ per pair. 1-NN DTW on a large training set is slow at test time
  without lower-bound pruning.
