<Definition>
Let $\Fcal_{t-1}$ be all information available at time $t - 1$, and $\Fcal_{t-1} \setminus x$ the same information
without the past of a series $x$. The series $x$ **Granger-causes** $y$ if the minimum
<Gloss name="mse">mean-squared-error</Gloss> forecast of $y_t$ from $\Fcal_{t-1}$ has a smaller error variance than the
forecast from $\Fcal_{t-1} \setminus x$ <Cite id="granger1969" />. The test restricts forecasts to linear functions of
$p$ lags and compares the two regressions with an F statistic.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

Fit the <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>

$$
y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^p a_i y_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^p b_i x_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad t = p + 1, \dots, T.
$$

- $H_0$: $b_1 = b_2 = \dots = b_p = 0$. The past of $x$ adds nothing to the linear forecast of $y_t$; $x$ does not
  Granger-cause $y$.
- $H_1$: at least one $b_i \neq 0$.

The test is directional. Testing whether $y$ Granger-causes $x$ swaps the roles and is a separate test. Both, one or
neither can hold. When both hold, the relationship is called feedback <Cite id="granger1969" />.

The regression is the $y$ equation of a bivariate <NoteLink to="vector-autoregression">vector autoregression</NoteLink>.
In a VAR with $K$ variables, "variable $k$ does not Granger-cause variable $j$" is the hypothesis that the $(j, k)$
entries of all coefficient matrices $\Amat_1, \dots, \Amat_p$ are zero <Cite id="lutkepohl2005" />.

## Assumptions

- Both series are <NoteLink to="stationarity">stationary</NoteLink>. With
  <NoteLink to="differencing-and-unit-roots">unit roots</NoteLink> the statistic does not have the F distribution.
- The lag length $p$ is large enough that $\varepsilon_t$ is serially uncorrelated.
- The errors have constant variance. The F distribution is exact for Gaussian errors and fixed regressors; with lagged
  dependent variables it holds asymptotically <Cite id="hamilton1994" />.
- All relevant variables are included. An omitted third series that drives both $x$ and $y$ with different delays can
  produce Granger causality between them.

## Test statistic

Let $n = T - p$ be the number of usable observations. Fit the **unrestricted** regression above, with $k = 2p + 1$
coefficients and residual sum of squares $\operatorname{RSS}_u$. Fit the **restricted** regression without the $x$
lags, with residual sum of squares $\operatorname{RSS}_r$. The statistic is

$$
F = \frac{(\operatorname{RSS}_r - \operatorname{RSS}_u)/p}{\operatorname{RSS}_u/(n - 2p - 1)}.
$$

The numerator is the drop in residual variance per restriction when the $x$ lags are added. The denominator is the
unbiased estimate of the error variance $\sigma^2$ under the unrestricted model.

**Why the statistic has an F distribution.** Least squares projects the response vector $\yvec$ onto the
<NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">column space</NoteLink> of the design matrix. Let $\Hmat_u$ and $\Hmat_r$ be the
projection matrices of the unrestricted and restricted designs; the restricted column space lies inside the unrestricted
one. Then

$$
\operatorname{RSS}_u = \yvec^\top(\eye - \Hmat_u)\yvec, \qquad
\operatorname{RSS}_r - \operatorname{RSS}_u = \yvec^\top(\Hmat_u - \Hmat_r)\yvec.
$$

Both $\eye - \Hmat_u$ and $\Hmat_u - \Hmat_r$ are projections, of ranks $n - 2p - 1$ and $p$, onto orthogonal subspaces.
Under $H_0$ the mean of $\yvec$ lies in the restricted column space, which both projections annihilate, so each
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> equals the same form in the error vector
$\varepsilonvec$. For $\varepsilonvec \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye)$, a quadratic form in a rank-$r$ projection divided by $\sigma^2$ is $\ChiSq_r$, and forms
in <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projections</NoteLink> are independent. The ratio of two independent
chi-squared variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom, has the <NoteLink to="f-distribution">F
distribution</NoteLink>:

$$
F \sim \FDist(p,\; n - 2p - 1) \quad \text{under } H_0.
$$

## Decision rule

Reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $F$ exceeds the $1 - \alpha$ quantile of $\FDist(p, n - 2p - 1)$, or equivalently
when the <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> $\prob(F_{p, n-2p-1} \ge F)$ is below $\alpha$. An asymptotically
equivalent Wald form uses $pF$ against $\ChiSq_p$.

## Effect size

The F statistic grows with the sample size for any fixed effect, so it does not measure strength. Two measures do:

- the proportional reduction in one-step forecast error variance, $1 - \operatorname{RSS}_u/\operatorname{RSS}_r$;
- the estimated coefficients $\hat b_i$, or the impulse response of $y$ to a shock in $x$ from the fitted VAR.

## Worked example

A bivariate series of $T = 200$ observations is tested with $p = 2$ lags, so $n = 198$ and the unrestricted regression
has $k = 5$ coefficients. The restricted regression of $y_t$ on a constant, $y_{t-1}$ and $y_{t-2}$ gives
$\operatorname{RSS}_r = 250$. Adding $x_{t-1}$ and $x_{t-2}$ gives $\operatorname{RSS}_u = 230$.

$$
F = \frac{(250 - 230)/2}{230/(198 - 5)} = \frac{10}{1.192} = 8.39.
$$

The 95% quantile of $\FDist(2, 193)$ is $3.04$ and the p-value is $0.0003$, so $H_0$ is rejected: the past of $x$
improves the linear forecast of $y$. The forecast error variance falls by $1 - 230/250 = 8\%$.

## Pitfalls

- **Prediction is not causation.** Granger causality is about incremental predictability. A variable that reacts to
  expectations, such as a futures price, Granger-causes the outcome it anticipates without causing it.
- **Omitted variables.** A common driver with different delays creates spurious Granger causality. Include plausible
  confounders in a multivariate VAR.
- **Non-stationary series.** Running the test on integrated series in levels gives non-standard distributions and too
  many rejections. Difference the series, or when they are <NoteLink to="cointegration">cointegrated</NoteLink> use a
  specification that keeps the long-run relation.
- **Lag length.** Too few lags leave autocorrelated errors and invalidate the F distribution. Too many lags waste
  degrees of freedom and reduce power. Choose $p$ by an information criterion before testing.
- **Sampling frequency.** Aggregating over time can hide or create Granger causality. An effect that occurs within one
  sampling interval appears as contemporaneous correlation, not as Granger causality.
