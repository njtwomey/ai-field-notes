<Definition>
**ARMA and ARIMA models** describe a time series $x_t$ as a linear filter applied to
<NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white noise</NoteLink> $\varepsilon_t$. An autoregressive (AR) part regresses
$x_t$ on $x_{t-1}, \dots, x_{t-p}$; a moving-average (MA) part adds the shocks
$\varepsilon_{t-1}, \dots, \varepsilon_{t-q}$. ARIMA applies the same model to the series after $d$ differences, and
seasonal ARIMA repeats the structure at the seasonal lag $m$.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The ARIMA family is the standard statistical model for a single series with serial dependence. It forecasts sales,
traffic, prices and sensor readings from their own history, with <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction
intervals</NoteLink> that follow from the model. A handful of parameters captures autocorrelation that would otherwise
need many lags. The family also serves as a baseline that newer forecasters are compared against, and as the error model
in regressions on time-ordered data <Cite id="box2015" />.

## Main ideas

**Two building blocks: past values and past shocks.** The <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model" /> predicts $x_t$ from its
own past and is stationary when the roots of its <NoteLink to="characteristic-polynomial">characteristic
polynomial</NoteLink> lie outside the unit circle. The <NoteLink to="moving-average-model" /> writes $x_t$ as a finite
sum of shocks, so it is always stationary and its <Gloss name="acf" /> cuts off after lag $q$. The
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-moving-average-model" /> combines the two, matching with a few parameters what a pure AR or
MA model needs many lags for.

**Differencing handles trends.** The <NoteLink to="autoregressive-integrated-moving-average-model" /> fits an ARMA model
to the $d$-th difference of the series. Its $d$ unit roots produce a stochastic trend, so its prediction intervals widen
without limit as the horizon grows. The <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> and <Gloss name="ses">simple
exponential smoothing</Gloss> are special cases. The <NoteLink to="seasonal-autoregressive-integrated-moving-average" />
model multiplies ordinary polynomials in the backshift operator $B$ by seasonal ones in $B^m$.

**A model is built in a cycle.** The <NoteLink to="box-jenkins-method" /> chooses $d$ from unit-root tests, $p$ and $q$
from the ACF and <Gloss name="pacf" />, estimates the parameters by maximum likelihood, and checks the residuals. The
<NoteLink to="ljung-box-test" /> asks whether the first $h$ residual autocorrelations are jointly zero. Correlated
residuals send the analyst back to identification <Cite id="hyndman2021" />.

**ARMA errors make regression on time series honest.**
<NoteLink to="regression-with-autoregressive-moving-average-errors" /> adds external regressors to the model.
<Gloss name="ols">Ordinary least squares</Gloss> with correlated errors stays unbiased but reports
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> that are usually too small; modelling the errors as ARMA
corrects them <Cite id="hamilton1994" />.

## How the notes fit together

Read the AR and MA notes first, then ARMA, ARIMA and seasonal ARIMA in that order: each adds one component to the
previous model. The Ljung–Box test and the Box–Jenkins method can be read once ARIMA is clear, since they use its
residuals. Regression with ARMA errors is last and assumes linear regression.
