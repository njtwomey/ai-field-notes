## Architecture

**The problem.** A pointwise scorer predicts $q_i \approx P(y_i = 1 \mid \text{features of item } i)$, the chance that
the user interacts with video $i$, and the feed is sorted by $q$. Similar videos get similar scores, so they sit next
to each other: once a basketball video is on the page, another one is worth less, yet sorting by $q$ shows a run of
them. Wilhelm et al. call two items similar when they are negatively correlated as interactions,
$P(y_i = 1, y_j = 1) < P(y_i = 1)\, P(y_j = 1)$, which is exactly the dependence a
<NoteLink to="determinantal-point-process" /> models.

**Why not heuristics.** Before DPPs, YouTube's policy layer enforced rules such as a cap on videos from one uploader.
The paper lists what goes wrong with rules of that kind: they ignore $q$, so they demote high-scoring and low-scoring
videos alike; every parameter needs a grid search that grows exponentially with the number of parameters; they have to
be retuned whenever the upstream models change; and they define no probability model, so they cannot score held-out
data. They also treat diversity as a goal that competes with utility, while the DPP treats redundancy as lost utility.

**Where the DPP sits.** The DPP is a last-layer model after the pointwise scorer. The teams improving $q$ and the
embeddings keep working independently, and the DPP consumes whatever they produce. The business-policy layer still runs
last.

**The kernel.** For each of the $N = 100$ videos the system supplies $q_i$ and a sparse embedding $\phivec_i$. With a
distance $D_{ij}$ computed from the embeddings,

$$
L_{ii} = q_i^2, \qquad L_{ij} = \alpha\, q_i q_j \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{D_{ij}}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr) \quad (i \ne j).
$$

At $\alpha = 1$ this is the quality-times-similarity kernel with a Gaussian similarity. $\alpha < 1$ shrinks every
similarity and treats all items as more diverse; $\alpha > 1$ makes them more alike and favours small sets, which fits
users who interact with few videos of a feed. Above 1 the matrix can lose positive semi-definiteness, so any negative
eigenvalues are set to zero, projecting $\Lmat$ back to the nearest <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive
semi-definite</Gloss> matrix. Of many embeddings tried (audio, frames, thumbnails, text), the best distance was the
Jaccard distance between sparse sets of item tokens.

## Training

Each training example is one homepage impression: the ordered list of videos shown and the subset $Y$ the user
interacted with. Wilhelm et al. used about 40,000 impressions from one day, and note the partial-label bias: only
videos the old system chose to show can be observed.

**Two parameters by grid search.** For $(\alpha, \sigma)$ they reranked each impression and measured the cumulative
gain $G = \sum_i y_i / j_i$, where $j_i$ is the new rank of item $i$. The best region had mid-range $\sigma$ and
$\alpha$ in the upper half of its range; there the kernels were mostly positive semi-definite and only an occasional
example needed projection.

**A learned kernel.** Grid search does not scale to more parameters, but a DPP has a likelihood. With kernel parameters
$\wvec$ and $M$ impressions,

$$
\log \mathcal{L}(\wvec) = \sum_{m=1}^M \Bigl[\log\det\bigl(\Lmat(\wvec)_{Y_m}\bigr) - \log\det\bigl(\Lmat(\wvec) +
\Imat\bigr)\Bigr],
$$

maximised by gradient ascent. The *deep Gramian kernel* replaces the scalar score by a vector $\qvec_i$ and sets

$$
L_{ij} = f(\qvec_i)\, \gvec(\phivec_i)^\top \gvec(\phivec_j)\, f(\qvec_j) + \delta\, \indicator[i = j],
$$

with $f$ a shallow network, $\gvec$ a deeper one that re-embeds the content, and a small $\delta$. The Gram form makes
$\Lmat$ positive semi-definite by construction, so no projection is needed.

## What is distinctive

**Windowed greedy ranking.** A feed is an ordered list, not a set. The serving algorithm asks the DPP for the most
likely set of $k$ videos, $\max_{\abs{Y} = k} \det(\Lmat_Y)$, by the greedy algorithm: add, $k$ times, the video that
most increases the determinant. Those $k$ videos, in the order greedy chose them, fill the top of the feed. The same is
repeated on the remaining videos until the feed is full, with $k$ about a dozen against a feed of several hundred. The
windows reflect that repulsion matters between videos seen together: videos 1 and 100 can be similar without harm.

**Diversity in service of utility.** The objective is the user's interactions, not a diversity score. Parameters are
chosen by how well the reranked feed puts the videos users engaged with near the top, so the amount of diversity is
learned from behaviour rather than set by hand.

## Comparison

In live experiments of about a week, three heuristics each lowered the "satisfied homepage watchers" metric, which
counts sessions from the homepage with significant watch time: fuzzy de-duplication (−0.05%), a sliding-window cap on
similar items (−0.26%) and a smooth penalty on scores of items similar to those already placed (−0.41%). The DPP raised
it by 0.63% and overall watch time by 0.52%; the deep Gramian kernel raised it by 1.72% but changed rankings enough to
disturb secondary business metrics and had not been deployed.

Direct interactions on the homepage barely moved. Watch time *originating* from the homepage rose, through the related
videos panel. Two long-term holdbacks, one rotating daily and one fixed, showed the effect growing over months for users
who kept seeing diversified feeds: a <NoteLink to="novelty-and-primacy-effects">learning effect</NoteLink> in which
users come back because the homepage keeps serving them.

The authors list the limits: the DPP is not personalised beyond $q$, since $\alpha$ and $\sigma$ are shared by all
users; how diversity needs vary by genre or by time is not modelled. Chen, Zhang and Zhou's fast greedy MAP algorithm
<Cite id="chen2018dpp" /> makes the windowed greedy step cheap at this scale.
