<Definition>
**LLM as a judge** is the use of a <Gloss name="llm">large language model</Gloss> to evaluate the outputs of another
system, as a scalable substitute for human raters. The judge receives the input, the output or outputs, and
instructions, and returns a score (**pointwise**), a preference between two outputs (**pairwise**), or scores on several
criteria (**rubric-based**) <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />.
</Definition>

## Why recommenders need it

Recommenders increasingly generate text: explanations, conversational replies, summaries of reviews. List metrics such
as <Gloss name="ndcg" /> do not apply to text, overlap metrics such as <Gloss name="bleu" /> agree poorly with human
judgement, and human studies are slow and expensive. For item lists too, offline metrics inherit exposure bias and
online tests are costly. An LLM judge instead rates how well recommendations fit a user.

## Designs

- **Pointwise scoring** rates one output against a rubric.
- **Pairwise comparison** asks which of two outputs is better, which is often more reliable than absolute scores
  <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />.
- **Rubrics** decompose the judgement into dimensions such as fluency, faithfulness and relevance and combine them;
  LLM-Rubric calibrates the combination to individual human judges <Cite id="hashemi2024llmrubric" />.
- **Multiple agents** debate or deliberate in roles, such as critic and defender, before a verdict
  <Cite id="chan2023chateval" />.
- **Personalised judges** condition on a description of the user. Fabbri et al. distilled 90 days of podcast listening
  into a natural-language profile and asked the judge whether recommended episodes fit it. In a study with 47
  participants the profile-aware judge matched human judgements with high fidelity and matched or beat a judge given the
  raw listening history. Longer profiles helped: accuracy rose from 0.51 with profiles built from 5 episodes to 0.59
  with 20 <Cite id="fabbri2025podcast" />.

## Validating the judge

A judge is itself a model that must be evaluated before its scores are trusted.

- **Agreement with humans.** Raw agreement, precision and recall, and chance-corrected agreement such as
  <NoteLink to="cohens-kappa">Cohen's kappa</NoteLink>.
- **Decision-level agreement.** Fabbri et al. report the judge's <Gloss name="auroc">ROC-AUC</Gloss> on user–episode
  relevance and **model selection agreement**, the fraction of cases where the judge prefers the same recommender as the
  human annotators <Cite id="fabbri2025podcast" />.
- **Indeterminacy.** For many items, several ratings are reasonable. Forcing humans and the judge to choose one rating
  per item biases validation; Guerdan et al. show that forced-choice validation can select judges up to 31% worse than
  validation with multi-label responses <Cite id="guerdan2025indeterminacy" />.

## Biases and attacks



- **<Gloss name="position-bias">Position bias</Gloss>.** In pairwise comparisons the verdict can depend on which answer
  is shown first; swapping the order changes results <Cite id="wang2023notfair" /> <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />.
- **Verbosity and self-preference.** Judges tend to favour longer answers and answers from their own model family
  <Cite id="zheng2023judge" />.
- **Superficial cues.** Authority cues such as fabricated references and presentation changes shift verdicts
  independently of content <Cite id="chen2024judgebias" />.
- **Master keys.** Content-free inputs such as a colon or "Let's solve this problem step by step" can elicit positive
  verdicts from reward-model judges, including leading proprietary models <Cite id="zhao2025masterkeys" />.

When a recommender is optimised against a judge, these weaknesses become targets: the system learns to please the judge
rather than the user, as Goodhart's law predicts.

## Mitigations

- Use explicit rubrics, reasoning before a verdict, and a few worked examples in the prompt.
- Randomise or swap the order in pairwise comparisons and average <Cite id="wang2023notfair" />.
- Use committees of different models, and allow the judge to abstain when unsure.
- Keep humans in the loop: a golden set of human-labelled items to validate against, adversarial red-teaming, and expert
  review of disagreements.

## Pitfalls

- **Stale context.** A judge built on historical behaviour cannot judge a recommendation for a trend that started after
  the history it was given.
- **Judging the judge on the same data.** Validate on items the prompt was not tuned on.
