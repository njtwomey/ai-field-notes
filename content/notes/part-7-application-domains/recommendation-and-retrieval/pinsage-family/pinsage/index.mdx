<Definition>
**PinSage** is a graph convolutional network that computes an embedding $\zvec_u \in \reals^{d}$ for every node $u$ of a
bipartite graph of pins (image items) and boards (user-curated collections of pins). Each layer aggregates a node's
**importance-based neighbourhood**: the $T$ nodes most often visited by short <NoteLink to="random-walk">random
walks</NoteLink> from $u$. The aggregation is a mean weighted by those visit counts, called **importance pooling**.
Training minimises a max-margin ranking loss on pairs of pins engaged with in sequence, with hard negatives added by a
curriculum. Pinterest trained and ran it on a graph with 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Pinterest wants an embedding for each of about 2 billion pins, for related-pin retrieval by
<NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> and as a feature in ranking models. Each pin
has an image and text annotations. Pins are also connected through boards: the graph has 2 billion pins, 1 billion
boards and over 18 billion pin–board edges <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

A spectral <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolutional network</NoteLink> multiplies features by
powers of the full normalised adjacency matrix. That requires the whole graph during training, which is infeasible at
this size. <NoteLink to="graphsage">GraphSAGE</NoteLink> removes the requirement by computing each node's embedding from
a sampled, fixed-size neighbourhood and shared aggregation weights <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />. PinSage keeps
that design and changes four things: how neighbourhoods are sampled, how they are pooled, how negatives are chosen, and
how training and inference are run on commodity hardware <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## The convolution

A PinSage layer, called `convolve`, maps node $u$'s current vector $\zvec_u$, its neighbours' vectors
$\set{\zvec_v : v \in \Ncal(u)}$ and neighbour weights $\set{\alpha_v}$ to a new vector
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />:

$$
\nvec_u = \gamma\paren{\set{\operatorname{ReLU}(\Qmat\zvec_v + \qvec) : v \in \Ncal(u)}, \alphavec}, \qquad
\zvec_u^{\mathrm{new}} = \frac{\operatorname{ReLU}(\Wmat\,[\zvec_u \Vert \nvec_u] + \wvec)}
{\norm{\operatorname{ReLU}(\Wmat\,[\zvec_u \Vert \nvec_u] + \wvec)}_2}.
$$

Here $[\cdot \Vert \cdot]$ is concatenation, $\Qmat, \qvec, \Wmat, \wvec$ are the layer's weights and $\gamma$ is a
symmetric pooling function. The weights are shared by every node and differ between layers. Write $\hvec_u^{(k)}$ for
node $u$'s vector after $k$ layers, with $\hvec_u^{(0)} = \xvec_u$, the input feature vector. Layer $k$ applies
`convolve` with the vectors $\hvec^{(k-1)}$ as its inputs $\zvec$, and its output $\zvec_u^{\mathrm{new}}$ is
$\hvec_u^{(k)}$. After $K$ layers a two-layer network gives the final embedding,
$\zvec_u = \Gmat_2 \operatorname{ReLU}(\Gmat_1 \hvec_u^{(K)} + \gvec)$. The paper reports that concatenating $\zvec_u$
with $\nvec_u$ works better than averaging them, and that the final normalisation stabilises training and makes
<Gloss name="ann-search">approximate nearest-neighbour search</Gloss> more efficient <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

For each pin, $\xvec_u$ concatenates a 4,096-dimensional visual embedding (the sixth fully connected layer of a VGG-16
classifier), a 256-dimensional Word2Vec-style embedding of its annotations and the log of its degree. Only pins have
features, so the model uses an even number of layers: pins feed boards, boards feed pins. Two layers are enough for a
pin to see other pins through the boards they share <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Importance-based neighbourhoods

GraphSAGE samples neighbours uniformly from the one-hop neighbourhood. PinSage instead simulates short random walks from
$u$, counts how often each node is visited, normalises the counts to sum to 1 and keeps the $T$ nodes with the highest
normalised counts <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />. The walks come from Pinterest's
<NoteLink to="pixie-random-walk">Pixie</NoteLink> system <Cite id="eksombatchai2018pixie" />.
The neighbourhood has a fixed size $T$, so the memory of each minibatch is bounded, and each neighbour carries
a score of how strongly the graph connects it to $u$, which the pooling step uses.

**Why visit counts measure importance.** Let $\Pmat$ be the <NoteLink to="markov-chain">transition matrix</NoteLink> of
the walk on the graph, so $(\Pmat^k)_{uv}$ is the probability that a walk from $u$ is at $v$ after $k$ steps. Suppose
each walk continues after every step with probability $1 - c$ and stops otherwise, so walk lengths are geometric. The
expected number of visits to $v \ne u$ during one walk is the sum over steps of the probability that the walk is still
running and is at $v$:

$$
\expect[\text{visits to } v] = \sum_{k \ge 1} (1-c)^{k-1} (\Pmat^k)_{uv}
= \frac{1}{c(1-c)} \cdot c \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-c)^k (\Pmat^k)_{uv}
= \frac{\pi_u(v)}{c(1-c)}.
$$

The $k = 0$ term is zero because $v \ne u$. The vector $\pivec_u = c \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-c)^k \evec_u\transpose \Pmat^k$
is the **personalised PageRank** of $u$, where $\evec_u$ is the indicator vector of node $u$ and $\pi_u(v)$ is entry
$v$. It is the stationary distribution of a walk that restarts at $u$ with probability $c$ at each step. It satisfies
$\pivec_u = c\,\evec_u\transpose + (1-c)\,\pivec_u \Pmat$, which is the fixed-point form of the same series. By the
<Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss>, the total count at $v$ over many walks, divided by the number of walks,
converges to the expectation. The counts are therefore proportional to $\pi_u(v)$, and so are the normalised counts.
This is the paper's statement that in the limit of infinitely many simulations the normalised counts approximate
personalised PageRank scores with respect to $u$ <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Importance pooling

PinSage sets $\gamma$ to a weighted mean, with weight $\alpha_v$ proportional to $v$'s normalised visit count
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />:

$$
\nvec_u = \sum_{v \in \Ncal(u)} \alpha_v \operatorname{ReLU}(\Qmat\zvec_v + \qvec), \qquad
\alpha_v = \frac{\pi_u(v)}{\sum_{w \in \Ncal(u)} \pi_u(w)}.
$$



**Worked example.** Take $T = 3$ neighbours with visit counts 6, 3 and 1. The weights are
$\alphavec = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1)$. If the transformed neighbour vectors are $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$, the pooled
vector is $0.6\,(1, 0) + 0.3\,(0, 1) + 0.1\,(1, 1) = (0.7, 0.4)$. A plain mean would give $(0.67, 0.67)$ to two
decimals, which lets the rarely visited third neighbour pull the result as much as the most visited one.

## Training

**Positive pairs.** A labelled pair $(q, i)$ is a pin $i$ that a user interacted with immediately after pin $q$. The
training set has 1.2 billion such pairs <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Loss.** For a positive pair, PinSage uses a max-margin (hinge) ranking loss over negatives $n_k$ drawn from a
negative distribution $P_n(q)$, with margin $\Delta$ <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />:

$$
J(\zvec_q, \zvec_i) = \expect_{n_k \sim P_n(q)} \max\set{0,\; \zvec_q\transpose\zvec_{n_k} - \zvec_q\transpose\zvec_i + \Delta}.
$$

Each term is zero once the positive's score beats the negative's by at least $\Delta$. Its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with respect to $\zvec_q$ is $\zvec_{n_k} - \zvec_i$ while the margin is
violated and $\zeros$ afterwards. A negative contributes to learning only while it scores within $\Delta$ of the
positive. The loss is a <NoteLink to="triplet-and-margin-losses">triplet margin loss</NoteLink> on
<NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink>.

**Shared negatives.** Each minibatch samples 500 negatives shared by all its examples, instead of separate negatives per
example. The paper observed no difference in quality and fewer embeddings to compute <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Why random negatives are too easy.** The system must rank about 1,000 relevant pins at the top of 2 billion. Take the
1,000 pins most related to $q$. The probability that a single uniform negative falls among them is
$1{,}000 / (2 \times 10^9) = 5 \times 10^{-7}$. The probability that at least one of 500 independent uniform negatives
does is $1 - (1 - 5 \times 10^{-7})^{500} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-4}$. Almost every minibatch therefore contains no
negative that is close to the query, and the hinge terms are almost all zero. The paper states the same point as a
resolution of 1 in 500 against a required resolution of 1 in 2 million <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Hard negatives.** For each positive pair, PinSage adds hard negatives: pins ranked 2,000 to 5,000 by personalised
PageRank from $q$, sampled at random from that range. They are related to $q$ but less related than $i$. Training on
them from the start doubled the number of epochs needed to converge <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Curriculum.** The first epoch uses no hard negatives. Epoch $n$ adds $n - 1$ hard negatives per item, so the task
gets harder as training proceeds, a form of curriculum learning <Cite id="bengio2009curriculum" />. Early epochs learn
the coarse structure from easy negatives, while the hinge terms are still non-zero for them. Later epochs refine the
fine distinctions that only hard negatives expose <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Systems

**Producer–consumer minibatches.** The adjacency lists and features of billions of nodes stay in <Gloss name="cpu" />
memory (500 GB in total). For each minibatch a CPU producer samples neighbourhoods, re-indexes the nodes involved into a
small subgraph, extracts the matching rows of the feature matrix and samples negatives. The <Gloss name="gpu" />
consumer receives this subgraph and runs the convolutions without further CPU–GPU communication. CPU work for the next
minibatch overlaps GPU work on the current one, which almost halved training time <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**MapReduce inference.** Computing each node's embedding separately recomputes shared neighbours many times. PinSage
instead computes layer by layer. One MapReduce job projects every pin with the first layer's dense transform. A second
job joins pins to their boards and pools them into board vectors. Two more jobs compute the next layer's pin vectors
from the boards. Every node's vector is computed once per layer, so the cost is linear in the number of edges rather
than in the number of nodes times the size of a $K$-hop neighbourhood <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Inductive training.** The model's parameters do not depend on the number of nodes, so it trains on a subgraph and
embeds the rest from their features and neighbourhoods. A subgraph of a tenth of the graph was enough, at a fraction of
the runtime <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Retrieval.** Related pins are the nearest neighbours of a query pin's embedding, found with
<NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hashing</NoteLink> and a two-level retrieval based on the
Weak AND operator <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Results

The largest single gain came from the neighbourhood. With hard negatives in both models, importance pooling over
random-walk neighbourhoods raised the offline hit rate from 46% to 67% over mean pooling on uniformly sampled neighbours
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />. Larger $T$ helped further, at a roughly proportional cost in training time. In production
<Gloss name="ab-test" plural /> on the home feed, PinSage embeddings raised the repin rate by 10% to 30% over
content-only embeddings <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

The embeddings also spread out more. Cosine similarities between random pairs of PinSage embeddings are less peaked than
those of the <NoteLink to="content-based-filtering">content embeddings</NoteLink> <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />. A wider
spread separates items of different relevance, and it lowers the collision rate of
<NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hashing</NoteLink>, which makes retrieval cheaper.

## Compared with LightGCN

| property       | LightGCN                   | PinSage                                        |
| -------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| node inputs    | trainable ID embeddings    | content features                               |
| neighbourhood  | all neighbours, full graph | top $T$ by random-walk visits                  |
| layer          | linear, normalised sum     | dense layers, importance pooling, concatenation |
| new nodes      | need retraining            | embedded from features                         |
| intended scale | benchmark graphs           | billions of nodes                              |

<NoteLink to="lightgcn">LightGCN</NoteLink> shows that when nodes are IDs without features, transformation layers and
non-linearities hurt <Gloss name="collaborative-filtering">collaborative filtering</Gloss>. PinSage shows that with rich
content features at web scale they help. The two results agree: whether to transform depends on whether nodes have
features worth transforming.

## Pitfalls


- **Walk settings change the model.** Walk length, number of walks and $T$ decide which signals reach a node.
- **Labels from the incumbent system.** The positive pairs came from engagement with Pixie's recommendations, so the
  paper compares with Pixie only in human evaluation, not offline <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.
