<Definition>
**Bias in recommender feedback** is any systematic difference between the interactions a system logs and users' true
preferences. It arises because the feedback is **observational**: the system chooses which items each user sees and
where, and users can only react to those. The resulting biases are confounding by the presentation policy
<Cite id="chen2023biassurvey" />.
</Definition>

## Implicit feedback as observational data

<NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">Implicit feedback</NoteLink> is abundant, cheap and continuously
generated, and it is what modern personalisation learns from. It is also not a clean measure of preference. In a
controlled experiment one would show items to users uniformly at random and read off their preferences. A live system
cannot do that without a poor experience, so it shows ranked lists of items it already believes relevant. That ranking
determines which interactions appear in the data the next model is trained on. Fitting models to such data without
correcting for the presentation policy makes offline performance a poor guide to online performance and causes lost
trust.

## One structure, many names

The named biases are not separate phenomena. Each is a case of the same causal structure.

<ConfoundingDag />

The variable of interest, relevance $R$, is confounded by the presentation $Z$ that the logging policy chose. Position
influences clicks independently of relevance. Because the policy puts popular or previously successful items in
prominent positions, features such as popularity become spuriously correlated with clicks. A model trained on clicks
learns the policy along with the preferences. Recognising this structure moves the field from heuristic fixes to the
methods of <NoteLink to="causal-inference-from-observational-data">causal inference</NoteLink>, which exist to separate
cause from correlation in observational data.

## A taxonomy

Surveys list four main biases <Cite id="chen2023biassurvey" />.

- **Selection bias.** The training data are not a representative sample of preferences, because users choose what to
  interact with from a list the system pre-selected. In personal search, queries with no clicks may be dropped from
  training, leaving only queries where users found something <Cite id="wang2016selection" />. Explicit ratings are
  biased the same way: users rate what they chose to watch, and rate more readily what they loved or hated. The data are
  <NoteLink to="missing-data-and-imputation">missing not at random</NoteLink> <Cite id="schnabel2016treatments" />.
- **Position bias.** Users examine and click higher positions more, whatever the items' relevance. Joachims et al.
  called this trust bias: users trust that the top results are best <Cite id="joachims2007clicks" />. Rank then affects
  <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rate</Gloss> independently of relevance. See
  <NoteLink to="click-models">click models</NoteLink>.
- **Exposure bias.** Users can interact only with items they were shown. An unobserved interaction is ambiguous: seen
  and not wanted, or never seen. Treating all unobserved pairs as negatives, as early models did, penalises every item
  the current system does not show.
- **Popularity bias.** Popular items are over-represented in logs, so models learn that recommending them is a safe way
  to reduce loss and under-recommend niche items that would suit particular users. This reduces personalisation and
  diversity. See <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink>.

The biases interact and compound over time through <NoteLink to="feedback-loops-in-recommendation">feedback
loops</NoteLink>.

## Consequences

- **Offline evaluation misleads.** A metric computed on logged clicks rewards models that imitate the logging policy.
  <NoteLink to="counterfactual-evaluation-of-recommenders">Counterfactual evaluation</NoteLink> estimates what a new
  recommender would earn instead.
- **The rich get richer.** Items shown often gather more interactions and are shown more.
- **Unfair exposure.** Providers of niche or new items receive less exposure than their relevance merits, and users
  with non-mainstream tastes receive worse recommendations.

The remedy starts with the probability that an interaction was observable at all, the
<NoteLink to="propensity-in-recommendation">propensity</NoteLink>, and continues with the methods in the
<NoteLink to="debiasing-toolkit">debiasing toolkit</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Correcting one bias at a time.** Position, popularity and selection act together. A position correction that ignores
  exposure still learns only about items that were shown.
- **Assuming randomisation where there was none.** A holdout of "random" recommendations that still filters by
  eligibility rules is not uniform; its propensities must be computed, not assumed.
