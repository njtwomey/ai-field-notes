<Definition>
**Collaborative filtering** predicts a user's interest in an item from the recorded interactions of many users with
many items, without features of either. It rests on one assumption: users who agreed in the past will agree again.
The data form a sparse matrix with a row per user and a column per item, and the methods fill in its missing entries.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Collaborative filtering recommends items that no content feature would link, such as a documentary to a fan of a
particular comedy, because the same people watch both. It needs only logs of ratings, clicks or purchases, so it
applies to any catalogue. It is the core of most recommenders and the baseline that newer models must beat. It fails
for users and items with no history, which is the subject of
<NoteLink to="cold-start-content-and-context" /> <Cite id="koren2009mf" />.

## Main ideas

**Recommend what neighbours liked.** <NoteLink to="neighbourhood-collaborative-filtering" /> predicts a rating from the
ratings that similar users gave the item, or from the user's ratings of similar items. Similarities such as cosine are
computed directly from the <NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">interaction matrix</NoteLink>. Item-based
neighbourhoods are stable and easy to explain.

**Compress the matrix into latent factors.** <NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation" /> approximates the
ratings matrix as a product of user factors and item factors, so each user and item gets a vector of $K$ numbers and a
rating is predicted by their inner product. The factors are fitted to the observed entries only, with an $\ell_2$
penalty, which makes this different from a plain singular value decomposition of a full matrix.

**Fit by exact alternating solves.** <NoteLink to="alternating-least-squares" /> fixes the item factors and solves one
ridge regression per user, then swaps roles. Every solve is exact and independent of the others, so each sweep
parallelises across machines.

**Treat absences as weak negatives.** <NoteLink to="weighted-matrix-factorisation" /> fits implicit feedback over every
user–item cell. Observed interactions are targets of 1 with a high confidence weight, and unobserved cells are targets
of 0 with a small weight <Cite id="hu2008implicit" />.

**Rank instead of regress.** <NoteLink to="bayesian-personalised-ranking" /> (BPR) asks that each user score an item
they interacted with above one they did not. It maximises a smooth surrogate of per-user AUC over sampled triples of
user, positive item and negative item <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.

## How the notes fit together

Read the neighbourhood note first; it is the simplest method and needs no training. Matrix factorisation follows, and
alternating least squares is one way to fit it. Weighted matrix factorisation and BPR are the two standard adaptations
to implicit feedback: the first keeps a <NoteLink to="pointwise-recommendation-losses">pointwise loss</NoteLink> and
reweights cells, the second switches to a pairwise loss.
