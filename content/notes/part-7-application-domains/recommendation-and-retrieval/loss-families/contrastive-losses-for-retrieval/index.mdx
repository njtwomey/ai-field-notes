<Definition>
A **contrastive retrieval loss** trains a query encoder and an item encoder so that each query's logged item scores
higher than a set of negative items. For a query with positive item $y$ and candidates $\mathcal{C} \ni y$, scores
$s_j$ and temperature $\tau > 0$, the <Gloss name="infonce" /> loss is

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{e^{s_y/\tau}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}} e^{s_j/\tau}},
$$

the cross-entropy of picking $y$ out of $\mathcal{C}$ <Cite id="oord2018" />. In retrieval the candidates are usually
the other items of the training batch, and the score is a dot product or a
<NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">cosine similarity</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A retriever must place a user's items near the user's vector and everything else further away. The loss cannot score
every item in the catalogue for every example, so it contrasts the positive with a handful of others and rewards the
positive for scoring higher than they do. Each negative is a sample of "everything else".

The softmax makes the negatives compete. A negative that already scores far below the positive holds almost no
probability and receives almost no <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. A negative that scores close to the
positive holds much of the probability and is pushed hard. The temperature controls how sharply this happens.

## Details

### InfoNCE in a two-tower model

A <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink> maps query $b$ to $\uvec_b \in \reals^d$ and item $j$ to
$\vvec_j \in \reals^d$. A batch holds $B$ positive pairs $(b, y_b)$. Write $s_{bc} = \uvec_b\transpose\vvec_{y_c}$
for the score of query $b$ against the positive item of pair $c$, forming a $B \times B$ matrix whose diagonal holds
the positives. The in-batch InfoNCE loss is the mean over rows of a softmax cross-entropy:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{q \to i} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \paren{-\frac{s_{bb}}{\tau} + \log\sum_{c=1}^{B} e^{s_{bc}/\tau}}.
$$

This is a <NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">sampled softmax</NoteLink> over the catalogue with the
batch as the sample. The batch items are drawn in proportion to their frequency $q_j$ among positives, so without a
correction the loss learns scores penalised by $\log q_j$. Subtracting $\tau \log q_{y_c}$ from each $s_{bc}$ before
dividing by $\tau$, the logQ correction, removes the penalty <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />.

### The link to mutual information

Take a query $\xvec$ and its positive $y_1$ from the joint distribution of queries and items, and $B - 1$ negatives
$y_2, \dots, y_B$ independently from the item marginal $p(y)$. For any critic $f(\xvec, y) = s/\tau$,

$$
I(X; Y) \ge \log B - \expect[\mathcal{L}],
$$

and the critic that minimises the expected loss is $f^*(\xvec, y) = \log p(y \mid \xvec) / p(y) + c(\xvec)$
<Cite id="oord2018" /> <Cite id="poole2019" />. In-batch items are samples from the item marginal, so the uncorrected
loss learns the <Gloss name="pmi">pointwise mutual information</Gloss> between query and item. This is the same
$-\log q_j$ penalty that the sampled-softmax analysis gives, reached by a different route. The bound can never exceed
$\log B$, so a larger batch allows a tighter bound. The full proof is in <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive
learning</NoteLink>.

### The gradient

Fix one query $b$ and drop the index. Let $p_c = e^{s_c/\tau} / \sum_{c'} e^{s_{c'}/\tau}$ be the softmax weight of
candidate $c$, with $c = y$ the positive. The loss is $\mathcal{L} = -s_y/\tau + \log\sum_c e^{s_c/\tau}$. The
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the log-sum-exp with respect to $s_c$ is $p_c/\tau$, so

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s_y} = -\frac{1 - p_y}{\tau}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s_c} = \frac{p_c}{\tau} \quad (c \ne y).
$$

The positive is pulled up by the probability it lacks. Each negative is pushed down by the probability it holds. The
pushes on the negatives sum to $(1 - p_y)/\tau$, the pull on the positive. With dot-product scores
$s_c = \uvec\transpose\vvec_c$, the chain rule gives the gradients on the vectors:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \uvec} = \frac{1}{\tau}\paren{\sum_c p_c \vvec_c - \vvec_y}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \vvec_c} = \frac{p_c - \indicator[c = y]}{\tau}\,\uvec.
$$

A gradient step moves the query vector towards its positive item and away from the softmax-weighted mean of the
candidates.

### What the temperature does

Two negatives $c$ and $k$ receive pushes in the ratio

$$
\frac{p_c}{p_k} = \exp\paren{\frac{s_c - s_k}{\tau}}.
$$

A small $\tau$ makes the ratio large, so the gradient concentrates on the negatives that score closest to the positive,
the hard negatives. A large $\tau$ spreads it evenly. Wang and Liu show that the temperature controls this hardness
weighting and that it trades the uniformity of the embedding against tolerance of semantically similar negatives
<Cite id="wang2021" />.

The two limits make this exact. Write $\bar s$ for the mean score over the $B$ candidates. As $\tau \to \infty$, a
first-order expansion $e^{s/\tau} \approx 1 + s/\tau$ gives $\log\sum_c e^{s_c/\tau} \approx \log B + \bar s/\tau$, so

$$
\mathcal{L} \approx \log B - \frac{s_y - \bar s}{\tau}.
$$

Every negative is pushed with equal weight $1/(B\tau)$, whatever its score. As $\tau \to 0$, the log-sum-exp
$\tau \log\sum_c e^{s_c/\tau}$ tends to $\max_c s_c$, so

$$
\tau\mathcal{L} \to \max_c s_c - s_y = \max\paren{0, \max_{c \ne y} s_c - s_y}.
$$

This is a <NoteLink to="triplet-and-margin-losses">triplet hinge loss</NoteLink> with margin zero against the single
hardest negative. The temperature interpolates between a loss that treats all negatives alike and one that sees only
the hardest.

### Cosine versus dot-product scores

Cosine scores normalise both vectors to unit length, so $s \in [-1, 1]$. The logits $s/\tau$ then differ by at most
$2/\tau$, and the positive's probability is at most

$$
p_y \le \frac{e^{1/\tau}}{e^{1/\tau} + (B - 1)e^{-1/\tau}} = \frac{1}{1 + (B - 1)e^{-2/\tau}}.
$$

With $B = 1024$ and $\tau = 1$ this is $0.0072$, and the loss cannot fall below $4.94$ nats. At $\tau = 0.1$ the
floor is $2 \times 10^{-6}$ nats. Cosine-scored losses need a small temperature to express confident predictions.
<Gloss name="clip" /> learns $\tau$ as a parameter <Cite id="radford2021" />.

Dot-product scores are unbounded, and a vector's norm becomes a free parameter. A large item norm raises the item's
score for every query that points its way, so popular items tend to acquire large norms. Cosine scores remove this
channel. Wu et al. find that the cosine form of the sampled softmax loss cannot learn vector magnitudes, which in
recommendation encode item popularity <Cite id="wu2024sampledsoftmaxrec" />.

### Symmetric losses

The loss above reads each row of the score matrix: for each query, pick its item among the batch items. The columns
give the reverse task: for each item, pick its query among the batch queries,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{i \to q} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{c=1}^{B} \paren{-\frac{s_{cc}}{\tau} + \log\sum_{b=1}^{B} e^{s_{bc}/\tau}}.
$$

<Gloss name="clip" /> trains on the average $\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_{q \to i} + \mathcal{L}_{i \to q})$ of
image-to-text and text-to-image losses <Cite id="radford2021" />. In recommendation, the reverse direction trains the
item tower to separate the users who chose an item from other users. It suits symmetric tasks such as item-to-item
retrieval, where both towers may share weights.

### Alignment and uniformity

With unit vectors, $\norm{\uvec - \vvec}^2 = 2 - 2\uvec\transpose\vvec$. Wang and Isola show that, as the number of
negatives grows, the InfoNCE loss splits into two terms <Cite id="wang2020" />. **Alignment** rewards small distances
between positive pairs. **Uniformity** penalises, for a scale $t > 0$ (Wang and Isola use $t = 2$), the log of the mean
of $e^{-t\norm{\vvec - \vvec'}^2}$ over pairs of encodings, which is smallest when the encodings are spread evenly over
the sphere. DirectAU trains a recommender on these two terms directly, with the squared distance between user and item
vectors as alignment and a uniformity term for users and one for items, and no sampled negatives
<Cite id="wang2022directau" />.

### Hard negatives and false negatives

In-batch negatives are mostly easy: a random item rarely resembles a user's choice. **Hard-negative mining** adds
negatives that the current model scores high, for example the top results of a
<NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> over the item index. Robinson et al. sample
negatives with probability proportional to $q_j e^{\beta s_j}$, so that a concentration parameter $\beta \ge 0$ sets the
hardness <Cite id="robinson2021hardnegatives" />.

The failure mode is the **<Gloss name="fn">false negative</Gloss>**: an item the user would like but has not interacted
with. The top of the model's ranking is exactly where unseen relevant items sit, so the hardest mined negatives are the
most likely to be false. In Facebook's embedding-based search, sampling hard negatives from rank positions 101 to 500 of
the model's results gave better recall than taking the hardest ones, and easy negatives remained necessary
<Cite id="huang2020ebr" />.

**Debiased contrastive loss** corrects for false negatives in expectation <Cite id="chuang2020debiased" />. Let
$\pi^+$ be the probability that a randomly drawn item is also a positive for the query. The marginal over candidates is
a mixture of the positive distribution $p^+$ and the <Gloss name="tn">true-negative</Gloss> distribution $p^-$:
$p = \pi^+ p^+ + (1 - \pi^+) p^-$. Solving for the expectation under true negatives gives

$$
\expect_{p^-}\brack{e^{s/\tau}} = \frac{\expect_{p}\brack{e^{s/\tau}} - \pi^+ \expect_{p^+}\brack{e^{s/\tau}}}{1 - \pi^+}.
$$

The loss replaces the average over sampled negatives with this estimate, using the sampled negatives for the first
expectation and extra positives of the same query, such as the user's other items, for the second. The estimate is
clipped below at $e^{-1/\tau}$, the smallest value $e^{s/\tau}$ can take with cosine scores
<Cite id="chuang2020debiased" />.

## Worked example

A batch holds three users and their three items. The cosine scores, rows for users and columns for items, are

$$
\Smat = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.3 & 0.1 \\ 0.5 & 0.6 & 0.2 \\ 0.0 & 0.4 & 0.7 \end{pmatrix},
$$

with each user's positive on the diagonal.

**At $\tau = 1$.** Row 2's softmax is $(0.351, 0.388, 0.260)$. User 2's loss is $-\log 0.388 = 0.946$ nats. The three
row losses average $0.832$. The gradients of user 2's loss with respect to its scores are $(0.351, -0.612, 0.260)$:
the two negatives are pushed down by similar amounts.

**At $\tau = 0.1$.** Row 2's softmax is $(0.265, 0.721, 0.013)$ and user 2's loss is $0.327$ nats. The gradients are
$(2.654, -2.786, 0.132)$. Item 1, at score $0.5$, is pushed $e^{(0.5 - 0.2)/0.1} = e^3 \approx 20$ times harder than
item 3, at $0.2$. If user 2 would in fact like item 1, a false negative, the low temperature spends most of the
update pushing it away.

**Symmetric loss at $\tau = 0.1$.** The column softmaxes give item-to-user losses $(0.049, 0.170, 0.009)$, averaging
$0.076$. The row losses average $0.128$. The symmetric loss is their mean, $0.102$.

the slate, the InfoNCE loss with $\tau = 1$ and the slate as the candidate set. Compare its gradient with the
<NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">pairwise logistic loss</NoteLink> and pointwise <Gloss name="bce">binary
cross-entropy</Gloss>: the softmax pushes each negative in proportion to its probability, so the highest-scoring
negatives receive most of the push.

## Assumptions

- Negatives are samples of items the query did not choose. With <NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">implicit
  feedback</NoteLink>, an unobserved item is unlabelled, not negative, so some negatives are false.
- The proposal of the negatives is known or estimable, if the loss is to be corrected towards the full softmax.
- For the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual-information</NoteLink> reading, negatives are independent draws from
  the item marginal.

## Pitfalls

- **Duplicates in a batch.** When the same item is the positive for two queries in a batch, each copy is a negative
  for the other query. Mask duplicate items, and duplicate users in the symmetric loss.
- **Temperature and <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink> interact.** The number of negatives
  and $\tau$ together decide which negatives dominate the gradient. Change one and retune the other.
- **Uncorrected popularity penalty.** In-batch negatives without the <Gloss name="logq-correction">logQ
  correction</Gloss> learn scores penalised by $\log q_j$, which under-recommends popular items. Zhou et al. argue that
  a contrastive loss with negatives drawn from the popularity distribution acts as an inverse-propensity correction for
  exposure bias, which can be wanted <Cite id="zhou2021clrec" />. Decide which behaviour is intended.
- **Mining the hardest negatives.** The top of the model's own ranking holds unseen relevant items. Mine from further
  down the ranking and keep easy negatives in the mix <Cite id="huang2020ebr" />.
- **Reading $\log B - \mathcal{L}$ as mutual information.** The bound saturates at $\log B$ and is a poor estimate
  when the true mutual information is large <Cite id="poole2019" />.
