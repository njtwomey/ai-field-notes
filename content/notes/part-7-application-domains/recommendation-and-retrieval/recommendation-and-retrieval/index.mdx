<Definition>
**Recommendation** chooses a short ranked list of items for a user, with no explicit query, from a catalogue that may
hold millions of items. **Retrieval** chooses and ranks documents or items for an explicit query. Both learn a scoring
function from logged interactions and features, and both deploy it in a pipeline that retrieves candidates cheaply and
ranks them carefully.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Recommenders choose what people see on streaming services, shops, social feeds and news sites, and search engines rank
the web, products and documents. These systems are among the largest deployments of machine learning. They raise
problems that most <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink> does not: labels come from the
system's own past choices, the output is a ranked list rather than a single prediction, catalogues are too large to
score in full for every request, and the true objective, long-run user value, is observed only through online
experiments <Cite id="covington2016youtube" />.

## Main ideas

**Learn preferences from many users at once.** <NoteLink to="collaborative-filtering" /> fills in the sparse user–item
matrix by neighbourhoods or by matrix factorisation, the method made standard by the
<NoteLink to="netflix-prize">Netflix Prize</NoteLink> <Cite id="koren2009mf" />.
<NoteLink to="feature-interaction-models" /> add side features through <Gloss name="fm">factorisation machines</Gloss>
and deep crossing networks, and the <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink> of the deep-recommender
chapter scales retrieval with <Gloss name="ann-search">approximate nearest-neighbour search</Gloss>.

**Use structure in the interactions.** <NoteLink to="sequential-recommendation-models" /> predict the next item from the
order of recent interactions. <NoteLink to="graph-based-recommendation" /> propagates signal over the user–item graph,
and <NoteLink to="pinsage-family" /> follows one company's graph and <NoteLink to="sequence-models">sequence
models</NoteLink> to web scale. <NoteLink to="cold-start-content-and-context" /> supplies the content and context
signals that new users and items need. <NoteLink to="generative-recommendation-losses">Generative
recommenders</NoteLink> replace scoring with generating item identifiers.

**Train for order, not for values.** <NoteLink to="recommendation-loss-families" /> compares pointwise, pairwise and
listwise losses and the sampled softmax used for retrieval. What matters is the order of the list, so losses are
surrogates for <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">ranking metrics</NoteLink>.

**Logs are biased by the system that produced them.** <NoteLink to="learning-to-rank-from-clicks" /> models how users
examine a ranked list and corrects clicks for position. <NoteLink to="bias-and-debiasing-in-recommendation" />
generalises this to exposure, popularity and <NoteLink to="feedback-loops">feedback loops</NoteLink>, and evaluates new
policies on old logs with propensity weights.

**Evaluation is part of the method.** <NoteLink to="evaluation-and-beyond-accuracy-objectives" /> covers
<NoteLink to="evaluating-retrieval-systems">test collections</NoteLink>, offline ranking metrics, tuned baselines,
<Gloss name="llmaaj">LLM judges</Gloss> and the objectives beyond relevance: diversity, novelty, coverage and fairness.
Final decisions are made by online experiments on live traffic.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="foundations-of-recommender-systems" />, which includes the map note
<NoteLink to="recommender-systems" />. Collaborative filtering comes next, since every later model extends or competes
with matrix factorisation. Feature-interaction, deep, sequential, graph and cold-start models can then be read in any
order. The loss-family chapter is best read after one or two model families. The click, bias and evaluation chapters
are cross-cutting and assume the foundations only. The chapters on classical, probabilistic and dense retrieval are
planned, and the PinSage family is a self-contained case study.
