<Definition>
A **multi-armed bandit algorithm** chooses one of $K$ arms in each of $T$ rounds, observes a random reward from that arm
only, and uses the rewards seen so far to make the next choice. Its quality is measured by **regret**: the expected
reward lost compared with always pulling the arm of highest mean. Every algorithm balances exploration, pulling arms to
learn their means, against exploitation, pulling the arm that currently looks best.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A website choosing among headlines, an advertiser among creatives, or a clinic among treatments gains from shifting
traffic to the better option as evidence accumulates, instead of splitting it evenly until a test ends. The same
algorithms appear inside recommenders, <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="model-based-reinforcement-learning">Monte Carlo tree search</NoteLink>. Bandit theory gives sharp answers
about how much exploration is necessary: regret can grow as slowly as $\log T$, and no algorithm does better on every
problem <Cite id="lai1985" />.

## Main ideas

**The problem and its yardstick.** The <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit" /> note defines the stochastic bandit, the
regret and its decomposition into the number of pulls of each suboptimal arm times its gap $\Delta_i$. The Lai–Robbins
bound sets the smallest regret any consistent algorithm can achieve, in terms of KL divergences between arms
<Cite id="lattimore2020" />.

**Separate or random exploration.** <NoteLink to="explore-then-commit" /> samples every arm a fixed number of times and
then commits. <NoteLink to="epsilon-greedy" /> exploits except for a random pull with probability $\varepsilon$. Both
are simple, and both need knowledge of the gaps to reach logarithmic regret.

**Optimism under uncertainty.** The <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm" /> (UCB1) pulls the arm with the
highest upper confidence bound on its mean, so an arm is explored exactly as long as it could plausibly be the best.
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-upper-confidence-bound" /> (KL-UCB) uses a tighter, KL-based bound and matches the
Lai–Robbins bound for Bernoulli rewards.

**Sampling from the posterior.** <NoteLink to="thompson-sampling" /> draws one value from each arm's posterior and
pulls the arm with the largest draw. It pulls each arm with its posterior probability of being the best and also
matches the lower bound. <NoteLink to="bayesian-and-frequentist-bandits" /> compares the two ways of scoring an
algorithm, by worst case over fixed means or by average over a prior, and the Gittins index that is optimal in the
Bayesian discounted setting.

**Weaker assumptions about rewards.** <NoteLink to="exp3" /> handles rewards chosen by an adversary, with regret of
order $\sqrt{KT\log K}$ against the best fixed arm <Cite id="bubeck2012" />. <NoteLink to="non-stationary-bandits" />
lets the means drift and forgets old rewards by discounting or a sliding window.

**Different goals.** <NoteLink to="best-arm-identification" /> scores only the final choice of arm, not the reward
earned along the way, and asks for the fewest samples that identify the best arm with a given confidence.
<NoteLink to="bandits-vs-a-b-tests" /> shows that low regret and a precise effect estimate conflict: no design is best
at both.

## How the notes fit together

ε-greedy next, then UCB1, KL-UCB and Thompson sampling, the main algorithms. The Bayesian and frequentist note follows
Thompson sampling. EXP3, non-stationary bandits and best-arm identification each relax one assumption and can be read
