<Definition>
**Interference** occurs when the outcome of one unit depends on the treatment assigned to other units. The standard
analysis of an <Gloss name="ab-test" /> assumes there is none, the **stable unit treatment value assumption** (SUTVA):
each unit's outcome depends only on its own assignment. Under interference, the difference in means between arms
estimates neither the effect of treating everyone nor the effect of treating one user. **Cluster randomisation** assigns
whole groups of connected units, such as cities, markets or communities in a social graph, to the same arm, so that most
interactions happen within an arm <Cite id="hudgens2008" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In a messaging app, a new feature that makes sending messages easier also changes what the recipients receive. If a
user is in control but their friends are in treatment, the control user's behaviour changes too, and the control arm
no longer shows what happens without the feature. In a marketplace with fixed inventory, a treatment that helps
buyers find cheap items lets treated buyers take items that control buyers would have bought. Treatment looks better
than control, partly because control got worse, and the lift disappears when everyone is treated
<Cite id="blake2014" />.

## Details

**A linear model.** Let unit $i$ have treatment indicator $T_i$, and let $f_i$ be the share of its neighbours that are
treated, for friends in a network or competitors for the same supply. Suppose

$$
\expect[Y_i] = \alpha + \beta\,T_i + \gamma f_i.
$$

$\beta$ is the direct effect of a unit's own treatment and $\gamma$ the spillover from its neighbours. The quantity a
launch decision needs is the **global treatment effect**, everyone treated against no one treated:
$(\alpha + \beta + \gamma) - \alpha = \beta + \gamma$.

**User-level randomisation.** Each unit is treated independently with probability $1/2$. A unit's neighbours are
assigned independently of the unit, so $\expect[f_i \mid T_i] = 1/2$ in both arms. The difference in arm means is

$$
\paren{\alpha + \beta + \tfrac{\gamma}{2}} - \paren{\alpha + \tfrac{\gamma}{2}} = \beta.
$$

The spillover appears equally in both arms and cancels. The estimate misses $\gamma$ entirely. When $\gamma < 0$, as
in the marketplace, the test overstates the global effect. When $\gamma > 0$, as in the messaging app, it
understates it.

**Cluster randomisation.** Partition units into clusters and treat each cluster with probability $1/2$. Let a share
$\phi$ of each unit's neighbours lie in its own cluster; the rest lie in other clusters, which are treated
independently with probability $1/2$. A treated unit has $\expect[f_i] = \phi + (1 - \phi)/2$ and a control unit
$\expect[f_i] = (1 - \phi)/2$. The difference in arm means is

$$
\beta + \gamma\paren{\phi + \tfrac{1 - \phi}{2}} - \gamma\,\tfrac{1 - \phi}{2} = \beta + \phi\gamma.
$$

The bias is $-(1 - \phi)\gamma$: the share of interactions that cross cluster boundaries. Clusters that contain most
of each unit's interactions give $\phi$ close to 1. Graph cluster randomisation chooses clusters by partitioning the
social graph to make $\phi$ large <Cite id="ugander2013" />.

**Worked example.** Take $\beta = 2\%$ and $\gamma = 3\%$, so the global effect is 5%. A user-level test estimates
2%. Cluster randomisation with $\phi = 0.6$ estimates $2\% + 0.6 \times 3\% = 3.8\%$. The remaining bias,
$0.4 \times 3\% = 1.2$ points, comes from the 40% of interactions between clusters.

**Variance under cluster randomisation.** The independent units of a cluster-randomised experiment are the clusters. If
outcomes within a cluster of size $m$ have intra-cluster correlation $\rho$, the variance of an arm mean is multiplied
by the design effect $1 + (m - 1)\rho$ relative to independent units; see
<NoteLink to="randomisation-and-analysis-units">randomisation and analysis units</NoteLink>. Large clusters capture more
interference and have fewer independent units, so bias and variance trade off through the choice of clusters. The
analysis must be done at the cluster level, or with <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> clustered
by it.

## Interference through shared training data

<NoteLink to="recommender-systems">Recommender systems</NoteLink> add a form of interference that needs no social link
at all. The arms of an experiment usually retrain on the platform's pooled logs, so data generated by users in arm B
train the model served in arm A, and the reverse. Each algorithm's measured performance then depends on its competitor.
Holtz et al. call this **symbiosis bias**: a violation of SUTVA's "no hidden treatments" condition, confirmed in a large
A/B test of a global recommender. Cluster-randomised designs, data-diverted designs (each arm trains only on its own
users' data) and designs that split both users and items between arms each reduce it, at their own cost
<Cite id="holtz2025symbiosis" />. See <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in
recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Detecting interference

- **Two levels of randomisation.** Randomising some users individually and others by cluster, and comparing the two
  estimates, tests for interference. Under SUTVA both estimate $\beta$; the difference estimates $\phi\gamma$.
- **Varying saturation.** Assigning clusters different treated shares, such as 20% or 80% of their members, and
  comparing untreated units across clusters measures the spillover onto control units <Cite id="hudgens2008" />.
- **Exposure models.** When the spillover depends on a known function of neighbours' assignments, such as the share
  treated, each unit's probability of each exposure can be computed from the design, and
  <NoteLink to="reweighting-biased-samples">inverse-probability weighting</NoteLink> estimates the effect of each
  exposure <Cite id="aronow2017" />.

## Assumptions

- The linear model is an illustration. The qualitative result, that user-level randomisation cancels any spillover
  shared equally by both arms, does not depend on linearity.
- Cluster randomisation assumes that interference between clusters is weak. It does not help when every unit
  interacts with every other, as in a single global auction; there, time-based designs such as
  <NoteLink to="switchback-experiments">switchback experiments</NoteLink> are used.

## Pitfalls

- **Analysing clusters as users.** A cluster-randomised experiment analysed with a user-level standard error has the
  inflated <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> of any unit mismatch.
- **Too few clusters.** With a handful of cities per arm, one unusual city can dominate the comparison. Pairing or
  stratifying similar clusters before randomising reduces this.
- **Assuming no interference because the product is not social.** Shared inventory, shared budgets, pricing
  algorithms and ranking systems that learn from all users create interference without any social feature
  <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
