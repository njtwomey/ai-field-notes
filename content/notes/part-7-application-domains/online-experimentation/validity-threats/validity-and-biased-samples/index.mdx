<Definition>
An experiment is **internally valid** when its estimate equals the causal effect for the users and period it covered,
and **externally valid** when that effect carries over to the users and period of the launch. The threats in this
chapter break one or the other even when randomisation and analysis are done correctly: the effect changes over time,
users affect each other's outcomes, or the sample differs from the population.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The standard analysis of an <Gloss name="ab-test" /> assumes that each user's outcome depends only on that user's own
variant, and that the effect measured during the test is the effect after launch. Both assumptions fail in common
products. A redesigned page draws extra clicks while it is new. A ride-hailing marketplace shares drivers between arms,
so a treatment that wins riders for one arm takes them from the other. An experiment run in one week or on one platform
may not represent the users a launch will reach. The notes here describe each failure and the design or reweighting that
addresses it <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.

## Main ideas

**Effects can change over time.** <NoteLink to="novelty-and-primacy-effects" /> describes treatment effects that start
large and decay as users get used to a change (novelty), or start small or negative and grow as users learn it
(primacy). A short experiment averages over this transient and misestimates the long-run effect. Plotting the effect by
day since first exposure and keeping long-term holdouts reveal it.

**Users can affect each other.** <NoteLink to="interference-and-cluster-randomisation" /> covers interference, where one
user's outcome depends on others' assignments, as in social networks and two-sided marketplaces. A user-level test then
misses the spillover between arms. Randomising whole clusters of connected users captures the spillover within each
cluster, at the cost of fewer independent units <Cite id="hudgens2008" />.

**Time can be randomised instead of users.** <NoteLink to="switchback-experiments" /> assigns whole periods, often per
region, to one variant, so concurrent users cannot interfere. Carryover from the previous period biases the estimate
towards zero, and discarding the start of each period removes it <Cite id="bojinov2023" />.

**A sample can be reweighted to a population.** <NoteLink to="reweighting-biased-samples" /> applies when the
experiment's users differ in mix from the launch population and the effect varies by segment. Weighting each segment's
effect by its population share recovers the population effect. The price is extra variance, measured by the
<NoteLink to="sample-weights">Kish effective sample size</NoteLink>.

## How the notes fit together

The four notes are independent. Read novelty and primacy first, since it is the threat present in almost every
product experiment. The interference note motivates the switchback design, so read it before the switchback note. The
reweighting note concerns external validity and can be read at any point.
