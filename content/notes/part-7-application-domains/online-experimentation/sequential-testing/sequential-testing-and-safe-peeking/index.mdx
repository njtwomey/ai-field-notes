<Definition>
A **sequential test** decides whether to stop an experiment while its data are still arriving, and controls the
probability of a <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss> over the whole sequence of looks rather than at one fixed
sample size. **Safe peeking** means that an analyst may inspect the result at any time, or at planned times, and stop
early, with the error rate still at the stated level $\alpha$.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Experimenters want to stop early: to ship a clear winner sooner, or to end a harmful change. A fixed-sample test forbids
this. Checking it repeatedly and stopping at the first $p < 0.05$ raises the <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive
rate</Gloss> far above 5%, as <NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping" /> shows. Sequential tests make stopping
early legitimate. Clinical trials have used them for decades for interim analyses <Cite id="jennison2000" />, and online
experimentation platforms use them to support dashboards that are watched continuously <Cite id="johari2022" />.

## Main ideas

**Plan the looks and raise the bar at each.** <NoteLink to="group-sequential-designs" /> analyse the data at a small
number of planned interim looks. The experiment stops when the $z$-statistic crosses a boundary that is higher than the
fixed-sample critical value, set so the total false-positive rate over all looks is $\alpha$. An alpha-spending
function fixes how much of $\alpha$ is used by each fraction of the planned sample, so the looks need not be equally
spaced.

**Average the likelihood ratio over possible effects.** The
<NoteLink to="mixture-sequential-probability-ratio-test" /> (mSPRT) averages the likelihood ratio for the effect over a
prior and rejects when the average reaches $1/\alpha$. Under the null hypothesis the averaged ratio is a non-negative
martingale, and Ville's inequality bounds the chance that it ever reaches $1/\alpha$. Its p-values and intervals are
therefore valid at any stopping time, chosen however the analyst likes.

**Evidence as a bet.** <NoteLink to="e-values-and-confidence-sequences" /> generalises the construction. An e-value is a
non-negative statistic whose expectation under the null is at most 1, and exceeding $1/\alpha$ is a level-$\alpha$
test. Products of sequential e-values form test martingales. Inverting them gives confidence sequences: intervals that
cover the true effect at every sample size simultaneously <Cite id="ramdas2023" />.

## How the notes fit together

Read <NoteLink to="group-sequential-designs" /> first. It is the classical approach and uses only the normal
distribution. The mSPRT note introduces continuous monitoring and the martingale argument. The e-value note places the
mSPRT in a general framework and adds confidence sequences, so it comes last.
