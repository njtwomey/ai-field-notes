<Definition>
**Online experimentation** assigns the users of a live product at random to variants of a feature, measures their
behaviour, and infers the causal effect of each variant. Fixed-allocation experiments, the
<Gloss name="ab-test" plural />, estimate an effect for a decision taken at the end.
<NoteLink to="bandits-vs-a-b-tests">Adaptive experiments</NoteLink>, the bandits, shift traffic towards better variants
while the experiment runs.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Most changes to web and app products are tested before launch: a new ranking model, a redesigned page, a different
price display. Intuition about which changes help is unreliable, and the effects that matter are often a fraction of a
percent. Randomised experiments on millions of users detect such effects and attribute them to the change
<Cite id="kohavi2020" />. Bandits apply when each user's experience matters during the test, as when choosing
headlines, adverts or recommendations that must earn reward while they are compared <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.

## Main ideas

**Design decides what the result means.** <NoteLink to="foundations-of-online-experimentation" /> sets the
<Gloss name="oec">overall evaluation criterion</Gloss> and guardrails, the
<NoteLink to="randomisation-and-analysis-units">randomisation and analysis units</NoteLink>, the sample size for a
target power, and the users to analyse. <NoteLink to="interleaving">Interleaving</NoteLink> and bandits are presented as
alternatives for other questions.

**Each metric needs the right variance.** <NoteLink to="a-b-test-analysis" /> computes intervals for
<Gloss name="cvr">conversion rates</Gloss>, heavy-tailed revenue, <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">ratio
metrics</NoteLink> and relative lifts, and reduces variance with pre-experiment data.

**A correct analysis can still answer the wrong question.** <NoteLink to="validity-and-biased-samples" /> treats effects
that change over time, interference between users and samples that differ from the launch population.
<NoteLink to="a-b-test-pitfalls" /> simulates the failures that come from how experiments are run: broken splits,
peeking, many metrics and <NoteLink to="winners-curse">the winner's curse</NoteLink>.

**Stopping early safely.** <NoteLink to="sequential-testing-and-safe-peeking" /> gives
<NoteLink to="group-sequential-designs">group-sequential designs</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="mixture-sequential-probability-ratio-test">always-valid p-values</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="e-values-and-confidence-sequences">confidence sequences</NoteLink>, which keep the
<Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> at its stated level however often the results are checked.

**Adapting the allocation.** <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit-algorithms" /> trade exploration against exploitation to
keep regret low, with <Gloss name="ucb" /> and <Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> as the main
algorithms. <NoteLink to="contextual-bandits-and-off-policy-learning" /> personalise the choice by context and evaluate
new policies on logged data with propensity weights.

## How the notes fit together

The topic has two halves. The A/B-test half runs from the foundations to analysis, then to validity threats, pitfalls
and sequential testing; the last three can be read in any order once analysis is clear. The bandit half runs from
multi-armed to <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandits</NoteLink> and can be read after the foundations
alone. The note on bandits versus A/B tests joins the halves.
