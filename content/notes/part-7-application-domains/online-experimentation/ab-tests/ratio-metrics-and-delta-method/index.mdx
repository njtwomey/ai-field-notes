<Definition>
A **ratio metric** in an experiment randomised by user is a ratio of two per-user totals summed over an arm:

$$
\hat R = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i} = \frac{\bar Y}{\bar X},
$$

where $X_i$ and $Y_i$ are user $i$'s totals, such as page views and clicks. The **delta method** approximates its
variance by linearising the ratio around the true means $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$:

$$
\var(\hat R) \approx \frac{\sigma_Y^2 - 2R\sigma_{XY} + R^2\sigma_X^2}{n\,\mu_X^2}, \qquad R = \frac{\mu_Y}{\mu_X},
$$

with $\sigma_X^2$, $\sigma_Y^2$ and $\sigma_{XY}$ the per-user variances and covariance <Cite id="deng2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Clicks per page view looks like a proportion, with page views as trials. It is not one, because page views are not
the randomisation unit. A user who views many pages contributes many correlated trials, and users differ in how likely
they are to click. The independent units are users, and each user contributes a pair $(X_i, Y_i)$. The ratio of two
averages of such pairs is a smooth function of two sample means, and the delta method gives the variance of any smooth
function of sample means.

## Details

**Derivation.** Write $\hat R = g(\bar Y, \bar X)$ with $g(y, x) = y/x$. A first-order
<NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> around $(\mu_Y, \mu_X)$ gives

$$
\hat R - R \approx \frac{1}{\mu_X}(\bar Y - \mu_Y) - \frac{\mu_Y}{\mu_X^2}(\bar X - \mu_X)
= \frac{1}{\mu_X}\paren{\bar Y - R\bar X},
$$

using $\mu_Y - R\mu_X = 0$. The right-hand side is $1/\mu_X$ times the sample mean of the per-user variable
$D_i = Y_i - RX_i$, which has mean zero. The users are independent, so

$$
\var(\hat R) \approx \frac{\var(D)}{n\mu_X^2} = \frac{\sigma_Y^2 - 2R\sigma_{XY} + R^2\sigma_X^2}{n\mu_X^2}.
$$

The estimate replaces every moment by its sample value. The neglected terms are of order $1/n$ relative to those kept,
so the approximation is accurate when $n$ is large and $\bar X$ is far from zero relative to its
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> <Cite id="casella2002" />.

**Linearisation.** The derivation shows that $\hat R$ behaves like the mean of $D_i = Y_i - \hat R X_i$, scaled by
$1/\bar X$. Computing this per-user variable turns a ratio metric into an ordinary per-user mean, so that the same code,
<NoteLink to="variance-reduction">variance-reduction</NoteLink> methods and tests apply to both
<Cite id="budylin2018" />.

**Comparing arms.** The arms are independent, so the variance of $\hat R_T - \hat R_C$ is the sum of the two arms'
delta-method variances. The relative difference $\hat R_T/\hat R_C - 1$ needs one more application of the delta
method; see <NoteLink to="relative-lift">relative lift</NoteLink>.

**Ratio of means and mean of ratios.** $\bar Y/\bar X$ weights each user by their page views. The average of per-user
<Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rates</Gloss> $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i Y_i/X_i$ weights users equally. They
estimate different quantities, and the choice is part of the metric's definition. The mean of ratios is an ordinary
per-user mean and needs no delta method, but it is undefined for users with $X_i = 0$.

## Worked example

An arm has $n = 10{,}000$ users. Page views per user have mean $\mu_X = 5$ and standard deviation $\sigma_X = 4$.
Clicks per user have mean $\mu_Y = 0.5$ and standard deviation $\sigma_Y = 1.2$. The correlation between a user's page
views and clicks is 0.5, so $\sigma_{XY} = 0.5 \times 4 \times 1.2 = 2.4$.

1. **The ratio.** $R = 0.5/5 = 0.1$.
2. **Delta method.** $\var(D) = 1.44 - 2 \times 0.1 \times 2.4 + 0.1^2 \times 16 = 1.12$, so
   $\var(\hat R) = 1.12/(10{,}000 \times 25) = 4.48 \times 10^{-6}$ and the standard error is 0.00212.
3. **Binomial formula.** Treating the 50,000 page views as independent trials gives
   $\sqrt{0.1 \times 0.9/50{,}000} = 0.00134$. It is too small by a factor of 1.58, and the variance by a factor of
   2.49.
4. **Consequence.** In an <Gloss name="aa-test" />, the naive statistic is approximately $\Gauss(0, 2.49)$, so a test at
   $\alpha = 0.05$ rejects with probability $2\paren{1 - \Phi(1.96/1.58)} = 0.214$, where $\Phi$ is the standard
   <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">normal distribution</NoteLink> function. One A/A test in five is declared
   significant.

A simulation checks the approximation. Page views per user were drawn from a
<NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial distribution</NoteLink> with mean 5, each user's click
probability from $\Beta(1, 9)$, and clicks from the binomial distribution given both. Over 2,000 simulated arms of 2,000
users, the standard deviation of $\hat R$ was 0.00375. The delta-method standard error averaged 0.00388 and the binomial
formula 0.00300.

## Assumptions

- **Independent randomisation units.** The pairs $(X_i, Y_i)$ are independent across users.
- **Large samples.** The linearisation and the normal approximation both need many users. Heavy-tailed denominators,
  such as a few users with thousands of page views, slow the convergence.
- **Denominators not affected by the treatment, or both effects reported.** If the treatment changes page views, the
  ratio can rise because the denominator fell. Reporting the numerator and denominator per user alongside the ratio
  shows which moved.

## Pitfalls

- **Binomial variance on the analysis unit.** Any variance formula that treats page views, sessions or impressions as
  independent when users were randomised is too small; see
  <NoteLink to="randomisation-and-analysis-units">randomisation and analysis units</NoteLink>.
- **Bootstrapping page views.** A <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> must resample users with all their page
  views. Resampling page views reproduces the binomial error.
- **Forgetting the covariance.** Omitting $-2R\sigma_{XY}$ overstates the variance when clicks and page views are
  positively correlated, which they usually are.
