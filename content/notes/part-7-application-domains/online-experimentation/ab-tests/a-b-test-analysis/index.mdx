<Definition>
**A/B test analysis** turns the logged outcomes of an experiment into an estimate of the treatment effect, a
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> and a confidence interval. The estimate is usually a difference
or ratio of means between arms. The standard error depends on the metric's distribution and on how its numerator and
denominator relate to the randomisation unit, so each kind of metric needs its own formula.
</Definition>

## What it is for

An experiment's decision rests on whether the interval for the effect excludes zero and on how large the effect might
be. A wrong variance formula gives a wrong interval even when the data are perfect. Too small a variance produces
false wins; too large a variance hides real ones. Online metrics are often skewed, defined per page view rather than
per user, or reported as percentage changes, and each of these breaks the textbook two-sample formula in a different
way <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.

## Main ideas

**Proportions have a known variance.** <NoteLink to="conversion-rate-metrics" /> treats each user as a Bernoulli trial
with variance $p(1 - p)$. Two arms are compared by the two-proportion $z$-test, which is equivalent to the $\chi^2$ test
on the 2×2 table of arm by outcome.

**Heavy tails cost sample size.** <NoteLink to="revenue-and-heavy-tailed-metrics" /> covers metrics that are zero for
most users and very large for a few. Their variance is large, the normal approximation needs very large samples, and an
experiment may need several times more users than for a <Gloss name="cvr">conversion rate</Gloss>. Capping extreme
values reduces the variance but changes the quantity being estimated.

**Ratios of totals need the delta method.** <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method" /> treats metrics such as
clicks per page view, where users are randomised but the metric divides two per-user totals. Treating page views as
independent understates the variance. The delta method linearises the ratio and gives the correct variance from
per-user sums <Cite id="deng2018" />.

**Percentage changes have asymmetric uncertainty.** <NoteLink to="relative-lift" /> reports the effect as
$\mu_T/\mu_C - 1$. The denominator is an estimate too, so its noise must enter the interval. The delta method gives a
symmetric interval; the log ratio and Fieller's method give asymmetric ones.

**Pre-experiment data reduces variance.**
<NoteLink to="controlled-experiments-using-pre-experiment-data" /> (CUPED) subtracts from each user's metric a multiple
of a covariate measured before the experiment, such as the same metric in the previous weeks. The covariate is
independent of assignment, so the estimate stays unbiased. Its variance falls by the factor $1 - \rho^2$, where $\rho$
is the correlation between covariate and metric <Cite id="deng2013" />.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="conversion-rate-metrics" />, the simplest case. Revenue and ratio metrics each relax one of
its assumptions and can be read in either order. The relative-lift note uses the delta method, so it follows the ratio
note. CUPED applies to any of the metrics and can be read last.
