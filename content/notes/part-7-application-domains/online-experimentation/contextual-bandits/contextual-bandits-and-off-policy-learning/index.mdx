<Definition>
A **contextual bandit** observes a context $x_t$, such as a user's features, chooses an action $a_t$, and sees the
reward of that action only. The goal is a **policy** $\pi(x)$ that maps contexts to actions with high expected reward.
**Off-policy learning** pursues the same goal from a log of contexts, actions and rewards collected by an earlier
policy, without running new experiments.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Personalisation is a contextual bandit. A news site picks an article for each visitor, an ad server picks an advert,
a recommender picks a slate, and the best choice depends on who is asking. The system sees the outcome only of what it
showed. Contextual bandit algorithms explore online while personalising <Cite id="li2010" />. Off-policy methods answer
a cheaper question first: how would a new policy have done on the traffic already logged? Done correctly, this screens
candidate policies before any <Gloss name="ab-test" /> <Cite id="dudik2011" />.

## Main ideas

**The policy is the object of learning.** The <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit" /> note defines the problem, the value
$V(\pi)$ of a policy and regret against the best policy, which can beat every single arm because it chooses differently
in different contexts.

**<NoteLink to="linear-models">Linear models</NoteLink> with exploration bonuses or posterior draws.**
<NoteLink to="linucb" /> models the expected reward as linear in features, fits it by ridge regression and pulls the arm
with the highest <Gloss name="ucb">upper confidence bound</Gloss> over an ellipsoid of plausible parameters.
<NoteLink to="linear-thompson-sampling" /> draws a parameter from the Gaussian posterior instead, and is the randomised
counterpart of LinUCB.

**Logs are usable only if they were randomised.** <NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities" /> defines the
propensity, the probability with which the logging policy chose the logged action. A log can evaluate a new policy only
if the old policy gave the new policy's actions positive probability and recorded those probabilities.

**Reweight logged rewards to evaluate a new policy.** <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation" /> estimates a target
policy's value from the log. Inverse propensity scoring (IPS) reweights each reward by the ratio of the two policies'
probabilities and is unbiased but high in variance. Self-normalised IPS reduces the variance, the direct method models
the rewards, and the doubly robust estimator combines the two and stays consistent if either part is correct.

**Optimise the estimate with care.** <NoteLink to="off-policy-learning" /> selects the policy that maximises an
off-policy value estimate. The maximum of a noisy estimate is optimistic, and IPS can favour policies that avoid the
logged actions; variance penalties and self-normalisation correct both <Cite id="lattimore2020" />.
