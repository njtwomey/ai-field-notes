<Definition>
**Choosing an experimentation method** means matching the method to the decision it informs. Four methods cover most
needs of a product such as a recommender: the **A/B test** (causal validation of a change), **interleaving** (relative
comparison of two rankers), the **multi-armed bandit** (earning while learning) and **<Gloss name="rl">reinforcement
learning</Gloss>** (learning a long-term policy). They are complements, not competitors <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
</Definition>

## Four methods, four objectives

| method | primary objective | efficiency | opportunity cost | output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <NoteLink to="a-b-test">A/B test</NoteLink> | causal validation of a <NoteLink to="novelty-and-primacy-effects">long-term effect</NoteLink> | slowest, most data | highest: fixed allocation to losers | absolute lift on business metrics, e.g. +2.1% revenue |
| <NoteLink to="interleaving">interleaving</NoteLink> | which of two rankers users prefer | highest for rankings | low: users still see half the incumbent | relative preference |
| <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">bandit</NoteLink> | reward during the experiment | converges faster than an A/B test | lowest by design | the current best arm |
| <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-for-recommendation">reinforcement learning</NoteLink> | long-term cumulative user value | a separate class | depends on exploration | a policy |

**A/B tests give unbiased effect estimates at the cost of regret.** Random assignment gives an unbiased estimate of the
<NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">average treatment effect</NoteLink> in the live system, with controlled
error rates. The fixed allocation that makes this possible also sends a large share of traffic to worse variants for the
whole test: the regret of <NoteLink to="best-arm-identification">pure exploration</NoteLink>. That cost is worth paying
for long-lived, high-stakes and hard-to-reverse decisions, such as a new core algorithm or a redesign
<Cite id="kohavi2020" />. See <NoteLink to="bandits-vs-a-b-tests">bandits versus A/B tests</NoteLink> for the formal
trade-off between regret and estimation.

## A maturity spectrum

One way to organise the methods is by the phase of development each serves.

1. **Screening.** Many candidate rankers differ in features or hyperparameters. Interleaving compares them quickly with
   little traffic and prunes the pool to one or two.
2. **Validation and launch.** The survivors face a full A/B test against production, which measures their absolute
   effect on revenue, retention and satisfaction. Netflix describes exactly this two-stage process <Cite
   id="aurisset2017interleaving" />.
3. **Continuous, tactical optimisation.** Components with a short shelf life, such as headlines, promotional banners or
   the order of carousels, are run by bandits that minimise regret. An A/B test can still compare a new bandit algorithm
   with the old one.
4. **Strategic, long-horizon optimisation.** Reinforcement learning, as a long-term investment, learns policies for the
   whole user journey and outcomes such as lifetime value.

Hybrids are common. Mui et al. inserted a short <Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson-sampling</Gloss> bandit between
offline evaluation and A/B testing of recommender algorithms at Expedia Group: up to six candidates could be screened
and the winner validated in about five weeks, where serial four-week A/B tests of six candidates would take six months
<Cite id="mui2019bandit" />.

## The cost of slow experimentation

Most ideas do not improve the metrics they target. Kohavi et al. report that at Microsoft about a third of tested ideas
improved their target metrics, and that other large organisations see similar or lower success rates
<Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
With a low success rate, the number of experiments per year sets the rate of improvement.

**Worked example.** With a success rate of 15%, a team that runs one experiment a month finds $12 \times 0.15 = 1.8$
improvements a year. Two a month gives $24 \times 0.15 = 3.6$; one a week gives $52 \times 0.15 = 7.8$. Quadrupling
velocity quadruples the yield.

Throughput also changes what gets tried. A team that can run few experiments tests only safe, incremental changes and
risks a local optimum; a team that can run many can afford bold ideas, some of which turn out to be the breakthroughs.
And experts are poor at predicting which ideas will win, which is why testing many beats choosing a few
<Cite id="kohavi2020" />. Investment in experimentation infrastructure is therefore an investment in the rate of
innovation, not only a cost saving.

## Criteria for choosing a method

1. **Learning or earning?** Durable knowledge for a critical decision calls for an A/B test. Maximum reward during a
   live, time-limited campaign calls for a bandit.
2. **Is it a ranking comparison that needs fast iteration?** Interleaving is the default screen for rankers.
3. **What is the decision's shelf life?** Evergreen decisions (a core algorithm, a redesign) need an A/B test;
   perishable ones (news headlines, promotions) suit bandits. See <NoteLink to="bandits-vs-a-b-tests">bandits versus A/B
   tests</NoteLink>.
4. **One interaction or a long relationship?** A discrete choice point suits a <NoteLink
   to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandit</NoteLink>; a multi-session journey optimised for lifetime value is a
   reinforcement-learning problem.
5. **How mature is the team?** A/B testing is the most accessible; interleaving and bandits need real-time
   infrastructure; reinforcement learning needs deep expertise, <Gloss name="ope">off-policy evaluation</Gloss> and
   heavy infrastructure.

## Pitfalls

- **Screening on the wrong signal.** Interleaving measures click preference. A ranker that wins on clicks can still lose
  on retention; the A/B stage exists to catch that.
- **Ignoring systemic biases.** Every method runs inside the recommender's feedback loops. See
  <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in recommendation</NoteLink>.
