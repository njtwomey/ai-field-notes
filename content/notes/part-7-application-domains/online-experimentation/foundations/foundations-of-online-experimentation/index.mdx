<Definition>
An **online controlled experiment** randomly assigns the users of a live product to two or more variants, records a
metric for each user, and compares the variants to estimate the causal effect of a change. The foundations are the
decisions made before any data arrive: what to measure, what to randomise, how many users to include, which users to
analyse and which method suits the question.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Web and app companies test most product changes this way: a new ranking model, a redesigned checkout page, a different
notification policy. Random assignment makes the variants comparable in every respect except the change, so a
difference in the metric beyond chance is attributed to the change. The design decisions set what that difference
means and how small a difference the experiment can detect. A badly chosen metric or unit makes even a large, clean
experiment answer the wrong question <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.

## Main ideas

**The A/B test is a two-sample comparison.** <NoteLink to="a-b-test" /> runs one experiment end to end: choose the
smallest lift worth detecting, size the sample, run a two-proportion $z$-test and read the confidence interval. The
rest of the chapter refines each of these steps <Cite id="kohavi2009" />.

**Decide what success means before the test.** The <NoteLink to="overall-evaluation-criterion" /> (OEC) is the metric,
or weighted combination of metrics, that the experiment is meant to improve. Guardrail metrics, such as page load time
or error rates, must not get worse, and each is checked by a non-inferiority test against a margin fixed in advance.

**The unit of randomisation sets the variance.**
<NoteLink to="randomisation-and-analysis-units" /> separates the unit assigned to a variant, usually a user, from the
unit a metric averages over, such as a page view. When the analysis unit is finer, its observations are correlated
within each user, and the naive variance is too small by the design effect.

**Sample size follows from the effect worth detecting.** <NoteLink to="a-b-test-power" /> gives the number of users per
arm needed to detect a difference $\delta$ with a chosen power. Combined with daily traffic and the share of users in
the experiment, the same formula fixes how long the test must run.

**Analyse only the users a change can affect.** <NoteLink to="triggering-and-dilution" /> restricts the analysis to
users who met the condition for the change, in both arms. The triggered effect is larger and less noisy; dilution
converts it back to the effect on all users.

**Other methods answer other questions.** <NoteLink to="interleaving" /> merges two rankers' lists into one, so each
user acts as their own control; it needs far fewer users but gives only a preference between rankers.
<NoteLink to="choosing-an-experimentation-method" /> compares A/B tests, interleaving, bandits and
<Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss> by the decision each one informs.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="a-b-test" />, which shows the whole procedure on one example. The OEC, unit and power notes
refine its design and can be read in that order. Triggering assumes the power note. Interleaving and the choice of
method can be read last, since they compare the A/B test with its alternatives.
