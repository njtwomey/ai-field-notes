<Definition>
An **A/B test pitfall** is a way in which an experiment that looks correctly run produces a misleading result: a
significant difference where none exists, an estimate biased by who was logged, or an effect larger than the truth.
Each note in this chapter simulates one pitfall with known ground truth, measures how often it misleads, and gives the
check or correction that prevents it.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The statistics of a single, pre-planned A/B test are simple. The failures come from how experiments are run at scale:
pipelines that drop users, analysts who check results daily, dashboards with dozens of metrics, and teams that ship
whatever reaches significance. These failures are common in practice and invisible in any single result
<Cite id="kohavi2020" />. Simulation shows their size directly, because the true effect is known.

## Main ideas

**Test the test.** The <NoteLink to="a-a-test" /> gives both groups the same experience and runs the usual analysis.
About $\alpha$ of such tests should come out significant, and their p-values should be uniform on $[0, 1]$. Any other
pattern exposes a broken split, metric pipeline or variance formula.

**Check that the split is the planned split.** <NoteLink to="sample-ratio-mismatch" /> (SRM) occurs when the logged
arm sizes differ from the planned allocation by more than chance, as judged by a $\chi^2$ test. The missing users
differ from the rest, so the comparison is biased, and the example produces a significant lift where the true lift is
zero.

**Looking repeatedly inflates <Gloss name="fp">false positives</Gloss>.**
<NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping" /> checks a test after every batch of users and stops at the first
$p < 0.05$. With no real difference the <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> reaches 19% with 10 looks and 37%
with 100. Group-sequential boundaries restore the planned rate <Cite id="johari2017" />.

**Many comparisons manufacture findings.** <NoteLink to="many-metrics-and-variants" /> runs an A/B/n test with 3
variants and 10 metrics, 30 comparisons in all. With no real effects about three quarters of such experiments still
show a significant result. Multiple-testing corrections and a single pre-declared primary metric prevent this.

**Significant estimates are exaggerated.** The <NoteLink to="winners-curse" /> shows that a test with low power reaches
significance only when its estimate lands far from the truth. Reported effects are then too large, sometimes several
times too large, and some have the wrong sign. These are type M and type S errors <Cite id="gelman2014" />.

## How the notes fit together

Read the A/A test first: it sets up the simulation used by the other notes and checks the analysis on data with no
effect. Sample ratio mismatch is a check on <NoteLink to="data-collection">data collection</NoteLink>. Peeking, many
metrics and the winner's curse are three ways of searching for significance, and can be read in any order.
