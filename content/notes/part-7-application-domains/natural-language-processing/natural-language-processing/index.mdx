<Definition>
**Natural language processing** (NLP) builds programs that read, transform or produce human language. Its methods
turn text into numbers that a model can use, model the probability of word sequences, and solve tasks whose inputs or
outputs are text. This topic covers the classical and neural foundations; the large pretrained transformers that now
dominate the field are treated in Part VI.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Text is the largest store of human knowledge and the main interface of many products: search queries, reviews,
support tickets, contracts, clinical notes. NLP sorts and routes such text, extracts names and facts from it,
translates and summarises it, and answers questions about it. Its representations also feed other systems: product
descriptions become features for recommenders, and captions become training signal for image models
<Cite id="jurafsky2025" />.

## Main ideas

**Text must become vectors.** <NoteLink to="text-representation-pipeline" /> follows text from raw strings to model
inputs: normalisation, <NoteLink to="tokenisation">tokenisation</NoteLink> into words or subwords such as
<Gloss name="bpe">byte-pair encoding</Gloss>, <NoteLink to="stop-words">stop-word removal</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="stemming">stemming</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="lemmatisation">lemmatisation</NoteLink>, then features
such as bags of words, n-grams and character shingles, weighted by <Gloss name="tf-idf" /> <Cite id="manning2008" />.
<NoteLink to="distributional-semantics">Distributional semantics</NoteLink> replaces counted features with dense
embeddings of words, sentences and documents, learned from the contexts in which words occur.

**Language has a probability.** The chapter on <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss> assigns a
probability to every word sequence by predicting each word from the ones before it. The
<NoteLink to="n-gram-language-model" /> estimates these probabilities from smoothed counts of short word sequences. The
<NoteLink to="neural-language-model" /> computes them with a network over learned embeddings, so that similar words
share statistical strength. Both are ancestors of the large language models of Part VI.

**Tasks fix the output.** <NoteLink to="natural-language-processing-tasks" /> poses the standard problems:
<NoteLink to="text-classification">text classification</NoteLink>, <Gloss name="nli">natural language inference</Gloss>,
<Gloss name="machine-translation">machine translation</Gloss>, <NoteLink to="summarisation">summarisation</NoteLink> and
<Gloss name="qa">question answering</Gloss>. Each defines an input, an output shape and a metric, and most are now
solved by fine-tuning or prompting one pretrained model.

**Some tasks label every token.** <NoteLink to="sequence-labelling" /> tags each token of a sentence, as in
<Gloss name="pos-tagging">part-of-speech tagging</Gloss> and <NoteLink to="named-entity-recognition" />. Spans are
encoded as token tags, and the dependence between neighbouring tags is modelled by <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov
models</Gloss>, conditional random fields or the <NoteLink to="bidirectional-lstm-conditional-random-field" /> tagger.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="text-representation-pipeline" />, since every later note needs text as tokens or vectors.
Language models follow and introduce the probabilistic view of text. The task chapter and the sequence-labelling
chapter both build on the representations and can be read in either order. Within the representation chapter, the
tokenisation notes come first, the count-based features next and the embeddings last.
