<Definition>
Let $\Omega_{\mathrm S} = \{(s_i, I_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be texts $s_i$ in a source language paired with images $I_i$, and
$\Omega_{\mathrm T} = \{(t_j, J_j)\}_{j=1}^{M}$ texts $t_j$ in a target language paired with images $J_j$. The two
collections need not describe the same items. Given a cross-lingual <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">text
embedding</NoteLink> $f$ and an image similarity $g$, each query $s_q$ retrieves the target text $t_{j^*}$ with the most
similar embedding. The image $J_{j^*}$ then ranks every source image by $g$. **Backretrieval** $\mathrm{BkR}@K$ is the
fraction of queries whose own image $I_q$ ranks in the top $K$ <Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The standard test of a cross-lingual text embedding is cross-lingual retrieval. A query document in one language ranks
documents in another language by <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink>, and the score is the recall at
$K$ of the query's known translation. That test needs a parallel corpus: pairs of documents known to say the same thing
in both languages. Parallel data outside standard benchmarks requires bilingual annotators familiar with the domain.
Machine-translated test sets bias model selection towards methods correlated with the translation system
<Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" />.

Images need no translation. A photo of chicken soup looks the same whether its caption is English or Spanish. Many
domains have captioned images in every language separately: social media posts, product listings and recipe sites.
Backretrieval uses the image as the pivot. If the embedding maps an English caption close to a Spanish caption about the
same thing, the Spanish caption's photo will look like the English caption's photo. Retrieving back from that photo to
the English images will then find the query's own photo near the top. If the embedding is poor, the retrieved Spanish
caption is about something else, its photo shows something else, and the query's photo ranks low.

Backretrieval evaluates the text embedding $f$ only. The image similarity $g$ is a fixed instrument. The method is
distinct from multimodal <Gloss name="machine-translation">machine translation</Gloss>, which uses images to improve the
translation model rather than to evaluate it.

## The procedure

The text embedding $f$ maps a text in either language to a vector $f(\cdot) \in \reals^n$. The image similarity
$g(I, I')$ returns a score for any two images. Fain, Twomey and Bollegala take $g$ to be the cosine similarity between
the last-layer features of a convolutional network pretrained on <Gloss name="imagenet" />
<Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" /> <Cite id="babenko2014neural" /> <Cite id="deng2009" />. Backretrieval computes four
steps for each query pair $(s_q, I_q) \in \Omega_{\mathrm S}$.

1. **Embed.** Compute $f(t_j)$ for every target text $t_j$ and $f(s_q)$ for the query.
2. **Retrieve.** Find the target text most similar to the query,
   $$
   j^*(q) = \argmax_{j \in \{1, \dots, M\}} \cos\LP f(s_q), f(t_j) \RP.
   $$
   The text $t_{j^*(q)}$ is the retrieved text and $J_{j^*(q)}$ its image.
3. **Retrieve back.** Rank every source image $I_i \in \Omega_{\mathrm S}$ by $g(J_{j^*(q)}, I_i)$ in descending order.
   The rank of the query's own image is
   $$
   \rho_q = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \indicator\left[ g(J_{j^*(q)}, I_i) > g(J_{j^*(q)}, I_q) \right].
   $$
4. **Score.** Backretrieval at cut-off $K$ is the recall at $K$ of the query's image,
   $$
   \mathrm{BkR}@K(f) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{q=1}^{N} \indicator[\rho_q \le K].
   $$

Fain et al. use $K = 10$ <Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" />. $\mathrm{BkR}@K$ lies in $[0, 1]$, and a higher
value is better. Step 2 keeps only the single nearest target text. The top-$K$ cut-off applies to the image ranking in
step 3. The formula for $\rho_q$ breaks ties in favour of the query; the paper does not specify a tie rule.

The quantity that Backretrieval approximates is the ground-truth cross-lingual retrieval score $\mathrm{XLR}@K$. It
needs a parallel set in which $t_q$ is the translation of $s_q$ for every $q$. Each query ranks all $N$ translations by
$\cos(f(s_q), f(t_j))$, and $\mathrm{XLR}@K$ is the fraction of queries whose translation $t_q$ ranks in the top $K$.
Backretrieval replaces the unknown correspondence $s_q \leftrightarrow t_q$ with the image comparison in step 3.

## Worked example

Four English captions with photos form $\Omega_{\mathrm S}$: (1) a dog running on grass, (2) a bowl of ramen, (3) a
red bus, (4) a cat on a sofa. Four German captions with their own photos form $\Omega_{\mathrm T}$: (A) a dog playing
in a park, (B) a noodle soup, (C) a train at a station, (D) a sleeping cat. No German caption is a translation of an
English one.

Step 2 with some embedding $f$ retrieves A for query 1, B for query 2, D for query 3 and A for query 4. Step 3 needs
the image similarities $g(J_j, I_i)$ between each retrieved German photo and all four English photos.

| German photo     | $I_1$ (dog) | $I_2$ (ramen) | $I_3$ (bus) | $I_4$ (cat) |
| ---------------- | ----------- | ------------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| $J_{\mathrm{A}}$ (dog)   | 0.82        | 0.10          | 0.25        | 0.55        |
| $J_{\mathrm{B}}$ (soup)  | 0.12        | 0.74          | 0.08        | 0.15        |
| $J_{\mathrm{D}}$ (cat)   | 0.50        | 0.14          | 0.18        | 0.79        |

The ranks of each query's own image follow row by row.

- Query 1 retrieved A. In row A, $I_1$ has the highest similarity, 0.82, so $\rho_1 = 1$.
- Query 2 retrieved B. In row B, $I_2$ has the highest similarity, 0.74, so $\rho_2 = 1$.
- Query 3 retrieved D. Row D sorts as $I_4$ (0.79), $I_1$ (0.50), $I_3$ (0.18), $I_2$ (0.14), so $\rho_3 = 3$.
- Query 4 retrieved A. Row A sorts as $I_1$ (0.82), $I_4$ (0.55), $I_3$ (0.25), $I_2$ (0.10), so $\rho_4 = 2$.

With $K = 1$, queries 1 and 2 are hits and $\mathrm{BkR}@1 = 2/4 = 0.5$. With $K = 2$, query 4 is also a hit and
$\mathrm{BkR}@2 = 3/4 = 0.75$. A random embedding would score $K/N$ in
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, 0.25 and 0.5 respectively (derived below). Query 4 shows how the
image step grades a near miss: the cat caption retrieved the dog caption, and a dog photo still ranks the cat photo
second. Query 3 shows a miss: no German caption mentions a bus, the embedding picked the cat, and the bus photo ranks
third.

Queries 1 and 4 both retrieved A, so they share the ranking in row A. Only one image can occupy rank 1 in that row, so
at most one of the two queries can be a hit at $K = 1$. This cap is the basis of the hubness argument below.

## Why it tracks ground-truth retrieval

Backretrieval is only useful if it orders embedding models the way the unavailable ground truth would. Fain et al.
sketch the argument under two assumptions <Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" />. The first is that retrieval with $g$
performs better than random. The second is that the probability of finding $I_q$ in step 3 depends only on whether the
retrieved text $t_{j^*(q)}$ is a correct match for $s_q$ under the ground-truth pairing. The paper states the conclusion
without a proof.

**An affine link.** Write $c_f(q) = 1$ if embedding $f$ retrieves a correct match for query $q$ and $c_f(q) = 0$
otherwise. Let $\pi_f = \frac{1}{N} \sum_q c_f(q)$ be the fraction of queries with a correct match. By the second
assumption, the hit indicator $h_f(q) = \indicator[\rho_q \le K]$ has

$$
\prob(h_f(q) = 1 \mid c_f(q) = 1) = \alpha, \qquad \prob(h_f(q) = 1 \mid c_f(q) = 0) = \beta,
$$

with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ fixed by the images and $g$, not by $f$. Averaging over queries gives

$$
\expect[\mathrm{BkR}@K(f)] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_q \LB \beta + (\alpha - \beta) c_f(q) \RB = \beta + (\alpha - \beta)\, \pi_f.
$$

The expected Backretrieval is an affine function of the ground-truth match rate $\pi_f$. The first assumption says that
a correct match makes the query's image easier to find than an incorrect one, so $\alpha > \beta$ and the slope is
positive. Two models with $\pi_{f_1} > \pi_{f_2}$ then have
$\expect[\mathrm{BkR}@K(f_1)] - \expect[\mathrm{BkR}@K(f_2)] = (\alpha - \beta)(\pi_{f_1} - \pi_{f_2}) > 0$. The
expected scores preserve the ground-truth order.

**With high probability.** Write $\delta = (\alpha - \beta)(\pi_{f_1} - \pi_{f_2})$. Suppose the hits of different
queries are independent given the retrievals. The differences $D_q = h_{f_1}(q) - h_{f_2}(q)$ lie in $[-1, 1]$ and
have mean $\delta$. <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> for their average over $N$
queries <Cite id="hoeffding1963" /> gives

$$
\prob\LP \mathrm{BkR}@K(f_1) \le \mathrm{BkR}@K(f_2) \RP = \prob\LP \bar D - \delta \le -\delta \RP \le
\exp\LP -\frac{2 N^2 \delta^2}{N \cdot 2^2} \RP = \exp\LP -\frac{N \delta^2}{2} \RP.
$$

The wrong order becomes exponentially unlikely in $N$. The gap $\delta$ shrinks with $\alpha - \beta$, so a weak image
similarity needs more queries, which is why evaluations use thousands of queries where the data allow. The
Hoeffding bound ignores the small variance of rare hits and is conservative when $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are both small.

The argument connects Backretrieval to the match rate $\pi_f$ of the top-1 retrieved text. The ground-truth metric is
$\mathrm{XLR}@K$ on a parallel set, which is a different number. Both increase as $f$ places texts with the same
meaning closer across languages. Whether their orders agree in practice is an empirical question.

**Random embeddings score $K/N$.** If $f$ carries no information, the retrieved image $J_{j^*(q)}$ is unrelated to
$I_q$. By symmetry $I_q$ is equally likely to hold any of the $N$ ranks, so $\prob(\rho_q \le K) = K/N$ and
$\expect[\mathrm{BkR}@K] = K/N$. This is $\beta$ in the affine link. With $N = 10\,000$ queries and $K = 10$, a random
embedding scores $K/N = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$, and a model is only informative to the extent that it beats this floor.

**Equality in the parallel limit.** Suppose the target set is a translation of the source set, $t_q$ translating $s_q$,
and each translation keeps its image, $J_q = I_q$. Suppose also that the images are distinct and each image is more
similar to itself than to any other, $g(I, I) > g(I, I')$ for $I' \ne I$. If $j^*(q) = q$, step 3 ranks from $J_q = I_q$
and puts $I_q$ first, so $\rho_q = 1$. If $j^*(q) = m \ne q$, step 3 ranks from $J_m = I_m$, which puts $I_m$ first, so
$\rho_q \ge 2$. Hence $\indicator[\rho_q \le 1] = \indicator[j^*(q) = q]$ for every query, and

$$
\mathrm{BkR}@1(f) = \mathrm{XLR}@1(f).
$$

For $K > 1$ the two differ. A retrieved wrong text whose image resembles $I_q$ still earns a hit, as query 4 did in the
worked example.

**Robustness to hubs.** In high-dimensional spaces a few points, called hubs, are the nearest neighbour of many others
<Cite id="radovanovic2010hubs" />. A hub target text $t_h$ retrieved by $m$ queries sends all $m$ of them to the same
ranking of source images from $J_h$. That ranking has exactly $K$ positions at or above rank $K$, and each position
holds one source image. At most $K$ of the $m$ queries can therefore be hits, however large $m$ is. A hub adds at most
$K/N$ to Backretrieval. Fain et al. state this robustness to hubness as part of their theoretical sketch
<Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" />.

The toy places every item's meaning at a point in the plane. The embedding under test rotates and perturbs the
target-language texts, so a larger rotation or noise is a worse cross-lingual model. The image-feature noise controls
how informative $g$ is: as it grows, $\alpha$ falls towards $\beta$, the swept points flatten towards the $K/N$ line,
and the correlation between the two metrics weakens.

## What the evidence shows

Fain, Twomey and Bollegala tested the metric itself on public datasets whose images carry independently written
captions in two languages, such as Multi30K (English and German) <Cite id="elliott2016multi30k" />
<Cite id="fain2021backretrieval" />. The captions of each image make $\mathrm{XLR}@10$ computable, so every evaluated
embedding model gives a point $(\mathrm{XLR}@10, \mathrm{BkR}@10)$. Across about ten multilingual embedding models,
Backretrieval correlated with the ground truth at a <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">Pearson</NoteLink> coefficient of
0.97 to 0.99 in every language direction. A baseline that correlates text distances directly with image distances,
without the retrieval step, reached at most 0.81. The two metrics live on different scales: $\mathrm{BkR}@10$ values
were several times smaller than $\mathrm{XLR}@10$ values, as the affine link predicts when $\alpha - \beta$ is well
below 1. Only the ordering of models transfers.

The same paper applied Backretrieval where no ground truth exists: recipes on the Cookpad platform, written separately
in five languages. Backretrieval guided the design of a recipe embedding. Scored per language pair, it showed that an
embedding trained with a monolingual <NoteLink to="triplet-and-margin-losses">triplet loss</NoteLink> left some
languages poorly aligned with the others, because nothing in that loss ties the languages together. Adding a
cross-lingual alignment penalty lifted the worst pairs. A blinded human study then compared that
embedding with the LaBSE model it started from <Cite id="feng2022labse" />. The rater found 2.3 times as many very
relevant cross-lingual suggestions from the new embedding, where Backretrieval had scored it about 1.8 times higher.
The study had one rater and 20 queries, so it is a sanity check rather than a validation.

## Assumptions

- **Paired image–text data in each language.** Every evaluated text has an image, and the image depicts what the text
  is about.
- **Comparable content across languages.** The source and target collections cover overlapping subjects. A query whose
  subject is absent from the target collection can only miss, whatever the embedding.
- **An informative image similarity.** $g$ must rank images of the same content above others more often than chance,
  so that $\alpha > \beta$.
- **Hits depend on the text match only.** The theoretical sketch assumes that the chance of recovering $I_q$ depends
  only on whether the retrieved text matches, not on other properties of the embedding.

## Pitfalls

- **Scores are not comparable across setups.** $\mathrm{BkR}$ is compressed by $\alpha - \beta$, which depends on the
  image model, the dataset and $N$. Compare models on the same data with the same $g$. Calibrating Backretrieval to the
  scale of the ground truth is listed by the authors as future work.
- **Near-duplicate images.** Datasets with many visually similar images (plated dishes, product photos on white
  backgrounds) push the query's image down the ranking even after a correct text match. $\alpha$ falls, $\delta$
  shrinks, and more queries are needed to separate models.
- **Image-model errors and domain shift.** An ImageNet-trained network may not separate fine distinctions such as two
  soups. Errors of $g$ reduce $\alpha - \beta$ but do not reverse the order of models, provided $g$ stays better than
  chance and does not depend on $f$.
- **Only visually grounded meaning is measured.** Two texts that differ in content a photo cannot show, such as
  cooking time or a vegan substitution, receive the same image evidence. Backretrieval cannot reward an embedding for
  separating them.
- **Several texts per image.** When one image carries several captions, as in Flickr30k and MS-COCO, a retrieved
  caption of a similar image counts as success. That matches the intended notion of relevance but differs from
  translation retrieval, which rewards only the exact counterpart.
- **Dependent queries.** The Hoeffding argument assumes independent hits. Queries that share a hub retrieval share one
  image ranking, so their hits are dependent and the effective number of queries is smaller than $N$.
