<Definition>
**WordPiece** learns a subword vocabulary bottom up, like <NoteLink to="byte-pair-encoding">byte-pair
encoding</NoteLink>, but merges the pair of symbols that most increases the likelihood of the training data under a
unigram model of the symbols <Cite id="schuster2012" />. The **unigram language model** tokeniser works top down. It
assigns each token $v$ in a vocabulary $\Vcal$ a probability $p(v)$, scores a segmentation $\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_K)$
of a string by $P(\xvec) = \prod_{i=1}^{K} p(x_i)$, and shrinks a large candidate vocabulary by removing the tokens
whose loss lowers the likelihood least <Cite id="kudo2018" />.
</Definition>

## WordPiece

### The merge criterion

Model the corpus as a sequence of independent symbols with probabilities $p(t) = c_t / N$, where $c_t$ is the count
of symbol $t$ and $N$ is the total number of symbols. The log-likelihood of the corpus is $\sum_t c_t \log p(t)$.

Merging symbols $a$ and $b$ into $ab$ replaces each of the $c_{ab}$ adjacent occurrences of $a\,b$ by one symbol. Each
such occurrence contributed $\log p(a) + \log p(b)$ before the merge and contributes $\log p(ab)$ after it. Holding the
other probabilities fixed, the change in log-likelihood is

$$
\Delta \ell(a, b) \approx c_{ab} \big(\log p(ab) - \log p(a) - \log p(b)\big) = c_{ab} \log \frac{p(ab)}{p(a)\, p(b)}.
$$

The logarithm is the <Gloss name="pmi">pointwise mutual information</Gloss> of $a$ and $b$. It is large when $a$ and $b$
occur together more often than <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> predicts.

Schuster and Nakajima describe WordPiece as choosing, at each step, the new unit that increases the training likelihood
most <Cite id="schuster2012" />. Google did not publish the training code. The widely used Hugging Face reimplementation
ranks pairs by

$$
\text{score}(a, b) = \frac{c_{ab}}{c_a\, c_b},
$$

which equals $p(ab) / (p(a) p(b))$ divided by $N$, so it ranks by the pointwise mutual information alone
<Cite id="huggingface2024wordpiece" />. Byte-pair encoding (BPE) ranks by $c_{ab}$ alone.

**Example.** Suppose `t` occurs 200 times, `h` 60 times and the pair `t h` 50 times, while `q` occurs 10 times, `u`
100 times and `q u` 10 times. BPE merges `t h` first, because $50 > 10$. WordPiece scores `t h` at $50 / (200 \cdot
60) = 0.0042$ and `q u` at $10 / (10 \cdot 100) = 0.01$, and merges `q u` first, because `q` is never seen without
`u`.

### Encoding

WordPiece marks every piece that continues a word, rather than the end of the word. <Gloss name="bert" /> writes the
continuation prefix as `##` and uses a vocabulary of 30,000 pieces <Cite id="devlin2019" />. A word is encoded greedily
from the left: take the longest vocabulary entry that is a prefix of the remaining characters, emit it, and repeat on
the rest with the `##` prefix. "unaffable" becomes `un`, `##aff`, `##able`. If at some point no entry matches, the whole
word becomes the <NoteLink to="one-hot-encoding-of-words">unknown token</NoteLink> `[UNK]`. Greedy longest-match-first
does not in general give the segmentation with the fewest pieces or the highest likelihood. Google's
<NoteLink to="machine-translation">neural machine translation</NoteLink> system used a WordPiece vocabulary of 8,000 to
32,000 units shared between source and target <Cite id="wu2016" />.

## The unigram language model tokeniser

### The model

A vocabulary $\Vcal$ carries probabilities with $\sum_{v \in \Vcal} p(v) = 1$. A string $X$ has a set $\Scal(X)$ of
candidate segmentations, every way of writing $X$ as a concatenation of tokens from $\Vcal$. The model treats the
tokens of a segmentation as independent:

$$
P(\xvec) = \prod_{i=1}^{K} p(x_i), \qquad \xvec \in \Scal(X).
$$

The tokeniser outputs the most probable segmentation $\xvec^\star = \argmax_{\xvec \in \Scal(X)} P(\xvec)$. The
segmentations of $X$ form a lattice over its character positions, with one edge per vocabulary token that spans a
substring. Viterbi finds $\xvec^\star$ in time proportional to the length of $X$ times the length of the longest token:
the best score at position $j$ is the maximum over tokens $v$ ending at $j$ of the best score at the start of $v$ plus
$\log p(v)$ <Cite id="kudo2018" />.

### Training by expectation-maximisation

The segmentation of each training string is not observed, so the probabilities are fitted by maximising the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> over segmentations,

$$
\Lcal = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \log \sum_{\xvec \in \Scal(X_s)} P(\xvec),
$$

with <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation–maximisation</NoteLink>. The E-step computes, for every token
$v$, its expected count: the number of times $v$ appears in a segmentation, averaged under the posterior $P(\xvec \mid
X_s) = P(\xvec) / \sum_{\xvec'} P(\xvec')$. The <Gloss name="forward-backward">forward–backward algorithm</Gloss> on the
lattice computes these <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> without listing the segmentations, as for a
<NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink>. The M-step sets each $p(v)$ proportional to its
expected count.

### Pruning the vocabulary

1. **Seed.** Start from a vocabulary much larger than the target, such as all characters plus the most frequent
   substrings of the corpus.
2. **Fit.** Run expectation–maximisation to fit $p(v)$.
3. **Score.** For each token $v$, compute its loss: the drop in $\Lcal$ when $v$ is removed from $\Vcal$.
4. **Prune.** Keep the tokens with the largest loss, such as the top 80%, and always keep the single characters so that
   every string stays encodable.
5. **Repeat** steps 2 to 4 until the vocabulary reaches the target size <Cite id="kudo2018" />.

A token with small loss is one whose strings are segmented almost as well without it.

### Worked example

Let the vocabulary hold `h`, `u`, `g` with probability 0.05 each, `s` with 0.10, `hu` with 0.08, `ug` with 0.10, `hug`
with 0.07 and `gs` with 0.03. The string "hugs" has six segmentations:

| Segmentation | $P(\xvec)$ | Posterior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `hug` `s` | $0.07 \cdot 0.10 = 0.0070$ | 0.674 |
| `hu` `gs` | $0.08 \cdot 0.03 = 0.0024$ | 0.231 |
| `h` `ug` `s` | $0.05 \cdot 0.10 \cdot 0.10 = 0.0005$ | 0.048 |
| `hu` `g` `s` | $0.08 \cdot 0.05 \cdot 0.10 = 0.0004$ | 0.039 |
| `h` `u` `gs` | $0.05 \cdot 0.05 \cdot 0.03 = 0.000075$ | 0.007 |
| `h` `u` `g` `s` | $0.05^3 \cdot 0.10 = 0.0000125$ | 0.001 |

The marginal probability is the sum, $0.0103875$. Viterbi returns `hug` `s`. The E-step credits `hug` with an expected
count of 0.674 and `hu` with $0.231 + 0.039 = 0.270$. The losses for pruning, with the other probabilities held fixed,
are $\log(0.0103875 / 0.0033875) = 1.12$ nats for `hug`, 0.27 for `gs` and 0.05 for `ug`. Of these three, `ug` is the
first candidate to prune: "hugs" loses little probability without it.

### Subword regularisation

The unigram model gives a probability to every segmentation, so training can sample a different segmentation of each
sentence each time it is seen, with probability proportional to $P(\xvec)^\alpha$ for a smoothing exponent $\alpha$.
Kudo reported that this makes translation models more robust, especially with little data <Cite id="kudo2018" />.
Evaluation uses the Viterbi segmentation.

## SentencePiece

SentencePiece is a library that trains and applies BPE and unigram tokenisers directly on raw text
<Cite id="kudo2018b" />. It does not pre-tokenise on spaces. It replaces each space by the ordinary symbol `▁`
(U+2581) and treats the text as one stream of characters, so the same procedure works for languages written without
spaces, and decoding is the concatenation of the pieces with `▁` turned back into a space. The vocabulary size is set
directly rather than through a number of merges.

## Comparison

| | BPE | WordPiece | Unigram LM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | bottom-up merges | bottom-up merges | top-down pruning |
| Selection | pair count $c_{ab}$ | $c_{ab} / (c_a c_b)$ | loss in marginal likelihood |
| Encoding | replay merges in order | greedy longest match | Viterbi on the lattice |
| Probabilities | none | none at encoding time | $P(\xvec) = \prod_i p(x_i)$ |
| Samples segmentations | no | no | yes |

## Pitfalls

- **"WordPiece" names two things.** The published description (likelihood gain) and the common reimplementation (a
  count ratio) are not the same criterion.
- **Independence is an approximation.** The unigram model ignores which token follows which. It scores segmentations
  well enough to choose among them, but it is not a language model of the text.
- **Greedy encoding can fail where another segmentation exists.** WordPiece emits `[UNK]` when the longest-prefix path
  dead-ends, even if a different split would have encoded the word.
