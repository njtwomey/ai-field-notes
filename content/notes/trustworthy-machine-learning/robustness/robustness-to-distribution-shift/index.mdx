<Definition>
Let a model be trained on a source distribution $P_S(\xvec, y)$ and used on a target distribution $P_T(\xvec, y)$. For
a classifier $h$, write the error on a distribution $P$ as $\varepsilon_P(h) = \Pr_{(\xvec, y) \sim P}(h(\xvec) \ne y)$.
**Robustness to distribution shift** is a small gap $\varepsilon_{P_T}(h) - \varepsilon_{P_S}(h)$ for the target
distributions the model will meet. **Domain adaptation** has unlabelled samples from $P_T$ at training time. **Domain
generalisation** has labelled samples from several source domains and none from the target. **Group robustness** asks
for low error on every one of a set of known subpopulations.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A model fits whatever regularities predict the label in its training data. Some of these hold everywhere, such as the
shape of a bird. Others hold only in the training data, such as water in the background of photographs of waterbirds.
Standard training has no reason to prefer the first kind. When the second kind breaks, accuracy falls, and nothing in the
source validation error warns of it. The kinds of shift, covariate, label and concept shift, are defined in
<NoteLink to="dataset-shift">dataset shift</NoteLink>. Label shift alone has an exact correction, described in
<NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction">prior-shift correction</NoteLink>. This note covers methods that change training.

## Evidence

- **Reproduced test sets.** Recht et al. rebuilt the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet test sets by following the original data
  collection. Accuracy dropped by 3% to 15% on CIFAR-10 and 11% to 14% on ImageNet, although the new images came from
  the same sources <Cite id="recht2019" />. Accuracy gains on the original test sets translated into larger gains on the
  new ones.
- **Corruptions.** ImageNet-C applies 15 common corruptions, such as noise, blur, weather and JPEG compression, at five
  severities. Hendrycks and Dietterich found negligible changes in relative corruption robustness from AlexNet to ResNet
  classifiers <Cite id="hendrycks2019" />.
- **Natural shifts.** WILDS collects 10 data sets with real shifts, such as tumour images from different hospitals and
  camera-trap images from different locations. On each, standard training gives substantially lower out-of-distribution
  than in-distribution performance, and existing robustness methods do not close the gap <Cite id="koh2021" />.

## Domain adaptation

**The bound.** Ben-David et al. bound the target error by quantities that can be estimated
<Cite id="bendavid2010" />. For two classifiers $h, h'$ in a hypothesis class $\Hcal$, let
$\varepsilon_P(h, h') = \Pr_{\xvec \sim P}(h(\xvec) \ne h'(\xvec))$ be how often they disagree. Define the
$\Hcal\Delta\Hcal$-divergence

$$
d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(P_S, P_T) = 2\sup_{h, h' \in \Hcal}\abs{\varepsilon_{P_S}(h, h') - \varepsilon_{P_T}(h, h')},
$$

and let $h^\star = \argmin_{h \in \Hcal}\big(\varepsilon_{P_S}(h) + \varepsilon_{P_T}(h)\big)$ with joint error
$\lambda = \varepsilon_{P_S}(h^\star) + \varepsilon_{P_T}(h^\star)$. Disagreement obeys the triangle inequality: if $h$
is wrong at $\xvec$, then either $h^\star$ is wrong or $h$ and $h^\star$ disagree. Applying it twice,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_{P_T}(h) &\le \varepsilon_{P_T}(h^\star) + \varepsilon_{P_T}(h, h^\star) \\
&\le \varepsilon_{P_T}(h^\star) + \varepsilon_{P_S}(h, h^\star) + \abs{\varepsilon_{P_T}(h, h^\star) - \varepsilon_{P_S}(h, h^\star)} \\
&\le \varepsilon_{P_T}(h^\star) + \varepsilon_{P_S}(h) + \varepsilon_{P_S}(h^\star) + \tfrac12 d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(P_S, P_T) \\
&= \varepsilon_{P_S}(h) + \tfrac12 d_{\Hcal\Delta\Hcal}(P_S, P_T) + \lambda.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is the source error. The second needs only unlabelled inputs: it is large when some pair of classifiers
in $\Hcal$ tells the domains apart. The third is small only if one classifier does well on both domains. No method can
reduce $\lambda$, so adaptation can fail when the labelling rule itself differs.

**Domain-adversarial training.** The bound suggests learning a representation in which the domains are
indistinguishable. DANN trains a feature extractor $G_f$, a label predictor $G_y$ on labelled source data and a domain
classifier $G_d$ that tries to tell source features from target features <Cite id="ganin2016" />. The feature extractor
minimises the label loss $\Lcal_y$ and maximises the domain loss $\Lcal_d$:

$$
\min_{G_f, G_y}\max_{G_d}\ \Lcal_y(G_f, G_y) - \lambda_d\,\Lcal_d(G_f, G_d).
$$

A **gradient reversal layer** implements the saddle point with ordinary backpropagation. It is the identity in the forward
pass and multiplies the gradient by $-\lambda_d$ in the backward pass, so one optimiser step moves $G_d$ to classify
domains better and $G_f$ to make that harder.

## Domain generalisation

With several labelled training domains $e \in \Ecal$ and an unseen test domain, the aim is a predictor that relies only on
relations stable across domains. **Invariant risk minimisation** (IRM) looks for a representation $\Phi$ on which the same
classifier $w$ is optimal in every training domain <Cite id="arjovsky2019" />. Its practical form IRMv1 penalises how far
a fixed scalar classifier $w = 1$ is from optimal in each domain:

$$
\min_{\Phi}\ \sum_{e \in \Ecal}\Big(R^e(\Phi) + \lambda\,\big\|\nabla_{w \mid w = 1} R^e(w \cdot \Phi)\big\|^2\Big),
$$

with $R^e$ the risk in domain $e$. The gradient is zero exactly when scaling the predictor would not reduce that domain's
risk.

Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz implemented many such methods in one testbed, DomainBed, with seven multi-domain data sets and
explicit rules for model selection. With careful implementation, empirical risk minimisation pooled over the training
domains was as good as any of them <Cite id="gulrajani2021" />. They argue that a domain generalisation method is
incomplete without a model-selection rule, because selecting on the test domain leaks the target.

## Group robustness

When the subpopulations are known, for example the four combinations of bird type and background, **group DRO**
minimises the worst group's expected loss instead of the average <Cite id="sagawa2020" />:

$$
\min_{\thetavec}\ \max_{g \in \Gcal}\ \expect_{(\xvec, y) \sim P_g}\big[\ell(\thetavec; \xvec, y)\big].
$$

Their stochastic algorithm keeps weights $q_g$ over groups, multiplies each by $\exp(\eta\,\ell_g)$ after a minibatch
with group loss $\ell_g$, renormalises, and takes a gradient step on the weighted loss $\sum_g q_g\ell_g$. Sagawa et al.
found that group DRO applied naively to overparameterised networks fails, and works only with strong regularisation, such as a
large $\ell_2$ penalty or early stopping. A network that fits the training data perfectly has zero worst-group training
loss too, so the failure is in generalisation on small groups. With regularisation, worst-group accuracy improved by 10
to 40 percentage points on their three tasks <Cite id="sagawa2020" />. When the groups are defined by a protected attribute, worst-group
accuracy is also a fairness requirement; see <NoteLink to="fairness-criteria">fairness criteria</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

A training set has two groups. Group 1 holds 95% of the data and a model errs on 1% of it. Group 2 holds 5% and the model
errs on 40% of it. The average error is $0.95 \cdot 0.01 + 0.05 \cdot 0.40 = 0.0295$, under 3%. The worst-group error is
0.40. If deployment reverses the proportions, the error becomes $0.05 \cdot 0.01 + 0.95 \cdot 0.40 = 0.381$. Group DRO
optimises the 0.40 directly, so its guarantee does not depend on the group proportions at deployment.

## Assumptions

- Every method needs something beyond the source sample: unlabelled target data, several domains, group labels, or an
  assumption about which part of $P(\xvec, y)$ stays fixed. Without any of these, a shift can make any predictor fail.
- Domain adaptation assumes that one classifier does well on both domains (small $\lambda$).

## Pitfalls

- **Aligning marginals can hurt.** Making $P_S(\Phi(\xvec))$ and $P_T(\Phi(\xvec))$ equal says nothing about aligning
  the classes. When label proportions differ between domains, forcing equal feature marginals maps target examples of
  one class onto source examples of another.
- **Model selection leaks.** Tuning on labelled target data turns a domain generalisation result into a small-sample
  adaptation result <Cite id="gulrajani2021" />.
- **Detection comes first.** A shift that is not noticed is not corrected. Monitor inputs and predictions with
  <NoteLink to="drift-detection">drift detection</NoteLink>, and prefer uncertainty estimates such as
  <NoteLink to="conformal-prediction">conformal prediction</NoteLink>, whose coverage guarantee assumes exchangeable data
  and so also fails under shift.
- **Fine-tuning is the pragmatic fix.** With a small labelled target set,
  <NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">fine-tuning</NoteLink> a pre-trained model often beats every
  adaptation method.
