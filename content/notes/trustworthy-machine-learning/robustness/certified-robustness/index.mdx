<Definition>
A classifier $g$ is **certifiably robust** at $\xvec$ with radius $R$ if $g(\xvec + \deltavec) = g(\xvec)$ for every
$\deltavec$ with $\norm{\deltavec}_2 < R$, and a procedure proves it. **Randomised smoothing** builds $g$ from any base
classifier $f : \reals^d \to \Ycal$ as

$$
g(\xvec) = \argmax_{c \in \Ycal}\ \Pr\big(f(\xvec + \epsilonvec) = c\big), \qquad \epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\I).
$$

If the top class $c_A$ has probability at least $p_A$ and every other class at most $p_B$, then $g$ is robust at $\xvec$
within $R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\big(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B)\big)$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal cdf
<Cite id="cohen2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An empirical defence against <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples">adversarial examples</NoteLink> survives the attacks
someone tried. A certificate holds for every attack inside the threat model. Randomised smoothing earns it by replacing
the prediction at a point with a vote over a Gaussian cloud around that point. Moving the input by $\deltavec$ moves the
cloud by $\deltavec$. Two Gaussian clouds whose centres are close overlap almost completely, so the vote can change only
a little. How far the input can move before the vote could flip depends on how lopsided the vote was and on the width
$\sigma$ of the cloud. Nothing about $f$ enters the argument, so $f$ can be any network.

Lecuyer et al. introduced smoothing with a guarantee derived from differential privacy <Cite id="lecuyer2019" />. Cohen,
Rosenfeld and Kolter proved the tight $\ell_2$ radius derived below <Cite id="cohen2019" />.

## Deriving the radius

Fix $\xvec$ and a perturbation $\deltavec$. Write $X = \xvec + \epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\xvec, \sigma^2\I)$ and
$Y = \xvec + \deltavec + \epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\xvec + \deltavec, \sigma^2\I)$. All that is known about $f$ is that the
set $A = \set{\zvec : f(\zvec) = c_A}$ has $\Pr(X \in A) \ge p_A$. The question is how small $\Pr(Y \in A)$ can be.

**Step 1: the worst set is a likelihood-ratio set.** Let $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$ be the two densities and
$A^\star = \set{\zvec : \mu_Y(\zvec)/\mu_X(\zvec) \le t}$ with $t$ chosen so that $\Pr(X \in A^\star) = p_A$. Take any
$A$ with $\Pr(X \in A) \ge p_A$. On $A \setminus A^\star$ the ratio exceeds $t$, so $\mu_Y > t\mu_X$ there; on
$A^\star \setminus A$ the ratio is at most $t$, so $\mu_Y \le t\mu_X$ there. Hence

$$
\Pr(Y \in A) - \Pr(Y \in A^\star) = \int_{A \setminus A^\star}\mu_Y - \int_{A^\star \setminus A}\mu_Y
\ge t\Big(\int_{A \setminus A^\star}\mu_X - \int_{A^\star \setminus A}\mu_X\Big) = t\big(\Pr(X \in A) - p_A\big) \ge 0.
$$

This is the argument of the <NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma">Neyman–Pearson lemma</NoteLink>: among sets of
probability at least $p_A$ under $X$, the likelihood-ratio set has the least probability under $Y$.

**Step 2: for Gaussians that set is a half-space.** The log ratio of two isotropic Gaussians with equal variance is

$$
\log\frac{\mu_Y(\zvec)}{\mu_X(\zvec)} = \frac{\norm{\zvec - \xvec}^2 - \norm{\zvec - \xvec - \deltavec}^2}{2\sigma^2}
= \frac{\deltavec^\top(\zvec - \xvec)}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\norm{\deltavec}^2}{2\sigma^2},
$$

which increases in $\deltavec^\top(\zvec - \xvec)$. So $A^\star = \set{\zvec : \deltavec^\top(\zvec - \xvec) \le \tau}$, a
half-space whose boundary is normal to $\deltavec$. Under $X$, $\deltavec^\top(X - \xvec) \sim
\Gauss(0, \sigma^2\norm{\deltavec}^2)$, so $\Pr(X \in A^\star) = p_A$ gives $\tau = \sigma\norm{\deltavec}\Phi^{-1}(p_A)$.
Under $Y$, $\deltavec^\top(Y - \xvec) \sim \Gauss(\norm{\deltavec}^2, \sigma^2\norm{\deltavec}^2)$, so

$$
\Pr\big(f(Y) = c_A\big) \ge \Pr(Y \in A^\star) = \Phi\Big(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \frac{\norm{\deltavec}}{\sigma}\Big).
$$

**Step 3: the same bound for the runner-up.** For any other class $c_B$, the set $B = \set{f = c_B}$ has
$\Pr(X \in B) \le p_B$. The same argument with the inequality reversed shows that the set with the most probability under
$Y$ is the opposite half-space, and $\Pr(f(Y) = c_B) \le \Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(p_B) + \norm{\deltavec}/\sigma\big)$.

**Step 4: compare.** The smoothed classifier still returns $c_A$ at $\xvec + \deltavec$ if the lower bound for $c_A$
exceeds the upper bound for every $c_B$. Since $\Phi$ is increasing, this holds when
$\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \norm{\deltavec}/\sigma > \Phi^{-1}(p_B) + \norm{\deltavec}/\sigma$, that is when

$$
\norm{\deltavec}_2 < R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B)\Big).
$$

With two classes, or with the bound $p_B = 1 - p_A$, the symmetry $\Phi^{-1}(1 - p) = -\Phi^{-1}(p)$ gives
$R = \sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_A)$.

The radius is tight. The worst case of Step 2 is attained by a linear base classifier whose boundary is normal to
$\deltavec$; for that $f$, any perturbation longer than $R$ in the direction of $\deltavec$ changes $g$
<Cite id="cohen2019" />. The radius grows without bound as $p_A \to 1$, and it scales with $\sigma$.

## Certifying with samples

$p_A$ is a probability over the noise and cannot be computed exactly for a network. Cohen et al.'s CERTIFY procedure
estimates it by Monte Carlo <Cite id="cohen2019" />:

1. Draw $n_0$ noisy copies of $\xvec$ and take the most frequent label of $f$ as the guess $\hat c_A$.
2. Draw $n$ fresh copies and count the $k$ with $f = \hat c_A$.
3. Compute a one-sided $(1 - \alpha)$ lower confidence bound $\underline{p_A}$ for the binomial proportion with the
   Clopper–Pearson interval <Cite id="clopper1934" />, and set $p_B = 1 - \underline{p_A}$.
4. If $\underline{p_A} > 1/2$, return $\hat c_A$ with radius $\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})$; otherwise abstain.

With probability at least $1 - \alpha$ over the sampling, a returned radius is correct. Using fresh samples in step 2
keeps the selection of $\hat c_A$ from biasing the bound. Cohen et al. used $n_0 = 100$, $n = 100{,}000$ and
$\alpha = 0.001$. On ImageNet they obtained a smoothed classifier with a certified top-1 accuracy of 49% under $\ell_2$
perturbations of norm less than $0.5$ (that is, $127/255$) <Cite id="cohen2019" />.

Large radii need many samples. If all $n$ samples agree, the tightest lower bound is $\underline{p_A} = \alpha^{1/n}$ and
the radius is $\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\alpha^{1/n})$, which grows only like $\sqrt{\log n}$.

## Worked example

Take $\sigma = 0.5$ and $n = 1000$ noisy samples, of which $k = 990$ return $c_A$. The point estimate
$\hat p_A = 0.99$ would suggest $R = 0.5\,\Phi^{-1}(0.99) = 0.5 \cdot 2.326 = 1.163$. The certificate uses the
Clopper–Pearson lower bound instead. At $\alpha = 0.001$ it is the $0.001$ quantile of $\Beta(990, 11)$,
$\underline{p_A} = 0.976$, and the certified radius is $0.5\,\Phi^{-1}(0.976) = 0.989$. With $n = 100{,}000$ samples that
all agree, $\underline{p_A} = 0.001^{1/100{,}000} = 0.99993$ and $R = 0.5 \cdot 3.811 = 1.906$.

## Other certificates

- **Interval bound propagation** pushes an axis-aligned box of inputs through the network layer by layer, bounding each
  activation by an interval. If the lower bound of the true logit exceeds the upper bounds of all others, the box is
  certified. Training against these bounds makes them tight enough to be useful <Cite id="gowal2018" />.
- **Convex relaxations** replace each ReLU by its convex hull over the known activation interval and solve, or bound
  through the dual, a linear program for the worst-case margin <Cite id="wong2018" />.

These methods certify $\ell_\infty$ balls deterministically and need access to the architecture. Randomised smoothing
certifies $\ell_2$ balls for any black-box $f$, holds only with probability $1 - \alpha$, and costs thousands of forward
passes per prediction.

## Assumptions

- The certificate is about $g$, not $f$. Deploying the base classifier forfeits it.
- The noise must be applied at test time exactly as in the analysis: isotropic Gaussian with the stated $\sigma$.
- $f$ must be accurate under noise of that size. Cohen et al. train $f$ with Gaussian data augmentation at the same
  $\sigma$ <Cite id="cohen2019" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Accuracy against radius.** Larger $\sigma$ allows larger radii but blurs the input, lowering clean accuracy and
  increasing abstentions. Report certified accuracy as a function of radius, not one number.
- **Norm mismatch.** An $\ell_2$ certificate of radius $R$ covers an $\ell_\infty$ ball only of radius $R/\sqrt d$. For
  images with $d = 150{,}528$ that is tiny.
- **Abstention is not robustness.** A procedure that abstains near every boundary certifies little; count abstentions as
  errors.
