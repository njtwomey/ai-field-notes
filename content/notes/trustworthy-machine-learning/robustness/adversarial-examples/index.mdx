<Definition>
Let $f$ be a classifier, $L(\thetavec, \xvec, y)$ its training loss with parameters $\thetavec$, and $\Scal$ a set of
allowed perturbations, usually the ball $\set{\deltavec : \norm{\deltavec}_p \le \epsilon}$. An **adversarial example**
for a correctly classified input $(\xvec, y)$ is a point $\xvec + \deltavec$ with $\deltavec \in \Scal$ and
$f(\xvec + \deltavec) \ne y$ <Cite id="szegedy2014" />. An **attack** searches $\Scal$ for such a $\deltavec$, usually by
maximising $L(\thetavec, \xvec + \deltavec, y)$. **Adversarial robustness** at $\xvec$ is the absence of any such point
in $\Scal$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Szegedy et al. found that image classifiers with human-level test accuracy change their prediction under perturbations
too small for a person to see, and that a perturbation found for one network often fools another network trained
separately <Cite id="szegedy2014" />.

The set $\Scal$ is the **threat model**: it states what the adversary may change. The $\ell_\infty$ ball says every
pixel may move by at most $\epsilon$. On images with 8-bit pixels scaled to $[0, 1]$, $\epsilon = 8/255$ is a common
choice.

In $n$ dimensions, many coordinates each moved by a tiny amount add up to a
large change in any linear function of the input. High-dimensional inputs therefore leave room for perturbations that are
small per coordinate and large in effect.

The search for an adversarial example is the same optimisation as the search for a
<NoteLink to="counterfactual-explanations">counterfactual explanation</NoteLink>: the smallest change to an input that
changes the prediction. The two differ in intent and in the distance used, not in the mathematics.

## The linear explanation

Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy argued that adversarial examples come from the linear behaviour of networks, not from
their nonlinearity <Cite id="goodfellow2015" />. Take a linear score $s(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$ with
$\wvec \in \reals^n$. A perturbation $\deltavec$ changes the score by $\wvec^\top\deltavec$. The largest change allowed by
$\norm{\deltavec}_\infty \le \epsilon$ solves

$$
\max_{\norm{\deltavec}_\infty \le \epsilon} \wvec^\top\deltavec = \max_{\abs{\delta_i} \le \epsilon}
\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\delta_i.
$$

The sum separates over coordinates. Each term $w_i\delta_i$ is largest at $\delta_i = \epsilon\,\sgn(w_i)$, where it
equals $\epsilon\abs{w_i}$. The maximiser is $\deltavec^\star = \epsilon\,\sgn(\wvec)$ and the maximum is

$$
\wvec^\top\deltavec^\star = \epsilon\norm{\wvec}_1.
$$

If the weights have average magnitude $m$, then $\norm{\wvec}_1 = mn$ and the change is $\epsilon mn$. The perturbation
size $\norm{\deltavec^\star}_\infty = \epsilon$ does not grow with $n$, but its effect grows linearly in $n$. The same
argument gives the distance from $\xvec$ to the decision boundary $s = 0$ in the $\ell_\infty$ norm:
$\abs{s(\xvec)}/\norm{\wvec}_1$. The Euclidean distance is $\abs{s(\xvec)}/\norm{\wvec}_2$. Since
$\norm{\wvec}_1$ can be up to $\sqrt n$ times $\norm{\wvec}_2$, a point far from the boundary in Euclidean distance can be
close to it in $\ell_\infty$ distance.

## Fast gradient sign method

A network is not linear, but <Gloss name="relu" /> networks are piecewise linear and are trained to operate in near-linear regimes. The
**fast gradient sign method** (FGSM) linearises the loss around $\xvec$:
$L(\thetavec, \xvec + \deltavec, y) \approx L(\thetavec, \xvec, y) + \nabla_{\xvec}L^\top\deltavec$. Maximising the linear
term over the $\ell_\infty$ ball is the problem above with $\wvec = \nabla_{\xvec}L$, so

$$
\xvec_{\text{adv}} = \xvec + \epsilon\,\sgn\big(\nabla_{\xvec}L(\thetavec, \xvec, y)\big).
$$

One backward pass computes the gradient with respect to the input <Cite id="goodfellow2015" />. With $\epsilon = 0.25$
on <Gloss name="mnist" />, FGSM gave a shallow softmax classifier an error rate of 99.9% and a maxout network an error rate of 89.4%.
The authors also showed the famous panda image: GoogLeNet labelled it "panda" with 57.7% confidence and, after an FGSM
perturbation with $\epsilon = 0.007$, "gibbon" with 99.3% confidence <Cite id="goodfellow2015" />.

## Projected gradient descent

FGSM takes one linear step. The loss is not linear over the whole ball, so iterating smaller steps finds stronger
attacks. **Projected gradient descent** (PGD) on the negative loss, for the $\ell_\infty$ ball, repeats

$$
\xvec^{(t+1)} = \proj_{\Scal(\xvec)}\Big(\xvec^{(t)} + a\,\sgn\big(\nabla_{\xvec}L(\thetavec, \xvec^{(t)}, y)\big)\Big),
$$

with step size $a$ and $\proj_{\Scal(\xvec)}$ the projection onto
$\set{\xvec' : \norm{\xvec' - \xvec}_\infty \le \epsilon}$. For a box this projection clips each coordinate of
$\xvec' - \xvec$ to $[-\epsilon, \epsilon]$. Madry et al. start PGD from a random point in the ball and restart it
several times <Cite id="madry2018" />. They found that the maxima PGD reaches from many random starts have similar loss
values, and they treat PGD as the strongest attack that uses only first-order information.

## Adversarial training

Adversarial training replaces the expected loss by the expected worst-case loss over the threat model. Madry et al. write it
as a saddle-point problem <Cite id="madry2018" />:

$$
\min_{\thetavec}\ \rho(\thetavec), \qquad
\rho(\thetavec) = \expect_{(\xvec, y) \sim \Dcal}\Big[\max_{\deltavec \in \Scal} L(\thetavec, \xvec + \deltavec, y)\Big].
$$

The inner maximisation is an attack. The outer minimisation is training. Danskin's theorem justifies the practical
algorithm: when the inner maximiser $\deltavec^\star$ is unique, the gradient of the inner maximum with respect to
$\thetavec$ is $\nabla_{\thetavec}L(\thetavec, \xvec + \deltavec^\star, y)$. Each training step therefore runs PGD on the
minibatch and takes an ordinary gradient step on the loss at the resulting adversarial inputs. Goodfellow et al. used
the cheaper FGSM inner step <Cite id="goodfellow2015" />.

Madry et al. trained networks this way against $\ell_\infty$ attacks. On MNIST with $\epsilon = 0.3$, the lowest accuracy
across the attacks they tried was 89.3%. On <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> with $\epsilon = 8$ on the 0–255 pixel scale, clean accuracy was
87.3% and the lowest accuracy under attack was 45.8% <Cite id="madry2018" />. Adversarial training costs one PGD run per
step, so it is several times slower than standard training.

## Worked example

A linear classifier on $224 \times 224$ colour images has $n = 3 \cdot 224 \cdot 224 = 150{,}528$ inputs. Suppose its
weights have average magnitude $m = 0.01$ and it gives an image the logit $s = 4.6$, a probability
$\sigma(4.6) = 0.990$ of the correct class, with $\sigma$ the logistic function. An $\ell_\infty$ perturbation of one
grey level, $\epsilon = 1/255$, moves the logit by up to

$$
\epsilon mn = \frac{0.01 \cdot 150{,}528}{255} = 5.90.
$$

The logit falls to $4.6 - 5.90 = -1.30$ and the predicted probability to $\sigma(-1.30) = 0.214$. The prediction flips,
yet no pixel changed by more than one grey level.

## Assumptions

- The threat model is part of the claim. Robustness to $\ell_\infty$ perturbations of size $8/255$ says nothing about
  rotations, translations, colour shifts or perturbations of size $16/255$.
- Attacks assume access to gradients (white-box) or to many queries of the model (black-box). Transfer from a
  substitute model gives black-box attacks with no queries at all <Cite id="szegedy2014" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Gradient masking.** A defence can make gradients useless without making the model robust, for example with
  non-differentiable preprocessing, randomness or saturated activations. Athalye, Carlini and Wagner examined nine
  defences from ICLR 2018: seven relied on such **obfuscated gradients**, and adapted attacks circumvented six of them
  completely and one partially <Cite id="athalye2018" />. A defence must be evaluated with attacks adapted to it.
- **Weak evaluation.** A few FGSM steps overstate robustness. Report PGD with random restarts, and check that accuracy
  falls to zero as $\epsilon$ grows.
- **Robustness costs accuracy.** Tsipras et al. proved that on a simple distribution the standard accuracy of a model
  and its robustness to adversarial perturbations trade off <Cite id="tsipras2019" />. In practice adversarially
  trained models lose clean accuracy.
- **Empirical is not certified.** Surviving known attacks does not prove that no adversarial example exists. Guarantees
  need <NoteLink to="certified-robustness">certified defences</NoteLink>.
