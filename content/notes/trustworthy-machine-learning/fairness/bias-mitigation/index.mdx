<Definition>
**Bias mitigation** changes a learning pipeline so that its classifier satisfies, or comes closer to satisfying, a group
<NoteLink to="fairness-criteria">fairness criterion</NoteLink> such as
<NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> or separation. **Pre-processing** changes the training data,
**in-processing** changes the training objective, and **post-processing** changes the predictions of a trained model
using the protected attribute $A$ <Cite id="barocas2023" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier trained to minimise error learns whatever associations the data contain. If the label $Y$ and the features
$X$ both depend on the group $A$, the classifier's decisions and errors differ between groups. Removing $A$ from the
features does not help when other features are correlated with it: postcode, name or purchase history carry the same
information. Mitigation targets a chosen criterion directly. Notation below: $\hat Y$ is a binary prediction, $R$ a
score, $\pi_a = \pr(Y = 1 \mid A = a)$ the base rate of group $a$.

## Mechanism

### Pre-processing

**Reweighing** assigns each training example with group $a$ and label $y$ the weight
<Cite id="kamiran2012" />

$$
w(a, y) = \frac{\pr(A = a)\,\pr(Y = y)}{\pr(A = a, Y = y)},
$$

with the probabilities estimated by counts. Under the weighted distribution, the
<NoteLink to="conditional-probability">joint probability</NoteLink> of $(a, y)$ is proportional to
$\pr(A = a, Y = y)\,w(a, y) = \pr(A = a)\,\pr(Y = y)$, which factorises. So the weighted training set has $A \perp Y$:
every group has the same base rate. A learner trained on it has no label-driven reason to give groups different positive
rates. The weights also preserve the group sizes, since $\sum_y \pr(A = a, Y = y)\,w(a, y) = \pr(A = a)$.

**Fair representations** learn a map from $\xvec$ to a representation $\zvec$ that predicts $Y$ but carries little
information about $A$. Zemel et al. map each input to a probability vector over $K$ learned prototypes and minimise a
weighted sum of three losses: the reconstruction error of $\xvec$, the classification error of $Y$, and the difference
between the groups' average prototype memberships <Cite id="zemel2013" />. The last term drives the representation, and
any classifier built on it, towards independence.

### In-processing

**Constraints.** Zafar et al. constrain a linear classifier with parameters $\thetavec$ through the empirical covariance
between the group indicator $z_i \in \set{0, 1}$ and the signed distance to the decision boundary,
$d_{\thetavec}(\xvec_i) = \thetavec^\top\xvec_i$ <Cite id="zafar2017" />:

$$
\abs{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_i - \bar z)\,\thetavec^\top\xvec_i} \le c.
$$

The constraint is linear in $\thetavec$, so <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> with it
remains a convex problem. The threshold $c$ trades accuracy for a smaller dependence of the decision on the group.

**Reductions.** Agarwal et al. write the criterion as linear constraints $\Mmat\,\muvec(Q) \le \cvec$ on the group-wise
rates $\muvec(Q)$ of a randomised classifier $Q$, a distribution over classifiers $h$ from a base class
<Cite id="agarwal2018" />. They solve the saddle-point problem of the Lagrangian

$$
L(Q, \lambdavec) = \text{err}(Q) + \lambdavec^\top\big(\Mmat\,\muvec(Q) - \cvec\big), \quad \lambdavec \ge 0.
$$

Each rate, such as $\pr(h(X) = 1 \mid A = a)$, is an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> of $h(X)$
weighted by a function of $(A, Y)$. For fixed $\lambdavec$, the Lagrangian is therefore an expectation of per-example
costs times $h(X)$, which is a cost-sensitive classification problem. Any learner that accepts example weights solves
it. An exponentiated-gradient update on $\lambdavec$ alternates with these best responses, and the returned $Q$ is a
mixture of the classifiers found.

**Adversarial debiasing.** A predictor with weights $\Wmat$ is trained against an adversary that predicts $A$ from the
prediction $\hat Y$, for independence, or from $(\hat Y, Y)$, for separation <Cite id="zhang2018adversarial" />. With
predictor loss $L_P$ and adversary loss $L_A$, the predictor moves along

$$
\nabla_{\Wmat} L_P - \proj_{\nabla_{\Wmat} L_A}\nabla_{\Wmat} L_P - \alpha\,\nabla_{\Wmat} L_A.
$$

The projection term removes the component of the predictor's step along $\nabla_{\Wmat} L_A$, and the last term moves
the predictor in the direction that increases $L_A$.

### Post-processing

Hardt, Price and Srebro derive a new predictor $\tilde Y$ from a trained score $R$ and the attribute $A$ alone
<Cite id="hardt2016" />.

**1. The achievable rates of one group form a <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex set</NoteLink>.** A
randomised rule for group $a$ predicts positive with probability $q(r)$ when $R = r$. Its rates are
$\text{FPR} = \expect[q(R) \mid Y = 0, A = a]$ and $\text{TPR} = \expect[q(R) \mid Y = 1, A = a]$. Both are linear in
$q$, so mixing two rules mixes their $(\text{FPR}, \text{TPR})$ points: the achievable set $C_a$ is convex. It contains
every threshold rule $q(r) = \indicator[r > \tau]$, so it contains the convex hull of the group's
<NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">ROC curve</NoteLink>. When the ROC curve is concave, no
rule based on $R$ has a higher <Gloss name="tpr" /> at a given <Gloss name="fpr" />, and $C_a$ above the diagonal is
exactly the region between the diagonal and the curve.

**2. <NoteLink to="group-fairness-metrics">Equalised odds</NoteLink> means a common point.** Separation for a binary
$\tilde Y$ requires the same $\gammavec = (\text{FPR}, \text{TPR})$ in every group. The feasible points are
$\bigcap_a C_a$, an intersection of convex sets.

**3. The expected loss is linear in $\gammavec$.** With costs $c_{\text{FP}}$ and $c_{\text{FN}}$,

$$
\expect[\ell(\tilde Y, Y)] = \sum_a \pr(A = a)\big[c_{\text{FP}}\,(1 - \pi_a)\,\gamma_0 + c_{\text{FN}}\,\pi_a\,(1 - \gamma_1)\big].
$$

Minimising a linear function over an intersection of convex polygons (ROC curves over finitely many thresholds) is a
<NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear program</NoteLink>. The optimum lies on the upper edge of the intersection,
which is the lower of the groups' <Gloss name="roc" /> hulls. Each group then reaches the common point by randomising:
between two thresholds, or between one threshold and a constant rule. Equal opportunity constrains only $\gamma_1$, so
each group chooses its own point on a common horizontal line.

<PostProcessing />

Post-processing needs no retraining and works with any model. Pleiss et al. show that when the score must also stay
calibrated within groups, only one weighted combination of the error rates can be equalised, and that post-processing
achieves it by replacing a random fraction of one group's scores with the group's base rate <Cite id="pleiss2017" />.

## Worked example

**Reweighing.** A training set of 100 people has 60 in group $a$, of whom 36 are positive, and 40 in group $b$, of whom
8 are positive. So $\pr(A = a) = 0.6$ and $\pr(Y = 1) = 0.44$. The weights are
$w(a, 1) = 0.6 \cdot 0.44 / 0.36 = 0.733$, $w(a, 0) = 0.6 \cdot 0.56 / 0.24 = 1.4$,
$w(b, 1) = 0.4 \cdot 0.44 / 0.08 = 2.2$ and $w(b, 0) = 0.4 \cdot 0.56 / 0.32 = 0.7$. The weighted base rate of group $a$
is $36 \cdot 0.733 / (36 \cdot 0.733 + 24 \cdot 1.4) = 26.4 / 60 = 0.44$, and that of group $b$ is
$8 \cdot 2.2 / (8 \cdot 2.2 + 32 \cdot 0.7) = 17.6 / 40 = 0.44$. The weighted group totals stay 60 and 40.

**Post-processing a binary classifier.** A classifier $\hat Y$ has $(\text{FPR}, \text{TPR}) = (0.2, 0.8)$ in group $a$
and $(0.1, 0.6)$ in group $b$. A derived predictor may flip $\hat Y$ at random, so each group's achievable set is the
parallelogram with corners $(0, 0)$, $(\text{FPR}, \text{TPR})$, $(1, 1)$ and $(1 - \text{FPR}, 1 - \text{TPR})$. The
upper edge of group $a$'s set starts along $\text{TPR} = 4\,\text{FPR}$; that of group $b$ continues past $(0.1, 0.6)$
along $\text{TPR} = 0.6 + \tfrac{0.4}{0.9}(\text{FPR} - 0.1)$. The two edges cross at $(0.15625, 0.625)$, the point of
the intersection with the largest $\text{TPR} - \text{FPR}$. Group $a$ reaches it by keeping $\hat Y$ with probability
$0.15625 / 0.2 = 0.78125$ and predicting negative otherwise. Group $b$ reaches it by predicting positive with
probability $0.0625$ and keeping $\hat Y$ otherwise, which gives
$(0.1 + 0.9 \cdot 0.0625,\ 0.6 + 0.4 \cdot 0.0625) = (0.15625, 0.625)$.

## Cost

- **Accuracy.** Every method restricts the set of classifiers, so its unconstrained error is a lower bound. In the
  worked example group $a$'s true-positive rate falls from 0.8 to 0.625.
- **Computation.** Reweighing and post-processing are cheap. Reductions retrain the base learner once per iteration.
  Adversarial training adds a second network and is unstable in the manner of <Gloss name="gan" plural />.
- **Randomisation.** Post-processing and reductions return randomised classifiers: two identical people can receive
  different decisions.

## Trade-offs

- **Stage.** Pre-processing makes no promise about the downstream model. Post-processing gives exact guarantees on the
  data it was fitted to but needs $A$ at prediction time. In-processing sits between them.
- **Use of the protected attribute.** Group-specific thresholds treat people differently according to $A$, which some
  legal systems forbid as disparate treatment even when the aim is to reduce disparate outcomes.
- **Generalisation.** Constraints enforced on a training sample hold on new data only approximately; small groups need
  held-out checks with <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>.
- **Which criterion.** Mitigation enforces one criterion. When base rates differ, it moves the classifier away from the
  others (see <NoteLink to="impossibility-of-fairness">the impossibility of fairness</NoteLink>).
