<Definition>
Let $V$ be a set of individuals, $O$ a set of outcomes, and $d : V \times V \to [0, \infty)$ a **similarity metric** that
says how alike two individuals are for the task at hand. A randomised classifier $M$ maps each individual $x$ to a
probability distribution $M(x)$ over $O$. $M$ is **individually fair** if it satisfies the <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">Lipschitz condition</NoteLink>

$$
D\big(M(x), M(y)\big) \le d(x, y) \quad \text{for all } x, y \in V,
$$

where $D$ is a distance between distributions, such as total variation <Cite id="dwork2012" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Group <NoteLink to="fairness-criteria">fairness criteria</NoteLink> compare averages over groups. A classifier can
equalise the acceptance rates of two groups while accepting qualified people in one group and random people in the other.
Individual fairness closes that gap by constraining pairs of people. Two applicants who are alike for the purpose of the
decision must receive similar distributions over decisions. Randomised outputs are what make the condition satisfiable:
a deterministic yes-or-no rule jumps from 0 to 1 somewhere, and two nearby people on either side of the jump would violate
any small bound.

## Details

### Distances between outcome distributions

Dwork et al. use two choices of $D$ <Cite id="dwork2012" />. The **total variation distance**
$D_{\text{tv}}(P, Q) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{o \in O}\abs{P(o) - Q(o)}$ equals the largest difference $\abs{P(E) - Q(E)}$ over
events $E \subseteq O$. The **relative $\ell_\infty$ distance** $D_\infty(P, Q) = \sup_o \log\max\set{P(o)/Q(o),\ Q(o)/P(o)}$
bounds the ratio of probabilities instead. The $D_\infty$ form mirrors <NoteLink to="differential-privacy">differential privacy</NoteLink>, which bounds the same log ratio for datasets that differ in one record; Dwork et al. note the connection <Cite id="dwork2012" />. For two outcomes, accept and reject, $D_{\text{tv}}$ is the difference of the
acceptance probabilities.

### Fair classification as a linear program

Let $L(x, o)$ be the loss of giving outcome $o$ to $x$, and let the variables $\mu_x(o) = \pr(M(x) = o)$ describe $M$ on a
finite population. The least-loss fair classifier solves

$$
\min_{\mu}\ \sum_{x \in V}\sum_{o \in O}\mu_x(o)\,L(x, o)
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad D_{\text{tv}}(\mu_x, \mu_y) \le d(x, y)\ \ \forall x, y, \qquad \mu_x \in \Delta(O)\ \ \forall x.
$$

The objective is linear in $\mu$. Each total-variation constraint becomes linear after introducing variables
$t_{xy}(o) \ge \abs{\mu_x(o) - \mu_y(o)}$, written as two linear inequalities, with $\frac{1}{2}\sum_o t_{xy}(o) \le d(x, y)$.
The whole problem is therefore a <NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear program</NoteLink> with a number of variables
and constraints polynomial in $\abs{V}$ and $\abs{O}$, solvable in polynomial time <Cite id="dwork2012" />. The same holds for
$D_\infty$, whose constraints $\mu_x(o) \le e^{d(x, y)}\mu_y(o)$ are also linear.

### From individual to group fairness

Individual fairness implies a bounded version of <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> (<NoteLink to="group-fairness-metrics">statistical parity</NoteLink>) between two groups $S$ and $T$,
viewed as distributions over individuals. The bound is the **earthmover distance** under $d$:

$$
d_{\text{EM}}(S, T) = \min_{h}\sum_{x, y} h(x, y)\,d(x, y),
$$

minimised over couplings $h$: joint distributions on $V \times V$ whose first marginal is $S$ and second is $T$. It is the
least average distance over which the individuals of $S$ must be moved to become the individuals of $T$.

**Claim.** If $M$ is $(D_{\text{tv}}, d)$-Lipschitz, then for every event $E \subseteq O$,
$\abs{\pr_{x \sim S}(M(x) \in E) - \pr_{y \sim T}(M(y) \in E)} \le d_{\text{EM}}(S, T)$ <Cite id="dwork2012" />.

**Proof.** Let $h$ be an optimal coupling. Because the marginals of $h$ are $S$ and $T$,

$$
\pr_{x \sim S}(M(x) \in E) - \pr_{y \sim T}(M(y) \in E) = \sum_{x, y} h(x, y)\big[\mu_x(E) - \mu_y(E)\big].
$$

Each bracket is at most $D_{\text{tv}}(\mu_x, \mu_y)$ in absolute value, by the event form of total variation, and that is
at most $d(x, y)$ by the Lipschitz condition. So the absolute value of the sum is at most
$\sum_{x, y} h(x, y)\,d(x, y) = d_{\text{EM}}(S, T)$. $\blacksquare$

When the two groups are close under $d$, individual fairness forces nearly equal acceptance rates. When they are far
apart, it permits different rates. Dwork et al. treat the second case separately: to impose statistical parity anyway,
they map $S$ onto $T$ with the optimal coupling and treat each member of $S$ as the members of $T$ it is matched to,
which they call fair affirmative action <Cite id="dwork2012" />.

## Worked example

Groups $S = \set{x_1, x_2}$ and $T = \set{y_1, y_2}$ each weight their two members equally. The metric gives
$d(x_1, y_1) = 0.1$, $d(x_2, y_2) = 0.3$ and distance 1 for every other pair of distinct people. The coupling that
matches $x_1$ with $y_1$ and $x_2$ with $y_2$ is optimal, so $d_{\text{EM}}(S, T) = 0.5 \cdot 0.1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.3 = 0.2$.

With two outcomes, the Lipschitz condition only requires $\abs{\mu_{x_1} - \mu_{y_1}} \le 0.1$ and
$\abs{\mu_{x_2} - \mu_{y_2}} \le 0.3$ for the acceptance probabilities, since the other pairs allow a difference of 1.
The classifier $\mu_{x_1} = 0.6$, $\mu_{y_1} = 0.5$, $\mu_{x_2} = 0.7$, $\mu_{y_2} = 0.4$ meets both constraints with equality.
Its acceptance rates are $0.65$ for $S$ and $0.45$ for $T$, a gap of exactly $0.2$. The bound is tight here.

## Counterfactual fairness

Kusner et al. replace the similarity metric with a causal model <Cite id="kusner2017" />. Given a structural causal model
with background variables $U$, a predictor $\hat Y$ is **counterfactually fair** if, for every individual with features
$X = x$ and attribute $A = a$, and every outcome $y$ and alternative attribute value $a'$,

$$
\pr\big(\hat Y_{A \leftarrow a}(U) = y \mid X = x, A = a\big) = \pr\big(\hat Y_{A \leftarrow a'}(U) = y \mid X = x, A = a\big).
$$

The prediction would have had the same distribution had the individual's attribute been different, with everything not
caused by $A$ held fixed. A predictor that uses only variables that are not descendants of $A$ in the causal graph
satisfies the condition. The criterion is only as credible as the assumed causal model (see
<NoteLink to="causal-inference-from-observational-data">causal inference from observational data</NoteLink>).

## Assumptions

- A task-specific metric $d$ exists and is known. Dwork et al. envisage it being set by a regulator or a panel with
  domain knowledge, and publicly auditable <Cite id="dwork2012" />.
- The classifier may randomise. Deterministic classifiers satisfy the Lipschitz condition only for metrics with gaps.

## Pitfalls

- **The metric is the whole problem.** A metric that encodes historical bias, for example by treating a correlate of
  $A$ as relevant, makes a biased classifier individually fair. Choosing $d$ is a normative act, not a technical one
  <Cite id="barocas2023" />.
- **Individual fairness does not imply group parity.** The earthmover bound allows large gaps between groups that are
  far apart under $d$.
- **Scale.** The linear program has a constraint for every pair of individuals, $O(\abs{V}^2)$ in total, and must be
  re-solved when new individuals arrive.
