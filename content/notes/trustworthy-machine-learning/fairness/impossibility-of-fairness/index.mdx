<Definition>
The **impossibility of fairness** is a pair of results about two groups with different base rates $p_1 \ne p_2$, where
the base rate $p_a$ is the fraction of group $a$ with $Y = 1$. For binary predictions, equal <NoteLink to="precision-recall-and-f-score">positive predictive value</NoteLink>,
equal <Gloss name="fn">false-negative</Gloss> rate and equal <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> cannot hold together unless the classifier
makes no false positives or predicts nobody positive <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />. For scores,
calibration within groups, balance for the positive class and balance for the negative class cannot hold together unless
the score is perfect <Cite id="kleinberg2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A risk score used in two groups is asked to satisfy two natural demands. The score should mean the same in each group:
of the people flagged high-risk, the same fraction should reoffend, whichever group they belong to. And the score should
err equally: people who will not reoffend should be flagged at the same rate in each group. The two demands are linked
by <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> through the base rate. If one group has a higher base rate, a
flag carries more evidence of the outcome in that group. Keeping the positive predictive value equal then forces the
error rates apart.

The results were prompted by the debate over the COMPAS recidivism score, which was calibrated across racial groups but
had unequal false-positive rates <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />.

## Binary predictions: Chouldechova's identity

Fix one group with base rate $p$, and write $\text{TPR}$, $\text{FPR}$ and $\text{FNR} = 1 - \text{TPR}$ for its <Gloss name="tp">true-positive</Gloss>,
<Gloss name="fp">false-positive</Gloss> and false-negative rates. The fraction of the group predicted positive and truly positive is
$p\,\text{TPR}$; the fraction predicted positive and truly negative is $(1 - p)\,\text{FPR}$. The positive predictive value
is the share of the first among all predicted positives:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{p\,\text{TPR}}{p\,\text{TPR} + (1 - p)\,\text{FPR}}.
$$

Solving for $\text{FPR}$ gives $(1 - p)\,\text{FPR}\,\text{PPV} = p\,\text{TPR}\,(1 - \text{PPV})$, so

$$
\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1 - p}\cdot\frac{1 - \text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot(1 - \text{FNR}).
$$

This holds in every group. If two groups share $\text{PPV}$ and $\text{FNR}$, the right-hand side differs only through
the factor $p/(1 - p)$, which is strictly increasing in $p$. Unequal base rates therefore force unequal false-positive
rates <Cite id="chouldechova2017" />. The only escapes are $\text{PPV} = 1$ with $\text{FPR} = 0$ in both groups, or
$\text{TPR} = 0$, which predicts nobody positive.

## Scores: the result of Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan

Let a score $s \in [0, 1]$ be assigned to each person. Kleinberg et al. define three conditions
<Cite id="kleinberg2017" />:

- **Calibration within groups.** In every group, among the people assigned score $v$, a fraction $v$ are positive.
- **Balance for the negative class.** The average score of negatives, $x_a$, is the same in every group.
- **Balance for the positive class.** The average score of positives, $y_a$, is the same in every group.

The balance conditions are the score analogues of equal false-positive and <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rates</Gloss>.

**Proof that the three conditions force equal base rates or perfect prediction.** Consider group $a$, with $N_a$ people,
of whom $n_a = p_a N_a$ are positive. Calibration within groups says that each score value $v$ holds a fraction $v$ of
positives. Summing the scores of everyone in the group, each bin of $m$ people with score $v$ contributes $v m$, which is
the number of positives in that bin. So the total score equals the number of positives:

$$
\sum_{i \in a} s_i = n_a.
$$

The same total, split by true class, is $(N_a - n_a)\,x_a + n_a\,y_a$. Equating the two and dividing by $N_a$,

$$
(1 - p_a)\,x_a = p_a\,(1 - y_a).
$$

Balance for both classes sets $x_a = x$ and $y_a = y$ in both groups. Two cases remain.

- If $y < 1$, dividing gives $x / (1 - y) = p_a / (1 - p_a)$ for both groups. The map $p \mapsto p/(1 - p)$ is strictly
  increasing, so $p_1 = p_2$: the base rates are equal.
- If $y = 1$, then $(1 - p_a)\,x = 0$, so $x = 0$ whenever $p_a < 1$. Every positive has score 1 and every negative has
  score 0: prediction is perfect.

Geometrically, each group constrains $(x, y)$ to a line through $(0, 1)$ with slope set by its base rate, and two lines
with different slopes meet only at $(0, 1)$ <Cite id="kleinberg2017" />. Kleinberg et al. also show that the conditions
cannot hold approximately unless the base rates are approximately equal or prediction is approximately perfect.

## Worked example

**Binary predictions.** Two groups have base rates $p_1 = 0.4$ and $p_2 = 0.2$. A classifier has $\text{PPV} = 0.7$ and
$\text{FNR} = 0.3$ in both. By the identity, group 1 has $\text{FPR} = \frac{0.4}{0.6}\cdot\frac{0.3}{0.7}\cdot 0.7 = 0.2$
and group 2 has $\text{FPR} = \frac{0.2}{0.8}\cdot\frac{0.3}{0.7}\cdot 0.7 = 0.075$. Negatives in group 1 are flagged
$2.67$ times as often as negatives in group 2, although the flag is equally reliable in both.

**Calibrated scores.** Group 1 has 100 people: 50 scored $0.2$ and 50 scored $0.6$. Calibration puts $10 + 30 = 40$
positives in it, so $p_1 = 0.4$. Group 2 has 100 people: 50 scored $0.1$ and 50 scored $0.3$, with $5 + 15 = 20$ positives,
so $p_2 = 0.2$.

| group | base rate $p_a$ | mean score of positives $y_a$ | mean score of negatives $x_a$ | $(1 - p_a)\,x_a$ | $p_a\,(1 - y_a)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.4 | $(10 \cdot 0.2 + 30 \cdot 0.6)/40 = 0.5$ | $(40 \cdot 0.2 + 20 \cdot 0.6)/60 = 0.333$ | 0.2 | 0.2 |
| 2 | 0.2 | $(5 \cdot 0.1 + 15 \cdot 0.3)/20 = 0.25$ | $(45 \cdot 0.1 + 35 \cdot 0.3)/80 = 0.1875$ | 0.15 | 0.15 |

Both scores are calibrated, and the identity $(1 - p_a)\,x_a = p_a\,(1 - y_a)$ holds in each group. Balance fails for both
classes: positives in group 2 receive half the average score of positives in group 1.

## Consequences

- **A choice is unavoidable.** With unequal base rates, a practitioner must choose which of sufficiency and separation
  to give up, or accept a trade-off between them. The <NoteLink to="fairness-criteria">fairness criteria</NoteLink> note
  proves the general form: separation and sufficiency are incompatible whenever $A$ and $Y$ are dependent and every
  joint event has positive probability.
- **Relaxations do not escape.** Pleiss et al. show that calibration is compatible with equalising a single weighted
  combination of the false-positive and false-negative rates, and that achieving it requires withholding information:
  randomly replacing some predictions in one group with the group's base rate <Cite id="pleiss2017" />.
- **Equal base rates remove the conflict** only in the population where they are equal. Base rates measured on a
  different population, or on labels recorded with bias, do not settle the question.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the result as "fairness is impossible".** It says that three particular statistical conditions cannot hold
  together. Which condition matters depends on the decision being made.
- **Ignoring estimation error.** Base rates, precision and error rates estimated on small groups are uncertain; an
  apparent violation can be sampling noise.
