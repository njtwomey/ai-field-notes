<Definition>
In **class-conditional label noise**, each training label $y \in \set{1, \dots, K}$ is replaced by an observed label
$\tilde y$ drawn from a row of a **noise transition matrix** $\Tmat$, with $T_{ij} = \Pr(\tilde y = j \mid y = i)$,
independently of the input $\xvec$. **Learning with noisy labels** means training on pairs $(\xvec, \tilde y)$ a
classifier that performs well on the clean labels $y$. **Loss correction** modifies the loss, using $\Tmat$, so that its
minimiser on noisy data is the minimiser of the original loss on clean data <Cite id="natarajan2013" />
<Cite id="patrini2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Annotators disagree, crowd workers make mistakes, and labels scraped from the web are wrong a few to tens of percent
of the time <Cite id="frenay2014" />. A flexible model fitted to such labels learns the noise: deep networks can fit
even completely random labels on standard image data sets <Cite id="zhang2017" />. If the pattern of mistakes is
known, it can be undone, because the noisy labels are a known mixture of the clean ones.

## How noise changes the posterior

By the law of total probability, the noisy class posterior mixes the clean one through $\Tmat$:

$$
\Pr(\tilde y = j \mid \xvec) = \sum_{i} \Pr(\tilde y = j \mid y = i)\Pr(y = i \mid \xvec) = \sum_i T_{ij}\,\Pr(y = i \mid \xvec),
$$

or, with $\pvec(\xvec)$ the vector of clean posteriors and $\tilde\pvec(\xvec)$ the noisy ones,
$\tilde\pvec(\xvec) = \Tmat^\top\pvec(\xvec)$. A classifier trained on noisy labels estimates $\tilde\pvec$, not $\pvec$.

**Binary case.** With labels $\pm 1$, write $\rho_+ = \Pr(\tilde y = -1 \mid y = +1)$ and
$\rho_- = \Pr(\tilde y = +1 \mid y = -1)$, with $\rho_+ + \rho_- < 1$. The clean posterior $\eta(\xvec) = \Pr(y = +1 \mid \xvec)$
and the noisy one $\tilde\eta(\xvec)$ satisfy

$$
\tilde\eta = (1 - \rho_+)\eta + \rho_-(1 - \eta) = (1 - \rho_+ - \rho_-)\,\eta + \rho_-.
$$

This is an increasing affine map, so ranking by $\tilde\eta$ is ranking by $\eta$. The clean Bayes rule predicts $+1$
when $\eta > 1/2$, which is when

$$
\tilde\eta > \frac{1 - \rho_+ - \rho_-}{2} + \rho_- = \frac{1 - \rho_+ + \rho_-}{2}.
$$

So the optimal clean classifier is the noisy posterior thresholded at $(1 - \rho_+ + \rho_-)/2$ instead of $1/2$
<Cite id="natarajan2013" />. With symmetric noise, $\rho_+ = \rho_-$, the threshold stays at $1/2$: the Bayes classifier
is unchanged, and noise only makes it harder to estimate. This is Angluin and Laird's **random classification noise**
<Cite id="angluin1988" />.

## Backward correction

The idea is to build a loss $\tilde\ell$ on noisy labels whose expectation over the noise equals the clean loss. For a
prediction $t$, collect the losses for each possible label into a vector
$\boldsymbol{\ell}(t) = (\ell(t, 1), \dots, \ell(t, K))$. The expected corrected loss given clean label $i$ is
$\sum_j T_{ij}\tilde\ell(t, j) = (\Tmat\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t))_i$. Requiring this to equal $\ell(t, i)$ for every $i$
gives

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t) = \Tmat^{-1}\boldsymbol{\ell}(t),
$$

which exists when $\Tmat$ is invertible <Cite id="patrini2017" />. For every $\xvec$ and $t$, the noisy-data risk of
$\tilde\ell$ then equals the clean-data risk of $\ell$, so minimising one minimises the other. In the binary case this
is the unbiased estimator of Natarajan et al. <Cite id="natarajan2013" />:

$$
\tilde\ell(t, y) = \frac{(1 - \rho_{-y})\,\ell(t, y) - \rho_{y}\,\ell(t, -y)}{1 - \rho_+ - \rho_-}.
$$

The corrected loss can be negative, and its variance grows as $\Tmat$ approaches singularity, since $\Tmat^{-1}$ then
has large entries. In practice it is less stable than forward correction.

## Forward correction

Forward correction keeps the model predicting clean posteriors $\hat\pvec(\xvec)$ and passes them through the noise
before the loss:

$$
\ell^{\to}(\hat\pvec, \tilde y) = \ell\big(\Tmat^\top\hat\pvec, \tilde y\big).
$$

For log loss this is $-\log(\Tmat^\top\hat\pvec)_{\tilde y}$: the model is scored on how well it predicts the noisy
label once the known corruption is applied. Log loss is minimised in expectation by the true distribution of the
label it scores, so the expected forward loss on noisy data is minimised when $\Tmat^\top\hat\pvec = \tilde\pvec =
\Tmat^\top\pvec$. When $\Tmat$ is invertible this forces $\hat\pvec = \pvec$: the model learns the clean posterior
<Cite id="patrini2017" />. In a neural network, forward correction is one extra linear layer, fixed to $\Tmat^\top$,
after the softmax during training, removed at test time.

## Worked example

Take $\rho_+ = 0.2$ and $\rho_- = 0.1$, so, ordering the classes as $(+1, -1)$,
$\Tmat = \begin{pmatrix}0.8 & 0.2\\ 0.1 & 0.9\end{pmatrix}$.

- **Threshold.** The clean Bayes rule is the noisy posterior thresholded at $(1 - 0.2 + 0.1)/2 = 0.45$.
- **Forward.** A model predicting clean probabilities $\hat\pvec = (0.8, 0.2)$ implies noisy probabilities
  $\Tmat^\top\hat\pvec = (0.8 \cdot 0.8 + 0.1 \cdot 0.2,\ 0.2 \cdot 0.8 + 0.9 \cdot 0.2) = (0.66, 0.34)$. Log loss is
  computed on these.
- **Backward.** The log losses of $\hat\pvec$ are $\boldsymbol{\ell} = (-\log 0.8, -\log 0.2) = (0.223, 1.609)$. With
  $\Tmat^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.7}\begin{pmatrix}0.9 & -0.2 \\ -0.1 & 0.8\end{pmatrix}$, the corrected losses are
  $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}} = (-0.173, 1.807)$. A noisy label $+1$ now earns a negative loss. Their expectation under clean
  label $+1$ is $0.8 \cdot (-0.173) + 0.2 \cdot 1.807 = 0.223$, the clean loss, as designed.

## Estimating the transition matrix

Both corrections need $\Tmat$. Without clean labels it can be estimated from **anchor points**: inputs that belong to
class $i$ with certainty, $\Pr(y = i \mid \xvec) = 1$. At such an $\xvec$, $\tilde\pvec(\xvec) = \Tmat^\top\evec_i$ is row $i$
of $\Tmat$. Patrini et al. fit a model to the noisy labels, take as the anchor for class $i$ the training input with
the highest predicted $\Pr(\tilde y = i \mid \xvec)$, or a high percentile of it for robustness, and read row $i$ from its
predicted noisy posterior <Cite id="patrini2017" />. **Confident learning** instead counts, per class, the examples whose
predicted probability of each other class exceeds that class's average self-confidence, estimating the joint
distribution of noisy and clean labels, and removes examples judged mislabelled <Cite id="northcutt2021" />.

## Other defences

- **Early stopping.** Deep networks fit the clean, consistent patterns before they memorise noisy labels
  <Cite id="arpit2017" />, so validation accuracy on clean data peaks before training accuracy does.
- **Small-loss selection.** Examples with large training loss early in training are disproportionately mislabelled.
  Training on the small-loss examples, or down-weighting the others, reduces the noise rate.
- **Robust losses.** Bounded losses, such as the mean absolute error on probabilities, are less affected by a
  mislabelled example than the unbounded log loss, at the cost of slower training.

## Assumptions

- The noise depends on the class only, not on $\xvec$. Real annotators err more on hard examples, which violates this.
- $\Tmat$ is invertible, and for binary problems $\rho_+ + \rho_- < 1$: labels still carry information.
- Anchor points exist, or $\Tmat$ is known from a small clean subset.

## Pitfalls

- **Clean validation data.** Model selection on noisy validation labels selects for fitting the noise. Keep a small
  clean set.
- **Misestimated $\Tmat$.** Corrections with a wrong $\Tmat$ can be worse than none, especially backward correction.
- **Noise versus ambiguity.** Some "noise" is genuine ambiguity: the input supports several labels. Correcting it as
  noise discards real uncertainty.
