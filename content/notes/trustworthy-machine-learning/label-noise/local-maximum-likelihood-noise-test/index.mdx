<Definition>
The **local maximum-likelihood test** of Yang, Poyiadzi, Twomey and Santos-Rodriguez tests $H_0\colon \alpha = \beta$
(<NoteLink to="label-noise-models">uniform label noise</NoteLink>) against $H_1\colon \alpha \ne \beta$ (class-conditional noise), where
$\alpha = \Pr(\tilde Y = -1 \mid Y = 1)$ and $\beta = \Pr(\tilde Y = 1 \mid Y = -1)$ <Cite id="yang2024" />. Like the parametric
test of Poyiadzi et al., it uses expert-supplied <NoteLink to="estimating-noise-rates">anchor points</NoteLink> whose true posterior is $1/2$, where the noisy posterior is
$1/2$ under $H_0$ and $(1 - \alpha + \beta)/2$ under $H_1$ <Cite id="poyiadzi2022" />. It estimates the posterior at each
anchor by local <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> instead of a global one, and uses a sandwich estimate of its variance.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$$
H_0\colon \alpha = \beta \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1\colon \alpha \ne \beta.
$$

The null is uniform noise, including no noise. Under $H_0$ the noisy posterior at an anchor is $1/2$; under $H_1$ it is
$(1 - \alpha + \beta)/2$.

## Assumptions

- **Smooth posterior.** $r(\xvec)$ is smooth; no parametric form is assumed.
- **Instance-independent noise**, bounded as in the parametric test.
- **Anchors** with $\eta(\xvec) = 1/2$ (strict) or $\eta(\xvec) = 1/2 + \epsilon$ with $\epsilon \sim \Unif([-\delta, \delta])$ (relaxed),
  supplied by the user and surrounded by enough data for a local fit.
- **Bias ignored.** Smoothing estimators are biased: the <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> is centred on $\bar r_n(\xvec) =
  \expect[\hat r_n(\xvec)]$, not on $r(\xvec)$ <Cite id="wasserman2006" />. Remedies include estimating the bias,
  undersmoothing and <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> bias correction. The test as proposed ignores the bias, which inflates its type I error
  (see Pitfalls).

## Test statistic

### Why a local fit

The parametric test fits one logistic regression to the whole data set and relies on the asymptotic normality of its
maximum-likelihood estimate (see <NoteLink to="hypothesis-testing-for-class-conditional-label-noise">hypothesis testing
for class-conditional label noise</NoteLink>). Its null distribution is correct only if the log-odds are linear in the
inputs. When the true boundary is curved, the fitted line does not pass through the anchors, the fitted posterior there
is not $1/2$ even without noise, and the test rejects clean data. Unless the data-generating process is known, the
parametric test can therefore report noise that is not there <Cite id="yang2024" />. A local likelihood fit makes no
global assumption about the shape of the log-odds, so it is less exposed to misspecification.

### Local logistic regression

The data are $(\Xmat, \yvec) = \set{(\xvec_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^N$ with $\xvec_i \in \reals^d$. The model is
$Y_i \sim \Bern(r(\xvec_i))$ with $r(\xvec) = \Pr(Y = 1 \mid X = \xvec)$ a smooth function in $[0, 1]$, and log-odds
$\xi(\xvec) = \operatorname{logit} r(\xvec) = \log\frac{r(\xvec)}{1 - r(\xvec)}$. Near a target point $\xvec$, approximate
$r$ at $\zvec$ by a local logistic function <Cite id="loader1999" />:

$$
r(\zvec) \approx g\big(\inner{\betavec}{A_p(\zvec - \xvec)}\big), \qquad g(u) = \frac{e^u}{1 + e^u},
$$

where $A_p$ is the vector of polynomial basis functions of order $p$. For two inputs and $p = 2$,
$A_2([v_0, v_1]) = [1, v_0, v_1, \frac12 v_0^2, v_0 v_1, \frac12 v_1^2]$. The **local log-likelihood** weights each point by
its distance to $\xvec$:

$$
\ell_{\xvec}(\betavec) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_{i,h}(\xvec)\,\ell\big(y_i, \inner{\betavec}{A_p(\xvec_i - \xvec)}\big), \qquad w_{i,h}(\xvec) = K\Big(\frac{\xvec - \xvec_i}{h}\Big),
$$

with $\ell(y, \xi) = y\log g(\xi) + (1 - y)\log(1 - g(\xi))$ the Bernoulli log-likelihood, $K$ the Gaussian kernel and $h$ the
**bandwidth**. With $\hat\betavec = \argmax_{\betavec}\ell_{\xvec}(\betavec)$, the estimate at $\xvec$ is the intercept's
probability, $\hat r(\xvec) = e^{\hat\beta_0}/(1 + e^{\hat\beta_0})$, because every other basis function is zero at $\zvec =
\xvec$. A fresh fit is made at each point of interest; nothing is assumed about $r$ far from $\xvec$. This is
<NoteLink to="local-regression">local regression</NoteLink> with a Bernoulli likelihood in place of squared error.

The bandwidth is chosen by leave-one-out cross-validated log-likelihood, $\sum_i \ell\big(Y_i, \hat\xi^{(-i)}(\xvec_i)\big)$,
where $\hat\xi^{(-i)}$ is fitted without point $i$. Sub-sampling the points reduces its cost.

### Sandwich variance

Write $\avec_i = A_p(\xvec_i - \xvec)$, $w_i = w_{i,h}(\xvec)$ and $p_i = g(\betavec^\top\avec_i)$. The estimate solves the local
score equation

$$
\svec(\hat\betavec) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i\,(y_i - \hat p_i)\,\avec_i = \zeros.
$$

A first-order expansion around the target $\betavec$ gives $\zeros \approx \svec(\betavec) - \Bmat(\hat\betavec - \betavec)$, with

$$
\Bmat = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i\,p_i(1 - p_i)\,\avec_i\avec_i^\top,
$$

so $\hat\betavec - \betavec \approx \Bmat^{-1}\svec(\betavec)$. The observations are independent with $\var(y_i) = p_i(1 - p_i)$, so
$\var(\svec) = \sum_i w_i^2\,p_i(1 - p_i)\,\avec_i\avec_i^\top$, estimated by

$$
\Cmat = \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat p_i)^2\,w_i^2\,\avec_i\avec_i^\top.
$$

The result is the sandwich form, a consistent estimator for local likelihood models
<Cite id="frolich2006" />:

$$
\hat\var[\hat\betavec_{\xvec}] = \Bmat^{-1}\Cmat\Bmat^{-1}.
$$

For an unweighted, correctly specified global fit, $\Cmat$ estimates $\Bmat$ and the sandwich collapses to $\Bmat^{-1}$, the
inverse information of the parametric test. With kernel weights, $\Bmat$ carries $w_i$ and $\Cmat$ carries $w_i^2$, so the
collapse fails and the sandwich is needed.

The delta method with $\hat r = g(\hat\beta_0)$ and $g' = g(1 - g)$ gives
$\var[\hat\eta(\xvec)] \approx \eta(\xvec)^2(1 - \eta(\xvec))^2\,\hat\var[\hat\betavec_{\xvec}]_{0,0}$. At an anchor, $\eta = 1/2$ and

$$
\var[\hat\eta(\xvec)] \approx \frac{1}{16}\,v(\xvec), \qquad v(\xvec) = \hat\var[\hat\betavec_{\xvec}]_{0,0}.
$$

Here $v$ is the intercept variance itself; the parametric test's $v$ includes the factor $1/16$.

**Two anchors.** Fits at different anchors $\xvec_j$ and $\xvec_l$ use the same observations, so they are correlated. From
$\hat\betavec_{\xvec_j} - \betavec_{\xvec_j} \approx \Bmat_{\xvec_j}^{-1}\svec_{\xvec_j}$,

$$
\cov\big[\hat\betavec_{\xvec_j}, \hat\betavec_{\xvec_l}\big] \approx \Bmat_{\xvec_j}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^N w_{i,\xvec_j}w_{i,\xvec_l}\,(y_i - \hat p_{i,\xvec_j})(y_i - \hat p_{i,\xvec_l})\,\avec_{i,\xvec_j}\avec_{i,\xvec_l}^\top\Big)\Bmat_{\xvec_l}^{-1},
$$

the cross-covariance of the two local score equations, and
$\cov(\hat\eta(\xvec_j), \hat\eta(\xvec_l)) = \frac{1}{16}\cov[\hat\betavec_{\xvec_j}, \hat\betavec_{\xvec_l}]_{0,0}$. Anchors far apart
relative to $h$ share almost no weight, and their covariance is near zero.

### The statistic

**One strict anchor.** Under $H_0$, $\hat\eta(\xvec) \sim \Gauss\big(\frac12, \frac{1}{16}v(\xvec)\big)$ and
$z = (\hat\eta(\xvec) - \frac12)/\sqrt{v(\xvec)/16}$.

**$k$ strict anchors.** With $\bar\eta = \frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k\hat\eta_i$, $\expect\bar\eta = \frac12$ and

$$
\var(\bar\eta) = \frac{1}{k^2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^k \var\hat\eta_i + 2\sum_{i=1}^k\sum_{j > i}\cov(\hat\eta_i, \hat\eta_j)\Big) = \frac{1}{16k^2}\,\operatorname{var}(\xvec_{[1:k]}),
$$

where $\operatorname{var}(\xvec_{[1:k]})$ is shorthand for the sum of all $k^2$ entries $v(\xvec_i)$ and
$\cov[\hat\betavec_{\xvec_i}, \hat\betavec_{\xvec_j}]_{0,0}$. The statistic is $z = (\bar\eta - \frac12)/\sqrt{\var(\bar\eta)}$.

**$k$ relaxed anchors.** As in the parametric test, the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total variance</NoteLink> adds a term for the spread of the anchors'
true posteriors, and the factor $\frac{1}{16}$ becomes $\frac{1}{16} - \frac{\delta^2}{6}$. For small $\delta$ the test keeps
$\var(\bar\eta) \approx \frac{1}{16k^2}\operatorname{var}(\xvec_{[1:k]})$.

## Decision rule

Reject $H_0$ at level $a$ when $\abs z > z_{1 - a/2}$; the two-sided <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is $2(1 - \Phi(\abs z))$, with $\Phi$ the standard
normal <Gloss name="cdf" />.

## Effect size

The effect is the shift $(\beta - \alpha)/2$ of the noisy posterior at the anchors. Power grows with that shift, with the
sample size $N$, which shrinks each local variance, and with the number $k$ of well-separated anchors, whose
nearly independent estimates average down the variance of $\bar\eta$. The local fit uses only the points near each
anchor, so for the same $N$ its variance is larger than the parametric one, and its power lower when the parametric
model is correct.

## Parametric or local

| topic | parametric test | local test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| flexibility | a linear log-odds; wrong if the boundary is curved | any smooth posterior; slower convergence |
| model fitting | one fit | one fit per anchor, plus bandwidth selection |
| bias | none if the model is correct | smoothing bias at every anchor |
| variance | inverse <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss> | sandwich $\Bmat^{-1}\Cmat\Bmat^{-1}$ |

Use the parametric test only when its linear log-odds assumption is known to hold; otherwise use the local one
<Cite id="yang2024" />. Whether a linear fit is adequate can itself be tested, for example with an F-test of $H_0\colon r(\xvec) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$ against a local fit <Cite id="wasserman2006" />.

In a simulation of this setup with $h = 1$ (100 runs per cell, level 0.05, $N \in \set{500, 1000}$ and
$k \in \set{1, 4, 16}$), the parametric test rejected clean labels in 18% to 92% of runs and the local test in 4% to 14%.
With $\beta = 0.1$ the local test rejected in 9% to 59% of runs, more often with more anchors and more data. The local
test's excess over 5% on clean data is the uncorrected smoothing bias.

## Worked example

A local fit at each of $k = 4$ anchors gives $\bar\eta = 0.62$, and the sum of all 16 entries of the intercept
covariance matrix is $1.92$.

- **Variance.** $\var(\bar\eta) = 1.92/(16 \cdot 16) = 0.0075$, so the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> is $0.0866$.
- **Statistic.** $z = 0.12/0.0866 = 1.39$, two-sided $p = 0.17$. $H_0$ is retained at $a = 0.05$.
- **More anchors.** With $k = 16$ anchors spread far apart relative to $h$, the cross-covariances are near zero and the
  sum grows roughly in proportion to $k$, to about $7.68$. Then $\var(\bar\eta) = 7.68/(16 \cdot 256) = 0.001875$, the standard
  error is $0.0433$, and the same $\bar\eta = 0.62$ gives $z = 2.77$, $p = 0.006$.

## Pitfalls

- **Empty neighbourhoods.** An anchor far from the data has few points with non-negligible weight, and the local fit is
  unstable or fails. Anchors must sit where the data are dense.
- **Smoothing bias.** Uncorrected bias shifts $\hat\eta$ at the anchors away from $1/2$ on clean data and inflates the
  type I error, most where the posterior is curved. Bootstrap bias correction, or a bootstrap estimate of the variance,
  reduces it.
- **Bandwidth.** A small $h$ gives few effective points and a large variance; a large $h$ drifts towards a global fit and
  its misspecification. Cross-validation selects $h$ for prediction, not for the test.
- **Slower convergence.** Nonparametric estimates converge more slowly than a correctly specified parametric one, so the
  local test has less power when the parametric model is right.
- **Anchors still matter.** The test inherits the parametric test's dependence on expert anchors, including the effect of
  relaxation. Where the anchors come from, and whether they are also in the training data, can change the conclusion.
  In a smart-home localisation study, readings on the stairs served as anchors between two floors. The test detected
  class-conditional noise when the stairs readings were also assigned to a floor in training, and not when they were
  held out: assigning the ambiguous stairs to one floor was itself the source of the noise <Cite id="yang2024" />.
