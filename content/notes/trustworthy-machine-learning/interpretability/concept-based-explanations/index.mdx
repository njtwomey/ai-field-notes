<Definition>
A **concept-based explanation** states how much a model's prediction depends on a concept that a person can name, such
as "striped" or "wheel", instead of on individual pixels or features. **Testing with concept activation vectors (TCAV)**
represents a concept $C$ by a direction $\vvec_C^l$ in the activation space of layer $l$ of a network, learned from
example inputs showing the concept. It scores class $k$ by the fraction of that class's inputs whose logit increases
when the activations move in the direction $\vvec_C^l$ <Cite id="kim2018tcav" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A pixel attribution for a zebra image highlights pixels. It does not say whether the network relies on stripes, on the
savannah background or on the animal's shape. The user has a concept in mind, "striped", and wants to test it. TCAV lets
the user define the concept by examples, such as a few dozen images of striped fabric, and asks the network how its
zebra logit responds to that concept <Cite id="kim2018tcav" />.

## Concept activation vectors

Split the network at layer $l$ into $f_l : \reals^n \to \reals^m$, mapping an input to the layer's activations, and
$h_{l,k} : \reals^m \to \reals$, mapping the activations to the logit of class $k$. Collect a set $P_C$ of inputs that
show concept $C$ and a set $N$ of random inputs. Train a linear classifier, such as
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, to separate $\set{f_l(\xvec) : \xvec \in P_C}$ from
$\set{f_l(\xvec) : \xvec \in N}$. The **concept activation vector** $\vvec_C^l$ is the unit normal to its decision
boundary, pointing towards the concept examples. The linear classifier's accuracy on held-out examples measures whether
layer $l$ represents the concept at all.

## Conceptual sensitivity

The **conceptual sensitivity** of class $k$ to concept $C$ at input $\xvec$ is the directional derivative of the logit
along the concept direction:

$$
S_{C,k,l}(\xvec) = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{h_{l,k}\big(f_l(\xvec) + \epsilon\,\vvec_C^l\big) - h_{l,k}\big(f_l(\xvec)\big)}{\epsilon}.
$$

Expanding $h_{l,k}$ to first order around $\avec = f_l(\xvec)$ gives
$h_{l,k}(\avec + \epsilon\vvec) = h_{l,k}(\avec) + \epsilon\,\nabla h_{l,k}(\avec)^\top\vvec + o(\epsilon)$, so

$$
S_{C,k,l}(\xvec) = \nabla h_{l,k}\big(f_l(\xvec)\big)^\top\,\vvec_C^l.
$$

It is one backward pass from the logit to layer $l$ followed by a dot product. A positive $S$ means that making the
activations more like the concept raises the class logit at this input.

## The TCAV score

For a set $X_k$ of inputs of class $k$, the TCAV score is the fraction with positive sensitivity
<Cite id="kim2018tcav" />:

$$
\mathrm{TCAV}_{C,k,l} = \frac{\abs{\set{\xvec \in X_k : S_{C,k,l}(\xvec) > 0}}}{\abs{X_k}} \in [0, 1].
$$

The score uses only the sign of $S$, so a few inputs with very large sensitivities cannot dominate it. A score near $1$
says the concept pushes the class up for almost every input of the class, and a score near $0$ that it pushes the class
down.

## Testing against random concepts

A CAV learned from a random set of images against another random set still points in some direction, and its TCAV score
is not in general $0.5$. Kim et al. therefore train CAVs for concept $C$ against many different random sets $N$, and also
CAVs for random "concepts" (random sets against random sets). A two-sided $t$-test compares the two samples of TCAV
scores. A concept is reported only if its scores differ significantly from the random ones, with a Bonferroni
correction for the number of concepts tested <Cite id="kim2018tcav" />. See
<NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

A layer has two activations. The CAV for "striped" is $\vvec_C = (0.6, 0.8)$, a unit vector. For five zebra images the
gradients of the zebra logit with respect to the activations are

| image | $\nabla h$ | $S = \nabla h^\top\vvec_C$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $(1.0, 0.5)$ | $0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00$ |
| 2 | $(-0.5, 0.2)$ | $-0.30 + 0.16 = -0.14$ |
| 3 | $(0.2, 0.3)$ | $0.12 + 0.24 = 0.36$ |
| 4 | $(-1.0, 0.4)$ | $-0.60 + 0.32 = -0.28$ |
| 5 | $(0.3, -0.1)$ | $0.18 - 0.08 = 0.10$ |

Three of five sensitivities are positive, so $\mathrm{TCAV} = 0.6$. Whether $0.6$ is evidence of a real dependence
depends on the spread of the scores of random CAVs; with five images it is not.

## Concept bottleneck models

TCAV explains a trained network post hoc. A **concept bottleneck model** builds concepts into the architecture: one part
predicts a vector of annotated concepts $\hat{\cvec}$ from the input, and a second part predicts the label from
$\hat{\cvec}$ alone <Cite id="koh2020" />. Every prediction is then a function of named concepts, and a user can correct a
wrong concept at test time and see the prediction change. The cost is that every training example needs concept
annotations.

## Assumptions

- The concept is linearly represented at layer $l$: a single direction separates concept activations from random ones.
- The example set $P_C$ shows the concept and little else. Striped fabric that is always red defines a concept of
  "red stripes".
- The first-order expansion describes $h_{l,k}$ near $f_l(\xvec)$.

## Pitfalls

- **Layer choice.** A concept can be represented at one layer and not at another. Report the CAV's classification
  accuracy and results for several layers.
- **Sensitivity is not use.** A positive directional derivative says what would happen if activations moved along
  $\vvec_C$. It does not show that the network's actual activations for class $k$ contain the concept.
- **Correlated concepts.** CAVs for correlated concepts, such as "striped" and "zebra-coloured", point in similar
  directions and cannot be separated by TCAV scores.
