<Definition>
An **intrinsically interpretable model** is a model whose form is restricted so that a person can read from it how
each prediction is made, without a second explanation model. Common forms are sparse linear models, generalised
additive models, decision trees, rule lists and scoring systems. The restriction is on the model class; the fitted
parameters or rules are the explanation, so the explanation is faithful by construction <Cite id="rudin2019" />.
</Definition>

## Why constrain the model

A <NoteLink to="interpretability">post-hoc explanation</NoteLink> approximates a black box and can be wrong about it. Rudin argues that for high-stakes decisions
on tabular data with meaningful features, interpretable models are often as accurate as black boxes, so a black box
adds little accuracy while its explanations lose faithfulness <Cite id="rudin2019" />. Interpretable models also expose errors in the data: a
readable model of pneumonia risk learned that asthma lowers the risk, because asthmatic patients were sent straight to
intensive care. The readable term exposed a pattern that a deployed black box would have acted on silently
<Cite id="caruana2015" />.

## Sparse linear models

A linear model $g(\expect[y \mid \xvec]) = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j$ explains each prediction as a sum of per-feature
terms $\beta_j x_j$, and the whole model by its coefficients. It is readable when few coefficients are nonzero. The
<NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> penalty $\lambda\sum_j\abs{\beta_j}$ sets coefficients exactly to zero and so
selects the features <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />. Coefficients are comparable only when features are on comparable
scales, and a coefficient is the effect of its feature with the others held fixed, which can differ from the feature's
marginal association with $y$.

## Additive models

A <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive model</NoteLink> replaces each linear term by a
smooth function, $g(\expect[y \mid \xvec]) = \beta_0 + \sum_j f_j(x_j)$. Each $f_j$ is a curve that can be plotted, and a
prediction is the sum of the curves' values at the input, so the per-feature contributions add up exactly to the
linear predictor. Additivity is what makes the model readable: a model with arbitrary interactions among $d$ features
has no low-dimensional picture. **Explainable boosting machines** fit the $f_j$ by cyclic boosting of shallow trees
and add a few pairwise terms $f_{jk}(x_j, x_k)$, each shown as a heatmap <Cite id="lou2013" /> <Cite id="nori2019" />.
See <NoteLink to="explainable-boosting-machines">explainable boosting machines</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="interpreting-generalised-additive-models">interpreting generalised additive models</NoteLink>.

## Trees and rule lists

A <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink> explains a prediction by the path from the root to a leaf. A
**rule list** is an ordered chain "if $r_1$ then $\hat y_1$, else if $r_2$ then $\hat y_2$, …, else $\hat y_0$", where each
$r_k$ is a conjunction of conditions on the features. The first rule that holds decides the prediction.

**Bayesian rule lists.** Letham et al. place a prior over rule lists that favours short lists with short rules, choose
the rules from pre-mined frequent patterns, and sample the posterior. On stroke prediction for patients with atrial
fibrillation they report a model as interpretable as the clinical CHADS2 score but more accurate
<Cite id="letham2015" />.

**Certifiably optimal rule lists.** CORELS minimises the regularised risk

$$
R(d) = \ell(d) + \lambda K
$$

over rule lists $d$, where $\ell(d)$ is the fraction of training points misclassified, $K$ the number of rules and
$\lambda$ the price of a rule in training error <Cite id="angelino2017" />. It searches the tree of rule lists by branch
and bound. Let $d_p$ be a prefix of $K_p$ rules and $\ell_p(d_p)$ the fraction of training points that are captured by
one of its rules and misclassified. Every list that starts with $d_p$ misclassifies those points too and has at least
$K_p$ rules, so its risk is at least

$$
b(d_p) = \ell_p(d_p) + \lambda K_p.
$$

If $b(d_p)$ is no smaller than the risk of the best list found so far, no extension of $d_p$ can beat that list, and the
whole subtree of extensions is pruned. With this and further bounds, CORELS returns a rule list together with a
certificate that no list has lower risk <Cite id="angelino2017" />.

## Worked example: pruning a rule-list search

Take $\lambda = 0.01$. The best list found so far has 2 rules and training error $0.12$, so its risk is
$0.12 + 0.02 = 0.14$.

- A prefix of 3 rules whose captured points include misclassified ones making up $0.115$ of the data has bound
  $b = 0.115 + 0.03 = 0.145 \ge 0.14$. Every list that starts with it is pruned without being evaluated.
- A prefix of 1 rule with captured error $0.05$ has $b = 0.05 + 0.01 = 0.06 < 0.14$. Its extensions stay in the search.

## Multiple instance pooling for time series

A deep time-series classifier maps a series $\Xmat_i = (\xvec_i^1, \dots, \xvec_i^t)$ of $t$ time points to a class. A
convolutional backbone $\psi_{FE}$ produces one embedding $\zvec_i^j \in \reals^d$ per time point. Standard models average
the embeddings over time (<NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">global average pooling</NoteLink>) and apply a classifier $\psi_{CLF}$:
$\hat\Ymat_i = \psi_{CLF}\big(\frac1t\sum_{j} \zvec_i^j\big)$. The averaging discards which time points mattered.

**MILLET** (multiple instance learning for locally explainable time-series classification) treats each series as a
bag in the sense of <NoteLink to="multiple-instance-learning">multiple instance learning</NoteLink>, with the time points as its instances, and replaces the averaging by a
pooling that produces a prediction for each time point <Cite id="early2024" />. The weakly supervised side of the method is in
<NoteLink to="millet">MILLET</NoteLink>. With an attention head
$a_i^j = \psi_{ATTN}(\zvec_i^j) \in [0, 1]$, the four poolings are:

| pooling | time-point output | series prediction $\hat\Ymat_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attention <Cite id="ilse2018" /> | $a_i^j$ | $\psi_{CLF}\big(\frac1t\sum_j a_i^j\zvec_i^j\big)$ |
| Instance | $\hat\yvec_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(\zvec_i^j)$ | $\frac1t\sum_j \hat\yvec_i^j$ |
| Additive | $\hat\yvec_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(a_i^j\zvec_i^j)$ | $\frac1t\sum_j \hat\yvec_i^j$ |
| Conjunctive | $\hat\yvec_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(\zvec_i^j)$ | $\frac1t\sum_j a_i^j\hat\yvec_i^j$ |

For Instance, Additive and Conjunctive pooling the series prediction is an average of per-time-point class scores
($\hat\yvec_i^j$, or $a_i^j\hat\yvec_i^j$ for Conjunctive). The score of time point $j$ for class $c$ is therefore its exact
additive share of the class-$c$ output, as a term of an additive model is a share of the linear predictor. A positive
score supports class $c$ and a negative one refutes it. The explanation comes from the same forward pass as the
prediction. Attention pooling gives only $a_i^j$, a weight that is the same for every class, so it cannot say which class
a time point supports <Cite id="early2024" />. In Conjunctive pooling, which Early et al. introduce, attention and
classification are applied to the embeddings in parallel, so a time point must be judged important by both heads to
matter.

The additivity is over time points, not over raw inputs: each embedding $\zvec_i^j$ depends on a window of the series
through the convolutions, so a time point's score describes that window. The constraint does not cost accuracy here:
replacing global average pooling by Conjunctive pooling kept or slightly raised accuracy on standard time-series
benchmarks <Cite id="early2024" />. By comparison, class activation mapping <Cite id="zhou2016" /> also reads
time-point scores from one forward pass, but post hoc, from the weights of a model trained with global average
pooling.

**Worked example.** A series of $t = 4$ time points has attention $\avec = (0.1, 0.9, 0.8, 0.1)$ and time-point scores for
class $c$ of $(-0.5, 2.0, 1.5, 0.2)$. Conjunctive pooling gives contributions $a^j\hat y^j_c = (-0.05, 1.8, 1.2, 0.02)$ and
the series score $(-0.05 + 1.8 + 1.2 + 0.02)/4 = 0.7425$. Time points 2 and 3 contribute $3.0$ of the total $2.97$.
Time point 1 weakly refutes class $c$.

## Assumptions

- The features are meaningful to the reader. A sparse linear model over thousands of pixel values is not readable.
- The constrained class is accurate enough for the task. On images, audio and text, the most accurate models are not
  sparse or additive in the raw inputs.

## Pitfalls

- **Interpretable is not causal.** A readable model shows what it learned from the data, including confounded
  associations.
- **Size limits.** A tree with hundreds of leaves or a GAM with hundreds of terms is interpretable in principle and not
  in practice. Sparsity is part of interpretability.
- **Collinearity.** When features are correlated, the split of credit between their coefficients or shape functions is
  unstable across refits.
