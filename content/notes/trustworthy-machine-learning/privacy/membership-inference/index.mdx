<Definition>
A **membership inference attack** receives a trained model $h_S$, learnt from a training set $S$ drawn from a
distribution $\Dcal$, and a record $z = (\xvec, y)$, and outputs a guess of whether $z \in S$
<Cite id="shokri2017" />. Treating "member" as the positive class, its **membership advantage** is

$$
\mathrm{Adv} = \mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR} = \Pr(\text{guess member} \mid z \in S) - \Pr(\text{guess member} \mid z \notin S),
$$

where non-members are fresh draws from $\Dcal$ <Cite id="yeom2018" />. Random guessing has advantage 0.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A model behaves differently on the data it was trained on. It is usually more confident and has lower loss there, most
of all on rare or mislabelled records that it had to memorise. An attacker who can query the model and compare its
behaviour on a record with its behaviour on typical unseen records learns whether the record was used. Membership alone
can be sensitive: being in the training set of a model of hospital discharges reveals a hospital stay
<Cite id="shokri2017" />.

## Attacks

**Shadow models.** Shokri et al. train several shadow models that imitate the target, on data from the same
distribution, so the attacker knows which records each shadow model saw. The prediction vectors of the shadow models on
their own training records and on held-out records, labelled "in" and "out", train an attack classifier, one per output
class <Cite id="shokri2017" />. They attacked models trained by commercial machine-learning services, which exposed only
predictions.

**Loss threshold.** Yeom et al. analysed a simpler attack <Cite id="yeom2018" />. Suppose the loss is bounded,
$0 \le \ell \le B$. The attacker computes the model's loss on $z$ and guesses "non-member" with probability
$\ell(h_S, z)/B$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Adv} = \Pr(\text{non-member} \mid z \notin S) - \Pr(\text{non-member} \mid z \in S)
= \frac{\expect_{z \sim \Dcal}[\ell(h_S, z)] - \expect_{z \sim S}[\ell(h_S, z)]}{B}.
$$

The numerator is the **generalisation gap**, the expected loss on fresh data minus the expected loss on the training
data. With the 0–1 loss, $B = 1$, and the advantage equals the gap between test and training error. Any overfitting is a
membership leak. The converse fails: Yeom et al. also construct models that leak membership without any generalisation
gap, so a small gap does not certify privacy.

**Likelihood ratio attack.** Balanced accuracy can stay near chance for an attack that confidently identifies a few
members and guesses at chance on the rest, yet such an attack exposes those members. Carlini et al. argue that attacks
should be compared by their <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rate</Gloss> at a low <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive
rate</Gloss>, such as 0.1% <Cite id="carlini2022" />. Their likelihood ratio attack (LiRA) treats each record as its own
hypothesis test. It trains shadow models with and without $z$, fits a Gaussian to the logit-scaled confidence
$\ln\frac{p}{1 - p}$ on the true label in each group, and scores $z$ by the likelihood ratio of the target model's
confidence under the two Gaussians. The <NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma">Neyman–Pearson lemma</NoteLink> says a
likelihood-ratio test is the most powerful test of its size, which is why this design works. LiRA is 10 times more
powerful than earlier attacks at low false-positive rates <Cite id="carlini2022" />.

## The differential privacy bound

<NoteLink to="differential-privacy">Differential privacy</NoteLink> limits every such attack. Fix a record $z$ and two
neighbouring training sets: $D$ contains $z$ and $D'$ does not. Any attack is a test that answers "member" when the
trained model lands in some set $\Scal$ of models. Its true-positive rate is $\mathrm{TPR} = \Pr(M(D) \in \Scal)$ and
its false-positive rate is $\mathrm{FPR} = \Pr(M(D') \in \Scal)$. If training is $\varepsilon$-differentially private,
the definition applied to $\Scal$ and to its complement gives

$$
\mathrm{TPR} \le e^\varepsilon\,\mathrm{FPR}, \qquad 1 - \mathrm{FPR} \le e^\varepsilon(1 - \mathrm{TPR}).
$$

The first inequality bounds TPR by a line through the origin, the second by a line through $(1, 1)$. The advantage
$\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR}$ is largest where the two lines meet. Solving
$e^\varepsilon F = 1 - e^{-\varepsilon}(1 - F)$ gives $F = 1/(e^\varepsilon + 1)$,
$\mathrm{TPR} = e^\varepsilon/(e^\varepsilon + 1)$ and

$$
\mathrm{Adv} \le \frac{e^\varepsilon - 1}{e^\varepsilon + 1}.
$$

This region of achievable (FPR, TPR) pairs characterises $\varepsilon$-differential privacy as a hypothesis test
<Cite id="kairouz2015" />. Yeom et al. prove the looser bound $\mathrm{Adv} \le e^\varepsilon - 1$ for their experiment,
in which the non-member is a fresh draw <Cite id="yeom2018" />.

## Worked example

A classifier has training error 0.02 and test error 0.15. The loss-threshold attack with 0–1 loss guesses "member"
exactly when the model classifies $z$ correctly. Its true-positive rate is $1 - 0.02 = 0.98$, its false-positive rate
$1 - 0.15 = 0.85$, and its advantage $0.98 - 0.85 = 0.13$, the generalisation gap.

If the model had been trained with $\varepsilon = 1$, no attack could exceed advantage $(e - 1)/(e + 1) = 0.462$. Yeom
et al.'s bound $e - 1 = 1.72$ would be vacuous. At $\varepsilon = 0.1$ the two bounds are $0.050$ and $0.105$.

## Assumptions

- The attacker has query access to the model's outputs, sometimes only the predicted label, and data from roughly the
  training distribution for shadow models or a threshold.
- Non-members come from the same distribution as members. If they do not, an attack can detect the distribution
  difference instead of membership.

## Pitfalls

- **Balanced accuracy overstates weak attacks and hides strong ones.** Report TPR at low FPR <Cite id="carlini2022" />.
- **Rare and mislabelled records are the most exposed.** A model can leak little on
  average and much about a few people.
- **Regularisation is not a guarantee.** Reducing overfitting lowers the advantage of loss-based attacks but bounds no
  other attack. Only differential privacy, for example through
  <NoteLink to="differentially-private-stochastic-gradient-descent">DP-SGD</NoteLink>, gives a bound.
