<Definition>
**Differentially private stochastic gradient descent** (DP-SGD) trains a model with parameters $\thetavec$ so that the
final parameters are $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differentially private with respect to the training examples
<Cite id="abadi2016dp" />. Each step samples a lot of examples, computes each example's gradient, clips it to $\ell_2$
norm at most $C$, adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation $\sigma C$ to the sum, and takes a step with the noisy
average. A **privacy accountant** converts the noise multiplier $\sigma$, the sampling probability $q$ and the number of
steps $T$ into $(\varepsilon, \delta)$.
</Definition>

## Problem

Neural networks can memorise individual training examples, and attacks such as
<NoteLink to="membership-inference">membership inference</NoteLink> exploit it. The Gaussian mechanism of
<NoteLink to="differential-privacy">differential privacy</NoteLink> needs a query with bounded sensitivity, but the
gradient of a loss has no a priori bound: one outlier can move the parameters arbitrarily far. Training also queries the
data thousands of times, and a naive composition of that many noisy steps gives a useless budget.

## Mechanism

Abadi et al.'s algorithm, for $N$ examples, lot size $L$, learning rate $\eta_t$, clipping norm $C$ and noise multiplier
$\sigma$ <Cite id="abadi2016dp" />:

1. **Sample.** Form the lot $\Lcal_t$ by including each example independently with probability $q = L/N$.
2. **Per-example gradients.** For each $i \in \Lcal_t$ compute $\gvec_i = \nabla_{\thetavec}\ell(\thetavec_t, \xvec_i)$.
3. **Clip.** Replace each by $\bar\gvec_i = \gvec_i/\max(1, \norm{\gvec_i}_2/C)$, which leaves it unchanged if its norm is
   at most $C$ and scales it to norm $C$ otherwise.
4. **Add noise.** $\tilde\gvec_t = \frac{1}{L}\big(\sum_{i \in \Lcal_t}\bar\gvec_i + \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2C^2\I)\big)$.
5. **Step.** $\thetavec_{t+1} = \thetavec_t - \eta_t\tilde\gvec_t$.

<DpSgdStep />

**Why clipping gives privacy.** Adding or removing one example changes the clipped sum by that example's clipped
gradient, whose $\ell_2$ norm is at most $C$. The sum therefore has $\ell_2$ sensitivity $C$, and step 4 is the Gaussian
mechanism with noise $\sigma$ times the sensitivity. The parameters $\thetavec_{t+1}$ are a function of $\thetavec_t$
and the noisy sum only, so by post-processing each step is as private as its noisy sum. Dividing by the fixed $L$
rather than the random lot size keeps the sensitivity at $C/L$ for the average.

**Why sampling helps.** Each example appears in a given lot with probability $q$ only. With probability $1 - q$ the step
reveals nothing about it. By **privacy amplification by subsampling**, a step that is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private
with respect to its lot, with $\varepsilon \le 1$, is $(O(q\varepsilon), q\delta)$-private with respect to the whole data
set <Cite id="abadi2016dp" />.

### The moments accountant

Privacy loss adds over steps, and so do its log moments. For a mechanism $M$ and neighbours $D, D'$, let the privacy
loss at output $o$ be $c(o) = \ln\big(\Pr(M(D) = o)/\Pr(M(D') = o)\big)$ and define the log moment

$$
\alpha_M(\lambda) = \max_{D, D'}\ \ln\expect_{o \sim M(D)}\big[e^{\lambda c(o)}\big].
$$

For steps run in sequence with independent noise, the privacy losses of the steps add and their exponentials multiply, so
$\alpha_M(\lambda) \le \sum_{t=1}^T\alpha_{M_t}(\lambda)$. A tail bound converts moments into $(\varepsilon, \delta)$.
By Markov's inequality, for any $\lambda > 0$,

$$
\Pr(c \ge \varepsilon) = \Pr\big(e^{\lambda c} \ge e^{\lambda\varepsilon}\big) \le e^{\alpha_M(\lambda) - \lambda\varepsilon}.
$$

As in the Gaussian mechanism, a mechanism whose privacy loss exceeds $\varepsilon$ with probability at most $\delta$ is
$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private, so $\delta = \min_\lambda\exp(\alpha_M(\lambda) - \lambda\varepsilon)$
<Cite id="abadi2016dp" />. Abadi et al. bound $\alpha(\lambda)$ for one subsampled Gaussian step by
$q^2\lambda(\lambda + 1)/((1 - q)\sigma^2) + O(q^3/\sigma^3)$ and prove that there are constants $c_1, c_2$ such
that, for $\varepsilon < c_1q^2T$, the algorithm is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private whenever

$$
\sigma \ge c_2\,\frac{q\sqrt{T\ln(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}.
$$

The strong composition theorem needs $\sigma$ larger by a factor $\sqrt{\ln(T/\delta)}$ <Cite id="abadi2016dp" />.

**Rényi differential privacy.** The log moment is a Rényi divergence in disguise: $\alpha_M(\lambda) =
\lambda D_{\lambda + 1}(M(D) \,\|\, M(D'))$, with $D_a(P \,\|\, Q) = \frac{1}{a - 1}\ln\expect_Q[(P/Q)^a]$. Mironov
defined $M$ to be $(a, \varepsilon_a)$-Rényi private if $D_a(M(D) \,\|\, M(D')) \le \varepsilon_a$ for all neighbours
<Cite id="mironov2017" />. Rényi privacy of a fixed order composes by adding $\varepsilon_a$. The Markov argument above
with $\lambda = a - 1$ converts it to $(\varepsilon_a + \ln(1/\delta)/(a - 1), \delta)$-privacy. Modern libraries
account for DP-SGD this way.

<Derivation title="Rényi divergence of the Gaussian mechanism">
Let $P = \Gauss(\mu_1, s^2)$ and $Q = \Gauss(\mu_2, s^2)$ with $\Delta = \mu_1 - \mu_2$. Then

$$
\expect_Q\Big[\Big(\frac{P}{Q}\Big)^a\Big] = \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}s}
\exp\Big(-\frac{a(x - \mu_1)^2 + (1 - a)(x - \mu_2)^2}{2s^2}\Big)\,dx.
$$

The exponent's quadratic part is $-(x - m)^2/(2s^2)$ with $m = a\mu_1 + (1 - a)\mu_2$, and completing the square leaves
the constant $a(a - 1)\Delta^2/(2s^2)$ in the exponent. The remaining Gaussian integral is 1, so

$$
D_a(P \,\|\, Q) = \frac{1}{a - 1}\cdot\frac{a(a - 1)\Delta^2}{2s^2} = \frac{a\Delta^2}{2s^2}.
$$

For a DP-SGD step without subsampling, $\Delta = C$ and $s = \sigma C$, so $\varepsilon_a = a/(2\sigma^2)$.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

**Without subsampling.** Full-batch noisy gradient descent with $\sigma = 4$ has Rényi privacy $\varepsilon_a =
a/(2\sigma^2) = a/32$ per step. After $T = 100$ steps, composition gives $100a/32$, and with $\delta = 10^{-5}$,

$$
\varepsilon = \min_{a > 1}\Big(\frac{100a}{32} + \frac{\ln 10^5}{a - 1}\Big).
$$

Setting the derivative to zero gives $a - 1 = \sqrt{\ln 10^5/(100/32)} = 1.92$ and $\varepsilon = 15.1$.

**With subsampling.** Abadi et al. use $q = 0.01$, $\sigma = 4$ and $\delta = 10^{-5}$. An epoch is $1/q = 100$ steps, so
100 epochs are $T = 10{,}000$ steps, a hundred times as many as above. The moments accountant gives $\varepsilon \approx
1.26$, against $9.34$ from the strong composition theorem. After 400 epochs the values are $2.55$ and $24.22$
<Cite id="abadi2016dp" />.

## Cost

- **Accuracy.** On MNIST, with a 60-dimensional PCA input layer, one hidden layer of 1,000 ReLU units and lots of 600, the
  non-private model reached 98.30%. The private models reached 90%, 95% and 97% test accuracy at
  $(0.5, 10^{-5})$, $(2, 10^{-5})$ and $(8, 10^{-5})$-differential privacy. On CIFAR-10 they reached 67%, 70% and 73% at
  $\varepsilon = 2$, 4 and 8 with $\delta = 10^{-5}$ <Cite id="abadi2016dp" />.
- **Compute and memory.** Per-example gradients break the usual batched backward pass, which sums gradients over the
  batch. Computing them costs extra memory proportional to the lot size times the number of parameters, or extra time.
- **Tuning.** Every training run and hyperparameter search on the private data spends budget.

## Trade-offs

- **Clipping norm $C$.** A small $C$ biases the update by shrinking large gradients. A large $C$ requires proportionally
  more noise, since the noise standard deviation is $\sigma C$.
- **Lot size.** Larger lots average the fixed noise over more examples, since the noise on the average gradient has
  standard deviation $\sigma C/L$, but raise $q$ and so the privacy cost per step.
- **Public data.** Pre-training on public data and fine-tuning privately reaches far better accuracy than private training
  from scratch. Abadi et al. treat CIFAR-100 as public and pre-train the convolutional layers on it before private
  training on CIFAR-10 <Cite id="abadi2016dp" />.
- **What is protected.** The guarantee covers each training example. It says nothing about the privacy of individuals
  whose data appears in many examples, unless contributions are bounded per individual.
