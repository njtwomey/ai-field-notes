<Definition>
Two data sets $D$ and $D'$ are **neighbouring** if one is obtained from the other by adding, removing or changing one
record. A randomised algorithm $M$ is **$(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differentially private** if, for every pair of
neighbouring data sets and every set $S$ of outputs,

$$
\Pr\big(M(D) \in S\big) \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr\big(M(D') \in S\big) + \delta,
$$

with probabilities over the randomness of $M$ <Cite id="dwork2006" /> <Cite id="dwork2014" />. The **privacy budget**
$\varepsilon \ge 0$ bounds how much one record can change the distribution of the output. $\delta \ge 0$ is a small
probability of exceeding that bound. With $\delta = 0$ the guarantee is **pure** $\varepsilon$-differential privacy.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Differential privacy is a property of the algorithm, not of a data set or an output. Whatever an observer sees, the
chance of seeing it would have been almost the same had any one person's record been left out. The observer can
therefore learn almost nothing about that person that they could not have learnt without the person's data. The
guarantee holds against any observer with any background knowledge, because it bounds a ratio of probabilities of the
algorithm's outputs, which no side information changes.

A small $\varepsilon$, such as $0.1$, keeps the ratio $e^\varepsilon$ close to 1. $\varepsilon = \ln 3 \approx 1.1$
allows one record to triple the probability of an output. $\delta$ must be much smaller than $1/n$ for $n$ records.
Otherwise the algorithm "publish one random record in full" satisfies the definition with $\delta = 1/n$.

## Sensitivity and the Laplace mechanism

Most private algorithms add noise to a deterministic query $f$, mapping data sets to $\reals^k$. The noise must hide the
largest change one record can cause. The **$\ell_1$ sensitivity** of $f$ is

$$
\Delta_1 f = \max_{D, D' \text{ neighbours}}\norm{f(D) - f(D')}_1.
$$

A count has $\Delta_1 f = 1$: one person changes it by at most one. The **Laplace mechanism** releases

$$
M(D) = f(D) + (Y_1, \dots, Y_k), \qquad Y_i \iid \Laplace(0, b), \quad b = \frac{\Delta_1 f}{\varepsilon}.
$$

**Proof of privacy.** The density of $M(D)$ at $\zvec \in \reals^k$ is $p_D(\zvec) = \prod_i \frac{1}{2b}e^{-\abs{z_i -
f_i(D)}/b}$. For neighbours $D, D'$, the ratio of densities is

$$
\frac{p_D(\zvec)}{p_{D'}(\zvec)} = \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\Big(\frac{\abs{z_i - f_i(D')} - \abs{z_i - f_i(D)}}{b}\Big)
\le \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\Big(\frac{\abs{f_i(D) - f_i(D')}}{b}\Big) = \exp\Big(\frac{\norm{f(D) - f(D')}_1}{b}\Big) \le e^{\varepsilon}.
$$

The first inequality is the triangle inequality $\abs{z - a'} - \abs{z - a} \le \abs{a - a'}$. The last uses the
definitions of $\Delta_1 f$ and $b$. Integrating $p_D \le e^\varepsilon p_{D'}$ over any set $S$ gives
$\Pr(M(D) \in S) \le e^\varepsilon\Pr(M(D') \in S)$: pure $\varepsilon$-differential privacy <Cite id="dwork2006" />.
The logarithm of the density ratio at the observed output is the **privacy loss**. The Laplace mechanism bounds it by
$\varepsilon$ at every output.

## The Gaussian mechanism

Gaussian noise has lighter tails and adds up more gracefully over many queries, but its density ratio is unbounded, so
it needs $\delta > 0$. Let $\Delta_2 f = \max\norm{f(D) - f(D')}_2$ be the $\ell_2$ sensitivity and release
$M(D) = f(D) + \Gauss(\zeros, s^2\I)$. Write $\vvec = f(D) - f(D')$ and draw the output on $D$ as
$\zvec = f(D) + s\gvec$ with $\gvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \I)$. The privacy loss is

$$
L = \ln\frac{p_D(\zvec)}{p_{D'}(\zvec)} = \frac{\norm{\vvec + s\gvec}^2 - \norm{s\gvec}^2}{2s^2}
= \frac{\norm{\vvec}^2}{2s^2} + \frac{\vvec^\top\gvec}{s} \sim \Gauss\Big(\frac{\norm{\vvec}^2}{2s^2}, \frac{\norm{\vvec}^2}{s^2}\Big).
$$

If $\Pr(L > \varepsilon) \le \delta$, the mechanism is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private. To see this, split any output set
$S$ into the part where $L \le \varepsilon$, where $p_D \le e^\varepsilon p_{D'}$, and the rest, whose probability under
$D$ is at most $\delta$:

$$
\Pr(M(D) \in S) \le e^\varepsilon\Pr(M(D') \in S) + \Pr(L > \varepsilon) \le e^\varepsilon\Pr(M(D') \in S) + \delta.
$$

Bounding the normal tail of $L$ gives Dwork and Roth's sufficient condition: for $\varepsilon \in (0, 1)$,
$s > \Delta_2 f\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon$ gives $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy
<Cite id="dwork2014" />. The condition is conservative, and exact calibration of $s$ from the tail of $L$ gives smaller
noise.

## Post-processing and composition

- **Post-processing.** If $M$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private and $g$ is any function that does not look at the data,
  then $g \circ M$ is $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private. For deterministic $g$ the event $g(M(D)) \in S$ is the event
  $M(D) \in g^{-1}(S)$, and the definition holds for that set. Rounding, thresholding or training a model on private
  outputs cannot weaken the guarantee.
- **Basic composition.** Running $M_1, \dots, M_k$ with budgets $(\varepsilon_j, \delta_j)$ on the same data is
  $(\sum_j\varepsilon_j, \sum_j\delta_j)$-private. For pure privacy with independent mechanisms the density ratios
  multiply, so the privacy losses add.
- **Advanced composition.** The privacy losses are random, and their sum concentrates. Dwork, Rothblum and Vadhan showed
  that $k$ adaptively chosen $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-private mechanisms are $(\varepsilon', k\delta + \delta')$-private
  for any $\delta' > 0$, with <Cite id="dwork2010" />

$$
\varepsilon' = \sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\,\varepsilon + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon - 1).
$$

For small $\varepsilon$ the total grows like $\sqrt k$ instead of $k$. Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath derived the exact
optimal composition <Cite id="kairouz2015" />.

- **Groups.** Changing $m$ records is $m$ steps between neighbours, so pure $\varepsilon$-privacy protects groups of
  size $m$ with budget $m\varepsilon$.

## Worked example

**Randomised response.** A survey asks a sensitive yes/no question. Each respondent flips a fair coin. On heads they
answer truthfully. On tails they flip again and answer "yes" on heads and "no" on tails <Cite id="warner1965" />. A true
"yes" is reported as "yes" with probability $\frac12 + \frac14 = \frac34$ and a true "no" with probability
$\frac14$. The largest ratio of the probabilities of one report under the two possible truths is
$\frac{3/4}{1/4} = 3$, so each response is $\varepsilon = \ln 3 = 1.10$ differentially private, with no trust in the
survey taker.

If the true proportion of "yes" is $p$, the proportion of reported "yes" is $q = \frac34 p + \frac14(1 - p) =
\frac12 p + \frac14$, so $\hat p = 2\hat q - \frac12$. Of $n = 1000$ respondents, 400 report "yes": $\hat q = 0.4$ and
$\hat p = 0.3$. The standard error is $2\sqrt{\hat q(1 - \hat q)/n} = 2\sqrt{0.24/1000} = 0.031$, against
$\sqrt{0.3 \cdot 0.7/1000} = 0.014$ for a direct survey. Privacy costs a factor of about 2 in standard error here.

**Laplace and Gaussian noise for a count.** With $\varepsilon = 0.5$, a count gets Laplace noise of scale
$b = 1/0.5 = 2$, standard deviation $\sqrt2 b = 2.83$. With $\delta = 10^{-5}$, the Gaussian mechanism's sufficient
condition asks for $s > \sqrt{2\ln(1.25 \times 10^5)}/0.5 = 9.69$.

**Composition.** One hundred queries at $\varepsilon = 0.1$ each cost $\varepsilon = 10$ by basic composition. Advanced
composition with $\delta' = 10^{-5}$ gives $\sqrt{200\ln 10^5}\cdot 0.1 + 100 \cdot 0.1\,(e^{0.1} - 1) = 4.80 + 1.05 =
5.85$.

## Assumptions

- The unit of privacy is one record. If one person contributes many records, protect the person by bounding their
  contributions or using group privacy.
- Sensitivity must be bounded for every possible data set, not only the observed one. Unbounded values are clipped
  before the query, as in <NoteLink to="differentially-private-stochastic-gradient-descent">DP-SGD</NoteLink>.
- The noise must be secret and drawn from the stated distribution. An observer who can predict or reconstruct the noise
  can subtract it.

## Pitfalls

- **The budget is spent.** Every query, including exploratory ones and hyperparameter searches, consumes budget.
  Reporting the $\varepsilon$ of the final model alone understates the cost.
- **Large $\varepsilon$ is weak.** Guarantees with $\varepsilon = 10$ allow one record to change output probabilities by a
  factor of $e^{10} \approx 22{,}000$. They may still defeat practical attacks such as
  <NoteLink to="membership-inference">membership inference</NoteLink>, but that is an empirical claim, not a guarantee.
- **Privacy is not secrecy of aggregates.** Differential privacy allows learning facts about the population, including
  facts that are sensitive for individuals who share the population's traits.
