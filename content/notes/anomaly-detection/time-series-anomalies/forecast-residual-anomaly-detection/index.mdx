<Definition>
Forecast-residual <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly detection</NoteLink> fits a forecasting model to the normal
behaviour of a time series. At each time $t$ it computes the one-step forecast $\hat{y}_{t \mid t-1}$ and an estimate
$\hat{\sigma}_t$ of the forecast's <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>, and flags $y_t$
when the standardised residual

$$
z_t = \frac{y_t - \hat{y}_{t \mid t-1}}{\hat{\sigma}_t}
$$

exceeds a threshold $\tau$ in absolute value. Equivalently, it flags values outside a
<NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction interval</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Problem

A threshold on the raw values of a series cannot find contextual anomalies. Web traffic of 5,000 requests per minute is
normal at noon and anomalous at 4 a.m. A detector needs the <NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink> at each
time, which depends on trend, seasonality and recent history. A forecasting model computes exactly that.

## Mechanism

1. **Fit a model of normal behaviour.** Any forecasting model works: seasonal naive, exponential smoothing,
   <Gloss name="arima" />, a <Gloss name="ssm">state-space model</Gloss> or a neural forecaster. The model must capture
   the regular patterns, since everything it misses ends up in the residual.
2. **Forecast one step ahead.** At time $t$, forecast $y_t$ from $y_1, \dots, y_{t-1}$.
3. **Standardise the residual.** Divide by the forecast's standard deviation. For a Gaussian state-space model the
   <NoteLink to="kalman-filter" /> gives it exactly as the innovation variance. Otherwise estimate it from recent
   residuals with a robust scale such as $1.4826$ times the <NoteLink to="z-score-and-robust-outlier-detection">median
   absolute deviation</NoteLink>, so that earlier anomalies do not inflate it.
4. **Threshold.** Flag $\abs{z_t} > \tau$.
5. **Protect the model's state.** Before the model updates on a flagged value, replace the value by its forecast.

Step 5 keeps an anomalous value out of the forecasts that follow it. For an <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive
model</Gloss>, a single anomalous value at $t_0$ enters the forecast of $y_{t_0 + 1}$ and produces a residual of the
opposite sign one step later (see <NoteLink to="time-series-anomaly-types" />). Updating a smoothing model on an anomaly
shifts its level and hides the anomalies that follow.

## Choosing the threshold

The threshold sets the false-alarm rate per time step, and a series has many steps. For Gaussian residuals,
$\prob(\abs{z_t} > \tau) = 2\paren{1 - \Phi(\tau)}$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function.

**Worked example.** A metric sampled once per minute has 1,440 values per day. The common choice $\tau = 3$ has a
per-step false-alarm rate of $0.0027$ and gives $1{,}440 \times 0.0027 = 3.9$ false alarms per day. For one false alarm
per 30 days, the per-step rate must be $\alpha = 1/(30 \times 1{,}440) = 1/43{,}200$. Solving
$2\paren{1 - \Phi(\tau)} = \alpha$ gives $\tau = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2) = 4.23$.

Residuals are rarely exactly Gaussian. Their tails are usually heavier, which makes Gaussian thresholds too low. Two
alternatives avoid the assumption.

- **Extreme value theory.** Fit a generalised Pareto distribution to the largest $\abs{z_t}$ and set $\tau$ for the
  target rate (see <NoteLink to="extreme-value-theory-for-anomalies" />).
- **Nonparametric dynamic thresholding.** Hundman and colleagues forecast spacecraft telemetry with
  <Gloss name="lstm" /> networks and smoothed the absolute residuals with an
  <NoteLink to="simple-exponential-smoothing">exponentially weighted moving average</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="hundman2018" />. Candidate thresholds are $\mu + c\sigma$ over a window, for a range of $c$. The chosen
  threshold is the one whose removal of the values above it reduces the mean and standard deviation of the smoothed
  residuals the most, penalised by the number of values and contiguous sequences it flags. A pruning step then drops
  flagged sequences whose peak residual is close to that of the next-highest one.

## Decomposition instead of a forecast

A seasonal decomposition gives a residual without a forecasting model. The seasonal hybrid extreme studentised deviate
test (S-H-ESD) of Hochenbaum, Vallis and Kejariwal removes the seasonal component found by STL decomposition and the
median of the series, then applies Rosner's generalised extreme studentised deviate test to the remainder with the
median and the median absolute deviation in place of the mean and standard deviation <Cite id="hochenbaum2017" />
<Cite id="rosner1983" />. It is an offline method: the decomposition uses the whole window, including values after each
candidate anomaly. See <NoteLink to="time-series-decomposition" /> and <NoteLink to="grubbs-test" />.

## Cost

The cost is that of running the forecaster once per step plus a constant for the test. Online forecasters such as
exponential smoothing and the Kalman filter update in constant time per step. Neural forecasters cost one forward
pass per step after training.

## Trade-offs

- **Model quality sets detection quality.** A model that misses the weekly pattern turns every weekend into an
  anomaly. A model that fits too closely, or adapts too fast, forecasts the anomaly itself and reports a small
  residual.
- **Level shifts.** After a persistent shift, every residual is large until the model adapts. The detector raises a long
  run of alarms for a single change. Pairing it with a change-point detector such as
  <NoteLink to="cumulative-sum-control-chart" /> separates the two.
- **One step against many.** Some anomalies, such as a slow drift, are small at each step and large only in
  accumulation. One-step residuals miss them; residuals of $h$-step forecasts, or cumulative sums of residuals, find
  them.
- **Multivariate series.** Forecasting each channel separately ignores anomalies in the relations between channels.
  A multivariate model with a joint residual covariance, tested with a Mahalanobis distance, catches them.
