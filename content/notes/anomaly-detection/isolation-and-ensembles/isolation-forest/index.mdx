<Definition>
An isolation forest <Cite id="liu2008" /> is an ensemble of random binary trees, each grown on a small random subsample
of the data. Every internal node splits on a randomly chosen feature at a value drawn uniformly between that feature's
minimum and maximum in the node. The <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly score</NoteLink> of a point is a decreasing function of its average depth over the
trees: points isolated near the root are anomalies.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most detectors model normal data and flag what does not fit. An isolation forest skips the model and measures how easy
a point is to separate from the rest.

Anomalies are few, and their feature values differ from those of normal points. A random cut through the data's range
is therefore more likely to fall between an anomaly and everything else than between two points in a dense cluster.
Repeated random cuts isolate an anomaly after a few splits. A point deep inside a cluster needs many, because every
cut that separates it must fall in the small gap between it and its neighbours.

## Algorithm

**Training.** For each of $t$ trees:

1. Draw a subsample of $\psi$ points without replacement.
2. Grow an isolation tree (iTree) recursively. At a node holding more than one point, pick a feature $q$ uniformly at
   random and a split value $v$ uniformly between the node's minimum and maximum of feature $q$. Send points with
   $x_q < v$ left and the rest right.
3. Stop a branch when its node holds one point, or when the depth reaches $\lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil$, the average
   depth of a balanced tree on $\psi$ points.

**Scoring.** Pass a point $\xvec$ down each tree. Its path length $h(\xvec)$ is the number of edges from the root to
the leaf it reaches, plus $c(m)$ if the leaf still holds $m > 1$ training points, to account for the subtree that
the depth limit did not grow. Average over trees to get $\expect[h(\xvec)]$ and compute

$$
s(\xvec, \psi) = 2^{-\expect[h(\xvec)]/c(\psi)}.
$$

The defaults in the original papers are $t = 100$ trees and $\psi = 256$ <Cite id="liu2012" />.

## The normalising constant

A path length on its own is hard to interpret, because it grows with the subsample size. It is divided by $c(\psi)$,
the average path length of a point in a random tree on $\psi$ points.

Liu, Ting and Zhou take $c$ from the analysis of binary search trees, where an unsuccessful search in a random tree of
$\psi - 1$ keys has the same structure as reaching a leaf of an iTree with $\psi$ leaves <Cite id="liu2008" />. The
constant follows from one assumption: a split sends a uniformly random number of the $n$ points to the left, anywhere
from $1$ to $n - 1$. This holds exactly for binary search trees built from a random order and approximately for iTrees.

Let $D(n)$ be the expected sum of leaf depths in a fully grown tree with $n$ leaves, so the average depth is
$a(n) = D(n)/n$. A tree with one leaf has $D(1) = 0$. At the root, all $n$ leaves move one level deeper, and the
subtrees have $j$ and $n - j$ leaves with $j$ uniform on $\set{1, \dots, n - 1}$:

$$
D(n) = n + \frac{1}{n - 1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \LB D(j) + D(n - j) \RB = n + \frac{2}{n - 1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} D(j).
$$

Multiply by $n - 1$ and subtract the same equation written for $n + 1$:

$$
n\,D(n + 1) - (n - 1)\,D(n) = (n + 1)n - n(n - 1) + 2D(n) = 2n + 2D(n).
$$

So $n\,D(n + 1) = (n + 1)\,D(n) + 2n$. Dividing by $n(n + 1)$ gives a recursion for the average depth:

$$
a(n + 1) = a(n) + \frac{2}{n + 1}.
$$

Starting from $a(2) = 1$, the sum telescopes to $a(n) = 2\sum_{j=2}^{n} 1/j = 2(H_n - 1)$, where
$H_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n} 1/j$ is the $n$-th harmonic number. Since $H_n = H_{n-1} + 1/n$,

$$
c(n) = a(n) = 2H(n - 1) - \frac{2(n - 1)}{n}.
$$

For large $n$, $H(n) \approx \log n + 0.5772$, so $c(n)$ grows like $2\log n$. For $\psi = 256$, $c(256) = 10.25$.

## Reading the score

The exponent is the average path length measured in units of the typical one.

- $\expect[h] \to 0$ gives $s \to 1$: the point is isolated at once, a clear anomaly.
- $\expect[h] = c(\psi)$ gives $s = 0.5$: the point is as hard to isolate as a typical one.
- $\expect[h] \to \psi - 1$, the deepest possible path, gives $s \to 0$.

Liu and colleagues read the scores as follows. Points with $s$ close to 1 are definite anomalies. Points with $s$ well
below $0.5$ are safely normal. If every point scores about $0.5$, the sample has no distinct anomaly.

**Worked example.** With $\psi = 256$ and $c(256) = 10.25$, a point with average path length 3 scores
$2^{-3/10.25} = 0.82$. A point with average path length 6 scores $0.67$. A point with average path length 12, deeper
than typical, scores $0.44$.

## Why subsample

Subsampling is part of the method, not only a shortcut. Liu and colleagues identify two failure modes of isolation on
the full data <Cite id="liu2008" />.

- **Swamping.** Normal points close to anomalies are hard to separate from them, and get high scores too.
- **Masking.** A dense group of anomalies needs many cuts to isolate each member, so the group looks normal.

A small subsample thins out both the clusters and the groups of anomalies, which makes anomalies easier to isolate.
In the figure, lowering $\psi$ from 256 to 32 changes the score map little. Small subsamples also make the
forest cheap: training costs $O(t\,\psi\log\psi)$ and scoring $n$ points costs $O(n\,t\log\psi)$, independent of the
size of the training data beyond the sampling.

## Assumptions

- **Anomalies are few and different.** A large fraction of anomalies, or anomalies that are not separated from normal
  data by the range of any feature, are not isolated early.
- **Axis-aligned structure is enough.** Cuts are parallel to the axes. Normal data concentrated along a diagonal leave
  empty corners that axis-aligned cuts do not reach early, which produces artefacts in the score.

## Pitfalls

- **Axis-parallel artefacts.** The score map shows bands of low scores along the axes of dense clusters, where cuts
  pass through the cluster's range. The extended isolation forest of Hariri, Kind and Brunner uses cuts with random
  slopes to remove them <Cite id="hariri2021" />.
- **Irrelevant features.** A cut on a feature that carries no anomaly signal wastes a level of the tree. With many such
  features, anomalies need longer paths and scores lose contrast.
- **Setting the contamination.** Libraries often threshold the score at a quantile implied by a `contamination`
  parameter. That sets the flagged fraction directly. It does not estimate how many anomalies the data contain.
- **Categorical features.** The uniform split between minimum and maximum assumes ordered numeric features. Encoded
  categories need care.
