<Definition>
An anomaly detector ensemble runs $B$ base detectors on the same data, converts each detector's scores to a common
scale, and combines them into one score per point. The base detectors differ in algorithm, hyperparameters, feature
subset or training subsample. Common combination rules are the mean, the maximum and the mean rank.
</Definition>

## Problem

Unsupervised anomaly detection has no labels for choosing a detector or tuning it. The choice of $k$ in a
<NoteLink to="local-outlier-factor" />, the kernel width of a <NoteLink to="one-class-support-vector-machine" /> or the features used
can change the ranking completely, and no validation set says which setting is right. The ranking of detectors also
changes from one data set to another. An ensemble hedges against a poor choice by combining many reasonable ones.

## Mechanism

### Why averaging helps

Aggarwal and Sathe treat an anomaly score as an estimate of an unknown ideal score and split its error into bias and
variance <Cite id="aggarwal2015" />. Randomised base detectors, such as detectors fitted to random subsamples or random
feature subsets, have variance from the randomisation. Averaging reduces it.

Let the $B$ base scores of a point be random variables $S_1, \dots, S_B$ with common variance $\sigma^2$ and common
pairwise correlation $\rho$. The variance of their mean is

$$
\var\paren{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} S_b} = \frac{1}{B^2}\LB B\sigma^2 + B(B - 1)\rho\sigma^2 \RB
= \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{(1 - \rho)\,\sigma^2}{B}.
$$

The first term does not shrink with $B$. Diverse base detectors, with low $\rho$, gain the most. With $\rho = 0.3$ and
$B = 10$ the variance falls to $0.37\sigma^2$; with $B = 100$ it falls only to $0.307\sigma^2$. The same formula explains
why a <NoteLink to="random-forest" /> decorrelates its trees.

The <NoteLink to="isolation-forest" /> is an ensemble of this kind. One isolation tree is a very noisy detector; the
average over a hundred trees is a stable one.

### Feature bagging

Lazarevic and Kumar introduced feature bagging for outlier detection <Cite id="lazarevic2005" />. In each of $B$
rounds, draw a number $d'$ uniformly between $\lfloor d/2 \rfloor$ and $d - 1$, pick $d'$ of the $d$ features at
random, and run LOF on those features only. Combine the $B$ score vectors by their sum, or by interleaving the rankings
of the rounds.

Random feature subsets help in two ways. They lower the correlation between rounds. They also give rounds in which
irrelevant features are absent, so anomalies visible in a few features are not drowned by noise in the others.

### Normalising scores

Base scores are rarely on one scale. A k-NN distance is in the units of the data. LOF is a ratio near 1. An isolation
forest score lies in $(0, 1)$. Averaging raw scores lets the detector with the largest range dominate. Kriegel and
colleagues proposed two steps <Cite id="kriegel2011" />:

1. **Regularise.** Transform each score so that 0 means normal and larger means more anomalous. For a ratio score such
   as LOF, use $\max(0, \text{LOF} - 1)$ or its logarithm. For a score that decreases with abnormality, negate it.
2. **Normalise.** Map each detector's scores to $[0, 1]$. Gaussian scaling uses the detector's mean $\mu$ and standard
   deviation $\sigma$ over the data: $\max\paren{0,\ \operatorname{erf}\paren{(s - \mu)/(\sigma\sqrt{2})}}$.

Simpler options are z-scores and ranks. Min-max scaling is fragile here: the maximum is usually the most anomalous
point, so one extreme score compresses all the others.

### Combining scores

- **Mean.** Reduces variance. A point that only one detector finds gets its high score divided by $B$.
- **Maximum.** Keeps a point that any detector finds, but adds the false alarms of every detector.
- **Average of maximum (AOM).** Split the detectors into groups, take the maximum within each group and average the
  maxima. It trades between the two <Cite id="aggarwal2015" />.

## Worked example

Two detectors score six points. Detector A sees an anomaly at point 4; detector B, using other features, sees one at
point 5.

| point               | 1     | 2     | 3     | 4        | 5        | 6     |
| ------------------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | -------- | -------- | ----- |
| A (distance)        | 0.5   | 0.6   | 0.4   | **3.0**  | 0.7      | 0.5   |
| B (ratio)           | 1.0   | 1.1   | 0.9   | 1.3      | **2.5**  | 1.0   |
| raw mean            | 0.75  | 0.85  | 0.65  | 2.15     | 1.60     | 0.75  |
| z-score A           | −0.49 | −0.38 | −0.60 | 2.22     | −0.27    | −0.49 |
| z-score B           | −0.54 | −0.36 | −0.73 | 0.00     | 2.18     | −0.54 |
| mean of z-scores    | −0.52 | −0.37 | −0.66 | 1.11     | 0.95     | −0.52 |
| max of z-scores     | −0.49 | −0.36 | −0.60 | 2.22     | 2.18     | −0.49 |

The z-scores use each detector's mean and population standard deviation over the six points. After normalisation,
both detectors contribute equally. The mean ranks points 4 and 5 first but halves their margin over the rest. The
maximum keeps both at the level each detector gave them.

## Cost

An ensemble of $B$ detectors costs $B$ times one detector. Subsampling each base detector, as the isolation forest
does, makes the total cost of $B$ detectors on subsamples of size $\psi$ comparable to one detector on all $n$ points
when $B\psi \approx n$.

## Trade-offs

- **Diversity against accuracy.** Diversity lowers the correlation term, but a base detector that is worse than random
  adds bias. With no labels, there is no direct check on base accuracy. Zimek, Campello and Sander discuss this as the
  central open problem of unsupervised ensembles <Cite id="zimek2014" />.
- **Mean against maximum.** The mean suits detectors that find the same anomalies with independent noise. The maximum
  suits detectors that find different anomalies, such as detectors on different feature subsets.
- **Interpretability.** A combined score cannot say which feature or which detector found the anomaly unless the base
  scores are kept.
