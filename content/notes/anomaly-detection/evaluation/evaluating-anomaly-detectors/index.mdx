<Definition>
Evaluating an anomaly detector means measuring how well its scores rank labelled anomalies above normal observations,
and how many false alarms it raises at the operating point where it will be used. Because anomalies are a small
fraction $\pi$ of the data, metrics that depend on $\pi$, such as precision and average precision, are the informative
ones. Time-series evaluation adds the question of how to credit a detection inside a labelled anomalous segment.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A detector's value is set by the alarms it raises. An operator can inspect a fixed number of alarms per day. If most
are false, the operator stops looking. The question is therefore how many of the raised alarms are real at a false-alarm
rate the operator can tolerate, not how well the detector separates the classes on average.

## Precision at low false-alarm rates

Let a detector at some threshold have true-positive rate $\text{TPR}$ (recall) and false-positive rate $\text{FPR}$,
and let anomalies make up a fraction $\pi$ of the data. Of $n$ observations, $\pi n\,\text{TPR}$ are true alarms and
$(1 - \pi) n\,\text{FPR}$ are false alarms, so

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\pi\,\text{TPR}}{\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1 - \pi)\,\text{FPR}}.
$$

When $\pi$ is small, the false-alarm term dominates unless $\text{FPR}$ is smaller still.

**Worked example.** With $\pi = 0.001$, a detector that finds 90% of anomalies at a false-positive rate of 1% has
precision

$$
\frac{0.001 \times 0.9}{0.001 \times 0.9 + 0.999 \times 0.01} = 0.083.
$$

Eleven of every twelve alarms are false. The same detector could have an area under the ROC curve above 0.99, since the
ROC curve is built from $\text{TPR}$ and $\text{FPR}$ alone and does not see $\pi$. The operating region that matters,
$\text{FPR}$ well below $\pi$, occupies a thin strip at the left edge of the ROC plot.

This is why detectors are compared by:

- **Average precision (AP)**, the area under the precision-recall curve computed as a step sum. A random score has
  expected AP equal to $\pi$, so an AP must be read against the contamination (see
  <NoteLink to="precision-recall-curve-and-average-precision" />).
- **Precision at $n$**, the fraction of true anomalies among the $n$ highest scores. With $n$ equal to the number of
  labelled anomalies, it is also called R-precision. Campos and colleagues recommend versions of these measures
  adjusted for chance, so that values are comparable across data sets with different $\pi$ <Cite id="campos2016" />.
- **Recall at a fixed alarm budget**, such as the recall at one false alarm per day. This is the measure closest to
  deployment.

The note <NoteLink to="evaluating-imbalanced-classifiers" /> treats the dependence of these metrics on prevalence in
general.

## Contamination and label noise

Anomaly labels are less reliable than class labels in most classification data.

- **Unlabelled anomalies.** Real data sets contain anomalies nobody labelled. A detector that finds one is charged
  with a false positive. Measured precision is then a lower bound on true precision, and the best detector can rank
  below one that agrees with the labeller's blind spots.
- **Anomalies by construction.** Many benchmarks are built by taking a classification data set and declaring a
  downsampled class to be the anomalies. Such anomalies may not be unusual in the sense a detector measures, and
  results depend strongly on which class was chosen <Cite id="campos2016" />.
- **Contaminated training data.** A semi-supervised detector evaluated in a benchmark whose training split contains
  anomalies is being tested in the unsupervised setting. Report the contamination of the training split alongside the
  results.
- **Threshold chosen on test labels.** Reporting the best F1 over all thresholds uses the test labels to choose the
  operating point. The number is an upper bound, not an estimate of deployed performance.

## Time series: the point-adjust protocol

Time-series anomaly benchmarks label segments: contiguous runs of anomalous time steps. A detector outputs a score per
step. Point-wise evaluation treats every step as a separate example, which punishes a detector that flags only part
of a segment.

The point-adjust protocol, used in the evaluation of the Donut detector <Cite id="xu2018" /> and widely adopted
afterwards, credits a whole segment once any step in it is flagged. All steps of a segment with at least one alarm
count as true positives; steps outside segments are scored as usual. F1 computed this way is written F1-PA.

### Why point adjustment inflates scores

Kim and colleagues showed that point adjustment rewards random detection <Cite id="kim2022" />. Consider a series of
$N$ steps with $S$ labelled segments of length $L$, and a detector that ignores the data and flags each step
independently with probability $p$.

- **Point-wise.** Precision is $\pi = SL/N$, the anomalous share, and recall is $p$. For small $p$ both are small.
- **Point-adjusted.** A segment is detected when at least one of its $L$ steps is flagged, which happens with
  probability $1 - (1 - p)^L$. Each detection turns $L$ steps into true positives. Expected recall is
  $1 - (1 - p)^L$, which approaches 1 when $pL$ is a few units. False positives remain $p(N - SL)$ in expectation.

**Worked example.** Take $N = 10{,}000$ steps and $S = 5$ segments of $L = 100$ steps, so $\pi = 0.05$. With $p = 0.05$
the point-wise F1 is $0.05$. Under point adjustment each segment is detected with probability $1 - 0.95^{100} = 0.994$.
The expected true positives are $500 \times 0.994 = 497$ and the expected false positives are $0.05 \times 9{,}500 =
475$, giving precision $0.51$ and F1-PA $0.68$. Lowering $p$ to $0.021$ raises F1-PA to $0.77$. A detector that ignores
its input matches many published results.

### Remedies

- **PA%K.** Kim and colleagues proposed crediting a segment only when at least $K\%$ of its steps are flagged, and
  reporting the area under the F1-PA%K curve over $K$ from 0 to 100 <Cite id="kim2022" />. In the example, the random
  detector's F1 falls from $0.68$ at $K = 0$ to $0.46$ at $K = 5$ and to $0.07$ at $K = 10$.
- **Range-based precision and recall.** Tatbul and colleagues defined precision and recall over ranges, with tunable
  rewards for existence, overlap size and position of the detection within a segment <Cite id="tatbul2018" />.
- **A random baseline.** Report the score of a random detector under the same protocol, so that readers can see how
  much of a result the protocol itself explains.

## Flawed benchmarks

Wu and Keogh examined the most used time-series anomaly benchmarks and found four flaws: many anomalies are trivial,
found by a one-line rule such as a threshold on a difference; some data sets label an unrealistically large share of
steps as anomalous; some labels are wrong; and anomalies tend to sit near the end of the series, which favours
detectors that flag late points <Cite id="wu2021" />. Their conclusion is that benchmark gains can be illusory. The note
<NoteLink to="discord-discovery-at-scale" /> describes the archive they proposed in response.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting only ROC AUC.** At low contamination it hides a high false-alarm load, as the worked example on
  precision shows.
- **Averaging across data sets without baselines.** AP and precision depend on $\pi$, which varies between data sets.
  Average chance-adjusted versions, or ranks.
- **Tuning on the test set.** Unsupervised detectors have hyperparameters too. Choosing them by test-set performance,
  even informally, overstates results.
- **Ignoring detection delay.** For streaming detection, an alarm an hour after a segment starts may be worthless. Add
  a delay limit or a reward that decreases with delay.
