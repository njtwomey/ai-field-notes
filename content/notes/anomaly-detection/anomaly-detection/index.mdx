<Definition>
An anomaly (outlier) is an observation that deviates so much from the other observations as to arouse suspicion that it
was generated by a different mechanism <Cite id="hawkins1980" />. Anomaly detection is the task of finding such
observations. A detector maps each observation $\xvec$ to an anomaly score $s(\xvec) \in \reals$, higher for less
typical observations, and flags $\xvec$ when $s(\xvec) > \tau$ for a threshold $\tau$.
</Definition>

## Kinds of anomaly

Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar sort anomalies into three kinds <Cite id="chandola2009" />.

- **Point anomaly.** A single observation is unusual with respect to the whole data set. A transaction of 50,000 euros
  on a card whose largest previous transaction was 300 euros is a point anomaly.
- **Contextual anomaly.** An observation is unusual in its context but would be normal in another. The context is
  given by contextual attributes such as time or location. A temperature of 25 °C is normal in July in Dublin and
  anomalous in January.
- **Collective anomaly.** A group of observations is unusual together, although each member may be normal on its own.
  A flat stretch in an electrocardiogram contains only normal voltage values, but a flat stretch lasting two seconds
  is anomalous.

Contextual and collective anomalies are most common in sequences. The note
<NoteLink to="time-series-anomaly-types" /> treats them for time series.

## Settings by available labels

The labels available at training time decide which methods apply.

- **Supervised detection.** Both normal and anomalous examples are labelled. The problem is binary classification
  under severe <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">class imbalance</NoteLink>. It works only when future anomalies resemble
  the labelled ones.
- **Semi-supervised detection (novelty detection).** Training data contain only normal observations. The detector
  learns a description of normality and scores new observations against it. <NoteLink to="one-class-support-vector-machine" /> and
  <NoteLink to="support-vector-data-description" /> were designed for this setting.
- **Unsupervised detection (outlier detection).** Training data are unlabelled and contain an unknown fraction of
  anomalies, the contamination. The detector must be robust to the anomalies it is trying to find. Robust statistics
  such as the median absolute deviation and the minimum covariance determinant were designed for this setting.

Most detectors can be used in either of the last two settings. The difference matters for fitting: an estimate of
normality fitted to contaminated data is pulled toward the anomalies, which lowers their scores. This effect is called
masking.

## Score, then threshold

Nearly every detector has two stages. The first computes a score. The second converts the score into a decision.

The score orders observations from most to least typical. Scores from different detectors live on different scales:
a z-score, a distance, a density ratio, a path length or a reconstruction error. Only the ordering is comparable
across detectors, which is why ranking metrics such as average precision are used to compare them.

The threshold sets the operating point. Three rules are common.

1. **A probability model of the score.** If the score of a normal observation has a known distribution, $\tau$ is its
   $(1 - \alpha)$ quantile and the false-alarm rate is $\alpha$. A squared Mahalanobis distance of a Gaussian
   observation in $p$ dimensions has a $\ChiSq(p)$ distribution, for example.
2. **An assumed contamination.** If a fraction $\nu$ of the data is expected to be anomalous, $\tau$ is the empirical
   $(1 - \nu)$ quantile of the training scores. The one-class SVM builds $\nu$ into its training objective.
3. **A model of the score's tail.** Extreme value theory fits a generalised Pareto distribution to the largest scores
   and extrapolates to quantiles beyond the observed data.

The false-alarm rate is set per observation. A detector with a false-alarm rate of $10^{-3}$ that scores one million
events per day raises about 1,000 false alarms per day. When anomalies are rarer than false alarms, most alarms are
false.

| <NoteLink to="one-class-support-vector-machine">One-class SVM</NoteLink>                         | signed distance to a kernel boundary                | normal data fill a region                | novelty detection, smooth boundaries        | kernel width choice, large $n$              |

No family dominates. Benchmark studies find that the ranking of detectors changes from data set to data set
<Cite id="campos2016" />. Ensembles that combine several detectors reduce the risk of choosing a poor one
(<NoteLink to="anomaly-detector-ensembles" />).

## Evaluation

Detectors are evaluated with labelled benchmark data. Because anomalies are rare, precision at a low false-alarm rate
and the area under the precision-recall curve carry more information than accuracy or the area under the ROC curve.
Time-series benchmarks add problems of their own, such as point-adjusted scoring and mislabelled segments. The note
<NoteLink to="evaluating-anomaly-detectors" /> covers these.

## Pitfalls

- **Anomaly is not error.** A flagged observation is unusual with respect to a model. It may be a data-entry error, a
  sensor fault, fraud or a genuine rare event. Deleting flagged points without inspection removes the rare events that
  are often the object of study.
- **The definition of normal is the model.** Each detector encodes a notion of normality: a Gaussian bulk, a
  neighbourhood density, a subspace. An anomaly that the notion cannot express is invisible to it.
- **Contamination masks anomalies.** A non-robust fit on contaminated data widens the description of normality until
  the anomalies look normal.
- **Features decide what can be found.** An anomaly in the timing of events cannot be found from a feature set that
  records only their amounts.
