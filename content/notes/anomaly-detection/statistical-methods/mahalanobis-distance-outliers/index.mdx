<Definition>
For a $p$-dimensional observation $\xvec$ and a distribution with mean $\muvec$ and covariance $\Sigmamat$, the squared
Mahalanobis distance is

$$
d^2(\xvec) = (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec).
$$

As an outlier score it flags $\xvec$ when $d^2(\xvec)$ exceeds a quantile of the $\ChiSq(p)$ distribution. In
unsupervised use, $\muvec$ and $\Sigmamat$ are replaced by robust estimates so that the outliers do not distort them.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Euclidean distance treats every direction alike. Correlated data spread out along some directions and are thin along
others. A point that is a short Euclidean distance from the centre, but lies across the thin direction, is more
unusual than a point far out along the long direction.

The Mahalanobis distance measures distance in standard deviations along each principal direction of the data. Its
contours are the ellipses of constant Gaussian density <Cite id="mahalanobis1936" />. For $p = 1$ it reduces to the
absolute z-score, $d = \abs{x - \mu}/\sigma$.

**Example.** Take $\muvec = \zeros$ and unit variances with correlation $0.8$, so
$\Sigmamat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.8 \\ 0.8 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The point $(2, 2)$ lies along the long axis. Its
Euclidean distance from the centre is $2.83$ and its Mahalanobis distance is $2.11$. The point $(1.5, -1.5)$ lies
across the short axis. Its Euclidean distance is only $2.12$, but its Mahalanobis distance is $4.74$.

## The chi-square threshold

If $\xvec \sim \MvGauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$ with known $\muvec$ and $\Sigmamat$, then $d^2(\xvec) \sim \ChiSq(p)$.

The proof whitens the data. $\Sigmamat$ is symmetric positive definite, so it has a Cholesky factorisation
$\Sigmamat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$ with $\Lmat$ invertible. Define $\zvec = \Lmat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)$. It is Gaussian with
mean $\zeros$ and covariance

$$
\Lmat^{-1}\Sigmamat\Lmat^{-\top} = \Lmat^{-1}\Lmat\Lmat^\top\Lmat^{-\top} = \eye,
$$

so its $p$ components are independent standard normal variables. Since
$\Sigmamat^{-1} = \Lmat^{-\top}\Lmat^{-1}$,

$$
d^2(\xvec) = (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Lmat^{-\top}\Lmat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec) = \zvec^\top\zvec = \sum_{j=1}^{p} z_j^2,
$$

a sum of $p$ squared independent standard normals, which is the definition of $\ChiSq(p)$.

The threshold for a false-alarm rate $\alpha$ is therefore the quantile $\chi^2_{p,\,1-\alpha}$. For $p = 2$ the
$\ChiSq(2)$ distribution is exponential with mean 2, so $\prob(d^2 > c) = e^{-c/2}$ and the threshold is
$c = -2\log\alpha$. At $\alpha = 0.025$ this gives $c = 7.38$, or $d = 2.72$. The threshold grows with dimension: the
same $\alpha$ gives $c = 12.83$ for $p = 5$.

With $\muvec$ and $\Sigmamat$ estimated from the same $n$ observations, the $\ChiSq(p)$ distribution holds only
approximately. The approximation is poor when $p$ is not small compared with $n$.

## Masking

The sample mean and sample covariance are as sensitive to outliers as the univariate mean and standard deviation. A
cluster of outliers pulls the mean toward itself and stretches the covariance ellipse toward it. The outliers then
have moderate distances and are not flagged. This is masking.

**Worked example.** Draw 170 points from the Gaussian above (correlation $0.8$) and add 30 outliers in a tight cluster
at $(2, -2)$, across the short axis. The outliers' true Mahalanobis distance is $d = 6.3$, far beyond the threshold
$2.72$. With the classical estimates from all 200 points, the sample correlation falls from about $0.8$ to $0.08$ and
none of the 30 outliers exceeds the $\chi^2_{2,\,0.975}$ threshold. With the minimum covariance determinant estimate
below, all 30 exceed it, along with 7 of the 170 normal points. At a false-alarm rate of 2.5%, about 4 of 170 are
expected.

## Minimum covariance determinant

The minimum covariance determinant (MCD) estimator of Rousseeuw <Cite id="rousseeuw1984" /> fits the mean and
covariance to the most concentrated part of the data. For a subset size $h$ with $n/2 \le h < n$, it finds the subset
$H$ of $h$ observations whose sample covariance matrix has the smallest determinant. The MCD location is the mean of
$H$ and the MCD scatter is its covariance, multiplied by a factor that makes it consistent for Gaussian data.

The determinant measures the volume of the covariance ellipsoid, so the MCD subset is the tightest ellipsoidal core of
the data. Up to $n - h$ observations can be moved anywhere without moving the estimate arbitrarily far. With
$h \approx (n + p + 1)/2$ the breakdown point is close to 50%.

### FAST-MCD

The exact MCD requires searching over all $\binom{n}{h}$ subsets. The FAST-MCD algorithm of Rousseeuw and Van Driessen
<Cite id="rousseeuw1999" /> replaces the search with a concentration step, the C-step:

1. Start from a subset $H_1$ of size $h$, with mean $\mvec_1$ and covariance $\Smat_1$ (divisor $h$).
2. Compute $d_1^2(\xvec_i) = (\xvec_i - \mvec_1)^\top \Smat_1^{-1} (\xvec_i - \mvec_1)$ for all $n$ points.
3. Let $H_2$ be the $h$ points with the smallest $d_1^2$. Compute its mean $\mvec_2$ and covariance $\Smat_2$.

Each C-step satisfies $\det\Smat_2 \le \det\Smat_1$, so iterating converges in a few steps. FAST-MCD runs C-steps from
many random starting subsets of size $p + 1$ and keeps the result with the smallest determinant. A final reweighting
step refits the mean and covariance to all points with $d^2 \le \chi^2_{p,\,0.975}$, which raises statistical
efficiency.

<Derivation title="Why a C-step cannot increase the determinant">
Write $\Amat = \Smat_1^{-1}$. For points in $H_1$, $\sum_{i \in H_1} d_1^2(\xvec_i) = \sum_{i \in H_1}
\trace\paren{\Amat (\xvec_i - \mvec_1)(\xvec_i - \mvec_1)^\top} = h \trace(\Amat\Smat_1) = h p$. $H_2$ holds the $h$
smallest distances, so $\sum_{i \in H_2} d_1^2(\xvec_i) \le h p$.

The mean of a set minimises the sum of any positive definite quadratic form about a centre. Replacing $\mvec_1$ by
$\mvec_2$ therefore does not increase the sum over $H_2$:

$$
h \trace(\Amat\Smat_2) = \sum_{i \in H_2} (\xvec_i - \mvec_2)^\top \Amat (\xvec_i - \mvec_2)
\le \sum_{i \in H_2} d_1^2(\xvec_i) \le h p.
$$

So $\trace(\Smat_1^{-1}\Smat_2) \le p$. The eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p$ of $\Smat_1^{-1}\Smat_2$ are
positive. By the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means,

$$
\paren{\frac{\det\Smat_2}{\det\Smat_1}}^{1/p} = \paren{\prod_j \lambda_j}^{1/p} \le \frac{1}{p}\sum_j \lambda_j \le 1.
$$

Hence $\det\Smat_2 \le \det\Smat_1$.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

- **Elliptical, unimodal normal data.** The distance measures departure from one ellipsoidal bulk. Data with several
  clusters or curved structure need a local method such as the <NoteLink to="local-outlier-factor" />.
- **Gaussian tails, for the threshold.** The $\ChiSq(p)$ quantile sets the false-alarm rate only for Gaussian data.
  Heavier tails produce more large distances.
- **$n$ well above $p$.** The covariance must be invertible, and the MCD needs a subset size $h > p$
  <Cite id="hubert2010" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Classical estimates on contaminated data.** A non-robust fit hides clustered outliers, as the worked example
  shows. Use the MCD or another robust estimator whenever the data may contain outliers.
- **Swamping.** A robust fit can also flag too many normal points, because the consistency correction is exact only
  for Gaussian data. The worked example flagged 7 normal points where 4 were expected.
- **High dimension.** In high dimension the chi-square quantiles grow and distances concentrate. A few directions
  carry the signal; <NoteLink to="principal-component-reconstruction-error" /> splits the distance into a part inside a principal
  subspace and a residual part.
