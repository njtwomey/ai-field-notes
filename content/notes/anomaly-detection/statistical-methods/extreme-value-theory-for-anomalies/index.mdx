<Definition>
Extreme value theory (EVT) describes the distribution of the largest values of a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> without assuming the
form of the whole distribution. Two limit theorems give its models. Maxima of large blocks follow a generalised extreme
value (GEV) distribution. Excesses over a high threshold follow a generalised Pareto distribution (GPD). In <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly
detection</NoteLink>, a GPD fitted to the largest anomaly scores gives the score threshold for a chosen false-alarm probability
$q$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A threshold for a false-alarm rate of $10^{-4}$ is the $1 - 10^{-4}$ quantile of the score of normal data. Two direct
estimates fail. A Gaussian model of the score gets the tail wrong when the score is skewed or heavy-tailed, and the
error grows the further into the tail it extrapolates. The empirical quantile needs many more than $10^4$ observations
to be stable, and it cannot go beyond the largest value observed.

EVT uses the observations that carry information about the tail, the largest ones, and fits a model whose form is
justified by a limit theorem. Only the tail's shape and scale are estimated, not the whole distribution.

## Block maxima and the GEV distribution

Let $M_n = \max(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ for independent draws from a distribution function $F$. If constants $a_n > 0$ and
$b_n$ exist such that $(M_n - b_n)/a_n$ converges in distribution to a non-degenerate limit $G$, then $G$ is a GEV
distribution <Cite id="fisher1928" /> <Cite id="gnedenko1943" />:

$$
G(z) = \exp\LB -\paren{1 + \xi\,\frac{z - \mu}{\sigma}}^{-1/\xi} \RB, \qquad 1 + \xi\,\frac{z - \mu}{\sigma} > 0,
$$

with location $\mu$, scale $\sigma > 0$ and shape $\xi$. The shape sets the tail type.

- $\xi = 0$, read as the limit $G(z) = \exp\paren{-e^{-(z - \mu)/\sigma}}$, is the Gumbel type. It is the limit for
  distributions with exponentially decaying tails, such as the Gaussian and the exponential.
- $\xi > 0$ is the Fréchet type, for tails that decay as a power, such as Student's $t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom,
  for which $\xi = 1/\nu$.
- $\xi < 0$ is the reversed Weibull type, for distributions with a finite upper end point, such as the uniform.

Fitting a GEV to the maxima of fixed blocks, such as daily maxima, discards every observation that is not a block
maximum. The peaks-over-threshold approach uses the data more efficiently.

## Peaks over threshold and the GPD

Fix a high threshold $t$ and consider the excess $Y = X - t$ given $X > t$. The Pickands–Balkema–de Haan theorem
<Cite id="pickands1975" /> <Cite id="balkema1974" /> states that, for every $F$ whose maxima have a GEV limit, the
excess distribution approaches a generalised Pareto distribution as $t$ approaches the upper end of $F$:

$$
\prob(X - t > y \mid X > t) \approx \paren{1 + \frac{\xi y}{\sigma_t}}^{-1/\xi}, \qquad y > 0,
$$

with the same shape $\xi$ as the GEV and a scale $\sigma_t$ that depends on $t$. For $\xi = 0$ the right side is
$e^{-y/\sigma_t}$: excesses over a high threshold are exponential.

<Derivation title="Why excesses are generalised Pareto">
This is the heuristic argument in Coles <Cite id="coles2001" />. Suppose $F^n(z) \approx G(z)$ for large $n$, with $G$ a
GEV distribution. Taking logarithms, $n \log F(z) \approx -\paren{1 + \xi (z - \mu)/\sigma}^{-1/\xi}$. For large $z$,
$F(z)$ is close to 1, so $\log F(z) \approx -(1 - F(z))$ and

$$
1 - F(z) \approx \frac{1}{n}\paren{1 + \xi\,\frac{z - \mu}{\sigma}}^{-1/\xi}.
$$

Apply this at $z = t + y$ and at $z = t$, and divide:

$$
\prob(X > t + y \mid X > t) = \frac{1 - F(t + y)}{1 - F(t)}
\approx \LB \frac{1 + \xi (t + y - \mu)/\sigma}{1 + \xi (t - \mu)/\sigma} \RB^{-1/\xi}
= \paren{1 + \frac{\xi y}{\sigma_t}}^{-1/\xi},
$$

with $\sigma_t = \sigma + \xi (t - \mu)$.
</Derivation>

## Setting a threshold from the tail model

Let $n$ be the number of observations and $N_t$ the number that exceed $t$. The probability of exceeding $t$ is
estimated by $N_t/n$. For $z > t$, split the event $X > z$ into exceeding $t$, then exceeding $z$ given $t$:

$$
\prob(X > z) = \prob(X > t)\,\prob(X > z \mid X > t) \approx \frac{N_t}{n}\paren{1 + \frac{\xi (z - t)}{\sigma}}^{-1/\xi},
$$

writing $\sigma$ for $\sigma_t$. Setting this equal to the target false-alarm probability $q$ and solving for $z$:

$$
\paren{1 + \frac{\xi (z_q - t)}{\sigma}}^{-1/\xi} = \frac{q n}{N_t}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
z_q = t + \frac{\sigma}{\xi}\LB \paren{\frac{q n}{N_t}}^{-\xi} - 1 \RB.
$$

As $\xi \to 0$ the bracket divided by $\xi$ tends to $-\log(qn/N_t)$, so $z_q = t - \sigma \log(qn/N_t)$, the quantile
of an exponential tail.

In practice $\xi$ and $\sigma$ are fitted by maximum likelihood to the $N_t$ excesses. $t$ is a moderately high
empirical quantile, such as the 98th percentile, which leaves enough excesses for the fit.

## Streaming thresholds: SPOT and DSPOT

Siffer and colleagues turned this procedure into a streaming detector, SPOT <Cite id="siffer2017" />. A calibration
batch sets $t$ as an empirical quantile and fits the GPD to the excesses, giving $z_q$. Each new value $x$ is then
handled in one of three ways.

- $x > z_q$: flag $x$ as an anomaly and leave the model unchanged, so that anomalies do not enter the tail estimate.
- $t < x \le z_q$: add $x - t$ to the excesses, refit $\xi$ and $\sigma$, and update $z_q$.
- $x \le t$: update only $n$.

DSPOT handles drift by applying SPOT to the difference between each value and a moving average of recent normal
values. Both need only the false-alarm probability $q$ as input.

## Worked example

Draw $n = 10{,}000$ values from Student's $t$ with 3 degrees of freedom, a heavy-tailed score distribution with true
shape $\xi = 1/3$. The target is $q = 10^{-4}$, whose true quantile is $22.2$.

In one sample, the 98th percentile is $t = 3.385$ with $N_t = 200$ excesses. The maximum-likelihood fit gives
$\hat\xi = 0.473$ and $\hat\sigma = 1.194$. Then $qn/N_t = 0.005$ and

$$
z_q = 3.385 + \frac{1.194}{0.473}\paren{0.005^{-0.473} - 1} = 3.385 + 2.528 \times 11.22 = 31.8.
$$

The estimate is too high in this sample because $\hat\xi$ overshoots $1/3$. Across 200 independent samples, the median
peaks-over-threshold estimate is $21.4$, and 80% of estimates fall between $16.7$ and $29.0$. A Gaussian model fitted to the same data
puts the threshold at $6.3$, and would flag the normal tail of the distribution as anomalous. The empirical $1 - q$
quantile rests on the single largest value or two in the sample.

## Choosing the initial threshold

The choice of $t$ trades bias against variance. A low $t$ gives many excesses but a poor GPD approximation. A high $t$
gives a good approximation from few excesses.

The mean excess gives a diagnostic. For a GPD with $\xi < 1$, $\expect(X - u \mid X > u) = \sigma_u/(1 - \xi)$, and
the scale changes with the threshold as $\sigma_u = \sigma_t + \xi (u - t)$ for $u > t$. The mean excess is therefore
linear in $u$ with slope $\xi/(1 - \xi)$ above any threshold where the GPD holds. Plotting the empirical mean excess
against $u$ and choosing $t$ where the plot becomes linear is the standard check <Cite id="coles2001" />.

## Assumptions

- **Independent, identically distributed scores.** Clustered exceedances, common in time series, violate <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>
  and make the fit overconfident. Declustering keeps one peak per cluster.
- **A GEV limit exists.** This holds for almost every continuous distribution met in practice, but not for all
  distributions.
- **A stationary tail.** The tail model is fitted to past scores. Drift in the score distribution needs a detrended
  score, as in DSPOT, or a moving window.

## Pitfalls

- **Large variance in $\hat\xi$.** The shape parameter is estimated from a few hundred excesses at most, and the
  threshold is sensitive to it, as the worked example shows. Report an interval, or constrain $\xi$ when the type of
  tail is known.
- **Anomalies in the calibration data.** Anomalies among the excesses inflate $\hat\xi$ and raise the threshold. Clean
  the calibration batch or use a robust fit.
- **Very rare targets.** Extrapolating to $q$ far below $1/n$ multiplies the model error. The theorem justifies the form
  of the tail, not an arbitrarily distant extrapolation.
