<Definition>
Support vector data description, introduced by Tax and Duin <Cite id="tax2004" />, finds a hypersphere with centre
$\avec$ and radius $R$ in the feature space of a kernel $k$ that contains most of the training data and has the
smallest possible volume. Points outside the sphere are allowed at a cost $C$ per unit of squared excess distance. A
new point is anomalous when its feature-space image lies outside the sphere.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A sphere is the simplest closed boundary around a set of points. In input space a sphere is too rigid to describe most
data. In the feature space of a Gaussian kernel, a sphere pulled back to input space becomes a flexible boundary that
follows the shape of the data. The boundary is defined by a few support vectors on its surface, as in a
<NoteLink to="support-vector-machine" />.

## Primal problem

For training points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ with feature map $\phi$, SVDD solves

$$
\min_{R,\, \avec,\, \xivec}\ R^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{n} \xi_i
\quad\text{subject to}\quad \norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \avec}^2 \le R^2 + \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0.
$$

$C$ trades the volume of the sphere against the number of points left outside.

## Dual problem

With multipliers $\alpha_i \ge 0$ for the sphere constraints and $\gamma_i \ge 0$ for $\xi_i \ge 0$, the Lagrangian is

$$
L = R^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i - \sum_i \alpha_i\paren{R^2 + \xi_i - \norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \avec}^2} - \sum_i \gamma_i \xi_i.
$$

Setting the derivatives with respect to $R$, $\avec$ and $\xi_i$ to zero gives

$$
\sum_i \alpha_i = 1, \qquad \avec = \sum_i \alpha_i \phi(\xvec_i), \qquad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C.
$$

The centre is a weighted mean of the mapped points. Substituting these conditions eliminates $R$, $\avec$ and $\xi$.
Expanding $\norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \avec}^2$ with $\avec = \sum_j \alpha_j \phi(\xvec_j)$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i = 1$
leaves

$$
\max_{\alphavec}\ \sum_i \alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \xvec_i) - \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C,\ \ \sum_i \alpha_i = 1.
$$

The constraints are feasible only if $nC \ge 1$. A point with $\alpha_i = C$ lies outside or on the sphere, so at most
$1/C$ points lie outside. Writing $C = 1/(\nu n)$ turns this into the same bound as the one-class SVM's: at most a
fraction $\nu$ of the training points are outside.

The squared distance of a new point $\zvec$ from the centre needs only kernel evaluations:

$$
\norm{\phi(\zvec) - \avec}^2 = k(\zvec, \zvec) - 2\sum_i \alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \zvec) + \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j).
$$

$\zvec$ is flagged when this exceeds $R^2$, where $R^2$ is the squared distance of any support vector with
$0 < \alpha_i < C$.

## Worked example

With the linear kernel $k(x, x') = x x'$ on the real line, the dual objective
$\sum_i \alpha_i x_i^2 - \paren{\sum_i \alpha_i x_i}^2$ is the variance of the points under the weights $\alpha_i$.
SVDD with a linear kernel finds the weighting of largest variance, and that variance is $R^2$.

Take the points $-1, 0, 2$ and a large $C$ (no points outside). Putting weight $\tfrac{1}{2}$ on each of $-1$ and $2$
gives the weighted mean $0.5$ and the weighted variance $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 + 4) - 0.5^2 = 2.25$. No weighting does better,
since the variance of values in $[-1, 2]$ is at most $(2 - (-1))^2/4 = 2.25$. So $\avec = 0.5$ and $R = 1.5$: the
smallest interval containing the points, $[-1, 2]$. The point $0$ has $\alpha = 0$ and does not affect the solution.

## Equivalence with the one-class SVM

When $k(\xvec, \xvec) = \kappa$ is the same constant for every $\xvec$, SVDD and the
<NoteLink to="one-class-support-vector-machine" /> give the same solution. Translation-invariant kernels such as the Gaussian kernel,
with $\kappa = 1$, have this property.

**Same dual.** With $\sum_i \alpha_i = 1$, the first term of the SVDD dual is $\sum_i \alpha_i \kappa = \kappa$, a
constant. Maximising the dual is then the same as minimising $\sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$ under
$0 \le \alpha_i \le C$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i = 1$. That is the one-class SVM dual with $C = 1/(\nu n)$, up to a factor of
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ that does not change the minimiser.

**Same decision function.** With equal $\alpha$, the sphere test reads

$$
\kappa - 2\sum_i \alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \zvec) + \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) \le R^2
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sum_i \alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \zvec) \ge \frac{1}{2}\paren{\kappa + \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) - R^2}.
$$

The right side is a constant, which plays the role of the one-class SVM's offset $\rho$. Both methods flag the same
points <Cite id="scholkopf2001b" /> <Cite id="tax2004" />. For kernels whose $k(\xvec, \xvec)$ varies, such as
polynomial kernels, the two differ.

## Kernel width

With the Gaussian kernel $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \exp\paren{-\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2/s^2}$, the width $s$ sets the
flexibility of the boundary <Cite id="tax2004" />. For $s$ much smaller than the distances between points, all
kernel values between distinct points are near zero, every point becomes a support vector with equal weight, and the
description behaves like a kernel density estimate thresholded at a level. For $s$ much larger than the data's extent,
the kernel is close to a quadratic function of distance and the description approaches a rigid sphere in input space.
Tax and Duin choose $s$ so that the fraction of support vectors, which estimates the leave-one-out error on normal
data, matches a target.

## Deep SVDD

Ruff and colleagues replaced the kernel with a neural network $\phi(\xvec; \Wmat)$ trained to map normal data close to
a fixed centre $\cvec$, minimising $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \norm{\phi(\xvec_i; \Wmat) - \cvec}^2$ plus weight decay
<Cite id="ruff2018" />. The distance to $\cvec$ is the anomaly score. The network must not have bias terms and $\cvec$
must not be learned: otherwise the trivial map $\phi \equiv \cvec$ reaches zero loss for all inputs, a failure called
hypersphere collapse.

## Assumptions

- **Normal data form one connected region in feature space.** With a Gaussian kernel of moderate width this still
  allows several clusters in input space.
- **Mostly clean training data.** $C$, or $\nu$, must allow at least as many points outside as there are anomalies in
  the training set.

## Pitfalls

- **Width and $C$ interact.** Both control how tightly the boundary follows the data, and neither can be tuned with
  labels in the unsupervised setting.
- **Cost.** Like the one-class SVM, training solves a quadratic programme with $n$ variables and needs the $n \times n$
  kernel matrix.
- **Feature scaling.** A single isotropic kernel width assumes features on comparable scales.
