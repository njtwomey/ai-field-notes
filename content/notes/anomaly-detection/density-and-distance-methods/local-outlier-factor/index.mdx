<Definition>
The local outlier factor of Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander <Cite id="breunig2000" /> scores a point $\xvec$ by the ratio
of its neighbours' local density to its own. The local density is the inverse of the mean reachability distance from
$\xvec$ to its $k$ nearest neighbours. A LOF near 1 means $\xvec$ is as dense as its neighbourhood; a LOF well above 1
means $\xvec$ is sparser than its neighbourhood and is a local outlier.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A global score such as the <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbour-anomaly-score">k-NN distance</NoteLink> compares every
point with one scale. Data often contain clusters of different density. A point one unit from a tight cluster whose
points are a tenth of a unit apart is anomalous. A point one unit from its neighbours in a diffuse cluster whose points
are all a unit apart is normal. The <Gloss name="knn" /> distance cannot tell these apart.

LOF compares a point with its own neighbourhood. It asks how much sparser the point is than the points next to it.
The answer does not depend on how dense the neighbourhood is in absolute terms.

## Definitions

Fix $k$ and a distance $\delta$. Let $\Dcal$ be the data.

1. The **k-distance** of a point $\ovec$, written $\kappa(\ovec)$, is its distance to its $k$-th nearest neighbour.
2. The **k-neighbourhood** $N_k(\xvec)$ is the set of points within distance $\kappa(\xvec)$ of $\xvec$, excluding
   $\xvec$. It has $k$ members, or more when distances tie.
3. The **reachability distance** of $\xvec$ from a neighbour $\ovec$ is
   $$
   \text{rd}(\xvec, \ovec) = \max\paren{\kappa(\ovec),\ \delta(\xvec, \ovec)}.
   $$
4. The **local reachability density** is the inverse mean reachability distance to the neighbours,
   $$
   \text{lrd}(\xvec) = \LB \frac{1}{\abs{N_k(\xvec)}} \sum_{\ovec \in N_k(\xvec)} \text{rd}(\xvec, \ovec) \RB^{-1}.
   $$
5. The **local outlier factor** is the mean ratio of the neighbours' density to the point's own:
   $$
   \text{LOF}(\xvec) = \frac{1}{\abs{N_k(\xvec)}} \sum_{\ovec \in N_k(\xvec)} \frac{\text{lrd}(\ovec)}{\text{lrd}(\xvec)}.
   $$

### Why the reachability distance

The reachability distance replaces a small distance by the neighbour's k-distance. All points closer to $\ovec$ than
$\kappa(\ovec)$ are treated as if they were exactly $\kappa(\ovec)$ away. Inside a cluster, the distance between two
close points fluctuates a lot from pair to pair. Clamping it at $\kappa(\ovec)$ removes that fluctuation, so points
deep in one cluster get nearly equal densities. For a point far from $\ovec$, the reachability distance is the ordinary
distance, so outliers are unaffected. The asymmetry, using $\kappa(\ovec)$ and not $\kappa(\xvec)$, is deliberate: the
neighbour's scale sets the floor.

### Why LOF is close to 1 inside any cluster

LOF is a ratio of densities, so it does not change when all distances are multiplied by a constant $c > 0$. Every
k-distance and every reachability distance is multiplied by $c$, every lrd is divided by $c$, and each ratio
$\text{lrd}(\ovec)/\text{lrd}(\xvec)$ is unchanged. A diffuse cluster that is a scaled copy of a tight cluster has the
same LOF values.

Deep inside a cluster where the density is roughly constant, a point and its neighbours have neighbourhoods of the same
size, so their lrd values are about equal and the ratio is about 1. Breunig and colleagues proved bounds of this kind:
for a point deep in a cluster, LOF lies between $1/(1 + \varepsilon)$ and $1 + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$
measures the variation of reachability distances within the cluster <Cite id="breunig2000" />. A point sparser than its
neighbours has a smaller lrd than they do, so the ratio exceeds 1.

## Worked example

Take nine points on a line, with absolute difference as the distance and $k = 2$:

- a tight cluster $A = \set{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}$,
- a diffuse cluster $B = \set{5, 6, 7, 8}$,
- a point $x = 1.0$ just outside $A$.

$B$ is $A$ multiplied by 10 and shifted by 5, so the two clusters differ only in scale.

**k-distances.** In $A$, the end points $0$ and $0.3$ have $\kappa = 0.2$ and the interior points $\kappa = 0.1$. In
$B$, the values are ten times larger: $2$ and $1$. The point $x$ has neighbours $0.3$ and $0.2$, so $\kappa(x) = 0.8$.
The k-NN score ranks $5$ and $8$ (score $2$) above $x$ (score $0.8$).

**Densities.** For the point $0$, the neighbours are $0.1$ and $0.2$, each with $\kappa = 0.1$. The reachability
distances are $\max(0.1, 0.1) = 0.1$ and $\max(0.1, 0.2) = 0.2$, with mean $0.15$, so $\text{lrd}(0) = 6.67$. Every
point in $A$ gets $6.67$. By the scaling argument, every point in $B$ gets $0.667$. For $x$, the reachability
distances are $\max(\kappa(0.3), 0.7) = \max(0.2, 0.7) = 0.7$ and $\max(\kappa(0.2), 0.8) = \max(0.1, 0.8) = 0.8$, with
mean $0.75$, so $\text{lrd}(x) = 1.33$.

**LOF.** Each point in $A$ has neighbours in $A$ with the same lrd, so its LOF is exactly $1$. The same holds in $B$.
The neighbours of $x$ both have lrd $6.67$, so

$$
\text{LOF}(x) = \frac{6.67}{1.33} = 5.
$$

LOF ranks $x$ first. Every other point gets a LOF of $1$, whatever the density of its cluster.

## Choosing k

$k$ sets the size of the neighbourhood that counts as local. Breunig and colleagues recommend $k$ of at least 10 to
avoid unstable densities, and at most the size of the smallest group of points that should count as a cluster rather
than as outliers. They suggest computing LOF over a range of $k$ and keeping each point's maximum
<Cite id="breunig2000" />.

## Cost

LOF needs the $k$ nearest neighbours of every point. Brute force costs $O(n^2 p)$ time. Spatial indices reduce this in
low dimension. The lrd of every point must be computed before any LOF, so scoring a new point requires the stored
k-distances and lrd values of the training data.

## Assumptions

- **Density varies slowly within a cluster.** LOF compares a point with its neighbours. If density changes sharply
  inside a cluster, the points where it changes get LOF values above 1.
- **Neighbours are meaningful.** As with any nearest-neighbour method, distances must be informative. In high
  dimension neighbourhoods become unstable.

## Pitfalls

- **No universal threshold.** LOF is a ratio without a reference distribution. A value of 1.5 can be a clear outlier
  in one data set and a normal fluctuation in another. Rank by LOF, or calibrate a threshold on held-out data.
- **Duplicates.** When more than $k$ points coincide, their k-distance is zero and their lrd is infinite. Implementations
  either deduplicate or add a small constant.
- **Border points.** Points on the boundary between a dense and a sparse cluster have neighbours in both and can get
  LOF values above 1 without being anomalous.
- **Many variants.** Connectivity-based, probabilistic and influence-based variants change how the neighbourhood and the
  reference set are chosen. Schubert, Zimek and Kriegel show that they share the same pattern: a local model of each
  point and a comparison with a reference set <Cite id="schubert2012" />.
