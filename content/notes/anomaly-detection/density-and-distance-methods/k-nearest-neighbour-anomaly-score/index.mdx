<Definition>
For a data set $\Dcal = \set{\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n}$ in $\reals^p$ and a distance $\delta$, let $r_k(\xvec)$ be the
distance from $\xvec$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbour in $\Dcal$, not counting $\xvec$ itself. The k-NN anomaly score
is $s(\xvec) = r_k(\xvec)$ <Cite id="ramaswamy2000" />. A common variant averages the distances to all $k$ nearest
neighbours <Cite id="angiulli2002" />. Points with the largest scores are reported as anomalies.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A normal point sits among other points, so its nearest neighbours are close. An anomaly sits apart, so even its
nearest neighbours are far. The score needs no model of the data's shape: clusters may be curved, elongated or
many.

Distance-based outlier detection began with Knorr and Ng, who called a point a $DB(\pi, D)$ outlier when at least a
fraction $\pi$ of the data lies farther than $D$ from it <Cite id="knorr1998" />. That definition gives a yes-or-no
label and needs the radius $D$. Ramaswamy, Rastogi and Shim replaced it with a ranking by $r_k$, which needs only $k$
and returns the top-$m$ outliers for any $m$ <Cite id="ramaswamy2000" />.

## The score is an inverse density estimate

The k-NN distance ranks points by an estimate of the data density. Let the data have density $f$. The ball of radius
$r$ around $\xvec$ has volume $V_p r^p$, where $V_p$ is the volume of the unit ball in $\reals^p$. For a small ball the
probability mass inside it is about $f(\xvec) V_p r^p$, so among $n$ points about $n f(\xvec) V_p r^p$ fall inside.
The ball of radius $r_k(\xvec)$ contains $k$ points by construction. Setting the two equal and solving for $f$ gives
the k-NN density estimate

$$
\hat{f}(\xvec) = \frac{k}{n\,V_p\,r_k(\xvec)^p}.
$$

For fixed $k$, $n$ and $p$, $\hat{f}$ is a decreasing function of $r_k$. Ranking points by decreasing $r_k$ is the same
as ranking them by increasing estimated density. The k-NN score is a low-density score. A kernel density estimate
gives a similar ranking with a fixed bandwidth in place of an adaptive radius.

## Worked example

Take five points on a line, $\set{0, 1, 2, 3, 10}$, with absolute difference as the distance.

| point | nearest distances | $r_1$ | $r_2$ | mean of 2 |
| ----- | ----------------- | ----- | ----- | --------- |
| 0     | 1, 2              | 1     | 2     | 1.5       |
| 1     | 1, 1              | 1     | 1     | 1         |
| 2     | 1, 1              | 1     | 1     | 1         |
| 3     | 1, 2              | 1     | 2     | 1.5       |
| 10    | 7, 8              | 7     | 8     | 7.5       |

Every score ranks $10$ first. With $k = 2$ the end points $0$ and $3$ score higher than the interior points $1$ and
$2$, because a point at the edge of a cluster has neighbours on one side only.

Now add a second anomaly at $10.5$. With $k = 1$ the two anomalies are each other's nearest neighbour, and both get
$r_1 = 0.5$, the lowest score in the set. With $k = 2$ their second neighbours are the points $3$, at distances $7$ and
$7.5$, and they rank first again. $k$ must exceed the size of the largest group of anomalies that sit together.

## Clusters of different density

The score compares every point's neighbourhood with a single global scale. When normal data form clusters of different
density, the points of a sparse cluster have larger k-NN distances than an anomaly just outside a dense cluster. The
score then ranks the sparse cluster's fringe above the anomaly.

## Cost

Computing $r_k$ for all $n$ points by brute force costs $O(n^2 p)$. Spatial indices such as k-d trees reduce this in low
dimension (see <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search" />). When only the top $m$ outliers are needed, a point can be
discarded as soon as $k$ neighbours closer than the current $m$-th largest score are found. With the data in random
order this pruning makes the search close to linear in $n$ in practice <Cite id="bay2003" />.

## Assumptions

- **Anomalies are isolated.** A point is anomalous because it is far from others. An anomaly inside a dense region,
  or a large group of anomalies, is not found.
- **One density scale.** Normal data are assumed to have similar density everywhere. Local methods remove this
  assumption.
- **A meaningful distance.** Features must be scaled so that the distance weighs them sensibly. Standardising each
  feature is the usual default.

## Pitfalls

- **High dimension.** As the dimension grows, distances to the nearest and farthest points become similar, and the
  k-NN score loses contrast (see <NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours" />). Irrelevant features add
  noise to every distance.
- **Choosing $k$.** Too small a $k$ lets small groups of anomalies hide each other, as the worked example shows. Too
  large a $k$ merges small normal clusters with their surroundings and flags them.
- **Duplicates.** Repeated identical points have zero distance to one another. With $k$ below the number of copies, a
  duplicated anomaly scores zero.
