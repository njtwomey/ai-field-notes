<Definition>
Deep robust one-class classification (DROCC), introduced by Goyal and colleagues <Cite id="goyal2020" />, assumes that
normal data lie on a low-dimensional manifold that the training set samples densely, so that points a small distance
$r$ away from every training point are anomalous. It trains a network $f_\theta : \reals^D \to \reals$ as a binary
classifier: training points are positives, and synthetic points generated near them, at distance between $r$ and
$\gamma r$ with $\gamma \ge 1$, are negatives. The negatives are chosen adversarially, as the points in that shell that
the current classifier finds most normal. The classifier's output is the <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly score</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="deep-support-vector-data-description">Deep SVDD</NoteLink> has only positive examples, and a constant
map satisfies it perfectly. Any objective that sees only normal data has this weakness. The cure used by
<NoteLink to="deep-semi-supervised-anomaly-detection">Deep SAD</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="fully-convolutional-data-description">FCDD</NoteLink> is to add negative examples. DROCC generates the
negatives itself, from a geometric assumption about where normal data live.

If normal data lie on a thin manifold, a point that has stepped off the manifold by a small distance $r$ is no longer
normal. Such points are close to the data in input space but on the wrong side of the boundary a good detector must
draw. A classifier that separates the training points from all their off-manifold neighbours has drawn a tight boundary
around the manifold. The hardest neighbours, the ones the classifier currently accepts, are found by <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> ascent,
as in adversarial training of a supervised classifier.

## Objective

For training points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$, a classification loss $\ell$ (cross-entropy), labels $+1$ for normal and
$-1$ for anomalous, and weights $\lambda, \mu > 0$, DROCC solves <Cite id="goyal2020" />

$$
\min_\theta\ \lambda\norm{\theta}^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\ell\paren{f_\theta(\xvec_i), +1}
+ \mu\max_{\tilde\xvec_i \in N_i(r)} \ell\paren{f_\theta(\tilde\xvec_i), -1}\bigg].
$$

The ideal set of negatives for point $i$ is

$$
N_i(r) = \set{\tilde\xvec : \norm{\tilde\xvec - \xvec_i} \le \gamma r,\ \ \norm{\tilde\xvec - \xvec_j} \ge r \text{ for all } j}:
$$

points within $\gamma r$ of $\xvec_i$ and at least $r$ from every training point. The upper bound $\gamma r$ keeps the
search local. The inner maximum finds, for each training point, the nearby off-manifold point the classifier is most
wrong about. The problem is a saddle point, like adversarial training, with the usual ball of perturbations replaced by
$N_i(r)$.

The constraint involving all $j$ couples every training point and is expensive. DROCC relaxes it to the annulus

$$
N_i(r) \approx \set{\tilde\xvec : r \le \norm{\tilde\xvec - \xvec_i} \le \gamma r},
$$

and relies on the manifold being low-dimensional: most directions out of the manifold leave it, so a point in the
annulus around $\xvec_i$ is rarely close to another training point. A smaller weight $\mu$ on the negatives guards
against the few that are <Cite id="goyal2020" />.

## Training: projected gradient ascent

Each training step finds the negatives, then updates $\theta$ <Cite id="goyal2020" />.

1. For each $\xvec_i$ in the batch, start from $\tilde\xvec_i = \xvec_i + \hvec$ with $\hvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$.
2. Repeat a few times: take a normalised ascent step on the negative loss,
   $\hvec \leftarrow \hvec + \eta\,\nabla_{\hvec}\ell / \norm{\nabla_{\hvec}\ell}$, then project $\xvec_i + \hvec$ back
   onto the annulus.
3. Take a gradient step on $\theta$ for
   $\lambda\norm{\theta}^2 + \sum_i \ell(f_\theta(\xvec_i), +1) + \mu\,\ell(f_\theta(\xvec_i + \hvec_i), -1)$.

A few initial epochs train on the positives alone. Goyal and colleagues set $\gamma = 2$ and choose $r$, $\lambda$,
$\mu$ and $\eta$ by validation.

### The projection onto the annulus

The projection of a point $\zvec$ onto the annulus $\set{\uvec : r \le \norm{\uvec - \xvec_i} \le \gamma r}$ keeps its
direction from $\xvec_i$ and clips its distance. Let $\beta = \norm{\zvec - \xvec_i}$ and let $\beta'$ be $\beta$
clipped to $[r, \gamma r]$. For any $\uvec$ in the annulus, the reverse triangle inequality gives

$$
\norm{\uvec - \zvec} \ge \abs{\norm{\uvec - \xvec_i} - \norm{\zvec - \xvec_i}} \ge \abs{\beta' - \beta},
$$

since $\norm{\uvec - \xvec_i}$ lies in $[r, \gamma r]$ and $\beta'$ is the point of that interval nearest $\beta$. The
point $\xvec_i + (\beta'/\beta)(\zvec - \xvec_i)$ lies in the annulus at distance exactly $\abs{\beta' - \beta}$ from
$\zvec$, so it attains the bound and is the projection. This is the closed form DROCC uses <Cite id="goyal2020" />.

## Why DROCC does not collapse

A constant classifier, $f_\theta(\xvec) = a$ for every $\xvec$, labels positives and negatives alike. With the
cross-entropy loss, its loss per training point is $\ell(a, +1) + \mu\,\ell(a, -1)$, which is bounded below by a
positive constant for every $a$. A classifier that separates the manifold from its $r$-shell drives both terms towards
zero. The objective asks for discrimination, so the trivial solution is never optimal <Cite id="goyal2020" />. This
is the same mechanism as outlier exposure <Cite id="hendrycks2019b" />, with the negatives generated from the training
data instead of taken from a separate corpus.

## Negatives that are available

DROCC extends to the case where a limited set of real negatives exists but cannot cover all anomalies, such as
detecting one spoken wake word against all other speech. Adding a cross-entropy term on the real negatives gives a
variant analogous to outlier exposure, DROCC–OE. A second variant, DROCC–<Gloss name="labelling-function" />, uses the real negatives to learn which
input features are noise, and measures the distance $r$ in a metric that down-weights them <Cite id="goyal2020" />.

## Assumptions

- **Normal data lie on a well-sampled, low-dimensional manifold** that is locally Euclidean at scale $r$. Where the
  data fill a full-dimensional region, the annulus around a training point contains other normal points, and DROCC
  learns to reject normal data.
- **Euclidean distance in input space is meaningful at scale $r$.** For images, a pixel-space perturbation of norm $r$
  may be imperceptible or may destroy the image, depending on $r$.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing $r$.** The radius sets how tight the boundary is, and there are no labels to set it. Too small, and
  negatives are indistinguishable from normal points. Too large, and the boundary is loose.
- **Cost.** Each training step runs several ascent steps, each a forward and backward pass, on every batch element.
  Training costs several times as much as for Deep SVDD.
- **Noisy features.** Irrelevant input dimensions inflate distances in every direction, so the annulus mixes
  meaningful and meaningless perturbations. DROCC–LF addresses this when some negatives are available.
