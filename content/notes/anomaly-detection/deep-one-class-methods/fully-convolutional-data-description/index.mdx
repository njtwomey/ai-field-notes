<Definition>
Fully convolutional data description (FCDD), introduced by Liznerski and colleagues <Cite id="liznerski2021" />, is a
deep one-class detector for images whose network contains only convolution and pooling layers. The network maps an
image $\Xmat$ to a $u \times v$ grid $\phi(\Xmat)$. Each cell is passed through the pseudo-Huber function
$a \mapsto \sqrt{a^2 + 1} - 1$ to give a non-negative heatmap $A(\Xmat)$. The mean of $A(\Xmat)$ is the <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly score</NoteLink>,
and $A(\Xmat)$, upsampled to the image size, shows which regions raised it. Training uses normal images and a set of
anomalous images, which may be unrelated natural images or synthetic corruptions.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="deep-support-vector-data-description" /> gives one score per image. An inspector who is told that a part
is defective also wants to know where. A <NoteLink to="semantic-segmentation">fully convolutional network</NoteLink> keeps spatial layout: each output cell depends only
on a patch of the input, its <NoteLink to="pooling-and-strides">receptive field</NoteLink>, and the cells sit in the same arrangement as their patches. If the
training loss asks every cell to be small on normal images, a large cell on a test image points at the patch that
caused it.

## The hypersphere classifier loss

FCDD adapts the hypersphere classifier of Ruff and colleagues <Cite id="ruff2020b" />. That classifier treats
$p(\text{normal} \mid \xvec) = \exp\paren{-h(\phi(\xvec) - \cvec)}$ as the probability that $\xvec$ is normal, where
$h(\avec) = \sqrt{\norm{\avec}^2 + 1} - 1$ is the pseudo-Huber function <Cite id="huber1964" />. Since $h \ge 0$ the
probability lies in $(0, 1]$, and it equals 1 only at the centre. With labels $y = 0$ for normal and $y = 1$ for
anomalous inputs, the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">binary cross-entropy</NoteLink> of this probability model is

$$
\ell(\xvec, y) = (1 - y)\, h(\phi(\xvec) - \cvec) - y\log\paren{1 - \exp\paren{-h(\phi(\xvec) - \cvec)}} .
$$

A normal input pays its distance to the centre, as in Deep SVDD. An anomaly pays $-\log(1 - e^{-h})$, which falls to
zero as $h$ grows and rises to $+\infty$ as $h \to 0$.

The pseudo-Huber function behaves like $\frac{1}{2}\norm{\avec}^2$ near the centre, since
$\sqrt{1 + s} = 1 + \frac{s}{2} + O(s^2)$ for small $s$, and like $\norm{\avec} - 1$ far from it. Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>,
$\avec / \sqrt{\norm{\avec}^2 + 1}$, has norm below 1. A few far-away normal points, such as mislabelled anomalies, cannot
dominate training as they would under the squared loss. For example, $h(0.1) = 0.005$ and $h(10) = 9.05$.

## The FCDD objective and score

Let $\phi$ map an image to a single-channel $u \times v$ grid, and let

$$
A(\Xmat) = \sqrt{\phi(\Xmat)^2 + 1} - 1,
$$

with all operations elementwise. The centre is absorbed into the last layer's bias. With $\bar A(\Xmat)$ the mean of the
$uv$ cells, FCDD minimises over the network weights <Cite id="liznerski2021" />

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - y_i)\,\bar A(\Xmat_i) - y_i\log\paren{1 - \exp\paren{-\bar A(\Xmat_i)}} .
$$

The anomaly score of a test image is $\bar A(\Xmat)$, and the grid $A(\Xmat)$ is the explanation.

### Why a learned centre does not collapse here

Deep SVDD must fix its centre and drop bias terms, because otherwise the constant map is optimal. FCDD learns the
centre and keeps biases. The anomalous term is what makes this safe. For a constant network, every image, normal or
anomalous, has the same mean $\bar A = a \ge 0$. The objective is then

$$
(1 - \pi)\,a - \pi\log\paren{1 - e^{-a}},
$$

where $\pi$ is the fraction of anomalous training images. As $a \to 0$ the second term tends to $+\infty$, and as
$a \to \infty$ the first does. A constant map can do no better than balance the two, while a network that separates the
two groups drives both terms towards zero. The constant map is no longer a minimiser, as long as anomalous examples are
present.

## Where the anomalous examples come from

**Outlier exposure.** For benchmarks where normal data are one class of natural images, the anomalous examples can be
random images from a large unrelated corpus such as <Gloss name="imagenet" /> <Cite id="liznerski2021" />. This practice is called
outlier exposure <Cite id="hendrycks2019b" />. The network learns what the normal class is not, from examples that
were never meant to resemble the test anomalies.

**Synthetic anomalies.** For defect detection, where anomalies are small flaws on an otherwise normal object, natural
images are uninformative. FCDD then corrupts normal images with "confetti noise", small coloured blobs pasted at random
places <Cite id="liznerski2021" />. The corruption mirrors the local character of real defects.

**A few real anomalies.** With a handful of labelled defective images and their ground-truth masks $\Ymat_i$, FCDD
trains per pixel on the upsampled heatmap $A'$, which is described below. With $m$ pixels per image, the objective becomes

$$
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \paren{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} (1 - Y_{ij})\,A'_{ij}}
- \log\paren{1 - \exp\paren{-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} Y_{ij}\,A'_{ij}}} .
$$

Normal pixels are pulled to zero and the defective pixels of each image are pushed up together. On the MVTec AD
defect benchmark <Cite id="bergmann2019" />, one labelled image per defect type raised the pixel-level AUC of the
heatmaps from 0.92 with confetti noise alone to 0.96 <Cite id="liznerski2021" />.

## Upsampling the heatmap

The grid $A(\Xmat)$, with $u \times v$ cells, is coarser than the image, with $H \times W$ pixels. Without ground-truth masks at training
time, an upsampling layer cannot be learned. FCDD upsamples with a fixed rule instead <Cite id="liznerski2021" />. Each
output cell has a receptive field in the input, centred on a pixel $\pvec$. The influence of input pixels on a
cell of a deep convolutional network decays roughly like a Gaussian with distance from the centre of its receptive
field. FCDD therefore spreads each cell's value over the image as a Gaussian bump:

$$
A'(\Xmat) = \sum_{\text{cells } k} A_k(\Xmat)\, G_\sigma(\,\cdot - \pvec_k), \qquad
G_\sigma(\qvec) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\exp\paren{-\frac{\norm{\qvec}^2}{2\sigma^2}} .
$$

Because the cell centres $\pvec_k$ lie on a regular grid with spacing equal to the network's total stride, this
sum is a transposed convolution with a fixed Gaussian kernel, whose size is the receptive field and whose stride is the
network's stride.

## Assumptions

- **Anomalies are local.** The heatmap is informative when the anomaly occupies a region comparable to a receptive
  field: a scratch, a stain, a missing screw. A global anomaly, such as a wrong object class, raises every cell.
- **The anomalous examples are informative.** Outlier exposure works when the corpus shares low-level statistics with
  normal data but differs in content. Synthetic corruptions work when they resemble real defects.

## Pitfalls

- **Explanations reveal shortcuts.** Liznerski and colleagues found that deep one-class models trained on one class of
  a public image data set scored images by watermarks in a corner rather than by content, a "Clever Hans" effect. The
  FCDD heatmap made the shortcut visible <Cite id="liznerski2021" />. A heatmap is a diagnostic as much as an output.
- **Receptive field size.** Too small, and each cell sees too little context to judge a patch. Too large, and the
  heatmap blurs.
- **Collapse returns without negatives.** With $y_i = 0$ for every image, the objective is Deep SVDD with a learned
  centre and bias terms, and the constant map is optimal.
