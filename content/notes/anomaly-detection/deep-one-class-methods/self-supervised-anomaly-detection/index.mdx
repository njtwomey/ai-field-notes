<Definition>
A self-supervised anomaly detector trains a network on normal data to solve a <NoteLink to="self-supervised-learning">pretext task</NoteLink> whose labels come from the
data themselves, and uses the network's performance on that task as a normality score. In the main family, the task is
to recognise which of $M$ fixed transformations $T_1, \dots, T_M$, such as rotations of an image, was applied to an
input. A normal input, transformed by $T_m$, is recognised as $T_m$. An anomalous input is not recognised reliably, and
its <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly score</NoteLink> is the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative log-likelihood</NoteLink> of the correct transformations, summed over $m$ <Cite id="golan2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A network that has learned to tell whether a photograph of a car is upright, upside down or on its side has learned
where the wheels and the roof are. That knowledge is specific to cars. Shown a rotated photograph of a cat, the same
network cannot tell which way is up and spreads its prediction over the rotations. The uncertainty reveals that the
input is unlike the training data.

The pretext task turns one-class learning into ordinary multi-class classification, with all the tools that work for
it. It also removes the collapse that troubles <NoteLink to="deep-support-vector-data-description">Deep SVDD</NoteLink>.

## Transformation prediction

Golan and El-Yaniv apply $M = 72$ transformations to every normal image: the compositions of a horizontal flip or not,
nine translations and four rotations by multiples of $90°$ <Cite id="golan2018" />. A classifier $\yvec(\cdot)$ with a
softmax output over $M$ classes is trained to predict $m$ from $T_m(\xvec)$. At test time the input is transformed by
every $T_m$, and the normality score combines the predicted probabilities of the correct classes,
$y_m(T_m(\xvec))$. Their full score fits a <NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution">Dirichlet distribution</NoteLink> to the softmax outputs of each transformation on
training data; a simpler version, which ranks inputs almost as well, is the summed log-probability
<Cite id="golan2018" />:

$$
n(\xvec) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \log y_m\paren{T_m(\xvec)} .
$$

The anomaly score is $-n(\xvec)$. Hendrycks and colleagues use the same idea with rotation prediction alone, the
pretext task of RotNet <Cite id="hendrycks2019c" /> <Cite id="gidaris2018" />.

### Beyond images: GOAD

Rotations and flips suit images. Bergman and Hoshen generalise the method to any data with random affine
transformations $T_m(\xvec) = \Wmat_m\xvec + \bvec_m$, and replace the softmax classifier with one based on distances
<Cite id="bergman2020" />. A network $f$ maps every transformed input to a feature space, and transformation $m$ has a
centre $\cvec_m = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i f(T_m(\xvec_i))$, the mean feature of the transformed training data. The
probability of transformation $m'$ given the transformed input $T_m(\xvec)$ is a softmax over negative squared
distances to the centres:

$$
P\paren{m' \mid T_m(\xvec)} = \frac{\exp\paren{-\norm{f(T_m(\xvec)) - \cvec_{m'}}^2}}
{\sum_{\tilde m} \exp\paren{-\norm{f(T_m(\xvec)) - \cvec_{\tilde m}}^2}} .
$$

Each transformation's data form a hypersphere in feature space, as in Deep SVDD, and the classifier tells the
hyperspheres apart. Treating the $M$ transformed copies as independent, the probability that $\xvec$ is normal is the
product of the probabilities of the correct transformations, and the anomaly score is

$$
s(\xvec) = -\sum_{m=1}^{M} \log \tilde P\paren{m \mid T_m(\xvec)} .
$$

Far from the training data, all distances are large and a small difference between them makes the softmax confidently
pick one transformation. $\tilde P$ therefore adds a small $\epsilon$ to each numerator term and $M\epsilon$ to the
denominator, which pushes the probabilities of distant points towards uniform <Cite id="bergman2020" />.

## Worked example: why GOAD needs ε

Take one-dimensional normal data concentrated near $x = 2$, two transformations $T_1(x) = x$ and $T_2(x) = -x$, and the
identity as the feature map. The centres are $c_1 = 2$ and $c_2 = -2$. For transformation 1,

$$
P(1 \mid x) = \frac{e^{-(x - 2)^2}}{e^{-(x - 2)^2} + e^{-(x + 2)^2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-8x}},
$$

and $P(2 \mid -x)$ has the same value, so without $\epsilon$ the score is $s(x) = 2\log(1 + e^{-8x})$.

| $x$                             | 2      | 1       | 0       | −2     | 10      |
| ------------------------------- | ------ | ------- | ------- | ------ | ------- |
| $s(x)$, $\epsilon = 0$          | 0.0000 | 0.0007 | 1.386 | 32.0  | 0.0000 |
| $s(x)$, $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$    | 0.0000 | 0.0007 | 1.386 | 27.4  | 1.386  |

Without $\epsilon$, the point $x = 10$, far from all the data, scores the same as the normal point $x = 2$: both
transformed copies land closer to the correct centre than to the wrong one. With $\epsilon$, both probabilities are
close to $\frac{1}{2}$ and the score is $2\log 2 = 1.386$, the score of maximal uncertainty. The point $x = -2$, whose
copies land on the wrong centres, scores highest in both cases. A transformation classifier flags points whose
transformations it confuses. It cannot flag points whose transformations it finds easy to tell apart, however far they
lie from the data.

## Contrastive learning with shifted instances

Contrastive learning trains an encoder $\zvec(\cdot)$ so that two augmented views of the same input are similar and
views of different inputs are dissimilar (see <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive learning</NoteLink>), as in SimCLR <Cite id="chen2020" />. Tack and colleagues observed that
some transformations should not be treated as augmentations <Cite id="tack2020" />. Rotating a natural image by $90°$
produces an image unlike any in the data set. CSI, contrastive learning on distributionally shifted instances, treats
such shifted copies as different instances, negatives in the contrastive loss, and also trains a head to predict which
shift was applied.

The detection score of CSI uses two properties of a contrastive representation: the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> of $\zvec(\xvec)$
to its nearest training example, and the norm $\norm{\zvec(\xvec)}$, which contrastive training tends to make larger for
in-distribution inputs <Cite id="tack2020" />:

$$
s_{\text{con}}(\xvec) = \max_i \operatorname{sim}\paren{\zvec(\xvec_i), \zvec(\xvec)} \cdot \norm{\zvec(\xvec)} .
$$

This is a normality score. CSI averages it over the shifted copies of the input and adds the log-probability of the
correct shift from the prediction head. The distinction CSI draws between augmentations and shifts is the one that
makes transformation prediction work: a transformation is informative only if normal data carry a recognisable
signature of it.

## Why self-supervision helps

**No collapse.** A constant network outputs the same prediction for every transformed input. Every transformation
appears equally often in training, so the best constant prediction is the uniform one, and its cross-entropy is
$\log M$: the loss of guessing, the worst a classifier needs to do, not the best. The objective can only be lowered
by features that depend on the input. The pretext task supplies the negatives that the one-class objectives lack.

**Features tied to the normal class.** The transformation can be predicted only from features that change under it
and are consistent across normal data: the orientation of objects, the position of typical parts. Such features are
specific to the normal class and are learned from normal data alone.

**A well-posed training signal.** Minimising a distance to a centre, as Deep SVDD does, does not say which features to
keep. Classification does. A review by Ruff and colleagues places these methods among classification-based detectors
for this reason and notes that whether self-supervision learns semantic features or low-level statistics remains open
<Cite id="ruff2021" />.

## Assumptions

- **Normal data are not invariant to the transformations.** Rotation prediction fails on textures, aerial images or
  cell images, which look the same at every orientation. The transformations must suit the data.
- **Anomalies change the signature.** An anomaly that shares the normal class's orientation cues, such as a defect on
  an otherwise normal part, may be classified confidently and missed.

## Pitfalls

- **Far anomalies can score as normal**, as the worked example shows. Regularising the probabilities, or combining the
  score with a distance, guards against it.
- **Cost at test time.** Every input is transformed $M$ times and passed through the network $M$ times.
- **Pretrained features often do better.** On image benchmarks, nearest-neighbour scores on features pretrained on
  large labelled data sets have matched or beaten self-supervised detectors trained on the normal class alone (see
  <NoteLink to="pretrained-features-for-anomaly-detection" />).
