<Definition>
Anomaly detection with pretrained features maps every input through a network $\psi$ pretrained on a large, unrelated
labelled data set, usually ImageNet classification <Cite id="deng2009" />, and scores a test input by its distance to
the nearest features of normal training data. With $\Ncal_k(\xvec)$ the $k$ training features nearest to
$\psi(\xvec)$, the score is

$$
s(\xvec) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{\zvec \in \Ncal_k(\xvec)} \norm{\psi(\xvec) - \zvec}^2 .
$$

Variants adapt $\psi$ to the normal data before scoring, or score local patches of an image to locate defects.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A one-class objective trained from scratch, such as
<NoteLink to="deep-support-vector-data-description">Deep SVDD</NoteLink>, has to discover which variations of the input
matter from normal data alone. Nothing in "map these images close together" says that the shape of an object matters
and the lighting does not. A network trained to separate a thousand object classes on over a million labelled images
has already learned that distinction. In its feature space, images of one class occupy a compact region, and images of
another class land elsewhere.

Anomaly detection then reduces to the oldest method there is: a point far from all normal points is anomalous (see
<NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbour-anomaly-score">k-nearest-neighbour anomaly score</NoteLink>). Bergman, Cohen and Hoshen showed that this baseline, applied to
ImageNet-pretrained ResNet features, outperformed the self-supervised detectors of its time on standard image
benchmarks, and was more robust to few training images and to contaminated training data <Cite id="bergman2020b" />.

## Why pretrained features often win

- **The training signal is stronger.** A one-class objective sees one class and no labels. Pretraining sees many
  classes and many labels. The features it learns separate semantic categories because that was its task.
- **Collapse is impossible without training.** A frozen network cannot collapse. Every one-class objective trained from
  scratch must be protected against the constant map; a nearest-neighbour score on frozen features needs no protection.
- **Normal data are often scarce.** A few hundred normal images are too few to learn good features but enough to
  populate a nearest-neighbour memory.

The advantage depends on transfer. Pretrained features describe natural photographs. For medical scans, spectra or
tabular records they may miss the variations that matter, and adapting or replacing them is necessary.

## Adapting the features without collapse

Fine-tuning the pretrained network on normal data should make normal features more compact. The obvious loss, the
compactness loss $\sum_i \norm{\psi(\xvec_i) - \cvec}^2$ with $\cvec$ the mean initial feature, is the Deep SVDD loss,
and a pretrained network has bias terms and batch normalisation. Trained to convergence, it collapses. Reiss and
colleagues found a broader problem: well before full collapse, the features lose discriminative properties they had
<Cite id="reiss2021" />.

**PANDA** (pretrained anomaly detection adaptation) fine-tunes with the compactness loss and limits the drift in two ways
<Cite id="reiss2021" />. The simplest is early stopping after a fixed number of epochs. The second borrows elastic
weight consolidation from continual learning <Cite id="kirkpatrick2017" />: it adds the penalty

$$
\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_j F_j\paren{\theta_j - \theta_j^0}^2,
$$

where $\theta^0$ are the pretrained weights and $F_j$ the diagonal of the Fisher information of the pretraining task.
Weights that mattered for ImageNet classification are held near their pretrained values; the others are free to adapt.

**Mean-shifted contrastive learning.** Contrastive learning is a stronger adaptation signal, but it fails here for a
reason Reiss and Hoshen identify <Cite id="reiss2023" />. The contrastive loss has two effects on normalised features:
views of the same image are pulled together, and all features are spread uniformly over the unit sphere. In a pretrained
space, normal images occupy a small cap of the sphere around their mean direction. Spreading them over the whole sphere
destroys exactly the compactness that separated them from anomalies.

The fix measures angles around the normal mean instead of the origin. Let $\cvec$ be the mean of the normalised
pretrained features of the training set. Each normalised feature is shifted to $\psi(\xvec)/\norm{\psi(\xvec)} - \cvec$,
and the contrastive loss is applied to the cosine similarities of these shifted vectors. Uniformity now spreads the
normal data around $\cvec$, not over the sphere, so it no longer pushes normal features away from the region they share
<Cite id="reiss2023" />. The anomaly score is the cosine distance to the nearest training features.

## Patch-level scores: PatchCore

Industrial inspection needs to find a scratch on an otherwise perfect part, and to say where it is. Whole-image features
average the scratch away. Patch-level methods keep a feature for every location.

SPADE scores each pixel by the distance of its feature to its nearest neighbours among the pixel features of normal
images <Cite id="cohen2020" />. PaDiM fits a Gaussian to the features at each image position and scores by Mahalanobis
distance <Cite id="defard2021" />. PatchCore, by Roth and colleagues, combines three choices <Cite id="roth2022" />:

1. **Locally aware, mid-level features.** Features come from intermediate layers of the pretrained network, which
   describe local structure rather than object class, averaged over a small neighbourhood of positions to widen their
   receptive field.
2. **A memory bank.** The patch features of all normal training images form a bank $\Mcal$. The score of a test patch
   is its distance to the nearest bank entry, and the image score is the largest patch score. The patch scores,
   upsampled and smoothed, form the segmentation map.
3. **A coreset.** The bank is subsampled to a fraction of its size, for speed, by greedy $k$-centre selection: start
   from one patch, and repeatedly add the patch farthest from every patch chosen so far.

The greedy rule minimises, within a factor of two, the largest distance from any bank patch to its nearest chosen patch
<Cite id="gonzalez1985" />. That is the right criterion for a nearest-neighbour score. A normal test patch is scored by
its distance to the nearest kept patch, and the greedy coreset keeps that distance small for every kind of normal patch,
including rare ones. Random subsampling keeps patches in proportion to how common they are, so rare normal patterns lose
their representatives and start to look anomalous. On the MVTec AD benchmark <Cite id="bergmann2019" />, PatchCore
reports image-level AUROC up to 99.6 % <Cite id="roth2022" />.

## Worked example: a coreset keeps the rare rivet

The figure builds a bank of 4,608 patches of $5 \times 5$ raw pixels from eight normal images of diagonal stripes. Each
image has one bright rivet in a random place, so rivet patches are a few percent of the bank. The test image has a
rivet at column 20, row 8, and a dark dent at column 10, row 18.

- With the full bank, the rivet patch scores $0.57$ and the dent patch $1.09$. The image score, the largest patch
  score, is $1.17$, next to the dent.
- A random 10 % of the bank, 461 patches, keeps few rivet patches. The rivet patch now scores $1.01$, and the largest
  patch score in the image, $1.28$, sits beside the rivet rather than the dent. The detector would point an inspector
  at the normal part.
- A greedy coreset of the same size keeps the rivet at $0.57$. The largest score, $1.21$, is beside the dent.

The raw-pixel features stand in for a pretrained network's features. The bank, the nearest-neighbour score, the maximum
over patches and the coreset are those of PatchCore.

## Assumptions

- **The pretraining domain resembles the target.** ImageNet features transfer to photographs of objects and textures.
  They transfer less well to data with different statistics.
- **Anomalies differ in the pretrained feature space.** A defect that the pretrained network's features ignore, such as
  a subtle change of colour in a channel the features discount, is invisible to the score.
- **The training set covers normal variation.** A nearest-neighbour score flags every normal pattern that is missing
  from the bank.

## Pitfalls

- **Memory and search cost.** The bank grows with the number of training images and patch positions. Coresets and
  <NoteLink to="approximate-nearest-neighbour-search">approximate nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> keep test time manageable.
- **Adaptation can hurt.** Fine-tuning on normal data without safeguards degrades the features it set out to improve.
- **Benchmarks favour the pretraining domain.** Results on natural-image benchmarks overstate what pretrained features
  do for data unlike ImageNet (see <NoteLink to="evaluating-anomaly-detectors">evaluating anomaly detectors</NoteLink>).
