<Definition>
Deep semi-supervised <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly detection</NoteLink> (Deep SAD), introduced by Ruff and colleagues <Cite id="ruff2020" />, trains a
network $\phi(\cdot\,; \Wcal)$ on $n$ unlabelled inputs $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ and $m$ labelled inputs
$(\tilde\xvec_1, \tilde y_1), \dots, (\tilde\xvec_m, \tilde y_m)$, where $\tilde y_j = +1$ marks a known normal input and
$\tilde y_j = -1$ a known anomaly. Unlabelled and labelled normal inputs are pulled towards a fixed centre $\cvec$, as in
<NoteLink to="deep-support-vector-data-description" />. Labelled anomalies are penalised by the inverse of their squared
distance from $\cvec$, which pushes them away. The anomaly score is $s(\xvec) = \norm{\phi(\xvec) - \cvec}^2$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In practice a few anomalies are often known: past frauds, a handful of defective parts, a flagged intrusion. A
supervised classifier trained on them learns what those anomalies look like and assumes future anomalies cluster
nearby. Anomalies need not cluster. The next fraud may look nothing like the last.

Deep SAD uses the labels without that assumption. It keeps the one-class goal, pulling normal data together, and adds
one instruction: whatever the network does, it must not map the known anomalies near the centre. Normal data are
concentrated; anomalies are only required to be spread out.

## Objective

With $d(\xvec) = \norm{\phi(\xvec; \Wcal) - \cvec}^2$ and weight-decay strength $\lambda > 0$, Deep SAD minimises
<Cite id="ruff2020" />

$$
\frac{1}{n + m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d(\xvec_i) + \frac{\eta}{n + m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} d(\tilde\xvec_j)^{\tilde y_j}
+ \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \norm{\Wmat^\ell}_F^2 .
$$

The exponent $\tilde y_j$ selects the loss. A labelled normal input, $\tilde y_j = +1$, pays $d$, the same quadratic
loss as the unlabelled data. A labelled anomaly, $\tilde y_j = -1$, pays $1/d$. The hyperparameter $\eta > 0$ weights
the labelled terms against the unlabelled ones; Ruff and colleagues found $\eta = 1$ to work consistently. With $m = 0$
the objective is the one-class Deep SVDD objective. The unlabelled term also encodes the assumption that most
unlabelled data are normal. In code, a small constant $\epsilon \approx 10^{-6}$ is added to $d$ in the denominator.

The same architectural rules as Deep SVDD apply: a fixed centre, set from the initial embeddings, and a network without
bias terms <Cite id="ruff2020" />.

### Choice of the inverse-distance loss

The anomaly loss must reward distance from $\cvec$. Three properties decide its form.

- **Bounded below.** The negative squared distance $-d$ also rewards distance, but it is unbounded below. The network
  can drive it to $-\infty$ by scaling the anomalies' embeddings without limit, and training diverges. Ruff and
  colleagues report exactly this <Cite id="ruff2020" />. The inverse $1/d$ is bounded below by zero.
- **Smooth.** A hinge such as $\max(0, r - d)$ has a margin $r$ to choose and a kink at the margin. Ruff and colleagues
  found hinge losses hard to optimise <Cite id="ruff2020" />.
- **A push that fades.** The gradient of $1/d$ with respect to the embedding $\zvec = \phi(\tilde\xvec)$ is

  $$
  \nabla_{\zvec}\, \frac{1}{d} = -\frac{2(\zvec - \cvec)}{d^2},
  $$

  of magnitude $2/d^{3/2}$. A labelled anomaly close to $\cvec$ gets a strong push outward. One already far away gets
  almost none, so the loss does not keep reshaping the network once the anomaly is out of the way.

A labelled anomaly with $d = 0$ would cost $\eta/\epsilon$. The constant map $\phi \equiv \cvec$ is therefore no
longer a minimiser as soon as one labelled anomaly exists: labelled anomalies make collapse expensive, not only
unreachable.

## The information-theoretic view

Ruff and colleagues read Deep SVDD and Deep SAD as statements about the entropy of the embedding
$\zvec = \phi(\xvec)$ <Cite id="ruff2020" />.

**Deep SVDD bounds the entropy of normal embeddings.** Let $\zvec \in \reals^p$ have covariance $\Sigmamat$. Among all
distributions with covariance $\Sigmamat$, the Gaussian has the largest
<NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>, so

$$
\entropy(\zvec) \le \frac{1}{2}\log\paren{(2\pi e)^p \det\Sigmamat}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is the product of the eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p \ge 0$ of $\Sigmamat$ and the trace is
their sum. The inequality of arithmetic and geometric means gives $\det\Sigmamat \le (\trace\Sigmamat / p)^p$. The trace
is the mean squared distance from the mean, and the mean minimises the mean squared distance over all points, so
$\trace\Sigmamat = \expect\norm{\zvec - \expect\zvec}^2 \le \expect\norm{\zvec - \cvec}^2$ for any $\cvec$. Together,

$$
\entropy(\zvec) \le \frac{p}{2}\log\paren{\frac{2\pi e}{p}\,\expect\norm{\zvec - \cvec}^2}.
$$

The one-class Deep SVDD data term is the empirical $\expect\norm{\zvec - \cvec}^2$. Minimising it minimises an upper
bound on the <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> of the normal embeddings.

**Deep SAD adds an entropy term for anomalies.** Pulling normal embeddings in lowers the entropy of
$\zvec^+ = \zvec \mid y = +1$. Penalising small distances of labelled anomalies keeps the anomalous embeddings
$\zvec^- = \zvec \mid y = -1$ spread out, which favours high entropy. Ruff and colleagues write the goal as an Infomax
objective with an entropy regulariser:

$$
\max_{p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}\ \operatorname{I}(\xvec; \zvec) + \beta\paren{\entropy(\zvec^-) - \entropy(\zvec^+)}, \qquad \beta > 0.
$$

The <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> term $\operatorname{I}(\xvec; \zvec)$ asks the embedding to keep information about the
input. Deep SVDD and Deep SAD serve it only indirectly, through pretraining the network as the encoder of an autoencoder
<Cite id="ruff2020" />. The entropy term encodes the modelling assumption: normal data come from one concentrated
distribution, anomalies from a broad mixture of everything else. No cluster assumption is placed on the anomalies.

## Worked example: three labels move the boundary

The figure trains a <Gloss name="relu" /> network $2 \to 16 \to 16 \to 2$ without bias terms for 200 full-batch Adam epochs on 150
normal points in two Gaussian blobs. An unseen class of 47 anomalies sits between the blobs, near $(0, 1.3)$. The
threshold is the 95th percentile of the training scores.

- With $\eta = 0$, the model is Deep SVDD. The network interpolates between the blobs, and 49 % of the unseen cluster
  scores above the threshold.
- With $\eta = 1$ and three labelled anomalies drawn from the same cluster, 96 % of the cluster is flagged. The
  labels raise the scores of the region between the blobs.
- The <Gloss name="auroc">area under the ROC curve</Gloss> on anomalies drawn uniformly over the square falls from $0.956$ to $0.911$. The push
  away from three points reshapes the map elsewhere too. Labels help most on anomalies like the labelled ones.

Moving the labelled anomalies moves the high-score region with them. A labelled anomaly placed inside a normal blob
fights the unlabelled term, and the two terms settle on a compromise that raises the scores of nearby normal points.

## Assumptions

- **Most unlabelled data are normal.** The unlabelled term pulls every unlabelled point towards $\cvec$. A large
  contamination is pulled in too.
- **Labelled anomalies are correct.** A mislabelled normal point pushes part of the normal region away.
- **Anomalies are not concentrated.** The objective asks only that they be spread out, which suits anomalies that come
  from many unrelated causes.

## Pitfalls

- **Labels bias the detector towards known anomalies.** The scores of unseen kinds of anomaly can fall, as in the
  worked example. Evaluate on anomaly types held out from the labelled set.
- **η weights the labelled terms against the unlabelled term.** A large $\eta$ makes the terms of a few labelled points outweigh the unlabelled term in the objective. On image benchmarks Ruff and colleagues found
  performance fairly stable for $\eta$ between $10^{-2}$ and $10^{2}$ <Cite id="ruff2020" />.
- **Auxiliary anomalies are not true anomalies.** Using a large corpus of unrelated data as the labelled anomalies,
  called outlier exposure <Cite id="hendrycks2019b" />, works when the corpus resembles future anomalies. For subtle
  defects of a normal object it may teach little.
