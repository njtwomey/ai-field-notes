<Definition>
Deep support vector data description (Deep SVDD), introduced by Ruff and colleagues <Cite id="ruff2018" />, trains a
neural network $\phi(\cdot\,; \Wcal) : \reals^D \to \reals^d$ with weights $\Wcal = \set{\Wmat^1, \dots, \Wmat^L}$ so
that the embeddings of normal training data lie inside a small hypersphere around a fixed centre $\cvec \in \reals^d$.
The <NoteLink to="anomaly-detection">anomaly score</NoteLink> of an input $\xvec$ is its squared distance from the centre, $s(\xvec) = \norm{\phi(\xvec; \Wcal^*) - \cvec}^2$,
where $\Wcal^*$ are the trained weights. The centre is fixed before training, and the network has no bias terms.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="support-vector-data-description">Kernel SVDD</NoteLink> fixes a feature map, given by a kernel, and
learns the smallest sphere that encloses the mapped data. Deep SVDD reverses the roles. It fixes the sphere's centre
and learns the feature map.

To pull every normal input close to one point, the network must keep the features that all normal inputs share and
discard the features in which they differ. An input that lacks the shared features lands far from $\cvec$. The network
is a learned compression of normality.

The danger is that the network finds a cheaper way to pull everything to $\cvec$: ignore the input altogether. A
constant map puts every input, normal or not, exactly on $\cvec$ at zero loss. Most of the design of Deep SVDD exists
to make that constant map unreachable.

## Objectives

Write $\phi(\xvec_i)$ for $\phi(\xvec_i; \Wcal)$ and $d_i = \norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \cvec}^2$ for the squared distance of
training point $i$ from the centre. Ruff and colleagues give two objectives <Cite id="ruff2018" />.

The **soft-boundary** objective copies the kernel SVDD primal with slack $\xi_i = \max(0, d_i - R^2)$:

$$
\min_{R,\, \Wcal}\ R^2 + \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\paren{0,\ d_i - R^2} + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \norm{\Wmat^\ell}_F^2 .
$$

$R$ is the radius, $\nu \in (0, 1]$ sets how many training points may fall outside, and $\lambda > 0$ weights the <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight
decay</Gloss>. The **one-class** objective drops the radius and penalises every distance:

$$
\min_{\Wcal}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \cvec}^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \norm{\Wmat^\ell}_F^2 .
$$

The one-class objective suits training data that are all, or nearly all, normal. The soft-boundary objective lets a
fraction $\nu$ of contaminated training points stay outside without pulling the sphere towards them.

Both objectives are trained by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> on $\Wcal$. The soft-boundary radius is not trained by
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> steps: every few epochs it is set to its optimal value given the current embeddings <Cite id="ruff2018" />.

### The optimal radius and the ν-property

For fixed weights, the soft-boundary objective is a function of $\rho = R^2 \ge 0$ alone:

$$
J(\rho) = \rho + \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max(0, d_i - \rho).
$$

$J$ is convex and piecewise linear. Moving $\rho$ up by a small amount raises the first term at rate $1$ and lowers the
sum at rate $1/(\nu n)$ for every point with $d_i > \rho$. The right <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is therefore
$1 - n_{>}(\rho)/(\nu n)$, with $n_{>}(\rho) = \abs{\set{i : d_i > \rho}}$. Moving $\rho$ down raises the sum for every
point with $d_i \ge \rho$, so the left derivative is $1 - n_{\ge}(\rho)/(\nu n)$. At a minimum the left derivative is at
most zero and the right derivative is at least zero:

$$
\frac{n_{>}(\rho^*)}{n} \le \nu \le \frac{n_{\ge}(\rho^*)}{n}.
$$

$\nu$ bounds the fraction of training points strictly outside the sphere from above, and the fraction outside or on it
from below. The optimal $R^*$ is the $(1 - \nu)$ quantile of the distances $\sqrt{d_i}$. This is the ν-property of the
<NoteLink to="one-class-support-vector-machine">one-class support vector machine</NoteLink>, recovered without a dual <Cite id="ruff2018" />.

## The anomaly score

The score is $s(\xvec) = \norm{\phi(\xvec; \Wcal^*) - \cvec}^2$. For the soft-boundary model, $s(\xvec) - R^{*2}$ is
positive outside the sphere and negative inside, so zero is a natural threshold. The model needs no stored training
data at test time: the weights and $\cvec$ are the whole description <Cite id="ruff2018" />.

## Hypersphere collapse

Both objectives are non-negative apart from weight decay. A network that outputs $\cvec$ for every input makes every
$d_i = 0$ and so attains the minimum of the data term. Such a network scores every input $0$ and detects nothing. Ruff
and colleagues call this **hypersphere collapse**, because the optimal radius collapses to zero <Cite id="ruff2018" />.
Three routes lead to it.

**A learned centre.** Set every weight matrix to zero. Each layer then outputs a constant, so the network outputs the
same vector $\cvec_0 = \phi(\xvec; \zeros)$ for every $\xvec$. For a network without bias terms and with $\sigma(0) = 0$,
such as <Gloss name="relu" />, $\cvec_0 = \zeros$. If $\cvec$ is a free variable, the choice $\Wcal = \zeros$, $\cvec = \cvec_0$, $R = 0$
makes the data term zero and the weight decay zero. The whole objective is zero, its lower bound, so this trivial
solution is a global minimum. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on $\cvec$ and $\Wcal$ together is pulled towards it. A fixed centre must
therefore differ from $\cvec_0$.

**Bias terms.** Suppose layer $\ell$ computes $\zvec^\ell = \sigma(\Wmat^\ell \zvec^{\ell - 1} + \bvec^\ell)$. With
$\Wmat^\ell = \zeros$, the layer outputs $\sigma(\bvec^\ell)$ whatever its input. The later layers receive a constant
and output a constant, and the bias and later weights can be chosen to make that constant $\cvec$, provided $\cvec$ is
reachable. The data term is then zero for any fixed $\cvec$. A single bias term anywhere in the network is enough.

**Bounded activations.** Let a unit have a monotone activation with $B = \sup_z \sigma(z) \ne 0$, and suppose one of its
inputs, feature $k$, is positive on every training point: $z^{(k)}_i > 0$ for all $i$. Inputs that come out of a ReLU
layer, or pixel intensities, are often of this kind. Give feature $k$ the weight $t$ and every other input weight zero.
As $t \to \infty$, $\sigma(t z^{(k)}_i) \to B$ uniformly over the finite training set, because
$\min_i z^{(k)}_i > 0$. The unit then outputs almost the constant $B$ on every training point and acts as a bias term
for the next layer, which reopens the previous route <Cite id="ruff2018" />. With weight decay the large weight $t$ has
a cost, so this route gives a near-collapse traded against $\lambda t^2$ rather than an exact one.

### Rules that prevent collapse

The three routes give three rules <Cite id="ruff2018" />:

1. **Fix $\cvec$, and fix it away from $\cvec_0$.** Ruff and colleagues set $\cvec$ to the mean of the initial network's
   embeddings of the training data. Any fixed $\cvec \ne \cvec_0$ blocks the first route. Placing $\cvec$ where the
   network already maps the data makes training faster and more stable. The reference implementation also pushes coordinates of
   $\cvec$ that are nearly zero out to a small $\pm\epsilon$, because a zero coordinate is matched by a unit whose
   incoming weights are all zero.
2. **Use no bias terms**, including the learnable shift of <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink>.
3. **Use unbounded activations**, such as ReLU, or activations bounded only by zero.

These rules exclude an exactly constant map, and the reason is homogeneity. A network without bias terms and with ReLU
activations is positively homogeneous: $\phi(\alpha\xvec) = \alpha\phi(\xvec)$ for every $\alpha \ge 0$, because
$\max(0, \alpha z) = \alpha\max(0, z)$ and every layer is linear otherwise. In particular $\phi(\zeros) = \zeros$, so
the input $\zeros$ always scores $\norm{\cvec}^2 > 0$, and scores along any ray from the origin follow the quadratic
$s(\alpha\xvec) = \norm{\alpha\phi(\xvec) - \cvec}^2$. The network cannot be constant. It can still learn a poor
description, for instance one that ignores most input directions, as the worked example shows.

Later work adds further safeguards, which a review by Ruff and colleagues lists: a reconstruction term from autoencoder
pretraining, freezing pretrained features, penalising low variance of the embeddings, and training with true, auxiliary
or synthetic anomalies as negatives <Cite id="ruff2021" />. Each of these makes the constant map suboptimal rather than
merely unreachable. <NoteLink to="deep-semi-supervised-anomaly-detection" />,
<NoteLink to="fully-convolutional-data-description" /> and
<NoteLink to="deep-robust-one-class-classification" /> are built on the last idea.

## Worked example: a linear network ignores all but one direction

Take a network with one bias-free linear layer, $\phi(\xvec) = \Wmat\xvec$, the one-class objective and weight decay
$\lambda$. Write $\bar\xvec = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xvec_i$ for the data mean and $\Mmat = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \xvec_i\xvec_i^\top$
for the second-moment matrix. Setting the gradient to zero,

$$
\frac{2}{n}\sum_i (\Wmat\xvec_i - \cvec)\xvec_i^\top + \lambda\Wmat = \zeros
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Wmat^* = \cvec\,\avec^\top, \qquad \avec = \paren{\Mmat + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\eye}^{-1}\bar\xvec .
$$

$\Wmat^*$ has rank one, and the score is $s(\xvec) = \norm{\cvec}^2\paren{1 - \avec^\top\xvec}^2$. The model measures
the input along the single direction $\avec$. It gives score zero to the whole hyperplane $\avec^\top\xvec = 1$, however
far along it a point lies.

Take the four points $(1, 0)$, $(3, 0)$, $(2, 1)$ and $(2, -1)$, the centre $\cvec = (1, 1)$ and $\lambda = 0$. Then
$\bar\xvec = (2, 0)$ and $\Mmat = \diag(4.5, 0.5)$, so $\avec = (4/9, 0)$ and
$s(\xvec) = 2\paren{1 - \tfrac{4}{9}x_1}^2$. The training scores are $0.617$ for $(1, 0)$, $0.222$ for $(3, 0)$ and
$0.025$ for each of $(2, \pm 1)$. The point $(2.25, 100)$, far from all the data, scores $0$. The origin scores
$\norm{\cvec}^2 = 2$.

If the data are centred, $\bar\xvec = \zeros$, then $\avec = \zeros$ and $\Wmat^* = \zeros$: every input scores
$\norm{\cvec}^2$. The linear network collapses even without bias terms, because it has no way to map the data to a
point other than through their mean. Deep SVDD depends on depth and nonlinearity to give the network enough ways to be
selective.

## Relation to kernel SVDD

Freeze the network and free the centre. The soft-boundary objective becomes

$$
\min_{R,\, \cvec}\ R^2 + \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\paren{0,\ \norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \cvec}^2 - R^2},
$$

which is the kernel SVDD primal with slack $\xi_i = \max(0, \norm{\phi(\xvec_i) - \cvec}^2 - R^2)$, cost
$C = 1/(\nu n)$ and the kernel $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \inner{\phi(\xvec)}{\phi(\xvec')}$ <Cite id="tax2004" />. Kernel SVDD
is soft-boundary Deep SVDD with the features frozen and the centre learned. Deep SVDD is kernel SVDD with the features
learned and the centre frozen. Learning both at once is what collapses.

The kernel of a frozen network becomes a classical kernel as the network widens. Let the features be one hidden layer of
$m$ ReLU units with random Gaussian weights, $\phi(\xvec) = m^{-1/2}\max(0, \Umat\xvec)$ with the entries of $\Umat$
drawn from $\Gauss(0, 1)$. By the law of large numbers, as $m \to \infty$,

$$
\inner{\phi(\xvec)}{\phi(\xvec')} \to \frac{\norm{\xvec}\norm{\xvec'}}{2\pi}\paren{\sin\theta + (\pi - \theta)\cos\theta},
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\xvec$ and $\xvec'$. This is the arc-cosine kernel of degree one
<Cite id="cho2009" />. So kernel SVDD with the arc-cosine kernel is the limit of soft-boundary Deep SVDD with an
infinitely wide, untrained hidden layer and a free centre. The arc-cosine kernel has $k(\xvec, \xvec) = \norm{\xvec}^2/2$,
which is not constant, so in this limit SVDD and the one-class SVM differ.

Deep SVDD also has a probabilistic reading. Minimising the mean squared distance to $\cvec$ minimises an upper bound on
the entropy of the embeddings, since among distributions with a given covariance the Gaussian has the largest entropy.
The note <NoteLink to="deep-semi-supervised-anomaly-detection" /> derives the bound <Cite id="ruff2020" />.

## Assumptions

- **Normal data share features that anomalies lack.** The network can only pull normal data together through features
  they have in common. Anomalies that share those features and differ elsewhere land near $\cvec$ and are missed.
- **Clean or lightly contaminated training data.** The one-class objective pulls every training point in, anomalies
  included. The soft-boundary objective tolerates a fraction $\nu$.
- **A useful initial network.** $\cvec$ is fixed from the initial embeddings, and training only moves the embeddings.
  Ruff and colleagues initialise the network with the encoder of a pretrained autoencoder <Cite id="ruff2018" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Collapse by the back door.** Batch normalisation with a learnable shift, a bias in any layer, or saturating
  activations reopen the trivial solution. A loss that falls towards zero while the scores of held-out anomalies fall
  with it is the symptom.
- **Homogeneity bias.** A bias-free ReLU network treats inputs along the same ray alike, up to scale. Centring or
  otherwise shifting the inputs changes which anomalies are easy to find.
- **No labels, no tuning.** The architecture, $\lambda$ and the number of epochs change the score substantially, and in
  the unsupervised setting nothing tells which choice is right (see <NoteLink to="evaluating-anomaly-detectors">evaluating anomaly detectors</NoteLink>).
