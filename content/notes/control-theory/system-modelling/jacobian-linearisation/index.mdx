<Definition>
Let $\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(\xvec, \uvec)$ with $\fvec$ continuously differentiable, and let $(\xvec^*, \uvec^*)$ be an
**equilibrium**: $\fvec(\xvec^*, \uvec^*) = \zeros$. The **linearisation** at that point is the linear system

$$
\dot{\delta\xvec} = \Amat\,\delta\xvec + \Bmat\,\delta\uvec, \qquad
\Amat = \frac{\partial \fvec}{\partial \xvec}\bigg|_{(\xvec^*, \uvec^*)}, \qquad
\Bmat = \frac{\partial \fvec}{\partial \uvec}\bigg|_{(\xvec^*, \uvec^*)},
$$

in the deviations $\delta\xvec = \xvec - \xvec^*$ and $\delta\uvec = \uvec - \uvec^*$. $\Amat$ is the $n \times n$
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> of $\fvec$ with respect to the state and $\Bmat$ the $n \times m$ Jacobian
with respect to the input.
</Definition>

## Derivation

Expand $\fvec$ in a first-order Taylor series around the equilibrium:

$$
\fvec(\xvec^* + \delta\xvec, \uvec^* + \delta\uvec) = \fvec(\xvec^*, \uvec^*) + \Amat\,\delta\xvec + \Bmat\,\delta\uvec
+ \rvec(\delta\xvec, \delta\uvec).
$$

Differentiability of $\fvec$ means the remainder is small compared with the deviation:
$\norm{\rvec} / \norm{(\delta\xvec, \delta\uvec)} \to 0$ as the deviation tends to zero. The first term vanishes
because the point is an equilibrium. Since $\xvec^*$ is constant, $\dot{\delta\xvec} = \dot{\xvec}$, so

$$
\dot{\delta\xvec} = \Amat\,\delta\xvec + \Bmat\,\delta\uvec + \rvec(\delta\xvec, \delta\uvec).
$$

Dropping $\rvec$ gives the linear model. An output map $\yvec = \hvec(\xvec, \uvec)$ is linearised the same way, with
$\Cmat = \partial\hvec/\partial\xvec$ and $\Dmat = \partial\hvec/\partial\uvec$ at the equilibrium and
$\delta\yvec = \yvec - \hvec(\xvec^*, \uvec^*)$.

The approximation is only local. The linear model is accurate while the deviations stay small enough that $\rvec$ is
negligible against the linear terms, and how small that is depends on the curvature of $\fvec$.

## What the linearisation says about stability

**Lyapunov's indirect method** relates the eigenvalues of $\Amat$ (with $\delta\uvec = \zeros$) to the stability of the
nonlinear equilibrium <Cite id="khalil2002" />:

- if every eigenvalue of $\Amat$ has a negative real part, the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable;
- if some eigenvalue has a positive real part, the equilibrium is unstable;
- if the eigenvalues with the largest real part lie on the imaginary axis, the linearisation decides nothing.

The first statement has a short proof with a quadratic <NoteLink to="lyapunov-stability">Lyapunov function</NoteLink>.
When $\Amat$ is stable there is a positive definite $\Pmat$ with $\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat = -\eye$. Along
trajectories of the nonlinear system, $V = \delta\xvec^\top\Pmat\,\delta\xvec$ changes at the rate

$$
\dot{V} = -\norm{\delta\xvec}^2 + 2\,\delta\xvec^\top\Pmat\,\rvec(\delta\xvec).
$$

Because $\norm{\rvec}/\norm{\delta\xvec} \to 0$, there is a ball around the equilibrium in which
$\norm{\rvec} \le \norm{\delta\xvec} / (4\norm{\Pmat})$. Inside it the second term is at most
$\tfrac12\norm{\delta\xvec}^2$, so $\dot{V} \le -\tfrac12\norm{\delta\xvec}^2 < 0$ away from the equilibrium, and
trajectories starting close enough converge.

The third case is genuine. $\dot{x} = -x^3$ and $\dot{x} = x^3$ both linearise at $x = 0$ to $\dot{\delta x} = 0$,
yet the first is asymptotically stable and the second is unstable. The neglected cubic term decides.

## Worked example: the pendulum

A pendulum of length $\ell$ with viscous damping and a torque input obeys
$\ddot{\theta} = -(g/\ell)\sin\theta - c\dot{\theta} + u$, where $\theta$ is the angle from the downward vertical,
$g$ the gravitational acceleration, $c$ the damping rate and $u$ the torque divided by $m\ell^2$. With
$\xvec = (\theta, \dot{\theta})$,

$$
\fvec(\xvec, u) = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ -(g/\ell)\sin x_1 - c x_2 + u \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \fvec}{\partial \xvec} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -(g/\ell)\cos x_1 & -c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \fvec}{\partial u} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

With $u^* = 0$ the equilibria are $\theta^* = 0$ (hanging down) and $\theta^* = \pi$ (inverted). Take
$g/\ell = 9.81\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ and $c = 0.5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

- **Hanging down**, $\cos 0 = 1$: $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -9.81 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$. The eigenvalues
  are $-0.25 \pm 3.12i$. The equilibrium is stable, and small swings oscillate at about $3.12$ rad/s while decaying
  as $e^{-0.25t}$.
- **Inverted**, $\cos\pi = -1$: $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 9.81 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$. The eigenvalues are
  $2.89$ and $-3.39$. The positive eigenvalue makes the equilibrium unstable: a small tilt grows as $e^{2.89t}$.

The inverted linearisation is the model used to design a balancing controller. The input matrix
$\Bmat = (0, 1)$ is the same at both equilibria. With it, state feedback $u = -\kvec^\top\delta\xvec$ can move both
eigenvalues into the left half-plane, and by the indirect method the nonlinear pendulum is then balanced locally.

## Linearising about a trajectory

The same expansion works around a nominal trajectory $(\xvec^*(t), \uvec^*(t))$ that satisfies
$\dot{\xvec}^* = \fvec(\xvec^*, \uvec^*)$ but is not constant. The Jacobians are then evaluated along the trajectory
and $\Amat(t)$, $\Bmat(t)$ vary with time. Trajectory tracking controllers and the
<NoteLink to="extended-and-unscented-kalman-filters">extended Kalman filter</NoteLink> use this time-varying
linearisation.

## Assumptions

- $\fvec$ is continuously differentiable at the equilibrium. Friction with a discontinuity at zero velocity, relays
  and saturations at their corners have no Jacobian there.
- The deviations stay small. The region in which the linear model is accurate is not given by the linearisation
  itself.

## Pitfalls

- **Local is not global.** A controller that stabilises the linearisation stabilises the nonlinear system only in
  some neighbourhood, whose size the linear analysis does not reveal. The figure shows how far the approximation
  holds for the pendulum.
- **Forgetting the offsets.** The linear model is in deviation variables. The input applied to the real system is
  $\uvec^* + \delta\uvec$ and the measured output must have $\hvec(\xvec^*, \uvec^*)$ subtracted before it is used.
- **Marginal cases.** Eigenvalues on the imaginary axis, such as an undamped pendulum's $\pm\sqrt{g/\ell}\,i$, leave
  stability to the nonlinear terms.
