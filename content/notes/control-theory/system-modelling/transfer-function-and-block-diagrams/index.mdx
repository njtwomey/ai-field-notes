<Definition>
The **transfer function** of a linear time-invariant system is the ratio $G(s) = Y(s)/U(s)$ of the Laplace transforms
of its output and input when the initial state is zero. For the state-space model $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec +
\Bmat\uvec$, $\yvec = \Cmat\xvec + \Dmat\uvec$ it is the $p \times m$ matrix of rational functions

$$
\Gmat(s) = \Cmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat + \Dmat.
$$

A **block diagram** connects transfer functions by signal lines, summing junctions and branch points. Its algebra
reduces any interconnection to one transfer function.
</Definition>

## The Laplace transform

The **Laplace transform** of a signal $f(t)$ defined for $t \ge 0$ is
$F(s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt$, for complex $s$ with real part large enough that the integral converges. Two
properties carry all the weight in control.

- **Derivatives become multiplication by $s$.** Integration by parts gives
  $\int_0^\infty \dot{f}(t)e^{-st}\,dt = \brack{f(t)e^{-st}}_0^\infty + s\int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt = sF(s) - f(0)$.
- **Convolution becomes multiplication.** The transform of $\int_0^t g(t - \tau)u(\tau)\,d\tau$ is $G(s)U(s)$, by
  the same change of variables that proves the <NoteLink to="convolution-theorem">convolution theorem</NoteLink>
  for the Fourier transform.

A linear differential equation therefore becomes an algebraic equation in $s$. The transform plays the role for
continuous-time systems that the <NoteLink to="z-transform">z-transform</NoteLink> plays for discrete-time systems.
Useful pairs: a unit step has transform $1/s$, and $e^{at}$ has transform $1/(s - a)$.

## From state space to transfer function

Transform the state equation with $\xvec(0) = \zeros$. The derivative rule gives
$s\Xmat(s) = \Amat\Xmat(s) + \Bmat\Umat(s)$, so $(s\eye - \Amat)\Xmat(s) = \Bmat\Umat(s)$. For any $s$ that is not an
eigenvalue of $\Amat$, the matrix $s\eye - \Amat$ is invertible, and

$$
\Xmat(s) = (s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat\Umat(s), \qquad \Ymat(s) = \paren{\Cmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat + \Dmat}\Umat(s).
$$

The inverse is the adjugate divided by the determinant: $(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(s\eye - \Amat) /
\det(s\eye - \Amat)$. Every entry of the adjugate is a polynomial of degree at most $n - 1$, and the determinant is
the characteristic polynomial of $\Amat$, of degree $n$. So each entry of $\Gmat(s)$ is a ratio of polynomials. The
denominator degree is at least the numerator degree (the transfer function is **proper**), and strictly greater when
$\Dmat = \zeros$ (**strictly proper**).

The **poles** of $G(s)$ are the roots of its denominator. They are eigenvalues of $\Amat$. An eigenvalue can fail to
appear as a pole when a zero of the numerator cancels it. The cancelled mode is then either not excited by the input
or not seen at the output, which the note on
<NoteLink to="controllability-and-observability">controllability and observability</NoteLink> makes precise. The
transfer function describes only the part of the system that is both.

The state-space model is not unique, but the transfer function is. Under a similarity transformation
$\Amat \to \Tmat^{-1}\Amat\Tmat$, $\Bmat \to \Tmat^{-1}\Bmat$, $\Cmat \to \Cmat\Tmat$, the factors of $\Tmat$ cancel
inside $\Cmat\Tmat(s\eye - \Tmat^{-1}\Amat\Tmat)^{-1}\Tmat^{-1}\Bmat = \Cmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat$.

## Frequency response

Drive a stable single-input single-output system with the complex exponential $u(t) = e^{i\omega t}$. The forced
response is $\int_0^t g(\tau)e^{i\omega(t - \tau)}\,d\tau = e^{i\omega t}\int_0^t g(\tau)e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\tau$,
where $g$ is the impulse response. As $t \to \infty$ the integral tends to $G(i\omega)$, because $g$ decays. The
steady-state output is $G(i\omega)e^{i\omega t}$. Taking real parts, the input $\cos\omega t$ produces
$\abs{G(i\omega)}\cos(\omega t + \angle G(i\omega))$. The **frequency response** $G(i\omega)$ is the transfer function
on the imaginary axis: its magnitude is the gain at frequency $\omega$ and its angle is the phase shift. The
<NoteLink to="frequency-response">discrete-time frequency response</NoteLink> is the same idea on the unit circle.

## Block diagram algebra

Three rules reduce any diagram of single-input single-output blocks.

- **Series.** $U \to G_1 \to G_2 \to Y$ gives $Y = G_2 G_1 U$.
- **Parallel.** Two blocks fed by the same input and summed give $Y = (G_1 + G_2)U$.
- **Feedback.** A forward block $G$ with a feedback block $H$ subtracted at the input satisfies $Y = G(R - HY)$.
  Solving for $Y$,

$$
Y = \frac{G}{1 + GH}\,R.
$$

The product $GH$ around the loop is the **loop transfer function**. The denominator $1 + GH$ is the
**return difference**. The closed-loop poles are the roots of $1 + GH = 0$, not the poles of $G$ or $H$, which is why
feedback can stabilise an unstable plant or destabilise a stable one.

## Sensitivity and complementary sensitivity

The standard feedback loop has a plant $P(s)$ and a controller $C(s)$. The controller acts on the error
$e = r - y_m$ between a reference $r$ and a measured output $y_m = y + n$ corrupted by sensor noise $n$. A disturbance
$d$ adds to the plant output: $y = PCe + d$. Write $L = PC$ for the loop transfer function. Substituting
$e = r - y - n$,

$$
y = L(r - y - n) + d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = \frac{L}{1 + L}\,r + \frac{1}{1 + L}\,d - \frac{L}{1 + L}\,n.
$$

Two functions of the loop appear:

$$
S = \frac{1}{1 + L}, \qquad T = \frac{L}{1 + L}, \qquad S + T = 1.
$$

- $S$, the **sensitivity function**, maps output disturbances to the output and the reference to the tracking error,
  $r - y = S r - S d + T n$.
- $T$, the **complementary sensitivity function**, maps the reference to the output and the sensor noise to the
  output with a minus sign.

The name of $S$ comes from its second meaning. A relative change in the plant, $dP/P$, produces a relative change in
the closed-loop transfer function $T$. Differentiating $T = PC/(1 + PC)$ with respect to $P$ gives
$dT/dP = C/(1 + PC)^2$, so

$$
\frac{dT/T}{dP/P} = \frac{dT}{dP}\,\frac{P}{T} = \frac{C}{(1 + PC)^2}\,\frac{P(1 + PC)}{PC} = \frac{1}{1 + PC} = S.
$$

Where $\abs{S}$ is small, the closed loop is insensitive to plant errors. This is the main reason to use feedback.

The identity $S + T = 1$ holds at every frequency, so $S$ and $T$ cannot both be small at the same frequency. Good
tracking and disturbance rejection need $\abs{S(i\omega)}$ small, and good noise rejection needs $\abs{T(i\omega)}$
small. The usual resolution is to split the frequency axis: a large loop gain $\abs{L}$ at low frequencies, where
references and disturbances live, makes $S \approx 1/L$ small; a small loop gain at high frequencies, where sensor
noise lives, makes $T \approx L$ small. The frequency where $\abs{L}$ crosses 1 is where the two regimes meet, and
where stability is decided.

## Worked example

The mass-spring-damper $\ddot{q} + 3\dot{q} + 2q = u$ has $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$,
$\Bmat = (0, 1)$, $\Cmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\Dmat = 0$. Then

$$
s\eye - \Amat = \begin{bmatrix} s & -1 \\ 2 & s + 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1} = \frac{1}{s^2 + 3s + 2}\begin{bmatrix} s + 3 & 1 \\ -2 & s \end{bmatrix},
$$

and $P(s) = \Cmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat = 1/(s^2 + 3s + 2)$. The same result follows from transforming
$\ddot{q} + 3\dot{q} + 2q = u$ directly: $(s^2 + 3s + 2)Q(s) = U(s)$.

Close the loop with a proportional controller $C = 10$. Then $L = 10/(s^2 + 3s + 2)$ and

$$
T(s) = \frac{10}{s^2 + 3s + 12}, \qquad S(s) = \frac{s^2 + 3s + 2}{s^2 + 3s + 12}.
$$

The closed-loop poles are the roots of $s^2 + 3s + 12$, at $-1.5 \pm 3.12i$: feedback moved the poles from $-1$ and
$-2$ to a faster, oscillatory pair. At $s = 0$, $T(0) = 10/12 = 0.833$ and $S(0) = 2/12 = 0.167$. A unit step
reference settles at $0.833$, leaving a steady-state error of $0.167$. A 10 % increase in the plant's static gain, from $0.5$ to $0.55$, raises
the closed-loop static gain from $0.833$ to $0.846$, a change of $1.5$ %. The sensitivity predicts
$0.167 \times 10\,\% = 1.7\,\%$ to first order.

## Assumptions

- The system is linear and time-invariant, and the transfer function describes it only from zero initial state. The
  free response from a nonzero initial state adds to the forced response.
- $S + T = 1$ holds for the single-loop configuration above. With the controller split between the reference path and
  the feedback path (two degrees of freedom), the response to the reference can be shaped separately, but $S$ and $T$
  still govern disturbances, noise and robustness.

## Pitfalls

- **Cancelling an unstable pole with a zero.** A controller zero placed on an unstable plant pole removes it from
  $PC$, but the mode is still in the loop. It appears in the transfer function from a disturbance at the plant input
  to the output, and the closed loop is internally unstable.
- **Judging a loop by $T$ alone.** A good reference response says nothing about the response to disturbances entering
  elsewhere. Internal stability needs all four transfer functions between the loop's inputs and outputs to be stable.
