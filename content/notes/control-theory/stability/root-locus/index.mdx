<Definition>
Let $G(s) = N(s)/D(s)$ be a rational open-loop transfer function with $m$ zeros (roots of $N$) and $n \ge m$ poles
(roots of $D$). Under negative feedback with gain $K$, the closed-loop poles are the roots of

$$
1 + KG(s) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D(s) + K N(s) = 0.
$$

The **root locus** is the set of these roots for all $K \ge 0$, drawn in the complex plane. It has $n$ branches, one
per closed-loop pole <Cite id="evans1950" />.
</Definition>

## Angle and magnitude conditions

$1 + KG(s) = 0$ means $KG(s) = -1$. A complex number equals $-1$ exactly when its magnitude is 1 and its angle is an
odd multiple of $180°$. Since $K > 0$ is real, this splits into two conditions:

$$
\angle G(s) = (2k + 1)\,180°, \qquad K = \frac{1}{\abs{G(s)}}.
$$

The **angle condition** decides whether $s$ lies on the locus at all, independently of $K$. The **magnitude
condition** then gives the gain that places a closed-loop pole there. Writing
$G(s) = \prod_i (s - z_i) / \prod_j (s - p_j)$ (after absorbing any leading constant into $K$), the angle of $G$ at
$s$ is the sum of the angles of the vectors from each zero to $s$ minus the sum of the angles of the vectors from each
pole to $s$. The magnitude is the product of the lengths from the zeros divided by the product of the lengths from the
poles. Evans turned these two geometric facts into rules for sketching the locus by hand <Cite id="evans1950" />.

## Rules for the shape

Each rule follows from the characteristic equation or the angle condition.

1. **Symmetry.** $D + KN$ has real coefficients, so its complex roots come in conjugate pairs. The locus is symmetric
   about the real axis.
2. **Start and end.** At $K = 0$ the equation is $D(s) = 0$: the branches start at the open-loop poles. Dividing by
   $K$ gives $D/K + N = 0$, which tends to $N(s) = 0$ as $K \to \infty$: $m$ branches end at the open-loop zeros.
3. **Asymptotes.** The other $n - m$ branches go to infinity. For large $\abs{s}$,
   $G(s) = s^{-(n-m)}\paren{1 + (\sum_j p_j - \sum_i z_i)/s + O(s^{-2})} \approx (s - \sigma)^{-(n-m)}$, with
   $\sigma = \paren{\sum_j p_j - \sum_i z_i}/(n - m)$. The equation $(s - \sigma)^{n-m} = -K$ has roots on straight
   lines from $\sigma$ at angles $(2k + 1)\,180° / (n - m)$. With $n - m = 2$ the asymptotes are vertical; with
   $n - m = 3$ two of them lean into the right half-plane at $\pm 60°$, so high gain eventually destabilises the loop.
4. **Real axis.** For a real $s$, each complex pair of poles or zeros contributes angles that cancel, and each real
   pole or zero to the left of $s$ contributes $0°$. Each real pole or zero to the right contributes $180°$. The angle
   condition therefore holds on the parts of the real axis that have an **odd** number of real poles and zeros to
   their right.
5. **Breakaway and break-in points.** Two branches meet on the real axis and leave it where the characteristic
   equation has a double root: $D + KN = 0$ and $D' + KN' = 0$. Eliminating $K = -D/N$ gives
   $D'N - DN' = 0$, which is $dK/ds = 0$ along the real axis.
6. **Imaginary-axis crossings.** The gain at which a branch crosses into the right half-plane, and the crossing
   frequency, come from the <NoteLink to="routh-hurwitz-criterion">Routh array</NoteLink> or from solving
   $D(i\omega) + KN(i\omega) = 0$ for real $\omega$ and $K$.
7. **Departure angles.** A branch leaves a complex pole $p$ at the angle that makes the angle condition hold for a
   point just next to $p$: $180°$ plus the sum of angles from the zeros to $p$ minus the sum of angles from the other
   poles to $p$.

## Worked example

Take $G(s) = 1/(s(s + 1)(0.2s + 1)) = 5/(s(s + 1)(s + 5))$. The poles are $0$, $-1$ and $-5$, with no zeros, so
$n - m = 3$.

- **Real axis.** The segments with an odd number of poles to the right are $[-1, 0]$ and $(-\infty, -5]$.
- **Asymptotes.** Centre $\sigma = (0 - 1 - 5)/3 = -2$, angles $60°$, $180°$ and $-60°$.
- **Breakaway.** With $D = s^3 + 6s^2 + 5s$ (using the gain $5K$), $dK/ds = 0$ means $D'(s) = 3s^2 + 12s + 5 = 0$,
  so $s = -0.473$ or $s = -3.528$. Only $-0.473$ lies on a real-axis segment. The poles from $0$ and $-1$ meet there
  at $K = 0.473 \times 0.527 \times 4.527 / 5 = 0.226$, then leave the axis as a complex pair.
- **Crossing.** The characteristic polynomial is $s^3 + 6s^2 + 5s + 5K$. At $s = i\omega$ the real part
  $-6\omega^2 + 5K$ and the imaginary part $-\omega^3 + 5\omega$ must both vanish, so $\omega = \sqrt5 = 2.236$ and
  $K = 6$.

The loop is stable for $0 < K < 6$. For $0.226 < K < 6$ the dominant poles are complex and the step response
overshoots. At $K = 1$ they sit at $-0.387 \pm 0.898i$, and at $K = 10$ at $0.216 \pm 2.780i$, which is unstable.

## Using the locus for design

The locus shows everything a single gain can achieve. If no point on it gives the damping and speed required, no
choice of $K$ will. The designer then adds poles and zeros to the controller to reshape it. A zero pulls branches
towards itself and bends the asymptotes left. Without the zero at $-1$, the third plant in the figure,
$(s + 1)/(s^2(s + 9))$, is unstable for every gain, because a double pole at the origin with a third pole gives
asymptotes at $\pm 60°$ and the branches leave the origin along the imaginary axis. This effect of a zero is how
<NoteLink to="lead-lag-compensation">lead compensation</NoteLink> works. A pole near the origin, as in
<NoteLink to="proportional-integral-derivative-control">integral action</NoteLink>, pulls branches right and slows the
loop.

A second-order pair $-\zeta\omega_n \pm i\omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ has damping ratio $\zeta = \cos\phi$, where
$\phi$ is its angle from the negative real axis. Lines of constant $\zeta$ are rays from the origin, so reading off
where the locus crosses the $\zeta = 0.7$ ray gives the gain for a well-damped response, provided those poles
dominate the others.

## Assumptions

- The gain enters linearly: the closed-loop poles are roots of $D + KN$. Any single real parameter that enters this
  way (a time constant, a pole location) can be studied with the same rules after rewriting the characteristic
  equation in this form.
- $K \ge 0$. The locus for $K < 0$, sometimes called the complementary root locus, uses the angle condition
  $\angle G = k \cdot 360°$ instead.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming the dominant poles tell the whole story.** A closed-loop zero near a dominant pole cancels part of its
  effect, and a zero in the right half-plane makes the step response start in the wrong direction. The locus shows
  poles only.
- **Time delays.** $e^{-s\tau}$ is not rational, and it adds infinitely many branches. Rational approximations such as
  Padé's reproduce the low-frequency behaviour only.
- **Numerical <NoteLink to="root-finding">root-finding</NoteLink> near multiple roots.** Plotting the locus by computing
  roots for a grid of $K$ is inaccurate where branches meet, because multiple roots are ill conditioned. The rules above
  give the exact breakaway points.
