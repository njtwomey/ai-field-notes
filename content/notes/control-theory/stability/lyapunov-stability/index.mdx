<Definition>
Let $\xvec^* = \zeros$ be an equilibrium of $\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(\xvec)$, so $\fvec(\zeros) = \zeros$. The equilibrium
is

- **stable** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $\norm{\xvec(0)} < \delta$ implies
  $\norm{\xvec(t)} < \varepsilon$ for all $t \ge 0$;
- **asymptotically stable** if it is stable and there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $\norm{\xvec(0)} < \delta$ implies
  $\xvec(t) \to \zeros$;
- **exponentially stable** if there are constants $c, \lambda, \delta > 0$ with
  $\norm{\xvec(t)} \le c\,e^{-\lambda t}\norm{\xvec(0)}$ whenever $\norm{\xvec(0)} < \delta$;
- **globally** asymptotically stable if convergence holds from every initial state.

These definitions are due to Lyapunov <Cite id="lyapunov1992" />. Any equilibrium can be moved to the origin by a
change of variables.
</Definition>

## Why the definitions are separate

Stability alone does not mean convergence. An undamped pendulum near its lowest point is stable: a small push gives a
small oscillation that stays small forever, but it never settles. Convergence alone does not mean stability either.
There are systems whose trajectories all return to the origin eventually, but only after an excursion whose size does
not shrink as the initial state approaches the origin. Asymptotic stability asks for both.

For a linear system $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec$ the distinctions collapse. The solution is
$\xvec(t) = e^{\Amat t}\xvec(0)$, a combination of modes $t^k e^{\lambda t}$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of $\Amat$.
The origin is asymptotically stable exactly when every eigenvalue has negative real part (a **Hurwitz** matrix), and
it is then exponentially and globally stable.

## The direct method

Lyapunov's **direct method** proves stability without solving the equation. It generalises the observation that a
mechanical system losing energy must come to rest. Let $V: \reals^n \to \reals$ be continuously differentiable with
$V(\zeros) = 0$ and $V(\xvec) > 0$ for $\xvec \ne \zeros$ (positive definite). Its rate of change along a trajectory
is, by the chain rule,

$$
\dot{V}(\xvec) = \nabla V(\xvec)^\top\dot{\xvec} = \nabla V(\xvec)^\top\fvec(\xvec).
$$

$\dot{V}$ is computed from $\fvec$ alone. No solution is needed.

- If $\dot{V}(\xvec) \le 0$ near the origin, the origin is stable.
- If $\dot{V}(\xvec) < 0$ for $\xvec \ne \zeros$ near the origin, the origin is asymptotically stable.
- If in addition $\dot{V} < 0$ everywhere except the origin and $V(\xvec) \to \infty$ as $\norm{\xvec} \to \infty$
  (**radially unbounded**), the origin is globally asymptotically stable.

A function with these properties is a **Lyapunov function**.

**Why the first statement holds.** Given $\varepsilon$, let $m$ be the minimum of $V$ on the sphere
$\norm{\xvec} = \varepsilon$. It is positive because $V$ is positive away from the origin and the sphere is compact.
Because $V$ is continuous and $V(\zeros) = 0$, there is a $\delta < \varepsilon$ with $V(\xvec) < m$ whenever
$\norm{\xvec} < \delta$. A trajectory starting in that ball has $V(\xvec(0)) < m$, and $V$ never increases along it,
so $V(\xvec(t)) < m$ for all $t$. The trajectory can therefore never reach the sphere where $V \ge m$, and stays inside
$\norm{\xvec} < \varepsilon$.

**Why the second holds.** $V(\xvec(t))$ is non-increasing and bounded below by 0, so it converges to some $c \ge 0$.
If $c > 0$, the trajectory stays in a region $\set{\xvec : c \le V(\xvec), \norm{\xvec} \le \varepsilon}$ that
excludes a neighbourhood of the origin. On that compact region the continuous function $\dot{V}$ has a negative
maximum $-\gamma$, so $V$ would decrease by at least $\gamma t$ and eventually become negative. That is impossible, so
$c = 0$, and $V(\xvec) \to 0$ forces $\xvec \to \zeros$.

The method is sufficient, not necessary in practice: failing to find a Lyapunov function proves nothing. Converse
theorems guarantee that one exists for every asymptotically stable equilibrium, but not how to construct it.

## LaSalle's invariance principle

$\dot{V} \le 0$ often holds with equality on a set larger than the origin. **LaSalle's invariance principle** says
that bounded trajectories then converge to the largest set of trajectories that stay entirely inside
$\set{\xvec : \dot{V}(\xvec) = 0}$ <Cite id="khalil2002" />.

The damped pendulum $\ddot{\theta} = -(g/\ell)\sin\theta - c\dot{\theta}$ with $c > 0$ has energy
$V = \tfrac12\dot{\theta}^2 + (g/\ell)(1 - \cos\theta)$, which is positive definite near $\theta = 0$. Its
derivative is

$$
\dot{V} = \dot{\theta}\ddot{\theta} + (g/\ell)\sin\theta\,\dot{\theta} = -c\,\dot{\theta}^2 \le 0.
$$

$\dot{V} = 0$ whenever $\dot{\theta} = 0$, at any angle, so the direct method gives only stability. But a trajectory
that stays in $\set{\dot{\theta} = 0}$ has $\ddot{\theta} = 0$ too, so $\sin\theta = 0$. Near the bottom this means
$\theta = 0$. By LaSalle the hanging equilibrium is asymptotically stable.

## Linear systems and the Lyapunov equation

For $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec$ try a quadratic function $V = \xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec$ with $\Pmat$ symmetric positive
definite. Then

$$
\dot{V} = \dot{\xvec}^\top\Pmat\xvec + \xvec^\top\Pmat\dot{\xvec} = \xvec^\top(\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat)\xvec.
$$

Asking for $\dot{V} = -\xvec^\top\Qmat\xvec$ with a chosen $\Qmat \succ 0$ gives the linear matrix equation

$$
\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat = -\Qmat,
$$

the **Lyapunov equation**. The following are equivalent: (a) $\Amat$ is Hurwitz; (b) for every symmetric
$\Qmat \succ 0$ the Lyapunov equation has a unique symmetric solution $\Pmat \succ 0$.

**(a) implies (b).** Define

$$
\Pmat = \int_0^\infty e^{\Amat^\top t}\Qmat e^{\Amat t}\,dt.
$$

The integral converges because every entry of $e^{\Amat t}$ decays exponentially when $\Amat$ is Hurwitz. $\Pmat$ is
symmetric, and $\xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec = \int_0^\infty \norm{\Qmat^{1/2}e^{\Amat t}\xvec}^2\,dt > 0$ for
$\xvec \ne \zeros$ because $e^{\Amat t}$ is invertible. Substituting,

$$
\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat = \int_0^\infty \frac{d}{dt}\paren{e^{\Amat^\top t}\Qmat e^{\Amat t}}dt
= \brack{e^{\Amat^\top t}\Qmat e^{\Amat t}}_0^\infty = \zeros - \Qmat.
$$

For uniqueness, suppose $\Pmat_1$ and $\Pmat_2$ both solve it. Their difference $\Delta$ satisfies
$\Amat^\top\Delta + \Delta\Amat = \zeros$, so $\frac{d}{dt}(e^{\Amat^\top t}\Delta e^{\Amat t}) = \zeros$. The
product is constant, equal to $\Delta$ at $t = 0$ and to $\zeros$ as $t \to \infty$. So $\Delta = \zeros$.

**(b) implies (a).** Let $\Amat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$ with $\vvec \ne \zeros$, possibly complex. Multiply the Lyapunov
equation by $\vvec^*$ on the left and $\vvec$ on the right:

$$
\bar{\lambda}\,\vvec^*\Pmat\vvec + \lambda\,\vvec^*\Pmat\vvec = -\vvec^*\Qmat\vvec
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = -\frac{\vvec^*\Qmat\vvec}{\vvec^*\Pmat\vvec} < 0.
$$

Every eigenvalue has negative real part.

The equation is linear in the $n(n+1)/2$ unknown entries of $\Pmat$ and is solved directly by the Bartels–Stewart
algorithm. The discrete-time counterpart, for $\xvec_{k+1} = \Amat\xvec_k$, asks $V$ to decrease from step to step:
$\Amat^\top\Pmat\Amat - \Pmat = -\Qmat$, with solution $\Pmat = \sum_{k \ge 0} (\Amat^\top)^k\Qmat\Amat^k$ when every
eigenvalue of $\Amat$ lies inside the unit circle.

## Worked example

Take $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $-1$ and $-2$) and $\Qmat = \eye$. With
$\Pmat = \begin{bmatrix} p_1 & p_2 \\ p_2 & p_3 \end{bmatrix}$,

$$
\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} -4p_2 & p_1 - 3p_2 - 2p_3 \\ p_1 - 3p_2 - 2p_3 & 2p_2 - 6p_3 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The diagonal gives $p_2 = 1/4$ and $p_3 = 1/4$. The off-diagonal gives $p_1 = 3/4 + 1/2 = 5/4$. So

$$
\Pmat = \frac14\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

whose leading principal minors $5/4$ and $\det\Pmat = 1/4$ are positive. $\Pmat$ is positive definite, with
eigenvalues $0.191$ and $1.309$, confirming that $\Amat$ is Hurwitz. Along every trajectory
$\dot{V} = -\norm{\xvec}^2 \le -V / 1.309$, so $V$ decays at least as fast as $e^{-0.76t}$.

## Assumptions

- $V$ is continuously differentiable and positive definite in a neighbourhood of the equilibrium. For global
  conclusions it must also be radially unbounded.
- The system is autonomous (no explicit time dependence). Time-varying systems need uniform bounds on $V$ and
  $\dot{V}$, and LaSalle's principle does not apply as stated; Barbalat's lemma replaces it.

## Pitfalls

- **Positive definite without radial unboundedness.** $V = x_1^2/(1 + x_1^2) + x_2^2$ is positive definite, but its
  level sets are unbounded for $V \ge 1$. A trajectory can escape to infinity while $V$ decreases, so $\dot{V} < 0$
  does not prove global stability with such a $V$.
- **Reading a failed search as instability.** A candidate $V$ with $\dot{V}$ positive somewhere proves nothing about
  the system; another $V$ may work. Instability needs its own theorem, such as Chetaev's.
- **Stable is not asymptotically stable.** $\dot{V} \le 0$ allows trajectories to circle forever at a constant level
  of $V$. Convergence needs $\dot{V} < 0$ or an invariance argument.
