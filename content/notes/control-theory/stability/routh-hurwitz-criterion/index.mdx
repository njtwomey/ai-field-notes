<Definition>
A real polynomial $p(s) = a_0 s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$ with $a_0 > 0$ is **Hurwitz** if all its roots have
negative real part. The **Routh–Hurwitz criterion** builds a triangular table, the **Routh array**, from the
coefficients. If no entry of its first column is zero, the number of sign changes down that column equals the number
of roots of $p$ with positive real part <Cite id="routh1877" />. $p$ is Hurwitz exactly when every entry of the first
column is positive.
</Definition>

## Stability and design parameters

A linear system is stable when the roots of its characteristic polynomial, the closed-loop poles, lie in the open left
half-plane. In a feedback loop those roots depend on a gain or other design parameter. Routh's test answers the
stability question with a few multiplications, and because it works with the coefficients symbolically it gives the
exact range of a parameter for which the loop is stable.

Routh published the array in 1877 <Cite id="routh1877" />. Hurwitz found an equivalent condition in terms of
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinants</NoteLink> in 1895 <Cite id="hurwitz1895" />.

## A necessary condition

Every coefficient of a Hurwitz polynomial is positive. A real polynomial factors into real linear factors
$(s + \alpha)$ for its real roots $-\alpha$ and quadratic factors $s^2 + \beta s + \gamma$ for its complex pairs
$-\sigma \pm i\omega$, with $\beta = 2\sigma$ and $\gamma = \sigma^2 + \omega^2$. If every root has negative real part,
then $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are all positive, and a product of polynomials with positive coefficients has
positive coefficients. A zero or negative coefficient therefore proves instability at once. For degree 1 and 2 the
condition is also sufficient. From degree 3 on it is not: $s^3 + s^2 + s + 6$ has positive coefficients and a pair of
roots at $0.5 \pm 1.66i$.

## The Routh array

Write the coefficients in two rows, alternating:

$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
s^n & a_0 & a_2 & a_4 & \cdots \\
s^{n-1} & a_1 & a_3 & a_5 & \cdots \\
s^{n-2} & b_1 & b_2 & b_3 & \cdots \\
s^{n-3} & c_1 & c_2 & \cdots & \\
\vdots & \vdots & & & \\
s^0 & \ast & & &
\end{array}
$$

Each new row is computed from the two rows above it, with missing entries taken as zero:

$$
b_j = \frac{a_1 a_{2j} - a_0 a_{2j+1}}{a_1}, \qquad c_j = \frac{b_1 a_{2j+1} - a_1 b_{j+1}}{b_1},
$$

and so on: the entry in column $j$ is minus the determinant of the $2 \times 2$ matrix formed by the first column and
column $j + 1$ of the two rows above, divided by the first entry of the row directly above. The array has $n + 1$
rows. The criterion counts the sign changes in the first column $a_0, a_1, b_1, c_1, \dots$.

## Why the count works

Split $p$ into two parts: $p_1(s) = a_0 s^n + a_2 s^{n-2} + \dots$ holds every second term starting with the leading
one, and $p_2(s) = a_1 s^{n-1} + a_3 s^{n-3} + \dots$ holds the rest. One part is even in $s$ and the other is odd. The
first two rows of the array are their coefficients. The third row is the coefficients of $p_1(s) - \mu s\,p_2(s)$ with
$\mu = a_0/a_1$, which cancels the leading term: its $s^{n-2}$ coefficient is $a_2 - a_0 a_3/a_1 = b_1$. So rows 2 and
3 together are the coefficients of a polynomial of degree $n - 1$:

$$
q(s) = p_2(s) + p_1(s) - \mu s\,p_2(s).
$$

Every later row applies the same step to $q$. The criterion follows by induction from one claim: if $p$ has no roots
on the imaginary axis, then $p$ and $q$ have the same number of right half-plane roots when $\mu > 0$, and $p$ has one
more when $\mu < 0$.

To prove the claim, deform $p$ into $q$ continuously:

$$
f_t(s) = p_2(s) + p_1(s) - t\mu s\,p_2(s), \qquad 0 \le t \le 1.
$$

The roots of $f_t$ move continuously with $t$, so a root can change half-plane only by crossing the imaginary axis.
On the imaginary axis an even polynomial is real and an odd polynomial is imaginary. $p_1$ and $s\,p_2$ have the same
parity, opposite to that of $p_2$. So $f_t(i\omega)$ splits into two parts along perpendicular directions,

$$
f_t(i\omega) = \underbrace{\paren{p_1(i\omega) - t\mu\,i\omega\,p_2(i\omega)}}_{\text{parity of } p_1}
+ \underbrace{p_2(i\omega)}_{\text{parity of } p_2},
$$

and $f_t(i\omega) = 0$ needs both to vanish. If $p_2(i\omega) = 0$, the first part reduces to $p_1(i\omega)$, and both
vanishing would make $i\omega$ a root of $p = p_1 + p_2$. So no root crosses the axis for $0 \le t < 1$.

The degree drops at $t = 1$. The two leading terms of $f_t$ are $(1 - t)a_0 s^n + a_1 s^{n-1}$, so as $t \to 1$ one
root escapes to infinity, approximately at $s = -a_1/((1 - t)a_0) = -1/((1 - t)\mu)$. When $\mu > 0$ it escapes
through the left half-plane, and $q$ keeps every right half-plane root of $p$. When $\mu < 0$ it escapes through the
right half-plane, taking one right half-plane root with it. A sign change between $a_0$ and $a_1$ is exactly
$\mu < 0$, so each sign change down the first column accounts for one right half-plane root.

## Worked example: a stable range of gain

A loop with open-loop transfer function $L(s) = K / (s(s + 1)(0.2s + 1))$ and unity negative feedback has
characteristic equation $1 + L = 0$, that is $0.2s^3 + 1.2s^2 + s + K = 0$. Multiply by 5 to get
$s^3 + 6s^2 + 5s + 5K$. The array is

$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
s^3 & 1 & 5 \\
s^2 & 6 & 5K \\
s^1 & (30 - 5K)/6 & 0 \\
s^0 & 5K &
\end{array}
$$

The first column is positive exactly when $30 - 5K > 0$ and $5K > 0$, so the loop is stable for $0 < K < 6$. At
$K = 6$ the $s^1$ row is zero. The row above it then defines the **auxiliary polynomial** $6s^2 + 30$, whose roots
$\pm i\sqrt5 = \pm 2.236i$ are the closed-loop poles on the imaginary axis. The loop oscillates at $2.236$ rad/s. The
same frequency appears as the phase crossover in the
<NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins">Bode plot</NoteLink> of this loop, where the gain margin is
$6$, or $15.6$ dB, at $K = 1$.

A second example counts unstable roots. For $s^4 + 2s^3 + 3s^2 + 4s + 5$ the rows are $(1, 3, 5)$, $(2, 4)$,
$(1, 5)$, $(-6)$ and $(5)$. The first column $1, 2, 1, -6, 5$ changes sign twice, so there are two right half-plane
roots. They are $0.288 \pm 1.416i$.

## Special cases

- **A zero first entry in a nonzero row.** Replace the zero by a small $\epsilon > 0$ and continue. The count of sign
  changes as $\epsilon \to 0^+$ is the number of right half-plane roots. A zero in the first column already shows the
  polynomial is not Hurwitz.
- **A whole row of zeros.** The polynomial has roots placed symmetrically about the origin, such as a pair
  $\pm i\omega$ or $\pm\alpha$. Replace the zero row with the coefficients of the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the auxiliary
  polynomial formed from the row above, and continue. The auxiliary polynomial's roots are those symmetric roots.

## Hurwitz determinants

Hurwitz's equivalent form uses the $n \times n$ matrix whose first row is $(a_1, a_3, a_5, \dots)$, second row
$(a_0, a_2, a_4, \dots)$, third row $(0, a_1, a_3, \dots)$, and so on, each pair of rows shifted one place right. $p$
is Hurwitz exactly when all $n$ leading principal minors $\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_n$ are positive
<Cite id="hurwitz1895" />. The first entries of the Routh array are ratios of consecutive minors,
$a_1 = \Delta_1$, $b_1 = \Delta_2 / \Delta_1$, $c_1 = \Delta_3 / \Delta_2$, which is why the two tests agree. For a
cubic $a_0 s^3 + a_1 s^2 + a_2 s + a_3$ both reduce to: all coefficients positive and $a_1 a_2 > a_0 a_3$.

## Assumptions

- The polynomial has real coefficients. Complex coefficients need a modified test.
- The test is for continuous-time stability (left half-plane). Discrete-time stability (unit disc) needs the Jury test,
  or the bilinear map $z = (1 + w)/(1 - w)$, which sends the unit disc to the left half-plane, followed by Routh.

## Pitfalls


- **Stability is not performance.** The test says nothing about how close the roots are to the imaginary axis. Shifting
  $s \to s - \sigma$ and testing again checks that every root has real part below $-\sigma$, a minimum decay rate.
- **The characteristic polynomial must be the right one.** Pole-zero cancellations between plant and controller remove
  roots from the transfer function but not from the system, so test $\det(s\eye - \Amat)$ of the full closed loop.
