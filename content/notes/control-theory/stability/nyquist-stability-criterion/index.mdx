<Definition>
Let $L(s)$ be the <NoteLink to="transfer-function-and-block-diagrams">loop transfer function</NoteLink> of a
negative-feedback loop, with $P$ poles in the open right half-plane. The **Nyquist plot** is the image of the imaginary
axis $s = i\omega$, $-\infty < \omega < \infty$, under $L$, closed at infinity. If it does not pass through $-1$ and
encircles $-1$ clockwise $N$ times, the closed loop has

$$
Z = N + P
$$

poles in the right half-plane <Cite id="nyquist1932" />. The closed loop is stable exactly when $N = -P$: the plot
encircles $-1$ anticlockwise once for each unstable open-loop pole, and not at all when the open loop is stable.
</Definition>

## Why it is useful

The closed-loop poles are the zeros of $1 + L(s)$. Computing them needs a model of $L$ as a ratio of polynomials. The
Nyquist criterion needs only $L(i\omega)$, the open-loop <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency
response</NoteLink>, which can be measured by driving the open loop with sinusoids. It handles time delays, which have
no finite set of poles, and it shows how far the loop is from instability, not just whether it is stable. Nyquist
derived it in 1932 for feedback amplifiers <Cite id="nyquist1932" />.

## The argument principle

Let $F(s)$ be rational and let $\Gamma$ be a simple closed curve in the complex plane, traversed clockwise, that passes
through no zero or pole of $F$. Write

$$
F(s) = c\,\frac{\prod_i (s - z_i)}{\prod_j (s - p_j)}, \qquad
\angle F(s) = \angle c + \sum_i \angle(s - z_i) - \sum_j \angle(s - p_j).
$$

As $s$ travels once around $\Gamma$, follow the angle of each factor $s - a$, the direction of the vector from $a$ to
$s$.

- If $a$ is **inside** $\Gamma$, the vector sweeps a full turn with $s$: its angle changes by $-360°$ (clockwise).
- If $a$ is **outside**, the vector swings back and forth and returns to its starting direction: the net change is
  $0°$.

Summing, the angle of $F$ changes by $-360° \times (Z_\Gamma - P_\Gamma)$, where $Z_\Gamma$ and $P_\Gamma$ count the
zeros and poles of $F$ inside $\Gamma$. A net angle change of $-360°$ is one clockwise turn of $F(s)$ around the
origin. So the image curve $F(\Gamma)$ encircles the origin clockwise

$$
N = Z_\Gamma - P_\Gamma
$$

times. This is the **argument principle**.

## From the argument principle to the criterion

Choose $F = 1 + L$. Its zeros are the closed-loop poles. Its poles are the poles of $L$, since adding 1 changes the
numerator only. Choose $\Gamma$ to enclose the whole right half-plane: go up the imaginary axis from $-iR$ to $iR$,
then back along the semicircle of radius $R$ through the right half-plane, and let $R \to \infty$. This is the
**Nyquist contour**, and it is clockwise. With it, $Z_\Gamma$ is the number $Z$ of unstable closed-loop poles and
$P_\Gamma$ the number $P$ of unstable open-loop poles.

Two simplifications make the image easy to draw.

- **The semicircle maps to a point.** If $L$ is strictly proper, $L(s) \to 0$ as $\abs{s} \to \infty$, so the whole
  semicircle maps to $F = 1$. The image of $\Gamma$ is the image of the imaginary axis.
- **Shift by one.** $F(s)$ encircling the origin is the same as $L(s) = F(s) - 1$ encircling $-1$.

So the number of clockwise encirclements of $-1$ by $L(i\omega)$, $\omega$ from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, is
$N = Z - P$, which is the criterion. For a real-coefficient $L$, $L(-i\omega)$ is the complex conjugate of $L(i\omega)$,
so the negative-frequency half is the mirror image of the positive half in the real axis.

If $L$ has poles on the imaginary axis, for example an integrator $1/s$, the contour detours around each of them
along a small semicircle into the right half-plane. That small detour maps to a large arc, which closes the plot at
infinity. For $1/s$ the arc turns $180°$ clockwise at a large radius as $\omega$ passes through 0.

## Worked examples

**A stable open loop.** $L(s) = K/(s + 1)^3$ has $P = 0$. Its phase is $-3\arctan\omega$, which reaches $-180°$ at
$\omega = \tan 60° = \sqrt3$. There $\abs{L} = K/(1 + 3)^{3/2} = K/8$, so the plot crosses the negative real axis at
$-K/8$. For $K < 8$ the crossing lies to the right of $-1$ and $N = 0$: the loop is stable. For $K > 8$ the crossing
lies to the left, the positive- and negative-frequency halves each pass around $-1$, and $N = 2$: two closed-loop poles
are unstable. The same answer comes from the <NoteLink to="routh-hurwitz-criterion">Routh array</NoteLink> of
$(s + 1)^3 + K = s^3 + 3s^2 + 3s + 1 + K$, which needs $3 \times 3 > 1 + K$.

**An unstable open loop.** $L(s) = K/((s - 1)(s + 2))$ has $P = 1$. At $\omega = 0$, $L = -K/2$, and as
$\omega \to \infty$, $L \to 0$. In between the phase is $-180° + \arctan\omega - \arctan(\omega/2)$, which never rises
above $-160.5°$ (reached at $\omega = \sqrt2$). The positive-frequency half is a small loop from $-K/2$ to the origin
just below the real axis, and the negative-frequency half mirrors it above. When $K > 2$ the plot passes around $-1$
once anticlockwise, $N = -1$, and $Z = N + P = 0$: feedback stabilises the loop. When $K < 2$ the point $-1$ lies
outside the curve, $N = 0$ and $Z = 1$. The characteristic polynomial $s^2 + s + K - 2$ confirms the threshold $K = 2$.

## Distance to $-1$ as robustness

A loop that is stable with its Nyquist plot passing close to $-1$ is fragile: a small change in gain or phase makes the
curve cross $-1$ and changes $N$. Three measures of that distance are standard.

- The **gain margin** is $1/\abs{L(i\omega_{180})}$, where $\omega_{180}$ is the frequency at which the plot crosses
  the negative real axis. It is the factor by which the gain can grow before the crossing reaches $-1$.
- The **phase margin** is $180° + \angle L(i\omega_c)$, where $\abs{L(i\omega_c)} = 1$. It is the extra
  <NoteLink to="lead-lag-compensation">phase lag</NoteLink> that would rotate the unit-circle crossing onto $-1$.
- The **stability margin** $s_m = \min_\omega \abs{1 + L(i\omega)}$ is the shortest distance from $-1$ to the curve.
  Since $\abs{1 + L} = 1/\abs{S}$, $s_m = 1/\max_\omega\abs{S(i\omega)}$. A small peak of the sensitivity function
  means a large distance to $-1$.

The first two are read more easily from the
<NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins">Bode plot</NoteLink>. The third links the Nyquist picture to the
sensitivity function and to <NoteLink to="h-infinity-control">robust control</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- $L$ is proper and has no pole-zero cancellations in the closed right half-plane between plant and controller. A
  cancelled unstable pole is invisible in $L$ but still present in the loop.
- $P$ must be known. The frequency response alone does not reveal how many open-loop poles are unstable.

## Pitfalls

- **Counting only positive frequencies.** The encirclement count uses the whole curve, $\omega$ from $-\infty$ to
  $\infty$, plus any arcs at infinity from imaginary-axis poles. Drawing half the curve and counting crossings is a
  common source of sign errors.
- **Reading gain and phase margins as the whole story.** A curve can have large gain and phase margins and still pass
  close to $-1$ between the two crossing frequencies. The stability margin $s_m$ catches this; the other two do not.
- **Plotting on a linear scale.** Large and small values of $\abs{L}$ both matter. Near $-1$ a linear scale is fine,
  but encirclements at low frequency, where $\abs{L}$ is large, can fall off the chart.
