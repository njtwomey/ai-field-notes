<Definition>
For the problem of minimising $\phi(\xvec(T)) + \int_t^T \ell(\xvec, \uvec)\,d\tau$ subject to
$\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(\xvec, \uvec)$ and $\uvec \in \Ucal$, the **value function** $V(\xvec, t)$ is the minimal cost
starting from state $\xvec$ at time $t$. Where it is differentiable it satisfies the **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman
equation**

$$
-\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}(\xvec, t) = \min_{\uvec \in \Ucal}\brack{\ell(\xvec, \uvec) + \nabla_{\xvec}V(\xvec, t)^\top\fvec(\xvec, \uvec)},
\qquad V(\xvec, T) = \phi(\xvec).
$$

The optimal control is the feedback law $\uvec^*(\xvec, t)$ that attains the minimum. For an infinite horizon with
time-invariant data, $V$ does not depend on $t$ and the left side is zero.
</Definition>

## Derivation from the principle of optimality

Bellman's **principle of optimality** says that the tail of an optimal trajectory is optimal for the problem that
starts where the tail starts <Cite id="bellman1957" />. For a short interval $[t, t + \Delta t]$ with a constant input
$\uvec$, the cost splits into the running cost over the interval and the optimal cost from wherever the state ends
up:

$$
V(\xvec, t) = \min_{\uvec \in \Ucal}\brack{\ell(\xvec, \uvec)\,\Delta t + V\paren{\xvec + \fvec(\xvec, \uvec)\Delta t,\; t + \Delta t}} + o(\Delta t).
$$

Expand the second term to first order, assuming $V$ is differentiable:

$$
V(\xvec + \fvec\Delta t, t + \Delta t) = V(\xvec, t) + \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\Delta t + \nabla_{\xvec}V^\top\fvec\,\Delta t + o(\Delta t).
$$

$V(\xvec, t)$ appears on both sides and cancels. Dividing by $\Delta t$ and letting $\Delta t \to 0$ gives the HJB
equation. The discrete-time version, $V_k(\xvec) = \min_{\uvec}[\ell(\xvec, \uvec) + V_{k+1}(\fvec(\xvec, \uvec))]$, is
the Bellman equation of dynamic programming, and in the stochastic setting of Markov decision processes it is the
<NoteLink to="bellman-equations">Bellman optimality equation</NoteLink> of reinforcement learning <Cite id="bertsekas2017" />.

The expression being minimised is the Hamiltonian $H(\xvec, \uvec, \lambdavec) = \ell + \lambdavec^\top\fvec$ with
$\lambdavec = \nabla_{\xvec}V$. So HJB reads $-\partial_t V = \min_{\uvec}H(\xvec, \uvec, \nabla V)$, and the
costate of <NoteLink to="pontryagin-maximum-principle">Pontryagin's principle</NoteLink> is the gradient of the value
function along the optimal trajectory.

## Sufficiency: the verification theorem

Pontryagin's conditions are necessary. HJB also gives a sufficient condition. Suppose a continuously differentiable
$W(\xvec, t)$ satisfies the HJB equation and its terminal condition. Take any admissible input and its trajectory.
Along it,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}W(\xvec(t), t) = \frac{\partial W}{\partial t} + \nabla W^\top\fvec(\xvec, \uvec) \ge -\ell(\xvec, \uvec),
$$

because HJB says $\partial_t W + \nabla W^\top\fvec + \ell \ge 0$ for every $\uvec$, with equality at the minimiser.
Integrating from $0$ to $T$, $\phi(\xvec(T)) - W(\xvec_0, 0) \ge -\int_0^T\ell\,dt$, which rearranges to
$J \ge W(\xvec_0, 0)$. The feedback law that attains the minimum achieves equality. So $W$ is the value function and
that law is optimal from every initial state. This is the **verification theorem**.

## The LQR from HJB

Take $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec$, $\ell = \xvec^\top\Qmat\xvec + \uvec^\top\Rmat\uvec$ and an infinite
horizon. Try $V(\xvec) = \xvec^\top\Pmat\xvec$ with $\Pmat$ symmetric, so $\nabla V = 2\Pmat\xvec$. HJB becomes

$$
0 = \min_{\uvec}\brack{\xvec^\top\Qmat\xvec + \uvec^\top\Rmat\uvec + 2\xvec^\top\Pmat(\Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec)}.
$$

Setting the gradient in $\uvec$ to zero, $2\Rmat\uvec + 2\Bmat^\top\Pmat\xvec = \zeros$, gives
$\uvec^* = -\Rmat^{-1}\Bmat^\top\Pmat\xvec$. Substituting back, and writing $2\xvec^\top\Pmat\Amat\xvec =
\xvec^\top(\Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat)\xvec$,

$$
0 = \xvec^\top\paren{\Qmat + \Amat^\top\Pmat + \Pmat\Amat - \Pmat\Bmat\Rmat^{-1}\Bmat^\top\Pmat}\xvec
\quad\text{for all } \xvec.
$$

The symmetric matrix in brackets must vanish: this is the continuous algebraic Riccati equation of the
<NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator">linear-quadratic regulator</NoteLink>. The quadratic guess works because the
Hamiltonian stays quadratic, which is special to linear dynamics with quadratic cost.

## Worked examples

**A scalar regulator.** For $\dot{x} = x + u$ with $\ell = x^2 + u^2$, try $V = px^2$. HJB is
$0 = \min_u[x^2 + u^2 + 2px(x + u)]$. The minimiser is $u = -px$, and substituting gives $0 = x^2(1 + 2p - p^2)$, so
$p = 1 + \sqrt2 = 2.414$ (the positive root, since $V \ge 0$). The optimal feedback is $u = -2.414x$ and the closed
loop is $\dot{x} = -\sqrt2\,x$: the unstable open-loop pole at $+1$ is reflected to $-1$ and pushed further left by
the state weight.

**A value function with a kink.** For time-optimal control of the double integrator $\dot{x}_1 = x_2$,
$\dot{x}_2 = u$, $\abs{u} \le 1$ (worked out in the note on Pontryagin's principle), the minimum time to reach the
origin from states to the right of the switching curve $x_1 = -\tfrac12 x_2\abs{x_2}$ is
$V = x_2 + 2\sqrt{c}$ with $c = x_1 + \tfrac12 x_2^2$. HJB for this problem is
$0 = \min_{\abs{u} \le 1}[1 + V_{x_1}x_2 + V_{x_2}u] = 1 + V_{x_1}x_2 - \abs{V_{x_2}}$. Here $V_{x_1} = 1/\sqrt{c}$ and
$V_{x_2} = 1 + x_2/\sqrt{c}$, which is positive in this region because $\sqrt{c} > -x_2$ there. Substituting,
$1 + x_2/\sqrt{c} - 1 - x_2/\sqrt{c} = 0$. The equation holds, and the minimiser $u = -\sgn V_{x_2} = -1$ is the first
arc of the bang-bang solution. On the switching curve the gradient of $V$ jumps, so $V$ is not differentiable there.

## Non-smooth value functions

The kink in the second example is typical. Value functions of problems with bounded inputs, obstacles or
non-convex costs are continuous but not differentiable everywhere, and the HJB equation has no classical solution.
The right notion is the **viscosity solution** of Crandall and Lions <Cite id="crandall1983" />. It replaces the
derivative at a kink by inequalities on smooth functions that touch $V$ from above or below. The value function is the
unique viscosity solution under mild conditions, which makes HJB a complete characterisation of optimality.

## Assumptions

- The dynamics and running cost are known and deterministic. With additive Brownian noise, the equation gains a
  second-order term $\tfrac12\trace(\Sigmamat\nabla^2V)$, where $\Sigmamat$ is the noise covariance per unit time.
- The minimum over $\Ucal$ exists at each $(\xvec, t)$.

## Pitfalls

- **The curse of dimensionality.** HJB is a partial differential equation on the whole state space. Grid-based
  solvers need a number of points exponential in the state dimension $n$, and are impractical beyond about five or six
  states. This is the reason for Pontryagin-based trajectory methods,
  <NoteLink to="model-predictive-control">model predictive control</NoteLink> and approximate dynamic programming.
- **Assuming smoothness.** The derivation above assumes a differentiable $V$. Solving for a classical solution where
  none exists gives wrong feedback laws near the kinks, which are often exactly where the control switches.
