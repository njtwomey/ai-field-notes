<Definition>
**Subspace identification** fits a linear state-space model

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = \Amat\xvec_k + \Bmat\uvec_k + \wvec_k, \qquad \yvec_k = \Cmat\xvec_k + \Dmat\uvec_k + \vvec_k
$$

to measured inputs $\uvec_k$ and outputs $\yvec_k$ using projections and a singular value decomposition, with no
iterative optimisation. The number of significant singular values estimates the state dimension $n$. N4SID is the
best-known algorithm of the family <Cite id="vanoverschee1994" />.
</Definition>

## Why state space directly

<NoteLink to="arx-and-prediction-error-methods">Prediction-error methods</NoteLink> for multi-input multi-output
systems need a parametrisation of the model, a choice of orders for many polynomials, and a nonconvex optimisation
with local minima. Subspace methods avoid all three. They need only the choice of $n$, which the data suggest, and
they are built from numerically reliable linear algebra. The state basis they return is arbitrary, which does not
matter because every basis gives the same input-output behaviour.

## The idea without noise: realisation from the impulse response

The **Markov parameters** of a system are its impulse response, $\Gmat_k = \Cmat\Amat^{k-1}\Bmat$ for $k \ge 1$.
Arrange them in a block **Hankel matrix**, constant along anti-diagonals:

$$
\Hmat = \begin{bmatrix} \Gmat_1 & \Gmat_2 & \cdots & \Gmat_j \\ \Gmat_2 & \Gmat_3 & \cdots & \Gmat_{j+1} \\
\vdots & & & \vdots \\ \Gmat_i & \Gmat_{i+1} & \cdots & \Gmat_{i+j-1} \end{bmatrix}
= \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \Cmat \\ \Cmat\Amat \\ \vdots \\ \Cmat\Amat^{i-1} \end{bmatrix}}_{\Ocal_i}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \Bmat & \Amat\Bmat & \cdots & \Amat^{j-1}\Bmat \end{bmatrix}}_{\Ccal_j}.
$$

The factorisation holds entry by entry: block $(r, c)$ is $\Cmat\Amat^{r-1}\cdot\Amat^{c-1}\Bmat =
\Gmat_{r+c-1}$. $\Ocal_i$ is the extended observability matrix and $\Ccal_j$ the extended controllability matrix. For a
minimal system both have rank $n$ once $i, j \ge n$, so $\rank\Hmat = n$. This is the **Ho–Kalman** realisation idea.

An SVD $\Hmat = \Pmat_n\Sigmamat_n\Qmat_n^\top$ (keeping the $n$ nonzero singular values) gives a factorisation
$\Ocal_i = \Pmat_n\Sigmamat_n^{1/2}$ and $\Ccal_j = \Sigmamat_n^{1/2}\Qmat_n^\top$, correct up to an invertible change
of state basis. The model follows from the structure of these factors.

- $\Cmat$ is the first block row of $\Ocal_i$, and $\Bmat$ the first block column of $\Ccal_j$.
- $\Amat$ comes from **shift invariance**. Deleting the last block row of $\Ocal_i$ gives $\Ocal_{i-1}$; deleting the
  first gives $\Ocal_{i-1}\Amat$, whose block rows are $\Cmat\Amat, \dots, \Cmat\Amat^{i-1}$. Both are
  computable from the estimated $\Ocal_i$, and $\Amat$ solves the overdetermined linear system between them by least
  squares.

## With inputs and noise

A measured impulse response is rarely available. Subspace methods work with block Hankel matrices built from the data
itself. Stack $i$ consecutive outputs into columns and split the data into "past" and "future" halves. Iterating the
state equation $i$ times gives the **data equation** for the future outputs $\Ymat_f$:

$$
\Ymat_f = \Ocal_i\Xmat_f + \Tmat_i\Umat_f + \text{noise},
$$

where $\Xmat_f$ holds the states at the start of each window, $\Umat_f$ the future inputs, and $\Tmat_i$ is a block
lower-triangular Toeplitz matrix of Markov parameters. The term $\Ocal_i\Xmat_f$ has rank $n$. The algorithm isolates
it in two steps.

1. **Remove the future inputs.** Project $\Ymat_f$ onto the orthogonal complement of the row space of $\Umat_f$, which
   annihilates $\Tmat_i\Umat_f$.
2. **Remove the noise.** Project onto the row space of the past inputs and outputs, which acts as an instrument: it
   is correlated with the state $\Xmat_f$ (the state summarises the past) but, for white noise, uncorrelated with the
   future noise.

N4SID combines the two into one **oblique projection** <Cite id="vanoverschee1994" />. The result is approximately
$\Ocal_i\hat{\Xmat}_f$, where $\hat{\Xmat}_f$ is a sequence of Kalman-filter state estimates. Its SVD reveals $n$ as the
number of singular values that stand clearly above the rest, and gives $\Ocal_i$ or the state sequence. From either,
$\Amat$, $\Bmat$, $\Cmat$, $\Dmat$ follow by least squares, and the residuals give the noise covariances and hence a
Kalman gain. Variants (MOESP, CVA) differ in the weightings applied before the SVD.

## Worked example

A system with two states, $\Amat = \diag(0.9, 0.5)$, $\Bmat = (1, 1)$ and $\Cmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$,
has Markov parameters $h_k = 0.9^{k-1} + 0.5^{k-1}$: $2$, $1.4$, $1.06$, $0.854$, $0.7186$. The $3 \times 3$ Hankel
matrix is

$$
\Hmat = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1.4 & 1.06 \\ 1.4 & 1.06 & 0.854 \\ 1.06 & 0.854 & 0.7186 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with singular values $3.64$, $0.139$ and $0$ (to machine precision). Two nonzero singular values give $n = 2$. Taking
$\Ocal_3 = \Pmat_2\Sigmamat_2^{1/2}$ and solving the shift equation for $\Amat$ gives a $2 \times 2$ matrix with
eigenvalues $0.9$ and $0.5$, the true poles, in a different state basis. With noisy data the third singular value is
small but not zero, and choosing $n$ means finding the gap.

## Assumptions

- The system is linear and time-invariant, and the noise is stationary and white in the innovation form.
- The input is persistently exciting and, for the basic algorithms, uncorrelated with the noise. Closed-loop data
  violate this and need dedicated variants.

## Pitfalls

- **No clear gap.** Real data often show singular values that decay gradually. The order must then be chosen by
  validating models of several orders on held-out data.
- **Not optimal.** Subspace estimates are consistent but not efficient: they do not minimise a likelihood. A common
  workflow uses them to initialise a prediction-error fit.
- **Window length.** The number of block rows $i$ must exceed the expected order. Larger $i$ averages more noise away
  but uses fewer independent columns.
