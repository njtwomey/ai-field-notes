<Definition>
**Pole placement** designs a state-feedback law $\uvec = -\Kmat\xvec$ for $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec$ so
that the closed-loop matrix $\Amat - \Bmat\Kmat$ has a prescribed set of eigenvalues, the closed-loop poles. The set
must be closed under complex conjugation. Every such set can be assigned exactly when $(\Amat, \Bmat)$ is
controllable. For a single input the gain is unique and given by **Ackermann's formula**
<Cite id="ackermann1972" />:

$$
\Kmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\Ccal^{-1}\alpha(\Amat),
$$

where $\Ccal = \begin{bmatrix} \Bmat & \Amat\Bmat & \cdots & \Amat^{n-1}\Bmat \end{bmatrix}$ and
$\alpha(s) = s^n + \alpha_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + \alpha_n$ is the desired characteristic polynomial.
</Definition>

## Problem

The eigenvalues of $\Amat$ fix how the uncontrolled system behaves: how fast it decays or diverges and how much it
oscillates. With the whole state measured, a linear controller can feed back every state variable, not only the
output. The question is which closed-loop behaviours such a controller can produce, and how to compute the gain. A
<NoteLink to="pid-control">PID controller</NoteLink> or a single gain on the <NoteLink to="root-locus">root
locus</NoteLink> moves the poles only along restricted paths. State feedback has $n$ free gains per input, one per
pole.

## Mechanism

### In controllable canonical form

Suppose the single-input system is in companion form, with characteristic polynomial
$\chi(s) = s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$:

$$
\Amat_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & 0 & 1 \\ -a_n & \cdots & -a_2 & -a_1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad \Bmat_c = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

With $\Kmat_c = \begin{bmatrix} k_n & \cdots & k_1 \end{bmatrix}$, the product $\Bmat_c\Kmat_c$ is zero except in the
last row, so $\Amat_c - \Bmat_c\Kmat_c$ is again a companion matrix, with last row
$-(a_n + k_n), \dots, -(a_1 + k_1)$. Its characteristic polynomial is
$s^n + (a_1 + k_1)s^{n-1} + \dots + (a_n + k_n)$. Matching it to $\alpha(s)$ coefficient by coefficient gives

$$
k_i = \alpha_i - a_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n.
$$

Each gain shifts one coefficient of the characteristic polynomial, independently of the others. Any $\alpha$ is
reachable, and the gain is unique.

### Any controllable system

A controllable single-input system can be brought to companion form by a change of state coordinates. Under
$\xvec = \Tmat\zvec$ the controllability matrix transforms as $\Ccal = \Tmat\Ccal_c$, so $\Tmat = \Ccal\Ccal_c^{-1}$,
which exists because both matrices are invertible. The gain in the original coordinates is
$\Kmat = \Kmat_c\Tmat^{-1}$. Conversely, if $(\Amat, \Bmat)$ is not controllable, the uncontrollable modes are
eigenvalues of $\Amat - \Bmat\Kmat$ for every $\Kmat$, since feedback of the state changes only what the input
reaches.

### Ackermann's formula

Ackermann's formula skips the change of coordinates. Write $\Amat_K = \Amat - \Bmat\Kmat$. Expanding powers of
$\Amat = \Amat_K + \Bmat\Kmat$ and using induction on $k$,

$$
\Amat^k = \Amat_K^k + \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Amat^j\Bmat\Kmat\Amat_K^{k-1-j}.
$$

The design makes $\alpha$ the characteristic polynomial of $\Amat_K$, so $\alpha(\Amat_K) = \zeros$ by the
Cayley–Hamilton theorem. With $\alpha_0 = 1$, summing the expansion with weights $\alpha_{n-k}$ gives

$$
\alpha(\Amat) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha_{n-k}\paren{\Amat^k - \Amat_K^k}
= \sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{n-k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\Amat^j\Bmat\Kmat\Amat_K^{k-1-j}
= \Ccal\begin{bmatrix} \wvec_0^\top \\ \vdots \\ \wvec_{n-1}^\top \end{bmatrix},
$$

where the row $\wvec_j^\top = \sum_{k=j+1}^{n}\alpha_{n-k}\Kmat\Amat_K^{k-1-j}$ collects the terms multiplying
$\Amat^j\Bmat$. The last row has a single term, $k = n$, with coefficient $\alpha_0 = 1$: $\wvec_{n-1}^\top = \Kmat$.
Multiplying by $\Ccal^{-1}$ and taking the last row gives the formula. It is compact for hand calculation and short
derivations. Numerically it inherits the poor conditioning of $\Ccal$, and library routines use orthogonal
transformations instead.

## Worked example: balancing the pendulum

The pendulum linearised about its inverted position, with $g/\ell = 9.81$ and damping $c = 0.5$ (see
<NoteLink to="jacobian-linearisation">linearisation</NoteLink>), is

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 9.81 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Bmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with eigenvalues $2.89$ and $-3.39$. Place both closed-loop poles at $-3$: $\alpha(s) = (s + 3)^2 = s^2 + 6s + 9$.

**Companion form.** $\Amat$ is already a companion matrix, with $\chi(s) = s^2 + 0.5s - 9.81$, so $a_1 = 0.5$ and
$a_2 = -9.81$. Then $k_1 = 6 - 0.5 = 5.5$ multiplies the angular velocity and $k_2 = 9 + 9.81 = 18.81$ multiplies the
angle: $\Kmat = \begin{bmatrix} 18.81 & 5.5 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Ackermann.** $\Ccal = \begin{bmatrix} \Bmat & \Amat\Bmat \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$,
with inverse $\begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, whose last row is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
Next, $\Amat^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 9.81 & -0.5 \\ -4.905 & 10.06 \end{bmatrix}$ and

$$
\alpha(\Amat) = \Amat^2 + 6\Amat + 9\eye = \begin{bmatrix} 18.81 & 5.5 \\ 53.955 & 16.06 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its first row, picked out by $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, is $\Kmat = \begin{bmatrix} 18.81 & 5.5 \end{bmatrix}$,
as before. The closed-loop matrix $\Amat - \Bmat\Kmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -9 & -6 \end{bmatrix}$ has a double
eigenvalue at $-3$. The torque needed is $18.81$ per radian of tilt, about twice the gravitational torque per radian,
because the feedback must first cancel gravity's destabilising $9.81$ and then add the stiffness $9$ that the target
polynomial asks for.

## Cost

Computing $\Kmat$ costs one eigenvalue assignment, $O(n^3)$. The controller itself is a matrix-vector product per
sample. It needs the full state, which is rarely measured; a <NoteLink to="state-observer">state observer</NoteLink>
supplies an estimate.

## Trade-offs

- **Speed against effort.** Poles further left give a faster response and need larger gains. The gain grows roughly
  with the distance the poles are moved, so aggressive placement saturates actuators and amplifies noise.
- **Where to put the poles.** The formula assigns any set but does not say which set is good. Common choices use a
  dominant second-order pair for the desired damping, with the remaining poles several times faster. The
  <NoteLink to="linear-quadratic-regulator">linear-quadratic regulator</NoteLink> replaces the choice of poles with a
  choice of cost weights and guarantees robustness margins that arbitrary placement does not.
- **Several inputs.** With $m > 1$ inputs the gain is not unique: $mn$ entries meet $n$ conditions. The freedom can
  also shape eigenvectors, and robust placement algorithms use it to make the closed-loop eigenvalues insensitive to
  model errors.
- **Zeros are unchanged.** State feedback moves poles but leaves the transmission zeros of the plant where they are.
  A right half-plane zero still limits performance.
