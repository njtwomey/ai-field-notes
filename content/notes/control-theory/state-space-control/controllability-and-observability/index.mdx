<Definition>
Consider $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec + \Bmat\uvec$, $\yvec = \Cmat\xvec$ with $\xvec \in \reals^n$.

- The system is **controllable** if for every initial state $\xvec_0$, target state $\xvec_1$ and time $T > 0$ there is
  an input on $[0, T]$ that takes $\xvec(0) = \xvec_0$ to $\xvec(T) = \xvec_1$.
- The system is **observable** if for every $T > 0$ the initial state $\xvec(0)$ is uniquely determined by the input
  and output on $[0, T]$.

Kalman's rank conditions <Cite id="kalman1960c" />: the system is controllable exactly when the
$n \times nm$ **controllability matrix** $\Ccal = \begin{bmatrix} \Bmat & \Amat\Bmat & \cdots & \Amat^{n-1}\Bmat \end{bmatrix}$
has rank $n$, and observable exactly when the $np \times n$ **observability matrix**
$\Ocal = \begin{bmatrix} \Cmat^\top & (\Cmat\Amat)^\top & \cdots & (\Cmat\Amat^{n-1})^\top \end{bmatrix}^\top$ has rank $n$.
</Definition>

## Why they matter

Feedback can only act on what the input moves and what the output reveals. A mode that the input cannot excite keeps
its own dynamics under any controller; if it is unstable, no controller stabilises the system. A mode that the output
never shows cannot be estimated, so a controller that needs it cannot compute it. Controllability is what makes
<NoteLink to="pole-placement">pole placement</NoteLink> possible, and observability is what makes a
<NoteLink to="state-observer">state observer</NoteLink> possible.

## The rank test in discrete time

The discrete-time system $\xvec_{k+1} = \Amat\xvec_k + \Bmat\uvec_k$ shows the idea most directly. Unrolling the
recursion from $\xvec_0$,

$$
\xvec_N = \Amat^N\xvec_0 + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Amat^{N-1-k}\Bmat\uvec_k
= \Amat^N\xvec_0 + \begin{bmatrix} \Bmat & \Amat\Bmat & \cdots & \Amat^{N-1}\Bmat \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} \uvec_{N-1} \\ \vdots \\ \uvec_0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The states reachable from $\xvec_0 = \zeros$ in $N$ steps form the column space of
$\begin{bmatrix} \Bmat & \cdots & \Amat^{N-1}\Bmat \end{bmatrix}$. This space grows with $N$, but it stops growing at
$N = n$. By the **Cayley–Hamilton theorem**, $\Amat$ satisfies its own characteristic polynomial
$\chi(s) = s^n + a_1 s^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$:

$$
\Amat^n = -a_1\Amat^{n-1} - \dots - a_n\eye.
$$

So $\Amat^n\Bmat$ is a combination of $\Bmat, \dots, \Amat^{n-1}\Bmat$, and by induction so is every higher power.
Every reachable state is reachable in $n$ steps, and every state is reachable exactly when $\rank\Ccal = n$.

## The rank test in continuous time

**Necessity.** By Cayley–Hamilton applied to each term of the exponential series, $e^{\Amat t}$ is a polynomial in
$\Amat$ of degree less than $n$ with time-dependent coefficients, $e^{\Amat t} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha_k(t)\Amat^k$.
The solution formula then gives

$$
\xvec(T) - e^{\Amat T}\xvec_0 = \int_0^T e^{\Amat(T - \tau)}\Bmat\uvec(\tau)\,d\tau
= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \Amat^k\Bmat\int_0^T \alpha_k(T - \tau)\uvec(\tau)\,d\tau,
$$

which lies in the column space of $\Ccal$ whatever the input. If $\rank\Ccal < n$, some targets cannot be reached.

**Sufficiency.** Define the **controllability Gramian**

$$
\Wmat_c(T) = \int_0^T e^{\Amat\tau}\Bmat\Bmat^\top e^{\Amat^\top\tau}\,d\tau.
$$

If $\Wmat_c(T)$ is invertible, the input

$$
\uvec(t) = \Bmat^\top e^{\Amat^\top(T - t)}\,\Wmat_c(T)^{-1}\paren{\xvec_1 - e^{\Amat T}\xvec_0}
$$

reaches the target: substituting it into the solution formula and changing variable to $\tau = T - t$ turns the
integral into $\Wmat_c(T)\Wmat_c(T)^{-1}(\xvec_1 - e^{\Amat T}\xvec_0)$. It remains to show that $\Wmat_c(T)$ is
invertible when $\rank\Ccal = n$. $\Wmat_c$ is positive semidefinite, and $\vvec^\top\Wmat_c\vvec =
\int_0^T\norm{\Bmat^\top e^{\Amat^\top\tau}\vvec}^2 d\tau$ is zero only if $\vvec^\top e^{\Amat\tau}\Bmat = \zeros$ for
all $\tau \in [0, T]$. Differentiating $k$ times at $\tau = 0$ gives $\vvec^\top\Amat^k\Bmat = \zeros$ for every $k$,
so $\vvec^\top\Ccal = \zeros$. When $\Ccal$ has full rank this forces $\vvec = \zeros$, so $\Wmat_c(T) \succ 0$.

<Derivation title="The Gramian measures control energy">
Among all inputs that take $\zeros$ to $\xvec_1$ in time $T$, the one above minimises the energy
$\int_0^T\norm{\uvec}^2dt$. Any other input that reaches $\xvec_1$ is $\uvec + \tilde{\uvec}$ with
$\int_0^T e^{\Amat(T - t)}\Bmat\tilde{\uvec}(t)\,dt = \zeros$. The cross term in the energy is
$2\int_0^T\uvec^\top\tilde{\uvec}\,dt = 2\,(\Wmat_c^{-1}\xvec_1)^\top\int_0^T e^{\Amat(T - t)}\Bmat\tilde{\uvec}\,dt = 0$,
so the energy is $\int\norm{\uvec}^2 + \int\norm{\tilde{\uvec}}^2 \ge \int\norm{\uvec}^2$. The minimum is

$$
\int_0^T\norm{\uvec}^2dt = \xvec_1^\top\Wmat_c(T)^{-1}\xvec_1.
$$

Directions along eigenvectors of $\Wmat_c$ with small eigenvalues are expensive to reach. A system can be controllable
in the rank sense and still need impractically large inputs in some directions.
</Derivation>

## Observability and duality

With the input known, its contribution to the output can be subtracted, so take $\uvec = \zeros$. Then
$\yvec(t) = \Cmat e^{\Amat t}\xvec_0$. Two initial states give the same output exactly when their difference
$\dvec$ satisfies $\Cmat e^{\Amat t}\dvec = \zeros$ for all $t$. Differentiating at $t = 0$, this is
$\Cmat\Amat^k\dvec = \zeros$ for all $k$, and by Cayley–Hamilton it suffices to check $k < n$: $\Ocal\dvec = \zeros$.
So the initial state is determined uniquely exactly when the null space of $\Ocal$ is $\set{\zeros}$, which is
$\rank\Ocal = n$.

Transposing $\Ocal$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} \Cmat^\top & \Amat^\top\Cmat^\top & \cdots \end{bmatrix}$, the
controllability matrix of the pair $(\Amat^\top, \Cmat^\top)$. Hence **duality**: $(\Amat, \Cmat)$ is observable exactly
when $(\Amat^\top, \Cmat^\top)$ is controllable. Every result about control has a twin about estimation. The
**observability Gramian** $\Wmat_o(T) = \int_0^T e^{\Amat^\top t}\Cmat^\top\Cmat e^{\Amat t}dt$ measures how much
output energy an initial state produces, $\int_0^T\norm{\yvec}^2dt = \xvec_0^\top\Wmat_o\xvec_0$. It is invertible
exactly when the system is observable.

## Mode by mode: the PBH test

The Popov–Belevitch–Hautus test states controllability one eigenvalue at a time: $(\Amat, \Bmat)$ is controllable
exactly when $\rank\begin{bmatrix} \lambda\eye - \Amat & \Bmat \end{bmatrix} = n$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of
$\Amat$. Equivalently, no left eigenvector $\wvec^\top\Amat = \lambda\wvec^\top$ satisfies $\wvec^\top\Bmat = \zeros$.
Such a $\wvec$ would pick out a combination of states, $\wvec^\top\xvec$, that evolves as
$\frac{d}{dt}(\wvec^\top\xvec) = \lambda\,\wvec^\top\xvec$ regardless of the input. That combination is an
uncontrollable mode. The dual test uses right eigenvectors with $\Cmat\vvec = \zeros$ for unobservable modes.

The weaker properties used by optimal control are mode-wise too. The system is **stabilisable** if every
uncontrollable mode is stable, and **detectable** if every unobservable mode is stable.

## The Kalman decomposition

A change of state coordinates splits any linear system into four parts: controllable and observable, controllable but
unobservable, uncontrollable but observable, and neither <Cite id="kalman1963" />. The transfer function
$\Cmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat$ depends only on the first part. The eigenvalues of the other parts are exactly the
ones that cancel between numerator and denominator. A state-space model is **minimal**, of the smallest possible order
for its transfer function, exactly when it is controllable and observable.

## Worked example

Take $\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $-1$ and $-2$) and
$\Bmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $\Amat\Bmat = (1, -3)$ and

$$
\Ccal = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det\Ccal = -1 \ne 0.
$$

The system is controllable. Now measure $y = x_1 + x_2$, so $\Cmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and
$\Cmat\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\Ocal = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \rank\Ocal = 1.
$$

The system is not observable. The null space of $\Ocal$ is spanned by $(1, -1)$, the eigenvector for $\lambda = -1$.
Starting from $\xvec_0 = (1, -1)$, the state is $e^{-t}(1, -1)$ and the output $x_1 + x_2$ is zero for all time. The
transfer function shows the cancellation:

$$
\Cmat(s\eye - \Amat)^{-1}\Bmat = \frac{s + 1}{(s + 1)(s + 2)} = \frac{1}{s + 2}.
$$

The hidden mode is stable, so the system is detectable and the loss is harmless for estimation. Had the hidden mode
been unstable, the output would look perfectly well behaved while the state diverged.

## Assumptions

- The rank tests apply to linear time-invariant systems. Time-varying systems use the Gramians over an interval, and
  nonlinear systems use Lie-bracket conditions that hold only locally.
- The tests give a yes or no answer. The Gramians' eigenvalues give the quantitative picture.

## Pitfalls

- **Rank in floating point.** $\Ccal$ is badly conditioned for even moderate $n$, because the columns $\Amat^k\Bmat$
  line up with the dominant eigenvector as $k$ grows. Numerical rank decisions should use the PBH test with a
  staircase (Hessenberg) algorithm, or the singular values of the Gramians.
- **Controllable is not easily controllable.** A tiny Gramian eigenvalue means a huge input is needed for that
  direction, which saturating actuators cannot supply.
- **Cancellations hide modes.** Checking stability of a transfer function misses unstable modes that are
  uncontrollable or unobservable. Check $\Amat$ itself.
