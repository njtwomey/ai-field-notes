<Definition>
The **Bode plot** of a loop transfer function $L(s)$ is a pair of graphs against $\log_{10}\omega$: the magnitude
$20\log_{10}\abs{L(i\omega)}$ in decibels (dB) and the phase $\angle L(i\omega)$ in degrees. For a loop whose open-loop
transfer function has no right half-plane poles:

- the **gain crossover frequency** $\omega_c$ solves $\abs{L(i\omega_c)} = 1$ (0 dB), and the **phase margin** is
  $\varphi_m = 180° + \angle L(i\omega_c)$;
- the **phase crossover frequency** $\omega_{180}$ solves $\angle L(i\omega_{180}) = -180°$, and the **gain margin**
  is $g_m = 1/\abs{L(i\omega_{180})}$, or $-20\log_{10}\abs{L(i\omega_{180})}$ dB.

The closed loop is stable when both margins are positive (in degrees and dB), for loops whose magnitude and phase each
cross their critical level once.
</Definition>

## Why logarithmic axes

A loop transfer function is a product of factors: gains, integrators, first-order lags $1/(1 + s/p)$, second-order
resonances, zeros and delays. The logarithm of a product is a sum, and the angle of a product is a sum of angles. On a
Bode plot each factor is drawn once and the curves add. Bode developed the method for feedback amplifiers
<Cite id="bode1945" />.

**Asymptotes of a first-order lag.** For $1/(1 + i\omega/p)$ with $p > 0$,

$$
20\log_{10}\abs{\frac{1}{1 + i\omega/p}} = -10\log_{10}\paren{1 + \frac{\omega^2}{p^2}}, \qquad
\angle\frac{1}{1 + i\omega/p} = -\arctan\frac{\omega}{p}.
$$

For $\omega \ll p$ the magnitude is about 0 dB and the phase about $0°$. For $\omega \gg p$ the magnitude is about
$-20\log_{10}(\omega/p)$, a straight line falling 20 dB per decade, and the phase about $-90°$. At the **break
frequency** $\omega = p$ the exact values are $-3$ dB and $-45°$. The phase changes over about two decades centred on
$p$. A zero gives the mirror image: $+20$ dB per decade and $+90°$. An integrator $1/s$ is a line of slope $-20$ dB
per decade through 0 dB at $\omega = 1$, with constant phase $-90°$. So a rough Bode plot can be drawn with a ruler.

## Margins from the Nyquist criterion

For an open-loop stable $L$, the <NoteLink to="nyquist-stability-criterion">Nyquist criterion</NoteLink> says the closed
loop is stable when the curve $L(i\omega)$ does not encircle $-1$. The point $-1$ has magnitude 1 and phase $-180°$.
The two margins measure how far $L$ is from $-1$ along each of those two coordinates.

- At $\omega_{180}$ the curve crosses the negative real axis at $-\abs{L(i\omega_{180})}$. Multiplying the loop gain
  by $g_m$ moves this crossing onto $-1$. The gain margin is the tolerable increase in gain.
- At $\omega_c$ the curve crosses the unit circle at angle $\angle L(i\omega_c)$. An extra phase lag of $\varphi_m$
  rotates that crossing onto $-1$. The phase margin is the tolerable extra phase lag.

A pure time delay $\tau$ multiplies $L$ by $e^{-i\omega\tau}$. It leaves the magnitude unchanged and subtracts
$\omega\tau$ radians from the phase. At $\omega_c$ this eats $\omega_c\tau$ of phase margin, so the loop stays stable
for delays up to the **delay margin**

$$
\tau_m = \frac{\varphi_m}{\omega_c}, \qquad \varphi_m \text{ in radians}.
$$

## Worked example

Take $L(s) = K/(s(s + 1)(0.2s + 1))$, with break frequencies at 1 and 5 rad/s. The magnitude falls at 20 dB per decade
below 1 rad/s, at 40 dB per decade between 1 and 5 rad/s, and at 60 dB per decade above. The phase is

$$
\angle L(i\omega) = -90° - \arctan\omega - \arctan(0.2\omega).
$$

- **Phase crossover.** $\arctan\omega + \arctan 0.2\omega = 90°$ means $\omega \cdot 0.2\omega = 1$, because two
  angles sum to $90°$ exactly when the product of their tangents is 1. So $\omega_{180} = \sqrt5 = 2.236$ rad/s.
  There $\abs{L} = K/(\sqrt5 \cdot \sqrt6 \cdot \sqrt{1.2}) = K/6$. The gain margin is $6/K$.
- **At $K = 1$.** The gain margin is 6, or $15.6$ dB. Solving $\abs{L(i\omega)} = 1$ numerically gives
  $\omega_c = 0.779$ rad/s, where the phase is $-136.8°$, so $\varphi_m = 43.2°$. The delay margin is
  $0.754 / 0.779 = 0.97$ s. The closed-loop step response overshoots by 25 %.
- **At $K = 2$.** The gain margin halves to 3 ($9.5$ dB), $\omega_c$ rises to $1.23$ rad/s and $\varphi_m$ falls to
  $25.4°$. The overshoot grows to 49 %.
- **At $K = 6$.** Both margins are zero. The closed loop has poles at $\pm i\sqrt5$ and oscillates at $\omega_{180}$,
  in agreement with the <NoteLink to="routh-hurwitz-criterion">Routh–Hurwitz</NoteLink> bound $K < 6$.

## Gain and phase are linked

For a stable **minimum-phase** transfer function, one with no zeros or poles in the right half-plane and no delay,
the phase is determined by the magnitude. Bode's gain-phase relation makes this exact <Cite id="bode1945" />. Its
practical form: where the magnitude falls at a steady $20n$ dB per decade over a wide band, the phase is close to
$-90n$ degrees. A loop whose magnitude crosses 0 dB with a slope of $-40$ dB per decade has phase near $-180°$ there
and little or no phase margin. Classical **loop shaping** therefore aims for a crossover slope of about $-20$ dB per
decade over a band around $\omega_c$, with steeper slopes only well above and below it. Right half-plane zeros and
delays add phase lag without changing the magnitude, which is why they limit the achievable bandwidth.

## Reading performance from the plot

- **Bandwidth.** The closed-loop bandwidth is close to $\omega_c$. A faster loop needs a higher crossover.
- **Damping.** For loops dominated by a second-order pair, the damping ratio is roughly $\zeta \approx \varphi_m/100$
  for $\varphi_m$ in degrees below about $70°$. Phase margins of $45°$ to $60°$ are common targets.
- **Steady-state accuracy.** The low-frequency magnitude sets the steady-state error: $1/(1 + L(0))$ for a step, zero
  when $L$ has an integrator.

## Assumptions

- The margins as defined assume the magnitude and phase each cross their critical level once, and that $L$ has no
  right half-plane poles. Otherwise use the full Nyquist criterion.
- Margins are computed for one loop at a time. In a multivariable system a perturbation can act on several loops at
  once, and single-loop margins can badly overstate robustness.

## Pitfalls

- **Good margins, small distance to $-1$.** The gain and phase margins measure distance to $-1$ along two particular
  directions. The Nyquist curve can pass close to $-1$ between the crossover frequencies while both margins look
  healthy. The peak of $\abs{S(i\omega)}$ measures the true distance.
- **Conditionally stable loops.** A phase that dips below $-180°$ and comes back gives several phase crossovers.
  Such a loop can become unstable if the gain is reduced, which saturation does in effect.
- **Unwrapping the phase.** Numerical phase is computed modulo $360°$. Plots and margin calculations need the
  continuous phase, which a delay makes fall without bound.
