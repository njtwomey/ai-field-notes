<Definition>
A **lead-lag compensator** is a controller built from first-order factors

$$
C(s) = K\,\frac{1 + s/z}{1 + s/p}, \qquad z, p > 0.
$$

With $z < p$ it is a **lead** compensator: its phase is positive, and it raises the gain at high frequency. With
$z > p$ it is a **lag** compensator: its phase is negative, and it raises the gain at low frequency. A lead-lag
compensator is the product of one of each.
</Definition>

## Problem

A proportional controller has one parameter. Raising it improves steady-state accuracy and speed, and reduces the
phase margin. Often no single gain satisfies both requirements. The compensator adds a zero and a pole that reshape
the loop's <NoteLink to="bode-plots-and-stability-margins">Bode plot</NoteLink> locally: a lead network adds phase
near the crossover frequency, and a lag network adds gain at low frequency, where phase margin is not decided.

## Mechanism

### Lead: how much phase and where

The phase of the lead factor is

$$
\varphi(\omega) = \arctan\frac{\omega}{z} - \arctan\frac{\omega}{p}.
$$

It is zero at $\omega = 0$ and as $\omega \to \infty$, and positive in between. Setting the derivative to zero,
$\frac{1/z}{1 + \omega^2/z^2} = \frac{1/p}{1 + \omega^2/p^2}$, which simplifies to $\omega^2 = zp$. The maximum lies at
the geometric mean of the zero and the pole, the midpoint on a logarithmic axis:

$$
\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{zp}.
$$

Write $\alpha = p/z > 1$. By the tangent subtraction formula, at $\omega_{\max}$

$$
\tan\varphi_{\max} = \frac{\omega/z - \omega/p}{1 + \omega^2/(zp)} = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha} - 1/\sqrt{\alpha}}{2}
= \frac{\alpha - 1}{2\sqrt{\alpha}}.
$$

A right triangle with opposite side $\alpha - 1$ and adjacent side $2\sqrt\alpha$ has hypotenuse
$\sqrt{(\alpha - 1)^2 + 4\alpha} = \alpha + 1$, so

$$
\sin\varphi_{\max} = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + 1}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{1 + \sin\varphi_{\max}}{1 - \sin\varphi_{\max}}.
$$

A ratio $\alpha = 3$ gives $30°$, $\alpha = 10$ gives $55°$. The price is gain: the lead factor's magnitude rises from
1 at low frequency to $\alpha$ at high frequency, and is $\sqrt\alpha$ at $\omega_{\max}$. The high-frequency gain
amplifies sensor noise, which limits $\alpha$ to about 10 to 20 in practice.

A lead compensator is a filtered PD controller: as $p \to \infty$ it becomes $K(1 + s/z)$. In
<NoteLink to="root-locus">root-locus</NoteLink> terms its zero pulls the branches to the left and its pole, further
left, limits the effect at high frequency.

### Lag: gain where it is needed

A lag factor with $z > p$ has magnitude $z/p = \beta$ at low frequency and 1 at high frequency. Placed so that both
$p$ and $z$ are well below the crossover frequency, typically $z \approx \omega_c/10$, it multiplies the static gain
by $\beta$ and so divides the steady-state error by about $\beta$, while changing the magnitude and phase near
$\omega_c$ only slightly. The phase it does add there is negative, about $-\arctan(\omega_c/p) + \arctan(\omega_c/z)$,
roughly $-5°$ when $z = \omega_c/10$ and $\beta$ is large. A lag compensator is a PI controller with the integrator
replaced by a pole close to the origin.

## Worked example

The plant $G(s) = 1/(s(s + 1))$ must track ramps with error at most $0.1$, so the velocity constant must be
$K_v = \lim_{s \to 0} sKG(s) = K \ge 10$. With $K = 10$ alone, $\abs{10/(i\omega(1 + i\omega))} = 1$ at
$\omega_c = 3.08$ rad/s, where the phase is $-90° - \arctan 3.08 = -162°$. The phase margin is $18°$, which gives a
badly underdamped response. The target is $45°$.

**Lead design.** The lead must supply about $45° - 18° = 27°$, plus a few degrees because adding it moves the
crossover to a higher frequency where the plant has more lag. Take $\varphi_{\max} = 32°$, so
$\alpha = (1 + \sin 32°)/(1 - \sin 32°) = 3.25$. The compensator adds $\sqrt\alpha$ of gain at $\omega_{\max}$, so
choose $\omega_{\max}$ where the uncompensated magnitude is $1/\sqrt\alpha$, that is $-5.1$ dB. This happens at
$4.19$ rad/s, which becomes the new crossover. Then $z = 4.19/\sqrt{3.25} = 2.32$ and
$p = 4.19\sqrt{3.25} = 7.56$:

$$
C(s) = 10\,\frac{1 + s/2.32}{1 + s/7.56}.
$$

The compensated loop crosses over at $4.19$ rad/s with a phase margin of $45.4°$, and $K_v = 10$ is unchanged because
the lead factor is 1 at $s = 0$.

**Lag design.** Instead, keep the crossover where the plant already has enough phase. The plant phase is
$-180° + 50° = -130°$ (target plus $5°$ for the lag's own phase) where $\arctan\omega = 40°$, at $\omega = 0.839$ rad/s.
There $\abs{10\,G} = 9.13$. A lag with $\beta = 9.13$ brings the magnitude down to 1 at that frequency while keeping
$K_v = 10$: put the zero a decade below, $z = 0.0839$, and the pole at $p = z/\beta = 0.0092$. The compensated loop
crosses over at $0.842$ rad/s with a phase margin of $44.8°$.

Both meet the specification. The lead design has five times the bandwidth, so it responds faster and passes more
noise. The lag design is slow, and its pole near the origin leaves a slow closed-loop pole whose small residue shows
up as a long tail in the step response.

## Cost

Each factor is a first-order filter: two coefficients and one state, a few operations per sample when implemented
digitally.

## Trade-offs

- **Lead** raises bandwidth and phase margin, and raises the high-frequency gain by $\alpha$, amplifying noise and
  demanding more actuator effort.
- **Lag** improves steady-state accuracy without lowering the margins much, at the cost of a slower response and a
  slow closed-loop pole near the lag's zero.
- **Lead-lag** combines the two: lag for low-frequency gain, lead for phase at crossover. A PID controller with a
  filtered derivative has the same structure, with the lag pole moved to the origin.
- The design rules are approximate. They assume that each factor acts on its own frequency band. Checking the final
  loop, as done above, is part of the method.
