<Definition>
For a prediction about $y$ at input $\xvec$, **aleatoric uncertainty** is the uncertainty that remains when the true
conditional distribution $p(y \mid \xvec)$ is known: noise, ambiguity, missing features. **Epistemic uncertainty** is
the uncertainty about which conditional distribution is the right one, caused by limited data or model knowledge. More
data can reduce epistemic uncertainty but not aleatoric uncertainty <Cite id="hullermeier2021" /> <Cite id="senge2014" />.
</Definition>

## Why separate them

The two kinds call for different actions. High aleatoric uncertainty says the case is inherently ambiguous. Deferring it
to an expert with the same information will not help, but collecting another feature might. High epistemic uncertainty
says the model has not seen enough cases like this one. More labelled data near $\xvec$ would help, and the input may
lie outside the training distribution. Active learning should query points of high epistemic uncertainty. Chow's
<NoteLink to="reject-option">reject option</NoteLink> reacts only to the ambiguity of the predicted distribution and
cannot tell the two apart.

## Decomposing the predictive entropy

Represent epistemic uncertainty by a distribution over models: a Bayesian posterior $p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal)$ over
parameters, or the members of an ensemble weighted equally. Each model gives a predictive distribution
$p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)$, and the overall prediction is their average,
$\bar p(y \mid \xvec) = \expect_{\thetavec}\brack{p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)}$.

The mutual information between the label and the model, given $\xvec$, is defined as
$I(y; \thetavec \mid \xvec) = \entropy[y \mid \xvec] - \entropy[y \mid \xvec, \thetavec]$, the reduction in entropy of
$y$ from learning $\thetavec$ (see <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink>). Here
$\entropy[y \mid \xvec]$ is the entropy of $\bar p$, and $\entropy[y \mid \xvec, \thetavec]$ is the expected entropy of
the individual models' predictions. Rearranging the definition,

$$
\underbrace{\entropy\brack{\bar p(\cdot \mid \xvec)}}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{\expect_{\thetavec}\brack{\entropy\brack{p(\cdot
\mid \xvec, \thetavec)}}}_{\text{aleatoric}} + \underbrace{I(y; \thetavec \mid \xvec)}_{\text{epistemic}}.
$$

The expected entropy is what would remain if we knew which model was right: aleatoric. The mutual information is large
when the models disagree, and zero when they all predict the same distribution: epistemic
<Cite id="hullermeier2021" />. It is non-negative because entropy is concave, so the entropy of the average is at least
the average of the entropies.

## Decomposing the predictive variance

For regression, Kendall and Gal let each model output a mean $\mu_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ and a noise variance
$\sigma^2_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ <Cite id="kendall2017" />. By the law of total variance, the variance of $y$ under the
averaged prediction is

$$
\var[y \mid \xvec] = \underbrace{\expect_{\thetavec}\brack{\sigma^2_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}}_{\text{aleatoric}} +
\underbrace{\var_{\thetavec}\brack{\mu_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}}_{\text{epistemic}}.
$$

The first term is the noise each model expects; the second is how much the models disagree about the mean. With Monte
Carlo dropout the models are samples of dropout masks <Cite id="gal2016" /> (see <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink>).

## The loss view

The decomposition of proper scoring rules gives a population version for a single fitted model with outputs $\svec$. With
$\qvec = P(y \mid \xvec)$ the true conditional distribution, the expected loss is the **epistemic loss**
$\expect[d(\svec, \qvec)]$ plus the **irreducible loss** $\expect[d(\qvec, \yvec)]$, where $d$ is the divergence of the
scoring rule <Cite id="kull2015" />. Irreducible loss is the loss of the Bayes-optimal model, the aleatoric part.
Epistemic loss splits further into calibration loss, which post-hoc calibration can remove, and grouping loss, which only
a better model can (see <NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions">decompositions of proper scoring rules</NoteLink>).
This view measures uncertainty after the fact, over a population. The entropy decomposition measures it for one input
before the label is seen.

## Worked example

Three models predict the probability of a binary label at one input. Entropies are in bits.

| model predictions | average $\bar p$ | total entropy | aleatoric | epistemic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0.9,\ 0.9,\ 0.9$ | $0.9$ | $0.469$ | $0.469$ | $0$ |
| $0.5,\ 0.5,\ 0.5$ | $0.5$ | $1$ | $1$ | $0$ |
| $0.99,\ 0.5,\ 0.01$ | $0.5$ | $1$ | $0.387$ | $0.613$ |

The last two rows have the same averaged prediction, $0.5$, and the same total entropy. In the second, every model agrees
that the case is a coin flip: the uncertainty is aleatoric. In the third, two models are confident and disagree: most of
the uncertainty is epistemic. The aleatoric part is the mean of the three models' entropies,
$(0.081 + 1 + 0.081)/3 = 0.387$, where $0.081$ is the entropy of a $0.99$ prediction.

## Pitfalls

- **The split depends on the model family.** Epistemic uncertainty is measured by disagreement within a chosen family and
  posterior approximation. A family that cannot represent the truth, or an ensemble whose members are too similar, reports
  too little epistemic uncertainty.
- **Aleatoric estimates need calibration.** If each model is overconfident, the expected entropy understates the
  aleatoric uncertainty and the decomposition misattributes it.
- **Aleatoric is relative to the features.** Uncertainty that is irreducible with the current features can become
  reducible when a new feature is measured.
