<Definition>
A **selective classifier** is a pair $(f, g)$ of a classifier $f$ and a selection function $g : \Xcal \to \set{0, 1}$. It
predicts $f(\xvec)$ when $g(\xvec) = 1$ and abstains otherwise. Its **coverage** is $\phi(f, g) = \expect[g(\xvec)]$, the
probability of predicting, and its **selective risk** is

$$
R(f, g) = \frac{\expect\brack{\ell(f(\xvec), y)\, g(\xvec)}}{\phi(f, g)},
$$

the expected loss on the cases it accepts. The **risk-coverage curve** plots selective risk against coverage
<Cite id="elyaniv2010" /> <Cite id="geifman2017" />.
</Definition>

## Costs or constraints

Chow's <NoteLink to="reject-option">reject option</NoteLink> fixes a cost $d$ for abstaining and minimises expected
loss. Many applications cannot name that cost. It is easier to say "the accepted predictions must have at most 2%
error" or "the system must handle at least 70% of cases". Selective classification states the problem that way: maximise
coverage subject to a bound on selective risk, or minimise selective risk at a given coverage.

The two views meet. Chow's rule with cost $d$ minimises error plus $d$ times the reject rate; at the reject rate it
produces, no rule has a lower error. So if the true posterior were known, thresholding its largest entry would trace the
best possible risk-coverage curve <Cite id="chow1970" />.

## Confidence scores and thresholds

In practice $g$ thresholds a **confidence-rate function** $\kappa(\xvec)$: $g_\theta(\xvec) = \indicator[\kappa(\xvec) \ge
\theta]$. Only the ordering of cases by $\kappa$ matters. Common choices are the largest softmax probability (softmax
response) and agreement across Monte Carlo dropout samples <Cite id="gal2016" />. Geifman and El-Yaniv found the two
nearly equivalent on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, and softmax response clearly better on ImageNet <Cite id="geifman2017" />.

A good $\kappa$ ranks the cases the classifier gets wrong below the ones it gets right. That is a ranking property, not
calibration: a confidence score that is badly calibrated but correctly ordered gives the same risk-coverage curve as a
calibrated one. The area under the risk-coverage curve (AURC) summarises the ranking over all thresholds.

## Guaranteeing a selective risk

Given a classifier $f$, a confidence function $\kappa$, a labelled set of $m$ held-out cases, a target risk $r^*$ and a
confidence level $\delta$, the **selection with guaranteed risk** (SGR) algorithm finds a threshold $\theta$ such that

$$
P\paren{R(f, g_\theta) > r^*} < \delta,
$$

with the probability over the draw of the held-out set <Cite id="geifman2017" />.

**The bound for one threshold.** Fix $\theta$. The accepted held-out cases are an independent sample from the accepted
region, so their number of errors is binomial with the true selective risk as its success probability. If $n_\theta$
cases are accepted and $e$ of them are misclassified, the one-sided upper confidence bound $b^*$ is the solution of

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{e} \binom{n_\theta}{j} b^j (1 - b)^{n_\theta - j} = \delta.
$$

Then the true selective risk exceeds $b^*$ with probability below $\delta$ <Cite id="geifman2017" />.

**Searching over thresholds.** Checking many thresholds on the same data would inflate the error probability. SGR sorts
the held-out cases by $\kappa$ and binary-searches for the lowest threshold whose bound is below $r^*$. It examines
$k = \lceil \log_2 m \rceil$ thresholds and uses $\delta/k$ for each bound, so by the union bound the returned threshold
satisfies the guarantee with probability at least $1 - \delta$.

## Worked example

A classifier is evaluated on $m = 2000$ held-out cases, with target risk $r^* = 0.04$ and $\delta = 0.001$. The binary
search takes $k = \lceil \log_2 2000 \rceil = 11$ steps, so each bound uses $\delta/11 = 9.1 \times 10^{-5}$.

| accepted cases | coverage | errors | empirical risk | upper bound $b^*$ | meets $r^* = 0.04$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 | 0.25 | 5 | 0.010 | 0.039 | yes |
| 1200 | 0.60 | 24 | 0.020 | 0.040 (0.0397) | yes |
| 1500 | 0.75 | 45 | 0.030 | 0.050 | no |

The threshold that accepts 1200 cases has twice the empirical risk of the one that accepts 500, but more cases make its
bound almost as tight. It meets the target at 60% coverage. The threshold accepting 1500 cases has an empirical risk of
3%, below the target, but its bound of 5% does not certify it.

## Pitfalls

- **Empirical risk below target is not a guarantee.** With few accepted cases, the bound can be far above the empirical
  risk. Report the bound, not only the observed error.
- **The guarantee is for the held-out distribution.** Under distribution shift, both the ranking quality of $\kappa$ and
  the error rate among accepted cases change.
- **Confidence is not correctness for unfamiliar inputs.** A network can be confident on inputs unlike its training data.
  Selection based on softmax response inherits that failure (see
  <NoteLink to="aleatoric-and-epistemic-uncertainty">aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty</NoteLink>).
