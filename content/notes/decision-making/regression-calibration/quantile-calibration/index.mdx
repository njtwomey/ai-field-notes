<Definition>
A probabilistic regression model predicts, for each input $\xvec_i$, a <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss> $F_i$ for the
target. It is **quantile-calibrated** if, for every level $p \in [0, 1]$,

$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \indicator\brack{y_i \le F_i^{-1}(p)} \to p \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,
$$

so that the predicted $p$-quantile is exceeded a fraction $1 - p$ of the time. Equivalently, the **<Gloss name="probability-integral-transform">probability integral
transform</Gloss>** (PIT) values $u_i = F_i(y_i)$ are uniformly distributed on $[0, 1]$ <Cite id="kuleshov2018" />
<Cite id="gneiting2007b" />.
</Definition>

## From classification to regression

A binary classifier is calibrated if the event happens in a fraction $p$ of the cases given probability $p$. A
regression model predicts a whole distribution, so there are many events to check: $y \le F_i^{-1}(p)$ for every
$p$. Quantile calibration asks that each of these events happen at the advertised rate, pooled over all cases. A
calibrated 90% central prediction interval, from the 5% to the 95% quantile, then contains 90% of the outcomes.

## Why the PIT is uniform

Let $Y$ have a continuous cdf $F$. For $u \in (0, 1)$, $F(Y) \le u$ holds exactly when $Y \le F^{-1}(u)$, so

$$
P(F(Y) \le u) = P(Y \le F^{-1}(u)) = F(F^{-1}(u)) = u.
$$

$F(Y)$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$. If each $F_i$ is the true <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> of $y_i$, each $u_i$ is uniform,
and so is their pooled distribution. Quantile calibration asks only for the pooled statement, $P(u \le p) = p$ averaged
over cases, which is the same as the fraction of $y_i \le F_i^{-1}(p)$ tending to $p$.

## Reading a PIT histogram

A histogram of the $u_i$ is the regression analogue of a reliability diagram.

- **Flat.** Consistent with quantile calibration.
- **U-shaped.** Too many outcomes fall in the tails of their predicted distributions: the predictions are too narrow,
  the model overconfident.
- **Hump-shaped.** Too few outcomes in the tails: the predictions are too wide.
- **Sloped.** Outcomes fall systematically on one side of the predicted median: the predictions are biased.

## Recalibration with isotonic regression

Kuleshov, Fenner and Ermon proposed a post-hoc fix that works for any model with predictive cdfs
<Cite id="kuleshov2018" />.

1. On a calibration set, compute $u_i = F_i(y_i)$.
2. For each $u_i$, compute the empirical frequency $\hat P(u_i) = \frac1n\abs{\set{j : u_j \le u_i}}$.
3. Fit an <NoteLink to="isotonic-calibration">isotonic regression</NoteLink> $R: [0, 1] \to [0, 1]$ to the pairs
   $(u_i, \hat P(u_i))$.
4. Use $R \circ F_i$ as the new cdf for each case.

The map $R$ estimates $P(u \le p)$, the rate at which the model's $p$-quantile is actually exceeded. Composing sends
each level to its observed rate, so the recalibrated model is quantile-calibrated on the calibration data. $R$ is
non-decreasing, so $R \circ F_i$ is still a valid cdf. The recalibrated $\tau$-quantile of case $i$ is
$F_i^{-1}(R^{-1}(\tau))$.

## Calibration is not enough

The **climatological** forecast, which predicts the marginal distribution of $y$ for every case, is quantile-calibrated
and ignores the inputs. Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery therefore judge probabilistic forecasts by maximising
**sharpness**, the concentration of the predictive distributions, subject to calibration <Cite id="gneiting2007b" />. A
proper <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">scoring rule</NoteLink> such as the <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)</NoteLink> rewards both at once.

Quantile calibration is also a marginal property. It pools every case, so a model can be calibrated overall while its
intervals are too wide for some inputs and too narrow for others. Recalibration with one global map $R$ cannot fix
that, because it applies the same correction to every case. Song, Diethe, Kull and Flach proposed the stronger
condition of <NoteLink to="distribution-calibration">distribution calibration</NoteLink> for this reason
<Cite id="songhao2019" />.

## Worked example

A model predicts $\Gauss(\mu_i, 1)$ for each case, and its means are right, but the true noise has <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> 2:
$y_i = \mu_i + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim \Gauss(0, 4)$.

**Miscalibration.** The PIT is $u_i = \Phi(\varepsilon_i)$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal cdf. The outcome falls
below the predicted 5% quantile $\mu_i - 1.645$ when $\varepsilon_i \le -1.645$, which has probability
$\Phi(-1.645/2) = 0.205$, not 0.05. The predicted 90% central interval $\mu_i \pm 1.645$ contains the outcome with probability
$2\Phi(0.8225) - 1 = 0.589$. The <NoteLink to="probabilistic-forecasting">PIT histogram</NoteLink> is U-shaped, with 20.5% of values below 0.05 and another 20.5% above
0.95.

**Recalibration map.** $P(u \le p) = P(\varepsilon \le \Phi^{-1}(p)) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(p)/2)$, which is the map $R$ that
<NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic regression</NoteLink> estimates with enough data. The recalibrated $\tau$-quantile solves $R(u) = \tau$, giving
$u = \Phi(2\Phi^{-1}(\tau))$ and quantile $\mu_i + 2\Phi^{-1}(\tau)$. Recalibration has doubled the predicted spread, which
is the right correction here because every case has the same noise.

## Pitfalls

- **Discrete or censored targets.** The PIT of a <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">discrete distribution</NoteLink> is not uniform even when the forecast is correct.
  Use a randomised PIT or a different check.
- **A flat PIT histogram does not certify good forecasts.** It is consistent with the climatological forecast. Report a
  proper score as well.
- **Recalibrating on the training set.** As for classifiers, the map must be fitted on data the model was not trained
  on.
