<Definition>
A probabilistic classifier outputs a probability vector $\svec = f(\xvec)$ over $K$ classes. It is **calibrated** if
$P(y = k \mid f(\xvec) = \svec) = s_k$ for every output $\svec$ and class $k$. **Post-hoc calibration** keeps the trained
model and learns a **calibration map** $\mu$ from its outputs to new probabilities $\mu(\svec)$, fitted on data not used
to train the model. The survey by Silva Filho, Song, Perello-Nieto, Santos-Rodriguez, Kull and Flach is the reference for
the whole topic <Cite id="silvafilho2023" />.
</Definition>

## Why calibrate

A calibrated probability can be used directly in a decision. The <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes decision
rule</NoteLink> compares $P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ with $c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})$, a threshold that
is correct only if the probability is. Calibrated outputs can be combined with other probabilities, adjusted to a new
class prior with <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, and shown to people as risks.

Many classifiers are not calibrated. <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">Naive Bayes</NoteLink> pushes probabilities towards 0 and 1 because it multiplies evidence from
correlated features. <Gloss name="svm">Support vector machines</Gloss> and boosted trees output scores whose distortion is roughly sigmoidal
<Cite id="niculescumizil2005" />. Modern neural networks trained with <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink> tend to be overconfident <Cite id="guo2017" />. Resampling
or reweighting the training data to fix <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">class imbalance</NoteLink> also distorts probabilities (see
<NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under class imbalance</NoteLink>).

## What calibration can and cannot fix

The <NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions">decomposition of proper scoring rules</NoteLink> states the limit
precisely. A model's expected log loss or Brier score is its calibration loss plus its refinement loss. A calibration map
that sends each output $\svec$ to the class frequencies $\expect[\yvec \mid \svec]$ removes the calibration loss and
leaves the refinement loss untouched. Calibration improves the probabilities a model reports. It cannot improve how well
the model separates the classes, and a strictly increasing binary map leaves the ROC curve unchanged.

## Components



- **Definitions.** For more than two classes, "calibrated" has at least three meanings, of increasing strength:
  confidence, classwise and multiclass calibration (see <NoteLink to="calibration-definitions">definitions of classifier
  calibration</NoteLink>).
- **Binary calibration maps.** <NoteLink to="platt-scaling">Platt scaling</NoteLink> fits a logistic function of the
  score. <NoteLink to="isotonic-calibration">Isotonic calibration</NoteLink> fits the best non-decreasing step function.
  <NoteLink to="beta-calibration">Beta calibration</NoteLink> fits a three-parameter family derived from beta-distributed
  scores, which contains the identity.
- **Multiclass calibration maps.** <NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">Temperature scaling</NoteLink> divides a network's
  logits by one fitted constant. <NoteLink to="dirichlet-calibration">Dirichlet calibration</NoteLink> fits a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink>
  of log-probabilities, the multiclass analogue of beta calibration.
- **Evaluation.** <NoteLink to="reliability-diagrams-and-consistency-bars">Reliability diagrams</NoteLink> show
  calibration, and <NoteLink to="estimating-calibration-error">calibration error estimators</NoteLink> measure it; both
  must allow for sampling noise.

## Data flow

1. Train the classifier on the training set.
2. Score a separate **calibration set** with the trained classifier. Using the training set would calibrate the
   classifier's fit to data it has memorised; cross-validated scores are an alternative when data are scarce
   <Cite id="niculescumizil2005" />.
3. Fit the calibration map by minimising a proper score (log loss for the parametric maps, squared error for <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic
   regression</NoteLink>) on (score, label) pairs from the calibration set.
4. At prediction time, pass each new case through the model and then the map.
5. Evaluate on a test set with a proper score and a calibration diagnostic.

Histogram binning, a simple nonparametric map, replaces each score by the fraction of positives among calibration
cases in the same score bin <Cite id="zadrozny2001" />. Binary maps extend to many classes by calibrating each class
against the rest and renormalising, as Zadrozny and Elkan proposed <Cite id="zadrozny2002" />. That handles each class's marginal probability separately and ignores how the
classes interact, which is why genuinely multiclass maps such as Dirichlet calibration exist.

## Choosing a map

| map | parameters | contains identity | shapes | data needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| logistic (Platt) | 2 | no, for probability inputs | sigmoids in the score | little |
| beta | 3 | yes | sigmoids, inverse sigmoids, skewed maps | little |
| isotonic | up to one per distinct score | yes | any non-decreasing step function | a lot |
| temperature | 1 | yes | uniform sharpening or softening | little |
| Dirichlet | $K(K + 1)$ | yes | linear maps of log-probabilities | moderate, with regularisation |

Parametric maps generalise from few calibration cases but can be the wrong shape. Isotonic regression fits any monotone
distortion but overfits small calibration sets. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana found that Platt scaling beats isotonic regression
for every one of nine learning methods when the calibration set is small, below roughly 200 to 1000 cases
<Cite id="niculescumizil2005" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Calibrating on the training data.** The model's outputs on its own training cases are more confident than on new
  cases. The map learns the wrong correction.
- **Evaluating on the calibration set.** A map evaluated on the data it was fitted to looks better than it is.
- **Calibration under shift.** A map fitted under one class prior or input distribution is not calibrated under
  another. A change of prior alone can be corrected with Bayes' theorem (see
  <NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction">prior-shift correction</NoteLink>); other shifts need new calibration data.
- **Calibrated on average is not calibrated for a subgroup.** Calibration conditions on the model output, not on the
  features. A model can be calibrated overall and miscalibrated for every subgroup of interest.
