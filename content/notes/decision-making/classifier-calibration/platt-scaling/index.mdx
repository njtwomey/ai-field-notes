<Definition>
**Platt scaling** maps a real-valued classifier score $f$ to a probability

$$
P(y = 1 \mid f) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(Af + B)},
$$

with $A$ and $B$ fitted by minimising <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink> on scores of data not used to train the classifier. Platt introduced it to
obtain probabilities from <Gloss name="svm">support vector machines</Gloss> <Cite id="platt1999" />. Applied to a score that is already a
probability it is also called **logistic calibration**.
</Definition>

## Problem

Many classifiers output scores that rank cases well but are not probabilities. A support vector machine outputs a signed
distance from its margin. Boosted trees output sums of votes. Even models that output numbers in $[0, 1]$ may distort
them, such as <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink> or a network trained on rebalanced classes. A decision rule that compares a probability with a
cost threshold needs a probability.

## Mechanism

**Why a sigmoid.** Suppose the score has a <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian distribution</NoteLink> in each class with a common variance:
$f \mid y = 1 \sim \Gauss(\mu_1, \sigma^2)$ and $f \mid y = 0 \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma^2)$, with prior $\pi = P(y = 1)$. By
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, the posterior log-odds is the log prior odds plus the log
likelihood ratio:

$$
\log\frac{P(y = 1 \mid f)}{P(y = 0 \mid f)} = \log\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi} - \frac{(f - \mu_1)^2 - (f - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} =
\frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sigma^2} f - \frac{\mu_1^2 - \mu_0^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}.
$$

The quadratic terms $f^2$ cancel because the variances are equal. The log-odds is linear in $f$, so the posterior is a
logistic function of $f$: Platt's form with $A = -(\mu_1 - \mu_0)/\sigma^2$ and $B$ the negative of the remaining
constant. Platt observed empirically that the class-conditional densities of SVM outputs are roughly like this between
the margins <Cite id="platt1999" />.

**Fitting.** Given held-out pairs $(f_i, y_i)$ and targets $t_i$, minimise the log loss

$$
-\sum_i \brack{t_i \log p_i + (1 - t_i)\log(1 - p_i)}, \qquad p_i = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(Af_i + B)}.
$$

This is one-dimensional <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> of the label on the score. The
objective is convex in $(A, B)$, and <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> converges in a few iterations.

**Regularised targets.** With $N_+$ positives and $N_-$ negatives in the calibration set, Platt replaces the labels 1
and 0 by

$$
t_+ = \frac{N_+ + 1}{N_+ + 2}, \qquad t_- = \frac{1}{N_- + 2}.
$$

These are the posterior means of a Bernoulli probability under a uniform prior after observing all-positive or
all-negative outcomes (Laplace's rule of succession). They stop the fit from driving $\abs{A}$ to infinity when the
calibration data are separable <Cite id="platt1999" />.

**Held-out scores.** The scores used to fit $A$ and $B$ must come from data the classifier was not trained on: a
separate calibration set, or out-of-fold scores from cross-validation. An SVM's training outputs cluster at the margins
$\pm 1$ and would give a sigmoid that is far too steep.

## Probability inputs and the identity

When the input is itself a probability $s \in [0, 1]$, logistic calibration fits $\sigma(\gamma s + \delta)$, with
$\sigma$ the logistic function. The identity map $s \mapsto s$ is not in this family, since the logit of the output
would have to equal $\log\paren{s/(1 - s)}$, which is not linear in $s$. Applied to an already calibrated model, logistic
calibration therefore makes it worse <Cite id="kull2017" />. The fix is to feed it the log-odds of the score,
$\sigma(\gamma \log\frac{s}{1 - s} + \delta)$, which contains the identity at $\gamma = 1$, $\delta = 0$. That is the
special case $a = b$ of <NoteLink to="beta-calibration">beta calibration</NoteLink>.

## Cost

Two parameters, fitted by a convex optimisation with a handful of Newton steps. Prediction costs one exponential.
Because it has so few parameters, Platt scaling needs little calibration data. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana found it
beat <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic regression</NoteLink> for all nine learning methods they tried when the calibration set had fewer than roughly 200 to
1000 cases, and it corrected the sigmoid-shaped distortions of boosted trees and SVMs well
<Cite id="niculescumizil2005" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Right shape or wrong shape.** The sigmoid is exact only under the equal-variance Gaussian model. Distortions of
  other shapes, such as the inverse-sigmoid curve of an underconfident model, or a skewed curve, cannot be fitted.
  <NoteLink to="isotonic-calibration">Isotonic calibration</NoteLink> fits any monotone shape at the cost of more data.
- **Monotone by construction.** With $A < 0$ the map is increasing, so rankings, the <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">ROC curve and AUC</NoteLink> are unchanged.
- **Multiclass use.** Fitting one sigmoid per class against the rest and renormalising works, but ignores interactions
  between classes. <NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">Temperature scaling</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="dirichlet-calibration">Dirichlet calibration</NoteLink> are the multiclass counterparts.

## Worked example

**From the Gaussian model.** Scores are $\Gauss(1, 1)$ for positives and $\Gauss(-1, 1)$ for negatives, with equal
priors. The log-odds is $(1 - (-1))/1 \cdot f - (1 - 1)/2 + 0 = 2f$, so $A = -2$, $B = 0$. A score of $f = 0.5$ maps to
$1/(1 + e^{-1}) = 0.731$, and a score of $0$, midway between the class means, maps to $0.5$.

**Regularised targets.** A calibration set has $N_+ = 30$ positives and $N_- = 70$ negatives. The targets are
$t_+ = 31/32 = 0.969$ and $t_- = 1/72 = 0.014$. Even a score far on the positive side is fitted towards $0.969$, not 1.
