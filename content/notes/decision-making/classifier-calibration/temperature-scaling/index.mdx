<Definition>
A $K$-class network outputs logits $\zvec \in \reals^K$ and probabilities $\operatorname{softmax}(\zvec)$.
**Temperature scaling** replaces them by

$$
\qvec = \operatorname{softmax}(\zvec / T), \qquad q_k = \frac{\exp(z_k / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)},
$$

with a single temperature $T > 0$ fitted by minimising the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink> of $\qvec$ on a held-out validation set. Guo, Pleiss,
Sun and Weinberger introduced it for neural networks and found it the most effective of the post-hoc methods they
compared <Cite id="guo2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Guo et al. found that modern deep networks are overconfident: their top-class probability is on average higher than
their accuracy, and more so for deeper and wider networks trained with <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> and little <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>
<Cite id="guo2017" />. Training continues to reduce log loss on the training set after accuracy saturates, which it does
by making the logits larger. The probabilities on new data end up too close to 0 and 1. Multiclass calibration maps with
many parameters can fix this but risk overfitting a validation set that is small relative to $K$.

## Mechanism

**The predicted class does not change.** Dividing every logit by the same positive $T$ preserves their order, so
$\argmax_k q_k = \argmax_k z_k$. Accuracy, and every metric that depends only on the predicted class, is unchanged.
Temperature scaling changes only how confident the probabilities are.

**What $T$ does.** For $T > 1$ the logits shrink towards each other and the distribution becomes flatter. For $T < 1$
it becomes sharper. As $T \to \infty$ the probabilities tend to uniform, and as $T \to 0$ to a one-hot vector on the
largest logit.

**Fitting is a convex one-dimensional problem.** Write $\beta = 1/T$. The log loss of one case with label $y$ is

$$
\ell(\beta) = -\beta z_y + \log\sum_j \exp(\beta z_j).
$$

The log-sum-exp of a linear function of $\beta$ is convex, and $-\beta z_y$ is linear, so $\ell$ is convex in $\beta$,
and so is its average over the validation set. Its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is

$$
\ell'(\beta) = -z_y + \sum_j q_j z_j,
$$

where $q_j$ is the softmax at $\beta$. Setting the average derivative to zero gives the optimality condition:
**the average of $\expect_{\qvec}[z]$, the expected logit under the calibrated probabilities, equals the average logit
of the true class**. A one-dimensional search (<NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink>, or bisection on $\ell'$) finds it in a few steps.

**Vector and matrix scaling.** Guo et al. also tried $\qvec = \operatorname{softmax}(\Wmat\zvec + \bvec)$ with diagonal
$\Wmat$ (vector scaling) or full $\Wmat$ (matrix scaling). Both reduce to temperature scaling when $\Wmat = \eye/T$ and
$\bvec = \zeros$. They have $2K$ and $K^2 + K$ parameters and overfit more easily <Cite id="guo2017" />.
<NoteLink to="dirichlet-calibration">Dirichlet calibration</NoteLink> applies the same <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink> to log-probabilities
and adds regularisation to control that.

## Cost

One parameter, fitted once on validation logits; prediction costs one division per logit. Nothing about the network
changes and no retraining is needed.

## Trade-offs

- **A single parameter corrects only uniform miscalibration.** Temperature scaling cannot fix a model
  that is overconfident in some regions and underconfident in others, or miscalibrated for some classes only. It targets
  <NoteLink to="calibration-definitions">confidence calibration</NoteLink>; classwise miscalibration can remain
  <Cite id="kull2019" />.
- **Binary case.** For two classes, temperature scaling is <NoteLink to="platt-scaling">logistic calibration</NoteLink> of the logit with no intercept,
  $\sigma(z/T)$. It cannot shift the probabilities, only stretch them.
- **Distribution shift.** $T$ is fitted to the validation distribution. Under a shift in inputs the network's
  overconfidence usually changes and the fitted $T$ is no longer right.

## Worked example

**Effect of $T$.** Logits $(2, 1, 0)$ give probabilities $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$ at $T = 1$, $(0.506, 0.307, 0.186)$
at $T = 2$, and $(0.867, 0.117, 0.016)$ at $T = 0.5$. The first class is predicted in all three cases.

**Fitting $T$.** A validation set has ten cases in three groups:

| logits | cases | true classes | accuracy | confidence at $T = 1$ | at $T = 1.466$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(3, 0, 0)$ | 4 | 1, 1, 1, 2 | 0.75 | 0.909 | 0.795 |
| $(0, 2, 0)$ | 3 | 2, 2, 1 | 0.67 | 0.787 | 0.662 |
| $(0, 0, 1)$ | 3 | 3, 3, 2 | 0.67 | 0.576 | 0.497 |

Minimising the mean log loss gives $T = 1.466$, reducing it from $0.875$ to $0.835$. At that temperature the average
expected logit and the average true-class logit are both $1.5$, as the optimality condition requires. The first two
groups are now close to calibrated. The third group, which was underconfident at $T = 1$, has become more so, because
one temperature must serve all three.
