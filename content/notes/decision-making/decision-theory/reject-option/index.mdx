<Definition>
A classifier with a **reject option** has $K + 1$ actions: predict one of the $K$ classes, or reject (abstain, defer
to a human). A wrong prediction costs 1, a correct one costs 0, and a rejection costs $d \in [0, 1]$ whatever the class.
**Chow's rule** is the Bayes decision rule for this loss: with posterior probabilities $p_k = P(y = k \mid \xvec)$,

$$
\delta^*(\xvec) =
\begin{cases}
\argmax_k p_k & \text{if } \max_k p_k \ge 1 - d, \\
\text{reject} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

<Cite id="chow1970" />.
</Definition>

## Deriving the rule

The Bayes decision rule takes the action with the smallest conditional risk (see
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes decision rule</NoteLink>). Predicting class $k$ is wrong with probability
$1 - p_k$, so its conditional risk is $R(k \mid \xvec) = 1 - p_k$. Rejecting costs $d$ with certainty. The best
prediction is the most probable class, with risk $1 - \max_k p_k$. Rejecting is better exactly when
$d < 1 - \max_k p_k$, that is, when $\max_k p_k < 1 - d$.

Two limits bound the useful range of $d$. At $d = 0$ rejection is free and every case short of certainty is rejected.
Since $\max_k p_k \ge 1/K$ always, a cost $d \ge 1 - 1/K$ means no case is ever rejected. For two classes that is
$d \ge 1/2$, and for $d < 1/2$ the reject region is the band $d < p < 1 - d$, where $p = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$.

## Unequal error costs

With two classes, a <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss> cost $c_{\text{FP}}$, a <Gloss name="fn">false negative</Gloss> cost $c_{\text{FN}}$ and a rejection cost $d$,
the three conditional risks are $p\, c_{\text{FN}}$ (predict 0), $(1 - p)\, c_{\text{FP}}$ (predict 1) and $d$ (reject).
Predicting 0 beats rejecting when $p \le d / c_{\text{FN}}$. Predicting 1 beats rejecting when $p \ge 1 - d / c_{\text{FP}}$.
The reject region is the interval between them,

$$
\frac{d}{c_{\text{FN}}} < p < 1 - \frac{d}{c_{\text{FP}}},
$$

which is non-empty only if $d\,(1/c_{\text{FN}} + 1/c_{\text{FP}}) < 1$, that is
$d < c_{\text{FP}} c_{\text{FN}} / (c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})$. A rejection more expensive than that is never worth
it: at the hardest posterior, $p^* = c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})$, the best prediction already costs
less.

## The error-reject trade-off

Write $t = 1 - d$ for the acceptance threshold on $\max_k p_k$. Each $t$ gives a reject rate $\rho(t)$, the probability
that $\max_k p_k < t$, and an error rate $E(t)$, the probability of accepting a case and misclassifying it. Lowering $d$
raises $t$, rejects more cases and removes errors. Chow showed that this curve has two properties
<Cite id="chow1970" />.

**The slope is $-d$.** Raising $t$ slightly moves into the reject region the accepted cases whose largest posterior is
just below the new threshold. Each such case has $\max_k p_k \approx t$, so it would have been misclassified with
probability $1 - t = d$. Rejecting an extra probability mass $\Delta\rho$ of cases therefore removes $d\,\Delta\rho$
expected errors, and $dE / d\rho = -d$. The first cases rejected (at $d$ near $1/2$ for two classes) remove about half
an error each; later ones, with posteriors near certainty, remove almost nothing.

**Every point is optimal for its reject rate.** Chow's rule with cost $d$ minimises $E + d\rho$ over all decision
rules, because that sum is its expected loss. Suppose another rule rejected the same fraction $\rho$ with a smaller error
$E' < E$. It would have $E' + d\rho < E + d\rho$, contradicting optimality. So no rule that rejects a fraction $\rho$ of
cases has a lower error rate. The cost $d$ acts as a <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> for a constraint on the reject rate.

## Worked example

**Three classes.** With rejection cost $d = 0.3$, the rule accepts a case when $\max_k p_k \ge 0.7$. A case with
posteriors $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ is rejected: predicting class 1 would cost $1 - 0.5 = 0.5$ in expectation, more than
$0.3$. A case with posteriors $(0.8, 0.1, 0.1)$ is predicted as class 1, at expected cost $0.2$.

**Binormal.** A score $x$ is $\Gauss(0, 1)$ for negatives and $\Gauss(2, 1)$ for positives, with equal priors. The
posterior is $p(x) = \sigma(2x - 2)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Without rejection ($d = 0.5$) the rule
predicts positive for $x \ge 1$ and has error rate $\Phi(-1) = 0.159$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal cdf. With
$d = 0.2$ the reject band is $p \in (0.2, 0.8)$, which is $x \in (0.307, 1.693)$. That rejects 33.4% of cases and cuts
the error rate to 4.5%. The expected loss falls from $0.159$ to $0.045 + 0.2 \times 0.334 = 0.112$. With $d = 0.05$ the
rule rejects 67.5% of cases for an error rate of 0.7%.

## Assumptions

- **Calibrated posteriors.** Chow's rule compares $\max_k p_k$ with a number. An overconfident model reports posteriors
  that are too high, so it rejects too few cases and its error rate on accepted cases is higher than $d$ implies.
  Calibrate first (see <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">classifier calibration</NoteLink>).
- **One rejection cost.** The rule assumes that deferring costs the same for every case. If the expert is slower on hard
  cases or has limited capacity, the problem becomes one of choosing which cases to defer under a budget, which is the
  setting of <NoteLink to="selective-classification">selective classification</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Ambiguity is not novelty.** The rule rejects cases that the model finds ambiguous between classes. An input unlike
  anything in the training data can still receive a confident posterior and be accepted. Detecting such inputs needs a
  measure of epistemic uncertainty or a density model, not the posterior alone (see
  <NoteLink to="aleatoric-and-epistemic-uncertainty">aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty</NoteLink>).
- **Reporting accuracy on accepted cases alone.** Any classifier looks accurate if it rejects enough. Report the error
  rate together with the reject rate, or the whole error-reject curve.
- **Learning with rejection is a different problem.** Training a model to abstain directly, with a surrogate loss for
  the reject option, can do better than thresholding a posterior when the posterior is poorly estimated
  <Cite id="bartlett2008" />.
