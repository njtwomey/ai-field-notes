<Definition>
Let $y \in \Ycal$ be an unknown state, $\xvec$ the observed features and $\Acal$ a set of actions. A loss function
$\ell(a, y) \ge 0$ gives the cost of taking action $a$ when the state is $y$. The **conditional risk** of action $a$ at
$\xvec$ is its posterior expected loss,

$$
R(a \mid \xvec) = \sum_{y \in \Ycal} \ell(a, y)\, P(y \mid \xvec).
$$

The **Bayes decision rule** is $\delta^*(\xvec) = \argmin_{a \in \Acal} R(a \mid \xvec)$. Its expected loss
$R^* = \expect_{\xvec}\brack{\min_a R(a \mid \xvec)}$ is the **Bayes risk**, the lowest expected loss that any decision
rule can achieve <Cite id="bishop2006" />.
</Definition>

## Why the pointwise minimum is optimal

A decision rule $\delta$ maps every feature vector to an action. Its risk is the expected loss over the joint
distribution of $\xvec$ and $y$. By the law of total expectation, this is the average over $\xvec$ of the conditional
risk of the action the rule takes:

$$
R(\delta) = \expect_{\xvec, y}\brack{\ell(\delta(\xvec), y)} = \expect_{\xvec}\brack{R(\delta(\xvec) \mid \xvec)}.
$$

For every $\xvec$, $R(\delta(\xvec) \mid \xvec) \ge \min_a R(a \mid \xvec) = R(\delta^*(\xvec) \mid \xvec)$. Taking
expectations of both sides gives $R(\delta) \ge R(\delta^*)$ for every rule $\delta$. The rule has no global
coupling: minimising the expected loss of the whole rule reduces to minimising the conditional risk at each
$\xvec$ separately. The Bayes rule needs two ingredients, the posterior $P(y \mid \xvec)$ and the loss $\ell$, and
nothing else about the problem.

## Zero-one loss gives the most probable class

When the actions are the classes and every error costs 1, $\ell(a, y) = \indicator[a \ne y]$. Then

$$
R(a \mid \xvec) = \sum_{y \ne a} P(y \mid \xvec) = 1 - P(a \mid \xvec),
$$

which is smallest for the class with the largest posterior. The Bayes rule under zero-one loss is the maximum a
posteriori (MAP) classifier, and its Bayes risk is the lowest achievable error rate, $\expect_{\xvec}[1 - \max_y
P(y \mid \xvec)]$. Predicting the most probable class is therefore a special case, correct only when all errors cost the
same.

## Two classes: a threshold on the posterior

With classes $y \in \set{0, 1}$, write $p = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$. Let a false positive cost $c_{\text{FP}} > 0$, a false
negative cost $c_{\text{FN}} > 0$, and correct decisions cost 0. The two conditional risks are

$$
R(\text{predict } 1 \mid \xvec) = (1 - p)\, c_{\text{FP}}, \qquad R(\text{predict } 0 \mid \xvec) = p\, c_{\text{FN}}.
$$

Predicting positive is optimal when $(1 - p)\, c_{\text{FP}} \le p\, c_{\text{FN}}$. Rearranging gives

$$
p \ge p^* = \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}}}.
$$

The optimal threshold depends on the costs only through their ratio. Equal costs give $p^* = 1/2$. A false negative
nine times as costly as a false positive gives $p^* = 0.1$. A general $2 \times 2$ cost matrix with non-zero costs
for correct decisions reduces to this form by subtracting each column's correct-decision cost; the note on
<NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive learning</NoteLink> works through that reduction
<Cite id="elkan2001" />.

## The same rule as a likelihood-ratio test

Write $\pi_1 = P(y = 1)$ and $\pi_0 = 1 - \pi_1$ for the class priors and $p(\xvec \mid y)$ for the class-conditional
densities. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, $p / (1 - p) = \paren{\pi_1\, p(\xvec \mid 1)} /
\paren{\pi_0\, p(\xvec \mid 0)}$. The condition $(1 - p)\, c_{\text{FP}} \le p\, c_{\text{FN}}$ is the same as
$p / (1 - p) \ge c_{\text{FP}} / c_{\text{FN}}$, which becomes

$$
\Lambda(\xvec) = \frac{p(\xvec \mid 1)}{p(\xvec \mid 0)} \ge \frac{\pi_0\, c_{\text{FP}}}{\pi_1\, c_{\text{FN}}}.
$$

The Bayes rule is a likelihood-ratio test. The threshold on $\Lambda$ combines the priors and the costs into one
number, so a change of prior and a change of costs that leave $\pi_0 c_{\text{FP}} / (\pi_1 c_{\text{FN}})$ fixed give
the same decisions. In ROC space the same number is the slope of the lines of equal expected cost (see
<NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-convex-hull">ROC convex hull</NoteLink>).

## Actions need not be classes

The action set is separate from the label set. A clinic might choose among sending a patient home, prescribing rest and
prescribing antibiotics, when the patient is healthy, has flu or has pneumonia. A classifier can defer a case to a human
at a fixed cost, which gives the <NoteLink to="reject-option">reject option</NoteLink>. The Bayes rule applies unchanged:
compute $R(a \mid \xvec)$ for each action and take the smallest.

## The Bayes risk is concave in the posterior

For two classes, each conditional risk is a linear function of $p$. The minimum conditional risk
$H(p) = \min_a R(a \mid p)$ is a minimum of linear functions, so it is concave and piecewise linear, as in the figure.
Its maximum is at the threshold $p^*$, where the decision is hardest. For zero-one loss $H(p) = \min(p, 1 - p)$. The
function $H$ plays the role of a generalised entropy. Averaging such minimum-risk functions over a range of costs
produces the expected-score functions of <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">proper scoring rules</NoteLink>: the Brier
score is proportional to the average loss of a decision maker who applies the Bayes threshold when the cost proportion
$c_{\text{FP}}/(c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}})$ is uniformly distributed (see
<NoteLink to="threshold-choice-methods">threshold choice methods</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

**Three actions, three states.** A patient is healthy, has flu or has pneumonia. The model gives posterior
probabilities $(0.6, 0.3, 0.1)$. The losses $\ell(a, y)$ are

| action | healthy | flu | pneumonia |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| send home | 0 | 2 | 20 |
| prescribe rest | 1 | 0 | 10 |
| antibiotics | 3 | 3 | 0 |

The conditional risks are $0.3 \times 2 + 0.1 \times 20 = 2.6$ for sending home,
$0.6 \times 1 + 0.1 \times 10 = 1.6$ for rest, and $0.6 \times 3 + 0.3 \times 3 = 2.7$ for antibiotics. The Bayes action
is to prescribe rest, although the most probable state is "healthy". The 10% chance of pneumonia makes sending the
patient home too costly.

**A binormal threshold.** A score $x$ is $\Gauss(0, 1)$ for negatives and $\Gauss(2, 1)$ for positives, with prior
$\pi_1 = 0.2$. A false negative costs 4 and a false positive 1. The likelihood ratio is
$\Lambda(x) = \exp(2x - 2)$ and the threshold on it is $\pi_0 c_{\text{FP}} / (\pi_1 c_{\text{FN}}) = 0.8 / 0.8 = 1$,
so the Bayes rule predicts positive when $x \ge 1$. Its risk per case is

$$
\pi_0\, c_{\text{FP}}\, P(x \ge 1 \mid 0) + \pi_1\, c_{\text{FN}}\, P(x < 1 \mid 1) = 0.8 \times 0.1587 + 0.8 \times
0.1587 = 0.254,
$$

with both probabilities equal to $1 - \Phi(1)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cdf. The accuracy-maximising rule
predicts positive when $p \ge 1/2$, which here means $\Lambda(x) \ge 4$, so $x \ge 1.693$. Under the stated costs that
rule has risk $0.340$, 34% more.

## Assumptions

- **The posterior is correct.** A plug-in rule uses an estimate $\hat p(y \mid \xvec)$. It is optimal only if the
  estimate is calibrated; see <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">classifier calibration</NoteLink>.
- **The losses are known and additive.** The rule minimises the average loss over many decisions. A single
  catastrophic outcome, or a loss that depends on other decisions (a budget, a quota), needs a different formulation.
- **The deployment distribution matches.** If the class priors change between training and deployment, the posterior
  must be adjusted before the rule is applied (see <NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive
  learning</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Treating 0.5 as the default threshold.** It is optimal only for equal error costs and a calibrated model.
- **Reporting accuracy for a cost-sensitive task.** The rule that maximises accuracy can have much higher expected
  cost, as the binormal example shows. Evaluate with the loss the decisions will incur.
- **Scores that are not probabilities.** A margin or a ranking score cannot be compared with $p^*$. Either calibrate it
  or pick the threshold on held-out data with the actual costs.
