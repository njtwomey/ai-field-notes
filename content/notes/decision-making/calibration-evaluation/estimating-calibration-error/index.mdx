<Definition>
For a binary model with prediction $p(\xvec)$, the **$L^r$ calibration error** is

$$
\text{CE}_r = \paren{\expect\abs{\expect[y \mid p(\xvec)] - p(\xvec)}^r}^{1/r}.
$$

$\text{CE}_1$ is the <Gloss name="ece">expected calibration error (ECE)</Gloss>. The **plug-in** (binned) estimator groups $n$ test cases into bins
$B_1, \dots, B_M$ and replaces $\expect[y \mid p]$ by the bin frequency $\bar y_m$ and $p$ by the bin mean $\bar p_m$:
$\widehat{\text{ECE}} = \sum_m \frac{n_m}{n}\abs{\bar y_m - \bar p_m}$, and
$\widehat{\text{CE}}_2^2 = \sum_m \frac{n_m}{n}(\bar y_m - \bar p_m)^2$ <Cite id="naeini2015" /> <Cite id="kumar2019" />.
</Definition>

## Two sources of error

The binned estimator makes two approximations, and they push in opposite directions.

- **Binning bias, downwards.** The estimator measures the calibration of a coarser model, one that predicts $\bar p_m$
  for every case in bin $m$. Averaging within a bin cancels miscalibration of opposite signs. Kumar, Liang and Ma proved
  that, in the population, the binned calibration error is never larger than the true calibration error of a model with
  continuous outputs, and can be much smaller <Cite id="kumar2019" />.
- **Estimation bias, upwards.** With a finite test set, $\bar y_m$ is a noisy estimate of the bin's true frequency. The
  absolute value or square of a noisy gap is positive even when the true gap is zero, so a calibrated model receives a
  positive ECE.

For the moderate test sets common in practice the second effect usually dominates, especially for well-calibrated
models <Cite id="roelofs2022" />.

## Size of the upward bias

Take bin $m$ with $n_m$ cases and write $\Delta_m = \expect[\bar y_m] - \bar p_m$ for its true gap. Given the
predictions, $\bar y_m$ is an average of independent Bernoulli labels, with variance
$\sigma_m^2 = \sum_{i \in B_m} p_i(1 - p_i)/n_m^2 \approx \bar p_m(1 - \bar p_m)/n_m$ for a calibrated model.

**Squared error.** Since $\expect[X^2] = (\expect X)^2 + \var X$,

$$
\expect\brack{(\bar y_m - \bar p_m)^2} = \Delta_m^2 + \sigma_m^2.
$$

The plug-in squared error overestimates by $\sum_m (n_m/n)\sigma_m^2 \approx \sum_m \bar p_m(1 - \bar p_m)/n$. With
roughly equal bin variances this is about $Mv/n$, where $v$ is the average of $p(1 - p)$. The bias grows linearly with
the number of bins and falls as $1/n$.

**Absolute error.** For a calibrated model, $\Delta_m = 0$ and $\bar y_m - \bar p_m$ is approximately
$\Gauss(0, \sigma_m^2)$. A zero-mean normal variable has $\expect\abs{X} = \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$. With equal-mass bins,
$n_m = n/M$ and $\sigma_m \approx \sqrt{vM/n}$, so

$$
\expect\brack{\widehat{\text{ECE}}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{vM}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{2Mv}{\pi n}}.
$$

The bias of the ECE falls only as $1/\sqrt{n}$. Halving it needs four times as many test cases.

## Debiased estimator

For the squared error, the upward bias is a variance, and a variance can be estimated. If the cases in a bin share a
probability $p$, then $\expect[\bar y_m(1 - \bar y_m)] = p(1 - p)(1 - 1/n_m)$, so $\bar y_m(1 - \bar y_m)/(n_m - 1)$ is
unbiased for $\sigma_m^2 = p(1 - p)/n_m$. Subtracting it bin by bin gives the **debiased estimator**

$$
\widehat{\text{CE}}_{2,\text{db}}^2 = \sum_m \frac{n_m}{n}\brack{(\bar y_m - \bar p_m)^2 - \frac{\bar y_m(1 - \bar y_m)}{n_m -
1}}
$$

<Cite id="brocker2012" /> <Cite id="kumar2019" />. It can be negative for a calibrated model, which is the price of
being unbiased. Kumar et al. showed that it needs a number of test cases proportional to $\sqrt{M}$ to reach a given
relative accuracy, where the plug-in estimator needs a number proportional to $M$ <Cite id="kumar2019" />.

## Choosing the bins

Roelofs, Cain, Shlens and Mozer measured the bias of estimators on simulated models whose true calibration error is
known <Cite id="roelofs2022" />. Equal-mass bins had lower bias than equal-width bins for every binning estimator.
They proposed **$\text{ECE}_\text{sweep}$**: use equal-mass bins, and choose the largest number of bins for which the
bin frequencies $\bar y_1, \dots, \bar y_M$ are still non-decreasing. The idea is that the true <NoteLink to="reliability-diagrams-and-consistency-bars">calibration curve</NoteLink> of a
reasonable model is monotone, so a non-monotone binned curve is fitting noise. In their experiments the debiased
estimator was best for perfectly calibrated models and $\text{ECE}_\text{sweep}$ for miscalibrated ones.

## Testing calibration instead of estimating it

A calibration error estimate needs a reference for how large it would be by chance. **Consistency resampling** provides
one: redraw every label from $\Bern(p_i)$, recompute the estimate, and repeat. The resampled values form the
distribution of the estimator under the hypothesis that the model is calibrated, and the proportion at least as large
as the observed value is a $p$-value <Cite id="vaicenavicius2019" />. The same procedure works for confidence and
classwise ECE <Cite id="silvafilho2023" />.

## Worked example

A perfectly calibrated model has predictions spread uniformly on $[0, 1]$, so $v = \expect[p(1 - p)] = 1/6$.

| $n$ | bins $M$ | predicted mean ECE $\sqrt{2Mv/(\pi n)}$ | simulated mean ECE | simulated mean $\widehat{\text{CE}}_2^2$ | predicted $Mv/n$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 10 | 0.103 | 0.101 | 0.0173 | 0.0167 |
| 1000 | 15 | 0.040 | 0.039 | 0.0025 | 0.0025 |
| 10000 | 15 | 0.013 | 0.012 | 0.00025 | 0.00025 |

The simulated values average 300 test sets of equal-width bins. A calibrated model scored on 1000 cases with 15 bins
reports an ECE near $0.04$. A published ECE of $0.03$ on such a test set is indistinguishable from perfect calibration.
The debiased squared estimator averaged within $0.0004$ of zero at every size.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing ECEs across <NoteLink to="metric-confidence-intervals">test-set sizes</NoteLink> or bin counts.** The bias differs, so the numbers are not comparable. Fix both,
  or use a debiased estimator.
- **Treating ECE as a loss to minimise.** ECE is not a proper <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">scoring rule</NoteLink>. Predicting the base rate everywhere gives an
  ECE near zero and no information (see <NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions">decompositions of proper scoring
  rules</NoteLink>).
- **Ignoring the binning bias.** A small binned ECE does not prove a continuous model is calibrated within bins.
