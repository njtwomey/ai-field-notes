<Definition>
Split the predicted probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_n$ of a binary event into bins $B_1, \dots, B_M$. For each bin, let
$\bar p_m$ be the mean prediction and $\bar y_m$ the observed frequency of the event. A **reliability diagram** plots
$\bar y_m$ against $\bar p_m$. A calibrated model's points lie near the diagonal <Cite id="murphy1977" />. **Consistency
bars** mark, for each bin, the range of $\bar y_m$ that a calibrated model would produce by chance, obtained by redrawing
every label from $\Bern(p_i)$ <Cite id="brocker2007" />.
</Definition>

## Reading the diagram

The diagonal is perfect calibration: the event happens in a fraction $p$ of the cases predicted $p$. Points below the
diagonal at high predictions and above it at low ones form a curve flatter than the diagonal: the model is
**overconfident**, its probabilities too close to 0 and 1. A curve steeper than the diagonal means **underconfidence**.
A curve shifted up or down at every point means the model under- or overestimates the base rate. The diagram shows where
a model is miscalibrated, which a single number such as the expected calibration error cannot (see
<NoteLink to="calibration-error">calibration error</NoteLink>).

The diagram shows calibration, not quality. A model that predicts the base rate for every case puts one point on the
diagonal. A histogram of the predictions beneath the diagram shows how spread out they are, their sharpness, and how
many cases stand behind each point <Cite id="niculescumizil2005" />.

## Construction choices

**Where to put each point.** Plotting $\bar y_m$ at the centre of bin $m$ instead of at the mean prediction $\bar p_m$
can make a calibrated model look miscalibrated by up to half a bin width, whenever the predictions within a bin are not
spread evenly <Cite id="brocker2007" />. Plot at $\bar p_m$.

**Equal width or equal mass.** Equal-width bins on $[0, 1]$ are easy to read but leave bins with few cases where
predictions are rare, often at the extremes. Equal-mass bins, each with $n/M$ cases, give every point the same precision
but crowd the points where predictions are dense.

**How many bins.** More bins show more detail and give each point fewer cases. With $n_m$ cases in a bin, the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> of $\bar y_m$ is roughly $\sqrt{\bar p_m(1 - \bar p_m)/n_m}$. Ten bins and a few hundred cases per bin is
a common compromise.

## Consistency bars

A point off the diagonal is evidence of miscalibration only if it is further off than sampling noise would put it.
Bröcker and Smith proposed **consistency resampling** <Cite id="brocker2007" />:

1. Keep the predictions $p_1, \dots, p_n$ and the binning fixed.
2. Draw surrogate labels $\tilde y_i \sim \Bern(p_i)$ independently. These are labels from a world in which the model is
   exactly calibrated.
3. Compute the surrogate observed frequency $\tilde{\bar y}_m$ for each bin.
4. Repeat many times. The 5% and 95% quantiles of $\tilde{\bar y}_m$ form the bar for bin $m$.

A point outside its bar is unlikely under calibration at that bin. Each bar is a pointwise 90% interval, so with ten
bins one bar is expected to be missed by chance even for a calibrated model. Bröcker and Smith also plot the diagram on
**probability paper**, rescaling the $y$-axis so that every bar has the same length, which makes departures comparable
across bins.

The bars have a closed-form approximation. Given the predictions in bin $m$, the surrogate frequency is an average of
independent Bernoulli variables, with mean $\bar p_m$ and variance $\sum_{i \in B_m} p_i(1 - p_i)/n_m^2$. For a bin of
reasonable size a normal approximation gives the bar $\bar p_m \pm 1.645\sqrt{\sum_{i \in B_m} p_i(1 - p_i)}/n_m$.

## Multiclass diagrams

For $K$ classes there are two common one-dimensional diagrams <Cite id="silvafilho2023" />. A **confidence reliability
diagram** bins the top-class probability and plots the accuracy of the predicted class, which checks confidence
calibration. A **classwise reliability diagram** draws one diagram per class, class $k$ against the rest, which checks
classwise calibration. Neither checks the whole probability vector (see <NoteLink to="calibration-definitions">definitions
of classifier calibration</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

A bin holds cases with predictions close to $0.3$, and the event occurred in 40% of them.

- **With 40 cases.** The standard deviation of the observed frequency under calibration is
  $\sqrt{0.3 \times 0.7/40} = 0.072$. The 90% bar is $0.3 \pm 1.645 \times 0.072 = [0.18, 0.42]$. The observed $0.4$ is
  inside: no evidence of miscalibration in this bin.
- **With 400 cases.** The standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.21/400} = 0.023$ and the bar is $[0.26, 0.34]$. The same
  observed $0.4$ is well outside: the model underestimates the probability in this bin.

The point is in the same place in both diagrams. Only the bars tell the two situations apart.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading noise as miscalibration.** Without bars, small bins at the extremes produce wild points that look like
  miscalibration.
- **Hiding miscalibration inside bins.** A bin averages over its cases. A model that is overconfident in one half of a
  bin and underconfident in the other can put a point on the diagonal.
- **Drawing on the calibration set.** A diagram of a calibrated model drawn on the data used to fit its <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">calibration map</NoteLink>
  is optimistic. Use held-out data.
