<Definition>
Let $\Ycal = \set{1, \dots, K}$ be a finite set of outcomes and $\qvec$ a probability vector over it. A **scoring rule**
$S(\qvec, y)$ is the loss charged for reporting $\qvec$ when outcome $y$ occurs. Its <NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink> under a distribution
$\pvec$ is $S(\qvec, \pvec) = \sum_y p_y\, S(\qvec, y)$. The rule is **proper** if

$$
S(\pvec, \pvec) \le S(\qvec, \pvec) \quad \text{for all } \pvec, \qvec,
$$

and **strictly proper** if equality holds only when $\qvec = \pvec$ <Cite id="gneiting2007" />.
</Definition>

This note orients scores as losses, lower being better, which is the convention in machine learning. Gneiting and
Raftery use the opposite orientation, with positive rewards; propriety then means maximisation at $\qvec = \pvec$
<Cite id="gneiting2007" />.

## Propriety as an incentive and as a training loss

A forecaster who believes the outcome has distribution $\pvec$ and is judged by $S$ reports whatever minimises their
expected score. Under a proper rule that report is $\pvec$ itself, so honesty is optimal. Under a strictly proper rule
it is the only optimal report. Propriety makes a scoring rule an incentive to report beliefs as they are
<Cite id="savage1971" />.

The same property makes a scoring rule a sound training loss. Minimising the expected score
$\expect_{\xvec, y}\brack{S(\qvec(\xvec), y)}$ over all functions $\qvec(\xvec)$ splits into minimising
$S(\qvec(\xvec), P(\cdot \mid \xvec))$ separately at each $\xvec$. For a strictly proper rule the unique minimiser is the
true <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink>, $\qvec(\xvec) = P(\cdot \mid \xvec)$. Training a classifier by log loss or Brier score
therefore targets calibrated probabilities, given a flexible enough model and enough data.

## Log loss is strictly proper

The log loss is $S(\qvec, y) = -\log q_y$. The excess expected loss of reporting $\qvec$ instead of $\pvec$ is

$$
S(\qvec, \pvec) - S(\pvec, \pvec) = -\sum_y p_y \log q_y + \sum_y p_y \log p_y = \sum_y p_y \log\frac{p_y}{q_y} = \KL(\pvec
\,\Vert\, \qvec).
$$

The <Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss> is non-negative by Jensen's inequality applied to the concave logarithm:
$-\KL(\pvec \,\Vert\, \qvec) = \sum_y p_y \log(q_y/p_y) \le \log \sum_y p_y (q_y / p_y) = \log \sum_y q_y \le 0$. Since
the logarithm is strictly concave, equality requires $q_y / p_y$ to be constant over the outcomes with $p_y > 0$, and
that constant must be 1, so $\qvec = \pvec$. The log loss is strictly proper.

## The Brier score is strictly proper

The Brier score is $S(\qvec, y) = \norm{\qvec - \evec_y}^2$, where $\evec_y$ is the one-hot vector for outcome $y$
<Cite id="brier1950" />. Since $\expect_{y \sim \pvec}[\evec_y] = \pvec$, adding and subtracting $\pvec$ inside the norm
gives

$$
S(\qvec, \pvec) = \expect_y\norm{(\qvec - \pvec) + (\pvec - \evec_y)}^2 = \norm{\qvec - \pvec}^2 + 2\inner{\qvec -
\pvec}{\expect_y[\pvec - \evec_y]} + \expect_y\norm{\pvec - \evec_y}^2.
$$

The middle term is zero because $\expect_y[\evec_y] = \pvec$. So $S(\qvec, \pvec) - S(\pvec, \pvec) = \norm{\qvec -
\pvec}^2$, which is positive for every $\qvec \ne \pvec$.

## The spherical score is strictly proper

The spherical loss is $S(\qvec, y) = 1 - q_y / \norm{\qvec}$. Its expected value is $1 - \inner{\pvec}{\qvec} /
\norm{\qvec}$. By the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink> $\inner{\pvec}{\qvec} \le \norm{\pvec}\norm{\qvec}$, with equality only
when $\qvec$ is a positive multiple of $\pvec$. Two probability vectors that are multiples of each other are equal. So
the expected loss is at least $1 - \norm{\pvec}$ and reaches it only at $\qvec = \pvec$.

## An improper rule: the linear score

The linear loss $S(\qvec, y) = 1 - q_y$ charges less the more probability the report gave to what
happened. Its expected value is $1 - \inner{\pvec}{\qvec}$, which is linear in $\qvec$ and is minimised at a vertex of the
simplex: put all probability on the most likely outcome. The linear loss rewards overconfidence. For two outcomes it
equals the absolute error $\abs{y - q}$ between the label and the reported probability of the positive class, so <Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean
absolute error</Gloss> of probabilities is improper too.

## Every binary proper rule is a mixture of cost-weighted losses

For two outcomes write $q$ for the reported probability of $y = 1$. For a cost proportion $c \in (0, 1)$, a <Gloss name="fp">false
positive</Gloss> costs $c$ and a <Gloss name="fn">false negative</Gloss> $1 - c$. The Bayes decision rule predicts positive when the probability is at
least $c$ (see <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes decision rule</NoteLink>). Charging a report $q$ the cost of
acting on it gives the **cost-weighted loss**

$$
\ell_c(q, y) = c\,\indicator[y = 0,\ q \ge c] + (1 - c)\,\indicator[y = 1,\ q < c].
$$

Each $\ell_c$ is proper. Under a true probability $p$, reporting $q$ leads to predicting positive if $q \ge c$, with
expected cost $c(1 - p)$, or negative, with expected cost $(1 - c)p$. The first is no worse exactly when $p \ge c$, and
the report $q = p$ makes that choice. So $q = p$ minimises the expected value of every $\ell_c$ at once.

A mixture $S(q, y) = \int_0^1 \ell_c(q, y)\, \nu(dc)$ with a non-negative weight $\nu$ is therefore proper, because the
honest report minimises every term of the integral. Schervish showed the converse: every proper scoring rule for a
binary outcome is such a mixture, up to terms that do not depend on the report <Cite id="schervish1989" />. If $\nu$
puts positive weight on every interval, the rule is strictly proper. The two common rules are:

- **Brier score**, $\nu(dc) = 2\,dc$: for $y = 1$, $\int_q^1 2(1 - c)\,dc = (1 - q)^2$; for $y = 0$,
  $\int_0^q 2c\,dc = q^2$.
- **Log loss**, $\nu(dc) = dc / \paren{c(1 - c)}$: for $y = 1$, $\int_q^1 dc / c = -\ln q$; for $y = 0$,
  $\int_0^q dc / (1 - c) = -\ln(1 - q)$.

A proper score is thus an average decision cost, over a distribution of cost ratios the forecaster does not know in
advance. The Brier score weights all cost proportions equally. The log loss weights extreme cost proportions, near 0
and 1, much more heavily, which is why it punishes confident mistakes so hard (see
<NoteLink to="threshold-choice-methods">threshold choice methods</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

An event has true probability $p = 0.7$. Three reports are scored in expectation, using the two-class Brier score
$\norm{\qvec - \evec_y}^2$:

| report $\qvec$ | log loss | Brier score | linear loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(0.7, 0.3)$, honest | $0.611$ | $0.420$ | $0.420$ |
| $(0.8, 0.2)$ | $0.639$ | $0.440$ | $0.380$ |
| $(1, 0)$ | $\infty$ | $0.600$ | $0.300$ |

The log and Brier losses are lowest for the honest report. The Brier excess for $(0.8, 0.2)$ is
$\norm{\qvec - \pvec}^2 = 0.1^2 + 0.1^2 = 0.02$, as derived above. The linear loss prefers the exaggerated reports and
is lowest for certainty.

## Beyond finite outcomes

For a real-valued outcome with predictive distribution $F$, the log score $-\log f(y)$ of the density and the
continuous ranked probability score are strictly proper among distributions with a density and a finite mean,
respectively (see <NoteLink to="continuous-ranked-probability-score-and-interval-scores">CRPS and interval scores</NoteLink>). Gneiting and Raftery give
the general theory, which characterises every proper scoring rule through a <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> of the reported
distribution <Cite id="gneiting2007" />; the note on <NoteLink to="bregman-divergences-and-scoring-rules">Bregman
divergences and scoring rules</NoteLink> derives that representation.

## Pitfalls

- **Propriety is a property of the expectation.** A single case can score better with a dishonest report. The guarantee
  is about the average over outcomes drawn from the true distribution.
- **Proper is not the same as informative.** Reporting the base rate for every case is honest if that is all one knows,
  and scores well on calibration while carrying no information about individual cases. The
  <NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions">decompositions</NoteLink> of a proper score separate the two.
- **Accuracy, <Gloss name="ece" /> and AUC are not proper scoring rules.** None of them is minimised in expectation only by the true
  probabilities.
