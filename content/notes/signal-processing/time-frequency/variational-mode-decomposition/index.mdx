<Definition>
**Variational mode decomposition** (VMD) writes a real signal $x(t)$ as a sum of $K$ modes $u_k(t)$, each concentrated
around a centre frequency $\omega_k$, by solving

$$
\min_{\{u_k\}, \{\omega_k\}} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \norm{\partial_t \brack{\paren{\delta(t) + \frac{i}{\pi t}} * u_k(t)}
e^{-i\omega_k t}}_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{k=1}^{K} u_k = x .
$$

The <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> with $\delta(t) + i/(\pi t)$ forms the analytic signal of $u_k$, the factor $e^{-i\omega_k t}$ shifts its
spectrum down to zero frequency, and the squared norm of the time <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> measures the bandwidth left
<Cite id="dragomiretskiy2014" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="empirical-mode-decomposition">Empirical mode decomposition</NoteLink> extracts modes one after another by
an algorithm with no objective. Its errors propagate: a poorly extracted first IMF distorts the residue from which all
later IMFs are sifted. It is sensitive to noise, because noise adds extrema. It cannot separate two tones whose
frequencies are within a factor of about 1.5 <Cite id="rilling2008" />. VMD keeps the aim of EMD, a few
narrowband modes whose frequencies are found from the data, but defines the modes as the minimiser of a stated
objective and extracts all of them at once.

## Mechanism

### Measuring a mode's bandwidth

Let $\hat u_k(\omega)$ be the Fourier transform of a real mode $u_k$. Its analytic signal
$u_k^{+} = \paren{\delta + i/(\pi t)} * u_k$ has the spectrum $2\hat u_k(\omega)$ for $\omega > 0$ and zero for
$\omega < 0$; see <NoteLink to="hilbert-transform">Hilbert transform</NoteLink>. Multiplying by $e^{-i\omega_k t}$
shifts that spectrum left by $\omega_k$, and differentiating in time multiplies the spectrum by $i\omega$. By
<NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">Parseval's theorem</NoteLink>, with the transform normalised so that
$\norm{g}_2^2 = \int \abs{\hat g(\omega)}^2 d\omega$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\norm{\partial_t \brack{u_k^{+}(t)\, e^{-i\omega_k t}}}_2^2
&= \int_{-\omega_k}^{\infty} \abs{i\omega \cdot 2\hat u_k(\omega + \omega_k)}^2 d\omega \\
&= 4\int_0^\infty (\omega - \omega_k)^2 \abs{\hat u_k(\omega)}^2 d\omega .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the second moment of the mode's one-sided power spectrum about $\omega_k$. It is small only when the mode's
power sits close to $\omega_k$, so minimising it makes each mode narrowband around its own centre frequency. A tone at
$\omega_k$ costs nothing; an amplitude or frequency modulation costs in proportion to the square of its spread.

### The augmented Lagrangian

The constraint $\sum_k u_k = x$ is enforced with a quadratic penalty and a <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> $\lambda(t)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(\{u_k\}, \{\omega_k\}, \lambda) ={}& \alpha \sum_k \norm{\partial_t \brack{u_k^{+} e^{-i\omega_k t}}}_2^2 \\
&+ \norm{x - \sum_k u_k}_2^2 + \left\langle \lambda,\, x - \sum_k u_k \right\rangle .
\end{aligned}
$$

The weight $\alpha > 0$ balances narrow bands against fidelity to $x$. VMD minimises $\mathcal{L}$ over each $u_k$ in
turn with the others fixed, then over each $\omega_k$, then takes a <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> ascent step on $\lambda$: the alternating
direction method of multipliers (ADMM) <Cite id="boyd2011" />; see
<NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">penalty and barrier methods</NoteLink>. Each step has a closed form in the
frequency domain.

### Mode update: a Wiener filter

Fix everything except $u_k$ and write $\hat r_k(\omega) = \hat x(\omega) - \sum_{i \ne k} \hat u_i(\omega) + \hat\lambda(\omega)/2$
for the residual that mode $k$ should explain. <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">Completing the square</NoteLink> turns the last two terms of $\mathcal{L}$ into
$\norm{r_k - u_k}_2^2$ plus a constant. By Parseval, and because a real signal's spectrum is conjugate-symmetric so that
the integral over all frequencies is twice the integral over $\omega > 0$, the objective in $u_k$ becomes

$$
\int_0^\infty \brack{4\alpha (\omega - \omega_k)^2 \abs{\hat u_k(\omega)}^2 + 2 \abs{\hat r_k(\omega) - \hat u_k(\omega)}^2} d\omega .
$$

The integrand involves $\hat u_k$ at one frequency only, so each frequency is minimised separately. Setting the
derivative with respect to $\hat u_k^*(\omega)$ to zero gives
$4\alpha(\omega - \omega_k)^2 \hat u_k - 2(\hat r_k - \hat u_k) = 0$, so

$$
\hat u_k(\omega) = \frac{\hat x(\omega) - \sum_{i \ne k} \hat u_i(\omega) + \hat\lambda(\omega)/2}{1 + 2\alpha(\omega - \omega_k)^2},
\qquad \omega > 0 .
$$

This is a <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filter</NoteLink> applied to the residual. A Wiener filter passes
frequency $\omega$ with gain $S(\omega)/(S(\omega) + N(\omega))$, where $S$ and $N$ are the signal and noise power. The
gain above has that form with $S/N = 1/\paren{2\alpha(\omega - \omega_k)^2}$: the update treats the mode as a signal
whose power falls off as the inverse square of the distance from $\omega_k$, observed in flat noise. The filter passes
$\omega_k$ unchanged and falls to half its gain at $\omega_k \pm 1/\sqrt{2\alpha}$, so $\alpha$ sets every mode's
bandwidth. Large $\alpha$ gives narrow modes and strong noise rejection; small $\alpha$ gives wide modes that can follow
modulation but also let noise in.

### Centre-frequency update: a centre of gravity

With the modes fixed, $\omega_k$ appears only in the bandwidth term, $\int_0^\infty (\omega - \omega_k)^2 \abs{\hat u_k(\omega)}^2 d\omega$.
Its derivative in $\omega_k$ is $-2\int_0^\infty (\omega - \omega_k)\abs{\hat u_k}^2 d\omega$, which is zero at

$$
\omega_k = \frac{\int_0^\infty \omega\, \abs{\hat u_k(\omega)}^2 d\omega}{\int_0^\infty \abs{\hat u_k(\omega)}^2 d\omega},
$$

the centre of gravity of the mode's power spectrum. Each iteration therefore filters the residual around the current
centre frequency and moves the centre frequency to where the filtered power lies. A centre frequency near a spectral
peak is pulled onto it.

### Multiplier update and the full iteration

The multiplier takes a dual ascent step, $\hat\lambda \leftarrow \hat\lambda + \tau\paren{\hat x - \sum_k \hat u_k}$,
which drives the modes to reproduce $x$ exactly. For noisy signals exact reconstruction would force the noise into the
modes, so $\tau = 0$ is used: the multiplier stays zero and the unexplained part is left out.

The whole algorithm works on the one-sided spectrum of the signal. Starting from $\hat u_k = 0$, $\hat\lambda = 0$ and
initial centre frequencies (zero, or spread evenly over the band), repeat the mode updates for $k = 1, \ldots, K$
(each using the modes already updated in this sweep), the centre-frequency updates and the multiplier update, until the
change in the modes between sweeps, $\sum_k \norm{\hat u_k^{n+1} - \hat u_k^{n}}^2$ (relative to
$\norm{\hat u_k^{n}}^2$ in the paper), falls below a tolerance. The
modes are the inverse transforms of the $\hat u_k$, extended to negative frequencies by conjugate symmetry. The
reference implementation first mirrors half of the signal onto each end to reduce boundary effects, and crops the
modes afterwards <Cite id="dragomiretskiy2014" />.

**Worked example.** Take $x[n] = \cos(2\pi \cdot 0.02\, n) + 0.5\cos(2\pi \cdot 0.1\, n) + 0.3\cos(2\pi \cdot 0.3\, n)$
for 512 samples, and run VMD with $K = 3$, $\tau = 0$, centre frequencies starting at 0, 1/6 and 1/3 cycles per sample,
and the reference code's bandwidth weight of 2,000 (the code writes the filter as $1/\paren{1 + \alpha(f - f_k)^2}$
with $f$ in cycles per sample). After one iteration the centre frequencies are 0.019, 0.104 and 0.300. After two they
are within 0.0003 of the tones, and after seven the change falls below $10^{-7}$ with the centre frequencies at
0.0197, 0.0998 and 0.2999. With $K = 2$ the modes lock onto the tones at
0.02 and 0.3, and the tone at 0.1 is left out: with $\tau = 0$ nothing forces it into a mode. With $K = 4$ the tone at
0.3 is split between two modes centred at 0.2997 and 0.3010.

## Comparison with EMD

- **Non-recursive.** VMD updates all modes together, so an error in one mode does not propagate into the next the way a
  bad IMF propagates into every later residue in EMD.
- **Bandwidth-limited modes.** A VMD mode is narrowband by construction, with a bandwidth set by $\alpha$. An EMD IMF
  is defined by its extrema, so it can be a nonlinear waveform, such as a wave with sharp crests, whose Fourier
  spectrum has harmonics. VMD splits such a waveform into harmonics, as a Fourier analysis would, whereas EMD keeps it
  whole.
- **Tone separation.** VMD separates tones that EMD merges. For two unit tones over 512 samples, the first IMF of EMD
  misses the faster tone by 100% relative error at frequencies 0.10 and 0.12 cycles per sample, while VMD with $K = 2$
  recovers both tones to within 0.3%. At 0.10 and 0.11 the error is 3.7%, and at 0.10 and 0.105, a separation of 2.6
  <Gloss name="dft" /> bins, it is 12%.
- **Robustness to noise.** The Wiener-like update attenuates noise away from each centre frequency, and $\tau = 0$
  leaves the rest unexplained, whereas in EMD noise creates extrema and IMFs of its own.
- **Choices made by the user.** VMD needs the number of modes $K$ and the bandwidth weight $\alpha$. EMD needs neither,
  and its number of IMFs follows from the data. A wrong $K$ drops or splits components.
- **An objective, but not a convex one.** The problem is convex in the modes for fixed centre frequencies, but not
  jointly. Different initial centre frequencies can converge to different decompositions.

## Cost

One <Gloss name="fft" /> of the mirrored signal, of length $2N$, then $O(KN)$ operations per iteration, plus one inverse FFT per mode.
Tens to a few hundred iterations are typical. Close centre frequencies and small $\alpha$ converge slowest: the two
close tones at 0.10 and 0.105 need 410 iterations at $\alpha = 2{,}000$ to reach a tolerance of $10^{-9}$, and 1,433 at
$\alpha = 500$.

## Trade-offs

- **Choosing $K$.** Common heuristics increase $K$ until two centre frequencies converge to nearly the same value, or
  inspect the spectrum for peaks. Neither is guaranteed.
- **Stationary modes.** Each mode has one centre frequency for the whole record. A chirp sweeping a wide band is
  either split across several modes or penalised heavily; EMD and synchrosqueezing follow it with one component.
- **Boundary effects.** Mirroring reduces, but does not remove, distortion at the ends of the record.
- **Many variants.** Extensions adapt the idea to two-dimensional images, to multichannel data and to time-varying
  centre frequencies, at the cost of more parameters.
