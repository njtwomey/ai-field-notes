<Definition>
**Empirical mode decomposition** (EMD) writes a signal as a finite sum of **intrinsic mode functions** (IMFs) and a
residue, $x(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} c_j(t) + r(t)$. An IMF is a function whose numbers of extrema and zero crossings differ
by at most one, and whose upper and lower envelopes are symmetric about zero. The IMFs are extracted one at a time by
**sifting**, from the fastest oscillation to the slowest, and the residue is a trend with at most one extremum
<Cite id="huang1998" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The <NoteLink to="instantaneous-frequency">instantaneous frequency</NoteLink> of a signal is meaningful only when the
signal has one oscillating component at each time. A sum of components gives a phase <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> that oscillates at the
beat frequency and can go negative. Band-pass filtering can split a signal into components, but only if the bands are
fixed in advance, and a component whose frequency drifts, or whose waveform is not sinusoidal, then leaks across
several bands. EMD splits the signal using its own extrema instead. The fastest oscillation present at each time is
separated from the slower motion it rides on, whatever its frequency, and the procedure repeats on what is left.

## Mechanism

### Intrinsic mode functions

Huang et al. define an IMF by two conditions <Cite id="huang1998" />:

1. over the whole signal, the number of local extrema and the number of zero crossings are equal or differ by one;
2. at every time, the mean of the upper envelope (through the local maxima) and the lower envelope (through the local
   minima) is zero.

The first condition forbids riding waves: an oscillation whose local minima are positive, or whose local maxima are
negative, adds extrema without adding zero crossings. The second condition makes the oscillation locally symmetric
about zero. Together they make an IMF a zero-mean oscillation with one extremum between consecutive zero crossings,
whose analytic signal has a well-behaved phase. A pure tone, an amplitude-modulated tone with a slow envelope and a
chirp are IMFs. A tone plus a constant offset is not.

### The sifting algorithm

Sifting extracts one IMF from a signal $x$. Start with $h_0 = x$ and, for $k = 1, 2, \ldots$:

1. **Find the extrema.** Record the indices of the local maxima of $h_{k-1}$ (samples larger than both neighbours) and
   of its local minima. A flat run of equal samples counts as one extremum at its middle. If there are fewer than three
   extrema in total, $h_{k-1}$ has no oscillation left and sifting stops without an IMF.
2. **Extend the extrema past the ends.** Reflect the two maxima and two minima nearest each end about a symmetry
   point, so that the envelopes are interpolated over the whole signal rather than extrapolated. The rule is given
   under end effects below.
3. **Build the envelopes.** Interpolate the maxima, with the reflected ones, by a cubic spline to get the upper envelope
   $u_k(t)$. Interpolate the minima in the same way to get the lower envelope $l_k(t)$.
4. **Subtract the local mean.** Form $m_k(t) = \tfrac12\paren{u_k(t) + l_k(t)}$ and set $h_k = h_{k-1} - m_k$.
5. **Test for an IMF.** Stop if a stopping criterion holds. Otherwise repeat from step 1 with $h_k$.

The first IMF is $c_1 = h_k$ at the stop. The next is sifted from $r_1 = x - c_1$, and in general $c_{j+1}$ is sifted
from $r_j = r_{j-1} - c_j$. The decomposition ends when the residue has fewer than three extrema, or when its range or
mean absolute value falls below a small tolerance. By construction the IMFs and the residue add up to $x$ exactly.

**Why subtracting the envelope mean isolates the fast component.** Take two tones,
$x(t) = \cos(\omega_1 t) + a\cos(\omega_2 t)$, with $0 < \omega_2 < \omega_1$ and amplitude ratio $a > 0$. Write
$f = \omega_2/\omega_1 < 1$. The derivative is $x'(t) = -\omega_1\sin(\omega_1 t) - a\omega_2\sin(\omega_2 t)$. At the
times where $\sin(\omega_1 t) = \pm 1$, the first term has magnitude $\omega_1$ and the second at most $a\omega_2$. When
$af < 1$, the sign of $x'$ at those times is set by the fast tone, so $x'$ changes sign between each such pair of
times: $x$ has an extremum in every half period of the fast tone. When $\omega_2 \ll \omega_1$, those extrema sit
where $\cos(\omega_1 t) = \pm 1$, and the maxima take the values $1 + a\cos(\omega_2 t)$ while the minima take
$-1 + a\cos(\omega_2 t)$. The two envelopes are then $\pm 1 + a\cos(\omega_2 t)$, their mean is $a\cos(\omega_2 t)$,
and $h_1 = x - m_1 = \cos(\omega_1 t)$. The envelopes sample the slow tone only once per period of the fast tone, so
this works only while the slow tone is slow enough to be interpolated from those samples. As $f$ grows towards one
half and beyond, the envelope mean misses part of the slow tone, and further sifts cannot recover it.

### Cubic-spline envelopes

The envelopes are <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">interpolating cubic splines</NoteLink>. A cubic spline
through $K$ knots is cubic between knots and twice continuously differentiable, and its second derivatives at the knots
solve a tridiagonal linear system in $O(K)$ operations. Huang et al. chose cubic splines because the envelopes, and so
the mean, are then smooth enough that subtracting the mean adds no new extrema of its own <Cite id="huang1998" />.
Rilling, Flandrin and Gonçalvès report that linear or polynomial interpolation needs more sifts and spreads single
components over adjacent IMFs <Cite id="rilling2003" />.

The end conditions of the spline matter less once the extrema are mirrored, because the ends of the envelope then lie
outside the signal. Implementations differ: the widget below uses not-a-knot conditions (a single cubic across the
first two and the last two intervals), which is SciPy's default, and natural conditions when there are only three
knots. A cubic spline can overshoot between widely spaced knots, so an upper envelope can dip below the signal. Shape-
preserving interpolants such as <NoteLink to="monotone-and-shape-preserving-interpolation">PCHIP</NoteLink> or Akima
splines avoid the overshoot but are only once differentiable.

### Stopping criteria

Sifting forever drives $h_k$ towards a signal of constant amplitude, which erases genuine amplitude modulation, so each
IMF needs a stopping rule. Three rules are in common use.

- **The SD criterion** <Cite id="huang1998" />. Stop when the size of the last change, relative to the signal,
  $\mathrm{SD}_k = \sum_t \abs{h_{k-1}(t) - h_k(t)}^2 / h_{k-1}(t)^2$, falls below a threshold, typically 0.2 to 0.3.
  The pointwise ratio is unstable where $h_{k-1}$ crosses zero, so implementations often use the ratio of sums
  $\sum_t (h_{k-1} - h_k)^2 / \sum_t h_{k-1}^2$ instead. The SD criterion does not check the IMF conditions.
- **The S-number** <Cite id="huang1999" />. Stop once the numbers of extrema and zero crossings have differed by at most
  one for $S$ consecutive sifts. This checks the first IMF condition directly and leaves the second to a fixed amount
  of extra sifting. Huang et al. studied the spread of the resulting Hilbert spectra over $S$ and recommended values
  from 4 to 8 <Cite id="huang2003" />.
- **The Rilling threshold** <Cite id="rilling2003" />. Define the mode amplitude $a(t) = \tfrac12\paren{u(t) - l(t)}$
  and the evaluation function $\sigma(t) = \abs{m(t)}/a(t)$, the local mean relative to the local amplitude. Stop when
  $\sigma(t) < \theta_1$ for all but a fraction $\alpha$ of the samples and $\sigma(t) < \theta_2$ everywhere, and the
  extrema and zero crossings differ by at most one. The defaults are $\theta_1 = 0.05$, $\theta_2 = 10\theta_1$ and
  $\alpha = 0.05$. The two thresholds demand a small mean almost everywhere while tolerating a few local excursions.

A fixed number of sifts, about 10, is also common. It makes every IMF the output of the same nonlinear operator, which
<NoteLink to="ensemble-empirical-mode-decomposition">ensemble EMD</NoteLink> relies on when it averages decompositions
<Cite id="wu2009" />.

**Worked example.** Take $x[n] = \cos(2\pi \cdot 0.06\, n) + 0.8\cos(2\pi \cdot 0.015\, n)$ for $n = 0, \ldots, 399$,
a fast tone of period 16.7 samples riding on a slow tone of period 66.7 samples. Before the first sift, $\sigma(t)$
exceeds 0.05 on 96% of the samples, with a maximum of 0.81, so the Rilling rule demands a sift. The first sift gives
$\mathrm{SD}_1 = 0.38$ (as a ratio of sums), above the 0.2 threshold, and leaves a candidate with 47 extrema and 48 zero
crossings. The candidate differs from the fast tone by 2.1% in relative root-mean-square error. The envelopes of that
candidate have $\max_t \sigma(t) = 0.029$, so the Rilling rule stops after one sift. The second sift gives
$\mathrm{SD}_2 = 3.6 \times 10^{-4}$, so the SD rule stops after two sifts, with an error of 0.9%. Eight more sifts
reduce the error only to 0.8%, and most of what remains is at the ends.

## End effects

The spline through the maxima is anchored only between the first and the last maximum. Beyond them it is extrapolated,
and a cubic extrapolated over half a period can swing far above or below the signal. The mean envelope is then wrong
near the ends, the error is subtracted into the IMF, and each further sift and each further IMF propagates it inward.

The standard remedy mirrors extrema <Cite id="rilling2003" />. At the left end, the two maxima and two minima nearest
the start are reflected about a symmetry point. The symmetry point is the first extremum if the first sample lies
between the values of the nearest maximum and minimum. Otherwise it is the first sample itself, which then also joins
the knots of the envelope it lies beyond. If a
reflected extremum would still land inside the signal, the first sample is used as the symmetry point instead. The
right end is treated in the same way. The reflected knots lie outside the signal, so the envelopes are interpolated
over every sample. Other remedies extend the signal itself by mirroring, by periodisation or by an autoregressive
prediction, and then discard the extension after sifting.

## Mode mixing

**Mode mixing** is one IMF containing oscillations of very different scales, or one oscillation split across several
IMFs. Its typical cause is intermittency. Suppose a fast burst appears in only part of a slow signal. Where the burst is
present, the first IMF captures the burst. Where it is absent, the fastest oscillation is the slow one, so the first
IMF captures the slow signal there. The first IMF then switches between two unrelated components, and every later IMF
inherits the gap. The IMF still satisfies both IMF conditions, so no stopping rule detects the problem.

The two-tone analysis above gives a second cause. When two components have frequencies within a factor of about 1.5
of each other, their extrema no longer sample either component well enough, and sifting returns them together as one
amplitude-modulated IMF <Cite id="rilling2008" />.

**Worked example.** Two unit-amplitude tones with frequency ratio $f = f_2/f_1$, sampled at 200 samples per period of
the faster tone for 2,000 samples and sifted 20 times, give a first IMF whose relative error from the faster tone, away
from the first and last 10% of samples, is 0.35% at $f = 0.3$, 3.0% at 0.5 and 8.4% at 0.6. At $f = 0.7$ the error
jumps to 52%, and at 0.8 to 90%: the two tones are no longer separated. The crossover depends on the amplitude ratio
and on the stopping rule.

Noise-assisted variants attack intermittency by adding white noise that populates every scale, so that each IMF has a
consistent band everywhere; see <NoteLink to="ensemble-empirical-mode-decomposition">ensemble EMD</NoteLink>
<Cite id="wu2009" />. <NoteLink to="variational-mode-decomposition">Variational mode decomposition</NoteLink> avoids
recursive extraction altogether.

## Cost

One sift costs $O(N)$ for $N$ samples: a pass to find extrema and two tridiagonal solves for the splines, evaluated at
every sample. A decomposition with $J$ IMFs and $S$ sifts per IMF costs $O(JSN)$. On broadband signals the IMFs halve
in frequency from one to the next, so $J$ is about $\log_2 N$. EMD is cheap for a single signal; its ensemble variants
multiply the cost by the ensemble size, typically 50 to 500.

## Where EMD sits

EMD is one of several ways to describe how the frequency content of a signal changes in time. They differ in how much
of the analysis adapts to the signal and in what can be proved about the result.

| Method | Adaptivity | Theory | Resolution | Typical uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <NoteLink to="short-time-fourier-transform">Short-time Fourier transform</NoteLink> | none: one window for all times and frequencies | linear, invertible, fully analysed | fixed time-frequency cell set by the window | speech and audio, stationary segments, spectrogram features |
| <NoteLink to="continuous-wavelet-transform">Wavelet transforms</NoteLink> | scale-dependent: the window shrinks as frequency rises (constant $Q$) | linear, invertible, fully analysed | fine time resolution at high frequency, fine <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">frequency resolution</NoteLink> at low frequency | transients, multiscale structure, denoising, compression |
| EMD with the <NoteLink to="hilbert-huang-transform">Hilbert–Huang transform</NoteLink> | fully data-driven, recursive, no basis | defined only by the algorithm; no uniqueness, orthogonality or stability results | one instantaneous frequency per IMF at every sample; limited by how well components separate | nonlinear and nonstationary data in geophysics, climate and biomedicine; trend removal |
| <NoteLink to="variational-mode-decomposition">Variational mode decomposition</NoteLink> | data-driven centre frequencies, user-set number of modes and bandwidth penalty | minimiser of a stated objective, solved by <Gloss name="admm" /> | separates closer tones than EMD and is more robust to noise; modes are band-limited | vibration and fault diagnosis, biomedical rhythms, preprocessing for forecasting |
| <NoteLink to="reassignment-and-synchrosqueezing">Synchrosqueezing</NoteLink> | fixed linear transform, then reassignment in frequency driven by the signal's phase | provable recovery of well-separated AM-FM components | sharp instantaneous-frequency ridges; invertible, so modes can be extracted along ridges | ECG, respiration and other oscillatory biomedical signals; a principled stand-in for EMD |

## Trade-offs

- **Defined only by an algorithm.** The decomposition is defined by an algorithm, not by an objective, so its output
  depends on the extremum detection, the interpolant, the end treatment, the stopping rule and the sampling rate.
  Synchrosqueezing was proposed as a transform with similar aims and proofs <Cite id="daubechies2011" />, and
  variational mode decomposition as a decomposition with an explicit objective <Cite id="dragomiretskiy2014" />.
- **Not orthogonal.** IMFs are only approximately orthogonal, so their energies need not add to the signal's energy.
- **Sensitivity to noise and sampling.** A small perturbation can add or remove extrema and change every later IMF.
  Extrema of a sampled signal are only located to within one sample, which needs several samples per period to be
  accurate.
- **Nonlinear and non-harmonic waveforms.** A triangle wave or an asymmetric oscillation can be one IMF. Fourier and
  wavelet analyses spread such a waveform over harmonics, while EMD keeps it whole, and its instantaneous frequency then
  varies within each period.
