<Definition>
**Empirical mode decomposition** writes a signal as a sum of **intrinsic mode functions** (IMFs) and a residue,
$x(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{J} c_j(t) + r(t)$. An IMF is a function whose numbers of extrema and zero crossings differ by at
most one, and whose upper and lower envelopes are symmetric about zero. The IMFs are found by **sifting** and ordered
from the fastest oscillation to the slowest <Cite id="huang1998" />.
</Definition>

## Sifting

To extract the first IMF from $x$:

1. Find the local maxima and minima of the current signal $h$, starting with $h = x$.
2. Interpolate the maxima with a cubic spline to form the upper envelope $u(t)$, and the minima to form the lower
   envelope $l(t)$.
3. Subtract their mean: $h \leftarrow h - \tfrac12 (u + l)$.
4. Repeat until $h$ satisfies the IMF conditions or a stopping rule, such as a fixed number of iterations or a small
   mean envelope.

The result is $c_1 = h$. Subtract it, $x \leftarrow x - c_1$, and repeat to find $c_2$, and so on, until the residue
is monotonic or has too few extrema to continue. Each sift removes the local mean, so what remains oscillates
symmetrically about zero at the fastest local scale, and each IMF has a well-behaved instantaneous frequency.

## The Hilbert–Huang transform

The IMFs are designed to be narrowband enough for the analytic signal to give meaningful amplitude and phase. The
**Hilbert spectrum** plots the instantaneous amplitude of each IMF against time and its instantaneous frequency, one
curve per IMF. Unlike a spectrogram, it has no window and so no fixed resolution trade-off; its resolution is limited
only by how well the IMFs separate the signal's components. See
<NoteLink to="instantaneous-frequency">instantaneous frequency</NoteLink>.

## When components separate

Sifting acts locally like a filter bank, but not a linear one, and whether two components end up in separate IMFs
depends on their frequency and amplitude ratios. For two tones, separation fails as the frequency ratio approaches 1:
both tones are then extracted together in one IMF <Cite id="rilling2008" />.

**Worked example.** Two unit-amplitude tones with frequency ratio $f_2/f_1$, sampled at 200 samples per period of the
faster tone and sifted 20 times with cubic-spline envelopes, give a first IMF whose relative error from the faster tone
is 0.5% at ratio 0.3, 1.9% at 0.5 and 7.4% at 0.6. At ratio 0.7 the error jumps to 54%, and at 0.8 to 91%: the two
tones are no longer separated. The exact crossover depends on the amplitude ratio and the stopping rule.

## Pitfalls and remedies

- **Mode mixing.** A single IMF can contain oscillations of very different scales, for example when an intermittent
  high-frequency burst appears in only part of the signal. **Ensemble EMD** adds many realisations of white noise,
  decomposes each, and averages the IMFs, so the noise populates every scale and the signal's components settle into
  consistent IMFs <Cite id="wu2009" />.
- **End effects.** Splines extrapolate badly beyond the first and last extrema, and errors propagate inward through
  successive sifts. Mirroring the signal at its ends reduces them.
- **No mathematical foundation.** The decomposition is defined by an algorithm, not an objective, so its output
  depends on the spline, the stopping rule and the number of sifts. Synchrosqueezing was proposed as a transform with
  similar aims and a proper theory <Cite id="daubechies2011" />; variational mode decomposition is another alternative.
- **Not orthogonal.** IMFs are only approximately orthogonal, and their energies need not add to the signal's energy.
