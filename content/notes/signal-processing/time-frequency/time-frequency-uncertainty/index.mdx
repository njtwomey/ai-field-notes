<Definition>
Let $g$ have unit energy, $\int \abs{g(t)}^2\, dt = 1$, with <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink>
$G(f) = \int g(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\, dt$. Treat $\abs{g(t)}^2$ and $\abs{G(f)}^2$ as densities, with means $\bar t$, $\bar f$ and <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviations</NoteLink>
$\sigma_t$, $\sigma_f$. Then

$$
\sigma_t\, \sigma_f \ge \frac{1}{4\pi},
$$

with equality exactly when $g$ is a Gaussian, $g(t) = c\, e^{-a (t - \bar t)^2 + i 2\pi \bar f t}$ with $a > 0$
<Cite id="gabor1946" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation

Shift and modulate so that $\bar t = 0$ and $\bar f = 0$; neither changes the spreads. Assume $g$ is smooth and
decays fast enough for the integrals below to be finite.

**Frequency spread through the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>.** The derivative $g'$ has transform $i 2\pi f\, G(f)$. By
<NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">Parseval's theorem</NoteLink>,

$$
\int \abs{g'(t)}^2\, dt = \int \abs{2\pi f\, G(f)}^2\, df = 4\pi^2 \sigma_f^2.
$$

**A covariance term.** Integrate $t \frac{d}{dt}\abs{g(t)}^2$ by parts. Since
$\frac{d}{dt}\abs{g}^2 = 2 \operatorname{Re}\paren{\bar g\, g'}$ and the boundary term vanishes,

$$
\int t\, \operatorname{Re}\paren{\bar g(t)\, g'(t)}\, dt = \tfrac12 \int t\, \frac{d}{dt}\abs{g(t)}^2\, dt =
-\tfrac12 \int \abs{g(t)}^2\, dt = -\tfrac12.
$$

**Cauchy–Schwarz.** Applied to the functions $t\, g(t)$ and $g'(t)$,

$$
\sigma_t^2 \cdot 4\pi^2 \sigma_f^2 = \int \abs{t\, g}^2\, dt \int \abs{g'}^2\, dt
\ge \abs{\int t\, \bar g\, g'\, dt}^2 \ge \paren{\int t\, \operatorname{Re}\paren{\bar g\, g'}\, dt}^2 = \tfrac14.
$$

Taking square roots, $2\pi \sigma_t \sigma_f \ge \tfrac12$, which is the bound.

**Equality.** Cauchy–Schwarz is tight only when $g' = \lambda\, t\, g$ for a constant $\lambda$, and the second
inequality only when $\lambda$ is real. The solution is $g(t) = c\, e^{\lambda t^2 / 2}$, square-integrable when
$\lambda < 0$: a Gaussian. Undoing the shift and modulation gives the general form.

## What it means for analysis

Every windowed analysis looks at a signal through a window with some $\sigma_t$ and hence $\sigma_f \ge 1/(4\pi
\sigma_t)$. Each coefficient of a short-time Fourier transform or wavelet transform therefore summarises a cell of
the time-frequency plane with area at least of order $1/(4\pi)$, a Heisenberg box. No choice of window can make both
sides small; windows differ only in how they shape the box and how close they come to the bound. This is why the
<NoteLink to="short-time-fourier-transform">short-time Fourier transform</NoteLink> trades time resolution for
<NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">frequency resolution</NoteLink>, and why the <NoteLink to="continuous-wavelet-transform">continuous wavelet transform</NoteLink>
changes the box's shape with frequency but not its area.

## Worked example

A Gaussian window with $\sigma_t = 10$ ms has $\sigma_f = 1/(4\pi \times 0.01) \approx 7.96$ Hz, exactly at the bound.
Measured numerically in the same way, the <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">Hann window</NoteLink> reaches $\sigma_t \sigma_f \approx 0.0817$, about 2.6% above
the bound, and the Blackman window about 0.4% above. For a rectangular window the frequency spread is infinite: its
spectrum decays only as $1/f$, so $\int f^2 \abs{G(f)}^2\, df$ diverges. The same happens for any window whose value
jumps at its edges, such as the Hamming window.

## Pitfalls

- **The definition of spread matters.** The bound uses standard deviations of the energy densities. Other widths,
  such as the null-to-null main-lobe width or the $-3$ dB bandwidth, obey different constants.
- **Uncertainty limits resolution of a single window, not of every method.** Parametric and adaptive methods, such as
  <NoteLink to="reassignment-and-synchrosqueezing">reassignment</NoteLink> or model-based frequency estimation, can
  locate isolated components far more precisely than the box size, by using assumptions about the signal.
