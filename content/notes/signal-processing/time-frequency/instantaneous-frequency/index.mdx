<Definition>
For a real signal $x(t)$, the **analytic signal** is $z(t) = x(t) + i\, \mathcal{H}\{x\}(t)$, where $\mathcal{H}$ is the
Hilbert transform; its spectrum is $2X(f)$ for $f > 0$ and zero for $f < 0$. Writing $z(t) = A(t)\, e^{i\varphi(t)}$
defines the **instantaneous amplitude** $A(t) = \abs{z(t)}$, the **instantaneous phase** $\varphi(t)$, and the
**instantaneous frequency**

$$
f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{d\varphi(t)}{dt}
$$

<Cite id="boashash1992" />.
</Definition>

## Why the analytic signal

A real signal $x(t) = A(t) \cos\varphi(t)$ does not determine $A$ and $\varphi$: infinitely many amplitude and phase
pairs give the same product. The analytic signal picks one pair by adding a specific imaginary part, the Hilbert
transform, which shifts every positive-frequency component by $-\pi/2$. For $x(t) = \cos(\omega t)$ it gives
$\mathcal{H}\{x\} = \sin(\omega t)$, so $z = e^{i\omega t}$, $A = 1$ and $f_i = \omega / (2\pi)$.
See <NoteLink to="hilbert-transform">Hilbert transform</NoteLink>.

For a modulated signal $x(t) = a(t) \cos\phi(t)$, the analytic signal is $a(t)\, e^{i\phi(t)}$ exactly when the
spectrum of $a$ lies entirely below the spectrum of $\cos\phi$ (Bedrosian's theorem). The recovered amplitude and
frequency are then the true modulations. When the envelope varies as fast as the carrier, the decomposition still
exists but no longer means amplitude and frequency modulation in any physical sense.

## Discrete estimation

With samples of the analytic signal $z[n]$, the phase difference between consecutive samples gives

$$
\hat f_i[n] = \frac{f_s}{2\pi} \arg\paren{z[n+1]\, z^*[n]},
$$

which avoids unwrapping, since $\arg\paren{z[n+1] z^*[n]}$ is the phase increment reduced to $(-\pi, \pi]$. It is exact
for a sampled complex exponential below the <NoteLink to="sampling-theorem">Nyquist frequency</NoteLink>.

## Multicomponent signals

The instantaneous frequency of a sum of components is not the set of their frequencies. For two tones
$z(t) = e^{i\omega_1 t} + a\, e^{i\omega_2 t}$ with $0 < a < 1$ and $\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1$, the phase
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is

$$
\varphi'(t) = \omega_1 + \Delta\omega\, \frac{a^2 + a\cos(\Delta\omega\, t)}{1 + a^2 + 2a\cos(\Delta\omega\, t)}.
$$

It oscillates at the beat frequency. At the peaks of the envelope, $\cos = 1$, it reaches
$\omega_1 + \Delta\omega\, a/(1 + a)$, between the two tones. At the troughs, $\cos = -1$, it reaches
$\omega_1 - \Delta\omega\, a/(1 - a)$, which lies below both tones and can be negative.

**Worked example.** Tones at 100 Hz and 110 Hz with $a = 0.9$ give an instantaneous frequency that swings between
$110 \cdot 0.9/1.9 + 100 \cdot 1/1.9 \approx 104.7$ Hz and $100 - 10 \times 0.9/0.1 = 10$ Hz, ten times a second. A
numerical phase derivative of the sampled signal confirms both extremes. Neither value is a frequency present in the
signal. A single instantaneous frequency is meaningful only for a signal with one narrowband component, which is why
methods such as <NoteLink to="empirical-mode-decomposition">empirical mode decomposition</NoteLink> first split a
signal into such components.

## Pitfalls

- **Noise.** The phase of $z$ is unstable where $\abs{z}$ is small; instantaneous-frequency estimates spike at
  envelope minima.
- **Edge effects of the discrete Hilbert transform.** An FFT-based analytic signal assumes periodicity; the first and
  last few cycles are distorted unless the signal is windowed or padded.
- **Negative frequencies are not an error of computation.** They are a correct property of the phase of a sum of
  components, and a sign that a single-component model does not fit.
