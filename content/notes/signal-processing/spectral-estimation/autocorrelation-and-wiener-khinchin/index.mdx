<Definition>
A process $x[n]$ is wide-sense stationary when its mean $\mu = \expect[x[n]]$ is constant and its autocovariance

$$
r[m] = \expect\brack{\paren{x[n + m] - \mu}\paren{x[n] - \mu}}
$$

depends only on the lag $m$. Its power spectral density is $S(\omega) = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} r[m]\, e^{-i\omega m}$,
with inverse $r[m] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} S(\omega)\, e^{i\omega m}\, d\omega$. The Wiener–Khinchin theorem
states that this transform is also the limit of the expected periodogram, so $S(\omega)$ is the density of the
process's power over frequency <Cite id="stoica2005" />.
</Definition>

## Properties of the autocovariance

- **Even.** $r[-m] = r[m]$, by stationarity: shift the time index by $m$.
- **Bounded by the variance.** $\abs{r[m]} \le r[0]$, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to the two centred
  samples.
- **Positive semidefinite.** For any coefficients $c_n$, $\sum_{j,k} c_j c_k\, r[j - k] = \var\brack{\sum_j c_j x[j]}
  \ge 0$. So every Toeplitz matrix $[r[j - k]]$ is positive semidefinite.

## The theorem

For samples $x[0], \dots, x[N-1]$ of a zero-mean process, the expected periodogram is (see
<NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink>)

$$
\expect\brack{\frac{1}{N}\abs{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-i\omega n}}^2}
= \sum_{\abs{m} < N}\paren{1 - \frac{\abs{m}}{N}} r[m]\, e^{-i\omega m} .
$$

If $\sum_m \abs{r[m]} < \infty$, each term tends to $r[m]e^{-i\omega m}$ as $N \to \infty$ and is bounded by
$\abs{r[m]}$, so by dominated convergence the sum tends to $S(\omega)$. This is the Wiener–Khinchin theorem:
$S(\omega)$ defined as the transform of $r[m]$ is the same quantity as the long-run average power per unit
frequency. Two consequences:

- **$S(\omega) \ge 0$.** It is a limit of expectations of squared magnitudes. Equivalently, positive semidefinite
  sequences are exactly those with non-negative transforms (Herglotz's theorem).
- **Power adds up.** Setting $m = 0$ in the inverse transform gives
  $r[0] = \var(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} S(\omega)\, d\omega$: the variance is the total area under the
  spectrum.

Sinusoids fit only in the generalised sense. A sinusoid with random phase, $A\cos(\omega_0 n + \phi)$, has
$r[m] = \tfrac{A^2}{2}\cos(\omega_0 m)$, which is not summable. Its spectrum is a pair of Dirac deltas of area
$\pi A^2/2$ each at $\pm\omega_0$: a line spectrum.

## Estimating the autocovariance

Two estimators are standard, after subtracting the sample mean:

$$
\hat r_b[m] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-\abs{m}} x[n]\, x[n + \abs{m}], \qquad
\hat r_u[m] = \frac{1}{N - \abs{m}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-\abs{m}} x[n]\, x[n + \abs{m}].
$$

- **Unbiased** $\hat r_u$ has $\expect[\hat r_u[m]] = r[m]$, apart from the effect of the estimated mean, but at lags
  near $N$ it averages very few products, and its variance grows without bound.
- **Biased** $\hat r_b = (1 - \abs{m}/N)\,\hat r_u$ shrinks large lags towards 0. Its bias is small where
  $\abs{m} \ll N$, its mean squared error is usually lower, and it is positive semidefinite, because its transform is
  the periodogram, which is never negative. Spectral estimators and Yule–Walker equations therefore use $\hat r_b$.

## Worked example

An AR(1) process $x[n] = a\, x[n-1] + e[n]$ with white $e[n]$ of variance $\sigma^2$ has $r[m] = \sigma^2 a^{\abs{m}}/(1 -
a^2)$: multiply the recursion by $x[n - m]$ and take expectations to get $r[m] = a\, r[m - 1]$ for $m \ge 1$, and use
$r[0] = a^2 r[0] + \sigma^2$. Its spectrum is

$$
S(\omega) = \sum_m \frac{\sigma^2 a^{\abs{m}}}{1 - a^2} e^{-i\omega m} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\abs{1 - a e^{-i\omega}}^2},
$$

summing the two geometric series for $m \ge 0$ and $m < 0$. For $a = 0.8$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$: $r[0] = 2.778$,
$S(0) = 1/(1 - 0.8)^2 = 25$ and $S(\pi) = 1/(1.8)^2 = 0.309$. Positive $a$ concentrates the power at low frequencies,
and the integral of $S$ over $[-\pi, \pi)$ divided by $2\pi$ returns $r[0] = 2.778$.

## Pitfalls

- **Correlation or covariance.** Some fields call $r[m]$ the autocorrelation even with the mean removed; signal
  processing often uses $\expect[x[n+m]x[n]]$ without centring. The difference is a spike at $\omega = 0$.
- **Normalised autocorrelation.** Dividing by $r[0]$ gives values in $[-1, 1]$ but removes the scale needed for the
  spectrum.
- **Non-stationarity.** Trends and changing variance violate the assumptions; a slowly decaying sample autocovariance
  is often a sign of a trend rather than long memory.
