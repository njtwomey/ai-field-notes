<Definition>
Welch's method splits $N$ samples into $K$ segments of length $L$, each starting $D$ samples after the previous one,
multiplies each segment by a window $w[n]$, and averages the resulting modified periodograms <Cite id="welch1967" />:

$$
\hat S_W(\omega) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \frac{1}{U}\abs{\sum_{n=0}^{L-1} w[n]\, x[n + kD]\, e^{-i\omega n}}^2,
\qquad U = \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} w[n]^2 .
$$

With a rectangular window and no overlap ($D = L$) it is Bartlett's method.
</Definition>

## Why averaging helps

A single <NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink> has a <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> equal to its mean at every frequency, however long the record. If the $K$ segment periodograms were
independent, each approximately $S(\omega)\chi^2_2/2$, their average would have mean $S(\omega)$ and variance

$$
\var\brack{\hat S_W(\omega)} \approx \frac{S(\omega)^2}{K},
$$

since the variance of a mean of $K$ independent variables is $1/K$ times theirs. The average is
$S(\omega)\chi^2_{2K}/(2K)$, a distribution with $\nu = 2K$ degrees of freedom, which gives
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>:
$\nu \hat S_W / \chi^2_{\nu, 1-\alpha/2} \le S \le \nu \hat S_W / \chi^2_{\nu, \alpha/2}$.

## Resolution and segment length

The expected value of each modified periodogram is the true spectrum convolved with the window's spectral kernel
$\abs{W(e^{i\omega})}^2 / U$, and $W$ is the transform of a window only $L$ samples long. Its main lobe is several times
$2\pi/L$ wide: for a <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">Hann window</NoteLink>, the half-power width is about
$1.44 \cdot 2\pi/L$. Two spectral peaks closer than this merge. The trade-off is therefore fixed by the segment length:

| Segment length $L$ | Segments $K$ (no overlap) | Resolution | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| long | few | fine, $\propto 1/L$ | high, $\propto 1/K$ |
| short | many | coarse | low |

With $N$ samples and no overlap, $K = N/L$, so resolution times the relative variance is roughly constant: halving $L$
doubles $K$ and halves the variance, but doubles the width of every spectral feature.

## Overlap

Welch's contribution beyond Bartlett was to overlap tapered segments. A window down-weights the data near each segment's
edges, so non-overlapping windowed segments waste information. Overlapping recovers it: with $D = L/2$, nearly twice
as many segments fit in the record. Overlapping segments are correlated, so the variance does not halve, but the
correlation is small for smooth windows. For segments $j$ steps apart, the correlation of their periodograms for white
noise is approximately

$$
\rho(j) = \paren{\frac{\sum_n w[n]\, w[n + jD]}{\sum_n w[n]^2}}^2,
$$

and Welch's variance formula is

$$
\frac{\var\brack{\hat S_W}}{S^2} \approx \frac{1}{K}\brack{1 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\paren{1 - \frac{j}{K}}\rho(j)} .
$$

For a Hann window at 50% overlap, $\rho(1) = 0.0278$ and $\rho(j) = 0$ for $j \ge 2$, so the overlapped segments are
almost as good as independent ones. Overlap beyond 50% adds little for Hann windows <Cite id="welch1967" />.

## Worked example

Take $N = 4{,}096$ samples of unit-variance white noise, Hann segments of $L = 256$, and 50% overlap. Then
$K = 2N/L - 1 = 31$ segments. The formula gives a relative variance of
$\tfrac{1}{31}(1 + 2 \cdot \tfrac{30}{31} \cdot 0.0278) = 0.0340$, a relative standard deviation of 0.184. A simulated
record gives an estimate with mean 1.011 and standard deviation 0.196, against 1 and 1 for the raw periodogram of the
same data. The resolution is about $1.44 \cdot 2\pi/256 \approx 0.035$ rad/sample.

## Pitfalls

- **Choose $L$ from the resolution you need.** Decide the narrowest feature that must be resolved, set $L$ from it, and
  accept the variance that follows; then add data if the estimate is still too noisy.
- **Scale by the window.** Dividing by $U = \sum w^2$ keeps the estimate unbiased for broadband noise. Dividing by
  $(\sum w)^2$ instead preserves the height of pure sinusoids. The two differ by the window's equivalent noise
  bandwidth.
- **Detrend each segment.** A mean or slow trend in a segment leaks into low frequencies; SciPy detrends each segment
  by default.
- **Non-stationary signals.** Averaging assumes the spectrum is the same in every segment. When it changes, the
  individual segment spectra are a spectrogram; see the <NoteLink to="short-time-fourier-transform">short-time Fourier
  transform</NoteLink>.
