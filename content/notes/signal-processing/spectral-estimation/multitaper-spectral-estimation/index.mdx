<Definition>
Thomson's multitaper estimate of a <Gloss name="power-spectral-density">power spectral density</Gloss> from $N$ samples is

$$
\hat S_{MT}(\omega) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\abs{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} v_k[n]\, x[n]\, e^{-i\omega n}}^2,
$$

where $v_0, \dots, v_{K-1}$ are the first $K$ discrete prolate spheroidal sequences (Slepian tapers) for a chosen
half-bandwidth $W$: orthonormal sequences whose spectra are as concentrated as possible in $\abs{f} \le W$
<Cite id="thomson1982" />.
</Definition>

## The problem it solves

A single tapered <NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink> uses a window to control leakage, but its variance
equals its mean squared, whatever the window. <NoteLink to="welch-method">Welch's method</NoteLink> reduces the
variance by cutting the record into segments, which throws away resolution: each segment is shorter than the record.
The multitaper method keeps the full record and instead computes several spectra from it with different tapers. If the
tapers are orthogonal and each has low leakage, the $K$ spectra are nearly uncorrelated, and averaging them divides the
variance by about $K$.

## Slepian tapers

Frequencies here are in cycles per sample, $f = \omega/2\pi$. For a sequence $v$ of length $N$ with transform
$V(f) = \sum_n v[n] e^{-i 2\pi f n}$, the fraction of its energy inside the band $\abs{f} \le W$ is

$$
\lambda = \frac{\int_{-W}^{W} \abs{V(f)}^2\, df}{\int_{-1/2}^{1/2} \abs{V(f)}^2\, df}
= \frac{v^\top A\, v}{v^\top v}, \qquad A_{nm} = \frac{\sin\paren{2\pi W(n - m)}}{\pi(n - m)},
$$

with $A_{nn} = 2W$. The second form follows by expanding $\abs{V}^2$ as a double sum and integrating each term over the
band. $\lambda$ is a <NoteLink to="spectral-theorem">Rayleigh quotient</NoteLink>, so it is maximised by the leading eigenvector of $A$, and the eigenvectors in
order of eigenvalue are the best-concentrated orthonormal sequences in turn <Cite id="slepian1978" />. These are the
Slepian tapers $v_k$, with concentrations $\lambda_0 > \lambda_1 > \dots$.

Two facts make them practical:

- **About $2NW$ of them are well concentrated.** The eigenvalues stay close to 1 up to $k \approx 2NW - 1$ and then
  drop sharply towards 0. Only these first $K \le 2NW - 1$ tapers are used.
- **They are cheap to compute.** $A$ commutes with a symmetric tridiagonal matrix with diagonal
  $\paren{\tfrac{N - 1 - 2n}{2}}^2 \cos 2\pi W$ and off-diagonal $\tfrac{n(N - n)}{2}$, so they share eigenvectors, and
  the tridiagonal problem is fast and numerically stable.

## Resolution and variance

Each eigenspectrum is the true spectrum smoothed by $\abs{V_k(f)}^2$, which is concentrated in $\abs{f} \le W$. The
estimate therefore resolves features about $2W$ apart, the full bandwidth. $W$ is chosen through the time-bandwidth
product $NW$: typical values are 2 to 4, giving $K = 3$ to 7 tapers and a resolution of $2W = 2NW/N$ cycles per
sample.

For white noise the eigenspectra are uncorrelated, since $\sum_n v_j[n] v_k[n] = 0$, and the estimate is
approximately $S(\omega)\chi^2_{2K}/(2K)$, with relative standard deviation $1/\sqrt{K}$.

Compared with Welch at the same variance, multitaper keeps better resolution, because every taper spans the whole
record. Its leakage is also low: all used tapers put almost all their energy in the band.

## Worked example

For $N = 128$ and $NW = 4$, the half-bandwidth is $W = 4/128 = 0.03125$ cycles per sample, or
$2\pi W \approx 0.196$ rad/sample. The concentrations of the first eight tapers are 1.00000, 1.00000, 1.00000, 0.99997,
0.99942, 0.99256, 0.93686 and 0.69905: seven tapers, $K = 2NW - 1$, keep at least 93.7% of their energy in the band,
and the eighth does not. With those seven, a simulated white-noise record gives an estimate whose standard deviation is
0.383 of its mean, against the theoretical $1/\sqrt{7} = 0.378$.

## Extensions

- **Adaptive weighting.** Thomson weights each eigenspectrum by frequency so that the higher-order tapers, which leak
  more, count less where the spectrum is steep <Cite id="thomson1982" />.
- **Harmonic F-test.** The same tapers give a test for a pure sinusoid at each frequency, separating line components
  from the continuous spectrum.
- **<NoteLink to="jackknife">Jackknife</NoteLink> <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>.** Leaving out one taper at a time gives variance estimates without distributional
  assumptions.

## Pitfalls

- **Too many tapers.** Tapers beyond $2NW - 1$ are poorly concentrated and leak badly; using them raises the bias for
  little gain in variance.
- **Resolution is set by $W$, not $N$.** Increasing $NW$ smooths the spectrum; features narrower than $2W$ are
  flattened.
- **Normalisation.** Tapers must have unit energy for the average to estimate the spectrum on the same scale as the
  periodogram.
