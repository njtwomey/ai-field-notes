<Definition>
Given samples $y_j$ at arbitrary times $t_j$, $j = 1, \dots, N$, with the mean removed, the Lomb–Scargle periodogram at
angular frequency $\omega$ is

$$
P(\omega) = \frac{1}{2}\brack{\frac{\paren{\sum_j y_j \cos\omega(t_j - \tau)}^2}{\sum_j \cos^2\omega(t_j - \tau)}
+ \frac{\paren{\sum_j y_j \sin\omega(t_j - \tau)}^2}{\sum_j \sin^2\omega(t_j - \tau)}},
\qquad \tan 2\omega\tau = \frac{\sum_j \sin 2\omega t_j}{\sum_j \cos 2\omega t_j} .
$$

It equals half the reduction in the residual sum of squares achieved by fitting $a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$ to the
data by least squares <Cite id="lomb1976" /> <Cite id="scargle1982" />.
</Definition>

## Why irregular sampling needs its own method

Many measurements are not taken on a grid: astronomical observations are interrupted by daylight and weather,
heartbeats arrive at irregular intervals, sensors drop samples. The DFT-based
<NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink> assumes equally spaced samples, and the two obvious workarounds both
distort the spectrum.

- **The direct transform.** Evaluating $\frac{1}{N}\abs{\sum_j y_j e^{-i\omega t_j}}^2$ at the actual times is
  possible, but on an irregular grid the cosine and sine sums are no longer orthogonal. The estimate then has no
  simple distribution under noise, and it changes if the time origin is shifted.
- **Interpolating to a grid.** Resampling onto a regular grid and using the <Gloss name="fft" /> fills gaps with invented values.
  Interpolation is also a smoothing operation: linear interpolation approximately convolves the data with a triangle,
  whose transform $\operatorname{sinc}^2$ attenuates high frequencies. Power is shifted towards low frequencies, and
  the estimate is biased where the gaps are long.

## Least squares at each frequency

At a fixed $\omega$, fit $y_j \approx a\cos\omega(t_j - \tau) + b\sin\omega(t_j - \tau)$ by least squares, with a time
offset $\tau$ still to be chosen. Writing $c_j = \cos\omega(t_j - \tau)$ and $s_j = \sin\omega(t_j - \tau)$, the normal
equations are

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \sum c_j^2 & \sum c_j s_j \\ \sum c_j s_j & \sum s_j^2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} \sum y_j c_j \\ \sum y_j s_j \end{bmatrix} .
$$

The offset $\tau$ is chosen to make the off-diagonal zero. Since $c_j s_j = \tfrac12\sin 2\omega(t_j - \tau)$, the
condition $\sum_j \sin 2\omega(t_j - \tau) = 0$ expands to
$\cos 2\omega\tau \sum_j \sin 2\omega t_j = \sin 2\omega\tau \sum_j \cos 2\omega t_j$, which is Lomb's definition of
$\tau$. With the columns orthogonal, the equations decouple:

$$
\hat a = \frac{\sum_j y_j c_j}{\sum_j c_j^2}, \qquad \hat b = \frac{\sum_j y_j s_j}{\sum_j s_j^2} .
$$

For a least-squares fit, the reduction in the residual sum of squares equals the explained sum of squares,
$\hat a \sum y_j c_j + \hat b \sum y_j s_j$. Substituting the estimates gives
$(\sum y_j c_j)^2 / \sum c_j^2 + (\sum y_j s_j)^2/\sum s_j^2$, which is twice $P(\omega)$. So the Lomb–Scargle
periodogram is the least-squares power of the best-fitting sinusoid at each frequency.

Three properties follow:

- **Time-shift invariance.** $\tau$ moves with the data, so shifting every $t_j$ by the same amount leaves $P$
  unchanged.
- **Agreement with the classical periodogram.** On an even grid at a <Gloss name="dft" /> frequency, $\sum c_j^2 = \sum s_j^2 = N/2$,
  and $P(\omega)$ reduces to $\frac{1}{N}\abs{\sum_j y_j e^{-i\omega t_j}}^2$.
- **A simple null distribution.** For <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">Gaussian white noise</NoteLink> of known variance $\sigma^2$, the two decoupled terms are
  independent squared Gaussians, so $P(\omega)/\sigma^2$ is exponentially distributed with mean 1 at any single
  frequency. This is what Scargle set out to achieve <Cite id="scargle1982" />.

## Significance and false alarms

Normalise by the variance, $P_N(\omega) = P(\omega)/\hat\sigma^2$. Under pure noise, at one frequency chosen in
advance, $\Pr(P_N > z) = e^{-z}$. Searching many frequencies raises the chance that some peak exceeds $z$ by
accident. If the search covers $M$ independent frequencies, the false-alarm probability of the highest peak is <Cite id="scargle1982" />

$$
\operatorname{FAP}(z) = 1 - \paren{1 - e^{-z}}^M .
$$

The difficulty is $M$. On an even grid the independent frequencies are the $N/2$ Fourier frequencies. For irregular
sampling, neighbouring frequencies are correlated in a way that depends on the sampling pattern, and scanning a finely
oversampled grid does not produce independent tests. $M$ is therefore usually estimated by simulating noise at the
actual sample times, or the false-alarm level is computed by <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink>. Using the variance estimated from the same
data, rather than a known $\sigma^2$, also changes the tail; the exponential law is the large-$N$ limit.

## The generalised, floating-mean periodogram

The classical form subtracts the sample mean first and fits only a sinusoid. When the sampling is uneven, the sample
mean is not the mean of the underlying signal: if observations cluster at one phase of the oscillation, subtracting
their average removes part of the signal and biases the fit. The generalised Lomb–Scargle periodogram fits
$y_j \approx a\cos\omega t_j + b\sin\omega t_j + c$ with the constant $c$ estimated jointly at each frequency, and
weights each point by $1/\sigma_j^2$ when measurement errors differ <Cite id="zechmeister2009" />. It is the same idea
with one more column in the least-squares problem, and it is the version to use when the data are few, noisy, or
unevenly phased.

## Frequency grid and the Nyquist limit

- **Grid spacing.** The <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">frequency resolution</NoteLink> is set by the total time span $T = t_N - t_1$: peaks narrower than about
  $2\pi/T$ rad/s cannot be separated. Evaluate on a grid finer than this, typically 5 to 10 points per $2\pi/T$, so
  that no peak falls between grid points.
- **Upper limit.** Irregular sampling has no single <NoteLink to="sampling-theorem">Nyquist frequency</NoteLink>.
  A common heuristic uses the pseudo-Nyquist frequency $\pi/\overline{\Delta t}$ from the mean spacing. However,
  irregular sampling can recover some information above this frequency. Regular structure in the sampling, such as
  observations only at night, creates strong aliases. The true limit is set by the finest common spacing of the
  times; if every $t_j$ is a multiple of $p$, frequencies above $\pi/p$ cannot be distinguished from lower ones.
  The pattern of aliases is the spectral window $\abs{\sum_j e^{-i\omega t_j}}^2$, which is worth computing alongside
  the periodogram <Cite id="vanderplas2018" />.

## Application: heart-rate variability

The intervals between successive heartbeats (RR intervals) are sampled at the beats themselves, so the series is
irregular by construction, and ectopic or missed beats create further gaps. Heart-rate variability analysis measures
the power in bands such as 0.04–0.15 Hz and 0.15–0.4 Hz. Laguna, Moody and Mark compared least-squares spectral
estimation of unevenly sampled heart-rate signals with FFT spectra of resampled series, on simulated and recorded
data <Cite id="laguna1998" />. The least-squares approach avoids the low-pass bias that interpolation introduces at
the higher frequencies, and it handles removed beats without inventing values.

## Worked example

Take seven samples at $t = (0, 1, 3, 4.5, 7, 8.2, 10.1)$ of $\sin 0.9t + 0.2\cos 2.3t$, with the mean removed, and
evaluate at $\omega = 0.9$. Lomb's condition gives $\tau = 0.296$. The decoupled coefficients are $\hat a = 0.226$ and
$\hat b = 0.894$, an amplitude of 0.922 for the fitted sinusoid, close to the true 1. The periodogram value is
$P(0.9) = 0.97488$, which equals half the reduction in residual sum of squares from the two-column least-squares fit to
the precision shown, and matches `scipy.signal.lombscargle(t, y, [0.9])`. SciPy takes angular frequencies and, with
`normalize=True`, divides by $\tfrac12\sum y_j^2$, giving 0.7795: the fraction of the variance the sinusoid explains.

In the figure's setting, with 240 samples at jittered times and the mean spacing 1 s, a component at $0.8\pi$ rad/s
with half the amplitude of one at $0.3\pi$ should sit 6.0 dB lower. Lomb–Scargle puts it 5.5 dB lower. A Hann
periodogram of the linearly interpolated series puts it 10.0 dB lower: about 4 dB of extra attenuation, matching the
$\operatorname{sinc}^2$ loss of linear interpolation at that frequency.

## Pitfalls

- **Angular or cyclic frequency.** SciPy's `lombscargle` expects angular frequency in rad per time unit, while
  Astropy's `LombScargle` uses cyclic frequency. Mixing them shifts every peak by a factor of $2\pi$.
- **Oversampled grids are not independent tests.** A finer grid makes peaks look smoother but adds no independent
  frequencies; the false-alarm probability does not improve with it.
- **Aliases from the sampling pattern.** Daily, seasonal or duty-cycled sampling produces aliases at the sampling
  pattern's frequencies. Check the spectral window before interpreting secondary peaks.
- **Non-sinusoidal signals.** The method fits one sinusoid per frequency; sharp, non-sinusoidal periodic signals put
  power into harmonics, and multi-term or phase-dispersion methods may detect them better.
