<Definition>
An autoregressive process of order $p$ is $x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\, x[n-k] + e[n]$, where $e[n]$ is white noise of
variance $\sigma^2$. Its <Gloss name="power-spectral-density">power spectral density</Gloss> is

$$
S(\omega) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\abs{1 - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k e^{-i\omega k}}^2} .
$$

Parametric spectral estimation fits $a_1, \dots, a_p$ and $\sigma^2$ to data and reports this spectrum
<Cite id="stoica2005" />.
</Definition>

## Why a model helps

Nonparametric estimates, from the <NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink> to Welch and multitaper, see the
spectrum through a window of length $N$ and cannot resolve features closer than about $2\pi/N$. An AR model instead
assumes a form for the spectrum, a smooth all-pole function with at most $p/2$ peaks, and estimates $p + 1$ numbers.
When the assumption fits, sharp peaks come out sharp from a short record, with no sidelobes. The AR form is also
general: any continuous spectrum can be approximated by an AR spectrum of high enough order.

The spectrum follows from the model because the process is white noise passed through the all-pole filter
$H(z) = 1/(1 - \sum_k a_k z^{-k})$, and filtering multiplies a spectrum by $\abs{H(e^{i\omega})}^2$.

## Yule–Walker equations

Multiply the model by $x[n - m]$ for $m \ge 1$ and take <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink>. The noise $e[n]$ is uncorrelated with past
values $x[n - m]$, so

$$
r[m] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\, r[m - k], \qquad m = 1, \dots, p,
$$

and for $m = 0$ the same step gives $\sigma^2 = r[0] - \sum_k a_k\, r[k]$. In matrix form, $R\,\avec = \rvec$, where
$R_{jk} = r[j - k]$ is a symmetric Toeplitz matrix. Replacing $r$ by the biased sample autocovariance gives the
Yule–Walker estimate. The biased estimator keeps $R$ positive definite, which guarantees a stable fitted
model.

## Levinson–Durbin recursion

The Toeplitz structure solves the equations in $O(p^2)$ operations instead of $O(p^3)$, by building the order-$m$
solution from the order-$(m - 1)$ one <Cite id="levinson1947" /> <Cite id="durbin1960" />. With $\sigma_0^2 = r[0]$,
for $m = 1, \dots, p$:

$$
\kappa_m = \frac{r[m] - \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} a_k^{(m-1)} r[m - k]}{\sigma_{m-1}^2}, \quad
a_k^{(m)} = a_k^{(m-1)} - \kappa_m\, a_{m-k}^{(m-1)}, \quad a_m^{(m)} = \kappa_m, \quad
\sigma_m^2 = \sigma_{m-1}^2\paren{1 - \kappa_m^2} .
$$

The $\kappa_m$ are the <NoteLink to="linear-prediction">reflection coefficients</NoteLink>, also the partial autocorrelations. Because each $\sigma_m^2$ is a
prediction-error variance and cannot be negative, $\abs{\kappa_m} \le 1$; and $\abs{\kappa_m} < 1$ for all $m$ is
exactly the condition for the fitted filter to be stable. The recursion also returns every lower-order model on the
way, which makes order selection cheap.

## Burg's method

Yule–Walker estimates the autocovariance first, and the biased estimator's taper on large lags blurs the result for
short records. Burg's method estimates each reflection coefficient directly from the data instead, choosing $\kappa_m$
to minimise the sum of forward and backward prediction-error powers:

$$
\kappa_m = \frac{2\sum_n f_{m-1}[n]\, b_{m-1}[n-1]}{\sum_n \paren{f_{m-1}[n]^2 + b_{m-1}[n-1]^2}},
$$

then updating the errors $f_m[n] = f_{m-1}[n] - \kappa_m b_{m-1}[n-1]$ and $b_m[n] = b_{m-1}[n-1] - \kappa_m f_{m-1}[n]$.
By the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality, $\abs{\kappa_m} \le 1$, so the model is always stable. Burg's estimates
are also the maximum-entropy spectrum: of all spectra whose inverse transform matches the first $p + 1$
autocovariances, the AR($p$) spectrum has the greatest <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink>, so it assumes the least about the unmeasured lags.

## Choosing the order

Too low an order cannot produce all the peaks; too high an order fits the noise and splits peaks into spurious ones.
Criteria balance the prediction-error variance against the number of parameters:

$$
\operatorname{AIC}(p) = N\log\hat\sigma_p^2 + 2p, \qquad \operatorname{BIC}(p) = N\log\hat\sigma_p^2 + p\log N .
$$

<Gloss name="aic" /> tends to choose orders that are too high for spectral work on long records; <Gloss name="bic" /> and the minimum description length
criterion penalise order more heavily.

## Worked example

Take the AR(2) process $x[n] = 1.3\,x[n-1] - 0.8\,x[n-2] + e[n]$ with $\sigma^2 = 1$. Its poles are the roots of
$z^2 - 1.3z + 0.8$, namely $0.65 \pm 0.614i$, with radius $\sqrt{0.8} = 0.894$ and angle 0.757 rad. Its autocovariances
are $r[0] = 5.806$, $r[1] = 4.194$ and $r[2] = 0.806$. Levinson–Durbin on these values gives

- $\kappa_1 = r[1]/r[0] = 0.7222$ and $\sigma_1^2 = 5.806\,(1 - 0.7222^2) = 2.778$;
- $\kappa_2 = (r[2] - 0.7222\, r[1])/2.778 = -0.8$, then $a_1 = 0.7222 - (-0.8)(0.7222) = 1.3$, $a_2 = -0.8$, and
  $\sigma_2^2 = 2.778\,(1 - 0.64) = 1$.

The recursion recovers the model exactly from exact autocovariances. The spectrum peaks at $\omega = 0.751$ rad, close
to the pole angle but not equal to it, with $S = 53.0$, or 17.2 dB. From 20,000 simulated samples, Burg's method
estimates $(1.302, -0.802)$ and Yule–Walker $(1.302, -0.801)$.

## Pitfalls

- **Peak location differs from pole angle.** A spectral peak sits near, not at, the angle of its pole pair; for close or broad
  peaks the difference matters.
- **Line splitting and bias.** Yule–Walker on short records biases peak locations; Burg can split a single sinusoid's
  peak in two, especially with a nonzero initial phase.
- **Noise in the data.** An AR process observed in additive white noise is an <Gloss name="arma" /> process, and a pure AR fit
  flattens its peaks.
