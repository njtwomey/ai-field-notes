<Definition>
The periodogram of samples $x[0], \dots, x[N-1]$ of a wide-sense stationary process is

$$
\hat S(\omega) = \frac{1}{N}\abs{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\, e^{-i\omega n}}^2 .
$$

It estimates the power spectral density $S(\omega) = \sum_m r[m] e^{-i\omega m}$, where $r[m]$ is the autocovariance.
Evaluated at the DFT frequencies $\omega_k = 2\pi k/N$ it is $\abs{X[k]}^2/N$, computed with one FFT
<Cite id="stoica2005" />.
</Definition>

## The periodogram is the transform of the sample autocovariance

Expand the squared magnitude as a double sum, $\abs{X}^2 = \sum_n \sum_{n'} x[n] x[n'] e^{-i\omega(n - n')}$, and group
the terms by the lag $m = n - n'$:

$$
\hat S(\omega) = \sum_{m=-(N-1)}^{N-1} \hat r[m]\, e^{-i\omega m}, \qquad
\hat r[m] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-\abs{m}} x[n]\, x[n + \abs{m}].
$$

So the periodogram is the Fourier transform of the biased sample autocovariance $\hat r[m]$, which divides by $N$
rather than by the number of terms $N - \abs{m}$. This mirrors the definition of $S(\omega)$ as the transform of
$r[m]$, which is why the periodogram is the natural first estimate. Because it is a squared magnitude, it is never
negative.

## Bias: a smoothed spectrum

For a zero-mean process, $\expect[x[n]x[n+m]] = r[m]$, so $\expect[\hat r[m]] = (1 - \abs{m}/N)\, r[m]$ for
$\abs{m} < N$. Taking expectations term by term,

$$
\expect\brack{\hat S(\omega)} = \sum_{\abs{m} < N}\paren{1 - \frac{\abs{m}}{N}} r[m]\, e^{-i\omega m}
= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} S(\nu)\, F_N(\omega - \nu)\, d\nu .
$$

The second form follows because multiplying $r[m]$ by the triangle $1 - \abs{m}/N$ convolves $S$ with the triangle's
transform, the Fejér kernel

$$
F_N(\omega) = \frac{1}{N}\paren{\frac{\sin(N\omega/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}}^2 .
$$

$F_N$ has a main lobe of width $4\pi/N$ and sidelobes that fall off slowly, the first about 13 dB below the peak. The
expected periodogram is therefore the true spectrum seen through this kernel. Two consequences follow:

- **Resolution.** Two spectral peaks closer than about $2\pi/N$ rad/sample merge into one.
- **Leakage.** Power from strong peaks spreads through the sidelobes and can bury weak components far away in
  frequency. Tapering the data with a window before the transform, the *modified periodogram*, trades a wider main
  lobe for lower sidelobes <Cite id="harris1978" />; see <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">spectral leakage
  and windows</NoteLink>.

As $N \to \infty$ the kernel concentrates at 0, and $\expect[\hat S(\omega)] \to S(\omega)$ wherever $S$ is
continuous. The periodogram is asymptotically unbiased.

## Variance: the periodogram is not consistent

Take Gaussian white noise with variance $\sigma^2$, so $S(\omega) = \sigma^2$. At a DFT frequency $\omega_k$ with
$0 < k < N/2$, the real and imaginary parts of $X[k]$ are sums of independent Gaussians:

$$
\operatorname{Re} X[k] = \sum_n x[n] \cos(\omega_k n), \qquad \operatorname{Im} X[k] = -\sum_n x[n]\sin(\omega_k n).
$$

Both have mean 0. Their variances are $\sigma^2 \sum_n \cos^2(\omega_k n) = \sigma^2 N/2$ and likewise
$\sigma^2 N/2$, and their covariance is proportional to $\sum_n \cos(\omega_k n)\sin(\omega_k n) = 0$, by the
orthogonality of the DFT basis. So

$$
\hat S(\omega_k) = \frac{(\operatorname{Re} X[k])^2 + (\operatorname{Im} X[k])^2}{N}
= \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\paren{Z_1^2 + Z_2^2}, \qquad Z_1, Z_2 \iid \Gauss(0, 1).
$$

$Z_1^2 + Z_2^2$ is $\chi^2_2$, an exponential variable with mean 2. The periodogram value is therefore exponential with
mean $\sigma^2$ and standard deviation $\sigma^2$, for every $N$. The relative error is 100%, however much data there
is. More data only produces more frequency bins, and values at different DFT frequencies are independent, so the
estimate gets more jagged, not smoother. The same holds approximately for coloured spectra:
$\hat S(\omega) \approx S(\omega)\, \chi^2_2 / 2$ <Cite id="stoica2005" />.

<PeriodogramNoise />

The fix is to trade frequency resolution for variance by averaging. <NoteLink to="welch-method">Welch's method</NoteLink>
averages periodograms of segments; <NoteLink to="multitaper-spectral-estimation">multitaper estimation</NoteLink>
averages periodograms of the same data under orthogonal tapers; smoothing the periodogram across neighbouring
frequencies does the same job directly.

## Worked example

Take $x = (1, 0, -1, 0)$, so $N = 4$. Its DFT is $X[k] = 1 - e^{-i\pi k}$, which gives $X = (0, 2, 0, 2)$. The
periodogram at the DFT frequencies $\omega_k = \pi k/2$ is $\abs{X[k]}^2/4 = (0, 1, 0, 1)$: all the power sits at
$\omega = \pi/2$, the frequency of the sequence, and its mirror image. Parseval's theorem checks the scaling: the mean
of the periodogram values is $\tfrac{1}{4}(0 + 1 + 0 + 1) = 0.5$, which equals the mean power
$\tfrac{1}{4}\sum x[n]^2 = 0.5$.

For white noise, the interior periodogram values of a simulated record of 4,096 unit-variance samples have mean 0.997
and standard deviation 0.975, close to the theoretical 1 and 1. With 256 samples the figures are 1.02 and 1.09: the
spread does not shrink.

## Pitfalls

- **Scaling conventions differ.** Libraries report one-sided densities (doubling interior bins), use frequency in Hz
  (dividing by the sample rate), or omit the $1/N$. Check that the integral of the estimate over frequency equals the
  signal's variance.
- **The mean leaks.** A nonzero mean puts a large spike at $\omega = 0$ whose sidelobes leak into low frequencies.
  Subtract the mean, and remove trends, first.
- **Zero-padding does not add resolution.** Padding evaluates the same smoothed estimate on a finer grid; see
  <NoteLink to="zero-padding-and-resolution">zero-padding and resolution</NoteLink>.
