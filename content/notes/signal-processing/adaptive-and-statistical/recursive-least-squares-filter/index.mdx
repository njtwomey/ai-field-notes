<Definition>
At time $n$ the RLS filter chooses the weights that minimise the exponentially weighted squared error over all data so
far,

$$
\mathcal{E}(\wvec) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda^{n-i} \paren{d[i] - \wvec^\top\xvec[i]}^2,
$$

with forgetting factor $0 < \lambda \le 1$. It computes the minimiser recursively, updating the previous solution with
each new sample instead of re-solving from scratch <Cite id="sayed2008" />.
</Definition>

## The exact solution at each step

Setting the gradient of $\mathcal{E}$ to zero gives the normal equations of weighted least squares:

$$
\Phimat[n]\,\wvec[n] = \zvec[n], \qquad
\Phimat[n] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{n-i}\xvec[i]\xvec[i]^\top, \qquad
\zvec[n] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{n-i}\xvec[i]\,d[i].
$$

$\Phimat$ and $\zvec$ are exponentially weighted estimates of the correlation matrix $\Rmat$ and cross-correlation
$\pvec$ of the <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filter</NoteLink>. Both update by one rank-one term per sample:

$$
\Phimat[n] = \lambda\,\Phimat[n-1] + \xvec[n]\xvec[n]^\top, \qquad \zvec[n] = \lambda\,\zvec[n-1] + \xvec[n]\,d[n].
$$

## The recursion via the matrix inversion lemma

Solving $\Phimat[n]\wvec = \zvec$ afresh costs $O(M^3)$. Instead, track $\Pmat[n] = \Phimat[n]^{-1}$. The matrix
inversion lemma, $(\Amat + \uvec\uvec^\top)^{-1} = \Amat^{-1} - \Amat^{-1}\uvec\uvec^\top\Amat^{-1}/(1 +
\uvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\uvec)$, applied with $\Amat = \lambda\Phimat[n-1]$ and $\uvec = \xvec[n]$, gives

$$
\Pmat[n] = \frac{1}{\lambda}\paren{\Pmat[n-1] - \kvec[n]\,\xvec[n]^\top\Pmat[n-1]}, \qquad
\kvec[n] = \frac{\Pmat[n-1]\,\xvec[n]}{\lambda + \xvec[n]^\top\Pmat[n-1]\,\xvec[n]}.
$$

The vector $\kvec[n]$ is the gain. It satisfies $\kvec[n] = \Pmat[n]\,\xvec[n]$, which follows by multiplying the
$\Pmat$ update by $\xvec[n]$. Substituting into $\wvec[n] = \Pmat[n]\zvec[n]$ with the $\zvec$ update, the terms
collapse to

$$
\xi[n] = d[n] - \wvec[n-1]^\top\xvec[n], \qquad \wvec[n] = \wvec[n-1] + \kvec[n]\,\xi[n].
$$

The update has the same form as <NoteLink to="least-mean-squares-filter">LMS</NoteLink>, a correction proportional to the a priori
error $\xi[n]$, but the fixed step $\mu\,\xvec[n]$ is replaced by $\Pmat[n]\,\xvec[n]$. Multiplying by the inverse
correlation matrix whitens the input, which is why RLS converges at the same rate whatever the eigenvalue spread.
Each step costs $O(M^2)$.

**Initialisation.** $\Pmat[0] = \delta^{-1}\eye$ with small $\delta$, equivalent to a weak ridge penalty
$\delta\lambda^n\norm{\wvec}^2$ added to $\mathcal{E}$, and $\wvec[0] = \zeros$.

## The forgetting factor

With $\lambda = 1$ every sample counts equally and $\wvec[n]$ is the ordinary least-squares solution on all the data,
suited to a stationary system. With $\lambda < 1$ a sample $k$ steps old is weighted by $\lambda^k$, so the effective
memory is

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^k = \frac{1}{1 - \lambda}
$$

samples. The filter then tracks a changing system, at the cost of noisier estimates from the shorter memory.

**Worked example.** $\lambda = 0.99$ gives a memory of 100 samples, and $\lambda = 0.999$ of 1000. For an 8-tap
filter, RLS typically converges in about $2M = 16$ samples, while LMS on strongly correlated input can need thousands.

## Relations

- **Kalman filter.** RLS is the Kalman filter for a state that is constant ($\lambda = 1$) or follows a random walk,
  with the weights as the state.
- **Least squares.** With $\lambda = 1$ and no noise, RLS reproduces
  <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> exactly, one row at a time.

## Pitfalls

- **Numerical stability.** In finite precision, $\Pmat[n]$ can lose symmetry and positive definiteness, especially
  with $\lambda < 1$ over long runs. Symmetrising $\Pmat$ each step, or using square-root or QR-decomposition forms,
  prevents this.
- **Cost.** $O(M^2)$ per sample is expensive for long filters. Fast transversal RLS algorithms reduce it to $O(M)$ for
  tap-delay-line inputs but are more fragile.
- **Burst sensitivity with $\lambda < 1$.** When the input is not persistently exciting, $\Pmat$ grows by $1/\lambda$
  each step and the next input burst causes a large jump in the weights.
