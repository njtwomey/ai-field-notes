<Definition>
Let the input be $x[n] = s[n - n_0] + v[n]$: a known finite signal $s$ of energy $E_s = \sum_n s^2[n]$, delayed by an
unknown $n_0$, in zero-mean white noise of variance $\sigma^2$. The matched filter has <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink>
$h[n] = s[-n]$, the time-reversed template, so its output is the cross-correlation

$$
y[n] = \sum_k s[k]\, x[n + k].
$$

Among all linear time-invariant filters it maximises the ratio of the output signal's peak power to the output noise
power, and the maximum is $E_s/\sigma^2$ <Cite id="turin1960" />.
</Definition>

## Why the time-reversed template is optimal

Pass $x$ through a filter $h$ and sample the output at time $n_0$. The signal part is
$\sum_k h[k]\,s[-k]$ and the noise part is $\sum_k h[k]\,v[n_0 - k]$, whose variance is $\sigma^2\sum_k h^2[k]$ since the
noise is white. The output <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratio</Gloss> is

$$
\mathrm{SNR}(h) = \frac{\paren{\sum_k h[k]\,s[-k]}^2}{\sigma^2 \sum_k h^2[k]}.
$$

By the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink>, $\paren{\sum_k h[k]\,s[-k]}^2 \le \sum_k h^2[k] \sum_k s^2[-k] = \norm{\hvec}^2 E_s$,
so

$$
\mathrm{SNR}(h) \le \frac{E_s}{\sigma^2},
$$

with equality exactly when $h[k]$ is proportional to $s[-k]$. Scaling $h$ changes signal and noise alike, so the
choice $h[n] = s[-n]$ is optimal. The maximum depends only on the signal's energy, not on its shape.

In the frequency domain, $H(e^{i\omega}) = S^*(e^{i\omega})$: the filter's magnitude follows the signal's spectrum,
emphasising the frequencies where the signal has energy, and its phase cancels the signal's phase so that every
frequency component peaks at the same time.

**Coloured noise.** If the noise has <NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">power spectrum</NoteLink> $S_{vv}(\omega)$, whitening first and then matching gives
$H(e^{i\omega}) \propto S^*(e^{i\omega})/S_{vv}(\omega)$: frequencies with more noise are weighted less.

## Worked example

Let $s = (1, 2, 1)$ in unit-variance white noise. Its energy is $E_s = 1 + 4 + 1 = 6$, so the matched filter
$h = (1, 2, 1)$ reversed, which is the same sequence, gives a peak output SNR of $6$, or $10\log_{10} 6 = 7.78$ dB.
A three-point averaging filter $h = (1, 1, 1)/3$ instead gives
$\paren{(1 + 2 + 1)/3}^2 / (3/9) = (16/9)/(1/3) = 5.33$, or 7.27 dB. The matched filter's advantage is the ratio of
$E_s \norm{\hvec}^2$ to $(\hvec \cdot \svec)^2$, the Cauchy–Schwarz gap.

## Detection and pulse compression

- **Detection.** Comparing $y[n]$ with a threshold is the optimal detector of a known signal in white Gaussian noise:
  the log-likelihood ratio of "signal present at $n_0$" against "noise only" is an affine function of $y[n_0]$.
- **Pulse compression.** Radar and sonar transmit long chirps and match-filter the echo. The energy of a long pulse
  gives a high SNR, while the chirp's autocorrelation has a narrow main lobe, so the echo is located as precisely as
  a short pulse would allow.
- **Template matching.** Correlating an image or a sensor stream with a template, and normalising for local energy,
  is the same operation.

## Pitfalls

- **The template must be right.** A mismatch in shape, frequency or time scale lowers the peak. A Doppler-shifted
  echo needs a bank of matched filters over the possible shifts.
- **Maximum SNR is not minimum distortion.** The matched filter does not reproduce the signal; its output is the
  signal's autocorrelation. To estimate the waveform itself, use a <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filter</NoteLink>.
- **Coloured noise needs whitening.** Using $h[n] = s[-n]$ in coloured noise is no longer optimal.
