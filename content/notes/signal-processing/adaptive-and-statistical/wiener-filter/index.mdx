<Definition>
Let $x[n]$ be an observed wide-sense stationary signal and $d[n]$ a desired signal jointly stationary with it. The
Wiener filter is the <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">linear time-invariant</NoteLink> filter $h$ whose output $y[n] = \sum_k h[k]\,x[n - k]$ minimises the
<Gloss name="mse">mean-squared error</Gloss> $\expect\brack{e^2[n]}$, with $e[n] = d[n] - y[n]$ <Cite id="wiener1949" />. With $M$ taps the
coefficients $\wvec = (h[0], \dots, h[M-1])$ solve the Wiener–Hopf equations

$$
\Rmat \wvec = \pvec, \qquad [\Rmat]_{jk} = r_{xx}[j - k], \qquad [\pvec]_j = r_{dx}[j] = \expect\brack{d[n]\,x[n - j]}.
$$
</Definition>

Three settings share this definition. In **denoising**, $x = s + v$ and $d = s$. In **prediction**, $d[n] = x[n + 1]$,
which is <NoteLink to="linear-prediction">linear prediction</NoteLink>. In **deconvolution**, $x$ is a blurred and noisy
version of $d$.

## The orthogonality principle

Collect the $M$ most recent inputs into $\xvec[n] = (x[n], x[n-1], \dots, x[n-M+1])$, so $y[n] = \wvec^\top\xvec[n]$
and

$$
J(\wvec) = \expect\brack{\paren{d[n] - \wvec^\top\xvec[n]}^2} = \sigma_d^2 - 2\wvec^\top\pvec + \wvec^\top\Rmat\wvec,
$$

with $\Rmat = \expect[\xvec\xvec^\top]$ and $\pvec = \expect[d\,\xvec]$. This is a quadratic in $\wvec$ with a
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive semidefinite</NoteLink> <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> $2\Rmat$. Setting the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $-2\pvec + 2\Rmat\wvec$ to zero gives the Wiener–Hopf
equations $\Rmat\wvec = \pvec$. Because the input is stationary, $[\Rmat]_{jk} = r_{xx}[j-k]$ depends only on $j - k$:
$\Rmat$ is a symmetric Toeplitz matrix.

The same condition, read differently, is the orthogonality principle. The gradient is $-2\expect[e[n]\,\xvec[n]]$, so at
the optimum

$$
\expect\brack{e[n]\,x[n - j]} = 0 \quad \text{for } j = 0, \dots, M - 1:
$$

the error is uncorrelated with every input the filter uses. Geometrically, $y[n]$ is the
<NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> of $d[n]$ onto the span of the inputs, in the
<NoteLink to="dot-product">inner product</NoteLink> $\inner{u}{v} = \expect[uv]$. The minimum error follows by substituting $\Rmat\wvec = \pvec$:

$$
J_{\min} = \sigma_d^2 - \wvec^\top\pvec = \sigma_d^2 - \pvec^\top\Rmat^{-1}\pvec.
$$

## The frequency-domain form

Let the filter be unconstrained: noncausal and infinitely long. The orthogonality condition must hold at every lag,
$\expect[e[n]\,x[n - j]] = 0$ for all $j$, which reads $r_{dx}[j] = \sum_k h[k]\, r_{xx}[j - k]$. This is a <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink>,
$r_{dx} = h * r_{xx}$. Taking the <Gloss name="dtft" /> of both sides turns it into a product of power spectra, so

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = \frac{S_{dx}(\omega)}{S_{xx}(\omega)}.
$$

For denoising, with $x = s + v$ and noise $v$ uncorrelated with $s$, $S_{dx} = S_{ss}$ and $S_{xx} = S_{ss} + S_{vv}$,
so

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = \frac{S_{ss}(\omega)}{S_{ss}(\omega) + S_{vv}(\omega)} = \frac{\mathrm{SNR}(\omega)}{1 + \mathrm{SNR}(\omega)}.
$$

The gain is close to 1 at frequencies where the signal dominates and close to 0 where the noise does. It never
exceeds 1, and it has zero phase.

The causal Wiener filter needs <NoteLink to="minimum-phase-filters">spectral factorisation</NoteLink> of $S_{xx}$,
which Wiener solved in his original treatment.

## Worked example: an AR(1) signal in white noise

Let $s[n] = 0.9\,s[n-1] + w[n]$ with unit-variance white $w$, so $\sigma_s^2 = 1/(1 - 0.81) = 5.263$ and
$r_{ss}[k] = 5.263 \times 0.9^{\abs{k}}$. Add white noise $v$ with $\sigma_v^2 = 5.263$, an <Gloss name="snr" /> of 0 dB. Then
$r_{xx}[k] = r_{ss}[k] + \sigma_v^2\,\delta[k]$ and $p[k] = r_{ss}[k]$.

- **One tap.** $w_0 = r_{ss}[0]/r_{xx}[0] = 5.263/10.526 = 0.5$, and $J_{\min} = 5.263 - 0.5 \times 5.263 = 2.632$:
  half the signal power.
- **Two taps.** Solving
  $\begin{bmatrix} 10.526 & 4.737 \\ 4.737 & 10.526 \end{bmatrix}\wvec = \begin{bmatrix} 5.263 \\ 4.737 \end{bmatrix}$
  gives $\wvec = (0.373, 0.282)$ and $J_{\min} = 1.963$.
- **More taps.** With 5 taps $J_{\min} = 1.619$, and with 10 taps $1.598$: the causal filter converges quickly because
  the signal's correlation decays as $0.9^k$.
- **Noncausal.** Integrating $S_{ss}S_{vv}/(S_{ss} + S_{vv})$ over frequency gives $J_{\min} = 1.147$. Using future
  samples as well as past ones lowers the error further.

## Relations

- **Least squares.** Replacing <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> by sample averages turns the Wiener–Hopf equations into the normal
  equations of least squares. The <NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter">recursive least-squares (RLS) filter</NoteLink> solves them recursively.
- **Adaptive filters.** The <NoteLink to="least-mean-squares-filter">least-mean-squares (LMS) filter</NoteLink> is <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> on
  $J(\wvec)$, converging to the Wiener solution without estimating $\Rmat$ and $\pvec$.
- **Toeplitz structure.** When $d[n] = x[n+1]$, the right-hand side is itself a shifted autocorrelation and the
  Levinson recursion solves the system in $O(M^2)$ operations <Cite id="levinson1946" />.
- **Spectral subtraction.** The frequency-domain gain $\mathrm{SNR}/(1 + \mathrm{SNR})$, applied frame by frame to a
  short-time spectrum with an estimated SNR, is the Wiener rule used in
  <NoteLink to="spectral-subtraction-and-denoising">speech enhancement</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Second-order statistics must be known.** In practice $\Rmat$ and $\pvec$ are estimated. For denoising,
  $r_{ss}$ is not observable directly; it is usually obtained as $r_{xx} - \sigma_v^2\delta$ with a noise estimate.
- **Linear and stationary only.** The Wiener filter is the best linear estimator. For Gaussian signals it is also
  the best estimator of any form; otherwise nonlinear estimators can do better. For nonstationary signals it must be
  recomputed or replaced by an adaptive or <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>.
- **Ill-conditioning.** When the input spectrum has deep nulls, $\Rmat$ is nearly singular and the taps become large
  and noise-sensitive. Diagonal loading, adding a small multiple of $\eye$, regularises the solution
  <Cite id="sayed2008" />.
