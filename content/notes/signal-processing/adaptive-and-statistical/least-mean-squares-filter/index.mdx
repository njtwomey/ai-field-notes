<Definition>
An LMS filter produces $y[n] = \wvec[n]^\top\xvec[n]$ from the tap-input vector
$\xvec[n] = (x[n], \dots, x[n - M + 1])$, compares it with a desired signal $d[n]$, and updates its weights by

$$
e[n] = d[n] - \wvec[n]^\top \xvec[n], \qquad \wvec[n + 1] = \wvec[n] + \mu\, e[n]\, \xvec[n],
$$

with step size $\mu > 0$ <Cite id="widrow1976" />. It needs no knowledge of the signal statistics and costs $O(M)$
operations per sample.
</Definition>

## Where the update comes from

The <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filter</NoteLink> minimises $J(\wvec) = \expect[e^2[n]] = \sigma_d^2 -
2\wvec^\top\pvec + \wvec^\top\Rmat\wvec$, whose <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is $\nabla J = -2(\pvec - \Rmat\wvec) = -2\expect[e[n]\,\xvec[n]]$.
Steepest descent would step against this gradient, but $\Rmat$ and $\pvec$ are unknown. LMS replaces the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> by
its current single-sample value, $\hat\nabla J = -2\,e[n]\,\xvec[n]$, and absorbs the factor 2 into $\mu$:

$$
\wvec[n+1] = \wvec[n] - \tfrac{\mu}{2}\hat\nabla J = \wvec[n] + \mu\, e[n]\, \xvec[n].
$$

This is <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink> on the <Gloss name="mse">mean-squared error</Gloss>, one sample at
a time.

## Convergence in the mean

Let $\wvec^\circ = \Rmat^{-1}\pvec$ be the Wiener solution and $\cvec[n] = \wvec[n] - \wvec^\circ$ the weight error.
Under the <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> assumption, that $\xvec[n]$ is independent of $\wvec[n]$, taking expectations of the update gives

$$
\expect\brack{\cvec[n + 1]} = \paren{\eye - \mu\Rmat}\expect\brack{\cvec[n]}.
$$

Write $\Rmat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top$. In the rotated coordinates $\Qmat^\top\cvec$ the modes decouple, and mode $i$
evolves as $(1 - \mu\lambda_i)^n$. Every mode decays exactly when $\abs{1 - \mu\lambda_i} < 1$ for all $i$, that is

$$
0 < \mu < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}.
$$

The slowest mode has time constant about $1/(\mu\lambda_{\min})$ samples. With $\mu$ near its limit, set by
$\lambda_{\max}$, the convergence time is governed by the eigenvalue spread $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$. White input
has spread 1 and converges fastest; strongly correlated input converges slowly.

## Excess error and the practical bound

Convergence in the mean is not the whole story. The weights keep jittering around $\wvec^\circ$, because each step
uses a noisy gradient. The steady-state mean-squared error exceeds the Wiener minimum $J_{\min}$ by the misadjustment

$$
\mathcal{M} = \frac{J_\infty - J_{\min}}{J_{\min}} \approx \frac{\mu\, \trace \Rmat}{2},
$$

valid for small $\mu$ <Cite id="sayed2008" />. Mean-square stability needs roughly $\mu < 2/\trace\Rmat$, a tighter
bound than $2/\lambda_{\max}$ because $\trace\Rmat = \sum_i \lambda_i \ge \lambda_{\max}$. For an input of power
$\sigma_x^2$, $\trace\Rmat = M\sigma_x^2$, the total tap-input power.

**Worked example.** With $M = 8$ taps and unit-variance white input, $\Rmat = \eye$, so $\lambda_{\max} = 1$ and
$\trace\Rmat = 8$. Convergence in the mean needs $\mu < 2$, but mean-square stability needs about $\mu < 0.25$. With
$\mu = 0.01$ the misadjustment is $0.01 \times 8/2 = 0.04$: the steady-state error is 4% above the Wiener minimum,
and the time constant is about $1/(0.01 \times 1) = 100$ samples.

## Normalised LMS

The bounds depend on the input power, which is often unknown or time-varying. Normalised LMS divides the step by the
instantaneous tap-input power,

$$
\wvec[n+1] = \wvec[n] + \frac{\tilde\mu}{\epsilon + \norm{\xvec[n]}^2}\, e[n]\, \xvec[n],
$$

with $0 < \tilde\mu < 2$ and a small $\epsilon$. It is stable regardless of the input scale, and is the default in
echo cancellation.

## Pitfalls

- **Correlated inputs converge slowly.** Speech and other coloured signals have large eigenvalue spreads. Transform-
  domain or subband LMS, or the <NoteLink to="recursive-least-squares-filter">RLS filter</NoteLink>, converge faster.
- **Speed against accuracy.** A large $\mu$ tracks changes quickly but leaves more excess error; a small $\mu$ is
  accurate but slow.
- **The independence assumption is false.** Successive tap vectors share $M - 1$ samples. The analysis is still a good
  guide for small $\mu$.
