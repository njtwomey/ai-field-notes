<Definition>
The real cepstrum of a signal $x[n]$ is the inverse Fourier transform of its log magnitude spectrum,

$$
c[q] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \log\abs{X(e^{i\omega})}\, e^{i\omega q}\, d\omega,
$$

computed in practice with the DFT. Its independent variable $q$ is called quefrency and has units of time. The complex
cepstrum uses the complex logarithm $\log X = \log\abs{X} + i\arg X$ with an unwrapped phase, and is invertible
<Cite id="childers1977" />.
</Definition>

## Why the logarithm separates convolved signals

Speech and many other signals are produced by exciting a filter with a source: $x = e * h$, with excitation $e$ and
impulse response $h$. In frequency the convolution is a product, $X = E\,H$, and the logarithm turns the product into
a sum:

$$
\log\abs{X(e^{i\omega})} = \log\abs{E(e^{i\omega})} + \log\abs{H(e^{i\omega})}.
$$

The inverse Fourier transform is linear, so the cepstrum is the sum of the two components' cepstra:
$c_x = c_e + c_h$. If the two occupy different quefrency ranges they can be separated by a window in quefrency, called a
lifter. This is homomorphic deconvolution: a nonlinear map, the logarithm, that turns convolution into addition, where
linear separation works <Cite id="oppenheim2004" />.

For voiced speech the separation is natural:

- **The vocal tract** $H$ has a smooth spectrum with a few formant peaks. Its log spectrum varies slowly in frequency,
  so $c_h$ is concentrated at low quefrencies.
- **The glottal excitation** $E$ is nearly periodic with period $P$ samples. Its spectrum has harmonics spaced
  $f_s/P$ apart, a ripple with a regular period in frequency, so $c_e$ has peaks at $q = P, 2P, \dots$

## Pitch and echo detection

A peak in the cepstrum at quefrency $P$ reveals a periodic ripple in the log spectrum, and hence a period $P$ in the
signal <Cite id="noll1967" />. The same holds for an echo: $x[n] = s[n] + \alpha s[n - D]$ has
$\abs{X} = \abs{S}\,\abs{1 + \alpha e^{-i\omega D}}$, and

$$
\log\abs{1 + \alpha e^{-i\omega D}} = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{m\ge1} \frac{(-1)^{m+1}\alpha^m}{m} e^{-i\omega m D}
$$

for $\abs\alpha < 1$, whose inverse transform has spikes at $q = D, 2D, \dots$ The first use of the cepstrum was exactly
this: finding echo delays in seismic records, and the name reverses the first four letters of "spectrum".

**Worked example.** A voiced frame with $f_0 = 200$ Hz sampled at 16 kHz has period $P = 16000/200 = 80$ samples,
5 ms. Its cepstrum has its highest peak between 2.5 and 12.5 ms at $q = 80$, giving $f_0 = 16000/80 = 200$ Hz.

## Liftering and spectral envelopes

Keeping only $c[q]$ for $\abs{q} < L$ and transforming back smooths the log spectrum: it removes the harmonic ripple
and keeps the envelope. The cut-off $L$ must be below the pitch period. The cepstrum of an all-pole model can also be
computed directly from <NoteLink to="linear-prediction">linear-prediction</NoteLink> coefficients by a recursion.
<NoteLink to="mfcc">Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients</NoteLink> apply the same idea to a mel-warped, filter-bank
spectrum, keeping the lowest dozen coefficients as a compact description of the envelope.

## Pitfalls

- **Logarithm of zero.** Spectral zeros send $\log\abs{X}$ to $-\infty$. A small floor is added before the logarithm.
- **Phase unwrapping.** The complex cepstrum needs a continuous phase, which is fragile with noise and zeros near the
  unit circle. The real cepstrum discards phase and is not invertible, but is robust.
- **Octave errors.** Weak fundamentals, or strong even harmonics, can put the largest peak at $2P$ or $P/2$. Pitch
  trackers constrain the search range and smooth over time.
- **Aliasing in quefrency.** A short DFT aliases long quefrencies. The frame must be several pitch periods long.
