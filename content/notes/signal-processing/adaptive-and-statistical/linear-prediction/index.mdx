<Definition>
The order-$p$ linear predictor of a wide-sense stationary signal $x[n]$ is

$$
\hat x[n] = \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\, x[n-k],
$$

with the coefficients chosen to minimise the mean-squared prediction error $E = \expect\brack{e^2[n]}$, where
$e[n] = x[n] - \hat x[n]$. The prediction-error filter $A(z) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^p a_k z^{-k}$ maps $x$ to $e$, and the
all-pole filter $1/A(z)$ maps $e$ back to $x$ <Cite id="makhoul1975" />.
</Definition>

## The normal equations

Linear prediction is the <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filter</NoteLink> with desired signal $d[n] = x[n]$ and
inputs $x[n-1], \dots, x[n-p]$. The orthogonality principle requires the error to be uncorrelated with every input,
$\expect\brack{e[n]\,x[n-j]} = 0$ for $j = 1, \dots, p$. Substituting $e[n]$ gives

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\, r[j - k] = r[j], \qquad j = 1, \dots, p,
$$

with $r[k] = \expect\brack{x[n]\,x[n-k]}$. In matrix form $\Rmat\avec = \rvec$, with $\Rmat$ the $p \times p$
symmetric Toeplitz matrix of $r[0], \dots, r[p-1]$ and $\rvec = (r[1], \dots, r[p])$. These are the Yule–Walker
equations of an <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss>. The minimum error is

$$
E_p = r[0] - \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k\, r[k].
$$

## The Levinson–Durbin recursion

Solving a general $p \times p$ system costs $O(p^3)$. The Toeplitz structure allows the order-$m$ solution to be built
from the order-$(m-1)$ one in $O(m)$ operations, so all orders up to $p$ cost $O(p^2)$ together
<Cite id="levinson1946" /> <Cite id="durbin1960" />. Starting from $E_0 = r[0]$, for $m = 1, \dots, p$:

$$
k_m = \frac{r[m] - \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} a_j^{(m-1)} r[m-j]}{E_{m-1}}, \qquad
a_m^{(m)} = k_m, \qquad
a_j^{(m)} = a_j^{(m-1)} - k_m\, a_{m-j}^{(m-1)}, \qquad
E_m = E_{m-1}\paren{1 - k_m^2}.
$$

The update works because of a symmetry of Toeplitz systems: the backward predictor, which predicts $x[n-m]$ from the
$m-1$ samples after it, has the same coefficients in reverse order. The new coefficient $k_m$ is chosen so that the
combined forward-and-backward correction makes the new error orthogonal to $x[n-m]$ as well. The $k_m$ are the
reflection coefficients, or partial correlations: $k_m$ is the correlation between the forward and backward prediction
errors of order $m-1$.

Two consequences follow from $E_m = E_{m-1}(1 - k_m^2)$. The error never increases with order. And since
$E_m \ge 0$, every $\abs{k_m} \le 1$; with a valid autocorrelation sequence, $\abs{k_m} < 1$ for all $m$, and then
$A(z)$ has all its zeros inside the unit circle, so the all-pole filter $1/A(z)$ is stable.

**Worked example.** Let $r = (1, 0.5, 0.2)$. Order 1: $k_1 = 0.5$, $a_1^{(1)} = 0.5$, $E_1 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$. Order 2:
$k_2 = (0.2 - 0.5 \times 0.5)/0.75 = -0.0667$, $a_2^{(2)} = -0.0667$,
$a_1^{(2)} = 0.5 - (-0.0667)(0.5) = 0.5333$, and $E_2 = 0.75\,(1 - 0.0044) = 0.7467$. Solving
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\avec = (0.5, 0.2)$ directly gives the same $\avec = (0.5333, -0.0667)$.

## The spectral envelope

If the prediction error is white with power $E_p$, the signal is modelled as white noise through $1/A(z)$, and its
<NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">power spectrum</NoteLink> is

$$
\hat S(\omega) = \frac{E_p}{\abs{A(e^{i\omega})}^2}.
$$

This all-pole spectrum matches the first $p + 1$ autocorrelation values of the signal exactly. It fits peaks better
than valleys, because the error criterion weights regions where the signal spectrum exceeds the model more heavily.
For speech this is what is wanted: the peaks are the formants, the resonances of the vocal tract, and a predictor of
order about twice the number of formants in the band traces them while ignoring the individual harmonics of the voice
<Cite id="rabiner2007" />.

## Uses

- **Speech coding.** Codecs transmit the predictor, as reflection coefficients or line spectral frequencies, plus a
  compact description of the error signal. Code-excited linear prediction (CELP) underlies most mobile-phone speech
  codecs.
- **Spectral estimation.** The all-pole spectrum is a high-resolution
  <NoteLink to="parametric-spectral-estimation">parametric spectral estimate</NoteLink>, the autoregressive or
  maximum-entropy method.
- **Features.** LPC coefficients, or the <NoteLink to="cepstrum">cepstrum</NoteLink> derived from them, were standard speech-recognition features before
  <NoteLink to="mel-frequency-cepstral-coefficients">MFCCs</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Autocorrelation or covariance method.** Estimating $r[k]$ from a windowed frame, the autocorrelation method,
  guarantees a stable filter. Minimising the error over the frame without windowing, the covariance method, fits
  better but can give an unstable $1/A(z)$.
- **Order.** Too low an order merges formants; too high an order fits harmonics and noise.
- **High-pitched voices.** When harmonics are widely spaced, the envelope is sampled coarsely and formant estimates
  are biased toward the nearest harmonic.
