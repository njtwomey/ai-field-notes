<Definition>
For a **wavelet** $\psi(t)$, a function with zero mean that is concentrated in time and in frequency, the
**continuous wavelet transform** of $x(t)$ at scale $a > 0$ and position $b$ is

$$
W_x(a, b) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \int x(t)\, \psi^*\paren{\frac{t - b}{a}}\, dt.
$$

The factor $a^{-1/2}$ gives every dilated wavelet the same energy. The **scalogram** is $\abs{W_x(a, b)}^2$
<Cite id="grossmann1984" />.
</Definition>

## Scale and frequency

In the frequency domain the transform is a bank of bandpass filters. With $\Psi(\omega)$ the Fourier transform of
$\psi$, the dilated wavelet $a^{-1/2}\psi(t/a)$ has transform $\sqrt{a}\, \Psi(a\omega)$, so

$$
W_x(a, b) = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{2\pi} \int X(\omega)\, \Psi^*(a\omega)\, e^{i\omega b}\, d\omega.
$$

If $\Psi$ peaks at $\omega_c$, scale $a$ analyses frequencies near $\omega_c / a$. Dilation by $a$ stretches the
wavelet by $a$ in time and compresses its spectrum by $a$: the time spread is proportional to $a$, the frequency
spread to $1/a$, and their product is fixed by the wavelet. The ratio of centre frequency to bandwidth, the quality
factor, is therefore the same at every scale. This is the constant-Q property: high frequencies are analysed with
short windows and low frequencies with long ones, in contrast to the single window of a
<NoteLink to="short-time-fourier-transform">short-time Fourier transform</NoteLink>. Each coefficient covers a
Heisenberg box of constant area whose shape changes with scale; see
<NoteLink to="time-frequency-uncertainty">time-frequency uncertainty</NoteLink>.

## Admissibility and reconstruction

For a real wavelet, the transform can be inverted when it satisfies the **admissibility condition**

$$
C_\psi = \int_0^\infty \frac{\abs{\Psi(\omega)}^2}{\omega}\, d\omega < \infty,
$$

and then

$$
x(t) = \frac{1}{C_\psi} \int_0^\infty \int W_x(a, b)\, \frac{1}{\sqrt a}\, \psi\paren{\frac{t - b}{a}}\, db\,
\frac{da}{a^2}
$$

<Cite id="mallat2009" />. For a complex analytic wavelet and a real signal, the same integral recovers only the
positive-frequency part $x_+$ of the signal, and $x = 2 \operatorname{Re} x_+$. For
a wavelet with an integrable, continuous transform, $C_\psi < \infty$ forces $\Psi(0) = 0$: the wavelet must have zero
mean. That is what makes it a wave.

The transform is highly redundant: a function of two continuous variables represents a function of one. The
<NoteLink to="discrete-wavelet-transform">discrete wavelet transform</NoteLink> samples the scales and positions on a
dyadic grid and, with suitable wavelets, removes the redundancy entirely.

## Common wavelets

- **Morlet:** $\psi(t) = \pi^{-1/4} e^{i\omega_0 t} e^{-t^2/2}$, a complex sinusoid in a Gaussian envelope. Its
  spectrum is a Gaussian centred on $\omega_0$. It is not exactly zero-mean: $\Psi(0)$ is proportional to
  $e^{-\omega_0^2/2}$, about $1.5 \times 10^{-8}$ for the usual $\omega_0 = 6$, so the correction term is dropped in
  practice. Being complex, it gives an amplitude and a phase at each point <Cite id="torrence1998" />.
- **Mexican hat** (Ricker): the negative second derivative of a Gaussian, $\psi(t) \propto (1 - t^2)\, e^{-t^2/2}$.
  Real, so its transform oscillates with the signal's phase.
- **Derivatives of Gaussians** of other orders detect edges and singularities.

## Worked example

For the Morlet wavelet with $\omega_0 = 6$, the centre frequency at scale $a$ seconds is
$\omega_0/(2\pi a) \approx 0.955/a$ Hz, so $a = 10$ ms analyses about 95.5 Hz. The Gaussian envelope of the dilated
wavelet has a standard deviation of $a$ in time, and its spectrum a standard deviation of $1/(2\pi a)$ in frequency.
At 100 Hz, with $a = \omega_0/(2\pi \times 100) \approx 9.5$ ms, that is a time spread of 9.5 ms and a frequency
spread of about 16.7 Hz; at 10 Hz the time spread is ten times larger and the frequency spread ten times smaller.

## Pitfalls

- **The cone of influence.** Near the ends of a finite signal, large-scale wavelets overhang the data. Coefficients
  within about one envelope width of an edge are unreliable.
- **Scale is not frequency.** The conversion $f = f_c / a$ uses the wavelet's centre frequency; for broad wavelets
  such as the Mexican hat the mapping is loose.
- **Normalisation conventions differ.** $a^{-1/2}$ preserves energy across scales; $a^{-1}$ preserves amplitude. The
  same sinusoid has different scalogram heights at different scales under the first convention.
