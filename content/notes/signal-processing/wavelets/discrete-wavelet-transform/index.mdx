<Definition>
The **discrete wavelet transform** represents a signal in an orthonormal basis of wavelets
$\psi_{j,k}(t) = 2^{-j/2}\, \psi(2^{-j} t - k)$ on the dyadic grid of scales $2^j$ and positions $k 2^j$. For sampled
signals it is computed by **Mallat's algorithm**: at each level, filter the current approximation $a_j$ with a lowpass
filter $h$ and a highpass filter $g$, and downsample both by two <Cite id="mallat1989" />:

$$
a_{j+1}[k] = \sum_n h[n - 2k]\, a_j[n], \qquad d_{j+1}[k] = \sum_n g[n - 2k]\, a_j[n],
$$

with $a_0 = x$ and $g[n] = (-1)^n h[L - 1 - n]$ for a filter of length $L$.
</Definition>

## The pyramid

Level 1 splits the signal into an approximation $a_1$ and details $d_1$, each half as long. Level 2 splits $a_1$
again, and so on. After $J$ levels the transform is $\set{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_J, a_J}$, with $N/2 + N/4 + \dots +
N/2^J + N/2^J = N$ coefficients in total, the same number as the signal. The details $d_j$ hold the content in the
octave $\pi/2^{j} < \abs{\omega} < \pi/2^{j-1}$ of the original rate; $a_J$ holds everything below $\pi / 2^J$.

Each level is one stage of a two-channel <NoteLink to="perfect-reconstruction-filter-banks">perfect-reconstruction
filter bank</NoteLink>, applied to the lowpass branch only. When $h$ and $g$ form an orthogonal pair, each stage is an
orthogonal transform, and so is the whole pyramid: energy is preserved,
$\sum_n x[n]^2 = \sum_j \sum_k d_j[k]^2 + \sum_k a_J[k]^2$, and the inverse runs the stages backwards with the same
filters, upsampling and summing.

## Cost

Level $j$ processes a signal of length $N / 2^{j-1}$ with two filters of $L$ taps and keeps half the outputs, costing
about $L N / 2^{j-1}$ multiplications. Summing the geometric series over all levels gives less than $2 L N$: the
whole transform is $O(N)$, cheaper than an FFT's $O(N \log N)$.

## Worked example

The Haar filters are $h = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $g = (1, -1)/\sqrt2$: each level replaces pairs of samples by their
scaled sum and difference. For $x = (4, 6, 10, 12, 8, 6, 5, 5)$:

- Level 1: $a_1 = (10, 22, 14, 10)/\sqrt2 \approx (7.071, 15.556, 9.899, 7.071)$ and
  $d_1 = (-2, -2, 2, 0)/\sqrt2 \approx (-1.414, -1.414, 1.414, 0)$.
- Level 2, from $a_1$: $a_2 = (32, 24)/2 = (16, 12)$ and $d_2 = (-12, 4)/2 = (-6, 2)$.
- Level 3, from $a_2$: $a_3 = 28/\sqrt2 \approx 19.799$ and $d_3 = 4/\sqrt2 \approx 2.828$.

The energy of the signal is $16 + 36 + 100 + 144 + 64 + 36 + 25 + 25 = 446$. The coefficients give
$392 + 8 + 36 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 0 = 446$, as orthogonality requires. Most of the energy sits in the single coarse
coefficient $a_3$, which is $\sqrt8$ times the mean; the details are small where the signal is locally smooth. This
concentration is what makes wavelet transforms useful for compression and denoising.

## Uses

- **Compression.** Piecewise-smooth signals and images have few large wavelet coefficients; JPEG 2000 codes a
  biorthogonal wavelet transform.
- **Denoising.** Thresholding the details removes noise while keeping edges. See
  <NoteLink to="wavelet-denoising">wavelet denoising</NoteLink>.
- **Features.** Energies of the detail bands summarise a signal's content by octave.

## Pitfalls

- **Boundaries.** Filtering a finite signal needs an extension at its ends. Periodic extension keeps the transform
  orthogonal but creates a jump where the ends meet; symmetric extension avoids the jump but needs symmetric,
  biorthogonal filters to stay invertible.
- **Shift variance.** Downsampling makes the coefficients depend on the alignment of the signal with the dyadic grid;
  shifting the input by one sample can change the coefficients completely. The undecimated (stationary) wavelet
  transform removes the downsampling and is shift invariant, at the cost of redundancy.
- **Length.** A $J$-level transform needs a length divisible by $2^J$, or padding.
