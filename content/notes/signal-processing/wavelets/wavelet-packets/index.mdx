<Definition>
The **wavelet packet decomposition** applies the two-channel split of the
<NoteLink to="discrete-wavelet-transform">discrete wavelet transform</NoteLink> to every subband, not only the
lowpass one. After $J$ levels it forms a full binary tree whose $2^j$ nodes at depth $j$ are subbands of equal width
$\pi / 2^j$. Any set of nodes whose frequency intervals tile $[0, \pi]$ without overlap gives an orthonormal basis
<Cite id="coifman1992" />.
</Definition>

## The tree and its bases

Each node holds the coefficients of one subband. Splitting a node with the orthogonal lowpass and highpass filters
and downsampling produces its two children, and the children together represent exactly the same signal as the
parent, because each split is an orthogonal transform. So any **admissible tree**, a set of leaves obtained by
choosing, at each node, either to stop or to split, gives an orthonormal basis.

The ordinary discrete wavelet transform is one such tree: it splits only the lowpass branch. The uniform tree that
splits everything to depth $J$ gives $2^J$ equal-width bands, like a <Gloss name="stft">short-time Fourier transform</Gloss>. Every choice in
between adapts the time-frequency tiling to the signal.

## How many bases

Let $B_J$ be the number of admissible trees of depth at most $J$ below a node. A node either stays a leaf, or splits
into two children each with $B_{J-1}$ choices, so

$$
B_J = B_{J-1}^2 + 1, \qquad B_0 = 1.
$$

This grows doubly exponentially: $B_J \ge 2^{2^{J-1}}$ for $J \ge 1$, with equality only at $J = 1$.

**Worked example.** The recursion gives $B_1 = 2$, $B_2 = 5$, $B_3 = 26$, $B_4 = 677$ and $B_5 = 458{,}330$ bases.
A depth-5 tree over 1024 samples already holds nearly half a million orthonormal bases; searching them by brute
force would be pointless.

## The best-basis algorithm

Choose an additive cost $\mathcal{C}$ of a coefficient vector, $\mathcal{C}(c) = \sum_k \phi(c_k)$. A common choice
is the entropy-like $\phi(c) = -c^2 \log c^2$, applied to coefficients normalised by the signal's energy. Additivity
makes the cost of a basis the sum of the costs of its leaves. The best basis is then found bottom-up by dynamic
programming:

1. Compute all nodes of the full tree, which costs $O(L N)$ per level and $O(L N J)$ in total.
2. At the deepest level, each node's best cost is its own cost.
3. Moving up, compare each node's own cost with the sum of its two children's best costs. Keep the smaller, and mark
   the node as a leaf or a split accordingly.
4. The marks at the root define the best basis.

Every node is visited once, so the search is linear in the size of the tree, $O(N \log N)$ for $J = \log_2 N$ levels
<Cite id="coifman1992" />. Additivity is what makes the local decision globally optimal: the best subtree under a
node does not depend on anything outside it.

## Uses

- **Compression and denoising** of signals whose energy sits in bands that the octave split of the ordinary DWT
  divides badly, such as narrowband components at high frequency.
- **Feature extraction**, taking energies of the best-basis subbands.
- **Audio coding**, where adaptive packet trees can approximate <NoteLink to="auditory-scales">critical bands</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Frequency order of the tree.** Splitting a highpass branch reverses the frequency order of its children, because
  downsampling folds the band. The natural (Paley) order of the tree is not frequency order; the Gray-code permutation
  restores it.
- **Leakage between packets.** Short filters split the band imperfectly, and packets deep in the tree inherit the
  leakage of every split above them.
- **The cost decides the basis.** <NoteLink to="entropy">Entropy</NoteLink> favours sparse representations; other costs, such as the number of
  coefficients above a threshold, pick different trees.
