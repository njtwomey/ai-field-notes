<Definition>
For observations $y[n] = f[n] + \sigma z[n]$ with white Gaussian noise $z$, **wavelet shrinkage** computes the
orthogonal <NoteLink to="discrete-wavelet-transform">discrete wavelet transform</NoteLink> of $y$, applies a threshold
rule to each detail coefficient, and inverts. The **hard** rule keeps coefficients with $\abs{c} > \lambda$ and zeroes
the rest; the **soft** rule $\eta_\lambda(c) = \operatorname{sgn}(c) \max(\abs{c} - \lambda, 0)$ also shrinks the
survivors by $\lambda$. The **universal threshold** is $\lambda = \sigma \sqrt{2 \ln N}$ <Cite id="donoho1994" />.
</Definition>

## Why thresholding works

An orthogonal transform maps white Gaussian noise to white Gaussian noise with the same variance: every coefficient of
$\sigma z$ is $\sigma$ times a standard normal, independently. A piecewise-smooth signal behaves differently. Its
details vanish wherever it is locally close to a polynomial of degree below the wavelet's number of
<NoteLink to="wavelet-families">vanishing moments</NoteLink>, so its energy concentrates in a few large coefficients
near edges and in the coarse approximation. The noise is spread thinly over all $N$ coefficients; the signal is
concentrated in a few. A threshold slightly above the noise level therefore removes most of the noise and little of the
signal, and, unlike a linear lowpass filter, it keeps edges sharp, because the large coefficients at an edge survive.

## The universal threshold

The largest of $N$ independent standard normals grows like $\sqrt{2 \ln N}$. Precisely,
$P\paren{\max_{k \le N} \abs{z_k} > \sqrt{2 \ln N}} \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, so with a threshold of
$\sigma\sqrt{2 \ln N}$ the probability that any pure-noise coefficient survives tends to zero. The resulting estimate
is, with high probability, at least as smooth as the true signal. Donoho and Johnstone showed that soft thresholding
at this level has a risk within a factor of order $\ln N$ of an ideal oracle that knows which coefficients to keep,
over wide classes of functions <Cite id="donoho1994" /> <Cite id="donoho1995" />.

The noise level is rarely known. The finest detail coefficients are mostly noise, so it is estimated robustly by their
<NoteLink to="z-score-and-robust-outlier-detection">median absolute deviation</NoteLink>,
$\hat\sigma = \operatorname{median}\abs{d_1[k]} / 0.6745$, where 0.6745 is the upper quartile of the standard normal.

## Worked example

For $N = 1024$ the universal threshold is $\sqrt{2 \ln 1024}\, \sigma \approx 3.72\, \sigma$; for $N = 4096$ it is
$4.08\, \sigma$. The convergence is slow: with $N = 1024$ pure-noise coefficients, the probability that at least one
exceeds $3.72$ in magnitude is still about 0.18.

For Donoho and Johnstone's HeaviSine signal, $f(t) = 4\sin(4\pi t) - \operatorname{sgn}(t - 0.3) -
\operatorname{sgn}(0.72 - t)$, sampled at 1024 points with $\sigma = 0.5$ and a six-level db4 transform with periodic
extension, the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> averaged over 50 noise draws is 0.248 for the noisy data,
0.038 after soft thresholding at the universal threshold, and 0.027 after hard thresholding. Soft thresholding shrinks
the large coefficients too, which biases the jumps; hard thresholding keeps them intact but leaves isolated noise spikes
where a noise coefficient just exceeds the threshold.

## Choosing the threshold

- **Universal** (VisuShrink): simple, conservative, visually smooth; tends to over-smooth.
- **SURE** (SureShrink): chooses a threshold per level to minimise Stein's unbiased estimate of the risk, adapting to
  how sparse each level is.
- **Cross-validation and Bayesian shrinkage** fit the threshold to the data at more cost.

## Pitfalls

- **Shift variance.** Thresholding a decimated transform produces artefacts near edges that depend on the signal's
  alignment with the dyadic grid. Averaging the estimates over all <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">circular
  shifts</NoteLink>, cycle spinning, removes most of them.
- **Coloured noise** has a different level in each band; estimate $\sigma$ per level.
- **Leave the approximation alone.** The coarse coefficients carry the signal's trend; thresholding them removes it.
