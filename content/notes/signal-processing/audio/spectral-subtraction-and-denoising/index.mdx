<Definition>
For a noisy signal $x = s + v$ with additive noise uncorrelated with the signal, spectral subtraction estimates the
clean short-time spectrum frame by frame as

$$
\abs{\hat S_t[k]}^2 = \max\paren{\abs{X_t[k]}^2 - \alpha\,\hat\sigma_v^2[k],\ \beta\abs{X_t[k]}^2}, \qquad
\hat S_t[k] = \abs{\hat S_t[k]}\, e^{i\arg X_t[k]},
$$

where $\hat\sigma_v^2[k]$ is the estimated noise power in bin $k$, $\alpha \ge 1$ an over-subtraction factor and $\beta$
a spectral floor. The enhanced signal is resynthesised by overlap-add <Cite id="boll1979" />.
</Definition>

## Why subtract powers

With the noise uncorrelated with the signal, $\expect\abs{X}^2 = \abs{S}^2 + \sigma_v^2$ in each bin. Subtracting the
expected noise power gives an estimate of the signal power, correct on average. The phase is left noisy: for moderate
SNR the ear is insensitive to phase errors within a frame, and the noisy phase is the best simple estimate.

## The noise estimate

The noise power spectrum must be estimated without the clean signal. Common sources are frames flagged as speech-free
by a voice-activity detector, a leading noise-only segment, or minimum statistics: the minimum of the smoothed power
over a window of a second or two, corrected for its bias, tracks the noise floor even during continuous speech.

## Musical noise

$\abs{X}^2$ in a noise-only bin fluctuates around $\sigma_v^2$, exponentially distributed for Gaussian noise. After
subtraction, most noise-only cells go to zero, but the ones where the noise happened to exceed its mean survive as
isolated peaks scattered in time and frequency. They are heard as short tones popping in and out: musical noise.

Two parameters trade it against speech distortion:

- **Over-subtraction** $\alpha > 1$ removes more than the mean noise, killing most random peaks, but also removes weak
  speech components.
- **A spectral floor** $\beta > 0$ keeps a little of the noisy spectrum everywhere, so the residue is a steady low-level
  hiss that masks the remaining peaks, instead of silence punctuated by tones.

## The Wiener rule and decision-directed estimation

Writing the enhancement as a gain, $\hat S = G\,X$, the frequency-domain <NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener
filter</NoteLink> is

$$
G_t[k] = \frac{\xi_t[k]}{1 + \xi_t[k]},
$$

with $\xi = \abs{S}^2/\sigma_v^2$ the a priori SNR. Estimating $\xi$ from the current frame alone reproduces spectral
subtraction's fluctuations. The decision-directed estimate blends the previous frame's enhanced power with the current
frame's evidence <Cite id="ephraim1984" />:

$$
\hat\xi_t[k] = a\,\frac{\abs{\hat S_{t-1}[k]}^2}{\hat\sigma_v^2[k]} + (1 - a)\max\paren{\gamma_t[k] - 1,\ 0}, \qquad
\gamma_t[k] = \frac{\abs{X_t[k]}^2}{\hat\sigma_v^2[k]},
$$

with $a \approx 0.98$. In noise-only regions the first term is small and changes slowly, so the gain stays low and
smooth instead of flickering, which largely removes musical noise. Ephraim and Malah derived the full MMSE short-time
spectral amplitude estimator; the Wiener gain with this $\hat\xi$ is its common simplification.

## Worked example

A gliding harmonic tone, from 200 to 300 Hz with 8 harmonics, in white noise at 0 dB SNR, sampled at 8 kHz and analysed
with 256-sample Hann frames at hop 128. The first 0.3 s is noise only and gives $\hat\sigma_v^2$. Measured as the SNR of
the enhanced magnitudes against the clean magnitudes, the noisy input scores $-0.2$ dB. Power subtraction with
$\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 0.02$ raises it to 6.0 dB, and the Wiener gain with the decision-directed estimate to 7.8 dB,
with much less visible musical noise.

## Pitfalls

- **Nonstationary noise.** A fixed noise estimate fails when the noise changes; the estimate must track it without
  absorbing speech.
- **Speech distortion at low SNR.** Weak consonants sit below the noise and are removed along with it. Every method
  trades noise reduction against distortion; listening tests and intelligibility measures, not SNR alone, decide.
- **Magnitude-only processing.** Noisy phase limits the achievable quality at low SNR. Modern systems estimate complex
  masks or waveforms directly with neural networks.
