<Definition>
An onset is the start of a musical event, such as a note's attack. Onset detection computes an onset envelope, or
novelty function, that rises sharply when new energy enters the signal, and marks its prominent peaks as onsets
<Cite id="bello2005" />. Beat tracking then finds the regular pulse that the onsets imply: first the tempo, then the
beat times.
</Definition>

## Onset envelopes

Onsets bring a rapid increase of energy, often broadband, at the attack. Several envelopes capture this:

- **Energy.** The rise in frame energy, $\max(0, E_t - E_{t-1})$. Simple, but blind to a new note that does not raise
  the total level.
- **<NoteLink to="spectral-features">Spectral flux</NoteLink>.** The summed increase in magnitude across
  <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">frequency bins</NoteLink>,

  $$
  \Phi_t = \sum_{k} \max\paren{0,\ \log\paren{1 + \gamma\abs{X_t[k]}} - \log\paren{1 + \gamma\abs{X_{t-1}[k]}}}.
  $$

  Half-wave rectification keeps only increases, since offsets are not onsets. Logarithmic compression with a constant
  $\gamma$ lets quiet high-frequency attacks count alongside loud low-frequency ones. Computing the flux on
  <NoteLink to="mel-filter-bank">mel bands</NoteLink> instead of bins, and taking the difference over one or two frames,
  are common refinements.
- **Phase deviation and complex domain.** A steady partial's phase advances predictably from frame to frame; at an
  onset the prediction fails. These catch soft onsets, like a legato violin note, that barely change the magnitude.

## Peak picking

Onsets are local maxima of the envelope that exceed an adaptive threshold, typically a
<NoteLink to="median-filter">moving median</NoteLink> or mean over a few hundred milliseconds plus a fixed margin. A
minimum spacing between onsets suppresses double detections. The moving threshold follows the music's dynamics, so quiet
passages are not missed and loud ones do not flood the output.

## Tempo and beats

The onset envelope of rhythmic music is approximately periodic at the beat period $T$. Its autocorrelation
$R(\ell) = \sum_t \tilde\Phi_t \tilde\Phi_{t+\ell}$, computed on the mean-removed envelope $\tilde\Phi$, peaks at
$\ell = T$ and its multiples. Weighting the autocorrelation by a prior on tempo, often centred near 120 BPM, and taking
the maximum gives the tempo. A tempogram repeats this over sliding windows to follow tempo changes.

Given the tempo, beat tracking chooses beat times $b_1 < b_2 < \dots$ to maximise the onset strength at the beats minus
a penalty for inter-beat intervals that deviate from $T$:

$$
C(\set{b_i}) = \sum_i \Phi_{b_i} - \alpha\sum_i \log^2\paren{\frac{b_i - b_{i-1}}{T}}.
$$

The objective decomposes over consecutive beats, so dynamic programming finds the best sequence in one pass
<Cite id="ellis2007" />.

## Worked example

With a sample rate of 8 kHz and a hop of 64 samples, the onset envelope has 125 frames per second. At 110 BPM the beat
period is $60/110 = 0.545$ s, or $0.545 \times 125 = 68.2$ frames. The autocorrelation peaks at lag 68, giving
$60 \times 125/68 = 110.3$ BPM. The tempo resolution at this hop is about 1.6 BPM near 110 BPM; a smaller hop, or
parabolic interpolation around the peak, refines it.

## Pitfalls

- **<NoteLink to="pitch-estimation">Octave errors</NoteLink> in tempo.** The autocorrelation also peaks at $2T$ and
  $T/2$, and listeners themselves disagree between tapping at 60 or 120 BPM. Evaluations accept either within a
  tolerance, or report both.
- **Soft onsets and vibrato.** Slow attacks give weak flux; vibrato and tremolo give spurious flux. Envelope choice
  depends on the instruments.
- **Frame timing.** An onset's detected time lags its true time by up to half a frame, plus any delay from the
  envelope's smoothing. Report times at the frame centre and compensate for known delays.
