<Definition>
SpecAugment is a family of random transformations applied to the log-mel spectrogram of each training example, not to
the waveform <Cite id="park2019" />. For a spectrogram with $\nu$ mel channels and $\tau$ frames, it applies:

- **time warping** with parameter $W$;
- $m_F$ **frequency masks**, each zeroing $f$ consecutive channels $[f_0, f_0 + f)$, with $f \sim U[0, F]$ and
  $f_0 \sim U[0, \nu - f)$;
- $m_T$ **time masks**, each zeroing $t$ consecutive frames $[t_0, t_0 + t)$, with $t \sim U[0, T]$,
  $t_0 \sim U[0, \tau - t)$, and $t \le p\,\tau$.
</Definition>

## The three operations

**Time warping.** A random point on the time axis, inside $(W, \tau - W)$, is moved left or right by a distance drawn
uniformly from $[0, W)$, and the spectrogram is stretched on one side and compressed on the other to follow. It
imitates small local changes of speaking rate. The original implementation uses an image-warping routine with
anchor points on the image boundary; a piecewise-linear resampling of the time axis has the same effect.

**Frequency masking.** Setting a band of channels to the mean value removes, for example, part of the formant
structure or a range of harmonics. The model must recognise the input from the remaining bands, which mimics partial
frequency loss from channels, codecs and noise. After per-channel mean normalisation the mean is 0, so masks are zeros.

**Time masking.** Zeroing a span of frames removes a short stretch of speech, forcing the model to use context from
either side. The upper bound $t \le p\tau$ keeps masks from covering most of a short utterance.

## Why it works

The transformations do not change the label: a warped or partly masked utterance still says the same words. They are
applied on the fly, cost almost nothing, and work on the features the network actually sees, so no audio
resynthesis is needed. Masking acts like structured dropout on the input, pushing the model away from relying on any
single band or instant. On LibriSpeech and Switchboard it turned recognisers that overfit into ones that underfit,
which then benefited from larger models and longer training <Cite id="park2019" />.

## Worked example

The published policies, for 80-channel inputs <Cite id="park2019" />:

| Policy | $W$ | $F$ | $m_F$ | $T$ | $p$ | $m_T$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LB | 80 | 27 | 1 | 100 | 1.0 | 1 |
| LD | 80 | 27 | 2 | 100 | 1.0 | 2 |
| SM | 40 | 15 | 2 | 70 | 0.2 | 2 |
| SS | 40 | 27 | 2 | 70 | 0.2 | 2 |

Under LD, each frequency mask covers on average $27/2 = 13.5$ of 80 channels, and two of them about a third of the
spectrum at most. With a 10 ms hop, a time mask of up to 100 frames removes up to a second of audio.

## Pitfalls

- **Masks must match the normalisation.** Zero means "average" only after mean normalisation. On unnormalised log-mel
  features, zero is a specific energy level, and masks become artificial loud or quiet bands.
- **Too strong for small inputs.** Policies tuned for long utterances can erase most of a one-second keyword or sound
  event. Scale $T$ and $F$ to the input size.
- **Training only.** Augmentation is applied during training; evaluation uses unaugmented spectrograms.
