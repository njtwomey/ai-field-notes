<Definition>
An auditory frequency scale is a monotone map from frequency $f$ in hertz to a perceptual unit, such that equal
intervals on the scale correspond approximately to equal perceived distances or to a fixed number of the ear's
frequency channels. The three in common use are the mel scale of pitch, the Bark scale of critical bands and the
ERB-number scale of auditory filters.
</Definition>

## The ear's frequency channels

The cochlea analyses sound with a bank of overlapping bandpass filters, each tuned to a place on the basilar membrane.
Two sounds within one filter's bandwidth interact strongly: they mask each other, and their loudnesses do not simply
add. The bandwidth grows with centre frequency, roughly constant at low frequencies and proportional to frequency above
about 1 kHz. The scales below count or approximate these channels, which is why spectral features for audio space
their frequency bins on them rather than in hertz.

## The mel scale

Stevens, Volkmann and Newman asked listeners to judge equal steps of pitch and defined the mel so that a 1000 Hz tone
has a pitch of 1000 mels <Cite id="stevens1937" />. The formula used almost everywhere today is a later analytical fit,

$$
m(f) = 2595 \log_{10}\paren{1 + \frac{f}{700}}, \qquad f(m) = 700\paren{10^{m/2595} - 1}.
$$

Below 700 Hz, $\log_{10}(1 + f/700) \approx f/(700 \ln 10)$ and the scale is nearly linear, $m \approx 1.61 f$. Well
above 700 Hz it is logarithmic. Some toolkits use a variant that is exactly linear below 1 kHz and logarithmic above.
The two differ by a few percent, enough to change features between libraries.

## The Bark scale

Zwicker divided the audible range into 24 critical bands and numbered them; the Bark is one critical band. An
analytical fit to his table is

$$
z(f) = 13\arctan\paren{0.00076 f} + 3.5\arctan\paren{\paren{f/7500}^2},
$$

with critical bandwidth $\Delta f_c = 25 + 75\paren{1 + 1.4 (f/1000)^2}^{0.69}$ Hz <Cite id="zwicker1980" />. A simpler,
invertible fit is $z = 26.81 f/(1960 + f) - 0.53$ <Cite id="traunmuller1990" />.

## The ERB scale

Glasberg and Moore measured auditory filter shapes with notched noise and summarised each filter by its equivalent
rectangular bandwidth: the width of a rectangular filter with the same peak gain that passes the same power of white
noise <Cite id="glasberg1990" />:

$$
\mathrm{ERB}(f) = 24.7\paren{\frac{4.37 f}{1000} + 1}\ \text{Hz}.
$$

The ERB-number scale counts filters: integrating $1/\mathrm{ERB}$ from 0 to $f$ gives

$$
E(f) = \int_0^f \frac{df'}{\mathrm{ERB}(f')} = 21.4 \log_{10}\paren{1 + \frac{4.37 f}{1000}}.
$$

ERBs are narrower than Zwicker's critical bands at low frequencies, and the ERB scale is the basis of the
<NoteLink to="gammatone-filter-bank">gammatone filter bank</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

At $f = 1000$ Hz:

- **Mel.** $2595\log_{10}(1 + 1000/700) = 2595\log_{10}(2.4286) = 1000.0$ mel, by construction.
- **Bark.** $13\arctan(0.76) + 3.5\arctan(0.0178) = 8.449 + 0.062 = 8.51$ Bark. The Traunmüller fit gives 8.53.
- **ERB.** $24.7 \times (4.37 + 1) = 132.6$ Hz wide, at ERB number $21.4\log_{10}(5.37) = 15.6$.
- **Critical bandwidth.** $25 + 75 \times 2.4^{0.69} = 162.2$ Hz, wider than the ERB.

At 100 Hz the ERB is 35.5 Hz against a critical bandwidth of 100.7 Hz; at 4000 Hz, 456 Hz against 685 Hz. Both grow
with frequency, but their low-frequency behaviour differs.

## Pitfalls

- **Several mel formulas.** The HTK form above and the Slaney form, linear to 1 kHz, give different filter placements.
  Features computed with one library do not match another's unless the variant is fixed.
- **Scales are averages.** They summarise listening tests on young listeners with normal hearing, at moderate levels.
  Filter bandwidths widen at high levels and with hearing loss.
- **Pitch is not frequency.** The mel scale describes pure tones. The pitch of a complex tone follows its fundamental,
  even when that component is missing.
