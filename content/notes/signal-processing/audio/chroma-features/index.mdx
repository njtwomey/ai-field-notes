<Definition>
The chroma vector of a frame is a 12-dimensional vector $\cvec = (c_0, \dots, c_{11})$ with one entry per pitch
class, C, C♯, …, B. Each entry sums the spectral energy at all frequencies whose nearest equal-tempered pitch belongs to
that class, over every octave:

$$
c_q = \sum_{k \,:\, \mathrm{pc}(f_k) = q} \abs{X[k]}^2, \qquad \mathrm{pc}(f) = \mathrm{round}\paren{69 + 12\log_2\frac{f}{440\ \mathrm{Hz}}} \bmod 12,
$$

usually normalised to unit maximum or unit norm <Cite id="muller2015" />. A sequence of chroma vectors over time is a
chromagram.
</Definition>

## Why fold octaves

Human pitch has two dimensions: height, which rises steadily with frequency, and chroma, which repeats every octave.
Notes an octave apart sound alike and play the same harmonic role. Harmony depends on which pitch classes sound, not on
the octaves or instruments that play them. Folding the spectrum onto 12 classes discards height and much of timbre, and
keeps exactly the information that chords and keys are made of.

## Computing chroma

1. **A pitch-aligned spectrum.** Either a long-window STFT, whose bins are then assigned to their nearest pitch class
   as in the definition, or a <NoteLink to="constant-q-transform">constant-Q transform</NoteLink> with 12 or 36 bins
   per octave, which aligns bins with semitones directly and has the same relative resolution in every octave.
2. **Fold.** Sum the energy of every bin belonging to each pitch class, over a range of about 60 Hz to 5 kHz.
3. **Normalise.** Scale each frame to unit maximum or norm, so chroma depends on the balance of classes, not loudness.
   Logarithmic compression before folding, and smoothing over time, make chroma more robust.

A long window is needed at low frequencies: adjacent semitones near 65 Hz are about 4 Hz apart, so resolving them needs
a window of a quarter of a second or more.

## Worked example

A C major triad in the fourth octave has fundamentals C4 = 261.63 Hz, E4 = 329.63 Hz and G4 = 392.00 Hz. Their MIDI
numbers are $69 + 12\log_2(261.63/440) = 60$, 64 and 67, so their pitch classes are $60 \bmod 12 = 0$ (C), 4 (E) and
7 (G). A4 = 440 Hz is MIDI 69, class 9 (A).

The harmonics of a single note are not all in its own class. The 2nd and 4th harmonics of C4 are C5 and C6. The 3rd,
784.9 Hz, is closest to G5 (783.99 Hz), class 7. The 5th, 1308.1 Hz, is closest to E6 (1318.5 Hz), class 4. So even a
single C adds energy to G and E, the notes of its own major triad, and bright instruments smear chroma more than
pure tones do.

## Uses

- **Chord recognition.** Compare each chroma vector with binary templates of the 24 major and minor triads, or feed
  chromagrams to a sequence model; smoothing over time, often with an HMM, enforces chord continuity.
- **Key detection.** Correlate a piece's average chroma with key profiles.
- **Alignment and cover-song detection.** Chromagrams of two performances can be aligned by dynamic time warping,
  since they share harmony even when tempo, instrumentation and octave differ. Circularly shifting the vector
  transposes the key.

## Pitfalls

- **Tuning.** Recordings not tuned to A4 = 440 Hz shift energy between neighbouring classes. Estimate the tuning offset
  first and shift the pitch mapping accordingly.
- **Harmonic leakage.** As in the example, overtones feed other classes. Harmonic-sum or log-compressed chroma, and
  learned chroma, reduce the effect.
- **Percussion and noise.** Broadband sounds spread energy over all classes. Harmonic–percussive separation before
  folding cleans the chromagram.
