<Definition>
For a frame with mel filter-bank energies $E_1, \dots, E_M$, the mel-frequency cepstral coefficients are the type-II
discrete cosine transform of the log energies,

$$
c_k = \sqrt{\frac{2 - \delta_{k0}}{M}}\ \sum_{m=1}^{M} \log E_m\ \cos\paren{\frac{\pi k\,(m - \tfrac12)}{M}}, \qquad
k = 0, \dots, K - 1,
$$

keeping the first $K \ll M$ coefficients, typically 13 of 20 to 40 bands. This orthonormal scaling is one of
several in use <Cite id="davis1980" />.
</Definition>

## The pipeline

1. **Pre-emphasis** (optional). Filter with $1 - 0.97 z^{-1}$ to boost high frequencies, which have less energy in
   voiced speech.
2. **Frames and power spectra.** Window 20 to 30 ms frames every 10 ms and take $\abs{X_t[k]}^2$.
3. **Mel filter bank.** Sum power into $M$ mel-spaced triangular bands, giving $E_m$.
4. **Logarithm.** $\log E_m$: loudness compression, and it turns a fixed channel response into an additive offset.
5. **DCT.** Transform the log energies and keep the first $K$ coefficients.
6. **Liftering, deltas, normalisation** (optional). Reweight, append time derivatives, and remove per-utterance means.

Steps 2 to 4 are the <NoteLink to="mel-spectrogram">log-mel spectrogram</NoteLink>. The DCT is what makes the result a
cepstrum: an inverse transform of a log spectrum, as in the <NoteLink to="cepstrum">cepstrum</NoteLink>, but on a
mel-warped, filter-bank-smoothed spectrum.

## Why the DCT

**It separates envelope from detail.** The log-mel energies of neighbouring bands vary slowly with band index where
the envelope is smooth. Low-order cosines capture this slow variation: $c_0$ is proportional to the total log
energy, $c_1$ to the overall spectral tilt, and the next few to the formant pattern. High-order coefficients capture
rapid variation across bands, which is mostly harmonic ripple and noise. Truncating to $K$ coefficients keeps the
envelope and discards the detail, so the features depend on what is said much more than on the speaker's pitch.

**It nearly decorrelates the features.** Neighbouring log-mel energies are strongly correlated. For a stationary
first-order Markov sequence with correlation $\rho$ close to 1, the DCT basis approaches the Karhunen–Loève transform,
the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, which decorrelates exactly <Cite id="ahmed1974" />. Nearly uncorrelated
coefficients suited the diagonal-covariance Gaussian mixture models of classical speech recognition, which could not
afford full covariances.

**Worked example.** Take $M = 4$ log energies $(2.0, 1.5, 0.5, 0.0)$, falling with frequency. The orthonormal DCT-II
gives $\cvec = (2.000, 1.577, 0.000, -0.112)$. $c_0 = (2.0 + 1.5 + 0.5 + 0.0)/2 = 2.0$ is the scaled mean; $c_1 = 1.577$
is large and positive because the energies fall steadily; $c_2 = 0$ because the sequence is antisymmetric about its
centre after removing the mean; $c_3$ is small. For a 20-band model with neighbour correlation $\rho = 0.95$, the
off-diagonal share of the covariance's squared entries falls from 91% for the raw bands to 0.8% after the DCT.

## Liftering and deltas

**Liftering.** High-order coefficients have small variance, and some recognisers weight them up with a sinusoidal
lifter, $c'_k = \paren{1 + \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\paren{\pi k / L}}\, c_k$, with $L = 22$ in HTK. The weights rise from 1
at $k = 0$ to 12 at $k = 11$, equalising the coefficients' ranges.

**Deltas.** A single frame describes a static spectrum. Appending time derivatives, estimated by regression over
$\pm N$ frames,

$$
\Delta c_{k,t} = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N} n\,\paren{c_{k,t+n} - c_{k,t-n}}}{2\sum_{n=1}^{N} n^2},
$$

and the same again for delta-deltas, adds dynamics <Cite id="furui1981" />. The classical speech feature vector is 13
MFCCs with deltas and delta-deltas: 39 numbers per 10 ms frame.

## Pitfalls

- **Implementations differ.** Mel formula, filter normalisation, log base, DCT scaling, whether $c_0$ or the frame
  log-energy is used, and the lifter all vary between toolkits.
- **Noise sensitivity.** Additive noise is not additive after the logarithm, so MFCCs degrade quickly in noise. Mean
  and variance normalisation per utterance helps with channel effects but not with additive noise.
- **Not for every model.** Neural networks trained on log-mel spectrograms usually do as well or better, since the DCT
  destroys the local frequency structure that convolutions exploit.
