<Definition>
The **type-1 polyphase decomposition** of a filter $H(z) = \sum_n h[n] z^{-n}$ with respect to $M$ is

$$
H(z) = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} z^{-m} E_m\paren{z^M}, \qquad E_m(z) = \sum_n h[nM + m] z^{-n}.
$$

The $m$-th **polyphase component** $e_m[n] = h[nM + m]$ keeps every $M$-th coefficient of $h$, starting at $m$
<Cite id="vaidyanathan1993" />.
</Definition>

## Where the identity comes from

Group the terms of $H(z)$ by the remainder of their index modulo $M$. Every integer is uniquely $n = kM + m$ with
$0 \le m < M$, so

$$
H(z) = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} \sum_k h[kM + m] z^{-(kM + m)} = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} z^{-m} \sum_k e_m[k] \paren{z^M}^{-k}
= \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} z^{-m} E_m\paren{z^M}.
$$

Nothing is approximated: the decomposition is a relabelling of the coefficients.

## The noble identities

Two exchanges let a filter move across a rate changer:

$$
\text{downsample by } M \text{, then } G(z) \;\equiv\; G\paren{z^M} \text{, then downsample by } M,
$$

$$
G(z) \text{, then upsample by } L \;\equiv\; \text{upsample by } L \text{, then } G\paren{z^L}.
$$

**Derivation of the first.** $G(z^M)$ has <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> $g$ upsampled by $M$: nonzero only at multiples of $M$.
Filtering with it, $v[n] = \sum_k g[k]\, x[n - kM]$, and downsampling gives
$v[Mn] = \sum_k g[k]\, x[(n - k)M]$. That is $g$ applied to the downsampled sequence $x[Mn]$, which is the left-hand
side. The second identity is the transpose of the first. Filters of the form $G(z^M)$ are exactly what the polyphase
decomposition produces, which is why the two combine.

## The polyphase decimator

A decimator computes $y[n] = \sum_k h[k] x[nM - k]$ and keeps one output in $M$. Substitute the polyphase
decomposition, $h[kM + m] = e_m[k]$:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} \sum_k e_m[k]\, x[(n - k)M - m] = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} (e_m * x_m)[n],
\qquad x_m[n] = x[nM - m].
$$

The input is dealt round-robin into $M$ low-rate streams $x_0, x_1, \dots$, each is filtered by its own short
subfilter at the output rate, and the results are summed. Every multiplication contributes to a kept output. For a
filter of $P$ taps the direct decimator spends $P$ multiplications per input sample, and the polyphase form spends
$P / M$.

The polyphase interpolator is the transpose: one input stream feeds $L$ subfilters $e_m$ running at the input rate,
and a commutator interleaves their outputs.

**Worked example.** A 96-tap anti-aliasing filter for decimation by $M = 4$ has four polyphase components of 24 taps.
The <NoteLink to="difference-equations">direct form</NoteLink> computes 96 multiplications for every input sample and discards three quarters of the results. The
polyphase form computes $4 \times 24 = 96$ multiplications per output sample, which is 24 per input sample, a
four-fold saving with bit-identical output. The equality of the two forms was checked numerically to within
$10^{-14}$ on random inputs.

## Uses

- **Efficient decimators and interpolators**, the main application.
- **Rational sample-rate conversion**, where a polyphase interpolator computes only the outputs the downsampler
  keeps. See <NoteLink to="sample-rate-conversion">sample-rate conversion</NoteLink>.
- **DFT filter banks**, which combine a polyphase network with an <Gloss name="fft" />. See
  <NoteLink to="filter-banks">filter banks</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Index conventions.** Type-1 and type-2 decompositions differ in whether the delays are $z^{-m}$ or
  $z^{-(M - 1 - m)}$; mixing them misaligns the commutator by one sample.
- **Filter length.** When $P$ is not a multiple of $M$, the subfilters have unequal lengths; pad $h$ with zeros to a
  multiple of $M$.
