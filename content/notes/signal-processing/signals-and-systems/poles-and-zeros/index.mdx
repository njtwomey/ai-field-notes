<Definition>
A rational transfer function factors as

$$
H(z) = \frac{B(z)}{A(z)} = b_0\,\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{M} (1 - q_k z^{-1})}{\prod_{k=1}^{N} (1 - p_k z^{-1})}.
$$

The $q_k$, where $H(z) = 0$, are its zeros, and the $p_k$, where $H(z)$ is infinite, are its poles. Together with the
gain $b_0$ they determine $H$ completely <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />. For real coefficients, complex poles and zeros
come in conjugate pairs.
</Definition>

## Stability from the poles

Expand $H(z)$ in partial fractions, as in the <NoteLink to="z-transform">z-transform</NoteLink> note. For distinct
poles, a causal system's impulse response is a sum of geometric sequences, one per pole:

$$
h[n] = \sum_{k} A_k\, p_k^{\,n}\, u[n] \quad (\text{plus a finite FIR part if } M \ge N).
$$

Each term is absolutely summable exactly when $\abs{p_k} < 1$. So a causal LTI system is BIBO stable if and only if
all its poles lie strictly inside the unit circle. A pole at radius $r$ contributes a mode that decays like $r^n$:
the closer to the circle, the longer it rings. A pole on or outside the circle gives a mode that does not decay.
Zeros do not affect stability.

## Frequency response as geometry

On the unit circle, each factor $1 - c\,z^{-1}$ becomes $e^{-i\omega}(e^{i\omega} - c)$, and
$\abs{e^{-i\omega}} = 1$. So

$$
\abs{H(e^{i\omega})} = \abs{b_0}\, \frac{\prod_k \abs{e^{i\omega} - q_k}}{\prod_k \abs{e^{i\omega} - p_k}}.
$$

The magnitude at frequency $\omega$ is the product of the distances from the point $e^{i\omega}$ on the unit circle to
every zero, divided by the product of its distances to every pole. Walking around the upper half of the circle from
$\omega = 0$ to $\pi$ reads off the response:

- a pole close to the circle makes a small distance in the denominator as the point passes it, and the response
  peaks near that angle;
- a zero on the circle makes the distance zero, and the response has a null there;
- the phase is the sum of the zero angles minus the sum of the pole angles.

**Worked example: a resonator.** Place a conjugate pole pair at $p = r e^{\pm i\theta}$. The transfer function is

$$
H(z) = \frac{1}{1 - 2r\cos\theta\, z^{-1} + r^2 z^{-2}}.
$$

For $r$ near 1, the response peaks close to $\omega = \theta$, and its 3 dB bandwidth is about $2(1 - r)$ radians,
because the peak is set by the distance $1 - r$ from the pole to the circle. With $\theta = \pi/4$ and $r = 0.95$ the
bandwidth is $0.103$ rad against the approximation $0.1$; at $r = 0.99$ it is $0.0201$ against $0.02$. Adding zeros
at $z = \pm 1$ forces nulls at 0 and at the Nyquist frequency and turns the resonator into a band-pass filter.

## Design by placement

Many simple filters are designed by placing poles and zeros directly:

- a **notch** puts zeros on the circle at the unwanted frequency and poles just inside at the same angle, so the
  response returns to 1 away from the notch; see <NoteLink to="notch-and-comb-filters">notch and comb
  filters</NoteLink>;
- a **resonator** puts a pole pair near the circle at the frequency to emphasise;
- an **all-pass** filter pairs each pole $p$ with a zero at $1/\bar p$, which gives unit magnitude at every frequency
  and changes only the phase.

Zeros inside the unit circle give a <NoteLink to="minimum-phase-filters">minimum-phase</NoteLink> system, which has an
inverse that is also causal and stable.

## Pitfalls

- **Coefficient quantisation moves poles.** Poles near the circle are sensitive to rounding of the coefficients of
  $A(z)$, and a high-order direct-form filter can become unstable. Implementations use cascades of second-order
  sections, each holding one conjugate pair.
- **Stability depends on the ROC.** Poles outside the unit circle are allowed for an anti-causal system, which is
  stable. The rule "inside the circle" is for causal systems.
