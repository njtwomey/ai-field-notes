<Definition>
Let $x(t)$ be band-limited: its Fourier transform $X(f)$ is zero for $\abs{f} \ge B$. If $x$ is sampled at rate
$f_s > 2B$, with period $T = 1/f_s$, the samples $x[n] = x(nT)$ determine $x$ exactly, and

$$
x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \operatorname{sinc}\paren{\frac{t - nT}{T}}, \qquad
\operatorname{sinc}(u) = \frac{\sin \pi u}{\pi u}.
$$

The rate $2B$ is the Nyquist rate, and $f_s/2$ is the Nyquist frequency <Cite id="shannon1949" />.
</Definition>

## Sampling in frequency

Model sampling as multiplying $x(t)$ by an impulse train, $x_s(t) = x(t)\sum_n \delta(t - nT)$. The impulse train is
periodic with period $T$, so it has a Fourier series; its coefficients are all $1/T$, which gives the pair

$$
\sum_n \delta(t - nT) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{1}{T}\sum_k \delta\paren{f - k f_s}.
$$

Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, and convolving with a shifted impulse shifts the spectrum. So

$$
X_s(f) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} X\paren{f - k f_s}.
$$

Sampling replicates the spectrum at every multiple of $f_s$. If $X(f)$ is zero outside $(-B, B)$ and $f_s > 2B$, the
replicas do not overlap: the copy centred at zero is intact, separated from its neighbours by a gap of width
$f_s - 2B$. If $f_s < 2B$, neighbouring copies overlap and add, and no filter can separate them: that is
<NoteLink to="aliasing">aliasing</NoteLink>.

## Reconstruction

When the replicas do not overlap, an ideal low-pass filter with gain $T$ for $\abs{f} < f_s/2$ and zero elsewhere
keeps only the central copy, which is $X(f)$ itself. The filter's impulse response is the inverse transform of that
rectangle,

$$
h(t) = T \int_{-f_s/2}^{f_s/2} e^{i 2\pi f t}\,df = T \cdot \frac{\sin(\pi f_s t)}{\pi t} = \operatorname{sinc}\paren{\frac{t}{T}}.
$$

Filtering the impulse train convolves each impulse $x[n]\,\delta(t - nT)$ with $h$, which places a shifted sinc at each
sample:

$$
x(t) = \sum_n x[n]\, \operatorname{sinc}\paren{\frac{t - nT}{T}}.
$$

Each sinc is 1 at its own sample time and 0 at every other sample time, so the sum passes through every sample and
interpolates between them.

**Worked example.** Audio on compact discs is sampled at 44.1 kHz. The Nyquist frequency is 22.05 kHz, just above
the roughly 20 kHz limit of human hearing. The gap between 20 and 22.05 kHz is the transition band left for the
analogue anti-aliasing filter, which must pass 20 kHz and block everything above 22.05 kHz before the converter.

## In practice

- **No real signal is band-limited.** A signal of finite duration has a spectrum that extends to infinity. An
  anti-aliasing filter before the sampler removes content above $f_s/2$ so that the theorem applies to what remains.
- **Ideal reconstruction is non-causal.** The sinc extends forever in both directions and decays only as $1/t$.
  Real converters use a zero-order hold followed by a finite analogue filter, or oversample so that a gentle filter
  suffices.
- **The sinc sum converges slowly.** Truncating the sum to a window of samples leaves errors near the edges; the
  figure sums 40 samples beyond each end of the plot for this reason.

## Pitfalls

- **The boundary case $f_s = 2B$ fails.** A sinusoid at exactly $f_s/2$ is sampled at the same phase every other
  sample; $\sin(\pi f_s t)$ is sampled as all zeros. The theorem needs the strict inequality $f_s > 2B$.
- **Bandwidth, not highest frequency, can be what matters.** A band-pass signal occupying $[f_1, f_2]$ can be sampled
  at a rate set by its bandwidth $f_2 - f_1$ rather than by $f_2$, provided the replicas land in the gaps. This is
  band-pass sampling <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
