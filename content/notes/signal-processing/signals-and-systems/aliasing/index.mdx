<Definition>
Aliasing is the ambiguity introduced by sampling: two continuous-time sinusoids whose frequencies differ by a whole
multiple of the sample rate $f_s$ produce identical samples. After sampling, a component at frequency $f$ is
indistinguishable from one at $f - k f_s$ for every integer $k$, and a real component above $f_s/2$ appears at the
folded frequency in $[0, f_s/2]$ <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## Why frequencies alias

Sample the complex exponential $e^{i 2\pi f t}$ at times $t = n/f_s$:

$$
x[n] = e^{i 2\pi f n / f_s}.
$$

Replace $f$ by $f + k f_s$ for any integer $k$:

$$
e^{i 2\pi (f + k f_s) n / f_s} = e^{i 2\pi f n / f_s}\, e^{i 2\pi k n} = e^{i 2\pi f n / f_s},
$$

because $e^{i 2\pi k n} = 1$ for integer $k$ and $n$. The samples are identical, so the frequencies $f + k f_s$ form an
equivalence class, and discrete time only sees the class. In normalised angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f/f_s$ this is
the statement that $e^{i\omega n}$ is $2\pi$-periodic in $\omega$.

## Folding for real signals

A real sinusoid $\cos(2\pi f t)$ contains the two exponentials at $\pm f$. Their aliases are $\pm f + k f_s$, and the
one landing in $[0, f_s/2]$ is the representative that is seen:

$$
f_{\text{apparent}} = \abs{f - k f_s}, \qquad k = \operatorname{round}(f / f_s).
$$

As $f$ increases from 0, the apparent frequency rises to $f_s/2$, then falls back to 0 at $f_s$, rises again to $f_s/2$
at $1.5 f_s$, and so on: the frequency axis folds back and forth at every multiple of $f_s/2$. A component between
$f_s/2$ and $f_s$ also has its phase negated, since it is the image of the negative-frequency exponential.

**Worked example.** At $f_s = 10$ Hz, a 13 Hz cosine has $k = \operatorname{round}(1.3) = 1$ and appears at
$\abs{13 - 10} = 3$ Hz. A 17 Hz cosine has $k = 2$ and appears at $\abs{17 - 20} = 3$ Hz as well. After sampling, 3,
13 and 17 Hz are the same signal.

## Preventing it

Aliasing cannot be undone once the samples exist, because the aliased component and the genuine one at the same
frequency have added together. The only remedy is to remove content above $f_s/2$ before sampling, with an analogue
anti-aliasing low-pass filter. The same rule applies inside a digital system: before reducing the sample rate by a
factor $M$, a digital low-pass filter must remove content above the new Nyquist frequency, $\pi/M$ in normalised
frequency. See <NoteLink to="decimation-and-interpolation">decimation and interpolation</NoteLink>.

## Where it shows up

- **Wagon wheels in film.** A camera sampling at 24 frames per second shows a wheel rotating at nearly 24 turns per
  second as turning slowly, or backwards.
- **Spectral estimates.** A harmonic above the Nyquist frequency lands in the spectrum as a spurious peak at its
  folded frequency, with nothing to mark it as an alias.
- **Machine learning on sensor data.** Downsampling a signal by keeping every $M$-th sample, without filtering first,
  folds high-frequency noise into the band the model sees.
