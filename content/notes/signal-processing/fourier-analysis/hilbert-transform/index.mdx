<Definition>
The Hilbert transform $\mathcal{H}\{x\}$ of a real signal multiplies its spectrum by $-i\,\operatorname{sgn}(f)$:
positive frequencies are delayed by $90°$ and negative ones advanced by $90°$, with magnitudes unchanged. In time it
is convolution with $1/(\pi t)$, taken as a principal value. The analytic signal

$$
z(t) = x(t) + i\,\mathcal{H}\{x\}(t)
$$

has the spectrum $2X(f)$ for $f > 0$ and $0$ for $f < 0$ <Cite id="gabor1946" />.
</Definition>

## Why the negative frequencies vanish

A real signal's spectrum is conjugate-symmetric, so the negative frequencies carry no information the positive ones
do not. In $Z(f) = X(f) + i\cdot\paren{-i\operatorname{sgn}(f)}X(f) = \paren{1 + \operatorname{sgn}(f)}X(f)$, the factor
is 2 for $f > 0$ and 0 for $f < 0$.

For a single cosine the picture is exact: $\mathcal{H}\{\cos\omega t\} = \sin\omega t$, so
$z(t) = \cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t = e^{i\omega t}$. The analytic signal of a real oscillation is a rotating phasor.

## Envelope and instantaneous frequency

Write the analytic signal in polar form, $z(t) = A(t)\,e^{i\phi(t)}$. Then

$$
A(t) = \abs{z(t)}, \qquad f_i(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{dt},
$$

are the instantaneous amplitude, or envelope, and the instantaneous frequency. For an amplitude-modulated signal
$x(t) = a(t)\cos(\omega_c t)$, with $a(t) > 0$ varying slowly, $z(t) = a(t)\,e^{i\omega_c t}$, so $A(t) = a(t)$ and
$f_i = \omega_c/2\pi$. The condition is Bedrosian's: the spectrum of $a(t)$ must lie entirely below $\omega_c$, so that
the product's positive-frequency part is exactly $a(t)$ times the carrier's. When the modulation's bandwidth reaches
the carrier frequency, the lower sideband crosses zero frequency and the envelope estimate breaks down. See
<NoteLink to="instantaneous-frequency">instantaneous frequency</NoteLink>.

## Computing it with the FFT

The standard discrete method builds the analytic signal directly from the DFT <Cite id="marple1999" />:

1. compute $X[k]$, the $N$-point DFT of $x$;
2. keep $X[0]$ and $X[N/2]$ unchanged, double $X[k]$ for $1 \le k < N/2$, and set $X[k] = 0$ for $N/2 < k < N$;
3. take the inverse DFT.

The result's real part is $x$ again, and its imaginary part is the discrete Hilbert transform. Keeping DC and Nyquist
once, rather than doubling them, is what makes the real part equal $x$ exactly.

<AnalyticEnvelope />

**Worked example.** A 60 Hz carrier modulated at 4 Hz with depth 0.6, sampled at 1 kHz for 1,024 samples: the FFT
envelope matches $1 + 0.6\cos(2\pi \cdot 4\, t)$ to within about 0.014 away from the first and last 100 samples.
SciPy's `hilbert` gives the same numbers. The residual error comes from the segment not holding a whole number of
cycles.

## Uses

- **Envelope detection.** Amplitude demodulation, onset detection in audio, and features such as the envelope of a
  vibration or EEG band.
- **Phase and synchrony.** The instantaneous phase of band-passed signals is used to measure phase locking between
  channels, for example in neuroscience.
- **Single-sideband modulation and quadrature signals.** Communications systems transmit the analytic signal's
  positive-frequency half to halve the bandwidth.
- **Hilbert–Huang transform.** Applied to the components from
  <NoteLink to="empirical-mode-decomposition">empirical mode decomposition</NoteLink>, it gives a time-frequency
  representation.

## Pitfalls

- **It only means something for narrow-band signals.** For a broadband signal the envelope and instantaneous frequency
  are defined but are not the amplitude and frequency of any component. Band-pass filter first.
- **Edge effects.** The FFT method assumes the segment repeats, so the envelope is distorted near both ends. Pad or
  discard the edges.
- **Instantaneous frequency can be negative or exceed the band.** Where the envelope is near zero, the phase can jump
  and its derivative spike. Smooth or weight by amplitude.
