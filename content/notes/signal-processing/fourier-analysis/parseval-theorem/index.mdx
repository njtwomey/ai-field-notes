<Definition>
Fourier transforms preserve energy and inner products. For the $N$-point DFT,

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \abs{x[n]}^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \abs{X[k]}^2,
\qquad
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,\overline{y[n]} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\,\overline{Y[k]}.
$$

The corresponding forms hold for the DTFT, $\sum_n \abs{x[n]}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\abs{X(e^{i\omega})}^2
d\omega$; for Fourier series, $\frac1T\int_0^T\abs{x}^2 = \sum_k \abs{c_k}^2$; and for the continuous transform,
$\int\abs{x(t)}^2dt = \int\abs{X(f)}^2df$ <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## Proof for the DFT

In matrix form the DFT is $\hat\xvec = \Fmat\xvec$ with $\Fmat^{\mathsf H}\Fmat = N\eye$; see the
<NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">DFT</NoteLink>. Then

$$
\sum_k \abs{X[k]}^2 = \hat\xvec^{\mathsf H}\hat\xvec = \xvec^{\mathsf H}\Fmat^{\mathsf H}\Fmat\xvec = N\,\xvec^{\mathsf H}\xvec
= N\sum_n \abs{x[n]}^2.
$$

The DFT is a rotation of $\mathbb{C}^N$ scaled by $\sqrt N$, and a rotation preserves lengths and angles. The same
argument with $\xvec^{\mathsf H}$ replaced by $\yvec^{\mathsf H}$ gives the inner-product form. Each continuous
version is the same statement for an orthogonal basis of complex exponentials.

## What it is used for

- **Power in frequency bands.** The energy between two frequencies is the sum of $\abs{X[k]}^2/N$ over the bins
  between them. This is how band powers, signal-to-noise ratios and spectral features are computed.
- **Power spectral density.** Dividing $\abs{X[k]}^2$ by the length turns energy into power per bin, the basis of the
  <NoteLink to="periodogram">periodogram</NoteLink>.
- **Noise calculations.** White noise of variance $\sigma^2$ spreads its power evenly over all bins, $\sigma^2$ per
  bin on average after scaling. This is why quantisation noise, of power $\Delta^2/12$, spreads evenly across the band;
  see <NoteLink to="quantisation">quantisation</NoteLink>.
- **Checking scaling.** Confirming that time-domain and frequency-domain energy agree catches wrong normalisations
  in FFT code.

## Worked example

For $x = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ the DFT is $X = (10, -2 + 2i, -2, -2 - 2i)$. The energy in time is $1 + 4 + 9 + 16 = 30$. In
frequency, $\abs{X}^2 = (100, 8, 4, 8)$, which sums to $120$, and $120/4 = 30$.

## Pitfalls

- **Normalisation.** The factor $1/N$ belongs wherever the transform's scaling puts it. With a unitary DFT, scaled by
  $1/\sqrt N$ in both directions, the two sums are equal without a factor.
- **Windowing changes the energy.** A window scales the signal, so energy measured from a windowed spectrum must be
  divided by the window's mean square, $\frac1N\sum w^2$, to estimate the energy of the unwindowed signal.
- **One-sided spectra.** For real signals, the energy in bins $1$ to $N/2 - 1$ counts twice if only the positive
  frequencies are summed; bins $0$ and $N/2$ count once.
