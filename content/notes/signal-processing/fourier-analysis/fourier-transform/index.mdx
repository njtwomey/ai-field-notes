<Definition>
The Fourier transform of a signal $x(t)$ and its inverse are

$$
X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f t}\,dt, \qquad
x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(f)\, e^{i 2\pi f t}\,df,
$$

with frequency $f$ in hertz. The transform exists for absolutely integrable $x$, and extends to square-integrable
signals and, as distributions, to impulses and sinusoids <Cite id="bracewell2000" />. With angular frequency
$\Omega = 2\pi f$, factors of $2\pi$ move into the inverse.
</Definition>

## From series to transform

A periodic signal is a sum of harmonics spaced $1/T$ apart; see <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier
series</NoteLink>. Let the period $T$ grow. The spacing shrinks, $T c_k$ approaches the value of a continuous function
at $f = k/T$, and the sum over harmonics becomes an integral over frequency. A non-periodic signal has a continuous
spectrum instead of discrete lines.

## Pairs

| $x(t)$ | $X(f)$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\delta(t)$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $\delta(f)$ |
| $e^{i 2\pi f_0 t}$ | $\delta(f - f_0)$ |
| $\cos(2\pi f_0 t)$ | $\tfrac12\delta(f - f_0) + \tfrac12\delta(f + f_0)$ |
| $\operatorname{rect}(t)$ (1 for $\abs{t} < \tfrac12$) | $\operatorname{sinc}(f) = \dfrac{\sin \pi f}{\pi f}$ |
| $e^{-\pi t^2}$ | $e^{-\pi f^2}$ |
| $e^{-a\abs{t}}$, $a > 0$ | $\dfrac{2a}{a^2 + (2\pi f)^2}$ |

**Worked example: the rectangle.** Integrating over the support,

$$
\int_{-1/2}^{1/2} e^{-i 2\pi f t}\,dt = \frac{e^{-i\pi f} - e^{i\pi f}}{-i 2\pi f} = \frac{\sin \pi f}{\pi f}.
$$

A pulse sharp in time spreads over all frequencies, with sidelobes decaying like $1/f$ because of its jumps.

**Worked example: the Gaussian.** The exponent of $e^{-\pi t^2} e^{-i 2\pi f t}$ is $-\pi(t + i f)^2 - \pi f^2$ by
<NoteLink to="completing-the-square">completing the square</NoteLink>. The remaining integral of $e^{-\pi(t + if)^2}$
equals $\int e^{-\pi t^2} dt = 1$ (shift the contour; see the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian
integral</NoteLink>), so the transform is $e^{-\pi f^2}$: the Gaussian is its own transform.

## Properties

Each follows by substitution in the defining integral.

| Property | Signal | Transform |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linearity | $a x_1 + b x_2$ | $a X_1 + b X_2$ |
| Time shift | $x(t - t_0)$ | $e^{-i 2\pi f t_0} X(f)$ |
| Modulation | $e^{i 2\pi f_0 t} x(t)$ | $X(f - f_0)$ |
| Scaling | $x(at)$ | $\frac{1}{\abs a} X(f/a)$ |
| Convolution | $(x * h)(t)$ | $X(f) H(f)$ |
| Multiplication | $x(t) h(t)$ | $(X * H)(f)$ |
| Differentiation | $x'(t)$ | $i 2\pi f\, X(f)$ |
| Conjugate symmetry | $x$ real | $X(-f) = \overline{X(f)}$ |
| Duality | $X(t)$ | $x(-f)$ |

- **Scaling** is the time-bandwidth trade-off: compressing a signal by $a$ stretches its spectrum by $a$. A signal
  cannot be arbitrarily short and arbitrarily narrow-band at once; see
  <NoteLink to="time-frequency-uncertainty">time-frequency uncertainty</NoteLink>.
- **Duality** comes from the near-symmetry of the two integrals, which differ only in the sign of the exponent. The
  rectangle gives a sinc, so a sinc gives a rectangle: the ideal low-pass filter's <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink>.
- **Convolution** turns filtering into multiplication by the <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink>; see the
  <NoteLink to="convolution-theorem">convolution theorem</NoteLink>.

## Energy

Plancherel's theorem says the transform preserves energy: $\int \abs{x(t)}^2 dt = \int \abs{X(f)}^2 df$. So
$\abs{X(f)}^2$ is an energy density over frequency. See <NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">Parseval's theorem</NoteLink>.

## Relation to sampled signals

Sampling a signal at rate $f_s$ makes its spectrum periodic with period $f_s$; see the
<NoteLink to="sampling-theorem">sampling theorem</NoteLink>. The transform of the resulting sequence is the
<NoteLink to="discrete-time-fourier-transform">discrete-time Fourier transform</NoteLink>, and sampling that in frequency gives the
<NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">DFT</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Conventions differ.** Engineers often use $\Omega$ with $1/2\pi$ in the inverse; physicists split
  $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ between the two; some fields reverse the sign of the exponent. Pairs and properties change
  accordingly.
- **Transforms of sinusoids are impulses.** A pure tone has infinite energy and no ordinary transform. Any finite
  recording of it has a spectrum with width about $1/(\text{duration})$.
