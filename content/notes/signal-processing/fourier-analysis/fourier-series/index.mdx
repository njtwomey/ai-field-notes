<Definition>
A function $x(t)$ with period $T$ has the Fourier series

$$
x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k\, e^{i 2\pi k t / T}, \qquad
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T} x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi k t / T}\,dt.
$$

The term with index $k$ is the $k$-th harmonic, at frequency $k/T$. For real $x$, $c_{-k} = \overline{c_k}$, and the
series can be written with cosines and sines <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## Coefficients by orthogonality

The harmonics are orthogonal over one period:

$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{i 2\pi k t/T}\, e^{-i 2\pi m t/T}\,dt = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T e^{i 2\pi (k - m) t / T}\,dt =
\begin{cases} 1 & k = m, \\ 0 & k \ne m, \end{cases}
$$

because for $k \ne m$ the integrand completes $k - m$ whole turns around the unit circle and integrates to zero.
Multiply the series by $e^{-i 2\pi m t/T}$, integrate over a period, and every term but the $m$-th vanishes, which
leaves $c_m$. The coefficient formula is the projection of $x$ onto each harmonic, as in an orthonormal basis.

## Worked example: the square wave

Let $x(t) = 1$ on $(0, T/2)$ and $-1$ on $(T/2, T)$. With $\theta = 2\pi t/T$,

$$
c_k = \frac{1}{2\pi}\paren{\int_0^{\pi} e^{-ik\theta}\,d\theta - \int_{\pi}^{2\pi} e^{-ik\theta}\,d\theta}
= \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{i\pi k}.
$$

The coefficient is $2/(i\pi k)$ for odd $k$ and $0$ for even $k$. Pairing $k$ with $-k$ gives

$$
x(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\paren{\sin\theta + \frac{\sin 3\theta}{3} + \frac{\sin 5\theta}{5} + \cdots}.
$$

Only odd harmonics appear, because the wave has half-wave symmetry, $x(t + T/2) = -x(t)$. The coefficients decay like
$1/k$, which is the signature of a jump; a continuous function with a jump in its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> has coefficients that
decay like $1/k^2$.

## The Gibbs phenomenon

Truncate the series at harmonic $K$. Away from the jumps the partial sums converge to the square wave. Next to each
jump they overshoot, and the overshoot does not shrink as $K$ grows: the first peak tends to

$$
\frac{2}{\pi}\operatorname{Si}(\pi) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \frac{\sin u}{u}\,du \approx 1.1790,
$$

an overshoot of $0.179$ on a jump of $2$, about 9% <Cite id="gibbs1899" />. What shrinks is its width: the peak sits
at a distance of about $T/(2K)$ from the jump. The convergence is pointwise but not uniform.

The same effect appears whenever a spectrum is cut off sharply: an ideal low-pass filter truncated to a finite <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse
response</NoteLink> ripples at its band edges, and a truncated impulse response rings. Tapering the coefficients instead of
cutting them, with a window, trades the overshoot for a wider transition; see
<NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">spectral leakage and windows</NoteLink>.

## Convergence and energy

If $x$ is square-integrable over a period, the partial sums converge to $x$ in mean square, and the energy splits
across the harmonics:

$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \abs{x(t)}^2\,dt = \sum_k \abs{c_k}^2.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="parseval-theorem">Parseval's theorem</NoteLink> for Fourier series. At a jump the series
converges to the midpoint of the two one-sided limits.

## Pitfalls

- **Periodic extension.** The series represents the periodic extension of whatever is on one period. If the two ends
  of the period do not match, the extension has a jump there, and the coefficients decay slowly.
- **Pointwise convergence can fail.** There are continuous functions whose Fourier series diverge at a point.
  Smoothness, not continuity, guarantees fast and uniform convergence.
