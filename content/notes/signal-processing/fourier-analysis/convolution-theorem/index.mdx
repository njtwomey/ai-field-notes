<Definition>
For every <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> in one domain is multiplication in the other. For sequences and the <Gloss name="dtft" />,

$$
y = x * h \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Y(e^{i\omega}) = X(e^{i\omega})\,H(e^{i\omega}).
$$

For the $N$-point <Gloss name="dft" /> the corresponding convolution is circular: $Y[k] = X[k]H[k]$ exactly when
$y[n] = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} x[m]\,h[(n - m) \bmod N]$ <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## Proof for the DTFT

Substitute the convolution into the transform and exchange the sums, which is valid when both sequences are
absolutely summable:

$$
Y(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_n \sum_m x[m]\,h[n - m]\,e^{-i\omega n}
= \sum_m x[m]\,e^{-i\omega m} \sum_n h[n - m]\,e^{-i\omega (n - m)} = X(e^{i\omega})\,H(e^{i\omega}).
$$

The inner sum is $H(e^{i\omega})$ for every $m$, after substituting $\ell = n - m$. The same steps with integrals prove
the continuous-time version, and with sums modulo $N$ the DFT version.

The dual statement also holds: multiplying two signals convolves their spectra. For the DTFT the spectral convolution
is periodic, $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} X(e^{i\theta}) W(e^{i(\omega - \theta)})\,d\theta$. This is why
windowing a signal smears its spectrum; see
<NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">spectral leakage and windows</NoteLink>.

## Circular convolution

The DFT treats a length-$N$ sequence as one period of a periodic one. Multiplying DFTs therefore convolves the two
periodic sequences over one period: indices are taken modulo $N$, and anything that would land at index $N$ or beyond
wraps around to the start.

A linear convolution of lengths $N_x$ and $N_h$ has $N_x + N_h - 1$ samples. If both sequences are padded with zeros to
a length

$$
L \ge N_x + N_h - 1,
$$

the circular convolution has room for the whole result and nothing wraps, so it equals the linear convolution.

<CircularWrap />

**Worked example.** Convolving six ones with $(1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25)$ linearly gives
$(1, 1.75, 2.25, 2.5, 2.5, 2.5, 1.5, 0.75, 0.25)$, nine samples. With $N = 6$, the last three samples wrap onto the
first three, giving $(2.5, 2.5, 2.5, 2.5, 2.5, 2.5)$: every output equals the full sum of $h$, because a constant
signal convolved circularly sees the whole response at every position. With $N = 9$ or more the result is exact.

## Fast convolution

Direct convolution costs $N_x N_h$ multiplications. With the <Gloss name="fft" />:

1. pad $x$ and $h$ to length $L \ge N_x + N_h - 1$, usually a power of two;
2. compute their FFTs;
3. multiply the spectra point by point;
4. take the inverse FFT.

This costs $O(L \log L)$. It wins when the response is long, above a few dozen samples. For a long or streaming
input, the signal is processed in blocks, each convolved by FFT and then stitched together; see
<NoteLink to="overlap-add-and-overlap-save">overlap-add and overlap-save</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Unpadded FFT filtering.** Multiplying the FFT of a signal by a <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink> and inverting, without padding,
  wraps the filter's tail onto the start of the block.
- **Filtering in frequency by zeroing bins.** Setting DFT bins to zero is multiplication by a rectangular response,
  which is circular convolution with a periodic sinc. It rings in time and wraps around.
