<Definition>
The discrete-time Fourier transform of a sequence $x[n]$ and its inverse are

$$
X(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]\, e^{-i\omega n}, \qquad
x[n] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} X(e^{i\omega})\, e^{i\omega n}\,d\omega,
$$

where $\omega$ is normalised angular frequency in radians per sample. The sum converges absolutely when
$\sum_n \abs{x[n]} < \infty$ <Cite id="oppenheim2010" />.
</Definition>

## Frequency is periodic

Replacing $\omega$ by $\omega + 2\pi$ multiplies each term by $e^{-i 2\pi n} = 1$, so $X(e^{i\omega})$ has period
$2\pi$. There is no frequency above $\pi$: a sequence cannot oscillate faster than alternating in sign every sample.
The interval $[-\pi, \pi)$ holds every distinct frequency, and $\omega = \pi$ is the Nyquist frequency. For a sequence
sampled at rate $f_s$, $\omega = 2\pi f/f_s$, so $\omega = \pi$ corresponds to $f = f_s/2$.

The inverse formula follows from the orthogonality of $e^{i\omega n}$ over one period:
$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{i\omega(n - m)}\,d\omega$ is 1 when $n = m$ and 0 otherwise.

## Relations to the other transforms

- **z-transform.** The DTFT is the <NoteLink to="z-transform">z-transform</NoteLink> evaluated on the unit circle,
  $z = e^{i\omega}$. It exists exactly when the region of convergence includes the circle.
- **Continuous-time transform.** If $x[n] = x_c(n/f_s)$ samples a continuous signal, then
  $X(e^{i\omega}) = f_s \sum_k X_c\paren{(\omega/2\pi - k) f_s}$: the continuous spectrum, scaled and repeated. See the
  <NoteLink to="sampling-theorem">sampling theorem</NoteLink>.
- **DFT.** The <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">DFT</NoteLink> of a length-$N$ sequence samples its DTFT at the $N$ frequencies
  $\omega_k = 2\pi k/N$.
- **Frequency response.** The DTFT of an <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> is the system's
  <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink>.

## Worked example: a rectangular pulse

Let $x[n] = 1$ for $n = 0, \dots, L-1$ and 0 otherwise. The DTFT is a finite geometric series:

$$
X(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} e^{-i\omega n} = \frac{1 - e^{-i\omega L}}{1 - e^{-i\omega}}
= e^{-i\omega (L-1)/2}\, \frac{\sin(\omega L/2)}{\sin(\omega/2)}.
$$

The last step factors $e^{-i\omega L/2}$ from the numerator and $e^{-i\omega/2}$ from the denominator and uses
$e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta$. The magnitude is the Dirichlet kernel. It equals $L$ at $\omega = 0$ and
first reaches zero at $\omega = \pm 2\pi/L$. The phase is linear, $-\omega(L-1)/2$: the pulse is symmetric about
$n = (L-1)/2$. This kernel is the spectrum of the rectangular window, so it sets the leakage of an unwindowed DFT;
see <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">spectral leakage and windows</NoteLink>.

## Properties

The DTFT shares the properties of the continuous transform, with frequency now periodic: a time shift by $n_0$
multiplies by $e^{-i\omega n_0}$; modulation by $e^{i\omega_0 n}$ shifts the spectrum circularly by $\omega_0$;
<NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> in time multiplies spectra; multiplication in time convolves spectra periodically over one period; a real
sequence has $X(e^{-i\omega}) = \overline{X(e^{i\omega})}$; and energy is preserved,
$\sum_n \abs{x[n]}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\abs{X(e^{i\omega})}^2 d\omega$.

## Pitfalls

- **A computer cannot evaluate the DTFT exactly.** It is a function of a continuous variable. In practice it is
  sampled on a grid with the DFT, after truncating the sequence, which is where leakage comes from.
- **Frequency units.** Normalised frequency may be quoted as $\omega$ in rad/sample, as $\omega/\pi$ (with 1 at
  Nyquist, as in MATLAB), or as $\omega/2\pi$ in cycles per sample (with 0.5 at Nyquist).
