<Definition>
The frequency resolution of an $N$-sample observation at rate $f_s$ is about $f_s/N$ hertz, the reciprocal of its
duration $N/f_s$. Zero-padding appends zeros before the DFT, so an $L$-point DFT of $N$ samples, $L > N$, samples the
same DTFT at spacing $f_s/L$. It interpolates the spectrum; it does not sharpen it <Cite id="smith2007" />.
</Definition>

## Two different spacings

Two quantities are often confused.

- **Bin spacing** is $f_s/L$ for an $L$-point DFT: how finely the spectrum is sampled. It can be made as small as
  desired by padding.
- **Resolution** is how close two components can be and still appear as separate peaks. It is set by the width of the
  window's main lobe, which is fixed by the number of genuine samples $N$: about $2 f_s/N$ null to null for the
  rectangular window, and more for tapered ones.

Padding does not change the spectrum being sampled. The padded sequence has the same nonzero samples, so its DTFT,
$\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-i\omega n}$, is unchanged; the $L$-point DFT merely evaluates it at $L$ points instead of
$N$. See <NoteLink to="dft">the DFT as samples of the DTFT</NoteLink>.

## Why duration sets resolution

A tone observed for $N$ samples appears as the window's transform centred on its frequency. For the rectangular
window that is the Dirichlet kernel, whose main lobe spans $\pm 2\pi/N$ rad/sample, or $\pm f_s/N$ Hz. Two tones
whose main lobes overlap heavily sum into a single bump. They separate once they are roughly one main-lobe half-width
apart, $\Delta f \gtrsim f_s/N$. This is the discrete form of the time-bandwidth trade-off; see
<NoteLink to="time-frequency-uncertainty">time-frequency uncertainty</NoteLink>.

<TwoTones />

**Worked example.** Tones at 100 and 105 Hz, sampled at 1 kHz. With $N = 100$ samples, the resolution is 10 Hz; the
spectrum shows one merged peak, and padding to 16 times the length only draws that single peak more smoothly. With
$N = 200$ samples, the resolution is 5 Hz and two peaks appear, near 99.4 and 105.6 Hz. The peaks are slightly
biased away from the true frequencies, because each tone's main lobe still overlaps the other.

## What padding is for

- **Reading peak locations.** Interpolating the spectrum lets the maximum of a peak be located to a fraction of a bin,
  which matters when the bins are coarse.
- **Fast lengths.** Padding to a power of two lets the <NoteLink to="fft">FFT</NoteLink> run fastest.
- **Linear convolution.** Padding to $N_x + N_h - 1$ turns circular convolution into linear; see the
  <NoteLink to="convolution-theorem">convolution theorem</NoteLink>.
- **Smoother plots.** A padded spectrum looks like the underlying continuous curve rather than a coarse polyline.

## Pitfalls

- **Padding hides leakage from the eye, not from the data.** A tone between bins leaks the same amount whatever the
  padding; padding only shows the sidelobes in finer detail.
- **Windows reduce resolution.** A Hann window doubles the main lobe, so tones need about twice the spacing to separate.
  Choosing a window is choosing between resolution and dynamic range.
- **Parametric methods can beat $f_s/N$.** Model-based estimators, which assume the signal is a sum of sinusoids, can
  resolve closer tones at high signal-to-noise ratio; see
  <NoteLink to="subspace-frequency-estimation">subspace frequency estimation</NoteLink>.
