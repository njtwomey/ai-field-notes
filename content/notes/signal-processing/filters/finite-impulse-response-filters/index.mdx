<Definition>
A finite <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> (FIR) filter of order $M$ computes

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\, x[n - k] .
$$

Its impulse response is the coefficient sequence itself, $h[n] = b_n$ for $0 \le n \le M$ and zero otherwise, so it is
finite. Its <NoteLink to="z-transform">transfer function</NoteLink> is the polynomial $H(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}$
<Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## Always stable

A filter is bounded-input bounded-output stable exactly when $\sum_n \abs{h[n]} < \infty$. For an FIR filter the sum
has $M + 1$ finite terms, so every FIR filter is stable, whatever its coefficients. In the $z$-plane, $H(z) =
z^{-M}\sum_k b_k z^{M-k}$ has all $M$ of its poles at $z = 0$, inside the unit circle. There is no feedback, so
coefficient rounding cannot make an FIR filter unstable or produce limit cycles, a practical advantage over
<NoteLink to="infinite-impulse-response-filters">infinite impulse response (IIR) filters</NoteLink>.

## Linear phase

If the coefficients are symmetric, $b_k = b_{M-k}$, the <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink>
factors as $e^{-i\omega M/2}$ times a real function of $\omega$, so every frequency is delayed by exactly $M/2$ samples
and waveforms pass through without phase distortion. The proof and the four symmetry types are in
<NoteLink to="linear-phase-and-group-delay">linear phase and group delay</NoteLink>. A causal IIR filter cannot have
exactly linear phase, so this property is the main reason to choose FIR.

## Transition width and filter length

Without feedback, an FIR filter's frequency selectivity comes only from its length. The width of its transition band is
inversely proportional to $M$: halving the transition width roughly doubles the number of coefficients. A low-pass
specification that an elliptic IIR filter meets with order 4 can need dozens of FIR taps; see <NoteLink
to="infinite-impulse-response-filter-design">IIR filter design</NoteLink>. A linear-phase FIR filter also delays the
signal by $M/2$ samples, which matters in real-time and feedback systems.

## Structures

- **<NoteLink to="difference-equations">Direct form</NoteLink>.** A tapped delay line: store the last $M$ inputs,
  multiply by the coefficients, and sum. It costs $M + 1$ multiplications and $M$ additions per output; symmetric
  coefficients halve the multiplications by adding the paired samples first.
- **Transposed direct form.** The same filter with the delays moved after the multipliers, which suits pipelined
  hardware and reduces the dynamic range of intermediate sums.
- **Cascade.** Factor $H(z)$ into second-order sections; useful when the zeros must be controlled individually.
- **<NoteLink to="convolution-theorem">Fast convolution</NoteLink>.** For long filters, block convolution with the
  <Gloss name="fft" /> costs $O(\log M)$ operations per sample instead of $O(M)$; see
  <NoteLink to="overlap-add-and-overlap-save">overlap-add and overlap-save</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

The five-point moving average $b_k = \tfrac15$, $k = 0, \dots, 4$, has transfer function

$$
H(z) = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=0}^{4} z^{-k} = \frac{1}{5}\,\frac{1 - z^{-5}}{1 - z^{-1}} .
$$

Its zeros are the fifth roots of unity other than $z = 1$: $e^{\pm 2\pi i/5}$ and $e^{\pm 4\pi i/5}$. A sinusoid at
$\omega = 2\pi/5$ or $4\pi/5$ is removed entirely, because five consecutive samples of it sum to zero. The input
$x = (5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)$ produces $y = (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0)$: the impulse response, scaled by 5. The filter delays by
$M/2 = 2$ samples and needs five multiplications per output, or one addition and one subtraction per output with a
running sum.

## Pitfalls

- **Delay grows with length.** A 101-tap linear-phase filter delays by 50 samples. Minimum-phase designs with the same
  magnitude response reduce the delay but give up linear phase.
- **Quantised coefficients.** Rounding coefficients to fixed point perturbs the zeros and raises stopband sidelobes,
  though it never threatens stability.
- **Edge effects.** The first $M$ outputs use samples before the signal starts, conventionally zeros; discard them or
  pad the signal deliberately.
