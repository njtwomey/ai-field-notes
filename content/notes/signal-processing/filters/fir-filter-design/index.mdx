<Definition>
FIR filter design chooses coefficients $h[0], \dots, h[M]$ whose frequency response meets a specification: passband
and stopband edges, the allowed passband ripple, and the required stopband attenuation. The window method multiplies
the ideal impulse response by a window of length $M + 1$; optimal methods such as Parks–McClellan minimise the
largest weighted error over the bands directly <Cite id="parks1972" />.
</Definition>

## The ideal low-pass filter

An ideal low-pass filter with cutoff $\omega_c$ has $H(e^{i\omega}) = 1$ for $\abs{\omega} < \omega_c$ and 0 otherwise.
Its impulse response is the inverse DTFT,

$$
h_d[n] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\omega_c}^{\omega_c} e^{i\omega n}\, d\omega = \frac{\sin(\omega_c n)}{\pi n},
$$

with $h_d[0] = \omega_c/\pi$. It is infinitely long and non-causal, so it cannot be implemented. Shifting it by $M/2$
and keeping $M + 1$ samples gives a causal, linear-phase approximation:
$h[n] = h_d[n - M/2]\, w[n]$ for $0 \le n \le M$, where $w[n]$ is the truncating window.

## Why truncation ripples: the Gibbs phenomenon

Multiplying by a window in time convolves the ideal response with the window's transform $W(e^{i\omega})$ in
frequency. With a rectangular window, $W$ is the Dirichlet kernel, whose sidelobes make the response overshoot at the
band edge by about 9% of the jump, and the overshoot does not shrink as $M$ grows: the sidelobes narrow but keep their
height. The peak stopband level stays near $-21$ dB for any length.

A tapered window has lower sidelobes but a wider main lobe. The two properties map directly onto the design: the
main-lobe width sets the transition width, and the sidelobe level sets the stopband attenuation and passband ripple.
For 101-tap low-pass designs with cutoff $0.3\pi$, the measured peak stopband levels are:

| Window | Peak stopband level | Main-lobe width (transition) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rectangular | $-21$ dB | $4\pi/(M+1)$ |
| Hann | $-44$ dB | $8\pi/M$ |
| Hamming | $-53$ dB | $8\pi/M$ |
| Blackman | $-75$ dB | $12\pi/M$ |

In every case the gain at $\omega_c$ is close to $-6$ dB, half amplitude, because the window is symmetric and the
ideal response jumps from 1 to 0 there <Cite id="harris1978" />.

## Kaiser's formulas

The Kaiser window has a parameter $\beta$ that trades main-lobe width against sidelobe level continuously. Kaiser
fitted empirical formulas that turn a specification into $\beta$ and a length. For a stopband attenuation of $A$ dB,
taking the same ripple $\delta = 10^{-A/20}$ in both bands, and a transition width $\Delta\omega$:

$$
\beta = \begin{cases} 0.1102\,(A - 8.7), & A > 50, \\ 0.5842\,(A - 21)^{0.4} + 0.07886\,(A - 21), & 21 \le A \le 50, \\
0, & A < 21, \end{cases}
\qquad M \approx \frac{A - 7.95}{2.285\,\Delta\omega} .
$$

The length formula is an estimate, accurate to a tap or two; check the result and adjust.

## Parks–McClellan: optimal equiripple design

The window method does not control the error directly: its ripple is largest near the band edges and decays away from
them, wasting accuracy. Parks and McClellan pose design as a minimax problem: over linear-phase filters of a given
length, minimise the largest weighted error $\max_\omega W(\omega)\abs{H(e^{i\omega}) - D(\omega)}$ over the specified
bands <Cite id="parks1972" />. The solution is characterised by the alternation theorem: the optimal filter's error
reaches its maximum magnitude, with alternating signs, at least $L + 2$ times, where $L$ is the number of free
cosine coefficients. The Remez exchange algorithm finds it by iterating on the set of extremal frequencies. The result
is equiripple, and for a given specification it is the shortest linear-phase FIR filter.

## Worked example

Specify a low-pass filter with passband edge $0.25\pi$, stopband edge $0.35\pi$, and 60 dB attenuation, so the
transition width is $\Delta\omega = 0.1\pi$ and $\delta = 0.001$ in both bands.

- **Kaiser.** $\beta = 0.1102\,(60 - 8.7) = 5.653$ and $M \approx (60 - 7.95)/(2.285 \times 0.1\pi) = 72.5$, which
  SciPy's `kaiserord` rounds to 74 taps. With the cutoff at the band centre, $0.3\pi$, 74 taps reach $-59.3$ dB at
  $0.35\pi$; 76 taps reach $-60.4$ dB with passband deviation 0.00101, meeting the specification.
- **Parks–McClellan.** With equal weights in both bands, 69 taps meet the same specification, reaching $-60.8$ dB. The
  equiripple design saves about 10% of the length, and so of the computation.

## Pitfalls

- **Specify in the right units.** SciPy's `firwin` and `remez` take frequencies relative to the Nyquist frequency
  by default, or in Hz with `fs`; a factor of 2 or $\pi$ is the most common design error.
- **High-pass needs odd length.** A symmetric even-length filter always has a zero at $\omega = \pi$ (type II), so it
  cannot be a high-pass filter.
- **Narrow transitions are expensive.** Length grows as $1/\Delta\omega$; halving the transition width doubles the
  taps, and the delay. An IIR design may be the better choice when linear phase is not needed.
