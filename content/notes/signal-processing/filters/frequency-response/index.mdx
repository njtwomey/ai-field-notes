<Definition>
The frequency response of a linear time-invariant system with impulse response $h[n]$ is the discrete-time Fourier
transform of $h$,

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} h[n]\, e^{-i\omega n} = \abs{H(e^{i\omega})}\, e^{i\angle H(e^{i\omega})} .
$$

The magnitude $\abs{H}$ is the gain at frequency $\omega$ and the argument $\angle H$ is the phase shift. For a filter
defined by a difference equation, $H(e^{i\omega}) = B(e^{i\omega})/A(e^{i\omega})$, the ratio of the numerator and
denominator polynomials evaluated on the unit circle <Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## Complex exponentials are eigenfunctions

Feed $x[n] = e^{i\omega n}$ into the system. By the convolution sum,

$$
y[n] = \sum_k h[k]\, e^{i\omega(n - k)} = e^{i\omega n}\sum_k h[k]\, e^{-i\omega k} = H(e^{i\omega})\, e^{i\omega n} .
$$

The output is the input multiplied by a complex constant: complex exponentials pass through an LTI system unchanged
in shape. For a real filter, $H(e^{-i\omega}) = H(e^{i\omega})^*$, and adding the responses to $e^{i\omega n}$ and
$e^{-i\omega n}$ gives

$$
\cos(\omega n + \phi) \;\mapsto\; \abs{H(e^{i\omega})}\cos\paren{\omega n + \phi + \angle H(e^{i\omega})} .
$$

A sinusoid comes out as a sinusoid of the same frequency, scaled and shifted. Because any signal is a superposition
of sinusoids, the frequency response describes the system completely.

## Rational filters

A filter defined by $\sum_k a_k y[n - k] = \sum_k b_k x[n - k]$ has, by the same substitution,

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = \frac{\sum_k b_k e^{-i\omega k}}{\sum_k a_k e^{-i\omega k}} = \frac{B(e^{i\omega})}{A(e^{i\omega})},
$$

which is the transfer function $H(z) = B(z)/A(z)$ evaluated on the unit circle $z = e^{i\omega}$. Factoring the
polynomials gives the geometric picture of <NoteLink to="poles-and-zeros">poles and zeros</NoteLink>: $\abs{H}$ is the
product of distances from $e^{i\omega}$ to the zeros, divided by the product of distances to the poles, times a
constant. The response dips near zeros close to the circle and peaks near poles close to it.

## Reading a response

- **Decibels.** Magnitudes are plotted as $20\log_{10}\abs{H}$. A gain of 0.5 is $-6.02$ dB, 0.1 is $-20$ dB, and the
  half-power point $\abs{H} = 1/\sqrt 2$ is $-3.01$ dB.
- **Bands.** The passband is where the gain is near 1, within an allowed ripple; the stopband is where it is below a
  required attenuation; the transition band lies between. A filter specification gives the band edges, the ripple and
  the attenuation.
- **Phase.** The phase is usually plotted unwrapped. Its slope gives the <NoteLink
  to="linear-phase-and-group-delay">group delay</NoteLink>, the delay of a narrowband signal's envelope; a straight
  line means every frequency is delayed equally.
- **Periodicity and symmetry.** $H(e^{i\omega})$ has period $2\pi$, and for real filters $\abs{H}$ is even and $\angle H$
  odd, so the range $0 \le \omega \le \pi$ says everything. With a sample rate $f_s$, $\omega = \pi$ is the Nyquist
  frequency $f_s/2$.

## Worked example

The three-tap smoother $h = (\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14)$ has

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 e^{-i\omega} + \tfrac14 e^{-2i\omega}
= e^{-i\omega}\paren{\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos\omega} = e^{-i\omega}\cos^2(\omega/2) .
$$

The magnitude is $\cos^2(\omega/2)$: 1 at $\omega = 0$, 0.5 ($-6.02$ dB) at $\omega = \pi/2$, and 0 at $\omega = \pi$.
The phase is $-\omega$, a delay of exactly one sample at every frequency, because the filter is symmetric about $n = 1$.
A sinusoid at $\omega = \pi/2$ comes out at half the amplitude, one sample late.

## Pitfalls

- **Steady state only.** The eigenfunction argument assumes the sinusoid has been present forever. A sinusoid switched
  on at $n = 0$ produces a transient before the output settles, lasting about as long as the impulse response.
- **Magnitude alone is not enough.** Filters with the same magnitude response can have very different phase and
  therefore different waveform distortion; see <NoteLink to="minimum-phase-filters">minimum-phase filters</NoteLink>.
- **The response exists only for stable filters.** If $h[n]$ is not absolutely summable, the sum defining
  $H(e^{i\omega})$ does not converge, and evaluating $B/A$ on the unit circle gives a formula with no physical meaning.
