<Definition>
An infinite <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> (IIR) filter computes each output from current and past inputs and past outputs:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\, x[n - k] - \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k\, y[n - k],
\qquad H(z) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k z^{-k}}{1 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k z^{-k}} .
$$

The feedback makes the impulse response infinitely long in general. The roots of the denominator are the filter's
poles <Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## Stability

Write $H(z)$ in partial fractions with distinct poles $p_k$: $H(z) = \sum_k \frac{c_k}{1 - p_k z^{-1}}$, plus a
polynomial if $M \ge N$. Each term is the transform of $c_k p_k^n$ for $n \ge 0$, so the causal impulse response is

$$
h[n] = \sum_k c_k\, p_k^{\,n}, \qquad n \ge 0 .
$$

This is absolutely summable exactly when every $\abs{p_k} < 1$. A causal IIR filter is therefore stable if and only if
all its poles lie strictly inside the unit circle; a pole on or outside the circle makes the response ring forever or
grow. Repeated poles add factors $n^j p_k^n$, which decay under the same condition. The distance of the poles from the
circle sets how long the response rings: a pole at radius $r$ decays by a factor $r$ per sample, with time constant
$-1/\ln r$ samples.

## Why use feedback

Poles near the unit circle create sharp peaks and steep transitions that FIR filters can only approximate with many
coefficients. For a typical low-pass specification, an elliptic IIR filter of order 4 does the work of an <Gloss name="fir" /> filter
with dozens of taps; see <NoteLink to="infinite-impulse-response-filter-design">IIR filter design</NoteLink>. The cost is in three places:

- **Phase.** A causal stable IIR filter cannot have exactly linear phase: linear phase needs a symmetric impulse
  response, which an infinite causal response cannot have. Its <NoteLink to="linear-phase-and-group-delay">group delay</NoteLink> varies with frequency, peaking near the
  band edge. <NoteLink to="zero-phase-filtering">Zero-phase filtering</NoteLink> removes the phase offline.
- **Numerical sensitivity.** Poles of a high-order polynomial move far when its coefficients are rounded, and a filter
  designed as stable can become unstable in fixed point. The standard remedy is to factor the filter into cascaded
  second-order sections (biquads), each with two poles and two zeros, which are far less sensitive.
- **Limit cycles.** Rounding inside a feedback loop can sustain small oscillations with zero input.

## Worked example

The one-pole low-pass filter, also called the exponential moving average,

$$
y[n] = a\, y[n - 1] + (1 - a)\, x[n], \qquad 0 < a < 1,
$$

has $H(z) = (1 - a)/(1 - a z^{-1})$, one pole at $z = a$, and impulse response $h[n] = (1 - a)a^n$: each past input is
weighted by a geometrically decaying factor. Its gain is 1 at $\omega = 0$, and

$$
\abs{H(e^{i\omega})}^2 = \frac{(1 - a)^2}{1 - 2a\cos\omega + a^2} .
$$

Setting this to $\tfrac12$ gives the half-power cutoff $\cos\omega_c = (-1 + 4a - a^2)/(2a)$. For $a = 0.9$,
$\omega_c = 0.1055$ rad/sample, about $0.034\pi$, and the time constant is $-1/\ln 0.9 = 9.5$ samples. Its mean delay,
$\sum_n n\, h[n] = a/(1 - a) = 9$ samples, matches a 19-tap moving average; the recursive filter needs one memory
element and two multiplications per sample instead of 19 stored inputs.

## Pitfalls

- **Design in sections.** Compute and apply high-order IIR filters as second-order sections (`sos` output in SciPy),
  not as one long numerator and denominator.
- **Transients.** The response to a sudden input lasts several time constants; the first outputs are unreliable.
- **Poles close to the circle.** Very narrow filters have poles within $10^{-3}$ of the unit circle, which demands high
  numerical precision and long settling.
