<Definition>
Zero-phase filtering of a finite record applies a filter $h$, reverses the result in time, applies $h$ again, and
reverses once more. The overall system has frequency response

$$
H_{\mathrm{zp}}(e^{i\omega}) = H(e^{i\omega})\, H^*(e^{i\omega}) = \abs{H(e^{i\omega})}^2,
$$

which is real and non-negative, so it introduces no phase shift at any frequency <Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## Why the phase cancels

Time reversal conjugates the spectrum of a real signal: if $u[n]$ has transform $U(e^{i\omega})$, then $u[-n]$ has
transform $U(e^{-i\omega}) = U^*(e^{i\omega})$. Follow a signal $x$ through the four steps:

1. Filter: $Y_1 = H X$.
2. Reverse: $Y_2 = Y_1^* = H^* X^*$.
3. Filter: $Y_3 = H Y_2 = H H^* X^*$.
4. Reverse: $Y_4 = Y_3^* = H^* H X = \abs{H}^2 X$.

The phase added on the forward pass is subtracted on the backward pass. The equivalent impulse response is the
autocorrelation of $h$, symmetric about $n = 0$, which is why it is non-causal: each output depends on inputs after it.

## What changes

- **No delay, no phase distortion.** Edges and peaks stay in place and symmetric pulses stay symmetric, whatever the
  filter's own phase. An IIR filter used this way gives the sharp magnitude response of feedback with the phase
  behaviour of a symmetric FIR filter.
- **Squared magnitude.** Every attenuation in dB doubles. The $-3$ dB cutoff of a Butterworth filter becomes a $-6$ dB
  point, and a design with 40 dB stopband attenuation gives 80 dB. Passband ripple doubles too. Design for half the
  requirements, or pre-compensate the cutoff.
- **Offline only.** The backward pass needs the whole record, so this is for recorded data, not real-time systems.

## Edge effects

A causal filter started from rest produces a transient while its internal state fills; the backward pass does the
same at the other end. Both ends of the output are therefore distorted over roughly the length of the impulse
response. Two remedies are standard, and SciPy's `filtfilt` uses both: pad the signal at each end, commonly by odd
reflection about the end value ($2x[0] - x[k]$), and start each pass from the steady-state initial conditions that
match the first sample. Gustafsson showed how to choose the initial states so that the forward–backward result
matches the backward–forward one <Cite id="gustafsson1996" />.

## Worked example

A second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with cutoff $0.1\pi$ has gain $1/\sqrt 2$ at the cutoff. Applied forward and
backward, its gain there is $\abs{H}^2 = 0.5$, or $-6.02$ dB, and its effective order is 4. Filtering a unit step at
$n = 80$ with a fourth-order Butterworth filter with cutoff $0.08\pi$: the causal output first exceeds 0.5 at
$n = 91$, eleven samples late, consistent with the filter's group delay of 10.3 samples at low frequencies. The
zero-phase output crosses 0.5 at $n = 80$, exactly at the step.

## Pitfalls

- **Not for causal analysis.** Zero-phase filtering uses future samples. Features computed from it leak information
  backwards in time, which invalidates forecasting or event-onset studies.
- **Short records.** When the record is not much longer than the impulse response, the edge transients dominate;
  reduce the order or the padding length accordingly.
- **Different from linear phase.** A linear-phase FIR filter is causal with a constant delay, which can be removed by
  shifting; forward–backward filtering has zero delay but always squares the magnitude response.
