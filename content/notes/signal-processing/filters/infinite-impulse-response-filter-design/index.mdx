<Definition>
The classical <Gloss name="iir" /> design procedure takes a normalised analog low-pass prototype $H_a(s)$ with cutoff 1 rad/s, scales
it to the required analog cutoff, and converts it to a digital filter by the bilinear transform

$$
s = \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{z - 1}{z + 1},
$$

where $T$ is the sample period. The four standard prototypes differ in where they place their error: Butterworth
nowhere in particular, Chebyshev type I in the passband, type II in the stopband, and elliptic in both
<Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## Analog prototypes

Each prototype is defined by its squared magnitude response on the $j\Omega$ axis:

- **Butterworth.** $\abs{H_a(j\Omega)}^2 = 1/\paren{1 + \Omega^{2N}}$. The first $2N - 1$ <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> are zero at
  $\Omega = 0$, so it is maximally flat; the response falls monotonically and is $-3$ dB at $\Omega = 1$. Its poles lie
  equally spaced on the left half of the unit circle.
- **Chebyshev type I.** $\abs{H_a}^2 = 1/\paren{1 + \varepsilon^2 T_N^2(\Omega)}$, with $T_N$ the Chebyshev polynomial.
  $T_N$ oscillates between $\pm 1$ on $\abs{\Omega} \le 1$ and grows fastest outside it of all polynomials of degree
  $N$, so the passband ripples by $1/(1 + \varepsilon^2)$ and the transition is steeper than Butterworth's. Its poles
  lie on an ellipse.
- **Chebyshev type II.** The inverse design: flat passband, equiripple stopband, and zeros on the $j\Omega$ axis, which
  become zeros on the unit circle.
- **Elliptic (Cauer).** Equiripple in both bands, using <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> elliptic functions. For a given order it has the
  narrowest transition band of any filter meeting given ripple limits, so it needs the lowest order.

## The bilinear transform

The bilinear transform replaces $s$ by $\frac{2}{T}\frac{z - 1}{z + 1}$, a rational map from the $z$-plane to the
$s$-plane, and so turns a rational $H_a(s)$ into a rational $H(z)$ of the same order. Put $z = e^{i\omega}$:

$$
s = \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{e^{i\omega} - 1}{e^{i\omega} + 1} = \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{e^{i\omega/2} - e^{-i\omega/2}}{e^{i\omega/2} + e^{-i\omega/2}}
= i\,\frac{2}{T}\tan\frac{\omega}{2} .
$$

So the unit circle maps onto the whole imaginary axis, with $\Omega = \frac{2}{T}\tan(\omega/2)$. The digital response
at $\omega$ equals the analog response at that $\Omega$: the frequency axis is compressed, but nothing aliases. Solving
for $z = (2/T + s)/(2/T - s)$ shows that $\abs{z} < 1$ exactly when $\operatorname{Re}s < 0$, so stable analog filters
map to stable digital filters.

The compression is nonlinear, which is why band edges must be *pre-warped*: to put a digital edge at $\omega_c$,
design the analog prototype with its edge at $\Omega_c = \frac{2}{T}\tan(\omega_c/2)$. Impulse invariance, the older
alternative, samples the analog <NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> instead; it keeps the frequency axis linear but aliases the analog
response, so it suits only band-limited low-pass designs.

## Choosing the order

For a Butterworth filter meeting a passband ripple of $R_p$ dB at $\Omega_p$ and an attenuation of $R_s$ dB at
$\Omega_s$ (both pre-warped), the order must satisfy

$$
N \ge \frac{\log_{10}\brack{\paren{10^{R_s/10} - 1}/\paren{10^{R_p/10} - 1}}}{2\log_{10}(\Omega_s/\Omega_p)},
$$

obtained by requiring the squared-magnitude formula to meet both limits. The Chebyshev and elliptic orders come from
similar formulas; SciPy's `buttord`, `cheb1ord`, `cheb2ord` and `ellipord` compute them.

## Worked example

Specify a low-pass filter with at most 1 dB ripple up to $0.2\pi$ and at least 40 dB attenuation from $0.3\pi$.
Pre-warping gives $\Omega_p = 2\tan(0.1\pi) = 0.650$ and $\Omega_s = 2\tan(0.15\pi) = 1.019$, with $T = 1$. The
Butterworth formula gives $N \ge 11.74$, so order 12. The required orders are:

| Design | Order |
| --- | --- |
| Butterworth | 12 |
| Chebyshev I | 6 |
| Chebyshev II | 6 |
| Elliptic | 4 |
| <Gloss name="fir" />, <NoteLink to="spectral-leakage-and-windows">Kaiser window</NoteLink> (40 dB, $\Delta\omega = 0.1\pi$) | 45 (46 taps) |

The elliptic filter needs about 9 multiplications per sample in <NoteLink to="difference-equations">direct
form</NoteLink>, against about 46 for the FIR design.

A fourth-order Butterworth filter with cutoff $0.3\pi$ is exactly $-3.01$ dB at $0.3\pi$ thanks to pre-warping.
Its <NoteLink to="linear-phase-and-group-delay">group delay</NoteLink> is 2.56 samples at DC and rises to 4.66
samples at $0.282\pi$, just below the cutoff. Without pre-warping, an analog cutoff of $0.3\pi$ rad/s would land at
$0.280\pi$.

## Pitfalls

- **Implement as second-order sections.** High-order designs as a single polynomial ratio lose precision badly; use
  `output='sos'`.
- **Ripple is steep but not free.** Chebyshev and elliptic filters have larger group-delay peaks and more ringing than
  Butterworth; transient-sensitive applications often prefer Butterworth or Bessel.
- **Band-pass and high-pass.** These are made from the low-pass prototype by frequency transformations before the
  bilinear transform; a band-pass filter of prototype order $N$ has order $2N$.
