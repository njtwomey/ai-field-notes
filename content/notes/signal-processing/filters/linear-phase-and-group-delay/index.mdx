<Definition>
For a filter with <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink> $H(e^{i\omega}) = \abs{H(e^{i\omega})}e^{i\phi(\omega)}$, with the phase $\phi$
unwrapped, the group delay and phase delay are

$$
\tau_g(\omega) = -\frac{d\phi(\omega)}{d\omega}, \qquad \tau_p(\omega) = -\frac{\phi(\omega)}{\omega},
$$

both in samples. A filter has linear phase when $\phi(\omega) = -\alpha\omega$ (plus a constant $\beta$ for
generalised linear phase), so that the group delay is the same constant $\alpha$ at every frequency
<Cite id="smith2007filters" />.
</Definition>

## What group delay measures

Consider a narrowband signal, a slowly varying envelope $e[n]$ on a carrier: $x[n] = e[n]\cos(\omega_0 n)$. Its
spectrum is concentrated near $\pm\omega_0$, where a smooth phase can be expanded to first order:

$$
\phi(\omega) \approx \phi(\omega_0) + \phi'(\omega_0)(\omega - \omega_0) = -\omega_0\tau_p(\omega_0) - (\omega - \omega_0)\tau_g(\omega_0) .
$$

A constant phase term shifts the carrier; a term linear in $\omega - \omega_0$ is a pure delay of the part of the signal
that varies slowly, the envelope. With the gain roughly constant over the band,

$$
y[n] \approx \abs{H(e^{i\omega_0})}\; e\paren{n - \tau_g(\omega_0)}\; \cos\paren{\omega_0\paren{n - \tau_p(\omega_0)}} .
$$

The envelope, which carries the information, arrives $\tau_g$ samples late; the carrier's phase is shifted by
$\tau_p$. When $\tau_g$ varies with frequency, the components of a broadband signal arrive at different times and its
waveform is distorted, even if every component's amplitude is preserved. This is phase distortion; transients smear
and pulses ring.

## Symmetric FIR filters have linear phase

Let $h[n] = h[M - n]$ for $n = 0, \dots, M$, with $M$ even. Pair each term with its mirror image around the centre
$M/2$:

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = e^{-i\omega M/2}\brack{h[M/2] + \sum_{k=1}^{M/2} 2h[M/2 - k]\cos(\omega k)} = e^{-i\omega M/2}A(\omega),
$$

because $h[M/2 - k]e^{-i\omega(M/2 - k)} + h[M/2 + k]e^{-i\omega(M/2 + k)} = h[M/2 - k]\,e^{-i\omega M/2}\,2\cos(\omega k)$.
$A(\omega)$ is real, so the phase is $-\omega M/2$ wherever $A > 0$ (with jumps of $\pi$ where $A$ changes sign, which
do not affect the delay). Every frequency is delayed by exactly $M/2$ samples. The same argument with odd $M$, or with
antisymmetric coefficients $h[n] = -h[M - n]$, gives the other three types:

| Type | Symmetry | Length $M + 1$ | Forced zeros | Unsuitable for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | symmetric | odd | none | — |
| II | symmetric | even | $\omega = \pi$ | high-pass, band-stop |
| III | antisymmetric | odd | $\omega = 0$ and $\pi$ | low-pass, high-pass |
| IV | antisymmetric | even | $\omega = 0$ | low-pass |

The forced zeros follow by evaluating the paired sum at $\omega = 0$ or $\pi$: for type II at $\omega = \pi$, the
pairs cancel because $\cos$ terms at half-integer offsets vanish. Antisymmetric types add a constant $\pi/2$ phase, which
makes them natural differentiators and Hilbert transformers.

Linear phase also constrains the zeros: if $z_0$ is a zero of a real linear-phase filter, so are $z_0^*$, $1/z_0$ and
$1/z_0^*$. Zeros come in reciprocal quadruples, or pairs on the unit circle.

A causal <Gloss name="iir" /> filter cannot have exactly linear phase: its infinite causal
<NoteLink to="linear-time-invariant-systems">impulse response</NoteLink> cannot be symmetric about any finite point.

## Worked example

The type I filter $h = (1, 2, 3, 2, 1)$ has $M = 4$ and

$$
H(e^{i\omega}) = e^{-2i\omega}\paren{3 + 4\cos\omega + 2\cos 2\omega},
$$

so its group delay is exactly 2 samples at every frequency, the centre of symmetry. A sixth-order Butterworth low-pass
filter with cutoff $0.35\pi$ behaves differently: its group delay is 3.31 samples at $0.1\pi$, 3.95 at $0.2\pi$, 6.13
at $0.3\pi$ and 7.12 at $0.34\pi$, while its phase delay over the same frequencies rises only from 3.21 to 4.20.
Passing a Gaussian tone burst at $0.2\pi$ through it moves the envelope's peak by 4 samples, matching the group delay.

## Pitfalls

- **Unwrap before differentiating.** The raw phase jumps by $2\pi$; differentiating it gives spikes. Compute group
  delay from the formula $\tau_g = \operatorname{Re}\{(\sum n h[n] e^{-i\omega n})/H(e^{i\omega})\}$ or from unwrapped
  phase.
- **Group delay is meaningless where the gain is zero.** Near zeros on the unit circle the phase jumps and the group
  delay spikes; nothing passes there anyway.
- **Negative group delay** is possible in narrow bands and does not violate causality: the envelope's peak is
  predicted from earlier samples, but no information arrives early.
