<Definition>
Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a subspace of $\reals^n$. The orthogonal projection of $\xvec$ onto $\mathcal{S}$ is the unique
$\pvec \in \mathcal{S}$ such that the residual $\xvec - \pvec$ is orthogonal to every vector in $\mathcal{S}$. It is
also the point of $\mathcal{S}$ closest to $\xvec$ in Euclidean distance <Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Onto a line

For the line spanned by a nonzero vector $\uvec$:

$$
\pvec = \frac{\uvec^\top \xvec}{\uvec^\top \uvec}\, \uvec.
$$

If $\uvec$ has unit length, this simplifies to $\pvec = (\uvec^\top \xvec)\, \uvec$. The scalar $\uvec^\top \xvec$ is
the coordinate of $\xvec$ along $\uvec$.

<Derivation title="Why the closest point has an orthogonal residual">
Minimise $g(c) = \norm{\xvec - c\uvec}^2 = \xvec^\top\xvec - 2c\, \uvec^\top\xvec + c^2 \uvec^\top\uvec$ over $c$.
Setting $g'(c) = 0$ gives $c = \uvec^\top\xvec / \uvec^\top\uvec$. At this $c$,
$\uvec^\top(\xvec - c\uvec) = \uvec^\top\xvec - c\, \uvec^\top\uvec = 0$: the residual is orthogonal to $\uvec$.

The converse holds too. For any other point $c'\uvec$, Pythagoras gives
$\norm{\xvec - c'\uvec}^2 = \norm{\xvec - \pvec}^2 + \norm{\pvec - c'\uvec}^2 \ge \norm{\xvec - \pvec}^2$.
</Derivation>

## Onto a subspace

Let the columns of $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times k}$ be a basis of $\mathcal{S}$. Write $\pvec = \Amat\cvec$. Requiring the
residual to be orthogonal to every column gives the normal equations $\Amat^\top(\xvec - \Amat\cvec) = \zeros$, so

$$
\pvec = \Pmat\xvec, \qquad \Pmat = \Amat(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}\Amat^\top.
$$

If the columns are orthonormal, collected in $\Qmat$, then $\Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$ and $\Pmat = \Qmat\Qmat^\top$.

## Properties of a projection matrix

- **Idempotent:** $\Pmat^2 = \Pmat$. Projecting twice changes nothing.
- **Symmetric:** $\Pmat^\top = \Pmat$. Symmetry distinguishes orthogonal projections from oblique ones.
- **Eigenvalues are 0 or 1.** Vectors in $\mathcal{S}$ are kept; vectors orthogonal to $\mathcal{S}$ are removed.
- **Rank** equals $\dim \mathcal{S}$, which also equals $\trace(\Pmat)$.
- **Complement:** $\eye - \Pmat$ projects onto the orthogonal complement $\mathcal{S}^\perp$.

## Least squares is a projection

In <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>, the fitted values
$\hat\yvec = \Xmat\hat\wvec$ lie in the column space of $\Xmat$. Least squares chooses the point of that space closest
to $\yvec$, so $\hat\yvec$ is the orthogonal projection of $\yvec$:

$$
\hat\yvec = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top \yvec.
$$

This $\Pmat$ is called the hat matrix, because it puts the hat on $\yvec$. The residuals $\yvec - \hat\yvec$ are
orthogonal to every column of $\Xmat$. In particular, when $\Xmat$ has a column of ones, the residuals sum to zero.

## Pitfalls

- **Forming the inverse.** Computing $(\Amat^\top\Amat)^{-1}$ squares the condition number. Use a QR factorisation,
  $\Amat = \Qmat\Rmat$, and project with $\Qmat\Qmat^\top$ instead.
- **Non-orthonormal bases.** $\Amat\Amat^\top$ is a projection only when the columns of $\Amat$ are orthonormal.
