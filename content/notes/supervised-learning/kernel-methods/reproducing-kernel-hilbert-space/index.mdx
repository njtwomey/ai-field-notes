<Definition>
A reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) on a set $\Xcal$ is a Hilbert space $\Hcal$ of functions $f : \Xcal \to \reals$
with a function $k : \Xcal \times \Xcal \to \reals$, the reproducing kernel, such that for every $\xvec \in \Xcal$

1. the function $k_{\xvec} = k(\xvec, \cdot)$ belongs to $\Hcal$, and
2. $f(\xvec) = \inner{f}{k_{\xvec}}_{\Hcal}$ for every $f \in \Hcal$ (the reproducing property).

Equivalently, $\Hcal$ is a Hilbert space of functions on which every evaluation $f \mapsto f(\xvec)$ is a continuous
linear functional <Cite id="aronszajn1950" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In most function spaces, such as $L^2$, a function is only defined up to its values on sets of measure zero, so
"the value at $\xvec$" means nothing. In an RKHS every function has a definite value at every point, and that value is
obtained geometrically, as the inner product with a fixed element $k_{\xvec}$ that represents the point $\xvec$. The
kernel is the inner product of two such representers: applying the reproducing property to $f = k_{\xvec'}$ gives

$$
k(\xvec, \xvec') = \inner{k_{\xvec}}{k_{\xvec'}}_{\Hcal}.
$$

So $\xvec \mapsto k_{\xvec}$ is a feature map into $\Hcal$ whose inner products are the kernel, which is exactly the
setting of the <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">kernel trick</NoteLink>. The RKHS is the canonical feature space of $k$.

The norm matters as much as the inner product. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives, for any $f, g \in \Hcal$,

$$
\abs{f(\xvec) - g(\xvec)} = \abs{\inner{f - g}{k_{\xvec}}_{\Hcal}} \le \norm{f - g}_{\Hcal}\sqrt{k(\xvec, \xvec)}.
$$

Functions that are close in norm are close at every point. A small norm forbids large values, and, for the kernels
below, large slopes or high frequencies. Penalising $\norm{f}_{\Hcal}$ is therefore a smoothness penalty whose meaning is
set by the kernel.

## Construction from a kernel

The Moore–Aronszajn theorem states that every symmetric positive semi-definite function $k$ is the reproducing kernel
of exactly one RKHS <Cite id="aronszajn1950" />. The construction is concrete.

1. **Finite combinations.** Let $\Hcal_0$ be the set of functions $f = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i k_{\xvec_i}$ for any finite
   set of points and coefficients.
2. **Inner product.** For $f = \sum_i a_i k_{\xvec_i}$ and $g = \sum_j b_j k_{\xvec'_j}$ define
   $\inner{f}{g} = \sum_{i,j} a_i b_j\, k(\xvec_i, \xvec'_j)$. This equals $\sum_j b_j f(\xvec'_j)$, which depends on
   $f$ only through its values, so it does not depend on how $f$ is written as a sum.
3. **Reproducing property.** Taking $g = k_{\xvec}$ gives $\inner{f}{k_{\xvec}} = \sum_i a_i k(\xvec_i, \xvec) = f(\xvec)$.
4. **Positive definiteness.** $\inner{f}{f} = \avec^\top\Kmat\avec \ge 0$ because the Gram matrix $\Kmat$ is
   positive semi-definite. If $\inner{f}{f} = 0$, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives
   $\abs{f(\xvec)} \le \sqrt{\inner{f}{f}}\sqrt{k(\xvec, \xvec)} = 0$ for every $\xvec$, so $f = 0$.
5. **Completion.** Add the limits of Cauchy sequences in $\Hcal_0$. By the pointwise bound above, a Cauchy sequence in
   norm converges at every point, so each limit is a genuine function.

Uniqueness follows because any RKHS with kernel $k$ contains every $k_{\xvec}$, hence $\Hcal_0$, and its inner product on
$\Hcal_0$ is forced by the reproducing property. For a finite combination, the squared norm is the quadratic form of
the Gram matrix:

$$
\norm{\textstyle\sum_i a_i k_{\xvec_i}}_{\Hcal}^2 = \avec^\top\Kmat\avec.
$$

## Examples

### The linear kernel

For $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \xvec^\top\xvec'$ on $\reals^D$, the representer of $\xvec$ is the linear function
$k_{\xvec}(\cdot) = \xvec^\top(\cdot)$. Finite sums of linear functions are linear, so
$\Hcal = \set{f_{\wvec}(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec : \wvec \in \reals^D}$ with
$\inner{f_{\wvec}}{f_{\vvec}}_{\Hcal} = \wvec^\top\vvec$. The reproducing property is
$\inner{f_{\wvec}}{k_{\xvec}} = \wvec^\top\xvec = f_{\wvec}(\xvec)$. The RKHS norm is the Euclidean norm of the weights,
so penalising it is ridge regression.

### The Brownian motion kernel

Let $k(x, x') = \min(x, x')$ on $[0, 1]$, the covariance of Brownian motion. The RKHS is the space of functions with
$f(0) = 0$ and a square-integrable derivative, with

$$
\inner{f}{g}_{\Hcal} = \int_0^1 f'(t)\, g'(t)\, dt, \qquad \norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \int_0^1 f'(t)^2\, dt.
$$

To check the reproducing property, note that $k_x(t) = \min(x, t)$ has derivative $1$ for $t < x$ and $0$ for $t > x$.
Then $\inner{f}{k_x}_{\Hcal} = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt = f(x) - f(0) = f(x)$. The norm is the total squared slope, a
roughness penalty.

### Worked example

With the Brownian motion kernel, take $f = k_{0.25} + k_{0.75}$. The Gram matrix of the points $0.25$ and $0.75$ is
$\begin{bmatrix} 0.25 & 0.25 \\ 0.25 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}$, so $\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \ones^\top\Kmat\ones = 1.5$.
Directly, $f$ has slope $2$ on $[0, 0.25]$, slope $1$ on $[0.25, 0.75]$ and slope $0$ after, and
$\int_0^1 f'^2 = 4 \times 0.25 + 1 \times 0.5 = 1.5$.

The function of smallest norm with $f(0.25) = 1$ and $f(0.75) = 0.5$ is $\sum_i \alpha_i k_{x_i}$ with
$\alphavec = \Kmat^{-1}\yvec = (5, -1)$, the <NoteLink to="representer-theorem">representer theorem</NoteLink> applied
to interpolation. It is the piecewise linear function through $(0, 0)$, $(0.25, 1)$ and $(0.75, 0.5)$, flat afterwards,
with squared norm $\yvec^\top\Kmat^{-1}\yvec = 16 \times 0.25 + 1 \times 0.5 = 4.5$. Linear interpolation is the
smoothest interpolation in this norm.

### Stationary kernels and frequencies

For a stationary kernel $k(x, x') = \kappa(x - x')$ on $\reals$ with Fourier transform $\hat\kappa(\omega) > 0$, the RKHS
norm is a weighted energy in frequency <Cite id="scholkopf2002" />:

$$
\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\abs{\hat f(\omega)}^2}{\hat\kappa(\omega)}\, d\omega.
$$

Frequencies where the kernel has little power are expensive. The exponential kernel $e^{-\abs{x - x'}}$ has
$\hat\kappa(\omega) = 2/(1 + \omega^2)$, so by Parseval's theorem
$\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \frac12\int(f^2 + f'^2)\,dx$, a Sobolev norm. The Gaussian kernel's transform decays like
$e^{-\ell^2\omega^2/2}$, so its RKHS contains only functions whose spectrum decays even faster, which are infinitely
differentiable.

## Mercer's theorem

Let $k$ be continuous and positive semi-definite on a compact set $\Xcal$ with a finite measure $\mu$. The integral
operator $(T f)(\xvec) = \int k(\xvec, \xvec') f(\xvec')\, d\mu(\xvec')$ has orthonormal eigenfunctions $e_i$ in $L^2(\mu)$
with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$, and

$$
k(\xvec, \xvec') = \sum_{i} \lambda_i\, e_i(\xvec)\, e_i(\xvec'),
$$

with absolute and uniform convergence <Cite id="mercer1909" />. The RKHS is then the set of
$f = \sum_i c_i e_i$ with $\sum_i c_i^2/\lambda_i < \infty$ (over $i$ with $\lambda_i > 0$), with
$\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \sum_i c_i^2/\lambda_i$. The reproducing property checks directly: $k_{\xvec}$ has coefficients
$\lambda_i e_i(\xvec)$, so $\inner{f}{k_{\xvec}}_{\Hcal} = \sum_i c_i\lambda_i e_i(\xvec)/\lambda_i = f(\xvec)$. The feature
map $\xvec \mapsto (\sqrt{\lambda_i}\, e_i(\xvec))_i$ realises the kernel as an inner product in $\ell^2$. Components
along eigenfunctions with small eigenvalues cost a lot of norm, which is how the RKHS norm encodes smoothness.

## Assumptions

- **Positive semi-definite kernel.** Only positive semi-definite kernels have an RKHS. A similarity function with
  indefinite Gram matrices does not define a Hilbert space of functions.
- **Functions, not equivalence classes.** RKHS elements are defined pointwise. $L^2$ is not an RKHS on $[0, 1]$,
  because evaluation at a point is not continuous there.

## Pitfalls

- **Gaussian process samples are not in the RKHS.** A zero-mean Gaussian process with kernel $k$ has the expansion
  $f = \sum_i \sqrt{\lambda_i}\, z_i\, e_i$ with independent $z_i \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. Its squared RKHS norm is
  $\sum_i \lambda_i z_i^2/\lambda_i = \sum_i z_i^2$, which diverges with probability one when infinitely many
  $\lambda_i$ are positive <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />. The RKHS describes the posterior mean, not typical sample paths.
- **Same function, different norms.** A function can belong to the RKHS of several kernels with different norms. The
  penalty $\norm{f}_{\Hcal}$ depends on the kernel, including its length-scale, not on $f$ alone.
- **The Gaussian RKHS is small.** Many continuous functions, such as $\abs{x}$, are not in the Gaussian kernel's RKHS.
  They can be approximated arbitrarily well by its elements, at the cost of a large norm.
