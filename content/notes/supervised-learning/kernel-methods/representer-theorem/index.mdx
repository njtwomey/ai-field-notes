<Definition>
Let $\Hcal$ be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel $k$ on an input set $\Xcal$, let
$\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_N \in \Xcal$ be training inputs, let $L : \reals^N \to \reals \cup \set{\infty}$ be any function
of the training values, and let $\Omega : [0, \infty) \to \reals$ be strictly increasing. Every minimiser of

$$
J(f) = L\paren{f(\xvec_1), \dots, f(\xvec_N)} + \Omega\paren{\norm{f}_{\Hcal}}
$$

over $f \in \Hcal$ has the form $f = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i\, k(\xvec_i, \cdot)$ for some $\alphavec \in \reals^N$
<Cite id="scholkopf2001" />. If $\Omega$ is only non-decreasing, at least one minimiser, if any exists, has this form.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A regularised learning problem over a function space has two parts: a loss that looks at the function only at the
training inputs, and a penalty on its size. In a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, evaluating $f$ at $\xvec_i$ is the
same as taking the inner product of $f$ with the function $k(\xvec_i, \cdot)$. Any part of $f$ orthogonal to all $N$
of those functions is therefore invisible to the loss. It still counts toward the norm, so the penalty removes it. What
is left lies in an $N$-dimensional subspace, whatever the dimension of $\Hcal$.

Kimeldorf and Wahba proved the first version, for squared loss, in their analysis of smoothing splines
<Cite id="kimeldorf1971" />. Schölkopf, Herbrich and Smola extended it to arbitrary losses and to any strictly
increasing penalty of the norm <Cite id="scholkopf2001" />.

## Proof

Let $\Scal = \Span\set{k(\xvec_1, \cdot), \dots, k(\xvec_N, \cdot)}$, a finite-dimensional and hence closed subspace of
$\Hcal$. Every $f \in \Hcal$ splits uniquely as

$$
f = f_{\parallel} + f_{\perp}, \qquad f_{\parallel} \in \Scal, \qquad \inner{f_{\perp}}{g}_{\Hcal} = 0 \text{ for all } g \in \Scal,
$$

by orthogonal projection onto $\Scal$. Two facts follow.

**The loss sees only $f_\parallel$.** The reproducing property states $f(\xvec) = \inner{f}{k(\xvec, \cdot)}_{\Hcal}$ for
every $f \in \Hcal$ and $\xvec \in \Xcal$. At a training input,

$$
f(\xvec_j) = \inner{f_{\parallel}}{k(\xvec_j, \cdot)}_{\Hcal} + \inner{f_{\perp}}{k(\xvec_j, \cdot)}_{\Hcal} = f_{\parallel}(\xvec_j) + 0,
$$

because $k(\xvec_j, \cdot) \in \Scal$ and $f_\perp$ is orthogonal to $\Scal$. So
$L(f(\xvec_1), \dots, f(\xvec_N)) = L(f_{\parallel}(\xvec_1), \dots, f_{\parallel}(\xvec_N))$.

**The penalty prefers $f_\parallel$.** By Pythagoras, $\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \norm{f_{\parallel}}_{\Hcal}^2 + \norm{f_{\perp}}_{\Hcal}^2$.
If $f_\perp \ne 0$ then $\norm{f}_{\Hcal} > \norm{f_{\parallel}}_{\Hcal}$, and since $\Omega$ is strictly increasing,
$\Omega(\norm{f}_{\Hcal}) > \Omega(\norm{f_{\parallel}}_{\Hcal})$.

Together, $J(f_\parallel) < J(f)$ whenever $f_\perp \ne 0$. A minimiser therefore has $f_\perp = 0$, so $f \in \Scal$
and $f = \sum_i \alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \cdot)$. If $\Omega$ is only non-decreasing, the inequality is $J(f_\parallel) \le J(f)$,
and replacing any minimiser $f$ by $f_\parallel$ gives a minimiser in $\Scal$. $\blacksquare$

## Consequences

Substituting $f = \sum_i \alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \cdot)$ turns the problem into one over $\alphavec \in \reals^N$. With the
Gram matrix $\Kmat_{ij} = k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$, the training values are $\Kmat\alphavec$ and the squared norm is

$$
\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\inner{k(\xvec_i, \cdot)}{k(\xvec_j, \cdot)}_{\Hcal} = \alphavec^\top\Kmat\alphavec,
$$

by the reproducing property applied to $k(\xvec_j, \cdot)$. The problem becomes

$$
\min_{\alphavec \in \reals^N}\ L(\Kmat\alphavec) + \Omega\paren{\sqrt{\alphavec^\top\Kmat\alphavec}}.
$$

The common kernel methods are instances.

| Method | Loss $L$ | Penalty $\Omega(t)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kernel ridge regression | $\sum_i (y_i - f(\xvec_i))^2$ | $\lambda t^2$ |
| Support vector machine (without bias) | $C\sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i f(\xvec_i))$ | $\frac12 t^2$ |
| Kernel logistic regression | $\sum_i \ln(1 + e^{-y_i f(\xvec_i)})$ | $\lambda t^2$ |
| Gaussian process MAP estimate | $-\sum_i \ln p(y_i \mid f(\xvec_i))$ | $\frac12 t^2$ |

For kernel ridge regression the reduced objective is $\norm{\yvec - \Kmat\alphavec}^2 + \lambda\alphavec^\top\Kmat\alphavec$.
Its gradient is $2\Kmat\brack{(\Kmat + \lambda\eye)\alphavec - \yvec}$, which vanishes at
$\alphavec = (\Kmat + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\yvec$, the <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge regression</NoteLink>
solution.

### Unpenalised terms

Many models add terms that are not penalised, such as the bias $b$ of the
<NoteLink to="kernel-svm">support vector machine</NoteLink> or a low-order polynomial trend in a spline. The semiparametric
representer theorem covers them. If $f = g + h$ with $g \in \Hcal$ penalised and $h$ in the span of $M$ fixed functions
$\psi_1, \dots, \psi_M$ not penalised, the minimiser has the form

$$
f = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \cdot) + \sum_{m=1}^{M}\beta_m\psi_m,
$$

by the same argument applied to $g$ with $h$ held fixed <Cite id="scholkopf2001" />.

## Assumptions

- **The loss depends on $f$ only through the training values.** A loss that also looks at derivatives or at other
  points needs the representers of those functionals, such as $\partial k(\xvec, \cdot)/\partial x_d$ for a derivative,
  added to the span.
- **The penalty is a monotone function of the RKHS norm.** A penalty on $\norm{\alphavec}_1$, or on a norm other than
  the RKHS norm, is a different problem, and its solutions need not lie in $\Scal$.
- **A reproducing kernel Hilbert space.** Point evaluation must be a continuous linear functional. In $L^2$ it is not,
  and the theorem fails.

## Pitfalls

- **Form is not sparsity.** The theorem gives $N$ coefficients but says nothing about how many are zero. Squared loss
  gives dense $\alphavec$; the hinge loss gives sparse $\alphavec$ because of its flat region.
- **Form is not uniqueness.** With a non-convex loss there can be several minimisers of the stated form.
- **Existence is assumed.** The theorem describes minimisers if they exist. With a convex, lower semicontinuous loss
  and $\Omega(t) \to \infty$ as $t \to \infty$, they do.
