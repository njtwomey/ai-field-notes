<Definition>
Given training pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ with $\xvec_i \in \reals^D$ and $y_i \in \set{-1, +1}$, the soft-margin support
vector machine (SVM) solves

$$
\min_{\wvec, b, \xivec}\ \frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad
y_i(\wvec^\top\xvec_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0,
$$

and classifies a new input by $\sgn(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)$ <Cite id="cortes1995" />. The slack $\xi_i$ measures how far
point $i$ violates the margin, and $C > 0$ sets the price of violations. With $C = \infty$ and separable data it is the
hard-margin SVM of Boser, Guyon and Vapnik <Cite id="boser1992" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

When two classes can be separated by a hyperplane, infinitely many hyperplanes do it. The SVM picks the one with the
widest empty band between the classes. A boundary far from every training point is robust to small perturbations of
the data and, by margin-based generalisation bounds, tends to generalise well.

The widest band touches a few points on each side. Those points, the support vectors, fix the solution: moving or
deleting any other point, as long as it stays outside the band, changes nothing. When the classes overlap, the soft
margin lets some points into the band or across the boundary, and $C$ trades the width of the band against the total
violation.

## The margin

The signed distance from a point $\xvec$ to the hyperplane $\wvec^\top\xvec + b = 0$ is
$(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)/\norm{\wvec}$. To see this, write $\xvec = \xvec_0 + t\,\wvec/\norm{\wvec}$ with $\xvec_0$ on the
hyperplane. Then $\wvec^\top\xvec + b = \wvec^\top\xvec_0 + b + t\norm{\wvec} = t\norm{\wvec}$, so
$t = (\wvec^\top\xvec + b)/\norm{\wvec}$.

The pair $(\wvec, b)$ can be rescaled by any positive constant without changing the hyperplane. Fix the scale so that
the closest points satisfy $y_i(\wvec^\top\xvec_i + b) = 1$. Their distance to the hyperplane is then $1/\norm{\wvec}$,
and the band between the planes $\wvec^\top\xvec + b = \pm 1$ has width $2/\norm{\wvec}$. Maximising the width is
minimising $\norm{\wvec}$, or equivalently $\frac12\norm{\wvec}^2$, subject to
$y_i(\wvec^\top\xvec_i + b) \ge 1$ for every $i$. This is the hard-margin problem. Adding slacks $\xi_i$ with a linear
penalty gives the soft-margin problem in the definition.

## The hinge loss

For fixed $\wvec$ and $b$, the best slack for point $i$ is the smallest one that satisfies both constraints:
$\xi_i = \max(0, 1 - y_i f(\xvec_i))$, with $f(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$. Substituting it removes the constraints:

$$
\min_{\wvec, b}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\paren{0,\ 1 - y_i f(\xvec_i)} + \frac{1}{2C}\norm{\wvec}^2.
$$

The SVM is therefore L2-regularised <Gloss name="erm">empirical risk minimisation</Gloss> with the hinge loss
$\ell(y, f) = \max(0, 1 - yf)$ <Cite id="hastie2009" />. The hinge loss is zero for points classified correctly with
margin at least 1 and grows linearly otherwise. <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</NoteLink>
minimises the same objective with the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink> $\ln(1 + e^{-yf})$, which is smooth and never exactly zero. The flat
part of the hinge loss is what makes the SVM solution sparse, and its lack of a probabilistic reading is why SVM
outputs are not probabilities.

## The dual problem

Introduce multipliers $\alpha_i \ge 0$ for the margin constraints and $\mu_i \ge 0$ for $\xi_i \ge 0$. The Lagrangian is

$$
L = \frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 + C\sum_i\xi_i - \sum_i\alpha_i\brack{y_i(\wvec^\top\xvec_i + b) - 1 + \xi_i} - \sum_i\mu_i\xi_i.
$$

For fixed primal variables, the maximum of $L$ over $\alpha_i, \mu_i \ge 0$ is the primal objective if every constraint
holds and $+\infty$ if any is violated. The primal problem is therefore $\min_{\wvec, b, \xivec}\max_{\alphavec, \muvec \ge \zeros} L$.
The <NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">dual problem</NoteLink> swaps the order, $\max_{\alphavec, \muvec}\min_{\wvec, b, \xivec} L$. Swapping never increases the
value, which is weak duality. For a convex quadratic objective with linear constraints that some point satisfies, as
here, the two values are equal.

The inner minimisation is unconstrained, so its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> vanish:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\wvec} = \wvec - \sum_i\alpha_i y_i\xvec_i = \zeros, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = -\sum_i\alpha_i y_i = 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\xi_i} = C - \alpha_i - \mu_i = 0.
$$

The first condition gives $\wvec = \sum_i\alpha_i y_i\xvec_i$. The third, with $\mu_i \ge 0$, gives $\alpha_i \le C$.
Substituting all three into $L$ cancels the slack terms and the bias term. The remaining terms are
$\frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 - \wvec^\top\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\xvec_i + \sum_i\alpha_i = -\frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 + \sum_i\alpha_i$.
The dual is

$$
\max_{\alphavec}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i - \frac12\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j\,\xvec_i^\top\xvec_j
\quad \text{subject to} \quad 0 \le \alpha_i \le C,\ \ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i y_i = 0.
$$

The dual is a quadratic programme in $N$ variables with box constraints and one equality. It depends on the inputs only
through the <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink> $\xvec_i^\top\xvec_j$. Replacing them with a kernel $k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$ gives the
<NoteLink to="kernel-support-vector-machine">kernel SVM</NoteLink>.

### Support vectors

At the optimum, <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">complementary slackness</NoteLink> holds for each constraint: $\alpha_i\brack{y_i f(\xvec_i) - 1 + \xi_i} = 0$ and
$\mu_i\xi_i = (C - \alpha_i)\xi_i = 0$. Each training point falls into one of three cases.

| $\alpha_i$ | Consequence | Position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $\xi_i = 0$, $y_i f(\xvec_i) \ge 1$ | outside the margin; no influence on $\wvec$ |
| $0 < \alpha_i < C$ | $\xi_i = 0$, $y_i f(\xvec_i) = 1$ | exactly on the margin |
| $C$ | $\xi_i \ge 0$, $y_i f(\xvec_i) \le 1$ | inside the margin or misclassified |

The points with $\alpha_i > 0$ are the support vectors. The weight vector $\wvec = \sum_i\alpha_i y_i\xvec_i$ is a
combination of them alone. Any support vector on the margin gives the bias, $b = y_i - \wvec^\top\xvec_i$; averaging
over all of them is numerically safer.

### Worked example

Take three points: $\xvec_1 = (0, 0)$ with $y_1 = -1$, and $\xvec_2 = (2, 0)$, $\xvec_3 = (0, 2)$ with label $+1$. The
problem is symmetric under swapping the two coordinates, so $\wvec = (a, a)$. The hard-margin constraints are
$-b \ge 1$ for $\xvec_1$ and $2a + b \ge 1$ for $\xvec_2$ and $\xvec_3$. Minimising $\norm{\wvec}^2 = 2a^2$ makes both
tight: $b = -1$ and $a = 1$. The solution is $\wvec = (1, 1)$, $b = -1$, a boundary $x_1 + x_2 = 1$ and a margin
width $2/\norm{\wvec} = \sqrt 2$.

In the dual, $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i = 0$ gives $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 + \alpha_3$, and
$\wvec = \alpha_2(2, 0) + \alpha_3(0, 2) = (1, 1)$ gives $\alpha_2 = \alpha_3 = 0.5$ and $\alpha_1 = 1$. The dual
objective is $\sum_i\alpha_i - \frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 = 2 - 1 = 1$, equal to the primal objective
$\frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 = 1$. A fourth point $(3, 3)$ with label $+1$ has $f = 5 > 1$, so $\alpha_4 = 0$ and adding it
changes nothing.

This example has no slack. A six-point soft-margin example, with points inside the margin and across the boundary, is
solved exactly as a quadratic programme and by <Gloss name="smo" /> in
<NoteLink to="solving-support-vector-machines">solving a support vector machine by hand</NoteLink>, including every
slack $\xi_i$.

## Training

The dual has $N$ variables and a dense $N \times N$ matrix, so general-purpose <Gloss name="qp">quadratic programming</Gloss> solvers scale
poorly. Sequential minimal optimisation (SMO) updates two multipliers at a time <Cite id="platt1998" />. Two is the
smallest number that can move while keeping $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i = 0$: fixing all others ties the pair to a line
segment inside the box $[0, C]^2$. The objective along that segment is a one-dimensional quadratic, minimised in
closed form and clipped to the box. Choosing the pair that most violates the optimality conditions and repeating
converges to the solution. Every step of an SMO run, with the
errors, the clipping and the bias update, is worked through on six points in
<NoteLink to="solving-support-vector-machines">solving a support vector machine by hand</NoteLink>. Linear SVMs on
large sparse data are usually trained in the primal instead, by <NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">coordinate descent</NoteLink> on the dual or <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradient methods</NoteLink> on the hinge loss.

## Assumptions

- **Two classes.** Multi-class problems combine binary SVMs, one class against the rest or one pair of classes at a
  time.
- **Scaled features.** The margin is measured in the <NoteLink to="vector-norms">Euclidean norm</NoteLink>, so features on larger scales dominate it.
  Standardise the features.
- **Linear boundary.** The linear SVM draws a hyperplane. Curved boundaries need the kernel form.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the output as a probability.** $f(\xvec)$ is a signed distance, not a probability. <NoteLink to="platt-scaling">Platt scaling</NoteLink> fits a
  logistic function to held-out decision values when probabilities are needed <Cite id="platt1999" />.
- **Choosing $C$ on training error.** Training error always falls as $C$ grows. Choose $C$ by cross-validation, on a
  logarithmic grid.
- **<NoteLink to="class-imbalance">Class imbalance</NoteLink>.** One $C$ for both classes lets the majority class dominate the slack total. Use a separate $C$
  per class, scaled inversely to class frequency.
- **Many support vectors.** With heavily overlapping classes most points end up at $\alpha_i = C$. The solution is
  then not sparse and prediction with a kernel is slow.
