<Definition>
A sparse Gaussian process introduces $M$ inducing inputs $\Zmat = (\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_M)$ and the inducing
variables $\uvec = f(\Zmat) \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat_{uu})$, the latent function's values there. It approximates the
joint prior of the training values $\fvec$ and test values $\fvec_*$ by assuming them conditionally independent given
$\uvec$, with an approximate training conditional $q(\fvec \mid \uvec)$ <Cite id="quinonero2005" />. Every
computation then involves only $M \times M$ matrices and $N \times M$ cross-covariances.
</Definition>

## Problem

Exact <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> regression factorises the $N \times N$ matrix
$\Kmat_{ff} + \sigma^2\eye$, in $O(N^3)$ time and $O(N^2)$ memory, for predictions and for every evaluation of the
marginal likelihood during hyperparameter fitting. At $N = 10^5$ the matrix alone takes 80 GB in double precision.
Most of that work is redundant when the function is smooth: a few hundred well-placed function values determine it
almost everywhere.

## Mechanism

### The Nyström matrix

Write $\Kmat_{fu} = k(\Xmat, \Zmat)$ for the $N \times M$ cross-covariance between training and inducing inputs,
$\Kmat_{uu} = k(\Zmat, \Zmat)$, and

$$
\Qmat_{ab} = \Kmat_{au}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\Kmat_{ub}
$$

for any two sets of inputs $a$ and $b$. By GP conditioning, $\expect[\fvec \mid \uvec] = \Kmat_{fu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\uvec$
and $\cov[\fvec \mid \uvec] = \Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}$. The matrix $\Qmat_{ff}$ has rank at most $M$ and is the
covariance of the conditional mean. It is the Nyström approximation of $\Kmat_{ff}$ <Cite id="williams2001" />. The
difference $\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}$ is positive semi-definite, being a conditional covariance.

### Three approximate priors

Each method keeps the exact prior $p(\uvec)$ and replaces the exact conditional
$p(\fvec \mid \uvec) = \Gauss(\Kmat_{fu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\uvec,\ \Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff})$ by a cheaper one.
Integrating $\uvec$ out gives a Gaussian marginal over the targets <Cite id="quinonero2005" />:

| Method | Training conditional $q(\fvec \mid \uvec)$ | Marginal $q(\yvec)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subset of regressors (SoR), DTC | $\Gauss(\Kmat_{fu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\uvec,\ \zeros)$ | $\Gauss(\zeros,\ \Qmat_{ff} + \sigma^2\eye)$ |
| FITC | $\Gauss(\Kmat_{fu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\uvec,\ \diag[\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}])$ | $\Gauss(\zeros,\ \Qmat_{ff} + \diag[\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}] + \sigma^2\eye)$ |

The subset of regressors makes $\fvec$ a deterministic function of $\uvec$. It is a finite linear model with $M$ basis
functions $k(\cdot, \zvec_m)$, so its predictive variance collapses to zero far from the inducing inputs. Deterministic
training conditional (DTC) uses the same training conditional but the exact test conditional, which restores the
term $\Kmat_{**} - \Qmat_{**}$ in the predictive variance. The fully independent training conditional (FITC) keeps the
exact conditional variance of each $f_i$ and drops only the correlations between them. It was introduced as the
sparse pseudo-input GP, with the inducing inputs optimised as hyperparameters <Cite id="snelson2006" />.

### Prediction in $O(NM^2)$

Write the marginal covariance as $\Qmat_{ff} + \Lambdamat$, with $\Lambdamat = \sigma^2\eye$ for DTC and
$\Lambdamat = \diag[\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}] + \sigma^2\eye$ for FITC. Both $\Lambdamat$ are diagonal. Define the
$M \times M$ matrix

$$
\Sigmamat = (\Kmat_{uu} + \Kmat_{uf}\Lambdamat^{-1}\Kmat_{fu})^{-1}.
$$

The Woodbury identity gives
$(\Qmat_{ff} + \Lambdamat)^{-1} = \Lambdamat^{-1} - \Lambdamat^{-1}\Kmat_{fu}\Sigmamat\Kmat_{uf}\Lambdamat^{-1}$,
which needs only $\Sigmamat$ and a diagonal inverse. Substituting into the Gaussian conditioning formulas gives the
predictive mean and variance at a test input with cross-covariance $\kvec_{u*} = k(\Zmat, \xvec_*)$:

$$
\mu_* = \kvec_{u*}^\top\Sigmamat\Kmat_{uf}\Lambdamat^{-1}\yvec, \qquad
\sigma_*^2 = k_{**} - \kvec_{u*}^\top\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\kvec_{u*} + \kvec_{u*}^\top\Sigmamat\kvec_{u*},
$$

where $k_{**} = k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*)$. SoR drops the first two terms of $\sigma_*^2$. Forming
$\Kmat_{uf}\Lambdamat^{-1}\Kmat_{fu}$ costs $O(NM^2)$ and everything else is $O(M^3)$ or cheaper. The log marginal
likelihood $\ln\Gauss(\yvec \mid \zeros, \Qmat_{ff} + \Lambdamat)$ costs the same through the matrix determinant
lemma, $\abs{\Qmat_{ff} + \Lambdamat} = \abs{\Sigmamat^{-1}}\,\abs{\Kmat_{uu}}^{-1}\abs{\Lambdamat}$.

### The variational free energy

DTC and FITC change the model and then fit it. The inducing inputs become extra hyperparameters of a different
model, and nothing ties that model to the exact GP. Titsias instead keeps the exact model and approximates its
posterior <Cite id="titsias2009" />. The variational family is

$$
q(\fvec, \uvec) = p(\fvec \mid \uvec)\, q(\uvec),
$$

with the exact conditional and a free distribution $q(\uvec)$. The <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower
bound</NoteLink> is

$$
\ln p(\yvec) \ge \Fcal = \int p(\fvec \mid \uvec)\, q(\uvec) \ln\frac{p(\yvec \mid \fvec)\, p(\fvec \mid \uvec)\, p(\uvec)}{p(\fvec \mid \uvec)\, q(\uvec)}\, d\fvec\, d\uvec.
$$

The conditional $p(\fvec \mid \uvec)$ cancels inside the logarithm, which is what makes the bound cheap. The integral
over $\fvec$ then involves only the Gaussian likelihood. With $\fvec \mid \uvec$ of mean
$\mvec_u = \Kmat_{fu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\uvec$ and covariance $\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}$,

$$
\int p(\fvec \mid \uvec)\ln p(\yvec \mid \fvec)\, d\fvec
= \ln\Gauss(\yvec \mid \mvec_u, \sigma^2\eye) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\trace(\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}),
$$

because $\expect\norm{\yvec - \fvec}^2 = \norm{\yvec - \mvec_u}^2 + \trace\cov[\fvec \mid \uvec]$. Write
$G(\uvec) = \Gauss(\yvec \mid \mvec_u, \sigma^2\eye)$. The bound becomes

$$
\Fcal = \int q(\uvec)\ln\frac{G(\uvec)\, p(\uvec)}{q(\uvec)}\, d\uvec - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\trace(\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}).
$$

The first term equals $\ln\int G(\uvec)p(\uvec)\,d\uvec$ minus the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> from $q(\uvec)$ to the normalised product $G(\uvec)p(\uvec)$. It
is maximised by making that divergence zero, so the optimal $q(\uvec)$ is proportional to $G(\uvec)p(\uvec)$, a
Gaussian. The integral $\int G(\uvec)p(\uvec)\,d\uvec$ is the marginal of $\yvec = \Kmat_{fu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\uvec + \varepsilonvec$
with $\uvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat_{uu})$, which is $\Gauss(\yvec \mid \zeros, \Qmat_{ff} + \sigma^2\eye)$. The
collapsed bound is

$$
\Fcal = \ln\Gauss(\yvec \mid \zeros, \Qmat_{ff} + \sigma^2\eye) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\trace(\Kmat_{ff} - \Qmat_{ff}).
$$

The first term is the DTC marginal likelihood. The trace term is the total prior variance of $\fvec$ that the
inducing variables fail to explain, divided by twice the noise variance. It penalises inducing inputs that leave
parts of the data poorly covered. It vanishes when $\Zmat = \Xmat$, where the bound equals the exact marginal
likelihood. The optimal $q(\uvec)$ gives the DTC predictive equations.

Because $\Fcal$ is a lower bound on the exact $\ln p(\yvec)$, the inducing inputs are variational parameters, not
model parameters. Maximising $\Fcal$ over $\Zmat$ and the hyperparameters jointly moves the approximation toward the
exact posterior and cannot overfit by adding inducing points. The DTC and FITC marginal likelihoods are not bounds.
In the figure, with length-scale 0.6 and ten evenly spaced inducing inputs, the DTC value (shown for SoR, which shares it) is $-29.6$ against
an exact $-34.2$, while the bound is $-114.2$.

### Stochastic variational inference

The collapsed bound still sums over all data inside a single Gaussian, so it cannot be evaluated on a minibatch.
Hensman, Fusi and Lawrence keep $q(\uvec) = \Gauss(\mvec, \Smat)$ explicit instead of optimising it out
<Cite id="hensman2013" />. The bound then separates over data points:

$$
\Fcal = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\expect_{q(f_i)}[\ln p(y_i \mid f_i)] - \KL\paren{q(\uvec)\,\|\,p(\uvec)},
$$

where $q(f_i)$ is Gaussian with mean $\kvec_{iu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\mvec$ and variance
$k_{ii} - \kvec_{iu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\kvec_{ui} + \kvec_{iu}\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\Smat\Kmat_{uu}^{-1}\kvec_{ui}$. An unbiased
estimate of the sum comes from a minibatch, so the stochastic variational GP (SVGP) trains by stochastic gradient
ascent in $O(M^3)$ per step, independent of $N$. The expectations are one-dimensional, so non-Gaussian likelihoods such
as classification need only quadrature <Cite id="hensman2015" />.

## Cost

| Method | Training | Prediction, mean / variance | Memory |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exact GP | $O(N^3)$ | $O(N)$ / $O(N^2)$ | $O(N^2)$ |
| SoR, DTC, FITC, VFE | $O(NM^2)$ | $O(M)$ / $O(M^2)$ | $O(NM)$ |
| SVGP | $O(BM^2 + M^3)$ per step, minibatch $B$ | $O(M)$ / $O(M^2)$ | $O(M^2)$ |

## Trade-offs

- **FITC versus VFE.** FITC's heteroscedastic diagonal lets it fit the data well with few inducing points, but it
  tends to underestimate the noise variance and can overfit, because its inducing inputs are free model parameters.
  VFE tends to overestimate the noise and to underfit with too few inducing points, but improves monotonically as
  inducing points are added <Cite id="bauer2016" />.
- **Number of inducing points.** $M$ must grow with the complexity of the function, roughly with the input range
  divided by the length-scale in one dimension. Short length-scales in high dimensions need many inducing points,
  and the approximation then loses its advantage.
- **Placement.** Initialise $\Zmat$ with a subset of the inputs or k-means centres, then optimise. In the figure,
  crowding the inducing inputs leaves regions uncovered, the trace term grows and the VFE band widens there.
- **Alternatives.** Random Fourier features give a sparse spectrum approximation with a fixed basis. Structured
  interpolation methods exploit grid structure instead of a low-rank one.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing marginal likelihoods across methods.** DTC and FITC define their own models. Their marginal likelihoods
  can exceed the exact one and are not bounds. Only the VFE bound is comparable to the exact value.
- **Degenerate SoR variance.** The subset-of-regressors predictive variance shrinks to zero far from the inducing
  inputs. Use DTC, FITC or VFE predictions instead.
- **Ill-conditioned $\Kmat_{uu}$.** Two inducing inputs that drift together make $\Kmat_{uu}$ nearly singular. Add
  jitter to its diagonal before factorising.
