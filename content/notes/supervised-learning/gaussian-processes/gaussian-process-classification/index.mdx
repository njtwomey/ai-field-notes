<Definition>
Binary <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> classification models labels $y_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ through a latent function
$f \sim \GP(0, k)$ and a sigmoid likelihood, $p(y_i \mid f_i) = \sigma(y_i f_i)$ with $f_i = f(\xvec_i)$
<Cite id="williams1998" />. Here $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ is the logistic sigmoid, or the standard normal cdf
$\Phi$ for the probit model. The prediction for a new input is the class probability averaged over the posterior of
the latent function,

$$
\bar\pi_* = p(y_* = +1 \mid \xvec_*, \yvec) = \int \sigma(f_*)\, p(f_* \mid \xvec_*, \yvec)\, df_*.
$$
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</NoteLink> squashes a linear function through the sigmoid. GP
classification squashes a random function instead, with the kernel controlling how smoothly the class probability
changes with the input. The latent function has no meaning of its own. Only $\sigma(f)$ is observed, through the
labels.

With a Gaussian likelihood the posterior over the latent values would be Gaussian and exact. The sigmoid likelihood
breaks conjugacy: the posterior $p(\fvec \mid \yvec) \propto p(\yvec \mid \fvec)\,\Gauss(\fvec \mid \zeros, \Kmat)$
has no closed form. Every practical method replaces it with a Gaussian $q(\fvec) = \Gauss(\fvec \mid \hat\fvec, \Sigmamat)$
and then predicts as if it were exact.

## The Laplace approximation

The <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> fits a Gaussian at the posterior mode, with
covariance equal to the inverse of the negative <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> of the log posterior there <Cite id="williams1998" />. The log
posterior, up to a constant, is

$$
\Psi(\fvec) = \ln p(\yvec \mid \fvec) - \tfrac12\fvec^\top\Kmat^{-1}\fvec.
$$

The likelihood factorises over data points, so its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is a vector with entries
$\partial\ln p(y_i \mid f_i)/\partial f_i$ and its Hessian is diagonal. Define

$$
\Wmat = -\nabla\nabla\ln p(\yvec \mid \fvec), \qquad \nabla\Psi = \nabla\ln p(\yvec \mid \fvec) - \Kmat^{-1}\fvec, \qquad
\nabla\nabla\Psi = -\Wmat - \Kmat^{-1}.
$$

For the logistic likelihood, with targets $t_i = (y_i + 1)/2 \in \set{0, 1}$ and $\pi_i = \sigma(f_i)$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink>
are $\partial\ln p(y_i \mid f_i)/\partial f_i = t_i - \pi_i$ and $W_{ii} = \pi_i(1 - \pi_i)$. The log-likelihood is
concave, so $\Wmat$ has non-negative entries, $\Psi$ is strictly concave and the mode $\hat\fvec$ is unique.

### Finding the mode

Setting $\nabla\Psi = \zeros$ gives the self-consistency condition

$$
\hat\fvec = \Kmat\,\nabla\ln p(\yvec \mid \hat\fvec).
$$

The mode cannot be read off, since the right side depends on $\hat\fvec$. <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> solves it. A Newton step
replaces $\fvec$ by $\fvec - (\nabla\nabla\Psi)^{-1}\nabla\Psi$, which simplifies to

$$
\fvec^{\text{new}} = (\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat)^{-1}\paren{\Wmat\fvec + \nabla\ln p(\yvec \mid \fvec)}.
$$

Newton's method on a concave function with this curvature typically converges in fewer than ten iterations. A
numerically stable implementation never inverts $\Kmat$. It factorises
$\Bmat = \eye + \Wmat^{1/2}\Kmat\Wmat^{1/2}$, whose eigenvalues are at least 1, by <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky decomposition</NoteLink>
<Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.

The approximation is

$$
q(\fvec \mid \yvec) = \Gauss\paren{\fvec \mid \hat\fvec, (\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat)^{-1}}.
$$

### Predicting the latent value

For a test input with cross-covariance $\kvec_* = k(\Xmat, \xvec_*)$, the GP prior gives the conditional
$p(f_* \mid \fvec) = \Gauss(f_* \mid \kvec_*^\top\Kmat^{-1}\fvec,\ k_{**} - \kvec_*^\top\Kmat^{-1}\kvec_*)$, with
$k_{**} = k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*)$. Averaging over $q$ by the laws of total <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> and total variance gives

$$
\expect_q[f_*] = \kvec_*^\top\Kmat^{-1}\hat\fvec = \kvec_*^\top\nabla\ln p(\yvec \mid \hat\fvec),
$$

using the mode condition, and

$$
\var_q[f_*] = k_{**} - \kvec_*^\top\Kmat^{-1}\kvec_* + \kvec_*^\top\Kmat^{-1}(\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat)^{-1}\Kmat^{-1}\kvec_*
= k_{**} - \kvec_*^\top(\Kmat + \Wmat^{-1})^{-1}\kvec_*.
$$

The second equality is the Woodbury identity
$(\Kmat + \Wmat^{-1})^{-1} = \Kmat^{-1} - \Kmat^{-1}(\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat)^{-1}\Kmat^{-1}$. The formulas match GP
regression with a noise variance $1/W_{ii}$ for each point: points classified confidently have small $W_{ii}$ and act
like very noisy observations. The mean is a weighted sum of kernels with weights $t_i - \hat\pi_i$, so points that are
already fitted well contribute little.

### Predicting the class probability

The class probability averages the sigmoid over $f_* \sim \Gauss(\mu_*, s_*^2)$, with $\mu_*$ and $s_*^2$ the moments
above. For the probit likelihood the integral is exact:
$\int\Phi(f)\Gauss(f \mid \mu, s^2)\,df = \Phi(\mu/\sqrt{1 + s^2})$. For the logistic sigmoid MacKay's approximation
$\bar\pi_* \approx \sigma(\kappa\mu_*)$ with $\kappa = (1 + \pi s_*^2/8)^{-1/2}$ follows from
$\sigma(z) \approx \Phi(\sqrt{\pi/8}\,z)$ <Cite id="mackay1992c" />. Averaging moves the probability toward $1/2$ by an
amount that grows with the latent uncertainty. The decision $\bar\pi_* > 1/2$ is the same as $\mu_* > 0$, but the
probability is better calibrated than $\sigma(\mu_*)$.

### Worked example

Take one training point with $y = +1$ and prior variance $k(\xvec, \xvec) = 4$. The mode solves
$\hat f = 4\,(1 - \sigma(\hat f))$, which gives $\hat f = 1.043$ and $\sigma(\hat f) = 0.739$. The curvature is
$W = 0.739 \times 0.261 = 0.193$, so the approximate posterior variance is $(1/4 + 0.193)^{-1} = 2.259$. The plug-in
probability at the training input is $0.739$. Averaging the sigmoid over $\Gauss(1.043, 2.259)$ gives $0.677$, and
MacKay's approximation gives $\sigma(1.043/\sqrt{1 + 2.259\pi/8}) = 0.681$.

### The approximate marginal likelihood

The kernel hyperparameters are fitted by the Laplace approximation to the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>. Expanding $\Psi$ to
second order about $\hat\fvec$ and integrating the Gaussian gives

$$
\ln q(\yvec \mid \Xmat) = -\tfrac12\hat\fvec^\top\Kmat^{-1}\hat\fvec + \ln p(\yvec \mid \hat\fvec) - \tfrac12\ln\abs{\Bmat},
$$

where the prior's normaliser $\abs{\Kmat}^{-1/2}$ and the <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>'s $\abs{\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat}^{-1/2}$ combine
through $\abs{\Kmat}\,\abs{\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat} = \abs{\eye + \Kmat\Wmat} = \abs{\Bmat}$ <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.

## Expectation propagation

<Gloss name="ep">Expectation propagation (EP)</Gloss> replaces each likelihood factor $p(y_i \mid f_i)$ by an unnormalised Gaussian site
$\tilde t_i(f_i)$ <Cite id="minka2001" />. It updates one site at a time: remove it from the current approximation,
multiply in the exact likelihood factor, and choose the new site so that the resulting Gaussian matches the mean and
variance of that tilted distribution. For the probit likelihood the required moments are in closed form. Where the
Laplace approximation matches curvature at the mode, EP matches moments, which suits the skewed posteriors of
classification. In a comparison on benchmark problems, EP gave predictive probabilities and marginal likelihoods much
closer to <Gloss name="mcmc">Markov chain Monte Carlo</Gloss> than the Laplace approximation, which underestimates the latent mean
<Cite id="kuss2005" />. EP costs a few times more and is not guaranteed to converge.

## Assumptions

- **Binary labels.** The multi-class version uses one latent function per class and a softmax likelihood, with
  $\Wmat$ block-structured rather than diagonal.
- **Gaussian approximation.** Both methods assume the posterior is close to Gaussian. With well-separated classes it is
  strongly skewed, and the Laplace approximation places the mean too close to zero.

## Pitfalls

- **Cubic cost.** Each Newton step costs $O(N^3)$, like exact GP regression, and sparse approximations are needed for
  large datasets.
- **Reporting $\sigma(\mu_*)$.** Squashing the latent mean ignores the latent variance and gives overconfident
  probabilities far from the data.
- **Large signal variance.** With separable data the marginal likelihood can keep increasing with $\sigma_f$, driving
  the latent function to extreme values. Bound $\sigma_f$ or put a prior on it.
