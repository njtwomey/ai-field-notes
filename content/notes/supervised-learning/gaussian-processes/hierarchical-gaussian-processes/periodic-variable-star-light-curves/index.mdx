<Definition>
The data are light curves of periodic variable stars from the Optical Gravitational Lensing Experiment (OGLE), as
distributed by the UCR time-series archive <Cite id="dau2019" />: 1329 Cepheids (CEPH), 2580 eclipsing binaries (EB) and 5326 RR Lyrae
(RRL), each a single phase-folded period resampled to 1024 points <Cite id="twomey2019" />. Some curves may carry the
wrong class label. The task is to rank the curves of a class so that curves that do not conform to the class come
first. The method is to fit a <NoteLink to="hierarchical-gaussian-process">hierarchical Gaussian process</NoteLink> to
training curves of the class and score each curve by its negative log marginal likelihood under that model
<Cite id="twomey2019" />. With labels available, EB curves are planted as known anomalies among CEPH and RRL curves,
and the ranking is scored by precision.
</Definition>

## Setup

### Light curves and periodic variable stars

A light curve is a time series of the brightness of one celestial object. Astronomers record brightness as a magnitude
$m = -2.5\log_{10} F + \text{const}$, where $F$ is the measured flux, so a brighter star has a smaller magnitude. A
periodic variable star repeats its light curve with a fixed period $P$. The three classes in the data vary for
different physical reasons <Cite id="sterken2005" />:

- **Cepheids** are pulsating supergiant stars with periods of days to months. The light curve rises quickly to
  maximum and declines slowly. The period of a Cepheid is tied to its luminosity, which makes Cepheids calibrators of
  the extragalactic distance scale <Cite id="twomey2019" />.
- **RR Lyrae** stars are pulsating stars with periods under about one day. Their light curves have a shape similar to
  Cepheid light curves, which makes the two classes hard to separate by shape alone <Cite id="twomey2019" />.
- **Eclipsing binaries** are pairs of stars whose orbit passes one in front of the other as seen from Earth. The light
  curve is nearly flat, with a dip at each eclipse. The EB curves are distinct from the other two classes
  <Cite id="twomey2019" />.

### Period finding and phase folding

A survey observes each star at irregular times $t_1, \dots, t_M$, spread over many periods. The period is estimated
first, for example with the <NoteLink to="lomb-scargle-periodogram">Lomb–Scargle periodogram</NoteLink>, which fits
sinusoids to unevenly sampled data. Given a period $P$ and a reference epoch $t_0$, each observation is assigned a
phase

$$
\phi_k = \frac{t_k - t_0}{P} - \left\lfloor \frac{t_k - t_0}{P} \right\rfloor \in [0, 1).
$$

Plotting brightness against $\phi_k$ folds all cycles onto one. The folded curve is then resampled onto a fixed grid.
In the UCR version of the OGLE data every curve has 1024 points and all curves share the same start and end
<Cite id="twomey2019" />. Choosing $t_0$ at a fixed feature, such as maximum brightness, aligns the phases of
different stars.

### The class model

For one class, let $\yvec_i \in \reals^{T}$ be curve $i$ on the common grid of $T = 1024$ inputs $\xvec$. The model is
the two-level hierarchy of Twomey et al. <Cite id="twomey2019" />:

$$
g(x) \sim \GP\paren{0, k_g(x, x')}, \qquad f_i(x) \sim \GP\paren{g(x), k_f(x, x')}, \qquad \yvec_i = f_i(\xvec) + \varepsilonvec_i, \quad \varepsilonvec_i \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \tau^{-1}\eye).
$$

The shared function $g$ is the prototypical light curve of the class. Each $f_i$ is one star's curve. It differs from
$g$ through properties of the star and through observing conditions such as the weather <Cite id="twomey2019" />.
Both covariance functions are Matérn kernels, chosen because the curves show both low-frequency oscillation and
high-frequency noise <Cite id="twomey2019" />. The hyperparameters are fitted by maximising the log marginal likelihood
of the stacked training curves with its analytic gradient.

### The anomaly score

A test curve $\yvec_*$ is scored by

$$
S(\yvec_*) = -\ln p(\yvec_* \mid \xvec_*, \thetavec),
$$

the negative log marginal likelihood under the class model with hyperparameters $\thetavec$ inferred from the
training curves <Cite id="twomey2019" />. Under the model a new star is a new replicate, so its curve is Gaussian with
mean equal to the posterior mean of $g$ and covariance equal to the posterior covariance of $g$ plus
$\Kmat_f + \tau^{-1}\eye$. The derivation and the reading of $S$ as a Mahalanobis distance are in
<NoteLink to="hgp-anomaly-detection">HGP anomaly detection</NoteLink>. The method is called HGPAD.

## Solution

### Periodic or non-periodic kernel

The curves are periodic, so a periodic covariance function on the shared level $k_g$ is the obvious candidate. It
buys little here. Each folded curve holds exactly one period, so there is no repetition within a curve for a periodic
kernel to exploit. Twomey et al. tried a periodic Matérn 3/2 kernel for $k_g$ <Cite id="twomey2019" />. The optimiser
usually settled on a short period that fits local wiggles rather than the one long cycle, with a lower log marginal
likelihood than the plain Matérn kernel. A solution with the correct period and a slightly higher marginal likelihood
existed, but only appeared after tens of random restarts and had implausibly tight error bars. The non-periodic Matérn
kernel was chosen because it is the one the optimiser reliably finds. The episode shows two properties of
<NoteLink to="gp-hyperparameter-learning">marginal-likelihood model selection</NoteLink>: a kernel that matches the
physics need not match the data layout, and the marginal likelihood has several local optima.

### Evaluation with planted anomalies

The anomaly labels are unknown, so the method is first checked on anomalies planted on purpose. CEPH and RRL curves are
the inliers and a few EB curves, whose flat-with-dips shape differs clearly, are mixed into a test set at about 10%.
With $n_a$ planted anomalies, precision is the fraction of anomalies among the $n_a$ highest-scoring curves (see
<NoteLink to="evaluating-anomaly-detectors">evaluating anomaly detectors</NoteLink>).

HGPAD reached a precision of 0.99 on the OGLE curves when trained on about seven curves, 1% of the available
training curves <Cite id="twomey2019" />. A <NoteLink to="k-means">$k$-means</NoteLink> detector with a cross-correlation
distance, designed for periodic light curves <Cite id="rebbapragada2009" />, found far fewer of the planted curves.
Two properties of the HGP explain the gap. The posterior over $g$ carries its own uncertainty, so a handful of training
curves does not overfit. The score uses the full covariance between phase points, so it weighs a departure by how
often class members show it, where a centroid distance weighs every departure equally.

The same protocol measures robustness to contaminated training data. A first model fitted to a small sample ranks a
validation set, and the fraction $Q$ with the lowest scores trains a second model. Precision stays high for small $Q$
and falls once $Q$ is large enough that anomalies enter the training set <Cite id="twomey2019" />.

### Unknown anomalies

Without labels, precision cannot be measured. Twomey et al. fit HGPAD to one class at a time, rank the held-out curves
of that class by score and inspect both ends of the ranking <Cite id="twomey2019" />. For each of CEPH, EB and RRL,
the five lowest-scoring curves closely match the inferred shared function $g$. The highest-scoring curves do not
resemble any of the prototypical curves and are likely to be real outliers. An expert then checks
them, and either assigns each to another existing class or recognises a new class of star <Cite id="twomey2019" />.

## Simulation

The figure builds a small synthetic version of the problem. Three classes of six stars each have templates shaped like a
Cepheid (a smoothed sawtooth), an RR Lyrae star (a steeper sawtooth) and an eclipsing binary (two dips half a period
apart). Every star adds its own smooth deviation, drawn from a Matérn 3/2 process, and noise, on 40 phase points. The
sixth Cepheid-like star is distorted by an amount $d$. Each star is scored leave-one-out: the class model is the HGP
posterior from the other five stars of its class, computed with the common-grid formulas.

At $d = 0$ the sixth star is an ordinary member and ranks tenth of 18. Blending it three quarters of the way toward
the eclipse shape, or adding a dip of three quarters of the full depth, moves it to the top of the ranking. A phase
shift of one eighth of a cycle ($d = 0.25$) puts it at the top at once, although its shape is unchanged. The
comparison is point by point in phase, so misalignment looks the same as a change of shape.

## What it illustrates

- **Replicates of a shared function.** Stars of one class are replicates of a class shape, which is the setting of the
  <NoteLink to="hierarchical-gaussian-process">hierarchical Gaussian process</NoteLink>. The model separates the class
  shape, the star-to-star variation and the measurement noise.
- **Likelihood as an anomaly score.** The score is a log predictive density, and on a common grid it ranks curves by a
  Mahalanobis distance from the class prediction (see <NoteLink to="hgp-anomaly-detection">HGP anomaly detection</NoteLink>).
- **Phase folding before comparison.** The comparison is point by point in phase, so period estimation with the
  <NoteLink to="lomb-scargle-periodogram">Lomb–Scargle periodogram</NoteLink> and a common epoch are prerequisites.
- **Kernel choice by marginal likelihood.** The periodic kernel matches the physics but not the data layout of one
  period per curve. The marginal likelihood prefers the non-periodic kernel at the optimum that is usually found (see
  <NoteLink to="covariance-functions">covariance functions</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="gp-hyperparameter-learning">hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>).
- **Evaluation with planted anomalies.** Precision in the top $n_a$ is measured by planting a known class as anomalies,
  and training contamination lowers it (see <NoteLink to="evaluating-anomaly-detectors">evaluating anomaly detectors</NoteLink>).

## Variations

- **Unaligned phases.** If the epochs $t_0$ differ between stars, identical shapes differ by a cyclic shift and the
  HGP scores them as anomalous. PCAD's phase search handles this case <Cite id="rebbapragada2009" />. With an HGP, the
  curves must be aligned first, for example by cross-correlation with the current estimate of $g$.
- **Irregular folded samples.** The HGP does not need a common grid. Each star can keep its own folded phases, at the
  cost of an $N \times N$ covariance over all observations instead of the common-grid shortcut.
- **Sub-classes.** A class with distinct sub-types, such as RR Lyrae stars pulsating in different modes, fits a deeper
  hierarchy with one function per sub-type between the class function and the stars <Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />.
- **Periodic kernel with more than one period.** A periodic kernel has more to gain when each series covers several
  cycles, as in unfolded survey data.
