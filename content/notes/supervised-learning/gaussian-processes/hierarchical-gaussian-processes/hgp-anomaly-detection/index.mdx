<Definition>
Hierarchical Gaussian process anomaly detection (HGPAD) finds whole time series that do not conform to a collection
<Cite id="twomey2019" />. It fits a <NoteLink to="hierarchical-gaussian-process">hierarchical Gaussian process</NoteLink>
to training series of one class, treating each series as a replicate of a shared latent function, with hyperparameters
$\thetavec$ set by maximum marginal likelihood. It scores a test series $\yvec_*$, observed at inputs $\xvec_*$, by
$S(\yvec_*) = -\ln p(\yvec_* \mid \xvec_*, \thetavec)$ under the fitted model, and ranks series by score.
</Definition>

## Problem

The task is to find anomalous series within a collection, not anomalous points within one series. Examples are a
light curve of a periodic variable star that has been assigned the wrong class <Cite id="twomey2019" />, or a heart
rate record unlike the others. Point-wise detectors such as <NoteLink to="forecast-residual-anomaly-detection">forecast-residual
detection</NoteLink> answer a different question. Distance-based methods compare a series with a few centroids and need
the number of centroids and a distance chosen in advance <Cite id="rebbapragada2009" />. A likelihood under a fitted
generative model is a natural score for whole series: a series that the model finds improbable is unlike the data the
model was fitted to <Cite id="chandola2009" />.

A single Gaussian process fitted to one series cannot represent a class. A Gaussian process fitted to the pooled points
of many series treats them as noisy samples of one function and attributes all between-series variation to noise. The
hierarchical Gaussian process separates the two: a shared function for the class shape, a deviation kernel for how
members of the class differ, and a noise level for measurement error.

## Mechanism

### 1. Fit a class model

Take $n$ training series of one class. Series $i$ has inputs $\xvec_i$ (times, or phases for folded periodic data) and
values $\yvec_i$. The model is

$$
g \sim \GP(0, k_g), \qquad f_i \mid g \sim \GP(g, k_f), \qquad y_{it} = f_i(x_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}, \quad \varepsilon_{it} \sim \Gauss(0, \tau^{-1}).
$$

The stacked training data $\hat{\yvec}$ have covariance $\Sigmamat$ with blocks
$\Kmat_g(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) + \delta_{ij}\paren{\Kmat_f(\xvec_i, \xvec_i) + \tau^{-1}\eye}$. The hyperparameters
$\thetavec$ of $k_g$, $k_f$ and $\tau$ maximise $\ln\Gauss(\hat{\yvec} \mid \zeros, \Sigmamat)$ by following its
analytic gradient <Cite id="twomey2019" /> (see <NoteLink to="gp-hyperparameter-learning">hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>).
Twomey et al. use Matérn kernels (the Matérn 3/2 form) for both $k_g$ and $k_f$, since light curves show both
low-frequency oscillation and high-frequency noise <Cite id="twomey2019" />.

### 2. Predictive density of a new series

A test series is a new replicate $f_*$ of the class, with its own deviation $h_* \sim \GP(0, k_f)$ independent of the
training data. Its observations $\yvec_* = g(\xvec_*) + h_*(\xvec_*) + \varepsilonvec_*$ are jointly Gaussian with
$\hat{\yvec}$, with

$$
\cov[\yvec_*, \hat{\yvec}] = \Kmat_g(\xvec_*, \hat{\xvec}), \qquad
\cov[\yvec_*] = \Kmat_g(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) + \Kmat_f(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) + \tau^{-1}\eye.
$$

The cross-covariance has no $k_f$ term because $h_*$ is independent of every training deviation. Gaussian conditioning
gives the predictive distribution $\yvec_* \mid \hat{\yvec} \sim \Gauss(\mvec_*, \Smat_*)$:

$$
\mvec_* = \Kmat_g(\xvec_*, \hat{\xvec})\,\Sigmamat^{-1}\hat{\yvec}, \qquad
\Smat_* = \underbrace{\Kmat_g(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) - \Kmat_g(\xvec_*, \hat{\xvec})\,\Sigmamat^{-1}\Kmat_g(\hat{\xvec}, \xvec_*)}_{\text{posterior covariance of } g} + \Kmat_f(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) + \tau^{-1}\eye.
$$

The mean is the posterior mean of the shared function. The covariance is the remaining uncertainty about $g$ plus the
spread of a class member around $g$ plus noise. As the number of training series grows, the first term shrinks and
$\Smat_*$ approaches $\Kmat_f + \tau^{-1}\eye$.

### 3. Score

The anomaly score is the negative log density of the whole test series <Cite id="twomey2019" />:

$$
S(\yvec_*) = -\ln p(\yvec_* \mid \xvec_*, \thetavec) = \tfrac12\, \rvec_*^\top\Smat_*^{-1}\rvec_* + \tfrac12\ln\abs{\Smat_*} + \tfrac{T_*}{2}\ln 2\pi, \qquad \rvec_* = \yvec_* - \mvec_*,
$$

where $T_*$ is the length of the test series. Twomey et al. write this as equations (13) and (14), with the
covariance parameters inferred from the training data, and call the method HGPAD <Cite id="twomey2019" />. The
earlier conference version builds its score from the same log marginal likelihood <Cite id="chen2018hgp" />.

The score has a direct reading. When every test series lies on the same inputs, $\ln\abs{\Smat_*}$ and the constant
are the same for all of them, and ranking by $S$ is ranking by the Mahalanobis distance $\rvec_*^\top\Smat_*^{-1}\rvec_*$
of the series from the class prediction (see
<NoteLink to="mahalanobis-distance-outliers">Mahalanobis-distance outliers</NoteLink>). The distance weights each
direction of departure by how much the class varies in that direction. A departure that class members commonly show,
such as a small smooth amplitude change, costs little. A departure in a direction of small predictive variance, such as a
sharp feature the class lacks, costs a lot. If $\yvec_*$ is drawn from the model, $\rvec_*^\top\Smat_*^{-1}\rvec_*$
follows a $\chi^2_{T_*}$ distribution, so $\expect[S] = \frac{T_*}{2}(1 + \ln 2\pi) + \frac12\ln\abs{\Smat_*}$. This
gives a reference level for the score of a typical member.

### 4. Rank and flag

Series are sorted by score. In an unsupervised setting the highest-scoring series are passed to an expert
<Cite id="twomey2019" />. When the number of true anomalies $n_a$ in a test set is known, Twomey et al. report
precision in the top $n_a$ ranked series <Cite id="twomey2019" />. This metric equals recall at the same cutoff (see
<NoteLink to="evaluating-anomaly-detectors">evaluating anomaly detectors</NoteLink>).

### Cleaning a contaminated training set

Anomalies in the training data widen the class model and lower the scores of similar test anomalies. Twomey et al.
chain two models to reduce this <Cite id="twomey2019" />. A first HGP is fitted to a small training set, about 1% of
the data. It scores a validation set. The fraction $Q$ of validation series with the lowest scores trains a second
HGP, which scores the test set. In their experiment, precision degrades once $Q$ rises above about 80%, which the
authors read as anomalies entering the training data <Cite id="twomey2019" />.

## Cost

Fitting costs $O(N^3)$ per evaluation of the marginal likelihood and its gradient, for $N$ stacked training points,
exactly as for a single GP on the pooled data. Scoring one test series of length $T_*$ costs $O(N^2T_* + T_*^3)$ with the
training factorisation reused. The cubic cost limited Twomey et al. to at most 10% of the OGLE light curves
<Cite id="twomey2019" />. When all series share one grid of $T$ inputs, the mean curve is sufficient for $g$ and both
fitting and scoring need only $T \times T$ factorisations (see the common-grid derivation in
<NoteLink to="hierarchical-gaussian-process">hierarchical Gaussian process</NoteLink>). Twomey et al. list low-rank
and inducing-point approximations as future work <Cite id="twomey2019" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Few parameters, no grid search.** The model has the hyperparameters of two kernels and a noise level, learned by
  gradient ascent on the marginal likelihood. Distance-based baselines need a number of centroids chosen by a
  criterion such as BIC <Cite id="twomey2019" />.
- **Small training sets.** The class model is a posterior with uncertainty, and the score accounts for it through
  $\Smat_*$. Twomey et al. report near-perfect precision from as few as seven training series
  <Cite id="twomey2019" />.
- **Alignment is assumed.** The model compares series input by input. Series that are phase-shifted or time-warped
  copies of the class shape receive high scores. Periodic data must be phase-folded to a common epoch first.
- **One model per class.** Scores from different class models are not on a common scale, since
  $\ln\abs{\Smat_*}$ differs between classes. Compare series within a class, or calibrate each class's scores
  separately.
- **Stationary kernels.** The kernels assume the same smoothness everywhere along the series. Twomey et al. argue this
  suits star light curves, which are stable processes, but note that sensor faults can need non-stationary kernels
  <Cite id="twomey2019" />.
