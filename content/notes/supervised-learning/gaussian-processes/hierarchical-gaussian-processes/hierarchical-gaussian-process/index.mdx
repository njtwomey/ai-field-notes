<Definition>
A hierarchical Gaussian process (HGP) models $n$ related functions $f_1, \dots, f_n$, called replicates, as
deviations from one shared function $g$ <Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />:

$$
g \sim \GP\paren{0, k_g}, \qquad f_i \mid g \sim \GP\paren{g, k_f}, \quad i = 1, \dots, n.
$$

The covariance function $k_g$ sets the prior on the shared function and $k_f$ sets the prior on each replicate's
deviation from it. Replicate $i$ is observed at inputs $x_{i1}, \dots, x_{iT_i}$ as
$y_{it} = f_i(x_{it}) + \varepsilon_{it}$, with independent noise $\varepsilon_{it} \sim \Gauss(0, \tau^{-1})$ of
precision $\tau$. The model is again a Gaussian process, indexed by the pair (replicate, input), with covariance
$k_g(x, x') + \delta_{ij}\, k_f(x, x')$ between $f_i(x)$ and $f_j(x')$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Repeated measurements of one process rarely coincide. Gene expression time courses from several biological
replicates follow one underlying profile, but each replicate is shifted and distorted a little
<Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />. Light curves of stars of one type follow one prototypical shape, and each star departs
from it through its own physical properties and the observing conditions <Cite id="twomey2019" />. Averaging the
replicates discards the replicate-specific structure and needs every replicate sampled at the same inputs. Fitting
each replicate alone wastes what the replicates share.

An HGP does both at once. The shared function $g$ carries what all replicates have in common. Each $f_i$ is $g$
plus a smooth deviation of its own. Data from every replicate inform $g$, and $g$ fills in each replicate wherever that
replicate has few or no observations. The balance is set by the two kernel variances: $\sigma_g^2$, the variance of
$g$, and $\sigma_f^2$, the variance of a replicate's deviation.

## The implied covariance

The conditional statement $f_i \mid g \sim \GP(g, k_f)$ says that $h_i = f_i - g$ is a zero-mean Gaussian process with
covariance $k_f$, independent of $g$ and of the other deviations. So each replicate is a sum of independent processes,

$$
f_i = g + h_i, \qquad g \sim \GP(0, k_g), \qquad h_i \sim \GP(0, k_f), \qquad g, h_1, \dots, h_n \text{ independent}.
$$

Every $f_i(x)$ is a linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables, so the collection $\set{f_i(x)}$ is jointly
Gaussian with mean zero. Its covariance follows from bilinearity:

$$
\cov[f_i(x), f_j(x')] = \cov[g(x), g(x')] + \cov[g(x), h_j(x')] + \cov[h_i(x), g(x')] + \cov[h_i(x), h_j(x')].
$$

The two middle terms are zero because $g$ is independent of every deviation. The last term is $k_f(x, x')$ when
$i = j$ and zero when $i \ne j$, because distinct deviations are independent. Therefore

$$
\cov[f_i(x), f_j(x')] = k_g(x, x') + \delta_{ij}\, k_f(x, x'),
$$

with $\delta_{ij} = 1$ when $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. Two points on the same replicate covary through both
kernels. Two points on different replicates covary only through the shared function. The same argument gives the
covariances with the shared function itself:

$$
\cov[f_i(x), g(x')] = k_g(x, x'), \qquad \cov[g(x), g(x')] = k_g(x, x').
$$

## The stacked data

Write $\xvec_i \in \reals^{T_i}$ and $\yvec_i \in \reals^{T_i}$ for the inputs and observations of replicate $i$, and
stack them as $\hat{\xvec} = (\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n)$ and $\hat{\yvec} = (\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n)$, vectors of
length $N = \sum_i T_i$. For two matrices of inputs write $\Kmat_g(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$ for the $T_i \times T_j$ matrix
with entries $k_g(x_{is}, x_{jt})$, and similarly $\Kmat_f$. Adding independent noise to the covariance above gives
$\hat{\yvec} \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat)$, where the block of $\Sigmamat$ for replicates $i$ and $j$ is

$$
\Sigmamat_{[i, j]} =
\begin{cases}
\Kmat_g(\xvec_i, \xvec_i) + \Kmat_f(\xvec_i, \xvec_i) + \tau^{-1}\eye & i = j, \\
\Kmat_g(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) & i \ne j.
\end{cases}
$$

This block structure is given by Hensman, Lawrence and Rattray <Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />. The diagonal blocks hold the
within-replicate covariance and the off-diagonal blocks hold the covariance shared through $g$. The replicates may be
observed at different inputs and in different numbers, since each block is evaluated at its own inputs. The log
marginal likelihood is the usual Gaussian process expression with $\Sigmamat$ in place of the Gram matrix plus noise:

$$
\ln p(\hat{\yvec} \mid \hat{\xvec}, \thetavec) = -\tfrac12\, \hat{\yvec}^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\hat{\yvec} - \tfrac12 \ln\abs{\Sigmamat} - \tfrac{N}{2}\ln 2\pi,
$$

where $\thetavec$ collects the hyperparameters of $k_g$, of $k_f$ and the noise precision $\tau$.

## Posterior over the shared and replicate functions

Prediction is Gaussian conditioning on $\hat{\yvec}$, exactly as in ordinary
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process regression</NoteLink>. Only the cross-covariances change. Let
$\kvec_g(x_*) \in \reals^N$ have entries $k_g(\hat{x}_m, x_*)$. For the shared function,
$\cov[\hat{\yvec}, g(x_*)] = \kvec_g(x_*)$, so

$$
\expect[g(x_*) \mid \hat{\yvec}] = \kvec_g(x_*)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\hat{\yvec}, \qquad
\var[g(x_*) \mid \hat{\yvec}] = k_g(x_*, x_*) - \kvec_g(x_*)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\kvec_g(x_*).
$$

For replicate $j$, the cross-covariance adds $k_f$ on the rows that belong to replicate $j$:
$\cvec_j(x_*) = \kvec_g(x_*) + \kvec_f^{(j)}(x_*)$, where $\kvec_f^{(j)}(x_*)$ has entries $k_f(\hat{x}_m, x_*)$ on
replicate $j$'s rows and zeros elsewhere. Then

$$
\expect[f_j(x_*) \mid \hat{\yvec}] = \cvec_j(x_*)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\hat{\yvec}, \qquad
\var[f_j(x_*) \mid \hat{\yvec}] = k_g(x_*, x_*) + k_f(x_*, x_*) - \cvec_j(x_*)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\cvec_j(x_*).
$$

One Cholesky factor of $\Sigmamat$ serves $g$ and all $n$ replicates.

### Shrinkage toward the shared function

The replicate posterior has a second form that shows how it borrows from the others. Condition first on $g$. Given
$g$, the data of replicate $j$ are $\yvec_j - g(\xvec_j) = h_j(\xvec_j) + \varepsilonvec_j$, and the other replicates
carry no information about $h_j$. Ordinary GP regression of $h_j$ on these residuals gives

$$
\expect[h_j(x_*) \mid \hat{\yvec}, g] = \kvec_f(x_*, \xvec_j)^\top\Amat_j^{-1}\paren{\yvec_j - g(\xvec_j)}, \qquad \Amat_j = \Kmat_f(\xvec_j, \xvec_j) + \tau^{-1}\eye.
$$

This is linear in $g$. Averaging over $g \mid \hat{\yvec}$ by the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total expectation</NoteLink> replaces $g$ with its posterior mean
$\hat g = \expect[g \mid \hat{\yvec}]$:

$$
\expect[f_j(x_*) \mid \hat{\yvec}] = \hat g(x_*) + \kvec_f(x_*, \xvec_j)^\top\Amat_j^{-1}\paren{\yvec_j - \hat g(\xvec_j)}.
$$

Each replicate's estimate is the shared estimate plus a GP smooth of that replicate's own residuals from it. Where
replicate $j$ has no nearby observations, $\kvec_f(x_*, \xvec_j) \approx \zeros$ and the estimate equals $\hat g$.
In the figure this happens on the right half of replicate 4.

### Worked example

Take $n = 3$ replicates, each observed once at the same input, with $y_1 = 1.0$, $y_2 = 1.4$, $y_3 = 0.4$. At one
input the kernels reduce to their variances: $\sigma_g^2 = 1$, $\sigma_f^2 = 0.25$ and $\tau^{-1} = 0.25$. The
covariance of $\yvec$ is $\sigma_g^2 \ones\ones^\top + (\sigma_f^2 + \tau^{-1})\eye$. Conditioning gives

$$
\hat g = \frac{n\sigma_g^2}{n\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_f^2 + \tau^{-1}}\, \bar y = \frac{3}{3.5} \times 0.9333 = 0.8000, \qquad
\var[g \mid \yvec] = \frac{\sigma_g^2 (\sigma_f^2 + \tau^{-1})}{n\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_f^2 + \tau^{-1}} = \frac{0.5}{3.5} = 0.1429,
$$

where $\bar y = 0.9333$ is the mean of the three observations. With one input, $\kvec_f^\top\Amat_j^{-1}$ is the
scalar $w = \sigma_f^2 / (\sigma_f^2 + \tau^{-1}) = 0.5$, so

$$
\hat f_j = (1 - w)\, \hat g + w\, y_j: \qquad \hat f_1 = 0.9, \quad \hat f_2 = 1.1, \quad \hat f_3 = 0.6.
$$

Each replicate moves halfway from its observation toward the shared estimate. A smaller deviation variance
$\sigma_f^2$ moves it further. With $\sigma_f^2 = 0$ all three estimates equal $\hat g$.

<Derivation title="Shrinkage by frequency when the kernels share a shape">
Suppose all replicates are observed on a common grid $\xvec$ of $T$ inputs and both kernels share one correlation
function $c$: $\Kmat_g = \sigma_g^2\Cmat$ and $\Kmat_f = \sigma_f^2\Cmat$, with eigendecomposition
$\Cmat = \Umat\Lambdamat\Umat^\top$. The smoother in the shrinkage form is

$$
\Kmat_f\Amat_j^{-1} = \sigma_f^2\Cmat\paren{\sigma_f^2\Cmat + \tau^{-1}\eye}^{-1} = \Umat\, \diag\paren{\frac{\sigma_f^2\lambda_m}{\sigma_f^2\lambda_m + \tau^{-1}}}\Umat^\top.
$$

Along eigenvector $m$ of $\Cmat$ the replicate keeps a fraction $\sigma_f^2\lambda_m / (\sigma_f^2\lambda_m + \tau^{-1})$
of its residual from $\hat g$ and gives the rest up to the shared estimate. For a smooth stationary kernel the large
eigenvalues belong to slowly varying eigenvectors. A replicate therefore keeps its own slow departures from $g$ and
is shrunk to $g$ in its fast, noise-dominated components. At one input $\Cmat = 1$ and the weight is the scalar $w$ of
the worked example.
</Derivation>

## Parameters and cost

An HGP with one kernel per level has the hyperparameters of two kernels and one noise precision. With squared
exponential or Matérn kernels these are $\sigma_g$, $\ell_g$, $\sigma_f$, $\ell_f$ and $\tau$: five numbers, against
three ($\sigma$, $\ell$, $\tau$) for a single Gaussian process. The count does not grow with the number of replicates,
because every replicate shares $k_f$. The hyperparameters are set by maximising the log marginal likelihood with its
analytic gradient, as for any Gaussian process (see
<NoteLink to="gp-hyperparameter-learning">hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>), so no grid search is needed
<Cite id="twomey2019" />.

Hensman, Lawrence and Rattray describe the model as having "only a few additional parameters over a regular GP" and
"negligible additional complexity" <Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />. The precise statement is this. Fitting an ordinary GP to
the $N$ pooled observations needs the Cholesky factor of an $N \times N$ matrix, at $O(N^3)$ time and $O(N^2)$ memory.
The HGP needs the Cholesky factor of $\Sigmamat$, which is also $N \times N$. Only the entries change: the diagonal
blocks add one evaluation of $k_f$ per entry. Posterior inference and optimisation use the same code as a standard
GP <Cite id="twomey2019" />. The HGP is more expensive than fitting $n$ separate GPs, one per replicate, which costs
$O(\sum_i T_i^3)$, because it couples the replicates.

<Derivation title="Replicates on a common grid: cost O(T³) instead of O((nT)³)">
When all $n$ replicates are observed on the same $T$ inputs, $\Sigmamat = \ones\ones^\top \otimes \Kmat_g + \eye_n \otimes \Amat$,
where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product and $\Amat = \Kmat_f + \tau^{-1}\eye_T$. Let $\Qmat$ be an $n \times n$
orthogonal matrix whose first column is $\ones/\sqrt{n}$. Then $\Qmat^\top\ones\ones^\top\Qmat = \diag(n, 0, \dots, 0)$
and

$$
(\Qmat \otimes \eye)^\top\Sigmamat(\Qmat \otimes \eye) = \diag\paren{n\Kmat_g + \Amat,\ \Amat,\ \dots,\ \Amat}.
$$

The rotated data are $\sqrt{n}\,\bar{\yvec}$, with $\bar{\yvec} = \frac1n\sum_i \yvec_i$, and $n - 1$ contrasts whose total
squared $\Amat^{-1}$-norm is $\sum_i \yvec_i^\top\Amat^{-1}\yvec_i - n\bar{\yvec}^\top\Amat^{-1}\bar{\yvec}$. Hence

$$
\ln\abs{\Sigmamat} = \ln\abs{n\Kmat_g + \Amat} + (n - 1)\ln\abs{\Amat},
$$

$$
\hat{\yvec}^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\hat{\yvec} = n\,\bar{\yvec}^\top\paren{n\Kmat_g + \Amat}^{-1}\bar{\yvec} + \sum_{i=1}^n \yvec_i^\top\Amat^{-1}\yvec_i - n\,\bar{\yvec}^\top\Amat^{-1}\bar{\yvec}.
$$

Two $T \times T$ factorisations replace one $nT \times nT$ factorisation. Only the first rotated component involves
$g$, so the mean curve $\bar{\yvec}$ is sufficient for $g$:

$$
g(\xvec) \mid \hat{\yvec} \sim \Gauss\paren{\Kmat_g\paren{\Kmat_g + \Amat/n}^{-1}\bar{\yvec},\ \Kmat_g - \Kmat_g\paren{\Kmat_g + \Amat/n}^{-1}\Kmat_g}.
$$

Hensman et al. derive the analogous identity for a cluster of genes measured at common times, which reduces the cost
from $O(N_i^3D^3)$ to $O(D^3)$ for $N_i$ genes on $D$ time points <Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />.
</Derivation>

## Deeper hierarchies

The construction nests. Each level's mean is the function one level up, and each level adds its own kernel
<Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />. For clusters of genes with replicates, cluster $c$ has a function $h_c$, gene $n$ in the
cluster has $g_n$, and replicate $r$ of that gene has $f_{nr}$:

$$
h_c \sim \GP(0, k_h), \qquad g_n \mid h_c \sim \GP(h_c, k_g), \qquad f_{nr} \mid g_n \sim \GP(g_n, k_f).
$$

The same decomposition into independent pieces gives

$$
\cov[f_{nr}(x), f_{n'r'}(x')] = k_h(x, x') + \delta_{nn'}\, k_g(x, x') + \delta_{nn'}\delta_{rr'}\, k_f(x, x')
$$

for two replicates in one cluster. The covariance of a cluster's data is block-structured at two levels. Hensman et al.
cluster genes by greedy agglomerative merging that maximises the summed log marginal likelihood of the clusters, and
re-optimise the hyperparameters between rounds <Cite id="hensman2013hgp" />. Each extra level adds one kernel's
hyperparameters.

## Relation to multi-output Gaussian processes

A multi-output Gaussian process models $n$ correlated functions jointly. The intrinsic coregionalisation model (ICM)
takes $\cov[f_i(x), f_j(x')] = B_{ij}\, c(x, x')$, with one correlation function $c$ and a positive semi-definite
$n \times n$ coregionalisation matrix $\Bmat$ <Cite id="bonilla2008" /> <Cite id="alvarez2012" />. The linear model of
coregionalisation (LMC) is a sum of such terms, $\sum_q B^{(q)}_{ij}\, c_q(x, x')$.

The HGP covariance is an LMC with two terms:

$$
k_g(x, x') + \delta_{ij}\, k_f(x, x') = [\ones\ones^\top]_{ij}\, k_g(x, x') + [\eye]_{ij}\, k_f(x, x').
$$

When the two kernels share one correlation function, $k_g = \sigma_g^2 c$ and $k_f = \sigma_f^2 c$, the two terms
merge into a single ICM:

$$
\cov[f_i(x), f_j(x')] = \paren{\sigma_g^2 + \delta_{ij}\sigma_f^2}\, c(x, x') = B_{ij}\, c(x, x'), \qquad \Bmat = \sigma_g^2\ones\ones^\top + \sigma_f^2\eye.
$$

The matrix $\Bmat$ has eigenvalue $\sigma_f^2 + n\sigma_g^2$ on $\ones$ and eigenvalue $\sigma_f^2$ on the
$(n-1)$-dimensional space orthogonal to $\ones$, so it is positive definite whenever $\sigma_f^2 > 0$. On a common
grid the stacked covariance is the Kronecker product $\Bmat \otimes \Cmat$. Conversely, an ICM whose $\Bmat$ has equal
diagonal entries $\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_f^2$ and equal off-diagonal entries $\sigma_g^2 \ge 0$ is an HGP with kernels of
shared shape.

The difference is in what is learned. A free-form $\Bmat$ has $n(n+1)/2$ parameters and a separate row for every
output. The HGP ties $\Bmat$ to two numbers, so the replicates are exchangeable. Its parameter count does not grow with
$n$, and a new replicate that was not in the training data has a known covariance with the others. This last property
is what lets an HGP score a new time series against a fitted population, as in
<NoteLink to="hgp-anomaly-detection">HGP anomaly detection</NoteLink>.

## Relation to hierarchical models in statistics

At a single input the HGP is the normal random-effects model of
<NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">hierarchical Bayesian statistics</NoteLink>: group values $f_i$ are drawn around a
population value $g$ with variance $\sigma_f^2$, and each is observed with noise $\tau^{-1}$. The worked example above is
partial pooling. Each estimate lies between the replicate's own observation (no pooling, $\sigma_f^2 \to \infty$) and
the shared estimate (complete pooling, $\sigma_f^2 = 0$) <Cite id="gelman2013" />. The HGP extends this from scalars to
functions. The pooling weight becomes the smoother $\Kmat_f\Amat_j^{-1}$, which pools by input location and, for a
shared kernel shape, by frequency.

## Assumptions

- **Exchangeable replicates.** Every replicate deviates from $g$ with the same kernel $k_f$. Replicates of different
  quality or scale need their own variances, or another level in the hierarchy.
- **Additive, aligned deviations.** A replicate differs from $g$ by an additive function of the same input. A
  replicate that is a time-shifted or time-warped copy of $g$ is not in the model and needs many deviation
  components to be represented.
- **Gaussian noise.** As in ordinary GP regression, the closed form needs a Gaussian likelihood.

## Pitfalls

- **Identifiability of the variance split.** With few replicates the marginal likelihood cannot separate $\sigma_g^2$
  from $\sigma_f^2$ well, and length-scales that are similar at the two levels trade off against each other. Report
  the fitted split with caution. Restart the optimiser from several initial values.
- **Cubic cost in the total number of points.** Stacking $n$ replicates of length $T$ gives an $nT \times nT$ matrix.
  Exploit a common grid (derivation above) or use
  <NoteLink to="sparse-gaussian-processes">sparse approximations</NoteLink> for large collections.
- **Shared function is not the mean curve.** $\hat g$ shrinks the average $\bar{\yvec}$ toward the prior mean zero by
  a factor near $n\sigma_g^2 / (n\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_f^2 + \tau^{-1})$. Centre the data, or add a mean function, when
  the curves share a large offset.
