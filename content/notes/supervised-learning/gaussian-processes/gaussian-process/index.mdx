<Definition>
A Gaussian process is a collection of <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $\set{f(\xvec) : \xvec \in \Xcal}$, indexed by an input set
$\Xcal$, such that every finite subset has a joint <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian distribution</NoteLink> <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />. A Gaussian process
is fixed by a mean function $m(\xvec) = \expect[f(\xvec)]$ and a covariance function
$k(\xvec, \xvec') = \cov[f(\xvec), f(\xvec')]$, and is written $f \sim \GP(m, k)$. For inputs
$\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_N$ the vector $\fvec = (f(\xvec_1), \dots, f(\xvec_N))$ is distributed as
$\Gauss(\mvec, \Kmat)$ with $m_i = m(\xvec_i)$ and $\Kmat_{ij} = k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A Gaussian process is a prior over functions that is stated one finite set of points at a time. The covariance
function says how strongly the values at two inputs move together. The squared exponential covariance

$$
k(x, x') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\paren{-\frac{(x - x')^2}{2\ell^2}}
$$

gives nearby inputs a correlation close to 1 and distant inputs a correlation close to 0. Here $\sigma_f^2$ is the
variance of $f(x)$ at every input and the length-scale $\ell$ is the distance over which the correlation decays. A
function drawn from this prior is smooth and wiggles on a scale of about $\ell$.

A sample from the prior on a plot is a draw of $\fvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat)$ at a few hundred grid inputs, drawn
as a line. Regression keeps the same prior and conditions it on the observed values. The functions consistent with
the data are pinned down near the observations and stay as uncertain as the prior far from them.

## The function-space view

A Gaussian process is well defined because the finite-dimensional distributions agree with each other. The
distribution of $(f(\xvec_1), f(\xvec_2))$ that the process specifies directly equals the marginal obtained from the
distribution of $(f(\xvec_1), f(\xvec_2), f(\xvec_3))$ by integrating out $f(\xvec_3)$. This holds because the marginal
of a <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> keeps the corresponding sub-vector
of the mean and sub-block of the covariance. By the Kolmogorov extension theorem, consistent finite-dimensional
distributions define a single process.

Every <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">Gram matrix</NoteLink> $\Kmat$ must be a valid covariance matrix, that is symmetric and
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive semi-definite</NoteLink>. A function $k$ with this property for
every finite set of inputs is a positive semi-definite kernel. The mean function is usually taken to be zero after
centring the targets. This note assumes $m = 0$ from here on.

The model makes no assumption about a parametric form for $f$. The number of quantities it tracks grows with the
number of observations, which is why Gaussian processes are called nonparametric.

## Regression with Gaussian noise

The data are $N$ inputs $\Xmat = (\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_N)$ and targets $\yvec \in \reals^N$ generated as

$$
y_i = f(\xvec_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad f \sim \GP(0, k), \qquad \varepsilon_i \iid \Gauss(0, \sigma_n^2),
$$

with the noise independent of $f$. The goal is the distribution of $\fvec_* = f(\Xmat_*)$ at $M$ test inputs
$\Xmat_*$. Write $\Kmat = k(\Xmat, \Xmat)$ for the $N \times N$ training Gram matrix, $\Kmat_* = k(\Xmat, \Xmat_*)$
for the $N \times M$ cross-covariance and $\Kmat_{**} = k(\Xmat_*, \Xmat_*)$ for the $M \times M$ test covariance.

### The joint distribution

The targets are the latent values plus independent noise, so $\cov[\yvec] = \Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye$ and
$\cov[\yvec, \fvec_*] = \cov[\fvec, \fvec_*] = \Kmat_*$. The pair $(\yvec, \fvec_*)$ is a linear function of the
jointly Gaussian vector $(\fvec, \fvec_*, \varepsilonvec)$, so it is jointly Gaussian:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \yvec \\ \fvec_* \end{bmatrix} \sim \Gauss\paren{\zeros,\
\begin{bmatrix} \Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye & \Kmat_* \\ \Kmat_*^\top & \Kmat_{**} \end{bmatrix}}.
$$

### Conditioning on the targets

Write $\Amat = \Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye$. For $\sigma_n^2 > 0$ the matrix $\Amat$ is positive definite and so invertible.
Define the residual vector

$$
\zvec = \fvec_* - \Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\yvec.
$$

The vector $\zvec$ is a linear function of $(\yvec, \fvec_*)$, so $(\zvec, \yvec)$ is jointly Gaussian. Its
cross-covariance with $\yvec$ is

$$
\cov[\zvec, \yvec] = \cov[\fvec_*, \yvec] - \Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\cov[\yvec, \yvec] = \Kmat_*^\top - \Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\Amat = \zeros.
$$

Jointly Gaussian vectors with zero cross-covariance are independent, so the distribution of $\zvec$ does not change
when $\yvec$ is observed. The mean of $\zvec$ is $\zeros$. Its covariance is

$$
\cov[\zvec] = \Kmat_{**} - 2\Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\Kmat_* + \Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\Amat\Amat^{-1}\Kmat_*
= \Kmat_{**} - \Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\Kmat_*.
$$

Given $\yvec$, the test values are $\fvec_* = \zvec + \Kmat_*^\top\Amat^{-1}\yvec$: an independent Gaussian plus a
known vector. The posterior is therefore

$$
\fvec_* \mid \yvec \sim \Gauss(\bar{\fvec}_*, \Sigmamat_*), \qquad
\bar{\fvec}_* = \Kmat_*^\top(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}\yvec, \qquad
\Sigmamat_* = \Kmat_{**} - \Kmat_*^\top(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}\Kmat_*.
$$

These are the Gaussian conditioning formulas, and $\Sigmamat_*$ is the
<NoteLink to="schur-complement">Schur complement</NoteLink> of $\Amat$ in the joint covariance
<Cite id="rasmussen2006" />. The predictive distribution of new noisy targets $\yvec_*$ adds the noise variance to the diagonal:
$\cov[\yvec_* \mid \yvec] = \Sigmamat_* + \sigma_n^2\eye$.

### Reading the posterior

For a single test input $\xvec_*$ with cross-covariance vector $\kvec_* = k(\Xmat, \xvec_*)$, the posterior mean is

$$
\bar f(\xvec_*) = \kvec_*^\top\alphavec = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_i\, k(\xvec_i, \xvec_*), \qquad \alphavec = (\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}\yvec.
$$

The mean is a weighted sum of $N$ kernel functions centred on the training inputs. It equals the prediction of
<NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge regression</NoteLink> with penalty $\lambda = \sigma_n^2$. The posterior variance

$$
\var[f(\xvec_*) \mid \yvec] = k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) - \kvec_*^\top(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}\kvec_*
$$

is the prior variance minus a non-negative term, because $(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}$ is positive definite. The
variance depends on the inputs but not on the targets $\yvec$. Far from every training input $\kvec_* \approx \zeros$,
so the mean returns to $0$ and the variance returns to the prior variance $k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*)$.

The posterior over functions is itself a Gaussian process, with mean function $\bar f$ and covariance function
$k(\xvec, \xvec') - k(\Xmat, \xvec)^\top(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}k(\Xmat, \xvec')$. Posterior samples on a grid
are draws from $\Gauss(\bar{\fvec}_*, \Sigmamat_*)$.

### Worked example

Take the <Gloss name="rbf-kernel">squared exponential kernel</Gloss> with $\sigma_f = 1$ and $\ell = 1$, noise variance $\sigma_n^2 = 0.1$, and two
observations $y = 1$ at $x = 0$ and $y = 0.5$ at $x = 1$. The training Gram matrix has off-diagonal entry
$e^{-1/2} = 0.6065$:

$$
\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye = \begin{bmatrix} 1.1 & 0.6065 \\ 0.6065 & 1.1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\alphavec = (\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}\yvec = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9461 \\ -0.0671 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

At $x_* = 0.5$ both training inputs are at distance $0.5$, so $\kvec_* = (0.8825, 0.8825)$. The posterior mean is
$0.8825 \times (0.9461 - 0.0671) = 0.7757$. The posterior variance is $1 - \kvec_*^\top(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}\kvec_* = 0.0873$,
a standard deviation of $0.295$. At $x_* = 3$, $\kvec_* = (0.0111, 0.1353)$, the posterior mean is $0.0014$ and the
variance is $0.978$, close to the prior variance of 1.

## Computation

The standard implementation factorises $\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$ by
<NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky decomposition</NoteLink> once, in $O(N^3)$ time and $O(N^2)$ memory
<Cite id="rasmussen2006" />. Then $\alphavec = \Lmat^{-\top}\Lmat^{-1}\yvec$ costs two triangular solves. Each
prediction costs $O(N)$ for the mean and $O(N^2)$ for the variance, through $\vvec = \Lmat^{-1}\kvec_*$ and
$\var = k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) - \vvec^\top\vvec$. The same factor gives the log <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>, which is used to set
the kernel's hyperparameters $\ell$, $\sigma_f$ and $\sigma_n$ (see
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>). The cubic cost limits exact inference to
tens of thousands of points. <NoteLink to="sparse-gaussian-processes">Sparse Gaussian processes</NoteLink> based on
inducing points reduce it to $O(NM^2)$ for $M$ inducing points.

## Assumptions

- **Gaussian noise.** The closed form needs a Gaussian likelihood. Classification and count data need approximate
  inference, as in <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification">Gaussian process classification</NoteLink>.
- **The kernel encodes the prior.** The <NoteLink to="covariance-functions">covariance function</NoteLink> sets smoothness, length-scale, periodicity and stationarity. A kernel
  that is wrong for the data gives confident, wrong predictions between the observations.
- **Fixed hyperparameters.** The formulas treat $\ell$, $\sigma_f$ and $\sigma_n$ as known. In practice they are
  estimated by maximising the marginal likelihood, and that uncertainty is not reflected in the posterior variance.

## Pitfalls

- **Numerical conditioning.** With small noise and a long length-scale, $\Kmat$ is nearly singular. Add a small jitter
  (about $10^{-6}\sigma_f^2$) to the diagonal before factorising, and never form $(\Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)^{-1}$
  explicitly.
- **Latent versus predictive variance.** The band for $f(\xvec_*)$ excludes the noise. The band for a new observation
  $y_*$ adds $\sigma_n^2$ and is wider. Mixing them up gives intervals that are too narrow or too wide.
- **Reversion to the mean far from data.** Away from the observations the mean decays to the prior mean, here zero.
  For extrapolating a trend, a zero-mean stationary kernel is a poor prior; add a linear or polynomial mean or kernel.
- **Unscaled inputs.** A single length-scale treats every input dimension alike. Standardise the inputs, or give each
  dimension its own length-scale by
  <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">automatic relevance determination</NoteLink>.
