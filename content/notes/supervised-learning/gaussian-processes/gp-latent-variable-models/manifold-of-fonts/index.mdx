<Definition>
Given $M = 46$ ordinary font files, build a continuous space of fonts in which every point generates a complete,
plausible typeface, the existing fonts sit at particular points, and moving between points changes the typeface
smoothly <Cite id="campbell2014" />. The space must be learned without supervision, from the fonts alone, with no
typographer's labels. It must also support editing: when a user drags part of one character, the other characters
should change to stay consistent with the edit.
</Definition>

## Setup

Campbell and Kautz's approach has two stages <Cite id="campbell2014" />. The first turns every font into a vector of the
same length whose entries mean the same thing in every font. The second fits a
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">Gaussian process latent variable model</NoteLink> (GP-LVM) to
those vectors.

<FontPipeline />

### The fonts

The 46 fonts are the TrueType files of a Windows 7 installation after removing non-Latin and symbol fonts and a few
fonts, such as handwriting faces, whose letters have inconsistent topology <Cite id="campbell2014" />. They span
humanist, garalde, didone, slab, grotesque and glyphic designs, with about as many serif as sans serif fonts. The
characters are the lower-case and upper-case letters and the digits, $H = 62$ in all.

### Dense correspondence between fonts

A generative model needs a universal parameterisation: a vector that contains everything needed to draw each character
and whose elements correspond across fonts. Then a small step in the model's space makes a small change to the glyphs.
Font files do not provide this. Two fonts draw the same letter with different numbers of <NoteLink to="bezier-curves">Bézier curves</NoteLink>, starting at
different places. Adobe Multiple Master fonts have the property by design, because a designer draws every master of a
glyph with control points in exact correspondence. Campbell and Kautz find the correspondence automatically for fonts
that were not designed that way.

A glyph outline is a closed curve $\uvec(t) = (x(t), y(t))$ with $t \in [0, 1]$. Each outline is densely sampled at
$N = 512$ points to give a polyline. The polylines are normalised to zero mean and unit variance, and the offset and
scale are kept for later. Characters with several outlines, such as "o" with its inner and outer contours, are matched
outline by outline after finding which outline of one font corresponds to which of another by the smallest squared
distance over all permutations. The starting point of each polyline is aligned by the cyclic shift that minimises the
summed squared distance between samples. Pairwise shifts are propagated over a minimum spanning tree of the fonts.

### The matching energy

The matching then moves the $N$ sample parameters $t_{i,m}$ of every font $m$ along its own outline, jointly for all
$M$ fonts, so that sample $i$ lands on the same feature in every font. With $\kappa_{i,m}$ the curvature of font $m$'s
outline at $t_{i,m}$ and $\bar\kappa_i$ its mean over fonts, the energy is

$$
E(\tvec) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\LB 1 - \exp\paren{-\frac{1}{2M\gamma}\sum_{m=1}^{M}(\kappa_{i,m} - \bar\kappa_i)^2}\RB +
\lambda_{\text{el}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\LB(t_{i+1,m} - t_{i,m}) - \frac1N\RB^2 + \lambda_\eta E_\eta,
$$

with indices taken modulo $N$ around the closed curve. The first term makes curvature consistent across fonts at each
sample. The exponential caps the cost of the very large curvature at sharp corners. The second, elastic, term keeps
samples spread evenly unless curvature pulls them together. $E_\eta$ matches outward normals near the top and bottom of
the glyph, which lets serifs of serif fonts match the plain terminals of sans serif fonts. The constraint
$t_{i+1,m} - t_{i,m} \ge 0$ keeps the samples in order. The problem is a constrained smooth optimisation over all
fonts' sample positions at once <Cite id="campbell2014" />.

Minimised directly, the energy falls into poor local minima in which sharp concavities are out of correspondence. The
remedy is coarse to fine. Each outline is written as an elliptic <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink>, a sum of harmonics in $t$. With only
the lowest harmonics, different fonts' versions of a character look alike even when their full outlines differ, and
matching is easy. The optimisation is run first on a low-pass version of the outlines and then repeated with more and
more harmonics, each run starting from the last.

### A font as one vector

After matching, sample $i$ of each outline corresponds across all fonts. Concatenating the $(x, y)$ coordinates of every
sample of every outline of every character gives one vector $\uvec_m$ per font. With $2N$ numbers per outline, $H = 62$
characters and some characters having several outlines, the dimension is $D \ge 2NH = 63{,}488$. The saved offsets and
scales from normalisation and the font metrics (horizontal and vertical advance) are appended, so they are interpolated
with the shapes. Subtracting the mean over fonts gives

$$
\yvec_m = \uvec_m - \bar\uvec, \qquad \bar\uvec = \frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\uvec_m.
$$

The mean vector $\bar\uvec$ is itself a font, the average of the 46. It lies between sans and serif, because the
training set mixes the two about equally.

## Solution

### The model

The $M = 46$ vectors $\yvec_m$ are the rows of $\Ymat$. The model is the GP-LVM with a <Gloss name="rbf-kernel">radial basis function kernel</Gloss> with
two hyperparameters, a variance and a width, and Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ <Cite id="campbell2014" />:

$$
p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat, \thetavec) = \prod_{d=1}^{D}\Gauss\paren{\yvec_{:,d} \mid \zeros, \Cmat(\Xmat, \Xmat \mid
\thetavec) + \sigma^2\eye_M}.
$$

The kernel matrix is $M \times M$: it couples the fonts, and the $D$ coordinates are independent given $\Xmat$. The
latent points $\Xmat$ and the hyperparameters $\thetavec$
maximise the log-likelihood jointly, by <Gloss name="cg">conjugate gradients</Gloss>. The latent points start at the <Gloss name="pca" /> projection of $\Ymat$.
The hyperparameters have a wide prior $\log\thetavec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$ and start at its mean. The fitted noise
variance was very small, which the authors read as evidence that the outlines do lie near a low-dimensional manifold.

The dimension of the data is large and the number of fonts small, which suits the GP-LVM. The likelihood depends on
the data only through the $46 \times 46$ matrix $\Ymat\Ymat^\top$, so the cost of each iteration does not grow with
the 63,488 coordinates.

### Generating a font

A latent point $\hat\xvec$ generates the posterior mean of the <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss>,

$$
\hat\yvec = \Cmat(\hat\xvec, \Xmat^*)\,\Cmat(\Xmat^*, \Xmat^*)^{-1}\,\Ymat,
$$

where $\Xmat^*$ are the fitted latent points. The inverse is computed once, and each new font costs one row of kernel
evaluations and a product. Adding $\bar\uvec$ back gives the outline vector $\hat\uvec = \hat\yvec + \bar\uvec$, from
which every character is drawn. The noise term is used in training only. Generation takes milliseconds, which is what makes an
interactive viewer possible <Cite id="campbell2014project" />.

Far from all training fonts, every kernel value is near zero, so $\hat\yvec \to \zeros$ and the manifold returns the
average font. The posterior variance, $c(\hat\xvec, \hat\xvec) - \Cmat(\hat\xvec, \Xmat^*)\Cmat(\Xmat^*,
\Xmat^*)^{-1}\Cmat(\Xmat^*, \hat\xvec)$, grows over the same regions. The paper shows it as a heatmap behind the latent
points, as an indication of where good fonts are likely: low variance near the fonts, where the model interpolates,
and high variance where it extrapolates.

### What the two-dimensional manifold shows

For display the latent space has $Q = 2$ dimensions. A straight line across it, from a sans region to a serif region,
generates a sequence of fonts in which serifs grow gradually, with no abrupt change between neighbouring points. Over
the manifold the authors observe smooth variation in the presence of serifs, in stroke contrast, thickness and angle,
and in aspect ratio, since glyphs are normalised to a fixed x-height <Cite id="campbell2014" />.

Two dimensions are too few. The Bayesian GP-LVM with an <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">automatic relevance determination (ARD) kernel</NoteLink>, run with a larger $Q$, keeps four latent dimensions
for the joint manifold of all 62 characters <Cite id="campbell2014" /> <Cite id="titsias2010" />. Squeezing a
four-dimensional manifold into two splits it into separate islands, which is visible in the 2D plots. Manifolds of
single characters can differ: the character "g" alone needs three dimensions. See
<NoteLink to="bayesian-gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">Bayesian GP-LVM</NoteLink> for how the dimension is chosen.

### Editing a font through the manifold

A generated font can be pulled back onto the manifold from any target outline. Starting from a latent point
$\tilde\xvec$, the generated outline $\tilde\yvec(\tilde\xvec)$ is compared with the target by the chamfer distance,
and $\tilde\xvec$ is moved down the numerically computed <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of that distance. The search is run from several
starting points, such as the training fonts' latent points, because the distance has local minima on the manifold
<Cite id="campbell2014" />.

Editing is this projection applied in small steps. The user grabs a point on the outline of one character and drags
it. Each small movement of the pointer sets targets for the outline vertices nearest the cursor. A gradient step on
the latent point reduces the distance between those vertices and the targets, and the new latent point regenerates the
whole font. Because every character is generated from the same latent point, the edit propagates. Making the base of a
"2" thinner makes the other digits thinner while keeping their stroke contrast. Dragging a serif of an "m" wider widens
the serifs of every character. The outline cannot leave the manifold: dragging beyond what the fonts support stops the
outline, and the variance heatmap shows the user approaching the edge.

## Simulation

The figure applies the same two stages to real fonts, on a smaller scale.

**Fonts.** The training set has 66 font instances drawn from 23 families in the Google Fonts repository, all under the
SIL Open Font License or the Apache License <Cite id="googlefonts2026" />. The families span grotesque and geometric
sans, humanist sans, serif, didone, slab serif and monospace designs. Variable fonts are instanced at several weights,
and some at a condensed width or in italic, so one family contributes up to five instances. EB Garamond is dropped
because its "P" has an open bowl and therefore one contour where the other fonts have two.

**Characters.** Each font contributes 23 capitals: every letter except K, Q and W, whose contour counts or shapes
differ between designs. Every font is scaled so that its "H" is 700 units tall.

**Correspondence.** The pipeline is a simplified version of the paper's stage 1.

1. Each Bézier outline is flattened to a polyline.
2. Outer contours are turned counter-clockwise and counters clockwise.
3. Counters are ordered by matching their centroids to a reference font's.
4. Each contour is resampled at 400 points by arc length and rotated to the cyclic shift that best matches the
   reference.
5. Every font is aligned to a template by <NoteLink to="dynamic-time-warping">dynamic time warping</NoteLink>
   <Cite id="sakoe1978" />. The matching features are position and unit tangent, with italics de-slanted, and a
   penalty on uneven stretching plays the role of the elastic term.
6. The alignment runs coarse to fine: once on heavily smoothed outlines, then again on lightly smoothed ones within
   a band around the coarse match.
7. The template is re-estimated as the mean aligned outline, and steps 5 and 6 are repeated three times.
8. The output samples are placed along the template. Every place where at least three fonts have a corner gets its own
   sample, and each font's corner is moved onto that sample. The remaining samples are spread along the outline,
   denser on curves.

The displayed word gets the densest sampling, 260 points around the "M". Concatenating every sample of every
contour of every character, and then the 23 advance widths, gives each font a vector of $D = 4{,}479$ integers.

**Fitting.** The vectors are centred and divided by one common scale. The 2-D GP-LVM then starts from the PCA
projection and takes 300 Adam steps on the log posterior. The steps run a few at a time between animation frames, so
the latent points can be watched moving into place. The cost of a step depends on the $66 \times 66$ matrix
$\Ymat\Ymat^\top$, not on $D$, and the whole fit takes about half a second on a laptop. Changing the set of fonts
refits the model from scratch.

<FontManifold />

The first latent dimension runs from serif designs to sans designs, with the slab serifs between them. The didones form
a group of their own, and so do the monospaced fonts. The instances of one family sit close together, and weight
varies within a family's small region of the latent space. Between training fonts the word changes gradually. Far
from every font the mean returns to the average font, a medium-weight serif, and the standard deviation approaches
its prior value.

Dragging the foot serif of the "P" edits the whole alphabet. In the training fonts a longer foot serif comes with
heavier strokes. Pulling the tip of a Baskerville serif outward therefore moves the latent point towards the bold and
black serif fonts, and every letter thickens, although only one point of one letter was dragged. Pushing the tip
inward moves towards the light serifs. In a sans font the same sample is the corner of the stem, and dragging it
outward thickens the stem and makes the whole font bolder. The tip stops following the pointer where the manifold has
no font with a longer or shorter serif.

The mean of a Gaussian process is a weighted sum of the training fonts in which some weights are negative. Near the
highest-contrast didones, those weights can push the two edges of a hairline stroke past each other. The hairline then
thins to nothing or crosses itself. The GP-LVM has no notion of a valid outline, so nothing in the model prevents this.

## Evidence

The paper's evidence is visual: the manifold, typeset samples across it, interpolation between fonts, the average font
and editing sequences <Cite id="campbell2014" />. Interpolation through matched outlines stays smooth where
interpolating signed distance transforms breaks topology. The paper reports no user study and no quantitative measure
of the quality of generated fonts.

## What it illustrates

- **The GP-LVM as a generative model for very high-dimensional data with few examples.** With $M = 46$ observations
  of dimension $D \ge 63{,}488$, the $M \times M$ form of the likelihood makes fitting fast. See
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">GP-LVM</NoteLink>.
- **Representation before modelling.** The GP-LVM interpolates vectors entry by entry. Without correspondence the
  interpolation of two fonts would blend unrelated parts of their outlines.
- **Predictive variance as a guide.** The Gaussian process variance marks where generated fonts are supported by data.
- **Choosing the latent dimension** with the <NoteLink to="bayesian-gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">Bayesian GP-LVM</NoteLink>.
- **Editing by projection onto a manifold.** A constraint on one part of the output moves the latent point, and the
  generative map fills in the rest.

## Limitations

- **Fixed topology.** Matching assumes the same number of outlines, in one-to-one correspondence, for a character in
  every font. Alternate forms such as the two-storey and single-storey "a" and "g" are treated as separate glyphs. A
  font missing a glyph can still be embedded with a GP-LVM that handles missing data, which also predicts the missing
  character.
- **Outlying glyphs.** A character far from every other font's version, such as a "J" with a long top stroke, can be
  matched wrongly. The error corrupts a small region of the manifold, usually near its edge.
- **No kerning or hinting.** Generated fonts carry neither. Kerning tables could be appended to the vector. Hinting is
  harder.
- **No typographic structure.** The method never identifies strokes, serifs or other parts. Everything it knows comes
  through the example fonts, which may limit extrapolation to new designs.

## Variations

- **More fonts or more characters.** The cost of fitting grows with the number of fonts $M$ as $O(M^3)$, and with the
  dimension $D$ only through $\Ymat\Ymat^\top$.
- **Single-character manifolds.** Fitting one character alone frees it from consistency with the others, which the
  authors suggest for logo design. Low-likelihood regions of such manifolds can produce artefacts.
- **A raster representation** avoids correspondence but gives the interpolation failures described above.
