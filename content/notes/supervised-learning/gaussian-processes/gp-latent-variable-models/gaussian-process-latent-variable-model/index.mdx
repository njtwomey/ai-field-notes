<Definition>
The Gaussian process latent variable model (GP-LVM) explains $N$ observations $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_N \in \reals^D$,
stacked as the rows of $\Ymat \in \reals^{N \times D}$, through $N$ unobserved latent points $\xvec_n \in \reals^Q$ with
$Q < D$, stacked as $\Xmat \in \reals^{N \times Q}$ <Cite id="lawrence2004" /> <Cite id="lawrence2005" />. Each output
dimension $d$ is an independent draw $f_d \sim \GP(0, k)$ evaluated at the latent points, plus Gaussian noise:
$y_{nd} = f_d(\xvec_n) + \varepsilon_{nd}$ with $\varepsilon_{nd} \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. Integrating out the
functions gives

$$
p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat, \thetavec) = \prod_{d=1}^{D} \Gauss\paren{\yvec_{:,d} \mid \zeros, \Kmat}, \qquad
\Kmat = k(\Xmat, \Xmat) + \sigma^2\eye_N,
$$

where $\yvec_{:,d} \in \reals^N$ is column $d$ of $\Ymat$ and $\thetavec$ holds the kernel hyperparameters and
$\sigma^2$. The latent points and $\thetavec$ are fitted by maximising this likelihood, usually with a standard normal
prior on each $\xvec_n$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process regression</Gloss> model maps known inputs to observed outputs. The GP-LVM keeps the model and drops the
inputs. It treats each observation as the output of an unknown smooth function at an unknown input, and searches for the
inputs that make the observations most probable under a Gaussian process prior on the function. The inputs found are a
low-dimensional embedding of the data. The fitted Gaussian process is a generative map back out: any point in the
latent space produces a predicted observation and an uncertainty.

The model is the dual of <NoteLink to="probabilistic-principal-component-analysis">probabilistic PCA</NoteLink>. Probabilistic PCA integrates
out the latent points and optimises the <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink>. The GP-LVM integrates out the map and optimises the latent points.
With a linear kernel both land on the principal subspace. Replacing the linear kernel by a nonlinear one gives <NoteLink to="dimensionality-reduction">nonlinear
dimensionality reduction</NoteLink> with the same likelihood.

## From probabilistic PCA to its dual

Probabilistic PCA models a centred observation as $\yvec_n = \Wmat\xvec_n + \epsilonvec_n$, with a $D \times Q$
matrix $\Wmat$, $\xvec_n \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye_Q)$ and $\epsilonvec_n \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye_D)$
<Cite id="tipping1999" />. Tipping and Bishop integrate out each $\xvec_n$ and optimise $\Wmat$.

Lawrence reverses the roles <Cite id="lawrence2005" />. Write $\wvec_d \in \reals^Q$ for row $d$ of $\Wmat$ and put
the prior $\wvec_d \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye_Q)$ independently for $d = 1, \dots, D$. Column $d$ of the data is then

$$
\yvec_{:,d} = \Xmat\wvec_d + \epsilonvec_{:,d},
$$

a linear function of the Gaussian vector $\wvec_d$ plus independent Gaussian noise. It is Gaussian with mean $\zeros$
and covariance $\Xmat\,\expect[\wvec_d\wvec_d^\top]\,\Xmat^\top + \sigma^2\eye_N = \Xmat\Xmat^\top + \sigma^2\eye_N$.
Different columns depend on different $\wvec_d$ and different noise, so they are independent:

$$
p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat) = \prod_{d=1}^{D}\Gauss\paren{\yvec_{:,d} \mid \zeros,\ \Xmat\Xmat^\top + \sigma^2\eye_N}.
$$

This is a product of $D$ Gaussian process <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihoods</NoteLink>, one per output dimension, all with the linear kernel
$k(\xvec, \xvec') = \xvec^\top\xvec'$ evaluated at the same inputs $\Xmat$. Lawrence calls it dual probabilistic PCA.
The covariance is now over the $N$ observations, not over the $D$ features: $\Kmat$ is $N \times N$.

## The linear kernel recovers PCA

With $\Kmat = \Xmat\Xmat^\top + \sigma^2\eye_N$ the log-likelihood is

$$
\Lcal(\Xmat) = -\frac{DN}{2}\log 2\pi - \frac D2\log\abs{\Kmat} - \frac12\trace\paren{\Kmat^{-1}\Ymat\Ymat^\top}.
$$

**<NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> with respect to $\Kmat$.** The identities $\partial\log\abs{\Kmat} = \trace(\Kmat^{-1}\partial\Kmat)$ and
$\partial\Kmat^{-1} = -\Kmat^{-1}(\partial\Kmat)\Kmat^{-1}$ (see
<NoteLink to="matrix-calculus-identities">matrix calculus identities</NoteLink>) give
$\partial\Lcal = \trace(\Gmat\,\partial\Kmat)$ with the symmetric $N \times N$ matrix

$$
\Gmat = \frac12\paren{\Kmat^{-1}\Ymat\Ymat^\top\Kmat^{-1} - D\,\Kmat^{-1}}.
$$

This expression holds for every kernel. Only $\partial\Kmat$ depends on the kernel.

**Stationary points.** For the linear kernel $\partial\Kmat = (\partial\Xmat)\Xmat^\top + \Xmat(\partial\Xmat)^\top$,
so $\partial\Lcal = 2\trace(\Xmat^\top\Gmat\,\partial\Xmat)$ and $\partial\Lcal/\partial\Xmat = 2\Gmat\Xmat$. Setting
it to zero and multiplying by $\Kmat/D$ on the left gives

$$
\frac1D\Ymat\Ymat^\top\Kmat^{-1}\Xmat = \Xmat.
$$

Write the singular value decomposition $\Xmat = \Umat\Lmat\Vmat^\top$, with $\Umat \in \reals^{N \times Q}$ having
orthonormal columns $\uvec_q$, $\Lmat = \diag(l_1, \dots, l_Q)$ and $\Vmat$ a $Q \times Q$ rotation. Then
$\Kmat = \Umat\Lmat^2\Umat^\top + \sigma^2\eye$, so $\Kmat\Umat = \Umat(\Lmat^2 + \sigma^2\eye)$ and
$\Kmat^{-1}\Umat = \Umat(\Lmat^2 + \sigma^2\eye)^{-1}$. Substituting and multiplying on the right by $\Vmat$,

$$
\frac1D\Ymat\Ymat^\top\Umat\,(\Lmat^2 + \sigma^2\eye)^{-1}\Lmat = \Umat\Lmat.
$$

Column $q$ with $l_q \ne 0$ reads $\frac1D\Ymat\Ymat^\top\uvec_q = (l_q^2 + \sigma^2)\uvec_q$. Each $\uvec_q$ is an
eigenvector of $\Smat = \frac1D\Ymat\Ymat^\top$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_q = l_q^2 + \sigma^2$, so
$l_q = \sqrt{\lambda_q - \sigma^2}$, which requires $\lambda_q > \sigma^2$. Every stationary point is

$$
\Xmat = \Umat_Q\paren{\Lambdamat_Q - \sigma^2\eye}^{1/2}\Vmat,
$$

where $\Umat_Q$ holds $Q$ eigenvectors of $\Smat$ and $\Lambdamat_Q$ their eigenvalues. This is the same equation as
for probabilistic PCA, with $\Smat$ now $N \times N$ and $\Xmat$ in the place of $\Wmat$. The same argument as Tipping and
Bishop's shows that the maximum keeps the $Q$ largest eigenvalues <Cite id="tipping1999" /> <Cite id="lawrence2005" />.

**Why this is <Gloss name="pca" />.** Take the thin <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">SVD</NoteLink>
$\Ymat = \Pmat\,\diag(s_1, s_2, \dots)\,\Rmat^\top$. Then $\Ymat\Ymat^\top = \Pmat\,\diag(s_1^2, s_2^2, \dots)\,\Pmat^\top$,
so the eigenvectors of $\Smat$ are the left singular vectors $\pvec_q$ and $\lambda_q = s_q^2/D$. The PCA scores of the
data, its projections onto the top $Q$ principal directions, are $\Ymat\Rmat_Q = \Pmat_Q\,\diag(s_1, \dots, s_Q)$. The
maximum-likelihood latent points are $\Pmat_Q\,\diag\paren{\sqrt{s_q^2/D - \sigma^2}}\,\Vmat$: the PCA scores with
column $q$ rescaled by $\sqrt{1/D - \sigma^2/s_q^2}$, up to a rotation. A linear GP-LVM is PCA.

## Nonlinear kernels

Any <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss> kernel can replace $\xvec^\top\xvec'$. The usual choice is the squared exponential

$$
k(\xvec, \xvec') = \sigma_f^2\exp\paren{-\frac{\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2}{2\ell^2}},
$$

with signal variance $\sigma_f^2$ and length-scale $\ell$ (see <NoteLink to="covariance-functions">covariance
functions</NoteLink>). The likelihood keeps the same form, $\Lcal = -\frac D2\log\abs{\Kmat} -
\frac12\trace(\Kmat^{-1}\Ymat\Ymat^\top) + \text{const}$, but it is no longer solved by an eigendecomposition. The
latent points are found by gradient ascent.

**Gradient in the latent points.** The <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> through $\Gmat$ gives
$\partial\Lcal/\partial x_{nq} = \trace(\Gmat\,\partial\Kmat/\partial x_{nq})$. Only row and column $n$ of $\Kmat$
depend on $\xvec_n$, and for the squared exponential $\partial k(\xvec_n, \xvec_m)/\partial\xvec_n =
-k(\xvec_n, \xvec_m)(\xvec_n - \xvec_m)/\ell^2$. Counting the row and the column,

$$
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\xvec_n} = -\frac{2}{\ell^2}\sum_{m=1}^{N} G_{nm}\,k(\xvec_n, \xvec_m)\,(\xvec_n - \xvec_m).
$$

The hyperparameter gradients follow from the same $\Gmat$, as in
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">GP hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>. The latent points and the
hyperparameters are optimised jointly <Cite id="lawrence2005" />.

**A prior on the latent points.** The likelihood with a stationary kernel depends on $\Xmat$ only through distances
divided by $\ell$. Scaling $\Xmat$ and $\ell$ together leaves it unchanged, and so does any rotation or translation of
$\Xmat$. Lawrence removes the scale redundancy with the prior $p(\Xmat) = \prod_n \Gauss(\xvec_n \mid \zeros, \eye_Q)$,
which adds $-\frac12\sum_n\norm{\xvec_n}^2$ to the objective and $-\xvec_n$ to its gradient <Cite id="lawrence2005" />.
The fit is then a <Gloss name="map-estimate">maximum a posteriori</Gloss> estimate of $\Xmat$.

**Cost.** The data enter only through the $N \times N$ matrix $\Ymat\Ymat^\top$, computed once. Each evaluation costs
$O(N^3)$ for the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink> of $\Kmat$ and $O(N^2 Q)$ for the gradient, independently of $D$. This makes the
GP-LVM cheap for very high-dimensional data with few observations, such as images or glyph outlines with tens of
thousands of coordinates. For large $N$, the <NoteLink to="sparse-gaussian-processes">sparse approximations</NoteLink>
reduce the cost to $O(NM^2)$ with $M$ inducing points.

**Initialisation.** The objective is not concave in $\Xmat$ and has many local optima. The standard start is the PCA
solution, which is the optimum of the linear model <Cite id="lawrence2005" />.

## Generating data from the latent space

After fitting, the latent points $\Xmat$ and the hyperparameters define an ordinary Gaussian process regression from
$\reals^Q$ to $\reals^D$. For a new latent point $\xvec_*$, write $\kvec_* = k(\Xmat, \xvec_*) \in \reals^N$. Every
output dimension shares the same inputs and kernel, so the prediction is

$$
p(\fvec_* \mid \xvec_*, \Xmat, \Ymat) = \Gauss\paren{\Ymat^\top\Kmat^{-1}\kvec_*,\ \paren{k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) -
\kvec_*^\top\Kmat^{-1}\kvec_*}\eye_D},
$$

where $\fvec_* \in \reals^D$ is the noise-free output. The mean is a weighted combination of the training observations
with weights $\Kmat^{-1}\kvec_*$. The variance is one number for all $D$ outputs. It is near zero close to the latent
points and rises to $\sigma_f^2$ far from them, where the mean returns to zero, the data mean. The heatmap in the figure
shows its square root. Plots of this variance behind the latent points are the standard way to display a GP-LVM
<Cite id="lawrence2005" />.

The map runs one way only. There is no formula for the latent point of a new observation $\yvec_*$. Finding it means
maximising $p(\yvec_* \mid \xvec_*)$ over $\xvec_*$ numerically, with several starting points because the objective is
multimodal. The <NoteLink to="back-constrained-gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">back-constrained GP-LVM</NoteLink> adds an explicit map from data
to latent space.

## What the embedding preserves

The GP-LVM keeps points apart that are far apart in the data. It does not keep together points that are close in the
data <Cite id="lawrence2006" />. The reason is that the mapping runs from the latent space to the data.

Take two latent points $\xvec_i$ and $\xvec_j$ at distance $r = \norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}$. Under the prior, the pair
$(f_d(\xvec_i), f_d(\xvec_j))$ is bivariate normal, each with variance $\sigma_f^2$ and covariance
$k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$. Their difference is normal with mean zero and variance

$$
\var\LB f_d(\xvec_i) - f_d(\xvec_j)\RB = 2\sigma_f^2 - 2k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)
= 2\sigma_f^2\paren{1 - e^{-r^2/2\ell^2}} \le \sigma_f^2\,\frac{r^2}{\ell^2},
$$

using $1 - e^{-u} \le u$. Summed over the $D$ independent output dimensions,

$$
\expect\LB\norm{\fvec(\xvec_i) - \fvec(\xvec_j)}^2\RB \le D\,\sigma_f^2\,\frac{r^2}{\ell^2}.
$$

Latent points close together must produce outputs close together. If two observations are far apart in the data but
their latent points are close, the likelihood of that pair is small. Maximising the likelihood therefore pushes such
points apart: the GP-LVM preserves dissimilarity.

The converse does not hold. When $r \gg \ell$, $k(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) \approx 0$ and the two outputs are independent draws
with the same distribution. Two identical observations can sit at distant latent points at almost no cost in
likelihood, because a Gaussian process can take the same value at two unrelated inputs. Nothing in the objective
keeps neighbours in the data together.

In the figure, PCA flattens the curve onto its two directions of largest variance, where it crosses
itself. The GP-LVM separates the two branches at the crossing, which are far apart in the four-dimensional data. To
do so it tears the loop: some consecutive points on the curve, neighbours in the data, end up far apart in the latent
space. Lawrence and Quiñonero-Candela report the same tearing on a motion-capture sequence, where the unconstrained
model splits a periodic run into several sub-sequences <Cite id="lawrence2006" />.

<Derivation title="The likelihood as a divergence between Gaussians">
Let $\Smat = \frac1D\Ymat\Ymat^\top$ and assume it is nonsingular. The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL
divergence</NoteLink> between two zero-mean Gaussians on $\reals^N$ is

$$
\KL\paren{\Gauss(\zeros, \Smat) \,\|\, \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat)} = \frac12\LB\trace\paren{\Kmat^{-1}\Smat} - N +
\log\abs{\Kmat} - \log\abs{\Smat}\RB.
$$

The GP-LVM log-likelihood is $\Lcal = -\frac D2\LB\log\abs{\Kmat} + \trace(\Kmat^{-1}\Smat)\RB - \frac{DN}{2}\log
2\pi$. Multiplying the divergence by $-D$ reproduces the terms of $\Lcal$ that involve $\Xmat$ and $\thetavec$, so

$$
\Lcal = -D\,\KL\paren{\Gauss(\zeros, \Smat) \,\|\, \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat)} + \text{const}.
$$

Fitting a GP-LVM matches the kernel matrix of the latent points to the matrix of <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink> between observations,
in the sense of this divergence <Cite id="lawrence2005" />. Classical <NoteLink to="multidimensional-scaling">
multidimensional scaling</NoteLink> matches the same two matrices by least squares on their entries. The KL divergence
weights the entries through $\Kmat^{-1}$ instead.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

- **Independent output dimensions with one shared kernel.** Every column of $\Ymat$ is a draw from the same Gaussian
  process. Output dimensions on very different scales should be standardised first, or given separate scales.
- **Isotropic Gaussian noise.** The noise variance $\sigma^2$ is the same for every observation and every dimension.
- **Centred data.** The prior mean of each $f_d$ is zero, so $\Ymat$ is centred before fitting and the mean is added
  back to predictions.
- **A fixed latent dimension $Q$.** The maximum-likelihood GP-LVM cannot choose $Q$ itself. A larger $Q$ always fits at
  least as well. The <NoteLink to="bayesian-gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">Bayesian GP-LVM</NoteLink> infers it.

## Pitfalls

- **Local optima.** Different initialisations give different embeddings with different likelihoods. Start from PCA,
  and compare restarts by their final objective.
- **Torn manifolds.** Neighbours in the data can be placed far apart, as derived above. Back constraints, or a dynamics
  prior on sequences (see <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-dynamical-models">Gaussian process dynamical
  models</NoteLink>), keep them together.
- **Overfitting with many latent dimensions.** Each observation has its own free latent point, so the number of
  parameters grows as $NQ$. With large $Q$ the model can place points to fit noise. On the Frey faces data, the <Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean
  absolute error</Gloss> of a standard GP-LVM in reconstructing missing pixels was 9.73 with $Q = 5$ and 19.69 with $Q = 10$
  <Cite id="titsias2010" />.
- **Axes carry no meaning.** A stationary kernel makes the likelihood invariant to rotations and translations of
  $\Xmat$. Only relative positions are identified.
- **No encoder.** Embedding a new observation needs an optimisation, not a function evaluation.
