<Definition>
The Bayesian Gaussian process latent variable model places the prior $p(\Xmat) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}\Gauss(\xvec_n \mid
\zeros, \eye_Q)$ on the latent points of a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">GP-LVM</NoteLink> and
integrates them out instead of optimising them <Cite id="titsias2010" />. The marginal likelihood
$p(\Ymat) = \int p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat)\,p(\Xmat)\,d\Xmat$ is intractable, so it is replaced by a lower bound

$$
\log p(\Ymat) \ge \sum_{d=1}^{D}\tilde\Fcal_d(q) - \KL\paren{q(\Xmat) \,\|\, p(\Xmat)}, \qquad
q(\Xmat) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}\Gauss(\xvec_n \mid \muvec_n, \Smat_n),
$$

with diagonal $\Smat_n$. Each $\tilde\Fcal_d$ is a closed-form bound that uses $M$ inducing inputs $\Zmat$. The bound
is maximised over the variational parameters $\set{\muvec_n, \Smat_n}$, the inducing inputs $\Zmat$, the kernel
hyperparameters and the noise precision $\beta$.
</Definition>

## Why integrate out the latent points

The standard GP-LVM fits one free latent point per observation by maximum likelihood. Two problems follow. The model
can overfit: with more latent dimensions it always fits at least as well, so the likelihood cannot choose the latent
dimension $Q$. And the fit returns a point, with no measure of how well each latent position is determined.

A Bayesian treatment fixes both. Integrating out $\Xmat$ gives the marginal likelihood $p(\Ymat)$, which penalises
unused flexibility. The approximate posterior $q(\Xmat)$ gives each latent point a mean and a variance. Combined with
the automatic relevance determination (ARD) kernel

$$
k(\xvec, \xvec') = \sigma_f^2\exp\paren{-\frac12\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\alpha_q(x_q - x_q')^2},
$$

with one inverse squared length-scale $\alpha_q \ge 0$ per latent dimension, the bound can switch dimensions off by
driving $\alpha_q$ to zero. Setting $Q$ generously and reading off the surviving $\alpha_q$ estimates the latent
dimensionality <Cite id="titsias2010" />.

## Why the integral is hard

The GP-LVM likelihood of column $d$ is $p(\yvec_{:,d} \mid \Xmat) = \Gauss(\yvec_{:,d} \mid \zeros, \Kmat_{NN} +
\beta^{-1}\eye_N)$, where $\Kmat_{NN} = k(\Xmat, \Xmat)$ and $\beta = 1/\sigma^2$ is the noise precision. Jensen's
inequality with any $q(\Xmat)$ gives the standard <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink>

$$
\log p(\Ymat) \ge \int q(\Xmat)\log p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat)\,d\Xmat - \KL\paren{q(\Xmat) \,\|\, p(\Xmat)}.
$$

The KL term between two Gaussians is available in closed form. The first term is not. The latent points sit inside
$(\Kmat_{NN} + \beta^{-1}\eye)^{-1}$ and $\log\abs{\Kmat_{NN} + \beta^{-1}\eye}$, and no Gaussian average of these
has a closed form.

## The variational bound

Titsias and Lawrence remove the inverse with the inducing-variable construction of
<NoteLink to="sparse-gaussian-processes">variational sparse Gaussian processes</NoteLink> <Cite id="titsias2009" />.

**Augment the model.** For each output dimension, introduce the latent function values $\fvec_d = f_d(\Xmat) \in
\reals^N$ and $M$ inducing values $\uvec_d = f_d(\Zmat) \in \reals^M$ at inducing inputs $\Zmat \in \reals^{M \times
Q}$. The Gaussian process prior factorises as $p(\fvec_d, \uvec_d) = p(\fvec_d \mid \uvec_d, \Xmat)\,p(\uvec_d)$ with

$$
p(\uvec_d) = \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat_{MM}), \qquad
p(\fvec_d \mid \uvec_d, \Xmat) = \Gauss\paren{\Kmat_{NM}\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\uvec_d,\ \Kmat_{NN} -
\Kmat_{NM}\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\Kmat_{MN}},
$$

where $\Kmat_{MM} = k(\Zmat, \Zmat)$ and $\Kmat_{NM} = k(\Xmat, \Zmat)$. Integrating out $\fvec_d$ and $\uvec_d$
returns the original likelihood for any $\Zmat$, so $\Zmat$ is a variational parameter, not part of the model.

<AugmentedModel />

**Bound the conditional likelihood.** Choose the variational distribution $q(\fvec_d, \uvec_d) = p(\fvec_d \mid
\uvec_d, \Xmat)\,\phi(\uvec_d)$. The conditional prior cancels inside the logarithm, and Jensen's inequality gives

$$
\log p(\yvec_{:,d} \mid \Xmat) \ge \int \phi(\uvec_d)\log\frac{\Gauss(\yvec_{:,d} \mid \Kmat_{NM}\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\uvec_d,
\beta^{-1}\eye)\,p(\uvec_d)}{\phi(\uvec_d)}\,d\uvec_d - \frac\beta2\trace\paren{\Kmat_{NN} - \Kmat_{NM}\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}
\Kmat_{MN}}.
$$

The trace term is the expected squared deviation of $\fvec_d$ from its conditional mean, scaled by $\beta/2$. The
latent points now appear only in $\Kmat_{NN}$ and $\Kmat_{NM}$, outside any inverse.

**Average over $q(\Xmat)$.** Take $\phi(\uvec_d)$ independent of $\Xmat$. Then the integrals over $\uvec_d$ and $\Xmat$
can be swapped. Expanding the Gaussian log density,

$$
\expect_{q(\Xmat)}\LB\log\Gauss(\yvec_{:,d} \mid \Kmat_{NM}\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\uvec_d, \beta^{-1}\eye)\RB
= \frac N2\log\frac{\beta}{2\pi} - \frac\beta2\LB\yvec_{:,d}^\top\yvec_{:,d} - 2\yvec_{:,d}^\top\Psimat_1\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}
\uvec_d + \uvec_d^\top\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\Psimat_2\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\uvec_d\RB,
$$

and the trace term averages to $\frac\beta2\LB\psi_0 - \trace(\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\Psimat_2)\RB$. Three expectations under
$q(\Xmat)$ carry all the dependence on the latent points:

$$
\psi_0 = \trace\,\expect_q\LB\Kmat_{NN}\RB, \qquad \Psimat_1 = \expect_q\LB\Kmat_{NM}\RB, \qquad
\Psimat_2 = \expect_q\LB\Kmat_{MN}\Kmat_{NM}\RB.
$$

$\Psimat_1$ is $N \times M$ and $\Psimat_2$ is $M \times M$. They are called the $\Psi$ statistics.

**Optimise $\phi(\uvec_d)$.** The averaged log density is quadratic in $\uvec_d$. The best $\phi$ is proportional to
$\exp(\expect_q[\log\Gauss(\cdot)])\,p(\uvec_d)$, a Gaussian, and the bound at that $\phi$ is the logarithm of a
Gaussian integral. Evaluating it gives <Cite id="titsias2010" />

$$
\tilde\Fcal_d = \log\LB\frac{\beta^{N/2}\,\abs{\Kmat_{MM}}^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{N/2}\,\abs{\beta\Psimat_2 +
\Kmat_{MM}}^{1/2}}\exp\paren{-\frac12\yvec_{:,d}^\top\Wmat\yvec_{:,d}}\RB - \frac{\beta\psi_0}{2} +
\frac\beta2\trace\paren{\Kmat_{MM}^{-1}\Psimat_2},
$$

with $\Wmat = \beta\eye_N - \beta^2\Psimat_1(\beta\Psimat_2 + \Kmat_{MM})^{-1}\Psimat_1^\top$. This is the sparse
Gaussian process bound of Titsias with $\trace\Kmat_{NN}$, $\Kmat_{NM}$ and $\Kmat_{MN}\Kmat_{NM}$ replaced by their
averages under $q(\Xmat)$. The full objective is $\sum_d\tilde\Fcal_d - \KL(q \,\|\, p)$, with

$$
\KL\paren{q(\Xmat) \,\|\, p(\Xmat)} = \frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\paren{\mu_{nq}^2 + S_{nq} - \log S_{nq} - 1}.
$$

The data enter through $\yvec_{:,d}^\top\Wmat\yvec_{:,d}$ summed over $d$, which is $\trace(\Wmat\Ymat\Ymat^\top)$. The
cost is $O(NM^2Q)$ per evaluation and linear in $D$ through a single precomputation, as for the ordinary GP-LVM. The
full derivation, with dynamical priors on $\Xmat$ and uncertain inputs, is in Damianou, Titsias and Lawrence
<Cite id="damianou2016" />.

## The Ψ statistics

The $\Psi$ statistics are averages of the kernel over Gaussian inputs. Each decomposes over data points:
$\Psimat_2 = \sum_n\Psimat_2^{(n)}$ with $(\Psimat_2^{(n)})_{mm'} = \expect_{q(\xvec_n)}[k(\xvec_n,
\zvec_m)k(\zvec_{m'}, \xvec_n)]$. For the ARD squared exponential kernel they are closed-form
<Cite id="titsias2010" />. With $\mu_{nq}$ and $S_{nq}$ the mean and variance of $q(x_{nq})$,

$$
\psi_0 = N\sigma_f^2, \qquad
(\Psimat_1)_{nm} = \sigma_f^2\prod_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{\exp\paren{-\frac{\alpha_q(\mu_{nq} - z_{mq})^2}{2(\alpha_q S_{nq} +
1)}}}{\sqrt{\alpha_q S_{nq} + 1}},
$$

$$
(\Psimat_2^{(n)})_{mm'} = \sigma_f^4\prod_{q=1}^{Q}\frac{\exp\paren{-\frac{\alpha_q(z_{mq} - z_{m'q})^2}{4} -
\frac{\alpha_q(\mu_{nq} - \bar z_q)^2}{2\alpha_q S_{nq} + 1}}}{\sqrt{2\alpha_q S_{nq} + 1}}, \qquad
\bar z_q = \frac{z_{mq} + z_{m'q}}{2}.
$$

<Derivation title="Deriving Ψ₁">
Consider one latent dimension and drop the indices $n$, $m$ and $q$. With $x \sim \Gauss(\mu, S)$ and $\delta = \mu -
z$,

$$
\expect\LB e^{-\alpha(x - z)^2/2}\RB = \int\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi S}}e^{-(x - \mu)^2/2S}\,e^{-\alpha(x - z)^2/2}\,dx.
$$

The exponent is quadratic in $x$. Completing the square, the product of the two Gaussian shapes in $x$ is proportional
to a Gaussian with precision $1/S + \alpha$, times $\exp\paren{-\frac{\delta^2}{2(S + 1/\alpha)}}$. The integral of the
normalised part is 1, and the prefactors combine to $\sqrt{(1/S)/(1/S + \alpha)} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha S + 1}$. Since
$\delta^2/(2(S + 1/\alpha)) = \alpha\delta^2/(2(\alpha S + 1))$,

$$
\expect\LB e^{-\alpha(x - z)^2/2}\RB = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha S + 1}}\exp\paren{-\frac{\alpha(\mu - z)^2}{2(\alpha S +
1)}}.
$$

The ARD kernel is a product over dimensions and $q(\xvec_n)$ factorises over dimensions, so the expectation is the
product of these one-dimensional factors times $\sigma_f^2$. $\Psimat_2$ follows the same way, since
$k(\xvec, \zvec_m)k(\xvec, \zvec_{m'})$ is again a Gaussian shape in $\xvec$, centred at $\bar\zvec$ with twice the
precision. The identity $(x - z_m)^2 + (x - z_{m'})^2 = 2(x - \bar z)^2 + (z_m - z_{m'})^2/2$ gives the extra factor
$\exp(-\alpha(z_m - z_{m'})^2/4)$.
</Derivation>

## Choosing the latent dimension

With $\alpha_q = 0$, latent dimension $q$ drops out of the kernel, and every factor for $q$ in the $\Psi$ statistics
equals 1. The expected log-likelihood then does not depend on $\mu_{nq}$ or $S_{nq}$. The only term left that does is
the KL divergence, which is minimised at $\mu_{nq} = 0$ and $S_{nq} = 1$, where dimension $q$'s contribution to the
KL is zero. A dimension is kept only when the gain in expected log-likelihood from using it exceeds the KL cost of
moving $q(x_{nq})$ away from the prior. In the maximum-likelihood GP-LVM there is no such cost, so extra dimensions
are used to fit noise.

Titsias and Lawrence fitted the Bayesian GP-LVM with $Q = 10$ and 50 inducing variables to 1000 twelve-dimensional
points of the multi-phase oil-flow data, with three classes of flow <Cite id="titsias2010" />. The bound switched off
7 of the 10 latent dimensions by setting their inverse length-scales to zero. In the two dominant dimensions, a nearest
neighbour classifier made 3 errors on the 1000 points. A standard sparse GP-LVM with $Q = 2$ made 26.

Campbell and Kautz used the same test on font outlines. The Bayesian GP-LVM gave four dimensions for the joint manifold
of all characters and three for the character "g" alone <Cite id="campbell2014" />. The
<NoteLink to="manifold-of-fonts">manifold of fonts</NoteLink> note describes that application.

## Prediction and missing data

A new observation $\yvec_*$ gets its own variational distribution $q(\xvec_*)$, found by maximising the bound on
$p(\Ymat, \yvec_*)$ with the training quantities held fixed. Because the $\Psi$ statistics decompose over points, only
the terms for $\xvec_*$ are new. When $\yvec_*$ is partly observed, the bound uses the observed coordinates to fit
$q(\xvec_*)$ and the model predicts the rest. On the Frey faces data, with half of each test image's pixels missing,
the mean absolute reconstruction error was 7.40 for the Bayesian GP-LVM trained once with 30 latent dimensions. The
best standard sparse GP-LVM, with 5 latent dimensions, had 9.73 <Cite id="titsias2010" />.

## Assumptions

- **A factorised Gaussian posterior.** $q(\Xmat)$ is independent across points, and each $\Smat_n$ is diagonal. The
  true posterior over latent points is multimodal (the likelihood is invariant to rotations and reflections of the
  latent space), and the bound picks one mode.
- **Kernels with tractable $\Psi$ statistics.** The ARD squared exponential and the linear kernels have closed forms.
  Other kernels need numerical integration.
- **A standard normal prior on each latent point.** Structured priors, such as a Gaussian process over time for
  sequences, fit the same framework <Cite id="damianou2016" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading relevance off $\alpha_q$.** The inverse length-scales are not always driven exactly to zero. On Frey faces
  many became small without vanishing <Cite id="titsias2010" />. A threshold on $\alpha_q$ is a judgement.
- **Initialisation still matters.** Means are usually initialised by PCA and variances near 0.5, as in the original
  experiments. The bound is non-convex.
- **The bound is a bound.** Comparing models by their bounds assumes the gap to $\log p(\Ymat)$ is similar across
  them.
