<Definition>
A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> dynamical model (GPDM) describes a sequence of observations $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_T \in \reals^D$
through latent states $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_T \in \reals^Q$ <Cite id="wang2008" />:

$$
\xvec_t = f(\xvec_{t-1}) + \nvec_{x,t}, \qquad \yvec_t = g(\xvec_t) + \nvec_{y,t},
$$

with zero-mean Gaussian noise $\nvec_{x,t}$ and $\nvec_{y,t}$. Each output of the transition function $f$ and of the
observation map $g$ has a Gaussian process prior, with kernels $k_X$ and $k_Y$. Both functions are integrated out. The
joint density $p(\Ymat, \Xmat) = p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat)\,p(\Xmat)$ is then available in closed form and is maximised over
the latent trajectory $\Xmat$ and the hyperparameters.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">GP-LVM</NoteLink> fitted to frames of a video or a
motion-capture sequence ignores their order. Consecutive frames can land far apart in the latent space, because the
GP-LVM does not keep neighbours together. The GPDM adds a prior over latent trajectories that prefers each state to be
a smooth function of the previous one. The fitted latent space then contains a path for the sequence, and running the
learned dynamics forward generates new sequences.

The model is a nonlinear <NoteLink to="state-space-representation">state-space model</NoteLink> in which neither the
transition nor the emission has a parametric form. It is the GP-LVM idea applied twice.

## The observation term

The observation map is the GP-LVM mapping. Stack the observations as the rows of $\Ymat \in \reals^{T \times D}$ and the
states as the rows of $\Xmat \in \reals^{T \times Q}$. With $\Kmat_Y = k_Y(\Xmat, \Xmat)$, including the noise
variance on the diagonal,

$$
p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat) = \prod_{d=1}^{D}\Gauss(\yvec_{:,d} \mid \zeros, \Kmat_Y).
$$

Wang, Fleet and Hertzmann also scale each output dimension $d$ by a learned weight $w_d$, collected in a diagonal matrix
$\Wmat$, so that data dimensions with different variances share one kernel <Cite id="wang2008" />.

## The dynamics prior

Write the transition as a linear combination of <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis functions</NoteLink>, $\xvec_t = \Amat^\top\phivec(\xvec_{t-1}) +
\nvec_{x,t}$, with a Gaussian prior on each column of $\Amat$. The same argument that turns <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>
into a Gaussian process applies. Collect the targets $\Xmat_{2:T} = (\xvec_2, \dots, \xvec_T)^\top$ and the inputs
$\Xmat_{1:T-1} = (\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_{T-1})^\top$. Latent dimension $q$ of the targets is a linear function of one
Gaussian column of $\Amat$ plus independent noise, so it is Gaussian with covariance
$\Kmat_X = k_X(\Xmat_{1:T-1}, \Xmat_{1:T-1})$, a $(T-1) \times (T-1)$ matrix with the noise on its diagonal. The $Q$
latent dimensions are independent given the inputs. With a Gaussian prior on the first state,

$$
p(\Xmat) = p(\xvec_1)\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{(T-1)Q}\abs{\Kmat_X}^Q}}
\exp\paren{-\frac12\trace\paren{\Kmat_X^{-1}\Xmat_{2:T}\Xmat_{2:T}^\top}}.
$$

This density is not Gaussian in $\Xmat$. Each state appears twice: as a target in $\Xmat_{2:T}$ and as an input inside
$\Kmat_X$. The density is high when every state is well predicted from the previous one by a smooth function.

**The dynamics kernel.** A linear kernel $k_X(\xvec, \xvec') = \alpha_1\xvec^\top\xvec' + \alpha_2^{-1}\delta_{\xvec,
\xvec'}$ gives a distribution over linear <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive models</Gloss>. Wang, Fleet and Hertzmann found that it could not
synthesise good walking, because ground contacts make the dynamics nonlinear <Cite id="wang2008" />. They use a linear
plus <Gloss name="rbf-kernel" />,

$$
k_X(\xvec, \xvec') = \alpha_1\exp\paren{-\frac{\alpha_2}{2}\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2} + \alpha_3\xvec^\top\xvec' +
\alpha_4^{-1}\delta_{\xvec, \xvec'}.
$$

The RBF term models nonlinear dynamics. The linear term lets predictions far from the training states fall back to
linear dynamics. $\alpha_4^{-1}$ is the variance of the process noise.

## Learning

The unknowns are the latent trajectory $\Xmat$, the kernel hyperparameters of both maps and the weights $\Wmat$. The
natural estimate maximises the joint posterior, that is, minimises

$$
-\log p(\Xmat, \bar\alphavec, \bar\betavec, \Wmat \mid \Ymat) = \Lcal_Y + \Lcal_X + \text{priors} + \text{const},
$$

where $\Lcal_Y = \frac D2\log\abs{\Kmat_Y} + \frac12\trace(\Kmat_Y^{-1}\Ymat\Wmat^2\Ymat^\top) - T\log\abs{\Wmat}$ comes
from the observation term, $\Lcal_X = \frac Q2\log\abs{\Kmat_X} + \frac12\trace(\Kmat_X^{-1}\Xmat_{2:T}\Xmat_{2:T}^\top)$
from the dynamics (plus the prior on $\xvec_1$), and $\bar\alphavec$ and $\bar\betavec$ are the two sets of kernel
hyperparameters. The priors on the hyperparameters are proportional to $\prod_i\alpha_i^{-1}$ and
$\prod_i\beta_i^{-1}$ <Cite id="wang2008" />.

**Balancing the two terms.** $\Lcal_Y$ sums over $D$ data dimensions and $\Lcal_X$ over $Q$ latent dimensions. With
$D \gg Q$ the observation term dominates, and the <Gloss name="map-estimate">maximum a posteriori</Gloss> trajectories can be as ragged as a GP-LVM
embedding. The balanced GPDM, proposed by Urtasun and colleagues, multiplies $\Lcal_X$ by $D/Q$ <Cite id="wang2008" />. Wang, Fleet and Hertzmann report
much smoother latent trajectories and better simulated walks with it, and note that the weighting has no probabilistic
justification. They also study fixing the hyperparameters by hand and a two-stage MAP procedure.

## Experiments on human motion

The data are motion-capture sequences from the Carnegie Mellon University database. Each pose is 50 numbers: 44 Euler
angles for the joints, three global torso angles and three global torso translational velocities <Cite id="wang2008" />.
The data are mean-subtracted, with no time warping. A model of two gait cycles from one subject (frames 1 to 260 of one
sequence, downsampled by a factor of 2) uses a three-dimensional latent space and <Gloss name="pca" /> initialisation. Its MAP latent
trajectory is much smoother than that of a three-dimensional GP-LVM on the same frames. Samples drawn from the learned
dynamics follow the training trajectories. A sample about four gait cycles long stays smooth and looks like a limit
cycle. With only the linear term in the dynamics kernel, samples drift away from the training data and long
trajectories are pulled toward the origin. A MAP model of four different walkers, in contrast, has small clusters of
latent positions separated by large jumps, which motivates the reweighting above. Further models cover four golf swings
by one golfer, and the models fill in missing frames of new sequences.

## Extensions

- **Multiple sequences.** Sequences share one latent space. The dynamics prior drops the transition from the last state
  of one sequence to the first of the next, and each first state gets its own prior <Cite id="wang2008" />.
- **Higher-order dynamics.** A second-order model $\xvec_t = f(\xvec_{t-1}, \xvec_{t-2}) + \nvec_{x,t}$ uses a kernel on
  pairs of consecutive states, which captures velocity.
- **Bayesian training.** Replacing the Markov prior by a Gaussian process over time, $\xvec(t) \sim \GP$, and
  integrating out $\Xmat$ variationally gives the variational GP dynamical system, built on the
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">Bayesian GP-LVM</NoteLink> bound <Cite id="damianou2016" />.

## Assumptions

- **Markov dynamics in a low-dimensional state.** The next state depends only on the current one (or the last few).
- **One smooth transition function.** The same $f$ applies everywhere in the latent space. Different regimes, such as
  walking and running, must be separated in the latent space for one smooth function to represent both.
- **Gaussian noise** in both the dynamics and the observations.

## Pitfalls

- **Imbalanced objective.** Without reweighting or fixed hyperparameters, the dynamics term is swamped by the
  observation term, and the latent trajectories are not smooth.
- **Cost.** Both kernel matrices are $T \times T$, so each evaluation costs $O(T^3)$.
- **Point estimates of the trajectory.** The MAP trajectory carries no uncertainty. Its apparent smoothness depends on
  the choices above rather than on a posterior.
