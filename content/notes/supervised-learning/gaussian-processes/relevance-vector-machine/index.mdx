<Definition>
The relevance vector machine (RVM) models a target as $y = \sum_{i=0}^{N} w_i\phi_i(\xvec) + \varepsilon$, with a bias
$\phi_0 = 1$ and one <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">kernel function</NoteLink> $\phi_i(\xvec) = k(\xvec, \xvec_i)$ centred
on each training input, and Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, \beta^{-1})$ <Cite id="tipping2001" />. Each
weight has its own zero-mean Gaussian prior $w_i \sim \Gauss(0, \alpha_i^{-1})$. The precisions $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$
are set by maximising the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>. Most $\alpha_i$
diverge, their weights are fixed at zero, and the training inputs that remain are the relevance vectors.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss> and <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge
regression</NoteLink> both predict with a sum of kernels centred on training inputs. Kernel ridge regression uses all of
them. The support vector machine uses a subset chosen by its loss. The RVM chooses the subset by
<NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayesian model selection</NoteLink>: it gives every basis function a
separate prior scale and lets the marginal likelihood decide which scales should be zero.

The pruning comes from the Occam penalty in the marginal likelihood. A basis function whose weight could be non-zero
adds prior variance to the targets. If the data do not need that variance, the
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> term of the marginal likelihood penalises it, and the best prior
variance for that weight is exactly zero. This is <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">automatic relevance
determination</NoteLink> applied to a kernel basis.

## The model

Let $\Phimat$ be the $N \times (N + 1)$ design matrix with entries $\Phi_{ni} = \phi_i(\xvec_n)$, and
$\Amat = \diag(\alpha_0, \dots, \alpha_N)$ the prior <Gloss name="precision-matrix">precision matrix</Gloss>. Given the
hyperparameters, this is <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>. The posterior
over the weights is Gaussian with

$$
\Sigmamat = (\Amat + \beta\Phimat^\top\Phimat)^{-1}, \qquad \muvec = \beta\Sigmamat\Phimat^\top\yvec,
$$

and the predictive distribution at a new input is Gaussian with mean $\muvec^\top\phivec(\xvec_*)$ and variance
$\beta^{-1} + \phivec(\xvec_*)^\top\Sigmamat\phivec(\xvec_*)$.

## The marginal likelihood

Define the energy $E(\wvec) = \frac\beta2\norm{\yvec - \Phimat\wvec}^2 + \frac12\wvec^\top\Amat\wvec$. The likelihood
times the prior is

$$
p(\yvec \mid \wvec)\,p(\wvec) = \paren{\frac{\beta}{2\pi}}^{N/2}\prod_i\paren{\frac{\alpha_i}{2\pi}}^{1/2} e^{-E(\wvec)}.
$$

The energy is quadratic in $\wvec$ with <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>
$\Sigmamat^{-1} = \Amat + \beta\Phimat^\top\Phimat$ and minimiser $\muvec$, so a second-order expansion about $\muvec$
is exact: $E(\wvec) = E(\muvec) + \frac12(\wvec - \muvec)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\wvec - \muvec)$. The
<NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink> over $\wvec$ gives
$(2\pi)^{(N+1)/2}\abs{\Sigmamat}^{1/2}e^{-E(\muvec)}$, and the log marginal likelihood is

$$
\Lcal(\alphavec, \beta) = \frac12\sum_i\ln\alpha_i + \frac N2\ln\beta - E(\muvec) - \frac12\ln\abs{\Sigmamat^{-1}} - \frac N2\ln 2\pi.
$$

### Re-estimation of the precisions

Differentiate $\Lcal$ with respect to one precision $\alpha_i$. Three terms depend on it.

- $\frac12\ln\alpha_i$ contributes $\frac{1}{2\alpha_i}$.
- $E(\muvec)$ depends on $\alpha_i$ directly and through $\muvec$. The indirect part vanishes because $\muvec$ minimises
  $E$, so the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of $E$ at $\muvec$ is zero. The direct part is
  $\frac12\mu_i^2$.
- $\frac12\ln\abs{\Sigmamat^{-1}}$ contributes $\frac12\trace(\Sigmamat\,\partial\Sigmamat^{-1}/\partial\alpha_i) =
  \frac12\Sigma_{ii}$, since $\partial\Sigmamat^{-1}/\partial\alpha_i$ has a single 1 at position $(i, i)$.

Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> to zero gives $1/\alpha_i - \mu_i^2 - \Sigma_{ii} = 0$.
Define

$$
\gamma_i = 1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii} \in [0, 1].
$$

The number $\gamma_i$ measures how well the data determine $w_i$. It is near 1 when the posterior variance
$\Sigma_{ii}$ is much smaller than the prior variance $1/\alpha_i$, and near 0 when the data leave the prior unchanged.
Rearranging the stationarity condition as $\alpha_i\mu_i^2 = \gamma_i$ gives MacKay's fixed-point update
<Cite id="mackay1992" />:

$$
\alpha_i^{\text{new}} = \frac{\gamma_i}{\mu_i^2}.
$$

For the noise precision, the terms $\frac N2\ln\beta$, $E(\muvec)$ and $\frac12\ln\abs{\Sigmamat^{-1}}$ contribute
$\frac{N}{2\beta} - \frac12\norm{\yvec - \Phimat\muvec}^2 - \frac12\trace(\Sigmamat\Phimat^\top\Phimat)$. The trace
simplifies, because $\beta\Phimat^\top\Phimat = \Sigmamat^{-1} - \Amat$:
$\trace(\Sigmamat\Phimat^\top\Phimat) = \beta^{-1}\trace(\eye - \Sigmamat\Amat) = \beta^{-1}\sum_i\gamma_i$. Setting
the derivative to zero gives

$$
\beta^{\text{new}} = \frac{N - \sum_i\gamma_i}{\norm{\yvec - \Phimat\muvec}^2}.
$$

The numerator $N - \sum_i\gamma_i$ is the number of data points minus the number of well-determined parameters. In
the noise-variance estimate $1/\beta$ it divides the residual sum of squares, the Bayesian analogue of the
degrees-of-freedom correction in an unbiased variance estimate. Training alternates between
computing $\Sigmamat$ and $\muvec$ and applying the two updates <Cite id="tipping2001" />.

### Why the weights go to zero

For a weight that the data do not support, $\mu_i$ shrinks toward zero faster than $\gamma_i$, and the update
$\alpha_i = \gamma_i/\mu_i^2$ grows without bound. The limit is exact, not a numerical accident. Fix every
hyperparameter except $\alpha_i$. With $\Cmat_{-i}$ the covariance of $\yvec$ without basis function $i$, define
$s_i = \phivec_i^\top\Cmat_{-i}^{-1}\phivec_i$ and $q_i = \phivec_i^\top\Cmat_{-i}^{-1}\yvec$, where $\phivec_i$ is
column $i$ of $\Phimat$. Then, up to terms that do not involve $\alpha_i$,

$$
\Lcal(\alpha_i) = \frac12\brack{\ln\alpha_i - \ln(\alpha_i + s_i) + \frac{q_i^2}{\alpha_i + s_i}},
$$

whose unique maximum is $\alpha_i = s_i^2/(q_i^2 - s_i)$ when $q_i^2 > s_i$ and $\alpha_i = \infty$ otherwise
<Cite id="tipping2003" />. A basis function survives only if its alignment with the targets exceeds the variance it
would add.

<Derivation title="The marginal likelihood as a function of one precision">
Integrating out the weights, $\yvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Cmat)$ with
$\Cmat = \beta^{-1}\eye + \sum_j\alpha_j^{-1}\phivec_j\phivec_j^\top = \Cmat_{-i} + \alpha_i^{-1}\phivec_i\phivec_i^\top$.
The matrix determinant lemma gives $\abs{\Cmat} = \abs{\Cmat_{-i}}(1 + s_i/\alpha_i)$. The
<NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">Sherman–Morrison formula</NoteLink> gives
$\Cmat^{-1} = \Cmat_{-i}^{-1} - \Cmat_{-i}^{-1}\phivec_i\phivec_i^\top\Cmat_{-i}^{-1}/(\alpha_i + s_i)$, so
$\yvec^\top\Cmat^{-1}\yvec = \yvec^\top\Cmat_{-i}^{-1}\yvec - q_i^2/(\alpha_i + s_i)$. Substituting into
$\Lcal = -\frac12(N\ln 2\pi + \ln\abs{\Cmat} + \yvec^\top\Cmat^{-1}\yvec)$ and using
$-\ln(1 + s_i/\alpha_i) = \ln\alpha_i - \ln(\alpha_i + s_i)$ gives the expression above. Its derivative is
$\frac{s_i^2 + \alpha_i(s_i - q_i^2)}{2\alpha_i(\alpha_i + s_i)^2}$, which is positive for every $\alpha_i$ when
$q_i^2 \le s_i$ and has a single zero otherwise.
</Derivation>

The same criterion gives a faster algorithm. Tipping and Faul start from an empty model and repeatedly add, re-estimate
or delete one basis function using $s_i$ and $q_i$, so the matrices stay as small as the final model
<Cite id="tipping2003" />.

### The effective prior on the weights

Tipping's paper places gamma hyperpriors on each $\alpha_i$ and lets them become flat on $\ln\alpha_i$. Integrating
$\alpha_i$ out of $\Gauss(w_i \mid 0, \alpha_i^{-1})$ under the flat limit gives the
<NoteLink to="prior-choice">improper prior</NoteLink> $p(w_i) \propto 1/\abs{w_i}$, which is sharply peaked at zero and
heavy-tailed <Cite id="tipping2001" />. Type-II maximum likelihood on $\alpha_i$ is a way of using this
sparsity-favouring prior without having to compute with it directly.

## Classification

For binary targets the likelihood is Bernoulli with $p(y = 1 \mid \xvec) = \sigma(\wvec^\top\phivec(\xvec))$, where
$\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. The weight posterior is no longer Gaussian. Tipping uses a
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> at the posterior mode, found by
<Gloss name="irls">iteratively reweighted least squares</Gloss>, and then applies the same $\alpha$ updates with
$\muvec$ and $\Sigmamat$ from the approximation <Cite id="tipping2001" />.

## Comparison with the support vector machine

| | Support vector machine | Relevance vector machine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output | a decision value or a point estimate | a predictive distribution |
| Sparsity | support vectors, often a large fraction of the data | relevance vectors, typically far fewer |
| Hyperparameters | $C$ (and $\epsilon$ for regression) by cross-validation | noise and precisions by marginal likelihood; kernel width by cross-validation or evidence |
| Kernel | must be <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss> | any <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis functions</NoteLink> |
| Training | convex quadratic programme | non-convex; $O(M^3)$ per iteration for $M$ active basis functions |

On benchmark problems Tipping reports test errors comparable to the support vector machine with an order of magnitude
fewer kernel functions <Cite id="tipping2001" />. Relevance vectors tend to be prototypical examples rather than
points near the decision boundary.

## Assumptions

- **A good basis.** The RVM selects among the given basis functions. It cannot adjust their width or place them
  anywhere other than on training inputs, so the kernel width still needs tuning.
- **Gaussian noise for regression.** The closed-form updates need a Gaussian likelihood.

## Pitfalls

- **Overconfident predictions away from the data.** The prior over functions is a finite sum of basis functions centred
  on the relevance vectors. Far from all of them every $\phi_i(\xvec_*) \approx 0$, so the predictive variance collapses
  to the noise level $\beta^{-1}$, exactly where uncertainty should be largest. A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian
  process</Gloss> does not have this defect. Adding a basis function at the test point repairs it
  <Cite id="rasmussen2005" />.
- **Local optima.** The marginal likelihood over $(\alphavec, \beta)$ is not concave. Different initialisations can
  prune different basis functions.
- **Cubic early cost.** The re-estimation updates start with all $N + 1$ basis functions and need an
  $O(N^3)$ inverse per iteration until pruning shrinks the set. Use the incremental algorithm for large $N$.
