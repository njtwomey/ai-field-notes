<Definition>
For a zero-mean <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> with kernel $k_{\thetavec}$ and Gaussian
noise of variance $\sigma_n^2$, the marginal likelihood is the probability of the targets with the latent function
integrated out,

$$
p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec) = \int p(\yvec \mid \fvec)\, p(\fvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec)\, d\fvec = \Gauss(\yvec \mid \zeros, \Kmat_y), \qquad \Kmat_y = \Kmat_{\thetavec} + \sigma_n^2\eye.
$$

Type-II maximum likelihood chooses the hyperparameters $\thetavec$, such as the length-scale $\ell$, the signal
variance $\sigma_f^2$ and the noise variance $\sigma_n^2$, by maximising $\ln p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec)$
<Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A Gaussian process has no weights to fit, but its kernel has parameters. The length-scale decides whether the model
explains the data with a rapidly varying function and little noise or a slowly varying function and much noise. The
training error cannot choose between them: it always prefers the shortest length-scale and zero noise, which
interpolate the data. A criterion must reward fit and penalise flexibility.

## Mechanism

### The log marginal likelihood

Under the model, $\yvec = \fvec + \varepsilonvec$ with $\fvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat)$ and
$\varepsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma_n^2\eye)$ independent. A sum of independent Gaussian vectors is Gaussian,
with the means and covariances added, so $\yvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Kmat + \sigma_n^2\eye)$. This is the integral in
the definition, done without integrating. Taking the logarithm of the multivariate normal density gives

$$
\ln p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec) =
\underbrace{-\tfrac12\, \yvec^\top\Kmat_y^{-1}\yvec}_{\text{data fit}}
\ \underbrace{-\ \tfrac12 \ln\abs{\Kmat_y}}_{\text{complexity}}
\ \underbrace{-\ \tfrac{N}{2}\ln 2\pi}_{\text{constant}}.
$$

- The **data-fit** term is the only term that involves $\yvec$. It is a Mahalanobis length of the targets. It is
  largest when the targets lie in directions in which $\Kmat_y$ has large variance.
- The **complexity** term depends only on the inputs and the hyperparameters. With eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of
  $\Kmat_y$, it equals $-\frac12\sum_i \ln\lambda_i$. It penalises covariance matrices that spread variance over many
  directions, which is what flexible kernels do.
- The **constant** is the same for every $\thetavec$.

### Occam's razor

The marginal likelihood is a normalised density over datasets: $\int p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec)\, d\yvec = 1$. A
short length-scale can generate many different target vectors, so it spreads its unit of probability thinly and gives
low density to each. A very long length-scale can generate only nearly constant vectors and gives almost no density to
the observed ones if they vary. The setting that maximises the density of the observed $\yvec$ is the simplest one
that can still produce it <Cite id="mackay1992" />. This automatic penalty on complexity is called Bayesian Occam's
razor <Cite id="mackay2003" />.

### Worked example

Take two observations, $y = 1$ at $x = 0$ and $y = 0.5$ at $x = 1$, a squared exponential kernel with $\sigma_f = 1$
and noise variance $\sigma_n^2 = 0.1$. The constant is $-\ln 2\pi = -1.8379$. As the length-scale grows the two
function values become more strongly correlated. The complexity term rises throughout, because the covariance
collapses toward one direction. Beyond $\ell = 1$ the data fit falls, because the targets differ:

| $\ell$ | data fit | complexity | $\ln p(\yvec \mid \ell)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | −0.520 | −0.088 | −2.446 |
| 1 | −0.456 | 0.086 | −2.208 |
| 2 | −0.571 | 0.421 | −1.988 |
| 5 | −0.792 | 0.695 | −1.935 |
| 20 | −0.885 | 0.774 | −1.949 |

The maximum is at $\ell = 4.15$, with $\ln p(\yvec \mid \ell) = -1.934$. Two points that differ by $0.5$ are better
explained, under noise variance $0.1$, by a nearly constant function than by a function that varies between them.

### The gradient

Hyperparameters are fitted by gradient ascent, usually with L-BFGS and on a log scale so that they stay positive. Two
matrix identities give the gradient. Differentiating $\Kmat_y\Kmat_y^{-1} = \eye$ with respect to a hyperparameter
$\theta_j$ gives $\partial\Kmat_y\,\Kmat_y^{-1} + \Kmat_y\,\partial(\Kmat_y^{-1}) = \zeros$, so

$$
\frac{\partial \Kmat_y^{-1}}{\partial\theta_j} = -\Kmat_y^{-1}\frac{\partial\Kmat_y}{\partial\theta_j}\Kmat_y^{-1}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \ln\abs{\Kmat_y}}{\partial\theta_j} = \trace\paren{\Kmat_y^{-1}\frac{\partial\Kmat_y}{\partial\theta_j}}.
$$

The second identity is proved below. Substituting both into the log marginal likelihood, and writing
$\alphavec = \Kmat_y^{-1}\yvec$,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\ln p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec)
= \tfrac12\,\alphavec^\top\frac{\partial\Kmat_y}{\partial\theta_j}\alphavec - \tfrac12\trace\paren{\Kmat_y^{-1}\frac{\partial\Kmat_y}{\partial\theta_j}}
= \tfrac12\trace\paren{(\alphavec\alphavec^\top - \Kmat_y^{-1})\frac{\partial\Kmat_y}{\partial\theta_j}},
$$

using $\alphavec^\top\Mmat\alphavec = \trace(\alphavec\alphavec^\top\Mmat)$ <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />. For the
squared exponential kernel the derivatives of the Gram matrix are elementwise:
$\partial\Kmat_{ij}/\partial\ln\ell = \Kmat_{ij}\, r_{ij}^2/\ell^2$ with $r_{ij} = \norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}$,
$\partial\Kmat/\partial\ln\sigma_f = 2\Kmat$, and $\partial\Kmat_y/\partial\ln\sigma_n = 2\sigma_n^2\eye$.

<Derivation title="The derivative of a log-determinant">
Let $\Amat$ be symmetric positive definite and $\Dmat$ symmetric. For small $\epsilon$,

$$
\ln\abs{\Amat + \epsilon\Dmat} = \ln\abs{\Amat} + \ln\abs{\eye + \epsilon\Amat^{-1}\Dmat}.
$$

The determinant of $\eye + \epsilon\Mmat$ is the product $\prod_i (1 + \epsilon\mu_i)$ over the eigenvalues $\mu_i$ of
$\Mmat$, which equals $1 + \epsilon\sum_i\mu_i + O(\epsilon^2) = 1 + \epsilon\trace\Mmat + O(\epsilon^2)$. Since
$\ln(1 + u) = u + O(u^2)$,

$$
\ln\abs{\Amat + \epsilon\Dmat} = \ln\abs{\Amat} + \epsilon\trace(\Amat^{-1}\Dmat) + O(\epsilon^2).
$$

With $\Amat = \Kmat_y(\thetavec)$ and $\Dmat = \partial\Kmat_y/\partial\theta_j$, the coefficient of $\epsilon$ is the
derivative.
</Derivation>

### Leave-one-out cross-validation

Cross-validation is the main alternative to the marginal likelihood. For a Gaussian process the leave-one-out
predictive distributions come from one factorisation. Write $\Pmat = \Kmat_y^{-1}$. The joint density of $\yvec$ is
proportional to $\exp(-\frac12\yvec^\top\Pmat\yvec)$. As a function of $y_i$ alone, with the other targets fixed, the
exponent is $-\frac12 P_{ii}y_i^2 - y_i\sum_{j \ne i}P_{ij}y_j$ plus a constant.
<NoteLink to="completing-the-square">Completing the square</NoteLink> shows that
$y_i$ given the other targets is Gaussian with

$$
\var[y_i \mid \yvec_{-i}] = \frac{1}{P_{ii}}, \qquad
\expect[y_i \mid \yvec_{-i}] = -\frac{1}{P_{ii}}\sum_{j \ne i}P_{ij}y_j = y_i - \frac{[\Pmat\yvec]_i}{P_{ii}}.
$$

The sum of the $N$ leave-one-out log predictive densities is then a criterion that costs no more than the marginal
likelihood <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.

## Cost

One evaluation of the log marginal likelihood needs the Cholesky factor $\Lmat$ of $\Kmat_y$, in $O(N^3)$ time. Then
$\alphavec$ costs $O(N^2)$ and $\ln\abs{\Kmat_y} = 2\sum_i\ln L_{ii}$ is free. The gradient needs $\Kmat_y^{-1}$ once,
another $O(N^3)$, and then $O(N^2)$ per hyperparameter for the trace of a product of two matrices. An optimiser
usually needs tens to a few hundred evaluations.

## Trade-offs

- **Point estimate versus integration.** Maximising over $\thetavec$ ignores the uncertainty in the hyperparameters.
  A fully Bayesian treatment places a prior on $\thetavec$ and integrates by Markov chain Monte Carlo
  <Cite id="neal1996" />, at a much higher cost. The difference matters most with little data.
- **Local maxima.** The surface over $(\ell, \sigma_n)$ is not concave. Short-length-scale, low-noise explanations and
  long-length-scale, high-noise explanations can both be local maxima. Restart from several initial values and compare
  the maxima by their marginal likelihood.
- **Marginal likelihood versus cross-validation.** The marginal likelihood trusts the model: it is optimal when the
  kernel family is right. Leave-one-out cross-validation measures predictive performance directly and is more robust
  when the kernel is misspecified, at the price of higher variance.
- **Overfitting with many hyperparameters.** With one length-scale per input dimension
  (<NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">automatic relevance determination</NoteLink>), or a neural-network feature
  map inside the kernel, the marginal likelihood can overfit like any other training objective.
