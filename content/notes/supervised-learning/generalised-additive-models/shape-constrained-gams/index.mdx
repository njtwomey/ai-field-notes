<Definition>
A **shape-constrained** smooth term $f_j$ of a GAM is required to be monotone increasing ($f_j' \ge 0$), monotone
decreasing ($f_j' \le 0$), convex ($f_j'' \ge 0$) or concave ($f_j'' \le 0$) on the range of its covariate, or to satisfy
several of these together. With a B-spline basis $f_j(x) = \sum_k\beta_kB_k(x)$, each shape follows from a sign
condition on the differences of the coefficients $\beta_k$, which the fit enforces by penalty or by reparameterisation
<Cite id="pya2015" />.
</Definition>

## Why constrain the shape

Subject knowledge often fixes the direction of an effect without fixing its form. The response to a dose of a toxin does
not fall as the dose rises. Demand does not rise with price. A credit score should not decrease as income increases, and
regulators may require that it does not. A benefit that saturates, such as the return on advertising spend, is
increasing and concave. An unconstrained smooth can violate such a constraint in regions with few data, where noise
decides the local slope. A constraint removes those fits from consideration. It lowers the variance of the estimate, and
the fitted curve can be shown to people who know the constraint must hold.

A constraint is a modelling assumption. If it is false, the fit is biased: a monotone fit to a curve that truly
decreases somewhere replaces that part with a flat segment.

## The derivative of a B-spline expansion

Consider cubic B-splines on evenly spaced knots with spacing $h$, as in <NoteLink to="p-splines">P-splines</NoteLink>. Every
basis function is a shifted copy of one **cardinal** B-spline, $B_k(x) = M_3((x - x_0)/h - k)$. The cardinal B-splines of
increasing degree come from repeated averaging over a unit window. $M_0$ is the indicator of $[0, 1)$, and

$$
M_p(u) = \int_{u-1}^{u}M_{p-1}(s)\,ds.
$$

The fundamental theorem of calculus gives the derivative directly:

$$
M_p'(u) = M_{p-1}(u) - M_{p-1}(u - 1).
$$

Apply this to $f(x) = \sum_k\beta_kM_3(u - k)$ with $u = (x - x_0)/h$ and $du/dx = 1/h$:

$$
f'(x) = \frac1h\sum_k\beta_k\brack{M_2(u - k) - M_2(u - k - 1)} = \frac1h\sum_k(\beta_k - \beta_{k-1})M_2(u - k).
$$

The second step shifts the index in the second sum, $\sum_k\beta_kM_2(u - k - 1) = \sum_k\beta_{k-1}M_2(u - k)$. The
derivative of a cubic B-spline expansion is a quadratic B-spline expansion whose coefficients are the first differences
$\Delta\beta_k = \beta_k - \beta_{k-1}$, divided by $h$. For general knots $t_k$ and degree $p$ the same argument gives
$f' = \sum_k p\,\Delta\beta_k/(t_{k+p} - t_k)\,B_{k,p-1}$ <Cite id="deboor1978" />.

Differentiating once more,

$$
f''(x) = \frac{1}{h^2}\sum_k\Delta^2\beta_k\,M_1(u - k), \qquad \Delta^2\beta_k = \beta_k - 2\beta_{k-1} + \beta_{k-2}.
$$

**Monotonicity.** B-splines are non-negative. If every $\Delta\beta_k \ge 0$, then $f'(x)$ is a sum of non-negative
terms, and $f$ is non-decreasing. The condition is sufficient but not necessary. Take six cubic B-splines on $[0, 3]$ with
$h = 1$ and $\betavec = (0, 1, 2, 1.9, 2.9, 3.9)$, so $\Delta\betavec = (1, 1, -0.1, 1, 1)$. The quadratic B-spline
$M_2$ takes the values $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$ at the knots of its support and $\tfrac18, \tfrac68, \tfrac18$ at the
midpoints of its three intervals. So $f'(1) = (1 - 0.1)/2 = 0.45$, and at the midpoint $x = 1.5$,
$f'(1.5) = (1 \times 1 - 0.1 \times 6 + 1 \times 1)/8 = 0.175$. The derivative stays positive although one difference is
negative.

**Convexity.** $M_1$ is the hat function: 1 at one knot, 0 at the neighbouring knots, linear in between. At each knot
exactly one hat equals 1, so $f''$ at that knot equals $\Delta^2\beta_k/h^2$ for one $k$, and $f''$ is linear between
knots. Hence $f'' \ge 0$ on the whole range if and only if $\Delta^2\beta_k \ge 0$ at every knot in the range. For cubic
splines on evenly spaced knots the convexity condition is necessary as well as sufficient. A basis of $K$ cubic
B-splines covering $[a, b]$ with $K - 3$ intervals has $K - 2$ knots in $[a, b]$ and $K - 2$ second differences, one per
knot.

Decreasing and concave shapes flip the signs. Shapes combine by imposing both sets of conditions: an increasing,
concave curve needs $\Delta\beta_k \ge 0$ and $\Delta^2\beta_k \le 0$.

## Enforcing a constraint with a penalty

pyGAM accepts `constraints='monotonic_inc'`, `'monotonic_dec'`, `'convex'` or `'concave'` on a spline term, or a list
of several. It enforces them with an **asymmetric penalty** that switches on only for violated differences
<Cite id="serven2018" />, the scheme of Bollaerts, Eilers and van Mechelen <Cite id="bollaerts2006" />. For
`monotonic_inc`, with current coefficients $\hat\betavec$, let $V = \set{k : \Delta\hat\beta_k < 0}$ be the violated
differences, and let $\dvec_k$ be the row of the first-difference matrix with $\dvec_k^\top\betavec = \Delta\beta_k$. The
constraint adds

$$
\betavec^\top\Cmat\betavec = \lambda_c\sum_{k \in V}\paren{\Delta\beta_k}^2 + \epsilon\norm{\betavec}^2, \qquad \Cmat =
\lambda_c\sum_{k \in V}\dvec_k\dvec_k^\top + \epsilon\eye,
$$

to the smoothing penalty, with $\lambda_c = 10^9$ and a diagonal loading $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ for numerical conditioning,
added only when $V$ is not empty. The other constraints use second differences and the opposite signs. pyGAM rebuilds $V$
and $\Cmat$ from the current coefficients at every <NoteLink to="penalised-irls-for-gams">penalised IRLS</NoteLink>
iteration, refactorises, and stops when the coefficients stop changing. For a tensor-product term it applies the
marginal constraint to every slice of the coefficient array along that margin.

The fit is an active-set method with a quadratic barrier. Differences that were violated are pushed to nearly zero, so
the curve has flat segments where the data would have it decrease. They stay slightly negative, of order $1/\lambda_c$
relative to the data scale: the penalty approximates the constraint and does not impose it exactly. Differences that
satisfy the constraint are left to the ordinary smoothing penalty. The method adds nothing to the fitting machinery
beyond a changing quadratic penalty. Standard errors and effective degrees of freedom computed from the final penalised
system treat the active set as fixed, although it was chosen from the data.

## Enforcing a constraint by reparameterisation

The shape constrained additive model (SCAM) of Pya and Wood builds the constraint into the parameters
<Cite id="pya2015" />. For a monotone increasing smooth, write

$$
\beta_1 = \gamma_1, \qquad \beta_k = \gamma_1 + \sum_{i=2}^{k}\exp(\gamma_i), \quad k \ge 2,
$$

so $\betavec = \Sigmamat\tilde\gammavec$ with $\tilde\gammavec = (\gamma_1, e^{\gamma_2}, \dots, e^{\gamma_K})^\top$ and
$\Sigmamat$ the lower-triangular matrix of ones. Then $\Delta\beta_k = e^{\gamma_k} > 0$ for every value of the
unconstrained working parameters $\gamma_k$, so the fit is strictly increasing by construction. Convexity uses the same
idea one level down: the second differences are the exponentials. The penalty acts on squared differences of the working
parameters, so heavy smoothing drives all the increments $e^{\gamma_k}$ to a common value and the fit to a straight
line.

The model is no longer linear in its parameters. SCAM maximises the penalised likelihood by Newton's method in
$\gammavec$ and selects the smoothing parameters by GCV, UBRE or AIC in an outer loop, as mgcv does for unconstrained
terms. The constraint holds exactly at every iteration. A flat segment corresponds to $\gamma_k \to -\infty$, which the
optimiser can only approach.

| | Penalty on violations (pyGAM) | Reparameterisation (SCAM) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constraint | approximate, violations of order $1/\lambda_c$ | exact, strictly monotone |
| Fitting | penalised IRLS with a changing active set | Newton on a non-linear penalised likelihood |
| Flat segments | reached easily | only approached |
| Smoothing parameters | as for the unconstrained model | GCV, UBRE or AIC with derivatives through the reparameterisation |

## Assumptions

- The constraint holds for the true effect over the whole covariate range.
- Evenly spaced knots, for the exact convexity equivalence. With uneven knots the difference conditions are rescaled by
  the knot spacings but remain sufficient.

## Pitfalls

- **Constraints on coefficients, not on the curve.** The monotone condition is only sufficient. A curve can be monotone
  with a negative coefficient difference, as in the worked example. The penalty therefore restricts slightly more than
  the stated shape.
- **Convex is not monotone.** A convex fit can still decrease at the left end. Combine constraints when both are meant.
- **A false constraint.** Where the data truly decrease, a monotone fit becomes flat and biased. Compare the constrained
  and unconstrained fits before trusting the constraint.
- **Concurvity.** A constraint on one term can be defeated by a correlated term that absorbs the opposite trend. The
  shape holds for the term, not for the marginal effect of the covariate on the prediction.
