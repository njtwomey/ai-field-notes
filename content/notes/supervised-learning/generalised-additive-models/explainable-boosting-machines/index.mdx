<Definition>
A **GA²M** is a generalised additive model with a small set $\Pcal$ of pairwise interactions,

$$
g\paren{\expect[y \mid \xvec]} = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^d f_j(x_j) + \sum_{(j,k) \in \Pcal}f_{jk}(x_j, x_k)
$$

<Cite id="lou2013" />. An **explainable boosting machine** (EBM) fits a GA²M by gradient boosting: it cycles through
the terms, and for each one fits a shallow tree on that term's features alone to the current pseudo-residuals, adding it
with a small learning rate. Each fitted term is a piecewise-constant function of one or two features
<Cite id="nori2019" />.
</Definition>

## From spline GAMs to boosted shape functions

A spline GAM represents each $f_j$ in a smooth basis and controls its wiggliness with a quadratic penalty. Lou, Caruana
and Gehrke compared other ways to build the shape functions of an additive model <Cite id="lou2012" />. They paired
several shape families (regression splines, single trees, bagged trees, boosted trees) with several fitting procedures
(penalised least squares, backfitting, gradient boosting). The most accurate combination on their benchmarks built each
shape function from many shallow trees, fitted by gradient boosting that cycles through the features, with bagging on
top. A tree on one feature is a step function of that feature, and a sum of step functions is again a step function, so
the model stays additive and every term can be drawn as a curve.

## Cyclic boosting

Bin each feature $x_j$ into at most $B$ ordered bins. Start from the constant $F = \beta_0$, the mean response (or its
link transform). Then, for rounds $m = 1, \dots, M$ and for each feature $j = 1, \dots, d$ in turn:

1. Compute the pseudo-residuals $r_i = -\partial\ell(y_i, F_i)/\partial F_i$; for squared loss, $r_i = y_i - F_i$.
2. Fit a tree with a few leaves to $\rvec$ using feature $j$ only. A split is a cut between two adjacent bins.
3. Update $f_j \leftarrow f_j + \nu h$ and $F_i \leftarrow F_i + \nu h(x_{ij})$ with a small learning rate $\nu$.

This is <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient boosting</NoteLink> <Cite id="friedman2001" /> with the base learner
restricted to one feature per step and the features visited in a fixed cycle.

**Why cycle, and why a small $\nu$.** Greedy boosting picks the feature with the best split at every step. When two
features are correlated, the first one chosen absorbs their shared signal, and the shape functions then depend on an
early choice. Visiting every feature in turn, with steps too small to capture much at once, lets correlated features
share the signal as the rounds accumulate. The final shapes then hardly depend on the order of the features. With $\nu =
1$ and full bin-level trees the cycle is backfitting with a bin-mean smoother; with small $\nu$ it is a damped version
of it.

**What it converges to.** Take squared loss and two-leaf trees. Let $S_L$ and $S_R$ be the residual sums in the two
leaves and $n_L$, $n_R$ their sizes. Adding the leaf means with step $\nu$ changes the residual sum of squares in the
left leaf by $\sum(r_i - c)^2 - \sum r_i^2 = -2cS_L + n_Lc^2$ with $c = \nu S_L/n_L$, which is $-\nu(2 - \nu)S_L^2/n_L$.
The total reduction is

$$
\nu(2 - \nu)\paren{\frac{S_L^2}{n_L} + \frac{S_R^2}{n_R}}.
$$

The residuals sum to zero after the intercept is fitted, and each update keeps it so, so $S_R = -S_L$. At a fixed point
no cut reduces the error, so $S_L = 0$ for every cut of every feature: every cumulative residual sum over the bins of
every feature is zero, and hence every bin's residual sum is zero. Those are the normal equations of least squares on
the indicator columns of all bins of all features. The limit of the boosting is the unpenalised least-squares additive
step-function fit. Regularisation comes from stopping before that limit, on a validation set, and from averaging over
bags. There is no penalty on roughness.

**Worked example.** Suppose feature $j$ has four bins with two points each and residual sums $(-3, -1, 1, 3)$. The
cumulative sums at the three possible cuts are $-3$, $-4$ and $-3$. With $S_R = -S_L$ the gain is $S_L^2(1/n_L + 1/n_R)$:
$9(1/2 + 1/6) = 6$, $16(1/4 + 1/4) = 8$ and $9(1/6 + 1/2) = 6$. The tree cuts between bins 2 and 3, with leaf means
$-4/4 = -1$ and $+1$. With $\nu = 0.1$ it adds $-0.1$ to $f_j$ on bins 1 and 2 and $+0.1$ on bins 3 and 4, and the
residual sum of squares falls by $0.1 \times 1.9 \times 8 = 1.52$.

## Pairwise interactions: FAST

A GA²M with all $\binom d2$ pairs would lose the model's readability and cost too much. Lou, Caruana, Gehrke and Hooker
rank the pairs first and keep the best few <Cite id="lou2013" />. After the main effects are fitted, each candidate pair
$(j, k)$ is scored by the best reduction in residual sum of squares that a simple interaction model achieves on the
residuals. The model cuts $x_j$ once and $x_k$ once and predicts the mean residual in each of the four quadrants.

The score is cheap because of cumulative sums. Bin the residuals into a $B_j \times B_k$ histogram of sums and counts in
$O(n)$. Form the two-dimensional prefix sums $H(a, b) = \sum_{a' \le a,\,b' \le b}(\text{bin sums})$ in $O(B_jB_k)$. For
cuts $(a, b)$ the four quadrant sums are then $H(a, b)$, $H(a, B_k) - H(a, b)$, $H(B_j, b) - H(a, b)$ and the remainder,
each in $O(1)$, and the same holds for the counts. The best quadrant model has reduction $\sum_q S_q^2/n_q$, so scoring a
pair costs $O(n + B_jB_k)$. The top pairs are added as terms $f_{jk}$ and boosted on the residuals of the main effects,
with trees that split on both features.

## InterpretML's EBM

InterpretML implements EBMs as `ExplainableBoostingRegressor` and `ExplainableBoostingClassifier`
<Cite id="nori2019" />. Beyond the algorithm above it:

- bins each feature, using up to 1024 bins for main effects and 64 per feature for interactions in version 0.7;
- holds out a validation set (15% by default) and stops when the validation loss has not improved for a set number of
  rounds;
- repeats the whole fit on several bootstrap samples ("outer bags", 14 by default) and averages the shape functions,
  which smooths them and gives an error band from the spread between bags;
- detects interactions with FAST, with the number of pairs set as a multiple of the number of features;
- centres every term to weighted mean zero over the training data and moves the means into the intercept. Full
  **purification**, which also moves a pair's marginal means into the main effects so that $f_{jk}$ averages to zero
  over each of its features, is the functional ANOVA convention <Cite id="lengerich2020" />; version 0.7 implements it
  but does not apply it by default;
- offers monotone constraints, and a post-hoc `monotonize` that replaces a term by its isotonic regression.

The defaults change between versions. Version 0.7 uses learning rates of 0.04 for regression and 0.015 for
classification and trees with two leaves. It starts with heavily regularised rounds that set the basic shapes, then
mixes the cyclic rounds with greedy steps that boost whichever term currently gains most.

## Intelligibility in practice

Caruana and colleagues fitted a GA²M to data on patients with pneumonia, to predict the risk of death
<Cite id="caruana2015" />. Its accuracy was close to that of the best black-box models on that task. Its term for asthma
lowered the predicted risk. The pattern was real in the data: patients with asthma were admitted directly to intensive
care and treated aggressively, which lowered their observed mortality. A model used to decide who can be treated at home
would have sent them home. A black-box model would have learned the same pattern without showing it. In the additive
model the term could be read, discussed with clinicians and edited. This is the case for **intelligible** models: each
term is a plot that a domain expert can check against knowledge, and a wrong term can be removed or corrected without
refitting the rest.

## Spline GAMs and EBMs compared

| | Penalised spline GAM | EBM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape of each term | smooth piecewise polynomial | step function on bins |
| Complexity control | quadratic roughness penalty, one $\lambda$ per term | learning rate, early stopping, bagging |
| Choosing the amount of smoothing | REML or GCV, from the training data | a held-out validation set |
| Uncertainty | Bayesian posterior bands, approximate p-values, effective degrees of freedom | spread across bootstrap bags |
| Interactions | chosen by the analyst: `te`, `ti` | detected automatically by FAST |
| Categorical and missing values | factor terms; missing values handled outside | bins, with a separate bin for missing |
| Beyond the data range | continues the penalty's null space, usually linearly | constant beyond the last bin |
| Shape constraints | penalty or reparameterisation | constrained boosting or post-hoc isotonic regression |
| Scaling in $n$ | $O(np^2)$ per iteration for $p$ basis functions | $O(n)$ per boosting step, many steps |

The two meet in the middle. A spline GAM gives smooth terms and principled inference. An EBM captures jumps and
thresholds without choosing knots, handles many features and pairs automatically, and needs a validation set in place
of a likelihood-based criterion. For an additive model, each term's contribution to a prediction is its exact
attribution, so the explanations that <NoteLink to="shapley-additive-explanations-for-trees">SHAP values</NoteLink> approximate for a
general model are read off directly.

## Assumptions

- The response depends on the features through main effects and a few pairwise interactions.
- The binning is fine enough to follow each effect, and the training data cover each bin.

## Pitfalls

- **Ragged shapes.** Step functions follow noise in sparsely populated bins. Bagging and more data smooth them; a sharp
  jump in a plot is not evidence of a real threshold unless it is stable across bags.
- **Confounded terms.** As in the pneumonia example, a term shows an association in the data, not the effect of an
  intervention.
- **Correlated features.** Cycling spreads the shared signal between correlated features, but how it is split is still
  not identified by the data. Read two correlated terms together.
- **Constant extrapolation.** Beyond the training range the prediction is flat, whatever the trend at the edge.
