<Definition>
**Model checking** for a generalised additive model asks whether each smooth's basis is large enough, whether the
residuals are consistent with the assumed family and link, and whether terms are confounded with each other.
**Term selection** decides which terms the model needs. With a **double penalty**, a second penalty on the null space
of each smooth's roughness penalty, smoothing parameter estimation can shrink an unneeded term all the way to zero
<Cite id="marra2011" />.
</Definition>

## Basis dimension

The penalty can only remove flexibility. A term with $k'$ free coefficients has at most $k'$ effective degrees of
freedom (derived in <NoteLink to="gam-inference-and-effective-degrees-of-freedom">GAM inference</NoteLink>). If the true
function needs more, the fit is biased whatever $\lambda$ is. Two checks detect this <Cite id="wood2017" />.

**Compare the edf with its cap.** An edf close to $k'$ means the penalty is barely acting and the basis sets the fit.
Refit with double the basis dimension. If the edf rises substantially, the original basis was too small.

**The k-index.** Sort the observations by the term's covariate $x_j$ and let $r_{(1)}, \dots, r_{(n)}$ be the
residuals in that order. The k-index is

$$
\hat k = \frac{\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\sum_{i=2}^n\paren{r_{(i)} - r_{(i-1)}}^2}{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2}.
$$

Suppose the residuals are $r_i = h(x_{ij}) + e_i$, with independent noise $e_i$ of variance $\sigma^2$ and a smooth
pattern $h$ that the fit has missed. Neighbours in $x_j$ share almost the same value of $h$, so $h$ cancels in the
differences, and $\expect(r_{(i)} - r_{(i-1)})^2 \approx 2\sigma^2$. The numerator estimates $\sigma^2$. The
denominator estimates $\sigma^2$ plus the mean of $h^2$. So $\hat k \approx 1$ when nothing is missed and $\hat k < 1$
when a pattern remains. A p-value comes from permutation: shuffling the residuals destroys any relation to $x_j$, and
the fraction of shuffled indices at or below $\hat k$ is the p-value. mgcv reports $\hat k$, the p-value, $k'$ and the
edf for each smooth in `gam.check` and `k.check` <Cite id="mgcvdocs" />. For smooths of several variables, neighbours
are found in the space of all the term's covariates.

**Worked example.** Four hundred points with $x$ uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $y = \sin(6\pi x) + \varepsilon$,
$\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 0.3^2)$: three full periods. A single penalised cubic spline with $p$ B-splines and the
penalty $\int f''^2$ is fitted with $\lambda$ chosen by REML, and the p-value uses 1000 permutations. With one smooth
and no separate intercept the edf cap is $p$.

| $p$ | edf   | $\hat k$ | p-value | residual SD |
| --- | ----- | -------- | ------- | ----------- |
| 6   | 5.65  | 0.19     | < 0.001 | 0.666       |
| 9   | 8.95  | 0.40     | < 0.001 | 0.458       |
| 15  | 13.82 | 0.95     | 0.16    | 0.295       |
| 27  | 19.70 | 0.97     | 0.22    | 0.292       |

With 6 and 9 functions the edf sits at the cap and the k-index is far below 1: the fit cannot follow three periods.
With 15 functions the k-index passes, but the edf of 13.8 is still close to 15. Doubling to 27 raises the edf to 19.7,
so 15 functions were constraining the fit a little, although the residual SD barely moves. The two checks answer
different questions and are used together.

## Residuals

- **Deviance residuals** $r_i = \sgn(y_i - \hat\mu_i)\sqrt{d_i}$, where $d_i$ is observation $i$'s contribution to the
  deviance, are plotted against the linear predictor, each covariate and any covariate not in the model. A trend
  points to a missing or too-smooth term. A funnel points to the wrong variance function. See
  <NoteLink to="deviance-and-glm-diagnostics">deviance and GLM diagnostics</NoteLink>.
- **Quantile plots** compare sorted residuals with their distribution under the fitted model. For discrete responses,
  **randomised quantile residuals** are exactly standard normal when the model is correct, which removes the banding
  of deviance residuals for counts and binary data <Cite id="dunn1996" />.
- **Overdispersion.** For Poisson and binomial families, the Pearson statistic $\sum_i(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2/V(\hat\mu_i)$
  divided by $n - \tau$ should be near 1. A value well above 1 calls for an estimated scale, a negative binomial
  family or a missing term.
- **Dependence.** Residuals ordered in time or space should not be autocorrelated. Correlated errors make GCV and REML
  choose too little smoothing, because correlated noise looks like signal.

## Concurvity

Concurvity is the nonlinear version of collinearity: one smooth is closely approximated by the others. The sum of the
terms stays well determined while the individual smooths trade signal and their bands widen. It is measured by how
much of $\hat f_j$ lies in the span of the other terms' bases. See <NoteLink to="gam-interpretation">interpreting
GAMs</NoteLink>. Concurvity also affects selection: when two terms carry the same information, a shrinkage method may
keep either one.

## Why the usual penalty cannot remove a term

In the Demmler–Reinsch basis of a smooth (see
<NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">smoothing penalties</NoteLink>), the penalised fit multiplies each
component of the data by $1/(1 + \lambda d_k)$. The components with $d_k = 0$ span the penalty's null space and are
multiplied by 1 for every $\lambda$. For a centred cubic spline smooth the null space is the centred line in $x_j$. Its
coefficient is the unpenalised least-squares slope, which is nonzero with probability one under continuous noise. So:

- the edf of the term never falls below about 1, the dimension of the null space;
- as $\lambda_j \to \infty$ the term becomes a straight line, not zero;
- a smoothing parameter criterion such as REML can make a term linear but cannot remove it.

## The double penalty

Write the term's penalty in its eigenbasis, $\Smat_j = \Umat\Lambdamat\Umat^\top$, and split $\Umat = [\Umat_+,
\Umat_0]$ into the eigenvectors with positive eigenvalues and those with zero eigenvalues. Marra and Wood add a second
penalty on the null space <Cite id="marra2011" />:

$$
\lambda_j\betavec_j^\top\Smat_j\betavec_j + \lambda_j^*\betavec_j^\top\Smat_j^*\betavec_j, \qquad \Smat_j^* =
\Umat_0\Umat_0^\top.
$$

$\Smat_j + \Smat_j^* = \Umat(\Lambdamat + \diag(\zeros, \ones))\Umat^\top$ is positive definite, so the two penalties
together have no null space. The argument that sends a singly penalised fit to its null space now sends it to zero:
as $\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_j^*$ both grow, the criterion at the fitted coefficients is bounded by the residual sum of
squares without the term, so $\hat\betavec_j^\top(\Smat_j + \Smat_j^*)\hat\betavec_j \to 0$ and $\hat\betavec_j \to
\zeros$. The two smoothing parameters are estimated with the others, by REML. Each term can end up wiggly ($\lambda_j$
small), linear ($\lambda_j$ large, $\lambda_j^*$ small) or absent (both large). In mgcv, `gam(..., select = TRUE)` adds
the extra penalty to every smooth <Cite id="mgcvdocs" />.

**Shrinkage smoothers** do the same with one smoothing parameter. They replace the zero eigenvalues of $\Smat_j$ by a
small multiple $\epsilon$ of its smallest positive eigenvalue, as in mgcv's `bs = "ts"` and `bs = "cs"`. The null-space
components are then shrunk by $1/(1 + \lambda_j\epsilon d_{\min})$, which tends to zero as $\lambda_j \to \infty$ but
only after the wiggly components have gone.

pyGAM has no null-space penalty. A term can be given two penalties, `s(1, penalties=['derivative', 'l2'], lam=[l1,
l2])`, where the ridge penalty `'l2'` acts on all coefficients rather than only the null space. The total penalty is
then full rank and the term can shrink to zero. With a derivative penalty of $10^6$, raising the ridge penalty from
$10^{-2}$ to $10^4$ took the edf of an irrelevant term from 0.98 to 0.002 in a check with 300 points.

**Worked example.** One hundred simulated data sets, each with 200 points, $y = \sin(2\pi x_1) + \varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 0.4^2)$ and an irrelevant second feature $x_2$. The model is $\beta_0 + f_1(x_1) +
f_2(x_2)$, each smooth a centred cubic spline with 13 B-splines and the $\int f''^2$ penalty, and all smoothing
parameters are chosen by REML.

| Penalty on $f_2$ | median edf of $f_2$ | edf $< 0.1$ | edf $< 0.5$ | edf $> 1.5$ |
| ---------------- | ------------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| $\int f_2''^2$ only | 1.02             | 0 of 100    | 0 of 100    | 28 of 100   |
| double penalty   | 0.25                | 45 of 100   | 60 of 100   | 10 of 100   |

With the usual penalty, the edf of $f_2$ was never below 1.01: the irrelevant term always kept its line. With the
double penalty, it was removed (edf below 0.1) in 45 data sets and kept small in most others.

## Comparing models by AIC

For a penalised model the parameter count in AIC is replaced by the effective degrees of freedom:

$$
\operatorname{AIC} = -2\ell(\hat\betavec) + 2\tau.
$$

This conditional AIC treats the smoothing parameters as known. Estimating them adds uncertainty that $\tau$ ignores,
and the criterion then leans towards the more complex model. Wood, Pya and Säfken replace $\tau$ by a corrected edf
that includes the uncertainty of $\log\hat\lambda$; mgcv's `AIC` uses it <Cite id="wood2016" />. pyGAM reports the
uncorrected version, plus 2 when the scale is estimated, and the small-sample AICc <Cite id="serven2018" />. AIC can
compare models with different terms fitted to the same response. REML scores cannot compare models with different
unpenalised terms, because the restricted likelihood depends on those terms.

## Assumptions

- The k-index assumes that residual variation is the same across $x_j$ and that neighbouring observations are
  independent. Residuals of a penalised fit are slightly correlated, so it is a screening tool, not an exact test.
- The double penalty assumes that shrinking a term to zero is a sensible default for every smooth.

## Pitfalls

- **Raising $k$ does not overfit.** With the penalty chosen by REML, a larger basis costs computation, not accuracy.
  An unnecessarily small basis costs accuracy.
- **A passing k-index is not proof.** In the example, 15 basis functions passed the k-index and were still slightly
  limiting.
- **Stepwise deletion by p-values.** p-values of smooth terms are approximate and conditional on $\lambda$. Repeated
  testing and deletion inflates the apparent significance of what remains. The double penalty selects terms in one
  fit.
- **Selection under concurvity.** When terms overlap, which one survives is unstable across samples.
