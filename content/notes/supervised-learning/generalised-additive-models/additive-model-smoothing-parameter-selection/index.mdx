<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="smoothing-and-penalised-splines">penalised spline</NoteLink> fit minimises $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\betavec}^2 + \sum_j\lambda_j\betavec^\top\Smat_j\betavec$,
where $\Smat_j$ is the roughness penalty matrix of term $j$. **Smoothing parameter selection** chooses the $\lambda_j \ge 0$
from the data. **Generalised cross-validation** (GCV) minimises an estimate of prediction error. **Restricted maximum likelihood** (REML) maximises the
likelihood of the data with all the coefficients integrated out under the Gaussian prior that the penalty defines,
which is flat on the unpenalised null space <Cite id="wood2017" />.
</Definition>

## The problem

Each $\lambda_j$ trades fidelity against smoothness for one term of a
<NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive model</NoteLink>. Small values interpolate the noise;
large values flatten the term to its unpenalised part, a straight line for a <NoteLink to="regression-splines">cubic spline</NoteLink> penalty (see
<NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">smoothing penalties</NoteLink>). The residual sum of
squares always prefers $\lambda = 0$, so it cannot choose. A GAM can have dozens of smoothing parameters, so the
criterion must be cheap to evaluate and smooth enough to optimise numerically.

For fixed $\lambda$, the fit is linear in $\yvec$: $\hat\yvec = \Amat_\lambda\yvec$, with **influence matrix**
$\Amat_\lambda = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$ and $\Smat_\lambda =
\sum_j\lambda_j\Smat_j$. Its trace $\tau = \trace\Amat_\lambda$ is the <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> of the model.

## Leave-one-out cross-validation without refitting

Let $\hat f^{(-i)}$ be the fit with observation $i$ left out. Replace $y_i$ by $\hat f^{(-i)}(x_i)$ and refit with all
$n$ observations. The fit $\hat f^{(-i)}$ minimises the criterion over the other $n - 1$ points and has zero error at
point $i$, so it also minimises the full criterion. The refit is therefore $\hat f^{(-i)}$ itself. Its value at $x_i$ is
linear in the modified data:

$$
\hat f^{(-i)}(x_i) = \sum_{k \ne i} A_{ik}y_k + A_{ii}\hat f^{(-i)}(x_i) = \hat y_i - A_{ii}y_i + A_{ii}\hat
f^{(-i)}(x_i).
$$

Rearranging gives the leave-one-out residual from a single fit:

$$
y_i - \hat f^{(-i)}(x_i) = \frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{1 - A_{ii}}, \qquad \operatorname{CV}(\lambda) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\paren{\frac{y_i - \hat y_i}{1 - A_{ii}}}^2.
$$

**GCV** replaces each $A_{ii}$ by the average $\tau/n$ <Cite id="craven1979" />:

$$
\operatorname{GCV}(\lambda) = \frac{n\norm{\yvec - \Amat_\lambda\yvec}^2}{(n - \tau)^2}.
$$

GCV needs only the trace of $\Amat_\lambda$, and it is invariant to rotations of the data, which ordinary
cross-validation is not. For a <Gloss name="glm" /> family, the residual sum of squares is replaced by the deviance of the
<NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">penalised IRLS</NoteLink> fit. When the scale parameter $\phi$ is known, as for
Poisson and binomial responses, the **UBRE** criterion $D/n - \phi + 2\phi\tau/n$, an unbiased estimate of scaled
prediction error akin to Mallows' $C_p$, is used instead.

Both criteria can be made to smooth more by counting each effective degree of freedom $\gamma > 1$ times, replacing
$\tau$ by $\gamma\tau$. Wood suggests $\gamma = 1.4$ as a guard against the occasional undersmoothed GCV choice
<Cite id="wood2017" />, and <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-models-in-practice">pyGAM</NoteLink> uses it by default in both criteria <Cite id="serven2018" />.

## REML: the penalty as a prior

Read the penalty as a Gaussian prior on the coefficients: $\betavec$ has density proportional to
$\exp\paren{-\betavec^\top\Smat_\lambda\betavec / (2\sigma^2)}$. The penalty is rank-deficient, with a null space of
dimension $M$ holding the unpenalised functions such as straight lines, so the prior is flat on that null space.
Maximising the posterior gives the penalised least-squares estimate. The smoothing parameters are now variance
parameters, and they can be estimated by maximising the **marginal likelihood**, the density of $\yvec$ with $\betavec$
integrated out. Because the null-space coefficients are integrated against a flat prior, this is restricted maximum
likelihood (REML) <Cite id="wahba1990" /> <Cite id="wood2011" />. The same prior gives the posterior covariance used
for credible bands; see <NoteLink to="additive-model-inference-and-effective-degrees-of-freedom">GAM inference</NoteLink>.

For a single smoothing parameter with $\Smat_\lambda = \lambda\Smat$ and $r = \rank\Smat$, the integral is
Gaussian and can be done by <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">completing the square</NoteLink>. Let $\Mmat = \Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\Smat$, $\hat\betavec =
\Mmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$, and $Q = \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\betavec}^2 + \lambda\hat\betavec^\top\Smat\hat
\betavec$, the minimised penalised criterion. Up to constants,

$$
-2\log L_R(\lambda, \sigma^2) = (n - M)\log\sigma^2 + \frac{Q}{\sigma^2} + \log\abs{\Mmat} - r\log\lambda.
$$

Setting $\sigma^2 = Q/(n - M)$ profiles out the variance:

$$
-2\log L_R(\lambda) = (n - M)\log Q + \log\abs{\Mmat} - r\log\lambda + \text{const}.
$$

The first term rewards fit. The last two form an Occam factor: $\log\abs{\Mmat} - r\log\lambda$ grows as $\lambda$ falls
and the fit becomes more flexible.

<Derivation title="Integrating out the coefficients">
The model is $\yvec \mid \betavec \sim \Gauss(\Xmat\betavec, \sigma^2\eye)$, with prior density
$(2\pi\sigma^2/\lambda)^{-r/2}\abs{\Smat}_+^{1/2}\exp\paren{-\lambda\betavec^\top\Smat\betavec/(2\sigma^2)}$, where
$\abs{\Smat}_+$ is the product of the nonzero eigenvalues. The exponent of the joint density is
$-\brack{\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\betavec}^2 + \lambda\betavec^\top\Smat\betavec}/(2\sigma^2)$. Completing the square in
$\betavec$ writes it as $-\brack{Q + (\betavec - \hat\betavec)^\top\Mmat(\betavec - \hat\betavec)}/(2\sigma^2)$. The
<NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink> over $\betavec \in \reals^p$ gives $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{p/2}\abs{\Mmat}^{-1/2}$. Collecting powers of
$\sigma^2$: $-n/2$ from the likelihood, $-r/2$ from the prior and $+p/2$ from the integral, a total of $-(n - M)/2$
since $p - r = M$. Taking $-2\log$ gives the expression above.
</Derivation>

## GCV or REML

Both criteria choose similar amounts of smoothing on average. They differ in variability. GCV curves are often flat
near their minimum and sometimes have a second minimum at small $\lambda$, so the GCV choice varies more between data
sets and occasionally undersmooths badly <Cite id="reiss2009" />. REML penalises overfitting more strongly and has a
clearer optimum. In 40 simulated data sets of the kind in the figure (100 points, noise standard deviation 0.3), the
standard deviation of the chosen $\log_{10}\lambda$ was 0.60 for GCV and 0.11 for REML. Wood recommends REML over GCV
for this reason <Cite id="wood2011" />.

With several smoothing parameters, both criteria are optimised by <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> over $\log\lambda_j$. Stable
computation of the <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> terms, whose penalty matrices overlap in rank, is the main technical difficulty that
Wood's method solves <Cite id="wood2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Correlated errors.** Both criteria assume independent errors. Autocorrelated noise looks like signal, and the
  chosen $\lambda$ is too small. Model the correlation or choose $\lambda$ with blocked cross-validation.
- **Terms that should vanish.** A penalty never removes the null space, so a useless term is still fitted as a straight
  line. An extra penalty on the null space lets REML shrink a whole term to zero; see
  <NoteLink to="additive-model-checking-and-term-selection">term selection</NoteLink>.
- **Reporting $\lambda$.** Its scale depends on the units of the feature. Report the effective degrees of freedom of each
  term.
