<Definition>
For $\tau \in (0, 1)$, the **asymmetric squared loss** of a residual $u$ is $\rho_\tau(u) = \abs{\tau - \indicator[u <
0]}\,u^2$: $\tau u^2$ above the prediction and $(1 - \tau)u^2$ below it. The **$\tau$-expectile** of a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink>
$Y$ with finite mean is $e_\tau = \argmin_m\expect[\rho_\tau(Y - m)]$ <Cite id="newey1987" />. An **expectile GAM**
models the conditional expectile as an additive predictor, $e_\tau(\xvec) = \alpha + \sum_jf_j(x_j)$, with penalised
smooth terms.
</Definition>

## The expectile and its first-order condition

The loss $\rho_\tau$ is continuously differentiable, with $\rho_\tau'(u) = 2\abs{\tau - \indicator[u < 0]}\,u$; the two
one-sided <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> at $u = 0$ are both 0. Write $L(m) = \expect[(m - Y)^+]$ for the expected shortfall below $m$ and
$U(m) = \expect[(Y - m)^+]$ for the expected excess above it. Differentiating $\expect[\rho_\tau(Y - m)]$ with respect to
$m$ and setting the result to zero gives

$$
\tau\,U(m) = (1 - \tau)\,L(m).
$$

Rearranged,

$$
\tau = \frac{L(m)}{L(m) + U(m)} = \frac{\expect[(m - Y)^+]}{\expect\abs{Y - m}}.
$$

The expectile is the point at which the expected shortfall below it is the fraction $\tau$ of the expected absolute
deviation. $L$ is non-decreasing, with $L'(m) = \pr(Y \le m)$, and $U$ is non-increasing, with $U'(m) = -\pr(Y > m)$.
So the right side increases strictly with $m$, and for each $\tau$ there is exactly one expectile. For $\tau = 1/2$ the
condition is $U(m) = L(m)$, that is $\expect[Y - m] = 0$: the $\frac12$-expectile is the mean. The same ratio, read as a
function of $m$, is a distribution function, so expectiles are the quantiles of a transformed distribution
<Cite id="jones1994" />.

Compare the pinball loss $\abs{\tau - \indicator[u < 0]}\,\abs{u}$, whose minimiser satisfies $\tau = \pr(Y \le m)$ and is
the $\tau$-quantile (see <NoteLink to="regression-losses">regression losses</NoteLink>). A quantile depends only on how
much probability lies on each side. An expectile depends on how far the mass lies, like a mean.

**Worked example.** Take the five values $1, 2, 3, 4, 10$. The mean, the $\frac12$-expectile, is 4. For $\tau = 0.7$
try $m$ between 4 and 10. Then $U = (10 - m)/5$ and $L = (4m - 10)/5$, and the condition $0.7(10 - m) = 0.3(4m - 10)$
gives $7 - 0.7m = 1.2m - 3$, so $e_{0.7} = 10/1.9 = 5.263$. Check: $0.7 \times 4.737 = 3.316$ and $0.3 \times (4.263 +
3.263 + 2.263 + 1.263) = 3.316$. The pinball loss at the same level is minimised by the fourth smallest value, 4, since
$n\tau = 3.5$. The single large value pulls the expectile up and leaves the quantile where it is.

For a standard normal $Z$, $L(m) = m\Phi(m) + \phi(m)$ and $U(m) = \phi(m) - m(1 - \Phi(m))$, with $\Phi$ and $\phi$ the
normal distribution and density functions. The 0.9-expectile is $0.862$, which is the $\Phi(0.862) = 0.806$ quantile.
Conversely the 0.95-quantile, $1.645$, is the 0.988-expectile. Expectiles are closer to the mean than quantiles of the
same level.

**Equivariance.** Replace $Y$ by $a + bY$ with $b > 0$. Both $L$ and $U$ scale by $b$ about the shifted point, so the
condition is unchanged and $e_\tau(a + bY) = a + b\,e_\tau(Y)$.

## Fitting by asymmetric least squares

With a penalised basis $e_\tau(\xvec_i) = \bvec_i^\top\betavec$, the estimate minimises

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\paren{y_i - \bvec_i^\top\betavec}^2 + \betavec^\top\Smat_\lambda\betavec, \qquad w_i = \begin{cases}\tau
& y_i > \bvec_i^\top\betavec, \\ 1 - \tau & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
$$

where $\Smat_\lambda = \sum_j\lambda_j\Smat_j$ is the total smoothing penalty. The weights depend on $\betavec$, so the
fit iterates: fix the weights at the current residual signs, solve the weighted penalised least-squares problem
$\hat\betavec = (\Bmat^\top\Wmat\Bmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Bmat^\top\Wmat\yvec$, recompute the signs, and repeat until
no point changes side. This is **least asymmetrically weighted squares** (LAWS) <Cite id="schnabel2009" />, the
penalised form of Newey and Powell's asymmetric least squares <Cite id="newey1987" />.

The iteration is <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink>. The objective is convex, continuously differentiable and piecewise quadratic: it is an
exact quadratic on each region where the residual signs are fixed. On the current region, the weighted problem is that
quadratic, so each step jumps to the minimiser of the local quadratic model, which is what a Newton step does. When the
signs no longer change, the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is zero and the fit is the global minimiser. In practice the signs settle within a
handful of iterations. For $\tau = \frac12$ all weights are equal and the fit is the ordinary penalised least-squares
smooth. The <NoteLink to="additive-model-smoothing-parameter-selection">smoothing parameters</NoteLink> can be chosen by cross-validating the asymmetric loss, or by treating the penalties as
<NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">random effects</NoteLink> and using the mixed-model machinery with the weights fixed <Cite id="sobotka2012" />.

## pyGAM's ExpectileGAM

<NoteLink to="generalised-additive-models-in-practice">pyGAM</NoteLink>'s `ExpectileGAM(terms, expectile=τ)` is a Gaussian GAM with identity link whose <NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">penalised IRLS</NoteLink> weights are
multiplied by $\tau$ where $y_i > \hat\mu_i$ and by $1 - \tau$ where $y_i \le \hat\mu_i$ <Cite id="serven2018" />. The
weights are recomputed at every iteration, which is the LAWS iteration above.

Its method `fit_quantile(X, y, quantile, max_iter=20, tol=0.01)` searches for the expectile level whose curve has a
target share of the training data below it. It fits at the current `expectile` (0.5 by default) if the model is not yet
fitted, then runs a bisection on the interval $[0, 1]$:

1. Compute the ratio $r$, the fraction of training points with $y_i$ strictly below the prediction.
2. If $\abs{r - q} \le$ `tol`, stop.
3. If $r < q$, the current expectile becomes the lower end of the interval; otherwise it becomes the upper end.
4. Set `expectile` to the midpoint of the interval and refit, starting from the previous coefficients.
5. Repeat, at most `max_iter` times.

The search matches the overall share of points below the curve, not the share at each $x$. It gives the conditional
quantile at every $x$ when the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distributions</NoteLink> differ only in location and scale, $Y = \mu(\xvec) +
s(\xvec)\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ independent of $\xvec$. By equivariance the $\tau$-expectile curve is then $\mu +
s\,e_\tau(\varepsilon)$ and the $q$-quantile curve is $\mu + s\,q_q(\varepsilon)$, so the single $\tau$ with
$e_\tau(\varepsilon) = q_q(\varepsilon)$ matches the quantile at every $x$. When the shape of the noise changes with
$\xvec$, the curve is correct only on average.

## Expectile or quantile regression

| | Expectile regression | Quantile regression <Cite id="koenker1978" /> |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loss | asymmetric squared, smooth | asymmetric absolute (pinball), kinked at 0 |
| Estimation | reweighted penalised least squares | <Gloss name="lp">linear programming</Gloss>, or smoothing of the kink |
| Meaning of level $\tau$ | balance of expected shortfall and excess | probability below the curve |
| Sensitivity to extreme $y$ | high, like the mean | low |
| Tail information | uses the size of tail values | uses only their count |

Expectiles are the better choice when the size of tail values matters, as for a tail-mean risk measure, or when the
smooth loss makes fitting and smoothing parameter selection easier. Quantiles are the better choice when the curve must
have a probability meaning, such as a reference range that should contain 90% of healthy patients.

## Crossing

In the population, curves for different $\tau$ never cross: at every $\xvec$ the expectile increases strictly with $\tau$,
as shown above. Curves fitted separately for each $\tau$ can cross in a sample, most often at extreme $\tau$ where few
points carry the large weight, or when each curve has its own smoothing parameter. One remedy fits all levels together
in a location-scale form, $e_\tau(\xvec) = \mu(\xvec) + c_\tau\,s(\xvec)$ with $s(\xvec) > 0$ and $c_\tau$ increasing in
$\tau$. Then $e_\tau - e_{\tau'} = (c_\tau - c_{\tau'})s(\xvec) > 0$ for $\tau > \tau'$ at every $\xvec$, so the curves
cannot cross <Cite id="schnabel2013" />.

## Assumptions

- The conditional distribution of $y$ has a finite mean at every $\xvec$.
- The expectile curve for each $\tau$ is smooth, with the usual GAM additivity.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading an expectile as a quantile.** A 0.9-expectile curve does not have 90% of the data below it. Check the empirical
  share, or use `fit_quantile` and remember that it matches the share only on average over $\xvec$.
- **Extreme levels.** At $\tau$ near 0 or 1 a few points carry nearly all the weight, and the curve follows them.
- **Heavy tails.** Like the mean, the expectile moves with outliers. A robust fit needs quantiles.
