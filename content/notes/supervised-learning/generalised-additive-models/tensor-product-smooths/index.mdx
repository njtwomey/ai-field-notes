<Definition>
Let $\avec(x) = (a_1(x), \dots, a_{k_1}(x))^\top$ and $\bvec(z) = (b_1(z), \dots, b_{k_2}(z))^\top$ be spline bases for
smooth functions of $x$ and of $z$, with penalty matrices $\Smat_1$ ($k_1 \times k_1$) and $\Smat_2$ ($k_2 \times k_2$).
The **tensor-product smooth** of $x$ and $z$ is

$$
f(x, z) = \sum_{i=1}^{k_1}\sum_{j=1}^{k_2}\beta_{ij}\,a_i(x)\,b_j(z),
$$

estimated with the penalty $\lambda_1\betavec^\top(\Smat_1 \otimes \eye_{k_2})\betavec +
\lambda_2\betavec^\top(\eye_{k_1} \otimes \Smat_2)\betavec$, one smoothing parameter per direction <Cite id="wood2006" />.
It is mgcv's `te(x, z)` and pyGAM's `te(0, 1)`.
</Definition>

## Building the basis

Start from a smooth of $x$ alone, $f_x(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i a_i(x)$. To let the shape in $x$ change with $z$, let every
coefficient $\alpha_i$ be a smooth function of $z$ in the second basis, $\alpha_i(z) = \sum_j \beta_{ij}b_j(z)$.
Substituting gives

$$
f(x, z) = \sum_i\paren{\sum_j\beta_{ij}b_j(z)}a_i(x) = \avec(x)^\top\Cmat\,\bvec(z),
$$

where $\Cmat$ is the $k_1 \times k_2$ matrix of coefficients $\beta_{ij}$. Starting from $z$ and letting its coefficients
vary with $x$ gives the same function, so the construction is symmetric in the two variables.

Stack $\Cmat$ row by row into $\betavec = (\beta_{11}, \dots, \beta_{1k_2}, \beta_{21}, \dots, \beta_{k_1k_2})^\top$,
so that the index of $z$ runs fastest. The basis function multiplying $\beta_{ij}$ is $a_i(x)b_j(z)$, so the row of the
model matrix for observation $(x_n, z_n)$ is the Kronecker product of the two marginal rows:

$$
\Xmat_{n\cdot} = \avec(x_n)^\top \otimes \bvec(z_n)^\top.
$$

The whole model matrix is the **row-wise Kronecker product** of the marginal model matrices $\Amat$ ($N \times k_1$) and
$\Bmat$ ($N \times k_2$): row $n$ of $\Xmat$ is the Kronecker product of row $n$ of $\Amat$ and row $n$ of $\Bmat$. $\Xmat$
has $k_1k_2$ columns. pyGAM computes it this way, as `a[:, :, None] * b[:, None, :]` reshaped to $N \times k_1k_2$
<Cite id="serven2018" />.

Two properties carry over from the marginal bases. B-splines sum to 1 at every point, and so do their products:
$\sum_{ij}a_i(x)b_j(z) = \sum_i a_i(x)\sum_j b_j(z) = 1$. A cubic B-spline row has at most 4 non-zero entries, so a
tensor-product row has at most $4 \times 4 = 16$.

**Worked example.** Take linear B-splines (hat functions) as both marginal bases. Suppose a point lies a quarter of the
way along a knot interval in $x$, so its non-zero marginal values are $(0.75, 0.25)$, and 60% of the way along an
interval in $z$, with values $(0.4, 0.6)$. Its four non-zero model-matrix entries are
$0.75 \times 0.4 = 0.30$, $0.75 \times 0.6 = 0.45$, $0.25 \times 0.4 = 0.10$ and $0.25 \times 0.6 = 0.15$, which sum
to 1. They are the weights of bilinear interpolation between the four surrounding coefficients.

## One penalty per direction

A roughness measure in the $x$ direction is the integrated squared second derivative across the whole surface. With
$f = \avec(x)^\top\Cmat\bvec(z)$,

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = \avec''(x)^\top\Cmat\,\bvec(z),
$$

so

$$
\int\!\!\int\paren{\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}}^2dx\,dz = \int\bvec(z)^\top\Cmat^\top\paren{\int\avec''(x)\avec''(x)^\top
dx}\Cmat\,\bvec(z)\,dz = \trace\paren{\Cmat^\top\Smat_1\Cmat\,\Gmat_2},
$$

with the marginal penalty $\Smat_1 = \int\avec''\avec''^\top dx$ and the marginal Gram matrix
$\Gmat_2 = \int\bvec\bvec^\top dz$. Expanding the trace, $\trace(\Cmat^\top\Smat_1\Cmat\Gmat_2) =
\sum_{ijkl}\beta_{ij}(\Smat_1)_{ik}(\Gmat_2)_{jl}\beta_{kl} = \betavec^\top(\Smat_1 \otimes \Gmat_2)\betavec$ in the
row-major ordering.

Wood's construction replaces $\Gmat_2$ by the identity <Cite id="wood2006" />. It reparameterises each marginal basis so
that its coefficients are values of the function at evenly spaced points. Then $\betavec^\top(\Smat_1 \otimes
\eye)\betavec = \sum_j \Cmat_{\cdot j}^\top\Smat_1\Cmat_{\cdot j}$ applies the one-dimensional penalty to the curve in $x$
held at each of the $k_2$ reference values of $z$, and sums. That sum is a Riemann-sum approximation of the integral over
$z$, with the grid spacing absorbed into $\lambda_1$. With P-spline marginals, the same penalty arises directly as the
sum of squared second differences along every column of $\Cmat$ <Cite id="eilers2003" />. The $z$ direction is
treated in the same way, so the fit minimises

$$
\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\betavec}^2 + \lambda_1\betavec^\top(\Smat_1 \otimes \eye_{k_2})\betavec +
\lambda_2\betavec^\top(\eye_{k_1} \otimes \Smat_2)\betavec,
$$

with solution $\hat\betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda_1\Smat_1 \otimes \eye + \lambda_2\eye \otimes
\Smat_2)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$. For a GLM response the same penalty enters
<NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">penalised IRLS</NoteLink>. pyGAM builds exactly this sum: for each margin $m$ it
forms the Kronecker product of identities with $\lambda_m$ times that margin's penalty in slot $m$, and adds the results.

**What is left unpenalised.** Take second-difference (or second-derivative) marginal penalties, whose null spaces are the
coefficient sequences linear in the index. The first penalty is zero when every column of $\Cmat$ is linear in $i$. The
second is zero when every row is linear in $j$. Both hold when $\beta_{ij} = p + qi + rj + sij$, a bilinear function of
the indices, so the joint null space has dimension $2 \times 2 = 4$. Evenly spaced B-splines reproduce linear functions
from linear coefficient sequences, so as $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \to \infty$ the fit tends to the least-squares surface
$p + qx + rz + sxz$.

## Scale invariance

Suppose $z$ is re-expressed in other units, $z' = cz$ (metres to millimetres is $c = 1000$). The marginal knots are
placed on the range or quantiles of the data, so they move with the data, and $b_j'(z') = b_j(z'/c) = b_j(z)$. The model
matrix does not change. The second derivative picks up $c^{-2}$ and $dz' = c\,dz$, so the marginal derivative penalty
becomes $\Smat_2' = c^{-3}\Smat_2$. The penalty $\lambda_2\betavec^\top(\eye \otimes \Smat_2')\betavec$ equals
$(c^{-3}\lambda_2)\betavec^\top(\eye \otimes \Smat_2)\betavec$: every fit available in the old units is available in
the new ones, at $\lambda_2' = c^3\lambda_2$. A smoothing parameter selected by GCV or REML over all $(\lambda_1,
\lambda_2)$ moves by that factor, and the fitted surface is identical. A difference penalty does not involve units at
all, so for P-spline marginals nothing changes. The tensor-product smooth is **scale invariant**.

An isotropic smoother lacks this property. The thin-plate penalty for two variables is
$J(f) = \int\!\!\int (f_{xx}^2 + 2f_{xz}^2 + f_{zz}^2)\,dx\,dz$; see
<NoteLink to="thin-plate-regression-splines">thin-plate regression splines</NoteLink>. Write the same function in the new
units as $g(x, z') = f(x, z'/c)$. Then $g_{xx} = f_{xx}$, $g_{xz'} = f_{xz}/c$ and $g_{z'z'} = f_{zz}/c^2$, and

$$
J(g) = c\int\!\!\int\paren{f_{xx}^2 + \frac{2}{c^2}f_{xz}^2 + \frac{1}{c^4}f_{zz}^2}dx\,dz.
$$

The three terms are reweighted relative to each other, in the ratio $1 : c^{-2} : c^{-4}$. The single $\lambda$
multiplies all of them, so no choice of $\lambda$ restores the original balance. In millimetres, curvature in $z$ is
weighted $10^{-12}$ relative to curvature in $x$. The fitted surface is then almost unpenalised in $z$ and stiff in $x$,
purely because of the units. Isotropy is right when both inputs are measured on the same scale, such as the two
coordinates of a map. It is wrong for pairs such as temperature and time.

## Interaction terms and the functional ANOVA decomposition

A surface $f(x, z)$ can be split as

$$
f(x, z) = f_0 + f_1(x) + f_2(z) + f_{12}(x, z),
$$

into a constant, two **main effects** and an **interaction**. The split is unique once each part is centred against the
others: $\sum_n f_1(x_n) = 0$, $\sum_n f_2(z_n) = 0$, and $f_{12}$ sums to zero over $x$ at every $z$ and over $z$ at every
$x$. This is the **functional ANOVA decomposition** <Cite id="stone1994" />. Fitting its parts as separate terms answers
a question that `te` alone cannot: is there an interaction at all, or do the effects add?

mgcv's `ti(x, z)` fits $f_{12}$ alone <Cite id="wood2017" />. It centres each marginal basis first. Let $\Amat$ be the
$N \times k_1$ marginal model matrix. The constraint $\ones^\top\Amat\alphavec = 0$ is one linear condition on
$\alphavec$. Let $\Zmat_1$ be a $k_1 \times (k_1 - 1)$ matrix whose columns span the null space of $\ones^\top\Amat$. The
centred basis $\tilde\avec(x) = \Zmat_1^\top\avec(x)$ has $k_1 - 1$ functions, each summing to zero over the data:
$\sum_n\tilde\avec(x_n) = \Zmat_1^\top\Amat^\top\ones = \zeros$. Build $\tilde\bvec$ in the same way and take the
tensor product of the centred bases. Every function in it has the required property. At any fixed $z$,

$$
\sum_n f_{12}(x_n, z) = \sum_{k,l}\beta_{kl}\,\tilde b_l(z)\sum_n\tilde a_k(x_n) = 0,
$$

and the same holds with the roles of $x$ and $z$ swapped.

The pieces fit together exactly. B-splines sum to 1, so the span of $\avec$ is the span of $\tilde\avec$ plus the
constant function, and likewise for $\bvec$. Multiplying out,

$$
\Span(\avec \otimes \bvec) = \Span(1) \oplus \Span(\tilde\avec) \oplus \Span(\tilde\bvec) \oplus \Span(\tilde\avec
\otimes \tilde\bvec),
$$

with dimensions $1 + (k_1 - 1) + (k_2 - 1) + (k_1 - 1)(k_2 - 1) = k_1k_2$. So `y ~ s(x) + s(z) + ti(x, z)` spans the same
functions as `y ~ te(x, z)` when the bases match. It gives the main effects and the interaction their own smoothing
parameters, and each term can be tested or dropped on its own. pyGAM 0.12 has no `ti` term. Its `te` term also absorbs
no centring constraint and relies on a tiny ridge on all coefficients to separate the constant from the intercept.

The figure reads the decomposition off a fitted `te` surface by averaging over an evenly spaced grid, which is the same
split with the uniform measure in place of the data.

## Basis size and cost

A tensor product of $d$ marginal bases has $\prod_m k_m$ coefficients. Two margins with 10 functions each give 100; three
give 1000. mgcv's default is $k = 5$ per margin, so `te(x, z)` has 25 coefficients and `te(x, z, w)` has 125
<Cite id="wood2017" />. pyGAM's default is 10 splines per margin. Each margin can have its own size, and should, when
one direction needs more flexibility: pyGAM takes `te(0, 1, n_splines=[10, 5])` and mgcv takes `te(x, z, k = c(10,
5))`.

Forming $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ costs $O(N p^2)$ for $p = \prod_m k_m$ columns in general, less when the sparsity of B-spline
rows is used, and the solve costs $O(p^3)$. The penalty matrices themselves are sparse Kronecker products and never need
to be formed densely.

## Assumptions

- The surface is smooth in each direction, at scales set by the separate $\lambda_m$.
- The marginal basis sizes are large enough in every direction. The penalty can only remove flexibility.
- The data cover the region where the surface is interpreted. A tensor product is defined on the whole rectangle of the
  two ranges, and in empty corners it extrapolates the penalty's null space.

## Pitfalls

- **Isotropic smooths of mixed units.** A thin-plate `s(x, z)` of variables in different units depends on the units.
  Use `te` unless both inputs share a scale.
- **Coefficient count.** Three or more margins multiply quickly. Reduce the marginal $k$ rather than dropping to an
  isotropic smooth.
- **Reading `te` as an interaction.** A `te` term contains the main effects. To ask whether an interaction exists, fit
  `s(x) + s(z) + ti(x, z)` and look at the `ti` term.
- **Concurvity with main effects.** Adding `s(x)` beside `te(x, z)` duplicates the main effect of $x$. The model is then
  identifiable only through the penalties.
