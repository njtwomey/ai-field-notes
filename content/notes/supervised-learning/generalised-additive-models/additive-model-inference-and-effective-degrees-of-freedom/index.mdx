<Definition>
Let a <Gloss name="gam" /> with model matrix $\Xmat$ and total penalty $\Smat_\lambda$ be fitted by penalised iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), with working
weights $\Wmat$ at convergence. Its **influence matrix** is $\Amat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat +
\Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat$ and its **effective degrees of freedom** (edf) are $\tau = \trace\Amat = \trace\Fmat$,
with $\Fmat = (\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat$. The edf of term $j$ is the sum of the
diagonal entries of $\Fmat$ that belong to its coefficients. Reading the penalty as a Gaussian prior gives the
**Bayesian posterior** $\betavec \mid \yvec \approx \Gauss(\hat\betavec, \Vmat_\beta)$ with $\Vmat_\beta =
(\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\phi$, where $\phi$ is the scale parameter <Cite id="wood2017" />.
</Definition>

## Effective degrees of freedom

**The influence matrix.** At convergence, $\hat\betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat
\zvec$ for the <NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">working response</NoteLink> $\zvec$ (see <NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">penalised IRLS</NoteLink>), so
the fitted linear predictor is $\hat\etavec = \Xmat\hat\betavec = \Amat\zvec$. For Gaussian data with the identity
link, $\Wmat = \eye$, $\zvec = \yvec$ and $\hat\yvec = \Amat\yvec$. The trace of a linear smoother is its
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>, and by the cyclic property of the
trace

$$
\trace\Amat = \trace\paren{\Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat} =
\trace\paren{(\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat} = \trace\Fmat.
$$

$\Amat$ is $n \times n$ and $\Fmat$ is only $p \times p$.

**What $\Fmat$ does.** Write $\Hmat = \Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat$. When $\Hmat$ is invertible, the unpenalised estimate is
$\tilde\betavec = \Hmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\zvec$, and

$$
\hat\betavec = (\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Hmat\,\Hmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\zvec = \Fmat\tilde\betavec.
$$

$\Fmat$ maps the unpenalised coefficients to the penalised ones. Without a penalty $\Fmat = \eye$ and $\tau = p$. The
diagonal entry $F_{kk}$ measures how much of the $k$-th unpenalised coefficient survives in its own penalised value.

**Per-term edf.** Let $J$ be the set of coefficient indices of term $j$. Its edf is $\tau_j = \sum_{k \in J}F_{kk}$.
The terms partition the coefficients, so the $\tau_j$ add up to $\tau$. An unpenalised coefficient, such as the
intercept or a parametric effect, has $F_{kk} = 1$ exactly. To see this, write

$$
\Fmat = (\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}(\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda - \Smat_\lambda) = \eye - (\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}
\Smat_\lambda.
$$

Column $k$ of $\Smat_\lambda$ is zero for an unpenalised coefficient, so column $k$ of $(\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}
\Smat_\lambda$ is zero and $F_{kk} = 1$.

**The upper bound.** $\Smat_\lambda$ is block diagonal, so the $J$ block of $(\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}\Smat_\lambda$
is $\Cmat_J\lambda_j\Smat_j$, where $\Cmat_J$ is the $J$ block of $(\Hmat + \Smat_\lambda)^{-1}$. Then

$$
\tau_j = \abs{J} - \lambda_j\trace\paren{\Cmat_J\Smat_j} = \abs{J} - \lambda_j\trace\paren{\Smat_j^{1/2}\Cmat_J
\Smat_j^{1/2}} \le \abs{J}.
$$

$\Cmat_J$ is a principal block of a positive definite matrix, so it is positive definite, and the trace in the middle
is non-negative. The edf of a term is at most its number of free coefficients: $k - 1$ for mgcv's `s(x, k = 10)`
after the sum-to-zero constraint, which leaves 9. As $\lambda_j \to \infty$, the penalised directions are removed and
$\tau_j$ falls towards the dimension of the penalty's null space: 1 for a centred <NoteLink to="regression-splines">cubic spline</NoteLink>, whose only unpenalised
function is the centred line. An edf close to $\abs{J}$ means the basis, not the penalty, is limiting the term; see
<NoteLink to="additive-model-checking-and-term-selection">model checking</NoteLink>.

mgcv also computes the alternative $\trace(2\Fmat - \Fmat^2)$, which enters its tests as reference degrees of freedom
<Cite id="wood2017" />. For a linear smoother $\Amat$, the expected residual sum of squares is
$\sigma^2\paren{n - \trace(2\Amat - \Amat^\top\Amat)}$ plus a bias term, which motivates this version
<Cite id="hastie1990" />.

## The penalty as a prior

Suppose the working data follow $\zvec \mid \betavec \sim \Gauss(\Xmat\betavec, \phi\Wmat^{-1})$. This is exact for a
Gaussian response with the identity link, and for other families it is the quadratic approximation of the
log-likelihood at the fit. Give $\betavec$ the prior density $\propto \exp\paren{-\betavec^\top\Smat_\lambda
\betavec/(2\phi)}$: a Gaussian with precision $\Smat_\lambda/\phi$, flat on the null space of $\Smat_\lambda$. The log
posterior is, up to a constant,

$$
-\frac{1}{2\phi}\brack{\norm{\Wmat^{1/2}(\zvec - \Xmat\betavec)}^2 + \betavec^\top\Smat_\lambda\betavec}.
$$

Let $\Mmat = \Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda$ and $\hat\betavec = \Mmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\zvec$. Expanding the
bracket gives $\betavec^\top\Mmat\betavec - 2\betavec^\top\Xmat^\top\Wmat\zvec + \zvec^\top\Wmat\zvec$. Since
$\Xmat^\top\Wmat\zvec = \Mmat\hat\betavec$, <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">completing the square</NoteLink> gives

$$
(\betavec - \hat\betavec)^\top\Mmat(\betavec - \hat\betavec) + \zvec^\top\Wmat\zvec - \hat\betavec^\top\Mmat
\hat\betavec.
$$

Only the first term depends on $\betavec$, so the posterior is Gaussian:

$$
\betavec \mid \yvec \sim \Gauss\paren{\hat\betavec, \Vmat_\beta}, \qquad \Vmat_\beta = \paren{\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat +
\Smat_\lambda}^{-1}\phi.
$$

The posterior mean is the penalised estimate. The scale $\phi$ is 1 for binomial and Poisson responses. Otherwise it
is estimated by the Pearson statistic $\hat\phi = \sum_i w_i(z_i - \hat\eta_i)^2/(n - \tau)$, which for Gaussian data
is $\norm{\yvec - \hat\yvec}^2/(n - \tau)$. This is the model of
<NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink> with a structured, partly <NoteLink to="prior-choice">improper
prior</NoteLink> <Cite id="wahba1983" />.

**The sampling covariance omits smoothing bias.** Treating $\zvec$ as random with covariance $\phi\Wmat^{-1}$, the frequentist
covariance of $\hat\betavec$ is $\Vmat_e = \Mmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat\Mmat^{-1}\phi$. It is smaller than
$\Vmat_\beta$:

$$
\Vmat_\beta - \Vmat_e = \Mmat^{-1}\paren{\Mmat - \Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat}\Mmat^{-1}\phi = \Mmat^{-1}\Smat_\lambda
\Mmat^{-1}\phi.
$$

The difference is the expected squared smoothing bias. The bias of $\hat\betavec$ for a true $\betavec$ is
$\expect\hat\betavec - \betavec = \Mmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat\betavec - \betavec = -\Mmat^{-1}\Smat_\lambda
\betavec$. Averaged over the prior, whose covariance on the penalised directions is $\phi\Smat_\lambda^-$ (a
<NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">generalised inverse</NoteLink>, with $\Smat_\lambda\Smat_\lambda^-\Smat_\lambda = \Smat_\lambda$), its <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">outer product</NoteLink> is

$$
\expect\brack{\Mmat^{-1}\Smat_\lambda\betavec\betavec^\top\Smat_\lambda\Mmat^{-1}} = \Mmat^{-1}\Smat_\lambda(\phi
\Smat_\lambda^-)\Smat_\lambda\Mmat^{-1} = \Mmat^{-1}\Smat_\lambda\Mmat^{-1}\phi.
$$

So $\Vmat_\beta = \Vmat_e + {}$average squared bias. Intervals from $\Vmat_e$ ignore the bias that the penalty
introduces and are centred on a biased estimate. Intervals from $\Vmat_\beta$ widen to allow for it.

## Credible bands

Term $j$ at a point $x$ is $f_j(x) = \bvec_j(x)^\top\betavec_j$, where $\bvec_j(x)$ is the vector of its (constrained)
<NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis functions</NoteLink> at $x$. Under the posterior, $f_j(x)$ is Gaussian with mean $\hat f_j(x)$ and variance
$\bvec_j(x)^\top\Vmat_j\bvec_j(x)$, where $\Vmat_j$ is the block of $\Vmat_\beta$ for term $j$. The pointwise 95%
band is

$$
\hat f_j(x) \pm 1.96\sqrt{\bvec_j(x)^\top\Vmat_j\bvec_j(x)}.
$$

A band for the mean response $\mu = g^{-1}(\eta)$ transforms the endpoints of the band for $\eta$, since $g^{-1}$ is
monotone.

**Across-the-function coverage.** The band is Bayesian, but its frequentist behaviour is known. Wahba proposed these
intervals for <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing splines</NoteLink> <Cite id="wahba1983" />, and Nychka explained their coverage
<Cite id="nychka1988" />. Pointwise, coverage is below nominal where the bias is large, such as at peaks, and above
nominal elsewhere. Averaged over the observed $x_i$, the coverage is close to nominal. The reason is the identity just
derived: the posterior variance approximates variance plus squared bias, and the average of that over the data is the
average squared error of the fit. Marra and Wood extended the argument to GAM components <Cite id="marra2012" />. The
coverage stays close to nominal unless a term is heavily oversmoothed, so that bias dominates the sampling
variability, which happens when the true term is close to its null space, a straight line. They also found that
including the uncertainty of the intercept in each term's band (mgcv's `seWithMean = TRUE`) improves coverage.

In 400 simulated data sets, each of 100 points with noise standard deviation 0.3 and $\lambda$ chosen by <Gloss name="reml" />, the
95% band from $\Vmat_\beta$ contained the true curve at 96% of the points on average, and the band from $\Vmat_e$ at
93%. The fraction varied between data sets: it was below 88% in one data set in ten.

The bands are pointwise. A simultaneous band, which covers the whole curve at once, is wider. It is obtained by
simulation: draw $\betavec^*$ from the posterior, compute $\max_x\abs{f_j^*(x) - \hat f_j(x)}/\operatorname{se}(x)$
for each draw, and replace 1.96 by the 95% quantile of that maximum.

## Posterior simulation

Draws $\betavec^* = \hat\betavec + \Lmat\uvec$, with $\Lmat\Lmat^\top = \Vmat_\beta$ and $\uvec \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$, give the posterior of any function of the model: a difference $f_j(a) - f_j(b)$, the location
of a maximum, a prediction on the response scale, or a whole curve. The same draws give simultaneous bands.

<PosteriorBands />

**Smoothing parameter uncertainty.** $\Vmat_\beta$ treats $\lambda$ as known. When it is estimated, the true
uncertainty is larger. Wood, Pya and Säfken add a first-order correction built from the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> of
$\hat\betavec$ with respect to $\log\lambda$ and the approximate covariance of $\log\hat\lambda$ from the REML
criterion; mgcv returns it from `vcov(b, unconditional = TRUE)` <Cite id="wood2016" />. pyGAM's `sample` method
instead refits $\lambda$ on response vectors simulated from the fitted model (`n_bootstraps=5` by default) and mixes
Gaussian draws around those fits <Cite id="serven2018" />.

## p-values for smooth terms

The hypothesis $f_j = 0$ is tested with a Wald statistic. Let $\hat\fvec_j = \Xmat_j\hat\betavec_j$ be the term's
values at the data and $\Vmat_{f_j} = \Xmat_j\Vmat_j\Xmat_j^\top$ their posterior covariance. The statistic is
$T = \hat\fvec_j^\top\Vmat_{f_j}^-\hat\fvec_j$ for a generalised inverse $\Vmat_{f_j}^-$. If $\Vmat_{f_j}$ has <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">full
rank</NoteLink> $\abs{J}$, $T$ is approximately $\chi^2$ with $\abs{J}$ degrees of freedom under the null. The penalty shrinks
most of those directions towards zero, so a test referred to this reference distribution has low power.

Wood's test uses a pseudo-inverse of rank $r$ close to the term's edf, keeping only the $r$ largest eigenvalues of
$\Vmat_{f_j}$, with a special treatment of non-integer $r$ <Cite id="wood2013" />. The statistic $T_r$ is referred to
a $\chi^2_r$ distribution, or to an $F$ distribution when $\phi$ is estimated. mgcv's `summary` reports these
<NoteLink to="p-value">p-values</NoteLink>. They condition on the estimated $\lambda$ and are approximate, but their null distribution is close to
uniform in simulations <Cite id="wood2013" />.

pyGAM computes the older statistic with the full-rank pseudo-inverse of the coefficient covariance and warns that its
p-values are too small when smoothing parameters are estimated <Cite id="serven2018" />. Use them to rule terms out,
not to declare terms significant.

## What pyGAM reports

`gam.summary()` prints the model-level statistics <Cite id="serven2018" />:

| Entry            | Definition                                                                                   |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Effective DoF    | $\tau = \trace\Fmat$                                                                          |
| Log Likelihood   | $\ell(\hat\betavec)$                                                                          |
| <Gloss name="aic" />              | $-2\ell(\hat\betavec) + 2\tau$, plus 2 when the scale is estimated                            |
| <Gloss name="aicc" />             | $\operatorname{AIC} + 2(\tau + 1)(\tau + 2)/(n - \tau - 2)$                                    |
| <Gloss name="gcv" /> or <Gloss name="ubre" />      | $nD/(n - \gamma\tau)^2$ when $\phi$ is unknown; $D/n + 2\gamma\tau\phi/n$ plus a constant when it is known, with $\gamma = 1.4$ |
| Scale            | $\hat\phi^{1/2}$ from the Pearson statistic; 1 for binomial and Poisson                       |
| Pseudo R-Squared | explained deviance $1 - D/D_0$, with $D_0$ the deviance of the constant model                |

The factor $\gamma = 1.4$ inflates the edf in GCV and UBRE, a correction Wood suggested against undersmoothing
<Cite id="wood2017" />. The UBRE value also carries an additive constant, so only differences between fits are
meaningful. Per term, the summary lists $\lambda_j$, the number of coefficients (Rank), $\tau_j$ (EDoF) and the
p-value.

The interval methods use the covariance stored in `gam.statistics_['cov']`. In pyGAM 0.12 this is the frequentist
covariance $\Vmat_e = \Mmat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat\Mmat^{-1}\phi$, not the Bayesian $\Vmat_\beta$. For the
logistic GAM of <NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">penalised IRLS</NoteLink>, the variances of $\hat\eta$ that
pyGAM uses at $x = 0.1, 0.5, 0.9$ match $\Vmat_e$ to seven digits and are 14% to 19% smaller than those from
$\Vmat_\beta$. pyGAM's bands therefore omit the allowance for smoothing bias and are narrower than mgcv's
<Cite id="serven2018" />:

- `confidence_intervals(X, width=0.95)`: an interval for the mean $\mu$ at each row of `X`, from the band for $\eta$
  mapped through $g^{-1}$. The quantile is Gaussian when $\phi$ is known and Student $t$ with $n - \tau$ degrees of
  freedom when it is estimated.
- `prediction_intervals(X, width=0.95)`: the same with the response variance added, for a new observation.
- `partial_dependence(term, X, width=0.95)`: $\hat f_j$ and its pointwise band on the link scale. pyGAM does not
  centre its smooths, so the curve has an arbitrary offset.
- `sample(X, y, quantity='coef' | 'mu' | 'y', n_draws=100)`: Gaussian draws of the coefficients, the means or new
  responses, centred on the fits and using the same covariance.

## Assumptions

- The quadratic approximation of the log-likelihood at the fit is adequate. For small samples with binary data it may
  not be.
- The smoothing parameters are treated as fixed unless a correction or <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> is used.
- The model is correct in its terms, family and link. The bands describe uncertainty within the model.

## Pitfalls

- **Pointwise, not simultaneous.** A pointwise band covers each $x$ separately. Claims about the whole curve, such as
  "the effect is monotone", need simultaneous bands or posterior draws.
- **Coverage is an average.** Near a sharp peak, where the bias is largest, the pointwise band covers less often than
  95%.
- **Uncentred bands.** Without the sum-to-zero constraint, as in pyGAM, a term's band includes an arbitrary share of
  the intercept's uncertainty. Compare differences between points, not levels.
- **p-values after selection.** Terms chosen because they looked significant have p-values that are too small.
