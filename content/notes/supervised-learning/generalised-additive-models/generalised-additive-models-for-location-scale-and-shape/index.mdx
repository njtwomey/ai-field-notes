<Definition>
A **generalised additive model for location, scale and shape** (GAMLSS) assumes $y_i \sim \Dcal(\theta_{i1}, \dots,
\theta_{iK})$ for a parametric family $\Dcal$ with up to four parameters, usually location $\mu$, scale $\sigma$ and two
shape parameters $\nu$ and $\tau$. Each parameter has its own link function $g_k$ and additive predictor,

$$
g_k(\theta_{ik}) = \eta_{ik} = \xvec_{ik}^\top\betavec_k + \sum_j f_{jk}(x_{ij}),
$$

with penalised smooth terms <Cite id="rigby2005" />.
</Definition>

## Beyond the mean

A <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">GLM</NoteLink> or GAM models the mean. Its variance follows from the mean
through the family's variance function, and its shape is fixed by the family. Many responses break that link. The spread
of body weight changes with age in a way that no fixed variance function of the mean describes. Insurance claim sizes
become more skewed for some policy types. Growth reference charts need the 3rd and 97th centiles as smooth functions of
age, and those depend on how the spread and skewness change, not only on the median. A GAMLSS gives each of these
features its own additive predictor. The fitted distribution at each $\xvec$ then yields any quantile, interval or tail
probability directly.

## Fitting by penalised likelihood

The estimate maximises the penalised log-likelihood

$$
\ell_p = \sum_i\log p\paren{y_i \mid \theta_{i1}, \dots, \theta_{iK}} - \frac12\sum_{k,j}\lambda_{jk}\,\betavec_{jk}^\top
\Smat_{jk}\betavec_{jk}.
$$

Rigby and Stasinopoulos's **RS algorithm** cycles over the parameters. It updates the predictor $\eta_k$ of one
parameter by penalised IRLS with the others held fixed, then moves to the next, until the likelihood stops changing
<Cite id="rigby2005" />. Each inner step is an ordinary penalised weighted least-squares fit, so any smoother that a GAM
can use works for any parameter.

**Worked example: a Gaussian location-scale model.** Let $y_i \sim \Gauss(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$ with $\eta_{i1} = \mu_i$
and $\eta_{i2} = \log\sigma_i$. With $r_i = y_i - \mu_i$, the log-likelihood of one observation is

$$
\ell_i = -\eta_{i2} - \tfrac12r_i^2e^{-2\eta_{i2}} + \text{const}.
$$

For $\mu$: the score is $r_i/\sigma_i^2$ and the expected information is $1/\sigma_i^2$. The IRLS working response is
$\mu_i + r_i = y_i$ with weight $1/\sigma_i^2$, so the location smooths are refitted by weighted penalised least squares,
with observations of large spread down-weighted.

For $\log\sigma$: the score is $\partial\ell_i/\partial\eta_{i2} = -1 + r_i^2/\sigma_i^2$ and the second derivative is
$-2r_i^2/\sigma_i^2$, whose expectation is $-2$ since $\expect[r_i^2] = \sigma_i^2$. A Fisher scoring step gives the
working response and weight

$$
z_{i2} = \eta_{i2} + \frac{r_i^2/\sigma_i^2 - 1}{2}, \qquad w_{i2} = 2.
$$

The scale smooths are refitted to $\zvec_2$ by penalised least squares with equal weights. An observation with residual
$r_i = 2$ where the current $\sigma_i = 1$ has $z_{i2} = 0 + (4 - 1)/2 = 1.5$ and pulls $\log\sigma$ upward there. One
with $r_i = 0.5$ has $z_{i2} = (0.25 - 1)/2 = -0.375$ and pulls it down. The algorithm alternates the two refits.

## Software

The R package gamlss implements the RS algorithm and a related algorithm of Cole and Green, with a large
library of distribution families <Cite id="stasinopoulos2007" />. mgcv fits several location-scale families, such as `gaulss` for
the Gaussian with smooth mean and standard deviation, by a direct Newton method on all predictors together, with REML
for the smoothing parameters <Cite id="wood2017" />. pyGAM has no multi-parameter families; its closest tool is the
<NoteLink to="expectile-gams">expectile GAM</NoteLink>, which models asymmetric features of the conditional
distribution without a parametric family.

## Assumptions

- The response follows the chosen parametric family at every $\xvec$. The family decides what "shape" means.
- Each parameter's predictor is additive in its covariates.

## Pitfalls

- **Too many predictors.** Four parameters with several smooths each can overfit, and shape parameters are estimated
  from the tails, which hold few data. Start with location and scale, and add shape terms when residual diagnostics
  demand them.
- **Family choice.** Different families with the same mean and variance can give very different extreme quantiles. Check
  normalised quantile residuals.
- **Identifiability across parameters.** A covariate can enter both the location and the scale predictor. Their effects
  can trade off when the data are few.
