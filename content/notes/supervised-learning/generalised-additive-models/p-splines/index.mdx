<Definition>
A **P-spline** is a B-spline regression with a difference penalty <Cite id="eilers1996" />. Take $p$ <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-splines</NoteLink> $b_1,
\dots, b_p$ of degree $q$ (usually 3) on equally spaced knots with spacing $h$, and let $\Bmat$ be the $n \times p$
matrix $B_{ik} = b_k(x_i)$. The P-spline estimate minimises

$$
\norm{\yvec - \Bmat\betavec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\Dmat_d\betavec}^2,
$$

where $\Dmat_d$ is the $(p - d) \times p$ matrix of $d$-th order differences, $(\Dmat_1\betavec)_k = \beta_{k+1} -
\beta_k$ and $\Dmat_d = \Dmat_1\Dmat_{d-1}$, and $\lambda \ge 0$ is the smoothing parameter. The solution is
$\hat\betavec = (\Bmat^\top\Bmat + \lambda\Dmat_d^\top\Dmat_d)^{-1}\Bmat^\top\yvec$.
</Definition>

## Construction

Eilers and Marx combined two ideas <Cite id="eilers1996" />.

1. **Use more knots than needed.** With 20 to 40 equally spaced knots, the basis can follow any curve the data could
   support, and the placement of individual knots stops mattering.
2. **Penalise the coefficients, not the function.** A B-spline coefficient $\beta_k$ is close to the value of $f$ near
   the peak of $b_k$, because the B-splines are local and sum to one. A smooth sequence of coefficients therefore gives
   a smooth function, and differences between neighbouring coefficients measure roughness.

A difference of order $d$ is the $d$-th power of the first difference. For $d = 2$, each row of $\Dmat_2$ is
$(1, -2, 1)$ placed along the diagonal, and $\Dmat_2^\top\Dmat_2$ is banded with five nonzero diagonals. For $p = 6$:

$$
\Dmat_2 = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1
\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\Dmat_2^\top\Dmat_2 = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
-2 & 5 & -4 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\
1 & -4 & 6 & -4 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & -4 & 6 & -4 & 1 \\
0 & 0 & 1 & -4 & 5 & -2 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The knots continue beyond the data at the same spacing. With $K$ spans of width $h = (b - a)/K$ on $[a, b]$ and knots
$t_j = a + (j - q)h$ for $j = 0, \dots, K + 2q$, there are $p = K + q$ B-splines, all of the same shape.

## Differences approximate derivatives

The difference penalty is a scaled, slightly reweighted version of the derivative penalty $\int f^{(d)}(x)^2dx$ of
<NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">smoothing penalties</NoteLink>. Write $b_{k,r}$ for the $k$-th
B-spline of degree $r$ on the knots. De Boor's <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> formula <Cite id="deboor1978" /> is

$$
b_{k,q}'(x) = q\paren{\frac{b_{k,q-1}(x)}{t_{k+q} - t_k} - \frac{b_{k+1,q-1}(x)}{t_{k+q+1} - t_{k+1}}}.
$$

With equal spacing, $t_{k+q} - t_k = qh$, so $b_{k,q}' = (b_{k,q-1} - b_{k+1,q-1})/h$. Substituting into $f =
\sum_k\beta_k b_{k,q}$ and collecting the terms that multiply each $b_{k,q-1}$,

$$
f'(x) = \frac1h\sum_k\beta_k\paren{b_{k,q-1}(x) - b_{k+1,q-1}(x)} = \frac1h\sum_k(\beta_k - \beta_{k-1})\,b_{k,q-1}(x).
$$

The derivative of a spline of degree $q$ is a spline of degree $q - 1$ whose coefficients are the first differences
divided by $h$. Repeating $d$ times,

$$
f^{(d)}(x) = h^{-d}\sum_k(\Delta^d\betavec)_k\,b_{k,q-d}(x),
$$

where $\Delta^d\betavec = \Dmat_d\betavec$ up to the indexing of its entries. Squaring and integrating over $[a, b]$
gives

$$
\int_a^b f^{(d)}(x)^2dx = h^{-2d}(\Dmat_d\betavec)^\top\Gmat(\Dmat_d\betavec), \qquad G_{kl} = \int_a^b
b_{k,q-d}(x)\,b_{l,q-d}(x)\,dx.
$$

$\Gmat$ is the <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">Gram matrix</NoteLink> of the B-splines of degree $q - d$. Each such B-spline integrates to $h$ over the real line,
because $\int b_{k,r} = (t_{k+r+1} - t_k)/(r + 1) = h$. The B-splines sum to one, so each row of $\Gmat$ sums to
$\int b_{k,q-d} = h$ in the interior and to less at the ends. Write $\tilde\Gmat = \Gmat/h$. Then

$$
\int_a^b f^{(d)}(x)^2dx = h^{1-2d}(\Dmat_d\betavec)^\top\tilde\Gmat(\Dmat_d\betavec),
$$

and the P-spline penalty is this expression with $\tilde\Gmat$ replaced by the identity. $\tilde\Gmat$ is an averaging
matrix: its entries are non-negative and its rows sum to at most 1. So its eigenvalues lie in $(0, 1]$, and

$$
\int_a^b f^{(d)}(x)^2dx \le h^{1-2d}\norm{\Dmat_d\betavec}^2.
$$

On a slowly varying difference sequence, averaging changes nothing and the two sides agree. On a rough one they
differ by a bounded factor. For cubic splines and $d = 2$, the B-splines of degree 1 are hat functions, $\tilde\Gmat$
has rows $(1/6, 2/3, 1/6)$, and an alternating sequence $(+1, -1, +1, \dots)$ is multiplied by $2/3 - 1/3 = 1/3$. For
$d = q$, the B-splines of degree 0 are the indicators of the $K$ spans, $\tilde\Gmat = \eye$, and the relation is
exact.

A numerical check with 20 spans ($h = 0.05$) and $d = 2$: for coefficients that follow one period of a sine, the ratio
of $\int f''^2$ to $h^{-3}\norm{\Dmat_2\betavec}^2$ is 0.984. For random coefficients it lies between 0.35 and 0.54.
For $d = 3$ it is 1 for every coefficient vector.

## The null space: polynomials

$\norm{\Dmat_d\betavec}^2 = 0$ exactly when every $d$-th difference of the coefficients is zero. The sequences $1, k,
\dots, k^{d-1}$ satisfy this, because each difference lowers the degree of a polynomial in $k$ by one. The solutions
of $\Delta^d\beta_k = 0$ form a space of dimension $d$, since the first $d$ values fix the rest by the recurrence, so
these $d$ sequences span it. With equally spaced knots, coefficients that are a polynomial of degree $j \le q$ in $k$
give a function that is a polynomial of degree $j$ in $x$: the B-splines reproduce polynomials <Cite id="deboor1978" />.
So for $d \le q + 1$ the penalty is zero exactly on the polynomials of degree below $d$.

The argument used for $\int f''^2$ applies unchanged. The penalised criterion at $\hat\betavec_\lambda$ is at most the
residual sum of squares of the least-squares polynomial of degree $d - 1$, so $\norm{\Dmat_d\hat\betavec_\lambda}^2 \to
0$ as $\lambda \to \infty$, and the fit tends to that polynomial. With 23 cubic B-splines and $\lambda = 10^{10}$, the
largest gap between the P-spline and the least-squares polynomial on 100 points is $1.7 \times 10^{-8}$ for $d = 1$,
$3.4 \times 10^{-7}$ for $d = 2$ and $4.8 \times 10^{-6}$ for $d = 3$, with <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> 1, 2 and 3.

## Conservation of moments

A P-spline fit reproduces the first $d$ moments of the data, whatever the value of $\lambda$ <Cite id="eilers1996" />.
The estimate solves the normal equations

$$
\Bmat^\top\Bmat\hat\betavec + \lambda\Dmat_d^\top\Dmat_d\hat\betavec = \Bmat^\top\yvec.
$$

For $j < d$, let $\uvec$ be the coefficients of the polynomial $x^j$ in the basis. Then $\Bmat\uvec$ is the vector of
$x_i^j$ and $\Dmat_d\uvec = \zeros$. Multiplying the normal equations on the left by $\uvec^\top$ removes the penalty
term:

$$
(\Bmat\uvec)^\top\Bmat\hat\betavec = (\Bmat\uvec)^\top\yvec \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^j\hat y_i =
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^j y_i, \qquad j = 0, \dots, d - 1.
$$

With $d = 2$, the fitted values have the same sum and the same first moment as the data, so the least-squares line
through the fit is the least-squares line through the data. The same argument works for any penalty whose null space
contains $x^j$, including $\int f''^2$. For a <Gloss name="glm" /> with its canonical link, the penalised score equations at
convergence give $\Bmat^\top(\yvec - \hat\muvec) = \lambda\Dmat_d^\top\Dmat_d\hat\betavec$, and the same step yields
$\sum_i x_i^j\hat\mu_i = \sum_i x_i^j y_i$: a logistic P-spline predicts exactly the observed number of events. In a
simulation with 100 points and 23 <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis functions</NoteLink>, the sums $\sum_i x_i^j(\hat y_i - y_i)$ for $j = 0, 1, 2, 3$ were
$0, 0, 0.0009, -0.039$ for $d = 2$ and $0, 0, 0, -0.0013$ for $d = 3$.

## How many basis functions

Once the fit is penalised, the number of basis functions matters little, provided it is large enough. The penalty sets
the smoothness; the basis only has to be able to follow the answer. On 100 points from $\sin(2\pi x)$ plus noise with
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> 0.3, and $d = 2$ with $\lambda$ chosen to give 7 effective degrees of freedom, the fits with
$p = 13$, 23 and 43 B-splines differ from the fit with $p = 83$ by at most 0.038, 0.013 and 0.003.

The value of $\lambda$ is tied to $p$. The difference penalty scales as $h^{2d-1}$ times the integral, so the same smoothness
needs a $\lambda$ that grows as $h^{-3}$ for $d = 2$. In the same example the $\lambda$ that gives 7 degrees of
freedom is 0.52, 7.1, 68 and 572 for $p = 13$, 23, 43 and 83, and $\lambda h^3$ stays between $5 \times 10^{-4}$ and
$1.1 \times 10^{-3}$. At a fixed $\lambda = 0.6$ the effective degrees of freedom are 6.8, 11.0, 18.2 and 29.5. A
$\lambda$ chosen for one basis size does not transfer to another.

<NoteLink to="generalised-additive-models-in-practice">pyGAM</NoteLink>'s smooth term `s()` is a P-spline with defaults `n_splines=20` ($p = 20$), `spline_order=3` (cubic),
`penalties='auto'`, which for a numerical feature means `'derivative'`, the second-order difference penalty
$\Dmat_2^\top\Dmat_2$, and `lam=0.6`
<Cite id="serven2018" />. In mgcv the same smoother is `s(x, bs = "ps")`, a cubic basis with a second-order
difference penalty by default <Cite id="wood2017" />. In both, $\lambda$ should be chosen from the data, not left at
its default.

## Comparison with O'Sullivan penalised splines

O'Sullivan used a B-spline basis with the <NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">exact penalty</NoteLink> $\int f''(x)^2dx$, the matrix $S_{kl} = \int b_k''b_l''$
<Cite id="osullivan1986" />. Wand and Ormerod call these **O'Sullivan splines** <Cite id="wand2008" />. The two
approaches share the basis and differ in the penalty.

| | P-splines | O'Sullivan splines |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Penalty | $\norm{\Dmat_d\betavec}^2$ | $\int f^{(d)}(x)^2dx$ |
| Computing the penalty | a banded integer matrix, any $d$ | integrals of B-spline derivatives |
| Knots | must be equally spaced | any placement, such as quantiles of $x$ |
| Meaning of $\lambda$ | depends on the knot spacing $h$ | depends on the units of $x$ |

With equally spaced knots the fits nearly coincide once $\lambda$ is rescaled by $h^{2d-1}$. On the example above with
23 B-splines and 7 effective degrees of freedom, the two $\lambda$ values differ by a factor of 6505 against
$h^{-3} = 8000$, and the fitted curves differ by at most 0.012. With unequal spacing the differences no longer
approximate derivatives, and the O'Sullivan penalty is the one to use <Cite id="wand2008" />.

The difference penalty is easy to modify <Cite id="eilers2015" />. Wrapping the differences around the ends gives a
cyclic smooth (pyGAM's `basis="cp"`; see <NoteLink to="cyclic-factor-and-by-terms">cyclic terms</NoteLink>).
Penalising only negative differences enforces monotonicity (see
<NoteLink to="shape-constrained-additive-models">shape-constrained GAMs</NoteLink>). Kronecker products of difference penalties
give tensor-product smooths (see <NoteLink to="tensor-product-smooths">tensor-product smooths</NoteLink>).

## Assumptions

- The knots are equally spaced. The derivative interpretation and the polynomial null space both rely on it.
- The basis is rich enough to follow the true curve, so that the penalty, not the basis, limits the fit.

## Pitfalls

- **Reusing $\lambda$.** $\lambda$ depends on $p$ through the knot spacing. Choose it by <Gloss name="gcv" /> or <Gloss name="reml" /> for each basis,
  and report effective degrees of freedom.
- **Order of the penalty.** $d$ sets the limit of heavy smoothing: a constant for $d = 1$, a line for $d = 2$, a
  quadratic for $d = 3$. A term that should shrink to zero needs an additional penalty on this null space.
- **Sparse regions.** With many knots, some spans may hold no data. The penalty still determines the coefficients
  there, by interpolating the coefficient sequence, so the fit stays defined. Its bands widen accordingly.
