<Definition>
A **generalised additive model** (GAM) for a response $y$ with features $x_1, \dots, x_d$ is

$$
g\paren{\expect[y \mid \xvec]} = \beta_0 + f_1(x_1) + f_2(x_2) + \dots + f_d(x_d),
$$

where $y$ follows an exponential-dispersion family as in a
<NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink>, $g$ is a monotone link function, and each
$f_j$ is an unknown smooth function estimated from the data <Cite id="hastie1986" />. Each $f_j$ is represented by a
<NoteLink to="regression-splines">spline basis</NoteLink> and estimated with a roughness penalty. With the identity link and Gaussian errors the model is an
**additive model**.
</Definition>

## Why additive

A fully nonparametric regression $f(x_1, \dots, x_d)$ needs data that fill a $d$-dimensional space. The amount of data
needed for a given accuracy grows exponentially with $d$. An additive model estimates $d$ functions of one variable,
each at the rate of a one-dimensional smoother. It gives up interactions between features in exchange. Each $f_j$ can
be plotted on its own, so the fitted model can be read term by term, like the coefficients of a linear model.
Selected interactions $f_{jk}(x_j, x_k)$, linear terms and factors can be added back as further terms.

## The term algebra

A GAM is written as a sum of terms. Each term contributes columns to one model matrix and, if it is smooth, a block
to one penalty. The two main software implementations use nearly the same algebra: the R package mgcv
<Cite id="wood2017" /> and the Python package pyGAM <Cite id="serven2018" />.

| Term                               | mgcv                      | pyGAM                     | Columns                                  | Penalty                            |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| smooth of one feature              | `s(x)`                    | `s(0)`                    | spline basis, $k$ functions              | $\lambda\int f''(x)^2dx$ or differences |
| linear effect                      | `x`                       | `l(0)`                    | the feature itself                       | none                               |
| factor (categorical) effect        | `g`, or `s(g, bs = "re")` | `f(0)`                    | one indicator per level                  | none, or a ridge penalty           |
| smooth interaction                 | `te(x, z)`, `ti(x, z)`    | `te(0, 1)`                | products of the marginal bases           | one penalty per margin             |
| varying coefficient $z\,f(x)$      | `s(x, by = z)`            | `s(0, by=1)`              | the basis of $x$ times $z$               | as for $f$                         |
| cyclic smooth                      | `s(x, bs = "cc")`         | `s(0, basis="cp")`        | basis with equal ends                    | wraps around                       |

The features are named in mgcv and indexed by column in pyGAM. `ti` is mgcv's pure interaction: the tensor product
with the <NoteLink to="factorial-designs">main effects</NoteLink> removed, so that `s(x) + s(z) + ti(x, z)` separates the main effects from the interaction.
Tensor products, cyclic, factor and `by` terms, shape constraints and the thin-plate basis have their own notes:
<NoteLink to="tensor-product-smooths">tensor-product smooths</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="cyclic-factor-and-by-terms">cyclic, factor and by terms</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="shape-constrained-additive-models">shape-constrained GAMs</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="thin-plate-regression-splines">thin-plate regression splines</NoteLink>.

A Poisson GAM with two smooths, an interaction and a factor reads, in the two packages,

```r
gam(y ~ s(x1) + s(x2) + te(x3, x4) + g, family = poisson, method = "REML")   # mgcv
```

```python
PoissonGAM(s(0) + s(1) + te(2, 3) + f(4))                                     # pyGAM
```

## Basis and penalty

Every smooth term is a basis expansion. Term $j$ has $p_j$ <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis functions</NoteLink> $b_{j1}, \dots, b_{jp_j}$ and coefficients
$\betavec_j \in \reals^{p_j}$:

$$
f_j(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{p_j}\beta_{jk}\,b_{jk}(x), \qquad \fvec_j = \Bmat_j\betavec_j,
$$

where $\Bmat_j$ is the $n \times p_j$ matrix with entries $b_{jk}(x_{ij})$ and $\fvec_j$ is the vector of the term's
values at the $n$ observations. <NoteLink to="stacking">Stacking</NoteLink> the columns of all terms gives one model matrix $\Xmat = [\ones, \Bmat_1,
\dots, \Bmat_d]$ and one coefficient vector $\betavec = (\beta_0, \betavec_1, \dots, \betavec_d)$. The linear
predictor is $\etavec = \Xmat\betavec$. A GAM with fixed bases is therefore a generalised linear model with many columns.

The columns are too many to fit without control. Each smooth carries a roughness penalty that is quadratic in its
coefficients, $\lambda_j\betavec_j^\top\Smat_j\betavec_j$, with a fixed <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss> matrix $\Smat_j$ and a
smoothing parameter $\lambda_j \ge 0$. For a cubic spline, $\betavec_j^\top\Smat_j\betavec_j = \int f_j''(x)^2dx$.
<NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">Smoothing penalties and curvature</NoteLink> derives $\Smat_j$ and
its null space, and <NoteLink to="p-splines">P-splines</NoteLink> replace the integral by differences of adjacent
coefficients. Embedding each $\Smat_j$ in the rows and columns of term $j$ gives the total penalty
$\Smat_\lambda = \sum_j \lambda_j\Smat_j$, a block-diagonal matrix. With Gaussian errors and the identity link, the
estimate minimises

$$
\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\betavec}^2 + \betavec^\top\Smat_\lambda\betavec, \qquad \hat\betavec = \paren{\Xmat^\top\Xmat +
\Smat_\lambda}^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec.
$$

The solution is a generalised ridge regression. The basis dimension $p_j$ sets an upper limit on the flexibility of
term $j$; the smoothing parameter $\lambda_j$ sets how much of it is used. Typical bases have 10 to 20 functions per term: mgcv's default
is $k = 10$ and pyGAM's is `n_splines=20`.

## Identifiability

The intercept and the smooths overlap. A constant can be moved from $\beta_0$ into any $f_j$ without changing the
model. With a spline basis this overlap is exact in the model matrix, and the penalty does not remove it.

**The model matrix is <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">rank deficient</NoteLink>.** <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-splines</NoteLink> form a partition of unity: $\sum_k b_{jk}(x) = 1$ for every $x$ in
the range of the basis. So $\Bmat_j\ones_{p_j} = \ones_n$, and the vector

$$
\vvec = (1, \; -\ones_{p_j}^\top, \; \zeros^\top)^\top
$$

(one in the intercept, minus one on every coefficient of term $j$, zero elsewhere) satisfies $\Xmat\vvec = \ones_n -
\Bmat_j\ones_{p_j} = \zeros$. Each smooth adds one such direction. In a check with an intercept and two smooths of
10 B-splines each, $\Xmat$ has 21 columns and rank 19.

**The penalty does not help.** A constant function has zero curvature, so $\Smat_j\ones_{p_j} = \zeros$ and
$\vvec^\top\Smat_\lambda\vvec = 0$. Along $\vvec$ both the fit and the penalty are unchanged: if $\hat\betavec$
minimises the penalised criterion, so does $\hat\betavec + c\vvec$ for every $c$. The matrix
$\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda$ is singular, and the individual $\hat f_j$ are not determined. Their sum is.

**The constraint.** The standard fix requires each smooth to sum to zero over the data:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n f_j(x_{ij}) = \ones^\top\Bmat_j\betavec_j = \cvec_j^\top\betavec_j = 0, \qquad \cvec_j = \Bmat_j^\top\ones.
$$

The constraint rules out the direction $\vvec$, because $\cvec_j^\top(-\ones_{p_j}) = -\sum_i\sum_k b_{jk}(x_{ij}) = -n
\ne 0$. It also makes each $\hat\fvec_j$ orthogonal to the intercept column. With Gaussian errors, the unpenalised
intercept's normal equation is $\ones^\top(\yvec - \Xmat\hat\betavec) = 0$. Every $\ones^\top\Bmat_j\hat\betavec_j$ is
zero, so $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y$. The intercept carries the overall level and each $\hat f_j$ carries only shape. The
centred smooth also has the narrowest confidence band of all the vertical shifts of $f_j$, because the uncertainty in
the level sits in $\hat\beta_0$ <Cite id="wood2017" />.

**Absorbing the constraint by a <Gloss name="qr">QR decomposition</Gloss>.** A linear constraint on $\betavec_j$ can be removed by
reparameterising. Take the QR decomposition of the $p_j \times 1$ matrix $\cvec_j$:

$$
\cvec_j = \Qmat_j\begin{pmatrix} r \\ \zeros\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Qmat_j = [\qvec_1, \Zmat_j],
$$

with $\Qmat_j$ orthogonal, so $\qvec_1 = \cvec_j/r$ and the $p_j - 1$ columns of $\Zmat_j$ are orthonormal and
orthogonal to $\cvec_j$. A vector satisfies $\cvec_j^\top\betavec_j = 0$ exactly when it is orthogonal to $\qvec_1$,
which is exactly when it lies in the span of $\Zmat_j$. So every constrained $\betavec_j$ is $\Zmat_j\tilde\betavec_j$
for one unconstrained $\tilde\betavec_j \in \reals^{p_j - 1}$. Substituting,

$$
\Bmat_j\betavec_j = (\Bmat_j\Zmat_j)\tilde\betavec_j, \qquad \betavec_j^\top\Smat_j\betavec_j =
\tilde\betavec_j^\top(\Zmat_j^\top\Smat_j\Zmat_j)\tilde\betavec_j.
$$

The term is refitted with basis $\Bmat_j\Zmat_j$ and penalty $\Zmat_j^\top\Smat_j\Zmat_j$, and no constraint remains.
In the check above, the 19 columns after absorption have rank 19, and every column of $\Bmat_j\Zmat_j$ has mean zero to
$10^{-16}$. mgcv applies $\Zmat_j$ as a Householder reflection without forming it <Cite id="wood2017" />. Each smooth
loses one coefficient: `s(x, k = 10)` has 9 free coefficients and at most 9 <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>.

pyGAM does not impose the constraint. It adds $\sqrt{\epsilon}\,\eye$, with $\epsilon$ the machine precision, to the
penalty, which picks one solution along each direction $\vvec$ <Cite id="serven2018" />. Its term curves therefore
carry arbitrary vertical offsets, and its intercept usually has an effective degree of freedom near zero. Differences
$\hat f_j(a) - \hat f_j(b)$ are unaffected.

## Fitting

For fixed smoothing parameters, a Gaussian additive model is fitted by the closed form above. For other families, the
penalised log-likelihood is maximised by **penalised iteratively reweighted least squares** (PIRLS). Each iteration
solves a penalised weighted least-squares problem,

$$
\betavec^{(t+1)} = \paren{\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat + \Smat_\lambda}^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Wmat\zvec,
$$

where $\zvec$ is the working response and $\Wmat$ the diagonal matrix of working weights of
<NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">IRLS</NoteLink>, both evaluated at $\betavec^{(t)}$.
<NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">Penalised IRLS</NoteLink> derives the step, its convergence and its failure
under separation. The smoothing parameters are chosen in an outer loop by GCV, UBRE or REML; see
<NoteLink to="additive-model-smoothing-parameter-selection">smoothing parameter selection</NoteLink>. Each PIRLS iteration costs
$O(np^2)$ for $n$ observations and $p$ coefficients in total.

## Backfitting and local scoring

Before <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">penalised regression splines</NoteLink> were practical, GAMs were fitted by **backfitting** <Cite id="hastie1986" />.
Setting the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the penalised criterion with respect to one block $\betavec_j$ to zero, with the other blocks
fixed, gives

$$
\hat\fvec_j = \Amat_j\,\rvec_j, \qquad \Amat_j = \Bmat_j\paren{\Bmat_j^\top\Bmat_j + \lambda_j\Smat_j}^{-1}\Bmat_j^\top,
\qquad \rvec_j = \yvec - \hat\beta_0\ones - \sum_{k \ne j}\hat\fvec_k.
$$

$\Amat_j$ is the one-dimensional smoother of term $j$ and $\rvec_j$ is the **partial residual**: the response with
every other term removed. Backfitting sets $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y$, then cycles through the terms, replacing each
$\hat\fvec_j$ by $\Amat_j\rvec_j$ minus its mean, until nothing changes. Each update exactly minimises the convex
criterion over one block, so backfitting is <NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">block coordinate descent</NoteLink> and the criterion never increases. For symmetric
smoothers with eigenvalues in $[0, 1]$, such as <NoteLink to="smoothing-and-penalised-splines">penalised splines</NoteLink>, it converges to a solution of the joint problem
<Cite id="buja1989" />. Any linear smoother can play the role of $\Amat_j$, including
<NoteLink to="local-regression">local regression</NoteLink>. For GLM families, **local scoring** runs a weighted
backfit on the working response inside each IRLS iteration <Cite id="hastie1990" />.

Backfitting converges slowly when one feature is close to a smooth function of another (**concurvity**; see
<NoteLink to="interpreting-generalised-additive-models">interpreting GAMs</NoteLink>). It also gives no direct route to <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> or
to the automatic choice of several smoothing parameters. Solving for all coefficients at once, as mgcv and pyGAM do,
provides both <Cite id="wood2011" />.

## Assumptions

- The effects of the features on $g(\mu)$ add up. An interaction that is not in the model is absorbed into the other
  terms or left in the residual.
- Each $f_j$ is smooth at the scale set by its basis dimension and penalty.
- The response family and link are correct, and the observations are independent given the features, as in any GLM.

## Pitfalls

- **Basis too small.** The penalty can only remove flexibility. If a term's effective degrees of freedom are close to
  its number of free coefficients, the basis may be limiting the fit; see
  <NoteLink to="additive-model-checking-and-term-selection">model checking</NoteLink>.
- **Extrapolation.** Outside the data, a penalised cubic spline continues along its end slope. Predictions there follow
  the penalty's null space, not the data.
- **Uncentred term plots.** Software that does not centre its smooths, such as pyGAM, shows each term up to an
  arbitrary constant. Compare differences, not levels.
- **Reading effects causally.** An additive term shows how the prediction changes with $x_j$ with the other features
  held fixed. With correlated features, that is not what happens when $x_j$ changes in the world.
