<Definition>
A **naive Bayes** classifier models the joint distribution of a class $y \in \set{1, \dots, K}$ and features
$\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_d)$ as

$$
p(y = k, \xvec) = \pi_k \prod_{j=1}^{d} p(x_j \mid y = k),
$$

with class priors $\pi_k$ and one univariate distribution per feature and class. The product is the assumption that
the features are **conditionally independent given the class**. It classifies a new $\xvec$ by the largest posterior
$p(y = k \mid \xvec)$, computed by <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## The decision rule

The posterior is the joint divided by $p(\xvec) = \sum_{k'} p(y = k', \xvec)$, which does not depend on $k$. The
most probable class therefore maximises the joint. Taking logarithms, which preserves the maximiser and turns the
product into a sum,

$$
\hat y(\xvec) = \argmax_k \Big[\log \pi_k + \sum_{j=1}^{d}\log p(x_j \mid y = k)\Big].
$$

Each feature adds its own log-likelihood term, independently of the others. For two classes the decision reads off the
**log-odds**

$$
\log\frac{p(y = 1 \mid \xvec)}{p(y = 0 \mid \xvec)} = \log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0} + \sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\frac{p(x_j \mid y = 1)}{p(x_j \mid y = 0)},
$$

a sum of one log-likelihood ratio per feature, which the classifier compares with zero.

## Why the assumption helps

Without it, $p(\xvec \mid y)$ is a joint distribution over $d$ features. For $d$ binary features that is
$2^d - 1$ parameters per class, far too many to estimate. Under conditional independence it is $d$ parameters per
class. Each factor $p(x_j \mid y)$ is estimated from the one-dimensional data of feature $j$ in class $k$, so naive
Bayes needs little data and trains in a single pass that costs $O(nd)$ for $n$ examples.

## Three event models

The name covers a family of models that differ in the choice of $p(x_j \mid y)$.

### Gaussian naive Bayes

For real features, $p(x_j \mid y = k) = \Gauss(x_j \mid \mu_{kj}, \sigma_{kj}^2)$, with the per-class sample mean and
variance of feature $j$. The log-likelihood $-\frac{(x_j - \mu_{kj})^2}{2\sigma_{kj}^2} - \log\sigma_{kj}$ is
quadratic in $x_j$, so the boundary between two classes is a quadric with axis-aligned terms only. If each feature's
variance is shared across classes, the $x_j^2$ terms cancel in the log-odds and the boundary is linear. This is
<NoteLink to="quadratic-discriminant-analysis">QDA</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">LDA</NoteLink> with diagonal covariance matrices.

### Multinomial naive Bayes

For text, let $x_w$ be the count of word $w$ from a vocabulary of $V$ words in a document. Class $k$ generates each
word position independently from a categorical distribution $\thetavec_k = (\theta_{k1}, \dots, \theta_{kV})$, so
$p(\xvec \mid y = k) \propto \prod_w \theta_{kw}^{x_w}$ and the log-posterior is

$$
\log\pi_k + \sum_{w=1}^{V} x_w \log\theta_{kw} + \text{const},
$$

linear in the counts <Cite id="mccallum1998" />. The maximum-likelihood estimate of $\thetavec_k$ maximises
$\sum_w N_{kw}\log\theta_{kw}$ subject to $\sum_w \theta_{kw} = 1$, where $N_{kw}$ is the total count of word $w$ in
class $k$'s training documents. A Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ gives $N_{kw}/\theta_{kw} = \lambda$ for every $w$, so
$\theta_{kw} \propto N_{kw}$ and $\hat\theta_{kw} = N_{kw}/N_k$ with $N_k = \sum_w N_{kw}$.

A word never seen in class $k$ gets $\hat\theta_{kw} = 0$. One occurrence of it in a test document then sets the class's
likelihood to zero, whatever the other words say. **Additive (Laplace) smoothing** adds a pseudo-count $\alpha > 0$
to every word:

$$
\hat\theta_{kw} = \frac{N_{kw} + \alpha}{N_k + \alpha V}.
$$

This is the posterior mean of $\thetavec_k$ under a symmetric Dirichlet prior with parameter $\alpha$. The choice
$\alpha = 1$ is Laplace's rule of succession.

### Bernoulli naive Bayes

Here $x_w \in \set{0, 1}$ records whether word $w$ appears, and $p(x_w = 1 \mid y = k) = \theta_{kw}$. Unlike the
multinomial model, the likelihood includes a factor $1 - \theta_{kw}$ for every absent word, so absence is evidence.
McCallum and Nigam found the multinomial model better on most text collections, especially with large vocabularies
<Cite id="mccallum1998" />.

## Worked example

A spam filter has a vocabulary of three words: *free*, *meeting* and *money*. The training set has 4 spam and 6 ham
documents, so $\pi_{\text{spam}} = 0.4$. The word counts summed over each class are $(8, 1, 6)$ for spam and
$(1, 9, 2)$ for ham. With $\alpha = 1$ and $V = 3$,

$$
\hat\thetavec_{\text{spam}} = \paren{\tfrac{9}{18}, \tfrac{2}{18}, \tfrac{7}{18}}, \qquad
\hat\thetavec_{\text{ham}} = \paren{\tfrac{2}{15}, \tfrac{10}{15}, \tfrac{3}{15}}.
$$

The per-word log-likelihood ratios $\log(\hat\theta_{\text{spam},w}/\hat\theta_{\text{ham},w})$ are $1.322$ for
*free*, $-1.792$ for *meeting* and $0.665$ for *money*. A new document "free money money" has counts $(1, 0, 2)$ and
log-odds

$$
\log\frac{0.4}{0.6} + 1 \cdot 1.322 + 2 \cdot 0.665 = -0.405 + 1.322 + 1.330 = 2.246,
$$

a spam probability of $1/(1 + e^{-2.246}) = 0.904$.

## Good at ranking, poor at probabilities

Conditional independence rarely holds. Correlated features are then counted as independent evidence. Suppose one
feature has a likelihood ratio of 3 between the classes and equal priors: the correct posterior is $3/4 = 0.75$. If
the data contain three identical copies of the feature, naive Bayes multiplies three ratios of 3 and reports
$27/28 = 0.964$. The log-odds are tripled.

The class decision is unchanged, because tripling the log-odds does not change its sign. So is the ordering of any
set of examples whose features are all duplicated alike, because tripling is monotone. Domingos and Pazzani show
that naive Bayes is optimal under 0–1 loss in many situations where its independence assumption is badly violated,
because only the arg max of the posterior has to be right, not its value <Cite id="domingos1997" />. Hand and Yu
review why the "idiot's Bayes" performs well in practice <Cite id="hand2001" />.

The probabilities, however, are pushed towards 0 and 1. Naive Bayes is typically overconfident, and its scores need
recalibration, for example by <NoteLink to="isotonic-calibration">isotonic regression</NoteLink>, before being used as probabilities <Cite id="zadrozny2001" />.
Real correlations are uneven across features, so the ranking is also distorted, but less than the probabilities.

## Assumptions

- Features are conditionally independent given the class.
- Each $p(x_j \mid y)$ has the chosen parametric form: Gaussian, categorical or Bernoulli.
- The training class frequencies estimate the deployment priors $\pi_k$. If they differ, adjust $\log\pi_k$.

## Pitfalls

- **Zero counts.** Without smoothing, one unseen feature value vetoes a class.
- **Overconfidence.** Do not threshold naive Bayes probabilities as if calibrated.
- **Redundant features.** Many near-copies of one signal swamp the others. Feature selection helps more than for
  discriminative models. <NoteLink to="tree-augmented-naive-bayes">Tree-augmented naive Bayes</NoteLink> conditions
  each feature on one other feature, which discounts a copy of its parent.
- **Asymptotic accuracy.** With enough data, a discriminative model such as
  <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> with the same linear form usually wins; with little
  data naive Bayes often wins. See <NoteLink to="generative-vs-discriminative">generative versus discriminative
  classifiers</NoteLink> <Cite id="ng2002" />.
