<Definition>
**Linear discriminant analysis** (LDA) assumes that the features $\xvec \in \reals^d$ of class $k \in \set{1, \dots, K}$
follow a <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate Gaussian</NoteLink> $\Gauss(\muvec_k, \Sigmamat)$ with a class-specific mean and a <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">covariance matrix</NoteLink>
$\Sigmamat$ common to all classes, and that the classes have priors $\pi_k$. It classifies by the largest posterior
$p(y = k \mid \xvec)$. Under this model, the boundary between any two classes is a hyperplane.
</Definition>

## Deriving the linear boundary

By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, $p(y = k \mid \xvec) \propto \pi_k\,
\Gauss(\xvec \mid \muvec_k, \Sigmamat)$. Take logarithms and write out the Gaussian density:

$$
\log p(y = k \mid \xvec) = \log\pi_k - \frac{1}{2}(\xvec - \muvec_k)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec_k)
- \frac{1}{2}\log\det(2\pi\Sigmamat) - \log p(\xvec).
$$

The last two terms are the same for every class, because $\Sigmamat$ is shared. Expand the <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink>:

$$
(\xvec - \muvec_k)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec_k) = \xvec^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\xvec - 2\xvec^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\muvec_k + \muvec_k^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\muvec_k.
$$

The term $\xvec^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\xvec$ does not depend on $k$ either. Dropping every class-independent term leaves
the **linear discriminant function**

$$
\delta_k(\xvec) = \xvec^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\muvec_k - \frac{1}{2}\muvec_k^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}\muvec_k + \log\pi_k,
$$

and LDA predicts $\argmax_k \delta_k(\xvec)$. Each $\delta_k$ is affine in $\xvec$. The boundary between classes $k$
and $l$ is the set where $\delta_k(\xvec) = \delta_l(\xvec)$:

$$
\wvec^\top\xvec + b = 0, \qquad \wvec = \Sigmamat^{-1}(\muvec_k - \muvec_l), \qquad
b = -\frac{1}{2}(\muvec_k + \muvec_l)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\muvec_k - \muvec_l) + \log\frac{\pi_k}{\pi_l},
$$

a hyperplane. The shared covariance is what makes it linear. With class-specific covariances the quadratic terms no
longer cancel; see <NoteLink to="quadratic-discriminant-analysis">quadratic discriminant analysis</NoteLink>.

For two classes the log-odds is $\delta_1(\xvec) - \delta_0(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$, so

$$
p(y = 1 \mid \xvec) = \sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec + b),
$$

the same functional form as <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>. The two methods differ
in how they estimate $\wvec$ and $b$: LDA from the class means and covariance, logistic regression from the
conditional likelihood of the labels. See <NoteLink to="generative-vs-discriminative">generative versus
discriminative classifiers</NoteLink>.

## Estimation

The maximum-likelihood estimates are the class frequencies $\hat\pi_k = n_k/n$, the class means
$\hat\muvec_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{y_i = k}\xvec_i$, and the **pooled** within-class covariance

$$
\hat\Sigmamat = \frac{1}{n - K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{y_i = k}(\xvec_i - \hat\muvec_k)(\xvec_i - \hat\muvec_k)^\top,
$$

where the divisor $n - K$ rather than $n$ makes the estimate unbiased. The model has $K d$ mean parameters and
$d(d + 1)/2$ covariance parameters in total, independent of $K$ for the covariance.

## Worked example

Two classes with equal priors have means $\muvec_0 = (0, 0)$ and $\muvec_1 = (2, 1)$ and shared covariance
$\Sigmamat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Its inverse is
$\Sigmamat^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.75}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. The boundary normal is

$$
\wvec = \Sigmamat^{-1}(\muvec_1 - \muvec_0) = \tfrac{1}{0.75}(2 - 0.5,\ -1 + 1) = (2, 0),
$$

and $b = -\frac{1}{2}(\muvec_0 + \muvec_1)^\top\wvec = -\frac{1}{2}(2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 0) = -2$. The boundary is the
vertical line $x_1 = 1$. It is not perpendicular to the difference of means $(2, 1)$: the correlation in $\Sigmamat$
tilts it. Classifying by the nearest mean in Euclidean distance would use the perpendicular bisector instead and make
more errors.

The error rate of this rule is $\Phi(-\Delta/2)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function and
$\Delta^2 = (\muvec_1 - \muvec_0)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\muvec_1 - \muvec_0)$ is the squared **Mahalanobis distance**
between the means. Here $\Delta^2 = (2, 1)\cdot(2, 0) = 4$, so the error is $\Phi(-1) = 0.159$.

<Derivation title="The error rate of two-class LDA">
Under class $k$, the score $s = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$ is a linear function of a Gaussian vector, so it is Gaussian with
mean $\wvec^\top\muvec_k + b$ and variance $\wvec^\top\Sigmamat\wvec$. With $\wvec = \Sigmamat^{-1}(\muvec_1 - \muvec_0)$,
the variance is $(\muvec_1 - \muvec_0)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\muvec_1 - \muvec_0) = \Delta^2$. With equal priors,
$b = -\frac{1}{2}(\muvec_0 + \muvec_1)^\top\wvec$, so the mean of $s$ is $\wvec^\top(\muvec_1 - \muvec_0)/2 = \Delta^2/2$
under class 1 and $-\Delta^2/2$ under class 0. A class-1 point is misclassified when $s < 0$, which has probability
$\Phi\big((0 - \Delta^2/2)/\Delta\big) = \Phi(-\Delta/2)$. By symmetry the same holds for class 0.
</Derivation>

## Fisher's criterion

Fisher derived the same direction without any Gaussian assumption <Cite id="fisher1936" />. He sought the projection
$z = \wvec^\top\xvec$ that best separates two classes: one that places the projected class means far apart relative
to the spread within each class. With sample means $\mvec_0$ and $\mvec_1$ and within-class scatter matrix
$\Smat_W = \sum_{k}\sum_{y_i = k}(\xvec_i - \mvec_k)(\xvec_i - \mvec_k)^\top$, the projected means differ by
$\wvec^\top(\mvec_1 - \mvec_0)$ and the projected within-class scatter is $\wvec^\top\Smat_W\wvec$. Fisher's criterion is
their ratio,

$$
J(\wvec) = \frac{\big(\wvec^\top(\mvec_1 - \mvec_0)\big)^2}{\wvec^\top\Smat_W\wvec}.
$$

$J$ does not change when $\wvec$ is scaled, so only its direction matters. Write $\dvec = \mvec_1 - \mvec_0$. Setting
the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> to zero,

$$
\nabla J = \frac{2(\wvec^\top\dvec)\,\dvec\,(\wvec^\top\Smat_W\wvec) - 2(\wvec^\top\dvec)^2\,\Smat_W\wvec}{(\wvec^\top\Smat_W\wvec)^2} = \zeros
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Smat_W\wvec = \frac{\wvec^\top\Smat_W\wvec}{\wvec^\top\dvec}\,\dvec.
$$

The fraction is a scalar, so $\Smat_W\wvec$ is parallel to $\dvec$ and

$$
\wvec \propto \Smat_W^{-1}(\mvec_1 - \mvec_0).
$$

Since $\hat\Sigmamat = \Smat_W/(n - 2)$, this is the direction of LDA's boundary normal. Fisher's argument gives the
direction only; the threshold on $z$ is a separate choice. The Gaussian model supplies the threshold that minimises the
error under its assumptions, including the prior term $\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)$ <Cite id="bishop2006" />.

## Reduced-rank LDA

With $K$ classes, the $K$ means span an affine subspace of dimension at most $K - 1$. After whitening by
$\Sigmamat^{-1/2}$, classification by $\delta_k$ is nearest-mean classification (with prior offsets), and distances
orthogonal to the subspace of the means are the same for every class. LDA therefore needs only the $K - 1$
coordinates within that subspace. Fisher's criterion generalises to the between-class scatter $\Smat_B$: the
directions maximising $\wvec^\top\Smat_B\wvec / \wvec^\top\Smat_W\wvec$ are the leading eigenvectors of
$\Smat_W^{-1}\Smat_B$, found in order of separation. Projecting onto the first two gives the best two-dimensional view
of class separation, a supervised alternative to principal components <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

## Assumptions

- Each class is Gaussian. The boundary remains a sensible linear rule when this fails mildly, and Fisher's derivation
  shows the direction has a meaning without it.
- All classes share one covariance. If they do not, the optimal boundary is quadratic.
- $\hat\Sigmamat$ is invertible, which requires $n - K \ge d$ and no collinear features.

## Pitfalls

- **High dimensions.** With $d$ close to $n$, $\hat\Sigmamat$ is nearly singular and its inverse amplifies noise.
  Shrink it towards a diagonal or scaled identity matrix, as in regularised discriminant analysis
  <Cite id="friedman1989" />.
- **Outliers.** Means and covariances are not robust, and one extreme point can tilt the boundary.
- **Unequal covariances.** When one class has much larger spread than another, the Bayes boundary is quadratic and
  the linear boundary from a pooled covariance departs from it, as the figure shows.
- **Name clash.** In topic modelling, LDA means latent Dirichlet allocation, an unrelated model.
