<Definition>
**Gradient boosting** fits a model $F(\xvec)$ that minimises a differentiable loss $\sum_{i=1}^n L(y_i, F(\xvec_i))$.
It starts from a constant $F_0$ and, for $m = 1, \dots, M$, computes the **pseudo-residuals**

$$
r_{im} = -\frac{\partial L(y_i, F)}{\partial F}\bigg|_{F = F_{m-1}(\xvec_i)},
$$

fits a regression tree $h_m$ to the pairs $(\xvec_i, r_{im})$ by least squares, and updates
$F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m$ with a learning rate $0 < \nu \le 1$. The prediction is $F_M(\xvec)$
<Cite id="friedman2001" />.
</Definition>

## Gradient descent in function space

Consider the training loss as a function of the $n$ predicted values $\fvec = (F(\xvec_1), \dots, F(\xvec_n))$ alone:
$\Lcal(\fvec) = \sum_i L(y_i, F_i)$. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on these $n$ numbers
moves them against the gradient,

$$
\fvec_m = \fvec_{m-1} - \rho_m \gvec_m, \qquad g_{im} = \frac{\partial L(y_i, F_i)}{\partial F_i}\bigg|_{\fvec_{m-1}}.
$$

This defines a model only at the training inputs. It says nothing about a new $\xvec$, and with enough steps it
reproduces the training targets exactly. Gradient boosting constrains each step to a function from a base class, a
small regression tree, that is defined everywhere. It picks the tree whose values at the training inputs are closest
in squared error to the negative gradient $-\gvec_m$: the least-squares fit of a tree to the pseudo-residuals
$r_{im} = -g_{im}$. This tree is the step direction. A line search then chooses the step length
<Cite id="friedman2001" />.

A tree with $J$ leaves predicts one constant per leaf region $R_{jm}$. Friedman refines the line search per leaf,
choosing each leaf's value to minimise the actual loss in that leaf:

$$
\gamma_{jm} = \argmin_\gamma \sum_{\xvec_i \in R_{jm}} L\big(y_i, F_{m-1}(\xvec_i) + \gamma\big).
$$

The tree's structure comes from the gradient; its leaf values come from the loss. The update is
$F_m(\xvec) = F_{m-1}(\xvec) + \nu \sum_j \gamma_{jm}\indicator[\xvec \in R_{jm}]$. The initial model is the best
constant, $F_0 = \argmin_c \sum_i L(y_i, c)$.

## Pseudo-residuals for common losses

**Squared error**, $L = \frac{1}{2}(y - F)^2$. The derivative with respect to $F$ is $-(y - F)$, so the pseudo-residual
is the ordinary residual $r_i = y_i - F_{m-1}(\xvec_i)$. The best leaf value is the mean residual in the leaf, which is
what the least-squares tree already stores. Gradient boosting with squared error is repeated fitting of trees to
residuals, and $F_0$ is the mean of $y$.

**Absolute error**, $L = \abs{y - F}$. The derivative is $-\sgn(y - F)$, so the pseudo-residual is
$r_i = \sgn(y_i - F_{m-1}(\xvec_i))$, which is $\pm 1$. The tree is fitted to signs only, so a point's influence on the
structure does not depend on how far off it is. The leaf value minimises
$\sum_{i \in R_{jm}}\abs{y_i - F_{m-1}(\xvec_i) - \gamma}$. The subgradient of this sum in $\gamma$ is the number of
residuals below $\gamma$ minus the number above, which changes sign at the median, so $\gamma_{jm}$ is the median of the
leaf's residuals. $F_0$ is the median of $y$. Both steps ignore the size of outliers, which makes this loss robust.

**Log loss** for $y \in \set{0, 1}$, with $F$ the log-odds and $p = \sigma(F) = 1/(1 + e^{-F})$. The loss is
$L = -y\log p - (1 - y)\log(1 - p) = \log(1 + e^F) - yF$. Its derivative is $\sigma(F) - y = p - y$, so the
pseudo-residual is

$$
r_i = y_i - p_i,
$$

the difference between the label and the current predicted probability. The leaf value has no closed form. One Newton
step from $\gamma = 0$ uses the second derivative $\partial^2 L/\partial F^2 = p(1 - p)$ and gives

$$
\gamma_{jm} = \frac{\sum_{i \in R_{jm}} (y_i - p_i)}{\sum_{i \in R_{jm}} p_i(1 - p_i)}.
$$

$F_0$ is the log-odds of the base rate. This is the model of <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic
regression</NoteLink> with the linear score replaced by a sum of trees. For $K$ classes, $K$ trees are fitted per stage,
one per class score, with pseudo-residuals $\indicator[y_i = k] - p_{ik}$ from the softmax.

### Worked example: one log-loss stage

Four of ten training labels are 1, so $F_0 = \log(0.4/0.6) = -0.405$ and every $p_i = 0.4$. A leaf holds three
points with labels 1, 1 and 0. Their pseudo-residuals are $0.6$, $0.6$ and $-0.4$, summing to $0.8$. Each has
$p(1 - p) = 0.24$, summing to $0.72$. The Newton leaf value is $\gamma = 0.8/0.72 = 1.111$.

With $\nu = 1$ the leaf's log-odds become $-0.405 + 1.111 = 0.706$ and its probability $0.669$, close to the leaf's
label fraction $2/3$ that an exact line search would reach. With $\nu = 0.1$ the log-odds become $-0.294$ and the
probability $0.427$: the stage moves only a tenth of the way, and later stages continue.

## Shrinkage, subsampling and tree size

<BoostingStages />

- **Shrinkage.** The learning rate $\nu$ scales every step. Friedman found that small values, $\nu \le 0.1$, give
  lower test error than $\nu = 1$, at the cost of proportionally more stages <Cite id="friedman2001" />. Small steps
  make the sequence of models change slowly, so the number of stages $M$ becomes a fine-grained regulariser. For
  linear base learners, forward stagewise fitting with infinitesimal steps traces a path close to the lasso path
  <Cite id="efron2004" />.
- **Number of stages.** Unlike <NoteLink to="random-forest">random forests</NoteLink>, boosting overfits as $M$ grows,
  because each stage reduces training loss further. $M$ is chosen by
  <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> on a validation set.
- **Subsampling.** **Stochastic gradient boosting** fits each tree to a random fraction of the training set, drawn
  without replacement, typically one half. This reduces variance and training time, and in Friedman's experiments
  improved accuracy <Cite id="friedman2002" />.
- **Tree size.** A tree with $J$ leaves can represent interactions among at most $J - 1$ features. Stumps ($J = 2$)
  give an additive model with no interactions. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman find $4 \le J \le 8$ works well in most
  applications <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

## Relation to AdaBoost

With the exponential loss $L = e^{-yF}$ for $y \in \set{-1, +1}$, the pseudo-residuals are
$r_i = y_i w_i$ with $w_i = e^{-y_i F(\xvec_i)}$, the label times the AdaBoost weight. Fit a classifier
$G(\xvec) \in \set{-1, +1}$ to them by least squares. Since $G_i^2 = 1$ and $y_i^2 = 1$,

$$
\sum_i (y_i w_i - G_i)^2 = \text{const} - 2\sum_i w_i y_i G_i = \text{const} - 2\sum_i w_i + 4\sum_i w_i\indicator[y_i \ne G_i],
$$

so the best $G$ minimises the weighted error, exactly the step of <NoteLink to="adaboost">AdaBoost</NoteLink>. Gradient
boosting generalises the AdaBoost recipe from one loss to any differentiable loss <Cite id="friedman2000" />.

## Assumptions

- The loss is differentiable in $F$, or has a subgradient as for absolute error.
- The base learner is a regression tree for every loss, including classification losses, because pseudo-residuals are
  real numbers.

## Pitfalls

- **Overfitting with $M$.** Test error has a minimum in $M$. Always monitor a validation set.
- **Learning rate and $M$ trade off.** Halving $\nu$ roughly doubles the $M$ needed. Tune them together, or fix a small
  $\nu$ and early-stop.
- **Extrapolation.** A sum of trees is constant beyond the range of the training inputs.
- **Sequential training.** Stages cannot be fitted in parallel. Libraries parallelise within a tree instead; see
  <NoteLink to="xgboost-lightgbm-catboost">XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost</NoteLink>.
