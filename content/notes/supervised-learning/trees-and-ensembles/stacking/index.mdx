<Definition>
**Stacking** (stacked generalisation) trains $L$ base models on a training set and a **meta-model** that predicts the
target from the base models' predictions. The meta-model is trained on **out-of-fold** predictions: for each training
point, each base model's prediction comes from a copy of that model fitted without the point. At test time the base
models, refitted on all the data, feed their predictions to the meta-model <Cite id="wolpert1992" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Several models fitted to the same data, say a linear model, a random forest and a nearest-neighbour regressor, make
different errors. A weighted combination can beat each of them. The weights must be learnt from data, and the obvious
choice of data, the training set, is wrong. On its own training points, a 1-nearest-neighbour regressor predicts every
target exactly. A meta-model fitted to in-sample predictions would give it all the weight, although its test error
may be the worst of the three. Weights must be fitted to predictions on points the base models did not train on.

## Mechanism

1. Split the $n$ training points into $K$ folds.
2. For each fold $k$ and each base model $l$, fit model $l$ on the other $K - 1$ folds and predict the points in fold
   $k$. After all folds, every training point $i$ has an out-of-fold prediction $\hat f_l^{(-i)}(\xvec_i)$ from each
   model.
3. Fit the meta-model to the $n$ pairs $\big((\hat f_1^{(-i)}(\xvec_i), \dots, \hat f_L^{(-i)}(\xvec_i)),\ y_i\big)$.
4. Refit each base model on all $n$ points. Predict a new $\xvec$ by applying the meta-model to their predictions.

The meta-model is usually simple. Breiman found that for regression a linear combination with **non-negative**
weights works well and that unconstrained weights often do not, because correlated base predictions give unstable,
large weights of opposite sign <Cite id="breiman1996b" />. For classification the meta-model is often a
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on the base models' predicted probabilities or
log-odds.

### Why out-of-fold predictions work

The meta-model estimates how well each base model generalises and how their errors co-vary. Out-of-fold predictions
are predictions on unseen points, so their errors behave like test errors, apart from each model being trained on
$(K - 1)/K$ of the data. Van der Laan, Polley and Hubbard proved an oracle property for this procedure, which they
call the **super learner**: asymptotically, the cross-validated choice of combination performs as well as the best
combination that could be chosen with knowledge of the true distribution <Cite id="vanderlaan2007" />.

### Worked example: two models

Take two regression models whose out-of-fold errors $e_1$ and $e_2$ have zero mean, variances $\sigma_1^2$ and
$\sigma_2^2$, and covariance $\sigma_{12}$. The combination $w\hat f_1 + (1 - w)\hat f_2$ has error
$we_1 + (1 - w)e_2$, with variance

$$
V(w) = w^2\sigma_1^2 + (1 - w)^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w(1 - w)\sigma_{12}.
$$

Setting $V'(w) = 2w\sigma_1^2 - 2(1 - w)\sigma_2^2 + 2(1 - 2w)\sigma_{12}$ to zero gives

$$
w^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_{12}}.
$$

With $\sigma_1^2 = 1$, $\sigma_2^2 = 1.5$ and $\sigma_{12} = 0.5$, the weight is $w^* = 1/1.5 = 0.667$ and the combined
error variance is $0.444 + 0.167 + 0.222 = 0.833$, below the better model's $1$. The weaker model still earns a third
of the weight because its errors are only partly correlated with the stronger model's. If $\sigma_{12}$ equals
$\sigma_1^2$, then $w^* = 1$: the second model adds nothing. A least-squares meta-model estimates these quantities from
the out-of-fold predictions.

## Cost

With $K$ folds and $L$ base models, stacking fits $KL$ models for the out-of-fold predictions, $L$ refits on all the
data and one meta-model. Prediction evaluates all $L$ base models. Cross-validating the whole stack to estimate its
own test error nests another loop of folds around this.

## Trade-offs

- **Blending** replaces the $K$ folds by one held-out split: the base models are fitted on one part and the meta-model
  on their predictions for the other. It is cheaper and simpler, and it uses less data for each stage.
- **Diversity matters more than number.** Stacking gains most from base models with different inductive biases, whose
  errors are weakly correlated. Five gradient-boosted models with different seeds add little to one.
- **Leakage through preprocessing.** Every step fitted on the data, such as target encoding or feature selection,
  must be refitted inside each fold. Otherwise the out-of-fold predictions are optimistic and the meta-model is
  miscalibrated.
- **Interpretability and maintenance.** A stack is several models to train, monitor and serve. In production, a
  single well-tuned model is often preferred unless the stack's gain is large.
- **Relation to bagging and boosting.** <NoteLink to="bagging">Bagging</NoteLink> averages many copies of one model
  with fixed equal weights. <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">Boosting</NoteLink> fits models sequentially to each
  other's errors. Stacking learns weights for a few different models fitted independently.
