<Definition>
**Bagging** draws $B$ bootstrap samples $\Dcal^{*1}, \dots, \Dcal^{*B}$, each of $n$ pairs drawn with replacement from
the training set $\Dcal$ of size $n$. It fits a model $\hat f^{*b}$ to each and predicts with their average

$$
\hat f_{\text{bag}}(\xvec) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \hat f^{*b}(\xvec)
$$

for regression, or with a majority vote or averaged class probabilities for classification <Cite id="breiman1996" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A deep <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink> has low bias and high variance: a different training
sample from the same distribution gives a different tree and a different prediction at the same $\xvec$. With many
independent training sets, averaging their models would cancel much of this variance. Only one training set exists.
The bootstrap <Cite id="efron1979" /> simulates new ones by resampling it.

## Mechanism

### Why averaging helps under squared loss

Fix an input $\xvec$ and a target $y$. Let $\varphi(\xvec, \Dcal)$ be the prediction of a model trained on a random
training set $\Dcal$, and $\varphi_A(\xvec) = \expect_\Dcal[\varphi(\xvec, \Dcal)]$ its average over training sets.
Expanding the square around $\varphi_A$, the cross term has expectation zero, so

$$
\expect_\Dcal\brack{(y - \varphi(\xvec, \Dcal))^2} = (y - \varphi_A(\xvec))^2 + \var_\Dcal\brack{\varphi(\xvec, \Dcal)}.
$$

The aggregated predictor $\varphi_A$ has error $(y - \varphi_A)^2$, lower than the average error of a single model by
exactly the model's variance <Cite id="breiman1996" />. The gain is large for unstable procedures, whose predictions
vary a lot between training sets, such as deep trees and subset selection. It is near zero for stable ones, such as
ridge regression or $k$-nearest neighbours with large $k$.

Bagging approximates $\varphi_A$ by averaging over bootstrap samples of the one training set. The approximation
matches $\varphi_A$ in variance reduction only to the extent that bootstrap samples behave like fresh draws. They do
not fully: bootstrap samples of the same $\Dcal$ overlap, so their models are correlated.

### Variance of an average of correlated predictors

Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_B$ be the predictions at $\xvec$ of $B$ bagged models. Over the randomness of both the training
set and the bootstrap draws, they are identically distributed, each with variance $\sigma^2$. Every pair has the same
correlation $\rho$, because every pair shares the training set $\Dcal$ and differs only in its bootstrap draws. The variance of their mean is

$$
\var\paren{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B Z_b} = \frac{1}{B^2}\paren{\sum_{b=1}^B \var(Z_b) + \sum_{b \ne b'} \cov(Z_b, Z_{b'})}
= \frac{1}{B^2}\paren{B\sigma^2 + B(B - 1)\rho\sigma^2}.
$$

Simplifying,

$$
\var\paren{\bar Z} = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2.
$$

The second term vanishes as $B$ grows; the first does not. The variance of an infinite bag is $\rho\sigma^2$, set by
how alike the models are. Adding trees never increases the variance and never causes overfitting in this sense; it
only stops helping. Reducing $\rho$ without increasing $\sigma^2$ much is the idea behind
<NoteLink to="random-forest">random forests</NoteLink> <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

### Worked example

With $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $\rho = 0.5$, a bag of $B = 10$ models has variance $0.5 + 0.5/10 = 0.55$ and an infinite bag
$0.5$. Bagging at most halves the variance. With $\rho = 0.1$, ten models give $0.1 + 0.9/10 = 0.19$ and the floor is
$0.1$. Decorrelating the models matters more than adding models once $B$ is a few times $1/\rho$.

### Bias

Bagging leaves the bias almost unchanged: the expected bagged prediction is the expected prediction of one model fitted
to a bootstrap sample. That model sees on average only $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 1 - e^{-1} \approx 63.2\%$ of the
distinct training points, so its bias can be slightly higher than that of a model fitted to all of $\Dcal$. Bagging a
high-bias model such as a stump gives a smoother high-bias model. Bühlmann and Yu show that for hard threshold
decisions, bagging acts as a smoother: it replaces the step of a split by a smooth ramp, which is where most of its
variance reduction comes from <Cite id="buhlmann2002" />.

### Classification

Two combination rules are common.

- **Majority vote** over the $B$ predicted classes.
- **Probability averaging**: average each model's estimated class probabilities and take the most probable class.

Probability averaging gives smoother, better-ranked scores and is what most libraries do. For 0–1 loss the
squared-loss argument above does not carry over. If $B$ votes at $\xvec$ were independent and each correct with
probability $q$, the majority would be correct with probability tending to 1 as $B$ grows when $q > 0.5$, and tending
to 0 when $q < 0.5$. Bagged votes are correlated, so the effect is weaker, but its direction is the same: bagging
makes a good classifier better and a poor one worse <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

### Out-of-bag error

Each bootstrap sample omits a given training point with probability $(1 - 1/n)^n$, which tends to $e^{-1} \approx
0.368$ as $n$ grows ($0.366$ at $n = 100$). About a third of the models therefore never saw point $i$. Averaging only
those models' predictions at $\xvec_i$ gives an **out-of-bag** prediction for point $i$, and the average loss of these
predictions is the **out-of-bag error**. It estimates the test error without a held-out set or cross-validation. Each
out-of-bag prediction uses about $0.37B$ models rather than $B$, so the estimate is slightly pessimistic for small $B$.

## Cost

Bagging costs $B$ times the training cost of the base model, and $B$ times its prediction cost. The $B$ fits are
independent and parallelise perfectly. The out-of-bag error is a by-product and needs no extra fits.

## Trade-offs

- **Loss of interpretability.** An average of 500 trees is not a readable set of rules.
- **Correlation limits the gain.** Bootstrap samples of the same data share most points, so bagged trees on data with
  one dominant feature all split on it first and stay strongly correlated. Feature subsampling in random forests
  addresses this.
- **Stable models gain nothing.** Bagging a linear least-squares fit returns roughly the same fit at $B$ times the
  cost.
- **Choice of $B$.** More trees never hurt accuracy. $B$ is set by when the out-of-bag error stops falling, usually a
  few hundred.
