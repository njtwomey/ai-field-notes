<Definition>
**Pruning** replaces a subtree of a fitted <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink> by a single leaf.
**Cost-complexity pruning** chooses, for a penalty $\alpha \ge 0$, the subtree $T$ of a large tree $T_0$ that minimises

$$
R_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\abs{T},
$$

where $R(T)$ is the training loss of $T$ and $\abs{T}$ is its number of leaves. **Weakest-link pruning** computes the
minimisers for all $\alpha$ at once, as a nested sequence of subtrees. Cross-validation then picks $\alpha$
<Cite id="breiman1984" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A tree grown until its leaves are pure fits the training labels exactly, including their noise. Its test error is
high because each small leaf rests on a few points. The tree's size must be controlled.

Stopping early, by refusing any split whose impurity decrease is below a threshold, is a poor control.
A split can be worthless alone and valuable for the splits it enables below it. On an XOR pattern, where the
class is $\indicator[x_1 > 0] \oplus \indicator[x_2 > 0]$, no single split on $x_1$ or $x_2$ decreases the impurity at
all, yet two levels of splits separate the classes perfectly. A stopping rule halts at the root. Breiman and
colleagues therefore grow the tree fully and prune it back <Cite id="breiman1984" />.

## Mechanism

### Cost-complexity

Measure $R(T)$ as the training loss summed over leaves and divided by the training size $n$: the misclassification
rate for classification, the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> for regression. The penalty $\alpha\abs{T}$ charges $\alpha$ per leaf.
At $\alpha = 0$ the full tree $T_0$ minimises $R_\alpha$. As $\alpha$ grows, smaller subtrees win. At a large enough
$\alpha$ the root alone, a single leaf, wins.

### The critical penalty of one node

Take an internal node $t$ of the current tree. Write $T_t$ for the branch rooted at $t$, $R(T_t)$ for the training loss
of its leaves and $R(t)$ for the loss that $t$ would have as a single leaf. The rest of the tree is unaffected by
what happens at $t$, so the choice between keeping the branch and collapsing it compares

$$
R(T_t) + \alpha\abs{T_t} \quad \text{with} \quad R(t) + \alpha.
$$

Collapsing is at least as good exactly when $\alpha\,(\abs{T_t} - 1) \ge R(t) - R(T_t)$, that is when

$$
\alpha \ge g(t) = \frac{R(t) - R(T_t)}{\abs{T_t} - 1}.
$$

The ratio $g(t)$ is the reduction in training loss that the branch buys per extra leaf. Splitting never increases
training loss, so $R(t) \ge R(T_t)$ and $g(t) \ge 0$.

### Weakest-link pruning

Weakest-link pruning repeatedly collapses the node with the smallest $g(t)$:

1. Set $k = 0$ and start from $T_0$.
2. Compute $g(t)$ for every internal node of $T_k$.
3. Let $\alpha_{k+1} = \min_t g(t)$. Collapse every node attaining the minimum, giving $T_{k+1}$.
4. Stop at the root; otherwise increase $k$ and return to step 2.

The result is a nested sequence $T_0 \supset T_1 \supset \dots \supset \set{\text{root}}$ with increasing penalties
$0 < \alpha_1 < \alpha_2 < \dots$. For every $\alpha \in [\alpha_k, \alpha_{k+1})$, the subtree $T_k$ is the smallest
minimiser of $R_\alpha$ among all subtrees of $T_0$ <Cite id="breiman1984" />. The optimal subtree for any penalty is
therefore one of at most $\abs{T_0}$ trees, not one of the exponentially many subtrees of $T_0$.

<Derivation title="Why each tree in the sequence beats its own pruned subtrees">
Write $C_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\abs{T}$. Both terms add over disjoint branches, so the cost of a tree is the sum of
the costs of its root's two child branches.

**Claim.** If $\alpha < g(s)$ for every internal node $s$ of a branch $T_t$, then every proper pruned subtree $T'$ of
$T_t$ has $C_\alpha(T') > C_\alpha(T_t)$.

**Proof, by induction on the size of $T_t$.** If $T'$ is the single leaf $t$, then $C_\alpha(T') = R(t) + \alpha$ and
$C_\alpha(T_t) = R(T_t) + \alpha\abs{T_t}$. Their difference is $R(t) - R(T_t) - \alpha(\abs{T_t} - 1)$, which is
positive because $\alpha < g(t)$. Otherwise $T'$ keeps the split at $t$ and prunes the child branches $T_L$ and $T_R$
to $T'_L$ and $T'_R$, at least one of them properly. By additivity,
$C_\alpha(T') = C_\alpha(T'_L) + C_\alpha(T'_R)$. By the induction hypothesis applied to each child branch, each
term is at least the corresponding $C_\alpha(T_L)$ or $C_\alpha(T_R)$, and strictly greater for a properly pruned
child. So $C_\alpha(T') > C_\alpha(T_L) + C_\alpha(T_R) = C_\alpha(T_t)$.

Applied to the whole tree $T_k$, whose internal nodes all have $g(t) \ge \alpha_{k+1}$, the claim shows that no
pruned subtree of $T_k$ beats it for $\alpha < \alpha_{k+1}$. The remaining step, that subtrees of $T_0$ which keep a
branch removed at an earlier step cannot beat $T_k$ either, uses the same additivity and is proved by Breiman et al.
<Cite id="breiman1984" />.
</Derivation>

### Worked example

A tree $T_0$ has root $t_1$ with two internal children $t_2$ and $t_3$. Node $t_2$ has leaves $t_4$ and $t_5$; node
$t_3$ has leaves $t_6$ and $t_7$. The training losses, as fractions of the whole training set, are:

| node        | $t_1$ | $t_2$ | $t_3$ | $t_4$ | $t_5$ | $t_6$ | $t_7$ |
| ----------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| $R$ as leaf | 0.40  | 0.20  | 0.10  | 0.05  | 0.10  | 0.07  | 0.02  |

The full tree has $\abs{T_0} = 4$ leaves and $R(T_0) = 0.05 + 0.10 + 0.07 + 0.02 = 0.24$.

- **Step 1.** $g(t_2) = (0.20 - 0.15)/1 = 0.05$, $g(t_3) = (0.10 - 0.09)/1 = 0.01$ and
  $g(t_1) = (0.40 - 0.24)/3 = 0.053$. The weakest link is $t_3$, so $\alpha_1 = 0.01$. Collapsing it gives $T_1$ with
  leaves $t_4, t_5, t_3$ and $R(T_1) = 0.25$.
- **Step 2.** $g(t_2) = 0.05$ and $g(t_1) = (0.40 - 0.25)/2 = 0.075$. Collapse $t_2$ at $\alpha_2 = 0.05$, giving
  $T_2$ with leaves $t_2, t_3$ and $R(T_2) = 0.30$.
- **Step 3.** $g(t_1) = (0.40 - 0.30)/1 = 0.10$. Collapse the root at $\alpha_3 = 0.10$.

So $T_0$ is optimal for $\alpha < 0.01$, $T_1$ for $0.01 \le \alpha < 0.05$, $T_2$ for $0.05 \le \alpha < 0.10$ and the
root for $\alpha \ge 0.10$. At $\alpha = 0.03$ the penalised losses are $0.36$ for $T_0$, $0.34$ for $T_1$, $0.36$ for
$T_2$ and $0.43$ for the root, confirming $T_1$. The subtree that collapses only $t_2$, with $R = 0.29$ and 3 leaves,
scores $0.38$ and is never optimal, which is why the sequence can skip it.

### Choosing the penalty

The training loss always prefers $T_0$, so $\alpha$ is chosen on held-out data. With $V$-fold cross-validation, grow
and prune a tree on each training fold, evaluate each fold's pruned sequence on its held-out fold at a grid of $\alpha$
values, and average the errors. The final tree is the member of the full-data sequence at the chosen $\alpha$. The
**one-standard-error rule** picks the largest $\alpha$ whose cross-validated error is within one <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the
minimum, which gives a smaller tree with statistically indistinguishable error <Cite id="breiman1984" />.

### Other pruning rules

- **Reduced-error pruning** (Quinlan) collapses a node whenever doing so does not increase the error on a separate
  pruning set. It needs held-out data but no penalty.
- **Pessimistic pruning** in C4.5 replaces each leaf's training error by an <Gloss name="ucb">upper confidence bound</Gloss> on its error rate
  and collapses a branch when the bound for the collapsed node is no worse. It needs no held-out data but its bound
  has no strict statistical justification.

## Cost

Weakest-link pruning visits each internal node a bounded number of times per step. A naive implementation recomputes
all $g(t)$ after every collapse, costing $O(\abs{T_0}^2)$. Updating only the ancestors of the collapsed node reduces
this to $O(\abs{T_0}\, D)$ for depth $D$. Cross-validation multiplies the cost of growing and pruning by the number of
folds.

## Trade-offs

- Pruning reduces variance and coarsens the partition into leaf regions, which adds bias. The pruned tree is still one tree, and its structure still
  changes with small changes in the data.
- <NoteLink to="random-forest">Random forests</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="bagging">bagging</NoteLink> usually leave
  their trees unpruned: averaging removes the variance that pruning would, without the bias.
- In <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient boosting</NoteLink>, each tree is small by design, and the penalty on
  leaves reappears as the $\gamma$ term in the split gain of <NoteLink to="xgboost-lightgbm-catboost">XGBoost</NoteLink>.
