<Definition>
A factorial hidden Markov model has $M$ hidden chains $\yvec^{(1)}, \dots, \yvec^{(M)}$, each a Markov chain over $K$
states with its own initial distribution $\pi_m$ and transition matrix $A_m$. The chains evolve independently, and
the observation at position $n$ depends on all of them:

$$
p\paren{\xvec, \yvec^{(1)}, \dots, \yvec^{(M)}} = \prod_{m=1}^{M} \brack{\pi_m\paren{y^{(m)}_1} \prod_{n=2}^{N}
A_m\paren{y^{(m)}_{n-1}, y^{(m)}_n}} \prod_{n=1}^{N} p\paren{x_n \mid y^{(1)}_n, \dots, y^{(M)}_n}
$$

<Cite id="ghahramani1997" />.
</Definition>

## Why several chains

A single <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">HMM</NoteLink> with $K^M$ states could represent the same joint behaviour,
but it would need a $K^M \times K^M$ transition matrix, and it would lose the fact that the causes are separate. The
factorial model keeps one small transition matrix per cause.

The standard application is energy disaggregation, also called non-intrusive load monitoring. A household meter reads
only the total power, and each appliance switches between states such as off and on. The model infers what each
appliance was doing from the total alone <Cite id="kolter2012" />. In the additive Gaussian form, each chain has a
power level per state and

$$
x_n = \sum_{m=1}^{M} P_m\paren{y_n^{(m)}} + \varepsilon_n, \qquad \varepsilon_n \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2).
$$

## Why exact inference is expensive

The chains are independent in the prior, but not in the posterior. Each observation depends on every chain, so
conditioning on $x_n$ couples them: a high reading makes "the kettle is on" and "the heater is on" competing
explanations. This is explaining away, and it means the posterior does not factorise over chains.

<FhmmGraph />

Exact inference therefore treats the $M$ chains as one HMM over the joint state
$s_n = (y_n^{(1)}, \dots, y_n^{(M)})$, which takes $K^M$ values. Its transition matrix is the Kronecker product
$A = A_1 \otimes \dots \otimes A_M$, and forward–backward costs $O(N K^{2M})$: one $K^M \times K^M$ matrix–vector
product per step.

The Kronecker structure helps but does not remove the exponential. Applying $A^\top$ to a vector of length $K^M$ one
factor at a time touches each of the $K^M$ entries with $K$ values of one chain, $M$ times, which costs
$O(M K^{M+1})$ per step instead of $O(K^{2M})$ <Cite id="ghahramani1997" />. The state space itself still grows as
$K^M$.

## Structured mean field

The standard approximation keeps the structure within each chain and drops the coupling between chains. The
variational family is

$$
q\paren{\yvec^{(1)}, \dots, \yvec^{(M)}} = \prod_{m=1}^{M} q_m\paren{\yvec^{(m)}},
$$

where each $q_m$ is itself an HMM over one chain. By the <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field
update</NoteLink>, the optimal $q_m$ is proportional to $\exp \expect_{q_{-m}}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \yvec)}$, the
expected log joint over the other chains.

For binary chains (off 0, on 1) with power $P_m$ when on, the log likelihood of $x_n$ is
$-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\paren{x_n - P_m y_n^{(m)} - R_n}^2$, where $R_n = \sum_{l \ne m} P_l y_n^{(l)}$ is the power of
the other appliances. Expanding the square and keeping only the terms that involve $y_n^{(m)}$, with
$\paren{y_n^{(m)}}^2 = y_n^{(m)}$ for a binary variable,

$$
\expect_{q_{-m}}\brack{\log p\paren{x_n \mid \cdot}} = \frac{y_n^{(m)}}{\sigma^2}\paren{P_m r_n^{(m)} -
\tfrac12 P_m^2} + \text{const}, \qquad r_n^{(m)} = x_n - \sum_{l \ne m} P_l\, \expect_{q_l}\brack{y_n^{(l)}}.
$$

So $q_m$ is an HMM with chain $m$'s own transitions and a variational emission: node potential 1 for off and
$\exp\brack{(P_m r_n^{(m)} - P_m^2/2)/\sigma^2}$ for on. The residual $r_n^{(m)}$ is the reading with the other
appliances' expected power removed. Each update is one ordinary forward–backward on a $K$-state chain, and a sweep over
all chains costs $O(N M K^2)$. Sweeping until nothing changes is coordinate ascent on the
<NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink>. With vector observations and general emission
weights the same derivation gives a variational emission built from the residual after removing every other chain's
expected contribution <Cite id="ghahramani1997" />.

Gibbs sampling is the other common approach: resample one chain at a time given the others, with forward
filtering–backward sampling on that chain.

## Worked example

Take four appliances: a 150 W fridge, a 1000 W heater, a 2000 W kettle and an 800 W microwave, with switching rates
that make their long-run probabilities of being on 0.44, 0.29, 0.11 and 0.12. A single reading of 1950 W with
$\sigma = 100$ W, ignoring the neighbouring time steps, gives these posteriors over combinations:

| appliances on | total power | posterior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kettle | 2000 W | 0.59 |
| fridge, heater, microwave | 1950 W | 0.24 |
| heater, microwave | 1800 W | 0.10 |
| fridge, kettle | 2150 W | 0.07 |

The combination that matches the reading exactly is not the most probable one. Three appliances being on at once is
less likely a priori than the kettle alone, and a 50 W mismatch costs little when $\sigma = 100$ W. The time
dimension usually resolves the ambiguity: a kettle is on for a few minutes, a heater for much longer, so the
neighbouring readings favour one explanation. That is what the chains' transition matrices contribute.

The figure shows the same effect over time. Exact inference and mean field usually agree, but where the evidence is
ambiguous, mean field can lock onto one explanation with near certainty while the exact posterior is split or favours
the other. Mean field minimises the reverse KL divergence $\KL(q \,\|\, p)$, which is mode-seeking: a factorised $q$
cannot represent "either the kettle, or the heater and the microwave", so it picks one.

## Related models

- **Dynamic Bayesian networks.** A factorial HMM is a dynamic Bayesian network with several hidden variables per time
  step and no edges between chains within a step. Coupled HMMs add such edges.
- **Discriminative counterpart.** A dynamic CRF (see <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field-variants">CRF variants</NoteLink>) models
  several interacting label chains conditionally on the observations, as a factorial HMM does generatively.

## Pitfalls

- **Identifiability.** Appliances with similar power levels, or combinations with similar totals, are hard to
  separate from the total alone. Prior knowledge of each appliance's power and usage pattern is usually needed.
- **Mean-field overconfidence.** The approximate posteriors are too sharp and can be confidently wrong on ambiguous
  stretches, as in the figure. Several restarts, or exact inference when $K^M$ is small, help.
- **Exponential cost.** Exact inference is only practical for a handful of chains; the cost grows as $K^{2M}$.
