<Definition>
A two-stage <Gloss name="svm" />/CRF classifier splits sequence labelling into two learning problems
<Cite id="hoefel2008" />. First, multiclass support vector machines are trained to label each position $\xvec_n$ on its
own, producing a vector of scores $\gvec(\xvec_n)$. Second, a linear-chain conditional random field is trained with
feature functions that depend on the input only through those scores. The SVMs supply accurate local evidence; the CRF
combines it with the dependencies between neighbouring labels.
</Definition>

## Problem

A sequence classifier needs two things: good evidence about each position, and knowledge of which labels tend to follow
which. <NoteLink to="structural-support-vector-machine">Max-margin Markov networks</NoteLink> <Cite id="taskar2003" />
learn both at once, in one large <NoteLink to="quadratic-programming">quadratic program</NoteLink> that couples margin
constraints with output correlations. That quadratic program is hard to scale and needs specialised software. The
two-stage method separates the two jobs. A margin-based classifier is good at the first, a CRF at the second, and each
can be trained with standard software <Cite id="hoefel2008" />.

In a handwritten word, the third and fourth letters of "fern" may look almost identical. A letter classifier alone
may read "fenn" or "ferr". The CRF's edge potentials score the pair "rn" higher than "nn" after "fe", so the
neighbouring labels resolve the ambiguity.

## Mechanism

### Stage one: scores for each position

Each position is classified on its own, as in unstructured learning. With $c$ labels, one-against-one SVMs give
$b = c(c - 1)/2$ scores per position, one for each pair of labels:

$$
\gvec(\xvec_n) = \paren{g_1(\xvec_n), \dots, g_b(\xvec_n)}.
$$

The whole vector is passed on, not only the winning label. A score vector says how confident the classifier is and which
labels it confused, which a single predicted label hides. <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic
regression</NoteLink> can replace the SVMs, giving a vector of $c$ class probabilities instead of $b$ margins.

### Stage two: a CRF on the scores

The CRF is the usual linear chain in the notation of <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random
fields</NoteLink>, with one change: its feature functions see the scores $\gvec(\xvec_n)$, not the raw input. Two
feature types carry them, one for single labels and one for pairs:

$$
f^{(1)}_z\paren{g_z(\xvec_n), y_n}, \qquad f^{(2)}_z\paren{g_z(\xvec_n), y_{n-1}, y_n}.
$$

They enter the node and edge potentials exactly as any other features:

$$
\psivec_n(y) = \exp\set{\sum_j \lambda_j f_j\paren{\emptyset, y, \xvec, n}}, \qquad
\Psimat_n(u, v) = \exp\set{\sum_j \lambda_j f_j\paren{u, v, \xvec, n}},
$$

where the $f_j$ include $f^{(1)}$ and $f^{(2)}$ alongside label-only features such as $f(y_n)$ and
$f(y_{n-1}, y_n)$. With indicator features, each real score is first discretised into a small number of levels, for
example by rounding it to an integer, so that "score $z$ is at level $l$ and the label is $y$" becomes one binary
feature. The weight of that feature learns how much the level is worth as evidence for $y$.

Training maximises the regularised conditional log-likelihood as for any CRF. The
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> for each weight is the observed feature count minus its
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> under the model, and forward–backward supplies the expectation.

### How the uncertainty propagates

The second stage is what turns independent local decisions into a joint one. Forward–backward computes each marginal
from the whole sequence,

$$
P(Y_n) \propto \alphavec_n \odot \psivec_n \odot \betavec_n,
$$

where $\alphavec_n$ summarises the scores before position $n$ and $\betavec_n$ those after it, each passed through
the edge potentials. A position with confident scores keeps its label, because its node potential dominates the
product. A position with ambiguous scores has a flat node potential, and the messages from its neighbours decide it.
Because the CRF sees the whole score vector and not only its maximum, it also learns which second choices of the
first stage tend to be right.

On the handwritten-word OCR benchmark of Taskar et al., the SVM/CRF combination was more accurate than either the SVMs
or a CRF on raw pixels, and matched the reported accuracy of max-margin Markov networks <Cite id="hoefel2008" />.

## Cost

Stage one costs as much as training the per-position classifiers, and one-against-one SVMs each see only two classes.
Stage two costs one forward–backward pass per sequence per gradient step, $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ for a sequence of length
$N$. The feature count is the main expense. With $b$ scores, $L$ levels per score and $c$ labels there are $bLc$
single-label score features and $bLc^2$ pairwise ones. For 26 letters, $b = 325$ pairwise SVMs and 17 levels give about
$3.7$ million pairwise features. Pruning features that never fire in training keeps this manageable.

## Trade-offs

- **Simplicity against joint training.** Each stage uses off-the-shelf software. The price is that stage one is not
  trained to help stage two: its scores are fitted for per-position accuracy, not for the CRF's likelihood.
- **Scores on training data are optimistic.** The stage-one scores on the examples the SVMs were trained on are more
  confident and more often correct than their scores on new data. A CRF trained on them learns to over-trust them. The
  remedy from <NoteLink to="stacking">stacking</NoteLink> is to train the second stage on out-of-fold scores: split the
  training set into folds, and score each fold with SVMs trained on the others <Cite id="wolpert1992" />.
- **Discretisation.** Too fine a binning creates many rare features; too coarse a binning throws away the score's
  magnitude.
- **First-order structure only.** A linear chain captures label pairs. Patterns over three or more labels, such as
  letter trigrams, need higher-order cliques.

## Relation to other ideas

- **Stacking.** The method is stacked generalisation <Cite id="wolpert1992" /> with a structured second stage: the
  first-stage outputs are the second stage's inputs.
- **Logistic regression as a CRF.** Logistic regression is a CRF with no label-pair features <Cite id="twomey2016" />.
  With logistic regression in stage one, stage one is that unstructured CRF, and stage two adds the structure back. See
  <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed">when is structure needed</NoteLink>.
- **Neural emissions.** The same division of labour appears in
  <NoteLink to="bidirectional-lstm-conditional-random-field">BiLSTM-CRF taggers</NoteLink>, where a network produces
  per-position scores and a CRF layer decodes them, and in hybrid speech recognisers that feed neural-network posteriors
  into an <Gloss name="hmm" />. See <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field-variants">CRF variants</NoteLink>.
