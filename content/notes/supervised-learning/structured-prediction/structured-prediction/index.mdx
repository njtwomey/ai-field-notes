<Definition>
Structured prediction is supervised learning where the output is a collection of dependent variables rather than a
single label: a label sequence $\yvec \in \Ycal^{N}$ for an observation sequence $\xvec$, a parse tree for a sentence,
or a label for every pixel of an image. The dependencies between output variables are modelled directly, so the
prediction for one position can use the predictions for its neighbours <Cite id="sutton2012" />.
</Definition>

## The setting

The notation follows Twomey, Diethe and Flach <Cite id="twomey2016" />. A dataset
$\Dcal = \set{(\xvec_m, \yvec_m)}_{m=1}^{M}$ holds $M$ sequences. Sequence $m$ has length $N_m$, its observations form
$\xvec_m \in \reals^{N_m \times D}$, and its labels form $\yvec_m \in \Ycal^{N_m}$; $\xvec_{m,n}$ and $\yvec_{m,n}$ are
the observation and label at position $n$. Examples:

- **Part-of-speech tagging:** $\xvec_m$ is a sentence and $\yvec_{m,n}$ the grammatical tag of word $n$.
- **Activity recognition:** $\xvec_m$ is a stream of sensor readings and $\yvec_{m,n}$ the activity at time $n$.
- **Speech and biological sequences:** phonemes from audio frames, or gene structure from DNA.

Neighbouring labels are strongly related: a determiner is usually followed by a noun or an adjective, and an activity
usually continues. Predicting each position independently discards this information. Whether that costs anything in
practice depends on the data, which <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed">when is structure needed</NoteLink>
analyses.

## The family

| model | models | normalisation | trained by | note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| logistic regression | $P(y_n \mid \xvec)$ per position | per position | log-loss | the unstructured baseline |
| hidden Markov model | $p(\xvec, \yvec)$ | local, generative | maximum likelihood, Baum–Welch | <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">HMM</NoteLink> |
| factorial HMM | $p(\xvec, \yvec^{(1)}, \dots, \yvec^{(M)})$ | local, generative | maximum likelihood, approximate | <NoteLink to="factorial-hidden-markov-model">FHMM</NoteLink> |
| maximum-entropy Markov model | $\prod_n P(y_n \mid y_{n-1}, \xvec)$ | local, discriminative | log-loss per position | <NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-markov-model">MEMM</NoteLink> |
| linear-chain CRF | $P(\yvec \mid \xvec)$ | global | log-loss over sequences | <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">CRF</NoteLink> |
| structural SVM | a score $\lambdavec^\top \Phi(\xvec, \yvec)$ | none | structured hinge loss | <NoteLink to="structural-svm">structural SVM</NoteLink> |

Three distinctions organise the table.

- **Generative or discriminative.** A generative model describes how the observations arise, $p(\xvec, \yvec)$, and
  obtains $p(\yvec \mid \xvec)$ by Bayes' theorem. A discriminative model describes $p(\yvec \mid \xvec)$ or a score
  directly, so its features can depend on the whole of $\xvec$ in overlapping ways without a model of $\xvec$.
- **Local or global normalisation.** Locally normalised models multiply conditional distributions, one per position.
  Globally normalised models divide a product of potentials by one normaliser for the whole sequence. Local
  normalisation in a discriminative model causes label bias, as the MEMM note shows.
- **Likelihood or margin.** CRFs maximise the conditional likelihood. Structural SVMs instead require the true
  labelling to beat every other labelling by a margin that grows with how wrong the other labelling is.

## Shared machinery

On a chain, every model in the family scores a labelling by a sum of terms that each involve at most two neighbouring
labels: node terms and edge terms, or in multiplicative form, node potentials $\psivec_n$ and edge potentials
$\Psimat_n$. That shape is what makes exact inference tractable.

- **Marginals and the normaliser** come from the sum-product recursion, forward–backward, in $O(N \abs{\Ycal}^2)$
  time instead of $O(\abs{\Ycal}^N)$. The derivation is in the hidden Markov model note and applies unchanged to CRFs.
- **The best labelling** comes from the max-product recursion, Viterbi, which replaces each sum by a maximum.
- **Trees** allow the same recursions from the leaves inwards, belief propagation. Graphs with cycles, such as image
  grids, need approximations such as loopy belief propagation, mean field, or graph cuts.

## Generative–discriminative pairs

Each generative model has a discriminative twin with the same graph. Naive Bayes and logistic regression are one pair:
fitting a logistic regression to the same features gives the conditional distribution that naive Bayes implies, but
with parameters chosen to predict the labels rather than to model the inputs. HMMs and linear-chain CRFs are the
sequence version of the same pair <Cite id="sutton2012" />.

The generative member usually learns faster from little data, and the discriminative member usually reaches lower
error with enough data, because it spends no parameters modelling $\xvec$ <Cite id="ng2002" />. Logistic regression is
itself the special case of a linear-chain CRF with no features that link neighbouring labels, which is the starting
point for asking when the structure is needed at all <Cite id="twomey2016" />.
