<Definition>
A two-stage SVM/CRF classifier splits sequence labelling into two learning problems <Cite id="hoefel2008" />. First,
multiclass support vector machines are trained to label each position $\xvec_n$ on its own, producing a vector of
scores $\gvec(\xvec_n)$. Second, a linear-chain conditional random field is trained with feature functions that
depend on the input only through those scores. The SVMs supply accurate local evidence; the CRF combines it with the
dependencies between neighbouring labels.
</Definition>

## The idea

A sequence classifier needs two things: good evidence about each position, and knowledge of which labels tend to
follow which. Max-margin Markov networks <Cite id="taskar2003" /> learn both at once, in one large quadratic program
that couples margin constraints with output correlations. Hoefel and Elkan separate them. A margin-based classifier
is good at the first job, a CRF at the second, and each can be trained with standard software.

In a handwritten word, the third and fourth letters of "fern" may look almost identical. A letter classifier alone
may read "fenn" or "ferr". The CRF knows that "rn" is a likelier pair than "nn" after "fe", and resolves the
ambiguity using the neighbours.

## Stage one: scores for each position

Each position is classified on its own, as in unstructured learning. With $c$ labels, one-against-one SVMs give
$b = c(c - 1)/2$ scores per position, one for each pair of labels:

$$
\gvec(\xvec_n) = \paren{g_1(\xvec_n), \dots, g_b(\xvec_n)}.
$$

For 26 letters this is 325 scores. One-against-one was more accurate than one-against-all on this task, trains
faster because each SVM sees only two classes, and passes more information to the second stage. Logistic regression
can replace the SVMs, giving a vector of class probabilities instead of margins.

## Stage two: a CRF on the scores

The CRF is the usual linear chain in the notation of <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random
fields</NoteLink>, with one change: its feature functions see the scores $\gvec(\xvec_n)$, not the raw input. Two new
feature types carry them, one for single labels and one for pairs:

$$
f^{(5)}_z\paren{g_z(\xvec_n), y_n}, \qquad f^{(6)}_z\paren{g_z(\xvec_n), y_{n-1}, y_n}.
$$

They enter the node and edge potentials exactly as any other features:

$$
\psivec_n(y) = \exp\set{\sum_j \lambda_j f_j\paren{\emptyset, y, \xvec, n}}, \qquad
\Psimat_n(u, v) = \exp\set{\sum_j \lambda_j f_j\paren{u, v, \xvec, n}},
$$

where the $f_j$ now include $f^{(5)}$ and $f^{(6)}$ alongside label-only features such as $f(y_n)$ and
$f(y_{n-1}, y_n)$. The features are binary, so each real score is discretised first. For SVMs, $g'_z = \lceil g_z
\rceil$; the scores lie roughly between $-12$ and $5$, which leaves at most 17 values per score. For logistic
regression, $g'_z = \lceil 10\, g_z \rceil$.

Training maximises the regularised conditional log-likelihood as for any CRF. The gradient for each weight is the
observed feature count minus its expectation under the model, and forward–backward supplies the expectation.

## How the uncertainty propagates

The second stage is what turns independent local decisions into a joint one. Forward–backward computes each marginal
from the whole sequence,

$$
P(Y_n) \propto \alphavec_n \odot \psivec_n \odot \betavec_n,
$$

where $\alphavec_n$ summarises the scores before position $n$ and $\betavec_n$ those after it, each passed through
the edge potentials. A position with confident scores keeps its label. A position with ambiguous scores is decided by
its neighbours, through the messages. Because the CRF sees the whole score vector and not only its maximum, it also
learns which second choices of the SVMs tend to be right.

## Results

Hoefel and Elkan evaluated the method on the handwritten-word OCR benchmark of Taskar et al.: 6,876 words of
$16 \times 8$ binary letter images. Average accuracy per character <Cite id="hoefel2008" />:

| Method | 10% training | 90% training |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logistic regression | 0.759 | 0.818 |
| SVM, quadratic kernel | 0.826 | 0.900 |
| CRF on the raw pixels | 0.793 | 0.856 |
| LR/CRF | 0.856 | 0.926 |
| SVM/CRF, quadratic kernel | 0.883 | 0.950 |
| M³N, quadratic kernel (Taskar et al.) | 0.87 | |

The two-stage method beat each of its components and the plain CRF, and matched max-margin Markov networks while
using only off-the-shelf software: LIBSVM for the first stage and a stochastic-gradient CRF trainer for the second.
The best result overall came from a conditional graphical model with cliques of three labels, which captures letter
triples that a first-order chain cannot.

## Relation to other ideas

- **Stacking.** The method is stacked generalisation <Cite id="wolpert1992" /> with a structured second stage: the
  first-stage outputs are the second stage's inputs.
- **Logistic regression as a CRF.** The LR/CRF variant connects to the view of logistic regression as a CRF with no
  label-pair features <Cite id="twomey2016" />: stage one is exactly that unstructured CRF, and stage two adds the
  structure back. See <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed">when is structure needed</NoteLink>.
- **Neural emissions.** The same division of labour appears in BiLSTM-CRF taggers, where a neural network produces
  per-position scores and a CRF layer decodes them, and in hybrid speech recognisers that feed neural-network
  posteriors into an HMM. See <NoteLink to="crf-variants">CRF variants</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Scores on training data are optimistic.** The stage-one scores on the examples the SVMs were trained on are more
  confident and more often correct than their scores on new data. A CRF trained on them learns to over-trust them.
  The standard remedy from stacking is to train the second stage on out-of-fold scores: split the training set into
  folds, and score each fold with SVMs trained on the others <Cite id="wolpert1992" />. The original paper does not
  describe this step.
- **Discretisation loses detail.** Rounding each score to an integer bins it coarsely. Too fine a binning creates
  many rare features; too coarse a binning throws away the score's magnitude.
- **Feature count.** With 325 scores, 17 levels and 26 labels, the pairwise score features can number in the
  millions. Pruning rare features, as the CRF software did, keeps training feasible.
