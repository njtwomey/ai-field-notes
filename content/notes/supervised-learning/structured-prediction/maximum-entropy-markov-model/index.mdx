<Definition>
A maximum-entropy Markov model (MEMM) is a discriminative model of a label sequence $\yvec$ given observations
$\xvec$ that factorises into one locally normalised conditional per position:

$$
P(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \prod_{n=1}^{N} P(y_n \mid y_{n-1}, \xvec), \qquad
P(y_n = v \mid y_{n-1} = u, \xvec) = \frac{\exp\set{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(u, v, \xvec, n)}}{\sum_{v'}
\exp\set{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(u, v', \xvec, n)}},
$$

with feature functions $\fvec$ and weights $\lambdavec$ as in a linear-chain conditional random field (CRF) <Cite id="mccallum2000" />.
</Definition>

## A chain of classifiers

Each factor is a <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">multinomial logistic regression</NoteLink>, a <NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression">maximum-entropy
classifier</NoteLink>, that predicts the next label from the previous label and any features of the whole observation sequence.
This was the attraction over a <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink>: features can be
overlapping, can look at any part of $\xvec$, and need no model of how the observations are generated.

- **Training.** The log-likelihood is a sum of independent local terms, one per position, each a softmax
  log-likelihood. It is convex, and needs no inference over the chain during training.
- **Decoding.** Viterbi works unchanged, with edge potentials $\Psimat_n(u, v) = P(y_{n+1} = v \mid y_n = u,
  \xvec)$ and the first factor folded into $\psivec_1$.

## Label bias

Every local factor sums to one over the next label, for every previous label and every input:

$$
\sum_{v} P(y_n = v \mid y_{n-1} = u, \xvec) = 1.
$$

So the probability mass that reaches state $u$ is passed on in full. The observation at step $n$ can only
redistribute it among $u$'s successors; it cannot reduce the total. A state with a single successor passes on all its
mass whatever it observes, and a state whose transitions are nearly deterministic almost ignores its observations.
This is the label-bias problem <Cite id="lafferty2001" />.

Two words share their first and last letters: states $1 \to 2 \to 3$ spell
"rib" and states $4 \to 5 \to 3$ spell "rob". From the start state, both paths begin on the letter r. Suppose a
fraction $p$ of the training words beginning with r were "rib", so the first local factor learns
$P(1 \mid 0, \text{r}) = p$ and $P(4 \mid 0, \text{r}) = 1 - p$.

States 1 and 4 each have one successor. Whatever feature connects state 2 to the letter i, for example a weight $w$
on $[y_2 = 2 \wedge x_2 = \text{i}]$, it enters the local softmax over the successors of state 1, and that softmax has
one term:

$$
P(y_2 = 2 \mid y_1 = 1, \xvec) = \frac{e^{w\,[x_2 = \text{i}]}}{e^{w\,[x_2 = \text{i}]}} = 1.
$$

The evidence cancels. For the input "rob", the two path probabilities are $p \cdot 1 \cdot 1$ and
$(1 - p) \cdot 1 \cdot 1$, independent of the middle letter. With $p = 0.6$, the MEMM labels "rob" as the "rib" path
with probability 0.6. No amount of training on the second letter can fix this, because the parameter it would need
cancels out of the model.

## How a CRF avoids it

A <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random field</NoteLink> uses the same features but normalises
once, over whole label sequences:
$P(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \exp\set{\sum_n \lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)} / Z(\xvec)$. Scores of
different paths are compared with each other, so the evidence weight no longer cancels. In the example, each path's
score is its start bias plus the evidence term:

$$
P(\text{rob path} \mid \text{rob}) = \frac{(1 - p)\, e^{w}}{p + (1 - p)\, e^{w}},
$$

which is 0.973 for $p = 0.6$ and $w = 4$. The prior share $p$ would have to exceed $e^w/(1 + e^w) = 0.982$ before
the CRF chose the wrong path.

A <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink> is locally normalised too, but in the generative
direction. Its factors are $A(u, v)$ and $b_v(x_n)$, and a small emission probability $b_2(\text{o})$ lowers the
probability of the whole "rib" path. Label bias is specific to models that normalise <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distributions</NoteLink> over
labels position by position.

## Trade-offs

- **For MEMMs.** Training is fast and decomposes by position, with no forward–backward pass. In practice they can
  perform well when transitions are not close to deterministic.
- **Against MEMMs.** Label bias, and the resulting preference for states with low-entropy transitions. CRFs replaced
  MEMMs in most applications for this reason <Cite id="lafferty2001" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Nearly deterministic transitions.** Label bias is worst when some states have few likely successors, as with
  multi-token entities or fixed spellings.
- **Exposure to the true previous label.** Each local classifier is trained with the true previous label as input, but
  at test time it receives its own prediction, so early mistakes can propagate.
