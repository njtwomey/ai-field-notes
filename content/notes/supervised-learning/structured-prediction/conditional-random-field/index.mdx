<Definition>
Let $\xvec \in \reals^{N \times D}$ be a sequence of $N$ observations and $\yvec \in \Ycal^N$ its sequence of labels. A
linear-chain conditional random field (CRF) with feature functions $\fvec$ and weights $\lambdavec \in \reals^J$ is

$$
P_{\text{CRF}}(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z_{\text{CRF}}} \prod_{n=1}^{N} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)\},
$$

where the partition function $Z_{\text{CRF}}$ sums the product over every label sequence, so that the probabilities sum to
one <Cite id="lafferty2001" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

The notation follows Twomey, Diethe and Flach <Cite id="twomey2016" />. A dataset holds $M$ observation–label pairs,
$\Dcal = \set{(\xvec_m, \yvec_m)}_{m=1}^{M}$. Sequence $m$ has length $N_m$, with observations $\xvec_m \in \reals^{N_m
\times D}$ and labels $\yvec_m \in \Ycal^{N_m}$. Position $n$ holds the token $\xvec_{m,n}$ and its tag $y_{m,n}$. The
subscript $m$ is dropped when a single sequence is meant.

## The model

**Feature functions.** A feature function $f_j$ returns a number, often 0 or 1, that describes the labels at a position
and the observations. There are two kinds. A unigram feature $f_j(\emptyset, y_n, \xvec, n)$ looks at the current label
only, for example "the label is *noun* and the word ends in *-ing*". A bigram feature $f_j(y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)$ looks
at the current and previous labels, for example "a *determiner* is followed by a *noun*". Both kinds are stacked into one
vector $\fvec$ of length $J$. The weights $\lambdavec$ are shared by every position, so the model does not change along
the sequence. At the first position there is no previous label, and only unigram features fire.

A feature may read the whole observation sequence $\xvec$, not only the token at position $n$. This is the main
advantage over a generative model: the observations are conditioned on, never modelled, so overlapping and long-range
features need no model of the dependencies among the observations.

**Potentials.** Split the exponent into the part that involves one label and the part that involves two. The node
potential at position $n$ is a vector over labels, and the edge potential between positions $n$ and $n + 1$ is a
$\abs{\Ycal} \times \abs{\Ycal}$ matrix:

$$
\psivec_n(y) = \exp\Big\{\sum_{j} \lambda_j f_j(\emptyset, y, \xvec, n)\Big\}, \qquad
\Psimat_n(u, v) = \exp\Big\{\sum_{j} \lambda_j f_j(u, v, \xvec, n + 1)\Big\},
$$

where $\Psimat_n$ collects the bigram features that fire at position $n + 1$. The exponential of a sum is a product, so
the model factorises into these potentials:

$$
P_{\text{CRF}}(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z_{\text{CRF}}} \prod_{n=1}^{N} \psivec_n(y_n) \prod_{n=1}^{N-1} \Psimat_n(y_n,
y_{n+1}).
$$

Each label interacts directly only with its neighbours. This chain structure is what makes exact inference cheap.

## Inference with forward–backward

The partition function $Z_{\text{CRF}}$ and the marginals $P(Y_n = y \mid \xvec)$ are sums over all $\abs{\Ycal}^N$ label
sequences. Forward–backward computes them in $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ time by passing messages along the chain
<Cite id="twomey2016" />.

**Forward messages.** Define $\alphavec_n(v)$ as the total weight of all label prefixes $y_1, \dots, y_{n-1}$ that lead
into $y_n = v$, excluding the node potential at $n$ itself:

$$
\alphavec_n(v) = \sum_{y_1, \dots, y_{n-1}} \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} \psivec_i(y_i) \prod_{i=1}^{n-2} \Psimat_i(y_i, y_{i+1})
\cdot \Psimat_{n-1}(y_{n-1}, v).
$$

The sum over $y_1, \dots, y_{n-2}$ inside this expression is exactly $\alphavec_{n-1}(y_{n-1})$. What remains is a sum over
$y_{n-1}$ alone. With $\gammavec_{n-1} = \alphavec_{n-1} \odot \psivec_{n-1}$, where $\odot$ is the elementwise product,

$$
\alphavec_n = \Psimat_{n-1}^\top \gammavec_{n-1}, \qquad \alphavec_1 = \ones.
$$

**Backward messages.** Symmetrically, $\betavec_n(u)$ is the total weight of all label suffixes that follow $y_n = u$.
With $\deltavec_{n+1} = \betavec_{n+1} \odot \psivec_{n+1}$,

$$
\betavec_n = \Psimat_n \deltavec_{n+1}, \qquad \betavec_N = \ones.
$$

**Marginals.** Every sequence with $y_n = y$ is a prefix into $y$, the node potential at $y$, and a suffix out of $y$.
So the unnormalised marginal is the product of the three, and summing it over labels gives the partition function:

$$
\widehat{P}(Y_n) = \alphavec_n \odot \psivec_n \odot \betavec_n, \qquad Z_{\text{CRF}} = \sum_{y' \in \Ycal}
\widehat{P}(Y_n = y'), \qquad P(Y_n) = \frac{\widehat{P}(Y_n)}{Z_{\text{CRF}}}.
$$

$Z_{\text{CRF}}$ comes out the same at every position, which is a useful check on an implementation. The pairwise
marginal of two neighbours follows the same reasoning, with the edge potential between them:

$$
P(Y_n = u, Y_{n+1} = v \mid \xvec) = \frac{\gammavec_n(u)\, \Psimat_n(u, v)\, \deltavec_{n+1}(v)}{Z_{\text{CRF}}}.
$$

The products grow or shrink exponentially with $N$, so implementations either rescale each message to sum to one and
accumulate the log scale factors, or work in log space.

### Worked example

Take $N = 3$ positions and two labels. Let the node potentials be $\psivec_1 = (2, 1)$, $\psivec_2 = (1, 1)$ and
$\psivec_3 = (1, 3)$, and let every edge potential be $\Psimat = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, which
favours neighbours with the same label.

- **Forward.** $\alphavec_1 = (1, 1)$. Then $\gammavec_1 = (2, 1)$ and $\alphavec_2 = \Psimat^\top\gammavec_1 = (7, 5)$.
  Then $\gammavec_2 = (7, 5)$ and $\alphavec_3 = (26, 22)$.
- **Backward.** $\betavec_3 = (1, 1)$. Then $\deltavec_3 = (1, 3)$ and $\betavec_2 = \Psimat\deltavec_3 = (6, 10)$. Then
  $\deltavec_2 = (6, 10)$ and $\betavec_1 = (28, 36)$.
- **Marginals.** $\widehat{P}(Y_1) = (1 \cdot 2 \cdot 28,\ 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 36) = (56, 36)$, so $Z_{\text{CRF}} = 92$ and
  $P(Y_1) = (0.61, 0.39)$. At the other positions $\widehat{P}(Y_2) = (42, 50)$ and $\widehat{P}(Y_3) = (26, 66)$. Both
  also sum to 92.

Enumerating all eight label sequences gives the same partition function and marginals.

## Decoding with Viterbi

The marginals answer "which label is most probable at position $n$?". Decoding asks a different question: "which whole
sequence is most probable?". Replacing each sum in the forward recursion by a maximum, and remembering which previous
label achieved it, gives the <Gloss name="viterbi">Viterbi algorithm</Gloss>, also $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$. In log space its recursion is

$$
s_n(v) = \log\psivec_n(v) + \max_{u}\big(s_{n-1}(u) + \log\Psimat_{n-1}(u, v)\big),
$$

and backtracking from the best final label recovers the path.

The two answers can differ. In the worked example, the first position on its own prefers label 1, with probability
0.61. But the most probable sequence is label 2 at every position, with weight $1 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 27$ and
probability $27/92 = 0.29$. The sequence that decodes each position separately, (1, 2, 2), has weight 18 and probability
0.20. Per-position decoding minimises the expected number of wrong labels; Viterbi maximises the probability of getting
the whole sequence right. When a task needs a coherent sequence, the Viterbi path is the one to report
<Cite id="twomey2016" />.

## Training

Given a dataset $\Dcal$, the weights maximise the conditional log-likelihood

$$
\ell(\lambdavec) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \Big( \sum_{n=1}^{N_m} \lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{m,n-1}, y_{m,n}, \xvec_m, n) - \log
Z_{\text{CRF}}(\xvec_m) \Big).
$$

**The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>.** The first term is linear in $\lambdavec$, with gradient $\sum_n \fvec(y_{m,n-1}, y_{m,n}, \xvec_m, n)$,
the feature counts of the observed labels. The partition function is $Z = \sum_{\yvec'} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top
F(\yvec')\}$ with $F(\yvec') = \sum_n \fvec(y'_{n-1}, y'_n, \xvec, n)$, so

$$
\nabla_{\lambdavec} \log Z = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{\yvec'} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top F(\yvec')\}\, F(\yvec') = \expect_{P(\yvec'
\mid \xvec)}\brack{F(\yvec')}.
$$

The gradient is therefore the empirical feature counts minus the counts the model expects:

$$
\nabla_{\lambdavec} \ell = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \Big( F_m(\yvec_m) - \expect_{P(\yvec' \mid \xvec_m)}\brack{F_m(\yvec')} \Big).
$$

At the optimum the two agree, so the fitted model reproduces the feature statistics of the data. Each feature touches at
most two neighbouring labels, so its <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> needs only the node and pairwise marginals from forward–backward:
$\expect[f_j] = \sum_n \sum_{u, v} P(Y_{n-1} = u, Y_n = v \mid \xvec)\, f_j(u, v, \xvec, n)$.

**Convexity.** $\log Z$ is a log-sum-exp of functions linear in $\lambdavec$, and log-sum-exp is convex. So $-\ell$ is
convex and has no spurious local optima. Its <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> is the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">covariance matrix</NoteLink> of $F$ under the model, which is <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive
semidefinite</NoteLink>.

**Regularisation and optimisation.** With many sparse features the likelihood can be increased without limit by
growing the weights of rare features. An L2 penalty $\tfrac{\sigma^{-2}}{2}\norm{\lambdavec}^2$ keeps the problem strictly
convex. The standard optimisers are <Gloss name="l-bfgs" /> on the full objective and <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> over sequences
<Cite id="sutton2012" />. Each gradient evaluation costs one forward–backward pass per sequence.

## Special cases

**<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</NoteLink>.** Set every bigram feature to zero. The edge potentials become matrices of ones, the positions
decouple, and the model becomes one logistic regression per position:

$$
P_{\text{LR}}(y_n \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z_{\text{LR}}} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_n, \xvec, n)\}.
$$

A CRF is therefore logistic regression plus label-to-label interactions <Cite id="twomey2016" />. Whether those
interactions are worth their cost is the subject of <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed">when is structure
needed</NoteLink>.

**The <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov model</Gloss>.** Choose unigram features that are indicators of (label, observation) pairs and bigram
features that are indicators of label pairs. The CRF then has the same factorisation as a hidden Markov model, with
log emission and transition probabilities as weights. The two form a <NoteLink to="generative-vs-discriminative">generative–discriminative pair</NoteLink>: the HMM fits
$p(\xvec, \yvec)$ and must model the observations; the CRF fits $p(\yvec \mid \xvec)$ and does not
<Cite id="sutton2012" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Label bias in locally normalised models.** A CRF normalises once over whole sequences. Models that normalise at
  each step, such as the <Gloss name="memm">maximum-entropy Markov model</Gloss>, can ignore the observations after a low-entropy transition; the
  CRF was introduced to fix this <Cite id="lafferty2001" />.
- **Features, not the model, carry most of the accuracy.** Performance depends mainly on the feature templates. A CRF
  with poor features loses to a well-featured logistic regression.
- **Training cost.** Every gradient step runs forward–backward on every sequence, at $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ each. With many
  labels or long-range structure, this dominates.
