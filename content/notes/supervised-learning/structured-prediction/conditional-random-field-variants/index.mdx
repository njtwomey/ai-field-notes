<Definition>
A conditional random field is any model of the form $P(\yvec \mid \xvec) = Z^{-1} \prod_{c} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top
\fvec_c(\yvec_c, \xvec)\}$, a normalised product of factors $c$ that each touch a small set of labels $\yvec_c$
<Cite id="sutton2012" />. The linear chain is the special case whose factors touch one label or two neighbours. Its
variants change which labels share a factor. That choice fixes what the model can express and what exact inference costs.
</Definition>

## The linear chain as the reference

In the notation of the <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random field</NoteLink> note, the linear chain
is

$$
P(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z_{\text{CRF}}} \prod_{n=1}^{N} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)\}.
$$

Forward–backward sums over all label sequences in $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ time, because each message only needs to carry one
label's worth of history. Every variant below either enlarges that history, which multiplies the cost, or creates loops,
which make exact inference expensive and usually replace it with an approximation.

## Higher-order CRFs

A CRF of order $k$ lets each factor see the previous $k$ labels:
$\exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{n-k}, \dots, y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)\}$. Grouping the last $k$ labels into a single
compound state turns it back into a first-order chain over $\abs{\Ycal}^k$ states, so forward–backward costs
$O(N\abs{\Ycal}^{k+1})$. Second order is common in <Gloss name="pos-tagging">part-of-speech tagging</Gloss>; beyond that the cost and the number of
parameters grow quickly.

## Semi-Markov CRFs

Many labelling tasks are about segments: a named entity spans several words, an activity lasts several minutes. A
semi-Markov CRF labels segments instead of positions <Cite id="sarawagi2004" />. A segmentation is a list of segments
$s_k = (t_k, u_k, y_k)$ with start $t_k$, end $u_k$ and label $y_k$, and

$$
P(\svec \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{k} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \gvec(y_{k-1}, y_k, \xvec, t_k, u_k)\}.
$$

Features can now describe a whole segment: its length, whether it is capitalised throughout, how well it matches a
dictionary entry. With segments of length at most $L$, forward–backward sums over the possible end of the previous
segment as well as its label, at cost $O(N L \abs{\Ycal}^2)$.

## Skip-chain and general CRFs

A skip-chain CRF adds factors between distant positions that the task suggests are related, for example two occurrences
of the same capitalised word in a document, which are likely to share a named-entity label <Cite id="sutton2012" />:

$$
P(\yvec \mid \xvec) \propto \prod_{n} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)\} \prod_{(a, b) \in
\Scal} \exp\{\muvec^\top \hvec(y_a, y_b, \xvec)\}.
$$

The skip edges create loops. Exact inference on a graph with loops costs exponentially in its treewidth, so in practice
general CRFs use approximate inference: <NoteLink to="loopy-belief-propagation">loopy belief propagation</NoteLink>, which runs the chain's message updates on the loopy
graph until they settle, <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>, or sampling. The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the likelihood still needs the
factor marginals, so training inherits the approximation.

## Dynamic and factorial CRFs

A dynamic CRF gives each position several labels, $\yvec_n = (y_n^{(1)}, \dots, y_n^{(C)})$, for example a part of speech
and a noun-phrase chunk tag, with factors within a time step and between consecutive time steps
<Cite id="sutton2007" />. When each layer forms its own chain and the layers interact only within a time step, the model
is a factorial CRF: the discriminative counterpart of the <NoteLink to="factorial-hidden-markov-model">factorial hidden
Markov model</NoteLink>. Treating the $C$ labels at a position as one compound label gives a linear chain with
$\abs{\Ycal}^C$ states, so exact inference costs $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^{2C})$. For more than a few layers, loopy belief
propagation over the grid of factors is the usual approach.

## Hidden-state CRFs

A hidden-state CRF predicts a single label $c$ for a whole sequence, such as the gesture in a video, through a sequence of
hidden states $\hvec = (h_1, \dots, h_N)$ that are never labelled <Cite id="quattoni2007" />:

$$
P(c \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{\hvec} \prod_{n} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(c, h_{n-1}, h_n, \xvec, n)\}.
$$

The sum over hidden states is forward–backward over the hidden chain, once per class, at cost
$O(C N \abs{\Hcal}^2)$ for $C$ classes and $\abs{\Hcal}$ hidden states. The latent sum makes the likelihood
non-convex, so training finds a local optimum.

## Neural CRFs

A neural CRF keeps the linear chain but computes the node potentials with a neural network instead of hand-written
features: $\psivec_n(y) = \exp\{g_{\thetavec}(\xvec)_{n, y}\}$, where $g_{\thetavec}$ is often a
bidirectional <Gloss name="lstm" /> or a transformer, and the edge potential is a learned $\abs{\Ycal} \times \abs{\Ycal}$ matrix of
transition scores <Cite id="huang2015" /> <Cite id="lample2016" />. Inference is unchanged, $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ per
sequence, and the conditional log-likelihood is optimised end to end by backpropagation through forward–backward. The
network supplies rich features; the CRF layer enforces consistency between neighbouring labels, such as never placing
an "inside an entity" tag directly after an "outside" tag.

## Comparison

| Variant | Factors couple | Exact inference cost | Training objective |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Linear chain | neighbouring labels | $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ | convex |
| Order $k$ | $k + 1$ consecutive labels | $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^{k+1})$ | convex |
| Semi-Markov | consecutive segments | $O(N L \abs{\Ycal}^2)$ | convex |
| Skip-chain, general | arbitrary label pairs | exponential in treewidth; approximate in practice | convex, approximate gradient |
| Dynamic, factorial | labels across layers and time | $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^{2C})$; approximate in practice | convex, approximate gradient |
| Hidden-state | hidden states and a sequence label | $O(C N \abs{\Hcal}^2)$ | non-convex |
| Neural | neighbouring labels, learned node potentials | $O(N\abs{\Ycal}^2)$ | non-convex in the network |

## Pitfalls

- **Cost grows with history, not with sequence length.** Each extra label of history multiplies inference by
  $\abs{\Ycal}$. Richer features of the observations are usually cheaper than a longer label history.
- **Approximate inference changes the objective.** With loopy belief propagation the gradient is only approximate, and
  training can oscillate or settle on a worse model than a simpler exact chain.
- **Structure may not be needed at all.** Before adding factors, check whether the labels are correlated beyond what the
  features already capture; see <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed">when is structure needed</NoteLink>.
