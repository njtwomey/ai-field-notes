<Definition>
A sequence labelling problem can be solved by a structured model, such as a <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional
random field</NoteLink>, whose labels interact, or by an unstructured model, such as
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> at each position, which sees the sequence only through
its features. Twomey, Diethe and Flach show that the unstructured model is the structured one with its label
interactions removed, identify exactly when interactions leave labels independent, and relate the cost of ignoring
structure to how fast the sequence's auto-correlations decay <Cite id="twomey2016" />.
</Definition>

## Logistic regression is a CRF without edges

The linear-chain CRF on a sequence $\xvec$ of length $N$ is

$$
P_{\text{CRF}}(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z_{\text{CRF}}} \prod_{n=1}^{N} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_{n-1}, y_n,
\xvec, n)\},
$$

with unigram features $f_j(\emptyset, y_n, \xvec, n)$ and bigram features $f_j(y_{n-1}, y_n, \xvec, n)$. Set every
bigram feature to zero. The edge potentials $\Psimat_n$ become matrices of ones, the product splits into one factor per
position, and the partition function splits the same way. What remains at each position is

$$
P_{\text{LR}}(y_n \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z_{\text{LR}}} \exp\{\lambdavec^\top \fvec(y_n, \xvec, n)\},
$$

a multinomial logistic regression that may use features of the whole sequence $\xvec$ <Cite id="twomey2016" />. So the
difference between the two models is only the bigram features. Context from the observations, such as the tokens either
side of position $n$, can enter the logistic regression through $\fvec$; only direct interactions between labels are lost.

## A rank-1 transition potential splits the chain

Even with bigram features present, some transition potentials leave the labels on either side independent.

**Theorem.** If the edge potential $\Psimat_{n-1}$ between positions $n - 1$ and $n$ has rank 1, then
$(Y_1, \dots, Y_{n-1}) \perp (Y_n, \dots, Y_N)$. If every edge potential has rank 1, the labels are fully independent,
$P(Y_1, \dots, Y_N) = \prod_n P(Y_n)$ <Cite id="twomey2016" />.

**Proof.** A rank-1 matrix is an outer product. By the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value
decomposition</NoteLink>, $\Psimat_{n-1} = \sigma\, \uvec \vvec^\top$. The forward message into position $n$ is then

$$
\alphavec_n = \Psimat_{n-1}^\top \gammavec_{n-1} = \sigma\, \vvec\, \uvec^\top \gammavec_{n-1} = c\, \vvec,
$$

where $c = \sigma\, \uvec^\top \gammavec_{n-1}$ is a scalar. Everything before position $n$ now enters only through the
constant $c$, which cancels when the marginal is normalised:

$$
P(Y_n) = \frac{\vvec \odot \psivec_n \odot \betavec_n}{\sum_{y'} \vvec(y')\, \psivec_n(y')\, \betavec_n(y')}.
$$

The same argument applied to the backward message $\betavec_{n-1} = \sigma\, \uvec\, \vvec^\top\deltavec_n$ shows that
$P(Y_{n-1})$ does not depend on anything after the edge. More generally, the joint weight of a whole sequence is a
product of a factor over $y_1, \dots, y_{n-1}$ (ending with $\uvec(y_{n-1})$) and a factor over $y_n, \dots, y_N$
(starting with $\vvec(y_n)$), so the two halves are independent. If every edge is rank 1, the chain falls apart into
single positions. $\square$

A full-rank transition potential, by contrast, couples every label to every other along the chain. The rank of the
transition potentials is therefore a direct measure of how much structure a trained CRF actually uses. Penalising the
nuclear norm of the transition matrices during training pushes them toward low rank, and so toward a model that
behaves like logistic regression.

## Dependence and the excess risk of ignoring structure

The theorem describes a fitted model. The paper also asks the statistical question: when the data are a dependent
sequence rather than independent examples, can an unstructured learner still reach the best possible risk, and how
fast?

The answer depends on how quickly the process forgets. A stationary process is **mixing** when events far apart in the
sequence become independent, and its correlations then decay to zero with the lag. The paper shows that regularised
empirical risk minimisation with the logistic loss, which is logistic regression, is consistent on such processes: its
risk converges to the smallest achievable risk <Cite id="twomey2016" />. For processes whose correlations decay
geometrically, it gives learning rates of the same form as the Bernstein-type bounds for independent data, with the
dependence entering through the rate of decay. The faster the correlations decay, the closer learning is to the
independent case.

The practical reading is intuitive. If the correlation between labels dies out within a few positions, then features
that describe a window of that width, such as $n$-grams of the surrounding tokens, capture nearly all the dependence,
and a per-position classifier with those features loses little by ignoring label interactions. If correlations persist
over long ranges, a fixed window cannot capture them and a structured model has something to add.

## Worked example: the occasionally dishonest casino

In the <NoteLink to="occasionally-dishonest-casino">occasionally dishonest casino</NoteLink>, a hidden die switches
between fair and loaded with probability $p_s$ at each roll, and the loaded die shows face $v$ with probability $p_v$ and
every other face with probability $(1 - p_v)/5$. The auto-correlation of the rolled faces can be derived exactly.

**The die.** With symmetric switching, the die at time $t + n$ is the same as at time $t$ with probability
$\tfrac12\paren{1 + (1 - 2p_s)^n}$. The correlation between the two dice, coded as 0 and 1, is therefore $(1 - 2p_s)^n$.

**The faces.** Given the dice, the faces are independent draws. So faces $n$ rolls apart are correlated only through
the dice, and by the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total covariance</NoteLink>

$$
\operatorname{corr}(X_t, X_{t+n}) = \frac{\var(\mu_S)}{\expect[\var(X \mid S)] + \var(\mu_S)}\, (1 - 2p_s)^n,
$$

where $\mu_S$ is the mean face of the die in use. The fair die has mean 3.5 and variance $35/12$.

With the paper's settings, face 1 loaded with $p_v = 0.5$ and switching probability $p_s = 0.05$
<Cite id="twomey2016" />, the loaded die has mean 2.5. The fraction in front is 0.075, so the lag-1 correlation is 0.0675,
and each further lag multiplies it by 0.9. It falls below 0.01 at lag 20. A simulation of 400,000 rolls gives 0.068,
0.061 and 0.054 at lags 1, 2 and 3, against 0.068, 0.061 and 0.055 from the formula.

The profile says how wide a feature window must be: here, about twenty rolls either side carry information about the
current die. In the paper's experiments, logistic regression with windowed features approximated the smoothing that a
CRF achieves on this problem, and on word hyphenation and smart-home activity recognition the two models reached
statistically indistinguishable F-scores: 0.966 against 0.963 for hyphenation and 0.788 against 0.798 for activity
recognition, logistic regression first <Cite id="twomey2016" />.

## Using auto-correlation to choose a model

1. **Estimate the auto-correlation profile** of the labels, or of features that track them, before choosing a model.
2. **Read off the decay length**, the lag beyond which the correlation is negligible.
3. **Design feature templates** that cover that window. A per-position classifier with those features is then a strong
   baseline, and often matches a structured model at a fraction of the training cost.
4. **Reach for a structured model** when correlations persist beyond any practical window, or when the output must be
   coherent as a whole; then decode with Viterbi rather than thresholding marginals.

## Pitfalls

- **Linear correlation can miss dependence.** If the loaded die favours face 3 or 4, the mean face barely changes and the
  linear auto-correlation is close to zero, although the die is as persistent as ever. Rank-based or other nonlinear
  measures of dependence avoid this.
- **Rank 1 exactly is rare.** Trained transition potentials are seldom exactly rank 1. A dominant first singular value
  means the coupling is weak rather than absent.
- **Probabilities and decisions differ.** A per-position model can produce jumpy probabilities where the true labels are
  smooth. When a coherent label sequence is needed, a structured decoder is still useful.
