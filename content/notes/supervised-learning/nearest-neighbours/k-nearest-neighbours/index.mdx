<Definition>
Given training pairs $(\xvec_1, y_1), \dots, (\xvec_n, y_n)$ and a distance on the inputs, the **$k$-nearest
neighbours** rule predicts at a query $\xvec$ from the set $N_k(\xvec)$ of the $k$ training points nearest to $\xvec$.
For classification it predicts the most frequent class in $N_k(\xvec)$; for regression it predicts the mean
$\hat f(\xvec) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\xvec)} y_i$. There is no training step beyond storing the data
<Cite id="fix1989" />.
</Definition>

## A local estimate of the posterior

The fraction of neighbours in class $c$, $\hat p(c \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k(\xvec)}\indicator[y_i = c]$,
estimates the posterior probability $p(y = c \mid \xvec)$. Majority voting then implements the
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes decision rule</NoteLink> with this estimate in place of the true posterior.

The estimate also follows from density estimation <Cite id="bishop2006" />. Let the smallest ball around $\xvec$ that
holds the $k$ neighbours have volume $V$, and let $k_c$ of them be in class $c$, which has $n_c$ training points. The
density of class $c$ near $\xvec$ is estimated by the fraction of its points in the ball per unit volume,
$\hat p(\xvec \mid c) = k_c/(n_c V)$, and the prior by $\hat p(c) = n_c/n$. The unconditional density is
$\hat p(\xvec) = k/(nV)$. <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> gives

$$
\hat p(c \mid \xvec) = \frac{\hat p(\xvec \mid c)\,\hat p(c)}{\hat p(\xvec)} = \frac{k_c}{n_c V}\cdot\frac{n_c}{n}\cdot\frac{nV}{k} = \frac{k_c}{k}.
$$

## Twice the Bayes error

Cover and Hart bounded the error of the 1-nearest-neighbour rule as the training set grows <Cite id="cover1967" />.
Consider two classes and write $\eta(\xvec) = p(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$. The Bayes classifier errs at $\xvec$ with
probability $r^*(\xvec) = \min(\eta(\xvec), 1 - \eta(\xvec))$, and the **Bayes error** is $R^* = \expect[r^*(\xvec)]$.

As $n \to \infty$, the nearest neighbour $\xvec'$ of a query $\xvec$ converges to $\xvec$ (for $\xvec$ in the support
of the input distribution). If $\eta$ is continuous, $\eta(\xvec') \to \eta(\xvec)$. The neighbour's label $y'$ and
the query's label $y$ are then two independent draws, each equal to 1 with probability $\eta(\xvec)$. The rule errs
when they differ:

$$
\Pr(y \ne y' \mid \xvec) \to \eta(1 - \eta) + (1 - \eta)\eta = 2\eta(\xvec)\big(1 - \eta(\xvec)\big) = 2r^*(\xvec)\big(1 - r^*(\xvec)\big),
$$

where the last step uses that $\eta(1 - \eta)$ is symmetric under $\eta \mapsto 1 - \eta$. The asymptotic 1-NN error is
the average over $\xvec$:

$$
R_{1\text{NN}} = \expect\brack{2r^*(1 - r^*)} = 2R^* - 2\expect[(r^*)^2] \le 2R^* - 2(R^*)^2 = 2R^*(1 - R^*),
$$

using $\expect[(r^*)^2] \ge (\expect[r^*])^2$, which is Jensen's inequality or the non-negativity of the variance. So

$$
R^* \le R_{1\text{NN}} \le 2R^*(1 - R^*) \le 2R^*.
$$

For $K$ classes the bound is $R^*\big(2 - \frac{K}{K - 1}R^*\big)$ <Cite id="cover1967" />. The lower bound holds
because no rule beats the Bayes classifier.

### Worked example

If the Bayes error is $R^* = 0.1$, the asymptotic 1-NN error lies between $0.1$ and $2 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 0.9 = 0.18$. If
$R^* = 0.01$, it lies between $0.01$ and $0.0198$. In the Cover–Hart phrase, half of the classification information
in an infinite sample is contained in the nearest neighbour. When the classes barely overlap, 1-NN is nearly optimal;
when they overlap a lot, it pays for copying one noisy label.

## Consistency with growing $k$

Averaging more labels removes the factor of 2. If $k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, the $k$-NN error
converges to $R^*$ for every distribution of the data: the rule is **universally consistent**
<Cite id="stone1977" /> <Cite id="devroye1996" />. The first condition makes the vote average over many labels, so
it estimates $\eta(\xvec)$ rather than sampling it once. The second keeps the neighbourhood shrinking, so the
neighbours come from ever closer to $\xvec$.

## Bias and variance

For regression with $y = f(\xvec) + \varepsilon$, noise of variance $\sigma^2$, and fixed training inputs, the expected
squared error of $k$-NN at a query $\xvec_0$ splits as

$$
\expect\brack{(y_0 - \hat f(\xvec_0))^2} = \sigma^2 + \Big[f(\xvec_0) - \frac{1}{k}\sum_{\ell = 1}^{k} f(\xvec_{(\ell)})\Big]^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{k},
$$

where $\xvec_{(\ell)}$ is the $\ell$-th nearest neighbour <Cite id="hastie2009" />. The prediction is the average of
$k$ targets whose noises are independent, so its variance is $\sigma^2/k$, and its mean is the average of $f$ over the
neighbours. The middle term is the squared **bias**: small for small $k$, when the neighbours are close to $\xvec_0$,
and growing with $k$ as further neighbours enter. The last term is the **variance**, falling as $1/k$. Small $k$ gives
jagged, high-variance fits; large $k$ smooths away structure. The same trade-off governs classification.

<KnnRegions />

In practice $k$ is chosen by cross-validation. The <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective number of parameters</NoteLink> of $k$-NN is roughly $n/k$: the
number of neighbourhoods of size $k$ that fit in the data.

## Distances and scaling

The rule is only as good as its distance. Euclidean distance on raw features lets the feature with the largest range
dominate, so features are standardised first. Irrelevant features add noise to every distance and should be removed
or down-weighted. Other choices, such as the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine distance</NoteLink> for text or a learnt Mahalanobis metric, change which
points count as near; see <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">pairwise distances</NoteLink>.

Common variants weight each neighbour's vote by a decreasing function of its distance, such as $1/d$, which reduces the
sensitivity to $k$.

## Assumptions

- Nearby inputs have similar targets: $\eta$ or $f$ is smooth with respect to the chosen distance.
- The training data cover the region where queries arise. $k$-NN cannot extrapolate.
- The dimension is low enough for neighbourhoods to be local. In high dimensions distances concentrate; see
  <NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours">the curse of dimensionality for neighbours</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Query cost.** A brute-force query costs $O(nd)$. Large data sets need an index; see
  <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink>.
- **Memory.** The model is the whole training set.
- **<NoteLink to="class-imbalance">Class imbalance</NoteLink>.** A large class dominates most neighbourhoods. Weight votes by inverse class frequency or compare
  $k_c/n_c$ across classes.
- **Ties.** With two classes, an odd $k$ avoids tied votes.
