## At a glance

Hervé Jégou and Matthijs Douze published their first compact-code search methods at INRIA and continued the work at
Facebook AI Research (FAIR), now Meta's Fundamental AI Research group. Most of the methods they and their colleagues
proposed now ship in <NoteLink to="faiss">FAISS</NoteLink>.

## Architecture

### Compact codes inside inverted files (2008–2011)

**Hamming embedding** attached to each vector in an inverted-file cell a short binary signature of its position within
the cell, and discarded candidates whose signature was far in Hamming distance from the query's <Cite id="jegou2008" />.
**Product quantisation** replaced the binary signature by a PQ code of the residual, and ranked with asymmetric distances
computed from look-up tables: the IVFADC index <Cite id="jegou2011" />. **Re-ranking with source coding** added a second,
finer code of what the first code missed, used to re-rank a short list, and introduced the BIGANN dataset of a billion
<Gloss name="sift" /> descriptors, still a standard benchmark <Cite id="jegou2011b" />.

### Polysemous codes (2016)

A PQ code is a tuple of centroid indices. The numbering of centroids is arbitrary, so it can be chosen. Douze, Jégou and
Perronnin choose it so that centroids close in space get indices at small Hamming distance <Cite id="douze2016" />. The
same bytes can then be read two ways: as a binary code, compared with XOR and popcount, or as a PQ code, compared with
look-up tables. Search filters most vectors by the cheap Hamming distance and ranks only the survivors by the asymmetric
distance. The paper reports under 0.3 ms per query per core on BIGANN.

### GPUs (2017)

FAISS was released with the <Gloss name="gpu" /> implementation of Johnson, Douze and Jégou <Cite id="johnson2019" />. Its k-selection,
WarpSelect, keeps candidate lists in registers and merges them with sorting networks within a warp. The paper reports
k-selection at up to 55% of peak, search 8.5 times faster than the previous GPU state of the art, and a <Gloss name="knn" /> graph over
a billion vectors in under 12 hours on 4 GPUs.

### Link and code (2018)

Graph indexes such as HNSW store full vectors, which does not fit a billion vectors in one server's memory. Douze,
Sablayrolles and Jégou store the vectors compressed and use the graph twice: to navigate, and to improve each vector's
reconstruction <Cite id="douze2018" />. A database vector $\xvec$ is close to its graph neighbours, so it is well
approximated by a combination of their decoded vectors. Collect the decoded vector of $\xvec$ and those of its $k$
neighbours as the columns of $\Nmat(\xvec) \in \reals^{d \times (k+1)}$. The least-squares weights are

$$
\betavec(\xvec) = \argmin_{\betavec} \norm{\xvec - \Nmat(\xvec)\betavec}^2 = \big(\Nmat^\top\Nmat\big)^{-1}\Nmat^\top\xvec.
$$

Storing $\betavec(\xvec)$ exactly would cost more than the code. Instead the weights are chosen from a small learnt
codebook of weight vectors, whose index costs a byte or so per vector. The refined estimate $\Nmat(\xvec)\betavec$
re-ranks candidates found by the graph walk. The method targets 64 to 128 bytes per vector and reports state-of-the-art
results at those sizes on the Deep1B and BIGANN billion-scale benchmarks <Cite id="douze2018" />.

### Spreading vectors (2019)

Learnt quantisers adapt codebooks to the data. Sablayrolles, Douze, Schmid and Jégou reverse this: they train a network,
the **catalyser**, that maps the data onto the unit sphere so that a fixed quantiser, such as the signs of the
coordinates or the integer points of a sphere, encodes it well <Cite id="sablayrolles2019" />. The training loss has two
parts. A triplet ranking loss preserves neighbourhoods. The **KoLeo** regulariser spreads the outputs uniformly. It is
derived from the Kozachenko–Leonenko estimator of <NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>, which is, up to constants, the average log
distance from each point to its nearest neighbour among $n$ samples:

$$
\Lcal_{\text{KoLeo}} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \rho_{n,i},
\qquad \rho_{n,i} = \min_{j \ne i}\norm{f(\xvec_i) - f(\xvec_j)}.
$$

Minimising it pushes each point away from its closest neighbour, with a force that weakens as they separate, so the
outputs fill the sphere. Uniform points use a fixed quantiser's cells evenly. The authors found that training without
the quantiser in the loop gives almost the same accuracy, so the catalyser can precede any quantiser.

### Benchmarks (2021, 2023)

Douze co-authored the results of the NeurIPS'21 billion-scale challenge, whose standard-hardware tracks used FAISS as the baseline,
and its 2023 successor on filtered, out-of-distribution, sparse and streaming search <Cite id="simhadri2022" />
<Cite id="simhadri2024" /> (see <NoteLink to="approximate-nearest-neighbour-benchmarking">benchmarking</NoteLink>).

### Neural quantisers (2024, 2025)

**QINCo** keeps <NoteLink to="additive-and-residual-quantisation">residual quantisation</NoteLink>'s greedy encoding but
lets a neural network produce the codebook of each step from the reconstruction so far <Cite id="huijben2024" />. The
authors report that 12-byte QINCo codes search more accurately than 16-byte codes of the previous best neural quantiser
on BigANN1M and Deep1M. **QINCo2** improves encoding and decoding and reports 44% lower <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> at 16 bytes on
BigANN <Cite id="vallaeys2025" />.

## What is distinctive

- **Compression first.** The line of work assumes that the vectors do not fit in memory uncompressed, and measures
  methods at a fixed number of bytes per vector.
- **Asymmetric distances.** Queries stay exact while database vectors are compressed. The idea recurs from PQ to QINCo.
- **Billion-scale evaluation.** The group introduced BIGANN and took part in running the billion-scale challenges, so methods
  were judged at a scale where the vectors do not fit in memory uncompressed.
- **Everything ends up in the library.** Methods are released as FAISS components that compose with the rest.

## Use and related approaches

FAISS indexes are used as components across Meta's research. Dense Passage Retrieval indexed 21 million Wikipedia
passages in an HNSW index from FAISS and answered 995 questions per second on a <Gloss name="cpu" /> server, returning 100 passages each
<Cite id="karpukhin2020" />. The nearest-neighbour <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> kNN-LM retrieves from a datastore of up to billions of
context vectors with a FAISS inverted file of 4096 cells and 64-byte codes, probing 32 cells per query
<Cite id="khandelwal2020" />. The FAISS paper describes an index of 1.5 trillion vectors <Cite id="douze2024" />.

Other groups took different routes to the same problem: graph-first indexes such as
<NoteLink to="hnsw">HNSW</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="graph-based-approximate-nearest-neighbours">DiskANN</NoteLink>, and loss-aware quantisation
in <NoteLink to="scann-and-anisotropic-quantisation">ScaNN</NoteLink>. FAISS includes HNSW and NSG <Cite id="douze2024" />, and its
GPU build can use NVIDIA's CAGRA graph index, so the approaches meet in one library.
