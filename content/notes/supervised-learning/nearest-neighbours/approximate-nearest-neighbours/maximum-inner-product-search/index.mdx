<Definition>
**Maximum inner product search** (MIPS) returns, for a query $\qvec \in \reals^d$ and stored vectors
$\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$, the index $\argmax_i \qvec^\top\xvec_i$, or the $k$ indices with the largest <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink>.
It differs from <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> whenever the stored vectors have different norms.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Many models score a pair by a dot product: a query embedding against item embeddings, a hidden state against the rows
of an output layer. Finding the top-scoring items is MIPS. Nearest-neighbour indexes rely on the triangle inequality
and on each point being its own nearest neighbour. The inner product has neither property. A stored vector $\xvec$ need
not have the largest inner product with itself: any $\yvec = 2\xvec$ scores $\xvec^\top\yvec = 2\norm{\xvec}^2$, more
than $\xvec^\top\xvec$. Long vectors dominate the results regardless of direction.

The identity

$$
\norm{\qvec - \xvec}^2 = \norm{\qvec}^2 + \norm{\xvec}^2 - 2\qvec^\top\xvec
$$

shows the link. For a fixed query, $\norm{\qvec}^2$ is a constant. If every stored vector has the same norm, the
smallest distance and the largest inner product pick the same point, and so does the largest cosine similarity. The
norms $\norm{\xvec}^2$ are what separate the problems. The reductions below make all stored norms equal by adding a
coordinate.

## Details

### Reduction by one extra coordinate

Let $\phi = \max_i \norm{\xvec_i}$. Bachrach et al. map stored vectors and queries to $\reals^{d+1}$
<Cite id="bachrach2014" />:

$$
\tilde\xvec = \Big(\sqrt{\phi^2 - \norm{\xvec}^2},\ \xvec\Big), \qquad \tilde\qvec = (0,\ \qvec).
$$

Every transformed stored vector has norm $\phi$, because
$\norm{\tilde\xvec}^2 = \phi^2 - \norm{\xvec}^2 + \norm{\xvec}^2 = \phi^2$. The extra coordinate of the query is 0, so
$\tilde\qvec^\top\tilde\xvec = \qvec^\top\xvec$ and $\norm{\tilde\qvec} = \norm{\qvec}$. Substituting into the identity,

$$
\norm{\tilde\qvec - \tilde\xvec}^2 = \norm{\qvec}^2 + \phi^2 - 2\qvec^\top\xvec.
$$

The first two terms are the same for every stored vector. The nearest $\tilde\xvec$ to $\tilde\qvec$ is therefore the
$\xvec$ with the largest inner product. Any nearest-neighbour index, exact or approximate, applied to the $\tilde\xvec$
solves MIPS.

### Symmetric and asymmetric LSH

Neyshabur and Srebro scale the data so that $\norm{\xvec} \le 1$ and use one transform for both sides,
$P(\xvec) = \big(\xvec, \sqrt{1 - \norm{\xvec}^2}\big)$, with a unit-norm query mapped to $(\qvec, 0)$
<Cite id="neyshabur2015" />. All transformed vectors are unit vectors, $P(\qvec)^\top P(\xvec) = \qvec^\top\xvec$, and
random-hyperplane <NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">LSH</NoteLink> for angles applies directly. They called
this **simple-LSH**; it is the same augmentation as above with $\phi = 1$.

Shrivastava and Li proposed earlier an **asymmetric** pair of transforms <Cite id="shrivastava2014" />. After scaling so
that $\norm{\xvec} \le U < 1$ and normalising the query,

$$
P(\xvec) = \big(\xvec,\ \norm{\xvec}^2,\ \norm{\xvec}^4,\ \dots,\ \norm{\xvec}^{2^m}\big), \qquad
Q(\qvec) = \big(\qvec,\ \tfrac12,\ \dots,\ \tfrac12\big).
$$

Then $\norm{P(\xvec)}^2 = \norm{\xvec}^2 + \norm{\xvec}^4 + \dots + \norm{\xvec}^{2^{m+1}}$, $\norm{Q(\qvec)}^2 = 1 + m/4$
and $Q(\qvec)^\top P(\xvec) = \qvec^\top\xvec + \tfrac12\big(\norm{\xvec}^2 + \dots + \norm{\xvec}^{2^m}\big)$. In
$\norm{Q - P}^2 = \norm{Q}^2 + \norm{P}^2 - 2Q^\top P$ all norm powers but the last cancel:

$$
\norm{Q(\qvec) - P(\xvec)}^2 = 1 + \frac m4 - 2\qvec^\top\xvec + \norm{\xvec}^{2^{m+1}}.
$$

Since $\norm{\xvec} < 1$, the last term vanishes quickly as $m$ grows, and Euclidean nearest-neighbour search on
$P(\xvec)$ ranks by inner product up to that small error.

### Direct methods

MIPS can also be solved without a reduction.

- **Trees.** Ram and Gray bound the largest inner product achievable inside a ball or a cone of stored vectors and
  prune branches that cannot beat the current best <Cite id="ram2012" />.
- **Quantisation.** Additive and product codes estimate $\qvec^\top\xvec$ as a sum of look-ups, which needs no norm
  term. Guo et al. quantise for inner products directly <Cite id="guo2016" />, and
  <NoteLink to="scann-and-anisotropic-quantisation">ScaNN</NoteLink> weights the quantisation error by its effect on
  large inner products <Cite id="guo2020" />.
- **Graphs and <NoteLink to="inverted-file-index">inverted files</NoteLink> with an inner-product score.** <Gloss name="hnsw" /> and IVF indexes run unchanged with $-\qvec^\top\xvec$
  in place of the distance. They lose their geometric justification but often work well in practice.

## Worked example

Take the query $\qvec = (1, 0)$ and two stored vectors $\xvec_a = (0.9, 0.1)$ and $\xvec_b = (2, 2)$. By <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">Euclidean
distance</NoteLink>, $\xvec_a$ is nearer: $\norm{\qvec - \xvec_a} = 0.141$ and $\norm{\qvec - \xvec_b} = 2.236$. By inner product,
$\xvec_b$ wins: $\qvec^\top\xvec_a = 0.9$ and $\qvec^\top\xvec_b = 2$.

The largest norm is $\phi = \norm{\xvec_b} = \sqrt 8 = 2.828$. The augmented vectors are
$\tilde\xvec_a = (\sqrt{8 - 0.82},\ 0.9,\ 0.1) = (2.680,\ 0.9,\ 0.1)$, $\tilde\xvec_b = (0,\ 2,\ 2)$ and
$\tilde\qvec = (0,\ 1,\ 0)$. The squared distances are
$\norm{\tilde\qvec - \tilde\xvec_a}^2 = 7.18 + 0.01 + 0.01 = 7.2$ and $\norm{\tilde\qvec - \tilde\xvec_b}^2 = 0 + 1 + 4 = 5$,
matching $\norm{\qvec}^2 + \phi^2 - 2\qvec^\top\xvec = 1 + 8 - 1.8$ and $1 + 8 - 4$. The nearest augmented vector is now
$\tilde\xvec_b$, the inner-product winner.

## Assumptions

- **A known maximum norm.** $\phi$ must bound every stored norm. Inserting a longer vector later requires a new $\phi$
  and re-transforming the data, or a margin chosen in advance.
- **Scores comparable across items.** MIPS returns the largest raw inner products. If the model's scores need
  calibration or a per-item bias, that bias can be appended as one more coordinate, with a 1 in the query.

## Pitfalls

- **Distorted geometry.** Vectors with small norms receive a large extra coordinate and sit far from every query in the
  augmented space. When norms vary widely, most points crowd at similar distances, and approximate indexes lose recall.
  Splitting the data into ranges of similar norm and reducing each range separately limits this.
- **Cosine is a different problem.** If the model is trained with cosine similarity, normalise the vectors and use
  ordinary nearest-neighbour search. MIPS on unnormalised vectors of such a model favours long vectors.
- **Normalising queries is harmless.** Scaling $\qvec$ by a positive constant scales every inner product by the same
  factor, so the ranking, and the MIPS answer, is unchanged.
