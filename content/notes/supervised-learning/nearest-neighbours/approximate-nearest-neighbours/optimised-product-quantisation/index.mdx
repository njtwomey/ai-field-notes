<Definition>
**Optimised product quantisation** (OPQ) chooses an orthogonal matrix $\Rmat \in \reals^{d \times d}$ and product
quantisation codebooks $\Ccal_1, \dots, \Ccal_M$ that jointly minimise the reconstruction error of the rotated data,

$$
\min_{\Rmat,\ \Ccal_1, \dots, \Ccal_M} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \norm{\Rmat\xvec_i - \hat\cvec(\Rmat\xvec_i)}^2
\quad \text{subject to } \Rmat^\top\Rmat = \eye,
$$

where $\hat\cvec(\cdot)$ is the nearest codeword in the product codebook $\Ccal_1 \times \dots \times \Ccal_M$
<Cite id="ge2013" />. Queries and database vectors are rotated by $\Rmat$ before encoding and search. Norouzi and Fleet
proposed the same model as Cartesian k-means <Cite id="norouzi2013" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="product-quantisation">Product quantisation</NoteLink> splits $\xvec$ into $M$ fixed groups of coordinates
and quantises each group independently. Two properties of the data then waste bits.

- **Correlation between groups.** If coordinate 1 in group 1 and coordinate 70 in group 2 move together, the product
  codebook spends codewords on combinations that never occur.
- **Unbalanced variance.** If one group carries most of the variance, its codebook of $K$ centroids is overloaded while
  another group's codebook quantises almost nothing.

A rotation does not change distances, since $\norm{\Rmat\xvec - \Rmat\yvec} = \norm{\xvec - \yvec}$ for orthogonal
$\Rmat$. So the search can happen in any rotated frame, and OPQ picks the frame in which the product codebook fits the
data best.

## Mechanism

### Alternating optimisation

Collect the training vectors as the columns of $\Xmat \in \reals^{d \times n}$. OPQ alternates two steps, each of which
cannot increase the objective <Cite id="ge2013" />.

1. **Fix $\Rmat$, update the codebooks.** Rotate the data, $\hat\Xmat = \Rmat\Xmat$. This is plain product
   quantisation of $\hat\Xmat$: run k-means in each subspace and encode. Collect the reconstructions (the concatenated
   chosen centroids) as the columns of $\Ymat \in \reals^{d \times n}$.
2. **Fix the codes, update $\Rmat$.** The reconstructions $\Ymat$ are now constants. Solve

   $$
   \min_{\Rmat} \norm{\Rmat\Xmat - \Ymat}_F^2 \quad \text{subject to } \Rmat^\top\Rmat = \eye.
   $$

This is the **<NoteLink to="procrustes-analysis">orthogonal Procrustes problem</NoteLink>**
<Cite id="schonemann1966" />. Expand the <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">Frobenius norm</NoteLink> with
$\norm{\Amat}_F^2 = \trace(\Amat^\top\Amat)$:

$$
\norm{\Rmat\Xmat - \Ymat}_F^2 = \trace(\Xmat^\top\Rmat^\top\Rmat\Xmat) + \norm{\Ymat}_F^2 - 2\trace(\Ymat^\top\Rmat\Xmat)
= \norm{\Xmat}_F^2 + \norm{\Ymat}_F^2 - 2\trace(\Rmat\Xmat\Ymat^\top).
$$

The first two terms do not depend on $\Rmat$ because $\Rmat^\top\Rmat = \eye$. The problem is to maximise
$\trace(\Rmat\Xmat\Ymat^\top)$. Take the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value
decomposition</NoteLink> $\Xmat\Ymat^\top = \Umat\Smat\Vmat^\top$, with $\Smat = \diag(s_1, \dots, s_d)$ and
$s_j \ge 0$. By the cyclic property of the trace,

$$
\trace(\Rmat\Umat\Smat\Vmat^\top) = \trace(\Vmat^\top\Rmat\Umat\,\Smat) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} z_{jj}\, s_j,
\qquad \Zmat = \Vmat^\top\Rmat\Umat.
$$

$\Zmat$ is a product of orthogonal matrices, so it is orthogonal and every entry satisfies $\abs{z_{jj}} \le 1$. The sum
is therefore at most $\sum_j s_j$, attained when $\Zmat = \eye$, that is

$$
\Rmat = \Vmat\Umat^\top.
$$

The alternation converges to a local minimum that depends on the starting rotation. Ge et al. warm-start each k-means
from the previous codebooks, running a single k-means iteration per outer step, and report that about 100 outer
iterations suffice <Cite id="ge2013" />.

### A parametric starting point

If the data are Gaussian, $\xvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat)$, rate-distortion theory bounds the
<Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> of any quantiser of a $d'$-dimensional Gaussian with $K$ codewords by
$E \ge K^{-2/d'}\, d' \,\abs{\Sigmamat}^{1/d'}$, and k-means comes within about 5% of this bound in Ge et al.'s
experiments <Cite id="ge2013" />. Applied to each of the $M$ subspaces of dimension $d' = d/M$, with
$\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}$ the covariance block of subspace $m$ after rotation, the total error is proportional to

$$
\sum_{m=1}^{M} \abs{\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}}^{M/d}.
$$

Two inequalities bound this sum from below. The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives
$\sum_m \abs{\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}}^{M/d} \ge M \prod_m \abs{\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}}^{1/d}$, with equality when all the
$\abs{\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}}$ are equal. Fischer's inequality for <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive
definite matrices</NoteLink> gives $\prod_m \abs{\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}} \ge \abs{\hat\Sigmamat} = \abs{\Sigmamat}$, with
equality when the off-diagonal blocks are zero. Together,

$$
\sum_{m=1}^{M} \abs{\hat\Sigmamat_{mm}}^{M/d} \ge M \abs{\Sigmamat}^{1/d}.
$$

The bound is met by a rotation with two properties: **<NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>** (the
subspaces are uncorrelated, which a rotation onto the <NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">principal
axes</NoteLink> achieves) and **balance** (the product of eigenvalues is the same in every subspace). Ge et al. reach
both with **eigenvalue allocation**: sort the eigenvalues of $\Sigmamat$ in decreasing order and assign each in turn to
the subspace whose eigenvalue product is currently smallest, among those not yet full. The principal directions, grouped
by subspace, define $\Rmat$. This parametric solution is optimal for Gaussian data and initialises the alternating
algorithm for real data.

## Worked example

Take $d = 4$, $M = 2$ and principal variances $8, 4, 2, 1$. Keeping the principal axes in order puts $\set{8, 4}$ in
subspace 1 and $\set{2, 1}$ in subspace 2. The objective is $\sqrt{32} + \sqrt{2} = 5.657 + 1.414 = 7.071$.

Eigenvalue allocation places 8 in subspace 1, then 4 in subspace 2 (product 1 is smaller than 8), then 2 in subspace 2
(product 4 is smaller than 8), then 1 in subspace 1, the only one not full. The subspaces are $\set{8, 1}$ and
$\set{4, 2}$, both with product 8. The objective is $\sqrt 8 + \sqrt 8 = 5.657$, equal to the lower bound
$2 \times 64^{1/4} = 5.657$. The Gaussian distortion bound falls by a factor $5.657/7.071 = 0.80$: 20% lower error at
the same code size.

## Cost

- **Training.** Each outer iteration costs one k-means pass per subspace, the rotation $\Rmat\Xmat$ in $O(d^2 n)$, and
  an SVD of a $d \times d$ matrix in $O(d^3)$.
- **Encoding and search.** Every vector and every query is multiplied by $\Rmat$ once, $O(d^2)$ each. Search is then
  unchanged product quantisation.
- **Dimension change.** In FAISS the transform `OPQ16_64` learns a $64 \times d$ matrix with orthonormal rows, so it
  can also reduce the dimension before a 16-sub-quantiser product quantiser (see <NoteLink to="faiss">FAISS</NoteLink>).

## Trade-offs

- **Gain depends on the data.** The rotation helps most when variance is concentrated in a few directions or strongly
  correlated across the default groups. For data whose coordinates are already independent and balanced it changes
  little.
- **Local optimum.** The alternating algorithm depends on its start. The eigenvalue allocation start is cheap and good.
- **A linear transform only.** OPQ keeps the product structure.
  <NoteLink to="additive-and-residual-quantisation">Additive quantisers</NoteLink> remove the split altogether and reach
  lower error at the same code size, at a higher encoding cost.
