<Definition>
Gaussian-process <NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink> places a zero-mean Gaussian-process prior with kernel $k(\xvec, \xvec')$ on a
latent function $f$, and relates it to an ordinal label $y \in \set{1, \dots, K}$ through thresholds
$b_1 < \dots < b_{K-1}$ and Gaussian noise of <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma$:

$$
P(y = k \mid f(\xvec)) = \Phi\LP\frac{b_k - f(\xvec)}{\sigma}\RP - \Phi\LP\frac{b_{k-1} - f(\xvec)}{\sigma}\RP,
$$

with $b_0 = -\infty$ and $b_K = +\infty$. The posterior over $f$ is not Gaussian; Chu and Ghahramani approximate it
with the Laplace approximation or expectation propagation, and choose the thresholds, the noise and the kernel's
parameters by maximising the approximate <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> <Cite id="chu2005gp" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="cumulative-link-model">ordered probit</NoteLink> cuts a linear score $\wvec^\top\xvec$ at
thresholds. Gaussian-process ordinal regression keeps the thresholds and replaces the linear score with a function
drawn from a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process" />. The class boundaries in input space then follow the level sets of a
smooth nonlinear function, and the model reports how uncertain the function is away from the data.

## The ordinal likelihood

The likelihood follows from the latent-variable view. Suppose the observed grade is the interval of the thresholds
into which $f(\xvec) + \delta$ falls, with $\delta \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. Then

$$
P(y = k \mid f) = P(b_{k-1} < f + \delta \le b_k) = P\LP\frac{b_{k-1} - f}{\sigma} < \frac{\delta}{\sigma} \le \frac{b_k - f}{\sigma}\RP
= \Phi(z_1) - \Phi(z_2),
$$

where $z_1 = (b_k - f)/\sigma$ and $z_2 = (b_{k-1} - f)/\sigma$. With $K = 2$ this is the probit likelihood of
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification" />. With a linear kernel $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \xvec^\top\xvec'$, it is a
Bayesian ordered probit.

**<NoteLink to="derivative">Derivatives</NoteLink> in $f$.** Write $p = \Phi(z_1) - \Phi(z_2)$ and $\phi$ for the standard normal density. Since
$\partial z_j/\partial f = -1/\sigma$ and $\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)$,

$$
\frac{\partial \log p}{\partial f} = -\frac{\phi(z_1) - \phi(z_2)}{\sigma p}, \qquad
-\frac{\partial^2 \log p}{\partial f^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\LB\LP\frac{\phi(z_1) - \phi(z_2)}{p}\RP^2 + \frac{z_1\phi(z_1) - z_2\phi(z_2)}{p}\RB.
$$

The second quantity is positive for every $f$: the Gaussian cdf difference is log-concave in $f$, so the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative
log-likelihood</NoteLink> is convex and the posterior has a single mode. An implementation computes $\log p$ in the tail that
both ends share, because $\Phi(z_1) - \Phi(z_2)$ underflows for $f$ far outside its interval when $\sigma$ is small.

## Laplace approximation

Let $\fvec = (f(\xvec_1), \dots, f(\xvec_N))$, $\Kmat$ the kernel matrix and $\yvec$ the labels. The log posterior is,
up to a constant,

$$
\Psi(\fvec) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(y_i \mid f_i) - \tfrac12 \fvec^\top\Kmat^{-1}\fvec.
$$

**Mode.** Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is $\nabla\log p(\yvec \mid \fvec) - \Kmat^{-1}\fvec$ and its <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> is $-\Wmat - \Kmat^{-1}$,
where $\Wmat$ is the diagonal matrix of the second derivatives above. <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> gives the update

$$
\fvec \leftarrow (\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat)^{-1}\big(\Wmat\fvec + \nabla\log p(\yvec \mid \fvec)\big),
$$

computed stably through the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink> of $\Bmat = \eye + \Wmat^{1/2}\Kmat\Wmat^{1/2}$ <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.
The figure halves the Newton step whenever $\Psi$ would decrease, which matters when $\sigma$ is small and the
likelihood is nearly a step. The Laplace approximation replaces the posterior by
$\Gauss(\hat\fvec, (\Kmat^{-1} + \Wmat)^{-1})$ around the mode $\hat\fvec$. See
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation" />.

**Prediction.** At a new input $\xvec_*$ with kernel vector $\kvec_*$, the approximate latent posterior is Gaussian with

$$
\mu_* = \kvec_*^\top\nabla\log p(\yvec \mid \hat\fvec), \qquad
v_* = k(\xvec_*, \xvec_*) - \kvec_*^\top(\Kmat + \Wmat^{-1})^{-1}\kvec_*.
$$

The class probabilities integrate the likelihood against this Gaussian. The integral has a closed form:

$$
P(y_* = k \mid \Dcal) = \Phi\LP\frac{b_k - \mu_*}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + v_*}}\RP - \Phi\LP\frac{b_{k-1} - \mu_*}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + v_*}}\RP.
$$

Uncertainty about $f$ adds to the observation noise, so the predicted classes spread out where data are sparse.

<Derivation title="The predictive class probability">
Write $\Phi((b - f)/\sigma) = P(f + \delta \le b)$ with $\delta \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$ independent of $f$. If
$f \sim \Gauss(\mu_*, v_*)$, the sum $f + \delta$ is Gaussian with mean $\mu_*$ and variance $v_* + \sigma^2$. So

$$
\int \Phi\LP\frac{b - f}{\sigma}\RP \Gauss(f; \mu_*, v_*)\, df = P(f + \delta \le b) = \Phi\LP\frac{b - \mu_*}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + v_*}}\RP.
$$

Applying this to both thresholds and subtracting gives the class probability.
</Derivation>

## Setting the thresholds

The thresholds, the noise $\sigma$ and the kernel parameters are hyperparameters. The Laplace approximation to the
log marginal likelihood is

$$
\log p(\yvec \mid \Xmat) \approx -\tfrac12 \hat\fvec^\top\Kmat^{-1}\hat\fvec + \sum_i \log p(y_i \mid \hat f_i) - \tfrac12\log\abs{\Bmat},
$$

and Chu and Ghahramani maximise it by gradient ascent, parametrising $b_1$ and the positive gaps
$b_k - b_{k-1}$ <Cite id="chu2005gp" />. The readout in the figure shows this quantity. Dragging a threshold into the
middle of a class's training points lowers it; the thresholds that best separate the posterior modes raise it.

**Expectation propagation** replaces the local Gaussian at the mode with a moment-matched Gaussian site for each
likelihood term. For the ordinal likelihood the moments of a Gaussian truncated to an interval are available in
closed form. Chu and Ghahramani derive both approximations and compare them on benchmark data <Cite id="chu2005gp" />.
Expectation propagation costs more per iteration, because every site is refined in turn. See <NoteLink to="expectation-propagation" />.

## Assumptions

- **A single latent function.** One $f$ is cut at all thresholds, so the model keeps the single-score structure of the
  cumulative model. The function is nonlinear, so the class regions in input space are not parallel bands.
- **Gaussian latent noise** of the same scale everywhere. The probit form comes from it.
- **Smoothness from the kernel.** The kernel's lengthscale sets how fast the grade can change with the input.

## Pitfalls

- **Cubic cost.** Exact inference costs $O(N^3)$ time and $O(N^2)$ memory, as for any Gaussian process. Sparse
  approximations apply.
- **Scale non-identifiability.** Multiplying $f$, the thresholds and $\sigma$ by a constant leaves the likelihood
  unchanged; the kernel amplitude fixes the scale of $f$. Fixing $\sigma$ or the amplitude avoids a flat direction in
  <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink>.
- **Empty classes.** A class with no training labels leaves its thresholds unconstrained by the likelihood.
