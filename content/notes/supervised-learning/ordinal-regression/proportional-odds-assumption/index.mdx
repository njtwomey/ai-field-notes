<Definition>
A cumulative logit model $\operatorname{logit} P(y > k \mid \xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec - \theta_k$ uses one weight vector
$\wvec$ for all $K - 1$ splits. The proportional odds assumption is that this restriction holds: the split-specific
weight vectors $\wvec_1, \dots, \wvec_{K-1}$ of the unrestricted model are equal. Brant's test estimates each $\wvec_k$
by a separate binary <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> of $y > k$ on $\xvec$ and tests
their equality with a Wald statistic that has a $\chi^2$ distribution with $D(K - 2)$ degrees of freedom under the null
<Cite id="brant1990" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

The unrestricted model, the **generalised ordered logit**, gives each split its own weights:

$$
\operatorname{logit} P(y > k \mid \xvec) = \wvec_k^\top\xvec - \theta_k, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K - 1.
$$

- **Null:** $\wvec_1 = \wvec_2 = \dots = \wvec_{K-1}$. The splits are parallel on the logit scale, and the model is the
  proportional odds model of <NoteLink to="cumulative-link-model" />.
- **Alternative:** at least one component of at least one $\wvec_k$ differs from the others.

A per-input version tests $w_{1j} = \dots = w_{K-1,j}$ for one input $j$. Rejecting for some inputs and not others
leads to the **partial proportional odds** model, in which only the offending inputs get split-specific weights
<Cite id="peterson1990" /> <Cite id="williams2006gologit" />.

## Assumptions

- The observations are independent.
- Each binary split $y > k$ follows a logistic regression in $\xvec$. The test checks equality of slopes, not the
  logistic form.
- Every split has enough observations on both sides for its logistic regression to be estimated. Sparse extreme
  classes make the slope estimates for the outer splits unstable.
- The sample is large enough for the estimates to be approximately Gaussian, which the $\chi^2$ reference distribution
  needs.

## Test statistic

**Separate fits.** For each $k$, fit the binary logistic regression of $t_{ik} = \indicator[y_i > k]$ on
$\tilde\xvec_i = (1, \xvec_i)$. Call the estimate $\hat\betavec_k$ (intercept and slopes), the fitted probabilities
$\pi_{ik}$, and the inverse <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss>
$\Vmat_k = (\sum_i \pi_{ik}(1 - \pi_{ik})\tilde\xvec_i\tilde\xvec_i^\top)^{-1}$.

**Their joint covariance.** The separate estimates come from the same data, so they are correlated. Each estimate
solves its score equation $\uvec_k(\betavec_k) = \sum_i \tilde\xvec_i (t_{ik} - \pi_{ik}) = \zeros$. A first-order
expansion around the true value gives $\hat\betavec_k - \betavec_k \approx \Vmat_k \uvec_k$, so

$$
\cov(\hat\betavec_k, \hat\betavec_l) \approx \Vmat_k \cov(\uvec_k, \uvec_l) \Vmat_l.
$$

The score covariance is a sum over independent examples of $\tilde\xvec_i\tilde\xvec_i^\top \cov(t_{ik}, t_{il})$. For
$k < l$, the event $y > l$ implies $y > k$, so $t_{ik} t_{il} = t_{il}$ and

$$
\cov(t_{ik}, t_{il}) = \expect[t_{il}] - \expect[t_{ik}]\expect[t_{il}] = \pi_{il}(1 - \pi_{ik}).
$$

Hence
$\cov(\hat\betavec_k, \hat\betavec_l) \approx \Vmat_k \LP\sum_i \pi_{il}(1 - \pi_{ik}) \tilde\xvec_i\tilde\xvec_i^\top\RP \Vmat_l$,
and $\cov(\hat\betavec_k, \hat\betavec_k) = \Vmat_k$ <Cite id="brant1990" />.

**Wald statistic.** Stack the slope parts (without the intercepts) into $\hat\bvec = (\hat\wvec_1, \dots,
\hat\wvec_{K-1}) \in \reals^{D(K-1)}$ with covariance $\Sigmamat$ assembled from the blocks above. Let $\Dmat$ be the
$D(K - 2) \times D(K - 1)$ matrix of adjacent differences, so that $\Dmat\hat\bvec$ stacks
$\hat\wvec_1 - \hat\wvec_2, \dots, \hat\wvec_{K-2} - \hat\wvec_{K-1}$. Under the null $\Dmat\bvec = \zeros$, and

$$
X^2 = (\Dmat\hat\bvec)^\top \big(\Dmat\Sigmamat\Dmat^\top\big)^{-1} (\Dmat\hat\bvec) \;\sim\; \chi^2_{D(K-2)}
$$

approximately. The degrees of freedom count the independent equalities: $K - 2$ per input. For a single input $j$, keep
only its rows, which gives $K - 2$ degrees of freedom.

**Likelihood-ratio alternative.** Fit both the proportional odds model and the generalised ordered logit by maximum
likelihood. Twice the difference in log-likelihood is also approximately $\chi^2_{D(K-2)}$ under the null. The
generalised ordered logit is harder to fit: its cumulative curves can cross, which gives negative class probabilities,
so the optimiser needs constraints. Brant's test avoids fitting it. A score test, which needs only the restricted fit,
is a third option <Cite id="peterson1990" />.

## Decision rule

Reject proportional odds at level $\alpha$ when $X^2$ exceeds the $1 - \alpha$ quantile of $\chi^2_{D(K-2)}$,
equivalently when the <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> $P(\chi^2_{D(K-2)} \ge X^2)$ is below $\alpha$. With
$K = 4$ and one input the reference is $\chi^2_2$, whose 0.95 quantile is 5.99.

## Effect size

The test says whether the slopes differ, not by how much. Report the separate slope estimates beside the shared one,
as in the figure's readouts. A practical measure is the largest change in any predicted class probability, over the
range of the inputs, between the proportional odds fit and the generalised ordered logit. A statistically significant
difference that moves no predicted probability by more than a few hundredths may not justify the larger model.

## Pitfalls

- **Large samples reject small departures.** With thousands of observations the test detects slope differences too small
  to matter. In the figure, 2000 observations with a spread of 0.1 give slopes of 1.19, 1.16 and 1.37 and a p-value of
  0.02.
- **Small samples miss large departures.** The outer splits have few observations on one side, so their slope estimates
  have large <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink>. In the figure, 100 observations with a spread of
  0.4 give slopes near 0.7 and 1.6, and the test still does not reject at the 0.05 level.
- **The logit link only.** Brant's covariance formula uses binary logistic fits. For a probit or other link, use the
  likelihood-ratio or score test with the matching generalised model.
- **Rejecting does not pick the alternative.** A failed test can point to a partial proportional odds model, to a
  location–scale model in which inputs also change the latent noise scale <Cite id="mccullagh1980" />, to a
  <Gloss name="continuation-ratio-model">continuation-ratio model</Gloss>, or to a nonlinear score. Each fits
  non-parallel splits in a different way.
