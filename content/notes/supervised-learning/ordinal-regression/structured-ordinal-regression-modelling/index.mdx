<Definition>
Structured ordinal regression modelling (StORM) maps an ordinal label $\hat y \in \set{1, \dots, K}$ to a binary
sequence $\yvec = (y_1, \dots, y_{K-1})$ with $y_n = \indicator[n < \hat y]$, and models $P(\yvec \mid \xvec)$ with a
linear-chain conditional random field over the $K - 1$ positions. Node $n$ has potential $\psivec_n = \exp\set{\Wmat_n
\xvec}$ with $\Wmat_n \in \reals^{2 \times D}$, and the edge between positions $n$ and $n + 1$ has potential
$\Psimat_n = \exp\set{\Umat_n \xvec} \in \reals^{2 \times 2}$. Unlike a standard chain CRF, no weights are shared
between positions. All parameters are learnt jointly by maximum likelihood <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.
</Definition>

## The up-to-$k$ encoding

The encoding writes a class as a run of ones followed by a run of zeros. With $K = 7$ classes,

$$
\hat y = 3 \mapsto (1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0), \qquad \hat y = 4 \mapsto (1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0), \qquad \hat y = 5 \mapsto (1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0).
$$

Bit $n$ answers the question "is $\hat y > n$?", so the code is the vector of cumulative binary targets used by
<NoteLink to="binary-decomposition-for-ordinal-regression">binary decomposition</NoteLink>. Two labels share every
bit outside the range between them. Classes 3 and 5 agree on the first two bits (both on) and the last two (both off),
and differ only in bits 3 and 4. The number of differing bits is exactly $\abs{\hat y - \hat y'}$. A model that
predicts the bits well therefore predicts close classes for close labels <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.

Only $K$ of the $2^{K-1}$ bit patterns are valid codes. A valid code never has a 0 followed by a 1. A chain model over
the bits can represent that constraint through its edges, which independent binary classifiers cannot.

## The model

StORM treats the $K - 1$ bits as a sequence and models them with a conditional random field, in the notation of
<NoteLink to="conditional-random-field" /> <Cite id="twomey2016" />. The input $\xvec \in \reals^D$ includes a
constant feature for the bias.

- **Node potentials.** $\psivec_n = \exp\set{\Wmat_n \xvec} \in \reals^2$ scores bit $n$ being 0 or 1.
- **Edge potentials.** $\Psimat_n = \exp\set{\Umat_n \xvec} \in \reals^{2 \times 2}$ scores each pair of values of bits
  $n$ and $n + 1$, with $\Umat_n \in \reals^{2 \times 2 \times D}$.

The probability of a bit sequence is the product of its potentials, normalised over all $2^{K-1}$ sequences:

$$
P(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \frac{1}{Z(\xvec)} \prod_{n=1}^{K-1} \psivec_n(y_n) \prod_{n=1}^{K-2} \Psimat_n(y_n, y_{n+1}).
$$

**No weight sharing.** A standard chain CRF uses the same weights at every position, because the dynamics of a
sentence or a sensor stream do not depend on the position. Here position $n$ is a specific threshold between classes
$n$ and $n + 1$, and an input may matter more for one threshold than another. StORM therefore gives every node and
edge its own weights. The parameters are $\Theta = \set{\Wmat_1, \dots, \Wmat_{K-1}, \Umat_1, \dots, \Umat_{K-2}}$.

**Inference.** The chain has $K - 1$ positions with 2 states each, so forward–backward computes $Z(\xvec)$ and every
marginal in $O(K)$ operations. With $\gammavec_n = \alphavec_n \odot \psivec_n$ and
$\deltavec_{n+1} = \betavec_{n+1} \odot \psivec_{n+1}$,

$$
\alphavec_{n+1} = \Psimat_n^\top \gammavec_n, \qquad \betavec_n = \Psimat_n \deltavec_{n+1}, \qquad \alphavec_1 = \betavec_{K-1} = \ones,
$$

$$
P(y_n \mid \xvec) = \frac{\alphavec_n \odot \psivec_n \odot \betavec_n}{Z}, \qquad
P(y_n = u, y_{n+1} = v \mid \xvec) = \frac{\gammavec_n(u)\, \Psimat_n(u, v)\, \deltavec_{n+1}(v)}{Z}.
$$

Every sequence has the same length, $K - 1$, so the messages can be passed for all training examples at once as
matrix operations <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.

**Learning.** The <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative log-likelihood</NoteLink> of the encoded labels,
$-\sum_{i} \log P(\yvec_i \mid \xvec_i, \Theta)$, is convex in $\Theta$, as for any log-linear model. Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with
respect to row $u$ of $\Wmat_n$ is the familiar "expected minus observed" form:

$$
\frac{\partial\, \mathrm{NLL}}{\partial \Wmat_n(u)} = \sum_{i} \big(P(y_{i,n} = u \mid \xvec_i) - \indicator[y_{i,n} = u]\big)\, \xvec_i,
$$

and the edge weights have the same form with pairwise marginals. Twomey et al. optimise it with <Gloss name="l-bfgs" /> and an
$\ell_2$ penalty chosen by cross-validation <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.

### Worked example

Take $K = 3$, so two bits and the valid codes $00$, $10$, $11$ for classes 1, 2, 3. Let the potentials at some input be
$\psivec_1 = (1, 2)$, $\psivec_2 = (2, 1)$ (entries for bit value 0 and 1), and an edge potential that forbids a 0
followed by a 1:

$$
\Psimat_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

- **Sequence weights.** $00$: $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 2$. $10$: $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 4$. $11$: $2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 2$.
  $01$: $1 \cdot 1 \cdot 0 = 0$. So $Z = 8$ and $P(\hat y = 1, 2, 3) = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25)$.
- **Forward.** $\alphavec_1 = (1, 1)$, $\gammavec_1 = (1, 2)$ and $\alphavec_2 = \Psimat_1^\top\gammavec_1 = (3, 2)$.
- **Marginal of bit 2.** $\alphavec_2 \odot \psivec_2 \odot \betavec_2 = (6, 2)$, which sums to $Z = 8$. So
  $P(y_2 = 1) = 0.25$, which is $P(\hat y = 3)$ because only class 3 has its second bit on.
- **Pairwise marginal.** With $\deltavec_2 = \betavec_2 \odot \psivec_2 = (2, 1)$,
  $P(y_1 = 1, y_2 = 0) = \gammavec_1(1)\,\Psimat_1(1, 0)\,\deltavec_2(0)/Z = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2/8 = 0.5$, which is
  $P(\hat y = 2)$.

## Predictions and queries

**Class predictions.** A CRF offers several predictions: the Viterbi path, per-position marginals, or the most
probable valid code. Twomey et al. use the Viterbi path <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />. The figures in this note
predict the most probable valid code.

**Invalid codes.** A 0 followed by a 1 encodes no class. Setting $\Psimat_n(0, 1) = 0$ forbids it, as in the worked
example. Twomey et al. leave the transition free and report early evidence that inputs whose predictions are invalid
tend to be outliers <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />. The figures report the mass on invalid codes.

**Interval queries.** For a valid code, $\hat y \ge a$ exactly when bit $a - 1$ is on, and $\hat y \le b$ exactly when
bit $b$ is off. So

$$
P(a \le \hat y \le b \mid \xvec) = P(y_{a-1} = 1, y_b = 0 \mid \xvec),
$$

a marginal of the chain. For neighbouring bits it is an edge marginal: $P(y_3 = 0, y_4 = 0)$, $P(y_3 = 1, y_4 = 0)$ and
$P(y_3 = 1, y_4 = 1)$ are $P(\hat y < 4)$, $P(\hat y = 4)$ and $P(\hat y > 4)$. Clinical scales are often recorded
finely and then grouped into bands such as "moderate"; a StORM model answers the banded question directly without
retraining <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.

The identity holds on valid codes only. The chain marginal $P(y_{a-1} = 1, y_b = 0 \mid \xvec)$ also counts every
invalid code with those two bits. With linear potentials, the scores of some invalid codes grow fastest as $\xvec$ moves
away from the data, so far from the training points almost all the mass is on invalid codes and the chain marginal can
be close to 1 for any interval. Conditioning on a valid code avoids this:

$$
P(a \le \hat y \le b \mid \xvec, \text{valid}) = \frac{\sum_{k=a}^{b} P(\hat y = k \mid \xvec)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} P(\hat y = k \mid \xvec)}.
$$

On the spiral with $K = 9$ and 70 points per class, the chain marginal for $2 \le \hat y \le 4$ exceeds 0.5 on 70% of
the plane beyond the data, and the conditional exceeds 0.5 nowhere there. The figure shows both, and the invalid-code
mass that separates them. The invalid mass is a signal in its own right: Twomey et al. note that invalid predictions
tend to fall on outlying data <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.

**Feature expansions.** With potentials linear in $\xvec$, the log-probability of every code is a linear function
of $\xvec$ minus the shared $\log Z(\xvec)$. The boundary between two codes is therefore a straight line, and the
region where one code is most probable is an intersection of half-planes, a convex polygon. A curved band of classes,
such as a turn of the spiral, is covered by a chain of such polygons, which is why the maps of a linear StORM are made
of straight-edged wedges and bands. Replacing $\xvec$ by features $\phivec(\xvec)$ in both the node and the edge
potentials leaves the model and its inference unchanged, and the boundaries become level sets of linear functions of
$\phivec$, which are curves in the plane. Twomey et al. use a Nyström approximation of a radial basis function kernel
for this <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />. The paper does not state its settings, so the figure uses 64 landmarks
sampled from the training points and the median distance between landmarks as the lengthscale. It also offers
polynomial expansions of degree 2 and 3. Every feature is standardised on the training set; the $\ell_2$ strength is
$10^{-3}$, or $10^{-2}$ for the 64 Nyström features, which overfit 100 training points at the smaller value. Far
from every landmark the Nyström features are constant, so there the model falls back on the class probabilities its
constant terms give, which can favour any class.

## Relation to other ordinal models

StORM sits in the decomposition family of the ordinal taxonomy: it models the answers to "is $\hat y > n$?"
<Cite id="gutierrez2016" />. Two special cases make its position precise.

**Without edges it is Frank and Hall.** Set every $\Umat_n = 0$. The edge potentials become matrices of ones, the
normaliser factorises, and $P(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \prod_n P(y_n \mid \xvec)$ with
$P(y_n = 1 \mid \xvec) = \sigma\big((\Wmat_n(1) - \Wmat_n(0))\xvec\big)$. The log-likelihood splits into $K - 1$
independent <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regressions</NoteLink> of $\indicator[\hat y > n]$ on $\xvec$, which is the decomposition of Frank and Hall
<Cite id="frank2001" />. The edges are what couple the bits: they let one model learn that bit $n + 1$ should switch off
once bit $n$ has. The analysis of <NoteLink to="when-is-structure-needed" /> makes the same point for sequence labels.

**Restricted to valid codes it is a reparametrised multinomial logit.** Suppose invalid codes are forbidden. The only
sequences left are the $K$ codes, and the score of class $k$ is a sum of the potentials its code switches on. Compare
the codes of classes $k + 1$ and $k$: they differ at bit $k$, at the edge into bit $k$ and at the edge out of it. The
log-ratio is therefore

$$
\log\frac{P(\hat y = k + 1 \mid \xvec)}{P(\hat y = k \mid \xvec)} = \vvec_k^\top\xvec,
$$

where $\vvec_k$ collects the difference of the node weights at bit $k$ and of the edge weights on either side. Each
$\vvec_k$ has its own free parameters, so the adjacent log-ratios are arbitrary linear functions of $\xvec$. That is the
same family of distributions as <NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression" />. The order enters through the
parametrisation: each weight matrix governs one boundary between neighbouring classes, so an $\ell_2$ penalty on the
weights shrinks neighbouring classes towards each other rather than every class towards the mean. The unrestricted
model adds the invalid codes to the normaliser.

## Evidence

Twomey et al. compare StORM with the <Gloss name="pom">ordered logit</Gloss>, nested binary classifiers and multinomial logistic regression, all
linear in their parameters, on synthetic manifolds (linear, sine, circle, spiral), ordinal versions of UCI regression
datasets, larger benchmark datasets and two Alzheimer's disease datasets <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />. StORM has the
best average rank on <Gloss name="macro-mae">macro-averaged mean absolute error</Gloss> and 0–1 loss in most comparisons. Its advantage is
statistically significant on the synthetic data, the larger datasets and the five-class UCI tasks. On the ten-class UCI
tasks and the healthcare data the ordered logit is not significantly worse. On the synthetic manifolds, the ordered logit cannot follow curved class boundaries, because its
single score gives parallel bands. Nyström features for a radial basis kernel let the linear StORM follow a spiral.

## Assumptions

- **Linear potentials.** Node and edge potentials are log-linear in $\xvec$. Nonlinear boundaries need a feature map,
  such as a <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">kernel approximation</NoteLink> or a neural network.
- **Enough data per class boundary.** Without weight sharing the model has $2D(K - 1) + 4D(K - 2)$ weights, more than
  multinomial logistic regression. Twomey et al. attribute StORM's larger gains on bigger datasets to this.

## Pitfalls

- **Invalid codes.** Unless forbidden, some probability goes to sequences that encode no class. Decide whether to
  renormalise over valid codes, to forbid invalid transitions, or to flag invalid predictions.
- **Prediction rule.** The Viterbi path, the most probable valid code and the per-bit marginals can disagree. Choose the
  rule that matches the evaluation metric.
