<Definition>
Ordinal regression learns a predictor $f: \Xcal \to \set{1, 2, \dots, K}$ from examples $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ whose labels
are ordered categories. The order $1 < 2 < \dots < K$ is known, but the distance between neighbouring categories is not.
A mistake that predicts $k$ for true class $y$ costs more the larger $\abs{k - y}$ is. Most ordinal models learn a real
score and $K - 1$ ordered cut points that divide the score into the $K$ categories
<Cite id="mccullagh1980" /> <Cite id="gutierrez2016" />.
</Definition>

## What makes a label ordinal

A label is ordinal when its values can be ranked but not measured. Star ratings from 1 to 5, a pain scale, the stage
of a disease, a credit grade and a survey answer from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree" are ordinal. The label
$y = 4$ is higher than $y = 2$. The label does not say that the step from 2 to 3 equals the step from 3 to 4, and it
does not say that 4 is twice 2.

Three kinds of target differ in the structure they carry:

| Target      | Example                | Order | Distances | Natural loss                  |
| ----------- | ---------------------- | ----- | --------- | ----------------------------- |
| Nominal     | species, colour        | no    | no        | 0–1 loss                      |
| Ordinal     | rating, disease grade  | yes   | no        | a cost that grows with $\abs{k - y}$ |
| Interval    | temperature, price     | yes   | yes       | squared or absolute error     |

Ordinal labels often come from a continuous quantity that was recorded coarsely. A clinician grades cognitive
decline as healthy, mild, moderate or severe; the decline itself is continuous
<Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />. This view, a hidden continuous score cut into intervals, underlies most of the
methods in this category.

## Why classification and regression both fall short

**Multiclass classification ignores the order.** A multiclass model such as
<NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression">multinomial logistic regression</NoteLink> gives class $k$ its own
weight vector $\wvec_k$ and sets $P(y = k \mid \xvec) \propto \exp(\wvec_k^\top \xvec)$. Relabel the classes by any
permutation $\pi$ and relabel the weight vectors the same way: every probability is unchanged, so the likelihood is
unchanged. The model therefore cannot use the fact that class 3 lies between classes 2 and 4. It fits $K$ weight
vectors where an ordinal model fits one score, and its 0–1 training loss charges predicting 5 for a true 1 the
same as predicting 2.

**Regression assumes equal spacing.** Coding the classes as the numbers $1, \dots, K$, fitting least squares and
rounding the prediction $\hat y = \wvec^\top\xvec$ to the nearest integer makes two hidden choices. It fixes the cut
points at $1.5, 2.5, \dots, K - 0.5$, and it treats every step between classes as the same size. If the residuals are
Gaussian with <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma$, the implied class probabilities are

$$
P(y = k \mid \xvec) = \Phi\LP\frac{k + \tfrac12 - \wvec^\top\xvec}{\sigma}\RP - \Phi\LP\frac{k - \tfrac12 - \wvec^\top\xvec}{\sigma}\RP,
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss>. This is a cumulative probit model whose cut points
are fixed rather than learnt. When the true categories have unequal widths, for example a narrow "moderate" band
between wide "mild" and "severe" bands, the fixed cut points put the boundaries in the wrong places.

**Ordinal models keep the order and learn the spacing.** An ordinal model learns one score $\eta(\xvec)$ and $K - 1$
cut points $\theta_1 < \dots < \theta_{K-1}$ from the data. The order of the classes is built in through the order of
the cut points. The spacing is a parameter, so a narrow category gets a narrow interval.

## A map of the methods

Gutiérrez et al. sort ordinal methods into three families <Cite id="gutierrez2016" />, and two later families fit
alongside them.

- **Naive approaches** treat the problem as nominal classification, as regression, or as cost-sensitive classification
  with a cost matrix that grows with $\abs{k - y}$ (<NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning" />).
- **Threshold models** assume a latent score cut at learnt thresholds.
  - <NoteLink to="cumulative-link-model">Cumulative link models</NoteLink> (proportional odds, ordered probit) are the
    likelihood-based classic <Cite id="mccullagh1980" />.
  - <NoteLink to="continuation-ratio-and-adjacent-category-models">Continuation-ratio and adjacent-category
    models</NoteLink> use other binary comparisons with the same linear score.
  - <NoteLink to="threshold-losses-for-ordinal-regression">Threshold losses</NoteLink> give margin-based versions:
    <Gloss name="svor">support vector ordinal regression</Gloss> and the <Gloss name="all-threshold-loss">all-threshold loss</Gloss> <Cite id="rennie2005" />.
  - <NoteLink to="ordering-constraints-on-ordinal-thresholds">Ordering constraints</NoteLink> keep the thresholds of
    any of these models in order, and their <Gloss name="kkt" /> multipliers show which neighbouring classes the data
    merge.
  - <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-ordinal-regression">Gaussian-process ordinal regression</NoteLink> replaces the
    linear score with a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> <Cite id="chu2005gp" />.
- **Decomposition models** split the task into binary questions "is $y > k$?".
  <NoteLink to="binary-decomposition-for-ordinal-regression">Binary decomposition</NoteLink> trains $K - 1$ classifiers
  and combines them <Cite id="frank2001" />.
- **Deep ordinal models** put an ordinal output layer on a neural network: shared-weight binary heads (<Gloss name="consistent-rank-logits" />),
  conditional heads (<Gloss name="corn" />) and soft ordinal targets (<Gloss name="sord" />). See
  <NoteLink to="deep-ordinal-regression" />.
- **Structured models** encode the label as a sequence of $K - 1$ bits and model the bits jointly.
  <NoteLink to="structured-ordinal-regression-modelling" /> fits a chain <Gloss name="crf">conditional random field</Gloss> to that encoding
  <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />.

Whether a cumulative model's single score is adequate is testable; see
<NoteLink to="proportional-odds-assumption" />.

## Datasets for the figures

Every figure in this category that shows two-dimensional data draws it from one generator, modelled on the synthetic
manifolds of Twomey et al. <Cite id="twomey2019ordinal" />. The paper projects ordinal data onto four curves in the
plane, named Linear, Sine, Circle and Spiral, with $K = 5$ or $K = 10$ classes, 100 training points and 1000 test
points. The paper does not give the curves' equations or the noise level; the ones below are this site's choices.

**The latent coordinate.** Each point has a hidden ordinal coordinate $t \in [0, 1]$, and class $k$ owns the
interval $[(k - 1)/K,\ k/K)$. Every class gets the same number of points: for each class, $t$ is drawn uniformly
within its interval. The training set has $m$ points per class and the test set 200 per class, so $mK$ training points
and $200K$ test points in all.

**The curve.** A curve $\gammavec: [0, 1] \to \reals^2$ carries $t$ into the plane, and the point is the curve plus
isotropic Gaussian noise,

$$
\xvec = \gammavec(t) + \sigma\, s(t)\, \epsilonvec, \qquad \epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye),
$$

where $\sigma$ is the noise level and $s(t)$ scales it along the curve. The four curves are

| Dataset | Curve $\gammavec(t)$                                                        | Noise scale $s(t)$  |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| linear  | $(5t - 2.5)\,(\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ)$                                 | 1                   |
| sine    | $\big(5t - 2.5,\ 1.5 \sin 4\pi t\big)$                                       | 1                   |
| arc     | $2.2\,(\cos a, \sin a) - (0, 0.3)$ with $a = -\pi/4 + 3\pi t/2$              | 1                   |
| spiral  | $r(\theta)\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ with $\theta = K\pi t/2$, $r = 0.4 + 0.3\,\theta$ | $r(t)/r(\tfrac12)$ |

The spiral gives every class a quarter turn, so $K$ classes wind $K/4$ turns: more classes make a longer spiral, not
a finer cut of the same one. Its radius grows linearly with the angle (an Archimedean spiral), so neighbouring turns
are $2\pi \times 0.3 \approx 1.9$ apart. Its noise is proportional to the radius, divided by the radius half-way
along the curve so that $\sigma$ has the same average size as on the other curves; the inner turns stay tight and the
outer turns spread, as in the paper's spiral. The plotted range follows the spiral's outer radius. The arc keeps a
fixed three quarters of a circle whatever $K$, because a quarter turn per class would pass a full turn and put the
last class next to the first.

**Controls.** Every figure offers the same controls: the curve, the number of classes $K$ from 3 to 10, the noise
$\sigma$, the random seed, and the training points per class $m$ from 10 to 100. The test set does not depend on $m$,
so metrics stay comparable across training sizes. Figures with a filled map also set its resolution and its fill: the
model's decision (the class it predicts), or its expected class
$\expect[y \mid \xvec] = \sum_k k\, P(y = k \mid \xvec)$. Classes are coloured along one diverging scale, dark blue for
class 1 to dark red for class $K$, in every figure.

## Comparing the models on shared data

The figure fits a chosen model to the training points and draws its expected class over the plane, with contours of
$\expect[y \mid \xvec]$ at the half-way values $1.5, 2.5, \dots$ between classes.

<OrdinalModelLab />

With the default settings ($K = 5$, $\sigma = 0.2$, 20 points per class, seed 1), the held-out metrics show four
patterns.

- **Linear.** Every model reaches a mean absolute error of 0.15, because one direction orders the classes. With
  $K = 10$ and 10 points per class the order starts to pay: the cumulative logit fits $2 + 9 = 11$ parameters and
  reaches 0.30, while multiclass logistic regression fits 27 free parameters and reaches 0.34.
- **Sine.** The classes still advance along $x_1$, so the single-score models stay close (0.13). The models with a
  boundary per split follow the waves slightly better (0.12).
- **Arc.** No single direction orders the classes. The single-score models reach about 0.30, and the models with a
  separate boundary per split (Frank & Hall, StORM, multiclass) reach 0.07.
- **Spiral.** The classes wind around each other. The single-score models, SVOR and regression-then-round fail (0.9
  and above), and Frank & Hall reaches 0.64. StORM and multiclass logistic regression both reach 0.07 with linear
  potentials. The nonlinear models follow the curve too: the GP reaches 0.07, Li & Lin's reduction with a small network
  0.07, and the neural heads 0.06 to 0.08.

The chooser offers every method in the table below except partial proportional odds, whose separate-slope fit is the
Frank & Hall fit.

## Evaluation

Ordinal predictions are scored with metrics that grow with the size of the error: <Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean absolute error</Gloss> on the class
index, its macro-average over classes, <Gloss name="qwk">quadratic weighted kappa</Gloss> and rank correlations such as Kendall's $\tau_b$. See
<NoteLink to="ordinal-classification-metrics" />. Accuracy alone treats an off-by-one error and an off-by-four error
the same, so it is a poor sole criterion for an ordinal task.
