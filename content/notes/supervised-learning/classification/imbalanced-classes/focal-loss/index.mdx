<Definition>
For a binary label $y \in \set{0, 1}$ and a predicted probability $p = \sigma(z)$ of class 1, with logit $z$, let
$p_t = p$ if $y = 1$ and $p_t = 1 - p$ if $y = 0$: the probability the model gives the true class. Cross-entropy is
$-\log p_t$. The **focal loss** is

$$
\text{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^\gamma\,\log p_t,
$$

with focusing parameter $\gamma \ge 0$ and class weight $\alpha_t = \alpha$ for positives and $1 - \alpha$ for
negatives. With $\gamma = 0$ it is weighted cross-entropy <Cite id="lin2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Lin et al. introduced focal loss for one-stage object detectors, which classify on the order of $10^5$ candidate boxes
per image, almost all of them background <Cite id="lin2017" />. Each easy background box is classified confidently and
contributes a small loss. There are so many of them that together they dominate the total loss and its gradient. The
few informative examples, the objects and the hard background boxes, then have little influence on training. Class
weights alone do not fix this: they rescale all negatives equally, easy and hard alike.

## Mechanism

### The modulating factor

The factor $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$ is close to 1 for a misclassified example ($p_t$ small) and close to 0 for a
well-classified one ($p_t$ near 1). With $\gamma = 2$, an example with $p_t = 0.9$ has its loss multiplied by
$0.1^2 = 0.01$, and one with $p_t = 0.968$ by about $0.001$ <Cite id="lin2017" />. The weight depends on the example's
difficulty rather than its class. Class imbalance is addressed indirectly: in a heavily imbalanced problem most easy
examples belong to the majority class.

### The gradient

Training follows the gradient of the loss with respect to the logit $z$. For $y = 1$, $p_t = \sigma(z)$ and
$dp_t/dz = p_t(1 - p_t)$. For $y = 0$, $p_t = 1 - \sigma(z)$ and $dp_t/dz = -p_t(1 - p_t)$. In both cases the
magnitude of the derivative is $p_t(1 - p_t)$ times the magnitude of the derivative with respect to $p_t$.

For cross-entropy, $d(-\log p_t)/dp_t = -1/p_t$, so the gradient magnitude is $1 - p_t$.

For focal loss with $\alpha_t = 1$, let $f(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^\gamma\log p_t$. By the product rule,

$$
\frac{df}{dp_t} = \gamma(1 - p_t)^{\gamma - 1}\log p_t - \frac{(1 - p_t)^\gamma}{p_t}.
$$

Multiplying by $p_t(1 - p_t)$ and taking the magnitude, using $\log p_t < 0$,

$$
\abs{\frac{\partial\,\text{FL}}{\partial z}} = (1 - p_t)^\gamma\brack{(1 - p_t) - \gamma\,p_t\log p_t}.
$$

Dividing by the cross-entropy gradient $1 - p_t$ gives the factor by which focal loss scales an example's gradient:

$$
(1 - p_t)^\gamma\brack{1 + \gamma\,\frac{p_t\,(-\log p_t)}{1 - p_t}}.
$$

As $p_t \to 1$, $-\log p_t \approx 1 - p_t$, so the bracket tends to $1 + \gamma$ and the factor behaves like
$(1 + \gamma)(1 - p_t)^\gamma$. Easy examples lose their gradient polynomially fast in $1 - p_t$. At $p_t = 0.9$ and
$\gamma = 2$ the factor is $0.01 \times (1 + 2 \times 0.9 \times 0.105/0.1) = 0.029$, a 35-fold reduction. For a hard
example, $p_t \to 0$, the factor tends to 1 and the gradient is unchanged.

### The class weight $\alpha$

$\alpha$ balances the classes as class weights do. Lin et al. found $\gamma = 2$ and $\alpha = 0.25$ worked best, which
gives positives, the rare class, the smaller weight. The modulating factor already removes most of the easy
negatives' loss, so the weight that best balances the remaining loss favours negatives <Cite id="lin2017" />.

### Prior initialisation

At the start of training a network with zero-mean output predicts $p = 0.5$ for every box. With about $10^5$ boxes of
background, the loss from background is then enormous and training can diverge. Lin et al. initialise the bias of the
final layer to $b = -\log\paren{(1 - \pi)/\pi}$, so that $\sigma(b) = \pi$ with $\pi = 0.01$, which is
$b = \operatorname{logit}(0.01) = -4.595$ <Cite id="lin2017" />. The initial prediction then matches the prior of the
rare class. This initialisation helps any model trained on imbalanced data, with or without focal loss.

### Focal loss is not a proper scoring rule

Cross-entropy is minimised in expectation by predicting the true posterior $\eta = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$. Focal loss is
not. At a point with posterior $\eta$, a prediction $q$ has expected loss $\eta f(q) + (1 - \eta) f(1 - q)$. With
$\gamma = 2$, the minimiser is $q^* = 0.538$ for $\eta = 0.6$, $q^* = 0.627$ for $\eta = 0.8$ and $q^* = 0.750$ for
$\eta = 0.95$. The minimiser is pulled towards 0.5: focal loss rewards underconfidence. The expected loss is symmetric
under swapping $q$ with $1 - q$ and $\eta$ with $1 - \eta$, so $q^* > 0.5$ exactly when $\eta > 0.5$ and decisions at
0.5 are preserved. Charoenphakdee et al. prove that focal loss is classification-calibrated but not strictly proper,
and give a transformation that recovers $\eta$ from $q^*$ <Cite id="charoenphakdee2021" />. For deep networks, whose
cross-entropy training makes them overconfident, the underconfidence bias can partly cancel the overconfidence, and
Mukhoti et al. found focal-loss networks better calibrated than cross-entropy ones <Cite id="mukhoti2020" />.

## Worked example

A detector sees $10^5$ easy background boxes with $p_t = 0.99$ and 100 object boxes with $p_t = 0.5$.

| | Cross-entropy | Focal loss, $\gamma = 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loss per easy negative | $-\log 0.99 = 0.01005$ | $0.01^2 \times 0.01005 = 1.005 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| Total over easy negatives | 1005 | 0.1005 |
| Loss per object | $-\log 0.5 = 0.693$ | $0.5^2 \times 0.693 = 0.173$ |
| Total over objects | 69.3 | 17.3 |
| Easy negatives : objects | 14.5 : 1 | 1 : 172 |

Under cross-entropy the easy negatives contribute 14.5 times the loss of the objects. Under focal loss the objects
contribute 172 times the loss of the easy negatives. The factor $\alpha$ is left at 1 here to isolate the effect of
$\gamma$.

## Cost

Focal loss costs one extra power and multiplication per example over cross-entropy. It adds two hyperparameters,
$\gamma$ and $\alpha$.

## Trade-offs

- **Probabilities are biased towards 0.5.** Use the outputs as scores, or recalibrate them on held-out data (see
  <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under imbalance</NoteLink>).
- **Hard examples include mislabelled ones.** Focal loss concentrates training on examples the model gets wrong,
  which includes label noise.
- **Compared with sampling hard examples.** Online hard example mining discards easy examples outright; focal loss
  down-weights them smoothly. Lin et al. report that focal loss outperformed it for their detector
  <Cite id="lin2017" />.
- **Compared with class weights.** <NoteLink to="class-weights">Class weights</NoteLink> reweight by class and preserve
  the proper scoring rule up to a known prior shift. Focal loss reweights by difficulty and does not. For a moderately
  imbalanced tabular problem, a weighted or unweighted cross-entropy with a moved threshold is the simpler choice.
