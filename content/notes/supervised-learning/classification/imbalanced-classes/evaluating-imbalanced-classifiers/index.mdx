<Definition>
Evaluating a classifier on <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">imbalanced data</NoteLink> means choosing metrics whose dependence on the prevalence
$\pi = P(y = 1)$ is known and reporting each against its value for an uninformative classifier. A metric is
**prevalence-invariant** if it depends only on the <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rate</Gloss> $\text{TPR} = P(\hat y = 1 \mid y = 1)$ and the
<Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> $\text{FPR} = P(\hat y = 1 \mid y = 0)$, and **prevalence-dependent** otherwise. The definitions of
the individual metrics are in <NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink> and the notes linked from
it.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A classifier's behaviour on positives and on negatives is a property of the classifier. How many of its alarms are
real depends also on how many positives there are to find. A metric that mixes the two answers a question about one
particular prevalence. That is right when the test set's prevalence is the deployment prevalence, and misleading when
it is not.

## Details

### Why ROC is invariant to the class ratio

TPR and FPR are each a probability conditional on the true class. Changing $\pi$ changes how many positives and
negatives there are, but under <NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction">label shift</NoteLink> it does not change $p(\xvec \mid y)$, and so it does not change the
distribution of scores within either class. Every point of the ROC curve, and every summary of it such as AUROC,
depends on the classifier and the class-conditional distributions only. Balanced accuracy, $(\text{TPR} + 1 -
\text{FPR})/2$, and informedness, $\text{TPR} - \text{FPR}$, are invariant for the same reason. See
<NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">ROC curve and AUC</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">accuracy and balanced accuracy</NoteLink>.

### Why precision is not

Precision is a probability conditional on the prediction, $P(y = 1 \mid \hat y = 1)$. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>,

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{P(\hat y = 1 \mid y = 1)\,P(y = 1)}{P(\hat y = 1)}
= \frac{\pi\,\text{TPR}}{\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1 - \pi)\,\text{FPR}}.
$$

In odds form, $\frac{\text{precision}}{1 - \text{precision}} = \frac{\text{TPR}}{\text{FPR}}\cdot\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}$.
Precision odds are the prior odds of a positive multiplied by the classifier's likelihood ratio $\text{TPR}/\text{FPR}$.
For precision to reach 0.5, the classifier needs $\text{FPR} \le \text{TPR}\,\pi/(1 - \pi)$. At $\pi = 0.001$ and
$\text{TPR} = 0.9$, that is an FPR below 0.0009. Any metric that contains precision inherits the dependence on $\pi$:
$F_1$, average precision and the precision-recall curve. The
<NoteLink to="precision-recall-curve-and-average-precision">precision-recall curve</NoteLink> note shows the PR curve
sinking with $\pi$ while the ROC curve stays put.

Saito and Rehmsmeier argue that the PR plot is the more informative of the two on imbalanced data, because it shows
the quantity that falls, the fraction of alarms that are real <Cite id="saito2015" />. The two curves contain the same
information at a fixed prevalence: each ROC point maps to one PR point, and one curve dominates another in ROC space
exactly when it dominates in PR space <Cite id="davis2006" />.

### MCC and accuracy move too

The <NoteLink to="matthews-correlation-coefficient">Matthews correlation coefficient</NoteLink> uses all four cells of
the confusion matrix and is 0 for chance at every prevalence, but its value for a fixed classifier still falls as
$\pi$ falls, because it contains precision and the negative predictive value. Accuracy,
$\pi\,\text{TPR} + (1 - \pi)(1 - \text{FPR})$, tends to the true-negative rate as $\pi \to 0$, so it rises for any
classifier with a low FPR as positives become rarer.

### Baselines

A metric value means little without the value an uninformative classifier gets at the same prevalence.

| Metric | Uninformative baseline | Value at $\pi = 0.01$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | $\max(\pi, 1 - \pi)$, by always predicting the majority | 0.99 |
| Balanced accuracy | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| AUROC | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| Precision, average precision | $\pi$, for a random ranking | 0.01 |
| $F_1$ | $2\pi/(1 + \pi)$, by predicting every case positive | 0.0198 |
| MCC | 0 | 0 |
| Brier score | $\pi(1 - \pi)$, by predicting $\pi$ for every case | 0.0099 |

The $F_1$ baseline follows from precision $\pi$ and recall 1: $F_1 = 2\pi/(\pi + 1)$. The Brier baseline follows from
predicting the constant $q = \pi$: the squared error is $(1 - \pi)^2$ with probability $\pi$ and $\pi^2$ with
probability $1 - \pi$, and $\pi(1 - \pi)^2 + (1 - \pi)\pi^2 = \pi(1 - \pi)$. A Brier score of 0.009 at $\pi = 0.01$
is barely better than knowing only the prevalence. The <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">log loss and Brier
score</NoteLink> note covers skill scores relative to such a baseline. <NoteLink to="precision-recall-gain-curves">Precision-recall-gain curves</NoteLink> rescale
precision and recall relative to the always-positive classifier so that the baseline is fixed <Cite id="flach2015" />.

### Comparing at the deployment prevalence

A test set is often enriched with positives, or drawn at a different time, so its prevalence $\pi_{\text{test}}$
differs from the deployment prevalence $\pi_{\text{dep}}$. The prevalence-invariant rates TPR and FPR estimated on the
test set carry over. Precision does not, but it can be recomputed at $\pi_{\text{dep}}$ from them with the formula
above. Two classifiers evaluated on test sets with different prevalences should be compared on TPR and FPR, or on
precision recomputed at one common prevalence, never on raw precision, $F_1$ or average precision.

### Protocol

- **Stratify** train, validation and test splits by class, so that every fold contains positives.
- **Count positives, not examples.** The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of a recall estimate from $n_1$ positives is
  $\sqrt{r(1 - r)/n_1}$. With 50 positives and recall 0.8, it is 0.057. Report
  <NoteLink to="metric-confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>.
- **Report a ranking metric and an operating point.** AUROC or average precision describe the scores; precision and
  recall at the chosen threshold describe the decisions. Choose the threshold on validation data, never on the test
  set.
- **Evaluate at the original prevalence.** Resampling or weighting belongs to training only.
- **Prefer costs when they are known.** Expected cost at the chosen threshold is the metric the application cares
  about <Cite id="provost2000" />.

## Worked example

A classifier has $\text{TPR} = 0.9$ and $\text{FPR} = 0.05$. Its metrics at three prevalences are:

| $\pi$ | Precision | $F_1$ | MCC | Accuracy | Balanced accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 0.947 | 0.923 | 0.851 | 0.925 | 0.925 |
| 0.1 | 0.667 | 0.766 | 0.746 | 0.945 | 0.925 |
| 0.01 | 0.154 | 0.263 | 0.360 | 0.9495 | 0.925 |

At $\pi = 0.01$, precision is $0.01 \times 0.9/(0.01 \times 0.9 + 0.99 \times 0.05) = 0.009/0.0585 = 0.154$: 85%
of alarms are false. Accuracy has risen to 0.9495 while every precision-based metric has collapsed. Balanced
accuracy is the same in every row. A model validated on a balanced test set with precision 0.947 would deliver
precision 0.154 in a deployment with 1% positives.

## Assumptions

- Prevalence invariance of TPR and FPR holds under label shift, when $p(\xvec \mid y)$ is the same in the test data and
  the deployment. If the positives themselves change, no metric transfers.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading AUROC alone.** A high AUROC is compatible with useless precision at low prevalence, as the worked example
  shows.
- **Comparing average precision across data sets** with different prevalences. Compare each to its own baseline $\pi$.
- **Macro-averaging over classes** gives the rare class equal weight; micro-averaging gives it almost none. State which
  (see <NoteLink to="averaging-multiclass-metrics">averaging multiclass metrics</NoteLink>).
