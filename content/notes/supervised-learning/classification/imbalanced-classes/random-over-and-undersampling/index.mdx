<Definition>
**Random undersampling** builds a training set by keeping every minority (positive) example and each majority
(negative) example independently with probability $\beta \in (0, 1]$. **Random oversampling** keeps every example and
adds copies of minority examples drawn at random with replacement. Both change the proportion of positives in the
training set, and so the prior that a model fitted to it learns. Neither changes the distribution of $\xvec$ within
each class <Cite id="he2009" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Some learners handle a rare class badly. A tree with a minimum leaf size cannot isolate a small minority region, and a
model whose loss is dominated by the majority spends its capacity there. Resampling the training set to a less
extreme ratio is the oldest remedy. It is also a way to cut training cost when the majority class is very large.

## Mechanism

### The effective prior

Let $\pi$ be the prevalence of positives in the original data. After undersampling, a fraction $\pi$ of examples are
positive and all are kept, while a fraction $1 - \pi$ are negative and a fraction $\beta$ of those are kept. The
training prevalence is

$$
\pi' = \frac{\pi}{\pi + \beta(1 - \pi)}.
$$

The training set is balanced, $\pi' = 0.5$, when $\beta = \pi/(1 - \pi)$. Oversampling that replicates each positive
$m$ times in <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> gives $\pi' = m\pi/\paren{m\pi + (1 - \pi)}$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $m$ shows
this equals undersampling with $\beta = 1/m$. In the prior, and in the posterior below, the two are the same
operation.

### The posterior after undersampling

Let $s \in \set{0, 1}$ indicate that an example is kept. Undersampling keeps positives with certainty and negatives
with probability $\beta$, and the decision does not look at $\xvec$:

$$
P(s = 1 \mid y = 1, \xvec) = 1, \qquad P(s = 1 \mid y = 0, \xvec) = \beta.
$$

Write $p = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ for the posterior in the original data. A model fitted to the undersampled data
estimates $p' = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec, s = 1)$. By Bayes' theorem, conditioning on $s = 1$,

$$
p' = \frac{P(s = 1 \mid y = 1, \xvec)\,p}{P(s = 1 \mid y = 1, \xvec)\,p + P(s = 1 \mid y = 0, \xvec)\,(1 - p)}
= \frac{p}{p + \beta(1 - p)}
$$

<Cite id="dalpozzolo2015" />. Since $\beta < 1$, $p' > p$ for every $p$ strictly between 0 and 1: the model
overestimates the probability of the minority class everywhere. Dividing $p'$ by $1 - p' = \beta(1 - p)/(p + \beta(1 -
p))$ gives the same result in odds,

$$
\frac{p'}{1 - p'} = \frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\frac{p}{1 - p},
\qquad
\operatorname{logit} p' = \operatorname{logit} p - \log\beta.
$$

Undersampling adds the constant $-\log\beta$ to the log-odds of every example. For a well-specified
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> this moves the intercept by $-\log\beta$ and leaves
the weights alone.

### Undoing the distortion

Solving $p' = p/(p + \beta(1 - p))$ for $p$ gives the correction

$$
p = \frac{\beta p'}{\beta p' - p' + 1}.
$$

In log-odds it subtracts back the shift: $\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} p' + \log\beta$. The
correction is exact when the model estimates $p'$ well. It is the special case of Elkan's base-rate adjustment in
which the base rate changes from $\pi'$ back to $\pi$; see <NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive
learning</NoteLink> <Cite id="elkan2001" />.

### Ranking and thresholds

The map from $p$ to $p'$ is strictly increasing. It therefore preserves the order of examples, and the ROC curve of
the true posterior is the same before and after the warp. Dal Pozzolo et al. conclude that the bias introduced by
undersampling does not affect the ranking but does affect the decisions and the calibration
<Cite id="dalpozzolo2015" />.

A threshold on $p'$ corresponds to a threshold on $p$. The rule $p' \ge 0.5$ is the rule $p \ge \beta/(1 + \beta)$.
With the balancing choice $\beta = \pi/(1 - \pi)$, $\beta/(1 + \beta) = \pi$: training on balanced data and
thresholding at 0.5 is the rule "predict positive when the posterior exceeds the prevalence", which maximises
balanced accuracy (see <NoteLink to="threshold-moving">threshold moving</NoteLink>). Conversely, a target threshold
$t$ on $p$ becomes $t' = t/\paren{t + \beta(1 - t)}$ on $p'$.

## Worked example

A data set has $n = 100\,000$ transactions, of which 1% are fraud: 1 000 positives and 99 000 negatives. Undersampling
to balance keeps each negative with probability $\beta = 0.01/0.99 = 1/99$, leaving about 1 000 negatives.

A model trained on the balanced set scores a transaction at $p' = 0.8$. The corrected probability is

$$
p = \frac{0.8/99}{0.8/99 - 0.8 + 1} = \frac{0.00808}{0.20808} = 0.0388.
$$

The transaction has a 3.9% chance of being fraud, not 80%. A score of $p' = 0.5$ corresponds to $p = 0.01$, the
prevalence, as the threshold argument predicts. If the costs call for flagging at $p \ge t = 0.05$, the threshold on
the balanced model's score is $t' = 0.05/(0.05 + 0.95/99) = 0.839$.

## Cost

Undersampling shrinks the training set to $n(\pi + \beta(1 - \pi))$ examples, about $2n\pi$ when balancing. Training
is faster by the same factor for learners linear in $n$. Oversampling grows the training set and the training time.

## Trade-offs

- **Undersampling discards information.** The negatives that are dropped carry information about
  $p(\xvec \mid y = 0)$, including near the decision boundary. The fitted model has higher variance. Averaging models
  trained on several undersampled sets recovers much of it: see
  <NoteLink to="ensemble-methods-for-imbalance">ensemble methods for imbalance</NoteLink>.
- **Oversampling duplicates.** Exact copies let flexible learners memorise individual minority points: a deep tree
  grows a leaf around each duplicated point. Duplicating adds no information, and in a likelihood it is the same as
  giving each positive a weight $m$, which <NoteLink to="class-weights">class weights</NoteLink> do without the copies.
  Synthetic oversampling (<NoteLink to="smote-and-variants">SMOTE</NoteLink>) avoids exact copies.
- **Resample after splitting.** Resampling before a train/validation split puts copies of one minority example on both
  sides of the split, and validation scores become optimistic. Evaluation must use data at the original prevalence.
- **Probabilities need correcting.** Without the correction above, predicted risks are inflated. In clinical risk
  models, resampling produced strongly overestimated risks without improving discrimination
  <Cite id="vandengoorbergh2022" />.
- **Informed undersampling** removes selected negatives rather than random ones, e.g. those that form Tomek links with
  positives or those far from any positive <Cite id="he2009" />. Selection then depends on $\xvec$, so
  $P(s = 1 \mid y = 0, \xvec)$ is no longer the constant $\beta$ and the correction above no longer holds exactly.
