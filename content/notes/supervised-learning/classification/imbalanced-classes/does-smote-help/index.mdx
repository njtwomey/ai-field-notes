<Definition>
The question is whether training on a rebalanced set, with SMOTE or with random over- or undersampling, gives a better
classifier than training on the original data and then choosing the decision threshold. Evaluations since 2022 that
include strong learners, <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">proper scoring rules</NoteLink> and threshold tuning mostly answer no
<Cite id="elor2022" /> <Cite id="vandengoorbergh2022" /> <Cite id="carriero2025" />.
</Definition>

## Why the early evidence looked better

<NoteLink to="smote-and-variants">SMOTE</NoteLink> was introduced with evaluations of <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision trees</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink>
compared at a fixed threshold, using <Gloss name="roc" /> and accuracy-type measures <Cite id="chawla2002" />. A model trained on data with
prevalence $\pi = 0.01$ and cut at $0.5$ predicts the minority class rarely, so any method that raises the training
prevalence raises recall at that fixed cut-off. The comparison mixes two changes: a different model and a different
effective threshold. Once the threshold is chosen for each model separately, most of the gap disappears.

## What rebalancing does to a probabilistic model

Random oversampling of the minority class by a factor $w$ is equivalent to weighting its examples by $w$. For a
well-specified probabilistic model this shifts the fitted log-odds by $\log w$ and changes nothing else (see
<NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling">random over- and undersampling</NoteLink>). A constant shift of the log-odds
preserves the ranking of every pair of examples, so the ROC curve and its area are unchanged. The same classifications
are therefore available from the original model by <NoteLink to="threshold-moving">moving the threshold</NoteLink>. The
shift itself is a <NoteLink to="calibration-error">calibration error</NoteLink>: the rebalanced model overestimates $P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ at the deployment
prevalence.

SMOTE is not an exact reweighting, because its synthetic points lie on segments between minority neighbours rather than
being drawn from $p(\xvec \mid y = 1)$. It keeps the minority mean and shrinks its covariance
<Cite id="blagus2013" />, which also moves the decision boundary. The same two consequences follow approximately: little
change in ranking, and a systematic upward bias in the predicted minority-class probability.

## Findings

- **Strong learners gain nothing.** Across many tabular data sets, balancing improved weak classifiers but did not
  improve strong ones, such as <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient-boosted trees</NoteLink>, once metrics, consistent algorithms and hyperparameter selection
  were handled carefully <Cite id="elor2022" />.
- **Discrimination unchanged, calibration broken.** For standard and ridge <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, random undersampling,
  random oversampling and SMOTE all produced strong overestimation of the minority-class probability without a better
  <Gloss name="auroc">area under the ROC curve</Gloss>. The gains in sensitivity and specificity were matched by shifting the threshold of the
  uncorrected model <Cite id="vandengoorbergh2022" />.
- **The same holds for machine-learning models.** In simulations across sample sizes, numbers of predictors and event
  fractions, models trained without correction had equal or better calibration than corrected ones in every scenario.
  The overestimation of risk was not always removed by recalibration <Cite id="carriero2025" />.
- **High dimensions.** With many more features than examples, SMOTE had little effect for most classifiers and biased
  $k$-nearest-neighbour classifiers towards the minority class <Cite id="blagus2013" />.

## Where rebalancing still helps

- **Weak or threshold-bound learners.** Models that output only a label, or whose threshold cannot be tuned, can
  benefit from a changed training prevalence <Cite id="elor2022" />.
- **Minibatch undersampling for speed.** Very rare classes in stochastic-gradient training can be undersampled in minibatches for
  speed, provided the probabilities are corrected afterwards.
- **Label-only goals.** When only the classification at one operating point matters, rebalancing and threshold moving
  reach similar results; threshold moving does so without distorting probabilities.

## Recommended default

Train on the original data with a proper scoring rule, evaluate with prevalence-aware measures (see
<NoteLink to="evaluating-imbalanced-classifiers">evaluating imbalanced classifiers</NoteLink>), and choose the threshold
from the costs of the two errors. Use rebalancing only when it is shown to help on held-out data, and recalibrate at the
deployment prevalence afterwards (see <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under imbalance</NoteLink>).
