<Definition>
A classifier with output $q(\xvec) \in [0, 1]$ is **calibrated** if, among cases given probability $q$, a fraction $q$
are positive: $P(y = 1 \mid q(\xvec) = q) = q$ for every $q$ (see <NoteLink to="calibration-error">calibration
error</NoteLink>). Under <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">class imbalance</NoteLink>, calibration is disturbed in two ways. The remedies for imbalance distort it
by a known factor. And the scarcity of positives makes calibration hard to estimate and to repair.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A model trained on balanced data learns posteriors for a training prior in which positives are as common as negatives. The ranking of cases is unaffected, but every probability for the rare class is too high. The size of the error is known exactly from how the training data were rebalanced, so it can be removed exactly.

## Details

### The distortion

Undersampling negatives by $\beta$, oversampling positives by $1/\beta$, or weighting positives by $w_1$ and negatives
by $w_0$ all change the training prior. Each multiplies the learnt posterior odds by a constant, $\kappa = 1/\beta$ or
$\kappa = w_1/w_0$ (see <NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling">random over- and undersampling</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="class-weights">class weights</NoteLink>). If $p$ is the true posterior and $q$ the model's output,

$$
\frac{q}{1 - q} = \kappa\,\frac{p}{1 - p}.
$$

The reliability curve plots the observed frequency of positives against the predicted probability. For this model,
among cases with output $q$ the true posterior is $p$ with odds $\frac{q}{\kappa(1 - q)}$, so

$$
P(y = 1 \mid q) = \frac{q}{q + \kappa(1 - q)}.
$$

For $\kappa > 1$ this lies below the diagonal at every $q$ strictly between 0 and 1: the model overestimates the
probability of the rare class everywhere. Van den Goorbergh et al. observed exactly this in clinical risk models
trained after undersampling, oversampling or <Gloss name="smote" /> <Cite id="vandengoorbergh2022" />.

### Undoing it analytically

When $\kappa$ is known and the model is well specified, the correction is exact:

$$
\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} q - \log\kappa,
\qquad
p = \frac{q}{q + \kappa(1 - q)}.
$$

For resampling by $\beta$ this is the Dal Pozzolo et al. correction <Cite id="dalpozzolo2015" />. For <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic
regression</NoteLink> it is a change of intercept. The correction needs no held-out data. It does not fix any other
miscalibration, such as overconfidence from overfitting or a misspecified model.

### Undoing it empirically

**Platt scaling** fits a one-feature logistic regression, $\hat p = \sigma(a\,z + b)$, on held-out data, where $z$ is
the model's score and $\sigma$ the logistic function <Cite id="platt1999" />. If $z = \operatorname{logit} q$, the
analytic correction is the special case $a = 1$, $b = -\log\kappa$. Platt scaling therefore removes the distortion
from resampling and, through $a \ne 1$, uniform over- or underconfidence as well. It must be fitted on data at the
deployment prevalence, not on resampled data. To avoid overfitting with few examples, Platt replaces the targets 1
and 0 by $(n_1 + 1)/(n_1 + 2)$ and $1/(n_0 + 2)$, where $n_1$ and $n_0$ count the positives and negatives in the
calibration set <Cite id="platt1999" />.

**Isotonic regression** fits the best non-decreasing step function from score to probability on held-out data
<Cite id="zadrozny2002" />. It can correct any monotone distortion, not only a sigmoid one. It needs more data: the
steps at high scores are set by the few positives there. Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana found Platt scaling better than
isotonic regression when the calibration set is small <Cite id="niculescumizil2005" />. With a rare class, the
calibration set is effectively small even when it has many examples.

**Temperature scaling** divides the logits of a multiclass network by one scalar fitted on held-out data
<Cite id="guo2017" />. It fixes overconfidence but cannot fix a shift of the prior, which needs a per-class bias as in
vector scaling or the prior adjustment of <NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction">prior-shift correction</NoteLink>.

### Measuring calibration with few positives

- **Most mass sits near zero.** With $\pi = 0.01$, most predictions are below 0.05. Equal-width reliability bins
  above 0.5 may hold a handful of cases, and the calibration error there is noise. Use equal-mass bins, or a smooth
  reliability curve with a confidence band.
- **Expected calibration error is dominated by the low-probability bins.** A model that predicts the constant $\pi$
  for every case has an expected calibration error of zero and does not separate positives from negatives. Pair calibration metrics with a
  discrimination metric.
- **Minority-class probabilities are the least reliable.** Wallace and Dahabreh show that probability estimates for
  the minority class are unreliable even when overall calibration looks good <Cite id="wallace2012" />. Measuring
  calibration separately within each class, e.g. a <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">Brier score</NoteLink> over the positives alone, exposes this.

### Order of operations

1. Train, with or without resampling or weights.
2. Correct the prior analytically if the training prior was changed on purpose.
3. Recalibrate on held-out data at the deployment prevalence if the model is miscalibrated for other reasons.
4. Choose the threshold on the calibrated probabilities (see <NoteLink to="threshold-moving">threshold
   moving</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

A logistic regression is trained with balanced class weights on data with $\pi = 0.02$, so
$\kappa = w_1/w_0 = 0.98/0.02 = 49$. For a case with model output $q = 0.9$, the posterior odds are
$0.9/0.1 = 9$, and the corrected odds are $9/49 = 0.184$, giving $p = 9/58 = 0.155$. A case with $q = 0.5$ has
corrected odds $1/49$ and $p = 1/50 = 0.02$, the prevalence. Without the correction, the model would report a 90%
probability for a case that is positive 15.5% of the time.

Platt scaling fitted to the logit on a large held-out set at $\pi = 0.02$ would find $a \approx 1$ and
$b \approx -\log 49 = -3.89$, reproducing the analytic correction.

## Assumptions

- The analytic correction assumes the resampling or weighting depends only on the class, not on $\xvec$, and that
  the model estimates the posterior of the resampled data well.
- Empirical recalibration assumes the held-out data come from the deployment distribution.

## Pitfalls

- **Recalibrating on resampled data** calibrates the model to the resampled prior and reintroduces the distortion.
- **Treating ranking scores as probabilities.** Calibration does not change <Gloss name="auroc" />, and good AUROC does not imply
  calibration.
- **Correcting twice.** A model corrected analytically and then recalibrated with Platt scaling on held-out data
  at the deployment prevalence stays calibrated; a model corrected analytically twice for the same $\kappa$
  underestimates the rare class.
