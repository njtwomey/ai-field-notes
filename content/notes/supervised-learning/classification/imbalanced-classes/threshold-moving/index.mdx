<Definition>
**Threshold moving** replaces the default decision rule "predict positive when $p \ge 0.5$" with "predict positive when
$p \ge t$", where $p = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ is the classifier's estimated probability that example $\xvec$ is
positive and $t \in (0, 1)$ is chosen for the task. The model is trained on the data as given. Only the decision
changes. The threshold is derived from misclassification costs when they are known, or chosen to optimise a metric
on held-out data when they are not <Cite id="elkan2001" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier trained by maximum likelihood estimates the posterior $p$. The rule $p \ge 0.5$ minimises the expected
number of errors, so it treats a false positive and a false negative as equally bad. Under class imbalance that rule
rarely predicts the rare class. When positives make up a fraction $\pi = 0.01$ of cases, an example needs evidence
strong enough to overturn prior odds of 99 to 1 before $p$ reaches 0.5. Most positives never get there. The resulting
classifier has high accuracy and low recall. That outcome is correct for the 0-1 loss and wrong for most applications
that care about the rare class <Cite id="provost2000" />.

## Mechanism

### The cost-optimal threshold

Let $c_{\text{FP}} > 0$ be the cost of a false positive and $c_{\text{FN}} > 0$ the cost of a false negative, with
correct decisions costing nothing. For an example with posterior $p$, predicting positive costs
$(1 - p)\,c_{\text{FP}}$ in expectation, because the example is negative with probability $1 - p$. Predicting
negative costs $p\,c_{\text{FN}}$. Predicting positive is the better choice when

$$
(1 - p)\,c_{\text{FP}} \le p\,c_{\text{FN}}
\iff
p \ge t^* = \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FP}} + c_{\text{FN}}}.
$$

Equal costs give $t^* = 0.5$. A false negative that costs 20 false positives gives $t^* = 1/21 \approx 0.048$. The
threshold depends only on the ratio of the two costs. The general case, with costs on correct decisions and more than
two classes, is in <NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive learning</NoteLink>.

### Where the prevalence enters

The prevalence $\pi = P(y = 1)$ does not appear in $t^*$. It is already inside $p$. By
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> in odds form, with likelihood ratio
$\Lambda(\xvec) = p(\xvec \mid y = 1) / p(\xvec \mid y = 0)$,

$$
\frac{p}{1 - p} = \Lambda(\xvec)\,\frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}.
$$

The rule $p \ge t^*$ is therefore the rule

$$
\Lambda(\xvec) \ge \frac{t^*}{1 - t^*}\cdot\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi} = \frac{c_{\text{FP}}}{c_{\text{FN}}}\cdot\frac{1 - \pi}{\pi}.
$$

The evidence an example must carry grows with the imbalance $(1 - \pi)/\pi$ and shrinks with the cost ratio
$c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}}$. When the cost ratio equals the imbalance, the two cancel and the rule becomes
$\Lambda(\xvec) \ge 1$: predict the class under which $\xvec$ is more likely. Threshold moving and resampling the
training set both act on this one quantity, which is why they can reach the same decisions.

### Thresholds that optimise a metric

Many applications state a metric rather than costs. For a calibrated $p$, the best threshold for two common metrics
follows from the posterior alone.

**Balanced accuracy.** Balanced accuracy is the mean of the true-positive rate and the true-negative rate. Write $P$
and $N$ for the numbers of positives and negatives, and FN and FP for the error counts. Then

$$
\text{BA} = \tfrac12\paren{1 - \tfrac{\text{FN}}{P}} + \tfrac12\paren{1 - \tfrac{\text{FP}}{N}}
= 1 - \paren{\tfrac{1}{2P}\,\text{FN} + \tfrac{1}{2N}\,\text{FP}}.
$$

Maximising BA is minimising total cost with $c_{\text{FN}} = 1/(2P)$ and $c_{\text{FP}} = 1/(2N)$. These costs are
inversely proportional to the class sizes, so their ratio is $c_{\text{FN}}/c_{\text{FP}} = N/P = (1 - \pi)/\pi$.
The cost-optimal threshold is

$$
t^*_{\text{BA}} = \frac{1/N}{1/N + 1/P} = \frac{P}{P + N} = \pi.
$$

Balanced accuracy is maximised by predicting positive when the posterior exceeds the prevalence. By the odds form
above, this is the rule $\Lambda(\xvec) \ge 1$. The same holds for the Youden index, which is $2\,\text{BA} - 1$.

**$F_1$.** For a calibrated classifier the $F_1$-optimal threshold is half the largest achievable $F_1$
<Cite id="lipton2014" />:

$$
t^*_{F_1} = \tfrac12 F_1^*.
$$

The threshold therefore lies below 0.5 whenever $F_1^* < 1$, and a classifier with $F_1^* = 0.4$ should predict
positive from $p = 0.2$ upwards.

<Derivation title="Why the F1-optimal threshold is half the optimal F1">
Write $F_1 = 2\,\text{TP}/(2\,\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN})$. The number of positives
$P = \text{TP} + \text{FN}$ is fixed, so the denominator is $D = \text{TP} + \text{FP} + P$, and the numerator is
$M = 2\,\text{TP}$. Consider adding one more example, with posterior $p$, to the predicted positives. In expectation
TP rises by $p$ and FP by $1 - p$, so $M$ rises by $2p$ and $D$ by 1. The new score exceeds the old one when

$$
\frac{M + 2p}{D + 1} > \frac{M}{D} \iff 2pD > M \iff p > \frac{M}{2D} = \frac{F_1}{2}.
$$

Adding examples in decreasing order of $p$ increases $F_1$ while each added $p$ exceeds half the current $F_1$, and
decreases it afterwards. At the optimum, the last example added has $p = F_1^*/2$. The argument treats counts as
expectations, so it holds for large test sets <Cite id="lipton2014" />.
</Derivation>

**More than two classes.** For $K$ classes with priors $\pi_k$, the rule that minimises the balanced error (the mean
over classes of each class's error rate) predicts $\argmax_k P(y = k \mid \xvec)/\pi_k$. This is the multiclass form of
$t^*_{\text{BA}} = \pi$. In logit form it subtracts $\log \pi_k$ from each logit, which is called post-hoc logit
adjustment <Cite id="menon2021" />.

### When the scores are not probabilities

The formulas above need calibrated probabilities. A classifier whose scores rank well but are miscalibrated, or one
trained on resampled data, needs one of two remedies. The first is to recalibrate its scores (see
<NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under imbalance</NoteLink>) and then apply the formula. The
second is to choose the threshold empirically: score a held-out set drawn at the deployment prevalence, evaluate the
cost or metric at every distinct score, and keep the best. The empirical route needs no calibration. It does need a
held-out set with enough positives: with 30 positives, one positive changes recall by 0.033, and the selected
threshold moves by as much between resamples.

## Worked example

Scores are $s \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ for negatives and $s \sim \Gauss(2, 1)$ for positives, and the prevalence is
$\pi = 0.05$. The posterior is exactly logistic in $s$: $\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit}\pi + 2s - 2$.
A false negative costs 20 times a false positive. Costs below are per case, in units of $c_{\text{FP}}$, and equal
$(1 - \pi)\,\text{FPR} + 20\,\pi\,(1 - \text{TPR})$.

| Threshold on $p$ | Threshold on $s$ | Recall | Precision | $F_1$ | Balanced accuracy | Cost per case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 (default) | 2.472 | 0.318 | 0.714 | 0.440 | 0.656 | 0.688 |
| $t^* = 1/21 = 0.048$ | 0.974 | 0.847 | 0.213 | 0.340 | 0.841 | 0.309 |
| $\pi = 0.05$ | 1.000 | 0.841 | 0.218 | 0.347 | 0.841 | 0.309 |
| 0.259 | 1.947 | 0.521 | 0.516 | 0.518 | 0.748 | 0.503 |

The default threshold misses 68% of positives and more than doubles the cost. The cost-optimal threshold $t^* = 0.048$
cuts the cost from 0.688 to 0.309. Here the cost ratio 20 is close to the imbalance $0.95/0.05 = 19$, so $t^*$ nearly
equals $\pi$ and the cost-optimal and balanced-accuracy-optimal thresholds almost coincide. The last row maximises
$F_1$ at 0.518, and its threshold on $p$ is 0.259, half of 0.518, as derived above. Each metric picks a different
operating point from the same model.

## Cost

Threshold moving adds no training cost. Choosing a threshold empirically needs one sort of the held-out scores,
$O(n \log n)$ for $n$ held-out examples, and one pass to evaluate the metric at each distinct score.

## Trade-offs

- **It changes decisions, not rankings.** The ROC curve, AUROC and average precision are unchanged, because they
  already sweep every threshold. A model that ranks badly stays bad.
- **It needs calibrated probabilities or representative held-out data.** A formula threshold applied to
  miscalibrated scores lands at the wrong operating point.
- **It does not add information about the rare class.** When the minority class has few examples, the estimated
  posterior is noisy in the minority region, and no threshold removes that variance.
- **Compared with resampling,** threshold moving reaches the same decision rule without discarding or duplicating data
  and keeps probabilities interpretable. Elkan recommends it over rebalancing for that reason <Cite id="elkan2001" />.
  See <NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling">random over- and undersampling</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="class-weights">class weights</NoteLink> for the training-time alternatives.
