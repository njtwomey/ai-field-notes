<Definition>
A binary classification problem is **imbalanced** when the prior probability $\pi = P(y = 1)$ of one class, the
minority or positive class, is much smaller than that of the other. The **imbalance ratio** is $(1 - \pi)/\pi$, the
number of majority examples per minority example. Fraud detection ($\pi \approx 0.001$), screening for a rare disease
and dense object detection are typical cases <Cite id="he2009" />. Class imbalance is a property of the data. It
becomes a problem only through the decision rule, the evaluation metric, the sample size or the learner.
</Definition>

## What imbalance does not break

A probabilistic classifier fitted by maximum likelihood minimises the average
<NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink>. Its target is the true posterior
$\eta(\xvec) = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$, whatever the prior $\pi$. To see this, fix $\xvec$ and let the model predict
probability $q$ for the positive class. The expected log loss at $\xvec$ is

$$
\ell(q) = -\eta \log q - (1 - \eta)\log(1 - q).
$$

Its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is $\ell'(q) = -\eta/q + (1 - \eta)/(1 - q)$, which is zero at
$q = \eta$, and $\ell''(q) > 0$, so $q = \eta$ is the unique minimum. The population log loss is the average of $\ell$
over $\xvec$, so it is minimised by predicting $\eta(\xvec)$ at every $\xvec$. The argument never uses $\pi$. The prior
enters $\eta$ through <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, and the model learns it along with
everything else.

For <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> the consequence is concrete. If the model is
well specified, $\operatorname{logit}\eta(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$, then changing the prior while the
class-conditional densities $p(\xvec \mid y)$ stay fixed changes only the intercept. By Bayes' theorem in log-odds,

$$
\operatorname{logit}\eta(\xvec) = \log\frac{p(\xvec \mid y = 1)}{p(\xvec \mid y = 0)} + \operatorname{logit}\pi,
$$

and only the second term depends on $\pi$. The maximum-likelihood estimate converges to the true $(\wvec, b)$ at any
$\pi$. Imbalance alone does not bias the fitted posterior.

## What imbalance does break

**The default threshold.** Predicting the class with the larger posterior, $\eta(\xvec) \ge 0.5$, minimises the
number of errors. When $\pi$ is small, few examples have $\eta(\xvec) \ge 0.5$, so the rule predicts the majority
class almost everywhere. That is optimal for the 0-1 loss and rarely what the application wants. The fix is a
different threshold, derived from costs or from the target metric: see
<NoteLink to="threshold-moving">threshold moving</NoteLink>.

**Accuracy as a metric.** A classifier that always predicts the majority class has accuracy $1 - \pi$. At
$\pi = 0.01$ that is 0.99 for a classifier that has learnt nothing. Accuracy, and every metric dominated by the
majority class, must be read against this baseline or replaced: see
<NoteLink to="evaluating-imbalanced-classifiers">evaluating imbalanced classifiers</NoteLink>.

**Variance from few minority examples.** The information about the minority class grows with the number of minority
examples $n\pi$, not with the total $n$. The simplest case is a model with an intercept only, which estimates $\pi$
by the sample proportion $\hat\pi$. Its variance is $\pi(1 - \pi)/n$. The log-odds $\operatorname{logit}\hat\pi$ has
derivative $1/\paren{\pi(1 - \pi)}$ with respect to $\hat\pi$, so by the delta method

$$
\var\paren{\operatorname{logit}\hat\pi} \approx \frac{1}{\pi^2(1 - \pi)^2}\cdot\frac{\pi(1 - \pi)}{n} = \frac{1}{n\pi(1 - \pi)} \approx \frac{1}{n\pi}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> on the log-odds scale is about
$1/\sqrt{\text{number of positives}}$. The same scaling governs the coefficients of a model with features: King and
Zeng show that the variance of logistic-regression estimates is driven by the number of events, and that in small
samples the maximum-likelihood estimate systematically underestimates the probability of rare events
<Cite id="king2001" />. Adding majority examples barely helps.

Japkowicz and Stephen found that the damage done by imbalance grows with the complexity of the concept and shrinks with
the size of the training set: imbalance hurts most when the minority class is split into small, rarely sampled regions
<Cite id="japkowicz2002" />.

**Learners that are not probabilistic or not well specified.** The argument above assumes a model that can represent
$\eta$ and a loss that is a proper scoring rule. Many learners violate one or both. A
<NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink> with a minimum leaf size cannot isolate small minority regions. A
$k$-nearest-neighbour vote in an overlap region is outvoted by the majority. A regularised or misspecified model spends
its limited capacity on the majority class, where most of the loss is. A neural network trained by
<Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> receives <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> dominated by
the many easy majority examples, which is the motivation for <NoteLink to="focal-loss">focal loss</NoteLink>.

**Deployment at a different prevalence.** A model trained at one prevalence and deployed at another produces
miscalibrated posteriors. This is label shift, and it applies to balanced training sets as much as to imbalanced ones:
see <NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction">prior-shift correction</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Scores are $s \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ for negatives and $s \sim \Gauss(2, 1)$ for positives, and $\pi = 0.01$. The
posterior is exactly $\operatorname{logit}\eta(s) = \operatorname{logit}(0.01) + 2s - 2$, so a model fitted to
enough data recovers it with no bias.

- **Default threshold.** $\eta(s) \ge 0.5$ when $s \ge 3.298$. The rule finds 9.7% of positives, with precision 0.668
  and accuracy 0.9905. The trivial rule "always negative" has accuracy 0.99.
  <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">Balanced accuracy</NoteLink> is 0.548.
- **Balanced-accuracy-optimal threshold.** $\eta(s) \ge \pi$ when $s \ge 1$. The rule finds 84.1% of positives, with
  precision 0.051 and balanced accuracy 0.841.

The posterior is identical in both cases. Only the decision differs.

- **Sample size.** With $n = 10\,000$ examples there are about 100 positives, and the standard error of the estimated
  log-odds is $1/\sqrt{10\,000 \times 0.01 \times 0.99} = 0.101$. A balanced sample of size $n$ has the same variance
  when $0.25\,n = 10\,000 \times 0.01 \times 0.99 = 99$, that is with $n = 396$ examples. The 9 900 extra negatives buy
  almost nothing.

## Remedies

Each remedy acts on one of the problems above.

| Remedy | What it changes | Problem it addresses | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Threshold moving | The decision rule $p \ge t$ | The default threshold | <NoteLink to="threshold-moving" /> |
| Random over- and undersampling | The training prior | Learners dominated by the majority; training cost | <NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling" /> |
| Class weights | The loss's weight per class | Same as resampling, without sampling noise | <NoteLink to="class-weights" /> |
| SMOTE and variants | Synthetic minority examples | Sparse minority regions for local learners | <NoteLink to="smote-and-variants" /> |
| Ensembles over balanced subsets | Many models on undersampled data | Information lost by undersampling | <NoteLink to="ensemble-methods-for-imbalance" /> |
| Focal loss | Per-example weight by difficulty | Gradients dominated by easy examples | <NoteLink to="focal-loss" /> |
| Prior-shift correction | The posterior at deployment | A different deployment prevalence | <NoteLink to="prior-shift-correction" /> |
| Recalibration | The map from score to probability | Probabilities distorted by the other remedies | <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance" /> |
| Prevalence-aware evaluation | The metric | Accuracy and <Gloss name="roc" /> hiding poor precision | <NoteLink to="evaluating-imbalanced-classifiers" /> |

The remedies appeared in roughly this order. Resampling and <NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning">cost-sensitive
learning</NoteLink> came first; Provost's 2000 position paper argued that many "imbalance problems" were evaluation and
decision problems in disguise <Cite id="provost2000" />. SMOTE (2002) and its variants, balanced ensembles (2004 to 2010)
and focal loss (2017) followed. He and Garcia survey the field up to 2009 <Cite id="he2009" />.

Recent evaluations favour the decision-level remedies. For logistic regression in clinical risk prediction,
undersampling, oversampling and SMOTE made the predicted risks badly miscalibrated without improving the
<Gloss name="auroc" /> <Cite id="vandengoorbergh2022" />. For strong learners such as
<NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient-boosted trees</NoteLink>, balancing the training data gave no gain over tuning
the threshold of a model trained on the original data <Cite id="elor2022" />.

## A default recipe

1. Fit a probabilistic model on the data as given, with a proper scoring rule such as log loss.
2. Evaluate with metrics that expose the minority class, reported against their chance baselines.
3. Choose the threshold from costs or from the target metric.
4. Resample or reweight only if the learner cannot represent the minority class otherwise, and then correct its
   probabilities.
5. If the deployment prevalence differs from the training prevalence, adjust the posteriors to it.
