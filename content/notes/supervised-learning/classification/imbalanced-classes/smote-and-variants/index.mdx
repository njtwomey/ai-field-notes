<Definition>
The **synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE)** adds artificial minority examples to a training set. For
each minority example $\xvec_i$, it finds the $k$ nearest minority examples, picks one of them, $\xvec_j$, at random,
and creates

$$
\xvec_{\text{new}} = \xvec_i + u\,(\xvec_j - \xvec_i), \qquad u \sim \Unif(0, 1),
$$

a point drawn uniformly on the segment between them. Repeating this $r$ times per minority example multiplies the
minority class by $1 + r$ <Cite id="chawla2002" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Random oversampling duplicates minority examples. A flexible learner then fits a tiny region around each copy: a
decision tree grows a leaf for it, and a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier claims only the copy's exact location. SMOTE
spreads the new examples out, so the minority decision region grows to cover the space between minority examples
rather than shrinking onto them. Chawla et al. combined it with undersampling of the majority and reported better ROC
performance than either form of random resampling across several data sets <Cite id="chawla2002" />.

## Mechanism

### SMOTE

Chawla et al. use $k = 5$ and express the amount of oversampling as a percentage: 200% creates $r = 2$ synthetic
points per minority example, each from a neighbour chosen at random among the five <Cite id="chawla2002" />.
Distances are Euclidean, so features must be on comparable scales. For nominal features, **SMOTE-NC** interpolates the
continuous features and gives each nominal feature the value held by the majority of the $k$ neighbours
<Cite id="chawla2002" />.

### What the interpolation assumes

A synthetic point is a valid minority example only if the segment between two minority examples lies inside the
minority region. The assumption holds when the minority class occupies a connected, roughly convex region at the scale
of $k$ neighbours. It fails in three ways, which the figure below shows.

- **Separated clusters.** If the minority class has two clusters and $k$ is larger than the smaller cluster, some
  segments cross the space between them, which may belong to the majority.
- **Label noise.** A mislabelled minority point inside the majority region generates synthetic points around itself
  and along segments reaching back to the real minority region.
- **Overlap.** Where the classes overlap, synthetic points land among majority points and push the learnt boundary
  into majority territory.

### SMOTE shrinks the minority distribution

Interpolation pulls points towards each other. Take two independent minority examples $X$ and $X'$ with mean $\muvec$
and covariance $\Sigmamat$, and an independent $U \sim \Unif(0, 1)$. The synthetic point is
$X_{\text{new}} = (1 - U)X + UX'$. Its mean is $\expect\brack{1 - U}\muvec + \expect\brack{U}\muvec = \muvec$. Centre
the variables, $\tilde X = X - \muvec$ and $\tilde X' = X' - \muvec$, so that
$X_{\text{new}} - \muvec = (1 - U)\tilde X + U\tilde X'$. The cross term has zero expectation because $\tilde X$ and
$\tilde X'$ are independent with mean zero. Hence

$$
\cov(X_{\text{new}}) = \expect\brack{(1 - U)^2}\Sigmamat + \expect\brack{U^2}\Sigmamat
= \paren{\tfrac13 + \tfrac13}\Sigmamat = \tfrac23\Sigmamat.
$$

For random pairs, SMOTE keeps the minority mean and shrinks its covariance to two thirds <Cite id="blagus2013" />.
Nearest-neighbour pairs are closer together, so the shrinkage is milder. In a simulation with 200 standard-normal
points in two dimensions, the variance of the synthetic points is 0.95, 0.89 and 0.80 times the original for
$k = 1$, 5 and 20. The synthetic points are also correlated with the real ones they were made from, so they add less
information than their number suggests. In high-dimensional data, Blagus and Lusa found that SMOTE helped few
classifiers and biased $k$-nearest-neighbour classifiers towards the minority class <Cite id="blagus2013" />.

### Borderline-SMOTE

Borderline-SMOTE generates only from minority examples near the class boundary <Cite id="han2005" />. For each minority
example it counts $m'$, the number of majority examples among its $m$ nearest neighbours in the whole training set.

- $m' = m$: every neighbour is majority. The example is treated as **noise** and does not generate.
- $m/2 \le m' < m$: the example is in **danger**, close to the boundary. It generates, using SMOTE's rule.
- $m' < m/2$: the example is **safe**, inside the minority region, and does not generate.

The variant concentrates new examples where the learner is most likely to err and ignores isolated points. A second
form, Borderline-SMOTE2, also interpolates towards majority neighbours, with $u$ restricted to $(0, 0.5)$ so that the
new point stays nearer the minority example <Cite id="han2005" />.

### ADASYN

ADASYN allocates synthetic examples in proportion to how hard each minority example is <Cite id="he2008" />. Let $G$
be the total number of synthetic examples to generate. For each minority example $\xvec_i$, let $\Delta_i$ be the
number of majority examples among its $K$ nearest neighbours, and $r_i = \Delta_i/K$. The normalised ratios
$\hat r_i = r_i/\sum_j r_j$ sum to 1, and $\xvec_i$ generates $g_i = \hat r_i G$ synthetic examples with SMOTE's rule.
Unlike Borderline-SMOTE, ADASYN keeps the points with $r_i = 1$ and gives them the largest share, so it amplifies label
noise.

## Worked example

A minority example $\xvec_i = (1, 2)$ has neighbour $\xvec_j = (3, 1)$, and the draw is $u = 0.25$. The synthetic
example is

$$
\xvec_{\text{new}} = (1, 2) + 0.25\,\paren{(3, 1) - (1, 2)} = (1, 2) + (0.5, -0.25) = (1.5, 1.75).
$$

With $m = 7$ for Borderline-SMOTE, a minority example with 7 majority neighbours is noise, one with 4, 5 or 6 is in
danger, and one with 0 to 3 is safe. With $K = 7$ in ADASYN, examples with 7, 4 and 1 majority neighbours have $r_i$
of 1, $4/7$ and $1/7$. If they were the only minority examples, $\hat r_i = 7/12$, $4/12$ and $1/12$, and with $G = 12$
they would generate 7, 4 and 1 synthetic examples.

## Cost

Finding the $k$ nearest minority neighbours of $n_1$ minority examples costs $O(n_1^2 d)$ by brute force in $d$
dimensions, less with a spatial index. Borderline-SMOTE and ADASYN search the whole training set, $O(n_1 n d)$. The
learner then trains on the enlarged set.

## Trade-offs

- **Probabilities are distorted.** Adding synthetic positives raises the training prevalence exactly as oversampling
  does. The correction from <NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling">random over- and undersampling</NoteLink>
  applies only approximately, because synthetic points are not drawn from $p(\xvec \mid y = 1)$. Recalibrate on
  held-out data at the deployment prevalence (see <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under
  imbalance</NoteLink>). In clinical risk models, SMOTE produced strongly overestimated risks without improving
  discrimination <Cite id="vandengoorbergh2022" />.
- **Strong learners gain little.** Across many data sets, balancing with SMOTE helped weak classifiers but did not
  improve gradient-boosted trees over training on the original data and tuning the threshold <Cite id="elor2022" />.
- **Resample inside cross-validation.** Synthetic points built from a validation example leak it into training.
- **Distances must mean something.** SMOTE needs scaled continuous features. For categorical, text or image data,
  interpolation between examples rarely produces a valid example; class-aware
  <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink> is the analogue there.
- **Cleaning.** Several combinations follow SMOTE with a cleaning step that removes examples whose neighbours are
  mostly of the other class, to undo the damage in overlap regions <Cite id="he2009" />.
