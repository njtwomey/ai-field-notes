<Definition>
Training with **class weights** minimises the weighted empirical risk

$$
\hat R_w(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_{y_i}\,\ell\paren{f(\xvec_i), y_i},
$$

where $\ell$ is the loss of prediction $f(\xvec_i)$ on label $y_i$ and $w_k > 0$ is the weight of class $k$. The
common "balanced" choice is $w_k = n/(K n_k)$ for $K$ classes with $n_k$ examples each, which gives every class the
same total weight. Weights are the deterministic counterpart of resampling the training set
<Cite id="zadrozny2003" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A loss summed over examples is dominated by the majority class. Resampling fixes the balance but discards data or
duplicates it, and adds sampling noise. Weighting changes the balance while every example is used exactly once.

## Mechanism

### Weighting equals resampling in expectation

Let $\pi_k = P(y = k)$ be the class priors and $R_k(f) = \expect\brack{\ell(f(\xvec), k) \mid y = k}$ the expected
loss on class $k$. The expected weighted risk is, by the law of total <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>,

$$
R_w(f) = \expect\brack{w_y\,\ell(f(\xvec), y)} = \sum_k \pi_k w_k R_k(f) = Z\sum_k \pi'_k R_k(f),
\qquad \pi'_k = \frac{w_k\pi_k}{Z},\quad Z = \sum_j w_j \pi_j.
$$

The last sum is the ordinary, unweighted risk under a distribution with the same class-conditional densities
$p(\xvec \mid y)$ and priors $\pi'_k$. The factor $Z$ does not depend on $f$. So minimising the weighted risk and
minimising the unweighted risk on data resampled to priors $\pi'$ have the same solution. Sampling each example with
probability proportional to its weight produces exactly that distribution, which is the cost-proportionate sampling of
Zadrozny et al. <Cite id="zadrozny2003" />.

### The learnt posterior

If $\ell$ is the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink>, the minimiser of the risk under priors $\pi'$ is the posterior under those priors. By
Bayes' theorem in odds, with likelihood ratio $\Lambda(\xvec) = p(\xvec \mid y = 1)/p(\xvec \mid y = 0)$, the weighted
model learns

$$
\frac{p_w}{1 - p_w} = \Lambda(\xvec)\,\frac{\pi'_1}{\pi'_0} = \Lambda(\xvec)\,\frac{w_1\pi_1}{w_0\pi_0}
= \frac{w_1}{w_0}\cdot\frac{p}{1 - p},
$$

where $p = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ is the true posterior. Weighting multiplies the posterior odds by $w_1/w_0$. This is
the same distortion as undersampling the negatives by $\beta = w_0/w_1$ (see
<NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling">random over- and undersampling</NoteLink>). Balanced weights for two
classes have $w_1/w_0 = n_0/n_1 = (1 - \pi)/\pi$, the <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">imbalance ratio</NoteLink>.

### Only the intercept moves in logistic regression

Suppose <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> is well specified:
$\operatorname{logit} p = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$ for some $(\wvec, b)$. Taking logs of the odds above,

$$
\operatorname{logit} p_w = \wvec^\top\xvec + b + \log\frac{w_1}{w_0}.
$$

This is again a logistic regression, with the same $\wvec$ and intercept $b + \log(w_1/w_0)$. The weighted log loss
is minimised by $p_w$ and the model family contains $p_w$, so the weighted maximum-likelihood estimate converges to
$\paren{\wvec, b + \log(w_1/w_0)}$. Subtracting
$\log(w_1/w_0)$ from the fitted intercept recovers the unweighted model.

King and Zeng derive the same correction for the reverse situation: data collected with a chosen fraction $\bar y$ of
events (case-control sampling) from a population whose true fraction is $\tau$. Their **prior correction** subtracts
$\log\brack{\frac{1 - \tau}{\tau}\cdot\frac{\bar y}{1 - \bar y}}$ from the intercept, which is the log of the ratio of
sample odds to population odds <Cite id="king2001" />. They also compare weighting each observation to undo the
sampling. Prior correction is more efficient when the model is correct. Weighting is more robust when it is not
<Cite id="king2001" />.

### When the model is misspecified

If no $(\wvec, b)$ reproduces the true posterior, the weighted and unweighted fits are different projections of it.
The weighted fit gives more influence to errors on the minority class, so it fits the posterior better where
positives are, at the expense of the majority region. The slope changes, and no intercept correction undoes that.
This is the one case where weighting changes the ranking of examples. It can improve decisions at a low threshold
when the model cannot fit the whole posterior. The figure shows this case with unequal class variances, where the true
log-odds is quadratic in the score.

## Worked example

Simulate $n = 200\,000$ scores with $\pi = 0.02$, $s \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ for negatives and $s \sim \Gauss(2, 1)$ for
positives. The true posterior is logistic with slope 2 and intercept $\operatorname{logit}(0.02) - 2 = -5.892$.

Balanced weights are $w_1 = 1/(2 \times 0.02) = 25$ and $w_0 = 1/(2 \times 0.98) = 0.510$. Their ratio is
$w_1/w_0 = 49$, and $\log 49 = 3.892$.

| Fit | Slope | Intercept |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unweighted | 1.969 | $-5.853$ |
| Weighted | 1.959 | $-1.946$ |
| Weighted, intercept minus $\log 49$ | 1.959 | $-5.838$ |

The slopes agree to within sampling error. The intercept moves by $3.907$, against the predicted $3.892$. After the
correction the weighted model's probabilities match the unweighted model's.

## Cost

Weighting costs nothing beyond the original training. Unlike undersampling, it does not shorten training.

## Trade-offs

- **Weights distort probabilities** exactly as resampling does. Correct them before using them as probabilities:
  subtract $\log(w_1/w_0)$ from the logit, or recalibrate (see
  <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under imbalance</NoteLink>).
- **For a well-specified model, weighting then correcting is the unweighted model.** Choosing the threshold on the
  unweighted model reaches the same decisions (see <NoteLink to="threshold-moving">threshold moving</NoteLink>).
  Weighting gives decisions that threshold moving cannot reach only with misspecified or capacity-limited models, and
  with learners that have no threshold to move.
- **Weights interact with regularisation.** The weighted data term has total weight $nZ$ instead of $n$, so a fixed
  penalty is relatively weaker or stronger than before, and the intercept shift is no longer exactly
  $\log(w_1/w_0)$.
- **Large weights raise gradient variance** in <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>: a minibatch that happens to contain one
  heavily weighted positive takes a large step.
- **Many classes.** With long-tailed class counts, weights $1/n_k$ can be extreme. Cui et al. weight by the inverse of an
  effective number of examples, $(1 - \beta^{n_k})/(1 - \beta)$ with $\beta$ close to 1, which saturates for large
  classes <Cite id="cui2019" />. Menon et al. instead add $\log\pi_k$ to each logit inside the softmax cross-entropy,
  the training-time form of logit adjustment <Cite id="menon2021" />.
