<Definition>
A **multiclass support vector machine** learns one score function $f_k(\xvec) = \wvec_k^\top\xvec$ per class
$k \in \set{1, \dots, K}$, predicts $\hat y = \argmax_k f_k(\xvec)$, and fits all $K$ weight vectors in a single convex
problem: a penalty $\frac12 \sum_k \norm{\wvec_k}^2$ plus a **multiclass hinge loss** that is zero when the true class's
score exceeds every other score by a margin of $1$. The **Weston–Watkins** loss adds a hinge for each class that
violates the margin <Cite id="weston1999multiclass" />, the **Crammer–Singer** loss charges only the largest violation
<Cite id="crammer2001multiclass" />, and the **Lee–Lin–Wahba** loss penalises the wrong classes' scores under a
sum-to-zero constraint <Cite id="lee2004multicategory" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The binary <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> asks the score $y f(\xvec)$ of each
training point to be at least $1$. With $K$ classes there is no single sign to get right. The natural replacement for
$y f(\xvec)$ is the gap between the score of the true class $y$ and the score of a competing class $k$:

$$
m_k(\xvec, y) = f_y(\xvec) - f_k(\xvec), \qquad k \ne y.
$$

The point is classified correctly when all $K - 1$ gaps are positive, and with margin when all are at least $1$.

A reduction such as <NoteLink to="one-versus-rest">one-versus-rest</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="one-versus-one">one-versus-one</NoteLink> trains binary machines separately and combines their outputs
afterwards. Each binary machine optimises its own margin, not the gaps between the scores used at prediction time. A
multiclass SVM puts the gaps themselves in the objective, so training and prediction use the same quantity. Training
solves one optimisation problem with $NK$ or $N(K-1)$ variables instead of several problems with at most $N$ variables
each.

## Details

Training data are pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, N$, with $\xvec_i \in \reals^D$ and
$y_i \in \set{1, \dots, K}$. Write $\Wmat$ for the $K \times D$ matrix with rows $\wvec_k^\top$,
$f_{ik} = \wvec_k^\top\xvec_i$, and $[z]_+ =
\max(0, z)$. A constant feature in $\xvec$ plays the role of the intercepts.

### Weston–Watkins

Weston and Watkins give each training point one slack variable per competing class <Cite id="weston1999multiclass" />:

$$
\min_{\Wmat, \xivec}\ \frac12 \sum_{k=1}^K \norm{\wvec_k}^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k \ne y_i} \xi_{ik}
\quad \text{subject to} \quad f_{i y_i} - f_{ik} \ge 1 - \xi_{ik},\ \ \xi_{ik} \ge 0 .
$$

For fixed $\Wmat$, each $\xi_{ik}$ is minimised separately, and the smallest value satisfying both constraints is
$\xi_{ik} = [1 - (f_{iy_i} - f_{ik})]_+$. Substituting it gives the unconstrained form with the **Weston–Watkins loss**

$$
\ell_{\text{WW}}(\fvec, y) = \sum_{k \ne y} \big[1 - (f_y - f_k)\big]_+ .
$$

Every class whose score comes within $1$ of the true class's score adds a hinge. For $K = 2$, with $f = f_1 - f_2$ and
$y \in \set{1, 2}$ coded as $\pm 1$, the loss is $[1 - yf]_+$, the binary <Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss>.

### Crammer–Singer

Crammer and Singer give each point a single slack, which must cover the worst competing class
<Cite id="crammer2001multiclass" />:

$$
\min_{\Wmat, \xivec}\ \frac12 \sum_{k=1}^K \norm{\wvec_k}^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^N \xi_i
\quad \text{subject to} \quad f_{iy_i} - f_{ik} \ge \Delta_{ik} - \xi_i \ \ \text{for all } k,
$$

with $\Delta_{ik} = 1$ if $k \ne y_i$ and $\Delta_{ik} = 0$ if $k = y_i$. The constraint for $k = y_i$ reads
$\xi_i \ge 0$. The smallest feasible slack is the largest violation, which gives the **Crammer–Singer loss**

$$
\ell_{\text{CS}}(\fvec, y) = \Big[1 - \big(f_y - \max_{k \ne y} f_k\big)\Big]_+ = \max_k \big(\Delta(y, k) + f_k\big) - f_y ,
$$

where $\Delta(y, k) = \indicator[k \ne y]$. The loss depends only on the gap to the strongest competitor. It is never
larger than the Weston–Watkins loss, because the largest of the $K - 1$ hinges is at most their sum.

### The Crammer–Singer dual

Attach multipliers $\alpha_{ik} \ge 0$ to the
$NK$ constraints, one for every point and class. The Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac12 \sum_k \norm{\wvec_k}^2 + C \sum_i \xi_i
- \sum_{i, k} \alpha_{ik} \big(\wvec_{y_i}^\top\xvec_i - \wvec_k^\top\xvec_i - \Delta_{ik} + \xi_i\big).
$$

Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> with respect to $\xi_i$ to zero gives
$\sum_k \alpha_{ik} = C$ for every $i$. The weight vector $\wvec_k$ appears with coefficient $+\sum_r \alpha_{ir}$ in
the term of every point with $y_i = k$, and with coefficient $-\alpha_{ik}$ in the term of every point, so

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \wvec_k} = \wvec_k - \sum_i \Big(\indicator[y_i = k] \sum_r \alpha_{ir} - \alpha_{ik}\Big)
\xvec_i = \zeros
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad
\wvec_k = \sum_i \tau_{ik}\, \xvec_i, \qquad \tau_{ik} = C\, \indicator[y_i = k] - \alpha_{ik} .
$$

The new variables $\tauvec_i = (\tau_{i1}, \dots, \tau_{iK})$ inherit two constraints from $\alpha_{ik} \ge 0$ and
$\sum_k \alpha_{ik} = C$:

$$
\tauvec_i \le C\, \evec_{y_i} \ \ \text{(elementwise)}, \qquad \ones^\top\tauvec_i = 0,
$$

where $\evec_{y}$ is the indicator vector of class $y$. The linear term becomes
$\sum_{i,k} \alpha_{ik}\Delta_{ik} = \sum_i \sum_{k \ne y_i} \alpha_{ik} = \sum_i (C - \alpha_{iy_i}) = \sum_i
\tau_{iy_i}$. Substituting $\wvec_k$ back, the quadratic terms combine to $-\frac12\sum_k \norm{\wvec_k}^2$, and
$\sum_k \wvec_k^\top\wvec_k = \sum_{i,j} (\xvec_i^\top\xvec_j)\, \tauvec_i^\top\tauvec_j$. The dual is

$$
\max_{\tauvec_1, \dots, \tauvec_N}\ -\frac12 \sum_{i,j} \kappa(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)\, \tauvec_i^\top\tauvec_j
+ \sum_i \tau_{iy_i}
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \tauvec_i \le C\evec_{y_i},\ \ \ones^\top\tauvec_i = 0,
$$

with $\kappa(\xvec_i, \xvec_j) = \xvec_i^\top\xvec_j$, or any kernel in its place, so that
$f_k(\xvec) = \sum_i \tau_{ik}\, \kappa(\xvec_i, \xvec)$ <Cite id="crammer2001multiclass" />.

The structure of this dual is what makes the problem solvable at scale:

- **One block per example.** The variables form $N$ blocks $\tauvec_i \in \reals^K$. Each block has its own constraints
  and no constraint links two blocks. The coupling is only through the quadratic term. A decomposition method can
  therefore optimise one block at a time with the others fixed, a $K$-variable problem with one equality and $K$ upper
  bounds, which Crammer and Singer solve exactly in $O(K \log K)$ time by sorting <Cite id="crammer2001multiclass" />.
- **No bias.** The primal has no intercepts $b_k$. Intercepts would add $K$ equality constraints
  $\sum_i \tau_{ik} = 0$ that couple all blocks. Leaving them out, or absorbing them into a constant feature, keeps the
  blocks independent.
- **Sign pattern.** For $k \ne y_i$, $\tau_{ik} = -\alpha_{ik} \le 0$, and $\tau_{iy_i} = -\sum_{k \ne y_i} \tau_{ik} \in
  [0, C]$. Point $i$ adds $\tau_{iy_i}\xvec_i$ to its own class's weight vector and subtracts $\abs{\tau_{ik}}\xvec_i$
  from competing classes' weight vectors.
- **Support vectors.** Points with $\tauvec_i = \zeros$ are those with gap at least $1$ to every class. They drop out of
  every $\wvec_k$, as in the binary machine.

The Weston–Watkins dual has $N(K - 1)$ multipliers, one per point and competing class (only the pairs that violate the
margin get non-zero multipliers), with a similar block structure. With intercepts it also carries $K$ equality
constraints across all points.

### Lee–Lin–Wahba

Lee, Lin and Wahba constrain the scores to sum to zero, $\sum_k f_k(\xvec) = 0$ for every $\xvec$, and penalise only the
wrong classes' scores <Cite id="lee2004multicategory" />. In the scaling used by Tewari and Bartlett the loss is

$$
\ell_{\text{LLW}}(\fvec, y) = \sum_{k \ne y} \big[1 + f_k\big]_+ ,
$$

so each wrong class is pushed down to $f_k \le -1$, and the constraint pushes the true class up
<Cite id="tewari2007consistency" />. Lee, Lin and Wahba state it with margin $1/(K - 1)$ in place of $1$, which rescales
$\fvec$ and changes nothing else. The loss never compares two scores directly.

### Fisher consistency

A loss is **Fisher-consistent** (classification-calibrated) if minimising its <NoteLink to="expectation">expected
value</NoteLink> given $\xvec$, over all score vectors $\fvec \in \reals^K$, gives a score vector whose unique largest
entry is the most probable class $\argmax_k p_k$, where $p_k = \pr(y = k \mid \xvec)$. With enough data and a rich
enough model, minimising a consistent loss then gives the <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes
classifier</NoteLink>. For two classes every convex margin loss with a negative derivative at $0$ is consistent (see
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">surrogate losses</NoteLink>). For $K \ge 3$ that is no longer enough
<Cite id="zhang2004multicategory" /> <Cite id="tewari2007consistency" />.

**The Crammer–Singer loss is inconsistent whenever no class has probability above $\tfrac12$.** The conditional risk is
$R(\fvec) = \sum_y p_y\, \ell_{\text{CS}}(\fvec, y)$. The constant vector $\fvec = \zeros$ has loss $1$ for every class,
so $R(\zeros) = 1$. Now take any $\fvec$, let $t$ be the class with the largest score, $s$ the class with the second
largest, and $\delta = f_t - f_s \ge 0$. For the true class $y = t$ the loss is $[1 - \delta]_+$. For any other true
class $y$, the strongest competitor is $t$, and $f_y \le f_s$, so the loss is $1 + f_t - f_y \ge 1 + \delta$. Hence

$$
R(\fvec) \ge p_t\,[1 - \delta]_+ + (1 - p_t)(1 + \delta).
$$

If $\delta \le 1$ the right side is $1 + \delta(1 - 2p_t)$. If $\delta > 1$ it is $(1 - p_t)(1 + \delta) > 2(1 - p_t)$.
When $\max_k p_k < \tfrac12$, both are at least $1$, and both exceed $1$ unless $\delta = 0$. So the minimum risk is
$1$, every minimiser has its two largest scores tied, and the argmax cannot pick out the Bayes class. The constant
vector attains the minimum: with $\pvec = (0.4, 0.3, 0.3)$ the Bayes class is 1, and $\fvec = (0, 0, 0)$ has
Crammer–Singer risk $1$. When some class has $p_k > \tfrac12$, the minimiser gives that class a gap of $1$ and the
others tied below it, which is correct. Tewari and Bartlett show that no choice of smooth hinge rescues the max-of-gaps
form <Cite id="tewari2007consistency" />.

**The Weston–Watkins loss with the hinge is inconsistent**, but for a different reason: its minimiser can tie the top
two classes when their probabilities are close. With $\pvec = (0.4, 0.35, 0.25)$ the conditional risk is minimised,
uniquely up to adding a constant to every score, at $\fvec = (0, 0, -1)$, with risk $1.75$. Classes 1 and 2 are tied
although class 1 is more probable. Replacing the hinge by a differentiable loss such as the squared hinge
$[1 - m]_+^2$ makes the sum-of-gaps form consistent <Cite id="tewari2007consistency" />.

**The Lee–Lin–Wahba loss is consistent with the plain hinge** <Cite id="lee2004multicategory" />
<Cite id="tewari2007consistency" />. Its minimiser sets $f_k = -1$ for every class except the most probable, which gets
$f = K - 1$.

<Derivation title="Why the Lee–Lin–Wahba minimiser is the Bayes class">
The conditional risk collects, for each class $k$, the probability that $k$ is wrong:

$$
R(\fvec) = \sum_y p_y \sum_{k \ne y} [1 + f_k]_+ = \sum_k (1 - p_k)\,[1 + f_k]_+ .
$$

Write $a_k = 1 + f_k$. The constraint $\sum_k f_k = 0$ becomes $\sum_k a_k = K$. Let $k^\star$ be the unique most
probable class. Then

$$
R = \sum_k (1 - p_k)[a_k]_+ \ \ge\ (1 - p_{k^\star}) \sum_k [a_k]_+ \ \ge\ (1 - p_{k^\star}) \sum_k a_k = (1 - p_{k^\star})K.
$$

The first inequality is an equality only if $[a_k]_+ = 0$ for every $k$ with $p_k < p_{k^\star}$. The second is an
equality only if no $a_k$ is negative. Both hold exactly when $a_k = 0$ for $k \ne k^\star$ and $a_{k^\star} = K$, that
is $f_k = -1$ for $k \ne k^\star$ and $f_{k^\star} = K - 1$. The minimiser is unique and its largest score is the Bayes
class. With $\pvec = (0.4, 0.3, 0.3)$ it is $\fvec = (2, -1, -1)$, with risk $0.6 \times 3 = 1.8$.
</Derivation>

Consistency is a population property over all score functions. With a restricted model, such as a linear or kernel
machine with a finite sample, it guarantees nothing, and an inconsistent loss can still work well in practice. It does
say which losses cannot recover the Bayes classifier however much data and capacity they are given. None of the three
hinge losses estimates class probabilities: their minimisers are piecewise constant in $\pvec$
<Cite id="zhang2004multicategory" />.

### Relation to the structural SVM

The <NoteLink to="structural-support-vector-machine">structural support vector machine</NoteLink> scores whole outputs
with $\lambdavec^\top\Phib(\xvec, y)$ and trains with the loss <Cite id="tsochantaridis2005" />

$$
\max_{y'} \big(\Delta(y, y') + \lambdavec^\top\Phib(\xvec, y')\big) - \lambdavec^\top\Phib(\xvec, y)
$$

Take the output set to be the $K$ classes, the joint feature map to place $\xvec$ in the block of class $y$, so that
$\lambdavec^\top\Phib(\xvec, y) = \wvec_y^\top\xvec$, and $\Delta$ to be the 0–1 loss. The structural loss is then
exactly $\ell_{\text{CS}}$. The Crammer–Singer machine is the structural SVM with the simplest output space, and
loss-augmented decoding is a maximum over $K$ numbers.

### Relation to multinomial logistic regression

<NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression">Multinomial logistic regression</NoteLink> uses the softmax cross-entropy

$$
\ell_{\text{CE}}(\fvec, y) = \log \sum_k e^{f_k} - f_y .
$$

The log-sum-exp is a smooth upper bound on the maximum: $\max_k f_k \le \log \sum_k e^{f_k} \le \max_k f_k + \log K$.
Replacing the maximum in $\max_k(\Delta(y,k) + f_k) - f_y$ by a log-sum-exp gives
$\log \sum_k e^{\Delta(y,k) + f_k} - f_y$, a smooth Crammer–Singer loss with margin; with $\Delta = 0$ it is the softmax
cross-entropy. The cross-entropy is differentiable and consistent, and its minimiser $f_k = \log p_k + c$ recovers the
class probabilities. The hinge losses are exactly zero once all gaps reach $1$, so their solutions depend only on the
support vectors; the cross-entropy is never zero and every point contributes to the fit.

### Comparison with reductions

Hsu and Lin compared the Weston–Watkins and Crammer–Singer machines with one-versus-rest, one-versus-one and the
decision <Gloss name="dag" />, all with the same kernels. Accuracy was similar across methods, the all-together machines
needed longer training, and one-versus-one and the <Gloss name="dag" /> were the most practical
<Cite id="hsu2002comparison" />. Rifkin and Klautau found that a well-tuned one-versus-rest scheme with regularised
binary classifiers is as accurate as the alternatives <Cite id="rifkin2004defense" />.

One-versus-rest with the hinge is itself inconsistent: each binary minimiser is $\sgn(2p_k - 1)$, which is $-1$ for
every class when none has $p_k > \tfrac12$ <Cite id="zhang2004multicategory" />. One-versus-one with the hinge is
consistent under voting, because if every pairwise machine picks the more probable class of its pair, the most probable
class wins all $K - 1$ of its contests and no other class can.

| Method | Problems solved | Variables per problem | Scores comparable across classes | Consistent with hinge |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-versus-rest | $K$ | $N$ | no, each binary machine is scaled separately | no |
| One-versus-one | $K(K-1)/2$ | about $2N/K$ | no, combined by voting | yes, by voting |
| Weston–Watkins | $1$ | $N(K-1)$ | yes | no; yes with squared hinge |
| Crammer–Singer | $1$ | $NK$ | yes | no |
| Lee–Lin–Wahba | $1$ | $NK$ with sum-to-zero | yes | yes |

## Worked example

Take $K = 3$, true class $y = 1$ and scores $\fvec = (0.3, 0.1, -0.4)$. The scores sum to zero, so the Lee–Lin–Wahba
loss applies directly. The prediction $\argmax_k f_k = 1$ is correct. The gaps are $f_1 - f_2 = 0.2$ and
$f_1 - f_3 = 0.7$; both are positive and both are below the margin $1$.

**Weston–Watkins.** Both competitors violate the margin:

$$
\ell_{\text{WW}} = [1 - 0.2]_+ + [1 - 0.7]_+ = 0.8 + 0.3 = 1.1 .
$$

Each active hinge contributes $+1$ to its competitor's score and $-1$ to the true class's. The
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is $\partial \ell / \partial \fvec = (-2, 1, 1)$.

**Crammer–Singer.** Only the strongest competitor, class 2, counts:

$$
\ell_{\text{CS}} = [1 - (0.3 - 0.1)]_+ = 0.8, \qquad \partial \ell / \partial \fvec = (-1, 1, 0).
$$

Class 3 receives no gradient although it is inside the margin. A subgradient step moves only the true class and the
strongest competitor.

**Lee–Lin–Wahba.** The wrong classes are both above $-1$:

$$
\ell_{\text{LLW}} = [1 + 0.1]_+ + [1 - 0.4]_+ = 1.1 + 0.6 = 1.7 .
$$

The gradient of the sum is $(0, 1, 1)$. The scores must stay on the plane $\sum_k f_k = 0$, so the usable direction is
its projection, $(0, 1, 1) - \tfrac23(1, 1, 1) = (-\tfrac23, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$. The true class's score rises only
through the constraint.

**Softmax cross-entropy.** With $e^{0.3} = 1.350$, $e^{0.1} = 1.105$ and $e^{-0.4} = 0.670$, the sum is $3.125$ and the
softmax probabilities are $(0.432, 0.354, 0.214)$. The loss is $-\log 0.432 = 0.840$ and the gradient is
$\operatorname{softmax}(\fvec) - \evec_1 = (-0.568, 0.354, 0.214)$. Every class receives a gradient in proportion to
its probability, and the loss would stay positive at any finite scores.

The four losses agree on the prediction and disagree on how to change the scores. Weston–Watkins pushes both competitors
down equally, Crammer–Singer only the nearest, Lee–Lin–Wahba pushes both wrong scores towards $-1$, and the
cross-entropy weights each competitor by its current probability.

## Assumptions

- The classes are exclusive: each input has one label. Multilabel problems need a different loss.
- The prediction rule is the largest score, and the loss counts all confusions as equally costly. Crammer–Singer and
  the structural form accept a general cost $\Delta(y, k)$ in place of the 0–1 loss.
- The kernel and $C$ are shared by all classes. A reduction can tune them per binary problem.

## Pitfalls

- **Training cost.** The dual has $NK$ or $N(K-1)$ variables and the kernel matrix is shared. Without a decomposition
  method that works block by block, training is much slower than $K$ binary machines <Cite id="hsu2002comparison" />.
- **Intercepts.** Adding a bias per class to the Crammer–Singer machine couples all dual blocks. Use a constant feature
  instead, which regularises the intercepts slightly.
- **Scores are not probabilities.** None of the multiclass hinge losses gives calibrated probabilities. Use the softmax
  cross-entropy, or calibrate the scores afterwards.
- **Reading consistency results.** The inconsistency of the Crammer–Singer and Weston–Watkins hinges concerns the
  population minimiser when no class is dominant. It does not predict poor accuracy on a given data set.
