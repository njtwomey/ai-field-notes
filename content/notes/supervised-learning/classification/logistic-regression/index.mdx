<Definition>
Logistic regression predicts $P(y = 1 \mid \xvec) = \sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)$ for a binary label
$y \in \{0, 1\}$. The logistic function is $\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$. It maps any real score to $(0, 1)$
<Cite id="cox1958" />.
</Definition>

## Model

The score $z = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$ is the log-odds of the positive class:
$z = \log \frac{p}{1 - p}$. Each weight is the change in log-odds per unit change of its feature.

<SigmoidExplorer />

## Decision boundary

The model predicts class 1 when $p \geq 0.5$, which is when $z \geq 0$. The boundary $\wvec^\top\xvec + b = 0$
is a hyperplane. Logistic regression is therefore a linear classifier.

## Training objective

The training set holds $n$ pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$. From here on the bias is absorbed into the weights: each
$\xvec_i$ gains a leading constant feature 1, and $\wvec \in \reals^d$ gains $b$ as its first entry, so
$z_i = \wvec^\top\xvec_i$. The $n \times d$ design matrix $\Xmat$ has the rows $\xvec_i^\top$. The vectors
$\pvec, \yvec \in \reals^n$ hold $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$ and $y_i$.

The model treats each label as a <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli</NoteLink> draw with success probability $p_i$:
$P(y_i \mid \xvec_i) = p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$. The labels are independent given the features, so the
likelihood is the product of these terms. The <Gloss name="mle" /> maximises it. Dividing the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative
log-likelihood</NoteLink> by $n$ gives the mean cross-entropy

$$
L(\wvec) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\big].
$$

Each term simplifies in the score. The logistic function gives $\log p_i = z_i - \log(1 + e^{z_i})$ and
$\log(1 - p_i) = -\log(1 + e^{z_i})$. Substituting both,

$$
L(\wvec) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell_i, \qquad \ell_i = \log\paren{1 + e^{z_i}} - y_i z_i.
$$

Software evaluates $\log(1 + e^{z})$ directly, as $\max(z, 0) + \log(1 + e^{-\abs{z}})$. Computing $p_i$ first loses
the term: in <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">double precision</NoteLink> $\sigma(z)$ rounds to exactly 1 once $z$ exceeds about 37, and $\log(1 - p_i)$
becomes $\log 0$. With labels
$t_i = 2y_i - 1 \in \{-1, 1\}$, the same term is $\ell_i = \log(1 + e^{-t_i z_i})$, the logistic loss of the margin
$t_i z_i$.

An L2 penalty adds $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\tilde\wvec}^2$, where $\tilde\wvec$ is $\wvec$ with the bias entry set to
0; the bias is not penalised. The penalised loss is the negative log-posterior divided by $n$ under the prior
$\tilde\wvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, (n\lambda)^{-1}\eye)$, so its minimiser is a
<Gloss name="map-estimate" /> <Cite id="murphy2012" />. The matrix $\Dmat = \diag(0, 1, \dots, 1)$ writes the
penalty as $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\wvec^\top\Dmat\wvec$.

## Gradient

The loss reaches the weights through a chain of three maps. The weights set each score, $z_i = \wvec^\top\xvec_i$.
The score sets the probability, $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$. The probability sets the example's loss,
$\ell_i = -\big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\big]$, and $L(\wvec) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \ell_i$. The <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>
gives the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of one term as the product of the derivatives along the chain:

$$
\nabla_\wvec \ell_i = \frac{d\ell_i}{dp_i}\,\frac{dp_i}{dz_i}\,\nabla_\wvec z_i.
$$

Each factor follows from its own map.

1. **The loss in the probability.** Differentiating the cross-entropy term,
   $$
   \frac{d\ell_i}{dp_i} = -\frac{y_i}{p_i} + \frac{1 - y_i}{1 - p_i} = \frac{p_i - y_i}{p_i(1 - p_i)}.
   $$
2. **The probability in the score.** Differentiating $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$,
   $$
   \sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\,\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big),
   $$
   so $dp_i/dz_i = p_i(1 - p_i)$.
3. **The score in the weights.** $z_i = \wvec^\top\xvec_i$ is linear in $\wvec$, so $\nabla_\wvec z_i = \xvec_i$.

The factor $p_i(1 - p_i)$ from step 2 cancels the denominator from step 1. In the score alone,
$d\ell_i/dz_i = p_i - y_i$, and in the weights $\nabla_\wvec \ell_i = (p_i - y_i)\,\xvec_i$. The cancellation happens
because the log-odds, the inverse of $\sigma$, is the canonical link of the Bernoulli distribution
<Cite id="mccullagh1989" />. Averaging over the $n$ examples,

$$
\gvec(\wvec) = \nabla L(\wvec) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_i - y_i)\,\xvec_i = \frac{1}{n}\Xmat^\top(\pvec - \yvec).
$$

The penalty adds $\lambda\Dmat\wvec$. Each example pulls the weights along its own feature vector, in proportion to
its residual $p_i - y_i \in (-1, 1)$. An example classified correctly with $p_i$ near $y_i$ contributes almost
nothing. The least-squares gradient $\frac{1}{n}\Xmat^\top(\Xmat\wvec - \yvec)$ of
<NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> has the same form, with $\Xmat\wvec$ in place of
$\sigma(\Xmat\wvec)$. In the figure above, the arrows at $\wvec$ are $-\gvec$ and $-\lambda\Dmat\wvec$ and their sum.

At an unpenalised minimum the gradient vanishes, so $\Xmat^\top\pvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec$. The first column of
$\Xmat$ is all ones, so its row of this equation reads $\sum_i p_i = \sum_i y_i$: the fitted probabilities sum to the
number of positive labels. Every other feature satisfies the same balance, weighted by that feature. The equation is
nonlinear in $\wvec$ because $\pvec$ is, so it has no closed-form solution. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> solves it iteratively
with $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta\,\gvec(\wvec)$ for a step size $\eta > 0$, at $O(nd)$ cost per step.

<Callout variant="warning">
The balance $\sum_i p_i = \sum_i y_i$ makes the probabilities right on average over the training set, and over every
group that a feature indicates. It does not make them right within groups the features cannot express. A misspecified
model, such as one missing a feature, can be miscalibrated there. The L2 penalty keeps the overall balance, because the bias
is not penalised, but breaks it for every other feature. It pulls the probabilities towards the overall rate
$\bar y = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i$, which they all equal in the limit $\lambda \to \infty$, even when accuracy is
unchanged.
</Callout>

## Hessian

The <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> $\Hmat$ is the $d \times d$ matrix of second derivatives, the
derivative of the gradient. It follows from the same chain. In $\nabla\ell_i = (p_i - y_i)\,\xvec_i$ only $p_i$
depends on $\wvec$, and steps 2 and 3 give $\nabla_\wvec p_i = p_i(1 - p_i)\,\xvec_i$. Differentiating each term,

$$
\Hmat(\wvec) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i(1 - p_i)\,\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top = \frac{1}{n}\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat,
\qquad \Smat = \diag(s_1, \dots, s_n), \quad s_i = p_i(1 - p_i).
$$

The penalty adds $\lambda\Dmat$. The labels $y_i$ do not appear in $\Hmat$. The weight $s_i$ is also the curvature of
example $i$'s loss in its score: $d^2\ell_i / dz_i^2 = s_i$.

**Reading the Hessian.** Each term is a product of two factors.

- $s_i = p_i(1 - p_i)$ is the variance of the label under the model. $y_i$ is a Bernoulli variable with mean $p_i$,
  so $\var(y_i \mid \xvec_i) = p_i(1 - p_i)$.
- $\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top$ is the example's scatter, a $d \times d$ <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">outer product</NoteLink>. The average
  $\frac{1}{n}\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ of these outer products is the scatter matrix of the features: the second moments
  $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_{ij}x_{ik}$ of every pair of features $j, k$.

$\Hmat$ is therefore the scatter matrix of the features with each example weighted by the variance of its label. An
example with $p_i$ near 0 or 1 has a label distribution close to a point mass. The model is certain of its label, the
variance is near 0, and the example adds almost no curvature, whether it is classified correctly or not. An example
on the boundary, $p_i = 1/2$, has the largest variance, $1/4$. The model is least certain of its label there, and the
loss is most sensitive to a change in its score. The weight is a variance because the probability's sensitivity to
the score, $dp_i/dz_i$, equals the Bernoulli variance $p_i(1 - p_i)$. Least squares, the loss of linear regression,
has the Hessian $\frac{1}{n}\Xmat^\top\Xmat$: the same scatter, with every weight equal to 1.

The same matrix is the <Gloss name="fisher-information" />. Under the model, the gradient $(p_i - y_i)\,\xvec_i$ of one
example's loss has mean $\zeros$ and covariance $\expect\brack{(p_i - y_i)^2}\,\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top =
p_i(1 - p_i)\,\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top$. Summed over the examples, the covariance of the log-likelihood's gradient is
$\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat = n\Hmat$. Its inverse $(\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat)^{-1}$, evaluated at the fitted weights,
approximates the covariance of the estimate $\hat\wvec$. Statistical software reports the square roots of its
diagonal as the weights' <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> <Cite id="hastie2009" />. A direction along which the uncertain examples
spread widely carries much information, and the weights along it are estimated precisely.

**Convexity.** For any direction $\vvec \in \reals^d$,

$$
\vvec^\top\Hmat\vvec = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i\,(\xvec_i^\top\vvec)^2 \geq 0,
$$

because every $s_i > 0$. The Hessian is <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive semidefinite</NoteLink> at every $\wvec$, so $L$ is convex, and any local
minimum is a global minimum <Cite id="bishop2006" />. The sum is 0 only when $\Xmat\vvec = \zeros$. If $\Xmat$ has
full column rank, $\Hmat$ is positive definite, $L$ is strictly convex, and there is at most one minimiser.

**A bound on the curvature.** A Bernoulli variance $p(1 - p)$ is at most $1/4$, at $p = 1/2$. Therefore
$\Hmat \preceq \frac{1}{4n}\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ everywhere, and the largest eigenvalue of $\Hmat$ never exceeds
$L_{\max} = \lambda_{\max}(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)/(4n)$, plus $\lambda$ with the penalty. Gradient descent with
$\eta = 1/L_{\max}$ lowers the loss at every step, since that is the step for which the quadratic upper bound
$L(\wvec) + \gvec^\top\vvec + \tfrac{L_{\max}}{2}\norm{\vvec}^2$ is minimised.

A confidently wrong example has a large gradient but almost no curvature. The quadratic model of its loss is nearly
flat.

## Newton's method and IRLS

<NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> replaces $L$ near the current $\wvec$ by its second-order
Taylor model $m(\vvec) = L(\wvec) + \gvec^\top\vvec + \tfrac{1}{2}\vvec^\top\Hmat\vvec$ and moves to the model's
minimiser. Setting $\nabla m = \gvec + \Hmat\vvec = \zeros$ gives the step $\vvec = -\Hmat^{-1}\gvec$. For logistic
regression without a penalty the factors $1/n$ cancel:

$$
\wvec_{\text{new}} = \wvec - \Hmat^{-1}\gvec = \wvec + \paren{\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat}^{-1}\Xmat^\top(\yvec - \pvec).
$$

**The weighted least-squares form.** Write $\wvec = (\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat\wvec$ and
collect terms:

$$
\wvec_{\text{new}} = \paren{\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat}^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Smat\big[\Xmat\wvec + \Smat^{-1}(\yvec - \pvec)\big]
= \paren{\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat}^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Smat\zetavec,
$$

with the **working response**

$$
\zeta_i = z_i + \frac{y_i - p_i}{p_i(1 - p_i)}.
$$

$\paren{\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat}^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Smat\zetavec$ is the solution of the weighted least-squares problem
$\min_{\wvec}\sum_i s_i(\zeta_i - \xvec_i^\top\wvec)^2$. A Newton step is therefore a weighted linear regression of
the working responses on the features. Each iteration recomputes the weights $s_i$ and the responses $\zeta_i$ at the
new $\wvec$, which gives the method its name, <Gloss name="irls" /> <Cite id="green1984" />.

The working response has a direct reading. It is the score at which the quadratic model of $\ell_i$ alone is
minimised: in the figure above, the diamond. It is also the first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> of the log-odds of $y_i$
about $p_i$, since $\frac{d}{dp}\log\frac{p}{1 - p} = \frac{1}{p(1 - p)}$. The weight $s_i$ says how firmly
example $i$ holds its score to $\zeta_i$. With the penalty, the same algebra gives

$$
\wvec_{\text{new}} = \paren{\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat + n\lambda\Dmat}^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Smat\zetavec.
$$

The Hessian of $L$ does not involve $\yvec$, so the observed information $n\Hmat$ equals its expectation over the
labels. Newton's method and Fisher scoring are therefore the same algorithm here. For every
<NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink> with its canonical link, IRLS is Newton's
method for the same reason <Cite id="mccullagh1989" />.

**The algorithm.**

1. Start at $\wvec = \zeros$, where every $p_i = 1/2$ and every $s_i = 1/4$.
2. Compute $p_i$, $s_i$ and $\zeta_i$ at the current $\wvec$.
3. Solve $(\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat + n\lambda\Dmat)\,\wvec_{\text{new}} = \Xmat^\top\Smat\zetavec$ by a <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky
   factorisation</NoteLink>, or by a <Gloss name="qr" /> of $\Smat^{1/2}\Xmat$ for better <NoteLink to="numerical-stability-and-conditioning">numerical stability</NoteLink>.
4. If the loss did not decrease, halve the step $\wvec_{\text{new}} - \wvec$ until it does.
5. Stop when the decrease in loss falls below a tolerance. Typical fits take 5 to 10 iterations.

**Convergence.** Near the minimiser, Newton's method converges quadratically: the number of correct digits roughly
doubles per iteration. Far from the minimiser, a full step can overshoot. Where most $s_i$ are small, $\Hmat$ is
small, the quadratic model is flat, and $-\Hmat^{-1}\gvec$ is long. The solver figure in the section on stochastic gradient
descent shows this: on its overlapping dataset, pure Newton converges from $\wvec = \zeros$ but diverges from
$(w_1, w_2) = (-3, 1)$. When $\Xmat$ has full column rank, $\Hmat$ is positive definite and $-\Hmat^{-1}\gvec$ points
downhill wherever $\gvec \neq \zeros$. When a minimiser also exists, because the classes overlap or $\lambda > 0$,
step 4, a backtracking <NoteLink to="line-search">line search</NoteLink>, makes the method converge to it from any
start <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.

**Cost.** Forming $\Xmat^\top\Smat\Xmat$ costs $O(nd^2)$ time and solving the $d \times d$ system $O(d^3)$, with
$O(d^2)$ memory. For $n \geq d$ one Newton step costs about as much as $d$ gradient steps, which cost $O(nd)$ each.
Gradient descent needs on the order of $\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)$ steps to come within $\epsilon$ of the minimum, where
$\kappa$ is the condition number of $\Hmat$ (see <NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">convergence of
gradient descent</NoteLink>). Ten Newton steps therefore cost less whenever $\kappa\log(1/\epsilon)$ exceeds about
$10d$: on ill-conditioned problems with few features.

## Worked example: an intercept-only model

Take a model with only the bias $b$, fitted to labels whose mean is $\bar y = 0.8$. Every $\xvec_i$ is the constant
1, so $L(b) = \log(1 + e^{b}) - \bar y\,b$, $L'(b) = \sigma(b) - \bar y$ and $L''(b) = \sigma(b)(1 - \sigma(b))$. The
gradient vanishes at $\sigma(b^\star) = 0.8$, so $b^\star = \log(0.8/0.2) = \log 4 = 1.386294$.

Newton's method takes $b \leftarrow b + (\bar y - p)/(p(1 - p))$ with $p = \sigma(b)$. Gradient descent with the safe
step $\eta = 1/L_{\max} = 4$ takes $b \leftarrow b + 4(\bar y - p)$. At $b = 0$, $p(1 - p) = 1/4$ exactly, so both
take the same first step to $b = 0.3/0.25 = 1.2$. After that they part:

| $t$ | Newton $b^{(t)}$ | error | gradient descent $b^{(t)}$ | error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | $-1.386$ | 0 | $-1.386$ |
| 1 | 1.2 | $-0.186$ | 1.2 | $-0.186$ |
| 2 | 1.376932 | $-9.4 \times 10^{-3}$ | 1.325901 | $-6.0 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 3 | 1.386268 | $-2.6 \times 10^{-5}$ | 1.365254 | $-2.1 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| 4 | 1.386294 | $-2.1 \times 10^{-10}$ | 1.378805 | $-7.5 \times 10^{-3}$ |

Newton's error is roughly squared at each step. Gradient descent's error shrinks by a constant factor of about 0.36.
That factor is $1 - L''(b^\star)/L_{\max} = 1 - 0.16/0.25$: the step uses the worst-case curvature $1/4$, while the
true curvature at the minimum is $0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$.

## Stochastic gradient descent

The gradient is an average of $n$ per-example gradients. A mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of $B$ examples drawn at random
gives an unbiased estimate of it:

$$
\gvec_{\mathcal{B}}(\wvec) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} (p_i - y_i)\,\xvec_i + \lambda\Dmat\wvec, \qquad
\expect\brack{\gvec_{\mathcal{B}}(\wvec)} = \gvec(\wvec).
$$

<Gloss name="sgd" /> takes $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta_t\,\gvec_{\mathcal{B}}(\wvec)$ at step $t$. Each step
costs $O(Bd)$, independent of $n$, and the data can arrive as a stream <Cite id="bottou2010" />. With $B = 1$ and no
penalty the update is $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec + \eta_t(y_i - p_i)\,\xvec_i$. The
<NoteLink to="perceptron">perceptron</NoteLink> update is the same with the hard prediction
$\hat y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ in place of $p_i$.

**Step sizes.** With a constant $\eta$ the iterates do not converge. They settle into a region around the minimiser
whose size grows with $\eta$ and with the variance of $\gvec_{\mathcal{B}}$. A step size that satisfies
$\sum_t \eta_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty$, such as $\eta_t = \eta_0 / (1 + t/\tau)$, makes the
iterates converge to the minimiser, when one exists and the gradient estimates have bounded variance
<Cite id="robbins1951" />. For a strongly convex loss, steps $\eta_t \propto 1/t$ of a suitable size make the
expected gap
$\expect[L(\wvec_t)] - L(\wvec^\star)$ fall as $O(1/t)$ <Cite id="bottou2018" />. The penalty makes the loss strongly convex in the penalised
weights; the unpenalised bias is strongly convex only through the data, near a minimiser that exists. Gradient
descent's gap falls geometrically, and Newton's faster still, but each of their steps costs a full pass over the
data.

The comparison favours SGD when $n$ is large. The fitted weights only need to be accurate to within their
statistical error, which shrinks as $n$ grows. When $n$ is large, a few passes of SGD reach that accuracy at a
fraction of the cost of optimising to machine precision <Cite id="bottou2010" />.

Averaging the SGD iterates reduces the final noise without a smaller step <Cite id="polyak1992" />. <NoteLink to="variance-reduction">Variance-reduced
methods</NoteLink> such as the stochastic average gradient (SAG) and its variant SAGA store one gradient per example and recover
geometric convergence on a finite training set <Cite id="schmidt2017" /> <Cite id="defazio2014" />.

## Approximate Newton methods

Several methods approximate the Newton step without forming or inverting $\Hmat$.

- **Newton–CG** solves $\Hmat\vvec = -\gvec$ approximately by conjugate gradients, which needs only products $\Hmat\vvec = \frac{1}{n}\Xmat^\top\big(\Smat(\Xmat\vvec)\big)$.
  Each product costs $O(nd)$ and never builds the $d \times d$ matrix. A <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods">trust region</NoteLink> replaces the line search in
  the widely used version for large sparse problems <Cite id="lin2008" />.
- **L-BFGS** builds an approximation to $\Hmat^{-1}$ from the last $m$ pairs of weight and gradient differences, at
  $O(md)$ extra cost per step <Cite id="nocedal2006" />. It is the default solver of scikit-learn's
  `LogisticRegression`; `newton-cholesky`, which is IRLS, is recommended there when $n \gg d$
  <Cite id="sklearn-linear-models" />. See <NoteLink to="quasi-newton-methods">quasi-Newton methods</NoteLink>.
- **The fixed curvature bound** $\frac{1}{4n}\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ of the Hessian section replaces $\Hmat$ in the Newton
  step. The matrix is factorised once, and every step decreases the loss <Cite id="bohning1988" />.

In a comparison of optimisers for logistic regression on large data sets, conjugate gradient and quasi-Newton
methods were the fastest <Cite id="minka2003" />.

## Comparison of solvers

| Method | Cost per iteration | Memory | Convergence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gradient descent | $O(nd)$ | $O(d)$ | linear; the rate depends on the <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> of $\Hmat$ |
| Newton / IRLS | $O(nd^2 + d^3)$ | $O(d^2)$ | quadratic near the minimum; 5 to 10 iterations |
| Newton–CG | $O(nd)$ per <NoteLink to="jacobian-vector-products-and-higher-order-derivatives">Hessian-vector product</NoteLink> | $O(d)$ | superlinear near the minimum |
| L-BFGS | $O(nd + md)$ | $O(md)$ | fast in practice; no quadratic guarantee |
| SGD | $O(Bd)$ per step | $O(d)$ | $O(1/t)$ with decaying steps; a noise floor with a constant step |

## Separable data

If a hyperplane separates the classes perfectly, the likelihood has no maximum. Scaling up any separating $\wvec$
lowers every $\ell_i$, so the weights grow without bound as the fit sharpens. A maximum-likelihood estimate exists
only when the classes overlap <Cite id="albert1984" />. As $\norm{\wvec}$ grows, every $p_i$ approaches 0 or 1, every
$s_i$ approaches 0 and $\Hmat$ approaches the zero matrix. Newton steps then grow longer, and IRLS software reports
fitted probabilities of exactly 0 or 1. The L2 penalty $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\tilde\wvec}^2$ keeps the weights
finite: it adds $\lambda\Dmat$ to $\Hmat$, which keeps the curvature in the weight directions at least $\lambda$.
