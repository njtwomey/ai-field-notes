<Definition>
**Poisson regression** is the <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink> with a
Poisson response and the log link. Count $y_i \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ observed over exposure $t_i > 0$ is modelled as

$$
y_i \sim \Poisson(\mu_i), \qquad \log\mu_i = \log t_i + \xvec_i^\top\betavec.
$$

The term $\log t_i$ has a fixed coefficient of 1 and is called an **offset**. The model says that the rate
$\mu_i / t_i$ is $\exp(\xvec_i^\top\betavec)$ <Cite id="mccullagh1989" />.
</Definition>

## Interpretation

Increasing feature $j$ by one unit, with the others fixed, multiplies the rate by $e^{\beta_j}$. This factor is the
**rate ratio** (in epidemiology, the incidence rate ratio). The effects are multiplicative: a model with
$e^{\beta_1} = 1.5$ and $e^{\beta_2} = 2$ predicts a rate three times higher when both features rise by one.

The exposure is the time, area or population over which the count accumulates: policy-years for insurance claims,
person-years for disease incidence, page views for clicks. Without the offset, a unit observed twice as long looks
twice as risky. Putting $\log t_i$ in as a feature with a free coefficient instead tests whether counts really scale
in proportion to exposure: the coefficient should be near 1.

## Fitting

The log link is the canonical link of the Poisson family. The log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\betavec) = \sum_{i=1}^n \brack{y_i\log\mu_i - \mu_i - \log y_i!},
$$

and its gradient is $\sum_i (y_i - \mu_i)\,\xvec_i$, since $\partial\mu_i/\partial\betavec = \mu_i\xvec_i$. The
maximum solves

$$
\Xmat^\top(\yvec - \hat\muvec) = \zeros.
$$

The Hessian is $-\Xmat^\top\diag(\muvec)\Xmat$, negative definite when $\Xmat$ has full column rank, so the maximum
is unique when it exists. <NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">IRLS</NoteLink> with weights $w_i =
\mu_i$ finds it. The estimated covariance of $\hat\betavec$ is $(\Xmat^\top\diag(\hat\muvec)\Xmat)^{-1}$.

With an intercept, the first score equation is $\sum_i y_i = \sum_i\hat\mu_i$: the model reproduces the total count.
With an indicator for a group, it reproduces that group's total.

## Worked example: claim rates in two groups

An insurer records 60 claims over 1000 policy-years for group A and 108 claims over 1200 policy-years for group B. Fit
$\log\mu_i = \log t_i + \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i$, with $x = 0$ for A and $x = 1$ for B.

The score equations for the intercept and the indicator make each group's fitted total equal its observed total.
Group A gives $1000\,e^{\beta_0} = 60$, and group B gives $1200\,e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1} = 108$. So

$$
\hat\beta_0 = \log 0.06 = -2.813, \qquad \hat\beta_1 = \log\frac{0.09}{0.06} = \log 1.5 = 0.405.
$$

Group B's claim rate is 1.5 times group A's. For the standard error, the information matrix at the fit is

$$
\Xmat^\top\diag(\hat\muvec)\Xmat = \begin{pmatrix} \hat\mu_A + \hat\mu_B & \hat\mu_B \\ \hat\mu_B & \hat\mu_B
\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 168 & 108 \\ 108 & 108 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The lower-right entry of its inverse is $(\hat\mu_A + \hat\mu_B)/(\hat\mu_A\hat\mu_B) = 1/60 + 1/108$, so
$\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_1) = \sqrt{1/60 + 1/108} = 0.161$. The Wald statistic is $z = 0.405/0.161 = 2.52$, with
two-sided p-value 0.012. The 95% interval for the rate ratio is $\exp(0.405 \pm 1.96 \times 0.161) = [1.09, 2.06]$.

## Overdispersion

The Poisson family fixes $\var(y) = \mu$. Counts are **overdispersed** when their variance exceeds their mean, which is
common: units differ in ways the features do not capture, and events cluster. The coefficient estimates remain
consistent if the mean model is right, but the standard errors are too small and tests reject too often.

The check is the Pearson dispersion estimate

$$
\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i},
$$

which should be near 1 for Poisson data. Values well above 1 call for a quasi-Poisson fit, which multiplies standard
errors by $\sqrt{\hat\phi}$, or for <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-and-overdispersion">negative binomial
regression</NoteLink> <Cite id="cameron2013" />.

## Assumptions

- Counts are independent given the features.
- The log rate is linear in the features.
- The variance equals the mean.
- Counts are proportional to exposure.

## Pitfalls

- **Excess zeros.** Data with more zeros than the fitted Poisson predicts, for example because many units can never
  have an event, need a zero-inflated or hurdle model.
- **Least squares on log counts.** Regressing $\log(y + 1)$ on $\xvec$ discards the Poisson variance structure and
  biases the coefficients, and the added 1 is arbitrary.
- **Omitting the offset.** Without exposure, coefficients mix the effect of a feature on the rate with its association
  with observation time.
