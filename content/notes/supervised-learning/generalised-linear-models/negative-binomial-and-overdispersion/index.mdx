<Definition>
Counts are **overdispersed** relative to a Poisson model when $\var(y_i \mid \xvec_i) > \expect[y_i \mid \xvec_i]$.
**Negative binomial regression** keeps the log-linear mean $\log\mu_i = \xvec_i^\top\betavec$ of
<NoteLink to="poisson-regression">Poisson regression</NoteLink> and replaces the Poisson with a negative binomial
distribution whose variance is $\mu_i + \alpha\mu_i^2$ for a dispersion parameter $\alpha \ge 0$. At $\alpha = 0$ it
reduces to the Poisson <Cite id="lawless1987" />.
</Definition>

## Where overdispersion comes from

Two mechanisms are common. **Unobserved heterogeneity**: units with the same features differ in their true rates,
because of variables that were not measured. **Contagion or clustering**: one event makes the next more likely, as with
repeat purchases or outbreaks. Both inflate the variance above the mean. A Poisson fit to such data has consistent
coefficients but <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> that are too small, often by a factor of two
or more <Cite id="cameron2013" />.

## The gamma–Poisson mixture

Let the rate of unit $i$ carry a multiplicative random effect: $y_i \mid \lambda_i \sim \Poisson(\lambda_i)$ with
$\lambda_i = \mu_i u_i$, where $u_i \sim \GammaD$ has mean 1 and variance $\alpha$. The mean and variance of $y_i$
follow from the laws of total expectation and total variance:

$$
\expect[y_i] = \expect\brack{\expect[y_i \mid \lambda_i]} = \expect[\lambda_i] = \mu_i,
$$

$$
\var(y_i) = \expect\brack{\var(y_i \mid \lambda_i)} + \var\paren{\expect[y_i \mid \lambda_i]} = \expect[\lambda_i] +
\var(\lambda_i) = \mu_i + \alpha\mu_i^2.
$$

Integrating the gamma density against the Poisson pmf gives the marginal distribution in closed form. With
$k = 1/\alpha$,

$$
P(y_i = y) = \frac{\Gamma(y + k)}{\Gamma(k)\, y!}\paren{\frac{k}{k + \mu_i}}^k\paren{\frac{\mu_i}{k + \mu_i}}^y,
$$

the <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial distribution</NoteLink> with mean $\mu_i$. This
quadratic-variance version is called NB2. The NB1 variant has $\var(y) = (1 + \alpha)\mu$, linear in the mean.

## Fitting

For fixed $\alpha$, the NB2 distribution is an exponential-dispersion family in $\mu$ with variance function
$V(\mu) = \mu + \alpha\mu^2$. Fitting $\betavec$ is therefore a <Gloss name="glm" /> fit by
<NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS)</NoteLink>, with
log-link weights

$$
w_i = \frac{\mu_i^2}{\mu_i + \alpha\mu_i^2} = \frac{\mu_i}{1 + \alpha\mu_i}.
$$

$\alpha$ is then re-estimated by maximum likelihood, or by moments, and the two steps alternate
<Cite id="lawless1987" />.

Under the Poisson, an observation with a large mean gets weight $\mu_i$, proportional to its size. Under the negative
binomial, the weight levels off at $1/\alpha$, so a few large counts no longer dominate the fit.

## Quasi-Poisson

The alternative keeps the Poisson score equations and assumes only $\var(y_i) = \phi\mu_i$
<Cite id="wedderburn1974" />. The coefficients are identical to the Poisson estimates, because $\phi$ cancels from the
score. The dispersion is estimated by the Pearson statistic $\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum_i (y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2 /
\hat\mu_i$, and every standard error is multiplied by $\sqrt{\hat\phi}$. Quasi-Poisson regression has no likelihood,
so it has no <Gloss name="aic" />.

The two models weight observations differently. Quasi-Poisson weights are proportional to $\mu_i$, as in the Poisson.
Negative binomial weights flatten for large $\mu_i$. When the fitted means span a wide range, the two sets of
coefficients can differ noticeably. A plot of $(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2$ against $\hat\mu_i$ shows which variance function
the data follow: linear for quasi-Poisson, quadratic for NB2 <Cite id="hilbe2011" />.

## Worked example: matching the variance

Counts in one cell of a design have mean 4 and variance 12.

- **Quasi-Poisson:** $12 = \phi \times 4$ gives $\phi = 3$. Poisson standard errors are too small by the factor
  $\sqrt 3 = 1.73$.
- **NB2:** $12 = 4 + \alpha \times 4^2$ gives $\alpha = 0.5$, that is $k = 2$. The gamma random effect has standard
  deviation $\sqrt{0.5} = 0.71$: rates vary by about 70% between units with the same features.

If another cell had mean 20, the quasi-Poisson model would predict variance 60 and NB2 would predict $20 + 0.5 \times
400 = 220$. Comparing cells with different means distinguishes the two.

## Testing for overdispersion

The Poisson model is the NB2 model with $\alpha = 0$. The likelihood-ratio statistic for $\alpha = 0$ against
$\alpha > 0$ does not follow $\ChiSq_1$, because $\alpha = 0$ lies on the boundary of the parameter space. Its null
distribution is an equal mixture of a point mass at 0 and $\ChiSq_1$, so the <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink>
is half the $\ChiSq_1$ tail probability <Cite id="cameron2013" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Overdispersion from a wrong mean.** A missing feature or a wrong link also inflates the Pearson statistic. Fix the
  mean model before choosing a variance model.
- **Excess zeros.** A negative binomial with small $k$ produces many zeros, but a separate zero-generating process
  calls for a zero-inflated or hurdle model instead.
- **Underdispersion.** Variance below the mean, as in counts with a hard ceiling, cannot be fitted by a negative
  binomial. Quasi-Poisson with $\phi < 1$ or a binomial model can.
