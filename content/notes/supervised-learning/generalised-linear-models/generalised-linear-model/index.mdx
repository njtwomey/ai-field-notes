<Definition>
A **generalised linear model** (GLM) for responses $y_1, \dots, y_n$ with feature vectors $\xvec_i \in \reals^p$ has
three parts <Cite id="nelder1972" />:

1. a **random component**: each $y_i$ is independent and follows an exponential-dispersion family with mean $\mu_i$;
2. a **systematic component**: the linear predictor $\eta_i = \xvec_i^\top\betavec$, with coefficients
   $\betavec \in \reals^p$;
3. a **link function** $g$, smooth and strictly monotone, with $g(\mu_i) = \eta_i$.

Linear regression, logistic regression and Poisson regression are GLMs with different families and links.
</Definition>

## Exponential-dispersion families

The random component is a density or mass function of the form

$$
f(y; \theta, \phi) = \exp\paren{\frac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{a(\phi)} + c(y, \phi)}.
$$

$\theta$ is the **canonical parameter**, $b$ is the **cumulant function**, $\phi > 0$ is the **dispersion
parameter**, and $a$ and $c$ are known functions. In most families $a(\phi) = \phi$. For fixed $\phi$ this is a
one-parameter <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> with sufficient statistic $y$
<Cite id="mccullagh1989" />.

The mean and variance of $y$ follow from $b$. Write $\ell(\theta) = \log f(y; \theta, \phi)$ for one observation. Two
identities hold for any regular likelihood: the score has mean zero, $\expect[\ell'(\theta)] = 0$, and
$\expect[\ell''(\theta)] + \expect[\ell'(\theta)^2] = 0$. Both come from differentiating $\int f\, dy = 1$ with respect
to $\theta$ once and twice. Here $\ell'(\theta) = (y - b'(\theta))/a(\phi)$ and $\ell''(\theta) = -b''(\theta)/a(\phi)$.
The first identity gives

$$
\expect[y] = \mu = b'(\theta).
$$

The second gives $-b''(\theta)/a(\phi) + \var(y)/a(\phi)^2 = 0$, so

$$
\var(y) = a(\phi)\, b''(\theta).
$$

$b'$ is strictly increasing because $b'' > 0$, so $\theta$ is a function of $\mu$. The **variance function** is
$V(\mu) = b''(\theta(\mu))$. With $a(\phi) = \phi$, $\var(y) = \phi V(\mu)$. The family determines how the variance
changes with the mean. This mean–variance relation is the main modelling assumption that a GLM adds to the choice of
link.

| Family | $\theta$ | $b(\theta)$ | $\phi$ | $V(\mu)$ | Canonical link $g(\mu)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\mu$ | $\theta^2/2$ | $\sigma^2$ | $1$ | $\mu$ (identity) |
| $\Poisson(\mu)$ | $\log\mu$ | $e^\theta$ | $1$ | $\mu$ | $\log\mu$ |
| $\Bern(\mu)$ | $\log\frac{\mu}{1 - \mu}$ | $\log(1 + e^\theta)$ | $1$ | $\mu(1 - \mu)$ | $\log\frac{\mu}{1 - \mu}$ (logit) |
| $\GammaD$, mean $\mu$, shape $\nu$ | $-1/\mu$ | $-\log(-\theta)$ | $1/\nu$ | $\mu^2$ | $-1/\mu$ (inverse) |
| Inverse Gaussian, mean $\mu$, shape $\lambda$ | $-1/(2\mu^2)$ | $-\sqrt{-2\theta}$ | $1/\lambda$ | $\mu^3$ | $-1/(2\mu^2)$ |

**Example: Poisson.** The Poisson pmf is $e^{-\mu}\mu^y/y! = \exp\paren{y\log\mu - \mu - \log y!}$. So $\theta =
\log\mu$, $b(\theta) = e^\theta$, $a(\phi) = 1$ and $c(y) = -\log y!$. Then $b'(\theta) = e^\theta = \mu$ and
$V(\mu) = b''(\theta) = \mu$: the variance equals the mean.

## Canonical links

The **canonical link** is the link that makes the linear predictor equal the canonical parameter, $\theta_i = \eta_i$.
Since $\mu = b'(\theta)$, the canonical link is $g = (b')^{-1}$. The table lists it for each family. The logit is the
canonical link of the Bernoulli family, which is why <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>
uses the logistic function.

With the canonical link the log-likelihood of the sample is

$$
\ell(\betavec) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{y_i\,\xvec_i^\top\betavec - b(\xvec_i^\top\betavec)}{a(\phi)} + \text{const}.
$$

Three consequences follow. The statistic $\Xmat^\top\yvec$ is sufficient for $\betavec$, where $\Xmat$ is the
$n \times p$ matrix with rows $\xvec_i^\top$. The Hessian $-\Xmat^\top\diag(b''(\eta_i))\Xmat / a(\phi)$ is negative
semi-definite, so $\ell$ is concave and any stationary point is a global maximum. The score equations are

$$
\Xmat^\top(\yvec - \muvec) = \zeros.
$$

With an intercept column, the first equation reads $\sum_i y_i = \sum_i \mu_i$: the fitted means sum to the observed
total. A non-canonical link is allowed and often preferable, for example the log link for gamma responses, whose
canonical link $-1/\mu$ cannot keep $\mu$ positive.

## Maximum likelihood

For any link, the chain rule through $\theta_i$, $\mu_i$ and $\eta_i$ gives the score for coefficient $j$:

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_j} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{y_i - \mu_i}{a(\phi)\, V(\mu_i)}\,
\frac{d\mu_i}{d\eta_i}\, x_{ij}.
$$

The factor $1/V(\mu_i)$ comes from $d\theta/d\mu = 1/b''(\theta) = 1/V(\mu)$. The dispersion $\phi$ multiplies every
term, so $\hat\betavec$ does not depend on it. The equations are nonlinear in $\betavec$. They are solved by
<NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">iteratively reweighted least squares</NoteLink>, which repeats a
weighted least-squares fit on a linearised response <Cite id="nelder1972" />.

The score depends on the family only through $V(\mu)$. Wedderburn used this to define **quasi-likelihood**: choose a
link and a variance function without a full distribution, and solve the same equations
<Cite id="wedderburn1974" />. The estimates remain consistent when only the mean is correctly specified.

## Dispersion and inference

For the Poisson and Bernoulli families $\phi = 1$. For the Gaussian, gamma and inverse Gaussian families $\phi$ is
unknown and is estimated after the fit, usually by the Pearson statistic

$$
\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n - p}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{V(\hat\mu_i)}.
$$

The large-sample covariance of $\hat\betavec$ is $\phi\,(\Xmat^\top\Wmat\Xmat)^{-1}$, where $\Wmat$ is the diagonal
IRLS weight matrix at convergence. Nested models are compared by the difference in their
<NoteLink to="deviance-and-generalised-linear-model-diagnostics">deviance</NoteLink>.

## Transforming the response is not the same model

A log link models $\log \expect[y] = \xvec^\top\betavec$. Least squares on $\log y$ models $\expect[\log y] =
\xvec^\top\betavec$. By Jensen's inequality $\expect[\log y] < \log\expect[y]$ whenever $y$ varies, so the two
coefficient vectors differ. The transformed fit also cannot use observations with $y = 0$ and assumes constant variance
on the log scale. The GLM keeps $y$ on its own scale and puts the nonlinearity in the mean.

## Assumptions

- Observations are independent given their features.
- The link is correct: $g(\mu)$ is linear in the features.
- The variance function is correct. If it is wrong but the mean is right, $\hat\betavec$ is still consistent, but the
  standard errors are wrong; a sandwich estimator or quasi-likelihood repairs them.

## Pitfalls

- **Overdispersion.** Count data often have variance well above the Poisson mean. Poisson standard errors are then too
  small. See <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-and-overdispersion">negative binomial regression and
  overdispersion</NoteLink>.
- **Separation.** For binary responses, a feature that separates the classes sends $\hat\betavec$ to infinity.
- **Coefficients live on the link scale.** With a log link, $e^{\beta_j}$ is a multiplicative effect on the mean. With
  a logit link, $e^{\beta_j}$ is an odds ratio, not a ratio of probabilities.
