<Definition>
**Gamma regression** is the <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink> with a gamma
response: $y_i > 0$ has mean $\mu_i$, shape $\nu$ and variance $\mu_i^2/\nu$, and $g(\mu_i) = \xvec_i^\top\betavec$.
The link is usually the log. **Tweedie regression** uses the family with variance function $V(\mu) = \mu^p$ for a
power $p$; $p = 2$ is the gamma, $p = 1$ the Poisson and $p = 0$ the Gaussian <Cite id="jorgensen1987" />.
</Definition>

## Gamma regression

The gamma density with mean $\mu$ and shape $\nu$ is

$$
f(y) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)\, y}\paren{\frac{\nu y}{\mu}}^\nu e^{-\nu y/\mu} = \exp\paren{\frac{y\,(-1/\mu) -
\log\mu}{1/\nu} + c(y, \nu)}.
$$

This is an exponential-dispersion family with <NoteLink to="exponential-family">canonical parameter</NoteLink> $\theta = -1/\mu$, cumulant function $b(\theta) =
-\log(-\theta)$ and dispersion $\phi = 1/\nu$. Then $V(\mu) = b''(\theta) = 1/\theta^2 = \mu^2$, so

$$
\var(y) = \phi\mu^2, \qquad \frac{\operatorname{sd}(y)}{\mu} = \sqrt\phi.
$$

The coefficient of variation is constant. Gamma regression suits positive quantities whose spread scales with their
size: costs, durations, rainfall amounts, concentrations. $\phi$ is the squared coefficient of variation and is
estimated by the Pearson statistic divided by $n - p$.

The canonical link is $-1/\mu$ (often written as the inverse link $1/\mu$), but it does not keep $\mu$ positive. The log
link does, and makes coefficients multiplicative: $e^{\beta_j}$ is the factor by which the mean changes per unit of
feature $j$. With the log link, gamma regression and least squares on $\log y$ both describe multiplicative effects.
They estimate different targets: the GLM models $\log\expect[y]$ and the log-scale regression models
$\expect[\log y]$. The GLM predicts the mean directly, without a retransformation correction.

## Tweedie models

The Tweedie family is the exponential-dispersion family with $\var(y) = \phi\mu^p$. It exists for $p \le 0$ and
$p \ge 1$, and $p = 3$ gives the inverse Gaussian <Cite id="jorgensen1987" />. The range $1 < p < 2$ is the useful new
case: there the distribution is a **compound Poisson–gamma**,

$$
y = \sum_{k=1}^N Z_k, \qquad N \sim \Poisson(\lambda), \qquad Z_k \iid \GammaD \text{ with shape } \alpha \text{ and
scale } \gamma,
$$

with $y = 0$ when $N = 0$. So $P(y = 0) = e^{-\lambda} > 0$, and $y$ is continuous above 0. This is the shape of an
insurance policy's annual loss: most policies have no claims, and the rest have a random number of claims of random
size <Cite id="smyth2002" />.

The mean is $\mu = \lambda\alpha\gamma$ and the variance is $\lambda\alpha(\alpha + 1)\gamma^2$. Rewriting these in
terms of $\mu$ gives the Tweedie form $\phi\mu^p$ with

$$
p = \frac{\alpha + 2}{\alpha + 1}, \qquad \phi = \frac{\lambda^{1 - p}(\alpha\gamma)^{2 - p}}{2 - p}.
$$

Since $\alpha > 0$, $p$ lies in $(1, 2)$. Highly variable claim sizes (small $\alpha$) push $p$ towards 2; nearly
constant claim sizes (large $\alpha$) push it towards 1.

<Derivation title="Mean and variance of the compound Poisson–gamma sum">
Condition on $N$. Each $Z_k$ has mean $\alpha\gamma$ and variance $\alpha\gamma^2$, so $\expect[y \mid N] =
N\alpha\gamma$ and $\var(y \mid N) = N\alpha\gamma^2$. By the laws of total expectation and variance, with
$\expect[N] = \var(N) = \lambda$:

$$
\expect[y] = \lambda\alpha\gamma, \qquad \var(y) = \lambda\alpha\gamma^2 + \alpha^2\gamma^2\lambda =
\lambda\alpha(\alpha + 1)\gamma^2.
$$

With $p = (\alpha + 2)/(\alpha + 1)$ and $\phi$ as above, $\phi\mu^p = \lambda^{1-p}(\alpha\gamma)^{2-p}
(\lambda\alpha\gamma)^p/(2 - p) = \lambda\alpha^2\gamma^2/(2 - p)$. Since $2 - p = \alpha/(\alpha + 1)$, this equals
$\lambda\alpha(\alpha + 1)\gamma^2$.
</Derivation>

## Fitting

For a fixed $p$, the Tweedie model is a GLM, fitted by <NoteLink to="iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">IRLS</NoteLink>
with the log link. Its density has no closed form, but the score equations need only $V(\mu) = \mu^p$, so the
coefficients do not require it. The density, needed to choose $p$ by likelihood, is evaluated by series expansions
<Cite id="smyth2002" />. A common practice is to fit over a grid of $p$ values and keep the best profile likelihood.

A Tweedie model of the total loss gives one model where the alternative is two: a Poisson model for claim counts and a
gamma model for claim sizes. The single model assumes the same features drive frequency and severity in a linked way.
The two-part model allows them to differ.

## Pitfalls

- **Zeros in a gamma model.** The gamma density is zero at $y = 0$, so exact zeros cannot be fitted. Use a Tweedie
  model or a two-part model rather than adding a small constant.
- **Non-constant coefficient of variation.** If the spread grows more slowly than the mean, a gamma model overweights
  small observations. A <NoteLink to="residual-diagnostics">residual plot</NoteLink> of $(y - \hat\mu)^2$ against $\hat\mu$ on log–log axes estimates the power $p$
  directly as its slope.
- **Canonical link.** The inverse link can produce negative fitted means, and IRLS then fails. Prefer the log link.
