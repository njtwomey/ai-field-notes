<Definition>
Given data $(x_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, with $x_i$ in an interval $[a, b]$, the **cubic smoothing spline** with
smoothing parameter $\lambda > 0$ is the function $\hat f$ that minimises

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \paren{y_i - f(x_i)}^2 + \lambda\int_a^b f''(x)^2\,dx
$$

over all functions with a square-integrable second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>. The first term rewards closeness to the data. The
second penalises curvature <Cite id="reinsch1967" />.
</Definition>

## The minimiser is a natural cubic spline

The search is over an infinite-dimensional set of functions, yet the answer is finite-dimensional. Let
$t_1 < \dots < t_m$ be the distinct values among the $x_i$.

This is Holladay's theorem for <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">interpolating cubic splines</NoteLink>.

**Claim.** For any candidate $g$, let $s$ be the <NoteLink to="regression-splines">natural cubic spline</NoteLink> with knots $t_1, \dots, t_m$ that interpolates
$g$ at the knots, $s(t_j) = g(t_j)$. Then $\int s''^2 \le \int g''^2$, with equality only if $g = s$
<Cite id="green1994" />.

**Proof.** Let $h = g - s$, so $h(t_j) = 0$ at every knot. Integrate by parts:

$$
\int_a^b s''h''\,dx = \brack{s''h'}_a^b - \int_a^b s'''h'\,dx.
$$

A natural spline is linear outside $[t_1, t_m]$, so $s'' = 0$ at $a$ and $b$, and the boundary term vanishes. On
each interval $(t_j, t_{j+1})$, $s$ is cubic, so $s'''$ is a constant $c_j$; outside $[t_1, t_m]$ it is zero. Hence

$$
\int_a^b s'''h'\,dx = \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} c_j\brack{h(t_{j+1}) - h(t_j)} = 0.
$$

So $\int s''h'' = 0$, and

$$
\int g''^2 = \int (s'' + h'')^2 = \int s''^2 + 2\int s''h'' + \int h''^2 = \int s''^2 + \int h''^2 \ge \int s''^2.
$$

Equality requires $h'' = 0$, so $h$ is linear; $h$ is zero at two or more knots, so $h = 0$.

Replacing any $g$ by its interpolant $s$ keeps the residual sum of squares, which depends only on the values at the
knots, and does not increase the penalty. The minimiser is therefore a natural cubic spline with knots at the
$t_j$.

## The fit is a linear smoother

Write $f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^m \theta_j N_j(x)$ in a basis of natural cubic splines with those knots. Let $\Nmat$ be the
$n \times m$ matrix $N_{ij} = N_j(x_i)$ and $\Omegamat$ the $m \times m$ matrix $\Omega_{jk} = \int N_j''N_k''$. The
criterion becomes a generalised ridge problem:

$$
\norm{\yvec - \Nmat\thetavec}^2 + \lambda\,\thetavec^\top\Omegamat\thetavec, \qquad \hat\thetavec =
\paren{\Nmat^\top\Nmat + \lambda\Omegamat}^{-1}\Nmat^\top\yvec.
$$

The fitted values are $\hat\yvec = \Smat_\lambda\yvec$ with the **smoother matrix**

$$
\Smat_\lambda = \Nmat\paren{\Nmat^\top\Nmat + \lambda\Omegamat}^{-1}\Nmat^\top.
$$

$\Smat_\lambda$ does not depend on $\yvec$, so the smoothing spline is a **linear smoother**. Unlike a least-squares <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">hat
matrix</NoteLink>, it is not a projection: $\Smat_\lambda^2 \ne \Smat_\lambda$ for $\lambda > 0$.

## Effective degrees of freedom

For a projection, the trace equals the number of fitted parameters. By analogy, the **effective degrees of freedom**
of a linear smoother is $\operatorname{df}_\lambda = \trace\Smat_\lambda$.

Suppose the $x_i$ are distinct, so $\Nmat$ is square and invertible. Then
$\Smat_\lambda = (\eye + \lambda\Kmat)^{-1}$ with $\Kmat = \Nmat^{-\top}\Omegamat\Nmat^{-1}$ (the **Reinsch form**).
$\Kmat$ is symmetric <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss> with eigenvalues $d_1 \le d_2 \le \dots \le d_n$. The two smallest are zero,
because linear functions have no curvature. The eigenvalues of $\Smat_\lambda$ are $1/(1 + \lambda d_k)$, so

$$
\operatorname{df}_\lambda = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{1 + \lambda d_k} = 2 + \sum_{k=3}^n \frac{1}{1 + \lambda d_k}.
$$

As $\lambda \to 0$, every term tends to 1 and $\operatorname{df} \to n$: the spline interpolates the data. As
$\lambda \to \infty$, only the two null-space terms remain and $\operatorname{df} \to 2$: the fit becomes the
least-squares line. Between these limits, $\operatorname{df}_\lambda$ decreases continuously, so it is a more
interpretable way to set the amount of smoothing than $\lambda$ itself, whose scale depends on the units of $x$.

The eigenvectors of $\Kmat$ behave like sinusoids of increasing frequency. The smoother leaves low-frequency components
almost untouched and shrinks high-frequency components strongly <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

## Choosing $\lambda$

<Gloss name="loo">Leave-one-out cross-validation</Gloss> has a closed form for any linear smoother whose leave-one-out fit equals the full fit
with $y_i$ replaced by its own prediction, which holds here:

$$
\operatorname{CV}(\lambda) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\paren{\frac{y_i - \hat f_\lambda(x_i)}{1 - S_{ii}}}^2.
$$

**Generalised cross-validation** replaces each $S_{ii}$ by their average $\trace\Smat_\lambda / n$
<Cite id="craven1979" />:

$$
\operatorname{GCV}(\lambda) = \frac{n\norm{\yvec - \Smat_\lambda\yvec}^2}{\paren{n - \trace\Smat_\lambda}^2}.
$$

REML, which treats the penalty as a Gaussian prior, is a common alternative; see
<NoteLink to="additive-model-smoothing-parameter-selection">smoothing parameter selection</NoteLink>.

## Computation and other views

The Reinsch algorithm computes the fit in $O(n)$ operations, using the banded structure of the natural spline
equations <Cite id="reinsch1967" />. B-spline bases give the same banded structure.

- **Equivalent kernel.** Away from the boundaries, a smoothing spline behaves like a kernel smoother with a kernel whose
  bandwidth varies as $\lambda^{1/4}$ times a power of the local design density <Cite id="silverman1984" />.
- **Bayesian view.** The smoothing spline is the posterior mean of $f$ under a Gaussian-process prior (an integrated
  <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Wiener process</NoteLink>) with a flat prior on linear functions <Cite id="kimeldorf1971" /> <Cite id="wahba1990" />. See
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Ties in $x$.** With repeated $x$ values, the knots are the distinct values, and the fit uses the means of the tied
  responses weighted by their counts.
- **Scale of $\lambda$.** $\lambda$ changes meaning when $x$ or $y$ is rescaled. Report the effective degrees of
  freedom.
- **Correlated errors.** Cross-validation assumes independent errors. With autocorrelated residuals, GCV chooses too
  small a $\lambda$ and the fit follows the noise.
