<Definition>
**Local regression** estimates a regression function $f$ at a target point $x_0$ by weighted least squares. Each
observation gets weight $w_i(x_0) = K\paren{\abs{x_i - x_0}/h(x_0)}$, where $K$ is a kernel that decreases to zero at
1 and $h(x_0)$ is a bandwidth. A polynomial of degree $q$ in $x - x_0$ is fitted with these weights, and its value at
$x_0$ is the estimate $\hat f(x_0)$. Repeating this at every target point traces a curve. **LOESS** uses the tricube
kernel $K(u) = (1 - u^3)^3$ for $u < 1$, a nearest-neighbour bandwidth, and $q = 1$ or 2 <Cite id="cleveland1979" />.
</Definition>

## Mechanism

At target $x_0$, solve

$$
\hat\betavec(x_0) = \argmin_{\betavec} \sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x_0)\paren{y_i - \sum_{k=0}^q \beta_k (x_i - x_0)^k}^2,
\qquad \hat f(x_0) = \hat\beta_0(x_0).
$$

Centring the polynomial at $x_0$ makes the intercept the fitted value at $x_0$. The **span** $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ sets
the bandwidth: $h(x_0)$ is the distance from $x_0$ to its $\lceil\alpha n\rceil$-th nearest observation. The window
therefore always contains the same number of points. It widens where data are sparse and narrows where they are dense.

The span controls the bias–variance trade-off. A small span follows the data closely and has high variance. A large
span averages over a wide window and is biased wherever $f$ curves. $\alpha$ is chosen by eye, by cross-validation, or
by an information criterion.

## A linear smoother

Let $\Xmat_0$ be the $n \times (q + 1)$ matrix with rows $(1, x_i - x_0, \dots, (x_i - x_0)^q)$ and $\Wmat_0 =
\diag(w_i(x_0))$. The weighted least-squares solution gives

$$
\hat f(x_0) = \evec_1^\top\paren{\Xmat_0^\top\Wmat_0\Xmat_0}^{-1}\Xmat_0^\top\Wmat_0\,\yvec = \sum_{i=1}^n l_i(x_0)\,y_i,
$$

where $\evec_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)^\top$. The weights $l_i(x_0)$ depend on the $x_i$ but not on the $y_i$, so LOESS is a
linear smoother, like a <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing spline</NoteLink>.
<NoteLink to="stacking">Stacking</NoteLink> $l_i(x_j)$ into a matrix $\Lmat$ gives $\hat\yvec = \Lmat\yvec$, and
$\trace\Lmat$ is the <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>
<Cite id="cleveland1988" />.

## Boundary bias of local averages and local lines

Degree $q = 0$ gives a weighted local average, the Nadaraya–Watson kernel estimate. It is biased at the edges of the
data. At the left boundary all neighbours lie to the right of $x_0$. If $f$ is increasing, their average exceeds
$f(x_0)$.

A local polynomial of degree $q$ fits any polynomial of degree at most $q$ exactly, so its weights reproduce such
polynomials:

$$
\sum_i l_i(x_0) = 1, \qquad \sum_i l_i(x_0)\,(x_i - x_0)^k = 0 \quad (k = 1, \dots, q).
$$

Expand $f$ about $x_0$: $f(x_i) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x_i - x_0) + \tfrac12 f''(x_0)(x_i - x_0)^2 + \dots$. The expected
estimate is $\sum_i l_i(x_0) f(x_i)$. For $q = 1$, the constraints remove the constant and linear terms, so

$$
\expect[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0) = \tfrac12 f''(x_0)\sum_i l_i(x_0)(x_i - x_0)^2 + \dots.
$$

The bias depends only on curvature, even at the boundary where the neighbours are one-sided. For $q = 0$ only the first
constraint holds. The bias then has the term $f'(x_0)\sum_i l_i(x_0)(x_i - x_0)$, which is large at the boundary,
where all $x_i - x_0$ share one sign <Cite id="hastie2009" />. Local quadratics also remove the curvature term at the
cost of more variance; they help at peaks and troughs.

## Robustness iterations

Least squares is sensitive to outliers, and so is each local fit. Cleveland's LOWESS (locally weighted scatterplot
smoothing) adds robustness weights <Cite id="cleveland1979" />. After a first fit, compute residuals $r_i$ and their
median absolute value $s$. Give each point the bisquare weight

$$
\delta_i = B\paren{\frac{r_i}{6s}}, \qquad B(u) = (1 - u^2)^2 \text{ for } \abs{u} < 1, \text{ else } 0,
$$

and refit with weights $\delta_i w_i(x_0)$. A point with a residual beyond six median absolute residuals gets weight
zero. Two iterations usually suffice.

## Several inputs

The same method works for $\xvec \in \reals^d$ with Euclidean distances and a local linear or quadratic surface. It
degrades quickly with $d$: a neighbourhood that holds a fixed fraction of the data must extend across most of the
range of each input once $d$ exceeds 3 or 4.

## Pitfalls

- **No closed-form curve.** The fit exists only as values at target points. Predicting at a new point requires the
  training data and a new local fit.
- **Cost.** Each target point needs a weighted least-squares fit over its neighbours, so a fit at $m$ points costs
  about $O(m\alpha n)$. Implementations fit at a grid of vertices and interpolate.
- **Span too small for the degree.** A local quadratic needs at least three distinct points in each window, and in
  practice many more. Small spans with $q = 2$ give erratic fits.
