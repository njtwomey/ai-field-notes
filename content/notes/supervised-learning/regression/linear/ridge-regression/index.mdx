<Definition>
Ridge regression fits $\yvec \approx \Xmat\wvec$ by minimising the penalised squared error

$$
L(\wvec) = \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}^2, \qquad \lambda \ge 0,
$$

where $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times d}$ holds the features, $\yvec \in \reals^n$ the targets and $\wvec \in \reals^d$ the
coefficients. The solution is $\hat\wvec_\lambda = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$
<Cite id="hoerl1970" />. At $\lambda = 0$ it is ordinary least squares; as $\lambda \to \infty$ every coefficient tends
to zero.
</Definition>

## Why penalise

Ordinary least squares (<NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>) is unbiased, but its variance
can be large. When two features are strongly correlated, the data pin down their sum well and their difference badly.
OLS then fits large coefficients of opposite sign that nearly cancel. On the diabetes data, the blood-serum
measurements s1 and s2 have correlation 0.90 and OLS coefficients $-37.7$ and $+22.7$. A small change in the data
moves such coefficients a long way. When $d > n$, or when a feature is an exact combination of others,
$\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ is singular and OLS has no unique solution at all.

The penalty $\lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$ trades a little bias for a large reduction in variance. Among coefficient vectors
that fit the data about equally well, it prefers the one with the smallest norm.

## The closed form

$L$ is a quadratic in $\wvec$. Expanding,

$$
L(\wvec) = \wvec^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)\wvec - 2\wvec^\top\Xmat^\top\yvec + \yvec^\top\yvec.
$$

Its gradient is $\nabla L = 2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)\wvec - 2\Xmat^\top\yvec$. Setting it to zero gives the
penalised normal equations

$$
(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)\,\wvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec.
$$

The matrix $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ is positive semi-definite: $\vvec^\top\Xmat^\top\Xmat\vvec = \norm{\Xmat\vvec}^2 \ge 0$.
Adding $\lambda\eye$ raises every eigenvalue by $\lambda$, so for $\lambda > 0$ the matrix is
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite</NoteLink>. It is therefore invertible, the solution is
unique whatever $\Xmat$ is, and the Hessian $2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)$ confirms it is the minimum. Ridge was
introduced for exactly this reason: to make ill-conditioned normal equations solvable <Cite id="hoerl1970" />. The
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> falls from $\sigma_1^2/\sigma_d^2$ to
$(\sigma_1^2 + \lambda)/(\sigma_d^2 + \lambda)$.

## Shrinkage along singular directions

The <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> shows what the penalty does.
Write $\Xmat = \Umat\Dmat\Vmat^\top$ with singular values $d_1 \ge \dots \ge d_p > 0$, left singular vectors
$\uvec_j$ and right singular vectors $\vvec_j$. Then $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = \Vmat\Dmat^2\Vmat^\top$ and
$\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye = \Vmat(\Dmat^2 + \lambda\eye)\Vmat^\top$, so

$$
\hat\wvec_\lambda = \Vmat(\Dmat^2 + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\Dmat\Umat^\top\yvec
= \sum_{j} \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda} \cdot \frac{\uvec_j^\top\yvec}{d_j}\, \vvec_j.
$$

The factor $\uvec_j^\top\yvec / d_j$ is the OLS coefficient along $\vvec_j$. Ridge multiplies it by
$d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda)$, which lies between 0 and 1. Directions with a large singular value, along which the features
vary a lot and the data determine the coefficient well, are barely touched. Directions with a small singular value are
shrunk hard. Ridge removes variance exactly where OLS has most of it <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

The fitted values are $\hat\yvec = \Xmat\hat\wvec_\lambda = \sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\,\uvec_j\uvec_j^\top\yvec$.
The trace of this linear map is the **effective degrees of freedom**

$$
\operatorname{df}(\lambda) = \sum_{j} \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda},
$$

which falls from $p$ (the OLS fit) at $\lambda = 0$ to 0 as $\lambda \to \infty$.

### Worked example: the diabetes data

The diabetes data have ten standardised features and 442 patients <Cite id="efron2004" />. The singular values of
$\Xmat$ run from $d_1 = 42.2$ down to $d_9 = 5.88$ and $d_{10} = 1.95$. The smallest direction is
$\vvec_{10} \approx -0.71\,\mathrm{s1} + 0.56\,\mathrm{s2} + 0.32\,\mathrm{s3} + 0.26\,\mathrm{s5}$: the contrast
between the two correlated serum measurements. OLS puts a coefficient of 51.1 on it.

At $\lambda = 100$ the shrinkage factors are 0.947 along $\vvec_1$ and $1.95^2/(1.95^2 + 100) = 0.036$ along
$\vvec_{10}$. The contrast's coefficient falls from 51.1 to 1.9, while the well-determined directions keep most of
theirs. The effective degrees of freedom are 6.59, and the condition number of the normal equations falls from 470 to 18. The coefficients of s1 and s2 go from $-37.7$ and $+22.7$ to $-2.1$
and $-3.7$. The training $R^2$ drops only from 0.518 to 0.506.

## Bayesian view

Suppose $y_i = \wvec^\top\xvec_i + \varepsilon_i$ with independent $\varepsilon_i \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, and give
the coefficients a <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian</NoteLink> prior $\wvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \tau^2\eye)$.
By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> the log posterior is, up to a constant,

$$
\log p(\wvec \mid \yvec) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\norm{\wvec}^2.
$$

Multiplying by $-2\sigma^2$ gives the ridge objective with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$. The ridge estimate is therefore
the posterior mode, and since the posterior is Gaussian, also its mean <Cite id="bishop2006" />. A strong prior belief
that coefficients are small (small $\tau^2$) is a large $\lambda$.

## Practice

- **Standardise the features.** The penalty treats every coefficient alike, so it depends on the units of each
  feature. Rescaling a feature by 1,000 divides its coefficient by 1,000 and makes it almost free. Standardise every
  column to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 before fitting, as the figure does.
- **Do not penalise the intercept.** Shrinking the intercept towards zero would make the fit depend on where the
  target's origin happens to be. Centre $\yvec$ and the columns of $\Xmat$, fit without an intercept, and recover it as
  $\bar y - \bar\xvec^\top\hat\wvec$. scikit-learn's `Ridge` does this by default.
- **Choose λ by validation.** Cross-validation over a logarithmic grid is standard. The efficient leave-one-out formula
  uses the same SVD for every $\lambda$.
- **Scaling conventions differ.** scikit-learn's `Ridge(alpha=λ)` minimises $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$.
  Objectives written with a mean squared error, $\frac{1}{2n}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2$,
  correspond to $\lambda = n\alpha$. Weight decay in neural-network training is the same penalty applied by
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **No selection.** Ridge never sets a coefficient to exactly zero, so it keeps every feature. For sparse models use
  the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> or the <NoteLink to="elastic-net">elastic net</NoteLink>.
- **Shrinkage is bias.** Ridge coefficients are biased towards zero, so they understate true effects. Report them as
  predictions, not as unbiased effect estimates.
- **Correlated features share weight.** Ridge splits the weight of a group of correlated features roughly evenly. That
  is stable, but no single coefficient then measures its feature's effect.
