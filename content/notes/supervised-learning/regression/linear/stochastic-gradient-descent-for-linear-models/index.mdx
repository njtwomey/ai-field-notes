<Definition>
<Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)</Gloss> for linear models minimises a penalised average loss

$$
F(\wvec, b) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(y_i - \wvec^\top\xvec_i - b\bigr)^2 + \alpha R(\wvec)
$$

by visiting the samples one at a time and stepping against the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of each
sample's term. The penalty $R$ is $\frac{1}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2$ for ridge, $\norm{\wvec}_1$ for the lasso, or the
<NoteLink to="elastic-net">elastic-net</NoteLink> mixture $\rho\norm{\wvec}_1 + \frac{1 - \rho}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2$
<Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The exact solvers for these models need the data at hand and cost more than one pass over it. The closed form for
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge</NoteLink> forms and factorises $\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye$, at
$O(nd^2 + d^3)$ time and $O(d^2)$ memory. <NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">Coordinate descent</NoteLink> for the
<NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> makes repeated $O(nd)$ passes over data held in memory. When $n$ runs to hundreds
of millions of rows, when $d$ is large and the features sparse, or when data arrive as a stream, neither is practical.
SGD needs $O(d)$ memory and $O(d)$ time per update, and it can learn from each batch as it arrives
<Cite id="bottou2010" />.

The objective $F$ is the same as the exact solvers' up to scaling. The lasso and elastic net match scikit-learn's
`Lasso` and `ElasticNet` with the same $\alpha$ and $\rho$. The ridge term $\frac{\alpha}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2$ matches
`Ridge` with $\lambda = n\alpha$.

## Mechanism

### The smooth part

For sample $i$ with residual $r_i = y_i - \wvec^\top\xvec_i - b$, the gradient of its loss term
$\frac{1}{2}r_i^2$ is $-r_i\xvec_i$ with respect to $\wvec$ and $-r_i$ with respect to $b$. With step size $\eta_t$ at
step $t$, the ridge update is

$$
\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta_t(-r_i\xvec_i + \alpha\wvec) = (1 - \eta_t\alpha)\,\wvec + \eta_t r_i\xvec_i, \qquad b \leftarrow b + \eta_t r_i.
$$

The penalty appears as **<Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>**: every step first multiplies the weights by
$1 - \eta_t\alpha$, then moves them towards fitting sample $i$. Because the <NoteLink to="expectation">expected
value</NoteLink> of the sample gradient, over a uniformly chosen $i$, is the full gradient of $F$, each step is an
unbiased estimate of a <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient-descent</NoteLink> step.

### The L1 part and exact zeros

$\abs{w_j}$ has no gradient at zero. The naive **subgradient** step uses $\sgn(w_j)$:
$w_j \leftarrow w_j + \eta_t r_i x_{ij} - \eta_t\alpha\,\sgn(w_j)$. It almost never lands exactly on zero. A weight
near zero is pushed across it, then pushed back, and jitters around zero indefinitely.

**Worked example.** Take $w_j = 0.004$, a data term $\eta_t r_i x_{ij} = 0$, and $\eta_t\alpha = 0.01$. The
subgradient step gives $w_j = 0.004 - 0.01 = -0.006$. The next step pushes it to $+0.004$, and so on: it never
settles at zero.

The fix is to **clip at zero**: apply the penalty step, but if it would cross zero, stop at zero. That is exactly the
<NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">soft-thresholding operator</NoteLink>
$S(v, \eta_t\alpha) = \sgn(v)\max(\abs{v} - \eta_t\alpha, 0)$ applied after the data step. It is the proximal operator
of the <NoteLink to="vector-norms">L1 norm</NoteLink>, the same operation that gives the lasso its zeros in coordinate
descent. In the example, the clipped step gives $w_j = 0$.

Clipping each step on its own still misses many zeros, because a single noisy sample can push a weight away from zero
again before the penalty has had a chance to act. The **cumulative penalty** method keeps track of the total L1
penalty $u_t = \alpha\sum_{s \le t}\eta_s$ that each weight should have received so far, and the penalty $q_j$ it has
actually received. After the data step it clips $w_j$ towards zero by up to $u_t + q_j$, the penalty still owed
<Cite id="tsuruoka2009" />. scikit-learn's `SGDRegressor` uses this method for its L1 and elastic-net penalties.

For the elastic net, the update applies the decay $1 - \eta_t\alpha(1 - \rho)$ from the L2 part and then the clipped
L1 step with strength $\alpha\rho$.

### Step sizes

For SGD to converge to the minimiser, the step sizes must satisfy the
<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">Robbins–Monro conditions</NoteLink> $\sum_t \eta_t = \infty$ and
$\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty$ <Cite id="robbins1951" />. The first lets the iterates travel any distance; the second makes
the noise from single samples die out. scikit-learn offers several schedules.

- **invscaling** (the default for `SGDRegressor`): $\eta_t = \eta_0/t^{p}$ with $\eta_0 = 0.01$ and $p = 0.25$. Since
  $\sum_t t^{-0.5}$ diverges, $\sum_t \eta_t^2 = \infty$, so the noise never fully dies out and the iterates keep
  fluctuating near the minimiser.
- **optimal**: $\eta_t = 1/(\alpha(t + t_0))$, which satisfies both conditions and is the rate theory recommends for
  strongly convex problems <Cite id="bottou2010" />.
- **constant**: $\eta_t = \eta_0$. The iterates never settle; they wander in a neighbourhood of the minimiser whose
  size grows with $\eta_0$.
- **adaptive**: constant until the loss stops improving, then divided by five.

**Averaging** reduces the fluctuation without shrinking the step. With `average=True` the reported weights are the
average of all iterates, which converges at the optimal rate even with larger steps <Cite id="polyak1992" />.

### Scale

A single step size serves every coefficient, so features on different scales need very different steps: a feature
measured in large units takes huge steps and diverges, one in small units barely moves. Standardise the features
before SGD. Even then, convergence slows as the
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> of $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ grows, because the loss surface is
steep in some directions and nearly flat in others.

## SGD against the exact solutions

The figure fits the standardised diabetes data (ten features, 442 patients) with `SGDRegressor` at $\alpha = 1$, one
point per epoch, for 60 epochs. The exact minimisers of the same objectives are 1923 (ridge), 1534 (lasso) and 1779
(elastic net with $\rho = 0.5$).

- With the default invscaling schedule, the objective gaps after 60 epochs are 0.60, 0.18 and 0.42: about $10^{-4}$ of
  the objective. The weights are within about 0.3 of the exact values, which are of order 10.
- With a constant step of 0.01, the lasso's gap stays near 19 from the tenth epoch on: the iterates wander without
  converging.
- scikit-learn's cumulative-penalty lasso finds exactly the exact solution's zero set, age, s2 and s4, but only from
  epoch 56. Plain subgradient SGD with the same schedule reaches a similar objective gap, 0.56, yet has no exact zeros
  at all after 60 epochs; its two smallest weights are 0.04 and 0.06 in absolute value.

## Cost

| | Exact solvers | SGD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Algorithm | Closed form (Cholesky, <Gloss name="qr" />, <Gloss name="svd" />) for <Gloss name="ols" /> and ridge; coordinate descent or <NoteLink to="least-angle-regression">LARS</NoteLink> for L1 penalties | One sample per update |
| Time | $O(nd^2 + d^3)$ for the closed form; $O(nd)$ per coordinate-descent pass, repeated to convergence | $O(d)$ per update, $O(nd)$ per epoch; sparse features cost only their nonzeros |
| Memory | The data, or $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ ($O(d^2)$) | $O(d)$ plus one sample |
| Accuracy | To machine precision | Close to the minimiser, with residual fluctuation set by the schedule |
| Sparsity | Exact zeros from the L1 penalty | Exact zeros only with truncation, and they can take many epochs to appear |
| Streaming | Refit from scratch | `partial_fit` updates the model batch by batch |

## Trade-offs

- **Use an exact solver when the data fit in memory.** On data sets of thousands to a few million rows, `Ridge`,
  `Lasso` and `ElasticNet` are fast, exact and have fewer settings to tune.
- **Use SGD at scale or online.** For very large or streaming data, or high-dimensional sparse features such as text,
  SGD is often the only practical option, and its approximation error is small next to the statistical error.
- **Tuning cost.** SGD adds a schedule, an initial step size, a number of epochs and a tolerance to the model's own
  $\alpha$ and $\rho$. The exact solvers need only the model's parameters.
- **Selection.** If exact zeros matter, as in <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink>, prefer an
  exact L1 solver, or run SGD long enough and check that the zero set has stopped changing.

## Pitfalls

- **Unscaled features.** Without standardisation, SGD diverges or stalls. Scaling is not optional here, as it can be
  for OLS.
- **Reading an unconverged fit.** After a fixed number of epochs the weights still fluctuate. Compare against an exact
  solver on a subsample, watch the training objective, or use averaging.
- **Mismatched penalties.** `SGDRegressor(alpha=a, penalty="l2")` corresponds to `Ridge(alpha=n * a)`, not
  `Ridge(alpha=a)`. The lasso and elastic-net scalings do match.
- **Shuffling.** Samples sorted by time or label make consecutive updates correlated and the path biased. Shuffle each
  epoch, which `SGDRegressor` does by default.
