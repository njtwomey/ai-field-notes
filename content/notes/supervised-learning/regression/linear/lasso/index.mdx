<Definition>
The lasso fits $\yvec \approx \Xmat\wvec$ by minimising

$$
L(\wvec) = \frac{1}{2n}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \alpha\norm{\wvec}_1, \qquad \norm{\wvec}_1 = \sum_{j=1}^d \abs{w_j},
$$

where $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times d}$ holds standardised features, $\yvec \in \reals^n$ the centred targets and
$\alpha \ge 0$ sets the penalty's strength <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />. For $\alpha$ above a threshold every
coefficient is exactly zero; as $\alpha$ falls, coefficients become nonzero one at a time.
</Definition>

## Why the L1 penalty gives zeros

The penalised problem has an equivalent constrained form: minimise $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ subject to
$\norm{\wvec}_1 \le t$, where each $\alpha$ corresponds to some budget $t$. The contours of the squared error are
ellipses centred on the <Gloss name="ols" /> solution. The solution is the point where the smallest such ellipse touches
the set $\set{\wvec : \norm{\wvec}_1 \le t}$.

That set is a diamond (in higher dimensions a cross-polytope) with its corners on the coordinate axes. A corner is a
point where some coordinates are exactly zero. An ellipse approaching the diamond from outside touches a corner for a
whole region of OLS positions, not just for special ones. The ridge constraint $\norm{\wvec}_2 \le t$ is a disc with
no corners: its contact point lies on an axis only when the OLS solution itself does <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

## Optimality conditions

$L$ is convex but not differentiable where a coefficient is zero, because $\abs{w}$ has a kink there. Its
**subdifferential** at 0 is the interval $[-1, 1]$: every slope between the two one-sided
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink>. A point minimises a <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex
function</NoteLink> exactly when zero lies in the subdifferential. The <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial
derivative</NoteLink> of the squared-error term with respect to $w_j$ is $-\frac{1}{n}\xvec_j^\top(\yvec - \Xmat\wvec)$,
where $\xvec_j$ is column $j$ of $\Xmat$. So $\hat\wvec$ is a lasso solution exactly when, for every $j$,

$$
\frac{1}{n}\xvec_j^\top(\yvec - \Xmat\hat\wvec) =
\begin{cases} \alpha\,\sgn(\hat w_j) & \text{if } \hat w_j \neq 0, \\ c_j \text{ with } \abs{c_j} \le \alpha & \text{if } \hat w_j = 0. \end{cases}
$$

The left side is the correlation between feature $j$ and the current residual. A feature is in the model only if its
correlation with the residual reaches $\alpha$ in absolute value; every active feature has exactly that correlation.
At $\wvec = \zeros$ the residual is $\yvec$, so all coefficients are zero precisely when
$\alpha \ge \alpha_{\max} = \max_j \abs{\xvec_j^\top\yvec}/n$.

## Soft thresholding

Take a single coefficient with the others held fixed. Let $\rvec_j = \yvec - \sum_{k \neq j}\xvec_k w_k$ be the
partial residual. A standardised column has $\norm{\xvec_j}^2 = n$, so the terms of $L$ that involve $w_j$ are

$$
\frac{1}{2n}\norm{\rvec_j - \xvec_j w_j}^2 + \alpha\abs{w_j}
= \tfrac{1}{2}w_j^2 - z_j w_j + \alpha\abs{w_j} + \text{const}, \qquad z_j = \frac{\xvec_j^\top\rvec_j}{n}.
$$

Zero must lie in the subdifferential $w_j - z_j + \alpha\,\partial\abs{w_j}$.

- If $w_j > 0$: $w_j = z_j - \alpha$, which is consistent when $z_j > \alpha$.
- If $w_j < 0$: $w_j = z_j + \alpha$, consistent when $z_j < -\alpha$.
- If $w_j = 0$: we need $z_j \in [-\alpha, \alpha]$.

The three cases combine into the **soft-thresholding** operator

$$
w_j = S(z_j, \alpha) = \sgn(z_j)\,\max(\abs{z_j} - \alpha,\ 0).
$$

$z_j$ is what OLS would set $w_j$ to given the other coefficients. The lasso moves it towards zero by $\alpha$ and stops
at zero. For comparison, the same one-coefficient problem with a ridge penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}w_j^2$ gives
$w_j = z_j/(1 + \lambda)$: a proportional shrink that never reaches zero.

**Worked example.** With $\alpha = 1$: $z_j = 3$ gives $w_j = 2$, $z_j = -1.5$ gives $w_j = -0.5$, and any
$z_j \in [-1, 1]$ gives exactly $w_j = 0$. When the columns of $\Xmat$ are orthogonal, the partial residuals do not
depend on the other coefficients, and the whole lasso solution is $S$ applied to each OLS coefficient.

## Coordinate descent

For correlated features there is no closed form. **<NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">Cyclic coordinate
descent</NoteLink>** updates one coefficient at a time with $w_j \leftarrow S(z_j, \alpha)$, cycling until the
coefficients stop changing <Cite id="friedman2010" />. Each update exactly minimises $L$ in its coordinate, so $L$ never
increases. Because the non-smooth part of $L$ is a sum of functions of single coordinates, coordinate descent converges
to the global minimum.

A full cycle costs $O(nd)$. Keeping the residual $\yvec - \Xmat\wvec$ up to date makes each update $O(n)$, and
coefficients at zero usually stay there, so later cycles touch only the
<NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">active set</NoteLink>. Solving for a decreasing grid of $\alpha$ values,
each warm-started from the previous solution, gives the whole path at little more than the cost of one fit. This is how
glmnet and scikit-learn's `Lasso` work.

## The lasso path on the diabetes data

On the diabetes data, with ten standardised features and 442 patients, $\alpha_{\max} = 45.2$. As $\alpha$ falls,
bmi enters first, then s5 at $\alpha = 42.3$, bp at 21.5 and s3 at 15.0. At $\alpha = 1$ seven coefficients are
nonzero and age, s2 and s4 are exactly zero. The training $R^2$ is 0.513, against 0.518 for OLS, and the 5-fold
cross-validated $R^2$ is 0.482 for both. The lasso reaches the same predictive accuracy with three fewer features.

Between the values of $\alpha$ where a feature enters or leaves, the active coefficients are linear in $\alpha$: on
that stretch the optimality conditions are linear equations in $\wvec$ and $\alpha$. The whole path is therefore
piecewise linear, which <NoteLink to="least-angle-regression">least-angle regression</NoteLink> exploits to compute it
exactly <Cite id="efron2004" />.

## Bayesian view

Give each coefficient an independent <NoteLink to="laplace-distribution">Laplace</NoteLink> prior with density
$p(w_j) \propto e^{-\abs{w_j}/b}$, and let the noise be Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2$. The negative log posterior
is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \frac{1}{b}\norm{\wvec}_1$ plus a constant. Dividing by
$n/\sigma^2$ gives the lasso objective with $\alpha = \sigma^2/(nb)$, so the lasso estimate is the posterior mode. The
posterior mean under the same prior is never exactly zero: the sparsity belongs to the mode, not to the posterior.

## Pitfalls

- **Standardise first.** As with ridge, the penalty depends on each feature's units. A feature measured in large units
  gets a small coefficient and is penalised less.
- **Correlated features.** From a group of correlated features, the lasso tends to keep one and zero the rest, and
  which one it keeps can change with small changes in the data. On the diabetes data it keeps s1 from
  $\alpha = 3.2$ but holds its correlate s2 at zero until $\alpha = 0.25$. The
  <NoteLink to="elastic-net">elastic net</NoteLink> adds a ridge term to share weight within such groups.
- **At most n features.** When $d > n$, the lasso selects at most $n$ features before it saturates.
- **Biased coefficients.** Every active coefficient is shrunk by $\alpha$, so large effects are underestimated.
  Refitting OLS on the selected features (the relaxed lasso) removes the shrinkage but not the selection's
  uncertainty.
- **Selection is not inference.** A feature that the lasso zeroes is not shown to have no effect, and
  <NoteLink to="p-value">p-values</NoteLink> from a model refitted after selection are too small.
