<Definition>
Linear regression predicts a real-valued target $y$ from a feature vector $\xvec \in \reals^d$ with
$\hat{y} = \wvec^\top \xvec + b$. The weights $\wvec$ and bias $b$ are fitted to data. Ordinary least
squares (OLS) chooses them to minimise the mean squared error.
</Definition>

## Model

The model is linear in its parameters. It is not required to be linear in the raw inputs. Features can be
transformed first, for example into polynomials, and the model stays linear.

Given $n$ samples, stack the features into a design matrix $X \in \reals^{n \times d}$ and the targets into
$\yvec \in \reals^n$. Absorb the bias by adding a column of ones to $X$.

## Objective

OLS minimises the mean squared error (MSE):

$$
L(\wvec) = \frac{1}{n} \norm{X\wvec - \yvec}^2
$$

MSE penalises large errors more than small ones. A single outlier can therefore move the fit a long way.

## Closed-form solution

Setting the gradient of $L$ to zero gives the normal equations:

$$
X^\top X \, \wvec = X^\top \yvec
$$

If $X^\top X$ is invertible, the solution is unique: $\wvec^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \yvec$
<Cite id="hastie2009" />. In code, solve the system with a least-squares routine. Do not form the inverse. The
inverse is slower and less numerically stable.

<Derivation title="Deriving the normal equations">
Expand the loss: $nL = \wvec^\top X^\top X \wvec - 2\wvec^\top X^\top \yvec + \yvec^\top\yvec$.

The gradient is $\nabla_\wvec(nL) = 2X^\top X\wvec - 2X^\top\yvec$.

Setting it to zero gives $X^\top X\wvec = X^\top\yvec$. The Hessian $2X^\top X$ is positive semi-definite,
so this stationary point is a minimum.
</Derivation>

## Gradient descent

The same minimum can be reached iteratively with <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. The
update is $\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta \tfrac{2}{n} X^\top (X\wvec - \yvec)$. Iteration is
preferred when $d$ is large, because the closed form costs $O(nd^2 + d^3)$.

## Probabilistic view

Assume $y = \wvec^\top\xvec + \varepsilon$ with Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$.
The maximum-likelihood estimate of $\wvec$ is then exactly the OLS solution <Cite id="bishop2006" />. This
view explains when MSE is the right loss: when errors are Gaussian and have constant variance.

## Assumptions

- The relationship between features and target is linear in the parameters.
- Errors have zero mean and constant variance.
- Errors are independent of each other.
- No feature is an exact linear combination of the others. Otherwise $X^\top X$ is singular.

<Callout variant="warning">
$R^2$ measures the fraction of target variance the model explains. A high $R^2$ does not show that the model is
correct. A curved relationship can still produce a high $R^2$ with a straight line.
</Callout>
