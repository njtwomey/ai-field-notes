<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ has a Gaussian distribution with mean $\mu \in \reals$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, written
$X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if its density is

$$
p(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\paren{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad x \in \reals.
$$

The standard normal distribution is $\Gauss(0, 1)$. Its density is written $\phi$ and its cdf $\Phi$.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Mean $\mu \in \reals$, <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma > 0$ |
| Support | $x \in \reals$ |
| pdf | $\frac{1}{\sigma}\phi\paren{\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}}$ |
| cdf | $\Phi\paren{\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}}$, no closed form |
| Mean, median, mode | $\mu$ |
| Variance | $\sigma^2$ |

## Where it comes from

The <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> makes the Gaussian the limit of sums. Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically
distributed</Gloss> with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Then the standardised sum
$\sqrt{n}\,(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma$ converges in distribution to $\Gauss(0, 1)$ as $n \to \infty$
<Cite id="wasserman2004" />. A quantity that is the sum of many small independent effects, such as a measurement error,
is therefore approximately Gaussian.

<Derivation title="Why sums tend to the Gaussian">
A sketch through <Gloss name="mgf">moment generating functions</Gloss>, assuming they exist near 0. Let $Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, with mean 0
and variance 1, and $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i/\sqrt n$. Independence gives $M_{S_n}(t) = M_Y(t/\sqrt n)^n$. Expanding
$\log M_Y(s) = \tfrac12 s^2 + O(s^3)$ around 0, because the mean is 0 and the variance 1,

$$
\log M_{S_n}(t) = n \log M_Y\paren{\frac{t}{\sqrt n}} = n\paren{\frac{t^2}{2n} + O\paren{n^{-3/2}}} \to \frac{t^2}{2}.
$$

The limit $e^{t^2/2}$ is the moment generating function of $\Gauss(0, 1)$. Convergence of moment generating functions
implies <NoteLink to="modes-of-convergence">convergence in distribution</NoteLink>. The general theorem needs only a finite variance and uses characteristic functions
instead.
</Derivation>

The Gaussian is also the maximum-entropy distribution on $\reals$ with a given mean and variance. Among all densities
with variance $\sigma^2$, the Gaussian has the largest <NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>, $\tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \sigma^2)$
<Cite id="bishop2006" />. Assuming a Gaussian adds no information beyond the first two moments.

<Derivation title="Maximum entropy">
Let $q$ be any density with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and $p$ the Gaussian with the same moments. Since
$\log p(x) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - (x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ depends on $x$ only through $(x - \mu)^2$,
$-\int q \log p = \tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \tfrac12 = H(p)$, the same value for every such $q$. Non-negativity of
the KL divergence then gives

$$
0 \le \KL(q \,\|\, p) = \int q \log q - \int q \log p = -H(q) + H(p),
$$

so $H(q) \le H(p)$, with equality only when $q = p$.
</Derivation>

## Properties

**Normalising constant.** The factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ makes $\phi$ integrate to 1. Square the integral and change to
<NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">polar coordinates</NoteLink>, $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, with area element $r\,dr\,d\theta$:

$$
\paren{\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2/2}\, dx}^2 = \int\!\!\int e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta = 2\pi \cdot 1.
$$

So $\int e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi}$. The substitution $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$ turns the general density into $\phi$
and contributes the factor $1/\sigma$.

**Mean and variance.** Substitute $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. The standard normal density is even, so
$\int z\,\phi(z)\,dz = 0$ and $\expect[X] = \mu + \sigma \expect[Z] = \mu$. For the variance, integrate by parts with
$\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)$:

$$
\expect[Z^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z \cdot z\phi(z)\, dz = \Big[-z\phi(z)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} +
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(z)\, dz = 0 + 1.
$$

So $\var(X) = \sigma^2 \var(Z) = \sigma^2$.

**Tails.** The tails fall faster than any exponential. For large $z$, $1 - \Phi(z) \approx \phi(z)/z$ (Mills' ratio).
About 68.3% of the mass lies within one standard deviation of the mean, 95.4% within two and 99.7% within three.

For example, if adult heights are $\Gauss(170, 10^2)$ in centimetres, the fraction taller than 190 cm is
$P(Z > (190 - 170)/10) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 0.023$, about one person in 44.

**<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">Linear maps</NoteLink>.** If $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $aX + b \sim \Gauss(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)$ for $a \ne 0$: the
density of $Y = aX + b$ is $p_X\paren{(y - b)/a}/\abs{a}$, which is the Gaussian density with those parameters.
Standardising, $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, reduces every Gaussian probability to $\Phi$.

**Closure under sums.** If $X_1 \sim \Gauss(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_2 \sim \Gauss(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are
independent, then $X_1 + X_2 \sim \Gauss(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. Means add and variances add.

**Quadratic log-density.** $\log p(x) = -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}$. Maximising a Gaussian likelihood
is minimising a sum of squares.

<Derivation title="Closure under sums">
The moment generating function of $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is $M(t) = \exp(\mu t + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 t^2)$. For the
standard normal, <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">complete the square</NoteLink>: $tz - z^2/2 = t^2/2 - (z - t)^2/2$, so
$\expect[e^{tZ}] = e^{t^2/2}\int \phi(z - t)\, dz = e^{t^2/2}$, and $X = \mu + \sigma Z$ gives the general form. For
independent variables the moment generating function of the sum is the product:

$$
M_{X_1 + X_2}(t) = \exp\paren{(\mu_1 + \mu_2)t + \tfrac12 (\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2) t^2},
$$

which is the moment generating function of $\Gauss(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. Moment generating functions
that exist near $t = 0$ determine the distribution.
</Derivation>

## Relations to other distributions

- **Multivariate normal.** The Gaussian is the one-dimensional case of the
  <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal distribution</NoteLink>.
- **χ².** If $Z_1, \dots, Z_k$ are independent standard normals, $\sum_i Z_i^2 \sim \ChiSq_k$, the
  <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">χ² distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Student t.** $Z / \sqrt{V/\nu}$ with $V \sim \ChiSq_\nu$ independent of $Z$ is
  <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">$\StudentT_\nu$</NoteLink>. As $\nu \to \infty$,
  $\StudentT_\nu \to \Gauss(0, 1)$.
- **Log-normal.** If $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $e^X$ is
  <NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal</NoteLink>.
- **Uniform.** Two independent uniforms give two independent standard normals through the
  <NoteLink to="box-muller-transform">Box–Muller transform</NoteLink>.
- **Limits.** For large parameters, the binomial, <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution">Poisson</NoteLink>, gamma and χ²
  distributions are approximately Gaussian.

## In machine learning

- **Squared error.** <NoteLink to="linear-regression">Linear regression</NoteLink> with Gaussian noise has a
  log-likelihood equal to minus the sum of squared residuals, up to constants. Least squares is maximum likelihood under
  that noise model.
- **Initialisation and noise.** Network weights are commonly initialised from a Gaussian, and <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss> add
  Gaussian noise step by step.
- **Latent variables.** <Gloss name="vae">Variational autoencoders</Gloss> use a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian approximate posterior over latent
  codes.

## Pitfalls

- **Outliers.** The light tails make a Gaussian likelihood sensitive to outliers: one point far from the mean dominates
  a sum of squares. Heavier-tailed models such as the
  <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t distribution</NoteLink> or the
  <NoteLink to="laplace-distribution">Laplace distribution</NoteLink> are more robust.
- **Bounded or skewed data.** Counts, proportions and positive quantities often violate the symmetric, unbounded
  support. Transform the data or use a distribution with the right support.
- **Parameterisation.** Some texts and libraries write $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with the variance, others with the
  standard deviation. NumPy's `normal(loc, scale)` takes $\sigma$.
