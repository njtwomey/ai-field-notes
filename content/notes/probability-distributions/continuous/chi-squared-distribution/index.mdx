<Definition>
Let $Z_1, \dots, Z_k$ be independent standard normal variables. The <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink>

$$
Q = \sum_{i=1}^{k} Z_i^2
$$

has a χ² distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom, written $Q \sim \ChiSq_k$. Its density is

$$
p(q \mid k) = \frac{1}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)}\, q^{k/2 - 1} e^{-q/2}, \qquad q > 0.
$$
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameter | Degrees of freedom $k > 0$ (an integer in the definition, any positive real in the density) |
| Support | $q > 0$ |
| pdf | See the definition |
| cdf | $P(k/2, q/2)$, the regularised lower incomplete <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma function</NoteLink> |
| Mean | $k$ |
| Variance | $2k$ |
| Mode | $\max(k - 2, 0)$ |

## Where it comes from

The squared length of a standard Gaussian vector in $k$ dimensions is $\ChiSq_k$.

The density follows in two steps. First, one square. For $q > 0$, $P(Z^2 \le q) = P(-\sqrt q \le Z \le \sqrt q)
= 2\Phi(\sqrt q) - 1$. Differentiating with the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>,

$$
p_{Z^2}(q) = 2\phi(\sqrt q) \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt q} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, q^{-1/2} e^{-q/2},
$$

which is the $\GammaD(1/2, 1/2)$ density, since $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt\pi$. Second, the sum. Independent gamma variables
with a common rate add their shapes (see <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distribution</NoteLink>), so a sum of
$k$ independent squares is $\GammaD(k/2, 1/2)$. Writing out that density gives the one in the definition.

The most important case is the sample variance. For $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with sample variance
$S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2$,

$$
\frac{(n - 1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \ChiSq_{n-1},
$$

and $S^2$ is independent of $\bar X$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />. One degree of freedom is lost because the deviations
are measured from $\bar X$, which is estimated from the same data. This result is what makes the
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of a Gaussian mean, and the t-statistic built from it, exact
rather than approximate.

For example, with $n = 10$ observations from a Gaussian with $\sigma^2 = 4$, the probability that the sample variance
exceeds 6 is $P\paren{\ChiSq_9 > 9 \times 6/4} = P(\ChiSq_9 > 13.5) \approx 0.14$. Small samples give sample variances
far from $\sigma^2$.

<Derivation title="Why the sample variance is χ² with n − 1 degrees of freedom">
Standardise, $Z_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, so $\zvec \sim \MvGauss(\zeros, \Imat_n)$. Choose an orthogonal matrix $\Qmat$
whose first row is $\ones^\top/\sqrt n$, and let $\wvec = \Qmat\zvec$. An orthogonal map preserves the standard
Gaussian, so the $W_i$ are independent standard normals. The first is $W_1 = \sqrt n\, \bar Z$. Lengths are preserved,
so

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n (Z_i - \bar Z)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 - n\bar Z^2 = \norm{\wvec}^2 - W_1^2 = \sum_{i=2}^n W_i^2.
$$

The right side is a sum of $n - 1$ independent squared standard normals, so it is $\ChiSq_{n-1}$. It does not involve
$W_1$, so it is independent of $\bar Z$, and hence $S^2$ is independent of $\bar X$. Multiplying by $\sigma^2$ gives
$(n - 1)S^2/\sigma^2 = \sum_i (Z_i - \bar Z)^2 \sim \ChiSq_{n-1}$.
</Derivation>

Karl Pearson introduced the distribution for his goodness-of-fit test on counts <Cite id="pearson1900" />.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** For a standard normal $Z$, $\expect[Z^2] = 1$. Integrating by parts with $\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)$,
$\expect[Z^4] = \int z^3 \cdot z\phi(z)\, dz = 3\int z^2\phi(z)\, dz = 3$, so $\var(Z^2) = 3 - 1 = 2$. The terms of $Q$
are independent, so $\expect[Q] = k \cdot 1 = k$ and $\var(Q) = k \cdot 2 = 2k$.

**A gamma distribution.** $\ChiSq_k = \GammaD(k/2, 1/2)$ in the shape–rate parameterisation. Every gamma property
carries over.

**Additivity.** If $Q_1 \sim \ChiSq_{k_1}$ and $Q_2 \sim \ChiSq_{k_2}$ are independent, then
$Q_1 + Q_2 \sim \ChiSq_{k_1 + k_2}$. This follows from the definition: $Q_1 + Q_2$ is a sum of $k_1 + k_2$ independent
squared standard normals.

**Shape.** For $k = 1$ the density is infinite at 0; for $k = 2$ it is $\Exp(1/2)$; for $k \ge 3$ it rises to a mode at
$k - 2$. As $k$ grows, $(Q - k)/\sqrt{2k}$ tends to $\Gauss(0, 1)$.


## Relations to other distributions

- **Gamma.** $\ChiSq_k = \GammaD(k/2, 1/2)$. See the <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Exponential.** $\ChiSq_2 = $ <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">$\Exp(1/2)$</NoteLink>.
- **Student t.** $Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$ with $V \sim \ChiSq_\nu$ independent of $Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ is
  <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">$\StudentT_\nu$</NoteLink>.
- **F.** $(V_1/d_1)/(V_2/d_2)$ with independent $V_i \sim \ChiSq_{d_i}$ is
  <NoteLink to="f-distribution">$\FDist(d_1, d_2)$</NoteLink>.
- **Multivariate normal.** If $\xvec \sim \MvGauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$ in $d$ dimensions, the squared Mahalanobis
  distance $(\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)$ is $\ChiSq_d$. Whitening explains it: with
  $\zvec = \Lmat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)$ for $\Sigmamat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$, $\zvec$ is standard normal and the distance is
  $\norm{\zvec}^2$ (see <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal distribution</NoteLink>).

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma">Likelihood-ratio tests</NoteLink>.** By Wilks' theorem, twice the log-likelihood ratio between nested models is approximately
  $\ChiSq_r$ under the null, where $r$ is the number of extra parameters <Cite id="wasserman2004" />. Deviance tests for
  <Gloss name="glm">generalised linear models</Gloss> use this.
- **<NoteLink to="feature-selection">Feature selection</NoteLink> and contingency tables.** The χ² test of <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> scores categorical features against a label,
  as in scikit-learn's `chi2` selector.
- **Outlier detection.** A point whose squared Mahalanobis distance exceeds a high $\ChiSq_d$ quantile is unusual under
  a fitted Gaussian.

## Pitfalls

- **Degrees of freedom.** Each estimated parameter removes one degree of freedom. Pearson's test on $m$ cells with $p$
  fitted parameters uses $m - 1 - p$, not $m$.
- **Approximations need counts.** Pearson's χ² statistic follows $\ChiSq$ only approximately. The approximation is poor
  when expected cell counts are small; a common rule asks for at least 5 per cell.
