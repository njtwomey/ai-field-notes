<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ has a Cauchy distribution with location $x_0 \in \reals$ and scale $\gamma > 0$, written
$X \sim \Cauchy(x_0, \gamma)$, if its density is

$$
p(x \mid x_0, \gamma) = \frac{1}{\pi\gamma\paren{1 + \paren{\frac{x - x_0}{\gamma}}^2}}, \qquad x \in \reals.
$$
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Location $x_0 \in \reals$, scale $\gamma > 0$ |
| Support | $x \in \reals$ |
| pdf | See the definition |
| cdf | $\tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{\pi}\arctan\paren{(x - x_0)/\gamma}$ |
| Quantile | $x_0 + \gamma \tan\paren{\pi(u - \tfrac12)}$ |
| Median, mode | $x_0$ |
| Mean, variance | Undefined |
| Interquartile range | $2\gamma$ |

## Where it comes from

**Ratio of Gaussians.** If $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent standard normals, then $Z_1/Z_2 \sim \Cauchy(0, 1)$
<Cite id="johnson1994" />. Dividing by a quantity that is often near zero produces the heavy tails.

**Random angles.** Shine a light from the point $(x_0, \gamma)$ towards the horizontal axis at a uniformly random angle
$\Theta \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ from the vertical. The point where the ray hits the axis is $X = x_0 + \gamma\tan\Theta$.
$\Theta$ has density $1/\pi$, and the map is increasing with
$dx/d\theta = \gamma\sec^2\theta = \gamma\paren{1 + \tan^2\theta}$. The change-of-variables formula gives

$$
p(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{1}{\pi\gamma\paren{1 + \paren{\frac{x - x_0}{\gamma}}^2}},
$$

the Cauchy density. Integrating it gives the cdf $\tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi\arctan\paren{(x - x_0)/\gamma}$, and inverting
the cdf gives the quantile function. Sampling a uniform $U$ and returning $x_0 + \gamma\tan\paren{\pi(U - \tfrac12)}$ is
therefore <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">inverse-transform sampling</NoteLink>. The quartiles are at
$u = 1/4$ and $3/4$, where $\tan(\mp\pi/4) = \mp 1$, so the interquartile range is $2\gamma$.

<Derivation title="The ratio of two Gaussians">
The pair $(Z_2, Z_1)$ has density $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$, which depends only on the distance from the
origin. Its angle $\Theta$ is therefore uniform on $(-\pi, \pi]$. The ratio $Z_1/Z_2 = \tan\Theta$. The tangent has
period $\pi$, so $\tan\Theta$ has the same distribution as the tangent of a uniform angle on $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, which is
$\Cauchy(0, 1)$ by the argument above.
</Derivation>

## Properties

**No mean.** $\expect\abs{X} = \infty$, because the density decays like $1/x^2$ and $\int \abs{x}/x^2\, dx$ diverges.
For the standard Cauchy,

$$
\int_0^R x \cdot \frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\, dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}\log(1 + R^2) \to \infty \quad (R \to \infty).
$$

Both halves of $\int x\, p(x)\, dx$ diverge, one to $+\infty$ and one to $-\infty$, so the integral is undefined rather
than zero. The symmetric principal value is 0, but a mean requires $\expect\abs{X} < \infty$. The mean does not exist,
and neither does any moment of order 1 or more.

**Stability.** If $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \Cauchy(x_0, \gamma)$ are independent, their average $\bar X_n$ is again
$\Cauchy(x_0, \gamma)$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />. The sample mean of a million Cauchy draws is exactly as spread out
as one draw. The <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss> and the <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> do not apply, because their assumptions of a finite
mean and variance fail.

**Robust summaries.** The median and interquartile range exist and estimate $x_0$ and $2\gamma$. The sample median is a
consistent estimator of $x_0$.

<Derivation title="Stability of the average">
The <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">characteristic function</NoteLink> of $\Cauchy(x_0, \gamma)$ is $\varphi(t) = \exp(i x_0 t - \gamma\abs{t})$. For the standard
case it follows from the Laplace density: $\int \tfrac12 e^{-\abs{x}}e^{itx}\,dx = 1/(1 + t^2)$, and Fourier inversion
turns this around to $\int \frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}e^{itx}\,dx = e^{-\abs{t}}$. Location and scale give the general form.
For the average of $n$ independent copies,

$$
\varphi_{\bar X_n}(t) = \varphi(t/n)^n = \exp\paren{i x_0 t - \gamma\abs{t}},
$$

which is the characteristic function of $\Cauchy(x_0, \gamma)$.
</Derivation>

## Relations to other distributions

- **Student t.** $\Cauchy(0, 1) = \StudentT_1$. See the <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t
  distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Gaussian.** The ratio of two independent standard Gaussians. The Cauchy and the Gaussian are both stable
  distributions; the Gaussian is the only stable law with finite variance.
- **Uniform.** $x_0 + \gamma\tan\paren{\pi(U - \tfrac12)}$ with $U \sim \Unif(0, 1)$.

## In machine learning

- **<Gloss name="t-sne" />.** The similarity kernel in the embedding space, $(1 + \norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}^2)^{-1}$, is a Cauchy density
  up to scale <Cite id="vandermaaten2008" />.
- **Heavy-tailed priors.** A half-Cauchy prior on a scale parameter puts most of its mass near zero while allowing large
  values, which makes it a common <NoteLink to="prior-choice">weakly informative prior</NoteLink> in <NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">hierarchical models</NoteLink> <Cite id="gelman2013" />.
- **Stress tests.** Cauchy noise is a standard test of whether an estimator or algorithm relies on finite moments.

## Pitfalls

- **Averaging does not help.** Monitoring the running mean of Cauchy-like data, such as some ratios of noisy quantities,
  shows sudden jumps that never die out. Use medians and quantiles.
- **The width parameter is not a <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>.** $\gamma$ is the half-width at half-maximum and half the
  interquartile range.
