<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ has a Laplace distribution with location $\mu \in \reals$ and scale $b > 0$, written
$X \sim \Laplace(\mu, b)$, if its density is

$$
p(x \mid \mu, b) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\paren{-\frac{\abs{x - \mu}}{b}}, \qquad x \in \reals.
$$
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Location $\mu \in \reals$, scale $b > 0$ |
| Support | $x \in \reals$ |
| pdf | $e^{-\abs{x - \mu}/b} / (2b)$ |
| cdf | $\tfrac12 e^{(x - \mu)/b}$ for $x < \mu$; $1 - \tfrac12 e^{-(x - \mu)/b}$ for $x \ge \mu$ |
| Quantile | $\mu - b\,\sgn(u - \tfrac12) \log(1 - 2\abs{u - \tfrac12})$ |
| Mean, median, mode | $\mu$ |
| Variance | $2b^2$ |

## Where it comes from

The Laplace distribution is two exponential distributions placed back to back. Each side of $\mu$ holds half the mass,
and the distance $\abs{X - \mu}$ is $\Exp(1/b)$ with mean $b$. Equivalently, if $E_1$ and $E_2$ are independent
$\Exp(1/b)$ variables, then $\mu + E_1 - E_2 \sim \Laplace(\mu, b)$ <Cite id="johnson1995" />. For
$d = E_1 - E_2 \ge 0$, convolving the two densities gives

$$
p(d) = \int_0^\infty \frac1b e^{-(d + y)/b}\cdot\frac1b e^{-y/b}\, dy = \frac{e^{-d/b}}{b^2}\int_0^\infty e^{-2y/b}\, dy
= \frac{1}{2b}e^{-d/b},
$$

and by symmetry $p(d) = e^{-\abs d/b}/(2b)$ for all $d$.

Pierre-Simon Laplace introduced it in 1774 as a law of errors, before the Gaussian took that role
<Cite id="johnson1995" />.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** The density is symmetric about $\mu$, so $\expect[X] = \mu$. The deviation $D = \abs{X - \mu}$ is
exponential with mean $b$, so $\expect[D^2] = \var(D) + \expect[D]^2 = b^2 + b^2 = 2b^2$. Since $(X - \mu)^2 = D^2$,
$\var(X) = \expect[(X - \mu)^2] = 2b^2$.

**Cusp at the mode.** The density has a corner at $\mu$: its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> jumps from $1/(2b^2)$ to $-1/(2b^2)$. The
distribution puts more mass very close to $\mu$ than a Gaussian with the same variance.

**Tails.** $P(\abs{X - \mu} > x) = e^{-x/b}$. The tails are heavier than Gaussian tails, which fall like $e^{-x^2}$, and
lighter than the power-law tails of the <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t distribution</NoteLink>. All
moments exist.

**Absolute-error log-density.** $\log p(x) = -\abs{x - \mu}/b + \text{const}$. For independent data $x_1, \dots, x_n$,
the maximum-likelihood estimate of $\mu$ minimises $\sum_i \abs{x_i - \mu}$. Away from the data points, the derivative
of this sum is the number of points below $\mu$ minus the number above. It changes sign where half the points lie on
each side, so the minimiser is the sample median. The Gaussian's squared error gives the sample mean instead.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Exponential.** $\abs{X - \mu} \sim \Exp(1/b)$, and the difference of two independent exponentials is Laplace. See
  the <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Gaussian.** The Gaussian has a squared-error log-density and a smooth peak. The Laplace has an absolute-error
  log-density and a cusp.
- **Scale <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">mixture of Gaussians</NoteLink>.** If $W \sim \Exp(1/(2b^2))$ (mean $2b^2$) and $X \mid W \sim \Gauss(\mu, W)$, then
  $X \sim \Laplace(\mu, b)$.
- **Exponential family.** With $\mu$ known, $\Laplace(\mu, b)$ is an
  <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> with natural parameter $-1/b$ and <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink>
  $\abs{x - \mu}$. With $\mu$ unknown it is not, because $\abs{x - \mu}$ cannot be split into a function of $x$ times a
  function of $\mu$.

<Derivation title="The scale mixture">
Take $\mu = 0$. The <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">characteristic function</NoteLink> of $\Gauss(0, w)$ is $e^{-wt^2/2}$. Averaging over $W \sim \Exp(\lambda)$
with rate $\lambda = 1/(2b^2)$,

$$
\expect\brack{e^{-Wt^2/2}} = \int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda w}e^{-wt^2/2}\, dw = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda + t^2/2}
= \frac{1}{1 + b^2t^2}.
$$

The characteristic function of $\Laplace(0, b)$ is $\int \frac{1}{2b}e^{-\abs{x}/b}e^{itx}\, dx = 1/(1 + b^2t^2)$, the
same.
</Derivation>

## In machine learning

- **The lasso.** <Gloss name="map-estimate">Maximum a posteriori</Gloss> estimation with independent $\Laplace(0, b)$ priors on regression weights and a
  Gaussian likelihood is L1-penalised least squares, the lasso <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />. The cusp of the prior at 0
  is why the lasso sets some weights exactly to zero.
- **Robust regression.** A Laplace likelihood gives least absolute deviations, which resist outliers better than least
  squares <Cite id="murphy2012" />.
- **<Gloss name="differential-privacy">Differential privacy</Gloss>.** The Laplace mechanism adds $\Laplace(0, \Delta/\varepsilon)$ noise to a query with
  sensitivity $\Delta$ to achieve $\varepsilon$-differential privacy <Cite id="dwork2006" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The maximum a posteriori estimate is not the posterior mean.** The lasso estimate is the posterior mode under a Laplace prior. The posterior mean under
  the same prior is not sparse. A Laplace prior does not make sparse weights probable; it makes the mode sparse.
- **Non-smooth objective.** The absolute value has no derivative at 0. <NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> methods need <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradients</NoteLink> or proximal
  steps to optimise Laplace log-likelihoods.
