<Definition>
A random variable $X$ has a beta distribution with shape parameters $\alpha > 0$ and $\beta > 0$, written
$X \sim \Beta(\alpha, \beta)$, if its density is

$$
p(x \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{x^{\alpha - 1}(1 - x)^{\beta - 1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)}, \qquad 0 < x < 1,
$$

where $\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta) = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)$ is the beta function.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Shapes $\alpha > 0$, $\beta > 0$ |
| Support | $x \in (0, 1)$ |
| pdf | $x^{\alpha - 1}(1 - x)^{\beta - 1} / \mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)$ |
| cdf | $I_x(\alpha, \beta)$, the regularised incomplete beta function |
| Mean | $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$ |
| Variance | $\alpha\beta / \paren{(\alpha + \beta)^2(\alpha + \beta + 1)}$ |
| Mode | $(\alpha - 1)/(\alpha + \beta - 2)$ for $\alpha, \beta > 1$ |

## Where it comes from

The beta distribution is the posterior of a coin's bias. Let $\theta$ be the probability of heads, with prior
$\theta \sim \Beta(\alpha, \beta)$. After $h$ heads and $t$ tails, the likelihood is $\theta^h (1 - \theta)^t$. By
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> the posterior is proportional to

$$
\theta^h (1 - \theta)^t \cdot \theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}
= \theta^{\alpha + h - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta + t - 1},
$$

so $\theta \mid \text{data} \sim \Beta(\alpha + h, \beta + t)$ <Cite id="gelman2013" />. The prior and the posterior are
in the same family. $\alpha$ and $\beta$ behave as counts of heads and tails seen before the data, and $\alpha + \beta$
is the prior's weight in flips. Here the coin flips are <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli</NoteLink>
trials, and the same update holds for a <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial</NoteLink> count of heads.

For example, a prior $\Beta(2, 2)$ has mean $1/2$. Seven heads and three tails give the posterior $\Beta(9, 5)$, with
mean $9/14 \approx 0.64$. The raw proportion, $0.7$, is pulled towards $1/2$ by the prior's four imaginary flips.

The beta distribution also arises from order statistics. The $k$-th smallest of $n$ independent $\Unif(0, 1)$ draws
has distribution $\Beta(k, n - k + 1)$ <Cite id="johnson1995" />. For the $k$-th smallest to lie in a short interval
$[x, x + dx]$, one draw must fall in the interval, $k - 1$ below it and $n - k$ above it. There are
$n!/\paren{(k - 1)!\,1!\,(n - k)!}$ ways to assign the draws to these three groups, so the density is

$$
\frac{n!}{(k - 1)!\,(n - k)!}\, x^{k - 1}(1 - x)^{n - k},
$$

which is the $\Beta(k, n - k + 1)$ density. It also shows that
$\mathrm{B}(k, n - k + 1) = (k - 1)!\,(n - k)!/n!$ for integers.

<Derivation title="The beta function in terms of the gamma function">
Multiply two gamma integrals and change variables to $s = x + y$ and $u = x/(x + y)$, with $x = su$, $y = s(1 - u)$ and
Jacobian $s$:

$$
\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta) = \int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha - 1}y^{\beta - 1}e^{-(x + y)}\, dx\, dy
= \int_0^\infty s^{\alpha + \beta - 1}e^{-s}\, ds \int_0^1 u^{\alpha - 1}(1 - u)^{\beta - 1}\, du
= \Gamma(\alpha + \beta)\,\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta).
$$

The same change of variables shows that $u = X/(X + Y)$ is $\Beta(\alpha, \beta)$ when $X \sim \GammaD(\alpha, 1)$ and
$Y \sim \GammaD(\beta, 1)$ are independent: the joint density factorises into a function of $s$ and a function of $u$.
</Derivation>

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** The moments are ratios of beta functions. Using
$\mathrm{B}(\alpha + 1, \beta) = \mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)\,\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$, which follows from
$\Gamma(z + 1) = z\Gamma(z)$,

$$
\expect[X] = \int_0^1 x \frac{x^{\alpha - 1}(1 - x)^{\beta - 1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)}\, dx
= \frac{\mathrm{B}(\alpha + 1, \beta)}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)} = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}, \qquad
\expect[X^2] = \frac{\mathrm{B}(\alpha + 2, \beta)}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)}
= \frac{\alpha(\alpha + 1)}{(\alpha + \beta)(\alpha + \beta + 1)}.
$$

Subtracting $\expect[X]^2$ gives $\var(X) = \alpha\beta/\paren{(\alpha + \beta)^2(\alpha + \beta + 1)}$. The mode is
where the derivative of the log-density, $(\alpha - 1)/x - (\beta - 1)/(1 - x)$, is zero:
$x = (\alpha - 1)/(\alpha + \beta - 2)$.

**Shape.** The two parameters give a wide range of shapes on $[0, 1]$:

- $\alpha = \beta = 1$ is the uniform distribution.
- $\alpha = \beta > 1$ is symmetric and peaked at $1/2$. Larger values concentrate the mass more.
- $\alpha, \beta < 1$ is U-shaped, with mass piled at both ends.
- $\alpha > \beta$ skews the mass towards 1, and $\alpha < \beta$ towards 0.

**Concentration.** Write $m = \alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$ for the mean and $\kappa = \alpha + \beta$ for the
concentration. The variance is $m(1 - m)/(\kappa + 1)$. The mean sets the location and $\kappa$ sets the certainty.

**Symmetry.** If $X \sim \Beta(\alpha, \beta)$, then $1 - X \sim \Beta(\beta, \alpha)$: substituting $y = 1 - x$ swaps
the two factors of the density.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Gamma.** If $X \sim \GammaD(\alpha, 1)$ and $Y \sim \GammaD(\beta, 1)$ are independent
  <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma</NoteLink> variables, then $X/(X + Y) \sim \Beta(\alpha, \beta)$, as the
  derivation above shows. This is how beta variables are usually sampled.
- **Dirichlet.** The <NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution">Dirichlet distribution</NoteLink> generalises the beta to
  probability vectors with $K$ entries. $\Dir(\alpha, \beta)$ on two categories is $\Beta(\alpha, \beta)$ for the first
  entry.
- **Binomial.** A binomial count whose success probability is beta-distributed has the beta-binomial distribution, which
  is overdispersed relative to the binomial.
- **F.** If $X \sim \Beta(d_1/2, d_2/2)$, then $d_2 X / \paren{d_1(1 - X)} \sim \FDist(d_1, d_2)$.

## In machine learning

- **Rates and click-through.** A/B tests and recommender systems model conversion rates with beta posteriors. Thompson
  sampling for Bernoulli bandits draws one sample from each arm's beta posterior and plays the largest
  <Cite id="murphy2012" />.
- **Smoothing.** The posterior mean $(h + \alpha)/(n + \alpha + \beta)$ shrinks raw proportions towards the prior mean.
  With $\alpha = \beta = 1$ it is Laplace's add-one rule.
- **Bounded outputs.** Beta likelihoods model targets that are proportions, such as the fraction of a budget spent.

## Pitfalls

- **Endpoints.** The density is infinite at 0 when $\alpha < 1$ and at 1 when $\beta < 1$. Data exactly at 0 or 1 have
  zero density under every beta distribution and must be handled separately.
- **Prior strength.** $\Beta(\alpha, \beta)$ is worth $\alpha + \beta$ observations. A prior such as $\Beta(50, 50)$
  outweighs a small experiment. The widget shows how many flips it takes to overcome it.
