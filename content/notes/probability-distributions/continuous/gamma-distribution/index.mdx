<Definition>
A random variable $X$ has a gamma distribution with shape $\alpha > 0$ and rate $\beta > 0$, written
$X \sim \GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$, if its density is

$$
p(x \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\, x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x}, \qquad x > 0,
$$

where $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t}\, dt$ is the gamma function. Some texts use the scale
$\theta = 1/\beta$ instead of the rate.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Shape $\alpha > 0$, rate $\beta > 0$ |
| Support | $x > 0$ |
| pdf | $\beta^\alpha x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x} / \Gamma(\alpha)$ |
| cdf | $P(\alpha, \beta x)$, the regularised lower incomplete gamma function |
| Mean | $\alpha/\beta$ |
| Variance | $\alpha/\beta^2$ |
| Mode | $(\alpha - 1)/\beta$ for $\alpha \ge 1$; the density is unbounded at 0 for $\alpha < 1$ |

## Where it comes from

In a Poisson process with rate $\beta$, the gaps between events are independent
<NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">$\Exp(\beta)$</NoteLink>. The waiting time $T_k$ to the $k$-th event is the sum
of $k$ gaps. The $k$-th event has happened by time $x$ exactly when at least $k$ events fall in $[0, x]$, and that count
is $\Poisson(\beta x)$:

$$
P(T_k \le x) = 1 - \sum_{j=0}^{k - 1} e^{-\beta x}\frac{(\beta x)^j}{j!}.
$$

Differentiating in $x$, the sum telescopes and leaves one term:

$$
p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \beta e^{-\beta x}\frac{(\beta x)^j}{j!}
- \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \beta e^{-\beta x}\frac{(\beta x)^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}
= \frac{\beta^k x^{k-1} e^{-\beta x}}{(k - 1)!}.
$$

This is $\GammaD(k, \beta)$, also called the Erlang distribution. The gamma distribution extends the shape $k$ from
integers to any $\alpha > 0$, replacing $(k - 1)!$ by $\Gamma(\alpha)$ <Cite id="johnson1994" />. The constant is the
one that makes the density integrate to 1. Substituting $t = \beta x$,

$$
\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta x}\, dx = \beta^{-\alpha}\int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1}e^{-t}\, dt
= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\beta^\alpha}.
$$

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** The normalising integral above, with $\alpha + r$ in place of $\alpha$, gives every moment:

$$
\expect[X^r] = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^\infty x^{\alpha + r - 1} e^{-\beta x}\, dx
= \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + r)}{\beta^{\alpha + r}}
= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + r)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\, \beta^r}.
$$

With $\Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha\Gamma(\alpha)$, $\expect[X] = \alpha/\beta$ and
$\expect[X^2] = \alpha(\alpha + 1)/\beta^2$, so
$\var(X) = \alpha(\alpha + 1)/\beta^2 - \alpha^2/\beta^2 = \alpha/\beta^2$. For integer $\alpha = k$ these are $k$ times
the exponential's mean $1/\beta$ and variance $1/\beta^2$, as a sum of $k$ independent gaps requires.

**Shape.** For $\alpha < 1$ the density is infinite at 0 and decreasing. For $\alpha = 1$ it is the exponential density.
For $\alpha > 1$ it rises from 0 to a single mode and then decays. The log-density $(\alpha - 1)\log x - \beta x$ has
derivative $(\alpha - 1)/x - \beta$, which is zero at the mode $(\alpha - 1)/\beta$. As $\alpha$ grows, the distribution
approaches $\Gauss(\alpha/\beta, \alpha/\beta^2)$, because it is a sum of many independent pieces.

**Tails.** The right tail behaves like $x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x}$. The exponential factor dominates, so the gamma is
light-tailed.

**Additivity.** If $X_1 \sim \GammaD(\alpha_1, \beta)$ and $X_2 \sim \GammaD(\alpha_2, \beta)$ are independent, then
$X_1 + X_2 \sim \GammaD(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta)$. The rates must be equal. For integer shapes this is the
waiting-time picture: wait for $\alpha_1$ events, then $\alpha_2$ more.

<Derivation title="Additivity for any shapes">
Convolve the densities and substitute $x = tu$:

$$
p_{X_1 + X_2}(t) = \int_0^t \frac{\beta^{\alpha_1}x^{\alpha_1 - 1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)}
\frac{\beta^{\alpha_2}(t - x)^{\alpha_2 - 1}e^{-\beta(t - x)}}{\Gamma(\alpha_2)}\, dx
= \frac{\beta^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2}\, t^{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 - 1} e^{-\beta t}}{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\Gamma(\alpha_2)}
\int_0^1 u^{\alpha_1 - 1}(1 - u)^{\alpha_2 - 1}\, du.
$$

The last integral is the beta function, $\Gamma(\alpha_1)\Gamma(\alpha_2)/\Gamma(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)$, which leaves the
$\GammaD(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \beta)$ density.
</Derivation>

**Scaling.** If $X \sim \GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$ and $c > 0$, then $cX \sim \GammaD(\alpha, \beta/c)$. The density of
$Y = cX$ is $p_X(y/c)/c \propto y^{\alpha - 1}e^{-(\beta/c) y}$.

**Conjugacy.** The gamma is the conjugate prior for the rate of a Poisson or exponential likelihood and for the
precision $1/\sigma^2$ of a Gaussian with known mean <Cite id="gelman2013" />. For $n$ Poisson counts $y_1, \dots, y_n$
and a prior $\lambda \sim \GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$, the posterior is

$$
\lambda \mid y_{1:n} \sim \GammaD\paren{\alpha + \textstyle\sum_i y_i,\ \beta + n}.
$$

The derivation multiplies the prior density by the likelihood and keeps only factors that depend on $\lambda$:

$$
p(\lambda \mid y_{1:n}) \propto \lambda^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta\lambda} \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda^{y_i}e^{-\lambda}
= \lambda^{\alpha + \sum_i y_i - 1} e^{-(\beta + n)\lambda},
$$

which is the unnormalised $\GammaD(\alpha + \sum_i y_i, \beta + n)$ density. The prior acts like $\alpha$ events
observed over $\beta$ intervals.

For example, a prior $\GammaD(2, 1)$ on a daily event rate has mean 2. Observing 3, 5 and 4 events on three days gives
the posterior $\GammaD(2 + 12, 1 + 3) = \GammaD(14, 4)$, with mean $3.5$. The sample mean, 4, is pulled slightly towards
the prior mean.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Exponential.** $\GammaD(1, \beta) = \Exp(\beta)$.
- **χ².** $\ChiSq_k = \GammaD(k/2, 1/2)$, the <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">χ² distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Inverse gamma.** If $X \sim \GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$, then $1/X \sim$
  <NoteLink to="inverse-gamma-distribution">$\InvGamma(\alpha, \beta)$</NoteLink>.
- **Beta and Dirichlet.** If $X_i \sim \GammaD(\alpha_i, 1)$ are independent, the normalised vector
  $(X_1, \dots, X_K)/\sum_j X_j$ is <NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution">$\Dir(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$</NoteLink>.
  With $K = 2$ its first coordinate is <NoteLink to="beta-distribution">$\Beta(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$</NoteLink>.
- **Negative binomial.** A Poisson count whose rate is gamma-distributed is
  <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink>. See the
  <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution">Poisson distribution</NoteLink>.

## In machine learning

- **Bayesian models.** Gamma priors on rates and precisions keep posteriors in closed form, which makes Gibbs sampling
  and variational inference straightforward <Cite id="bishop2006" />.
- **Positive targets.** Gamma regression models positive, right-skewed targets such as insurance claim sizes or
  durations, with a log link.
- **Sampling other distributions.** Beta and Dirichlet samples are normalised gamma samples.

## Pitfalls

- **Rate or scale.** NumPy's `gamma(shape, scale)` and SciPy's `gamma(a, scale=…)` take $\theta = 1/\beta$. Passing a
  rate where a scale is expected inverts the mean.
- **Vague priors.** $\GammaD(\epsilon, \epsilon)$ with small $\epsilon$ is not uninformative. It concentrates mass near
  0, and posteriors can be sensitive to $\epsilon$ when data are few <Cite id="gelman2013" />.
