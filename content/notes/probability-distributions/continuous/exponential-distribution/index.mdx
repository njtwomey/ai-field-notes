<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ has an exponential distribution with rate $\lambda > 0$, written $X \sim \Exp(\lambda)$, if its
density is

$$
p(x \mid \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x \ge 0.
$$

$X$ is a waiting time. $\lambda$ is the expected number of events per unit time, so $1/\lambda$ is the expected wait.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameter | Rate $\lambda > 0$ (some texts use the scale $\theta = 1/\lambda$) |
| Support | $x \ge 0$ |
| pdf | $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ |
| cdf | $1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ |
| Quantile | $-\log(1 - u)/\lambda$ |
| Mean | $1/\lambda$ |
| Variance | $1/\lambda^2$ |
| Median | $\log 2 / \lambda$ |

## Where it comes from

Suppose events arrive at a constant rate $\lambda$: in any short interval of length $h$, an event occurs with
probability $\lambda h + o(h)$, independently of the past. The probability of no event in $[0, x]$ then satisfies
$S(x + h) = S(x)(1 - \lambda h)$, so $S'(x) = -\lambda S(x)$ and $S(x) = e^{-\lambda x}$. The waiting time to the first
event has <NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival function</NoteLink> $e^{-\lambda x}$, which is the exponential distribution <Cite id="johnson1994" />.

The density is the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the cdf $1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, which is $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$.

For example, if a light bulb's lifetime is exponential with mean 1,000 hours, then $\lambda = 1/1000$ per hour. The
probability that it lasts more than 2,000 hours is $e^{-2} \approx 0.135$, and its median lifetime is
$1000 \log 2 \approx 693$ hours, shorter than the mean because the density is skewed to the right.

The exponential is the continuous counterpart of the
<NoteLink to="geometric-distribution">geometric distribution</NoteLink>. Split time into steps of length $h$ with
success probability $\lambda h$ per step. The number of steps to the first success is geometric, and the waiting time
tends to $\Exp(\lambda)$ as $h \to 0$.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** Integrate by parts:

$$
\expect[X] = \int_0^\infty x \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \Big[-x e^{-\lambda x}\Big]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty
e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \frac{1}{\lambda}.
$$

A second <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink> gives

$$
\expect[X^2] = \int_0^\infty x^2 \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \int_0^\infty 2x e^{-\lambda x}\, dx
= \frac{2}{\lambda}\expect[X] = \frac{2}{\lambda^2}.
$$

So $\var(X) = \frac{2}{\lambda^2} - \frac{1}{\lambda^2} = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$. The median solves
$1 - e^{-\lambda m} = 1/2$, giving $m = \log 2/\lambda$.

**Memorylessness.** For all $s, t \ge 0$,

$$
P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s + t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = P(X > t).
$$

Having waited $s$ units does not change the distribution of the remaining wait. The exponential is the only continuous
distribution on $[0, \infty)$ with this property <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.

<Derivation title="Only the exponential is memoryless">
Let $S(x) = P(X > x)$ satisfy $S(s + t) = S(s)S(t)$ for all $s, t \ge 0$, with $S$ non-increasing and $0 < S(1) < 1$.
Write $S(1) = e^{-\lambda}$ with $\lambda > 0$. Repeated use of the identity gives $S(n) = e^{-\lambda n}$ for integers
$n$, and $S(1/m)^m = S(1)$ gives $S(1/m) = e^{-\lambda/m}$. Together these give $S(q) = e^{-\lambda q}$ for every
positive rational $q$. A non-increasing function that agrees with $e^{-\lambda x}$ on the rationals equals it
everywhere, because each $x$ is squeezed between rationals from above and below.
</Derivation>

**Constant hazard.** The hazard rate, $p(x)/P(X > x)$, is the density of an event at $x$ given survival to $x$. For the
exponential it equals $\lambda$ at every $x$. Lifetimes of parts that wear out have increasing hazards and are not
exponential.

**Minimum of exponentials.** If $X_i \sim \Exp(\lambda_i)$ are independent, then
$\min_i X_i \sim \Exp(\sum_i \lambda_i)$, and the minimum is $X_j$ with probability $\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i$.
By <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>,
$P(\min_i X_i > x) = \prod_i P(X_i > x) = e^{-(\sum_i \lambda_i)x}$. The probability that the minimum is $X_j$ is

$$
P\paren{X_j < \min_{i \ne j} X_i} = \int_0^\infty \lambda_j e^{-\lambda_j x}\, e^{-(\sum_{i \ne j}\lambda_i)x}\, dx
= \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_i \lambda_i}.
$$

**Scaling.** If $X \sim \Exp(1)$, then $X/\lambda \sim \Exp(\lambda)$, since
$P(X/\lambda > x) = P(X > \lambda x) = e^{-\lambda x}$. The rate is an inverse scale.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Poisson.** In a <NoteLink to="poisson-process">Poisson process</NoteLink> with rate $\lambda$, the gaps between events are independent $\Exp(\lambda)$, and
  the number of events in an interval of length $t$ is $\Poisson(\lambda t)$. See the
  <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution">Poisson distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Gamma.** $\Exp(\lambda) =$ <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">$\GammaD(1, \lambda)$</NoteLink>. The sum of $k$
  independent $\Exp(\lambda)$ waits is $\GammaD(k, \lambda)$, the waiting time to the $k$-th event.
- **χ².** $\Exp(1/2) = \ChiSq_2$, the <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">χ² distribution</NoteLink> with two
  degrees of freedom.
- **Laplace.** The difference of two independent $\Exp(1/b)$ variables is
  <NoteLink to="laplace-distribution">$\Laplace(0, b)$</NoteLink>.
- **Uniform.** If $U \sim \Unif(0, 1)$, then $-\log(U)/\lambda \sim \Exp(\lambda)$. This is
  <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">inverse-transform sampling</NoteLink> with the quantile function above.

## In machine learning

- **Survival analysis.** The exponential is the baseline model for time-to-event data, such as time to churn or to
  failure. Models with a non-constant hazard, such as the Weibull, generalise it.
- **Priors on scales.** An exponential prior on a positive scale parameter favours small values while keeping every
  positive value possible.
- **Simulation.** Event-driven simulations of queues and of continuous-time <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chains</NoteLink> draw exponential waiting
  times.

## Pitfalls

- **Rate or scale.** Libraries differ. NumPy's `exponential(scale)` and SciPy's `expon(scale=…)` take $1/\lambda$, not
  $\lambda$.
- **Memorylessness rarely holds for lifetimes.** Components that age, customers who habituate and processes with
  seasonality have hazards that change with time. Check the empirical hazard before assuming a constant rate.
