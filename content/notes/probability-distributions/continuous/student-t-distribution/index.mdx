<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $T$ has a Student t distribution with $\nu > 0$ degrees of freedom, written $T \sim \StudentT_\nu$,
if its density is

$$
p(t \mid \nu) = \frac{\Gamma\paren{\frac{\nu + 1}{2}}}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\paren{\frac{\nu}{2}}}\paren{1 +
\frac{t^2}{\nu}}^{-(\nu + 1)/2}, \qquad t \in \reals.
$$

A location $\mu$ and scale $s$ give the location-scale family $\mu + sT$.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameter | Degrees of freedom $\nu > 0$ |
| Support | $t \in \reals$ |

| cdf | $1 - \tfrac12 I_{\nu/(\nu + t^2)}\paren{\tfrac{\nu}{2}, \tfrac12}$ for $t \ge 0$, by symmetry otherwise |
| Mean | 0 for $\nu > 1$, otherwise undefined |
| Variance | $\nu/(\nu - 2)$ for $\nu > 2$; infinite for $1 < \nu \le 2$ |

## Where it comes from

William Gosset, writing as "Student", derived the distribution of the standardised mean of a small Gaussian sample
<Cite id="student1908" />. Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with sample mean $\bar X$ and sample
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $S$. Replacing the unknown $\sigma$ by $S$ in the z-statistic gives

$$
\frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim \StudentT_{n-1}.
$$

The extra spread relative to $\Gauss(0, 1)$ is the uncertainty in $S$. The statistic has this form because
$Z = (\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n) \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, $V = (n - 1)S^2/\sigma^2$ is
<NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">$\ChiSq_{n-1}$</NoteLink>, the two are independent for Gaussian data, and the
unknown $\sigma$ cancels in $Z/\sqrt{V/(n - 1)}$. This is the basis of the
<NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="pooled-two-sample-t-test">pooled
two-sample</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired</NoteLink> t-tests, and of
<NoteLink to="welch-t-test">Welch's t-test</NoteLink>.

In general, if $Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $V \sim \ChiSq_\nu$ are independent, then
$T = Z/\sqrt{V/\nu} \sim \StudentT_\nu$. The density follows by conditioning on $V$. Given $V = v$, $T$ is
$\Gauss(0, \nu/v)$. Averaging this density over the $\ChiSq_\nu$ density of $V$,

$$
p(t) = \int_0^\infty \sqrt{\frac{v}{2\pi\nu}}\, e^{-t^2 v/(2\nu)}
\cdot \frac{v^{\nu/2 - 1}e^{-v/2}}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)}\, dv
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu}\,2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)} \int_0^\infty v^{\frac{\nu + 1}{2} - 1}
e^{-\frac{v}{2}\paren{1 + t^2/\nu}}\, dv.
$$

The integral is a gamma integral, $\Gamma\paren{\frac{\nu + 1}{2}}\paren{\frac{2}{1 + t^2/\nu}}^{(\nu + 1)/2}$.
Collecting the powers of 2 leaves

$$
p(t) = \frac{\Gamma\paren{\frac{\nu + 1}{2}}}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\paren{\frac{\nu}{2}}}
\paren{1 + \frac{t^2}{\nu}}^{-(\nu + 1)/2},
$$

the density in the definition.

For example, with $\nu = 5$, $P(\abs{T} > 2) \approx 0.102$, more than twice the Gaussian value of $0.046$. A 95%
interval therefore uses the multiplier $2.571$ instead of $1.96$.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** Use the ratio form $T = Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$ with $Z$ and $V$ independent. For $\nu > 1$,
$\expect[T] = \expect[Z]\, \expect[(V/\nu)^{-1/2}] = 0$, since $\expect[Z] = 0$ and the second factor is finite. For the
variance, $\expect[T^2] = \expect[Z^2]\,\nu\,\expect[1/V]$. With $V \sim \ChiSq_\nu = \GammaD(\nu/2, 1/2)$, the gamma
moment formula gives $\expect[1/V] = \frac{\Gamma(\nu/2 - 1)}{\Gamma(\nu/2)} \cdot \frac12 = \frac{1}{\nu - 2}$ for
$\nu > 2$. So $\var(T) = \nu/(\nu - 2)$. For $\nu \le 2$ the integral $\expect[1/V]$ diverges, and the variance is
infinite.

**Heavy tails.** The density decays like $\abs{t}^{-(\nu + 1)}$, a power law, while the Gaussian decays like
$e^{-t^2/2}$. Moments of order $k$ exist only for $k < \nu$. Small $\nu$ makes large values common.

<TailsExplorer />

**Limits.** As $\nu \to \infty$, $\StudentT_\nu \to \Gauss(0, 1)$: $V/\nu$ is an average of $\nu$ squared standard
normals and tends to 1, and in the density $\paren{1 + t^2/\nu}^{-(\nu + 1)/2} \to e^{-t^2/2}$. At $\nu = 1$ it is the
<NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution">Cauchy distribution</NoteLink>, which has no mean.

**Scale <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">mixture of Gaussians</NoteLink>.** If $\tau^2 \sim$
<NoteLink to="inverse-gamma-distribution">$\InvGamma(\nu/2, \nu/2)$</NoteLink>, with shape $\nu/2$ and scale $\nu/2$,
and $T \mid \tau^2 \sim \Gauss(0, \tau^2)$, then $T \sim \StudentT_\nu$. A t variable is a Gaussian whose variance is
itself random. Occasional large variances produce the heavy tails.

<Derivation title="The scale mixture">
This is the ratio form in disguise. Write $\tau^2 = \nu/V$ with $V \sim \ChiSq_\nu$. Then $T = Z\tau = Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$.
The reciprocal of a $\ChiSq_\nu = \GammaD(\nu/2, 1/2)$ variable is $\InvGamma(\nu/2, 1/2)$, and multiplying by $\nu$
scales the second parameter to $\nu/2$.
</Derivation>

## Relations to other distributions

- **Gaussian.** The limit as $\nu \to \infty$. See the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian
  distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Cauchy.** $\StudentT_1$ is the standard Cauchy distribution.
- **χ² and inverse gamma.** The ratio and mixture representations above.
- **F.** If $T \sim \StudentT_\nu$, then $T^2 = Z^2/(V/\nu) \sim$
  <NoteLink to="f-distribution">$\FDist(1, \nu)$</NoteLink>, because $Z^2 \sim \ChiSq_1$.

## In machine learning

- **Robust regression.** A t likelihood for residuals down-weights outliers: their influence on the fit is bounded,
  whereas under a Gaussian likelihood it grows linearly with the residual <Cite id="lange1989" />.
- **<Gloss name="t-sne" />.** t-SNE measures similarity in the low-dimensional embedding with a $\StudentT_1$ kernel. Its heavy tails let
  dissimilar points sit far apart and relieve crowding <Cite id="vandermaaten2008" />.
- **Bayesian predictive distributions.** The posterior predictive of a Gaussian with unknown mean and variance under a
  <NoteLink to="conjugate-priors">conjugate prior</NoteLink> is a t distribution.

## Pitfalls

- **Undefined moments.** For $\nu \le 2$ the sample variance does not converge, and for $\nu \le 1$ neither does the
  sample mean. Estimates computed from such data are unstable.
- **Degrees of freedom are not sample size.** In a one-sample t-test, $\nu = n - 1$. In Welch's test, $\nu$ is a
  non-integer approximation. Using $n$ instead of the correct $\nu$ understates the tails for small samples.
