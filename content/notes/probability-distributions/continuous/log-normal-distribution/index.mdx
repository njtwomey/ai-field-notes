<Definition>
A positive <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ has a log-normal distribution with parameters
$\mu \in \reals$ and $\sigma > 0$, written $X \sim \LogNorm(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if $\log X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Its
density is

$$
p(x \mid \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\paren{-\frac{(\log x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \qquad x > 0.
$$

$\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of $\log X$,
not of $X$.
</Definition>

## Where it comes from

The log-normal is the multiplicative counterpart of the Gaussian. Let $X = Y_1 Y_2 \cdots Y_n$ be a product of
independent positive factors. Then $\log X = \sum_i \log Y_i$ is a sum, and by the <Gloss name="clt">central limit
theorem</Gloss> it is approximately Gaussian when the $\log Y_i$ have finite variance. So $X$ is approximately
log-normal. Quantities that grow by random percentages, such as an investment or a population, follow this pattern
<Cite id="limpert2001" />.

The density follows by <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink>. $X = e^Y$ with
$Y \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and the inverse map $y = \log x$ has <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>
$1/x$. So

$$
p_X(x) = p_Y(\log x)\,\abs{\frac{dy}{dx}}
= \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\paren{-\frac{(\log x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \cdot \frac1x.
$$

The factor $1/x$ is what skews the density to the right. The cdf is
$P(X \le x) = P(Y \le \log x) = \Phi\paren{(\log x - \mu)/\sigma}$, and the median solves $\log x = \mu$.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** Write $X = e^Y$ with $Y \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The Gaussian <Gloss name="mgf">moment
generating function</Gloss> is $\expect[e^{tY}] = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}$. Setting $t = 1$ and $t = 2$:

$$
\expect[X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad \expect[X^2] = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2}.
$$

So $\var(X) = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2} - e^{2\mu + \sigma^2} = (e^{\sigma^2} - 1)\, e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}$.

The mode maximises the log-density $-\log x - (\log x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$. Its derivative in $\log x$ is
$-1 - (\log x - \mu)/\sigma^2$, which is zero at $\log x = \mu - \sigma^2$.

For example, if incomes are $\LogNorm(10, 0.8^2)$, the median income is $e^{10} \approx 22{,}000$ and the mean is
$e^{10 + 0.32} \approx 30{,}300$. The mean exceeds the median by the factor $e^{\sigma^2/2} \approx 1.38$.

**Right skew.** The mode, median and mean satisfy $e^{\mu - \sigma^2} < e^\mu < e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. The gap between
them grows with $\sigma$. For small $\sigma$ the distribution is close to a Gaussian with mean $e^\mu$; for large
$\sigma$ it has a long right tail.

**Multiplicative spread.** About 68% of the mass lies between $e^{\mu}/e^{\sigma}$ and $e^{\mu} \cdot e^{\sigma}$. The
factor $e^\sigma$, the geometric standard deviation, describes spread as a ratio rather than a difference
<Cite id="limpert2001" />.

**Products and powers.** If $X_1 \sim \LogNorm(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_2 \sim \LogNorm(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are
independent, then $X_1 X_2 \sim \LogNorm(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. For $a \neq 0$,
$X^a \sim \LogNorm(a\mu, a^2\sigma^2)$.

**Tails.** All moments exist, $\expect[X^k] = e^{k\mu + k^2\sigma^2/2}$, but the tail is heavier than any exponential.
The moment generating function is infinite for every $t > 0$.

<Derivation title="Products and powers">
$\log(X_1X_2) = \log X_1 + \log X_2$ is a sum of independent Gaussians, so it is
$\Gauss(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$. $\log X^a = a\log X$ is $\Gauss(a\mu, a^2\sigma^2)$. The moment
generating function is infinite for $t > 0$ because $e^{tx}$ grows faster than the density
$\propto e^{-(\log x)^2/(2\sigma^2)}/x$ decays: in terms of $y = \log x$ the integrand is
$\exp\paren{te^y - (y - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$, and $te^y$ dominates any quadratic.
</Derivation>

## Relations to other distributions

- **Gaussian.** $\log X$ is <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian</NoteLink>, by definition.
- **Gamma.** Both model positive right-skewed data (see <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma
  distribution</NoteLink>). The gamma has an exponential right tail, while the log-normal tail is heavier. The two are
  often hard to tell apart from moderate samples.

## In machine learning

- **Skewed targets.** Regressing $\log y$ with squared error assumes a log-normal target. Predictions of
  $\exp(\widehat{\log y})$ estimate the median of $y$, not its mean.
- **Positive parameters.** Placing a Gaussian on the logarithm of a positive parameter, such as a length-scale or a
  <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>, puts a log-normal prior on it. Random search over learning rates
  samples them log-uniformly for the same reason: their effect is multiplicative.
- **Systems metrics.** Request latencies and file sizes are often roughly log-normal, which is why they are summarised
  with medians and percentiles.

## Pitfalls

- **Mean of the logs is not the log of the mean.** $e^{\expect[\log X]} = e^\mu$ is the median. Back-transforming a
  mean fitted on the log scale underestimates $\expect[X]$ by the factor $e^{\sigma^2/2}$.
- **Zeros.** The support excludes 0. Data with exact zeros need an offset, which changes the model, or a separate
  model for the zeros.
