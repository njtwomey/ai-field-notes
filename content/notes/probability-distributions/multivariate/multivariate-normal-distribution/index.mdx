<Definition>
A random vector $\xvec \in \reals^d$ has a multivariate normal distribution with mean $\muvec \in \reals^d$ and
covariance $\Sigmamat \in \reals^{d \times d}$, symmetric and positive definite, written
$\xvec \sim \MvGauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$, if its density is

$$
p(\xvec \mid \muvec, \Sigmamat) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \det(\Sigmamat)^{1/2}} \exp\paren{-\frac12 (\xvec - \muvec)^\top
\Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec)}.
$$

The quantity $\Delta^2 = (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec)$ is the squared Mahalanobis distance from
$\xvec$ to $\muvec$.
</Definition>

## Where it comes from

**<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">Linear maps</NoteLink> of independent Gaussians.** Let $\zvec \in \reals^d$ have independent $\Gauss(0, 1)$ entries and let
$\Lmat$ be any <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">invertible matrix</NoteLink> with $\Lmat\Lmat^\top = \Sigmamat$, such as the
<NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink>. Then
$\xvec = \muvec + \Lmat\zvec \sim \MvGauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$. Every multivariate normal is a stretched, rotated and
shifted cloud of independent standard normals. This is also how samples are drawn.

The density follows from this construction. $\zvec$ has density
$\prod_i \phi(z_i) = (2\pi)^{-d/2}\exp\paren{-\tfrac12\zvec^\top\zvec}$, because its entries are independent. The map
$\xvec = \muvec + \Lmat\zvec$ is inverted by $\zvec = \Lmat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)$, whose <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian determinant</NoteLink> is
$\det(\Lmat^{-1}) = \det(\Lmat)^{-1}$. Since $\det(\Sigmamat) = \det(\Lmat)^2$, this is $\det(\Sigmamat)^{-1/2}$. The
exponent becomes

$$
\zvec^\top\zvec = (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Lmat^{-\top}\Lmat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)
= (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec).
$$

The <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink> multiplies the density of $\zvec$ by the
Jacobian determinant, which gives the density in the definition. The mean is
$\expect[\xvec] = \muvec + \Lmat\expect[\zvec] = \muvec$, and the covariance is

$$
\cov(\xvec) = \expect\brack{(\xvec - \muvec)(\xvec - \muvec)^\top} = \Lmat\,\expect[\zvec\zvec^\top]\,\Lmat^\top =
\Lmat\Lmat^\top = \Sigmamat.
$$

**<NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">Multivariate central limit theorem</NoteLink>.** Sums of many independent random vectors with finite covariance are
approximately multivariate normal.

In two dimensions, $\Sigmamat$ has the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">variances</NoteLink> $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ on the diagonal and $\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$
off it, where $\rho$ is the correlation. The same matrix can be written in principal form,
$\Sigmamat = a^2\uvec_1\uvec_1^\top + b^2\uvec_2\uvec_2^\top$, with perpendicular unit vectors $\uvec_1, \uvec_2$: the
cloud is stretched by $a$ along $\uvec_1$ and by $b$ along $\uvec_2$. These are the eigenvectors of $\Sigmamat$, and
$a^2, b^2$ its eigenvalues.

## Properties

**Elliptical contours.** The density is constant where $\Delta^2$ is constant. These level sets are ellipsoids centred
at $\muvec$. Their axes point along the eigenvectors of $\Sigmamat$, with half-lengths proportional to the square roots
of the eigenvalues. $\Delta^2 = \zvec^\top\zvec$ is a sum of $d$ independent squared standard normals, so
$\Delta^2 \sim \ChiSq_d$, and the ellipsoid $\Delta^2 \le c$ holds probability $P(\ChiSq_d \le c)$.

**Linear maps.** For a matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times d}$ and a vector $\bvec \in \reals^m$,
$\Amat\xvec + \bvec \sim \MvGauss(\Amat\muvec + \bvec, \Amat\Sigmamat\Amat^\top)$ <Cite id="bishop2006" />. The <Gloss name="mgf">moment
generating function</Gloss> of $\xvec$ is
$\expect[e^{\tvec^\top\xvec}] = \exp\paren{\tvec^\top\muvec + \tfrac12\tvec^\top\Sigmamat\tvec}$, from the
one-dimensional Gaussian case applied to $\tvec^\top\xvec$. For $\yvec = \Amat\xvec + \bvec$,

$$
\expect[e^{\svec^\top\yvec}] = e^{\svec^\top\bvec}\,\expect[e^{(\Amat^\top\svec)^\top\xvec}]
= \exp\paren{\svec^\top(\Amat\muvec + \bvec) + \tfrac12\svec^\top\Amat\Sigmamat\Amat^\top\svec},
$$

the Gaussian form with the stated parameters.

**Marginals and conditionals.** Split $\xvec = (\xvec_a, \xvec_b)$ with the matching blocks of $\muvec$ and
$\Sigmamat$. The marginal is $\xvec_a \sim \MvGauss(\muvec_a, \Sigmamat_{aa})$: drop the other coordinates. The
conditional is also Gaussian <Cite id="bishop2006" />:

$$
\xvec_a \mid \xvec_b \sim \MvGauss\paren{\muvec_a + \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}(\xvec_b - \muvec_b),\
\Sigmamat_{aa}
- \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{ba}}.
$$

The conditional mean is linear in $\xvec_b$, and the conditional covariance does not depend on $\xvec_b$ at all.

The conditional follows from a decorrelation. Let $\Kmat = \Sigmamat_{ab}\Sigmamat_{bb}^{-1}$ and
$\rvec = \xvec_a - \Kmat\xvec_b$. $\rvec$ and $\xvec_b$ are jointly Gaussian, being a linear map of $\xvec$, and

$$
\cov(\rvec, \xvec_b) = \Sigmamat_{ab} - \Kmat\Sigmamat_{bb} = \zeros,
$$

so $\rvec$ is independent of $\xvec_b$. Its mean is $\muvec_a - \Kmat\muvec_b$ and its covariance is
$\Sigmamat_{aa} - \Kmat\Sigmamat_{ba}$. Given $\xvec_b$, $\xvec_a = \rvec + \Kmat\xvec_b$ is $\rvec$ shifted by a known
amount, which gives the conditional above.

For example, take two standard normals with correlation $\rho = 0.8$. Observing $x_2 = 2$ gives
$x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \Gauss(0.8 \times 2,\ 1 - 0.8^2) = \Gauss(1.6, 0.36)$. The observation moves the mean most of the way
towards 2 and cuts the variance from 1 to 0.36.

**Uncorrelated means independent.** For jointly Gaussian variables, zero covariance implies <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>: with
$\Sigmamat$ diagonal, the <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> in the exponent is a sum of one-coordinate terms, and the density factorises.
This is false for general random variables, and false for Gaussians that are not jointly Gaussian.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Gaussian.** The case $d = 1$. See the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian distribution</NoteLink>.
- **χ².** The squared Mahalanobis distance is $\ChiSq_d$. See the
  <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">χ² distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Wishart.** The scatter matrix of $n$ independent $\MvGauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat)$ vectors is Wishart. See the
  <NoteLink to="wishart-distribution">Wishart distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Multivariate t.** A multivariate normal whose covariance is scaled by an inverse-gamma variable is a multivariate
  Student t, with heavier tails.

## In machine learning

- **Gaussian mixture models.** A <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> is a weighted
  sum of multivariate normals, one per cluster.
- **<Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss>.** A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions whose values at any finite set of inputs
  are multivariate normal. Prediction is the conditioning formula above <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.
- **Linear-Gaussian models.** <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="factor-analysis">factor analysis</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="probabilistic-principal-component-analysis">probabilistic PCA</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> all
  combine linear maps with Gaussian noise, so every posterior stays Gaussian <Cite id="murphy2012" />.
- **Reparameterisation.** <Gloss name="vae">Variational autoencoders</Gloss> sample $\muvec + \Lmat\zvec$ so that <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> flow through
  $\muvec$ and $\Lmat$ <Cite id="kingma2014" />. This is the
  <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Positive definiteness.** An estimated covariance can be singular or nearly so, for example with more dimensions
  than samples. Add a small multiple of the identity or use shrinkage before inverting.
- **Numerical evaluation.** Compute $\log\det\Sigmamat$ and $\Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)$ from a Cholesky factor.
  Forming $\Sigmamat^{-1}$ explicitly loses accuracy.
- **Parameter count.** A full covariance has $d(d + 1)/2$ free entries, which grows quadratically. Diagonal or low-rank
  covariances trade flexibility for fewer parameters.
