<Definition>
A random vector $\thetavec = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_K)$ has a Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameters
$\alphavec = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$, all positive, written $\thetavec \sim \Dir(\alphavec)$, if it lies on the
probability simplex $\set{\thetavec : \theta_k \ge 0,\ \sum_k \theta_k = 1}$ with density

$$
p(\thetavec \mid \alphavec) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\prod_{k=1}^{K} \Gamma(\alpha_k)} \prod_{k=1}^{K}
\theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1}, \qquad \alpha_0 = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k.
$$

The density is with respect to the $(K - 1)$-dimensional volume of the simplex.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Concentrations $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K > 0$; total $\alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k$ |
| Support | The simplex: $\theta_k \ge 0$, $\sum_k \theta_k = 1$ |
| Mean | $\expect[\theta_k] = \alpha_k/\alpha_0$ |
| Variance | $\var(\theta_k) = \alpha_k(\alpha_0 - \alpha_k) / \paren{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}$ |
| Covariance | $\cov(\theta_j, \theta_k) = -\alpha_j\alpha_k / \paren{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}$ for $j \neq k$ |
| Mode | $(\alpha_k - 1)/(\alpha_0 - K)$ when every $\alpha_k > 1$ |

## Where it comes from

**Posterior of category probabilities.** Suppose each observation falls in one of $K$ categories with probabilities
$\thetavec$, and category $k$ is seen $n_k$ times. The likelihood is $\prod_k \theta_k^{n_k}$. With prior
$\thetavec \sim \Dir(\alphavec)$, the posterior is proportional to $\prod_k \theta_k^{\alpha_k + n_k - 1}$, so

$$
\thetavec \mid n_{1:K} \sim \Dir(\alpha_1 + n_1, \dots, \alpha_K + n_K).
$$

The likelihood is that of $n$ <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical</NoteLink> draws, or of one
<NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial</NoteLink> count vector, which differs only by a constant. For
example, a uniform prior $\Dir(1, 1, 1)$ and counts $(5, 2, 0)$ give the posterior $\Dir(6, 3, 1)$, with mean
$(0.6, 0.3, 0.1)$. The third category keeps probability $0.1$ although it was never observed.

As for the <NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta distribution</NoteLink>, each $\alpha_k$ acts as a prior count for
category $k$ <Cite id="bishop2006" />.

**Normalised gammas.** If $G_k \sim \GammaD(\alpha_k, 1)$ are independent, then
$(G_1, \dots, G_K)/\sum_j G_j \sim \Dir(\alphavec)$ <Cite id="frigyik2010" />. This is how Dirichlet samples are drawn,
with a fast gamma sampler such as that of Marsaglia and Tsang <Cite id="marsaglia2000" />.

The normalised-gamma construction also produces the density. The $G_k$ have joint density
$\prod_k g_k^{\alpha_k - 1}e^{-g_k}/\Gamma(\alpha_k)$. Change variables to the total $s = \sum_k g_k$ and the
proportions $\theta_k = g_k/s$ for $k < K$, with $\theta_K = 1 - \sum_{k<K}\theta_k$. Then $g_k = s\theta_k$, and the
Jacobian of the map from $(s, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_{K-1})$ to $(g_1, \dots, g_K)$ is $s^{K-1}$. So the joint density
of $s$ and $\thetavec$ is

$$
\prod_k \frac{(s\theta_k)^{\alpha_k - 1}}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)}\, e^{-s}\, s^{K-1}
= \frac{\prod_k \theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1}}{\prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k)} \cdot s^{\alpha_0 - 1}e^{-s}.
$$

It factorises into a function of $\thetavec$ and a function of $s$, so the proportions are independent of the total.
Integrating out $s$ gives $\Gamma(\alpha_0)$, which leaves the Dirichlet density. With $K = 2$ this is the argument for
the beta distribution.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** By aggregation (below), $\theta_k \sim \Beta(\alpha_k, \alpha_0 - \alpha_k)$. The beta mean and
variance formulas give $\expect[\theta_k] = \alpha_k/\alpha_0$ and

$$
\var(\theta_k) = \frac{\alpha_k(\alpha_0 - \alpha_k)}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}.
$$

For the covariance, $\theta_j + \theta_k \sim \Beta(\alpha_j + \alpha_k, \alpha_0 - \alpha_j - \alpha_k)$, and
$\cov(\theta_j, \theta_k) = \tfrac12\paren{\var(\theta_j + \theta_k) - \var(\theta_j) - \var(\theta_k)}$. Substituting
the three beta variances and simplifying gives $-\alpha_j\alpha_k/\paren{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0 + 1)}$.

**Mean and concentration.** The mean $\alphavec/\alpha_0$ sets where the mass sits on the simplex. The total
$\alpha_0$ sets how tightly it concentrates: every variance shrinks like $1/(\alpha_0 + 1)$.

**Sparsity.** When every $\alpha_k < 1$, the density is highest near the corners and edges of the simplex. Typical
samples then put most of their mass on a few categories. When every $\alpha_k = 1$, the distribution is uniform on the
simplex.

**Negative correlation.** The entries sum to 1, so a larger $\theta_j$ leaves less for the others. Every pair of
entries has negative covariance.

**Aggregation.** Merging categories adds their parameters:
$(\theta_1 + \theta_2, \theta_3, \dots, \theta_K) \sim \Dir(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \dots, \alpha_K)$. Each
marginal is beta: $\theta_k \sim \Beta(\alpha_k, \alpha_0 - \alpha_k)$. The proof uses the gamma construction:
$\theta_1 + \theta_2 = (G_1 + G_2)/\sum_j G_j$, and $G_1 + G_2 \sim \GammaD(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2, 1)$ independently of
the other $G_j$, so the merged vector is again a normalised gamma vector.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Beta.** $\Dir(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ is $\Beta(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ for $\theta_1$.
- **Gamma.** Normalised independent gammas with a common rate are Dirichlet. See the
  <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distribution</NoteLink>.
- **Categorical and multinomial.** The Dirichlet is the conjugate prior of the
  <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial</NoteLink> distributions. Integrating $\thetavec$ out of a
  multinomial gives the Dirichlet-multinomial distribution.
- **Dirichlet process.** The Dirichlet process extends the Dirichlet to infinitely many categories and underlies
  nonparametric mixture models <Cite id="frigyik2010" />.

## In machine learning

- **Topic models.** Latent Dirichlet allocation gives each document a $\Dir(\alphavec)$ distribution over topics and
  each topic a Dirichlet distribution over words. Small $\alpha$ makes documents use few topics <Cite id="blei2003" />.
- **Smoothing counts.** The posterior mean $(n_k + \alpha_k)/(n + \alpha_0)$ is additive smoothing for categorical
  probabilities, as in naive Bayes and n-gram language models.
- **Uncertainty over class probabilities.** Evidential and prior networks output Dirichlet parameters instead of a
  single softmax vector, so that low $\alpha_0$ signals low confidence <Cite id="malinin2018" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Parameters are not probabilities.** $\alphavec = (1, 1, 1)$ and $\alphavec = (100, 100, 100)$ have the same mean
  but very different spread. Report $\alpha_0$ along with the mean.
- **Small α and numerics.** With $\alpha_k \ll 1$, samples can have entries that underflow to 0 in floating point. Work
  with log-gamma samples or clip entries when taking logarithms.
