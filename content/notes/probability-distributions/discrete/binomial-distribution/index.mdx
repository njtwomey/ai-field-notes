<Definition>
A <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ has a binomial distribution with $n \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$ trials and success probability
$p \in [0, 1]$, written $X \sim \Binom(n, p)$, if

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n.
$$

$X$ is the number of successes in $n$ independent trials that each succeed with probability $p$.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Trials $n \in \{1, 2, \dots\}$, success probability $p \in [0, 1]$ |
| Support | $k \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$ |
| pmf | $\binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$ |
| Mean | $np$ |
| Variance | $np(1 - p)$ |
| Mode | $\lfloor (n + 1)p \rfloor$ (also $(n + 1)p - 1$ when $(n + 1)p$ is an integer) |

## Where it comes from

Run $n$ independent $\Bern(p)$ trials. A particular sequence with $k$ successes and $n - k$ failures has probability
$p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$, whatever the order. There are $\binom{n}{k}$ such sequences, one for each choice of the $k$
positions that succeed. Summing over them gives the pmf <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.

The probabilities sum to 1 by the binomial theorem:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} = \paren{p + (1 - p)}^n = 1.
$$

For example, with $n = 10$ and $p = 0.3$, the probability of exactly 3 successes is
$\binom{10}{3}\, 0.3^3\, 0.7^7 = 120 \times 0.027 \times 0.0824 \approx 0.267$.

The order of the trials does not affect the probability. Only the number of successes matters, so $X$ carries all the
information the trials hold about $p$.

## Properties

**Sum of indicators.** Write $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ with $X_i \iid \Bern(p)$. Most properties follow from this form.

**Mean and variance.** Each $X_i$ has mean $p$ and variance $p(1 - p)$. Linearity of expectation gives
$\expect[X] = \sum_i \expect[X_i] = np$. The $X_i$ are independent, so their covariances are zero and the variances
add:

$$
\var(X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \var(X_i) = np(1 - p).
$$

Computing $\sum_k k \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$ directly gives the same mean, but the indicator form avoids the
sum.

**Concentration of the proportion.** The success fraction $\hat p = X/n$ has mean $p$ and variance $p(1 - p)/n$. Its
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>, $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$, shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$. Quadrupling $n$ halves the uncertainty in $\hat p$.

**Closure under sums.** If $X_1 \sim \Binom(n_1, p)$ and $X_2 \sim \Binom(n_2, p)$ are independent with the same $p$,
then $X_1 + X_2 \sim \Binom(n_1 + n_2, p)$. The proof is the indicator form: $X_1$ counts successes in $n_1$ trials,
$X_2$ in $n_2$ further independent trials, and the sum counts successes in all $n_1 + n_2$ trials. With different
success probabilities the sum is not binomial.

<Derivation title="The mode">
The ratio of successive probabilities is

$$
\frac{P(X = k)}{P(X = k - 1)} = \frac{\binom{n}{k}}{\binom{n}{k - 1}} \cdot \frac{p}{1 - p}
= \frac{(n - k + 1)\,p}{k\,(1 - p)}.
$$

The ratio is at least 1 exactly when $(n - k + 1)p \ge k(1 - p)$, that is when $k \le (n + 1)p$. The pmf therefore rises
up to $k = \lfloor (n + 1)p \rfloor$ and falls after it. When $(n + 1)p$ is an integer, the ratio equals 1 at
$k = (n + 1)p$, and $k - 1$ is a mode too.
</Derivation>

**Normal approximation.** For large $n$ with $p$ away from 0 and 1, $\Binom(n, p)$ is approximately
$\Gauss(np, np(1 - p))$. This is the de Moivre–Laplace theorem, a special case of the <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss>.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Bernoulli.** $\Binom(1, p) = \Bern(p)$.
- **Poisson.** As $n \to \infty$ with $np \to \lambda$, $\Binom(n, p)$ tends to
  <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution">$\Poisson(\lambda)$</NoteLink>.
- **Hypergeometric.** Drawing $n$ items without replacement from a finite population gives a
  <NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">hypergeometric</NoteLink> count. It tends to the binomial when the
  population is much larger than $n$.
- **Multinomial.** With $K$ outcomes per trial instead of two, the vector of counts is
  <NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial</NoteLink>. Each single count is binomial.
- **Beta.** A <NoteLink to="beta-distribution">$\Beta(a, b)$</NoteLink> prior on $p$ and $k$ successes give a
  $\Beta(a + k, b + n - k)$ posterior. The prior density $p^{a - 1}(1 - p)^{b - 1}$ times the likelihood
  $p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}$ is $p^{a + k - 1}(1 - p)^{b + n - k - 1}$, the unnormalised $\Beta(a + k, b + n - k)$ density.

## In machine learning

- **Uncertainty in accuracy.** If test examples are independent, the number classified correctly out of $n$ is
  $\Binom(n, a)$, where $a$ is the true accuracy. With $n = 1000$ and $a = 0.9$ the standard error of the measured
  accuracy is $\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1 / 1000} \approx 0.0095$. Differences of one or two percentage points between models
  on such a test set are within noise.
- **Conversion rates.** Clicks out of impressions and conversions out of visitors are modelled as binomial counts in
  <Gloss name="ab-test" plural />.
- **Testing a proportion.** The <NoteLink to="binomial-test">binomial test</NoteLink> compares an observed count with
  its $\Binom(n, p_0)$ distribution under a null value $p_0$.

## Pitfalls

- **Dependent trials.** Examples from the same user, document or patient are correlated. The count then varies more
  than $np(1 - p)$, and binomial standard errors are too small.
- **Unequal probabilities.** A sum of independent Bernoulli trials with different $p_i$ has variance
  $\sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)$, which is at most $n\bar p(1 - \bar p)$ for the mean probability $\bar p$. Treating it as
  $\Binom(n, \bar p)$ overstates the variance.
- **Wald intervals.** The interval $\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}$ has poor coverage when $n$ is small or
  $p$ is near 0 or 1. It collapses to a single point when $\hat p$ is 0 or 1. Wilson or Agresti–Coull intervals behave
  better <Cite id="brown2001" />.
