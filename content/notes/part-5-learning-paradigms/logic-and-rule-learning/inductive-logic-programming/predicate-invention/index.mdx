<Definition>
In an <NoteLink to="inductive-logic-programming">inductive logic programming</NoteLink> problem with background
knowledge $B$ and examples $E^+$ and $E^-$, a hypothesis $H$ **invents a predicate** when it uses a predicate symbol
that occurs neither in $B$ nor in the examples <Cite id="cropper2021poppi" />. The invented predicate is defined by
clauses of $H$ and used in the bodies of other clauses of $H$. **Predicate invention** is the search for such
hypotheses: the learner chooses the new predicate's arity, its definition and where it is called, with no examples of
it.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A learner is given the mother and father facts of a family and examples of grandparent. Without a parent predicate,
the definition needs one clause for each way two generations can combine:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{grandparent}(A, B) &\leftarrow \text{mother}(A, C) \land \text{mother}(C, B), \\
\text{grandparent}(A, B) &\leftarrow \text{mother}(A, C) \land \text{father}(C, B),
\end{aligned}
$$

and two more for father followed by mother and father followed by father. Four clauses of three literals make 12
literals. A learner that may invent a predicate writes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{grandparent}(A, B) &\leftarrow \text{inv}(A, C) \land \text{inv}(C, B), \\
\text{inv}(A, B) &\leftarrow \text{mother}(A, B), \\
\text{inv}(A, B) &\leftarrow \text{father}(A, B),
\end{aligned}
$$

three clauses and 7 literals. The new symbol $\text{inv}$ is parent, a concept nobody supplied
<Cite id="cropper2021poppi" />. The program is shorter because $\text{inv}$ is defined once and used twice.

Shorter programs are easier to find. The number of programs an <Gloss name="ilp" /> learner considers grows
exponentially with the size of the smallest solution, so a change of vocabulary that shrinks the solution shrinks the
search. An invented predicate is also a new concept that later tasks can reuse, the logic-programming form of learning
a representation <Cite id="kramer2020history" />.

## Details

### What invention buys

- **Compression.** A definition used $u$ times saves the length of its body $u - 1$ times, at the cost of its own
  clauses. The grandparent program saves 5 literals.
- **Expressivity under a bias.** A language bias that limits clause bodies, such as the two-literal chain metarule
  $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, C) \land R(C, B)$, cannot express great-grandparent, which needs three parent steps. With an
  invented grandparent it can: $\text{greatgrandparent}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, C) \land \text{inv}(C, B)$.
- **Recursion through helpers.** Many recursive programs need an auxiliary predicate, such as reversing a list with an
  accumulator. When the helper is not in $B$, it must be invented.
- **Exponential compression by chaining.** Moving $k$ steps along a line with a single clause needs $k$ body literals.
  Invented predicates that each double the previous one need about $\log_2 k$ clauses. Cropper and Morel report a
  program of 10 literals that moves right 16 times, against the 17 literals of the single clause
  <Cite id="cropper2021poppi" />.

### Why it is hard

Without invention, the vocabulary of the hypotheses is fixed by $B$. With invention, each new symbol adds a choice of
arity, of argument positions and of definition, and none of its facts are observed, so it cannot be scored on its own.
A learner that scores clauses one at a time, such as <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">Progol</NoteLink>, has nothing to score an invented clause against. Early
surveys list the open questions as when to invent, what arguments to give the new predicate and how to keep the
enlarged space searchable <Cite id="stahl1993" />, and predicate invention remains one of the main open problems of the
field <Cite id="cropper2022ilp30" />.

### Inverse resolution

The first systems invented predicates by running resolution backwards. CIGOL's **intra-construction**, one of the
W-operators, takes two clauses with the same head and a shared part of the body, $p \leftarrow A \land B$ and
$p \leftarrow A \land C$, and replaces them with $p \leftarrow A \land q$, $q \leftarrow B$ and $q \leftarrow C$, where
$q$ is new <Cite id="muggleton1988cigol" />. Resolving the new clauses on $q$ gives back the originals, so the rewrite
keeps every consequence. The operator compresses when the shared part is long, but it offered no complete search: the
learner applied it where a user or a heuristic pointed.

### Invention as search

Modern systems put invented symbols into the hypothesis space and let the search decide.

- **Meta-interpretive learning.** In Metagol, a predicate variable in a metarule's body may be bound to a fresh symbol,
  whose definition is then built by further metarule instances while the examples are proved
  (<NoteLink to="meta-interpretive-learning">meta-interpretive learning</NoteLink>). Iterative deepening on the number
  of clauses returns a program with the fewest clauses, so invention happens when it saves clauses
  <Cite id="muggleton2015mil" />. Hocquette and Muggleton give a bottom-up form of invention for
  meta-interpretive learning and prove it complete <Cite id="hocquette2020bottomup" />.
- **Learning from failures.** Poppi extends <NoteLink to="learning-from-failures">Popper</NoteLink> by allowing invented
  symbols in the heads and bodies of generated programs, with no metarules and no user-given arity. Popper's
  elimination constraint prunes non-recursive programs that contain a clause proving no positive. An invented
  predicate always appears in a body, so Poppi replaces it with a redundancy constraint over clause dependencies that
  is sound for optimal solutions <Cite id="cropper2021poppi" />.

### Beyond logic programs

Statistical predicate invention clusters the objects and relations of a database jointly and uses the clusters as new
predicates in a <NoteLink to="markov-logic-networks">Markov logic network</NoteLink> <Cite id="kok2007spi" />. Library
learning in program synthesis does the same for functions: DreamCoder adds subprograms shared by many solutions to its
language (<NoteLink to="program-synthesis-from-examples">program synthesis from examples</NoteLink>)
<Cite id="ellis2021dreamcoder" />. The hidden units of a neural network play a similar role without names or
definitions that a person can read <Cite id="kramer2020history" />.

## Worked example

The figure uses the family database extended by two generations: Ann is the mother of Bob and Cid, Bob the father of
Dee and Eve, Cid the mother of Fay, Eve the mother of Gus, Gus the father of Hal and Hal the father of Ivy. The
metarules are ident and chain, and the clause bound is 4.

1. **Mother and father facts.** Splitting the eight parent facts by the parent's sex gives four mother facts and four
   father facts. The grandparent positives are (ann, dee), (ann, eve), (ann, fay), (bob, gus), (eve, hal) and (gus,
   ivy). They use all four combinations: mother then father (Ann, Bob, Dee), mother then mother (Ann, Cid, Fay), father
   then mother (Bob, Eve, Gus) and father then father (Gus, Hal, Ivy).
2. **Without invention.** No program of 1, 2 or 3 clauses proves all six positives, because each chain clause covers one
   combination. At 4 clauses Metagol finds the four combinations after 1160 search events: 12 literals.
3. **With invention.** At 3 clauses, the chain clause $\text{grandparent}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{inv1}(A, C) \land
   \text{inv1}(C, B)$ with $\text{inv1}$ defined by ident twice, once on mother and once on father, proves every
   positive and none of the five negatives. Iterative deepening reaches it before the 4-clause bound: 3 clauses, 7
   literals. The search takes 12616 events, ten times more than without invention, because every open predicate now has
   one more possible binding.
4. **Intra-construction gives the same program.** The four clauses of step 2 share the head. Pairing the two that start
   with $\text{mother}(A, C)$ factors out $\text{inv}(C, B) \leftarrow \text{mother}(C, B)$ and
   $\text{inv}(C, B) \leftarrow \text{father}(C, B)$; the father pair factors the same way with the same
   $\text{inv}$. The two remaining grandparent clauses differ only in their first literal. A second intra-construction
   factors it out into a new predicate defined by mother and by father, the same definition as $\text{inv}$, and
   merging the two gives the 3-clause program.
5. **Great-grandparent.** With positives (ann, gus), (bob, hal) and (eve, ivy), no program of at most 4 clauses over
   parent exists without invention: chain bodies have two literals, and recursive programs prove negatives such as
   (ann, eve). With invention, the program $\text{greatgrandparent}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, C) \land
   \text{inv1}(C, B)$, $\text{inv1}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, C) \land \text{parent}(C, B)$ is found after 314
   events. $\text{inv1}$ is grandparent.
6. **Great-great-grandparent.** The invented grandparent is used twice: $\text{greatgreatgrandparent}(A, B) \leftarrow
   \text{inv1}(A, C) \land \text{inv1}(C, B)$. Two clauses of three literals describe four generations.

## Assumptions

- **The bias admits the useful predicate.** An invented predicate is built from the same metarules or declarations as
  every other clause. If parent could only be defined by a clause shape the bias excludes, it is never invented.
- **Exact examples.** Metagol and Popper accept a program only if it proves every positive and no negative. An invented
  predicate that fits a mislabelled example is accepted as readily as a correct one.
- **The size measure matches the preference.** Metagol minimises clauses and Popper literals. Invention happens when it
  lowers that measure, not when it would yield a more general or more readable program.

## Pitfalls

- **Invention when nothing forces it.** Drop (gus, ivy) from the grandparent positives and the father-then-father
  combination disappears from the examples. Three chain clauses without invention then prove every remaining positive,
  and Metagol returns them although invention is allowed, because the invented program also has three clauses and the
  search tries background predicates first. The returned program misses every father-then-father pair.
- **Search cost.** Each invented symbol adds bindings at every open predicate. In the grandparent task the search grows
  from 1160 to 12616 events. Systems bound the number of invented symbols and their arity.
- **Uninterpretable symbols.** The learner names its predicates inv1 and inv2. An invented predicate can fit the
  training examples without matching any concept a person would recognise, and two runs can invent different
  predicates for one task.
- **Redundant inventions.** A predicate defined only as a renaming of a background predicate, $\text{inv}(A, B)
  \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, B)$, adds a clause and saves nothing. Such a definition is never part of a smallest
  program, but the search still generates and tests the programs that contain it unless a constraint excludes them.
