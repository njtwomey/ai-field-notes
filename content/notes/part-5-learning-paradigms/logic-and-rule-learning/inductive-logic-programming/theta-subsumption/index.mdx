<Definition>
Treat a clause as the set of its literals. A clause $C$ **θ-subsumes** a clause $D$, written $C \succeq D$, when there
is a substitution $\theta$ with $C\theta \subseteq D$: applying $\theta$ to every literal of $C$ gives literals that all
occur in $D$ <Cite id="plotkin1972thesis" />. For <NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">Horn clauses</NoteLink>
this means $\theta$ maps the head of $C$ onto the head of $D$ and each body literal of $C$ onto some body literal of
$D$. $C$ is then at least as general as $D$, and $C \models D$. Two clauses that θ-subsume each other are
**equivalent**, and a clause equivalent to no proper subset of itself is **reduced**.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A general clause says less than a specific one. $\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Z) \land
\text{parent}(Z, Y)$ holds of any $X$ and $Y$ joined by a chain of two parent links. The clause

$$
\text{grandparent}(\text{ann}, \text{dee}) \leftarrow \text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{bob}) \land
\text{parent}(\text{bob}, \text{dee}) \land \text{female}(\text{dee})
$$

talks about two named people and adds a condition on Dee. The substitution
$\set{X \mapsto \text{ann}, Y \mapsto \text{dee}, Z \mapsto \text{bob}}$ turns the first clause into a subset of the
second. Every situation that satisfies the specific clause's body therefore satisfies the general clause's body.

θ-subsumption captures the two ways a clause becomes more specific: binding variables (to constants, to other variables
or to compound terms) and adding literals. It is a purely syntactic test, so a learner can compare two hypotheses
without enumerating the examples they cover. That makes it the working generality order of
<NoteLink to="inductive-logic-programming">inductive logic programming</NoteLink>
(<NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">generality ordering and refinement</NoteLink>).

The same test decides coverage. Write an example $e$ with ground background facts $B$ as the clause $e \leftarrow B$.
A non-recursive definite clause $C$ covers $e$ exactly when $C$ θ-subsumes $e \leftarrow B$: $\theta$ maps the head onto
$e$ and every body literal onto a fact. The specific clause above is such an example clause with part of $B$ as its
body.

## Details

### Subsumption is one-way matching

The test differs from <NoteLink to="unification-and-resolution">unification</NoteLink> in two ways. Only the variables
of $C$ may be bound; those of $D$ are renamed apart and treated as constants, so the test is **matching**, not
unification. And $\theta$ must work for all literals of $C$ at once: binding $Z \mapsto \text{bob}$ for one literal
constrains every other literal that contains $Z$. A substitution need not be injective. Several variables of $C$ may map
to the same term, and several literals of $C$ may map onto the same literal of $D$.

### θ-subsumption implies entailment

If $C\theta \subseteq D$ then $C \models D$. A clause is universally quantified, so $C$ entails every instance of it,
in particular $C\theta$. A clause is a disjunction of its literals, and $C\theta \subseteq D$ means $D$ is the
disjunction $C\theta \lor R$ for the remaining literals $R$. Every model of $C\theta$ makes one of its literals true,
hence one of $D$'s, so $C\theta \models D$, and $C \models D$ follows by transitivity.

The converse fails. The clause $C = p(f(X)) \leftarrow p(X)$ entails $D = p(f(f(Y))) \leftarrow p(Y)$: resolving $C$
with a renamed copy of itself gives $D$. No substitution maps $C$ into $D$, because mapping the head forces
$X \mapsto f(Y)$, and then the body becomes $p(f(Y))$, which is not in $D$. Gottlob showed that this gap needs
recursion: if $C$ does not resolve with itself and $D$ is not a tautology, then $C \models D$ implies that $C$
θ-subsumes $D$ <Cite id="gottlob1987" />. For the non-recursive clauses that most rule learners produce, θ-subsumption
and entailment coincide.

### The order, equivalence and reduction

θ-subsumption is reflexive (take the empty substitution) and transitive (compose the substitutions), so it is a
quasi-order. It is not antisymmetric. The clauses $p(X) \leftarrow q(X, Y) \land q(X, Z)$ and
$p(X) \leftarrow q(X, Y)$ θ-subsume each other: $\set{Z \mapsto Y}$ maps the longer onto the shorter, and the empty
substitution maps the shorter into the longer. They cover the same examples, and the literal $q(X, Z)$ adds nothing.

Plotkin proved that every clause has a **reduced** equivalent, obtained by deleting literals while the clause stays
equivalent, and that the reduced form is unique up to renaming of variables <Cite id="plotkin1972thesis" />. On reduced
clauses, modulo renaming, θ-subsumption is a partial order. It is moreover a lattice. The least upper bound of two
clauses is their <NoteLink to="least-general-generalisation">least general generalisation</NoteLink>. For general
clauses the greatest lower bound is their union after renaming the variables of one apart, the least specific clause
that both θ-subsume <Cite id="nienhuyscheng1997" />. The union of two Horn clauses has two heads, so a Horn learner
specialises by unifying the heads instead.

### Refinement under θ-subsumption

Shapiro's downward refinement operator specialises a clause in three ways <Cite id="shapiro1983" />: add a literal whose
arguments are new variables, unify two variables, or replace a variable by a constant or by a compound term with new
variables. Each step gives a clause that the original θ-subsumes. No refinement operator for θ-subsumption is ideal,
that is locally finite, proper and complete at once <Cite id="nienhuyscheng1997" />, so practical systems bound the
clause length and the number of new variables (<NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">generality ordering and
refinement</NoteLink>).

### Testing subsumption is NP-complete

A certificate for $C \succeq D$ is the substitution itself, which is checked in time polynomial in the clause sizes, so
the problem is in NP. It is also NP-hard <Cite id="kapur1986" />.

<Derivation title="NP-hardness by reduction from graph 3-colouring">
Take a graph $G$ with vertices $v_1, \dots, v_n$. Let $C$ be the clause with one variable $X_i$ per vertex and the
body literal $e(X_i, X_j)$ for each edge $\set{v_i, v_j}$. Let $D$ be the clause whose body lists the six ordered pairs
of distinct colours, $e(r, g), e(g, r), e(r, b), e(b, r), e(g, b), e(b, g)$, both with the same nullary head. A
substitution with $C\theta \subseteq D$ maps each $X_i$ to a colour such that every edge of $G$ becomes a pair of
different colours, which is a proper 3-colouring of $G$. Conversely, a proper 3-colouring read as a substitution maps
every literal of $C$ into $D$. So $C$ θ-subsumes $D$ exactly when $G$ is 3-colourable, which is NP-complete. The
reduction is polynomial: $C$ has one literal per edge and $D$ has six.
</Derivation>

Practical subsumption testers treat the problem as constraint satisfaction <Cite id="gottlob1985" />. They map first the
literals of $C$ with the fewest candidate targets, propagate the bindings, and backtrack on failure. Restricted clause
languages are polynomial. In a determinate clause each body literal has at most one match once the variables before
it are bound, so matching proceeds without choice.

## Worked example

The first preset of the figure tests whether
$C = \text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Z) \land \text{parent}(Z, Y)$ θ-subsumes
$D = \text{grandparent}(\text{ann}, \text{dee}) \leftarrow \text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{cid}) \land
\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{bob}) \land \text{parent}(\text{bob}, \text{dee}) \land \text{female}(\text{dee})$.
The literals of $C$ are mapped in order, the head first.

1. **Head.** $\text{grandparent}(X, Y)$ matches $\text{grandparent}(\text{ann}, \text{dee})$ with $X \mapsto \text{ann}$
   and $Y \mapsto \text{dee}$.
2. **First body literal.** $\text{parent}(X, Z)$ is now $\text{parent}(\text{ann}, Z)$. Its first candidate in $D$,
   $\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{cid})$, matches with $Z \mapsto \text{cid}$.
3. **Second body literal.** $\text{parent}(Z, Y)$ is now $\text{parent}(\text{cid}, \text{dee})$. None of the three
   parent literals of $D$ is that literal: three clashes.
4. **Backtrack.** The second literal has no target left, so the search undoes $Z \mapsto \text{cid}$ and gives the first
   body literal its next target, $\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{bob})$, with $Z \mapsto \text{bob}$.
5. **Second body literal again.** $\text{parent}(\text{bob}, \text{dee})$ clashes with the first two parent literals of
   $D$ and matches the third.
6. **Result.** $\theta = \set{X \mapsto \text{ann}, Y \mapsto \text{dee}, Z \mapsto \text{bob}}$ maps all three literals
   of $C$ into $D$. The search took 11 steps after the start: 4 matches, 5 clashes, 1 backtrack and the final
   success.
   $\text{female}(\text{dee})$ is the image of no literal, which is allowed: $D$ may have extra literals.

The other presets show the remaining cases. The daughter pair fails: with $X \mapsto \text{ann}$ and
$Y \mapsto \text{cid}$, $\text{parent}(Y, X)$ needs $\text{parent}(\text{cid}, \text{ann})$, which $D$ lacks. The
recursive pair fails at the body after the head forces $X \mapsto f(Y)$, although $C \models D$. The longer clause maps
both of its $q$ literals onto the single $q(A, B)$, so it subsumes the shorter one and is equivalent to it. The triangle
preset asks for a closed walk of length 3 in a 4-cycle, which does not exist because the cycle is bipartite. The search
needs 227 steps, 176 of them clashes, before it fails: the cost of NP-hard matching on an 8-literal clause.

## Assumptions

- **Clauses are sets.** Order and repetition of literals do not matter, which is why a literal of $D$ can be the image
  of several literals of $C$.
- **Variables of the two clauses are distinct.** $D$'s variables are renamed apart before the test and act as
  constants. $p(X)$ θ-subsumes $p(f(X))$ through $\set{X \mapsto f(X')}$ once $D$'s $X$ is renamed to $X'$. Without
  renaming, the binding $X \mapsto f(X)$ would be circular and the test would wrongly fail.
- **Generality is judged without background knowledge.** Plain θ-subsumption ignores $B$. Relative subsumption, $C
  \succeq_B D$ when $C$ θ-subsumes $D$ extended by the facts of $B$, accounts for background knowledge and underlies the
  <NoteLink to="least-general-generalisation">relative least general generalisation</NoteLink> and the
  <NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">bottom clause</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating subsumption as entailment.** For self-recursive clauses a hypothesis can entail another without
  θ-subsuming it, so a learner that prunes by θ-subsumption may discard a recursive clause that is more general than it
  seems.
- **Counting redundant literals as conditions.** A clause with a literal that a substitution can fold onto another,
  such as $q(X, Z)$ next to $q(X, Y)$ with $Z$ unused, is no more specific. Without reduction, a learner may report and
  score equivalent clauses as different.
- **Underestimating test cost.** Each coverage test of a long clause against a large example clause is an NP-hard
  problem. Clauses with many literals sharing many free variables, as in the triangle preset, can take exponential time;
  bounding new variables and ordering literals by their candidate counts keeps tests fast in practice.
- **Confusing direction.** $C \succeq D$ means $C$ is the more general clause and has the fewer or more abstract
  literals. Specialising a hypothesis moves from $C$ to some $D$ below it.
