<Definition>
**Propositionalisation** transforms a <NoteLink to="multi-table-data">relational learning</NoteLink> problem into an
attribute-value one. The examples are **individuals**, such as customers, each linked to any number of rows in other
tables, such as orders. A set of **first-order features** is constructed, each a query with one free variable for the
individual, such as $f(I) \leftarrow \text{order}(I, O) \land \text{late}(O) \land \text{high}(O)$. Evaluating every
feature on every individual gives a table with one row per individual and one column per feature, on which any
propositional learner can be trained <Cite id="kramer2001propositionalisation" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A churn model predicts, for each customer, whether the customer leaves. The evidence sits in a second table: one
customer has two orders, another has three, a third has one. A <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink>, a
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> or a gradient-boosted ensemble needs a fixed number of
columns per customer. The orders do not fit that shape.

Joining the two tables gives one row per order, with the customer's label copied onto each row. That table has the
wrong unit. A customer who left because of one bad order contributes a good order labelled "churned" as well, and a
model trained on the rows learns that good orders cause churn.

<NoteLink to="inductive-logic-programming">Inductive logic programming</NoteLink> keeps the orders as facts and learns
clauses over them. Propositionalisation keeps the propositional learner and moves the relational work into
<NoteLink to="feature-construction">feature construction</NoteLink>. It asks questions about each customer's set of
orders: "does the customer have a late order?", "how many orders were placed online?", "does the customer have an order
that was both late and expensive?". Each answer is one column. Once the columns exist, any tabular learner applies, with
its tuning tools and its speed.

The last question differs from the first two in a way that decides what can be learnt. "Has a late order and has an
expensive order" can be true of a customer whose late order was cheap and whose expensive order was on time. "Has an
order that was late and expensive" requires both properties of the same order. A feature that names the order with a
variable, $O$, and tests it twice expresses the second. A per-attribute count or a pair of per-attribute features
cannot.

## Details

### Individuals and their parts

Propositionalisation applies to **individual-centred** problems: each example is one object of a target table, and the
other tables describe its parts or its history through a chain of foreign keys. Customers and their orders, molecules
and their atoms, patients and their visits are individual-centred. A problem whose target relates two objects, such as
<NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-rule-mining">link prediction in a knowledge graph</NoteLink>, is not: there is no single
individual to describe.

Each individual owns a set of rows in each child table, of varying size. The same structure appears in
<NoteLink to="multiple-instance-learning">multiple-instance learning</NoteLink>, where each example is a bag of
instances <Cite id="dietterich1997" />. An existential feature "some row of the bag passes these tests" is the standard
multiple-instance assumption written as a feature. Attribute-value, multiple-instance and relational representations
form a hierarchy of increasing expressiveness, and propositionalisation maps a problem one or two levels down it
<Cite id="deraedt2008" />.

### First-order features

A first-order feature is a conjunction of literals with one global variable $I$, the individual, and local variables
for its parts. The feature $f(I) \leftarrow \text{order}(I, O) \land \text{late}(O) \land \text{high}(O)$ has one local
variable, $O$, and is true of a customer when some order of the customer is late and of high value. A feature is
**minimal** when it cannot be split into two features that share no local variable. The conjunction
$\text{order}(I, O_1) \land \text{late}(O_1) \land \text{order}(I, O_2) \land \text{high}(O_2)$ splits into "has a late
order" and "has a high-value order", so it is two features, not one <Cite id="lavrac2020embeddings" />.

For one child table with $m$ categorical attributes of $v$ values each, a feature that tests $j$ distinct attributes
of one row is fixed by choosing the attributes and their values. With at most $k$ tests per feature there are

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{k} \binom{m}{j} v^{j}
$$

candidate features. Three binary attributes and $k = 2$ give $3 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 4 = 18$. The count grows
exponentially in $k$, and longer chains of foreign keys (orders, then the items of each order) multiply it again.

### Constructions

The systems differ in which features they generate and how they filter them <Cite id="krogel2003comparative" />.

| Construction | Features | Example system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attribute tests | tests on the individual's own arguments, with no local variables and no joins | LINUS <Cite id="lavrac1991linus" /> |
| Existential conjunctions | every minimal conjunction allowed by mode declarations, above a support threshold | RSD <Cite id="zelezny2006rsd" /> |
| Aggregation | counts, sums, means, minima and maxima over each child table | RELAGGS <Cite id="krogel2001relaggs" /> |
| Words | `table_attribute_value` items and conjunctions of two items of one row, weighted like text | Wordification <Cite id="perovsek2015wordification" /> |

LINUS, an early system, transformed an <Gloss name="ilp" /> problem into a table, ran a propositional rule learner, and
translated the rules back into clauses. Its features could not introduce local variables or join literals, so a
customer's many orders were out of reach <Cite id="lavrac2020embeddings" />. RSD generates every feature that the user's
mode declarations allow up to a given length, keeps the minimal ones that hold for enough individuals, and passes the
table to <NoteLink to="subgroup-discovery">subgroup discovery</NoteLink> or any other learner. RELAGGS summarises child
tables by aggregate functions, the same operation as <NoteLink to="relational-feature-aggregation">relational and
aggregate features</NoteLink> in <NoteLink to="feature-engineering">feature engineering</NoteLink>. Wordification turns
each individual into a document whose words are its attribute values and pairs of values from one row, and weights them
by <NoteLink to="term-frequency-inverse-document-frequency">TF-IDF</NoteLink>, so text-classification tools apply
<Cite id="lavrac2020embeddings" />.

### Propositionalisation against direct clause learning

An ILP learner such as <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink> searches clauses and evaluates each against the examples as
it goes. Propositionalisation is a static step: it generates the features once, without the labels, and leaves the
search to the propositional learner. A clause whose body is one minimal feature corresponds to a single column. A clause
whose body combines several features corresponds to a conjunction of columns, which a rule or tree learner finds. The
two coincide whenever the target is a <Gloss name="dnf" /> of features that the generator produced. They part when the
target needs a feature longer than the generator's bound, or a relation between two parts, such as two orders placed on
the same day: the generator must have produced that exact feature, while a clause learner can build it from the
literals.

### Practice

Relational data in industry is usually flattened by hand-written aggregates or by deep feature synthesis
(<NoteLink to="automated-feature-engineering">automated feature discovery</NoteLink>). Existential features of the RSD
kind matter where the target depends on a combination of properties of one part, which aggregates of single columns
miss. Lavrač, Škrlj and Robnik-Šikonja show that propositionalisation and
<NoteLink to="dimensionality-reduction">embedding methods</NoteLink> are two forms of one transformation into a table,
and combine Wordification with learned embeddings <Cite id="lavrac2020embeddings" />. Relational deep learning goes
further and trains a <NoteLink to="graph-neural-networks">graph neural network</NoteLink> on the database's row graph,
which learns its own aggregations <Cite id="fey2024" />.

## Worked example

The database in the figure has eight customers, each with a plan (basic or premium) and a label, churned or not, and
fifteen orders, each with a delivery (late or on time), a value (high or low) and a channel (web or store). The labels
follow one rule: a customer churned exactly when one of their orders was late and of high value. Amy, Dan and Fin
churned.

1. **The joined table.** Joining orders to customers gives 15 rows. Amy's two orders are (late, high, web) and
   (on time, low, store); both rows carry Amy's label, churned, so the on-time, low-value order is a positive example.
2. **Single-test features.** With one test per feature there are $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ candidates, such as "has a late
   order". "Has a low-value order" holds of exactly the customers who have a web order, so the coverage filter drops
   it, and 5 remain. Ben's orders are (late, low, store) and (on time, high, web). Amy and Ben both have a late, an
   on-time, a high-value, a low-value, a web and a store order, and both are on the basic plan. Their rows are
   identical, their labels differ, and no learner on this table classifies both correctly.
3. **Counts.** RELAGGS-style counts give Amy and Ben the same vector: 2 orders, 1 late, 1 on time, 1 high, 1 low, 1 web
   and 1 store. Counting each attribute separately loses which values occur together in one order.
4. **Two-test features.** With up to two tests, the $3 \cdot 4 = 12$ pairs join the 6 single tests, and all 12 survive
   the filters, for 17 features. The feature "has an order with delivery = late and value = high" is 1 for Amy, Dan
   and Fin and 0 for the other five: it equals the label. Ben's late order is cheap and his expensive order was on
   time, so the feature is 0 for him.
5. **The learner.** A <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision list</NoteLink> learnt by
   <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">sequential covering</NoteLink> on the 17 features plus the plan needs one rule: if
   the late-and-high feature is 0, the customer stays; otherwise the customer churns. It makes no training error. On the
   single-test table it makes at least one error, at Amy or Ben.
6. **Wordification.** With pairs of words, each customer's document contains `order_delivery_late__order_value_high`
   once for each late high-value order. The vocabulary has 18 words, and the same one-rule list is learnt.

## Assumptions

- **The problem is individual-centred.** Each example is one object whose parts hang off it. Targets that relate two
  objects, or recursive targets such as ancestor, have no such form.
- **The target is a combination of bounded features.** A generator with at most $k$ tests per feature can only supply
  conjunctions of $k$ tests on one part. A target such as "two orders on consecutive days" needs a feature with two
  local variables and a relation between them.
- **Attributes are categorical or discretised.** Equality tests on numbers produce one feature per value. Numeric
  attributes are binned first, or summarised by aggregates.

## Pitfalls

- **Feature explosion.** The candidate count grows as $v^k$ per attribute combination, and most features hold for few
  individuals. With many features and few individuals a propositional learner finds spurious conjunctions; support
  thresholds and <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink> are needed.
- **Aggregates lose co-occurrence.** Counts of single attribute values cannot tell Amy from Ben. Aggregating over
  conditions, such as the count of late high-value orders, recovers it, but that condition is itself a first-order
  feature that someone had to construct.
- **Joining before aggregating multiplies rows.** Two child tables joined to the customer before aggregation give one
  row per pair of child rows, and every sum is inflated
  (<NoteLink to="relational-feature-aggregation">relational and aggregate features</NoteLink>).
- **Leakage through child rows.** Features computed from orders placed after the moment of prediction leak the future
  into training (<NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>). Every child table needs a time cutoff per
  example.
- **Static features miss the target.** The generator runs before the learner and does not see the labels, so a feature
  one test longer than the bound is never produced. A learner that searches clauses directly, such as
  <NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">Progol</NoteLink>, extends its hypotheses where the data demand it.
