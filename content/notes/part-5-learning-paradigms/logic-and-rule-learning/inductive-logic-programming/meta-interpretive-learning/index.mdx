<Definition>
A **metarule** is a second-order <NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">Horn clause</NoteLink> whose predicate
symbols are variables, such as the **chain** metarule $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, C) \land R(C, B)$, with predicate
variables $P, Q, R$ and first-order variables $A, B, C$. **Meta-interpretive learning** proves the positive examples
with a **meta-interpreter**, a Prolog program that executes other programs. When a goal cannot be proved from the
background knowledge or the program built so far, the meta-interpreter may instantiate a metarule, binding its predicate
variables to predicate symbols. Each instantiation, a **metasubstitution**, is a clause of the learnt program. A program
that proves every positive example and no negative one is a solution <Cite id="muggleton2014mil" />
<Cite id="muggleton2015mil" />. **Metagol** is the standard implementation.
</Definition>

## Problem

Clause-by-clause learners such as <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">Progol</NoteLink> score each clause on its own. A recursive definition of
ancestor needs two clauses that only work together: the recursive clause covers no example until the base case exists,
and the base case alone covers only the parent pairs. A definition of great-grandparent in clauses of two body literals
needs a helper predicate, grandparent, that the background does not provide. Neither the helper nor its use scores
well alone, so a greedy clause search does not find them.

Meta-interpretive learning searches for whole programs. It treats learning as proving: a program is a solution when the
examples follow from it, so the learner runs a proof of the examples and lets the proof build the program it needs. The
metarules fix the shapes clauses may take, which is a strong and explicit language bias. Within that bias, recursion
and new predicates come for free: a metarule's body may call the target itself, or a predicate that does not exist yet
and is defined by another metarule instance.

## Mechanism

### Metarules

Most work uses a small set of dyadic metarules, over predicates with two arguments <Cite id="cropper2020metarules" />.

| Name | Metarule |
| --- | --- |
| ident | $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, B)$ |
| inverse | $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(B, A)$ |
| precon | $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A) \land R(A, B)$ |
| postcon | $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, B) \land R(B)$ |
| chain | $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, C) \land R(C, B)$ |
| tailrec | $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, C) \land P(C, B)$ |

Chain with $R = P$ is tailrec, so ident and chain together can express the recursive ancestor definition. The choice
of metarules is the choice of hypothesis language. Cropper and Tourret show that some sets can be reduced to smaller
ones that derive the same clauses <Cite id="cropper2020metarules" />.

### The meta-interpreter

The learner proves a list of goals while threading a program $\Pi$, a list of metasubstitutions:

1. **No goals left.** Succeed with $\Pi$.
2. **Background goal.** If the first goal's predicate is in the background knowledge, prove it from $B$ and continue
   with the rest.
3. **Reuse a clause.** Otherwise, take a metasubstitution of $\Pi$ whose head predicate is the goal's predicate, unify
   the goal with the head of its metarule, and prove the metarule's body followed by the rest.
4. **New clause.** If $\Pi$ has fewer than $n$ clauses, take a metarule, bind $P$ to the goal's predicate, add the new
   metasubstitution to $\Pi$, and prove its body followed by the rest. The body's predicate variables are bound while
   the body is proved: to a background predicate, to the target, or to a new **invented** predicate whose definition is
   itself a new clause.

The positives are proved together, so a clause added for one example is reused for the next. Backtracking enumerates
alternative programs. When every positive is proved, the program is checked against the negatives with no new clauses
allowed; a program that proves a negative is rejected and the search backtracks. The whole search runs under
**iterative deepening** on $n$: programs of 1 clause first, then 2, and so on. The first solution found is therefore a
program with the fewest clauses <Cite id="muggleton2015mil" />.

### Termination

A metarule such as tailrec can recurse forever when the proof chooses badly. Metagol imposes an order on predicate
symbols and allows a clause to call only predicates lower in the order, except through a recursive metarule whose
recursion must decrease its first argument in an order on constants <Cite id="muggleton2015mil" />. Implementations also
bound the proof depth. The figure uses a depth bound and forbids left recursion, a clause whose first body literal calls
its own head.

### Size of the hypothesis space

A metarule with $j$ body literals has $j + 1$ predicate variables. With $p$ predicate symbols available and $m$
metarules, there are at most $m \cdot p^{j + 1}$ distinct clauses, and so at most $\paren{m p^{j + 1}}^n$ programs of
$n$ clauses. The space is exponential in the program size $n$, which is why iterative deepening, starting from small
programs, matters, and why every extra metarule or predicate symbol has a cost <Cite id="cropper2020metarules" />.

## Worked example

The background holds the six parent facts of the family: Ann is the parent of Bob and Cid, Bob of Dee and Eve, Cid of
Fay, Eve of Gus. The metarules are ident and chain. The target is ancestor, with positives (ann, bob), (ann, dee),
(ann, gus) and (cid, fay), and negatives (bob, ann), (dee, bob) and (cid, gus).

1. **One clause, ident.** Proving $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{bob})$ adds $\text{ancestor}(A, B) \leftarrow
   Q(A, B)$ and binds $Q$ to parent, because $\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{bob})$ is a fact. Reusing the clause for
   $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{dee})$ fails: Dee is not Ann's child. No other clause may be added.
2. **One clause, chain.** $\text{ancestor}(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, C) \land R(C, B)$ binds $Q$ to parent with
   $C = \text{bob}$, and then needs $R(\text{bob}, \text{bob})$. Parent fails. Binding $R$ to ancestor makes the clause
   recursive, but with a single clause there is no base case, and every branch ends at a person with no child. The
   one-clause search takes 29 events and finds no program.
3. **Two clauses.** The ident clause with parent again proves $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{bob})$. For
   $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{dee})$ the learner first adds a second ident clause. Its only possible binding,
   parent, would duplicate the first clause, so it is dropped, and a chain clause is tried.
4. **Chain with parent and parent.** $\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{bob})$ and $\text{parent}(\text{bob},
   \text{dee})$ prove $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{dee})$. Reusing the clauses for
   $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{gus})$ fails: Gus is three generations below Ann.
5. **Chain with parent and ancestor.** Backtracking rebinds $R$ to ancestor:
   $\text{ancestor}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, C) \land \text{ancestor}(C, B)$. Now
   $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{dee})$ follows from $\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{bob})$ and the base clause
   for (bob, dee), $\text{ancestor}(\text{ann}, \text{gus})$ from the chain Ann, Bob, Eve, Gus, and
   $\text{ancestor}(\text{cid}, \text{fay})$ from the base clause.
6. **Negatives.** Neither (bob, ann), (dee, bob) nor (cid, gus) follows from the two clauses, so the program is
   accepted at event 68:

```prolog
ancestor(A, B) :- parent(A, B).
ancestor(A, B) :- parent(A, C), ancestor(C, B).
```

The great-grandparent task shows invention. The only positive is (ann, gus), and the negatives are (ann, eve), (bob,
gus) and (ann, dee). No single clause with two body literals over parent works. With two clauses, the learner first
tries the recursive program $\text{greatgrandparent}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, C) \land
\text{greatgrandparent}(C, B)$ with the base case $\text{greatgrandparent}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(A, B)$. It
proves the positive, and it also proves the negative (ann, eve), so it is rejected. So is the variant with a
grandparent-shaped base case, which proves (ann, eve) through the base case alone. The accepted program invents a
predicate:

```prolog
greatgrandparent(A, B) :- parent(A, C), inv1(C, B).
inv1(A, B) :- parent(A, C), parent(C, B).
```

$\text{inv1}$ is grandparent, a predicate the learner was never given (<NoteLink to="predicate-invention">predicate
invention</NoteLink>).

## Cost

- **Exponential in program size.** The number of programs grows as $(m p^{j + 1})^n$, so programs beyond about 5 to
  10 clauses are out of reach for plain Metagol. Iterative deepening repeats the work of smaller bounds at each level,
  a constant-factor overhead.
- **Each proof is a search.** Binding an open predicate tries every background predicate, the target and the invented
  ones, and each choice runs a proof. Background predicates with many facts multiply the branching.
- **Few examples.** The examples are proved, not counted, so a handful of examples is typical. Recursive programs are
  often learnt from one to five positives.

## Trade-offs

- **Whole programs against single clauses.** Proving the examples together finds mutually dependent clauses, recursion
  and invented predicates. Clause-by-clause learners scale to more examples and tolerate noise better.
- **Metarules as bias.** A user who knows the shape of the solution writes few metarules and gets a fast search; the
  wrong metarules make the target inexpressible. Choosing them is the main design decision
  <Cite id="cropper2020metarules" />.
- **Exact consistency.** Metagol requires every positive to be proved and no negative. One mislabelled example makes
  the target unlearnable, and Metagol returns a larger program that fits the error, or none.
- **Successors.** <NoteLink to="learning-from-failures">Popper</NoteLink> drops metarules for a declaration of
  predicates and size bounds, and turns each failed program into constraints that prune whole regions of the program
  space <Cite id="cropper2021popper" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Too many metarules.** Each metarule adds $p^{j+1}$ clauses to the choices at every level of the search, and the
  number of programs grows as the $n$-th power of the number of clauses. Adding precon, postcon and inverse to ident
  and chain multiplies the search for a 3-clause program many times over on the same task.
- **Non-terminating proofs.** Without ordering constraints or a depth bound, a recursive metarule can call itself
  forever. Left-recursive clauses must be excluded.
- **Uninformative negatives.** Negatives that no plausible program proves do not constrain the search. The
  great-grandparent negatives were chosen to reject the recursive programs: without (ann, eve) the learner would accept
  the recursive ancestor-like program, which is wrong.
- **Invented predicates without meaning.** The learner names them inv1, inv2. An invented predicate can be correct
  for the training examples without matching any concept a person would name.
