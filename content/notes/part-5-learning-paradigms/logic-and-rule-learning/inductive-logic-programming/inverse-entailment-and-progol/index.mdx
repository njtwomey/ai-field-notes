<Definition>
**Inverse entailment** turns the requirement that a clause $H$, with background knowledge $B$, entails an example $e$
into a bound on $H$. Muggleton's **bottom clause** $\bot(B, e)$ is the most specific clause, within a language bias,
that together with $B$ entails $e$; every clause $H$ the learner considers must θ-subsume it
<Cite id="muggleton1995progol" />. **Progol** learns a program by <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">sequential
covering</NoteLink>: it picks an uncovered positive example, builds its bottom clause by **saturation** under user-given
**mode declarations**, searches the clauses between the empty body and $\bot(B, e)$ for the one with the best
compression score, adds that clause and removes the positives it covers. **Aleph** is a widely used reimplementation of
the same scheme <Cite id="srinivasan2001aleph" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A top-down learner such as <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink> starts from an empty body and considers every literal
the background predicates allow. Most of those literals are irrelevant to every example, and the greedy choice can
commit to one of them early. A bottom-up learner based on the
<NoteLink to="least-general-generalisation">relative least general generalisation</NoteLink> uses only literals that
the examples share, but the relative lgg of two examples over a large background has a number of literals that grows
with the square of the background, and with its $n$-th power for $n$ examples.

Progol takes the useful part of each. It builds a specific clause from one example, as a bottom-up learner does, but
only out of literals the language bias allows and the background makes true of that example. It then searches top-down,
from the empty body, but only among clauses whose literals come from that specific clause. The bottom clause bounds the
search from below, and the empty body from above, so the search space is finite and every candidate is relevant to at
least one positive example.

## Mechanism

### Inverting entailment

A clause $H$ explains an example $e$ when $B \land H \models e$. By contraposition this holds exactly when
$B \land \neg e \models \neg H$. For a clause $H$, the negation $\neg H$ is, after replacing its variables by new
constants, a conjunction of ground literals. Let $\neg\bot$ be the conjunction of all ground literals true in every
model of $B \land \neg e$. Every literal of $\neg H$ is among them, so $\neg\bot \models \neg H$, and by contraposition
$H \models \bot$: every hypothesis that explains $e$ is at least as general as $\bot$. Muggleton restricted this
condition to θ-subsumption: Progol searches only clauses $H$ with $H \succeq \bot$ <Cite id="muggleton1995progol" />.
For a <NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">definite clause</NoteLink>, $\bot$ has the example as its head and,
as its body, the ground facts derivable from $B$ that concern the example, with constants replaced
<NoteLink to="cyclic-factor-and-by-terms">by variables</NoteLink>.

The restriction to θ-subsumption makes the search finite and decidable. It also makes it incomplete: a clause that
entails the example only through a recursive call to itself need not θ-subsume the bottom clause, so Progol can miss
some recursive definitions.

### Mode declarations and saturation

Mode declarations fix which literals may appear. A head mode such as `modeh(daughter(+person, +person))` says the target
is called with both arguments bound. A body mode such as `modeb(parent(+person, -person))` says a parent literal may be
added when its first argument is a person already in the clause, and binds its second argument as a new person. An
argument marked `#` must be a constant. Progol's modes also carry a **recall**, the number of answers of a literal to
keep, and a type for each argument.

**Saturation** builds $\bot(B, e)$ for a ground example $e$:

1. **Head.** Replace the constants in $e$'s input positions by variables, one per distinct constant. Those constants
   are the **known terms**.
2. **Layer $i$.** For each body mode, fill its input positions with known terms of the right types in every possible
   way, and query $B$ for the answers. Each answer gives a ground literal. Replace its constants by variables, the same
   constant always by the same variable, and add it to the body if it is new.
3. **New terms.** The constants in output positions of the new literals join the known terms for the next layer.
4. **Stop** after a fixed number of layers, the **variable depth** $i$, or when no literal is new.

### Searching above the bottom clause

Every clause in the search has the head of $\bot$ and a body that is a subset of $\bot$'s body. A literal may join a
body only when its input variables already occur in the head or in an earlier literal, which keeps clauses connected.
The search runs general to specific. Progol uses a best-first search guided by a compression measure that rewards the
positives a clause covers and penalises the negatives it covers and its length <Cite id="muggleton1995progol" />. Aleph
offers several orders and scores; its compression score is

$$
f(C) = P - N - L + 1,
$$

with $P$ and $N$ the positive and negative examples $C$ covers and $L$ the number of literals in $C$, including the
head <Cite id="srinivasan2001aleph" />. Coverage is computed by running each example as a query against $B \cup
\set{C}$.

Two pruning rules follow from the generality order. A clause that covers no negative is not refined, because adding
literals cannot cover more positives and only lengthens it. A clause $C$ whose refinements can score at most
$P - 0 - (L + 1) + 1 = P - L$ is pruned when that bound does not beat the best score found so far.

### The covering loop

1. While some positive example is uncovered: pick one, the **seed**.
2. Saturate it to $\bot(B, e)$ with depth $i$.
3. Search the clauses above $\bot(B, e)$ and keep the best one that meets the noise bound (the number of negatives a
   clause may cover).
4. Add it to the program and remove the positives it covers.

Aleph's defaults bound the variable depth to 2 and the clause length to 4 literals <Cite id="srinivasan2001aleph" />.

## Worked example

The background is the family database: Ann is the parent of Bob and Cid, Bob of Dee and Eve, Cid of Fay, Eve of Gus;
Ann, Cid, Dee, Eve and Fay are female. The target is daughter, with positives (cid, ann), (dee, bob), (eve, bob) and
(fay, cid), and negatives (bob, ann), (gus, eve), (ann, cid) and (dee, ann). The modes are
`daughter(+person, +person)` for the head and `female(+person)`, `parent(+person, -person)` and
`parent(-person, +person)` for the body, with variable depth 1.

1. **Seed and head.** The seed is $\text{daughter}(\text{cid}, \text{ann})$. The head is $\text{daughter}(A, B)$ with
   $A$ for Cid and $B$ for Ann; the known terms are Cid and Ann.
2. **female(+person).** Both Ann and Cid are female: $\text{female}(B)$ and $\text{female}(A)$.
3. **parent(+person, −person).** Ann's children are Bob and Cid, giving $\text{parent}(B, C)$ with $C$ for Bob and
   $\text{parent}(B, A)$. Cid's child Fay gives $\text{parent}(A, D)$ with $D$ for Fay.
4. **parent(−person, +person).** Cid's parent is Ann, which gives $\text{parent}(B, A)$ again, and Ann has no parent.
   No new literal.
5. **Bottom clause.** $\text{daughter}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{female}(B) \land \text{female}(A) \land
   \text{parent}(B, C) \land \text{parent}(B, A) \land \text{parent}(A, D)$. Its five body literals span a lattice of
   $2^5 = 32$ clauses.
6. **Top of the search.** The empty body covers all 4 positives and 4 negatives: $f = 4 - 4 - 1 + 1 = 0$.
   $\text{female}(A)$ covers 4 positives and 2 negatives, (ann, cid) and (dee, ann), so $f = 4 - 2 - 2 + 1 = 1$.
   $\text{parent}(B, A)$ also scores 1: it covers the negatives (bob, ann) and (gus, eve).
7. **Best clause.** The twelfth clause evaluated, $\text{daughter}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{female}(A) \land
   \text{parent}(B, A)$, covers 4 positives and no negative: $f = 4 - 0 - 3 + 1 = 2$. It is not refined.
8. **Pruning.** $\text{parent}(B, C) \land \text{parent}(B, A)$ covers 4 positives and 2 negatives with 3 literals.
   Any refinement scores at most $4 - 3 = 1 < 2$, so it is pruned. The search evaluates 24 of the 32 clauses and
   returns the best one, which covers every positive, so the covering loop stops.

With variable depth 2 the bottom clause grows to 8 body literals, adding $\text{female}(D)$ and Bob's two children, and
the search evaluates 32 clauses to reach the same answer. Each seed in the figure has a different bottom clause, of 4 or
5 literals, and every one leads to the same clause.

## Cost

- **Saturation.** The bottom clause grows with the number of answers of each mode and with the variable depth: each
  layer can multiply the known terms by the fan-out of the relations. Recall bounds and depths of 2 or 3 keep it to
  tens or hundreds of literals.
- **Search.** With $k$ literals in the bottom clause, the lattice has up to $2^k$ clauses; a clause-length bound of
  $m$ limits it to $\sum_{j \le m} \binom{k}{j}$. Pruning by coverage removes most of them in practice.
- **Coverage tests.** Each clause is tested against every example by <NoteLink to="unification-and-resolution">SLD
  resolution</NoteLink>, with a depth bound. On large example sets the tests dominate, and Aleph can sample examples or
  cache coverage.
- **Covering loop.** One saturation and one search per clause learnt, so the cost scales with the number of clauses in
  the final program rather than with the number of examples.

## Trade-offs

- **Bounded complete search against greedy search.** Within the bottom clause and the length bound, the search finds
  the best-scoring clause, including clauses whose literals pay off only together. FOIL's hill climbing is faster and
  can miss them.
- **Depends on the seed.** The bottom clause contains only literals true of the seed. An atypical seed yields a bottom
  clause that lacks the literals the target needs, and the clause found covers few other positives. Aleph can saturate
  several seeds and keep the best clause.
- **Mode declarations as prior knowledge.** Modes encode what the user knows about how predicates are called. Good
  modes make the search small; wrong modes exclude the target.
- **Clause by clause.** Like FOIL, Progol learns one clause at a time, so it struggles with recursive programs whose
  base case and recursive case must be learnt together, and it cannot invent predicates.
  <NoteLink to="meta-interpretive-learning">Meta-interpretive learning</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="learning-from-failures">learning from failures</NoteLink> search whole programs instead.

## Pitfalls

- **Bottom clauses that are too large.** A depth of 3 over a dense background can produce thousands of literals, and
  every coverage test of a long clause is an expensive θ-subsumption problem. Start with depth 1 or 2 and tight recall.
- **Missing literals from the background.** Saturation reads facts from $B$. A fact missing about the seed removes the
  literal from the bottom clause and from every clause above it.
- **Score ties.** Compression ties are common on small data; on the daughter task, $\text{female}(A)$ and
  $\text{parent}(B, A)$ both score 1. The result then depends on the search order.
- **Noise bound set to zero.** With no tolerated negatives, one mislabelled negative example rules out the right
  clause, and Progol returns a long clause that steers around it, or the seed itself as a fact.
