<Definition>
A knowledge graph is a set of facts $r(s, o)$, each a relation $r$ between a subject $s$ and an object $o$. A **closed
rule** $B_1 \land \dots \land B_k \Rightarrow r(x, y)$ is a <NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">Horn
clause</NoteLink> over binary atoms in which every variable occurs at least twice and the atoms are connected through
shared variables. For the set of pairs $(x, y)$ for which the body holds, the rule's **support** is the number of those
pairs with $r(x, y)$ in the graph; its **head coverage** is the support divided by the number of $r$ facts; its
**standard confidence** is the support divided by the number of body pairs; and its **PCA confidence** divides the
support only by the body pairs whose subject $x$ has at least one $r$ fact, following the **partial completeness
assumption** <Cite id="galarraga2013amie" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Knowledge graphs such as Wikidata hold hundreds of millions of facts, and they are incomplete in a specific way. A
missing fact is unknown, not false (<NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-data">knowledge graph data</NoteLink>). The
negatives of classical <NoteLink to="inductive-logic-programming">inductive logic programming</NoteLink> come from the
closed-world assumption, which reads every missing fact as false. On a knowledge graph that penalises a correct rule
for every fact it predicts that nobody has recorded yet, which is exactly the use a rule is wanted for.

Rule mining on knowledge graphs answers two needs. It finds regularities, such as "spouses live in the same city", that
explain the graph to a person. And it predicts missing links, as <NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-embeddings">knowledge
graph embeddings</NoteLink> do, with each prediction justified by a rule. The miners keep the clause language of
<Gloss name="ilp" /> but change three things: they restrict the rule shape so the search scales, they treat absence with
a weaker assumption than the closed world, and they mine every rule above a threshold rather than one program, as
association-rule mining does for itemsets.

## Mechanism

### The language

Rules have binary atoms whose arguments are variables, at most a few atoms long. AMIE mines rules of at most three
atoms, the head included, by default <Cite id="galarraga2013amie" />. A **path rule** of length 2 links $x$ to $y$
through one intermediate variable $z$:

$$
s(x, z) \land t(z, y) \Rightarrow r(x, y),
$$

and its variants with $s(z, x)$ or $t(y, z)$ reverse an atom's direction. Length-1 rules $s(x, y) \Rightarrow r(x, y)$
and $s(y, x) \Rightarrow r(x, y)$ capture sub-relations, synonyms and inverse or symmetric relations. Requiring closed
rules means every rule predicts a specific pair $(x, y)$ rather than a dangling existential.

### The partial completeness assumption

Write $\text{body}(R)$ for the pairs $(x, y)$ for which the body of rule $R$ holds, and $K_r$ for the $r$ facts of the
graph. The three measures are

$$
\text{supp}(R) = \abs{\text{body}(R) \cap K_r}, \qquad
\text{conf}(R) = \frac{\text{supp}(R)}{\abs{\text{body}(R)}}, \qquad
\text{pca}(R) = \frac{\text{supp}(R)}{\abs{\set{(x, y) \in \text{body}(R) : \exists y'\, r(x, y') \in K_r}}}.
$$

Standard confidence treats every body pair without a head fact as a counter-example: the closed world. The partial
completeness assumption says that if the graph knows at least one $r$ fact about a subject $x$, it knows all of them.
A prediction $r(x, y)$ for such an $x$ is then a genuine counter-example. A prediction for a subject with no $r$ fact is
unknown and left out of the denominator <Cite id="galarraga2013amie" />. The assumption suits relations with few
objects per subject, such as birthplace or citizenship, and fails for relations such as "acted in" whose coverage per
subject is patchy.

### Search and pruning

AMIE grows rules from a head atom $r(x, y)$ with three <NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">refinement
operators</NoteLink> <Cite id="galarraga2013amie" />:

- **Dangling atom.** Add an atom with one existing and one new variable, such as $s(x, z)$.
- **Instantiated atom.** Add an atom with one existing variable and a constant.
- **Closing atom.** Add an atom whose two variables already occur, which can close the rule.

Support never increases when an atom is added, because the body can only hold for fewer pairs. Head coverage is
support divided by a constant, so it is anti-monotone too, and AMIE prunes every rule whose head coverage falls below a
threshold together with all its refinements. AMIE's defaults are a head coverage of 0.01, a PCA confidence of 0.1 and
three atoms. The supports are computed by joins over an in-memory index of the facts; AMIE+ and AMIE 3 add bounds that
skip confidence computations and parallel, exact counting <Cite id="galarraga2015amieplus" />
<Cite id="lajus2020amie3" />.

<Derivation title="Why support is anti-monotone">
Let $R'$ be $R$ with one more body atom $A$. A pair $(x, y)$ is in $\text{body}(R')$ when some binding of the other
variables makes every atom of $R$'s body and $A$ true. The same binding makes every atom of $R$'s body true, so
$(x, y) \in \text{body}(R)$. Hence $\text{body}(R') \subseteq \text{body}(R)$, and intersecting both with $K_r$ gives
$\text{supp}(R') \le \text{supp}(R)$. A rule below the support or head coverage threshold therefore has no refinement
above it, and its whole subtree can be skipped, as in <NoteLink to="pattern-mining">frequent itemset mining</NoteLink>.
</Derivation>

### Sampling instead of enumerating: AnyBURL

AnyBURL works bottom-up <Cite id="meilicke2019anyburl" />. It samples a random path in the graph that starts with a
fact $r(a, b)$, such as $r(a, b), s(a, c), t(c, b)$, and generalises the path into the rules it instantiates, such as
$s(x, z) \land t(z, y) \Rightarrow r(x, y)$, plus acyclic rules with constants. Each rule's confidence is estimated on a
sample of its body pairs. Longer runs sample more paths and find more rules, so it is an anytime algorithm and can stop
at any time budget.

### Predicting links

To answer a query $r(x, ?)$, every rule with head $r$ proposes the objects $y$ for which its body holds and $r(x, y)$ is
missing. AnyBURL ranks candidates by **maximum aggregation**: by the confidence of the best rule that proposes them,
with ties broken by the next-best rule <Cite id="meilicke2019anyburl" />. The ranking is evaluated like an
<NoteLink to="text-embedding-models">embedding model</NoteLink>'s, by filtered mean reciprocal rank and hits at $k$
(<NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-and-mean-reciprocal-rank">mean average precision and mean reciprocal
rank</NoteLink>).

### Differentiable rule learners

Neural LP writes a path rule as a product of relation adjacency matrices, so that $M_s M_t$ counts the paths from $x$ to
$y$ through $s$ then $t$. It learns soft attention weights over which relations to multiply, end to end by
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>, and reads rules off the weights
<Cite id="yang2017neurallp" />. DRUM keeps the matrix view and adds low-rank, recurrent parameterisations that represent
several rules for one head <Cite id="sadeghian2019drum" />. Both are forms of
<NoteLink to="differentiable-inductive-logic-programming">differentiable inductive logic programming</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

The figure's graph has 14 entities and 26 facts over five relations: marriedTo, hasChild, livesIn, locatedIn and
citizenOf. It is incomplete on purpose: Fay has no home and no citizenship, Gus has no citizenship, and only Eli's side
of his marriage to Fay is recorded. Dia is a French citizen living in Rome, and Hal lives in Milan, away from his
parents in Rome.

**The citizenship rule** $\text{livesIn}(x, z) \land \text{locatedIn}(z, y) \Rightarrow \text{citizenOf}(x, y)$:

1. **Body pairs.** Seven people have a home, and each home city lies in one country: (ann, france), (ben, france),
   (cal, italy), (dia, italy), (eli, france), (gus, france) and (hal, italy).
2. **Support.** Five pairs are citizenOf facts: Ann, Ben, Cal, Eli and Hal. $\text{supp} = 5$, and the graph has 6
   citizenOf facts, so the head coverage is $5 / 6 = 0.83$.
3. **Standard confidence.** $5 / 7 = 0.71$. The two other pairs, (dia, italy) and (gus, france), count as errors.
4. **PCA confidence.** Gus has no citizenOf fact at all, so (gus, france) is unknown and leaves the denominator. Dia
   has one, France, so (dia, italy) is a counter-example. $\text{pca} = 5 / 6 = 0.83$.
5. **Predictions.** $\text{citizenOf}(\text{gus}, \text{france})$, plausible, and $\text{citizenOf}(\text{dia},
   \text{italy})$, which the graph contradicts under the partial completeness assumption.

**The symmetry rule** $\text{marriedTo}(y, x) \Rightarrow \text{marriedTo}(x, y)$ has five body pairs, the reverses of
the five marriage facts. Four are facts, so $\text{conf} = 4 / 5 = 0.80$. The fifth, (fay, eli), concerns Fay, who has
no marriedTo fact, so $\text{pca} = 4 / 4 = 1.00$ and the rule predicts $\text{marriedTo}(\text{fay}, \text{eli})$ with
the highest score.

**A spurious rule.** $\text{livesIn}(x, z) \land \text{livesIn}(y, z) \Rightarrow \text{marriedTo}(x, y)$, "people who
live in the same city are married", has support 4 of 5 and PCA confidence $4 / 6 = 0.67$. Its counter-examples are
Ann and Ben's son Gus, who lives with them: the rule predicts that Ann is married to Gus. Small graphs make such
coincidences frequent, which is why thresholds on support and confidence, and type constraints on the variables,
matter.

## Cost

- **Rules.** With $\abs{\mathcal{R}}$ relations there are $2\abs{\mathcal{R}}$ length-1 bodies and
  $4\abs{\mathcal{R}}^2$ length-2 path bodies per head relation, so $O(\abs{\mathcal{R}}^3)$ path rules of three
  atoms. Each extra atom multiplies the count by about $\abs{\mathcal{R}}$ times the number of argument patterns.
- **Support counting.** Each candidate needs a join of the body's relations. A relation with hub entities, such as a
  country with millions of residents, makes the join large; the confidence denominator is the expensive part, and the
  AMIE+ bounds avoid computing it for rules that cannot pass <Cite id="galarraga2015amieplus" />.
- **Sampling.** AnyBURL's cost is set by its time budget rather than by the size of the rule space, at the price of
  missing rare rules and estimating confidence from samples.

## Trade-offs

- **Rules against embeddings.** A rule states its reason and applies to entities never seen in training if their
  neighbourhood matches. Embeddings capture graded similarity that no short rule expresses. AnyBURL's rule-based
  predictions are competitive with embedding models on standard link-prediction benchmarks
  <Cite id="meilicke2019anyburl" />.
- **Exhaustive against sampled.** AMIE finds every rule above the thresholds, which suits explaining a graph. AnyBURL
  trades completeness for speed and suits link prediction under a time limit.
- **Standard against PCA confidence.** PCA confidence is the better estimate of precision on incomplete graphs for
  functional relations. On relations that are not functional, it overestimates, because one recorded object stops
  counting as evidence that the others are absent.
- **Relation to FOIL.** The miners use the same clause language as <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink>, but they mine
  all rules above thresholds rather than a covering set, and they never read missing facts as negatives
  <Cite id="quinlan1990" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Inverse relations leak.** A graph that stores both hasChild and its inverse yields length-1 rules with confidence 1
  that predict test facts from their own inverses, inflating link-prediction scores
  (<NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-data">knowledge graph data</NoteLink>).
- **Direction of the assumption.** The partial completeness assumption is applied to the subject. For a relation that is
  more complete from the object side, such as hasChild read from the child, the measure must use the object instead.
- **Redundant rules.** Variants that differ only in an atom's direction, such as marriedTo$(x, z)$ and marriedTo$(z, x)$
  in the spouse-home rule, appear as separate rules with near-identical scores and inflate rule counts.
- **Coincidences on small or skewed graphs.** A rule with support 2 can reach confidence 1 by chance. A support
  threshold of a few facts per rule, and evaluation on held-out facts, separate regularities from coincidences.
