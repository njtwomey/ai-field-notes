<Definition>
An **inductive logic programming** problem has three inputs: **background knowledge** $B$, a logic program; a set
$E^+$ of **positive examples**; and a set $E^-$ of **negative examples**, both sets of ground atoms of a **target
predicate**. A solution is a **hypothesis** $H$, a set of definite clauses from a **hypothesis space** $\Hcal$, that is
**complete**, $B \cup H \models e$ for every $e \in E^+$, and **consistent**, $B \cup H \not\models e$ for every
$e \in E^-$ <Cite id="muggleton1991ilp" />. The examples are covered by entailment, so the learnt program can name
objects through variables, chain relations and call itself.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A propositional rule learner sees each example as a row of a table with fixed columns. "Bob is a grandparent of Gus"
fits no such row. The fact relates two people, and it is true because of a third person, Eve, who is Bob's child and
Gus's parent. The relevant evidence lives in other facts of a database, and a different example may need a different
number of them.

<Gloss name="ilp" /> keeps the data as facts and writes hypotheses as clauses with variables:

$$
\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Z) \land \text{parent}(Z, Y).
$$

The variable $Z$ stands for whichever person links $X$ and $Y$. One clause covers every grandparent pair in any family,
of any size. A clause can also mention its own head, so a two-clause program defines ancestor for chains of any length.
Rule learners over attribute vectors cannot express either, unless the relevant joins are computed in advance as
features (<NoteLink to="propositionalisation-and-relational-learning">propositionalisation and relational
learning</NoteLink>).

The background knowledge does two jobs. It holds the facts about the examples, here the parent relation. It also
supplies the vocabulary of the hypotheses: a learner can only write clauses over predicates that $B$ defines, so a
user who adds a well-chosen predicate to $B$ makes a hard concept easy to learn. Few examples often suffice, because a
short clause that explains every positive and no negative is rarely an accident.

## Details

### Problem setting

<NoteLink to="propositional-and-first-order-logic-for-learning">Propositional and first-order logic for
learning</NoteLink> defines coverage: $H$ covers an example $e$ when $B \cup H \models e$. Muggleton and De Raedt's
**normal setting** states four conditions on a solution <Cite id="muggleton1994ilp" />.

1. **Prior satisfiability.** $B$ together with the negation of every negative example is consistent: the background
   does not already entail a negative.
2. **Posterior satisfiability.** $B \cup H$ together with the negations of the negatives is consistent: $B \cup H$
   entails no negative example.
3. **Prior necessity.** $B \not\models E^+$: some positive is not already explained, so there is something to learn.
4. **Posterior sufficiency.** $B \cup H \models E^+$: the hypothesis explains every positive.

For definite clauses, entailment of a ground atom is membership in the least Herbrand model of $B \cup H$
(<NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">Horn clauses and logic programs</NoteLink>). A learner tests coverage by
running each example as a query with <NoteLink to="unification-and-resolution">SLD resolution</NoteLink>, usually with a
depth or step bound, because a candidate recursive program may not terminate.

When the negatives are not given, most systems take them from the **closed-world assumption**: every ground atom of the
target that is not a positive is negative. With $c$ constants, a binary target has $c^2$ ground atoms. Seven people give
49 grandparent pairs, and the 4 true ones leave 45 closed-world negatives.

### The hypothesis space and its bias

The space of all clauses over the predicates of $B$ is infinite: a body can add literals with new variables without end.
Every ILP system fixes a **language bias** that makes the space finite or searchable
<Cite id="nienhuyscheng1997" />.

- **Size bounds.** At most $k$ body literals, $v$ variables or $m$ clauses.
- **Mode declarations.** Each argument of a predicate is marked input (bound before the literal is called), output
  (bound by it) or constant. `parent(+person, -person)` may only extend a clause from a person already named.
- **Metarules.** Second-order templates such as $P(A, B) \leftarrow Q(A, C) \land R(C, B)$, whose predicate variables
  $P, Q, R$ the learner fills in (<NoteLink to="meta-interpretive-learning">meta-interpretive learning</NoteLink>).
- **Types and determinacy.** Arguments carry types, and some systems allow only literals whose output is determined by
  their input.

The bias is the <NoteLink to="inductive-bias">inductive bias</NoteLink> of the learner. A bias that is too weak makes
the search intractable, and one that is too strong excludes the target.

### Searching the space

Clauses are ordered by generality. The order used in practice is
<NoteLink to="theta-subsumption">θ-subsumption</NoteLink>: $C$ is at least as general as $D$ when a substitution maps
every literal of $C$ onto a literal of $D$. It implies entailment, so a more general clause covers at least the examples
of a more specific one. The order gives the search a direction
(<NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">generality ordering and refinement</NoteLink>), and the classical
systems differ in which way they walk it.

| Approach | Starts from | Moves by | Example system |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Top-down | the clause with an empty body | adding the literal with the best score | <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink> <Cite id="quinlan1990" /> |
| Bottom-up | the examples as clauses | <NoteLink to="least-general-generalisation">least general generalisation</NoteLink> | Golem <Cite id="muggleton1990golem" /> |
| Bounded by a bottom clause | the most specific clause entailing one example | top-down search of clauses above it | <NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">Progol, Aleph</NoteLink> <Cite id="muggleton1995progol" /> |
| Meta-interpretive | the examples as goals | proving them while instantiating metarules | Metagol <Cite id="muggleton2015mil" /> |
| Generate, test and constrain | an empty set of constraints | turning each failed program into constraints | <NoteLink to="learning-from-failures">Popper</NoteLink> <Cite id="cropper2021popper" /> |

Most of these learn one clause at a time and wrap the clause search in
<NoteLink to="sequential-covering">sequential covering</NoteLink>: learn a clause that covers some positives and no
negatives, remove the positives it covers, and repeat. Meta-interpretive learning and Popper search for whole programs
instead, which lets them learn mutually dependent clauses, recursion and
<NoteLink to="predicate-invention">invented predicates</NoteLink> that a clause-by-clause learner cannot find.

### Scoring and noise

The exact definition demands zero errors. Real data have mislabelled examples and missing facts, so practical systems
relax consistency and score clauses. FOIL uses an information gain, Progol and Aleph a compression score that rewards
positives covered and penalises negatives and clause length, and both accept a clause that covers a few negatives when
the gain from the positives outweighs them. A length penalty is a form of the
<NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">minimum description length</NoteLink> principle: a clause is worth keeping
when it is shorter than the list of examples it explains.

### Where inductive logic programming sits

ILP is the first-order case of rule learning: a propositional rule set is a logic program whose literals are attribute
tests (<NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision lists and rule sets</NoteLink>). It is also a form of
<NoteLink to="program-synthesis-from-examples">program synthesis from examples</NoteLink> restricted to logic programs.
Three extensions address its weaknesses. <NoteLink to="statistical-relational-learning">Statistical relational
learning</NoteLink> attaches probabilities or weights to clauses. <NoteLink to="knowledge-graph-rule-mining">Rule mining
on knowledge graphs</NoteLink> scales clause search to millions of facts by restricting the rule shape.
<NoteLink to="differentiable-inductive-logic-programming">Differentiable ILP</NoteLink> relaxes the discrete search to
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on clause weights. The modern view of the field is surveyed
by Cropper and Dumančić <Cite id="cropper2022ilp30" />.

## Worked example

The family database has seven people over four generations. $B$ holds six parent facts, drawn as arrows in the
figure: Ann is the parent of Bob and Cid, Bob of Dee and Eve, Cid of Fay, and Eve of Gus. Five people are female. The
target is $\text{grandparent}/2$.

1. **Examples.** $E^+$ has the four true pairs: (ann, dee), (ann, eve), (ann, fay) and (bob, gus). $E^-$ has five
   chosen pairs: (ann, bob) is a parent pair, (cid, gus) and (bob, fay) are unrelated, (dee, ann) is reversed, and
   (ann, gus) is a great-grandparent pair.
2. **Prior necessity.** $B$ defines no grandparent fact, so $B$ entails no positive and a hypothesis is needed.
3. **The empty body.** $\text{grandparent}(X, Y)$ covers every pair: 4 positives and all 5 negatives. It is complete
   and as inconsistent as a clause can be.
4. **One literal.** $\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Y)$ covers no positive and one negative,
   (ann, bob). $\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Z)$ says only that $X$ has a child. It covers all
   4 positives and 4 negatives; it misses only (dee, ann), because Dee has no child.
   $\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(Z, Y)$ says only that $Y$ has a parent, and also covers 4 and 4.
5. **Two literals joined by $Z$.** $\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Z) \land \text{parent}(Z, Y)$
   requires the child of $X$ to be the parent of $Y$. For (ann, dee), $Z = \text{bob}$ satisfies both literals. For the
   negative (ann, gus), Ann's children are Bob and Cid, and neither is Gus's parent, so the query fails. The clause
   covers 4 positives and 0 negatives: it is complete and consistent.
6. **A literal too many.** Adding $\text{female}(X)$ keeps consistency but loses (bob, gus), because Bob is not female.
   The clause is consistent and not complete.

Steps 3 to 6 move down the generality order: each added literal can only remove covered examples. The shared variable
$Z$ is what separates step 5 from step 4. The literals $\text{parent}(X, Z)$ and $\text{parent}(W, Y)$ with different
variables cover the same 4 negatives as $\text{parent}(X, Z)$ alone, because nothing ties the two people together. The
ancestor task in the figure needs two clauses, one of them recursive; the non-recursive pair of clauses misses (ann,
gus), three generations apart.

## Assumptions

- **The background knowledge contains the right vocabulary.** A learner can only combine predicates that $B$ defines.
  Without parent facts, no clause over the remaining predicates separates grandparents from other pairs.
- **Examples are ground facts, and negatives are trustworthy.** Closed-world negatives assume $B$ and $E^+$ are
  complete. An unlisted true fact becomes a <Gloss name="fn">false negative</Gloss> that forbids the correct clause.
- **Coverage is decidable within the bounds.** Entailment over arbitrary clauses is undecidable. Systems bound proof
  depth or restrict $B$ and $H$ to Datalog, where the least model is finite.
- **The target is expressible in the bias.** A clause-length bound of 2 cannot express great-grandparent without an
  invented predicate, however many examples are given.

## Pitfalls

- **Search cost grows with the bias.** Each extra allowed literal multiplies the candidates, and each coverage test is a
  theorem-proving problem. Large background knowledge bases need indexing, sampling of negatives or a restricted rule
  shape.
- **Greedy search misses literals that only pay off together.** A literal that introduces a new variable, such as
  $\text{parent}(X, Z)$, often removes few negatives on its own. Top-down systems add lookahead or prefer such
  literals when no literal has a positive gain (<NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink>).
- **Recursive hypotheses can loop.** A candidate such as $\text{ancestor}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{ancestor}(X, Z) \land
  \text{parent}(Z, Y)$ makes depth-first proof search run forever. Coverage tests need a depth bound, and a bound that
  is too small makes correct recursive clauses look incomplete.
- **Too few negatives give over-general clauses.** With only the negatives (dee, ann) and (gus, bob), the clause
  $\text{grandparent}(X, Y) \leftarrow \text{parent}(X, Z) \land \text{parent}(W, Y)$ is complete and consistent. It
  says only that $X$ has a child and $Y$ has a parent, so it also covers unrelated pairs such as (cid, gus).
- **Noise breaks exact consistency.** One mislabelled negative can rule out the correct clause. Systems that allow a
  noise threshold trade this against accepting genuinely wrong clauses.
