<Definition>
A **generalisation** of two terms $s$ and $t$ is a term $g$ with substitutions $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ such that
$g\theta_1 = s$ and $g\theta_2 = t$. The **least general generalisation** $\operatorname{lgg}(s, t)$ is a
generalisation that every other generalisation is more general than: for each generalisation $g'$ there is a $\sigma$
with $g'\sigma = \operatorname{lgg}(s, t)$. For clauses, $\operatorname{lgg}(C, D)$ is the most specific clause that
<NoteLink to="theta-subsumption">θ-subsumes</NoteLink> both $C$ and $D$. Plotkin proved that the lgg of two terms, and
of two clauses, exists and is unique up to renaming of variables <Cite id="plotkin1972thesis" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A bottom-up learner starts from the examples and generalises only as far as they force it. For conjunctions of attribute
tests that step is easy: keep the tests two examples share and drop the rest, as Find-S does
(<NoteLink to="concept-learning-and-version-spaces">concept learning and version spaces</NoteLink>). First-order
examples have structure. The atoms $\text{daughter}(\text{mary}, \text{ann})$ and $\text{daughter}(\text{eve},
\text{tom})$ share no constant, yet the pattern "the first argument is female and the second is her parent" holds of
both. A generaliser has to replace constants <NoteLink to="cyclic-factor-and-by-terms">by variables</NoteLink>, and it
has to decide which constants become the same variable.

Replacing every differing constant by its own fresh variable gives a generalisation that is too general. For
$f(a, g(a))$ and $f(b, g(b))$ it gives $f(X, g(Y))$, which forgets that both terms repeat their first argument inside
$g$. The least general generalisation $f(X, g(X))$ keeps that regularity. It is the bottom-up counterpart of
<NoteLink to="unification-and-resolution">unification</NoteLink>: unification finds the most general common instance
of two terms, anti-unification the most specific common generalisation.

## Mechanism

### Anti-unification of terms

Keep a table of pairs of subterms, each pair with its variable. Compute $\operatorname{lgg}(s, t)$ recursively:

1. If $s$ and $t$ are identical, return $s$.
2. If $s = f(s_1, \dots, s_k)$ and $t = f(t_1, \dots, t_k)$ have the same function symbol and arity, return
   $f(\operatorname{lgg}(s_1, t_1), \dots, \operatorname{lgg}(s_k, t_k))$.
3. Otherwise, if the pair $(s, t)$ is in the table, return its variable; if not, add it with a new variable and return
   that.

The table is the whole difference from the too-general answer: the same pair of subterms always becomes the same
variable <Cite id="plotkin1972thesis" />. The pair $(a, b)$ is a different pair from $(b, a)$, so
$\operatorname{lgg}(f(a, b), f(b, a)) = f(X, Y)$.

<Derivation title="Why the result is a least general generalisation">
**It is a generalisation.** Let $g = \operatorname{lgg}(s, t)$ and let $\theta_1$ map each table variable to the first
term of its pair and $\theta_2$ to the second. By induction on the recursion, $g\theta_1 = s$ and $g\theta_2 = t$: case
1 returns $s$ itself, case 2 applies the substitutions argument by argument, and case 3 returns a variable that
$\theta_1$ maps to $s$ and $\theta_2$ to $t$.

**It is the least one.** Let $h$ be any generalisation, $h\sigma_1 = s$ and $h\sigma_2 = t$. Build $\lambda$ with
$h\lambda = g$ by induction on $h$. If $h$ is a variable $X$, set $X\lambda = \operatorname{lgg}(X\sigma_1, X\sigma_2)$
computed with the same table. If $h = f(h_1, \dots, h_k)$, then $s$ and $t$ both start with $f$, so case 2 applies and
the arguments are handled by induction. A variable $X$ that occurs twice in $h$ maps to the same pair
$(X\sigma_1, X\sigma_2)$ at both places, and the table gives that pair one variable, so $\lambda$ is well defined. Hence
$h\lambda = g$, and $h$ is at least as general as $g$.
</Derivation>

### Clauses

The lgg of two clauses pairs their literals. Every literal of $C$ is paired with every literal of $D$ that has the same
predicate symbol and sign, and each pair is generalised by anti-unification with one table shared across
<Gloss name="ovo">all pairs</Gloss>. The heads pair with each other. The result is

$$
\operatorname{lgg}(C, D) = \set{\operatorname{lgg}(l, m) : l \in C,\; m \in D,\; l \text{ and } m \text{ compatible}}.
$$

The lgg θ-subsumes $C$ through $\theta_1$ and $D$ through $\theta_2$, and every clause that θ-subsumes both also
θ-subsumes it <Cite id="nienhuyscheng1997" />. It is the least upper bound of $C$ and $D$ in the θ-subsumption
lattice. The clause usually contains redundant literals, and Plotkin's reduction removes them by deleting each literal
that the rest of the clause can absorb under some substitution.

### Relative least general generalisation

A learner has background knowledge $B$ as well as examples. Plotkin's lgg **relative to** ground background facts $B$
generalises each example together with everything known: the relative lgg of $e_1$ and $e_2$ is

$$
\operatorname{rlgg}(e_1, e_2) = \operatorname{lgg}(e_1 \leftarrow B,\; e_2 \leftarrow B).
$$

Each literal of $B$ is paired with each compatible literal of $B$, so the raw clause has up to $\abs{B}^2$ body
literals. Two kinds of literal add nothing relative to $B$ and can be dropped: ground literals, which pair a fact with
itself and are facts of $B$; and groups of literals whose variables are linked to the head through no chain of shared
variables, which $B$ satisfies whatever the head's values. Reduction then removes the redundant literals among the
rest.

**Golem.** Muggleton and Feng's Golem learns <NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">definite clauses</NoteLink>
bottom-up with the relative lgg <Cite id="muggleton1990golem" />. It computes the relative lggs of randomly chosen pairs
of positive examples, keeps the one that covers the most positives and no negatives, and generalises it further with the
lgg of more positives while it stays consistent. Covered positives are removed, and the search repeats for the remaining
ones. Golem keeps the clauses finite by allowing only **determinate** literals, whose new variables have exactly one
value given the earlier ones.

## Worked example

**Terms.** Generalise $f(a, g(a))$ and $f(b, g(b))$, the first preset of the figure.

1. **Root.** Both terms are $f$ with two arguments: compare the arguments.
2. **First argument.** $a$ and $b$ differ. The pair $(a, b)$ is new, so it becomes $A$.
3. **Second argument.** $g(a)$ and $g(b)$ are both $g$ with one argument: compare it.
4. **Inside $g$.** The pair $(a, b)$ is in the table, so it becomes $A$ again.
5. **Result.** $\operatorname{lgg} = f(A, g(A))$ after four comparisons. $\theta_1 = \set{A \mapsto a}$ and
   $\theta_2 = \set{A \mapsto b}$ recover the inputs.

For the lists $[1, 2, 3]$ and $[4, 2, 5]$, read as chains of list cells, the shared middle element survives:
$[A, 2, B]$.

**Clauses.** Generalise $C = \text{daughter}(\text{mary}, \text{ann}) \leftarrow \text{female}(\text{mary}) \land
\text{female}(\text{ann}) \land \text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{mary})$ and
$D = \text{daughter}(\text{eve}, \text{tom}) \leftarrow \text{female}(\text{eve}) \land \text{parent}(\text{tom},
\text{eve})$.

1. **Heads.** $(\text{mary}, \text{eve})$ becomes $A$ and $(\text{ann}, \text{tom})$ becomes $B$:
   $\text{daughter}(A, B)$.
2. **$\text{female}(\text{mary})$ with $\text{female}(\text{eve})$.** The pair $(\text{mary}, \text{eve})$ is $A$:
   $\text{female}(A)$.
3. **$\text{female}(\text{ann})$ with $\text{female}(\text{eve})$.** The pair $(\text{ann}, \text{eve})$ is new and
   becomes $C$: $\text{female}(C)$.
4. **$\text{parent}(\text{ann}, \text{mary})$ with $\text{parent}(\text{tom}, \text{eve})$.** The pairs are $B$ and
   $A$: $\text{parent}(B, A)$.
5. **Reduction.** $\text{daughter}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{female}(A) \land \text{female}(C) \land
   \text{parent}(B, A)$ has the redundant literal $\text{female}(C)$: the substitution $\set{C \mapsto A}$ folds it onto
   $\text{female}(A)$. The reduced lgg is $\text{daughter}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{female}(A) \land \text{parent}(B, A)$.

**Relative lgg.** Take the background $B$ of seven facts from the
<NoteLink to="propositional-and-first-order-logic-for-learning">first-order logic</NoteLink> note: Ann is the parent of
Mary and Tom, Tom of Eve and Ian, and Ann, Mary and Eve are female. Generalise the examples $\text{daughter}(\text{mary},
\text{ann})$ and $\text{daughter}(\text{eve}, \text{tom})$ relative to $B$.

1. **Raw clause.** The four parent facts pair in $4 \times 4 = 16$ ways and the three female facts in
   $3 \times 3 = 9$, so the lgg of $e_1 \leftarrow B$ and $e_2 \leftarrow B$ has 25 body literals.
2. **Ground literals.** The 7 pairs of a fact with itself give the facts of $B$, true for any head values: dropped.
3. **Unlinked literals.** 13 literals, such as $\text{parent}(H, I)$ and $\text{female}(I)$, share no variable with
   $\text{daughter}(A, B)$, even through other literals: dropped.
4. **Linked literals.** Five remain: $\text{parent}(B, A)$, three literals $\text{parent}(B, \cdot)$ with a fresh child
   variable, and $\text{female}(A)$.
5. **Reduction.** Each $\text{parent}(B, \cdot)$ folds onto $\text{parent}(B, A)$. The relative lgg is
   $\text{daughter}(A, B) \leftarrow \text{parent}(B, A) \land \text{female}(A)$, the correct definition, learnt from
   two positive examples and no negatives.

## Cost

- **Terms.** Anti-unification visits each pair of aligned subterms once, so it runs in time linear in the size of the
  smaller term, with a table lookup per differing pair.
- **Clauses.** The lgg of $C$ and $D$ has up to $\abs{C} \cdot \abs{D}$ literals. The lgg of $n$ clauses, taken pair by
  pair, has up to the product of their sizes, which grows exponentially in $n$.
- **Relative lgg.** With $\abs{B}$ background facts the raw clause has up to $\abs{B}^2$ literals for two examples and
  $\abs{B}^n$ for $n$. Golem's determinacy restriction and depth bound keep it polynomial.
- **Reduction.** Deciding whether a literal is redundant is a θ-subsumption test, which is NP-complete, and reduction
  makes one test per literal. Reducing the raw 25-literal relative lgg directly takes over a minute in the engine's
  greedy reducer; dropping ground and unlinked literals first leaves 5 literals and takes milliseconds.

## Trade-offs

- **Bottom-up against top-down.** The lgg never invents a condition the examples do not share, so it suits small, clean
  data and finds long clauses that a greedy <NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">top-down search</NoteLink>
  would not assemble (<NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink>). It ignores the negatives until a clause is tested, and one
  atypical positive generalises a clause far beyond what the other examples support.
- **Specific answers.** The lgg is the most specific consistent generalisation, the opposite end of the version space
  from the clause a top-down learner returns. Specific clauses generalise less to unseen examples.
- **Relative lgg against bottom clauses.** <NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">Progol's bottom
  clause</NoteLink> saturates one example within mode declarations and then searches above it, which bounds the clause
  by the language bias instead of by the product of the background. It is the usual choice in modern bottom-up-bounded
  systems such as Aleph.

## Pitfalls

- **Forgetting the table.** Generalising each differing pair to a fresh variable loses every co-reference, and the
  result is too general: $f(X, g(Y))$ instead of $f(X, g(X))$.
- **Treating variables of the inputs as shared.** The inputs' variables are generalised like constants. A variable $X$
  in $s$ and a variable $X$ in $t$ are different subterms unless the two terms were meant to share it.
- **Unreduced clauses.** The raw clause lgg repeats conditions under different variable names, which misleads clause
  length penalties and slows every later coverage test.
- **Noise.** A single positive example from a different concept, or a missing background fact, removes the shared
  literal it lacks from every later generalisation, and bottom-up search has no way to recover it.
