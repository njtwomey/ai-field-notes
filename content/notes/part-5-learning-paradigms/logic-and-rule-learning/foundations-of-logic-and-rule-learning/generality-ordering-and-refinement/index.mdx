<Definition>
A hypothesis $g$ is **more general than or equal to** a hypothesis $h$, written $g \succeq h$, when every instance
that $h$ covers is also covered by $g$. The relation orders the hypothesis space $\Hcal$ from the most general
hypothesis $\top$, which covers everything, to the most specific, $\bot$, which covers nothing. A **downward refinement
operator** $\rho : \Hcal \to 2^{\Hcal}$ maps a hypothesis to a set of hypotheses below it in the order, and an
**upward refinement operator** maps it to a set above it <Cite id="shapiro1983" />. A **top-down** learner starts at
$\top$ and applies a downward operator; a **bottom-up** learner starts from specific hypotheses, usually built from the
examples, and applies an upward operator or generalises directly.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The space of rules is enormous. Three binary attributes already allow 28 distinct conjunctions, and first-order clauses
form an infinite space. A learner cannot test every rule, but it does not need to: the generality order tells it which
rules are worth testing.

Generality is monotone in coverage. If a rule covers a negative example, every more general rule covers it too. If a
rule misses a positive example, every more specific rule misses it too. A learner that adds conditions to a rule can
therefore only lose coverage, and a learner that drops conditions can only gain it. Each step in the order moves
coverage in a known direction, which turns the search for a consistent rule into a walk with a sense of direction, and
lets whole regions of the space be skipped without testing them.

A refinement operator makes the walk concrete. It says, for the rule in hand, which rules are its immediate neighbours
in the order: add one condition, bind one variable, replace one value by "any". Every practical rule learner is a search
strategy (greedy, beam, best-first, branch and bound) over the graph that a refinement operator defines.

## Details

### Semantic and syntactic generality

The definition above is **semantic**: it compares the sets of instances two hypotheses cover. Testing it directly means
enumerating instances, which is impossible for infinite instance spaces. Learners use a **syntactic** order that
implies the semantic one and can be checked on the hypotheses alone.

- **Conjunctions of attribute tests.** $g \succeq h$ when every test of $g$ is a test of $h$. Dropping a condition
  generalises and adding one specialises <Cite id="flach2012" />.
- **First-order clauses.** $C \succeq D$ under **$\theta$-subsumption** when some substitution $\theta$ maps every
  literal of $C$ onto a literal of $D$. $\theta$-subsumption implies entailment ($C \models D$) but not conversely,
  and it is the order that most <Gloss name="ilp">inductive logic programming</Gloss> systems search
  (<NoteLink to="theta-subsumption">theta-subsumption</NoteLink>).
- **Entailment relative to background knowledge.** $C \succeq D$ when $B \cup \set{C} \models D$. It is the most
  faithful order and is undecidable in general, so systems approximate it.

The syntactic orders are **quasi-orders**: reflexive and transitive, but two different hypotheses can be equivalent,
each above the other. $p(X) \leftarrow q(X, Y)$ and $p(X) \leftarrow q(X, Y) \land q(X, Z)$ $\theta$-subsume each
other. Working with one representative of each equivalence class, a **reduced** clause, turns the quasi-order into a
partial order.

### The lattice of conjunctions

For conjunctions the order is a **lattice**: any two hypotheses have a least upper bound and a greatest lower bound.
The least upper bound of two conjunctions keeps the tests they share; it is their
<NoteLink to="least-general-generalisation">least general generalisation</NoteLink>. The greatest lower bound keeps
the tests of both, and is $\bot$ when they require different values of one attribute. With $n$ attributes, attribute
$j$ taking $k_j$ values, the lattice has $\prod_j (k_j + 1)$ conjunctions plus $\bot$, arranged in levels by the number
of tests. Its **Hasse diagram** draws an edge from each hypothesis to each hypothesis exactly one step below it.

### Refinement operators

For a downward operator $\rho$, write $\rho^*(h)$ for every hypothesis reachable from $h$ by repeated refinement.
Three properties decide whether a search with $\rho$ is sound and complete <Cite id="nienhuyscheng1997" />.

- **Locally finite:** $\rho(h)$ is finite and computable for every $h$, so each step can enumerate the neighbours.
- **Proper:** every member of $\rho(h)$ is strictly below $h$, so the search never revisits an equivalent hypothesis.
- **Complete:** every hypothesis strictly below $h$ is equivalent to some member of $\rho^*(h)$, so no hypothesis is
  out of reach.

An operator with all three properties is **ideal**. For conjunctions the operator "replace one '?' by a value" is
ideal. For clauses under $\theta$-subsumption no ideal operator exists, because some clauses have no finite set of
refinements from which every more specific clause can be reached, so practical operators give up properness or
completeness
<Cite id="nienhuyscheng1997" />. A further property, **optimality**, requires every hypothesis to be reached along
exactly one path from $\top$. An optimal operator turns the refinement graph into a tree, so a search never generates
the same rule twice. Fixing an order on attributes, and adding only tests on attributes later than the last one added,
makes the conjunction operator optimal.

### Monotonicity and pruning

Let $p(h)$ and $n(h)$ be the numbers of positive and negative examples $h$ covers. For $g \succeq h$, coverage gives
$p(g) \geq p(h)$ and $n(g) \geq n(h)$. Two pruning rules follow <Cite id="mitchell1982" />.

- A top-down search can discard $h$ when $p(h)$ is too small to be useful, since no refinement of $h$ covers more
  positives. Branch-and-bound rule learners use $p(h)$ as an optimistic bound on any score that grows with positives
  covered (<NoteLink to="certifiably-optimal-rule-lists">certifiably optimal rule lists</NoteLink>).
- A bottom-up search can discard $h$ as soon as $n(h)$ exceeds the number of negatives it is allowed to cover, since no
  generalisation of $h$ covers fewer.

### Top-down and bottom-up search

**Top-down (general to specific).** Start at $\top$. While the current hypothesis covers negatives, choose a refinement
from $\rho(h)$ by a heuristic such as $p - n$, precision, or <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink>'s information gain
(<NoteLink to="rule-quality-measures">rule quality measures</NoteLink>). The rule growers of
<NoteLink to="sequential-covering">sequential covering</NoteLink> (CN2, <Gloss name="ripper" />) and FOIL work this way.
Top-down search tolerates noise well, because a heuristic can stop at a rule that covers a few negatives, and it returns
general rules.

**Bottom-up (specific to general).** Start from a most specific hypothesis built from an example: the example itself for
conjunctions, a bottom clause for first-order learning. Generalise just enough to cover further positives, by least
general generalisation or an upward operator. Find-S and Golem work this way. Bottom-up search is driven by the data, so
it never considers conditions the examples do not support, and it returns specific rules.

**Both ends.** <NoteLink to="concept-learning-and-version-spaces">Candidate elimination</NoteLink> moves the specific
boundary up and the general boundary down at once. <NoteLink to="inverse-entailment-and-progol">Progol</NoteLink>
searches top-down but only among clauses that $\theta$-subsume a bottom clause, which bounds the space from below
<Cite id="deraedt2008" />.

## Worked example

Take three binary attributes $a$, $b$ and $c$, and write a conjunction by its literals: $a\bar c$ means
$a = 1 \land c = 0$. The lattice has $3^3 = 27$ conjunctions plus $\bot$, in five levels: $\top$, 6 single literals, 12
pairs, 8 full assignments and $\bot$. The downward operator gives $\top$ six refinements, each single literal four (two
values for each of the two untested attributes), and each pair two. Each full assignment has one refinement, $\bot$.
That is $6 + 24 + 24 + 8 = 62$ edges in the Hasse diagram.

The examples are two positives, $(a, b, c) = (1, 0, 0)$ and $(1, 1, 0)$, and one negative, $(0, 1, 0)$.

1. **Top-down, step 0.** $\top$ covers both positives and the negative. Score its six refinements by $p - n$:
   $a$ covers 2 positives and 0 negatives (score 2); $\bar a$ covers no positive and is dropped; $\bar b$ covers 1 and 0
   (score 1); $b$ covers 1 and 1 (score 0); $\bar c$ covers 2 and 1 (score 1); $c$ covers no positive.
2. **Top-down, step 1.** Move to $a$. It covers no negative, so the search stops and returns $a$.
3. **Bottom-up, step 0.** Start at $\bot$.
4. **Bottom-up, step 1.** Generalise to cover $(1, 0, 0)$: the full assignment $a \bar b \bar c$.
5. **Bottom-up, step 2.** Generalise to cover $(1, 1, 0)$. The positives disagree on $b$, so $\bar b$ is dropped:
   $a \bar c$. Every positive is covered and the negative is not, so the search returns $a \bar c$.
6. **The consistent hypotheses** are those above $a\bar c$ that exclude $(0, 1, 0)$: $a\bar c$ and $a$. Top-down search
   returned the most general one and bottom-up search the most specific one.
7. **Adding the negative $(1, 1, 1)$.** Now $a$ covers a negative (score $2 - 1 = 1$, tied with $\bar c$ and $\bar b$).
   Ties go to the refinement with more positives, then to the first in order, so the top-down search takes $a$
   and then refines it: $a\bar c$ covers 2 positives and 0 negatives (score 2) and beats
   $a\bar b$ (score 1). Both searches now return $a\bar c$, the only consistent hypothesis left.

## Assumptions

- **Coverage is monotone in the order.** The pruning rules and the direction of search rely on it. Hypothesis languages
  with negation in rule bodies, or <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">rule lists</NoteLink> in which a later
  rule's coverage depends on the earlier ones, break it.
- **A refinement operator that matches the language.** The operator must reach every hypothesis the learner may need.
  Bias in the operator, such as a bound on rule length or on the number of new variables, is part of the learner's
  <NoteLink to="inductive-bias">inductive bias</NoteLink>.
- **Cheap coverage tests.** Every step evaluates the coverage of every candidate. For conjunctions this is a scan of
  the data; for first-order clauses each test is a theorem-proving problem.

## Pitfalls

- **Greedy myopia.** A greedy top-down search commits to one refinement per step and never revisits it. When the
  conditions of a good rule are informative only together, every single refinement scores alike. On the four instances
  of $a$ and $b$ labelled by $a \ne b$, each of $a$, $\bar a$, $b$ and $\bar b$ covers one positive and one negative,
  so $p - n$ gives no direction. Beam search, lookahead and bottom-up seeding reduce the problem.
- **Generating the same rule many times.** A non-optimal operator reaches $a b$ from $a$ and from $b$. Without
  duplicate detection or an optimal operator, the search evaluates each rule once per path.
- **Syntactic and semantic order differ.** Two syntactically different hypotheses can cover exactly the same examples,
  and $\theta$-subsumption misses generality relations that entailment captures, such as between recursive clauses.
  A search guided by the syntactic order can then miss a hypothesis or test equivalent ones repeatedly.
- **Unbounded first-order refinement.** Adding literals with new variables never ends: $p(X) \leftarrow q(X, Y_1)
  \land \dots \land q(X, Y_k)$ exists for every $k$. Systems bound clause length, variable depth and the number of new
  variables per literal.
