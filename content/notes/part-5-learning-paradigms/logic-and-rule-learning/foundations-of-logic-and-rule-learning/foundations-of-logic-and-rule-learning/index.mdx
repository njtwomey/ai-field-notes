<Definition>
The foundations of logic and rule learning are the logical languages in which rules are written, the semantics that
decides when a rule predicts an example, the proof procedures that compute those predictions, and the ordering of rules
by generality that a learner searches. Every method of the later chapters, from
<NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision lists</NoteLink> to inductive logic programming and neuro-symbolic
models, combines these four pieces.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A rule learner returns a hypothesis that a person can read: "a day is good for a picnic if it is sunny and the wind is
weak", or "$X$ is a grandparent of $Z$ if $X$ is a parent of some $Y$ who is a parent of $Z$". To learn such
hypotheses, a system needs a language to write them in, a test of whether a hypothesis covers an example, and a way to
move from one candidate hypothesis to a better one. This chapter supplies all three, first for attribute-value data and
then for relational data, where examples are sets of objects and relations between them.

## Main ideas

**Logic as a hypothesis language.** Propositional logic describes examples by a fixed set of features and fits tabular
data. First-order logic adds variables, relations and quantifiers, so one rule can describe examples with any number of
related objects. A hypothesis covers an example when the example follows from the hypothesis and the background
knowledge. <NoteLink to="propositional-and-first-order-logic-for-learning">Propositional and first-order logic for
learning</NoteLink> defines interpretations, entailment, clauses and the two coverage settings.

**Logic programs and their meaning.** Rules with a single conclusion are Horn clauses. A set of them is a logic program
whose meaning is its least Herbrand model, the facts obtained by applying the rules until nothing new follows.
<NoteLink to="horn-clauses-and-logic-programs">Horn clauses and logic programs</NoteLink> derives that model by
iterating the immediate-consequence operator and explains the closed-world reading of absent facts.

**Proof by unification and resolution.** Answering one question against a program does not require the whole model.
Unification matches a goal against a rule head with the least commitment, and SLD resolution chains rules backwards from
the query to the facts. <NoteLink to="unification-and-resolution">Unification and resolution</NoteLink> runs both step
by step and shows where Prolog's depth-first search fails to terminate.

**Learning as search through consistent hypotheses.** Concept learning keeps every hypothesis that agrees with the
labelled examples. For conjunctions the version space is stored by its two boundaries and updated by candidate
elimination. <NoteLink to="concept-learning-and-version-spaces">Concept learning and version spaces</NoteLink> works a
six-example problem to convergence and shows how noise and a too-narrow language make the version space collapse.

**The generality order.** Hypotheses are ordered by the examples they cover, and coverage is monotone in that order.
Refinement operators list the neighbours of a hypothesis, top-down by adding conditions or bottom-up by generalising
from examples. <NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">Generality ordering and refinement
operators</NoteLink> defines the properties a good operator needs and compares the two search directions on a Hasse
diagram.

## How the notes fit together

Start with propositional and first-order logic; every other note uses its vocabulary. The two logic-programming notes
come next: the Horn-clause note gives the declarative meaning, and the unification note gives the procedure that
computes it, which is also the coverage test of most relational learners. The concept-learning note can be read right
after the first note, since it needs only propositional conjunctions. The generality note builds on it and supplies the
search vocabulary (refinement, top-down, bottom-up, pruning by coverage) that the later chapters use without
redefining.
