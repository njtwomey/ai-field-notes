<Definition>
**Concept learning** infers a Boolean function $c : \Xcal \to \set{0, 1}$, the target concept, from training examples
$\Dcal = \set{(x_1, c(x_1)), \dots, (x_m, c(x_m))}$, by searching a hypothesis space $\Hcal$. A hypothesis $h$ is
**consistent** with $\Dcal$ when $h(x_i) = c(x_i)$ for every example. The **version space** $VS_{\Hcal, \Dcal}$ is the
set of all hypotheses in $\Hcal$ consistent with $\Dcal$ <Cite id="mitchell1982" />. When $\Hcal$ is ordered by
generality, the version space is determined by two boundary sets: $S$, its maximally specific members, and $G$, its
maximally general members. The **candidate-elimination algorithm** updates $S$ and $G$ one example at a time.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most learners return one hypothesis. A version-space learner keeps every hypothesis that the data have not ruled out.
Each positive example eliminates the hypotheses that exclude it, and each negative example eliminates those that
include it. What remains is exactly the set of hypotheses the learner still considers possible.

Keeping the whole set makes the learner's uncertainty explicit. A new instance on which every remaining hypothesis
agrees can be labelled with certainty, given that the target is in $\Hcal$. An instance on which they disagree marks
what the data have not yet settled, and labelling it is the most useful next query. When a single hypothesis remains,
the target has been identified.

The set is usually far too large to list, but it has structure. Hypotheses can be ordered from specific to general by
what they cover. The consistent hypotheses form a contiguous band in that order, so it is enough to store its two
edges. The specific edge $S$ moves up when a positive example forces more generality, and the general edge $G$ moves
down when a negative example forces more specificity. Learning is complete when the edges meet.

## Details

### Conjunctive hypotheses

Instances are described by $n$ attributes, attribute $j$ taking one of $k_j$ values. A conjunctive hypothesis states,
for each attribute, either a required value or "?" (any value). It is written as a tuple, such as
$\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle$ for "sky is sunny and wind is weak", and classifies an instance as
positive when every stated value matches. Two extreme hypotheses bound the language: $\langle ?, \dots, ? \rangle$
covers every instance, and the empty hypothesis $\emptyset$ covers none. The language has $1 + \prod_j (k_j + 1)$
distinct hypotheses. Mitchell's six-attribute EnjoySport example has 96 instances and 973 distinct conjunctions
<Cite id="mitchell1997" />, out of $2^{96}$ possible concepts. That restriction is the learner's
<NoteLink to="inductive-bias">inductive bias</NoteLink>.

### The generality order

$h_1$ is **more general than or equal to** $h_2$, written $h_1 \succeq h_2$, when every instance that $h_2$ covers is
covered by $h_1$. For conjunctions the test is syntactic: $h_1 \succeq h_2$ when every attribute that $h_1$ constrains
is constrained to the same value by $h_2$. The relation is a partial order <Cite id="flach2012" />, and
<NoteLink to="generality-ordering-and-refinement">generality ordering and refinement</NoteLink> studies it as a search
space. Two operations on it drive concept learning.

- The **minimal generalisation** of $h$ that covers an instance $x$ replaces by "?" every attribute where $h$
  disagrees with $x$. It is unique for conjunctions, and for $h = \emptyset$ it is $x$ itself.
- The **minimal specialisations** of $h$ that exclude $x$ replace one "?" of $h$ by a value different from $x$'s. There
  are $\sum_{j : h_j = ?} (k_j - 1)$ of them.

The **Find-S** algorithm uses only the first. It starts at $\emptyset$ and generalises minimally for each positive
example, ignoring the negatives. It returns the most specific conjunction that covers all the positives, which is the
single member of $S$ when the data are noise-free and the target is a conjunction.

### The boundary representation

For a hypothesis space ordered by $\succeq$, the boundaries are

$$
S = \set{s \in VS : \text{no } h \in VS \text{ has } s \succ h}, \qquad G = \set{g \in VS : \text{no } h \in VS \text{ has } h \succ g},
$$

where $\succ$ is the strict order. The **version-space representation theorem** states that, for finite $\Hcal$,

$$
VS_{\Hcal, \Dcal} = \set{h \in \Hcal : \exists s \in S,\; \exists g \in G,\; g \succeq h \succeq s}.
$$

Every hypothesis between some member of $S$ and some member of $G$ is consistent, and every consistent hypothesis lies
between them <Cite id="mitchell1982" />.

<Derivation title="The representation theorem">
**Every hypothesis between the boundaries is consistent.** Let $g \succeq h \succeq s$ with $s \in S$ and $g \in G$.
For a positive example $x$, $s$ covers $x$ because $s$ is consistent, so $h$ covers $x$ because $h \succeq s$. For a
negative example $x$, $g$ does not cover $x$, and $h$ covers only instances $g$ covers, so $h$ does not cover $x$.
Thus $h$ is consistent.

**Every consistent hypothesis lies between the boundaries.** Let $h \in VS$. If $h \in G$, take $g = h$. Otherwise
some $h' \in VS$ is strictly more general than $h$; if $h' \notin G$, repeat from $h'$. $\Hcal$ is finite and each
step moves strictly up the order, so the chain ends at a member $g$ of $G$ with $g \succeq h$. The same argument
downwards gives $s \in S$ with $h \succeq s$.
</Derivation>

### Candidate elimination

Start with $S = \set{\emptyset}$ and $G = \set{\langle ?, \dots, ? \rangle}$, which bound the whole of $\Hcal$. For
each example $x$:

- **If $x$ is positive:** remove from $G$ every member that does not cover $x$. Replace each member $s$ of $S$ that
  does not cover $x$ by its minimal generalisations that cover $x$ and lie below some member of $G$. Remove from $S$
  any member more general than another member of $S$.
- **If $x$ is negative:** remove from $S$ every member that covers $x$. Replace each member $g$ of $G$ that covers $x$
  by its minimal specialisations that exclude $x$ and lie above some member of $S$. Remove from $G$ any member less
  general than another member of $G$.

The two cases are mirror images. A positive example can only move $S$ up and shrink $G$; a negative example can only
move $G$ down and shrink $S$. If $S$ or $G$ becomes empty, no hypothesis of $\Hcal$ is consistent with the data. For
conjunctions $S$ never has more than one member, because the minimal generalisation is unique.

### Classifying with a version space

The version space labels a new instance $x$ by the vote of its members. Because every member lies between the
boundaries, the vote is unanimous in two cases that the boundaries decide alone. Every member covers $x$ exactly when
every member of $S$ covers it. No member covers $x$ exactly when no member of $G$ covers it. Otherwise the members
disagree, and the learner can abstain or report the fraction voting positive. The
<NoteLink to="bayes-point-machine">Bayes point machine</NoteLink> takes the same view for linear classifiers: it
predicts with the centre of mass of the version space instead of any single member.

### How many examples

If the learner can choose its queries, an instance that half the members cover removes half of them whatever its label.
At least $\lceil \log_2 \abs{VS} \rceil$ queries are then needed in the worst case, and that many suffice when such an
instance exists at every step <Cite id="mitchell1997" />. <NoteLink to="stream-based-active-learning">Stream-based
active learning</NoteLink> queries labels in the region where the version space disagrees for the same reason. For
examples drawn at random, any consistent learner over a finite space returns, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, a
hypothesis with error at most $\epsilon$ once

$$
m \geq \frac{1}{\epsilon}\paren{\ln \abs{\Hcal} + \ln \frac{1}{\delta}}
$$

<Cite id="haussler1988" />. Conjunctions of $n$ Boolean attributes have $\abs{\Hcal} = 3^n + 1$, so a number of
examples linear in $n$ suffices, which is the classical result that conjunctions are
<NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC learnable</NoteLink> <Cite id="valiant1984" />.

## Worked example

A picnic is good or bad depending on four attributes: sky (sunny, cloudy, rainy), temperature (warm, cold), humidity
(normal, high) and wind (weak, strong). There are $3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 24$ possible days and
$1 + 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 109$ conjunctions. The six training examples are:

| # | sky | temp | humidity | wind | good picnic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | sunny | warm | normal | weak | yes |
| 2 | sunny | warm | high | weak | yes |
| 3 | rainy | cold | high | strong | no |
| 4 | sunny | cold | high | weak | yes |
| 5 | cloudy | warm | normal | weak | no |
| 6 | sunny | warm | normal | strong | no |

1. **Start.** $S_0 = \set{\emptyset}$ and $G_0 = \set{\langle ?, ?, ?, ? \rangle}$. All 109 hypotheses are in the
   version space.
2. **Example 1 (positive).** $G$ covers it. The only member of $S$, $\emptyset$, does not, and its minimal
   generalisation is the example itself: $S_1 = \set{\langle \text{sunny}, \text{warm}, \text{normal}, \text{weak}
   \rangle}$. The version space holds the $2^4 = 16$ conjunctions that keep or drop each of the four values.
3. **Example 2 (positive).** It differs from $S_1$ only in humidity, so humidity becomes "?":
   $S_2 = \set{\langle \text{sunny}, \text{warm}, ?, \text{weak} \rangle}$. The version space has $2^3 = 8$ members.
4. **Example 3 (negative).** $S_2$ already excludes it. $\langle ?, ?, ?, ? \rangle$ covers it, so it is replaced by its
   minimal specialisations that exclude $\langle \text{rainy}, \text{cold}, \text{high}, \text{strong} \rangle$. There
   are $2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5$: sky = sunny, sky = cloudy, temp = warm, humidity = normal and wind = weak. Only those above
   $S_2$ survive, which drops sky = cloudy and humidity = normal:
   $G_3 = \set{\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, ? \rangle, \langle ?, \text{warm}, ?, ? \rangle, \langle ?, ?, ?, \text{weak}
   \rangle}$. Of the 8 previous members only $\langle ?, ?, ?, ? \rangle$ covered the negative, so 7 remain.
5. **Example 4 (positive).** $\langle ?, \text{warm}, ?, ? \rangle$ excludes this cold day, so it leaves $G$. $S$
   generalises temperature: $S_4 = \set{\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle}$. Three hypotheses remain:
   $S_4$ itself, $\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, ? \rangle$ and $\langle ?, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle$.
6. **Example 5 (negative).** $\langle ?, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle$ covers this cloudy day. Its only minimal
   specialisation above $S_4$ is $\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle$, which is less general than
   $\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, ? \rangle$, so it is removed. $G_5 = \set{\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, ? \rangle}$ and
   two hypotheses remain. They agree on 20 of the 24 days: 4 sunny days with weak wind are positive, 16 days that are
   not sunny are negative, and they disagree on the 4 sunny days with strong wind.
7. **Example 6 (negative).** A sunny day with strong wind, one of the disputed days. $\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, ?
   \rangle$ covers it and is specialised to $\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle$. Now $S_6 = G_6 =
   \set{\langle \text{sunny}, ?, ?, \text{weak} \rangle}$: the concept "sky is sunny and wind is weak" is identified.

The version space shrank $109 \to 16 \to 8 \to 7 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1$. Example 6 was the most informative possible
query at step 6, because it was a day on which the two remaining hypotheses disagreed.

## Assumptions

- **The target is in $\Hcal$.** Candidate elimination returns the hypotheses consistent with the data within the
  chosen language. If the target is outside it, the version space can collapse to empty or converge to a wrong
  hypothesis.
- **Noise-free labels.** One mislabelled example removes the true concept from the version space, and the boundaries
  never recover it.
- **A finite, discrete language with a computable order.** The representation theorem needs every chain to end at a
  boundary member, and the updates need minimal generalisations and specialisations. Conjunctions over discrete
  attributes have both; intervals over real attributes need care at the ends.

## Pitfalls

- **Noise collapses the version space.** The "repeated day with the opposite label" sequence in the figure repeats
  example 4 as a negative. No conjunction can label the same day both ways, so $S$ and $G$ empty at example 7, and the
  learner has no answer at all. Practical rule learners such as <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">sequential
  covering</NoteLink> trade consistency for accuracy instead.
- **A disjunctive target is outside the language.** For the target "sky is sunny or wind is weak", two positives
  ($\langle \text{sunny}, \text{warm}, \text{normal}, \text{strong} \rangle$ and $\langle \text{rainy}, \text{warm},
  \text{normal}, \text{weak} \rangle$) force $S$ to $\langle ?, \text{warm}, \text{normal}, ? \rangle$, which covers the
  negative $\langle \text{cloudy}, \text{warm}, \text{normal}, \text{strong} \rangle$. The version space is empty after
  three examples. Allowing arbitrary disjunctions would remove the inductive bias, and an unbiased version space never
  labels an unseen instance unanimously <Cite id="mitchell1980" />.
- **$G$ can grow exponentially.** With negatives first, the figure's $G$ grows to 13 members after two examples before
  the positives prune it to one. Haussler showed that for conjunctions the size of $G$ can grow exponentially in the
  number of examples <Cite id="haussler1988" />. $S$ stays a single conjunction, which is why Find-S remains practical
  when $G$ does not.
- **Order changes the path, not the result.** The final version space depends only on the set of examples. The sizes of
  the boundaries along the way depend on the order.
