<Definition>
**Boolean rule learning** fits a classifier written as a formula in <Gloss name="dnf">disjunctive normal form</Gloss>,
an OR of **clauses** each of which is an AND of binary features, or in conjunctive normal form, an AND of ORs. A
two-class DNF rule set predicts positive when any clause holds. <Gloss name="brcg" /> states the fit as an integer
program that minimises a training loss under a budget on the total size of the clauses, and solves its linear relaxation
by **column generation**, creating a clause only when the relaxation's dual prices show that it can lower the objective
<Cite id="dash2018brcg" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A two-class <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">rule set</NoteLink> is a DNF formula: "predict high risk if
(fewer than 5 accounts) or (at least 7 accounts and debt above 1000)". Its rules can be read in any order, which a user
study found easier than accounting for the negated rules above each line of a decision list
<Cite id="lakkaraju2016ids" />. <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">Sequential covering</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="ripper">RIPPER</NoteLink> learn such sets greedily, one rule at a time, with no stated objective and no
measure of how far the result is from the best set <Cite id="cohen1995" />. Methods that do optimise an objective, such
as Bayesian rule sets and interpretable decision sets, first mine a pool of candidate rules and then choose among them,
so a good clause outside the pool is never considered <Cite id="wang2017brs" /> <Cite id="lakkaraju2016ids" />.

The number of possible clauses is exponential in the number of binary features, so no method can list them all on
realistic data. Column generation, a standard device of large-scale <Gloss name="lp">linear programming</Gloss>, works
with a small set of clauses and finds missing improving ones by solving a pricing problem. It keeps the optimisation and
the certificate of <NoteLink to="certifiably-optimal-rule-lists">certifiably optimal rule lists</NoteLink> and changes
the model, an unordered set instead of a list, and the source of candidates, generated on demand instead of pre-mined.

## Mechanism

### Binary features

Every input is turned into binary features. A categorical attribute gives one indicator per level and the negation of
each, $X_j = x$ and $X_j \ne x$. A numeric attribute gives threshold comparisons and their negations, $X_j \le t$ and
$X_j > t$, at a few quantiles such as the deciles <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />. A clause is a set of these features, and a
row satisfies it when every feature in it is 1.

### The master integer program

Let $\Pcal$ and $\Zcal$ be the training rows with label 1 and 0, $\Kcal$ the set of all clauses, and $\Kcal_i$ the
clauses that row $i$ satisfies. A binary variable $w_k$ selects clause $k$, and $\xi_i \ge 0$ marks a positive row that
no selected clause covers. The **Hamming loss** counts each uncovered positive once and each negative once per selected
clause that covers it, the number of clauses one would have to add or remove to classify every row correctly. The master
integer program is <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{\text{MIP}} = \min_{\xi, w} \quad & \sum_{i \in \Pcal} \xi_i + \sum_{i \in \Zcal}\sum_{k \in \Kcal_i} w_k \\
\text{subject to} \quad & \xi_i + \sum_{k \in \Kcal_i} w_k \ge 1, \quad \xi_i \ge 0, \qquad i \in \Pcal, \\
& \sum_{k \in \Kcal} c_k w_k \le C, \\
& w_k \in \{0, 1\}, \qquad k \in \Kcal.
\end{aligned}
$$

The **complexity** of clause $k$ is $c_k = 1 + \abs{k}$, one plus its number of features, and $C$ is the budget. The
Hamming loss is linear in $w$, which the 0-1 error is not, and it is an upper bound on the 0-1 error. The two agree when
no negative row is covered by two selected clauses. A CNF formula is learned with the same program after negating the
labels and the features, by De Morgan's laws.

### Column generation

The **master LP** drops the integrality of $w$. It still has one column per clause. Column generation solves it over a
small set of clauses, the **restricted master LP**, and adds clauses one at a time.

1. Solve the restricted master LP. It starts feasible with no clauses at all: $\xi_i = 1$ for every positive row.
2. Read the dual prices: $\mu_i \ge 0$ for the covering constraint of positive row $i$, $\lambda \ge 0$ for the budget.
3. **Pricing.** The reduced cost of a clause not yet in the LP is its objective coefficient minus the dual prices of the
   constraints it enters:

   $$
   \bar c = \sum_{i \in \Zcal} \delta_i - \sum_{i \in \Pcal} \mu_i \delta_i + \lambda c,
   $$

   where $\delta_i = 1$ when row $i$ satisfies the clause. A clause with $\bar c < 0$ would lower the LP objective if it
   were added. Dash et al. find the clause of least reduced cost by a second integer program over which features to
   include <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />.
4. If the least reduced cost $z_{\text{CG}}$ is negative, add that clause and return to step 1. Otherwise the restricted
   LP is optimal for the full master LP.

The reduced cost reads naturally. A clause pays one unit for each negative it covers and earns $\mu_i$ for each positive
it covers, where $\mu_i$ is the current marginal value of covering row $i$; it also pays $\lambda$ per unit of
complexity, the current marginal value of the budget.

### Bounds and the final rule set

Every rule set within the budget is feasible for the master LP, and the Hamming loss is an integer, so
$\lceil z_{\text{MLP}} \rceil \le z_{\text{MIP}}$. Column generation need not run to the end to give a bound. At any
iteration,

$$
z_{\text{MIP}} \ge \Big\lceil z_{\text{RMLP}} + \tfrac{C}{2}\, z_{\text{CG}} \Big\rceil,
$$

because every clause has $c_k \ge 2$, so a rule set within the budget has at most $C/2$ clauses, each lowering the
objective by at most $\abs{z_{\text{CG}}}$ <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />. The rule set itself comes from solving the
**restricted master integer program**, the master program over the generated clauses only, with an off-the-shelf
solver. Its Hamming loss minus the bound is a certificate of how far it can be from optimal; when the two meet, the rule
set is optimal.

<Derivation title="The bound from an unfinished column generation">
Let $(\mu, \lambda)$ be optimal duals of the restricted master LP, so $z_{\text{RMLP}} = \sum_{i \in \Pcal} \mu_i -
\lambda C$ by strong duality, and $\mu_i \le 1$, since the reduced cost $1 - \mu_i$ of $\xi_i$ is non-negative at the
optimum. For any feasible point $(\xi, w)$ of the full master LP, write each clause's objective coefficient as its
reduced cost plus its dual terms: $\sum_{i \in \Zcal} \delta_{ik} = \bar c_k + \sum_{i \in \Pcal} \mu_i \delta_{ik} -
\lambda c_k$. Substituting into the objective gives

$$
\sum_{i \in \Pcal} \xi_i + \sum_k \Big(\bar c_k + \sum_{i \in \Pcal}\mu_i \delta_{ik} - \lambda c_k\Big) w_k
= \sum_k \bar c_k w_k + \sum_{i \in \Pcal} \mu_i \Big(\xi_i + \sum_k \delta_{ik} w_k\Big) + \sum_{i \in \Pcal}(1 - \mu_i)\xi_i
- \lambda \sum_k c_k w_k.
$$

The covering constraints make the second sum at least $\sum_i \mu_i$, the third sum is non-negative, and the budget
makes the last term at least $-\lambda C$. Every $\bar c_k \ge z_{\text{CG}}$ (clauses already in the restricted LP have
$\bar c_k \ge 0 \ge z_{\text{CG}}$), and $\sum_k w_k \le \frac12 \sum_k c_k w_k \le C/2$. With $z_{\text{CG}} \le 0$ the
first sum is at least $\frac{C}{2} z_{\text{CG}}$. Hence the objective is at least $z_{\text{RMLP}} + \frac{C}{2}
z_{\text{CG}}$ for every feasible point, in particular for every rule set within the budget, and rounding up is valid
because the Hamming loss is an integer.
</Derivation>

### Larger data

The pricing integer program grows with the number of rows times the number of features. For data sets with more than a
few thousand rows or a few hundred binary features, Dash et al. stop the pricing solver at a time limit, continue with a
greedy beam search over clauses of up to five features, and for the largest data price on random samples of rows and
features <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />. The optimality certificate is then lost, but every clause still lowers the correct
objective.

## Worked example

Use the Titanic data with "survived" as the positive label: 711 positives and 1490 negatives. The binary features are
the four class levels, their four negations, the two sexes and the two ages. Identical rows are merged into 24 groups
with counts, and each count multiplies its row's terms in the objective. Take $C = 6$ and clauses of at most three
features.

**Iteration 0.** No clause exists, so every positive is uncovered: $\xi_i = 1$ and $z_{\text{RMLP}} = 711$. Each
covering constraint is tight and costs one unit per positive row, so $\mu_i = 1$ per row; the budget is unused, so
$\lambda = 0$. The reduced cost of a clause is then the negatives it covers minus the positives it covers. The best
clause is $\text{class} \ne \text{3rd} \wedge \text{sex} = \text{female}$: it covers 254 survivors and 20 deaths, so
$\bar c = 20 - 254 = -234$. It joins.

**Iteration 1.** With that clause at weight 1, the LP objective is $711 - 254 + 20 = 477$. Pricing proposes
$\text{class} = \text{crew} \wedge \text{sex} = \text{female}$ at $\bar c = -17$, which the LP then leaves at weight 0:
its rows are already covered by the first clause, and the duals were degenerate.

**Iterations 2 to 4.** The clause $\text{class} \ne \text{3rd} \wedge \text{class} \ne \text{crew} \wedge \text{age} =
\text{child}$ joins next. It costs 4 against the remaining budget of 3, so the LP uses it at weight 0.75 and the
objective falls to 465; the budget is now binding and $\lambda = 4$. Pricing then finds the cheaper
$\text{class} \ne \text{3rd} \wedge \text{age} = \text{child}$ at $\bar c = -4$, which covers the same 30 surviving
first- and second-class children with complexity 3, and the objective falls to 461. In iteration 4 one more clause,
second-class women, joins at weight 0.

**Iteration 5.** No clause has negative reduced cost, so the LP is optimal at 461, and the bound is
$\lceil 461 + 3 \times 0 \rceil = 461$. The restricted integer program selects

$$
(\text{class} \ne \text{3rd} \wedge \text{sex} = \text{female}) \vee (\text{class} \ne \text{3rd} \wedge \text{age} = \text{child}),
$$

"survived if not in third class and a woman or a child". No death is covered twice, so its Hamming loss equals its 0-1
error, 461 people. Loss and bound meet: no rule set within the budget does better. The same 461 errors are the minimum
that <NoteLink to="certifiably-optimal-rule-lists">CORELS</NoteLink> certifies over rule lists of these attributes.

**A tighter budget.** With $C = 4$ a rule set holds one clause of up to three features or two single-feature
clauses. The LP optimum is $471.67$, a fractional mixture of
clauses, so the bound is 472, while the best rule set, the first clause alone, makes 477 errors. The gap of 5 is the
integrality gap of the relaxation.

$(x_1 \wedge x_2) \vee (x_3 \wedge \neg x_4)$ with 10% of labels flipped, the method recovers the planted formula at
$C = 6$, and its 44 errors are exactly the flipped labels.

## Cost

Each iteration solves one linear program, cheap at these sizes, and one pricing problem, an integer program that is
NP-hard in general. Dash et al. call data sets with fewer than a couple of thousand rows and a few hundred binary
features small; on those the full framework runs to provable optimality <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />. Larger data sets
need the time limits, heuristics and sampling described above, and the final restricted integer program is solved by a
branch-and-bound solver. Merging identical rows, as in the example, shrinks every program when the features are few.

## Trade-offs

- **Against greedy covering.** The rule set optimises a stated objective and comes with a bound. On 16 data sets, the
  column-generation method dominated the accuracy-complexity trade-off of three other optimisation-based rule-set
  learners on 8, and when tuned for accuracy alone it was competitive with RIPPER, sometimes with much simpler rules
  <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />.
- **Against rule lists.** A DNF set needs no order, and each rule can be read alone. A
  <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision list</NoteLink> can be exponentially shorter for some concepts,
  and CORELS optimises 0-1 error directly rather than a Hamming upper bound <Cite id="angelino2018" />.
- **Against pre-mined candidates.** Generating clauses from dual prices avoids a mining threshold that might exclude
  the best clause. Pre-mining is simpler to implement and needs no integer-programming solver.
- **Budget against penalty.** A complexity budget $C$ states the readable size directly. A penalty on complexity in
  the objective is an equivalent alternative, and both can be combined <Cite id="dash2018brcg" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Hamming loss is not 0-1 error.** A negative covered by two clauses counts twice. Rule sets with overlapping clauses
  are penalised more than their 0-1 error warrants.
- **Binarisation limits the rules.** Thresholds exist only at the chosen quantiles, and a categorical attribute with
  many levels gives many features.
- **Optimal on the training data.** The certificate concerns training loss. Choose $C$ by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or by the size a reader can audit.
- **Degenerate iterations.** Clauses with negative reduced cost can enter at weight zero, as in iteration 1 of the
  example. The LP objective then stalls for a few iterations without the method being stuck.
- **Ties.** Several rule sets can share the optimal loss; the certificate covers the loss, not the choice of set.
