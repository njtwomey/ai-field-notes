<Definition>
A **rule quality measure** scores a candidate rule $a \to c$ by the training rows it covers. With $P$ positive rows
(class $c$) and $N$ negative rows (every other class) in the data, a rule that covers $p$ positives and $n$ negatives
gets a score $h(p, n)$. Rule learners use the score to choose which condition to add to a rule, which rule to keep and
when to stop. Every common measure depends on $(p, n)$ and the totals alone, so each is a field over the rectangle
$[0, N] \times [0, P]$, called **coverage space** <Cite id="furnkranz2005" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A rule should be **consistent**, covering few negatives, and **complete**, covering many positives. The two goals pull
apart. Adding a condition to a rule removes rows from its coverage, which can only lower $p$ and $n$. A rule refined
until it covers a single positive row is perfectly consistent and almost useless. A rule that covers everything is
complete and says nothing.

Each measure fixes an exchange rate between the two goals. Its **isometrics**, the curves in coverage space along which
it is constant, show the rate directly. A learner that climbs the measure moves a rule across these curves, so the
shape of the isometrics decides which rules a learner prefers before any data are seen <Cite id="furnkranz2005" />.

## Details

### Coverage space

A rule is a point $(n, p)$ with $0 \le n \le N$ and $0 \le p \le P$. The empty rule, which covers nothing, sits at the
origin. The rule that covers every row sits at $(N, P)$. The perfect rule sits at $(0, P)$. The diagonal from $(0, 0)$
to $(N, P)$ holds the rules whose precision equals the prior $\pi = P/(P + N)$: such a rule has the class mix of a
random sample and has learned nothing. Dividing the axes by $N$ and $P$ turns coverage space into <Gloss name="roc" />
space, with the <Gloss name="fpr">false positive rate</Gloss> $n/N$ and the <Gloss name="tpr">true positive rate</Gloss>
$p/P$ as coordinates (see <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-and-precision-recall-curves">ROC and
precision-recall curves</NoteLink>). Adding a condition to a rule moves its point towards the origin, never away.

### Precision and its smoothed forms

**Precision** $h_{\text{prec}} = p/(p + n)$ is the share of covered rows that are positive. Its isometrics are lines
through the origin, since $p/(p + n) = \theta$ is the line $p = \frac{\theta}{1 - \theta}\, n$. Every rule on one line
scores the same however many rows it covers, so a rule covering one positive and no negative scores 1, the maximum.
Greedy learners that maximise precision grow rules until they are pure and end with many tiny rules.

The **Laplace estimate** adds one pseudo-count per class, $h_{\text{Lap}} = (p + 1)/(p + n + 2)$ for two classes and
$(p + 1)/(p + n + K)$ for $K$ classes. It is the posterior mean of the rule's precision under a uniform prior, and CN2
uses it to rank rules <Cite id="clark1991" />. Setting $(p + 1)/(p + n + 2) = \theta$ gives
$p + 1 = \frac{\theta}{1 - \theta}(n + 1)$: the isometrics are lines through $(-1, -1)$, rotated just below the
origin. A rule covering one positive scores $2/3$; one covering 100 positives and no negatives scores $101/102$.

The **m-estimate** shrinks precision towards the prior $\pi$ with weight $m \ge 0$:

$$
h_m = \frac{p + m\pi}{p + n + m}.
$$

Its isometrics are lines through the point $(-m(1 - \pi), -m\pi)$ on the extension of the diagonal below the origin.
At $m = 0$ it is precision. As $m$ grows the pivot moves away and the lines become parallel to the diagonal, so the
measure orders rules as weighted relative accuracy does (below) <Cite id="furnkranz2005" />. The single parameter $m$
therefore slides between the most specific and the most general preference.

<Derivation title="The m-estimate tends to weighted relative accuracy">
Write $h_m = \pi + \frac{p - \pi(p + n)}{p + n + m}$, which is $h_m$ after adding and subtracting $\pi$. For
$m \gg p + n$ the denominator is close to $m$, so $h_m \approx \pi + \frac{1}{m}\big(p(1 - \pi) - n\pi\big)$. The
term in brackets equals $\frac{pN - nP}{P + N}$, which is $(P + N)$ times weighted relative accuracy. Up to a constant
offset and a positive factor, which do not change the ranking of rules, $h_m$ is weighted relative accuracy in the
limit.
</Derivation>

### Accuracy and weighted relative accuracy

**Rule accuracy** is the accuracy of the classifier that predicts positive inside the rule and negative outside:
$(p + N - n)/(P + N)$. For fixed $P$ and $N$ it orders rules as $p - n$, and its isometrics are the 45° lines
$p - n = \text{const}$. A cost-weighted variant $p - \gamma n$ tilts them.

**<Gloss name="wracc">Weighted relative accuracy</Gloss>** multiplies the coverage of a rule by the gain of its
precision over the prior <Cite id="lavrac1999" />:

$$
h_{\text{WRAcc}} = \frac{p + n}{P + N}\left(\frac{p}{p + n} - \frac{P}{P + N}\right) = \frac{pN - nP}{(P + N)^2}
= \frac{PN}{(P + N)^2}\left(\frac{p}{P} - \frac{n}{N}\right).
$$

The last form shows that weighted relative accuracy ranks rules by the true positive rate minus the false positive
rate. Its isometrics are parallel to the diagonal, it is zero on the diagonal and negative below it. It rewards large
rules that are better than chance, and it is the default quality of
<NoteLink to="subgroup-discovery">subgroup discovery</NoteLink>, where the aim is to describe unusual parts of the
data rather than to classify.

### Information gain and significance

**FOIL gain** measures a refinement against its parent rule. If the parent covers $(p_0, n_0)$ and the refined rule
covers $(p, n)$, then

$$
h_{\text{FOIL}} = p\left(\log_2\frac{p}{p + n} - \log_2\frac{p_0}{p_0 + n_0}\right).
$$

The bracket is the reduction in the bits needed to signal that a covered row is positive, and the factor $p$ counts
the positives that keep the rule <Cite id="quinlan1990" />. A refinement must keep many positives and raise precision
to score well. <NoteLink to="foil">FOIL</NoteLink> and the growing phase of <NoteLink to="ripper">RIPPER</NoteLink> use
it <Cite id="cohen1995" />.

The **likelihood ratio statistic** of CN2 tests whether the covered rows' class mix differs from the prior:

$$
\Lambda = 2\left(p \ln \frac{p}{e_p} + n \ln \frac{n}{e_n}\right), \qquad e_p = (p + n)\pi,\ e_n = (p + n)(1 - \pi).
$$

Under the hypothesis that the rule is unrelated to the class, $\Lambda$ is approximately
<NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">$\chi^2$</NoteLink> with $K - 1$ degrees of freedom. CN2 keeps a rule only if
$\Lambda$ exceeds the 95% or 99% quantile, which stops it from learning rules that fit noise <Cite id="clark1989" />.

**Description length** scores a whole <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">rule set</NoteLink>: the bits to write
down the rules plus the bits to list the training rows they misclassify (see
<NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">minimum description length</NoteLink>). A rule is worth adding when it saves
more exception bits than it costs. RIPPER stops adding rules by this criterion <Cite id="cohen1995" />.

### Search heuristics and stopping criteria

Measures play two roles in a learner <Cite id="furnkranz2012" />. A **search heuristic** chooses among refinements of
one rule, and it only has to order candidates correctly. A **stopping criterion** decides whether a rule, or one more
condition, is worth having at all, and it needs an absolute threshold: a <NoteLink to="significance-level">significance
level</NoteLink>, a minimum precision or a description length budget. Precision makes a poor search heuristic, because
it prefers tiny pure rules, and a usable stopping criterion as a minimum precision. The likelihood ratio makes a sound
stopping criterion and a poor search heuristic, because it rewards any deviation from the prior, including rules that
cover mostly negatives. CN2 therefore searches by entropy or the Laplace estimate and filters by the likelihood ratio
<Cite id="clark1991" />.

## Worked example

Take the Titanic data with "survived" as the positive class: $P = 711$ survivors and $N = 1490$ deaths among
$P + N = 2201$ people, so $\pi = 0.323$. Four candidate rules cover:

| rule | $p$ | $n$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: class = 1st ∧ age = child | 6 | 0 |
| B: class = 2nd ∧ age = child | 24 | 0 |
| C: class = 1st ∧ sex = female | 141 | 4 |
| D: sex = female | 344 | 126 |

**Precision.** A and B score $1$, C scores $141/145 = 0.972$, D scores $344/470 = 0.732$. The ranking is A = B, C, D.

**Laplace.** A scores $7/8 = 0.875$, B $25/26 = 0.962$, C $142/147 = 0.966$, D $345/472 = 0.731$. One pseudo-count per
class costs A more than C, and C overtakes both pure rules: C, B, A, D.

**m-estimate with $m = 10$.** The pseudo-counts are $m\pi = 3.23$ positive and $6.77$ negative. A scores
$(6 + 3.23)/16 = 0.577$, B $(24 + 3.23)/34 = 0.801$, C $(141 + 3.23)/155 = 0.931$, D $(344 + 3.23)/480 = 0.723$. The
ranking is C, B, D, A: the smallest rule drops to last.

**Weighted relative accuracy.** The denominator is $2201^2 = 4\,844\,401$. A scores
$6 \cdot 1490/4\,844\,401 = 0.0018$, B $0.0074$, C $(141 \cdot 1490 - 4 \cdot 711)/4\,844\,401 = 0.0428$ and D
$(344 \cdot 1490 - 126 \cdot 711)/4\,844\,401 = 0.0873$. The ranking is D, C, B, A, the reverse of precision.

**FOIL gain against the empty rule.** The empty rule's precision is $\pi$, and $\log_2 \pi = -1.630$. A gains
$6 \cdot 1.630 = 9.8$ bits, B $39.1$, C $141\,(\log_2 0.972 + 1.630) = 224.2$ and D
$344\,(\log_2 0.732 + 1.630) = 405.9$. The ranking is D, C, B, A.

The same four rules come out in three different orders. Precision picks the 6 first-class children, the m-estimate
and Laplace pick the 145 first-class women, and weighted relative accuracy and FOIL gain pick all 470 women. The
choice of measure is the main design decision of a <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">sequential covering</NoteLink>
learner, and that note runs CN2 on these data with several of them.

## Assumptions

- **Counts from the training data.** Every measure treats $p$ and $n$ as fixed, though they are random. A rule chosen
  as the maximum of many candidates has an optimistic $p/(p + n)$, the more so the more candidates were compared.
- **One rule at a time.** The measures score a rule without regard to the other rules of the model. A covering
  algorithm handles the interaction by removing covered rows before scoring the next rule.
- **The prior as reference.** The m-estimate, weighted relative accuracy and the likelihood ratio measure a rule
  against $\pi$. In a covering loop the prior changes after every rule, because rows are removed.

## Pitfalls

- **Precision as a search heuristic.** It prefers the smallest pure rule, and on noisy data it learns one rule per
  row.
- **Weighted relative accuracy for classification.** It prefers general rules with modest precision, good for
  describing subgroups and often too coarse for a classifier.
- **Absolute values of FOIL gain.** The gain depends on the parent rule, so gains of refinements of different rules
  are not comparable.
- **Significance after search.** The $\chi^2$ threshold of the likelihood ratio assumes one test, while the learner
  compares thousands of candidates. The test is a heuristic filter, not a calibrated p-value.
