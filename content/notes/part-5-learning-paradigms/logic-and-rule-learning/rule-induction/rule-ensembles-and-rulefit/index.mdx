<Definition>
A **rule ensemble** is a model

$$
F(\xvec) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k r_k(\xvec) + \sum_{j=1}^{d} b_j\, l_j(x_j),
$$

in which each **rule** $r_k(\xvec) \in \{0, 1\}$ is a conjunction of conditions on single inputs, such as
$\indicator[x_1 \le 0.71]\,\indicator[x_2 \le 0.52]$, and each $l_j$ is a clipped copy of input $x_j$.
<Gloss name="rulefit" /> takes the rules from the nodes of a boosted tree ensemble and estimates the coefficients by the
<NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink>, which sets most of them to zero <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient-boosted</NoteLink> ensemble of a few hundred trees predicts well and cannot
be read <Cite id="friedman2001" />. A single tree or a short <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">rule
list</NoteLink> can be read and often predicts worse. The ensemble's trees overlap heavily: many of them repeat a split
near the root, and many of their leaves describe nearly the same region. Its prediction gives every tree the same weight
after shrinkage, so the redundancy is never removed.

RuleFit keeps the ensemble's pool of candidate regions and replaces the fixed weights by a sparse fit. It keeps from
<NoteLink to="rules-from-decision-trees">rules from decision trees</NoteLink> the reading of a tree node as a
conjunctive rule. It changes two things: every node becomes a rule, not only the leaves, and the rules are combined
additively with fitted coefficients instead of in a list. The result is a sparse linear model on interpretable features,
with an accuracy close to the ensemble it came from <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />.

## Mechanism

### Generating the rules

The trees come from boosting on random subsamples. Tree $m$ is fitted to the pseudo-residuals of the current model on a
subsample of $\eta < n$ rows drawn without replacement, and the model is updated with shrinkage $\nu$:
$F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu f_m$. Friedman and Popescu found small shrinkage, $\nu \approx 0.01$, and small subsamples,
$\eta \lesssim n/2$, to work best for generating rules <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />. They draw the number of
leaves of each tree at random, $t_m = 2 + \lfloor \gamma \rfloor$ with $\gamma$ exponential of mean $\bar L - 2$, so the
pool mixes short rules for main effects with longer rules for interactions.

Every node of a tree except the root defines one rule: the product of the indicator functions on the path from the root
to the node. A node reached by $x_{14} \le u$ and then $x_{32} \notin \{a, b, c\}$ gives
$r(\xvec) = \indicator[x_{14} \le u]\,\indicator[x_{32} \notin \{a, b, c\}]$. A tree with $t_m$ leaves has
$2(t_m - 1)$ non-root nodes, so the pool holds $K = \sum_m 2(t_m - 1)$ rules. Rules that cover exactly the same
training rows are kept once.

### Linear terms

Trees approximate a linear effect by a staircase, which takes many rules. RuleFit therefore adds each input as a linear
term, clipped at its $\beta$ and $1 - \beta$ quantiles to resist outliers, $l_j(x_j) = \min(\delta_j^+, \max(\delta_j^-,
x_j))$ with $\beta \approx 0.025$. Each linear term is rescaled to standard deviation 0.4 before fitting. The constant
0.4 is the average standard deviation $\sqrt{s(1 - s)}$ of a rule whose support $s$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$:
$\int_0^1 \sqrt{s(1 - s)}\,ds = \pi/8 \approx 0.39$. A linear term then enters the penalty on the same footing as a
typical rule.

### Fitting the coefficients

The coefficients solve the lasso

$$
\min_{a_0, \avec, \bvec} \frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(y_i - a_0 - \sum_k a_k r_k(\xvec_i) - \sum_j b_j l_j(x_{ij})\Big)^2
+ \alpha\Big(\sum_k \abs{a_k} + \sum_j \abs{b_j}\Big),
$$

along a decreasing sequence of penalties $\alpha$, by <NoteLink to="regularisation-paths">pathwise coordinate
descent</NoteLink> <Cite id="friedman2010" />. The $\ell_1$ penalty sets coefficients exactly to zero, which is what
makes the model short <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />. The penalty is chosen by cross-validation or on held-out rows. Other
losses work the same way: Friedman and Popescu use the Huber loss for
<NoteLink to="robust-and-quantile-regression">robust regression</NoteLink> and a squared ramp loss for classification
<Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />.

### Support decides which rules survive

The rules enter the lasso unstandardised, on their 0/1 scale. That choice decides which rules the penalty removes
first. Consider one rule $r$ of support $s$, the share of training rows where $r = 1$, fitted with a free intercept.
Write $\Delta = \bar y_{\text{in}} - \bar y_{\text{out}}$ for the difference between the mean target where the rule
holds and where it does not. The centred rule $r - s$ has variance $s(1 - s)$ and covariance with $y$

$$
z = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (r_i - s)\, y_i = s(1 - s)\,\Delta.
$$

Minimising $\frac{1}{2n}\sum_i (y_i - a_0 - a r_i)^2 + \alpha \abs{a}$ over $a_0$ and $a$ gives the soft-thresholded
least-squares coefficient

$$
\hat a = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(z)\,\max(\abs{z} - \alpha, 0)}{s(1 - s)}.
$$

The rule survives only when $s(1 - s)\abs{\Delta} > \alpha$. A rule that holds for few rows, or for almost all of them,
needs a much larger effect $\Delta$ to enter than a rule that splits the data in half. Rules supported by a handful of
rows are exactly those whose $\Delta$ is estimated worst, so the unstandardised penalty lowers the variance of the
fitted model <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />. Standardising the rules would weaken the threshold to
$\sqrt{s(1 - s)}\,\abs{\Delta} > \alpha$. On the full path the same quantity orders the start: the path begins at
$\alpha_{\max} = \max_k \abs{z_k}$, below which the first rule enters.

### Reading the model

The **importance** of a rule is the absolute coefficient of its standardised version, $I_k = \abs{a_k}\sqrt{s_k(1 -
s_k)}$, and of a linear term $I_j = \abs{b_j}\,\mathrm{sd}(l_j)$. Both measure how much the prediction varies because of
the term across the training data. A local version, $\abs{a_k}\,\abs{r_k(\xvec) - s_k}$, gives the change in the
prediction at one point if the rule were removed and the intercept adjusted. Ranking terms by importance gives the short
list an analyst reads. The paper also derives <NoteLink to="random-forest">variable importances</NoteLink> and
interaction statistics from the fitted terms <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />.

## Worked example

**Four rules by hand.** Take eight rows with $x = 1, 2, \dots, 8$ and $y = (1, 1, 1, 1, 5, 5, 5, 9)$, so $\bar y = 3.5$.
Two stumps from a boosted ensemble split at $x = 4.5$ and $x = 7.5$, and their four non-root nodes give

| rule | support $s$ | $\bar y_{\text{in}}$ | $\bar y_{\text{out}}$ | $\Delta$ | $z = s(1 - s)\Delta$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $r_1 = \indicator[x \le 4.5]$ | 0.5 | 1 | 6 | $-5$ | $-1.25$ |
| $r_2 = \indicator[x > 4.5]$ | 0.5 | 6 | 1 | 5 | 1.25 |
| $r_3 = \indicator[x \le 7.5]$ | 0.875 | 2.71 | 9 | $-6.29$ | $-0.6875$ |
| $r_4 = \indicator[x > 7.5]$ | 0.125 | 9 | 2.71 | 6.29 | 0.6875 |

$r_1$ and $r_2$ are complements, so with a free intercept they carry the same information, and the lasso uses one of
them; likewise $r_3$ and $r_4$.

- **Where the path starts.** $\alpha_{\max} = \max_k \abs{z_k} = 1.25$. The half-and-half split enters first.
- **The support effect.** The rule $r_4$, which isolates the single row with $y = 9$, has the larger effect, $6.29$
  against $5$. Its support $1/8$ shrinks $s(1 - s)$ to $0.109$ against $0.25$, so $\abs{z_4} = 0.69$ against
  $\abs{z_1} = 1.25$.
- **One penalty.** At $\alpha = 0.7$, $r_1$ alone would get $\hat a_1 = -(1.25 - 0.7)/0.25 = -2.2$, less than half
  its least-squares value $-5$. $r_4$ alone would get $\hat a_4 = 0$, since $0.69 < 0.7$.

**A linear term.** An input uniform on $[0, 1]$ has standard deviation $1/\sqrt{12} = 0.289$. RuleFit multiplies it by
$0.4/0.289 = 1.39$ before fitting, and divides the fitted coefficient by the same factor to report it on the input's
scale.

**Friedman's benchmark.** The figure fits RuleFit to 400 rows of Friedman's first benchmark, ten uniform inputs and
$y = 10\sin(\pi x_1 x_2) + 20(x_3 - \tfrac12)^2 + 10 x_4 + 5 x_5 + \varepsilon$ with noise variance 1; inputs $x_6$ to
$x_{10}$ do not affect $y$. Fifty boosted trees with four leaves each give 300 rules, 287 after merging rules with equal
coverage. Along the lasso path, the held-out <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> falls from 24.1, the error of
predicting the training mean, to a minimum of 3.43 with 83 terms, 78 rules and 5 linear terms. The boosted ensemble that
supplied the rules has held-out error 5.60, and the best lasso on the ten linear terms alone 7.11. The most important
term is the linear term in $x_4$, matching the $10 x_4$ of the formula; the next is $x_1 \le 0.71 \wedge x_2 \le 0.52$,
a corner of the $\sin(\pi x_1 x_2)$ interaction where the target is low. With 52 terms the held-out error is 4.39, still
below the ensemble's.

## Cost

Generating the rules costs the boosting run: $M$ trees, each fitted on $\eta$ rows. Building the design matrix costs
$O(nK)$ for $K$ rules on $n$ rows. Each sweep of <NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">coordinate descent</NoteLink> costs
$O(nK)$ or less with sparse columns, and a path of a few dozen penalties with warm starts takes a modest multiple of one
fit <Cite id="friedman2010" />. In the paper's simulations, 500 trees with six leaves gave $K = 5000$ rules, of which
typically about 10% had nonzero coefficients in the final model <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />. Prediction needs only
the nonzero terms.

## Trade-offs

- **Against the ensemble.** On 100 simulated regression and classification problems, RuleFit with rules from 500 trees
  was on average slightly more accurate than the boosted and post-processed tree ensembles it was compared with
  <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />. The model has tens of terms instead of hundreds of trees.
- **Against rule lists.** A rule list explains a prediction by one rule. RuleFit sums every rule that holds, with
  coefficients that are adjustments given the other terms, so a prediction can involve dozens of overlapping rules. It
  is a sparse additive model on rule features, closer to a <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised
  additive model</NoteLink> than to a decision list.
- **Rules and linear terms.** Linear terms help most when many inputs act nearly linearly; when the target is strongly
  nonlinear, the rules carry the fit and the linear terms add little <Cite id="friedman2008rulefit" />.
- **Choice of penalty.** The lasso picks one of several correlated rules more or less arbitrarily. Bayesian shrinkage
  with a <NoteLink to="sparse-bayesian-priors">horseshoe prior</NoteLink> that also favours short, well-supported rules
  keeps the rule structure in the prior instead <Cite id="nalenz2018horseshoe" />. The package imodels implements
  RuleFit beside other rule learners behind one interface <Cite id="singh2021imodels" />.
- **Relation to rule extraction.** RuleFit refits a model on the rules. <NoteLink
  to="rule-extraction-from-tree-ensembles">Rule extraction from tree ensembles</NoteLink> instead selects rules that
  imitate the ensemble.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading coefficients as effects.** A rule's coefficient is its contribution given every other term, and
  complementary or nested rules share credit. Read importances and the rules' own definitions together.
- **Arbitrary choice among near-duplicates.** Two rules covering almost the same rows can swap in and out of the model
  between bootstrap samples. Check stability before reporting individual rules.
- **Noise terms at the best penalty.** The penalty with the smallest held-out error keeps some terms with tiny effects;
  in the figure, linear terms in the noise inputs $x_8$ and $x_9$ enter with small coefficients. A slightly larger
  penalty gives a shorter model at little cost.
- **Too many terms to read.** Dozens of rules are fewer than hundreds of trees but still a lot. Report the top terms by
  importance and the error of the truncated model.
- **Clipped linear terms.** Winsorising flattens the linear terms beyond the training quantiles, so predictions do not
  extrapolate a linear trend beyond them.
