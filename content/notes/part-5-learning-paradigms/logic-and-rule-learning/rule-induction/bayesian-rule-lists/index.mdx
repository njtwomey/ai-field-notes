<Definition>
**<Gloss name="brl">Bayesian rule lists</Gloss>** treat a decision list as an unknown with a prior and a posterior
<Cite id="letham2015" />. The rules' antecedents come from a fixed collection $\Acal$ mined in advance, every
conjunction of attribute values with enough support. The prior on a list $d = (a_1, \dots, a_m)$ of distinct antecedents
favours small $m$ and short antecedents; each rule's class distribution has a Dirichlet prior and is integrated out,
which gives a closed-form <NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>.
Metropolis-Hastings samples lists from the posterior by moving, adding and removing antecedents. Each rule of a sampled
list reports a posterior class probability with a credible interval.
</Definition>

## Problem

Greedy rule learners such as <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">CN2</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="ripper">RIPPER</NoteLink> return one list, built one rule at a time, with no measure of how much better
it is than the lists they did not try. Their size is controlled by stopping heuristics rather than by a stated
preference. Their rules carry class labels, not calibrated probabilities. A clinician who replaces a risk score with a
learned list needs a short list, probabilities with uncertainty, and a search that is not trapped by its first rule.

Bayesian rule lists answer each point with standard Bayesian machinery. The preference for short lists becomes a
prior with two interpretable parameters. Accuracy becomes a likelihood that rewards rules whose captured rows are
homogeneous. The search becomes sampling, which visits lists in proportion to their posterior probability, so a
better list found later replaces an early one. The cost is a search space of permutations, which is made tractable by
restricting rules to a pre-mined collection of antecedents. The method keeps the model form of
<NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision lists</NoteLink> and the pre-mined rules of
<NoteLink to="association-rule-classification">associative classifiers</NoteLink>; it changes how a list is chosen.

## Mechanism

### Pre-mined antecedents

The candidates $\Acal$ are all conjunctions of at most $C$ conditions whose support, the fraction of training rows they
cover, is at least a threshold. For binary or categorical features this is frequent itemset mining (see
<NoteLink to="pattern-mining">frequent itemsets and association rules</NoteLink>); any complete miner returns the same
set. Numeric features are first cut at interpretable thresholds or quantiles <Cite id="letham2015" />. In the stroke
study of Letham et al., a support of 10% and $C = 2$ left about 2200 antecedents.

### The prior

A list is generated in three steps, with $\lambda > 0$ and $\eta > 0$ the prior means of the list length and of the
number of conditions per antecedent:

1. Draw the length $m$ from a Poisson($\lambda$) truncated to $\{0, \dots, \abs{\Acal}\}$.
2. For each position $j$, draw the antecedent's cardinality $c_j$ from a Poisson($\eta$) truncated to the
   cardinalities $R_j$ that still have unused antecedents.
3. Draw the antecedent $a_j$ uniformly among the unused antecedents of cardinality $c_j$.

The prior probability of a list is the product of these choices:

$$
p(d \mid \Acal, \lambda, \eta) = \frac{\lambda^m / m!}{\sum_{k=0}^{\abs{\Acal}} \lambda^k/k!}
\prod_{j=1}^{m} \frac{\eta^{c_j} / c_j!}{\sum_{k \in R_j} \eta^k / k!} \cdot \frac{1}{\#\{a \in \Acal \setminus
\{a_1, \dots, a_{j-1}\} : \abs{a} = c_j\}}.
$$

Long lists pay a Poisson factor for $m$ and a uniform choice factor for each antecedent. Long antecedents pay a Poisson
factor for $c_j$, and their choice factor is larger because there are more of them.

### The likelihood

Rule $j$ of the list captures the rows its antecedent covers that no earlier antecedent covers; the default rule $j = 0$
captures the rest. Write $N_{j,l}$ for the number of rows of class $l \in \{1, \dots, L\}$ captured by rule $j$,
$\nvec_j = (N_{j,1}, \dots, N_{j,L})$ for its count vector, and
$\thetavec_j$ for the class distribution of rule $j$, with prior $\thetavec_j \sim \Dir(\alphavec)$. Given $d$, the
captured labels are independent categorical draws, so

$$
p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, d, \thetavec) = \prod_{j=0}^{m} \prod_{l=1}^{L} \theta_{j,l}^{N_{j,l}}.
$$

Integrating each $\thetavec_j$ against its Dirichlet density, whose normaliser is the multivariate Beta function
$B(\alphavec) = \prod_l \Gamma(\alpha_l)/\Gamma(\sum_l \alpha_l)$, gives

$$
\int \prod_l \theta_{j,l}^{N_{j,l}} \,\frac{\prod_l \theta_{j,l}^{\alpha_l - 1}}{B(\alphavec)}\, d\thetavec_j
= \frac{B(\alphavec + \nvec_j)}{B(\alphavec)},
$$

because the integrand is an unnormalised Dirichlet density with parameters $\alphavec + \nvec_j$. The marginal
likelihood of a list is the product over its rules:

$$
p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, d, \alphavec) = \prod_{j=0}^{m} \frac{\Gamma(\sum_l \alpha_l)}{\prod_l \Gamma(\alpha_l)}
\cdot \frac{\prod_l \Gamma(N_{j,l} + \alpha_l)}{\Gamma(\sum_l (N_{j,l} + \alpha_l))}.
$$

A rule whose captured rows are all of one class has a large factor; a rule whose rows are evenly mixed has a small
one. Splitting rows into more homogeneous groups always raises the likelihood. The prior is what makes extra rules
pay. Letham et al. set $\alpha_l = 1$, a uniform prior on each $\thetavec_j$ <Cite id="letham2015" />.

### Sampling

The posterior $p(d \mid \Xmat, \yvec) \propto p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, d, \alphavec)\, p(d \mid \Acal, \lambda, \eta)$ is
known up to a constant, which is what <NoteLink to="metropolis-hastings">Metropolis-Hastings</NoteLink> needs. From the
current list $d$ of length $m$, one of three proposals is chosen uniformly:

- **move**: take one antecedent and put it at another position, probability $1/(m(m - 1))$ for a given result;
- **add**: insert an unused antecedent at one of $m + 1$ positions, probability $1/((\abs{\Acal} - m)(m + 1))$;
- **remove**: delete one antecedent, probability $1/m$.

The proposed list $d^*$ is accepted with probability
$\min\big(1, \frac{p(d^* \mid \Xmat, \yvec)\, Q(d \mid d^*)}{p(d \mid \Xmat, \yvec)\, Q(d^* \mid d)}\big)$, where $Q$ is
the proposal probability. An add and the remove that undoes it are each other's reverse, so their probabilities enter
the ratio together <Cite id="letham2015" />. Letham et al. run three chains from prior draws, discard the first half
of each and check convergence with the Gelman-Rubin statistic.

### Predictions and a point estimate

Given a list, the posterior of rule $j$'s class distribution is $\Dir(\alphavec + \nvec_j)$. The predicted probability
of class $l$ for an input captured by rule $j$ is its mean, $(\alpha_l + N_{j,l})/\sum_k(\alpha_k + N_{j,k})$, and the
Dirichlet gives a credible interval. Averaging predictions over all sampled lists gives the most accurate classifier,
BRL-post, which is no longer one readable list. For a single list, Letham et al. take BRL-point: the sampled list of
highest posterior among those whose length and average cardinality are closest to their posterior means. They prefer it
to the <Gloss name="map-estimate">maximum a posteriori</Gloss> list, which the prior pushes towards fewer rules than the
posterior average <Cite id="letham2015" />.

## Worked example

Use the Titanic data (2201 people, 711 survivors) with the 26 antecedents of at most two conditions on class, sex and
age that cover at least 1% of the people: 8 single conditions and 18 pairs. Set $\lambda = 3$, $\eta = 1$ and
$\alpha_l = 1$.

**The empty list** predicts the overall rate for everyone. Its prior is the probability of length 0,
$e^{-3}$ up to the truncation, so $\log p(d) = -3.00$. Its likelihood has one factor,
$B(1491, 712)/B(1, 1)$, and $\log p(\yvec \mid d) = -1388.42$, which is $-0.631$ nats per person.

**One rule: "sex = male ∧ age = adult".** The prior has three factors.

- Length 1: $\log(3^1/1!) - \log\sum_{k=0}^{26} 3^k/k! = 1.099 - 3.000 = -1.901$.
- Cardinality 2 out of the available $\{1, 2\}$: $\log\frac{1^2/2!}{1^1/1! + 1^2/2!} = \log\frac13 = -1.099$.
- One of 18 antecedents of cardinality 2: $\log\frac{1}{18} = -2.890$.

The log prior is $-5.890$, 2.9 nats below the empty list. The rule captures 1329 deaths and 338 survivals; the default
captures 161 and 373. The log likelihood is $-1174.09$, a gain of 214.3 nats. The rule's survival probability is
$(338 + 1)/(1667 + 2) = 0.203$ and the default's $(373 + 1)/(534 + 2) = 0.698$.

**Three rules: adult men, then 3rd class, then 1st class.** The captured counts (died, survived) are $(1329, 338)$,
$(141, 103)$, $(4, 146)$ and, for the default, $(16, 124)$. The log prior is $-10.32$, 4.4 nats below the one-rule
list; the log likelihood is $-1087.20$, a further gain of 86.9 nats. The list reads

1. if male and adult, survival probability 0.203 (95% interval 0.184 to 0.223),
2. else if 3rd class, 0.423 (0.362 to 0.485),
3. else if 1st class, 0.967 (0.934 to 0.989),
4. else 0.880 (0.822 to 0.928).

Letham et al. give a list of this form for these data <Cite id="letham2015" />.

**The chain** run for 2000 steps (the figure's default) finds a five-rule list with log posterior $-1093.94$:
3rd-class men 0.17, other 3rd class 0.46, children 0.97, women 0.92, 2nd-class men 0.09, everyone else (1st-class and
crew men) 0.24. Its log likelihood is 8.6 nats higher than the three-rule list's and its log prior 5.0 nats lower. The
posterior odds are $e^{-1093.94 + 1097.52} = e^{3.58} \approx 36$ in its favour. With 2201 rows the likelihood
decides between lists of similar size, and the prior decides only how many rules a gain of a few nats has to buy.

## Cost

Mining costs one pass of a frequent itemset miner. Each Metropolis-Hastings step evaluates one proposed list, which
assigns every training row to its first matching rule: $O(m\,n)$ for $n$ rows, or $O(m\,n/64)$ with bit vectors.
Chains of tens of thousands of steps are typical. Letham et al. report about 21 minutes per fold on 12,586 patients and
about 2200 antecedents <Cite id="letham2015" />. Scalable Bayesian rule lists keep the model, restrict it to two
classes, cache the captured bit vectors of list prefixes and bound which antecedents can appear, and run about two
orders of magnitude faster <Cite id="yang2017sbrl" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Against greedy learners.** BRL can find lists that greedy covering misses, because a sampled list is judged as a
  whole. It needs a chain and convergence checks instead of one pass.
- **Against certifiably optimal lists.** <NoteLink to="certifiably-optimal-rule-lists">CORELS</NoteLink> optimises
  training error plus a penalty per rule and proves optimality. BRL scores lists by the marginal likelihood, which
  rewards homogeneous captured groups and gives probabilities, but offers no guarantee that the chain has found the
  best list <Cite id="angelino2018" />.
- **Probabilities.** Each rule reports a calibrated probability with an interval, which a classification list does
  not. On stroke risk after atrial fibrillation, BRL-point reached a mean AUC of 0.756 against 0.721 for the CHADS2
  score, with a list of similar size <Cite id="letham2015" />.
- **Hyperparameters.** $\lambda$ and $\eta$ have a direct meaning, the expected number of rules and of conditions per
  rule, and the support threshold and $C$ bound what can be learned at all.

## Pitfalls

- **Mixing.** The space of ordered subsets is huge and the posterior is multimodal; local moves can stay in one mode
  for many steps. Run several chains from different starts and compare them.
- **The pre-mined collection.** A rule whose antecedent is not in $\Acal$ cannot be learned. A support threshold that
  is too high removes small, precise rules; one that is too low makes $\Acal$ large and the chain slow.
- **Rules that capture nothing.** A rule placed after rules that cover all its rows captures no row, adds nothing to
  the likelihood and costs only prior. Report captured counts with every rule.
- **The large-data regime.** With many rows the likelihood swamps the prior, and the posterior favours long lists.
  The prior means $\lambda$ and $\eta$ then have to be set small, or a point estimate chosen for size.
