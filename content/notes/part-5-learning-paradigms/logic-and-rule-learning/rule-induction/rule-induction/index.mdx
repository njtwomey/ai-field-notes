<Definition>
**Rule induction** learns models made of if-then rules from examples described by attributes. Each rule pairs a
conjunction of conditions, such as $\text{sex} = \text{female} \wedge \text{class} \ne \text{3rd}$, with a prediction.
The rules are combined into an ordered decision list, an unordered rule set, or a weighted sum, and the learning methods
range from greedy search one rule at a time to exact optimisation over all lists or sets of a given size
<Cite id="furnkranz2012" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Rules are the model of choice when the model itself has to be read, checked and applied by hand: clinical scores,
credit and justice decisions, operating procedures and surrogates that summarise a black-box model. For such tabular
problems, short rule models are often about as accurate as far larger ones <Cite id="rudin2019" />. Rules also serve
data exploration, where the aim is to describe interesting parts of the data rather than to predict. This chapter covers
rules over attribute-value data; rules over relations between objects are the subject of
<NoteLink to="inductive-logic-programming">inductive logic programming</NoteLink>.

## Main ideas

**How rules combine.** A decision list fires the first rule whose conditions hold; a rule set lets every matching rule
vote, and for two classes it is a formula in disjunctive normal form. Trees, lists and sets describe the same functions
at very different sizes. <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">Decision lists and rule sets</NoteLink> defines
the forms, converts between them and proves that lists of short rules are learnable in polynomial time.

**Scoring a candidate rule.** Every learner needs to compare rules by the positives and negatives they cover.
Precision, the Laplace and m-estimates, FOIL gain, weighted relative accuracy and description length weigh consistency
against coverage differently, and the choice decides which rules a learner prefers. <NoteLink
to="rule-quality-measures">Rule quality measures</NoteLink> draws them as isometrics in coverage space.

**Greedy covering.** <NoteLink to="sequential-covering">Sequential covering</NoteLink> learns one rule, removes the rows
it covers and repeats; <NoteLink to="additive-and-residual-quantisation">AQ</NoteLink> and CN2 are its classic forms.
<NoteLink to="ripper">RIPPER</NoteLink> adds pruning on held-out rows, a description-length stopping rule and a revision
pass, which makes covering robust to noise. <NoteLink to="rules-from-decision-trees">Rules from decision
trees</NoteLink> reads rules off a tree's paths instead and simplifies them with a pessimistic error estimate.

**Mining first, selecting second.** Frequent-pattern miners enumerate every conjunction above a support threshold.
<NoteLink to="association-rule-classification">Association-rule classification</NoteLink> ranks the class rules mined
this way and keeps those that still classify a training row correctly. <NoteLink
to="rule-ensembles-and-rulefit">RuleFit</NoteLink> takes the nodes of a boosted tree ensemble as its candidates and
weights them with the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink>, giving a sparse
<NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">additive model</NoteLink> of rules. <NoteLink to="subgroup-discovery">Subgroup
discovery</NoteLink> uses the same search to report descriptions whose target rate departs most from the population.

**Optimising the whole model.** Greedy learners never revisit a choice. <NoteLink to="bayesian-rule-lists">Bayesian
rule lists</NoteLink> sample lists of pre-mined rules from a posterior that favours short lists.
<NoteLink to="certifiably-optimal-rule-lists">Certifiably optimal rule lists</NoteLink> find the list with the least
penalised training error by branch and bound and prove that no better list exists. <NoteLink
to="boolean-rule-learning">Boolean rule learning</NoteLink> does the same for unordered rule sets by integer
programming, generating clauses from dual prices instead of mining them <Cite id="rudin2022" />.

## How the notes fit together

Start with decision lists and rule sets, then rule quality measures; every later note uses both. Sequential covering
comes next and RIPPER builds on it. Rules from decision trees needs only the first note and a decision tree. The mining
notes (association-rule classification, RuleFit, subgroup discovery) can be read in any order after the quality
measures; RuleFit also needs the lasso and <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient boosting</NoteLink>. Bayesian rule
lists precede certifiably optimal rule lists, which share their pre-mined antecedents and their Titanic example. Boolean
rule learning comes last and needs <Gloss name="lp">linear programming</Gloss>. Most notes use the same Titanic table,
so their lists and error counts can be compared directly: 461 errors is the fewest any classifier of class, sex and age
can make, and several methods reach it.
