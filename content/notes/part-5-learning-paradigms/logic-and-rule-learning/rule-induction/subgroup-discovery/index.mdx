<Definition>
**Subgroup discovery** searches a table with a target attribute for the $k$ **descriptions**, conjunctions of
conditions on the other attributes such as $\text{age} \ge 50 \wedge \text{smoker} = \text{yes}$, whose covered rows
have a target distribution most unlike that of the whole table. A **quality measure** scores each description; the
usual measures grow with both the number of rows covered and the difference between the target inside them and in the
population. The result is a ranked list of individually interesting subgroups, not a classifier
<Cite id="wrobel1997" /> <Cite id="atzmueller2015subgroup" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

An analyst studying hospital outcomes wants statements such as "smokers aged 50 or more have an adverse outcome rate of
0.70, against 0.30 overall". A classifier does not provide them. Its rules must jointly cover every row and are judged
by joint accuracy, so a rule that is interesting on its own can be absent, and a rule in a
<NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision list</NoteLink> means something only after the rules above it.
<NoteLink to="pattern-mining">Association rules</NoteLink> are judged one at a time, but they have no target and favour
small rules of high confidence.

Subgroup discovery keeps the hypothesis language of <NoteLink to="rule-induction">rule induction</NoteLink>,
conjunctions of attribute tests, and changes the goal. Each description is scored alone, descriptions may overlap
freely, and the score rewards large subgroups whose target departs from the population. Klösgen's Explora and Wrobel's
MIDOS introduced the task in the mid-1990s <Cite id="wrobel1997" />. Later work adapted covering learners to it, as
CN2-SD did, and frequent-pattern miners, as SD-Map did <Cite id="lavrac2004cn2sd" /> <Cite id="atzmueller2006sdmap" />.
The same search over slices of a data set underlies <NoteLink to="slice-analysis">slice analysis</NoteLink> of model
errors.

## Mechanism

### Descriptions and their covers

A **selector** tests one attribute: $\text{region} = \text{north}$ for a nominal attribute, $\text{age} \ge 52$ or
$\text{income} \le 45$ for a numeric one at a cut point chosen in advance. A description $d$ is a conjunction of
selectors, and its **cover** is the set of rows meeting all of them. Adding a selector can only shrink the cover. Write
$N$ for the rows of the table and $N^+$ for the rows whose binary target is positive, so that $p_0 = N^+/N$ is the
population rate. A description covering $n$ rows, $n^+$ of them positive, has subgroup rate $p = n^+/n$.

In the coverage-space notation of <NoteLink to="rule-quality-measures">rule quality measures</NoteLink>, $n^+$ is the
positives $p$ a rule covers and $n - n^+$ its negatives; the letters differ because subgroup discovery reserves $p$ for
the rate.

### Quality measures

The **Klösgen family** of qualities multiplies a power of the relative size by the rise in rate:

$$
q_a(d) = \left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^{a} (p - p_0), \qquad 0 \le a \le 1.
$$

The exponent $a$ sets the exchange rate between size and unusualness <Cite id="atzmueller2015subgroup" />.

- $a = 1$ gives **<Gloss name="wracc">weighted relative accuracy</Gloss>**, $\frac{n}{N}(p - p_0)$
  <Cite id="lavrac1999" />. It equals the <Gloss name="tpr">true positive rate</Gloss> minus the <Gloss name="fpr">false
  positive rate</Gloss> of the description, times the constant $p_0(1 - p_0)$, so it favours large subgroups that are
  moderately unusual.
- $a = \frac12$ orders subgroups as the one-sample binomial test statistic
  $z = \sqrt{n}\,(p - p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)}$ does, since $z = q_{1/2}\sqrt{N}/\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)}$.
- $a = 0$ gives the **added value** $p - p_0$, which ignores size; the **lift** $p/p_0$ orders subgroups the same way.
  Both prefer tiny pure subgroups and are used with a minimum size.

For a numeric target, the <NoteLink to="mean-shift">mean shift</NoteLink> $n^a(\mu - \mu_0)/\sigma_0$ plays the same
role, with $\mu$ the subgroup's mean, $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ the population's mean and standard deviation. Asking for
subgroups where the target is lower than in the population, or different in either direction, changes the sign or takes
the absolute value.

### Searching the description space

With $S$ selectors there are about $S^D$ descriptions of up to $D$ selectors. Three properties keep the search
manageable.

- **Minimum support is anti-monotone.** A description that covers fewer than $n_{\min}$ rows has no refinement that
  covers more, so it is neither reported nor refined.
- **Optimistic estimates.** Every refinement of $d$ covers a subset of its rows. For $0 \le a \le 1$, no subset of a
  cover with $n^+$ positives scores more than its positives alone, which gives the bound
  $q_a(d') \le \left(n^+/N\right)^{a} (1 - p_0)$ for every refinement $d'$ of $d$. When the bound is below the quality
  of the current $k$-th best subgroup, the whole branch below $d$ is skipped. This is branch and bound over the
  refinement lattice, the same device that <NoteLink to="certifiably-optimal-rule-lists">certifiably optimal rule
  lists</NoteLink> use over rule-list prefixes.
- **Counting from a prefix tree.** SD-Map treats selectors as items and builds an FP-tree whose nodes count both the
  rows and the positive rows on their path. The counts $(n, n^+)$ of every conjunction are read from the tree without
  scanning the data again, so any count-based quality is evaluated exhaustively <Cite id="atzmueller2006sdmap" />.

Heuristic searches trade completeness for speed. Beam search keeps the best $w$ descriptions at each depth. CN2-SD runs
<Gloss name="cn2" />'s beam search with weighted relative accuracy and replaces CN2's covering step by **weighted
covering**: a row covered by an accepted rule is not removed but has its weight reduced, so later rules are steered
towards rows not yet described <Cite id="lavrac2004cn2sd" />.

<Derivation title="Why the positives alone bound every refinement">
Fix a subset of the cover with $k$ positives and $m$ negatives, $n' = k + m$. Its quality is
$q = N^{-a}\big[k(k + m)^{a - 1} - p_0 (k + m)^{a}\big]$. The derivative in $m$ is
$N^{-a}\big[(a - 1)k(k + m)^{a - 2} - a p_0 (k + m)^{a - 1}\big]$. Both terms are non-positive for $0 \le a \le 1$, so
adding negatives never raises the quality, and the best subset with $k$ positives has $m = 0$ and quality
$(k/N)^a (1 - p_0)$. That value increases with $k$, and $k \le n^+$. Hence every refinement scores at most
$(n^+/N)^a (1 - p_0)$.
</Derivation>

### Redundancy

The top $k$ by any quality are often near-copies: "smoker", "smoker and BMI at most 30", "smoker and income above 25".
A redundancy filter keeps the better of two subgroups whose covers overlap heavily, measured by the Jaccard index of
their covers, or of which one description contains the other <Cite id="vanleeuwen2012diverse" />. Weighted covering in
CN2-SD pursues the same aim during the search.

### Beyond a single target rate

When the target is a model rather than a rate, such as a correlation between two variables or the slope of a
regression, the quality compares the model fitted inside the subgroup with the model fitted on the whole table. That
generalisation is called exceptional model mining, and the search machinery above carries over, except that most of
its qualities have no optimistic estimate <Cite id="atzmueller2015subgroup" />.

## Worked example

A table of $N = 20$ patients has $N^+ = 6$ adverse outcomes, so $p_0 = 0.3$. Five candidate descriptions cover:

| description | $n$ | $n^+$ | $p$ | $q_0$ | $q_{1/2}$ | $q_1$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: smoker = yes | 8 | 5 | 0.625 | 0.325 | 0.206 | 0.130 |
| B: smoker = yes ∧ age ≥ 50 | 3 | 3 | 1 | 0.700 | 0.271 | 0.105 |
| C: sex = male | 10 | 4 | 0.400 | 0.100 | 0.071 | 0.050 |
| D: age ≥ 50 | 9 | 4 | 0.444 | 0.144 | 0.097 | 0.065 |
| E: region = north | 6 | 3 | 0.500 | 0.200 | 0.110 | 0.060 |

**Computing the qualities.** For A, the rise in rate is $0.625 - 0.3 = 0.325$, which is $q_0$. The relative size is
$8/20 = 0.4$, so $q_{1/2} = \sqrt{0.4} \times 0.325 = 0.632 \times 0.325 = 0.206$ and $q_1 = 0.4 \times 0.325 = 0.130$.
For B, the rise is $0.7$ and the relative size $0.15$: $q_{1/2} = 0.387 \times 0.7 = 0.271$ and $q_1 = 0.105$.

**The exponent decides.** Added value and the binomial quality rank B first: three patients, all with the outcome.
Weighted relative accuracy ranks A first: eight patients, five with the outcome. B's purity cannot make up for covering
only 15% of the table once size enters linearly.

**Pruning.** Run a top-1 search for weighted relative accuracy, level by level. After the single selectors, the best is
A with 0.130. The optimistic estimate of E is $(3/20)(1 - 0.3) = 0.105 < 0.130$, so no refinement of E can win, and all
of them are skipped unseen. C and D each have $n^+ = 4$ and bound $0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.14 > 0.130$, so their refinements
must be examined. A's own bound is $0.25 \times 0.7 = 0.175$; its refinement B scores 0.105 and does not displace it.

The figure runs the same search on 800 synthetic patients. An outcome rate of 0.7 was planted among smokers aged 50 or
more, a rate of 0.45 among northerners who never exercise, and 0.2 for everyone else. With $a = 1$, the best description
found is "smoker = yes" (260 rows, rate 0.51), just ahead of "age ≥ 52 ∧ smoker = yes" (130 rows, rate 0.71). With
$a = \frac12$ the order flips, and with $a = 0$ small chance subgroups such as western smokers (46 rows) enter the top
five. Age is cut at 34, 52 and 67, so the planted threshold 50 cannot be expressed. For $a = 1$ and $a = \frac12$ the
planted description itself would score higher than anything found.

## Cost

An exhaustive search evaluates up to $\sum_{j \le D} \binom{S}{j}$ descriptions, each in $O(N/32)$ word operations when
covers are bit sets. With tens of selectors and $D \le 3$ that is thousands of descriptions, a fraction of a second.
Optimistic-estimate pruning removes most of the lattice for weighted relative accuracy, and less for small $a$, whose
bound $(n^+/N)^a(1 - p_0)$ stays large for small covers. SD-Map's prefix tree costs two passes over the data plus the
size of the tree <Cite id="atzmueller2006sdmap" />. Beam search costs $O(w\, S\, D)$ evaluations and can miss the best
subgroup.

## Trade-offs

- **The exponent $a$.** It is the analyst's statement of what "interesting" means. Weighted relative accuracy reports
  broad patterns, the binomial quality balances size and strength, and lift reports strong local effects that need a
  minimum size to be credible.
- **Exhaustive against heuristic search.** Exhaustive search with pruning returns the true top $k$ in the language;
  beam search scales to more selectors and deeper descriptions.
- **Fixed against adaptive cut points.** Fixed equal-frequency cuts allow counting tricks like SD-Map; cuts recomputed
  inside each description adapt to the subgroup but rule out exhaustive counting.
- **Description against prediction.** Subgroups overlap and leave rows uncovered. A set of good subgroups is not a good
  classifier, and an accurate classifier is not a good set of subgroups <Cite id="herrera2011subgroup" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Multiple testing.** A search over thousands of descriptions finds some that look unusual by chance. Report the
  quality on held-out rows, or compare it with the best quality found on data with the target permuted (see
  <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>).
- **Redundant results.** Without a redundancy filter the top $k$ is one subgroup described $k$ ways.
- **Discretisation hides the true boundary.** A planted threshold that is not a cut point is approximated by a
  neighbouring cut, as in the figure.
- **Description is not explanation.** A subgroup with a high rate may owe it to an attribute outside the description
  that is correlated with it.
- **Tiny subgroups at small $a$.** With $a$ near 0 and a low minimum support, the top results are a handful of rows
  each.
