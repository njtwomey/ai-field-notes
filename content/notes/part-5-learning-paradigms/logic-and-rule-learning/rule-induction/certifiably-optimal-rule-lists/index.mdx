<Definition>
**<Gloss name="corels">Certifiably optimal rule lists</Gloss>** are <NoteLink to="decision-lists-and-rule-sets">decision
lists</NoteLink> that minimise the regularised training risk

$$
R(d) = \ell(d) + \lambda K
$$

over all lists $d$ of $K$ distinct antecedents drawn from a pre-mined collection, where $\ell(d)$ is the fraction of
training rows the list misclassifies and $\lambda \ge 0$ is the price of one rule in training error. CORELS finds such
a list by branch and bound over the tree of rule-list prefixes. Lower bounds on the risk of every extension of a
prefix prune most of the tree, and when the search ends the list it returns carries a certificate that no list of
those antecedents has lower risk <Cite id="angelino2018" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Greedy learners build a list one rule at a time and never revisit a choice, so their lists can be longer or less
accurate than necessary. <NoteLink to="bayesian-rule-lists">Bayesian rule lists</NoteLink> search more widely by
sampling, but a chain gives no guarantee that it has seen the best list. For a model that will be audited or used for
high-stakes decisions, the guarantee matters: a reviewer can then ask whether a shorter or more accurate list exists,
and receive the answer "not among these antecedents" <Cite id="rudin2019" />.

Exhaustive search is hopeless in general. With $M$ antecedents there are $\sum_{K=0}^{M} M!/(M - K)!$ ordered lists,
about $e \cdot M!$; for $M = 8$ that is 109,601 lists and for $M = 26$ more than $10^{26}$. CORELS keeps the objective
of BRL's point estimate in a simpler form, error plus a penalty, and replaces sampling by an exact search that can
prove most of the space irrelevant. It is the same pre-mined, rule-list model as BRL; only the search and the
objective change.

## Mechanism

### Prefixes and the search tree

A list $d$ is a **prefix** $d_p = (p_1, \dots, p_K)$ of antecedents followed by a default rule. Each rule predicts the
majority class of the rows it captures, the rows it covers that no earlier rule covers, and the default predicts the
majority class of the rows the prefix leaves. The prefixes form a tree: the root is the empty prefix and the children of
$d_p$ append one unused antecedent. Every node is also a complete list, with its default rule.

### The hierarchical lower bound

Split the error of a list into the error of the prefix, $\ell_p(d_p)$, the fraction of rows that a rule of the prefix
captures and misclassifies, and the error of the default rule, $\ell_0$. Define

$$
b(d_p) = \ell_p(d_p) + \lambda K.
$$

Then $b(d_p) \le R(d)$ for the list $d$ with prefix $d_p$, since $\ell_0 \ge 0$. More importantly, $b(d_p)$ bounds
every list that starts with $d_p$. An extension keeps every rule of $d_p$ in place, so those rules capture the same rows
and make the same mistakes; the extension can only add mistakes and rules. Hence

$$
b(d_p) \le b(d'_p) \le R(d') \quad \text{for every prefix } d'_p \text{ that starts with } d_p,
$$

which is Theorem 1 of Angelino et al. <Cite id="angelino2018" />. Let $R^c$ be the risk of the best list found so far.
If $b(d_p) \ge R^c$, no list in the subtree of $d_p$ can beat it, and the whole subtree is pruned unseen.

### Further bounds

The basic bound prunes a prefix once its own mistakes are too many. Several more bounds use the structure of the
problem <Cite id="angelino2018" />.

- **One-step lookahead.** Every child of $d_p$ has at least one more rule, so its risk is at least $b(d_p) + \lambda$.
  If $b(d_p) + \lambda \ge R^c$, the prefix is evaluated as a list but its children are never generated.
- **Prefix length.** An optimal list has $\lambda K \le R^c$, so $K \le \lfloor R^c/\lambda \rfloor$. The empty list has
  risk at most $\frac12$ for two classes, so $K \le \lfloor 1/(2\lambda) \rfloor$ from the start.
- **Minimum support.** In an optimal list, every antecedent captures at least a fraction $\lambda$ of the rows: deleting
  a rule that captures less would save $\lambda$ and cost at most the rows it captured. A child whose new antecedent
  captures fewer rows is pruned with its subtree. The tighter version requires at least a fraction $\lambda$ of rows
  **correctly** classified by each rule.
- **Permutations.** Two prefixes with the same antecedents in different orders capture the same rows in total and
  leave the same rows to everything after them. Their extensions differ only in the prefix error, so only the
  permutation with the smallest bound needs to be kept.
- **Equivalent points.** Rows with identical antecedent values but different labels are classified identically by
  every list, so every list misclassifies at least the minority label of each such group. Adding these unavoidable
  errors among the rows not yet captured to $b(d_p)$ gives a tighter bound for the prefix and its subtree.

The proof of the minimum support bound is short and is in the derivation below. Together the bounds also justify
pre-mining: an antecedent with support below $\lambda$ can never appear in an optimal list, so mining with that
support threshold loses nothing.

<Derivation title="Why every rule of an optimal list captures at least a fraction λ of the rows">
Let $d^*$ be optimal with $K$ rules, and let rule $i$ capture a fraction $s_i$ of the rows. Delete rule $i$ to get a
list $d$ with $K - 1$ rules, keeping every other rule in place and re-choosing the labels of later rules and the
default. The rows that rule $i$ captured now fall to later rules or the default; in the worst case all of them are
misclassified, so $\ell(d) \le \ell(d^*) + s_i$. The penalty falls by $\lambda$, so
$R(d) \le R(d^*) + s_i - \lambda$. Optimality of $d^*$ requires $R(d^*) \le R(d)$, hence $s_i \ge \lambda$. Counting
only the rows rule $i$ classifies correctly, since its misclassified rows can only become correct, gives the tighter
condition that the correctly classified fraction is at least $\lambda$.
</Derivation>

### The algorithm and the certificate

CORELS keeps a queue of prefixes, initially the root, and the best list $d^c$ with risk $R^c$
<Cite id="angelino2018" />.

1. Pop a prefix $d_p$. If $b(d_p) \ge R^c$ (with the equivalent-points term added), discard it.
2. Evaluate the list with prefix $d_p$ and its default rule. If its risk is below $R^c$, it becomes the best list.
3. If $b(d_p) + \lambda < R^c$, generate the children of $d_p$, discard those that fail the support, permutation or
   bound tests, and push the rest.
4. Repeat until the queue is empty.

Every list not evaluated lies in a pruned subtree, and every pruning step is justified by a bound that holds for every
list in the subtree. When the queue empties, $d^c$ is optimal: that argument is the certificate. The queue can be
breadth first, best first by lower bound, or ordered by other priorities; the order changes the running time, not the
answer. The implementation uses bit vectors for captured rows, a prefix tree that shares work between parents and
children, and a symmetry map for the permutation bound.

## Worked example

Use the Titanic data (2201 people, 711 survivors) with the eight single-condition antecedents (class = 1st, 2nd, 3rd,
crew; sex = male, female; age = child, adult) and $\lambda = 0.005$, the price of 11 misclassified people per rule. The
figure below replays this search.

**Step 1, the root.** The empty list predicts "died" for everyone: $R = 711/2201 = 0.323$, the first best risk. Its
bound is 0, so its eight children are generated.

**Step 2, "class = 1st → survived".** The rule captures 325 people, 122 of whom died:
$b = 122/2201 + 0.005 = 0.060$. As a list, the default "died" for the other 1876 misclassifies 508 survivors, so
$R = (122 + 508)/2201 + 0.005 = 0.291$, the new best.

**Step 6, "sex = male → died".** It captures 1731 men and boys, 367 of whom survived: $b = 0.167 + 0.005 = 0.172$. The
default "survived" for the 470 women and girls misclassifies 126, so $R = (367 + 126)/2201 + 0.005 = 0.229$, the new
best.

**A bound prune.** At step 14 the queue reaches "class = 1st → survived, then age = adult → died". Its two rules already
misclassify $0.263$ of the rows, so $b = 0.263 + 0.010 = 0.273 \ge 0.229$: neither this list nor any of its extensions
is evaluated.

**An equivalent-points prune.** At step 7 the child "sex = female → survived, then class = 2nd → died" has bound
$b = (126 + 25)/2201 + 0.010 = 0.079$, far below 0.229. The rows it leaves are the men and boys outside 2nd class.
Within each group of identical passengers (1st-class men, 3rd-class boys, 3rd-class men, crew men) both outcomes occur,
and every list misclassifies the minority of each group: $57 + 13 + 75 + 192 = 337$ people, $0.153$ of the rows. The
bound rises to $0.079 + 0.153 = 0.232 \ge 0.229$, and the subtree is pruned.

**A permutation prune.** At step 3 the child "class = 2nd, then class = 1st" captures the same rows as "class = 1st,
then class = 2nd", which is already queued with a bound no larger. It is discarded.

**The optimum.** At step 30 the search evaluates

1. if class = 3rd then died (706 captured, 178 errors),
2. else if sex = female then survived (274 captured, 20 errors),
3. else if age = child then survived (16 captured, 0 errors),
4. else died (1205 captured, 263 errors),

with $\ell = 461/2201 = 0.209$ and $R = 0.209 + 3 \times 0.005 = 0.224$. Two more steps empty the queue. The search
generated 63 of the 109,601 lists and evaluated 14 of them, and the certificate says no list of these antecedents has
risk below 0.2245. Its training error, 461 people, is the smallest any classifier of class, sex and age can reach.

**The bounds matter.** With the hierarchical bound alone the same search takes 682 steps. With the 26 antecedents of up
to two conditions and $\lambda = 0.01$, all bounds together certify the optimum, "3rd class died, else adult men died,
else survived", in 171 steps, while the hierarchical bound alone had not finished after 20,000 evaluations.

## Cost

The worst case is exponential in the number of antecedents. In practice the bounds decide the running time. Each
evaluated prefix costs one pass over the captured bit vectors, $O(n/64)$ word operations per rule for $n$ rows, and the
queue and the permutation map take memory proportional to the prefixes kept. On data sets with thousands of rows and
hundreds of antecedents, Angelino et al. report that CORELS usually finds the optimal list in seconds and certifies it
within about ten minutes; the optimum is always found long before the proof is complete <Cite id="angelino2018" />. The
cost grows fast as $\lambda$ falls, because the prefix-length and support bounds weaken in proportion.

## Trade-offs

- **Optimality against expressiveness.** The guarantee holds only over the pre-mined antecedents. Rules outside the
  collection, such as long conjunctions or thresholds that were not mined, are not considered.
- **Against greedy learners.** CORELS's lists are often shorter at equal training accuracy, as in the example, where
  three rules match the best accuracy attainable. Greedy covering scales to far larger antecedent sets.
- **Against Bayesian rule lists.** BRL gives probabilities and uncertainty; CORELS gives a classifier and a proof.
  BRL's prior on length plays the role of $\lambda$ <Cite id="letham2015" />.
- **Accuracy.** On recidivism data from Broward County, Florida, CORELS found rule lists of three rules on age, sex
  and prior offences that were about as accurate as the proprietary COMPAS risk tool <Cite id="angelino2018" />.
- **Related methods.** Scalable Bayesian rule lists borrow CORELS-style bounds for sampling <Cite id="yang2017sbrl" />,
  and the same branch-and-bound recipe yields optimal sparse <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision trees</NoteLink> and
  scoring systems <Cite id="rudin2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing $\lambda$.** It sets the exchange rate between rules and errors, and the search time. Choose it by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or by the size a reader can audit, and report it.
- **Optimal on the training data.** The certificate is about training risk. A list optimal on one sample can differ
  from the list optimal on another; check stability across folds.
- **Imbalanced classes.** With 0–1 loss, the optimum for a rare class can be the empty list predicting the majority.
  Weight the errors or change the objective before optimising.
- **Ties.** Many lists can share the optimal risk. The certificate covers the risk, not the uniqueness of the list.
