<Definition>
Pool a sample $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_{n_p}$ from $p$, labelled $y = 1$, with a sample $\xvec'_1, \dots,
\xvec'_{n_q}$ from $q$, labelled $y = 0$. Train a probabilistic classifier for $P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$. The density
ratio estimate is
$$
\hat r(\xvec) = \frac{n_q}{n_p}\, \frac{\hat P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)}{\hat P(y = 0 \mid \xvec)} ,
$$
the classifier's odds times the ratio of the sample sizes <Cite id="qin1998" /> <Cite id="bickel2009" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="density-ratio-estimation">density ratio</NoteLink> is needed, but only samples from $p$ and $q$ are
available. Dividing two density estimates is unreliable wherever either density is small. Classification is the most
developed area of machine learning, with fast solvers, regularisation and model selection for many model classes. The
classification route reuses all of it, and it needs only a classifier whose probabilities are trustworthy.

## Mechanism

### Odds are ratios

The pooled sample is a draw from the mixture in which a point comes from $p$ with probability
$\pi = n_p / (n_p + n_q)$ and from $q$ with probability $1 - \pi$. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem" />,
$$
P(y = 1 \mid \xvec) = \frac{\pi\, p(\xvec)}{\pi\, p(\xvec) + (1 - \pi)\, q(\xvec)} , \qquad
P(y = 0 \mid \xvec) = \frac{(1 - \pi)\, q(\xvec)}{\pi\, p(\xvec) + (1 - \pi)\, q(\xvec)} .
$$
The denominators cancel in the odds:
$$
\frac{P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)}{P(y = 0 \mid \xvec)} = \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}\, \frac{p(\xvec)}{q(\xvec)} =
\frac{n_p}{n_q}\, r(\xvec) .
$$
Multiplying the odds by $n_q / n_p$ removes the sample sizes and leaves $r(\xvec)$. Any classifier that estimates
$P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ consistently therefore estimates $r$ consistently.

### Logistic regression

<NoteLink to="logistic-regression" /> models the log odds as $\wvec^\top \phivec(\xvec)$ for a feature map
$\phivec$ whose first entry is 1. The log ratio estimate is then
$$
\log \hat r(\xvec) = \log \frac{n_q}{n_p} + \wvec^\top \phivec(\xvec) ,
$$
a log-linear model of the ratio. Qin studied this model in statistics, as the semiparametric two-sample density ratio
model, and showed that the logistic-regression estimate of $\wvec$ is its maximum-likelihood estimate
<Cite id="qin1998" />. The weights minimise the mean log loss over the pooled sample, usually with a penalty
$\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2$.

The features decide which ratios the model can represent. Two Gaussian densities with a shared covariance have a log
ratio linear in $\xvec$. Two Gaussians with different covariances have a log ratio quadratic in $\xvec$, so the
features $1$, $\xvec$ and the products $x_a x_b$ represent it exactly. Gaussian bumps $K(\xvec, \cvec_l)$ represent
smooth log ratios near their centres and flatten beyond them.

### The same estimator under another name

Logistic regression on the pooled sample minimises the expected logistic loss, and in the Bregman framework of
<NoteLink to="density-ratio-estimation" /> that loss is the density ratio objective generated by
$f(t) = t \log t - (1 + t) \log(1 + t)$. Every proper loss for class-probability estimation corresponds to a density
ratio objective in the same way <Cite id="menon2016" />. The same construction appears as the domain classifier in
<NoteLink to="importance-weighting-for-covariate-shift">covariate-shift correction</NoteLink>, as the noise classifier
of <Gloss name="nce" /> <Cite id="gutmann2010" />, as the discriminator of a
<NoteLink to="generative-adversarial-network" />, as the <NoteLink to="classifier-two-sample-test" />, and as the
likelihood ratio trick of simulation-based inference <Cite id="cranmer2020" />.

## Cost

The cost is that of training the classifier on $n_p + n_q$ points. For logistic regression with $k$ features, one
gradient evaluation costs $O((n_p + n_q) k)$ and the problem is convex, so a quasi-Newton solver converges in tens of
iterations. Evaluating $\hat r$ at a new point costs one evaluation of the classifier.

## Trade-offs

- **Calibration matters, accuracy does not.** The ratio is read from the predicted probabilities. A classifier with
  high accuracy but miscalibrated probabilities gives a wrong ratio, so
  <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration" /> is part of the estimator. Classifiers trained with proper losses are
  calibrated in the limit; boosted trees and large neural networks often need recalibration.
- **Separable samples.** When the two samples barely overlap, an unpenalised logistic regression drives its weights
  to infinity and the estimated ratio to 0 or $\infty$. The penalty $\lambda$ keeps the weights finite and the
  ratio bounded; it is chosen by cross-validation on the log loss.
- **Probabilities near 0 or 1.** The odds $\hat P / (1 - \hat P)$ amplify small errors in $\hat P$ near 1. Clipping
  $\hat P$ or the ratio limits the damage at the cost of bias.
- **Symmetric treatment.** The logistic loss treats the two samples alike, so it spends its accuracy where the
  densities overlap. <Gloss name="kliep" /> and <Gloss name="ulsif" /> weight errors by one density, $p$ or $q$,
  which suits importance weighting more directly.

## Worked example

Take $q = \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $p = \Gauss(1, 0.6^2)$, the default of the figure.

1. **Write out the log ratio.** $\log p(x) = -\log 0.6 - \tfrac{1}{2}\log 2\pi - (x - 1)^2 / 0.72$ and
   $\log q(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log 2\pi - x^2 / 2$.
2. **Subtract.** $\log r(x) = 0.5108 - (x^2 - 2x + 1)/0.72 + x^2/2 = -0.8889\, x^2 + 2.7778\, x - 0.8781$.
3. **Read off the features.** The log ratio is quadratic, so logistic regression with features $1$, $x$ and $x^2$
   can represent it exactly. Linear features $1$ and $x$ cannot: the best they can do is a straight line, which
   overestimates the ratio far out on both sides.
4. **Add the sample sizes.** With $n_p = 100$ and $n_q = 200$, the logistic model's log odds at the optimum are
   $\log r(x) + \log(n_p / n_q) = -0.8889\, x^2 + 2.7778\, x - 0.8781 - 0.6931$. The intercept absorbs the
   $-0.6931$, and subtracting it again recovers $\log r$.
5. **Check a point.** At $x = 1$, $\log r(1) = -0.8889 + 2.7778 - 0.8781 = 1.0108$, so $r(1) = 2.75$: near the
   mean of $p$, points are 2.75 times as dense under $p$ as under $q$.
