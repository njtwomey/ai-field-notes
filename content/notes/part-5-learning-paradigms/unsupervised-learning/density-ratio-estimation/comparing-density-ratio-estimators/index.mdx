<Definition>
The denominator is $q = \Gauss(0, 1)$ and the numerator $p = \Gauss(1, 0.6^2)$. Draw $n$ points from each, for $n$
from 10 to 1000, with each larger sample containing the smaller ones. Estimate $r(x) = p(x)/q(x)$ on $[-4, 5]$ by four
methods, and measure each estimate $\hat r$ by its squared error $\tfrac{1}{2}\expect_q[(\hat r - r)^2]$ and by its
generalised Kullback–Leibler error $\expect_p[\log(r/\hat r)] + \expect_q[\hat r] - 1$.
</Definition>

## Setup

- **The truth.** $\log r(x) = -0.8889\, x^2 + 2.7778\, x - 0.8781$, derived in
  <NoteLink to="density-ratio-by-classification" />. It peaks at $x = 1.5625$, where $r = 3.64$, and falls towards 0
  on both sides because $p$ is narrower than $q$.
- **Divided kernel density estimates.** $\hat p / \hat q$, each a Gaussian
  <NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density estimate</NoteLink> with Scott's bandwidth.
- **<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</NoteLink>.** Features $1$ and 20 Gaussian bumps of width
  $\sigma$ centred on points of the pooled sample, with the penalty $\lambda = 0.01$, fitted to convergence.
- **<Gloss name="kliep" />.** 50 <Gloss name="rbf-kernel">Gaussian kernels</Gloss> of width $\sigma$ centred on points
  of the sample from $p$, 80 steps of accelerated projected gradient.
- **<Gloss name="ulsif" />.** The same 50 kernels, with $\lambda$ chosen from $\set{0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1}$ by
  three-fold <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> at every $n$.

The three kernel methods share the width $\sigma$, so they differ only in their losses and their regularisation.

## Solution

The errors are integrals over the grid, computed from the true densities. The squared error is the quantity uLSIF
minimises in expectation. The Kullback–Leibler error is the divergence from $p$ to $\hat r\, q$ without the
normalisation, which KLIEP minimises. Neither error favours the logistic loss, which weights the two samples
symmetrically. All three losses belong to the Bregman family of <NoteLink to="density-ratio-estimation" />, so the
comparison is between three choices of the same principle <Cite id="sugiyama2012bregman" />.

## What it illustrates

- **Plug-in division fails in the tails.** With small samples, the quotient of two
  <NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density estimates</NoteLink> is far off wherever either sample has
  no points, and its error is many orders of magnitude above the others. It improves as the samples fill the tails,
  which is the reason <NoteLink to="density-ratio-estimation" /> argues for fitting the ratio directly.
- **Regularisation decides small samples.** uLSIF chooses its penalty by cross-validation at every size and stays
  close from the start <Cite id="kanamori2009ulsif" />. KLIEP and logistic regression, with no tuned penalty, follow
  individual points of small samples. KLIEP's width can be cross-validated on the held-out log-likelihood
  <Cite id="sugiyama2008kliep" />, and the classifier's penalty on the held-out log loss.
- **The two errors look at different places.** The squared error weights mistakes by $q$, so it is dominated by the
  tails of $q$ where $r$ is small. The Kullback–Leibler error weights the log of the ratio by $p$, so it is dominated by
  the region where $p$ has mass. An estimator chosen for
  <NoteLink to="importance-weighting-for-covariate-shift">importance weighting</NoteLink> should be judged by the error
  that matters for the weights.

## Variations

- **A wider numerator.** With the standard deviation of $p$ above 1, the true ratio grows without bound in both tails
  and $\expect_q[r^2]$ can be infinite. Every estimator then struggles in the tails; the relative density ratio is
  the usual remedy <Cite id="yamada2013" />.
- **A larger shift.** Moving the mean of $p$ away from 0 puts $p$'s mass where $q$ has few points. All errors grow,
  and the <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss> of the weights falls.
- **The kernel width.** A small $\sigma$ lets every kernel method overfit; a large one flattens the ratio towards a
  constant. The shared $\sigma$ in the figure shows how sensitive each method is to it.
- **More dimensions.** Kernel density estimates degrade quickly with dimension, while the direct methods degrade
  more slowly <Cite id="sugiyama2012book" />.
