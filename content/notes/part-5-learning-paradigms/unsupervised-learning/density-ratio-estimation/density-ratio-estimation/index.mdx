<Definition>
Let $p(\xvec)$ and $q(\xvec)$ be two probability densities on $\reals^d$, with $q(\xvec) > 0$ wherever
$p(\xvec) > 0$. Their **density ratio** is $r(\xvec) = p(\xvec)/q(\xvec)$. **Density ratio estimation** builds an
estimate $\hat r(\xvec)$ from a sample $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_{n_p}$ drawn from $p$ and a sample
$\xvec'_1, \dots, \xvec'_{n_q}$ drawn from $q$. The direct methods fit $\hat r$ itself and never form an estimate of
$p$ or of $q$ <Cite id="sugiyama2012book" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The ratio converts averages under $q$ into averages under $p$. For any function $f$,
$$
\expect_p\brack{f(\xvec)} = \int f(\xvec)\, p(\xvec)\, d\xvec = \int f(\xvec)\, r(\xvec)\, q(\xvec)\, d\xvec =
\expect_q\brack{r(\xvec) f(\xvec)} .
$$
A point drawn from $q$ and given weight $r(\xvec)$ therefore counts as a point drawn from $p$. This is
<NoteLink to="importance-sampling" /> with $q$ as the proposal. In importance sampling both densities are known
formulas. In density ratio estimation they are known only through samples, so the weights must be learned.

The first figure fixes $q = \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $p = \Gauss(m, s^2)$, uses the true ratio, and shows what the weights
do. The weighted histogram of the sample from $q$ follows $p$. Weighted averages of $f$ over that sample agree with
$\expect_p[f]$, and the plain averages do not. The <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss>
$(\sum_j w_j)^2 / \sum_j w_j^2$, with $w_j = r(\xvec'_j)$, counts how many unweighted points the weighted sample is
worth. It falls as $p$ moves away from $q$ or becomes wider than $q$, because a few points in the tail of $q$ then
carry most of the weight.

## Details

### Uses

- **Covariate shift.** A model is trained on inputs from $q$ and used on inputs from $p$. Weighting each training
  loss by $r(\xvec)$ makes the training risk an unbiased estimate of the risk in use <Cite id="shimodaira2000" />;
  <NoteLink to="importance-weighting-for-covariate-shift" /> covers this case.
- **Divergences and two-sample tests.** Every $f$-divergence is an expectation of a function of $r$, such as
  $\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \expect_p[\log r(\xvec)]$, so an estimate of $r$ gives an estimate of the divergence
  <Cite id="nguyen2010" />. A divergence estimated well above zero is evidence that the two samples come from
  different distributions, which <NoteLink to="two-sample-testing" /> makes precise.
- **Mutual information.** Mutual information is the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the product of the marginals to
  the joint distribution, so it is estimated from the ratio of the joint density to the product of the marginals.
- **Outlier and change detection.** With $p$ the density of new data and $q$ that of reference data, a small
  $r(\xvec)$ marks a point that is rare among the new data relative to the reference; comparing a recent window
  against an older one detects a change <Cite id="sugiyama2012book" />.
- **Learning by classification.** <Gloss name="nce" /> trains an unnormalised model by classifying data against
  noise, which estimates the ratio of the data density to the noise density <Cite id="gutmann2010" />. The
  discriminator of a <NoteLink to="generative-adversarial-network" /> estimates the ratio of the data density to the
  generator's density. Simulation-based inference estimates likelihood ratios the same way, from simulator output
  <Cite id="cranmer2020" />.

### Why not divide two density estimates

The plug-in estimate $\hat p(\xvec)/\hat q(\xvec)$, from two <NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density
estimates</NoteLink>, solves a harder problem than the one asked. A density estimate has to be accurate everywhere,
including where the ratio is flat and where the ratio is irrelevant. Vapnik's principle advises against solving a
more general problem as an intermediate step when only a special case is needed <Cite id="vapnik1998" />.

The quotient also amplifies errors. Where $q$ is small, $\hat q$ rests on a handful of points, and a small absolute
error in $\hat q$ is a large relative error in $1/\hat q$. Beyond the last point of either sample each kernel
estimate decays as a Gaussian of its bandwidth, so the quotient there is a ratio of two kernel tails. Its value is set
by the bandwidth and by the outermost few points, not by $p$ and $q$. The second figure shows this on a log scale. The
error grows with the dimension $d$, because kernel density estimates need a sample that grows exponentially in $d$
for a fixed accuracy.

### One principle behind the direct methods

Each direct method chooses a model $\hat r$ and a measure of its distance from $r$ whose sample estimate needs no
knowledge of $p$ or $q$. Let $f$ be a strictly convex, differentiable function on $(0, \infty)$. The **Bregman
divergence** generated by $f$, averaged under $q$, is
$$
\mathrm{BR}_f(r \,\|\, \hat r) = \int q(\xvec) \brack{f(r(\xvec)) - f(\hat r(\xvec)) - f'(\hat r(\xvec))
\paren{r(\xvec) - \hat r(\xvec)}} d\xvec .
$$
It is zero only when $\hat r = r$ wherever $q > 0$, and positive otherwise
(<NoteLink to="bregman-divergences-and-scoring-rules" />). The term $\int q f(r)$ does not depend on $\hat r$ and is
dropped. In the term $\int q\, f'(\hat r)\, r$, the product $q\, r$ is $p$. What remains is
$$
J_f(\hat r) = \expect_q\brack{f'(\hat r(\xvec))\, \hat r(\xvec) - f(\hat r(\xvec))} -
\expect_p\brack{f'(\hat r(\xvec))} ,
$$
and both expectations are estimated by averages over the two samples. Minimising the sample version of $J_f$ over a
model class is a density ratio estimator for each choice of $f$ <Cite id="sugiyama2012bregman" />.

| $f(t)$ | $J_f(\hat r)$ up to a constant | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\tfrac{1}{2}(t - 1)^2$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}\expect_q[\hat r^2] - \expect_p[\hat r]$ | <NoteLink to="least-squares-importance-fitting">least-squares importance fitting</NoteLink> |
| $t \log t - t$ | $\expect_q[\hat r] - \expect_p[\log \hat r]$ | <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-importance-estimation-procedure"><Gloss name="kliep" /></NoteLink> |
| $t \log t - (1 + t)\log(1 + t)$ | $\expect_q[\log(1 + \hat r)] + \expect_p[\log(1 + 1/\hat r)]$ | <NoteLink to="density-ratio-by-classification">logistic regression</NoteLink> |

<Derivation title="The three rows">
For $f(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}(t - 1)^2$, $f'(t) = t - 1$, so $f'(t)\,t - f(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}t^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}$ and
$J_f = \tfrac{1}{2}\expect_q[\hat r^2] - \expect_p[\hat r] + \tfrac{1}{2}$.

For $f(t) = t \log t - t$, $f'(t) = \log t$, so $f'(t)\,t - f(t) = t$ and $J_f = \expect_q[\hat r] -
\expect_p[\log \hat r]$.

For $f(t) = t \log t - (1 + t)\log(1 + t)$, $f'(t) = \log t - \log(1 + t) = \log\paren{t/(1 + t)}$. Then
$f'(t)\,t - f(t) = -t\log(1 + t) + (1 + t)\log(1 + t) = \log(1 + t)$, and
$-f'(t) = \log\paren{(1 + t)/t} = \log(1 + 1/t)$. With $\hat r = e^{g}$ for a score $g$, the two terms are the
logistic losses $\log(1 + e^{g})$ on the sample from $q$ (label 0) and $\log(1 + e^{-g})$ on the sample from $p$
(label 1).
</Derivation>

The choice of $f$ sets where errors cost most. The squared loss weights errors by $q$ and is bounded below for any
$\hat r$, which makes it a linear least-squares problem for a linear model. The Kullback–Leibler loss weights the log
of $\hat r$ by $p$, so it penalises underestimates where $p$ has mass. The logistic loss treats the two samples
symmetrically. Every proper loss for class-probability estimation gives a density ratio estimator in this way, and
the correspondence runs in both directions <Cite id="menon2016" />.

### Models and hyperparameters

The kernel methods use the model $\hat r(\xvec) = \sum_{l=1}^b \alpha_l K(\xvec, \cvec_l)$ with the
<Gloss name="rbf-kernel">Gaussian kernel</Gloss> $K(\xvec, \cvec) = \exp\paren{-\norm{\xvec - \cvec}^2 / 2\sigma^2}$.
The centres $\cvec_l$ are $b$ points of the sample from $p$, because the ratio is large where $p$ has mass. The width
$\sigma$ and any penalty are chosen by cross-validation on the objective $J_f$ itself, whose held-out value needs only
the two samples <Cite id="kanamori2009ulsif" />.

## Assumptions

- **Support.** $q(\xvec) > 0$ wherever $p(\xvec) > 0$. Where $q$ has no mass the ratio is undefined, and no sample
  from $q$ can reveal what $p$ does there.
- **Independent samples.** Each sample is drawn independently from its density.
- **A bounded ratio, in practice.** When $p$ has heavier tails than $q$, $r$ is unbounded and the weights have
  infinite variance. The **relative density ratio** $p/(\beta p + (1 - \beta) q)$ for some $\beta \in (0, 1)$ is
  bounded by $1/\beta$ and is estimated by the same methods <Cite id="yamada2013" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Weight collapse.** An estimated ratio can be accurate on average and still put most of the weight on a few
  points. The effective sample size of the weights shows this before the weights are used.
- **Errors where it matters.** Each loss is an average under $p$ or $q$. Errors in regions that neither sample
  visits are not penalised, and an estimator can be wildly wrong there while its loss stays small.
- **Normalisation.** The mean of $\hat r$ over the sample from $q$ should be close to 1. A large departure means the
  estimate is biased or the model class is too poor.
