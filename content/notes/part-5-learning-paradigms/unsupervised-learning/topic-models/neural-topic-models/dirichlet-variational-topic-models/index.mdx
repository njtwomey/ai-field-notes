<Definition>
A **Dirichlet variational topic model** is a <NoteLink to="neural-topic-models">neural topic model</NoteLink> whose
encoder outputs the parameters of a Dirichlet distribution
$q_{\phivec}(\thetavec_d \mid \wvec_d) = \Dir(\hat\alphavec_{\phivec}(\wvec_d))$ over a document's topic proportions, or
of independent <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distributions</NoteLink> over unnormalised proportions, instead
of a Gaussian in the softmax basis. The prior stays a true $\Dir(\alphavec)$ or gamma. The <Gloss name="elbo">evidence
lower bound</Gloss> is maximised by stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>, which need a
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of a Dirichlet or gamma sample with respect to its parameters. Four
constructions supply it: <NoteLink to="rejection-sampling">rejection-sampling</NoteLink> reparameterisation
<Cite id="naesseth2017" />, <NoteLink to="reparameterisations-of-common-distributions">implicit
reparameterisation</NoteLink> <Cite id="figurnov2018" />, an approximate inverse cdf <Cite id="joo2020dirvae" />, and a
Weibull surrogate <Cite id="zhang2018whai" />.
</Definition>

## Dirichlet versus logistic-normal posteriors

<NoteLink to="prodlda">AVITM and ProdLDA</NoteLink> replace the Dirichlet with a logistic normal, a Gaussian in the
softmax basis. The substitute is poor when the Dirichlet's parameters are below 1. That is the regime topic modellers
use to make each document concentrate on a few topics. A $\Dir(\alpha)$ with $\alpha < 1$ puts most of its mass near the
simplex's faces, with density rising without bound towards them; a logistic normal's density goes to zero there. A
Dirichlet posterior also has the right support by construction, and the <Gloss name="kl">KL divergence</Gloss> between
two Dirichlets has a closed form. The obstacle is the gradient.

## The Dirichlet through gamma variables

Every construction below samples a Dirichlet by normalising independent gamma draws. If $g_k \sim \operatorname{Gamma}
(\alpha_k, 1)$ independently for $k = 1, \dots, K$, then

$$
\thetavec = \frac{(g_1, \dots, g_K)}{\sum_k g_k} \sim \Dir(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K) .
$$

<Derivation title="Normalised gammas are Dirichlet">
The joint density of the gammas is $\prod_k g_k^{\alpha_k - 1}e^{-g_k}/\Gamma(\alpha_k)$. Change variables to the total
$s = \sum_k g_k$ and the proportions $\theta_k = g_k/s$ for $k < K$, with $\theta_K = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\theta_k$. The
inverse map is $g_k = s\theta_k$, whose <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian determinant</NoteLink> is $s^{K-1}$. The joint
density of $(s, \thetavec)$ is

$$
\prod_k\frac{(s\theta_k)^{\alpha_k - 1}}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)}\,e^{-s}\,s^{K-1} = \frac{\prod_k\theta_k^{\alpha_k -
1}}{\prod_k\Gamma(\alpha_k)}\;s^{\alpha_0 - 1}e^{-s},
$$

with $\alpha_0 = \sum_k\alpha_k$. It factorises. Integrating out $s$ gives $\Gamma(\alpha_0)$, so $\thetavec$ has
density $\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\prod_k\Gamma(\alpha_k)}\prod_k\theta_k^{\alpha_k - 1}$, the Dirichlet, independent
of the total $s \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha_0, 1)$.
</Derivation>

Normalisation is differentiable, so the problem reduces to differentiating a gamma draw $g \sim \operatorname{Gamma}
(\alpha, 1)$ with respect to its shape $\alpha$. The rate needs no work: $g/\beta \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,
\beta)$ is a scale transform.

The KL term needs no sampling. With $\expect_{\Dir(\hat\alphavec)}[\log\theta_k] = \psi(\hat\alpha_k) -
\psi(\hat\alpha_0)$, where $\psi$ is the digamma function, taking the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>
of the log ratio of the two densities gives

$$
\KL\paren{\Dir(\hat\alphavec) \,\|\, \Dir(\alphavec)} = \log\frac{\Gamma(\hat\alpha_0)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0)} -
\sum_k\log\frac{\Gamma(\hat\alpha_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)} + \sum_k(\hat\alpha_k - \alpha_k)\paren{\psi(\hat\alpha_k) -
\psi(\hat\alpha_0)} .
$$

## Rejection-sampling reparameterisation

Gamma variates are generated by rejection sampling. Marsaglia and Tsang's sampler for $\alpha \ge 1$ draws $\epsilon
\sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, proposes $g = h(\epsilon, \alpha) = (\alpha - \tfrac13)\paren{1 + \epsilon/\sqrt{9\alpha - 3}}^3$,
and accepts with a probability that makes accepted values exactly $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, 1)$. The map $h$ is
smooth in $\alpha$, but the accept step depends on $\alpha$ too.

Naesseth et al. treat the **accepted** $\epsilon$ as the noise <Cite id="naesseth2017" />. Its density is the
proposal density reweighted by the acceptance: if $s(\epsilon)$ is the density of $\epsilon$, $r(g \mid \alpha)$ the
density of the proposal $g = h(\epsilon, \alpha)$, and $q(g \mid \alpha)$ the target gamma density, then accepted
noise has density

$$
\pi(\epsilon \mid \alpha) = s(\epsilon)\,\frac{q\paren{h(\epsilon, \alpha) \mid \alpha}}{r\paren{h(\epsilon, \alpha)
\mid \alpha}} .
$$

This depends on $\alpha$, so differentiating $\expect_{\pi}[f(h(\epsilon, \alpha))]$ under the integral gives two terms,
by the product rule:

$$
\nabla_\alpha\expect_q[f(g)] = \underbrace{\expect_{\pi(\epsilon \mid \alpha)}\brack{f'(h)\,\nabla_\alpha
h(\epsilon, \alpha)}}_{\text{reparameterisation}} + \underbrace{\expect_{\pi(\epsilon \mid \alpha)}\brack{f(h)\,
\nabla_\alpha\log\frac{q(h(\epsilon, \alpha) \mid \alpha)}{r(h(\epsilon, \alpha) \mid \alpha)}}}_{\text{correction}} .
$$

The first term is the usual <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">pathwise gradient</NoteLink>. The second is a
score-function term that corrects for the $\alpha$-dependence of acceptance. Marsaglia and Tsang's acceptance rate is
high, so $q/r$ is nearly flat and the correction has low variance. For $\alpha < 1$, the sampler draws
$\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha + B, 1)$ and multiplies by $B$ uniform powers $u_i^{1/(\alpha + i - 1)}$, which pushes
the rejection step into the high-acceptance regime.

Burkhardt and Kramer built a Dirichlet <Gloss name="vae" /> topic model on the rejection-sampling estimator, and
factorised the Dirichlet parameter into a sparse binary vector and a smoothness vector so that sparsity and smoothness
are set separately <Cite id="burkhardt2019dvae" />.

## Implicit reparameterisation

A sample $g$ from any sampler satisfies $F(g; \alpha) = u$ for the cdf $F$ and some uniform $u$. Hold $u$ fixed and
differentiate both sides with respect to $\alpha$:

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial g}\frac{\partial g}{\partial\alpha} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial\alpha} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial\alpha} = -\frac{\partial F(g; \alpha)/\partial\alpha}{q(g
\mid \alpha)} .
$$

The sample can come from a rejection sampler, and the inverse cdf is never needed. Only the derivative of the cdf with
respect to the shape is, and Figurnov et al. compute it for the gamma by series expansions and numerical approximations
<Cite id="figurnov2018" />. The result is an exact pathwise gradient with no correction term. Figurnov et al. trained
<Gloss name="latent-dirichlet-allocation" /> with a Dirichlet posterior this way. Modern libraries use this estimator
when a gamma, beta or Dirichlet sample is drawn with gradients enabled.

## An approximate inverse cdf

For small $\alpha$ the gamma cdf has a simple asymptote. Near zero, $e^{-t} \approx 1$, so

$$
F(x; \alpha, \beta) = \int_0^x\frac{\beta^\alpha t^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta t}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,dt \approx
\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{x^\alpha}{\alpha} = \frac{(\beta x)^\alpha}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)} .
$$

Solving $F(x) = u$ for $x$ gives

$$
F^{-1}(u; \alpha, \beta) \approx \frac1\beta\paren{u\,\alpha\,\Gamma(\alpha)}^{1/\alpha},
$$

a closed-form, differentiable function of $\alpha$ and uniform noise $u$. The Dirichlet VAE of Joo et al. samples each
gamma component with this formula and normalises <Cite id="joo2020dirvae" />. The approximation is accurate when $x$ is
small, which covers most of the mass when $\alpha$ is well below 1. Joo et al. use a gamma KL divergence between
posterior and prior components with a shared rate, which has a closed form. They report that the Dirichlet latent
shows neither the decoder-weight collapse of a Gaussian VAE nor the latent-value collapse of a stick-breaking VAE.

## A Weibull surrogate: Weibull hybrid autoencoding inference (WHAI)

The Weibull distribution with shape $k$ and scale $\lambda$ has cdf $1 - e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$, which inverts in closed
form:

$$
x = \lambda\paren{-\log(1 - u)}^{1/k}, \qquad u \sim \Unif(0, 1) .
$$

Its density $\frac{k}{\lambda}(x/\lambda)^{k-1}e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}$ resembles a gamma density: a power of $x$ times a
decaying exponential. Zhang et al. use Weibull posteriors to approximate gamma-distributed latent variables
<Cite id="zhang2018whai" />. The KL divergence from a Weibull to a gamma with shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$ is
analytic. With $\expect[\log x] = \log\lambda - \gamma_{\mathrm E}/k$, $\expect[(x/\lambda)^k] = 1$ and $\expect[x] =
\lambda\Gamma(1 + 1/k)$ under the Weibull, where $\gamma_{\mathrm E}$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant, the expected log
densities give

$$
\KL\paren{\operatorname{Weibull}(k, \lambda) \,\|\, \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)} = \frac{\gamma_{\mathrm E}\alpha}{k}
- \alpha\log\lambda + \log k + \beta\lambda\Gamma\paren{1 + \tfrac1k} - \gamma_{\mathrm E} - 1 - \alpha\log\beta +
\log\Gamma(\alpha) .
$$

<Derivation title="The Weibull-to-gamma KL divergence">
Under the Weibull, $y = (x/\lambda)^k$ is standard exponential, so $\expect[y] = 1$ and $\expect[\log y] =
-\gamma_{\mathrm E}$, which gives $\expect[\log x] = \log\lambda - \gamma_{\mathrm E}/k$. The expected Weibull log
density is

$$
\log k - k\log\lambda + (k - 1)\paren{\log\lambda - \tfrac{\gamma_{\mathrm E}}{k}} - 1 = \log k - \log\lambda -
\gamma_{\mathrm E} + \tfrac{\gamma_{\mathrm E}}{k} - 1 .
$$

The expected gamma log density is $\alpha\log\beta - \log\Gamma(\alpha) + (\alpha - 1)\paren{\log\lambda -
\gamma_{\mathrm E}/k} - \beta\lambda\Gamma(1 + 1/k)$. Subtracting the second from the first and collecting the
$\log\lambda$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm E}/k$ terms gives the formula.
</Derivation>

WHAI's generative model is deep latent Dirichlet allocation, a Poisson gamma belief network. Word counts are Poisson,
$\wvec_d \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\Phimat^{(1)}\thetavec_d^{(1)})$, and each layer's non-negative hidden units are gamma
distributed with shape given by the layer above, $\thetavec_d^{(l)} \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\Phimat^{(l+1)}
\thetavec_d^{(l+1)}, 1/c^{(l+1)}_d)$. The columns of each $\Phimat^{(l)}$ are topics, which become more general with
depth. The inference network runs a deterministic network upwards from the counts and then samples Weibull variables
downwards, layer by layer. It is "hybrid" because the topics $\Phimat^{(l)}$ are sampled by stochastic-gradient MCMC
while the per-document variables use the autoencoder <Cite id="zhang2018whai" />.

## Worked example

**Weibull to gamma.** For $\operatorname{Weibull}(k = 2.3, \lambda = 1.7)$ and $\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha = 3,
\beta = 1.5)$, the terms are $\gamma_{\mathrm E}\alpha/k = 0.753$, $-\alpha\log\lambda = -1.592$, $\log k = 0.833$,
$\beta\lambda\Gamma(1 + 1/k) = 2.550 \times 0.886 = 2.259$, $-\gamma_{\mathrm E} - 1 = -1.577$, $-\alpha\log\beta =
-1.216$ and $\log\Gamma(3) = 0.693$. They sum to $0.153$ nats, which matches
<NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical integration</NoteLink> of the definition.

**Approximate inverse cdf.** For $\operatorname{Gamma}(0.1, 1)$ and $u = 0.5$, the approximation gives
$(0.5 \times 0.1 \times \Gamma(0.1))^{10} = (0.475)^{10} = 5.9 \times 10^{-4}$, equal to the exact median to two
significant figures. At $u = 0.9$ it gives $0.212$ against the exact $0.266$: the approximation is poor in the upper
tail, where $e^{-t} \approx 1$ fails. At $\alpha = 1$ and $u = 0.5$ it gives $0.5$ against the exact $\log 2 = 0.693$.

## Assumptions

- **Conditional independence of words given $\thetavec_d$**, as in LDA; the decoder is usually LDA's mixture.
- **An accurate gamma gradient.** Implicit and rejection-sampling gradients are exact in expectation. The inverse-cdf
  approximation and the Weibull surrogate change the distribution being optimised.
- **A mean-field posterior over the $K$ gamma components.** The Dirichlet posterior cannot represent correlations
  between topic proportions beyond those induced by normalisation.

## Pitfalls

- **Small shapes are numerically delicate.** Gamma draws with $\alpha \ll 1$ underflow to zero in floating point, and
  their log is $-\infty$. Clamp the draws or work with the log of the gamma variable.
- **Encoder outputs must stay positive.** Map the network's output to $\hat\alphavec$ through a softplus or exponential,
  and bound it below, because very small $\hat\alpha_k$ make the gradients large.
- **The Weibull cannot match every gamma.** For gamma shapes near zero or very large, the best Weibull is a poor fit,
  and the bound loosens <Cite id="zhang2018whai" />.
- **Comparing with logistic-normal models.** A Dirichlet posterior with a true Dirichlet prior and a logistic normal
  with a Laplace-approximated prior optimise different bounds, so their ELBOs are not comparable. Compare held-out
  likelihood estimates or <NoteLink to="topic-model-evaluation">topic quality</NoteLink> instead.
