<Definition>
A **topic model** explains a collection of documents by $K$ topics. Each topic is a distribution over a vocabulary of
$V$ words, and each document mixes the topics in its own proportions. Fitting the model recovers both from word counts
alone, without labels. Most topic models are probabilistic latent variable models built on latent Dirichlet allocation
(LDA) <Cite id="blei2012" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Topic models summarise and organise corpora too large to read: news archives, scientific literature, transcripts and
social-media posts. The topics give a browsable index of themes, the per-document proportions serve as compact features
for search and <NoteLink to="classification">classification</NoteLink>, and changes in proportions track themes across
time, authors or metadata. The same models apply wherever data come as groups of discrete tokens, such as genotypes,
images quantised into <NoteLink to="bag-of-visual-words-topic-models">visual words</NoteLink>, and streams of sensor
events.

## Main ideas

**Documents as bags of words.** The basic models ignore word order and keep only counts. A corpus becomes a $V \times D$
count matrix for $D$ documents, and a topic model approximates it by a low-rank product of word–topic and topic–document
factors. <NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis" /> does this with a truncated SVD;
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-latent-semantic-analysis">probabilistic latent semantic analysis</NoteLink> and LDA do it
with a generative mixture model.

**A generative story with priors.** <NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation" /> draws each document's topic
proportions from a Dirichlet, then draws each word by first choosing a topic <Cite id="blei2003" />. The priors make the
model apply to new documents and regularise rare words and short documents.

**Approximate inference.** The LDA posterior has no closed form. Collapsed Gibbs sampling and variational EM are the two
classical approximations, and the choice between them follows each extension through the chapter: models that keep
every Dirichlet–categorical pair conjugate are fitted by Gibbs samplers, and the others by variational methods.

**Changing one assumption at a time.** Each extension relaxes one assumption of LDA: topics fixed over time, mixtures
owned by documents, words as the only observation, independent topics, a fixed flat set of $K$ topics, or documents long
enough to estimate a mixture. <NoteLink to="topic-model-family" /> maps the extensions by the assumption each one drops.

**Neural networks as inference machines.** <NoteLink to="neural-topic-models" /> train an encoder that maps a document
to its topic proportions by amortised variational inference. Other neural models place topics in a word-embedding space
or cluster <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence embeddings</NoteLink>. Evaluation by perplexity, coherence and
human judgement applies to all of them, and the measures often disagree.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The chapter assumes the <NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution" /> and <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical
distribution</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation–maximisation</NoteLink>, and either
<NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling" /> or <NoteLink to="variational-inference">variational inference</NoteLink>. The neural
models also need the <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational autoencoder</NoteLink>.

Topic models are a form of <NoteLink to="dimensionality-reduction">dimensionality reduction</NoteLink> for counts and a
soft version of <NoteLink to="clustering">clustering</NoteLink>, in which each document belongs to several clusters.
Dense <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink> and the pretrained encoders of
<NoteLink to="representation-learning">representation learning</NoteLink> now supply document representations in many
tasks that once used topic proportions.
