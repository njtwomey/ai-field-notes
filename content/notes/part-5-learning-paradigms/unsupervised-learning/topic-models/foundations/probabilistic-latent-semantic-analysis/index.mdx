<Definition>
**Probabilistic latent semantic analysis** (PLSA), or the **aspect model**, describes a corpus by the counts $n(d, w)$
of word $w$ in document $d$ <Cite id="hofmann1999" />. Each word occurrence has an unobserved topic
$z \in \set{1, \dots, K}$, and given the topic, the word is independent of the document:

$$
P(w \mid d) = \sum_{z=1}^K P(z \mid d)\,P(w \mid z).
$$

The parameters are the topic–word distributions $P(w \mid z)$ and one topic mixture $P(z \mid d)$ per training
document. They maximise the log-likelihood $\ell = \sum_{d,w} n(d, w)\log P(w \mid d)$.
</Definition>

## From LSA to a probabilistic model

<NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis">Latent semantic analysis</NoteLink> factorises the term–document matrix with a
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">truncated SVD</NoteLink>. The factors can be negative and have no
probabilistic meaning. PLSA keeps the idea of a low-rank factorisation but makes each factor a probability distribution.
In matrix form, the $D \times V$ matrix of $P(w \mid d)$ is the product of a $D \times K$ matrix of topic mixtures and a
$K \times V$ matrix of topics, both with non-negative rows that sum to 1. The fitting criterion is the likelihood of the
observed counts, a <Gloss name="kl">KL divergence</Gloss> between the empirical word distributions and the model, rather
than squared error.

## EM derivation

Treat the topic of each occurrence as missing data. The **E-step** computes its posterior by
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>:

$$
P(z \mid d, w) = \frac{P(z \mid d)\,P(w \mid z)}{\sum_{z'}P(z' \mid d)\,P(w \mid z')}.
$$

The **M-step** maximises the expected complete-data log-likelihood

$$
Q = \sum_{d,w} n(d, w)\sum_z P(z \mid d, w)\LB\log P(z \mid d) + \log P(w \mid z)\RB
$$

subject to $\sum_w P(w \mid z) = 1$ for each $z$ and $\sum_z P(z \mid d) = 1$ for each $d$. The two sets of parameters
separate. For $P(w \mid z)$, the Lagrangian $\sum_{d,w} n(d, w)P(z \mid d, w)\log P(w \mid z) - \lambda_z(\sum_w P(w
\mid z) - 1)$ is stationary where $P(w \mid z) = \sum_d n(d, w)P(z \mid d, w)/\lambda_z$. The constraint fixes
$\lambda_z$ as the sum over $w$ of the numerator. The same argument applies to $P(z \mid d)$:

$$
P(w \mid z) = \frac{\sum_d n(d, w)\,P(z \mid d, w)}{\sum_{d,w'} n(d, w')\,P(z \mid d, w')}, \qquad
P(z \mid d) = \frac{\sum_w n(d, w)\,P(z \mid d, w)}{n(d)},
$$

with $n(d) = \sum_w n(d, w)$ the length of document $d$. Each is an expected count, normalised. As with any
<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">EM algorithm</NoteLink>, the likelihood never decreases, and the result depends
on the initialisation.

## Relation to non-negative matrix factorisation and latent Dirichlet allocation

- **NMF.** PLSA's likelihood is, up to constants, the generalised KL divergence between the count matrix and a
  non-negative product $\Wmat\Hmat$. Gaussier and Goutte showed that
  <NoteLink to="non-negative-matrix-factorisation">non-negative matrix factorisation</NoteLink> with the KL divergence
  and PLSA optimise the same objective, and that NMF's multiplicative updates, suitably normalised, are PLSA's EM
  updates <Cite id="gaussier2005" />.
- **LDA.** <NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">Latent Dirichlet allocation</NoteLink> puts a Dirichlet prior on
  each document's mixture and on each topic <Cite id="blei2003" />. PLSA's mixtures are free parameters, one set per
  training document. Their number grows linearly with the corpus, which invites overfitting, and the model defines no
  probability for a new document. LDA is a full generative model of documents and handles new ones by inference.

## Tempered EM and new documents

Hofmann reduced overfitting with **tempered EM**: the E-step posterior is raised to a power $0 < \tau \le 1$ and
renormalised, which flattens it. $\tau$ is lowered until the likelihood of held-out data stops improving
<Cite id="hofmann2001" />.

To place a new document, **folding in** runs EM on that document alone, updating only its mixture $P(z \mid d)$ and
keeping the topics fixed.

## Worked example

Two documents over three words, with counts $n(d_1, \cdot) = (2, 1, 0)$ and $n(d_2, \cdot) = (0, 1, 2)$, and $K = 2$.
Start from $P(w \mid z_1) = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$, $P(w \mid z_2) = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$ and $P(z \mid d) = (0.5, 0.5)$ for
both documents. The initial log-likelihood is $-6.607$.

- **E-step.** For word 1 in either document, $P(z_1 \mid d, w_1) = \frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times
  0.2} = 0.714$. For word 2 it is $0.5$, and for word 3 it is $0.286$.
- **M-step, topics.** The expected count of word 1 from topic 1 is $2 \times 0.714 = 1.429$; of word 2,
  $1 \times 0.5 + 1 \times 0.5 = 1$; of word 3, $2 \times 0.286 = 0.571$. Normalising by their sum, 3, gives
  $P(w \mid z_1) = (0.476, 0.333, 0.190)$. By symmetry $P(w \mid z_2) = (0.190, 0.333, 0.476)$.
- **M-step, mixtures.** For $d_1$, the expected number of words from topic 1 is $2 \times 0.714 + 1 \times 0.5 =
  1.929$ out of 3, so $P(z_1 \mid d_1) = 0.643$. By symmetry $P(z_1 \mid d_2) = 0.357$.
- The log-likelihood rises to $-6.130$.

## Pitfalls

- **Overfitting.** The number of parameters, $K(V - 1) + D(K - 1)$, grows with the corpus. Use tempered EM, held-out
  data, or LDA.
- **Local maxima.** Different initialisations give different topics. Run several and keep the best likelihood.
- **No generative model for new documents.** Folding in is a heuristic step that fits a new document's mixture by
  maximum likelihood.
