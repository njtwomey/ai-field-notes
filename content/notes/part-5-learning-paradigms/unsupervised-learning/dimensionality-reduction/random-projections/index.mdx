<Definition>
A **random projection** maps $\xvec \in \reals^D$ to $\Rmat\xvec \in \reals^k$, where $\Rmat$ is a $k \times D$ matrix
of independent entries with mean 0 and variance $1/k$, for example $R_{lj} \sim \Gauss(0, 1/k)$. The
**Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma** states that for any $0 < \varepsilon < 1$ and any $n$ points in $\reals^D$, if
$k \ge 4\ln n/(\varepsilon^2/2 - \varepsilon^3/3)$ then a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink>
$f : \reals^D \to \reals^k$ exists with

$$
(1 - \varepsilon)\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2 \le \norm{f(\xvec_i) - f(\xvec_j)}^2 \le (1 + \varepsilon)
\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2 \quad \text{for all } i, j,
$$

and a Gaussian random projection is such a map with probability at least $1/n$ <Cite id="johnson1984" />
<Cite id="dasgupta2003" />.
</Definition>

## Dimension that depends on the number of points only

<Gloss name="pca" /> finds the best $k$-dimensional subspace for a particular data set, but it gives no guarantee for
any individual pair of points: two points that differ only along a discarded direction are mapped to the same place. A
random projection ignores the data. It keeps every pairwise distance up to a small factor, and the dimension it needs
grows with the logarithm of the number of points $n$. The ambient dimension $D$ does not appear in the bound at all. Ten
thousand points in a million dimensions and ten thousand points in a hundred thousand dimensions need the same $k$.

## Proof of the lemma

The proof follows Dasgupta and Gupta <Cite id="dasgupta2003" />. Fix one vector $\uvec = \xvec_i - \xvec_j \ne
\zeros$. The argument shows that $\norm{\Rmat\uvec}^2$ is close to $\norm{\uvec}^2$ with overwhelming probability, and
then takes a union bound over all pairs.

**The projection is unbiased.** Row $l$ of $\Rmat$ gives the coordinate $(\Rmat\uvec)_l = \sum_j R_{lj}u_j$. Its mean
is 0, and its variance is $\sum_j \var(R_{lj})u_j^2 = \norm{\uvec}^2/k$ because the entries are independent. Hence

$$
\expect\norm{\Rmat\uvec}^2 = \sum_{l=1}^k \expect(\Rmat\uvec)_l^2 = k\cdot\frac{\norm{\uvec}^2}{k} = \norm{\uvec}^2.
$$

This holds for any entry distribution with mean 0 and variance $1/k$.

**Gaussian entries give a chi-squared law.** With Gaussian entries, each coordinate $(\Rmat\uvec)_l$ is a sum of
independent Gaussians, so $(\Rmat\uvec)_l \sim \Gauss(0, \norm{\uvec}^2/k)$, independently across $l$. Therefore

$$
Y = \frac{k\norm{\Rmat\uvec}^2}{\norm{\uvec}^2} \sim \chi^2_k,
$$

a <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared variable</NoteLink> with $k$ degrees of freedom and mean $k$. Its
<NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">moment generating function</NoteLink> is
$\expect e^{tY} = (1 - 2t)^{-k/2}$ for $t < \frac12$.

**Upper tail.** For any $0 < t < \frac12$, <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's
inequality</NoteLink> applied to $e^{tY}$ gives

$$
\pr\paren{Y \ge (1 + \varepsilon)k} \le e^{-t(1 + \varepsilon)k}(1 - 2t)^{-k/2}.
$$

The exponent $-t(1 + \varepsilon)k - \frac k2\ln(1 - 2t)$ has <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>
$-(1 + \varepsilon)k + k/(1 - 2t)$, which vanishes at $1 - 2t = 1/(1 + \varepsilon)$, that is
$t = \varepsilon/(2(1 + \varepsilon))$. Substituting,

$$
\pr\paren{Y \ge (1 + \varepsilon)k} \le \paren{(1 + \varepsilon)e^{-\varepsilon}}^{k/2} \le
\exp\paren{-k\paren{\frac{\varepsilon^2}{4} - \frac{\varepsilon^3}{6}}},
$$

where the last step uses $\ln(1 + \varepsilon) \le \varepsilon - \varepsilon^2/2 + \varepsilon^3/3$.

**Lower tail.** Similarly, for $t > 0$, $\pr\paren{Y \le (1 - \varepsilon)k} \le e^{t(1 - \varepsilon)k}(1 +
2t)^{-k/2}$. The optimum is at $1 + 2t = 1/(1 - \varepsilon)$, and

$$
\pr\paren{Y \le (1 - \varepsilon)k} \le \paren{(1 - \varepsilon)e^{\varepsilon}}^{k/2} \le
\exp\paren{-\frac{k\varepsilon^2}{4}},
$$

using $\ln(1 - \varepsilon) \le -\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2/2$. This is smaller than the upper-tail bound.

**Union bound.** Each of the $n(n-1)/2$ pairs fails in one of two tails with probability at most
$2\exp\paren{-k(\varepsilon^2/4 - \varepsilon^3/6)}$. If $k \ge 4\ln n/(\varepsilon^2/2 - \varepsilon^3/3)$, then
$k(\varepsilon^2/4 - \varepsilon^3/6) \ge 2\ln n$, and each pair fails with probability at most $2/n^2$. The
probability that any pair fails is at most $n(n-1)/2 \cdot 2/n^2 = 1 - 1/n$. So a random $\Rmat$ succeeds with
probability at least $1/n$, which proves that a suitable map exists. Drawing $\Rmat$ again until every pair passes takes
$n$ draws on average. Raising the constant 4 to $4 + 2\beta$ makes a single draw fail with probability at most
$n^{-\beta}$.

The bound $k = O(\log n/\varepsilon^2)$ cannot be improved in general: Larsen and Nelson constructed $n$ points for
which every linear or nonlinear map with distortion $1 \pm \varepsilon$ needs $\Omega(\log n/\varepsilon^2)$ dimensions
<Cite id="larsen2017" />.

## Sparse and structured projections

Multiplying by a dense Gaussian matrix costs $O(kD)$ per point. Achlioptas showed that entries
$\sqrt{3/k}\cdot\set{+1, 0, -1}$ with probabilities $\frac16, \frac23, \frac16$, or simply $\pm 1/\sqrt k$ with equal
probability, satisfy the same bound <Cite id="achlioptas2003" />. The first has two thirds of its entries zero and
saves two thirds of the multiplications. Li, Hastie and Church went further: nonzero entries with probability $1/s$,
with $s = \sqrt D$, save a factor $\sqrt D$ <Cite id="li2006" />.

The price of sparsity appears in the variance. Write the unscaled entries as $r_j$ with $\expect r_j = 0$,
$\expect r_j^2 = 1$ and $\expect r_j^4 = \kappa$, and let $z = \sum_j r_ju_j$. Expanding $z^4$ and keeping the terms
with nonzero mean,

$$
\expect z^4 = \kappa\sum_j u_j^4 + 3\sum_{j \ne m}u_j^2u_m^2 = 3\norm{\uvec}^4 + (\kappa - 3)\sum_j u_j^4,
$$

so $\var(z^2) = 2\norm{\uvec}^4 + (\kappa - 3)\sum_j u_j^4$. Averaging $k$ independent rows,

$$
\var\paren{\frac{\norm{\Rmat\uvec}^2}{\norm{\uvec}^2}} = \frac1k\paren{2 + (\kappa - 3)\frac{\sum_j
u_j^4}{\norm{\uvec}^4}}.
$$

Gaussian entries and Achlioptas's three-valued entries both have $\kappa = 3$, so their variance is $2/k$ for every
vector. Random signs have $\kappa = 1$ and a smaller variance. Entries with sparsity $s$ have $\kappa = s$: on a dense
vector the ratio $\sum_j u_j^4/\norm{\uvec}^4$ is of order $1/D$ and the extra term is negligible, but on a vector
with a few large coordinates the ratio is near 1, and the projection often misses those coordinates entirely.

Structured transforms reduce the cost further. The fast Johnson–Lindenstrauss transform first spreads each vector out
with a randomised Hadamard transform, in $O(D\log D)$, and then applies a sparse projection <Cite id="ailon2009" />.
Kane and Nelson gave projections with only $O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)$ nonzeros per column <Cite id="kane2014" />.

## Worked example

- **Target dimension.** For $n = 10^6$ points and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the lemma asks for
  $k \ge 4\ln(10^6)/(0.005 - 0.000333) = 4 \times 13.816/0.004667 = 11{,}842$ dimensions. For $\varepsilon = 0.5$ it
  asks for $4 \times 13.816/(0.125 - 0.0417) = 664$. Both hold for $D = 10^5$ and $D = 10^9$ alike. For $n = 10^4$ and
  $\varepsilon = 0.1$ the requirement drops to 7,895.
- **The tail bound is loose.** For $k = 100$ and $\varepsilon = 0.2$, the exact probability that $\chi^2_{100}$
  exceeds 120 is 0.084. The bound $\exp(-100(0.01 - 0.00133)) = 0.420$ is five times larger. In the figure, a Gaussian
  projection of the dense points to 400 dimensions keeps all 1770 pairs within a factor $1 \pm 0.254$, while the
  guarantee is only $1 \pm 0.32$.
- **Spread.** With Gaussian entries the ratio $\norm{\Rmat\uvec}^2/\norm{\uvec}^2$ has standard deviation
  $\sqrt{2/k}$: 0.20 at $k = 50$ and 0.071 at $k = 400$.

## Relatives

- **Locality-sensitive hashing.** The signs of random projections, $\sgn(\rvec_l^\top\xvec)$, are the random-hyperplane
  hash functions of <NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hashing</NoteLink> for
  <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink>.
- **Random projection trees** split space along <NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere">random directions</NoteLink>;
  see <NoteLink to="tree-based-approximate-nearest-neighbours">tree-based nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink>.
- **Random features.** <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">Random Fourier features</NoteLink> are a random
  projection followed by a cosine, and approximate a shift-invariant kernel.

## Pitfalls

- **Not for visualisation.** The guarantee needs hundreds or thousands of dimensions. A random projection to two
  dimensions preserves nothing reliably.
- **Squared distances, not structure.** Distances are kept approximately, but a projection chosen without looking at
  the data does not concentrate signal in the first coordinates, as PCA does.
- **Inner products.** Inner products are preserved only up to an additive error proportional to
  $\varepsilon\norm{\xvec}\norm{\yvec}$, which is large relative to small inner products.
- **Sparse data and sparse matrices.** Very sparse projections of vectors with a few large entries have much larger
  variance; see the formula above.
