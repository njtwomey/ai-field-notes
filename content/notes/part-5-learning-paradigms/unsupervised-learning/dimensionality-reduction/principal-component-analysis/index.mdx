<Definition>
Let $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n \in \reals^d$ be data with mean $\bar\xvec$, and let
$\Smat = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (\xvec_i - \bar\xvec)(\xvec_i - \bar\xvec)^\top$ be the $d \times d$ sample covariance.
**Principal component analysis** (PCA) computes the <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>
$\Smat = \Umat\Lambdamat\Umat^\top$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$ and orthonormal
eigenvectors $\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_d$, the **principal directions**. The $j$-th **principal component score** of point
$i$ is $z_{ij} = \uvec_j^\top(\xvec_i - \bar\xvec)$. Reducing to $k$ dimensions keeps the first $k$ scores
<Cite id="hotelling1933" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $n$, $d$ | Number of points; number of features. |
| $\Xmat$ | The $n \times d$ centred data matrix, with rows $(\xvec_i - \bar\xvec)^\top$. |
| $\Smat$ | Sample covariance $\frac1n\Xmat^\top\Xmat$. Some texts divide by $n - 1$; the directions are the same. |
| $\uvec_j$, $\lambda_j$ | $j$-th principal direction (unit vector in $\reals^d$) and the variance along it. |
| $\Umat_k$ | The $d \times k$ matrix $[\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_k]$. |
| $\zvec_i$ | Scores of point $i$: $\zvec_i = \Umat_k^\top(\xvec_i - \bar\xvec) \in \reals^k$. |
| $\hat\xvec_i$ | Reconstruction $\bar\xvec + \Umat_k\zvec_i$. |

From here on the data are centred, $\bar\xvec = \zeros$, so $\Smat = \frac1n\sum_i \xvec_i\xvec_i^\top$.

## First view: maximum variance

Project every point onto a unit vector $\uvec$. The projections $\uvec^\top\xvec_i$ have mean zero and variance

$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (\uvec^\top\xvec_i)^2 = \uvec^\top\paren{\frac1n\sum_i \xvec_i\xvec_i^\top}\uvec =
\uvec^\top\Smat\uvec.
$$

Hotelling's first principal direction maximises this variance over unit vectors <Cite id="hotelling1933" />. With a
<NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> $\lambda$ for the constraint $\uvec^\top\uvec = 1$,
the stationarity condition of $\uvec^\top\Smat\uvec - \lambda(\uvec^\top\uvec - 1)$ is

$$
2\Smat\uvec - 2\lambda\uvec = \zeros \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Smat\uvec = \lambda\uvec.
$$

So every stationary direction is an eigenvector of $\Smat$. At an eigenvector the variance is
$\uvec^\top\Smat\uvec = \lambda\uvec^\top\uvec = \lambda$. The maximum is therefore the largest eigenvalue
$\lambda_1$, attained at its eigenvector $\uvec_1$.

The second direction maximises $\uvec^\top\Smat\uvec$ over unit vectors orthogonal to $\uvec_1$. Expand any such
$\uvec$ in the orthonormal eigenbasis, $\uvec = \sum_{j \ge 2} c_j\uvec_j$ with $\sum_j c_j^2 = 1$. Then
$\uvec^\top\Smat\uvec = \sum_{j \ge 2} \lambda_j c_j^2 \le \lambda_2$, with equality at $\uvec = \uvec_2$. By the same
argument the $j$-th direction is $\uvec_j$. The scores along different directions are uncorrelated, because
$\frac1n\sum_i (\uvec_j^\top\xvec_i)(\uvec_l^\top\xvec_i) = \uvec_j^\top\Smat\uvec_l = \lambda_l\uvec_j^\top\uvec_l = 0$
for $j \ne l$.

## Second view: minimum reconstruction error

Pearson asked for the line, or plane, that lies closest to the points in squared perpendicular distance
<Cite id="pearson1901" />. For a $k$-dimensional subspace with orthonormal basis $\Umat_k$, the closest point to
$\xvec$ is its <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> $\Pmat\xvec$ with
$\Pmat = \Umat_k\Umat_k^\top$. The projection and the residual are orthogonal, so by Pythagoras

$$
\norm{\xvec_i}^2 = \norm{\Pmat\xvec_i}^2 + \norm{\xvec_i - \Pmat\xvec_i}^2.
$$

Average over the points. The left side is $\frac1n\sum_i\norm{\xvec_i}^2 = \trace(\Smat)$, the total variance. The
first term on the right is the variance retained by the projection, $\trace(\Umat_k^\top\Smat\Umat_k) =
\sum_{j=1}^k \uvec_j^\top\Smat\uvec_j$. The second is the mean squared reconstruction error. Hence

$$
\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \norm{\xvec_i - \Umat_k\Umat_k^\top\xvec_i}^2}_{\text{reconstruction error}} =
\trace(\Smat) - \underbrace{\trace(\Umat_k^\top\Smat\Umat_k)}_{\text{retained variance}}.
$$

The total variance does not depend on the subspace. **Minimising the reconstruction error and maximising the retained
variance are the same problem.** The optimum is the span of $\uvec_1, \dots, \uvec_k$: the retained variance is at
most $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k$ (derivation below), and those eigenvectors attain it. The minimum error is the sum
of the discarded eigenvalues, $\sum_{j > k}\lambda_j$.

<ProjectionAxis />

<Derivation title="Why no k-dimensional subspace retains more than the top k eigenvalues (Ky Fan)">
Let $\Umat_k$ have orthonormal columns and put $\Bmat = \Umat^\top\Umat_k$, where $\Umat$ holds all eigenvectors of
$\Smat$. $\Bmat$ also has orthonormal columns. Then

$$
\trace(\Umat_k^\top\Smat\Umat_k) = \trace(\Bmat^\top\Lambdamat\Bmat) = \sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j c_j, \qquad
c_j = \sum_{m=1}^k B_{jm}^2.
$$

Each $c_j$ is a diagonal entry of the projection $\Bmat\Bmat^\top$, so $0 \le c_j \le 1$, and
$\sum_j c_j = \trace(\Bmat^\top\Bmat) = k$. A weighted sum of $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ with weights in
$[0, 1]$ that sum to $k$ is at most $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k$, reached by putting weight 1 on the first $k$.
</Derivation>

## Computing PCA with the SVD

Take the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> of the centred data
matrix, $\Xmat = \Qmat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$, with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots$. Then

$$
\Smat = \frac1n\Xmat^\top\Xmat = \frac1n\Vmat\Sigmamat\Qmat^\top\Qmat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top =
\Vmat\,\frac{\Sigmamat^2}{n}\,\Vmat^\top.
$$

This is an eigendecomposition of $\Smat$. The principal directions are the right singular vectors, $\uvec_j =
\vvec_j$, and the variances are $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2/n$. The scores are $\Xmat\Vmat = \Qmat\Sigmamat$: the left
singular vectors scaled by the singular values. Truncating to $k$ terms gives
$\Xmat_k = \Qmat_k\Sigmamat_k\Vmat_k^\top$, the rank-$k$ matrix closest to $\Xmat$ in
<NoteLink to="matrix-norms">Frobenius norm</NoteLink> (the Eckart–Young theorem) <Cite id="eckart1936" />, which is the
reconstruction-error view restated for the whole data matrix.

The SVD route never forms $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$, whose condition number is the square of that of $\Xmat$, so it is more
accurate. It costs $O(nd\min(n, d))$. Randomised and iterative methods compute only the top $k$ components, in about
$O(ndk)$.

## Choosing the number of components

- **Explained variance.** Component $j$ explains the fraction $\lambda_j/\sum_l\lambda_l$ of the total variance. Keep
  enough components to reach a target such as 90% or 95%.
- **Scree plot.** Plot $\lambda_j$ against $j$ and keep the components before the curve levels off into a flat
  "scree" of noise.
- **Kaiser's rule.** On standardised data, keep components with $\lambda_j > 1$: those that explain more than one
  original feature does. It often keeps too many <Cite id="jolliffe2002" />.
- **Downstream performance.** When PCA feeds a model, choose $k$ by cross-validating the model.

## Standardisation

PCA is not invariant to the scale of each feature. A feature measured in millimetres has $100^2$ times the variance of
the same feature in decimetres, and it dominates the first component. When features have different units, divide each
by its standard deviation first. This is PCA of the correlation matrix instead of the covariance matrix. When features
share units and their variances are meaningful, as for pixel intensities, keep the covariance
<Cite id="jolliffe2016" />.

## Worked example

Five centred points in the plane: $(-2, -1)$, $(-1, -1)$, $(0, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(2, 1)$. Then
$\sum_i x_{i1}^2 = 10$, $\sum_i x_{i1}x_{i2} = 6$ and $\sum_i x_{i2}^2 = 4$, so

$$
\Smat = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

- **Eigenvalues.** $\trace\Smat = 2.8$ and $\det\Smat = 1.6 - 1.44 = 0.16$, so
  $\lambda^2 - 2.8\lambda + 0.16 = 0$ and $\lambda_{1,2} = 1.4 \pm \sqrt{1.96 - 0.16} = 1.4 \pm 1.342$, that is
  $\lambda_1 = 2.742$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.058$.
- **Direction.** $(\Smat - \lambda_1\Imat)\uvec = \zeros$ gives $u_2/u_1 = (\lambda_1 - 2)/1.2 = 0.618$, so
  $\uvec_1 = (0.851, 0.526)$, at $31.7°$ to the first axis.
- **Scores.** $z_i = \uvec_1^\top\xvec_i = -2.227, -1.376, 0, 1.376, 2.227$. Their mean square is $2.742 = \lambda_1$.
- **Explained variance.** The first component explains $2.742/2.8 = 97.9\%$ of the variance. The mean squared
  reconstruction error with one component is $\lambda_2 = 0.058$.
- **SVD.** The singular values of the $5 \times 2$ data matrix are $3.703$ and $0.540$; $3.703^2/5 = 2.742$ and
  $0.540^2/5 = 0.058$, as the SVD route predicts.

## Relatives

- A linear <NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink> with squared error learns the same subspace as PCA.
- <NoteLink to="probabilistic-principal-component-analysis">Probabilistic PCA</NoteLink> is a latent-variable model
  whose maximum-likelihood solution is PCA, and <NoteLink to="factor-analysis">factor analysis</NoteLink> relaxes its
  isotropic noise.
- <NoteLink to="multidimensional-scaling">Classical multidimensional scaling</NoteLink> with Euclidean distances gives
  the same coordinates as the PCA scores.
- <NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis">Latent semantic analysis</NoteLink> is a truncated SVD of a term–document
  matrix, usually without centring.
- <NoteLink to="kernel-principal-component-analysis">Kernel PCA</NoteLink> replaces $\Xmat\Xmat^\top$ by a kernel matrix
  and finds nonlinear components.
- <NoteLink to="random-projections">Random projections</NoteLink> reduce dimension without looking at the data and
  keep all pairwise distances approximately.

## Pitfalls

- **Centre first.** Without centring, the first direction points toward the mean rather than along the spread.
- **Signs are arbitrary.** $\uvec_j$ and $-\uvec_j$ are both unit eigenvectors. Different libraries return different
  signs.
- **Variance is not relevance.** The high-variance directions need not be the ones that predict a target or separate
  classes. <NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">Linear discriminant analysis</NoteLink> uses the labels instead.
- **Outliers.** Squared error lets a few extreme points rotate the components toward them.
- **Linear only.** PCA finds a flat subspace. Data on a curved manifold needs a nonlinear method such as
  <NoteLink to="isomap">Isomap</NoteLink> or
  <NoteLink to="t-distributed-stochastic-neighbour-embedding">t-SNE</NoteLink>.
