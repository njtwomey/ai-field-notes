<Definition>
For observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with sample mean $\bar{x}$ and sample <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> $s$, the z-score of $x_i$ is $z_i = (x_i - \bar{x})/s$. The robust z-score replaces the mean by the
median $m = \operatorname{med}_j x_j$ and the standard deviation by the scaled median absolute deviation,

$$
r_i = \frac{x_i - m}{1.4826\,\text{MAD}}, \qquad \text{MAD} = \operatorname{med}_j \abs{x_j - m}.
$$

An observation is flagged when $\abs{z_i}$ or $\abs{r_i}$ exceeds a threshold, usually 3 or 3.5.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A z-score measures how far a value lies from the centre of the data, in units of the data's spread. For Gaussian data,
$\abs{z} > 3$ occurs with probability $0.0027$, so a value beyond three standard deviations is unusual.

The weakness is that the centre and spread are estimated from the same data that contain the outliers. One large
value pulls the mean toward itself and inflates the standard deviation by an even larger factor, because deviations
are squared. The outlier's own z-score falls. Several outliers can each hide the others. This effect is called
masking.

The median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) resist this. The median is the middle value, so moving the
largest value further out does not change it. The MAD is the median of the absolute deviations from the median, so it
is also unchanged by a minority of extreme values.

## A z-score has a ceiling

In a sample of size $n$, no z-score can exceed $(n - 1)/\sqrt{n}$, however extreme the value is. This bound is
Samuelson's inequality. It follows from Cauchy–Schwarz.

Shift the data so that $\bar{x} = 0$ and let $d = x_i$ be the value under study. The other $n - 1$ values sum to $-d$.
By the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink>, $n - 1$ numbers with sum $-d$ have a sum of
squares of at least $d^2/(n - 1)$. The total sum of squares therefore satisfies

$$
\sum_j x_j^2 \ge d^2 + \frac{d^2}{n - 1} = \frac{n\,d^2}{n - 1}.
$$

Dividing by $n - 1$ gives $s^2 \ge n\,d^2/(n - 1)^2$, so

$$
\abs{z_i} = \frac{\abs{d}}{s} \le \frac{n - 1}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

Equality holds when all other values are equal. For $n = 10$ the ceiling is $2.85$, so the rule $\abs{z} > 3$ can
never flag anything in a sample of ten. For $n = 12$ the ceiling is $3.18$.

## The consistency constant 1.4826

The MAD of Gaussian data is smaller than the standard deviation. The constant rescales it so that it estimates
$\sigma$ when the data are Gaussian.

Let $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The population MAD is the value $M$ with $\prob(\abs{X - \mu} \le M) = 1/2$.
Standardising with $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$ and the standard normal distribution function $\Phi$,

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{Z} \le M/\sigma} = 2\Phi(M/\sigma) - 1 = \tfrac{1}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Phi(M/\sigma) = \tfrac{3}{4}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \Phi^{-1}(0.75)\,\sigma = 0.6745\,\sigma.
$$

So $\sigma = M/0.6745 = 1.4826\,M$. The same constant appears in the modified z-score of Iglewicz and Hoaglin,
$0.6745\,(x_i - m)/\text{MAD}$, which equals $r_i$ <Cite id="iglewicz1993" />. They recommend flagging values with a
modified z-score above 3.5 in absolute value. For Gaussian data that threshold flags a fraction $0.00047$ of
observations.

## Breakdown point

The breakdown point of an estimator is the smallest fraction of observations that, moved arbitrarily far, can move the
estimate arbitrarily far. The mean and the standard deviation have breakdown point $1/n$: one observation suffices. The
median and the MAD have breakdown point close to $1/2$ <Cite id="hampel1974" />. The robust z-score therefore
keeps a sensible centre and scale until almost half the data are outliers.

The price is efficiency. For Gaussian data the scaled MAD has an asymptotic efficiency of 37% relative to the standard
deviation, so it is noisier from the same sample. Rousseeuw and Croux proposed the scale estimators $S_n$ and $Q_n$,
which keep the 50% breakdown point with efficiencies of 58% and 82% <Cite id="rousseeuw1993" />.

## Worked example

Twelve measurements are

$$
9.8,\ 10.1,\ 10.0,\ 9.9,\ 10.2,\ 10.0,\ 9.7,\ 10.3,\ 10.1,\ 9.9,\ 14.0,\ 15.0.
$$

The last two are outliers. The sample mean is $\bar{x} = 10.75$ and the sample standard deviation is $s = 1.77$. The
z-scores of $14.0$ and $15.0$ are $1.83$ and $2.40$. Neither passes the threshold 3, and neither could pass it by much,
since the ceiling for $n = 12$ is $3.18$.

The median is $m = 10.05$. The absolute deviations from it, sorted, are

$$
0.05,\ 0.05,\ 0.05,\ 0.05,\ 0.15,\ 0.15,\ 0.15,\ 0.25,\ 0.25,\ 0.35,\ 3.95,\ 4.95.
$$

The MAD is the mean of the sixth and seventh values, $0.15$, and the robust scale is $1.4826 \times 0.15 = 0.222$. The
robust z-scores of $14.0$ and $15.0$ are $17.8$ and $22.3$. Every other robust z-score lies between $-1.57$ and $1.12$.
Both outliers are flagged by a wide margin, and no normal value is flagged.

## Hampel identifier and Hampel filter

The Hampel identifier flags $x_i$ when $\abs{x_i - m} > t \cdot 1.4826\,\text{MAD}$, which is the rule
$\abs{r_i} > t$ with $t = 3$ by default. Applied to a time series in a sliding window of $2w + 1$ points centred on
each observation, with $m$ and the MAD computed within the window, it becomes the Hampel filter
<Cite id="pearson2002" />. A flagged value is replaced by the window median. The Hampel filter is a
<NoteLink to="median-filter" /> that changes only the values it judges to be outliers, so it leaves clean stretches of
the signal untouched.

## Tukey's fences

Tukey's boxplot rule flags values below $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ or above $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$, where $Q_1$ and $Q_3$
are the quartiles and $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$ <Cite id="tukey1977" />. The quartiles have breakdown point
$1/4$. For Gaussian data $Q_3 - \mu = 0.6745\,\sigma$ and $\text{IQR} = 1.349\,\sigma$, so the upper fence lies at
$\mu + 2.698\,\sigma$. The rule flags a fraction $0.0070$ of Gaussian observations, more than the $0.0027$ of the
three-sigma rule.

## Assumptions

- **A symmetric bulk.** The median and the MAD describe a symmetric distribution well. For skewed data, a symmetric
  threshold flags too many values on the long side and too few on the short side. Transforming the data first, for
  example by a logarithm for positive right-skewed data, restores symmetry.
- **A Gaussian bulk, for the false-alarm rate.** The constant $1.4826$ and the tail probabilities above assume that
  the normal observations are Gaussian. For heavier-tailed data the same threshold flags more normal values.
- **One variable at a time.** Each variable is screened separately. An observation can be unremarkable on every
  variable and still anomalous jointly; the <NoteLink to="mahalanobis-distance-outliers">Mahalanobis
  distance</NoteLink> handles that case.

## Pitfalls

- **Masking by the mean and standard deviation.** The classical z-score is unreliable for outlier detection even with
  one outlier in a small sample, as the ceiling $(n - 1)/\sqrt{n}$ shows. Leys and colleagues recommend the MAD in its
  place <Cite id="leys2013" />.
- **A zero MAD.** When more than half the values are identical, the MAD is zero and every other value has an infinite
  robust z-score. Discrete or heavily rounded data need another scale, such as $Q_n$ or a small floor on the MAD.
- **Many tests at once.** Screening $n$ values at a per-value false-alarm rate $\alpha$ gives about $n\alpha$ false
  alarms. For a sample-wide error rate, test the most extreme value with the <NoteLink to="grubbs-test" /> or divide
  $\alpha$ by $n$.
