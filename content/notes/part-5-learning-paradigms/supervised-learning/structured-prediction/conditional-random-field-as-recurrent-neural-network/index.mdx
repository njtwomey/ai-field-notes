<Definition>
A fully connected (dense) CRF over the $N$ pixels of an image assigns label $x_i \in \Lcal = \set{1, \dots, L}$ to pixel
$i$ with energy

$$
E(\xvec) = \sum_i \psi_u(x_i) + \sum_{i < j} \psi_p(x_i, x_j), \qquad
\psi_p(l, l') = \mu(l, l') \sum_{m=1}^{M} w^{(m)} k^{(m)}(\fvec_i, \fvec_j),
$$

where $\psi_u$ is a unary cost from a network, $\mu$ is a label-compatibility function, $k^{(m)}$ are
<Gloss name="rbf-kernel">Gaussian kernels</Gloss> on pixel features $\fvec_i$ (position, and position with colour) and
$w^{(m)}$ their weights. CRF-RNN computes approximate marginals by mean-field inference and writes each mean-field
iteration as a stack of <Gloss name="cnn" /> layers. $T$ iterations form a recurrent network whose parameters, $w^{(m)}$
and $\mu$, are learnt jointly with the CNN that supplies the unaries <Cite id="zheng2015" />.
</Definition>

## The problem

A <NoteLink to="semantic-segmentation">fully convolutional network</NoteLink> labels every pixel from its receptive
field and does not enforce agreement between neighbouring labels, so its segmentations have coarse, blobby boundaries
<Cite id="long2015" />. A dense CRF encourages pixels that are close and similar in colour to take compatible labels,
which snaps labels to image edges <Cite id="krahenbuhl2011" />. Used as a separate post-processing step, the CRF cannot
influence what the network learns. CRF-RNN makes CRF inference a differentiable part of the network.

## Mean-field inference for the dense CRF

The CRF defines $P(\xvec) \propto \exp\{-E(\xvec)\}$. Exact marginals are intractable because every pixel is connected
to every other. Mean field approximates $P$ by a product of independent per-pixel distributions,
$Q(\xvec) = \prod_i Q_i(x_i)$, chosen to minimise $\KL(Q \,\|\, P)$. The coordinate update for one factor holds the
others fixed and sets $\log Q_i(l)$ to the expected log-probability under them (see
<NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>):

$$
\log Q_i(l) = \expect_{Q_{-i}}\big[-E(\xvec) \mid x_i = l\big] + \text{const}.
$$

Only the terms of $E$ that involve $x_i$ depend on $l$. The unary term contributes $-\psi_u(l)$. Each pairwise term with
pixel $j$ contributes $-\sum_{l'} Q_j(l')\, \psi_p(l, l')$. Write $U_i(l) = -\psi_u(l)$ for the network's score.
Substituting the Gaussian form of $\psi_p$ and exchanging the sums,

$$
Q_i(l) = \frac{1}{Z_i} \exp\Big\{ U_i(l) - \sum_{l' \in \Lcal} \mu(l, l') \sum_{m=1}^{M} w^{(m)}
\sum_{j \neq i} k^{(m)}(\fvec_i, \fvec_j)\, Q_j(l') \Big\},
$$

with $Z_i$ normalising over labels. The update needs, for each label and each kernel, the sum
$\sum_{j \neq i} k^{(m)}(\fvec_i, \fvec_j) Q_j(l')$. That is a Gaussian filter applied to the map $Q(l')$, computed in
time linear in $N$ with the permutohedral lattice <Cite id="adams2010" /> <Cite id="krahenbuhl2011" />.

## One iteration as CNN layers

Reading the update from the inside out gives five operations, each an ordinary network layer
<Cite id="zheng2015" />:

| Step | Operation | As a layer | Parameters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Message passing | $\tilde Q^{(m)}_i(l) = \sum_{j \neq i} k^{(m)}(\fvec_i, \fvec_j) Q_j(l)$ | $M$ Gaussian filters (one spatial, one bilateral) | kernel widths, fixed |
| Weighting | $\check Q_i(l) = \sum_m w^{(m)} \tilde Q^{(m)}_i(l)$ | $1 \times 1$ convolution, $M$ channels in | $w^{(m)}$, one set per label |
| Compatibility | $\hat Q_i(l) = \sum_{l'} \mu(l, l') \check Q_i(l')$ | $1 \times 1$ convolution, $L$ channels in and out | $\mu$, learnt |
| Adding unaries | $\breve Q_i(l) = U_i(l) - \hat Q_i(l)$ | element-wise subtraction | none |
| Normalising | $Q_i(l) = \operatorname{softmax}_l\, \breve Q_i(l)$ | softmax over labels | none |

Every step is differentiable in its inputs and parameters. The Gaussian filter's
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is the same filter applied to the incoming gradient, because the kernel
matrix is symmetric. The compatibility $\mu$ is initialised to the Potts model, $\mu(l, l') = [l \neq l']$, and then
learnt, so it can penalise label pairs that rarely touch more than pairs that often do; the learnt matrix is not
required to be symmetric <Cite id="zheng2015" />.

## Unrolling into a recurrent network

Let $f_{\thetavec}(U, Q, I)$ be one iteration, with $\thetavec$ the weights $w^{(m)}$ and $\mu$, and $I$ the image. The
iterations form a recurrent network with hidden state $Q$:

$$
H_1(t) = \begin{cases} \operatorname{softmax}(U), & t = 0,\\ H_2(t - 1), & 0 < t \le T, \end{cases}
\qquad H_2(t) = f_{\thetavec}(U, H_1(t), I),
$$

with the output $H_2(T)$ after $T$ iterations. The same parameters are used at every step, as in any
<NoteLink to="recurrent-neural-network">recurrent network</NoteLink>, and their gradients are sums over steps computed
by <NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">backpropagation through time</NoteLink>. The unaries $U$ enter every
step, so the gradient reaches the CNN both directly and through each iteration. Mean field on the dense CRF converges in
fewer than about 10 iterations, so the unrolled network is shallow in time and does not suffer badly from vanishing or
exploding gradients. Zheng et al. trained with $T = 5$ and tested with $T = 10$ <Cite id="zheng2015" />.

## Worked example

Take three pixels on a line at positions $f = 0, 1, 2$, two labels, one Gaussian kernel $k(f_i, f_j) = \exp\{-(f_i -
f_j)^2/2\}$ with $w = 1$, the Potts compatibility, and unary scores $U_1 = (2, 0)$, $U_2 = (0.2, 0)$, $U_3 = (0, 1.5)$.
Pixel 1 strongly prefers label 1, pixel 3 prefers label 2, and pixel 2 is almost undecided.

- The kernel weights are $k_{12} = k_{23} = 0.607$ and $k_{13} = 0.135$.
- Initialisation, $Q = \operatorname{softmax}(U)$: $Q_1 = (0.881, 0.119)$, $Q_2 = (0.550, 0.450)$,
  $Q_3 = (0.182, 0.818)$.
- Messages to pixel 2: $\tilde Q_2 = k_{21} Q_1 + k_{23} Q_3 = (0.645, 0.568)$. With the Potts model the penalty for
  label $l$ is the message for the other label, $\hat Q_2 = (0.568, 0.645)$.
- Update: $Q_2 \propto \exp\{(0.2 - 0.568,\ 0 - 0.645)\}$, so $Q_2 = (0.569, 0.431)$.
- After three iterations, $Q_1 = (0.882, 0.118)$, $Q_2 = (0.578, 0.422)$, $Q_3 = (0.213, 0.787)$.

Pixel 2 moves towards label 1 because its label-1 neighbour is more confident than its label-2 neighbour. Pixel 3 is
pulled slightly towards label 1 for the same reason. In CRF-RNN the weight $w$ and the compatibility would be learnt,
with the widths fixed, so that such smoothing improves the segmentation loss.

## Results

On the reduced PASCAL VOC 2012 validation set, mean <Gloss name="iou">intersection over union</Gloss> rose from 61.3%
for FCN-8s alone, to 63.7% with a dense CRF applied afterwards, to 69.6% with CRF-RNN trained end to end (68.3%, 69.5%
and 72.9% with additional <Gloss name="coco" /> training data). On the VOC 2012 test set CRF-RNN reached 72.0% without
COCO and 74.7% with it <Cite id="zheng2015" />.

## Relation to other CRF models

The linear-chain <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">CRF</NoteLink> is a tree, so forward–backward gives exact
marginals and a neural CRF can backpropagate through exact inference (see
<NoteLink to="conditional-random-field-variants">CRF variants</NoteLink>). The dense CRF is a loopy graph over millions
of pixel pairs, so inference must be approximate; CRF-RNN backpropagates through a fixed number of approximate inference
steps instead. The same idea, training a model through an unrolled inference algorithm, applies to
<NoteLink to="loopy-belief-propagation">loopy belief propagation</NoteLink> and other iterative procedures.

## Pitfalls

- **Truncation is part of the model.** The network is trained for a fixed $T$. The parameters learnt make the $T$-step
  approximation good, not the exact CRF marginals.
- **Mean-field overconfidence.** Minimising $\KL(Q \,\|\, P)$ under-represents uncertainty, so the output marginals are
  sharper than the true CRF marginals.
- **Memory.** Backpropagation through time stores $Q$ for every iteration, $O(TNL)$ memory for $N$ pixels and $L$
  labels.
