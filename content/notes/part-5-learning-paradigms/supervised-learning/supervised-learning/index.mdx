<Definition>
**Supervised learning** learns a function $f$ that maps an input $\xvec$ to an output $y$ from training pairs
$(\xvec_1, y_1), \dots, (\xvec_n, y_n)$ drawn from an unknown distribution. The goal is low expected loss on new pairs
from the same distribution, not on the training pairs. The output may be a number (regression), a class
(classification), an ordered category, or a structured object such as a label sequence <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Most deployed machine learning is supervised: predicting prices, demand and risk, classifying images, documents and
transactions, ranking search results, and tagging every word of a sentence. Whenever examples of the right answer can
be collected, supervised learning turns them into a predictor. The topics in this chapter are the model families that
do so, from linear models fitted in closed form to ensembles of thousands of trees.

## Main ideas

**Minimise a loss on data, control complexity.** Every method chooses $f$ from a model class to make a training loss
small, and limits the class or penalises complexity so that the fit generalises. Regularisation, priors, smoothing
parameters, tree depth and the number of neighbours are all forms of this control, and
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> usually sets it.

**Probabilistic and non-probabilistic models.** Some models give a full predictive distribution: generalised linear
models, generative classifiers and Gaussian processes. Others give a score or a decision, such as the support vector
machine. A probability supports decisions under any costs and can be checked for calibration.

**Parametric, nonparametric and kernel models.** A <NoteLink to="linear-models">linear model</NoteLink> has a fixed
number of parameters. <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours" />, <NoteLink to="gaussian-processes" /> and
<NoteLink to="kernel-methods-and-support-vector-machines">kernel machines</NoteLink> let complexity grow with the data,
through the training points themselves or a kernel between them.
<NoteLink to="trees-and-ensembles">Tree ensembles</NoteLink> grow complexity adaptively by splitting where the data
demand it.

**Generative and discriminative models.** <NoteLink to="generative-classifiers" /> model how inputs arise within each
class and classify by Bayes' theorem. Discriminative models such as <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic
regression</NoteLink> model the label given the input directly. The first need fewer examples; the second reach lower
error with many.

**Outputs with structure.** When the output is a sequence or a ranking of categories, independent predictions waste
information. <NoteLink to="structured-prediction" /> predicts many dependent outputs jointly, and
<NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink> respects the order of categories whose spacing is
unknown.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="regression">regression</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="classification">classification</NoteLink>. They introduce least squares, logistic regression and the
  penalties that the later topics reuse.
- <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">Generalised linear models</NoteLink> unify linear and logistic regression
  under exponential-family responses, and <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive
  models</NoteLink> replace each linear term with a smooth function.
- <NoteLink to="generative-classifiers" /> and <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink> need only
  probability and distances, and can be read early.
- <NoteLink to="kernel-methods-and-support-vector-machines" /> and <NoteLink to="gaussian-processes" /> share their
  kernels and are best read together.
- <NoteLink to="trees-and-ensembles" /> stands apart and is the practical default for tabular data.
- <NoteLink to="structured-prediction" /> and <NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink> build on
  logistic regression and the <Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss>.
