<Definition>
A radial kernel has the form $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \kappa(\lambda\norm{\xvec - \xvec'})$ for a fixed profile $\kappa$
and a scale $\lambda > 0$, such as the Gaussian <Gloss name="rbf-kernel" />
$k(\xvec, \xvec') = \exp(-\lambda^2 \norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2)$. The **median heuristic** draws a few hundred to a few
thousand random pairs of points from the data, computes their distances, and sets $\lambda$ to the inverse of the
median distance. Smola's version also takes the inverses of the 0.1 and 0.9 quantiles and chooses among the three by
<NoteLink to="cross-validation" /> <Cite id="smola2010kernelwidth" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The scale $\lambda$ decides what a kernel method can learn. If $\lambda$ is large, $\kappa$ is near zero for almost
every pair, the kernel matrix is close to the identity, and each point is similar only to itself: the method memorises
the training set. If $\lambda$ is small, $\kappa$ is near $\kappa(0)$ for every pair, the kernel matrix is close to a
constant, and the method sees little beyond the mean. Useful values lie in between, but where depends on the units of
the data, which are arbitrary. A grid search over many orders of magnitude costs one fit per grid point.

## Mechanism

The argument $\lambda\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}$ should be of order 1 for typical pairs, so that the kernel separates near
pairs from far ones. Setting $\lambda = 1/d_{0.5}$, with $d_{0.5}$ the median pairwise distance, achieves this
directly. For the Gaussian kernel a pair at the median distance then has kernel value $e^{-1} \approx 0.37$; half of
all pairs have larger values and half smaller.

Measuring distances in the data, rather than assuming them, is what makes the rule robust. Schölkopf's earlier
heuristic scaled the kernel by the input dimension $d$, since $d$ independent standardised coordinates give a mean
squared distance of $2d$. Correlated coordinates break that assumption: a hundred coordinates that move together have
pairwise distances like those of a few independent ones. The quantiles of observed distances account for this
automatically <Cite id="smola2010kernelwidth" />.

The three candidates span the useful range. The inverse of the 0.1 quantile gives a local kernel, under which most
pairs are nearly unrelated. The inverse of the 0.9 quantile gives a smooth kernel, under which most pairs are
similar. One of the three is usually close enough to the cross-validated optimum that a finer search changes little.

### Conventions

Kernels are written with different scale parameters, and the heuristic must be translated. For
$\exp(-\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2 / 2\sigma^2)$, setting $\lambda = 1/d_{0.5}$ corresponds to $\sigma = d_{0.5}/\sqrt{2}$.
Many statistical papers, and the <NoteLink to="kernel-two-sample-test" /> notes on this site, set $\sigma = d_{0.5}$
directly; the factor $\sqrt 2$ rarely matters next to the spread of the three candidates. Garreau, Jitkrittum and
Kanagawa study the median heuristic's large-sample behaviour <Cite id="garreau2017" />.

## Cost

Sampling $m$ pairs and computing their distances costs $O(md)$ in $d$ dimensions, and the quantiles cost
$O(m \log m)$; $m = 1000$ is plenty. Computing all $n^2$ distances is unnecessary: the quantiles of a random sample of
pairs are accurate to a few percent. Cross-validation then needs three fits in place of a full grid.

## Trade-offs

- **Labels are ignored.** The heuristic looks only at the inputs. When the target varies on a much finer or coarser
  scale than the inputs are spread, cross-validation over a wider range is needed.
- **One scale for every coordinate.** A coordinate in large units dominates every distance. Standardise features
  first, or learn one scale per coordinate, as <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination" /> does.
- **High dimensions.** Distances between random points concentrate as the dimension grows, so the 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9
  quantiles approach each other and the three candidates become nearly the same.
- **Not for every task.** In the <NoteLink to="kernel-two-sample-test" />, the median heuristic is a safe default but
  can miss a difference at a scale far from the typical distance.

## Worked example

The figure's default: the two-spirals data set with 200 points and noise 0.1, an RBF support vector machine with
$C = 1$, and seed 1. The engine writes the Gaussian kernel as $\exp(-\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2 / 2\ell^2)$, so
$\lambda = 1/d$ becomes the lengthscale $\ell = d/\sqrt{2}$.

1. **Sample distances.** Over 1000 random pairs of distinct points, the 0.1 quantile is $0.297$, the median $0.739$
   and the 0.9 quantile $1.300$.
2. **Convert to widths.** The candidates are $\ell = 0.210$, $0.523$ and $0.920$.
3. **Cross-validate each.** Five-fold accuracy is $0.865 \pm 0.055$ at $\ell = 0.210$, $0.585 \pm 0.060$ at
   $0.523$ and $0.530 \pm 0.091$ at $0.920$.
4. **Compare with the grid.** The best of 13 widths from $0.03$ to $10$ scores $0.875$, within one standard error of
   the 0.1-quantile candidate.
5. **Read the result.** The spirals' arms are closer together than a typical pair of points, so the median width
   blurs neighbouring arms into one and does little better than chance. The 0.1-quantile candidate resolves them. On
   the two-moons data, whose classes differ at the scale of a typical distance, the same procedure gives $1.000$,
   $0.990$ and $0.910$, and the median would have done.
