<Definition>
**Nearest-neighbour methods** keep the training data and answer each query from the stored points closest to it under
a distance, usually Euclidean or cosine. Prediction is a vote or an average over those neighbours. The topic has two
halves: the statistical question of how well neighbours predict, and the algorithmic question of how to find them fast
among millions or billions of points.
</Definition>

## What it is for

<NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours" /> is a classifier and a regressor with no training step and one main parameter, the
number of neighbours $k$. It is a standard baseline and a building block of density estimates, anomaly scores and
imputation. Fast neighbour search has become infrastructure in its own right: embedding-based retrieval, recommendation,
deduplication and <NoteLink to="retrieval-augmented-generation">retrieval-augmented generation</NoteLink> all reduce to
finding the stored vectors nearest a query vector.

## Main ideas

**Local averaging is consistent.** As the number of training points grows, the error of the 1-nearest-neighbour rule
is at most twice the Bayes error <Cite id="cover1967" />. Letting $k$ grow, but slower than the data, makes the rule
converge to the Bayes rule. Small $k$ gives low bias and high variance; large $k$ the reverse.

**Distance loses meaning in high dimensions.** In $d$ dimensions, a neighbourhood that holds a fixed fraction of
uniform data must span almost the full range of each coordinate, and the distances from a query to all points
concentrate around one value. <NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours" /> quantifies both effects. In
practice, neighbours work in high dimensions only when the data lie near a low-dimensional structure or the distance
is learnt.

**Exact search prunes by bounds.** A linear scan costs $O(nd)$ per query. <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search" />
introduces indexes that skip regions which cannot hold a closer point. <NoteLink to="space-partitioning-trees" />
develops the trees that do this: k-d trees split coordinates, ball trees and vantage-point trees need only a metric.
All of them are fast in low dimensions and degrade towards a scan as $d$ grows.

**Approximate search trades recall for speed.** <NoteLink to="approximate-nearest-neighbour-search" /> returns most of
the true neighbours in far less time. Locality-sensitive hashing, product quantisation, inverted files and navigable
graphs such as HNSW are its main families. They are compared by the trade-off curve of recall against queries per
second.
