<Definition>
A **ball tree** over points in a metric space with distance $\delta$ is a binary tree whose every node holds a centre
$\cvec$ and a radius $\rho = \max_{\xvec \in \text{node}} \delta(\cvec, \xvec)$, so the ball $B(\cvec, \rho)$ contains
all of the node's points <Cite id="omohundro1989" />. The children split the node's points; their balls may overlap.
Leaves hold at most $m$ points.
</Definition>

## Problem

Find the $k$ points nearest a query when the data do not suit axis-aligned boxes: clouds near a rotated or curved
low-dimensional set, or objects with a distance but no coordinates (strings under edit distance, sets under Jaccard
distance). A ball needs only a centre, a radius and the triangle inequality <Cite id="uhlmann1991" />.

## Mechanism

### Mental model

- **Build:** halve the points as a <NoteLink to="k-d-tree">$k$-d tree</NoteLink> would, and wrap each half in the
  smallest ball around its centroid that covers it.
- **Search:** a ball whose nearest possible point, $\rho$ inside its surface, is farther than the best found so far is
  skipped without looking inside.

### Building

```text
build(points):
    c ← centroid of points;  ρ ← max distance from c to a point   # the node's ball
    if |points| ≤ m: return leaf(points, c, ρ)
    j ← the coordinate with the largest spread;  t ← median of x_j
    return node(c, ρ,
                build(points with x_j < t),
                build(points with x_j ≥ t))
```

Without coordinates, the split instead picks a point $\avec$ far from a random point, a point $\bvec$ far from $\avec$,
and sends each point to the nearer of the two. Omohundro compared five construction methods, including bottom-up
merging, which builds tighter balls at more cost <Cite id="omohundro1989" />.

### Searching

```text
r ← ∞                                          # k-th best distance so far
search(node):
    if δ(q, node.c) − node.ρ > r:  PRUNE         # the whole ball is farther than r
    elif node is a leaf:           SCAN          # distances to its points; keep the k best; update r
    else:                          DESCEND
        visit the child with the smaller bound first, then the other
```

**Pruning, and why it is safe.** For any point $\xvec$ in the ball, the triangle inequality gives
$\delta(\qvec, \cvec) \le \delta(\qvec, \xvec) + \delta(\xvec, \cvec)$ and $\delta(\xvec, \cvec) \le \rho$, so

$$
\delta(\qvec, \xvec) \ge \delta(\qvec, \cvec) - \rho .
$$

The bound costs one distance, to the centre. When it exceeds $r$, every point in the ball is farther than the $k$
points held, and the subtree is skipped. When $\qvec$ is inside the ball the bound is negative and the node is always
opened.

**Backtracking.** As in every space-partitioning tree, `search` recurses into the child with the smaller bound and
returns to its parent when that subtree is finished; the parent then tests its other child against the $r$ that the
first subtree left behind.

## Worked example

The same eight points as in the $k$-d tree note, leaf size 2, query $\qvec = (6.6, 4.6)$, $k = 1$. The splits are the
$k$-d tree's; each node now carries a ball.

| step | node | centre, radius | bound | $r$ before | decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | root | $(5, 3.63)$, 4.78 | 0 | $\infty$ | descend |
| 2 | right half | $(7.5, 3.75)$, 2.80 | 0 | $\infty$ | descend |
| 3 | leaf $\set{(7,6), (9,5)}$ | $(8, 5.5)$, 1.12 | 0.55 | $\infty$ | scan: $r = 1.46$ |
| 4 | leaf $\set{(6,3), (8,1)}$ | $(7, 2)$, 1.41 | 1.22 | 1.46 | scan: no improvement |
| 5 | left half | $(2.5, 3.5)$, 3.81 | 0.44 | 1.46 | descend |
| 6 | leaf $\set{(2,4), (4,7)}$ | $(3, 5.5)$, 1.80 | 1.91 | 1.46 | prune |
| 7 | leaf $\set{(1,1), (3,2)}$ | $(2, 1.5)$, 1.12 | 4.43 | 1.46 | prune |

The answer is again $(7,6)$, but with 4 distances instead of the $k$-d tree's 2. A ball around a node's points is
looser than their bounding box (step 4: bound 1.22 against the box's 1.60), and the left half's big ball reaches
almost to the query (step 5). Balls win on curved or rotated data and on general metrics; boxes win on data spread
along the axes.

## Cost

| | build | memory | query |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ball tree | $O(n \log n)$ top-down | $O(n)$ | sublinear when the intrinsic dimension is low |

## Trade-offs

- **Overlap.** Sibling balls may overlap, so a query near both opens both. Boxes of a $k$-d tree never overlap.
- **Bound cost.** Each bound is one full distance, $O(d)$, against $O(1)$ per split in a $k$-d tree.
- **Metric only.** The bound holds for any metric. It fails for scores that break the triangle inequality, such as
  cosine similarity used directly; the angle $\arccos$ of the cosine is a metric and works.
- **Dimension.** Like every space-partitioning tree, a ball tree degrades towards a scan as the intrinsic dimension
  grows <Cite id="samet2006" />.
