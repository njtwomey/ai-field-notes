<Definition>
A **$k$-d tree** over points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n \in \reals^d$ is a binary tree whose internal nodes each hold a
coordinate $j$ and a threshold $t$: points with $x_j < t$ go to the left child, the rest to the right
<Cite id="bentley1975" />. Each node therefore owns an axis-aligned box. Leaves hold at most $m$ points.
</Definition>

## Problem

Find the $k$ points nearest a query $\qvec$ without computing all $n$ distances. A $k$-d tree spends $O(n \log n)$ once
on an index, then answers queries by ruling out whole boxes of points with one bound each.

## Mechanism

### Mental model

- **Build:** cut the cloud in half with a line through the median of its widest coordinate, then cut each half the same
  way, until each piece holds at most $m$ points.
- **Search:** go straight to the piece containing $\qvec$ and scan it. Then walk back up, and look into a neighbouring
  piece only if it could hold something closer than the best found so far.

### Building

```text
build(points):
    if |points| ≤ m: return leaf(points)
    j ← the coordinate with the largest spread (max − min) among points
    t ← the median of x_j over points
    box ← the bounding box of points            # used by the search's bound
    return node(j, t, box,
                build(points with x_j < t),
                build(points with x_j ≥ t))
```

The median halves the points at every level, so the depth is $\lceil \log_2(n/m) \rceil$ and the build costs
$O(n \log n)$. Splitting the widest coordinate keeps boxes from becoming long and thin.

### Searching

```text
r ← ∞                                     # k-th best distance so far
search(node):
    if gap(q, node.box) > r:  PRUNE        # the whole box is farther than r
    elif node is a leaf:      SCAN         # distances to its points; keep the k best; update r
    else:                     DESCEND
        visit the child whose box is nearer to q first, then the other
```

Here $\text{gap}(\qvec, B)$ is the distance from $\qvec$ to the box $B = [\bvec^{\text{lo}}, \bvec^{\text{hi}}]$, zero
when $\qvec$ is inside:

$$
\text{gap}(\qvec, B)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \max\bigl(0,\; b^{\text{lo}}_i - q_i,\; q_i - b^{\text{hi}}_i\bigr)^2 .
$$

**Backtracking.** The nearer child's box usually contains $\qvec$, so the first chain of descents ends at the leaf
around the query, and its scan gives a small $r$. Each recursive call then returns to its parent, which visits its
second child. That visit starts with the prune test against the current $r$, so most second children are skipped with
one bound and no distances.

**Pruning, and why it is safe.** For any point $\xvec$ in box $B$, each coordinate difference is at least the gap in
that coordinate, $\abs{q_i - x_i} \ge \max(0, b^{\text{lo}}_i - q_i, q_i - b^{\text{hi}}_i)$, because $x_i$ lies in
$[b^{\text{lo}}_i, b^{\text{hi}}_i]$. Squaring and summing gives $\norm{\qvec - \xvec}_2 \ge \text{gap}(\qvec, B)$.
So when the gap exceeds $r$, every point in the box is farther than the $k$ points already held, and skipping the box
loses nothing. Friedman, Bentley and Finkel showed that the expected number of nodes examined is $O(\log n)$ for fixed
$d$ <Cite id="friedman1977" />.

## Worked example

Eight points, leaf size $m = 2$, query $\qvec = (6.6, 4.6)$, $k = 1$:

$$
(1,1),\ (2,4),\ (3,2),\ (4,7),\ (6,3),\ (7,6),\ (8,1),\ (9,5).
$$

**Build.** The root splits $x_1$ at $6$ ($x_1$ spans 8 and $x_2$ spans 6, so $x_1$ is wider). The left half
$\set{(1,1), (2,4), (3,2), (4,7)}$ splits $x_2$ at $4$; the right half $\set{(6,3), (7,6), (8,1), (9,5)}$ splits $x_2$
at $5$. The four leaves hold two points each.

**Search,** as traced by the implementation:

| step | node | box | gap | $r$ before | decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | root | $[1,9] \times [1,7]$ | 0 | $\infty$ | descend; right child nearer ($q_1 = 6.6 \ge 6$) |
| 2 | right half | $[6,9] \times [1,6]$ | 0 | $\infty$ | descend; upper child nearer (gap 0.57 against 1.60) |
| 3 | leaf $\set{(7,6), (9,5)}$ | $[7,9] \times [5,6]$ | 0.57 | $\infty$ | scan: distances 1.46 and 2.43, $r = 1.46$ |
| 4 | leaf $\set{(6,3), (8,1)}$ | $[6,8] \times [1,3]$ | 1.60 | 1.46 | prune: $1.60 > 1.46$ |
| 5 | left half | $[1,4] \times [1,7]$ | 2.60 | 1.46 | prune: $2.60 > 1.46$ |

The answer is $(7,6)$ at distance 1.46, found with 2 distance computations instead of 8. Steps 4 and 5 are the
backtracking: after the first leaf, control returns to the right half and then to the root, and each pending child is
pruned by its gap alone.

## Cost

| | build | memory | query, low $d$ | query, high $d$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $k$-d tree | $O(n \log n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(\log n)$ expected | approaches $O(nd)$ |
| scan | none | $O(n)$ | $O(nd)$ | $O(nd)$ |

## Trade-offs

- **Dimension.** A ball of radius $r$ crosses about $2^d$ boxes of comparable size, so the tree beats a scan only when
  $n \gg 2^d$; in practice up to about 10 to 20 dimensions <Cite id="weber1998" />.
- **Shape.** Boxes fit data spread along the axes. Clouds near a rotated or curved low-dimensional set suit the balls
  of a <NoteLink to="ball-tree">ball tree</NoteLink>.
- **Metric.** The gap bound needs coordinates and an $\ell_p$ distance, not a general metric.
- **Updates.** Insertions go to the leaf whose box holds the point; many of them unbalance the tree until it is rebuilt.
