<Definition>
A **generative classifier** fits a model of the joint distribution $p(\xvec, y) = p(y)\,p(\xvec \mid y)$, usually by
maximising the joint likelihood $\sum_i \log p(\xvec_i, y_i)$, and classifies with the posterior
$p(y \mid \xvec) \propto p(y)\,p(\xvec \mid y)$. A **discriminative classifier** fits $p(y \mid \xvec)$, or a decision
function, directly, usually by maximising the conditional likelihood $\sum_i \log p(y_i \mid \xvec_i)$. Two models
form a **generative–discriminative pair** when the posteriors the generative model can express are exactly the
discriminative model's hypothesis class <Cite id="ng2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A generative model explains how the features arise in each class, which is more than classification needs. It spends
parameters on the distribution of $\xvec$, and if that distribution is modelled wrongly the resulting boundary is
wrong too. A discriminative model spends its parameters on the boundary alone. The generative model's extra
assumptions are also extra information: when they are right, or nearly right, they let the model learn from less data.

## The naive Bayes–logistic regression pair

Take $d$ binary features and <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink> with
$p(x_j = 1 \mid y = k) = \theta_{kj}$. Each feature's log-likelihood ratio is

$$
\log\frac{p(x_j \mid y = 1)}{p(x_j \mid y = 0)} = x_j\log\frac{\theta_{1j}}{\theta_{0j}} + (1 - x_j)\log\frac{1 - \theta_{1j}}{1 - \theta_{0j}},
$$

which is affine in $x_j$. The log-odds is a sum of these plus $\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)$, so it has the form
$\wvec^\top\xvec + b$, and naive Bayes's posterior is $\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)$ for particular $\wvec$ and $b$. That
is the model of <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>. Conversely, any $\wvec$ and $b$ can
be produced by some choice of the $\theta_{kj}$ and $\pi_k$. The two share a hypothesis class, the linear classifiers,
and differ only in how they choose a member of it:

- naive Bayes sets each $\theta_{kj}$ to a class frequency, independently of the other features;
- logistic regression chooses $\wvec$ and $b$ jointly to maximise the conditional likelihood of the labels.

The same holds for Gaussian features: <NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">linear discriminant
analysis</NoteLink> (LDA) is the generative partner of logistic regression on continuous inputs. In sequence labelling,
the <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink> and the linear-chain
<NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random field</NoteLink> form a pair.

## Two results of Ng and Jordan

Ng and Jordan compared the two members of such a pair by their error $\varepsilon(h)$ as the training size $n$
grows <Cite id="ng2002" />.

**Asymptotic error.** As $n \to \infty$, naive Bayes's parameters converge to the true class frequencies and its
classifier converges to a fixed linear rule $h_{\text{Gen}, \infty}$. Logistic regression converges to the linear rule
$h_{\text{Dis}, \infty}$ that maximises the expected conditional log-likelihood, which, for this class, is the best
linear classifier under the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink>. When the naive Bayes
assumption holds, both limits are the Bayes-optimal classifier. When it fails, naive Bayes's limit is some other linear
rule, generally a worse one:

$$
\varepsilon(h_{\text{Dis}, \infty}) \le \varepsilon(h_{\text{Gen}, \infty}).
$$

Strictly, logistic regression minimises log loss, not error rate, so this ordering is the typical case rather than
a theorem for every distribution. Ng and Jordan state it for the classifier that minimises training error within the
linear class <Cite id="ng2002" />.

**Rate of convergence.** The generative model approaches its asymptote much faster. Naive Bayes estimates each of its
$2d$ parameters from its own feature's counts. By <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's
inequality</NoteLink>, one frequency is within $\epsilon$ of its limit with probability at least
$1 - 2e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$ from $n$ samples. For all $2d$ to be accurate at once, a union bound needs
$2 \cdot 2d\, e^{-2n\epsilon^2} \le \delta$, that is

$$
n \ge \frac{1}{2\epsilon^2}\log\frac{4d}{\delta},
$$

a sample size that grows like $O(\log d)$. Once every parameter is close to its limit, naive Bayes's error is close to
$\varepsilon(h_{\text{Gen}, \infty})$ (under a mild condition that few examples sit near the limiting boundary). A
discriminative learner over linear classifiers in $d$ dimensions needs a number of examples that grows like $O(d)$ to
come close to $\varepsilon(h_{\text{Dis}, \infty})$, because the class has
<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> $d + 1$ and the parameters are estimated jointly
<Cite id="ng2002" />.

Together: the generative model starts better and plateaus higher; the discriminative model starts worse and
eventually wins. The learning curves cross, and on many of Ng and Jordan's data sets naive Bayes was ahead at small
$n$ and logistic regression at large $n$.

## Worked example: crossing learning curves

Take 40 binary features, conditionally independent given a balanced class, with $p(x_j = 1 \mid y = 1)$ evenly spaced
from $0.35$ to $0.65$ and $p(x_j = 1 \mid y = 0) = 1 - p(x_j = 1 \mid y = 1)$. Append five exact copies of the first
feature, so the naive Bayes assumption fails for 6 of the 45 features. Fit Bernoulli naive Bayes (smoothing
$\alpha = 1$) and $L_2$-penalised logistic regression (scikit-learn defaults) on $n$ examples. The table averages the
test error over 100 training sets, each tested on 50,000 fresh examples.

| $n$                 | 10    | 20    | 50    | 200   | 1000  | 5000  |
| ------------------- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| naive Bayes         | 0.315 | 0.277 | 0.250 | 0.246 | 0.255 | 0.256 |
| logistic regression | 0.352 | 0.284 | 0.214 | 0.163 | 0.136 | 0.129 |

Naive Bayes reaches its plateau near $0.25$ by $n = 50$. It never improves further, because it counts the duplicated
feature six times however much data it sees. Logistic regression starts worse and keeps improving, since it can learn
to split the weight of the duplicated feature among its copies. The curves cross between $n = 20$ and $n = 50$.

## Other differences

- **Missing features and unlabelled data.** A generative model can marginalise a missing feature out of $p(\xvec, y)$
  and can use unlabelled $\xvec$ through $p(\xvec) = \sum_k p(\xvec, y = k)$, for example with
  <Gloss name="em-algorithm">expectation maximisation</Gloss>. A discriminative model has no model of $\xvec$ to do
  either <Cite id="bishop2006" />.
- **Feature engineering.** A discriminative model can use arbitrary, overlapping features of $\xvec$ without
  modelling their dependence. This freedom is the main reason for conditional random fields over hidden Markov models.
- **Outliers and density.** A generative model assigns a density to every $\xvec$, so it can flag inputs unlike any
  training data; a discriminative model is confident far from the data whenever the boundary says so.
- **Class priors.** A generative model separates $p(y)$ from $p(\xvec \mid y)$, so a shift in class frequencies is
  corrected by changing $\pi_k$ alone.

## Pitfalls

- **"Discriminative is always better" is false** for small data or when the generative model is nearly correct.
- **The comparison concerns a pair.** A flexible generative model, such as a
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture</NoteLink> per class, is not compared with logistic regression
  by these results; its hypothesis class differs.
- **Regularisation shifts the crossing.** A strongly penalised logistic regression behaves more like a generative
  model with a strong prior, and moves the crossing point.
