<Definition>
For $K$ classes, **one-versus-one** trains a binary classifier $f_{ij}$ for every pair $i < j$, using only the training
examples of classes $i$ and $j$. There are $K(K-1)/2$ classifiers. Classifier $f_{ij}$ estimates
$r_{ij}(\xvec) \approx \pr(y = i \mid y \in \set{i, j}, \xvec)$, and $r_{ji} = 1 - r_{ij}$. A prediction combines the
pairwise answers by a majority vote, by a decision DAG that evaluates $K - 1$ of them, or by **pairwise coupling**,
which finds class probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$ consistent with all the $r_{ij}$ <Cite id="knerr1990pairwise" />
<Cite id="hastie1998pairwise" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A binary learner must be turned into a $K$-class classifier. <NoteLink to="one-versus-rest">One-versus-rest</NoteLink>
asks $K$ questions of the form "class $k$ or any other class?". Each question uses all $n$ examples, is imbalanced
about $1:(K-1)$ when classes are equally frequent, and asks a linear learner to separate one class from a union of
classes that may surround it. Three classes along a line show the difficulty: no single threshold separates the middle
class from the two outer ones, while every pair of classes is separated by one threshold.

One-versus-one asks the easier questions "class $i$ or class $j$?". Pairwise linear separability is a weaker condition
than one-against-the-rest linear separability, which is why Knerr, Personnaz and Dreyfus built multiclass networks from
one linear separator per pair <Cite id="knerr1990pairwise" />. The cost is that each classifier answers a question
about only two classes, and at prediction time it is asked about inputs from all $K$.

## Mechanism

### Training

For each pair $i < j$, keep the $n_{ij}$ training examples with $y \in \set{i, j}$, label class $i$ positive and class
$j$ negative, and train $f_{ij}$. The $K(K-1)/2$ problems are independent and can be trained in parallel. Each problem
is as balanced as the two classes are.

### Majority voting

Each classifier casts one vote. The votes for class $i$ are

$$
v_i(\xvec) = \sum_{j \ne i} \indicator\left[r_{ij}(\xvec) > \tfrac12\right],
$$

and the prediction is $\argmax_i v_i(\xvec)$, the **max-wins** rule <Cite id="fuernkranz2002roundrobin" />. The votes
sum to $K(K-1)/2$.

**What voting guarantees.** Let the true class be $y$. If the $K - 1$ classifiers that involve $y$ are all right, $y$
wins all its contests and has $v_y = K - 1$. Any other class $i$ lost to $y$, so it can win at most its $K - 2$ other
contests: $v_i \le K - 2$. Class $y$ wins outright, whatever the other $(K-1)(K-2)/2$ classifiers say.

**Voting is Bayes-consistent under exact pairwise probabilities.** Write $p_k = \pr(y = k \mid \xvec)$. The exact
pairwise probability is

$$
r_{ij} = \pr(y = i \mid y \in \set{i, j}, \xvec) = \frac{p_i}{p_i + p_j},
$$

which exceeds $1/2$ exactly when $p_i > p_j$. Class $i$ then collects one vote for every class less probable than it,
so the votes rank the classes by $p_k$, and the most probable class, the Bayes decision, gets $K - 1$ votes.

**Incompetent classifiers.** A trained $f_{ij}$ is not the exact pairwise probability. It was fitted on classes $i$ and
$j$ only, and for an input of class $k \notin \set{i, j}$ it extrapolates into a region with no training data. Its
answer there is arbitrary, and the classifier is called **incompetent** for that input. Voting is guaranteed to survive
them only while every competent classifier, one that involves $y$, is right. One wrong competent classifier leaves $y$
with $K - 2$ votes, and the incompetent votes decide whether another class reaches $K - 2$ or more.

**Ties.** Several classes can share the maximum vote, most often when the pairwise answers form a cycle ($i$ beats
$j$, $j$ beats $k$, $k$ beats $i$). Common tie-breaks are the class prior, the sum of the pairwise scores
$\sum_{j \ne i} r_{ij}$, or the coupled probabilities below.

### Pairwise coupling

Voting discards how confident each classifier is. **Pairwise coupling** keeps it by finding one probability vector
$\pvec = (p_1, \dots, p_K)$ whose implied pairwise probabilities $\mu_{ij} = p_i / (p_i + p_j)$ are as close as possible
to the estimates $r_{ij}$. With $K - 1$ free unknowns (the $p_i$ sum to one) and $K(K-1)/2$ estimates, the system is
overdetermined for $K \ge 3$ and usually inconsistent, so coupling is a fit.

Hastie and Tibshirani measure closeness by the weighted <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler
divergence</NoteLink> between the pairwise <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli distributions</NoteLink>
<Cite id="hastie1998pairwise" />:

$$
\ell(\pvec) = \sum_{i < j} n_{ij} \left[ r_{ij} \log\frac{r_{ij}}{\mu_{ij}} + r_{ji} \log\frac{r_{ji}}{\mu_{ji}} \right].
$$

**The fixed point.** Drop the terms that do not depend on $\pvec$. Since $\log \mu_{ij} = \log p_i - \log(p_i + p_j)$
and $r_{ij} + r_{ji} = 1$, minimising $\ell$ is maximising

$$
\sum_{i < j} n_{ij} \left[ r_{ij} \log p_i + r_{ji} \log p_j - \log(p_i + p_j) \right].
$$

This is the log-likelihood of a <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Bradley–Terry model</NoteLink> with strengths
$p_i$, in which classes $i$ and $j$ play $n_{ij}$ games and $i$ wins a fraction $r_{ij}$ of them. The objective is
unchanged when $\pvec$ is multiplied by a constant, so the constraint $\sum_i p_i = 1$ can be imposed after
maximising. Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> with respect to $p_i$ to zero,

$$
\sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij} \left[ \frac{r_{ij}}{p_i} - \frac{1}{p_i + p_j} \right] = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}\, r_{ij} = \sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}\, \mu_{ij}.
$$

At the optimum each class's observed wins equal its expected wins under $\pvec$. Rearranging the stationarity condition
gives the iteration Hastie and Tibshirani proposed:

$$
p_i \leftarrow p_i \, \frac{\sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}\, r_{ij}}{\sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}\, \mu_{ij}}, \qquad
\text{then renormalise } \pvec.
$$

A fixed point of the update satisfies the stationarity condition. The update is Zermelo's iteration for Bradley–Terry
strengths, a minorise–maximise algorithm whose objective increases at every step. If the $r_{ij}$ are exact,
$\mu_{ij} = r_{ij}$ is attainable, $\ell = 0$, and coupling returns the true $\pvec$.

**The method used in LIBSVM.** Wu, Lin and Weng proposed two further criteria <Cite id="wu2004pairwise" />. The second
starts from the identity that exact probabilities satisfy: $r_{ij} = p_i/(p_i + p_j)$ is equivalent to
$r_{ji}\, p_i = r_{ij}\, p_j$. It minimises the squared violation of that identity,

$$
\min_{\pvec} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sum_{j \ne i} \left( r_{ji}\, p_i - r_{ij}\, p_j \right)^2
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_i p_i = 1,\ p_i \ge 0.
$$

The objective is $2\pvec^\tr\Qmat\pvec$ for the <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss>
matrix $\Qmat$ with $Q_{ii} = \sum_{s \ne i}
r_{si}^2$ and $Q_{ij} = -r_{ji}\, r_{ij}$. Dropping the sign constraints, the Lagrange conditions are the linear system

$$
\Qmat\pvec = b\,\ones, \qquad \ones^\tr\pvec = 1,
$$

with $b = \pvec^\tr\Qmat\pvec$ the optimal value. Wu, Lin and Weng showed that its solution is non-negative, so the
dropped constraints hold. One $K \times K$ solve replaces an iteration. LIBSVM applies it to pairwise probabilities
obtained by fitting a <NoteLink to="platt-scaling">Platt sigmoid</NoteLink> to each pairwise <Gloss name="svm" />'s
decision values <Cite id="chang2011" />.

<Derivation title="The quadratic form of the Wu–Lin–Weng criterion">
Each unordered pair $\set{i, j}$ appears twice in the double sum, as $(r_{ji}p_i - r_{ij}p_j)^2$ and as
$(r_{ij}p_j - r_{ji}p_i)^2$, which are equal. The coefficient of $p_i^2$ collects $r_{ji}^2$ from both, for every
$j \ne i$: $2\sum_{j \ne i} r_{ji}^2 = 2Q_{ii}$. The coefficient of $p_ip_j$ collects $-2r_{ji}r_{ij}$ from both:
$-4r_{ji}r_{ij} = 4Q_{ij}$. Since $2\pvec^\tr\Qmat\pvec = 2\sum_i Q_{ii}p_i^2 + 4\sum_{i<j} Q_{ij}p_ip_j$, the objective
equals $2\pvec^\tr\Qmat\pvec$. As a sum of squares it is non-negative for every $\pvec$, so $\Qmat$ is positive
semi-definite.

With multiplier $\lambda$ for $\ones^\tr\pvec = 1$, the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> condition is
$4\Qmat\pvec = \lambda\ones$. Write $b = \lambda / 4$. Multiplying $\Qmat\pvec = b\ones$ by $\pvec^\tr$ and using
$\ones^\tr\pvec = 1$ gives $b = \pvec^\tr\Qmat\pvec$.
</Derivation>

### Decision DAG

Voting evaluates all $K(K-1)/2$ classifiers. Platt, Cristianini and Shawe-Taylor's **decision DAG** evaluates $K - 1$
<Cite id="platt2000dags" />. Keep a list of candidate classes, initially all $K$. Evaluate the classifier for the first
and last classes in the list and remove the loser. After $K - 1$ tests one class remains. The tests form a
<Gloss name="dag" /> of $K(K-1)/2$ nodes, one per pair, in which every path from the root to a leaf passes $K - 1$
nodes. With a <Gloss name="svm" /> at each node (DAGSVM), Platt and co-authors bounded the generalisation error in terms
of the margins at the nodes.

A DAG never needs an incompetent classifier to decide between competent ones: a pair is tested only while both classes
are still candidates. The prediction depends on the order of the list whenever the pairwise answers contain a cycle,
and it gives no probabilities.

### One-versus-one as an output code

In the language of <NoteLink to="error-correcting-output-codes">error-correcting output codes</NoteLink>, one-versus-one
is a ternary code with a column for each pair $(i, j)$: $+1$ in row $i$, $-1$ in row $j$, and $0$ elsewhere, where $0$
means the column never trains on that class <Cite id="allwein2000reducing" />. Hamming decoding charges $1$ for a
disagreeing entry and $1/2$ for a zero. Row $i$ has zeros in the $(K-1)(K-2)/2$ columns that do not involve class $i$,
the same number for every row. The zeros therefore add the same constant to every distance, and minimising the distance
maximises the number of columns involving $i$ that agree with row $i$: Hamming decoding is majority voting. Any two
rows are both non-zero in one column only, so the code guarantees no correction, as the voting argument above showed.

## Worked example

Take $K = 4$ and one input $\xvec$ with these pairwise outputs $r_{ij}$ (the table gives $r_{ij}$ for $i < j$;
$r_{ji} = 1 - r_{ij}$):

| Pair | $(1,2)$ | $(1,3)$ | $(1,4)$ | $(2,3)$ | $(2,4)$ | $(3,4)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $r_{ij}$ | $0.60$ | $0.45$ | $0.90$ | $0.70$ | $0.80$ | $0.70$ |

**Vote.** Class 1 beats 2 and 4 and loses to 3. Class 2 beats 3 and 4. Class 3 beats 1 and 4. Class 4 wins nothing. The
votes are $(2, 2, 2, 0)$: a three-way tie produced by the cycle $1 \succ 2 \succ 3 \succ 1$. If the true class is 1, the
single wrong competent classifier is $f_{13}$, and one error was enough to lose the guarantee.

**Hastie–Tibshirani coupling.** Take $n_{ij}$ equal. The observed wins $\sum_{j \ne i} r_{ij}$ are

$$
(0.60 + 0.45 + 0.90,\; 0.40 + 0.70 + 0.80,\; 0.55 + 0.30 + 0.70,\; 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.30) = (1.95,\; 1.90,\; 1.55,\; 0.60).
$$

Iterating the update from $\pvec = (1/4, 1/4, 1/4, 1/4)$ converges to

$$
\pvec = (0.358,\; 0.338,\; 0.230,\; 0.075),
$$

at which the expected wins $\sum_{j \ne i} p_i/(p_i + p_j)$ are $(1.95, 1.90, 1.55, 0.60)$, equal to the observed ones.
Coupling breaks the tie in favour of class 1, which lost its one contest narrowly ($0.45$) and won its others by more.

**Wu–Lin–Weng coupling.** The matrix $\Qmat$ is

$$
\Qmat = \begin{pmatrix}
0.4725 & -0.2400 & -0.2475 & -0.0900 \\
-0.2400 & 0.4900 & -0.2100 & -0.1600 \\
-0.2475 & -0.2100 & 0.7825 & -0.2100 \\
-0.0900 & -0.1600 & -0.2100 & 1.9400
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where, for example, $Q_{11} = r_{21}^2 + r_{31}^2 + r_{41}^2 = 0.40^2 + 0.55^2 + 0.10^2 = 0.4725$ and
$Q_{12} = -r_{21} r_{12} = -0.40 \times 0.60 = -0.24$. Solving $\Qmat\pvec = b\ones$, $\ones^\tr\pvec = 1$ gives
$\pvec = (0.347, 0.333, 0.242, 0.078)$ with $b = 0.0169$. The two couplings agree on the order of the classes and differ
by about $0.01$ in each probability.

**Decision DAG.** With the list $(1, 2, 3, 4)$: $f_{14}$ gives $0.90$, so 4 leaves; $f_{13}$ gives $0.45$, so 1 leaves;
$f_{23}$ gives $0.70$, so the DAG predicts class 2 after three tests. Over all $24$ orderings of the list, classes 1, 2
and 3 each win $8$ times. The DAG resolves the tie by the order of the list, not by the evidence.

## Cost

Let the $n$ training examples be split evenly, $n/K$ per class, and let training a binary classifier on $m$ examples
cost $c\,m^\alpha$. One-versus-rest trains $K$ classifiers on $n$ examples each; one-versus-one trains $K(K-1)/2$
classifiers on $2n/K$ examples each. The ratio of total costs is

$$
\frac{\text{one-versus-one}}{\text{one-versus-rest}}
= \frac{\frac{K(K-1)}{2}\left(\frac{2n}{K}\right)^{\alpha}}{K\, n^{\alpha}}
= \frac{2^{\alpha-1}(K-1)}{K^{\alpha}}.
$$

For a learner linear in $m$ ($\alpha = 1$) the ratio is $(K-1)/K$: one-versus-one is slightly cheaper, since each
example appears in $K - 1$ problems instead of $K$. For a learner quadratic in $m$ ($\alpha = 2$) the ratio is
$2(K-1)/K^2 \approx 2/K$, so with $K = 10$ one-versus-one costs about a fifth as much.
<NoteLink to="kernel-support-vector-machine">Kernel support vector machine</NoteLink> solvers are typically superlinear
in the number of examples, and Hsu and Lin found one-versus-one and the decision DAG the fastest to train among the
<NoteLink to="multiclass-support-vector-machines">multiclass support vector machine</NoteLink> methods they compared,
with accuracy as good as the others <Cite id="hsu2002comparison" />. LIBSVM uses one-versus-one for multiclass problems
for this reason <Cite id="chang2011" />.

Prediction evaluates $K(K-1)/2$ classifiers for voting or coupling and $K - 1$ for a DAG. Coupling adds an iteration or
one $K \times K$ linear solve. Storage is $K(K-1)/2$ models, which grows quadratically: $4{,}950$ classifiers for
$K = 100$. For kernel machines the growth is milder than it looks, because each pairwise model keeps support vectors
from two classes only and all models share one set of training points and one kernel cache.

## Trade-offs

| Property | One-versus-one | One-versus-rest | Output codes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Binary problems | $K(K-1)/2$ | $K$ | $L$, chosen |
| Examples per problem | about $2n/K$ | $n$ | $n$ (dense codes) |
| Class balance within a problem | as balanced as the two classes | about $1:(K-1)$ | depends on the column |
| Evaluations to predict | $K(K-1)/2$, or $K - 1$ with a DAG | $K$ | $L$ |
| Ties | common, from cycles | when scores are equal or uncalibrated | when errors reach half the minimum distance $d$ |
| Probabilities | by pairwise coupling | by normalising calibrated scores | not directly |

- **Easier problems, more of them.** Pairwise problems are smaller, more balanced and more often linearly separable. The
  number of models grows quadratically in $K$, which for $K = 1{,}000$ means $499{,}500$ models to store and to evaluate
  at prediction time.
- **Incompetent classifiers.** Every prediction consults classifiers that never saw the input's class. Voting and
  coupling both weight their answers like any other; the DAG avoids some of them but becomes order-dependent.
- **No error correction.** One wrong competent classifier can change the prediction. Codes with larger minimum distance,
  described in <NoteLink to="error-correcting-output-codes">error-correcting output codes</NoteLink>, trade more and
  harder binary problems for tolerance of such errors.
- **Accuracy in practice.** With well-tuned regularised classifiers the reductions perform similarly: Rifkin and
  Klautau found one-versus-rest as accurate as one-versus-one and output codes on their benchmarks
  <Cite id="rifkin2004defense" />. The choice then rests on training cost, prediction cost and whether probabilities are
  needed.
