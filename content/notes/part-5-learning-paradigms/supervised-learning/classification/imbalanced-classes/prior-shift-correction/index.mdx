<Definition>
**Label shift** is a change between the training distribution $p_{\text{train}}$ and the deployment distribution
$p_{\text{new}}$ in which the class priors change, $\pi_{\text{new}}(y) \ne \pi_{\text{train}}(y)$, while the
class-conditional densities do not: $p_{\text{new}}(\xvec \mid y) = p_{\text{train}}(\xvec \mid y)$. **Prior-shift
correction** re-weights a trained classifier's posteriors to the deployment priors. When those priors are unknown, it
first estimates them from unlabelled deployment data <Cite id="saerens2002" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier is trained at one prevalence and used at another. A screening model is built on a case-control sample
with half its cases diseased and deployed in a clinic where 2% are. A model is trained on balanced resampled data. A
fraud rate rises during an attack. In each case the model's posteriors carry the training prior, and they are wrong
at deployment even though the relation between features and class has not changed.

## Mechanism

### The posterior adjustment

By Bayes' theorem in each population,

$$
p_{\text{train}}(y \mid \xvec) = \frac{p(\xvec \mid y)\,\pi_{\text{train}}(y)}{p_{\text{train}}(\xvec)},
\qquad
p_{\text{new}}(y \mid \xvec) = \frac{p(\xvec \mid y)\,\pi_{\text{new}}(y)}{p_{\text{new}}(\xvec)},
$$

with the same $p(\xvec \mid y)$ in both by the label-shift assumption. Solving the first for $p(\xvec \mid y)$ and
substituting into the second,

$$
p_{\text{new}}(y \mid \xvec) = \frac{p_{\text{train}}(\xvec)}{p_{\text{new}}(\xvec)}\cdot p_{\text{train}}(y \mid \xvec)\,\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(y)}{\pi_{\text{train}}(y)}.
$$

The first factor does not depend on $y$. It is fixed by the requirement that the posteriors sum to 1 over classes:

$$
p_{\text{new}}(y \mid \xvec) = \frac{p_{\text{train}}(y \mid \xvec)\,\pi_{\text{new}}(y)/\pi_{\text{train}}(y)}{\sum_{k} p_{\text{train}}(k \mid \xvec)\,\pi_{\text{new}}(k)/\pi_{\text{train}}(k)}
$$

<Cite id="saerens2002" />. Each class's posterior is multiplied by the ratio of its new prior to its old one and the
result renormalised. For two classes this is a shift in log-odds by
$\operatorname{logit}\pi_{\text{new}} - \operatorname{logit}\pi_{\text{train}}$, Elkan's base-rate adjustment
<Cite id="elkan2001" />. The adjustment is exact only if $p_{\text{train}}(y \mid \xvec)$ is calibrated for the
training distribution.

### Estimating the new priors by EM

When the deployment priors are unknown, unlabelled deployment data $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_N$ identify them. The
deployment density is a mixture, $p_{\text{new}}(\xvec) = \sum_k \pi_{\text{new}}(k)\,p(\xvec \mid k)$, whose
components are fixed and whose weights are the unknown priors. The log-likelihood of the weights is

$$
L(\pi) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\sum_k \pi(k)\,p(\xvec_i \mid k).
$$

<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">Expectation-maximisation</NoteLink> for mixture weights alternates two steps,
starting from $\pi^{(0)} = \pi_{\text{train}}$ <Cite id="saerens2002" />.

- **E-step.** Compute each example's posterior under the current priors. By the adjustment above, this needs only the
  classifier's output:
  $p^{(s)}(k \mid \xvec_i) \propto p_{\text{train}}(k \mid \xvec_i)\,\pi^{(s)}(k)/\pi_{\text{train}}(k)$. The unknown
  densities $p(\xvec \mid k)$ never have to be evaluated, because they enter only through ratios that the classifier
  already encodes.
- **M-step.** Set each prior to the average posterior responsibility:
  $\pi^{(s+1)}(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i p^{(s)}(k \mid \xvec_i)$.

The M-step is the maximiser of the expected complete-data log-likelihood
$\sum_i\sum_k p^{(s)}(k \mid \xvec_i)\log\pi(k)$ subject to $\sum_k\pi(k) = 1$: setting the
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the Lagrangian to zero gives
$\pi(k) \propto \sum_i p^{(s)}(k \mid \xvec_i)$. Each iteration does not decrease $L$. At the fixed point, the estimated
prior equals the mean of the posteriors adjusted to it, a self-consistency that the naive estimate lacks. The naive
estimate, the mean of the unadjusted posteriors, is the first EM iterate. It is biased towards $\pi_{\text{train}}$,
because every posterior still carries the training prior.

### Black-box shift estimation

EM needs calibrated posteriors. Black-box shift estimation (BBSE) needs only hard predictions and a labelled
validation set from the training distribution <Cite id="lipton2018" />. Let $\hat y$ be the classifier's prediction.
Under label shift the conditional $p(\hat y \mid y)$ is the same in both populations, because $\hat y$ is a function of
$\xvec$ and $p(\xvec \mid y)$ does not change. Hence, with weights $w_k = \pi_{\text{new}}(k)/\pi_{\text{train}}(k)$,

$$
p_{\text{new}}(\hat y = j) = \sum_k p(\hat y = j \mid y = k)\,\pi_{\text{new}}(k)
= \sum_k p_{\text{train}}(\hat y = j, y = k)\,w_k.
$$

In matrix form $\qvec = \Cmat\wvec$, where $\Cmat_{jk} = p_{\text{train}}(\hat y = j, y = k)$ is the joint
<NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink> on training-distribution data and
$q_j = p_{\text{new}}(\hat y = j)$ is the rate of each prediction on deployment data. Both are estimable, the first from
labelled validation data and the second from unlabelled deployment data, so $\hat\wvec = \hat\Cmat^{-1}\hat\qvec$. BBSE
requires $\Cmat$ to be invertible, which holds when the classifier is better than chance on every class. It works with a
classifier that is uncalibrated or poor, at the cost of higher variance.

For two classes, with true- and <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rates</Gloss> <Gloss name="tpr" /> and FPR, the
deployment predicted-positive rate is $q = \text{TPR}\,\pi_{\text{new}} + \text{FPR}\,(1 - \pi_{\text{new}})$, so

$$
\hat\pi_{\text{new}} = \frac{q - \text{FPR}}{\text{TPR} - \text{FPR}}.
$$

Alexandari et al. found that EM applied after a calibration step that corrects systematic bias (bias-corrected
<NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">temperature scaling</NoteLink>) matches or beats BBSE and its variants
<Cite id="alexandari2020" />.

## Worked example

**Adjustment.** A model trained on a balanced sample, $\pi_{\text{train}} = 0.5$, outputs $p = 0.8$. The deployment
prior is $0.1$. The odds $0.8/0.2 = 4$ are multiplied by $(0.1/0.9)/(0.5/0.5) = 1/9$, giving odds $4/9$ and
$p_{\text{new}} = 4/13 = 0.308$.

**EM.** The same model scores five unlabelled deployment cases at $0.9, 0.6, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1$. Their mean, $0.42$, is
the first EM estimate. The E-step with $\pi = 0.42$ multiplies each case's odds by $0.42/0.58 = 0.724$, giving adjusted
posteriors $0.867, 0.521, 0.237, 0.153, 0.075$ with mean $0.370$. The next iterate is $0.340$, and the sequence
converges to $\hat\pi_{\text{new}} = 0.291$, where the adjusted posteriors $0.787, 0.381, 0.149, 0.093, 0.044$ average
to $0.291$ again.

**BBSE.** On validation data the classifier has $\text{TPR} = 0.8$ and $\text{FPR} = 0.1$. On deployment data it
predicts positive for 15% of cases. The estimate is $\hat\pi_{\text{new}} = (0.15 - 0.1)/(0.8 - 0.1) = 0.071$. The raw
rate of positive predictions, 0.15, overstates the prevalence by a factor of two, because <Gloss name="fp">false
positives</Gloss> from the large negative class outnumber the <Gloss name="tp">true positives</Gloss>.

## Cost

The adjustment is one multiplication per class per example. EM costs $O(NK)$ per iteration for $N$ examples and $K$
classes and typically converges in tens of iterations. BBSE needs one $K \times K$ solve.

## Trade-offs

- **Label shift is an assumption.** If $p(\xvec \mid y)$ also changes, as when a new kind of fraud appears, neither the
  adjustment nor the estimates are valid. Lipton et al. use the same statistics to test for label shift
  <Cite id="lipton2018" />.
- **EM needs calibration; BBSE needs a good confusion matrix.** EM with miscalibrated posteriors converges to the wrong
  prior. BBSE's error grows as $\Cmat$ approaches singularity and with few validation examples of the rare class.
- **Decisions change too.** A cost-optimal threshold on calibrated posteriors stays the same, but the posteriors
  crossing it change. Applying the adjustment is equivalent to moving the threshold on the unadjusted posterior.
- **Resampled or weighted training** is a deliberate prior shift. The same adjustment undoes it: see
  <NoteLink to="random-over-and-undersampling">random over- and undersampling</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration under imbalance</NoteLink>.
