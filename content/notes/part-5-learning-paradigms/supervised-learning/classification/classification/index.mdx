<Definition>
**Classification** is <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink> with a discrete target. Given
training pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ with $y_i \in \set{1, \dots, K}$, a classifier assigns a class to each new input. Under
0–1 loss the best possible rule picks the class with the largest posterior probability, $\argmax_k P(y = k \mid \xvec)$,
so many classifiers estimate those probabilities and then decide.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Classifiers filter spam, detect fraud, triage medical images, route support tickets and label every token or pixel in
larger systems. Two settings recur in practice and each has a chapter here. In the first, one class is rare, as with
fraud or disease, and accuracy becomes misleading. In the second, there are many classes, and the problem is solved
either by one model with $K$ outputs or by combining binary classifiers.

## Main ideas

**Score, then threshold.** Most classifiers compute a score or a probability and compare it with a threshold. The
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule" /> sets the threshold from the costs of each error, and the same model serves
different costs by moving it. <NoteLink to="logistic-regression" /> is the reference model: a linear score passed
through the logistic function, fitted by minimising the cross-entropy, a convex loss.

**Probabilities need care.** A classifier's scores are useful as probabilities only when they are calibrated. Training
choices such as class weights, resampling and focal loss change the learnt probabilities in known ways, and
<NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">classifier calibration</NoteLink> repairs them after training.

**Rare classes change evaluation more than learning.** <NoteLink to="class-imbalance" /> shows that a small class prior
does not bias a well-specified probabilistic model. It leaves few minority examples, makes accuracy and the $0.5$
threshold misleading, and invites remedies such as SMOTE that distort probabilities and rarely help a strong classifier
once the threshold is tuned.

**Many classes, directly or by reduction.** <NoteLink to="multiclass-classification" /> contrasts models with $K$
outputs, such as multinomial logistic regression and the multiclass SVM, with reductions to binary problems:
one-versus-rest, one-versus-one, error-correcting output codes and nested dichotomies. A reduction is decoded through a
code matrix, and codewords far apart in Hamming distance correct errors of individual binary classifiers.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>. The other chapters use it as their
  running example.
- <NoteLink to="class-imbalance" /> and <NoteLink to="multiclass-classification">multiclass classification</NoteLink>
  are independent of each other. A one-versus-rest reduction creates imbalanced binary problems, so the imbalance
  chapter is useful background for it.

Most classification models live in neighbouring topics, organised by how they model the data.
<NoteLink to="generative-classifiers" /> model $p(\xvec \mid y)$. <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours" /> votes among
neighbours. <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">Support vector machines</NoteLink> maximise a margin.
<NoteLink to="trees-and-ensembles" /> split the feature space. <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification" /> puts a
Gaussian-process prior on a latent score. <NoteLink to="structured-prediction" /> predicts many interdependent labels at
once, and <NoteLink to="ordinal-regression">ordinal regression</NoteLink> predicts ordered ones.
