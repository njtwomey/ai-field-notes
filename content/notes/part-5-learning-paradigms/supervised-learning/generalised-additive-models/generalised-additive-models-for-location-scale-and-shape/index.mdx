<Definition>
A **generalised additive model for location, scale and shape** (GAMLSS) assumes $y_i \sim \Dcal(\theta_{i1}, \dots,
\theta_{iK})$ for a parametric family $\Dcal$ with up to four parameters, usually location $\mu$, scale $\sigma$ and two
shape parameters $\nu$ and $\tau$. Each parameter has its own link function $g_k$ and additive predictor,

$$
g_k(\theta_{ik}) = \eta_{ik} = \xvec_{ik}^\top\betavec_k + \sum_j f_{jk}(x_{ij}),
$$

with penalised smooth terms <Cite id="rigby2005" />.
</Definition>

## Beyond the mean

A <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model (GLM)</NoteLink> or generalised additive model (GAM)
models the mean. Its variance follows from the mean through the family's variance function, and its shape is fixed by
the family. Many responses break that link. The spread of body weight changes with age in a way that no fixed variance
function of the mean describes. Insurance claim sizes become more skewed for some policy types. Growth reference charts
need the 3rd and 97th centiles as smooth functions of age, and those depend on how the spread and skewness change, not
only on the median. A GAMLSS gives each of these features its own additive predictor. The fitted distribution at each
$\xvec$ then yields any quantile, interval or tail probability directly.

## Each parameter as a smooth function

A GAMLSS is a generative model: fix the curves $\eta_k(x)$ and the conditional law of $y$ follows at every $x$. In the
figure each $\eta_k(x) = \sum_j c_{kj}B_j(x)$ is a cubic B-spline with six coefficients, drawn as control points on the
link scale. The parameter itself is $\theta_k(x) = g_k^{-1}(\eta_k(x))$. The location $\mu$ moves the distribution. The
scale $\sigma$ widens or narrows it without moving its centre. A shape parameter $\nu$ changes the tails (Student t) or
the skewness (Box–Cox Cole–Green), so the centiles spread unequally about the median. A log link maps every real $\eta$
to a positive $\sigma$. An identity link sets $\sigma = \eta$, and the distribution is undefined wherever that curve
crosses zero.

## Fitting by penalised likelihood

The estimate maximises the penalised log-likelihood

$$
\ell_p = \sum_i\log p\paren{y_i \mid \theta_{i1}, \dots, \theta_{iK}} - \frac12\sum_{k,j}\lambda_{jk}\,\betavec_{jk}^\top
\Smat_{jk}\betavec_{jk}.
$$

Rigby and Stasinopoulos's **RS algorithm** cycles over the parameters. It updates the predictor $\eta_k$ of one
parameter by <NoteLink to="penalised-iteratively-reweighted-least-squares">penalised iteratively reweighted least
squares (IRLS)</NoteLink> with the others held fixed, then moves to the next, until the global deviance
$\mathrm{GD} = -2\ell$ stops changing <Cite id="rigby2005" />. Each inner step is an ordinary penalised weighted
least-squares fit, so any smoother that a GAM can use works for any parameter. The step for $\theta_k$ needs only the
family's score $\partial\ell_i/\partial\theta_{ik}$ and expected second derivative
$\expect[\partial^2\ell_i/\partial\theta_{ik}^2]$. The cross derivatives between parameters are not used.

```text
θ_k ← initial values; β_k ← least-squares fit of g_k(θ_k) on X_k
repeat                                       # one RS cycle
  for k = 1 … K                              # μ, then σ, then ν
    repeat                                   # penalised IRLS
      d_i ← dθ_ik/dη_ik
      u_i ← (∂ℓ_i/∂θ_ik) d_i                 # score in η
      w_i ← −E[∂²ℓ_i/∂θ_ik²] d_i²            # information in η
      z_i ← η_ik + u_i / w_i                 # working response
      β_k ← solve (X_kᵀWX_k + λ_k S_k) β = X_kᵀWz
      while GD + Σ λ βᵀSβ rises: halve the step
    until the penalised deviance settles
    λ_k ← (rank S_k − λ_k tr(H_k⁻¹S_k)) / (β_kᵀS_kβ_k)
until GD changes by less than 0.001
```

Here $\Hmat_k = \Xmat_k^\top\Wmat\Xmat_k + \lambda_k\Smat_k$. The update of $\lambda_k$ is the Fellner–Schall fixed
point of the working model's marginal likelihood, in which $\betavec_k$ has a Gaussian prior with precision
$\lambda_k\Smat_k$ <Cite id="wood2017fellner" />. This local maximum-likelihood step is optional; without it $\lambda_k$
stays fixed. At fixed $\lambda$ every accepted step lowers the penalised deviance, so the penalised deviance never rises
from one cycle to the next. The effective degrees of freedom of $\theta_k$ are
$\operatorname{tr}(\Hmat_k^{-1}\Xmat_k^\top\Wmat\Xmat_k)$. The generalised Akaike information criterion
$\mathrm{GAIC}(\kappa) = \mathrm{GD} + \kappa\,\mathrm{df}$ uses their sum over the parameters as $\mathrm{df}$;
$\kappa = 2$ gives the AIC.

**Worked example: a Gaussian location-scale model.** Let $y_i \sim \Gauss(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$ with $\eta_{i1} = \mu_i$
and $\eta_{i2} = \log\sigma_i$. With $r_i = y_i - \mu_i$, the log-likelihood of one observation is

$$
\ell_i = -\eta_{i2} - \tfrac12r_i^2e^{-2\eta_{i2}} + \text{const}.
$$

For $\mu$: the score is $r_i/\sigma_i^2$ and the expected information is $1/\sigma_i^2$. The <Gloss name="irls" />
working response is $\mu_i + r_i = y_i$ with weight $1/\sigma_i^2$, so the location smooths are refitted by weighted
penalised least squares, with observations of large spread down-weighted.

For $\log\sigma$: the score is $\partial\ell_i/\partial\eta_{i2} = -1 + r_i^2/\sigma_i^2$ and the second
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is $-2r_i^2/\sigma_i^2$, whose expectation is $-2$ since
$\expect[r_i^2] = \sigma_i^2$. A Fisher scoring step gives the working response and weight

$$
z_{i2} = \eta_{i2} + \frac{r_i^2/\sigma_i^2 - 1}{2}, \qquad w_{i2} = 2.
$$

The scale smooths are refitted to $\zvec_2$ by penalised least squares with equal weights. An observation with residual
$r_i = 2$ where the current $\sigma_i = 1$ has $z_{i2} = 0 + (4 - 1)/2 = 1.5$ and pulls $\log\sigma$ upward there. One
with $r_i = 0.5$ has $z_{i2} = (0.25 - 1)/2 = -0.375$ and pulls it down. The algorithm alternates the two refits.

## Worked example: growth centiles

A growth reference chart gives the centiles of a body measurement as smooth functions of age. The synthetic data in the
figure hold 1000 measurements at ages uniform on $[0, 18]$ years, drawn from a Box–Cox Cole–Green (BCCG) law. In BCCG,
$z = ((y/\mu)^\nu - 1)/(\nu\sigma)$ is standard normal (truncated so that $y > 0$). Then $\mu$ is the median, $\sigma$
is close to the coefficient of variation and $\nu$ is the Box–Cox power that makes $y$ symmetric. This is the LMS
method of growth charts <Cite id="cole1992" />. The true $\sigma$ rises from 0.10 to 0.18 with age, and the true $\nu$
falls from 1 (symmetric) to $-1$ (right-skewed). Each parameter that gets a smooth has a P-spline with 12 basis
functions and $\lambda$ chosen by local maximum likelihood. The other parameters are constants.

The mean-only model is a GAM with a constant $\sigma$. Its median follows the data, but its centiles are parallel to the
median. They are too wide for infants, where no point falls outside them, and too narrow for adolescents, where 19.4% of
the points fall outside instead of 6%. A smooth $\sigma$ sets the width at each age but keeps the normal's symmetry. For
adolescents no point then falls below the 3rd centile, and 5.4% lie above the 97th. The BCCG fit models the skewness
through $\nu$ and puts close to 3% in each tail at every age. Its GAIC is the lowest of the three. Its global deviance
falls from 16656 at the starting values to 5756 after one cycle and 5699 after three. Local maximum likelihood then
stiffens the $\sigma$ and $\nu$ smooths, and the global deviance rises to 5702.7 at convergence.

## Software

The R package gamlss implements the RS algorithm and a related algorithm of Cole and Green, with a large library of
distribution families <Cite id="stasinopoulos2007" />. mgcv fits several location-scale families, such as `gaulss` for
the Gaussian with smooth mean and standard deviation, by a direct Newton method on all predictors together, with
<Gloss name="reml" /> for the <NoteLink to="additive-model-smoothing-parameter-selection">smoothing
parameters</NoteLink> <Cite id="wood2017" />. pyGAM has no multi-parameter families; its closest tool is the
<NoteLink to="expectile-generalised-additive-models">expectile GAM</NoteLink>, which models asymmetric features of the
<NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> without a parametric family.

## Assumptions

- The response follows the chosen parametric family at every $\xvec$. The family decides what "shape" means.
- Each parameter's predictor is additive in its covariates.

## Pitfalls

- **Too many predictors.** Four parameters with several smooths each can overfit, and shape parameters are estimated
  from the tails, which hold few data. Start with location and scale, and add shape terms when
  <NoteLink to="residual-diagnostics">residual diagnostics</NoteLink> demand them.
- **Family choice.** Different families with the same mean and variance can give very different extreme quantiles. Check
  normalised quantile residuals.
- **Identifiability across parameters.** A covariate can enter both the location and the scale predictor. Their effects
  can trade off when the data are few.
