<Definition>
A back-constrained Gaussian process latent variable model replaces each free latent point of a
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">GP-LVM</NoteLink> by the output of a parametric map from data
space to latent space <Cite id="lawrence2006" />:

$$
x_{nq} = g_q(\yvec_n; \Amat), \qquad n = 1, \dots, N, \quad q = 1, \dots, Q.
$$

The parameters $\Amat$ of the map $\gvec$ and the kernel hyperparameters are fitted by maximising the GP-LVM likelihood
$p(\Ymat \mid \Xmat, \thetavec)$ at $\Xmat = \gvec(\Ymat; \Amat)$. A common choice is a kernel map
$g_q(\yvec) = \sum_{m=1}^{N} a_{mq}\,k_{\text{bc}}(\yvec, \yvec_m)$ with
$k_{\text{bc}}(\yvec, \yvec') = \exp\paren{-\frac\gamma2\norm{\yvec - \yvec'}^2}$.
</Definition>

## Why constrain the latent points

The GP-LVM maps latent points to data. A smooth map sends nearby latent points to nearby outputs. The likelihood
therefore keeps apart observations that are far apart in the data. It does nothing to keep together observations that
are close in the data, because a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> can take equal values at two distant inputs.
A fitted GP-LVM can tear a connected manifold into pieces and place neighbours far apart <Cite id="lawrence2006" />.

Most nonlinear embedding methods, such as <NoteLink to="isomap">Isomap</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="locally-linear-embedding">locally linear embedding</NoteLink>, preserve local distances instead. Back
constraints add that property to the GP-LVM. A smooth map in the other direction, from data to latent space, sends
nearby observations to nearby latent points. The model then has two maps, one each way, as in an
<NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink>. The back constraint plays the encoder. The Gaussian process plays a
probabilistic decoder, with the decoder's functions integrated out rather than parametrised.

## Local distances are preserved

For the kernel map, the difference between two latent coordinates is

$$
x_{iq} - x_{jq} = \sum_{m=1}^{N} a_{mq}\paren{k_{\text{bc}}(\yvec_i, \yvec_m) - k_{\text{bc}}(\yvec_j, \yvec_m)}.
$$

As a function of the distance $r$, $e^{-\gamma r^2/2}$ has <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>
$-\gamma r e^{-\gamma r^2/2}$, whose magnitude is largest at $r = 1/\sqrt\gamma$, where it equals $\sqrt{\gamma/e}$. So
$e^{-\gamma r^2/2}$ is Lipschitz in $r$ with constant $\sqrt{\gamma/e}$. By the triangle inequality, $\abs{\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_m} - \norm{\yvec_j - \yvec_m}} \le
\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}$. Together,

$$
\abs{x_{iq} - x_{jq}} \le \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{e}}\,\paren{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\abs{a_{mq}}}\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}.
$$

Observations close in the data have latent coordinates close together. The inverse width $\gamma$ sets how close:
a small $\gamma$ makes the map smooth over large distances, and a large $\gamma$ lets it bend between nearby
observations. The GP-LVM likelihood still pushes dissimilar observations apart, so the fitted embedding respects both
directions.

## Fitting

The likelihood is the GP-LVM likelihood, so its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> in the latent points is
unchanged. With $\Kmat_{\text{bc}}$ the $N \times N$ matrix of $k_{\text{bc}}(\yvec_n, \yvec_m)$, the latent points are
$\Xmat = \Kmat_{\text{bc}}\Amat$, and the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\Amat} = \Kmat_{\text{bc}}^\top\,\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\Xmat}.
$$

Any differentiable map works. Lawrence and Quiñonero-Candela also use a <Gloss name="mlp">multilayer perceptron</Gloss>,
where the number of hidden units sets the smoothness <Cite id="lawrence2006" />. To start from <Gloss name="pca" />,
$\Amat$ is set so that $\Kmat_{\text{bc}}\Amat$ is as close as possible to the PCA scores in squared distance.

The constrained model has a lower likelihood than the free one at its best. That is expected: it searches a subset of
the latent configurations. Lawrence and Quiñonero-Candela report the same on 102-dimensional motion-capture data, 34
markers in three coordinates, of a subject breaking into a run <Cite id="lawrence2006" />. The free GP-LVM reached a
log-likelihood of 1,543 but split the periodic run into several sub-sequences. The back-constrained model, with an
<Gloss name="rbf-kernel" /> map and $\gamma = 10^{-3}$, reached 1,000 and traced a squashed spiral that followed the
stride cycle. On a vowel data set, nearest-neighbour classification in the latent space made 458 errors with Isomap, 226
with the GP-LVM and 155 with the back-constrained GP-LVM, against 24 in the original data space.

## Embedding new data

The back constraint is a function of the data. A new observation $\yvec_*$ gets the latent point
$\gvec(\yvec_*; \Amat)$ by one evaluation. The unconstrained GP-LVM needs a numerical optimisation per new point,
started from several places because the objective is multimodal.

## Assumptions

- **Smoothness in the data space is meaningful.** The back constraint uses Euclidean distance between raw
  observations. If that distance is a poor measure of similarity, for example between images under small shifts, the
  constraint pulls the wrong points together.
- **The map's capacity is right.** The width $\gamma$ of a kernel map, or the size of a network, is chosen by hand.

## Pitfalls

- **Too smooth a map.** A small $\gamma$ forces far-apart observations together, which is the failure the GP-LVM avoids.
  With $\gamma$ too large the map interpolates any latent configuration, and the constraint has no effect.
- **A lower likelihood is not a worse model.** The back-constrained fit trades likelihood for neighbourhood
  preservation. Compare embeddings with a criterion that matches the purpose, such as neighbour preservation or a
  downstream task.
