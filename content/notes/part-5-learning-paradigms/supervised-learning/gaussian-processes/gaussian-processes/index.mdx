<Definition>
A **Gaussian process** (GP) is a distribution over functions in which the values at any finite set of inputs are jointly
Gaussian, with covariance given by a kernel $k(\xvec, \xvec')$. Used as a prior, it lets
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> act on whole functions. With Gaussian noise the posterior is
again a Gaussian process, with a closed-form mean and variance at every input <Cite id="rasmussen2006" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Gaussian processes give predictions with calibrated uncertainty from small and medium data sets. They are the standard
surrogate model in Bayesian optimisation, where the predictive variance decides where to evaluate an expensive function
next. They model time series, spatial fields (where GP <NoteLink to="regression">regression</NoteLink> is called
kriging), physical simulators and replicated curves. Their marginal likelihood fits kernel hyperparameters without a
validation set and can switch off irrelevant inputs.

## Main ideas

**The kernel is the model.** A GP has no weights to fit. Its assumptions about smoothness, length-scale, periodicity
and trend all live in the kernel. <NoteLink to="covariance-functions" /> covers the standard kernels and the rule that
sums and products of kernels are kernels, which builds structured models from simple parts.

**A GP is Bayesian linear regression with infinitely many features.**
<NoteLink to="gaussian-processes-and-bayesian-linear-regression" /> shows that a linear model with a Gaussian prior on
its weights is a GP whose kernel is an inner product of features. Kernels such as the squared exponential correspond
to infinitely many features, which only the function-space view can handle.

**The marginal likelihood chooses the kernel.** <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning" /> maximises
$\ln p(\yvec \mid \Xmat, \thetavec)$, which balances data fit against a complexity penalty. Giving each input its own
length-scale is <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">automatic relevance determination</NoteLink>, which
drives irrelevant inputs out of the model. The same mechanism applied to weights gives the sparse
<NoteLink to="relevance-vector-machine">relevance vector machine</NoteLink>.

**Beyond Gaussian likelihoods.** For class labels the posterior is no longer Gaussian.
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification" /> approximates it by a Laplace approximation or by expectation
propagation.

**Cost is the main limit.** Exact inference solves an $N \times N$ linear system, at $O(N^3)$ time and $O(N^2)$
memory. <NoteLink to="sparse-gaussian-processes" /> summarise the function by $M \ll N$ inducing points, at
$O(NM^2)$. <NoteLink to="scalable-and-deep-gaussian-processes" /> covers iterative solvers, structured kernels and
state-space forms, and stacks GPs into deep models.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The chapter assumes the <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal distribution</NoteLink>, in
particular conditioning a joint Gaussian, <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite
matrices</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition" />. <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression" /> is
the best stepping stone into the function-space view.

GPs connect to several other topics. <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression" /> gives the same predictive mean without
the variance. A smoothing spline is a GP posterior mean under a particular prior. A <NoteLink to="kalman-filter" />
performs GP regression in linear time for kernels with a state-space form. A neural network with a random prior on its
weights becomes a GP as its hidden layers grow infinitely wide, as
<NoteLink to="gaussian-processes-and-bayesian-linear-regression" /> shows, and the
<NoteLink to="neural-tangent-kernel">neural tangent kernel</NoteLink> describes the training of such wide networks.
