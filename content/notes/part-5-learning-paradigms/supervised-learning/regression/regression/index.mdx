<Definition>
**Regression** is <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink> with a continuous target. Given
training pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ with $\xvec_i \in \reals^d$ and $y_i \in \reals$, it learns a function $f$ such that
$f(\xvec)$ predicts $y$ for new inputs. Under squared-error loss the best possible predictor is the conditional mean
$\expect[y \mid \xvec]$, so most regression methods estimate that mean.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Regression predicts prices, demand, dosages, temperatures and any other quantity on a continuous scale. It also
describes how a quantity depends on its inputs: a linear model's weights and a smoother's fitted curve are read as
effects. Regression models are components of larger systems, as the output layer of a neural network, as the base
learners of <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient boosting</NoteLink>, and as the calibration map of a classifier.

## Main ideas

**Least squares is the reference point.** Minimising the squared error of a linear model gives a closed-form solution
and, under Gaussian noise, the maximum-likelihood fit. <NoteLink to="linear-regression" /> develops it, and every other
method in the chapter changes either the model class or the objective.

**Penalties control complexity.** With many or correlated features, a penalty on the weights trades a little bias for a
large reduction in variance. Ridge shrinks every weight, the lasso sets some to zero, and the elastic net does both.
<NoteLink to="linear-models" /> covers these and the algorithms that fit them.

**Nonlinear functions, linear fitting.** Replacing the inputs with fixed nonlinear transformations keeps the model
linear in its parameters. Splines, smoothing splines and local regression all fit curved functions with weighted or
penalised least squares. <NoteLink to="nonlinear-regression" /> covers them.

**Uncertainty comes from a probabilistic model.** A point prediction says nothing about its reliability.
<NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression" /> gives a predictive distribution, and a smoothing spline has the same
mean as a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process" /> posterior under a matching prior.

## How the notes fit together

- <NoteLink to="linear-models" /> comes first: least squares, its penalties, and their Bayesian reading.
- <NoteLink to="nonlinear-regression" /> builds on it and needs ridge regression for the smoothing penalties.

Several neighbouring topics extend regression in one direction each. <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">Generalised
linear models</NoteLink> change the noise distribution, for counts, positive values and binary outcomes.
<NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">Generalised additive models</NoteLink> combine smooth functions of several
inputs. <NoteLink to="ordinal-regression" /> handles ordered categories. <NoteLink to="gaussian-processes" />,
<NoteLink to="kernel-methods-and-support-vector-machines">kernel ridge regression</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="trees-and-ensembles">trees and ensembles</NoteLink> give other nonparametric routes to $f$.
