<Definition>
A **linear model** for <NoteLink to="regression">regression</NoteLink> predicts a continuous target $y$ from a feature
vector $\xvec \in \reals^d$ as $\hat y = \wvec^\top\xvec + b$. The weights $\wvec \in \reals^d$ and the intercept $b$
are chosen to minimise a loss on the training data, usually the squared error, plus an optional penalty on $\wvec$. The
topic covers that loss, the penalties, and the algorithms that fit them.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Linear models are the default first model for a continuous target. They fit in closed form or in one pass over the
data, and each weight reads as the change in prediction per unit change in one feature with the others held fixed.
They serve as baselines, as the final layer of many neural networks, and as the building block of
<NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis expansions</NoteLink>, splines and generalised linear models. With many
features, the penalised variants also select or stabilise the features that matter.

## Main ideas

**Least squares has a closed form.** Minimising the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> gives the normal
equations $\Xmat^\top\Xmat\wvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec$, where $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times d}$ stacks the $n$ training inputs
as rows and $\yvec \in \reals^n$ holds the targets. The fitted values are the orthogonal projection of $\yvec$ onto the
column space of $\Xmat$. Under Gaussian noise, least squares is also the maximum-likelihood fit.
<NoteLink to="linear-regression" /> develops both views.

**Penalties trade variance for bias.** When features are many or correlated, least-squares weights have high variance.
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression" /> adds $\lambda\norm{\wvec}_2^2$ and shrinks every weight, most along the directions in
which the data carry least information. <NoteLink to="lasso">The lasso</NoteLink> adds $\alpha\norm{\wvec}_1$ and sets
some weights exactly to zero, so it selects features. <NoteLink to="elastic-net">The elastic net</NoteLink> mixes the
two, keeping exact zeros while sharing weight across groups of correlated features.

**A penalty defines a path of solutions.** Each value of the penalty strength gives a different model, from all weights
zero to the unpenalised fit. <NoteLink to="least-angle-regression" /> computes the whole lasso path for about the cost
of one least-squares fit. The strength itself is chosen by <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>
or by the marginal likelihood.

**The same objectives can be fitted by stochastic gradients.** When the data do not fit in memory,
<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-for-linear-models">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink> fits ridge, lasso
and elastic-net models one example at a time, at $O(d)$ cost per update. It reaches the exact solution only
approximately.

**Penalties are priors.** Ridge is the posterior mode under a Gaussian prior on $\wvec$, and the lasso is the mode
under a Laplace prior. <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression" /> keeps the whole Gaussian posterior, which gives
predictive uncertainty and lets the marginal likelihood set the prior and noise precisions.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>. Every other note in the topic modifies its
  objective or its fitting algorithm.
- <NoteLink to="ridge-regression" /> and <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> come next and can be read in either
  order. The elastic net and least-angle regression both build on the lasso.
- The SGD note needs ridge and the lasso. Bayesian linear regression needs only linear regression and Bayes' theorem.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="vector-norms">vector
norms</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> for ridge, and
<NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> for least-angle regression.
<NoteLink to="regularisation" /> places these penalties among the other ways to control complexity.

The topic leads in several directions. <NoteLink to="nonlinear-regression" /> keeps the model linear in its weights but
replaces the features with nonlinear transformations of them. <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">Generalised linear
models</NoteLink> keep the linear predictor but change the response distribution, which gives logistic and
<NoteLink to="poisson-regression">Poisson regression</NoteLink>. <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression" /> and
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian processes</NoteLink> take ridge regression and Bayesian linear regression to
infinitely many features.
