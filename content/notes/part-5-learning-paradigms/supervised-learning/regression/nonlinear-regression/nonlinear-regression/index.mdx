<Definition>
**Nonlinear regression** fits a function $f$ with $y \approx f(x)$ when the relationship between an input $x$ and a
continuous target $y$ is not a straight line. The methods in this topic make $f$ flexible while keeping the fit cheap:
most stay linear in their parameters, so the machinery of least squares still applies. They differ in how they control
flexibility, by the number of basis functions, by a roughness penalty, by a window width, or by a shape constraint.
</Definition>

## What it is for

These methods model dose-response curves, growth curves, calibration maps and seasonal effects, where the shape of
$f$ is unknown and a line underfits. They are the building blocks of
<NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive models</NoteLink>, which add one smooth function per
input. <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression" /> is also a standard way to calibrate classifier scores into probabilities.

## Main ideas

**Linear in the weights, nonlinear in the input.** A <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis expansion</NoteLink> writes
$f(x) = \sum_m \beta_m h_m(x)$ for fixed functions $h_m$, such as powers of $x$ or piecewise polynomials. The weights
$\beta_m$ enter linearly, so least squares, ridge penalties and <Gloss name="glm">generalised-linear-model</Gloss>
fitting apply unchanged.

**Piecewise polynomials beat global ones.** A single high-degree polynomial oscillates wildly near the ends of the
data. <NoteLink to="regression-splines">Regression splines</NoteLink> join low-degree polynomials smoothly at chosen
knots, and B-splines give a numerically stable basis for them. Natural splines are linear beyond the boundary knots,
which tames the ends.

**Penalise roughness instead of choosing knots.** <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">Smoothing splines</NoteLink> put a
knot at every data point and add a penalty $\lambda\int f''(x)^2\,dx$ on curvature. The single parameter $\lambda$ then
sets the flexibility, measured by the <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of
freedom</NoteLink>, and is chosen by cross-validation or <Gloss name="gcv">generalised cross-validation</Gloss>.

**Fit locally.** <NoteLink to="local-regression">Local regression</NoteLink> (LOESS) estimates $f(x_0)$ from a
weighted least-squares line or quadratic through the points near $x_0$. The window width plays the role of $\lambda$.
Smoothing splines, regression splines and LOESS are all linear smoothers: the fitted values are a matrix times
$\yvec$.

**Constrain the shape instead of the smoothness.** <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">Isotonic regression</NoteLink>
assumes only that $f$ is non-decreasing. Its solution is a step function, computed in linear time after sorting by the
pool-adjacent-violators algorithm.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="basis-expansions">basis expansions</NoteLink>. It turns nonlinear regression into linear
  regression on new features.
- <NoteLink to="regression-splines" /> specialise the basis to piecewise polynomials, and
  <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing splines</NoteLink> replace knot selection with a penalty. Read them in that
  order.
- <NoteLink to="local-regression" /> and <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic regression</NoteLink> stand alone
  and need only linear regression.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> and, for smoothing splines,
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>, since a roughness penalty is a generalised ridge penalty.
<NoteLink to="cross-validation" /> chooses the smoothing parameters.

The topic leads to <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive models</NoteLink>, which combine
smooths of several inputs with non-Gaussian responses. <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression" /> and
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian processes</NoteLink> reach the same kind of smooth fit through a kernel; a
smoothing spline is the posterior mean under a particular Gaussian process prior.
<NoteLink to="trees-and-ensembles">Decision trees and boosting</NoteLink> give a different, piecewise-constant route to
nonlinear fits.
