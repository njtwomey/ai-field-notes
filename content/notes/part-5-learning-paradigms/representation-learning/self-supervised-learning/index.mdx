<Definition>
Self-supervised learning trains an encoder $f$ on unlabelled data by constructing a supervised **pretext task** from
the data itself: predicting a hidden part of an input from the rest, or recognising which inputs are views of the same
source. The pretext task is discarded after training. The encoder's representation $f(x)$ is kept and reused for
downstream tasks, by probing or by fine-tuning on a small labelled set.
</Definition>

## Why it works

Labels are expensive and unlabelled data is plentiful. A pretext task needs no annotation, so it can use all of the
data. The pretext task is chosen so that solving it requires the same knowledge that
downstream tasks need. Predicting a masked word requires syntax and meaning. Matching two crops of a photograph requires
recognising the object in both.

The representation transfers only to the extent that solving the pretext task requires the knowledge that downstream
tasks need. A pretext task with a shortcut, such as filling in a pixel from its neighbours, is solved without learning
anything transferable.

## The collapse problem

Many pretext tasks compare two views $x_A$ and $x_B$ of one input and ask their embeddings to agree. Agreement alone
cannot be the objective. The loss $\expect\brack{\norm{f(x_A) - f(x_B)}^2}$ is non-negative, and a constant encoder
$f(x) = \cvec$ makes every term zero. The minimum is attained by an encoder that carries no information.

Every self-supervised method avoids this in one of four ways, and the families below are defined by which one they use.

1. **Reconstruct the input.** The target is part of the input itself, such as a masked word or a masked image patch. A
   constant encoder cannot predict varying targets, so it has high loss.
2. **Contrast with negatives.** The encoder must also tell the positive view apart from other inputs. A constant encoder
   cannot, and its loss is $\log N$ for $N$ candidates.
3. **Regularise the batch.** A term computed across the batch requires each embedding dimension to vary and to be
   decorrelated from the others. A constant encoder violates it.
4. **Break the symmetry.** One branch predicts a slowly moving or gradient-stopped copy of the other. The constant
   solution still exists but is not reached by the training dynamics.

## Components



- <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">Word embeddings</NoteLink>: word2vec predicts the words around each word, an early
  large-scale pretext task on text <Cite id="mikolov2013" />.
- <NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">Masked language modelling</NoteLink>: BERT predicts randomly hidden tokens
  from both sides of their context <Cite id="devlin2019" />.
- <NoteLink to="masked-image-modelling">Masked image modelling</NoteLink>: the masked autoencoder reconstructs the
  pixels of 75% of an image's patches from the rest <Cite id="he2022" />.
- <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">Contrastive learning</NoteLink>: InfoNCE picks the positive view from a set of
  candidates, and bounds the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between views
  <Cite id="oord2018" /> <Cite id="chen2020" />.
- <NoteLink to="non-contrastive-learning">Non-contrastive learning</NoteLink>: BYOL, SimSiam, Barlow Twins and VICReg
  use positive pairs only <Cite id="grill2020" /> <Cite id="zbontar2021" />.

## Data flow

1. **Make the pretext task.** Mask part of each input, or make two views with random augmentations
   (<NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>), or pair data from two modalities such as images and
   captions (<NoteLink to="clip">CLIP</NoteLink>).
2. **Encode.** A backbone network, usually a transformer or a convolutional network, maps each input or view to a
   representation $\hvec$. Many methods add a small head, such as a projector or a decoder, that maps $\hvec$ to the
   space where the loss is computed.
3. **Compute the pretext loss** and train the backbone and head together.
4. **Discard the head.** The head specialises to the pretext task. The backbone's output is the representation.
5. **Evaluate and transfer.** Measure the frozen representation with linear or nearest-neighbour probes
   (<NoteLink to="embedding-evaluation">embedding evaluation</NoteLink>), or fine-tune it on a labelled task
   (<NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">transfer learning and fine-tuning</NoteLink>).

## Variants

| Family           | Pretext task                           | Against collapse                 | Examples                       |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Hand-crafted     | predict a transformation of the input  | varying targets                  | rotation prediction            |
| Masked           | predict hidden parts of the input      | varying targets                  | word2vec, BERT, masked autoencoder |
| Autoregressive   | predict the next token                 | varying targets                  | GPT-style <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss>      |
| Contrastive      | identify the positive among negatives  | negatives                        | CPC, SimCLR, MoCo, CLIP        |
| Non-contrastive  | make two views agree                   | batch regularisers or asymmetry  | BYOL, SimSiam, Barlow Twins    |

Hand-crafted pretext tasks came first. Rotating an image by 0, 90, 180 or 270 degrees and training a network to predict
the rotation forces it to recognise the orientation of objects, and so the objects <Cite id="gidaris2018" />. Such tasks
were overtaken by contrastive and masked methods, which do not depend on one designed shortcut-free task.

Masked and autoregressive prediction dominate in language, where tokens are discrete and a softmax over the vocabulary
gives an exact likelihood. Joint-embedding methods, contrastive and non-contrastive, were developed mainly for images,
where predicting pixels exactly wastes capacity on detail. Masked autoencoders showed that pixel prediction also works
for images when most of the image is hidden <Cite id="he2022" />.

A common geometric view explains joint-embedding methods. Their losses favour **alignment**, related inputs mapping
close together, and **uniformity**, all embeddings spreading over the space <Cite id="wang2020" />. Negatives, batch
regularisers and asymmetric updates are different ways of obtaining the second property.
