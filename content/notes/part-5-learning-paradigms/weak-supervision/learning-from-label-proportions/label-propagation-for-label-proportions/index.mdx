<Definition>
**Label propagation for learning with label proportions** (LP-LLP) is a transductive, graph-based method for recovering
instance labels from bag proportions, proposed by Poyiadzi, Santos-Rodriguez and Twomey <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />. Each
point starts with its bag's proportion as a soft label. Soft labels are smoothed over a
<NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">similarity graph</NoteLink> by label propagation. The smoothed scores are then
projected onto the set in which each bag's scores lie in $[0, 1]$ and sum to the bag's number of positives. The two
steps alternate, and a point is labelled positive if its final score exceeds $1/2$.
</Definition>

## Problem

The data are $n$ points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n \in \reals^{d}$ with hidden labels, partitioned into $K$ disjoint bags
$B_1, \dots, B_K$. For each bag the class proportions $\pivec_k = (\pi_{k,1}, \dots, \pi_{k,c})$ are known
<Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />. The goal is a label for every point. Activity recognition in smart homes is a typical
source of such data: a resident will not label every minute of sensor data, but will give summaries such as hours
slept and hours of television per day.

Earlier methods each rest on a restrictive assumption. The <NoteLink to="mean-map-and-invcal">mean map</NoteLink>
assumes that a point is independent of its bag given its label. Inverse calibration models a bag by its mean, which
fails when the bag's data have high variance <Cite id="ruping2010" />. ∝<Gloss name="svm" /> avoids both but is a
non-convex integer programme <Cite id="yu2013" />. LP-LLP brings the two principles of graph-based semi-supervised
learning, local smoothness and global structure, to label proportions without assuming how bags were formed.

## Mechanism

### Label propagation

Let $\Wmat$ be a similarity matrix with $W_{ij} = \exp(-\gamma\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2)$, $\gamma > 0$, and
$W_{ii} = 0$. Let $\Dmat$ be diagonal with $D_{ii} = \sum_j W_{ij}$, and $\Smat = \Dmat^{-1}\Wmat$, which has rows
summing to one. With a label matrix $\Ymat \in \reals^{n \times c}$ and $0 < \alpha < 1$, label propagation iterates
<Cite id="zhou2004lgc" />

$$
\Fmat(t + 1) = \alpha\Smat\Fmat(t) + (1 - \alpha)\Ymat, \qquad \Fmat(0) = \Ymat.
$$

**The limit.** A fixed point satisfies $\Fmat = \alpha\Smat\Fmat + (1 - \alpha)\Ymat$, so
$(\Imat - \alpha\Smat)\Fmat = (1 - \alpha)\Ymat$. $\Smat$ is row-stochastic, so its eigenvalues have modulus at most 1
and those of $\alpha\Smat$ at most $\alpha < 1$. The matrix $\Imat - \alpha\Smat$ is therefore invertible and the
iteration converges to

$$
\Fmat^* = (1 - \alpha)(\Imat - \alpha\Smat)^{-1}\Ymat = (1 - \alpha)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(\alpha\Smat)^t\,\Ymat.
$$

$\alpha$ balances the information from neighbours against the point's own label.

**A <NoteLink to="random-walk">random-walk</NoteLink> reading.** $(\Smat^t)_{ij}$ is the probability that a walker
starting at node $i$, stepping to a neighbour with probability proportional to the edge weight, is at node $j$ after $t$
steps. The series says that $f_i$ is a weighted count of the labels the walker visits, with visits after $t$ steps
discounted by $\alpha^t$. Early visits are near $i$ and count most <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.

**Relation to weighted $k$-nearest neighbours.** Expanding the series for one binary score, with normalised weights
$\bar{w}_{ij} = W_{ij}/\sum_j W_{ij}$ and neighbourhood $\Ncal(i) = \set{j : W_{ij} \ne 0}$,

$$
f_i \propto y_i + \alpha\sum_{j \in \Ncal(i)}\bar{w}_{ij}y_j + \alpha^2\sum_{j}\ \sum_{h \in \Ncal(i) \cap \Ncal(j)}\bar{w}_{ih}\bar{w}_{hj}y_j + \cdots.
$$

The first-order term is a weighted <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours" /> vote: local smoothness. The second-order
term runs over common neighbours of $i$ and $j$, so a point $j$ that shares many close neighbours with $i$ influences
it more: points on the same structure get the same label. This is the global-structure principle
<Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.

### From labels to proportions

In <Gloss name="llp" /> no point has a label. Restrict to two classes. Each point receives its bag's proportion as a
soft label,

$$
\hat{y}_i = \pi_{k,1} \quad\text{for } \xvec_i \in B_k,
$$

a prior probability of being positive. Propagating $\hat{\yvec}$ alone does not reproduce the proportions: nothing
stops a bag from ending with more or fewer high scores than it has positives.

**The regularisation view.** Label propagation minimises

$$
Q(\fvec) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2 + \lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_i - y_i)^2,
$$

a smoothness term plus a fit term weighted by $\lambda > 0$. With binary scores, the bag constraints are a linear system
$\Amat\fvec = \bvec$ with $\Amat \in \reals^{K \times n}$, $A_{ki} = 1$ if $\xvec_i \in B_k$ and 0 otherwise, and
$b_k = n_{k,1}$, the number of positives in bag $k$. Minimising $Q$ over $\fvec \in \set{0, 1}^n$ subject to
$\Amat\fvec = \bvec$ is an integer programme, intractable in general <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.

**The mass-preserving relaxation.** Instead of fixing the number of points of each class in each bag, fix the total
**mass** of scores in each bag. If $\fvec$ were unconstrained, one large $f_i$ could supply a bag's whole mass and the
constraint would say nothing. Restricting scores to $[0, 1]$ prevents this:

$$
\fvec^* = \argmin_{\fvec \in [0, 1]^n} Q(\fvec) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Amat\fvec = \bvec.
$$

Each bag must place exactly $n_{k,1}$ units of mass on its points, at most one unit per point.

### The algorithm

LP-LLP solves the relaxed problem in two alternating steps <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.

1. Compute $\Wmat$ and $\Smat$. Set $f_i^{(0)} = \pi_{k,1}$ for $\xvec_i \in B_k$.
2. **Propagate:** $\fvec \leftarrow (1 - \alpha)(\Imat - \alpha\Smat)^{-1}\fvec$.
3. **Project** onto $\set{\fvec : \Amat\fvec = \bvec} \cap [0, 1]^n$ by alternating projections <Cite id="boyd2003" />:
   shift each bag's scores by a common amount so that they sum to $b_k$, clip every score to $[0, 1]$, and repeat. Both
   sets are convex, and alternating projections converge to a point in their intersection when it is non-empty.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the scores stop changing.
5. Label $\hat{y}_i = 1$ if $f_i^* > 1/2$ and 0 otherwise.

The factor $1 - \alpha$ in step 2 rescales all scores equally before the projection and does not change the order of
scores within a bag, so it can be dropped. The final scores are not calibrated probabilities. They are constrained
values that act as ranks within each bag, and the constraint gives control over the predicted bag proportions.

### Worked example

Six points form three bags: $B_A = \set{1, 2}$ with $\pi_A = 0.5$, $B_B = \set{3, 4}$ with $\pi_B = 1$, and
$B_C = \set{5, 6}$ with $\pi_C = 0$. The graph has edges $1\!-\!3$, $3\!-\!4$, $2\!-\!5$ and $5\!-\!6$ of weight 1, and
a weak edge $1\!-\!2$ of weight 0.2. Point 1 is close to the all-positive bag, point 2 to the all-negative bag. Take
$\alpha = 0.5$.

The initial scores are $\fvec^{(0)} = (0.5, 0.5, 1, 1, 0, 0)$. Row 1 of $\Smat$ puts weight $0.2/1.2 = 1/6$ on point 2
and $5/6$ on point 3. One propagation step gives

$$
(1 - \alpha)(\Imat - \alpha\Smat)^{-1}\fvec^{(0)} = (0.654,\ 0.346,\ 0.901,\ 0.951,\ 0.099,\ 0.049).
$$

Projection: bag A already sums to $b_A = 1$. Bag B must sum to 2 with both scores at most 1, so both become 1. Bag C
must sum to 0 with both scores at least 0, so both become 0. After one round
$\fvec = (0.654, 0.346, 1, 1, 0, 0)$; after a second, $(0.734, 0.266, 1, 1, 0, 0)$. Bag A's single positive is
assigned to point 1. The proportion of bag A carries no information about which of its points is positive; the graph
supplies it, through the neighbouring bags whose proportions are extreme.

## Cost

The similarity matrix costs $O(n^2)$ to build. The inverse of $\Imat - \alpha\Smat$ costs $O(n^3)$ and is the
bottleneck; it is computed once and reused in every round. <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">Low-rank
approximations</NoteLink> trade accuracy for speed. The projection step costs $O(n)$ per sweep
<Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.

## Trade-offs

- **No assumption on bag formation.** LP-LLP does not require points to be independent of their bags given their
  labels, which the mean map needs <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.
- **Multiclass.** The formulation extends to $c$ classes: rows of $\Fmat$ live on the probability simplex and columns
  are constrained by the bag proportions.
- **Transductive.** Scores exist only for points in the graph. New points must be added to the graph or scored from
  their neighbours.
- **Graph quality.** The graph depends on $\gamma$. Too small and the graph connects across classes; too large and it
  fragments. No labels are available to tune it. Poyiadzi et al. pick $\gamma$ from a grid by the largest
  $\bar{\fvec}^\top\Smat\bar{\fvec}$, where $\bar{\fvec}$ is the vector of final scores: the value under which the
  recovered scores agree most with their neighbours <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.
- **Scale.** The $O(n^3)$ inverse limits the direct method to a few thousand points without approximation.
- **Where it helps most.** On data whose classes form non-linear clusters, such as XOR, and on raw wrist-worn
  accelerometer data from the SPHERE smart-home challenge <Cite id="twomey2016sphere" />, LP-LLP was more accurate than
  ∝SVM and inverse calibration, and its advantage was largest when the bag proportions differed most
  <Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />. When all proportions sit near the class prior, every method has little to work with.
- **Asking for proportions.** The same label format suits <Gloss name="active-learning">active learning</Gloss>: instead
  of querying one instance's label, the learner asks for a bag's proportions, a question that annotators find easier to
  answer. Poyiadzi et al. show that learning stays effective with such queries <Cite id="poyiadzi2019" />.
