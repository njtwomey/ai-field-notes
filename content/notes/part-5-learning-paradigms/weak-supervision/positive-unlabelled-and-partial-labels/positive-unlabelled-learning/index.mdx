<Definition>
In **positive-unlabelled (PU) learning** each training example has an input $\xvec$, a hidden class
$y \in \set{0, 1}$ and an observed indicator $s \in \set{0, 1}$ that it is labelled. Only positives can be labelled:
$\pr(s = 1 \mid \xvec, y = 0) = 0$. The unlabelled set mixes positives and negatives. The task is to learn
$f(\xvec) = \pr(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ from examples of $(\xvec, s)$ alone <Cite id="elkan2008" />.
</Definition>

## Where the setting arises

Many data sets record only one class. A database of proteins with a known function lists positives; the rest of the
proteome is unlabelled, not negative. A user's clicks are positives; unclicked items are unlabelled. A disease
registry records diagnosed cases; undiagnosed people are unlabelled. Training an ordinary classifier with unlabelled
as negative treats every hidden positive as a label error, and the classifier underestimates $\pr(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$
everywhere <Cite id="bekker2020" />.

## The SCAR assumption and sampling scenarios

**SCAR.** The labelled positives are **selected completely at random** from all positives: the probability that a
positive is labelled does not depend on $\xvec$,

$$
\pr(s = 1 \mid \xvec, y = 1) = \pr(s = 1 \mid y = 1) = c.
$$

The constant $c$ is the **label frequency**. Equivalently, $s$ and $\xvec$ are conditionally independent given $y$
<Cite id="elkan2008" />. The weaker **SAR** assumption lets the labelling probability depend on $\xvec$ through a
**propensity score** $e(\xvec) = \pr(s = 1 \mid \xvec, y = 1)$ <Cite id="bekker2020" />.

**Two sampling scenarios.** In the **single-training-set** scenario, one sample is drawn from $p(\xvec, y, s)$ and the
labelled examples are a subset of it. In the **case-control** scenario, the positives are one sample from
$p(\xvec \mid y = 1)$ and the unlabelled set is an independent sample from the marginal $p(\xvec)$
<Cite id="bekker2020" />. Elkan and Noto work in the first; the risk estimators of
<NoteLink to="non-negative-positive-unlabelled-learning" /> work in the second.

## The Elkan–Noto correction

Train any probabilistic classifier to separate labelled from unlabelled examples. It estimates the **non-traditional
classifier** $g(\xvec) = \pr(s = 1 \mid \xvec)$. Under SCAR, because $s = 1$ implies $y = 1$,

$$
g(\xvec) = \pr(s = 1, y = 1 \mid \xvec) = \pr(y = 1 \mid \xvec)\,\pr(s = 1 \mid y = 1, \xvec) = c\,f(\xvec).
$$

So $f(\xvec) = g(\xvec)/c$ <Cite id="elkan2008" />. Three consequences follow.

1. **Ranking needs no correction.** $f$ is an increasing function of $g$, so $g$ ranks examples exactly as $f$ does.
   The <Gloss name="auroc">area under the ROC curve</Gloss> is unaffected.
2. **Thresholds do.** $g \le c$ everywhere. With $c < 1/2$, thresholding $g$ at $1/2$ labels every example negative.
   The Bayes decision $f(\xvec) > 1/2$ is $g(\xvec) > c/2$.
3. **The prior follows.** Summing over a sample of $m$ examples, $n$ of them labelled, gives
   $\pr(y = 1) = \pr(s = 1)/c \approx n/(mc)$.

**Estimating $c$.** On a validation set $V$ with labelled subset $P$, Elkan and Noto propose

$$
e_1 = \frac{1}{\abs{P}}\sum_{\xvec \in P} g(\xvec).
$$

For a labelled $\xvec$, $g(\xvec) = c\,f(\xvec)$. If positives and negatives do not overlap, $f(\xvec) = 1$ on the
support of the positives, so every term equals $c$ and $e_1 = c$. When the classes overlap, $f(\xvec) < 1$ for some
labelled points and $e_1 = c\,\expect[f(\xvec) \mid y = 1] < c$: the estimate is biased low. Two alternatives are
$e_2 = \sum_{P} g / \sum_{V} g$ and $e_3 = \max_{V} g$, the latter justified by $g \le c$. Elkan and Noto recommend
$e_1$ for its lower variance <Cite id="elkan2008" />.

**Weighting the unlabelled examples.** The same identity gives the posterior of an unlabelled example. By Bayes'
theorem, with $\pr(s = 0 \mid \xvec, y = 1) = 1 - c$,

$$
w(\xvec) = \pr(y = 1 \mid \xvec, s = 0) = \frac{(1 - c)\,f(\xvec)}{1 - g(\xvec)} = \frac{1 - c}{c}\,\frac{g(\xvec)}{1 - g(\xvec)}.
$$

A second classifier is then trained on labelled examples as positives with weight 1, and every unlabelled example
duplicated: once as positive with weight $w(\xvec)$ and once as negative with weight $1 - w(\xvec)$
<Cite id="elkan2008" />.

**Worked example.** Let $c = 0.4$. An unlabelled example has $g(\xvec) = 0.3$. Its posterior is
$f(\xvec) = 0.3/0.4 = 0.75$, so it is probably positive, even though a classifier that treats unlabelled as negative
would score it 0.3. Its weight as an unlabelled example is $w = (0.6/0.4) \times (0.3/0.7) = 0.643$.

## Estimating the class prior

Most PU methods need the class prior $\pi = \pr(y = 1)$ or, equivalently, the label frequency. Under SCAR in the
single-training-set scenario, $\pi = \pr(s = 1)/c$. In the case-control scenario, $\pi$ is the proportion of positives
in the unlabelled distribution, $p(\xvec) = \pi\,p(\xvec \mid y = 1) + (1 - \pi)\,p(\xvec \mid y = 0)$. Estimating it is
a **<NoteLink to="estimating-noise-rates">mixture proportion estimation</NoteLink>** problem, and it is not identifiable
without an assumption such as the existence of regions where only positives occur. Bekker and Davis review estimators
built on this idea <Cite id="bekker2020" />.

## Assumptions

- **SCAR is often false.** Diagnosed patients are the more severe cases; clicked items are the more visible ones. When
  labelling depends on $\xvec$, the correction by a constant $c$ is biased. SAR methods model the propensity
  $e(\xvec)$, which requires further assumptions to be identifiable <Cite id="bekker2020" />.
- **The model for $g$ must be well specified.** $e_1$ is exact only if $g$ equals $\pr(s = 1 \mid \xvec)$. A
  miscalibrated $g$ passes its error to $f$.

## Pitfalls

- **Unlabelled as negative.** The naive approach is biased by exactly the factor $c$ in the posterior. Because it still
  ranks correctly, a ranking metric can hide the problem. Thresholding at $1/2$ labels every example negative when
  $c < 1/2$.
- **Overlapping classes bias $e_1$ low.** The corrected posterior then overshoots, and some corrected values exceed 1.
- **Relation to one-class methods.** <NoteLink to="one-class-support-vector-machine" /> and other novelty detectors use
  positives only and ignore the unlabelled data. PU learning uses the unlabelled data to learn where the negatives are.
