<Definition>
**Consistency regularisation** adds to the supervised loss a term that penalises how much a model's prediction on an
unlabelled input changes when the input or the network is perturbed. The perturbation is a label-preserving change such
as noise, a <NoteLink to="augmentation-principles">data augmentation</NoteLink> or dropout. The **Π-model** compares the
predictions from two independently perturbed forward passes of the same input; **temporal ensembling** compares one
perturbed prediction with an <Gloss name="ema">exponential moving average</Gloss> of the same input's earlier
predictions <Cite id="laine2017temporal" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A classifier trained on a handful of labels can place its boundary anywhere that separates those labels. Most such
boundaries cut through regions where unlabelled inputs are dense. A boundary that passes close to an input makes the
prediction for that input sensitive: a small change to the input moves the predicted class probability a long way.
Consistency regularisation asks the prediction at every unlabelled input to stay put under small perturbations. The
only way to satisfy that request at every input is to keep the boundary away from all of them, in the gaps between
clusters. This is the smoothness and cluster assumption of <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">semi-supervised
learning</NoteLink> written as a loss.

The unlabelled inputs carry no class information on their own. The labelled loss decides which side of a gap each
cluster belongs to; the consistency term decides where in the gap the boundary runs. The term never needs a label, so
every unlabelled input contributes.

The idea appeared as pseudo-ensembles, which ask a network and its dropout-perturbed copies to agree
<Cite id="bachman2014pseudoensembles" />, and as a transformation-stability loss over repeated random augmentations
<Cite id="sajjadi2016stochastic" />. Laine and Aila named the two-pass version the Π-model and introduced temporal
ensembling <Cite id="laine2017temporal" />. Every method of this chapter keeps the same template and changes one
part of it: where the target comes from, which perturbation is applied, or which inputs count.

## Details

### The objective

Let $\Dcal_L = \{(\xvec_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_L}$ be the labelled set, with inputs $\xvec_i \in \reals^d$ and classes
$y_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, and let $\Dcal_U = \{\xvec_u\}_{u=1}^{n_U}$ be the unlabelled inputs. A network with
parameters $\thetavec$ maps an input to a probability vector $f_{\thetavec}(\xvec) \in [0, 1]^K$ through a softmax. A
perturbation is a random map $\xvec \mapsto \tilde\xvec$ drawn afresh at every use: Gaussian noise, an
<NoteLink to="data-augmentation">augmentation</NoteLink> such as a crop or flip, or a dropout mask inside the network.
Every consistency method minimises

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec) = \frac{1}{n_L} \sum_{i=1}^{n_L} -\log f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_i)_{y_i}
+ \lambda(t)\, \frac{1}{n_U} \sum_{u=1}^{n_U} D\big(\hat\yvec_u,\, f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_u)\big),
$$

where $\hat\yvec_u \in [0, 1]^K$ is a target distribution for input $\xvec_u$, $D$ is a distance between probability
vectors, and $\lambda(t) \ge 0$ is the weight of the unsupervised term at training step $t$. The methods differ in
three choices.

| method | target $\hat\yvec_u$ | perturbation | $D$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Π-model | $f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_u')$, a second perturbed pass | noise, augmentation, dropout | squared error |
| temporal ensembling | running average of past $f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_u)$ | the same | squared error |
| <NoteLink to="mean-teacher">mean teacher</NoteLink> | $f_{\thetavec'}(\tilde\xvec_u')$ with averaged weights $\thetavec'$ | the same | squared error |
| <NoteLink to="virtual-adversarial-training">virtual adversarial training</NoteLink> | $f_{\thetavec}(\xvec_u)$, unperturbed | the most damaging small shift | KL divergence |
| <NoteLink to="unsupervised-data-augmentation">UDA</NoteLink> | sharpened $f_{\thetavec}(\xvec_u)$ | strong augmentation | KL divergence |
| <NoteLink to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink> | one-hot arg max of a weak view, if confident | strong augmentation | cross-entropy |

In the Π-model, $D(\pvec, \qvec) = \frac{1}{K}\norm{\pvec - \qvec}^2$, and the gradient flows through both passes.
Laine and Aila found the squared error slightly better than cross-entropy for this term
<Cite id="laine2017temporal" />. The squared error is bounded, so one confidently wrong target costs at most
$2/K$, while a cross-entropy against a confident wrong target grows without limit.

### What the penalty measures

For Gaussian input noise the Π-model penalty has a closed form to first order. Let $\tilde\xvec = \xvec +
\epsilonvec$ and $\tilde\xvec' = \xvec + \epsilonvec'$ with $\epsilonvec, \epsilonvec' \sim \Gauss(\zeros,
\sigma^2\Imat)$ independent, and let $\Jmat(\xvec) \in \reals^{K \times d}$ be the Jacobian of $f_{\thetavec}$ at
$\xvec$. A first-order Taylor expansion gives $f_{\thetavec}(\xvec + \epsilonvec) \approx f_{\thetavec}(\xvec) +
\Jmat\epsilonvec$, so the difference of the two passes is $\Jmat(\epsilonvec - \epsilonvec')$. The vector
$\deltavec = \epsilonvec - \epsilonvec'$ is Gaussian with covariance $2\sigma^2\Imat$. Then

$$
\expect\norm{f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec) - f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec')}^2
\approx \expect\big[\deltavec^\top \Jmat^\top \Jmat \deltavec\big]
= \trace\big(\Jmat^\top\Jmat\, \expect[\deltavec\deltavec^\top]\big)
= 2\sigma^2 \norm{\Jmat(\xvec)}_F^2 .
$$

The second step writes a scalar as its own trace and moves the expectation inside; the third uses
$\expect[\deltavec\deltavec^\top] = 2\sigma^2\Imat$. The Π-model with small noise therefore penalises the squared
Frobenius norm of the network's Jacobian at the unlabelled inputs. The Jacobian is large near a decision boundary,
where the predicted probabilities change fastest, and small far from it. Minimising its norm at every unlabelled
input pushes the boundary into regions with few unlabelled inputs, the <NoteLink
to="low-density-separation">low-density separation</NoteLink> that <NoteLink
to="entropy-minimisation">entropy minimisation</NoteLink> reaches by a different loss.

The approximation holds while $\sigma$ is small against the distance over which the prediction curves. With larger
noise the penalty compares predictions at points far apart, and it starts to average the prediction across the gap
between classes. Augmentations are not Gaussian, and the same argument then reads as a penalty on the derivative of the
prediction along the directions the augmentation moves the input.

### Temporal ensembling

The Π-model's target is a single noisy forward pass. Temporal ensembling replaces it with an average over training.
For each unlabelled input it keeps a vector $\mvec_u \in \reals^K$, initialised to zero. After each epoch, in which the
input's perturbed prediction was $\zvec_u = f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_u)$, the average is updated:

$$
\mvec_u \leftarrow \alpha \mvec_u + (1 - \alpha)\, \zvec_u, \qquad \hat\yvec_u = \frac{\mvec_u}{1 - \alpha^{t}},
$$

with momentum $\alpha \in [0, 1)$ ($\alpha = 0.6$ in the paper) and $t$ the number of updates so far. The division
corrects the start-up bias: if every $\zvec_u$ equalled a constant $\bar\zvec$, then after $t$ updates $\mvec_u =
(1 - \alpha)\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\alpha^s\bar\zvec = (1 - \alpha^t)\bar\zvec$, and the corrected target is $\bar\zvec$.
The target is a constant in the loss, so each input needs one forward pass per step instead of two. Its cost is
memory, $n_U \times K$ numbers, and staleness: the target moves once per epoch, which is slow on a large unlabelled
set. <NoteLink to="mean-teacher">Mean teacher</NoteLink> removes both by averaging weights instead of predictions
<Cite id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />.

### The weight schedule

A constant output satisfies the consistency term exactly, since it has zero Jacobian everywhere. Early in training,
when the predictions are poor, a large $\lambda$ can pull the network towards such a degenerate solution before the
labelled loss has fixed the classes. Laine and Aila ramp the weight up from zero along $\lambda(t) = \lambda_{\max}
\exp(-5(1 - t/R)^2)$ over the first $R$ steps, $R$ being 80 epochs in their runs, and report that a fast ramp-up gets
stuck in a degenerate solution <Cite id="laine2017temporal" />.

## Worked example

One input dimension, two classes, a logistic model $p(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-a(x - c)})$ for the probability of class 2,
with steepness $a = 4$ and boundary $c$. The output vector is $(1 - p, p)$. Three unlabelled inputs sit at $x = -1$,
$x = -0.2$ and $x = 1$. The noise has $\sigma = 0.1$. The question is which boundary position the consistency term
prefers, with the steepness held fixed.

**Step 1: the per-input penalty.** With two classes the output vector changes by $(-\Delta p, \Delta p)$, so
$\frac{1}{K}\norm{\cdot}^2 = (\Delta p)^2$. The first-order result gives an expected penalty of $2\sigma^2 p'(x)^2$,
with slope $p'(x) = a\,p(x)(1 - p(x))$.

**Step 2: boundary at $c = 0$.** At $x = -0.2$: $p = 1/(1 + e^{0.8}) = 0.310$, $p' = 4 \times 0.310 \times 0.690 =
0.856$, penalty $2 \times 0.01 \times 0.856^2 = 0.0146$. At $x = \pm 1$: $p = 0.018$ or $0.982$, $p' = 0.071$,
penalty $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ each. Total $0.0148$.

**Step 3: boundary at $c = 0.6$.** At $x = -0.2$: $p = 0.039$, $p' = 0.151$, penalty $4.5 \times 10^{-4}$. At
$x = 1$: $p = 0.832$, $p' = 0.559$, penalty $0.0063$. At $x = -1$: under $10^{-6}$. Total $0.0067$.

**Step 4: boundary at $c = 0.4$.** Both $x = -0.2$ and $x = 1$ are $0.6$ away: $p = 0.083$ and $0.917$, $p' =
0.305$, penalty $0.0019$ each. Total $0.0037$, the minimum over $c$. The midpoint of the widest gap between
unlabelled inputs wins.

**Step 5: the approximation.** Averaging the exact squared difference over the two Gaussian draws gives totals of
$0.0144$, $0.0071$ and $0.0043$ for $c = 0$, $0.6$ and $0.4$. The first-order values are within 15%, and the ordering
is the same.

The figure runs the same mechanism on two moons with eight labels. The labelled-only network places a boundary that
fits the eight labels and cuts across both moons; the consistency term moves it into the gap. On the default data
seed the accuracy on the 192 unlabelled points rises from 84.4% (labels only) to above 99% after 600 steps with the
Π-model at $\sigma = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 10$.

## Assumptions

- **Smoothness and clusters.** The penalty helps only if points of one class form dense regions separated by sparse
  ones. With heavily overlapping classes there is no gap to move the boundary into, and the term only flattens the
  prediction.
- **Label-preserving perturbations.** A perturbation that can change the true class teaches the network to ignore
  real evidence. Gaussian noise must be small against the gap between classes; an augmentation must keep the class
  (a horizontal flip preserves most object classes, not digits such as 6 and 9).
- **Shared classes.** The unlabelled set should hold the classes of the labelled set. Unlabelled inputs from other
  classes pull the boundary towards them, and Oliver et al. measured accuracy falling as the share of such inputs
  grew <Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />.

## Pitfalls

- **A weak baseline.** Gains are often reported against a supervised baseline that was tuned less carefully than the
  semi-supervised method. A strong supervised baseline with the same augmentation and regularisation closes much of
  the reported gap <Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />.
- **Too fast a ramp-up.** A large consistency weight at the start of training can lock the network into a constant
  or near-constant output <Cite id="laine2017temporal" />.
- **Noise of the wrong scale.** In the figure, at $\sigma = 0.4$ the jittered copies of points on one moon land on the
  other. After 600 steps the accuracy on the unlabelled points is 68.2%, 93.8% and 90.6% on data seeds 1 to 3,
  against 91.1%, 99.5% and 91.1% at $\sigma = 0.1$; on seed 1 it falls below the labelled-only network's 77.6%.
- **Validation on a large labelled set.** Tuning $\lambda$, $\sigma$ and the ramp-up on a validation set with many
  more labels than the training set is a form of supervision the method does not account for
  <Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />.

## In practice

Current semi-supervised image and text classifiers apply consistency through a confidence-thresholded pseudo-label from
a weakly augmented view, as <NoteLink to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink> and its <NoteLink
to="adaptive-confidence-thresholds">adaptive-threshold successors</NoteLink> do <Cite id="sohn2020" />; the Π-model and
temporal ensembling survive as baselines and as the simplest instance of the template. The Unified
<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-learning">Semi-supervised Learning</NoteLink> Benchmark (USB) evaluates these methods
starting from pretrained transformers rather than from random initialisation <Cite id="wang2022usb" />, and starting
from a <NoteLink
to="self-supervised-pretraining-for-semi-supervised-learning">self-supervised pretrained</NoteLink> network is a strong
alternative on its own. The same agreement between perturbed views drives
<NoteLink to="self-supervised-learning">self-supervised learning</NoteLink> without labels, where an averaged-weights
teacher supplies the target, as in <NoteLink
to="self-distillation-and-dino">self-distillation with DINO</NoteLink>.
