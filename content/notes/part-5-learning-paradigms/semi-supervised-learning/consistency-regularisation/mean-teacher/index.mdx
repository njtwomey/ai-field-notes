<Definition>
**Mean teacher** is a consistency-regularisation method with two networks of the same architecture. The **student** is
trained by <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on the labelled loss plus a consistency cost: the
squared difference between its prediction on a perturbed unlabelled input and the **teacher's** prediction on another
perturbation of the same input. The teacher is never trained by gradient; after every update its weights become an
<Gloss name="ema">exponential moving average</Gloss> (EMA) of the student's,
$\thetavec' \leftarrow \alpha\thetavec' + (1 - \alpha)\thetavec$ <Cite
id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="consistency-regularisation">Consistency regularisation</NoteLink> trains a network to agree with a
target on each unlabelled input. The Π-model takes the target from a second noisy pass of the same network. That
target is as noisy as the network: a single forward pass under random noise, from weights that change at every step.
Temporal ensembling averages each input's predictions over epochs, which removes some of the noise, but its target for
an input changes only once per epoch, when that input is next visited <Cite id="laine2017temporal" />. On a large
dataset an epoch is thousands of steps, so the targets reflect an old network. The averages also take memory for
every unlabelled input, and they cannot follow a stream of new data.

Mean teacher keeps the idea of averaging and moves it from predictions to weights. One set of averaged weights serves
every input, is updated at every step, and costs one extra copy of the parameters.

## Mechanism

### The two networks

Let $f_{\thetavec}(\xvec) \in [0, 1]^K$ be the student's softmax output and $f_{\thetavec'}(\xvec)$ the teacher's, with
the same architecture and $\thetavec'$ initialised to the student's starting weights. Each step draws two independent
perturbations of each unlabelled input $\xvec_u$, such as noise, augmentation or a dropout mask: $\tilde\xvec_u$ for
the student and $\tilde\xvec'_u$ for the teacher. The student minimises

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec) = \frac{1}{n_L}\sum_{i=1}^{n_L} -\log f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_i)_{y_i}
+ \lambda(t)\, \frac{1}{n_U}\sum_{u=1}^{n_U} \frac{1}{K}\norm{f_{\thetavec}(\tilde\xvec_u) - f_{\thetavec'}(\tilde\xvec'_u)}^2 ,
$$

with the teacher's output treated as a constant. After the optimiser updates $\thetavec$, the teacher follows:

$$
\thetavec'_t = \alpha\,\thetavec'_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha)\,\thetavec_t, \qquad 0 \le \alpha < 1 .
$$

### What the average holds

Unrolling the update shows the teacher as a weighted average of every student so far:

$$
\thetavec'_t = (1 - \alpha)\sum_{s=1}^{t} \alpha^{t-s}\,\thetavec_s + \alpha^t\,\thetavec_0 .
$$

The weights $(1 - \alpha)\alpha^{t-s}$ decay geometrically with age, so the average covers roughly the last $1/(1 -
\alpha)$ steps: 10 steps for $\alpha = 0.9$, 100 for $0.99$, 1000 for $0.999$. The term $\alpha^t\thetavec_0$ is the
initial weights' remaining share, which falls below 5% after $3/(1 - \alpha)$ steps.

Averaging weights is the same idea as Polyak averaging of an optimiser's iterates. It makes sense for a network
because successive students lie close together along one training trajectory, where the network is close to linear in
its weights, so the average network predicts close to the average of the predictions. Two independently trained
networks have no such relation, and averaging their weights produces nonsense; the teacher works because it starts at
the student and follows it.

### Why the teacher's targets are better

The student's weights jump around with the noise of each minibatch and each perturbation. The average cancels much of
that noise while keeping the direction of progress, so the teacher's predictions are more stable and, after enough
steps, usually more accurate than the student's. In Tarvainen and Valpola's training curves the averaged network
predicts more accurately than the student after an initial period <Cite id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />. A better
target gives the student a better consistency signal, which in turn improves the teacher. When the loop is cut, with an
averaged network kept for evaluation only and the student's own predictions as targets, learning is slower and
overfitting starts earlier.

Two limits mark the range of $\alpha$. At $\alpha = 0$ the teacher is the current student, and the method is the
Π-model with the gradient blocked through one pass. As $\alpha \to 1$ the teacher stays at its initial weights and
its targets carry no information. Tarvainen and Valpola used $\alpha = 0.99$ during the ramp-up of the consistency
weight and $0.999$ after it: the student improves fast early, so the teacher should forget quickly, and slowly later,
when a long memory helps <Cite id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />.

### The consistency cost

The paper uses the squared error between probability vectors, as the Π-model does. It also tried a family of costs
between squared error and KL divergence and found squared error best in its setting <Cite
id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />. The squared error is bounded, so an unlabelled input on which the teacher is
confidently wrong cannot dominate the gradient.

## Worked example

A one-weight model $p(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-\theta x})$ for the probability of class 2, decay $\alpha = 0.6$, teacher and
student both starting at $\theta_0 = 0$.

**Step 1: three student updates.** Gradient descent moves the student to $\theta_1 = 1.0$, $\theta_2 = 1.5$ and
$\theta_3 = 1.8$.

**Step 2: the teacher follows.** $\theta'_1 = 0.6 \times 0 + 0.4 \times 1.0 = 0.40$; $\theta'_2 = 0.6 \times 0.40 +
0.4 \times 1.5 = 0.84$; $\theta'_3 = 0.6 \times 0.84 + 0.4 \times 1.8 = 1.224$.

**Step 3: the weights in the average.** The unrolled form gives $\theta'_3 = 0.4\,\theta_3 + 0.24\,\theta_2 +
0.144\,\theta_1 + 0.216\,\theta_0 = 0.72 + 0.36 + 0.144 + 0 = 1.224$. The initial weight still has a share of
$0.6^3 = 0.216$.

**Step 4: the consistency cost at one input.** Take the unlabelled input $x = 1$, perturbed to $1.1$ for the student
and left at $1$ for the teacher. The student predicts $p = 1/(1 + e^{-1.8 \times 1.1}) = 0.879$ and the teacher
$p' = 1/(1 + e^{-1.224}) = 0.773$. With two classes the cost $\frac{1}{K}\norm{\cdot}^2$ is $(p - p')^2 =
0.106^2 = 0.0112$.

**Step 5: its gradient.** The derivative of $(p - p')^2$ with respect to the student's weight, with $p'$ held
constant, is $2(p - p')\,p(1 - p)\,\tilde x = 2 \times 0.106 \times 0.879 \times 0.121 \times 1.1 = 0.025$. Gradient
descent lowers $\theta_3$ towards the teacher, by $0.025$ times the step size and the weight $\lambda$. Where the
labelled loss pushes the student on, the teacher's lag pulls it back towards the recent consensus.

In the figure, on two moons with eight labels, the decay sets the trade between noise and lag. On the default data
seed, with $\lambda = 30$ and $\sigma = 0.1$, the student's accuracy over the second half of a 600-step run has a
standard deviation of 2.8 percentage points at $\alpha = 0$ and 0.7 at $\alpha = 0.97$, and the teacher's 0.4 at
$\alpha = 0.97$ and under 0.1 at $\alpha = 0.99$. The mean accuracy falls as $\alpha$ grows (from 90.4% at $\alpha = 0$
to 84.9% for the teacher at $\alpha = 0.99$), because 600 full-batch steps are short against a memory of 100 steps.
The small problem shows the smoothing, not the accuracy gain: the gain in the paper comes from long runs of noisy
minibatch training on images, where the student's noise is large.

## Cost

Mean teacher stores a second copy of the weights and runs a forward pass of the teacher on each unlabelled input per
step, without a backward pass. The EMA update costs one multiply-add per parameter per step. Compared with temporal
ensembling it saves the $n_U \times K$ table of averaged predictions and refreshes its targets every step instead of
every epoch.

## Trade-offs

- **The decay.** A small $\alpha$ gives a noisy teacher; a large one gives a stale teacher. The useful range spans
  about an order of magnitude, and performance degrades quickly outside it <Cite id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />.
- **Noise is still needed.** With neither input augmentation nor dropout the two predictions agree trivially and the
  consistency cost carries no signal; the paper's ablation found that either input augmentation or dropout was
  necessary for passable results <Cite id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />.
- **Teacher and student coupled.** The teacher is an average of the student, so a student that drifts into a wrong
  solution drags the teacher after it. The two networks are not independent, unlike the two classifiers of
  <NoteLink to="co-training">co-training</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **<NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">Batch normalisation</NoteLink> statistics.** A teacher with batch normalisation
  needs running statistics that belong to its own weights. Implementations either average the student's running
  statistics along with the weights or recompute them from the teacher's own forward passes.
- **Averaging when it hurts.** <NoteLink to="mixmatch">MixMatch</NoteLink>'s ablation found that guessing labels with an
  EMA of the weights slightly raised its error <Cite id="berthelot2019mixmatch" />. The teacher helps when the targets
  are the bottleneck, not in every pipeline.
- **Evaluate the right network.** The teacher is usually the better classifier; reporting the student understates the
  method, and the two differ most early in training.

## In practice

The averaged-weights teacher outlived the method that introduced it. <NoteLink to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink>
evaluates an exponential moving average of its weights <Cite id="sohn2020" />. Self-supervised methods such as
<Gloss name="byol" /> take their targets from an EMA teacher with no labels at all <Cite id="grill2020" />, and
<NoteLink to="self-distillation-and-dino">DINO</NoteLink> does the same with a centred and sharpened teacher.
