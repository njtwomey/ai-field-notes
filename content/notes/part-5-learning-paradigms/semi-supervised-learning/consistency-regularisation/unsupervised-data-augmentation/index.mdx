<Definition>
**Unsupervised data augmentation** (<Gloss name="uda" form="short" />) is consistency regularisation in which the
perturbation is a strong, label-preserving <NoteLink to="augmentation-principles">data augmentation</NoteLink> of the
kind used in <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink>: RandAugment for images, back-translation
for text. The model's sharpened prediction on the original unlabelled example is the target for its prediction on the
augmented copy. **Training-signal annealing** limits overfitting to the few labelled examples by removing from the
supervised loss those the model already predicts with high confidence <Cite id="xie2020uda" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Early consistency methods perturbed inputs with Gaussian noise, dropout or small shifts (<NoteLink
to="consistency-regularisation">Π-model</NoteLink>) or with a computed adversarial direction (<NoteLink
to="virtual-adversarial-training">VAT</NoteLink>). These perturbations are small and generic. They test the model's
smoothness in a tiny neighbourhood and teach no invariance that the data's domain is known to have. Supervised
learning had meanwhile found augmentations that change an input a great deal and keep its class, such as
AutoAugment's and RandAugment's image policies and back-translation for sentences. Xie et al. asked whether the
quality of the perturbation, not its smallness, is what makes consistency training work, and answered by plugging the
strongest supervised augmentations into the consistency loss <Cite id="xie2020uda" />.

A second problem comes from the label counts. With 20 labelled reviews and tens of thousands of unlabelled ones, a
large network fits the labelled examples in a few hundred steps, long before the unlabelled term has shaped the
boundary. UDA's training-signal annealing holds the labelled loss back until the unlabelled data have had their
effect.

## Mechanism

### The consistency term

Let $p_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid \xvec) \in [0, 1]^K$ be the model's class probabilities from logits $\zvec(\xvec)$, and
let $\hat\xvec \sim q(\hat\xvec \mid \xvec)$ be a strong augmentation. The target is computed from the original,
unaugmented example with a fixed copy $\tilde\thetavec$ of the parameters (no gradient), and it is **sharpened** with a
softmax temperature $\tau$:

$$
p^{\text{sharp}}_{\tilde\thetavec}(k \mid \xvec) = \frac{\exp(z_k(\xvec)/\tau)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j(\xvec)/\tau)} .
$$

With $\tau = 1$ the target is the plain prediction; as $\tau \to 0$ it tends to the one-hot vector of the arg max.
**Confidence masking** keeps the term only for examples whose unsharpened confidence exceeds a threshold $\beta$. On a
batch $\Bcal$ of unlabelled examples the unsupervised loss is

$$
\Lcal_U = \frac{1}{|\Bcal|} \sum_{\xvec \in \Bcal} \indicator\Big[\max_k p_{\tilde\thetavec}(k \mid \xvec) > \beta\Big]\,
\mathrm{CE}\big(p^{\text{sharp}}_{\tilde\thetavec}(\cdot \mid \xvec),\, p_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid \hat\xvec)\big),
$$

with $\mathrm{CE}(\pvec, \qvec) = -\sum_k p_k \log q_k$. The cross-entropy and the KL divergence from the target differ
by the target's entropy, a constant in $\thetavec$, so they have the same gradient. For <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> the
paper uses $\tau
= 0.4$ and $\beta = 0.8$ <Cite id="xie2020uda" />. The total loss adds $\lambda\Lcal_U$ to the supervised cross-entropy,
with $\lambda = 1$ in most experiments.

### The augmentations

- **Images: RandAugment.** Each strongly augmented image applies a fixed number of operations drawn uniformly from a
  list of about 14 (rotation, shear, translation, colour, contrast, brightness, sharpness, posterise, solarise,
  equalise and others), with a magnitude drawn at random <Cite id="cubuk2020randaugment" />. It needs no search on
  labelled data, unlike AutoAugment.
- **Text: back-translation** (see <NoteLink to="text-augmentation">text augmentation</NoteLink>). A sentence is
  translated into another language and back by two translation models, with sampling at a softmax temperature that
  trades diversity against faithfulness (0.7 to 0.9 worked best). The paraphrase keeps the meaning and changes the words
  and their order.
- **Text: <Gloss name="tf-idf" /> word replacement.** For topic classification, uninformative words with low TF-IDF
  scores are replaced and the keywords are kept.

The augmentation's job is to be diverse, so that the consistency term tests many directions, and valid, so that the
class does not change. Gaussian noise is valid but not diverse; an unconstrained edit is diverse but not valid. The
paper found a strong correlation between how well an augmentation works in supervised learning and how well it
works in consistency training <Cite id="xie2020uda" />.

### Training-signal annealing

Let $T$ be the total number of training steps and $K$ the number of classes. At step $t$ the threshold is

$$
\eta_t = \alpha_t\Big(1 - \frac{1}{K}\Big) + \frac{1}{K},
$$

rising from $1/K$ to 1 as the schedule $\alpha_t$ rises from 0 to 1. A labelled example whose true-class probability
exceeds $\eta_t$ is removed from the supervised loss at that step. Three schedules release the labelled signal at
different times:

$$
\alpha_t^{\log} = 1 - e^{-5t/T}, \qquad \alpha_t^{\text{linear}} = \frac{t}{T}, \qquad
\alpha_t^{\exp} = e^{5(t/T - 1)} .
$$

The log schedule releases most of the signal early, the exp schedule most of it at the end. Xie et al. recommend the
exp schedule when the model overfits easily (few labels, an easy task) and the log schedule when it does not
<Cite id="xie2020uda" />. The annealing caps how confident the model can become on a labelled example, so the
unlabelled term keeps a say in where the boundary goes.

## Worked example

Two classes, $\tau = 0.4$, $\beta = 0.8$, total $T = 400$ steps.

**Step 1: confidence masking.** An unlabelled example with logits $(2.0, 1.0)$ has $p = (0.731, 0.269)$. Its
confidence $0.731$ is below $\beta = 0.8$, so it contributes nothing this step. A second example with logits $(3.0,
0.5)$ has $p = (0.924, 0.076)$ and passes.

**Step 2: sharpening.** Dividing the second example's logits by $\tau = 0.4$ gives $(7.5, 1.25)$ and the target
$(0.998, 0.002)$.

**Step 3: the loss on the augmented copy.** The model predicts $\qvec = (0.8, 0.2)$ on the strongly augmented copy.
The cross-entropy against the sharpened target is $-(0.998 \ln 0.8 + 0.002 \ln 0.2) = 0.226$. Against the
unsharpened target it would be $0.328$.

**Step 4: the gradient.** With respect to the copy's first logit, the gradient is $q_1 - p^{\text{target}}_1$: $0.8 -
0.998 = -0.198$ with sharpening, $0.8 - 0.924 = -0.124$ without. Sharpening raises the pull towards the confident
class by 60%, which is the low-entropy push of <NoteLink to="entropy-minimisation">entropy minimisation</NoteLink>.

**Step 5: training-signal annealing at $t = 100$.** With $K = 2$, $\eta_{100} = 0.857$ under the log schedule, $0.625$
under the linear one and $0.512$ under the exp one. A labelled example with true-class probability $0.9$ is removed
under all three. One with probability $0.6$ stays under the log and linear schedules and is removed under the exp
schedule. By $t = 200$ the thresholds are $0.959$, $0.750$ and $0.541$.

In the figure, on two moons with eight labels and the default seed, the run with a strong jitter of sd 0.3 reaches
99.0% accuracy on the 192 unlabelled points after 400 steps, and the same run with a weak jitter of sd 0.05 stays at
86.5%, close to the 84.4% of the labelled points alone. On data seeds 1 and 3 the strong copy also wins (89.1% against
84.4%, and 87.5% against 82.8%). Annealing does not help on this problem: with 8 labels and 400 steps the network
does not overfit the labels, and withholding them only slows learning (98.4% with the log schedule, 87.0% with the
linear and 86.5% with the exp schedule on the default seed).

## Cost

Each step adds, per unlabelled example, a forward pass on the original without gradients and a forward and backward
pass on the augmented copy. The paper's image runs use an unlabelled batch several times larger than the labelled
batch, so the unlabelled term dominates the cost. The augmentations themselves are cheap for images; back-translation
needs two translation models and is run once, offline, to produce a fixed set of paraphrases per sentence.

## Trade-offs

- **Soft against hard targets.** UDA keeps a soft, sharpened target and a separate confidence mask; <NoteLink
  to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink> replaces both by a hard pseudo-label with a threshold and adds a weak view for the
  target <Cite id="sohn2020" />, removing $\tau$ as a hyperparameter.
- **Augmentation quality.** The method is only as good as its augmentation. Where no strong, valid augmentation is
  known, as for most tabular data, UDA has nothing to add over smaller perturbations.
- **Annealing schedule.** The right schedule depends on how fast the model overfits the labels, which is hard to know
  in advance with a small validation set.

## Pitfalls

- **Augmentations that change the class.** A strong augmentation that occasionally flips the class (a crop that
  removes the object, a back-translation that negates a sentence) trains the model to agree with the wrong label.
- **Out-of-domain unlabelled data.** Unlabelled examples from a different distribution add confident but meaningless
  targets. The paper filters unlabelled data with a model trained on the labelled data and keeps the examples it
  classifies most confidently in each class.
- **Annealing in the wrong regime.** Training-signal annealing slows learning where the model does not overfit, as the
  figure shows; it is a remedy for a large model on very few labels.

## Results that carried the idea

UDA reported an error rate of 5.43% on CIFAR-10 with 250 labels, and 4.20% on IMDb sentiment with 20 labelled reviews,
better than the best model of the time trained on all 25,000 labelled reviews <Cite id="xie2020uda" />. The text result
made the point that consistency with good augmentation, plus a pretrained <Gloss name="language-model">language
model</Gloss>, can replace most of the labels.
