<Definition>
**FixMatch** is a semi-supervised training rule for classifiers with two augmentations of each unlabelled example: a
weak one (a flip and a small shift for images) and a strong one (a sequence of heavy distortions). The model's
prediction on the weak view gives a hard pseudo-label, kept only if its confidence reaches a threshold $\tau$ (0.95 in
the paper). The model is then trained with cross-entropy to predict that pseudo-label on the strong view, alongside the
usual cross-entropy on the labelled examples <Cite id="sohn2020" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The methods before FixMatch built their unlabelled targets from many parts. <NoteLink to="mixmatch">MixMatch</NoteLink>
averaged predictions over several augmentations, sharpened them with a temperature and mixed examples with mixup;
ReMixMatch added distribution alignment and augmentation anchoring <Cite id="berthelot2020remixmatch" />; <NoteLink
to="unsupervised-data-augmentation">UDA</NoteLink> sharpened soft targets, masked uncertain ones and annealed the
labelled loss <Cite id="xie2020uda" />. Each part brought hyperparameters, and tuning many hyperparameters is
unreliable when a validation set must be small.

Each of the two older ideas on its own has a failure. <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">Pseudo-labelling</NoteLink>
computes the label and the loss on the same input, so a wrong confident label is confirmed at every step, and the
network learns nothing new from a point it already predicts confidently. <NoteLink
to="consistency-regularisation">Consistency regularisation</NoteLink> with soft targets lets an uncertain prediction
pull the other view towards uncertainty. FixMatch keeps one part of each: the confidence threshold and hard label of
pseudo-labelling, and the two views of consistency regularisation, with the target always taken from the easier view.

## Mechanism

### The loss

A minibatch holds $B$ labelled pairs $(\xvec_b, y_b)$ and $\mu B$ unlabelled inputs $\uvec_u$, with $\mu$ the ratio
of unlabelled to labelled batch size ($\mu = 7$ in the paper). Write $p_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid \xvec) \in [0, 1]^K$ for
the network's class probabilities over $K$ classes, $\alpha(\cdot)$ for a random weak augmentation and $\Acal(\cdot)$
for a random strong one. For each unlabelled input FixMatch computes the weak-view prediction and its arg max,

$$
\qvec_u = p_{\thetavec}(\cdot \mid \alpha(\uvec_u)), \qquad \hat y_u = \argmax_k q_{u,k},
$$

and treats both as constants: no gradient flows through them. The loss is

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} -\log p_{\thetavec}\big(y_b \mid \alpha(\xvec_b)\big)
+ \lambda_u \frac{1}{\mu B} \sum_{u=1}^{\mu B} \indicator\Big[\max_k q_{u,k} \ge \tau\Big]
\big({-\log p_{\thetavec}(\hat y_u \mid \Acal(\uvec_u))}\big),
$$

with unlabelled weight $\lambda_u = 1$. The unlabelled sum is divided by the whole batch $\mu B$, not by the number of
points above the threshold. A point below the threshold therefore contributes zero, and the unlabelled term grows as
more points become confident. The paper adds no ramp-up of $\lambda_u$: early in training few points reach $\tau$, so
the threshold supplies the schedule <Cite id="sohn2020" />.

### Why the two views matter

The pseudo-label term on its own is the same as in pseudo-labelling: a hard target and a cross-entropy, which minimise
the prediction entropy and push the boundary away from the data. The change is where the target and the prediction
come from. The target uses the weak view, on which the network is most likely to be right. The loss uses the strong
view, a distorted copy that the network finds harder. A point whose weak view the network already labels confidently
still produces a large loss if its strong view is predicted differently, so the network keeps learning from it. That
loss is a consistency penalty between the two views, in the sense of <NoteLink
to="consistency-regularisation">consistency regularisation</NoteLink>, with a one-hot target.

The paper's ablation makes the roles concrete <Cite id="sohn2020" />. Replacing the weak view by a strong one for
label guessing made training diverge early. Removing augmentation from the label-guessing view made the network
overfit its guessed labels. Replacing the strong view by a weak one in the loss reached 45% accuracy and then
collapsed to 12%. The figure reproduces the last case on two moons: with the strong jitter set equal to the weak one,
the boundary barely moves from where the eight labels put it.

### Sharpening as a limit

UDA and MixMatch replace the hard label by a sharpened distribution, $q_k^{1/T} / \sum_j q_j^{1/T}$ with temperature
$T$. As $T \to 0$ the sharpened distribution tends to the one-hot vector of the arg max, so FixMatch's target is the
zero-temperature limit. Sohn et al. found that adding a temperature to the thresholded loss gave no clear gain and
only added a hyperparameter <Cite id="sohn2020" />.

### Augmentations

For images the weak augmentation flips horizontally with probability ½ (except on digit data) and translates by up
to 12.5% of the image size. The strong augmentation is RandAugment <Cite id="cubuk2020randaugment" /> or CTAugment,
each followed by Cutout, which blanks a random square. RandAugment applies a fixed number of operations drawn from a
list (rotation, shear, colour, contrast, posterise and others) at a random magnitude, so it needs no learned policy.
In the paper's ablation, removing either Cutout or CTAugment raised the error rate <Cite id="sohn2020" />. The
figure uses Gaussian jitter in two dimensions: a small standard deviation for the weak view and a large one for the
strong view.

## Worked example

Two labelled and four unlabelled examples ($B = 2$, $\mu = 2$), three classes, $\tau = 0.95$ and $\lambda_u = 1$.

**Step 1: the labelled term.** The network gives the true class probabilities $0.8$ and $0.6$ on the weakly
augmented labelled examples. The labelled term is $\tfrac12(-\log 0.8 - \log 0.6) = \tfrac12(0.223 + 0.511) = 0.367$.

**Step 2: weak views and the mask.** The weak-view predictions are

| point | $\qvec_u$ | $\max_k q_{u,k}$ | kept at $\tau = 0.95$ | $\hat y_u$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $(0.97, 0.02, 0.01)$ | 0.97 | yes | 1 |
| 2 | $(0.10, 0.85, 0.05)$ | 0.85 | no | 2 |
| 3 | $(0.01, 0.03, 0.96)$ | 0.96 | yes | 3 |
| 4 | $(0.50, 0.45, 0.05)$ | 0.50 | no | 1 |

**Step 3: strong views.** On the strong views the network gives $(0.70, 0.20, 0.10)$ for point 1 and $(0.15, 0.25,
0.60)$ for point 3. Their cross-entropies against the pseudo-labels are $-\log 0.70 = 0.357$ and $-\log 0.60 =
0.511$. Points 2 and 4 contribute 0.

**Step 4: the unlabelled term.** The sum is divided by $\mu B = 4$, not by the 2 kept points:
$(0.357 + 0.511)/4 = 0.217$. The total loss is $0.367 + 0.217 = 0.584$.

**Step 5: the gradient.** For point 1 the gradient of its cross-entropy with respect to the strong view's logits is
$\pvec - \evec_1 = (-0.30, 0.20, 0.10)$, scaled by $1/4$. The step raises the logit of class 1 on the strong
view, so the network learns to predict on the distorted copy what it already predicts on the clean one.

**Step 6: a lower threshold.** At $\tau = 0.8$ point 2 also passes, with pseudo-label 2. Its strong view gives
$(0.30, 0.40, 0.30)$ and cross-entropy $-\log 0.40 = 0.916$. The unlabelled term becomes $(0.357 + 0.916 +
0.511)/4 = 0.446$. The less confident point brings the largest loss and the largest gradient, which is right if its
label is right and harmful if not.

In the figure, on two moons with eight labels and the default seed, FixMatch at $\tau = 0.95$ raises the accuracy on the
192 unlabelled points from 84.4% (labels only) to 94.8% after 400 steps. On each of the first three data seeds, raising
$\tau$ from 0.6 to 0.99 lowers the share of points above the threshold at step 400 from 99% to between 78% and 83%. On
seeds 1 and 3 it raises the accuracy of their pseudo-labels from 90% and 91% to 94% and 99%; on seed 2 they are nearly
all right at every $\tau$. This is the quality against quantity trade-off the paper reports.

## Cost

Each step runs a forward pass on the weak views of $\mu B$ unlabelled inputs without gradients, and a forward and
backward pass on their strong views and on the $B$ labelled inputs. With $\mu = 7$ a step costs roughly eight times a
supervised step on the labelled batch alone. The paper trains for $2^{20}$ steps with batch size $B = 64$,
<NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">SGD with momentum</NoteLink> 0.9, a cosine learning-rate decay from 0.03, and
reports the <Gloss name="ema">exponential moving average</Gloss> of the weights <Cite id="sohn2020" />. With these
settings FixMatch reached 94.93% accuracy on <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> with 250 labels and 88.61% with 40 (four per
class).

## Trade-offs

- **One threshold for every class and every step.** A fixed $\tau$ admits few pseudo-labels early in training and
  favours classes the network learns first. <NoteLink to="adaptive-confidence-thresholds">Adaptive
  thresholds</NoteLink> (FlexMatch, FreeMatch, SoftMatch) lower it per class or set it from the model's confidence
  <Cite id="zhang2021flexmatch" />.
- **Hard against soft targets.** A hard label discards the network's uncertainty between the runner-up classes; soft
  targets keep it but let uncertain predictions drag each other towards uniform. The threshold makes the hard label
  safe only to the extent that confidence tracks accuracy, which depends on <NoteLink
  to="classifier-calibration">calibration</NoteLink>.
- **A domain-specific strong augmentation.** The gain comes from a strong augmentation that changes the input a lot and
  keeps its class. Images and text (by back-translation) have one. Tabular data, <NoteLink to="time-series">time
  series</NoteLink> and many scientific inputs do not, and FixMatch there falls back towards plain pseudo-labelling.

## Pitfalls

- **Confirmation bias survives.** A wrong pseudo-label above the threshold is still trained on, now on two views at
  once. Overconfident networks admit more of them.
- **Class mismatch.** Unlabelled examples of classes absent from the labelled set get confident pseudo-labels from the
  known classes and pull the boundary towards them.
- **Barely supervised runs vary.** With one label per class the paper reports test accuracy between 48.58% and 85.32%
  over four runs on different label sets, with a median of 64.28% <Cite id="sohn2020" />. Which examples are labelled
  matters as much as how many.
- **Baselines.** Current benchmarks such as USB start from pretrained networks <Cite id="wang2022usb" />, and a strong
  supervised fine-tune of a pretrained network is the baseline FixMatch must beat; see <NoteLink
  to="evaluating-semi-supervised-learning">evaluating semi-supervised learning</NoteLink>.
