<Definition>
**Open-set semi-supervised learning** is semi-supervised classification in which the unlabelled set contains points of
classes that no labelled point belongs to. The task is still to classify the $K$ known classes. A method must decide,
for each unlabelled point, whether it is an **inlier** from a known class, to be used as ordinary unlabelled data, or an
**outlier** from an unseen class, to be left out or down-weighted. The setting is also called semi-supervised learning
under class distribution mismatch.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The standard benchmark makes unlabelled data by hiding labels, so every unlabelled point belongs to a known class.
Unlabelled data collected for real do not: a pile of web images used to help a ten-class animal classifier contains
cars, food and text. A semi-supervised method treats each unlabelled point as a member of some known class.
<NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">Pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> assigns it the most probable known class; a generative model
assigns it a responsibility for each known class; <NoteLink to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink> trains the network to
predict a known class for it under strong augmentation. An outlier therefore becomes a wrongly labelled training point,
and a whole unseen class becomes a whole cluster of wrong labels pulling on the decision boundary.

The remedy is to recognise the outliers first. That is the problem of
<NoteLink to="out-of-distribution-detection">out-of-distribution detection</NoteLink> (<Gloss name="ood" /> detection),
solved here with a twist: the detector itself must be trained from the few labels, and it decides what the
semi-supervised learner sees.

## Details

### How much harm

Oliver, Odena, Raffel, Cubuk and Goodfellow measured the harm on <Gloss name="cifar-10" />. Six animal classes, with 400
labels each, formed the labelled task. The unlabelled data came from four classes, and the experiment varied how many of
those four were among the six. With all four inside, the methods gained; as the overlap fell, they lost accuracy, and
with no overlap several of them did worse than the supervised baseline <Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />. Guo and
colleagues report that existing deep semi-supervised methods fell below <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised
learning</NoteLink> in about 40% of their unseen-class settings <Cite id="guo2020safe" />.

The harm depends on where the outliers lie. An unseen class far from every known class attracts whatever the model
offers it: a known class's region in pseudo-labelling, a component in a mixture model. That pull moves the boundary
between the known classes. An unseen class that looks like a known class is assigned to it confidently and blurs it. An
unseen class between two known classes fills the low-density gap that the boundary should cross, which removes the
signal that <NoteLink to="low-density-separation">low-density separation</NoteLink> relies on.

### Scoring the unlabelled points

Every open-set method computes an inlier score $s(\xvec)$ for each unlabelled point and uses it to filter or weight the
point. The scores come from out-of-distribution detection.

- **Maximum softmax probability.** The classifier's confidence $\max_k p_\theta(k \mid \xvec)$ is a baseline OOD score
  <Cite id="hendrycks2017baseline" />. The confidence threshold of pseudo-labelling and FixMatch is already this filter.
  It fails for outliers on which the network is confidently wrong, which neural networks often are far from the
  training data.
- **Class-conditional distances.** Fit a Gaussian to each known class in some feature space and score a point by its
  distance to the nearest class mean, standardised by the class spread. Far from every class means unseen. The figure
  uses this score on the raw inputs.
- **<Gloss name="ovr">One-versus-all</Gloss> heads.** OpenMatch adds to FixMatch one binary classifier per known class,
  each deciding "class $k$ or not class $k$". A point that every head rejects is an outlier. An extra
  <NoteLink to="consistency-regularisation">consistency loss</NoteLink> makes the heads' outputs stable under
  augmentation, which sharpens the detection, and points flagged as outliers are left out of the FixMatch loss
  <Cite id="saito2021openmatch" />.
- **Curriculum filtering.** Yu, Ikami, Irie and Aizawa train an OOD score jointly with the classifier and alternate:
  update the network, re-estimate each unlabelled point's OOD score, select the points with low scores as inliers for
  the next round of semi-supervised training <Cite id="yu2020mtc" />.
- **Uncertainty-aware distillation.** UASD of Chen, Zhu, Li and Gong builds soft targets for the unlabelled points
  from the network's own predictions accumulated during training, and drops points whose targets are not confident,
  which limits how far errors on outliers propagate <Cite id="chen2020uasd" />.
- **Learned weights.** DS3L learns a weight for every unlabelled point by bi-level optimisation, so that training on
  the weighted points lowers the labelled loss; points that hurt get weight near zero <Cite id="guo2020safe" />.

### What a filter costs

A filter makes two kinds of error. It keeps some outliers, and those do the harm the filter was meant to stop. It also
removes some inliers, and those are unlabelled data the method no longer uses. A filter set to remove a share $\alpha$
of a known class's points, as a test at level $\alpha$ does, removes about $\alpha$ of the useful data when its model of
the known classes is correct, and more when the model is estimated from few labels. Both errors are largest for
**near** outliers, unseen classes close to a known class, which no score separates well. Far outliers are easy to
remove and do the most damage when kept.

## Worked example

Two known classes lie on the line. Class 0 has labels at $-1.6$ and $-0.8$; class 1 has labels at $0.8$ and $1.6$. The
filter is a Gaussian novelty test at level $\alpha = 0.01$.

1. **Class means.** $\hat\mu_0 = -1.2$ and $\hat\mu_1 = 1.2$.
2. **Pooled variance.** Each labelled point lies 0.4 from its class mean, so the sum of squared deviations is
   $4 \times 0.16 = 0.64$. Two class means used up two of the four degrees of freedom, so
   $\hat\sigma^2 = 0.64 / (4 - 2) = 0.32$, a standard deviation of 0.57.
3. **The score.** For an unlabelled point $x$, the score is
   $s(x) = \min_k (x - \hat\mu_k)^2 / \hat\sigma^2$. If $x$ came from a known Gaussian class, $s$ would follow a
   $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom, so the p-value is $P(\chi^2_1 \ge s)$. The point is removed when
   the p-value is below $\alpha$, which for one degree of freedom means $s > 6.63$, a distance of more than
   $\sqrt{6.63} \times 0.57 = 1.46$ from the nearest class mean.
4. **Four unlabelled points.**

| $x$ | nearest mean | $s(x)$ | p-value | decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.9 | 1.2 | $0.49/0.32 = 1.53$ | 0.216 | keep (a typical class 1 point) |
| 2.5 | 1.2 | $1.69/0.32 = 5.28$ | 0.022 | keep |
| 0.0 | $\pm 1.2$ | $1.44/0.32 = 4.50$ | 0.034 | keep |
| 3.5 | 1.2 | $5.29/0.32 = 16.5$ | 0.00005 | remove |

5. **Reading the table.** A point from an unseen class centred at 3.5 is removed. A point at 2.5 passes, whether it
   belongs to class 1's tail or to an unseen class next to it: the filter cannot tell. A point at 0, between the two
   classes, passes too, because it is only 1.2 from each mean. With $\alpha = 0.05$ the cut-off falls to $s > 3.84$,
   and the points at 2.5 and 0 are removed as well, together with about 5% of the genuine class points.

The figure runs this filter in front of <NoteLink to="generative-semi-supervised-learning">semi-supervised EM</NoteLink>
with one Gaussian per known class. With 4 labels per class and an unseen class at 3.5 making up 60% of the unlabelled
data, EM on all the data puts one Gaussian on the unseen class and stretches the other over both known classes: the test
error rises from 0.024 without outliers to 0.35. With the filter, which removes about 90% of the unseen points, the
error stays at 0.024. Moving the unseen class to 2.5 shows the near case: the filter removes about a third of its
points, and at high contamination the filtered model does no better than the unfiltered one, and here somewhat worse,
because the filter also cuts the tail of class 1.

## Assumptions

- **The known classes are the task.** Open-set semi-supervised learning classifies only the $K$ known classes; it does
  not discover the unseen ones. Discovering them is novel class discovery, or open-world semi-supervised learning,
  where the unseen classes must also be clustered.
- **The inlier score generalises from few labels.** Every score above is estimated from the labelled data or from a
  network trained mostly on them. With very few labels, the score's notion of a known class is itself uncertain.
- **The test set holds only known classes.** If outliers also appear at test time, the classifier needs a
  <NoteLink to="reject-option">reject option</NoteLink> as well (see <NoteLink to="selective-classification">selective
  classification</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Trusting confidence.** The confidence threshold of pseudo-labelling removes only the outliers the network is unsure
  about. Outliers far from the training data often get confident predictions and pass.
- **Filtering too hard.** A strict filter removes the hard inliers, the unusual points of known classes, which are the
  unlabelled points that carry the most information about the boundary.
- **Benchmarks with far outliers only.** Mismatch made by adding a distant dataset (digits to animals) is easy to
  filter. Results on near outliers, such as unseen animal classes among animal classes, say more about a method.
- **Ignoring class proportions.** Removing outliers changes the class proportions of the remaining unlabelled data if
  the filter removes one known class's tail more than another's.
