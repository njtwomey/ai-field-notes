<Definition>
**Semi-supervised regression** learns a real-valued function $f$ from $l$ labelled pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$, with
$y_i \in \reals$, and $u$ unlabelled inputs, usually with $u \gg l$. The unlabelled inputs enter through one of two
assumptions: that $f$ changes slowly where the inputs are dense or along the low-dimensional set they lie on
(smoothness, used by graph methods such as Laplacian regularised least squares), or that two different predictors of $y$
should agree on every input (used by co-regularisation and <NoteLink to="co-training">co-training</NoteLink>
regressors).
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most semi-supervised methods were built for classification, and their assumptions talk about class boundaries. The
<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">cluster assumption</NoteLink> says points of one cluster share a class; the
low-density assumption says the boundary avoids dense regions. A regression function has no boundary to place.
<NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">Pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> relies on a classifier's confidence, and a regressor's
prediction carries no confidence by itself.

What survives is smoothness. If the inputs lie along a curve, a surface or in clusters, a regression target that
depends on position along that structure changes little between nearby points of the structure, even when points that
are close in the plane belong to different parts of it. Unlabelled inputs reveal the structure, so they say which
points count as nearby. The second idea that survives is agreement. Two regressors that see the data differently, two
feature sets or two kernels, should give the same value at every input; their disagreement on unlabelled inputs is a
penalty that needs no target.

## Details

### Graph smoothness

Build a <NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">similarity graph</NoteLink> with weights $W_{ij} \ge 0$ over all $n = l + u$
inputs and let $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Wmat$ be its Laplacian, so that
$\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec = \tfrac12\sum_{i, j} W_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2$ measures how much the values
$\fvec = (f(\xvec_1), \dots, f(\xvec_n))$ change across edges.

The simplest graph regressor fixes $f$ at the labelled points and minimises $\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec$ over the unlabelled
values. The minimiser is the **harmonic function** of Zhu, Ghahramani and Lafferty: every unlabelled value is the
weighted average of its neighbours' values <Cite id="zhu2003" />. Setting the derivative of
$\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec$ with respect to an unlabelled $f_j$ to zero gives $2\sum_i W_{ji}(f_j - f_i) = 0$, which is

$$
f_j = \frac{\sum_i W_{ji} f_i}{\sum_i W_{ji}}.
$$

On a path graph with unit weights this is linear interpolation between the labels, measured in steps along the path.
<NoteLink to="label-propagation">Label propagation</NoteLink> computes the same function for classification, with
class indicators in place of real targets. It is transductive: it gives values at the graph's nodes only.

**Laplacian regularised least squares** (<Gloss name="laprls" />) gives a function of any input. It fits
$f = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \cdot)$ in a <Gloss name="rkhs">reproducing kernel Hilbert space</Gloss> with
kernel $k$ by minimising

$$
\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}(y_i - f(\xvec_i))^2 + \gamma_A\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 + \frac{\gamma_I}{n^2}\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec,
$$

whose coefficients solve one linear system, $(\Jmat\Kmat + \gamma_A l\,\Imat + \tfrac{\gamma_I l}{n^2}\Lmat\Kmat)
\alphavec = \Jmat\yvec$ <Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />. <NoteLink to="manifold-regularisation">Manifold
regularisation</NoteLink> derives it. For regression the targets $y_i$ are the real values themselves. With
$\gamma_I = 0$ it is <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge regression</NoteLink> on the labelled points.
As $\gamma_A \to 0$ with $\gamma_I$ small next to the fit, it approaches the harmonic interpolation. As $\gamma_I$ grows
large, it flattens $f$ towards a single constant on each connected component of the graph, because a constant has zero
graph penalty and the labelled squared error is then the only cost.

### Too many unlabelled points

Graph smoothness has a failure that classification hides. Nadler, Srebro and Zhou studied the limit of infinitely many
unlabelled points for the harmonic method in $d \ge 2$ dimensions <Cite id="nadler2009infinite" />. In that limit the
graph penalty becomes the integral of $\norm{\nabla f}^2$, and in two or more dimensions a function can match the
labels at isolated points while being almost constant everywhere else, with narrow spikes at the labels, at
arbitrarily small cost. The estimate converges to such a function, which predicts the same value away from the labels.
For classification, thresholding a nearly constant function can still give sensible classes; for regression the
predictions are useless. A positive ambient weight $\gamma_A$, enough labels, or a penalty with higher derivatives
(an iterated Laplacian) prevents the collapse.

### Agreement between two regressors

**Co-regularised least squares** of Brefeld, Gärtner, Scheffer and Wrobel fits two kernel regressors $f_1$ and $f_2$,
one per view of the inputs or one per kernel <Cite id="brefeld2006coreg" />. Each fits the labels with a ridge penalty,
and a third term makes them agree on the unlabelled inputs $\zvec_1, \dots, \zvec_u$:

$$
\sum_{v = 1}^{2}\brack{\sum_{i=1}^{l}(y_i - f_v(\xvec_i))^2 + \nu\norm{f_v}_{\Hcal_v}^2}
+ \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{u}\paren{f_1(\zvec_j) - f_2(\zvec_j)}^2.
$$

With $f_v$ expanded over all $n$ inputs, the minimiser solves a linear system of size $2n$, cubic in the number of
unlabelled points; Brefeld and colleagues also give a semi-parametric approximation that is linear in it. The prediction
is the average $\tfrac12(f_1 + f_2)$. With $\lambda = 0$ the two regressors are independent kernel ridge regressions.
The agreement term helps when each view's errors are different: where one regressor extrapolates badly, the other
pulls it back.

**COREG** of Zhou and Li is the co-training form <Cite id="zhou2005coreg" />. Two $k$-nearest-neighbour regressors use
different distance metrics. In each round, each regressor picks the unlabelled point whose pseudo-labelled addition
most reduces its squared error on that point's labelled neighbours, and passes the point, with its prediction as the
target, to the other regressor's training set. The error reduction plays the part of a classifier's confidence. The
final prediction averages the two regressors.

### Consistency for deep regressors

<NoteLink to="consistency-regularisation">Consistency regularisation</NoteLink> carries over to regression without
change, because its target is the teacher's prediction, not a class. <NoteLink to="mean-teacher">Mean teacher</NoteLink>
penalises the squared difference between the student's output and an <Gloss name="ema">exponential moving
average</Gloss> of its own weights' output on perturbed inputs <Cite id="tarvainen2017meanteacher" />; for a regressor
the outputs are real values. Pseudo-labelling a regressor needs a substitute for confidence, such as the spread of an
ensemble's predictions or a predictive variance.

## Worked example

Six points lie on an arc of the unit circle at angles $0°, 50°, \dots, 250°$. Their target is their position along the
arc, $y = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$. The two ends are labelled, $y_0 = 0$ and $y_5 = 5$, and the four middle points are
unlabelled.

1. **The geometry.** Neighbouring points are a chord of $2\sin 25° = 0.845$ apart. The two labelled ends are 110° apart
   on the circle, a chord of $2\sin 55° = 1.638$: in the plane, point 5 is about as close to point 0 as point 2 is
   (1.532), although it is five steps away along the arc.
2. **The graph.** Joining each point to its nearest neighbour gives the path $0 - 1 - 2 - 3 - 4 - 5$ with unit
   weights: the 1.638 between the ends is longer than every neighbour's chord, so the ends are not joined.
3. **The harmonic solution.** On the path, each unlabelled value is the mean of its two neighbours,
   $f_j = \tfrac12(f_{j-1} + f_{j+1})$, so the values lie on a line from $f_0 = 0$ to $f_5 = 5$:
   $\fvec = (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)$. The graph has recovered the position along the arc exactly.
4. **Kernel ridge regression.** With a <Gloss name="rbf-kernel">Gaussian kernel</Gloss> of lengthscale 1, $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \exp(-\norm{\xvec -
   \xvec'}^2/2)$, and $\gamma_A = 10^{-5}$, the fit through the two labels gives
   $(0.00, -0.21, 0.40, 1.44, 3.55, 5.00)$. The kernel value between the two labelled points is
   $e^{-1.638^2/2} = 0.261$, so to keep $f(\text{point } 0)$ at 0 the fit gives point 0 the coefficient $-1.40$,
   cancelling point 5's coefficient 5.37 there. That negative coefficient drags point 1 below zero. The middle values
   are far from 1, 2, 3, 4.
5. **LapRLS.** With $\gamma_A = 10^{-5}$ and $\gamma_I/n^2 = 0.001$, the fitted values are
   $(0.00, 0.98, 1.98, 2.97, 4.00, 5.00)$, close to the harmonic solution. Raising the graph weight to
   $\gamma_I/n^2 = 0.1$ gives $(0.19, 1.11, 2.04, 2.96, 3.89, 4.81)$: the values stay evenly spaced but the ends are
   pulled towards their mean, 2.5. In the limit of a very large $\gamma_I$ all six values tend to 2.5.

The figure runs the same comparison on 150 points along a spiral, where the neighbouring arms are close in the plane and
far apart along the curve. With five labels spread along the curve, data seed 1, lengthscale 0.6, $\gamma_A = 10^{-3}$
and $\gamma_I = 10^4$, kernel ridge regression has an error of 0.35 on the unlabelled points and LapRLS 0.11.

## Assumptions

- **The target is smooth along the structure the inputs lie on.** A target that jumps between neighbouring points of
  the graph is fitted badly by any graph penalty.
- **The graph follows the structure.** An edge that short-cuts between two arms of the spiral lets values leak between
  them, which is the error of kernel ridge regression that the graph was meant to avoid.
  <NoteLink to="graph-construction">Graph construction</NoteLink> decides which points count as neighbours (see
  <NoteLink to="graph-construction-for-semi-supervised-learning">graph construction for semi-supervised
  learning</NoteLink>).
- **The views are each predictive, with different errors.** Co-regularisation helps only if each regressor can predict
  the target on its own and the two make different mistakes.

## Pitfalls

- **Extrapolation.** Beyond the outermost labels along the graph, the harmonic solution is flat: it repeats the
  nearest label's value. LapRLS inherits this. Labels at the ends of the range matter more than labels in the middle.
- **Spikes in high dimensions.** With many unlabelled points and few labels in two or more dimensions, the pure graph
  penalty lets the fit collapse to a constant with spikes at the labels <Cite id="nadler2009infinite" />. Keep the
  ambient penalty and check predictions at points far from every label.
- **An intrinsic weight that is too large.** It flattens the fit towards the mean of the labels, as Step 5 shows. Choose
  $\gamma_I$ by cross-validation on the labelled points; the figure's error curve has a clear minimum.
- **Pseudo-labels without uncertainty.** <NoteLink to="self-training">Self-training</NoteLink> a regressor on its own
  predictions with no measure of their reliability reinforces its errors, as confirmation bias does in classification.
