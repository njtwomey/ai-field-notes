<Definition>
**Self-supervised pretraining for semi-supervised learning** uses the unlabelled data twice. First, an encoder is
trained on all inputs, labelled and unlabelled, with a self-supervised objective that needs no labels. Second, the
encoder with a classification head is fine-tuned on the labelled subset. Third, the fine-tuned model labels the
unlabelled inputs with its predicted class probabilities, and a student network is trained to match them. Chen,
Kornblith, Swersky, Norouzi and Hinton set out this three-step recipe as SimCLRv2 <Cite id="chen2020big" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The semi-supervised methods of the earlier chapters, such as <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">pseudo-labelling</NoteLink>
and <NoteLink to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink>, train one network from scratch on a labelled loss and an unlabelled
loss together. The unlabelled data shape the network only through the task: every unlabelled loss is defined by the
classifier's own predictions. With very few labels those predictions are poor, and the network's features are learned
from little signal.

Self-supervised learning separates the two jobs. A pretext task, such as making the representations of two augmented
views of one image agree, trains the encoder on the unlabelled data without any classifier. If the pretext task groups
inputs the way the classes do, the classes become simple regions in representation space, and a small head on top needs
few labels to find them. The unlabelled data have done the clustering before the labels arrive. The labels only name
the clusters.

The third step uses the unlabelled data again, this time for the task. The fine-tuned model is a good classifier; its
predictions on the unlabelled data are soft labels, and a student trained on them inherits the classifier's knowledge.
The student can be the same network (self-distillation, which refines it) or a smaller one (which compresses it). This
step is <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">knowledge
distillation</NoteLink> at once.

## Details

### The three steps

**Pretraining.** SimCLRv2 pretrains with the contrastive loss of SimCLR <Cite id="chen2020" />: two random augmentations
of each image pass through the encoder and a projection head, and the InfoNCE loss pulls each pair together and pushes
the other images of the batch apart (see <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive learning</NoteLink>). It
changes three things. The encoder is larger, up to a ResNet-152 three times wider than standard with selective kernels.
The projection head is deeper, three layers instead of two. And the fine-tuning step keeps part of the head
<Cite id="chen2020big" />.

**Fine-tuning.** SimCLR discarded the projection head after pretraining. SimCLRv2 keeps its first layer, adding a fully
connected layer to the encoder, and fine-tunes the whole network with a new linear classifier on the labelled examples.
Fine-tuning from the middle of the head gives better accuracy than from its input when labels are few
<Cite id="chen2020big" />.

**Distillation.** The fine-tuned network $f^{T}$ is frozen and serves as the teacher. With logits $f(\xvec)$ and a
temperature $\tau > 0$, write $P(y \mid \xvec; \tau) = \exp(f(\xvec)_y/\tau) / \sum_{y'}\exp(f(\xvec)_{y'}/\tau)$. The
student $f^{S}$ minimises the cross-entropy between the teacher's and its own tempered predictions over the
unlabelled set $\Dcal$:

$$
\mathcal{L}^{\text{distill}} = -\sum_{\xvec \in \Dcal}\sum_{y} P^{T}(y \mid \xvec; \tau)\,\log P^{S}(y \mid \xvec; \tau).
$$

No true labels appear in the loss <Cite id="chen2020big" />. This is the distillation loss of Hinton, Vinyals and Dean
<Cite id="hinton2015" /> applied to unlabelled data. The gradient of one term with respect to the student's logits is
$(\pvec^{S} - \pvec^{T})/\tau$: the student's probabilities are pulled towards the teacher's. As $\tau \to 0$ the
teacher's distribution becomes one-hot at its arg max, and the loss becomes pseudo-labelling with hard labels and no
threshold. SimCLRv2 uses $\tau = 0.1$ when the student has the teacher's architecture and $\tau = 1$ for a smaller
student <Cite id="chen2020big" />. When labels are plentiful, the loss can be mixed with the labelled cross-entropy,
$(1 - \alpha)$ times the one plus $\alpha$ times the other.

### What the recipe gains

On <Gloss name="imagenet" /> with 1% of the labels (about 13 images per class) and 10% of the labels, a ResNet-50
student distilled from a larger SimCLRv2 teacher reached 73.9% and 77.5% top-1 accuracy. A ResNet-50 trained with
supervision on all labels reached 76.6% <Cite id="chen2020big" />. Two findings carry the idea.

- **Bigger models are more label-efficient.** The gain from a larger encoder was largest at 1% of the labels. Growing
  the encoder ten times, from ResNet-50 to a ResNet-152 twice as wide, improved label efficiency ten times
  <Cite id="chen2020big" />. The fewer the labels, the more the
  pretrained capacity matters, because the labels no longer have to teach the features.
- **The unlabelled data matter at distillation too.** A ResNet-50 trained on the 1% labels alone reached 12.3%; the same
  network trained on a teacher's soft labels over the labelled and unlabelled images reached 68.9%, with the teacher at
  70.6% <Cite id="chen2020big" />. Most of the teacher's knowledge reaches the student only through the unlabelled
  inputs.

### Why it works: the representation does the clustering

Self-supervised pretraining moves the <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">cluster assumption</NoteLink> from
input space to representation space. HaoChen, Wei, Gaidon and Ma make the link exact for one loss
<Cite id="haochen2021spectral" />. Build the **augmentation graph** whose nodes are all possible augmented inputs and
whose edge weights are the probabilities that two of them come from the same natural input. Their
<NoteLink to="theory-of-contrastive-learning">spectral contrastive loss</NoteLink>, a contrastive objective on network
outputs, is minimised by a representation spanned by the graph's leading eigenvectors: a spectral embedding of the
augmentation graph. If the classes are well-connected subgraphs, with few edges between them, the eigenvectors are
nearly constant on each class, and a linear head on the representation separates the classes. A linear head in a few
dimensions can be fitted from a few labels, however complex the class boundary is in input space.

This is the same mechanism as <NoteLink to="manifold-regularisation">graph-based semi-supervised learning</NoteLink>,
with the graph built from augmentations instead of from distances. The figure uses it directly: the representation is
<NoteLink to="laplacian-eigenmaps">Laplacian eigenmaps</NoteLink> of a nearest-neighbour graph, which is the spectral
embedding a spectral contrastive loss recovers when the neighbour graph plays the augmentation graph.

### Pretraining against semi-supervised training

The recipe and training from scratch compete and combine. Zhai, Oliver, Kolesnikov and Beyer add a self-supervised loss
(predicting image rotations, or exemplar invariance) to the labelled loss in one training run, and combine it with
semi-supervised losses (S4L) <Cite id="zhai2019s4l" />. Zoph and colleagues find on <Gloss name="coco" /> detection that
the value of ImageNet pretraining falls as augmentation gets stronger and labelled data more plentiful, and turns
negative with all of COCO's labels, while <NoteLink to="self-training">self-training</NoteLink> improved accuracy by 1.3
to 3.4 <Gloss name="ap" /> at every dataset size <Cite id="zoph2020rethinking" />. Pretraining buys the most where
labels are fewest.

In current practice the pretraining step is usually someone else's: a vision encoder such as
<NoteLink to="self-distillation-and-dino">DINOv2</NoteLink> or <Gloss name="clip" />'s image tower, or a pretrained
<Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss>, is downloaded and fine-tuned (see
<NoteLink to="foundation-models">foundation models</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">transfer learning and fine-tuning</NoteLink>). The USB benchmark starts
every semi-supervised method from such a pretrained network, so its results measure what semi-supervised training adds
on top of pretraining <Cite id="wang2022usb" />. Oliver and colleagues had already found that fine-tuning from a related
labelled dataset beat every semi-supervised method trained from scratch on <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> with 4,000 labels
<Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />. A fair comparison therefore puts the semi-supervised method and the fine-tuned
baseline on the same pretrained network (see <NoteLink to="evaluating-semi-supervised-learning">evaluating
semi-supervised learning</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

**One distillation term.** A teacher gives logits $(2.0, 0.5, -1.0)$ over three classes for an unlabelled input; the
student's logits are $(1.0, 1.0, 0.0)$.

1. **Teacher probabilities at $\tau = 1$.** The exponentials are $e^{2} = 7.389$, $e^{0.5} = 1.649$ and
   $e^{-1} = 0.368$, which sum to 9.406, so $\pvec^{T} = (0.786, 0.175, 0.039)$. The teacher prefers class 1 and keeps
   some belief in class 2.
2. **Student probabilities.** $e^{1} = 2.718$ twice and $e^{0} = 1$ sum to 6.437, so
   $\pvec^{S} = (0.422, 0.422, 0.155)$.
3. **The loss.** $-\sum_y p^{T}_y \log p^{S}_y = 0.786 \times 0.862 + 0.175 \times 0.862 + 0.039 \times 1.862 = 0.901$.
4. **The gradient.** $(\pvec^{S} - \pvec^{T})/\tau = (-0.363, 0.247, 0.116)$. A gradient step raises the student's
   first logit and lowers the other two, the second more than the third, as the teacher's ranking says.
5. **A colder teacher.** At $\tau = 0.1$ the teacher's logits become $(20, 5, -10)$ and $\pvec^{T} = (1.000, 0.000,
   0.000)$ to three decimals. The target is the hard pseudo-label of class 1, and the teacher's preference for class 2
   over class 3 is gone. The temperature sets how much of the teacher's ranking of the wrong classes the student learns.

**Label efficiency.** The figure runs the recipe on two circles with 200 points: a two-dimensional spectral embedding of
all 200 inputs, a logistic head on the labelled points, and a distilled random-feature student. With data seed 1 and 2
labels per class, the head classifies all of the unlabelled points correctly, while
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on random features of the raw inputs reaches 0.668 and
self-training 0.663. The supervised model reaches 0.970 at 16 labels per class. On two blobs whose class cuts across
both blobs, the embedding separates the blobs and ignores the class, and at 16 labels per class the pretrained model
reaches 0.808 against 0.929 for supervised training.

## Assumptions

- **The pretext task respects the classes.** Augmentations define what the representation ignores. Colour jitter makes
  it ignore colour; if colour decides the class, the representation has discarded the signal. The "class cuts across
  the clusters" case in the figure is this failure in miniature.
- **The pretraining data cover the task's inputs.** A representation pretrained on natural images transfers poorly to
  very different inputs: Oliver and colleagues obtained no useful transfer from ImageNet to SVHN street numbers
  <Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />.
- **The teacher is better than the student would be alone.** Distillation copies the teacher's mistakes along with its
  knowledge, so a poor teacher limits the student.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing against a scratch baseline only.** A semi-supervised method trained from scratch that beats a scratch
  supervised baseline may still lose to fine-tuning a pretrained network on the labels alone.
- **<NoteLink to="embedding-evaluation">Linear probes</NoteLink> as the only measure.** A representation that does well
  under a linear probe can rank differently after full fine-tuning; SimCLRv2 fine-tunes the whole network from the
  middle of the projection head <Cite id="chen2020big" />.
- **Leaking test inputs into pretraining.** Pretraining on all available inputs is legitimate only if the test inputs
  are not among them, unless the deployment is itself transductive.
- **Choosing the temperature by test accuracy.** $\tau$ and the student's size are hyperparameters and, like all others,
  must be chosen on labelled validation data.
