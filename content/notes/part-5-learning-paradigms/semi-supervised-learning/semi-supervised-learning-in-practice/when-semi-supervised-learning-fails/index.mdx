<Definition>
A semi-supervised method **fails** on a task when, trained on the same labelled points, it has a higher test error with
the unlabelled data than without them. Every semi-supervised method links the input density $p(\xvec)$ to the class
probabilities $p(y \mid \xvec)$ through an assumption, and it fails when that link is false for the task. **Safe
<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-learning">semi-supervised learning</NoteLink>** designs methods whose error, by
construction or with high probability, is no worse than that of the supervised learner on the same labels.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A supervised learner uses the labelled points only. A semi-supervised learner adds a bet: that the shape of
$p(\xvec)$, seen through the unlabelled points, says where the class boundary lies. The
<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">smoothness, cluster, low-density and manifold assumptions</NoteLink> are
different forms of the bet. When the bet is right, the unlabelled points act like extra labels. When it is wrong, they
act like wrong labels, and there are many more of them than of the right ones.

The harm grows with the number of unlabelled points. A method that weighs each unlabelled point like a labelled one is
dominated by the unlabelled data once there are many more of them. Its estimate then converges to what the unlabelled
data favour under the method's assumption, not to what the labels support. More unlabelled data make the estimate more
precise and, under a wrong assumption, more precisely wrong.

## Details

### Misspecified generative models

A generative method fits a model $p_\theta(\xvec, y)$ by maximising the labelled log-likelihood plus the marginal
log-likelihood of the unlabelled points, usually by
<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation-maximisation</NoteLink> with the labelled points' class
responsibilities held fixed (<NoteLink to="generative-semi-supervised-learning">generative semi-supervised
learning</NoteLink>). With $l$ labelled pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ and $u$ unlabelled inputs $\xvec_j$, and a weight
$\lambda \in [0, 1]$ on the unlabelled term, the objective is

$$
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{l} \log p_\theta(\xvec_i, y_i) + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{u} \log \sum_{k} p_\theta(\xvec_j, k).
$$

Nigam, McCallum, Thrun and Mitchell introduced the weight $\lambda$ for <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive
Bayes</NoteLink> text classifiers, because EM with $\lambda = 1$ can lower accuracy when the model's assumptions are
violated; they chose $\lambda$ by <Gloss name="loo">leave-one-out cross-validation</Gloss> on the labelled documents
<Cite id="nigam2000em" />.

Divide $\ell$ by $l + \lambda u$. As $u/l \to \infty$ with $\lambda > 0$ fixed, the second term dominates, and its
average converges to the expected marginal log-likelihood $\expect_{p(\xvec)}\brack{\log p_\theta(\xvec)}$. The estimate
therefore converges to $\theta_U$, the parameter whose marginal $p_\theta(\xvec)$ is closest to the true $p(\xvec)$ in
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>. With labels alone it converges to
$\theta_L$, the parameter whose joint $p_\theta(\xvec, y)$ is closest to the true joint. The labels then only name the
components of $\theta_U$.

If the model family contains the true distribution, $\theta_U$ and $\theta_L$ coincide, and the unlabelled data only
lower the variance of the estimate. Castelli and Cover showed that, for a correct two-component mixture known from
unlabelled data, the excess error over the Bayes error falls exponentially in the number of labels
<Cite id="castelli1995exponential" />.
If the family is wrong, $\theta_U$ and $\theta_L$ differ, and nothing guarantees that $\theta_U$ classifies as well as
$\theta_L$. Cozman, Cohen and Cirelo analysed this case and showed that unlabelled data can then raise the
classification error even in settings where more labelled data would lower it: the unlabelled data remove variance and
add bias <Cite id="cozman2003mixture" />.

### A boundary through a dense region

Methods built on the cluster or low-density assumption, such as the
<NoteLink to="transductive-support-vector-machine">transductive support vector machine</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="entropy-minimisation">entropy minimisation</NoteLink>, move the decision boundary out of dense regions.
When the true boundary crosses a dense region, for example two classes that split one Gaussian blob along a line through
its centre, the methods push the boundary to the edge of the blob and label most of it as one class. Graph methods fail
the same way when the <NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">similarity graph</NoteLink> joins points of different classes:
<NoteLink to="label-propagation">label propagation</NoteLink> carries labels along the graph's edges, wherever they go
(see <NoteLink to="graph-construction-for-semi-supervised-learning">graph construction for semi-supervised
learning</NoteLink>).

### Unlabelled points from other classes

A benchmark builds its unlabelled set by hiding labels, so every unlabelled point belongs to a labelled class. Real
unlabelled data contain other classes. A pseudo-labelling or consistency method assigns each such point to some known
class, often confidently, and trains on it. Oliver and colleagues held six animal classes of <Gloss name="cifar-10" />
as labelled data and drew unlabelled data from four classes; as fewer of the four were among the six, accuracy fell, and
with no overlap several methods did worse than the supervised baseline <Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />.
<NoteLink to="open-set-semi-supervised-learning">Open-set semi-supervised learning</NoteLink> treats this case. A milder
form is a shift in class proportions: if the unlabelled data hold the classes in different proportions from the labelled
data, a method that matches its predictions to the labelled proportions mislabels the excess.

### Self-reinforced mistakes

<NoteLink to="self-training">Self-training</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">pseudo-labelling</NoteLink>
train on their own predictions. A wrong pseudo-label raises the probability of the wrong class at the next step, so the
mistake grows more confident and is never revisited. Arazo and colleagues named this **confirmation bias**
<Cite id="arazo2020pseudo" />. It fails worst when the labelled-only model is systematically wrong on a whole region:
the region is pseudo-labelled wrongly and with high confidence.

### Causal direction

Schölkopf and colleagues give a reason why some tasks cannot gain at all. If the input $\xvec$ causes the label $y$
and the mechanism $p(y \mid \xvec)$ is chosen independently of the input distribution $p(\xvec)$, then $p(\xvec)$
contains no information about $p(y \mid \xvec)$, and a better estimate of $p(\xvec)$ from unlabelled data cannot
improve the classifier. In the anticausal direction, $y$ causes $\xvec$, $p(\xvec) = \sum_y p(y)p(\xvec \mid y)$ is
built from the class-conditional densities, and the assumptions of semi-supervised learning can hold
<Cite id="scholkopf2012causal" />. Predicting a disease from its cause is causal; recognising a digit from its image is
anticausal, because the digit the writer intended produced the pixels.

### Safe semi-supervised learning

A safe method guarantees, by construction or with high probability, that the unlabelled data do not raise the error
above the supervised learner's.

- **Choose how much to trust the unlabelled data.** Select $\lambda$, including $\lambda = 0$, by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> on the labelled points. The guarantee is only as good as
  the validation estimate, which is noisy with few labels (see
  <NoteLink to="evaluating-semi-supervised-learning">evaluating semi-supervised learning</NoteLink>).
- **Hedge between candidate boundaries.** The safe semi-supervised <Gloss name="svm" /> (S4VM) of Li and Zhou finds
  several large-margin separators of the labelled and unlabelled data and returns the labelling of the unlabelled points
  that maximises the worst-case gain over the supervised SVM across those candidates <Cite id="li2011safe" />.
- **Constrain the solution to the labels' span.** Implicitly constrained least squares considers only classifiers that
  are least-squares solutions for some soft labelling of the unlabelled points, and picks among them the one that fits
  the labelled data best. Krijthe and Loog prove that in one dimension it is never worse than the supervised least
  squares classifier in squared loss <Cite id="krijthe2017icls" />.
- **Down-weight suspicious unlabelled points.** For unlabelled data from unseen classes, DS3L learns a weight for each
  unlabelled point by bi-level optimisation, so that the model trained on the weighted data has a low labelled loss.
  The weights can fall to zero, which recovers the supervised model <Cite id="guo2020safe" />.

The theory says when a guarantee is possible. Singh, Nowak and Zhu derived finite-sample error bounds under the cluster
assumption and showed that unlabelled data help when the clusters are close enough that the labelled sample cannot tell
them apart but the much larger unlabelled sample can <Cite id="singh2008unlabeled" />. Mey and Loog survey the results
on when semi-supervised learning can improve on <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink> at all
<Cite id="mey2019improvability" />.

## Worked example

Take two equally likely classes on the line. Class 0 has two modes, $\tfrac12\Gauss(-2.5, 0.6^2) +
\tfrac12\Gauss(2.0, 0.6^2)$; class 1 sits between them, $\Gauss(0.5, 0.6^2)$. The Bayes classifier assigns class 1
roughly to the interval between the modes and has error 0.077. The model fits one Gaussian per class, which is wrong for
class 0.

**Step 1: the labels alone, in the limit of many labels.** Maximum likelihood fits each class's mean and variance to its
own points. Class 0 gets mean $\tfrac12(-2.5) + \tfrac12(2.0) = -0.25$ and variance
$0.6^2 + \tfrac14(2.0 + 2.5)^2 = 0.36 + 5.06 = 5.42$, a broad Gaussian with standard deviation 2.33. Class 1 gets
mean 0.5 and variance 0.36. The narrow class 1 density exceeds the broad class 0 density on $(-0.49, 1.60)$, so the
rule assigns class 1 there and class 0 elsewhere. Its error is 0.104, close to the Bayes error, although the model of
class 0 is wrong.

**Step 2: unlabelled data alone, in the limit.** With many unlabelled points EM fits two Gaussians to the input density
$p(x) = \tfrac14\Gauss(-2.5, 0.36) + \tfrac14\Gauss(2.0, 0.36) + \tfrac12\Gauss(0.5, 0.36)$. The best two-Gaussian
fit, found by EM, has a narrow component at $-2.54$ (weight 0.24, standard deviation 0.57) for the left mode and a
broad one at 0.97 (weight 0.76, standard deviation 0.96) covering class 1 and class 0's right mode. The input density
has three bumps, and two Gaussians must merge two of them.

**Step 3: naming the components.** The labels decide which component is which class. Nearly all of the left
component's mass is class 0. The right component holds 0.5 of class 1 and 0.26 of class 0, so it becomes class 1. The
rule now assigns class 1 to every $x > -1.34$, which includes all of class 0's right mode. That mode carries probability
0.25, and the error is 0.257.

**Step 4: the comparison.** Adding unlabelled data moves the error from 0.104 towards 0.257. The unlabelled data did
their job, a better fit to $p(x)$, and that fit is the wrong one for classification. Down-weighting them with
$\lambda < 1$ changes the rate, not the limit: the objective still converges to Step 2 as $\lambda u$ grows. Choosing
$\lambda = 0$ by validation, as a safe method would, keeps the error at the supervised value.

## Assumptions

- **The comparison is with the same labels.** Failure is defined against the supervised learner trained on the same
  labelled points with equally tuned hyperparameters. A weak baseline can hide a failure.
- **The labels are clean.** With noisy labels, the supervised baseline is itself biased, and a semi-supervised method
  can do better or worse for reasons unrelated to its assumption.
- **The test distribution matches the labelled one.** If deployment data resemble the unlabelled pool more than the
  labelled set, the comparison changes, and the problem becomes one of <NoteLink to="domain-adaptation">domain
  adaptation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Testing on curated unlabelled data.** A method validated with unlabelled data made by hiding labels has never met
  unlabelled data from other classes or in other proportions. Test with the unlabelled data that deployment will supply.
- **Assuming more unlabelled data is safer.** Under a wrong assumption the error grows with the number of unlabelled
  points, as Step 4 shows.
- **Trusting a single run.** With few labels, the gain from unlabelled data varies a great deal across draws of the
  labelled set. One lucky draw can hide a method that usually hurts.
- **Skipping the $\lambda = 0$ option.** Any tuning of the unlabelled weight should include ignoring the unlabelled
  data, so that the supervised model can win the selection.
