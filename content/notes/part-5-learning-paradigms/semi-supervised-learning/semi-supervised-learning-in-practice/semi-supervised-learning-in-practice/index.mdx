<Definition>
Semi-supervised learning in practice is the part of the subject concerned with whether, when and how unlabelled data
improve a model on a real task, rather than with the mechanism of any one method. It covers how to measure a method's
gain fairly, the ways unlabelled data make a model worse, unlabelled data that contain classes the labels never
mention, the alternative of pretraining on the unlabelled data, and the settings beyond classification: regression and
clustering.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The earlier chapters of <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-learning">semi-supervised learning</NoteLink> describe methods:
<NoteLink to="self-training">self-training</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="consistency-regularisation">consistency
regularisation</NoteLink>, graph propagation, generative models. Each rests on an assumption that ties the input density
to the labels, and each was developed on benchmarks made by hiding most labels of a clean, fully labelled dataset. A
practitioner starts from the other end: a few hundred labels, a large and messy unlabelled collection, and often a
pretrained network. The questions are practical. Will the unlabelled data help this task? How large is the gain over the
best model the labels alone can train? What happens if the unlabelled collection contains other things? Is it better to
pretrain on it than to train a semi-supervised model?

## Main ideas

**A gain is measured against a tuned baseline.** The supervised baseline must get the same architecture, augmentation
and hyperparameter search as the semi-supervised method, and the validation set must come out of the same small label
budget. Oliver and colleagues showed that under these rules many published gains shrink, and that fine-tuning a
network pretrained on a related labelled dataset can beat every semi-supervised method trained from scratch
<Cite id="oliver2018realistic" />. <NoteLink to="evaluating-semi-supervised-learning">Evaluating semi-supervised
learning</NoteLink> sets out the protocol and why small validation sets cannot rank close methods.

**Unlabelled data can hurt.** A semi-supervised method fails when its assumption is false: a generative model of the
wrong form, a class boundary through a dense region, unlabelled points from other classes, or self-training that
confirms its own mistakes. The harm grows with the number of unlabelled points. Safe methods keep the supervised model
as a fallback. <NoteLink to="when-semi-supervised-learning-fails">When semi-supervised learning fails</NoteLink> works
through each case.

**Real unlabelled data contain unseen classes.** When the unlabelled collection holds classes outside the task, each
such point becomes a wrongly labelled training point. <NoteLink to="open-set-semi-supervised-learning">Open-set
semi-supervised learning</NoteLink> scores each unlabelled point as inlier or outlier and filters or down-weights the
outliers before training.

**Pretraining is the strongest competitor.** Self-supervised pretraining uses the unlabelled data without any
classifier, then fine-tunes on the labels and distils the result back onto the unlabelled data. With a large enough
network this recipe needs far fewer labels than training from scratch <Cite id="chen2020big" />.
<NoteLink to="self-supervised-pretraining-for-semi-supervised-learning">Self-supervised pretraining for semi-supervised
learning</NoteLink> describes the recipe and when it beats semi-supervised training.

**The ideas extend beyond classification.** Regression loses the class-boundary assumptions but keeps smoothness and
agreement between views (<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-regression">semi-supervised regression</NoteLink>). Clustering
gains from side information in the form of seeds or must-link and cannot-link pairs
(<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-clustering">semi-supervised clustering</NoteLink>).

## How the notes fit together

Start with evaluation: it defines what a gain is, and every later note measures against the same baseline. The note on
failure comes next and explains the losses that a fair evaluation reveals. Open-set learning is the most common failure
in practice and has its own note, which assumes the failure note. Self-supervised pretraining is the alternative to
compare against before adopting any semi-supervised method. The notes on regression and clustering apply the same
assumptions to other outputs and can be read in either order; the regression note builds on
<NoteLink to="manifold-regularisation">manifold regularisation</NoteLink>, and the clustering note on
<NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink>.
