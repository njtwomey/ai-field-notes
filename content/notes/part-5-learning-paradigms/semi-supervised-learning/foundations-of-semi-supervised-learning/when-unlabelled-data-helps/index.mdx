<Definition>
The **value of unlabelled data** for a learning problem is the reduction in the number of labelled examples needed to
reach a given accuracy when the learner also sees unlabelled inputs. It is measured by comparing two sample
complexities for a hypothesis class $\Hcal$: the supervised one, $m_{\mathrm{SL}}(\epsilon, \delta)$, and the
semi-supervised one, $m_{\mathrm{SSL}}(\epsilon, \delta)$, the number of labels needed when unlabelled inputs are free.
The value is large when the target is **compatible** with the input distribution under a stated assumption, and
bounded by the capacity of the class (a factor of 2 for thresholds on the line) when nothing is assumed
<Cite id="balcan2010discriminative" />
<Cite id="bendavid2008unlabeled" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC learning</NoteLink> the number of labels a learner needs
grows with the size of the hypothesis class $\Hcal$, through $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ for a finite class. Labels are spent
ruling out hypotheses. Unlabelled data can rule out hypotheses too, but only by a criterion that does not use labels:
a hypothesis that cuts through a dense cluster, or passes close to many inputs, is judged implausible before any label
is seen. If the criterion is right about the target, the labels have far fewer hypotheses to choose among, and fewer
labels are needed.

The criterion is a <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">semi-supervised assumption</NoteLink>. If it is right,
the gain can be large: in the best case, unlabelled data fix the shape of the decision regions and the labels only name
them. If it is wrong, the criterion discards the target, and no number of labels brings it back. If no criterion is
assumed, theory shows that knowing the input distribution exactly helps by at most a constant factor for basic classes
on the line.

## Details

### Compatibility and the unlabelled error

Balcan and Blum extend the PAC model with a **compatibility function** $\chi : \Hcal \times \Xcal \to [0, 1]$ that
scores how well hypothesis $h$ fits input $\xvec$ <Cite id="balcan2010discriminative" />. A margin assumption gives
$\chi(h, \xvec) = 1$ when $\xvec$ lies at least a distance $\gamma$ from the boundary of $h$ and 0 otherwise; a
cluster assumption gives $\chi(h, \xvec) = 1$ when $h$ labels the whole cluster containing $\xvec$ alike. The
**unlabelled error** of $h$ is its expected incompatibility,

$$
\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{unl}}(h) = 1 - \expect_{\xvec \sim D}[\chi(h, \xvec)],
$$

with $D$ the input distribution. It is estimated from unlabelled inputs alone. For a tolerance $\tau \in [0, 1]$, the
**compatible class** is

$$
\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\tau) = \set{h \in \Hcal : \mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{unl}}(h) \le \tau}.
$$

A distribution is helpful for $\chi$ when $\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\tau)$ is much smaller than $\Hcal$ for small $\tau$.

### The labels needed when the target is compatible

Take a finite class $\Hcal$ and assume the **doubly realisable** case: the target $c^* \in \Hcal$ has zero error and
zero unlabelled error. The learner keeps the hypotheses that have zero empirical unlabelled error on $u$ unlabelled
inputs and zero training error on $\ell$ labelled examples, and returns any one of them.

1. A hypothesis with $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{unl}}(h) > \epsilon$ looks fully compatible on $u$ independent inputs with
   probability at most $(1 - \epsilon)^u \le e^{-\epsilon u}$. By the <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink>
   over $\Hcal$, all such hypotheses are discarded with probability at least $1 - \delta/2$ once
   $\abs{\Hcal}e^{-\epsilon u} \le \delta/2$.
2. The survivors then lie in $\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\epsilon)$. A survivor with error above $\epsilon$ is consistent with
   $\ell$ labelled examples with probability at most $e^{-\epsilon\ell}$, and the union bound over
   $\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\epsilon)$ discards all of them with probability at least $1 - \delta/2$ once
   $\abs{\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\epsilon)}e^{-\epsilon \ell} \le \delta/2$.

Solving both conditions gives Balcan and Blum's Theorem 4:

$$
u \ge \frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln\frac{2}{\delta}\right), \qquad
\ell \ge \frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\ln\abs{\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\epsilon)} + \ln\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
$$

suffice for every returned hypothesis to have error at most $\epsilon$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$
<Cite id="balcan2010discriminative" />. The supervised bound for the same class is
$\ell \ge (\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$. The unlabelled sample pays the cost of the whole class; the
labelled sample pays only for the compatible part. The same paper extends the result to infinite classes, with growth
functions of $\Hcal_{D, \chi}$ in place of its size, and to targets that are only partly compatible, for which the
compatible class must be widened to tolerance $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{unl}}(c^*) + 2\epsilon$.

### Identifiable mixtures: exponentially fewer labels

Castelli and Cover studied the strongest form of the cluster assumption. The inputs come from a two-component mixture
$p(\xvec) = \eta f_1(\xvec) + (1 - \eta) f_0(\xvec)$, one component per class, and the family of mixtures is
**identifiable**: unlabelled data determine $\eta$, $f_0$ and $f_1$, up to which component is which. With unlimited
unlabelled data, only one binary question remains, and a likelihood-ratio test on $n$ labelled examples answers it. The
excess risk then falls exponentially in $n$,

$$
R(\hat h_n) - R^* \le \exp\left(n \ln\left(2\sqrt{\eta(1 - \eta)} \int \sqrt{f_0(\xvec) f_1(\xvec)}\, d\xvec\right) + o(n)\right),
$$

with $R^*$ the Bayes risk <Cite id="castelli1995exponential" /> <Cite id="mey2019improvability" />. A supervised
learner on the same problem has an excess risk that falls polynomially in $n$. Labelled examples are exponentially
valuable once the unlabelled data have done their part; Castelli and Cover later measured the relative value of the
two kinds of sample when the mixing proportion $\eta$ is unknown <Cite id="castelli1996relative" />.

### No assumption: a constant factor at most

Ben-David, Lu and Pál asked what knowing $D$ exactly is worth when nothing links $D$ to the labels
<Cite id="bendavid2008unlabeled" />. For thresholds $h_t(x) = \indicator[x \le t]$ on the real line in the realisable
case, a supervised learner needs $\ln(1/\delta)/\epsilon$ labels. Any semi-supervised learner that knows an absolutely
continuous $D$ still needs at least about $\ln(1/\delta)/(2.01\,\epsilon)$ for some target. Knowing the whole input
distribution saves at most a factor of 2. A learner of Kääriäinen achieves it by returning the threshold that splits the
interval of consistent thresholds into two halves of equal probability under $D$. The authors conjectured a
constant-factor limit for every class. Darnstädt, Simon and Szörényi proved a limit that depends on the class: for a
finite class containing both constant functions the gap is at most $O(\ln\abs{\Hcal})$, and a class of finite
<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> has a similar bound, but for classes of infinite VC
dimension the gap can be arbitrarily large <Cite id="darnstadt2013unlabeled" />. Without an assumption tying labels to
$D$, the value of unlabelled data is bounded by the capacity of the class.

### Finite samples under the cluster assumption

Singh, Nowak and Zhu make the cluster assumption quantitative in $d$ dimensions <Cite id="singh2008unlabeled" />. The
classes are unions of regions separated by a margin $\gamma$. With $m$ unlabelled inputs, the regions can be resolved
when $\gamma$ is larger than a scale of order $(\ln^2 m / m)^{1/d}$.
<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-learning">Semi-supervised learning</NoteLink> can beat every supervised learner when the
margin is resolvable from the unlabelled data but too small to be found from the labelled data alone. When the margin is
large, labelled data find it unaided; when it is smaller than the unlabelled data can resolve, neither finds it.
Unlabelled data help in a window of margins, and the window moves with $m$.

## Worked example

Take inputs on a grid of 1000 points in $[0, 1]$. The class $\Hcal$ is the unions of at most 5 intervals of grid
points, each union fixed by at most 10 endpoints chosen among 1001 positions, so
$\abs{\Hcal} \approx \binom{1001}{10}$ and

$$
\ln\abs{\Hcal} \approx \sum_{k=992}^{1001} \ln k - \ln 10! = 69.04 - 15.10 = 53.94 .
$$

Take $\epsilon = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.05$, so $1/\epsilon = 20$.

**Supervised.** In the realisable case, $\ell \ge 20(53.94 + \ln 20) = 20(53.94 + 3.00) = 1138.7$, so 1139 labels
suffice.

**Semi-supervised, compatible target.** Suppose $D$ puts probability $0.1$ on each of 10 clusters separated by empty
gaps, and the target labels every cluster alike. Use the cluster compatibility: $\chi(h, x) = 1$ when $h$ gives the
whole cluster of $x$ one label. A hypothesis that splits a cluster has unlabelled error at least $0.1 > \epsilon$, so
$\Hcal_{D, \chi}(\epsilon)$ holds only hypotheses that split no cluster. Up to differences on the empty gaps, these
are labellings of the 10 clusters, at most $2^{10} = 1024$ of them, and $\ln 1024 = 6.93$. Theorem 4 gives

$$
\ell \ge 20\left(6.93 + \ln 40\right) = 20(6.93 + 3.69) = 212.4,
\qquad
u \ge 20\left(53.94 + 3.69\right) = 1152.5 .
$$

So 213 labels and 1153 unlabelled inputs suffice, against 1139 labels without the unlabelled data: about 5.3 times
fewer labels. The bound uses the cluster structure only through the count 1024; the true requirement is lower, since
one label per cluster would name all of them.

**Uniform inputs.** If $D$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$, the inputs form no clusters, and a cluster compatibility removes no
hypothesis. Ben-David, Lu and Pál's result for thresholds says that in this case even the exact $D$ is worth at most a
halving of the labels.

**The figure.** The same contrast appears on the real line with a threshold learner. The supervised learner returns the
midpoint of the interval of thresholds with the fewest training errors. The semi-supervised learner restricts itself to
the troughs of a <NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density estimate</NoteLink> of the unlabelled inputs
(its compatible class) and picks the trough with the fewest training errors. With two Gaussian classes
$\Gauss(\mp 2, 1)$, separation $s = 4$, the Bayes risk is $\Phi(-2) = 0.0228$. Averaged over 100 draws with $u = 200$,
the supervised risk falls from $0.050$ at one label per class to $0.027$ at 64, and the semi-supervised risk is $0.024$
from one label per class on. For these two classes the mixture bound of Castelli and Cover has rate
$2\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}\int\sqrt{f_0 f_1} = e^{-s^2/8} = e^{-2}$, so the excess risk of the mixture learner falls like
$e^{-2n}$. With uniform inputs and true boundary $t^* = 0.3$, the troughs of the estimate are noise and miss $t^*$: the
supervised risk falls from $0.124$ to $0.004$, and the semi-supervised risk stays between $0.14$ and $0.17$ however many
labels it gets.

## Assumptions

- **Compatibility of the target.** All the gains above assume $\mathrm{err}_{\mathrm{unl}}(c^*)$ is zero or small. The
  assumption is about the labels, so unlabelled data cannot check it.
- **Same distribution.** Unlabelled and labelled inputs come from the same $D$; otherwise $\Hcal_{D, \chi}$ is
  computed for the wrong distribution.
- **Enough unlabelled data.** The labelled bound holds only once $u$ is large enough to estimate the unlabelled error
  of every hypothesis, which costs $\ln\abs{\Hcal}/\epsilon$ inputs. In the mixture result the unlabelled sample is
  infinite; with finite $u$, the estimated components carry an error that the labels cannot remove, and the figure
  with $u = 20$ shows the semi-supervised learner losing its advantage.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the bounds as predictions.** The bounds are worst-case sufficient conditions. A five-fold smaller bound is
  not a five-fold smaller error; experiments with a tuned supervised baseline are the test (see
  <NoteLink to="evaluating-semi-supervised-learning">evaluating semi-supervised learning</NoteLink>).
- **An incompatible target is fatal, not neutral.** The compatible class excludes the target, so the learner converges
  to the best compatible hypothesis, whose error does not shrink with more labels. This is the uniform panel of the
  figure and the subject of <NoteLink to="when-semi-supervised-learning-fails">when semi-supervised learning
  fails</NoteLink>.
- **Constant factors still matter.** A factor of 2 in labels is a factor of 2 in annotation cost. The worst-case
  results bound the gain without assumptions; they do not say the gain is worthless.
- **Capacity and the gap.** The largest gaps proved without assumptions use classes of infinite VC dimension
  <Cite id="darnstadt2013unlabeled" />. For the finite-capacity models used in practice, large gains come from
  assumptions, which is why every practical method is designed around one (<NoteLink
  to="semi-supervised-assumptions">semi-supervised assumptions</NoteLink>).
