<Definition>
**Noisy Student training** is iterated <NoteLink to="self-training">self-training</NoteLink> with a separate student
<Cite id="xie2020" />. A teacher network is trained on the labelled images. The teacher, run without noise, gives a soft
or hard pseudo-label to every unlabelled image. A student as large as the teacher or larger is trained on the labelled
and pseudo-labelled images with noise added to the student: data augmentation on its inputs, and dropout and stochastic
depth in its layers. The student then becomes the teacher, and the loop repeats.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">Pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> lets one network label data for itself, so its errors come
back as targets. <NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">Knowledge distillation</NoteLink> uses a separate student, but
trains it to match a teacher, and a student that matches its teacher exactly can never be better than it. Noisy
Student keeps distillation's teacher-student split and changes the goal: the student should exceed the teacher.

Two changes make that possible. The student is given more capacity than the teacher, so it can fit more than the
teacher knew. The student is noised while the teacher is not, so the student cannot reach a low loss by copying the
teacher's function: it must give the teacher's answer for every augmented version of an image and for every dropout
sub-network of itself. Xie and colleagues describe this as "knowledge expansion", the opposite of distillation's
compression <Cite id="xie2020" />.

## Mechanism

### The loop

Let $\set{(\xvec_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^{n}$ be the labelled images and $\set{\tilde\xvec_j}_{j=1}^{m}$ the unlabelled ones,
with $m \gg n$. Write $f(\xvec; \theta)$ for a network's class probabilities and $f^{\text{noised}}$ for the same
network with noise switched on.

1. Train a teacher $\theta_t$ to minimise $\frac1n\sum_i \ell\bigl(y_i, f^{\text{noised}}(\xvec_i; \theta_t)\bigr)$, the
   cross-entropy $\ell$ on labelled images (noise here is ordinary regularisation).
2. Pseudo-label the clean unlabelled images with the clean teacher: $\tilde y_j = f(\tilde\xvec_j; \theta_t)$, a soft
   distribution, or its one-hot arg max.
3. Train an equal-or-larger student $\theta_s$ to minimise
   $$
   \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\bigl(y_i, f^{\text{noised}}(\xvec_i; \theta_s)\bigr)
   + \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ell\bigl(\tilde y_j, f^{\text{noised}}(\tilde\xvec_j; \theta_s)\bigr).
   $$
4. Make the student the new teacher and return to step 2.

The student is trained from scratch: initialising it from the teacher was sometimes worse and still needed long
training. Each batch holds far more unlabelled images than labelled ones (14 to 28 unlabelled per labelled image on
<Gloss name="imagenet" />) <Cite id="xie2020" />.

### The noise

Xie and colleagues used two kinds <Cite id="xie2020" />.

- **Input noise:** RandAugment, which applies two random image operations of magnitude 27 to each image
  <Cite id="cubuk2020randaugment" />. The student must give a translated or recoloured image the label that the
  teacher gave the clean one. See <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>.
- **Model noise:** <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> of rate 0.5 on the final layer
  <Cite id="srivastava2014" />, and stochastic depth, which skips each residual block at random during training, with
  survival probability 0.8 for the last block and linearly higher for earlier ones <Cite id="huang2016stochastic" />.
  The clean teacher acts like the average of all its dropout sub-networks, an ensemble, while the noised student must
  match that ensemble with each single sub-network.

Removing the noise from the student's unlabelled images lowers the gain. With a 130M-image unlabelled set and an
EfficientNet-B5 teacher, the student reached 85.1% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet with noise, 84.3% with augmentation,
dropout and stochastic depth removed, and 84.0% for the supervised teacher <Cite id="xie2020" />. Noising the teacher
when it labels also hurts (84.4%): the pseudo-labels should come from the strongest, clean model.

### Filtering and balancing

The unlabelled images (300M from JFT) were labelled by the teacher, and those whose top confidence was below 0.3 were
dropped as probably out of domain. Each of the 1000 ImageNet classes then kept its 130,000 most confident images, and
classes with fewer were filled by duplicating images, so the student saw $1000 \times 130{,}000 = 130$M images, 81M of
them unique <Cite id="xie2020" />. Balancing keeps the pseudo-labelled set at the class proportions of the labelled
one; it mattered most for small models.

### Soft or hard pseudo-labels

Both work; soft labels were slightly better for out-of-domain unlabelled data <Cite id="xie2020" />. A soft label
carries the teacher's uncertainty, as in distillation <Cite id="hinton2015" />. Without student noise a soft label is a
fixed point: a student that reproduces the teacher's function attains the loss's minimum on unlabelled data and gets
no gradient from them. The worked example makes this concrete.

## Worked example

**The noiseless student copies.** One unlabelled image reaches a final layer with features $\mathbf h = (1.2, 0.8)$,
weights $(1, 1)$ and bias $-1$, so the teacher's logit is $z = 1.2 + 0.8 - 1 = 1.0$ and its soft pseudo-label is
$t = \sigma(1.0) = 0.731$. A student with the same weights and no noise predicts $0.731$ too. The cross-entropy
$-\bigl(t \log q + (1 - t)\log(1 - q)\bigr)$ at $q = t$ is the entropy of $t$, $0.582$, its minimum over $q$: the
student has nothing to learn from this image.

**The noised student cannot copy.** Train the same student with dropout of rate $0.5$ on the two features: each is
kept with probability $\tfrac12$ and, when kept, doubled. Four masks are equally likely:

| features kept | logit                  | student's $q$ | cross-entropy with $t = 0.731$ |
| ------------- | ---------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------ |
| both          | $2(1.2 + 0.8) - 1 = 3.0$ | 0.953         | 0.855                          |
| first only    | $2(1.2) - 1 = 1.4$     | 0.802         | 0.597                          |
| second only   | $2(0.8) - 1 = 0.6$     | 0.646         | 0.599                          |
| neither       | $-1$                   | 0.269         | 1.044                          |

The expected loss is $0.774$, well above the floor of $0.582$. The teacher's weights are no longer a minimiser: to
lower the loss the student must change its weights so that every masked sub-network predicts close to $0.731$, which
means spreading the evidence over features rather than relying on their sum. At test time the student runs without
dropout, which approximates the average of these sub-networks, as the teacher's clean prediction did.

**Generations on two moons.** The figure runs the loop on 200 points of two moons with four labels per class. The
teacher is a <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on 40
<NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">random Fourier features</NoteLink>; each student has 80 features at a shorter
lengthscale, is trained with three jittered copies of every point (standard deviation 0.15) and with dropout of rate 0.3
on its features, and pseudo-labels every unlabelled point. With data seed 7 the teacher has test accuracy $0.921$ and
8.3% of its pseudo-labels are wrong. The same larger student without noise reaches $0.910$ and reproduces the teacher's
pseudo-labels exactly, so the loop stops after one generation. The noisy students reach $0.938$, $0.944$ and $0.949$ in
generations 1 to 3, and the share of wrong pseudo-labels falls to 6.3%. Jitter of 0.35 is too much: the second student
falls to $0.898$.

## Cost

Each generation trains a student from scratch on $n + m$ images, and the students grow. On ImageNet the best result
took three generations, from an EfficientNet-B7 teacher to EfficientNet-L2 students of 480M parameters, whose training
takes about five times as long as B7's; accuracy rose from 87.6% to 88.1% and then 88.4% <Cite id="xie2020" />. The
teacher's labelling pass over 300M images is cheap by comparison. The method is expensive in compute and simple in
code: no new losses, only the ordinary cross-entropy on a larger, noised dataset.

## Trade-offs

- **Noise strength.** Too little noise makes the student a copy; too much makes it unable to fit the pseudo-labels.
  The ImageNet settings were not tuned extensively <Cite id="xie2020" />, but the figure shows the failure at both ends.
- **Student size.** A student no larger than the teacher gains less; Xie and colleagues found a large student
  important <Cite id="xie2020" />. Size and noise work together: capacity to fit more, noise to stop it fitting only
  the teacher.
- **Joint training against pretraining.** Yalniz and colleagues first trained the student on pseudo-labelled data and
  then fine-tuned it on labels, raising a ResNet-50 to 81.2% top-1 with a billion unlabelled images
  <Cite id="yalniz2019billion" />. Noisy Student trains on both at once, which was simpler and better in its
  experiments.
- **Self-training against pretraining.** On <Gloss name="coco" /> detection, self-training with strong augmentation
  helped at every labelled-data size (by 1.3 to 3.4 <Gloss name="ap" />), while ImageNet pretraining hurt when all
  labels were used <Cite id="zoph2020rethinking" />.
- **A fixed teacher against a learning one.** Meta Pseudo Labels updates the teacher from the student's performance on
  labelled data, so the pseudo-labels improve during training <Cite id="pham2021meta" />.

## Pitfalls

- **A biased teacher stays biased.** Noise corrects scattered errors, where a few wrong labels sit among many right
  ones nearby. A teacher wrong on a whole region labels it consistently, and the student learns the region
  consistently wrong.
- **Out-of-domain unlabelled data.** Without the confidence filter, images of other kinds are forced into some class.
  The 0.3 threshold is low because the filter only needs to reject what is clearly off-domain.
- **Evaluation leakage.** A large web-scale unlabelled set can contain near-duplicates of test images, which flatters
  the student.
- **Weak-to-strong settings.** A strong pretrained model fine-tuned on a weaker model's labels already exceeds its
  supervisor without noise, but recovers only part of the gap to training on true labels <Cite id="burns2023weak" />.
  See <NoteLink to="weak-to-strong-generalisation">weak-to-strong generalisation</NoteLink>.
