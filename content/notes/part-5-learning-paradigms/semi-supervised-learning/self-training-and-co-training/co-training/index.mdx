<Definition>
**Co-training** applies to inputs that come as two **views** $\xvec = (\xvec^{(1)}, \xvec^{(2)})$, each enough on its
own to predict the class. One classifier is trained per view on the labelled set. In each round each classifier labels
the unlabelled points it is most confident about, and those points join the shared labelled set with its labels, so
each classifier learns from the other's confident predictions. Blum and Mitchell introduced the method and proved that
it learns from unlabelled data when the two views are independent given the class <Cite id="blum1998" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="self-training">Self-training</NoteLink> lets a model label data for itself. The points it labels are
the ones it is already confident about, far from its own boundary, so they teach it little, and its mistakes come back
to it as targets. Both problems come from one model judging its own work.

Co-training keeps the self-training loop and changes who labels. Two classifiers look at the same example through
different features. A point that is easy for one view can be hard for the other: classifier 1 labels it confidently,
and classifier 2 receives a correct label on a point near its own boundary, which it could not have labelled itself.
Classifier 1's mistakes are also less harmful to classifier 2, because they depend on view 1's features, which
classifier 2 does not see.

Blum and Mitchell's example is web-page classification <Cite id="blum1998" />. View 1 is the
<NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> on a page; view 2 is the bag of words in the anchor text of
hyperlinks that point to the page. Either view tells whether a page is a course home page, and the words a page uses
about itself say little about the words other pages use to link to it. Starting from 12 labelled pages, co-training cut
the test error of the page-text classifier from 12.9% to 6.2% and of the combined classifier from 11.1% to 5.0%.

## Mechanism

### The algorithm

Let $L$ be the labelled set and $U$ the unlabelled set. Fix the number of points $p_k$ of each class $k$ that each
classifier labels per round, a pool size $u$ and a number of rounds.

1. Draw a pool $U'$ of $u$ points at random from $U$.
2. Train $h_1$ on view 1 of $L$, and $h_2$ on view 2 of $L$.
3. Let $h_1$ label, for each class $k$, the $p_k$ pool points it gives the highest probability of class $k$; let $h_2$
   do the same. Move these points, with those labels, from $U'$ to $L$.
4. Refill $U'$ to $u$ points from $U$, and return to step 2.

Labels are added for good: unlike most self-training, co-training never revises a label. The final classifier combines
the two views, for example by multiplying their class probabilities,
$\hat p(y \mid \xvec) \propto \hat p_1(y \mid \xvec^{(1)})\,\hat p_2(y \mid \xvec^{(2)})$, which is the right
combination when the views are independent given the class. In Blum and Mitchell's experiment both classifiers were
<NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink>, with $p = 1$ course page and $n = 3$ other pages per classifier per
round, a pool of $u = 75$ and 30 rounds, starting from 12 labelled pages <Cite id="blum1998" />. The small pool forces
the classifiers to choose among points that are more typical of the whole distribution than the extreme points of all of
$U$ would be.

### The two assumptions

Co-training needs two things of the views.

- **Sufficiency.** Each view alone determines the class: there are classifiers $f_1$ and $f_2$ with
  $f_1(\xvec^{(1)}) = f_2(\xvec^{(2)}) = y$ for (almost) every example.
- **Conditional independence.** Given the class, the views are independent:
  $p(\xvec^{(1)}, \xvec^{(2)} \mid y) = p(\xvec^{(1)} \mid y)\,p(\xvec^{(2)} \mid y)$.

Under independence the labels that $h_2$ hands to view 1 behave like correct labels corrupted by
**<NoteLink to="label-noise-models">random classification noise</NoteLink>** whose rate depends only on the true class.
Which view-1 inputs receive a wrong label does not depend on those inputs. Learning from labels with such noise is
possible whenever the noise rates $\alpha$ (a class-1 point labelled 0) and $\beta$ (a class-0 point labelled 1) satisfy
$\alpha + \beta < 1$ (see <NoteLink to="learning-with-noisy-labels">learning with noisy labels</NoteLink>). So any weak
classifier on view 2 that is better than chance supplies enough signal for view 1 to be learnt from unlabelled data
<Cite id="blum1998" />.

<Derivation title="Why conditional independence makes the noise random">
Let $h$ be any classifier on view 2 and $y$ the true class. The label $h(\xvec^{(2)})$ given to a view-1 input
$\xvec^{(1)}$ of class $y$ has

$$
P\bigl(h(\xvec^{(2)}) = 1 \mid \xvec^{(1)}, y\bigr) = P\bigl(h(\xvec^{(2)}) = 1 \mid y\bigr),
$$

because $\xvec^{(2)}$, and so $h(\xvec^{(2)})$, is independent of $\xvec^{(1)}$ given $y$. The probability of a wrong
label is therefore $\alpha = P(h = 0 \mid y = 1)$ for every class-1 input and $\beta = P(h = 1 \mid y = 0)$ for every
class-0 input, whatever $\xvec^{(1)}$ is: class-conditional random noise. The noisy label still carries the class,
since $P(h = 1 \mid y = 1) - P(h = 1 \mid y = 0) = 1 - \alpha - \beta > 0$ when $\alpha + \beta < 1$. Without
independence, $P(h = 1 \mid \xvec^{(1)}, y)$ varies with $\xvec^{(1)}$: the wrong labels concentrate on the view-1
inputs that resemble the other class, which is exactly where they bias the boundary.
</Derivation>

### Weaker conditions

Conditional independence rarely holds. Balcan, Blum and Yang replaced it by **expansion**: for any sets of points that
the two classifiers label confidently, the points confidently labelled in exactly one view must be a sizeable fraction
of those labelled in both or in neither <Cite id="balcan2004cotraining" />. Expansion is enough for iterative
co-training to work when each view has a strong learner, and it explains why co-training runs for many rounds rather
than one. Nigam and Ghani found that, on text, methods that use a natural split of the features beat those that do not,
and that a random split of a redundant feature set can still help <Cite id="nigam2000cotraining" />.

## Worked example

Each input has two one-dimensional views $a$ and $b$. The classifier of each view puts its threshold midway between
the class means of its labelled values, and its confidence on a point is the distance from that threshold. In truth,
class 1 has $a > 0$ and $b > -0.6$. Two points are labelled, and four are not:

| point | $a$  | $b$  | label (true class) |
| ----- | ---- | ---- | ------------------ |
| P1    | 1.0  | 2.0  | 1                  |
| P2    | −1.0 | −2.0 | 0                  |
| U1    | 2.2  | −0.4 | (1)                |
| U2    | 0.3  | 1.6  | (1)                |
| U3    | −1.8 | −1.2 | (0)                |
| U4    | −0.2 | −2.4 | (0)                |

**Round 1.** Both thresholds start at 0, the midpoints of $\pm 1$ and $\pm 2$. Each classifier labels one point of each
class. Classifier $a$ is most confident about U1 ($a = 2.2$, class 1) and U3 ($a = -1.8$, class 0). Classifier $b$ is
most confident about U2 ($b = 1.6$, class 1) and U4 ($b = -2.4$, class 0). All four labels are correct.

**What view $b$ receives.** U1 has $b = -0.4$, below $b$'s threshold of 0: classifier $b$ would have called it class
0, and with confidence $0.4$ it is the point $b$ is least sure about. Classifier $a$ labels it class 1 with the largest
confidence of all.

**Round 2.** View $b$'s class-1 values are now $2.0$, $-0.4$ and $1.6$, with mean $1.067$; its class-0 values are
$-2.0$, $-1.2$ and $-2.4$, with mean $-1.867$. The new threshold is their midpoint, $-0.4$, closer to the true $-0.6$.
View $a$'s means become $1.167$ and $-1.0$, and its threshold moves to $0.083$.

**Against self-training.** If classifier $b$ labelled for itself, it would add its own confident points U2 and U4.
Its class means would be $1.8$ and $-2.2$ and its threshold $-0.2$: half as far towards the truth, because the points it
chose lie far from its boundary.

The figure runs the same loop on 200 two-view points with uniform densities in each view, two labels per class, two
points per class per classifier per round and a pool of 50. With data seed 2 and independent views, view $b$'s test
accuracy rises from $0.754$ to $0.908$ by round 25, when every unlabelled point has been labelled, against $0.913$ for
the best classifier of one view, and 6 of the 196 added labels are wrong. View $b$ self-trained alone with the same
budget reaches $0.881$. The product of the two views reaches $0.971$. With dependence 1 the views are copies, both reach
$0.903$, and 26 of the 196 added labels are wrong.

## Cost

Each round trains two classifiers and predicts the pool with each, so a run of $R$ rounds costs $2R$ fits of
the base learner on a labelled set that grows by $2\sum_k p_k$ points per round. With a pool, prediction costs $O(u)$
per round instead of $O(\abs{U})$.

## Trade-offs

- **Natural against manufactured views.** A natural split (page text and anchor text, an image and its caption, audio
  and video) gives the most independent errors. Without one, random feature splits or different learners on the same
  features (<NoteLink to="tri-training-and-disagreement-methods">tri-training</NoteLink>) manufacture diversity, at the
  cost of weaker guarantees <Cite id="nigam2000cotraining" /> <Cite id="zhou2010disagreement" />.
- **Labels per round.** Adding few points per round keeps each addition reliable but needs many rounds. Fixing the
  number per class, as Blum and Mitchell did with one course page to three others, keeps the added labels at the class
  proportions of the data.
- **Permanent labels.** Never revising a label is cheap and stops the loop from oscillating, but a wrong label stays
  for the whole run.
- **Deep networks.** Two networks on one input have no natural views and tend to collapse into the same function. Deep
  co-training keeps them apart with <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples">adversarial examples</NoteLink> that one network
  must classify differently from the other <Cite id="qiao2018deep" />. Co-teaching applies the exchange to noisy labels:
  each network picks the examples with small loss for the other to train on <Cite id="han2018" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Correlated views.** When the views share information beyond the class, the labels one classifier gives the other
  are wrong on exactly the inputs that confuse both. The figure's dependence knob shows the added-label error rate
  rising from 3% to 13% as the views go from independent to copies.
- **An insufficient view.** If one view cannot predict the class, its confident labels are noise, and it pulls the
  better view down. Each view should be checked alone on the labelled data first.
- **Class drift.** If one classifier is biased towards a class, it keeps adding that class, and the shared set drifts
  from the true proportions; fixed per-class quotas prevent this.
- **The pool matters.** Labelling from all of $U$ picks the most extreme points, which carry little information about
  the boundary; Blum and Mitchell obtained better results with a small pool <Cite id="blum1998" />.
