<Definition>
Self-training and co-training are **wrapper methods** of semi-supervised learning: they take an ordinary supervised
learner, use it to predict labels for unlabelled points, and refit it on the points whose predicted labels look
reliable <Cite id="vanengelen2020survey" />. The methods differ in who labels the data (the model itself, a separate
teacher, or other learners) and in how a predicted label is judged reliable (a confidence threshold, agreement between
learners, or a bound on <NoteLink to="label-noise">label noise</NoteLink>).
</Definition>

## What it is for

A few labelled examples and many unlabelled ones are the common case in practice: labels cost expert time, inputs are
collected automatically. A wrapper method uses the unlabelled inputs without changing the learner, so the same idea
serves <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink> on text, <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision trees</NoteLink>
on tables and <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink> on images. The predicted labels,
called **pseudo-labels**, turn the problem back into <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink>
on a larger, partly noisy training set. They help when most confident predictions are right, which is what the cluster
and low-density assumptions guarantee (see <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">semi-supervised
assumptions</NoteLink>).

The same pattern appears well beyond classical semi-supervised learning: large models labelling data to train smaller
or newer ones, <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss> generating labels for text (see
<NoteLink to="labelling-with-language-models">labelling with language models</NoteLink>), and strong models trained on
weak models' outputs (see <NoteLink to="weak-to-strong-generalisation">weak-to-strong generalisation</NoteLink>).

## Main ideas

**Labelling for oneself.** <NoteLink to="self-training">Self-training</NoteLink> fits a model, adds its confident
predictions on unlabelled points as labels and refits, round after round. The boundary moves away from dense regions,
because confident predictions there are mostly right and their cross-entropy rewards boundaries that leave unlabelled
points far from probability one half.

**Thresholds and confirmation bias.** <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">Pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> runs the same idea
inside one training run of a network, minibatch by minibatch. A confidence threshold trades the number of
pseudo-labels against their error rate. A wrong pseudo-label pushes the model further towards its mistake
(confirmation bias), which thresholds, ramp-up schedules, soft labels and curricula mitigate. Training on hard
pseudo-labels minimises a lower bound on the entropy of the predictions that vanishes where the entropy does, which
links it to
<NoteLink to="entropy-minimisation">entropy minimisation</NoteLink>.

**A separate, noised student.** <NoteLink to="noisy-student">Noisy Student</NoteLink> labels data with a clean teacher
and trains a larger student under augmentation, dropout and stochastic depth, which then becomes the teacher. The
noise stops the student from copying the teacher; the capacity lets it exceed the teacher. It is
<NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">knowledge distillation</NoteLink> turned towards expansion.

**Labelling for each other.** <NoteLink to="co-training">Co-training</NoteLink> trains one classifier per view of the
data, and each labels confident points for the other. If the views are independent given the class, one view's
mistakes look like random label noise to the other, which a learner can tolerate. Without views,
<NoteLink to="tri-training-and-disagreement-methods">tri-training and disagreement methods</NoteLink> create
diversity with bootstrap samples or different algorithms, and trust a point when the other learners agree on it
<Cite id="zhou2010disagreement" />.

**When labels from a model hurt.** All these methods fail the same way: if a model is confidently wrong on a whole
region, its pseudo-labels make the error permanent, and unlabelled data then lower accuracy below the supervised
baseline <Cite id="chapelle2006" />. Calibrated confidences, class-balanced additions and a strong supervised baseline
for comparison are part of every careful use (see
<NoteLink to="when-semi-supervised-learning-fails">when semi-supervised learning fails</NoteLink>).
