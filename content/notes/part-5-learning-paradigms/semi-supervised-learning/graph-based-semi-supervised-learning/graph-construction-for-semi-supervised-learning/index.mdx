<Definition>
**Graph construction** for <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-learning">semi-supervised learning</NoteLink> turns $n$
labelled and unlabelled inputs $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n \in \reals^d$ into a weighted graph, the input of label
propagation, <NoteLink to="label-spreading">label spreading</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="manifold-regularisation">manifold regularisation</NoteLink>. It makes two choices: a **neighbourhood
rule** that decides which pairs are joined, and a **weighting** that gives each edge a weight $W_{ij} \ge 0$. The result
is a symmetric affinity matrix $\Wmat$ ($n \times n$, zero diagonal, $W_{ij} = 0$ for pairs that are not joined).
</Definition>

## Intuition

Every graph method moves label information along edges and penalises disagreement across them. A propagation rule cannot
recover a boundary that the graph does not show. If the graph has no edge between two dense regions, labels stay on
their side; if a few edges join two classes, labels leak across them whatever the rule. Zhu's survey of the field holds
that constructing a good graph matters more than choosing among the graph methods <Cite id="zhu2005survey" />, and
comparisons of construction methods find that accuracy depends heavily on the graph <Cite id="jebara2009bmatching" />
<Cite id="desousa2013graph" />.

A good graph for semi-supervised learning has three properties:

- **Strong edges within classes.** Points of the same class are joined by paths of strong edges, so a few labels reach
  the whole class.
- **Weak or absent edges across classes.** The class boundary runs through the low-density gap, where the edges are
  few and weak, so cutting them is cheap.
- **Every component labelled.** A connected component with no labelled point receives no score at all.

The first and third ask for a dense graph; the second asks for a sparse one. The construction choices below trade one
against the other. The general construction of graphs from data, beyond semi-supervised learning, is covered in
<NoteLink to="graph-construction">graph construction</NoteLink>.

## Details

### Neighbourhood rules

Let $\Ncal_k(i)$ be the set of the $k$ points nearest to $\xvec_i$, by Euclidean distance unless a better metric is
known <Cite id="vonluxburg2007" />.

- **Symmetrised $k$-nearest-neighbour graph.** Join $i$ and $j$ when $j \in \Ncal_k(i)$ or $i \in \Ncal_k(j)$. Every
  point has at least $k$ edges, so the graph is rarely disconnected. A point near many others, a hub, can collect far
  more than $k$ edges.
- **Mutual $k$-nearest-neighbour graph.** Join $i$ and $j$ only when both $j \in \Ncal_k(i)$ and $i \in \Ncal_k(j)$.
  Edges into sparse regions are dropped, which removes many bridges between classes, and isolated outliers become
  separate components.
- **$\varepsilon$-ball graph.** Join $i$ and $j$ when $\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j} \le \varepsilon$. One radius serves the
  whole space, so dense regions get many edges and sparse regions few or none.
- **b-matching.** Choose the edges so that every point has exactly $b$ of them and the total length of the chosen edges
  is smallest. Jebara, Wang and Chang solve this by <Gloss name="bp">belief propagation</Gloss> and find it more robust
  than $k$-nearest neighbours, because no point can become a hub <Cite id="jebara2009bmatching" />. The figure below
  uses a greedy approximation: add the shortest pairs in order while both ends have fewer than $b$ edges.
- **Full graph.** Join every pair. The weights alone then decide the structure, and the cost of storing and solving
  grows as $n^2$ and $n^3$.

### Edge weights

- **Binary.** $W_{ij} = 1$ for every edge. An edge across a gap counts as much as an edge inside a cluster.
- **Gaussian (heat kernel).** $W_{ij} = \exp(-\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2/2\sigma^2)$, with bandwidth $\sigma > 0$ in
  the units of the inputs. Long edges get small weights, so the graph energy barely charges for cutting them. A
  $\sigma$ much larger than the distances makes all weights close to 1, the binary case; a $\sigma$ much smaller than
  the nearest-neighbour distances makes all weights close to 0 and the propagation numerically fragile.
- **Local scaling.** $W_{ij} = \exp(-\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2/(\sigma_i\sigma_j))$, with $\sigma_i$ the distance from
  $\xvec_i$ to its $m$-th nearest neighbour ($m = 7$ in the original proposal), adapts the bandwidth to the local
  density <Cite id="zelnikmanor2004selftuning" />.

### Sparsification and scale

A sparse graph keeps memory at $O(nk)$ and an iteration of propagation at $O(nk)$ for $n$ points and $k$ edges each.
Exact $k$-nearest-neighbour search costs $O(n^2d)$ for $n$ points in $d$ dimensions; for large $n$ the graph is built
with <NoteLink to="approximate-nearest-neighbour-search">approximate nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink>. Sparsification
also removes most long edges, which are the edges that cross classes.

## Worked example

Six points lie on a line: class A at $0, 0.4, 0.8$ and class B at $1.5, 1.9, 2.3$. The gap between the classes, 0.7,
is less than twice the spacing within a class, 0.4. The point at 0 is labelled A and the point at 1.9 is labelled B.
Each graph below is followed by the harmonic solution of <NoteLink to="label-propagation">label propagation</NoteLink>,
written as the score for A at the unlabelled points $0.4, 0.8, 1.5, 2.3$; a score above $1/2$ means A.

**1-nearest-neighbour graph, binary weights.** Each point's nearest neighbour is 0.4 away, inside its class. The edges
are $0 - 0.4$, $0.4 - 0.8$, $1.5 - 1.9$ and $1.9 - 2.3$: two components, one per class, each with one label. The scores
are $1, 1, 0, 0$, all correct. One more class-A point without the label at 0 would leave its whole component
unassigned.

**Mutual 2-nearest-neighbour graph, binary weights.** The two <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest
neighbours</NoteLink> of 0.8 are 0.4 (distance 0.4) and 1.5 (distance 0.7), and those of 1.5 are 1.9 and 0.8. The pair
$0.8 - 1.5$ is mutual, so one edge crosses the gap. The graph is a path of five unit edges, and the harmonic solution
interpolates linearly along it: $0.75, 0.50, 0.25, 0$. Point 0.8 is a tie. The $\varepsilon$-ball graph with
$\varepsilon = 0.75$ is the same path.

**3-nearest-neighbour graph, binary weights.** Eleven edges, five of them across the classes ($0 - 1.5$, $0.4 - 1.5$,
$0.8 - 1.5$, $0.8 - 1.9$, $0.8 - 2.3$). The scores are $0.667, 0.500, 0.500, 0.333$: points 0.8 and 1.5 are ties, and
45% of the edge weight crosses the classes.

**The same 3-nearest-neighbour graph, Gaussian weights with $\sigma = 0.4$.** An edge of length 0.4 gets weight
$e^{-0.5} = 0.607$; the gap edge of length 0.7 gets $e^{-1.53} = 0.216$; the edge from 0 to 1.5 gets $0.001$. The
share of weight across the classes falls from 45% to 9%. The scores become $0.834, 0.692, 0.177, 0.033$, all correct.

The four graphs use the same points, the same labels and the same propagation rule. The answers range from exact to
two ties. The figure repeats the experiment in two dimensions with each rule.

## Assumptions

- **Distance reflects class.** Every rule above starts from distances in input space. If the class depends on a few of
  many features, or the features have different units, the nearest neighbours are of the wrong kind. Standardising
  the inputs, learning a metric (<NoteLink to="metric-learning">metric learning</NoteLink>) or building the graph on the
  embeddings of a pretrained network fixes the distance before the graph is built.
- **Classes are separated by low density.** Sparse graphs and Gaussian weights cut long edges. That helps only when the
  class boundary runs through a gap. When two classes overlap in a dense region, no construction separates them, and
  the graph joins them by many short edges.
- **The unlabelled points come from the same distribution as the test points.** The graph models the density of the
  points it contains.

## Pitfalls

- **Tuning without labels.** The number of neighbours, the radius and $\sigma$ are chosen with only a handful of labels
  for validation. De Sousa, Rezende and Batista found that accuracy depends heavily on these parameters, with no setting
  that worked across data sets; in their comparison, mutual $k$-nearest-neighbour graphs with Gaussian weights did best
  but became unstable for small $k$ <Cite id="desousa2013graph" />.
- **Disconnection.** The mutual and $\varepsilon$ rules, and small $k$, leave unlabelled components. Their points get
  no score, and an accuracy that counts them as errors falls sharply.
- **Hubs.** In high dimensions some points appear among the nearest neighbours of many others. In a symmetrised
  $k$-nearest-neighbour graph a hub collects many edges and spreads its label widely. Mutual neighbours and b-matching
  bound the degree <Cite id="jebara2009bmatching" />.
- **Binary weights with large $k$.** Each extra neighbour adds edges of growing length. Without distance weights, the
  long edges across the gap carry as much label as the short ones inside a class.
- **Too small a bandwidth.** With $\sigma$ well below the typical neighbour distance, every weight underflows towards
  zero and the linear systems become ill-conditioned.
