<Definition>
Given $l$ labelled pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$, $u$ unlabelled inputs, a kernel $k$ with <Gloss name="rkhs">reproducing
kernel Hilbert space</Gloss> $\Hcal$, and the Laplacian $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Wmat$ of a
<NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">similarity graph</NoteLink> over all $n = l + u$ inputs, **manifold regularisation**
chooses

$$
f^* = \argmin_{f \in \Hcal}\; \frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l} V(y_i, f(\xvec_i)) + \gamma_A\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2
+ \frac{\gamma_I}{n^2}\,\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec,
$$

where $V$ is a loss, $\fvec = (f(\xvec_1), \dots, f(\xvec_n))$ holds the function's values at all $n$ inputs, and
$\gamma_A > 0$ and $\gamma_I \ge 0$ weigh the two penalties. The minimiser is
$f^*(\xvec) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i k(\xvec_i, \xvec)$, a function defined on the whole input space
<Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Graph methods such as <NoteLink to="label-propagation">label propagation</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="label-spreading">label spreading</NoteLink> assign scores to the nodes of a graph and nothing else. They
are transductive: a point that arrives after the graph is built has no score until it is added to the graph and the
propagation is run again. Supervised kernel methods such as <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge
regression</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> produce a function
of any input, but they see only the labelled points. With two labels, their decision boundary is set by where those two
points happen to lie.

Manifold regularisation, introduced by Belkin, Niyogi and Sindhwani, combines the two
<Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />. It keeps the supervised kernel model, its loss and its penalty on the function's norm.
It adds one term, the graph penalty of label propagation evaluated on the function's values at the labelled and
unlabelled points. The result is a classifier or regressor that uses the unlabelled data and predicts new inputs.

## Mechanism

### Two penalties

The **ambient penalty** $\gamma_A\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2$ is the usual kernel regulariser. It measures how rough $f$ is over
the whole input space, whatever the data look like.

The **intrinsic penalty** measures how much $f$ changes along the data. Expanding the quadratic form,

$$
\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i, j = 1}^{n} W_{ij}\paren{f(\xvec_i) - f(\xvec_j)}^2,
$$

the energy of <NoteLink to="label-propagation">label propagation</NoteLink>. It is large when $f$ takes different values
at points joined by a strong edge, and it ignores how $f$ behaves away from the data. When the inputs lie on a
low-dimensional manifold $\Mcal$ and the graph is built with Gaussian weights from a growing sample, the normalised form
$\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec/n^2$ approximates $\int_{\Mcal}\norm{\nabla_{\Mcal}f}^2\, dP$, the squared gradient of $f$ along
the manifold averaged over the input density <Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />. The factor $1/n^2$ keeps $\gamma_I$ on
the same scale as the sample grows. A function with a small intrinsic penalty is nearly constant on each dense region
and changes in the gaps between them, which is the <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">cluster
assumption</NoteLink>.

The two penalties split the work. With $\gamma_I = 0$ the method is the supervised kernel method. As $\gamma_A \to 0$,
the values of $f$ at the $n$ points are free, and the problem becomes label propagation with the labels as a soft
constraint: fit the labels, penalise the graph energy. As $\gamma_I$ grows, $f$ becomes nearly constant on every
connected component of the graph.

### A representer theorem

The objective depends on $f$ through three things: its values at the $l$ labelled points, its values at all $n$
points, and its norm. The argument of the <NoteLink to="representer-theorem">representer theorem</NoteLink> applies with
the training set enlarged to all $n$ points. Split $f = f_\parallel + f_\perp$, with $f_\parallel$ in the span of
$k(\xvec_1, \cdot), \dots, k(\xvec_n, \cdot)$ and $f_\perp$ orthogonal to it. The reproducing property gives
$f(\xvec_j) = \inner{f}{k(\xvec_j, \cdot)}_{\Hcal} = f_\parallel(\xvec_j)$ at every one of the $n$ points, so the loss
and the intrinsic penalty do not see $f_\perp$. The norm satisfies
$\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \norm{f_\parallel}_{\Hcal}^2 + \norm{f_\perp}_{\Hcal}^2$, so any $f_\perp \ne 0$ raises the
objective. The minimiser therefore has the form

$$
f^*(\xvec) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\, k(\xvec_i, \xvec)
$$

with one coefficient for each labelled **and** unlabelled point <Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />. The unlabelled points
become centres of the expansion, and the learning problem reduces to $n$ numbers.

### Laplacian regularised least squares

With the squared loss $V(y, f) = (y - f)^2$ the coefficients have a closed form. Let $\Kmat$ be the $n \times n$ Gram
matrix over all inputs, so $\fvec = \Kmat\alphavec$ and $\norm{f}_{\Hcal}^2 = \alphavec^\top\Kmat\alphavec$. Let
$\Jmat = \diag(1, \dots, 1, 0, \dots, 0)$ mark the $l$ labelled points (listed first) and let $\yvec$ hold their
targets, padded with zeros for the unlabelled points. The objective is

$$
J(\alphavec) = \frac{1}{l}\norm{\yvec - \Jmat\Kmat\alphavec}^2 + \gamma_A\,\alphavec^\top\Kmat\alphavec
+ \frac{\gamma_I}{n^2}\,\alphavec^\top\Kmat\Lmat\Kmat\alphavec.
$$

It is a convex quadratic in $\alphavec$. Its gradient, using $\Jmat^\top\Jmat = \Jmat$ and the symmetry of $\Kmat$ and
$\Lmat$, is

$$
\nabla J = \frac{2}{l}\Kmat\paren{\Jmat\Kmat\alphavec - \yvec} + 2\gamma_A\Kmat\alphavec
+ \frac{2\gamma_I}{n^2}\Kmat\Lmat\Kmat\alphavec
= \frac{2}{l}\Kmat\brack{\Jmat\Kmat\alphavec - \yvec + \gamma_A l\,\alphavec + \frac{\gamma_I l}{n^2}\Lmat\Kmat\alphavec}.
$$

Setting the bracket to zero gives the **Laplacian regularised least squares** (LapRLS) solution
<Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />:

$$
\alphavec^* = \paren{\Jmat\Kmat + \gamma_A l\,\I + \frac{\gamma_I l}{n^2}\Lmat\Kmat}^{-1}\yvec.
$$

With $\gamma_I = 0$, the rows of the unlabelled points read $\gamma_A l\,\alpha_i = 0$, so their coefficients vanish,
and the labelled rows reduce to $(\Kmat_{LL} + \gamma_A l\,\I)\alphavec_L = \yvec_L$: kernel ridge regression with
penalty $\lambda = \gamma_A l$. For two classes the targets are $\pm 1$ and a new input is assigned the sign of
$f^*(\xvec)$.

<Derivation title="Laplacian support vector machine">
With the <Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss> $V(y, f) = \max(0, 1 - yf)$ the same representer theorem holds, and
the problem becomes a support vector machine with a modified kernel. Belkin et al. derive the dual: the $l$ multipliers
$\betavec$ solve the standard SVM quadratic programme

$$
\betavec^* = \argmax_{\betavec}\; \sum_{i=1}^{l}\beta_i - \frac{1}{2}\betavec^\top\Qmat\betavec
\quad\text{subject to}\quad \sum_{i=1}^{l}\beta_i y_i = 0,\; 0 \le \beta_i \le \frac{1}{l},
$$

with $\Qmat = \Ymat\Jmat_l\Kmat\paren{2\gamma_A\I + \frac{2\gamma_I}{n^2}\Lmat\Kmat}^{-1}\Jmat_l^\top\Ymat$, where
$\Jmat_l = [\I \;\; \zeros]$ is the $l \times n$ selector of the labelled points and $\Ymat = \diag(y_1, \dots, y_l)$.
The $n$ expansion coefficients follow from the linear system
$\alphavec^* = \paren{2\gamma_A\I + \frac{2\gamma_I}{n^2}\Lmat\Kmat}^{-1}\Jmat_l^\top\Ymat\betavec^*$
<Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />. A standard SVM solver therefore trains a Laplacian SVM once $\Qmat$ is formed. With
$\gamma_I = 0$, $\Qmat$ is the usual SVM matrix and the unlabelled coefficients vanish.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Six inputs lie on a line: a left cluster at $0, 1, 2$ and a right cluster at $3.5, 4.5, 5.5$. The point at 0 is
labelled $+1$ and the point at 3.5 is labelled $-1$; the other four are unlabelled. The kernel is Gaussian with
lengthscale 1, $k(x, x') = \exp(-(x - x')^2/2)$, and $\gamma_A = 0.05$.

**Step 1: kernel ridge regression ($\gamma_I = 0$).** Only the two labelled points get coefficients. Their kernel value
is $k(0, 3.5) = e^{-6.125} = 0.002$, so $(\Kmat_{LL} + 0.1\,\I)\alphavec_L = (1, -1)$ gives
$\alphavec_L \approx (0.911, -0.911)$. The value at the unlabelled point 2 is

$$
f(2) = 0.911\,k(0, 2) - 0.911\,k(3.5, 2) = 0.911 \times (0.135 - 0.325) = -0.173.
$$

Point 2 sits in the left cluster but is 1.5 from the negative label and 2 from the positive one, so it is classified
$-1$. The boundary is at $x = 1.75$, halfway between the labels: $f(1.75) = 0$.

**Step 2: the graph.** Join each point to its nearest neighbour with weight 1. The edges are $0 - 1$, $1 - 2$,
$3.5 - 4.5$ and $4.5 - 5.5$; there is no edge across the gap of 1.5 between 2 and 3.5. The Laplacian is block diagonal,
one block per cluster:

$$
\Lmat = \begin{pmatrix}\Bmat & \zeros \\ \zeros & \Bmat\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\Bmat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 1\end{pmatrix},
$$

and $\fvec^\top\Lmat\fvec$ is the sum of the four squared differences along the edges.

**Step 3: LapRLS.** Take $\gamma_I/n^2 = 1$ ($\gamma_I = 36$ for $n = 6$). Solving the LapRLS system gives fitted values

$$
\fvec = (0.820,\; 0.781,\; 0.710,\; -0.819,\; -0.803,\; -0.757)
$$

at $0, 1, 2, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5$. Point 2 is now $+1$, with nearly the same value as its cluster. The labelled values have
dropped from 0.909 to about 0.82 in magnitude: the graph term traded a little fit at the labels for agreement along
the edges.

**Step 4: new inputs.** The function predicts points that were not in the graph. At $x = 1.75$, where kernel ridge
regression gave exactly 0, LapRLS gives $f(1.75) = 0.806$. At $x = 2.75$, in the gap, it gives $-0.063$, close to the
new boundary. Raising $\gamma_I/n^2$ to 100 makes the values within each cluster equal to two decimals
($0.810, 0.809, 0.808$), the limit in which the graph term is zero.

## Cost

- **Fitting.** LapRLS solves one dense $n \times n$ system, $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory, with $n$ the number of
  labelled plus unlabelled points. Forming $\Lmat\Kmat$ costs $O(n^2 k)$ on a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph. LapSVM adds
  a quadratic programme in only $l$ variables.
- **Training in the primal.** Melacci and Belkin solve LapSVM in the primal by preconditioned <Gloss name="cg">conjugate
  gradient</Gloss> with <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink>, which cuts the cost from $O(n^3)$ to
  $O(kn^2)$ with an empirically small number of steps $k$ <Cite id="melacci2011lapsvm" />.
- **Prediction.** A new input costs $n$ kernel evaluations, one per labelled or unlabelled point, against $l$ for a
  supervised kernel machine. The unlabelled points are part of the model.

## Trade-offs

- **Induction against simplicity.** Label propagation needs only a graph and a solve; manifold regularisation adds a
  kernel and a second penalty, and in return predicts new inputs. When all test points are known in advance,
  propagation over the full graph is cheaper and needs no kernel.
- **Three hyperparameters plus a graph.** $\gamma_A$, $\gamma_I$ and the kernel width are tuned with few labels. Belkin
  et al. split one supervised penalty between the two terms in a fixed ratio, $\gamma_A l = 0.005$ and
  $\gamma_I l/n^2 = 0.045$ on handwritten digits, and report that the conclusions held across a wide range of values
  <Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />.
- **Any loss.** The intrinsic penalty is added to the objective, not built into the solver. Squared loss gives LapRLS,
  hinge loss gives LapSVM, and other losses give other members of the family, each extended to new points by the same
  representer theorem.

**Use today.** The graph penalty outlived the kernel machines. Weston, Ratle and Collobert add a graph-based embedding
penalty on unlabelled pairs to the loss of a deep network, at the output or at every layer, and train it by
<Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> <Cite id="weston2008embedding" />. Kipf and Welling list label
propagation and manifold regularisation as the graph-Laplacian regularisation family that the
<NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolutional network</NoteLink> replaced on citation graphs, by
conditioning the model on the graph instead of penalising it <Cite id="kipf2017" />. In its kernel form, LapRLS is a
common method for <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-regression">semi-supervised regression</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **The graph decides the boundary.** The intrinsic term pulls the boundary into regions where the graph has weak
  edges. If an edge joins two classes, the penalty pulls them together; see
  <NoteLink to="graph-construction-for-semi-supervised-learning">graph construction</NoteLink>.
- **The graph term alone degenerates with many unlabelled points.** With a fixed number of labels, the graph-Laplacian
  solution becomes uninformative as the number of unlabelled points grows in input dimension 2 or more: it tends to a
  function that is nearly constant except for spikes at the labelled points <Cite id="nadler2009infinite" />. The
  result applies to label propagation and to manifold regularisation with a small $\gamma_A$.
- **Scaling of $\gamma_I$.** The paper's $1/n^2$ factor and the alternative $1/\sum_{ij}W_{ij}$ for sparse graphs
  change the meaning of a given $\gamma_I$ by orders of magnitude <Cite id="belkin2006manifold" />. A value copied from
  another implementation is only meaningful with its normalisation.
- **Memory.** The model stores all $n$ training inputs, and the dense solve limits the exact method to tens of thousands
  of points.
- **Assumption.** The method helps only when the classes follow the graph's dense regions, the cluster or manifold
  assumption. When a class boundary crosses a dense region, the intrinsic term pushes the boundary out of it and
  misclassifies the region.
