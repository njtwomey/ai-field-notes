<Definition>
The **M2 model** of Kingma, Rezende, Mohamed and Welling is a <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational
autoencoder</NoteLink> with two latent variables: a class label $y \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ and a continuous code
$\zvec \in \reals^m$ <Cite id="kingma2014semi" />. The generative model draws $y \sim \Cat(\pivec)$ and
$\zvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ independently and then $\xvec \sim p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid y, \zvec)$. The inference
model has two networks: a classifier $q_{\phivec}(y \mid \xvec)$ and an encoder $q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec, y)$. For
a labelled point the label is observed and only $\zvec$ is inferred; for an unlabelled point both are inferred. The
networks maximise the sum of the two kinds of evidence lower bound plus $\alpha$ times the log-likelihood of the
classifier on the labelled points, and the classifier is the model's prediction rule.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="generative-semi-supervised-learning">Generative semi-supervised learning</NoteLink> fits a model of
$p(\xvec, y)$ and lets unlabelled points contribute their marginal likelihood. With a Gaussian or multinomial class
model, the fit is exact <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">EM</NoteLink>, but the model is too simple for images,
audio or any input whose classes are not blobs. A <Gloss name="vae" /> supplies a flexible density for such inputs: a
neural decoder, an amortised encoder and a bound that is trained by stochastic gradients. M2 combines the two. It keeps
the semi-supervised recipe (labels observed for some points, summed out for others) and replaces the class-conditional
Gaussian by a decoder network.

Kingma and coauthors first tried a simpler route, M1: train an ordinary VAE on all inputs, then fit a classifier to the
encoder means of the labelled points. M1 uses unlabelled data only to learn features. M2 makes the label part of the
generative model, so the decoder must explain each input through its class, and the unlabelled points train the
classifier directly <Cite id="kingma2014semi" />.

The extra variable separates two kinds of variation. The label carries what distinguishes the classes, and the code
carries everything else, which Kingma and coauthors call style: for handwritten digits, whether the writing is upright
or slanted. Fixing $\zvec$ and changing $y$ draws the same style in every class.

## Mechanism

### The labelled bound

For a labelled pair $(\xvec, y)$ only $\zvec$ is latent. The bound is the conditional
<NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">ELBO</NoteLink> of a <NoteLink to="conditional-variational-autoencoder">conditional
VAE</NoteLink> plus the log-prior of the label. Writing it as a loss to minimise,

$$
\Lcal(\xvec, y) = -\expect_{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec, y)}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid y, \zvec)}
+ \KL\paren{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec, y) \,\|\, \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)} - \log \pi_y \;\ge\; -\log p(\xvec, y).
$$

The encoder is Gaussian,
$q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec, y) = \Gauss(\muvec_{\phivec}(\xvec, y), \diag \sigmavec^2_{\phivec}(\xvec))$, so the
<Gloss name="kl" /> term has a closed form and the expectation is estimated with the
<NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink>.

### The unlabelled bound

For an unlabelled input, $y$ is latent too. Take the variational posterior
$q_{\phivec}(y, \zvec \mid \xvec) = q_{\phivec}(y \mid \xvec)\, q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec, y)$. The bound on
$-\log p(\xvec)$ is

$$
\Ucal(\xvec) = \sum_{y=1}^{K} q_{\phivec}(y \mid \xvec)\, \Lcal(\xvec, y) - \entropy\brack{q_{\phivec}(y \mid \xvec)}.
$$

The classifier weights the labelled bound of each candidate class, and the entropy term rewards spreading probability
over classes. The sum over $y$ is exact: each unlabelled input passes through the encoder and decoder once per class.

<Derivation title="The unlabelled bound from the evidence lower bound">
Apply the ELBO to the joint latent $(y, \zvec)$ with
$q(y, \zvec \mid \xvec) = q(y \mid \xvec)\, q(\zvec \mid \xvec, y)$:

$$
\log p(\xvec) \ge \expect_{q(y, \zvec \mid \xvec)}\brack{\log p(\xvec \mid y, \zvec) + \log \pi_y + \log p(\zvec)
- \log q(y \mid \xvec) - \log q(\zvec \mid \xvec, y)}.
$$

Take the expectation over $y$ outside, as a sum weighted by $q(y \mid \xvec)$. For each $y$, the inner expectation over
$\zvec$ of every term except $-\log q(y \mid \xvec)$ is $-\Lcal(\xvec, y)$. The remaining term gives
$-\sum_y q(y \mid \xvec) \log q(y \mid \xvec) = \entropy[q(y \mid \xvec)]$. So
$\log p(\xvec) \ge \sum_y q(y \mid \xvec)\,(-\Lcal(\xvec, y)) + \entropy[q(y \mid \xvec)] = -\Ucal(\xvec)$
<Cite id="kingma2014semi" />.
</Derivation>

### What the unlabelled bound asks of the classifier

Hold the per-class bounds $\Lcal(\xvec, y)$ fixed and minimise $\Ucal$ over the class probabilities $q_y$. The function
$\sum_y q_y \Lcal_y + \sum_y q_y \log q_y$ is a free energy, minimised under $\sum_y q_y = 1$ by

$$
q_y^\star = \frac{\exp(-\Lcal(\xvec, y))}{\sum_{k} \exp(-\Lcal(\xvec, k))}, \qquad
\Ucal^\star = -\log \sum_{k} \exp(-\Lcal(\xvec, k)).
$$

The derivative of the Lagrangian in $q_y$ is $\Lcal_y + \log q_y + 1 + \nu$, and setting it to zero gives
$q_y \propto e^{-\Lcal_y}$. Since $-\Lcal(\xvec, y)$ is a lower bound on $\log p(\xvec, y)$, $q^\star$ is the generative
model's estimate of the posterior $p(y \mid \xvec)$. The unlabelled term therefore trains the classifier towards the
class under which the decoder explains the input best. This is the semi-supervised signal, in the same way that the
E-step of semi-supervised EM sets an unlabelled point's responsibilities to its posterior.

### The objective

Summed over the labelled set $L$ and the unlabelled set $U$, the bounds give
$\Jcal = \sum_{(\xvec, y) \in L} \Lcal(\xvec, y) + \sum_{\xvec \in U} \Ucal(\xvec)$. In $\Jcal$ the classifier appears
only in the unlabelled term, so it never learns from the labels directly. Kingma and coauthors add a classification
loss:

$$
\Jcal^{\alpha} = \Jcal + \alpha\, \expect_{(\xvec, y) \sim L}\brack{-\log q_{\phivec}(y \mid \xvec)},
$$

with $\alpha = 0.1 N$ for $N$ training points in all their experiments <Cite id="kingma2014semi" />. All three networks
are trained jointly on $\Jcal^\alpha$ by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>, without alternating
steps.

M1 and M2 can be stacked. **M1+M2** trains M1 first and then fits M2 to the M1 codes instead of the raw inputs, so the
class-plus-style model works in a space where classes are already better separated.

## Worked example

Take one unlabelled input and two classes. Suppose the encoder and decoder give the per-class bounds
$\Lcal(\xvec, 0) = 3.1$ and $\Lcal(\xvec, 1) = 5.4$ nats: the decoder explains the input better as class 0. Suppose the
classifier currently says $q = (0.8, 0.2)$.

**The bound.** The weighted bounds are $0.8 \times 3.1 = 2.48$ and $0.2 \times 5.4 = 1.08$. The entropy is
$-(0.8 \log 0.8 + 0.2 \log 0.2) = 0.178 + 0.322 = 0.500$ nats. So $\Ucal = 2.48 + 1.08 - 0.500 = 3.06$.

**The direction of the classifier's update.** With $q_0 = 1 - q_1$, the derivative of $\Ucal$ in $q_1$ is
$\Lcal_1 - \Lcal_0 + \log(q_1 / q_0) = 2.3 + \log 0.25 = 2.3 - 1.386 = 0.914$. It is positive, so
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> lowers $q_1$: the classifier moves further towards class 0.

**Where it stops.** The derivative is zero when $\log(q_1/q_0) = -2.3$, that is
$q = (1/(1 + e^{-2.3}),\; e^{-2.3}/(1 + e^{-2.3})) = (0.909, 0.091)$. There
$\Ucal^\star = -\log(e^{-3.1} + e^{-5.4}) = 3.004$, below the $3.06$ at the start.

The classifier does not become certain. A gap of 2.3 nats between the class bounds gives odds of $e^{2.3} \approx 10$ to
1.  The entropy term keeps it from claiming more confidence than the decoder supports. If the decoder ignored the label,
   $\Lcal(\xvec, 0) = \Lcal(\xvec, 1)$, the minimiser would be $q = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and the unlabelled point would
   teach the classifier nothing.

With 4 labels per class and the default seed, M2 labels 99% of the unlabelled points correctly after 400 steps and the

explains it, not by its distance to the few labelled points.

## Cost

Each unlabelled input costs $K$ passes through the encoder and decoder, one per class, against one pass for a labelled
input. Kingma and coauthors give the cost of an M2 update as $K$ times that of the plain VAE
<Cite id="kingma2014semi" />. For ten classes this is affordable; for hundreds it is not. Jang and coauthors replace the
sum over $y$ by a single reparameterised sample of $y$ with the Gumbel-softmax relaxation, which made training twice as
fast for 10 classes and 9.9 times as fast for 100, with the same classification accuracy on <Gloss name="mnist" />
<Cite id="jang2017" />.

In the figure, one M2 step takes about 4 ms, about six times the cost of a step of the classifier alone.

## Trade-offs

- **Against semi-supervised EM.** M2 keeps the logic of generative semi-supervised learning and drops the Gaussian class
  model. The decoder can describe curved or high-dimensional classes, and the classifier gives predictions for new
  inputs in one forward pass. The price is a bound instead of the exact likelihood, stochastic gradients instead of
  closed-form updates, and many more hyperparameters.
- **<NoteLink to="stacking">Stacking</NoteLink> and auxiliary variables.** On permutation-invariant MNIST with 100
  labels, M2 on raw pixels had 11.97% test error, and M1+M2 3.33% <Cite id="kingma2014semi" />. Maaløe and coauthors
  added an auxiliary latent variable that makes the classifier and encoder more expressive, and reached 0.96% on the
  same task <Cite id="maaloe2016auxiliary" />. The gains came from better inference networks, not from a different
  semi-supervised principle.
- **Against discriminative methods.** Semi-supervised VAEs model every pixel of every input, most of which is irrelevant
  to the class. <NoteLink to="consistency-regularisation">Consistency regularisation</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> spend no capacity on generation and lead the image
  benchmarks. Semi-supervised VAEs remain useful when the generative model is wanted for itself: conditional generation,
  style-content separation, or data where a decoder with the right structure is easy to write down.

## Pitfalls

- **A decoder that ignores the label.** If the code $\zvec$ alone can explain the inputs, the per-class bounds are equal
  and the unlabelled term carries no class information, as the worked example shows. A flexible decoder and a wide code
  make this likely. On two moons (200 points, 4 labels per class), whose classes one curve can trace end to end, the
  figure's networks with $\alpha/N = 0.1$ learned decoders that were nearly the same for both labels, and M2 labelled
  70% of the unlabelled points correctly against 91% for the classifier alone.
- **Classes attached the wrong way round.** The unlabelled term is symmetric in the class names. If the labelled terms
  are too weak, the model can learn the right clusters and name them backwards. On the figure's data with
  $\alpha/N = 0.1$, Kingma and coauthors' setting, seed 1 still reaches 93%, but seed 5 learns both waves and names them
  the wrong way round: 5% accuracy, with a labelled cross-entropy of 5.7 nats. At $\alpha/N = 5$ both seeds exceed 98%.
  Small problems need a larger $\alpha$ than the paper's.
- **The class prior is fixed.** M2 uses a fixed $\pivec$, usually uniform. If the unlabelled data are imbalanced, the
  entropy term and the uniform prior push the classifier towards balanced predictions.
- **Bounds are not accuracies.** A lower $\Jcal^\alpha$ means a better generative fit.
  <NoteLink to="model-selection">Model selection</NoteLink> by the bound can choose a model with a worse classifier, so
  <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> and hyperparameters should use held-out labelled points.
