<Definition>
A **semi-supervised generative adversarial network** trains a classifier with $K + 1$ outputs as the discriminator of a
<NoteLink to="generative-adversarial-network">GAN</NoteLink> <Cite id="goodfellow2014" />: one output per real class and
one for "generated" <Cite id="salimans2016" /> <Cite id="odena2016semi" />. Labelled points train it as an ordinary
$K$-class classifier. Unlabelled points train it to give low probability to "generated", and the generator's samples to
give high probability to it. With the logit of "generated" fixed at 0 and $Z(\xvec) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} e^{l_k(\xvec)}$ for
the class logits $l_k$, the probability that $\xvec$ is real is $D(\xvec) = Z(\xvec) / (Z(\xvec) + 1)$. Salimans and
coauthors trained the generator by **feature matching**: it matches the mean of an intermediate layer of the
discriminator on real and generated points.
</Definition>

## Problem

The <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-variational-autoencoders">semi-supervised VAE</NoteLink> learns from unlabelled data
by modelling them: it needs a decoder that explains every input, and it pays for every pixel the classifier does not
need. A GAN's discriminator already learns features of the data without labels, because telling real from generated
points requires them. The semi-supervised GAN makes that discriminator the classifier itself, so the features learned
from unlabelled data serve the classification directly. The method keeps the generative idea of using $p(\xvec)$ and
drops the explicit density: the unlabelled points enter only through the real-versus-generated game.

## Mechanism

### The discriminator's loss

Let the discriminator output logits $l_1(\xvec), \dots, l_{K+1}(\xvec)$ and class probabilities
$p(y = k \mid \xvec) = e^{l_k} / \sum_j e^{l_j}$. Adding the same function to every logit leaves the probabilities
unchanged, so $l_{K+1} = 0$ can be fixed without loss. Then $p(y = K+1 \mid \xvec) = 1/(Z + 1)$, and
$1 - p(y = K + 1 \mid \xvec) = Z/(Z + 1) = D(\xvec)$ is the discriminator of the ordinary GAN.

Assume half the training batch is real and half generated. The cross-entropy of the $(K+1)$-class classifier splits into
two parts <Cite id="salimans2016" />:

$$
L_{\text{sup}} = -\expect_{(\xvec, y) \sim L}\brack{\log p(y \mid \xvec, y \le K)}, \qquad
L_{\text{unsup}} = -\expect_{\xvec \sim U}\brack{\log D(\xvec)} - \expect_{\zvec}\brack{\log\paren{1 - D(G(\zvec))}}.
$$

$L_{\text{sup}}$ is the ordinary $K$-class cross-entropy of the labelled points, since $p(y \mid \xvec, y \le K)$ is the
softmax of $l_1, \dots, l_K$ alone. $L_{\text{unsup}}$ is the GAN game value with $D = Z/(Z + 1)$. The discriminator
minimises $L_{\text{sup}} + L_{\text{unsup}}$. The unlabelled points never say which class they belong to; they say only
that they belong to some real class, which raises $Z$ at their positions.

The two losses agree on what the best discriminator is. Both are minimised by
$e^{l_k(\xvec)} = c(\xvec)\, p(y = k, \xvec)$ for $k \le K$ and $e^{l_{K+1}(\xvec)} = c(\xvec)\, p_G(\xvec)$, for any
positive function $c$ <Cite id="salimans2016" />. So adding $L_{\text{unsup}}$ does not move the optimum of the
supervised problem; it adds information about where the data lie.

### The generator

The generator is trained either on the usual GAN loss or by feature matching. With $\fvec(\xvec)$ the activations of an
intermediate layer of the discriminator, feature matching minimises

$$
\norm{\expect_{\xvec \sim U} \fvec(\xvec) - \expect_{\zvec} \fvec(G(\zvec))}_2^2 .
$$

Salimans and coauthors found that feature matching gave much better classifiers than a generator trained to produce
realistic samples, even though the latter's samples looked better <Cite id="salimans2016" />.

### Why a bad generator helps

Dai and coauthors explained this result. If the generator were perfect, $p_G = p$, the generated points would carry no
information that the unlabelled points do not: for every optimum of the supervised objective there is an optimum of the
$(K+1)$-class objective with the same class predictions, so the unsupervised term cannot rule out a poor classifier
<Cite id="dai2017bad" />. What helps is a generator whose samples fall where the data are sparse, which they call a
**complement generator**. The discriminator must call those regions "generated", so the class logits are pushed down
there, and the boundary between real classes is forced into them. This is
<NoteLink to="low-density-separation">low-density separation</NoteLink> by another route: the generated points mark the
low-density regions, and the discriminator learns not to put a real class there. Feature matching works because its
samples fill the gaps between the classes rather than reproduce the classes.

Two methods of the same year use the same game. Odena proposed the $(K+1)$-class discriminator independently and named
it the semi-supervised GAN, SGAN <Cite id="odena2016semi" />. Springenberg's CatGAN has a $K$-way discriminator that
should be certain on real points and uncertain on generated ones, an entropy criterion close to
<NoteLink to="entropy-minimisation">entropy minimisation</NoteLink> <Cite id="springenberg2016catgan" />.

## Worked example

Take $K = 2$ classes and one unlabelled real point with class logits $l = (2, -1)$.

**Probabilities.** $Z = e^2 + e^{-1} = 7.389 + 0.368 = 7.757$. The three probabilities are $e^2/(Z + 1) = 0.844$,
$e^{-1}/(Z + 1) = 0.042$ and $1/(Z + 1) = 0.114$ for "generated". The point is real with probability
$D = 7.757/8.757 = 0.886$.

**Loss and gradient for the real point.** Its unsupervised loss is $-\log D = 0.121$. The derivative of $-\log D$ in
$l_k$ is $-(1 - D)\, e^{l_k}/Z$. With $e^{l_k}/Z = (0.953, 0.047)$, the derivatives are $-0.109$ and $-0.005$.
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> raises both logits, mostly the larger: the unlabelled point
pushes the classifier to be more confident in the class it already favours.

**Loss and gradient for a generated point.** Take a generated point with logits $(0.5, 0.3)$. Then
$Z = 1.649 + 1.350 = 2.999$, $D = 0.750$, and the loss $-\log(1 - D) = \log(Z + 1) = 1.386$. The derivative of
$\log(Z + 1)$ in $l_k$ is $D\, e^{l_k}/Z = 0.750 \times (0.550, 0.450) = (0.412, 0.338)$. Gradient descent lowers both
class logits, so the region around the generated point drifts towards "generated".

Together the two updates raise $Z$ where the data are and lower it where the generator puts its samples. When those
samples lie between the classes, the boundary between class 1 and class 2 runs through a region claimed by "generated".

At the defaults (500 steps, seed 1) the labels-only classifier labels 85% of the unlabelled points correctly, the
feature-matching GAN 95% and the complement generator 93%. Over seeds 1 to 6 at 600 steps the averages are 84% with
labels only, 89% with feature matching, 85% with the non-saturating generator and 91% with the complement generator. The
generator that tries hardest to imitate the data helps least.

## Cost

Each update trains two networks. The discriminator sees the labelled batch, an unlabelled batch and a generated batch;
the generator needs a forward pass through itself and the discriminator. In the figure one step costs about 3.5 ms
against 0.6 ms for the classifier alone. GAN training also needs more tuning than a classifier: Salimans and coauthors
used weight normalisation and Gaussian noise on the output of each layer of the discriminator on <Gloss name="mnist" />
<Cite id="salimans2016" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Results.** On permutation-invariant MNIST with 100 labels, the feature-matching GAN made 93 errors in 10,000 test
  images; on <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> with 4,000 labels its error was 18.6% <Cite id="salimans2016" />. Dai and
  coauthors trained the generator towards a complement distribution and added a conditional entropy term to the
  discriminator, and reached 14.4% on CIFAR-10 <Cite id="dai2017bad" />.
- **Against the generative semi-supervised family.** Unlike
  <NoteLink to="generative-semi-supervised-learning">semi-supervised EM</NoteLink> and M2, the semi-supervised GAN has
  no likelihood and makes no explicit assumption about the shape of the classes. Its assumption is low-density
  separation, enforced through generated points in the gaps.
- **Good samples and good classifiers conflict.** A generator that matches $p(\xvec)$ makes the unsupervised term
  uninformative. The same model cannot be the best sampler and the best semi-supervised classifier
  <Cite id="dai2017bad" />.
- **Against later methods.** <NoteLink to="consistency-regularisation">Consistency regularisation</NoteLink> methods
  such as <NoteLink to="fixmatch">FixMatch</NoteLink> reach lower error on the same image benchmarks with one network
  and no adversarial game <Cite id="sohn2020" />, so semi-supervised GANs are now used mainly when a generator is wanted
  as well.

## Pitfalls

- **An adversarial game to tune.** <Gloss name="learning-rate">Learning rates</Gloss>, the balance of updates and the
  architecture of both networks matter, and the classifier's accuracy can oscillate while the game settles. The Player
  in the figure shows the accuracy moving from checkpoint to checkpoint.
- **Collapsed generators.** A generator whose samples collapse onto a few places marks only those places as generated,
  and the rest of the low-density space stays unclaimed. Dai and coauthors added a term that raises the entropy of the
  generator for this reason <Cite id="dai2017bad" />.
- **The complement is hard to construct in high dimensions.** In two dimensions, sampling a box and discarding points
  near the data gives a complement generator directly. In image space no such box exists, which is why the generator is
  learned and the complement is defined in the discriminator's feature space.
- **Sample quality is not evidence of classifier quality.** The <NoteLink to="inception-score">Inception
  score</NoteLink> or a visual check of the samples says nothing about the classifier; only held-out labelled data do.
