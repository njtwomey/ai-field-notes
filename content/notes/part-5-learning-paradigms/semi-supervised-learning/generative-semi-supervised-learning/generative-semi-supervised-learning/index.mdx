<Definition>
**Generative semi-supervised learning** fits a <NoteLink to="weak-supervision-benchmarks">joint model</NoteLink>
$p(\xvec, y \mid \thetavec) = \pi_y\, p(\xvec \mid y, \thetavec)$ of inputs and labels to $\ell$ labelled pairs and $u$
unlabelled inputs at once. A labelled pair contributes its joint log-likelihood $\log p(\xvec_i, y_i \mid \thetavec)$.
An unlabelled input contributes its marginal log-likelihood $\log \sum_y p(\xvec_j, y \mid \thetavec)$, in which the
missing label is summed out. The parameters are fitted by
<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation-maximisation</NoteLink> with the missing labels as the latent
variables, and the classifier is the posterior $p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec)$ <Cite id="nigam2000em" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A classifier fitted to a handful of labels has high variance. With two labelled points per class, the class means of a
<NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">linear discriminant</NoteLink> are each the average of two points, and the
boundary can sit almost anywhere between the classes. Unlabelled points are often plentiful, but they carry information
about $p(\xvec)$ only. They can improve the classifier only if the model ties $p(\xvec)$ to $p(y \mid \xvec)$, which is
the role of the <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-assumptions">semi-supervised assumptions</NoteLink>.

A generative classifier makes that tie explicit. Its input density is the mixture

$$
p(\xvec \mid \thetavec) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, p(\xvec \mid y = k, \thetavec),
$$

so the same parameters $\thetavec$ that set the class posteriors also set the shape of $p(\xvec)$. Fitting the mixture
to the unlabelled points pins down the components; the labels then say which component is which class. Castelli and
Cover showed that when the mixture is identifiable, labelled points are needed only to name the components, and the
error then falls exponentially fast in the number of labels <Cite id="castelli1995exponential" />.

The method sits between two older ones. A supervised generative classifier
(<Gloss name="linear-discriminant-analysis" />, <Gloss name="qda" />, <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink>)
fits the same model to the labelled points alone. A <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture
model</NoteLink> fits it to all points with every label missing, and the labels are attached afterwards
(cluster-then-label). Semi-supervised EM keeps the model of the first and the data of the second, and lets both kinds of
point shape the fit from the first iteration <Cite id="zhu2009intro" />.

## Mechanism

### The objective

Let $L$ index the labelled points and $U$ the unlabelled ones, with $K$ classes. Write
$p_k(\xvec) = p(\xvec \mid y = k, \thetavec)$ for the class-conditional density. The objective is the log-likelihood of
everything observed:

$$
J(\thetavec) = \sum_{i \in L} \log \pi_{y_i}\, p_{y_i}(\xvec_i)
+ \lambda \sum_{j \in U} \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, p_k(\xvec_j).
$$

The weight $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ scales the unlabelled term. With $\lambda = 1$, $J$ is the plain likelihood. With
$\lambda = 0$, $J$ is the supervised likelihood and the unlabelled points are ignored. Nigam and coauthors introduced
intermediate values as EM-$\lambda$ <Cite id="nigam2000em" />.

The first sum splits into one term per class and has a closed-form maximiser. The second sum has a logarithm of a sum,
which does not split. EM handles it by treating the labels of $U$ as latent variables.

### The two steps

Collect the class probabilities of each point in responsibilities $r_{ik} \in [0, 1]$ with $\sum_k r_{ik} = 1$.

- **E-step.** For an unlabelled point, $r_{jk} = \pi_k p_k(\xvec_j) / \sum_m \pi_m p_m(\xvec_j)$, its posterior under
  the current parameters. For a labelled point, $r_{ik} = 1$ if $k = y_i$ and 0 otherwise. The labelled responsibilities
  are clamped: they never change.
- **M-step.** Refit each class from all points, each weighted by $w_i r_{ik}$, where $w_i = 1$ for labelled and
  $w_i = \lambda$ for unlabelled points.

For Gaussian classes with one shared covariance (the model behind <Gloss name="latent-dirichlet-allocation" />), the
M-step is a set of weighted averages. With $N_k = \sum_i w_i r_{ik}$ and $N = \sum_i w_i$,

$$
\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{N}, \qquad
\muvec_k = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_i w_i r_{ik}\, \xvec_i, \qquad
\Sigmamat = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_i w_i r_{ik}\, (\xvec_i - \muvec_k)(\xvec_i - \muvec_k)^\top.
$$

These are the formulas of EM for a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture</NoteLink>, with two changes:
labelled points enter with hard, fixed responsibilities, and unlabelled points are down-weighted by $\lambda$. The start
is the fit to the labelled points alone, so iteration 0 is the supervised classifier.

<Derivation title="Why the M-step is a weighted average">
Replace each unknown indicator $[y_j = k]$ of an unlabelled point by its expectation $r_{jk}$ under the current
parameters. The expected complete-data log-likelihood is then

$$
Q(\thetavec) = \sum_i \sum_{k} w_i r_{ik} \paren{\log \pi_k - \tfrac12 \log \det(2\pi\Sigmamat)
- \tfrac12 (\xvec_i - \muvec_k)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec_i - \muvec_k)}.
$$

Setting the gradient in $\muvec_k$ to zero gives $\sum_i w_i r_{ik} \Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec_i - \muvec_k) = \zeros$, so
$\muvec_k$ is the weighted mean. Maximising over $\pivec$ under $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$ with a Lagrange multiplier gives
$\pi_k \propto N_k$. Differentiating in $\Sigmamat^{-1}$, with
$\partial \log\det \Amat / \partial \Amat = \Amat^{-\top}$, gives the weighted pooled scatter. Each $w_i$ multiplies a
whole term of $Q$, so the weighted problem has the same solution as an unweighted one in which point $i$ is repeated
$w_i$ times.
</Derivation>

Each iteration never decreases $J$. The monotonicity argument of
<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation-maximisation</NoteLink> applies term by term: the unlabelled term is
$\lambda$ times a marginal likelihood whose E-step bound touches it, and the labelled term has no latent variable and
needs no bound <Cite id="dempster1977" />.

## Worked example

Take one input dimension, two classes with known unit variances and equal weights $\pi_0 = \pi_1 = \tfrac12$, so that
only the means $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ are fitted. One point is labelled in each class: $x = -1$ in class 0 and $x = 1$ in
class 1. Five points are unlabelled: $-2$, $0.5$, $2$, $3$ and $4$.

**Start.** The labelled-only fit is $\mu_0 = -1$, $\mu_1 = 1$. With equal variances and weights, the log-odds of class 1
at $x$ is $(\mu_1 - \mu_0)\,x - (\mu_1^2 - \mu_0^2)/2 = 2x$, so the boundary is at $x = 0$.

**E-step.** The posterior of class 1 is the logistic function of the log-odds, $r_{j1} = 1/(1 + e^{-2x_j})$. For the
five unlabelled points this gives $0.018$, $0.731$, $0.982$, $0.998$ and $1.000$. The point at $0.5$ is assigned to
class 1 with probability $0.73$.

**M-step.** Class 1 collects the labelled point at 1 with weight 1 and each unlabelled point with weight $r_{j1}$:
$N_1 = 1 + 0.018 + 0.731 + 0.982 + 0.998 + 1.000 = 4.728$, and the weighted sum of positions is
$1 + (-2)(0.018) + (0.5)(0.731) + (2)(0.982) + (3)(0.998) + (4)(1.000) = 10.285$. So $\mu_1 = 10.285 / 4.728 = 2.175$.
Class 0 collects weight $N_0 = 1 + 0.982 + 0.269 + 0.018 + 0.002 + 0.000 = 2.272$ and positions summing to $-2.785$, so
$\mu_0 = -1.226$.

**Next E-step.** The boundary $(\mu_0 + \mu_1)/2$ has moved from $0$ to $0.475$. The point at $0.5$ now has $r = 0.522$,
almost undecided.

**Convergence.** Repeating the two steps converges to $\mu_0 = -1.005$ and $\mu_1 = 2.333$, with the boundary at
$0.664$. The point at $0.5$ ends with $r = 0.367$: it has changed class. The three unlabelled points at 2, 3 and 4
pulled the class-1 mean to the right, and the boundary followed them into the gap between $-2$ and $2$. The labelled
pair alone put the boundary at 0, midway between two points; the unlabelled points moved it to where the data are
sparse.

## Cost

One iteration costs $O((\ell + u)\,K d^2)$ for the responsibilities and scatter matrices in $d$ dimensions, plus
$O(K d^3)$ to factor the covariances. The number of iterations is the larger cost. EM converges at a rate set by the
fraction of missing information, and with a few labels among many unlabelled points almost all the label information is
missing. The figure's runs take 15 to 60 iterations to settle, where EM on the fully labelled data would need one.

## Trade-offs

- **The model must be right.** Unlabelled data move the parameters towards the best fit of $p(\xvec)$, not towards the
  best classifier. When the classes really are the model's shape, the two coincide and the unlabelled points cut the
  variance of the fit. When they are not, the best fit of $p(\xvec)$ can put the boundary in the wrong place, and more
  unlabelled data make that boundary more certain. Cozman and coauthors showed that unlabelled data can then raise the
  error while more labelled data would lower it. In their asymptotic analysis the maximum-likelihood estimate converges
  to a limit that depends on the ratio of labelled to unlabelled points: under a wrong model the unlabelled data change
  the bias of the estimator, not only its variance <Cite id="cozman2003mixture" />.
- **Hedging with $\lambda$.** A small $\lambda$ keeps the labelled points in charge and lets the unlabelled ones adjust
  the fit. Nigam and coauthors chose $\lambda$ by <Gloss name="loo">leave-one-out cross-validation</Gloss> on the
  labelled data. On their web page data the best $\lambda$ was larger when there were fewer labels: with very few
  labels, heavy reliance on the unlabelled data helped despite the model's errors, and with more labels it hurt. With
  the tuned $\lambda$, large unlabelled sets no longer degraded accuracy <Cite id="nigam2000em" />.
- **A more flexible model.** Giving each class several mixture components, as
  <NoteLink to="mixture-discriminant-analysis">mixture discriminant analysis</NoteLink> does, lets a class be
  multimodal. Nigam and coauthors used this with naive Bayes for text classes made of several sub-topics
  <Cite id="nigam2000em" />. The cost is more parameters to fit from the same few labels.
- **Where it is used.** Mixture models fitted by semi-supervised EM suit low-dimensional data with a credible parametric
  form, and <NoteLink to="text-classification">text classification</NoteLink> with naive Bayes, where Nigam and
  coauthors cut the error by up to 30% <Cite id="nigam2000em" />. For images and other high-dimensional inputs the
  class-conditional density is not Gaussian or multinomial in any useful sense. There the generative route continues
  through deep latent-variable models (<NoteLink to="semi-supervised-variational-autoencoders">semi-supervised
  variational autoencoders</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="semi-supervised-generative-adversarial-networks">semi-supervised
  GANs</NoteLink>), and the strongest methods since about 2020 are discriminative:
  <NoteLink to="pseudo-labelling">pseudo-labelling</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="fixmatch">consistency
  regularisation</NoteLink> on top of
  <NoteLink to="self-supervised-pretraining-for-semi-supervised-learning">self-supervised pretraining</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="sohn2020" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Misspecification looks like success.** The objective $J$ rises at every iteration whether or not accuracy does. On
  the skewed data set in the figure, the objective per point climbs while accuracy falls from 94% to 75%. Only held-out
  labelled data reveal the damage; the note on <NoteLink to="when-semi-supervised-learning-fails">when semi-supervised
  learning fails</NoteLink> collects the cases.
- **Local maxima.** EM converges to a stationary point near its start. Starting from the labelled-only fit keeps the
  components attached to the right classes, but with one label per class the start is poor and the run can end with a
  component that covers parts of two classes.
- **Degenerate covariances.** A covariance estimated from a few labelled points can be singular, and per-class
  covariances can collapse onto a single point. A ridge on the starting covariance and a small floor on every variance
  prevent both.
- **<NoteLink to="class-weights">Class weights</NoteLink> follow the unlabelled data.** The M-step estimates $\pi_k$
  mostly from the unlabelled points. If the unlabelled pool has different class proportions from the deployment data, or
  contains classes absent from the labels, the priors and the boundary shift with it.
- **Text-specific behaviour does not transfer.** Results with multinomial naive Bayes on documents depend on the
  smoothing of word counts and on the length of the documents. They are not evidence that EM helps for a Gaussian model
  of other data.
