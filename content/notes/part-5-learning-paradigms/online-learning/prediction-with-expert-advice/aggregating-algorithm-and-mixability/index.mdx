<Definition>
Let $\lambda(\gamma, y)$ be the loss of prediction $\gamma \in \Gamma$ on outcome $y \in \Ycal$. The loss is
**$\eta$-mixable**, for $\eta > 0$, if for every finite set of predictions $\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_N$ and weights
$\wvec \in \Delta_N$ there is a prediction $\gamma \in \Gamma$ with

$$
\lambda(\gamma, y) \le -\frac1\eta\ln\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\,e^{-\eta\lambda(\gamma_i, y)} \quad\text{for every } y \in \Ycal.
$$

The **aggregating algorithm** (AA) keeps weights $w_{t,i} \propto \pi_ie^{-\eta L_{t-1,i}}$, where $\pi$ is a prior
over experts and $L_{t-1,i}$ expert $i$'s cumulative loss, and predicts a $\gamma_t$ that satisfies this inequality for
the experts' current predictions <Cite id="vovk1998" />.
</Definition>

## Notation

Expert $i$ predicts $\gamma_{t,i}$ in round $t$ and has cumulative loss
$L_{t,i} = \sum_{s \le t}\lambda(\gamma_{s,i}, y_s)$. The learner's cumulative loss is
$\hat L_T = \sum_t\lambda(\gamma_t, y_t)$. The right side of the mixability inequality, as a function of $y$, is the
**generalised prediction** $g_t(y) = -\frac1\eta\ln\sum_iw_{t,i}e^{-\eta\lambda(\gamma_{t,i}, y)}$. It is a soft minimum
of the experts' losses on outcome $y$: it lies between $\min_i\lambda(\gamma_{t,i}, y)$ and the weighted average of the
losses.

## The regret bound

Let $W_t = \sum_i\pi_ie^{-\eta L_{t,i}}$, with $W_0 = 1$. The weights are $w_{t,i} = \pi_ie^{-\eta L_{t-1,i}}/W_{t-1}$,
so

$$
\sum_iw_{t,i}e^{-\eta\lambda(\gamma_{t,i}, y_t)} = \frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}}.
$$

Mixability says the learner's loss in round $t$ is at most $-\frac1\eta\ln(W_t/W_{t-1})$. Summing over rounds, the
logarithms telescope:

$$
\hat L_T \le -\frac1\eta\ln W_T \le -\frac1\eta\ln\paren{\pi_ie^{-\eta L_{T,i}}} = L_{T,i} + \frac{\ln(1/\pi_i)}{\eta}
$$

for every expert $i$. With a uniform prior, $\hat L_T \le \min_iL_{T,i} + (\ln N)/\eta$ <Cite id="vovk1998" />.

The bound does not grow with $T$. Compare <NoteLink to="hedge-and-exponential-weights">Hedge</NoteLink>, whose analysis
has an extra $\eta T/8$ term: for linear losses no single prediction achieves the soft minimum, so each round adds
$\eta/8$ to the bound. For a mixable loss the learner can always find a prediction at least as good as the soft minimum,
and the per-round cost disappears. The <NoteLink to="lower-bounds-for-online-learning">$\sqrt{T}$ lower bound</NoteLink>
does not apply to mixable losses.

## Log loss: the Bayesian mixture

Let the prediction be a distribution $q$ over $\Ycal$ and $\lambda(q, y) = -\ln q(y)$. With $\eta = 1$,
$e^{-\lambda(q_i, y)} = q_i(y)$, and the mixability condition asks for a $q$ with $q(y) \ge \sum_iw_iq_i(y)$ for all
$y$. The mixture $q = \sum_iw_iq_i$ satisfies it with equality. So <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log
loss</NoteLink> is $1$-mixable, and the AA predicts the mixture of the experts' distributions.

The weights $w_{t,i} \propto \pi_i\prod_{s<t}q_{s,i}(y_s)$ are the posterior probabilities of the experts given the
outcomes so far, by <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem" />. The AA under log loss is Bayesian model averaging, and the regret
bound says the Bayesian predictive distribution loses at most $\ln(1/\pi_i)$ nats more than any model $i$ in the prior,
on every sequence. No assumption that any model is true is needed. Because each round's prediction is the posterior
predictive, the product of the predictions equals the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal
likelihood</NoteLink>, and the telescoping above is the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>
$p(y_{1:T}) = \prod_tp(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1})$. <NoteLink to="sequential-bayesian-updating" /> covers the same recursion
for continuous parameters, where the regret against the best parameter grows as $\tfrac{d}{2}\ln T$ rather than staying
constant.

## Square loss

Take binary outcomes $y \in \set{0, 1}$, predictions $\gamma \in [0, 1]$ and $\lambda(\gamma, y) = (\gamma - y)^2$. This
loss is $2$-mixable, and a valid prediction is

$$
\gamma = \frac12 + \frac{g(0) - g(1)}{2},
$$

where $g$ is the generalised prediction with $\eta = 2$ <Cite id="vovk2001" />. The AA therefore has regret at most
$(\ln N)/2$ under square loss.

Predicting the weighted average $\sum_iw_i\gamma_i$ does not achieve this. Square loss on $[0, 1]$ is only
$\tfrac12$-exp-concave: $e^{-\alpha(\gamma - y)^2}$ is concave in $\gamma$ when $(\gamma - y)^2 \le 1/(2\alpha)$, that
is for $\alpha \le \tfrac12$. For an <NoteLink to="online-newton-step-and-exp-concavity">exp-concave loss</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="expectation">Jensen's inequality</NoteLink> applied to the concave function $e^{-\alpha\lambda(\cdot, y)}$
shows that the weighted average satisfies the mixability inequality with $\eta = \alpha$. Averaging gives regret
$2\ln N$; the AA's substitution gives $(\ln N)/2$, four times less.

## Worked example

**Log loss.** Three experts forecast the probability of $y = 1$ as $0.9$, $0.5$ and $0.2$ in every round. The outcomes
are $1, 1, 0, 1, 1$. The experts' cumulative log losses are $2.724$, $3.466$ and $6.661$ nats. The Bayesian mixture,
starting from a uniform prior, loses $3.420$ nats. Its regret against the best expert is $0.696$, below
$\ln 3 = 1.099$. The final posterior weights are $(0.669, 0.318, 0.013)$.

**Square loss.** Two experts with equal weight predict $0.2$ and $0.9$. With $\eta = 2$, the generalised prediction is
$g(0) = -\tfrac12\ln\tfrac12(e^{-0.08} + e^{-1.62}) = 0.2895$ and
$g(1) = -\tfrac12\ln\tfrac12(e^{-1.28} + e^{-0.02}) = 0.2317$. The AA predicts
$\gamma = 0.5 + (0.2895 - 0.2317)/2 = 0.5289$. Its losses, $0.2797$ if $y = 0$ and $0.2219$ if $y = 1$, are below $g(0)$
and $g(1)$. The average $0.55$ would lose $0.3025 > g(0)$ if $y = 0$, so it violates the $2$-mixability inequality.

## Absolute loss is not mixable

Take $\lambda(\gamma, y) = \abs{\gamma - y}$ with $y \in \set{0, 1}$, two experts predicting $0$ and $1$, and equal
weights. The generalised prediction is $c = -\frac1\eta\ln\tfrac12(1 + e^{-\eta})$ for both outcomes. Because
$\ln$ is strictly concave, $\ln\tfrac12(1 + e^{-\eta}) > \tfrac12(0 - \eta)$, so $c < \tfrac12$. Mixability would need
$\gamma \le c$ (for $y = 0$) and $1 - \gamma \le c$ (for $y = 1$), so $1 \le 2c < 1$, which is impossible. No
$\eta > 0$ works, and for absolute loss the $\sqrt{T\ln N}$ regret of Hedge is the best possible
<Cite id="haussler1998" />.

## Assumptions

- The loss must be mixable for some $\eta > 0$. The largest such $\eta$ is the loss's **mixability constant**, and the
  regret bound is $(\ln N)/\eta$ with that constant.
- The AA must compute a prediction that satisfies the inequality. For log loss and square loss there is a closed form;
  for other losses it is a small optimisation problem each round.
- The bound compares with the best expert. It holds for every sequence, with no probabilistic assumptions.

## Pitfalls

- **Averaging is not the aggregating algorithm.** For square loss the weighted average has a four times larger bound.
  The two coincide only for log loss.
- **The regret against an expert depends on its prior mass.** With a non-uniform prior the regret against expert $i$ is
  at most $\ln(1/\pi_i)/\eta$. With infinitely many experts, as with a continuous parameter, the prior density near the
  best expert sets the regret.
- **Constant regret needs a fixed comparator.** Against a sequence of experts that switches, the AA's weights
  concentrate and it adapts slowly, like Hedge. Fixed share applies to the AA as well (see
  <NoteLink to="tracking-the-best-expert" />).
