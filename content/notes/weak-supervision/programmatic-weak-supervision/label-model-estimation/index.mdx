<Definition>
A **label model** is a latent-variable model $p(\lambdavec, Y)$ of the outputs $\lambdavec = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$ of
$m$ <Gloss name="labelling-function">labelling functions (LFs)</Gloss> and the unobserved true label $Y$. **Label model estimation** recovers its parameters,
chiefly each LF's accuracy and the dependencies between LFs, from the unlabelled label matrix alone. The main methods are
maximum likelihood by sampling, pseudolikelihood for dependency structure <Cite id="bach2017" />, matrix completion on the
inverse covariance <Cite id="ratner2019" />, and closed-form triplet solutions <Cite id="fu2020" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The original <Gloss name="data-programming">data-programming</Gloss> label model is fitted by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> on the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>, with
<NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink> for the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> of models with dependencies <Cite id="ratner2016" />. This is slow and must be rerun
every time a user edits an LF. It also needs the dependency structure between LFs, which users rarely know. Three lines
of work address these problems: learning the structure, replacing sampling by linear algebra, and replacing iteration by
closed forms.

## Mechanism 1: learning the dependency structure

Bach, He, Ratner and Ré select dependencies by maximising an $\ell_1$-regularised **marginal pseudolikelihood**
<Cite id="bach2017" />. For each LF $j$ in turn, they model the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> of $\lambda_j$ given all other
LF outputs, with $Y$ summed out, as a <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> containing candidate dependencies between $j$ and every other LF.
Each conditional involves only one observed variable and the latent $Y$, so its gradient is exact and needs no sampling.
The $\ell_1$ penalty zeroes most candidate dependency weights; those that survive a threshold are kept. The amount of
unlabelled data needed to recover the true structure scales sublinearly in the number of possible dependencies for a
broad class of models. In simulations the method was 100 times faster than a maximum-likelihood approach and selected
one quarter as many extraneous dependencies. On real information-extraction applications it gave an average 1.5 F1
points of improvement <Cite id="bach2017" />.

Varma et al. later recover the structure from the observable covariance of the LF outputs, whose inverse is the sum
of a sparse matrix (the dependencies) and a low-rank matrix (the effect of the hidden $Y$), separated by robust <Gloss name="pca" />
<Cite id="varma2019" />.

## Mechanism 2: matrix completion (Snorkel MeTaL)

Ratner et al.'s multi-task label model recovers accuracies from second moments <Cite id="ratner2019" />. Let $\psi(O)$ be
<NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">indicator variables</NoteLink> for the outputs of the LFs (the observable cliques) and $\psi(S)$ the indicator of $Y$ (the separator).
Write their joint covariance and its inverse in blocks:

$$
\Sigmamat = \cov\LB\psi(O \cup S)\RB = \begin{pmatrix}\Sigmamat_O & \Sigmamat_{OS} \\ \Sigmamat_{OS}^\top & \Sigmamat_S\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\Kmat = \Sigmamat^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}\Kmat_O & \Kmat_{OS} \\ \Kmat_{OS}^\top & \Kmat_S\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$\Sigmamat_O$ is observable from the label matrix. $\Sigmamat_S$ is the variance of the class indicator, a function of the
class balance. $\Sigmamat_{OS}$, the covariance between each LF and the true label, is unobservable and holds the
accuracies.

**Step 1: a rank-one correction.** The block-inversion formula gives

$$
\Kmat_O = \Sigmamat_O^{-1} + c\,\Sigmamat_O^{-1}\Sigmamat_{OS}\Sigmamat_{OS}^\top\Sigmamat_O^{-1}, \qquad
c = \LP\Sigmamat_S - \Sigmamat_{OS}^\top\Sigmamat_O^{-1}\Sigmamat_{OS}\RP^{-1} > 0.
$$

With $\zvec = \sqrt{c}\,\Sigmamat_O^{-1}\Sigmamat_{OS}$ this reads

$$
\Kmat_O = \Sigmamat_O^{-1} + \zvec\zvec^\top.
$$

**Step 2: zeros from the graph.** In a Gaussian or discrete graphical model with a junction tree, the inverse
covariance of the clique indicators is zero between variables that share no edge. So $(\Kmat_O)_{ij} = 0$ for every pair
of LFs $(i, j) \in \Omega$ that are conditionally independent given $Y$. On those entries

$$
0 = (\Sigmamat_O^{-1})_{ij} + z_iz_j, \qquad (i, j) \in \Omega.
$$

$\Sigmamat_O^{-1}$ is known, so this is a rank-one matrix completion problem for $\zvec$, solved by minimising
$\norm{\Sigmamat_O^{-1} + \zvec\zvec^\top}_\Omega$ over the known-zero entries.

**Step 3: back out the accuracies.** From the definition of $\zvec$, $\Sigmamat_{OS} = \Sigmamat_O\zvec/\sqrt{c}$. Substituting
into the definition of $c$ gives $1/c = \Sigmamat_S - \zvec^\top\Sigmamat_O\zvec/c$, so $c = (1 + \zvec^\top\Sigmamat_O\zvec)/\Sigmamat_S$.
The accuracies follow from $\Sigmamat_{OS}$ and the observed means.

**Identifiability.** Taking logs of the squared equations gives a linear system $\log z_i^2 + \log z_j^2 = \log(\Sigmamat_O^{-1})_{ij}^2$
over $(i, j) \in \Omega$. It determines every $z_i^2$ when the graph of independent pairs contains an odd cycle, the
smallest being a triangle: three mutually conditionally independent LFs. The signs are determined within each connected
component of that graph up to one global flip, fixed by assuming the LFs are better than random on average
<Cite id="ratner2019" />.

**Worked example.** Three independent LFs with outputs in $\set{-1, +1}$, no abstentions and balanced classes have
$\cov(\lambda_i, Y) = a_i = (0.6, 0.5, 0.3)$. Then $\Sigmamat_O$ has unit diagonal and off-diagonal entries $a_ia_j$:
$0.30$, $0.18$ and $0.15$. Its inverse has off-diagonal entries $-0.3133$, $-0.1549$ and $-0.1102$. Solving
$z_iz_j = 0.3133, 0.1549, 0.1102$ gives $\zvec = (0.664, 0.472, 0.233)$. Then $c = 1 + \zvec^\top\Sigmamat_O\zvec = 1.995$ and
$\Sigmamat_{OS} = \Sigmamat_O\zvec/\sqrt{c} = (0.6, 0.5, 0.3)$: the covariances with the unseen label are recovered exactly.
As a check, $\Sigmamat_O^{-1} + \zvec\zvec^\top$ is diagonal.

The multi-task version handles LFs that label different, related sub-tasks at different granularities. On three
fine-grained classification problems it gained 20.2 accuracy points on average over a traditional supervised
approach, 6.8 over majority vote and 4.1 over a <NoteLink to="weak-supervision">weak-supervision</NoteLink> method that models tasks separately
<Cite id="ratner2019" />.

## Mechanism 3: triplets in closed form (FlyingSquid)

Fu et al. observe that for binary tasks the needed moments have closed forms <Cite id="fu2020" />. Encode each LF as
observed variables $v_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ (abstentions are split into a pair of variables), and let $a_i = \expect[v_iY]$,
the accuracy on the $[-1, 1]$ scale.

**The triplet identity.** If $v_i \perp v_j \mid Y$, then $v_iY \perp v_jY$. Because $Y^2 = 1$,

$$
a_ia_j = \expect[v_iY]\,\expect[v_jY] = \expect[v_iv_jY^2] = \expect[v_iv_j].
$$

The product of two accuracies is the observable agreement rate. For three mutually conditionally independent
variables, the three equations $a_ia_j = M_{ij}$, $a_ia_k = M_{ik}$, $a_ja_k = M_{jk}$ with $M_{ij} = \expect[v_iv_j]$ give

$$
\abs{a_i} = \sqrt{\abs{\frac{M_{ij}\,M_{ik}}{M_{jk}}}}, \qquad
\abs{a_j} = \sqrt{\abs{\frac{M_{ij}\,M_{jk}}{M_{ik}}}}, \qquad
\abs{a_k} = \sqrt{\abs{\frac{M_{ik}\,M_{jk}}{M_{ij}}}}.
$$

Each variable appears in many triplets; FlyingSquid averages (or takes the median of) the estimates over all of them,
and resolves signs by assuming better-than-random sources. The accuracies then map linearly to the label model's clique
marginals, and inference uses the junction tree. There is no iteration and no step size to tune.

FlyingSquid matched or exceeded previous label models on benchmark tasks (by up to 4.9 F1 points), learnt parameters
170 times faster on average, and up to 4,000 times faster on video tasks with temporal dependencies. Its speed allows
an online, windowed version that updates the label model while the end model trains, which outperformed majority vote
by up to 15.7 F1 points <Cite id="fu2020" />.

## Cost

| Method | Needs | Cost |
| ------ | ----- | ---- |
| Maximum likelihood with Gibbs sampling | structure given | iterative, sampling at every step |
| Pseudolikelihood structure learning | candidate dependencies | one convex fit per LF |
| Matrix completion (MeTaL) | structure given | one inversion of $\Sigmamat_O$ and a small non-convex fit |
| Triplets (FlyingSquid) | structure given | one pass for $\Mmat$, then closed form |

All second-moment methods need one pass over the label matrix to form $\Mmat$ or $\Sigmamat_O$, $O(nm^2)$, after which the
cost is independent of $n$.

## Trade-offs

- **The structure must be right.** Every method needs to know which LFs are conditionally independent. Assuming
  <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> for correlated LFs inflates their estimated accuracies, as the figure shows. Structure learning reduces
  but does not remove this risk.
- **Enough independent LFs.** Identifiability needs triangles of conditionally independent LFs with overlapping
  coverage. Sparse LFs that rarely vote together give noisy moment estimates, and a near-zero $M_{jk}$ in a denominator
  makes the triplet estimate unstable.
- **Moments versus likelihood.** Moment methods are fast and need little or no iterative optimisation, but they use
  only pairwise agreement statistics and can be statistically less efficient than a well-converged
  likelihood fit.
- **In practice.** On the WRENCH benchmark no label model dominates, and majority vote remains a strong baseline; see
  <NoteLink to="weak-supervision-benchmarks" />.
