<Definition>
After <NoteLink to="snorkel" />, the same group and others extended programmatic supervision in four directions: running
it on industrial data at scale (Snorkel DryBell), generating the <Gloss name="labelling-function">labelling functions</Gloss> automatically (Snuba), programming
other parts of the training pipeline with the same kind of user-written functions (transformation functions for
augmentation, slicing functions for critical subsets), and using <Gloss name="llm">large language models</Gloss> as labelling functions. The
survey by Zhang et al. organises the field around these components <Cite id="zhang2022survey" />.
</Definition>

## Snorkel DryBell at Google

Bach et al. deployed <NoteLink to="weak-supervision">weak supervision</NoteLink> on three classification tasks at Google <Cite id="bach2019" />. Snorkel DryBell
kept Snorkel's three stages and changed three things.

1. **Flexible ingestion of organisational knowledge.** Labelling functions were written from templates over existing
   resources: source heuristics, content heuristics, internal models (topic models, named-entity recognisers) and
   knowledge graphs.
2. **Cross-feature production serving.** Many useful signals are **non-servable**: too slow, too expensive or unavailable
   when a prediction is needed, such as aggregate statistics, crawler results or large internal models. DryBell uses
   them only as labelling functions, to label training data for an end model defined over cheap **servable** features.
3. **Sampling-free, distributed execution.** The <NoteLink to="data-programming">generative label model</NoteLink> was reimplemented as a TensorFlow computation
   graph that needs no <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink>, and labelling functions run in a MapReduce pipeline. Weak supervision over more
   than 6 million data points ran in under 30 minutes.

**Results.** In topic classification, ten labelling functions over 684,000 unlabelled points matched a model trained on
80,000 hand labels and came within 4.6 F1 points of one trained on 175,000. In product classification, after a change
of category invalidated the existing <NoteLink to="complementary-labels">negative labels</NoteLink>, eight labelling functions matched 12,000 hand labels and came
within 5.1 F1 points of 50,000. In real-time event classification, 140 sources defined over non-servable features
trained a deep network over real-time event features; compared with an unweighted combination of the sources, it
identified 58% more events of interest with a 4.5% quality improvement on an internal metric. Across the content tasks,
the non-servable sources gave an average 52% gain in final F1 <Cite id="bach2019" />.

The deployment's lesson is that the most valuable weak supervision in an organisation is often knowledge that already
exists in a form that cannot be served, and weak supervision is a way to transfer it into a servable model.

## Snuba: generating labelling functions

Writing labelling functions still takes expert time, much of it spent on repetitive choices such as thresholds and
text patterns. Varma and Ré's **Snuba** (also called Reef) generates them from a small labelled set and a large
unlabelled set <Cite id="varma2018" />. Each iteration runs three components.

- **Synthesiser.** Fits many simple models, such as decision stumps, <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regressions</NoteLink> and nearest-neighbour rules,
  over small subsets of user-defined **primitives** (bag-of-words features, bounding-box attributes). Each heuristic
  abstains where its confidence is low, trading coverage for accuracy.
- **Pruner.** Ranks candidates by a weighted average of their F1 on the labelled set and their diversity, the Jaccard
  distance between the unlabelled points they label and those the committed set already labels, and adds the best one
  to the committed set.
- **Verifier.** Fits a label model to the committed heuristics and uses the small labelled set to test whether their
  noise is still within what the label model can handle. Generation stops when it is not.

Snuba generated heuristics in under five minutes that performed up to 9.74 F1 points better than the best known
user-defined heuristics developed over many days, and outperformed semi-supervised learning by up to 14.35 F1 points
<Cite id="varma2018" />.

## Transformation functions: learning augmentation (TANDA)

Data augmentation is another place where domain knowledge enters as small functions: rotate an image, swap a word for
a synonym. Composing them well is hard, because a sequence of individually safe transformations can destroy the label.
Ratner et al.'s **TANDA** has users write **transformation functions** and learns a generative sequence model over
them, trained adversarially on unlabelled data so that transformed points stay within the data distribution. The
learnt model is robust to misspecified functions. It improved accuracy by 4.0 points on <Gloss name="cifar-10" />, 1.4 F1 points on the
ACE relation-extraction task and 3.4 points on a medical imaging data set over standard heuristic augmentation
<Cite id="ratner2017tanda" />. See <NoteLink to="data-augmentation" />.

## Slicing functions: critical subsets

Aggregate metrics hide failures on small subsets that matter: cyclists in autonomous driving, questions in dialogue.
Chen et al.'s **slice-based learning** lets users write **slicing functions**, heuristics that mark membership of a
critical subset, in the same style as labelling functions <Cite id="chen2019slice" />. The model learns a
slice-expert representation for each slice on a shared backbone, and an attention mechanism combines them according to
the predicted slice membership. The approach improved on baselines by up to 19.0 F1 on slices and 4.6 F1 overall, with
a parameter-efficient representation.

## Language models as labelling functions

A prompted large language model is a labelling function that anyone can write in natural language. Smith et al. ask
the model several distinct questions about each example, map each answer to a vote or an abstention, and denoise the
votes with the Snorkel label model before training an end classifier <Cite id="smith2022" />. On the WRENCH benchmark
this reduced errors by 19.5% on average relative to zero-shot prompting, and matched or exceeded classifiers trained
from hand-engineered rules. Arora et al.'s **Ask Me Anything** applies the same idea to prompting itself: several
imperfect <Gloss name="qa">question-answering</Gloss> prompts give noisy votes with different accuracies and dependencies, and weak supervision
combines them. It lifted performance by 10.2% on average over few-shot prompting, and let the 6-billion-parameter GPT-J
match or exceed few-shot <Gloss name="gpt-3" /> (175 billion parameters) on 15 of 20 benchmarks <Cite id="arora2022" />. See <NoteLink
to="prompting" />.

## Common threads

- **Code as supervision.** Labelling functions, transformation functions and slicing functions all let a domain expert
  program the training data rather than label it, and all need a model that tolerates the functions' errors.
- **Servability.** Supervision may use any signal available at training time; the end model uses only what is
  available at prediction time.
- **The label model is replaceable.** Every system above treats label aggregation as a module, from Gibbs-sampled <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor
  graphs</NoteLink> to TensorFlow graphs to closed forms (see <NoteLink to="label-model-estimation" />).
- **Evaluation needs a benchmark.** Reported gains depend heavily on the labelling functions used, which motivated the
  standardised comparisons in <NoteLink to="weak-supervision-benchmarks" />.
