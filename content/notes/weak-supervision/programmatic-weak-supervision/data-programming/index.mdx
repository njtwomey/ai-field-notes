<Definition>
**Data programming** is a paradigm for building training sets without hand labelling, introduced by Ratner, De Sa, Wu,
Selsam and Ré <Cite id="ratner2016" />. Users write $m$ **labelling functions** (LFs)
$\lambda_i : \Xcal \to \set{-1, 0, +1}$, each encoding a heuristic that labels some inputs and **abstains** (outputs 0)
on the rest. A **generative label model** $p(\Lambda, Y)$ over the LF outputs $\Lambda$ and the true label $Y$ is fitted
to unlabelled data, and its posterior $p(Y \mid \Lambda = \lambdavec(\xvec))$ serves as a probabilistic training label
for a discriminative **end model**.
</Definition>

## Labelling functions

A labelling function is ordinary code. For relation extraction from biomedical text, one LF labels a gene–disease
pair positive if it appears in a curated knowledge base; another labels it negative if the text between the mentions
matches "not cause"; a third combines a pattern with a second knowledge base <Cite id="ratner2016" />. LFs may be
wrong, may overlap, and may conflict. A manual annotation is a special case: an LF that looks up the hand label. The
user's effort moves from labelling thousands of examples to writing tens of functions.

The LF outputs on $n$ unlabelled points form the **label matrix** $\Lmat \in \set{-1, 0, +1}^{n \times m}$. Majority
vote over each row is the simplest aggregation. It ignores that some LFs are more accurate than others and that some are
near-copies of each other.

## The label model

In the simplest model, LFs label independently given the true class. LF $i$ labels a point (does not abstain) with
probability $\beta_i$ and, when it labels, is correct with probability $\alpha_i$. With balanced classes
<Cite id="ratner2016" />,

$$
\mu_{\alphavec, \betavec}(\Lambda, Y) = \frac{1}{2}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\LP\beta_i\alpha_i\indicator[\Lambda_i = Y] + \beta_i(1 - \alpha_i)\indicator[\Lambda_i = -Y] + (1 - \beta_i)\indicator[\Lambda_i = 0]\RP.
$$

The parameters are fitted by maximising the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> of
the observed LF outputs, summing out $Y$:

$$
(\hat{\alphavec}, \hat{\betavec}) = \argmax_{\alphavec, \betavec}\ \sum_{\xvec \in S}\log\sum_{y' \in \set{-1, 1}}\mu_{\alphavec, \betavec}(\lambdavec(\xvec), y').
$$

This is the Dawid–Skene model with LFs in place of annotators, one accuracy per LF, and abstentions; see <NoteLink
to="dawid-skene-model" />. Ratner et al. solve it by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>.

## Why agreements identify accuracies

The label model is fitted without a single true label. The information comes from how often LFs agree.

Let $a_i = \expect[\lambda_i Y]$, the LF's accuracy on the $[-1, 1]$ scale weighted by its coverage. Under the model,
$\expect[\lambda_i \mid Y] = \beta_i\alpha_i Y - \beta_i(1 - \alpha_i)Y = \beta_i(2\alpha_i - 1)Y$, so
$a_i = \beta_i(2\alpha_i - 1)$. <NoteLink to="independence">Conditional independence</NoteLink> gives, for $i \ne j$,

$$
\expect[\lambda_i\lambda_j] = \expect\LB\expect[\lambda_i \mid Y]\,\expect[\lambda_j \mid Y]\RB = a_ia_j\,\expect[Y^2] = a_ia_j.
$$

The left-hand side is observable: it is the average of $\lambda_i\lambda_j$ over the unlabelled data, agreements
counting $+1$, disagreements $-1$ and abstentions 0. Each pair of LFs gives one equation in the unknowns $a_i$.

- **Two LFs are not enough.** One equation $a_1a_2 = \expect[\lambda_1\lambda_2]$ cannot separate $a_1$ from $a_2$: a
  very accurate LF paired with a mediocre one looks the same as two moderately accurate ones.
- **Three conditionally independent LFs suffice.** Three equations in three unknowns give
  $\abs{a_1} = \sqrt{\abs{\expect[\lambda_1\lambda_2]\,\expect[\lambda_1\lambda_3] / \expect[\lambda_2\lambda_3]}}$ and
  its permutations.
- **Signs need an assumption.** Flipping the signs of all $a_i$ together, which amounts to swapping the class names,
  leaves every product unchanged. Assuming the LFs are better than random on average, $a_i > 0$ on average, fixes the
  sign.
- **Coverage must overlap.** If two LFs never vote on the same point, $\expect[\lambda_i\lambda_j] = 0$ carries no
  information. Every LF must co-vote with others often enough to estimate the products.

$\beta_i$ is observable directly as the fraction of points on which LF $i$ votes, so the accuracy follows as
$\alpha_i = (1 + a_i/\beta_i)/2$. The closed-form **triplet method** built on this identity, and estimators that
handle correlated LFs, are the subject of <NoteLink to="label-model-estimation" />.

**Worked example.** Three LFs with coverage 1 have observed products $\expect[\lambda_1\lambda_2] = 0.30$,
$\expect[\lambda_1\lambda_3] = 0.18$ and $\expect[\lambda_2\lambda_3] = 0.15$. Then
$a_1 = \sqrt{0.30 \times 0.18 / 0.15} = 0.6$, $a_2 = 0.30/0.6 = 0.5$ and $a_3 = 0.18/0.6 = 0.3$. The accuracies are
$\alpha = (1 + a)/2 = (0.8, 0.75, 0.65)$.

## From the label model to training labels

For a point with LF outputs $\lambdavec$, the posterior under the independent model is a weighted vote. For balanced
classes, every non-abstaining LF adds its log-likelihood ratio:

$$
\log\frac{p(Y = 1 \mid \lambdavec)}{p(Y = -1 \mid \lambdavec)} = \sum_{i:\,\lambda_i \ne 0}\lambda_i\log\frac{\alpha_i}{1 - \alpha_i}.
$$

With accuracies $(0.9, 0.6, 0.6)$ and votes $(+1, -1, -1)$, majority vote says $-1$. The label model gives log-odds
$\log 9 - 2\log 1.5 = \log 4$, so $p(Y = 1 \mid \lambdavec) = 0.8$: one accurate LF outweighs two weak ones.

**Noise-aware training.** The end model $\wvec$ is trained on the expected loss under the label model's posterior
<Cite id="ratner2016" />:

$$
\hat{\wvec} = \argmin_{\wvec}\ \frac{1}{\abs{S}}\sum_{\xvec \in S}\expect_{(\Lambda, Y) \sim \mu_{\hat{\alphavec}, \hat{\betavec}}}\LB\log\LP1 + e^{-\wvec^\top f(\xvec)Y}\RP \,\Big|\, \Lambda = \lambdavec(\xvec)\RB + \rho\norm{\wvec}^2.
$$

For logistic loss this is <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> with soft targets. The end
model sees the features, so it generalises to points on which every LF abstains, and it can learn signals that no LF
encodes.

## Guarantees

Ratner et al. prove that, if the data are generated by some member of the model family, if the class label is
independent of the features given the LF outputs, and if the end-model learner has bounded generalisation risk
$\chi$, then with $m \ge 2000$ LFs and $\abs{S} \ge \frac{356}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{m}{3\epsilon}$ unlabelled points the
expected parameter errors are at most $m\epsilon^2$ <Cite id="ratner2016" />. Under the same conditions, the end
model's excess risk is at most $\chi + \frac{\epsilon}{27\rho}$. The theorem assumes $0.3 \le \beta_i \le 0.5$ and
$0.8 \le \alpha_i \le 0.9$. It uses $m \ge 2000$ only to simplify the statement. The error falls as
$\tilde{O}(\abs{S}^{-1/2})$ in the number of **unlabelled** points, the same rate that labelled data would give, while
the user supplies a fixed number of LFs.

## Dependencies between LFs

LFs written by the same person are often correlated. Ratner et al. let users declare dependencies of four types
<Cite id="ratner2016" />: **similar** (two LFs on near-identical inputs), **fixing** (one LF votes mainly to correct
another's mistakes), **reinforcing** (one LF votes mainly to confirm another) and **exclusive** (two LFs never vote
together). The label model becomes a <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink>
$\mu_{\thetavec}(\Lambda, Y) = Z_{\thetavec}^{-1}\exp(\thetavec^\top h(\Lambda, Y))$ with base factors $Y$,
$\Lambda_iY$, $\Lambda_i$, $\Lambda_i^2Y$ and $\Lambda_i^2$, plus one factor per declared dependency. Learning the
dependency structure automatically is covered in <NoteLink to="label-model-estimation" />.

## Results

On relation extraction in genomics, pharmacogenomics and news, data programming improved on a distant-supervision
baseline by 2.34 F1 points on average. On the 2014 TAC-KBP Slot Filling challenge it would have set a new winning score,
and with <Gloss name="lstm" /> features it scored 5.98 F1 points above a state-of-the-art LSTM baseline, which would
have placed second <Cite id="ratner2016" />. Data programming became the core of the <NoteLink to="snorkel" /> system.

## Assumptions and pitfalls

- **Conditional independence.** The independent model counts two copies of one heuristic as two independent votes, and
  their high agreement inflates both estimated accuracies. The figure in <NoteLink to="label-model-estimation" />
  shows this.
- **Better than random.** The sign of the solution is fixed by assuming LFs beat chance on average. A set dominated by
  adversarial or inverted LFs is learnt upside down.
- **Class balance.** The simple model assumes balanced classes. Imbalanced problems need a class prior in the model.
- **Coverage.** Points on which every LF abstains get the prior as their label. Training the end model only on covered
  points is common but discards data.
