<Definition>
**Learning from crowds** is the problem of training a classifier $f : \Xcal \to \Ycal$ when each training item
$\xvec_i$ has labels $y_i^1, \dots, y_i^R$ from $R$ annotators of unknown reliability and no gold label. The method of
Raykar et al. estimates the classifier, each annotator's reliability and the hidden true labels jointly by maximum
likelihood <Cite id="raykar2010" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two separate steps are the obvious approach: aggregate the labels (by majority vote or the <NoteLink
to="dawid-skene-model" />), then train a classifier on the aggregate. The aggregation step ignores the features. Yet a
classifier trained on the other items predicts a label from an item's features, and that prediction should
count when the annotators disagree. Joint estimation lets the classifier act as one more voter, weighted by how well
it fits.

## The model

Binary labels, $y_i \in \set{0, 1}$. The classifier is <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, $p_i = \pr(y_i = 1 \mid \xvec_i) = \sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec_i)$.
Annotator $j$ is a **two-coin** model with

$$
\alpha^j = \pr(y^j = 1 \mid y = 1) \ \text{(sensitivity)}, \qquad \beta^j = \pr(y^j = 0 \mid y = 0) \ \text{(specificity)}.
$$

Given the true label, annotators respond independently of each other and of $\xvec$. Define the probability of item
$i$'s responses under each true label:

$$
a_i = \prod_{j=1}^{R}(\alpha^j)^{y_i^j}(1 - \alpha^j)^{1 - y_i^j}, \qquad
b_i = \prod_{j=1}^{R}(\beta^j)^{1 - y_i^j}(1 - \beta^j)^{y_i^j}.
$$

Marginalising the true label, the likelihood is

$$
\pr(\text{data} \mid \wvec, \alphavec, \betavec) = \prod_{i=1}^{N}\LB a_i p_i + b_i(1 - p_i)\RB.
$$

## EM

Treat the true labels as missing data <Cite id="raykar2010" />.

**E-step.** The posterior probability that item $i$ is positive, given its features and responses, is

$$
\mu_i = \pr(y_i = 1 \mid y_i^1, \dots, y_i^R, \xvec_i) = \frac{a_i p_i}{a_i p_i + b_i(1 - p_i)}.
$$

**M-step.** The expected complete-data log-likelihood is
$\sum_i \mu_i\log(p_i a_i) + (1 - \mu_i)\log\LB(1 - p_i)b_i\RB$. Setting its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> to zero gives closed forms for the
annotators,

$$
\alpha^j = \frac{\sum_i \mu_i\,y_i^j}{\sum_i \mu_i}, \qquad \beta^j = \frac{\sum_i (1 - \mu_i)(1 - y_i^j)}{\sum_i (1 - \mu_i)},
$$

and, for the classifier, the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $\sum_i (\mu_i - \sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec_i))\xvec_i$. That is the gradient of
logistic regression with <NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">soft targets</NoteLink> $\mu_i$. The M-step for $\wvec$ is therefore an ordinary weighted logistic
regression, solved by Newton–Raphson. Any classifier that accepts soft labels can replace it.

**The posterior as a weighted vote.** Taking the log-odds of $\mu_i$,

$$
\operatorname{logit}(\mu_i) = \wvec^\top\xvec_i + c + \sum_{j=1}^{R} y_i^j\LB\operatorname{logit}(\alpha^j) + \operatorname{logit}(\beta^j)\RB,
\qquad c = \sum_{j=1}^{R}\log\frac{1 - \alpha^j}{\beta^j}.
$$

The estimated truth is a <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">linear combination</NoteLink> of the classifier's log-odds and the annotators' votes. Annotator $j$'s
weight is $\operatorname{logit}(\alpha^j) + \operatorname{logit}(\beta^j)$: zero for an annotator with
$\alpha^j + \beta^j = 1$ (a coin that ignores the item), and negative for one who is worse than that
<Cite id="raykar2010" />.

**Without features.** Dropping $\xvec$ and replacing $p_i$ by a prevalence $p$ recovers the binary Dawid–Skene model.
Raykar et al. also place beta priors on $\alpha^j$ and $\beta^j$, which keeps the estimates away from 0 and 1 for
annotators with few labels, and extend the model to multiclass, ordinal and regression targets.

## Item difficulty: GLAD

Dawid–Skene and Raykar et al. give each annotator one error model for all items. Whitehill et al.'s **GLAD** also
models how hard each item is <Cite id="whitehill2009" />. Labeller $i$ has expertise $\alpha_i \in (-\infty, \infty)$;
image $j$ has inverse difficulty $\beta_j \in (0, \infty)$. The probability of a correct label is

$$
\pr(L_{ij} = Z_j \mid \alpha_i, \beta_j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\alpha_i\beta_j}},
$$

with $Z_j$ the true label. As an image gets harder ($\beta_j \to 0$), every labeller approaches a <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flip</NoteLink>. A
labeller with $\alpha_i = 0$ is a coin flip on every image, and $\alpha_i < 0$ marks an adversarial labeller whose
labels are systematically wrong. The parameters are fitted by EM, with gradient ascent in the M-step. Whitehill et al.
report that GLAD outperforms majority vote and is robust to both noisy and adversarial labellers.

## Assumptions and pitfalls

- **<NoteLink to="independence">Conditional independence</NoteLink> of annotators.** Raykar et al. note that the assumption is not true in general: an
  advanced cancer that is easy to detect is labelled positive by almost every radiologist <Cite id="raykar2010" />.
  GLAD's difficulty parameter captures part of this dependence.
- **Local optima.** EM finds a local maximum. Starting from majority vote places it in the basin where annotators are
  better than random.
- **The classifier can overrule the crowd.** A classifier that fits the training set too well dominates the posterior
  and can override correct but minority labels. Regularising the classifier or its prior matters more here than in
  ordinary training.
- **Relation to programmatic labels.** Replace annotators by labelling functions that may abstain and the same
  latent-variable structure becomes the label model of <NoteLink to="data-programming" />.
