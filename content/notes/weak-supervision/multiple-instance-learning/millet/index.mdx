<Definition>
**MILLET** (Multiple Instance Learning for Locally Explainable Time series classification) is a framework for making
deep time-series classifiers inherently interpretable. A time series $X_i = \set{\xvec_i^1, \dots, \xvec_i^t}$ is a
multiple-instance **bag** whose **instances** are its $t$ time points, and the series label $Y_i$ is the bag label.
The global average pooling of the classifier is replaced by a MIL pooling that produces a prediction for each time
point as part of the forward pass. Early, Cheung, Cutajar, Xie, Kandola and Twomey proposed it and evaluated it on 85
UCR datasets <Cite id="early2024" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Deep time-series classifiers such as FCN, ResNet and InceptionTime share one structure <Cite id="early2024" />. A
feature extractor $\psi_{FE}$ (the **backbone**) maps a univariate series $X_i$ to one $d$-dimensional embedding per time
point, $\Zmat_i = [\zvec_i^1, \dots, \zvec_i^t] \in \reals^{t \times d}$. **Global average pooling** (GAP) averages the
embeddings and a classifier $\psi_{CLF}$ maps the average to class logits:

$$
\Zmat_i = \psi_{FE}(X_i), \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \psi_{CLF}\LP\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\zvec_i^j\RP.
$$

The authors identify two drawbacks. The per-time-point representations are averaged with equal weight, so their
localisation is lost. And the model gives no account of which stretches of the series (the **class-conditional
discriminatory motifs**) drove the prediction. Post-hoc explainers such as SHAP need hundreds of forward passes per
explanation.

Time-series classification has the same label structure as multiple-instance learning: labels exist for whole series
and not for time points. In MIL terms, GAP followed by a classifier is mean-embedding pooling, which produces no
instance-level predictions.

## The three requirements

A MILLET model must satisfy three requirements <Cite id="early2024" />.

1. **Time series as MIL bags.** Each time point is an instance; each series is a bag with the series label.
2. **Time-point predictions.** The model must output predictions for individual time points, inherently, not through
   a post-hoc method.
3. **Temporal ordering.** The model must respect the order of time points, unlike classical MIL, which treats
   instances as independent and identically distributed.

The backbones satisfy the first and third through their convolutions. The pooling satisfies the second.

## Mechanism: four MIL poolings

Each pooling takes the embeddings $\zvec_i^j$ and returns series logits $\hat{Y}_i \in \reals^{c}$ for $c$ classes. An
attention head $\psi_{ATTN}$ returns a weight $a_i^j \in [0, 1]$ per time point.

**Attention** (from attention-based MIL <Cite id="ilse2018" />) weights embeddings before classifying their average:

$$
a_i^j = \psi_{ATTN}(\zvec_i^j), \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \psi_{CLF}\LP\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t} a_i^j \zvec_i^j\RP.
$$

**Instance** (from mi-Net <Cite id="wang2018mi" />) classifies every time point and averages the predictions:

$$
\hat{\yvec}_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(\zvec_i^j) \in \reals^{c}, \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\hat{\yvec}_i^j.
$$

**Additive** (from Additive MIL <Cite id="javed2022" />) classifies attention-weighted embeddings:

$$
\hat{\yvec}_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(a_i^j \zvec_i^j), \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\hat{\yvec}_i^j.
$$

**Conjunctive** (new in MILLET) applies the attention head and the classifier to the embeddings in parallel, then
scales each time point's prediction by its attention:

$$
a_i^j = \psi_{ATTN}(\zvec_i^j), \qquad \hat{\yvec}_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(\zvec_i^j), \qquad
\hat{Y}_i = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t} a_i^j\,\hat{\yvec}_i^j.
$$

The name says that a discriminatory time point must be judged important by both heads. Because the classifier cannot
rely on the attention head to rescale the embeddings first, the two heads are trained in parallel rather than in
sequence, which the authors expect to make the model more robust.

In the paper's architecture the attention head is two linear layers with an internal dimension of 8, a $\tanh$ and a
sigmoid. A sigmoid replaces the softmax of attention-based MIL because the series can be long. The classifier is one
linear layer. The poolings output logits; a softmax is applied afterwards.

## Where the interpretations come from

Each pooling yields its interpretation from quantities computed in the forward pass <Cite id="early2024" />.

- **Instance** gives class scores per time point, $\hat{\yvec}_i \in \reals^{t \times c}$. A positive score for class
  $k$ at time $j$ supports class $k$; a negative score refutes it.
- **Additive** and **Conjunctive** give time-point predictions and attention. They are combined into
  $\hat{\yvec}_i^{*} = [a_i^1\hat{\yvec}_i^1, \dots, a_i^t\hat{\yvec}_i^t] \in \reals^{t \times c}$.
- **Attention** gives only the weights $a_i \in [0, 1]^t$. They measure importance for all classes at once and do not
  say which class a time point supports.

For Conjunctive the interpretation is exact: the series logit for class $k$ is the average of
$a_i^j \hat{y}_{i,k}^j$, so the interpretation is a decomposition of the logit into per-time-point terms.

**Instance pooling and CAM.** With a linear classifier $\psi_{CLF}(\zvec) = \Wmat\zvec + \bvec$, averaging is linear,
so

$$
\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}(\Wmat\zvec_i^j + \bvec) = \Wmat\LP\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\zvec_i^j\RP + \bvec.
$$

Instance pooling computes the same series logits as GAP followed by the same layer. Its time-point scores
$\Wmat\zvec_i^j + \bvec$ are, up to the bias, the class activation map (CAM) of the GAP model. The paper records the
consequence that Instance has exactly as many parameters as GAP. The Instance models in the paper differ from the GAP
models through the three enhancements below, not through the pooling function itself.

## Model design

MILLET was applied to three backbones, FCN, ResNet and InceptionTime <Cite id="ismailfawaz2020" />, each producing
embeddings of size $d = 128$. Four poolings on three backbones give 12 new models <Cite id="early2024" />. Three
enhancements were added.

1. **Positional encoding.** Fixed sinusoidal encodings are added to the embeddings after feature extraction, so that
   the time-point heads can use position. Under GAP this information would be averaged away. See
   <NoteLink to="positional-encoding" />.
2. **Replicate padding.** Zero padding in the convolutions made the interpretations favour the start and end of the
   series, because padding with zeros created a false signal. Padding with the boundary value removed the bias.
3. **Dropout.** Dropout with $p = 0.1$ after the positional encoding, to limit overfitting in the new heads.

As in the original InceptionTime, each model is an ensemble of five networks trained from different initialisations.
All models were trained with Adam at a fixed learning rate of 0.001 for 1500 epochs with cross-entropy loss, without
hyperparameter tuning.

## Evaluation of interpretations

Two metrics score an interpretation, following Early et al.'s earlier work on MIL interpretability
<Cite id="early2024" />.

- **AOPCR** needs no time-point labels. Order the time points by importance for class $c$, remove them most relevant
  first, and average the drop in the model's class-$c$ output over the removals. Subtract the same quantity for a
  random ordering (averaged over three random orderings). A positive value means the interpretation found time points
  that the prediction depends on. For speed, time points are removed in blocks of 5% of the length, up to 50%.
- **NDCG@n** needs time-point labels. If $n$ time points are truly discriminatory, it scores how many of them appear in
  the first $n$ places of the ordering, with a $1/\log_2(j + 1)$ discount at rank $j$. See <NoteLink to="ndcg" />.

The UCR datasets have no time-point labels, so the authors built a synthetic dataset, **WebTraffic**, in which the
discriminatory locations are known. Each series is a week at a 10-minute sample rate ($t = 1008$) with daily and weekly
seasonality. Class 0 has no signature. Classes 1 to 9 inject a signature: Spikes, Flip, Skew, Noise, Cutoff, Average,
Wander, Peak and Trough. Every signature except Spikes occupies one random window of 36 to 288 time points. The
training and test sets each hold 500 series, 50 per class.

MILLET had the best AOPCR and NDCG@n for every backbone, except NDCG@n on InceptionTime, where CAM scored 0.707 against
0.704. The authors attribute this to sparsity: MILLET's explanations may cover only part of a discriminatory region,
such as the start and end of a Cutoff window. SHAP, run with 500 samples, scored negative AOPCR on two backbones, which
is worse than a random ordering. MILLET was over 800 times faster than SHAP.

**UCR predictive performance.** On 85 univariate UCR datasets <Cite id="dau2019" />, Conjunctive was the best pooling
averaged over backbones, raising accuracy from $0.841 \pm 0.009$ (GAP) to $0.846 \pm 0.009$ <Cite id="early2024" />.

Conjunctive InceptionTime had the best balanced accuracy, above Hydra-MR and HIVE-COTE 2 (HC2). HC2 is a
meta-ensemble of several classifiers and is listed for reference only. On the other metrics it was competitive but not
the best. For every backbone, Conjunctive pooling improved on GAP in accuracy and balanced accuracy. AUROC rose or
stayed equal, and NLL fell for FCN and ResNet but rose slightly for InceptionTime, from 1.078 to 1.085. Against
InceptionTime, HC2 and Hydra-MR, Conjunctive InceptionTime won or drew on 48, 49 and 52 of the 85 datasets.

**UCR interpretability.** Without time-point labels only AOPCR applies. Averaged over backbones, the best MILLET
AOPCR was 6.00 against 5.71 for GAP with CAM <Cite id="early2024" />. The authors name Conjunctive the best pooling
for interpretability; in their appendix table it has the highest mean AOPCR for the ensembled models (5.756), while
Additive has the highest for single models (5.977). Attention
pooling performed poorly, as expected from interpretations that are not class-specific. A trade-off appeared: more
complex backbones predicted better and explained worse. MILLET dominated GAP on the Pareto front for FCN and
InceptionTime but not for ResNet, where the ensembled models lost interpretability.

**Cost.** The attention head adds at most 0.4% to the parameter count. On the UCR Fish dataset, training time rose by up
to 6.0% and inference time by up to 7.5% relative to GAP. A SHAP explanation for the same model took over 6 seconds.

## Trade-offs

- **Sparse explanations.** MILLET tends to highlight a few time points of each motif. Sparsity helps when explaining
  long series and improves AOPCR, but can lower NDCG@n when the ground truth is a whole region.
- **Attention alone is not enough.** Attention weights are not class-specific and scored worst among the poolings on
  interpretability; the class-specific poolings (Instance, Additive, Conjunctive) are the ones to use for explanations.
- **Small training sets.** Conjunctive InceptionTime was worse than GAP InceptionTime on UCR datasets with few training
  series and better on large ones.
- **Scope.** The paper covers univariate series of fixed length. Variable lengths need no change. Multivariate series
  would need interpretations per channel as well as per time point.
- **Components are confounded.** An ablation on three datasets found that each addition (MIL pooling, positional
  encoding, replicate padding, dropout, ensembling) improved mean balanced accuracy, but the gains depend on the
  order in which the components were added.
