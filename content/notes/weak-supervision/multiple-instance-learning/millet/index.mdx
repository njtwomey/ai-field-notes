<Definition>
**MILLET** (Multiple Instance Learning for Locally Explainable Time series classification) is a framework for making
deep time-series classifiers inherently interpretable <Cite id="early2024" />. A time series
$X_i = \set{\xvec_i^1, \dots, \xvec_i^t}$ is a multiple-instance **bag** whose **instances** are its $t$ time points,
and the series label $Y_i$ is the bag label. The global average pooling of the classifier is replaced by a <NoteLink to="attention-based-multiple-instance-learning">MIL pooling</NoteLink>
that produces a class score for each time point as part of the forward pass. Those scores are the explanation.
</Definition>

## Problem

Deep time-series classifiers such as FCN, <Gloss name="resnet" /> and InceptionTime share one structure <Cite id="ismailfawaz2020" />. A
feature extractor $\psi_{FE}$ (the **backbone**) maps a univariate series $X_i$ to one $d$-dimensional embedding per time
point, $\Zmat_i = [\zvec_i^1, \dots, \zvec_i^t] \in \reals^{t \times d}$. **Global average pooling** (GAP) averages the
embeddings and a classifier $\psi_{CLF}$ maps the average to class logits:

$$
\Zmat_i = \psi_{FE}(X_i), \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \psi_{CLF}\LP\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\zvec_i^j\RP.
$$

The average gives every time point equal weight, so the model's output says nothing about which stretches of the series
(the **discriminatory motifs**) drove the prediction. Post-hoc explainers such as <Gloss name="shap" /> recover this by perturbing the
input, at a cost of hundreds of forward passes per explanation, and their answer is an approximation of the model.

Time-series classification has the label structure of <NoteLink to="multiple-instance-learning">multiple-instance
learning</NoteLink>: labels exist for whole series and not for time points. In MIL terms, GAP followed by a classifier
is mean-embedding pooling, which produces no instance-level predictions. MIL offers poolings that do.

## Mechanism

### Time series as bags

Three properties make a MILLET model. Each time point is an instance and each series a bag with the series label. The
model outputs a prediction for every time point as part of its forward pass, not through a post-hoc method. And the
model respects temporal order, unlike classical MIL, which treats instances as exchangeable. The convolutional backbone
supplies the first and third: each embedding $\zvec_i^j$ summarises a window of the series around time $j$. The pooling
supplies the second.

### Four MIL poolings

Each pooling takes the embeddings $\zvec_i^j$ and returns series logits $\hat{Y}_i \in \reals^{c}$ for $c$ classes. An
attention head $\psi_{ATTN}$ returns a weight $a_i^j \in [0, 1]$ per time point.

**Attention** (from attention-based MIL <Cite id="ilse2018" />) weights embeddings before classifying their average:

$$
a_i^j = \psi_{ATTN}(\zvec_i^j), \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \psi_{CLF}\LP\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t} a_i^j \zvec_i^j\RP.
$$

**Instance** (from mi-Net <Cite id="wang2018mi" />) classifies every time point and averages the predictions:

$$
\hat{\yvec}_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(\zvec_i^j) \in \reals^{c}, \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\hat{\yvec}_i^j.
$$

**Additive** (from Additive MIL <Cite id="javed2022" />) classifies attention-weighted embeddings:

$$
\hat{\yvec}_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(a_i^j \zvec_i^j), \qquad \hat{Y}_i = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\hat{\yvec}_i^j.
$$

**Conjunctive** (introduced with MILLET) applies the attention head and the classifier to the embeddings in parallel,
then scales each time point's prediction by its attention:

$$
a_i^j = \psi_{ATTN}(\zvec_i^j), \qquad \hat{\yvec}_i^j = \psi_{CLF}(\zvec_i^j), \qquad
\hat{Y}_i = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t} a_i^j\,\hat{\yvec}_i^j.
$$

The name says that a time point must be judged important by both heads to matter. In Additive pooling the classifier
sees embeddings already rescaled by attention. In Conjunctive pooling it does not, so each head must be informative on
its own. The attention head ends in a sigmoid rather than the softmax of attention-based MIL: a softmax over thousands
of time points would drive every weight towards zero.

### Where the interpretations come from

Each pooling yields its interpretation from quantities computed in the forward pass <Cite id="early2024" />.

- **Instance** gives class scores per time point, $\hat{\yvec}_i \in \reals^{t \times c}$. A positive score for class
  $k$ at time $j$ supports class $k$; a negative score refutes it.
- **Additive** and **Conjunctive** give time-point predictions and attention. They are combined into
  $\hat{\yvec}_i^{*} = [a_i^1\hat{\yvec}_i^1, \dots, a_i^t\hat{\yvec}_i^t] \in \reals^{t \times c}$.
- **Attention** gives only the weights $a_i \in [0, 1]^t$. They measure importance for all classes at once and do not
  say which class a time point supports.

For Instance and Conjunctive pooling the interpretation is exact: the series logit for class $k$ is the average of the
time-point terms $\hat{y}_{i,k}^j$ or $a_i^j \hat{y}_{i,k}^j$, so the interpretation is a decomposition of the logit
into per-time-point shares. Nothing is approximated, so the explanation is faithful by construction, as the terms of an
<NoteLink to="intrinsically-interpretable-models">additive model</NoteLink> are.

**Instance pooling and CAM.** With a linear classifier $\psi_{CLF}(\zvec) = \Wmat\zvec + \bvec$, averaging is linear,
so

$$
\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}(\Wmat\zvec_i^j + \bvec) = \Wmat\LP\frac{1}{t}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\zvec_i^j\RP + \bvec.
$$

Instance pooling computes the same series logits as GAP followed by the same layer, with the same parameters. Its
time-point scores $\Wmat\zvec_i^j + \bvec$ are, up to the bias, the class activation map (CAM) <Cite id="zhou2016" /> of
the GAP model. CAM is therefore already a MIL interpretation, and the gain from the other poolings comes from the
attention head.

### Design choices that affect the interpretation

- **Positional encoding.** Fixed sinusoidal encodings are added to the embeddings after the backbone, so that the
  time-point heads can use position. Under GAP this information would be averaged away. See
  <NoteLink to="positional-encoding" />.
- **Replicate padding.** Zero padding in the convolutions creates a false signal at both ends of the series, and the
  interpretations then favour the start and the end. Padding with the boundary value removes the bias.

### Evaluating the interpretations

A per-time-point interpretation is a ranking of time points, and it can be scored in two ways (see
<NoteLink to="evaluating-explanations">evaluating explanations</NoteLink>). **AOPCR** removes time points in order of
importance and measures how fast the class output falls, relative to random orderings; it needs no ground truth.
**NDCG@n** compares the ranking with the $n$ time points known to be discriminatory; see <NoteLink to="normalised-discounted-cumulative-gain" />. Real
benchmarks such as the UCR archive <Cite id="dau2019" /> have no time-point labels, so NDCG@n needs synthetic data in
which the motifs are injected at known positions. Early et al. built such a data set, WebTraffic, with one class per
kind of injected signature <Cite id="early2024" />.

## Cost

The attention head adds at most 0.4% to the parameter count, and Instance pooling adds nothing. The interpretation comes
from the same forward pass as the prediction, so explaining a series costs one forward pass instead of the hundreds
that a perturbation method needs. On WebTraffic, MILLET produced its interpretations over 800 times faster than SHAP,
and they were more faithful by AOPCR <Cite id="early2024" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Accuracy is kept.** Replacing GAP by a MIL pooling does not cost accuracy. On 85 <Gloss name="ucr-archive" /> data sets, Conjunctive pooling
  raised mean accuracy over GAP for each of the three backbones <Cite id="early2024" />.
- **Attention alone is not enough.** Attention weights are not class-specific. Of the four poolings, Attention gave the
  least useful interpretations; the class-specific poolings (Instance, Additive, Conjunctive) are the ones to use.
- **Sparse explanations.** MILLET tends to highlight a few time points of each motif, such as the two ends of a window.
  Sparsity helps AOPCR, since removing those few points changes the output, but lowers NDCG@n when the ground truth is a
  whole region.
- **Stronger backbones explain less.** Across backbones, the more complex ones predicted better and gave less faithful
  interpretations by AOPCR, so the choice of backbone trades accuracy against explanation quality <Cite id="early2024" />.
- **Scope.** The method was developed for univariate series. Variable lengths need no change. Multivariate series need
  interpretations per channel as well as per time point.
