<Definition>
**Attention-based MIL pooling** maps a bag of $K$ instance embeddings $\hvec_1, \dots, \hvec_K \in \reals^{M}$ to one
bag embedding

$$
\zvec = \sum_{k=1}^{K} a_k \hvec_k, \qquad
a_k = \frac{\exp\set{\wvec^\top \tanh(\Vmat \hvec_k)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\set{\wvec^\top \tanh(\Vmat \hvec_j)}},
$$

with trainable $\Vmat \in \reals^{L \times M}$ and $\wvec \in \reals^{L}$. A classifier maps $\zvec$ to the bag-label
probability $\theta(X) = \pr(Y = 1 \mid X)$. Ilse, Tomczak and Welling introduced it and trained the whole model by
maximising the Bernoulli log-likelihood of the bag labels <Cite id="ilse2018" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="multiple-instance-learning">multiple-instance</NoteLink> model must give the same output for any
ordering of a bag's instances, and must accept bags of any size. Max pooling encodes the standard MI assumption but
passes gradient to one instance per bag, so training is slow and brittle. Mean pooling passes gradient to every
instance but lets one positive instance be drowned by many negatives. Neither is trainable, and neither says which
instances matter.

## Mechanism

**Permutation-invariant bag functions.** A scoring function $S(X)$ of a set is permutation-invariant if and only if it
can be written as $S(X) = g\LP\sum_{\xvec \in X} f(\xvec)\RP$ for suitable $f$ and $g$ <Cite id="zaheer2017" />. Any
MIL model therefore has three parts: an instance transformation $f$, a symmetric pooling $\sigma$, and a bag-level
transformation $g$ <Cite id="ilse2018" />. There are two placements of the classifier.

- **Instance-level.** $f$ is an instance classifier returning a score per instance; $\sigma$ pools the scores; $g$ is
  the identity. The instance scores are directly interpretable, but an instance classifier trained without instance
  labels can be poor.
- **Embedding-level.** $f$ maps each instance to an embedding $\hvec_k$; $\sigma$ pools embeddings into $\zvec$; $g$
  classifies $\zvec$. This usually classifies bags better but loses per-instance scores.

Attention pooling is an embedding-level pooling that keeps a per-instance quantity: the weight $a_k$.

**Why the weighted sum is permutation-invariant.** Let $\phi(\hvec) = \exp\set{\wvec^\top\tanh(\Vmat\hvec)} > 0$. Then

$$
\zvec = \frac{\sum_k \phi(\hvec_k)\,\hvec_k}{\sum_k \phi(\hvec_k)}.
$$

The numerator and the denominator are both sums over the set, so both are unchanged by reordering. $\zvec$ is a
function of two set sums, $\sum_k \phi(\hvec_k)\hvec_k$ and $\sum_k \phi(\hvec_k)$, which is the form
$g(\sum_k f(\hvec_k))$ with $f(\hvec) = (\phi(\hvec)\hvec, \phi(\hvec))$ and $g$ the division. The theorem above
therefore covers it.

**Why the weights are normalised.** The weights are non-negative and sum to one, so $\zvec$ is a convex combination of
the embeddings and stays on their scale whatever the bag size. Without normalisation, a bag of 100 instances would
produce an embedding about ten times larger than a bag of 10.

**What the weights can express.** If $\wvec^\top\tanh(\Vmat\hvec)$ is the same for every instance, the weights are all
$1/K$ and attention reduces to mean pooling. If one instance's score exceeds the others by a large margin, its weight
tends to 1 and attention approaches max pooling of that instance's embedding. Training moves between these extremes as
the data require. The $\tanh$ lets the score take both signs.

**Gated attention.** $\tanh$ is nearly linear on $[-1, 1]$, which limits the relations the score can express. Ilse
et al. add a sigmoid gate with a second matrix $\Umat \in \reals^{L \times M}$ <Cite id="ilse2018" />:

$$
a_k \propto \exp\set{\wvec^\top\LP\tanh(\Vmat\hvec_k) \odot \operatorname{sigm}(\Umat\hvec_k)\RP}.
$$

## Worked example

A bag has three two-dimensional embeddings $\hvec_1 = (1, 0)$, $\hvec_2 = (0, 1)$, $\hvec_3 = (2, 1)$, and the attention
scores $\wvec^\top\tanh(\Vmat\hvec_k)$ come out as $(0.5, -1.0, 2.0)$. The softmax gives weights
$a = (0.175, 0.039, 0.786)$. The bag embedding is
$\zvec = 0.175\,(1, 0) + 0.039\,(0, 1) + 0.786\,(2, 1) = (1.747, 0.825)$. Mean pooling would give $(1, 0.667)$.
The third instance dominates the attention-pooled embedding, and its weight of 0.786 is the model's statement that it
is the key instance.

## Evidence

Ilse et al. used the same architecture and training settings as MI-Net <Cite id="wang2018mi" />. On the five classical
benchmarks (Musk1, Musk2, Fox, Tiger, Elephant) attention and gated attention were on a par with the best classical
methods: for example 0.892 and 0.900 accuracy on Musk1, against 0.874 for mi-SVM and 0.889 for mi-Graph
<Cite id="ilse2018" />. On bags of MNIST digits, labelled positive if they contain a 9, attention pooling reached
higher AUC than max, mean and MI-SVM baselines when there were few training bags. On the Colon Cancer histopathology
set, attention reached 0.904 accuracy and 0.968 AUC, against 0.860 and 0.940 for embedding-level mean pooling
<Cite id="ilse2018" />.

## Cost

The attention head adds $\Vmat$ and $\wvec$ ($L(M + 1)$ parameters, or $L(2M + 1)$ with gating) and one pass over the
instances. It is linear in the bag size, like mean pooling.

## Trade-offs

- **Weights are not class evidence.** A large $a_k$ says that instance $k$ shaped the bag embedding. It does not say
  which class the instance supports, or whether it argues for or against the prediction. In a multi-class problem the
  same weights serve every class. <NoteLink to="millet">MILLET</NoteLink> adds instance-level class predictions for
  this reason.
- **Instances are treated as independent.** The softmax ignores order and neighbourhood. For time series, images and
  text this discards structure, unless the embeddings already carry it (from convolutions or positional encodings).
- **Softmax over long bags.** Normalising across thousands of instances makes each weight tiny and the sum dominated
  by a few. Implementations for long sequences sometimes replace the softmax with an element-wise sigmoid.
