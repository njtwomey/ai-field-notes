<Definition>
In the case-control PU setting, $n_p$ positives are drawn from $p_p(\xvec) = p(\xvec \mid y = +1)$ and $n_u$ unlabelled
points from the marginal $p(\xvec) = \pi\,p_p(\xvec) + (1 - \pi)\,p_n(\xvec)$, with class prior $\pi$ known. The
**unbiased PU risk** (uPU) rewrites the classification risk using only these two samples. The **non-negative PU
risk** (nnPU) clips the part of it that estimates the negative-class risk at zero, which stops flexible models from
overfitting <Cite id="kiryo2017" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

For a decision function $g : \Xcal \to \reals$ and a margin loss $\ell(t, y)$, write the partial risks

$$
R_p^{+}(g) = \expect_{p_p}\LB\ell(g(\xvec), +1)\RB, \quad
R_p^{-}(g) = \expect_{p_p}\LB\ell(g(\xvec), -1)\RB, \quad
R_n^{-}(g) = \expect_{p_n}\LB\ell(g(\xvec), -1)\RB, \quad
R_u^{-}(g) = \expect_{p}\LB\ell(g(\xvec), -1)\RB.
$$

The risk that ordinary positive-negative (PN) learning estimates is

$$
R(g) = \pi R_p^{+}(g) + (1 - \pi) R_n^{-}(g).
$$

The term $R_n^{-}$ needs negatives, which PU data do not contain.

## Mechanism: the unbiased risk

Because $p = \pi p_p + (1 - \pi)p_n$, an expectation under $p$ splits into the two classes:

$$
R_u^{-}(g) = \pi\,\expect_{p_p}\LB\ell(g, -1)\RB + (1 - \pi)\,\expect_{p_n}\LB\ell(g, -1)\RB = \pi R_p^{-}(g) + (1 - \pi)R_n^{-}(g).
$$

Solving for the negative term, $(1 - \pi)R_n^{-}(g) = R_u^{-}(g) - \pi R_p^{-}(g)$. Substituting,

$$
R(g) = \pi R_p^{+}(g) - \pi R_p^{-}(g) + R_u^{-}(g).
$$

Every term is an expectation under $p_p$ or $p$, so replacing each by its sample mean gives an unbiased estimator
$\hat{R}_{pu}(g)$ <Cite id="duplessis2014" />. Positive examples appear twice: once as positives and once, with
negative weight, as "unlabelled points that are known not to be negatives".

**Which losses.** du Plessis, Niu and Sugiyama first used losses with $\ell(t, +1) + \ell(t, -1) = 1$, such as the ramp
loss, for which the estimator reduces to cost-sensitive classification of P against U; these losses are non-convex
<Cite id="duplessis2014" />. They then observed that if

$$
\ell(t, +1) - \ell(t, -1) = -t,
$$

the two positive terms combine into a linear one, $\pi R_p^{+} - \pi R_p^{-} = -\pi\,\expect_{p_p}[g(\xvec)]$, and the
objective $-\pi\,\expect_{p_p}[g(\xvec)] + \expect_{p}[\ell(g(\xvec), -1)]$ is convex whenever $\ell$ is. The squared
loss $(t - 1)^2/4$, the logistic loss and the **double hinge loss** $\max\set{0, (1 - t)/2, -t}$ satisfy the condition
<Cite id="duplessis2015" />.

## Mechanism: the non-negative risk

The population identity guarantees $R_u^{-}(g) - \pi R_p^{-}(g) = (1 - \pi)R_n^{-}(g) \ge 0$. Its empirical
counterpart $\hat{R}_u^{-}(g) - \pi\hat{R}_p^{-}(g)$ has no such guarantee. A flexible model can push $g$ to large
positive values on the training positives, which makes $\hat{R}_p^{-}$ large, and the subtraction then drives the
estimate negative. The model is rewarded for memorising the positives. Kiryo et al. observed this with a multilayer
perceptron separating even from odd MNIST digits: the uPU training risk kept falling and went negative while the test
risk rose <Cite id="kiryo2017" />.

nnPU replaces the subtraction by its positive part:

$$
\tilde{R}_{pu}(g) = \pi\hat{R}_p^{+}(g) + \max\set{0,\ \hat{R}_u^{-}(g) - \pi\hat{R}_p^{-}(g)}.
$$

The estimator is biased, but Kiryo et al. show that the bias decays exponentially as $n_p$ and $n_u$ grow, that the
estimator is consistent, and that under mild conditions its mean squared error is smaller than uPU's
<Cite id="kiryo2017" />.

**Training with mini-batches.** For each mini-batch, compute $r = \hat{R}_u^{-} - \pi\hat{R}_p^{-}$. If $r \ge -\beta$,
take an ordinary step on the uPU objective. Otherwise take a step along $-\nabla r$, with the step size discounted by
$\gamma \in [0, 1]$: the model is pushed to increase the negative-risk estimate back towards zero. With $\beta = 0$ and
$\gamma = 1$, the default in the paper's experiments, this is gradient descent on the clipped objective with the sign
of the clipped term reversed when it is negative <Cite id="kiryo2017" />.

## Worked example

Let $\pi = 0.4$. On a mini-batch a deep network gives $\hat{R}_p^{+} = 0.05$ (the positives are fitted),
$\hat{R}_p^{-} = 2.0$ (the positives would be very wrong as negatives) and $\hat{R}_u^{-} = 0.5$. The uPU estimate is
$0.4 \times 0.05 + 0.5 - 0.4 \times 2.0 = -0.28$, a negative risk, which is impossible for a non-negative loss. nnPU gives
$0.4 \times 0.05 + \max\set{0, 0.5 - 0.8} = 0.02$, and flags the batch for a corrective step.

## Cost

Both estimators cost the same as PN training on $n_p + n_u$ points: one forward pass per point and two loss
evaluations per positive. The class prior $\pi$ must be supplied or estimated separately (see <NoteLink
to="positive-unlabelled-learning" />).

## Trade-offs

- **Class prior.** Both estimators need $\pi$. An error in $\pi$ biases the risk directly, because $\pi$ weights both
  positive terms.
- **Loss choice.** uPU with a convex loss satisfying the linear-odd condition is a convex problem for linear models.
  nnPU is designed for flexible models, where convexity is lost anyway, and its experiments use the sigmoid loss.
- **When PU beats PN.** Kiryo et al. recall that uPU is likely to outperform PN learning with $n_n$ labelled negatives
  when $\pi/\sqrt{n_p} + 1/\sqrt{n_u} < (1 - \pi)/\sqrt{n_n}$, provided the model class is not too flexible; unlabelled
  data are cheap, so $n_u$ can be large <Cite id="kiryo2017" />.
- **SCAR still applies.** Both estimators assume the positives are a random sample of all positives; selection bias
  breaks them <Cite id="bekker2020" />.
- **The same device elsewhere.** Rewriting a risk on unobserved labels as a signed combination of risks on observed
  data, then correcting negative empirical terms, is also the basis of <NoteLink to="complementary-labels">
  complementary-label learning</NoteLink>.
