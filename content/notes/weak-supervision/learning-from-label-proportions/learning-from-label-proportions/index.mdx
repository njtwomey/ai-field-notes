<Definition>
In **learning from label proportions** (LLP), $n$ instances $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ are partitioned into $K$
disjoint bags $B_1, \dots, B_K$. The instance labels $y_i \in \set{1, \dots, c}$ are hidden. For each bag the
**proportions** $\pivec_k = (\pi_{k,1}, \dots, \pi_{k,c})$ are given, with
$\pi_{k,h} = \abs{\set{i \in B_k : y_i = h}}/\abs{B_k}$. The task is to learn an instance classifier, or to recover the
instance labels, from the bags and their proportions <Cite id="quadrianto2009" />.
</Definition>

## Where the setting arises

Proportions are released when individual labels are private, expensive or never recorded. Election results are
published per district, not per voter. Disease counts are published per area. A participant in a health study will
say "today I slept 8 hours and watched TV for 2 hours" but will not label every minute of sensor data
<Cite id="poyiadzi2018" />.

LLP spans a range: $n$ bags of size one is ordinary supervised learning, and one bag holding
everything reveals only the class prior.

## The bag likelihood

Consider two classes, $y_i \in \set{0, 1}$, and a probabilistic instance model $p_i = \pr(y_i = 1 \mid \xvec_i)$.
Assume instance labels are conditionally independent given the instances. The observation for bag $k$ is the count
$N_k = \sum_{i \in B_k} y_i = \pi_{k}\abs{B_k}$. The count is a sum of independent Bernoulli variables with different
means, so it follows a **Poisson-binomial** distribution:

$$
\pr(N_k = m \mid B_k) = \sum_{A \subseteq B_k,\ \abs{A} = m}\ \prod_{i \in A} p_i \prod_{i \in B_k \setminus A}(1 - p_i).
$$

The exact log-likelihood of the proportions is $\sum_k \log\pr(N_k = \pi_k\abs{B_k} \mid B_k)$. It can be evaluated by
a recursion over the instances in $O(\abs{B_k}^2)$ per bag, but it is awkward to differentiate through at scale.

**The proportion loss.** The mean of the count is $\expect[N_k] = \sum_{i \in B_k} p_i$, so the expected proportion is
the bag's mean prediction $\bar{p}_k = \frac{1}{\abs{B_k}}\sum_{i \in B_k} p_i$. Matching the mean gives the
**proportion loss**, the cross-entropy between the given and the predicted proportion:

$$
\ell_k = -\LB\pi_k\log\bar{p}_k + (1 - \pi_k)\log(1 - \bar{p}_k)\RB.
$$

$\ell_k$ is minimised exactly when $\bar{p}_k = \pi_k$. For $c$ classes it is $-\sum_h \pi_{k,h}\log\bar{p}_{k,h}$.
Deep LLP trains a network with this loss by averaging the softmax outputs within each bag <Cite id="ardehaly2017" />.
The loss constrains only the average prediction of each bag, so it cannot by itself say which instances in a bag are
positive. That information must come from the model class and from differences between bags.

## When instance labels can be recovered

In a bag with at least two instances and a proportion strictly between 0 and 1, the proportion does not determine which
instances are positive: permuting labels within the bag leaves every proportion unchanged. What can be identified is the
posterior $p(y \mid \xvec)$, and instance labels are recovered to the extent that the posterior is close to 0 or 1.

**The <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> assumption.** Suppose that, given its class, an instance's
distribution does not depend on its bag <Cite id="quadrianto2009" />:

$$
p(\xvec \mid y, k) = p(\xvec \mid y).
$$

This holds when bags are formed at random. It fails when bags are defined by a variable related to $\xvec$, such as
geography.

**Derivation.** Under the assumption, each bag's instance distribution is a mixture of the class-conditional
distributions with the bag's proportions as weights:

$$
p(\xvec \mid k) = \sum_{h=1}^{c} p(\xvec \mid y = h, k)\,\pr(y = h \mid k) = \sum_{h=1}^{c} \pi_{k,h}\,p(\xvec \mid y = h).
$$

Stack the $K$ bag distributions in a vector $\mathbf{p}_{\text{bag}}(\xvec)$ and the $c$ class-conditionals in
$\mathbf{p}_{\text{class}}(\xvec)$. With the $K \times c$ proportion matrix $\Pimat = [\pi_{k,h}]$,

$$
\mathbf{p}_{\text{bag}}(\xvec) = \Pimat\,\mathbf{p}_{\text{class}}(\xvec) \quad\text{for every } \xvec.
$$

The left-hand side is observable: each bag is a sample from its own distribution. If $\Pimat$ has full column rank $c$,
the system has the unique solution
$\mathbf{p}_{\text{class}}(\xvec) = (\Pimat^\top\Pimat)^{-1}\Pimat^\top\mathbf{p}_{\text{bag}}(\xvec)$. The
class-conditional distributions are then determined, and with the overall class prior, so is $p(y \mid \xvec)$ by Bayes'
theorem.

If $\Pimat$ has rank below $c$, identification fails. In the extreme case every bag has the same proportions $\pivec$.
Then every bag has the same distribution, the marginal $p(\xvec)$, and any split of $p(\xvec)$ into
$\sum_h \pi_h\,p_h(\xvec)$ is equally consistent with the data. Full column rank needs at least $c$ bags whose
proportion vectors are linearly independent; for two classes, two bags with different proportions.

**Worked example.** Two bags of one-dimensional data have proportions of class 1 equal to $0.8$ and $0.3$, and
observed bag means $1.4$ and $-0.1$. The class means $\mu_1, \mu_0$ solve

$$
\begin{pmatrix}0.8 & 0.2 \\ 0.3 & 0.7\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_1 \\ \mu_0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1.4 \\ -0.1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is $0.8 \times 0.7 - 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.5$, so
$\mu_1 = (0.7 \times 1.4 - 0.2 \times (-0.1))/0.5 = 2$ and $\mu_0 = (0.8 \times (-0.1) - 0.3 \times 1.4)/0.5 = -1$. Had
both bags had proportion $0.5$, the matrix would be singular and the two class means could not be separated. The same
inversion of means is the core of the <NoteLink
to="mean-map-and-invcal">mean map</NoteLink>.

The sample version is noisier: the bag means are estimated from $\abs{B_k}$ instances, and the error of the inversion
grows as $\Pimat$ approaches rank deficiency. Bags with similar proportions carry little information, which is why
performance falls as bags grow and their proportions converge to the prior.

## Families of methods

- **Mean-operator methods.** Estimate the class-conditional means from the bag means, then fit a model that needs only
  those means. The mean map uses the independence assumption above; the Laplacian mean map relaxes it by smoothing
  across similar bags <Cite id="patrini2014" />. See <NoteLink to="mean-map-and-invcal" />.
- **Bag-level regression.** Inverse calibration fits a support vector regression from each bag's mean decision value
  to the logit of its proportion. See <NoteLink to="mean-map-and-invcal" />.
- **Latent-label max-margin methods.** ∝<Gloss name="svm" /> <Cite id="yu2013" /> treats the instance labels
  $y_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ as variables and solves

  $$
  \min_{\yvec, \wvec, b}\ \frac{1}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\LP y_i, \wvec^\top\phi(\xvec_i) + b\RP + C_p\sum_{k=1}^{K}\abs{\tilde{p}_k(\yvec) - \pi_k},
  $$

  with $\tilde{p}_k(\yvec)$ the fraction of $+1$ labels in bag $k$ and $L$ the <Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge
  loss</Gloss>. It alternates between an SVM step with labels fixed and a label step per bag. It makes no independence
  assumption.
- **Proportion losses.** Train any probabilistic model by the proportion loss on bag-mean predictions
  <Cite id="ardehaly2017" />.
- **Sampling methods.** Kück and de Freitas sample instance labels consistent with the proportions by
  <Gloss name="mcmc" /> within a generative model <Cite id="kuck2005" />.
- **Graph methods.** Propagate each bag's proportion as a soft label over a similarity graph, then enforce each bag's
  total. See <NoteLink to="label-propagation-for-label-proportions" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Bags aligned with features.** When bags are defined by location, time or demographic group, the independence
  assumption fails and mean-operator methods are biased. Methods that constrain predictions per bag, such as ∝SVM and
  graph propagation, do not rely on it.
- **Evaluation.** Accuracy on held-out instances needs instance labels, which LLP data lack by definition. Papers
  evaluate on data whose true labels were hidden to form the bags.
- **Proportions are counts.** A proportion of 0.5 from a bag of 2 and from a bag of 200 are different evidence. The
  Poisson-binomial likelihood accounts for bag size; the proportion loss does not.
