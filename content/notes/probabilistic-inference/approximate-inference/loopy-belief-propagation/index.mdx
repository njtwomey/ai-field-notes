<Definition>
Loopy belief propagation applies the <NoteLink to="belief-propagation">belief propagation</NoteLink> updates to a graph
that has cycles. For a pairwise <Gloss name="mrf">Markov random field</Gloss> with node potentials $\psi_i$ and edge
potentials $\psi_{ij}$, every message starts at a constant and is repeatedly updated by

$$
m_{j \to i}(x_i) \propto \sum_{x_j} \psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)\, \psi_j(x_j) \prod_{k \in N(j) \setminus i} m_{k \to j}(x_j),
$$

until the messages converge. The beliefs $b_i(x_i) \propto \psi_i(x_i)\prod_{j \in N(i)} m_{j \to i}(x_i)$ approximate
the marginals <Cite id="yedidia2003" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Exact inference on a graph with cycles costs time exponential in its treewidth, through the
<NoteLink to="junction-tree-algorithm">junction tree algorithm</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="variable-elimination">variable elimination</NoteLink>. A $100 \times 100$ grid of pixels, or the parity
checks of an error-correcting code, is far beyond reach. The belief propagation updates are local and cost the same on
any graph. Loopy belief propagation (loopy BP) runs them anyway, accepting that the derivation that makes them exact on
trees no longer holds.

## Mechanism

1. Initialise every message $m_{j \to i}$ to the uniform vector.
2. Update messages by the rule above, either all at once from the previous round (a **parallel** or flooding schedule)
   or one at a time using the newest values (a **sequential** schedule). Normalise each message to sum to 1.
3. Stop when the largest change in any message falls below a tolerance, or after a maximum number of rounds.
4. Report the beliefs $b_i$ and, for each edge, $b_{ij}(x_i, x_j) \propto \psi_{ij}\, \psi_i\, \psi_j
   \prod_{k \in N(i) \setminus j} m_{k \to i}(x_i) \prod_{l \in N(j) \setminus i} m_{l \to j}(x_j)$.

**Why it is approximate.** On a tree, the message from $j$ to $i$ summarises the part of the graph behind $j$, and
that part shares nothing with the rest. On a cycle, a message eventually travels around the loop and returns to its
source. Evidence at one node is then counted again and again, and the beliefs become too confident. The error is
small when cycles are long and couplings weak, because information decays as it travels around a long loop.

**Damping** replaces each new message by a weighted average of the new and the old value,
$m \leftarrow (1 - \alpha)\, m^{\mathrm{new}} + \alpha\, m^{\mathrm{old}}$. It slows the updates and often turns an
oscillation into convergence, to the same fixed points.

## The Bethe free energy

For any distribution $b$ over $\xvec$, the variational free energy $F(b) = \expect_b[E(\xvec)] - \entropy[b]$, with
energy $E = -\sum_{ij}\log\psi_{ij} - \sum_i \log\psi_i$, satisfies $F(b) \ge -\log Z$, with equality at $b = p$. This
is the <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink> with the sign reversed. Minimising it over
all distributions is as hard as exact inference.

On a tree, the joint distribution is determined by its node and edge marginals,
$p(\xvec) = \prod_{ij} p_{ij}(x_i, x_j) \prod_i p_i(x_i)^{1 - d_i}$, where $d_i$ is the degree of node $i$. Its
<NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> is therefore a sum of local entropies:
$\entropy[p] = \sum_{ij}\entropy[p_{ij}] - \sum_i (d_i - 1)\,\entropy[p_i]$. The **Bethe free energy** uses this formula
on any graph, as a function of node and edge **pseudo-marginals** $b_i$ and $b_{ij}$:

$$
F_{\mathrm{Bethe}} = \sum_{ij}\sum_{x_i, x_j} b_{ij} \log\frac{b_{ij}}{\psi_{ij}\,\psi_i\,\psi_j}
- \sum_i (d_i - 1) \sum_{x_i} b_i \log\frac{b_i}{\psi_i}.
$$

It is minimised subject to local consistency: each $b_{ij}$ is normalised and sums over $x_j$ to $b_i$. The
pseudo-marginals need not be the marginals of any joint distribution. **Theorem.** The fixed points of loopy belief
propagation are exactly the stationary points of the Bethe free energy under these constraints
<Cite id="yedidia2005" />. On a tree the Bethe free energy is exact, its minimum is $-\log Z$, and BP finds it. On a
graph with cycles, $-F_{\mathrm{Bethe}}$ at a fixed point approximates $\log Z$ but is not a bound in general.

<Derivation title="Fixed points from the Lagrangian">
Attach a multiplier $\lambda_{ji}(x_i)$ to each constraint $\sum_{x_j} b_{ij}(x_i, x_j) = b_i(x_i)$, and further
multipliers for normalisation. Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the Lagrangian with
respect to $b_{ij}(x_i, x_j)$ to zero gives

$$
b_{ij}(x_i, x_j) \propto \psi_{ij}\,\psi_i\,\psi_j\, \exp\paren{\lambda_{ji}(x_i) + \lambda_{ij}(x_j)}.
$$

Setting the derivative with respect to $b_i(x_i)$ to zero gives, for $d_i > 1$,

$$
b_i(x_i) \propto \psi_i(x_i)\, \exp\paren{\frac{1}{d_i - 1}\sum_{j \in N(i)} \lambda_{ji}(x_i)}.
$$

Now define messages through the multipliers by
$\lambda_{ji}(x_i) = \log\prod_{k \in N(i) \setminus j} m_{k \to i}(x_i)$. Each incoming message appears in $d_i - 1$ of
the $d_i$ multipliers at node $i$, so $\sum_j \lambda_{ji} = (d_i - 1)\sum_k \log m_{k \to i}$, and the node belief
becomes $b_i \propto \psi_i\prod_{k \in N(i)} m_{k \to i}$. The edge belief becomes the expression in step 4 of the
mechanism. Imposing the constraint $\sum_{x_j} b_{ij} = b_i$ and cancelling the common factors
$\psi_i \prod_{k \in N(i) \setminus j} m_{k \to i}$ leaves

$$
m_{j \to i}(x_i) \propto \sum_{x_j}\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)\,\psi_j(x_j)\prod_{l \in N(j) \setminus i} m_{l \to j}(x_j),
$$

which is the belief propagation update. Stationary points of the constrained Bethe free energy and fixed points of
the messages therefore correspond <Cite id="yedidia2003" />.
</Derivation>

## Worked example: a triangle of spins

Take three Ising spins $x_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ joined in a triangle, with $\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j) = e^{J x_i x_j}$,
$\psi_i(x_i) = e^{h x_i}$, $J = 1$ and $h = 0.2$.

- **Exact.** Summing over the eight configurations gives $\log Z = 3.909$ and $P(x_i = +1) = 0.758$ for each spin.
- **Loopy BP.** By symmetry every message is the same. The iteration converges in about 40 parallel rounds to beliefs
  $b_i(+1) = 0.915$, and the Bethe estimate of $\log Z$ is $3.700$.

The belief overstates the marginal: the evidence from the node potentials $\psi_i$ travels around the triangle and is
counted again. Removing one edge leaves a chain of three spins, a tree, on which the same updates give the exact
marginals $(0.716, 0.732, 0.716)$ and the exact $\log Z = 3.085$.

## Where it works

Loopy BP decodes turbo codes and low-density parity-check codes close to channel capacity; turbo decoding turned out to
be an instance of loopy BP <Cite id="mceliece1998" />. It is widely used for stereo matching, image segmentation and
other grid-structured problems. An empirical study found that when loopy BP converges its marginals are usually
accurate, and that non-convergence, visible as oscillation, is a warning sign of poor approximation
<Cite id="murphy1999" />.

## Variants

- **Max-product.** Replacing sums by maxima gives loopy max-product, an approximate method for the most probable
  configuration.
- **Generalised belief propagation.** Passing messages between clusters of nodes corresponds to the Kikuchi free energy,
  a better approximation to the entropy that accounts for short cycles inside clusters <Cite id="yedidia2005" />.
- **Expectation propagation.** With continuous variables and
  <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential-family</NoteLink> messages, the same scheme is
  <NoteLink to="expectation-propagation">expectation propagation</NoteLink>.
- **Convex variants.** Tree-reweighted belief propagation replaces the Bethe entropy by a convex upper bound, which
  gives a unique fixed point and an upper bound on $\log Z$ <Cite id="wainwright2008" />.

## Trade-offs

- **No guarantee of convergence.** Strong couplings, frustrated cycles (such as antiferromagnetic couplings around an
  odd cycle) and tightly coupled loops can make the messages oscillate. Damping and sequential schedules help but do
  not guarantee convergence <Cite id="koller2009" />.
- **Multiple fixed points.** The Bethe free energy can have several stationary points, and the result can depend on
  the initial messages and the schedule.
- **Overconfidence.** Double-counted evidence makes beliefs too extreme, especially with short cycles and strong
  couplings.
- **Cheap.** Each round costs $O(\abs{E}\, k^2)$ for $k$ states per variable, the same as one sweep of exact BP on a
  tree. Compared with <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>, which
  keeps only node marginals, it keeps pairwise information and is usually more accurate.
