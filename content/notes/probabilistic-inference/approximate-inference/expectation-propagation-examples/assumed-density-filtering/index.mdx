<Definition>
Assumed density filtering (ADF) approximates a posterior $p(\thetavec) \propto p_0(\thetavec)\prod_{i=1}^n t_i(\thetavec)$
by a member of an exponential family $\Fcal$, such as the Gaussians. Start from $q_0 = p_0$. For $i = 1, \dots, n$, form
$\hat p_i \propto q_{i-1}\,t_i$ and set $q_i$ to the member of $\Fcal$ with the same expected sufficient statistics as
$\hat p_i$, which minimises $\KL(\hat p_i \,\|\, q_i)$. The answer is $q_n$ <Cite id="minka2001thesis" /> <Cite id="opper1998" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

An exact posterior with non-Gaussian factors is not Gaussian, and its normaliser and moments are integrals without a
closed form. One factor at a time is easy: a Gaussian times one probit, step or mixture factor has closed-form moments.
ADF strings these easy steps together, keeping a Gaussian at every stage, so that the cost is one small integral per
factor. It suits streaming data, where each observation is seen once, and it is the first pass of
<NoteLink to="expectation-propagation">expectation propagation</NoteLink> (EP).

## Mechanism

Each ADF step has three parts:

1. **Multiply.** $\hat p_i(\thetavec) = q_{i-1}(\thetavec)\,t_i(\thetavec)/Z_i$ with
   $Z_i = \int q_{i-1}(\thetavec)\,t_i(\thetavec)\,d\thetavec$. $\hat p_i$ is the exact posterior if $q_{i-1}$ were the
   exact posterior of the first $i - 1$ factors.
2. **Project.** Set $q_i = \argmin_{q \in \Fcal}\KL(\hat p_i \,\|\, q)$. For an exponential family with sufficient
   statistics $\tvec(\thetavec)$ the minimiser satisfies $\expect_{q_i}[\tvec] = \expect_{\hat p_i}[\tvec]$. For a
   Gaussian, $q_i$ has the mean and covariance of $\hat p_i$.
3. **Accumulate the evidence.** $\prod_i Z_i$ approximates the marginal likelihood $\int p_0\prod_i t_i\,d\thetavec$,
   because each $Z_i$ is the predictive probability of factor $i$ under the current approximation.

For a Gaussian $q_{i-1} = \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ in one dimension, step 2 needs only $\log Z_i$ as a function of $\mu$:
$\expect_{\hat p_i}[\theta] = \mu + \sigma^2\,\partial_\mu\log Z_i$ and
$\var_{\hat p_i}[\theta] = \sigma^2 + \sigma^4\,\partial^2_\mu\log Z_i$. The derivation of these identities, and of the
closed forms for step and probit factors, is in <NoteLink to="expectation-propagation-truncated-gaussian">a Gaussian times a step function</NoteLink>.

### ADF as a product of sites

Every ADF step can be read as replacing factor $t_i$ by a Gaussian **site**
$\tilde t_i(\thetavec) = Z_i\,q_i(\thetavec)/q_{i-1}(\thetavec)$. A ratio of Gaussians is an exponentiated quadratic, so
the site is Gaussian in shape, possibly with negative precision. Multiplying the prior by all the sites reproduces
$q_n$ exactly: the product telescopes, $p_0\prod_i \tilde t_i = q_n\prod_i Z_i$ <Cite id="minka2001thesis" />.

This reading shows what goes wrong. Site $i$ is fitted against $q_{i-1}$, the approximation built from the factors
before it, and is never updated when later factors arrive. A factor absorbed early is summarised where the early
approximation had its mass, which may not be where the final posterior lies. The result depends on the order.

EP keeps the sites and revisits them. To refine site $i$, it removes that site from the current $q$ to get a **cavity**
$q^{\setminus i} \propto q/\tilde t_i$, which contains all the other factors' information, and repeats the ADF step
against that cavity. At a fixed point, every site is consistent with every other, and the order of visits no longer
matters.

## Worked example: two censored readings

A quantity $\theta$ has prior $\Gauss(0, 4)$. A sensor reports $\theta + n$ with noise $n \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, but only
relative to a threshold. Reading A says $\theta + n_A > 2$; reading B says $\theta + n_B < 0$. The factors are

$$
t_A(\theta) = \Phi(\theta - 2), \qquad t_B(\theta) = \Phi(-\theta),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cdf. The readings disagree: one says $\theta$ is large, the other that it is small.

For a cavity $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and a factor $\Phi\paren{y(\theta - c)}$ with sign $y = \pm 1$ and threshold $c$,
let $z = y(\mu - c)/\sqrt{1 + \sigma^2}$, $v = \phi(z)/\Phi(z)$ and $w = v(v + z)$. The tilted moments are

$$
\text{mean} = \mu + \frac{y\,\sigma^2 v}{\sqrt{1 + \sigma^2}}, \qquad
\text{variance} = \sigma^2 - \frac{\sigma^4 w}{1 + \sigma^2}.
$$

**Order A then B.**

1. Absorb $t_A$ into $\Gauss(0, 4)$. $z = (0 - 2)/\sqrt 5 = -0.8944$, $v = 1.4412$, $w = 0.7881$. Mean
   $= 4 \times 1.4412/\sqrt 5 = 2.5782$; variance $= 4 - 16 \times 0.7881/5 = 1.4781$.
2. Absorb $t_B$ into $\Gauss(2.5782, 1.4781)$. $z = -2.5782/\sqrt{2.4781} = -1.6378$, $v = 2.0566$, $w = 0.8614$. Mean
   $= 2.5782 - 1.4781 \times 2.0566/\sqrt{2.4781} = 0.6471$; variance $= 1.4781 - 1.4781^2 \times 0.8614/2.4781 = 0.7187$.

**Order B then A.**

1. Absorb $t_B$ into $\Gauss(0, 4)$. $z = 0$, $v = 0.7979$, $w = 0.6366$. Mean $= -4 \times 0.7979/\sqrt 5 = -1.4273$;
   variance $= 4 - 16 \times 0.6366/5 = 1.9628$.
2. Absorb $t_A$ into $\Gauss(-1.4273, 1.9628)$. $z = -3.4273/\sqrt{2.9628} = -1.9911$, $v = 2.3654$, $w = 0.8852$.
   Mean $= -1.4273 + 1.9628 \times 2.3654/\sqrt{2.9628} = 1.2700$; variance $= 0.8118$.

**Exact.** Numerical integration of $\Gauss(\theta \mid 0, 4)\,\Phi(\theta - 2)\,\Phi(-\theta)$ on a fine grid gives mean
$0.8617$ and variance $0.5537$.

The two orders give means of $0.647$ and $1.270$, on either side of the exact $0.862$, and both overstate the variance.
The first factor absorbed is fitted against the prior, which is broad and centred at 0. With A first, its site is
$\tilde t_A \propto \Gauss(\theta \mid 4.09, 2.34)$ (precision $0.427$); A's evidence is summarised as if $\theta$ were
near the prior, and B's later pull is not balanced against it.

**EP.** Keep both sites and revisit them. In sweep 2, the cavity for A is the prior times the site of B, which after
the first pass is $\Gauss(-0.8745, 1.0364)$. Absorbing $t_A$ into this cavity gives $q = \Gauss(0.8583, 0.5688)$ and a
new site for A with precision $0.793$ and mean $2.97$. Refitting B against its new cavity gives
$\Gauss(0.8621, 0.5554)$. A third sweep changes the fourth decimal place only: EP converges to $\Gauss(0.8617, 0.5553)$
from either order. The mean agrees with the exact mean to four decimals, and the variance is within 0.3 per cent. A
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> at the mode gives $\Gauss(0.8650, 0.5404)$.

## Cost

One ADF step costs one tilted-moment computation plus an update of the Gaussian. For scalar factors of the form
$t_i(\thetavec) = f(\xvec_i\transpose\thetavec)$ on a $d$-dimensional Gaussian, the tilted moments are one-dimensional
and the covariance changes by a rank-one update, $O(d^2)$ per factor and $O(nd^2)$ for the pass. EP repeats the pass a
few times, typically 4 or 5 <Cite id="minka2001thesis" />, and stores one site per factor.

## Trade-offs

- **Order dependence.** The result depends on the order of the factors. Processing similar observations together is
  especially bad: the approximation commits to one region before it sees data elsewhere <Cite id="minka2001thesis" />.
  EP removes the dependence at the cost of storing the sites and repeating the passes.
- **Streaming.** ADF needs each factor once and stores nothing per factor, so it works on unbounded streams where EP
  cannot. Opper's online Bayesian learning for neural networks and perceptrons is ADF with a Gaussian
  <Cite id="opper1998" />.
- **Always proper.** The cavity at every ADF step is the previous posterior approximation, which is a proper Gaussian,
  so ADF never meets the negative-variance cavities that can stall EP.
- **Kalman filtering.** For a linear-Gaussian state-space model every tilted distribution is already Gaussian and ADF is
  exact: it is the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>. With non-linear observations ADF gives a
  moment-matching filter, an alternative to linearisation in the extended Kalman filter.
