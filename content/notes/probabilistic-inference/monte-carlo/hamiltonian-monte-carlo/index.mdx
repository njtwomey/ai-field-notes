<Definition>
Let the target be $\pi(\qvec) \propto \exp(-U(\qvec))$ on $\reals^d$, with **potential energy** $U = -\log\tilde\pi$.
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo introduces a momentum $\pvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Mmat)$ and the **Hamiltonian**

$$
H(\qvec, \pvec) = U(\qvec) + \tfrac12 \pvec^\top \Mmat^{-1} \pvec.
$$

Each iteration draws a fresh momentum, simulates Hamiltonian dynamics for $L$ leapfrog steps of size $\epsilon$ to reach
$(\qvec^*, \pvec^*)$, and accepts $\qvec^*$ with probability $\min\set{1, \exp\paren{H(\qvec, \pvec) - H(\qvec^*, \pvec^*)}}$.
The chain leaves $\pi$ invariant <Cite id="duane1987" /> <Cite id="neal2011" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="metropolis-hastings">Random-walk Metropolis</NoteLink> proposes blind moves. To keep the acceptance rate
reasonable in $d$ dimensions its step must shrink like $d^{-1/2}$, and it explores by a random walk, so the distance
covered grows only as the square root of the number of steps. Correlated targets make this worse: the step must suit
the narrowest direction. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo uses the gradient $\nabla U$ to propose distant points that still
have high probability, by following a trajectory that stays on a level set of the joint density of position and
momentum.

## Hamiltonian dynamics

Treat $\qvec$ as the position of a particle on the surface $U$ and $\pvec$ as its momentum. Hamilton's equations are

$$
\frac{d\qvec}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial \pvec} = \Mmat^{-1}\pvec, \qquad
\frac{d\pvec}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial \qvec} = -\nabla U(\qvec).
$$

The particle speeds up downhill and slows uphill. The dynamics has three properties that make it a valid proposal
mechanism <Cite id="neal2011" />.

- **Energy is conserved.** By the chain rule,
  $\frac{dH}{dt} = \nabla_{\qvec} H^\top \frac{d\qvec}{dt} + \nabla_{\pvec} H^\top \frac{d\pvec}{dt}
  = \nabla_{\qvec} H^\top \nabla_{\pvec} H - \nabla_{\pvec} H^\top \nabla_{\qvec} H = 0$. The joint density
  $\propto e^{-H}$ is the same at every point of a trajectory.
- **Volume is preserved.** The flow's velocity field $(\nabla_{\pvec} H, -\nabla_{\qvec} H)$ has divergence
  $\sum_i \paren{\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i \partial p_i} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i \partial q_i}} = 0$.
  A divergence-free flow maps any region to a region of the same volume (Liouville's theorem).
- **The dynamics is reversible.** Running the flow for time $t$, negating the momentum and running for time $t$ again
  returns to the start with negated momentum.

Energy conservation means an exact trajectory would always be accepted. Volume preservation means no Jacobian term
enters the acceptance probability. Reversibility makes the proposal its own inverse after a momentum flip, which is
what detailed balance needs.

## The leapfrog integrator

Hamilton's equations have no closed-form solution in general. The **leapfrog** integrator takes a half step in
momentum, a full step in position, and another half step in momentum:

$$
\pvec_{1/2} = \pvec - \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(\qvec), \qquad
\qvec' = \qvec + \epsilon\, \Mmat^{-1} \pvec_{1/2}, \qquad
\pvec' = \pvec_{1/2} - \tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(\qvec').
$$

Consecutive half steps in momentum merge, so $L$ steps cost $L$ gradient evaluations.

**Volume-preserving.** Each of the three updates is a **shear**: it changes one of $\qvec$ and $\pvec$ by an amount
that depends only on the other. The Jacobian of a shear is block triangular with identity blocks on the diagonal, so
its determinant is 1. A composition of maps with determinant 1 has determinant 1.

**Reversible.** Start from $(\qvec', -\pvec')$ and apply a leapfrog step. The first half step gives
$-\pvec' - \frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(\qvec') = -\pvec_{1/2}$. The position step gives
$\qvec' - \epsilon\,\Mmat^{-1}\pvec_{1/2} = \qvec$. The last half step gives
$-\pvec_{1/2} - \frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla U(\qvec) = -\pvec$. The step returns exactly to $(\qvec, -\pvec)$, and so do $L$
steps.

**Not energy-conserving.** The leapfrog is exact in volume and reversibility but only approximate in energy. The
energy error over a trajectory of fixed length is $O(\epsilon^2)$, and it does not drift: leapfrog trajectories
oscillate around the true energy instead of accumulating error.

## The acceptance step

Define the proposal map $F(\qvec, \pvec) = (\qvec^*, -\pvec^*)$: run $L$ leapfrog steps, then negate the momentum.
By reversibility, $F(F(\zvec)) = \zvec$, and $F$ preserves volume. Target the joint density
$\pi(\qvec, \pvec) \propto e^{-H(\qvec, \pvec)}$, whose $\qvec$-marginal is $\pi(\qvec)$. Accept with

$$
\alpha(\zvec) = \min\set{1, \frac{\pi(F(\zvec))}{\pi(\zvec)}} = \min\set{1, e^{H(\zvec) - H(F(\zvec))}},
$$

using $H(\qvec, -\pvec) = H(\qvec, \pvec)$. This satisfies detailed balance. For any region $A$, the probability of
jumping from $A$ to $F(A)$ is $\int_A \min\set{\pi(\zvec), \pi(F(\zvec))}\, d\zvec$. The probability of jumping back
is $\int_{F(A)} \min\set{\pi(\wvec), \pi(F(\wvec))}\, d\wvec$. Substituting $\wvec = F(\zvec)$, with unit Jacobian and
$F(F(\zvec)) = \zvec$, turns the second integral into the first.

Resampling $\pvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Mmat)$ at the start of each iteration is a
<NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs</NoteLink> update of the momentum, which also leaves $\pi(\qvec, \pvec)$ invariant.
The negation of the momentum does not matter after resampling and is never implemented. Discarding $\pvec$ leaves
samples of $\pi(\qvec)$.

## Worked example: one leapfrog step

Take $U(q) = q^2/2$, a standard Gaussian target, with $M = 1$, $\epsilon = 0.5$ and start $(q, p) = (1, 0)$, so
$H = 0.5$.

- **Half step.** $p_{1/2} = 0 - 0.25 \times 1 = -0.25$.
- **Position.** $q' = 1 + 0.5 \times (-0.25) = 0.875$.
- **Half step.** $p' = -0.25 - 0.25 \times 0.875 = -0.46875$.
- **Energy.** $H' = 0.875^2/2 + 0.46875^2/2 = 0.38281 + 0.10986 = 0.49268$. Then $\Delta H = -0.0073$, and the
  proposal is accepted with probability 1.

The exact flow for time 0.5 reaches $(\cos 0.5, -\sin 0.5) = (0.878, -0.479)$, close to the leapfrog point.

**Stability.** For $U(q) = q^2/(2\sigma^2)$, one leapfrog step is a linear map with determinant 1 and trace
$2 - \epsilon^2/\sigma^2$. Its eigenvalues $\lambda$ satisfy $\lambda^2 - (2 - \epsilon^2/\sigma^2)\lambda + 1 = 0$. When
$\abs{2 - \epsilon^2/\sigma^2} < 2$ they are complex with modulus 1 and trajectories stay bounded; otherwise one is real
with modulus above 1 and trajectories grow exponentially. The leapfrog is stable exactly when $\epsilon < 2\sigma$. For
a multivariate target the narrowest direction sets the limit, $\epsilon < 2\sigma_{\min}$. With $\sigma = 1$, the step
size $\epsilon = 0.5$ above is well inside the limit of 2.

<Derivation title="The trace of the leapfrog map">
With $U'(q) = q/\sigma^2$, the three updates give

$$
q' = \paren{1 - \tfrac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}} q + \epsilon\, p, \qquad
p' = -\tfrac{\epsilon}{\sigma^2}\paren{1 - \tfrac{\epsilon^2}{4\sigma^2}} q + \paren{1 - \tfrac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}} p.
$$

The diagonal entries sum to $2 - \epsilon^2/\sigma^2$. The determinant is
$\paren{1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}}^2 + \frac{\epsilon^2}{\sigma^2}\paren{1 - \frac{\epsilon^2}{4\sigma^2}} = 1$, as
volume preservation requires. The eigenvalues of a $2 \times 2$ matrix with determinant 1 have product 1; they lie on
the unit circle exactly when the discriminant $\text{trace}^2 - 4$ is negative.
</Derivation>

## Tuning

- **Step size $\epsilon$.** Too large and $\Delta H$ grows, so proposals are rejected; beyond the stability limit the
  trajectory diverges. Too small and each trajectory costs many gradients. Implementations adapt $\epsilon$ during
  warm-up to reach a target acceptance rate, 0.8 by default in Stan, by dual averaging <Cite id="hoffman2014" />.
- **Trajectory length $\epsilon L$.** Too short and HMC behaves like a random walk. Too long and the trajectory turns
  back towards its start, wasting computation.
- **Mass matrix $\Mmat$.** Setting $\Mmat^{-1}$ to an estimate of the posterior covariance rescales the target to be
  roughly isotropic, which removes the constraint that the narrowest direction imposes on $\epsilon$. Warm-up estimates
  a diagonal or dense $\Mmat$ from early draws.

**The No-U-Turn Sampler** (NUTS) removes the need to choose $L$. It builds a trajectory by repeatedly doubling it,
forwards or backwards in time at random, until the two ends start to move towards each other: the distance between the
end points $\qvec^+ - \qvec^-$ stops growing when $(\qvec^+ - \qvec^-)^\top \pvec^\pm < 0$ at either end. It then samples
a point from the trajectory in a way that preserves detailed balance <Cite id="hoffman2014" />. NUTS with warm-up
adaptation of $\epsilon$ and $\Mmat$ is the default sampler of Stan and PyMC; see
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">probabilistic programming</NoteLink>.

## Cost

Each iteration costs $L$ gradient evaluations of $\log\tilde\pi$. In $d$ dimensions the step size needed for a fixed
acceptance rate shrinks only as $d^{-1/4}$, against $d^{-1/2}$ for random-walk Metropolis, so HMC needs about
$O(d^{1/4})$ gradients per effective sample on smooth targets <Cite id="neal2011" />. Automatic differentiation
supplies the gradients.

## Trade-offs

- **Needs gradients.** The target must be differentiable in continuous parameters. Discrete parameters must be summed
  out or sampled by another kernel.
- **Divergences are diagnostic.** In regions of high curvature, such as the neck of a hierarchical model's funnel, the
  leapfrog becomes unstable and the energy error explodes. These **divergent transitions** mean the sampler cannot
  explore that region, and the estimates may be biased. Reparameterising (for example the non-centred form of a
  hierarchical model) usually removes them <Cite id="betancourt2017" />.
- **Multimodality remains hard.** A trajectory conserves energy approximately, so it cannot climb over a deep valley of
  low density between modes. Momentum resampling changes the energy level only gradually.
- **Heavy tails.** In the tails of a heavy-tailed target the gradient is small and trajectories are slow to return.
- **One leapfrog step.** With $L = 1$, HMC is the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm; see
  <NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink>.
