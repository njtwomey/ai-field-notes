<Definition>
Let $\thetavec \in \reals^d$ have prior $p(\thetavec)$ and let the data $x_1, \dots, x_N$ be independent given
$\thetavec$. **Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics** (SGLD) draws a random minibatch $S_t$ of $n$ indices at step $t$
and updates

$$
\thetavec_{t+1} = \thetavec_t + \frac{\epsilon_t}{2}\paren{\nabla\log p(\thetavec_t) + \frac{N}{n}\sum_{i \in S_t}
\nabla\log p(x_i \mid \thetavec_t)} + \etavec_t, \qquad \etavec_t \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \epsilon_t\eye),
$$

with step sizes that satisfy $\sum_t \epsilon_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \epsilon_t^2 < \infty$. It skips the
Metropolis–Hastings test. As $t \to \infty$ the iterates sample the posterior $p(\thetavec \mid x_{1:N})$
<Cite id="welling2011" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink> samples a posterior using its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, but every step
needs the gradient of the full log likelihood, a sum over all $N$ data points. MALA's acceptance test needs the full log
posterior as well. On a dataset of millions of points, one exact step costs a pass over the data, and thousands of steps
are needed. <Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> optimises the same objective with a cheap unbiased gradient from a minibatch.
SGLD replaces the full-data gradient in Langevin dynamics with that minibatch gradient.

## Mechanism

### Two sources of noise

The minibatch sum, scaled by $N/n$, is an unbiased estimate of the full-data gradient
$\gvec(\thetavec) = \sum_{i=1}^N \nabla\log p(x_i \mid \thetavec)$. For a minibatch drawn with replacement its
covariance is $\frac{N^2}{n}\Vmat(\thetavec)$, where $\Vmat(\thetavec)$ is the covariance of a single per-example
gradient $\nabla\log p(x_i \mid \thetavec)$ over a uniformly chosen $i$. <NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">Sampling without replacement</NoteLink> multiplies it by
$1 - (n - 1)/(N - 1)$, which is 0 at $n = N$.

Write the SGLD step as the exact Langevin step plus an error:

$$
\thetavec_{t+1} = \thetavec_t + \frac{\epsilon_t}{2}\nabla\log p(\thetavec_t \mid x_{1:N}) +
\underbrace{\frac{\epsilon_t}{2}\paren{\hat\gvec_t - \gvec(\thetavec_t)}}_{\text{minibatch noise}} +
\underbrace{\etavec_t}_{\text{injected noise}}.
$$

The two noise terms have covariances

$$
\frac{\epsilon_t^2}{4}\,\frac{N^2}{n}\,\Vmat(\thetavec_t) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \epsilon_t\eye.
$$

The minibatch term is of order $\epsilon_t^2$ and the injected term of order $\epsilon_t$. Their ratio along a direction
where a per-example gradient has variance $v$ is

$$
\frac{\epsilon_t N^2 v}{4n},
$$

which tends to 0 as $\epsilon_t \to 0$. At small steps the update is therefore the Langevin update with step
$h = \epsilon_t/2$, driven by the correct amount of Gaussian noise, and the minibatch error is negligible beside it
<Cite id="welling2011" />. The same argument removes the need for a Metropolis test: the discretisation error of a
Langevin step vanishes as the step shrinks, so the acceptance probability of MALA would tend to 1.

### The step-size schedule

A fixed step leaves two biases: the discretisation bias of the unadjusted Langevin algorithm, of order $\epsilon$, and
the extra spread from minibatch noise, whose ratio to the injected noise is proportional to $\epsilon$. A decreasing
schedule removes both. The two conditions play the same roles as in the Robbins–Monro analysis of SGD:

- $\sum_t \epsilon_t = \infty$. Each step advances the underlying diffusion by time $\epsilon_t/2$, so the total
  simulated time is infinite and the chain can reach every part of the posterior from any start.
- $\sum_t \epsilon_t^2 < \infty$. The total variance contributed by minibatch noise, $\sum_t \epsilon_t^2 N^2 v/(4n)$,
  is finite, so its effect on late iterates dies out.

Welling and Teh used $\epsilon_t = a(b + t)^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma \in (0.5, 1]$ <Cite id="welling2011" />. Teh,
Thiery and Vollmer proved consistency and a <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss>, and showed that the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> of a
posterior average is smallest with $\epsilon_t \propto t^{-1/3}$, where it falls as $t^{-1/3}$ <Cite id="teh2016" />.
That rate is slower than the $t^{-1}$ of exact <Gloss name="mcmc" />, the price of skipping the Metropolis test.

Because consecutive iterates advance the diffusion by different amounts of time, a posterior <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> is estimated
with step-size weights,

$$
\expect\brack{f(\thetavec) \mid x_{1:N}} \approx \frac{\sum_t \epsilon_t f(\thetavec_t)}{\sum_t \epsilon_t},
$$

so that each iterate counts in proportion to the time it represents <Cite id="welling2011" />.

### From optimisation to sampling

Early in a run the steps are large. The drift $\frac{\epsilon_t}{2}\nabla\log p$ moves the iterate a distance of order
$\epsilon_t$ per step, while the injected noise moves it only $\sqrt{\epsilon_t}$ in a <NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere">random direction</NoteLink>, and far from
the mode the drift wins. The minibatch noise also exceeds the injected noise while $\epsilon_t > 4n/(N^2 v)$. SGLD then
behaves like <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink> and heads to a
region of high posterior density. Near the mode the drift shrinks, the <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">step decays</NoteLink> and the injected noise takes over, so
the iterates spread out over the posterior instead of converging to a point. Welling and Teh proposed discarding
iterates until the minibatch-to-injected noise ratio, computed with the largest eigenvalue of $\Vmat$, falls below a
small threshold, and collecting samples after that <Cite id="welling2011" />. One program is both a MAP optimiser
and a posterior sampler; only the stage of the schedule differs.

## Worked example: when the injected noise takes over

Take $N = 10{,}000$ examples, minibatches of $n = 100$, and a direction in which a per-example gradient has variance
$v = 1$. The ratio of minibatch to injected noise variance is $\epsilon N^2 v/(4n) = \epsilon \times 10^8/400 = 2.5
\times 10^5\, \epsilon$.

- At $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ the ratio is 25. The minibatch noise dominates. The total noise per step has 26 times
  the variance Langevin dynamics prescribes, which on a Gaussian posterior samples at temperature 26: about
  $\sqrt{26} \approx 5$ times too wide.
- The two are equal at $\epsilon^* = 4n/(N^2 v) = 4 \times 10^{-6}$.
- At $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ the ratio is 0.25. The injected noise dominates and SGLD is close to exact Langevin dynamics.

If each example contributes <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss> 1 in that direction, the posterior standard deviation is about
$1/\sqrt{N} = 0.01$. At $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ one step moves the iterate by noise of standard deviation
$\sqrt{\epsilon} = 0.001$, a tenth of the posterior width. A <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> needs about $(0.01/0.001)^2 = 100$ steps to
cross the posterior. The step must be small enough for the injected noise to dominate, and that makes mixing slow in
directions where the posterior is wide.

## Variants

- **Preconditioned SGLD** (pSGLD). The posterior of a deep network has very different curvature in different
  directions, and one step size cannot suit all of them. Li et al. scale the drift by a diagonal matrix $\Gmat_t$ built
  from a running average of squared gradients, as in <NoteLink to="adagrad-and-rmsprop">RMSprop</NoteLink>, and inject
  noise with covariance $\epsilon_t\Gmat_t$ to match <Cite id="li2016psgld" />. A correction term involving the
  <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of $\Gmat$ is needed for exactness; it is small when the preconditioner changes slowly and is often
  dropped.
- **Cyclical SG-MCMC** (cSG-MCMC). Zhang et al. restart the step size periodically with a cosine schedule. Each cycle
  begins with large steps that behave like optimisation and move the iterate to a new region, and ends with small steps
  that sample around the mode found <Cite id="zhang2020csgmcmc" />. The cycles visit several modes of a multimodal
  posterior, which a single decreasing schedule rarely does.
- **Stochastic gradient Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (SGHMC). Replacing the gradient in
  <NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo">Hamiltonian Monte Carlo</NoteLink> by a minibatch estimate adds noise to the
  momentum, and the dynamics no longer leave the target invariant; without a Metropolis test the momentum grows and
  the chain drifts away. Chen, Fox and Guestrin add a friction term $-\Cmat\Mmat^{-1}\rvec\, dt$ to the momentum
  equation and reduce the injected noise to $\Gauss(\zeros, 2(\Cmat - \hat\Bmat)\,dt)$, where $\hat\Bmat$ estimates the
  minibatch noise, so that friction and noise balance <Cite id="chen2014sghmc" />. The result is second-order Langevin
  dynamics with momentum, analogous to <NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">SGD with momentum</NoteLink>.

## Cost

One SGLD step costs one minibatch gradient and $d$ Gaussian draws, the same as an SGD step. No full-data pass and no
acceptance test are needed. Storing samples is the dominant extra cost for large models: each sample of a neural
network is a full copy of its weights, so samples are thinned and only a few tens are kept for prediction.

## Trade-offs

- **Bias at any finite step.** Without a Metropolis test, SGLD is exact only in the limit of vanishing steps. In
  practice schedules stop decaying at a small constant, and the samples carry a bias of order that step.
- **Slow mixing at small steps.** Keeping the minibatch noise below the injected noise requires
  $\epsilon \lesssim 4n/(N^2 v)$, which shrinks with the square of the dataset size. Directions of large posterior
  variance then take many steps to explore.
- **Bayesian deep learning.** SGLD and its variants give a posterior over network weights at roughly the cost of
  training. Predictions average the network's output over the stored samples, $p(y \mid \xvec) \approx \frac{1}{S}
  \sum_s p(y \mid \xvec, \thetavec_s)$, which improves calibration and uncertainty on inputs far from the data compared
  with a single trained network <Cite id="zhang2020csgmcmc" />. Deep ensembles and variational methods compete for the
  same role.
- **Tuning.** The initial step, decay rate, burn-in and thinning all affect the result, and standard <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo-diagnostics">MCMC diagnostics</NoteLink>
  are weaker because the chain is not exactly reversible.
