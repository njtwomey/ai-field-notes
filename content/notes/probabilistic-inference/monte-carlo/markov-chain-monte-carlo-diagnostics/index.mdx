<Definition>
MCMC diagnostics are statistics computed from the output of several <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chains</NoteLink>, run from dispersed starting
points, that detect failure to converge and quantify precision. The main ones are trace plots, the potential scale
reduction factor $\hat R$, which compares variance between chains with variance within them
<Cite id="gelman1992" />, and the effective sample size, which converts correlated draws into an equivalent number of
independent draws <Cite id="geyer1992" />. Current practice uses rank-normalised split-$\hat R$ and bulk and tail
effective sample sizes <Cite id="vehtari2021" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> sampler converges to its target only as the
number of steps grows without bound. A finite run can fail in two ways. It may not have reached the target yet, still
remembering its starting point or confined to one mode. Or it may have reached the target but mix so slowly that its
draws carry little information. Neither failure is visible in a single chain's summary statistics. Diagnostics cannot
prove convergence, but they detect the common failures.

## Trace plots

A trace plot shows each parameter's value against the iteration number, one line per chain. Chains that have converged
and mix well overlap and look like stationary noise around a common level. Warning signs are a trend at the start
(warm-up not finished), chains at different levels (different modes or not yet converged), long flat stretches
(proposals rejected), and slow wandering (high autocorrelation).

## The potential scale reduction factor

Run $m$ chains of $n$ draws each, and let $\theta_{ij}$ be draw $i$ of chain $j$. Let $\bar\theta_j$ be the mean of chain
$j$, $\bar\theta$ the mean of all draws, and $s_j^2$ the sample variance within chain $j$. Define the between-chain and
within-chain variances

$$
B = \frac{n}{m - 1}\sum_{j=1}^{m} \paren{\bar\theta_j - \bar\theta}^2, \qquad W = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} s_j^2,
$$

a pooled estimate of the target variance, and the ratio

$$
\hat V = \frac{n - 1}{n}\, W + \frac{1}{n}\, B, \qquad \hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\hat V}{W}}
$$

<Cite id="gelman1992" />.

**Why it works.** If every chain samples the target, both $W$ and $\hat V$ estimate the target variance and $\hat R$
is close to 1. If the chains started far apart and have not yet mixed, two things happen. Each chain has explored only
part of the distribution, so $W$ underestimates the variance. The chains' means disagree, so $B$ is large and $\hat V$
overestimates it. Both push $\hat R$ above 1. As $n \to \infty$ the chains converge, $B/n \to 0$, and $\hat R \to 1$.
The name reflects the interpretation: $\hat R$ is the factor by which the scale of the current distribution of draws
might shrink if the chains were run for ever.

**Split $\hat R$.** Cutting each chain in half and treating the halves as $2m$ chains detects a chain that drifts
within its run, which the original statistic misses when all chains drift together.

**Rank normalisation.** $\hat R$ uses means and variances, which do not exist for heavy-tailed targets and are
insensitive to differences in scale. Vehtari et al. replace every draw by the normal quantile of its rank among all
draws, then compute split-$\hat R$ on these normal scores, and repeat on the folded draws $\abs{\theta - \operatorname{median}}$
to detect chains with the same location but different scales. They recommend using samples only when $\hat R < 1.01$
<Cite id="vehtari2021" />. The older threshold of 1.1 is too permissive.

## Effective sample size

Draws from a Markov chain are autocorrelated. For a stationary chain with autocorrelations $\rho_k$ at lag $k$, the
variance of the average of $N$ draws is approximately $\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\tau$, with integrated autocorrelation time

$$
\tau = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_k.
$$

The **effective sample size** is $\mathrm{ESS} = N/\tau$: the number of independent draws that would give the same
variance.

**Estimating $\tau$.** The sample autocorrelations $\hat\rho_k$ are noisy at large lags, and summing all of them gives
an estimate with variance that does not shrink. Geyer's **initial positive sequence** estimator sums the pairs
$\hat\rho_{2k} + \hat\rho_{2k+1}$ while they remain positive, and stops at the first negative pair. For a reversible
chain the true pair sums are positive and decreasing, which justifies the truncation <Cite id="geyer1992" />.

**Several chains.** Computing the autocorrelations from the combined variance $\hat V$ rather than per chain penalises
chains that disagree, so the ESS falls when $\hat R$ is high <Cite id="vehtari2021" />.

**Bulk and tail.** The **bulk-ESS** is computed on rank-normalised draws and measures precision for the centre of the
distribution, such as means and medians. The **tail-ESS** is the smaller of the ESS values for the indicators of being
below the 5% and above the 95% quantiles, and measures precision for interval endpoints. Both should exceed about 100
per chain <Cite id="vehtari2021" />.

**Monte Carlo <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>.** The standard error of a posterior mean estimate is
$\mathrm{MCSE} = \hat\sigma/\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}}$, where $\hat\sigma$ is the posterior standard deviation. Report
estimates to the number of digits the MCSE supports.

## Worked example

**$\hat R$ for two disagreeing chains.** Chain 1 has draws $(1.0, 1.2, 0.8, 1.0)$ and chain 2 has
$(2.0, 2.2, 1.8, 2.0)$. So $m = 2$ and $n = 4$.

- Chain means are 1.0 and 2.0; the overall mean is 1.5.
- Each chain has sample variance $(0 + 0.04 + 0.04 + 0)/3 = 0.0267$, so $W = 0.0267$.
- $B = \frac{4}{1}\paren{0.5^2 + 0.5^2} = 2$.
- $\hat V = \frac34 \times 0.0267 + \frac14 \times 2 = 0.520$.
- $\hat R = \sqrt{0.520/0.0267} = 4.42$.

Each chain looks stable on its own, but the chains sample different regions, and $\hat R$ is far above 1.

**ESS for an autoregressive chain.** Suppose a chain behaves like an AR(1) process with coefficient $\phi = 0.9$, so
$\rho_k = 0.9^k$. Then $\tau = 1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1} 0.9^k = 1 + 2 \times 9 = 19$, which equals $(1 + \phi)/(1 - \phi)$. A
run of $N = 1000$ draws has $\mathrm{ESS} = 1000/19 = 52.6$, and the MCSE of the mean is
$\sigma/\sqrt{52.6} = 0.138\,\sigma$. Estimating the mean to within $0.01\,\sigma$ would need an ESS of $10^4$, or about
190,000 draws.

## Sampler-specific diagnostics

<NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo">Hamiltonian Monte Carlo</NoteLink> has diagnostics of its own
<Cite id="betancourt2017" />:

- **Divergent transitions.** A trajectory whose energy error blows up signals a region of high curvature that the
  integrator cannot follow. Even a few divergences mean the posterior may be explored with bias.
- **Maximum tree depth.** NUTS trajectories that hit the depth limit suggest a poorly scaled posterior and slow
  exploration.
- **Energy diagnostics.** A low ratio of the variance of energy changes between iterations to the variance of the
  energy (the E-BFMI) means momentum resampling explores the energy levels slowly, typically because of heavy tails.

## Trade-offs

- **Necessary, not sufficient.** All chains can miss the same mode. Diagnostics compare chains with each other, not
  with the target. Dispersed starting points make shared failures less likely.
- **Per quantity.** Diagnostics are computed per parameter or per derived quantity. A model can have good ESS for its
  means and poor ESS for its variance parameters. Check the quantities that the analysis reports.
- **Warm-up is excluded.** Diagnostics apply to the draws kept after warm-up. Adaptation of step sizes during warm-up
  breaks the Markov property, so those draws are discarded.
- **Simulation-based checks.** For a new sampler or model, simulation-based calibration, which fits many simulated
  datasets and checks the ranks of the true parameters among the posterior draws, tests the whole inference pipeline.
