<Definition>
Let $\xvec$ have density $p$ and let $f$ be a function with $\mu = \expect_p[f(\xvec)]$ finite. Given independent
samples $\xvec^{(1)}, \dots, \xvec^{(N)} \sim p$, the Monte Carlo estimator of $\mu$ is

$$
\hat\mu_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f\paren{\xvec^{(i)}}.
$$

It is unbiased, it converges to $\mu$ as $N \to \infty$, and when $\sigma^2 = \var_p(f(\xvec))$ is finite its
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ <Cite id="metropolis1949" />
<Cite id="owen2013" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Probabilistic inference is full of integrals: posterior means, predictive densities,
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihoods</NoteLink> and expected losses. Each is an expectation
$\int f(\xvec)\, p(\xvec)\, d\xvec$. Any integral $\int g(\xvec)\, d\xvec$ over a bounded region can be written this way
too, with $p$ uniform on the region and $f = g \cdot \text{volume}$. Deterministic quadrature on a grid with $m$ points
per axis needs $m^d$ evaluations in $d$ dimensions, which is infeasible for $d$ beyond a handful. Monte Carlo
integration replaces the grid with random points. Its accuracy depends on the variance of $f$, not on the dimension.

## Mechanism

**Unbiased.** Each term has expectation $\mu$, so by linearity $\expect[\hat\mu_N] = \mu$.

**Variance.** The terms are independent, so their variances add:

$$
\var(\hat\mu_N) = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \var\paren{f(\xvec^{(i)})} = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}.
$$

The standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$. Halving it needs four times the samples.

**Consistent.** By the <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">strong law of large numbers</NoteLink>, $\hat\mu_N \to \mu$
with probability 1 whenever $\mu$ is finite, even if $\sigma^2$ is infinite.

**Error bars.** When $\sigma^2$ is finite, the <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> gives
$\sqrt{N}(\hat\mu_N - \mu) \to \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$ in distribution. Replacing $\sigma^2$ by the sample variance
$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_i \paren{f(\xvec^{(i)}) - \hat\mu_N}^2$ gives an approximate 95%
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> $\hat\mu_N \pm 1.96\, \hat\sigma/\sqrt{N}$. The same
samples give both the estimate and its error.

**Dimension.** The rate $N^{-1/2}$ holds in every dimension. A product rule of order $r$ on a grid has error
$O(N^{-r/d})$ with $N$ total points in $d$ dimensions, which beats Monte Carlo only while $r/d > 1/2$
<Cite id="caflisch1998" />. The dimension enters Monte Carlo only through $\sigma^2$, which can still grow with $d$.

## Worked example: estimating π

Let $\xvec = (u, v)$ be uniform on the unit square and $f(\xvec) = 4\, \indicator[u^2 + v^2 \le 1]$. The quarter disc
has area $\pi/4$, so $\expect[f] = \pi$. The indicator is Bernoulli with $q = \pi/4 = 0.785$, so

$$
\sigma = 4\sqrt{q(1 - q)} = 4\sqrt{0.785 \times 0.215} = 1.642.
$$

With $N = 10^4$ samples the standard error is $1.642/100 = 0.0164$, and the 95% interval has half-width $0.032$. The
estimate typically has two correct decimal places. One more decimal place needs $10^6$ samples. This is the typical
behaviour of Monte Carlo integration: cheap to reach low accuracy, expensive to reach high accuracy.

## Variance reduction

The error is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, so reducing $\sigma$ is equivalent to taking more samples. Several estimators keep the
expectation and reduce the variance <Cite id="owen2013" />.

- **Importance sampling** draws from a different density that puts more samples where $\abs{f}\,p$ is large, and
  reweights. See <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink>.
- **Control variates** subtract a correlated function $g$ with known mean. The estimator averages
  $f(\xvec) - c\,\paren{g(\xvec) - \expect[g]}$, which is unbiased for any constant $c$.
- **Antithetic variates** pair each sample with a mirrored one, such as $u$ and $1 - u$ for a uniform $u$. When $f$ is
  monotone the two values are negatively correlated, and their average varies less than two independent values.
- **Stratification** splits the domain into regions and samples each in proportion to its probability, which removes
  the variance due to how many samples land in each region.
- **Quasi-Monte Carlo** replaces random points by low-discrepancy sequences, with error close to $O(N^{-1})$ for smooth
  integrands of moderate dimension <Cite id="caflisch1998" />.

<Derivation title="The optimal control variate">
Let $h_c(\xvec) = f(\xvec) - c\,(g(\xvec) - \expect[g])$. Its variance is

$$
\var(h_c) = \var(f) - 2c\,\cov(f, g) + c^2 \var(g).
$$

This is a quadratic in $c$, minimised where its derivative $-2\cov(f, g) + 2c\var(g)$ is zero, at
$c^* = \cov(f, g)/\var(g)$. Substituting,

$$
\var(h_{c^*}) = \var(f) - \frac{\cov(f, g)^2}{\var(g)} = \var(f)\paren{1 - \rho^2},
$$

where $\rho$ is the correlation between $f(\xvec)$ and $g(\xvec)$. A control variate with $\abs{\rho} = 0.9$ removes 81%
of the variance, which is worth five times as many samples. In practice $c^*$ is estimated from the same samples, the
regression slope of $f$ on $g$.
</Derivation>

## Trade-offs

- **Sampling $p$ must be possible.** Plain Monte Carlo needs independent samples from $p$, obtained for example by
  <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">inverse transform sampling</NoteLink> or
  <NoteLink to="rejection-sampling">rejection sampling</NoteLink>. Posteriors usually cannot be sampled directly;
  <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> extend the method to them.
- **Slow convergence.** The rate $N^{-1/2}$ cannot be improved by more computing alone. Low-dimensional smooth integrals
  are better done by quadrature.
- **Infinite variance.** If $\var(f) = \infty$, the estimate still converges but the central limit theorem fails, and
  the sample variance gives meaningless error bars. For example, with $x$ uniform on $(0, 1)$ and $f(x) = x^{-1/2}$,
  the mean is 2 but $\expect[f^2] = \int_0^1 x^{-1}\, dx$ diverges. The estimate converges slowly, with occasional large
  jumps.
- **Rare events.** For a probability $q$ estimated by an indicator, the relative standard error is
  $\sqrt{(1 - q)/(Nq)}$. Estimating $q = 10^{-6}$ to 10% relative error needs about $10^8$ samples. Importance sampling
  is the usual fix.
