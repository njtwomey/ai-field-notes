<Definition>
**Item response theory** (IRT) models the responses $x_{pi} \in \set{0, 1}$ of persons $p$ to test items $i$. Each
person has a latent ability $\theta_p \in \reals$. Each item has a curve $P_i(\theta)$, its **item characteristic
curve**, giving the probability of a correct answer at ability $\theta$. Responses are independent given the
abilities. The **four-parameter logistic** (4PL) curve is

$$
P_i(\theta) = c_i + (d_i - c_i)\,\sigma\paren{a_i(\theta - b_i)}, \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
$$

with difficulty $b_i$, discrimination $a_i > 0$, lower asymptote $0 \le c_i < d_i$ and upper asymptote $d_i \le 1$. The
3PL fixes $d_i = 1$, the 2PL also fixes $c_i = 0$, and the **Rasch** or 1PL model also fixes $a_i = 1$
<Cite id="rasch1960" /> <Cite id="lord1968" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A test question is an opponent with a fixed strength. A person "beats" the item by answering it correctly. Under the
Rasch model,

$$
\pr(x_{pi} = 1) = \sigma(\theta_p - b_i) = \frac{e^{\theta_p}}{e^{\theta_p} + e^{b_i}},
$$

which is the <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Bradley–Terry model</NoteLink> for a game between person $p$ and
item $i$. Every result for Bradley–Terry carries over: only differences $\theta_p - b_i$ are identified, the fit is a
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, and the maximum likelihood needs a connected pattern
of successes and failures. The difference from sports is that the items never play each other and the persons never play
each other; every game is between the two groups.

Each parameter of the 4PL curve has one job.

- **Difficulty $b_i$** shifts the curve along the ability axis. In the 1PL and 2PL, $b_i$ is the ability at which the
  chance of success is $\tfrac12$.
- **Discrimination $a_i$** sets the slope at $b_i$, which is $a_i(d_i - c_i)/4$. A steep item separates people just
  below $b_i$ from those just above it; a flat item gives little information at any ability.
- **Guessing floor $c_i$** is the success rate of a person with very low ability, for example about $1/4$ on a
  four-option multiple-choice item.
- **Ceiling $d_i$** is below 1 when able people sometimes slip, through carelessness or a badly worded item
  <Cite id="barton1981" />.

## Logistic and normal ogive

The first IRT models used the **normal ogive** $\Phi\paren{a_i(\theta - b_i)}$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal
cdf <Cite id="lord1968" />. This is the Thurstone form of a paired comparison; the logistic curve is the Bradley–Terry
form. The two are close after rescaling: $\abs{\Phi(z) - \sigma(1.702\,z)} < 0.0095$ for all $z$ (checked numerically).
A normal-ogive discrimination $a$ therefore corresponds to a logistic discrimination of about $1.702\,a$. Many programs
write the logistic model as $\sigma\paren{D a_i(\theta - b_i)}$ with $D = 1.7$ or $1.702$ so that its parameters can be
read on the normal-ogive scale <Cite id="camilli1994" />. The constant $D$ rescales $a_i$ and leaves the fit unchanged.

## Information

How precisely a test measures ability depends on where the ability lies. For one Bernoulli response with success
probability $P(\theta)$, the log-likelihood is $\ell(\theta) = x\log P + (1 - x)\log(1 - P)$, and its
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is

$$
\ell'(\theta) = \frac{x - P(\theta)}{P(\theta)\paren{1 - P(\theta)}}\, P'(\theta).
$$

The response has mean $P$ and variance $P(1 - P)$, so the <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss>,
the variance of $\ell'$, is

$$
I_i(\theta) = \frac{P_i'(\theta)^2}{P_i(\theta)\paren{1 - P_i(\theta)}} .
$$

For the 4PL curve, $P_i'(\theta) = a_i(d_i - c_i)\,s(1 - s)$ with $s = \sigma\paren{a_i(\theta - b_i)}$.

- **2PL.** With $c_i = 0$ and $d_i = 1$, $P_i' = a_i P_i(1 - P_i)$, and the information simplifies to
  $I_i(\theta) = a_i^2 P_i(\theta)\paren{1 - P_i(\theta)}$. It peaks at $\theta = b_i$ with value $a_i^2/4$.
- **3PL.** Guessing lowers the information everywhere, because a correct answer might be a guess. The peak also moves
  above the difficulty, to $\theta = b_i + \frac{1}{a_i}\log\frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 8c_i}}{2}$ <Cite id="lord1968" />. For
  $a_i = 1.5$ and $c_i = 0.2$ the peak is at $b_i + 0.178$ with height 0.383, against 0.5625 at $b_i$ without guessing.

Responses are independent given $\theta$, so information adds. The **test information** is
$I(\theta) = \sum_i I_i(\theta)$, and the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the
maximum-likelihood ability estimate is approximately $1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}$. A test built from items of one difficulty
measures well near that difficulty and poorly elsewhere. A test for a broad population needs items spread along the
ability axis.

## Estimating abilities and item parameters

With the item parameters known, a person's ability is estimated from their responses by maximum likelihood, or by the
posterior mode or mean under a prior. With the abilities unknown too, the item parameters are calibrated by marginal
maximum likelihood, integrating the abilities out, usually with the <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">EM
algorithm</NoteLink> of Bock and Aitkin <Cite id="bock1981" />. Both are derived, with a worked 2PL example, in
<NoteLink to="ability-estimation-and-item-calibration">ability estimation and item calibration</NoteLink>.

Items scored in more than two categories, and tests that measure more than one ability, need the extensions in
<NoteLink to="polytomous-and-multidimensional-item-response-models">polytomous and multidimensional item response
models</NoteLink>. With test instances as items and trained models as respondents, the same models describe datasets and
benchmarks; see <NoteLink to="item-response-theory-for-machine-learning" /> and, for probabilities as responses, the
<NoteLink to="beta-cubed-item-response-model">β³ model</NoteLink>.

## Online updates and adaptive testing

An **adaptive test** gives each person the item with the most information at their current ability estimate, then
re-estimates. Each item is then close to a fair contest, as a matchmaker in a game picks an opponent of similar skill.

The <NoteLink to="skill-rating">skill-rating</NoteLink> view gives a cheap alternative to refitting. After each answer,
update both the person and the item with the <NoteLink to="elo-rating">Elo</NoteLink> rule,
$\theta_p \leftarrow \theta_p + K(x - P)$ and $b_i \leftarrow b_i - K(x - P)$. This is one stochastic-gradient step on
the Rasch log-likelihood. Adaptive learning systems use it to track students and calibrate questions at the same time
<Cite id="pelanek2016" />. A Bayesian version keeps a Gaussian belief over each $\theta_p$ and $b_i$ and updates it as
<NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink> does, with the uncertainty acting as a per-person and per-item
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>.

## Assumptions

- **One dimension.** A single ability explains all items. Tests that mix skills need multidimensional models.
- **Local <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.** Given $\theta_p$, responses to different items are
  independent. Items that share a reading passage or build on each other violate this.
- **Monotonicity.** The chance of a correct answer rises with ability.
- **Invariance.** An item's parameters are the same for every person. An item that is harder for one group than
  another at equal ability shows **differential item functioning** and breaks this assumption.

## Pitfalls

- **Arbitrary scale.** Adding a constant to all $\theta_p$ and all $b_i$ changes nothing, and in the 2PL scaling
  every $\theta_p$ and every $b_i$ by $k$ while dividing every $a_i$ by $k$ changes nothing either.
  Abilities mean something only relative to the reference population that fixed the scale.
- **Guessing and slipping are hard to estimate.** The floor $c_i$ is informed only by low-ability people and the
  ceiling $d_i$ only by high-ability people. With few of them, the 3PL and 4PL estimates are unstable, and priors on
  $c_i$ and $d_i$ are common.
- **All-correct and all-wrong patterns.** A person who answers every item correctly has no finite maximum-likelihood
  ability, just as an undefeated player has no finite Bradley–Terry strength. A prior fixes this.
