at AISTATS 2019 as "β³-IRT: A New Item Response Model and its Applications" <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />. The model, its

<Definition>
The **β³ item response model** describes a response $p_{ij} \in (0, 1)$ of respondent $i$ to item $j$ by a
<NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta distribution</NoteLink>,

$$
p_{ij} \sim \Beta(\alpha_{ij}, \beta_{ij}), \qquad \alpha_{ij} = \paren{\frac{\theta_i}{\delta_j}}^{a_j}, \qquad
\beta_{ij} = \paren{\frac{1 - \theta_i}{1 - \delta_j}}^{a_j},
$$

with ability $\theta_i \in (0, 1)$, difficulty $\delta_j \in (0, 1)$ and discrimination $a_j \in \reals$. The priors are
$\theta_i \sim \Beta(1, 1)$, $\delta_j \sim \Beta(1, 1)$ and $a_j \sim \Gauss(1, \sigma_0^2)$
<Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Binary item response models score each answer right or wrong. A classifier's output is richer: a probability for the
correct class. A classifier that gives the correct class 0.49 and one that gives it 0.01 are both wrong, and a binary
model treats them alike. The β³ model keeps the probability as the response.

Ability and difficulty live on $(0, 1)$, and the model compares them by ratio. When $\theta_i > \delta_j$, the ratio
$\theta_i/\delta_j$ exceeds 1 and $(1 - \theta_i)/(1 - \delta_j)$ falls below 1, so with $a_j > 0$ the first shape
parameter $\alpha_{ij}$ is the larger and the response leans towards 1. When $\theta_i < \delta_j$ it leans towards 0.
The discrimination $a_j$ is a power that sharpens or flattens this comparison, and a negative power reverses it.

## The item characteristic curve

The mean of $\Beta(\alpha, \beta)$ is $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$. Dividing through by $\alpha_{ij}$,

$$
\expect[p_{ij}] = \frac{1}{1 + \beta_{ij}/\alpha_{ij}}, \qquad \frac{\beta_{ij}}{\alpha_{ij}} =
\paren{\frac{1 - \theta_i}{\theta_i}\cdot\frac{\delta_j}{1 - \delta_j}}^{a_j} =
\exp\paren{-a_j\paren{\operatorname{logit}\theta_i - \operatorname{logit}\delta_j}},
$$

with $\operatorname{logit} u = \log\paren{u/(1 - u)}$. The item characteristic curve is therefore

$$
\expect[p_{ij} \mid \theta_i, \delta_j, a_j] = \sigma\paren{a_j\paren{\operatorname{logit}\theta_i -
\operatorname{logit}\delta_j}}, \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} .
$$

It is the <NoteLink to="item-response-theory">2PL</NoteLink> curve with ability and difficulty replaced by their logits.
The mean response is $\tfrac12$ at $\theta_i = \delta_j$, so $\delta_j$ is a location parameter as in the 2PL. Since
$d\operatorname{logit}\theta/d\theta = 1/\paren{\theta(1 - \theta)}$ and $\sigma'(0) = \tfrac14$, the slope there is
$a_j/\paren{4\delta_j(1 - \delta_j)}$ <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

On the logit scale every curve is a logistic. On the ability scale $(0, 1)$ the shape depends on $a_j$, because the
logit stretches both ends. Near $\theta = 0$, $\operatorname{logit}\theta \approx \log\theta$, so for $a_j > 0$

$$
\expect[p_{ij}] \approx \exp\paren{a_j(\log\theta - \operatorname{logit}\delta_j)} = \paren{\frac{1 - \delta_j}{\delta_j}}^{a_j}\theta^{a_j},
$$

a power of $\theta$, and by symmetry $1 - \expect[p_{ij}]$ is proportional to $(1 - \theta)^{a_j}$ near $\theta = 1$.

- **$a_j > 1$.** The slope $\propto a_j\theta^{a_j - 1}$ is zero at both ends. The curve is flat at the ends and steep
  in the middle: a **sigmoid**, like the 2PL.
- **$a_j = 1$.** The slope is finite and non-zero at the ends. The curve is $\theta(1 - \delta_j)/\paren{\theta(1 -
  \delta_j) + \delta_j(1 - \theta)}$, a ratio of linear functions with no inflection point in $(0, 1)$.
- **$0 < a_j < 1$.** The slope is infinite at both ends. The curve is steep at the ends and flat in the middle: an
  **anti-sigmoid**. Such an item separates the weakest and the strongest respondents from the rest, and says little
  about respondents near $\delta_j$.
- **$a_j < 0$.** The curve decreases: a decreasing sigmoid for $a_j < -1$ and a decreasing anti-sigmoid for
  $-1 < a_j < 0$. Stronger respondents give lower responses.

A logistic curve on $\reals$ cannot produce the anti-sigmoid. In the student-answer data of the paper about 46% of
questions were fitted with $\abs{a_j} < 1$, a non-logistic shape <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

## The response distribution

The mean is one summary; the beta distribution also sets the spread. At $\theta_i = \delta_j$ both ratios equal 1, so
$\alpha_{ij} = \beta_{ij} = 1$ for every $a_j$, and the response is uniform on $(0, 1)$. A respondent whose ability
matches an item's difficulty is maximally uncertain about it. Away from $\delta_j$ one shape parameter grows and the
other shrinks, and the mass moves to one end.

## Worked example

An item has difficulty $\delta = 0.6$ and discrimination $a = 2$. For a classifier of ability $\theta = 0.8$,

$$
\alpha = \paren{\tfrac{0.8}{0.6}}^2 = 1.778, \qquad \beta = \paren{\tfrac{0.2}{0.4}}^2 = 0.25, \qquad
\expect[p] = \frac{1.778}{1.778 + 0.25} = 0.877 .
$$

The logit form agrees: $2\paren{\operatorname{logit}0.8 - \operatorname{logit}0.6} = 2(1.386 - 0.405) = 1.962$ and
$\sigma(1.962) = 0.877$. Because $\beta < 1$, the density rises without bound towards $p = 1$: the median response is
0.970, and the chance of a response below $\tfrac12$ is 0.067. For a weaker classifier with $\theta = 0.3$,
$\alpha = 0.25$, $\beta = 3.06$ and the mean response is 0.075.

Now take an item with $\delta = 0.2$ and $a = -1.5$. For the classifier with $\theta = 0.8$, $\alpha = 4^{-1.5} = 0.125$
and $\beta = 0.25^{-1.5} = 8$, so the expected probability of the given label is 0.015. A strong classifier is expected
to reject the label almost completely: the signature of a wrong label.



## Inference

The paper fits the model in two ways <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

- **Maximum likelihood**, by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> on the
  <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">log loss</NoteLink> of the predicted mean response, for predicting
  students' answers.
- **<Gloss name="vi">Variational inference</Gloss>** for classifiers, to obtain posteriors over all latent variables.
  The variational posteriors of $\theta_i$ and $\delta_j$ are logit-normal, an approximation to the beta that admits the <NoteLink
  to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink>. Optimisation is coordinate ascent: abilities and
  difficulties are updated to convergence with the discriminations fixed, then the discriminations are updated, and the
  cycle repeats. The implementation used the <NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">probabilistic
  programming</NoteLink> library Edward <Cite id="chen2019beta3code" />.

The alternation addresses a symmetry. A response near 1 means $\alpha_{ij} > 1 > \beta_{ij}$, which holds when
$\theta_i > \delta_j$ with $a_j > 0$ and also when $\theta_i < \delta_j$ with $a_j < 0$. The likelihood alone cannot
choose, and the prior $a_j \sim \Gauss(1, 1)$ expresses the assumption that positive discrimination is the more common
case <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

## β³ for classifiers

Each respondent is a classifier and each item a test instance. The response is the probability that classifier $i$
gives the labelled class of instance $j$, $p_{ij} = \sum_k \indicator(y_j = k)\,p_{ijk}$ <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

- **Instance parameters.** Instances near the class boundary get high difficulty and low discrimination, and instances
  far from it get low difficulty and high discrimination. Most instances with
  <NoteLink to="item-response-theory-for-machine-learning">negative discrimination</NoteLink> had flipped labels, and
  without noise in the test labels no negative discriminations were inferred <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.
- **Ability as a metric.** Solving the mean curve for ability gives the ability that one mean response implies,
  $\operatorname{logit}\theta_i = \operatorname{logit}\delta_j + \operatorname{logit}(\bar p_{ij})/a_j$. Each instance
  pulls the ability towards its own implied value, with strength set by its parameters. A low response on a hard, weakly
  discriminating instance lowers the ability little, whereas it enters log loss and the Brier score with full weight.
  Ability therefore ranks classifiers differently from accuracy, log loss and the Brier score
  <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.
- **Robustness to noisy test labels.** The inferred abilities of the classifiers stayed nearly constant as the fraction
  of flipped test labels rose towards one half <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />. The flipped instances are explained by
  negative discriminations, not by lower abilities.

Unlike the paper's variational fit, the figure below fits the posterior mode, by
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> ascent on unconstrained $\operatorname{logit}\theta_i$,
$\operatorname{logit}\delta_j$ and $a_j$. The $\Beta(1, 1)$ prior on $\theta_i$ becomes the logistic density on
$u_i = \operatorname{logit}\theta_i$, whose log-derivative is $1 - 2\sigma(u_i)$. The gradient of the log-density of one
response with respect to $\alpha$ is $\log p - \psi(\alpha) + \psi(\alpha + \beta)$, with $\psi$ the digamma function,
and the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> through $\alpha = \exp\paren{a(\log\theta - \log\delta)}$ gives
the rest. Started with every $a_j = 1$, a mislabelled instance settles at a local optimum where it is very hard with
near-zero discrimination. The figure starts each $a_j$ at $\pm 1$ by the sign of the instance's correlation with the
classifiers' mean responses, which reaches a higher posterior.

## Relation to other continuous item response models

Noel and Dauvier's beta item response model also draws responses from a beta distribution, with shape parameters
$e^{(\theta_i - \delta_j)/2}$ and $e^{-(\theta_i - \delta_j)/2}$ on the real line <Cite id="noel2007" />. Its mean is
the logistic $\sigma(\theta_i - \delta_j)$, a one-parameter curve with fixed slope. The β³ model adds a discrimination,
moves ability and difficulty to $(0, 1)$, and through that bounded scale gains the non-logistic shapes
<Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

## Assumptions

- **Binary classification.** The response is one probability. The paper proposes a Dirichlet in place of the beta for
  several classes <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.
- **Clean training data.** Noise-detection by negative discrimination works when the classifiers were trained on few
  noisy labels. Noise in the training set lowers every classifier's ability and hides the noisy test instances
  <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.
- **Responses inside $(0, 1)$.** A response of exactly 0 or 1 has zero density under most beta distributions. Hard
  classifiers such as a 1-nearest-neighbour rule must be smoothed or clipped, as in the figure.

## Pitfalls

- **Non-identifiability.** The same response pattern can be explained by $\theta_i > \delta_j$ with $a_j > 0$ or by
  $\theta_i < \delta_j$ with $a_j < 0$. The prior mean of $a_j$ and the initialisation decide which, and a poor start
  leaves mislabelled instances looking merely hard.
- **Scale is relative.** Only $\operatorname{logit}\theta_i - \operatorname{logit}\delta_j$ enters the mean, so
  abilities and difficulties are comparable with each other within one fit, not across fits.
- **Calibration matters.** The response is a probability, so a poorly <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">calibrated
  </NoteLink> but accurate classifier can get a lower ability than a calibrated one of equal accuracy. That is the point
  of the metric, and a reason not to read ability as accuracy.
