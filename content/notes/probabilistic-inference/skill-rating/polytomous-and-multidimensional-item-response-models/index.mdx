<Definition>
A **polytomous** item response model gives, for an item scored in ordered categories $k = 0, 1, \dots, m$, the
probability $\pr(X = k \mid \theta)$ of each score as a function of ability $\theta$. A **multidimensional** model gives
each person an ability vector $\thetavec \in \reals^D$ and each item a vector of discriminations $\avec_i$, with the
probability of success depending on $\avec_i^\top\thetavec$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Many items are not simply right or wrong. An essay is marked 0 to 4, a survey answer runs from "strongly disagree" to
"strongly agree", and a maths problem earns partial credit for correct steps. Two ideas turn the binary
<NoteLink to="item-response-theory">item response model</NoteLink> into a model for ordered scores. The **cumulative**
idea models "score at least $k$" as a binary item for each $k$. The **adjacent-category** idea models each step from
$k - 1$ to $k$ as a binary contest.

Likewise, a test may measure more than one ability, such as algebra and reading. A word problem needs both. A
multidimensional model gives each item a direction in ability space.

## Graded response model

Samejima's **graded response model** treats each threshold as a 2PL item with a shared discrimination
<Cite id="samejima1969" />:

$$
\pr(X \ge k \mid \theta) = \sigma\paren{a(\theta - b_k)}, \quad k = 1, \dots, m, \qquad b_1 < b_2 < \dots < b_m,
$$

with $\pr(X \ge 0) = 1$ and $\pr(X \ge m + 1) = 0$. Category probabilities are differences of neighbouring curves:
$\pr(X = k) = \pr(X \ge k) - \pr(X \ge k + 1)$. The ordering of the $b_k$ keeps every difference positive.

**Example.** Take $a = 1.5$, thresholds $b = (-1, 0.5, 1.5)$, and $\theta = 0$. The cumulative probabilities are
$\sigma(1.5) = 0.8176$, $\sigma(-0.75) = 0.3208$ and $\sigma(-2.25) = 0.0953$. The category probabilities are
$(0.1824, 0.4968, 0.2255, 0.0953)$ for scores 0 to 3.

## Partial credit models

Masters' **partial credit model** treats each step from $k - 1$ to $k$ as a Rasch item with its own step difficulty
$\delta_k$ <Cite id="masters1982" />. Conditional on the score being $k - 1$ or $k$, the chance of $k$ is
$\sigma(\theta - \delta_k)$. Chaining these conditionals gives

$$
\pr(X = k \mid \theta) = \frac{\exp\paren{\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\theta - \delta_j)}}{\sum_{h=0}^{m}\exp\paren{\sum_{j=1}^{h}(\theta - \delta_j)}},
$$

a softmax over categories whose logits are cumulative sums (the empty sum for $k = 0$ is 0). Unlike the graded model,
the step difficulties need not be ordered: a step can be harder than the next one.

The **generalised partial credit model** multiplies each $(\theta - \delta_j)$ by an item discrimination $a$
<Cite id="muraki1992" />. It is to the partial credit model what the 2PL is to the Rasch model.

**Example.** With $\delta = (-1, 0.5, 1.5)$ and $\theta = 0$, the cumulative sums are $0, 1, 0.5, -1$. Their
exponentials $1, 2.718, 1.649, 0.368$ sum to 5.735, giving probabilities $(0.174, 0.474, 0.288, 0.064)$ for scores 0
to 3. With the same numbers as thresholds, the graded response model with $a = 1.5$ gave a similar but not identical
distribution: the two families are different models, not reparameterisations.

Both examples were checked numerically.

## Multidimensional models

The **compensatory** multidimensional 2PL model is

$$
\pr(x_{pi} = 1 \mid \thetavec_p) = \sigma\paren{\avec_i^\top\thetavec_p + d_i},
$$

with an intercept $d_i$ in place of $-a_i b_i$ <Cite id="reckase2009" />. Strength in one dimension compensates for
weakness in another, because only the weighted sum matters. The model is a logistic
<NoteLink to="factor-analysis">factor analysis</NoteLink> of binary data: $\avec_i$ are the item loadings and
$\thetavec_p$ the person's factor scores.

Two properties follow from the linear form.

- **Rotation.** Replacing $\thetavec$ by $\Rmat\thetavec$ and every $\avec_i$ by $\Rmat^{-\top}\avec_i$ leaves every
  probability unchanged. As in factor analysis, the dimensions need constraints or a rotation to be interpreted.
- **Direction of measurement.** An item measures best along its own direction $\avec_i/\norm{\avec_i}$, with
  multidimensional discrimination $\norm{\avec_i}$. A test measures an ability combination well only if some items
  point that way.

Estimation extends the one-dimensional methods: <NoteLink to="ability-estimation-and-item-calibration">marginal maximum likelihood</NoteLink> with EM over a multidimensional quadrature
grid for few dimensions, and Monte Carlo or variational methods for more.

## Assumptions

- **Ordered categories.** Both polytomous families assume the categories are ordered along the ability. Unordered
  options, such as which distractor a person chose, need a nominal model.
- **Local independence** holds given the whole ability vector, not given a single summary score.

## Pitfalls

- **Sparse categories.** A category that almost nobody uses gives a threshold or step difficulty with a large
  <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>. Merging it with a neighbour is common.
- **Reversed step difficulties** in the partial credit model are not an error in themselves, but they mean the middle
  category is never the most likely score at any ability.
- **Too many dimensions.** Every extra dimension adds a loading to every item and a coordinate to every person, and
  the loadings are identified only up to rotation. Confirmatory structures, in which each item loads on a named subset
  of dimensions, are easier to estimate and to interpret.
