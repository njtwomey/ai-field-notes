<Definition>
Let $M$ models $i = 1, \dots, M$ each predict the labels of $N$ test instances $j = 1, \dots, N$, and let
$x_{ij} \in \set{0, 1}$ record whether model $i$ labels instance $j$ correctly. **Item response theory for machine
learning** fits an <NoteLink to="item-response-theory">item response model</NoteLink> to the $M \times N$ matrix with the
instances as items and the models as respondents <Cite id="martinezplumed2016" /> <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
Under the two-parameter logistic (2PL) model,

$$
\pr(x_{ij} = 1) = \sigma\paren{a_j(\theta_i - b_j)}, \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}},
$$

where $\theta_i$ is the **ability** of model $i$, $b_j$ the **difficulty** of instance $j$ and $a_j$ its
**discrimination**, which may be negative.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A benchmark is a test, and every model sat it. In a classroom, a question that strong students get right and weak
students get wrong is a good question. A question that everyone gets right says nothing about who is stronger. A question
that weak students get right and strong students get wrong is suspect: the answer key is probably wrong.

The same three cases appear among test instances. An instance far from the class boundary is right for every model and
carries no information about which model is better. An instance near the boundary separates good models from poor ones.
An instance with a wrong label is "answered correctly" only by models that fail to learn the pattern around it, so the
chance of a correct answer falls as ability rises. Item response theory gives each case a parameter.

The <NoteLink to="instance-hardness">instance hardness</NoteLink> of an instance, one minus the share of models that get
it right, merges these cases. Under the 2PL, the expected share of correct answers over a population of abilities
$\theta \sim p(\theta)$ is $\int \sigma\paren{a_j(\theta - b_j)}\,p(\theta)\,d\theta$, which depends on $a_j$ and $b_j$
together. A correctly labelled instance at the boundary and a wrongly labelled instance in the middle of a class can have
the same hardness and opposite discriminations <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.

## The parameters, read for classifiers

- **Difficulty $b_j$** is the ability at which a model has an even chance of labelling instance $j$ correctly. Instances
  near the class boundary and in small classes have high difficulty <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
- **Discrimination $a_j$** is the steepness of the item characteristic curve at $b_j$: how sharply the instance
  separates models just below $b_j$ from those just above. An instance that every model gets right has a flat curve and
  is useless for ranking.
- **Negative discrimination** means that stronger models are more often wrong. In psychometrics such items are flawed
  and are removed. In machine learning they are informative: on a toy dataset with 10% of labels flipped on purpose,
  every flipped instance received a negative discrimination, along with a few instances on the boundaries between
  classes <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
- **Guessing $c_j$**, the lower asymptote of the 3PL, does not match chance accuracy. With classifiers as respondents it
  bore no relation to the number or proportion of classes and acted as an extra degree of freedom in the fit
  <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
- **Ability $\theta_i$** is a model's skill on the test's scale. It is not a monotone function of accuracy, because
  answers are weighted by the discrimination of the instances they are given on.

## Why a wrong label gives a negative slope

Take two instances with the same features $\xvec$ and opposite labels in a two-class problem. A model that predicts one
label for $\xvec$ is right on exactly one of them, so $\pr(x_{i1} = 1 \mid \theta) = 1 - \pr(x_{i2} = 1 \mid \theta)$
for every ability. If one item characteristic curve rises with ability, the other falls
<Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />. The same holds on average for an instance with a flipped label: a model that learns
the pattern around $\xvec$ well predicts the true label with high probability and the given, wrong label with low
probability. The better the model, the lower its chance of matching the given label, and the fitted $a_j$ is negative.

The argument also shows the method's limit. It detects a label that disagrees with the features, not the event of a
flip. A correctly labelled instance that lies deep inside the other class gets a negative discrimination too, and a
flipped label that happens to agree with the features cannot be detected by any model.

## Ability is weighted by discrimination

With the instance parameters fixed, the 2PL log-likelihood of model $i$ is
$\ell(\theta_i) = \sum_j \brack{x_{ij}\log P_{ij} + (1 - x_{ij})\log(1 - P_{ij})}$ with
$P_{ij} = \sigma\paren{a_j(\theta_i - b_j)}$. Since $\partial P_{ij}/\partial\theta_i = a_j P_{ij}(1 - P_{ij})$, its
derivative is

$$
\ell'(\theta_i) = \sum_j a_j\paren{x_{ij} - P_{ij}} ,
$$

and the maximum-likelihood ability solves $\sum_j a_j x_{ij} = \sum_j a_j P_{ij}(\hat\theta_i)$
<Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />. The left side is a weighted score in which a correct answer on instance $j$ counts
$a_j$. A correct answer on a highly discriminating instance counts most. A correct answer on an instance with negative
discrimination lowers the score. Two models with equal accuracy therefore get different abilities when they are right on
different instances, and a model that "fits the noise" is penalised. Removing instances with negative discrimination
before estimating abilities made ability and accuracy much more closely related, and in most cases put an oracle
classifier that is always right back at the top <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.

A **classifier characteristic curve** plots one model's accuracy against instance difficulty, in bins. It shows where on
the difficulty scale a model's errors lie: two models with equal accuracy may differ on easy instances or only on hard
ones <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.

## Uses of the fitted parameters

- **Informative test sets.** The 2PL information of instance $j$ at ability $\theta$ is $a_j^2 P_j(\theta)\paren{1 -
  P_j(\theta)}$, largest at $\theta = b_j$. A small set of high-information instances at the abilities of interest
  ranks models almost as well as the full test set. For large language models, a fitted IRT model picks about 100
  representative MMLU questions out of 14,000 and estimates a new model's accuracy from its answers to them, with about
  2% average error across benchmarks <Cite id="polo2024tinybenchmarks" />. Adaptive testing goes further and picks each
  next instance by the model's current estimated ability.
- **Benchmark curation.** Instances with negative or near-zero discrimination add noise to a leaderboard. On NLP
  leaderboards, an IRT model of subject skill and item difficulty and discriminability identified annotation errors,
  found the examples most worth annotating, and measured how reliable the rankings are <Cite id="rodriguez2021" />.
  Lalor, Wu and Yu fitted an IRT model to many human answers on a textual-entailment test set, then placed NLP systems
  on the human ability scale; a high accuracy did not always mean a high ability <Cite id="lalor2016" />.
- **Label-noise detection.** Negative discrimination is a population-based detector of label errors, usable alongside
  single-model methods such as <NoteLink to="detecting-and-cleaning-label-errors">confident learning</NoteLink>.
- **Comparing models at the instance level.** Abilities are on one scale for a given test, whatever mix of easy and hard
  instances it contains. Accuracy is not comparable across datasets of different difficulty, and averaging ranks across
  datasets discards the size of the differences <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
- **Continuous responses.** Binary responses discard the models' probabilities. The <NoteLink
  to="beta-cubed-item-response-model">β³ model</NoteLink> fits the probability of the correct class directly, so that a
  confident error counts more than a hesitant one <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

## Worked example

Four models A, B, C and D answer five instances ($1$ = correct):

| model | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | correct |
| ----- | - | - | - | - | - | ------- |
| A     | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 4       |
| B     | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 3       |
| C     | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 3       |
| D     | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 2       |

The instance hardness, the share of wrong answers, is $0$, $0.25$, $0.5$, $0.75$ and $0.5$. Instances 3 and 5 have the
same hardness. The pattern differs. Instance 3 is right for A and B and wrong for C and D. Instance 5 is right only for
C and D, whose scores on the other four instances are 2 and 1, the two lowest.

A 2PL fitted by joint maximum a posteriori estimation, with priors $\theta_i \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$,
$a_j \sim \Gauss(0.5, 1)$ and $a_j b_j \sim \Gauss(0, 3^2)$, gives (checked with scipy):

| instance | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| -------- | - | - | - | - | - |
| $a_j$    | 0.43 | 1.05 | 1.28 | 1.04 | $-0.64$ |
| $b_j$    | $-6.05$ | $-1.04$ | 0.03 | 1.09 | 0.03 |

and abilities $\theta_A = 1.11$, $\theta_B = 0.53$, $\theta_C = -0.47$ and $\theta_D = -1.05$.

- Instance 1 is right for everyone. Its difficulty is far below every ability, and its discrimination stays near the
  prior mean of 0.5 because the data carry no information about it.
- Instances 2, 3 and 4 have positive discrimination and difficulties that rise with their hardness.
- Instance 5 has negative discrimination. The fitted chance of a correct answer falls from 0.67 for D to 0.33 for A. It
  is the instance to inspect for a wrong label.
- B and C both have 3 correct answers, yet $\theta_B - \theta_C = 1.0$. C's extra correct answer is on instance 5, and
  by the score equation above a correct answer there counts $a_5 = -0.64$, against B's on instance 3, which counts
  $1.28$.

## Assumptions

- **One ability.** A single $\theta_i$ explains all instances. A linear model and a nearest-neighbour model fail in
  different regions of the feature space, so their errors may need several dimensions.
- **Local independence.** Given the abilities, responses are independent. Models from the same family, or trained on
  the same data, share errors beyond what ability explains.
- **A varied population.** The fit needs models over a range of abilities. Martínez-Plumed and colleagues added random,
  majority-class and oracle classifiers to their 121 real ones, because with real classifiers alone many easy instances
  were right for every model and their curves were flat or singular <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
- **Clean training data.** Label noise in the training set lowers every model's ability and hides the test-set noise.
  Negative discrimination detects noisy test labels best when the models were trained on clean data
  <Cite id="chen2019beta3" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Class imbalance mimics noise.** When good classifiers neglect a minority class, a 2PL fit can give the whole
  minority class negative discrimination. A 3PL, and estimating the IRT parameters on a class-balanced resample of the
  test responses, removed most of these spurious negatives <Cite id="martinezplumed2019" />.
- **Software that forbids negative discrimination.** Psychometric packages often bound $a_j$ below by a positive
  number, since negative items are errors in a test for people. For label-noise detection the bound must be removed.
- **Arbitrary scale and sign.** Abilities and difficulties are identified only up to shift and scale, and in the 2PL
  flipping the signs of every $\theta_i$, $a_j$ and $b_j$ gives the same fit. Initialise abilities from accuracy, or put
  a prior with positive mean on $a_j$, so that strong models get high ability.
- **Cross-validated responses.** Responses must come from models that did not train on the instance.
