<Definition>
**TrueSkill Through Time** (TTT) models each player $i$ by a sequence of skills $s_i^1, s_i^2, \dots$, one per time
step (a year in the original application), with $s_i^t \sim \Gauss(s_i^{t-1}, \tau^2)$. The games played in step $t$
depend on the skills at $t$ through the <NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink> game model. Expectation
propagation runs over the whole factor graph, forward and backward in time, until the messages converge. The result is
a Gaussian belief about every player's skill at every time step, given all games <Cite id="dangauthier2007" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

TrueSkill is a filter. It processes each game once, in order, and never revisits it. That has two costs
<Cite id="dangauthier2007" />.

- **Order dependence.** Games inside one period have no meaningful order, but the filtered ratings depend on the order
  in which they are processed.
- **No backward flow.** If A beats B, and B later turns out to be strong, A's win was worth more than it looked at the
  time. A filter cannot go back and raise A's past rating.

For live matchmaking only the current rating matters, and filtering is enough. For history, such as comparing chess
players of different eras, or for fitting a model's parameters to archived games, the estimate at each time should use
all the evidence.

## Model

The factor graph is TrueSkill's, repeated for every time step and joined along each player's history.

- **Skill chains.** $s_i^{t_0} \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ at a player's first step, and
  $s_i^t \sim \Gauss(s_i^{t-1}, \tau^2)$ after that. This is a Gaussian random walk, a one-dimensional linear-Gaussian
  state-space model per player.
- **Games.** Each game in step $t$ adds TrueSkill's performance, difference and outcome factors on the skills at $t$.
  Two players who meet in step $t$ are joined through their game factor, so the graph couples all chains.

The graph has cycles: two players who meet in two different years form a loop through their two chains. Inference is
therefore approximate, as in <NoteLink to="loopy-belief-propagation">loopy belief propagation</NoteLink>.

## Inference

Two kinds of message are needed.

**Within a step: EP with cavities.** Each game keeps the Gaussian message it last sent to each of its players. To
revisit a game, divide that message out of the player's current belief to get the cavity, the belief based on
everything except this game. Run the TrueSkill update on the cavities. Store the new belief divided by the cavity as the
game's new message. A second visit therefore replaces the game's evidence rather than counting it twice
<Cite id="dangauthier2007" />. Visiting all games of a step repeatedly until the messages stop changing removes the
dependence on game order. The first sweep is exactly the filtering pass.

**Along a chain: forward and backward.** Let the belief about $s_i^{t+1}$ from everything except the link to $s_i^t$ be
$\Gauss(m, v)$. The link factor $\Gauss(s_i^{t+1}; s_i^t, \tau^2)$ turns it into a message to $s_i^t$ by integrating
over $s_i^{t+1}$:

$$
\int \Gauss(s_i^{t+1};\, s_i^t,\, \tau^2)\, \Gauss(s_i^{t+1};\, m,\, v)\, ds_i^{t+1} = \Gauss(s_i^t;\, m,\, v + \tau^2).
$$

The backward message is the later evidence, widened by the drift. The belief about $s_i^t$ is the product of three
Gaussians: the forward message from $t - 1$, the messages from the games at $t$, and the backward message from $t + 1$.
Products of Gaussians add precisions and precision-weighted means. One forward sweep followed by one backward sweep is
the <NoteLink to="kalman-smoother">Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother</NoteLink> for this chain. Because the game messages
change when the chain messages change, TTT alternates the two until nothing changes.

## Player-specific draw margins

Chess draws are common, and more common among strong players and in some eras. TTT-D gives each player a draw margin
$\varepsilon_i^t > 0$ that follows its own random walk. A game between $i$ and $j$ is a win for $i$ when
$p_i - p_j > \varepsilon_j^t$, a win for $j$ when $p_j - p_i > \varepsilon_i^t$, and a draw otherwise. A player with a
large margin is hard to beat, which captures the ability to force a draw <Cite id="dangauthier2007" />.

## Application

Dangauthier and co-authors applied TTT to about 3.5 million chess games from 150 years
<Cite id="dangauthier2007" />. The smoothed skill curves show players rising and declining over whole careers and put
players who never met on one scale, through chains of common opponents. They report that overall playing strength rose
over the period, and that modelling each player's ability to force a draw gives better predictions than a shared
margin.

The same batch inference is used in TrueSkill 2, where the whole history is refitted before each season and the online
updates run in between <Cite id="minka2018" />.

## Cost

Each sweep costs $O(G + PT)$ for $G$ games and $P$ players over $T$ steps: one TrueSkill update per game and one
Gaussian message per chain link. Convergence takes a modest number of sweeps. Every message must be stored, so memory
also grows with $G + PT$.

## Trade-offs

- **Batch only.** Adding one game can change every past estimate. Live systems use filtering and run smoothing
  offline.
- **No convergence guarantee.** EP on a graph with cycles can oscillate. Damping the messages usually fixes this.
- **Time step.** A coarse step, such as a year, assumes skill constant within it. A fine step, such as one per game,
  captures fast change but adds variables and needs a smaller $\tau$ per step.
- **Comparisons across eras.** Chains of common opponents link eras, but the scale can still drift slowly over long
  gaps with few links. The random-walk prior determines how much.
