<Definition>
A **paired-comparison model** assigns each of $m$ items a strength and gives the probability that item $i$ is preferred
to, or beats, item $j$ as a function of the difference in strengths. The **Bradley–Terry** model uses the logistic
function $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$:

$$
\pr(i \succ j) = \sigma(\theta_i - \theta_j) = \frac{\pi_i}{\pi_i + \pi_j}, \qquad \pi_i = e^{\theta_i} > 0
$$

<Cite id="bradley1952" />. The **Thurstone** model uses the standard normal cdf $\Phi$ and a noise scale $\sigma > 0$:
$\pr(i \succ j) = \Phi\paren{(s_i - s_j)/(\sqrt 2\,\sigma)}$ <Cite id="thurstone1927" />.
</Definition>

## Random utility

Both models are random-utility models. On each comparison, item $i$ produces a random utility $u_i = s_i + \epsilon_i$, with
noise terms independent across items and comparisons. The item with the larger utility wins, so
$\pr(i \succ j) = \pr(\epsilon_j - \epsilon_i < s_i - s_j)$. The model is fixed by the distribution of the noise
difference.

- **Gaussian noise.** With $\epsilon_i \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, the difference $\epsilon_j - \epsilon_i$ is
  $\Gauss(0, 2\sigma^2)$ and the win probability is $\Phi\paren{(s_i - s_j)/(\sqrt 2\sigma)}$. This is Thurstone's
  "case V", in which all items share one noise variance and the noises are uncorrelated <Cite id="thurstone1927" />.
- **Gumbel noise.** With standard Gumbel noise, the difference of two independent terms has the standard logistic
  distribution, and the win probability is $\sigma(s_i - s_j)$. This is Bradley–Terry. The same noise gives the
  softmax over many items, by the <NoteLink to="gumbel-max-trick">Gumbel-max trick</NoteLink>, which leads to the
  <NoteLink to="plackett-luce-model">Plackett–Luce model</NoteLink>.

<Derivation title="The difference of two Gumbels is logistic">
A standard Gumbel variable has cdf $F(x) = \exp(-e^{-x})$ and density $f(x) = e^{-x}\exp(-e^{-x})$. For independent
$\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j$,

$$
\pr(\epsilon_j - \epsilon_i < z) = \int f(x)\, F(x + z)\, dx = \int e^{-x} \exp\paren{-e^{-x}(1 + e^{-z})}\, dx .
$$

Substitute $y = e^{-x}$, so $dy = -e^{-x}dx$: the integral is $\int_0^\infty \exp\paren{-y(1 + e^{-z})}\,dy =
1/(1 + e^{-z}) = \sigma(z)$.
</Derivation>

The two curves have the same shape. With the scale matched, $\abs{\Phi(z) - \sigma(1.702 z)} < 0.0095$ for every $z$
(checked numerically), so a Thurstone strength in units of $\sqrt 2\sigma$ times 1.702 is close to a Bradley–Terry
strength. Rankings from the two models almost always agree. The logistic tails are heavier, so Bradley–Terry
assigns higher probability to upsets between very unequal players.

Only differences enter either model. Adding a constant to every $\theta_i$ leaves every probability unchanged, so the
strengths are **identified only up to a shift**. Fix it by setting $\sum_i \theta_i = 0$ or $\theta_1 = 0$.

## Maximum likelihood

Let $w_{ij}$ be the number of times $i$ beat $j$, $n_{ij} = w_{ij} + w_{ji}$ the number of games between them, and
$W_i = \sum_j w_{ij}$ the total wins of $i$. The Bradley–Terry log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\thetavec) = \sum_{i \ne j} w_{ij}\paren{\theta_i - \log\paren{e^{\theta_i} + e^{\theta_j}}}.
$$

Each game is one observation of a <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> with no intercept,
feature vector $\evec_i - \evec_j$ and weights $\thetavec$. The log-likelihood is concave. Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_i} = W_i - \sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}\, \sigma(\theta_i - \theta_j),
$$

so at the maximum every item's observed wins equal its expected wins.

### Zermelo's iteration as an MM algorithm

Setting the gradient to zero and solving for $\pi_i$ with the other strengths held at their current values gives a
fixed-point update. Zermelo proposed it in 1929 <Cite id="zermelo1929" />. Hunter showed that it is a
**minorise–maximise** (MM) algorithm, which guarantees that the likelihood never decreases <Cite id="hunter2004" />.

The difficult term is $-\log(\pi_i + \pi_j)$. For any $x, y > 0$, concavity of the logarithm gives
$\log x \le \log y + (x - y)/y$, that is,

$$
-\log x \ \ge\ 1 - \log y - \frac{x}{y},
$$

with equality at $x = y$. Apply it with $x = \pi_i + \pi_j$ and $y = \pi_i^{(k)} + \pi_j^{(k)}$, the values at
iteration $k$. The result is a surrogate that lies below $\ell$ everywhere and touches it at the current point:

$$
Q(\pivec \mid \pivec^{(k)}) = \sum_{i \ne j} w_{ij}\brack{\log \pi_i - \frac{\pi_i + \pi_j}{\pi_i^{(k)} + \pi_j^{(k)}}} + \text{const}.
$$

$Q$ separates across items. Collect the terms in $\pi_i$: $W_i \log \pi_i$ from the wins, and
$-\pi_i \sum_{j \ne i} n_{ij}/(\pi_i^{(k)} + \pi_j^{(k)})$ from every game $i$ played, won or lost. Setting the
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> to zero gives

$$
\pi_i^{(k+1)} = \frac{W_i}{\sum_{j \ne i} \dfrac{n_{ij}}{\pi_i^{(k)} + \pi_j^{(k)}}}.
$$

Maximising $Q$ cannot decrease $Q$, and $\ell \ge Q$ with equality at $\pivec^{(k)}$, so
$\ell(\pivec^{(k+1)}) \ge Q(\pivec^{(k+1)} \mid \pivec^{(k)}) \ge Q(\pivec^{(k)} \mid \pivec^{(k)}) = \ell(\pivec^{(k)})$.
The same argument underlies <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation maximisation</NoteLink>. After each
iteration, rescale $\pivec$ to fix the shift; the rescaling does not change $\ell$.

### When the maximum exists

If some item has won every game it played, its likelihood keeps increasing as $\theta_i \to \infty$, and no finite
maximum exists. The general condition is due to Zermelo and to Ford: a finite maximum exists, and is unique after
fixing the shift, if and only if for every split of the items into two non-empty groups, some item in each group has
beaten some item in the other <Cite id="hunter2004" />. Equivalently, the directed graph with an edge $i \to j$ whenever
$i$ beat $j$ is strongly connected. Sparse data often break the condition. A Gaussian prior on $\thetavec$, which adds
a ridge penalty, restores a unique finite estimate.

## Worked example

Four players A to D play an uneven round robin. The win matrix, with rows beating columns, is

| | A | B | C | D | wins | games |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | – | 2 | 3 | 1 | 6 | 8 |
| B | 1 | – | 2 | 2 | 5 | 9 |
| C | 0 | 1 | – | 2 | 3 | 9 |
| D | 1 | 1 | 1 | – | 3 | 8 |

Start from $\pi_i = 1$, so every denominator term is $n_{ij}/2$ and the first update is wins divided by half the games
played: $\pi_A = 6/4 = 1.5$, $\pi_B = 5/4.5 = 1.111$, $\pi_C = 3/4.5 = 0.667$, $\pi_D = 3/4 = 0.75$. Centred on the log
scale, $\thetavec^{(1)} = (0.451, 0.151, -0.360, -0.242)$. The iteration converges to

$$
\hat\thetavec = (0.863,\ 0.173,\ -0.558,\ -0.479),
$$

which a direct numerical maximisation with scipy reproduces to four decimals. C and D both won three games, but D
played one game fewer, so D ranks higher. The model predicts that A beats B with probability
$\sigma(0.863 - 0.173) = 0.666$. The graph of wins is strongly connected (for example A beat B, B beat C, C beat D and
D beat A), so the maximum exists.

## Ties

A comparison can end level. Two extensions of Bradley–Terry add one parameter for ties.

- **Rao–Kupper.** A tie occurs when the utilities differ by less than a threshold $\eta > 0$. With $\nu = e^\eta \ge 1$,
  $\pr(i \succ j) = \pi_i/(\pi_i + \nu\pi_j)$ and
  $\pr(i = j) = (\nu^2 - 1)\pi_i\pi_j/\paren{(\pi_i + \nu\pi_j)(\nu\pi_i + \pi_j)}$ <Cite id="rao1967" />. This is
  the logistic version of the draw margin in <NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink>.
- **Davidson.** $\pr(i \succ j) = \pi_i/D$ and $\pr(i = j) = \nu\sqrt{\pi_i\pi_j}/D$, with
  $D = \pi_i + \pi_j + \nu\sqrt{\pi_i\pi_j}$ and $\nu \ge 0$ <Cite id="davidson1970" />. The ratio of win to loss
  probability stays $\pi_i/\pi_j$, as in the model without ties, and ties are most likely between equal items.

Counting a tie as half a win for each side, as <NoteLink to="elo-rating">Elo</NoteLink> does, keeps the plain
Bradley–Terry likelihood with fractional outcomes and needs no extra parameter.

## Assumptions

- **One dimension.** Strength is a single number, so preferences are transitive in probability: if
  $\pr(i \succ j) > 1/2$ and $\pr(j \succ k) > 1/2$ then $\pr(i \succ k) > 1/2$. Rock–paper–scissors cycles cannot be
  represented.
- **Independent comparisons.** Each game is an independent draw given the strengths. Order effects, fatigue and
  home advantage need extra terms, such as an additive home bonus inside $\sigma(\cdot)$.
- **Fixed strengths.** The fit pools all games. Strengths that change over time need a dynamic model, such as
  <NoteLink to="glicko-and-glicko-2">Glicko</NoteLink> or <NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale is arbitrary.** Bradley–Terry strengths are in log-odds, Thurstone strengths in units of the noise. Compare
  strengths only within one fit, or convert with the factor 1.702.
- **Unconnected data.** Two groups that never meet have no common scale. Their relative strengths are not identified,
  whatever the fit reports.
- **Selection by matchmaking.** When opponents are chosen by current rating, the set of comparisons depends on past
  outcomes. The likelihood is still valid, because the choice of pairs depends only on observed data, but the
  <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> of rarely compared pairs are large.
