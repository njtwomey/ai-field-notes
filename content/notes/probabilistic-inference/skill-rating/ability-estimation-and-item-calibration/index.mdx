<Definition>
Given item characteristic curves $P_i(\theta)$ and a person's responses $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \set{0, 1}$, **ability
estimation** finds the person's ability $\theta$ from the likelihood

$$
L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P_i(\theta)^{x_i}\paren{1 - P_i(\theta)}^{1 - x_i},
$$

by maximising it (<Gloss name="mle" />), by maximising $L(\theta)\,p(\theta)$ for a prior $p$ (MAP), or by taking the posterior mean
(EAP). **Item calibration** estimates the item parameters from the responses of many persons, whose abilities are
unknown.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A test is a small set of contests against items of known strength. Ability estimation is then the problem of rating
one player from a few results against fixed opponents, as in a <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Bradley–Terry
fit</NoteLink> with every opponent's strength known. Calibration is the harder problem in which the opponents' strengths
are unknown too, and every person plays only a few of them.

## Ability by maximum likelihood

For the <Gloss name="2pl" /> model, $P_i(\theta) = \sigma\paren{a_i(\theta - b_i)}$ and $P_i' = a_i P_i(1 - P_i)$. The log-likelihood
$\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \brack{x_i\log P_i + (1 - x_i)\log(1 - P_i)}$ has <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> and negative second derivative

$$
\ell'(\theta) = \sum_i a_i\paren{x_i - P_i(\theta)}, \qquad -\ell''(\theta) = \sum_i a_i^2 P_i(\theta)\paren{1 - P_i(\theta)} = I(\theta).
$$

The second derivative does not depend on the responses, so it equals the test information $I(\theta)$ and is positive:
$\ell$ is concave. **Newton's method** is therefore also Fisher scoring:

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta + \frac{\sum_i a_i\paren{x_i - P_i(\theta)}}{\sum_i a_i^2 P_i(\theta)\paren{1 - P_i(\theta)}} .
$$

The estimate satisfies $\sum_i a_i x_i = \sum_i a_i P_i(\hat\theta)$: the discrimination-weighted score equals its
expected value. In the Rasch model all $a_i = 1$, so the raw score alone determines $\hat\theta$. The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> is
$1/\sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}$, from the asymptotic normality of maximum-likelihood estimates.

The maximum is finite only when the answers are mixed. All correct pushes $\hat\theta$ to $+\infty$ and all wrong to
$-\infty$. Short tests and adaptive tests often start with such patterns.

## Ability with a prior

With a prior $\theta \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ on the population's scale, the log-posterior adds $-\theta^2/2$:

- **MAP.** Newton's method on $\ell(\theta) - \theta^2/2$ uses the score $\ell'(\theta) - \theta$ and the curvature
  $I(\theta) + 1$. The estimate is always finite and is pulled towards the prior mean. The approximate posterior
  <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> is $1/\sqrt{I(\hat\theta) + 1}$.
- **EAP.** The posterior mean $\expect[\theta \mid x] = \int \theta\, L(\theta)\phi(\theta)\,d\theta \big/ \int
  L(\theta)\phi(\theta)\,d\theta$ is a one-dimensional integral, computed by quadrature on a grid of $\theta$ values.
  It needs no iteration, exists for every response pattern, and gives the posterior standard deviation from the same
  grid <Cite id="bock1982" />.

Both shrink towards the prior mean, most for short tests. The MLE is unbiased only asymptotically and has large variance
for few items.

## Worked example: a 2PL ability estimate

Three items have $(a_i, b_i) = (1.0, -1.0)$, $(1.5, 0.0)$ and $(2.0, 1.0)$. A person answers the first two correctly
and the third incorrectly: $x = (1, 1, 0)$.

1. **Start at $\theta = 0$.** $P = \paren{\sigma(1), \sigma(0), \sigma(-2)} = (0.7311, 0.5, 0.1192)$. The score is
   $1.0 \times 0.2689 + 1.5 \times 0.5 + 2.0 \times (-0.1192) = 0.7805$. The information is
   $1.0 \times 0.1966 + 2.25 \times 0.25 + 4.0 \times 0.1050 = 1.1791$. Newton step: $\theta = 0.7805/1.1791 = 0.662$.
2. **At $\theta = 0.662$.** $P = (0.8405, 0.7297, 0.3371)$, score $-0.1093$, information $1.4718$. Step:
   $\theta = 0.662 - 0.0743 = 0.5877$.
3. **At $\theta = 0.5877$.** Score $-0.0006$, information $1.4544$. The next step changes $\theta$ by $-0.0004$.

The MLE is $\hat\theta = 0.587$ with standard error $1/\sqrt{1.4543} = 0.829$. With a standard normal prior, the MAP
estimate is 0.344 (posterior sd about 0.651) and the EAP estimate is 0.312 (posterior sd 0.674). The prior pulls the
estimate towards 0, and it narrows the uncertainty, because three items carry little information. These numbers
agree with scipy and with the `girth` Python package, whose `ability_mle`, `ability_map` and `ability_eap` give
0.5873, 0.3437 and 0.3122.

## Item calibration

**Joint maximum likelihood** (JML) maximises the likelihood over all $\theta_p$ and all item parameters together,
alternating between the two sets. It is simple but inconsistent for the item parameters when the number of items per
person stays fixed: every new person adds a new $\theta_p$, so the number of parameters grows with the data. Persons
with all-correct or all-wrong patterns must also be dropped.

**Conditional maximum likelihood** (Rasch model only). The raw score $r_p = \sum_i x_{pi}$ is sufficient for $\theta_p$,
because the Rasch likelihood of person $p$ is

$$
\frac{\exp\paren{\theta_p r_p - \sum_i x_{pi} b_i}}{\prod_i \paren{1 + e^{\theta_p - b_i}}} .
$$

Conditioning on $r_p$ removes $\theta_p$, leaving a likelihood in the difficulties alone <Cite id="rasch1960" />.

**Marginal maximum likelihood** (MML) treats abilities as draws from a population, $\theta_p \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, and
maximises the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> of the item parameters,

$$
\prod_p \int \prod_i P_i(\theta)^{x_{pi}}\paren{1 - P_i(\theta)}^{1 - x_{pi}}\, \phi(\theta)\, d\theta .
$$

Bock and Aitkin maximise it with <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation maximisation</NoteLink>, replacing
the integral by a sum over quadrature points $\theta_q$ with weights $w_q$ <Cite id="bock1981" />.

- **E-step.** For each person, compute the posterior weight of each quadrature point,
  $\pi_{pq} \propto w_q \prod_i P_i(\theta_q)^{x_{pi}}\paren{1 - P_i(\theta_q)}^{1 - x_{pi}}$. Sum them into the
  expected number of persons at each point, $\bar n_q = \sum_p \pi_{pq}$, and the expected number of correct answers to
  item $i$ there, $\bar r_{iq} = \sum_p \pi_{pq} x_{pi}$.
- **M-step.** Each item separately: fit $P_i(\theta_q)$ to $\bar r_{iq}$ successes out of $\bar n_q$ trials, a small
  weighted <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on the quadrature points.

The standard normal population fixes the scale that is otherwise identified only up to shift (and, in the 2PL, a
stretch). Abilities are then estimated person by person with the item parameters held fixed, by MLE, MAP or EAP.

## Adaptive testing

A computerised adaptive test alternates two steps: estimate $\theta$ from the answers so far, usually by EAP so that the
estimate exists from the first answer, then give the unused item with the largest information $I_i(\hat\theta)$. The
test stops when the posterior standard deviation falls below a target or a maximum length is reached
<Cite id="bock1982" />. For a 2PL item bank, maximum information means choosing items with $b_i$ near the current
estimate and large $a_i$.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating calibrated parameters as exact.** Ability standard errors from $1/\sqrt{I}$ ignore the uncertainty in the
  item parameters. With a small calibration sample the reported precision is optimistic.
- **Item exposure.** Maximum-information selection reuses the same highly discriminating items for everyone near a
  given ability. Operational tests randomise among near-best items to keep items secret.
- **Prior mismatch.** MAP and EAP shrink towards the prior mean. For a person far from the calibration population, a
  short test reports an ability that is too close to the average.
