<Definition>
On a <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> $p(\xvec) \propto \prod_a f_a(\xvec_{\partial a})$ that is a tree, the **sum-product algorithm** sends
a message along each edge in each direction:

$$
\mu_{i \to a}(x_i) = \prod_{b \in \partial i \setminus a} \mu_{b \to i}(x_i), \qquad
\mu_{a \to i}(x_i) = \sum_{\xvec_{\partial a \setminus i}} f_a(\xvec_{\partial a}) \prod_{j \in \partial a \setminus i} \mu_{j \to a}(x_j).
$$

The marginal of each variable is the normalised product of its incoming messages,
$p(x_i) \propto \prod_{a \in \partial i} \mu_{a \to i}(x_i)$ <Cite id="kschischang2001" />. The algorithm is exact on
trees and was introduced by Pearl for polytree <NoteLink to="bayesian-network">Bayesian networks</NoteLink> <Cite id="pearl1988" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="variable-elimination">Variable elimination</NoteLink> computes one marginal by summing out the other
variables in a good order. Computing the marginal of every variable that way repeats almost all of the work for each
variable. On a tree the intermediate sums can be shared: the sum over a subtree does not depend on which variable is
being queried, only on the edge through which the subtree is attached. Belief propagation computes each such sum once,
as a message, and reuses it for every query.

## Mechanism

Here $\partial i$ is the set of factors that use variable $x_i$ and $\partial a$ the set of variables used by factor
$f_a$. A leaf variable sends the constant message $\mu_{i \to a} = 1$. A leaf factor sends
$\mu_{a \to i}(x_i) = f_a(x_i)$. Every other node sends a message to a neighbour once it has received messages from all
its other neighbours. A convenient schedule picks any root, sends messages from the leaves to the root, then from the
root back to the leaves. Every edge then carries one message in each direction.

After both sweeps:

- **Variable marginals.** $p(x_i) \propto \prod_{a \in \partial i} \mu_{a \to i}(x_i)$.
- **Factor marginals.** The joint of the variables of one factor is
  $p(\xvec_{\partial a}) \propto f_a(\xvec_{\partial a}) \prod_{j \in \partial a} \mu_{j \to a}(x_j)$.
- **Partition function.** $Z = \sum_{x_i} \prod_{a \in \partial i} \mu_{a \to i}(x_i)$, the same for every $i$.
- **Evidence.** Fixing observed variables in the factors before passing messages gives the conditional marginals given
  the evidence, and $Z$ becomes the probability of the evidence.

### Derivation on a tree

Fix a variable $x_i$. Removing $x_i$ from the tree splits the other nodes into one subtree per neighbouring factor
$a \in \partial i$. Every factor lies in exactly one subtree, so the joint splits as

$$
\prod_b f_b = \prod_{a \in \partial i} F_a(x_i, X_a),
$$

where $F_a$ is the product of the factors in the subtree hanging from $a$ and $X_a$ is the set of variables in that
subtree. The subtrees share no variables other than $x_i$, so the sum over all other variables distributes over the
product:

$$
p(x_i) \propto \sum_{\text{all but } x_i} \prod_{a \in \partial i} F_a(x_i, X_a)
= \prod_{a \in \partial i} \underbrace{\sum_{X_a} F_a(x_i, X_a)}_{\mu_{a \to i}(x_i)}.
$$

This defines the factor-to-variable message as the sum over everything behind the edge. It satisfies the recursion in
the definition. The subtree behind $a$ consists of $f_a$ and, for each other variable $x_j$ of $f_a$, the subtree
hanging from $x_j$ away from $a$. Write $G_j(x_j, X_j)$ for the product of the factors in the subtree hanging from
$x_j$. Then $F_a = f_a \prod_{j \in \partial a \setminus i} G_j$. The $G_j$ share no variables, so

$$
\mu_{a \to i}(x_i) = \sum_{\xvec_{\partial a \setminus i}} f_a(\xvec_{\partial a}) \prod_{j \in \partial a \setminus i}
\underbrace{\sum_{X_j} G_j(x_j, X_j)}_{\mu_{j \to a}(x_j)}.
$$

The subtree hanging from $x_j$ is in turn made of the subtrees behind the factors $b \in \partial j \setminus a$, so
$G_j = \prod_{b} F_b$ and $\mu_{j \to a}(x_j) = \prod_{b \in \partial j \setminus a} \mu_{b \to j}(x_j)$. Both message
equations are the distributive law applied at one node. The derivation needs the subtrees to be disjoint, which is
exactly what a tree guarantees.

## Pairwise Markov random fields

For a <NoteLink to="markov-random-field">Markov random field</NoteLink> with node potentials $\psi_i(x_i)$ and edge
potentials $\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)$, the factor-to-variable and variable-to-factor messages combine into one message per
directed edge of the undirected graph:

$$
m_{j \to i}(x_i) = \sum_{x_j} \psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)\, \psi_j(x_j) \prod_{k \in N(j) \setminus i} m_{k \to j}(x_j), \qquad
p(x_i) \propto \psi_i(x_i) \prod_{j \in N(i)} m_{j \to i}(x_i),
$$

where $N(j)$ is the set of neighbours of node $j$. On a chain the left-to-right messages are the forward vectors and
the right-to-left messages are the backward vectors of the <Gloss name="forward-backward">forward–backward algorithm</Gloss> of a
<NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink>. For Gaussian potentials the messages are Gaussian,
and the two sweeps are the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="kalman-smoother">Kalman smoother</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

A chain of three binary variables $x_1 - x_2 - x_3$, each in $\set{0, 1}$, has node potentials
$\psi_1 = (0.7, 0.3)$, $\psi_2 = (0.4, 0.6)$ and $\psi_3 = (0.2, 0.8)$, listing the values at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$, and
the same edge potential on both edges, $\psi(u, v) = 2$ if $u = v$ and $1$ otherwise.

**Inward messages to $x_2$.**

$$
m_{1 \to 2}(x_2) = \sum_{x_1} \psi(x_1, x_2)\,\psi_1(x_1) = (2 \times 0.7 + 0.3,\ 0.7 + 2 \times 0.3) = (1.7, 1.3),
$$

and by the same rule $m_{3 \to 2} = (2 \times 0.2 + 0.8,\ 0.2 + 2 \times 0.8) = (1.2, 1.8)$.

**Marginal of $x_2$.** $\psi_2 \odot m_{1 \to 2} \odot m_{3 \to 2} = (0.816, 1.404)$, where $\odot$ is the element-wise
product. The sum is $Z = 2.22$, so $p(x_2) = (0.368, 0.632)$.

**Outward messages.** $m_{2 \to 1}(x_1) = \sum_{x_2} \psi(x_1, x_2)\,\psi_2(x_2)\,m_{3 \to 2}(x_2) = (2.04, 2.64)$, so
$p(x_1) \propto (0.7 \times 2.04,\ 0.3 \times 2.64) = (1.428, 0.792)$, which normalises to $(0.643, 0.357)$. The same sum
is $Z = 2.22$ again. Likewise $m_{2 \to 3} = (2.14, 2.24)$ and $p(x_3) = (0.193, 0.807)$.

Four messages give all three marginals. Summing the joint over its eight configurations gives the same numbers.

## Max-product

Replacing every sum by a maximum gives the **max-product** algorithm. The maximum distributes over products of
non-negative factors as the sum does, $\max_x a\, g(x) = a \max_x g(x)$ for $a \ge 0$, so the derivation carries over
unchanged. The product of incoming messages at $x_i$ is then the **max-marginal**, $\max_{\xvec : x_i = v} p(\xvec)$ up to
the constant $Z$. Working with logarithms turns products into sums, and the algorithm is called **max-sum**.

To recover the most probable configuration, take the maximising value at the root, then pass back towards the leaves,
choosing at each variable the value that attained the maximum in the message given its already chosen neighbour. This
backtracking avoids inconsistent choices when the maximum is tied.

In the example, the max-product messages into $x_2$ are $(\max(1.4, 0.3), \max(0.7, 0.6)) = (1.4, 0.7)$ from $x_1$ and
$(0.8, 1.6)$ from $x_3$. The max-marginal of $x_2$ is $(0.4 \times 1.4 \times 0.8,\ 0.6 \times 0.7 \times 1.6) = (0.448,
0.672)$, so $x_2 = 1$. Backtracking, $x_1$ maximises $\psi_1(x_1)\psi(x_1, 1) = (0.7, 0.6)$, so $x_1 = 0$, and $x_3$
maximises $\psi(1, x_3)\psi_3(x_3) = (0.2, 1.6)$, so $x_3 = 1$. The most probable configuration is $(0, 1, 1)$, with
probability $0.672/2.22 = 0.303$. On a chain this is the <Gloss name="viterbi">Viterbi algorithm</Gloss>.

## Cost

A factor-to-variable message from a factor with $d$ variables of $k$ states costs $O(k^d)$. Each edge carries two
messages, so the total cost is linear in the number of edges and, for a pairwise model, $O(\abs{E}\, k^2)$. Computing
every marginal costs about twice as much as computing one by variable elimination.

## Trade-offs

- **Trees only, for exactness.** On a graph with cycles the derivation fails, because the subtrees are no longer
  disjoint. The <NoteLink to="junction-tree-algorithm">junction tree algorithm</NoteLink> clusters variables into a
  tree and runs the same messages on the clusters, at a cost exponential in the cluster size. Running the local
  updates on the cyclic graph anyway gives <NoteLink to="loopy-belief-propagation">loopy belief propagation</NoteLink>,
  which is approximate.
- **Numerical range.** Messages are products of many numbers and underflow on long chains. Normalising each message to
  sum to 1 changes nothing but a constant, which cancels in the marginals; the constants must be tracked separately if
  $Z$ is wanted.
- **Max-marginals are not marginals.** The configuration that maximises each marginal separately need not be the most
  probable joint configuration. Use max-product for the joint mode and sum-product for per-variable probabilities.
