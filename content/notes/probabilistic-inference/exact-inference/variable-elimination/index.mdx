<Definition>
Variable elimination computes a marginal $p(\xvec_Q)$ of a distribution given as a product of factors,
$p(\xvec) \propto \prod_a f_a(\xvec_{\partial a})$, by eliminating the other variables one at a time in a chosen
order. Eliminating $x_j$ multiplies together the factors that contain $x_j$, sums $x_j$ out of the product, and puts the
resulting factor back in the set <Cite id="zhang1994" /> <Cite id="dechter1999" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A marginal of a <NoteLink to="bayesian-network">Bayesian network</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="markov-random-field">Markov random field</NoteLink> is a sum of the joint over all the other variables.
With $n$ variables of $k$ states each, the joint has $k^n$ entries, so summing it directly is infeasible beyond a few
dozen binary variables. The joint is a product of small factors, and most factors do not contain most variables.
Multiplication distributes over addition, $\sum_x a\, g(x) = a \sum_x g(x)$, so a sum over $x_j$ need only touch the
factors that contain $x_j$.

## Mechanism

Start with the set $\Fcal$ of all factors, with any evidence fixed in them. For each variable $x_j$ in the elimination
order, excluding the query variables:

1. Remove from $\Fcal$ every factor that contains $x_j$, and multiply them into one factor $\phi_j$. Its scope is $x_j$
   together with every other variable in those factors.
2. Sum out $x_j$: $\tau_j = \sum_{x_j} \phi_j$. The new factor $\tau_j$ has the scope of $\phi_j$ minus $x_j$.
3. Add $\tau_j$ to $\Fcal$.

When only the query variables remain, multiply the remaining factors. The result is proportional to $p(\xvec_Q)$, or to
$p(\xvec_Q, \text{evidence})$ when evidence was fixed; normalising gives the marginal or the conditional
$p(\xvec_Q \mid \text{evidence})$. The unnormalised total, summed over $\xvec_Q$ as well, is the partition function
$Z$ or the probability of the evidence.

Every step is exact. The algorithm only reorders the sum by the distributive law.

## Worked example

Take the alarm network with $B \to A \leftarrow E$, $p(B = 1) = 0.001$, $p(E = 1) = 0.002$ and
$p(A = 1 \mid B, E) = 0.95, 0.94, 0.29, 0.001$ for $(B, E) = (1, 1), (1, 0), (0, 1), (0, 0)$. The query is
$p(A = 1) = \sum_{b, e} p(b)\, p(e)\, p(A = 1 \mid b, e)$.

**Eliminate $E$.** The factors that contain $E$ are $p(e)$ and $p(A = 1 \mid b, e)$. Their product summed over $e$ is a
factor of $b$ alone:

$$
\tau(b) = \sum_e p(e)\, p(A = 1 \mid b, e), \qquad
\tau(1) = 0.002 \times 0.95 + 0.998 \times 0.94 = 0.94002, \qquad
\tau(0) = 0.002 \times 0.29 + 0.998 \times 0.001 = 0.001578.
$$

**Eliminate $B$.** The factors that contain $B$ are $p(b)$ and $\tau(b)$:

$$
p(A = 1) = 0.001 \times 0.94002 + 0.999 \times 0.001578 = 0.002516.
$$

The direct sum has four terms, each a product of three numbers. Here the saving is small. It grows exponentially with
the number of variables.

## Cost and the induced width

The cost of a step is the size of the table $\phi_j$, $k^{\abs{\text{scope}(\phi_j)}}$. The scope depends on the order.

Track the scopes on an undirected graph with an edge between any two variables that share a factor. Eliminating $x_j$
creates a factor over all of its current neighbours, so in the graph the neighbours become a clique: new **fill edges**
join any of them not already adjacent, and $x_j$ is deleted. The graph with every fill edge added is the **induced
graph** of the order. The **induced width** $w$ of the order is the size of the largest set of neighbours met at
elimination, which is the size of the largest clique of the induced graph minus one. The largest table has $k^{w+1}$
entries, and there are $n$ eliminations, so the total cost is

$$
O\paren{n\, k^{w + 1}}.
$$

The smallest induced width over all orders is the **treewidth** of the graph. Exact inference is exponential in the
treewidth and linear in the number of variables.

**The order matters.** Take a star: a centre $c$ joined to $m$ leaves, each edge with a pairwise factor. Eliminating a
leaf first creates a factor over $c$ alone, at cost $k^2$. Eliminating all leaves costs $O(m k^2)$, and the induced
width is 1. Eliminating the centre first multiplies all $m$ factors into a table over $c$ and every leaf, of size
$k^{m+1}$, and joins every pair of leaves by a fill edge. The induced width is $m$.

**Some graphs are expensive under any order.** A tree has treewidth 1. A chain is the special case that gives the
forward algorithm of a hidden Markov model. A cycle has treewidth 2. An $L \times L$ grid has treewidth $L$, so exact
inference on a $100 \times 100$ image model needs tables with $k^{101}$ entries. Finding an order of minimum width is
NP-hard, and exact inference in Bayesian networks is NP-hard in general <Cite id="cooper1990" />. Greedy heuristics work
well in practice: **min-degree** eliminates the variable with fewest current neighbours, and **min-fill** eliminates the
one whose elimination adds the fewest fill edges <Cite id="koller2009" />.

<Derivation title="Why a new factor's scope is the current neighbourhood">
Before any elimination, two variables are adjacent exactly when they share a factor. Suppose this holds before
eliminating $x_j$. The factors multiplied into $\phi_j$ are those containing $x_j$, so the scope of $\phi_j$ minus $x_j$
is exactly the set of current neighbours of $x_j$. The new factor $\tau_j$ contains all of them, so they all share a
factor afterwards, which is what the fill edges record. Every other factor is unchanged. By induction the graph with
fill edges always records which variables share a factor, and each $\phi_j$ has scope $x_j$ plus its neighbours at the
moment of elimination.
</Derivation>

## Variants

- **Max-product elimination.** Replacing each sum by a maximum computes $\max_{\xvec} p(\xvec)$, the probability of the
  most probable configuration. Recording the maximising value of each eliminated variable, as a function of its
  neighbours, recovers the configuration by a backward pass. On a chain this is the Viterbi algorithm. The same order
  and the same cost apply, since the maximum distributes over products of non-negative factors just as the sum does.
- **Log space.** Working with log-factors turns products into sums and sums into log-sum-exp, which avoids underflow.

## Trade-offs

- **One query at a time.** Variable elimination answers one marginal per run. Computing the marginal of every variable
  would repeat most of the work $n$ times. <NoteLink to="belief-propagation">Belief propagation</NoteLink> on a tree
  and the <NoteLink to="junction-tree-algorithm">junction tree algorithm</NoteLink> on a general graph cache the
  intermediate factors as messages and obtain every marginal for about twice the cost of one.
- **Exponential in the width.** When the treewidth is large, as in grids and densely connected networks, no order
  helps. Approximate methods such as <NoteLink to="loopy-belief-propagation">loopy belief propagation</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="variational-inference">variational inference</NoteLink> are then needed.
- **Pruning.** Variables that are neither queried, observed nor ancestors of either can be removed from a Bayesian
  network before elimination, because their factors sum to 1.
