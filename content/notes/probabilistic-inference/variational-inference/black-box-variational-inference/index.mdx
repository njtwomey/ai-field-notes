<Definition>
Black-box variational inference fits a parametric approximation $q_{\phivec}(\zvec)$ to the posterior of a model
$p(\xvec, \zvec)$ by stochastic gradient ascent on the evidence lower bound

$$
\Lcal(\phivec) = \expect_{q_{\phivec}}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec) - \log q_{\phivec}(\zvec)},
$$

using unbiased Monte Carlo estimates of $\nabla_{\phivec}\Lcal$ computed from samples of $q_{\phivec}$. The model enters
only through evaluations of $\log p(\xvec, \zvec)$ (and, for the reparameterisation estimator, its gradient in
$\zvec$), so no model-specific derivation is needed <Cite id="ranganath2014" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Coordinate-ascent and <NoteLink to="stochastic-variational-inference">stochastic variational inference</NoteLink> need
conditionally conjugate models, and each new model needs its expectations derived by hand. Most models of interest,
such as a logistic regression with a Gaussian prior or a hierarchical model with non-conjugate likelihoods, have
ELBOs with no closed form. Black-box variational inference estimates the gradient of the ELBO by sampling instead of
computing the ELBO analytically. The same code then fits any model that can report its log joint, which is what makes
automatic inference in <NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">probabilistic programming</NoteLink> systems possible.

## Mechanism

The ELBO is an expectation under a distribution that depends on $\phivec$, so the gradient cannot be moved inside the
expectation directly. Two identities make it an expectation again.

### The score-function gradient

Differentiate under the integral with $\nabla q_{\phivec} = q_{\phivec}\nabla\log q_{\phivec}$, and note that the
derivative of $-\log q_{\phivec}$ inside the integrand contributes $-\expect_q\brack{\nabla\log q_{\phivec}} = 0$:

$$
\nabla_{\phivec}\Lcal = \expect_{q_{\phivec}}\brack{\nabla_{\phivec}\log q_{\phivec}(\zvec)\,\paren{\log p(\xvec, \zvec) - \log q_{\phivec}(\zvec)}}.
$$

Averaging the bracket over samples $\zvec^{(s)} \sim q_{\phivec}$ gives an unbiased estimate that needs only
evaluations of $\log p(\xvec, \zvec)$ and the score $\nabla_{\phivec}\log q_{\phivec}$ <Cite id="ranganath2014" />. It
works for discrete latent variables and non-differentiable models. See the
<NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function estimator</NoteLink> for the identity in general.

Its variance is often too large to use directly. Ranganath et al. reduce it in two ways <Cite id="ranganath2014" />:

- **Rao–Blackwellisation.** With a mean-field $q$, the gradient for the parameters of factor $j$ needs only the terms
  of $\log p$ that involve $\zvec_j$, its Markov blanket. The other terms have zero expected contribution and only add
  noise.
- **Control variates.** Subtracting a baseline $b$ from $\log p - \log q$ leaves the expectation unchanged, since the
  score has mean zero. A baseline near the ELBO removes most of the noise that comes from the overall level of
  $\log p - \log q$.

### The reparameterisation gradient

If $\zvec = \gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)$ with $\epsilonvec \sim p(\epsilonvec)$ free of $\phivec$, for example
$\zvec = \muvec + \Lmat\epsilonvec$ with $\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$, the expectation can be taken over
$\epsilonvec$ and the gradient passes through the sample:

$$
\nabla_{\phivec}\Lcal = \expect_{p(\epsilonvec)}\brack{\nabla_{\phivec}\paren{\log p\paren{\xvec, \gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)} - \log q_{\phivec}\paren{\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)}}}.
$$

This estimator uses the gradient $\nabla_{\zvec}\log p(\xvec, \zvec)$, which tells it in which direction the log joint
increases. It typically has much lower variance than the score-function estimator, often low enough to use a single
sample per step <Cite id="kingma2014" /> <Cite id="mohamed2020" />. It needs continuous latent variables and a
differentiable model. See the <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink>.

### Automatic differentiation variational inference

ADVI combines the reparameterisation gradient with automatic transformations <Cite id="kucukelbir2017" />. It maps every
constrained latent variable to the real line (a positive scale through a logarithm, a probability through a logit),
adding the log-Jacobian of the map to the log joint, then fits a Gaussian, mean-field or full-rank, in the
unconstrained space. The user writes only the model. Stan and PyMC implement ADVI.

### The algorithm

1. Initialise $\phivec$.
2. Repeat: draw $S$ samples from $q_{\phivec}$ (or from $p(\epsilonvec)$); compute the gradient estimate; update $\phivec$
   with a step size that decreases or is adapted, for example by Adam.
3. Monitor a smoothed Monte Carlo estimate of the ELBO, which is noisy.

When the data are large, the log joint is also estimated on a minibatch, scaling the likelihood term by $N/\abs{\Bcal}$.
The gradient then has two sources of noise, the samples of $\zvec$ and the choice of minibatch, hence the name doubly
stochastic.

## Worked example: the variance of the two estimators

Take the model $z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, $x \mid z \sim \Gauss(z, 1)$ with one observation $x = 1.5$, and
$q(z) = \Gauss(m, s^2)$ at $m = -1$, $s = 1.2$. The exact gradient of the ELBO with respect to $m$ is
$\partial\Lcal/\partial m = (x - m) - m = 3.5$.

- **Reparameterisation.** With $z = m + s\epsilon$, the entropy of $q$ does not depend on $m$, and
  $\partial_z\log p(x, z) = (x - z) - z$. The single-sample estimate is $x - 2(m + s\epsilon) = 3.5 - 2.4\,\epsilon$.
  Its mean is 3.5 and its standard deviation is $2s = 2.4$.
- **Score function.** The single-sample estimate is $\frac{z - m}{s^2}\paren{\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)}$. Its mean is
  3.5 and its standard deviation, computed by simulation, is 8.01. With the baseline $b = \Lcal = -5.30$ it is 5.55.

The score-function estimator needs $(8.01/2.4)^2 = 11$ times as many samples for the same precision, or 5.3 times with
the baseline. The gap grows with the dimension of $\zvec$, because the score-function estimator correlates one scalar,
$\log p - \log q$, with every coordinate of the score.

## Cost

Each step costs $S$ evaluations of the log joint, and of its gradient for the reparameterisation estimator. Compared
with <NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo">Hamiltonian Monte Carlo</NoteLink>, which needs many gradient evaluations
per draw, BBVI with one sample per step is cheap per iteration and converges in hundreds to thousands of steps. The
result is an approximation limited by the family of $q$.

## Trade-offs

- **Score function or reparameterisation.** Use the reparameterisation gradient for continuous, differentiable models.
  Use the score function for discrete latent variables or black-box simulators, with baselines and Rao–Blackwellisation.
- **Noisy objective.** The ELBO is estimated, not computed, so convergence is judged on a smoothed trace. Step sizes
  that are too large make the variational parameters oscillate; a decreasing schedule or an adaptive optimiser is needed.
- **The family limits accuracy.** A mean-field Gaussian cannot represent correlations or skew. Full-rank Gaussians
  cost $O(d^2)$ parameters; normalising flows add flexibility at more cost.
- **Amortisation.** Fitting a separate $q$ for every data point in a latent-variable model is slow. A shared encoder
  network, as in the <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational autoencoder</NoteLink>, amortises the fit; see
  <NoteLink to="amortised-inference">amortised inference</NoteLink>.
