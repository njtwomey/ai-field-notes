<Definition>
Stochastic <Gloss name="vi">variational inference</Gloss> (SVI) maximises the <Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower
bound</Gloss> (ELBO) of a model with global latent variables $\betavec$ and local latent variables $\zvec_n$, one per
data point, by stochastic optimisation. Each step samples a data point or a minibatch, fits its local variational
parameters, forms the value $\hat\lambdavec$ that the global variational parameters would take if the whole dataset were
copies of the sample, and moves the current parameters towards it:

$$
\lambdavec \leftarrow (1 - \rho_t)\,\lambdavec + \rho_t\,\hat\lambdavec.
$$

This update is a step of size $\rho_t$ along the natural <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the ELBO
<Cite id="hoffman2013" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Coordinate ascent for <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational inference</NoteLink>
alternates between updating every local factor $q(\zvec_n)$ and updating the global factor $q(\betavec)$. The global
update sums over all $N$ data points, so each iteration is a full pass through the data. Early in the fit the global
parameters are poor, and the local updates computed from them are wasted work. For a topic model of millions of
documents, a single pass can take hours before the global topics improve at all <Cite id="hoffman2013" />. SVI updates
the global parameters after every minibatch.

## Model class

SVI applies to models of the form

$$
p(\betavec, \zvec_{1:N}, \xvec_{1:N}) = p(\betavec) \prod_{n=1}^{N} p(\xvec_n, \zvec_n \mid \betavec),
$$

in which the complete conditional of the global variables is in an <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential
family</NoteLink>,

$$
p(\betavec \mid \xvec, \zvec) \propto h(\betavec) \exp\paren{\etavec_g(\xvec, \zvec)^\top \tvec(\betavec)}, \qquad
\etavec_g(\xvec, \zvec) = \alphavec + \sum_{n=1}^N \tvec_n(\xvec_n, \zvec_n),
$$

where $\alphavec$ is the natural parameter of a <NoteLink to="conjugate-priors">conjugate prior</NoteLink> and $\tvec_n$
is the contribution of data point $n$. The local complete conditionals are also in exponential families. Mixture models,
<Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov models</Gloss>, topic models such as <Gloss name="latent-dirichlet-allocation">latent
Dirichlet allocation</Gloss> <Cite id="blei2003" />, and many hierarchical regressions have this form.

The variational family is mean field, with $q(\betavec \mid \lambdavec)$ in the same family as the complete conditional
and natural parameter $\lambdavec$. The coordinate-ascent update of the global factor is

$$
\lambdavec^* = \expect_{q(\zvec)}\brack{\etavec_g(\xvec, \zvec)} = \alphavec + \sum_{n=1}^N \expect_{q(\zvec_n)}\brack{\tvec_n(\xvec_n, \zvec_n)}.
$$

## Mechanism

### The natural gradient

A Euclidean gradient in $\lambdavec$ measures distance between parameter vectors, not between the distributions they
define. The **natural gradient** premultiplies the gradient by the inverse <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher
information</Gloss> $\Gmat(\lambdavec)$ of $q(\betavec \mid \lambdavec)$, which measures change in terms of the KL
divergence between nearby distributions <Cite id="amari1998" />. For an exponential family with log-normaliser $A$, the
Fisher information is $\Gmat(\lambdavec) = \nabla^2 A(\lambdavec)$.

Hold the local factors at their optimum and view the ELBO as a function of $\lambdavec$. Up to terms that do not depend
on $\lambdavec$, the global part of the ELBO is
$\expect_q\brack{\log p(\betavec \mid \xvec, \zvec)} - \expect_q\brack{\log q(\betavec \mid \lambdavec)}$. Using
$\expect_q[\tvec(\betavec)] = \nabla A(\lambdavec)$,

$$
\Lcal(\lambdavec) = \nabla A(\lambdavec)^\top \paren{\expect_q[\etavec_g] - \lambdavec} + A(\lambdavec) + \text{const}.
$$

Differentiating, the terms $\pm\nabla A(\lambdavec)$ cancel:

$$
\nabla_{\lambdavec}\Lcal = \nabla^2 A(\lambdavec)\paren{\expect_q[\etavec_g] - \lambdavec}, \qquad
\Gmat(\lambdavec)^{-1}\nabla_{\lambdavec}\Lcal = \expect_q[\etavec_g] - \lambdavec.
$$

The natural gradient is the difference between the coordinate-ascent target and the current value. A natural-gradient
step of size 1 is exactly the coordinate-ascent update. A step of size $\rho$ moves a fraction $\rho$ of the way
there <Cite id="hoffman2013" />.

### Noisy natural gradients

The target $\expect_q[\etavec_g]$ contains a sum over all data points. Pick one index $n$ uniformly at random, fit its
local factor $q(\zvec_n)$ given the current $\lambdavec$, and form

$$
\hat\lambdavec = \alphavec + N\, \expect_{q(\zvec_n)}\brack{\tvec_n(\xvec_n, \zvec_n)}.
$$

This is the global update for a dataset of $N$ copies of point $n$. Its expectation over the random index is the full
sum, so $\hat\lambdavec - \lambdavec$ is an unbiased estimate of the natural gradient. A minibatch $\Bcal$ replaces the
factor $N$ by $N/\abs{\Bcal}$ and sums over the batch. The update is

$$
\lambdavec_{t+1} = \lambdavec_t + \rho_t\paren{\hat\lambdavec_t - \lambdavec_t} = (1 - \rho_t)\,\lambdavec_t + \rho_t\,\hat\lambdavec_t.
$$

With steps satisfying the <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">Robbins–Monro conditions</NoteLink>
$\sum_t \rho_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \rho_t^2 < \infty$, the iterates converge to a local optimum of the ELBO
<Cite id="robbins1951" />. A common schedule is $\rho_t = (t + \tau)^{-\kappa}$ with $\kappa \in (0.5, 1]$ and delay
$\tau \ge 0$.

### The algorithm

1. Initialise $\lambdavec$.
2. Repeat: sample a minibatch; for each point in it, optimise the local parameters given $\lambdavec$; form
   $\hat\lambdavec$; set $\lambdavec \leftarrow (1 - \rho_t)\lambdavec + \rho_t\hat\lambdavec$.

## Worked example

Observations $x_n \sim \Gauss(\mu, 1)$, $n = 1, \dots, N$, with prior $\mu \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. There are no local
variables. A Gaussian $q(\mu) = \Gauss(m, s^2)$ has natural parameters $\lambdavec = (m/s^2, -1/(2s^2))$, so
$m = \lambda_1/(-2\lambda_2)$ and $s^2 = 1/(-2\lambda_2)$. The prior has $\alphavec = (0, -1/2)$, and each observation
contributes $\tvec_n = (x_n, -1/2)$. The exact posterior has
$\lambdavec^* = \paren{\sum_n x_n,\ -(1 + N)/2}$.

Take $N = 1000$, start at the prior $\lambdavec = (0, -0.5)$, sample $x_n = 0.8$, and use $\rho = 0.1$.

- **Intermediate parameter.** $\hat\lambdavec = (0 + 1000 \times 0.8,\ -0.5 - 1000 \times 0.5) = (800, -500.5)$, the
  posterior for 1000 copies of 0.8.
- **Update.** $\lambdavec = 0.9 \times (0, -0.5) + 0.1 \times (800, -500.5) = (80, -50.5)$.
- **Moments.** $s^2 = 1/101$ and $m = 80/101 = 0.792$.

The second component already has the right form and reaches $-500.5$ as the steps accumulate. The first is an
exponentially weighted average of $N x_n$ over the sampled points. With $\rho_t = 1/t$ it is the plain average of
the $N x_n$, which converges to $\sum_n x_n$: the exact posterior.

## Cost

Each step costs one minibatch's local updates plus an $O(\dim \lambdavec)$ global update, independent of $N$. SVI often
reaches a good ELBO before one full pass of coordinate ascent has finished. It also processes streaming data, since it
never needs the whole dataset at once.

## Trade-offs

- **Conjugacy is required.** The closed-form natural gradient needs exponential-family complete conditionals. Models
  without them use <NoteLink to="black-box-variational-inference">black-box variational inference</NoteLink>, which
  estimates ordinary gradients by Monte Carlo.
- **Step size trades noise against progress.** Too large a $\rho_t$ gives noisy parameters; too small gives slow
  progress. Larger minibatches reduce the noise of $\hat\lambdavec$ and allow larger steps.
- **Local optima.** As with coordinate ascent, the result depends on initialisation.
- **Local parameters are refitted each time.** A point's local factor is recomputed whenever it is sampled.
  <NoteLink to="amortised-inference">Amortised inference</NoteLink> replaces these per-point optimisations with a
  learned function of the data.
