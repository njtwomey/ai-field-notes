<Definition>
In amortised inference, the approximate posterior for an observation $\xvec$ is the output of a learned function,
$q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)$, whose parameters $\phivec$ are shared across all observations. A neural network, the
**inference network** or **encoder**, maps $\xvec$ to the parameters of a distribution over $\zvec$, for example a
mean and a diagonal covariance. The network is trained once, on many observations, so that the cost of inference is
spread, or amortised, over every later query <Cite id="kingma2014" /> <Cite id="rezende2014" />.
</Definition>

## The problem it solves

In a latent-variable model with one latent $\zvec_n$ per observation $\xvec_n$, standard
<NoteLink to="variational-inference">variational inference</NoteLink> gives each observation its own variational
parameters $\lambdavec_n$ and optimises each by an inner loop. With $N$ observations there are $N$ sets of parameters.
Every new observation needs its own optimisation from scratch. When $N$ is in the millions, or when inference must run
in real time, this is too slow.

Amortisation replaces the $N$ separate optimisations by regression. The optimal $\lambdavec_n^*$ is some function of
$\xvec_n$; the inference network learns an approximation of that function from the data.

## Mechanism

**Training by the ELBO.** Maximise the sum of the evidence lower bounds of all observations over the shared parameters:

$$
\Lcal(\phivec) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \expect_{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec_n)}\brack{\log p(\xvec_n, \zvec) - \log q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec_n)}.
$$

Minibatches of observations and the <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink> give
unbiased stochastic gradients, as in <NoteLink to="black-box-variational-inference">black-box variational
inference</NoteLink>. When the generative model has parameters $\thetavec$ too, they are trained jointly on the same
objective. That is the <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational autoencoder</NoteLink>: the inference
network is the encoder and $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)$ the decoder.

**Training on simulations.** When the model can be simulated but its likelihood cannot be evaluated, draw pairs
$(\zvec, \xvec)$ from the joint, $\zvec \sim p(\zvec)$ then $\xvec \sim p(\xvec \mid \zvec)$, and maximise
$\expect_{p(\zvec, \xvec)}\brack{\log q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}$. Split the joint as $p(\xvec)\,p(\zvec \mid \xvec)$:

$$
-\expect_{p(\zvec, \xvec)}\brack{\log q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}
= \expect_{p(\xvec)}\brack{\KL\paren{p(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\, q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}} + \expect_{p(\xvec)}\brack{\entropy\brack{p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}}.
$$

The entropy term does not depend on $\phivec$. Maximising the log-likelihood of the simulated latents therefore
minimises the forward KL divergence to the true posterior, averaged over the data the model generates, without ever
evaluating that posterior. This is **neural posterior estimation**, a form of simulation-based inference
<Cite id="papamakarios2016" /> <Cite id="cranmer2020" />. The forward KL makes the fitted posteriors mass-covering rather
than mode-seeking.

## The amortisation gap

For one observation, let $q^*_{\xvec}$ be the best member of the variational family, found by optimising for $\xvec$
alone. The gap between the log evidence and the ELBO of the amortised posterior splits into two parts
<Cite id="cremer2018" />:

$$
\log p(\xvec) - \operatorname{ELBO}\paren{q_{\phivec}(\cdot \mid \xvec)}
= \underbrace{\log p(\xvec) - \operatorname{ELBO}(q^*_{\xvec})}_{\text{approximation gap}}
+ \underbrace{\operatorname{ELBO}(q^*_{\xvec}) - \operatorname{ELBO}\paren{q_{\phivec}(\cdot \mid \xvec)}}_{\text{amortisation gap}}.
$$

The approximation gap is the cost of the family, for example a diagonal Gaussian. The amortisation gap is the cost of
predicting the parameters from $\xvec$ with a network of limited capacity, trained on finite data, rather than
optimising them. Cremer et al. found that in trained variational autoencoders the amortisation gap is often the larger
of the two <Cite id="cremer2018" />. **Semi-amortised** inference closes part of it: use the encoder's output as the
starting point for a few steps of per-observation optimisation, and train the encoder through those steps
<Cite id="kim2018" />.

## Worked example: a linear Gaussian model

Let $z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $x \mid z \sim \Gauss(z, \sigma^2)$. By Bayes' theorem the posterior is

$$
p(z \mid x) = \Gauss\paren{\frac{x}{1 + \sigma^2},\ \frac{\sigma^2}{1 + \sigma^2}}.
$$

- **Per-observation inference.** Fitting $q_n = \Gauss(m_n, s_n^2)$ to each of $N$ observations means $2N$ variational
  parameters and $N$ optimisations. For $N = 10^6$ that is two million numbers.
- **Amortised inference.** An encoder $q_{\phivec}(z \mid x) = \Gauss(a x + b, c^2)$ has three parameters. The
  posterior mean is linear in $x$ and the posterior variance does not depend on $x$, so the family contains the exact
  posterior for every $x$ at $a = 1/(1 + \sigma^2)$, $b = 0$ and $c^2 = \sigma^2/(1 + \sigma^2)$. For $\sigma^2 = 1$,
  $a = 0.5$ and $c^2 = 0.5$. Maximising the summed ELBO recovers these values, and both gaps are zero. A new
  observation $x = 2$ gets its posterior $\Gauss(1, 0.5)$ from one multiplication.
- **A limited encoder.** If the encoder is restricted to a constant mean, $a = 0$, it cannot follow the posterior mean
  as $x$ varies. The ELBO is then below its per-observation optimum for every $x \ne 0$, by the amortisation gap.

In nonlinear models the map from $\xvec$ to the optimal variational parameters is nonlinear, and the encoder's
capacity determines how small the gap can be.

## Assumptions

- **A distribution of queries.** The encoder is accurate for observations like those it was trained on. For
  observations far from the training data, its output can be arbitrarily poor, and nothing in the output signals it.
- **Local latent variables.** Amortisation suits models with one latent per observation. Global parameters shared by
  all observations are fitted directly.

## Pitfalls

- **The encoder can hide a poor posterior.** A good reconstruction or a high ELBO does not show that each
  $q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)$ is close to its posterior. Compare with per-observation optimisation on a sample of
  data to measure the amortisation gap.
- **Joint training can bias the model.** When the generative model and the encoder are trained together, the model can
  adapt to the encoder's limitations, for example by making its posteriors easy to approximate. In a variational
  autoencoder this contributes to posterior collapse.
- **Simulation-based training depends on the prior.** Neural posterior estimation trained on simulations from the prior
  is accurate where the prior puts mass. Observed data in the tails of the prior predictive get poorly trained
  posteriors.
