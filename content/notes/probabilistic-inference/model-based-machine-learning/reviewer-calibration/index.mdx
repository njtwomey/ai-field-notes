<Definition>
Each paper $p$ has an unknown quality $q_p$ and each reviewer $r$ an unknown bias $b_r$. The score reviewer $r$ gives
paper $p$ is

$$
y_{rp} = \mu + q_p + b_r + \epsilon_{rp}, \qquad q_p \sim \Gauss(0, \alpha_q), \quad b_r \sim \Gauss(0, \alpha_b), \quad
\epsilon_{rp} \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2),
$$

observed only for the pairs $(r, p)$ that were assigned. **Reviewer calibration** computes the posterior over qualities
and ranks papers by $\expect[q_p \mid \yvec]$ instead of by their mean raw score.
</Definition>

## Why raw means fail

The mean of the $n_p$ scores of paper $p$, with reviewer set $R_p$, is

$$
\bar y_p = \mu + q_p + \frac{1}{n_p}\sum_{r \in R_p} b_r + \bar\epsilon_p.
$$

The middle term is the average bias of whoever happened to review the paper. With three reviews per paper it does not
average out. It is also not independent of quality. Papers are assigned by topic, so a sub-area with harsh reviewers
depresses all its papers together. Two papers of equal quality can then differ in raw mean by more than the gap between
a strong paper and a weak one.

Every reviewer scores several papers, and every paper is scored by several reviewers,
so each bias is seen repeatedly. The data can separate $q_p$ from $b_r$ as long as the bipartite graph of reviewers and
papers is connected.

## The posterior

Stack the unknowns into $\thetavec = (q_1, \dots, q_P, b_1, \dots, b_R)$. Each review is one row of a design matrix
$\Amat$ with a 1 in the column of its paper and a 1 in the column of its reviewer, so $\yvec - \mu = \Amat\thetavec +
\epsilonvec$. The prior and likelihood are Gaussian, so the posterior is Gaussian, and its log density is, up to a
constant,

$$
-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\yvec - \mu - \Amat\thetavec}^2 - \frac{1}{2\alpha_q}\sum_p q_p^2 - \frac{1}{2\alpha_b}\sum_r b_r^2.
$$

Setting its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> to zero gives the posterior mean:

$$
\paren{\frac{\Amat\transpose\Amat}{\sigma^2} + \Lambdamat}\thetavec = \frac{\Amat\transpose(\yvec - \mu)}{\sigma^2},
\qquad \Lambdamat = \diag\paren{\alpha_q^{-1}, \dots, \alpha_q^{-1}, \alpha_b^{-1}, \dots, \alpha_b^{-1}}.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>, with a separate ridge for qualities and biases. It is the model John Platt introduced at NIPS
in 2002, fitted as regularised least squares, and related to a two-way <Gloss name="anova">analysis of variance</Gloss> with <NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">random effects</NoteLink>
<Cite id="lawrence2014calibration" />. Lawrence and Cortes rewrote it as a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> whose covariance between two
reviews is $\alpha_q$ if they share a paper, plus $\alpha_b$ if they share a reviewer, plus $\sigma^2$ if they are the
same review, and fitted the variances to the data <Cite id="lawrence2014calibration" />.

### Why the prior is needed

Without the prior, the likelihood cannot tell $q_p + c$ and $b_r - c$ from $q_p$ and $b_r$ for any constant $c$: every
score is unchanged. $\Amat\transpose\Amat$ is singular, with the null direction $(\ones_P, -\ones_R)$. The prior
removes it by preferring the split in which qualities and biases are both small. The same holds within each connected
component of the review graph separately, which is why an isolated cluster of papers and reviewers cannot be compared
with the rest by any model.

### How much a paper moves

For intuition, suppose the biases were known. Then each paper's posterior mean is the average of its bias-corrected
scores, shrunk towards the mean:

$$
\expect[q_p \mid \yvec, \bvec] = \frac{n_p / \sigma^2}{n_p / \sigma^2 + 1 / \alpha_q}\; \frac{1}{n_p}\sum_{r \in R_p}(y_{rp} - \mu - b_r).
$$

Calibration subtracts each reviewer's bias from their scores, and the prior pulls papers with few reviews towards the
middle. The joint solution does both at once, with each bias estimated from all of that reviewer's papers.

## The ordinal model of SIGKDD'09

Real reviews are not continuous scores but recommendations on a scale such as strong reject to strong accept, and
reviewers also report their expertise. The model used for SIGKDD 2009 builds both in <Cite id="flach2010kdd" />
<Cite id="infernetreviewers" />:

1. **Quality.** Each submission has a latent quality $q_p$ with a Gaussian prior centred mid-scale.
2. **Expertise sets the noise.** The latent score of a review is $\Gauss(q_p, 1/\lambda_e)$, where the precision
   $\lambda_e$ depends on the reviewer's self-declared expertise level $e$ and has a Gamma prior. Experts' scores are
   trusted more, by an amount learned from the data.
3. **Personal thresholds.** Reviewer $r$ turns the latent score into a recommendation through their own thresholds
   $\theta_{r,1} < \dots < \theta_{r,L-1}$. Each is drawn around a fixed nominal threshold, $\theta_{r,\ell} \sim
   \Gauss(\theta_\ell, 1/a_r)$, where the reviewer's accuracy $a_r$ has a Gamma prior.
4. **Observation.** The recommendation is level $\ell$ exactly when the latent score falls between thresholds $\ell - 1$
   and $\ell$.

<OrdinalReviewGraph />

A harsh reviewer is one whose thresholds sit high; an erratic reviewer is one whose thresholds scatter far from the
nominal ones. The threshold factors are the ordinal version of the probit factor of the
<NoteLink to="email-reply-classifier">email reply classifier</NoteLink>, and <Gloss name="ep">expectation propagation</Gloss> handles them the
same way. In the Infer.NET example the learned precision rises with declared expertise, from informed outsider to
knowledgeable to expert, as the model allows but does not force <Cite id="infernetreviewers" />.

## Dependence on the review assignment

Calibration can only use the review graph it is given. Assignment decides which biases are
confounded with which papers. An assignment that mixes reviewers across sub-areas makes calibration more reliable, and
an assignment that walls off a sub-area with its own reviewers makes it impossible. The SIGKDD'09 organisers also
automated the earlier stages, bidding and assignment of papers to reviewers <Cite id="flach2010kdd" />.

## Assumptions

- **Bias is a constant shift.** A reviewer is equally harsh on every paper. Reviewers who are harsh only on some topics,
  or who compress the scale, need a scale parameter or per-reviewer thresholds as in the ordinal model.
- **Noise is shared.** One $\sigma^2$ for all reviews in the linear model. The ordinal model relaxes this through
  expertise.
- **Quality is one number.** Disagreement about what makes a paper good lands in $\epsilon_{rp}$. In the NIPS 2014
  experiment about half of the variation in quality scores was subjective in this sense <Cite id="cortes2021nips" />;
  calibration cannot remove it.

## Pitfalls

- **Calibrating unbiased reviewers.** With no real biases, estimating six extra parameters from 36 reviews adds a little
  noise. In the figure, setting all biases to zero makes the calibrated ranking slightly worse than the raw one.
- **Few reviews per reviewer.** A reviewer with one review has a bias estimate equal to the prior; the calibration then
  does nothing for their paper. The benefit of calibration comes only from reviewers with several papers.
- **Using calibrated scores as the decision.** Calibrated scores are one input to a programme committee, not a verdict.
  The model has no access to the content of the reviews.
