<Definition>
Consider linear classifiers $f_\wvec(\xvec) = \sgn(\wvec\transpose\xvec)$ with a posterior $\pr(\wvec \mid \Dcal)$ after
seeing labelled data $\Dcal$. The Bayes-optimal classifier predicts by the posterior vote
$\sgn\paren{\expect_{\wvec \mid \Dcal}[f_\wvec(\xvec)]}$, which is in general not linear. The **Bayes point** is the
single weight vector $\wvec_{\text{bp}}$ whose classifier agrees best with that vote on future inputs. Herbrich,
Graepel and Campbell approximate it by the centre of mass of the version space <Cite id="herbrich2001" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A support vector machine picks one classifier among all those that separate the data: the one with the largest margin.
A Bayesian treatment keeps all of them, weighted by the posterior, and predicts by their vote. The vote generalises
better but costs an integral over classifiers for every prediction. The Bayes point machine keeps the Bayesian
prediction and the cost of a single linear classifier.

## Mechanism

### Version space

For separable data and no label noise, the likelihood is 1 for every $\wvec$ that classifies all points correctly and 0
otherwise. Only the direction of $\wvec$ matters, so the classifiers live on the unit sphere. The consistent ones form
the **version space**, the intersection of the half-spaces $\{\wvec : y_n\,\wvec\transpose\xvec_n > 0\}$ with the
sphere. Under a uniform prior on directions, the posterior is uniform on the version space.

- The **maximum-margin** classifier maximises $\min_n y_n\,\wvec\transpose\xvec_n$ over unit vectors. When every input
  has the same length, as with a Gaussian kernel, it is the centre of the largest ball that fits inside the version space
  <Cite id="herbrich2001" />. It depends only on the constraints that touch that ball, the support vectors.
- The **Bayes point** is approximated by the centre of mass of the version space. It depends on the whole shape of the
  region, so it moves when the region is elongated or lopsided.

### Why the posterior mean

Suppose the posterior is Gaussian, $\wvec \mid \Dcal \sim \Gauss(\mvec, \Smat)$. For a new input $\xvec$ the
activation $a = \wvec\transpose\xvec$ is Gaussian with mean $\mvec\transpose\xvec$ and variance
$\xvec\transpose\Smat\xvec$. The posterior vote is

$$
\expect_{\wvec \mid \Dcal}\brack{\sgn(\wvec\transpose\xvec)} = 2\,\pr(a > 0) - 1
= 2\,\Phi\paren{\frac{\mvec\transpose\xvec}{\sqrt{\xvec\transpose\Smat\xvec}}} - 1,
$$

whose sign is the sign of $\mvec\transpose\xvec$, because $\Phi(u) > \frac12$ exactly when $u > 0$. For every input,
the vote of the whole posterior equals the prediction of the single classifier $\mvec$. A Gaussian posterior therefore
has its mean as its Bayes point, exactly. For a true posterior that is not Gaussian, such as the uniform density on a
polyhedral version space, the identity holds approximately, and the centre of mass is the natural estimate
<Cite id="herbrich2001" />.

### Finding it

Herbrich, Graepel and Campbell estimate the centre of mass by **kernel billiards**: a ball bounces around inside the
version space, reflecting off the constraint walls, and its time-averaged position approximates the centre of mass.
Everything is written in terms of inner products, so it works with kernels. A second sampler averages many perceptron
solutions, each trained on a different random ordering of the data <Cite id="herbrich2001" />.

Minka recasts the problem as Bayesian inference in a probit model <Cite id="minka2001" />:

$$
\wvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat), \qquad y_n = \sgn(\wvec\transpose\xvec_n + \epsilon_n), \quad \epsilon_n \sim \Gauss(0, \beta^2).
$$

<BpmGraph />

As $\beta \to 0$ the likelihood becomes the version-space indicator. Expectation propagation approximates the posterior
by a Gaussian, one site per data point, each site a Gaussian in the projection $\wvec\transpose\xvec_n$. Its mean is
the Bayes point estimate. In Minka's experiments EP trained Bayes point machines faster than billiards and was at least as
accurate <Cite id="minka2001thesis" />. The site updates are worked through in the
<NoteLink to="expectation-propagation-probit-regression">EP for probit regression</NoteLink> note. The label noise
$\beta$ also makes the model usable on data that no linear boundary separates.

## The Infer.NET classifier

Infer.NET's general-purpose classifier is a Bayes point machine, and its documentation builds it from explicit
assumptions in the model-based style <Cite id="infernetbpmmodel" />:

1. Feature values are always observed.
2. The order of the instances does not matter.
3. The prediction is a linear discriminant of the features.
4. Gaussian noise is added to the score, which makes it a probit model.
5. The weights have factorised heavy-tailed priors: Gaussians whose precision has a Gamma prior with a Gamma-distributed
   rate. This makes the classifier invariant to rescaling a feature and robust to features with large values.
6. For several classes, class labels have no order. Each class $c$ has its own weight vector and score, and the class with
   the largest noisy score wins.

The same structure, with the weights of each user drawn around shared population weights, is the community Bayes
point machine used for personalised activity recognition in
<NoteLink to="active-transfer-learning">active transfer learning</NoteLink> <Cite id="diethe2016active" />.

## Cost

EP costs $O(N d^2)$ per pass over $N$ points in $d$ dimensions with a full covariance, or $O(N^3)$ per pass in the kernel
form, as for <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification">Gaussian process classification</NoteLink>. Prediction costs one inner
product, like an SVM, and returns a probability $\Phi\paren{\mvec\transpose\xvec / \sqrt{\beta^2 + \xvec\transpose\Smat\xvec}}$.

## Trade-offs

- **Against the SVM.** The Bayes point uses every point, not only the support vectors, so the solution is not sparse.
  With zero training error allowed, Bayes point machines consistently beat support vector machines in Herbrich, Graepel
  and Campbell's experiments; with soft margins the gain was smaller <Cite id="herbrich2001" />.
- **Probabilities for free.** The Gaussian posterior gives calibrated uncertainty for each prediction, which an SVM
  lacks.
- **Approximation.** EP's Gaussian is a projection. In weight space it matches the mean and covariance of the true
  posterior only approximately, and its mean is an estimate of the centre of mass.
- **Online use.** One pass of EP with no revisiting is <NoteLink to="adpredictor">AdPredictor</NoteLink>'s update, which
  processes a stream once and forgets it.
