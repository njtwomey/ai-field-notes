<Definition>
A **gate** encloses part of a <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> and is labelled with a **key** value. It is attached to a discrete
**selector** variable $c$. When $c$ equals the key, the factors inside the gate are on; otherwise they are replaced by
the constant 1. A set of gates with the same selector and different keys, one per value of $c$, represents
$\prod_k f_k(\xvec)^{\indicator[c = k]}$: exactly one of the enclosed sub-models explains the variables it touches
<Cite id="minka2008gates" />.
</Definition>

## What a gate shows that a single factor hides

A mixture $\pr(x \mid c) = \prod_k f_k(x)^{\indicator[c = k]}$ can be drawn as one big factor joining $c$, $x$ and all
the parameters of all components. Given $c = k$, $x$ depends on component $k$'s parameters only,
and not on the others: a *context-specific* <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> that no ordinary factor graph shows. Message passing also has
a nested structure, the component messages computed inside and mixed outside, which the single factor hides. The gate
draws both. It is drawn as a dashed box with its key in a corner and an edge from the selector
<Cite id="minka2008gates" />.

## Messages through a gate

Let the gate with key $k$ contain the factor $f_k(x)$, and let $x$ send the message $\mu_{x}(x)$ into the gated factors.

**To the selector.** The gate multiplies the factor graph by $f_k(x)$ when $c = k$ and by 1 otherwise. Summing out
$x$ against its incoming message gives the message to $c$:

$$
\mu_{\text{gate} \to c}(c = k) = \int f_k(x)\, \mu_x(x)\, dx = Z_k.
$$

$Z_k$ is the evidence for the contents of gate $k$, given everything outside it. The selector's posterior is its prior
times these evidences, so posterior model probabilities come out of ordinary message passing.

**To a variable outside.** The factor seen by $x$, after summing out $c$ against its incoming message
$\mu_c(k) = \pr(c = k)$, is the mixture

$$
\sum_k \mu_c(k)\, f_k(x).
$$

In <Gloss name="bp">belief propagation</Gloss> this mixture is the message. In <Gloss name="ep">expectation propagation</Gloss> the belief it produces is projected back
to the family of $x$, which amounts to weighting each component's contribution by the posterior probability of its key.
In variational message passing the message is $\exp\paren{\sum_k \mu_c(k) \log f_k(x)}$, a geometric mixture
<Cite id="minka2008gates" />.

## Worked example: a clinical trial

Five patients take a placebo and five take a new treatment. One control and four treated patients recover
<Cite id="infernetclinical" />. Does the treatment have an effect?

- The selector $e$ says whether it does, with $\pr(e = \text{true}) = 0.5$.
- In the gate $e = \text{true}$, the control and treated groups have separate recovery rates $\rho_c$ and $\rho_t$, each
  with a uniform $\Beta(1, 1)$ prior.
- In the gate $e = \text{false}$, one rate $\rho \sim \Beta(1, 1)$ serves everyone.

<TrialGraph />

The message from each gate to $e$ is the evidence of its sub-model. For $n$ Bernoulli outcomes with $k$ successes under
a $\Beta(1, 1)$ rate,

$$
\int_0^1 \rho^k (1 - \rho)^{n - k}\, d\rho = B(1 + k,\; 1 + n - k),
$$

where $B$ is the <NoteLink to="gamma-function">Beta function</NoteLink>. The two gates send

$$
Z_{\text{true}} = B(2, 5)\, B(5, 2) = \frac{1}{30} \cdot \frac{1}{30} = \frac{1}{900}, \qquad
Z_{\text{false}} = B(6, 6) = \frac{5!\,5!}{11!} = \frac{1}{2772}.
$$

With equal prior probabilities, $\pr(e = \text{true} \mid \text{data}) = Z_{\text{true}} / (Z_{\text{true}} +
Z_{\text{false}}) = 2772 / 3672 = 0.755$. Infer.NET prints the same number, 0.7549, from the same gated model
<Cite id="infernetclinical" />. The ratio $Z_{\text{true}} / Z_{\text{false}} = 3.08$ is the Bayes factor, and the
model comparison is exactly that of <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factors</NoteLink>, done by
message passing inside one model.

## Where gates appear

- **Mixture models.** The component indicator of each data point is the selector; each component's likelihood sits in
  a gate. A <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture</NoteLink> is the simplest case.
- **Crowd labels.** In <NoteLink to="community-bayesian-classifier-combination">Bayesian classifier
  combination</NoteLink> the true class selects which row of a worker's <NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink> generates their label.
- **Knowing or guessing.** In <NoteLink to="difficulty-versus-ability">difficulty versus ability</NoteLink> a binary
  "knows the answer" variable selects between "response equals the true answer" and "response is uniform".
- **Latent classes.** In the <NoteLink to="allergy-sensitisation-model">allergy sensitisation model</NoteLink> each
  child's class selects the parameters of their sensitisation chain <Cite id="winn2023asthma" />.
- **Interventions.** Winn uses gates to express the difference between observing a variable and setting it, so that
  causal questions become inference in one gated model <Cite id="winn2012causality" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Evidence is prior-sensitive.** The message $Z_k$ integrates over each sub-model's prior. A vague prior on a
  parameter inside one gate lowers that gate's evidence and biases selection towards the simpler sub-model.
- **Approximate evidence.** With expectation propagation inside a gate, $Z_k$ is the expectation propagation
  approximation of the evidence, and model probabilities inherit its error.
- **Symmetric keys.** When the gated sub-models are exchangeable, as in a mixture with identical priors, the selector's
  posterior is symmetric at the start and message passing needs a random initialisation to break the tie.
