<Definition>
Dr Black has been murdered. Exactly one of two suspects did it: Major Grey or Miss Auburn. The unknown murderer is a
<NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $m \in \{\text{Grey}, \text{Auburn}\}$ with a prior $\pr(m)$. Each clue $c$ found at the scene is a
random variable with a <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> $\pr(c \mid m)$. The task is to compute the posterior
$\pr(m \mid \text{clues})$ <Cite id="winn2023murder" />.
</Definition>

## The model, one assumption at a time

The book builds the model by writing down one assumption per quantity, then turning each into a probability.

1. **Prior.** Before any evidence, Grey is the murderer with probability 0.3 and Auburn with probability 0.7:
   $\pr(m = \text{Grey}) = 0.3$.
2. **Weapon.** The weapon $w$ is either a revolver or a dagger. Grey, an old soldier, would choose the revolver with
   probability 0.9. Auburn would choose it with probability 0.2.
3. **Hair.** The clue $h$ is whether one of Grey's grey hairs is found at the scene. If Grey is the murderer he leaves
   one with probability 0.5. If Auburn is the murderer she plants one with probability 0.05.
4. **<NoteLink to="independence">Conditional independence</NoteLink>.** Given the murderer, the choice of weapon tells nothing about the hair:
   $\pr(w, h \mid m) = \pr(w \mid m)\,\pr(h \mid m)$.

The joint distribution is the product of one factor per assumption:

$$
\pr(m, w, h) = \pr(m)\,\pr(w \mid m)\,\pr(h \mid m).
$$

Its <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> has one variable node for each of $m$, $w$ and $h$ and one factor node for each term. There is no edge
between $w$ and $h$, and no factor joins them. The missing edge is assumption 4.

## Inference as message passing

Observing a clue fixes its variable. The factor $\pr(w \mid m)$ then depends on $m$ alone, and it sends $m$ a message,
the column of its table at the observed value. For a revolver the message is

$$
\mu_{w \to m}(m) = \pr(w = \text{revolver} \mid m) = \begin{cases} 0.9 & m = \text{Grey}, \\ 0.2 & m = \text{Auburn}. \end{cases}
$$

The posterior of $m$ is the normalised product of every message arriving at $m$:

$$
\pr(m \mid w, h) = \frac{\pr(m)\,\mu_{w \to m}(m)\,\mu_{h \to m}(m)}{\sum_{m'} \pr(m')\,\mu_{w \to m}(m')\,\mu_{h \to m}(m')}.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> written on the graph. It follows from the factorisation.
Condition the joint on the observed values and sum over nothing, since every other variable is observed:
$\pr(m \mid w, h) \propto \pr(m, w, h) = \pr(m)\,\pr(w \mid m)\,\pr(h \mid m)$, and each observed factor is its message.
An unobserved clue sends the message $\sum_{w} \pr(w \mid m) = 1$, which changes nothing. On a tree such as this one,
the rule "multiply the incoming messages" is the sum-product algorithm of
<NoteLink to="belief-propagation">belief propagation</NoteLink> <Cite id="kschischang2001" />.

### The numbers

With the revolver alone, the unnormalised posterior is $0.3 \times 0.9 = 0.27$ for Grey and $0.7 \times 0.2 = 0.14$ for
Auburn. Normalising gives

$$
\pr(m = \text{Grey} \mid w = \text{revolver}) = \frac{0.27}{0.27 + 0.14} = 0.659.
$$

Adding the hair multiplies by $0.5$ and $0.05$: $0.27 \times 0.5 = 0.135$ and $0.14 \times 0.05 = 0.007$, so

$$
\pr(m = \text{Grey} \mid w = \text{revolver}, h = \text{found}) = \frac{0.135}{0.142} = 0.951.
$$

A dagger instead of the revolver gives $0.3 \times 0.1 = 0.03$ against $0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56$, and Grey's probability
falls to $0.051$. Messages multiply, so the order in which the clues arrive does not change the final answer. Each
intermediate posterior is the prior for the next clue.

## What the example shows

- **A model is a set of assumptions.** Every number in the answer traces back to one sentence in the list above. A
  disagreement about the verdict is a disagreement about an assumption, and it can be located.
- **The factor graph is the model.** It shows which quantities depend on which. The absent edge between weapon and hair
  is as much a modelling decision as any probability.
- **Inference is mechanical.** Once the model is written down, the posterior follows by multiplying messages. No
  algorithm was designed for this problem. This separation of model from inference is the idea behind
  <NoteLink to="model-based-machine-learning">model-based machine learning</NoteLink>.
- **Weak clues compound.** Neither clue alone is conclusive. The revolver moves Grey from 0.3 to 0.66. The hair has a
  likelihood ratio of $0.5 / 0.05 = 10$ and takes him to 0.95.

## Assumptions

- **Exactly one of two suspects.** The prior puts zero probability on anyone else. A third suspect would need a third
  state of $m$ and a row in every table.
- **Clues independent given the murderer.** If Grey carried the revolver because he knew it would incriminate someone
  else, the weapon and his behaviour at the scene would be linked, and a factor joining $w$ and $h$ would be needed.
- **Known probabilities.** The tables are fixed numbers. Chapter 2 of the same book, on
  <NoteLink to="assessing-skills">assessing skills</NoteLink>, starts to learn such numbers from data.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading a likelihood as a posterior.** $\pr(w = \text{revolver} \mid m = \text{Grey}) = 0.9$ is not the probability
  that Grey did it given a revolver. The two differ by the prior and the normaliser.
- **Double counting.** Two reports of the same hair are one clue. Entering them as two independent factors squares the
  likelihood ratio and overstates the evidence.
