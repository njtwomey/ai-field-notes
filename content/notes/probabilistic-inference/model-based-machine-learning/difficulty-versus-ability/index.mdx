<Definition>
Participants $p$ answer multiple-choice questions $q$ with $R$ options. Participant $p$ has ability $a_p$, question $q$
has difficulty $d_q$ and discrimination (a precision) $\tau_q$, and an unknown correct answer $y_q$. The participant
knows the answer, $c_{pq} = 1$, with probability $\Phi\paren{\sqrt{\tau_q}\,(a_p - d_q)}$. A participant who knows
responds $r_{pq} = y_q$; one who does not responds uniformly at random. Given the responses, inference returns
posteriors over abilities, difficulties, discriminations and the correct answers <Cite id="bachrach2012dare" />.
</Definition>

## What it adds to item response theory

<NoteLink to="item-response-theory">Item response theory</NoteLink> models the probability of a correct response from
ability and difficulty, and needs to know which responses are correct. The DARE model (difficulty, ability, response
estimation) treats the answer key as one more latent variable. The same model then serves two settings
<Cite id="bachrach2012dare" />:

- **Testing.** The answers are known. Inference estimates abilities and question parameters, as in IRT.
- **Crowdsourcing.** The answers are unknown, as when a crowd labels data. Inference estimates the answers from the
  responses, trusting able participants more. A few questions with known answers, a gold set, sharpen the abilities
  and so the answers.

## The model

**Knowing.** The probit comes from a noisy comparison. Let the participant's advantage be $t_{pq} = a_p - d_q$, and let
them know the answer when $t_{pq} + \epsilon > 0$ with $\epsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 1/\tau_q)$:

$$
\pr(c_{pq} = 1 \mid a_p, d_q, \tau_q) = \pr\paren{\epsilon > -t_{pq}} = \Phi\paren{\sqrt{\tau_q}\, t_{pq}}.
$$

A large $\tau_q$ makes the question sharply discriminating: people above its difficulty know it and people below do not.
A small $\tau_q$ makes knowing nearly a <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flip</NoteLink> whatever the ability, the mark of a badly worded question.

**Responding.** The response is a mixture selected by $c_{pq}$, drawn as two <NoteLink to="gates-in-factor-graphs">gates</NoteLink>
<Cite id="minka2008gates" />. Marginalising $c_{pq}$ with $\pi_{pq} = \pr(c_{pq} = 1)$,

$$
\pr(r_{pq} = r \mid y_q) = \pi_{pq}\,\indicator[r = y_q] + \frac{1 - \pi_{pq}}{R}.
$$

Priors are Gaussian on abilities and difficulties, Gamma on discriminations and uniform on the correct answers.

## Grading without an answer key

The message from one response to the correct answer $y_q$ follows from the mixture above. As a function of $y_q$ it
takes one value when $y_q$ equals the response and another otherwise, so it acts on $y_q$ through the ratio

$$
\frac{\pr(r_{pq} \mid y_q = r_{pq})}{\pr(r_{pq} \mid y_q \ne r_{pq})} = \frac{\pi_{pq} + (1 - \pi_{pq})/R}{(1 - \pi_{pq})/R} = 1 + \frac{R\,\pi_{pq}}{1 - \pi_{pq}}.
$$

Multiplying the messages from all participants, the log-posterior of each candidate answer $y$ is

$$
\log \pr(y_q = y \mid \text{responses}) = \sum_{p:\, r_{pq} = y} \log\paren{1 + \frac{R\,\pi_{pq}}{1 - \pi_{pq}}} + \text{const}.
$$

Each response is a vote for the option it chose, weighted by how likely the responder was to know. If every participant
has the same $\pi_{pq}$, every vote has the same weight and the posterior mode is the majority answer. Unequal
abilities turn majority vote into ability-weighted voting, and the abilities themselves are estimated from agreement
with the inferred answers. The loop between the two is what message passing iterates. On synthetic data from the model,
the Infer.NET example recovers 99% of the correct answers against 97% for majority vote <Cite id="infernetdifficulty" />.

## Inference

The graph is loopy, and the messages through the probit and the gates are approximate, so the implementation runs
<Gloss name="ep">expectation propagation</Gloss> to convergence <Cite id="minka2001" />. Abilities, difficulties and discriminations are
strongly correlated in the posterior, and a <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">factorised approximation</NoteLink> converges slowly or fails. The Infer.NET example
updates participants and questions sequentially, refreshing all variables after each one, which makes EP converge in a
few iterations <Cite id="infernetdifficulty" />.

## Adaptive testing

The posterior over a participant's ability says which question would be most informative to ask next. The paper picks
the question with the largest expected reduction in the <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> of the ability posterior. For a Gaussian posterior the
entropy is $\frac12 \log(2\pi e\sigma^2)$, so this is the question whose answer is expected to shrink the posterior
variance most, averaged over the model's prediction of the response <Cite id="bachrach2012dare" />. On a standard
intelligence test, adaptive questioning reached a given accuracy of the ability estimate with fewer questions than a
fixed test <Cite id="bachrach2012dare" />.

## Assumptions

- **One ability.** A single number per person. Tests of several skills need the multi-skill structure of
  <NoteLink to="assessing-skills">assessing skills</NoteLink> or a vector of abilities.
- **Uniform guessing.** A participant who does not know picks uniformly. Attractive wrong options, which draw more
  guesses, break this; a per-question confusion distribution would fix it at the cost of parameters, as in
  <NoteLink to="community-bayesian-classifier-combination">Bayesian classifier combination</NoteLink>.
- **Independent responses** given the latent variables. Copying between participants is not modelled.

## Pitfalls

- **A crowd that is mostly wrong.** If most participants share a misconception, the model infers the misconception as
  the answer and rates those who got it right as weak. A gold set of known answers anchors the scale.
- **Difficult questions.** For questions few people know, the inferred answer is unreliable. In the paper, the model
  inferred the correct answer for 46 of the 60 questions of the test; the authors attribute the misses to very difficult
  items that few participants answered correctly <Cite id="bachrach2012dare" />.
