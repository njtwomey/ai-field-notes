<Definition>
User $u$ is described by a feature vector $\xvec \in \reals^n$ and item $j$ by $\yvec \in \reals^m$, including one-hot
identity features. **Matchbox** maps them to $K$-dimensional trait vectors $\svec = \Umat\xvec$ and $\tvec = \Vmat\yvec$,
with a factorised Gaussian prior on every entry of $\Umat$ and $\Vmat$. The latent rating is
$r \sim \Gauss(\svec\transpose\tvec + b, \beta^2)$, with a bias $b$ that is itself a weighted sum of features. The
observation is $r$ itself, its sign, or a star rating through thresholds specific to the user <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A web recommender has to predict, for each user, which items they will value, from sparse ratings. It must say
something sensible about a new user or a new item, which have no ratings, from metadata such as age or genre. It must
accept different kinds of feedback, learn from a stream of ratings without retraining, and follow tastes that drift.
Plain <NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">matrix factorisation</NoteLink> handles none of the
first three.

## Mechanism

### The bilinear model

Row $k$ of $\Umat$ holds the contribution of each user feature to trait $k$, so a user's trait $s_k = \sum_i u_{ki}
x_i$ adds up contributions from all their features. The same holds for items. The predicted affinity is the inner
product in trait space <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />:

$$
\expect[r \mid \svec, \tvec, b] = \svec\transpose\tvec + b = \sum_{k=1}^{K} s_k t_k + b.
$$

- **Collaborative filtering as a special case.** If the only features are one-hot identities, $\xvec = \evec_u$ and
  $\yvec = \evec_j$, then $\svec$ and $\tvec$ are columns of $\Umat$ and $\Vmat$, and the model is Bayesian matrix
  factorisation.
- **Cold start.** With metadata features, a new user's traits come from their age, location and so on, whose weights
  were learned from other users. As the user rates items, their identity feature's weights move away from the prior and
  the recommendations become personal. This is a <NoteLink to="hybrid-recommenders">hybrid recommender</NoteLink> in
  one model, and the same structure, features to vectors to an inner product, is the
  <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink> with linear towers.

### Feedback models

The rating layer is interchangeable <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />:

- **Absolute rating.** Observe $r$ directly.
- **Binary.** Observe $c = \indicator[r > 0]$, a like or a click. This is a probit layer, as in the
  <NoteLink to="email-reply-classifier">email reply classifier</NoteLink>.
- **Ordinal.** Observe a star rating $\ell \in \{1, \dots, L\}$. User $u$ has thresholds $b_{u1} < \dots < b_{u,L-1}$,
  each with a Gaussian prior, and gives $\ell$ stars when the latent rating falls between thresholds $\ell - 1$ and
  $\ell$. Each user can use the scale differently: a generous user's thresholds sit low.

The book makes the thresholds shared by all users, on the assumption that two people with the same affinity for a film
give it the same number of stars, and fixes the middle threshold at zero so that thresholds and biases cannot trade off
<Cite id="winn2023recommender" />.

### Dynamics

Tastes, popularity and personal rating scales drift. Each latent variable follows a Gaussian random walk between time
steps, $w^{(t+1)} \sim \Gauss(w^{(t)}, \gamma^2)$. In online training this adds $\gamma^2$ to the variance of each
belief once per step.

## Inference

### Messages

Every factor except two is linear-Gaussian: the sums that form traits, the sum that forms $\tilde r$ and the noise.
Their messages are exact Gaussian sum-product messages <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />. The threshold factors of the
feedback layer are handled by expectation propagation, as for a probit likelihood. The remaining factor is the product
$z_k = s_k t_k$.

### The product factor

Given a message about $z_k$, the exact message to $s_k$ is not Gaussian. If $z_k$ is likely positive, then $s_k$ and
$t_k$ are either both positive or both negative, and the message on $s_k$ has two modes, one for each sign of $t_k$.
The moment-matching projection of EP covers both modes. The resulting Gaussian message is wider than it should be, and
with repeated updates the variances keep growing <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />.

Matchbox instead uses the variational message for this factor, the one that minimises the reversed divergence
$\KL(q \,\|\, p)$ locally, as in <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">mean-field variational
inference</NoteLink> <Cite id="winn2005vmp" /> <Cite id="minka2005" />. Let the message arriving at the product from
$z_k$ be $\Gauss(z_k; m_z, v_z)$, and let $q(t_k)$ have mean $\langle t\rangle$ and second moment $\langle t^2\rangle$.
The variational message to $s_k$ is the exponentiated expected log factor:

$$
\log \mu_{\times \to s}(s) = \expect_{q(t)}\LB -\frac{(m_z - s\,t)^2}{2 v_z} \RB + \text{const}
= -\frac{\langle t^2\rangle}{2 v_z}\, s^2 + \frac{m_z \langle t\rangle}{v_z}\, s + \text{const},
$$

a Gaussian in $s$ with

$$
\mu_{\times \to s}(s) = \Gauss\paren{s;\; \frac{m_z\,\langle t\rangle}{\langle t^2\rangle},\; \frac{v_z}{\langle t^2\rangle}}.
$$

The message to $t_k$ swaps the roles of $s$ and $t$. The message to $z_k$ is Gaussian with mean $\langle s\rangle
\langle t\rangle$ and variance $\langle s^2\rangle\langle t^2\rangle - \langle s\rangle^2 \langle t\rangle^2$. The
precision of the message to $s$ grows with $\langle t^2\rangle = \var[t] + \langle t\rangle^2$: an *uncertain* $t$
produces a *confident* message about $s$. Stern et al. point out that this is an undesirable property of the
approximation <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />. It is also what makes the solution commit to one mode. Minimising
$\KL(q \,\|\, p)$ is mode-seeking, so the variational solution picks one of the equivalent sign configurations, which is
what breaks the symmetry of trait space.

### Schedule and online training

For one rating, the schedule runs the feature sums up to the traits, iterates the messages through the product, sum,
noise and feedback factors until the predicted rating stops changing, and sends the result back down to $\Umat$,
$\Vmat$ and the bias weights. The updated marginals become the prior for the next rating and the messages are
discarded. This single pass is <NoteLink to="assumed-density-filtering">assumed density filtering</NoteLink>: memory
grows with the number of users, items and features, not with the number of ratings. Several full EP passes over stored
messages improve accuracy when the cost is affordable <Cite id="stern2009matchbox" />.

## Symmetries

The likelihood depends on $\svec\transpose\tvec$ only. Rotating trait space, $\svec \to \Rmat\svec$ and
$\tvec \to \Rmat\tvec$ with $\Rmat$ orthogonal, leaves every prediction unchanged, and so does flipping the sign of a
trait for all users and items together. The exact posterior therefore has many equivalent modes, and its marginals are
centred at zero. A useful approximation must pick one mode. The book fixes the traits of the first two films to
$(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, which pins the rotation <Cite id="winn2023recommender" />; the variational product message
breaks the remaining symmetry during inference.

## Cost

One update touches the active features of one user and one item, and costs $O(K(n_{\text{active}} + m_{\text{active}}))$
per iteration of the local schedule. Memory holds a mean and a variance per weight. Predictions for a user are inner
products of their trait vector with every item's, the same retrieval problem as for any
<NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink>.

## Trade-offs

- **Uncertainty.** Each prediction comes with a variance. New items can be ranked by an optimistic score, or sampled,
  to explore.
- **Approximation.** The factorised posterior ignores correlations between the traits of a user and those of the items
  they rated. The book finds the posteriors of most films broad after training, because most films have few ratings
  <Cite id="winn2023recommender" />.
- **Linear towers.** Features enter linearly. Interactions between features, or text and images, need either crafted
  features or a neural tower, which gives up closed-form messages.
- **Implicit feedback.** Unrated items are not negative examples. The binary model needs sampled negatives or a model of
  exposure, as discussed in <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">biases in recommender feedback</NoteLink>.
