<Definition>
A Bayesian network over <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $x_1, \dots, x_n$ is a <Gloss name="dag">directed acyclic graph</Gloss> $G$ with one node per variable,
together with one <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> $p(x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$ per node, where $\mathrm{pa}(i)$ is the
set of parents of node $i$ in $G$. The joint distribution is the product of these conditionals:

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p\paren{x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}}.
$$

The graph encodes <NoteLink to="independence">conditional independences</NoteLink> that hold for every choice of the conditionals <Cite id="pearl1988" />.
</Definition>

## Factorisation

The chain rule of probability writes any joint distribution, in any order of the variables, as

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}).
$$

Order the variables so that every parent precedes its children; a directed acyclic graph always admits such a
topological order. A Bayesian network asserts that each factor depends only on the parents:
$p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}) = p(x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$. Each missing edge is therefore an independence
assumption. Conversely, the product of any conditionals $p(x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$ is a normalised joint
distribution: summing out the last variable in the topological order removes its factor, because a conditional
distribution sums to 1, and the argument repeats down the order.

The factorisation is the reason Bayesian networks are compact. For $n$ binary variables the full joint table has
$2^n - 1$ free parameters. A network in which each node has at most $k$ parents needs at most $n\,2^k$, one Bernoulli
parameter per node and per configuration of its parents.

The factorisation is also a recipe for sampling, called **ancestral sampling**: draw each variable in topological order
from its conditional, given the already drawn values of its parents.

## The local Markov property

The factorisation is equivalent to the **local Markov property**: each variable is conditionally independent of its
non-descendants given its parents <Cite id="koller2009" />. A
related set is the **Markov blanket** of node $i$: its parents, its children and its children's other parents. Given its
Markov blanket, $x_i$ is independent of every other variable.

<Derivation title="The Markov blanket from the factorisation">
The conditional of $x_i$ given all other variables is proportional to the joint, viewed as a function of $x_i$:

$$
p(x_i \mid \xvec_{-i}) \propto p\paren{x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}} \prod_{j \,:\, i \in \mathrm{pa}(j)} p\paren{x_j \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(j)}}.
$$

Factors that do not contain $x_i$ cancel on normalisation. The remaining factors mention $x_i$, its parents, its
children $x_j$ and the other parents of those children. Nothing else appears, so the conditional depends only on the
Markov blanket.
</Derivation>

## Three canonical structures

Every question about independence in a Bayesian network reduces to three structures on three nodes $a$, $b$ and $c$.
Each is analysed directly from the factorisation.

**Chain**, $a \to c \to b$. The joint is $p(a)\,p(c \mid a)\,p(b \mid c)$. Conditioning on $c$:

$$
p(a, b \mid c) = \frac{p(a)\,p(c \mid a)\,p(b \mid c)}{p(c)} = p(a \mid c)\,p(b \mid c),
$$

using $p(a)\,p(c \mid a) = p(a \mid c)\,p(c)$ by <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>. So $a \perp b \mid c$. Without conditioning,
$p(a, b) = p(a) \sum_c p(c \mid a)\,p(b \mid c)$, and the sum depends on $a$ in general, so $a$ and $b$ are dependent.
Observing the middle of a chain blocks the flow of information.

**Fork**, $a \leftarrow c \to b$. The joint is $p(c)\,p(a \mid c)\,p(b \mid c)$. Dividing by $p(c)$ gives
$p(a, b \mid c) = p(a \mid c)\,p(b \mid c)$, so $a \perp b \mid c$. Without conditioning,
$p(a, b) = \sum_c p(c)\,p(a \mid c)\,p(b \mid c)$ is a mixture and does not factorise in general. A common cause makes
its effects dependent until the cause is observed.

**Collider**, $a \to c \leftarrow b$, also called a v-structure. The joint is $p(a)\,p(b)\,p(c \mid a, b)$. Without
conditioning, summing out $c$ removes its factor, and $p(a, b) = p(a)\,p(b)$: the two causes are independent.
Conditioning on $c$ gives

$$
p(a, b \mid c) = \frac{p(a)\,p(b)\,p(c \mid a, b)}{p(c)},
$$

which does not factorise in $a$ and $b$ when $p(c \mid a, b)$ couples them. Observing a common effect makes its causes
dependent. This is **explaining away**. The same holds when any descendant of $c$ is observed, since a descendant
carries information about $c$.

## d-separation

Let $A$, $B$ and $C$ be disjoint sets of nodes. An undirected path between a node of $A$ and a node of $B$ is
**blocked** by $C$ if it contains a node $m$ such that either

- the arrows meet head-to-tail ($\to m \to$) or tail-to-tail ($\leftarrow m \to$) at $m$, and $m \in C$; or
- the arrows meet head-to-head ($\to m \leftarrow$) at $m$, and neither $m$ nor any descendant of $m$ is in $C$.

$A$ and $B$ are **d-separated** by $C$ if every such path is blocked. The two conditions are the three canonical
structures applied at each interior node of the path. If $A$ and $B$ are d-separated by $C$, then
$A \perp B \mid C$ in every distribution that factorises over the graph <Cite id="pearl1988" />. Conversely, if they
are not d-separated, the independence fails for almost every choice of the conditionals, so d-separation finds all
the independences that the graph alone implies <Cite id="koller2009" />.

## Worked example: explaining away

A burglary $B$ or an earthquake $E$ can set off an alarm $A$. The graph is the collider $B \to A \leftarrow E$, with
$p(B = 1) = 0.001$, $p(E = 1) = 0.002$ and

| $B$ | $E$ | $p(A = 1 \mid B, E)$ |
| --- | --- | -------------------- |
| 1   | 1   | 0.95                 |
| 1   | 0   | 0.94                 |
| 0   | 1   | 0.29                 |
| 0   | 0   | 0.001                |

The joint is $p(B)\,p(E)\,p(A \mid B, E)$. Summing it over $B$ and $E$ with $A = 1$ gives $p(A = 1) = 0.002516$.

- **Alarm observed.** The terms with $B = 1$ sum to $0.001 \times (0.002 \times 0.95 + 0.998 \times 0.94) = 0.000940$,
  so $p(B = 1 \mid A = 1) = 0.000940 / 0.002516 = 0.374$. The alarm raises the probability of a burglary from 0.001 to
  0.374.
- **Alarm and earthquake observed.** Fix $E = 1$ as well:

  $$
  p(B = 1 \mid A = 1, E = 1) = \frac{0.001 \times 0.95}{0.001 \times 0.95 + 0.999 \times 0.29} = 0.0033.
  $$

The earthquake explains the alarm, and the probability of a burglary falls back to 0.0033. $B$ and $E$ are independent
a priori, but they are dependent given $A$, as the collider analysis predicts.

## Inference

Inference in a Bayesian network means computing a conditional such as $p(B \mid A = 1)$: sum the joint over the
unobserved variables that are not queried, then normalise. Exact inference is NP-hard in general
<Cite id="cooper1990" />. Its cost is governed by the structure of the graph. For graphs whose moral graph is a tree,
<NoteLink to="belief-propagation">belief propagation</NoteLink> computes every marginal in linear time;
<NoteLink to="variable-elimination">variable elimination</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="junction-tree-algorithm">junction tree algorithm</NoteLink> handle general graphs at a cost exponential in
their treewidth. Approximate methods include
<NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="variational-inference">variational inference</NoteLink>.

Familiar models are Bayesian networks. <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">Naive Bayes</NoteLink> is a fork with the class as the
common parent of every feature, and
<NoteLink to="tree-augmented-naive-bayes">tree-augmented naive Bayes</NoteLink> adds a tree of edges among the features. A
<NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink> is a chain of hidden
states, each with one observed child.

## Assumptions

- **Acyclicity.** The graph has no directed cycles. A feedback loop must be unrolled in time, as in a dynamic Bayesian
  network.
- **Faithfulness is not assumed.** The graph guarantees the independences that d-separation finds. Particular numbers
  in the conditionals can create extra independences that the graph does not show.

## Pitfalls

- **Edges are not causal claims.** A Bayesian network is a factorisation. The graphs $a \to b$ and $b \to a$ represent
  the same set of distributions. A causal reading needs further assumptions.
- **Conditioning on a collider.** Selecting data on a common effect creates dependence between its causes. This is the
  source of selection bias and Berkson's paradox.
- **Different graphs, same independences.** Two graphs imply the same independences exactly when they have the same
  skeleton and the same v-structures <Cite id="koller2009" />. The chain, the reversed chain and the fork are indistinguishable from
  independence information alone.
