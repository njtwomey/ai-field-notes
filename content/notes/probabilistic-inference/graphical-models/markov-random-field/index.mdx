<Definition>
A Markov random field over random variables $\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is an undirected graph $G$ with one node per
variable, together with a non-negative **potential** $\psi_C(\xvec_C)$ for each clique $C$ of $G$. The joint
distribution is

$$
p(\xvec) = \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{C} \psi_C(\xvec_C), \qquad Z = \sum_{\xvec} \prod_{C} \psi_C(\xvec_C),
$$

where $\xvec_C$ is the set of variables in $C$ and the **partition function** $Z$ normalises the product (an integral
replaces the sum for continuous variables) <Cite id="koller2009" />.
</Definition>

## Potentials and energies

A clique is a set of nodes that are all joined to each other. Potentials need not be probabilities and need not sum to
1; they are local scores of compatibility. Only their product, normalised by $Z$, is a distribution. When every
potential is positive it can be written $\psi_C = \exp(-E_C)$, and the distribution takes the **Gibbs** or
**Boltzmann** form

$$
p(\xvec) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\paren{-\sum_C E_C(\xvec_C)}.
$$

The sum of clique energies is the energy of the configuration. Low energy means high probability. Potentials of the
form $E_C(\xvec_C) = -\thetavec_C^\top \fvec_C(\xvec_C)$, linear in parameters $\thetavec_C$, give a log-linear model,
an exponential family whose sufficient statistics are the features $\fvec_C$ <Cite id="wainwright2008" />.

A product over the maximal cliques is enough, since a potential on a smaller clique can be absorbed into a maximal
clique that contains it. Many models use only pairwise potentials, one per edge, even when larger cliques exist.

## Separation implies independence

The graph encodes independences by separation. For disjoint sets of nodes $A$, $B$ and $S$, $S$ **separates** $A$ from
$B$ if every path from a node of $A$ to a node of $B$ passes through $S$. The **global Markov property** states that
separation implies $\xvec_A \perp \xvec_B \mid \xvec_S$.

Any distribution of the form above has this property. Let $A'$ be the set of nodes reachable from $A$ without passing
through $S$, and let $B'$ be the remaining nodes outside $S$, so $B \subseteq B'$. A clique cannot contain a node of
$A'$ and a node of $B'$, because those two nodes would be adjacent and the second would then be reachable from $A$.
Every clique therefore lies inside $A' \cup S$ or inside $B' \cup S$. Grouping the potentials accordingly,

$$
p(\xvec) = \frac{1}{Z}\, f(\xvec_{A'}, \xvec_S)\, g(\xvec_{B'}, \xvec_S).
$$

A joint density of the form $f(\xvec_{A'}, \xvec_S)\, g(\xvec_{B'}, \xvec_S)$ gives
$p(\xvec_{A'}, \xvec_{B'} \mid \xvec_S) \propto f\, g$, a product of a function of $\xvec_{A'}$ and a function of
$\xvec_{B'}$ for each fixed $\xvec_S$. So $\xvec_{A'} \perp \xvec_{B'} \mid \xvec_S$, and marginalising gives
$\xvec_A \perp \xvec_B \mid \xvec_S$.

Two weaker properties follow. The **local Markov property**: each variable is independent of all others given its
neighbours. The neighbours of a node are its Markov blanket. The **pairwise Markov property**: two non-adjacent
variables are independent given all the other variables.

## The Hammersley–Clifford theorem

The converse needs positivity. **Theorem (Hammersley–Clifford).** If $p(\xvec) > 0$ for every $\xvec$ and $p$
satisfies the pairwise Markov property with respect to $G$, then $p$ factorises as a product of positive potentials
over the cliques of $G$ <Cite id="besag1974" />.

For positive distributions, the factorisation, the global, the local and the pairwise Markov properties are therefore
equivalent. Without positivity they are not. Some distributions with zeros satisfy every Markov property of a graph
and still do not factorise over its cliques <Cite id="koller2009" />.

## Worked example: the Ising model

The Ising model has spins $x_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ on the nodes of a graph, a coupling $J$ on each edge and an external
field $h$ <Cite id="ising1925" />:

$$
p(\xvec) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\paren{J \sum_{(i, j) \in E} x_i x_j + h \sum_i x_i}.
$$

Positive $J$ favours neighbours that agree. Take a chain of three spins, $x_1 - x_2 - x_3$, with $h = 0$. The joint is
$\exp\paren{J(x_1 x_2 + x_2 x_3)}/Z$.

- **Partition function.** Substitute the bond variables $s = x_1 x_2$ and $t = x_2 x_3$. For each value of $x_2$, the
  map from $(x_1, x_3)$ to $(s, t)$ is a bijection onto $\set{-1, +1}^2$, so
  $Z = \sum_{x_2} \sum_{s, t} e^{Js} e^{Jt} = 2\,(2\cosh J)^2 = 8\cosh^2 J$. For $J = 1$, $Z = 19.05$.
- **Correlations.** The joint of $(s, t)$ is $e^{Js} e^{Jt}$ up to a constant, so $s$ and $t$ are independent, each
  with $\expect[s] = (e^J - e^{-J})/(e^J + e^{-J}) = \tanh J$. Then $\expect[x_1 x_2] = \tanh J = 0.762$ and
  $\expect[x_1 x_3] = \expect[s t] = \tanh^2 J = 0.580$ for $J = 1$.
- **Separation.** $x_2$ separates $x_1$ from $x_3$. Given $x_2$, the joint is $e^{J x_1 x_2} e^{J x_2 x_3}$, a product of
  a function of $x_1$ and a function of $x_3$, so $x_1 \perp x_3 \mid x_2$. Marginally they are correlated, with
  $\expect[x_1 x_3] = 0.580$.

On a chain of length $m$ the same argument gives $\expect[x_1 x_m] = \tanh^{m-1} J$: correlation decays geometrically
along the chain. On a two-dimensional lattice it does not always decay. Above a critical coupling, distant spins stay
correlated, which is the model's phase transition.

## Directed and undirected graphs

A Bayesian network converts to a Markov random field by **moralisation**: join every pair of parents that share a child,
then drop the directions. Each conditional $p(x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$ becomes a potential on the clique
formed by $x_i$ and its parents, and $Z = 1$. The moral graph can lose independences. The collider $a \to c \leftarrow b$
has $a \perp b$ marginally; its moral graph is a triangle, which implies no independence.

Neither family contains the other <Cite id="bishop2006" />. A collider has no undirected equivalent. A four-cycle
$a - b - c - d - a$, with $a \perp c \mid \set{b, d}$ and $b \perp d \mid \set{a, c}$, has no directed equivalent.
Undirected models suit symmetric interactions such as neighbouring pixels or spins. Directed models suit generative
stories with a causal order. A <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> represents both.

## Assumptions

- **Positivity for the converse.** Hammersley–Clifford needs $p > 0$. Hard constraints, such as zero-probability
  configurations, can break the equivalence of the Markov properties.
- **The graph fixes the cliques, not the potentials.** The same graph admits a single potential over a large clique or
  only pairwise potentials. The graph alone does not say which.

## Pitfalls

- **Potentials are not marginals.** $\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)$ is not $p(x_i, x_j)$, and changing one potential changes every
  marginal through $Z$. Local potentials cannot be read as local probabilities.
- **The partition function is hard.** Computing $Z$ sums over every configuration, $2^n$ of them for $n$ binary
  variables, and is #P-hard in general. Maximum-likelihood learning needs $\nabla \log Z$, which equals the expected
  features under the model, so every gradient step needs inference. The
  <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random field</NoteLink> conditions on the inputs to keep this
  tractable for chains.
- **Sampling is not ancestral.** There is no topological order to sample in. Samplers such as
  <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink> iterate local updates instead.
