## Architecture

Both models are stacks of <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> blocks
<Cite id="vaswani2017" /> with <NoteLink to="learned-absolute-position-embeddings">learned position embeddings</NoteLink> and <Gloss name="gelu" /> activations. BERT-Base was given the same size as
GPT on purpose, so that the two could be compared <Cite id="devlin2019" />.

**GPT** applies a <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink>: token $t$ attends only to tokens $1, \dots, t$.
Its representation of a token therefore depends only on the text to its left <Cite id="radford2018" />.

**BERT** has no mask on attention. Every token attends to the whole input, so its representation depends on both sides.
BERT also adds a learned segment embedding that marks whether a token belongs to the first or the second of two packed
sentences <Cite id="devlin2019" />.

## Training

**GPT** minimises the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative log-likelihood</NoteLink> of each token given the tokens before it, over contiguous 512-token
passages of BooksCorpus, a collection of over 7,000 unpublished books <Cite id="radford2018" />. For a downstream task,
the input is rewritten as one token sequence with start, delimiter and end tokens. A linear layer on the final token's
representation is fine-tuned, with the language-modelling loss kept as an auxiliary objective
<Cite id="radford2018" />.

**BERT** cannot be trained left to right: with bidirectional attention, each token could see itself in the layers above.
Instead it uses a <NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">masked language model</NoteLink>. 15% of token positions are
chosen at random. Of these, 80% are replaced by a `[MASK]` token, 10% by a random token and 10% are left unchanged, and
the model predicts the original tokens <Cite id="devlin2019" />. A second objective, **next-sentence prediction**, asks
whether sentence B followed sentence A in the corpus. For fine-tuning, a classifier reads the final representation of a
`[CLS]` token placed at the start of every input.

## What is distinctive

**GPT** established the two-stage recipe that both models follow: generative pre-training of one transformer on
unlabelled text, then <Gloss name="sft">supervised fine-tuning</Gloss> of the same network with a small task-specific head. It improved the state
of the art on 9 of the 12 tasks it studied <Cite id="radford2018" />.

**BERT** showed that, for understanding tasks, conditioning on both sides of each token helps more than model size.
Its ablations on BERT-Base isolate the objective. Replacing the masked objective by left-to-right training, and dropping
next-sentence prediction, lowered development-set scores on every task tested <Cite id="devlin2019" />:

| Pre-training (BERT-Base architecture) | MNLI-m | QNLI | MRPC | SST-2 | <Gloss name="squad" /> 1.1 (F1) |
| ------------------------------------- | ------ | ---- | ---- | ----- | -------------- |
| Masked LM + next sentence             | 84.4   | 88.4 | 86.7 | 92.7  | 88.5           |
| Masked LM only                        | 83.9   | 84.9 | 86.5 | 92.6  | 87.9           |
| Left to right only                    | 82.1   | 84.3 | 77.5 | 92.1  | 77.8           |

The largest loss is on SQuAD, where the answer span must be located using text on both sides of it: 10.7 F1 points.

## Comparison

**Direction of context.** GPT's representation of a token sees only its left context; BERT's sees both sides. This is the
main architectural difference, and the ablation above attributes most of BERT's advantage on these tasks to it.

**Generation.** GPT is a <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss>: it assigns a probability to every continuation and generates text token by
token. BERT's masked objective does not define a probability over sequences, so BERT cannot generate text left to right.
This is why the GPT line, not BERT, led to the <NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">large generative
language models</NoteLink>.

**Data and batch.** BERT was trained on about four times as many words as GPT, 3,300M against 800M, with four times the
<NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink>. BERT's authors list these differences and argue from the ablation that the objective, not the data, explains
most of the gain <Cite id="devlin2019" />.

**Efficiency of the objective.** GPT receives a training signal at every position. BERT's masked objective predicts only
the 15% of positions that are masked, so it extracts fewer predictions from each sequence. BERT also introduces a
mismatch: `[MASK]` appears in pre-training but never in fine-tuning, which the 80/10/10 replacement rule reduces.

**Tokenisers.** GPT uses <NoteLink to="byte-pair-encoding">byte-pair encoding</NoteLink> with 40,000 merges; BERT uses
<NoteLink to="wordpiece-and-unigram-tokenisation">WordPiece</NoteLink> with a 30,000-token vocabulary. Both split rare
words into subword units.
