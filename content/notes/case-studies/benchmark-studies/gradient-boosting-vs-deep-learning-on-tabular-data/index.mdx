## The two studies

**Grinsztajn et al.** built a benchmark to remove side issues that often decide tabular comparisons
<Cite id="grinsztajn2022" />. Training sets were truncated to 10,000 rows, missing data were removed, classification was
binarised and balanced, and <NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">high-cardinality categorical features</NoteLink> were dropped. The 45 datasets formed four groups: 15
classification and 19 regression tasks with numerical features only, and 7 classification and 14 regression tasks with
categorical features too. Each model was tuned by random search, and the result was recorded as a function of the number
of search iterations, averaged over 15 random orderings of the search. This measures how good each model is after a
given tuning effort, not only at its best.

**Shwartz-Ziv and Armon** asked whether deep models proposed for tabular data work outside the datasets in their own
papers <Cite id="shwartzziv2022" />. They took TabNet, NODE and DNF-Net, each with the three datasets from its paper, a
1D-CNN, and two datasets used by none of them. Every model was tuned with the same Bayesian search.

## Results

**Grinsztajn et al.** Tree-based models outperformed every neural network at every random-search budget, on every one of
the four groups <Cite id="grinsztajn2022" />. The gap stayed wide after many search iterations, although each iteration
is slower for a network. Removing categorical features narrowed the gap but did not close it, so categorical variables
are not the main weakness of networks. With training sets of 50,000 rows, the gap was usually smaller.

**Shwartz-Ziv and Armon.** Each deep model performed best on the datasets from its own paper and worse elsewhere. XGBoost
outperformed the deep models on 8 of the 11 datasets. Averaged over the datasets each model had not been designed on,
the relative deterioration from the best model on each dataset was <Cite id="shwartzziv2022" />:

| Model                           | Average relative deterioration (%) |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Ensemble of deep models + XGBoost | 2.32                             |
| XGBoost                         | 3.34                               |
| 1D-CNN                          | 7.56                               |
| TabNet                          | 10.51                              |
| DNF-Net                         | 11.96                              |
| NODE                            | 14.21                              |

XGBoost also reached good hyperparameters in fewer search iterations, and trained more than an order of magnitude
faster than the deep models.

## Why trees win on these datasets

Grinsztajn et al. tested three explanations by transforming the data and watching which models were affected
<Cite id="grinsztajn2022" />.

**Irregular target functions.** Smoothing the training targets with a Gaussian kernel hurt the tree-based models
markedly and barely affected the networks. The targets are therefore not smooth functions of the features. Trees fit
piecewise-constant functions and represent sharp changes easily; neural networks are biased toward smooth,
low-frequency functions.

**Uninformative features.** Removing up to half of the features, in order of <NoteLink to="random-forest">random-forest</NoteLink> importance, barely changed a
gradient-boosted tree's accuracy, so many tabular features carry little information. Removing them narrowed the gap
between MLP-like networks and the rest; adding noise features widened it.

**Rotation invariance.** An <Gloss name="mlp" /> is unchanged by rotating the input features. If every input $\xvec$ is replaced by
$\Qmat\xvec$ for an orthogonal matrix $\Qmat$, the first layer's $\Wmat\xvec$ becomes $\Wmat\Qmat\xvec$, and the weights
$\Wmat\Qmat^\top$ reproduce the original network exactly. With a rotation-symmetric initialisation, such as independent
Gaussian weights, <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> follows the same path in the rotated coordinates. A rotation-invariant learner cannot
favour the original axes, so it cannot find and discard an irrelevant feature without first recovering the orientation.
Ng proved that such learners need a number of samples that grows at least linearly in the number of irrelevant features
<Cite id="ng2004" />. A tree splits on one original feature at a time and is not rotation-invariant. After random
rotations of the datasets, the ranking reversed: networks beat trees, and <Gloss name="resnet" plural /> beat FT-Transformers
<Cite id="grinsztajn2022" />. Tabular features have individual meanings, such as age or weight, and a learner that uses
the original axes benefits from that.

## Comparison

**Scope.** Grinsztajn et al. is broader: 45 datasets, both tree families, and two recent tabular <NoteLink to="transformer">transformers</NoteLink>,
FT-Transformer <Cite id="gorishniy2021" /> and SAINT. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon is narrower but tests a specific failure:
models that looked strong on the datasets chosen by their authors.

**Agreement.** Both studies find tree ensembles, in particular <NoteLink to="xgboost-lightgbm-catboost">XGBoost</NoteLink>
<Cite id="chen2016b" />, more accurate than published deep architectures on typical tabular data, and cheaper to tune.

**Where networks help.** Neither study shows networks to be useless on tables. The ensemble of XGBoost with deep models
was the best model in Shwartz-Ziv and Armon, and the gap in Grinsztajn et al. narrowed with larger training sets.
Networks also remain the natural choice when a table sits beside text or images, or when the model must be trained end
to end with other components.

**Limits.** Both studies used medium-sized datasets. Grinsztajn et al. also removed missing values and high-cardinality
categorical features, which are common in practice and which <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient-boosting</NoteLink> libraries handle natively.
