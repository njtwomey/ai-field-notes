## Setup

**M4** asked for point forecasts, and optionally 95% <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink>, for 100,000 series drawn from six domains
<Cite id="makridakis2020" />. Point forecasts were scored by two scale-free errors. The symmetric mean absolute percentage
error (see <NoteLink to="percentage-errors">percentage errors</NoteLink>) divides each absolute error by the average of
the forecast and the actual value. The mean absolute scaled error (see <NoteLink to="scaled-errors-and-mean-absolute-scaled-error">MASE</NoteLink>)
divides the forecast's <Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean absolute error</Gloss> by the in-sample error of a seasonal naive forecast. The **overall weighted
average** (OWA) divides each by its value for **Naive2**, a naive forecast applied to seasonally adjusted data, and
averages the two ratios. Naive2 scores OWA 1 by construction; lower is better.

**M5** used real sales from Walmart: 3,049 products sold in 10 stores in California, Texas and Wisconsin, with prices,
promotions and a calendar of events <Cite id="mcompetitions2020" />. The 30,490 product-store series aggregate into 12
levels, from the total down to product-store, giving 42,840 series in all. Many bottom-level series are intermittent, with
many days of zero sales. The measure is the **weighted root mean squared scaled error** (WRMSSE). Each series' error is
scaled by the in-sample one-step naive error, weighted by the series' share of dollar sales, and the 12 levels count
equally <Cite id="makridakis2022" />.

## The winners

**M4: ES-RNN.** Smyl's method fits, for each series, the level and seasonal components of an
<NoteLink to="error-trend-seasonal-models">exponential smoothing</NoteLink> model. It removes them to normalise the series, and feeds the
normalised values to a recurrent network with dilated <NoteLink to="long-short-term-memory">LSTM</NoteLink> layers that is trained across
all series at once <Cite id="smyl2020" />. The exponential-smoothing parameters are learned jointly with the network. The
statistical part handles each series' scale and seasonality; the network learns patterns shared across series. The
second-placed method, **FFORMA**, is a meta-learner: it computes features of each series and uses <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient-boosted trees</NoteLink>
to choose weights for combining nine standard forecasting methods <Cite id="monteromanso2020" />.

**M5: LightGBM.** The winner combined, with equal weights, <NoteLink to="xgboost-lightgbm-catboost">LightGBM</NoteLink>
models trained on the product-store series using data per store, per store and category, and per store and department
<Cite id="makridakis2022" />. Each model was fitted across
many series rather than one model per series.

## What each competition showed

**M4: combinations won, pure machine learning lost.** Twelve of the seventeen most accurate methods were combinations of
forecasts, mostly of statistical methods <Cite id="makridakis2020" />. The two best methods both used machine learning,
but together with statistical models, not instead of them. Every pure machine-learning method ranked below the
statistical benchmarks Theta and the combination benchmark. The organisers' own <Gloss name="mlp" /> and <Gloss name="rnn" /> benchmarks, fitted to each
series separately, scored OWA 1.642 and 1.482: worse than Naive2 <Cite id="mcompetitions2018" />. A year later, the pure
deep-learning model <NoteLink to="n-beats">N-BEATS</NoteLink>, trained across all series, reached OWA 0.795 on the M4
data, 3% better than the winner <Cite id="oreshkin2020" />. The M4 network benchmarks were fitted to each series
separately; N-BEATS, like the winner, learned from all series at once.

**M5: machine learning won on real, related series.** In M5 the series share products, stores and calendar effects,
and come with explanatory variables. A tree ensemble trained across series could use all of this. The winner beat the
best benchmark by 22.4% overall. The gain was uneven across the hierarchy <Cite id="mcompetitions2020" />:

| Level                      | Winner (WRMSSE) | Bottom-up exponential smoothing | Improvement |
| -------------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------- |
| Total sales (level 1)      | 0.199           | 0.426                           | 53%         |
| Product by store (level 12) | 0.884           | 0.915                           | 3%          |

At the product-store level, where most series are intermittent and noisy, the winner was only 3% better than
exponential smoothing. The large gains came from aggregate levels, where learning shared patterns across series pays off
most.

## Comparison

**Data.** M4 series are unrelated, mostly short, and carry no covariates. M5 series form one hierarchy, are daily, and
come with prices, promotions and events. Methods that learn across series have more to exploit in M5.

**Winning method.** Both winners learned across series. M4's winner kept a statistical model per series and learned a
shared correction; M5's winner used a single gradient-boosted model per group of series with engineered features.

**Role of combinations.** Both competitions confirmed that combining forecasts improves accuracy. The M5 winner itself
was an equal-weighted combination of several LightGBM models <Cite id="makridakis2022" />.

**Statistical benchmarks.** In M4, simple benchmarks (Theta, the exponential-smoothing combination) beat most
submissions, including every pure machine-learning one. In M5, the best benchmark was beaten by a wide margin overall,
but it stayed close at the bottom of the hierarchy.

**Measures shape conclusions.** OWA weights every series equally, so M4 rewards accuracy on the many short yearly and
quarterly series. WRMSSE weights by sales value and gives each aggregation level equal weight, so M5 rewards accuracy on
high-selling products and on aggregates.
