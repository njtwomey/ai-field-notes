<Definition>
**Label noise** is the corruption of training labels, so that an example's recorded label $\tilde y$ differs from its
true class $y$ with some probability. The notes in this topic model how the flips arise, estimate how often they happen,
train classifiers that tolerate them, find the mislabelled examples, and test which kind of noise a dataset has.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Labels come from crowd workers, heuristics, weak supervision, clinical records and automatic pipelines, and all of them
make mistakes. Benchmark <NoteLink to="test-sets">test sets</NoteLink> contain errors too: a study of ten common
datasets estimated an average of over 3% of test labels to be wrong <Cite id="northcutt2021pervasive" />. Noise in
training labels biases the learned posterior, moves the decision boundary and can be memorised by a flexible model.
Noise in test labels distorts the comparison of models <Cite id="frenay2014" />.

## Main ideas

**Say what the flips depend on.** <NoteLink to="label-noise-models" /> distinguishes uniform noise, which depends on
nothing, class-conditional noise, which depends on the true class through a transition matrix $\Tmat$ with $T_{ij} =
\Pr(\tilde y = j \mid y = i)$, and instance-dependent noise, which also depends on the input. Uniform noise leaves the
Bayes classifier unchanged. Class-conditional noise with unequal flip rates moves it.

**The flip rates must be estimated.** Noise rates are not identifiable from noisy labels alone.
<NoteLink to="estimating-noise-rates" /> uses anchor points, inputs whose clean posterior is 0 or 1, where the noisy
posterior reads off the flip rates directly. Mixture proportion estimation gives the same rates under weaker
assumptions.

**Correct the loss, or choose a loss that does not need correcting.** <NoteLink to="learning-with-noisy-labels" />
corrects the loss with the transition matrix, either backward, by applying $\Tmat^{-1}$ to the vector of losses, or
forward, by passing the model's predicted probabilities through $\Tmat$ before the loss <Cite id="natarajan2013" />.
<NoteLink to="noise-robust-losses" /> takes the other route. A symmetric loss, such as
<Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean absolute error</Gloss>, has the same minimiser under uniform noise as under clean
labels. Cross-entropy is not symmetric, and generalised cross-entropy interpolates between the two.

**Find and fix the errors.** <NoteLink to="detecting-and-cleaning-label-errors" /> flags examples whose label is
disputed by a model trained on the noisy data. Confident learning counts the examples whose predicted probability of
another class exceeds that class's mean self-confidence, which estimates the joint distribution of given and true
labels.

**Test which noise is present.** The correction methods assume a noise model, and the assumption can be tested.
<NoteLink to="hypothesis-testing-for-class-conditional-label-noise" /> gives a $z$-test of uniform against
class-conditional noise at anchor points where the true posterior is one half.
<NoteLink to="local-maximum-likelihood-noise-test" /> makes the test nonparametric with local
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="label-noise-models" />, which defines the transition matrix and the three kinds of noise.
Estimating noise rates comes next, since loss correction needs the rates. Learning with noisy labels and noise-robust
losses are the two ways to train. Detecting label errors cleans the data instead. The two hypothesis-test notes come
last; the local test refines the parametric one.
