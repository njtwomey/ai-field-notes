<Definition>
**Fairness** in machine learning asks whether a model's predictions or decisions disadvantage people because of a
protected attribute $A$, such as sex or ethnicity. Group fairness compares statistics of a score $R$ and the target $Y$
across the groups defined by $A$. Individual fairness asks that similar people receive similar outcomes.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Models now inform lending, hiring, policing, medical triage and content moderation. A model trained to maximise accuracy
on historical data reproduces the patterns in that data, including unequal treatment of groups, and can amplify them.
Removing $A$ from the inputs does not prevent this, because other features act as proxies for it. The notes here make
fairness precise enough to measure, show which definitions conflict, and give methods that enforce a chosen definition
<Cite id="barocas2023" />.

## Main ideas

**Three group criteria.** <NoteLink to="fairness-criteria" /> writes group fairness as conditional-independence
statements. Independence, $R \perp A$, asks for equal positive rates across groups (demographic parity). Separation,
$R \perp A \mid Y$, asks for equal error rates within
each true class (equalised odds). Sufficiency, $Y \perp A \mid
R$, asks that a score mean the same in every group, which calibration within groups gives.

**The criteria conflict.** <NoteLink to="impossibility-of-fairness" /> shows that when base rates differ between groups,
a classifier cannot equalise precision and both error rates across them, and a score cannot be calibrated within groups
and balanced for both classes, unless prediction is perfect. Choosing a criterion is therefore a choice about which
errors matter, not a technical detail.

**Similar people, similar outcomes.** <NoteLink to="individual-fairness" /> asks for a Lipschitz condition: the
distance between two people's outcome distributions must not exceed a task-specific distance between the people. The
condition needs that distance to be specified, which moves the ethical judgement into the metric. Counterfactual
fairness instead asks whether a decision would change had the protected attribute been different, in a causal model.

**Enforce a criterion at one of three stages.** <NoteLink to="bias-mitigation" /> acts before training, by reweighing
examples or learning fair representations, during training, by constraints, reductions or adversaries, or after
training, by choosing group-specific thresholds. The post-processing method of Hardt, Price and Srebro solves a linear
program over randomised thresholds to achieve equalised odds <Cite id="hardt2016" />.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="fairness-criteria" />, which defines the vocabulary. The impossibility note requires it and
explains why a single fair classifier does not exist under unequal base rates. Individual fairness is an alternative
to the group criteria and can be read independently. Bias mitigation comes last and applies whichever criterion has
been chosen.
