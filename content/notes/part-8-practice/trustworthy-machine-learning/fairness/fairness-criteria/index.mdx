<Definition>
Let $Y$ be the target variable, $A$ a protected attribute that splits the population into groups, and $R$ a score or
prediction computed by a classifier. The three **group fairness criteria** of Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan are
<Cite id="barocas2023" />:

- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink>:** $R \perp A$. The distribution of the score is the same in
  every group.
- **Separation:** $R \perp A \mid Y$. Within each true class, the distribution of the score is the same in every group.
- **Sufficiency:** $Y \perp A \mid R$. Given the score, the probability of each outcome is the same in every group.

</Definition>

## Intuition

Each criterion names a different quantity that must not depend on group membership. Independence constrains the
decisions alone: a lender approves the same share of applicants in each group. Separation constrains the errors: people
who would repay are approved at the same rate in each group, and people who would default are approved at the same rate
in each group. Sufficiency constrains the meaning of the score: an applicant scored $0.3$ defaults with the same
probability whichever group the applicant belongs to.

The criteria are statements about <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink>, not about a particular
metric. The <NoteLink to="group-fairness-metrics">group fairness metrics</NoteLink> measure how far a given classifier
is from each criterion, as differences or ratios of confusion-matrix rates.

## The criteria for binary predictions and for scores

For a binary prediction $R = \hat Y \in \set{0, 1}$ and a binary target, each criterion reduces to equal rates across
groups $a$:

| criterion | binary form, equal for all $a$ | named constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| independence | $\pr(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = a)$ | demographic parity |
| separation | $\pr(\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = y, A = a)$ for $y = 0, 1$ | equalised odds <Cite id="hardt2016" /> |
| sufficiency | $\pr(Y = 1 \mid \hat Y = \hat y, A = a)$ for $\hat y = 0, 1$ | equal positive and negative predictive values |

Separation for binary predictions equalises the <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rate</Gloss> ($y = 1$) and the
<Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> ($y = 0$). Constraining the true-positive rate alone is the relaxation
called equal opportunity <Cite id="hardt2016" />.

For a score $R \in [0, 1]$, sufficiency is closely tied to calibration. A score is **calibrated within groups** when
$\pr(Y = 1 \mid R = r, A = a) = r$ for every $r$ and $a$. A score calibrated within groups satisfies sufficiency,
because $\pr(Y = 1 \mid R = r, A = a) = r$ does not depend on $a$. Conversely, if $R$ satisfies sufficiency, the
recalibrated score $r \mapsto \pr(Y = 1 \mid R = r)$ is calibrated within every group <Cite id="barocas2023" />.

Separation for scores requires every group to have the same ROC curve, not only the same operating point.

## Why the criteria exclude each other

Suppose the base rates differ between groups, so $A$ and $Y$ are dependent: $A \not\perp Y$. Then each pair of criteria
is incompatible, except in degenerate cases <Cite id="barocas2023" />.

### Independence and sufficiency

Independence gives $p(a \mid r) = p(a)$. Sufficiency gives $p(a \mid y, r) = p(a \mid r)$. Together, for every
$(a, y, r)$,

$$
p(a, y, r) = p(a \mid y, r)\,p(y, r) = p(a \mid r)\,p(y, r) = p(a)\,p(y, r).
$$

So $A \perp (Y, R)$. Summing over $r$ gives $p(a, y) = p(a)\,p(y)$, which is $A \perp Y$. This contradicts the
assumption $A \not\perp Y$. Independence and sufficiency cannot hold together when base rates differ.

### Independence and separation

Take a binary target and write $\pi_a = \pr(Y = 1 \mid A = a)$ for the base rate of group $a$. By the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink>, and then by separation, which makes
$p(r \mid y, a) = p(r \mid y)$,

$$
p(r \mid a) = \pi_a\,p(r \mid Y = 1) + (1 - \pi_a)\,p(r \mid Y = 0) = p(r \mid Y = 0) + \pi_a\big[p(r \mid Y = 1) - p(r \mid Y = 0)\big].
$$

Independence requires $p(r \mid a)$ to be the same for every group $a$. The first term does not depend on $a$. If two
groups have $\pi_a \ne \pi_{a'}$, the second term is the same for both only when $p(r \mid Y = 1) = p(r \mid Y = 0)$ for
every $r$, which is $R \perp Y$. So independence and separation hold together, with unequal base rates, only for a score
that carries no information about $Y$.

### Separation and sufficiency

Assume every combination of $(a, r, y)$ has positive probability. Separation gives $p(a \mid r, y) = p(a \mid y)$, and
sufficiency gives $p(a \mid r, y) = p(a \mid r)$. So $p(a \mid y) = p(a \mid r)$ for every pair $(r, y)$. The left side
does not depend on $r$ and the right side does not depend on $y$, so both equal a constant $c_a$ that depends on $a$
only. Averaging $p(a \mid y) = c_a$ over $y$ gives $p(a) = c_a$, so $p(a \mid y) = p(a)$ for every $y$. That is
$A \perp Y$, contradicting the assumption. Separation and sufficiency are therefore incompatible when base rates differ
and all joint probabilities are positive. A perfect predictor escapes the result because it violates positivity: the
events $\set{R = 1, Y = 0}$ and $\set{R = 0, Y = 1}$ have probability zero.

The quantitative form of the last result, for binary predictions and for calibrated scores, is the
<NoteLink to="impossibility-of-fairness">impossibility of fairness</NoteLink> of Chouldechova and of Kleinberg et al.
<Cite id="chouldechova2017" /> <Cite id="kleinberg2017" />.

## Choosing a criterion

The choice among the criteria is a normative decision about what the score is used for. Independence ignores the target
entirely, so it can be met by a classifier that is accurate in one group and random in the other. Separation accepts
different decision rates when base rates differ, but asks that errors fall equally on each group. Sufficiency asks that
a score mean the same thing for everyone.

Sufficiency often holds approximately for an unconstrained model fitted to enough data <Cite id="barocas2023" />.
If $R = \pr(Y = 1 \mid X)$ is the true posterior and $A$ is a function of the features $X$, then
$\pr(Y = 1 \mid R = r, A = a) = \expect[\pr(Y = 1 \mid X) \mid R = r, A = a] = r$, so $R$ is calibrated within groups.

All three criteria are **observational**: they depend only on the joint distribution of $(R, A, Y)$. Two scenarios with
the same joint distribution but different causal mechanisms receive the same verdict. Criteria stated for individuals,
such as <NoteLink to="individual-fairness">individual fairness</NoteLink>, or in terms of a causal model address this.

## Assumptions

- The protected attribute $A$ is recorded and correctly measured for the evaluation data.
- $Y$ is the outcome of interest. When $Y$ is itself a biased measurement, such as arrests standing in for offences,
  separation and sufficiency are defined relative to the biased label.

## Pitfalls

- **Satisfying a criterion is not the same as being fair.** Each criterion is a necessary condition under one view of
  fairness, and each can be met by a harmful classifier.
- **Criteria hold for groups, not subgroups.** A classifier can satisfy separation for $A$ = sex and for $A$ = race
  separately while violating it at their intersections.
- **Estimates are noisy.** Equality of rates is tested on finite samples; small groups need
  <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>.
