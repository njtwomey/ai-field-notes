<Definition>
**Robustness** is the ability of a model to keep its accuracy when its inputs differ from the training data. The
difference may be natural, a shift in the distribution of inputs or labels between training and deployment, or
adversarial, a small perturbation chosen to make the model fail.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Most learning theory assumes that training and test data are drawn from the same distribution. Deployed models break
that assumption: hospitals use different scanners, cameras see new weather, and user behaviour changes over time.
Benchmarks built from such shifts show large drops in accuracy for models that score well in distribution
<Cite id="koh2021" />. Separately, models with high test accuracy can be fooled by perturbations too small for a person
to notice, which matters wherever an attacker controls the input.

## Main ideas

**Natural distribution shift.** <NoteLink to="robustness-to-distribution-shift" /> covers the setting where the test
distribution differs from the training one <Cite id="quinonero2009" />. <NoteLink to="domain-adaptation">Domain
adaptation</NoteLink> bounds the error on a target domain by the error on the source, a divergence between the two
domains and the best joint error. <NoteLink to="domain-generalisation">Domain generalisation</NoteLink>, invariant risk
minimisation and group distributionally robust optimisation train for domains or groups never seen, or for the worst
case among known groups.

**Adversarial examples.** <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples" /> are inputs $\xvec + \deltavec$ with a small
perturbation, such as $\norm{\deltavec}_\infty \le \epsilon$, that change a prediction. The fast gradient sign method
takes one step along the sign of the input gradient, and projected gradient
descent iterates it. Adversarial training minimises the loss at the worst perturbation found by such an
attack <Cite id="madry2018" />. Its robustness holds only against the attacks it was tested on.

**Certificates.** A defence that is only tested can be broken by a stronger attack.
<NoteLink to="certified-robustness" /> proves that no perturbation smaller than a radius $R$ changes the prediction.
Randomised smoothing predicts the class a base model returns most often under Gaussian noise, and gives an $\ell_2$
radius computed from the top two class probabilities, estimated by sampling.

## How the notes fit together
