<Definition>
**Trustworthy machine learning** studies the properties a model needs, beyond accuracy on a held-out test set, before
people can rely on its predictions. This topic covers five: correct behaviour when training labels are wrong,
explanations people can check, fair treatment of groups and individuals, stable behaviour when inputs change, and
protection of the people whose data trained the model.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A test-set score assumes clean labels, a test distribution equal to the deployment distribution and a single number as
the whole story. Deployed models break each assumption. Labels are noisy, inputs drift or are manipulated, errors fall
unevenly on different groups, regulators and users ask for reasons, and trained models can leak their training data.
Each subtopic here defines one of these failures precisely, measures it and gives methods that reduce it.

## Main ideas

**Labels can be wrong.** <NoteLink to="label-noise" /> models how labels get flipped, estimates the flip rates, corrects
the training loss or uses a loss robust to the flips, and finds the mislabelled examples. It also tests which kind of
noise a dataset has.

**Predictions can be explained.** <NoteLink to="interpretability" /> splits into models whose structure is their
explanation, such as sparse linear and additive models, and post-hoc explanations of a trained model: feature
attributions, counterfactuals and <NoteLink to="concept-based-explanations">concept-based explanations</NoteLink>. An
explanation is useful only if it is faithful to the model's computation, which must be measured rather than assumed
<Cite id="doshivelez2017" />.

**Decisions can be unfair.** <NoteLink to="fairness" /> writes group fairness as conditional-independence criteria on a
score, a protected attribute and the target. When base rates differ, the main criteria cannot all hold at once, so a
choice between them is unavoidable <Cite id="barocas2023" />. <NoteLink to="individual-fairness">Individual
fairness</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="bias-mitigation">bias-mitigation</NoteLink> methods complete the topic.

**Inputs can change.** <NoteLink to="robustness" /> covers natural distribution shift, with
<NoteLink to="domain-adaptation">domain adaptation</NoteLink> and group-robust training, and
<NoteLink to="adversarial-examples">adversarial examples</NoteLink>, with adversarial training and
<NoteLink to="certified-robustness">certified defences</NoteLink>.

**Training data can leak.** <NoteLink to="privacy-in-machine-learning" /> measures leakage with
<NoteLink to="membership-inference">membership inference attacks</NoteLink> and bounds it with
<Gloss name="differential-privacy">differential privacy</Gloss>, which <Gloss name="dp-sgd" /> applies to training
<Cite id="dwork2014" />. <NoteLink to="federated-learning">Federated learning</NoteLink> trains without collecting the
data centrally.

**The properties interact.** Adversarial training usually lowers accuracy on clean inputs. Differential privacy adds
noise to training and lowers accuracy. Fairness constraints need group labels, which are themselves sensitive data. A
trustworthy system therefore makes explicit trade-offs among these properties and accuracy.

## How the notes fit together

The five subtopics are independent and each opens with its own overview. <NoteLink to="label-noise" /> is the closest to
the evaluation material of Part IV and makes a natural start. Fairness builds on the decision rules and error rates of
classification. Robustness and privacy are both about threats from outside the training distribution, one to accuracy
and one to the data. Interpretability can be read at any point.
