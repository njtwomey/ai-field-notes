<Definition>
A positional encoding gives a <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> information about the order of its $n$
input tokens. An **absolute** scheme attaches a vector to each position $t$. A **relative** scheme changes the attention
score between query position $i$ and key position $j$ so that position enters only through the offset $j - i$. Without
either, a transformer encoder returns the same set of outputs for every ordering of its input tokens.
</Definition>

## Why attention needs position

Let $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times d}$ hold one token embedding per row. Self-attention computes

$$
f(\Xmat) = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Xmat \Wmat_Q (\Xmat \Wmat_K)^{\tr}}{\sqrt{d_k}}} \Xmat \Wmat_V,
$$

with the softmax applied to each row. Reordering the tokens multiplies $\Xmat$ on the left by a permutation matrix
$\Pmat$, which has one 1 in each row and column and satisfies $\Pmat^{\tr} \Pmat = \eye$.

The score matrix of the permuted input is

$$
\Pmat \Xmat \Wmat_Q (\Pmat \Xmat \Wmat_K)^{\tr} = \Pmat \paren{\Xmat \Wmat_Q \Wmat_K^{\tr} \Xmat^{\tr}} \Pmat^{\tr}
= \Pmat \Smat \Pmat^{\tr}.
$$

Multiplying by $\Pmat$ on the left reorders the rows of $\Smat$. Multiplying by $\Pmat^{\tr}$ on the right reorders the
entries within every row in the same way. The softmax of a row does not depend on the order of its entries, since each
entry is divided by the sum of the exponentials of all of them. So the row-wise softmax commutes with both reorderings:
$\operatorname{softmax}(\Pmat \Smat \Pmat^{\tr}) = \Pmat \operatorname{softmax}(\Smat) \Pmat^{\tr}$. Writing
$\Amat = \operatorname{softmax}(\Smat)$,

$$
f(\Pmat \Xmat) = \Pmat \Amat \Pmat^{\tr} \Pmat \Xmat \Wmat_V = \Pmat \Amat \Xmat \Wmat_V = \Pmat f(\Xmat).
$$

Self-attention is **permutation-equivariant**: permuting the inputs permutes the outputs in the same way and changes
nothing else. The feed-forward sublayer, <NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink> act on each row separately, so they are equivariant
too. A whole encoder is therefore equivariant. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce the same three output
vectors, attached to different positions.

A <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink> breaks the symmetry, because position $t$ sees exactly $t$ keys.
<NoteLink to="decoder-only-transformers">Causal decoders</NoteLink> can learn position without any encoding; see
<NoteLink to="nope-and-implicit-position">NoPE and implicit position</NoteLink>.

## Design choices

**Absolute or relative.** An absolute scheme labels each token with its index $t$. A relative scheme labels each pair
with $j - i$. Relative schemes treat a phrase the same wherever it occurs and have no table that runs out at long
lengths.

**Where position enters.**

- *Added to the input, once.* $\xvec_t + \pvec_t$ before the first layer. Sinusoidal and learned absolute embeddings.
- *Added inside attention, every layer.* Offset vectors added to keys (Shaw et al.), or separate content and position
  terms (Transformer-XL).
- *A bias on the score, every layer.* A scalar added to each logit: learned per bucket (<Gloss name="t5" />) or a fixed
  linear penalty (ALiBi).
- *A rotation of queries and keys, every layer.* A position-dependent orthogonal map applied before the dot product
  (RoPE, xPos).

**Fixed or learned.** Sinusoids, rotations and ALiBi's slopes have no trained parameters. Learned tables and biases fit
the data but exist only for the positions or buckets seen in training.

## Choosing a scheme

- **Length beyond training.** Learned tables stop at $T_{\max}$. Sinusoidal and rotary models are defined everywhere but
  degrade beyond their training length. ALiBi, the T5 bias and NoPE extrapolate further in perplexity
  <Cite id="press2022" /> <Cite id="kazemnejad2023" />. Rescaling rotary frequencies after training is covered in
  <NoteLink to="long-context-methods">long-context methods</NoteLink>.
- **Quality at the training length.** Differences are small. At 1.3 billion parameters on the Pile, perplexities ranged
  from 12.51 (ALiBi) to 13.10 (no encoding) <Cite id="haviv2022" />.
- **Compatibility with fast attention.** Rotations are applied to queries and keys before the attention kernel, and
  ALiBi's bias can be generated inside it. Shaw-style vectors change every pair's score and need a custom kernel.
- **Compatibility with the <Gloss name="kv-cache" />.** Rotary keys are rotated by their own position once and stay
  valid. Absolute encodings added at the input also work. Schemes where a key's representation depends on the query's
  position would force recomputation; none of the schemes above does.

## Assumptions

- Tokens form a single sequence. Images, video and tables use two- or three-dimensional variants; see
  <NoteLink to="rotary-position-embedding-variants">xPos and RoPE variants</NoteLink>.
- Relative schemes assume that the meaning of a relation between two tokens depends on their distance and not on where
  the pair sits.

## Pitfalls

- **Equivariance is not invariance.** Without position, an encoder does not ignore order in its per-token outputs; it
  carries each token's output along with the token. Pooling those outputs, as a classifier does, then loses order
  entirely.
- **Position in every layer or only the first.** Absolute encodings enter once and must survive the residual stream.
  Relative schemes act in every layer. Mixing an absolute encoding with a relative one rarely helps: Shaw et al. found
  no gain from adding sinusoids back <Cite id="shaw2018" />.
- **Evaluation on length.** A flat perplexity curve beyond the training length can mean the model ignores distant tokens
  rather than uses them.
