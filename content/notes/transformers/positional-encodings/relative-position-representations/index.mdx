<Definition>
In self-attention with relative position representations, the score and output of query position $i$ are
$$
e_{ij} = \frac{\qvec_i^{\tr} (\kvec_j + \avec^K_{r_{ij}})}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad
\ovec_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} \paren{\vvec_j + \avec^V_{r_{ij}}}, \qquad r_{ij} = \max\paren{-K, \min(K, j - i)},
$$
where $\alpha_{ij}$ is the softmax of $e_{ij}$ over $j$ and $\avec^K_r, \avec^V_r \in \reals^{d_k}$ are learned vectors for
each of the $2K + 1$ clipped offsets $r = -K, \dots, K$ <Cite id="shaw2018" />. No position vector is added to the
input.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An absolute scheme labels each token with where it is. A relative scheme labels each pair of tokens with how far apart
they are. For many relations in language, distance is what matters: an adjective usually modifies the noun right after
it, wherever the pair sits in the sentence. Shaw et al. model the input as a fully connected graph whose edges carry a
label for the offset, and let attention read the labels <Cite id="shaw2018" />.

Clipping makes the scheme finite. Offsets beyond $\pm K$ share one vector each. The model learns fine distinctions
between nearby tokens and treats all far tokens alike, which also lets it run on sequences longer than any seen in
training.

## Shaw et al.: offset vectors inside attention

**Scores.** The key at position $j$ is shifted by $\avec^K_{r_{ij}}$ before the dot product. The score splits into a
content term and a position term:

$$
\qvec_i^{\tr} (\kvec_j + \avec^K_{r_{ij}}) = \qvec_i^{\tr} \kvec_j + \qvec_i^{\tr} \avec^K_{r_{ij}}.
$$

The second term depends on the query's content and the offset, not on $i$ or $j$ separately. Shifting both positions by
the same amount leaves every score unchanged.

**Values.** Adding $\avec^V_{r_{ij}}$ to each value lets the output carry offset information forward. The ablation
found it unnecessary for translation: with $\avec^K$ alone the base model scored 25.8 <Gloss name="bleu" />, the same as with both, and
with $\avec^V$ alone 25.3 <Cite id="shaw2018" />.

**Parameters and sharing.** With the vectors shared across heads, each layer has $2(2K+1) d_k$ parameters. With
separate vectors for each of $h$ heads, it has $h$ times as many. Shaw et al. describe sharing across heads as the
memory-saving implementation, but report that the base experiments used $K = 16$ and separate vectors per layer and
head <Cite id="shaw2018" />.

**Computation.** The position term cannot be written as one <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink> of $\Qmat$ with a fixed matrix, because the
vector used depends on the pair $(i, j)$. Shaw et al. form the $n \times n \times d_k$ tensor of offset vectors once, share
it across heads and batch, and compute the term with reshaped matrix products. Training ran 7% fewer steps per second
than the baseline.

**Results.** On WMT 2014 translation, replacing sinusoids with relative representations raised BLEU from 26.5 to 26.8
(base) and 27.9 to 29.2 (big) on English–German, and from 38.2 to 38.7 (base) and 41.2 to 41.5 (big) on English–French.
Adding the absolute sinusoids back gave no further gain. Any clipping distance $K \ge 2$ gave about the same BLEU; with
$K = 0$, which removes all position information, the base model fell to 12.5 <Cite id="shaw2018" />.

## Transformer-XL: a decomposition of the absolute score

Dai et al. derived a relative scheme by rewriting the absolute one <Cite id="dai2019" />. With token embeddings
$\evec_i$, absolute encodings $\pvec_i$ and a single head, written with column vectors so that
$\qvec_i = \Wmat_Q (\evec_i + \pvec_i)$, the absolute score between positions $i$ and $j$ is

$$
(\evec_i + \pvec_i)^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_K (\evec_j + \pvec_j)
= \underbrace{\evec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_K \evec_j}_{(a)}
+ \underbrace{\evec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_K \pvec_j}_{(b)}
+ \underbrace{\pvec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_K \evec_j}_{(c)}
+ \underbrace{\pvec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_K \pvec_j}_{(d)}.
$$

They make three changes:

1. Replace the key's absolute encoding $\pvec_j$ in terms (b) and (d) with a relative encoding $\rvec_{i-j}$, a
   sinusoid of the offset with no learned parameters.
2. Replace the query-side factor $\pvec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr}$ in terms (c) and (d) with learned vectors
   $\uvec^{\tr}$ and $\wvec^{\tr}$ that are the same at every query position.
3. Use separate key projections $\Wmat_{K,E}$ for content and $\Wmat_{K,R}$ for position.

The result is

$$
e_{ij} = \underbrace{\evec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_{K,E} \evec_j}_{\text{content}}
+ \underbrace{\evec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_Q^{\tr} \Wmat_{K,R} \rvec_{i-j}}_{\text{content-dependent position}}
+ \underbrace{\uvec^{\tr} \Wmat_{K,E} \evec_j}_{\text{global content bias}}
+ \underbrace{\wvec^{\tr} \Wmat_{K,R} \rvec_{i-j}}_{\text{global position bias}}.
$$

Shaw et al.'s scheme corresponds to the first two terms with $\Wmat_{K,R} \rvec_{i-j}$ replaced by a learned
$\avec^K$. Term three lets some tokens attract attention from every query. Term four gives each head a preferred
distance, independent of content.

Transformer-XL needed relative positions for its segment recurrence. It caches the hidden states of the previous segment
and lets the current segment attend to them. With absolute encodings, the first token of every segment would carry the
same position label as the first token of the cached segment, and the model could not tell them apart. With relative
encodings only the offset matters, and it is well defined across the boundary. The model reached a perplexity of 18.3
on WikiText-103 <Cite id="dai2019" />.

## Worked example

Take $K = 2$ and a query at position $i = 5$ over keys at positions $j = 0, \dots, 5$. The offsets
$j - i = -5, -4, -3, -2, -1, 0$ clip to $-2, -2, -2, -2, -1, 0$, so the four earliest keys share $\avec^K_{-2}$.

Let the content scores all be 0, to isolate the position term, and let $\qvec_i = (1, 1)$, $d_k = 2$,
$\avec^K_{-2} = (0, 0)$, $\avec^K_{-1} = (1, 0)$ and $\avec^K_0 = (0.5, 0.5)$. The position terms
$\qvec_i^{\tr} \avec^K_r / \sqrt{2}$ are $0, 0, 0, 0, 0.707, 0.707$. The softmax gives weights
$0.124$ to each of the four clipped keys and $0.252$ to each of the two nearest.

Move the same query to position $i = 50$ over keys $0, \dots, 50$. The two nearest keys again score $0.707$ and the 49
earlier keys score 0, since all clip to $-2$. The scores depend only on the offsets. The weights change only because more
keys share the softmax: $e^{0.707} / (49 + 2e^{0.707}) = 0.038$ for each of the nearest two and $0.019$ for each of the
others.

## Assumptions

- The relation between two tokens depends on their offset, not their absolute positions.
- Beyond $K$ tokens the exact distance does not matter to one layer. Stacked layers can combine short offsets to reach
  further.

## Pitfalls

- **Memory.** A naive implementation materialises an $n \times n \times d_k$ tensor per layer. Sharing across heads and
  batch, as in Shaw et al.'s efficient implementation, keeps it to one tensor per layer.
- **Not compatible with fused attention kernels as written.** The position term changes the score of every pair, so an
  attention kernel must support the extra term. Schemes that add a scalar bias per offset, such as the
  <NoteLink to="t5-relative-position-bias">T5 bias</NoteLink>, or that transform queries and keys before the dot
  product, such as <NoteLink to="rotary-position-embedding">RoPE</NoteLink>, are easier to fuse.
- **Sign conventions.** Papers differ on whether the offset is $j - i$ or $i - j$. Mixing conventions between training
  and inference reverses the model's sense of direction.
