<Definition>
**QK normalisation** applies a normalisation $N$, usually layer normalisation or RMSNorm per head, to the queries and keys
before the scores are formed:
$$
\operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{N(\Xmat \Wmat_Q)\, N(\Xmat \Wmat_K)^{\tr}}{\sqrt{d_k}}}
$$
<Cite id="dehghani2023" />. **Logit soft-capping** replaces each logit $s$ by $c \tanh(s / c)$ for a constant $c > 0$,
so every logit lies in $(-c, c)$ <Cite id="gemmateam2024" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

**Logits grow with the weights.** The score between positions $a$ and $b$ is
$\xvec_a^{\tr} \Wmat_Q \Wmat_K^{\tr} \xvec_b / \sqrt{d_k}$. Scaling both $\Wmat_Q$ and $\Wmat_K$ by $c$ scales every score
by $c^2$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $\abs{\qvec^{\tr} \kvec} \le \norm{\qvec} \norm{\kvec}$, and nothing in
the architecture bounds the norms of queries and keys. The inputs are normalised in a pre-norm block, but the
projection weights are not. As their norms grow during training, the largest logits can grow quadratically.

**Large logits stall learning.** Large logit gaps make the softmax nearly one-hot. The Jacobian of the softmax,
$\diag(\alphavec) - \alphavec \alphavec^{\tr}$, then has every entry near 0, so the gradient reaching the queries and keys
vanishes (see <NoteLink to="scaled-dot-product-attention">scaled dot-product attention</NoteLink>). Zhai et al. call
this **attention entropy collapse** and link it to oscillating or diverging loss <Cite id="zhai2023entropy" />.

**Observed at scale.** Training ViT-22B, Dehghani et al. saw the loss diverge after a few thousand steps in models of
about 8 billion parameters. Without normalisation, attention logits grew above 50,000 in magnitude and the attention
weights became one-hot <Cite id="dehghani2023" />. Wortsman et al. reproduced this **attention logit growth** instability
in small models trained at high learning rates, and found QK layer normalisation equally effective there
<Cite id="wortsman2023" />.

## Mechanism

**QK normalisation bounds the logits.** Layer normalisation without its learned gain gives each normalised vector
entries with mean 0 and variance 1, so its norm is exactly $\sqrt{d_k}$. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

$$
\abs{\frac{N(\qvec)^{\tr} N(\kvec)}{\sqrt{d_k}}} \le \frac{\sqrt{d_k} \cdot \sqrt{d_k}}{\sqrt{d_k}} = \sqrt{d_k}.
$$

With learned gains $\gammavec_q, \gammavec_k$ the bound becomes $\sqrt{d_k}\, \max_i \abs{\gamma_{q,i}} \max_i
\abs{\gamma_{k,i}}$. The logits can still grow, but only through a few gain parameters rather than through the full
projection matrices. The same holds for RMSNorm.

**Henry et al.'s variant.** QKNorm divides queries and keys by their $\ell_2$ norms, so the score is the cosine
similarity, and multiplies by a learned scalar $g$ in place of $1/\sqrt{d_k}$. Every logit then lies in $[-g, g]$
<Cite id="henry2020" />.

**Soft-capping bounds the logits directly.** For $\abs{s} \ll c$, $\tanh(s/c) \approx s/c - (s/c)^3/3$, so

$$
c \tanh\paren{\frac{s}{c}} \approx s - \frac{s^3}{3c^2},
$$

which is almost the identity. For $\abs{s} \gg c$ the output approaches $\pm c$. The derivative is
$\operatorname{sech}^2(s/c)$: 1 at $s = 0$, $0.42$ at $s = c$ and near 0 beyond. Large logits therefore stop changing the
output and receive almost no gradient. Soft-capping on output logits goes back to Bello et al.'s pointer networks
<Cite id="bello2016" />.

**Where they are used.** Gemma 2 soft-caps attention logits at $c = 50$ and the final vocabulary logits at $c = 30$
<Cite id="gemmateam2024" />. Gemma 3 replaced the attention soft-capping with QK normalisation
<Cite id="gemmateam2025" />.

## Worked example

**Soft-capping at $c = 50$.** A logit of 1 stays $1.000$. A logit of 10 becomes $50 \tanh(0.2) = 9.869$, with gradient
factor $0.961$. A logit of 60 becomes $41.68$, with gradient factor $0.305$. A logit of $200$ becomes $49.97$, with
gradient factor $0.001$. At $c = 30$, as on Gemma 2's output logits, a logit of 100 becomes $29.92$.

The cap still allows sharp attention: two logits at $+50$ and $-50$ give a weight ratio of $e^{100}$. Soft-capping
removes the extreme logits, not the ability to focus.

**QK normalisation at $d_k = 128$.** Without gains the largest possible logit is $\sqrt{128} = 11.3$. Two keys whose
logits differ by that much get weights in the ratio $e^{11.3} \approx 8 \times 10^4$, enough for a head to focus
almost entirely on one key, but far from the logits above 50,000 reported without normalisation.

## Cost

QK normalisation adds two normalisations per head per token, $O(n d)$ work beside the $O(n^2 d)$ of attention, and
$2 d_k$ gain parameters per layer if gains are shared across heads. Soft-capping adds a $\tanh$ per logit. Both are
cheap in arithmetic, but a per-logit function must be supported inside a fused attention kernel, whereas QK
normalisation happens before the kernel.

## Trade-offs

- **Bounded logits limit sharpness.** QK normalisation without gains caps how peaked a head can be. The learned gains or
  scale restore the range, and the bound on them is what keeps training stable.
- **Interaction with RoPE.** In rotary models the normalisation can be applied before or after the rotation. Rotations
  preserve norms, so the bound holds either way.
- **Other remedies.** Zhai et al. reparametrise every linear layer with spectral normalisation and a learned scale,
  which lower-bounds attention entropy <Cite id="zhai2023entropy" />. Lower learning rates also delay logit growth, at
  the cost of slower training. See <NoteLink to="training-stability-at-scale">training stability at scale</NoteLink>
  for the other instabilities, such as output-logit divergence, and their fixes.
