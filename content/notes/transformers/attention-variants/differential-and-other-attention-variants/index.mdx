<Definition>
**Differential attention** projects the input to two query–key pairs $(\Qmat_1, \Kmat_1)$, $(\Qmat_2, \Kmat_2)$ of width
$d$ and one value matrix $\Vmat$ of width $2d$, and outputs
$$
\operatorname{DiffAttn}(\Xmat) = \paren{\operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat_1 \Kmat_1^{\tr}}{\sqrt{d}}}
- \lambda \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat_2 \Kmat_2^{\tr}}{\sqrt{d}}}} \Vmat,
$$
with a learned scalar $\lambda$ <Cite id="ye2024" />. **Talking-heads attention** inserts learned $h \times h$
<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear maps</NoteLink> across the head dimension of the logits, before the softmax,
and of the weights, after it <Cite id="shazeer2020talking" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A softmax head spreads some weight over every key. When the relevant key is one among thousands, the small weights on
the rest add up. Ye et al. call this attention noise and liken their remedy to a differential amplifier or
noise-cancelling headphones: compute two attention maps and subtract one from the other, so weight that both maps put
on the same irrelevant tokens cancels <Cite id="ye2024" />.

Talking-heads attention starts from a different complaint. In <Gloss name="mha">multi-head attention</Gloss> every head
computes its weights alone, and heads interact only after the output projection. Letting the heads' logits and weights
mix lets each head's weights depend on all the heads' query–key comparisons.

## Differential attention

**Common-mode cancellation.** Write each map as signal plus a shared noise component: $\Amat_1 = \Smat_1 + \Nmat$ and
$\Amat_2 = \Smat_2 + \Nmat$. Then

$$
\Amat_1 - \lambda \Amat_2 = \Smat_1 - \lambda \Smat_2 + (1 - \lambda) \Nmat.
$$

The shared part is scaled by $1 - \lambda$ and vanishes at $\lambda = 1$. Nothing forces the second map to learn only
noise; the training objective decides what each map captures.

**The rows no longer form distributions.** Each row of $\Amat_1 - \lambda \Amat_2$ sums to $1 - \lambda$, and entries
can be negative. The output is a signed combination of values.

**Re-parameterised $\lambda$.** To keep the learning dynamics of $\lambda$ in step with the other weights, it is written
as
$\lambda = \exp(\lambdavec_{q1} \cdot \lambdavec_{k1}) - \exp(\lambdavec_{q2} \cdot \lambdavec_{k2}) + \lambda_{\text{init}}$
with learned vectors $\lambdavec_{\cdot}$ and a constant $\lambda_{\text{init}} = 0.8 - 0.6 \exp(-0.3(l - 1))$ for layer
$l$. This gives $0.2$ in layer 1, $0.67$ in layer 6 and nearly $0.8$ in deep layers <Cite id="ye2024" />.

**Per-head normalisation.** Differential heads produce sparser patterns with statistics that vary between heads. Each
head's output is normalised with <Gloss name="rmsnorm" /> and multiplied by the fixed factor $1 - \lambda_{\text{init}}$
before the output projection. Removing this normalisation raised validation loss in the ablations, from 3.062 to 3.122,
and made training unstable.

**Matched cost.** Each differential head uses two query–key pairs of width $d$ and values of width $2d$. Halving the
number of heads keeps parameters and compute equal to a standard <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>.

**Results.** Ye et al. report that the Differential Transformer matched a Llama-style transformer's language-modelling
loss with about 65% of the parameters or training tokens, across models from 830 million to 13 billion parameters. In
1.4-billion-parameter ablations the validation loss fell from 3.087 to 3.062. Throughput was 9% to 12% lower with their
FlashAttention-based implementation <Cite id="ye2024" />.

## Talking-heads attention

Let $L_{h}[a, b]$ be head $h$'s logit for query $a$ and key $b$. Talking-heads attention computes

$$
L'_{h}[a, b] = \sum_{h'} P^{(l)}_{h' h}\, L_{h'}[a, b], \qquad
W_h = \operatorname{softmax}_b(L'_h), \qquad
W'_{h}[a, b] = \sum_{h'} P^{(w)}_{h' h}\, W_{h'}[a, b],
$$

and uses $W'_h$ to average values. The maps $\Pmat^{(l)}$ and $\Pmat^{(w)}$ add only $2h^2$ parameters per layer but
$O(n^2 h^2)$ multiply-adds, since they act on every entry of the $n \times n$ maps. On <Gloss name="t5" /> with 12
heads, talking-heads attention lowered the pretraining log-perplexity from 1.678 to 1.641 and raised SQuAD F1 from 90.87
to 91.38. Unlike multi-head attention, it kept improving as the number of heads grew to 48 with narrower heads
<Cite id="shazeer2020talking" />.

## Worked example

Take four keys and one query. The first map scores the keys $1, 3, 1, 1$: key 2 is relevant. Its weights are
$\Amat_1 = (0.096, 0.711, 0.096, 0.096)$, so 29% of the weight falls on irrelevant keys. The second map scores all
keys equally, $\Amat_2 = (0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25)$.

- With $\lambda = 0.8$, $\Amat_1 - \lambda \Amat_2 = (-0.104, 0.511, -0.104, -0.104)$. The row sums to
  $0.2 = 1 - \lambda$.
- With $\lambda = 0.385$, the irrelevant entries cancel: $0.096 - 0.385 \times 0.25 = 0.000$, leaving
  $(0, 0.615, 0, 0)$. The head reads key 2 alone, which one softmax could do only with an infinitely large score gap.

## Other variants

- **Softmax with a spare slot.** Adding 1 to the softmax denominator lets a head's weights sum to less than 1, so it
  can attend to nothing <Cite id="miller2023" />; see <NoteLink to="attention-sinks">attention sinks</NoteLink>.
- **Bounded logits.** Normalising queries and keys or soft-capping logits keeps attention from collapsing; see
  <NoteLink to="query-key-normalisation-and-logit-soft-capping">QK normalisation and logit soft-capping</NoteLink>.
- **Cheaper attention.** Variants that change which pairs are scored, or replace the softmax with a kernel so cost grows
  linearly in length, are covered in <NoteLink to="efficient-attention">efficient attention</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="sliding-window-and-sparse-attention">sliding-window and sparse attention</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="linear-attention">linear attention</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- Differential attention assumes the noise in two maps is similar enough to cancel. The benefit is an empirical finding,
  not a guarantee.
- Talking-heads attention assumes heads benefit from sharing comparisons. Its cost grows with $h^2 n^2$, which limits
  it to moderate head counts.

## Pitfalls

- **Signed weights.** Tools that read attention maps as probability distributions do not apply to differential
  attention, whose rows can be negative and sum to $1 - \lambda$.
- **Kernel support.** Talking-heads attention mixes heads between the score and softmax steps, which standard fused
  kernels that process one head at a time cannot do. Differential attention can reuse standard kernels by running two
  attentions and subtracting.
- **Comparisons across papers.** Reported gains are measured against specific baselines, model sizes and training
  budgets. A gain at one scale need not persist at another.
