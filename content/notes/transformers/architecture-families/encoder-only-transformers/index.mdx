<Definition>
  An encoder-only transformer is the encoder half of the <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>: a stack of
  blocks with self-attention and no mask, so the output vector at every position depends on every input token. It maps a
  sequence of $n$ tokens to $n$ vectors in $\reals^d$. It is pre-trained on unlabelled text with a denoising objective
  and then fine-tuned, or used to produce embeddings. BERT is the standard example <Cite id="devlin2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Understanding a word often needs the words after it: in "the bank of the river", "river" decides the sense of "bank".
A left-to-right model builds the vector for "bank" before it has read "river". An encoder builds every vector from the
whole sentence at once. The price is that it has no left-to-right factorisation of the text, so it cannot generate text
one token at a time.

## Why an encoder cannot generate

With no mask, the representation at position $i$ is a function of all $n$ tokens. A prediction read from position $i$
is therefore a conditional $p(x_i \mid \text{the other tokens})$, not a factor of

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}).
$$

Products of the conditionals $p(x_i \mid x_{\setminus i})$ do not in general give the joint distribution, so sampling
from them one at a time does not sample text.

Appending a token changes the representation at every earlier position, because every position attends to the new one.
None of the previous computation can be reused, so there is no <NoteLink to="key-value-cache">key-value (KV)
cache</NoteLink>, and extending a sequence by one token costs a full forward pass.

## BERT and its successors

**BERT** takes one or two text segments, `[CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP]`, adds learned position and segment embeddings, and
trains with two objectives. Masked language modelling predicts 15% of tokens from the rest (see <NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">masked language modelling</NoteLink>). Next sentence prediction classifies
whether segment B followed segment A in the corpus <Cite id="devlin2019" />. The final vector of `[CLS]` feeds sentence
classifiers; the final vector of each token feeds tagging and span extraction.

**RoBERTa** found that removing next sentence prediction "matches or slightly improves downstream task performance",
and that training longer on more data with larger batches and dynamic masking closes most of the gap to later models <Cite id="liu2019" />. Next sentence prediction has since been dropped from most encoders.

### ELECTRA: a loss at every position

Masked language modelling computes a loss at the 15% of positions that were masked. The other 85% of each sequence
contributes only context. ELECTRA replaces the task with **replaced-token detection** <Cite id="clark2020electra" />.

1. A small **generator** $G$, itself a masked <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss>, fills the masked positions with sampled tokens.
2. A **discriminator** $D$, the encoder being trained, reads the corrupted sequence $\hat\xvec$ and predicts for every
   position $t$ whether $\hat x_t$ is the original token.

The discriminator loss is a <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">binary cross-entropy</NoteLink> summed over all $n$ positions:

$$
\Lcal_{\text{Disc}} = -\sum_{t=1}^{n} \Big( \indicator[\hat x_t = x_t] \log D(\hat\xvec, t) +
\indicator[\hat x_t \ne x_t] \log\paren{1 - D(\hat\xvec, t)} \Big).
$$

A sampled token that happens to equal the original counts as original. The two networks are trained together on
$\Lcal_{\text{MLM}}(G) + \lambda\,\Lcal_{\text{Disc}}(D)$ with $\lambda = 50$. The discriminator loss is not propagated
into the generator, since sampling is not differentiable, so the generator is trained by maximum likelihood rather than
adversarially. Generators a quarter to half the size of the discriminator worked best. After pre-training the generator
is discarded <Cite id="clark2020electra" />.

The gain comes from the denser signal. In ELECTRA's ablation, a discriminator trained only on the masked 15% of
positions scored 82.4 on <Gloss name="glue" />, close to BERT's 82.2, while the full model scored 85.0. ELECTRA-Small, trained on one <Gloss name="gpu" />
for four days, outperformed <Gloss name="gpt" />, which used 30 times more compute. ELECTRA-Large performed comparably to RoBERTa and
XLNet with less than a quarter of their pre-training compute <Cite id="clark2020electra" />.

### DeBERTa: separate content and position

BERT adds a <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">position embedding</NoteLink> to each token embedding, so one vector carries both. DeBERTa keeps a content vector
$\Hmat_i$ and a <NoteLink to="relative-position-representations">relative-position embedding</NoteLink> for each pair, and expands the attention score into content-to-content,
content-to-position and position-to-content terms <Cite id="he2021deberta" />. Let $\Pmat \in \reals^{2k \times d}$
hold one learned embedding per relative-distance bucket, shared across layers. With content projections
$\Qmat^c = \Hmat\Wmat_{q,c}$, $\Kmat^c = \Hmat\Wmat_{k,c}$ and projected relative-position embeddings
$\Qmat^r = \Pmat\Wmat_{q,r}$, $\Kmat^r = \Pmat\Wmat_{k,r}$,

$$
\tilde A_{ij} = \underbrace{\qvec^c_i \cdot \kvec^c_j}_{\text{content-to-content}} +
\underbrace{\qvec^c_i \cdot \kvec^r_{\delta(i,j)}}_{\text{content-to-position}} +
\underbrace{\kvec^c_j \cdot \qvec^r_{\delta(j,i)}}_{\text{position-to-content}},
\qquad \Amat = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\tilde\Amat}{\sqrt{3d}}}.
$$

The relative distance is clipped to a window of $2k$ buckets:

$$
\delta(i, j) = \begin{cases} 0 & i - j \le -k, \\ 2k - 1 & i - j \ge k, \\ i - j + k & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}
$$

with $k = 512$ in pre-training. The position-to-position term is dropped because, with relative positions, it adds
little. The divisor $\sqrt{3d}$ replaces $\sqrt d$ because the score is a sum of three dot products
<Cite id="he2021deberta" />.

Relative positions alone cannot tell apart two words in the same local context, as in "a new store opened beside the
new mall" with "store" and "mall" masked. DeBERTa therefore adds absolute positions just before the softmax layer
that predicts masked tokens, which it calls the enhanced mask decoder. A 1.5-billion-parameter DeBERTa scored 89.9
on SuperGLUE against a human baseline of 89.8 <Cite id="he2021deberta" />.

## Worked example

**Training signal per sequence.** For a 512-token sequence, masked language modelling predicts
$\lceil 0.15 \times 512 \rceil = 77$ tokens. Replaced-token detection makes 512 binary decisions. The decisions carry
less information each, a single bit against a choice among tens of thousands of tokens, but every position of every
layer receives a <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> from its own target.

**DeBERTa buckets.** With $k = 4$: $i - j = 2$ gives $\delta = 6$; $i - j = -3$ gives $\delta = 1$; $i - j = 4$ and
$i - j = 9$ both give $\delta = 7$; $i - j = -6$ gives $\delta = 0$. Eight relative-position embeddings cover all
distances.

## Uses

- **Classification and tagging.** A linear layer on the `[CLS]` vector or on each token vector, fine-tuned with the
  encoder; see <NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">transfer learning and fine-tuning</NoteLink>.
- **Embeddings and retrieval.** Pooled encoder outputs serve as <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence
  embeddings</NoteLink> for search and clustering. An encoder computes each document's vector once, independently of
  the query.
- **Comparison with decoders.** <NoteLink to="bert-vs-gpt">BERT and GPT</NoteLink> compares the first encoder and the
  first decoder of the pre-training era at the same size.

## Assumptions

- The task's input is available in full before any output is needed.
- Outputs are one vector per token, or a fixed set of labels, not free text.

## Pitfalls

- **Maximum length.** Learned absolute positions fix the maximum input length, 512 tokens for BERT. Longer documents
  must be split.
- **Pre-training and fine-tuning mismatch.** The `[MASK]` token appears in pre-training but never in fine-tuning
  inputs. BERT's 80/10/10 replacement rule reduces the mismatch; ELECTRA's discriminator never sees `[MASK]` at all.
- **Similarity from raw outputs.** Pre-trained encoder vectors are trained for token prediction, not for comparison
  by <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink>. Retrieval models fine-tune them with a contrastive objective first.
