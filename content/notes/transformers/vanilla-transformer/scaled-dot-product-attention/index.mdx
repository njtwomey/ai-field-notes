<Definition>
Given $n$ queries $\Qmat \in \reals^{n \times d_k}$, $m$ keys $\Kmat \in \reals^{m \times d_k}$ and $m$ values
$\Vmat \in \reals^{m \times d_v}$, scaled dot-product attention returns
$$
\operatorname{Attention}(\Qmat, \Kmat, \Vmat) = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\frac{\Qmat \Kmat^{\tr}}{\sqrt{d_k}}} \Vmat
\in \reals^{n \times d_v},
$$
where the softmax normalises each row of the $n \times m$ score matrix <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Attention is a soft lookup table. Each value $\vvec_j$ is stored under a key $\kvec_j$. A query $\qvec$ is compared
with every key, and the result is an average of all the values, weighted by how well each key matches. A hard lookup
would return the value of the single best key. The softmax makes the lookup differentiable, so queries, keys and values
can all be learned. With the keys as stored patterns, the same computation is one update of a
<NoteLink to="modern-hopfield-network">modern Hopfield network</NoteLink>, an <Gloss name="associative-memory">associative memory</Gloss> with inverse
temperature $1/\sqrt{d_k}$.

The <NoteLink to="dot-product">dot product</NoteLink> measures the match. It is large when $\qvec$ and $\kvec_j$ point
the same way and are long. Row $i$ of the output is

$$
\ovec_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \alpha_{ij} \vvec_j, \qquad
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\qvec_i^{\tr} \kvec_j / \sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_{l=1}^{m} \exp(\qvec_i^{\tr} \kvec_l / \sqrt{d_k})}.
$$

In **self-attention**, queries, keys and values are all linear projections of the same sequence $\Xmat$:
$\Qmat = \Xmat \Wmat_Q$, $\Kmat = \Xmat \Wmat_K$, $\Vmat = \Xmat \Wmat_V$. Every position reads from every other
position. In <NoteLink to="encoder-decoder-cross-attention">cross-attention</NoteLink>, queries come from one sequence
and keys and values from another, as in the decoder of the encoder–decoder <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>.

## Scaling by the square root of the key dimension

Vaswani et al. justify the scaling with a variance argument, given in a footnote of the transformer paper
<Cite id="vaswani2017" />. Suppose the components $q_1, \dots, q_{d_k}$ and $k_1, \dots, k_{d_k}$ of $\qvec$ and
$\kvec$ are mutually <NoteLink to="independence">independent random variables</NoteLink> with mean 0 and variance 1, as at initialisation. Their dot product
$\qvec^{\tr} \kvec = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i$ has mean

$$
\expect\brack{\qvec^{\tr} \kvec} = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \expect[q_i]\, \expect[k_i] = 0.
$$

Because the mean is 0, the variance is the expected square. Expanding the square of the sum gives

$$
\var\paren{\qvec^{\tr} \kvec} = \expect\brack{\paren{\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i}^2}
= \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \expect\brack{q_i^2}\, \expect\brack{k_i^2}
+ \sum_{i \neq j} \expect[q_i]\, \expect[q_j]\, \expect[k_i]\, \expect[k_j].
$$

Independence lets each expectation factor into a product. Every cross term with $i \neq j$ contains the factor
$\expect[q_i] = 0$, so the cross terms vanish. Each diagonal term is $\expect[q_i^2]\,\expect[k_i^2] = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$,
so

$$
\var\paren{\qvec^{\tr} \kvec} = d_k.
$$

The standard deviation of the scores grows as $\sqrt{d_k}$. The scaled score $\qvec^{\tr}\kvec / \sqrt{d_k}$ has
variance $d_k / d_k = 1$ for every $d_k$. The argument uses only independence, zero mean and unit variance. Gaussian
components, $q_i, k_i \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, satisfy these conditions and are the convenient case, but any distribution
with those two moments gives the same result.

If the components have variances $\sigma_q^2$ and $\sigma_k^2$ instead of 1, the same argument gives
$\var(\qvec^{\tr}\kvec) = d_k \sigma_q^2 \sigma_k^2$. The $\sqrt{d_k}$ factor removes the dependence on $d_k$ but not
on $\sigma_q$ and $\sigma_k$. Queries and keys whose norms grow during training still push the scores apart; see
<NoteLink to="query-key-normalisation-and-logit-soft-capping">QK normalisation and logit soft-capping</NoteLink>.

Dividing the scores by a constant $\tau$ is a softmax **temperature**. The scaled form is
$\operatorname{softmax}(\svec / \tau)$ with $\tau = \sqrt{d_k}$. As $\tau \to \infty$ the weights tend to uniform,
$1/m$ each. As $\tau \to 0$ they concentrate on the largest score.

Without the scaling, the spread of the scores depends on the <NoteLink to="multi-head-attention">head dimension</NoteLink>. A wider head gives more widely spread
scores, which push the softmax towards a one-hot vector, so the effective temperature of the softmax would change
with $d_k$. With the scaling, the scale of the scores is independent of $d_k$, and the head dimension can be changed
without retuning the temperature.

Large scores harm training because of the shape of the softmax. With weights $\alphavec = \operatorname{softmax}(\svec)$,
differentiating $\alpha_i = e^{s_i} / \sum_l e^{s_l}$ with the <NoteLink to="derivative">quotient rule</NoteLink> gives

$$
\frac{\partial \alpha_i}{\partial s_j} = \alpha_i (\delta_{ij} - \alpha_j), \qquad
\frac{\partial \alphavec}{\partial \svec} = \diag(\alphavec) - \alphavec \alphavec^{\tr}.
$$

When the scores are spread widely, one weight approaches 1 and the rest approach 0. Every entry
$\alpha_i(\delta_{ij} - \alpha_j)$ then approaches 0: either $\alpha_i \approx 0$, or $\alpha_i \approx 1$ and
$\delta_{ij} - \alpha_j \approx 0$. The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with respect to the scores vanishes, and so does the gradient with
respect to the queries and keys that produced them. Unscaled attention in high dimension starts training in this
saturated regime.

## Worked example

With $d_k = 64$, unscaled scores have standard deviation $\sqrt{64} = 8$. Two keys whose scores differ by one standard
deviation get weights in the ratio $e^{8} \approx 2981$. After scaling, the same two keys differ by 1 in score and
their weights are in the ratio $e^{1} \approx 2.72$. Both keys still contribute, and the gradient reaches both.

## Cost

Forming $\Qmat \Kmat^{\tr}$ costs $O(n m d_k)$ and multiplying the weights by $\Vmat$ costs $O(n m d_v)$. The score
matrix has $nm$ entries. For self-attention over a sequence of length $n$, time and memory grow as $n^2$. All $n^2$
scores are computed with <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix products</NoteLink> and no sequential dependence, so attention parallelises across positions,
unlike a recurrent network.

## Comparison with additive attention

<NoteLink to="bahdanau-attention">Additive attention</NoteLink> scores with a one-hidden-layer network,
$\vvec^{\tr} \tanh(\Wmat_1 \qvec + \Wmat_2 \kvec)$. Dot-product scoring needs no extra parameters and is one matrix
product, which is faster on hardware built for matrix multiplication. Vaswani et al. report that the two perform
similarly for small $d_k$, and that additive attention does better than unscaled dot-product attention for large $d_k$.
The scaling closes that gap <Cite id="vaswani2017" />. The unscaled dot form was used earlier by Luong et al.
<Cite id="luong2015" />.

## Assumptions

- The variance argument assumes independent components with unit variance. After training, queries and keys are
  correlated and not unit-variance; the scaling is a choice for initialisation and a stable default, not an exact
  normalisation.
- Attention treats its keys as a set. Permuting the key–value pairs together leaves the output unchanged. Order must be added
  separately, for example with a <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encoding</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Numerical overflow.** Compute the softmax as $\exp(s_j - \max_l s_l)$ divided by the sum of the same terms. The
  result is unchanged and never overflows; see <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax and log-sum-exp</NoteLink>.
- **Quadratic memory.** Storing the $n \times n$ weights for long sequences exhausts memory. Fused implementations
  compute the output in blocks without storing the full matrix.
- **Masked positions.** Setting a score to $-\infty$ before the softmax gives that position weight exactly 0. This is
  how padding and <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal masking</NoteLink> are implemented.
