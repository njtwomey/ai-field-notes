<Definition>
A **β-VAE** is a <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational autoencoder</NoteLink> trained to maximise

$$
\Lcal_\beta(\thetavec, \phivec; \xvec) = \expect_{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec
\mid \zvec)} - \beta\, \KL\paren{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec)},
$$

with a weight $\beta > 0$ on the <Gloss name="kl" /> term <Cite id="higgins2017" />. $\beta = 1$ is the ordinary VAE. Higgins et al.
used $\beta > 1$ to learn **disentangled** representations, in which each latent coordinate $z_j$ responds to one
generative factor of the data, such as position, scale or rotation, and is insensitive to the others.
</Definition>

## Rate and distortion

Average the two terms over the data distribution and name them:

$$
D = -\expect_{\xvec}\,\expect_{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)}, \qquad
R = \expect_{\xvec}\, \KL\paren{q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec)}.
$$

$D$ is the **distortion**, the expected reconstruction loss. $R$ is the **rate**, measured in nats. $R$ is an upper
bound on the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between $\xvec$ and $\zvec$ under the
encoder, so it limits how much the code can say about the input <Cite id="alemi2018" />. The β-VAE minimises
$D + \beta R$. The ordinary <Gloss name="elbo" /> is $-(D + R)$, and many $(R, D)$ pairs can give the same ELBO: a model with an
ignored latent ($R = 0$) and a model with a highly informative latent can score alike <Cite id="alemi2018" />. The
weight $\beta$ selects a point on the trade-off. Higgins et al. motivated the objective as the Lagrangian of maximising
the reconstruction term subject to the constraint $\KL \le \epsilon$ <Cite id="higgins2017" />.

## The optimal encoder for a given β

For a fixed decoder and a fixed $\xvec$, the best encoder distribution has a closed form. Write
$\tilde{p}_\beta(\zvec \mid \xvec) = p(\zvec)\, p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)^{1/\beta} / Z_\beta(\xvec)$, where
$Z_\beta(\xvec)$ normalises it. Then for any $q$,

$$
\expect_q\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)} - \beta\,\KL(q \,\|\, p) = \beta\,\expect_q\brack{\log
\frac{p(\zvec)\, p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)^{1/\beta}}{q(\zvec)}} = \beta \log Z_\beta(\xvec) - \beta\,\KL\paren{q
\,\|\, \tilde{p}_\beta(\cdot \mid \xvec)}.
$$

The first equality moves $\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)$ into the logarithm as the power $1/\beta$ after
factoring out $\beta$. The second substitutes $Z_\beta\,\tilde{p}_\beta$ for the numerator. The objective is largest
when $q = \tilde{p}_\beta$. The likelihood enters with exponent $1/\beta$. With $\beta > 1$ it is flattened and the
optimal $q$ lies closer to the prior; with $\beta < 1$ it is sharpened and $q$ is narrower than the posterior. At
$\beta = 1$, $\tilde{p}_1$ is the exact posterior.

rises.

## Why a larger β can disentangle

The prior $\Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ has independent coordinates. The KL term penalises each coordinate's deviation from
its prior separately. When the rate budget is tight, the cheapest way to reconstruct well is to spend information on
the few factors that explain most of the reconstruction error, each in its own coordinate, and leave the other
coordinates at the prior. Burgess et al. describe the effect as an information bottleneck: under limited capacity the
model encodes first the factor that gives the largest improvement in reconstruction, then the next
<Cite id="burgess2018" />. They also proposed raising a target capacity $C$ gradually during training, with objective
$\expect_q\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec \mid \zvec)} - \gamma\abs{\KL - C}$, so that factors are added one at a
time <Cite id="burgess2018" />.

Disentanglement is inspected with **latent traversals**: encode an image, vary one coordinate $z_j$ across a range
with the others fixed, and decode. In a disentangled model each traversal changes one attribute.

## Worked example

Take the model in the figure with $w = 2$, $s = 0.5$ and a single data point $x = 1$. The optimal encoder, rate and
expected <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative log-likelihood</NoteLink> (distortion) are:

| $\beta$ | mean of $q$ | <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of $q$ | rate (nats) | distortion (nats) |
| ------- | ----------- | ------------------------- | ----------- | ----------------- |
| 0.25    | 0.492       | 0.124                     | 1.716       | 0.349             |
| 1       | 0.471       | 0.243                     | 1.057       | 0.703             |
| 4       | 0.400       | 0.447                     | 0.485       | 1.906             |
| 16      | 0.250       | 0.707                     | 0.128       | 4.726             |

The ELBO $-(D + R)$ is $-1.760$ at $\beta = 1$, equal to $\log p(x)$. At $\beta = 4$ it is $-2.391$ and at
$\beta = 0.25$ it is $-2.065$: moving $\beta$ either way from 1 lowers the ELBO, since the ELBO is maximised by the
exact posterior.

## Pitfalls

- **Disentanglement is not identifiable without inductive biases.** Locatello et al. proved that, without assumptions
  on the model and the data, unsupervised disentanglement is impossible: infinitely many entangled representations
  give the same marginal distribution of the data. In a large study they found that random seeds and hyperparameters
  mattered more than the choice of method <Cite id="locatello2019" />.
- **Worse reconstructions and samples.** $\beta > 1$ raises distortion. Images become blurrier.
- **β depends on the scale of the likelihood.** The reconstruction term is a sum over pixels, so the same $\beta$
  means different trade-offs for images of different sizes or for different decoder variances.
- **Warm-up is a different use.** Increasing the KL weight from 0 to 1 during training (KL annealing) fights posterior
  collapse. The final model is an ordinary VAE, not a β-VAE.
