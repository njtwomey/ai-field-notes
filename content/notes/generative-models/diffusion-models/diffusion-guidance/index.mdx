<Definition>
Let $\cvec$ be a condition, such as a class label or a text prompt, and $\xvec_t$ a noisy sample at diffusion step
$t$. **Guidance** replaces the conditional score $\nabla_{\xvec_t} \log p_t(\xvec_t \mid \cvec)$ during sampling by

$$
\nabla_{\xvec_t} \log p_t(\xvec_t \mid \cvec) + w\, \nabla_{\xvec_t} \log p_t(\cvec \mid \xvec_t), \qquad w \ge 0.
$$

- **Classifier guidance** computes the second term with a separately trained classifier $p_{\phivec}(\cvec \mid
  \xvec_t, t)$ <Cite id="dhariwal2021" />.
- **Classifier-free guidance** (CFG) computes it from one diffusion network evaluated with and without the condition,
  giving the guided noise prediction <Cite id="ho2022" />

$$
\tilde{\epsilonvec}_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, \cvec) = (1 + w)\,\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, \cvec) -
w\,\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, \varnothing).
$$
</Definition>

## Problem

A conditional <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss> trained on pairs $(\xvec, \cvec)$ learns $p(\xvec \mid \cvec)$, and its samples are
diverse but often only loosely related to the condition. Images of a class may be ambiguous; images for a text prompt
may miss parts of it. <Gloss name="gan" plural /> had a truncation trick that trades diversity for quality. Guidance is the corresponding
control for diffusion models.

## Mechanism

**Classifier guidance.** By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>, $\log p(\xvec \mid \cvec) = \log p(\xvec) + \log p(\cvec \mid \xvec) - \log
p(\cvec)$. The last term does not depend on $\xvec$, so it vanishes under the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>:

$$
\nabla_{\xvec} \log p(\xvec \mid \cvec) = \nabla_{\xvec} \log p(\xvec) + \nabla_{\xvec} \log p(\cvec \mid \xvec).
$$

Scaling the classifier term by $\gamma$ gives $\nabla_{\xvec}\log p(\xvec) + \gamma\nabla_{\xvec}\log p(\cvec \mid
\xvec)$, the score of the tilted density $p(\xvec)\, p(\cvec \mid \xvec)^\gamma$, renormalised. With $\gamma > 1$ the
sampler favours inputs that the classifier assigns to $\cvec$ with high confidence. Dhariwal and Nichol trained a
classifier on noisy images $\xvec_t$ with the timestep as input and added its gradient to the mean of each reverse step
<Cite id="dhariwal2021" />. Starting from a conditional model instead of $p(\xvec)$ gives the form in the definition
with $w = \gamma - 1$.

**Classifier-free guidance.** Bayes' theorem also gives the classifier's gradient in terms of two generative scores,
$\nabla \log p(\cvec \mid \xvec) = \nabla \log p(\xvec \mid \cvec) - \nabla \log p(\xvec)$. Substituting into the
guided score,

$$
\nabla \log p(\xvec \mid \cvec) + w\paren{\nabla \log p(\xvec \mid \cvec) - \nabla \log p(\xvec)} = (1 + w)\nabla \log
p(\xvec \mid \cvec) - w\,\nabla \log p(\xvec).
$$

A noise predictor is a scaled negative score, $\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t) = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla
\log p_t(\xvec_t)$, so the same combination of noise predictions gives $\tilde{\epsilonvec}_{\thetavec}$. Ho and
Salimans train a single network for both terms: during training the condition is replaced by a null token
$\varnothing$ with a fixed probability, 0.1 or 0.2 in their experiments <Cite id="ho2022" />. At sampling time the
network is evaluated twice per step, once with $\cvec$ and once with $\varnothing$, and $\tilde{\epsilonvec}$ replaces
$\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}$ in any sampler, such as <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-implicit-models">DDIM</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

**Tilted densities.** In the figure's model the classes are $\Gauss(-1, 1)$ and $\Gauss(1, 1)$ with equal prior
probability, so $p(A \mid x) = 1/(1 + e^{2x})$. <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">Numerical integration</NoteLink> of the tilted density gives:

| $w$ | mean   | <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> | mean $p(A \mid x)$ |
| --- | ------ | ------------------ | ------------------ |
| 0   | −1.00  | 1.00               | 0.775              |
| 1   | −1.29  | 0.863              | 0.855              |
| 2   | −1.44  | 0.803              | 0.890              |
| 4   | −1.62  | 0.743              | 0.923              |
| 8   | −1.81  | 0.689              | 0.949              |

Each increase in $w$ makes samples more confidently class A and less diverse.

**One guided prediction.** At one step and one pixel, the conditional prediction is $\epsilon_{\thetavec}(x_t, c) =
0.3$ and the unconditional one is $\epsilon_{\thetavec}(x_t, \varnothing) = 0.1$. With $w = 3$, the guided prediction
is $4 \times 0.3 - 3 \times 0.1 = 0.9$. The guided prediction extrapolates past the conditional one, away from the
unconditional one.

## Cost

- Classifier guidance: one diffusion network evaluation and one classifier forward and backward pass per step, plus a
  separately trained noise-aware classifier.
- Classifier-free guidance: two diffusion network evaluations per step, often batched together. No extra model.
  Training is unchanged apart from dropping the condition at random.

## Trade-offs

- **Fidelity against diversity.** Larger $w$ raises per-sample quality and agreement with the condition, and lowers
  diversity. On <Gloss name="imagenet" />, increasing the guidance weight improved the <NoteLink to="inception-score">inception
  score</NoteLink> steadily, while the <NoteLink to="frechet-inception-distance">FID</NoteLink>, which also penalises
  lost diversity, was best at a small weight <Cite id="ho2022" />.
- **Weight conventions differ.** Ho and Salimans write $(1 + w)$ and $w$. Many libraries instead expose a guidance
  scale $\gamma = 1 + w$, so that $\gamma = 1$ means no guidance.
- **Artefacts at large weights.** Very large weights push pixel values outside the data range and give oversaturated,
  high-contrast images.
- **Not exact sampling.** The guided score at each noise level is not the score of the noised tilted distribution, so
  the sampler does not draw exactly from $p(\xvec \mid \cvec)\, p(\cvec \mid \xvec)^w$. The tilted density describes
  the direction of the effect, not the exact output distribution.
- **Classifier gradients can be adversarial.** Gradients of a classifier can increase its confidence through
  imperceptible changes rather than meaningful ones. Classifier-free guidance avoids a separate classifier and has
  largely replaced classifier guidance.
