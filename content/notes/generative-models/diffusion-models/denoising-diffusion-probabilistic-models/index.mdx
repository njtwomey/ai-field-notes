<Definition>
A **denoising diffusion probabilistic model** (DDPM) is a latent-variable model with latents $\xvec_1, \dots,
\xvec_T$ of the same dimension $d$ as the data $\xvec_0$ <Cite id="sohldickstein2015" /> <Cite id="ho2020" />.

- A fixed **forward process** adds noise with a variance schedule $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T \in (0, 1)$:
  $q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_{t-1}) = \Gauss\paren{\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,\xvec_{t-1},\, \beta_t\Imat}$.
- A learned **reverse process** starts from $p(\xvec_T) = \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ and removes noise step by step:
  $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t) = \Gauss\paren{\muvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t),\, \sigma_t^2\Imat}$.

With $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$, Ho et al. train a network
$\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t)$ to predict the added noise by minimising

$$
L_{\text{simple}}(\thetavec) = \expect_{t,\, \xvec_0,\, \epsilonvec}\brack{\norm{\epsilonvec -
\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}\paren{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec,\; t}}^2},
$$

with $t$ uniform on $\set{1, \dots, T}$ and $\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ <Cite id="ho2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Destroying structure is easy: add a little noise, many times. After enough steps any data distribution becomes
indistinguishable from a standard Gaussian. If each step adds only a little noise, the reverse of each step is also
close to Gaussian, so a network needs only to predict the mean of a Gaussian for each small step
<Cite id="sohldickstein2015" />. Generating a sample runs the learned reversal from pure noise back to data.

## The forward process in closed form

Write $\xvec_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,\xvec_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec_t$ with independent $\epsilonvec_t
\sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$. Claim: given $\xvec_0$,

$$
q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_0) = \Gauss\paren{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\Imat}.
$$

**Proof by induction on $t$.** For $t = 1$, $\bar\alpha_1 = \alpha_1$ and the claim is the definition. Suppose it holds
for $t - 1$, so $\xvec_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar\epsilonvec$ with
$\bar\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ independent of $\epsilonvec_t$. Substituting,

$$
\xvec_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}\,\bar\epsilonvec +
\sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec_t.
$$

The sum of two independent zero-mean Gaussians is a zero-mean Gaussian whose variance is the sum of the variances:
$\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + 1 - \alpha_t = 1 - \alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1} = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$. The mean coefficient
is $\sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$. This proves the claim for $t$.

Two consequences follow. First, training can jump to any step in one draw: $\xvec_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 +
\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec$, with no need to simulate the chain. Second, the process is
**variance-preserving**: if $\xvec_0$ has unit variance, so does every $\xvec_t$. Because $\bar\alpha_t \to 0$ as $t$
grows, $q(\xvec_T \mid \xvec_0) \approx \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ for every $\xvec_0$, which matches the reverse
process's starting distribution.

## The reverse step given the clean data

The true reverse step $q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t)$ depends on the whole data distribution and is intractable. Once
$\xvec_0$ is also given, it is a Gaussian (derivation below):

$$
q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t, \xvec_0) = \Gauss\paren{\tilde{\muvec}_t,\, \tilde\beta_t\Imat}, \qquad
\tilde{\muvec}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 -
\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_t, \qquad \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t.
$$

Substituting $\xvec_0 = \paren{\xvec_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec} / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ from the closed
form writes the mean in terms of the noise:

$$
\tilde{\muvec}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\paren{\xvec_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilonvec}.
$$

<Derivation title="The Gaussian posterior q(x_{t−1} | x_t, x_0)">
By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> and the Markov property, $q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t, \xvec_0) \propto q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_{t-1})\,
q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_0)$. Both factors are Gaussian in $\xvec_{t-1}$, and every coordinate behaves the same way, so
work with one coordinate. The log of the product is, up to terms without $x_{t-1}$,

$$
-\frac{(x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1})^2}{2\beta_t} - \frac{(x_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0)^2}{2(1 -
\bar\alpha_{t-1})}.
$$

This is a quadratic in $x_{t-1}$, so the posterior is Gaussian. <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">Completing the
square</NoteLink>, the precision is the coefficient of $-x_{t-1}^2/2$:

$$
\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}} = \frac{\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t(1 -
\bar\alpha_{t-1})} = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})},
$$

using $\alpha_t + \beta_t = 1$ and $\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1} = \bar\alpha_t$. Its inverse is $\tilde\beta_t$. The mean
is $\tilde\beta_t$ times the coefficient of $x_{t-1}$, which is $\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_t/\beta_t +
\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0/(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})$. Multiplying out gives $\tilde\mu_t$.
</Derivation>

## From the evidence lower bound (ELBO) to noise prediction

The model is trained by maximising the <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink> with
$q(\xvec_{1:T} \mid \xvec_0)$ as the variational distribution. Unlike a <Gloss name="vae" />'s encoder, this
distribution has no parameters. The negative ELBO is

$$
L = \expect_q\brack{-\log \frac{p(\xvec_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_{\thetavec}(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t)}{\prod_{t=1}^T
q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_{t-1})}}.
$$

For $t \ge 2$, rewrite each forward factor with Bayes' theorem conditioned on $\xvec_0$, which the Markov property
allows: $q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_{t-1}) = q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_{t-1}, \xvec_0) = q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t, \xvec_0)\,
q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_0) / q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_0)$. The ratios $q(\xvec_t \mid \xvec_0) / q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid
\xvec_0)$ telescope to $q(\xvec_T \mid \xvec_0) / q(\xvec_1 \mid \xvec_0)$, and the loss separates into one term per
step <Cite id="ho2020" />:

$$
L = \underbrace{\KL\paren{q(\xvec_T \mid \xvec_0) \,\|\, p(\xvec_T)}}_{L_T} + \sum_{t=2}^T
\underbrace{\KL\paren{q(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t, \xvec_0) \,\|\, p_{\thetavec}(\xvec_{t-1} \mid \xvec_t)}}_{L_{t-1}}
\underbrace{- \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec_0 \mid \xvec_1)}_{L_0},
$$

each <Gloss name="kl" /> term averaged over $q$. $L_T$ has no parameters and is close to 0. Each $L_{t-1}$ compares two
Gaussians with fixed covariances, $\tilde\beta_t\Imat$ for the posterior and $\sigma_t^2\Imat$ for the model, so it
reduces to the squared distance between their means:
$L_{t-1} = \norm{\tilde{\muvec}_t - \muvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t)}^2 / (2\sigma_t^2)$ plus a constant.

The network could output $\muvec_{\thetavec}$ directly. Ho et al. instead mirror the noise form of $\tilde{\muvec}_t$,

$$
\muvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\paren{\xvec_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t)},
$$

so the difference of means is a multiple of the difference of noises:

$$
L_{t-1} = \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\, \expect_{\xvec_0, \epsilonvec}\brack{\norm{\epsilonvec
- \epsilonvec_{\thetavec}\paren{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec,\; t}}^2}.
$$

$L_{\text{simple}}$ drops the weight in front and samples $t$ uniformly. Relative to the true bound, this down-weights
the small-$t$ terms, where the noise is slight and denoising is easy. With $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ and the linear
schedule below, the dropped weight is $0.50$ at $t = 1$, $0.27$ at $t = 2$, and between $0.005$ and $0.010$ from
$t = 100$ on. Ho et al. found that the unweighted loss gave better samples than the true bound <Cite id="ho2020" />.

Predicting $\epsilonvec$ is equivalent to predicting the clean data, $\hat{\xvec}_0 = \paren{\xvec_t - \sqrt{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}} / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, and to estimating the score of the noised data,
$\nabla \log q(\xvec_t) \approx -\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t) / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$. The last equivalence
links DDPMs to <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score-based generative models</NoteLink>.

## Algorithms

**Training.** Repeat: draw $\xvec_0$ from the data, $t \sim \Unif\set{1, \dots, T}$ and $\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros,
\Imat)$; take a <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> step on $\norm{\epsilonvec - \epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec, t)}^2$.

**Sampling.** Draw $\xvec_T \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$. For $t = T, \dots, 1$, draw $\zvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros,
\Imat)$ if $t > 1$, else set $\zvec = \zeros$, and set

$$
\xvec_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\paren{\xvec_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\,
\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t)} + \sigma_t\zvec.
$$

Ho et al. used $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ and found $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ gave similar results <Cite id="ho2020" />.

The network is a <Gloss name="u-net" /> that receives $t$ through a sinusoidal embedding, as in the
<NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encoding</NoteLink> of transformers.

## Worked example

Ho et al. used $T = 1000$ and a schedule rising linearly from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$
<Cite id="ho2020" />. The cumulative products and the <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratio</Gloss> $\mathrm{SNR}_t = \bar\alpha_t / (1 -
\bar\alpha_t)$ are:

| $t$  | $\bar\alpha_t$        | $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ (signal) | $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ (noise) | $\mathrm{SNR}_t$      |
| ---- | --------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| 1    | 0.9999                | 1.000                          | 0.010                             | 9999                  |
| 100  | 0.897                 | 0.947                          | 0.321                             | 8.71                  |
| 250  | 0.524                 | 0.724                          | 0.690                             | 1.10                  |
| 500  | 0.0786                | 0.280                          | 0.960                             | 0.085                 |
| 1000 | $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$  | 0.0064                         | 1.000                             | $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$  |

Signal and noise are about equal near $t = 250$. By $t = 500$ the noise dominates, and at $t = 1000$ a pixel keeps
$0.6\%$ of its original amplitude. With $\xvec_0 = 0.7$ and $\epsilon = -1.3$ at $t = 250$, the noised value is
$0.724 \times 0.7 + 0.690 \times (-1.3) = -0.390$.

## Pitfalls

- **Slow sampling.** Sampling runs the network $T$ times, e.g. 1000 evaluations per image.
  <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-implicit-models">Denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM)</NoteLink> and
  <Gloss name="ode" /> solvers reduce this to tens of steps.
- **The linear schedule destroys low-resolution images too early.** For $64 \times 64$ images, much of the second half
  of the linear schedule is nearly pure noise. Nichol and Dhariwal proposed a cosine schedule for $\bar\alpha_t$ that
  adds noise more evenly and also learned the reverse variances <Cite id="nichol2021" />.
- **$L_{\text{simple}}$ is not the likelihood.** It gives good samples but poor log-likelihoods. Reporting likelihood
  needs the weighted bound.
- **$\bar\alpha_T$ is not exactly 0.** A small amount of signal remains at $t = T$. Sampling starts from pure noise,
  which the network never saw in training.
