<Definition>
The **score** of a density $p$ on $\reals^d$ is the gradient of its logarithm, $\svec(\xvec) = \nabla_{\xvec} \log
p(\xvec)$, a vector field on $\reals^d$. A **score-based generative model** trains a network
$\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \sigma)$ to approximate the score of the data distribution convolved with Gaussian noise of
scale $\sigma$, for a range of scales, and generates samples by moving noise along the learned score field
<Cite id="song2019" /> <Cite id="song2021" />. The score here is a gradient with respect to the data $\xvec$. It is not
the gradient with respect to parameters used by the <NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function
estimator</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Why learn the score

An energy-based density $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = \exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec)) / Z(\thetavec)$ has a normalising constant
$Z(\thetavec) = \int \exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec))\, d\xvec$ that is intractable in high dimensions. Maximum likelihood
needs it. The score does not:

$$
\nabla_{\xvec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = -\nabla_{\xvec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec) - \underbrace{\nabla_{\xvec} \log
Z(\thetavec)}_{=\,0}.
$$

The score also suffices for sampling. Langevin dynamics draws samples from $p$ using only $\nabla \log p$: with step
size $\eta > 0$ and $\zvec_k \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$,

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k + \frac{\eta}{2}\, \nabla_{\xvec} \log p(\xvec_k) + \sqrt{\eta}\, \zvec_k.
$$

As $\eta \to 0$ and the number of steps grows, the distribution of $\xvec_k$ converges to $p$ under regularity
conditions. The gradient term climbs towards high density; the noise keeps the chain from collapsing onto a mode.
<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink> has its own note, which derives why $p$ is stationary and
how the step size biases the chain.

## Score matching

The obvious objective, $\tfrac12\expect_p\norm{\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec) - \nabla \log p(\xvec)}^2$, contains the
unknown score of the data. Hyvärinen showed that it equals, up to a constant that does not depend on $\thetavec$,

$$
J(\thetavec) = \expect_{p}\brack{\tfrac12\norm{\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}^2 + \trace\paren{\nabla_{\xvec}
\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}},
$$

which needs only samples from $p$ <Cite id="hyvarinen2005" />. The proof in one dimension: expanding the square gives
$\tfrac12 s_{\thetavec}^2$, a term free of $\thetavec$, and the cross term $-\expect_p\brack{s_{\thetavec}(x)\,(\log
p)'(x)}$. Since $p\,(\log p)' = p'$, <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink> moves the
derivative onto $s_{\thetavec}$:

$$
-\int s_{\thetavec}(x)\, p'(x)\, dx = -\brack{s_{\thetavec}\, p}_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int s_{\thetavec}'(x)\, p(x)\, dx
= \expect_p\brack{s_{\thetavec}'(x)},
$$

assuming $s_{\thetavec}(x)p(x) \to 0$ in the tails. In $d$ dimensions the same step applies to each coordinate and the
derivatives sum to the trace. The trace of the Jacobian needs $d$ backward passes, which is too expensive for images.

## Denoising score matching

Perturb each data point with Gaussian noise, $\tilde{\xvec} = \xvec + \sigma\epsilonvec$. The perturbed density is
$q_\sigma(\tilde{\xvec}) = \int p(\xvec)\, \Gauss(\tilde{\xvec}; \xvec, \sigma^2\Imat)\, d\xvec$. Vincent showed that
matching its score is equivalent, up to a constant, to regressing onto the score of the known noise kernel
<Cite id="vincent2011" />:

$$
J_{\text{DSM}}(\thetavec) = \tfrac12\, \expect_{\xvec,\, \tilde{\xvec}}\brack{\norm{\svec_{\thetavec}(\tilde{\xvec}) -
\nabla_{\tilde{\xvec}} \log \Gauss(\tilde{\xvec}; \xvec, \sigma^2\Imat)}^2} = \tfrac12\, \expect_{\xvec,
\epsilonvec}\brack{\norm{\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec + \sigma\epsilonvec) + \frac{\epsilonvec}{\sigma}}^2}.
$$

Everything in this loss is computable. The network learns to point from a noisy sample back towards the clean one,
scaled by $1/\sigma$, which is the task of a denoising autoencoder. Because the optimum is $\svec_{\thetavec} = \nabla
\log q_\sigma$, the learned field is the score of the smoothed density, not of $p$ itself; the two agree as
$\sigma \to 0$.

<Derivation title="Why denoising score matching targets the smoothed score">
Expand $\tfrac12\expect_{q_\sigma}\norm{\svec_{\thetavec}(\tilde{\xvec}) - \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde{\xvec})}^2$.
The only term that couples $\thetavec$ and the unknown score is $-\expect_{q_\sigma}\brack{\svec_{\thetavec}^\top
\nabla \log q_\sigma}$. Using $q_\sigma \nabla \log q_\sigma = \nabla q_\sigma$ and differentiating under the integral,

$$
\int \svec_{\thetavec}(\tilde{\xvec})^\top \nabla q_\sigma(\tilde{\xvec})\, d\tilde{\xvec} = \iint
\svec_{\thetavec}(\tilde{\xvec})^\top \nabla_{\tilde{\xvec}} \log \Gauss(\tilde{\xvec}; \xvec, \sigma^2\Imat)\;
\Gauss(\tilde{\xvec}; \xvec, \sigma^2\Imat)\, p(\xvec)\, d\xvec\, d\tilde{\xvec}.
$$

The right side is the cross term of $J_{\text{DSM}}$. The squared-norm terms in $\svec_{\thetavec}$ are identical in
both objectives, and the remaining terms do not depend on $\thetavec$. So the two objectives differ by a constant. The
kernel score is $\nabla_{\tilde{\xvec}} \log \Gauss(\tilde{\xvec}; \xvec, \sigma^2\Imat) = -(\tilde{\xvec} -
\xvec)/\sigma^2 = -\epsilonvec/\sigma$.
</Derivation>

## Many noise levels and annealed Langevin dynamics

A score learned at a single small $\sigma$ fails for sampling, for two reasons identified by Song and Ermon
<Cite id="song2019" />. In regions of low data density there are few training points, so the score estimate is poor
exactly where a chain started from noise begins. And Langevin dynamics mixes slowly between separated modes, so it
recovers their relative weights badly.

Large noise fills the space and connects the modes, but blurs the data. The noise-conditional score network (NCSN)
therefore trains one network $\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \sigma)$ on a geometric sequence $\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 > \dots
> \sigma_L$, with the denoising loss at level $\sigma$ weighted by $\sigma^2$ so that all levels contribute similarly.
**Annealed Langevin dynamics** samples by running a few Langevin steps at $\sigma_1$, using the result to start the
chain at $\sigma_2$, and so on down to $\sigma_L$ <Cite id="song2019" />.

In the figure's score view, the field at small $t$ has steep, separate basins around each mode and is nearly flat in
the gaps between them. At large $t$ it is close to the straight line $-x$, the score of $\Gauss(0, 1)$, and a sampler
started anywhere is pulled towards the centre.

## The stochastic differential equation view

Song et al. took the number of noise levels to infinity <Cite id="song2021" />. A **forward SDE** diffuses data into
noise over $t \in [0, T]$:

$$
d\xvec = \fvec(\xvec, t)\, dt + g(t)\, d\wvec,
$$

where $\wvec$ is a standard Wiener process, $\fvec$ the drift and $g$ the diffusion coefficient. Write $p_t$ for the
marginal density of $\xvec_t$. Anderson showed that the process run backwards in time is also a diffusion
<Cite id="anderson1982" />:

$$
d\xvec = \brack{\fvec(\xvec, t) - g(t)^2\, \nabla_{\xvec} \log p_t(\xvec)}\, dt + g(t)\, d\bar{\wvec},
$$

where $dt$ is a negative time step and $\bar{\wvec}$ a Wiener process in reverse time. The only unknown is the score of
each marginal. A network $\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, t)$ is trained by denoising score matching at random $t$, and a
numerical SDE solver integrates the reverse SDE from $\xvec_T \sim p_T$ to $t = 0$.

Two families of forward SDEs recover the earlier models:

- **Variance preserving (VP):** $d\xvec = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,\xvec\, dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\, d\wvec$. It is the
  continuous limit of the DDPM step $\xvec_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,\xvec_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\zvec$, since
  $\sqrt{1 - \beta} \approx 1 - \beta/2$ for small $\beta$.
- **Variance exploding (VE):** $d\xvec = \sqrt{d\sigma^2(t)/dt}\; d\wvec$. It is the continuous limit of NCSN, whose
  noise scale grows without a shrinking drift.

In the VP case the <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-probabilistic-models">DDPM</NoteLink> noise predictor and the score
are the same network up to scale: $\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t) = -\epsilonvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec_t, t) / \sqrt{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}$.

**Probability flow ODE.** The deterministic ODE

$$
\frac{d\xvec}{dt} = \fvec(\xvec, t) - \tfrac12\, g(t)^2\, \nabla_{\xvec} \log p_t(\xvec)
$$

has the same marginals $p_t$ as the SDE <Cite id="song2021" />. It maps each noise sample to one data sample and back,
so it defines a continuous normalising flow: exact log-likelihoods follow from the instantaneous
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink>, and high-order ODE solvers need far fewer steps than
the SDE. <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-implicit-models">DDIM</NoteLink> is a discretisation of this ODE.

<Derivation title="Why the reverse SDE and the probability flow ODE have the right marginals">
The marginals of the forward SDE obey the Fokker–Planck equation

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot (\fvec\, p_t) + \tfrac12 g^2\, \Delta p_t.
$$

Since $\Delta p_t = \nabla \cdot (p_t \nabla \log p_t)$, the diffusion term can be moved into the drift:

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot \brack{\paren{\fvec - \tfrac12 g^2 \nabla \log p_t}\, p_t}.
$$

This is the continuity equation of the ODE $d\xvec/dt = \fvec - \tfrac12 g^2 \nabla \log p_t$, which proves the
probability flow claim. For the reverse SDE, write the same equation as
$\partial_t p_t = -\nabla \cdot \brack{\paren{\fvec - g^2 \nabla \log p_t}\, p_t} - \tfrac12 g^2 \Delta p_t$. In the
reversed time $\tau = T - t$ this reads $\partial_\tau p = -\nabla \cdot \brack{-\paren{\fvec - g^2\nabla \log p}\, p} +
\tfrac12 g^2 \Delta p$, the Fokker–Planck equation of an SDE with drift $-(\fvec - g^2 \nabla \log p)$ and diffusion
$g$ in $\tau$. Converting back to $t$ gives the reverse SDE. Anderson's result is stronger: it matches whole paths, not
only marginals <Cite id="anderson1982" />.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

For a Gaussian $p = \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ in one dimension the score is $s(x) = -(x - \mu)/\sigma^2$: it points
towards the mean with strength proportional to the distance.

- **Langevin step.** For $p = \Gauss(0, 1)$, start at $x = 2$ with $\eta = 0.1$ and noise draw $z = 0.5$. The update
  is $x = 2 + 0.05 \times (-2) + \sqrt{0.1} \times 0.5 = 2 - 0.1 + 0.158 = 2.058$. The drift moves the sample towards
  the mode; this noise draw happens to push it further out.
- **Denoising target.** With $\sigma = 0.5$, a clean point $x = 1$ and $\epsilon = 0.4$, the noisy input is
  $\tilde{x} = 1.2$ and the regression target is $-\epsilon/\sigma = -0.8$.
- **Score of the smoothed density.** If the data are $\Gauss(0, 1)$, the perturbed density is $\Gauss(0, 1 + \sigma^2)
  = \Gauss(0, 1.25)$ and its score at $\tilde{x} = 1.2$ is $-1.2/1.25 = -0.96$. The single-sample target $-0.8$
  differs from it; the average of the target over all clean points consistent with $\tilde{x} = 1.2$ equals $-0.96$.

## Pitfalls

- **The trace term does not scale.** Exact score matching needs the Jacobian trace; use denoising or sliced score
  matching for high-dimensional data.
- **Noise-level design matters.** The largest $\sigma$ must be comparable to the largest distance between data points,
  or chains stay trapped. Karras et al. showed that noise schedules, loss weights and samplers can be chosen
  separately, and tuned them jointly for large gains in quality per network evaluation <Cite id="karras2022" />.
- **Learned scores are not conservative.** A network output need not be the gradient of any function. Sampling works
  regardless, but the energy interpretation is only approximate.
