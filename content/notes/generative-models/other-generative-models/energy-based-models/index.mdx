<Definition>
An **energy-based model** (EBM) assigns every input $\xvec \in \reals^d$ a scalar **energy** $E_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$,
computed by any function with parameters $\thetavec$, typically a neural network, and defines the density

$$
p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = \frac{\exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec))}{Z_{\thetavec}}, \qquad Z_{\thetavec} = \int
\exp(-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec))\, d\xvec.
$$

Low energy means high probability. The normalising constant $Z_{\thetavec}$ is an integral over the whole input space
and is intractable for images or other high-dimensional data. Training and sampling therefore use only energy
differences and energy <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> <Cite id="lecun2006" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Most generative models constrain their architecture so that the density is normalised by construction: an
<Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss> multiplies normalised conditionals, and a <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flow</NoteLink> must be invertible. An EBM drops the
constraint. Any network that outputs a number defines a valid density, provided $Z_{\thetavec}$ is finite. The cost
moves to training and sampling, which must work without knowing $Z_{\thetavec}$.

The energy is a landscape over input space. Training carves valleys where the data lie and raises ridges elsewhere.
Sampling rolls points downhill with noise. Classical models are special cases: a
<NoteLink to="markov-random-field">Markov random field</NoteLink> has an energy that is a sum of clique potentials, and
an <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> has an energy linear in its parameters,
$E_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = -\thetavec^\top \phivec(\xvec)$.

## Details

### The maximum-likelihood gradient

The log-likelihood of one point is $\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec) = -E_{\thetavec}(\xvec) - \log Z_{\thetavec}$. The
gradient of the first term is available by backpropagation. For the second, differentiate under the integral:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} \log Z_{\thetavec} = \frac{1}{Z_{\thetavec}} \int \nabla_{\thetavec}\, e^{-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec')}\,
d\xvec' = -\int \frac{e^{-E_{\thetavec}(\xvec')}}{Z_{\thetavec}}\, \nabla_{\thetavec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec')\, d\xvec' =
-\expect_{\xvec' \sim p_{\thetavec}}\brack{\nabla_{\thetavec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec')}.
$$

Averaging over the data distribution $p_{\text{data}}$ gives the gradient of the expected log-likelihood:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}\, \expect_{p_{\text{data}}}\brack{\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)} = \underbrace{-\expect_{\xvec \sim
p_{\text{data}}}\brack{\nabla_{\thetavec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}}_{\text{positive phase}} + \underbrace{\expect_{\xvec'
\sim p_{\thetavec}}\brack{\nabla_{\thetavec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec')}}_{\text{negative phase}}.
$$

A step along this gradient lowers the energy at data points (the positive phase) and raises it at points the model
currently finds likely (the negative phase). The gradient is zero when the model's samples and the data give the same
expected $\nabla_{\thetavec} E$. For an exponential family this is moment matching: $\expect_{p_{\thetavec}}[\phivec]
= \expect_{p_{\text{data}}}[\phivec]$. In code the gradient is obtained from the surrogate loss

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec) = \frac1B \sum_{b=1}^B E_{\thetavec}(\xvec_b^+) - \frac1B \sum_{b=1}^B
E_{\thetavec}(\operatorname{sg}(\xvec_b^-)),
$$

with data points $\xvec^+$, model samples $\xvec^-$ and a stop-gradient $\operatorname{sg}$, because the samples are
treated as fixed inputs <Cite id="du2019" />.

### Sampling the negative phase with Langevin dynamics

The negative phase needs samples from $p_{\thetavec}$. The score of an EBM is $\nabla_{\xvec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)
= -\nabla_{\xvec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$, free of $Z_{\thetavec}$, and one backward pass computes it. So
<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink> applies directly:

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - h\, \nabla_{\xvec} E_{\thetavec}(\xvec_k) + \sqrt{2h}\, \xivec_k, \qquad \xivec_k \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \eye).
$$

Each training step therefore contains an inner sampling loop of $K$ Langevin steps, each costing about one forward and
backward pass. Running every chain to convergence at every parameter update is unaffordable, and the practical methods
differ in how they cut this cost.

### Contrastive divergence

Hinton's **contrastive divergence** (CD-$k$) starts each negative chain at a training point and runs only $k$ steps,
often one <Cite id="hinton2002poe" />. A model that already fits the data leaves data points almost unchanged after $k$
steps, so the two phases cancel. A model that does not fit moves them, and the gradient pushes the energy to correct
that local movement. CD-$k$ is biased: it approximately follows the gradient of
$\KL(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_{\thetavec}) - \KL(p_k \,\|\, p_{\thetavec})$, where $p_k$ is the distribution after $k$
steps, not of the likelihood. Its chains never travel far from the data, so the model can keep spurious low-energy
regions away from the data that the chains never visit. It was introduced for products of experts and restricted
Boltzmann machines, where the $k$ steps are block Gibbs sweeps rather than Langevin steps.

### Persistent contrastive divergence

**Persistent contrastive divergence** (PCD) keeps one set of chains for the whole of training. Each parameter update
continues the chains from where the previous update left them <Cite id="tieleman2008" />. The parameters change little
per update, so the chains stay close to equilibrium for the current model, and a few steps per update accumulate into
long runs. PCD explores far from the data and removes spurious modes that CD misses. It fails when the <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> is
large enough that the model changes faster than the chains can follow.

### Short-run MCMC and its heuristics

Training EBMs on images revived these ideas with Langevin dynamics on convolutional energy networks. Two schools
emerged, and both use heuristics that the theory does not require.

- **Short-run, non-persistent chains.** Nijkamp et al. start every chain from the same noise distribution and run a
  fixed number $K$ of Langevin steps, never to convergence <Cite id="nijkamp2019" />. The learned energy together with
  its $K$-step sampler is a good generator, whose samples match the data, but the energy is then not a faithful density
  model: running the chains longer drifts away from the data.
- **Replay buffers.** Du and Mordatch keep a buffer of past negative samples. Each new chain starts from a buffer
  sample with probability 0.95 and from uniform noise otherwise <Cite id="du2019" />. The buffer acts like PCD's
  persistent chains, and the 5% of fresh starts keep the samples diverse.
- **Reduced noise.** Implementations use a gradient step and a noise scale chosen separately, typically with noise far
  below $\sqrt{2h}$. A Langevin step with gradient coefficient $c$ and noise <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma$ samples
  $p_{\thetavec}^{1/T}$ at temperature $T = \sigma^2/(2c)$, a sharpened version of the model. The chains reach
  low-energy regions in fewer steps at the price of an inexact negative phase.
- **Regularisation.** Spectral normalisation limits how sharply the energy can change, which keeps Langevin steps
  stable. A weak penalty on the squared energies of positive and negative samples stops the energy values from
  drifting to large magnitudes while their difference is unchanged <Cite id="du2019" />.

In the figure, persistent chains with the correct noise approach the maximum-likelihood fit. Short-run chains from
noise produce samples that match the data while the density itself fits worse. Reduced noise produces samples that sit
too close to the energy minima, and the model compensates by spreading its density out.

### Score matching: training without samples

<NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">Score matching</NoteLink> avoids the negative phase altogether. It fits the
model score $-\nabla_{\xvec} E_{\thetavec}$ to the score of the data by minimising
$\expect_{p_{\text{data}}}\brack{\tfrac12\norm{\nabla_{\xvec} E_{\thetavec}}^2 - \trace\paren{\nabla_{\xvec}^2
E_{\thetavec}}}$, which contains neither $Z_{\thetavec}$ nor samples from the model <Cite id="hyvarinen2005" />. The
trace of the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> is expensive in high dimensions; denoising score matching replaces it with a regression onto the
score of a noise kernel. <NoteLink to="diffusion-models-as-stochastic-differential-equations">Score-based diffusion</NoteLink> models take this route and learn the score directly, without an explicit
energy <Cite id="song2019" />. **Noise-contrastive estimation** is a third route: it trains a classifier to separate
data from samples of a known noise distribution, and the classifier's logit recovers the energy with
$\log Z_{\thetavec}$ as a learned scalar <Cite id="gutmann2010" />.

## Worked example: one gradient step for a Gaussian energy

Take the one-dimensional energy $E_\theta(x) = \theta x^2/2$ with $\theta > 0$. The model is $\Gauss(0, 1/\theta)$ and
$\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x) = x^2/2$. Suppose the data have mean 0 and variance 2, and the current parameter is
$\theta = 1$.

- **Positive phase.** $-\expect_{p_{\text{data}}}[x^2/2] = -2/2 = -1$.
- **Negative phase.** $\expect_{p_\theta}[x^2/2] = 1/(2\theta) = 0.5$.
- **Gradient.** $-1 + 0.5 = -0.5$. Gradient ascent decreases $\theta$, which widens the model towards the data.

The gradient is zero when $1/(2\theta) = 1$, at $\theta = 1/2$: the model variance $1/\theta$ equals the data variance
of 2. A Langevin sampler with step $h$ would instead estimate the negative phase from a stationary variance of
$1/(\theta(1 - h\theta/2))$, the step-size bias of the unadjusted
<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin algorithm</NoteLink> on a Gaussian. With $h = 0.5$ at
$\theta = 1/2$ the estimated variance is $2/(1 - 0.125) = 2.29$. Training settles where the sampler's variance, not the
model's, equals 2: at $\theta = 2 - \sqrt2 = 0.586$, a model variance of $1.71$, narrower than the data.

## Pitfalls

- **No likelihood to monitor.** $Z_{\thetavec}$ is unknown, so the training log-likelihood cannot be reported.
  Progress is judged by samples, which come from an approximate sampler.
- **Instability.** When the energies of samples and data separate by a large margin, gradients grow and training
  diverges. Large EBMs often need restarts, smaller learning rates or more sampling steps.
- **Normalisability.** Nothing in an arbitrary network guarantees $Z_{\thetavec} < \infty$. An energy that decreases
  without bound in some direction lets Langevin chains run off to infinity.
- **Mixing between modes.** Langevin chains cross low-density regions slowly, so the negative phase can miss modes and
  misjudge their relative weights.
