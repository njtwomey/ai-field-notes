<Definition>
A **perceptron** maps an input $\xvec \in \reals^d$ to a label in $\set{-1, +1}$ by thresholding a linear score:
$\hat y = \sgn(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)$, with weights $\wvec \in \reals^d$ and bias $b \in \reals$. The **perceptron
learning rule** visits the training pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ one at a time. When a pair is misclassified, the rule adds
$y_i\xvec_i$ to $\wvec$ and $y_i$ to $b$; otherwise it changes nothing <Cite id="rosenblatt1958" />.
</Definition>

## The model

Rosenblatt introduced the perceptron in 1958 as a model of a neuron that fires when a weighted sum of its inputs
exceeds a threshold <Cite id="rosenblatt1958" />. The decision boundary $\wvec^\top\xvec + b = 0$ is a hyperplane. The
weight vector $\wvec$ is normal to that hyperplane and points towards the region labelled $+1$.

The bias is absorbed by appending a constant 1 to every input. With $\tilde\xvec = (\xvec, 1)$ and
$\tilde\wvec = (\wvec, b)$, the score is $\tilde\wvec^\top\tilde\xvec$. The rest of this note uses the augmented form
and drops the tildes.

A pair $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ is classified correctly when $y_i\,\wvec^\top\xvec_i > 0$. The product $y_i\,\wvec^\top\xvec_i$
is positive on the correct side of the boundary and negative on the wrong side.

## The learning rule

Start from $\wvec_0 = \zeros$. Cycle through the training set. At each pair with $y_i\,\wvec^\top\xvec_i \le 0$,
update

$$
\wvec \leftarrow \wvec + y_i\xvec_i.
$$

The update raises the score of the misclassified pair by $\norm{\xvec_i}^2$ in the direction of its label:
$y_i(\wvec + y_i\xvec_i)^\top\xvec_i = y_i\,\wvec^\top\xvec_i + \norm{\xvec_i}^2$. One update does not always fix the
pair, and it can break pairs that were correct. The convergence theorem shows that on separable data this does not
continue forever.

The rule is <NoteLink to="sgd-for-linear-models">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink> with step size 1 on the
**perceptron loss** $\ell(\wvec) = \max(0, -y\,\wvec^\top\xvec)$. The gradient of that loss is $-y\xvec$ on a mistake
and $\zeros$ otherwise. Scaling the step size scales every $\wvec_k$ by the same factor, so the sequence of predictions
does not depend on it.

## Convergence theorem

The data are **linearly separable with margin** $\gamma > 0$ if some unit vector $\uvec$ satisfies
$y_i\,\uvec^\top\xvec_i \ge \gamma$ for every $i$. Let $R = \max_i \norm{\xvec_i}$.

**Theorem** (Novikoff). On data separable with margin $\gamma$, the perceptron makes at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ updates,
in any order of presentation <Cite id="novikoff1962" />.

The proof tracks two quantities after the $k$-th update, $\wvec_k = \wvec_{k-1} + y\xvec$ for some misclassified pair
$(\xvec, y)$.

1. **Alignment with $\uvec$ grows linearly.** $\uvec^\top\wvec_k = \uvec^\top\wvec_{k-1} + y\,\uvec^\top\xvec \ge
   \uvec^\top\wvec_{k-1} + \gamma$. From $\wvec_0 = \zeros$, induction gives $\uvec^\top\wvec_k \ge k\gamma$.
2. **Length grows at most like $\sqrt{k}$.** $\norm{\wvec_k}^2 = \norm{\wvec_{k-1}}^2 + 2y\,\wvec_{k-1}^\top\xvec +
   \norm{\xvec}^2$. The middle term is at most 0, because the pair was misclassified. The last term is at most $R^2$.
   Induction gives $\norm{\wvec_k}^2 \le kR^2$.

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality with $\norm{\uvec} = 1$ gives $\uvec^\top\wvec_k \le \norm{\wvec_k}$. Combining the two
bounds:

$$
k\gamma \le \uvec^\top\wvec_k \le \norm{\wvec_k} \le \sqrt{k}\,R
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k \le \paren{\frac{R}{\gamma}}^2.
$$

The bound depends on neither the dimension $d$ nor the number of training pairs. It depends only on the ratio of the
data's radius to its margin. After at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ updates no pair is misclassified, and a full pass without an
update ends training.

## Worked example: logical AND

The four inputs of AND, with labels $-1$ for false and $+1$ for true, augmented with a constant 1, are

$$
\xvec_1 = (0, 0, 1),\ y_1 = -1; \quad
\xvec_2 = (0, 1, 1),\ y_2 = -1; \quad
\xvec_3 = (1, 0, 1),\ y_3 = -1; \quad
\xvec_4 = (1, 1, 1),\ y_4 = +1.
$$

**First pass.** From $\wvec_0 = (0, 0, 0)$, every score is 0, which counts as a mistake. Pair 1 gives
$\wvec_1 = (0, 0, -1)$. Pairs 2 and 3 now score $-1$ with label $-1$, so they are correct. Pair 4 scores $-1$ with
label $+1$, so $\wvec_2 = (0, 0, -1) + (1, 1, 1) = (1, 1, 0)$.

**Later passes.** Cycling continues for 18 updates in 8 passes. The ninth pass makes no update, and training ends at
$\wvec = (3, 2, -4)$. The scores of the four inputs are $-4, -2, -1, +1$, all with the correct sign.

**The bound.** The unit vector $\uvec = (2, 2, -3)/\sqrt{17}$ gives $y_i\,\uvec^\top\xvec_i = (3, 1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{17}$,
so the margin is $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{17}$. The largest input is $\xvec_4$, with $R^2 = 3$. The theorem guarantees at
most $(R/\gamma)^2 = 3 \times 17 = 51$ updates. The 18 observed updates are within the bound.

## XOR and the limits of one layer

XOR labels $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 0)$ as $+1$ and $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$ as $-1$. No line separates them. Suppose
$w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b$ were positive at the first two points and negative at the last two. Adding the two positive
inequalities gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b > 0$. Adding the two negative ones gives $w_1 + w_2 + 2b < 0$. The contradiction
shows that no perceptron computes XOR.

On non-separable data the perceptron never stops. Its weight vector cycles through a bounded set, and the final
iterate depends on when training is halted. Minsky and Papert catalogued such limits of single-layer perceptrons
<Cite id="minsky1969" />. A hidden layer removes them: XOR is the AND of OR and NAND, each of which one perceptron
computes. The <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">multilayer perceptron</NoteLink> stacks such units, with
differentiable activations in place of the sign so that it can be trained by gradient descent.

## Assumptions

- The labels are $\pm 1$ and the classes are linearly separable, for convergence. Without separability there is no
  guarantee of any kind for the last iterate.
- The theorem measures the margin in the augmented space, so the bias enters $R$ and $\gamma$. Centring the inputs
  reduces $R$ and the bound.

## Pitfalls

- **The solution is not unique.** The perceptron stops at the first separating hyperplane it finds, often close to
  some training points. <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</NoteLink> and the support vector
  machine choose among separating hyperplanes; the perceptron does not.
- **Order dependence.** A different presentation order gives a different final $\wvec$. Averaging the weight vectors
  over all steps, the averaged perceptron, reduces this variance.
- **Scale of the score.** $\wvec^\top\xvec$ has no probabilistic meaning. The perceptron outputs a label, never a
  probability.
