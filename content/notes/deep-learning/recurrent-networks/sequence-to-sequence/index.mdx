<Definition>
A sequence-to-sequence (encoder–decoder) model has two recurrent networks. The encoder reads the input
$x_1, \dots, x_S$ and summarises it as a context vector $\cvec$, usually its final hidden state. The decoder is a
conditional language model: starting from $\cvec$, it outputs a distribution over the next token given the tokens
generated so far, until it emits an end-of-sequence token <Cite id="sutskever2014" /> <Cite id="cho2014" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Translation, summarisation and speech recognition map a sequence to another sequence. The lengths differ and the
alignment between them is unknown and not monotone. A single <NoteLink to="recurrent-neural-network">RNN</NoteLink>
that emits one output per input cannot express this. The encoder–decoder separates reading from writing. The encoder
reads the whole input first. The decoder then writes as many tokens as it needs.

## Model

Factor the probability of an output $\yvec = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$ by the chain rule of probability. No independence
assumption is made:

$$
p(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(y_t \mid y_1, \dots, y_{t-1}, \xvec).
$$

The model makes each factor a function of a decoder state $\svec_t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cvec &= \hvec_S, \qquad \hvec_j = f_{\text{enc}}(\hvec_{j-1}, x_j), \\
\svec_t &= f_{\text{dec}}(\svec_{t-1}, y_{t-1}, \cvec), \qquad \svec_0 = \cvec, \\
p(y_t \mid y_{<t}, \xvec) &= \operatorname{softmax}(\Vmat \svec_t + \bvec)_{y_t}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $f_{\text{enc}}$ and $f_{\text{dec}}$ are LSTM or GRU updates, $y_0$ is a start-of-sequence token and $\Vmat$
maps the state to one score per vocabulary token. The decoder state $\svec_t$ depends on the input only through
$\cvec$. Sutskever et al. start the decoder from $\cvec$. Cho et al. also feed $\cvec$ to every decoder step and to
the output layer.

## Training

Given pairs $(\xvec, \yvec)$, maximise the log-likelihood. The log of the product is a sum, so the loss is the sum of
per-step cross-entropies:

$$
\Lcal(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, \xvec).
$$

During training, the decoder input at step $t$ is the true previous token $y_{t-1}$, not the model's own prediction.
This is **teacher forcing**. It makes every step's loss available in one forward pass. The gradient flows back through
the decoder, through $\cvec$, and into the encoder, so both networks are trained jointly.

## Decoding

The most probable output $\argmax_{\yvec} p(\yvec \mid \xvec)$ is intractable: with vocabulary size $V$, there are
$V^T$ candidates of length $T$.

- **Greedy decoding** picks $\argmax_{y} p(y \mid y_{<t}, \xvec)$ at each step.
- **Beam search** keeps the $B$ highest-scoring prefixes. At each step it extends each by every token, scores the
  extensions by their total log-probability, and keeps the best $B$. Greedy decoding is beam search with $B = 1$.

## Worked example

Take a two-token vocabulary $\set{a, b}$ and outputs of length 2. Suppose $p(a) = 0.6$ and $p(b) = 0.4$ at step 1.
After $a$, both tokens have probability $0.5$. After $b$, $p(a \mid b) = 0.9$.

- Greedy picks $a$ at step 1, then either token: $p = 0.6 \cdot 0.5 = 0.30$.
- Beam search with $B = 2$ keeps both prefixes. The four sequences score $0.30$, $0.30$, $0.4 \cdot 0.9 = 0.36$ and
  $0.4 \cdot 0.1 = 0.04$. It returns $(b, a)$ with $p = 0.36$.

The locally best first token did not start the best sequence. Beam search recovers the better sequence because it
delays the decision.

## Practical findings

Sutskever et al. used four-layer LSTMs with 1000 units per layer. Reversing the order of the source sentence, but not
the target, raised the BLEU score of a single model on WMT'14 English to French from 25.9 to 30.6
<Cite id="sutskever2014" />. Reversal puts the first source words next to the first target words, which creates
short-range dependencies at the start of the output. An ensemble of five reversed LSTMs reached 34.8 BLEU.

## Assumptions

- The whole input is available before decoding starts. Streaming tasks, such as simultaneous translation, need another
  design.
- A fixed-size vector $\cvec$ can hold all the information the decoder needs.

## Pitfalls

- **The bottleneck.** Every input, whatever its length, is squeezed into one vector of the same size. The BLEU score of
  an encoder–decoder without attention falls steadily as the source sentence gets longer
  <Cite id="bahdanau2015" />. <NoteLink to="bahdanau-attention">Attention</NoteLink> removes the bottleneck by letting
  the decoder look at every encoder state.
- **Exposure bias.** With teacher forcing the decoder never sees its own mistakes during training. At test time an
  early error feeds into every later step.
- **Length bias in beam search.** Every extra token multiplies the probability by a factor below 1, so beam search
  favours short outputs. Scores are often divided by a power of the length.
