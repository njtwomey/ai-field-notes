<Definition>
**Early stopping** splits off a validation set, evaluates the validation loss every $k$ training steps, and keeps a copy
of the parameters with the lowest validation loss seen so far. Training stops when the validation loss has not improved
for $P$ consecutive evaluations, the **patience**. The returned model is the saved copy, not the last iterate
<Cite id="prechelt1998" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A network with enough parameters to memorise its training set reaches low training loss by fitting noise. The training
loss falls throughout training. The loss on unseen data typically falls, reaches a minimum, and rises as the network
starts to fit the noise. The number of training steps is therefore a hyperparameter that controls capacity, and the best
value is not known in advance.

## Mechanism

Training from small initial weights, the parameters move away from the initialisation step by step. Stopping early
limits how far they travel. For a quadratic loss the limit is equivalent to an L2 penalty
<Cite id="goodfellow2016" />.

Take the loss $L(\thetavec) = \tfrac12(\thetavec - \thetavec^*)^\top\Hmat(\thetavec - \thetavec^*)$ with minimiser
$\thetavec^*$ and Hessian $\Hmat$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_k > 0$, and start gradient descent at $\thetavec_0 = \zeros$
with step size $\eta < 1/\lambda_{\max}$. In the eigenbasis of $\Hmat$ each coordinate evolves independently:
$\theta_{t+1,k} - \theta_k^* = (1 - \eta\lambda_k)(\theta_{t,k} - \theta_k^*)$. After $\tau$ steps,

$$
\theta_{\tau,k} = \brack{1 - (1 - \eta\lambda_k)^\tau}\,\theta_k^*.
$$

Adding the penalty $\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\norm{\thetavec}^2$ and minimising exactly, as in
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>, gives
$\theta_k = \lambda_k\theta_k^*/(\lambda_k + \alpha) = \brack{1 - \alpha/(\lambda_k + \alpha)}\theta_k^*$. The two
shrinkage factors agree when $(1 - \eta\lambda_k)^\tau = \alpha/(\lambda_k + \alpha)$. For $\eta\lambda_k \ll 1$ and
$\lambda_k \ll \alpha$, take logarithms and use $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$ for small $x$:
$\tau\ln(1 - \eta\lambda_k) \approx -\tau\eta\lambda_k$ and $\ln\paren{\alpha/(\lambda_k + \alpha)} \approx -\lambda_k/\alpha$.
Equating,

$$
\alpha \approx \frac{1}{\tau\eta}.
$$

Directions of high curvature are fitted within a few steps; directions of low curvature, where the data carries little
information about $\thetavec$, stay near zero. The number of steps plays the role of the inverse penalty strength.

## Worked example

With $\eta = 0.01$ and $\tau = 100$, the matching penalty is $\alpha \approx 1/(100 \times 0.01) = 1$. The fraction of
$\theta_k^*$ recovered in each eigen-direction:

| Curvature $\lambda_k$ | Early stopping, $1 - (1 - 0.01\lambda_k)^{100}$ | Ridge, $\lambda_k/(\lambda_k + 1)$ |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| 0.01                  | 0.0100                                           | 0.0099                             |
| 0.1                   | 0.0952                                           | 0.0909                             |
| 1                     | 0.634                                            | 0.500                              |
| 10                    | 1.000                                            | 0.909                              |

The two agree where $\lambda_k \ll \alpha$, as the derivation assumed. In high-curvature directions early stopping shrinks
less than ridge. It behaves more like a sharp cut-off: directions with $\lambda_k \gg \alpha$ are recovered almost fully.

## Cost

Evaluating the validation loss costs one forward pass over the validation set per evaluation, and keeping the best copy
costs one extra set of parameters. Early stopping saves the training steps after the stopping point, which often
outweighs both.

## Trade-offs

- **Noisy validation curves.** The validation loss of a network rarely has a single minimum. It fluctuates and can have
  several local minima. Prechelt compared stopping criteria based on the relative rise above the best loss, on that rise
  relative to training progress, and on consecutive increases, and found that slower criteria bought small improvements
  in generalisation at a large cost in training time <Cite id="prechelt1998" />.
- **Data spent on validation.** The validation set is not used for training. A common remedy is to record the stopping
  step, then retrain on all the data for that many steps.
- **Interaction with schedules.** With a decaying
  <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedule</NoteLink>, the validation loss often drops at the end of
  the schedule. Stopping in the middle of a cosine schedule leaves a model trained at a large step size.
- **Selection bias.** Choosing the best of many validation evaluations makes the reported validation loss optimistic.
  Report performance on a separate test set.
