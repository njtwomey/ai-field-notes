<Definition>
A graph convolutional network (GCN) layer maps node features $\Hmat \in \reals^{N \times F}$ on a graph with adjacency
matrix $\Amat \in \set{0, 1}^{N \times N}$ to
$$
\Hmat' = \sigma\paren{\hat\Amat \Hmat \Wmat}, \qquad \hat\Amat = \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2} \tilde\Amat \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2},
\qquad \tilde\Amat = \Amat + \I,
$$
where $\tilde\Dmat$ is the diagonal degree matrix of $\tilde\Amat$, $\Wmat \in \reals^{F \times F'}$ is a learned weight
matrix and $\sigma$ is an elementwise nonlinearity <Cite id="kipf2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Row $i$ of $\hat\Amat \Hmat$ is a weighted sum of the features of node $i$ and its neighbours. Written per node, with
$\tilde d_i = 1 + \deg(i)$,

$$
\hvec_i' = \sigma\paren{\sum_{j \in \Ncal(i) \cup \set{i}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde d_i \tilde d_j}}\, \Wmat^{\tr} \hvec_j}.
$$

Each node averages its neighbourhood, including itself, and then applies the same linear map. This is a
<NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing</NoteLink> layer with the message
$\Wmat^{\tr} \hvec_j / \sqrt{\tilde d_i \tilde d_j}$ and sum aggregation. The factor $1/\sqrt{\tilde d_i \tilde d_j}$
keeps the scale of features independent of degree. A hub with 1000 neighbours does not get a vector 1000 times larger
than a leaf.

## Derivation from spectral filtering

The rule comes from defining convolution on a graph through a Fourier transform, then simplifying.

**Graph Fourier transform.** Let $\Dmat$ be the degree matrix of $\Amat$. The normalised graph Laplacian is

$$
\Lmat = \I - \Dmat^{-1/2} \Amat \Dmat^{-1/2}.
$$

$\Lmat$ is real and symmetric, so it has an eigendecomposition $\Lmat = \Umat \Lambdamat \Umat^{\tr}$ with orthonormal
eigenvectors. Its eigenvalues lie in $[0, 2]$ (see the derivation below). The eigenvectors play the role of Fourier
modes: small eigenvalues belong to vectors that vary slowly across edges, large ones to vectors that alternate. The
graph Fourier transform of a signal $\xvec \in \reals^N$ is $\hat\xvec = \Umat^{\tr} \xvec$.

**Spectral filter.** By analogy with the <NoteLink to="convolution-theorem">convolution theorem</NoteLink>, a filter
multiplies each frequency by a gain $g_\theta(\lambda)$:

$$
g_\theta \star \xvec = \Umat\, g_\theta(\Lambdamat)\, \Umat^{\tr} \xvec.
$$

This needs the eigendecomposition, which costs $O(N^3)$, and a dense $N \times N$ product per filter.

**Polynomial filters are local.** Let $g_\theta(\lambda) = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k \lambda^k$. Since
$\Umat \Lambdamat^k \Umat^{\tr} = \Lmat^k$,

$$
g_\theta \star \xvec = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k \Lmat^k \xvec.
$$

No eigendecomposition is needed. $(\Lmat^k)_{ij}$ is zero when nodes $i$ and $j$ are more than $k$ hops apart, because
$\Lmat$ has non-zeros only on the diagonal and on edges, and each power extends the reach by one hop. A degree-$K$
polynomial filter is therefore $K$-localised. Defferrard et al. used Chebyshev polynomials of this form
<Cite id="defferrard2016" />.

**First order.** Kipf and Welling take $K = 1$. In the Chebyshev form the filter uses the rescaled Laplacian
$2\Lmat / \lambda_{\max} - \I$; approximating $\lambda_{\max} \approx 2$ gives

$$
g_\theta \star \xvec \approx \theta_0 \xvec + \theta_1 (\Lmat - \I) \xvec = \theta_0 \xvec - \theta_1 \Dmat^{-1/2} \Amat \Dmat^{-1/2} \xvec.
$$

Tie the two parameters, $\theta = \theta_0 = -\theta_1$, to reduce overfitting:

$$
g_\theta \star \xvec \approx \theta\paren{\I + \Dmat^{-1/2} \Amat \Dmat^{-1/2}} \xvec.
$$

**Renormalisation.** The matrix $\I + \Dmat^{-1/2} \Amat \Dmat^{-1/2} = 2\I - \Lmat$ has eigenvalues in $[0, 2]$, and
$2$ is always one of them. Stacking layers multiplies by it repeatedly, so some components grow like $2^k$ and others
shrink. This causes exploding or vanishing activations and gradients. Kipf and Welling replace it with
$\tilde\Dmat^{-1/2} (\Amat + \I) \tilde\Dmat^{-1/2}$: add the self-loops to the adjacency matrix before normalising
<Cite id="kipf2017" />. The resulting $\hat\Amat$ has largest eigenvalue exactly 1.

**Many channels.** With $F$ input channels and $F'$ filters, each filter has one parameter per input channel. Collecting
them in $\Wmat$ gives $\hat\Amat \Hmat \Wmat$, and a nonlinearity gives the layer.

<Derivation title="Eigenvalues of the normalised Laplacian lie in [0, 2]">
Let $\Amat$ be symmetric with non-negative entries and every degree $d_i > 0$. Write $y_i = x_i / \sqrt{d_i}$. Then
$$
\xvec^{\tr} \Lmat \xvec = \sum_i x_i^2 - \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} y_i y_j = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} y_i^2 - \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} y_i y_j
= \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} (y_i - y_j)^2 \ge 0,
$$
using $x_i^2 = d_i y_i^2 = \sum_j a_{ij} y_i^2$ and symmetry of $\Amat$. In the same way,
$$
\xvec^{\tr} (2\I - \Lmat) \xvec = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} (y_i + y_j)^2 \ge 0.
$$
So every Rayleigh quotient of $\Lmat$ lies in $[0, 2]$, and so do its eigenvalues. The value $0$ is attained by
$\xvec = \Dmat^{1/2} \ones$, where all $y_i$ are equal, so $\I - \Lmat = \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2}$ has eigenvalue $1$
and $2\I - \Lmat$ has eigenvalue $2$. The value 2 would require $y_i = -y_j$ across every edge, which is impossible
when a node has a self-loop ($a_{ii} > 0$ forces $y_i = 0$). Applied to $\tilde\Amat = \Amat + \I$, this shows that the
eigenvalues of $\hat\Amat = \I - \tilde\Lmat$ lie in $(-1, 1]$.
</Derivation>

## Semi-supervised node classification

Kipf and Welling classify the nodes of a citation graph when only a few are labelled. A two-layer GCN computes

$$
\Zmat = \operatorname{softmax}\paren{\hat\Amat\, \operatorname{ReLU}\paren{\hat\Amat \Xmat \Wmat^{(0)}}\, \Wmat^{(1)}},
$$

with the softmax applied to each row, and minimises the cross-entropy over the labelled nodes only. Unlabelled nodes still
shape the result, because their features flow into the labelled nodes' two-hop neighbourhoods. With 20 labelled nodes per
class, the model reached 81.5% accuracy on Cora, 70.3% on Citeseer and 79.0% on Pubmed <Cite id="kipf2017" />.

## Worked example

Take the path graph $1{-}2{-}3$. Adding self-loops gives degrees $\tilde d = (2, 3, 2)$, and

$$
\hat\Amat = \begin{bmatrix}
\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 6} & 0 \\
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt 6} & \tfrac{1}{3} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 6} \\
0 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 6} & \tfrac{1}{2}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For example, $\hat A_{12} = 1/\sqrt{\tilde d_1 \tilde d_2} = 1/\sqrt{6} = 0.408$. A feature that is 1 on node 1 and 0
elsewhere becomes $\hat\Amat \xvec = (0.5, 0.408, 0)$ after one layer, ignoring $\Wmat$ and $\sigma$. It spreads one
hop. The eigenvalues of $\hat\Amat$ are $1$, $1/2$ and $-1/6$, all in $(-1, 1]$.

## Oversmoothing

Repeated multiplication by $\hat\Amat$ converges, on a connected graph, to the projection onto its top eigenvector
$\tilde\Dmat^{1/2} \ones$. All node features become proportional to $\sqrt{\tilde d_i}$ and carry no information about
the starting signal. A GCN layer is a form of Laplacian smoothing, and stacking many layers over-smooths
<Cite id="li2018" />. This is why GCNs are usually two or three layers deep.

## Assumptions

- **Homophily.** Connected nodes tend to share labels. Averaging over neighbours then helps. On graphs where neighbours
  tend to differ, a GCN can do worse than ignoring the edges.
- The graph is undirected; $\hat\Amat$ is symmetric.

## Pitfalls

- **Depth.** More layers widen the receptive field but smooth the features. Residual connections or skip connections to
  the input are needed beyond a few layers.
- **Transductive by default.** The original formulation uses the whole graph, including test nodes, at training time.
  New graphs need the layer applied with their own $\hat\Amat$.
- **Isolated nodes.** Without self-loops a node of degree 0 would divide by zero. The self-loops in $\tilde\Amat$ make
  every $\tilde d_i \ge 1$.
