<Definition>
Multi-head attention with $h$ heads maps queries, keys and values of model width $d$ to
$$
\operatorname{MultiHead}(\Qmat, \Kmat, \Vmat) = [\text{head}_1 \,\Vert\, \cdots \,\Vert\, \text{head}_h]\, \Wmat_O,
\qquad \text{head}_i = \operatorname{Attention}(\Qmat \Wmat_Q^{i}, \Kmat \Wmat_K^{i}, \Vmat \Wmat_V^{i}),
$$
where $\Wmat_Q^i, \Wmat_K^i \in \reals^{d \times d_k}$, $\Wmat_V^i \in \reals^{d \times d_v}$,
$\Wmat_O \in \reals^{h d_v \times d}$, $\Vert$ concatenates along the feature axis, and $\operatorname{Attention}$ is
<NoteLink to="scaled-dot-product-attention">scaled dot-product attention</NoteLink>. Usually $d_k = d_v = d/h$
<Cite id="vaswani2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

One attention head gives each position one set of weights over the other positions, and so one weighted average. A
word in a sentence may need several things at once: its syntactic head, the noun a pronoun refers to, the previous
word. A single softmax must compromise between them.

Several heads remove the compromise. Each head has its own projections, so it compares positions in its own subspace
and produces its own weights. One head can attend to the previous token while another attends to a distant antecedent.
The output projection $\Wmat_O$ mixes the heads' results back into one vector per position.

## Why several heads are more expressive

Row $i$ of one head's output is $\sum_j \alpha_{ij} \vvec_j$ with $\alpha_{ij} \ge 0$ and $\sum_j \alpha_{ij} = 1$. It
is a convex combination of the value vectors, so it lies in their convex hull. With one head, every output is a single
point of that hull, mapped by $\Wmat_O$.

Split $\Wmat_O$ into blocks $\Wmat_O^1, \dots, \Wmat_O^h$ of $d_v$ rows, one per head. Multiplying the concatenation by
$\Wmat_O$ is the same as summing the heads' separately projected outputs:

$$
\operatorname{MultiHead}_i = \sum_{r=1}^{h} \paren{\sum_j \alpha^{r}_{ij}\, \vvec^{r}_j}\, \Wmat_O^{r}.
$$

Each head contributes its own weighted average with its own weights $\alpha^r$. The output is a sum of $h$
independently chosen averages rather than one. Vaswani et al. describe this as letting the model attend to information
from different representation subspaces at different positions, which averaging in a single head inhibits
<Cite id="vaswani2017" />.

## Parameters and cost

With $d_k = d_v = d/h$, the projections for all heads together have $3 \cdot h \cdot d \cdot (d/h) = 3d^2$ weights, and
$\Wmat_O$ has $d^2$. The total, $4d^2$, does not depend on $h$ and equals that of a single head of full width.

The score matrices cost $h \cdot n^2 \cdot (d/h) = n^2 d$ multiply-adds for a sequence of length $n$, also independent
of $h$. More heads do increase memory for the weights, $h n^2$ values instead of $n^2$.

In practice all heads are computed at once. One $d \times d$ matrix produces all queries, which are reshaped to
$n \times h \times d/h$ and processed as a batch of $h$ attentions.

## Worked example

The base transformer has $d = 512$ and $h = 8$ heads, so each head has $d_k = d_v = 64$ <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.

- Projection weights: $4 \cdot 512^2 = 1{,}048{,}576$. With biases, $1{,}048{,}576 + 4 \cdot 512 = 1{,}050{,}624$.
- Scores for a sequence of $n = 1000$ tokens: $8 \cdot 1000^2 = 8 \times 10^6$ weights per layer.
- Each head's scores are divided by $\sqrt{64} = 8$, not by $\sqrt{512}$. The scale follows the width of the head.

In the paper's ablation, one head of width 512 scored 0.9 BLEU lower than eight heads of width 64. Very many narrow
heads, 32 of width 16, were also worse than eight <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.

## Variants that share keys and values

During autoregressive decoding, each layer caches the keys and values of all earlier tokens (see
<NoteLink to="kv-cache">KV caching</NoteLink>). The cache holds $2 h d_k$ numbers per token per layer.

- **Multi-query attention** keeps $h$ query heads but a single key head and a single value head, shared by all query
  heads. The cache shrinks by a factor of $h$ <Cite id="shazeer2019" />.
- **Grouped-query attention** divides the query heads into $g$ groups, each sharing one key and value head. It
  interpolates between multi-head ($g = h$) and multi-query ($g = 1$) attention and recovers most of the quality of
  multi-head attention <Cite id="ainslie2023" />.

## Assumptions

- Useful relations between positions can be found in low-dimensional subspaces of width $d/h$.
- A fixed number of heads per layer is enough; the model cannot add heads for harder inputs.

## Pitfalls

- **Heads are not all needed.** After training, many heads can be removed at test time with little loss in accuracy
  <Cite id="michel2019" />. Head count is a capacity choice, not a count of distinct functions.
- **Interpreting heads.** Individual heads sometimes align with recognisable patterns, such as attending to the previous
  token. Most do not have a single clean role.
- **Head width too small.** With a fixed $d$, raising $h$ shrinks $d_k$. Very narrow heads have low-rank score matrices,
  since $\Qmat \Wmat_Q^i (\Kmat \Wmat_K^i)^{\tr}$ has rank at most $d_k$.
