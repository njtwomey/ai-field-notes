<Definition>
**Agglomerative hierarchical clustering** takes $n$ points and a distance $d(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$ between points. It
starts from $n$ singleton clusters. At each of $n - 1$ steps it merges the two clusters $A$ and $B$ with the smallest
**linkage** $D(A, B)$, a distance between sets of points, and records the value of $D$ as the height of the merge. The
output is a binary tree, the **dendrogram**, whose leaves are the points and whose internal nodes are the merges
<Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Linkages

The linkage decides which clusters count as close. The four common choices, for clusters $A$ and $B$ of sizes $n_A$
and $n_B$ with means $\muvec_A$ and $\muvec_B$:

| Linkage | $D(A, B)$ | Clusters it favours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single | $\min_{\xvec \in A, \yvec \in B} d(\xvec, \yvec)$ | Long, chained, any shape |
| Complete | $\max_{\xvec \in A, \yvec \in B} d(\xvec, \yvec)$ | Compact, similar diameter |
| Average (UPGMA) | $\frac{1}{n_A n_B} \sum_{\xvec \in A} \sum_{\yvec \in B} d(\xvec, \yvec)$ | Between single and complete |
| Ward | $\frac{n_A n_B}{n_A + n_B} \norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_B}^2$ | Compact, similar size, as in k-means |

Single linkage looks only at the closest pair. A chain of points between two groups makes their single-linkage
distance small, so single linkage merges the groups early. This is the **chaining** effect. Complete linkage looks at
the farthest pair and splits elongated groups instead. Ward's linkage is the increase in the within-cluster sum of
squares caused by the merge <Cite id="ward1963" />. Each Ward step is the greedy step that keeps the
<NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> objective as small as possible.

## Why Ward's linkage is the increase in squared error

Let $\operatorname{SSE}(C) = \sum_{\xvec \in C} \norm{\xvec - \muvec_C}^2$. Merge $A$ and $B$ into $C$, with
$n = n_A + n_B$ and $\muvec_C = (n_A\muvec_A + n_B\muvec_B)/n$. For a point $\xvec \in A$, expand around $\muvec_A$:

$$
\norm{\xvec - \muvec_C}^2 = \norm{\xvec - \muvec_A}^2 + 2(\xvec - \muvec_A)^\top(\muvec_A - \muvec_C)
+ \norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_C}^2.
$$

Summed over $\xvec \in A$, the middle term vanishes because $\sum_{\xvec \in A}(\xvec - \muvec_A) = \zeros$. The same
holds for $B$. So

$$
\operatorname{SSE}(C) = \operatorname{SSE}(A) + \operatorname{SSE}(B) + n_A\norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_C}^2
+ n_B\norm{\muvec_B - \muvec_C}^2.
$$

Substituting $\muvec_A - \muvec_C = \frac{n_B}{n}(\muvec_A - \muvec_B)$ and
$\muvec_B - \muvec_C = -\frac{n_A}{n}(\muvec_A - \muvec_B)$, the last two terms sum to
$\paren{\frac{n_A n_B^2}{n^2} + \frac{n_B n_A^2}{n^2}}\norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_B}^2 =
\frac{n_A n_B}{n}\norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_B}^2$. The increase in squared error is therefore

$$
\operatorname{SSE}(A \cup B) - \operatorname{SSE}(A) - \operatorname{SSE}(B) = \frac{n_A n_B}{n_A + n_B}\norm{\muvec_A -
\muvec_B}^2.
$$

The factor $n_A n_B/(n_A + n_B)$ penalises merging two large clusters, so Ward's method tends to produce clusters of
similar size.

## The Lance–Williams update

Recomputing $D$ from the points after every merge is wasteful. Lance and Williams showed that all four linkages can be
updated from the old cluster distances alone <Cite id="lance1967" />. After merging $A$ and $B$, the distance from any
other cluster $K$ to $A \cup B$ is

$$
D(K, A \cup B) = \alpha_A D(K, A) + \alpha_B D(K, B) + \beta D(A, B) + \gamma \abs{D(K, A) - D(K, B)}.
$$

The coefficients for each linkage follow from its definition:

- **Single.** $\alpha_A = \alpha_B = \tfrac12$, $\beta = 0$, $\gamma = -\tfrac12$. For numbers $a$ and $b$,
  $\tfrac12 a + \tfrac12 b - \tfrac12\abs{a - b} = \min(a, b)$. The closest pair between $K$ and $A \cup B$ is the
  closer of the closest pairs between $K$ and $A$ and between $K$ and $B$.
- **Complete.** The same with $\gamma = +\tfrac12$, which gives $\max(a, b)$.
- **Average.** $\alpha_A = n_A/(n_A + n_B)$, $\alpha_B = n_B/(n_A + n_B)$, $\beta = \gamma = 0$. The sum of all
  $n_K(n_A + n_B)$ pairwise distances splits into the pairs with $A$ and the pairs with $B$, so the mean over
  $A \cup B$ is the size-weighted mean of the two means.
- **Ward** (with $D$ the Ward linkage). $\alpha_A = (n_A + n_K)/(n_A + n_B + n_K)$,
  $\alpha_B = (n_B + n_K)/(n_A + n_B + n_K)$, $\beta = -n_K/(n_A + n_B + n_K)$, $\gamma = 0$.

<Derivation title="Derivation of the Ward coefficients">
Write $\Delta_{PQ} = \frac{n_P n_Q}{n_P + n_Q}\norm{\muvec_P - \muvec_Q}^2$ and let $N = n_A + n_B + n_K$. The mean of
$A \cup B$ is $\muvec_C = (n_A\muvec_A + n_B\muvec_B)/(n_A + n_B)$. Expand
$\norm{\muvec_K - \muvec_C}^2$ with weights $w_A = n_A/(n_A + n_B)$ and $w_B = n_B/(n_A + n_B)$: for any vectors,
$\norm{\muvec_K - w_A\muvec_A - w_B\muvec_B}^2 = w_A\norm{\muvec_K - \muvec_A}^2 + w_B\norm{\muvec_K - \muvec_B}^2 -
w_A w_B\norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_B}^2$, which is the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">variance decomposition</NoteLink> of a two-point distribution. Multiply by
$n_K(n_A + n_B)/N$:

$$
\Delta_{K,C} = \frac{n_K n_A}{N}\norm{\muvec_K - \muvec_A}^2 + \frac{n_K n_B}{N}\norm{\muvec_K - \muvec_B}^2 -
\frac{n_K n_A n_B}{N(n_A + n_B)}\norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_B}^2.
$$

Now $\frac{n_K n_A}{N}\norm{\muvec_K - \muvec_A}^2 = \frac{n_K + n_A}{N}\Delta_{KA}$, the same for $B$, and the last
term is $\frac{n_K}{N}\Delta_{AB}$. That gives the coefficients above.
</Derivation>

The update makes the naive algorithm $O(n^2)$ in memory and $O(n^3)$ in time: $n - 1$ merges, each scanning the
distance matrix. Better algorithms reach $O(n^2)$ time for all four linkages; for single linkage, SLINK is one
<Cite id="mullner2011" />.

## The dendrogram and cutting it

The dendrogram draws each merge as a horizontal bar at its height. For single, complete, average and Ward linkage the
heights never decrease from one merge to the next, so the tree has no crossings. Cutting the tree at height $t$ keeps
the merges below $t$ and yields a flat clustering. Cutting between two consecutive heights gives the same clusters, so
the tree encodes the clusterings for every number of clusters from $n$ down to 1 at once. A long vertical gap between
consecutive merges suggests a natural cut.

<DendrogramCut />

The height at which two points first join the same cluster is their **cophenetic distance**. It satisfies the
ultrametric inequality $c(\xvec, \zvec) \le \max(c(\xvec, \yvec), c(\yvec, \zvec))$. The correlation between
cophenetic and original distances measures how faithfully the tree represents the data.

## Worked example

Five points on a line: $0, 1, 3, 7, 8$, with $d(x, y) = \abs{x - y}$.

The first two merges are the same for every linkage: $\set{0, 1}$ and $\set{7, 8}$, both at height 1. The remaining
three clusters are $\set{0, 1}$, $\set{3}$ and $\set{7, 8}$.

- **Single.** $D(\set{0,1}, \set{3}) = 2$ and $D(\set{3}, \set{7,8}) = 4$, so $\set{0, 1, 3}$ forms at height 2. The
  last merge is at $\min = 7 - 3 = 4$.
- **Complete.** $D(\set{0,1}, \set{3}) = 3$ and $D(\set{3}, \set{7,8}) = 5$, so $\set{0, 1, 3}$ forms at height 3. The
  last merge is at $\max = 8 - 0 = 8$.
- **Average.** $D(\set{0,1}, \set{3}) = (3 + 2)/2 = 2.5$. The last merge is at the mean of the six distances
  between $\set{0, 1, 3}$ and $\set{7, 8}$: $(7 + 8 + 6 + 7 + 4 + 5)/6 = 37/6 \approx 6.17$.
- **Ward.** Merging $\set{0, 1}$ with $\set{3}$ adds $\frac{2 \cdot 1}{3}(3 - 0.5)^2 = 4.17$ to the squared error.
  Merging $\set{0, 1}$ with $\set{7, 8}$ would add $\frac{2 \cdot 2}{4}(7.5 - 0.5)^2 = 49$, so $\set{0, 1, 3}$ forms
  first. The last merge adds $\frac{3 \cdot 2}{5}(7.5 - \tfrac43)^2 = 45.63$.

All four linkages give the same two-cluster solution, $\set{0, 1, 3}$ and $\set{7, 8}$. They disagree about how far
apart the clusters are, which changes where a height threshold would cut.

Libraries often report the Ward height as $\sqrt{2 \times \text{increase}}$, so that two single points merge at their
distance: $\sqrt{2 \cdot 4.17} = 2.89$ and $\sqrt{2 \cdot 45.63} = 9.55$ here. The numbers match SciPy's `linkage`.

## Distinguishing structure from noise

Agglomerative clustering performs $n - 1$ merges on any input. It returns a full dendrogram for data with groups and
for data without groups, and a cut at any height returns clusters in both cases. The dendrogram therefore shows how
the algorithm grouped the points. The dendrogram does not show whether the data has groups. That question needs a
comparison: with data that has no groups, and with the same procedure run on perturbed copies of the data.

The figure runs one pipeline on two data sets of the same size $n$ and dimension $d$: standardise each feature to mean
0 and standard deviation 1, build the tree, cut it.

- **Structured data.** Fisher's Iris data <Cite id="fisher1936" />: 150 flowers, 50 from each of three species of
  iris (setosa, versicolor, virginica), with four measurements in centimetres (sepal length, sepal width, petal length,
  petal width). The alternative is three Gaussian clusters with unit variance whose centres form a triangle of side 6
  in the first two coordinates.
- **Random data.** $n$ points drawn uniformly from the bounding box of the standardised structured data, or from a
  single standard Gaussian in $d$ dimensions. Neither has groups.

<StructureOrNoise />

The two dendrograms look alike. Both end in a few long branches near the root, and both give clean-looking clusters
when cut. The checks below tell them apart. Each check is a number computed from the tree or the data, and each number
means something only in comparison with its value on data without groups.

### Merge heights and the jump at the cut

Let $h_1 \le h_2 \le \dots \le h_{n-1}$ be the merge heights. A cut with $k$ clusters applies the first $n - k$ merges.
The **jump ratio** at that cut is $h_{n-k+1}/h_{n-k}$: the height of the first merge above the cut divided by the height
of the last merge below it. A large ratio is a long vertical gap in the dendrogram, the usual visual cue for a natural
cut.

Noise also produces jumps near the root. Few merges happen there, so consecutive heights are far apart. With Ward's
linkage, the cost $\frac{n_A n_B}{n_A + n_B}\norm{\muvec_A - \muvec_B}^2$ grows with the cluster sizes $n_A$ and
$n_B$, so the last merges are the tallest on any data. On uniform noise of the size and shape of the standardised
Iris data, the Ward jump ratio at $k = 2$ has median 1.14 and 95th percentile 1.32 over 200 draws. On Iris it is 2.16,
well outside that range.

### Cophenetic correlation

The **cophenetic correlation** is the <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">Pearson correlation</NoteLink>, over all $n(n-1)/2$ pairs $i < j$, between the original
distances $d_{ij}$ and the cophenetic distances $c_{ij}$, the heights at which $i$ and $j$ first join
<Cite id="sokal1962" />. The cophenetic correlation measures how faithfully the tree reproduces the distances. The
cophenetic correlation does not measure whether groups exist. On standardised Iris it is 0.75 to 0.85 across the four
linkages. On uniform noise of the same shape, its 5th to 95th percentile range with Ward's linkage is 0.48 to 0.54.
In two dimensions, uniform noise reaches about 0.65 with complete, average and Ward linkage, a value often read as a
fair fit. No threshold on the cophenetic correlation separates structure from noise without a reference.

### Comparing with a null reference: the gap statistic

Let $W_k$ be the within-cluster sum of squares of the $k$-cluster cut. $W_k$ falls as $k$ grows on any data. For
uniform data in a region of volume $V$ in $d$ dimensions, $k$ compact clusters each cover a volume of about $V/k$, with
a radius proportional to $(V/k)^{1/d}$. Each point's squared distance to its cluster mean is therefore proportional to
$(V/k)^{2/d}$, so

$$
\log W_k \approx \text{const} - \frac{2}{d}\log k.
$$

Real groups make $\log W_k$ fall faster than this baseline up to the true number of groups, and at the baseline rate
afterwards. The **gap statistic** measures the extra fall against data drawn from a reference distribution with no
groups <Cite id="tibshirani2001" />:

$$
\operatorname{Gap}(k) = \expect^*\LB\log W_k^*\RB - \log W_k.
$$

Here $W_k^*$ comes from the same procedure, linkage included, applied to a reference data set of $n$ points drawn
uniformly from the bounding box of the data. The expectation is a mean over $B$ reference sets, with <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>
$s_k = \text{sd}_k\sqrt{1 + 1/B}$, where $\text{sd}_k$ is the standard deviation of the $B$ values of $\log W_k^*$. The
rule chooses the smallest $k$ with $\operatorname{Gap}(k) \ge \operatorname{Gap}(k+1) - s_{k+1}$. The rule can choose
$k = 1$, and on uniform noise it does. The
<NoteLink to="choosing-the-number-of-clusters">choosing the number of clusters</NoteLink> note treats the gap
statistic in full, beside the elbow, silhouette and <Gloss name="bic" />.

The reference defines what "no groups" means. A single Gaussian is concentrated near its mean and sparse near the edges
of its bounding box, so its $W_1$ is smaller than that of uniform data over the same box, and $\operatorname{Gap}(k)$ is
positive for every $k$. $\operatorname{Gap}(k)$ falls as $k$ grows, so the rule still chooses $k = 1$. The value of the
gap at one cut can mislead; the rule, which compares consecutive values, does not in this case.

### The Hopkins statistic: clustering tendency

The **Hopkins statistic** tests whether the points are spread uniformly, before any clustering is run
<Cite id="hopkins1954" />. Draw $m$ points uniformly from the bounding box, with $m$ much smaller than $n$, and let
$u_i$ be the distance from the $i$th of them to its nearest data point. Draw $m$ of the data points and let $w_i$ be the
distance from the $i$th of them to its nearest other data point. In $d$ dimensions,

$$
H = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^m u_i^d}{\sum_{i=1}^m u_i^d + \sum_{i=1}^m w_i^d}.
$$

Hopkins and Skellam worked in the plane with squared distances; the power $d$ extends the statistic to $d$ dimensions.
Under uniformity, $H$ has a known distribution. Model the data as a <NoteLink to="poisson-process">Poisson process</NoteLink> of intensity $\lambda$ points per
unit volume, and let $v_d$ be the volume of the <NoteLink to="vector-norms">unit ball</NoteLink> in $d$ dimensions. The ball of radius $r$ around any fixed
location contains no data point with probability $e^{-\lambda v_d r^d}$, the Poisson probability of zero points in
volume $v_d r^d$. So the nearest-point distance $R$ satisfies $\pr(\lambda v_d R^d > t) = e^{-t}$: the quantity
$\lambda v_d R^d$ is $\Exp(1)$. This holds for $u_i$, measured from a random location. It also holds for $w_i$,
measured from a data point, because the other points of a Poisson process are again a Poisson process of the same
intensity. So all $2m$ values $u_i^d$ and $w_i^d$ are independent exponentials with the same rate $\lambda v_d$, when
$m$ is small enough that the nearest-neighbour balls rarely overlap. A sum of $m$ such exponentials is
$\GammaD(m, \lambda v_d)$, and for independent gamma variables with the same rate, $G_1/(G_1 + G_2) \sim \Beta(m, m)$.
Therefore

$$
H \sim \Beta(m, m) \quad \text{under uniformity}, \qquad \expect[H] = \tfrac12.
$$

Clustered data have short within-cluster distances $w_i$ and long distances $u_i$ from empty space, so $H$
approaches 1. Regularly spaced data push $H$ towards 0. In the figure, $H$ is about 0.99 for Iris and about 0.5 for uniform noise,
with $m = n/10$ and the mean over 10 draws.

The Hopkins statistic tests uniformity, not grouping. A single Gaussian is not uniform over its bounding box, so its
$H$ is between about 0.8 and 0.95 in the figure, although it has one group. A high $H$ shows only that the data is not uniform
over its bounding box.

### Worked example: the Hopkins statistic

Six points in the plane ($d = 2$), in two tight triples: $(0, 0)$, $(0.5, 0)$, $(0, 0.5)$ and $(4, 4)$, $(3.5, 4)$,
$(4, 3.5)$. The bounding box is $[0, 4]^2$. Take $m = 2$.

- **Sampled data points.** $(0, 0)$ and $(4, 4)$. Each has its nearest neighbour at distance $w = 0.5$, so
  $\sum w_i^2 = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$.
- **Uniform points.** Suppose the two uniform draws are $(2, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$. The nearest data point to $(2, 1)$ is
  $(0.5, 0)$, with $u_1^2 = 1.5^2 + 1^2 = 3.25$. The nearest data points to $(1, 3)$ are $(0, 0.5)$ and $(3.5, 4)$,
  tied, with $u_2^2 = 1^2 + 2.5^2 = 7.25$. So $\sum u_i^2 = 10.5$.
- **Statistic.** $H = 10.5/(10.5 + 0.5) = 0.955$.

Under uniformity $H \sim \Beta(2, 2)$, with density $6h(1 - h)$ and distribution function $3h^2 - 2h^3$. The
probability of a value at least as large is $1 - (3 \cdot 0.955^2 - 2 \cdot 0.955^3) = 0.006$. With only two draws of
each kind, one test is noisy; in practice $m$ is about $n/10$ and $H$ is averaged over several draws. The numbers match
SciPy's `cKDTree` <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> and `scipy.stats.beta`.

### Stability under resampling

A cluster that reflects structure in the data reappears when the data is perturbed. A cluster that the algorithm cut
out of noise changes from one perturbation to the next. Hennig measures this cluster by cluster
<Cite id="hennig2007" />:

1. Cluster the full data and cut it into $k$ clusters.
2. Draw a resample: a <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> sample, or a subsample without replacement. Cluster it with the same method and the
   same $k$.
3. For each original cluster $C$, keep only its points that are in the resample, $C'$. Find the resampled cluster $D$
   with the highest **Jaccard similarity** $J(C', D) = \abs{C' \cap D}/\abs{C' \cup D}$.
4. Repeat for $B$ resamples and average $J$ for each cluster.

A mean Jaccard near 1 means the cluster is recovered almost exactly every time. The figure uses $B = 20$ subsamples of
80% of the points and reports the mean over clusters. With Ward's linkage, Iris cut into two clusters scores about
0.99, and uniform noise scores about 0.45 to 0.65.

Stability measures reproducibility, not meaning. Single linkage on noise splits off one or two outlying points and
keeps everything else in one cluster. The same split recurs in every subsample, so its stability is close to 1. The
cluster sizes in the figure show this failure: a "clustering" of 149 points and 1 point is not a finding.

**pvclust** assesses each cluster of a dendrogram with a <NoteLink to="parametric-bootstrap-and-bootstrap-hypothesis-tests">bootstrap p-value</NoteLink> <Cite id="suzuki2006" />. The bootstrap
probability $\text{BP}$ of a cluster is the fraction of bootstrap trees that contain exactly that set of points. <Gloss name="bp" /> is
biased. The multiscale bootstrap corrects the bias by resampling $n' = n/\sigma^2$ points for several scales
$\sigma^2$, computing $z(\sigma^2) = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \text{BP}(\sigma^2))$ with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution
function, and fitting $\sigma z(\sigma^2) = v + c\sigma^2$. The **approximately unbiased** (AU) <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is
$1 - \Phi(v - c)$, whereas the plain bootstrap gives $\text{BP} = 1 - \Phi(v + c)$ <Cite id="shimodaira2004" />. The
parameter $v$ is a signed distance from the data to the boundary of the region of data sets that produce the cluster,
and $c$ is the curvature of that boundary. A cluster with an AU value above 0.95 is supported at the 5% level.

### Linkage imposes shape

Every linkage produces clusters of its preferred shape, whether the data has them or not. Cut into two clusters,
uniform noise gives two halves of similar size under Ward's linkage, 78 and 72 points in one draw of 150. The balance
comes from the factor $n_A n_B/(n_A + n_B)$, which makes merging two large clusters expensive, not from the data. Complete
linkage also returns compact groups of similar diameter. Single linkage returns one large cluster and a few outliers.
Compact, balanced Ward clusters are therefore no evidence of structure: Ward returns them on any input.

### Reading the Iris result

Iris cut into two clusters with Ward's linkage separates the 49 setosa flowers from the rest (one setosa flower falls
with the other species). Every check agrees that this split is real: the jump ratio is 2.16 against a noise 95th
percentile of 1.32, the stability is about 0.99, and the Hopkins statistic is about 0.99. Cut into three clusters, Ward
returns groups of 49, 30 and 71 flowers. The second and third mix versicolor and virginica (27 and 23 versicolor with
2 and 48 virginica), and their stability falls to about 0.7. Iris has three species but two well-separated groups in
these four measurements, because versicolor and virginica overlap in sepal and petal size. The three-cluster cut looks
as clean in the dendrogram as the two-cluster cut. Only the checks, and the species labels, show that the third
cluster is not supported by the data.

The gap rule is no guide to the species count. On Iris it chooses $k = 2$ with single and average linkage and $k = 4$
or 5 with complete and Ward linkage. The gap statistic detects departure from uniformity, and the elongated versicolor
and virginica cloud departs from uniformity at several scales.

## Divisive clustering

Divisive clustering builds the tree from the top: it starts with one cluster and splits recursively, for instance with
2-means at each node. It is less common, because the best split of a set is expensive to find exactly. Agglomerative
merges are greedy and cheap.

## Pitfalls

- **Merges are final.** A merge made early is never undone, even if later merges show it was wrong. The tree is not a
  global optimum of any objective.
- **Linkage changes the answer.** Single linkage finds chained, non-convex clusters but merges groups joined by
  noise. Complete and Ward linkage prefer compact clusters and split elongated ones.
- **Scaling.** Distances depend on the units of each feature. Standardise first, or choose the distance deliberately.
- **Quadratic memory.** The distance matrix has $n(n - 1)/2$ entries, which limits the method to tens of thousands of
  points on one machine.
- **Choosing the cut.** The height or number of clusters is a separate decision; see
  <NoteLink to="choosing-the-number-of-clusters">choosing the number of clusters</NoteLink>.
