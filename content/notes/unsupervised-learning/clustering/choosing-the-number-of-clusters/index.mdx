<Definition>
Choosing the number of clusters means selecting $k$ for a clustering method from the data alone, without true labels.
Every method fits a clustering for each candidate $k = 1, \dots, K$ and scores it with a criterion that does not
improve automatically with $k$. The chosen $k$ optimises that criterion.
</Definition>

## Why the objective cannot choose k

Let $W_k$ be the within-cluster sum of squares of the best clustering with $k$ clusters:
$W_k = \sum_{j=1}^k \sum_{\xvec_i \in C_j} \norm{\xvec_i - \muvec_j}^2$. Take the best clustering with $k$ clusters and
split any cluster with at least two distinct points into two. The squared error of the split cluster cannot increase,
because each half is at least as close to its own mean as to the old mean. So $W_{k+1} \le W_k$, and $W_n = 0$ when
every point is its own cluster. The same holds for the log-likelihood of a mixture model, which never decreases as
components are added. Every criterion below counteracts this monotone improvement in one of three ways: by looking
for diminishing returns, by comparing with data that has no clusters, or by penalising complexity.

## Elbow method

Plot $W_k$ against $k$ and choose the $k$ after which the curve flattens, the "elbow" <Cite id="thorndike1953" />.
Adding a cluster that splits a true cluster reduces $W_k$ much less than adding one that separates two true
clusters. The elbow is a visual judgement and has no formal definition. On data without clear clusters the curve bends
smoothly and no $k$ stands out. The <NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> note shows the curve interactively.

## Silhouette

The silhouette of point $i$ is $s_i = (b_i - a_i)/\max(a_i, b_i)$, where $a_i$ is its mean distance to the other
points in its cluster and $b_i$ its mean distance to the nearest other cluster <Cite id="rousseeuw1987" />. The mean
silhouette over all points is highest when clusters are tight and well separated. Choose the $k$ that maximises it.
The silhouette is undefined for $k = 1$, so it cannot conclude that the data has no cluster structure. The
<NoteLink to="internal-clustering-indices">internal clustering indices</NoteLink> note covers the silhouette with the
Calinski–Harabasz, Davies–Bouldin and Dunn indices.

## Gap statistic

The gap statistic compares $\log W_k$ with its expected value under a reference distribution that has no clusters
<Cite id="tibshirani2001" />. $W_k$ falls with $k$ even for structureless data, because more centres always cover the
space more finely. The reference measures that baseline fall, and the gap measures the extra fall that only real
clusters produce:

$$
\operatorname{Gap}(k) = \expect^*\LB\log W_k^*\RB - \log W_k.
$$

Here $W_k^*$ is the within-cluster sum of squares of the same clustering method applied to a data set drawn from the
reference distribution, of the same size as the data. The usual reference is uniform over the bounding box of the data,
or over the box aligned with its <Gloss name="pca">principal components</Gloss>. The expectation is estimated by the
mean over $B$ reference data sets, typically $B = 10$ to $50$. Let $\text{sd}_k$ be the
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of the $B$ values of $\log W_k^*$, and
$s_k = \text{sd}_k\sqrt{1 + 1/B}$, which also accounts for the error of the mean. The rule is to choose the smallest $k$
with

$$
\operatorname{Gap}(k) \ge \operatorname{Gap}(k + 1) - s_{k+1}.
$$

This picks the $k$ after which the gap stops increasing by more than its noise. Unlike the silhouette, the gap
statistic can choose $k = 1$.

## Information criteria for mixture models

A probabilistic clustering such as a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> has a
likelihood, so it can use a penalised likelihood. With maximised log-likelihood $\ell_k$, $p_k$ free parameters and $n$
points,

$$
\BIC(k) = -2\ell_k + p_k \log n.
$$

Lower is better. The penalty grows with the number of parameters, so the criterion has a minimum. <Gloss name="bic" />
approximates $-2\log p(\Xmat \mid k)$, the log <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> of
the model with $k$ components, and choosing its minimum approximates choosing the most probable $k$
<Cite id="fraley2002" />. BIC answers a question about the density, not about the clusters: a single non-Gaussian
cluster may need several Gaussian components to fit well, and BIC will count them all.

## Other criteria

- **Eigengap.** In <NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">spectral clustering</NoteLink>, choose $k$ so that the first
  $k$ eigenvalues of the graph Laplacian are small and the next one is much larger.
- **Dendrogram gaps.** In <NoteLink to="hierarchical-clustering">hierarchical clustering</NoteLink>, cut where the
  heights of consecutive merges jump.
- **Stability.** Cluster many subsamples or perturbed copies of the data and measure how often pairs of points stay
  together. The true $k$ tends to give the most reproducible clusterings.
- **Density-based methods.** <NoteLink to="density-based-spatial-clustering">DBSCAN</NoteLink> does not take $k$; the
  number of clusters follows from its density threshold.

## Worked example

Take 300 points from four Gaussian blobs in the plane with standard deviation 0.8 (scikit-learn's `make_blobs` with
`random_state=2`). Two of the four blobs sit close together. For each $k$, k-means gives $W_k$, the mean silhouette and
the gap statistic ($B = 20$ uniform reference sets over the bounding box). A Gaussian mixture with full covariances
gives BIC.

| $k$ | $W_k$ | Silhouette | $\operatorname{Gap}(k)$ | $s_k$ | BIC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 7658.8 | — | 0.227 | 0.031 | 3123.3 |
| 2 | 3336.0 | 0.583 | 0.419 | 0.036 | 2673.4 |
| 3 | 760.4 | **0.718** | 1.527 | 0.028 | 2418.5 |
| 4 | 394.9 | 0.669 | 1.779 | 0.042 | **2393.7** |
| 5 | 351.6 | 0.595 | 1.673 | 0.039 | 2398.1 |
| 6 | 315.8 | 0.476 | 1.569 | 0.033 | 2422.6 |

- **Elbow.** $W_k$ drops by 77% from $k = 2$ to 3 and by 48% from 3 to 4, then by 11% from 4 to 5. The elbow lies at
  3 or 4, depending on the reader.
- **Silhouette.** It peaks at $k = 3$. Merging the two close blobs gives cleaner separation than keeping them apart.
- **Gap.** The rule first holds at $k = 4$: $\operatorname{Gap}(4) = 1.779 \ge \operatorname{Gap}(5) - s_5 = 1.673 -
  0.039 = 1.634$. At $k = 3$ it fails, since $1.527 < 1.779 - 0.042$.
- **BIC.** It is smallest at $k = 4$.

The criteria disagree because they define a cluster differently. The silhouette rewards separation; the gap
statistic and BIC reward any real reduction in spread or improvement in fit.

## Pitfalls

- **No single right answer.** The number of clusters depends on the scale of interest. Two close blobs are one cluster
  at a coarse scale and two at a fine one.
- **Criteria inherit the method's assumptions.** $W_k$, the silhouette and the gap statistic favour compact, convex
  clusters. They mislead for elongated, nested or density-defined clusters.
- **Local optima.** Every criterion assumes the clustering for each $k$ is close to optimal. Use several restarts per
  $k$.
- **Report the curve.** A table or plot of the criterion over $k$ shows how clear the choice is. A single chosen number
  hides a flat curve.
