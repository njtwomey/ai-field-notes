<Definition>
Fix an origin $t_0$ and a bin width $h > 0$, and divide the line into bins $B_k = [t_0 + kh,\, t_0 + (k + 1)h)$. For a
sample $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $\nu_k$ points in bin $B_k$, the **density histogram** is

$$
\hat f(x) = \frac{\nu_k}{nh} \qquad \text{for } x \in B_k.
$$

It is non-negative and integrates to $\sum_k \nu_k h/(nh) = 1$, so it is a density.
</Definition>

## Counts, frequencies and densities

A histogram of raw counts $\nu_k$ changes scale with $n$ and with $h$. Dividing by $n$ gives the fraction of the sample
in each bin, and dividing again by $h$ turns that fraction into a density, a probability per unit length. Only the
density scaling can be compared with a <Gloss name="pdf">probability density function</Gloss> or with histograms that use different bins.

## Bias and variance

Fix $x$ in bin $B$ with centre $c$, and let $p = \int_B f(y)\,dy$ be the probability of the bin. The count $\nu$ of
points in $B$ is <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial</NoteLink>, $\nu \sim \Binom(n, p)$, so

$$
\expect[\hat f(x)] = \frac{p}{h}, \qquad \var[\hat f(x)] = \frac{p(1 - p)}{nh^2}.
$$

**Variance.** For a small bin $p \approx hf(x)$, so $\var[\hat f(x)] \approx f(x)/(nh)$. Summed over bins, the
integrated variance is $\sum_k p_k(1 - p_k)/(nh) \approx 1/(nh)$, because $\sum_k p_k = 1$ and $\sum_k p_k^2$ is of
order $h$.

**Bias.** Expanding $f$ around the bin centre, $p/h = f(c) + O(h^2)$ and $f(x) = f(c) + f'(c)(x - c) + O(h^2)$. The
bias is $p/h - f(x) = -f'(c)(x - c) + O(h^2)$. It is linear in the distance from the centre: the estimate is flat,
the density is sloped. The squared bias integrated over one bin is
$f'(c)^2\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}u^2\,du = f'(c)^2h^3/12$. Summed over bins, this approximates
$\frac{h^2}{12}\int f'(x)^2\,dx = h^2R(f')/12$, with $R(f') = \int f'^2$.

The asymptotic mean integrated squared error is the sum

$$
\operatorname{AMISE}(h) = \frac{1}{nh} + \frac{h^2R(f')}{12}.
$$

Setting the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $-1/(nh^2) + hR(f')/6$ to zero gives <Cite id="scott1979" />

$$
h^* = \paren{\frac{6}{R(f')\,n}}^{1/3}, \qquad \operatorname{AMISE}(h^*) \propto n^{-2/3}.
$$

The optimal width shrinks as $n^{-1/3}$. The error falls as $n^{-2/3}$, more slowly than the $n^{-4/5}$ of a
<NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density estimate</NoteLink>, because the histogram's bias is first
order in $h$ instead of second order.

## Bin-width rules

- **Scott's rule.** For a Gaussian density with standard deviation $\sigma$, $R(f') = 1/(4\sqrt\pi\,\sigma^3)$. Then
  $h^* = (24\sqrt\pi)^{1/3}\sigma n^{-1/3} = 3.49\,\sigma n^{-1/3}$, with $\sigma$ estimated by the sample standard
  deviation <Cite id="scott1979" />.
- **Freedman–Diaconis rule.** $h = 2\,\text{IQR}\,n^{-1/3}$, with IQR the interquartile range
  <Cite id="freedman1981" />. The IQR is not inflated by outliers or heavy tails, so the rule is more robust than
  Scott's. For a Gaussian, $\text{IQR} = 1.349\sigma$ and the rule gives $2.70\,\sigma n^{-1/3}$, somewhat narrower
  bins.
- **Sturges' rule.** Use $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil + 1$ bins over the range of the data <Cite id="sturges1926" />. The
  number of bins grows only logarithmically with $n$, so for large samples the bins are too wide and the histogram
  oversmooths.

All three rules assume a roughly unimodal density. For multimodal data, where $R(f')$ is larger than for one Gaussian,
they choose bins that are too wide.

## Dependence on the origin

Two histograms with the same width but different origins can suggest different shapes: one mode or two, a skew to the
left or the right. The origin is an arbitrary choice that the data do not determine. The **averaged shifted
histogram** averages $m$ histograms whose origins are shifted by $h/m$. As $m$ grows it approaches a kernel density
estimate with a triangular kernel, which removes the dependence on the origin <Cite id="silverman1986" />.

## Worked example

For $n = 1000$ points from a standard Gaussian ($\sigma = 1$, $\text{IQR} = 1.349$):

- **Scott.** $h = 3.49 \times 1000^{-1/3} = 3.49/10 = 0.349$.
- **Freedman–Diaconis.** $h = 2 \times 1.349/10 = 0.270$.
- **Sturges.** $\lceil\log_2 1000\rceil + 1 = 10 + 1 = 11$ bins. A sample of 1000 standard Gaussians typically spans
  about 6.5, so each bin is about $0.6$ wide, nearly twice Scott's width.

Doubling the sample to $n = 2000$ narrows Scott's width by a factor of $2^{1/3} = 1.26$, to $0.277$.

## Pitfalls

- **Unequal bins.** With bins of different widths, the heights must be densities, $\nu_k/(nh_k)$. Plotting raw counts
  exaggerates the wide bins.
- **Edges on data values.** Rounded data put many points exactly on bin edges. Choose edges between the recorded
  values, or the assignment rule decides the shape.
- **Discontinuous estimate.** The histogram is a step function even when the density is smooth. Use a kernel density
  estimate when derivatives or a smooth curve matter.
- **Many dimensions.** The number of bins grows as $(1/h)^d$, and most are empty for moderate $d$.
