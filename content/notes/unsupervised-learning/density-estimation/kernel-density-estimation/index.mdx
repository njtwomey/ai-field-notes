<Definition>
Given a sample $x_1, \dots, x_n$ from an unknown density $f$ on $\reals$, a **kernel density estimate** is

$$
\hat f_h(x) = \frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^n K\paren{\frac{x - x_i}{h}},
$$

where the **kernel** $K$ is a symmetric probability density, usually with $\int u^2K(u)\,du = 1$, and the
**bandwidth** $h > 0$ sets its width <Cite id="rosenblatt1956" /> <Cite id="parzen1962" />. Each term is a density, so
$\hat f_h$ is a density: it is non-negative and integrates to 1.
</Definition>

## A smoothed histogram without bins

A <NoteLink to="histogram-density-estimation">histogram</NoteLink> counts points in fixed bins, so its shape depends on
where the bins start, and it is a step function. A kernel density estimate centres a bin, the kernel, on every point
instead. The estimate is as smooth as the kernel and does not depend on an origin. Common kernels are the Gaussian
$K(u) = e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$, the Epanechnikov kernel $K(u) \propto 1 - u^2$ on a bounded interval, and the box
kernel, which gives a moving histogram. The choice of kernel matters much less than the choice of bandwidth.

## Bias and variance

The estimate at a fixed $x$ is a mean of $n$ independent terms $K_h(x - x_i)$, with $K_h(u) = K(u/h)/h$. Let $\mu_2 =
\int u^2K(u)\,du$ and $R(g) = \int g(u)^2\,du$ for any function $g$.

**Bias.** Substitute $y = x - hu$ in the expectation of one term:

$$
\expect\LB\hat f_h(x)\RB = \int \frac1h K\paren{\frac{x - y}{h}}f(y)\,dy = \int K(u)\,f(x - hu)\,du.
$$

Expand $f(x - hu) = f(x) - hu\,f'(x) + \tfrac12h^2u^2 f''(x) + o(h^2)$ by
<NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor's theorem</NoteLink>. The kernel integrates to 1 and is symmetric, so
$\int uK(u)\,du = 0$. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{bias}\LB\hat f_h(x)\RB = \tfrac12h^2\mu_2 f''(x) + o(h^2).
$$

The estimate is too low at peaks, where $f'' < 0$, and too high in troughs. The bias does not depend on $n$; only a
smaller $h$ reduces it.

**Variance.** The terms are independent, so $\var[\hat f_h(x)] = \frac1n\var[K_h(x - X)] \le \frac1n\expect[K_h(x -
X)^2]$. The same substitution gives

$$
\expect\LB K_h(x - X)^2\RB = \frac1h\int K(u)^2 f(x - hu)\,du = \frac{f(x)R(K)}{h} + o(1/h),
$$

and $(\expect K_h)^2 = f(x)^2 + o(1)$ is of lower order. Therefore

$$
\var\LB\hat f_h(x)\RB = \frac{f(x)R(K)}{nh} + o\paren{\frac{1}{nh}}.
$$

The effective number of points in a window of width $h$ is about $nhf(x)$, so a smaller $h$ averages fewer points and
the estimate varies more.

## The optimal bandwidth

The mean integrated squared error, $\operatorname{MISE} = \expect\int(\hat f_h - f)^2$, is the integrated squared
bias plus the integrated variance. Integrating the leading terms over $x$ gives the asymptotic MISE:

$$
\operatorname{AMISE}(h) = \frac{h^4\mu_2^2R(f'')}{4} + \frac{R(K)}{nh}.
$$

The first term grows with $h$ and the second falls. Setting the derivative $h^3\mu_2^2R(f'') - R(K)/(nh^2)$ to zero
gives

$$
h^* = \paren{\frac{R(K)}{\mu_2^2\,R(f'')\,n}}^{1/5}, \qquad \operatorname{AMISE}(h^*) \propto n^{-4/5}.
$$

The optimal bandwidth shrinks slowly, as $n^{-1/5}$, and the error falls as $n^{-4/5}$, slower than the $n^{-1}$ of a
correctly specified parametric model. The formula depends on $R(f'')$, the roughness of the unknown density. Rougher
densities need smaller bandwidths.

## Choosing the bandwidth in practice

- **Normal reference rule.** Assume $f$ is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma$. Then
  $R(f'') = 3/(8\sqrt\pi\,\sigma^5)$. With a Gaussian kernel, $R(K) = 1/(2\sqrt\pi)$ and $\mu_2 = 1$, and the formula
  gives $h^* = (4/3)^{1/5}\sigma n^{-1/5} = 1.06\,\sigma n^{-1/5}$.
- **Silverman's rule of thumb.** Replace $\sigma$ by $\min(\hat\sigma, \text{IQR}/1.34)$, which is robust to outliers
  and heavy tails, and the constant by 0.9 <Cite id="silverman1986" />:
  $h = 0.9\min(\hat\sigma, \text{IQR}/1.34)\,n^{-1/5}$. The interquartile range of a Gaussian is $1.34\sigma$. Both
  rules oversmooth multimodal densities, whose $R(f'')$ is much larger than a single Gaussian's.
- **Plug-in methods** estimate $R(f'')$ from the data with a pilot bandwidth. The Sheather–Jones method is a reliable
  default <Cite id="sheather1991" />.
- **Cross-validation** chooses $h$ to maximise the leave-one-out log-likelihood $\sum_i\log\hat f_{h,-i}(x_i)$, or to
  minimise an unbiased estimate of the integrated squared error.

## Worked example

**One evaluation.** Data $\set{0, 1, 3}$, Gaussian kernel, $h = 1$. At $x = 1$ the three scaled distances are $1$, $0$
and $-2$, with kernel values $0.2420$, $0.3989$ and $0.0540$. Then $\hat f(1) = (0.2420 + 0.3989 + 0.0540)/3 = 0.2316$.

**Rule of thumb.** For $n = 100$ points from a standard Gaussian, the normal reference rule gives
$h = 1.06 \times 100^{-1/5} = 0.422$ and Silverman's rule gives $0.9 \times 100^{-1/5} = 0.358$.

**A bimodal density.** In the figure the density is $0.7\,\Gauss(0, 1) + 0.3\,\Gauss(3, 0.5^2)$. For the sample of
$n = 200$, Silverman's rule gives $h = 0.52$ and an integrated squared error of $0.0072$. The bandwidth with the
smallest error on that sample is $0.27$, with error $0.0029$. The rule assumes one Gaussian mode, so it smooths the
narrow second mode away.

## More than one dimension

In $d$ dimensions, $\hat f(\xvec) = \frac1n\sum_i \abs{\Hmat}^{-1/2}K\paren{\Hmat^{-1/2}(\xvec - \xvec_i)}$ with a
bandwidth matrix $\Hmat$. The optimal error rate becomes $n^{-4/(4 + d)}$, so the sample size needed for a given
accuracy grows exponentially with $d$. Kernel density estimation is practical in a few dimensions. Beyond that,
parametric models such as a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> are used.

## Pitfalls

- **One global bandwidth.** A single $h$ cannot suit a narrow peak and a broad tail at once. Adaptive estimators use a
  wider kernel where the data are sparse.
- **Boundaries.** For data on $[0, \infty)$, kernels near 0 spill mass below 0, and the estimate is biased down at the
  boundary. Reflect the data, or transform to an unbounded scale first.
- **Undersmoothing looks like detail.** Small bandwidths produce spurious bumps that are easy to over-interpret.
- **Cost.** A direct evaluation at $m$ points costs $O(nm)$. Binning with the fast Fourier transform, or tree methods,
  make large $n$ affordable.
