<Definition>
The **dynamic topic model** (DTM) is a topic model for documents grouped into ordered time slices $t = 1, \dots, T$
<Cite id="blei2006dtm" />. Topic $k$ at slice $t$ is a vector of <NoteLink to="exponential-family">natural
parameters</NoteLink> $\betavec_{t,k} \in \reals^V$, and its word distribution is
$\operatorname{softmax}(\betavec_{t,k})$. The natural parameters follow a Gaussian <NoteLink to="random-walk">random
walk</NoteLink>, $\betavec_{t,k} \mid \betavec_{t-1,k} \sim \Gauss(\betavec_{t-1,k}, \sigma^2\I)$. Within a slice,
documents are generated as in <NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)</NoteLink>,
with logistic-normal topic proportions whose mean $\alphavec_t$ also drifts.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A topic in a long-running corpus changes its vocabulary. An electronics topic in 1920 uses "valve" and "tube"; the same
topic in 1960 uses "transistor"; in 1990 it uses "chip". LDA fits one fixed word distribution per topic to the whole
corpus. That fixed distribution averages the eras and describes none of them. Fitting LDA separately to each slice gives
topics that match their era, but the topics of different slices are not aligned: topic 3 in 1920 has no relation to
topic 3 in 1960.

The DTM keeps $K$ topics throughout and lets each one move a little between consecutive slices. Topic $k$ is then a
trajectory, and its word distribution at any slice is estimated from that slice's documents and, through the chain,
from neighbouring slices.

## Generative process

With $T$ slices, $K$ topics, a vocabulary of $V$ words, drift variances $\sigma^2$ and $\delta^2$, and a
document-level variance $a^2$:

1. For each topic $k$, draw $\betavec_{1,k}$ from a broad Gaussian, then
   $\betavec_{t,k} \sim \Gauss(\betavec_{t-1,k}, \sigma^2\I)$ for $t = 2, \dots, T$.
2. Draw the mean topic proportions $\alphavec_t \sim \Gauss(\alphavec_{t-1}, \delta^2\I)$, a vector in $\reals^K$.
3. For each document $d$ in slice $t$:
   1. Draw $\etavec_d \sim \Gauss(\alphavec_t, a^2\I)$ and set $\thetavec_d = \operatorname{softmax}(\etavec_d)$.
   2. For each word position $n$, draw $z_{dn} \sim \Cat(\thetavec_d)$ and then
      $w_{dn} \sim \Cat(\operatorname{softmax}(\betavec_{t, z_{dn}}))$.

The softmax maps a vector $\betavec \in \reals^V$ to the probability vector with entries
$\exp(\beta_w)/\sum_{v}\exp(\beta_v)$.

The Dirichlet prior of LDA is replaced by Gaussians on natural parameters because a Dirichlet cannot express "close to
the previous value" with a controllable spread and remain tractable along a chain. A Gaussian random walk can: the
variance $\sigma^2$ sets how far a topic moves per slice. With $\sigma^2 = 0$ every slice shares one topic, as in a
static model with a logistic-normal prior. With $\sigma^2 \to \infty$ the slices decouple.

## Inference

The posterior over the chains is intractable, because the softmax links the Gaussian $\betavec_{t,k}$ to categorical
words. Blei and Lafferty use a <NoteLink to="variational-inference">variational approximation</NoteLink> that makes each
topic chain look like a <NoteLink to="linear-gaussian-state-space-models">linear-Gaussian state-space model</NoteLink>
<Cite id="blei2006dtm" />.

**Variational observations.** For each topic and slice, introduce free parameters $\hat\betavec_{t,k}$ and pretend that
they are noisy observations of the chain,
$\hat\betavec_{t,k} \mid \betavec_{t,k} \sim \Gauss(\betavec_{t,k}, \hat\nu^2\I)$. The variational posterior
$q(\betavec_{1:T,k})$ is defined as the exact posterior of the chain given these pseudo-data. That posterior is Gaussian
and is computed exactly by a <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink> followed by a smoother. The
pseudo-observations are then moved by <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> ascent on the
<Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower bound</Gloss>, so that the smoothed chain explains the real words.

**The forward filter.** The covariance is isotropic, so each word coordinate runs its own scalar filter. Let $m_{t-1}$
and $V_{t-1}$ be the filtered mean and variance of one coordinate $\beta_{t-1}$ given $\hat\beta_{1:t-1}$. The random
walk adds variance $\sigma^2$, so the prediction for slice $t$ is
$\beta_t \mid \hat\beta_{1:t-1} \sim \Gauss(m_{t-1}, P_t)$ with $P_t = V_{t-1} + \sigma^2$. Multiplying this prior by
the pseudo-likelihood $\Gauss(\hat\beta_t \mid \beta_t, \hat\nu^2)$ and <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">completing
the square</NoteLink> gives a Gaussian whose precision is the sum of the two precisions and whose mean is the
precision-weighted average:

$$
V_t = \paren{\frac{1}{P_t} + \frac{1}{\hat\nu^2}}^{-1} = \frac{P_t\,\hat\nu^2}{P_t + \hat\nu^2}, \qquad
m_t = \frac{\hat\nu^2}{P_t + \hat\nu^2}\,m_{t-1} + \frac{P_t}{P_t + \hat\nu^2}\,\hat\beta_t.
$$

The weight $P_t/(P_t + \hat\nu^2)$ is the Kalman gain. A large drift variance makes $P_t$ large and the gain close to
one, so the estimate follows the current slice. A small drift variance keeps the estimate near the previous slice.

**The backward smoother.** The filter uses only past slices. The <NoteLink to="kalman-smoother">Rauch–Tung–Striebel
smoother</NoteLink> adds the future. With smoothed moments $\tilde m_t$ and $\tilde V_t$ and gain
$J_{t-1} = V_{t-1}/(V_{t-1} + \sigma^2)$,

$$
\tilde m_{t-1} = m_{t-1} + J_{t-1}\paren{\tilde m_t - m_{t-1}}, \qquad
\tilde V_{t-1} = V_{t-1} + J_{t-1}^2\paren{\tilde V_t - P_t}.
$$

<Derivation title="Why the smoother has this form">
The pair $(\beta_{t-1}, \beta_t)$ given $\hat\beta_{1:t-1}$ is jointly Gaussian with means $(m_{t-1}, m_{t-1})$,
variances $(V_{t-1}, P_t)$ and covariance $V_{t-1}$, since $\beta_t = \beta_{t-1} + \epsilon$ with independent noise.
Conditioning a Gaussian on one coordinate gives
$\expect[\beta_{t-1} \mid \beta_t, \hat\beta_{1:t-1}] = m_{t-1} + (V_{t-1}/P_t)(\beta_t - m_{t-1})$, and future
pseudo-observations add no information about $\beta_{t-1}$ once $\beta_t$ is known (the chain is Markov). Averaging over
the smoothed distribution of $\beta_t$ replaces $\beta_t$ by $\tilde m_t$, which is the mean formula with
$J_{t-1} = V_{t-1}/P_t$. The conditional variance is $V_{t-1} - V_{t-1}^2/P_t$; adding the variance
$J_{t-1}^2\tilde V_t$ contributed by the uncertain $\beta_t$ gives $V_{t-1} + J_{t-1}^2(\tilde V_t - P_t)$.
</Derivation>

**The word term.** The evidence lower bound needs $\expect_q[\log p(w \mid \betavec_{t,k})]$ for each word, which
contains $\expect_q\brack{\log\sum_v \exp\beta_{t,k,v}}$. The logarithm is concave, so it lies below its tangent at any
$\zeta > 0$: $\log x \le x/\zeta - 1 + \log\zeta$. Under the Gaussian $q$,
$\expect_q[\exp\beta_v] = \exp(\tilde m_v + \tilde V_v/2)$, so

$$
\expect_q\brack{\log\sum_v e^{\beta_v}} \le \frac{1}{\zeta}\sum_v \exp\paren{\tilde m_v + \tilde V_v/2} - 1 + \log\zeta,
$$

which is tight at $\zeta = \sum_v \exp(\tilde m_v + \tilde V_v/2)$. With this bound the lower bound is a smooth function
of the pseudo-observations, and its <NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria">gradient flows</NoteLink> back through the
smoother. Document-level updates for $\etavec_d$ and the topic assignments are those of the
<NoteLink to="correlated-topic-model">correlated topic model</NoteLink> with a diagonal covariance.

## Worked example

**One filter step.** A word coordinate has filtered moments $m_{t-1} = 0$ and $V_{t-1} = 0.5$. The drift variance is
$\sigma^2 = 0.1$, the pseudo-observation variance is $\hat\nu^2 = 0.4$, and the pseudo-observation at slice $t$ is
$\hat\beta_t = 1$.

- Prediction: $P_t = 0.5 + 0.1 = 0.6$.
- Gain: $0.6/(0.6 + 0.4) = 0.6$.
- Update: $m_t = 0.4 \times 0 + 0.6 \times 1 = 0.6$ and $V_t = 0.6 \times 0.4/1.0 = 0.24$.

The estimate moves 60% of the way to the new pseudo-observation. With $\sigma^2 = 0.01$ the gain is
$0.51/0.91 = 0.56$, and the estimate moves less.

**Smoothing in the figure.** The figure replaces the fitted pseudo-observations by the log frequencies of each word in
each slice, with the delta-method variance $1/(n + \tfrac12)$ of a log count $n$. The smoothed curve is then the
filter-and-smoother output above, mapped back through the softmax. With 60 tokens per slice and $\sigma = 0.3$ the
smoothed estimate is closer to the truth on average than both the raw frequencies and the static topic.

## Continuous time

Time slices force a choice of granularity. Fine slices give many parameters and few documents per slice; coarse slices
blur changes. The **continuous-time dynamic topic model** replaces the discrete random walk with
<NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink> <Cite id="wang2008cdtm" />. Between timestamps $s_{j-1}$ and
$s_j$ the natural parameters change by a Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2(s_j - s_{j-1})$, so the prediction step of the
filter becomes $P_j = V_{j-1} + \sigma^2\,(s_j - s_{j-1})$. Documents can then carry exact timestamps, and slices with
no documents cost nothing.

## Assumptions

- **A fixed set of $K$ topics.** Topics drift but are never born or retired. A new subject must borrow an existing
  topic.
- **Smooth drift.** A Gaussian random walk favours small steps. Abrupt vocabulary shifts are smoothed over several
  slices.
- **Exchangeable documents within a slice.** Order inside a slice is ignored.

## Pitfalls

- **Drift variance.** $\sigma^2$ trades smoothness against responsiveness, as the figure shows. It is usually set by
  hand or by held-out likelihood.
- **Cost.** Each variational iteration runs a filter and smoother for every topic and every vocabulary word, which is
  $O(KVT)$ per pass on top of the per-document updates.
- **Compared with <NoteLink to="topics-over-time">topics over time</NoteLink>.** The DTM changes what a topic says.
  Topics over time keeps topics fixed and models when each topic is used. The two answer different questions.
