<Definition>
The **biterm topic model** (BTM) is a topic model for corpora of short texts such as tweets, queries and titles
<Cite id="yan2013btm" />. A **biterm** is an unordered pair of distinct word positions in the same short text. The model
has one topic mixture $\thetavec \sim \Dir(\alpha)$ for the whole corpus. Each biterm draws a topic
$z \sim \Cat(\thetavec)$, and both of its words are drawn from that topic, $w_1, w_2 \sim \Cat(\phivec_z)$.
</Definition>

## Why short texts break LDA

<NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">LDA</NoteLink> learns topics from word co-occurrence within documents. The
evidence is carried by each document's mixture $\thetavec_d$: two words go into the same topic if they appear together
in documents whose mixture favours that topic. A tweet of eight words gives little information about an eight-component
mixture, and most word pairs co-occur in only one or two texts. The posterior of $\thetavec_d$ stays close to its prior,
and topics come out noisy.

Two remedies change what the model shares:

- **One topic per text.** The Dirichlet multinomial mixture, fitted by the collapsed sampler GSDMM, assumes each short
  text has exactly one topic <Cite id="yin2014gsdmm" />. With one topic there is no per-text mixture to estimate.
- **Corpus-level co-occurrence.** BTM drops documents from the model altogether. It pools all co-occurring pairs from
  all texts and fits one mixture to them. A pair seen in a hundred tweets is a hundred pieces of evidence that its two
  words share a topic.

## Generative process

A text of $\ell$ words yields $\ell(\ell - 1)/2$ biterms. The set of biterms of the whole corpus is $\Bcal$.

1. For each topic $k$, draw $\phivec_k \sim \Dir(\beta)$.
2. Draw one corpus-wide topic mixture $\thetavec \sim \Dir(\alpha)$.
3. For each biterm $b = (w_{b1}, w_{b2}) \in \Bcal$, draw $z_b \sim \Cat(\thetavec)$, then $w_{b1} \sim \Cat(\phivec_{z_b})$
   and $w_{b2} \sim \Cat(\phivec_{z_b})$.

A biterm model generates each pair from a single topic, so both words must belong to it. This is the mixture-of-unigrams
assumption applied to pairs instead of documents: the smallest unit that still carries co-occurrence.

## The collapsed Gibbs update

Integrating out $\thetavec$ and $\phivec$ gives the same Dirichlet integrals as LDA <Cite id="griffiths2004" />, with
biterms in place of word positions and one "document". Let $n_k$ be the number of biterms assigned to topic $k$ and
$n_{kw}$ the number of word occurrences of $w$ in those biterms, so $\sum_w n_{kw} = 2n_k$. Resampling biterm $b$ with
words $w_1 \ne w_2$ adds one biterm to $n_k$ and one occurrence each to $n_{kw_1}$ and $n_{kw_2}$. The topic factor
$\prod_w\Gamma(n_{kw} + \beta)/\Gamma(2n_k + V\beta)$ gains two words, so its denominator rises by two steps:

$$
p(z_b = k \mid \zvec^{\neg b}, \Bcal) \propto \paren{n_k^{\neg b} + \alpha}\,
\frac{\paren{n_{kw_1}^{\neg b} + \beta}\paren{n_{kw_2}^{\neg b} + \beta}}
{\paren{2n_k^{\neg b} + V\beta}\paren{2n_k^{\neg b} + 1 + V\beta}}.
$$

If $w_1 = w_2$, the numerator is $(n_{kw}^{\neg b} + \beta)(n_{kw}^{\neg b} + 1 + \beta)$. Yan and co-authors write the
denominator as $(2n_k + V\beta)^2$, which agrees with the exact form to first order when topics hold many words. After
sampling, $\hat\theta_k = (n_k + \alpha)/(\abs{\Bcal} + K\alpha)$ and
$\hat\phi_{kw} = (n_{kw} + \beta)/(2n_k + V\beta)$.

## Topics of a single text

The model has no $\thetavec_d$, so a text's topic proportions are derived afterwards. Each biterm's topic posterior is
$p(z \mid b) \propto \hat\theta_z\hat\phi_{zw_1}\hat\phi_{zw_2}$. Averaging over the text's biterms, weighted by their
frequency in the text, gives

$$
p(z \mid d) = \sum_{b \in d} p(z \mid b)\,p(b \mid d).
$$

## Worked example

**Counting biterms.** A query "cheap flights to rome" has 4 words and $4 \times 3/2 = 6$ biterms, among them
(cheap, flights), (flights, rome) and (cheap, rome). A tweet of 12 words has 66.

**One Gibbs update.** There are $K = 2$ topics, $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 0.01$ and $V = 1000$, so $V\beta = 10$. Biterm
$b$ is (flights, rome). With $b$ removed, topic 1 has 100 biterms, with "flights" 10 times and "rome" 8 times; topic 2 has
150 biterms, with "flights" twice and "rome" 20 times.

- Topic 1: $101 \times 10.01 \times 8.01/(210 \times 211) = 0.183$.
- Topic 2: $151 \times 2.01 \times 20.01/(310 \times 311) = 0.063$.

Normalised, $p(z_b = 1) = 0.744$. Topic 2 favours "rome" but rarely produces "flights", and a biterm needs both words
from one topic. With the squared denominator the probability is $0.744$ to three places.

## Assumptions

- **Both words from one topic.** Every co-occurring pair is assumed topically coherent. In a long document most pairs
  are not, which is why BTM is for short texts.
- **No document-level structure.** Two texts with the same biterms are indistinguishable.

## Pitfalls

- **Quadratic cost.** The number of biterms grows with the square of text length. For longer texts, restrict biterms to
  words within a sliding window.
- **Common words.** A frequent word forms biterms with everything. Remove <NoteLink to="stop-words">stop words</NoteLink> before extracting biterms.
- **Choosing between BTM and one-topic-per-text models.** When texts are genuinely about one subject, a Dirichlet
  multinomial mixture is simpler and also clusters the texts. BTM suits texts that can touch several subjects.
