<Definition>
The **pachinko allocation model** (PAM) is a topic model whose topics are nodes of a <Gloss name="dag">directed acyclic graph</Gloss>
<Cite id="li2006pam" />. Leaves are distributions over words. Every interior node has, in each document, a distribution
over its children drawn from a Dirichlet. A word is generated by walking from the root, choosing a child at each interior
node from that node's distribution, until it reaches a leaf, and drawing the word from the leaf. In the four-level PAM, a
root points to $S$ super-topics, which point to $K$ sub-topics, which emit words.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A pachinko machine drops a ball through a field of pins, and the ball bounces left or right until it lands in a slot.
In PAM, each word falls through the topic graph in the same way. The probability of each bounce is specific to the
document.

<NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">Latent Dirichlet allocation</NoteLink> (LDA) is a graph of one level: the root points to $K$ topics, and a
single Dirichlet decides how often each is used. The Dirichlet cannot make two topics co-occur. PAM adds a level of
super-topics between the root and the topics. A super-topic is a distribution over topics, and a document that uses a
super-topic uses its topics together. A super-topic for "machine learning applications" may put weight on the topics
"neural networks", "images" and "speech", so these co-occur. Unlike the
<NoteLink to="correlated-topic-model">correlated topic model</NoteLink>, which encodes pairwise correlations in one
covariance matrix, PAM can express correlations among groups of topics, and a topic can belong to several groups.

## Generative process (four levels)

With $S$ super-topics, $K$ sub-topics, Dirichlet parameters $\alphavec_0 \in \reals^S$ for the root and
$\alphavec_s \in \reals^K$ for each super-topic $s$, and a topic prior $\beta$:

1. For each sub-topic $k$, draw $\phivec_k \sim \Dir(\beta)$ over the $V$ words.
2. For each document $d$:
   1. Draw root proportions $\thetavec_d \sim \Dir(\alphavec_0)$ over super-topics.
   2. For each super-topic $s$, draw $\thetavec_{ds} \sim \Dir(\alphavec_s)$ over sub-topics.
   3. For each word position $n$: draw a super-topic $s_{dn} \sim \Cat(\thetavec_d)$, a sub-topic
      $z_{dn} \sim \Cat(\thetavec_{d, s_{dn}})$ and a word $w_{dn} \sim \Cat(\phivec_{z_{dn}})$.

The document's effective mixture over sub-topics is $\sum_s\theta_{ds}\,\thetavec_{ds}$, a mixture of Dirichlet draws.
Its covariance structure is richer than a single Dirichlet's: sub-topics that share a super-topic rise and fall together
with that super-topic's weight.

With $S = 1$, PAM is LDA. With $S = K$ and each super-topic owning one sub-topic, it is LDA
with a redundant level. With a tree instead of a graph and one path per document, it resembles
<NoteLink to="hierarchical-latent-dirichlet-allocation">hierarchical LDA</NoteLink>, which uses a tree in which interior nodes also emit words.

## The Gibbs update

All the per-document Dirichlets are conjugate to the categorical choices below them, and the topics are conjugate to
the words. Integrating all of them out follows the LDA argument <Cite id="griffiths2004" /> once for each Dirichlet. For
word $i$ of document $d$ with type $w$, the super-topic and the sub-topic are resampled jointly:

$$
p(s_i = s, z_i = k \mid \cdot) \propto
\frac{n_{ds}^{\neg i} + \alpha_{0s}}{n_d^{\neg i} + \sum_{s'}\alpha_{0s'}}\;
\frac{n_{dsk}^{\neg i} + \alpha_{sk}}{n_{ds}^{\neg i} + \sum_{k'}\alpha_{sk'}}\;
\frac{n_{kw}^{\neg i} + \beta}{n_k^{\neg i} + V\beta}.
$$

Here $n_{ds}$ counts words of document $d$ that passed through super-topic $s$, $n_{dsk}$ counts those among them that
went on to sub-topic $k$, and $n_{kw}$ and $n_k$ are the topic–word counts. Each factor is the collapsed predictive of
one choice on the word's path: the root's choice of $s$, the super-topic's choice of $k$, and the sub-topic's choice of
$w$. The first denominator is the same for all $(s, k)$ and can be dropped; the second cannot, because it depends on $s$.
Each update costs $O(SK)$.

The Dirichlet parameters $\alphavec_s$ carry the correlation structure and must be learned. Li and McCallum estimate
them from the sampled counts by moment matching.

## Worked example

A document has $S = 2$ super-topics and $K = 3$ sub-topics, with $\alpha_{0s} = 0.5$ and $\alpha_{sk} = 0.1$. With
word $i$ removed, 6 words passed through super-topic 1, split $(5, 1, 0)$ over the sub-topics, and 4 words through
super-topic 2, split $(0, 1, 3)$. The word factors $(n_{kw} + \beta)/(n_k + V\beta)$ are $(0.02, 0.1, 0.05)$.

For $(s, k) = (1, 1)$: $6.5 \times (5.1/6.3) \times 0.02 = 0.105$. The six unnormalised values are

| | $k = 1$ | $k = 2$ | $k = 3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $s = 1$ | 0.105 | 0.113 | 0.005 |
| $s = 2$ | 0.002 | 0.115 | 0.162 |

Normalised, sub-topic 2 has probability $0.454$, and it is reached equally through either super-topic. Sub-topic 3 has
probability $0.333$, almost all through super-topic 2, which is the only context in which the document uses it.

## Assumptions

- **Fixed graph.** The structure of the directed acyclic graph and the number of nodes are chosen in advance. Nonparametric versions learn
  them.
- **Leaves emit words.** In the basic model only the lowest level has word distributions.

## Pitfalls

- **Many parameters.** Each super-topic has a Dirichlet over all sub-topics, $SK$ parameters in total, and they are
  estimated from counts that are themselves being sampled.
- **Identifiability.** Super-topics are as exchangeable as topics, so they suffer the same label switching.
