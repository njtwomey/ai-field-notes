<Definition>
**Activity discovery with topic models** treats the event stream of a smart home's environmental sensors as a corpus
<Cite id="chen2016adltm" />. A **word** is one sensor event, the pair of a sensor's identity and its reading, so $M$
binary sensors give $2M$ words. A **document** is a segment of the stream, cut where the resident changes location. A
**topic** is a distribution over sensor words, and a topic that explains many segments corresponds to an activity of
daily living such as sleeping, cooking or using the toilet. Chen, Diethe and Flach's ADL™ model gives each segment a
single topic, which generates both the segment's events and the transitions between consecutive events.
</Definition>

## Why topic models suit sensor homes

Smart homes built for health monitoring, such as those of the SPHERE project <Cite id="twomey2016sphere" />, record
motion sensors, door contacts, pressure mats and appliance switches. These sensors are cheap and less intrusive than
cameras. Their events are discrete tokens, and an activity shows as a characteristic mix of them: cooking fires the
fridge door, the hob and the kitchen motion sensor in some order and some proportion. That is the structure a topic model
looks for: groups of tokens that co-occur within units.

Supervised activity recognition needs labelled data, which is costly to collect in a real home and never covers every
activity a resident performs. A topic model needs no labels. It finds the recurring patterns, and a person names them
afterwards, or uses them directly: the durations and times of a "bedroom" pattern are a record of sleep whether or not
anyone labelled a night as sleep.

## Words from sensors

Each event is a timestamp, a sensor identifier and a reading, such as "M007 ON" at 00:04:09. The word is the
concatenation of the identifier and the reading, so "M007 ON" and "M007 OFF" are different words. Continuous sensors such
as thermometers reflect the environment more than the resident and are left out. For $M$ binary sensors the vocabulary
has $2M$ words, a few dozen in a typical home against tens of thousands in a text corpus. The vocabulary is small, so
each topic is estimated from many tokens per word.

Other quantities can be tokens too. Chen and co-authors note that the hour of day at each event could be a second,
parallel stream of words, so that topics also learn when activities happen.

## Documents from segmentation

Sensor data have no natural documents. Fixed sliding windows are the usual choice for supervised learning, but
consecutive windows share almost all their events, and a topic model would treat them as independent documents. Instead,
the stream is cut at changes of location, because each room is associated with a small set of activities.

Moving between rooms is not always a change of activity. A person cooking steps into the hall and back. The
segmentation therefore imposes a minimum duration $t_{\text{th}}$:

1. Walk through the stream one location run at a time, where a run is a maximal sequence of consecutive events in the
   same room.
2. At the end of each run, if the current document has lasted longer than $t_{\text{th}}$, close it and start a new one
   at the next run. Otherwise keep extending it into the next room.

The threshold trades two errors. A short threshold gives pure documents, one activity each, but splits one occurrence of
an activity into many fragments. A long threshold gives fewer fragments but merges different activities into one
document. Chen and co-authors measure the first by the segmentation error, the fraction of events in each document that
do not belong to its dominant activity, and the second by the fragment ratio, the number of documents per occurrence of
an activity.

## The ADL™ model

An activity occupies the whole of a well-segmented document. The model therefore uses a **mixture**, not LDA's
admixture: each document $d$ has one topic $z_d$. Two sequences are generated from it:

- **Unigrams** $t_{d1}, \dots, t_{dM_d}$, a bag of sensor words drawn independently from the topic's distribution
  $\nuvec_{z_d}$ over $H$ unigram types.
- **Bigrams**, the sequence $w_{d1}, \dots, w_{dN_d}$ read as a first-order Markov chain: each $w_{dn}$ is drawn from
  row $w_{d,n-1}$ of the topic's $V \times V$ transition matrix $\Phimat_{z_d}$.

The generative process, with a corpus-level $\thetavec$:

1. Draw $\thetavec \sim \Dir(\alpha)$ over $K$ topics.
2. For each topic $k$, draw $\nuvec_k \sim \Dir(\gamma)$, and each row of $\Phimat_k$ from $\Dir(\beta)$.
3. For each document $d$: draw $z_d \sim \Cat(\thetavec)$, then each unigram $t_{dm} \sim \Cat(\nuvec_{z_d})$ and each
   bigram $w_{dn} \sim \Cat(\Phimat_{z_d}[w_{d,n-1}, \cdot])$ for $n \ge 2$.

Bigrams matter because order carries information. Moving from the fridge to the hob says more about cooking than either
event alone. The bigram part is Wallach's bigram topic model <Cite id="wallach2006" /> restricted to one topic per
document.

## Collapsed Gibbs sampling

Integrating out $\thetavec$, $\nuvec$ and $\Phimat$ leaves only the document topics. Resampling $z_d$ multiplies three
factors, by Bayes' theorem:

$$
p(z_d = k \mid \zvec_{\neg d}, \cdot) \propto p(z_d = k \mid \zvec_{\neg d})\;p(\tvec_d \mid z_d = k, \cdot)\;
p(\wvec_d \mid z_d = k, \cdot).
$$

**The topic prior.** Collapsing the Dirichlet over $\thetavec$ gives the Pólya urn: with $C_k^{\neg d}$ other documents
in topic $k$, $p(z_d = k \mid \zvec_{\neg d}) \propto C_k^{\neg d} + \alpha$.

**The unigrams.** The whole bag moves at once, so its probability is the Dirichlet-multinomial predictive of $M_d$ new
draws. Adding the tokens one at a time, the $m$-th token $h$ has predictive probability equal to its count so far, plus
$\gamma$, over the total so far, plus $H\gamma$:

$$
p(\tvec_d \mid z_d = k, \cdot) = \prod_{m=1}^{M_d}\frac{C_{hk}^{\neg d} + c_{d,<m}(h) + \gamma}{C_{\cdot k}^{\neg d} + (m - 1) + H\gamma},
\qquad h = t_{dm},
$$

where $C_{hk}^{\neg d}$ counts unigram $h$ in topic $k$ outside document $d$, and $c_{d,<m}(h)$ counts it among the
document's first $m - 1$ tokens. The bigram factor is the same product over transitions, with counts of $j \to i$ in
the numerator and counts of $j \to \text{anything}$ in the denominator, for each row $j$ of $\Phimat_k$.

Chen and co-authors write both factors with only the counts from other documents, dropping $c_{d,<m}$ and $m - 1$. That
approximation treats the tokens of the document as independent given the other documents' counts. It is close when the
document is short relative to the topic's counts, and it understates repeated words otherwise, as the worked example
shows. The figure below uses the exact form.

**Self-transitions.** Most consecutive sensor events come from the same sensor: "M007 ON" is followed by "M007 OFF".
These self-transitions dominate the bigram counts and say little about the activity. Chen and co-authors count bigrams
by document frequency, the number of documents containing each transition, rather than the number of occurrences. A
transition that fires hundreds of times in one document then counts once, and transitions between different sensors
keep their weight. Unigrams are counted by term frequency.

## What the topics are used for

Once every document has a topic, the stream is compressed into triples of start time, duration and topic. In one of the
homes studied, about 130,000 events became about 2,800 segments <Cite id="chen2016adltm" />. Simple analyses on the
triples then answer care questions:

- **Sleep.** The segments of a bedroom topic that last longer than an hour and start at night give sleep times and
  durations. The frequency of short bathroom segments between them gives night-time toilet visits.
- **Sub-activities.** A kitchen topic covers breakfast, lunch and dinner. <NoteLink to="k-means">$k$-means</NoteLink> on
  the start times of its segments separates the three meals.
- **Anomalies.** A $z$-score on segment durations within a topic flags unusual episodes, such as a kitchen appliance
  switched on and left for two hours with no other activity.

## Worked example

**Exact against approximate unigram probability.** A topic has 10 unigram tokens outside document $d$, among them
"bed ON" twice and "bed OFF" twice. The vocabulary has $H = 28$ words and $\gamma = 5/H = 0.179$, so $H\gamma = 5$.
Document $d$ is "bed ON", "bed OFF", "bed ON", "bed OFF".

- Exact: $\frac{2.179}{15} \cdot \frac{2.179}{16} \cdot \frac{3.179}{17} \cdot \frac{3.179}{18} = 6.53 \times 10^{-4}$.
  The second "bed ON" sees one earlier copy in the document, so its numerator is $3.179$, and every token raises the
  denominator by one.
- Approximate: $(2.179/15)^4 = 4.45 \times 10^{-4}$.

The exact probability is 1.47 times larger. Repeated sensor events are the norm in a segment, and the exact marginal
rewards a topic for explaining a repeated word once it has seen it, which the approximation does not.

**Segmentation.** In the figure's simulated home, a threshold of zero makes one document per room visit, about 15 a day.
Raising the threshold to 30 minutes reduces the count to about 9 a day, and the share of events whose topic matches
their activity falls, because documents begin to span two activities.

## Assumptions

- **One activity per segment.** The mixture assumption is right only when the segmentation is. A document that spans
  two activities is forced into one topic.
- **Location signals activity.** Segmentation relies on activities being tied to rooms. Activities that move through
  the house, such as cleaning, are split.
- **Single resident.** Two people generate interleaved streams that neither the segmentation nor the one-topic model
  separates.

## Pitfalls

- **Topics are rooms first.** With location-based documents, topics tend to mirror rooms. Two activities in one room,
  such as sleeping and dressing, separate only when they use different sensors or different transitions.
- **Choosing $K$.** Chen and co-authors choose $K$ larger than the number of locations. Too large a $K$ leaves topics
  that no document uses after convergence, which is harmless; too small a $K$ merges activities.
- **Naming topics.** Mapping a topic to a named activity still needs some labelled data or a person's inspection of the
  topic's sensors and timing. <NoteLink to="context-modulation-and-sensor-transfer">Sensor context</NoteLink> and
  learned sensor topologies <Cite id="twomey2017topologies" /> are complementary ways of injecting the structure of the
  home.
