<Definition>
The **admixture model** of STRUCTURE infers population structure from multilocus genotypes <Cite id="pritchard2000" />.
There are $K$ ancestral populations, each with its own allele frequencies $\pvec_{k\ell}$ at every locus $\ell$. Each
individual $i$ has ancestry proportions $\qvec_i \sim \Dir(\alpha)$. Each of the individual's two allele copies at each
locus first draws a population of origin $z \sim \Cat(\qvec_i)$, and then an allele from $\pvec_{z\ell}$. With
individuals as documents, allele copies as words and populations as topics, this is
<NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">latent Dirichlet allocation</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## The problem

A genotype records, for each of $L$ loci, the two alleles that an individual carries, one from each parent. Human groups
that were separated for many generations differ in allele frequencies. Given genotypes and no labels, population
genetics asks how many ancestral populations there were, what their allele frequencies were, and how much of each
individual's genome descends from each one. An individual with parents from two populations has about half of their
allele copies from each: they are **admixed**.

## The model

With $N$ individuals, $L$ loci, $J_\ell$ alleles at locus $\ell$ and $K$ populations:

1. For each population $k$ and locus $\ell$, draw allele frequencies $\pvec_{k\ell} \sim \Dir(\lambda, \dots, \lambda)$
   over the $J_\ell$ alleles.
2. For each individual $i$, draw ancestry proportions $\qvec_i \sim \Dir(\alpha, \dots, \alpha)$ over the $K$
   populations.
3. For each locus $\ell$ and each copy $a \in \set{1, 2}$, draw the population of origin
   $z_{\ell a}^{(i)} \sim \Cat(\qvec_i)$, then the observed allele $x_{\ell a}^{(i)} \sim \Cat(\pvec_{z_{\ell a}^{(i)}\ell})$.

Pritchard, Stephens and Donnelly also give a model without admixture, in which each individual comes wholly from one
population, $z^{(i)} \sim \Cat(\qvec)$ for all its loci. That is a mixture model of genotypes, the analogue of a mixture
of unigrams for text.

## The correspondence with LDA

| LDA | STRUCTURE |
| --- | --- |
| document $d$ | individual $i$ |
| word position $n$ | allele copy $(\ell, a)$ |
| topic $k$ | ancestral population $k$ |
| topic proportions $\thetavec_d$ | ancestry proportions $\qvec_i$ |
| topic–word distribution $\phivec_k$ | allele frequencies $\pvec_{k1}, \dots, \pvec_{kL}$ |
| topic assignment $z_{dn}$ | population of origin $z_{\ell a}^{(i)}$ |

One difference is the vocabulary. In text, every word position draws from the same vocabulary of $V$ words. In a
genotype, position $(\ell, a)$ draws from the alleles of locus $\ell$ only, and a population is a separate distribution
at each locus. A population is therefore a product of $L$ small <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical distributions</NoteLink> rather than one large one.
Formally, STRUCTURE is LDA in which the vocabulary is the disjoint union of all loci's alleles, and each word position is
constrained to its locus's part. The other difference is that documents all have the same length, $2L$.

STRUCTURE was published in 2000, three years before LDA <Cite id="blei2003" />. The two models were developed
independently, for genotypes and for text, and share their generative process.

## Inference

Pritchard and co-authors use a <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampler</NoteLink> that keeps $\qvec$ and $\pvec$ rather than integrating them out. Each full
conditional follows from <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> and Dirichlet–categorical conjugacy:

- **Origins.** Given $\qvec_i$ and $\pvec$, each allele copy is independent:
  $p(z_{\ell a}^{(i)} = k \mid \cdot) \propto q_{ik}\,p_{k\ell x}$, where $x = x_{\ell a}^{(i)}$ is the observed allele.
- **Allele frequencies.** Given the origins, the alleles assigned to population $k$ at locus $\ell$ are categorical
  draws from $\pvec_{k\ell}$, so $\pvec_{k\ell} \mid \cdot \sim \Dir(\lambda + n_{k\ell 1}, \dots, \lambda + n_{k\ell J_\ell})$,
  where $n_{k\ell j}$ counts copies of allele $j$ at locus $\ell$ assigned to population $k$.
- **Ancestry.** Given the origins, $\qvec_i \mid \cdot \sim \Dir(\alpha + m_{i1}, \dots, \alpha + m_{iK})$, where
  $m_{ik}$ counts the individual's $2L$ copies assigned to population $k$.

Integrating $\qvec$ and $\pvec$ out gives the collapsed sampler of LDA, with counts per locus in the topic factor.

**Choosing $K$.** Pritchard and co-authors approximate the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> $p(\Xmat \mid K)$ from the sampler's
output and compare values of $K$. The approximation is rough, and in practice $K$ is chosen by combining it with the
biological interpretation, as the number of topics is in text.

## Worked example

**One origin update.** An individual has current ancestry proportions $\qvec_i = (0.7, 0.3)$. At a biallelic locus,
population 1 carries allele 2 with frequency $0.1$ and population 2 with frequency $0.8$. The individual carries allele 2.

- Population 1: $0.7 \times 0.1 = 0.07$.
- Population 2: $0.3 \times 0.8 = 0.24$.

Normalised, $p(z = 2) = 0.774$. The allele is most likely inherited from population 2 even though most of the genome is
from population 1, because allele 2 is eight times more frequent there.

**Ancestry update.** With $L = 100$ loci and $\alpha = 1$, suppose 130 of the individual's 200 copies are assigned to
population 1 and 70 to population 2. The ancestry proportions are then drawn from $\Dir(131, 71)$, whose mean is
$(0.649, 0.351)$ and whose <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> per coordinate is about $0.034$.

## Assumptions

- **Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium within populations.** The two copies at a locus are independent draws given their
  origins.
- **Linkage equilibrium.** Loci are independent given origins. Nearby loci on a chromosome are inherited together,
  which the basic model ignores. The linkage model lets origins along a chromosome follow a <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="falush2003" />.
- **Exchangeable individuals.** Relatives in the sample violate this and can appear as a spurious population.

## Pitfalls

- **$K$ is a model choice.** The inferred populations are the best $K$-component explanation of the data, not
  necessarily real historical groups. Continuous variation with distance, for example, is split into arbitrary clusters.
- **Label switching.** Population labels are arbitrary across runs, as topic labels are.
- **Uneven sampling.** A heavily sampled group tends to get its own cluster, and a sparsely sampled one tends to be
  absorbed into a neighbour.
