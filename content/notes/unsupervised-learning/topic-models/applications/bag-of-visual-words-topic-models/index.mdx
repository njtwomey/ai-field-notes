<Definition>
A **topic model for images** treats an image as a document of **visual words**. Local patches are extracted and
described by vectors, and each descriptor is replaced by the index of its nearest centre in a codebook learned by
<NoteLink to="k-means">$k$-means</NoteLink>. The image is then a bag of discrete tokens, and
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-latent-semantic-analysis">pLSA</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">LDA</NoteLink> finds **themes**: distributions over visual words that
recur across images, such as foliage, sky or the parts of an object category.
</Definition>

## Visual words

Text arrives in discrete words. Images do not, so a vocabulary must be built. The construction was introduced for
retrieving objects in video <Cite id="sivic2003" />:

1. **Patches.** Take many small regions of each image, either on an evenly spaced grid or around points found by an
   interest detector.
2. **Descriptors.** Describe each patch by a vector that is robust to small changes of viewpoint and lighting, such as
   the 128-dimensional SIFT descriptor <Cite id="lowe2004" />; see
   <NoteLink to="feature-detection-and-descriptors">feature detection and descriptors</NoteLink>.
3. **Codebook.** Cluster a large sample of descriptors from training images with $k$-means into $V$ centres. Each
   centre is a visual word.
4. **Quantisation.** Map every descriptor to its nearest centre. The image becomes a list of word indices and, ignoring
   position, a vector of $V$ counts: a <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink>.

<VisualWordsPipeline />

A visual word is not a word in the linguistic sense. It is a cell of a partition of descriptor space. Two patches of the
same texture can fall on either side of a boundary between cells, and one cell can contain patches from unrelated
objects. These are the visual analogues of synonymy and polysemy, and they are the reason that topic models, which
group co-occurring words, help: a theme gathers the several words that the same material produces.

## Discovering objects without labels

Sivic and co-authors applied pLSA to bags of visual words from unlabelled images of several object categories
<Cite id="sivic2005" />. The topics that emerged corresponded largely to the categories, such as faces, motorbikes and
aeroplanes, with a separate topic for background clutter. Because every visual word comes from a known patch, the
posterior topic of each word shows where in the image a topic is present. Colouring the patches by their most likely
topic segments the object from the background, without any object having been labelled.

## Scene categories: the theme model

Fei-Fei and Perona used LDA to classify natural scenes <Cite id="feifei2005" />. Their Theme Model 1 adds an observed
category $c_d$ for each training image. Each category has its own Dirichlet parameters $\alphavec_c$ over $K$ shared
themes, and the themes $\phivec_k$ are distributions over the $V$ codewords:

1. Choose a category $c_d$.
2. Draw the image's theme mixture $\thetavec_d \sim \Dir(\alphavec_{c_d})$.
3. For each patch $n$, draw a theme $z_{dn} \sim \Cat(\thetavec_d)$ and a codeword $x_{dn} \sim \Cat(\phivec_{z_{dn}})$.

A category is thus a characteristic mix of shared themes. A mountain scene mixes rock, sky and snow themes; a coast
scene mixes water, sand and sky. The same sky theme serves both.

**Classification.** By Bayes' theorem with a uniform prior over categories,
$p(c \mid \xvec) \propto p(\xvec \mid c)$, where $p(\xvec \mid c)$ is the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> of the image's codewords
under category $c$'s Dirichlet, with $\thetavec$ and $\zvec$ integrated out. That integral is intractable, as in LDA,
and is replaced by the <NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">variational lower bound</NoteLink>. The image is assigned to the category with the largest bound. On 13
scene categories, where chance is 7.7%, the model classified 64% of test images correctly <Cite id="feifei2005" />.

## Worked example

**Document length.** An image of $250 \times 300$ pixels sampled on a grid with 10-pixel spacing has
$25 \times 30 = 750$ patches, so it is a document of 750 visual words.

**Quantisation.** A two-dimensional descriptor $\xvec = (1, 2)$ and three centres $(0, 0)$, $(2, 2)$ and $(1, 4)$ have
squared distances $5$, $1$ and $4$. The descriptor becomes visual word 2.

**Classification.** Three categories give log marginal likelihood bounds of $-1510$, $-1502$ and $-1520$ for an image.
With a uniform prior the posterior probabilities are proportional to $e^{-8}$, $1$ and $e^{-18}$, which normalise to
$0.0003$, $0.9997$ and $1.5 \times 10^{-8}$. Summed over hundreds of patches, small per-word differences produce a
near-certain decision.

## Assumptions

- **Bag of words.** The position of each patch is discarded. A face and a scrambled face have the same bag. Spatial
  extensions add patch coordinates to the model.
- **A fixed, discrete vocabulary.** The quality of the topics is bounded by the codebook: too few centres merge
  distinct patches, too many split one appearance across words.
- **Exchangeable patches.** Overlapping patches from a dense grid are strongly dependent, which the model treats as
  independent evidence.

## Pitfalls

- **Overconfidence.** As the worked example shows, treating hundreds of dependent patches as independent makes
  posteriors near-certain even when the classifier is wrong.
- **Codebook from training data.** The codebook is learned on training descriptors, and test images from another
  distribution quantise poorly.
- **Superseded for accuracy.** Learned convolutional features now outperform bags of visual words for
  <NoteLink to="image-classification">image classification</NoteLink>. The topic-model view remains useful where the
  aim is unsupervised discovery of recurring parts, and as a clear example of how LDA applies to any data that can be
  tokenised.
