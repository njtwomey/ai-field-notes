<Definition>
**Routine discovery with topic models** applies <NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">LDA</NoteLink> to the output
of wearable sensors <Cite id="huynh2008" />. A classifier or a clustering of short windows of acceleration turns the
signal into a stream of discrete tokens, one per fraction of a second. The tokens in a sliding window of about 30 minutes form a
document. Each topic is a distribution over tokens, an **activity pattern**, and the topic proportions of successive
windows describe which patterns make up each part of the day: commuting, office work, lunch, dinner.
</Definition>

## Two time scales

Activities such as walking, sitting or queuing are visible in a few seconds of acceleration. Routines such as office
work or a lunch break last from tens of minutes to hours, and they are not defined by any one movement. Office work is
mostly sitting at a desk, with some walking, standing and talking. Lunch is walking, queuing, picking up food and sitting
to eat. A routine is a characteristic mixture of short activities, and different instances of one routine contain the
same activities in different orders and amounts.

That is the structure of a topic: a distribution over words that recurs in different proportions across documents. A
document of 30 minutes of tokens is a mixture of the patterns active during that half hour. LDA finds the patterns and
infers their mixture for every window, without routine labels.

## Words and documents

**Words from a classifier.** Huynh, Fritz and Schiele trained a <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes classifier</NoteLink> on labelled short activities,
using the mean and variance of acceleration at the wrist and in a pocket, plus the time of day <Cite id="huynh2008" />.
Each window of a fraction of a second gets a posterior probability for each activity. The words are the activity labels.

**Words without labels.** The same pipeline works with no activity labels at all. The feature vectors are clustered by
<NoteLink to="k-means">$k$-means</NoteLink> with $N$ clusters, and the words are cluster indices, as <NoteLink to="bag-of-visual-words-topic-models">visual words</NoteLink> are
cluster indices of image patches.

**Soft counts.** Hard labels propagate every classifier error into the documents. Instead, each sample contributes
fractional weights to several words: the classifier's posteriors, or for clusters, weights that decay with the distance
$d_j$ to each centre,

$$
\omega_j = \frac{\exp(-d_j/\sigma)}{\sum_{j'}\exp(-d_{j'}/\sigma)},
$$

where $\sigma$ controls how quickly weight falls off. Summing the weights over a window and rounding gives the word
counts of the document. A sample that lies between two clusters contributes to both.

**Documents.** Windows of 30 minutes, shifted by 2.5 minutes, give one document per time step. Consecutive documents
overlap heavily. This is deliberate: the document at each time is a summary of the preceding half hour, and its topic
proportions form a smooth trace over the day.

## What the topics show

On one person's recordings, with ten topics and a small Dirichlet parameter $\alpha = 0.01$ that favours few topics per
window, several topics aligned with annotated routines <Cite id="huynh2008" />. Two desk-activity topics were active
during office work. Lunch split into two topics, one for the canteen (walking, queuing, picking up food, eating) and one
for a visit to a café afterwards. A dinner topic contained eating and washing dishes. Commuting appeared as a combination
of topics rather than a single one. One topic matched no annotated routine and turned out to be a colleague's
presentation: a genuine pattern the annotation scheme had not anticipated.

To recognise routines, the topic proportions of each window become a feature vector, and a nearest-neighbour classifier
trained on a few annotated days maps them to routine labels. The topic representation reduces the recognition problem
from the raw sensor stream to a $T$-dimensional vector per window.

## Worked example

**Soft cluster weights.** A sample lies at distances $0.5$, $1.0$ and $2.0$ from three cluster centres, and
$\sigma = 0.5$. The unnormalised weights are $e^{-1} = 0.368$, $e^{-2} = 0.135$ and $e^{-4} = 0.018$, which normalise
to $0.705$, $0.259$ and $0.035$. The sample contributes most of a token to cluster 1 and a quarter of a token to
cluster 2.

**Window size.** With accelerometer features every 0.4 seconds, a 30-minute document spans $30 \times 60/0.4 = 4500$
samples. With a 2.5-minute shift, a day of 12 recorded hours gives $12 \times 60/2.5 = 288$ documents.

## Compared with the smart-home model

Both this approach and <NoteLink to="smart-home-activity-discovery">activity discovery in smart homes</NoteLink> turn
sensor streams into documents, but they make opposite choices:

- **Documents.** Here, fixed overlapping windows; in the smart home, segments cut at changes of location.
- **Topics per document.** Here, an admixture: a window mixes several patterns. In the smart home, a mixture: a
  segment is one activity.
- **Level.** Here, topics sit above activities and describe routines. In the smart home, topics are the activities.

The choice follows from the length of the document relative to the phenomenon. A document longer than one activity
needs an admixture; a document cut to fit one activity does not.

## Assumptions

- **Order within a window is ignored.** A routine is a bag of activities. Walking then sitting and sitting then walking
  give the same document.
- **Independent documents.** Overlapping windows are treated as independent, although they share most of their
  tokens.
- **A good token stream.** Topics are only as good as the tokens. Classifier confusions between similar activities
  appear inside topics.

## Pitfalls

- **Window length.** A window much shorter than a routine sees only part of it; a much longer window mixes routines.
  Thirty minutes suits routines of tens of minutes to hours.
- **One subject.** Routines learned for one person need not transfer to another.
- **Rare routines.** A pattern that occurs once may not form its own topic, or may take one that a common routine needs.
