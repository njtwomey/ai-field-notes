<Definition>
**W-LDA** is a topic model trained as a Wasserstein <NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink> <Cite id="nan2019wlda" /> <Cite id="tolstikhin2018wae" />.
An encoder network maps a document's <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> $\wvec_d$ to topic proportions $\thetavec_d =
\operatorname{softmax}(f_{\phivec}(\wvec_d))$, deterministically. A decoder maps $\thetavec_d$ to a word distribution
$\hat\wvec_d = \operatorname{softmax}(\Bmat^\top\thetavec_d + \bvec)$, with $\Bmat \in \reals^{K \times V}$. Training
minimises

$$
\frac1D\sum_{d=1}^D c\paren{\wvec_d, \hat\wvec_d} + \lambda\,\operatorname{MMD}_k\paren{Q_\Theta, \Dir(\alphavec)},
$$

where $c(\wvec, \hat\wvec) = -\sum_v w_v\log\hat w_v$ is the reconstruction cross-entropy, $Q_\Theta$ is the
distribution of the encoded $\thetavec_d$ over the whole corpus, and $\operatorname{MMD}_k$ is the <Gloss name="mmd">maximum mean
discrepancy</Gloss> with a kernel $k$ on the simplex.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational autoencoder</NoteLink> penalises the KL divergence from each
document's posterior $q(\thetavec \mid \wvec_d)$ to the prior. Every document is pulled towards the prior separately.
That per-document pull is what drives component collapse: an encoder output that matches the prior pays no KL cost, so a
latent coordinate that is not yet useful is pushed to carry no information.

A Wasserstein autoencoder asks for less. Only the **aggregate** of the encodings, the distribution of $\thetavec_d$ as
$d$ ranges over the corpus, must look like the prior. Individual documents can be encoded to sharp, specific points,
as long as the cloud of all points has the prior's shape. For a topic model with a sparse Dirichlet prior, the
requirement reads: across the corpus, most documents should use few topics, and the topics should be used in the
proportions the prior implies.

## Where the objective comes from

Tolstikhin et al. showed that for a decoder $g$ that maps a latent code deterministically to the data space, the <NoteLink to="optimal-transport">optimal
transport</NoteLink> cost between the data distribution $P_W$ and the model's distribution can be written as an optimisation over
encoders <Cite id="tolstikhin2018wae" />:

$$
W_c(P_W, P_G) = \inf_{Q(\thetavec \mid \wvec):\; Q_\Theta = P_\Theta}\;\expect_{P_W}\expect_{Q(\thetavec \mid
\wvec)}\brack{c\paren{\wvec, g(\thetavec)}} ,
$$

where $Q_\Theta = \expect_{P_W}[Q(\thetavec \mid \wvec)]$ is the aggregate posterior and $P_\Theta$ the prior. The
constraint $Q_\Theta = P_\Theta$ is replaced by a penalty $\lambda D(Q_\Theta, P_\Theta)$ for a divergence $D$ between
distributions that can be estimated from samples. W-LDA takes $P_\Theta = \Dir(\alphavec)$, the cross-entropy as the
cost $c$, and the maximum mean discrepancy as $D$ <Cite id="nan2019wlda" />. See
<NoteLink to="wasserstein-distances">Wasserstein distances</NoteLink> for the transport cost itself.

## Maximum mean discrepancy on the simplex

For a kernel $k$ with feature map $\varphi$ into a <NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space">reproducing kernel
Hilbert space</NoteLink> $\Hcal$, the maximum mean discrepancy between distributions $Q$ and $P$ is the distance between
their mean embeddings <Cite id="gretton2012" />:

$$
\operatorname{MMD}_k(Q, P) = \norm{\expect_{\thetavec \sim Q}[\varphi(\thetavec)] - \expect_{\thetavec' \sim
P}[\varphi(\thetavec')]}_{\Hcal} .
$$

Expanding the squared norm as an <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner product</NoteLink> and using $\inner{\varphi(\thetavec)}{\varphi(\thetavec')} =
k(\thetavec, \thetavec')$,

$$
\operatorname{MMD}_k^2 = \expect_{Q,Q}[k(\thetavec, \tilde\thetavec)] + \expect_{P,P}[k(\thetavec',
\tilde\thetavec')] - 2\,\expect_{Q,P}[k(\thetavec, \thetavec')] .
$$

With $m$ encoded documents $\thetavec_1, \dots, \thetavec_m$ from a minibatch and $m$ prior draws $\thetavec'_1,
\dots, \thetavec'_m$, replacing each expectation by an average over distinct pairs gives an unbiased estimate:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{MMD}}^2 = \frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i \ne j}k(\thetavec_i, \thetavec_j) + \frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i
\ne j}k(\thetavec'_i, \thetavec'_j) - \frac{2}{m^2}\sum_{i,j}k(\thetavec_i, \thetavec'_j) .
$$

No density of $Q_\Theta$ is needed, only samples. This is why the encoder can be deterministic.

**The kernel.** A Gaussian kernel on Euclidean distance treats the simplex as flat, but sparse vectors near its faces
matter most for topic models. W-LDA uses the **information diffusion kernel**, based on the <NoteLink to="isomap">geodesic distance</NoteLink> after the
map $\theta_k \mapsto \sqrt{\theta_k}$, which sends the simplex onto a piece of the unit sphere
<Cite id="nan2019wlda" />:

$$
k(\thetavec, \thetavec') = \exp\paren{-\arccos^2\paren{\sum_{k=1}^K\sqrt{\theta_k\theta'_k}}} .
$$

The sum inside the arccosine is the Bhattacharyya coefficient of the two distributions, the cosine of the angle between
their square-root vectors. The geodesic distance is more sensitive near the simplex's boundary than the Euclidean
distance, which suits sparse topic proportions.

## Training details that matter

- **Dirichlet noise.** Before decoding, W-LDA mixes the encoding with a fresh prior draw, $\thetavec_d^+ = (1 -
  \eta)\thetavec_d + \eta\,\thetavec^{\text{noise}}$ with $\thetavec^{\text{noise}} \sim \Dir(\alphavec)$, and decodes
  $\thetavec_d^+$. Nan et al. found that $\eta = 0.5$ gave markedly more coherent topics than no noise. Sparse
  Dirichlet noise leaves the large entries of $\thetavec_d$ large, so it does not wash out the signal; it spreads the
  inputs the decoder sees over the simplex <Cite id="nan2019wlda" />.
- **Scaling the reconstruction.** The cross-entropy of a document of length $s$ is of order $s\log V$, far larger than
  the MMD. W-LDA divides it by $s\log V$, its value when the decoder is uniform and the document repeats one word, so
  that $\lambda$ does not need tuning per corpus.
- **Top words.** Topic $k$ is described by the largest entries of row $k$ of $\Bmat$, the decoder's response to
  $\thetavec = \evec_k$.
- **MMD rather than an adversary.** The Wasserstein autoencoder also admits an adversarial divergence, but Nan et al.
  could not train it on Dirichlet priors: the discriminator's <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> vanished, and the encoder stopped matching the
  prior.

## Worked example

**One kernel value.** For $\thetavec = (0.8, 0.1, 0.1)$ and $\thetavec' = (0.1, 0.8, 0.1)$, the Bhattacharyya
coefficient is $\sqrt{0.08} + \sqrt{0.08} + \sqrt{0.01} = 0.666$. Its arccosine is $0.842$ radians, and
$k = \exp(-0.842^2) = 0.492$.

**What the MMD detects.** Draw $m = 500$ prior samples from $\Dir(0.1, \dots, 0.1)$ with $K = 10$ and compare three
sets of 500 encodings with the unbiased estimate.

- Encodings drawn from the same prior: $\widehat{\operatorname{MMD}}^2 = 4 \times 10^{-5}$, zero up to sampling
  error.
- Every document encoded to the uniform vector $(0.1, \dots, 0.1)$, a collapsed encoder: $0.45$.
- Encodings drawn from $\Dir(1, \dots, 1)$, spread over the simplex but not sparse: $0.21$.

The penalty is large both for a collapsed encoder and for an encoder whose outputs use too many topics per document.

## Assumptions

- **Deterministic encodings.** W-LDA gives a point estimate of $\thetavec_d$, not a posterior. There is no per-document
  uncertainty.
- **A decoder of <Gloss name="prodlda" />'s form.** The word distribution is a softmax of a linear function of $\thetavec_d$, so topics
  combine as a product of experts.
- **Enough samples per minibatch.** The MMD compares distributions from minibatch samples. Small minibatches give noisy
  estimates.

## Pitfalls

- **No likelihood.** W-LDA is not trained on a bound on $\log p(\wvec)$, so its perplexity is not comparable with that
  of <Gloss name="latent-dirichlet-allocation" /> or VAE-based models. Compare <NoteLink to="topic-model-evaluation">topic coherence</NoteLink> and diversity instead.
- **The unbiased estimate can be negative.** With few samples, the estimate of $\operatorname{MMD}^2$ can fall below
  zero when the distributions are close. It is still a valid, unbiased gradient signal.
- **Kernel choice changes what is matched.** The MMD compares distributions only through the kernel. Nan et al. chose
  the diffusion kernel because Euclidean distances are insensitive to differences near the simplex's faces, where sparse
  topic proportions live <Cite id="nan2019wlda" />.
