<Definition>
A topic model is **evaluated** along three axes, each with its own measures.

- **Predictive fit.** Held-out **perplexity**, $\exp\paren{-\sum_d\log p(\wvec_d)/\sum_d N_d}$ over test documents
  $\wvec_d$ of lengths $N_d$: lower means the model assigns more probability to unseen text.
- **Interpretability of each topic.** **Coherence** scores a topic by how often its top $M$ words co-occur in a
  reference corpus, for example the mean normalised pointwise mutual information over pairs of top words. **Human
  judgement** measures it directly, for example by whether people can spot an intruder word inserted among a topic's
  top words.
- **Coverage of the topic set.** **Topic diversity** is the fraction of distinct words among the top 25 words of all
  topics.

The measures disagree often enough that no single one ranks models reliably <Cite id="chang2009tealeaves" />
<Cite id="hoyle2021coherence" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A topic model has two uses that pull apart. As a density model of text, it should predict held-out words well. As a tool
for reading a corpus, its topics should be lists of words that a person recognises as one subject. A model can predict
words well by spreading probability over many generic words, which makes topics blurry, or by using many topics in
every document, which makes each topic less distinct. Measures of the first use therefore do not certify the second.

## Perplexity

Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood per word, the reciprocal of the geometric mean of
the probabilities the model gives to the test words (see
<NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy and perplexity</NoteLink>). A perplexity of $P$ means the
model is as uncertain, on average, as a uniform choice among $P$ words.

For a topic model, $\log p(\wvec_d) = \log\int p(\wvec_d \mid \thetavec)\,p(\thetavec)\,d\thetavec$ is itself
intractable. Wallach et al. showed that common estimators, such as the harmonic mean of likelihoods from posterior
samples, are badly biased, and proposed better ones, including a left-to-right sequential estimator
<Cite id="wallach2009evaluation" />. A variational model reports its <Gloss name="elbo" />, which is a lower bound; comparing an ELBO with
another model's likelihood estimate favours the other model. In **document completion**, the model infers
$\thetavec_d$ from half of each test document and is scored on the other half, which avoids fitting $\thetavec_d$ to
the words it is then scored on.

## Human judgement: reading tea leaves

Chang et al. designed two tasks that measure interpretability directly <Cite id="chang2009tealeaves" />.

- **Word intrusion.** Show a person the top five words of a topic plus one **intruder**, a word with low probability in
  this topic and high probability in another. If the topic is coherent, the intruder stands out. The score is the
  fraction of people who pick it.
- **Topic intrusion.** Show a document's title and snippet with its three most probable topics and one improbable
  topic. The score measures how well people find the improbable one.

Across <Gloss name="latent-dirichlet-allocation" />, <Gloss name="plsa" /> and the <Gloss name="correlated-topic-model">correlated topic model</Gloss> on news and encyclopedia corpora, held-out likelihood and the human
scores were not aligned: the models and settings with better predictive likelihood often produced topics that people
found less interpretable <Cite id="chang2009tealeaves" />. Perplexity stopped being accepted as a proxy for topic
quality.

## Coherence

Coherence scores approximate human judgement from word co-occurrence. Let $v_1, \dots, v_M$ be a topic's top $M$ words,
usually $M = 10$.

**UMass coherence** uses the training corpus itself <Cite id="mimno2011coherence" />. With $D(v)$ the number of
documents containing $v$ and $D(v, v')$ the number containing both,

$$
C_{\text{UMass}} = \sum_{m=2}^{M}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}\log\frac{D(v_m, v_l) + 1}{D(v_l)} .
$$

Each term is the log of a smoothed <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink> that a document containing the higher-ranked top word
$v_l$ also contains $v_m$. The score is at most about zero and larger is better.

**PMI and NPMI coherence** use an external reference corpus, typically Wikipedia, and count co-occurrence within sliding
windows of a fixed number of words <Cite id="newman2010coherence" />. With $p(v)$ the fraction of windows containing
$v$ and $p(v, v')$ the fraction containing both, the pointwise mutual information is
$\operatorname{PMI}(v, v') = \log\frac{p(v, v')}{p(v)\,p(v')}$ (see
<NoteLink to="co-occurrence-matrices-and-pointwise-mutual-information">PMI</NoteLink>). PMI is unbounded and grows for rare pairs, so it is
normalised <Cite id="bouma2009npmi" />:

$$
\operatorname{NPMI}(v, v') = \frac{\operatorname{PMI}(v, v')}{-\log p(v, v')} \in [-1, 1] .
$$

The bounds follow from $p(v, v') \le \min(p(v), p(v'))$. If the words only ever occur together, $p(v, v') = p(v) =
p(v')$, so $\operatorname{PMI} = \log\frac{1}{p(v, v')} = -\log p(v, v')$ and NPMI is $1$. If they are independent,
PMI is $0$. As $p(v, v') \to 0$ with the marginals fixed, $\operatorname{PMI} = \log p(v, v') - \log p(v)p(v')$, and
dividing by $-\log p(v, v')$ gives $-1 + \log(p(v)p(v'))/\log p(v, v') \to -1$. A topic's NPMI coherence is the mean
over its $\binom{M}{2}$ pairs, and the model's is the mean over topics. NPMI correlates better with human ratings than
PMI does <Cite id="aletras2013coherence" /> <Cite id="lau2014coherence" />.

**$C_V$** is the measure that Röder et al. found to correlate best with human ratings in a systematic search over the
space of coherence measures <Cite id="roder2015coherence" />. It differs from NPMI coherence in three ways.

1. **Context vectors.** Each top word $v_m$ is represented by the vector of its NPMI with every top word,
   $\uvec_m = \paren{\operatorname{NPMI}(v_m, v_1), \dots, \operatorname{NPMI}(v_m, v_M)}$.
2. **One word against the set.** Each $\uvec_m$ is compared with the sum of all context vectors of the topic,
   $\sum_{m'}\uvec_{m'}$, by <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink>. Two words that never co-occur can still support each other if they
   co-occur with the same other words, which is an indirect confirmation.
3. **Boolean sliding windows** over the reference corpus estimate the probabilities.

$C_V$ is the mean of the $M$ cosine similarities.

## Topic diversity

A model can score high coherence by repeating one coherent topic many times. **Topic diversity** is the fraction of
unique words among the top 25 words of all $K$ topics <Cite id="dieng2020etm" />. It is $1$ when no two topics share a
top word and near $1/K$ when all topics are the same. Dieng et al. define **topic quality** as coherence times
diversity. Diversity is the direct check for component collapse in <NoteLink to="neural-topic-models">neural topic models</NoteLink>.

## The automated coherence debate

Coherence measures were validated against human judgement on classical models such as LDA. Neural topic models were
then compared with each other mainly by automated coherence. Hoyle et al. compared automated coherence with human topic
ratings and word intrusion for LDA and two neural models on two standard corpora <Cite id="hoyle2021coherence" />. They
report two gaps.

- **A validation gap.** Automated coherence declared a winning model in cases where the human evaluations did not
  distinguish the models, so automated scores alone could not establish that one model's topics were better.
- **A standardisation gap.** Published comparisons used different reference corpora, window sizes, numbers of top words,
  preprocessing and coherence variants, so reported numbers are rarely comparable across papers.

The practical conclusions are to fix and report every setting of a coherence measure, to compute it on a large external
reference corpus, to pair it with diversity, and to confirm important differences with a human task such as word
intrusion.

## Worked example

**Perplexity.** A model assigns probabilities $0.1$, $0.05$ and $0.2$ to the three words of a held-out document. The
log-likelihood is $\log(0.001) = -6.908$ nats, $-2.303$ per word, so the perplexity is $e^{2.303} = 10$. The geometric
mean of the three probabilities is $0.1$, and $1/0.1 = 10$.

**NPMI.** In $1000$ windows, word $a$ occurs in $100$, word $b$ in $50$, and both in $20$. Under <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> they would
co-occur in $100 \times 50/1000 = 5$ windows. $\operatorname{PMI} = \log\frac{0.02}{0.1 \times 0.05} = \log 4 = 1.386$
and $\operatorname{NPMI} = 1.386/(-\log 0.02) = 1.386/3.912 = 0.354$.

**Topic diversity.** Three topics have top-five words (game, team, season, the, said),
(market, bank, rate, the, said) and (rain, storm, wind, the, said). Of the 15 slots, 11 hold distinct words, so the diversity is $11/15 = 0.733$. The
shared words "the" and "said" signal that <NoteLink to="stop-words">stop words</NoteLink> have leaked into every topic.

## Assumptions

- **Top words summarise a topic.** Coherence and intrusion look only at the top $M$ words. A topic with a coherent head
  and a noisy tail scores well.
- **Co-occurrence in the reference corpus reflects meaning in the modelled corpus.** Wikipedia co-occurrence may not
  capture the vocabulary of legal filings or clinical notes.
- **Perplexity needs a likelihood.** Clustering-based models and Wasserstein autoencoders have none, so perplexity
  cannot compare them with probabilistic models.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing $K$ by perplexity alone.** Held-out perplexity often keeps improving as $K$ grows, while topics fragment and
  become harder to read.
- **Scoring on the training corpus.** UMass coherence uses the training corpus, which rewards topics that fit its
  co-occurrences, not topics that are meaningful.
- **Comparing numbers across papers.** Coherence values depend on the reference corpus, window size, $M$ and
  preprocessing <Cite id="hoyle2021coherence" />. Recompute every baseline under one protocol.
- **Coherence without diversity.** Many copies of one coherent topic score well on coherence. Report both.
