<Definition>
Let $\xvec \in \reals^p$ and $\yvec \in \reals^q$ be two random vectors observed on the same items, with covariances
$\Sigmamat_{xx}$ and $\Sigmamat_{yy}$ (positive definite) and cross-covariance
$\Sigmamat_{xy} = \cov(\xvec, \yvec) = \Sigmamat_{yx}^\top$. **Canonical correlation analysis** finds
$\avec_1 \in \reals^p$ and $\bvec_1 \in \reals^q$ that maximise

$$
\rho = \corr(\avec^\top\xvec, \bvec^\top\yvec) = \frac{\avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xy}\bvec}
{\sqrt{\avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xx}\avec}\sqrt{\bvec^\top\Sigmamat_{yy}\bvec}}.
$$

The maximum $\rho_1$ is the first **canonical correlation**, and $u_1 = \avec_1^\top\xvec$, $v_1 = \bvec_1^\top\yvec$
are the first pair of **canonical variates**. Later pairs maximise the same correlation subject to being uncorrelated
with all earlier variates. There are $\min(p, q)$ pairs <Cite id="hotelling1936" />.
</Definition>

## Two views of the same items

CCA applies when each item is measured in two ways: gene expression and clinical measurements of the same patients,
images and captions, audio and video of the same speaker, the same text in two languages. Each view has its own
features. CCA asks which linear combination of one view's features tracks which linear combination of the other's.
Unlike <NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">PCA</NoteLink>, which looks for variance within one set, CCA looks
for correlation between two. Unlike regression, it treats both sets symmetrically.

## Derivation as a generalised eigenproblem

The correlation does not change when $\avec$ or $\bvec$ is rescaled, so fix the scales:
$\avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xx}\avec = 1$ and $\bvec^\top\Sigmamat_{yy}\bvec = 1$. The problem becomes: maximise
$\avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xy}\bvec$ subject to the two constraints. The Lagrangian is

$$
\Lcal = \avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xy}\bvec - \frac{\lambda}{2}\paren{\avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xx}\avec - 1} -
\frac{\mu}{2}\paren{\bvec^\top\Sigmamat_{yy}\bvec - 1}.
$$

Setting the gradients in $\avec$ and $\bvec$ to zero gives

$$
\Sigmamat_{xy}\bvec = \lambda\Sigmamat_{xx}\avec, \qquad \Sigmamat_{yx}\avec = \mu\Sigmamat_{yy}\bvec.
$$

Multiply the first equation by $\avec^\top$ and the second by $\bvec^\top$. The constraints turn the right sides into
$\lambda$ and $\mu$, and both left sides equal $\avec^\top\Sigmamat_{xy}\bvec$. So $\lambda = \mu = \rho$, the
correlation being maximised. In block form,

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \zeros & \Sigmamat_{xy} \\ \Sigmamat_{yx} & \zeros \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \avec \\ \bvec \end{pmatrix} = \rho
\begin{pmatrix} \Sigmamat_{xx} & \zeros \\ \zeros & \Sigmamat_{yy} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \avec \\ \bvec \end{pmatrix},
$$

a symmetric generalised eigenproblem. Its eigenvalues come in pairs $\pm\rho_j$. Eliminating $\bvec =
\Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{yx}\avec/\rho$ from the first equation gives a problem in $\avec$ alone:

$$
\Sigmamat_{xy}\Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{yx}\,\avec = \rho^2\,\Sigmamat_{xx}\,\avec.
$$

The squared canonical correlations are the generalised eigenvalues, and $\avec_1$ is the eigenvector of the largest.

## Equivalent SVD

Substitute $\tilde\avec = \Sigmamat_{xx}^{1/2}\avec$ and $\tilde\bvec = \Sigmamat_{yy}^{1/2}\bvec$. The constraints
become $\norm{\tilde\avec} = \norm{\tilde\bvec} = 1$, and the objective becomes $\tilde\avec^\top\Kmat\tilde\bvec$ with

$$
\Kmat = \Sigmamat_{xx}^{-1/2}\Sigmamat_{xy}\Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1/2}.
$$

The maximum of $\tilde\avec^\top\Kmat\tilde\bvec$ over unit vectors is the largest singular value of $\Kmat$, attained
at its top left and right singular vectors. So the canonical correlations are the singular values of the whitened
cross-covariance $\Kmat$, and $\avec_j = \Sigmamat_{xx}^{-1/2}\tilde\avec_j$, $\bvec_j = \Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1/2}
\tilde\bvec_j$ come from its singular vectors. The orthogonality of singular vectors gives the uncorrelatedness of the
later pairs: $\cov(u_j, u_k) = \avec_j^\top\Sigmamat_{xx}\avec_k = \tilde\avec_j^\top\tilde\avec_k = 0$ for $j \ne k$.
Every canonical correlation lies in $[0, 1]$, since $\Kmat$'s singular values are correlations.

In practice the covariances are replaced by their sample estimates. Each whitening step is a
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive-definite</NoteLink> square root or a Cholesky factor.

## Special cases

- **One variable on one side.** With $p = 1$, there is one canonical correlation. The first equation above gives
  $\bvec \propto \Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{yx}$, the least-squares regression coefficients of $x$ on $\yvec$, and
  $\rho$ is the multiple correlation coefficient: $\rho^2$ is the $R^2$ of that regression.
- **Class indicators.** When $\yvec$ is a one-hot encoding of class labels, the canonical directions for $\xvec$ are
  the directions of <NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">linear discriminant analysis</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

## Worked example

**One variable against two.** Let $x$ be scalar with variance 1 and $\yvec = (y_1, y_2)$ with
$\Sigmamat_{yy} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\Sigmamat_{yx} = (0.6, 0.6)^\top$. Then
$\Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{yx} = (0.4, 0.4)^\top$, since $\Sigmamat_{yy}(0.4, 0.4)^\top = (0.6, 0.6)^\top$. The
squared canonical correlation is $\rho^2 = \Sigmamat_{xy}\Sigmamat_{yy}^{-1}\Sigmamat_{yx} = 0.6 \cdot 0.4 +
0.6 \cdot 0.4 = 0.48$, so $\rho = 0.693$. The combination $y_1 + y_2$ correlates with $x$ more strongly than either
$y_1$ or $y_2$ alone, whose correlation is $0.6$.

**Two against two.** Let $\Sigmamat_{xx} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\Sigmamat_{yy} =
\Imat$ and $\Sigmamat_{xy} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.2 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}$. The singular values of
$\Kmat = \Sigmamat_{xx}^{-1/2}\Sigmamat_{xy}$ are $0.579$ and $0.279$. The first canonical correlation, $0.579$, exceeds
the largest correlation between any single $x_i$ and $y_j$, which is $0.5$.

## Regularised and nonlinear CCA

With fewer items than features, the sample $\Sigmamat_{xx}$ or $\Sigmamat_{yy}$ is singular and CCA can find perfect
spurious correlations. **Regularised CCA** replaces $\Sigmamat_{xx}$ by $\Sigmamat_{xx} + \kappa\Imat$, and likewise
for $\yvec$. **Kernel CCA** applies CCA in the feature spaces of two kernels and needs regularisation for the same
reason <Cite id="hardoon2004" />. Deep CCA replaces the linear maps by neural networks.

## Pitfalls

- **Overfitting.** The canonical correlations are maximised on the sample, so they are biased upward, strongly so when
  $p + q$ is not small compared with $n$. Assess them on held-out data or with permutation tests.
- **Directions are hard to read.** Canonical weights $\avec$ change a lot when features are correlated. The
  correlations between each feature and the canonical variates, called loadings or structure coefficients, are more
  stable to interpret.
- **Linear and second-order.** CCA sees only linear relations and only covariances.
