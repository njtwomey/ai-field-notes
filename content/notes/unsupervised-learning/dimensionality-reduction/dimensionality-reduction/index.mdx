<Definition>
**Dimensionality reduction** takes $n$ points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n \in \reals^D$ and returns points
$\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n \in \reals^m$ with $m < D$, often $m = 2$ or $3$ for plots and $m$ in the tens or hundreds for
features. Each method names a property of the data that the $\yvec_i$ must keep, such as variance, pairwise distances,
geodesic distances along a manifold, nearest-neighbour relations or the likelihood of a generative model. A method is
**parametric** when it returns a function $f : \reals^D \to \reals^m$ that also maps new points.
</Definition>

## Why reduce dimension

- **Visualisation.** A person can read a scatter plot in two or three dimensions only.
- **Compression and speed.** Storage, distance computations and model fitting all cost at least $O(D)$ per point.
- **Noise.** Low-variance directions often hold mostly noise. Discarding them can improve a downstream model.
- **Intrinsic structure.** Data generated by a few underlying factors lie near an $m$-dimensional **manifold**, a
  surface that looks like $\reals^m$ in every small patch. The **intrinsic dimension** $m$ is the number of those
  factors. Images of one object under rotation about one axis form a curve ($m = 1$), whatever the number of pixels $D$.

No map from $\reals^D$ to $\reals^m$ keeps everything. Some sets of $n$ points need on the order of
$\log n/\varepsilon^2$ dimensions to keep all pairwise distances within a factor $1 \pm \varepsilon$
<Cite id="larsen2017" />, so a two-dimensional picture of high-dimensional data must distort something
<Cite id="chari2023" />. Each method chooses what to distort.

## Four distinctions

**Linear or nonlinear.** A linear method returns $\yvec = \Wmat^\top(\xvec - \bar\xvec)$ for a $D \times m$ matrix
$\Wmat$. PCA, probabilistic PCA, factor analysis, ICA, NMF, CCA and random projections are linear. They cannot unroll a
curved manifold: two points on different layers of a rolled sheet project to nearby points. Nonlinear methods replace
straight-line geometry with a graph, a kernel or a neural network.

**Global or local.** A global method tries to keep all pairwise relations, including large distances. Classical MDS
and Isomap are global: their squared-error objectives are dominated by the largest distances. A local method keeps
only relations among near neighbours and leaves large distances free. LLE, Laplacian eigenmaps, t-SNE and UMAP are
local. Local methods separate clusters and unroll folds, but the distances between clusters and their relative sizes
carry little meaning <Cite id="wang2021b" />.

**Spectral or iterative.** Spectral methods reduce the problem to the top or bottom eigenvectors of an $n \times n$
matrix: a <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">Gram matrix</NoteLink>, a <NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">graph Laplacian</NoteLink> or a Markov matrix. They need no random seed. When the kept eigenvalues are distinct, their global optimum is unique up to the sign of each
eigenvector.
Neighbour-embedding methods (t-SNE, UMAP, PaCMAP) minimise a non-convex loss by <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>. Their results depend
on the initialisation and the seed.

**Parametric or not.** A parametric method learns a map $f$, so a new point costs one evaluation of $f$. A
non-parametric method returns only the coordinates $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n$ of the training points, and a new point
needs an **out-of-sample extension**.

## Out-of-sample extension by the Nyström formula

Every spectral method computes eigenvectors of an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix $\Kmat$ with entries
$K_{ij} = \kappa(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)$ for some data-dependent function $\kappa$ <Cite id="ham2004" />. Kernel PCA uses a
kernel, classical MDS uses $-\tfrac12$ times the double-centred squared distances, Isomap the same with geodesic
distances, and Laplacian eigenmaps and diffusion maps a normalised weight matrix. Write the $k$-th eigenvector as
$\vvec_k$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_k$. Row $i$ of $\Kmat\vvec_k = \lambda_k\vvec_k$ reads

$$
v_{ik} = \frac{1}{\lambda_k}\sum_{j=1}^n \kappa(\xvec_i, \xvec_j)\,v_{jk}.
$$

The right side is defined for any point $\xvec$, not only for $\xvec_i$. Replacing $\xvec_i$ by a new point $\xvec$
gives the **Nyström extension**

$$
v_k(\xvec) = \frac{1}{\lambda_k}\sum_{j=1}^n \kappa(\xvec, \xvec_j)\,v_{jk},
$$

which agrees with $v_{ik}$ at every training point. The embedding of $\xvec$ is $v_k(\xvec)$ with the same scaling the
method applies to $\vvec_k$. Bengio and colleagues derived these extensions for LLE, Isomap, MDS, Laplacian eigenmaps
and spectral clustering, with the data-dependent $\kappa$ of each method <Cite id="bengio2003b" />. The cost is
$O(n)$ kernel evaluations per new point. Neighbour-embedding methods have no such formula; their parametric variants
train a neural network instead.

## Choosing a method

- **Features for a model.** Start with <NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">PCA</NoteLink>: it is fast, has one
  solution and maps new points. Use <NoteLink to="random-projections">random projections</NoteLink> when $D$ is so large
  that even PCA is too slow, and an <NoteLink to="autoencoder">autoencoder</NoteLink> when the structure is nonlinear
  and data are plentiful.
- **A plot of clusters.** t-SNE or UMAP, initialised with PCA, read with the cautions of
  <NoteLink to="evaluating-embeddings">evaluating embeddings</NoteLink>.
- **A continuous manifold.** Isomap when the manifold is flat after unrolling and has no holes, Laplacian eigenmaps or
  diffusion maps when only local structure is trusted, t-SNE with high exaggeration or UMAP with many neighbours when
  $n$ is large.
- **Two views of the same items.** <NoteLink to="canonical-correlation-analysis">CCA</NoteLink>.
- **Uncertainty or missing values.** A generative model: probabilistic PCA, factor analysis or a
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-latent-variable-model">Gaussian process latent variable model</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale.** Every distance-based method depends on the units of the features. Standardise features with different
  units first.
- **Preprocess with PCA.** For $D$ in the thousands, reducing to about 50 principal components before a nonlinear
  method removes noise and speeds up the neighbour search.
- **Hyperparameters change the picture.** The neighbourhood size $k$, the kernel width or the perplexity decides which
  structure a nonlinear method sees. In the figure, Isomap on the Swiss roll changes from a clean unrolling to a fold
  between $k = 7$ and $k = 8$.
- **Axes have no meaning** in nonlinear embeddings, and the orientation of spectral embeddings is fixed only up to sign.
