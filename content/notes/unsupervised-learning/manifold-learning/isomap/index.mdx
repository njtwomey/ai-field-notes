<Definition>
**Isomap** takes points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n \in \reals^D$ and a neighbourhood size $k$ (or radius
$\varepsilon$) <Cite id="tenenbaum2000" />. It

1. builds a graph that joins each point to its $k$ nearest neighbours, with edge lengths $\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}$;
2. computes the shortest-path distance $d_G(i, j)$ between every pair of points in the graph;
3. applies classical <NoteLink to="multidimensional-scaling">multidimensional scaling</NoteLink> to the matrix of
   $d_G$ and keeps the top $m$ coordinates.
</Definition>

## Straight-line distance is the wrong distance

Data often lie near a low-dimensional curved surface, a **manifold**, inside a high-dimensional space. Images of one
object under different rotations form a curve; the "Swiss roll" is a sheet rolled up in three dimensions. Two points
on opposite layers of the roll are close in straight-line distance but far apart along the sheet. PCA and classical MDS
use straight-line distances, so they project the layers on top of each other.

The distance along the manifold is the **geodesic** distance. On a manifold that can be unrolled without stretching,
geodesic distances are the Euclidean distances of the unrolled coordinates. Isomap estimates them and hands them to
MDS, which then finds those coordinates.

## Geodesics from a neighbourhood graph

Over short distances a smooth manifold is nearly flat, so for near neighbours the straight-line distance approximates
the geodesic distance. A long geodesic is approximated by a chain of short hops. The shortest path through the
neighbourhood graph is the chain of hops with the smallest total length. It is computed by Dijkstra's algorithm from
every point, $O(n^2\log n + n^2 k)$ in total, or by Floyd–Warshall in $O(n^3)$.

Tenenbaum, de Silva and Langford proved that the graph distances converge to the true geodesic distances as the
sampling density increases, provided the neighbourhoods are small compared with the curvature of the manifold and the
gaps between its folds <Cite id="tenenbaum2000" />. If the manifold is isometric to a convex region of $\reals^m$, the
geodesic distances are Euclidean in $m$ dimensions, and classical MDS recovers the coordinates up to rotation and
translation.

## Choosing k

The neighbourhood size trades two failures against each other:

- **Too small.** The graph falls into disconnected pieces, and some geodesic distances are infinite. Isomap then embeds
  each connected component separately, or fails.
- **Too large.** Edges jump across gaps between folds of the manifold. One such **short-circuit** edge makes many
  shortest paths cut across, and the embedding collapses the folds.

The residual variance, $1 - R^2$ between $d_G$ and the embedded distances, plotted against the dimension $m$, shows the
intrinsic dimension as the point where it stops falling.

## Worked example

Five points lie on a unit semicircle at angles $0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°$. Join points closer than $\varepsilon = 0.8$.

- Adjacent points are $2\sin(22.5°) = 0.765$ apart; the next nearest are $1.414$ apart. The graph is a chain.
- The graph distance between the two ends is $4 \times 0.765 = 3.061$. The true arc length is $\pi = 3.142$ and the
  straight-line distance is $2$.
- The graph distances are $0.765\abs{i - j}$. Classical MDS on them gives one nonzero eigenvalue, $5.858$, and the
  coordinates $(1.531, 0.765, 0, -0.765, -1.531)$ up to sign: the semicircle unrolled onto a line with equal spacing.
- Classical MDS on the straight-line distances instead needs two dimensions (eigenvalues $3$ and $0.834$). Its first
  coordinate, $(-1, -0.707, 0, 0.707, 1)$, bunches the points near the ends.

## Pitfalls

- **Short circuits.** A single edge across a fold changes the embedding globally. Noise makes short circuits more likely.
- **Holes and non-convexity.** If the manifold has a hole, geodesics bend around it, and the distances are not
  Euclidean in any flat domain. The embedding is distorted.
- **Cost.** All-pairs shortest paths and a dense $n \times n$ eigendecomposition limit plain Isomap to a few thousand
  points. Landmark Isomap computes distances from a subset of landmark points only.
- **Curvature.** A manifold that cannot be flattened without stretching, such as a sphere, has no distance-preserving
  map to the plane. Isomap then gives the best classical-MDS compromise.
