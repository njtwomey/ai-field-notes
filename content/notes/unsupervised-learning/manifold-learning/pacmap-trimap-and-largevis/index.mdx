<Definition>
A **neighbour embedding** places points $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n \in \reals^2$ by minimising a loss with an
attractive term over pairs that are neighbours in a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph of the data and a repulsive term over
other pairs. <NoteLink to="t-distributed-stochastic-neighbour-embedding">t-SNE</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="uniform-manifold-approximation-and-projection">UMAP</NoteLink> are the best known.
**LargeVis** maximises the likelihood of graph edges with sampled negative pairs <Cite id="tang2016" />. **TriMap**
asks each sampled triplet $(i, j, k)$ to keep $j$ closer to $i$ than $k$ <Cite id="amid2019" />. **PaCMAP** attracts
neighbours and sampled mid-range pairs and repels random pairs, with weights that change over three phases
<Cite id="wang2021b" />.
</Definition>

## The shared recipe

Every method in this family does four things:

1. Build a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph of the data, exactly or approximately.
2. Choose which pairs or triplets enter the loss: graph edges for attraction, and non-edges, usually sampled, for
   repulsion.
3. Choose a similarity kernel in the embedding, usually the Cauchy kernel $s(d) = (1 + d^2)^{-1}$ of t-SNE.
4. Optimise from an initial layout, often PCA or Laplacian eigenmaps, by gradient descent.

The methods differ in steps 2 and 3, and those choices decide the balance between local and global structure.

## LargeVis

Tang and colleagues built the neighbour graph with random projection trees refined by neighbour exploring: the
neighbours of a point's neighbours are candidate neighbours of the point <Cite id="tang2016" />. Edge weights $w_{ij}$
are t-SNE's symmetrised affinities. The probability of an edge in the embedding is $f(\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j})$ with
$f(d) = (1 + d^2)^{-1}$, and the objective is

$$
\sum_{(i, j) \in E} w_{ij}\log f\paren{\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}} + \sum_{(i, j) \notin E}\gamma\log\paren{1 -
f\paren{\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}}},
$$

maximised by stochastic gradient ascent. Each step samples an edge with probability proportional to $w_{ij}$ and a few
negative points $j'$ from a noise distribution, instead of summing over all $n^2$ non-edges. The cost per step does
not depend on $n$. UMAP's optimisation follows the same scheme.

## TriMap

TriMap keeps relative, not absolute, similarities <Cite id="amid2019" />. For a triplet $(i, j, k)$ in which $j$ is
nearer to $i$ than $k$ is, the loss is

$$
\ell_{ijk} = \frac{s(\yvec_i, \yvec_k)}{s(\yvec_i, \yvec_j) + s(\yvec_i, \yvec_k)}, \qquad s(\yvec, \yvec') = \paren{1
+ \norm{\yvec - \yvec'}^2}^{-1}.
$$

$\ell_{ijk}$ lies in $(0, 1)$. It approaches 0 when $k$ is placed much farther from $i$ than $j$ is, and 1 when the
order is reversed. Most triplets pair a near neighbour $j$ with a random point $k$; a few pair two random points, which
carries information about large distances. Each triplet is weighted by how clearly its order holds in the data. TriMap
starts from PCA and uses full-batch gradient descent with momentum.

## PaCMAP

Wang, Huang, Rudin and Shaposhnik compared the losses of t-SNE, UMAP, TriMap and others by plotting their gradients on
triplets, and derived principles that good losses share <Cite id="wang2021b" />. Attraction on neighbours should
dominate once the far point of a triplet is reasonably far, and repulsion should dominate only when the far point is
too close. Their loss uses $\tilde d_{ab} = \norm{\yvec_a - \yvec_b}^2 + 1$ and three kinds of pairs:

$$
\mathrm{Loss} = w_{NB}\sum_{\text{neighbours}}\frac{\tilde d_{ij}}{10 + \tilde d_{ij}} + w_{MN}\sum_{\text{mid-near}}
\frac{\tilde d_{ik}}{10000 + \tilde d_{ik}} + w_{FP}\sum_{\text{further}}\frac{1}{1 + \tilde d_{il}}.
$$

- **Neighbour pairs.** Each point's 10 nearest neighbours, by a distance scaled by the local density.
- **Mid-near pairs.** For each point, sample 6 other points and keep the second nearest. These pairs are close, but
  not neighbours, and they carry the arrangement of groups.
- **Further pairs.** Random pairs, repelled.

**The forces.** The magnitude of each force is the derivative of its term with respect to the embedding distance $d$.
Since $\tilde d = d^2 + 1$, the neighbour force is $20d/(11 + d^2)^2$. It rises from 0, peaks at $d = \sqrt{11/3} =
1.91$ and then decays as $20/d^3$: a neighbour placed far away is barely pulled back, so one bad pair cannot drag a
cluster. The mid-near force is $20000d/(10001 + d^2)^2$, which grows almost linearly up to $d = 58$, far beyond any
cluster: a weak spring between groups. The repulsive force is $2d/(2 + d^2)^2$, which peaks at $d = 0.82$ and is
negligible beyond a few units: it separates points that overlap, not clusters that are already apart.

**The schedule.** For the first 100 iterations $w_{NB} = 2$, $w_{FP} = 1$ and $w_{MN}$ falls linearly from 1000 to 3.
The strong mid-near springs arrange the clusters globally. For iterations 101 to 200, $w_{NB} = 3$ and $w_{MN} = 3$.
For iterations 201 to 450, $w_{MN} = 0$ and $w_{NB} = 1$, so the last phase refines local structure alone. PaCMAP
starts from PCA.

## The attraction–repulsion spectrum

Böhm, Berens and Kobak placed t-SNE, UMAP, ForceAtlas2 and Laplacian eigenmaps on one axis <Cite id="bohm2022" />.
Write t-SNE's gradient with the attractive term multiplied by an **exaggeration** $\rho$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\yvec_i} \propto \rho\sum_j p_{ij}w_{ij}(\yvec_i - \yvec_j) - \frac1Z\sum_j w_{ij}^2(\yvec_i -
\yvec_j), \qquad w_{ij} = \paren{1 + \norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}^2}^{-1}.
$$

$\rho = 1$ is t-SNE, and $\rho = 12$ is the early exaggeration of its first iterations. Increasing $\rho$
strengthens attraction:

- **$\rho \approx 4$** gives embeddings very similar to UMAP's.
- **$\rho \approx 30$** gives embeddings similar to ForceAtlas2, a force-directed graph layout.
- **$\rho \to \infty$.** Strong attraction shrinks the embedding until all neighbour distances are small, so
  $w_{ij} \approx 1$. The attractive term becomes $\rho\sum_j p_{ij}(\yvec_i - \yvec_j)$, the gradient of
  $\frac\rho2\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}^2$. That is the cost of
  <NoteLink to="laplacian-eigenmaps">Laplacian eigenmaps</NoteLink>, with repulsion acting only to fix the scale.

The trade-off is between continuous and discrete structure. More attraction joins clusters and shows continuous
trajectories; more repulsion separates clusters and keeps local neighbourhoods. On MNIST, the fraction of each point's
15 nearest neighbours that remain neighbours in the embedding fell from 0.34 at $\rho = 1$ to 0.12 at $\rho = 4$ and
0.06 at $\rho = 30$.

**Why UMAP sits at $\rho \approx 4$.** In the full gradient, each attractive force on a point is matched by about
$n/k$ repulsive ones, one for every non-neighbour. UMAP's negative sampling draws a fixed number $m$ of repulsive
partners per attractive edge, usually 5. The effective repulsion is therefore scaled down by about $km/n$, which falls
as $n$ grows <Cite id="bohm2022" />. UMAP does not minimise the cross-entropy it states; Damrich and Hamprecht derived the
loss that its sampling actually optimises <Cite id="damrich2021" />.

## Local and global structure

- **Initialisation carries global structure.** t-SNE and UMAP started from PCA or Laplacian eigenmaps keep much more of
  the arrangement of clusters than when started at random <Cite id="kobak2021" />.
- **Mid-range information.** TriMap's random triplets and PaCMAP's mid-near pairs put some large-scale distances into
  the loss itself, so the global layout depends less on the initialisation <Cite id="wang2021b" />.
- **No method keeps everything.** Stronger attraction or more mid-range terms cost local accuracy, as the spectrum
  shows. Measure both with the tools of <NoteLink to="evaluating-embeddings">evaluating embeddings</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

The forces of the PaCMAP loss at embedding distances $d = 1, 3, 10$, per unit weight:

| $d$ | Neighbour pull $\frac{20d}{(11 + d^2)^2}$ | Mid-near pull, $w_{MN} = 1000$ | Repulsion $\frac{2d}{(2 + d^2)^2}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.139 | 0.200 | 0.222 |
| 3 | 0.150 | 0.599 | 0.050 |
| 10 | 0.016 | 1.960 | 0.002 |

At $d = 1$ repulsion is the strongest force. At $d = 10$ repulsion has almost vanished, the neighbour pull is about a
ninth of its value at $d = 1$, and in the first phase the mid-near springs dominate. This ordering of forces with
distance lets the first phase arrange clusters and the last phase refine them.

## Pitfalls

- **Same reading rules as t-SNE.** Cluster sizes and gaps remain unreliable, whichever loss is used.
- **Parameters are the method.** Exaggeration, the number of negative samples and the pair ratios move an embedding
  along the attraction–repulsion spectrum. Two embeddings that disagree may both be faithful to different scales.
- **No out-of-sample map.** None of these methods gives a function for new points; see
  <NoteLink to="parametric-and-neural-embeddings">parametric embeddings</NoteLink>.
