<Definition>
**Multidimensional scaling** (MDS) takes an $n \times n$ matrix of dissimilarities $\delta_{ij}$ between $n$ items and
returns points $\yvec_1, \dots, \yvec_n \in \reals^k$ whose Euclidean distances $\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}$
approximate $\delta_{ij}$. **Classical MDS** computes the points in closed form from an eigendecomposition
<Cite id="torgerson1952" />. **Metric MDS** minimises a stress function numerically, and **non-metric MDS** matches
only the rank order of the dissimilarities <Cite id="kruskal1964" />.
</Definition>

## Only distances are given

Many data sets come as comparisons, not coordinates: travel times between cities, how often subjects confuse two
sounds, edit distances between strings, distances along a graph. MDS turns such a table into a map. The result is
determined only up to rotation, reflection and translation, because these leave all distances unchanged.

## Classical MDS: from distances to a Gram matrix

Suppose the dissimilarities are exact Euclidean distances between unknown centred points $\xvec_i \in \reals^p$,
stacked as the rows of $\Xmat$. Let $\Bmat = \Xmat\Xmat^\top$ be the **Gram matrix** of inner products,
$b_{ij} = \xvec_i^\top\xvec_j$. The squared distances are

$$
d_{ij}^2 = \norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2 = b_{ii} + b_{jj} - 2b_{ij}.
$$

In matrix form, with $\cvec = (b_{11}, \dots, b_{nn})^\top$ and $\Dmat^{(2)}$ the matrix of squared distances,
$\Dmat^{(2)} = \cvec\ones^\top + \ones\cvec^\top - 2\Bmat$. To recover $\Bmat$, apply the centring matrix
$\Jmat = \Imat - \frac1n\ones\ones^\top$ on both sides. Since $\Jmat\ones = \zeros$, the two rank-one terms vanish:

$$
\Jmat\Dmat^{(2)}\Jmat = -2\Jmat\Bmat\Jmat.
$$

The points are centred, so $\Jmat\Xmat = \Xmat$ and $\Jmat\Bmat\Jmat = \Bmat$. Therefore

$$
\Bmat = -\tfrac12\Jmat\Dmat^{(2)}\Jmat.
$$

This is **double centring**: subtract each row mean and each column mean of the squared distances, add back the grand
mean, and multiply by $-\tfrac12$. Entry by entry, $b_{ij} = -\frac12\paren{d_{ij}^2 - \bar d_{i\cdot}^2 - \bar
d_{\cdot j}^2 + \bar d_{\cdot\cdot}^2}$.

$\Bmat$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite. Its eigendecomposition $\Bmat = \Qmat\Lambdamat\Qmat^\top$, with
$\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots$, gives the coordinates

$$
\Ymat = \Qmat_k\Lambdamat_k^{1/2},
$$

whose Gram matrix is $\Ymat\Ymat^\top = \Qmat_k\Lambdamat_k\Qmat_k^\top$. If the distances come from points in
$\reals^p$, at most $p$ eigenvalues are nonzero, and $k = p$ reproduces every distance exactly.

For $k$ smaller than the rank, $\Qmat_k\Lambdamat_k\Qmat_k^\top$ is the best rank-$k$ approximation of $\Bmat$ in
Frobenius norm (Eckart–Young). Classical MDS therefore minimises the **strain**
$\norm{\Bmat - \Ymat\Ymat^\top}_F$ over $k$-dimensional configurations.

## Classical MDS is PCA

With Euclidean distances computed from a centred data matrix, let $\Xmat = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat^\top$ be its
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink>. Then $\Bmat = \Xmat\Xmat^\top =
\Umat\Sigmamat^2\Umat^\top$, so $\Qmat_k = \Umat_k$, $\Lambdamat_k^{1/2} = \Sigmamat_k$ and $\Ymat = \Umat_k\Sigmamat_k$.
These are the principal component scores of <NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">PCA</NoteLink>. Classical MDS
works from the $n \times n$ Gram matrix and PCA from the $p \times p$ covariance matrix; the smaller one is cheaper to
decompose. In ecology the method is also called **principal coordinates analysis**.

## Non-Euclidean dissimilarities

When the $\delta_{ij}$ are not Euclidean distances, for example graph distances or subjective ratings, $\Bmat$ can have
negative eigenvalues. Classical MDS keeps the largest positive ones. The size of the negative eigenvalues, compared with
the positive ones, measures how far the dissimilarities are from Euclidean. <NoteLink to="isomap">Isomap</NoteLink>
applies classical MDS to shortest-path distances in a neighbourhood graph.

## Metric and non-metric MDS

**Metric MDS** minimises a stress function directly, for example

$$
\operatorname{Stress}(\Ymat) = \sum_{i < j} w_{ij}\paren{\delta_{ij} - \norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}}^2,
$$

with weights $w_{ij}$, often 1 or $\delta_{ij}^{-2}$. Unlike strain, stress compares distances rather than inner
products, and it allows missing dissimilarities ($w_{ij} = 0$). It has no closed form. The SMACOF algorithm minimises it
by majorisation: at each step it minimises a quadratic upper bound that touches the stress at the current configuration
<Cite id="borg2005" />.

**Non-metric MDS** assumes only that larger dissimilarities should map to larger distances. Kruskal's algorithm
alternates between fitting a monotone (isotonic) regression of the distances on the dissimilarities and moving the
points to reduce the stress against the fitted values <Cite id="kruskal1964" />. It suits ordinal data such as
similarity ratings.

## Worked example

Four points at the corners of a unit square, in cyclic order. Adjacent corners have squared distance 1 and opposite
corners 2:

$$
\Dmat^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

- Every row and column mean is $1$, and the grand mean is $1$. Double centring gives
  $b_{ij} = -\frac12(d_{ij}^2 - 1 - 1 + 1) = -\frac12(d_{ij}^2 - 1)$, so the diagonal is $\frac12$, adjacent pairs get
  $0$ and opposite pairs $-\frac12$.
- $\Bmat$ has eigenvalues $1, 1, 0, 0$: the configuration is exactly two-dimensional.
- Taking the two eigenvectors scaled by $\sqrt1$ gives the coordinates $(0, -0.707)$, $(-0.707, 0)$, $(0, 0.707)$,
  $(0.707, 0)$: a square of side 1 centred at the origin, rotated by $45°$. Its squared distances are 1 for adjacent
  and 2 for opposite corners, as required.

## Pitfalls

- **Only relative positions.** The configuration is unique only up to rotation, reflection and translation. Use
  <NoteLink to="procrustes-analysis">Procrustes analysis</NoteLink> to compare two MDS solutions.
- **Large distances dominate.** Squared errors weight large dissimilarities most, so classical MDS preserves the global
  layout and can distort local neighbourhoods.
- **Quadratic memory.** The dissimilarity matrix has $n^2$ entries. Landmark methods embed a subset and place the rest
  by triangulation.
