<Definition>
A **self-organising map** (SOM) has $u$ units at fixed positions $\rvec_1, \dots, \rvec_u$ on a lattice, usually a
two-dimensional square or hexagonal grid, and a prototype $\wvec_j \in \reals^D$ for each unit
<Cite id="kohonen1982" />. For each training input $\xvec$ it finds the **best-matching unit**
$c = \argmin_j\norm{\xvec - \wvec_j}$ and updates every prototype:

$$
\wvec_j \leftarrow \wvec_j + \eta(t)\,h_{cj}(t)\,(\xvec - \wvec_j), \qquad h_{cj}(t) = \exp\paren{-\frac{\norm{\rvec_j
- \rvec_c}^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}},
$$

with a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> $\eta(t)$ and a neighbourhood width $\sigma(t)$, in lattice
units, that both shrink over training. A point is mapped to the lattice position $\rvec_c$ of its best-matching unit.
</Definition>

## Competition and cooperation

The SOM combines two rules. **Competition**: only the unit closest to the input wins. Moving only the winner toward the
input is online <NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink>, which spreads prototypes over the data but puts them in no
particular order. **Cooperation**: the winner's lattice neighbours move too, by an amount that falls off with their
distance from the winner on the lattice, not in data space. Units adjacent on the lattice are repeatedly pulled toward
the same inputs, so their prototypes end up near each other. The lattice then lies over the data like a sheet, and
nearby lattice positions describe nearby regions of the input space. This is the **topology-preserving** property: the
map from inputs to lattice positions keeps neighbourhoods.

## Where the update comes from

Fix the winner $c$ for one input. The update is a stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> step on the
local cost

$$
e(\xvec) = \frac12\sum_j h_{cj}\norm{\xvec - \wvec_j}^2,
$$

because $\partial e/\partial\wvec_j = -h_{cj}(\xvec - \wvec_j)$, and a step of size $\eta$ against it is exactly the SOM
rule. Each prototype is pulled toward the input with weight $h_{cj}$. The winner $c$ itself depends on the prototypes
and changes abruptly when an input crosses the boundary between two units' regions, so the sum of $e$ over the data is
not a smooth function of the prototypes. The SOM is therefore not plain <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> on a fixed cost, and its convergence is analysed differently from that of k-means.

**The k-means limit.** When $\sigma \to 0$, $h_{cj} = 1$ for $j = c$ and 0 otherwise. Only the winner moves, by
$\eta(\xvec - \wvec_c)$. With $\eta = 1/n_c$, where $n_c$ counts the inputs unit $c$ has won, $\wvec_c$ is the running
mean of those inputs: online k-means.

**The batch SOM.** At convergence, the expected update over the data is zero for every unit. With a fixed assignment
$c(i)$ of each training point to its winner, $\sum_i h_{c(i)j}(\xvec_i - \wvec_j) = \zeros$ gives

$$
\wvec_j = \frac{\sum_i h_{c(i)j}\,\xvec_i}{\sum_i h_{c(i)j}},
$$

a weighted mean of the points won by unit $j$ and its lattice neighbours. The **batch map** alternates this update
with reassigning the winners, as k-means alternates means and assignments <Cite id="kohonen2013" />. It needs no
learning rate.

## Training schedule

Kohonen describes two phases <Cite id="kohonen1990" />:

- **Ordering.** A wide neighbourhood, a large part of the lattice, and a learning rate near 0.5. Each update moves
  large parts of the map together, which untangles the lattice and sets its global orientation.
- **Convergence.** A neighbourhood of one or two units and a small learning rate, for many more steps. Prototypes
  settle on the local structure of the data.

If $\sigma$ shrinks too fast, the lattice can freeze with a twist or a fold, a topological defect that later
updates cannot remove.

## Reading a map

- **Component planes.** Colour the lattice by one coordinate of the prototypes to see how a feature varies across the
  map.
- **U-matrix.** Colour each unit by the mean distance between its prototype and its lattice neighbours' prototypes.
  Large values mark gaps in the data, so clusters appear as regions of small values separated by ridges.
- **Quality.** The **quantisation error** is the mean distance from each point to its best-matching prototype. The
  **topographic error** is the fraction of points whose best and second-best units are not adjacent on the lattice. A
  low topographic error means the lattice is not folded over the data.

## Worked example

Three units on a line at lattice positions $0, 1, 2$ with prototypes $w = (0, 0.5, 1)$ in one dimension. The input
is $x = 0.6$, with $\eta = 0.5$ and $\sigma = 1$.

- The best-matching unit is unit 2, at distance $0.1$.
- The neighbourhood weights are $h = (e^{-1/2}, 1, e^{-1/2}) = (0.607, 1, 0.607)$.
- The updates are $w_1 = 0 + 0.5 \times 0.607 \times 0.6 = 0.182$, $w_2 = 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.1 = 0.55$ and
  $w_3 = 1 + 0.5 \times 0.607 \times (-0.4) = 0.879$.

Units 1 and 3 moved, although they did not win, and the order $w_1 < w_2 < w_3$ is kept. Online k-means would have
moved only unit 2.

## Pitfalls

- **Fixed lattice.** The number of units and the shape of the lattice are chosen in advance. A lattice of lower
  dimension than the data folds to fill it, as the chain of 40 units does in the figure.
- **Border effects.** Units on the edge of the lattice have neighbours on one side only and are pulled inward. In the
  figure the outer prototypes stop short of the edges of the square.
- **No density model.** A SOM gives no likelihood and no principled way to choose its size. Distances on the lattice
  are not distances in the data.
- **Scale.** As with k-means, features must be on comparable scales before training.
