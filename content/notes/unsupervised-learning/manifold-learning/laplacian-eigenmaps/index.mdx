<Definition>
**Laplacian eigenmaps** take points $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ and a symmetric weight matrix $\Wmat$ with $w_{ij} > 0$
only for neighbours, typically $w_{ij} = \exp\paren{-\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2/t}$ on a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph
<Cite id="belkin2003" />. With degrees $d_i = \sum_j w_{ij}$, $\Dmat = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_n)$ and the **graph
Laplacian** $\Lmat = \Dmat - \Wmat$, the method solves the generalised eigenproblem $\Lmat\fvec = \lambda\Dmat\fvec$,
orders the eigenvalues $0 = \lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots$, and embeds point $i$ as
$\yvec_i = (f_1(i), \dots, f_m(i))$, discarding the constant eigenvector $\fvec_0$.
</Definition>

## Keep neighbours close

A neighbourhood graph records which points are close on the manifold. A good embedding keeps those points close. The
cost of a one-dimensional embedding $y_1, \dots, y_n$ is the weighted sum of squared distances between neighbours,

$$
\frac12\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}(y_i - y_j)^2,
$$

which is large when strongly connected points are placed far apart. Pairs that are not neighbours have $w_{ij} = 0$
and cost nothing, however far apart they are placed. The method is therefore local: it makes no attempt to preserve
large distances.

## Derivation

**The cost is a <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink> in $\Lmat$.** Expand the square and use the symmetry of $\Wmat$:

$$
\frac12\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}(y_i - y_j)^2 = \frac12\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}(y_i^2 + y_j^2) - \sum_{i,j} w_{ij}y_iy_j =
\sum_i d_iy_i^2 - \sum_{i,j} w_{ij}y_iy_j = \yvec^\top\Lmat\yvec.
$$

Every term on the left is non-negative, so $\Lmat$ is positive semidefinite. Each row of $\Lmat$ sums to
$d_i - \sum_j w_{ij} = 0$, so $\Lmat\ones = \zeros$.

**Constraints remove the trivial solutions.** Setting every $y_i = 0$, or every $y_i$ to the same constant, gives cost
zero. Two constraints exclude these. The scale constraint $\yvec^\top\Dmat\yvec = 1$ fixes the size of the embedding
and weights each point by its degree, so that points in dense regions count more. The centring constraint
$\yvec^\top\Dmat\ones = 0$ excludes the constant vector. The problem is

$$
\min_{\yvec}\ \yvec^\top\Lmat\yvec \quad \text{subject to} \quad \yvec^\top\Dmat\yvec = 1,\ \ \yvec^\top\Dmat\ones = 0.
$$

**Stationarity gives a generalised eigenproblem.** The Lagrangian $\yvec^\top\Lmat\yvec - \lambda(\yvec^\top\Dmat\yvec
- 1)$ has <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $2\Lmat\yvec - 2\lambda\Dmat\yvec$, which vanishes when

$$
\Lmat\yvec = \lambda\Dmat\yvec.
$$

At such a $\yvec$ the cost is $\yvec^\top\Lmat\yvec = \lambda\yvec^\top\Dmat\yvec = \lambda$. The constant vector solves
the eigenproblem with $\lambda_0 = 0$ but violates the centring constraint. Eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are
$\Dmat$-orthogonal, $\fvec_a^\top\Dmat\fvec_b = 0$, so every other eigenvector satisfies it. The minimum is therefore
the eigenvector $\fvec_1$ of the smallest nonzero eigenvalue.

**Several dimensions.** For an $m$-dimensional embedding $\Ymat \in \reals^{n \times m}$ the cost is
$\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\norm{\yvec_i - \yvec_j}^2/2 = \trace(\Ymat^\top\Lmat\Ymat)$, with the constraint
$\Ymat^\top\Dmat\Ymat = \Imat$. Substituting $\Zmat = \Dmat^{1/2}\Ymat$ turns this into minimising
$\trace(\Zmat^\top\Dmat^{-1/2}\Lmat\Dmat^{-1/2}\Zmat)$ over matrices with orthonormal columns. The minimum is attained by
the bottom eigenvectors of the normalised Laplacian $\Dmat^{-1/2}\Lmat\Dmat^{-1/2}$, by the same trace argument as in
<NoteLink to="principal-component-analysis">PCA</NoteLink>. Mapping back, $\Ymat = \Dmat^{-1/2}\Zmat$ consists of the
generalised eigenvectors $\fvec_1, \dots, \fvec_m$, and the minimum cost is $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_m$.

## The continuous picture

Belkin and Niyogi justified the method through the **Laplace–Beltrami operator** $\Delta$ of the manifold
<Cite id="belkin2003" />. For a smooth function $f$ on the manifold, nearby points $\xvec$ and $\xvec + \deltavec$
map to values that differ by about $\nabla f(\xvec)^\top\deltavec$. The average squared stretch of the map is
therefore measured by $\int\norm{\nabla f}^2$, which <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink> turns into $\int f\,\Delta f$. Minimising it
subject to $\int f^2 = 1$ and $\int f = 0$ gives the eigenfunctions of $\Delta$ with the smallest nonzero eigenvalues.
The graph Laplacian is the discrete version: $\yvec^\top\Lmat\yvec$ plays the role of $\int\norm{\nabla f}^2$. Belkin
and Niyogi showed that with heat-kernel weights $e^{-\norm{\xvec_i - \xvec_j}^2/t}$, and $t$ shrinking as the sample
grows, the graph Laplacian converges to the Laplace–Beltrami operator. With the simpler weights $w_{ij} = 1$ for
neighbours the method still works and has one parameter fewer.

## Repeated directions on elongated manifolds

The eigenfunctions of a flat rectangle $[0, a] \times [0, b]$, with no flux across the boundary, are
$\cos(\pi pu/a)\cos(\pi qv/b)$ for integers $p, q \ge 0$, with eigenvalues $\pi^2(p^2/a^2 + q^2/b^2)$. The first
nonconstant one is $\cos(\pi u/a)$, along the long side. The next is either $\cos(\pi v/b)$, along the short side, or
$\cos(2\pi u/a)$, a second harmonic of the long side. The harmonic comes first when $4/a^2 < 1/b^2$, that is when
$a > 2b$. Then the second embedding coordinate is a function of the first, and the embedding is an arc rather than a
rectangle. A Swiss roll that is long compared with its height shows exactly this in the explorer of
<NoteLink to="dimensionality-reduction">dimensionality reduction</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Four points on a line, joined in a path $1 - 2 - 3 - 4$ with unit weights. The degrees are $(1, 2, 2, 1)$ and

$$
\Lmat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

- The generalised eigenvalues of $\Lmat\fvec = \lambda\Dmat\fvec$ are $0, 0.5, 1.5, 2$.
- The eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 0.5$ is $\fvec_1 = (-2, -1, 1, 2)/(2\sqrt3) = (-0.577, -0.289, 0.289, 0.577)$.
  Check: $\fvec_1^\top\Dmat\fvec_1 = (4 + 2 + 2 + 4)/12 = 1$, $\fvec_1^\top\Dmat\ones = (-2 - 2 + 2 + 2)/(2\sqrt3) = 0$,
  and the cost over the three edges is $(1 + 4 + 1)/12 = 0.5 = \lambda_1$.
- The embedding keeps the order of the path, but not its equal spacing: the middle gap is twice each end gap.
- Without the degree weighting, the bottom nonconstant eigenvector of $\Lmat$ alone is
  $(0.653, 0.271, -0.271, -0.653)$, with eigenvalue $2 - \sqrt2 = 0.586$. Its gaps are $0.383, 0.541, 0.383$, a ratio
  of $\sqrt2$ rather than 2.

## Relatives

- **Spectral clustering** computes the same eigenvectors and clusters their rows with k-means; see
  <NoteLink to="spectral-clustering">spectral clustering</NoteLink>, which relates the eigenvectors to relaxed graph
  cuts <Cite id="vonluxburg2007" />.
- **Diffusion maps** use the same generalised eigenvectors, scaled by powers of $1 - \lambda$, and add a
  normalisation that removes the effect of sampling density; see <NoteLink to="diffusion-maps">diffusion maps</NoteLink>.
- **Kernel PCA.** Laplacian eigenmaps are <NoteLink to="kernel-principal-component-analysis">kernel PCA</NoteLink> with the pseudo-inverse of
  $\Lmat$ as the kernel matrix. New points are embedded with the Nyström extension <Cite id="bengio2003b" />.
- **Neighbour embeddings.** <Gloss name="umap" /> initialises its layout with Laplacian eigenmaps. <Gloss name="t-sne" /> with a very large exaggeration
  of its attractive forces approaches Laplacian eigenmaps <Cite id="bohm2022" />; see
  <NoteLink to="pacmap-trimap-and-largevis">the attraction–repulsion spectrum</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Disconnected graphs.** Each connected component adds another zero eigenvalue, and the corresponding eigenvectors
  are constant on components. The embedding then encodes which component a point is in, not where it lies. Check
  connectivity, or embed components separately.
- **Harmonics.** On elongated manifolds the second coordinate repeats the first, as derived above. Inspect more
  eigenvectors, or use <Gloss name="ltsa" /> or <Gloss name="isomap" /> when the full shape matters.
- **Parameters.** The neighbourhood size $k$ and the kernel width $t$ decide the graph. Too large a $k$ short-circuits
  folds; too small disconnects the graph.
- **Distances are not preserved.** Only neighbour relations enter the cost, so distances in the embedding have no
  direct meaning.
