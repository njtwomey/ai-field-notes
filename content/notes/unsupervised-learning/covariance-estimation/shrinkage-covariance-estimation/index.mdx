<Definition>
Let $\Smat$ be the $p \times p$ sample covariance of $n$ observations and $m = \trace(\Smat)/p$ the mean of its
eigenvalues. The **linear shrinkage estimator** with intensity $\delta \in [0, 1]$ is

$$
\hat\Sigmamat_\delta = (1 - \delta)\Smat + \delta m\Imat.
$$

The **Ledoit–Wolf estimator** chooses $\delta$ from the data to minimise the expected squared Frobenius error
$\expect\norm{\hat\Sigmamat_\delta - \Sigmamat}^2$, where $\norm{\Amat}^2 = \trace(\Amat\Amat^\top)/p$
<Cite id="ledoit2004" />.
</Definition>

## The sample covariance is poor in high dimensions

The sample covariance is unbiased, but it estimates $p(p + 1)/2$ numbers from $np$ values. When $p$ is not small
compared with $n$, its errors are large and systematic:

- **Eigenvalues spread out.** The largest sample eigenvalues are too large and the smallest too small. For
  $\Sigmamat = \Imat$, whose eigenvalues are all 1, the sample eigenvalues spread over
  $\LB(1 - \sqrt{p/n})^2,\, (1 + \sqrt{p/n})^2\RB$ as $n$ and $p$ grow in proportion. This is the Marchenko–Pastur law
  <Cite id="marchenko1967" />. With $p/n = 0.5$ the range is $[0.09, 2.91]$.
- **Singularity.** When $p \ge n$, $\Smat$ has rank at most $n - 1$ and cannot be inverted. Methods that need
  $\Smat^{-1}$, such as <NoteLink to="linear-discriminant-analysis">linear discriminant analysis</NoteLink>,
  Mahalanobis distances and portfolio optimisation, fail or become unstable.

Shrinkage trades a little bias for a large reduction in variance, as in
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>.

## What shrinkage does to the eigenvalues

$\Smat$ and $m\Imat$ share eigenvectors, so $\hat\Sigmamat_\delta$ keeps the eigenvectors of $\Smat$ and maps each
eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ to $(1 - \delta)\lambda_i + \delta m$. Every eigenvalue moves toward the mean by the same
fraction, which counteracts the spreading. The smallest eigenvalue becomes at least $\delta m > 0$, so the estimate is
positive definite and its <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> is at most
$\paren{(1 - \delta)\lambda_{\max} + \delta m}/(\delta m)$.

## The optimal intensity

Let $\mu = \trace(\Sigmamat)/p$. Consider shrinking toward the unknown $\mu\Imat$ first, and define

$$
\alpha^2 = \norm{\Sigmamat - \mu\Imat}^2, \qquad \beta^2 = \expect\norm{\Smat - \Sigmamat}^2.
$$

$\alpha^2$ measures how far the true covariance is from a multiple of the identity. $\beta^2$ is the sampling error of
$\Smat$. The error of the shrunk estimate is

$$
\expect\norm{\delta\mu\Imat + (1 - \delta)\Smat - \Sigmamat}^2 =
\expect\norm{\delta(\mu\Imat - \Sigmamat) + (1 - \delta)(\Smat - \Sigmamat)}^2 = \delta^2\alpha^2 + (1 - \delta)^2
\beta^2.
$$

The cross term vanishes because $\expect[\Smat - \Sigmamat] = \zeros$ and $\mu\Imat - \Sigmamat$ is fixed. Setting the
derivative $2\delta\alpha^2 - 2(1 - \delta)\beta^2$ to zero gives

$$
\delta^* = \frac{\beta^2}{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}, \qquad \text{error} = \frac{\alpha^2\beta^2}{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}.
$$

The optimal error is smaller than both $\beta^2$, the error of $\Smat$, and $\alpha^2$, the error of the target. Shrink
a lot when the sample is noisy compared with how far the truth is from the target, and little otherwise. Because
$\expect[\Smat] = \Sigmamat$, the quantity $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$ equals $\expect\norm{\Smat - \mu\Imat}^2$, which can be
estimated from the data.

## The Ledoit–Wolf estimates

Ledoit and Wolf replace each unknown by a consistent estimate, with observations $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n$ centred at
their mean <Cite id="ledoit2004" />:

- $m = \trace(\Smat)/p$ estimates $\mu$;
- $d^2 = \norm{\Smat - m\Imat}^2$ estimates $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$;
- $\bar b^2 = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{k=1}^n\norm{\xvec_k\xvec_k^\top - \Smat}^2$ estimates $\beta^2$, because $\Smat$ is the
  mean of the $n$ matrices $\xvec_k\xvec_k^\top$ and the variance of a mean of $n$ terms is their variance over $n$;
- $b^2 = \min(\bar b^2, d^2)$ keeps the estimate of $\beta^2$ no larger than the total.

The estimated intensity is $\hat\delta = b^2/d^2 \in [0, 1]$. As $n$ and $p$ grow together, $\hat\delta$ converges to
the optimal intensity and the estimator's error to the optimal error. Nothing about the distribution beyond finite
fourth moments is assumed.

## Variants

- **Other targets.** Shrinking toward the diagonal of $\Smat$ keeps each variance and shrinks only the correlations.
  Schäfer and Strimmer give intensities for six targets <Cite id="schafer2005" />.
- **Oracle approximating shrinkage** (OAS) derives a different intensity under a Gaussian assumption. It converges
  faster when $n$ is small <Cite id="chen2010" />.
- **Nonlinear shrinkage** moves each eigenvalue by its own amount instead of the same fraction.

## Worked example

**Two dimensions.** A sample covariance $\Smat = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues
$1.5 \pm \sqrt{0.5}$, that is $2.207$ and $0.793$, and $m = 1.5$. With $\delta = 0.3$,
$\hat\Sigmamat = 0.7\Smat + 0.45\Imat = \begin{pmatrix} 1.85 & 0.35 \\ 0.35 & 1.15 \end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are
$0.7 \times 2.207 + 0.45 = 1.995$ and $0.7 \times 0.793 + 0.45 = 1.005$. The condition number falls from $2.78$ to
$1.99$.

**Fifty dimensions.** Take $\Sigmamat_{ij} = 0.5^{\abs{i - j}}$ with $p = 50$; its eigenvalues range from $0.33$ to
$2.98$. Averaged over 200 simulated samples, with scikit-learn's `ledoit_wolf`:

| $n$ | $\hat\delta$ | Error of $\Smat$ | Error of Ledoit–Wolf |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 25 | 0.716 | 1.953 | 0.518 |
| 50 | 0.592 | 1.017 | 0.406 |
| 100 | 0.437 | 0.510 | 0.292 |
| 200 | 0.283 | 0.257 | 0.186 |
| 1000 | 0.074 | 0.051 | 0.048 |

Errors are $\norm{\hat\Sigmamat - \Sigmamat}^2 = \trace\paren{(\hat\Sigmamat - \Sigmamat)^2}/p$. At $n = 25$ the
shrinkage estimate is almost four times as accurate as $\Smat$. As $n$ grows the intensity falls toward 0 and the two
estimates converge.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale.** Shrinking toward $m\Imat$ assumes the variables have comparable variances. Standardise first, or shrink
  toward the diagonal.
- **The loss is Frobenius.** $\hat\delta$ minimises squared error of the covariance itself. It is not tuned for the
  inverse, for eigenvectors or for a downstream task; cross-validating $\delta$ on the task can do better.
- **No sparsity.** Linear shrinkage keeps every correlation nonzero. For a sparse inverse covariance, use the
  <NoteLink to="graphical-lasso">graphical lasso</NoteLink>.
