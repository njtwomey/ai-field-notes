<Definition>
A first-order method minimises a function $f$ using its values and gradients, never its <NoteLink to="derivative">second
derivatives</NoteLink>. Each iteration costs about one gradient evaluation, so first-order methods scale to problems
with millions or billions of variables. Their theory bounds how fast they converge in terms of the smoothness constant
$L$ and the strong-convexity constant $\mu$ of $f$ <Cite id="nesterov2004" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every neural network is trained by a first-order method. Gradient descent with momentum, Adam and their relatives all
take steps built from gradients, and backpropagation supplies those gradients at the cost of about one forward pass. The
convergence theory explains why a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> must stay below $2/L$, why badly
conditioned problems converge slowly, and why momentum helps. Proximal methods solve lasso and other sparse regression
problems. Coordinate descent fits <Gloss name="glm">generalised linear models</Gloss> with $\ell_1$ and elastic-net
penalties. Mirror descent underlies the exponentiated gradient and multiplicative-weights algorithms of
<NoteLink to="online-learning">online learning</NoteLink>.

## Main ideas

**Step against the gradient.** <NoteLink to="gradient-descent" /> updates
$\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k - \eta\nabla f(\xvec_k)$. The step size must stay below a limit set by the curvature, and
momentum speeds progress along narrow valleys.

**Rates depend on smoothness and curvature.** The note on <NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">convergence of
gradient descent</NoteLink> proves that, for an $L$-smooth convex $f$ and step $1/L$, the error falls as $O(1/k)$. If
$f$ is also $\mu$-strongly convex, the error shrinks by a factor $1 - \mu/L$ per step. The
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> $\kappa = L/\mu$ sets the number of steps.

**Extrapolation is optimal.** <NoteLink to="accelerated-gradient-methods" /> take the gradient step from an extrapolated
point. Nesterov's method reaches $O(1/k^2)$ on smooth <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex
functions</NoteLink>, which no first-order method can beat in the worst case.

**One coordinate at a time.** <NoteLink to="coordinate-descent" /> minimises over one coordinate at a time, exactly or
with a coordinate gradient step. It converges for smooth convex functions and for a smooth function plus a separable
penalty, such as the lasso.

**Split the smooth from the nonsmooth.** <NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods" /> minimise $f + g$ with $f$ smooth
and $g$ convex but possibly nonsmooth. A gradient step on $f$ is followed by the proximal operator of $g$. For the
$\ell_1$ norm the proximal operator is soft-thresholding, which gives <Gloss name="ista" />, and adding acceleration
gives <Gloss name="fista" />.

**Change the geometry.** <NoteLink to="mirror-descent" /> replaces the squared
Euclidean distance in the gradient step by a Bregman divergence suited to the feasible set. On the
probability simplex with negative entropy it becomes the exponentiated gradient
update, with a rate that depends on $\log n$ instead of $n$.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="hessian" />, the
<NoteLink to="taylor-series" /> and the definitions in <NoteLink to="convex-analysis">convex analysis</NoteLink>.

<NoteLink to="second-order-methods" /> add curvature to the step. <NoteLink to="stochastic-optimisation" /> replaces the
gradient by a noisy estimate from a minibatch, which is how deep networks are trained. The optimisers used in practice,
<NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">momentum and Nesterov momentum</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="adagrad-and-rmsprop" />
and <NoteLink to="adam" />, and the <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedules</NoteLink> that drive
them, are covered with neural network training.
