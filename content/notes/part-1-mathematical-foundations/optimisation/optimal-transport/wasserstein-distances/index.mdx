<Definition>
Let $(\Xcal, d)$ be a metric space, $p \ge 1$, and $\mu, \nu$ probability distributions on $\Xcal$ with finite $p$-th
moments. The **$p$-Wasserstein distance** is

$$
W_p(\mu, \nu) = \paren{\min_{\pi \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int d(x, y)^p\, d\pi(x, y)}^{1/p},
$$

where $\Pi(\mu, \nu)$ is the set of couplings: joint distributions of $(X, Y)$ with $X \sim \mu$ and $Y \sim \nu$
<Cite id="villani2009" />. $W_p$ is a metric on distributions. $W_1$ between histograms is the <NoteLink
to="earth-movers-distance">earth mover's distance</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A coupling pairs each draw from $\mu$ with a draw from $\nu$. $W_p$ is the smallest $L^p$ norm of the distance between
paired draws, $\paren{\expect\, d(X, Y)^p}^{1/p}$, over all pairings. Moving a distribution by a vector $\svec$ moves
every draw by $\norm{\svec}$, so $W_p$ between a distribution and its translate is exactly $\norm{\svec}$ for every
$p$: pairing each point with its own translate costs $\norm{\svec}$, and no pairing can do better because the means
differ by $\svec$ (Jensen's inequality gives $\expect\norm{X - Y} \ge \norm{\expect X - \expect Y}$).

The distance sees geometry. It changes continuously as mass moves, whether or not the supports of $\mu$ and $\nu$
overlap. Divergences that compare densities point by point, such as <Gloss name="kl" />, Jensen–Shannon and total
variation, do not.

## $W_p$ is a metric

**Symmetry** holds because swapping the coordinates of a coupling of $(\mu, \nu)$ gives a coupling of $(\nu, \mu)$ with
the same cost. **Identity**: $W_p(\mu, \mu) = 0$ through the coupling $X = Y$. Conversely, if $W_p(\mu, \nu) = 0$, an
optimal coupling puts all its mass on $\set{x = y}$, so $X = Y$ almost surely and $\mu = \nu$.

The **triangle inequality** uses the **gluing** of two plans. For histograms $\avec$, $\bvec$, $\cvec$ on points
$x_i$, $y_j$, $z_k$, let $\Pmat$ be an optimal plan from $\avec$ to $\bvec$ and $\Qmat$ an optimal plan from $\bvec$
to $\cvec$. Define the three-way array $R_{ijk} = P_{ij} Q_{jk} / b_j$ for $b_j > 0$. It describes random points
$(X, Y, Z)$ with $(X, Y) \sim \Pmat$ and $(Y, Z) \sim \Qmat$: summing over $k$ gives $P_{ij}$, and summing over $i$
gives $Q_{jk}$. The marginal of $(X, Z)$ is therefore a coupling of $\avec$ and $\cvec$. Using it, the triangle
inequality for $d$, and Minkowski's inequality for the $L^p$ norm,

$$
W_p(\avec, \cvec) \le \paren{\expect\, d(X, Z)^p}^{1/p} \le \paren{\expect\paren{d(X, Y) + d(Y, Z)}^p}^{1/p}
\le \paren{\expect\, d(X, Y)^p}^{1/p} + \paren{\expect\, d(Y, Z)^p}^{1/p} = W_p(\avec, \bvec) + W_p(\bvec, \cvec).
$$

For general distributions the same argument uses the gluing lemma, which builds $(X, Y, Z)$ from
<NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distributions</NoteLink> <Cite id="villani2009" />.

Two further properties follow from the definition. $W_p$ increases with $p$: for any coupling, $\paren{\expect\,
d^p}^{1/p} \le \paren{\expect\, d^q}^{1/q}$ when $p \le q$ by Jensen's inequality, so $W_p \le W_q$. And convergence in
$W_p$ is equivalent to <NoteLink to="modes-of-convergence">weak convergence</NoteLink> together with convergence of
$p$-th moments <Cite id="villani2009" />.

## One dimension

On $\reals$ with $d(x, y) = \abs{x - y}$, the monotone coupling $X = F^{-1}(U)$, $Y = G^{-1}(U)$ with $U \sim
\Unif(0, 1)$ is optimal for every convex cost $\abs{x - y}^p$ with $p \ge 1$ <Cite id="villani2009" />. Hence

$$
W_p(\mu, \nu)^p = \int_0^1 \abs{F^{-1}(u) - G^{-1}(u)}^p\, du .
$$

For $p = 1$ this equals the area between the <Gloss name="cdf" plural />, $\int \abs{F - G}$
<Cite id="vallender1974" />; the derivation is in the <NoteLink to="earth-movers-distance">earth mover's
distance</NoteLink> note. Between two samples of equal size, $W_p$ pairs the sorted values.

## $W_1$ and Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality

For $p = 1$ the distance has a dual form in which no coupling appears:

$$
W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{\norm{f}_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1} \paren{\expect_{\mu}\brack{f(X)} - \expect_{\nu}\brack{f(Y)}},
$$

where the supremum is over functions with $\abs{f(x) - f(y)} \le d(x, y)$ for all $x, y$.

**The easy direction.** For any 1-Lipschitz $f$ and any coupling $\pi$,
$\expect_\mu f - \expect_\nu f = \expect_\pi\brack{f(X) - f(Y)} \le \expect_\pi\, d(X, Y)$. Minimising the right-hand
side over $\pi$ gives $\expect_\mu f - \expect_\nu f \le W_1$.

**Equality.** General <NoteLink to="kantorovich-duality">Kantorovich duality</NoteLink> states that the optimal
transport cost equals the largest value of $\expect_\mu \varphi + \expect_\nu \psi$ over pairs of functions with
$\varphi(x) + \psi(y) \le c(x, y)$ for all $x, y$. For $c = d$ this reduces to one Lipschitz function in two steps.

1. Replacing $\psi$ by the $c$-transform $\varphi^c(y) = \inf_x \paren{d(x, y) - \varphi(x)}$ keeps the pair feasible
   and can only increase $\psi$, so it does not lower the objective. $\varphi^c$ is an infimum of functions
   $y \mapsto d(x, y) - \varphi(x)$, each 1-Lipschitz in $y$ by the triangle inequality, so $\varphi^c$ is
   1-Lipschitz. Transforming again, $\varphi$ can be replaced by the 1-Lipschitz $(\varphi^c)^c$ in the same way.
2. For a 1-Lipschitz $f$, $f^c = -f$. Taking $x = y$ in the infimum gives $f^c(y) \le -f(y)$. The Lipschitz bound
   $f(x) - f(y) \le d(x, y)$ gives $d(x, y) - f(x) \ge -f(y)$ for every $x$, so $f^c(y) \ge -f(y)$.

The dual pair is therefore $(f, -f)$ with $f$ 1-Lipschitz, and the dual objective is $\expect_\mu f - \expect_\nu f$.
The <NoteLink to="wasserstein-gan">Wasserstein GAN</NoteLink> uses this form: a neural network constrained to be
1-Lipschitz plays the role of $f$ <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />.

## $W_2$ between Gaussians

For $\mu = \Gauss(\mvec_1, \Sigmamat_1)$ and $\nu = \Gauss(\mvec_2, \Sigmamat_2)$ on $\reals^d$ with the Euclidean
distance <Cite id="dowson1982" />,

$$
W_2^2(\mu, \nu) = \norm{\mvec_1 - \mvec_2}^2 + \trace\paren{\Sigmamat_1 + \Sigmamat_2
- 2\paren{\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}\Sigmamat_2\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}}^{1/2}} .
$$

**Derivation.** For any coupling of $X \sim \mu$ and $Y \sim \nu$ with cross-covariance $\Kmat = \cov(X, Y)$,

$$
\expect\norm{X - Y}^2 = \norm{\mvec_1 - \mvec_2}^2 + \trace\Sigmamat_1 + \trace\Sigmamat_2 - 2\trace\Kmat .
$$

The cost depends on the coupling only through $\trace\Kmat$, which must be maximised. The joint covariance
$\begin{bmatrix} \Sigmamat_1 & \Kmat \\ \Kmat^\top & \Sigmamat_2 \end{bmatrix}$ must be
<Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss>. For invertible $\Sigmamat_1, \Sigmamat_2$, that
holds exactly when $\Kmat = \Sigmamat_1^{1/2}\Mmat\Sigmamat_2^{1/2}$ for a matrix $\Mmat$ with all
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular values</NoteLink> at most 1 (the
<NoteLink to="schur-complement">Schur complement</NoteLink> $\eye - \Mmat\Mmat^\top$ must be positive semi-definite).
Then

$$
\trace\Kmat = \trace\paren{\Mmat\, \Sigmamat_2^{1/2}\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}} \le \sum_i s_i\paren{\Sigmamat_2^{1/2}\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}} ,
$$

because the trace of a product with a contraction is at most the sum of singular values $s_i$ (von Neumann's trace
inequality). Equality holds for $\Mmat = \Vmat\Umat^\top$ built from the <Gloss name="svd">singular value
decomposition</Gloss> $\Sigmamat_2^{1/2}\Sigmamat_1^{1/2} = \Umat\Smat\Vmat^\top$. The squared singular values of
$\Sigmamat_2^{1/2}\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}$ are the eigenvalues of $\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}\Sigmamat_2\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}$, so the sum
of singular values is $\trace\paren{\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}\Sigmamat_2\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}}^{1/2}$. A jointly Gaussian pair with
this cross-covariance exists, which gives the formula. The optimal coupling is the
<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink> $T(\xvec) = \mvec_2 + \Amat(\xvec - \mvec_1)$ with $\Amat = \Sigmamat_1^{-1/2}\paren{\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}\Sigmamat_2
\Sigmamat_1^{1/2}}^{1/2}\Sigmamat_1^{-1/2}$.

The argument used only the first two moments. For any distributions with these means and covariances, the right-hand
side is therefore a lower bound on $W_2^2$, attained by the Gaussians. The <NoteLink
to="frechet-inception-distance">Fréchet inception distance</NoteLink> is this formula applied to Gaussians fitted to
network features.

**Worked example.** In one dimension the formula is $W_2^2 = (m_1 - m_2)^2 + (\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)^2$. Between
$\Gauss(0, 1)$ and $\Gauss(1, 4)$, with <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviations</NoteLink> 1 and 2,
$W_2^2 = 1 + 1 = 2$ and $W_2 = 1.414$. The optimal map is $T(x) = 1 + 2x$, which is also the quantile map
$G^{-1}(F(x))$. Sorting $10^5$ draws from each distribution and pairing them in order gives a value within 0.02 of 2.

## Disjoint supports

Let $\mu = \delta_0$ and $\nu = \delta_\theta$, point masses at $0$ and $\theta \ne 0$. The only coupling pairs $0$ with
$\theta$, so $W_p = \abs{\theta}$. The density-based divergences do not depend on $\theta$:

- total variation $= 1$ for every $\theta \ne 0$;
- <Gloss name="js">Jensen–Shannon divergence</Gloss> $= \log 2$ for every $\theta \ne 0$;
- $\KL(\mu \,\|\, \nu) = \infty$ for every $\theta \ne 0$.

The same holds for two uniform distributions on $[0, 1]$ and $[\theta, \theta + 1]$ once $\abs{\theta} \ge 1$. The
divergences are constant in $\theta$, so their <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with respect to $\theta$ is
zero or undefined. $W_p$ is $\abs{\theta}$, with gradient $\pm 1$ pointing towards the other distribution. This is the
argument for training generative models with $W_1$: a generator whose samples lie on a low-dimensional set that misses
the data still gets a useful gradient <Cite id="arjovsky2017" />.

## Assumptions

- **Finite moments.** $W_p(\mu, \nu)$ is finite when both distributions have finite $p$-th moments. Heavy-tailed
  distributions can have $W_2 = \infty$ and finite $W_1$.
- **A ground metric.** The distance is only as meaningful as $d$. Pixels compared with the Euclidean distance make two
  images of the same object shifted by a few pixels far apart.

## Pitfalls

- **Slow estimation in high dimension.** For a distribution on $\reals^d$ with $d > 2$ and its empirical measure from
  $N$ samples, $\expect W_1$ decays like $N^{-1/d}$ <Cite id="fournier2015" />. Plug-in estimates are biased and need
  exponentially many samples in $d$. Entropic regularisation and slicing improve this rate.
- **Computation.** Exact $W_p$ between $N$-point samples is an assignment problem of cost about $O(N^3)$. The closed
  forms hold only in one dimension and between Gaussians.
- **$W_1$ and $W_2$ differ.** They share the metric axioms but not the geometry. $W_2$ has the Riemannian-like
  structure that gives geodesics and barycentres, and $W_1$ has the Lipschitz dual.
