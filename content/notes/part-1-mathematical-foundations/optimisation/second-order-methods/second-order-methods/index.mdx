<Definition>
A second-order method minimises a function $f$ using its curvature as well as its gradient. Each step minimises a
quadratic model of $f$ built from the gradient and the Hessian, or an approximation to the Hessian. Near a minimum the
quadratic model is accurate, so these methods converge in far fewer iterations than first-order methods, at a higher
cost per iteration <Cite id="nocedal2006" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Second-order methods are the default for problems with up to a few thousand variables:
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> and other <Gloss name="glm">generalised linear
models</Gloss>, <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> hyperparameters, <Gloss name="mle">maximum-likelihood</Gloss>
fits in statistics, and calibration maps. L-BFGS handles much larger smooth
problems with little memory. Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt fit nonlinear least-squares models such as
<NoteLink to="camera-calibration">camera calibration</NoteLink>, bundle adjustment and curve fitting. In deep learning,
approximations to curvature appear in natural-gradient methods and in optimisers that precondition each layer. Line
searches and trust regions also make first-order methods robust.

## Main ideas

**Jump to the minimum of the quadratic model.** <NoteLink to="newtons-method" /> steps to
$\xvec_k - \nabla^2 f(\xvec_k)^{-1}\nabla f(\xvec_k)$, the minimiser of the second-order Taylor model. Near a minimum
with positive definite Hessian it converges quadratically, roughly doubling the number of correct digits per step. Far
from a minimum it can diverge or move towards a maximum.

**Choose how far to go.** A <NoteLink to="line-search" /> picks the step length along a descent direction. The Armijo
condition demands a sufficient decrease, the Wolfe curvature condition rules out steps that are too short, and
backtracking shrinks the step until Armijo holds.

**Build the curvature from gradients.** <NoteLink to="quasi-newton-methods" /> replace the Hessian by an
approximation that matches observed gradient differences, the secant condition. BFGS makes a rank-two update that stays
positive definite. L-BFGS stores only the last few pairs of steps and gradient differences, so its memory is linear in
the number of variables.

**Limit the step to where the model is trusted.** <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods" /> minimise the quadratic model
within a ball of radius $\Delta$. The method compares the actual decrease with the predicted one, then accepts the step
and may enlarge $\Delta$, or rejects it and shrinks $\Delta$. This handles Hessians that are not positive definite.

**Exploit least-squares structure.** For an objective $\tfrac12\norm{\rvec(\xvec)}^2$,
<NoteLink to="gauss-newton-and-levenberg-marquardt">Gauss–Newton</NoteLink> approximates the Hessian by
$\Jmat^\top\Jmat$, using only first <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> of the residuals.
Levenberg–Marquardt adds damping and moves between Gauss–Newton and <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink>.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="hessian" />, the
<NoteLink to="taylor-series" /> and <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite matrices</NoteLink>.
Comparison with <NoteLink to="first-order-methods">first-order methods</NoteLink> helps.

Newton's method for minimisation is <NoteLink to="root-finding">root finding</NoteLink> applied to the gradient.
<NoteLink to="penalty-and-barrier-methods">Interior-point methods</NoteLink> in
<NoteLink to="constrained-optimisation">constrained optimisation</NoteLink> take Newton steps on barrier problems. The
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation" /> reuses the Hessian at the optimum as a posterior precision.
