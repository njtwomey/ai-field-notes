<Definition>
Given an iterate $\xvec$ and a descent direction $\pvec$ with $\nabla f(\xvec)^\top\pvec < 0$, a **line search** chooses
a step length $\alpha > 0$ and sets $\xvec^+ = \xvec + \alpha\pvec$. With $\phi(\alpha) = f(\xvec + \alpha\pvec)$ and
constants $0 < c_1 < c_2 < 1$, the **Wolfe conditions** are

$$
\phi(\alpha) \le \phi(0) + c_1\alpha\phi'(0) \quad \text{(sufficient decrease)}, \qquad
\phi'(\alpha) \ge c_2\phi'(0) \quad \text{(curvature)}.
$$

The first alone is the **Armijo condition** <Cite id="armijo1966" /> <Cite id="wolfe1969" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Descent methods choose a direction: the negative <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, a
<NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton</NoteLink> direction or a
<NoteLink to="quasi-newton-methods">quasi-Newton</NoteLink> direction. They still need a length. A fixed step requires
knowing the smoothness constant $L$, which is rarely available and varies across the domain. Too long a step increases
$f$. Too short a step wastes iterations. The **exact line search** $\alpha = \argmin_{\alpha > 0}\phi(\alpha)$ is too
expensive for anything but quadratics, and not needed. A line search accepts any step that makes enough progress, and
finds one with few function evaluations.

## Mechanism

### Sufficient decrease

The slope of $\phi$ at 0 is $\phi'(0) = \nabla f(\xvec)^\top\pvec < 0$. The <NoteLink to="derivative">tangent
line</NoteLink> $\phi(0) + \alpha\phi'(0)$ predicts the decrease for small $\alpha$. The Armijo condition accepts
$\alpha$ if the actual decrease is at least a fraction $c_1$ of the predicted one. Typical values are $c_1 = 10^{-4}$.
Requiring *some* decrease, $\phi(\alpha) <
\phi(0)$, is not enough: the decreases can shrink so fast that the iterates converge to a non-stationary point.

### Curvature

The Armijo condition holds for every small enough $\alpha$, so it cannot prevent tiny steps. The curvature condition
requires the slope at $\alpha$ to have risen to at least $c_2$ times the initial slope. A step at which $\phi$ is still
falling steeply is too short. Typical values are $c_2 = 0.9$ for Newton and quasi-Newton directions and $c_2 = 0.1$ for
nonlinear <Gloss name="cg">conjugate gradients</Gloss>. The **strong Wolfe** conditions replace the curvature condition
with $\abs{\phi'(\alpha)} \le c_2\abs{\phi'(0)}$, which also excludes steps far past the minimum along the line.

The curvature condition is what quasi-Newton methods need. It gives
$\yvec^\top\svec = \alpha(\nabla f(\xvec^+) - \nabla f(\xvec))^\top\pvec = \alpha(\phi'(\alpha) - \phi'(0)) \ge
\alpha(c_2 - 1)\phi'(0) > 0$, which keeps the BFGS approximation positive definite.

### Backtracking

**Backtracking** starts from a trial step $\alpha_0$ (1 for Newton-type directions) and multiplies it by
$\rho \in (0, 1)$, typically $\tfrac12$, until the Armijo condition holds. The steps it rejects are too long, so the
step it accepts is not much too short, and the curvature condition is usually not checked.

**Backtracking terminates, and the step it accepts is not too small.** Take $\pvec = -\nabla f(\xvec)$ and $f$
$L$-smooth. The <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">descent lemma</NoteLink> gives
$\phi(\alpha) \le \phi(0) - \alpha(1 - L\alpha/2)\norm{\nabla f}^2$. The Armijo condition asks for
$\phi(\alpha) \le \phi(0) - c_1\alpha\norm{\nabla f}^2$. It therefore holds whenever $1 - L\alpha/2 \ge c_1$, that is
for every $\alpha \le 2(1 - c_1)/L$. Backtracking stops at the first trial step below this threshold, so the accepted
step satisfies $\alpha \ge \min(\alpha_0,\ 2\rho(1 - c_1)/L)$. The guaranteed decrease per iteration is then a constant
fraction of the one with the ideal step $1/L$, and the convergence rates of
<NoteLink to="convergence-of-gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> carry over without knowing $L$
<Cite id="boyd2004" />.

### Zoutendijk's theorem

For general descent directions, let $\theta_k$ be the angle between $\pvec_k$ and $-\nabla f(\xvec_k)$. If $f$ is
bounded below and $L$-smooth, and every step satisfies the Wolfe conditions, then

$$
\sum_k \cos^2\theta_k\,\norm{\nabla f(\xvec_k)}^2 < \infty
$$

<Cite id="nocedal2006" />. So if the directions stay away from orthogonal to the gradient ($\cos\theta_k \ge \delta >
0$), the gradients tend to zero. This is the global convergence guarantee of line-search methods.

<Derivation title="Proof of Zoutendijk's theorem">
The curvature condition and the Lipschitz gradient give a lower bound on the step:
$(c_2 - 1)\nabla f_k^\top\pvec_k \le (\nabla f_{k+1} - \nabla f_k)^\top\pvec_k \le L\alpha_k\norm{\pvec_k}^2$, so
$\alpha_k \ge \frac{(c_2 - 1)\nabla f_k^\top\pvec_k}{L\norm{\pvec_k}^2}$. Substitute into the sufficient decrease
condition, using $\nabla f_k^\top\pvec_k < 0$:

$$
f_{k+1} \le f_k - c_1\frac{1 - c_2}{L}\frac{(\nabla f_k^\top\pvec_k)^2}{\norm{\pvec_k}^2}
= f_k - c_1\frac{1 - c_2}{L}\cos^2\theta_k\norm{\nabla f_k}^2.
$$

Summing over $k$, the left side telescopes and $f$ is bounded below, so the sum of the decreases is finite.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Minimise $f(\xvec) = \tfrac12(x_1^2 + 10x_2^2)$ from $\xvec = (1, 1)$, where $f = 5.5$ and $\nabla f = (1, 10)$. Take
$\pvec = -\nabla f$, so $\phi'(0) = -101$. Backtrack from $\alpha_0 = 1$ with $\rho = \tfrac12$ and $c_1 = 10^{-4}$.

| $\alpha$ | $\phi(\alpha)$ | Armijo bound $5.5 - 0.0101\alpha$ | accepted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 405 | 5.4899 | no |
| 0.5 | 80.1 | 5.4950 | no |
| 0.25 | 11.5 | 5.4975 | no |
| 0.125 | 0.695 | 5.4987 | yes |

The accepted step is $0.125$. The exact minimiser along the line is $\alpha^* = \gvec^\top\gvec/\gvec^\top\Amat\gvec =
0.101$, with $\Amat = \diag(1, 10)$. The smoothness constant is $L = 10$, and the threshold from the analysis above is
$2(1 - c_1)/L \approx 0.2$. Backtracking found a step within a factor of 2 of that threshold with four function
evaluations.

## Cost

Each trial costs one function evaluation. Backtracking usually accepts the first trial for Newton-type directions near
the solution, so each iteration there costs one function evaluation. Wolfe line searches also evaluate gradients at
trial points. They use bracketing and cubic interpolation to find an acceptable point in a few trials
<Cite id="nocedal2006" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Line search versus trust region.** A line search fixes the direction and chooses the length. A
  <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods">trust-region method</NoteLink> fixes a maximum length and chooses the direction.
  Trust regions handle indefinite Hessians more naturally.
- **Stochastic settings.** With minibatch gradients, $\phi$ is itself noisy and the conditions cannot be checked
  reliably. Deep learning uses <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedules</NoteLink> instead.
- **Initial trial step.** For <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>, a good $\alpha_0$ is the
  previous accepted step times a small factor such as 2, so the search can grow as well as shrink.
