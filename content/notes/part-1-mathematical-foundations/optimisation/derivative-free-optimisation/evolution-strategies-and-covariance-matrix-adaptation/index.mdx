<Definition>
An **evolution strategy** (ES) minimises $f : \reals^n \to \reals$ by repeating three steps: sample $\lambda$ candidates
$\xvec_i = \mvec + \sigma\yvec_i$ with $\yvec_i \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Cmat)$; rank them by $f$; and move the mean $\mvec$
towards a weighted average of the best $\mu$ of them. The **<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">covariance
matrix</NoteLink> adaptation evolution strategy** (CMA-ES) also updates $\Cmat$ from the successful steps and adapts
$\sigma$ from the length of the recent path of the mean <Cite id="hansen2001" /> <Cite id="hansen2016" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Gradient-free problems in moderate dimension (tens to a few thousand variables) are common: tuning controllers or
simulators, fitting models with non-differentiable components, and policy search in <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement
learning</Gloss>. The objective can be noisy, multimodal and badly scaled.
<NoteLink to="nelder-mead">Nelder–Mead</NoteLink> degrades beyond about ten variables. Random search wastes evaluations
because it draws each candidate independently of earlier evaluations. An evolution strategy keeps a search distribution
and learns its location, scale and shape from ranked samples.

## Mechanism

### Sample, rank, recombine

At each generation, draw $\lambda$ samples $\xvec_i = \mvec + \sigma\yvec_i$. Sort them so that
$f(\xvec_{1:\lambda}) \le f(\xvec_{2:\lambda}) \le \dots$. With positive weights $w_1 \ge \dots \ge w_\mu$ summing to 1,
update the mean along the weighted average of the best steps:

$$
\mvec \leftarrow \mvec + \sigma\sum_{i=1}^\mu w_i\yvec_{i:\lambda}.
$$

Only the ranks of the $f$ values are used, not the values. The method is therefore invariant to any strictly
increasing transformation of $f$: minimising $f$, $e^f$ or $f^3$ gives the same iterates.

### Adapting the covariance

The **rank-$\mu$ update** estimates the covariance of the successful steps:

$$
\Cmat \leftarrow (1 - c_\mu)\Cmat + c_\mu\sum_{i=1}^\mu w_i\,\yvec_{i:\lambda}\yvec_{i:\lambda}^\top.
$$

Steps that led to good values are over-represented in the sum, so the distribution stretches in directions of
improvement. On a quadratic $\tfrac12\xvec^\top\Hmat\xvec$, the covariance learns a shape close to $\Hmat^{-1}$
<Cite id="hansen2016" />. Sampling from $\Gauss(\zeros, \Hmat^{-1})$ makes the problem look perfectly conditioned, which
is what <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink> achieves with the
<NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>. CMA-ES learns it from rankings alone.

The **rank-one update** adds $c_1\pvec_c\pvec_c^\top$, where the **evolution path** $\pvec_c$ is an exponentially
weighted sum of successive mean displacements. Consecutive steps in the same direction reinforce each other, so the
path detects a consistent direction that single generations would miss.

### Adapting the step size

**Cumulative step-size adaptation** keeps a second path $\pvec_\sigma$ of mean displacements, whitened by
$\Cmat^{-1/2}$. Under random selection, its length has the distribution of the norm of a standard Gaussian vector.
If the path is longer than that, successive steps are correlated and the step size should grow. If shorter, steps
cancel each other and it should shrink:

$$
\sigma \leftarrow \sigma\exp\LP\frac{c_\sigma}{d_\sigma}\LP\frac{\norm{\pvec_\sigma}}{\expect\norm{\Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)}} - 1\RP\RP.
$$

Default values for $\lambda$, $\mu$, $w_i$ and the learning rates $c_\mu, c_1, c_c, c_\sigma, d_\sigma$ depend only on
$n$ and are rarely tuned; the default population is $\lambda = 4 + \lfloor 3\ln n\rfloor$ <Cite id="hansen2016" />.

### The natural-gradient view

Consider the expected objective under the search distribution,
$J(\thetavec) = \expect_{\xvec \sim p_{\thetavec}}[f(\xvec)]$, with $\thetavec = (\mvec, \Cmat)$. The
<NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function identity</NoteLink> gives its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> without differentiating $f$:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} J = \expect_{\xvec \sim p_{\thetavec}}\LB f(\xvec)\,\nabla_{\thetavec}\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)\RB.
$$

For the mean of a Gaussian, $\nabla_\mvec\log p = \Cmat^{-1}(\xvec - \mvec)$ and the
<Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss> is $\Cmat^{-1}$. The **natural gradient** premultiplies by
the inverse Fisher matrix <Cite id="amari1998" />, which cancels the $\Cmat^{-1}$. Up to constant factors, the natural
gradients are

$$
\tilde\nabla_\mvec J = \expect\LB f(\xvec)(\xvec - \mvec)\RB, \qquad
\tilde\nabla_\Cmat J = \expect\LB f(\xvec)\LP(\xvec - \mvec)(\xvec - \mvec)^\top - \Cmat\RP\RB.
$$

Replace $f$ by rank-based weights (positive for good samples, zero or negative for bad ones) and estimate the
expectations from the $\lambda$ samples. A step along these estimates is exactly the weighted mean update and the
rank-$\mu$ covariance update above. These two updates of CMA-ES are thus natural-gradient steps on the parameters of its
sampling distribution; the rank-one update and step-size adaptation are not derived this way <Cite id="wierstra2014" />
<Cite id="hansen2016" />. The natural gradient makes the updates independent of how the distribution is parameterised.

### Simple ES as a gradient estimator

Fixing $\Cmat = \Imat$ and using raw $f$ values gives a Monte Carlo estimate of the gradient of the Gaussian-smoothed
objective $J(\mvec) = \expect_{\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)}[f(\mvec + \sigma\epsilonvec)]$:

$$
\nabla J(\mvec) = \frac1\sigma\expect\LB f(\mvec + \sigma\epsilonvec)\,\epsilonvec\RB \approx \frac{1}{\lambda\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^\lambda f(\mvec + \sigma\epsilonvec_i)\,\epsilonvec_i.
$$

Evaluating each perturbation in pairs $\pm\epsilonvec_i$ (antithetic sampling) cancels the constant part of $f$ and
reduces the variance. This estimator scales to policies with millions of parameters on thousands of workers, because
each worker needs only a random seed and a scalar return <Cite id="salimans2017" />.

## Worked example

Take $f(x) = x^2$ in one dimension, mean $m = 1.5$ and $\sigma = 0.3$. With $x = m + \sigma\epsilon$,

$$
\frac1\sigma\expect\LB(m + \sigma\epsilon)^2\epsilon\RB = \frac1\sigma\LP m^2\expect\epsilon + 2m\sigma\expect\epsilon^2 + \sigma^2\expect\epsilon^3\RP = 2m = 3,
$$

since $\expect\epsilon = \expect\epsilon^3 = 0$ and $\expect\epsilon^2 = 1$. The estimator recovers $f'(m) = 3$ exactly
in expectation. A Monte Carlo average over $2\times10^6$ samples gives $3.007$. The smoothed objective is
$J(m) = m^2 + \sigma^2$, which has the same minimiser as $f$. For nonconvex $f$, smoothing with larger $\sigma$
removes small local minima. This smoothing is one reason evolution strategies cope with rugged objectives.

## Cost

Each generation costs $\lambda$ evaluations of $f$, which can run in parallel. The covariance update and sampling cost
$O(n^2)$ per sample, and the <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink> used to sample from $\Cmat$
costs $O(n^3)$, amortised over several generations. This limits full CMA-ES to a few thousand variables. Diagonal and
low-rank variants scale further.

## Trade-offs

- **Evaluations.** An ES typically needs hundreds to thousands of evaluations per dimension on hard problems. When each
  evaluation is expensive, Bayesian optimisation (see <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter
  search</NoteLink>) is more economical.
- **Gradients available.** When $f$ is differentiable and cheap to differentiate, gradient-based methods use far fewer
  evaluations. The ES gradient estimator's variance grows with the dimension.
- **Invariance.** CMA-ES is invariant to rotations of the search space and to monotone transformations of $f$. Few
  other methods have both.
- **Multimodality.** Restart strategies with increasing population (IPOP-CMA-ES) handle many local minima. A single run
  converges to one basin.
