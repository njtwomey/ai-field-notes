<Definition>
For $F(\xvec) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f_i(\xvec)$, **SVRG** (stochastic variance reduced gradient) keeps a snapshot
$\tilde\xvec$ and its full <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $\nabla F(\tilde\xvec)$, and steps along

$$
\gvec_k = \nabla f_i(\xvec_k) - \nabla f_i(\tilde\xvec) + \nabla F(\tilde\xvec), \qquad i \sim \Unif\set{1, \dots, n},
$$

refreshing the snapshot every $m$ steps <Cite id="johnson2013" />. **SAGA** stores the last gradient $\alphavec_j$ seen
for every $j$ and steps along $\nabla f_i(\xvec_k) - \alphavec_i + \frac1n\sum_j\alphavec_j$, then sets
$\alphavec_i \leftarrow \nabla f_i(\xvec_k)$ <Cite id="defazio2014" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">SGD</NoteLink> with a constant step stalls at a noise floor
proportional to the step times the gradient variance. The variance does not vanish at the optimum: at $\xvec^*$ the
average gradient is zero, but the individual $\nabla f_i(\xvec^*)$ are not. Decreasing steps remove the floor but slow
convergence to $O(1/k)$. Full <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> converges linearly but costs
$n$ gradients per step.

When $F$ is a finite sum, as a training loss over a fixed dataset is, the noise has structure: it comes from a fixed,
finite set of functions that are revisited. Variance-reduced methods exploit this to combine the cheap steps of
<Gloss name="sgd" /> with the linear rate of gradient descent.

## Mechanism

### A control variate

To estimate the mean of a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$, subtract a correlated variable
$Y$ whose mean is known and add the mean back: $Z = X - Y + \expect Y$. Then $\expect Z = \expect X$, and

$$
\var(Z) = \var(X) + \var(Y) - 2\cov(X, Y).
$$

When $X$ and $Y$ are strongly positively correlated, $\var(Z)$ is much smaller than $\var(X)$. This is the
**control variate** technique from Monte Carlo estimation.

SVRG applies it with $X = \nabla f_i(\xvec)$ and $Y = \nabla f_i(\tilde\xvec)$, using the same random index $i$ for
both. The mean of $Y$ over $i$ is the full gradient $\nabla F(\tilde\xvec)$, computed once per snapshot.

**The estimate is unbiased.** Averaging over $i$, $\expect[\gvec_k] = \nabla F(\xvec_k) - \nabla F(\tilde\xvec) +
\nabla F(\tilde\xvec) = \nabla F(\xvec_k)$.

**Its variance vanishes as the iterates converge.** The variance of a vector is at most its second moment, so

$$
\expect\norm{\gvec_k - \nabla F(\xvec_k)}^2 \le \expect_i\norm{\nabla f_i(\xvec_k) - \nabla f_i(\tilde\xvec)}^2 \le L^2\norm{\xvec_k - \tilde\xvec}^2,
$$

when each $f_i$ is $L$-smooth. As $\xvec_k$ and $\tilde\xvec$ both approach $\xvec^*$, the bound goes to zero. The two
gradients share the same example, so their difference cancels the part of the noise that comes from which example was
drawn. Only the part that comes from the iterate having moved since the snapshot remains.

### SVRG

1. Compute the full gradient $\nabla F(\tilde\xvec)$ at the snapshot: $n$ gradient evaluations.
2. Run $m$ inner steps $\xvec \leftarrow \xvec - \eta\gvec_k$ with the corrected gradient, each costing two
   gradient evaluations.
3. Set the snapshot to the last (or an averaged) inner iterate and repeat.

For $\mu$-strongly convex $F$ and $L$-smooth $f_i$, with $\eta$ a small constant fraction of $1/L$ and
$m$ of order $\kappa = L/\mu$, each outer loop reduces the expected suboptimality by a constant factor
<Cite id="johnson2013" />. The total cost to reach accuracy $\epsilon$ is $O((n + \kappa)\log(1/\epsilon))$ gradient
evaluations.

### SAG and SAGA

SAG and SAGA avoid the periodic full pass by storing one gradient per example. **SAG** (stochastic average gradient)
steps along the average of the stored gradients after refreshing one of them <Cite id="leroux2012" />
<Cite id="schmidt2017" />. The average is a biased estimate of $\nabla F(\xvec_k)$, which complicates the analysis.
**SAGA** uses the unbiased control-variate form in the definition. Both have the same $O((n + \kappa)\log(1/\epsilon))$
complexity. SAGA also handles a nonsmooth regulariser through a proximal step.

For linear models, $\nabla f_i(\xvec) = \ell'(\avec_i^\top\xvec)\avec_i$, so storing the scalar $\ell'$ is enough and
the memory is $O(n)$ rather than $O(nd)$.

## Worked example

Least squares with $f_i(\wvec) = \tfrac12(\avec_i^\top\wvec - y_i)^2$ on five examples: rows $\avec_i$ equal to
$(1, 2), (2, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1), (3, 1)$ and targets $\yvec = (-0.7, 1.8, -0.9, -0.4, 2.2)$. The minimiser is
$\wvec^* = (0.967, -0.9)$.

- **SGD at the optimum.** The individual gradients at $\wvec^*$ average to zero, but their mean squared norm is
  $0.199$. That is the variance an SGD step carries even at the solution.
- **SVRG with a nearby snapshot.** With $\tilde\wvec = \wvec^* + (0.05, 0)$, the variance of the SVRG estimate at
  $\wvec^*$ is $0.030$, about 6.5 times smaller. With $\tilde\wvec = \wvec^* + (0.01, 0)$ it is $0.0012$. For least
  squares the correction $\nabla f_i(\wvec) - \nabla f_i(\tilde\wvec) = \avec_i\avec_i^\top(\wvec - \tilde\wvec)$
  depends only on $\wvec - \tilde\wvec$, so the variance scales exactly with $\norm{\wvec - \tilde\wvec}^2$: dividing
  the distance by 5 divides the variance by 25.

## Cost

| Method | Gradient evaluations to reach $\epsilon$ (strongly convex) | Extra memory |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gradient descent | $O(n\kappa\log(1/\epsilon))$ | none |
| SGD, decreasing steps | $O(\kappa\sigma^2/(\mu\epsilon))$ | none |
| SVRG | $O((n + \kappa)\log(1/\epsilon))$ | one snapshot and its gradient |
| SAG, SAGA | $O((n + \kappa)\log(1/\epsilon))$ | one gradient (or scalar) per example |

For $n$ and $\kappa$ both large, $n + \kappa$ is much smaller than $n\kappa$.

## Trade-offs

- **Finite sums only.** The methods need to revisit examples. For streaming data or
  <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>, where every sample is new, the reference gradients go
  stale and the benefit disappears.
- **Deep networks.** Variance-reduced methods have not beaten tuned <NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">SGD with
  momentum</NoteLink> on deep networks <Cite id="bottou2018" />. The snapshot gradients go stale quickly when the loss
  is highly nonconvex, and <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> and data augmentation break
  the finite-sum structure.
- **Modest accuracy.** When only a few passes over the data are affordable, plain SGD makes more progress per pass at
  the start. Variance reduction pays off when high accuracy is needed, as in convex models trained to convergence.
- **Step size.** A constant step of order $1/L$ works. With SGD, a constant step that large would sit on a high noise
  floor.
