<Definition>
Stochastic optimisation minimises an objective using random, unbiased estimates
of its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. In machine learning the objective is an average loss over $n$
training examples, and the estimate is the average gradient over a small random minibatch. The theory describes how the
noise in these estimates limits accuracy, and how step sizes, batch sizes and variance reduction control it
<Cite id="bottou2018" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Training data sets are too large for a full gradient at every step. <Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient
descent</Gloss> touches only a minibatch, so its cost per step does not grow with the data set, and it makes progress
long before a full pass is complete. Every deep network is trained this way. The theory explains why a constant learning
rate stalls at a noise floor, why learning rates are decayed, why averaging the iterates helps, and why doubling the
batch size helps only up to a point. It also guides how to scale the learning rate with the batch size when training on
many accelerators.

## Main ideas

**Unbiased noise still converges.** <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory" /> steps along noisy gradients
whose mean is the true gradient. For convex $f$ with bounded gradients the averaged iterate reaches error
$O(1/\sqrt{K})$ after $K$ steps. With a constant step on a strongly convex $f$, the error falls quickly to a noise floor
proportional to the step. Steps with $\sum_k \eta_k = \infty$ and $\sum_k \eta_k^2 < \infty$ remove the floor, the
conditions of Robbins and Monro <Cite id="robbins1951" />.

**Bigger batches, less noise, diminishing returns.** <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size" /> shows that
averaging $B$ example gradients divides the gradient variance by $B$. Up to a critical batch size, set by the ratio of
gradient noise to signal, a larger batch allows a proportionally larger step. Beyond it, extra examples buy little.

**Noise that vanishes at the optimum.** <NoteLink to="variance-reduction" /> methods such as SVRG, SAG and SAGA, for a
finite sum of $n$ losses, correct each stochastic gradient with a stored reference gradient. The estimate stays
unbiased, its variance vanishes at the optimum, and a constant step converges linearly.
