<Definition>
Convex analysis studies convex sets, which contain the segment between any two of their points, and convex functions,
whose graph lies below every chord. For a convex function every local minimum is a global minimum, and simple
first-order conditions certify optimality. The theory supplies the optimality conditions, the duality and the
convergence guarantees of convex optimisation <Cite id="boyd2004" />
<Cite id="rockafellar1970" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Many classical learning problems are convex: least squares, ridge and lasso regression,
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, support vector machines and <Gloss name="mle">maximum
likelihood</Gloss> in <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential families</NoteLink>. For these a solver finds the
global optimum, and its rate of convergence can be proved. Deep networks are not convex, but convex analysis still
supplies the vocabulary of smoothness, strong convexity and subgradients that their optimisers use. Nonsmooth penalties
such as the $\ell_1$ norm and the <Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss> need subgradients. Conjugate functions
turn a problem into its dual, which is how the support vector machine reaches its kernel form.

## Main ideas

**Convexity is a chord condition.** A function $f$ is convex when
$f(\theta\xvec + (1 - \theta)\yvec) \le \theta f(\xvec) + (1 - \theta)f(\yvec)$ for every $\theta \in [0, 1]$. The note
on <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex sets and functions</NoteLink> gives the equivalent tests for
differentiable functions: the graph lies above every tangent, or the Hessian is positive semidefinite. A zero gradient
then certifies a global minimum.

**Kinks have many supporting lines.** A <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradient</NoteLink> of a convex $f$ at $\xvec$ is
the slope of any line through $(\xvec, f(\xvec))$ that stays below the graph. At a kink there are many of them, and the
set $\partial f(\xvec)$ replaces the gradient. A point minimises $f$ if and only if $\zeros \in \partial f(\xvec)$.

**Functions have duals.** The <NoteLink to="conjugate-functions">convex conjugate</NoteLink>
$f^*(\yvec) = \sup_{\xvec}(\yvec^\top\xvec - f(\xvec))$ describes $f$ by its supporting hyperplanes instead of its
values. It is always convex, it satisfies the Fenchel–Young inequality $f(\xvec) + f^*(\yvec) \ge \yvec^\top\xvec$,
and for closed convex $f$ conjugating twice returns $f$.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="hessian" /> and
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite matrices</NoteLink>.

<NoteLink to="first-order-methods" /> prove their rates for convex and strongly convex functions, and proximal and

<NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink>
are convex problems with nonsmooth terms.
