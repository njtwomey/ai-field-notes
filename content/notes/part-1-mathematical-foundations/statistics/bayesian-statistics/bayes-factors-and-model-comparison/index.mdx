<Definition>
For two models $M_0$ and $M_1$ with parameters $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$, the
**<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>** (evidence) of model $M_j$ is
$p(y \mid M_j) = \int p(y \mid \theta_j, M_j)\, p(\theta_j \mid M_j)\, d\theta_j$. The **Bayes factor** is their ratio,

$$
B_{10} = \frac{p(y \mid M_1)}{p(y \mid M_0)}, \qquad
\frac{P(M_1 \mid y)}{P(M_0 \mid y)} = B_{10} \times \frac{P(M_1)}{P(M_0)}.
$$

It is the factor by which the data change the odds of $M_1$ against $M_0$ <Cite id="kass1995" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The marginal likelihood is the prior predictive probability of the observed data: the average of the likelihood over the
model's prior. A model that spreads its prior over many parameter values predicts many possible data sets, so it gives
each one a small probability. A simpler model makes sharper predictions. If the data fall where the simpler model
predicted, it has the larger marginal likelihood. This automatic penalty for flexibility is the
**<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">Bayesian Occam's razor</NoteLink>**. The penalty depends on
how widely the prior is spread, even over values the data rule out.

The posterior-odds formula follows from <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> applied to the model
index: $P(M_j \mid y) \propto p(y \mid M_j)\,P(M_j)$, and the normaliser cancels in the ratio.

Kass and Raftery <Cite id="kass1995" /> give a scale for reading $B_{10}$: 1 to 3 is barely worth mentioning, 3 to 20
positive, 20 to 150 strong, and above 150 very strong evidence for $M_1$.

## Worked example: testing a coin for fairness

$M_0$ fixes $\theta = 0.5$. $M_1$ gives $\theta$ a uniform prior. With $k$ heads in $n$ tosses,

$$
p(y \mid M_0) = \binom nk 2^{-n}, \qquad
p(y \mid M_1) = \int_0^1 \binom nk \theta^k(1 - \theta)^{n - k}\, d\theta = \binom nk B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = \frac{1}{n + 1}.
$$

The last step uses $B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = k!\,(n - k)!/(n + 1)!$. Under $M_1$ every $k$ from 0 to $n$ is equally likely.
For outcomes whose two-sided <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink>, from the normal approximation to the binomial,
is about 0.05:

| $n$ | $k$ | $z$ | two-sided $p$ | $B_{01}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 60 | 2.00 | 0.046 | 1.1 |
| 1,000 | 531 | 1.96 | 0.050 | 3.7 |
| 10,000 | 5,098 | 1.96 | 0.050 | 11.7 |
| 100,000 | 50,310 | 1.96 | 0.050 | 36.9 |

Each row rejects $M_0$ at the 5% level, yet the Bayes factor favours $M_0$, and more strongly as $n$ grows.

## Lindley's paradox

With $z = (k - n/2)/\sqrt{n/4}$, the normal approximation to the binomial gives
$\binom nk 2^{-n} \approx \phi(z)/\sqrt{n/4}$, where $\phi$ is the standard normal density. Therefore

$$
B_{01} = (n + 1)\binom nk 2^{-n} \approx \frac{2(n + 1)}{\sqrt n}\,\phi(z) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2n}{\pi}}\; e^{-z^2/2}.
$$

At fixed $z$, and so at a fixed p-value, $B_{01}$ grows like $\sqrt n$. For any
<NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink> there is a sample size at which a result that is just
significant is strong evidence for the null. This is **Lindley's paradox** <Cite id="lindley1957" />.

The two methods answer different questions. The p-value measures how surprising the data are under $M_0$ alone. The
Bayes factor compares $M_0$ with a specific alternative. When $n$ is large, $\hat\theta = k/n$ is within about
$1/\sqrt n$ of 0.5. The uniform prior of $M_1$ spreads its mass over $[0, 1]$ and assigns only about $1/\sqrt n$ of
it to values that close to 0.5, so $M_1$ predicted the data poorly.

## Sensitivity to the prior

For a normal mean with known $\sigma$, test $M_0: \mu = 0$ against $M_1: \mu \sim \Gauss(0, \tau^2)$. The sample mean
$\bar y$ is sufficient, with $\bar y \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2/n)$ under $M_0$ and $\bar y \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2/n +
\tau^2)$ under $M_1$. The ratio of these two densities at $\bar y$, with $z = \sqrt n\,\bar y/\sigma$, is

$$
B_{01} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{n\tau^2}{\sigma^2}}\; \exp\paren{-\frac{z^2}{2}\cdot\frac{n\tau^2}{\sigma^2 + n\tau^2}}.
$$

As $\tau \to \infty$ the exponential tends to $e^{-z^2/2}$ and the square root grows without bound, so $B_{01} \to
\infty$ for any data. With $n = 100$, $\sigma = 1$ and $z = 1.96$, the prior scales $\tau = 0.1, 1, 10, 100$ give
$B_{01} = 0.54, 1.5, 14.7, 146$. The same data favour $M_1$ or strongly favour $M_0$ depending on a prior scale that
barely changes the posterior for $\mu$ under $M_1$.

Consequences:

- An <NoteLink to="prior-choice">improper prior</NoteLink>, $p(\theta) \propto 1$, has an arbitrary constant. The
  constant cancels in a posterior but not in a marginal likelihood. Bayes factors need proper priors on every parameter
  that is not shared by both models.
- Bayes factors are only as meaningful as the priors within the models. Report how $B_{10}$ changes over a range of
  reasonable priors.
- $-2\log B_{10}$ is approximated by the difference in BIC, which corresponds to a unit-information prior; see
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-information-criterion">Bayesian information criterion</NoteLink>.

## Predictive model comparison: LOO and WAIC

An alternative compares models by how well their <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">posterior predictive
distributions</NoteLink> predict new data, measured by the expected log pointwise predictive density.
<NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS leave-one-out</NoteLink> estimates Bayesian
leave-one-out <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> from draws of the full posterior by
<NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink>, and the shape $\hat k$ of a Pareto fit to the largest
weights flags the observations where the estimate is unreliable <Cite id="vehtari2017" />. The
<NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">widely applicable information criterion</NoteLink> subtracts the
summed posterior variance of each observation's log-likelihood from the log pointwise predictive density
<Cite id="watanabe2010" />. It is asymptotically equal to leave-one-out and remains valid for singular models such as
mixtures and neural networks. Both criteria use the posterior, so a vague prior that the data overwhelm hardly affects
them; the Bayes factor uses the prior predictive distribution and is sensitive to such a prior.

## Assumptions

- Both models' priors are proper and chosen with the same care as the likelihoods.
- For <NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">posterior model probabilities</NoteLink>, the candidate set contains a
  model close to the truth. When every model is wrong, Bayes factors still pick one with increasing confidence as $n$
  grows; predictive criteria are then the better target.

## Pitfalls

- **Default vague priors.** A wide prior under the alternative inflates $B_{01}$. Choose the scale from the problem.
- **Computing the marginal likelihood from posterior draws.** The harmonic-mean estimator
  $\paren{\frac1S\sum_s 1/p(y \mid \theta^{(s)})}^{-1}$ has infinite variance in common models. Use bridge sampling,
  nested sampling or a closed form.
- **Treating $B_{10}$ as a posterior probability.** Multiplying it by the prior odds gives the posterior odds $o$, and
  the posterior probability of $M_1$ is $o/(1 + o)$.
- **Comparing WAIC or <Gloss name="loo" /> values without their <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
  errors</NoteLink>.** Both are sums over observations; compare the pointwise differences and their standard error.
