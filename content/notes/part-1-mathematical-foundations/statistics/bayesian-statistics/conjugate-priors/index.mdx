<Definition>
A family $\Fcal$ of <Gloss name="prior">prior distributions</Gloss> is **conjugate** to a likelihood $p(y \mid \theta)$
if, for every prior in $\Fcal$ and every data set $y$, the posterior
$p(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$ is also in $\Fcal$. The update then maps prior hyperparameters to
posterior hyperparameters in closed form, with no integration <Cite id="gelman2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Bayes' rule multiplies the prior density by the likelihood. If the prior density has the same functional form in
$\theta$ as the likelihood, the product keeps that form and only the exponents change. The prior then behaves like data
already seen: its hyperparameters count **pseudo-observations**, and the posterior adds the real observations to them.
A $\Beta(3, 7)$ prior on a success probability carries the weight of 10
earlier trials with 3 successes.

## Beta–Binomial

Let $k$ be the number of successes in $n$ independent trials with success probability $\theta$, so
$p(k \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1 - \theta)^{n - k}$. Take the prior $\theta \sim \Beta(a, b)$, with density
$\theta^{a - 1}(1 - \theta)^{b - 1}/B(a, b)$ on $[0, 1]$. Drop every factor that does not involve $\theta$:

$$
p(\theta \mid k) \propto \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k} \cdot \theta^{a - 1}(1 - \theta)^{b - 1}
= \theta^{a + k - 1}(1 - \theta)^{b + n - k - 1}.
$$

The right-hand side is the kernel of a Beta density. A density is determined by its kernel, because the normalising
constant is fixed by the requirement that it integrates to 1. So

$$
\theta \mid k \sim \Beta(a + k,\; b + n - k).
$$

The prior counts $a$ successes and $b$ failures. The posterior mean is

$$
\expect[\theta \mid k] = \frac{a + k}{a + b + n} = \frac{a + b}{a + b + n}\cdot\frac{a}{a + b} + \frac{n}{a + b + n}\cdot\frac{k}{n},
$$

a weighted average of the prior mean $a/(a + b)$ and the sample proportion $k/n$. The weight on the data is
$n/(\kappa + n)$ with **prior strength** $\kappa = a + b$. The posterior variance
$(a + k)(b + n - k)/[(\kappa + n)^2(\kappa + n + 1)]$ shrinks at rate $1/n$.

<BetaUpdate />

The posterior predictive probability of $\tilde k$ successes in $m$ new trials is the **beta-binomial** distribution,

$$
p(\tilde k \mid k) = \int_0^1 \binom{m}{\tilde k}\theta^{\tilde k}(1 - \theta)^{m - \tilde k}\, p(\theta \mid k)\, d\theta
= \binom{m}{\tilde k}\frac{B(a + k + \tilde k,\; b + n - k + m - \tilde k)}{B(a + k,\; b + n - k)}.
$$

The integral is the normalising constant of a Beta density with the exponents added, divided by the posterior's
constant.

## Gamma–Poisson

Let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ be independent counts with $y_i \sim \Poisson(\lambda)$, and take $\lambda \sim \GammaD(\alpha,
\beta)$ with shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$, density proportional to $\lambda^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta\lambda}$. The
likelihood is $\prod_i \lambda^{y_i}e^{-\lambda}/y_i! \propto \lambda^{S}e^{-n\lambda}$ with $S = \sum_i y_i$. Then

$$
p(\lambda \mid y) \propto \lambda^{S}e^{-n\lambda}\cdot\lambda^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta\lambda}
= \lambda^{\alpha + S - 1}e^{-(\beta + n)\lambda},
\qquad \lambda \mid y \sim \GammaD(\alpha + S,\; \beta + n).
$$

The prior counts $\alpha$ events in $\beta$ units of exposure. The posterior mean $(\alpha + S)/(\beta + n)$ is a
weighted average of the prior mean $\alpha/\beta$ and the sample mean $\bar y = S/n$, with weight $n/(\beta + n)$ on the
data. The <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">posterior predictive distribution</NoteLink> of a new count is
<NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink>.

**Worked example.** The prior $\GammaD(2, 1)$ has mean 2. Five days give counts $3, 5, 4, 6, 2$, so $S = 20$ and
$n = 5$. The posterior is $\GammaD(22, 6)$, with mean $22/6 = 3.67$ and central 95% interval $[2.30, 5.35]$. The sample
mean is 4.0; the prior pulls the estimate toward 2 with weight $1/6$.

## Normal–Normal with known variance

Let $y_1, \dots, y_n \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known, and take $\mu \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$.
Writing $\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 + n(\bar y - \mu)^2$ and dropping terms free of $\mu$,

$$
\log p(\mu \mid y) = -\frac{(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2} - \frac{n(\mu - \bar y)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}.
$$

This is a quadratic in $\mu$ with leading coefficient $-\tfrac12\paren{1/\tau_0^2 + n/\sigma^2}$, so the posterior is
Gaussian. Matching it to $-(\mu - \mu_n)^2/(2\tau_n^2)$ by completing the square gives

$$
\frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad
\mu_n = \tau_n^2\paren{\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}}.
$$

Precisions add, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean. The
prior is worth $\sigma^2/\tau_0^2$ observations. For $\mu_0 = 0$, $\tau_0^2 = 1$, $\sigma^2 = 4$, $n = 4$ and $\bar y =
2$, the two precisions are both 1, the posterior precision is 2, and $\mu \mid y \sim \Gauss(1, 0.5)$.

## Normal–Inverse-Gamma

When both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are unknown, the conjugate prior is

$$
\sigma^2 \sim \InvGamma(a_0, b_0), \qquad \mu \mid \sigma^2 \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma^2/\kappa_0).
$$

The prior variance of $\mu$ scales with $\sigma^2$, so $\kappa_0$ counts prior observations of the mean. The posterior
has the same form with

$$
\kappa_n = \kappa_0 + n, \quad \mu_n = \frac{\kappa_0\mu_0 + n\bar y}{\kappa_n}, \quad a_n = a_0 + \frac n2, \quad
b_n = b_0 + \frac12\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n(\bar y - \mu_0)^2}{2\kappa_n}.
$$

Integrating out $\sigma^2$, the marginal posterior of $\mu$ is a <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t
distribution</NoteLink> with $2a_n$ degrees of freedom, location $\mu_n$ and squared scale $b_n/(a_n\kappa_n)$. The
heavier-than-Gaussian tails come from the uncertainty in $\sigma^2$. The same prior on regression weights and noise
variance gives the conjugate <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>.

<Derivation title="The Normal–Inverse-Gamma update">
Write $s = \sigma^2$. The joint prior density is proportional to

$$
s^{-a_0 - 1}e^{-b_0/s}\cdot s^{-1/2}\exp\paren{-\frac{\kappa_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2}{2s}},
$$

and the likelihood is $s^{-n/2}\exp\paren{-\frac{1}{2s}\brack{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 + n(\mu - \bar y)^2}}$.
Their product is $s^{-(a_0 + n/2) - 1}\,s^{-1/2}\exp(-Q/s)$ with

$$
Q = b_0 + \frac12\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 + \frac12\brack{\kappa_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + n(\mu - \bar y)^2}.
$$

<NoteLink to="completing-the-square">Complete the square</NoteLink> in $\mu$:
$\kappa_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + n(\mu - \bar y)^2 = \kappa_n(\mu - \mu_n)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n}{\kappa_n}(\bar y - \mu_0)^2$,
which can be checked by expanding both sides. So $Q = b_n + \frac{\kappa_n}{2}(\mu - \mu_n)^2$, and the posterior is

$$
s^{-a_n - 1}e^{-b_n/s}\cdot s^{-1/2}\exp\paren{-\frac{\kappa_n(\mu - \mu_n)^2}{2s}},
$$

which is $\InvGamma(a_n, b_n)$ for $s$ times $\Gauss(\mu_n, s/\kappa_n)$ for $\mu$ given $s$.

For the marginal of $\mu$, integrate $s$ out. The integrand is an unnormalised inverse-gamma density in $s$ with shape
$a_n + \tfrac12$ and scale $b_n + \tfrac{\kappa_n}{2}(\mu - \mu_n)^2$, so the integral is proportional to

$$
\paren{b_n + \frac{\kappa_n}{2}(\mu - \mu_n)^2}^{-(a_n + 1/2)} \propto
\paren{1 + \frac{1}{2a_n}\cdot\frac{(\mu - \mu_n)^2}{b_n/(a_n\kappa_n)}}^{-(2a_n + 1)/2},
$$

the kernel of a Student t density with $\nu = 2a_n$ degrees of freedom, location $\mu_n$ and squared scale
$b_n/(a_n\kappa_n)$.
</Derivation>

## Why these families: the exponential family

Every <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> likelihood has a conjugate prior. Write the
likelihood of one observation as $p(y \mid \theta) = h(y)\exp\paren{\eta(\theta)^\top T(y) - A(\theta)}$, with natural
parameter $\eta(\theta)$, <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink> $T(y)$ and log-partition
$A(\theta)$. The family

$$
p(\theta \mid \chi, \nu) \propto \exp\paren{\eta(\theta)^\top\chi - \nu A(\theta)}
$$

is conjugate. Multiplying by the likelihood of $n$ independent observations adds $\sum_i T(y_i)$ to $\chi$ and $n$ to
$\nu$:

$$
p(\theta \mid y) \propto \exp\paren{\eta(\theta)^\top\paren{\chi + \textstyle\sum_i T(y_i)} - (\nu + n)A(\theta)}.
$$

$\nu$ is the number of pseudo-observations and $\chi$ their summed sufficient statistics. Each update above is this
rule. For the Bernoulli, $\eta = \log\frac{\theta}{1 - \theta}$, $T(y) = y$ and $A = -\log(1 - \theta)$, and the
conjugate family is the Beta.

| Likelihood | Conjugate prior | Posterior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\Binom(n, \theta)$ | $\Beta(a, b)$ | $\Beta(a + k, b + n - k)$ |
| $\Poisson(\lambda)$, $n$ counts | $\GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$ | $\GammaD(\alpha + \sum y_i, \beta + n)$ |
| $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2$ known | $\Gauss(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$ | $\Gauss(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ |
| $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, both unknown | Normal–Inverse-Gamma | Normal–Inverse-Gamma |
| $\Cat(\thetavec)$, counts $\nvec$ | $\Dir(\alphavec)$ | $\Dir(\alphavec + \nvec)$ |
| $\Exp(\lambda)$, $n$ times | $\GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$ | $\GammaD(\alpha + n, \beta + \sum y_i)$ |

## Assumptions

- The observations are conditionally independent given $\theta$, with the stated likelihood.
- The prior belongs to the conjugate family. Conjugacy is a convenience of computation. A prior should be chosen to
  express knowledge, and a mixture of conjugate priors is still conjugate when a single member cannot express it.

## Pitfalls

- **Choosing the family for convenience.** A Beta prior cannot put mass on two separated regions; a Gamma prior cannot
  have a heavy left tail. When the conjugate family does not express the prior knowledge, use a different prior and
  approximate the posterior numerically.
- **Hidden prior strength.** $\Beta(50, 50)$ carries the weight of 100 trials. State $\kappa$ next to $n$.
- **Parameterisations.** The Gamma distribution has shape–rate and shape–scale forms. The update adds $n$ to the rate,
  which is $1/\text{scale}$.
