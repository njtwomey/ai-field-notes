<Definition>
Experimental design decides how units are assigned to treatments before any data are collected, so that the effect of
a treatment on an outcome can be estimated without bias and with small variance. Its central tool is randomisation,
which makes the treated and untreated groups alike in everything except the treatment
<Cite id="fisher1935b" /> <Cite id="imbens2015" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every A/B test of a product change, every ablation study of a model and every clinical trial is an experiment.
Randomisation is what lets the measured difference be read as a causal effect. Blocking on a strong predictor of the
outcome, such as a user's previous activity, shrinks the variance of the estimate and the sample needed. Factorial
designs test several changes in one experiment and measure how they interact. When an experiment is impossible, the
same framework says which observational comparisons can still be trusted.

## Main ideas

**Each unit has two potential outcomes.** In <NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">randomised controlled
experiments</NoteLink>, unit $i$ has outcome $Y_i(1)$ if treated and $Y_i(0)$ if not, and only one is ever observed.
Random assignment makes treatment independent of both, so the difference in group means is an unbiased estimate of the
average treatment effect.

**Compare like with like.** <NoteLink to="blocking-and-stratification" /> groups similar units and randomises within
each group. Differences between blocks cancel from the estimated effect, which cuts its variance by the fraction of
outcome variance they explain. A paired design is blocking with blocks of two.

**Vary several factors at once.** A <NoteLink to="factorial-designs">factorial design</NoteLink> runs every
combination of the levels of several factors. In a $2^k$ design every main effect and interaction is estimated from all
$N$ runs. A fractional factorial runs a subset of the combinations, at the cost of confounding some effects with
others.

**Without randomisation, adjust for confounders.**
<NoteLink to="causal-inference-from-observational-data" /> estimates an effect when treatment was not randomised. If
measured covariates block every back-door path from treatment to outcome, and every stratum contains both treated and
untreated units, the effect is identified and estimated by stratification, regression or propensity weighting.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">randomised controlled experiments</NoteLink>, which defines
potential outcomes and the treatment effect for every later note. <NoteLink to="blocking-and-stratification" /> and
<NoteLink to="factorial-designs">factorial designs</NoteLink> refine the design of an experiment and can be read in
either order. <NoteLink to="causal-inference-from-observational-data" /> comes last: it applies the same definitions
when the design was not under control.
