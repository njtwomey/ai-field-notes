<Definition>
Estimation uses a sample to approximate an unknown quantity of the population it came from, such as a mean, a proportion
or a model parameter $\theta$. An estimator $\hat\theta$ is a function of the data, so it is a
<NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> with its own sampling distribution. Estimation theory studies
how close that distribution sits to $\theta$ and how to report the remaining uncertainty <Cite id="casella2002" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every number reported about a model is an estimate. A test-set accuracy estimates the accuracy on future data, an
<Gloss name="ab-test" /> estimates the lift of a new variant, and training a model estimates its parameters. Each
estimate needs an error bar to be useful: two accuracies that differ by less than their standard errors do not separate
two models. Effect sizes say whether a statistically detectable difference is large enough to matter.
Expectation–maximisation fits mixture models, <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov models</Gloss> and models with missing
data.

## Main ideas

**An estimate varies from sample to sample.** The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> is the
standard deviation of an estimator across repeated samples. For the mean of $n$ independent observations it is
$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, estimated by $s/\sqrt{n}$. Halving it takes four times as much data.

**Intervals carry the uncertainty.** A <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> comes from a
procedure that contains the true parameter in a stated fraction of repeated samples. It is also the set of parameter
values that a level-$\alpha$ test would not reject, which ties estimation to hypothesis testing.

**Size, not only significance.** An <NoteLink to="effect-size">effect size</NoteLink> measures how large an effect is.
Raw effects keep the outcome's units. Standardised effects such as Cohen's $d$ divide by a standard deviation so that
studies on different scales can be compared.

**Some summaries lose nothing.** A <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic" /> carries all the information a sample has about
a parameter. The factorisation theorem finds one by splitting the likelihood. For an
<NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> the sufficient statistic has a fixed dimension whatever
the sample size.

**Hidden variables need iteration.** <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation" /> finds maximum-likelihood estimates when
some variables are unobserved. Each iteration builds a lower bound on the log-likelihood that touches it at the current
parameters, then maximises the bound, so the likelihood never decreases.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">variance
and covariance</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink>.
Expectation–maximisation also uses the <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence" />.

<NoteLink to="hypothesis-testing" /> turns estimates into decisions. <NoteLink to="resampling" /> estimates standard
errors and intervals where no formula exists. <NoteLink to="bayesian-statistics" /> replaces the sampling distribution
by a posterior over the parameter, and <NoteLink to="credible-intervals-and-highest-posterior-density">credible
intervals</NoteLink> replace confidence intervals. In learning, <Gloss name="mle">maximum likelihood estimation</Gloss>
is the principle behind most training objectives, and expectation–maximisation fits the
<NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model" />.
