<Definition>
Let $T \ge 0$ be the time until an event, with density $f$ and distribution function $F(t) = P(T \le t)$. The **survival
function** is $S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t)$. The **<NoteLink to="bayesian-online-changepoint-detection">hazard
function</NoteLink>** is

$$
h(t) = \lim_{\Delta \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + \Delta \mid T \ge t)}{\Delta} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)},
$$

the instantaneous event rate at time $t$ among units that have not yet had the event. The **cumulative hazard** is
$H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du$ <Cite id="kalbfleisch2002" />.
</Definition>

## Hazard versus density

A density answers "how likely is the event at $t$, from the start". A hazard answers "how likely is the event in the
next instant, given that it has not happened yet". The second question is the natural one for failures, deaths and
customer churn. It is also what data provide: at each time, the units still under observation and the events among
them. The hazard is a rate, with units of events per unit time, and is not bounded by 1.

## From hazard to survival

The <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink> in the definition is
$\paren{F(t + \Delta) - F(t)}/S(t)$. Dividing by $\Delta$ and letting $\Delta \to 0$ gives $h(t) = f(t)/S(t)$. Since
$f = F' = -S'$,

$$
h(t) = -\frac{S'(t)}{S(t)} = -\frac{d}{dt}\log S(t).
$$

Integrate from 0 to $t$ and use $S(0) = 1$, so $\log S(0) = 0$:

$$
\log S(t) = -\int_0^t h(u)\,du = -H(t), \qquad S(t) = \exp\paren{-H(t)}.
$$

The density follows as $f(t) = h(t)\exp(-H(t))$. So any one of $f$, $S$, $h$ and $H$ determines the other three. Any
non-negative $h$ with $\int_0^\infty h = \infty$ defines a proper distribution. If the integral is finite, a fraction
$e^{-H(\infty)}$ never has the event, a **cure fraction**.

The mean time to event is the area under the survival curve:

$$
\expect[T] = \int_0^\infty t\,f(t)\,dt = \int_0^\infty \int_0^t du\, f(t)\,dt = \int_0^\infty \int_u^\infty f(t)\,dt\,du =
\int_0^\infty S(u)\,du,
$$

by swapping the order of integration. The median is the time $t$ at which $S(t) = 1/2$.

## Shapes of hazard

- **Constant:** $h(t) = \lambda$ gives $H(t) = \lambda t$ and $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, the
  <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>. The chance of the event in the next
  instant does not depend on how long the unit has lasted: the distribution is memoryless.
- **Monotone:** the Weibull hazard $h(t) = (k/\sigma)(t/\sigma)^{k-1}$, with shape $k$ and scale $\sigma$, gives
  $S(t) = \exp\paren{-(t/\sigma)^k}$ <Cite id="weibull1951" />. It rises for $k > 1$ (wear-out), is constant at
  $k = 1$, and falls for $k < 1$ (early failures that thin out the weak units).
- **Bathtub:** high early, low in the middle, high late. Human mortality over a lifetime and many manufactured products
  follow this pattern. It needs a more flexible form, such as a piecewise-constant hazard.

## Worked example

A component fails with constant hazard $\lambda = 0.1$ per month. Then $S(12) = e^{-1.2} = 0.301$: 30% of components
last a year. The median lifetime solves $e^{-0.1t} = 1/2$, so $t = \log 2 / 0.1 = 6.93$ months. The mean is
$\int_0^\infty e^{-0.1t}dt = 10$ months. The mean exceeds the median because the distribution has a long right tail.

A second component has a Weibull lifetime with $k = 2$ and $\sigma = 10$ months. Its hazard $h(t) = t/50$ is 0.1 at 5
months and 0.2 at 10 months: an old component is twice as likely to fail in the next month as one half its age. The
median solves $(t/10)^2 = \log 2$, giving $t = 10\sqrt{\log 2} = 8.33$ months.

## Discrete time

When time is measured in discrete periods $1, 2, \dots$, the hazard is a probability, $h_j = P(T = j \mid T \ge j)$.
Surviving past period $j$ means surviving each period in turn, so

$$
S(j) = \prod_{m=1}^j (1 - h_m).
$$

This product is the form of the <NoteLink to="kaplan-meier-estimator">Kaplan–Meier estimator</NoteLink>. For small
$h_m$, $\log(1 - h_m) \approx -h_m$, and the product approaches $\exp(-\sum_m h_m)$, the continuous-time formula.

## Pitfalls

- **A continuous-time hazard is not a probability.** It is a rate and can exceed 1. Over a short interval
  $\Delta$ the event probability is about $h\Delta$.
- **Population hazards mix individuals.** If individuals have different constant hazards, the population hazard falls
  over time, because high-risk individuals leave the risk set first. A falling observed hazard need not mean that any
  individual's risk falls.
