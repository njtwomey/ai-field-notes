<Definition>
Let $t_1 < \dots < t_k$ be the distinct observed event times, $n_j$ the number of units at risk just before $t_j$ and
$d_j$ the number of events at $t_j$. The **Nelson–Aalen estimator** of the cumulative hazard $H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du$
is

$$
\hat H(t) = \sum_{j:\, t_j \le t}\frac{d_j}{n_j}.
$$

It is a step function that starts at 0 and rises at each event time <Cite id="nelson1972" /> <Cite id="aalen1978" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation

Count the events over time with the process $N(t)$, the number of events observed by time $t$, and let $Y(t)$ be the
number of units at risk just before $t$. Each unit at risk has the event in $[t, t + dt)$ with probability $h(t)\,dt$,
so

$$
\expect\brack{dN(t) \mid \text{past}} = Y(t)\,h(t)\,dt.
$$

The increment $dN(t) - Y(t)h(t)\,dt$ has conditional mean zero. Dividing by $Y(t)$ where it is positive suggests the
estimate $d\hat H(t) = dN(t)/Y(t)$ for $h(t)\,dt$. Summing the increments over the event times gives $\hat H$. The
estimator is approximately unbiased for $H(t)$ as long as units remain at risk up to $t$ <Cite id="aalen1978" />.

The same result follows from the discrete-hazard likelihood used for the
<NoteLink to="kaplan-meier-estimator">Kaplan–Meier estimator</NoteLink>. That likelihood gives the hazard estimate
$\hat h_j = d_j/n_j$ at each event time. Kaplan–Meier multiplies the survival factors $1 - \hat h_j$; Nelson–Aalen adds
the hazards $\hat h_j$.

**Variance.** Given the risk sets, $d_j$ is approximately Poisson with mean $n_jh_j$, so $\var(d_j/n_j) \approx
h_j/n_j$, estimated by $d_j/n_j^2$. Increments at different times are uncorrelated, so

$$
\widehat{\var}\paren{\hat H(t)} = \sum_{j:\, t_j \le t}\frac{d_j}{n_j^2}.
$$

## Survival from the cumulative hazard

Since $S(t) = e^{-H(t)}$, the estimator $\tilde S(t) = \exp(-\hat H(t))$ also estimates survival. It is called the
Fleming–Harrington or Breslow estimator. Because $e^{-x} \ge 1 - x$ for every $x$, each factor $e^{-d_j/n_j}$ is at
least $1 - d_j/n_j$, so $\tilde S(t) \ge \hat S_{\mathrm{KM}}(t)$. The two are close while $d_j/n_j$ is small and
diverge in the tail, where risk sets are small.

## Worked example

Ten patients have follow-up times in months $3, 5^+, 6, 6, 8, 10^+, 12, 15^+, 17, 20^+$, where $^+$ marks a censored
time.

| $t_j$ | $n_j$ | $d_j$ | $\hat H(t_j)$ | se | $e^{-\hat H}$ | Kaplan–Meier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 10 | 1 | 0.100 | 0.100 | 0.905 | 0.900 |
| 6 | 8 | 2 | 0.350 | 0.203 | 0.705 | 0.675 |
| 8 | 6 | 1 | 0.517 | 0.263 | 0.597 | 0.563 |
| 12 | 4 | 1 | 0.767 | 0.363 | 0.465 | 0.422 |
| 17 | 2 | 1 | 1.267 | 0.618 | 0.282 | 0.211 |

The two survival estimates agree to within 0.005 at the first event, where $d_j/n_j = 0.1$, and differ by 0.07 at the
last, where $d_j/n_j = 0.5$.

## Uses

- **Hazard plotting.** Nelson introduced the estimator to check parametric models graphically <Cite id="nelson1972" />.
  For an exponential model, $H(t) = \lambda t$, so $\hat H$ against $t$ should be a straight line through the origin.
  For a Weibull model, $\log H(t) = k\log t - k\log\sigma$, so $\log\hat H$ against $\log t$ should be a straight line
  with slope $k$. See <NoteLink to="parametric-survival-models">parametric survival models</NoteLink>.
- **Checking proportional hazards.** Under the Cox model, the curves $\log\hat H$ for two groups differ by a constant
  vertical shift. Crossing or converging curves suggest that proportional hazards fail.
- **Baseline hazard of the Cox model.** Breslow's estimator of the Cox baseline cumulative hazard is the Nelson–Aalen
  estimator with each unit at risk weighted by its relative hazard $\exp(\betavec^\top\xvec)$. See
  <NoteLink to="cox-proportional-hazards">Cox proportional hazards</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Tied event times.** $d_j/n_j$ treats tied events as simultaneous. Treating them as distinct and consecutive gives
  $\sum_{l=0}^{d_j - 1} 1/(n_j - l)$ instead, which is slightly larger. The difference matters only with many ties.
- **The cumulative hazard is not a probability.** $\hat H$ can exceed 1. It is the expected number of events for a unit
  that stays at risk, if events could repeat.
