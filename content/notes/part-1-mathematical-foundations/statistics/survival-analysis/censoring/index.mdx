<Definition>
An event time $T$ is **censored** when the data show only that $T$ lies in some interval rather than its exact value.
Under **right censoring**, the most common kind, unit $i$ is observed until time $C_i$, and the data are
$t_i = \min(T_i, C_i)$ and the event indicator $\delta_i = \indicator[T_i \le C_i]$. A censored unit ($\delta_i = 0$)
is known to survive past $t_i$ <Cite id="kalbfleisch2002" />.
</Definition>

## Kinds of censoring

- **Right censoring.** The event has not happened by the last observation: the study ends, the patient moves away,
  the customer is still active at the data snapshot. $T_i > t_i$.
- **Left censoring.** The event happened before the first observation, at an unknown time. $T_i \le t_i$.
- **Interval censoring.** The event happened between two inspections. $a_i < T_i \le b_i$.

Right censoring arises from different mechanisms. **Type I** censoring stops observation at a fixed time.
**Administrative** censoring stops it at a common calendar date for units that entered at different times. **Type
II** censoring stops the study after a fixed number of events. **Random** censoring comes from dropout.

**Truncation** differs from censoring. Under **left truncation**, or delayed entry, a unit enters the data only if it
survived to its entry time; units that failed earlier are never seen. Censored units are in the data with partial
information. Truncated units are missing entirely, and each observed unit's contribution must be conditioned on its
survival to entry.

## The likelihood under independent censoring

Assume **independent censoring**: $C_i$ is independent of $T_i$, given the features. Let the censoring times have
density $g$ and <NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival function</NoteLink> $G$. A unit with an observed
event at $t$ has $T = t$ and $C > t$, with probability density $f(t)\,G(t)$. A censored unit has $C = t$ and $T > t$,
with density $g(t)\,S(t)$. The likelihood of $n$ independent units is

$$
\prod_{i=1}^n \brack{f(t_i)\,G(t_i)}^{\delta_i}\brack{g(t_i)\,S(t_i)}^{1 - \delta_i} = \underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^n
f(t_i)^{\delta_i}S(t_i)^{1 - \delta_i}}_{\text{event-time model}} \times \underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^n g(t_i)^{1 -
\delta_i}G(t_i)^{\delta_i}}_{\text{censoring model}}.
$$

If the censoring distribution has no parameters in common with the event-time distribution (**non-informative
censoring**), the second factor is a constant for inference about $T$. Using $f = hS$, the working likelihood is

$$
L = \prod_{i=1}^n f(t_i)^{\delta_i}S(t_i)^{1 - \delta_i} = \prod_{i=1}^n h(t_i)^{\delta_i}S(t_i).
$$

Every unit contributes its survival up to $t_i$. Units with an event also contribute the hazard at $t_i$. Censored units
are neither discarded nor treated as events. Left- and interval-censored units contribute $F(t_i)$ and $S(a_i) -
S(b_i)$ instead.

## Why simple fixes are biased

Two tempting shortcuts both bias survival downwards.

**Dropping censored units** keeps only the units whose events were seen, which are those with $T_i \le C_i$. Short
event times are more likely to be seen before censoring, so the kept sample over-represents them. With exponential
event times of rate $\lambda$ and exponential censoring of rate $c$, the observed time $\min(T, C)$ is exponential with
rate $\lambda + c$, independently of which came first. The kept units therefore have mean time $1/(\lambda + c)$
rather than $1/\lambda$.

**Treating censoring times as event times** uses every unit, but records survivors as failures at their censoring
time. It also gives mean $1/(\lambda + c)$ in the exponential case, and it counts events that did not happen.

For $\lambda = 0.08$ and $c = 0.05$ per month, the true mean event time is 12.5 months and both shortcuts estimate 7.7
months.

<Derivation title="The minimum of two independent exponentials">
$P(\min(T, C) > t) = P(T > t)P(C > t) = e^{-\lambda t}e^{-ct} = e^{-(\lambda + c)t}$, so $\min(T, C) \sim
\Exp(\lambda + c)$. Also $P(T < C, \min(T, C) > t) = \int_t^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda s}e^{-cs}\,ds =
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda + c}e^{-(\lambda + c)t}$, which factorises into $P(T < C) = \lambda/(\lambda + c)$ times
$P(\min(T, C) > t)$. The minimum is therefore independent of which variable attains it, and conditioning on an
observed event leaves the distribution of the observed time unchanged at $\Exp(\lambda + c)$.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Ten patients have follow-up times in months $3, 5^+, 6, 6, 8, 10^+, 12, 15^+, 17, 20^+$, where $^+$ marks a censored
time. Estimate $S(12)$, the probability of surviving beyond 12 months.

- **Dropping censored units** leaves 6 event times, of which 1 exceeds 12: $\hat S(12) = 1/6 = 0.17$.
- **Treating censored times as events** gives 3 of 10 beyond 12: $\hat S(12) = 0.30$.
- The <NoteLink to="kaplan-meier-estimator">Kaplan–Meier estimator</NoteLink>, which uses each censored patient while
  at risk, gives $\hat S(12) = 0.42$.

The two shortcuts disagree with each other and with the estimator that uses the censored information correctly.

## Pitfalls

- **Informative censoring.** If patients drop out because they are getting worse, the censored patients were at higher
  risk than those who stayed, and every standard method overestimates survival.
  <NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> of $T$ and $C$ cannot be tested from the data
  <Cite id="leung1997" />. It must be argued from how censoring arose.
- **Competing events.** Death from another cause is not censoring for a cause-specific question if the target is the
  probability of the event actually occurring. Treating it as censoring estimates survival in a world where the
  competing cause is removed.
- **Censoring by design in machine-learning data.** Churn and time-to-conversion datasets built from a snapshot are
  right-censored at the snapshot date. Training a classifier on "churned within 90 days" labels treats recent customers
  who have not had 90 days yet as non-churners.
