<Definition>
Survival analysis studies the time $T \ge 0$ until an event, such as death, machine failure, customer churn or a
loan default. Its defining difficulty is censoring: for many units the event has not happened by the end of observation,
so only a lower bound on $T$ is known. Its methods estimate and compare the distribution of $T$ while using the partial
information that censored units carry <Cite id="kalbfleisch2002" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Time-to-event questions are common in applied machine learning: when a customer will churn, when a component will fail,
how long a patient will survive a treatment, how long a user stays in a session. Treating these as regression on the
observed times, or as classification with a fixed horizon, either drops the censored units or mislabels them, and both
bias the result. Survival models use every unit correctly. The hazard function also connects to point processes and to
<NoteLink to="change-point-detection">change-point detection</NoteLink>, where it gives the
probability that the current regime ends at each step.

## Main ideas

**Four functions describe one distribution.** The <NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival
function</NoteLink> $S(t) = P(T > t)$ gives the chance of lasting beyond $t$. The hazard $h(t) = f(t)/S(t)$ is the
event rate at $t$ among units still at risk. With the cumulative hazard $H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du$, $S(t) = e^{-H(t)}$,
so any one of the density, survival, hazard and cumulative hazard determines the others.

**Censored units still carry information.** Under independent <NoteLink to="censoring">censoring</NoteLink>, a unit
whose event was seen contributes $f(t)$ to the likelihood, and a unit censored at $t$ contributes $S(t)$. Dropping
censored units, or treating their censoring times as event times, biases survival downwards.

**Nonparametric estimates from the risk set.** The <NoteLink to="kaplan-meier-estimator" /> multiplies, over event
times, the fraction of units at risk that survive each one. The <NoteLink to="nelson-aalen-estimator" /> sums the
fraction that fail, estimating the cumulative hazard. The <NoteLink to="log-rank-test">log-rank test</NoteLink> compares
groups by summing observed minus expected events at each event time.

**Regression on the hazard.** The <NoteLink to="cox-proportional-hazards">Cox model</NoteLink> sets the hazard of a
unit with features $\xvec$ to $h_0(t)\exp(\betavec^\top\xvec)$, an unspecified baseline times a relative risk. The
partial likelihood estimates $\betavec$ without estimating $h_0$, and $e^{\beta_j}$ is the hazard ratio per unit of
$x_j$.

**Assuming a distribution.** <NoteLink to="parametric-survival-models" /> fit an exponential, Weibull, log-normal or
other distribution by <Gloss name="mle">maximum likelihood</Gloss> over censored data. Accelerated failure time models
stretch or shrink time by $e^{\betavec^\top\xvec}$. The Weibull model is both an accelerated failure time and a
proportional hazards model.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival and hazard functions</NoteLink>, then
<NoteLink to="censoring">censoring</NoteLink>, which all later notes require. The
<NoteLink to="kaplan-meier-estimator" /> and <NoteLink to="nelson-aalen-estimator" /> are the nonparametric estimators,

<NoteLink to="cox-proportional-hazards" /> and <NoteLink to="parametric-survival-models">parametric survival
