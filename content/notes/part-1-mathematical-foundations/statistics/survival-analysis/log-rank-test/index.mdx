<Definition>
The **log-rank test** tests whether two groups have the same <NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival
function</NoteLink>, using right-censored event times. At each distinct event time it forms the $2 \times 2$ table of
group by event, computes the number of events expected in group 1 if the groups had equal hazards, and sums observed
minus expected across times. The standardised sum is compared with a standard
<NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">normal distribution</NoteLink> <Cite id="mantel1966" /> <Cite id="peto1972" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0: S_1(t) = S_2(t)$ for all $t$, equivalently $h_1(t) = h_2(t)$: the two groups share one hazard function. The
alternative is that they differ. The test has the most power against **proportional hazards** alternatives,
$h_1(t) = c\,h_2(t)$ with $c \ne 1$.

## Assumptions

- <NoteLink to="censoring">Censoring</NoteLink> is independent of the event times within each group.
- Units are independent.
- Censoring patterns may differ between groups; the test conditions on who is at risk.

## Test statistic

Let $t_1 < \dots < t_k$ be the distinct event times in the pooled data. At $t_j$, let $n_j$ units be at risk, $n_{1j}$
of them in group 1, and let $d_j$ events occur, $d_{1j}$ of them in group 1.

Condition on the margins of the $2 \times 2$ table at $t_j$: $n_j$, $n_{1j}$ and $d_j$. Under $H_0$ every unit at risk
is equally likely to be among the $d_j$ that fail, so the number of group-1 events is the number of group-1 units in a
random draw of $d_j$ from $n_j$ without replacement. That count is
<NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">hypergeometric</NoteLink>, with

$$
E_{1j} = \expect[d_{1j}] = d_j\frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}, \qquad V_j = \var(d_{1j}) = d_j\,\frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}\paren{1 -
\frac{n_{1j}}{n_j}}\frac{n_j - d_j}{n_j - 1}.
$$

The last factor is the finite-population correction for drawing without replacement. Sum over event times:

$$
O_1 = \sum_j d_{1j}, \qquad E_1 = \sum_j E_{1j}, \qquad V = \sum_j V_j, \qquad Z = \frac{O_1 - E_1}{\sqrt V}.
$$

The terms $d_{1j} - E_{1j}$ have mean zero given everything before $t_j$, so they are uncorrelated across times. A
<Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> for such sums gives $Z \approx \Gauss(0, 1)$ under $H_0$, and
$Z^2 \approx \ChiSq_1$. For $G$ groups, the vector of $O_g - E_g$ with its
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">covariance matrix</NoteLink> gives a $\ChiSq_{G-1}$ statistic.

The test is the score test of $\beta = 0$ in the <NoteLink to="cox-proportional-hazards">Cox model</NoteLink> with a
group indicator, when there are no tied event times.

## Decision rule

Reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $Z^2 > \ChiSq_{1, 1 - \alpha}$, the $1 - \alpha$ quantile ($3.84$ for
$\alpha = 0.05$). The <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is $P(\ChiSq_1 \ge Z^2)$. The sign of $O_1 - E_1$ shows
which group has more events than expected.

## Worked example

A treated group (A) and a control group (B) have follow-up times in months, with $^+$ marking censoring:

- A: $6, 9^+, 11, 14, 18^+, 21$
- B: $2, 4, 5^+, 7, 8, 12$

| $t_j$ | $n_{Aj}$ | $n_{Bj}$ | $d_j$ | $d_{Aj}$ | $E_{Aj}$ | $V_j$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 6 | 6 | 1 | 0 | 0.500 | 0.250 |
| 4 | 6 | 5 | 1 | 0 | 0.545 | 0.248 |
| 6 | 6 | 3 | 1 | 1 | 0.667 | 0.222 |
| 7 | 5 | 3 | 1 | 0 | 0.625 | 0.234 |
| 8 | 5 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 0.714 | 0.204 |
| 11 | 4 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0.800 | 0.160 |
| 12 | 3 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 0.750 | 0.188 |
| 14 | 3 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1.000 | 0 |
| 21 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1.000 | 0 |

Group A had $O_A = 4$ events against $E_A = 6.60$ expected, and $V = 1.506$. So $Z = (4 - 6.60)/\sqrt{1.506} =
-2.12$ and $Z^2 = 4.49$, with p-value 0.034. The treated group had fewer events than equal hazards predict. Once group
B is empty, from 14 months on, each event is certain to be in group A and adds nothing to the variance.

## Effect size

The ratio $(O_A/E_A)/(O_B/E_B)$ estimates the hazard ratio of A to B. Here $E_B = 9 - 6.60 = 2.40$ with $O_B = 5$, so
the ratio is $(4/6.60)/(5/2.40) = 0.29$. The <NoteLink to="cox-proportional-hazards">Cox model</NoteLink> estimates the
hazard ratio by <Gloss name="mle">maximum likelihood</Gloss> with a <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence
interval</NoteLink>, and is preferred for reporting. Differences in median survival or in restricted mean survival time
are alternatives that do not assume proportional hazards.

## Weighted versions

Multiplying each time's $d_{1j} - E_{1j}$ by a weight $w_j$, and its variance by $w_j^2$, gives a family of tests.
Weight $w_j = n_j$ gives the Gehan–Wilcoxon test, and $w_j = \hat S(t_j)$ gives the Peto–Peto test. Both emphasise early
differences, when many units are at risk <Cite id="peto1972" />. Choose the weights before seeing the data.

## Pitfalls

- **Crossing hazards.** If one group does better early and worse late, positive and negative differences cancel, and
  the test can miss a real difference. Look at the Kaplan–Meier curves first.
- **Not an estimate.** A small p-value says the curves differ, not by how much. Report a hazard ratio or a difference in
  survival at a fixed time.
- **Stratification.** If a strong prognostic factor is unbalanced between groups, compute $O - E$ and $V$ within each
  stratum and add them: the stratified log-rank test.
