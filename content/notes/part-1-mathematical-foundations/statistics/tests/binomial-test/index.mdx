<Definition>
The binomial test compares the number of successes $S$ in $n$ independent trials, each succeeding with probability $p$,
against the null value $p_0$. Under $H_0: p = p_0$, $S \sim \Binom(n, p_0)$ exactly, so the
<NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is a sum of binomial probabilities and needs no approximation
<Cite id="casella2002" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0: p = p_0$. The alternative is $H_1: p \ne p_0$ (two-sided), or $p > p_0$ or $p < p_0$ (one-sided), fixed before
the data are seen.

## Assumptions

- The $n$ trials are independent.
- Every trial has the same success probability $p$.
- $n$ is fixed in advance. If sampling stops at a fixed number of successes, the count of trials follows a
  <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial distribution</NoteLink> instead, and the p-value
  differs.

## Test statistic

The number of successes $S = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, where $X_i = 1$ when trial $i$ succeeds. $S$ is sufficient for $p$, so
no other function of the trials carries more information about $p$; see
<NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink>.

**Null distribution.** Take any particular sequence of $n$ outcomes with $k$ successes, such as
$1, 1, 0, \dots, 0$. The trials are independent, so its probability is the product of the per-trial probabilities:
$p_0^k(1 - p_0)^{n-k}$, whatever the order. The number of sequences with exactly $k$ successes is the number of ways to
choose which $k$ of the $n$ positions succeed, $\binom{n}{k}$. The sequences are disjoint events, so under $H_0$

$$
P_{H_0}(S = k) = \binom{n}{k} p_0^k (1 - p_0)^{n - k}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, n,
$$

the <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial distribution</NoteLink> $\Binom(n, p_0)$. The derivation uses only
<NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>, a constant $p$ and a fixed $n$, so the p-values below are exact: no
large-sample approximation is involved.

## Decision rule

For an observed count $s$:

- Upper-tailed: $p = P_{H_0}(S \ge s)$.
- Lower-tailed: $p = P_{H_0}(S \le s)$.
- Two-sided: $p = \sum_{k \,:\, P_{H_0}(S = k) \le P_{H_0}(S = s)} P_{H_0}(S = k)$, the
  <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">total probability</NoteLink> of the outcomes no more likely than $s$. For
  $p_0 = 1/2$ the distribution is symmetric and this equals twice the smaller tail.

Reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $p \le \alpha$. For the <NoteLink to="one-and-two-sided-tests">upper-tailed
test</NoteLink> this rule has level $\alpha$. Let $c$ be the smallest count with $P_{H_0}(S \ge c) \le \alpha$. The
p-value $P_{H_0}(S \ge s)$ decreases as $s$ grows, so $p \le \alpha$ exactly when $s \ge c$, and the probability of
rejecting under $H_0$ is $P_{H_0}(S \ge c) \le \alpha$. Because $S$ is discrete, this size is often well below $\alpha$;
see <NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink>.

## Example: is the coin fair?

A coin lands heads $s = 15$ times in $n = 20$ tosses. Test $H_0: p = 1/2$ against $H_1: p \ne 1/2$.

$$
P_{H_0}(S \ge 15) = 2^{-20}\sum_{k=15}^{20} \binom{20}{k} = 0.0207.
$$

By symmetry $P_{H_0}(S \le 5) = 0.0207$ too, so the two-sided p-value is $0.041$. At $\alpha = 0.05$ the test rejects
fairness. The estimated heads probability is $\hat p = 0.75$, and the exact 95% Clopper–Pearson interval is
$[0.51, 0.91]$ <Cite id="clopper1934" />. It excludes 0.5, as the duality between tests and
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">intervals</NoteLink> requires. The interval is wide: 20 tosses cannot pin down the
bias.

## Normal approximation

Each trial $X_i$ is a <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli</NoteLink> variable with mean $p_0$ and variance
$\expect[X_i^2] - p_0^2 = p_0 - p_0^2 = p_0(1 - p_0)$ under $H_0$. $S$ is a sum of $n$ independent such terms, so its
mean is $np_0$ and, since variances of independent terms add, its variance is $np_0(1 - p_0)$. For large $n$ the
<Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> gives the statistic

$$
Z = \frac{S - np_0}{\sqrt{np_0(1 - p_0)}} \approx \Gauss(0, 1).
$$

This is the one-proportion <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink>. For the coin, $z = (15 - 10)/\sqrt 5 = 2.24$ and the
two-sided p-value is $0.025$, smaller than the exact $0.041$.

**Continuity correction.** Each integer $k$ is a bar of width 1 from $k - 1/2$ to $k + 1/2$. The event $S \ge 15$
covers the bars from $14.5$ upwards, so the normal tail should start at $14.5$, not at $15$
<Cite id="yates1934" />:

$$
z_{\text{cc}} = \frac{\abs{s - np_0} - 1/2}{\sqrt{np_0(1 - p_0)}} = \frac{4.5}{\sqrt 5} = 2.01, \qquad p = 0.044.
$$

The corrected value is close to the exact one. The correction matters most for $n$ between about 20 and 100. For
larger $n$ the difference disappears. The normal approximation is poor when $np_0$ or $n(1 - p_0)$ is below about 10;
the exact test is always available and should then be used.

## Effect size

Report $\hat p = s/n$ with a confidence interval for $p$, or the difference $\hat p - p_0$. For the coin, heads came up
75% of the time against 50% expected, with the interval above.

## Pitfalls

- **Dependent trials.** Repeated measurements on the same person, or batches of items made together, are not
  independent. The variance of $S$ is then larger than binomial and the test rejects too often.
- **Varying probability.** If $p$ changes between trials, for example across production shifts, $S$ is not binomial.
- **Normal approximation near the edges.** For rare events such as a 1% defect rate, $np_0$ is small unless $n$ is in
  the thousands. Use the exact test.
- **Sign test.** Testing whether the median of paired differences is zero with the binomial test on the signs of the
  differences, $p_0 = 1/2$, is the sign test. It discards the sizes of the differences and has less power than the
  <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> when the differences are normal.
