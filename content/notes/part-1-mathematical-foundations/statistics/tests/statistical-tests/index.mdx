<Definition>
A statistical test is a fixed procedure for one kind of question: it names the null and alternative hypotheses, the
assumptions under which its error rates hold, a test statistic and the distribution of that statistic under the null.
The tests in this topic differ in what they compare (a proportion, a mean, two means or two whole distributions) and in
what they assume about the data <Cite id="lehmann2005" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Machine learning practice runs tests constantly, often without naming them. Comparing a model's accuracy with a
baseline rate is a binomial test. Comparing two models on the same test examples is a paired test. Comparing a metric
between two user groups in an A/B test is a two-sample test of means, and Welch's version is the safe default.
Detecting drift between training and deployment data, or checking that a generative model's samples match real data, is
a two-sample test of distributions. Picking the right test, and knowing its assumptions, decides whether the reported
p-value means what it claims.

## Main ideas

**Tests of a proportion or a mean.** The <NoteLink to="binomial-test">binomial test</NoteLink> checks a success
probability against a stated value using the exact binomial distribution. The <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink>
compares a mean with a stated value when the standard deviation is known, and large-sample tests of proportions take the
same form. The <NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample t-test</NoteLink> replaces the known standard deviation by an
estimate and refers the result to Student's t distribution.

**Paired versus independent samples.** The <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> compares two
measurements on the same units by testing their differences, which removes between-unit variation and gains power. For
two independent groups, the <NoteLink to="pooled-two-sample-t-test">pooled two-sample t-test</NoteLink> assumes one
shared variance, and <NoteLink to="welch-t-test" /> does not. Welch's test keeps its false-positive rate close to
$\alpha$ when the variances differ, where the pooled test does not.

**Tests of whole distributions.** A t-test sees only a difference in means. The
<NoteLink to="two-sample-testing">two-sample tests of distributions</NoteLink> compare the samples as wholes, by the
largest gap between empirical distribution functions (Kolmogorov–Smirnov), by distances between and within samples
(<NoteLink to="energy-distance">energy distance</NoteLink>), by kernel mean embeddings (<Gloss name="mmd">maximum mean
discrepancy</Gloss>) or by the held-out accuracy of a classifier.

**Every test has assumptions.** <NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> of observations underlies all of
them. The t-tests also assume approximately normal means, which the <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss>
supplies for moderate samples. When an assumption fails, <NoteLink to="permutation-tests">permutation tests</NoteLink>
and the bootstrap build the null distribution from the data instead.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="binomial-test">binomial test</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink>,
the simplest tests. The <NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample t-test</NoteLink> builds on the z-test, and the
<NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> is a one-sample t-test on differences. The
<NoteLink to="pooled-two-sample-t-test">pooled two-sample t-test</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="welch-t-test" /> handle
two independent groups and are best read together. The subtopic on two-sample tests of distributions comes last, opening
with <NoteLink to="two-sample-testing">two-sample tests of distributions</NoteLink> and ending with a simulation that
compares the power of six tests.
