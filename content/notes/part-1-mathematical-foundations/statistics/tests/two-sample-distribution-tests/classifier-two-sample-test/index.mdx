<Definition>
The **classifier two-sample test** (<Gloss name="c2st" />) compares a sample from $P$ with a sample from $Q$ of the same
size. Each sample is split in two. A classifier is trained on the first halves to predict which distribution a point
came from, and the number $c$ of correct labels on the $n_\text{te}$ held-out points is the statistic. Under $P = Q$,
$c$ has mean $n_\text{te}/2$, and the upper tail of $\mathrm{Binomial}(n_\text{te}, \tfrac12)$ gives a valid
<NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> for it <Cite id="lopezpaz2017c2st" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The <NoteLink to="kernel-two-sample-test" /> and the energy test compare points through a kernel or a distance chosen
in advance. For images, text or mixed tabular data, a generic distance sees mostly irrelevant variation, and power
falls. Classifiers are built to find the features that separate two groups, with architectures made for each kind of
data. A classifier that separates the samples better than chance is direct evidence that they differ.

## Mechanism

### Accuracy measures the difference

If the two samples are the same size, the best possible classifier has accuracy
$\tfrac12\paren{1 + \mathrm{TV}(P, Q)}$, where $\mathrm{TV}(P, Q) = \sup_A \abs{P(A) - Q(A)}$ is the total variation
distance. Accuracy $\tfrac12$ is therefore what a perfect classifier achieves when $P = Q$, and any reliable excess
over $\tfrac12$ shows $P \ne Q$. The held-out accuracy of a real classifier estimates a lower bound on that best
accuracy, so a weak classifier loses power but never makes the test invalid.

### The null law

The classifier is trained on data independent of the held-out points. Under $H_0$ every point, whatever its label, is a
draw from the same distribution, so the trained classifier predicts "from $Q$" with the same probability $\pi$ for every
held-out point. With half of the held-out points from each sample, the count of correct labels is then the sum of a
$\mathrm{Binomial}(n_\text{te}/2, 1 - \pi)$ and an independent $\mathrm{Binomial}(n_\text{te}/2, \pi)$. Its mean is
$n_\text{te}/2$ for every $\pi$. A sum of independent trials whose success probabilities average $\tfrac12$ has an upper
tail, from one above its mean, no heavier than that of $\mathrm{Binomial}(n_\text{te}, \tfrac12)$
<Cite id="hoeffding1956" />, with equality when $\pi = \tfrac12$. The binomial tail is therefore a valid p-value,
slightly conservative when the classifier leans towards one label (<NoteLink to="binomial-test" />). No permutation is
needed, whatever the classifier.

### Reading the classifier

The trained classifier also explains the rejection. Points the classifier labels confidently are where the distributions
differ most, and its probabilities estimate the ratio of the two densities
(<NoteLink to="density-ratio-by-classification" />). In machine-learning practice the same procedure is called
<NoteLink to="adversarial-validation" />: a classifier between training and test features that does better than chance
warns of <NoteLink to="drift-detection">drift</NoteLink>.

## Cost

One classifier fit on half the data and one pass of predictions over the other half. For a neural network this costs
the same as any training run; for nearest neighbours it costs $O(n_\text{te}\, n_\text{tr})$ distance evaluations.

## Trade-offs

- **Half the data trains, half tests.** Only the held-out half counts towards the statistic, which costs power
  compared with tests that use every point. Cross-fitting, with the roles of the halves swapped, uses all points
  but needs a permutation null.
- **Accuracy is coarse.** It counts only which side of $\tfrac12$ each probability falls. Statistics that use the
  probabilities themselves, such as the mean difference in predicted log odds, are often more powerful
  <Cite id="liu2020deepkernel" />.
- **The classifier is a modelling choice.** A classifier that cannot represent the difference, such as a linear one
  for a change of spread, has no power against it. Tune it on the training half only; tuning on the held-out half
  invalidates the null law.

## Worked example

A classifier is trained on half of two samples of 20 points each and scored on the 20 held-out points.

1. **Count.** It labels $c = 15$ of the 20 held-out points correctly, an accuracy of $0.75$.
2. **Null law.** If $P = Q$, $c \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(20, \tfrac12)$.
3. **Upper tail.** $\prob(c \ge 15) = \sum_{k=15}^{20} \binom{20}{k} 2^{-20} =
   (15504 + 4845 + 1140 + 190 + 20 + 1)/1048576 = 21700/1048576 = 0.0207$.
4. **Conclude.** At $\alpha = 0.05$ the test rejects $P = Q$. With 14 correct the p-value would be $0.0577$, and it
   would not.
