<Definition>
Let $X, X'$ be independent draws from a distribution $P$ on $\reals^d$ and $Y, Y'$ independent draws from $Q$, all with
finite means. The **energy distance** between $P$ and $Q$ is
$$
\mathcal{E}(P, Q) = 2\,\expect\norm{X - Y} - \expect\norm{X - X'} - \expect\norm{Y - Y'} .
$$
It is non-negative and zero only when $P = Q$ <Cite id="szekely2013" />. The **energy test** compares two samples by
the estimate of $\mathcal{E}$ and rejects $P = Q$ when it is large compared with its values under permutation
<Cite id="baringhaus2004" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

If two samples come from the same distribution, a point is on average as far from points of the other sample as from
points of its own. If they differ, cross-sample distances are longer on average than within-sample distances, and
$\mathcal{E}$ measures by how much. The name comes from an analogy with the potential energy of two clouds of
charges.

In one dimension the energy distance has a picture. It is twice the area under the squared gap between the two
<Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution functions</Gloss> $F$ and $G$:
$$
\mathcal{E}(P, Q) = 2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \paren{F(t) - G(t)}^2\, dt .
$$
The <NoteLink to="kolmogorov-smirnov-test" /> uses only the largest gap; the energy distance adds up all of them.

## Details

### The estimate and the test

From samples $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $y_1, \dots, y_m$, replacing each expectation by an average over all pairs gives
$$
\hat{\mathcal{E}} = \frac{2}{nm} \sum_{i, j} \norm{x_i - y_j} - \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i, i'} \norm{x_i - x_{i'}} -
\frac{1}{m^2} \sum_{j, j'} \norm{y_j - y_{j'}} ,
$$
the energy distance between the two empirical distributions, which is never negative. The
<NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">test statistic</NoteLink> is $\frac{nm}{n + m}\hat{\mathcal{E}}$, and its
null law comes from relabelling the pooled sample (<NoteLink to="permutation-tests" />). One distance matrix of the
pooled sample serves every relabelling.

### No width to choose

The energy distance uses distances as they are. Scaling both distributions by $c > 0$ scales $\mathcal{E}$ by $c$, and
rotating or translating both leaves it unchanged, so there is no kernel width to tune. The price is that the scale of
each coordinate matters: a feature measured in large units dominates the distances.

### A kernel in disguise

For the kernel $k(\xvec, \yvec) = \tfrac{1}{2}\paren{\norm{\xvec} + \norm{\yvec} - \norm{\xvec - \yvec}}$, the energy
distance is twice the squared <NoteLink to="maximum-mean-discrepancy" />:
$\mathcal{E}(P, Q) = 2\,\mathrm{MMD}^2_k(P, Q)$ <Cite id="sejdinovic2013" />. The energy test is therefore a
<NoteLink to="kernel-two-sample-test" /> with one particular kernel, and statements about one carry over to the other.
Replacing $\norm{\cdot}$ by $\norm{\cdot}^\beta$ with $0 < \beta < 2$ gives a family of such distances; at $\beta = 2$
the distance reduces to twice the squared distance between the means and stops seeing anything else.

<Derivation title="The one-dimensional identity">
For independent $X \sim F$ and $Y \sim G$ on the line, $\abs{X - Y} = \int \paren{\indicator\set{X \le t < Y} +
\indicator\set{Y \le t < X}} dt$. Taking expectations, $\expect\abs{X - Y} = \int \paren{F(1 - G) + G(1 - F)}\, dt =
\int (F + G - 2FG)\, dt$. In the same way, $\expect\abs{X - X'} = 2\int (F - F^2)\, dt$ and
$\expect\abs{Y - Y'} = 2\int (G - G^2)\, dt$. Then
$2\expect\abs{X - Y} - \expect\abs{X - X'} - \expect\abs{Y - Y'} = 2\int (F + G - 2FG - F + F^2 - G + G^2)\, dt =
2\int (F - G)^2\, dt$. Applied to the empirical distributions, the same steps give the identity for the sample
estimate exactly.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

- **Finite means.** The expectations of distances must exist. For heavy-tailed data, such as Cauchy samples, use
  $\norm{\cdot}^\beta$ with $\beta < 1$.
- **Comparable units across coordinates**, or standardised features.
- **Independent samples** of independent draws, for the permutation test.

## Pitfalls

- **High dimensions.** Like kernel tests, the energy test loses power against a fixed mean shift as irrelevant
  dimensions are added <Cite id="ramdas2015" />.
- **Quadratic cost.** The estimate needs every pairwise distance, $O((n + m)^2 d)$ work and memory for the matrix.
- **Not the Wasserstein distance.** Both are built from distances, but the <NoteLink to="wasserstein-distances" />
  measures the cost of moving one distribution onto the other, while the energy distance averages pairwise
  distances. In one dimension the first integrates $\abs{F - G}$ and the second $(F - G)^2$.

## Worked example

Take $x = (0, 1)$ and $y = (2, 3)$.

1. **Cross distances.** The four pairs are 2, 3, 1 and 2 apart, with mean 2.
2. **Within distances.** Over all four ordered pairs of $x$, counting each point with itself, the distances are
   $0, 1, 1, 0$, with mean $0.5$. The same holds for $y$.
3. **Energy distance.** $\hat{\mathcal{E}} = 2 \times 2 - 0.5 - 0.5 = 3$.
4. **Check by area.** On $[0, 1)$, $F = \tfrac12$ and $G = 0$; on $[1, 2)$, $F = 1$ and $G = 0$; on $[2, 3)$,
   $F = 1$ and $G = \tfrac12$. The area under $(F - G)^2$ is $0.25 + 1 + 0.25 = 1.5$, and twice it is 3.
