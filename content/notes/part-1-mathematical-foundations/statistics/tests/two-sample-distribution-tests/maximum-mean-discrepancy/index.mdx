<Definition>
Let $k$ be a positive-definite kernel on a set $\Xcal$, with <NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space" />
$\Hcal$ and feature map $\phivec(\xvec) = k(\xvec, \cdot)$. The **mean embedding** of a distribution $P$ is
$\muvec_P = \expect_{X \sim P}\brack{\phivec(X)} \in \Hcal$. The **maximum mean discrepancy** between $P$ and $Q$ is
$$
\mathrm{MMD}(P, Q) = \norm{\muvec_P - \muvec_Q}_{\Hcal}
= \sup_{\norm{f}_{\Hcal} \le 1} \paren{\expect_{P}\brack{f(X)} - \expect_{Q}\brack{f(Y)}} .
$$
If the kernel is **characteristic**, as the Gaussian kernel is on $\reals^d$, $\mathrm{MMD}(P, Q) = 0$ only when
$P = Q$ <Cite id="gretton2012" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The mean embedding summarises a distribution by the average of a kernel bump centred on each of its points. For the
Gaussian kernel $k(\xvec, \yvec) = \exp(-\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 / 2\sigma^2)$, the embedding evaluated at a point
$\tvec$, $\muvec_P(\tvec) = \expect_P\brack{k(X, \tvec)}$, is the density of $P$ smoothed at scale $\sigma$. Two
distributions are compared through these smoothed densities.

The supremum form reads MMD as a contest. Among all functions $f$ of bounded size in $\Hcal$, find the one whose mean
differs most between $P$ and $Q$. That function is the **witness**, $f^\star \propto \muvec_P - \muvec_Q$: positive
where $P$ is denser at the kernel's scale and negative where $Q$ is. MMD is the mean difference it achieves.

The kernel decides which differences count. The linear kernel $k(\xvec, \yvec) = \xvec^\top\yvec$ embeds a
distribution as its mean, so its MMD is the distance between means. The polynomial kernel of degree 2 adds the second
moments. A characteristic kernel embeds every feature of the distribution, so every difference has a witness.

The figure draws samples from $P = \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ and from a Gaussian $Q$ with a movable mean and a
correlation $\rho$, over the empirical witness. With equal means and $\rho \ne 0$ both marginals are $\Gauss(0, 1)$,
and the witness picks out the diagonal along which $Q$ stretches.

## Details

### MMD from kernel evaluations

Expanding the squared norm with the reproducing property $\inner{\phivec(\xvec)}{\phivec(\yvec)} = k(\xvec, \yvec)$
gives
$$
\mathrm{MMD}^2(P, Q) = \expect\brack{k(X, X')} + \expect\brack{k(Y, Y')} - 2\,\expect\brack{k(X, Y)} ,
$$
with $X, X' \sim P$ and $Y, Y' \sim Q$ all independent. Only kernel values between points appear. The embeddings, which
may be infinite-dimensional, are never formed.

### Two estimators

From samples $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $y_1, \dots, y_m$, the **unbiased estimator** averages over distinct pairs:
$$
\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}{}^2_u = \frac{1}{n(n - 1)} \sum_{i \ne i'} k(x_i, x_{i'}) +
\frac{1}{m(m - 1)} \sum_{j \ne j'} k(y_j, y_{j'}) - \frac{2}{nm} \sum_{i, j} k(x_i, y_j) .
$$
It is a U-statistic, its expectation is exactly $\mathrm{MMD}^2$, and it can be negative when $P = Q$. The **biased
estimator** keeps the $i = i'$ and $j = j'$ terms; it is the squared distance between the two empirical mean
embeddings, so it is never negative, and its expectation exceeds $\mathrm{MMD}^2$ by a term of order $1/n + 1/m$. Both
cost $O((n + m)^2)$ kernel evaluations <Cite id="gretton2012" />.

### The kernel width

For the Gaussian kernel, the width $\sigma$ sets the scale of differences that MMD sees. As $\sigma \to 0$ the kernel
matrix approaches the identity and MMD counts only coincident points; as $\sigma \to \infty$ every kernel value
approaches 1 and the leading term of MMD is the difference of means. The <NoteLink to="median-heuristic">median
heuristic</NoteLink> sets $\sigma$ to the median distance between pooled points <Cite id="garreau2017" />. Choosing
$\sigma$ to maximise an estimate of test power on held-out data does better when the difference lives at another scale
<Cite id="gretton2012b" />.

### Relatives

The <NoteLink to="energy-distance" /> is the squared MMD for the kernel built from Euclidean distances
<Cite id="sejdinovic2013" />. <NoteLink to="kernel-inception-distance" /> is the squared MMD between image features
with a polynomial kernel. MMD is used as a training loss to align feature distributions in
<NoteLink to="feature-alignment-methods">domain adaptation</NoteLink>, and kernel mean matching reweights a sample to
make its embedding match another's (<NoteLink to="importance-weighting-for-covariate-shift" />). The
<NoteLink to="wasserstein-distances" /> also compare distributions by a supremum over test functions, with
1-Lipschitz functions in place of the RKHS ball.

<Derivation title="The supremum equals the norm">
For $f \in \Hcal$, the reproducing property gives $f(\xvec) = \inner{f}{\phivec(\xvec)}$, and taking
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> gives $\expect_P[f(X)] = \inner{f}{\muvec_P}$. So
$\expect_P[f(X)] - \expect_Q[f(Y)] = \inner{f}{\muvec_P - \muvec_Q}$. By the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz
inequality</NoteLink> this is at most $\norm{f}\,\norm{\muvec_P - \muvec_Q} \le \norm{\muvec_P - \muvec_Q}$ when
$\norm{f} \le 1$, with equality at $f = (\muvec_P - \muvec_Q)/\norm{\muvec_P - \muvec_Q}$. The supremum is therefore the
norm, attained by the witness.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

- **A bounded or integrable kernel.** The embedding exists when $\expect\brack{\sqrt{k(X, X)}}$ is finite, which
  holds for every bounded kernel, such as the Gaussian.
- **A characteristic kernel** for MMD to be a metric on distributions. The Gaussian and Laplace kernels on
  $\reals^d$ are characteristic; the linear and <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">polynomial kernels</NoteLink> are not.

## Pitfalls

- **The biased estimator is never zero.** It is positive even when $P = Q$, so it cannot be compared with zero
  directly; a test needs a null law (<NoteLink to="kernel-two-sample-test" />).
- **Scale and units.** A Gaussian kernel uses one width for every coordinate. Standardise features first, or a
  coordinate measured in large units dominates the distance.
- **Quadratic cost.** For large samples, the linear-time estimator evaluates the kernel terms only on disjoint
  pairs of points, in $O(n + m)$ time, at a cost in power <Cite id="gretton2012" />.

## Worked example

Take $x = (0, 1)$ and $y = (2, 3)$ on the line with the Gaussian kernel of width $\sigma = 1$, so
$k(a, b) = e^{-(a - b)^2/2}$.

1. **Kernel values.** Points 1 apart give $e^{-0.5} = 0.6065$, 2 apart $e^{-2} = 0.1353$, 3 apart
   $e^{-4.5} = 0.0111$, and every point with itself gives 1.
2. **Within-sample terms, unbiased.** Each sample has one distinct pair, 1 apart: both averages are $0.6065$.
3. **Between-sample term.** The four pairs are 2, 3, 1 and 2 apart:
   $(0.1353 + 0.0111 + 0.6065 + 0.1353)/4 = 0.2221$.
4. **Unbiased estimate.** $0.6065 + 0.6065 - 2 \times 0.2221 = 0.7689$.
5. **Biased estimate.** The within-sample averages now include the self-pairs:
   $(1 + 1 + 2 \times 0.6065)/4 = 0.8033$ each. So $0.8033 + 0.8033 - 0.4441 = 1.1624$, larger than the unbiased value
   by the self-pair terms.
