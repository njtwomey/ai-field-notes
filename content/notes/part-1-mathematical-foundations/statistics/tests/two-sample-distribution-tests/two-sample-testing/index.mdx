<Definition>
Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be independent draws from a distribution $P$ and $y_1, \dots, y_m$ independent draws from a
distribution $Q$, the two samples independent of each other. The **two-sample problem** tests the
<NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> $H_0: P = Q$ against the alternative
$H_1: P \ne Q$. A test of this alternative is **consistent** if, for every fixed pair $P \ne Q$, the probability that
it rejects tends to 1 as $n$ and $m$ grow.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="welch-t-test">t-tests</NoteLink> answer a narrower question: do the two means differ? Two
distributions can share a mean and differ in their spread, their shape, or, in more than one dimension, in the
dependence between coordinates. A t-test sees none of these. A test of distributions compares the samples as wholes,
so any difference, of any kind, eventually shows.

The figure draws a sample from $\Gauss(0, 1)$ and a sample from an alternative that differs by a mean shift, a
change of spread, or a change of shape that keeps the mean at 0 and the variance at 1. The readouts give the
<NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> of six tests on the same two samples. Only the tests of whole
distributions reject the change of shape.

## Details

### A statistic and a null law

Each test of distributions has two parts. The **statistic** measures how far apart the two empirical distributions
are; it is near zero when the samples look alike and grows with the difference. The **null law** is the statistic's
distribution when $P = Q$, against which the observed value is judged.

| Test | Statistic | Data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <NoteLink to="kolmogorov-smirnov-test">Kolmogorov–Smirnov</NoteLink> | largest gap between the two empirical cdfs | one dimension |
| <NoteLink to="energy-distance">Energy distance</NoteLink> | mean distances between and within the samples | any dimension |
| <NoteLink to="maximum-mean-discrepancy">Maximum mean discrepancy</NoteLink> | distance between kernel mean embeddings | anything with a kernel |
| <NoteLink to="classifier-two-sample-test">Classifier test</NoteLink> | held-out accuracy of a classifier between the samples | anything a classifier takes |

### Permutation gives the null law

Under $H_0$ all $n + m$ points are independent draws from one distribution, so every assignment of the pooled points
to groups of sizes $n$ and $m$ is equally likely. Recomputing the statistic on random reassignments gives its null law
for the data at hand, and the share of reassignments at least as extreme as the observed one is a valid p-value
(<NoteLink to="permutation-tests" />). This works for any statistic, so the choice of statistic is free to follow
power alone. Kolmogorov–Smirnov in one dimension and the classifier test also have exact null laws of their own.

### Power and what it depends on

Every test above rejects a true $H_0$ with probability $\alpha$. They differ in their **power**, the probability of
rejecting a given false $H_0$ (<NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors" />). Power depends on the kind of
difference, the sample sizes and the dimension. For a fixed mean shift, the power of kernel and distance tests falls
as irrelevant dimensions are added <Cite id="ramdas2015" />. For images, text and other structured data, classifier
tests and kernels learned from part of the data recover much of the lost power <Cite id="lopezpaz2017c2st" />
<Cite id="liu2020deepkernel" />. <NoteLink to="comparing-two-sample-tests" /> measures power for the six tests in
the figure.

### Uses

Two-sample tests check whether training and deployment data differ (<NoteLink to="drift-detection" />), whether a
generative model's samples match the data, whether a simulator matches observations, and whether two experimental
conditions change an outcome's distribution and not only its mean. A <NoteLink to="density-ratio-estimation">density
ratio</NoteLink> estimated between the samples answers the next question: where and by how much they differ.

## Assumptions

- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink>.** The points within each sample are independent, and the
  samples are independent of each other. Autocorrelated data, such as time series, break the exchangeability that
  permutation relies on and inflate the <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss>.
- **Identical distribution within each sample.** A sample that mixes several sources tests the mixture.

## Pitfalls

- **Rejection says that, not how.** A small p-value says the distributions differ. The MMD witness function, the
  classifier's predictions or the location of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov gap show where.
- **Large samples find tiny differences.** With enough data every real difference is significant. Report the size of
  the statistic, such as the MMD or the held-out accuracy, beside the p-value.
- **One test per question.** Testing every feature of a data set separately multiplies <Gloss name="fp">false
  positives</Gloss>; correct for <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink> or test the joint
  distribution once.
