<Definition>
The **two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test** compares a sample $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with a sample $y_1, \dots, y_m$ of
real numbers by the statistic
$$
D_{n,m} = \sup_t \abs{\hat F_n(t) - \hat G_m(t)} ,
$$
where $\hat F_n(t)$ and $\hat G_m(t)$ are the fractions of each sample at or below $t$. A large $D_{n,m}$ rejects the
hypothesis that both samples come from the same continuous distribution. The **one-sample** test compares one sample's
$\hat F_n$ with a fully specified continuous cdf $F$ in the same way <Cite id="massey1951" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0$: the two samples come from the same continuous distribution, $F = G$. $H_1$: $F \ne G$, two-sided. The
one-sided versions use $\sup_t (\hat F_n - \hat G_m)$ or $\sup_t (\hat G_m - \hat F_n)$ and test whether one sample's
values tend to be smaller.

## Assumptions

- **Independent samples** of independent draws.
- **Continuous distributions.** The exact null law assumes no ties. With ties, as in rounded or discrete data, the
  test is conservative: its true <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> is below $\alpha$.

## Test statistic

$D_{n,m}$ is the largest vertical distance between two step functions, so it is attained at one of the pooled data
points and is computed by one pass through the sorted pooled sample. Its null law is **distribution-free**: if $F = G$
is continuous, applying $F$ to every point turns both samples into uniform samples without changing any gap, so the
law of $D_{n,m}$ depends only on $n$ and $m$.

## Decision rule

Reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $D_{n,m}$ exceeds the $1 - \alpha$ quantile of its null law. For small samples
the null law is computed exactly by counting splits of the pooled sample <Cite id="smirnov1948" />. For large
samples, $\sqrt{nm/(n + m)}\, D_{n,m}$ converges under $H_0$ to the **Kolmogorov distribution**, with
$$
\prob\paren{K > \lambda} = 2 \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k-1} e^{-2k^2\lambda^2} ,
$$
so at $\alpha = 0.05$ the test rejects when $D_{n,m} > 1.358\sqrt{(n + m)/(nm)}$.

## Effect size

$D_{n,m}$ is itself an <NoteLink to="effect-size">effect size</NoteLink> on a fixed scale from 0 to 1: the largest
difference, over all thresholds $t$, between the shares of the two samples below $t$. It estimates the population
distance $\sup_t \abs{F(t) - G(t)}$.

## Pitfalls

- **Weak in the tails.** The empirical cdfs vary most near their middle and least in their tails, and the supremum
  weighs every point alike, so differences in the tails need large samples to show. The Anderson–Darling test weights
  the gap by $1/\sqrt{F(1 - F)}$ to correct this.
- **One maximum.** A change of spread opens two gaps of opposite sign, on either side of the centre, and $D$ uses only
  the larger. Statistics that add up every gap, such as the <NoteLink to="energy-distance" />, are more powerful
  against such changes.
- **Estimated parameters.** The one-sample test against a normal cdf whose mean and variance were estimated from the
  same data is too conservative; the Lilliefors correction or a parametric bootstrap gives the right null law.
- **One dimension only.** The ordering behind the cdf has no natural extension to several dimensions. For
  multivariate samples use the <NoteLink to="energy-distance" /> or
  <NoteLink to="maximum-mean-discrepancy">MMD</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Test $x = (1.2, 2.3, 3.1)$ against $y = (0.4, 0.9, 1.5, 2.0)$, so $n = 3$ and $m = 4$.

1. **Sort the pooled sample.** $0.4, 0.9, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0, 2.3, 3.1$.
2. **Step through it.** At each point record $\hat F_3$ and $\hat G_4$: $(0, \tfrac14)$, $(0, \tfrac12)$,
   $(\tfrac13, \tfrac12)$, $(\tfrac13, \tfrac34)$, $(\tfrac13, 1)$, $(\tfrac23, 1)$, $(1, 1)$.
3. **Take the largest gap.** The gaps are $0.25, 0.5, 0.167, 0.417, 0.667, 0.333, 0$, so $D_{3,4} = 2/3$, at
   $t = 2.0$: by then every $y$ has appeared but only one $x$.
4. **Count the splits.** There are $\binom{7}{3} = 35$ ways to choose which three pooled points form $x$. Under $H_0$
   each is equally likely, and 14 of them give $D \ge 2/3$.
5. **Conclude.** The exact <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is $14/35 = 0.4$. Samples this small cannot reject
   $H_0$, even though every $y$ lies below two of the three $x$.
