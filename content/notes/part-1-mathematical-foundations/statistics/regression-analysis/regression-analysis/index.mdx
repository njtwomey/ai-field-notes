<Definition>
Regression analysis is the statistical study of a fitted regression model, beyond the fit itself. It asks how uncertain
each coefficient is, whether the model's assumptions hold, and which observations drive the result. For the linear model
$\yvec = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonvec$ with Gaussian noise, these questions have exact answers
<Cite id="seber2003" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A regression is often fitted to learn about the coefficients, not only to predict. A coefficient's sign and size claim
how the outcome changes with a predictor, and that claim needs a <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>
and a test. Diagnostics show when the claim is unreliable: a curved relation fitted by a line, noise whose variance
grows with the fitted value, a few points that move the whole fit, or predictors so correlated that their separate
effects cannot be told apart. The same tools apply to linear probes of neural network representations, to calibration
fits and to the linear baselines that every model is compared against.

## Main ideas

**The coefficients have a known distribution.** Under Gaussian noise the least-squares estimate is
$\hat\betavec \sim \Gauss(\betavec, \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1})$, independent of the residual variance. The note on
<NoteLink to="ordinary-least-squares-inference">inference for least-squares coefficients</NoteLink> derives t tests and
intervals for single coefficients, F tests for nested models, and confidence and
<NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink>.

**Residuals reveal broken assumptions.** <NoteLink to="residual-diagnostics" /> read the assumptions off the residuals
$\evec = (\Imat - \Hmat)\yvec$. Residual-versus-fitted plots show nonlinearity and changing variance, and Q–Q plots
show departures from normality. Sandwich standard errors stay valid when the variance is not constant.

**Some points pull harder than others.** <NoteLink to="leverage-and-influence">Leverage</NoteLink> $h_{ii}$ measures
how far observation $i$ lies from the other inputs and how strongly it pulls its own fitted value. Influence combines
leverage with the residual, and Cook's distance measures how far the fit moves when the point is removed.

**Correlated predictors blur individual effects.** <NoteLink to="multicollinearity" /> inflates the variance of each
coefficient by the variance inflation factor $1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ comes from regressing predictor $j$ on the
others. Predictions within the range of the data are unaffected.
