<Definition>
Predictors are **multicollinear** when some <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">linear combination</NoteLink> of
the columns of the design matrix $\Xmat$ is nearly zero. For predictor $j$, let $R_j^2$ be the
<NoteLink to="r-squared-and-explained-variance">coefficient of determination</NoteLink> from regressing $\xvec_j$ on the
other predictors and an intercept. The **variance inflation factor** is

$$
\operatorname{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2},
$$

the factor by which $\var(\hat\beta_j)$ exceeds its value for a predictor uncorrelated with the others
<Cite id="marquardt1970" />. Its reciprocal $1 - R_j^2$ is the **tolerance**.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A regression coefficient $\beta_j$ is the effect of changing $x_j$ with the other predictors held fixed. If $x_j$ is
almost a linear function of the others, the data contain little variation in $x_j$ with the others held fixed. The
estimate of $\beta_j$ then rests on that small residual variation and is noisy. The fit as a whole can still be good:
the data determine the combined effect of the correlated predictors, not how to split it among them.

## Derivation of the VIF

Under the model $\yvec = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonvec$ with $\cov(\epsilonvec) = \sigma^2\Imat$, the coefficient variance
is $\var(\hat\beta_j) = \sigma^2\brack{(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}}_{jj}$
(<NoteLink to="ordinary-least-squares-inference">OLS inference</NoteLink>). Order the columns as
$\Xmat = [\xvec_j\ \ \Xmat_{-j}]$, where $\Xmat_{-j}$ holds the intercept and the other predictors. By the formula for
the inverse of a partitioned matrix, the top-left entry of $(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$ is the reciprocal of the
<NoteLink to="schur-complement">Schur complement</NoteLink>,

$$
\brack{(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}}_{jj} = \frac{1}{\xvec_j^\top\xvec_j - \xvec_j^\top\Xmat_{-j}(\Xmat_{-j}^\top\Xmat_{-j})^{-1}\Xmat_{-j}^\top\xvec_j}
= \frac{1}{\xvec_j^\top(\Imat - \Hmat_{-j})\xvec_j},
$$

where $\Hmat_{-j}$ projects onto the columns of $\Xmat_{-j}$. The denominator is the residual sum of squares
$\text{RSS}_j$ from regressing $\xvec_j$ on the other predictors. By the definition of $R_j^2$, $\text{RSS}_j =
\text{SST}_j(1 - R_j^2)$, with $\text{SST}_j = \sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2$. Therefore

$$
\var(\hat\beta_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{\text{SST}_j}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}.
$$

The first factor is the variance for a predictor uncorrelated with the others ($R_j^2 = 0$). The second is
$\operatorname{VIF}_j$. The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> grows by
$\sqrt{\operatorname{VIF}_j}$, and a <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> for $\beta_j$
widens by the same factor.

The same Schur complement explains why $\var(\hat\beta_j)$ depends on $\text{RSS}_j$: $\hat\beta_j$ equals the slope
from regressing $\yvec$ on the residual of $\xvec_j$ after removing the other predictors (the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell
theorem), and that residual has sum of squares $\text{RSS}_j$.

## Two predictors

With two predictors of correlation $\rho$, $R_1^2 = R_2^2 = \rho^2$ and $\operatorname{VIF} = 1/(1 - \rho^2)$:

| $\rho$ | 0.5 | 0.9 | 0.95 | 0.99 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VIF | 1.33 | 5.26 | 10.3 | 50.3 |
| SE factor $\sqrt{\text{VIF}}$ | 1.15 | 2.29 | 3.20 | 7.09 |

For standardised predictors (mean 0, variance 1) and $n$ observations, $\cov(\hat\beta_1, \hat\beta_2) =
\frac{\sigma^2}{n(1 - \rho^2)}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Two combinations behave very
differently:

$$
\var(\hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2) = \frac{2\sigma^2(1 - \rho)}{n(1 - \rho^2)} = \frac{2\sigma^2}{n(1 + \rho)}, \qquad
\var(\hat\beta_1 - \hat\beta_2) = \frac{2\sigma^2}{n(1 - \rho)}.
$$

With $\rho = 0.9$, $n = 50$ and $\sigma = 1$, each coefficient has standard deviation 0.32 and their sum has 0.15. The
sum is estimated more precisely than with uncorrelated predictors (0.20); the difference has standard deviation
0.63.

## Other diagnostics

- **Pairwise correlations** detect only dependence between two predictors. Three predictors can be exactly collinear
  ($x_3 = x_1 + x_2$) with modest pairwise correlations. The VIF catches every linear dependence involving $x_j$.
- **Condition number.** Scale the columns of $\Xmat$ to unit length and compute the ratio of the largest to smallest
  singular value, $\kappa = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ (<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink>).
  Belsley, Kuh and Welsch treat condition indices above 30 as a sign of strong dependence, and use the share of each
  coefficient's variance attached to each small singular value to find which predictors are involved
  <Cite id="belsley1980" />.
- **Rules of thumb.** VIF above 5 or 10 is often called high. The threshold matters less than whether the resulting
  standard errors are too wide for the question asked.

## Remedies

Multicollinearity is a property of the data, not a violation of any assumption. The estimates remain unbiased and the
standard errors remain correct; they are large because the data are uninformative about the separate effects.

- **Do nothing** if the goal is prediction for inputs that follow the same correlation pattern, or if the coefficient of
  interest is not among the correlated ones.
- **Estimate the combination** the data determine, such as $\beta_1 + \beta_2$, or combine the predictors into one
  index.
- **Collect data that break the correlation,** or design the experiment so that predictors vary independently.
- **Regularise.** <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge regression</NoteLink> adds $\lambda\Imat$ to $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$,
  which bounds the variance at the cost of bias <Cite id="marquardt1970" />. Principal-component regression drops the
  directions of smallest variance.
- **Drop a predictor** only if the model without it still answers the question; dropping a confounder biases the
  others.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading insignificant coefficients as absent effects.** Two correlated predictors can each have $p > 0.5$ while the
  F test that both are zero gives $p < 0.001$.
- **Unstable signs.** A coefficient can change sign when a correlated predictor is added or a few observations change.
- **Extrapolating off the data's pattern.** Predictions where the correlated predictors move independently are highly
  uncertain, because the data contain no such inputs.
- **Polynomial and interaction terms.** $x$ and $x^2$ are highly correlated when $x$ is far from 0. Centring $x$
  before forming powers and products removes most of this, without changing the fitted values.
