<Definition>
A hypothesis test decides between a null hypothesis $H_0$, a default claim such as "no effect", and an alternative
$H_1$. It summarises the data by a test statistic and rejects $H_0$ when the statistic falls in a region that has small
probability under $H_0$. The theory chooses that region to bound the rate of false
rejections while detecting real effects as often as possible <Cite id="lehmann2005" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Testing answers whether an observed difference could be chance. In machine learning it decides whether one model really
beats another on a test set, whether an <Gloss name="ab-test" /> shows a real lift, whether a feature is associated with
the outcome, and whether deployment data have drifted from training data. Power calculations decide how large an
experiment must be. Multiple-testing corrections keep false discoveries in check when many models, metrics or features
are compared at once.

## Main ideas

**A default, an alternative and a statistic.** <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses" /> fix what is tested.
A test statistic summarises the data, and $H_0$ is rejected when the statistic falls in a critical region whose
probability under $H_0$ is at most $\alpha$.

**The p-value measures surprise under the null.** A <NoteLink to="p-value" /> is the probability, computed assuming
$H_0$, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true. The
<NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink> $\alpha$ is the largest false-rejection probability
allowed, fixed before the data are seen.

**Two errors trade off.** <NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors" /> are rejecting a true null and failing to reject a
false one, with probabilities $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Power, $1 - \beta$, is the probability of detecting a real effect.
<NoteLink to="one-and-two-sided-tests" /> decide whether departures in one direction or both count, and a one-sided
test gains power in its direction at the cost of the other.

**The likelihood ratio is the best statistic.** The <NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma" /> shows that, for one simple
hypothesis against another, rejecting when the likelihood ratio exceeds a threshold gives the most powerful test of its
size. It explains the form of the z-test, the t-tests and many others.

**Plan the sample, correct for many tests.** <NoteLink to="sample-size-planning" /> chooses how much data a test needs
to reach a target power against the smallest effect worth detecting. <NoteLink to="multiple-testing" /> corrections
control an error rate over a whole family of tests. Bonferroni and Holm bound the chance of any false rejection, and
Benjamini–Hochberg bounds the expected share of false discoveries.
