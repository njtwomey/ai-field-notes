<Definition>
Part I collects the mathematics that machine learning is written in. It has four sections. Mathematics covers
deterministic tools: vectors and matrices, <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> and integrals, differential
equations and numerical computation. Probability covers uncertainty and randomness. Statistics covers inference from
data. Optimisation covers finding the best parameters. The later parts of the notes assume these ideas and link back to
them <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A supervised learning problem shows all four sections at once. The data are a matrix $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times d}$,
one row per example, and the model is a function $f_{\thetavec}$ built from <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix
products</NoteLink> and nonlinearities. The model defines a probability distribution over labels, and the loss is the
negative log-likelihood of the training data under it. Training minimises that loss by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic
gradient descent</Gloss>, with <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> from
<NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation">automatic differentiation</NoteLink>. Evaluation estimates the model's
performance on new data, with a <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink>, and tests whether it
beats a baseline. Each step uses a different section of Part I: linear algebra and calculus for the model, probability
for the loss, optimisation for training, statistics for evaluation.

The same mathematics reaches further. <Gloss name="diffusion-model">Diffusion models</Gloss> are
<Gloss name="sde">stochastic differential equations</Gloss>. <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">Normalising
flows</NoteLink> are changes of variables. <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">Kernel methods</NoteLink> are linear algebra in
feature spaces. <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss> are
<NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal distributions</NoteLink> over functions.
<Gloss name="vi">Variational inference</Gloss> is optimisation over distributions. Learning theory is concentration of
measure applied to the gap between training and test error.

## Main ideas

**Linear structure.** Vectors, matrices and the <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear maps</NoteLink> between them
describe data, model layers and covariances. Decompositions such as the
<NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>, the <Gloss name="svd">singular value
decomposition</Gloss> and the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factorisation</NoteLink> turn matrix
computations into simple operations along special directions. Projections give least squares, and
<NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite matrices</NoteLink> give covariances and curvature.

**Local approximation.** Derivatives give the best linear approximation of a function near a point, and the Hessian adds
the quadratic term. The chain rule differentiates compositions, which is how backpropagation computes gradients.
<NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansions</NoteLink> underlie <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> and the analysis of equilibria.

**Uncertainty as distributions.** Probability assigns distributions to unknown quantities and combines them by
conditioning and marginalisation. A handful of named families model most data. Averages concentrate around their
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> at rate $1/\sqrt{n}$, which makes estimation possible and gives
learning theory its bounds.

**Inference from finite samples.** Statistics attaches uncertainty to every estimate, controls the error rates of
decisions, and says how data must be collected for a causal conclusion. Frequentist and
<NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian inference</NoteLink> answer these questions in two different ways.

**Optimisation as the engine of learning.** Convexity decides whether a problem has a single global minimum. Smoothness
and conditioning decide how fast gradient methods converge.
Constraints enter through multipliers and duality. Stochastic gradients make training on large data sets possible.

The sections depend on each other in one direction. Mathematics comes first, and within it linear algebra and calculus.
Probability needs <NoteLink to="integration">integration</NoteLink> and linear algebra. Statistics needs probability.
Optimisation needs calculus and linear algebra, and its stochastic methods need probability. A reader who wants the
shortest route to training a model can read linear algebra, <NoteLink to="functions-of-several-variables">functions of
several variables</NoteLink>, probability theory, the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian
distribution</NoteLink>, gradient descent and stochastic optimisation, in that order.

## Prerequisites and next steps

Part I assumes school algebra and the idea of a function. Every other topic is built up inside it.

Part II, on signals, systems and information, builds on <NoteLink to="fourier-analysis">Fourier analysis</NoteLink>,
differential equations and probability, starting from <NoteLink to="information-theory">information theory</NoteLink>.
Part IV, on the principles of learning, turns the probability and statistics of Part I into learning theory and
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-inference">probabilistic inference</NoteLink>. Part V applies them to concrete learning
methods, from <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> to
<NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning">reinforcement learning</NoteLink>, and Part VI to neural architectures trained by
the optimisers of Part I.
