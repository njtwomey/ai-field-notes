<Definition>
The calculus of functions of one variable studies how a function
$f : \reals \to \reals$ changes. Its central object is the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $f'(a)$, the
instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at a point $a$. Its central results bound a function by its derivatives and
approximate a smooth function near a point by a polynomial <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every training loop in machine learning moves parameters along derivatives. A loss restricted to one direction in
parameter space is a function of one variable, so the one-variable results describe what one step of
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> does. The derivative of an activation function decides
whether a neural network passes a useful signal backwards. Likelihoods are products of small numbers, and the logarithm
turns them into sums that a computer can store. <NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> checking compares an
automatic derivative with a <Gloss name="finite-difference">finite difference</Gloss>, which is the definition of the
derivative evaluated at a small step.

## Main ideas

**The derivative is a limit of secant slopes.** The slope of the line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(a + h, f(a + h))$
tends to $f'(a)$ as $h \to 0$. The same limit makes $f(a) + f'(a)\,h$ the best linear approximation to $f(a + h)$.
The sum, product, quotient and chain rules then give the derivative of any expression built from standard functions.
The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> note develops all of this.

**The exponential is the function equal to its own derivative.** Its inverse, the natural logarithm, turns products into
sums. Machine learning computes likelihoods in log space for this reason, and the log-sum-exp trick keeps those
computations finite. The note on the <NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">exponential and logarithm</NoteLink>
derives both functions.

**Measuring angles in radians makes the circular functions differentiate cleanly.** With $x$ in radians,
$\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$, which rests on the limit $\sin h / h \to 1$. The note on
<NoteLink to="derivatives-of-trigonometric-functions">derivatives of the trigonometric functions</NoteLink> proves this
and extends it to the other circular functions.

**A bound on the derivative bounds the function.** The <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean value theorem</NoteLink>
says that a function differentiable on an interval has a tangent parallel to the chord somewhere inside it. A bound
$\abs{f'} \le L$ then gives $\abs{f(x) - f(y)} \le L\,\abs{x - y}$, a Lipschitz bound. Applied to the derivative itself,
the same argument gives the step-size rule for gradient descent.

**A smooth function is a polynomial near any point.** A <NoteLink to="taylor-series" /> builds that polynomial from the
derivatives at the point. Truncating it after the linear term gives gradient descent. Truncating it after the quadratic
term gives Newton's method.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>, which every other note uses. The
<NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">exponential and logarithm</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="derivatives-of-trigonometric-functions">derivatives of the trigonometric functions</NoteLink> apply the
rules to the two families of functions that appear most often in models. The <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean
value theorem</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="taylor-series" /> turn derivatives into statements about the function
over a whole interval, and they are the two results that optimisation theory uses most.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume algebra and the idea of a function. The trigonometric derivatives also assume the
<NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">sine and cosine</NoteLink> from <NoteLink to="trigonometry">trigonometry</NoteLink>.

The <NoteLink to="functions-of-several-variables">functions of several variables</NoteLink> overview extends the
derivative to the gradient, the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>. <NoteLink to="integration" /> reverses differentiation.
<NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation" /> computes derivatives of whole programs. In optimisation,
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="newtons-method" /> are the first- and
second-order Taylor approximations put to work.
