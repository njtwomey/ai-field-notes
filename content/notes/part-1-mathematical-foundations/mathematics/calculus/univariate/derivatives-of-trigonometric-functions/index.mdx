<Definition>
For $x$ measured in radians, the six trigonometric functions have the derivatives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\sin x &= \cos x, & \frac{d}{dx}\cos x &= -\sin x, & \frac{d}{dx}\tan x &= \sec^2 x, \\
\frac{d}{dx}\sec x &= \sec x\tan x, & \frac{d}{dx}\csc x &= -\csc x\cot x, & \frac{d}{dx}\cot x &= -\csc^2 x,
\end{aligned}
$$

each wherever the function is defined. All six follow from one limit, $\lim_{h \to 0} (\sin h)/h = 1$
<Cite id="stewart2016" />.
</Definition>

## The limit of sin h / h

Take $0 < h < \pi/2$ and the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit circle</NoteLink> centred at $O$. Let $A = (1, 0)$, let
$P = (\cos h, \sin h)$ be the point at angle $h$, and let $T = (1, \tan h)$ be where the ray $OP$ meets the tangent line
$x = 1$. Three regions are nested:

- the triangle $OAP$, with base $OA = 1$ and height $\sin h$, of area $\tfrac12\sin h$;
- the circular sector $OAP$, of area $\tfrac12 h$ (a sector of angle $h$ radians on a circle of radius 1);
- the triangle $OAT$, with base 1 and height $\tan h$, of area $\tfrac12\tan h$.

The first lies inside the second and the second inside the third, so

$$
\tfrac12\sin h \le \tfrac12 h \le \tfrac12\tan h.
$$

Every term is positive. Dividing by $\tfrac12\sin h$ gives $1 \le h/\sin h \le 1/\cos h$, and taking reciprocals
reverses the inequalities:

$$
\cos h \le \frac{\sin h}{h} \le 1.
$$

As $h \to 0^+$, $\cos h \to 1$, so $\sin h/h$ is squeezed to 1. Both $\sin h$ and $h$ change sign with $h$, so the ratio
is even and the limit from the left is also 1 <Cite id="spivak2008" />.

<SqueezeLimit />

The argument uses the sector area $\tfrac12 h$, which holds only when $h$ is in radians. In degrees the sector of
$h°$ has area $\tfrac12 \cdot \pi h/180$, and the same squeeze gives $\sin(h°)/h \to \pi/180$.

A second limit follows. Multiplying by $1 + \cos h$ and using $1 - \cos^2 h = \sin^2 h$,

$$
\frac{1 - \cos h}{h} = \frac{\sin^2 h}{h(1 + \cos h)} = \frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{\sin h}{1 + \cos h}
\to 1 \cdot \frac{0}{2} = 0.
$$

## The derivatives of sine and cosine

The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of $\sin$ at $x$ is the limit of the difference quotient. The
angle-sum formula $\sin(x + h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h$ (see
<NoteLink to="trigonometric-identities">trigonometric identities</NoteLink>) separates the parts that depend on $h$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} &= \sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h} \\
&\to \sin x \cdot 0 + \cos x \cdot 1 = \cos x.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same steps with $\cos(x + h) = \cos x\cos h - \sin x\sin h$ give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\cos(x + h) - \cos x}{h} &= \cos x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} - \sin x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h} \\
&\to -\sin x.
\end{aligned}
$$

Geometrically, the point $(\cos x, \sin x)$ moves around the unit circle at unit speed as $x$ increases, because $x$ is
arc length. Its velocity is tangent to the circle, a quarter turn ahead of the position: $(-\sin x, \cos x)$. The two
components of the velocity are the two derivatives.

## Tangent and the reciprocal functions

The quotient rule gives the rest:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\tan x &= \frac{\cos x\cos x - \sin x\,(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x, \\
\frac{d}{dx}\sec x &= \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x)^{-1} = -(\cos x)^{-2}(-\sin x) = \sec x\tan x, \\
\frac{d}{dx}\csc x &= \frac{d}{dx}(\sin x)^{-1} = -(\sin x)^{-2}\cos x = -\csc x\cot x, \\
\frac{d}{dx}\cot x &= \frac{-\sin x\sin x - \cos x\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\frac{1}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc^2 x.
\end{aligned}
$$

By the Pythagorean identity, $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$. The functions named with "co" pick up a minus sign, because each
is its partner evaluated at $\pi/2 - x$ and the chain rule contributes $-1$.

## The inverse functions

The inverse function rule differentiates $\arcsin$, $\arccos$ and $\arctan$. For $\theta = \arcsin y$ with
$-1 < y < 1$, differentiating $\sin\theta = y$ gives $\cos\theta\cdot\theta' = 1$. On the principal branch
$[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ the cosine is non-negative, so $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$ and

$$
\frac{d}{dy}\arcsin y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dy}\arccos y = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}}, \qquad
\frac{d}{dy}\arctan y = \frac{1}{1 + y^2},
$$

the last from $\sec^2\theta\cdot\theta' = 1$ and $\sec^2\theta = 1 + \tan^2\theta = 1 + y^2$ (see
<NoteLink to="inverse-trigonometric-functions">inverse trigonometric functions</NoteLink>). The derivative of
$\arctan$ is a rational function, so $\arctan y = \int_0^y dt/(1 + t^2)$, an integral that appears whenever a Cauchy
density is integrated.

## Higher derivatives and oscillation

Differentiating $\sin$ repeatedly cycles with period 4:

$$
\sin x \to \cos x \to -\sin x \to -\cos x \to \sin x.
$$

Two consequences follow.

- **The oscillator equation.** Both $\sin$ and $\cos$ satisfy $y'' = -y$, and so does every combination
  $A\cos x + B\sin x$. With a frequency $\omega$, the chain rule gives
  $\frac{d}{dt}\sin(\omega t) = \omega\cos(\omega t)$ and $y'' = -\omega^2 y$, the equation of a mass on a spring.
  Differentiating a sinusoid multiplies its amplitude by $\omega$ and advances its phase by $\pi/2$.
- **The Taylor series.** At $x = 0$ the derivatives of $\sin$ are $0, 1, 0, -1, 0, 1, \dots$, so its
  <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> is $\sin x = x - x^3/3! + x^5/5! - \cdots$, and similarly
  $\cos x = 1 - x^2/2! + x^4/4! - \cdots$. Combined with the exponential series, these give
  <NoteLink to="eulers-formula">Euler's formula</NoteLink>. Truncating after the first term gives the small-angle
  approximations $\sin x \approx x$ and $\cos x \approx 1 - x^2/2$.

Networks with sine activations, such as SIREN, use the cycle directly: the derivative of a sine layer is a phase-shifted
sine layer, so derivatives of the fitted function are themselves well behaved <Cite id="sitzmann2020" />.

## Worked example

Differentiate $f(x) = x^2\sin 3x$ and evaluate at $x = \pi/6$. The product and chain rules give

$$
f'(x) = 2x\sin 3x + 3x^2\cos 3x.
$$

At $x = \pi/6$, $3x = \pi/2$, so $\sin 3x = 1$ and $\cos 3x = 0$:
$f'(\pi/6) = 2 \cdot \pi/6 \cdot 1 + 0 = \pi/3 \approx 1.047$.

Check the limit numerically: $\sin(0.1)/0.1 = 0.99833$, $\sin(0.01)/0.01 = 0.99998$, and the bounds give
$\cos(0.1) = 0.99500 \le 0.99833 \le 1$.

## Pitfalls

- **Degrees.** In degrees every derivative carries a factor $\pi/180$: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x°) = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos(x°)$.
- **Forgetting the chain rule.** $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(\omega x) = \omega\cos(\omega x)$, not $\cos(\omega x)$.
  High-frequency features have large derivatives.
- **Powers.** $\frac{d}{dx}\sin^2 x = 2\sin x\cos x = \sin 2x$, by the chain rule on $(\sin x)^2$; it is not
  $\cos^2 x$.
- **Circular reasoning.** Proving $\sin h/h \to 1$ with L'Hôpital's rule uses $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$, which is
  what the limit is needed to prove. The squeeze argument avoids this.
