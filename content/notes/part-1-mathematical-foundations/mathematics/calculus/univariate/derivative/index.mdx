<Definition>
The **derivative** of a function $f : \reals \to \reals$ at a point $a$ is

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h},
$$

when this limit exists and is finite. The fraction is the **difference quotient**: the slope of the straight line
through the two points $(a, f(a))$ and $(a + h, f(a + h))$ of the graph. The derivative is the slope of the graph at
$a$, the rate at which $f$ changes there. A function whose derivative exists at $a$ is **differentiable** at $a$
<Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## The idea: a rate of change at an instant

A stone dropped from rest falls $s(t) = 5t^2$ metres in its first $t$ seconds, ignoring air resistance. Its speed
changes all the time. The question is how fast it moves at exactly $t = 2$ seconds.

An average speed needs two times. Between $t = 2$ and $t = 2 + h$ the stone covers $s(2 + h) - s(2)$ metres in $h$
seconds, so its average speed over that interval is

$$
\frac{s(2 + h) - s(2)}{h} = \frac{5(4 + 4h + h^2) - 20}{h} = \frac{20h + 5h^2}{h} = 20 + 5h \ \text{m/s}.
$$

The division by $h$ is allowed because $h \ne 0$. Shorter intervals give averages that settle on one number:

| interval length $h$ (s) | 1 | 0.1 | 0.01 | 0.001 | $-0.1$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| average speed (m/s) | 25 | 20.5 | 20.05 | 20.005 | 19.5 |

No interval, however short, gives the speed at the instant $t = 2$ itself: with $h = 0$ the quotient is $0/0$. The
averages approach 20 from above when $h > 0$ and from below when $h < 0$. The speed at $t = 2$ is defined as this limit,
20 m/s. That number is the derivative $s'(2)$. A speedometer reports it.

The same construction applies to any quantity that depends on another. The derivative of a production cost with respect
to the number of units made is the marginal cost: the extra cost of one more unit, in the limit of small changes. The
derivative of a training loss with respect to a weight is the change in loss per unit change of that weight.

## Secant lines

Geometrically, the average rate of change is a slope. Take two points on the graph of $f$: $P = (a, f(a))$ and
$Q = (a + h, f(a + h))$, with $h \ne 0$. The **secant line** is the straight line through $P$ and $Q$. Its run is the
horizontal distance $h$ between the points, and its rise is the vertical distance $f(a + h) - f(a)$. Its slope is rise
over run, which is the difference quotient <Cite id="strang2016calculus" />:

$$
\text{slope of } PQ = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}.
$$

The name comes from the Latin _secare_, to cut: the line cuts the curve at two points <Cite id="harper-secant" />. The
secant line is unrelated to the trigonometric function $\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta$, one of the
<NoteLink to="tangent-and-reciprocal-trigonometric-functions">reciprocal trigonometric functions</NoteLink>, which
shares the word for a historical reason: on the unit-circle diagram that defines it, $\sec\theta$ is the length of a
line segment that cuts the circle.

The secant slope depends on two points, so it describes $f$ over an interval. For the falling stone it is the average
speed over $[2, 2 + h]$. To describe $f$ at the single point $a$, the second point must move towards the first.

## The tangent line as a limit of secants

As $h$ shrinks, $Q$ slides along the curve towards $P$, and the secant pivots about $P$. For a smooth curve the secant
settles on one line through $P$: the **tangent line**, from the Latin _tangere_, to touch
<Cite id="harper-tangent" />. Near $P$ the tangent touches the curve without cutting across it, except at an
inflection point. The tangent's slope is the limit of the secant slopes, which is the derivative, so the tangent line is

$$
y = f(a) + f'(a)\,(x - a).
$$

### What the limit means

The difference quotient $q(h) = \tfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ is a function of $h$ defined for every small $h$ except
$h = 0$. The statement $\lim_{h \to 0} q(h) = L$ means that $q(h)$ is as close to $L$ as desired for every $h$ close
enough to 0: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $\abs{q(h) - L} < \varepsilon$ whenever
$0 < \abs{h} < \delta$ <Cite id="spivak2008" />. The value at $h = 0$ is never used. The right panel of the figure above
plots $q(h)$ against $h$. The derivative is the height that curve approaches at the gap $h = 0$.

The limit must be the same from both sides. The **right derivative** $f'_+(a)$ is the limit of $q(h)$ over $h > 0$
only, and the **left derivative** $f'_-(a)$ is the limit over $h < 0$. The derivative $f'(a)$ exists exactly when both
one-sided limits exist, are finite and are equal, and then $f'(a)$ is their common value. A function can fail this in
two ways: the two one-sided limits differ (a corner), or the quotient grows without bound (a vertical tangent). Both
cases are treated under differentiability below.

## The best linear approximation

The tangent line approximates $f$ near $a$:

$$
f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h.
$$

The approximation is better than any other line's in a precise sense. Write its error as
$r(h) = f(a + h) - f(a) - f'(a)\,h$. Dividing by $h$ gives

$$
\frac{r(h)}{h} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} - f'(a) \to 0 \quad \text{as } h \to 0.
$$

So $f(a + h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + o(h)$, where $o(h)$ denotes a term that vanishes faster than $h$: its ratio to $h$
tends to 0. Conversely, if some number $m$ makes $f(a + h) - f(a) - m h = o(h)$, dividing by $h$ shows that the
difference quotient tends to $m$, so $m = f'(a)$. The derivative is therefore the unique slope for which a line through
$(a, f(a))$ matches $f$ to first order. Any other slope $m \ne f'(a)$ leaves an error of about $(f'(a) - m)\,h$, which
shrinks only as fast as $h$.

For $f(x) = x^2$ the error is exact: $(a + h)^2 = a^2 + 2a\,h + h^2$, so $f'(a) = 2a$ and $r(h) = h^2$. Halving $h$
quarters the error. For a function with a second derivative the error behaves in the same way,
$r(h) \approx \tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)\,h^2$, which the <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> makes exact.
This is why a differentiable curve looks straight under magnification: on a window of width $h$ around $a$, the gap
between curve and tangent is of order $h^2$, a vanishing fraction of the window.

The linear-approximation form is the one that generalises. In several variables the slope becomes the
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, and the <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink> becomes
the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>.

## The derivative as a function

Computing $f'(a)$ at every point $a$ where it exists defines a new function $f'$, the **derivative function**. Its
value at $x$ is the slope of $f$ at $x$. Where $f$ increases, $f' > 0$; where $f$ decreases, $f' < 0$. At a peak or a
trough of a differentiable $f$ the tangent is horizontal and $f' = 0$.

## Derivatives from first principles

Each derivative below comes from the definition: expand $f(a + h)$, subtract $f(a)$, cancel the factor $h$ (allowed,
because $h \ne 0$), then let $h \to 0$ in what remains.

**Square, $f(x) = x^2$.**

$$
\frac{(a + h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - a^2}{h} = \frac{2ah + h^2}{h} = 2a + h \to 2a.
$$

So $f'(x) = 2x$. The term $h^2$ in the numerator becomes $h$ after dividing and disappears in the limit.

**Cube, $f(x) = x^3$.** Expanding $(a + h)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3$ gives

$$
\frac{(a + h)^3 - a^3}{h} = \frac{3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3}{h} = 3a^2 + 3ah + h^2 \to 3a^2.
$$

**Any positive integer power, $f(x) = x^n$.** The binomial theorem expands
$(a + h)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k} h^k$. The $k = 0$ term is $a^n$, which cancels, and the $k = 1$ term
is $n a^{n-1} h$. Every other term contains $h^2$ or a higher power. Dividing by $h$,

$$
\frac{(a + h)^n - a^n}{h} = n a^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2} a^{n-2} h + \dots + h^{n-1} \to n a^{n-1}.
$$

Only the term linear in $h$ survives: the derivative reads off the first-order coefficient of the expansion.

**Reciprocal, $f(x) = 1/x$, for $a \ne 0$.** Put the two fractions over a common denominator:

$$
\frac{1}{h}\paren{\frac{1}{a + h} - \frac{1}{a}} = \frac{1}{h} \cdot \frac{a - (a + h)}{a(a + h)}
= \frac{-1}{a(a + h)} \to -\frac{1}{a^2}.
$$

The derivative is negative everywhere: $1/x$ decreases on each side of 0. At $a = 0$ the function is undefined, so it
has no derivative there.

**Square root, $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, for $a > 0$.** Direct substitution gives $0/0$. Multiplying the numerator and
denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{a + h} + \sqrt{a}$ (rationalising) turns the difference of roots into a difference
of squares, $(\sqrt{a + h})^2 - (\sqrt{a})^2 = h$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{a + h} - \sqrt{a}}{h}
&= \frac{\sqrt{a + h} - \sqrt{a}}{h} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{a + h} + \sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{a + h} + \sqrt{a}}
= \frac{h}{h\paren{\sqrt{a + h} + \sqrt{a}}} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{a + h} + \sqrt{a}} \to \frac{1}{2\sqrt{a}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

At $a = 0$ only $h > 0$ is allowed, and the quotient $\sqrt{h}/h = 1/\sqrt{h}$ grows without bound: the graph of
$\sqrt{x}$ leaves the origin vertically.

The last two results fit the power rule with exponents $-1$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$: $(x^{-1})' = -x^{-2}$ and
$(x^{1/2})' = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}$. The rule $(x^r)' = r x^{r-1}$ holds for every real $r$, as the table of standard
derivatives below shows.

## Notation

Three notations are in use, named after Lagrange, Leibniz and Newton.

- **Lagrange:** $f'(x)$, $f''(x)$, $f'''(x)$, then $f^{(n)}(x)$ for the $n$th derivative. It names the derivative as a
  function, and $f'(a)$ is its value at $a$.
- **Leibniz:** for $y = f(x)$, the derivative is $\frac{dy}{dx}$, or $\frac{d}{dx} f(x)$, where $\frac{d}{dx}$ is the
  operator that maps a function to its derivative. Its value at $a$ is written $\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x = a}$.
  The second derivative is $\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}$.
- **Newton:** a dot over a quantity that depends on time, $\dot{x} = \frac{dx}{dt}$ and $\ddot{x} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$.
  It is used for velocities and accelerations in mechanics and for state equations in
  <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">ordinary differential equations</NoteLink>.

The Leibniz symbol $\frac{dy}{dx}$ recalls the quotient $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ it is the limit of, where
$\Delta x = h$ and $\Delta y = f(x + h) - f(x)$. It is not a fraction: $dy$ and $dx$ are not numbers in this definition,
and the symbol is read as a whole. It nevertheless behaves like a fraction in the one-variable rules. The chain rule
reads $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$, as if $du$ cancelled, and the derivative of an inverse function
reads $\frac{dx}{dy} = 1 / \frac{dy}{dx}$. The analogy fails elsewhere. The second derivative $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ is not
$\paren{\frac{dy}{dx}}^2$. For three variables tied by one equation $F(x, y, z) = 0$, the partial derivatives satisfy
$\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -1$, not $+1$.

### Higher derivatives

The derivative of $f'$ is the **second derivative** $f''$. It measures how fast the slope changes. For the falling
stone, $s(t) = 5t^2$ gives velocity $\dot{s}(t) = 10t$ m/s and acceleration $\ddot{s}(t) = 10$ m/s², the rate at which
the velocity changes. On a graph, $f'' > 0$ means the slope increases from left to right and the graph bends upwards; on
an interval where $f'' \ge 0$, $f$ is <Gloss name="convex-function">convex</Gloss> on that interval and lies above each
of its tangent lines there. $f'' < 0$ means the graph bends downwards. The second derivative refines the linear
approximation to a quadratic one, $f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(a)\,h^2$, and at a point where
$f'(a) = 0$ the sign of $f''(a)$ tells a minimum ($f''(a) > 0$) from a maximum ($f''(a) < 0$).

## Differentiability

**Differentiable functions are continuous.** If $f'(a)$ exists, then

$$
f(a + h) - f(a) = h \cdot \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \to 0 \cdot f'(a) = 0,
$$

so $f(a + h) \to f(a)$ as $h \to 0$. A jump in the graph therefore rules out a derivative.

**Continuity is not sufficient.** Three continuous functions show the ways a derivative can fail to exist at 0.

- **A corner: $\abs{x}$.** The difference quotient at 0 is $\abs{h}/h$, which is $+1$ for every $h > 0$ and $-1$ for
  every $h < 0$. The one-sided derivatives are $f'_+(0) = 1$ and $f'_-(0) = -1$. They differ, so $f'(0)$ does not
  exist. Every line through the origin with slope between $-1$ and $1$ stays below the graph, and none is the tangent.
- **A kink: <Gloss name="relu" />, $\max(0, x)$.** The left derivative at 0 is 0 and the right derivative is 1. Deep
  learning frameworks still need a number at 0, and use a fixed value there, usually 0. Any value in $[0, 1]$ is a
  <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradient</NoteLink>: the slope of a line through the kink that stays below the
  function. Inputs of exactly 0 occur in finite-precision training, and in 16-bit and 32-bit arithmetic the value
  chosen for $\text{ReLU}'(0)$ can change the computed gradients and the trained network <Cite id="bertoin2021" />.
- **A vertical tangent: $\sqrt[3]{x}$.** The quotient at 0 is $h^{1/3}/h = h^{-2/3}$, which tends to $+\infty$ from
  both sides. The secants approach the vertical line $x = 0$, a tangent line with no finite slope. The derivative is
  required to be a real number, so $\sqrt[3]{x}$ is not differentiable at 0.

Continuous functions can be worse still. The Weierstrass function is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere,
and the sample paths of <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink> share that property.

## The rules of differentiation

Derivatives of combinations of functions follow from the derivatives of the parts. Each rule below follows from the
definition. In each, $f$ and $g$ are differentiable at the point $a$.

**Linearity.** For constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the difference quotient of $\alpha f + \beta g$ is $\alpha$ times
that of $f$ plus $\beta$ times that of $g$. Limits of sums are sums of limits, so

$$
(\alpha f + \beta g)' = \alpha f' + \beta g'.
$$

**Product rule.** Picture $f(a)\,g(a)$ as the area of a rectangle with sides $f(a)$ and $g(a)$. When $a$ moves by $h$,
the sides grow by $\Delta f = f(a + h) - f(a)$ and $\Delta g = g(a + h) - g(a)$. The area grows by three strips:
$f\,\Delta g$ along one side, $g\,\Delta f$ along the other, and the corner $\Delta f\,\Delta g$. Each $\Delta$ is
proportional to $h$ to first order, so the corner is of order $h^2$ and drops out after dividing by $h$. The algebra
adds and subtracts $f(a + h)\,g(a)$ in the numerator and groups the terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(a+h)g(a+h) - f(a)g(a)}{h}
&= f(a + h)\,\frac{g(a + h) - g(a)}{h} \\
&\quad + g(a)\,\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As $h \to 0$, $f(a + h) \to f(a)$ because $f$ is continuous, and the two quotients tend to $g'(a)$ and $f'(a)$. Hence

$$
(fg)'(a) = f(a)\,g'(a) + f'(a)\,g(a).
$$

**Chain rule.** For a composition $f(g(x))$, rates multiply. If $u = g(x)$ changes 3 times as fast as $x$ and
$y = f(u)$ changes 2 times as fast as $u$, then $y$ changes 6 times as fast as $x$. The proof uses the linear
approximation twice. Let $k = g(a + h) - g(a) = g'(a)\,h + o(h)$. Then

$$
f(g(a + h)) = f(g(a) + k) = f(g(a)) + f'(g(a))\,k + o(k).
$$

Since $k$ is at most a constant times $\abs{h}$ for small $h$, $o(k)$ is also $o(h)$. Substituting $k$ gives
$f(g(a + h)) = f(g(a)) + f'(g(a))\,g'(a)\,h + o(h)$, and the uniqueness of the linear approximation gives

$$
(f \circ g)'(a) = f'(g(a))\,g'(a).
$$

The shortcut of writing $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}$ and taking
limits fails when $\Delta u = 0$ for arbitrarily small $h$, as for a constant $g$; the linear-approximation proof has no
such gap <Cite id="spivak2008" />. The <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">multivariable chain rule</NoteLink>
extends the rule to functions of several variables, where the factors become Jacobian matrices.
<NoteLink to="backpropagation">Backpropagation</NoteLink> applies it to a neural network, one layer at a time.

**Quotient rule.** First, the reciprocal: by the first-principles result $(1/t)' = -1/t^2$ and the chain rule,
$(1/g)' = -g'/g^2$ wherever $g \ne 0$. The product rule applied to $f \cdot (1/g)$ then gives

$$
\paren{\frac{f}{g}}' = \frac{f'}{g} - \frac{f\,g'}{g^2} = \frac{f'g - f\,g'}{g^2}.
$$

## Derivatives of standard functions

| $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ | Derivation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| constant $c$ | $0$ | The quotient is $(c - c)/h = 0$ for every $h$. |
| $x^n$, integer $n \ge 1$ | $n x^{n-1}$ | Binomial expansion, above. |
| $x^r$, real $r$, $x > 0$ | $r x^{r-1}$ | $x^r = e^{r \log x}$; the chain rule gives $e^{r \log x} \cdot r/x = r x^{r-1}$. |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ | $\tfrac{e^{x+h} - e^x}{h} = e^x\,\tfrac{e^h - 1}{h}$, and $\tfrac{e^h - 1}{h} \to 1$. |
| $b^x$, $b > 0$ | $b^x \log b$ | $b^x = e^{x \log b}$ and the chain rule. |
| $\log x$, $x > 0$ | $1/x$ | Differentiate $e^{\log x} = x$ with the chain rule: $e^{\log x}(\log x)' = 1$. |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | $\sin(x + h) - \sin x = \sin x\,(\cos h - 1) + \cos x \sin h$; use $\tfrac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$, $\tfrac{\cos h - 1}{h} \to 0$. |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | The same expansion of $\cos(x + h)$. |
| $\tan x$ | $1/\cos^2 x = \sec^2 x$ | The quotient rule on $\sin x / \cos x$, with $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$. |

Here $\log$ is the natural logarithm. The limit $(e^h - 1)/h \to 1$ is the defining property of $e$; the
<NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">exponential and logarithm</NoteLink> note derives it. The trigonometric rows
hold with $x$ in <NoteLink to="angles-and-radians">radians</NoteLink>, and the
<NoteLink to="derivatives-of-trigonometric-functions">derivatives of the trigonometric functions</NoteLink> note derives
them in full. The limit $\tfrac{\sin h}{h} \to 1$ is a statement about radians; with $x$ in degrees,
$\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos x$.

## Worked examples with the rules

**A product.** For $f(x) = x^2 e^{-x}$, the product rule with the chain rule for $e^{-x}$ gives

$$
f'(x) = 2x\,e^{-x} + x^2 \cdot (-e^{-x}) = x\,e^{-x}(2 - x).
$$

The derivative is zero at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. It is negative for $x < 0$, positive between 0 and 2, and negative after
2, so $f$ has a minimum at 0 and a maximum at 2, where $f(2) = 4e^{-2} \approx 0.541$. The figure above plots this
function and its derivative.

**The sigmoid.** For $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$, the reciprocal rule $(1/g)' = -g'/g^2$ with $g = 1 + e^{-x}$ and
$g' = -e^{-x}$ gives

$$
\sigma'(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(x)\bigr).
$$

The derivative is largest at $x = 0$, where it equals $1/4$, and it decays towards 0 in both tails. That decay is why
saturated sigmoid units pass back almost no gradient in backpropagation.

## Numerical derivatives

A derivative can be estimated from values of $f$ alone by keeping $h$ small but nonzero. Two finite differences are
standard <Cite id="nocedal2006" />:

$$
D_+(h) = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}, \qquad D_0(h) = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a - h)}{2h}.
$$

$D_+$ is the **forward difference**, the secant slope itself. $D_0$ is the **central difference**, the slope of the
secant through the two points either side of $a$.

**Truncation error.** For a twice-differentiable $f$, Taylor's theorem gives
$f(a + h) = f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(\xi)\,h^2$ for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $a + h$. Substituting,

$$
D_+(h) = f'(a) + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(\xi)\,h,
$$

so the error of the forward difference is of order $h$. For the central difference, expand both $f(a + h)$ and
$f(a - h)$ to third order. The terms $f(a)$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}f''(a)\,h^2$ appear with the same sign in both and cancel
in the subtraction, which leaves

$$
D_0(h) = f'(a) + \tfrac{1}{6} f'''(\eta)\,h^2
$$

for some $\eta$ in $[a - h, a + h]$. The central difference has error of order $h^2$: halving $h$ quarters the error. It
needs $f(a + h)$ and $f(a - h)$, while the forward difference can reuse a known $f(a)$ and needs only $f(a + h)$.

**Rounding error.** In <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">floating-point arithmetic</NoteLink> each value of $f$
carries a relative error up to the unit roundoff $u$, about $1.1 \times 10^{-16}$ in double precision
<Cite id="higham2002" />. When $h$ is small, $f(a + h)$ and $f(a)$ agree in most of their leading digits, and their
difference keeps only the last few, which are mostly rounding error. That error, of size about $u\,\abs{f(a)}$, is then
divided by $h$. The total error of the forward difference is about

$$
E_+(h) \approx \frac{M}{2}\,h + \frac{2u\,\abs{f(a)}}{h},
$$

with $M$ a bound on $\abs{f''}$ near $a$. The first term falls with $h$ and the second grows. Setting the derivative of
$E_+$ to zero gives the best step $h^\ast = 2\sqrt{u\,\abs{f(a)}/M}$, of order $\sqrt{u} \approx 10^{-8}$ when $f$ and
$f''$ are of order 1, and a best error of the same order. The same argument for the central difference balances
$h^2$ against $u/h$, which gives $h^\ast$ of order $u^{1/3} \approx 5 \times 10^{-6}$ and an error of order
$u^{2/3} \approx 10^{-11}$ <Cite id="nocedal2006" />. A step proportional to $\max(1, \abs{a})$ keeps the relative
size of $h$ fixed when $a$ is large.

<FiniteDifferenceError />

Finite differences cost one or two evaluations of $f$ per input variable, which is too slow for a model with millions
of parameters. <Gloss name="autodiff" /> instead applies the rules of differentiation to each elementary operation of a
program. It has no step $h$, so it has neither truncation error nor cancellation, and its result is exact up to
rounding <Cite id="baydin2018" />. <NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">Forward mode</NoteLink> carries a derivative
along with each value, for instance with <NoteLink to="dual-numbers">dual numbers</NoteLink>. Reverse mode, used by
backpropagation, carries derivatives backwards from the output.

## In machine learning

- **Training** needs the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter.
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> moves each parameter against its derivative, because the
  linear approximation says that a small step $-\eta f'(a)$, for a step size $\eta > 0$, changes $f$ by about
  $-\eta f'(a)^2 \le 0$.
- **<NoteLink to="activation-functions">Activation functions</NoteLink>** are chosen partly for their derivatives:
  ReLU has derivative 1 on its active side and does not saturate there, unlike the sigmoid.
- **Gradient checking** compares a hand-written or automatic derivative with a central difference at a few random
  points. For a well-scaled function the central difference is accurate to about $u^{2/3} \approx 10^{-11}$ in double
  precision, so a disagreement many orders of magnitude larger points to a bug in the derivative code.

## Pitfalls

- **Secant line and secant function.** The secant line through two points of a graph has nothing to do with the
  trigonometric $\sec x = 1/\cos x$, apart from the Latin root.
- **The derivative at a point is a number; the derivative is a function.** $f'(a)$ is the slope at one point, and $f'$
  collects those slopes for every point. Evaluating before differentiating gives the derivative of a constant, which is
  0.
- **Continuity is not enough.** $\abs{x}$, ReLU and $\sqrt[3]{x}$ are continuous at 0 and not differentiable there.
- **A zero derivative does not mean an extremum.** $f(x) = x^3$ has $f'(0) = 0$ and increases through 0. A stationary
  point is a candidate for a minimum or maximum, and the second derivative or the sign of $f'$ on each side decides.
- **A derivative need not be continuous.** $f(x) = x^2 \sin(1/x)$ with $f(0) = 0$ is differentiable everywhere, with
  $f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} h \sin(1/h) = 0$, but $f'(x) = 2x\sin(1/x) - \cos(1/x)$ oscillates between about $-1$ and 1
  as $x \to 0$ <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
- **Leibniz notation is not a fraction.** $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \ne \paren{\frac{dy}{dx}}^2$, and the cancellation picture
  fails for partial derivatives.
- **Degrees.** $(\sin x)' = \cos x$ holds only for $x$ in radians.
- **Step size in finite differences.** Too large an $h$ gives truncation error; too small an $h$ subtracts nearly equal
  floating-point numbers and loses precision. In double precision, $h \approx 10^{-8}$ suits the forward difference and
  $h \approx 10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ the central difference, for arguments and values of order 1.
