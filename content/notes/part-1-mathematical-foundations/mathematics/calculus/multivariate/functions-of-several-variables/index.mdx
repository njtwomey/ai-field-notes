<Definition>
The calculus of several variables studies functions $f : \reals^n \to \reals^m$. Near
a point, a differentiable $f$ behaves like a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink>, and the matrix
of that map collects every first partial derivative. For a scalar function ($m = 1$) that matrix has one row, whose
transpose is the gradient, and the matrix of second partial derivatives is the Hessian <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A model with $n$ parameters has a loss $L : \reals^n \to \reals$. Training needs the gradient $\nabla L$, the vector of
its $n$ partial derivatives. A neural network is a composition of layers, each a map between spaces of several
variables, and the chain rule turns the composition into a product of Jacobians. The Hessian of the loss describes the
shape of the landscape near a minimum: how wide the valley is, and how badly conditioned. Changes of variables in
probability, as in <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flows</NoteLink>,
scale densities by the absolute <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> of a Jacobian.

## Main ideas

**Hold all but one variable fixed.** A <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivative</NoteLink> is the
ordinary derivative along one coordinate axis. Partials can exist where the function is not differentiable.
Continuous partials guarantee differentiability and make the mixed second partials equal.

**The gradient points uphill.** The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $\nabla f(\xvec)$ is the vector of
partials of a scalar function. The derivative in a unit direction $\uvec$ is $\nabla f(\xvec)^\top \uvec$, which is
largest when $\uvec$ points along the gradient. Gradient descent steps the other way. The gradient is zero at every
interior minimum, maximum and saddle point.

**The Jacobian is the best linear approximation.** The <NoteLink to="jacobian" /> $\Jmat$ of a map
$\reals^n \to \reals^m$ is the $m \times n$ matrix of first partials, so $f(\xvec + \hvec) \approx f(\xvec) +
\Jmat\hvec$. When $m = n$, $\abs{\det \Jmat}$ is the factor by which the map scales small volumes.

**Derivatives of compositions multiply.** The <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">multivariable chain
rule</NoteLink> writes the Jacobian of $g \circ f$ as the product of the two Jacobians. A long chain of maps gives a
long product. Multiplying it from the input end is <NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">forward-mode automatic
differentiation</NoteLink>. Multiplying it from the output end is backpropagation.

**The Hessian is curvature.** The <NoteLink to="hessian" /> is the symmetric matrix of second partials. It is the
quadratic term of the second-order Taylor expansion. Its eigenvalues classify a stationary point as a minimum (all
positive), a maximum (all negative) or a saddle (mixed signs). Its <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition
number</NoteLink> sets how slowly gradient descent converges.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink>, then the
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. The <NoteLink to="jacobian" /> generalises the gradient to vector outputs,
and the <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">multivariable chain rule</NoteLink> needs both. The
<NoteLink to="hessian" /> comes last because it uses the gradient, the partials and the eigenvalues of a symmetric
matrix.

The sibling topic on matrix calculus applies the same ideas to functions of matrices: its notes on
<NoteLink to="matrix-calculus-identities">matrix calculus identities</NoteLink> and on
<NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax and log-sum-exp</NoteLink> derive the gradients that appear in least
squares, Gaussian likelihoods and classifiers.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of a function of one variable and the basic objects
of <NoteLink to="linear-algebra">linear algebra</NoteLink>: vectors, matrices, determinants and eigenvalues.

<NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation" /> computes gradients and Jacobian products of programs. In optimisation,
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> uses the gradient, <NoteLink to="newtons-method" /> uses the
Hessian, and <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers" /> use gradients of constraints. In probability, the
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink> uses the Jacobian determinant.
