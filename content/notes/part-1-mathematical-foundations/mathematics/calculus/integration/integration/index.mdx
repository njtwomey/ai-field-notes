<Definition>
Integration adds up a function over an interval or a region. The definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the limit of
Riemann sums, the total area of thin rectangles under the graph of $f$. The fundamental theorem of calculus makes
integration the inverse of differentiation, so most integrals are computed from an antiderivative
<Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Probability is built on integrals. A density integrates to one, an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> is
an integral of a value against a density, and a marginal distribution integrates other variables out. Bayesian inference
divides by the evidence, an integral over all parameters, which is rarely available in closed form. The normalising
constants of the Gaussian, gamma, beta, Dirichlet and <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t
distributions</NoteLink> come from two integrals, the Gaussian integral and the gamma function. When no closed form
exists, an integral is approximated by a Gaussian at its peak, by quadrature or by
<NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration" />.

## Main ideas

**Integration undoes differentiation.** The <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of
calculus</NoteLink> has two parts. The running integral $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ has derivative $f(x)$, and
$\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$ for any antiderivative $F$.

**Two rules transform integrals.** The <NoteLink to="substitution-rule">substitution rule</NoteLink> is the chain rule
read backwards and changes the variable of integration; the factor $g'(x)$ it introduces is the one-dimensional version
of the Jacobian factor that transforms densities. <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts" /> is the product rule read
backwards and moves a derivative from one factor to the other. It gives the formula for an expectation as the area above
a <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss>, and Stein's identity for Gaussians.

**Integrals in several dimensions are done one variable at a time.**
<NoteLink to="multiple-integrals" /> over a region of the plane or space are computed as repeated one-dimensional
integrals, in either order, when the integral of the absolute value is finite. The same conditions, due to Fubini and
Tonelli, decide when a sum and an integral can be swapped, as the note on
<NoteLink to="interchanging-sums-and-integrals">interchanging sums and integrals</NoteLink> shows.

**Two integrals normalise most distributions.** The <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral" />
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}$ is evaluated by squaring it and switching to
<NoteLink to="polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</NoteLink>. The <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma
function</NoteLink> $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t}\,dt$ extends the factorial to real arguments.

**A peaked integrand is nearly Gaussian.** The <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation" /> replaces the logarithm of the
integrand by its second-order Taylor expansion at the peak. Applied to a posterior, it
gives a Gaussian centred at the <Gloss name="map-estimate">maximum a posteriori</Gloss> estimate and an approximation to
the evidence that leads to the <Gloss name="bic">Bayesian information criterion</Gloss>.

**Integrating against a function, not a variable.** The <NoteLink to="riemann-stieltjes-integral" />
$\int_a^b f\,dg$ weights $f$ by the increments of $g$. It unifies sums and integrals, and its failure for Brownian
paths is the reason the <NoteLink to="ito-integral" /> exists.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink>. The
<NoteLink to="substitution-rule">substitution rule</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by
parts</NoteLink> follow from it. The <NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">multiple integrals</NoteLink> note and the note
on <NoteLink to="interchanging-sums-and-integrals">interchanging sums and integrals</NoteLink> extend integration to
several variables and to infinite sums. The <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral" /> needs polar coordinates in a double
integral, and the <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma function</NoteLink> needs integration by parts. The
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation" /> uses both and comes last among the applied notes. The
<NoteLink to="riemann-stieltjes-integral" /> is a bridge to <NoteLink to="stochastic-calculus">stochastic
calculus</NoteLink>.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> and the chain rule. The multivariate notes use the
<NoteLink to="jacobian" /> from <NoteLink to="functions-of-several-variables">functions of several variables</NoteLink>.

Integration leads into probability through the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink> for densities and the
<NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution" />. In statistics it leads to <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference" />, where the
evidence is an integral. When an integral has no closed form, <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical
integration</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration" /> approximate it.
