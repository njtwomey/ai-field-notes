<Definition>
Calculus studies how functions change and how quantities accumulate. Differential calculus measures instantaneous rates
of change through <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> and uses them to approximate functions locally by
lines, planes and quadratics. Integral calculus adds up a quantity over an interval or a region. The
<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink> makes the two operations
inverse to each other <Cite id="spivak2008" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Training a model is minimising a loss, and every practical minimiser follows derivatives. The
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the loss with respect to the parameters gives the direction of
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">steepest descent</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> gives the
curvature that sets step sizes and convergence rates, and backpropagation computes the gradient of a whole network by
the chain rule. Integration is the language of probability: densities integrate to
one, expectations and marginals are integrals, and normalising constants such as $\sqrt{2\pi}$ come from evaluating
them. The <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink> and the change of variables formula for
densities sit where the two halves meet <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.

## Main ideas

**The derivative is the best local linear approximation.** For a function of one variable, $f(a + h) \approx f(a) +
f'(a)\,h$. For a function of several variables the derivative becomes a matrix, the
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>, and for a scalar loss a vector, the gradient. Every derivative in machine
learning is an instance of this one idea.

**<NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansions</NoteLink> drive optimisation.**
Keeping the first-order term of a Taylor expansion gives gradient descent. Keeping the second-order term, with the
Hessian, gives <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink>. Bounds on derivatives turn into bounds on how
far a step can safely go.

**Derivatives of compositions multiply.** The chain rule writes the derivative of a composition as a product of
Jacobians. The order in which that product is evaluated separates forward-mode from
<NoteLink to="backpropagation">reverse-mode automatic differentiation</NoteLink>, and reverse mode is backpropagation.

**Integration undoes differentiation and normalises distributions.** Antiderivatives evaluate integrals. Substitution,
<NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink> and changes of order handle the rest. The
<NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma
function</NoteLink> normalise most <NoteLink to="named-distributions">named distributions</NoteLink>.

## How the notes fit together

The topic has five subtopics.

- <NoteLink to="functions-of-one-variable" /> introduces the derivative, the
  <NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">exponential and logarithm</NoteLink>, the
  <NoteLink to="mean-value-theorem">mean value theorem</NoteLink> and Taylor series. Start here.
- <NoteLink to="functions-of-several-variables" /> extends the derivative to <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial
  derivatives</NoteLink>, the gradient, the Jacobian, the chain rule and the Hessian.
- <NoteLink to="integration" /> covers the fundamental theorem, integration techniques,
  <NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">multiple integrals</NoteLink>, the Gaussian and gamma integrals, and the Laplace
  approximation.
- Matrix calculus, starting at <NoteLink to="matrix-calculus-identities">matrix calculus identities</NoteLink>,
  differentiates functions of vectors and matrices by the differential method, and derives the gradients of
  <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax and log-sum-exp</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation" /> computes exact derivatives of programs in forward and reverse mode.

Functions of one variable come first. Functions of several variables and integration can then be read in either order.
Matrix calculus and automatic differentiation need the <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">multivariable chain
rule</NoteLink>.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume algebra and the idea of a function. The multivariable notes use vectors and matrices from
<NoteLink to="linear-algebra">linear algebra</NoteLink>, and the trigonometric derivatives use
<NoteLink to="trigonometry">trigonometry</NoteLink>.

<NoteLink to="differential-equations" /> describe systems by their rates of change.
<NoteLink to="calculus-of-variations" /> optimises over functions. <NoteLink to="numerical-methods" /> computes
derivatives, roots and integrals in floating point. In optimisation, <NoteLink to="first-order-methods">first-order
methods</NoteLink> use the gradient and <NoteLink to="second-order-methods">second-order methods</NoteLink> the Hessian.
In probability, the <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> are applications of integration.
