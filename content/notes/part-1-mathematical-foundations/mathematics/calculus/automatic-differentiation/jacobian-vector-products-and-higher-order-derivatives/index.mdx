<Definition>
For $\fvec : \reals^n \to \reals^m$ with <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>
$\Jmat = \partial\fvec/\partial\xvec \in \reals^{m \times n}$ at $\xvec$:

- the **Jacobian-vector product** (JVP) maps a tangent $\vvec \in \reals^n$ to $\Jmat\vvec \in \reals^m$, and is what
  <NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">forward mode</NoteLink> computes;
- the **vector-Jacobian product** (VJP) maps a cotangent $\uvec \in \reals^m$ to $\uvec^\top\Jmat \in \reals^n$, and is
  what reverse mode computes.

Each costs a small constant multiple of evaluating $\fvec$, and neither forms $\Jmat$ <Cite id="griewank2008" />.
</Definition>

## Why these two are the primitives

A program is a composition $\fvec = \fvec_L \circ \dots \circ \fvec_1$, so by the
<NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">chain rule</NoteLink> its Jacobian is a product,
$\Jmat = \Jmat_L \cdots \Jmat_1$. Multiplying that product by a vector can be done without ever multiplying two matrices
together:

$$
\Jmat\vvec = \Jmat_L\bigl(\cdots(\Jmat_2(\Jmat_1\vvec))\bigr), \qquad
\uvec^\top\Jmat = \bigl(((\uvec^\top\Jmat_L)\Jmat_{L-1})\cdots\bigr)\Jmat_1.
$$

The first is evaluated right to left, in the same order as the program runs: that is forward mode, and it can proceed
alongside the evaluation, as in <NoteLink to="dual-numbers">dual numbers</NoteLink>. The second is evaluated left to
right, from the output back to the input: that is reverse mode, which must first run the program and keep its
intermediate values, as in <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>. Each step is a
<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">matrix-vector product</NoteLink> with one local Jacobian, which a primitive
operation supplies cheaply, usually without forming it. Every <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> a library
offers is built from these two products.

## Jacobians, and choosing a mode

The full Jacobian comes one column or one row at a time:

- $n$ JVPs with the basis vectors $\evec_1, \dots, \evec_n$ give its $n$ columns;
- $m$ VJPs with $\evec_1, \dots, \evec_m$ give its $m$ rows.

So forward mode is cheaper when there are few inputs ($n < m$), and reverse mode when there are few outputs ($m < n$).
A gradient is the extreme case, $m = 1$: one VJP with $u = 1$ gives the whole gradient, whatever $n$ is. This is why
training uses reverse mode.

Libraries batch the passes: JAX's `jacfwd` and `jacrev`, and PyTorch's `torch.func.jacfwd` and `jacrev`, map the JVP or
VJP over all basis vectors at once with `vmap` <Cite id="bradbury2018" />.

## Hessian-vector products

The <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> $\Hmat$ of a scalar $f : \reals^n \to \reals$ has $n^2$ entries, too many
to form for a model with millions of parameters. Many methods only need its product with a vector:
Newton–<Gloss name="cg" /> steps, curvature along a direction, eigenvalue estimates, and the trace estimate
$\trace(\Hmat) \approx \expect[\vvec^\top\Hmat\vvec]$.

**Forward-over-reverse.** The gradient $\gvec(\xvec) = \nabla f(\xvec)$ is itself a function from $\reals^n$ to
$\reals^n$, computed by reverse mode, and its Jacobian is $\Hmat$. So $\Hmat\vvec$ is a JVP of the gradient function:

$$
\Hmat\vvec = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\nabla f(\xvec + t\vvec)\right|_{t=0}.
$$

Running forward mode through the gradient computation gives $\Hmat\vvec$ exactly. Reverse mode costs a constant multiple
of evaluating $f$, and a JVP of that computation costs a constant multiple more, so a Hessian-vector product costs a
constant multiple of one function evaluation, independent of $n$ <Cite id="pearlmutter1994" />. In JAX this is
`jax.jvp(jax.grad(f), (x,), (v,))`. Pearlmutter introduced the technique in 1994 as the $\mathcal{R}$-operator.

**Worked example.** For $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 x_2 + \sin x_2$, the gradient is $(2x_1x_2,\; x_1^2 + \cos x_2)$ and the
Hessian is $\begin{bmatrix} 2x_2 & 2x_1 \\ 2x_1 & -\sin x_2 \end{bmatrix}$. At $\xvec = (1, 2)$ with $\vvec = (1, 0)$,
the gradient along the line $\xvec + t\vvec$ is $(4(1 + t),\; (1 + t)^2 + \cos 2)$, and its derivative at $t = 0$ is
$(4, 2)$. That is the first column of $\Hmat$ at $(1, 2)$, as it should be for $\vvec = \evec_1$, obtained without
writing down $\Hmat$.

## Higher orders

Nesting the two products gives any mixed derivative: a JVP of a JVP gives a second <NoteLink to="gradient">directional
derivative</NoteLink>, and a VJP of a VJP gives reverse-over-reverse Hessian products. Each level multiplies the cost by
a constant, so the cost of a $k$-th derivative along chosen directions grows exponentially in $k$ when computed by
nesting. Taylor-mode differentiation propagates a truncated <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink>
through the program instead, giving the first $k$ derivatives along a direction at a cost that grows only polynomially
in $k$ <Cite id="griewank2008" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Forming what can be multiplied.** Building $\Hmat$ or $\Jmat$ explicitly costs $n$ passes and $O(n^2)$ or
  $O(mn)$ memory. When only products are needed, keep them as products.
- **Choosing the wrong mode.** A Jacobian with many inputs and few outputs computed by `jacfwd` costs $n$ passes where
  `jacrev` would cost $m$.
- **Non-smooth functions.** Where a function is not twice differentiable, such as <Gloss name="relu" /> networks at
  their kinks, a Hessian-vector product returns the derivative of whichever piece was evaluated.
