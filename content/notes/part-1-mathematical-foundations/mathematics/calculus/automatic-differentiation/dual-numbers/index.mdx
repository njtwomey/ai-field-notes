<Definition>
A dual number is an expression $a + b\varepsilon$ with real $a$ and $b$, where $\varepsilon$ is a formal symbol with
$\varepsilon \ne 0$ and $\varepsilon^2 = 0$ <Cite id="clifford1873" />. The real part $a$ carries a value and the dual
part $b$ carries a derivative. Every differentiable function extends to dual numbers by

$$
f(a + b\varepsilon) = f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon,
$$

so evaluating a program on $x + \varepsilon$ returns $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ together.
</Definition>

## Arithmetic

Dual numbers add and multiply like polynomials in $\varepsilon$, with every $\varepsilon^2$ dropped:

$$
(a + b\varepsilon) + (c + d\varepsilon) = (a + c) + (b + d)\varepsilon, \qquad
(a + b\varepsilon)(c + d\varepsilon) = ac + (ad + bc)\varepsilon + bd\,\varepsilon^2 = ac + (ad + bc)\varepsilon.
$$

Division follows from the product: when $c \ne 0$,
$(a + b\varepsilon)/(c + d\varepsilon) = a/c + (bc - ad)/c^2\,\varepsilon$, which can be checked by multiplying back.
The dual parts obey the sum, product and <NoteLink to="derivative">quotient rules</NoteLink> of differentiation. These
rules follow from $\varepsilon^2 = 0$ alone.

## Why $f(a + b\varepsilon) = f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon$

**The Taylor view.** For an analytic $f$, expand about $a$ with step $b\varepsilon$:

$$
f(a + b\varepsilon) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(b\varepsilon)^k
= f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon + \frac{f''(a)}{2}b^2\varepsilon^2 + \dots = f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon.
$$

Every term with $k \ge 2$ contains $\varepsilon^k = 0$, so the series stops after two terms, exactly and not
approximately. Truncating a <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> normally introduces an error. Here the
truncation is free, because the step $\varepsilon$ is nilpotent. The first-order term, which a finite difference
estimates with error, is the whole answer.

For a polynomial the argument needs no series at all. The binomial expansion gives
$(a + b\varepsilon)^n = a^n + n a^{n-1} b\,\varepsilon$, since every later term contains $\varepsilon^2$, and sums of
such terms give $p(a + b\varepsilon) = p(a) + p'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon$.

**Beyond analytic functions.** A function such as $\abs{x}$ or <Gloss name="relu" /> has no Taylor series at every
point, so the formula $f(a + b\varepsilon) := f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon$ is taken as the definition of $f$ on dual
numbers wherever $f$ is differentiable. The definition is consistent with arithmetic because it respects composition:

$$
f\bigl(g(a + b\varepsilon)\bigr) = f\bigl(g(a) + g'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon\bigr) = f(g(a)) + f'(g(a))\,g'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon.
$$

The dual part is $(f \circ g)'(a)\,b$, which is the chain rule. Together with the product rule above, this says the map
$a + b\varepsilon \mapsto$ (value, derivative) turns every program built from primitives into its derivative program.
Each primitive needs only its own rule, such as $\sin(a + b\varepsilon) = \sin a + b\cos a\,\varepsilon$, and the
composition takes care of the rest <Cite id="griewank2008" />.

## Worked example

Take $f(x) = x^2 \sin x$ at $x = 1$ and evaluate on $1 + \varepsilon$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= 1 + \varepsilon, \\
x^2 &= (1 + \varepsilon)(1 + \varepsilon) = 1 + 2\varepsilon, \\
\sin x &= \sin 1 + \cos 1\,\varepsilon, \\
x^2 \sin x &= (1 + 2\varepsilon)(\sin 1 + \cos 1\,\varepsilon) = \sin 1 + (2\sin 1 + \cos 1)\,\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

The real part is $f(1) = \sin 1 \approx 0.8415$. The dual part is $2\sin 1 + \cos 1 \approx 2.2232$, which matches the
product rule, $f'(x) = 2x\sin x + x^2\cos x$ at $x = 1$.

## Several variables

For $f : \reals^n \to \reals$, seed the inputs with a direction $\vvec$, $\xvec + \vvec\,\varepsilon$. The dual part of
the output is the <NoteLink to="gradient">directional derivative</NoteLink> $\nabla f(\xvec)^\top\vvec$, so a full
gradient needs $n$ passes, one per basis vector. The same holds for vector outputs: one pass gives one
<Gloss name="jvp">Jacobian-vector product</Gloss>. This is <NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">forward-mode automatic
differentiation</NoteLink>.

An alternative uses one symbol per input, $\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n$ with $\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j = 0$
for all $i, j$. Seeding $x_i + \varepsilon_i$ gives $f(\xvec) + \sum_i \partial_i f(\xvec)\,\varepsilon_i$: the whole
gradient in one pass, carried as a vector of $n$ dual parts. The arithmetic per operation is then $n$ times larger, so
the total cost is the same.

## Hyper-dual numbers

Second derivatives need a second nilpotent symbol. A hyper-dual number is
$a + b\varepsilon_1 + c\varepsilon_2 + d\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2$, with $\varepsilon_1^2 = \varepsilon_2^2 = 0$ but
$\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 \ne 0$ <Cite id="fike2011" />. The Taylor argument now keeps the mixed term:

$$
f(a + \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2) = f(a) + f'(a)\,\varepsilon_1 + f'(a)\,\varepsilon_2 + f''(a)\,\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2,
$$

because $(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)^2 = 2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2$, the $k = 2$ term contributes
$\tfrac12 f''(a) \cdot 2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2$, and every higher power vanishes. For $x^2 \sin x$ at $x = 1$ the
$\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2$ part is $2\sin 1 + 4\cos 1 - \sin 1 \approx 3.0027$, the exact second derivative.

## Complex-step differentiation

A close cousin uses the imaginary unit instead of $\varepsilon$. For real-analytic $f$ and small $h$,

$$
f(x + ih) = f(x) + ih f'(x) - \frac{h^2}{2}f''(x) - \frac{ih^3}{6}f'''(x) + \dots, \qquad
f'(x) \approx \frac{\operatorname{Im} f(x + ih)}{h},
$$

with error $O(h^2)$ <Cite id="martins2003" />. Unlike a finite difference, the formula subtracts nothing, so there is no
cancellation: $h = 10^{-20}$ is fine and the result is exact to machine precision. For $x^2 \sin x$ at 1, a forward
difference with $h = 10^{-8}$ is off by about $10^{-8}$, and with $h = 10^{-12}$ by $2 \times 10^{-4}$ as
<NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding error</NoteLink> takes over; the complex step with $h = 10^{-20}$
matches $2.22324427548\ldots$ to every digit.

Because $i^2 = -1$ rather than 0, the complex step is an approximation, relies on complex arithmetic being available
and analytic, and breaks on functions such as $\abs{x}$ that are not complex-analytic. Dual numbers give the same
derivative exactly, for any differentiable primitive.

## History

Clifford introduced dual numbers in 1873, as part of his biquaternions for describing rigid motions
<Cite id="clifford1873" />. Their use for derivatives is the idea behind forward-mode automatic differentiation; see
the <NoteLink to="history-of-automatic-differentiation">history of automatic differentiation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Only first derivatives.** Plain dual numbers give first derivatives. Higher orders need hyper-dual numbers,
  nested duals, or truncated Taylor arithmetic.
- **Branches ignore the dual part.** `if x > 0` compares real parts only, so at a kink the result is the derivative of
  whichever branch was taken, not a subgradient.
- **Cost grows with the number of inputs.** A gradient of $n$ inputs costs $n$ forward passes, or one pass with $n$
  dual parts. For many inputs and one output, reverse mode (<NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>)
  is cheaper.
