<Definition>
An **iterative linear solver** computes a sequence $\xvec_0, \xvec_1, \dots$ that converges to the solution $\xvec^*$
of $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$, where $\Amat$ is $n \times n$ and non-singular. It accesses $\Amat$ only through products
$\Amat\vvec$ (and, for stationary methods, its diagonal and triangular parts). A **stationary method** splits
$\Amat = \Mmat - \Nmat$ with $\Mmat$ easy to invert and iterates $\Mmat\xvec_{k+1} = \Nmat\xvec_k + \bvec$. A
**Krylov method** chooses $\xvec_k$ from $\xvec_0 + \Kcal_k$, where
$\Kcal_k = \Span\set{\rvec_0, \Amat\rvec_0, \dots, \Amat^{k-1}\rvec_0}$ and $\rvec_0 = \bvec - \Amat\xvec_0$
<Cite id="saad2003" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Direct methods such as the <NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">LU</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky</NoteLink> decomposition cost $O(n^3)$ operations for a dense matrix. For
a sparse matrix, elimination fills in zeros, so the factors can be far denser than $\Amat$. A discretised
<Gloss name="pde">partial differential equation</Gloss> or a graph Laplacian can have millions of unknowns and a handful
of non-zeros per row. An iterative method costs one sparse <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">matrix-vector
product</NoteLink> per step, $O(\text{nnz}(\Amat))$, and stores only a few vectors. It is worthwhile when it converges
in far fewer than $n$ steps.

The speed of every method depends on the spectrum of $\Amat$. The number of steps grows with the
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> $\kappa$: linearly for the simple stationary methods and as
$\sqrt\kappa$ for conjugate gradient.

## Stationary methods: Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel

Write $\Amat = \Dmat + \Lmat + \Umat$, with $\Dmat$ the diagonal and $\Lmat$ and $\Umat$ the strictly lower and upper
triangular parts.

- **Jacobi** takes $\Mmat = \Dmat$. Each unknown is updated from the previous iterate:
  $x_i^{(k+1)} = \paren{b_i - \sum_{j \ne i}A_{ij}x_j^{(k)}}/A_{ii}$.
- **Gauss–Seidel** takes $\Mmat = \Dmat + \Lmat$. It uses each new value as soon as it is computed:
  $x_i^{(k+1)} = \paren{b_i - \sum_{j < i}A_{ij}x_j^{(k+1)} - \sum_{j > i}A_{ij}x_j^{(k)}}/A_{ii}$.

**Convergence.** The solution satisfies $\Mmat\xvec^* = \Nmat\xvec^* + \bvec$. Subtracting it from the iteration gives
the error recursion

$$
\evec_{k+1} = \Gmat\evec_k, \qquad \Gmat = \Mmat^{-1}\Nmat, \qquad \evec_k = \Gmat^k\evec_0.
$$

$\Gmat^k \to \zeros$ if and only if the spectral radius $\rho(\Gmat)$, the largest eigenvalue modulus, is below 1. The
error then shrinks by about $\rho(\Gmat)$ per step, so reducing it by a factor $\epsilon$ takes about
$\log\epsilon/\log\rho(\Gmat)$ steps.

- If $\Amat$ is strictly diagonally dominant, $\abs{A_{ii}} > \sum_{j \ne i}\abs{A_{ij}}$ for every row, then Jacobi
  converges. The Jacobi matrix $-\Dmat^{-1}(\Lmat + \Umat)$ has row sums of absolute values below 1, so its
  $\infty$-norm, and hence its spectral radius, is below 1.
- If $\Amat$ is symmetric positive definite, Gauss–Seidel converges <Cite id="golub2013" />.

**Successive over-relaxation** (SOR) extrapolates each Gauss–Seidel update by a factor $\omega \in (1, 2)$. With the
best $\omega$, it can reduce the number of steps from $O(\kappa)$ to $O(\sqrt\kappa)$ on model problems.

## Conjugate gradient

Let $\Amat$ be symmetric positive definite. Solving $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ is then equivalent to minimising the convex
quadratic

$$
\phi(\xvec) = \tfrac12\xvec^\top\Amat\xvec - \bvec^\top\xvec,
$$

whose <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is $\Amat\xvec - \bvec = -\rvec$, the negative residual. The error is
measured in the **energy norm** $\norm{\evec}_\Amat = \sqrt{\evec^\top\Amat\evec}$, and $\phi(\xvec) - \phi(\xvec^*) = \frac12\norm{\xvec -
\xvec^*}_\Amat^2$.

<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> with <NoteLink to="line-search">exact line search</NoteLink>
on $\phi$ (steepest descent) zigzags across narrow valleys. Its error contracts by $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step
in the energy norm. Conjugate gradient removes the zigzag by never undoing progress made along earlier directions.

**Conjugate directions.** Vectors $\pvec_0, \dots, \pvec_{n-1}$ are **$\Amat$-conjugate** if $\pvec_i^\top\Amat\pvec_j
= 0$ for $i \ne j$. They are linearly independent, so the initial error expands as $\xvec^* - \xvec_0 =
\sum_i\alpha_i\pvec_i$. Multiplying by $\pvec_j^\top\Amat$ isolates one coefficient:
$\alpha_j = \pvec_j^\top\Amat(\xvec^* - \xvec_0)/\pvec_j^\top\Amat\pvec_j = \pvec_j^\top\rvec_0/\pvec_j^\top\Amat
\pvec_j$. Adding the terms one at a time, $\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k + \alpha_k\pvec_k$, reaches $\xvec^*$ after $n$
steps. Each step is also an exact line search on $\phi$ along $\pvec_k$, because $\pvec_k^\top\rvec_k =
\pvec_k^\top\rvec_0$ (the earlier steps change $\rvec$ only along the $\Amat\pvec_j$, which are orthogonal to
$\pvec_k$).

**The algorithm.** Conjugate gradient builds the directions on the fly from the residuals <Cite id="hestenes1952" />.
From $\xvec_0$, set $\rvec_0 = \bvec - \Amat\xvec_0$ and $\pvec_0 = \rvec_0$, then repeat:

$$
\alpha_k = \frac{\rvec_k^\top\rvec_k}{\pvec_k^\top\Amat\pvec_k}, \quad
\xvec_{k+1} = \xvec_k + \alpha_k\pvec_k, \quad
\rvec_{k+1} = \rvec_k - \alpha_k\Amat\pvec_k, \quad
\beta_k = \frac{\rvec_{k+1}^\top\rvec_{k+1}}{\rvec_k^\top\rvec_k}, \quad
\pvec_{k+1} = \rvec_{k+1} + \beta_k\pvec_k.
$$

Each step costs one product $\Amat\pvec_k$, two <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink> and three vector
updates. The new direction is the new residual made $\Amat$-conjugate to the previous direction only. It is then
automatically conjugate to all earlier ones.

<Derivation title="Why one correction makes the new direction conjugate to all earlier ones">
By induction, $\Span\set{\pvec_0, \dots, \pvec_k} = \Span\set{\rvec_0, \dots, \rvec_k} = \Kcal_{k+1}$. Because each
step is an exact line search along conjugate directions, $\xvec_{k+1}$ minimises $\phi$ over $\xvec_0 + \Kcal_{k+1}$.
At that minimiser the gradient is orthogonal to the subspace: $\rvec_{k+1} \perp \Kcal_{k+1}$. In particular the
residuals are mutually orthogonal.

For $j < k$, $\Amat\pvec_j$ lies in $\Kcal_{j+2} \subseteq \Kcal_{k+1}$, so $\rvec_{k+1}^\top\Amat\pvec_j = 0$. The
residual $\rvec_{k+1}$ is therefore already conjugate to $\pvec_0, \dots, \pvec_{k-1}$, and only its component along
$\pvec_k$ must be removed. The coefficient that removes it is $\beta_k = -\rvec_{k+1}^\top\Amat\pvec_k/\pvec_k^\top
\Amat\pvec_k$. Substituting $\Amat\pvec_k = (\rvec_k - \rvec_{k+1})/\alpha_k$ and using the orthogonality of the
residuals gives $\beta_k = \rvec_{k+1}^\top\rvec_{k+1}/(\alpha_k\pvec_k^\top\Amat\pvec_k) = \rvec_{k+1}^\top
\rvec_{k+1}/\rvec_k^\top\rvec_k$. The same orthogonality gives $\pvec_k^\top\rvec_k = \rvec_k^\top\rvec_k$, the form of
$\alpha_k$ above.
</Derivation>

## The convergence bound

Every $\xvec \in \xvec_0 + \Kcal_k$ has error $\xvec - \xvec^* = q(\Amat)(\xvec_0 - \xvec^*)$ for a polynomial $q$ of
degree at most $k$ with $q(0) = 1$. Conjugate gradient picks the best such $\xvec$ in the energy norm. Expand the
initial error in the orthonormal eigenvectors of $\Amat$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. Then $\norm{q(\Amat)\evec_0}_\Amat^2 =
\sum_i q(\lambda_i)^2\lambda_ic_i^2$, where $c_i$ are the coefficients, and

$$
\frac{\norm{\evec_k}_\Amat}{\norm{\evec_0}_\Amat} \le \min_{q(0) = 1,\ \deg q \le k}\ \max_i\abs{q(\lambda_i)}.
$$

Two consequences follow. If $\Amat$ has only $d$ distinct eigenvalues, the polynomial $\prod_{j=1}^d(1 -
\lambda/\lambda_j)$ vanishes on all of them, so conjugate gradient terminates in $d$ steps. And if the eigenvalues lie
in $[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$ with $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$, a shifted Chebyshev polynomial
gives

$$
\frac{\norm{\evec_k}_\Amat}{\norm{\evec_0}_\Amat} \le 2\paren{\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}}^k.
$$

<Derivation title="The Chebyshev bound">
The Chebyshev polynomial $T_k$ satisfies $\abs{T_k(z)} \le 1$ on $[-1, 1]$ and $T_k(z) = \frac12\brack{(z +
\sqrt{z^2 - 1})^k + (z - \sqrt{z^2 - 1})^k} \ge \frac12(z + \sqrt{z^2 - 1})^k$ for $z > 1$. Map
$[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$ onto $[-1, 1]$ by $z(\lambda) = (\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min} -
2\lambda)/(\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})$, and take $q(\lambda) = T_k(z(\lambda))/T_k(z(0))$, so that $q(0) = 1$.
On the eigenvalues $\abs{q} \le 1/T_k(z_0)$ with $z_0 = z(0) = (\kappa + 1)/(\kappa - 1)$. Then

$$
z_0 + \sqrt{z_0^2 - 1} = \frac{\kappa + 1 + 2\sqrt\kappa}{\kappa - 1} = \frac{(\sqrt\kappa + 1)^2}{(\sqrt\kappa -
1)(\sqrt\kappa + 1)} = \frac{\sqrt\kappa + 1}{\sqrt\kappa - 1},
$$

so $1/T_k(z_0) \le 2\paren{(\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)}^k$ <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
</Derivation>

For large $\kappa$ the factor per step is about $1 - 2/\sqrt\kappa$, so reducing the error by $\epsilon$ takes about
$\frac12\sqrt\kappa\log(2/\epsilon)$ steps. Steepest descent, Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel need $O(\kappa)$ steps on the
same problems. When $\kappa = 10^4$, that is the difference between hundreds and tens of thousands of steps.

## Worked example: a $2 \times 2$ system

Solve

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\xvec = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\xvec^* = \paren{\tfrac{1}{11}, \tfrac{7}{11}} = (0.0909, 0.6364),
$$

from $\xvec_0 = \zeros$.

- **Jacobi:** $x_1 \leftarrow (1 - x_2)/4$ and $x_2 \leftarrow (2 - x_1)/3$, both from the old values. The iterates are
  $(0.250, 0.667)$, $(0.083, 0.583)$, $(0.104, 0.639)$, $(0.090, 0.632)$. The iteration matrix has eigenvalues
  $\pm 1/\sqrt{12} = \pm 0.289$, so each step shrinks the error by about $0.29$.
- **Gauss–Seidel:** the second update uses the new $x_1$. The iterates are $(0.250, 0.583)$, $(0.104, 0.632)$,
  $(0.092, 0.636)$. Its spectral radius is $1/12 = 0.083$, the square of Jacobi's.
- **Conjugate gradient:** $\rvec_0 = \pvec_0 = (1, 2)$ and $\Amat\pvec_0 = (6, 7)$, so $\alpha_0 = 5/20 = 0.25$,
  $\xvec_1 = (0.25, 0.5)$ and $\rvec_1 = (-0.5, 0.25)$. Then $\beta_0 = 0.3125/5 = 0.0625$ and $\pvec_1 = (-0.4375,
  0.375)$. The second step, with $\alpha_1 = 0.364$, lands exactly on $\xvec^* = (0.0909, 0.6364)$: two steps for two
  unknowns.

## Worked example: the Poisson equation

Discretise $-\nabla^2u = f$ on the unit square with a $30 \times 30$ grid of unknowns and the five-point stencil. The
matrix has $n = 900$ rows, at most five non-zeros per row, and $\kappa = 389$. To reduce the energy-norm error by
$10^6$ from a random right-hand side:

| Method              | Predicted steps                               | Observed |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------- |
| Jacobi              | $\log 10^{-6}/\log\cos(\pi/31) = 2\,686$      | $2\,469$ |
| Gauss–Seidel        | half of Jacobi, $1\,343$                      |          |
| Conjugate gradient  | bound $2(\dots)^k \le 10^{-6}$ at $k = 143$   | $80$     |

The bound for conjugate gradient is pessimistic, because it assumes the worst spread of eigenvalues. The number of
steps for Jacobi grows like $m^2$ for an $m \times m$ grid, and for conjugate gradient like $m$.

## Preconditioning

The number of steps depends on $\kappa$, and preconditioning reduces it. A **preconditioner** is a
symmetric positive definite $\Mmat \approx \Amat$ whose systems $\Mmat\zvec = \rvec$ are cheap to solve. Preconditioned
conjugate gradient runs the same iteration on the transformed system and converges at the rate set by
$\kappa(\Mmat^{-1}\Amat)$. Common choices are the diagonal of $\Amat$ (Jacobi preconditioning), incomplete Cholesky
factorisations that drop fill-in, and multigrid cycles. A good preconditioner can make the number of steps nearly
independent of the problem size <Cite id="saad2003" />.

## Beyond symmetric positive definite systems

- **GMRES** minimises the residual over the Krylov subspace for any non-singular $\Amat$. It must store every
  direction, so it is restarted after a fixed number of steps.
- **MINRES** handles symmetric indefinite systems with short recurrences.
- **BiCGSTAB** handles non-symmetric systems with short recurrences, at the price of irregular convergence.

## In machine learning

- **Gaussian processes.** Exact <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian-process</NoteLink> inference needs
  $(\Kmat + \sigma^2\eye)^{-1}\yvec$. Conjugate gradient needs only kernel matrix-vector products, which run in batches
  on <Gloss name="gpu" plural /> and scale to far larger $n$ than a Cholesky factorisation.
- **Newton-CG.** Second-order optimisers solve the Newton system $\Hmat\Delta\wvec = -\gvec$ approximately with a few
  conjugate gradient steps, using <NoteLink to="jacobian-vector-products-and-higher-order-derivatives">Hessian-vector
  products</NoteLink> from automatic differentiation instead of forming $\Hmat$.
- **Least squares.** Conjugate gradient on the normal equations, in the numerically safer forms CGLS and LSQR, solves
  large sparse regression problems.

## Pitfalls

- **Conjugate gradient needs symmetric positive definite $\Amat$.** On an indefinite or non-symmetric matrix it can
  break down or diverge.
- **Loss of orthogonality.** In <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">floating-point arithmetic</NoteLink> the
  residuals gradually lose orthogonality. Convergence is delayed rather than destroyed, but finite termination in $n$
  steps does not hold in practice.
- **Stopping on the residual.** A small residual $\norm{\rvec_k}$ bounds the error only up to a factor
  $\kappa$: $\norm{\evec_k}/\norm{\xvec^*} \le \kappa\norm{\rvec_k}/\norm{\bvec}$.
