<Definition>
Numerical methods compute mathematical quantities approximately, with finite-precision arithmetic and a finite number of
steps. Numerical analysis studies how large the resulting errors are, where they come from and how algorithms keep them
small. Its two sources of error are rounding, from storing numbers in finitely many bits, and truncation, from stopping
an infinite process early <Cite id="suli2003" /> <Cite id="higham2002" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every model is trained and evaluated in floating point. Softmax and log-likelihoods overflow unless computed in
<NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">log space</NoteLink>. <NoteLink to="mixed-precision-training">Mixed-precision
training</NoteLink> stores weights in 16 bits and has to manage the rounding that follows. Solving the linear systems of
<Gloss name="gp">Gaussian processes</Gloss> and least squares requires a stable factorisation and an awareness of the
condition number. Quantile functions are inverted by root finding. <NoteLink to="expectation">Expectations</NoteLink>
over one or two dimensions are computed by quadrature, and large sparse systems are solved by conjugate gradient.
<NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> checking compares derivatives with <Gloss name="finite-difference">finite
differences</Gloss> whose step size balances rounding against truncation.

## Main ideas

**Every operation rounds.** <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic" /> stores a sign, a $p$-bit significand and an
exponent. Each stored value and each operation is exact up to a relative error at most the unit roundoff $u = 2^{-p}$,
about $10^{-16}$ in double precision. Subtracting nearly equal numbers magnifies errors made earlier.

**Problems are conditioned, algorithms are stable.** In the note on
<NoteLink to="numerical-stability-and-conditioning">numerical stability and conditioning</NoteLink>, the condition
number $\kappa$ of a problem bounds how much a relative change in its input moves its output. A backward-stable
algorithm returns the exact answer to a slightly perturbed input. The forward error is then at most about $\kappa$ times
the backward error.

**Equations are solved by iteration.** <NoteLink to="root-finding" /> solves $f(x) = 0$. Bisection always converges,
linearly. Newton's method converges quadratically near a simple root but can diverge. The secant method needs no
derivative and converges with order about 1.618.

**Integrals become weighted sums.** <NoteLink to="numerical-integration" /> approximates $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ by
$\sum_i w_i f(x_i)$. The composite trapezoid and Simpson rules have errors $O(h^2)$ and $O(h^4)$ in the spacing $h$.
Gaussian quadrature with $n$ nodes is exact for polynomials of degree $2n - 1$.

**Large systems are solved by matrix-vector products.** <NoteLink to="iterative-linear-solvers" /> approach the
solution of $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ without factorising $\Amat$. Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel iterations converge when the
spectral radius of their iteration matrix is below 1. Conjugate gradient, for symmetric positive definite $\Amat$,
converges at a rate set by $\sqrt{\kappa}$.
