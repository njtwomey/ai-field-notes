<Definition>
Let $f$ be a problem that maps data $x$ to a solution $f(x)$, and let $\hat f$ be an algorithm that computes it in
floating-point arithmetic.

- The **relative condition number** of $f$ at $x$ is
  $\kappa(x) = \lim_{\delta \to 0}\sup_{\norm{\Delta x} \le \delta\norm x}\dfrac{\norm{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}/\norm{f(x)}}{\norm{\Delta x}/\norm x}$.
- The **forward error** of $\hat f$ is $\norm{\hat f(x) - f(x)}/\norm{f(x)}$.
- The **backward error** of $\hat f$ is the smallest $\norm{\Delta x}/\norm x$ such that $\hat f(x) = f(x + \Delta x)$.

An algorithm is **backward stable** if its backward error is $O(u)$ for every input, where $u$ is the unit roundoff
<Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two separate things can go wrong in a numerical computation. The problem may be sensitive: tiny changes in the data
change the answer a lot. No algorithm can fix that, because the data are already uncertain, at least by the rounding
error of storing them. Or the algorithm may be poor: it introduces errors much larger than the data's own uncertainty.

Conditioning measures the first, stability the second. A
backward-stable algorithm returns the exact answer to a question that differs from the one asked by a relative
amount of order $u$. If the data are only known to that accuracy anyway, the algorithm has done as well as possible.

## Condition numbers

For a differentiable scalar function, a first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> gives $f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) \approx
f'(x)\Delta x$. Dividing by $f(x)$ and by $\Delta x/x$,

$$
\kappa(x) = \abs{\frac{xf'(x)}{f(x)}}.
$$

- **Multiplication** $f(x) = cx$: $\kappa = 1$. Always well-conditioned.
- **Square root**: $\kappa = 1/2$.
- **Subtraction** $f(a, b) = a - b$: $\kappa = (\abs a + \abs b)/\abs{a - b}$, unbounded when $a \approx b$. This is
  the source of catastrophic cancellation in
  <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">floating-point arithmetic</NoteLink>.
- **Logarithm**: $\kappa = 1/\abs{\log x}$, which is $10^8$ at $x = 1 + 10^{-8}$. Near $x = 1$ the input
  $1 + \epsilon$ should be passed to `log1p` as $\epsilon$.
- **Solving $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$**: $\kappa(\Amat) = \norm{\Amat}\norm{\Amat^{-1}}$, the
  <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number of the matrix</NoteLink>. In the 2-norm it is the ratio of the
  largest to the smallest singular value.

A condition number of $10^k$ means that about $k$ significant digits can be lost in the answer.

## Forward error from backward error

Suppose an algorithm is backward stable: $\hat f(x) = f(x + \Delta x)$ with $\norm{\Delta x}/\norm x \le cu$ for a
modest constant $c$. By the definition of the condition number, to first order,

$$
\frac{\norm{\hat f(x) - f(x)}}{\norm{f(x)}} = \frac{\norm{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}}{\norm{f(x)}} \lesssim
\kappa(x)\,\frac{\norm{\Delta x}}{\norm x} \le c\,\kappa(x)\,u.
$$

This is the central rule of thumb of numerical analysis:

$$
\text{forward error} \lesssim \text{condition number} \times \text{backward error}.
$$

It splits the analysis in two. Perturbation theory of the problem gives $\kappa$. Rounding-error analysis of the
algorithm gives the backward error. Neither needs the other <Cite id="higham2002" />.

## Stable and unstable algorithms

Backward-stable algorithms for common problems:

- **Summation and dot products.** Recursive summation computes the exact sum of $x_i(1 + \delta_i)$ with
  $\abs{\delta_i} \le (n - 1)u$.
- **Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting** (the <NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">LU
  decomposition</NoteLink>) is backward stable in practice. Growth of the entries during elimination can in principle be
  $2^{n-1}$, but it is almost never large <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
- **Householder <NoteLink to="qr-decomposition">QR</NoteLink>** and
  **<NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky</NoteLink>** factorisations are backward stable.

An unstable algorithm can ruin a well-conditioned problem. Computing $e^{-20}$ from its Taylor series $\sum_k(-20)^k/k!$
is an example. The problem is well-conditioned, with $\kappa = 20$. But the series has terms as large as
$4.3 \times 10^7$ that alternate in sign and cancel to leave $2 \times 10^{-9}$. Each term carries a rounding error of
about $u \times 4.3 \times 10^7 \approx 5 \times 10^{-9}$, larger than the answer. In double precision the series gives
$5.0 \times 10^{-9}$ instead of $2.06 \times 10^{-9}$. Computing $1/e^{20}$, a sum of positive terms followed by a
division, is accurate to full precision.

## Worked example: the Hilbert matrix

The $n \times n$ Hilbert matrix has entries $H_{ij} = 1/(i + j - 1)$. It is symmetric positive definite and badly
conditioned. Set $\xvec = \ones$, compute $\bvec = \Hmat\xvec$, and solve $\Hmat\hat\xvec = \bvec$ with LU and partial
pivoting in double precision:

| $n$ | $\kappa_2(\Hmat)$     | $\max_i\abs{\hat x_i - 1}$ | relative residual        |
| --- | --------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| 5   | $4.8 \times 10^{5}$   | $1.7 \times 10^{-11}$      | $0$                      |
| 10  | $1.6 \times 10^{13}$  | $8.5 \times 10^{-4}$       | $9.2 \times 10^{-17}$    |
| 12  | $1.7 \times 10^{16}$  | $0.14$                     | $5.8 \times 10^{-17}$    |

The relative residual $\norm{\Hmat\hat\xvec - \bvec}/(\norm{\Hmat}\norm{\hat\xvec})$ measures the backward error. It
stays at the level of $u = 1.1 \times 10^{-16}$ for every $n$: the solver is backward stable. The forward error grows
with $\kappa$. At $n = 10$, $\kappa u \approx 2 \times 10^{-3}$, and the observed error is $8.5 \times 10^{-4}$. At
$n = 12$, $\kappa u \approx 2$, and no digit of the solution can be trusted. The error comes from the conditioning of
the problem, not from the algorithm.

## Worked example: least squares

Fit a degree-11 polynomial to 50 points on $[0, 1]$ in the monomial basis. The design matrix $\Amat$ ($50 \times 12$)
has $\kappa(\Amat) = 1.2 \times 10^8$. Two algorithms solve the same problem:

- **Normal equations**, $\Amat^\top\Amat\wvec = \Amat^\top\yvec$. Forming $\Amat^\top\Amat$ squares the condition
  number, to $1.3 \times 10^{16}$. With exact coefficients equal to 1, the computed coefficients are off by up to
  $0.22$.
- **QR**, $\Amat = \Qmat\Rmat$ and $\Rmat\wvec = \Qmat^\top\yvec$. It works with $\Amat$ directly, and the coefficients
  are off by $1.7 \times 10^{-9}$, consistent with $\kappa(\Amat)u$.

The normal equations are not unstable in the backward sense for the matrix $\Amat^\top\Amat$. They are an unstable
algorithm for the least-squares problem, because they replace it with a problem whose condition number is the square
of the original.

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="feature-scaling">Feature scaling</NoteLink>.** Features on very different scales make
  $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ ill-conditioned. Standardising them improves the conditioning of
  <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> and the convergence of
  <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> methods.
- **Regularisation.** <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge regression</NoteLink> solves
  $(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\eye)\wvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec$. Adding $\lambda$ raises the smallest eigenvalue, so the
  condition number falls from $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ to
  $(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda)/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda)$.
- **Jitter.** <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian-process</Gloss> code adds a small multiple of the identity to a kernel matrix
  before its Cholesky factorisation. The kernel matrix is positive definite in exact arithmetic but can have eigenvalues
  near $u$ times its norm, which rounding can push negative.

## Pitfalls

- **Small residual is not small error.** A backward-stable solver always gives a small residual. The error in
  $\xvec$ can still be large when $\kappa$ is large.
- **Inverting to solve.** Computing $\Amat^{-1}$ and then $\Amat^{-1}\bvec$ costs more and is less accurate than
  solving with a factorisation.
- **Squaring the condition number.** Forming $\Amat^\top\Amat$, as the normal equations do, loses twice as many
  digits as working with $\Amat$.
