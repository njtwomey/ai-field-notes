<Definition>
Let $\vvec(\xvec, t)$ be a velocity field on $\reals^d$. The **transport equation** for a quantity $u(\xvec, t)$ carried
by the flow is

$$
\partial_tu + \vvec\cdot\nabla u = 0,
$$

and the **continuity equation** for a density $p(\xvec, t)$ whose mass moves with the flow is

$$
\partial_tp + \nabla\cdot\paren{p\,\vvec} = 0.
$$

Both are solved along **characteristics**, the paths $\dot{\xvec} = \vvec(\xvec, t)$ <Cite id="evans2010" />. Along a
characteristic $u$ is constant, while $\log p$ changes at rate $-\nabla\cdot\vvec$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A river carries a patch of dye downstream. Two different things can be said about it.

- The **colour** at each drop of water does not change as the drop moves: a red drop stays red. Colour is a label
  carried by each drop. That is transport.
- The **amount** of dye per litre can change. Where the river narrows and speeds up, the dye stretches out along the
  stream and its concentration falls; where the river slows, it bunches up. The total amount of dye stays fixed. That
  is continuity.

A probability density is an amount, not a label. If every sample of a distribution moves with the same
<Gloss name="ode" />, the density obeys the continuity equation. This is the equation behind continuous normalising
flows, the probability flow ODE of <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss> and flow matching.

## The advection equation

The simplest case has a constant velocity $c$ in one dimension:

$$
\partial_tu + c\,\partial_xu = 0, \qquad u(x, 0) = u_0(x).
$$

The solution is $u(x, t) = u_0(x - ct)$: the initial profile slides to the right at speed $c$ without changing shape.
Checking, $\partial_tu = -c\,u_0'$ and $\partial_xu = u_0'$, so the equation holds.

The reason is the **method of characteristics**. Follow a point moving with the flow, $x(t) = x_0 + ct$. By the chain
rule, the <NoteLink to="derivative">rate of change</NoteLink> of $u$ seen by that point is

$$
\frac{d}{dt}u\paren{x(t), t} = \partial_tu + \dot x\,\partial_xu = \partial_tu + c\,\partial_xu = 0.
$$

So $u$ is constant along each line $x = x_0 + ct$, and $u(x, t) = u_0(x_0) = u_0(x - ct)$. A <Gloss name="pde" /> in two
variables has become an ODE along each characteristic.

**Worked example.** With $u_0(x) = e^{-x^2}$ and $c = 2$, $u(3, 1) = u_0(3 - 2) = e^{-1} = 0.368$.

With a variable velocity $v(x)$, the characteristics are solutions of $\dot x = v(x)$. For $v(x) = x$ they are
$x = x_0e^t$, so the transport equation $\partial_tu + x\,\partial_xu = 0$ has solution $u(x, t) = u_0(xe^{-t})$: the
profile is stretched horizontally by $e^t$ but keeps its height.

## The continuity equation from a small box

Let $p(x, t)$ be a density of particles on a line, each moving with velocity $v(x, t)$. The **flux** $J = p\,v$ is the
rate at which mass crosses a point, left to right. The mass in an interval $[a, b]$ changes only by what crosses its
ends:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_a^b p\,dx = J(a, t) - J(b, t) = -\int_a^b \partial_xJ\,dx.
$$

This holds for every interval, so the integrands must agree:

$$
\partial_tp + \partial_x\paren{p\,v} = 0.
$$

In $d$ dimensions the same argument uses a small box and the divergence theorem: mass inside changes by the net flux
through the surface, which is $-\int\nabla\cdot(p\,\vvec)\,dV$. The equation holds for any conserved quantity carried by
a flow: mass of fluid, charge, probability.

Expanding the divergence separates two effects:

$$
\partial_tp + \vvec\cdot\nabla p = -p\,\nabla\cdot\vvec.
$$

The left side is the rate of change of $p$ seen by a particle moving with the flow. The right side says that this rate
is set by the divergence: where the flow spreads ($\nabla\cdot\vvec > 0$) the density falls. Along a characteristic,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\log p\paren{\xvec(t), t} = -\nabla\cdot\vvec\paren{\xvec(t), t}.
$$

This is the instantaneous change of variables used by <NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">continuous
normalising flows</NoteLink>, and Liouville's formula from <NoteLink to="vector-fields-and-flows">vector fields and
flows</NoteLink> seen from a fixed point in space. When $\nabla\cdot\vvec = 0$ the continuity and transport equations
coincide: density is then constant along each characteristic.

**Worked example (stretching).** Take $v(x) = x$ and $p_0 = \Gauss(0, 1)$. The characteristics are $x = x_0e^t$ and
$\partial_xv = 1$, so $\log p$ falls by $t$ along each. Hence

$$
p(x, t) = p_0\paren{xe^{-t}}\,e^{-t},
$$

which is the density of $\Gauss(0, e^{2t})$. Compare the transport solution $u_0(xe^{-t})$ above: the continuity
solution has the extra factor $e^{-t}$ that keeps the total mass at 1.

**Worked example (contraction).** With $v(x) = -x$ and $p_0 = \Gauss(2, 1)$, each particle follows $x = x_0e^{-t}$, so
$X_t = e^{-t}X_0$ is Gaussian with mean $2e^{-t}$ and variance $e^{-2t}$. At $t = 1$, $p_1 = \Gauss(0.736, 0.135)$.
The density along each path rises by the factor $e^{t}$, because $\partial_xv = -1$.

## Eulerian and Lagrangian views

The same motion has two descriptions.

- **Eulerian:** stand at a fixed point $\xvec$ and watch $p(\xvec, t)$ change. That is the PDE.
- **Lagrangian:** ride with a particle and watch its position and the density around it. That is the ODE
  $\dot{\xvec} = \vvec$ together with $\frac{d}{dt}\log p = -\nabla\cdot\vvec$.

Characteristics convert one into the other. Sampling uses the Lagrangian view: draw a starting point and integrate the
ODE. Likelihood evaluation uses both: integrate the ODE and accumulate the divergence.

When $\vvec$ is Lipschitz, characteristics never cross, and the solution stays smooth. When the velocity depends on
the density itself, as in traffic flow or Burgers' equation $\partial_tu + u\,\partial_xu = 0$, characteristics can
cross and the solution forms a **shock**. The fixed velocity fields of generative models do not have this problem.

## Many velocity fields, one density path

Given a path of densities $p_t$, any velocity field that satisfies the continuity equation with it carries $p_0$ to
$p_t$. Such fields are not unique: adding $\wvec/p$ with $\nabla\cdot\wvec = 0$ leaves the equation unchanged.
Generative models exploit this freedom to replace random dynamics with deterministic ones.

**The heat equation as a flow.** The <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink>
$\partial_tp = D\,\partial_x^2p$ describes particles that diffuse randomly. Since $\partial_xp = p\,\partial_x\log p$,
it can be rewritten as

$$
\partial_tp + \partial_x\paren{p\,v} = 0, \qquad v = -D\,\partial_x\log p.
$$

So deterministic particles moving with velocity $v = -D\,\partial_x\log p_t$, down the
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the log-density, have exactly the same density at every time as the
randomly diffusing ones.

**Worked example (the heat flow of a Gaussian).** Start from $p_0 = \Gauss(c, \sigma_0^2)$ with $D = \tfrac12$. The heat
equation gives $p_t = \Gauss(c, \sigma_t^2)$ with $\sigma_t^2 = \sigma_0^2 + t$. Its score is
$\partial_x\log p_t = -(x - c)/\sigma_t^2$, so the velocity is $v = (x - c)/(2\sigma_t^2)$. Along a path, $\frac{d}{dt}
\log(x - c) = 1/(2\sigma_t^2) = \frac{d}{dt}\log\sigma_t$, so

$$
x(t) = c + (x_0 - c)\,\frac{\sigma_t}{\sigma_0}.
$$

Every particle moves directly away from the centre, scaled by the growing width. With $\sigma_0 = 0.5$ at $t = 1$,
$\sigma_1 = \sqrt{1.25}$ and the factor is $\sqrt 5 = 2.236$: a particle that started at $c + 0.5$ is at $c + 1.118$. A
random walker from the same start could be anywhere, but the histogram of all walkers and all flow particles is the
same Gaussian. The "heat flow" field in the figure is this velocity.

In a diffusion model the same rewriting of the <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink>
gives the <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability flow ODE</NoteLink>
$\dot{\xvec} = \fvec - \tfrac12g^2\nabla\log p_t$ <Cite id="song2021" />. It shares the marginals of the noising process
and maps noise to data deterministically; see <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score-based generative
models</NoteLink>.

**Flow matching** runs the argument the other way <Cite id="lipman2023" />. Choose a path from noise to data, for
example $\xvec_t = (1 - t)\,\xvec_0 + t\,\xvec_1$ with $\xvec_0$ Gaussian and $\xvec_1$ a data point. Each such pair has
the constant velocity $\xvec_1 - \xvec_0$. The velocity field $\vvec(\xvec, t) = \expect\brack{\xvec_1 - \xvec_0 \mid
\xvec_t = \xvec}$ satisfies the continuity equation for the marginal densities of $\xvec_t$. A network trained by
regression to predict $\xvec_1 - \xvec_0$ from $(\xvec_t, t)$ learns this conditional mean, and integrating it from
$t = 0$ to $1$ turns Gaussian samples into data samples.

Among all fields that move $p_0$ to $p_1$ in unit time, the one with least kinetic energy $\int\!\int p\norm{\vvec}^2$
is given by <NoteLink to="optimal-transport">optimal transport</NoteLink>; its particles travel in straight lines at
constant speed <Cite id="villani2009" />.

## Assumptions

- $\vvec$ is Lipschitz in $\xvec$, so each characteristic exists and is unique and the flow is invertible.
- Mass is conserved. Sources or sinks, such as births and deaths in a population, add a term on the right:
  $\partial_tp + \nabla\cdot(p\,\vvec) = s$.

## Pitfalls

- **Transport and continuity are different.** $\partial_tu + \vvec\cdot\nabla u = 0$ keeps values; $\partial_tp +
  \nabla\cdot(p\,\vvec) = 0$ keeps mass. They agree only for divergence-free flows.
- **The density along a path is not the density at a point.** $\frac{d}{dt}\log p = -\nabla\cdot\vvec$ holds following
  a particle. At a fixed point, $\partial_tp$ also includes $-\vvec\cdot\nabla p$, the density being carried past.
- **Matching densities is not matching paths.** The probability flow ODE and the noising <Gloss name="sde" /> have the
  same marginal densities at every time, but not the same joint distribution over paths.
