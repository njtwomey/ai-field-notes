<Definition>
A **neural ordinary differential equation** maps an input $\hvec(0) = \xvec$ to an output $\hvec(T)$ by solving

$$
\dot{\hvec}(t) = \fvec_{\thetavec}\paren{\hvec(t), t}, \qquad t \in [0, T],
$$

where $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ is a neural network with parameters $\thetavec$ and the solution is computed by a numerical
ODE solver. The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of a loss $L(\hvec(T))$ with respect to $\thetavec$ is
computed by the **adjoint method**, which solves a second ODE backwards from $T$ to $0$ <Cite id="chen2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <Gloss name="resnet">residual network</Gloss> updates its hidden state layer by layer,

$$
\hvec_{k+1} = \hvec_k + \fvec_{\thetavec}(\hvec_k, k).
$$

That is one step of <NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers">Euler's method</NoteLink> with step size 1 for the ODE
$\dot{\hvec} = \fvec_{\thetavec}(\hvec, t)$. A neural ODE takes the limit of many small layers: depth becomes a
continuous time, and the network specifies a <NoteLink to="vector-fields-and-flows">vector field</NoteLink> rather than
a sequence of updates. The forward pass carries the input along the field's flow. The solver, not the architect,
decides how many evaluations of $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ that takes.

## Forward pass

Any solver can compute $\hvec(T)$. In practice an adaptive Runge–Kutta solver such as Dormand–Prince is used, so the
number of evaluations of $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ adapts to how hard the trajectory is to follow. Because the output is the
flow map $\phi_T$ of a Lipschitz field, the map from $\hvec(0)$ to $\hvec(T)$ is invertible: running the ODE backwards
recovers the input.

## The adjoint method

Backpropagating through every internal step of the solver works, but it stores every intermediate state. The adjoint
method computes the same gradient with memory that does not grow with the number of steps.

Define the **adjoint** $\avec(t) = \partial L/\partial\hvec(t)$, the sensitivity of the loss to the state at time $t$.
Its value at the end is known: $\avec(T) = \partial L/\partial\hvec(T)$.

**Derivation through Euler steps.** Discretise with step $h$: $\hvec_{k+1} = \hvec_k + h\,\fvec(\hvec_k)$. The
<NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> (ordinary <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>)
gives, with $\avec_k$ as a row vector,

$$
\avec_k = \avec_{k+1}\,\frac{\partial\hvec_{k+1}}{\partial\hvec_k} = \avec_{k+1}\paren{\Imat + h\,\frac{\partial
\fvec}{\partial\hvec}(\hvec_k)}.
$$

Rearranging, $(\avec_{k+1} - \avec_k)/h = -\avec_{k+1}\,\partial\fvec/\partial\hvec$. As $h \to 0$ this becomes the
**adjoint equation**

$$
\dot{\avec}(t) = -\avec(t)\,\frac{\partial\fvec_{\thetavec}}{\partial\hvec}\paren{\hvec(t), t}.
$$

The parameters are shared by every step, so their gradient sums the contribution of each step. Step $k$ depends on
$\thetavec$ through $h\,\fvec_{\thetavec}(\hvec_k)$, contributing $\avec_{k+1}\,h\,\partial\fvec/\partial\thetavec$.
The sum becomes an integral:

$$
\frac{dL}{d\thetavec} = \int_0^T \avec(t)\,\frac{\partial\fvec_{\thetavec}}{\partial\thetavec}\paren{\hvec(t), t}\,dt.
$$

Both right sides are <Gloss name="vjp">vector–Jacobian products</Gloss>, the operation reverse-mode automatic
differentiation computes in one backward pass through $\fvec_{\thetavec}$, without forming a
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>.

The backward pass solves three ODEs together from $T$ to $0$: the state $\hvec(t)$ backwards (recomputing it rather
than storing it), the adjoint $\avec(t)$, and the running parameter-gradient integral. It needs memory for one state,
not for the whole trajectory. The same equations appear in optimal control, where the adjoint is the costate of the
<NoteLink to="pontryagin-maximum-principle">Pontryagin maximum principle</NoteLink>.

**Worked example (a one-parameter ODE).** Let $\dot h = \theta h$ with $h(0) = h_0$ and loss $L = h(T)$. The exact
answer is $h(T) = h_0e^{\theta T}$, so $dL/d\theta = T h_0e^{\theta T}$. The adjoint method gives the same:

- $a(T) = \partial L/\partial h(T) = 1$ and $\partial f/\partial h = \theta$, so $\dot a = -\theta a$ and
  $a(t) = e^{\theta(T - t)}$.
- $\partial f/\partial\theta = h(t) = h_0e^{\theta t}$, so
  $dL/d\theta = \int_0^T e^{\theta(T - t)}h_0e^{\theta t}\,dt = \int_0^T h_0e^{\theta T}\,dt = T h_0e^{\theta T}$.

With $\theta = 0.5$, $T = 2$ and $h_0 = 1$: $L = e = 2.718$ and $dL/d\theta = 2e = 5.437$.

## Continuous normalising flows

Suppose $\zvec(0)$ has a known density $p_0$, such as a standard Gaussian, and $\zvec(T) = \phi_T(\zvec(0))$ is the
output of the ODE. The density of $\zvec(t)$ along one trajectory obeys the **instantaneous change of variables**

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t\paren{\zvec(t)} = -\nabla\cdot\fvec = -\trace\paren{\frac{\partial\fvec}{\partial\zvec}}.
$$

The reason is Liouville's formula from <NoteLink to="vector-fields-and-flows">vector fields and flows</NoteLink>: the
flow scales small volumes at rate $\nabla\cdot\fvec$, and probability mass is conserved, so the density falls at that
rate. Integrating gives

$$
\log p_T\paren{\zvec(T)} = \log p_0\paren{\zvec(0)} - \int_0^T \trace\paren{\frac{\partial\fvec}{\partial\zvec}}\,dt.
$$

A **continuous normalising flow** trains $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ by maximum likelihood with this formula. To score a data
point $\xvec$, the ODE is solved backwards from $\zvec(T) = \xvec$ to $\zvec(0)$, accumulating the trace integral on
the way. Compared with a discrete <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flow</NoteLink>, which needs a
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink>, only a trace is needed, and $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ can be any network.

**Worked example (a linear field).** For $\fvec(\zvec) = \Amat\zvec$ with $\Amat = \diag(0.5, -0.2)$, the trace is
$0.3$ everywhere. Every trajectory loses $0.3$ nats of log-density per unit time. After $t = 1$ the density at
$\zvec(1)$ is $e^{-0.3} = 0.741$ times the density at $\zvec(0)$, because the flow has stretched areas by
$e^{0.5}e^{-0.2} = e^{0.3}$.

The exact trace of a $d \times d$ Jacobian needs $d$ vector–Jacobian products. The
**Hutchinson estimator** replaces it by an unbiased estimate from one product <Cite id="hutchinson1989" />. For a random
vector $\epsilonvec$ with $\expect[\epsilonvec\epsilonvec^\top] = \Imat$, such as independent $\pm1$ entries,

$$
\expect\brack{\epsilonvec^\top\Jmat\epsilonvec} = \trace\paren{\Jmat\,\expect\brack{\epsilonvec\epsilonvec^\top}} =
\trace\Jmat.
$$

FFJORD uses this estimate inside the ODE, which makes continuous flows practical in high dimensions
<Cite id="grathwohl2019" />.

**Worked example (Hutchinson by hand).** For $\Jmat = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, the four sign
vectors give $\epsilonvec^\top\Jmat\epsilonvec = 10$ for $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$, and $0$ for $(1, -1)$ and $(-1, 1)$.
Their average is $5 = \trace\Jmat$. A single draw is either 10 or 0, so the estimate is unbiased but noisy.

## What a neural ODE cannot represent

The flow of a Lipschitz field is a homeomorphism: continuous, invertible, and in one dimension order-preserving.
Trajectories cannot cross. So no neural ODE on $\reals$ maps $1 \mapsto -1$ and $-1 \mapsto 1$. In two dimensions, no
flow followed by a linear read-out can separate the points of a disc from the points of a ring that surrounds it, since
the flow cannot move the disc out through the ring <Cite id="dupont2019" />. A residual network can, because a finite
Euler step with a large $\fvec$ can jump trajectories past each other.

**Augmented neural ODEs** add extra zero-initialised dimensions to the state, so trajectories can move around each other
in the larger space. Alternatively, <NoteLink to="stochastic-vector-field-mixtures">stochastic vector field
mixtures</NoteLink> introduce multi-modal vector fields with dynamic routing to handle branching, splitting, and
trajectory crossing directly.

## Training without simulation

Maximum-likelihood training of a continuous flow solves an ODE for every example at every step. **Flow matching** avoids
this <Cite id="lipman2023" />. It chooses a path of densities $p_t$ from noise to data and a velocity field $\uvec_t$
that carries it, meaning that $p_t$ and $\uvec_t$ satisfy the
<NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">continuity equation</NoteLink>. It then fits
$\fvec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, t) \approx \uvec_t(\xvec)$ by regression. Sampling still solves the learned ODE, but training
needs no solver. The <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability flow ODE</NoteLink> of a
<NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score-based diffusion model</NoteLink> is a continuous normalising flow of
the same kind, whose field is built from a learned score.

## In machine learning

- **Irregular time series.** A latent ODE evolves a hidden state between observations at arbitrary times, rather than
  in fixed steps.
- **Generative models.** Continuous normalising flows, flow matching and probability-flow samplers all use a neural
  ODE to map a Gaussian to data.
- **Memory-limited training.** The adjoint method trades memory for an extra backward solve.
- **Physical models.** Part of $\fvec$ can be a known physical law and part a network, with the solver respecting the
  structure of both <Cite id="kidger2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Adjoint gradients are approximate.** Solving $\hvec(t)$ backwards reconstructs the forward trajectory only up to
  solver error, and for dissipative dynamics the backward solve is unstable. The adjoint gradient can then disagree
  with the gradient of what the forward solver computed. Backpropagating through the solver, with checkpointing,
  gives the exact gradient of the discrete computation.
- **Cost grows during training.** As $\fvec_{\thetavec}$ becomes more complex, adaptive solvers take more steps.
  Regularising the field's smoothness keeps the number of evaluations down.
- **Stiff learned dynamics.** A trained field can become stiff, and explicit solvers then need tiny steps.
