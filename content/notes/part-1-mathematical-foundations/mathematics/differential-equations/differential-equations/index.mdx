<Definition>
A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives. An ordinary differential equation (ODE)
$\dot{\xvec} = \fvec(t, \xvec)$ gives the rate of change of a state $\xvec(t) \in \reals^d$ as a function of the state
and time. A partial differential equation (PDE) relates the partial derivatives of a function of several variables, such
as a density $p(\xvec, t)$ that changes in space and time <Cite id="hirsch2013" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Differential equations describe systems that evolve continuously: physical systems, populations, chemical reactions and
the dynamics that <NoteLink to="control-theory">control theory</NoteLink> steers. In machine learning they appear in
three ways. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> with a small step follows the gradient-flow ODE
$\dot{\thetavec} = -\nabla L(\thetavec)$, so its behaviour near a minimum is the stability of an equilibrium. Neural
ODEs and continuous normalising flows define a model as the solution of an ODE whose right-hand side is a network.
<Gloss name="diffusion-model">Diffusion models</Gloss> and flow-matching models move a simple density into the data
density, and the PDEs of the topic describe how that density evolves.

## Main ideas

**A rate and a starting point determine a path.** An <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">ODE</NoteLink> with
an initial value defines a trajectory. The trajectory exists and is unique when $\fvec$ is Lipschitz in $\xvec$. A
<NoteLink to="first-order-linear-odes">first-order linear ODE</NoteLink> is solved by an integrating factor and relaxes
exponentially.

**Linear systems are solved by eigenvalues.** The system $\dot{\xvec} = \Amat\xvec$ has solution
$e^{\Amat t}\xvec_0$, and the <NoteLink to="linear-systems-and-the-matrix-exponential">matrix exponential</NoteLink>
splits it into modes along the eigenvectors of $\Amat$. Real parts of the eigenvalues set growth or decay, and
imaginary parts set rotation.

**Near an equilibrium, every system looks linear.** <NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria" /> linearises $\fvec$ at a
state where $\fvec = \zeros$. The equilibrium attracts when every eigenvalue of the Jacobian has negative real part.
Nonlinear systems also branch and become unpredictable, as <NoteLink to="bifurcations-and-chaos">bifurcations and
chaos</NoteLink> shows with period doubling and Lyapunov exponents.

**A flow moves every point at once.** The <NoteLink to="vector-fields-and-flows">flow</NoteLink> of an ODE maps each
starting point to its position at time $t$. It is invertible, and its Jacobian determinant is the exponential of the
integrated divergence of $\fvec$. A flow therefore transports densities, as a normalising flow does.

**Solvers step through time.** <NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers" /> advance the state in steps of size $h$. Euler's
method has global error $O(h)$, Runge–Kutta methods reach $O(h^p)$, and stiff equations need implicit steps.
<NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations" /> put a network inside the solver and differentiate through it
with the adjoint ODE. <NoteLink to="stochastic-vector-field-mixtures" /> extend them to trajectories that cross or
branch.

**Densities obey PDEs.** <NoteLink to="partial-differential-equations" /> involve derivatives in several variables. The
<NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink> spreads a density by Gaussian convolution and is the limit of a
random walk. The <NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">continuity equation</NoteLink> conserves probability
mass that moves with a velocity field.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">ordinary differential equations</NoteLink>, then
<NoteLink to="first-order-linear-odes">first-order linear ODEs</NoteLink> and the matrix exponential. Stability, flows
and chaos form the qualitative theory and build on the linear case. <NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers" /> is the
bridge to computation and comes before neural ODEs and their mixture extension. The three PDE notes come last: the
general introduction, then the heat equation, then the transport and continuity equations, which reuse flows.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">fundamental theorem of calculus</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="jacobian" /> and the <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>. The PDE notes use
<NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink>, and the heat equation uses the
<NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>.

Adding noise turns an ODE into a stochastic differential equation, the subject of
<NoteLink to="stochastic-calculus">stochastic calculus</NoteLink>. The <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation" />
generalises the heat and continuity equations to diffusions, and the
<NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability-flow ODE</NoteLink> turns a diffusion back into an ODE. In learning,
<NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flows</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score
matching and score-based generative models</NoteLink> use these equations to generate data, and
<NoteLink to="state-space-representation">state-space representation</NoteLink> carries linear systems into control.
