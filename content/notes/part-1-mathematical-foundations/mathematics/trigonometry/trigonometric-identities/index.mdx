<Definition>
A **trigonometric identity** is an equation between expressions in the trigonometric functions that holds for every
value of the angles at which both sides are defined. The central ones are the **angle-sum formulas**: for all real
$\alpha$ and $\beta$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos(\alpha + \beta) &= \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta, \\
\sin(\alpha + \beta) &= \sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every identity below follows from these two, from $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, and from the parity relations
$\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ and $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$ <Cite id="roy2010" />.
</Definition>

## Angle sums from rotations

Let $R(\theta)$ be the map of the plane that rotates every vector anticlockwise about the origin by the angle $\theta$.
Rotation is linear: it maps the parallelogram spanned by two vectors onto the parallelogram spanned by their images, so
it preserves sums, and it preserves scalar multiples because it preserves lengths and directions along a line. A linear
map of the plane is the <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">matrix</NoteLink> whose columns are the images of the
basis vectors $\evec_1 = (1, 0)$ and $\evec_2 = (0, 1)$ <Cite id="strang2016" />.

- $\evec_1$ is the point at angle 0 on the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit circle</NoteLink>, so $R(\theta)\evec_1$
  is the point at angle $\theta$, $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.
- $\evec_2$ is the point at angle $\pi/2$, so $R(\theta)\evec_2$ is the point at angle $\theta + \pi/2$. A quarter turn
  maps $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$, so that point is $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$.

Hence

$$
R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Rotating by $\beta$ and then by $\alpha$ is the same as rotating by $\alpha + \beta$. Composition of linear maps is
<NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix multiplication</NoteLink>, so $R(\alpha)R(\beta) = R(\alpha + \beta)$.
The first column of the product is $R(\alpha)$ applied to $(\cos\beta, \sin\beta)$:

$$
R(\alpha)R(\beta)\,\evec_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta \\
\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The first column of $R(\alpha + \beta)$ is $(\cos(\alpha + \beta), \sin(\alpha + \beta))$. Equating the two columns
entry by entry gives both angle-sum formulas.

In words: the rotated axes are $\uvec = (\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$ and $\vvec = (-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)$. The point at
angle $\alpha + \beta$ is the point at angle $\beta$ measured in the rotated frame,
$\cos\beta\,\uvec + \sin\beta\,\vvec$, and its two coordinates are the two formulas.

Replacing $\beta$ by $-\beta$ and using parity gives the **difference formulas**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos(\alpha - \beta) &= \cos\alpha\cos\beta + \sin\alpha\sin\beta, \\
\sin(\alpha - \beta) &= \sin\alpha\cos\beta - \cos\alpha\sin\beta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing the sine formula by the cosine formula, and then numerator and denominator by $\cos\alpha\cos\beta$, gives

$$
\tan(\alpha + \beta) = \frac{\tan\alpha + \tan\beta}{1 - \tan\alpha\tan\beta}.
$$

## Double and half angles

Setting $\beta = \alpha$ gives the **double-angle formulas**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin 2\alpha &= 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha, \\
\cos 2\alpha &= \cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha = 2\cos^2\alpha - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2\alpha,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the last two forms use $\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1$. Solving them for the squares gives the
**power-reduction formulas**

$$
\cos^2\alpha = \frac{1 + \cos 2\alpha}{2}, \qquad \sin^2\alpha = \frac{1 - \cos 2\alpha}{2}.
$$

A squared sinusoid is therefore a sinusoid at twice the frequency, offset by $\tfrac12$. Over a whole period its average
is $\tfrac12$, which is why the mean power of a sinusoid of amplitude $A$ is $A^2/2$. Substituting $\alpha = \theta/2$
gives the half-angle formulas, $\cos^2(\theta/2) = (1 + \cos\theta)/2$ and $\sin^2(\theta/2) = (1 - \cos\theta)/2$.

## Products and sums

Adding and subtracting the sum and difference formulas cancels one product each time:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos\alpha\cos\beta &= \tfrac12\big[\cos(\alpha - \beta) + \cos(\alpha + \beta)\big], \\
\sin\alpha\sin\beta &= \tfrac12\big[\cos(\alpha - \beta) - \cos(\alpha + \beta)\big], \\
\sin\alpha\cos\beta &= \tfrac12\big[\sin(\alpha + \beta) + \sin(\alpha - \beta)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

These are the **product-to-sum formulas**. With $\alpha = mt$ and $\beta = nt$, a product of sinusoids at frequencies
$m$ and $n$ is a sum of sinusoids at $m - n$ and $m + n$. Writing $a = \alpha + \beta$ and $b = \alpha - \beta$ in the
third line and reading it backwards gives a **sum-to-product formula**,

$$
\sin a + \sin b = 2\sin\frac{a + b}{2}\cos\frac{a - b}{2}.
$$

Two sinusoids at close frequencies add to a fast oscillation at their mean frequency inside a slow envelope at half
their difference: the beats heard when two strings are slightly out of tune.

## Harmonic addition

A cosine and a sine with the same frequency combine into one sinusoid. For real $a$ and $b$, not both zero,

$$
a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta = r\cos(\theta - \varphi), \qquad r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2},
$$

where $\varphi$ is the angle with $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi) = (a, b)/r$, computed as $\varphi = \atantwo(b, a)$. The
proof expands the right side with the difference formula:
$r\cos(\theta - \varphi) = r\cos\varphi\cos\theta + r\sin\varphi\sin\theta = a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta$. The pair
$(r, \varphi)$ is the <NoteLink to="polar-coordinates">polar form</NoteLink> of the point $(a, b)$.
<NoteLink to="linear-regression">Linear regression</NoteLink> on the two features $\cos(\omega t)$ and $\sin(\omega t)$
therefore fits a sinusoid of known frequency $\omega$ with unknown amplitude and phase, a model that is nonlinear in the
phase but linear in $(a, b)$.

## Why the identities matter in machine learning

- **Orthogonality of Fourier modes.** For whole numbers $m \ne n$, the product-to-sum formula gives
  $\int_0^{2\pi} \sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,dx = \tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi} \big[\cos((m - n)x) - \cos((m + n)x)\big]\,dx = 0$,
  because each cosine runs through a whole number of periods and integrates to zero. With $m = n \ge 1$ the integral is
  $\pi$. These relations let a <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink> read off each coefficient by one
  integral.
- **Rotary position embeddings.** <Gloss name="rope" /> rotates each pair of coordinates of a query at position $m$ by
  $R(m\theta)$ and of a key at position $n$ by $R(n\theta)$. A rotation matrix satisfies $R(\alpha)^\top = R(-\alpha)$,
  so the score is $(R(m\theta)\qvec)^\top R(n\theta)\kvec = \qvec^\top R((n - m)\theta)\kvec$ by the composition rule.
  The score depends on the positions only through the offset $n - m$ <Cite id="su2021" />.
- **<NoteLink to="sinusoidal-positional-encoding">Sinusoidal positional encodings</NoteLink>.** The encoding of position
  $p + k$ is a fixed rotation of the encoding of $p$ in each frequency pair, by the angle-sum formulas. Vaswani et al.
  chose sinusoids so that attention could learn to attend by relative position <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.
- **<NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">Random Fourier features</NoteLink>.** For $b$ uniform on $[0, 2\pi]$, the
  product-to-sum formula gives $2\cos(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)\cos(\wvec^\top\yvec + b) = \cos(\wvec^\top(\xvec - \yvec)) +
  \cos(\wvec^\top(\xvec + \yvec)
  + 2b)$. The second term averages to zero over $b$, which leaves a function of $\xvec - \yvec$ alone
  <Cite id="rahimi2007" />.

## Worked example

Compute $\cos 75°$ exactly. Write $75° = 45° + 30°$:

$$
\cos 75° = \cos 45°\cos 30° - \sin 45°\sin 30° = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12
= \frac{\sqrt6 - \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.2588.
$$

Combine $3\cos t + 4\sin t$ into one sinusoid. Here $r = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ and $\varphi = \atantwo(4, 3) \approx
0.9273$ rad, so $3\cos t + 4\sin t = 5\cos(t - 0.9273)$. Its maximum value is 5, reached at $t \approx 0.9273$.

## Pitfalls

- **Linearity does not hold.** $\sin(\alpha + \beta) \ne \sin\alpha + \sin\beta$ and $\sin 2\alpha \ne 2\sin\alpha$
  in general. At $\alpha = \beta = \pi/2$, $\sin(\alpha + \beta) = 0$ while $\sin\alpha + \sin\beta = 2$.
- **Sign of the cross term.** The cosine formula subtracts $\sin\alpha\sin\beta$; the sine formula adds its cross term.
  The rotation matrix, with $-\sin\theta$ above the diagonal, is a reliable way to recover the signs.
- **Phase from arctan.** $\varphi = \arctan(b/a)$ is wrong by $\pi$ when $a < 0$. Use $\atantwo(b, a)$.
- **Rotation direction.** $R(\theta)$ rotates anticlockwise for $\theta > 0$ in a right-handed coordinate system.
  Image coordinates, with $y$ pointing down, reverse the visible direction.
