<Definition>
An **angle** is the figure formed by two rays that share an endpoint, the **vertex**. Its size in **radians** is the
length of the arc that the two rays cut from a circle of radius 1 centred at the vertex. On a circle of radius $r$ the
arc has length

$$
s = r\theta,
$$

so $\theta = s/r$ is a ratio of two lengths. A full turn is $2\pi$ radians, and $360$ **degrees**
<Cite id="bipm2019" />.
</Definition>

## Why the ratio depends only on the angle

Two circles centred at the vertex, of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$, are scaled copies of each other. Scaling by $r_2/r_1$ about
the vertex maps the first circle onto the second and maps the arc between the two rays onto the arc between the same
rays. Scaling multiplies every length by the same factor, so the arc lengths satisfy $s_2/s_1 = r_2/r_1$, which is
$s_1/r_1 = s_2/r_2$. The ratio of arc to radius is the same on every circle about the vertex. It measures the opening
of the rays and nothing else.

The number $\pi$ is defined as the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, so the circumference is $2\pi r$.
A full turn therefore has arc length $2\pi r$ and measures $2\pi r / r = 2\pi$ radians. A half turn is $\pi$, a quarter
turn (a right angle) is $\pi/2$.

The radian is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no physical dimension. The SI defines it as $1\ \text{rad} =
1\ \text{m}/\text{m} = 1$ <Cite id="bipm2019" />. Writing "rad" records which convention a number follows; it does not
change the arithmetic.

## Degrees and other units

A degree is $1/360$ of a turn. Since $360° = 2\pi$ radians, the conversions are

$$
\theta_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\pi}{180}\,\theta_{\text{deg}}, \qquad \theta_{\text{deg}} = \frac{180}{\pi}\,\theta_{\text{rad}}.
$$

One radian is $180/\pi \approx 57.2958°$. The common angles are

| Degrees | 0   | 30      | 45      | 60      | 90      | 180     | 270      | 360      |
| ------- | --- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | -------- | -------- |
| Radians | 0   | $\pi/6$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/3$ | $\pi/2$ | $\pi$   | $3\pi/2$ | $2\pi$   |

Two further units appear in software and instruments. A **turn** is the full revolution itself, so $\theta_{\text{turn}}
= \theta_{\text{rad}}/(2\pi)$. A **gradian** is $1/400$ of a turn, so a right angle is 100 gradians.

## Signed angles and angles beyond a turn

In the plane, an angle is measured from the positive $x$-axis to a ray from the origin. Anticlockwise rotation is
positive and clockwise rotation is negative. Angles that differ by a whole number of turns, $\theta$ and
$\theta + 2\pi k$ for an integer $k$, point along the same ray; they are **coterminal**. A rotation by $3\pi$ ends where
a rotation by $\pi$ ends.

Software therefore stores a direction as one representative angle, usually in $[0, 2\pi)$ or in $(-\pi, \pi]$. The
signed difference between two directions $a$ and $b$ must be reduced to the same range:

$$
\Delta = \big((b - a + \pi) \bmod 2\pi\big) - \pi,
$$

with $\bmod$ returning a value in $[0, 2\pi)$. Without the reduction, headings of $350°$ and $10°$ appear $340°$ apart
instead of $20°$.

Averaging has the same problem. The arithmetic mean of the stored numbers $350$ and $10$ is $180$, the opposite
direction to both. The **circular mean** averages the unit vectors $(\cos a_i, \sin a_i)$, where $\cos a$ and $\sin a$
are the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of the point at angle $a$ on the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit circle</NoteLink>,
and takes the direction of the result. For $350°$ and $10°$ the two vectors are mirror images in the $x$-axis, their
average lies on the positive $x$-axis, and the circular mean is $0°$. Feature pipelines encode cyclic quantities, such
as an hour of the day $h$, as the pair $(\cos(2\pi h/24), \sin(2\pi h/24))$ for the same reason: hour 23 and hour 0 then
lie close together.

## Sector area

A sector of angle $\theta$ is the fraction $\theta/(2\pi)$ of the disc of area $\pi r^2$, because equal angles cut
congruent sectors. Its area is

$$
A = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} \cdot \pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \theta.
$$

On the unit circle the area is $\theta/2$: the angle in radians is twice the area of its sector. This form of the
definition carries over to the hyperbola, where it defines the parameter of the
<NoteLink to="hyperbolic-functions">hyperbolic functions</NoteLink>.

## Why calculus uses radians

Let $\sin\theta$ be the height of the point at angle $\theta$ on the unit circle, measured in radians. For a small
angle, the arc from $(1, 0)$ to that point has length $\theta$, and the vertical drop from the point to the $x$-axis
has length $\sin\theta$. The arc and the drop end at the same point and become indistinguishable as $\theta \to 0$, so
$\sin\theta/\theta \to 1$. A proof by comparing areas is in
<NoteLink to="derivatives-of-trigonometric-functions">derivatives of the trigonometric functions</NoteLink>, and the
limit gives $\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$ with no constant <Cite id="stewart2016" />.

In degrees, the function is $\theta \mapsto \sin(\pi\theta/180)$, and the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain
rule</NoteLink> gives

$$
\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{180}\right) = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos\left(\frac{\pi\theta}{180}\right).
$$

Every derivative, Taylor coefficient and differential equation then carries a factor of $\pi/180$. The same holds
in physics. A point on a wheel of radius $r$ turning at $\omega$ radians per second moves at speed $v = r\omega$. A
frequency $f$ in turns per second corresponds to $\omega = 2\pi f$ radians per second. Libraries follow the same
convention: `math.sin`, `numpy.sin` and `torch.sin` all expect radians <Cite id="roy2010" />.

## Worked example

A wheel of radius $r = 0.3$ m turns through $150°$.

- **Angle.** $\theta = 150 \cdot \pi/180 = 5\pi/6 \approx 2.618$ rad.
- **Arc length.** A point on the rim travels $s = r\theta = 0.3 \times 2.618 \approx 0.785$ m.
- **Sector area.** The sector swept by one spoke has area $\tfrac12 r^2\theta = 0.5 \times 0.09 \times 2.618 \approx
  0.118\ \text{m}^2$.

Two compass headings, $a = 350°$ and $b = 10°$, have the raw difference $b - a = -340°$. The wrapped difference is
$((-340 + 180) \bmod 360) - 180 = 200 - 180 = 20°$, a turn of $20°$ anticlockwise.

## Pitfalls

- **Degrees passed to radian functions.** `sin(30)` is $\sin(30\ \text{rad}) \approx -0.988$, not $0.5$. Convert with
  `math.radians` or `numpy.deg2rad` first.
- **Unwrapped differences.** Subtracting stored angles gives errors of up to a full turn near the wrap point. Reduce
  differences to $(-\pi, \pi]$, and average directions as vectors.
- **Hertz and radians per second.** $\omega = 2\pi f$. A sinusoid written $\sin(\omega t)$ with $\omega$ in hertz runs
  $2\pi$ times too slowly.
- **Angles as features.** Feeding a raw angle to a model places $359°$ far from $0°$. The pair
  $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ removes the discontinuity.
