<Definition>
The **polar coordinates** $(r, \theta)$ of a point in the plane are its distance $r \ge 0$ from the origin, the
**radial coordinate**, and the angle $\theta$ from the positive $x$-axis to the ray through the point, the **angular
coordinate**, measured anticlockwise in radians. They are related to the Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ by

$$
x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \theta = \atantwo(y, x).
$$

The angle is determined up to a whole number of turns, and not at all at the origin <Cite id="stewart2016" />.
</Definition>

## Converting between the two systems

**Polar to Cartesian.** The point at angle $\theta$ on the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit circle</NoteLink> is
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Scaling it by $r$ moves it along the same ray to distance $r$, so
$(x, y) = r(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.

**Cartesian to polar.** Squaring and adding the two equations gives
$x^2 + y^2 = r^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r^2$, so $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. The angle is the direction of the
vector $(x, y)$. The ratio $y/x = \tan\theta$ fixes $\theta$ only up to a half turn, since $(x, y)$ and $(-x, -y)$ give
the same ratio, so the angle is computed with the two-argument arctangent, $\theta = \atantwo(y, x) \in (-\pi, \pi]$,
which uses the signs of both coordinates (see <NoteLink to="inverse-trigonometric-functions">inverse trigonometric
functions</NoteLink>).

**Non-uniqueness.** $(r, \theta)$ and $(r, \theta + 2\pi k)$ name the same point for every integer $k$, and the origin
is $(0, \theta)$ for every $\theta$. Many texts also allow $r < 0$, with $(-r, \theta)$ meaning $(r, \theta + \pi)$:
the point on the opposite ray. That convention lets a single formula such as $r = 2\cos 3\theta$ draw a whole curve.

## Curves in polar form

An equation $r = f(\theta)$ describes a curve swept out as the ray turns: at each angle, the point sits at distance
$f(\theta)$ along the ray. Curves with rotational structure have short polar equations.

- **Circle about the origin:** $r = a$. A circle through the origin with centre $(a, 0)$: $r = 2a\cos\theta$, since
  $r^2 = 2ar\cos\theta$ is $x^2 + y^2 = 2ax$.
- **Line through the origin:** $\theta = \theta_0$.
- **Spiral of Archimedes:** $r = a\theta$. The radius grows by $2\pi a$ per turn.
- **Rose:** $r = a\cos(k\theta)$, with $k$ petals for odd $k$ and $2k$ for even $k$.
- **Cardioid:** $r = a(1 + \cos\theta)$, a heart shape with a cusp at the origin.

## The area element

A small change $dr$ in the radius and $d\theta$ in the angle sweep out a region bounded by two arcs and two rays. Its
sides have lengths $dr$ along the ray and $r\,d\theta$ along the arc, because an arc of angle $d\theta$ on a circle of
radius $r$ has length $r\,d\theta$. To first order it is a rectangle, so its area is

$$
dA = r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

The factor $r$ is the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> of
the map $(r, \theta) \mapsto
(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$:

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} \partial x/\partial r & \partial x/\partial\theta \\ \partial y/\partial r & \partial y/\partial\theta
\end{pmatrix} = \det\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}
= r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r.
$$

A disc of radius $R$ has area $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^R r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot R^2/2 = \pi R^2$. More generally, the
region swept by $r = f(\theta)$ between $\theta = \alpha$ and $\theta = \beta$ is made of thin sectors of area
$\tfrac12 f(\theta)^2\,d\theta$, so its area is $\tfrac12\int_\alpha^\beta f(\theta)^2\,d\theta$.

Three results used in machine learning rest on this element.

- **The Gaussian integral.** $\big(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}dx\big)^2 = \iint e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\,dx\,dy =
  \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi$. The factor $r$ makes the inner integral elementary
  (see <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>).
- **The Box–Muller transform.** A pair of independent standard normals, written in polar form, has a uniform angle and a
  squared radius that is exponential, which turns two uniform draws into two normal draws
  (see <NoteLink to="box-muller-transform">Box–Muller transform</NoteLink>).
- **Sampling a disc.** Choosing $r$ uniform on $[0, R]$ crowds points near the centre, because the area within radius
  $r$ grows like $r^2$. A uniform point in the disc takes $r = R\sqrt{U}$ for $U$ uniform on $[0, 1]$ (see
  <NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere">sampling a disk or sphere</NoteLink>).

## Polar features

A dataset whose classes form concentric rings cannot be split by a straight line in $(x, y)$. In polar coordinates the
radius alone separates the classes, so a linear model on $(r, \theta)$, or on $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, does what a linear
model on $(x, y)$ cannot. Adding $x^2 + y^2$ as a feature is the same idea, and it is the simplest example of a feature
map that makes a problem linearly separable.

## Higher dimensions and complex numbers

- **Cylindrical coordinates** add a height $z$ to $(r, \theta)$, with volume element $r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$.
- **Spherical coordinates** $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$ give $x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta$, $y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta$,
  $z = \rho\cos\phi$, with $\phi$ the angle from the $z$-axis and volume element
  $\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\phi$.
- **Complex numbers.** The polar form of $z = x + iy$ is $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = re^{i\theta}$, with
  modulus $r = \abs{z}$ and argument $\theta$. Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their
  arguments (see <NoteLink to="eulers-formula">Euler's formula</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

Convert $(x, y) = (-1, \sqrt{3})$ to polar form. The radius is $r = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$. The point is in the second
quadrant, so $\theta = \atantwo(\sqrt{3}, -1) = \arctan(-\sqrt{3}) + \pi = -\pi/3 + \pi = 2\pi/3$. Check:
$2(\cos(2\pi/3), \sin(2\pi/3)) = 2(-1/2, \sqrt{3}/2) = (-1, \sqrt{3})$.

Find the area inside the cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$. With the sector formula,

$$
A = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi} (1 + \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi} \LP 1 + 2\cos\theta + \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2} \RP d\theta
= \frac12\LP 2\pi + 0 + \pi \RP = \frac{3\pi}{2},
$$

using $\cos^2\theta = (1 + \cos 2\theta)/2$ and the fact that $\cos\theta$ and $\cos 2\theta$ integrate to zero over a
full turn.

## Pitfalls

- **$\theta = \arctan(y/x)$.** It returns the wrong quadrant for $x < 0$ and fails at $x = 0$. Use $\atantwo(y, x)$.
- **The jump at $\pm\pi$.** A point moving smoothly across the negative $x$-axis has an angle that jumps by $2\pi$.
  Features or losses built on $\theta$ should use $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ or unwrap the angle.
- **Forgetting the factor $r$.** Integrating $f(r, \theta)\,dr\,d\theta$ without the factor $r$ gives the wrong area,
  probability or expectation.
- **Uniform $(r, \theta)$ is not uniform in the plane.** Points with $r$ and $\theta$ both uniform cluster near the
  origin. Sample $r = R\sqrt{U}$ instead.
