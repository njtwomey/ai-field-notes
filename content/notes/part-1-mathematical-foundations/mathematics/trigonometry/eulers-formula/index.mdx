<Definition>
A **complex number** is $z = x + iy$ with real $x$, $y$ and $i^2 = -1$. It is drawn as the point $(x, y)$ of the
**complex plane**, with **modulus** $\abs{z} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. For complex $z$ the exponential is defined by its
series, $e^z = \sum_{k=0}^\infty z^k/k!$, which converges for every $z$. **Euler's formula** states that for every real
$\theta$,

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.
$$

So $e^{i\theta}$ is the point at angle $\theta$ on the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit circle</NoteLink>
$\abs{z} = 1$ <Cite id="euler1748" />.
</Definition>

## Derivation from the series

The <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> of sine and cosine about 0 come from their
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink>. The derivatives of $\sin$ cycle through $\cos$, $-\sin$, $-\cos$,
$\sin$ (see <NoteLink to="derivatives-of-trigonometric-functions">derivatives of the trigonometric
functions</NoteLink>), so at 0 the derivatives of $\sin$ are $0, 1, 0, -1, \dots$ and those of $\cos$ are
$1, 0, -1, 0, \dots$. Both series converge for every real $\theta$:

$$
\cos\theta = 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \cdots, \qquad
\sin\theta = \theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots.
$$

Now put $z = i\theta$ into the exponential series. The powers of $i$ repeat with period 4: $i^0 = 1$, $i^1 = i$,
$i^2 = -1$, $i^3 = -i$, $i^4 = 1$. So

$$
e^{i\theta} = 1 + i\theta - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} - i\frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + i\frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots
$$

The series converges absolutely, so its terms can be regrouped. The even-indexed terms are real and form the series of
$\cos\theta$. The odd-indexed terms are $i$ times the series of $\sin\theta$. Hence
$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ <Cite id="roy2010" />.

<SeriesPartialSums />

<Derivation title="Derivation from a differential equation">
Let $z(\theta) = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. Differentiating the real and imaginary parts separately gives
$z'(\theta) = -\sin\theta + i\cos\theta = i(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = iz(\theta)$, and $z(0) = 1$. The function
$e^{i\theta}$ satisfies the same equation, because differentiating its series term by term multiplies it by $i$. Their
quotient $q(\theta) = z(\theta)e^{-i\theta}$ has derivative $iz e^{-i\theta} - iz e^{-i\theta} = 0$, so it is constant,
and $q(0) = 1$. Hence $z(\theta) = e^{i\theta}$.

The equation $z' = iz$ also explains the picture. Multiplying by $i$ turns a vector a quarter turn, so the velocity is
always perpendicular to the position and has the same length. A point that starts at 1 and moves this way stays on the
unit circle and travels along it at unit speed, so after time $\theta$ it has covered arc length $\theta$: it is at
angle $\theta$.
</Derivation>

## Multiplication adds angles

Every nonzero complex number has a **polar form** $z = re^{i\alpha}$, with $r = \abs{z}$ and the **argument**
$\alpha = \atantwo(y, x)$ (see <NoteLink to="polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</NoteLink>). The exponential law
$e^{a}e^{b} = e^{a + b}$ holds for complex $a$ and $b$, because the product of the two series rearranges into the series
of $e^{a + b}$ by the binomial theorem. Therefore

$$
re^{i\alpha} \cdot se^{i\beta} = rs\,e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}.
$$

Moduli multiply and arguments add. Multiplying by $e^{i\beta}$ rotates the plane by $\beta$, the action of the rotation
matrix $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\beta & -\sin\beta \\ \sin\beta & \cos\beta \end{pmatrix}$ on $(x, y)$. Multiplying by $i =
e^{i\pi/2}$ is a quarter turn.

Expanding both sides of $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}$ gives the angle-sum formulas at once:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta)
&= (\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) \\
&\quad + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta),
\end{aligned}
$$

and the real and imaginary parts must equal $\cos(\alpha + \beta)$ and $\sin(\alpha + \beta)$. Every
<NoteLink to="trigonometric-identities">trigonometric identity</NoteLink> becomes a rule for exponents.

## De Moivre's formula and roots of unity

Raising $e^{i\theta}$ to a whole power $n$ multiplies the angle by $n$:

$$
(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta.
$$

This is **De Moivre's formula**. Expanding the left side with the binomial theorem and comparing parts gives
multiple-angle formulas. For $n = 3$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos 3\theta &= \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta, \\
\sin 3\theta &= 3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta - \sin^3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

The equation $z^n = 1$ has the $n$ solutions $\omega_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}$ for $k = 0, \dots, n - 1$, the **roots of
unity** of order $n$, equally spaced around the unit circle. For $n \ge 2$ they sum to zero: $\sum_k \omega_1^k$ is a
geometric series with ratio $\omega_1 \ne 1$ and equals $(1 - \omega_1^n)/(1 - \omega_1) = 0$.

## Sine and cosine as exponentials

Adding and subtracting $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ and $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$ gives

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.
$$

A real sinusoid is the sum of two counter-rotating **phasors** $e^{\pm i\theta}$. Replacing $i\theta$ by a real $x$ in
the same formulas gives the <NoteLink to="hyperbolic-functions">hyperbolic functions</NoteLink>
$\cosh x = (e^x + e^{-x})/2$ and $\sinh x = (e^x - e^{-x})/2$, so $\cos(ix) = \cosh x$. At $\theta = \pi$ the formula
gives **Euler's identity**, $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$.

## Fourier analysis

The complex exponentials $e^{int}$, for integer $n$, are the building blocks of Fourier analysis
<Cite id="bracewell2000" />.

- **Orthogonality in one line.** For an integer $k \ne 0$, $\int_0^{2\pi} e^{ikt}\,dt = \big[e^{ikt}/(ik)\big]_0^{2\pi} =
  0$ because $e^{2\pi ik} = 1$. With $k = m - n$, this gives $\int_0^{2\pi} e^{imt}\overline{e^{int}}\,dt = 2\pi$ when
  $m = n$ and 0 otherwise. The real-valued orthogonality of sines and cosines follows by taking real and imaginary
  parts.
- **Series.** A <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink> $f(t) = \sum_n c_n e^{int}$ has coefficients
  $c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(t)e^{-int}\,dt$, each read off by orthogonality. One complex coefficient carries
  both the amplitude $\abs{c_n}$ and the phase $\arg c_n$ of a frequency.
- **The discrete transform.** The <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">discrete Fourier transform</NoteLink> of
  length $N$ evaluates the signal against the roots of unity $e^{-2\pi ikn/N}$.
- **Shifts and derivatives.** Delaying a signal by $\tau$ multiplies $e^{i\omega t}$ by $e^{-i\omega\tau}$, a rotation.
  Differentiating multiplies it by $i\omega$. Both become multiplications in the frequency domain.

The same algebra appears elsewhere. <Gloss name="rope">Rotary position embeddings</Gloss> treat each pair of coordinates
as a complex number and multiply it by $e^{im\theta}$ at position $m$; the product of a rotated query and the conjugate
of a rotated key then depends only on the offset between positions <Cite id="su2021" />. A linear system
$\xvec' = \Amat\xvec$ whose matrix has complex eigenvalues $a \pm ib$ has solutions built from $e^{at}e^{\pm ibt}$,
which oscillate at frequency $b$ (see <NoteLink to="linear-systems-and-the-matrix-exponential">linear systems and the
matrix exponential</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

Compute $(1 + i)^8$. The modulus of $1 + i$ is $\sqrt2$ and its argument is $\pi/4$, so $1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$ and

$$
(1 + i)^8 = (\sqrt2)^8 e^{i \cdot 8\pi/4} = 16\,e^{2\pi i} = 16.
$$

The cube roots of unity are $1$, $e^{2\pi i/3} = -\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$ and
$e^{4\pi i/3} = -\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$. Their sum is $1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$.

## Pitfalls

- **Powers of complex numbers are multivalued.** $e^{i\theta}$ has period $2\pi$, so $\log z$ is defined only up to
  adding $2\pi ik$. Rules such as $(e^a)^b = e^{ab}$ hold for integer $b$ but can fail otherwise: on the
  <NoteLink to="inverse-trigonometric-functions">principal branch</NoteLink> $(e^{2\pi i})^{1/2} = 1^{1/2} = 1$, while
  $e^{2\pi i \cdot 1/2} = e^{\pi i} = -1$.
- **The argument jumps.** $\arg z$ computed by $\atantwo$ lies in $(-\pi, \pi]$ and jumps by $2\pi$ across the negative
  real axis. A sequence of phases must be unwrapped before it is differentiated.
- **Sign conventions in transforms.** Some fields write $e^{-i\omega t}$ for the forward transform and some
  $e^{+i\omega t}$, and engineering texts write $j$ for $i$. Results agree up to a conjugate.
- **Real signals have two exponentials per frequency.** $\cos\omega t$ contributes at $+\omega$ and at $-\omega$, each
  with half the amplitude.
