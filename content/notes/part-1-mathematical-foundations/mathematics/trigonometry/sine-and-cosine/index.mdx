<Definition>
The **unit circle** is the circle of radius 1 centred at the origin, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. For a real number $\theta$, let
$P(\theta)$ be the point reached by travelling an arc of length $\abs{\theta}$ around the unit circle from $(1, 0)$,
anticlockwise when $\theta > 0$ and clockwise when $\theta < 0$. The **cosine** and **sine** of $\theta$ are its
coordinates:

$$
P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta).
$$

Both are functions from $\reals$ to $[-1, 1]$, and $\theta$ is the angle of $P(\theta)$ in radians
<Cite id="roy2010" />.
</Definition>

## The unit circle

Every real number is an angle, so $\sin$ and $\cos$ are defined on the whole real line. Adding a full turn returns to
the same point, so

$$
\cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta, \qquad \sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta.
$$

Both functions are **periodic** with period $2\pi$, the smallest positive shift that leaves them unchanged. The signs
follow the quadrant of $P(\theta)$: cosine is positive in the right half of the circle and sine in the upper half.

The values at a few angles follow from the geometry of the circle.

- **$\theta = \pi/4$.** The point lies on the line $y = x$, so $x^2 + x^2 = 1$ and
  $\cos(\pi/4) = \sin(\pi/4) = \sqrt{2}/2 \approx 0.7071$.
- **$\theta = \pi/6$.** The points $P(\pi/6)$ and $P(-\pi/6)$ and the origin form a triangle with two sides of length 1
  and an angle of $\pi/3$ between them, so it is equilateral. The chord between the two points therefore has length 1.
  Its half, the height of $P(\pi/6)$, is $\sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$, and $\cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt{1 - 1/4} = \sqrt{3}/2$.
- **$\theta = \pi/3$.** Reflecting in the line $y = x$ swaps the coordinates and maps the angle $\pi/6$ to
  $\pi/2 - \pi/6 = \pi/3$, so $\cos(\pi/3) = 1/2$ and $\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2$.

| $\theta$      | $0$ | $\pi/6$      | $\pi/4$      | $\pi/3$      | $\pi/2$ | $\pi$ | $3\pi/2$ |
| ------------- | --- | ------------ | ------------ | ------------ | ------- | ----- | -------- |
| $\cos\theta$  | 1   | $\sqrt{3}/2$ | $\sqrt{2}/2$ | $1/2$        | 0       | $-1$  | 0        |
| $\sin\theta$  | 0   | $1/2$        | $\sqrt{2}/2$ | $\sqrt{3}/2$ | 1       | 0     | $-1$     |

## Symmetries

Reflections and rotations of the circle give the relations between values at different angles.

- **Parity.** Reflecting in the $x$-axis maps $P(\theta)$ to $P(-\theta)$ and $(x, y)$ to $(x, -y)$. So
  $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ and $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$: cosine is even and sine is odd.
- **Quarter turn.** Rotating the plane anticlockwise by $\pi/2$ maps $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$ and $P(\theta)$ to
  $P(\theta + \pi/2)$. So $\cos(\theta + \pi/2) = -\sin\theta$ and $\sin(\theta + \pi/2) = \cos\theta$. The sine curve
  is the cosine curve shifted right by $\pi/2$.
- **Half turn.** Rotating by $\pi$ maps $(x, y)$ to $(-x, -y)$, so $\cos(\theta + \pi) = -\cos\theta$ and
  $\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta$.
- **Supplementary angles.** Reflecting in the $y$-axis maps $P(\theta)$ to $P(\pi - \theta)$, so
  $\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta$ and $\cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta$.

## The Pythagorean identity

The point $P(\theta)$ lies on the unit circle, so its coordinates satisfy the circle's equation:

$$
\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1.
$$

The notation $\sin^2\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$. Geometrically, the segment from the origin to $P(\theta)$ is the
hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $\abs{\cos\theta}$ and $\abs{\sin\theta}$, and the identity is Pythagoras'
theorem for that triangle. It also bounds both functions: $\abs{\sin\theta} \le 1$ and $\abs{\cos\theta} \le 1$.

## Right triangles

Take a right triangle with an acute angle $\theta$, hypotenuse $c$, the leg opposite $\theta$ of length $a$ and the
leg adjacent to it of length $b$. Place the vertex at $\theta$ on the origin and the adjacent leg along the positive
$x$-axis. The hypotenuse then runs from the origin towards $P(\theta)$, and the triangle is the triangle with vertices
$(0, 0)$, $(\cos\theta, 0)$ and $P(\theta)$ scaled by $c$. Similar triangles have proportional sides, so

$$
a = c\sin\theta, \qquad b = c\cos\theta,
$$

that is,

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}.
$$

The right-triangle definition covers only $0 < \theta < \pi/2$. The unit-circle definition extends it to every real
angle and agrees with it there <Cite id="stewart2016" />. The word sine comes from the Latin _sinus_, a mistranslation
of an Arabic transliteration of the Sanskrit word for a half-chord <Cite id="miller2025" />. Cosine is the sine of the
complementary angle: $\cos\theta = \sin(\pi/2 - \theta)$.

## Sinusoids: amplitude, frequency and phase

A **sinusoid** is a function of time $t$ of the form

$$
y(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi) + c,
$$

with **amplitude** $A > 0$, **angular frequency** $\omega > 0$ in radians per unit time, **phase** $\varphi$ in radians
and offset $c$. The argument $\omega t + \varphi$ grows by $2\pi$ when $t$ grows by $T = 2\pi/\omega$, so the
**period** is $T$ and the **frequency** is $f = 1/T = \omega/(2\pi)$ cycles per unit time. The curve oscillates between
$c - A$ and $c + A$. Changing $\varphi$ shifts it left by $\varphi/\omega$; a cosine is the sinusoid with
$\varphi = \pi/2$. Any sum $a\cos(\omega t) + b\sin(\omega t)$ of a cosine and a sine with the same frequency is again a
sinusoid of that frequency, with amplitude $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$; the proof uses the angle-sum formulas in
<NoteLink to="trigonometric-identities">trigonometric identities</NoteLink>.

## Where sine and cosine appear in machine learning

- **Periodic signals.** A <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink> writes a periodic signal as a sum of
  sinusoids whose frequencies are whole multiples of a base frequency. Seasonality in a time series is modelled the
  same way, with a few sine and cosine terms of the seasonal period.
- **Cyclic features.** An hour of the day $h$ is encoded as $(\cos(2\pi h/24), \sin(2\pi h/24))$, a point on the unit
  circle. Hours 23 and 0 are then neighbours, as they are in time.
- **Positions in a sequence.** The <NoteLink to="sinusoidal-positional-encoding">sinusoidal positional
  encoding</NoteLink> of the original transformer gives position $p$ the coordinates $\sin(p\,\omega_k)$ and
  $\cos(p\,\omega_k)$ for a geometric range of frequencies $\omega_k$ <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.
- **Kernels.** <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">Random Fourier features</NoteLink> approximate a shift-invariant
  kernel with cosines of <NoteLink to="random-projections">random projections</NoteLink> of the input.

## Worked example

Find $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ for $\theta = 2\pi/3$, which is $120°$.

The point $P(2\pi/3)$ is the reflection of $P(\pi/3)$ in the $y$-axis, since $\pi - \pi/3 = 2\pi/3$. Reflection in the
$y$-axis negates $x$ and keeps $y$, so $\cos(2\pi/3) = -1/2$ and $\sin(2\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}/2 \approx 0.8660$. Check:
$1/4 + 3/4 = 1$.

A ladder of length 5 m leans against a wall at $60°$ to the ground. The ladder is the hypotenuse. The top is
$5\sin(\pi/3) = 5\sqrt{3}/2 \approx 4.33$ m above the ground, and the foot is $5\cos(\pi/3) = 2.5$ m from the wall.

## Pitfalls

- **$\sin^2\theta$ and $\sin^{-1}\theta$.** A superscript 2 squares the value, but a superscript $-1$ denotes the
  <NoteLink to="inverse-trigonometric-functions">inverse function</NoteLink> $\arcsin$, not $1/\sin\theta$.
- **Degrees.** Library functions take radians. $\sin(90)$ is $\sin(90\ \text{rad}) \approx 0.894$.
- **Rounding at exact zeros.** In floating point, $\pi$ is not exactly representable, so `sin(math.pi)` returns about
  $1.2 \times 10^{-16}$ rather than 0. Compare with a tolerance.
- **Phase sign.** $\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$ shifts the curve left by $\varphi/\omega$, not right. Sources that write
  $\sin(\omega(t - t_0))$ shift right by $t_0$.
