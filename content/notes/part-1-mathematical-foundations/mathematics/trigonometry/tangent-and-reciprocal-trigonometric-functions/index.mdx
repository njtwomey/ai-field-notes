<Definition>
For an angle $\theta$ in radians, the **tangent**, **secant**, **cosecant** and **cotangent** functions are

$$
\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}, \qquad \sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}, \qquad
\csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}, \qquad \cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}.
$$

$\tan$ and $\sec$ are defined wherever $\cos\theta \ne 0$, that is for $\theta \ne \pi/2 + k\pi$ with $k$ an integer.
$\csc$ and $\cot$ are defined wherever $\sin\theta \ne 0$, that is for $\theta \ne k\pi$ <Cite id="roy2010" />.
</Definition>

## The tangent is a slope

The point at angle $\theta$ on the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit circle</NoteLink> is
$P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. The line through the origin and $P(\theta)$ rises $\sin\theta$ over a run of
$\cos\theta$, so its slope is $\tan\theta$ and its equation is $y = x\tan\theta$. More generally, any non-vertical line
that makes the angle $\alpha$ with the positive $x$-axis has slope $\tan\alpha$, and a line of slope $m$ has inclination
$\alpha = \arctan m$.

A vertical line has no slope. Accordingly $\tan\theta$ is undefined at $\theta = \pi/2 + k\pi$, where $\cos\theta = 0$,
and $\abs{\tan\theta} \to \infty$ as $\theta$ approaches those angles. The graph has a vertical asymptote at each.

Rotating by a half turn negates both coordinates and leaves their ratio unchanged:

$$
\tan(\theta + \pi) = \frac{-\sin\theta}{-\cos\theta} = \tan\theta.
$$

So the tangent has period $\pi$, half the period of sine and cosine. It is odd, $\tan(-\theta) = -\tan\theta$, because
sine is odd and cosine is even.

## Tangent and secant as lengths

The names come from a picture. Draw the **tangent line** to the unit circle at $A = (1, 0)$: the vertical line $x = 1$,
which touches the circle at $A$ only. Extend the line from the origin $O$ through $P(\theta)$ until it meets $x = 1$ at
a point $T$. The triangle $O$, $(\cos\theta, 0)$, $P(\theta)$ and the triangle $O$, $A$, $T$ share the angle $\theta$
at $O$ and both have a right angle, so they are similar. The first has horizontal side $\cos\theta$; the second has
horizontal side 1, so it is the first scaled by $1/\cos\theta$. Its other sides are

$$
AT = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta, \qquad OT = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} = \sec\theta.
$$

The tangent is the length of a segment of the tangent line. The secant is the length of a segment of the line $OT$,
which cuts the circle at $P(\theta)$ and at the opposite point $-P(\theta)$: a **secant line of the circle**, a line
that cuts a curve at two points. The Latin _tangens_ means touching and _secans_ means cutting. Thomas Fincke introduced
_secans_ for the trigonometric quantity in 1583 <Cite id="miller2025" />. The same construction with the horizontal
tangent line $y = 1$ at $(0, 1)$ gives $\cot\theta$ along that line and $\csc\theta$ from the origin, which is why those
two carry the prefix "co": they are the tangent and secant of the complementary angle, $\cot\theta =
\tan(\pi/2 - \theta)$ and $\csc\theta = \sec(\pi/2 - \theta)$.

## Pythagorean identities

Dividing $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ by $\cos^2\theta$ and by $\sin^2\theta$ gives

$$
1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta, \qquad 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta,
$$

wherever the functions are defined. The first is Pythagoras' theorem in the triangle $O$, $A$, $T$, whose legs are 1
and $\tan\theta$ and whose hypotenuse is $\sec\theta$. It also shows $\abs{\sec\theta} \ge 1$ and
$\abs{\csc\theta} \ge 1$: the secant and cosecant never take values in $(-1, 1)$.

## The secant function and the secant line

Calculus uses the word secant in its geometric sense, not for the function $\sec$. A **secant line** of the graph of
$f$ is the line through two of its points, $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$. Its slope is the difference quotient

$$
m = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $f'(a)$ is the limit of this slope as $b \to a$, when the secant
line turns into the tangent line at $a$. The **secant function** $\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta$ is a number attached to
an angle. The two share a Latin root and nothing else: the definition of the derivative does not involve $1/\cos$, and
$\sec\theta$ is not a slope.

The tangent function, by contrast, is connected to slopes. A secant line with slope $m$ makes the angle $\arctan m$ with
the $x$-axis, and the derivative $f'(a)$ is the tangent of the angle that the tangent line at $a$ makes with the
$x$-axis. The figure shows both senses of secant side by side.

## Graphs

- **$\tan$** takes every real value on each interval $(-\pi/2 + k\pi, \pi/2 + k\pi)$ and increases on it.
- **$\cot$** takes every real value on each interval $(k\pi, (k + 1)\pi)$ and decreases on it.
- **$\sec$** has period $2\pi$, is even, and lies in $(-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)$. It has a minimum of 1 at
  $\theta = 2k\pi$ and a maximum of $-1$ at $\theta = (2k + 1)\pi$.
- **$\csc$** is $\sec$ shifted right by $\pi/2$, since $\csc\theta = 1/\sin\theta = 1/\cos(\theta - \pi/2)$.

The derivatives are $\tan' = \sec^2$, $\sec' = \sec\tan$, $\csc' = -\csc\cot$ and $\cot' = -\csc^2$, derived with the
quotient rule in <NoteLink to="derivatives-of-trigonometric-functions">derivatives of the trigonometric
functions</NoteLink>.

## Where they appear

- **Slopes and grades.** A road that rises $5°$ has grade $\tan 5° \approx 0.0875$, or 8.75%.
- **Camera geometry.** In a <NoteLink to="pinhole-camera-model">pinhole
  camera</NoteLink> with focal length $f$ and an image of width $W$, the horizontal field of view $\phi$ satisfies
  $\tan(\phi/2) = W/(2f)$.
- **Heavy tails.** If $\theta$ is uniform on $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, then $\tan\theta$ has the standard
  <NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution">Cauchy distribution</NoteLink>. The tangent maps the bounded angle onto the whole
  line, and the steep ends of its graph produce the heavy tails.

## Worked example

Take $\theta = \pi/3$, where $\cos\theta = 1/2$ and $\sin\theta = \sqrt{3}/2$.

- $\tan(\pi/3) = (\sqrt{3}/2)/(1/2) = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$.
- $\sec(\pi/3) = 2$, $\csc(\pi/3) = 2/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.155$ and $\cot(\pi/3) = 1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.577$.
- Check: $1 + \tan^2(\pi/3) = 1 + 3 = 4 = \sec^2(\pi/3)$.

A line through $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 4)$ has slope $m = 2/3$. Its inclination is $\arctan(2/3) \approx 0.588$ rad
$\approx 33.7°$, and $\tan(33.7°) \approx 0.667$ recovers the slope.

## Pitfalls

- **$\sec$ versus $\arccos$.** $\sec\theta = 1/\cos\theta$ and $\cos^{-1}x = \arccos x$ are different functions. The
  superscript $-1$ denotes the inverse function.
- **Near the asymptotes.** $\pi/2$ is not exactly representable, so `tan(math.pi / 2)` returns about
  $1.6 \times 10^{16}$ rather than an error. Code that relies on $\tan$ being undefined must test the angle itself.
- **Recovering an angle from a slope.** $\arctan m$ returns an angle in $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, a line's inclination. A
  direction, which distinguishes $(1, 1)$ from $(-1, -1)$, needs
  <NoteLink to="inverse-trigonometric-functions">atan2</NoteLink>.
- **Notation.** Some texts write $\operatorname{cosec}$ for $\csc$ and $\operatorname{tg}$ for $\tan$.
