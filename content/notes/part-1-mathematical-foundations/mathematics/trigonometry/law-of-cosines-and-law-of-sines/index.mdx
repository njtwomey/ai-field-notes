<Definition>
Label a triangle's vertices $A$, $B$, $C$, write the same letters for the interior angles there, and let $a$, $b$, $c$
be the lengths of the sides opposite them. The **law of cosines** states

$$
c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C,
$$

and likewise for the other two angles. The **law of sines** states

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R,
$$

where $R$ is the radius of the circumscribed circle, the circle through the three vertices <Cite id="stewart2016" />.
</Definition>

## The law of cosines from coordinates

Place the vertex $C$ at the origin and the side $CB$ along the positive $x$-axis, so $B = (a, 0)$. The vertex $A$ is
at distance $b$ from $C$ in the direction that makes the angle $C$ with the $x$-axis, so
$A = (b\cos C, b\sin C)$. The side $c$ is the distance from $A$ to $B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c^2 &= (b\cos C - a)^2 + (b\sin C)^2 \\
&= b^2\cos^2 C - 2ab\cos C + a^2 + b^2\sin^2 C \\
&= a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C,
\end{aligned}
$$

using $\cos^2 C + \sin^2 C = 1$. The placement covers every triangle, because any triangle can be moved and rotated
into it without changing its sides or angles.

The term $-2ab\cos C$ corrects Pythagoras' theorem for angles other than a right angle.

- **$C = \pi/2$:** $\cos C = 0$ and $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, Pythagoras' theorem.
- **$C$ acute:** $\cos C > 0$, so $c^2 < a^2 + b^2$. The side opposite an acute angle is shorter than the hypotenuse
  of a right angle with the same legs.
- **$C$ obtuse:** $\cos C < 0$, so $c^2 > a^2 + b^2$. Euclid stated this case geometrically, as a rectangle added to
  the two squares <Cite id="euclid1996" />.

Solving for the angle gives $\cos C = (a^2 + b^2 - c^2)/(2ab)$, so three sides determine every angle.

## Vector form and the dot product

Write the sides from $C$ as vectors, $\avec = \overrightarrow{CB}$ and $\bvec = \overrightarrow{CA}$, so that
$\overrightarrow{AB} = \avec - \bvec$ and $\theta = C$ is the angle between $\avec$ and $\bvec$. Expanding the squared
length with the <NoteLink to="dot-product">dot product</NoteLink> gives

$$
\norm{\avec - \bvec}^2 = (\avec - \bvec)^\top(\avec - \bvec) = \norm{\avec}^2 + \norm{\bvec}^2 - 2\,\avec^\top\bvec.
$$

The law of cosines gives the same length as $\norm{\avec}^2 + \norm{\bvec}^2 - 2\norm{\avec}\norm{\bvec}\cos\theta$.
Comparing the two,

$$
\avec^\top\bvec = \norm{\avec}\norm{\bvec}\cos\theta.
$$

This is the geometric meaning of the dot product in the plane and in space, where the angle between two vectors is the
angle of the triangle they span <Cite id="strang2016" />. Read in the other direction, the same expansion computes
distances from inner products, $\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 = \norm{\xvec}^2 + \norm{\yvec}^2 - 2\,\xvec^\top\yvec$. Pairwise
distance matrices are computed this way, as one <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink> plus two
vectors of squared norms. For unit vectors the expansion reduces to $\norm{\xvec - \yvec}^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta$, so
ranking normalised embeddings by Euclidean distance gives the same order as ranking them by
<NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">cosine similarity</NoteLink>.

## The law of sines

Drop the height $h$ from $A$ onto the line through $B$ and $C$. In the right triangle with hypotenuse $b$ and angle $C$,
$h = b\sin C$; in the one with hypotenuse $c$ and angle $B$, $h = c\sin B$. (When an angle is obtuse the foot of the
height falls outside the side, and $\sin(\pi - B) = \sin B$ gives the same result.) The area is half base times
height:

$$
\text{area} = \tfrac12 a h = \tfrac12 ab\sin C = \tfrac12 ac\sin B,
$$

and repeating with another height adds $\tfrac12 bc\sin A$. Dividing the three equal expressions $\tfrac12 bc\sin A$,
$\tfrac12 ac\sin B$, $\tfrac12 ab\sin C$ by $\tfrac12 abc$ gives

$$
\frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c}.
$$

<Derivation title="The common ratio is the diameter of the circumscribed circle">
Let $O$ be the centre of the circle through $A$, $B$, $C$, with radius $R$. Draw the diameter from $B$ through $O$ to
the point $D$ on the circle. By the inscribed angle theorem, angles subtending the same arc are equal, so the angle at
$D$ in the triangle $BCD$ equals the angle $A$ when $A$ and $D$ lie on the same side of $BC$, and equals $\pi - A$
otherwise. An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle, so the triangle $BCD$ has its right angle at $C$, and
$BC = BD\sin(\angle D)$. Since $BD = 2R$ and $\sin(\pi - A) = \sin A$, $a = 2R\sin A$. The same argument at the other
vertices gives $b = 2R\sin B$ and $c = 2R\sin C$.
</Derivation>

## Solving triangles

A triangle is fixed, up to position, by three of its six parts, provided at least one is a side. The two laws recover
the rest.

- **Three sides (SSS).** The law of cosines gives each angle as an $\arccos$.
- **Two sides and the included angle (SAS).** The law of cosines gives the third side.
- **Two angles and a side (ASA, AAS).** The third angle is $\pi$ minus the other two, and the law of sines gives the
  remaining sides.
- **Two sides and a non-included angle (SSA).** The law of sines gives $\sin$ of a second angle, and $\arcsin$ has two
  candidate angles, $\theta$ and $\pi - \theta$. There may be zero, one or two triangles: the ambiguous case.

Measuring angles from two known points to a distant third and solving the triangle is **triangulation**, the basis of
classical surveying. Stereo vision recovers depth the same way, from the angles at which two cameras see the same point
(see <NoteLink to="epipolar-geometry-and-stereo">epipolar geometry and stereo</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

A triangle has $a = 5$, $b = 7$ and $C = 60°$.

- **Third side.** $c^2 = 25 + 49 - 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot \tfrac12 = 39$, so $c = \sqrt{39} \approx 6.245$.
- **Angle A.** $\sin A = a\sin C / c = 5 \times 0.8660 / 6.245 \approx 0.6934$. Side $a$ is the shortest, so $A$ is the
  smallest angle and acute: $A = \arcsin 0.6934 \approx 43.9°$.
- **Angle B.** $B = 180° - 60° - 43.9° = 76.1°$. Check with the law of cosines:
  $\cos B = (a^2 + c^2 - b^2)/(2ac) = (25 + 39 - 49)/(2 \cdot 5 \cdot 6.245) \approx 0.2402$, and
  $\arccos 0.2402 \approx 76.1°$.
- **Circumradius.** $2R = c/\sin C = 6.245/0.8660 \approx 7.211$, so $R \approx 3.606$.
- **Area.** $\tfrac12 ab\sin C = \tfrac12 \cdot 35 \cdot 0.8660 \approx 15.16$.

## Pitfalls

- **$\arcsin$ returns only acute angles.** The law of sines cannot distinguish $B$ from $\pi - B$. Compute the largest
  angle with the law of cosines, or check that the angles sum to $\pi$.
- **Thin triangles.** For a nearly flat triangle, $(a^2 + b^2 - c^2)/(2ab)$ is close to $\pm 1$, where $\arccos$
  loses about half the significant digits. Angles from coordinates are more accurate through $\atantwo$ of the cross and
  dot products, or through Kahan's formula <Cite id="kahan2006" />.
- **Which angle the formula uses.** In $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$, $C$ is the angle between sides $a$ and $b$,
  opposite $c$.
- **Units.** Angles inside $\cos$ and $\sin$ must be in radians when computed by a library.
