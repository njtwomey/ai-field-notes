<Definition>
The **inverse trigonometric functions** are the inverses of $\sin$, $\cos$ and $\tan$ restricted to their **principal
branches**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\arcsin &: [-1, 1] \to [-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}], & \arcsin y &= \theta \iff \sin\theta = y,\ \theta \in [-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}], \\
\arccos &: [-1, 1] \to [0, \pi], & \arccos y &= \theta \iff \cos\theta = y,\ \theta \in [0, \pi], \\
\arctan &: \reals \to (-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}), & \arctan y &= \theta \iff \tan\theta = y,\ \theta \in (-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The **two-argument arctangent** $\atantwo(y, x)$, for $(x, y) \ne (0, 0)$, is the unique $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$ with
$(x, y) = r(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ <Cite id="roy2010" />.
</Definition>

## Why a branch must be chosen

A function has an inverse only if it is one-to-one. Sine is not: $\sin\theta = 1/2$ holds at $\theta = \pi/6$, at
$5\pi/6$, and at every angle that differs from these by a whole number of turns. The inverse function must pick one
answer, and the convention is to restrict $\sin$ to an interval on which it is one-to-one and still takes every value
in $[-1, 1]$.

- **Sine** increases from $-1$ to $1$ on $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$, the right half of the unit circle.
- **Cosine** decreases from $1$ to $-1$ on $[0, \pi]$, the upper half of the unit circle.
- **Tangent** increases from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ on $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, one full period.

The value that the inverse returns is the **principal value**. Every other solution follows from the symmetries of the
function. For an integer $k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin\theta = y &\iff \theta = \arcsin y + 2\pi k \ \text{ or } \ \theta = \pi - \arcsin y + 2\pi k, \\
\cos\theta = y &\iff \theta = \pm\arccos y + 2\pi k, \\
\tan\theta = y &\iff \theta = \arctan y + \pi k.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second family for sine comes from $\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta$; the sign for cosine comes from
$\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$; tangent has a single family because its period is $\pi$.

<PrincipalBranches />

The graph of each inverse is the graph of the restricted function reflected in the line $y = x$, because reflection
swaps inputs and outputs. $\arcsin$ and $\arctan$ are odd and increasing; $\arccos$ is decreasing.

## Identities

- **Complementary angles.** $\arcsin y + \arccos y = \pi/2$ for $y \in [-1, 1]$. The angle
  $\theta = \pi/2 - \arcsin y$ lies in $[0, \pi]$ and has $\cos\theta = \sin(\arcsin y) = y$, so it is $\arccos y$.
- **Reciprocal arguments.** $\arctan y + \arctan(1/y) = \pi/2$ for $y > 0$, and $-\pi/2$ for $y < 0$.
- **Compositions.** $\cos(\arcsin y) = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$, with the positive root because $\cos$ is non-negative on
  $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$. Similarly $\sin(\arccos y) = \sqrt{1 - y^2}$ and $\cos(\arctan y) = 1/\sqrt{1 + y^2}$.
- **Order matters.** $\sin(\arcsin y) = y$ for every $y \in [-1, 1]$, but $\arcsin(\sin\theta) = \theta$ only for
  $\theta \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$. For example $\arcsin(\sin\pi) = \arcsin 0 = 0$.

<Derivation title="Derivatives of the inverse functions">
Let $\theta = \arcsin y$, so $\sin\theta = y$ with $\theta \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$. Differentiating both sides with respect
to $y$ by the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> gives $\cos\theta \cdot \theta' = 1$, so

$$
\frac{d}{dy}\arcsin y = \frac{1}{\cos\theta} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - y^2}}, \qquad -1 < y < 1,
$$

using the composition identity above. The same steps give $\frac{d}{dy}\arccos y = -1/\sqrt{1 - y^2}$. For
$\theta = \arctan y$, $\tan\theta = y$ and $\sec^2\theta \cdot \theta' = 1$, so

$$
\frac{d}{dy}\arctan y = \frac{1}{\sec^2\theta} = \frac{1}{1 + \tan^2\theta} = \frac{1}{1 + y^2}.
$$

The derivatives of $\arcsin$ and $\arccos$ are unbounded near $\pm 1$; the derivative of $\arctan$ is at most 1
everywhere.
</Derivation>

## atan2

The angle of a point $(x, y)$ is not $\arctan(y/x)$. The ratio $y/x$ is the same for $(x, y)$ and $(-x, -y)$, two
opposite directions, so $\arctan$ cannot tell them apart and always answers in the right half-plane,
$(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$. The ratio is also undefined when $x = 0$. The two-argument function keeps both signs. In terms of
$\arctan$,

$$
\atantwo(y, x) = \begin{cases}
\arctan(y/x) & x > 0, \\
\arctan(y/x) + \pi & x < 0,\ y \ge 0, \\
\arctan(y/x) - \pi & x < 0,\ y < 0, \\
\pi/2 & x = 0,\ y > 0, \\
-\pi/2 & x = 0,\ y < 0.
\end{cases}
$$

At the origin the angle is undefined; the C, Python and NumPy libraries return 0 for $\atantwo(0, 0)$ by convention.
Every case follows from the definition: for $x < 0$ the point lies in the left half-plane, opposite the direction that
$\arctan$ reports, so a half turn is added or subtracted to land in $(-\pi, \pi]$ <Cite id="python2025atan2" />.

<Atan2Quadrants />

$\atantwo$ is the standard way to recover an angle from coordinates:

- the heading of a velocity or displacement vector, in tracking, robotics and navigation;
- the argument $\arg z = \atantwo(\operatorname{Im} z, \operatorname{Re} z)$ of a complex number, and so the phase
  of each coefficient of a <NoteLink to="discrete-fourier-transform">discrete Fourier transform</NoteLink>;
- the angle $\varphi$ in the <NoteLink to="polar-coordinates">polar form</NoteLink> of a point, and the phase in the
  harmonic-addition formula $a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta = r\cos(\theta - \varphi)$;
- the circular mean of a set of angles $\theta_i$, $\atantwo\big(\sum_i \sin\theta_i, \sum_i\cos\theta_i\big)$.

## Angles between vectors, computed accurately

The angle between two nonzero vectors $\xvec$ and $\yvec$ is usually written
$\theta = \arccos\big(\xvec^\top\yvec / (\norm{\xvec}\norm{\yvec})\big)$, from the <NoteLink to="dot-product">dot
product</NoteLink>. Near $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi$ this formula loses about half the significant digits. The
derivative of $\arccos$ is unbounded at $\pm 1$, so a <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">rounding error</NoteLink>
of size $\epsilon$ in the cosine becomes an error of size about $\sqrt{2\epsilon}$ in the angle. Rounding can also push
the computed cosine slightly above 1, where $\arccos$ returns NaN. Clipping the cosine to $[-1, 1]$ removes the NaN but
not the lost accuracy <Cite id="kahan2006" />. Kahan recommends

$$
\theta = 2\arctan\frac{\norm{\norm{\yvec}\,\xvec - \norm{\xvec}\,\yvec}}{\norm{\norm{\yvec}\,\xvec + \norm{\xvec}\,\yvec}},
$$

which stays accurate at every angle because $\arctan$ is <NoteLink to="condition-number">well conditioned</NoteLink>
<Cite id="kahan2006" />. In two dimensions, $\theta = \abs{\atantwo(x_1y_2 - x_2y_1, \xvec^\top\yvec)}$ does the same.

## Worked example

Find the angle of the point $(-3, 4)$.

- **Wrong.** $\arctan(4/(-3)) = \arctan(-4/3) \approx -0.9273$ rad $\approx -53.13°$, a direction in the fourth
  quadrant. The point is in the second quadrant.
- **Right.** $x < 0$ and $y \ge 0$, so $\atantwo(4, -3) = -0.9273 + \pi \approx 2.2143$ rad $\approx 126.87°$. Check:
  $r = 5$ and $5(\cos 2.2143, \sin 2.2143) \approx (-3.000, 4.000)$.

Solve $\sin\theta = -1/2$ for $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. The principal value is $\arcsin(-1/2) = -\pi/6$. The two families
are $-\pi/6 + 2\pi k$ and $\pi + \pi/6 + 2\pi k$, so the solutions in $[0, 2\pi)$ are $7\pi/6$ and $11\pi/6$.

## Pitfalls

- **Notation.** $\sin^{-1} y$ means $\arcsin y$, not $1/\sin y = \csc y$. Libraries spell the functions `asin`,
  `acos`, `atan`, and NumPy uses `arcsin`, `arccos`, `arctan`, `arctan2`.
- **Argument order.** C, Python and NumPy take `atan2(y, x)`, $y$ first. Excel's `ATAN2(x_num, y_num)` takes $x$ first
  <Cite id="microsoft2025atan2" />.
- **Domain errors from rounding.** A cosine similarity computed as $1.0000000000000002$ makes `acos` return NaN. Clip
  to $[-1, 1]$ or use the $\arctan$ formula above.
- **The jump at $\pm\pi$.** $\atantwo$ jumps by $2\pi$ when a point crosses the negative $x$-axis. A sequence of phases
  must be unwrapped (`numpy.unwrap`) before it is differentiated or averaged.
- **One solution is not all solutions.** $\arcsin$, $\arccos$ and $\arctan$ return one angle. Equations such as
  $\sin\theta = y$ on a longer interval need the general solutions.
