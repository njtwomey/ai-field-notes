<Definition>
**Hyperbolic space** $\mathbb{H}^d$ is the unique complete, simply connected $d$-dimensional Riemannian manifold
with constant negative sectional curvature $K = -1$.

In the **Poincaré ball model**, hyperbolic space is represented by the open unit ball:
$$
\mathbb{B}^d = \set{\xvec \in \reals^d : \norm{\xvec} < 1},
$$
equipped with the Riemannian metric tensor:
$$
g_\xvec = \lambda_\xvec^2\, \Imat_d, \quad \text{where } \lambda_\xvec = \frac{2}{1 - \norm{\xvec}^2} \text{ is the conformal factor.}
$$
Because the metric is proportional to the identity matrix $\Imat_d$, angles in the model are identical to Euclidean angles (**conformal**),
while infinitesimal distances scale up dramatically as $\norm{\xvec} \to 1$.
</Definition>

## Why trees break Euclidean space

Many real-world datasets exhibit hierarchical or power-law structure: taxonomies like WordNet, ontology graphs,
phylogenetic trees, and organizational networks. In a regular tree with branching factor $b$:

- Number of nodes at tree depth $h$: $N(h) = b^h$ (exponential growth).
- Total nodes up to depth $h$: $\frac{b^{h+1} - 1}{b - 1}$.

In Euclidean space $\reals^d$, the volume of a ball of radius $r$ scales only **polynomially** with radius:
$$
\operatorname{Vol}_{\reals^d}(r) = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)}\, r^d \propto r^d.
$$
To embed a tree such that tree distances $d_T(u, v)$ correspond faithfully to geometric distances $\norm{\uvec - \vvec}_2$,
one must place leaves at radius $r \propto h$. But the surface area in $\reals^d$ grows as $r^{d-1}$, while the number of leaves
grows as $b^h = b^{\alpha r} = e^{r \ln b}$.

Because exponential growth outpaces any polynomial growth, embedding a deep tree into Euclidean space forces leaves to crowd
together arbitrarily close, causing catastrophic **distortion** and destroying relational distance semantics unless the embedding
dimension $d$ is made artificially massive.

In contrast, in hyperbolic space $\mathbb{H}^d$, the volume of a geodesic ball of radius $r$ grows **exponentially**:
$$
\operatorname{Vol}_{\mathbb{H}^d}(r) = 2\pi \int_0^r \sinh^{d-1}(t)\, dt \propto e^{(d-1)r}.
$$
Hyperbolic space has "exponentially more room at the boundary" than Euclidean space. A 2-dimensional hyperbolic disk
accommodates an arbitrarily deep branching tree with constant edge length and bounded distortion <Cite id="nickel2017" />.

## The Poincaré disk model

The Poincaré disk $\mathbb{B}^2 = \set{(x, y) \in \reals^2 : x^2 + y^2 < 1}$ provides an intuitive 2D window into hyperbolic geometry.

### Hyperbolic distance

The geodesic distance between two points $\uvec, \vvec \in \mathbb{B}^d$ is given analytically by:

$$
d_{\mathbb{B}}(\uvec, \vvec) = \operatorname{arcosh}\paren{1 + 2\, \frac{\norm{\uvec - \vvec}^2}{(1 - \norm{\uvec}^2)(1 - \norm{\vvec}^2)}}.
$$

Notice two crucial properties:
1. When $\uvec = \mathbf{0}$, the distance to any point $\vvec$ simplifies to:
   $$
   d_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbf{0}, \vvec) = 2 \operatorname{artanh}(\norm{\vvec}) = \ln\paren{\frac{1 + \norm{\vvec}}{1 - \norm{\vvec}}}.
   $$
   As $\norm{\vvec} \to 1$, the distance $d_{\mathbb{B}}(\mathbf{0}, \vvec) \to \infty$. The Euclidean boundary of the disk is an infinite distance away.
2. Two points near the boundary with a small Euclidean separation $\norm{\uvec - \vvec}$ can have an enormous hyperbolic distance because the denominator $(1 - \norm{\uvec}^2)(1 - \norm{\vvec}^2)$ vanishes quadratically.

### Geodesics

Geodesics in the Poincaré disk are circular arcs that intersect the boundary circle $\partial \mathbb{B} = \set{\xvec : \norm{\xvec} = 1}$
at right angles ($90^\circ$). When a geodesic passes through the origin, it degenerates into a straight Euclidean diameter.

Because geodesics curve inwards toward the center of the disk, the shortest path between two points near the boundary travels
"up" toward the center and back "down"—mirroring the path in a tree graph that traverses up to the lowest common ancestor (LCA).

## Hyperbolic arithmetic: Möbius operations

Because hyperbolic space is curved, standard vector addition $\uvec + \vvec$ is not an isometry and generally maps points outside the disk.
The hyperbolic counterpart to translation is **Möbius addition** <Cite id="chami2019" />:

$$
\uvec \oplus \vvec = \frac{(1 + 2\inner{\uvec, \vvec} + \norm{\vvec}^2)\uvec + (1 - \norm{\uvec}^2)\vvec}{1 + 2\inner{\uvec, \vvec} + \norm{\uvec}^2 \norm{\vvec}^2}.
$$

Möbius addition satisfies:
- $\uvec \oplus \mathbf{0} = \uvec$ and $\uvec \oplus (-\uvec) = \mathbf{0}$.
- It is generally non-commutative: $\uvec \oplus \vvec \neq \vvec \oplus \uvec$.
- It is an isometry of the Poincaré metric: $d_{\mathbb{B}}(\uvec \oplus \xvec, \uvec \oplus \yvec) = d_{\mathbb{B}}(\xvec, \yvec)$.

Using Möbius operations, the **exponential map** $\exp_\xvec: T_\xvec \mathbb{B}^d \to \mathbb{B}^d$ and **logarithmic map**
$\log_\xvec: \mathbb{B}^d \to T_\xvec \mathbb{B}^d$ take closed forms:

$$
\exp_\xvec(\vvec) = \xvec \oplus \paren{\tanh\paren{\frac{\lambda_\xvec \norm{\vvec}}{2}} \frac{\vvec}{\norm{\vvec}}},
$$

$$
\log_\xvec(\yvec) = \frac{2}{\lambda_\xvec}\, \operatorname{artanh}(\norm{-\xvec \oplus \yvec})\, \frac{-\xvec \oplus \yvec}{\norm{-\xvec \oplus \yvec}}.
$$

These maps allow one to perform standard neural operations—such as multi-layer perceptrons, convolutions, and attention mechanisms—directly
on hyperbolic representations by projecting tangent activations back and forth via $\exp$ and $\log$.

## Representation learning in hyperbolic space

Hyperbolic geometry alters how neural embeddings represent relationships:

1. **Poincaré embeddings of taxonomies:** In the seminal work by Nickel & Kiela (2017), WordNet is embedded into a 5-dimensional Poincaré ball. The root (`entity`) settles near the origin $\mathbf{0}$, while specific leaves (`golden retriever`, `poodle`) migrate out towards the boundary $\norm{\xvec} \approx 0.99$. The norm $\norm{\xvec}$ naturally encodes **generality** or depth, while the angular direction encodes **semantics**.
2. **Hyperbolic Graph Neural Networks (HGCN):** Real-world networks frequently exhibit scale-free degree distributions and hierarchical clustering. Graph convolution operations applied directly in hyperbolic space avoid the graph distortion bottleneck typical of Euclidean embeddings <Cite id="chami2019" />.
3. **Riemannian optimization:** Training hyperbolic parameters requires Riemannian SGD (RSGD) or Riemannian Adam. In each step, the Euclidean gradient is scaled by $(1 - \norm{\thetavec}^2)^2 / 4$ and retracted back onto the ball via the exponential map or Möbius addition.
