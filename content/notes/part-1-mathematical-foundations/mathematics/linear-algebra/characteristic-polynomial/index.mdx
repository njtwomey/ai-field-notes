<Definition>
The **characteristic polynomial** of a square matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is

$$
p_{\Amat}(\lambda) = \det(\lambda\eye - \Amat).
$$

It is a polynomial in $\lambda$ of degree $n$ with leading coefficient 1. The equation $p_{\Amat}(\lambda) = 0$ is the
**characteristic equation**, and its roots are the eigenvalues of $\Amat$ <Cite id="strang2016" />.
</Definition>

## Why the roots are the eigenvalues

A number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $\Amat$ when $\Amat\vvec = \lambda\vvec$ for some nonzero vector $\vvec$, an
eigenvector. The equation rearranges to $(\lambda\eye - \Amat)\vvec = \zeros$. A nonzero solution exists exactly when
$\lambda\eye - \Amat$ is singular, and a square matrix is singular exactly when its
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is zero. So $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue if and only if
$p_{\Amat}(\lambda) = 0$.

The degree is $n$ because of how the determinant is expanded. The determinant is a sum of products, each taking one
entry from every row and every column. Only the product of the diagonal entries,
$(\lambda - a_{11})(\lambda - a_{22})\cdots(\lambda - a_{nn})$, contains $\lambda$ $n$ times, and it contributes
$\lambda^n$ with coefficient 1. Some texts define the polynomial as $\det(\Amat - \lambda\eye)$, which equals
$(-1)^n p_{\Amat}(\lambda)$ and has the same roots.

A real polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ roots over the complex numbers, counted with multiplicity, and the complex ones
come in conjugate pairs. So every $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues in $\complex$, and a real matrix can have
none that are real: the rotation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + 1$ and
eigenvalues $\pm i$.

## Coefficients: from the trace to the determinant

Over the complex numbers the polynomial factors through its roots,
$p_{\Amat}(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_n)$. Expanding the product and
expanding the determinant give two expressions for each coefficient:

$$
p_{\Amat}(\lambda) = \lambda^n - c_1\lambda^{n-1} + c_2\lambda^{n-2} - \dots + (-1)^n c_n,
$$

where $c_k$ is the sum of all products of $k$ distinct eigenvalues, and also the sum of the determinants of all
$k \times k$ principal submatrices of $\Amat$ (those that keep the same $k$ rows as columns) <Cite id="horn2012" />. The
two ends are the familiar cases.

- **$k = 1$:** $c_1 = \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n = \trace(\Amat)$. In the determinant, only the diagonal product
  contains $\lambda^{n-1}$, and its $\lambda^{n-1}$ coefficient is $-(a_{11} + \dots + a_{nn})$.
- **$k = n$:** $c_n = \lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n = \det\Amat$. Setting $\lambda = 0$ gives
  $p_{\Amat}(0) = \det(-\Amat) = (-1)^n\det\Amat$.

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix this is the whole polynomial, $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \trace(\Amat)\,\lambda + \det\Amat$,
with roots $\tfrac{1}{2}\trace(\Amat) \pm \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}\trace(\Amat)^2 - \det\Amat}$. The eigenvalues are real
when $\trace(\Amat)^2 \ge 4\det\Amat$ and a complex-conjugate pair otherwise.

## Similar matrices share it

Matrices $\Amat$ and $\Bmat = \Smat^{-1}\Amat\Smat$, for an invertible $\Smat$, describe the same
<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink> in two bases. They have the same characteristic polynomial:

$$
\det(\lambda\eye - \Smat^{-1}\Amat\Smat) = \det\big(\Smat^{-1}(\lambda\eye - \Amat)\Smat\big)
= \det(\Smat^{-1})\det(\lambda\eye - \Amat)\det(\Smat) = \det(\lambda\eye - \Amat).
$$

The middle step uses $\lambda\eye = \Smat^{-1}(\lambda\eye)\Smat$ and the product rule for determinants. The
eigenvalues, the trace and the determinant are therefore properties of the map, not of the basis. The converse fails:
$\eye_2$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ both have $p(\lambda) = (\lambda - 1)^2$, but only the
identity is similar to the identity.

## Algebraic and geometric multiplicity

The **algebraic multiplicity** of an eigenvalue is its multiplicity as a root of $p_{\Amat}$. The **geometric
multiplicity** is the number of independent eigenvectors it has, the dimension of the
<NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">null space</NoteLink> of $\Amat - \lambda\eye$. The geometric multiplicity is at
least 1 and at most the algebraic one <Cite id="horn2012" />.

The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue 1 with algebraic multiplicity 2. Its
$\Amat - \eye = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has rank 1, so the geometric multiplicity is 1. A matrix
has a basis of eigenvectors, and so an <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>, exactly when the
two multiplicities agree for every eigenvalue. A matrix whose $n$ eigenvalues are distinct always does.

## The Cayley–Hamilton theorem

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation <Cite id="horn2012" />:

$$
p_{\Amat}(\Amat) = \Amat^n - c_1\Amat^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n c_n\eye = \zeros.
$$

For a diagonalisable matrix $\Amat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^{-1}$, every power is $\Amat^k = \Vmat\Lambdamat^k\Vmat^{-1}$,
so $p_{\Amat}(\Amat) = \Vmat\,p_{\Amat}(\Lambdamat)\,\Vmat^{-1}$. The diagonal matrix $p_{\Amat}(\Lambdamat)$ has
entries $p_{\Amat}(\lambda_i) = 0$, so $p_{\Amat}(\Amat) = \zeros$.

<Derivation title="Extending the proof to every matrix">
Matrices with $n$ distinct eigenvalues are dense: every $\Amat$ is a limit of them. The Schur decomposition writes
$\Amat = \Qmat\Tmat\Qmat^{*}$ with $\Qmat$ unitary and $\Tmat$ upper triangular, the eigenvalues of $\Amat$ on the
diagonal of $\Tmat$. Adding $\Dmat/m$ to $\Tmat$, for a diagonal $\Dmat$ with distinct entries, gives matrices
$\Amat_m = \Qmat(\Tmat + \Dmat/m)\Qmat^{*}$ whose eigenvalues are distinct for all large $m$ and which tend to $\Amat$.
Each $\Amat_m$ is diagonalisable, so $p_{\Amat_m}(\Amat_m) = \zeros$. The coefficients of $p_{\Amat}$ are polynomials in
the entries of $\Amat$, so $p_{\Amat}(\Amat)$ is a continuous function of $\Amat$, and the limit gives
$p_{\Amat}(\Amat) = \zeros$.
</Derivation>

The theorem makes $\Amat^n$ a combination of $\eye, \Amat, \dots, \Amat^{n-1}$, and so every higher power too. Any
polynomial in $\Amat$ reduces to one of degree below $n$. When $\det\Amat \ne 0$, multiplying the equation by
$\Amat^{-1}$ gives the <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">inverse</NoteLink> as such a polynomial:

$$
\Amat^{-1} = \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{\det\Amat}\left(\Amat^{n-1} - c_1\Amat^{n-2} + \dots + (-1)^{n-1}c_{n-1}\eye\right).
$$

## Every polynomial is a characteristic polynomial

For any polynomial $q(\lambda) = \lambda^n + a_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + a_1\lambda + a_0$, the **companion matrix**

$$
\Cmat = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_0 \\
1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_1 \\
0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & -a_2 \\
\vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

has $p_{\Cmat} = q$ <Cite id="horn2012" />. Finding the roots of a polynomial and finding the eigenvalues of a matrix
are therefore the same problem. A linear recurrence $x_t = -a_{n-1}x_{t-1} - \dots - a_0 x_{t-n}$ becomes a first-order
system when its last $n$ values are stacked into a state vector, with a transition matrix whose characteristic
polynomial is $q$. The recurrence is stable when every root of $q$ lies inside the <NoteLink to="sine-and-cosine">unit
circle</NoteLink>. For an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">autoregressive model</NoteLink> this is the stationarity
condition, often stated for the reversed polynomial $1 + a_{n-1}z + \dots + a_0z^n$, whose roots are the reciprocals and
must lie outside the unit circle.

## Worked example

Take

$$
\Amat = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The coefficients come from the principal submatrices.

- $c_1 = \trace(\Amat) = 2 + 3 + 2 = 7$.
- $c_2$ adds the three $2 \times 2$ principal minors: rows and columns $\{1, 2\}$ give $2 \cdot 3 - 0 = 6$,
  $\{1, 3\}$ give $2 \cdot 2 - 1 = 3$, and $\{2, 3\}$ give $3 \cdot 2 - 0 = 6$, so $c_2 = 15$.
- $c_3 = \det\Amat$. Expanding along the second row gives $3\,(2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1) = 9$.

So $p_{\Amat}(\lambda) = \lambda^3 - 7\lambda^2 + 15\lambda - 9 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3)^2$. The eigenvalues are 1
and 3, with 3 a double root. The checks hold: $1 + 3 + 3 = 7$, $1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 3 = 15$ and
$1 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 9$.

The double root has algebraic multiplicity 2. The matrix

$$
\Amat - 3\eye = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

has rank 1, so its null space has dimension 2, spanned by $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$. The geometric multiplicity is
also 2, and $\Amat$ is diagonalisable. Cayley–Hamilton gives $\Amat^3 = 7\Amat^2 - 15\Amat + 9\eye$, and the inverse
$\Amat^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{9}(\Amat^2 - 7\Amat + 15\eye)$.

## Pitfalls

- **Computing eigenvalues through the polynomial.** The roots of a polynomial can move far when its coefficients change
  slightly, so forming $p_{\Amat}$ and solving it loses accuracy. Numerical software computes eigenvalues by the
  <Gloss name="qr" /> algorithm instead <Cite id="golub2013" />, and finds polynomial roots the other way round, as
  the eigenvalues of the companion matrix <Cite id="edelman1995" />.
- **Sign conventions.** $\det(\Amat - \lambda\eye)$ and $\det(\lambda\eye - \Amat)$ differ by $(-1)^n$. The roots
  agree, but formulas for the coefficients change sign.
- **The polynomial does not determine the matrix up to basis.** Equal characteristic polynomials give equal
  eigenvalues, trace and determinant, but the matrices need not be similar, as the identity and the shear above show.
- **A double root does not mean too few eigenvectors.** A repeated eigenvalue can still have a full set, as in the
  worked example and in $\eye$. Only the geometric multiplicity decides.
