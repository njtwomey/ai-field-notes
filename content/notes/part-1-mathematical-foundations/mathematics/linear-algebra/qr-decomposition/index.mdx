<Definition>
The (thin) QR decomposition of $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ with $m \ge n$ is

$$
\Amat = \Qmat\Rmat,
$$

where $\Qmat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ has orthonormal columns, $\Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$, and
$\Rmat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ is upper triangular. If $\Amat$ has linearly independent columns, $\Rmat$ is invertible
and the factorisation with a positive diagonal in $\Rmat$ is unique <Cite id="trefethen1997" />.
</Definition>

The full QR decomposition extends $\Qmat$ to a square orthogonal matrix in $\reals^{m \times m}$ and pads $\Rmat$
with $m - n$ zero rows. The extra columns of $\Qmat$ are an orthonormal basis of the orthogonal complement of the
<NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">column space</NoteLink>.

## What the factors mean

Column $j$ of $\Amat$ is $\avec_j = \sum_{i \le j} r_{ij}\qvec_i$. $\Qmat$ is an orthonormal basis for the column
space of $\Amat$, and column $j$ of $\Rmat$ holds the coordinates of $\avec_j$ in that basis. $\Rmat$ is triangular
because the first $j$ columns of $\Amat$ span the same space as the first $j$ columns of $\Qmat$. The
<NoteLink to="gram-schmidt">Gram–Schmidt process</NoteLink> constructs exactly these factors, which proves that the
decomposition exists whenever the columns are independent.

<Derivation title="Why the factorisation is unique">
Suppose $\Amat = \Qmat_1\Rmat_1 = \Qmat_2\Rmat_2$, both with positive diagonals. Then
$\Amat^\top\Amat = \Rmat_1^\top\Rmat_1 = \Rmat_2^\top\Rmat_2$, using $\Qmat^\top\Qmat = \eye$. Rearranging gives
$\Rmat_1\Rmat_2^{-1} = \Rmat_1^{-\top}\Rmat_2^\top$. The left side is upper triangular and the right side lower
triangular, so both are a diagonal matrix $\Dmat$. Then $\Rmat_1 = \Dmat\Rmat_2$ and
$\Rmat_1^\top\Rmat_1 = \Rmat_2^\top\Dmat^2\Rmat_2 = \Rmat_2^\top\Rmat_2$, so $\Dmat^2 = \eye$. Positive diagonals force
$\Dmat = \eye$, so $\Rmat_1 = \Rmat_2$ and $\Qmat_1 = \Amat\Rmat_1^{-1} = \Qmat_2$. The same argument shows that $\Rmat$
is the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky factor</NoteLink> of $\Amat^\top\Amat$.
</Derivation>

## Least squares

To minimise $\norm{\Amat\xvec - \bvec}^2$, split $\bvec$ into its part in the column space, $\Qmat\Qmat^\top\bvec$, and
the rest, $(\eye - \Qmat\Qmat^\top)\bvec$. Substituting $\Amat = \Qmat\Rmat$ splits the residual the same way:

$$
\Amat\xvec - \bvec = \underbrace{\Qmat(\Rmat\xvec - \Qmat^\top\bvec)}_{\text{in the column space}}
- \underbrace{(\eye - \Qmat\Qmat^\top)\bvec}_{\text{orthogonal to it}}.
$$

The two pieces are orthogonal, because $\Qmat^\top(\eye - \Qmat\Qmat^\top) = \Qmat^\top - \Qmat^\top = \zeros$.
Pythagoras applies, and $\norm{\Qmat\yvec} = \norm{\yvec}$ because $\Qmat$ has orthonormal columns, so

$$
\norm{\Amat\xvec - \bvec}^2 = \norm{\Rmat\xvec - \Qmat^\top\bvec}^2 + \norm{(\eye - \Qmat\Qmat^\top)\bvec}^2.
$$

The second term does not depend on $\xvec$. The first is zero when

$$
\Rmat\xvec = \Qmat^\top\bvec,
$$

which <NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">back substitution</NoteLink> solves. $\Qmat\Qmat^\top\bvec$ is the
<NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> of $\bvec$ onto the column space, and the
minimum residual norm is the length of the part of $\bvec$ outside it.

The normal equations $\Amat^\top\Amat\xvec = \Amat^\top\bvec$ give the same solution in exact arithmetic. Their matrix
has condition number $\kappa(\Amat)^2$, so they lose twice as many digits. The QR route works with $\Rmat$, whose
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> equals $\kappa(\Amat)$: $\norm{\Amat\xvec} =
\norm{\Qmat\Rmat\xvec} = \norm{\Rmat\xvec}$ for every $\xvec$, so $\Amat$ and $\Rmat$ have the same
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular values</NoteLink> <Cite id="higham2002" />. For a square invertible
$\Amat$ the same steps solve $\Amat\xvec = \bvec$ without forming the <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">inverse</NoteLink>:
$\Rmat\xvec = \Qmat^\top\bvec$.

## Worked example

Let

$$
\Amat = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 4 & -1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\bvec = \begin{bmatrix} 11 \\ -2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Factorise.** The first column has length 5, so $\qvec_1 = \tfrac{1}{5}(3, 4, 0)$ and $r_{11} = 5$. Then
$r_{12} = \qvec_1^\top\avec_2 = \tfrac{1}{5}(9 - 4) = 1$. Removing that component leaves
$\avec_2 - \qvec_1 = \tfrac{1}{5}(12, -9, 20)$, whose length is $r_{22} = 5$. So
$\qvec_2 = \tfrac{1}{25}(12, -9, 20)$ and

$$
\Rmat = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

**Solve.** $\Qmat^\top\bvec = \paren{\tfrac{33 - 8}{5}, \tfrac{132 + 18 + 100}{25}} = (5, 10)$. Back substitution on
$\Rmat\xvec = (5, 10)$ gives $x_2 = 2$ and $5x_1 + 2 = 5$, so $\xvec = (0.6, 2)$.

**Check.** $\Amat\xvec = (7.8, 0.4, 8)$, so the residual is $\bvec - \Amat\xvec = (3.2, -2.4, -3)$. It is orthogonal to
both columns of $\Amat$: $3 \cdot 3.2 - 4 \cdot 2.4 = 0$ and $3 \cdot 3.2 + 2.4 - 12 = 0$. Its length, 5, is the
smallest residual any $\xvec$ can achieve.

## Computing QR

- **Householder reflections** zero the entries below the diagonal one column at a time, each with an orthogonal
  reflection. They are backward stable and are what LAPACK and NumPy use. The cost is about $2mn^2 - \tfrac{2}{3}n^3$
  operations <Cite id="golub2013" />.
- **Givens rotations** zero one entry at a time. They suit sparse or structured matrices and updates when a row is
  added.
- **Gram–Schmidt** builds $\Qmat$ column by column. Its classical form can lose orthogonality in floating point; the
  modified form is better but still depends on the condition number.

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="linear-regression">Linear regression</NoteLink>.** Solving the least-squares problem through QR of the
  design matrix is the standard method in statistical software. It avoids forming $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$.
- **Orthogonalisation.** QR of a random Gaussian matrix gives a random orthogonal matrix, used to initialise weights.
  Randomised <Gloss name="svd" /> uses QR to orthonormalise a sketch of the column space.
- **Eigenvalues.** The QR algorithm, which repeatedly factors and recombines $\Amat_{k+1} = \Rmat_k\Qmat_k$, is the
  classical method for computing all eigenvalues of a dense matrix.

## Pitfalls

- **Rank deficiency.** If the columns of $\Amat$ are dependent, some $r_{jj}$ is zero and back substitution fails.
  QR with column pivoting orders the columns so that small $r_{jj}$ appear last, which reveals the numerical rank. The
  <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink> then gives the minimum-norm solution.
- **Signs.** $\Qmat$ and $\Rmat$ are unique only up to the signs of the columns of $\Qmat$ and rows of $\Rmat$.
  Libraries may return a negative diagonal in $\Rmat$.
- **Thin versus full.** The full $\Qmat$ is $m \times m$. For tall data it costs far more memory than the thin
  $m \times n$ factor, which is all least squares needs.
