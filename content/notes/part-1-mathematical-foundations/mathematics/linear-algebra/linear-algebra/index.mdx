<Definition>
Linear algebra studies vectors, the spaces they span and the linear maps between those spaces. A matrix
$\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ represents a linear map from $\reals^n$ to $\reals^m$, and every question about the
map becomes a question about the matrix. Its results describe what a matrix does geometrically and how to compute with
it stably <Cite id="strang2016" /> <Cite id="golub2013" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Data sets are matrices with one row per example. A linear layer of a neural network is a matrix-vector product, and a
batch of examples turns it into a matrix product. Least-squares regression is an orthogonal projection. Principal
component analysis is an eigendecomposition of a <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">covariance matrix</NoteLink>, or
a singular value decomposition of the centred data. Gaussian likelihoods need log-determinants and solves with
covariance matrices, which a Cholesky factor provides. Attention scores are dot products. The condition number of the
<NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> sets how fast <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>
converges. Kernel methods replace dot products with a kernel function and work with the Gram matrix of all pairs.

## Main ideas

**Vectors have length and angle.** The <NoteLink to="dot-product">dot product</NoteLink> measures
alignment and gives cosine similarity. <NoteLink to="vector-norms" /> measure length, with $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$ and
$\ell_\infty$ as the common cases. The <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">span</NoteLink> of a set of vectors
is everything they can combine to reach, and an independent spanning set is a basis.

**A matrix is a map.** The <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">matrix–vector product</NoteLink> combines the columns of
a matrix with weights from the vector. <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication" /> composes two maps. The
<NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">rank</NoteLink> counts the output dimensions a map reaches, and the null space holds
the inputs it sends to zero. The <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is the factor by which the map scales
volume. The <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">matrix inverse</NoteLink> exists exactly when that factor is nonzero.

**Orthogonality gives closest points.** An <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal projection</NoteLink> finds
the closest point of a subspace, and least squares is such a projection. The <NoteLink to="gram-schmidt" /> process
builds an orthonormal basis, and the <NoteLink to="qr-decomposition" /> records it as a factorisation that solves
least-squares problems stably.

**Eigenvectors are the directions a matrix only scales.** The roots of the
<NoteLink to="characteristic-polynomial">characteristic polynomial</NoteLink> are the eigenvalues, and the
<NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink> turns powers and exponentials of a matrix into scalar
operations along its eigenvectors. The <NoteLink to="spectral-theorem">spectral theorem</NoteLink> guarantees real
eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors for a symmetric matrix. <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices" /> are the
symmetric matrices with positive eigenvalues: covariances, Gram matrices and the Hessians of strictly convex functions.

**Factorisations make computation cheap and stable.** The <NoteLink to="lower-upper-decomposition">LU
decomposition</NoteLink> records Gaussian elimination. The <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition" /> factors a positive
definite matrix as $\Lmat\Lmat^\top$. The <NoteLink to="schur-complement" /> eliminates a block of variables and is the
covariance of a Gaussian after conditioning, and <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">completing the square</NoteLink>
reads off Gaussian means and covariances. The <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value
decomposition</NoteLink> factors any matrix into rotation, scaling and rotation, gives the best low-rank approximation
and defines the <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse" />.

**Size and sensitivity.** <NoteLink to="matrix-norms" /> measure how much a matrix can stretch a vector. The
<NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> bounds how much a relative error in the data can grow in the
solution of a linear system. A condition number of $10^k$ can cost about $k$ significant digits.

**Linear methods in feature spaces.** The <NoteLink to="kernel-trick">kernel trick</NoteLink> runs a linear method in a
high-dimensional feature space through a kernel function alone. <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features" /> approximate a
shift-invariant kernel with explicit random cosine features, so that kernel methods scale linearly in the number of
examples.

## How the notes fit together

Read in the listed order. The first eight notes, from the dot product to the inverse, build the language of vectors and
maps that every other note uses. The orthogonality notes, from projection to QR, can be read before or after the
eigenvalue notes. The eigenvalue notes lead to positive definite matrices, which the Cholesky decomposition, the Schur
complement and completing the square need. The singular value decomposition draws on both orthogonality and
eigenvalues, and the pseudoinverse, matrix norms and condition number build on it. The two kernel notes come last and
need only positive definite matrices.
