<Definition>
The calculus of variations optimises over functions instead of numbers. Its objects
are functionals, maps from a whole function to a number, such as $J[y] = \int L(x, y, y')\,dx$. Its central result, the
Euler–Lagrange equation, turns the condition for a stationary functional into a
<NoteLink to="differential-equations">differential equation</NoteLink> for the unknown function
<Cite id="gelfand1963" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Many quantities in machine learning are functionals. The entropy, the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the
<Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower bound</Gloss> map a density to a number. <Gloss name="vi">Variational
inference</Gloss> maximises the evidence lower bound over a family of densities, and the mean-field update is the
stationary point of a functional. The Gaussian, exponential and other exponential families are the densities that
maximise entropy under moment constraints. <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">Smoothing splines</NoteLink> minimise a
penalised functional, and the optimal-control equations of <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss> and
<NoteLink to="control-theory">control theory</NoteLink> descend from the same variational principle.

## Main ideas

**Functions have gradients too.** A <NoteLink to="functionals-and-functional-derivatives">functional</NoteLink> maps a
function to a number. Its functional derivative $\delta F / \delta f$ is the function whose
<NoteLink to="dot-product">inner product</NoteLink> with a small perturbation $\eta$ gives the first-order change in
$F$. Setting it to zero is the analogue of setting a gradient to zero.

**Stationary integrals satisfy a differential equation.** For $J[y] = \int L(x, y, y')\,dx$ with fixed end values, the
<NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation" /> $\partial L / \partial y - \frac{d}{dx}\,\partial L / \partial y' = 0$ holds
along every stationary $y$. The optimisation over an infinite-dimensional space
becomes a boundary-value problem.

**The founding problem has a cycloid for its answer.** The <NoteLink to="brachistochrone-problem">brachistochrone
problem</NoteLink>, posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1696, asks which curve carries a bead between two points in least time.
Its solution by the Euler–Lagrange equation is a cycloid.

**Constraints enter through multipliers.** In <NoteLink to="constrained-variational-problems">constrained variational
problems</NoteLink>, each integral constraint, such as a density integrating to 1, is added to the integrand with a
Lagrange multiplier. The Euler–Lagrange equation is then applied to the combined integrand, exactly as in
finite-dimensional <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers" />.

**Maximum entropy gives exponential families.** The density with the largest entropy subject to fixed
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> $\expect[T_i(X)]$ has the form
$p(x) \propto \exp\paren{\sum_i \lambda_i T_i(x)}$. The note on
<NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-distributions">maximum-entropy distributions</NoteLink> derives the uniform, exponential
and Gaussian distributions this way.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by
parts</NoteLink>, which moves the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> off the perturbation in the
Euler–Lagrange derivation, and for the last note the <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence" />.

The finite-dimensional counterpart is <NoteLink to="constrained-optimisation">constrained optimisation</NoteLink>. The
<NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> note studies the distributions that maximum entropy
produces. Optimal control extends the Euler–Lagrange equation to controlled systems through the
<NoteLink to="pontryagin-maximum-principle">Pontryagin's maximum principle</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="hamilton-jacobi-bellman-equation" />. Variational inference applies the same calculus to approximate
posteriors.
