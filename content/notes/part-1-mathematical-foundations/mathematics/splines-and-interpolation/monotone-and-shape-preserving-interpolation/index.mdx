<Definition>
An interpolant is **shape-preserving** if it keeps a qualitative property of the data: monotone data gives a monotone
curve, and the curve stays within the range of neighbouring data. **PCHIP** is the piecewise cubic Hermite
interpolant whose slopes are chosen so that monotone data give a monotone $C^1$ curve <Cite id="fritsch1980" />
<Cite id="fritsch1984" />. **Akima's method** chooses slopes by a local weighted average designed to avoid wiggles
without solving a global system <Cite id="akima1970" />.
</Definition>

## Why cubic splines overshoot

A <NoteLink to="cubic-spline-interpolation">cubic spline</NoteLink> has a continuous second
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>. On a step such as $y = 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1$ at $x = 0, 1, \dots, 5$, the
spline must bend upward to climb the step and bend back to level off, and $C^2$ forbids it from doing either abruptly.
It spreads the bending into the flat parts, which it can only do by dipping below 0 before the step and rising above 1
after it. The natural spline reaches $-0.109$ and $1.109$: an overshoot of 10.9% of the data range. The slopes at each
node are set by the whole data set through the tridiagonal system, so a single step causes ripples that decay only
geometrically away from it.

Overshoot matters when the values have a meaning that forbids it: a <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution
function</Gloss> must not decrease, a concentration must not go negative, a lookup table for a monotone physical law
must stay monotone.

## When a Hermite piece is monotone

A cubic Hermite piece on $[x_i, x_{i+1}]$ has values $y_i < y_{i+1}$, slopes $d_i, d_{i+1}$, width $h$ and chord slope
$\Delta = (y_{i+1} - y_i)/h > 0$. Rescale to $s \in [0, 1]$ and $q = (y - y_i)/(y_{i+1} - y_i)$. The piece becomes
$q(0) = 0$, $q(1) = 1$ with slopes $\alpha = d_i/\Delta$ and $\beta = d_{i+1}/\Delta$. From the Hermite basis,

$$
q(s) = (-2s^3 + 3s^2) + \alpha(s^3 - 2s^2 + s) + \beta(s^3 - s^2),
$$

$$
q'(s) = 6s(1 - s) + \alpha(1 - s)(1 - 3s) + \beta\,s(3s - 2).
$$

The piece is monotone iff $q'(s) \ge 0$ on $[0, 1]$. Since $q'(0) = \alpha$ and $q'(1) = \beta$, the condition
$\alpha, \beta \ge 0$ is necessary.

**Sufficient condition: $0 \le \alpha, \beta \le 3$.** For fixed $s$, $q'(s)$ is an affine function of
$(\alpha, \beta)$. The set of $(\alpha, \beta)$ with $q'(s) \ge 0$ for one $s$ is a half-plane. The set with
$q'(s) \ge 0$ for all $s$ is an intersection of half-planes, hence convex. A
<NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex set</NoteLink> that contains the four corners of the square $[0, 3]^2$
contains the whole square. Check the corners:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\alpha, \beta) = (0, 0):&\quad q' = 6s(1 - s) \ge 0, \\
(3, 0):&\quad q' = 6s - 6s^2 + 3(1 - 4s + 3s^2) = 3(1 - s)^2 \ge 0, \\
(0, 3):&\quad q' = 6s - 6s^2 + 3(3s^2 - 2s) = 3s^2 \ge 0, \\
(3, 3):&\quad q' = 12s^2 - 12s + 3 = 3(2s - 1)^2 \ge 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

So every slope pair in $[0, 3]^2$ gives a monotone piece. Fritsch and Carlson characterised the full monotone region,
which is larger: it adds a region bounded by an ellipse beyond the square <Cite id="fritsch1980" />. The square is
the convenient part because it constrains each slope on its own, so slopes can be chosen one node at a time. A node's
slope enters two pieces, and each piece only asks that the slope be at most 3 times its own chord slope.

If $\Delta = 0$, monotonicity forces $d_i = d_{i+1} = 0$, and the piece is flat.

## PCHIP slopes

PCHIP sets the slope at an interior node to zero if the neighbouring chord slopes $\Delta_{i-1}$ and $\Delta_i$ differ
in sign or either is zero: the data has a local extremum there, and the curve should too. Otherwise it uses a weighted
harmonic mean <Cite id="fritsch1984" />:

$$
\frac{w_1 + w_2}{d_i} = \frac{w_1}{\Delta_{i-1}} + \frac{w_2}{\Delta_i}, \qquad
w_1 = 2h_i + h_{i-1}, \quad w_2 = h_i + 2h_{i-1}.
$$

**The slopes meet the sufficient condition.** Take both chord slopes positive. Dropping the second term on the right
can only increase $d_i$, so $d_i \le \frac{w_1 + w_2}{w_1}\Delta_{i-1}$. With $w_1 + w_2 = 3(h_{i-1} + h_i)$ and
$w_1 = h_{i-1} + 2h_i \ge h_{i-1} + h_i$, this gives $d_i \le 3\Delta_{i-1}$. The same argument with the first term
dropped gives $d_i \le 3\Delta_i$. So $d_i/\Delta \in [0, 3]$ for both pieces that meet at node $i$. With equal
spacing the weights are equal and $d_i = 2\Delta_{i-1}\Delta_i/(\Delta_{i-1} + \Delta_i)$, which is at most
$2\min(\Delta_{i-1}, \Delta_i)$.

At the two end nodes scipy uses a three-point one-sided estimate, set to zero if its sign differs from the first chord
slope and capped at 3 times that slope if the first two chord slopes differ in sign. The end slopes then also satisfy
the condition. Every piece is monotone where the data are, and the curve has an extremum only at data nodes where the
data have one.

The harmonic mean is small when either chord slope is small. Near a flat stretch the curve flattens, which is what
keeps it from overshooting. The price is that PCHIP is only $C^1$, and its curvature jumps at the nodes.

## Akima's method

Akima's slope at node $i$ is a weighted average of the two chord slopes on either side,

$$
d_i = \frac{\abs{\Delta_{i+1} - \Delta_i}\,\Delta_{i-1} + \abs{\Delta_{i-1} - \Delta_{i-2}}\,\Delta_i}
{\abs{\Delta_{i+1} - \Delta_i} + \abs{\Delta_{i-1} - \Delta_{i-2}}},
$$

with two extra chord slopes extrapolated linearly at each end <Cite id="akima1970" />. The weight on each side is how
much the chord slopes change on the other side. If the data are straight over the two intervals to the right,
$\Delta_{i+1} = \Delta_i$, the slope is exactly that chord slope. This makes the curve follow straight runs and keeps
wiggles local. The **modified Akima** (makima) variant adds $\abs{\Delta_{i+1} + \Delta_i}/2$ to each weight to handle
flat regions.

Akima's slopes are not constrained to $[0, 3]\Delta$, so monotone data can give a non-monotone curve. For
$y = 0, 0.5, 0.6, 2, 2.1, 4, 4.1, 4.2$ at $x = 0, \dots, 7$, Akima's slopes at $x = 1$ and $x = 2$ are both 0.406,
against a chord slope of 0.1 between them: $\alpha = \beta = 4.06$. The curve rises to 0.556 at $x \approx 1.33$ and
then falls. PCHIP gives slopes 0.167 and 0.187 there and stays monotone.

**Worked example.** For the step data $y = 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1$, every node has a zero chord slope on at least one side.
PCHIP sets every slope to zero, and the curve is flat, then an S-shaped cubic from $(2, 0)$ to $(3, 1)$, then flat
again. Its range is exactly $[0, 1]$. Akima's weights also give zero slopes here.

## Trade-offs

- **Smoothness.** PCHIP and Akima are $C^1$. A cubic spline is $C^2$. For smooth data the spline is more accurate:
  PCHIP's error is $O(h^3)$ at best, and lower near extrema where it forces zero slopes.
- **Locality.** Both methods compute each slope from at most five nearby points. Changing one data value changes the
  curve only nearby, and no linear system is solved.
- **Not linear in the data.** Scaling the data scales a PCHIP interpolant, as it does a spline. Adding two data sets
  does not in general add their PCHIP interpolants, because the slope rules involve signs and harmonic means.
- **Related ideas.** For noisy monotone data, fit rather than interpolate: <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic
  regression</NoteLink> or <NoteLink to="shape-constrained-additive-models">shape-constrained smooths</NoteLink>.
