<Definition>
This part of the notes collects the deterministic mathematics that machine learning uses: the algebra of vectors and
matrices, the calculus of functions of one and several variables, equations for systems that change in time, and the
numerical methods that carry all of it out on a computer. Probability, statistics and optimisation build on it and
have their own topics <Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A model is a function with parameters, and its data are vectors. Linear algebra describes the function: a neural network
layer is a <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink>, a covariance is a positive definite matrix,
and <Gloss name="pca">principal component analysis</Gloss> is an
<NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">eigendecomposition</NoteLink>. Calculus describes how the function changes with its
parameters, which is what training needs: the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> points downhill, the
<NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> measures curvature, and <NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation">automatic
differentiation</NoteLink> computes both for any program. Differential equations describe continuous-time models, from
<NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">neural ODEs</NoteLink> to the processes behind
<Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss>. Numerical methods decide whether any of these computations gives
the right digits in floating point.

## Main ideas

**<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">Linear maps</NoteLink> and their decompositions.** Most computations reduce to
solving linear systems, projecting onto subspaces and decomposing matrices into simpler factors. The eigendecomposition,
the <Gloss name="svd">singular value decomposition</Gloss> and the <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky
factorisation</NoteLink> turn hard matrix operations into easy ones along special directions.

**Local linear and quadratic approximation.** The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink>, the gradient and the
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> give the best linear approximation of a function near a point, and the
Hessian adds the quadratic term. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton's method</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace
approximation</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="stability-analysis">stability analysis</NoteLink> of dynamical systems all
rest on these approximations.

**Composition and the chain rule.** Models are compositions of simple maps. The chain rule turns the derivative of a
composition into a product of Jacobians, and evaluating that product from the output end is backpropagation.

**Continuous change.** Differential equations specify a system by its rate of change. Their solutions are flows that
move points, and through the continuity and <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equations</NoteLink> they move probability
densities.

**Computing with finite precision.** <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">Floating-point arithmetic</NoteLink>
rounds every operation. Conditioning measures how much a problem amplifies those errors, and stable algorithms keep them
small.

## How the notes fit together

- <NoteLink to="trigonometry" /> covers angles, the circular and <NoteLink to="hyperbolic-functions">hyperbolic
  functions</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="eulers-formula">Euler's formula</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="linear-algebra" /> covers vectors, matrices, projections, eigenvalues, the standard decompositions,
  conditioning and kernels.
- <NoteLink to="calculus" /> covers derivatives and integrals of one and several variables, matrix calculus and
  automatic differentiation.
- <NoteLink to="calculus-of-variations" /> optimises over functions, through the
  <NoteLink to="euler-lagrange-equation">Euler–Lagrange equation</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="differential-equations" /> covers ordinary and <Gloss name="pde">partial differential equations</Gloss>,
  their stability and their numerical solution.
- <NoteLink to="splines-and-interpolation" /> covers piecewise polynomials, from cubic splines to
  <NoteLink to="b-splines">B-splines</NoteLink> and <Gloss name="nurbs" />.
- <NoteLink to="numerical-methods" /> covers floating point, conditioning, <NoteLink to="root-finding">root
  finding</NoteLink>, quadrature and iterative solvers.
- <NoteLink to="geometry" /> covers <NoteLink to="metric-spaces-and-geodesics">metric spaces</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="riemannian-geometry-and-manifolds">Riemannian manifolds</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="hyperbolic-geometry-and-embeddings">hyperbolic geometry</NoteLink>.

Start with linear algebra and calculus, which every other topic uses. Trigonometry is needed for polar coordinates,
Fourier methods and rotations, and is best read before calculus. Differential equations, the calculus of variations
and geometry build on both linear algebra and calculus. Numerical methods and splines can be read once derivatives and
<NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> are familiar.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume school algebra and the idea of a function.

<NoteLink to="probability" /> uses <NoteLink to="integration">integration</NoteLink> to define
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> and linear algebra to describe covariances.
<NoteLink to="optimisation" /> uses gradients, Hessians and convexity. <NoteLink to="statistics" /> uses projections for
regression and calculus for <Gloss name="mle">maximum likelihood</Gloss>. Beyond
<NoteLink to="mathematical-foundations">Part I</NoteLink>, every learning method, from
<NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> to transformers, is built from these pieces.
