<Definition>
Let a histogram $\avec \in \reals_{\ge 0}^n$ sit on points $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and a histogram $\bvec \in \reals_{\ge
0}^m$ on points $y_1, \dots, y_m$, with equal total mass $\sum_i a_i = \sum_j b_j$. Let $C_{ij} = d(x_i, y_j)$ be the
**ground distance**. The **earth mover's distance** is the least cost of a <NoteLink to="optimal-transport">transport
plan</NoteLink> <Cite id="rubner2000" />:

$$
\operatorname{EMD}(\avec, \bvec) = \min_{\Pmat \in \reals_{\ge 0}^{n \times m}} \sum_{i,j} C_{ij} P_{ij}
\quad \text{subject to} \quad \Pmat\ones = \avec,\ \ \Pmat^\top\ones = \bvec .
$$

$P_{ij}$ is the mass moved from $x_i$ to $y_j$. The EMD is the Wasserstein-1 distance between the two histograms.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Picture $\avec$ as piles of earth and $\bvec$ as holes of the same total volume. Moving mass $P_{ij}$ over distance
$C_{ij}$ costs $C_{ij} P_{ij}$. The EMD is the cheapest way to fill every hole using every pile. Two histograms that
differ only by a shift of their mass by distance $s$ are at EMD $s$, however little they overlap. A bin-by-bin
comparison such as total variation, <Gloss name="kl" /> or <Gloss name="js">Jensen–Shannon divergence</Gloss> sees only
whether mass sits in the same bin, not how far it must travel. It gives the same value for a shift of one bin and a
shift of a hundred bins once the supports stop overlapping.

## The linear program

The plan $\Pmat$ has $nm$ nonnegative entries. The $n$ row-sum constraints say that each point $x_i$ sends out all
of its mass $a_i$; the $m$ column-sum constraints say that each point $y_j$ receives exactly its mass $b_j$.
The objective $\inner{\Cmat}{\Pmat} = \sum_{ij} C_{ij}P_{ij}$ is
linear. So the EMD is a <NoteLink to="linear-programming">linear program</NoteLink> in standard form, the classical
**transportation problem** <Cite id="kantorovich1942" />. Three consequences follow.

- **A solution exists.** The feasible set is nonempty, because $\Pmat = \avec\bvec^\top / \sum_i a_i$ satisfies both
  constraints, and it is bounded, because $0 \le P_{ij} \le \min(a_i, b_j)$. A linear objective attains its minimum on a
  nonempty bounded polytope.
- **An optimal plan is sparse.** An optimum is attained at a vertex. The constraint matrix has $n + m$ rows but rank
  $n + m - 1$, because the row sums and the column sums both add to the total mass. A vertex therefore has at most
  $n + m - 1$ nonzero entries. Of the $nm$ pairs of pile and hole, at most $n + m - 1$ carry mass.
- **It can be solved exactly.** The network simplex method specialises the simplex method to this graph structure. Its
  cost is roughly $O(n^3 \log n)$ for $n = m$ <Cite id="peyre2019" />. When $n = m$ and every point has mass $1/n$, some
  optimal plan is a permutation matrix (the vertices of the set of doubly stochastic matrices are permutations), and the
  problem is the assignment problem solved by the Hungarian algorithm <Cite id="kuhn1955" />.

The EMD is a metric on histograms of equal mass whenever the ground distance is a metric. Symmetry holds because
$\Pmat^\top$ is a plan from $\bvec$ to $\avec$ with the same cost. The triangle inequality is proved by composing plans
(see <NoteLink to="wasserstein-distances">Wasserstein distances</NoteLink>).

## One dimension: the area between the CDFs

On the real line with $d(x, y) = \abs{x - y}$, the EMD has a closed form. Let $F$ and $G$ be the
<Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution functions</Gloss> of the two distributions, normalised to total mass 1. Then <Cite id="vallender1974" />

$$
\operatorname{EMD} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \abs{F(t) - G(t)}\, dt = \int_0^1 \abs{F^{-1}(u) - G^{-1}(u)}\, du .
$$

**Derivation.** Write the distance between two points as the length of the interval between them:
$\abs{x - y} = \int \abs{\indicator\set{x \le t} - \indicator\set{y \le t}}\, dt$. The integrand is 1 exactly for the
$t$ lying between $x$ and $y$. For any plan $\pi$, a joint distribution of $(X, Y)$ with marginals $F$ and $G$,
exchanging the order of integration gives

$$
\expect_\pi\abs{X - Y} = \int \expect_\pi\abs{\indicator\set{X \le t} - \indicator\set{Y \le t}}\, dt
\ge \int \abs{\expect_\pi\indicator\set{X \le t} - \expect_\pi\indicator\set{Y \le t}}\, dt = \int \abs{F(t) - G(t)}\, dt .
$$

The inequality is $\expect\abs{Z} \ge \abs{\expect Z}$. So every plan costs at least the area between the CDFs. The
**monotone plan** attains it. Draw $U \sim \Unif(0, 1)$ and set $X = F^{-1}(U)$, $Y = G^{-1}(U)$. Both are
nondecreasing functions of the same $U$, so for each $t$ the events $\set{X \le t} = \set{U \le F(t)}$ and
$\set{Y \le t} = \set{U \le G(t)}$ are nested. The difference $\indicator\set{X \le t} - \indicator\set{Y \le t}$
then has one sign, and $\expect\abs{Z} = \abs{\expect Z}$ holds for every $t$. The monotone plan is optimal and the
lower bound is the EMD. The quantile form follows from the same coupling: $\expect\abs{X - Y} = \int_0^1
\abs{F^{-1}(u) - G^{-1}(u)}\, du$.

For histograms on the equally spaced bins $1, \dots, n$ with CDFs $F_k = \sum_{i \le k} a_i$ and $G_k = \sum_{j \le k}
b_j$, the integral is a sum, $\operatorname{EMD} = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \abs{F_k - G_k}$. The cost is $O(n)$ instead of a
linear program. For two samples of equal size $N$, the monotone plan pairs the sorted values, so the EMD is
$\tfrac1N \sum_k \abs{x_{(k)} - y_{(k)}}$ and costs one sort.

The monotone plan is optimal for every convex cost $h(x - y)$, not only for $\abs{x - y}$. For $\abs{x - y}$ it is one
of several optimal plans: moving one unit from 1 to 3 costs the same as moving it from 1 to 2 and from 2 to 3.

## Worked example

Four bins at $1, 2, 3, 4$ hold $\avec = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)$ and $\bvec = (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4)$. The CDFs are
$F = (0.4, 0.7, 0.9, 1)$ and $G = (0.1, 0.3, 0.6, 1)$. The differences are $0.3, 0.4, 0.3, 0$, so

$$
\operatorname{EMD} = 0.3 + 0.4 + 0.3 = 1.0 .
$$

The monotone plan fills $\bvec$ from left to right: bin 1 sends $0.1$ to bin 1, $0.2$ to bin 2 and $0.1$ to bin 3; bin
2 sends $0.2$ to bin 3 and $0.1$ to bin 4; bin 3 sends $0.2$ to bin 4; bin 4 keeps its $0.1$. Its cost is
$0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.1 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.1 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 1 = 1.0$. It has 7 nonzero entries, the maximum
$n + m - 1$. The linear-programming solver `ot.emd` <Cite id="flamary2021" /> returns the same cost with a different
plan, which sends $0.3$ from bin 1 directly to bin 4; for the cost $\abs{x - y}$ both are optimal.

The <NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon">total variation distance</NoteLink> between $\avec$ and $\bvec$ is
$0.4$ and would be the same if the bins were ten times further apart; the EMD would be ten times larger.

## Image retrieval and signatures

Rubner, Tomasi and Guibas introduced the name and used the EMD to compare colour and texture distributions of images
<Cite id="rubner2000" />. They represent each image by a **signature**: a short list of cluster centres in a feature
space, such as colour in CIE-Lab, each with a weight equal to the fraction of pixels in the cluster. Signatures of
different images have different centres and different lengths, so no bin-by-bin comparison applies. The EMD compares
them directly, because the ground distance between centres enters the cost. It also tolerates small shifts in colour,
because nearby colours cost little to match, which bin-by-bin histogram distances do not.

Rubner et al. also allow unequal total masses. They then require the plan to move all the mass of the lighter
signature, bound the other side by its weights, and divide the cost by the mass moved. With unequal masses this
normalised version is no longer a metric. <NoteLink to="unbalanced-and-partial-transport">Unbalanced and partial
transport</NoteLink> treat the unequal-mass case more systematically.

## Point clouds

A point cloud of $N$ points is a histogram with mass $1/N$ on each point. The EMD between two clouds of equal size is
the assignment problem: the average distance between matched points under the best one-to-one matching. It is used as a
reconstruction loss for generated point clouds, as is the **Chamfer distance**, the average distance from each point to
its nearest neighbour in the other cloud, summed in both directions <Cite id="fan2017" />. The Chamfer distance ignores
how many points go where: a cloud that piles all its points on half of the target can score well. The EMD requires a
bijection, so it penalises unequal density, at the price of solving an assignment problem.

## Assumptions

- **Equal total mass.** The plan must move all of $\avec$ and fill all of $\bvec$. Histograms are normalised first, or
  an unbalanced formulation is used.
- **A meaningful ground distance.** The EMD is only as good as $d$. Between histograms of unordered categories, with
  $d = 1$ for every pair of distinct categories, the EMD reduces to total variation.

## Pitfalls

- **Cost.** An exact solver costs roughly cubic time in the number of bins, which is prohibitive above a few thousand
  points. Entropic regularisation (Sinkhorn) and slicing trade exactness for speed.
- **Sample estimates in high dimension.** The EMD between two samples of size $N$ from the same $d$-dimensional
  distribution shrinks only like $N^{-1/d}$ <Cite id="fournier2015" />, so it is a biased, slow-converging estimate of
  the population value.
- **The name.** Signal processing uses EMD for <Gloss name="empirical-mode-decomposition">empirical mode
  decomposition</Gloss>, which is unrelated.
