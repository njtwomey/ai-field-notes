<Definition>
A population holds $N$ items, of which $K$ are successes. Draw $n$ items without replacement, each subset of size $n$
being equally likely. The number of successes drawn, $X$, has a hypergeometric distribution,
$X \sim \Hypergeom(N, K, n)$, with

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{N - K}{n - k}}{\binom{N}{n}}, \qquad \max(0, n - N + K) \le k \le \min(n, K).
$$
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameters | Population $N$, successes $K \le N$, draws $n \le N$ |
| Support | $k$ from $\max(0, n - N + K)$ to $\min(n, K)$ |
| pmf | $\binom{K}{k}\binom{N - K}{n - k} / \binom{N}{n}$ |
| Mean | $np$, with $p = K/N$ |
| Variance | $np(1 - p)\,\frac{N - n}{N - 1}$ |

## Where it comes from

There are $\binom{N}{n}$ equally likely samples of size $n$. A sample with exactly $k$ successes chooses $k$ of the $K$
successes and $n - k$ of the $N - K$ failures, in $\binom{K}{k}\binom{N - K}{n - k}$ ways. The ratio is the pmf
<Cite id="wasserman2004" />. The support bounds say that the sample cannot hold more successes than exist, nor more
failures than exist. The probabilities sum to 1 because every sample of size $n$ has some number of successes:
$\sum_k \binom{K}{k}\binom{N - K}{n - k} = \binom{N}{n}$, which is Vandermonde's identity.

For example, a box of $N = 20$ parts holds $K = 5$ defective ones. An inspector draws $n = 4$. The probability of
exactly one defective part is $\binom{5}{1}\binom{15}{3}/\binom{20}{4} = 5 \times 455 / 4845 \approx 0.470$, and the
probability of finding none is $\binom{15}{4}/\binom{20}{4} = 1365/4845 \approx 0.282$.

## Properties

**Mean and variance.** Label the $K$ success items and let $Z_i$ indicate that success item $i$ is drawn, so
$X = \sum_{i=1}^K Z_i$. Each item is in the sample with probability $n/N$, so each $Z_i$ is $\Bern(n/N)$ and
$\expect[X] = nK/N = np$, exactly as for the binomial with $p = K/N$. For $i \ne j$, both items are drawn with
probability $\binom{N - 2}{n - 2}/\binom{N}{n} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{N(N - 1)}$, so

$$
\cov(Z_i, Z_j) = \frac{n(n - 1)}{N(N - 1)} - \frac{n^2}{N^2} = -\frac{n(N - n)}{N^2(N - 1)}.
$$

Adding $K$ variances and $K(K - 1)$ covariances,

$$
\var(X) = K\frac{n}{N}\paren{1 - \frac{n}{N}} - K(K - 1)\frac{n(N - n)}{N^2(N - 1)}
= n\frac{K}{N}\paren{1 - \frac{K}{N}}\frac{N - n}{N - 1}.
$$

**Finite-population correction.** The variance is the binomial variance times $(N - n)/(N - 1)$. The draws are
negatively dependent: taking a success out of the population makes the next success slightly less likely. The factor is
close to 1 when $n$ is a small fraction of $N$. It falls to 0 at $n = N$, when the whole population is drawn and
$X = K$ with certainty.

**Symmetry.** $\Hypergeom(N, K, n)$ and $\Hypergeom(N, n, K)$ are the same distribution. Choosing which $n$ items to
draw and choosing which $K$ items are successes play interchangeable roles. Algebraically, expanding the binomial
coefficients writes both pmfs as $\frac{K!\,(N - K)!\,n!\,(N - n)!}{N!\,k!\,(K - k)!\,(n - k)!\,(N - K - n + k)!}$,
which is symmetric in $K$ and $n$.

**Binomial limit.** As $N \to \infty$ with $K/N \to p$ and $n$ fixed, $\Hypergeom(N, K, n)$ tends to $\Binom(n, p)$.
Drawing a few items from a large population is nearly the same with or without replacement.

<Derivation title="The binomial limit">
Write the pmf as $\binom{n}{k}$ times a ratio of falling factorials, where
$x^{\underline{j}} = x(x - 1)\cdots(x - j + 1)$:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} \frac{K^{\underline{k}}\,(N - K)^{\underline{n - k}}}{N^{\underline{n}}}
= \binom{n}{k} \prod_{i=0}^{k - 1} \frac{K - i}{N - i} \prod_{j=0}^{n - k - 1} \frac{N - K - j}{N - k - j}.
$$

With $n$ and $k$ fixed and $K/N \to p$, each of the first $k$ factors tends to $p$ and each of the last $n - k$ tends
to $1 - p$. The limit is $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n - k}$.
</Derivation>

## Relations to other distributions

- **Binomial.** The <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial</NoteLink> is the same experiment with replacement.
  It has the same mean and a larger variance.
- **Multivariate hypergeometric.** With $c$ kinds of item instead of two, the counts of each kind drawn without
  replacement follow the multivariate hypergeometric distribution. It relates to the
  <NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial</NoteLink> as the hypergeometric relates to the binomial.

## In machine learning

- **Fisher's exact test.** In a $2 \times 2$ contingency table with fixed row and column totals, the count in one cell
  is hypergeometric under the hypothesis of no association. Fisher's exact test computes its
  <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> from this distribution <Cite id="fisher1935" />.
- **Enrichment analysis.** Given a list of $n$ items selected by a model, such as genes or documents, the number that
  belong to a category of $K$ items out of $N$ is hypergeometric if the selection ignores the category. A small
  upper-tail probability indicates that the category is over-represented.
- **Chance baselines for retrieval.** A random ranking of $N$ items, $K$ of them relevant, places a
  $\Hypergeom(N, K, n)$ number of relevant items in its top $n$. Random precision at $n$ therefore averages $K/N$.

## Pitfalls

- **Using the binomial for large samples.** When the sample is a large fraction of the population, the binomial
  overstates the variance. Survey estimates and audits of small batches need the finite-population correction.
- **Parameter names differ.** SciPy's `hypergeom(M, n, N)` takes the population size, the number of successes and the
  number of draws, in that order and with those letters. NumPy's `hypergeometric(ngood, nbad, nsample)` takes the
  successes, the failures and the draws. Match the arguments by meaning, not by letter.
