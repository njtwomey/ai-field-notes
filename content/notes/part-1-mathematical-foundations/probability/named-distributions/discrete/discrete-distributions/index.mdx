<Definition>
A discrete distribution assigns probabilities to a finite or countable set of outcomes, such as $\{0, 1\}$, the
integers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ or a set of $K$ categories. It is described by a probability mass function $p(k)$, with
$p(k) \ge 0$ and $\sum_k p(k) = 1$. The named discrete distributions are the families that arise from simple counting
experiments built out of independent trials <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Most labels in machine learning are discrete. A binary label is a Bernoulli variable, a class label is a categorical
variable, and the output of a classifier is a categorical distribution over classes. Cross-entropy loss is the negative
log-likelihood of a categorical distribution, and <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> fits
a Bernoulli one. Counts appear as word frequencies in documents, clicks on items, arrivals in a time window and reads in
a gene-expression experiment. The binomial, Poisson and negative binomial distributions are the standard likelihoods for
such counts, and the choice between them decides how the model treats variance.

## Main ideas

**Everything starts from one trial.** The <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution" /> describes one trial with two
outcomes, 1 with probability $p$ and 0 with probability $1 - p$. The <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical
distribution</NoteLink> extends it to $K$ unordered outcomes with probabilities $\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K$ that sum to 1.

**Counting successes over $n$ trials.** Summing $n$ independent Bernoulli trials gives the
<NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial distribution</NoteLink>, with mean $np$ and variance $np(1 - p)$. Counting
each category over $n$ independent categorical draws gives the <NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial
distribution</NoteLink>, whose counts are each binomial and are negatively correlated with each other. Drawing without
replacement from a finite population gives the <NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">hypergeometric
distribution</NoteLink>, whose variance is smaller than the binomial's by the finite-population correction.

**Counting trials until success.** The <NoteLink to="geometric-distribution">geometric distribution</NoteLink> counts
the trials up to the first success and has mean $1/p$. It is the only memoryless distribution on the positive integers.
The <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial distribution</NoteLink> counts the failures before
the $r$-th success.

**Counting rare events at a rate.** The <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution" /> models the number of events in a fixed
interval when events occur independently at an average rate $\lambda$. Its mean and variance both equal $\lambda$. It is
the limit of the binomial when $n$ is large, $p$ is small and $np = \lambda$ stays fixed.

**Variance relative to the mean.** Many real counts vary more than a Poisson allows. A Poisson whose rate is itself
gamma-distributed is negative binomial, and its variance exceeds its mean. This makes the negative binomial the
standard model for overdispersed counts.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution" />. The <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial
distribution</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="geometric-distribution">geometric distribution</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial distribution</NoteLink> are all built from sequences of
Bernoulli trials and are best read in that order. The <NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">hypergeometric
distribution</NoteLink> is the binomial without replacement. The <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution" /> is the
binomial's limit for rare events. The <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical distribution</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial distribution</NoteLink> repeat the Bernoulli and binomial for $K$
categories, and lead into the multivariate distributions.
