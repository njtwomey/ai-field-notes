<Definition>
A continuous distribution describes a real-valued random variable through a
probability density $p(x) \ge 0$ with $\int p(x)\,dx = 1$. The probability of an
interval is the integral of the density over it, and any single point has probability zero. The named continuous
distributions are <NoteLink to="named-distributions">parametric families</NoteLink> with a known density, known moments
and known relations to one another <Cite id="johnson1994" /> <Cite id="johnson1995" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Continuous distributions model the noise in regression, the prior and posterior over a parameter, the waiting time to an
event, a proportion between 0 and 1, and the reference distribution of a
<NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">test statistic</NoteLink>. Their log-densities become
<Gloss name="loss-function">loss functions</Gloss>. A Gaussian likelihood gives squared error, and a Laplace likelihood
gives absolute error. A Gaussian prior gives the ridge penalty, and a Laplace prior gives the lasso penalty. Student t,
chi-squared and F distributions supply the critical values of classical tests. Gamma, inverse-gamma and beta
distributions are the conjugate priors for rates, variances and probabilities.

## Main ideas

**The Gaussian is the centre of the family.** The <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution" /> is the limit of standardised
sums, is closed under linear maps and sums, and has a quadratic log-density. Several other families are functions of
Gaussians. The exponential of a Gaussian is <NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal</NoteLink>. A sum of $k$
squared standard normals is <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared</NoteLink>. A standard normal divided by
the square root of an independent scaled chi-squared variable is <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student
t</NoteLink>. A ratio of two independent scaled chi-squared variables is <NoteLink to="f-distribution">F</NoteLink>.

**Waiting times and rates.** The <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink> is the
waiting time to an event that occurs at constant rate $\lambda$, and it is the only memoryless continuous distribution.
The <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distribution</NoteLink> is the waiting time to the $\alpha$-th event, and
the chi-squared distribution is a special case of it. The <NoteLink to="inverse-gamma-distribution">inverse-gamma
distribution</NoteLink> is the law of $1/X$ for gamma $X$.

**Distributions on a bounded interval.** The <NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta distribution</NoteLink> lives on
$[0, 1]$ and models probabilities and proportions. Its two parameters act as prior counts of successes and failures.

**Tails decide robustness.** The Gaussian's tails fall like $e^{-x^2/2}$. The
<NoteLink to="laplace-distribution" /> has exponential tails and a sharp peak. The Student t has polynomial tails, and
with one degree of freedom it is the <NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution" />, which has no mean and no variance. A model
with heavier-tailed noise is less moved by outliers.

**Conjugacy links priors to likelihoods.** The beta is conjugate to the Bernoulli and binomial, the gamma to the
Poisson rate and the Gaussian precision, and the inverse-gamma to the Gaussian variance. With a conjugate prior, the
posterior stays in the prior's family and its parameters update by adding counts or sums.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution" />. The <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared
distribution</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution" /> and <NoteLink to="f-distribution" /> follow from it,
in that order, and together they form the sampling distributions of classical statistics. The
<NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma
distribution</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="inverse-gamma-distribution">inverse-gamma distribution</NoteLink> form a
second chain. The <NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta distribution</NoteLink> stands on its own. The
<NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal distribution</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="laplace-distribution" /> and
<NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution" /> are best read against the Gaussian, as variations in skew and tail weight.
