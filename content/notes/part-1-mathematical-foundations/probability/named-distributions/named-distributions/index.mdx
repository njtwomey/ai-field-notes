<Definition>
A named distribution is a parametric family of probability distributions with a
standard name, a known probability mass or density function, and known moments. Each family is indexed by a few
parameters, such as the success probability $p$ of a Bernoulli or the mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ of a Gaussian.
The families are linked by limits, sums, transformations and conjugacy, so learning their relations is as useful as
learning each one <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A probabilistic model is assembled from named distributions. The likelihood of a classifier is categorical, of a
regression Gaussian, of a count model Poisson or <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative
binomial</NoteLink>. Priors in Bayesian models are beta, gamma, Dirichlet or Wishart, chosen for conjugacy.
<NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">Test statistics</NoteLink> have chi-squared, t and F reference
distributions. Noise in a <Gloss name="vae">variational autoencoder</Gloss>, a <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion
model</Gloss> or a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> is
<NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink>. Choosing the family decides what the
model can express: whether counts can be overdispersed, whether outliers are tolerated, whether values are bounded
<Cite id="bishop2006" />.

## Main ideas

**Families are built from simple experiments.** Repeating a Bernoulli trial gives the binomial, geometric and negative
binomial distributions. Waiting times in a <NoteLink to="poisson-process">Poisson process</NoteLink> give the
exponential and gamma distributions. Sums of squared Gaussians give the chi-squared distribution, and ratios built from
Gaussians and chi-squared variables give the t and <NoteLink to="f-distribution">F distributions</NoteLink>.

**Limits connect discrete and continuous families.** The binomial with many trials and a small success probability tends
to the Poisson. Standardised sums of almost any variables tend to the Gaussian, by the <Gloss name="clt">central limit
theorem</Gloss>.

**Conjugate pairs keep Bayesian updates in closed form.** The beta is conjugate to the Bernoulli and binomial, the
gamma to the Poisson rate and the Gaussian precision, the Dirichlet to the categorical and multinomial, and the Wishart
to the <Gloss name="precision-matrix">precision matrix</Gloss> of a multivariate normal.

**Most families share one form.** The Bernoulli, categorical, Poisson, Gaussian, gamma, beta and Dirichlet distributions
are all exponential families, with a density of the form $h(x)\exp\paren{\etavec^\top T(x) - A(\etavec)}$. Their
<Gloss name="mle">maximum-likelihood</Gloss> estimates match moments of the
<NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink> $T(x)$.

## How the notes fit together

The topic has three subtopics.

- <NoteLink to="discrete-distributions" /> covers trials, counts and categories: Bernoulli, binomial, geometric,
  negative binomial, hypergeometric, Poisson, categorical and multinomial.
- <NoteLink to="continuous-distributions" /> covers real-valued quantities: the Gaussian and its relatives (log-normal,
  chi-squared, Student t, F), the exponential, gamma and inverse-gamma, the beta, and the heavy-tailed Laplace and
  Cauchy.
- <NoteLink to="multivariate-distributions" /> covers the multivariate normal, the Dirichlet and the Wishart.

Read the discrete families first, then the continuous ones, then the multivariate ones. Each subtopic starts with its
simplest family, and the others are defined in terms of it.
