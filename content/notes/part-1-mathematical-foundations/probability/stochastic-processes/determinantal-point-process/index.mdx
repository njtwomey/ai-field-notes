<Definition>
A **<Gloss name="dpp" />** on a ground set $\Ycal = \set{1, \dots, N}$ is a random subset $Y \subseteq \Ycal$. In
its *L-ensemble* form it is given by a symmetric <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite" /> matrix $\Lmat \in
\reals^{N \times N}$:

$$
P(Y = S) = \frac{\det(\Lmat_S)}{\det(\Lmat + \Imat)}, \qquad S \subseteq \Ycal,
$$

where $\Lmat_S$ is the submatrix of rows and columns indexed by $S$ and $\det(\Lmat_\emptyset) = 1$. Equivalently, a
*marginal kernel* $\Kmat = \Lmat(\Lmat + \Imat)^{-1}$, with eigenvalues in $[0, 1]$, gives every inclusion
probability: $P(A \subseteq Y) = \det(\Kmat_A)$. Macchi introduced the process to model fermions, particles that
cannot share a state <Cite id="macchi1975" />; Kulesza and Taskar brought it to machine learning as a model of diverse
subsets <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Write $\Lmat = \Bmat^\top \Bmat$, so that item $i$ is a column vector $\bvec_i$ and $L_{ij} = \bvec_i^\top \bvec_j$. The
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> of a Gram matrix is the squared volume of the parallelotope its
vectors span:

$$
\det(\Lmat_S) = \operatorname{vol}^2\bigl(\set{\bvec_i : i \in S}\bigr).
$$

A long vector is a good item. Two vectors pointing the same way span almost no area, so a subset that contains both is
improbable however good each is. A <NoteLink to="poisson-process" /> or any set of independent
<NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flips</NoteLink> has no such interaction; a DPP is the simplest model in
which items repel.

## Details

### The normaliser

The probabilities sum to 1 because $\sum_{S \subseteq \Ycal} \det(\Lmat_S) = \det(\Lmat + \Imat)$. The determinant is
linear in each column. Column $j$ of $\Lmat + \Imat$ is $\lvec_j + \evec_j$, so expanding every column gives $2^N$
terms, one for each choice $S$ of columns that keep $\lvec_j$ while the rest keep $\evec_j$:

$$
\det(\Lmat + \Imat) = \sum_{S \subseteq \Ycal} \det\bigl[\,\lvec_j \text{ for } j \in S,\ \evec_j \text{ for } j \notin S\,\bigr]
= \sum_{S \subseteq \Ycal} \det(\Lmat_S).
$$

Each term's columns $\evec_j$ select row $j$ and column $j$ alone, so expanding along them leaves the minor
$\det(\Lmat_S)$. The sum over $2^N$ subsets costs one $N \times N$ determinant.

### Repulsion

For two items, $P(\set{i, j} \subseteq Y) = K_{ii} K_{jj} - K_{ij}^2 \le P(i \in Y)\, P(j \in Y)$. Every pair is
negatively correlated, more so the larger $\abs{K_{ij}}$. A DPP can only repel: it cannot model items that tend to
appear together.

### Quality and diversity

Split each vector into a length and a direction, $\bvec_i = q_i \phivec_i$ with $\norm{\phivec_i} = 1$. Then
$L_{ij} = q_i \, S_{ij} \, q_j$ with similarity $S_{ij} = \phivec_i^\top \phivec_j$, and

$$
\det(\Lmat_S) = \Bigl(\prod_{i \in S} q_i^2\Bigr) \det(\Smat_S).
$$

The first factor rewards good items; the second rewards items unlike each other. Diversified recommendation uses this
form, with $q_i$ from a relevance model and $\Smat$ from item embeddings <Cite id="wilhelm2018dpp" />.

### The size of a sample

Let $\Lmat = \sum_n \lambda_n \vvec_n \vvec_n^\top$ be the <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition" />. Then $\Kmat$ has the
same eigenvectors with eigenvalues $\lambda_n / (\lambda_n + 1)$, and the sample size is distributed as a sum of
independent Bernoulli variables with those means. In particular

$$
\expect\abs{Y} = \operatorname{tr}\Kmat = \sum_{n=1}^N \frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n + 1}.
$$

Scaling $\Lmat$ by $\alpha$ scales every $\lambda_n$ and so sets the typical size. A *k-DPP* conditions on
$\abs{Y} = k$, for applications that need a fixed number of items <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />.

### Sampling: the spectral algorithm

Sampling looks hard: there are $2^N$ subsets. Hough, Krishnapur, Peres and Virág showed that a DPP is a mixture of
simpler processes, which gives an exact sampler in two phases <Cite id="hough2006" />.

**Why it works.** By the Cauchy–Binet formula, with $\Vmat_{S,J}$ the rows $S$ and columns $J$ of the eigenvector
matrix,

$$
\det(\Lmat_S) = \sum_{\abs{J} = \abs{S}} \det(\Vmat_{S,J})^2 \prod_{n \in J} \lambda_n .
$$

Divide by $\det(\Lmat + \Imat) = \prod_n (1 + \lambda_n)$ and regroup:

$$
P(Y = S) = \sum_{J \subseteq \Ycal}
\underbrace{\prod_{n \in J} \frac{\lambda_n}{1 + \lambda_n} \prod_{n \notin J} \frac{1}{1 + \lambda_n}}_{\text{choose eigenvectors } J}
\;\underbrace{\det(\Vmat_{S,J})^2 \, \indicator[\abs{S} = \abs{J}]}_{\text{an elementary DPP}} .
$$

The first factor is the probability of keeping each eigenvector $n$ independently with probability
$\lambda_n / (1 + \lambda_n)$. The second is an *elementary* (projection) DPP with kernel
$\Kmat^J = \Vmat_{:,J} \Vmat_{:,J}^\top$, which always returns exactly $\abs{J}$ items.

**The algorithm** <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />:

```text
input: eigenpairs (λₙ, vₙ) of L
phase 1:  V ← { vₙ : keep n with probability λₙ / (λₙ + 1) }   # fixes |Y| = |V|
phase 2:  Y ← ∅
          while V is not empty:
              pick i with probability Σ_{v ∈ V} v_i² / |V|   # = K^V_ii / |V|
              Y ← Y ∪ {i}
              # project away from e_i:
              V ← an orthonormal basis of { v ∈ span V : v_i = 0 }
return Y
```

The update in phase 2 removes the component of the kept subspace along item $i$. Points similar to $i$ have rows of
$\Vmat$ that resemble row $i$, so their probability falls with it. In the figure below the colour drains around each
pick, which is the repulsion seen happening.

<Derivation title="Why phase 2 samples the elementary DPP">
Let $\Vmat$ ($N \times k$) have orthonormal columns. The elementary DPP gives a $k$-set $S$ probability
$\det(\Vmat_S)^2$, with $\Vmat_S$ the $k \times k$ submatrix of rows $S$; these sum to 1 by Cauchy–Binet on
$\det(\Vmat^\top\Vmat) = 1$.

The first pick is $i$ with probability $\norm{\Vmat_{i,:}}^2 / k$. Rotate the basis (the process depends only on the
span) so that one column $\uvec$ carries all of row $i$, $\norm{\Vmat_{i,:}} = \abs{u_i}$, and the other $k - 1$
columns $\Vmat'$ have $i$-th entry 0. Expanding $\det(\Vmat_S)$ along row $i$ gives
$\det(\Vmat_S)^2 = u_i^2 \det(\Vmat'_{S \setminus i})^2$: what remains is the elementary DPP of
$\Vmat'$, the basis that phase 2 keeps.

By induction, an ordered sequence $i_1, \dots, i_k$ has probability
$\prod_t \norm{\Vmat^{(t)}_{i_t,:}}^2 / (k - t + 1) = \det(\Vmat_S)^2 / k!$. Summing over the $k!$ orders of $S$ gives
$\det(\Vmat_S)^2$.
</Derivation>

<DppSampler />

**Cost.** The eigendecomposition costs $O(N^3)$ once and is reused for every sample. Phase 2 as written
re-orthonormalises $k$ vectors of length $N$ after each of its $k$ picks, $O(N k^3)$ per sample with $k = \abs{Y}$.
When $\Lmat = \Bmat^\top\Bmat$ with $\Bmat$ of size $D \times N$ and $D \ll N$, the same algorithm runs on the
$D \times D$ matrix $\Bmat\Bmat^\top$, and the cost no longer grows as $N^3$ <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />.

### Phase 2 by Gram–Schmidt on the rows

Phase 2 can orthogonalise the other way. Write the kept eigenvectors as the columns of $\Vmat$ ($N \times k$) and let
$\yvec_i \in \reals^k$ be row $i$, so the elementary kernel is $K^V_{ij} = \yvec_i^\top \yvec_j$. After the points $S$
have been picked, the next pick is $i$ with probability

$$
P(i \mid S) = \frac{K^V_{ii} - \Kmat^V_{i,S} (\Kmat^V_S)^{-1} \Kmat^V_{S,i}}{k - \abs{S}}
= \frac{\bigl\lVert \yvec_i - \Pmat_S \yvec_i \bigr\rVert^2}{k - \abs{S}},
$$

where $\Pmat_S$ projects onto the span of the picked rows $\set{\yvec_j : j \in S}$. The numerator is the Schur
complement of the elementary kernel, which is the squared residual of row $i$ after the picked rows are projected out.
Tremblay, Barthelmé and Amblard sample this way <Cite id="tremblay2018dpp" />:

```text
r_i ← ‖y_i‖² for every i;  E ← []        # E: orthonormalised picked rows
repeat k times:
    pick i with probability r_i / (k − |E|)
    # one Gram–Schmidt step, on a k-vector:
    e ← y_i − Σ_{e' ∈ E} (e'ᵀ y_i) e';  e ← e / ‖e‖
    E ← E ∪ {e}
    r_j ← r_j − (eᵀ y_j)²  for every j                  # update every residual
```

Each pick costs $O(Nk)$, so phase 2 costs $O(Nk^2)$ instead of $O(Nk^3)$. The probabilities are those of the elimination
version at every step, so the two give identical samples from identical random numbers; the figure's two spectral modes
show this. The residual $r_i$ is the quantity $d_i^2$ that greedy <Gloss name="map-estimate" /> maximises below,
computed for the elementary kernel: the sampler draws in proportion to it, the greedy search takes its largest value.

### Sampling without an eigendecomposition

The chain rule gives a third exact sampler that works on $\Kmat$ directly <Cite id="poulson2019dpp" />
<Cite id="launay2020dpp" />. Visit the items in a fixed order and decide each in turn. Item $j$ joins with its current
inclusion probability $K_{jj}$, and the kernel of the remaining items is then conditioned on the outcome:

$$
\Kmat \leftarrow \Kmat - \frac{\Kmat_{:,j} \Kmat_{j,:}}{K_{jj}} \;\text{ if } j \in Y, \qquad
\Kmat \leftarrow \Kmat - \frac{\Kmat_{:,j} \Kmat_{j,:}}{K_{jj} - 1} \;\text{ if } j \notin Y .
$$

Both updates are one step of Gaussian elimination with pivot $K_{jj}$ or $K_{jj} - 1$, so a whole sample costs one
modified <Gloss name="lu" /> (or, for symmetric $\Kmat$, $\Lmat\Dmat\Lmat^\top$) factorisation, $O(N^3)$, with no
eigendecomposition. Joining lowers the probabilities of similar items, since $K_{ii} - K_{ij}^2 / K_{jj} \le K_{ii}$.
Being left out raises them, since $K_{ii} + K_{ij}^2 / (1 - K_{jj}) \ge K_{ii}$. Poulson shows that dense and sparse
factorisation codes can be reused almost unchanged <Cite id="poulson2019dpp" />.

<Derivation title="Why the two conditional kernels are Schur complements">
Let $A$ be a set of items not containing $j$, $\bvec = \Kmat_{A,j}$ and $c = K_{jj}$. Joining: by the Schur complement,
$P(A \cup \set{j} \subseteq Y) = \det(\Kmat_{A \cup j}) = c \det(\Kmat_A - \bvec\bvec^\top / c)$. Dividing by
$P(j \in Y) = c$ gives $P(A \subseteq Y \mid j \in Y) = \det(\Kmat_A - \bvec\bvec^\top / c)$, so the conditional
process is a DPP with that kernel.

Being left out: $P(A \subseteq Y, j \notin Y) = \det(\Kmat_A) - \det(\Kmat_{A \cup j})$. The determinant is linear in
its last row, and the last row $(\bvec^\top, c - 1)$ is the last row of $\Kmat_{A \cup j}$ plus $(0, \dots, 0, -1)$.
Expanding, then taking the Schur complement of the pivot $c - 1$,

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} \Kmat_A & \bvec \\ \bvec^\top & c - 1 \end{pmatrix} = \det(\Kmat_{A \cup j}) - \det(\Kmat_A)
= (c - 1)\det\Bigl(\Kmat_A - \frac{\bvec\bvec^\top}{c - 1}\Bigr) .
$$

Hence $P(A \subseteq Y, j \notin Y) = -(c - 1) \det(\cdot) = (1 - c) \det(\Kmat_A - \bvec\bvec^\top / (c - 1))$, and
dividing by $P(j \notin Y) = 1 - c$ gives the second kernel.
</Derivation>

The three samplers draw from the same distribution. The spectral ones pay for the eigendecomposition once and then
sample cheaply, which suits many samples from one kernel. The sequential one pays $O(N^3)$ per sample but needs only
$\Kmat$ and a factorisation routine, which suits one sample from a kernel that changes, and kernels that are not
symmetric.

### Finding the most likely subset

Recommendation and summarisation need one good diverse set rather than a random one: the mode
$\argmax_S \det(\Lmat_S)$. Finding it exactly is NP-hard <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />, so the set is built greedily,
adding at each step the item that multiplies the determinant most.

By the <NoteLink to="schur-complement" />, adding item $i$ to $S$ multiplies the determinant by

$$
\frac{\det(\Lmat_{S \cup \set{i}})}{\det(\Lmat_S)} = L_{ii} - \Lmat_{i,S} \Lmat_S^{-1} \Lmat_{S,i} = d_i^2 ,
$$

the squared distance from $\bvec_i$ to the span of the vectors already chosen. Chen, Zhang and Zhou keep the
<NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition" /> $\Lmat_S = \Cmat\Cmat^\top$ and, for every candidate, the vector
$\cvec_i = \Cmat^{-1}\Lmat_{S,i}$, so that $d_i^2 = L_{ii} - \norm{\cvec_i}^2$. After item $j$ joins,

$$
e_i = \frac{L_{ji} - \cvec_j^\top \cvec_i}{d_j}, \qquad \cvec_i \leftarrow (\cvec_i, e_i), \qquad
d_i^2 \leftarrow d_i^2 - e_i^2 ,
$$

which costs $O(Nk)$ per step and $O(Nk^2)$ for $k$ items, instead of a determinant per candidate per step
<Cite id="chen2018dpp" />. Switch the figure to greedy MAP to watch the gains $d_i^2$ collapse around each pick.

## Worked example

Two items with $\Lmat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}$: equal quality, similarity $\rho$. Then
$\det(\Lmat + \Imat) = 4 - \rho^2$ and

| $S$ | $\det(\Lmat_S)$ | $\rho = 0$ | $\rho = 0.9$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\emptyset$ | 1 | 0.250 | 0.313 |
| $\set{1}$ | 1 | 0.250 | 0.313 |
| $\set{2}$ | 1 | 0.250 | 0.313 |
| $\set{1, 2}$ | $1 - \rho^2$ | 0.250 | 0.060 |

With $\rho = 0$ the items are independent fair coins. With $\rho = 0.9$ each item appears with probability
$0.313 + 0.060 = 0.373$, and both together with probability 0.060, against $0.373^2 = 0.139$ were they independent.
The kernel moved probability from the redundant pair to the empty set and the singletons.

## Assumptions

- **A symmetric positive semi-definite kernel.** Every principal minor must be non-negative to be a probability. A
  similarity that is not positive semi-definite (cosine similarity of arbitrary scores, a thresholded graph) has to be
  projected or replaced.
- **Only repulsion.** All pairs are negatively correlated, so a DPP cannot express items that belong together.
- **A finite ground set** in this note. Continuous DPPs on $\reals^d$ exist and model, for example, the eigenvalues of
  random matrices <Cite id="hough2006" />; sampling them needs the kernel's spectral expansion.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale sets the size.** Multiplying $\Lmat$ by $\alpha$ changes the expected sample size, not only the "strength" of
  repulsion. Compare kernels at a fixed expected size or use a k-DPP.
- **Learning the kernel.** Fitting $\Lmat$ by <Gloss name="mle">maximum likelihood</Gloss> is non-convex. Low-rank
  factorisations $\Lmat = \Bmat^\top\Bmat$ make it tractable and are the usual choice <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />.
- **MAP is not a sample.** Greedy MAP returns one deterministic set. A slate shown many times to many users may need
  samples, so that the same items do not always win.
