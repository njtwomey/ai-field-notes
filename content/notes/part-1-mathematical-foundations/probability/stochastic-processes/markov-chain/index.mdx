<Definition>
A **Markov chain** on a countable state space $\Scal$ is a sequence of <NoteLink to="random-variable">random
variables</NoteLink> $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ in $\Scal$ with the **Markov property**: for every $t$ and all states,

$$
\prob(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i, X_{t-1} = i_{t-1}, \dots, X_0 = i_0) = \prob(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i).
$$

The chain is **time-homogeneous** when this probability does not depend on $t$. It is then written $P_{ij}$, and the
**transition matrix** $\Pmat = (P_{ij})$ has non-negative entries and rows summing to 1. The chain is specified by
$\Pmat$ and the initial distribution of $X_0$ <Cite id="norris1997" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The current state carries everything the past can say about the future. A board game whose moves depend only on the
square and a die roll is a Markov chain; the history of how a player reached the square is irrelevant. The rows of
$\Pmat$ are the "where next" distributions, one per state.

Two questions organise the theory. Where does the chain go in $n$ steps? Matrix powers answer it. Where does it spend
its time in the long run? The stationary distribution answers it, for chains that neither get stuck nor cycle.

## The $n$-step transition probabilities

Write $P^{(n)}_{ij} = \prob(X_{t+n} = j \mid X_t = i)$. To go from $i$ to $j$ in $m + n$ steps, the chain passes
through some state $k$ at step $m$. The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink>
over $k$, with the Markov property to forget how $k$ was reached, gives the **Chapman–Kolmogorov equations**:

$$
P^{(m+n)}_{ij} = \sum_{k \in \Scal}\prob(X_m = k \mid X_0 = i)\,\prob(X_{m+n} = j \mid X_m = k) =
\sum_{k \in \Scal}P^{(m)}_{ik}P^{(n)}_{kj}.
$$

The right side is the $(i, j)$ entry of a <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink>. Since
$P^{(1)} = \Pmat$, induction gives $P^{(n)} = \Pmat^n$. If the distribution of $X_0$ is the row vector $\muvec_0$, the
distribution of $X_n$ is $\muvec_n = \muvec_0\Pmat^n$. Each step multiplies the distribution by $\Pmat$ on the right.

## Classifying states

- **Communication.** State $j$ is **accessible** from $i$ if $P^{(n)}_{ij} > 0$ for some $n \ge 0$. States $i$ and
  $j$ **communicate** if each is accessible from the other. Communication is an equivalence relation, so it splits
  $\Scal$ into classes. A chain with a single class is **irreducible**: every state can reach every other.
- **Recurrence.** A state $i$ is **recurrent** if a chain started at $i$ returns to $i$ with probability 1, and
  **transient** otherwise. A recurrent state is **positive recurrent** if the expected return time
  $m_i = \expect[T_i \mid X_0 = i]$ is finite, where $T_i = \min\set{t \ge 1 : X_t = i}$.
- **Period.** The **period** of $i$ is $d(i) = \gcd\set{n \ge 1 : P^{(n)}_{ii} > 0}$. The state is **aperiodic** if
  $d(i) = 1$. A self-loop, $P_{ii} > 0$, makes a state aperiodic.

Recurrence, positive recurrence and the period are class properties: all states of a class share them. In a finite
irreducible chain every state is positive recurrent <Cite id="norris1997" />.

Recurrence has a test in terms of the $n$-step probabilities. Let $f$ be the probability of returning to $i$ from $i$.
Each return starts afresh, by the strong Markov property, so the number of visits to $i$ is geometric: it equals $k$
with probability $f^{k-1}(1 - f)$, and its mean is $1/(1 - f)$. The expected number of visits is also
$\sum_{n \ge 0}P^{(n)}_{ii}$. Hence

$$
i \text{ is recurrent} \iff f = 1 \iff \sum_{n \ge 0}P^{(n)}_{ii} = \infty.
$$

This criterion decides recurrence of the <NoteLink to="random-walk">simple random walk</NoteLink> in each dimension.

## Stationary distributions

A distribution $\pivec$ on $\Scal$ is **stationary** if $\pivec\Pmat = \pivec$, that is, $\pi_j = \sum_i \pi_iP_{ij}$
for every $j$. A chain started from $\pivec$ has distribution $\pivec$ at every step. In matrix terms, $\pivec$ is a
left eigenvector of $\Pmat$ with eigenvalue 1, normalised to sum to 1.

**Existence and uniqueness.** An irreducible chain has a stationary distribution if and only if it is positive
recurrent. The stationary distribution is then unique, and

$$
\pi_i = \frac{1}{m_i}.
$$

A state visited once every $m_i$ steps on average is occupied a fraction $1/m_i$ of the time. Every finite irreducible
chain therefore has exactly one stationary distribution <Cite id="norris1997" />.

<Derivation title="Constructing the stationary distribution">
Fix a state $k$ and start the chain at $k$. Let $\gamma_j$ be the expected number of visits to $j$ before the first
return to $k$:

$$
\gamma_j = \expect\brack{\sum_{t=0}^{T_k - 1}\indicator\set{X_t = j} \bigm| X_0 = k}.
$$

Then $\gamma_k = 1$ and $\sum_j \gamma_j = \expect[T_k \mid X_0 = k] = m_k$. A visit to $j$ at time $t + 1 \le T_k$
comes from a visit to some $i$ at time $t < T_k$, followed by a step $i \to j$. Because $X_{T_k} = X_0 = k$, the
visits at times $1, \dots, T_k$ have the same count as those at times $0, \dots, T_k - 1$. Summing over $t$ and taking
expectations gives $\gamma_j = \sum_i \gamma_iP_{ij}$, so $\gammavec$ is invariant. Positive recurrence makes its
total $m_k$ finite, and $\pivec = \gammavec/m_k$ is a stationary distribution with $\pi_k = 1/m_k$.

For uniqueness in a finite irreducible chain, suppose $\pivec\Pmat = \pivec$ and $\pivec'\Pmat = \pivec'$. Take the
largest $c$ with $\pivec' - c\pivec \ge 0$ entrywise. The vector $\vvec = \pivec' - c\pivec$ is non-negative,
invariant, and zero in some state. Invariance gives $v_j = \sum_i v_i P^{(n)}_{ij}$ for every $n$. If $v_j = 0$ then
$v_i = 0$ for every $i$ that can reach $j$, which by irreducibility is every state. So $\pivec' = c\pivec$, and
normalisation gives $c = 1$.
</Derivation>

## Convergence to stationarity

**Convergence theorem.** If a chain is irreducible, aperiodic and positive recurrent, with stationary distribution
$\pivec$, then for every starting state $i$ and every $j$

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}P^{(n)}_{ij} = \pi_j.
$$

Both conditions are needed. A reducible chain can be trapped in a class that
depends on the start. A periodic chain, such as $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$ deterministically, has the stationary
distribution $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ but its $n$-step probabilities cycle forever.

<Derivation title="Proof by coupling">
Run two copies of the chain independently: $X$ from state $i$ and $Y$ from the stationary distribution $\pivec$, so
$Y_n \sim \pivec$ for all $n$. The pair $(X_n, Y_n)$ is a Markov chain on $\Scal \times \Scal$ with transition
probabilities $P_{ik}P_{jl}$. Aperiodicity and irreducibility of $\Pmat$ imply that $P^{(n)}_{ij} > 0$ for all large
$n$, for every pair $i, j$. The product chain is then irreducible, and it has the stationary distribution
$\pi_k\pi_l$, so it is positive recurrent. It therefore hits the diagonal $\set{(k, k)}$ at some finite time $\tau$,
with probability 1.

After $\tau$, let $X$ follow $Y$: set $X_n' = X_n$ for $n < \tau$ and $X_n' = Y_n$ for $n \ge \tau$. By the strong
Markov property $X'$ is still a chain started at $i$. Then

$$
\abs{P^{(n)}_{ij} - \pi_j} = \abs{\prob(X_n' = j) - \prob(Y_n = j)} \le \prob(X_n' \ne Y_n) = \prob(\tau > n) \to 0.
$$

The same inequality bounds the <NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon">total variation distance</NoteLink>, $\max_A\abs{\prob(X_n \in A) - \pivec(A)} \le
\prob(\tau > n)$ <Cite id="levin2017" />.
</Derivation>

**Rate.** For a finite chain the distance to $\pivec$ shrinks geometrically. Expanding $\muvec_0$ in the left
eigenvectors of $\Pmat$, the component along $\pivec$ is fixed and every other component is multiplied by its
eigenvalue at each step. The slowest-decaying term goes as $\abs{\lambda_2}^n$, where $\lambda_2$ is the eigenvalue of
second-largest modulus. The **spectral gap** $1 - \abs{\lambda_2}$ sets the **mixing time**, the number of steps needed
to come within a given total variation distance of $\pivec$ <Cite id="levin2017" />.

**Ergodic theorem.** For an irreducible positive recurrent chain and any $f$ with $\sum_i\pi_i\abs{f(i)} < \infty$,

$$
\frac1n\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}f(X_t) \to \sum_i \pi_if(i) \quad \text{almost surely},
$$

from any starting state and without aperiodicity. This is the <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss> for Markov
chains. It justifies estimating expectations under $\pivec$ by averaging along one long run, which is how
<NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> works.

## Worked example: weather

A day is sunny (S), cloudy (C) or rainy (R). Tomorrow's weather depends only on today's through

$$
\Pmat = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix},
$$

with rows and columns in the order S, C, R. The chain is irreducible, because every entry is positive, and aperiodic,
because every state has a self-loop.

**Two days ahead.** Starting from rain, $\muvec_0 = (0, 0, 1)$. One step gives the third row, $\muvec_1 = (0.2, 0.3,
0.5)$. A second step gives $\muvec_2 = \muvec_1\Pmat$:

$$
\muvec_2 = (0.2 \cdot 0.7 + 0.3 \cdot 0.3 + 0.5 \cdot 0.2,\ \dots) = (0.33, 0.31, 0.36).
$$

**Stationary distribution.** The equations $\pivec = \pivec\Pmat$ with $\pi_S + \pi_C + \pi_R = 1$ read

$$
\pi_S = 0.7\pi_S + 0.3\pi_C + 0.2\pi_R, \qquad \pi_C = 0.2\pi_S + 0.4\pi_C + 0.3\pi_R.
$$

Their solution is $\pivec = (21, 13, 12)/46 = (0.457, 0.283, 0.261)$. Substituting confirms it: $0.7 \cdot 21 + 0.3
\cdot 13 + 0.2 \cdot 12 = 21$. In the long run 45.7% of days are sunny, and the mean time between sunny days is
$m_S = 46/21 = 2.19$ days.

**Speed of convergence.** The eigenvalues of $\Pmat$ are $1$ and $(3 \pm \sqrt 3)/10$, that is $0.473$ and $0.127$.
The distance to $\pivec$ shrinks by a factor of about $0.47$ per day. From rain, the total variation distance after 10
days is $3.2 \times 10^{-4}$: the forecast ten days out is the climate.

## In machine learning

- **Markov chain Monte Carlo** designs a chain whose stationary distribution is a target posterior, then averages
  along the chain.
- **Hidden Markov models** put a Markov chain on unobserved states and emit observations from each; see
  <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink>.
- **<Gloss name="rl">Reinforcement learning</Gloss>** adds actions and rewards to a Markov chain to form a
  <NoteLink to="markov-decision-process">Markov decision process</NoteLink>. Each fixed policy induces a Markov chain on
  states.
- **PageRank** is the stationary distribution of a random surfer who follows a random link, or jumps to a random page
  with a small probability. The jump makes the chain irreducible and aperiodic, so the stationary distribution
  exists, is unique and is found by <NoteLink to="eigendecomposition">power iteration</NoteLink>.
- **$n$-gram language models** are Markov chains on words or on tuples of words; see
  <NoteLink to="n-gram-language-model">$n$-gram language model</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Markov property.** The next state depends on the past only through the present. A process with longer memory can
  often be made Markov by enlarging the state, for example to the last $k$ values.
- **Time homogeneity.** The transition matrix is the same at every step. Inhomogeneous chains, such as
  <NoteLink to="simulated-annealing">simulated annealing</NoteLink> with a falling temperature, need a separate
  analysis.

## Pitfalls

- **A stationary distribution is not a limit.** Periodic chains have one but do not converge to it.
- **Uniqueness needs irreducibility.** A chain with two closed classes has a stationary distribution on each, and
  every mixture of them is stationary.
- **Row vectors.** Distributions multiply $\Pmat$ from the left, $\muvec\Pmat$. Functions of the state, such as
  expected future rewards, multiply from the right, $\Pmat\fvec$. Mixing the two conventions transposes the matrix.
