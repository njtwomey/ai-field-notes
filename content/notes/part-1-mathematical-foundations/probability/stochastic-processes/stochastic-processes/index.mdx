<Definition>
A stochastic process is a collection of <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $\{X_t\}$ indexed by
a set $T$, usually time. With $T = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ it is a random sequence, and with $T = [0, \infty)$ a random
function of continuous time. A process is described by how its values depend on one another: through independent
increments, through the Markov property, or through a conditional-mean property <Cite id="grimmett2020" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Sequences and event times are everywhere in machine learning. Markov chains are the backbone of Markov chain Monte
Carlo, hidden Markov models, n-gram <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss> and Markov decision processes.
Poisson processes model arrivals, clicks and failures, and connect to <NoteLink to="survival-analysis">survival
analysis</NoteLink>. Brownian motion is the noise in <Gloss name="sde">stochastic differential equations</Gloss>, and
therefore in <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss>. Martingales give the anytime-valid confidence
sequences of sequential testing and the concentration inequalities for functions of many variables. Determinantal point
processes choose diverse subsets, as in diversified recommendation.

## Main ideas

**Sums of independent steps.** A <NoteLink to="random-walk" /> $S_n = \xi_1 + \dots + \xi_n$ adds independent steps. The
simple random walk on the integer lattice returns to its start with probability 1
in one and two dimensions, and with probability below 1 in three or more.

**The future depends on the present only.** A <NoteLink to="markov-chain" /> moves between states with transition
probabilities $P_{ij}$ that depend only on the current state. The $n$-step transition probabilities are the matrix power
$\Pmat^n$. An irreducible, aperiodic finite chain converges to a unique stationary distribution
$\pivec = \pivec\Pmat$.

**Fair games keep their mean.** A <NoteLink to="martingale" /> satisfies $\expect[M_{n+1} \mid \Fcal_n] = M_n$. By the
optional stopping theorem, no bounded stopping rule changes its expected value. Doob's inequality bounds its running
maximum.

**Events at random times.** A <NoteLink to="poisson-process" /> with rate $\lambda$ has $\Poisson(\lambda t)$ events in
any interval of length $t$, independent counts in disjoint intervals and independent $\Exp(\lambda)$ gaps between
events. Thinning and superposition preserve the Poisson property.

**A continuous-time random walk.** <NoteLink to="brownian-motion" /> $B_t$ has independent Gaussian increments
$B_t - B_s \sim \Gauss(0, t - s)$ and continuous paths. It is the scaling limit of random walks, a Gaussian process with
covariance $\min(s, t)$ and a martingale. Its quadratic variation on $[0, t]$ is $t$, which is why ordinary
calculus fails for it.

**Random subsets that repel.** A <NoteLink to="determinantal-point-process" /> assigns a subset $S$ probability
proportional to $\det(\Lmat_S)$ for a positive semi-definite kernel $\Lmat$. Similar items lower the determinant, so
the process favours diverse subsets.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>, the simplest process. The
<NoteLink to="markov-chain" /> generalises it to any transition rule, and the
<NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink> takes the conditional-mean view of the same walks. The
<NoteLink to="poisson-process" /> and <NoteLink to="brownian-motion" /> are the two basic continuous-time processes, one
for counts and one for continuous paths. The <NoteLink to="determinantal-point-process">determinantal point
process</NoteLink> stands apart: it is a point process over a fixed set, and it relies on
<NoteLink to="linear-algebra">linear algebra</NoteLink> more than on time.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total
expectation and variance</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="poisson-distribution" /> and
<NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution" />
and the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink>. The determinantal point process uses the
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive definite
matrices</NoteLink>.

<NoteLink to="stochastic-calculus" /> builds integrals and <NoteLink to="differential-equations">differential
equations</NoteLink> on Brownian motion. The worked example of <NoteLink to="gamblers-ruin">gambler's ruin</NoteLink>
applies random walks and martingales. In learning, <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo" /> samples posteriors with
Markov chains, the <NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink> adds observations to a hidden
chain, the <NoteLink to="markov-decision-process" /> adds actions and rewards, and the
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process" /> generalises Brownian motion to other covariances.
