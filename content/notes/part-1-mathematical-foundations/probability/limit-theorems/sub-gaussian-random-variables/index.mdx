<Definition>
A real <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ with mean $\mu$ is **sub-Gaussian with variance
proxy $\sigma^2$**, or $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, if

$$
\expect\brack{e^{\lambda(X - \mu)}} \le e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2} \qquad \text{for every } \lambda \in \reals.
$$

The right side is the <Gloss name="mgf">moment-generating function</Gloss> of a $\Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$ variable. A
$\sigma$-sub-Gaussian variable has Gaussian-type tails, $\prob(\abs{X - \mu} \ge t) \le 2e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}$, and a
variance of at most $\sigma^2$. Every variable taking values in an interval $[a, b]$ is sub-Gaussian with
$\sigma = (b - a)/2$ <Cite id="vershynin2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A Gaussian's tail probability $\prob(X - \mu \ge t)$ falls like $e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}$: doubling the deviation
multiplies the exponent by four. Many variables that are not Gaussian have tails at least this light: bounded variables,
random signs, mixtures of Gaussians with a common variance and bounded means. The sub-Gaussian condition names this
class by a single number, the variance proxy $\sigma^2$, and asks for the comparison on the moment-generating function
rather than on the tail.

The moment-generating function is chosen because it multiplies over independent variables, so the variance proxies of
independent sub-Gaussian variables add, exactly as Gaussian variances do. The tail bound of
a sum then follows. This is the mechanism behind <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's
inequality</NoteLink> and behind most concentration arguments in learning theory.

The variance proxy is an upper bound on the variance, not the variance itself. It describes the worst behaviour of the
tail, and for an asymmetric variable it can be much larger than the variance.

## Definition and first consequences

**The tail bound.** Let $X$ be $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and let $t > 0$. The
<NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds">Chernoff bound</NoteLink> applies
<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's inequality</NoteLink> to $e^{\lambda(X - \mu)}$ for any
$\lambda > 0$:

$$
\prob(X - \mu \ge t) \le e^{-\lambda t}\,\expect\brack{e^{\lambda(X - \mu)}} \le \exp\paren{-\lambda t + \frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2}}.
$$

The exponent is a quadratic in $\lambda$ with its minimum at $\lambda = t/\sigma^2$. Substituting this value gives the
exponent $-t^2/\sigma^2 + t^2/(2\sigma^2) = -t^2/(2\sigma^2)$, so

$$
\prob(X - \mu \ge t) \le e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
$$

The definition holds for negative $\lambda$ too, so $-X$ is also $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian and the lower tail has the same
bound. Adding the two tails gives $\prob(\abs{X - \mu} \ge t) \le 2e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}$.

**The variance is at most the proxy.** Both sides of the definition equal 1 at $\lambda = 0$. Expanding each to second
order in $\lambda$ gives $1 + \lambda^2\var(X)/2 + o(\lambda^2)$ on the left, because the first-order term
$\lambda\,\expect[X - \mu]$ is zero, and $1 + \lambda^2\sigma^2/2 + o(\lambda^2)$ on the right. Dividing the inequality
by $\lambda^2$ and letting $\lambda \to 0$ gives $\var(X) \le \sigma^2$. The smallest valid $\sigma^2$ is the **optimal
variance proxy**. A variable whose optimal proxy equals its variance is called strictly sub-Gaussian.

**Equivalent conditions.** Up to constant factors in $\sigma$, the following say the same thing about a centred
variable <Cite id="vershynin2018" />:

- the moment-generating function condition above;
- a tail condition, $\prob(\abs{X} \ge t) \le 2e^{-t^2/K_1^2}$ for all $t \ge 0$;
- a moment condition, $(\expect\abs{X}^p)^{1/p} \le K_2\sqrt p$ for all $p \ge 1$;
- an integrability condition, $\expect[e^{X^2/K_3^2}] \le 2$.

Each $K_i$ is within a universal constant factor of $\sigma$. The moment condition says the $p$-th moments of a
sub-Gaussian variable grow no faster than those of a Gaussian, like $\sqrt p$ after taking the $p$-th root.

<Derivation title="From the tail to the moments">
Let $X$ be centred with $\prob(\abs{X} \ge t) \le 2e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}$, the tail bound of a $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian
variable. For a non-negative variable, $\expect[Y] = \int_0^\infty \prob(Y \ge s)\,ds$. With $Y = \abs{X}^p$ and the
substitution $s = t^p$, $ds = pt^{p-1}dt$:

$$
\expect\abs{X}^p = \int_0^\infty p t^{p-1}\prob(\abs{X} \ge t)\,dt \le 2p\int_0^\infty t^{p-1}e^{-t^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dt.
$$

Substitute $u = t^2/(2\sigma^2)$, so $t = \sigma\sqrt{2u}$ and $dt = \sigma\,du/\sqrt{2u}$. The integral becomes

$$
2p\int_0^\infty (2\sigma^2u)^{(p-1)/2}e^{-u}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2u}}\,du = 2p\,\sigma^p\,2^{p/2 - 1}\int_0^\infty u^{p/2 - 1}e^{-u}\,du = p\,2^{p/2}\sigma^p\,\Gamma(p/2).
$$

<NoteLink to="gamma-function">Stirling's formula</NoteLink> gives $\Gamma(p/2)^{1/p} \le C\sqrt p$ for a universal
constant $C$, so $(\expect\abs{X}^p)^{1/p} \le C'\sigma\sqrt p$. At $p = 2$ the bound reads
$\expect[X^2] \le 4\sigma^2$, four times looser than the direct argument above, which is the price of going through the
tail.
</Derivation>

## Examples

**Gaussian.** For $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\expect[e^{\lambda(X - \mu)}] = e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}$ exactly. The
<NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian</NoteLink> is $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, and strictly so.

**Random signs.** A Rademacher variable $\varepsilon$ takes the values $-1$ and $+1$ with probability $\frac12$ each.
Its moment-generating function is $\cosh\lambda = \sum_{k \ge 0}\lambda^{2k}/(2k)!$. Each term satisfies
$(2k)! \ge 2^k k!$, because $(2k)!/k! = (k + 1)(k + 2)\cdots(2k)$ is a product of $k$ factors, each at least 2. So

$$
\cosh\lambda \le \sum_{k \ge 0}\frac{(\lambda^2/2)^k}{k!} = e^{\lambda^2/2},
$$

and $\varepsilon$ is 1-sub-Gaussian. Its variance is also 1, so it is strictly sub-Gaussian. Random signs are the noise
of <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink>.

**Bounded variables (Hoeffding's lemma).** If $a \le X \le b$, then
$\expect[e^{\lambda(X - \mu)}] \le e^{\lambda^2(b - a)^2/8}$ for every $\lambda$. Comparing with the definition,
$\sigma^2 = (b - a)^2/4$, so $X$ is $\frac{b - a}{2}$-sub-Gaussian. The proof bounds $e^{\lambda x}$ by the chord of the
<NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> on $[a, b]$ and expands the logarithm of the result
to second order; it is given in the note on <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>. The
proxy $(b - a)^2/4$ is the largest variance any law on $[a, b]$ can have, attained by the two-point law with mass
$\frac12$ at each end.

**Bernoulli variables.** A <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli</NoteLink>$(p)$ variable lies in $[0, 1]$,
so Hoeffding's lemma gives the proxy $\frac14$. Its optimal proxy is

$$
\sigma^2_{\text{opt}} = \frac{1 - 2p}{2\ln\paren{(1 - p)/p}},
$$

which tends to $\frac14$ as $p \to \frac12$ <Cite id="arbel2020" />. For $p = 0.1$ the variance is $0.09$, the optimal
proxy is $0.182$ and Hoeffding's proxy is $0.25$. The optimal proxy is twice the variance. A Bernoulli variable is
strictly sub-Gaussian only at $p = \frac12$: the rare value $1$ makes the upper tail heavier than a Gaussian with the
same variance would allow <Cite id="arbel2020" />.

## Sums and tails

**Proxies add under <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_m$ be independent, with
$X_i$ $\sigma_i$-sub-Gaussian and mean $\mu_i$. The moment-generating function of a sum of independent variables is the
product of theirs, so

$$
\expect\brack{e^{\lambda\sum_i(X_i - \mu_i)}} = \prod_{i=1}^m \expect\brack{e^{\lambda(X_i - \mu_i)}} \le \prod_{i=1}^m e^{\lambda^2\sigma_i^2/2} = e^{\lambda^2\sum_i\sigma_i^2/2}.
$$

The sum is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy $\sum_i\sigma_i^2$. Scaling by a constant $c$ multiplies the proxy by
$c^2$, because $\lambda$ can absorb $c$. The mean $\bar X = \frac1m\sum_i X_i$ of $m$ variables with a common proxy
$\sigma^2$ is therefore sub-Gaussian with proxy $\sigma^2/m$, and the tail bound gives

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{\bar X - \expect\bar X} \ge t} \le 2\exp\paren{-\frac{mt^2}{2\sigma^2}}.
$$

**Hoeffding's inequality is the bounded case.** For variables in $[a, b]$, $\sigma^2 = (b - a)^2/4$, and the bound
becomes $2\exp(-2mt^2/(b - a)^2)$, which is Hoeffding's inequality. For losses in $[0, 1]$ this is the
"concentrate" move of the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>: the
empirical risk of one fixed hypothesis is within $\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$ of its true risk with probability at least
$1 - \delta$.

**Weighted sums of random signs.** For a fixed vector $\avec \in \reals^m$ and independent Rademacher signs
$\varepsilon_i$, the sum $\sum_i a_i\varepsilon_i$ is $\norm{\avec}_2$-sub-Gaussian, since each term is
$\abs{a_i}$-sub-Gaussian. This one fact, combined with a bound on the maximum of finitely many sub-Gaussian variables,
proves <NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's finite class lemma</NoteLink> for Rademacher complexity.

**Maxima.** The maximum of $N$ variables, each $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian with mean zero, satisfies
$\expect\max_{i \le N} X_i \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$, with no independence assumption. The maximum grows only like the
square root of the logarithm of the number of variables. The proof and its refinements are in
<NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequalities</NoteLink>.

## Sub-exponential variables

Some variables have tails heavier than a Gaussian's but still exponential. The square of a Gaussian is the standard
example: $\prob(Z^2 \ge t) = \prob(\abs Z \ge \sqrt t)$ falls like $e^{-t/2}$, not like $e^{-t^2}$. Its
moment-generating function is infinite for $\lambda \ge \frac12$, so no sub-Gaussian bound can hold for every $\lambda$.

A variable $X$ with mean $\mu$ is **sub-exponential with parameters $(\nu, \alpha)$** if

$$
\expect\brack{e^{\lambda(X - \mu)}} \le e^{\lambda^2\nu^2/2} \qquad \text{for } \abs\lambda < 1/\alpha.
$$

The condition is the sub-Gaussian one, required only near $\lambda = 0$ <Cite id="wainwright2019" />. Every
sub-Gaussian variable is sub-exponential with $\alpha = 0$.

**The tail has two regimes.** For $t \ge 0$,

$$
\prob(X - \mu \ge t) \le \begin{cases} e^{-t^2/(2\nu^2)}, & 0 \le t \le \nu^2/\alpha, \\ e^{-t/(2\alpha)}, & t > \nu^2/\alpha. \end{cases}
$$

Small deviations look Gaussian with variance $\nu^2$; large deviations decay only exponentially
<Cite id="wainwright2019" />. To derive this, apply the Chernoff bound with $0 \le \lambda < 1/\alpha$, which gives the
exponent $-\lambda t + \lambda^2\nu^2/2$. Its unconstrained minimiser $\lambda = t/\nu^2$ is allowed when
$t < \nu^2/\alpha$, and gives $-t^2/(2\nu^2)$ as before. For larger $t$ the exponent is still decreasing at the
boundary, so take $\lambda \uparrow 1/\alpha$. The exponent becomes $-t/\alpha + \nu^2/(2\alpha^2)$, and
$\nu^2/\alpha \le t$ makes the second term at most $t/(2\alpha)$, which leaves $-t/(2\alpha)$.

**Example: the chi-squared variable.** Let $Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. For $\lambda < \frac12$,
$\expect[e^{\lambda(Z^2 - 1)}] = e^{-\lambda}/\sqrt{1 - 2\lambda}$, and this is at most $e^{2\lambda^2}$ for
$\abs\lambda < \frac14$. So $Z^2$ is sub-exponential with $(\nu, \alpha) = (2, 4)$ <Cite id="wainwright2019" />. Sums
behave as for sub-Gaussian variables: the parameters $\nu^2$ add and $\alpha$ takes the maximum. A
<NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared</NoteLink> variable with $m$ degrees of freedom is a sum of $m$
independent copies, with parameters $(2\sqrt m, 4)$. Its first regime ends at $\nu^2/\alpha = m$, so for $t \in (0, 1)$,

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{\frac{1}{m}\chi^2_m - 1} \ge t} \le 2e^{-mt^2/8}.
$$

**Products of sub-Gaussians.** The square of a $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian variable, and the product of two sub-Gaussian
variables, are sub-exponential <Cite id="vershynin2018" />. Squared errors of sub-Gaussian noise, empirical variances
and <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink> of random vectors are therefore sub-exponential. Their sums
concentrate by <NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's inequality</NoteLink>, which is the sub-exponential tail
bound written with the variance.

## Worked example

**Random signs.** Let $T = \varepsilon_1 + \dots + \varepsilon_{100}$ be a sum of 100 independent random signs. Each
sign is 1-sub-Gaussian, so $T$ is 10-sub-Gaussian ($\sigma^2 = 100$). The probability that $T$ reaches 30 is at most

$$
\prob(T \ge 30) \le e^{-30^2/(2 \cdot 100)} = e^{-4.5} = 0.011.
$$

The exact value is the probability of at least 65 heads in 100 fair flips, $0.0018$. The bound is six times too large,
which is typical of exponential bounds at moderate deviations; the missing factor is polynomial in the deviation.
Cantelli's one-sided Chebyshev bound, $\sigma^2/(\sigma^2 + t^2) = 100/1\,000 = 0.1$, is nine times weaker again.

**A mean of losses.** A classifier is tested on $m = 100$ independent points, each with 0–1 loss in $[0, 1]$. The test
error is the mean of the losses. Each loss has proxy $\frac14$, so the mean has proxy $1/(4m) = 0.0025$.

1. **Bound at $t = 0.1$.** $\prob(\abs{\bar X - \mu} \ge 0.1) \le 2e^{-0.01/(2 \cdot 0.0025)} = 2e^{-2} = 0.27$.
2. **With the optimal proxy.** If the true error rate is $p = 0.1$, the optimal proxy $0.182$ applies to each loss. The
   bound becomes $2e^{-100 \cdot 0.01/(2 \cdot 0.182)} = 2e^{-2.75} = 0.13$.
3. **The truth.** The exact probability that a Binomial$(100, 0.1)$ count is 0 or at least 20 is $0.0020$.
4. **Sample size.** To guarantee $\abs{\bar X - \mu} < 0.05$ with probability $0.95$ for every error rate, Hoeffding's
   proxy needs $m \ge \ln(2/0.05)/(2 \cdot 0.05^2) = 738$ test points.

The sub-Gaussian bound with the worst-case proxy is valid for every error rate, but at $p = 0.1$ it is more than a
hundred times too large. The variance $0.09$ is much smaller than the proxy, and no sub-Gaussian bound can use it,
because the proxy must also control the upper tail of the rare value 1. A bound that uses the variance is
<NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's inequality</NoteLink>; the exact binomial tail is used in
<NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bounds</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **The proxy is not the variance.** A sub-Gaussian bound with $\sigma^2$ set to the sample variance is not a valid
  bound for asymmetric variables. The optimal proxy of a Bernoulli$(0.01)$ variable is $0.11$, eleven times its variance
  of $0.0099$.
- **"Sub-Gaussian" without a constant says little.** Every bounded variable is sub-Gaussian. Bounds are only as good as
  the proxy that is plugged in.
- **Heavy tails are not covered.** Variables with polynomial tails, such as a Student-$t$ or a Pareto variable, have
  infinite moment-generating functions for every $\lambda > 0$ and are neither sub-Gaussian nor sub-exponential.
  Averages of such variables need median-of-means estimators or truncation.
- **Independence is needed for adding proxies.** The maximal inequality needs no independence, but the sum rule does.
  For dependent sequences, <NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink> versions such as the
  <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">Azuma–Hoeffding inequality</NoteLink> replace it.
