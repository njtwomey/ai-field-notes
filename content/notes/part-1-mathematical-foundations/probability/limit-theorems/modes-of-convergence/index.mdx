<Definition>
Let $X, X_1, X_2, \dots$ be <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> on a common
<NoteLink to="probability-space">probability space</NoteLink> $(\Omega, \Fcal, \prob)$, with distribution functions
$F, F_1, F_2, \dots$.

- **Almost surely:** $X_n \to X$ a.s. if $\prob\paren{\set{\omega : X_n(\omega) \to X(\omega)}} = 1$.
- **In probability:** $X_n \to X$ in probability if $\prob(\abs{X_n - X} > \epsilon) \to 0$ for every $\epsilon > 0$.
- **In $L^p$** ($p \ge 1$): $X_n \to X$ in $L^p$ if $\expect\abs{X_n - X}^p \to 0$. For $p = 2$ this is convergence
  in mean square.
- **In distribution:** $X_n \to X$ in distribution if $F_n(x) \to F(x)$ at every $x$ where $F$ is continuous.

Convergence in distribution concerns only the distributions, so the $X_n$ need not share a probability space
<Cite id="grimmett2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The modes differ in what they compare.

- **Almost sure** convergence follows each outcome $\omega$ separately and asks whether the sequence of numbers
  $X_n(\omega)$ converges. It fails only on a set of outcomes of probability 0.
- **In probability** asks, at each large $n$, whether $X_n$ is close to $X$ with high probability. The rare outcomes
  where $X_n$ is far away may differ from one $n$ to the next.
- **In $L^p$** asks that the average size of the error, raised to the power $p$, vanishes. Rare but huge errors can
  prevent it.
- **In distribution** asks only that histograms of $X_n$ come to look like the histogram of $X$. It says nothing about
  $X_n$ and $X$ being close as random variables.

The <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">strong law of large numbers</NoteLink> is an almost sure statement, the weak
law a statement in probability, and the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> a
statement in distribution.

## Implications

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\text{a.s.} & & & & \\
& \searrow & & & \\
& & \text{in probability} & \Longrightarrow & \text{in distribution} \\
& \nearrow & & & \\
L^q \Rightarrow L^p \ (q \ge p) & & & &
\end{array}
$$

**$L^p$ implies in probability.** <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's inequality</NoteLink>
applied to $\abs{X_n - X}^p$ gives

$$
\prob(\abs{X_n - X} > \epsilon) = \prob\paren{\abs{X_n - X}^p > \epsilon^p} \le \frac{\expect\abs{X_n - X}^p}{\epsilon^p}
\to 0.
$$

**$L^q$ implies $L^p$ for $q \ge p$.** Jensen's inequality for the <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex
function</NoteLink> $u \mapsto u^{q/p}$ gives $(\expect\abs{Y}^p)^{q/p} \le \expect\abs{Y}^q$. With $Y = X_n - X$, the
$L^p$ error is at most the $L^q$ error.

**Almost surely implies in probability.** Let $A_n = \set{\sup_{m \ge n}\abs{X_m - X} > \epsilon}$. The events $A_n$
decrease in $n$, and on the probability-1 set where $X_m \to X$, every outcome eventually leaves $A_n$. Continuity of
probability along a decreasing sequence gives $\prob(A_n) \to 0$. Since $\set{\abs{X_n - X} > \epsilon} \subseteq
A_n$, the claim follows.

**In probability implies in distribution.** Fix a continuity point $x$ of $F$ and $\epsilon > 0$. If $X_n \le x$, then
either $X \le x + \epsilon$ or $\abs{X_n - X} > \epsilon$. Therefore

$$
F_n(x) \le F(x + \epsilon) + \prob(\abs{X_n - X} > \epsilon).
$$

The same reasoning with the roles swapped gives $F(x - \epsilon) \le F_n(x) + \prob(\abs{X_n - X} > \epsilon)$. Let
$n \to \infty$: $F(x - \epsilon) \le \liminf F_n(x) \le \limsup F_n(x) \le F(x + \epsilon)$. Let $\epsilon \to 0$:
continuity of $F$ at $x$ gives $F_n(x) \to F(x)$.

**In distribution to a constant implies in probability.** If $X_n \to c$ in distribution, then
$\prob(\abs{X_n - c} > \epsilon) \le F_n(c - \epsilon) + 1 - F_n(c + \epsilon) \to 0 + 1 - 1 = 0$, because the limit
distribution function jumps from 0 to 1 at $c$.

## Counterexamples

No other implication holds in general. Let $U$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$.

- **In probability but not almost surely.** Write $n = 2^k + j$ with $0 \le j < 2^k$, and let $X_n =
  \indicator\set{j/2^k \le U < (j + 1)/2^k}$. The indicator's interval sweeps across $[0, 1]$ at width $2^{-k}$, then
  again at half the width, and so on. $\prob(X_n = 1) = 2^{-k} \to 0$, so $X_n \to 0$ in probability and in every
  $L^p$. But every $U$ is covered once in every sweep, so $X_n(\omega) = 1$ infinitely often and $X_n$ converges for
  no outcome.
- **Almost surely but not in $L^1$.** Let $X_n = n\,\indicator\set{U < 1/n}$. For each outcome with $U > 0$, $X_n = 0$
  once $n > 1/U$, so $X_n \to 0$ almost surely. But $\expect[X_n] = 1$ for every $n$, so $X_n \not\to 0$ in $L^1$.
- **In distribution but not in probability.** Let $Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $X_n = -Z$ for every $n$. Every $X_n$ has
  the distribution of $Z$, so $X_n \to Z$ in distribution. But $\abs{X_n - Z} = 2\abs{Z}$ does not shrink.

Two partial converses hold. Convergence in probability implies almost sure convergence along a subsequence, chosen
so that $\sum_k\prob(\abs{X_{n_k} - X} > 2^{-k}) < \infty$, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma. Convergence in probability
implies $L^1$ convergence exactly when the $X_n$ are uniformly integrable <Cite id="durrett2019" />.

## Continuous mapping and Slutsky's theorem

**Continuous mapping theorem.** If $g$ is continuous and $X_n \to X$ in one of three senses (almost surely, in
probability or in distribution), then $g(X_n) \to g(X)$ in the same sense <Cite id="vandervaart1998" />. For almost
sure convergence this is immediate from the continuity of $g$ at each outcome.

**Slutsky's theorem.** If $X_n \to X$ in distribution and $Y_n \to c$ in probability for a constant $c$, then

$$
X_n + Y_n \to X + c, \qquad Y_nX_n \to cX, \qquad X_n/Y_n \to X/c \ (c \ne 0),
$$

all in distribution. Convergence in distribution of each
sequence separately does not determine the joint distribution, but a constant limit does: $(X_n, Y_n) \to (X, c)$ in
distribution jointly, and the continuous mapping theorem applied to $(x, y) \mapsto x + y$, $xy$ or $x/y$ gives the
three results.

**Example: the $t$-statistic.** Let $\bar X_n$ and $\hat\sigma_n$ be the sample mean and
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of $n$ independent draws with mean $\mu$ and
variance $\sigma^2$. The central limit theorem gives $\sqrt n(\bar X_n -
\mu)/\sigma \to \Gauss(0, 1)$. The law of large numbers gives $\hat\sigma_n \to \sigma$ in probability, so
$\sigma/\hat\sigma_n \to 1$ in probability. Slutsky's theorem gives

$$
\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\hat\sigma_n} = \frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\hat\sigma_n}
\to \Gauss(0, 1)
$$

in distribution. This justifies large-sample <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink> that
plug in an estimated standard deviation, whatever the distribution of the data.

**Delta method.** If $\sqrt n(T_n - \theta) \to \Gauss(0, \tau^2)$ and $g$ is differentiable at $\theta$, then
$\sqrt n(g(T_n) - g(\theta)) \to \Gauss(0, g'(\theta)^2\tau^2)$. The proof writes $g(T_n) - g(\theta) = (g'(\theta) +
r_n)(T_n - \theta)$ with $r_n \to 0$ in probability and applies Slutsky's theorem. The
<NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">delta method</NoteLink> gives <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
errors</NoteLink> for ratios of means in <Gloss name="ab-test" plural />.

## In machine learning

- **Consistency** of an estimator means convergence in probability to the true parameter. Strong consistency means
  almost sure convergence.
- **<Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>** convergence results are stated in $L^2$ (expected squared
  distance to the optimum) or almost surely, depending on the step-size schedule.
- **Asymptotic normality** of maximum-likelihood estimators, used for standard errors and Wald tests, is convergence
  in distribution combined with Slutsky's theorem.

## Pitfalls

- **Convergence in distribution does not make variables close.** Two sequences can have the same limiting
  distribution and be independent of each other.
- **Moments need not converge.** Almost sure convergence does not imply convergence of means, as the $n\,\indicator\set{U
  < 1/n}$ example shows.
- **Sums of limits in distribution.** If $X_n \to X$ and $Y_n \to Y$ in distribution, $X_n + Y_n$ need not converge to
  $X + Y$. Slutsky's theorem requires one limit to be a constant.
