<Definition>
Limit theorems describe the behaviour of sums and averages of many <NoteLink to="random-variable">random
variables</NoteLink>. Asymptotic results, such as the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, say what
happens as the number of variables $n$ grows without bound <Cite id="durrett2019" />. Concentration inequalities give
explicit bounds at each finite $n$ on the probability that an average strays from
its mean <Cite id="boucheron2013" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Machine learning estimates expectations by averages. The training loss estimates the expected loss, a test-set accuracy
estimates the accuracy on future data, and a Monte Carlo estimate averages samples. The law of large numbers says these
averages converge. The central limit theorem gives their approximate distribution, which yields standard errors and
confidence intervals. Concentration inequalities give guarantees that hold for every sample size, and they are the main
tool of learning theory: generalisation bounds, <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bounds</NoteLink> and the
confidence widths of bandit algorithms all come from them.

## Main ideas

**Random variables converge in several senses.** The note on <NoteLink to="modes-of-convergence">modes of
convergence</NoteLink> separates almost sure convergence, convergence in probability, convergence in mean $p$-th power
and convergence in distribution, and records which implies which.

**Averages converge to the mean.** The <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">law of large numbers</NoteLink> says the mean
$\bar X_n$ of $n$ independent draws with finite mean $\mu$ converges to $\mu$. With finite variance the weak law follows
from Chebyshev's inequality, with deviations of order $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$.

**Fluctuations around the mean are Gaussian.** The <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink>
says the standardised sum $(S_n - n\mu) / (\sigma\sqrt{n})$ converges in distribution to $\Gauss(0, 1)$, whatever the
distribution of one draw. The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the error of this approximation.

**Exponential moments give exponential tails.** A <NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds">Chernoff bound</NoteLink> applies
Markov's inequality to $e^{tX}$ and optimises over $t$, so tail probabilities of sums decay exponentially in $n$.
<NoteLink to="sub-gaussian-random-variables" /> are those whose moment-generating function is dominated by a
Gaussian's, and their tails fall like $e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$.

**Named inequalities for common settings.** <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality" /> bounds sums of independent bounded
variables, so a mean of $n$ draws in $[0, 1]$ deviates by more than $\epsilon$ with probability at most
$e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$. <NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality" /> adds the variance and is much tighter when the variance is
small. <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality" /> extends Hoeffding's bound from sums to any function that changes little
when one input changes.

**The largest of many variables grows slowly.** <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities" /> bound the expected maximum of $N$
sub-Gaussian variables by $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$, without independence. This logarithmic growth is why a model can be
selected from many candidates with a modest sample.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="modes-of-convergence">modes of convergence</NoteLink>, which the two classical theorems use.
The <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">law of large numbers</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> form the asymptotic half of the topic. The
concentration half starts with <NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds" />. Sub-Gaussian variables, Hoeffding's, Bernstein's and
McDiarmid's inequalities follow in that order, each building on the previous ones. The
<NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequalities</NoteLink> note comes last and also uses the
<NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink>.
