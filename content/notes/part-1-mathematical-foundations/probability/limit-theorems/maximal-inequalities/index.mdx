<Definition>
Let $X_1, \dots, X_N$ be <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink>, not necessarily independent, each
with mean zero and <NoteLink to="sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian</NoteLink> with variance proxy $\sigma^2$:
$\expect e^{\lambda X_i} \le e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}$ for every real $\lambda$. The **sub-Gaussian maximal inequality**
states that

$$
\expect\brack{\max_{1 \le i \le N} X_i} \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}, \qquad
\expect\brack{\max_{1 \le i \le N}\abs{X_i}} \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln(2N)}.
$$

More generally, a **maximal inequality** is any bound on the size of $\max_i X_i$, or of $\sup_t X_t$ over an infinite
index set, in terms of the tails of the individual variables <Cite id="boucheron2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

One standard Gaussian exceeds 3 with probability $0.0013$. Among a thousand of them, a value above 3 is likely. The
largest of $N$ variables is pushed out into the tail, and a maximal inequality says how far.

The <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> locates the level that the maximum rarely exceeds. Each $X_i$
exceeds $t$ with probability at most $e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$. The probability that some $X_i$ exceeds $t$ is at most
$N e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$, which is small once $t$ passes $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$. For $t$ above that level, the maximum
exceeds $t$ with small probability; below it the union bound gives no control. The bound in expectation makes this
precise.

The growth is slow. Raising $N$ from 10 to a million moves the bound from $2.1\sigma$ to $5.3\sigma$. Lighter tails
give slower growth still, and heavier tails give faster growth: for variables that only have a finite variance
$\sigma^2$, the best general bound is $\expect\max_i\abs{X_i} \le \sqrt{\expect\sum_i X_i^2} = \sigma\sqrt N$, from
<Gloss name="jensens-inequality" />. The logarithm is what sub-Gaussian tails buy.

The bound holds whatever the dependence between the variables. Identical
copies $X_1 = \dots = X_N$ have $\expect\max_i X_i = 0$, yet the bound is still $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$. Bounds that
use the dependence, such as <NoteLink to="chaining-and-dudley-entropy-integral">chaining</NoteLink>, start from this
one and apply it at many scales.

## The sub-Gaussian maximal inequality

Three forms are used, each from the same two ingredients: the sub-Gaussian
<Gloss name="mgf">moment-generating function</Gloss> bound and the replacement of a maximum by a sum.

- **In expectation:** $\expect\max_i X_i \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$.
- **With absolute values:** $\expect\max_i\abs{X_i} \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln(2N)}$. The variables
  $X_1, \dots, X_N, -X_1, \dots, -X_N$ are $2N$ sub-Gaussian variables with the same proxy, and their maximum is
  $\max_i\abs{X_i}$.
- **With high probability:** for every $\delta \in (0, 1)$,
  $\prob\paren{\max_i X_i \ge \sigma\sqrt{2\ln(N/\delta)}} \le \delta$. Each $X_i$ has the tail
  $\prob(X_i \ge t) \le e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$, by a <NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds">Chernoff bound</NoteLink>. The union
  bound over the $N$ variables gives $N e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$, which equals $\delta$ at $t = \sigma\sqrt{2\ln(N/\delta)}$.

If the variables have different proxies $\sigma_i^2$, the bounds hold with $\sigma = \max_i\sigma_i$.

## Derivation

The proof in expectation passes the maximum through an exponential, where it can be bounded by a sum. Write
$Z = \max_i X_i$ and fix $\lambda > 0$.

1. **Exponentiate (Jensen).** The function $u \mapsto e^{\lambda u}$ is convex, so
   <Gloss name="jensens-inequality" /> gives $e^{\lambda\expect Z} \le \expect e^{\lambda Z}$.
2. **Replace the maximum by a sum.** The exponential is increasing, so $e^{\lambda Z} = \max_i e^{\lambda X_i}$. Every
   term is positive, so the maximum is at most the sum:

   $$
   \expect e^{\lambda Z} \le \sum_{i=1}^N\expect e^{\lambda X_i}.
   $$

   This step uses linearity of expectation only, so it needs no <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.
3. **Apply the sub-Gaussian bound** to each term: $\expect e^{\lambda X_i} \le e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}$. Steps 1 to 3
   give $e^{\lambda\expect Z} \le N e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}$.
4. **Take logarithms and divide by $\lambda$:**

   $$
   \expect Z \le \frac{\ln N}{\lambda} + \frac{\lambda\sigma^2}{2}.
   $$

5. **Optimise $\lambda$.** The first term falls and the second rises with $\lambda$. The
   <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $-\ln N/\lambda^2 + \sigma^2/2$ vanishes at
   $\lambda = \sqrt{2\ln N}/\sigma$, where both terms equal $\sigma\sqrt{\ln N/2}$. Their sum is $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$.

Step 2 is where the bound loses. The sum of $N$ exponentials is close to their maximum only when one term dominates,
which is the case for independent variables with large $N$. For correlated variables many terms are close to the
largest, and the sum overcounts.

## How tight it is

For $N$ independent standard Gaussians ($\sigma = 1$) the expected maximum can be computed by
<NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical integration</NoteLink>:

| $N$ | $\expect\max_i X_i$ | $\sqrt{2\ln N}$ | ratio |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10 | 1.539 | 2.146 | 0.72 |
| 100 | 2.508 | 3.035 | 0.83 |
| 1,000 | 3.241 | 3.717 | 0.87 |
| 1,000,000 | 4.863 | 5.257 | 0.93 |

The ratio tends to 1 as $N \to \infty$, so the constant $\sqrt 2$ cannot be improved for independent variables. The
convergence is slow, because the expected maximum carries a negative correction of order
$\ln\ln N/\sqrt{\ln N}$.

For dependent variables the bound can be far from tight, as the identical-copies example shows. For a
<Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> $(X_t)$, the right measure of dependence is the distance
$d(s, t) = \sqrt{\expect(X_s - X_t)^2}$. Sudakov's minoration gives a lower bound in terms of how many points of the
index set are far apart in $d$, and the chaining argument of Dudley gives an upper bound in terms of how many balls of
each radius cover it <Cite id="talagrand2014" />.

## Worked example: the best of a thousand models

A team evaluates $N = 1{,}000$ models on one test set of $m = 1{,}000$ points and reports the best test error. How
optimistic is the reported number?

1. **Each deviation is sub-Gaussian.** For model $j$, the test error $\hat R_j$ is the mean of $m$ independent losses in
   $[0, 1]$. A variable in an interval of width 1 is $\frac12$-sub-Gaussian
   (<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's lemma</NoteLink>), and a mean of $m$ independent such variables is
   $\frac{1}{2\sqrt m}$-sub-Gaussian. So, with $R_j$ the true error of model $j$, the optimism $R_j - \hat R_j$ is
   sub-Gaussian with $\sigma = 1/(2\sqrt{1000}) = 0.0158$.
2. **Apply the maximal inequality.** The deviations of different models share the same test points and are dependent.
   The inequality does not need independence:

   $$
   \expect\max_j(R_j - \hat R_j) \le 0.0158 \times \sqrt{2\ln 1000} = 0.0158 \times 3.717 = 0.059.
   $$

3. **With confidence $95\%$.** The high-probability form gives
   $0.0158 \times \sqrt{2\ln(1000/0.05)} = 0.0158 \times 4.451 = 0.070$.
4. **Against what happens.** Suppose every model has true error $0.3$ and the models' errors are independent. Each
   optimism then has <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sqrt{0.21/1000} = 0.0145$,
   and the Gaussian approximation with the table above gives an expected maximum of $0.0145 \times 3.241 = 0.047$. The
   bound, $0.059$, is 1.25 times that.
5. **Sample size.** Keeping the expected optimism below $0.01$ needs $\sqrt{2\ln N}/(2\sqrt m) \le 0.01$, which is
   $m \ge (3.717/0.02)^2 = 34{,}539$ test points.

A single model evaluated on 1,000 points has an expected optimism of zero. Choosing the best of 1,000 shifts it by up to
six percentage points of error. This is why a test set used for selection no longer gives an unbiased estimate, and why
<NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bounds</NoteLink> for a selected model need a union bound (see also
<NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">adaptive data analysis</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple
testing</NoteLink>).

## In learning theory

The inequality bounds the Rademacher complexity of a finite class. <NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's
lemma</NoteLink> applies it to the Rademacher sums $\frac1m\sum_i\sigma_i a_i$, one per vector $\avec$ in the projection
of a class on a sample. Each sum is sub-Gaussian with proxy $\norm{\avec}^2/m^2$, so the
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> of a projection with $N$ members and largest norm
$r$ is at most $r\sqrt{2\ln N}/m$. For $N = 1{,}000$ labellings with values $\pm 1$ on $m = 100$ points, $r = 10$ and
the bound is $10 \times 3.717/100 = 0.37$. Chaining applies the same inequality to the links between finer and finer
covers of an infinite class.

## Pitfalls

- **Centre first.** The inequality is for mean-zero variables. For variables with means $\mu_i$, it bounds
  $\expect\max_i(X_i - \mu_i)$, and $\expect\max_i X_i$ can be as large as $\max_i\mu_i + \sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$.
- **The proxy, not the variance.** The constant is the largest variance proxy. A variable with small variance but a
  long tail has a large proxy, and the bound uses that.
- **Heavy tails break the logarithm.** For variables with only polynomial tails the maximum grows polynomially in $N$.
  The bound $\sigma\sqrt N$ from the second moment is then the right order.
- **The bound ignores dependence.** For strongly correlated variables it overstates the maximum. A tighter bound needs
  the correlation structure, through chaining or a direct calculation.
