<Definition>
Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss> with finite mean
$\mu = \expect[X_1]$, and let $\bar X_n = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ be the mean of the first $n$ draws.

- The **weak law of large numbers** states that $\bar X_n$ converges to $\mu$ in probability: for every $\epsilon > 0$,
  $\prob(\abs{\bar X_n - \mu} \ge \epsilon) \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
- The **strong law of large numbers** states that $\bar X_n$ converges to $\mu$ almost surely:
  $\prob(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar X_n = \mu) = 1$.

Both laws need only $\expect\abs{X_1} < \infty$ <Cite id="durrett2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A single draw $X_i$ can be far from $\mu$. The mean of $n$ draws averages the deviations $X_i - \mu$, and independent
deviations mostly cancel. The <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of $\bar X_n$ is
$\sigma/\sqrt n$, where $\sigma^2 = \var(X_1)$. The spread of the sample mean therefore shrinks to zero, and the
distribution of $\bar X_n$ piles up at $\mu$.

The two laws differ in what they promise about one infinite sequence of draws. The weak law says that at each fixed
large $n$, a deviation of size $\epsilon$ is improbable. The strong law says that for almost every sequence, the
deviations eventually stay below $\epsilon$ forever.

## The weak law from Chebyshev's inequality

Assume in addition that $\sigma^2 = \var(X_1)$ is finite. Expectation is linear, so

$$
\expect[\bar X_n] = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \expect[X_i] = \mu.
$$

Variances of independent variables add, and scaling a variable by $1/n$ scales its variance by $1/n^2$:

$$
\var(\bar X_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \var(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$

<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Chebyshev's inequality</NoteLink> bounds the probability that a
<NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> lies at least $\epsilon$ from its mean by its variance divided
by $\epsilon^2$. Applied to $\bar X_n$:

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{\bar X_n - \mu} \ge \epsilon} \le \frac{\var(\bar X_n)}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}.
$$

The right side tends to 0 as $n \to \infty$ for every fixed $\epsilon > 0$. This proves the weak law for
distributions with finite variance. The proof uses independence only to make the variances add, so it holds for
pairwise uncorrelated variables with a common mean and bounded variances.

## Worked example: how many coin flips

A coin lands heads with unknown probability $p$. The estimate $\bar X_n$ is the fraction of heads in $n$ flips. How
large must $n$ be so that $\abs{\bar X_n - p} < 0.01$ with probability at least 0.95?

Each flip is $\Bern(p)$ with variance $p(1-p) \le 1/4$. Chebyshev's bound is at most $0.05$ when

$$
\frac{1/4}{n \cdot 0.01^2} \le 0.05 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n \ge \frac{1/4}{0.05 \times 10^{-4}} = 50\,000.
$$

This $n$ is guaranteed for every $p$ and uses nothing but the variance. Sharper tools give smaller answers:
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> gives $n \ge 18\,445$, and the normal
approximation from the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> gives about $9\,604$. The
normal approximation is not a guarantee at finite $n$; the two inequalities are.

## The weak law with only a finite mean

Finite variance is not needed. Khinchin's weak law assumes only $\expect\abs{X_1} < \infty$
<Cite id="grimmett2020" />. One proof truncates: split each $X_i$ into a bounded part, to which the Chebyshev argument
applies, and a tail whose contribution vanishes because the mean is finite. The shorter proof below uses
<NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">characteristic functions</NoteLink>.

<Derivation title="The weak law from characteristic functions">
Let $\varphi(t) = \expect[e^{itX_1}]$ be the characteristic function of $X_1$. Because $\expect\abs{X_1} < \infty$,
$\varphi$ is differentiable at 0 with $\varphi'(0) = i\mu$, so $\varphi(t) = 1 + i\mu t + o(t)$ as $t \to 0$.
Independence turns the characteristic function of a sum into a product, so

$$
\expect\brack{e^{it\bar X_n}} = \varphi(t/n)^n = \paren{1 + \frac{i\mu t}{n} + o\paren{\frac1n}}^n \to e^{i\mu t}.
$$

The limit $e^{i\mu t}$ is the characteristic function of the constant $\mu$. Lévy's continuity theorem then gives
$\bar X_n \to \mu$ in distribution. Convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability:
$\prob(\abs{\bar X_n - \mu} \ge \epsilon) = F_n(\mu - \epsilon) + 1 - F_n((\mu + \epsilon)^-)$, and both terms tend to 0
because $\mu \pm \epsilon$ are continuity points of the limit distribution function.
</Derivation>

## The strong law

The strong law is stronger than the weak law. Convergence almost surely implies convergence in probability, and the
converse fails in general (see <NoteLink to="modes-of-convergence">modes of convergence</NoteLink>). Kolmogorov proved
the strong law for independent identically distributed variables with a finite mean. Etemadi later showed that
pairwise independence suffices <Cite id="durrett2019" />. The proof below assumes a finite fourth moment, which makes
it shorter than the general proof.

<Derivation title="The strong law under a finite fourth moment">
Assume $\expect[X_1^4] = K < \infty$ and, without loss of generality, $\mu = 0$. Let $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$. Expanding
$S_n^4$ gives terms $X_iX_jX_kX_l$. Any term in which some index appears exactly once has expectation 0, by
independence and $\expect[X_i] = 0$. The surviving terms are the $n$ terms $X_i^4$ and the $3n(n-1)$ terms
$X_i^2X_j^2$ with $i \ne j$. Therefore

$$
\expect[S_n^4] = nK + 3n(n-1)\sigma^4 \le Cn^2
$$

for a constant $C$. Markov's inequality applied to $S_n^4$ gives

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{\bar X_n} \ge \epsilon} = \prob\paren{S_n^4 \ge n^4\epsilon^4} \le \frac{Cn^2}{n^4\epsilon^4} =
\frac{C}{\epsilon^4 n^2}.
$$

The sum over $n$ of the right side is finite. The first Borel–Cantelli lemma states that if
$\sum_n \prob(A_n) < \infty$ then with probability 1 only finitely many of the events $A_n$ occur. With
$A_n = \set{\abs{\bar X_n} \ge \epsilon}$, the mean $\bar X_n$ eventually stays within $\epsilon$ of 0, almost surely.
Taking $\epsilon = 1/k$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$ and intersecting the countably many probability-1 events gives
$\bar X_n \to 0$ almost surely.
</Derivation>

## When the law fails

The laws need a finite mean. The standard <NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution">Cauchy distribution</NoteLink> has
$\expect\abs{X_1} = \infty$. Its characteristic function is $e^{-\abs t}$, so the characteristic function of
$\bar X_n$ is $(e^{-\abs{t}/n})^n = e^{-\abs t}$. The mean of $n$ Cauchy draws therefore has the same Cauchy
distribution as one draw. Averaging does not concentrate it at all, which the Cauchy option in the figure shows.

## In machine learning

- **Empirical risk.** The training loss $\frac1n\sum_i \ell(f(\xvec_i), y_i)$ of a fixed model $f$ converges to its
  expected loss. Learning theory needs this uniformly over a class of models, which the law of large numbers alone
  does not give.
- **Monte Carlo estimates.** <NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo integration</NoteLink> estimates
  $\expect[f(X)]$ by an average of $f$ over samples. The strong law guarantees that the estimate converges whenever
  $\expect\abs{f(X)}$ is finite.
- **Frequencies as probabilities.** The fraction of times an event occurs in independent trials converges to its
  probability. This justifies estimating probabilities by counting.

## Assumptions

- **Identical distribution** can be relaxed. The Chebyshev proof needs only uncorrelated variables with common mean and
  bounded variances.
- **Independence** can be relaxed to weak dependence. Ergodic theorems give laws of large numbers for stationary
  sequences and <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chains</NoteLink>.
- **A finite mean** cannot be dropped, as the Cauchy example shows.

## Pitfalls

- **The law gives no rate.** The weak law says the deviation probability tends to 0, not how fast. Chebyshev gives the
  rate $1/n$; <NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds">Chernoff bounds</NoteLink> give exponential rates for light-tailed
  variables.
- **Heavy tails converge slowly.** A distribution with finite mean but infinite variance satisfies the law, but its
  sample mean can be far from $\mu$ for very large $n$.
- **The law is not a law of averages.** After a run of heads, the next flip is still fair. The fraction of heads
  converges because the early excess is diluted by later flips, not because later flips compensate for it.
