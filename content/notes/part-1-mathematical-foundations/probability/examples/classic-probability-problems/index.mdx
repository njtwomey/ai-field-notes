<Definition>
The classic probability problems are short, concrete puzzles whose answers
surprise most people on first meeting. Each one isolates a rule of probability, such as conditioning, the base rate, the
complement of an event or first-step analysis, and shows where intuition goes wrong. Each note states the problem,
solves it exactly and checks the answer by simulation <Cite id="blitzstein2019" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The same mistakes that these problems expose recur in machine learning practice. Ignoring the base rate inflates the
apparent precision of a classifier for a rare class. Aggregating over a confounder can reverse a comparison between two
models or two treatments. Collisions in hashing, embedding tables and random seeds follow the birthday bound. Evaluating
how long a sampler takes to visit every state is a coupon-collector question. The dishonest casino is the standard first
example of a hidden Markov model, and its algorithms carry over to speech, biology and sequence labelling.

## Main ideas

**Counting complements.** The <NoteLink to="birthday-problem">birthday problem</NoteLink> computes the chance of no
shared birthday and subtracts it from one. With 23 people a shared birthday is more likely than not. Collisions become
likely once the group size reaches about the square root of the number of possible values.

**Waiting in stages.** The <NoteLink to="coupon-collector-problem">coupon collector's problem</NoteLink> splits the wait
to collect all $n$ coupon types into independent geometric stages. The expected total is $n H_n \approx n \ln n$, and
the last few coupons take most of the time.

**Bayes' theorem against intuition.** In <NoteLink to="medical-diagnosis-base-rate">medical diagnosis and the base
rate</NoteLink>, a positive result from a 99%-sensitive test for a disease with 1% prevalence gives only about a 17%
chance of disease, because <Gloss name="fp">false positives</Gloss> from the large healthy group outnumber
<Gloss name="tp">true positives</Gloss>. In the <NoteLink to="monty-hall-problem">Monty Hall problem</NoteLink>,
switching doors wins with probability 2/3, and the answer depends on the host's rule for opening a door.

**Aggregation can reverse a comparison.** <NoteLink to="simpsons-paradox" /> shows a treatment that wins within each
group and loses overall, because it was given mostly to the harder cases. The resolution is to condition on the
variable that affects both treatment and outcome.

**First-step analysis.** <NoteLink to="gamblers-ruin" /> conditions on the outcome of the first bet to get a recursion
for the chance of reaching a target before going broke. The chance is $i/N$ in a fair game and close to zero for a
distant target when the odds are slightly unfavourable.

**Inferring a hidden state.** In the <NoteLink to="occasionally-dishonest-casino">occasionally dishonest
casino</NoteLink>, a casino switches secretly between a fair and a loaded die. The
<Gloss name="forward-backward">forward–backward algorithm</Gloss> gives the probability that each roll used the loaded
die, and the <Gloss name="viterbi">Viterbi algorithm</Gloss> gives the most probable sequence of dice.

## How the notes fit together

The problems are independent and can be read in any order. The <NoteLink to="birthday-problem">birthday
problem</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="medical-diagnosis-base-rate">base-rate example</NoteLink>
need the least background. The <NoteLink to="monty-hall-problem">Monty Hall problem</NoteLink> follows the base-rate
example as a second use of Bayes' theorem. The <NoteLink to="coupon-collector-problem">coupon collector's
problem</NoteLink> needs the geometric distribution, and <NoteLink to="gamblers-ruin">gambler's ruin</NoteLink> uses the
law of total probability in a recursion. The <NoteLink to="occasionally-dishonest-casino">occasionally dishonest
casino</NoteLink> combines conditioning, marginalisation and Bayes' theorem over a sequence and is the most demanding.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem" /> and the
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> from <NoteLink to="probability-theory">probability theory</NoteLink>.
The coupon collector also uses the <NoteLink to="geometric-distribution">geometric distribution</NoteLink>.

Gambler's ruin is a <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> stopped at two barriers, and its fair case is a
<NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink>. The dishonest casino leads to the
<NoteLink to="hidden-markov-model">hidden Markov model</NoteLink>. Simpson's paradox leads to
<NoteLink to="causal-inference-from-observational-data">causal inference from observational data</NoteLink>, and base
rates lead to the precision of a classifier on imbalanced classes.
