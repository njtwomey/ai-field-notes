<Definition>
Probability is the mathematics of uncertain outcomes. It assigns numbers between 0 and 1 to events, combines them by
fixed rules, and describes random quantities by their distributions. From these rules it derives the behaviour of
averages, the laws of named families of distributions, methods to simulate random quantities, and the theory of
processes that evolve randomly in time <Cite id="blitzstein2019" /> <Cite id="grimmett2020" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Machine learning models uncertainty almost everywhere. A classifier outputs a probability distribution over labels, a
<Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> a distribution over the next token, and a generative model a
distribution over images. Losses are negative log-probabilities and risks are
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink>. Training draws random minibatches, and many models inject noise on
purpose, through dropout, the <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink> or the forward
process of a <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss>. Guarantees about generalisation,
<NoteLink to="metric-confidence-intervals">confidence intervals for metrics</NoteLink> and the exploration bonuses of
bandit algorithms all come from limit theorems and concentration inequalities.

## Main ideas

**Conditioning is the core operation.** Conditional probability updates beliefs on evidence, the
<NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> factorises joint distributions, the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> marginalises latent variables, and
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> reverses a conditional. <NoteLink to="graphical-models">Graphical
models</NoteLink>, <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive models</Gloss> and <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian
inference</NoteLink> are organised around these rules.

**A few families do most of the work.** The Bernoulli, categorical, Gaussian, Poisson, gamma, beta and
<NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution">Dirichlet distributions</NoteLink>, and their relatives, model most labels,
counts, rates, proportions and noise. Most of them are <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential
families</NoteLink>, and several form conjugate pairs.

**Averages concentrate.** The <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss> says averages converge to expectations. The
<Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> says their fluctuations are Gaussian of size $1/\sqrt{n}$. Concentration
inequalities such as Hoeffding's give non-asymptotic bounds, which learning theory needs.

**Randomness can be manufactured and differentiated.** Uniform random numbers become draws from any distribution by
transformation, rejection or reweighting. Monte Carlo averages estimate integrals in any dimension. The score-function
and reparameterisation estimators give <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> of expectations, so models with
random components can be trained.

**Random evolution in time.** <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chains</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="random-walk">random
walks</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="martingale">martingales</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="poisson-process">Poisson
processes</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink> model sequences and paths. Stochastic
calculus extends ordinary calculus to Brownian paths, and it is the mathematics
behind diffusion models.
