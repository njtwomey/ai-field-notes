<Definition>
Sampling produces random draws from a given distribution, starting from a source of independent uniform numbers on
$[0, 1]$. Simulation uses such draws to estimate quantities that are hard to compute exactly, chiefly expectations
$\expect_p[f(\xvec)]$, by averaging. Both rest on transformations of random variables and on the law of large numbers
<Cite id="devroye1986" /> <Cite id="owen2013" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Generative models produce output by sampling: a <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> samples the next
token from a <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical distribution</NoteLink>, and a variational autoencoder
decodes a sampled latent vector. <Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>, dropout and data augmentation
all draw random numbers at every step. Integrals over many dimensions, such as posterior expectations, expected rewards
and marginal likelihoods, are estimated by Monte Carlo averages.
Training a model whose loss is itself an expectation over samples needs the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>
of that expectation, which the score-function and reparameterisation estimators supply <Cite id="mohamed2020" />.

## Main ideas

**Transforming a variable transforms its density.** The <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables
formula</NoteLink> gives the density of $Y = g(X)$ as the density of $X$ at $g^{-1}(y)$ divided by the absolute Jacobian
determinant of $g$. Every exact sampler below is a transformation chosen so that this formula produces the target.

**Exact samplers from uniforms.** <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling" /> passes a uniform through the quantile
function. <NoteLink to="rejection-sampling" /> proposes from an easier density and accepts in proportion to the ratio of
densities. The <NoteLink to="box-muller-transform" /> turns two uniforms into two independent Gaussians through a radius
and an angle. Special shapes have their own recipes: <NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere" /> and
<NoteLink to="sampling-the-simplex">sampling the simplex</NoteLink>. The <NoteLink to="gumbel-max-trick" /> samples a
categorical variable by adding Gumbel noise to its logits and taking the argmax.

**Averages estimate expectations.** <NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration" /> estimates $\expect_p[f(\xvec)]$ by the
average of $f$ over $N$ independent draws. The estimate is unbiased, and its error shrinks as $1/\sqrt{N}$ in any
dimension. <NoteLink to="importance-sampling" /> draws from a proposal $q$ and reweights by $p/q$, which can reduce
variance or make sampling possible when $p$ itself is hard to draw from.

**Gradients of expectations.** The <NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function estimator</NoteLink> writes
$\nabla_{\thetavec}\expect[f(\xvec)]$ as $\expect[f(\xvec)\nabla_{\thetavec}\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)]$, which needs
only values of $f$ and works for discrete variables, at the cost of high variance. The
<NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink> writes the sample as a differentiable
function of parameter-free noise, so gradients pass through it with low variance. The note on
<NoteLink to="reparameterisations-of-common-distributions">reparameterisations of common distributions</NoteLink> lists
these functions for common families and what cannot be reparameterised. The Gumbel-softmax relaxation extends the idea
to categorical variables.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables for densities</NoteLink>, which justifies every
transformation. The exact samplers come next, from inverse transforms to the Gumbel-max trick.
<NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration" /> and <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> switch
from producing draws to using them. The three gradient notes come last: score function, then reparameterisation, then
the catalogue of reparameterisations.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="jacobian" /> and the named distributions in
<NoteLink to="continuous-distributions">continuous distributions</NoteLink>. Monte Carlo error rates come from the
<NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">law of large numbers</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central
limit theorem</NoteLink>.

When exact sampling is impossible, <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo" /> builds a Markov chain whose stationary
distribution is the target, and <NoteLink to="particle-filter">particle filters</NoteLink> apply importance sampling
sequentially. The reparameterisation trick trains the <NoteLink to="variational-autoencoder">variational
autoencoder</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="black-box-variational-inference">black-box variational inference</NoteLink>,
and the score-function estimator is the policy gradient of <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss>.
