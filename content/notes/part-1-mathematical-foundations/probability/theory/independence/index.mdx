<Definition>
Events $A$ and $B$ are independent, written $A \perp B$, if $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$.
<NoteLink to="random-variable">Random variables</NoteLink> $X$ and $Y$ are independent if
$P(X \in S, Y \in T) = P(X \in S)\,P(Y \in T)$ for all sets $S$ and $T$; equivalently, their joint pmf or density
factorises, $p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y)$. $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $Z$, written $X \perp Y \mid Z$, if
$p(x, y \mid z) = p(x \mid z)\,p(y \mid z)$ for every $z$ with $p(z) > 0$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Meaning

When $P(B) > 0$, independence is equivalent to $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$: learning that $B$ occurred leaves the
probability of $A$ unchanged. The product form is used as the definition because it is symmetric and also covers
events of probability zero.

For two rolls of a fair die, "the first roll is 6" and "the second roll is 6" are independent: the
<NoteLink to="conditional-probability">joint probability</NoteLink> is $1/36 = (1/6)(1/6)$. For one roll, "even" and "at
most 3" are not independent: $P(\set{2}) = 1/6$, but $P(\text{even})\,P(\le 3) = 1/4$.

Independence is a property of the probability measure, not of the events as sets. Disjoint events with positive
probability are never independent: if one occurs, the other cannot.

## Several events

Events $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are **mutually independent** if the product rule holds for every subcollection:
$P(A_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap A_{i_k}) = P(A_{i_1}) \cdots P(A_{i_k})$. **Pairwise** independence, the product rule for
pairs only, is weaker.

**Example.** Flip two fair coins. Let $A$ be "the first is heads", $B$ "the second is heads" and $C$ "the two coins
agree". Each pair is independent, since every pairwise intersection has probability $1/4$. But $A \cap B$ implies $C$,
so $P(A \cap B \cap C) = 1/4 \ne 1/8$. The three events are not mutually independent.

## Consequences

For independent random variables $X$ and $Y$:

- Any functions of them are independent: $g(X) \perp h(Y)$.
- $\expect[XY] = \expect[X]\,\expect[Y]$, so their covariance is zero.
- The variance of their sum is the sum of variances: $\var(X + Y) = \var(X) + \var(Y)$.
- The density of $X + Y$ is the convolution of their densities.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the consequences">
**Functions.** $\set{g(X) \in S}$ is the event $\set{X \in g^{-1}(S)}$, and likewise for $h(Y)$. The definition of
independence applies to the preimage sets, so $P(g(X) \in S, h(Y) \in T) = P(g(X) \in S)\, P(h(Y) \in T)$.

**Products.** For discrete variables, the joint pmf factorises, so the double sum splits:
$\expect[XY] = \sum_{x, y} xy\, p(x)\, p(y) = \paren{\sum_x x\, p(x)}\paren{\sum_y y\, p(y)}$. The continuous case
is the same with integrals. Then $\cov(X, Y) = \expect[XY] - \expect[X]\expect[Y] = 0$.

**Variances.** $\var(X + Y) = \var(X) + \var(Y) + 2\cov(X, Y)$ for any $X$ and $Y$, and the covariance is zero.

**Convolution.** The cdf of $S = X + Y$ integrates the factorised joint density over the region $x + y \le s$:

$$
P(S \le s) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x) \int_{-\infty}^{s - x} f_Y(y)\, dy\, dx.
$$

Differentiating in $s$ gives $f_S(s) = \int f_X(x)\, f_Y(s - x)\, dx$.
</Derivation>

Zero covariance does not imply independence. If $X \sim \Unif(-1, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$, then $\cov(X, Y) = \expect[X^3] =
0$, yet $Y$ is a function of $X$.

## Conditional independence

$X \perp Y \mid Z$ means that once $Z$ is known, $Y$ carries no further information about $X$:
$p(x \mid y, z) = p(x \mid z)$. This form follows from the definition: $p(x \mid y, z) = p(x, y \mid z)/p(y \mid z) =
p(x \mid z)\,p(y \mid z)/p(y \mid z)$. Conditional and unconditional independence are separate properties.

- **Conditional independence without independence.** Let $Z$ be a coin's unknown bias, and $X$ and $Y$ two flips of
  that coin. Given $Z$ the flips are independent. Without $Z$ they are dependent: a head on the first flip makes a
  high bias more likely, which raises the probability of a head on the second. For a bias uniform on $[0, 1]$,
  $P(\text{head}) = \expect[Z] = 1/2$ but $P(\text{two heads}) = \expect[Z^2] = 1/3 > 1/4$.
- **Independence without conditional independence.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent fair coins and $Z = X + Y$. Then
  $X \perp Y$, but given $Z = 1$, learning $X$ determines $Y$. Conditioning on a common effect creates dependence. This
  is called explaining away, or Berkson's paradox.

Graphical models are built from conditional independence statements: a Bayesian network encodes which variables are
independent of which given their parents <Cite id="koller2009" />.

## In machine learning

- **The iid assumption.** Training data are usually modelled as independent and identically distributed. The
  likelihood of a dataset then factorises into a product over points, and the log-likelihood into a sum.
- **<NoteLink to="naive-bayes">Naive Bayes</NoteLink>** assumes the features are conditionally independent given the
  class, $p(\xvec \mid y) = \prod_j p(x_j \mid y)$. The assumption is rarely true, but it reduces the number of
  parameters from exponential to linear in the number of features.
- **Latent-variable models.** In a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> the data
  points are independent given their component assignments and the parameters, but not marginally.

## Pitfalls


- **Uncorrelated is not independent**, except in special cases such as jointly Gaussian variables.
- **Selection creates dependence.** Analysing only the cases selected by some outcome conditions on that outcome, and
  can induce correlations between causes that are independent in the population.
