<Definition>
Let $A_1, A_2, \dots$ be finitely or countably many events in a <NoteLink to="probability-space">probability
space</NoteLink>. The **union bound**, or Boole's inequality, states

$$
\prob\paren{\bigcup_{i} A_i} \le \sum_{i} \prob(A_i).
$$

The events may be dependent in any way. The bound is an equality when the events are pairwise disjoint, and it can
exceed 1, in which case it says nothing <Cite id="grimmett2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The probability of a union counts each outcome once. The sum $\sum_i \prob(A_i)$ counts an outcome once for every
event that contains it. An outcome in three of the events is counted three times. The sum can therefore only
overcount, and it overcounts exactly by the probability mass of the overlaps.

The union bound is the standard way to turn many statements, each true with high probability, into one statement that
is true for all of them at once. If each of $N$ bad events has probability at most $\delta/N$, then with probability at
least $1 - \delta$ none of them happens. This is how a guarantee for one fixed hypothesis becomes a guarantee for every
hypothesis in a finite class.

## Statement

**Finite form.** For events $A_1, \dots, A_N$, $\prob(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_N) \le \sum_{i=1}^N \prob(A_i)$.

**Countable form.** For events $A_1, A_2, \dots$, $\prob(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^\infty \prob(A_i)$,
where the right side may be infinite.

**Complement form.** Taking complements, the probability that every one of the events $A_i^c$ happens is at least
$1 - \sum_i \prob(A_i)$. This is the form used in learning theory: "with probability at least $1 - \delta$, every
statement in the list holds".

**Bonferroni's lower bound.** Subtracting the pairwise overlaps gives a lower bound,

$$
\sum_i \prob(A_i) - \sum_{i < j} \prob(A_i \cap A_j) \le \prob\paren{\bigcup_i A_i},
$$

so the union bound's overcount is at most $\sum_{i < j}\prob(A_i \cap A_j)$. Alternately adding and subtracting
higher-order intersections gives the inclusion–exclusion formula, whose partial sums alternate between upper and lower
bounds <Cite id="grimmett2020" />.

## Proof

**Two events.** The events $A$ and $B \setminus A$ are disjoint and their union is $A \cup B$, so additivity gives
$\prob(A \cup B) = \prob(A) + \prob(B \setminus A)$. Since $B \setminus A \subseteq B$, monotonicity gives
$\prob(B \setminus A) \le \prob(B)$. Hence $\prob(A \cup B) \le \prob(A) + \prob(B)$. Induction on $N$ extends this to
any finite list.

**Countably many events.** Define the disjoint events $B_1 = A_1$ and
$B_i = A_i \setminus (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{i-1})$ for $i \ge 2$. Each $B_i$ is a subset of $A_i$. Every outcome in the
union of the $A_i$ lies in exactly one $B_i$, the one for the first $A_i$ that contains it, so
$\bigcup_i B_i = \bigcup_i A_i$. Countable additivity on the disjoint $B_i$ and monotonicity on each $B_i \subseteq A_i$
give

$$
\prob\paren{\bigcup_i A_i} = \prob\paren{\bigcup_i B_i} = \sum_i \prob(B_i) \le \sum_i \prob(A_i).
$$

**The same proof with indicators.** At every outcome, $\indicator[\bigcup_i A_i] \le \sum_i \indicator[A_i]$: the left
side is 1 when at least one event happens, and the right side counts how many happen. Taking
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> of both sides gives the bound. The two sides differ exactly on
outcomes that lie in two or more events.

## Tightness

The union bound is an equality when the events are disjoint, and its overcount is largest when they coincide.

- **Disjoint events.** The bound is an equality.
- **Independent rare events.** For $N$ <NoteLink to="independence">independent events</NoteLink> of probability $p$, the
  exact probability of the union is $1 - (1 - p)^N$. With $p = 0.001$ and $N = 100$, the exact value is $0.0952$ and the
  bound is $0.1$. With $N = 1\,000$, the exact value is $0.632$ and the bound is $1$, which says nothing. The bound is
  close while $Np$ is small.
- **Identical events.** For $N$ copies of one event of probability $p$, the union has probability $p$ and the bound is
  $Np$. The overcount factor is $N$.

In learning theory the events are "hypothesis $h$ has empirical risk far from its true risk". Two hypotheses that
disagree on few inputs have nearly the same empirical risk on every sample, so their bad events nearly coincide. A
class with many similar hypotheses is then close to the "identical events" case, and the union bound wastes a factor
close to the number of hypotheses. The capacity measures of <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform
convergence</NoteLink> exist to remove this waste. <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">Symmetrisation</NoteLink> replaces the
infinitely many hypotheses by the finitely many labellings they produce on a sample, and the union bound is then taken
over labellings only.

When the bound is combined with an exponential tail, $N$ events enter the deviation $\epsilon$ only through $\ln N$. If
each event has probability at most $2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}$, the union has probability at most $2Ne^{-2m\epsilon^2}$.
Setting this equal to $\delta$ and solving for $\epsilon$ gives

$$
\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\ln N + \ln(2/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

The class size $N$ enters beside $\ln(2/\delta)$, as if the confidence parameter had been divided by $N$. Multiplying
the number of hypotheses by $e$ increases $\epsilon$ by the same amount as dividing $\delta$ by $e$.

## Weighted union bounds

The confidence budget $\delta$ need not be split evenly. Give event $A_i$ the budget $\delta_i = \delta\,\pi_i$ with
weights $\pi_i \ge 0$ that sum to at most 1. If $\prob(A_i) \le \delta_i$ for every $i$, the union bound gives
$\prob(\bigcup_i A_i) \le \sum_i \delta\pi_i \le \delta$. The weights can be chosen before seeing data to favour some
events, and they make the countable form usable: a countable list with $\pi_i = 2^{-i}$ has a finite total.

Applied to a countable hypothesis class with a prior $\pi$, this gives the <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam
bound</NoteLink>, where $\ln(1/\pi(h))$ replaces $\ln N$ and plays the role of the description length of $h$ in nats. In
multiple <NoteLink to="hypothesis-testing">hypothesis testing</NoteLink> the even split $\delta_i = \delta/N$ is the
Bonferroni correction, which controls the probability of any false rejection among $N$ tests (see
<NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>).

## Worked example: a finite class of classifiers

A class $\Hcal$ holds $N = 1\,000$ classifiers, fixed before the data are seen. A sample $S$ of $m = 1\,000$ labelled
points is drawn independently, and $\hat R_S(h)$ is the fraction of training mistakes of $h$. The goal is a statement
that holds for every $h \in \Hcal$ at confidence $1 - \delta = 0.95$. This is the "make it uniform" move of the
<NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>.

1. **One hypothesis.** For a fixed $h$, the 0–1 losses are independent draws in $[0, 1]$ with mean $R(h)$.
   <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> gives
   $\prob(\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \ge \epsilon) \le 2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}$.
2. **All hypotheses.** The bad event for the class is the union over $h$ of the bad events for each $h$. The union bound
   gives $\prob(\exists h \in \Hcal: \abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \ge \epsilon) \le 2Ne^{-2m\epsilon^2}$. No independence
   between hypotheses is needed, and their bad events are dependent because all of them are evaluated on the same
   sample.
3. **Invert.** Setting $2Ne^{-2m\epsilon^2} = \delta$ gives
   $\epsilon = \sqrt{\ln(2N/\delta)/(2m)} = \sqrt{\ln(40\,000)/2\,000} = \sqrt{10.60/2\,000} = 0.073$.
4. **Compare with one hypothesis.** For $N = 1$ the same calculation gives $\sqrt{\ln(40)/2\,000} = 0.043$. A thousand
   hypotheses multiply $\epsilon$ by $1.7$, not by a thousand.
5. **Sample size.** For $\epsilon = 0.05$ the bound needs $m \ge \ln(2N/\delta)/(2\epsilon^2) = 10.60/0.005 = 2\,120$
   points. A single fixed hypothesis would need $738$.

With probability at least $0.95$, every one of the 1,000 classifiers has a training error within $0.073$ of its true
error. The statement then holds for the classifier chosen by <Gloss name="erm" /> on the same sample, because it holds
for all of them. The bound is not vacuous at $m = 1\,000$. When the classifiers are thresholds on a fine grid, most of
their bad events overlap, and the true probability of a deviation of $0.073$ is far smaller than $0.05$.

## Pitfalls

- **The list of events must be fixed before the data.** The union bound covers the events listed in advance. A
  hypothesis invented after looking at the sample is not on the list, and its deviation is not controlled.
- **Uncountable classes.** The bound applies to finitely or countably many events. A class indexed by a real parameter,
  such as thresholds on a line, has uncountably many hypotheses. A direct union bound gives an infinite sum, and the
  argument needs <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink> first.
- **A bound above 1 is not an error.** It means the events overlap so much, or are so many, that the bound carries no
  information. The remedy is a finer argument, not a different $\delta$.
- **Correlation does not invalidate the bound.** The union bound never needs independence. Correlation only makes it
  loose.
