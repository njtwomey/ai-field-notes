<Definition>
A probability space is a triple $(\Omega, \Fcal, P)$. The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of possible outcomes. The
event space $\Fcal$ is a $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$: a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ that contains $\Omega$ and is
closed under complements and countable unions. The probability measure $P : \Fcal \to [0, 1]$ satisfies $P(\Omega) = 1$
and countable additivity: for disjoint events $A_1, A_2, \dots$,
$P\paren{\bigcup_i A_i} = \sum_i P(A_i)$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## The three parts

| Part | Symbol | Role | Die example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sample space | $\Omega$ | Every outcome that can occur. | $\set{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}$ |
| Event space | $\Fcal$ | The subsets of $\Omega$ that have a probability. | All $2^6 = 64$ subsets |
| Probability measure | $P$ | The probability of each event. | $P(A) = \abs{A} / 6$ |

An **event** is an element of $\Fcal$, a set of outcomes. The event "the roll is even" is $\set{2, 4, 6}$, with
probability $1/2$. An outcome $\omega \in \Omega$ is not itself an event. The singleton $\set{\omega}$ is an event when
it belongs to $\Fcal$, as every singleton does in the die example.

The three conditions on $P$ are Kolmogorov's axioms: probabilities are non-negative, the whole space has probability
1, and probabilities of disjoint events add. Every other rule of probability follows from them.

## Consequences of the axioms

For events $A$ and $B$:

- **Complement.** $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$. In particular $P(\emptyset) = 0$.
- **Monotonicity.** If $A \subseteq B$, then $P(A) \le P(B)$.
- **Inclusion–exclusion.** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$.
- **Union bound.** $P\paren{\bigcup_i A_i} \le \sum_i P(A_i)$ for any countable collection, disjoint or not.

Each follows from the axioms by splitting a set into disjoint pieces.

**Complement.** $A$ and $A^c$ are disjoint with union $\Omega$, so $P(A) + P(A^c) = P(\Omega) = 1$. Taking $A = \Omega$
gives $P(\emptyset) = 0$.

**Monotonicity.** If $A \subseteq B$, then $B$ is the disjoint union of $A$ and $B \setminus A$, so $P(B) = P(A) + P(B
\setminus A) \ge P(A)$.

**Inclusion–exclusion.** $A \cup B$ is the disjoint union of $A$ and $B \setminus A$, and $B$ is the disjoint union of
$A \cap B$ and $B \setminus A$. Subtracting the second additivity equation from the first gives inclusion–exclusion.

**Union bound.** Let $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_i = A_i \setminus \bigcup_{j < i} A_j$. The $B_i$ are disjoint, have the
same union as the $A_i$, and satisfy $B_i \subseteq A_i$. Additivity and monotonicity give
$P\paren{\bigcup_i A_i} = \sum_i P(B_i) \le \sum_i P(A_i)$.

## Continuous sample spaces

For a number drawn uniformly from $[0, 1]$, the sample space is $\Omega = [0, 1]$ and the probability of an interval is
its length: $P([a, b]) = b - a$. Every single point then has probability zero, yet the whole interval has probability
1. Countable additivity does not contradict this, because $[0, 1]$ is uncountable and cannot be written as a countable
union of points.

In this setting $\Fcal$ cannot contain every subset of $[0, 1]$. There are subsets, such as the Vitali set, to which
no length can be assigned consistently with countable additivity and translation invariance
<Cite id="billingsley2012" />. The standard choice is the Borel $\sigma$-algebra: the smallest $\sigma$-algebra that
contains every interval. It contains every set that arises in practice, such as intervals, their countable unions and
intersections, and open and closed sets.

For finite or countable $\Omega$ this subtlety disappears, and $\Fcal$ can be the set of all subsets.

## In machine learning

The sample space is almost never written down. A training run draws an initialisation, a data order, mini-batches and
dropout masks, and $\Omega$ would have to describe all of them at once. Models instead work with
<NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink>, the numerical quantities computed from an outcome, and with
the distributions those quantities inherit. The probability space guarantees that these quantities, and every event
built from them, have well-defined probabilities.

## Pitfalls

- **Probability zero is not impossibility.** Under a continuous distribution every individual value has probability
  zero, yet one of them occurs.
- **Outcomes versus events.** $P$ is defined on sets of outcomes. Writing $P(\omega)$ for an outcome means
  $P(\set{\omega})$, and is meaningful only when $\set{\omega}$ is an event.
