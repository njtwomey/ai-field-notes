<Definition>
Probability theory is the mathematics of
uncertain outcomes. It starts from a probability space, a set of outcomes with a probability assigned to each event, and
derives rules for combining, conditioning and averaging those probabilities. Random variables attach numbers to
outcomes, and expectations summarise their distributions <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A probabilistic model of data is a joint distribution, and learning, prediction and inference are operations on it. The
<NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> of conditional probability factorises the joint distribution of a
sequence, which is how autoregressive <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss> assign probabilities to text.
Marginalising latent variables with the law of total probability defines mixture models. Bayes' theorem turns a
likelihood and a prior into a posterior. Losses are expectations over the data distribution, and their estimates are
averages. Tail inequalities and the union bound turn averages into guarantees, and they are the first step of every
generalisation bound.

## Main ideas

**Events, conditioning and independence.** A <NoteLink to="probability-space" /> $(\Omega, \Fcal, P)$ fixes the
outcomes, the events and their probabilities. <NoteLink to="conditional-probability" /> $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B) /
P(B)$ updates probabilities once $B$ is known, and rearranging it gives the chain rule.
<NoteLink to="independence">Independent</NoteLink> events leave each other's probabilities unchanged, and conditional
independence is the same property under $P(\cdot \mid C)$.

**Splitting into cases and reversing conditionals.** The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total
probability</NoteLink> computes a probability by summing over disjoint cases, and for random variables it is
marginalisation. <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem" /> reverses a conditional: it gives $P(A \mid B)$ from $P(B \mid A)$, the
prior $P(A)$ and the evidence $P(B)$.

**Random variables and their summaries.** A <NoteLink to="random-variable" /> is a function of the outcome, and its
distribution is induced by the probabilities of the outcomes. The <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> is
its probability-weighted average. <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance" /> measure spread and joint variation, and the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">laws of total expectation and variance</NoteLink> average conditional means and
split variance into within-group and between-group parts. The
<NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">moment-generating function</NoteLink> $\expect[e^{tX}]$ encodes all moments
and turns sums of independent variables into products.

**Bounds that hold for every distribution.** <NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's and Chebyshev's
inequalities</NoteLink> bound tail probabilities by the mean and the variance. The <NoteLink to="union-bound">union
bound</NoteLink> bounds the probability that any of several events happens by the sum of their probabilities, with no
independence needed. Both are loose, and both are the starting points of the sharper bounds among the limit theorems.

**One form for many distributions.** An <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> has density
$h(x)\exp\paren{\etavec^\top T(x) - A(\etavec)}$. The Bernoulli, Poisson, Gaussian and
<NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distributions</NoteLink> all have this form, the log-partition function $A$
generates the moments of $T(x)$, and <Gloss name="mle">maximum likelihood</Gloss> becomes moment matching.

## How the notes fit together

Read in the listed order. The first five notes, from the probability space to Bayes' theorem, work with events. The
<NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> note moves to numbers, and the notes on expectation, variance,
total expectation and the moment-generating function build on each other. The two inequality notes need only expectation
and variance. The <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> note comes last because it uses
expectations, variances and moment generation together.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes assume sets, sums and, for continuous variables, <NoteLink to="integration">integration</NoteLink>.

The named families of distributions are in <NoteLink to="discrete-distributions">discrete distributions</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="continuous-distributions">continuous distributions</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="multivariate-distributions">multivariate distributions</NoteLink>. <NoteLink to="limit-theorems" />
describe what happens to averages of many random variables. The <NoteLink to="classic-probability-problems">classic
probability problems</NoteLink> overview applies these rules to classic problems such as
<NoteLink to="monty-hall-problem">the Monty Hall problem</NoteLink> and base-rate reasoning. In statistics,
<NoteLink to="bayesian-inference" /> applies Bayes' theorem to model parameters, and in learning theory the union bound
starts every bound for a finite hypothesis class.
