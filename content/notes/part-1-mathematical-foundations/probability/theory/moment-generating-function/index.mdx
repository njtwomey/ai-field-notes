<Definition>
The moment-generating function (MGF) of a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ is

$$
M_X(t) = \expect\brack{e^{tX}},
$$

defined for the real $t$ at which the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> is finite. The MGF is said to
exist if it is finite on an open interval around $t = 0$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Moments as derivatives

Expanding the exponential, $e^{tX} = \sum_{k \ge 0} t^k X^k / k!$, gives

$$
M_X(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{\expect[X^k]}{k!}\, t^k, \qquad M_X^{(k)}(0) = \expect[X^k].
$$

Taking the expectation term by term is valid when $M_X$ is finite on an interval around 0. Differentiating the series
$k$ times and setting $t = 0$ removes every term except $\expect[X^k]\, t^k / k!$, whose $k$-th
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is $\expect[X^k]$. The MGF is a power series whose coefficients are the
moments.

For $X \sim \Exp(\lambda)$ (see <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>),

$$
M_X(t) = \int_0^\infty e^{tx}\, \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\, dx = \lambda \int_0^\infty e^{-(\lambda - t)x}\, dx =
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}, \qquad t < \lambda.
$$

The integral diverges for $t \ge \lambda$. Then $M_X'(0) = 1/\lambda$ and $M_X''(0) = 2/\lambda^2$, so $\var(X) =
2/\lambda^2 - 1/\lambda^2 = 1/\lambda^2$.

## Common MGFs

| Distribution | $M_X(t)$ | Valid for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\Bern(p)$ | $1 - p + p e^t$ | all $t$ |
| $\Binom(n, p)$ | $(1 - p + p e^t)^n$ | all $t$ |
| $\Poisson(\lambda)$ | $\exp\paren{\lambda(e^t - 1)}$ | all $t$ |
| $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\exp\paren{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2 / 2}$ | all $t$ |
| $\Exp(\lambda)$ | $\lambda / (\lambda - t)$ | $t < \lambda$ |
| $\GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$ | $\paren{\beta / (\beta - t)}^\alpha$ | $t < \beta$ |

The $\GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$ row uses shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$, as in the
<NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distribution</NoteLink> note.

<Derivation title="Derivations of the table">
**Bernoulli.** $\expect[e^{tX}] = (1 - p)e^0 + p e^t$. The binomial is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoullis, so its MGF
is the $n$-th power, by the sum property below.

**Poisson.** $\sum_{k \ge 0} e^{tk} e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k / k! = e^{-\lambda} \sum_k (\lambda e^t)^k / k! =
e^{-\lambda} e^{\lambda e^t}$.

**Gaussian.** For $Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, <NoteLink to="completing-the-square">complete the square</NoteLink> in the
exponent: $tz - z^2/2 = -(z - t)^2/2 + t^2/2$. So

$$
M_Z(t) = e^{t^2/2} \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(z - t)^2/2}\, dz = e^{t^2/2},
$$

because the integrand is the density of $\Gauss(t, 1)$. For $X = \mu + \sigma Z$ the affine rule gives $e^{\mu t}
e^{\sigma^2 t^2 / 2}$.

**Gamma.** With density $\beta^\alpha x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x} / \Gamma(\alpha)$,

$$
M_X(t) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^\infty x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-(\beta - t)x}\, dx =
\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{(\beta - t)^\alpha}, \qquad t < \beta,
$$

using the gamma integral $\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-c x}\, dx = \Gamma(\alpha) / c^\alpha$ for $c > 0$. The
exponential is the case $\alpha = 1$.
</Derivation>

## Properties

- **Uniqueness.** If $M_X$ and $M_Y$ exist and agree on an interval around 0, then $X$ and $Y$ have the same
  distribution.
- **Sums.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $M_{X + Y}(t) = M_X(t)\, M_Y(t)$.
- **Affine maps.** $M_{aX + b}(t) = e^{bt} M_X(at)$.

The sum and affine rules follow directly from the definition. For independent $X$ and $Y$, the variables $e^{tX}$ and
$e^{tY}$ are also independent, and the expectation of a product of independent variables is the product of
expectations:

$$
M_{X + Y}(t) = \expect\brack{e^{tX} e^{tY}} = \expect\brack{e^{tX}}\, \expect\brack{e^{tY}} = M_X(t)\, M_Y(t).
$$

For the affine rule, $e^{t(aX + b)} = e^{bt} e^{(at)X}$, and the constant $e^{bt}$ moves outside the expectation.
Uniqueness is a deeper result about Laplace transforms, and it is not proved here <Cite id="billingsley2012" />.

Together these identify the distributions of sums. For independent $X \sim \Poisson(\lambda_1)$ and $Y \sim
\Poisson(\lambda_2)$, $M_{X+Y}(t) = \exp\paren{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)(e^t - 1)}$, which is the MGF of
$\Poisson(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$. So $X + Y \sim \Poisson(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$. The same argument shows that sums
of independent Gaussians are Gaussian and that the binomial is a sum of Bernoullis.

## Related transforms

- **Cumulant-generating function.** $K_X(t) = \log M_X(t)$. Its derivatives at 0 are the cumulants: $K_X'(0)$ is the
  mean and $K_X''(0)$ the variance. Cumulants of independent sums add. For the
  <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink> of an
  <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink>, $K(t) = A(\eta + t) - A(\eta)$, which is why the
  log-partition function generates the moments.
- **Characteristic function.** $\varphi_X(t) = \expect\brack{e^{itX}}$ exists for every distribution, because
  $\abs{e^{itX}} = 1$. It determines the distribution and multiplies under independent sums. The standard proof of the
  <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> uses it.
- **Probability-generating function.** For a count $X \in \set{0, 1, 2, \dots}$, $G_X(s) = \expect[s^X]$, and
  $P(X = k) = G_X^{(k)}(0)/k!$. It equals $M_X(\log s)$.

## Tail bounds

For $t > 0$, Markov's inequality applied to $e^{tX}$ gives $P(X \ge a) \le e^{-ta} M_X(t)$. Minimising over $t$ is the
Chernoff bound. A finite MGF near 0 implies exponentially decaying tails; see
<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov and Chebyshev inequalities</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **The MGF may not exist.** Heavy-tailed distributions such as the Cauchy, Student $t$ and log-normal have
  $M_X(t) = \infty$ for every $t > 0$. The log-normal has all moments finite but no MGF, and its moments do not
  determine its distribution.
- **Agreement at a point is not enough.** Uniqueness needs the MGFs to agree on an interval around 0.
