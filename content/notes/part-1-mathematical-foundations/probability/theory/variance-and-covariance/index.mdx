<Definition>
For <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $X$ and $Y$ with finite second moments and means $\mu_X$
and $\mu_Y$,

$$
\var(X) = \expect\brack{(X - \mu_X)^2}, \qquad \cov(X, Y) = \expect\brack{(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)}, \qquad
\corr(X, Y) = \frac{\cov(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\var(X)\,\var(Y)}}.
$$

The standard deviation is $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\var(X)}$. For a random vector $\xvec$ with mean $\muvec$, the covariance
matrix is $\Sigmamat = \expect\brack{(\xvec - \muvec)(\xvec - \muvec)^\top}$, with entries $\Sigma_{ij} = \cov(X_i,
X_j)$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Meaning

Variance measures spread: how far, on average in squared units, $X$ falls from its mean. The standard deviation is in
the units of $X$. Covariance measures whether two variables deviate from their means in the same direction. It is
positive when large $X$ tends to come with large $Y$, and negative when large $X$ comes with small $Y$.

Correlation removes the units. It always lies in $[-1, 1]$, with $\abs{\corr(X, Y)} = 1$ exactly when
$Y = aX + b$ almost surely for some $a \ne 0$.

<Derivation title="Why correlation lies in [−1, 1]">
Let $U = X - \mu_X$ and $V = Y - \mu_Y$. For every real $t$, the variable $tU + V$ has a non-negative second moment:

$$
0 \le \expect\brack{(tU + V)^2} = t^2 \var(X) + 2t \cov(X, Y) + \var(Y).
$$

A quadratic in $t$ that is never negative has at most one real root, so its discriminant is not positive:
$4\cov(X, Y)^2 - 4\var(X)\var(Y) \le 0$. Dividing by $4\var(X)\var(Y)$ gives $\corr(X, Y)^2 \le 1$. This is the
<NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink> for expectations. Equality means the quadratic touches
zero at some $t_0$, so $\expect[(t_0 U + V)^2] = 0$ and $V = -t_0 U$ almost surely: $Y$ is an affine function of $X$.
</Derivation>

## Computing formulas

Expanding the product inside the expectation and applying linearity, with $\mu_X$ and $\mu_Y$ constants,

$$
\cov(X, Y) = \expect[XY - \mu_Y X - \mu_X Y + \mu_X \mu_Y] = \expect[XY] - \mu_Y \mu_X - \mu_X \mu_Y + \mu_X \mu_Y
= \expect[XY] - \expect[X]\,\expect[Y].
$$

Setting $Y = X$ gives the variance: $\var(X) = \expect[X^2] - \paren{\expect[X]}^2$.

For a fair die, $\expect[X] = 7/2$ and $\expect[X^2] = 91/6$, so $\var(X) = 91/6 - 49/4 = 35/12 \approx 2.92$.

The formula $\var(X) = \expect[X^2] - \paren{\expect[X]}^2$ subtracts two large numbers when the mean is large compared
with the spread. In floating point this loses precision, so numerical code computes the mean first and then averages
squared deviations, or uses Welford's one-pass update.

## Algebra

For constants $a, b, c$ and random variables $X, Y, Z$:

- $\var(aX + b) = a^2 \var(X)$. Shifting does not change spread.
- Covariance is **bilinear**: $\cov(aX + bY, Z) = a\cov(X, Z) + b\cov(Y, Z)$, and $\cov(X, X) = \var(X)$.
- $\var(X + Y) = \var(X) + \var(Y) + 2\cov(X, Y)$.
- $\var\paren{\sum_i X_i} = \sum_i \var(X_i) + 2\sum_{i < j} \cov(X_i, X_j)$.
- For a random vector and a constant matrix $\Amat$, $\cov(\Amat\xvec + \bvec) = \Amat \Sigmamat \Amat^\top$.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the algebra rules">
**Affine maps.** $aX + b$ has mean $a\mu_X + b$, so its deviation from the mean is $a(X - \mu_X)$. Squaring and
taking expectations gives $a^2 \var(X)$. The shift $b$ cancels.

**Bilinearity.** The deviation of $aX + bY$ from its mean is $a(X - \mu_X) + b(Y - \mu_Y)$. Multiply by
$Z - \mu_Z$ and take expectations. Linearity of expectation splits the result into $a\cov(X, Z) + b\cov(Y, Z)$.
Symmetry, $\cov(X, Y) = \cov(Y, X)$, gives linearity in the second argument too.

**Sums.** By bilinearity,

$$
\var\paren{\sum_i X_i} = \cov\paren{\sum_i X_i, \sum_j X_j} = \sum_i \sum_j \cov(X_i, X_j)
= \sum_i \var(X_i) + 2 \sum_{i < j} \cov(X_i, X_j).
$$

The diagonal terms are the variances, and each off-diagonal pair appears twice.

**<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">Linear maps</NoteLink> of vectors.** $\Amat\xvec + \bvec$ has mean
$\Amat\muvec + \bvec$ and deviation $\Amat(\xvec - \muvec)$. Its covariance is
$\expect\brack{\Amat(\xvec - \muvec)(\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Amat^\top} = \Amat\Sigmamat\Amat^\top$, because the constant
$\Amat$ moves outside the expectation.

**Sample mean.** For uncorrelated $X_1, \dots, X_n$ with variance $\sigma^2$, the sum rule gives
$\var\paren{\sum_i X_i} = n\sigma^2$. The affine rule with $a = 1/n$ gives $\var(\bar X) = n\sigma^2 / n^2 =
\sigma^2 / n$.
</Derivation>

If the $X_i$ are <NoteLink to="independence">independent</NoteLink>, or merely uncorrelated, the cross terms vanish.
For $n$ uncorrelated variables with common variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean $\bar X$ has variance $\sigma^2 / n$
and standard deviation $\sigma / \sqrt n$. Averaging reduces noise at rate $1/\sqrt n$.

## The covariance matrix

$\Sigmamat$ is symmetric and <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">positive semidefinite</NoteLink>: for every
vector $\wvec$, $\wvec^\top \Sigmamat \wvec =
\var(\wvec^\top \xvec) \ge 0$. The variance of any <NoteLink to="span-and-linear-independence">linear
combination</NoteLink> of the components is a quadratic form in $\Sigmamat$. Its eigenvectors are the directions of
greatest and least variance, which is the basis of <Gloss name="pca">principal component analysis</Gloss>. $\Sigmamat$
is singular exactly when some linear combination of the components is constant.

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">Bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink>.** The expected squared error of
  an estimator is its squared bias plus its variance.
- **Mini-batch <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>.** A gradient averaged over a batch of $B$ independent
  examples has variance proportional to $1/B$. Larger batches give less noisy steps.
- **Gaussian models.** The covariance matrix parameterises the
  <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate Gaussian</NoteLink>. Diagonal covariance, as in a
  diagonal <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture</NoteLink>, assumes zero correlation between features
  within each component.

## Pitfalls

- **Zero correlation is not independence.** For $X \sim \Unif(-1, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$, the correlation is zero although
  $Y$ is determined by $X$.
- **Correlation measures linear association only.** A strong but non-monotone relation can have correlation near zero.
- **Variances of differences add.** $\var(X - Y) = \var(X) + \var(Y) - 2\cov(X, Y)$, which exceeds either variance when
  $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated.
- **Infinite variance.** Heavy-tailed distributions, such as Student $t$ with two or fewer degrees of freedom, have no
  finite variance, and sample variances do not converge.
