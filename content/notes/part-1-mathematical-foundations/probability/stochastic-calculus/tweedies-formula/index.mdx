<Callout title="Not Tweedie distributions">
This note is about Tweedie's formula for posterior means under Gaussian noise. The Tweedie family of distributions,
with variance $\phi\mu^p$, is a different result by the same statistician, Maurice Tweedie; see
<NoteLink to="gamma-and-tweedie-regression">gamma and Tweedie regression</NoteLink>.
</Callout>

<Definition>
Let $\xvec \in \reals^d$ have any prior density or distribution, and observe $\yvec = \xvec + \sigma\epsilonvec$ with
$\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$ independent of $\xvec$ and $\sigma$ known. Let $p(\yvec)$ be the marginal
density of $\yvec$. **Tweedie's formula** states

$$
\expect[\xvec \mid \yvec] = \yvec + \sigma^2\,\nabla_{\yvec}\log p(\yvec).
$$

More generally, for $\yvec = a\xvec + s\epsilonvec$ with known $a > 0$ and $s > 0$,
$\expect[\xvec \mid \yvec] = \paren{\yvec + s^2\nabla\log p(\yvec)}/a$. Robbins published it in 1956 and credited it to
Tweedie <Cite id="robbins1956" />; Efron revived it for large-scale inference <Cite id="efron2011" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: move towards where observations are common

A noisy observation $y$ is the true value plus noise. If observations near $y$ are more common on its left than on its
right, then $y$ is more likely a value from the left pushed right by noise than the reverse, simply because there are
more candidates on the left. The best guess of the true value is therefore to the left of $y$. The slope of
$\log p(y)$ says which side has more candidates and by how much, and the noise variance $\sigma^2$ says how far noise
could have moved the value.

The formula never uses the prior of $x$. The density of the noisy
observations already contains all the information about the prior that the posterior mean needs. The density of
observations can be estimated from the observations themselves, which is the basis of empirical Bayes.

## Derivation

The marginal density of $\yvec$ is a Gaussian blur of the prior:

$$
p(\yvec) = \int p(\xvec)\,\varphi_\sigma(\yvec - \xvec)\,d\xvec, \qquad \varphi_\sigma(\uvec) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-d/2}
e^{-\norm{\uvec}^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
$$

The Gaussian kernel has <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $\nabla_{\yvec}\varphi_\sigma(\yvec - \xvec) = -\frac{\yvec - \xvec}{\sigma^2}
\varphi_\sigma(\yvec - \xvec)$. Differentiate under the integral:

$$
\nabla p(\yvec) = \int p(\xvec)\,\frac{\xvec - \yvec}{\sigma^2}\,\varphi_\sigma(\yvec - \xvec)\,d\xvec.
$$

Divide by $p(\yvec)$. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>
$p(\xvec)\varphi_\sigma(\yvec - \xvec)/p(\yvec) = p(\xvec \mid \yvec)$, so

$$
\nabla\log p(\yvec) = \frac{\nabla p(\yvec)}{p(\yvec)} = \int \frac{\xvec - \yvec}{\sigma^2}\,p(\xvec \mid \yvec)\,
d\xvec = \frac{\expect[\xvec \mid \yvec] - \yvec}{\sigma^2}.
$$

Rearranging gives the formula. The only property of the noise used is that the gradient of the Gaussian kernel is the
kernel times a linear function; for other <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential-family</NoteLink> noise the same
argument gives a similar formula.

<Derivation title="Second-order Tweedie: the posterior covariance">
Differentiate $\nabla\log p(\yvec) = (\expect[\xvec \mid \yvec] - \yvec)/\sigma^2$ once more. Differentiating the
posterior mean under the integral, with $\nabla_{\yvec}\log p(\xvec \mid \yvec) = (\xvec - \yvec)/\sigma^2 -
\nabla\log p(\yvec)$, gives $\nabla_{\yvec}\expect[\xvec \mid \yvec] = \cov[\xvec \mid \yvec]/\sigma^2$. Hence

$$
\cov[\xvec \mid \yvec] = \sigma^2\paren{\eye + \sigma^2\,\nabla^2\log p(\yvec)}.
$$

For the Gaussian prior of worked example 1 the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> is $-1/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)$,
and the formula gives $\sigma^2\tau^2/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)$, the familiar posterior variance.
</Derivation>

## Worked example 1: a Gaussian prior recovers shrinkage

Let $x \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \tau^2)$. Then $y \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \tau^2 + \sigma^2)$ and
$\frac{d}{dy}\log p(y) = -(y - \mu_0)/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)$. Tweedie's formula gives

$$
\expect[x \mid y] = y - \frac{\sigma^2(y - \mu_0)}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2} = \frac{\tau^2\,y + \sigma^2\,\mu_0}{\tau^2 +
\sigma^2},
$$

the usual precision-weighted average: the observation is shrunk towards the prior mean by the factor
$\sigma^2/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)$. With $\mu_0 = 0$, $\tau^2 = 1$, $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $y = 2$, the posterior mean is 1:
half the observation is attributed to noise.

## Worked example 2: a two-point prior

Let $x = \pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Then $p(y) = \tfrac12\varphi_\sigma(y - 1) + \tfrac12\varphi_\sigma(y +
1)$, and

$$
\frac{d}{dy}\log p(y) = \frac{-(y - 1)\varphi_\sigma(y - 1) - (y + 1)\varphi_\sigma(y + 1)}{\sigma^2\paren{\varphi_\sigma(y
- 1) + \varphi_\sigma(y + 1)}} = \frac{-y + \tanh(y/\sigma^2)}{\sigma^2},
$$

using $\varphi_\sigma(y - 1)/\varphi_\sigma(y + 1) = e^{2y/\sigma^2}$. Tweedie's formula gives
$\expect[x \mid y] = \tanh(y/\sigma^2)$, which is also what Bayes' rule gives directly: the posterior probability of
$+1$ is $1/(1 + e^{-2y/\sigma^2})$. With $\sigma = 1$ and $y = 2$ the posterior mean is $\tanh 2 = 0.964$. The
denoiser is not linear: it snaps observations to the nearer of the two prior values, softly when noise is large.

**A mixture check.** For the prior $0.3\,\Gauss(-1.5, 0.3^2) + 0.7\,\Gauss(1, 0.3^2)$, noise $\sigma = 0.6$ and
$y = 0.2$, integrating Bayes' rule on a grid and applying Tweedie's formula with a finite-difference slope of
$\log p(y)$ both give $0.772$.

## Empirical Bayes and selection bias

Robbins' point was that $p(y)$ can be estimated from many observations without any model for the prior
<Cite id="robbins1956" />. Efron applied this to selection bias <Cite id="efron2011" />. When thousands of effects are
measured with noise and the largest are reported, the largest observations are mostly lucky noise, and they
overestimate their true effects.

A simulation shows the size of the effect. Draw 1,000 true effects $x_i \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and observe
$y_i = x_i + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. The 20 largest observations average $3.51$, but their true
effects average only $1.89$. Now estimate $\log p(y)$ by fitting a degree-4 polynomial to the log of the histogram
counts of the $y_i$ (a <NoteLink to="poisson-regression">Poisson regression</NoteLink>, as Efron does), differentiate
it, and apply Tweedie's formula to each of the 20. The corrected estimates average $1.82$, close to the truth, and close
to the $1.76$ that the true posterior mean $y/2$ would give. The correction used no knowledge of the prior.

## Why a denoiser is a score estimator

Train a function $D(\yvec)$ to remove noise by minimising $\expect\norm{D(\xvec + \sigma\epsilonvec) - \xvec}^2$ over
clean data $\xvec$ and noise $\epsilonvec$. The minimiser of a squared error is the conditional mean, so the optimal
denoiser is $D^*(\yvec) = \expect[\xvec \mid \yvec]$. By Tweedie's formula,

$$
\nabla\log p_\sigma(\yvec) = \frac{D^*(\yvec) - \yvec}{\sigma^2},
$$

where $p_\sigma$ is the density of the noisy data. A trained denoiser therefore gives the score of the noised data
distribution, and the residual $D(\yvec) - \yvec$ points uphill in density. This is the identity behind
<NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">denoising score matching</NoteLink> <Cite id="vincent2011" />, and it is
why a denoiser implicitly contains a prior that can be sampled from <Cite id="kadkhodaie2021" />.

In a variance-preserving <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss> the noised sample is $\xvec_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec$, the scaled form with $a = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and $s^2 = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$. Tweedie's
formula gives two equivalent predictions:

$$
\hat\xvec_0 = \expect[\xvec_0 \mid \xvec_t] = \frac{\xvec_t + (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla\log p_t(\xvec_t)}{\sqrt{\bar
\alpha_t}}, \qquad \expect[\epsilonvec \mid \xvec_t] = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla\log p_t(\xvec_t).
$$

The second follows from the first and $\epsilonvec = (\xvec_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0)/\sqrt{1 -
\bar\alpha_t}$. So a network trained to predict the noise, as in
<NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-probabilistic-models">denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM)</NoteLink>
<Cite id="ho2020" />, is a scaled score network, and its prediction of the clean image $\hat\xvec_0$ is Tweedie's
posterior mean. <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-implicit-models">Denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIM)</NoteLink>
step through this $\hat\xvec_0$, and the <NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">reverse-time
stochastic differential equation</NoteLink> consumes the same score <Cite id="song2021" />.

## Assumptions

- **Gaussian noise of known variance**, independent of the signal. The noise variance must be the one at which $p$ is
  evaluated: the score of $p_\sigma$ for one $\sigma$ does not give the posterior mean at another.
- **Additive noise.** For Poisson counts the analogue is Robbins' formula $\expect[\lambda \mid y] = (y + 1)\,p(y +
  1)/p(y)$, which uses the ratio of neighbouring frequencies instead of a
  <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> <Cite id="robbins1956" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The mean is not a sample.** $\expect[\xvec \mid \yvec]$ averages over all clean signals consistent with $\yvec$. For
  images at high noise it is a blurry average, which is why diffusion models take many small steps instead of jumping to
  $\hat\xvec_0$.
- **Noisy slopes.** In empirical Bayes the formula needs the derivative of an estimated log-density. Derivatives of
  density estimates are noisy in the tails, exactly where the largest observations sit; a smooth parametric fit, such as
  a low-degree log-polynomial, is more stable than a histogram.
- **Scaling.** For $\yvec = a\xvec + s\epsilonvec$ the formula divides by $a$ and uses $s^2$, not $\sigma^2$. Forgetting
  the division by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ is a common bug in diffusion code.
