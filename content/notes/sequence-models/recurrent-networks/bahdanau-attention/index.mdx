<Definition>
Bahdanau attention computes, for decoder step $i$, a score $e_{ij} = \vvec_a^{\tr} \tanh(\Wmat_a \svec_{i-1} +
\Umat_a \hvec_j)$ between the previous decoder state $\svec_{i-1}$ and every encoder state $\hvec_j$. A softmax over
$j$ turns the scores into weights $\alpha_{ij}$, and the context vector $\cvec_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} \hvec_j$ is fed to
the decoder at step $i$ <Cite id="bahdanau2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A plain <NoteLink to="sequence-to-sequence">sequence-to-sequence model</NoteLink> passes the whole input to the decoder
through one fixed-size vector. Long inputs do not fit. A human translator does not memorise a sentence and then write
the translation. They look back at the relevant source words while writing each target word.

Attention does the same. The encoder keeps one vector per source position. When the decoder writes target word $i$, it
scores how relevant each source position is, and reads a weighted average of the source vectors. The weights form a
soft alignment between target and source words, learned without any alignment labels.

## Mechanism

**Encoder.** A bidirectional <Gloss name="rnn" /> reads the source $x_1, \dots, x_S$ forwards and backwards. The annotation of position
$j$ concatenates the two states, $\hvec_j = [\overrightarrow{\hvec}_j; \overleftarrow{\hvec}_j]$. It summarises the
whole sentence with a focus on the words around $x_j$.

**Alignment scores.** A one-hidden-layer network, the alignment model, scores each pair:

$$
e_{ij} = a(\svec_{i-1}, \hvec_j) = \vvec_a^{\tr} \tanh\paren{\Wmat_a \svec_{i-1} + \Umat_a \hvec_j}.
$$

This is additive attention: the two inputs are projected and added before the nonlinearity.

**Weights.** Normalise over source positions with a softmax:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{S} \exp(e_{ik})}.
$$

**Context and decoder.** The context is $\cvec_i = \sum_{j=1}^{S} \alpha_{ij} \hvec_j$. The decoder state is
$\svec_i = f(\svec_{i-1}, y_{i-1}, \cvec_i)$ and the output distribution is $p(y_i \mid y_{<i}, \xvec) = g(y_{i-1},
\svec_i, \cvec_i)$. Every step has its own context, so the input is no longer compressed into one vector.

## Properties of the weighted-average context

**Expected annotation.** The weights $\alpha_{i1}, \dots, \alpha_{iS}$ are non-negative and sum to 1, so they form a
probability distribution over source positions. The context is the expected annotation under that distribution:
$\cvec_i = \expect_{j \sim \alpha_i}[\hvec_j]$. When one score dominates, $\cvec_i \approx \hvec_{j^\star}$ and the
decoder copies one position. When scores are flat, the decoder reads an average.

**Differentiability.** A hard choice of one position would have no <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. The softmax average is differentiable in
every score, so the alignment model is trained by backpropagation along with the rest of the network, from translation
pairs alone.

**Short gradient paths.** The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the context with respect to an annotation, holding the weights fixed, is
$\partial \cvec_i / \partial \hvec_j = \alpha_{ij} \I$. The loss at output step $i$ reaches source position $j$ through
one weighted sum, whatever the distance between them. Without attention the same signal must pass back through every
decoder step and every encoder step in between, and decays as described in
<NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">backpropagation through time</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Take $S = 3$ source positions with two-dimensional annotations $\hvec_1 = (1, 0)$, $\hvec_2 = (0, 1)$ and
$\hvec_3 = (1, 1)$, and alignment scores $e = (1.0, 2.0, 0.5)$ at some decoder step. The softmax gives

$$
\alpha = \frac{(e^{1}, e^{2}, e^{0.5})}{e^{1} + e^{2} + e^{0.5}} = (0.231, 0.629, 0.140).
$$

The context is $\cvec = 0.231\,(1, 0) + 0.629\,(0, 1) + 0.140\,(1, 1) = (0.371, 0.769)$. Most weight falls on position
2, whose score was highest by 1. A difference of 1 in score is a factor of $e \approx 2.72$ in weight.

## Results

On WMT'14 English to French, the attention model with a 50-word training limit (RNNsearch-50) scored 26.75 <Gloss name="bleu" />, against
17.82 for the same encoder–decoder without attention (RNNenc-50) <Cite id="bahdanau2015" />. The score of the
attention model did not fall as sentences grew longer, while that of the plain encoder–decoder did. The learned weights,
drawn as a matrix, show mostly monotone alignments with local reorderings, such as French adjective–noun order.

## Variants

Luong et al. compared scoring functions <Cite id="luong2015" />:

- **Dot:** $e_{ij} = \svec_i^{\tr} \hvec_j$.
- **General (multiplicative):** $e_{ij} = \svec_i^{\tr} \Wmat_a \hvec_j$.
- **Concat (additive):** Bahdanau's form.

They also used the current decoder state $\svec_i$ rather than the previous one. The dot form, with scaling, became the
<NoteLink to="scaled-dot-product-attention">scaled dot-product attention</NoteLink> of the
<NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- Each output token depends mainly on a few input positions, and which ones can be predicted from the decoder state.
- The whole source sequence is available when decoding starts.

## Pitfalls

- **Cost grows with both lengths.** Every decoder step scores every source position, so a pair of lengths $S$ and $T$
  costs $O(ST)$ score evaluations.
- **<NoteLink to="attention-head-analysis">Attention is not explanation</NoteLink>.** Attention weights show where the decoder read, not why it produced its output.
  Different weight patterns can give the same prediction.
