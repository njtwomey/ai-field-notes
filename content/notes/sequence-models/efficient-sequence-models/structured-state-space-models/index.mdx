<Definition>
A **structured state-space sequence layer** (S4) maps an input sequence $u_0, \dots, u_{L-1} \in \reals$ to an output
sequence $y_0, \dots, y_{L-1} \in \reals$ through a hidden state $\hvec_k \in \reals^N$ (or $\complex^N$). It starts
from a continuous-time linear system with parameters $\Amat \in \reals^{N \times N}$, $\Bmat \in \reals^{N \times 1}$
and $\Cmat \in \reals^{1 \times N}$, discretises it with a learned step size $\Delta > 0$, and computes

$$
\hvec_k = \bar{\Amat}\hvec_{k-1} + \bar{\Bmat}u_k, \qquad y_k = \Cmat\hvec_k, \qquad \hvec_{-1} = \zeros.
$$

The matrix $\Amat$ has a special structure (diagonal plus low rank, or diagonal) that makes the layer's convolution
kernel cheap to compute. A deep S4 model applies one such layer to each of $D$ channels independently, then mixes
channels with a linear layer and a nonlinearity <Cite id="gu2022s4" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="recurrent-neural-network">recurrent network</NoteLink> reads a sequence one token at a time. It needs
only a fixed-size state at generation time, but its training cannot run in parallel over time, and a nonlinear
recurrence makes <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> vanish or explode over long spans. A
<NoteLink to="convolution">convolution</NoteLink> trains in parallel but sees only as far back as its kernel is long.

A **linear** recurrence has both properties at once. Because the state update is linear and the same at every step, the
output is a fixed linear function of the past inputs: a convolution. The layer can therefore be trained as a long
convolution, computed with the <NoteLink to="fast-fourier-transform">FFT</NoteLink>, and run at generation time as a recurrence with an
$N$-dimensional state. The nonlinearity sits between layers, not inside the recurrence.

The continuous-time starting point matters for two reasons. The step size $\Delta$ becomes a learnable time scale,
separate from the dynamics $\Amat$. And the theory of continuous systems supplies a good initialisation of $\Amat$,
HiPPO, which makes the state a compressed summary of the input's history.

## Discretisation

The continuous-time system is

$$
\dot{\hvec}(t) = \Amat\hvec(t) + \Bmat u(t), \qquad y(t) = \Cmat\hvec(t),
$$

with the usual direct term $\Dmat u(t)$ treated as a separate skip connection. The input sequence is read as samples
$u_k = u(k\Delta)$ of an underlying signal. Discretisation turns $(\Amat, \Bmat)$ into a pair $(\bar{\Amat}, \bar{\Bmat})$
that advances the state by one sample.

### Zero-order hold

Assume the input is constant, $u(t) = u_k$, over the interval from $(k-1)\Delta$ to $k\Delta$. The solution of a linear
system over an interval of length $\Delta$ is (see <NoteLink to="state-space-representation">state-space
representation</NoteLink>)

$$
\hvec(k\Delta) = e^{\Delta\Amat}\hvec((k-1)\Delta) + \int_0^{\Delta} e^{s\Amat}\,ds\;\Bmat u_k.
$$

For invertible $\Amat$, the integral is $\Amat^{-1}(e^{\Delta\Amat} - \eye)$, because the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of
$\Amat^{-1}e^{s\Amat}$ with respect to $s$ is $e^{s\Amat}$. So

$$
\bar{\Amat} = e^{\Delta\Amat}, \qquad \bar{\Bmat} = \Amat^{-1}\paren{e^{\Delta\Amat} - \eye}\Bmat.
$$

This is exact for piecewise-constant inputs. It is the same zero-order-hold discretisation used in digital control.

### Bilinear transform

Integrate $\dot{\hvec} = \Amat\hvec + \Bmat u$ over one step and approximate the integral of $\Amat\hvec$ by the
<NoteLink to="numerical-integration">trapezoidal rule</NoteLink>, and the integral of $\Bmat u$ by $\Delta\Bmat u_k$:

$$
\hvec_k - \hvec_{k-1} = \frac{\Delta}{2}\Amat\paren{\hvec_k + \hvec_{k-1}} + \Delta\Bmat u_k.
$$

Collect the $\hvec_k$ terms on the left: $(\eye - \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Amat)\hvec_k = (\eye + \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Amat)\hvec_{k-1} + \Delta\Bmat u_k$.
Multiplying by the inverse of the left factor gives

$$
\bar{\Amat} = \paren{\eye - \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Amat}^{-1}\paren{\eye + \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Amat}, \qquad
\bar{\Bmat} = \paren{\eye - \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\Amat}^{-1}\Delta\Bmat.
$$

The original S4 uses the bilinear transform <Cite id="gu2022s4" />. S4D reports little empirical difference between
the two <Cite id="gu2022s4d" />. Both map an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $\Amat$ with negative real part to an eigenvalue of
$\bar{\Amat}$ inside the unit circle: $\abs{e^{\Delta\lambda}} = e^{\Delta \operatorname{Re}\lambda} < 1$, and
$\abs{1 + \Delta\lambda/2} < \abs{1 - \Delta\lambda/2}$ whenever $\operatorname{Re}\lambda < 0$. A stable continuous
system gives a stable recurrence for every step size.

## The convolution kernel

Unroll the recurrence from $\hvec_{-1} = \zeros$:

$$
\hvec_0 = \bar{\Bmat}u_0, \qquad
\hvec_1 = \bar{\Amat}\bar{\Bmat}u_0 + \bar{\Bmat}u_1, \qquad
\hvec_2 = \bar{\Amat}^2\bar{\Bmat}u_0 + \bar{\Amat}\bar{\Bmat}u_1 + \bar{\Bmat}u_2.
$$

By induction, $\hvec_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \bar{\Amat}^{j}\bar{\Bmat}u_{k-j}$: if it holds for $k-1$, then
$\bar{\Amat}\hvec_{k-1} + \bar{\Bmat}u_k$ multiplies every term by one more $\bar{\Amat}$ and adds the $j = 0$ term.
Applying $\Cmat$,

$$
y_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \bar K_j\,u_{k-j}, \qquad
\bar K = \paren{\Cmat\bar{\Bmat},\ \Cmat\bar{\Amat}\bar{\Bmat},\ \Cmat\bar{\Amat}^2\bar{\Bmat},\ \dots,\ \Cmat\bar{\Amat}^{L-1}\bar{\Bmat}} \in \reals^L.
$$

The output is the causal convolution $y = \bar K * u$ of the input with the **SSM convolution kernel** $\bar K$
<Cite id="gu2022s4" />. The kernel depends on the parameters and not on the input. That is the linear time-invariant
property, and it gives the layer two equivalent modes:

- **Convolutional mode, for training.** Compute $\bar K$ once per forward pass, then convolve. With the FFT, a
  convolution of length $L$ costs $O(L \log L)$ and runs in parallel over the whole sequence.
- **Recurrent mode, for generation.** Keep $\hvec_k$ and apply one update per new input. Each step costs a
  <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">matrix-vector product</NoteLink>, independent of how many tokens came before. There is no cache that grows with the sequence,
  unlike a transformer's <NoteLink to="key-value-cache">KV cache</NoteLink>.

The <NoteLink to="frequency-response">frequency response</NoteLink> of the layer follows from the recurrence. Take the
<NoteLink to="z-transform">z-transform</NoteLink> of $\hvec_k = \bar{\Amat}\hvec_{k-1} + \bar{\Bmat}u_k$ to get
$\hvec(z) = (\eye - \bar{\Amat}z^{-1})^{-1}\bar{\Bmat}u(z)$, so the transfer function is
$H(z) = \Cmat(\eye - \bar{\Amat}z^{-1})^{-1}\bar{\Bmat}$. Each eigenvalue $\bar\lambda$ of $\bar{\Amat}$ is a pole. A
pole near the unit circle gives a long, slowly decaying kernel and a sharp peak in $\abs{H(e^{i\theta})}$ at the
frequency $\theta = \arg\bar\lambda$.

### Worked example

Take a scalar system, $N = 1$, with $A = -1$, $B = 1$, $C = 1$ and step $\Delta = 0.5$.

- Zero-order hold: $\bar A = e^{-0.5} = 0.6065$ and $\bar B = (e^{-0.5} - 1)/(-1) = 0.3935$. The kernel is
  $\bar K_k = 0.3935 \times 0.6065^k = (0.3935, 0.2387, 0.1447, 0.0878, \dots)$.
- Bilinear: $\bar A = (1 - 0.25)/(1 + 0.25) = 0.6$ and $\bar B = 0.5/1.25 = 0.4$. The kernel is
  $(0.4, 0.24, 0.144, 0.0864, \dots)$, close to the zero-order-hold kernel.

With the zero-order-hold kernel and input $u = (1, 0, 2, 0)$, the recurrence gives $h_0 = 0.3935$,
$h_1 = 0.6065 \times 0.3935 = 0.2387$, $h_2 = 0.6065 \times 0.2387 + 0.3935 \times 2 = 0.9317$ and
$h_3 = 0.6065 \times 0.9317 = 0.5651$. The convolution gives $y_2 = \bar K_2 u_0 + \bar K_1 u_1 + \bar K_0 u_2 =
0.14474 + 0 + 0.78694 = 0.93168$ and $y_3 = \bar K_3 u_0 + \bar K_1 u_2 = 0.08780 + 0.47730 = 0.56510$. The two modes agree.

## Computing the kernel

The convolutional mode is only useful if $\bar K$ is cheap. Computed naively, $\bar K$ needs $L$ successive products with
the $N \times N$ matrix $\bar{\Amat}$: $O(N^2 L)$ time <Cite id="gu2022s4" />.

If $\Amat$ is diagonal, $\Amat = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N)$, the discretised matrix is diagonal too, with
entries $\bar\lambda_n$, and the kernel is a sum of $N$ geometric sequences:

$$
\bar K_k = \sum_{n=1}^{N} C_n \bar B_n \bar\lambda_n^{\,k}.
$$

This is a product of the vector $(C_n\bar B_n)_n$ with the $N \times L$ Vandermonde matrix $[\bar\lambda_n^{\,k}]$, which
costs $O(NL)$ directly <Cite id="gu2022s4d" />. Any diagonalisable $\Amat$ can be brought to this form by a change of
basis $\hvec = \Vmat\tilde{\hvec}$, which changes $(\Amat, \Bmat, \Cmat)$ to
$(\Vmat^{-1}\Amat\Vmat, \Vmat^{-1}\Bmat, \Cmat\Vmat)$ and leaves the map from $u$ to $y$ unchanged.

S4 could not use this directly. The HiPPO matrix below is diagonalisable only by a matrix $\Vmat$ with entries that
grow like $2^{4N/3}$, so the change of basis is numerically useless <Cite id="gu2022s4" />. S4 instead writes the HiPPO
matrix as **normal plus low rank**, $\Amat = \Vmat\Lambdamat\Vmat^* - \Pmat\Qmat^\tr$ with unitary $\Vmat$ and rank 1 or 2
for $\Pmat\Qmat^\tr$. It evaluates the kernel's generating function $\sum_k \bar K_k \zeta^k$ at the $L$-th roots of
unity, where powers of $\bar{\Amat}$ become a <NoteLink to="matrix-inverse">matrix inverse</NoteLink>. The Woodbury identity removes the low-rank term, the
diagonal part reduces to a Cauchy kernel $1/(\omega_j - \zeta_k)$, and an inverse FFT recovers $\bar K$. The total is
$\tilde O(N + L)$ operations <Cite id="gu2022s4" />.

### Diagonal state spaces

Gupta, Gu and Berant found that dropping the low-rank term and keeping only the diagonal part matches S4
<Cite id="gupta2022" />. S4D then made the diagonal version the standard one <Cite id="gu2022s4d" />:

- $\Amat$ is diagonal and complex. The real part is parameterised as $-e^{a}$, so it is always negative and the layer
  is always stable.
- Eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, so only $N/2$ are stored, and the kernel is twice the real part of the sum over
  them.
- Initialisations include **S4D-Lin**, $\lambda_n = -\tfrac12 + i\pi n$, and **S4D-Real**, $\lambda_n = -(n+1)$, which has
  the same spectrum as the HiPPO matrix.

Each complex pair is a damped oscillator. Its kernel is $e^{-\alpha k\Delta}$ times a sinusoid of frequency $\omega\Delta$
radians per step, for $\lambda = -\alpha \pm i\omega$. A layer with $N/2$ pairs builds its kernel from $N/2$ such
components, so it acts as a learned bank of filters at different decay rates and frequencies.

## HiPPO initialisation

A randomly initialised linear SSM performs poorly: the solution of a linear <Gloss name="ode" /> is exponential, so its gradients vanish
or explode over long sequences. HiPPO (high-order polynomial projection operators) chooses $\Amat$ and $\Bmat$ so that the
state has a meaning <Cite id="gu2020hippo" />. At every time $t$, $\hvec(t)$ holds the coefficients of the best
approximation of the input history $u(s)$, $s \le t$, by a degree-$(N-1)$ polynomial, under a chosen weighting of the
past. For the scaled Legendre measure, which weights the whole history uniformly, the matrix is

$$
A_{nk} = -\begin{cases}
(2n+1)^{1/2}(2k+1)^{1/2} & n > k, \\
n + 1 & n = k, \\
0 & n < k,
\end{cases}
$$

for $n, k = 0, \dots, N-1$. The state then remembers the whole input history at a resolution set by $N$, instead of
forgetting it exponentially. Replacing a random $\Amat$ by this matrix raised accuracy on sequential <Gloss name="mnist" /> from 60% to
98% in the work that preceded S4 <Cite id="gu2022s4" />.

## Results and use

S4 was the first model to solve the Path-X task of the Long Range Arena benchmark, a binary classification of images
flattened to sequences of 16,384 pixels. It reached 91% accuracy on sequential <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> without <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>, and
generated autoregressively 60 times faster than a comparable transformer by using the recurrent mode
<Cite id="gu2022s4" />.

Its weakness is language. A time-invariant kernel applies the same filter whatever the tokens are, so the layer cannot
decide from the content of a token whether to store or ignore it. <NoteLink to="mamba">Mamba</NoteLink> makes
$\Delta$, $\Bmat$ and $\Cmat$ functions of the input to fix this, at the cost of the convolutional mode.

<Derivation title="Why the bilinear transform keeps stable poles inside the unit circle">
For a scalar eigenvalue $\lambda = -\alpha + i\omega$ with $\alpha > 0$, the bilinear map gives
$\bar\lambda = (1 + \Delta\lambda/2)/(1 - \Delta\lambda/2)$. Write $z = \Delta\lambda/2 = -a + ib$ with $a > 0$. Then

$$
\abs{1 + z}^2 = (1 - a)^2 + b^2, \qquad \abs{1 - z}^2 = (1 + a)^2 + b^2.
$$

Their difference is $(1 + a)^2 - (1 - a)^2 = 4a > 0$, so $\abs{\bar\lambda} < 1$. Eigenvalues on the imaginary axis
($a = 0$) map to the unit circle, and the whole left half-plane maps into the open unit disc. The zero-order hold gives
$\abs{e^{\Delta\lambda}} = e^{-\alpha\Delta} < 1$ directly.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

- **Linearity in time.** The recurrence is linear and its parameters do not depend on the input. Nonlinearity comes
  only from the layers around it.
- **Uniform sampling.** One step $\Delta$ per channel is shared by all positions. Changing $\Delta$ at test time adapts
  the model to a different sampling rate without retraining <Cite id="gu2022s4" />.
- **Single-input, single-output channels.** Each of the $D$ channels has its own SSM with state size $N$, so a layer
  holds $DN$ state numbers.

## Pitfalls

- **Unstable parameterisation.** If the real parts of the eigenvalues are free parameters, training can push them
  positive and the recurrent mode then explodes, even if the truncated kernel looked fine. Parameterise them as
  negative.
- **Kernel length.** The convolutional mode materialises a kernel as long as the training sequence. Generation beyond
  that length needs the recurrent mode, which is exact for any length.
- **Real versus complex.** Complex diagonal states need the real part of the output taken consistently in both modes.
  Mixing the conventions gives a factor of 2 error between training and generation.
