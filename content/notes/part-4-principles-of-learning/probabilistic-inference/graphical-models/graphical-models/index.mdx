<Definition>
A **graphical model** represents a joint distribution $p(\xvec)$ over many <NoteLink to="random-variable">random
variables</NoteLink> by a graph. Nodes stand for variables, and the edges, or their absence, state how the distribution
factorises into local terms. The same graph lists conditional independences that every distribution with that
factorisation satisfies.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A joint distribution over $d$ binary variables has $2^d - 1$ free parameters, too many to specify or learn when $d$ is
in the tens. Most real problems have structure: a symptom depends on a few diseases, a pixel on its neighbours, a hidden
state on the previous one. A graphical model writes that structure down. It reduces the number of parameters, makes
independence assumptions explicit and checkable, and lets inference algorithms exploit the factorisation
<Cite id="koller2009" />. Hidden Markov models, <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filters</NoteLink>,
<Gloss name="crf">conditional random fields</Gloss>, <NoteLink to="topic-models">topic models</NoteLink> and the skill
models of <Gloss name="trueskill" /> are all graphical models.

## Main ideas

**Directed graphs factorise by parents.** A <NoteLink to="bayesian-network" /> is a <Gloss name="dag">directed acyclic
graph</Gloss> with $p(\xvec) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid \xvec_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})$. Each factor is a conditional distribution,
so the graph often reads as a story of how the data were generated. Conditional independences follow from d-separation,
built from three local structures: the chain, the fork and the collider. The collider gives explaining away, where two
independent causes become dependent once their common effect is observed.

**Undirected graphs factorise by cliques.** A <NoteLink to="markov-random-field" /> uses an undirected graph and
non-negative potentials on its cliques, $p(\xvec) = \frac{1}{Z}\prod_C \psi_C(\xvec_C)$. Separation in the graph
implies conditional independence. For positive distributions the Hammersley–Clifford theorem gives the converse. The
normaliser $Z$ couples all variables and is the main obstacle to learning and inference.

**Factor graphs show the exact factorisation.** Two different factorisations can share one undirected graph. A
<NoteLink to="factor-graph" /> adds a node for each factor, so it shows the factorisation exactly. Message-passing
algorithms are stated most simply on factor graphs <Cite id="bishop2006" />, and directed and undirected models both
convert to them.

**Structure can be learned.** When the graph is unknown it can be estimated from data. <NoteLink to="chow-liu-trees" />
finds the tree-structured distribution closest in KL divergence to the data distribution, as a maximum-weight spanning
tree with mutual-information weights.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="bayesian-network" />, then the <NoteLink to="markov-random-field" />, which treats the
undirected case. The factor graph requires the Markov random field and prepares for the inference topics. Chow–Liu trees
require the Bayesian network and show structure learning in the one case where it has an exact, efficient solution.
