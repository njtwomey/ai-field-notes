<Definition>
The **Elo rating system** gives each player a single rating $R$ <Cite id="elo1978" />. When player A, rated $R_A$,
plays player B, rated $R_B$, A's **expected score** is

$$
E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-(R_A - R_B)/400}} ,
$$

and after the game A's rating becomes $R_A + K(S_A - E_A)$, where $S_A$ is 1 for a win, $\tfrac12$ for a draw and 0
for a loss, and $K > 0$ is the **K-factor**. B's rating changes by the same amount with the opposite sign.
</Definition>

## Problem

Chess federations need a number for each player that predicts results and can be updated after every tournament without
refitting the whole history. Arpad Elo's system, adopted by the World Chess Federation (FIDE) in 1970, meets both needs
with one rule <Cite id="herbrich2006" />. The rule looks like a heuristic, "move towards the result by a fixed fraction
of the surprise", but it is an online fit of the <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Bradley–Terry model</NoteLink>.

## Mechanism: a gradient step on Bradley–Terry

Write the expected score with the logistic function $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$. Since $10^{-x} = e^{-x\ln 10}$,

$$
E_A = \sigma\paren{\frac{R_A - R_B}{s}}, \qquad s = \frac{400}{\ln 10} = 173.7 .
$$

This is the Bradley–Terry probability that A beats B, with strengths $\theta = R/s$. Score the prediction with the
log-loss of <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, treating $S_A$ as the target:

$$
L = -S_A \log E_A - (1 - S_A)\log(1 - E_A).
$$

Using $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> with respect to the
two ratings are

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial R_A} = -\frac{S_A - E_A}{s}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial R_B} = +\frac{S_A - E_A}{s}.
$$

One step of <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> with step size $\eta$ gives

$$
R_A \leftarrow R_A + \frac{\eta}{s}(S_A - E_A), \qquad R_B \leftarrow R_B - \frac{\eta}{s}(S_A - E_A).
$$

This is the Elo update with $K = \eta/s$. Elo is <NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent">online gradient
descent</NoteLink> on the Bradley–Terry log-likelihood, one game at a time, with a constant learning rate. Three
consequences follow.

- **The update is zero-sum.** The two <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> are equal and opposite, so the sum of
  all ratings in a closed pool never changes.
- **Draws fit without a tie model.** A draw is a target of $\tfrac12$ in the log-loss. The update moves the higher-rated
  player down and the lower-rated one up.
- **The fixed point is the maximum-likelihood fit.** In <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> the step is
  zero only when each player's expected score matches the average actual score, which is the Bradley–Terry likelihood
  equation.

### The 400-point scale

The constant 400 sets the units. A 400-point gap gives expected score $1/(1 + 10^{-1}) = 10/11 = 0.909$, so odds of 10
to 1. A 200-point gap gives 0.760. Elo's own formulation assumed that each player's performance in a game is normally
distributed with <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> 200 points, which makes the win
probability a Gaussian cdf, the <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Thurstone model</NoteLink>
<Cite id="elo1978" />. Most federations now use the logistic curve, whose heavier tails give weaker players slightly
more chance of an upset. The two curves differ by less than 0.01 at matched scale.

### The K-factor as a learning rate

$K$ trades tracking speed against noise. Let a player's true rating be $R^*$ against opponents rated exactly at
their true strength, and write $e_t = R_t - R^*$ for the error after $t$ games. With $p = E(R^*)$ the true expected
score, linearising $E$ around $R^*$ gives $E(R_t) \approx p + g\, e_t$ with $g = p(1 - p)/s$. The update becomes

$$
e_{t+1} = (1 - Kg)\, e_t + K \xi_t, \qquad \xi_t = S_t - p ,
$$

where $\xi_t$ has mean 0 and variance $p(1 - p)$. This is a first-order autoregression.

- **Adaptation time.** A change in $R^*$ decays as $(1 - Kg)^t$, with a time constant of $1/(Kg) = s/\paren{K p(1 - p)}$
  games. For evenly matched play, $p = \tfrac12$, it is $4s/K$: 22 games for $K = 32$.
- **Stationary noise.** The stationary variance is $K^2 p(1 - p)/\paren{1 - (1 - Kg)^2} \approx K^2 p(1-p)/(2Kg) =
  Ks/2$ for small $Kg$. The rating's standard deviation around the truth is $\sqrt{Ks/2}$, independent of $p$: 53 points
  for $K = 32$ and 29 points for $K = 10$. A simulation of 200,000 games reproduces both values to within 2%.

Halving the rating's standard deviation requires dividing $K$ by four, which multiplies the adaptation time by four.
Federations give new and young players a larger $K$ and established players a smaller one for this reason. A fixed $K$
cannot be right for every player. <NoteLink to="glicko-and-glicko-2">Glicko</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink> replace it by giving each player an uncertainty that acts as a personal
learning rate.

## Worked example

A is rated 1600 and B 1400, with $K = 32$.

1. **Expected score.** $E_A = 1/(1 + 10^{-200/400}) = 1/(1 + 0.3162) = 0.7597$, and $E_B = 0.2403$.
2. **B wins.** $R_A = 1600 + 32(0 - 0.7597) = 1575.69$ and $R_B = 1400 + 32(1 - 0.2403) = 1424.31$. The total, 3000,
   is unchanged.
3. **A draw instead.** $R_A = 1600 + 32(0.5 - 0.7597) = 1591.69$ and $R_B = 1408.31$.
4. **A wins instead.** $R_A = 1600 + 32 \times 0.2403 = 1607.69$. The expected win earns 7.69 points; the upset
   cost 24.31.



## Rating inflation and deflation

When both players share one $K$ and no floor or bonus applies, updates are zero-sum, and the average rating of a
pool changes only when players join or leave. The average true strength can change for other reasons, and the
two drift apart.

- **Improving players deflate the pool.** A player who improves wins points from opponents whose strength has not
  changed. Their ratings fall below their true strength. A pool with many fast-improving juniors deflates.
- **Departures move points.** A player who retires takes their rating out of the pool. Leaving after gaining points
  removes points; leaving after losing them leaves the points behind.
- **Entry ratings.** A newcomer starts at a fixed rating. A newcomer weaker than that rating loses points to the pool
  and inflates everyone else; a stronger one deflates it.
- **Floors create points.** A rule that ratings cannot fall below a floor adds points whenever it binds, which inflates
  the pool.

Federations counter these effects with bonus points, rating floors, and different $K$ for new players. None of them is
exact, because the Elo rating has no absolute anchor: it measures strength relative to the current pool.

## Cost

One update costs one exponential and a few additions. The state is one number per player. No history is stored.

## Trade-offs

- **No uncertainty.** The rating carries no measure of its uncertainty, so a new player's rating is treated like a
  veteran's. Provisional periods with a larger $K$ patch this. <NoteLink to="glicko-and-glicko-2">Glicko</NoteLink>
  models it.
- **Order dependence.** Online updates depend on the order of games. A batch
  <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Bradley–Terry fit</NoteLink> does not.
- **Two players only.** Team games and free-for-all games need extensions, such as averaging pairwise updates;
  <NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink> handles them in its model.
- **Other uses.** The same update estimates student ability and question difficulty in adaptive learning systems, where
  it is an online fit of the Rasch model of <NoteLink to="item-response-theory">item response theory</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="pelanek2016" />.
