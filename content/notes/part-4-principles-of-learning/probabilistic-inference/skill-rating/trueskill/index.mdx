<Definition>
**TrueSkill** is a Bayesian <NoteLink to="skill-rating">skill rating</NoteLink> system for games with any number of
players and teams <Cite id="herbrich2006" />. Player $i$ has an unknown skill $s_i$ with a Gaussian belief
$\Gauss(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$. In a game each player gives a performance $p_i \sim \Gauss(s_i, \beta^2)$, a team's
performance is the sum of its players' performances, and teams finish in the order of their performances, with a draw
when two teams differ by at most a margin $\varepsilon$. After each game, expectation propagation on the model's
<NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> returns a new Gaussian belief for every player.
</Definition>

## Problem

An online game must pair players of similar skill within seconds, often before they have played more than a handful
of games. The rating system sees only who won. <NoteLink to="elo-rating">Elo</NoteLink> keeps one number per player
and moves it by a fixed step, so a new player's rating takes dozens of games to settle, and Elo has no rule for teams,
for more than two teams, or for draws. TrueSkill was built for Xbox Live to solve these problems
<Cite id="herbrich2006" />.

## The model, one assumption at a time

**Skill and performance.** Each player has a skill $s_i$, a real number that stays fixed during a game. In one game the
player performs at $p_i = s_i + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i \sim \Gauss(0, \beta^2)$, independent across players and
games. The player with the higher performance wins. The <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> $\beta$ sets the scale: a skill gap of $\beta$ is a fixed amount of advantage, whatever the game.

With known skills, player 1 beats player 2 when $p_1 - p_2 > 0$. The difference $p_1 - p_2$ is Gaussian with mean
$s_1 - s_2$ and variance $2\beta^2$, so

$$
\pr(\text{1 beats 2} \mid s_1, s_2) = \Phi\paren{\frac{s_1 - s_2}{\sqrt 2\, \beta}},
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cdf. This is Thurstone's probit model of
<NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">paired comparisons</NoteLink>. Winn and Bishop's example has Jill with skill 15,
Fred with skill 12.5 and $\beta = 5$. Jill wins with probability $\Phi(2.5/\sqrt{50}) = 0.638$ <Cite id="winn2023" />.

**Uncertain skill.** Skills are not known, so each carries a Gaussian belief $s_i \sim \Gauss(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$. A new
player starts at $\Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$. Xbox Live used $\mu_0 = 25$, $\sigma_0 = 25/3$, $\beta = \sigma_0/2$ and
$\tau = \sigma_0/100$, where $\tau$ is defined below <Cite id="herbrich2006" />. Integrating the skills out, the
difference $d = p_1 - p_2$ is Gaussian with mean $t = \mu_1 - \mu_2$ and variance

$$
c^2 = 2\beta^2 + \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 ,
$$

so the predicted win probability is $\Phi(t/c)$. Uncertainty about either skill pulls the prediction towards 1/2.

**Draws.** A game is drawn when $\abs{d} \le \varepsilon$, for a **draw margin** $\varepsilon \ge 0$. Player 1 wins when
$d > \varepsilon$. Between two equally skilled players with $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 0$, the draw probability is
$2\Phi\paren{\varepsilon/(\sqrt 2\beta)} - 1$. Setting this to the observed draw rate $p_{\text{draw}}$ gives
$\varepsilon = \sqrt 2\, \beta\, \Phi^{-1}\paren{(1 + p_{\text{draw}})/2}$. For teams of $n_1$ and $n_2$ players,
$\sqrt 2$ becomes $\sqrt{n_1 + n_2}$ <Cite id="herbrich2006" />.

**Teams.** A team's performance is the sum of its members' performances, $t_j = \sum_{i \in A_j} p_i$, where $A_j$ is
the set of players in team $j$. Only the team outcome is observed. Assigning credit for it to the individual players is
an inference problem, which Winn and Bishop call **credit assignment** <Cite id="winn2023" />.

**Several teams.** With $k$ teams, the outcome is a ranking. Sort the teams by finishing position and take the
differences $d_j = t_{(j)} - t_{(j+1)}$ between neighbours. The ranking is observed exactly when every
$d_j > \varepsilon$, or $\abs{d_j} \le \varepsilon$ for a tied pair. The $k - 1$ neighbour comparisons imply the rest,
so the other pairs need no factors.

**Skills change.** Before each game a player's skill takes a Gaussian <NoteLink to="random-walk">random-walk</NoteLink>
step, $s_i^{\text{new}} \sim \Gauss(s_i, \tau^2)$. The belief widens from $\sigma_i^2$ to $\sigma_i^2 + \tau^2$. Without
this **dynamics factor** $\sigma_i$ would fall towards zero with every game and the rating would freeze.

The joint density of skills, performances and differences is the product of the factors in the graph
<Cite id="kschischang2001" />. For one game the graph is a tree: skills feed performances, performances feed team
sums, team sums feed differences, and each difference ends in an outcome factor.

## Why exact inference fails

Take two players and the outcome "1 wins". The posterior of $s_1$ is its Gaussian prior times the probability of the
observed outcome given $s_1$, which is a Gaussian cdf in $s_1$:

$$
p(s_1 \mid \text{1 wins}) \propto \Gauss(s_1; \mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\; \Phi\paren{\frac{s_1 - \mu_2 - \varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\beta^2 + \sigma_2^2}}}.
$$

A Gaussian times a Gaussian cdf is a skew-normal density, not a Gaussian. After $n$ games the exact posterior is a
Gaussian times $n$ such cdfs, with parameters that grow with every game <Cite id="winn2023" />. TrueSkill keeps a
single Gaussian per player instead. After each game it replaces the exact posterior with the Gaussian of the same mean
and variance. This projection is the step of <NoteLink to="expectation-propagation">expectation propagation</NoteLink>
(EP) <Cite id="minka2001" />, and one pass of it over a stream of games is called Gaussian density filtering or
<Gloss name="assumed-density-filtering">assumed density filtering</Gloss>.

## The two-player update

The update needs the posterior mean and variance of each skill after one game. Take the case "1 wins" with prior
variances already widened by $\tau^2$.

**Step 1: skills and difference are jointly Gaussian.** Before the outcome is seen, $s_1$ and
$d = p_1 - p_2$ are jointly Gaussian with $\cov(s_1, d) = \sigma_1^2$, $\var(d) = c^2$ and $\expect[d] = t$. The
conditional of $s_1$ given $d$ is therefore Gaussian with a mean linear in $d$:

$$
\expect[s_1 \mid d] = \mu_1 + \frac{\sigma_1^2}{c^2}(d - t), \qquad \var(s_1 \mid d) = \sigma_1^2 - \frac{\sigma_1^4}{c^2}.
$$

The outcome depends on the skills only through $d$. By the laws of total expectation and total variance, the posterior
moments of $s_1$ follow from those of $d$ given the outcome:

$$
\expect[s_1 \mid \text{win}] = \mu_1 + \frac{\sigma_1^2}{c^2}\paren{\expect[d \mid \text{win}] - t},
$$

$$
\var(s_1 \mid \text{win}) = \sigma_1^2 - \frac{\sigma_1^4}{c^2} + \frac{\sigma_1^4}{c^4}\var(d \mid \text{win}).
$$

**Step 2: moments of a truncated Gaussian.** Given "1 wins", $d$ is $\Gauss(t, c^2)$ truncated to $d > \varepsilon$.
Standardise with $z = (d - t)/c$, which is standard normal truncated to $z > -u$ for
$u = (t - \varepsilon)/c$. The standard normal density satisfies $z\phi(z) = -\phi'(z)$, so

$$
\expect[z \mid z > -u] = \frac{\int_{-u}^{\infty} z\phi(z)\,dz}{\Phi(u)} = \frac{\phi(-u)}{\Phi(u)} = \frac{\phi(u)}{\Phi(u)}.
$$

Write $x = t/c$ and $a = \varepsilon/c$, so that $u = x - a$, and define

$$
v(x, a) = \frac{\phi(x - a)}{\Phi(x - a)}, \qquad w(x, a) = v(x, a)\paren{v(x, a) + x - a}.
$$

Then $\expect[d \mid \text{win}] = t + c\,v$ and $\var(d \mid \text{win}) = c^2 (1 - w)$. The variance is derived in
the collapsed block below. The same moments, as a single EP step, are worked through in
<NoteLink to="expectation-propagation-truncated-gaussian">a Gaussian times a step function</NoteLink>.

**Step 3: substitute.** Putting the moments of $d$ into step 1 gives the TrueSkill update for the winner:

$$
\mu_1 \leftarrow \mu_1 + \frac{\sigma_1^2}{c}\, v\paren{\frac{t}{c}, \frac{\varepsilon}{c}}, \qquad
\sigma_1^2 \leftarrow \sigma_1^2 \brack{1 - \frac{\sigma_1^2}{c^2}\, w\paren{\frac{t}{c}, \frac{\varepsilon}{c}}}.
$$

The loser's update has $\mu_2$ decreased by $\frac{\sigma_2^2}{c} v$ and the same variance rule with $\sigma_2$
<Cite id="herbrich2006" />. A draw replaces $v$ and $w$ by the moments of $d$ truncated to $\abs{d} \le \varepsilon$:

$$
v_{\text{draw}}(x, a) = \frac{\phi(-a - x) - \phi(a - x)}{\Phi(a - x) - \Phi(-a - x)},
$$

$$
w_{\text{draw}}(x, a) = v_{\text{draw}}^2 + \frac{(a - x)\phi(a - x) + (a + x)\phi(a + x)}{\Phi(a - x) - \Phi(-a - x)}.
$$

<Derivation title="Variance of a truncated Gaussian">
Let $z$ be standard normal truncated to $z > \alpha$, with $\lambda = \phi(\alpha)/(1 - \Phi(\alpha))$ its mean. Using
$z^2\phi(z) = \phi(z) - (z\phi(z))'$, which follows from $\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)$,

$$
\int_\alpha^\infty z^2 \phi(z)\,dz = \paren{1 - \Phi(\alpha)} + \alpha\phi(\alpha),
$$

so $\expect[z^2 \mid z > \alpha] = 1 + \alpha\lambda$ and $\var(z \mid z > \alpha) = 1 + \alpha\lambda - \lambda^2$.
With $\alpha = -u$ the mean is $\lambda = \phi(u)/\Phi(u) = v$ and the variance is
$1 - uv - v^2 = 1 - v(v + u) = 1 - w$. Scaling back by $c$ gives $\var(d \mid \text{win}) = c^2(1 - w)$.

In step 1,
$\var(s_1 \mid \text{win}) = \sigma_1^2 - \sigma_1^4/c^2 + \sigma_1^4(1 - w)/c^2 = \sigma_1^2(1 - w\sigma_1^2/c^2)$.

For a draw, $z$ is truncated to $-a - x < z \le a - x$, and the same two integrals over a finite interval give
$v_{\text{draw}}$ and $w_{\text{draw}}$.
</Derivation>

Three properties of the update follow from its form.

- **Surprise sets the step.** $v$ is large when $x - a$ is negative, that is, when the winner was expected to lose, and
  falls to zero for an expected win. An upset moves both means a lot; a predictable result moves them little.
- **Uncertainty sets the share.** Each player's mean moves by $\sigma_i^2 v/c$. The player with the larger $\sigma_i$
  absorbs more of the change. A new player's rating moves fast; an established player's barely moves.
- **Every game shrinks $\sigma$.** $0 < w < 1$, so the variance factor lies between $1 - \sigma_i^2/c^2$ and 1. The
  $\tau^2$ added before each game stops $\sigma$ reaching zero.

## Message passing on the whole graph

For team games and multi-team rankings, the update is computed by passing messages on the factor graph
<Cite id="herbrich2006" />. Represent each Gaussian by its <NoteLink to="exponential-family">natural
parameters</NoteLink>, the precision $1/\sigma^2$ and the precision-weighted mean $\mu/\sigma^2$. Then multiplying
Gaussians adds natural parameters, and dividing subtracts them.

1. **Down the tree.** The prior factor sends $\Gauss(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2 + \tau^2)$ to $s_i$. The performance factor
   sends $\Gauss(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2 + \tau^2 + \beta^2)$ to $p_i$. The sum factor sends a Gaussian to $t_j$ whose mean is
   the sum of the members' means and whose variance is the sum of their variances. The difference factor does the same
   for $d_j$ with a sign. All these messages are exact, because every factor above the outcome is Gaussian or linear,
   as in <NoteLink to="belief-propagation">belief propagation</NoteLink> on a Gaussian tree.
2. **At the outcome.** The outcome factor $\indicator[d_j > \varepsilon]$ is not Gaussian. EP forms the marginal of
   $d_j$ as the incoming Gaussian times the indicator, projects it to the Gaussian with the same mean and variance
   (the $v$ and $w$ functions above), and divides by the incoming message. The quotient is the Gaussian message the
   outcome factor sends up.
3. **Between neighbouring differences.** With three or more teams, team $j$'s performance feeds both $d_{j-1}$ and
   $d_j$. Updating one difference changes the message into the next. The inner loop visits the difference nodes in
   turn, each time dividing out its own previous message before recomputing it, until the messages stop changing.
4. **Up the tree.** The final messages from the difference nodes travel back through the sum and performance factors to
   each $s_i$. Each skill's new belief is its prior times the message from below.

For two players, steps 1 to 4 reproduce the closed-form update derived above. For a team game, the same derivation with
$c^2 = \sum_{i}(\beta^2 + \sigma_i^2)$ over all players in both teams gives each winning player
$\mu_i \leftarrow \mu_i + (\sigma_i^2/c)\, v$ and each losing player the mirror image. Team-mates split the credit in
proportion to their variances.

Across games TrueSkill makes a single forward pass: each game's message is computed once and never revised. This is
assumed density filtering, and its result depends on the order of the games. Re-running EP over past games, and
smoothing along each player's history, is <NoteLink to="trueskill-through-time">TrueSkill Through Time</NoteLink>
<Cite id="dangauthier2007" />.

## Leaderboards and matchmaking

**Conservative skill.** Xbox Live ranked players by $\mu_i - 3\sigma_i$, a lower bound that the skill exceeds with
probability $\Phi(3) = 0.9987$ under the player's belief <Cite id="herbrich2006" />. A new player starts at
$25 - 3 \times 25/3 = 0$. The score rises as games reduce $\sigma$, even without an increase in $\mu$, so the top of the
board holds players who are both good and well measured.

**Match quality.** A fair game is one whose outcome is least predictable. TrueSkill scores a candidate game by its draw
probability as $\varepsilon \to 0$, relative to the largest possible value. For two players this is the density of $d$
at 0, $\Gauss(0; t, c^2)$, divided by its value $1/\sqrt{2\pi\, 2\beta^2}$ when $t = 0$ and $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 0$:

$$
q = \sqrt{\frac{2\beta^2}{c^2}}\; \exp\paren{-\frac{t^2}{2c^2}} .
$$

The first factor penalises uncertainty and the second a gap in means. Two new players at the default prior have
$q = \sqrt{2\beta^2/(2\beta^2 + 2\sigma_0^2)} = \sqrt{0.2} = 0.447$. The matchmaker picks games with high $q$.

## Worked example: an upset

Player A has $\mu_A = 30$, $\sigma_A = 4$. Player B has $\mu_B = 22$, $\sigma_B = 6$. Use the Xbox defaults
$\beta = 25/6$ and $\tau = 25/300$, and a draw probability of 0.1. B wins.

1. **Dynamics.** $\sigma_A^2 = 16 + \tau^2 = 16.0069$ and $\sigma_B^2 = 36.0069$.
2. **Draw margin.** $\varepsilon = \sqrt 2 \times 4.1667 \times \Phi^{-1}(0.55) = 5.8926 \times 0.12566 = 0.7405$.
3. **Scale.** $c^2 = 2 \times 17.3611 + 16.0069 + 36.0069 = 86.7361$, so $c = 9.3132$.
4. **From the winner's side.** $t = \mu_B - \mu_A = -8$, so $x = t/c = -0.8590$, $a = \varepsilon/c = 0.0795$ and
   $x - a = -0.9385$. Before the game B's win had probability $\Phi(-0.9385) = 0.1740$.
5. **Corrections.** $\phi(-0.9385) = 0.25683$, so $v = 0.25683/0.17399 = 1.4761$ and
   $w = 1.4761 \times (1.4761 - 0.9385) = 0.7936$.
6. **Winner B.** $\mu_B = 22 + (36.0069/9.3132) \times 1.4761 = 22 + 3.8662 \times 1.4761 = 27.707$, and
   $\sigma_B^2 = 36.0069 \times (1 - 0.41513 \times 0.7936) = 24.145$, so $\sigma_B = 4.914$.
7. **Loser A.** $\mu_A = 30 - (16.0069/9.3132) \times 1.4761 = 27.463$, and
   $\sigma_A^2 = 16.0069 \times (1 - 0.18455 \times 0.7936) = 13.663$, so $\sigma_A = 3.696$.

B's mean rises by 5.71 and A's falls by 2.54: B's larger $\sigma$ takes most of the correction. The conservative
scores move from 18 to 16.37 for A and from 4 to 12.97 for B. These numbers agree with scipy and with the `trueskill`
Python package (0.4.5), which reports A at $(27.463, 3.696)$ and B at $(27.707, 4.914)$.

Winn and Bishop's first example makes the same point with more extreme beliefs. Jill, a newcomer, has belief
$\Gauss(120, 40^2)$; Fred has $\Gauss(100, 5^2)$; $\beta = 5$ and there are no draws or dynamics. Jill wins. Then
$c = \sqrt{1600 + 25 + 50} = 40.93$ and $x = 20/40.93 = 0.4887$, $v = 0.5150$ and $w = 0.5169$. Jill moves to
$\Gauss(140.1, 28.5^2)$ and Fred only to $\Gauss(99.7, 4.98^2)$ <Cite id="winn2023" />.

## What the model-based view adds

Winn and Bishop use TrueSkill to teach a way of building models <Cite id="winn2023" />. Each assumption is stated in
words, then becomes one factor. When the data disagree with the model, the fix is a changed assumption, not a tuned
formula. Their chapter adds draws, then several players, then teams, then changing skills, each as a small change to the
graph. The inference code does not change in kind: the same local message computations run on a larger graph.
<NoteLink to="trueskill-2">TrueSkill 2</NoteLink> continues the same process with more observed quantities
<Cite id="minka2018" />.

## Cost

A two-player update is a few arithmetic operations and two evaluations of $\phi$ and $\Phi$. A game with $n$ players in
$k$ teams costs $O(n)$ for the messages down and up and $O(k)$ per sweep of the inner loop over differences, which
converges in a few sweeps. In the original experiments, TrueSkill took at most twice the time of an Elo update
<Cite id="herbrich2006" />. Each player's state is two numbers.

## Trade-offs

- **Gaussian and additive assumptions.** Team performance is a plain sum of player performances. Squads that queue
  together perform better than their sum, which <NoteLink to="trueskill-2">TrueSkill 2</NoteLink> models with an offset
  <Cite id="minka2018" />.
- **Only the ranking is used.** A narrow win and a rout give the same update. Scores, kills and other statistics carry
  information that the model discards.
- **Order dependence.** A single forward pass gives different ratings for the same games in a different order. EP
  sweeps over the history remove this.
- **Not zero-sum.** Means move by $\sigma_i^2 v/c$, so the winner's gain and the loser's loss differ when their
  variances differ. The total of the $\mu_i$ is not conserved, unlike Elo's total rating.
- **Choice of $\beta$ and $\tau$.** $\beta$ measures how much luck the game has, and $\tau$ how fast skill drifts. Both
  are set per game mode, by hand or by maximising the likelihood of past outcomes.
