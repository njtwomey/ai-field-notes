<Definition>
A **skill rating** system assigns each competitor $i$ a latent strength $s_i$ from the observed outcomes of contests,
such as wins, losses, draws or finishing orders. A probability model links strengths to outcomes, usually through the
difference $s_i - s_j$. An estimation method turns a history of outcomes into strengths, either in one batch fit or by
updating after every game.
</Definition>

## The problem

Skill is not observed. Only outcomes are, and outcomes are noisy: a stronger player sometimes loses. Four facts shape
every method in this family.

- **Only differences matter.** Outcome probabilities depend on $s_i - s_j$, so adding a constant to every strength
  changes nothing. A rating scale needs an anchor, such as a starting value or a mean of zero.
- **Data arrive as a stream.** Games are played continuously, and ratings are wanted after each one, so most systems
  update online.
- **Evidence is uneven.** A player with three games is known far less well than one with three thousand. Systems that
  track uncertainty move the first rating quickly and the second slowly.
- **Skill changes.** Players improve, decline and return after breaks. A model that treats skill as fixed stops
  learning.

ML is maximum likelihood, MM the minorise–maximise iteration, SGD stochastic gradient descent and EP expectation
propagation. Plackett–Luce and the TrueSkill family also model rankings of more than two competitors; the others
compare two at a time.

## Three choices that separate the models

**Logit or probit.** The logistic curve of Bradley–Terry and the Gaussian cdf of Thurstone differ by less than 0.01 once
their scales are matched, so the choice rarely changes a ranking. The logistic form gives simple
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> and closed-form odds. The Gaussian form composes: sums of Gaussian
performances stay Gaussian, which is what lets TrueSkill handle teams exactly up to the outcome factor.

**Point estimate or belief.** Elo keeps one number per player and moves it by a fixed step $K$. Glicko and TrueSkill
keep a mean and a variance. The variance acts as a per-player <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> that
starts large and shrinks with evidence, which is why a Bayesian system ranks a new player correctly after far fewer
games. The same structure appears in the <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>, whose gain plays the
role of $K$.

**Online or batch.** An online update uses each game once, in order, like
<NoteLink to="online-gradient-descent">online gradient descent</NoteLink>. A batch fit, such as maximum likelihood for
Bradley–Terry or smoothing in TrueSkill Through Time, lets later games revise the estimates for earlier ones. Batch
estimates are more accurate for history; online ones are what a live system can afford.

### Tracking non-stationary skill
 
When players improve, decline, or return after long breaks, online filters face a fundamental bias-variance trade-off: a
high update gain tracks sudden shifts quickly but amplifies noise during stable play. Tracking systems that maintain
rating variance (such as Glicko and TrueSkill) widen their uncertainty during inactive periods to facilitate fast
re-calibration, whereas Elo maintains a constant step size regardless of elapsed time. Retrospective smoothers like
TrueSkill Through Time eliminate lag entirely by propagating information symmetrically across all matches.

## Components

- <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Paired-comparison models</NoteLink>: Thurstone and Bradley–Terry, their
  maximum-likelihood fit and ties.
- <NoteLink to="plackett-luce-model">Plackett–Luce model</NoteLink>: rankings of several items.
- <NoteLink to="elo-rating">Elo rating</NoteLink>: the online gradient step on Bradley–Terry.
- <NoteLink to="glicko-and-glicko-2">Glicko and Glicko-2</NoteLink>: Elo with a rating deviation and a volatility.
- <NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink>: a <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink> for teams and
  rankings, solved by expectation propagation.
- <NoteLink to="trueskill-through-time">TrueSkill Through Time</NoteLink>: smoothing skill trajectories over a whole
  history.
- <NoteLink to="trueskill-2">TrueSkill 2</NoteLink>: more observations per game and learned parameters.
- <NoteLink to="item-response-theory">Item response theory</NoteLink>: the same model for people answering test
  questions, from the Rasch model to the 4PL.
- <NoteLink to="ability-estimation-and-item-calibration">Ability estimation and item calibration</NoteLink>: fitting
  abilities and item parameters, and adaptive testing.
- <NoteLink to="polytomous-and-multidimensional-item-response-models">Polytomous and multidimensional item response
  models</NoteLink>: graded and partial-credit scores, and several abilities at once.

## Beyond games

The same models rank anything compared in pairs. Preference data for <Gloss name="language-model">language
models</Gloss> are fitted with the Bradley–Terry model to train a reward model, as in
<NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback">reinforcement learning from human feedback</NoteLink>. Sports
leagues, chess federations, online matchmaking, crowd-sourced comparisons of images or model outputs, and adaptive
testing all use one of the models above.
