<Definition>
Let $n$ items have strengths $\pi_1, \dots, \pi_n > 0$, or log-strengths $\theta_i = \log\pi_i$. The **Plackett–Luce
model** gives a ranking $\rho = (\rho_1, \dots, \rho_n)$, where $\rho_1$ is the item ranked first, the probability

$$
\pr(\rho) = \prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\pi_{\rho_k}}{\sum_{m=k}^{n} \pi_{\rho_m}}
= \prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{e^{\theta_{\rho_k}}}{\sum_{m=k}^{n} e^{\theta_{\rho_m}}}
$$

<Cite id="plackett1975" />. Each factor is the probability of choosing the next item from those not yet ranked, in
proportion to strength <Cite id="luce1959" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A ranking is built from the top. The winner is chosen from all $n$ items by a softmax of the log-strengths. That item
is removed, and second place is chosen from the remaining $n - 1$ by the same rule. The last factor is always 1.

For two items the model is <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">Bradley–Terry</NoteLink>:
$\pr(i \text{ above } j) = \pi_i/(\pi_i + \pi_j)$. The first factor alone is the multinomial logit, the choice model of
<NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression">multinomial logistic regression</NoteLink> with one score per item.

## Luce's choice axiom

Luce derived the choice rule from one assumption about choices from sets <Cite id="luce1959" />. The **choice axiom**
says that the relative probability of choosing $i$ over $j$ does not depend on which other options are available:

$$
\frac{\pr(i \mid S)}{\pr(j \mid S)} = \frac{\pr(i \mid \set{i, j})}{\pr(j \mid \set{i, j})} \quad \text{for every set } S \ni i, j .
$$

This property is also called <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> of irrelevant alternatives. Define
$\pi_i = \pr(i \mid S)/\pr(r \mid S)$ for a fixed reference item $r$; the axiom makes this ratio the same for every $S$
containing $i$ and $r$. Then $\pr(i \mid S) \propto \pi_i$ within any $S$, and normalising gives
$\pr(i \mid S) = \pi_i/\sum_{j \in S}\pi_j$. Applying this rule repeatedly to the shrinking set of unranked items gives
the Plackett–Luce product.

## Random utility

The same model arises from noisy scores. Let $u_i = \theta_i + g_i$ with independent standard Gumbel noise $g_i$, and
rank the items by $u_i$. By the <NoteLink to="gumbel-max-trick">Gumbel-max trick</NoteLink>, the top item is $i$ with
probability $\operatorname{softmax}(\thetavec)_i$. Conditional on which item is largest, the remaining noise terms are
still independent Gumbels shifted by their $\theta$, so the second place follows the softmax over the rest, and so on.
The ranking by $u$ therefore has the Plackett–Luce distribution. Sampling a ranking costs one sort of $n$ perturbed
scores.

Replacing the Gumbel noise by Gaussian noise gives a Thurstonian ranking model. Its probabilities have no product form,
and computing them requires an $(n-1)$-dimensional <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>. The
multi-team ranking in <NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink> is of this Gaussian kind, which is why TrueSkill
needs approximate inference.

## Fitting

For rankings $\rho^{(1)}, \dots, \rho^{(N)}$ the log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\thetavec) = \sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{n_r - 1}\brack{\theta_{\rho^{(r)}_k} - \log\sum_{m=k}^{n_r} e^{\theta_{\rho^{(r)}_m}}},
$$

a sum of log-softmax terms, concave in $\thetavec$ and invariant to adding a constant. Its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is observed minus expected choice counts. The minorisation used for
Bradley–Terry, $-\log x \ge 1 - \log y - x/y$ applied to each denominator, gives an MM update of the same form as
Zermelo's iteration <Cite id="hunter2004" />:

$$
\pi_i^{\text{new}} = \frac{w_i}{\sum_{r}\sum_{k} \delta_{irk} \big/ \sum_{m=k}^{n_r} \pi_{\rho^{(r)}_m}},
$$

where $w_i$ counts the choice stages at which $i$ was chosen (every rank except last) and $\delta_{irk} = 1$ when item
$i$ was still unranked at stage $k$ of ranking $r$ and that stage was a real choice ($k < n_r$). As with Bradley–Terry,
a finite maximum needs every item to have been beaten and to have beaten some other item, in the sense of a strongly
connected comparison graph.

**Partial rankings.** A top-$k$ list, such as the first three finishers of a race, uses the first $k$ factors only.
Rankings of different subsets of items combine in one likelihood, because each ranking involves only its own items.

## Learning to rank

In <Gloss name="ltr">learning to rank</Gloss>, $\theta_i = f(\xvec_i)$ is a model's score for document $i$ given a
query. **<Gloss name="listnet" />** trains the scores by the cross-entropy between the top-one probabilities of the true
and the predicted ranking, which are the first Plackett–Luce factor, a softmax <Cite id="cao2007" />.
**<Gloss name="listmle" />** maximises the full Plackett–Luce likelihood of the correct ranking <Cite id="xia2008" />.
Both are covered with other losses in <NoteLink to="ranking-losses">ranking losses</NoteLink>. Sampling rankings by
sorting Gumbel-perturbed scores is also how stochastic rankers and policy-gradient methods for ranking draw from a
Plackett–Luce policy.

## Pitfalls

- **Independence of irrelevant alternatives.** Adding a near-copy of item $i$ takes probability from every item in
  proportion, not mainly from $i$. Real choices among similar options violate this.
- **Top-heavy information.** Early factors choose from many items and carry most of the information. The order among
  the last few items is decided by a few near-binary choices.
- **Not the same as pairwise fitting.** Splitting each ranking into its $\binom{n}{2}$ pairs and fitting Bradley–Terry
  counts the same evidence many times and gives overconfident <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink>,
  although the point estimates are often close.
