<Definition>
**TrueSkill 2** is a <NoteLink to="skill-rating">skill rating</NoteLink> system for team-based online games that keeps
TrueSkill's single Gaussian skill per player and its game model, and changes the assumptions about how skills and match
data are generated <Cite id="minka2018" />. It adds offsets for squads and for experience, predicts each player's kill
and death counts and decision to quit from their performance, and links a player's skills across game modes. Its
parameters are learned from historical matches rather than set by hand.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="trueskill">TrueSkill</NoteLink> uses only which team won. A match record in a shooter contains much more:
who played together as a squad, how many matches each player has played, each player's kills and deaths, who quit, and
the player's record in other game modes. Game studios also asked for properties that constrain any fix
<Cite id="minka2018" />:

- a single skill number per player, so existing matchmakers keep working;
- cheap online updates;
- no hand tuning after launch;
- **aligned incentives**: a player should raise their rating only by playing the game well. A quantity that a player
  controls directly, such as how often they heal themselves, is a poor signal of skill.

## Metric-driven modelling

The model was built by a loop the authors call metric-driven modelling <Cite id="minka2018" />. Partition players by a
feature, such as squad size or the kill rate in their previous match. Compare their actual win rate with the win rate
the model predicted. A gap shows an assumption that the data contradict. Change that assumption, the smallest change
that closes the gap, and repeat. This is the model-based approach of <NoteLink to="model-based-machine-learning">Winn
and Bishop</NoteLink> applied to a production system <Cite id="winn2023" />: each fix is a change to the generative
model, and the inference code is regenerated from the model description, here with
<NoteLink to="infer-net">Infer.NET</NoteLink>.

## Mechanism: the changed assumptions

Write $s_i$ for player $i$'s skill and $p_i$ for their performance in a match. The scale is fixed by setting the
performance noise to $\beta = 1$.

**Squads.** Friends who queue together win more often than the sum of their skills predicts, and the gap grows with
squad size. TrueSkill 2 adds an offset that depends on the size of the player's squad:

$$
p_i \sim \Gauss\paren{s_i + \operatorname{squadOffset}(\text{squad size}),\ \beta^2},
$$

with one learned offset per squad size and $\operatorname{squadOffset}(1) = 0$.

**Experience.** New players win less often than their ratings predict, and the gap closes over their first matches.
Classic TrueSkill's <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> is unbiased. TrueSkill 2 biases it by an amount
that depends on the number of matches $n_i$ the player has played in the mode, capped at 200, with drift per match
$\gamma$:

$$
s_i^{\text{next}} \sim \Gauss\paren{s_i + \operatorname{experienceOffset}(\min(n_i, 200)),\ \gamma^2}.
$$

Each match now nudges the rating up slightly whatever the result. The nudge is small next to a win or a loss.

**Kills and deaths.** A player's kill count $k_i$ is modelled from their own performance and the performance of the
opposing team, scaled by time played $T_i$:

$$
k_i \sim \max\paren{0,\ \Gauss\paren{(w_p\, p_i + w_o\, p_i^{o})\,T_i,\ v\,T_i}},
$$

with learned weights, $w_p > 0$ and $w_o < 0$ for kills, and the signs reversed for deaths. Here $p_i^{o}$ is the
opposing team's performance, averaged so that it works for teams of any size. The mean and variance both scale with
time played, as the Halo 5 data showed, but the variance is not equal to the mean, so a truncated Gaussian replaces a
Poisson model. The counts depend on performance, not on a separate kill skill. The team outcome still determines
performance first, and the counts only redistribute credit within a team: the player with more kills gets more of the
win.

**Quitting.** Players who quit tend to be underperforming. TrueSkill 2 models an underperformance event
$\text{under}_i$, true when $p_i - p_i^{o}$ plus Gaussian noise falls below a threshold, and

$$
\text{quit}_i = \text{unrelated}_i \ \text{or}\ \paren{\text{related}_i \ \text{and}\ \text{under}_i},
$$

with learned probabilities for the two Boolean causes. Observing a quit lowers the inferred performance of the quitter.
In the online updater a quit is an extra observation added only when a player quits, so matches without quits cost the
same as in TrueSkill.

**Game modes.** Skills in different modes are correlated. Each player has a base skill and one offset per mode, and the
skill in mode $d$ is $s_{id} = w_d\, \text{base}_i + \text{offset}_{id}$ with $w_d \ge 0$. A player who starts a new
mode borrows their rating from the others. Once they have played that mode enough, games elsewhere barely change it.

## Learning the parameters

The model has many parameters per game mode: prior mean and variance, drift per match and per unit of idle time, draw
margin, squad offsets, 200 experience offsets, and the kill, death and quit weights. Each parameter touches millions of
factors, so its posterior is sharp. TrueSkill 2 therefore treats each parameter as a point estimate. It runs batch
<Gloss name="ep">expectation propagation</Gloss> over the whole history, as in
<NoteLink to="trueskill-through-time">TrueSkill Through Time</NoteLink>, computes each parameter's
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> from the EP messages into it, and updates the parameters with the Rprop
optimiser. About 100 forward and backward sweeps are enough <Cite id="minka2018" />.

## Results

On Halo 5 data, trained on 23 million matches and tested on the next 3 million, TrueSkill 2 predicted the winning team
in 68% of test matches against 52% for classic TrueSkill with tuned parameters <Cite id="minka2018" />. Each change
also closed the gap in the metric that motivated it, such as the win rate of large squads. TrueSkill 2 has rated
players in Gears of War 4 since its launch in 2016, with online updates during a season and a batch refit before each
new one.

## Cost

An online update is still one EP pass on a per-match <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink>, now with a few
extra factors per player. The state per player is a base skill, an offset per mode played, and an experience count.
Parameter learning is an offline batch job over the history.

## Trade-offs

- **Incentives constrain the model.** Statistics that a player can inflate without helping the team are left out, even
  when they would improve prediction. Kills enter only through performance, with a monotone link, so more kills never
  lower a rating.
- **Completion is rewarded.** To penalise quitting, the model must slightly favour completing, so a player who completes
  a match with almost no play time gains a little. The paper points out this side effect.
- **Game-specific.** Squad, kill and quit models suit online shooters. Other games need their own metrics and their own
  changes.
- **Data per mode.** The model needs enough historical matches per mode. Modes with fewer than about 1000 matches borrow
  parameters from a similar popular mode.
