<Definition>
Let $\pi(\xvec)$ be a distribution over $\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_d)$ and write $\xvec_{-j}$ for all components except
$x_j$. The **full conditional** of $x_j$ is $\pi(x_j \mid \xvec_{-j})$. A Gibbs sampler updates the state by drawing

$$
x_j \sim \pi(x_j \mid \xvec_{-j})
$$

for each $j$ in turn (a **systematic scan**) or for a randomly chosen $j$ (a **random scan**), always conditioning on
the current values of the others. The chain has $\pi$ as its stationary distribution <Cite id="geman1984" />
<Cite id="gelfand1990" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Sampling a high-dimensional joint distribution directly is hard, but its one-dimensional conditionals are often easy. In
a <NoteLink to="bayesian-network">Bayesian network</NoteLink> or a <NoteLink to="markov-random-field">Markov random
field</NoteLink>, the full conditional of a variable depends only on its <Gloss name="markov-blanket">Markov
blanket</Gloss>, and in conjugate Bayesian models it is a standard distribution. Gibbs sampling builds a
<NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> sampler from these conditionals alone, with
no proposal to tune and no rejections.

## Invariance of the target distribution

A Gibbs update of $x_j$ is a <NoteLink to="metropolis-hastings">Metropolis–Hastings</NoteLink> move with the proposal
$q(\yvec \mid \xvec) = \pi(y_j \mid \xvec_{-j})$ for $\yvec_{-j} = \xvec_{-j}$, and zero otherwise. The proposal changes
only $x_j$. Since $\yvec_{-j} = \xvec_{-j}$, write $\pi(\xvec) = \pi(x_j \mid \xvec_{-j})\,\pi(\xvec_{-j})$ and likewise
for $\yvec$. The Metropolis–Hastings ratio is

$$
\frac{\pi(\yvec)\, q(\xvec \mid \yvec)}{\pi(\xvec)\, q(\yvec \mid \xvec)}
= \frac{\pi(y_j \mid \xvec_{-j})\,\pi(\xvec_{-j}) \cdot \pi(x_j \mid \xvec_{-j})}
{\pi(x_j \mid \xvec_{-j})\,\pi(\xvec_{-j}) \cdot \pi(y_j \mid \xvec_{-j})} = 1.
$$

Every proposal is accepted. Each single-variable update therefore satisfies detailed balance and leaves $\pi$
invariant. A systematic scan composes these updates. The composition leaves $\pi$ invariant because each step does,
although it is not itself reversible. A random scan is a mixture of the updates and is reversible.

Invariance does not guarantee ergodicity. If $\pi$ puts zero probability on part of the path between two regions, a
sampler that changes one coordinate at a time may be unable to cross. For example, if $x_1 = x_2$ with probability 1,
no single-coordinate update can move either variable.

## Full conditionals in graphical models

The full conditional is proportional to the joint as a function of $x_j$, so every factor that does not contain $x_j$
cancels. In a Markov random field only the potentials that touch $x_j$ remain.

**<NoteLink to="ising-model">Ising model</NoteLink>.** With spins $x_i \in \set{-1, +1}$, couplings $J$ between
neighbours and field $h$,

$$
\pi(x_i = +1 \mid \xvec_{-i}) = \frac{e^{a_i}}{e^{a_i} + e^{-a_i}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2 a_i}}, \qquad
a_i = J \sum_{j \in N(i)} x_j + h,
$$

where $N(i)$ is the set of neighbours of $i$. Each update needs only the neighbouring spins. This is the "heat bath"
algorithm of statistical physics, which Geman and Geman applied to image restoration <Cite id="geman1984" />.

## Worked example: a correlated Gaussian

**From covariance to correlation.** Let $(y_1, y_2)$ be jointly Gaussian with means $\mu_1, \mu_2$, variances
$\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ and covariance $\sigma_{12}$. Their correlation is $\rho = \sigma_{12}/(\sigma_1\sigma_2)$,
which lies in $[-1, 1]$ by the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink>. Standardise each
coordinate, $x_i = (y_i - \mu_i)/\sigma_i$. Each $x_i$ has mean 0 and variance 1, and
$\cov(x_1, x_2) = \sigma_{12}/(\sigma_1\sigma_2) = \rho$. The covariance matrix of $\xvec = (x_1, x_2)$ is therefore the
correlation matrix

$$
\Sigmamat = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det\Sigmamat = 1 - \rho^2, \qquad
\Sigmamat^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - \rho^2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $\abs{\rho} < 1$ the joint density is the <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate
normal</NoteLink> density with the <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink>
$\xvec\transpose\Sigmamat^{-1}\xvec$ written out:

$$
p(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\exp\paren{-\frac{x_1^2 - 2\rho\, x_1 x_2 + x_2^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)}}.
$$

**The full conditionals.** The conditional density is $p(x_1 \mid x_2) = p(x_1, x_2)/p(x_2)$, and $p(x_2)$ does not
depend on $x_1$. As a function of $x_1$ the conditional is therefore proportional to the joint density.
<NoteLink to="completing-the-square">Complete the square</NoteLink> in $x_1$:

$$
x_1^2 - 2\rho\, x_1 x_2 + x_2^2 = (x_1 - \rho\, x_2)^2 + (1 - \rho^2)\, x_2^2.
$$

The second term does not involve $x_1$, so it joins the normalising constant:

$$
p(x_1 \mid x_2) \propto \exp\paren{-\frac{(x_1 - \rho\, x_2)^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)}}.
$$

This is a Gaussian density in $x_1$ with mean $\rho\, x_2$ and variance $1 - \rho^2$. The joint density is symmetric in
$x_1$ and $x_2$, so the same argument with the roles swapped gives the other conditional. The full conditionals are

$$
x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \Gauss(\rho\, x_2,\ 1 - \rho^2), \qquad x_2 \mid x_1 \sim \Gauss(\rho\, x_1,\ 1 - \rho^2).
$$

Knowing $x_2$ shifts the mean of $x_1$ by $\rho$ standard deviations per standard deviation of $x_2$, and shrinks its
variance from 1 to $1 - \rho^2$. Undoing the standardisation gives the general form,
$y_1 \mid y_2 \sim \Gauss\paren{\mu_1 + \rho\,\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(y_2 - \mu_2),\ \sigma_1^2(1 - \rho^2)}$.

**The same result from the <NoteLink to="factor-graph">factor graph</NoteLink>.** Multiplying out the quadratic form
splits the joint density into three factors, one for each entry of the <Gloss name="precision-matrix">precision
matrix</Gloss> $\Lambdamat = \Sigmamat^{-1}$:

$$
p(x_1, x_2) \propto \underbrace{e^{-x_1^2/(2(1 - \rho^2))}}_{\phi_1(x_1)}\;
\underbrace{e^{-x_2^2/(2(1 - \rho^2))}}_{\phi_2(x_2)}\;
\underbrace{e^{\rho\, x_1 x_2/(1 - \rho^2)}}_{\psi_{12}(x_1, x_2)}.
$$

The full conditional of $x_1$ is proportional to the product of the factors that involve $x_1$, here
$\phi_1\psi_{12}$. The factor $\phi_2$ is constant in $x_1$ and drops out: it is the $(1 - \rho^2)x_2^2$ term that the
completed square discarded. The other variables that those factors touch form the Markov blanket of $x_1$, here
$\set{x_2}$. The same argument holds for a zero-mean Gaussian in any dimension:

$$
x_j \mid \xvec_{-j} \sim \Gauss\paren{-\frac{1}{\Lambda_{jj}}\sum_{k \ne j}\Lambda_{jk}\, x_k,\ \frac{1}{\Lambda_{jj}}}.
$$

The Markov blanket of $x_j$ is the set of $x_k$ with $\Lambda_{jk} \ne 0$, so a Gibbs update reads only the neighbours
of $x_j$ in the graph. In the bivariate case $\Lambda_{11} = 1/(1 - \rho^2)$ and $\Lambda_{12} = -\rho/(1 - \rho^2)$,
which give the mean $\rho\, x_2$ and the variance $1 - \rho^2$ found above.

A sweep draws $x_1' = \rho\, x_2 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,\epsilon_1$, then
$x_2' = \rho\, x_1' + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,\epsilon_2$ with $\epsilon_1, \epsilon_2 \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. Substituting the
previous sweep's $x_2 = \rho\, x_1 + \dots$ shows that the sequence of $x_1$ values is an autoregressive process,

$$
x_1^{(t+1)} = \rho^2\, x_1^{(t)} + \text{independent Gaussian noise},
$$

with lag-$k$ autocorrelation $\rho^{2k}$. Its <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo-diagnostics">integrated
autocorrelation time</NoteLink> is

$$
\tau = 1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho^{2k} = \frac{1 + \rho^2}{1 - \rho^2}.
$$

For $\rho = 0.9$, $\tau = 1.81/0.19 = 9.5$: each sweep is worth about a tenth of an independent sample. For
$\rho = 0.99$, $\tau = 99.5$. The conditionals have standard deviation $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$, 0.14 for $\rho = 0.99$,
while the distribution extends about $\sqrt{1 + \rho} = 1.41$ along its long axis. Axis-parallel steps of size 0.14
explore a distance of 1.41 only by a slow <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>.

## Variants

- **Blocked Gibbs.** Sample strongly dependent variables jointly from their conditional given the rest. In the example,
  drawing $(x_1, x_2)$ jointly gives independent samples. In a <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov model</Gloss>, the whole
  state sequence can be drawn at once by forward filtering and backward sampling.
- **Collapsed Gibbs.** Integrate some variables out analytically and sample the rest from the resulting marginal. The
  sampler then works in a lower dimension, with less dependence, and never samples the integrated variables
  <Cite id="liu1994collapsed" />. The standard sampler for <Gloss name="latent-dirichlet-allocation">latent Dirichlet
  allocation</Gloss> integrates out the topic proportions and word distributions and resamples only the topic of each
  word <Cite id="griffiths2004" />.
- **Metropolis-within-Gibbs.** When a full conditional cannot be sampled directly, replace its exact draw by one
  Metropolis–Hastings step that leaves that conditional invariant. The composition still leaves $\pi$ invariant.
- **<NoteLink to="data-augmentation">Data augmentation</NoteLink>.** Add latent variables that make every full
  conditional a distribution that can be sampled directly, such as the component labels of a mixture model, and sample
  them alongside the parameters.

## Trade-offs

- **No tuning, no rejections.** For conditionally conjugate
  models every step is a draw from a standard distribution <Cite id="casella1992" />.
- **Needs tractable conditionals.** Non-conjugate models need Metropolis-within-Gibbs or other samplers.
- **Correlation slows it down.** Coordinate-wise moves are short when variables are strongly correlated, as the worked
  example shows. Reparameterising or blocking helps; <NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo">Hamiltonian Monte
  Carlo</NoteLink> moves along correlated directions directly.
- **Multimodality.** In an Ising model with strong coupling, flipping from mostly $+1$ to mostly $-1$ requires passing
  through configurations of low probability, one spin at a time. The sampler can stay in one phase for a very long time.
- **Relation to mean field.** <NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">Mean-field variational
  inference</NoteLink> has the same structure, visiting one variable at a time and using its Markov blanket, but it
  updates a distribution over each variable deterministically instead of drawing a value.
