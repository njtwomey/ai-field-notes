<Definition>
**Markov chain Monte Carlo methods** draw samples from a distribution $\pi$, usually a posterior, by simulating a
<NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink> whose stationary distribution is $\pi$. Any expectation
$\expect_\pi[f]$ is then estimated by an average over the samples. The methods need $\pi$ only up to a normalising
constant.
</Definition>

## What it is for

<NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian inference</NoteLink> needs expectations under a posterior $p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal) \propto p(\Dcal \mid
\thetavec)\,p(\thetavec)$: posterior means, credible intervals and predictive distributions. The normaliser $p(\Dcal)$
is an integral that is rarely tractable. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) avoids it, because each step compares the
target density at two points, and their ratio cancels the normaliser <Cite id="robert2004" />.
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">Probabilistic programming</NoteLink> systems such as Stan run MCMC by default,
and MCMC is the reference answer against which the deterministic approximations of the neighbouring topics are judged.

## Main ideas

**A chain whose long-run distribution is the target.** <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo" /> states the two
conditions a sampler needs. Detailed balance makes $\pi$ stationary, and ergodicity makes the chain converge to it.
Averages along one long chain then estimate expectations, with an error set by the autocorrelation of the chain rather
than by the number of steps alone.

**Propose and accept.** <NoteLink to="metropolis-hastings" /> builds a valid chain from any proposal distribution by
accepting each proposed move with a probability chosen to satisfy detailed balance. The step size of the proposal sets
a trade-off between acceptance rate and distance moved. <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling" /> resamples one variable, or a
block, at a time from its full conditional distribution. It is a Metropolis–Hastings move that is always accepted, and
it suits <NoteLink to="graphical-models" />, where full conditionals depend only on a variable's neighbours.

**Use the gradient to move far.** Random-walk proposals explore slowly in high dimensions.
<NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo" /> adds a momentum variable and simulates Hamiltonian dynamics with the leapfrog
integrator, which makes long moves that are almost always accepted <Cite id="neal2011" />.
<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics" /> takes gradient steps on $\log\pi$ plus Gaussian noise. Its discretisation bias is
removed by a Metropolis–Hastings test (MALA).

**Sample with minibatches.** On large datasets each full gradient costs a pass over the data.
<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-langevin-dynamics" /> uses minibatch gradients with injected noise and a decreasing
step size. The injected noise eventually dominates the minibatch noise, which turns <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic
gradient descent</Gloss> into an approximate posterior sampler.

**Check before trusting.** A chain can look stable while missing part of the distribution.
<NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo-diagnostics" /> covers trace plots, the potential scale reduction factor $\hat R$
computed from several chains, and the <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss>, which discounts autocorrelation
<Cite id="gelman2013" />.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo" />, which every other note requires. Metropolis–Hastings comes next,
and Gibbs sampling and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo are special or extended cases of it. Langevin dynamics leads to its
stochastic-gradient form. The diagnostics note applies to every sampler and can be read straight after the first note.
