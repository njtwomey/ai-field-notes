<Definition>
**Message-passing approximations** compute approximate marginals of a distribution that factorises over a graph, by
exchanging local messages between factors and variables until they settle. They keep the form of
<NoteLink to="belief-propagation" /> but drop one of the conditions that make it exact: a tree-shaped graph, or messages
that can be represented exactly.
</Definition>

## What it is for

<NoteLink to="exact-inference">Exact inference</NoteLink> costs time exponential in the treewidth of the graph. Grids of
pixels, error-correcting codes and dense networks of interacting variables are far beyond it. Models with continuous
variables and non-Gaussian factors, such as probit or robust likelihoods, have messages with no closed form at all. The
methods here give fast, deterministic answers in both cases. Loopy belief propagation decodes turbo and low-density
parity-check codes, and expectation propagation drives <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-classification">Gaussian process
classifiers</NoteLink> and the TrueSkill rating system.

## Main ideas

**Run the tree algorithm on a graph with cycles.** <NoteLink to="loopy-belief-propagation" /> applies the sum-product
updates to a graph with cycles and iterates until the messages stop changing. Its beliefs are not exact marginals, and
the iteration need not converge. When it does converge, its fixed points are exactly the stationary points of the Bethe
free energy, an approximation to $-\log Z$ <Cite id="yedidia2005" />. That result explains when the method works and
leads to generalised versions on clusters of variables.

**Project each message onto a tractable family.** <NoteLink to="expectation-propagation" /> approximates a posterior
that is a product of factors by giving each factor an exponential-family site. Each update removes one site to form a
cavity, multiplies in the true factor and matches the moments of the result. Moment matching minimises a local forward
Kullback–Leibler divergence, which tends to cover all the mass of the true posterior.

**Both minimise local divergences.** Belief propagation and expectation propagation can be derived as minimising a
divergence between the true distribution and its approximation one factor at a time <Cite id="minka2005" />.
<NoteLink to="mean-field-variational-inference">Mean-field variational inference</NoteLink> minimises the reverse
divergence and tends to underestimate variance. The choice of divergence explains much of the difference between the
methods.

**No objective, no guarantee.** Neither method decreases a single objective at every step. Both can oscillate, and
expectation propagation can produce sites with negative precision. Damping, power expectation propagation and
double-loop algorithms restore convergence, at extra cost.
