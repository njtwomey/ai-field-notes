<Definition>
**Exact inference** computes, without approximation, quantities of a graphical model such as the marginal $p(x_i)$ of
one variable, the conditional distribution of some variables given observed ones, the normaliser $Z$ or the most
probable joint assignment. The algorithms in this topic exploit the factorisation of $p(\xvec)$ to avoid summing over
all joint states.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A model with $n$ variables of $k$ states each has $k^n$ joint states, so a brute-force sum is hopeless beyond a few
dozen variables. Many useful models still allow exact answers: chains such as hidden Markov models, trees, and graphs
whose cycles are small. In these models exact inference gives filtering and smoothing, decoding of the most likely
sequence, and the gradients needed to fit parameters. It also defines what the approximate methods approximate
<Cite id="koller2009" />.

## Main ideas

**Push sums inside products.** <NoteLink to="variable-elimination" /> computes a marginal by summing out one variable at
a time. Each sum touches only the factors that mention the variable, and it produces a new factor over that variable's
neighbours. The cost is $O(n k^{w+1})$, where $w$ is the induced width of the elimination order. Finding the order with
the smallest width is itself NP-hard, so heuristics choose it.

**Reuse the intermediate results as messages.** On a tree, the intermediate factors of variable elimination can be
stored and shared between queries. <NoteLink to="belief-propagation" /> passes these messages along the edges of a
factor graph, one sweep inward and one outward, and obtains every marginal at about twice the cost of one
<Cite id="kschischang2001" />. Replacing sums by maxima gives the max-product algorithm, which finds the most probable
assignment; on a chain it is the <Gloss name="viterbi">Viterbi algorithm</Gloss>.

**Turn a graph with cycles into a tree.** Messages on a graph with cycles can circulate and double-count evidence. The
<NoteLink to="junction-tree-algorithm" /> triangulates the graph, groups its maximal cliques into a tree with the
running intersection property, and runs belief propagation between cliques. The answer is exact, and the cost is
exponential in the size of the largest clique.

**Treewidth sets the cost.** All three algorithms are exponential in the same quantity: the induced width of the best
elimination order, which equals the treewidth of the graph. Grids, dense graphs and most models with many interacting
latent variables have large treewidth. For them exact inference is intractable and an approximation is needed.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="variable-elimination" />, which states the problem and its cost. Belief propagation requires
it and shares its work across all marginals. The junction tree algorithm requires both and extends them to graphs with
cycles.
