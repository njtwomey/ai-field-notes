<Definition>
**ROC analysis** studies classifiers as points or curves in ROC space, the unit square of
<Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> against <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rate</Gloss>, together with the
class prior $\pi$ and the error costs $c_{\text{FP}}$ and $c_{\text{FN}}$. Every metric and every expected cost of a
$2 \times 2$ <NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink> becomes a function on this square. The analysis
asks which classifiers can be optimal, for which operating conditions, and what a summary score assumes about those
conditions.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area" /> note defines the curve and its area. ROC
analysis uses the same space to answer decision questions. A team with several candidate classifiers may not know the
deployment costs or the class balance. ROC analysis shows which classifiers to keep, which threshold to use once the
costs are known, and how far a reported metric such as AUROC or $F_1$ depends on unstated assumptions about costs
<Cite id="fawcett2006" />.

## Main ideas

**Metrics are contours in ROC space.** Any metric of a confusion matrix is a function of the two rates and a skew ratio
$c$ that combines the class ratio and the cost ratio. <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-isometrics" />
draws its contours. Accuracy contours are straight lines of slope $c$, and precision contours pivot on the origin. The
shape of a metric's contours shows which trade-off it rewards <Cite id="flach2003" />.

**Only the convex hull can be optimal.** Expected cost is linear in the two rates, so it is constant along iso-cost
lines. Randomising between two classifiers reaches every point on the segment between them. Together these facts mean
that only classifiers on the <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-convex-hull" /> can minimise expected cost,
each for an interval of iso-cost slopes. Each hull slope is a likelihood ratio.

**Costs on the horizontal axis.** <NoteLink to="cost-curves" /> plot normalised expected cost against the probability
cost, a single number that combines prior and costs. Each ROC point becomes a line, and the lower envelope of the lines
is dual to the ROC convex hull. Cost curves show directly by how much one classifier beats another at each operating
condition.

**Fixing the <Gloss name="pr">precision-recall curve</Gloss>.** Precision-recall space lacks the linear interpolation
and the universal baseline of ROC space. <NoteLink to="precision-recall-gain-curves" /> rescale precision and recall
harmonically relative to the prevalence. In the new space the always-positive classifier sits at a corner, $F_1$
isometrics are straight, and the area under the curve relates to expected $F_1$.

**The threshold rule chooses the metric.** <NoteLink to="threshold-choice-methods" /> averages a classifier's loss over
a uniform distribution of cost proportions, under different rules for setting the threshold. A fixed threshold gives the
<NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink>, a threshold equal to the cost proportion gives the
<NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">Brier score</NoteLink>, a rate-based threshold gives a linear function of AUROC,
and the optimal threshold gives the convex-hull <NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions">refinement loss</NoteLink>
<Cite id="hernandezorallo2012" />.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the isometrics, which set up ROC space with skew. The convex hull comes next and is the result the rest
rely on. Cost curves and precision-recall-gain curves both require the hull: the first redraws it as a function of
costs, the second carries its properties into precision-recall space. The threshold-choice note comes last and links
the geometry to the proper scoring rules.
