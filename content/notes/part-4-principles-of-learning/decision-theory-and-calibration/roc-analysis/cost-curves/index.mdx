<Definition>
Let $p(+)$ be the probability of the positive class, $C(-\mid+)$ the cost of a <Gloss name="fn">false negative</Gloss>
and $C(+\mid-)$ the cost of a <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss>. The **probability cost** is

$$
\text{PC}(+) = \frac{p(+)\, C(-\mid+)}{p(+)\, C(-\mid+) + p(-)\, C(+\mid-)} \in [0, 1].
$$

A classifier with <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> FPR and false-negative rate
$\text{FNR} = 1 - \text{TPR}$ has **normalised expected cost**
$\text{NE}[C] = \text{FNR}\cdot\text{PC}(+) + \text{FPR}\cdot\paren{1 - \text{PC}(+)}$. A **cost curve** plots
$\text{NE}[C]$ against $\text{PC}(+)$ <Cite id="drummond2006" />.
</Definition>

## From expected cost to a straight line

The expected cost per case of a classifier, with correct decisions costing nothing, is

$$
\expect[C] = p(+)\, C(-\mid+)\, \text{FNR} + p(-)\, C(+\mid-)\, \text{FPR}.
$$

The largest value it can take is $p(+) C(-\mid+) + p(-) C(+\mid-)$, reached by a classifier that gets everything wrong.
Dividing by that maximum gives a cost in $[0, 1]$. Both weights, divided by the same maximum, become $\text{PC}(+)$ and
$1 - \text{PC}(+)$:

$$
\text{NE}[C] = \frac{\expect[C]}{p(+) C(-\mid+) + p(-) C(+\mid-)} = \text{FNR}\cdot\text{PC}(+) + \text{FPR}\cdot\paren{1 -
\text{PC}(+)}.
$$

For a fixed classifier, $\text{NE}[C]$ is linear in $\text{PC}(+)$. It runs from $\text{FPR}$ at $\text{PC}(+) = 0$,
where only negatives matter, to $\text{FNR}$ at $\text{PC}(+) = 1$, where only positives matter. A whole
<Gloss name="roc" /> point is one straight line on the cost plot. Changes in prior and changes in costs that leave
$\text{PC}(+)$ fixed are indistinguishable, which is the same fact as the single iso-cost slope of ROC analysis.

## Duality with ROC space

An iso-cost line in ROC space has slope $m = p(-) C(+\mid-) / \paren{p(+) C(-\mid+)}$ (see
<NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-convex-hull">ROC convex hull</NoteLink>). In terms of the probability
cost,

$$
m = \frac{1 - \text{PC}(+)}{\text{PC}(+)}.
$$

So each operating condition is a slope in ROC space and a vertical line on the cost plot. Each classifier is a point in
ROC space and a line on the cost plot. This is point-line duality. The classifiers that are optimal for some operating
condition are the vertices of the ROC convex hull in one view and the lines that touch the **lower envelope** in the
other. At each $\text{PC}(+)$, the lower envelope is the least normalised cost achievable by choosing among the
classifiers, which is the vertex that an iso-cost line of slope $m$ touches <Cite id="drummond2006" />.

The two trivial classifiers are the diagonals. Always predicting negative has $\text{FNR} = 1$, $\text{FPR} = 0$, so
$\text{NE} = \text{PC}(+)$. Always predicting positive has $\text{NE} = 1 - \text{PC}(+)$. Together they form a tent
peaking at $0.5$. A classifier is useful only where its line lies below the tent.

## What cost curves show that ROC curves hide

- **Cost differences are vertical distances.** The gap between two classifiers at a given $\text{PC}(+)$ is read
  directly off the $y$-axis. In ROC space the same difference is the distance between two parallel iso-cost lines,
  which is hard to judge by eye.
- **The operating range.** The interval of $\text{PC}(+)$ over which a classifier beats both trivial classifiers is
  where its line lies below the tent. A classifier with a good
  <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-curve-and-area">AUC</NoteLink> can be useless for the operating
  conditions that matter.
- **Averaging and confidence.** Averaging cost curves over cross-validation folds, or drawing confidence bands on
  $\text{NE}[C]$, is a vertical operation on the plot. Drummond and Holte give such procedures
  <Cite id="drummond2006" />.

## Area under the lower envelope

The area under a lower envelope is the expected normalised cost when $\text{PC}(+)$ is uniformly distributed on $[0, 1]$
and the best threshold is chosen for each value. Hernández-Orallo, Flach and Ferri showed that this area equals the
<NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions">refinement loss</NoteLink> of the classifier's scores after they are
calibrated along the ROC convex hull, with the Brier score computed as if the two classes were equally frequent
<Cite id="hernandezorallo2012" />. It is a measure of ranking quality under optimal threshold choice, which sits
alongside AUC and the Brier score as one of several <NoteLink to="threshold-choice-methods">threshold choice
methods</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Classifier $A$ has $\text{FPR} = 0.1$ and $\text{TPR} = 0.5$, so its cost line runs from $(0, 0.1)$ to $(1, 0.5)$.
Classifier $B$ has $\text{FPR} = 0.3$ and $\text{TPR} = 0.85$, a line from $(0, 0.3)$ to $(1, 0.15)$.

**Where they cross.** Setting $0.1 + 0.4\,\text{PC} = 0.3 - 0.15\,\text{PC}$ gives $\text{PC} = 0.2/0.55 = 0.364$.
The dual check: the ROC segment from $A$ to $B$ has slope $(0.85 - 0.5)/(0.3 - 0.1) = 1.75$, and
$(1 - 0.364)/0.364 = 1.75$.

**Operating ranges.** $A$ beats always-negative when $0.1 + 0.4\,\text{PC} < \text{PC}$, that is $\text{PC} > 1/6$, and
beats always-positive when $\text{PC} < 0.9/1.4 = 0.643$. $B$ beats the trivial classifiers for
$0.261 < \text{PC} < 0.824$. The lower envelope is always-negative up to $\text{PC} = 0.167$, then $A$ up to $0.364$,
then $B$ up to $0.824$, then always-positive.

**One operating condition.** With prevalence $p(+) = 0.2$, a false negative costing 4 and a false positive costing 1,
$\text{PC}(+) = 0.8/(0.8 + 0.8) = 0.5$. There $A$ costs $0.30$ and $B$ costs $0.225$, so $B$ is the better choice.
Multiplying back by the normaliser $0.8 + 0.8 = 1.6$ gives expected costs of $0.48$ and $0.36$ per case.

## Pitfalls

- **Normalised cost is not cost.** $\text{NE}[C]$ divides by a quantity that changes with the operating condition.
  Compare classifiers at one $\text{PC}(+)$, not the same classifier across different ones, unless you multiply back.
- **Lines from thresholds chosen on the test set** give an optimistic lower envelope, as for the ROC hull.
