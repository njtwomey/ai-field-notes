<Definition>
**Decision theory** chooses actions that minimise expected loss given a probability distribution over the unknown
outcome. **Calibration** is the property that makes this work in practice: a model is calibrated when, among the cases
it gives probability $p$, a fraction $p$ turn out positive. This topic covers both, together with the scores that
reward good probabilities and the methods that quantify uncertainty.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A classifier's output is useful only once it drives a decision: treat or not, flag or not, show or not. When the two
kinds of error cost different amounts, the best threshold on a probability is not $0.5$, and it moves with the costs and
the class balance <Cite id="elkan2001" />. That threshold is optimal only if the probabilities are correct. Modern
classifiers, deep networks in particular, are often overconfident, so their probabilities need checking and
correcting before they set thresholds, combine with other evidence or feed downstream systems
<Cite id="silvafilho2023" />.

## Main ideas

**Decide by expected loss.** The <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule" /> takes the action with the least posterior
expected loss, and no rule does better. For two classes it is a threshold on $P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ set by the costs.
<NoteLink to="cost-sensitive-learning" /> derives that threshold and shows how rebalancing the training data achieves
the same effect. The <NoteLink to="reject-option" /> adds a third action, to abstain at a cost.

**Read decisions off <Gloss name="roc" /> space.** Expected cost is linear in the
<Gloss name="fp">false-positive</Gloss> and <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rates</Gloss>, so costs and class priors
become straight lines in ROC space. <NoteLink to="receiver-operating-characteristic-analysis" /> uses this geometry to
find which classifiers can be optimal and which threshold to use once costs are known.

**Score probabilities with proper scoring rules.** A <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule" /> is minimised in expectation
by reporting the true distribution, so it rewards honest probabilities. Log loss and the
<NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">Brier score</NoteLink> are the standard examples.
<NoteLink to="bregman-divergences-and-scoring-rules" /> shows that every proper score comes from a concave entropy, and
<NoteLink to="scoring-rule-decompositions" /> splits its expectation into a calibration loss and a refinement loss
<Cite id="gneiting2007" />.

**Correct the probabilities after training.** <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration" /> fits a map from a model's scores
to calibrated probabilities on held-out data. <NoteLink to="platt-scaling">Platt scaling</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic regression</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="beta-calibration">beta
calibration</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="temperature-scaling">temperature scaling</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="dirichlet-calibration">Dirichlet calibration</NoteLink> differ in the shape of the map and the number of
parameters. Calibration has several definitions for many classes, set out in <NoteLink to="calibration-definitions" />.

**Measure calibration with care.** <NoteLink to="reliability-diagrams-and-consistency-bars" /> plots observed frequency
against predicted probability. <NoteLink to="estimating-calibration-error" /> shows that binned
<NoteLink to="calibration-error">calibration errors</NoteLink> are biased upwards by sampling noise and downwards by
binning. For regression, <NoteLink to="quantile-calibration" /> and <NoteLink to="distribution-calibration" /> ask
whether predicted quantiles or densities match the observed targets.

**Quantify uncertainty and act on it.** <NoteLink to="uncertainty-quantification" /> separates aleatoric from
<NoteLink to="aleatoric-and-epistemic-uncertainty">epistemic uncertainty</NoteLink>, builds
<NoteLink to="conformal-prediction">conformal prediction sets</NoteLink> with guaranteed coverage, and abstains when the
risk of a prediction is too high.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule" />, which every later subtopic uses. Cost-sensitive learning and ROC
analysis apply it to two-class decisions. Proper scoring rules come next and define what a good probability is. The
calibration subtopics, from maps to their evaluation and then to regression, follow from the calibration loss of the
decompositions. Uncertainty quantification draws on the reject option and closes the chapter.
