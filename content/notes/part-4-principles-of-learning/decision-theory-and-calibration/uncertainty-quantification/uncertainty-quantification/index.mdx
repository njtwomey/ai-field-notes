<Definition>
**Uncertainty quantification** attaches to each prediction a statement of how much it can be trusted, such as a
predictive distribution, a set of plausible labels, an interval or a decision to abstain. It separates uncertainty that
more data would remove from uncertainty inherent in the problem, and it turns either kind into guarantees or actions.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A point prediction gives no sign of when it is likely to be wrong. Medical triage, autonomous systems and automated
moderation need that sign, so that uncertain cases go to a person or a safer default. Calibrated probabilities are one
answer, covered in <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration" />. The notes here cover the other answers: decomposing
uncertainty by its source, prediction sets with coverage guarantees, and abstention rules.

## Main ideas

**Two sources of uncertainty.** <NoteLink to="aleatoric-and-epistemic-uncertainty" /> defines aleatoric uncertainty as
the randomness of $y$ given $\xvec$, which no amount of data removes, and epistemic uncertainty as ignorance about the
model, which data reduce <Cite id="hullermeier2021" />. Over an ensemble or a posterior, the entropy of the predictive
distribution splits into the expected entropy of each member plus the mutual information between $y$ and the model.
The second term measures disagreement between members and is the usual estimate of epistemic uncertainty.

**Coverage from exchangeability alone.** <NoteLink to="conformal-prediction" /> turns any model into prediction sets
$C(\xvec)$ with $P(y \in C(\xvec)) \ge 1 - \alpha$ in finite samples. It assumes only that calibration and test data
are exchangeable. The guarantee is marginal over test points, not conditional on each $\xvec$
<Cite id="angelopoulos2021" />.

**Abstaining to control risk.** <NoteLink to="selective-classification" /> pairs a classifier with a rule that decides
whether to predict. It trades coverage, the fraction of cases accepted, against selective risk, the error on accepted
cases. A confidence threshold set on held-out data can guarantee a target selective risk with high probability.

**Ambiguity and novelty in one rule.** A classifier can be unsure because a case lies between classes or because it
lies far from all training data. <NoteLink to="background-check" /> adds a background class to a $k$-class classifier,
using a relative density of the foreground data, so one decision rule rejects both ambiguous cases and outliers.

## How the notes fit together

Start with the aleatoric and epistemic decomposition, which names the quantities the other notes act on. Conformal
prediction and selective classification are independent of each other: the first outputs sets, the second abstains.
Background Check extends abstention to cases unlike the training data and comes last. Both abstention notes require
the <NoteLink to="reject-option" /> from decision theory.
