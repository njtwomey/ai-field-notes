<Definition>
**Principles of learning** are the ideas that apply to every learning method, whatever its model class. They answer six
questions: why a rule fitted to a finite sample predicts new data, how to choose among models, how to measure a model,
how to turn its predictions into decisions, what objective to train it on, and how to compute with a probabilistic model
once data arrive.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Part V describes learning paradigms and Part VI neural architectures. Each method in those parts has the same needs: an
objective to minimise, a way to set its complexity, a test-set score, and, when it outputs probabilities, a check that
they are trustworthy. Probabilistic methods also need an inference algorithm. This Part collects these shared
ingredients once, so that each method note can refer to them rather than repeat them. A reader who knows this Part can
place a new method by asking which loss it minimises, which inference it uses and which metric judges it
<Cite id="hastie2009" />.

## Main ideas

**Generalisation has a theory.** A model is trained to reduce error on its training sample, but it is judged on new
data. <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory" /> studies the gap between the two. <Gloss name="pac">Probably
approximately correct</Gloss> learning asks how many examples a finite class needs.
<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">Uniform convergence</NoteLink> extends the answer to infinite classes through
capacity measures such as the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink>. Margin, norm, stability and
<NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink> tie the gap to properties of the learned rule or the
algorithm rather than the class <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. The <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition" />
splits error into a part from a restricted model and a part from a finite sample. <NoteLink to="double-descent">Double
descent</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink> describe models that interpolate
their training data and still generalise.

**Complexity is chosen from data.** The theory explains why complexity must be controlled.
<NoteLink to="model-selection" /> gives the practical methods: <NoteLink to="cross-validation" /> to estimate test
error, <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information criteria</NoteLink> to penalise the training fit,
<NoteLink to="regularisation" /> to restrict a model continuously, and <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search" /> to search
many settings at once. Selection and assessment use separate data, because the selected model's validation score is
optimistic.

**Metrics must match the use.** <NoteLink to="evaluation-metrics" /> collects the scores used on held-out data, by task:
<NoteLink to="classification-metrics">confusion-matrix metrics</NoteLink> and curves for classifiers, error measures for
regression and forecasting, and metrics for rankings, clusterings, images, generated samples, text and audio. Each
metric rewards a particular behaviour. Accuracy rewards the majority class under imbalance, and <Gloss name="auroc" />
ignores whether scores are probabilities. A metric on a finite test set is an estimate and carries a confidence
interval.

**Predictions become decisions.** <NoteLink to="decision-theory-and-calibration" /> turns predicted probabilities into
actions that minimise expected loss, with the <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule" />, cost-sensitive thresholds and
<NoteLink to="reject-option">reject options</NoteLink>. The rule is only as good as the probabilities, so the topic also
covers <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">proper scoring rules</NoteLink>, which reward honest probabilities,
<NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">calibration maps</NoteLink> that correct them after training, and
<NoteLink to="uncertainty-quantification">uncertainty quantification</NoteLink> with
<NoteLink to="conformal-prediction">conformal prediction sets</NoteLink>.

**The loss chooses what is learned.** <NoteLink to="loss-functions" /> covers the training objectives by task.
<NoteLink to="regression-losses">Squared loss</NoteLink> estimates a mean, absolute loss a median and the
<NoteLink to="robust-and-distributional-regression-losses">pinball loss</NoteLink> a quantile.
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">Convex surrogates</NoteLink> replace the 0–1 loss and keep its minimiser. Ranking and
contrastive losses train orderings and embeddings, and <NoteLink to="approximate-softmax">approximate softmax</NoteLink>
methods make losses over millions of classes affordable.

**Probabilistic models need inference.** <NoteLink to="probabilistic-inference" /> computes posteriors, marginals and
evidence. <NoteLink to="graphical-models" /> describe the structure, <NoteLink to="exact-inference" /> exploits it when
the treewidth is small, and <Gloss name="mcmc">Markov chain Monte Carlo</Gloss>, <NoteLink to="variational-inference" />
and <NoteLink to="message-passing-approximations">message-passing approximations</NoteLink> handle the rest.
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">Probabilistic programming</NoteLink> automates the choice, and
<NoteLink to="model-based-machine-learning">model-based machine learning</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="skill-rating">skill rating</NoteLink> show whole models built and solved this way
<Cite id="murphy2022" />.

## How the notes fit together

The six chapters are largely independent, and each opens with its own overview. A reader new to the field can take
them in this order:

- <NoteLink to="loss-functions" /> first, since every later chapter assumes an objective.
- <NoteLink to="evaluation-metrics" /> and <NoteLink to="model-selection" /> next, which together say how a trained
  model is judged and tuned.
- <NoteLink to="decision-theory-and-calibration" />, which builds on the probabilistic metrics.
- <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory" />, which explains why held-out error is the right target and when the
  training error approximates it. Its notes are more mathematical and can be read later.
- <NoteLink to="probabilistic-inference" />, which is self-contained and needed mainly for the Bayesian and
  latent-variable models of Parts V and VI.

Within each chapter the overview lists a starting note, and the prev and next arrows follow the reading order.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The Part assumes the probability and statistics of Part I: <NoteLink to="expectation" />,
<NoteLink to="conditional-probability" />, <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem" /> and the common distributions. Learning theory
also uses the concentration inequalities of Part I, and the information-theoretic quantities of Part II, such as the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence" />, recur in losses, scoring rules and variational inference. The ideas here
are applied in Part V, which organises methods by learning paradigm, and in Part VI, which trains neural architectures
with these losses and evaluates them with these metrics. Part VIII returns to evaluation in deployment, with fairness,
robustness and monitoring.
