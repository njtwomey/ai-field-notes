<Definition>
A **pipeline** is the whole procedure that turns raw training data into a fitted predictor: every preprocessing step
whose parameters are estimated from data, followed by the model. **Cross-validating a pipeline** refits every step on
each training fold and applies the fitted steps, unchanged, to that fold's held-out rows. The estimate is then an
estimate of the error of the pipeline. A step fitted once on all the rows before splitting has seen every held-out row,
and the estimate is of a different procedure, one that had access to the test data <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="cross-validation">Cross-validation</NoteLink> imitates deployment: a model is built from some data and
asked about data it has never seen. The imitation holds only if nothing about the held-out rows reaches the model. A
preprocessing step is part of the model, whatever the code calls it. A scaler's mean, an imputer's fill value, an
encoder's table of category means and a feature selector's list of columns are parameters estimated from data, exactly
like regression coefficients. Fitting them on all the rows and then cross-validating only the final model is like
fitting the coefficients on all the rows and then scoring them on some of the same rows.

How much this matters depends on what the step learns. A step that uses the targets can copy information about each
held-out label into that row's features, and the estimate can be wildly optimistic. A step that uses only the inputs
learns about the distribution of the held-out inputs, which usually biases the estimate only slightly but not always
<Cite id="moscovich2022" />. Neither bias is needed: putting the step inside the folds costs one refit per fold.

## What counts as fitting

Any step with a parameter computed from the data is fitted. Grouped by what they read:

- **Steps that read the targets.** Target (mean) encoding of categories; supervised
  <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink> by correlation, a univariate test or a model's
  importances; supervised discretisation; outlier removal by residual; class reweighting by observed class frequencies.
  These leak the most.
- **Steps that change the rows.** Oversampling, undersampling and <NoteLink to="smote-and-variants">SMOTE</NoteLink>.
  Resampling before splitting puts copies of a minority example, or synthetic points interpolated from it, on both
  sides of the split. The held-out rows must stay as they are; resampling is applied to the training fold only
  <Cite id="santos2018" />.
- **Steps that read only the inputs.** Standardisation, imputation by mean or median, <Gloss name="pca">principal
  component analysis</Gloss>, vocabularies and document frequencies for text, the categories of a one-hot encoding.
  Their leak is a leak of the held-out input distribution.
- **Choices made by looking at results.** Hyperparameters, the number of components, the threshold of a classifier,
  <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> on a validation score and the choice among model families are
  fitted too. Tuning them on the same folds that report the error needs <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
  cross-validation</NoteLink>.

Steps that need no data, such as a fixed logarithm, a unit conversion or a hand-written rule, can sit outside.

The most damaging case, feature selection on all the rows, has its own note:
<NoteLink to="selection-inside-cross-validation">selection inside cross-validation</NoteLink>. Ambroise and McLachlan
showed on microarray data that selecting genes on all the samples and then cross-validating the classifier can drive the
estimated <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink> close to zero while the error of the
procedure is far higher <Cite id="ambroise2002" />.

### Why input-only steps are not always safe

An input-only step fitted on all $n$ rows makes the training rows of each fold look a little more like its held-out
rows than fresh data would. For a scaler fitted to many rows the effect is small: a held-out row $\xvec$ moves the
fitted mean by $(\xvec - \bar\xvec)/n$. The effect grows with the number of parameters the step estimates relative to
$n$. Moscovich and Rosset analysed three common input-only steps before regression: selecting features by their
variance, grouping rare categories, and rescaling. Each biases the cross-validation estimate, the bias can have either
sign, it can be large when samples are small and features many, and it can change which model is selected
<Cite id="moscovich2022" />.

## Worked example

Take $n = 100$ rows with one categorical input of 20 categories, 5 rows in each, and a target $y_i \iid
\Gauss(0, 1)$ that is independent of the category. No model can predict $y$: the smallest possible
<Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> is the noise variance, 1. The pipeline is **target encoding** followed by
least squares. Target encoding replaces each category $c$ by the mean $\bar y_c$ of the training targets in that
category <Cite id="micci2001" />.

**Encoding outside the folds.** Encode all 100 rows once. Row $i$ in category $c$ gets $z_i = \bar y_c$, an average of
5 labels that includes its own $y_i$.

1. The least-squares slope of $y$ on $z$ over the rows is exactly 1. Within category $c$ every $z_i$ equals $\bar y_c$,
   so $\sum_{i \in c} y_i(z_i - \bar z) = n_c\bar y_c(\bar y_c - \bar z) = \sum_{i \in c} z_i(z_i - \bar z)$, and
   summing over categories makes the numerator of the slope equal to its denominator. The intercept is 0 because $\bar y = \bar
   z$.
2. The fitted value of row $i$ is therefore $\bar y_c$, and its residual is $y_i - \bar y_c$. A deviation from a group
   mean that includes the point itself has expected square $(1 - 1/n_c)\sigma^2 = 0.8$.
3. Ten-fold cross-validation of the regression on these encoded rows refits the slope and intercept on 90 rows, but
   every held-out row still carries its own label inside $z_i$. The estimate is close to 0.8; over 200 simulated data
   sets its mean is 0.84.

The estimate says the pipeline beats the noise floor by 16%, which is impossible.

**The truth.** Fit the pipeline to all 100 rows and score a new row in category $c$. Its encoding $\bar y_c$ is an
average of 5 training labels that are independent of the new $y$, and the slope is 1, so the error is
$\var(y) + \var(\bar y_c) = 1 + 1/5 = 1.2$. The same 200 simulations give 1.20.

**Encoding inside the folds.** Each training fold fits its own encoder on 90 rows, about 4.5 per category, and the
held-out rows are encoded with those training means. The held-out labels no longer enter their own features. The
estimate averages 1.30 over the 200 data sets. It is a little above the truth because each fold's pipeline sees fewer
rows per category, but it reports correctly that the pipeline does worse than predicting the mean.

**A better pipeline.** The pipeline itself is poor: on the training rows the in-sample encoding still contains each
row's own label, so the regression learns to trust the encoding (slope 1). Encoding the training rows out of fold, as
<NoteLink to="mean-target-encoding">mean target encoding</NoteLink> recommends, makes the learned slope close to 0
(mean $-0.13$ in the simulations), and the true error falls to 1.03. Cross-validating that pipeline gives 1.035. Honest
cross-validation is what reveals that the first pipeline needs fixing.

## Assumptions

The estimate of a correctly cross-validated pipeline inherits the assumptions of cross-validation itself: rows
exchangeable between folds, and folds that reproduce how new data will differ from the training data. Rows that cluster
by group, location or time need folds that respect the clustering; see
<NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="spatial-and-blocked-cross-validation">spatial and blocked cross-validation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Preprocessing in a notebook cell before the split.** The most common form. Bundle every fitted step and the model
  into one pipeline object and hand only that object to the cross-validation routine, as scikit-learn's `Pipeline` and
  the engine's `pipeline` do; see <NoteLink to="preprocessing-pipelines">preprocessing pipelines</NoteLink>.
- **Encoding or selecting on the train-plus-validation set, then tuning.** The leak reaches the tuning as well as the
  estimate, so the chosen hyperparameters favour whatever the leak rewards.
- **Resampling the whole data set.** Oversampling or SMOTE before the split makes copies of held-out minority examples,
  or points interpolated from them, appear in training, and the estimate is over-optimistic <Cite id="santos2018" />.
- **Deduplication and filtering after the split decision.** Removing near-duplicates or outliers with statistics
  computed over all rows is a fitted step. Deduplicate before splitting, using rules that do not look at the targets,
  and fit any data-driven filter inside the folds.
- **Checking only the score.** A leak shows as a score that is too good, but a moderate leak looks like a moderate
  improvement. Controls with shuffled labels, which should score at chance, catch it more reliably; see
  <NoteLink to="detecting-leakage">detecting leakage</NoteLink> <Cite id="kaufman2012" />.
