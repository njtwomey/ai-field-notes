<Definition>
Let $\Dcal$ be a training sample of $n$ examples drawn from a distribution $P$, $\hat f_\Dcal$ the model a learning
procedure fits to it, and $\ell$ a loss. The **conditional test error** is the expected loss of that one model on a new
example, $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = \expect\brack{\ell(y, \hat f_\Dcal(\xvec)) \mid \Dcal}$ with $(\xvec, y) \sim P$
independent of $\Dcal$. The **expected test error** averages it over training samples,
$\operatorname{Err} = \expect_\Dcal\brack{\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal}$. The
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> estimate $\operatorname{CV}$ is closer, in
<Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss>, to $\operatorname{Err}$ than to $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$, and its
correlation with $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ is weak <Cite id="hastie2009" /> <Cite id="bates2024" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A practitioner who cross-validates and then fits the final model to all the data wants to know how that final model
will do. That is $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$: a property of one fitted model. Cross-validation never scores that model.
It scores $K$ other models, each fitted to a different subset of $\Dcal$, on points those models did not see. What it
measures is how the *procedure* does when handed a training set of about $n(K - 1)/K$ examples from $P$. The fold
models are a small sample of the models the procedure would produce on other training sets, so their average error
estimates the average over training sets, $\operatorname{Err}$.

The two targets differ because $\Dcal$ is random. Some training samples are lucky and give a model better than
average; others are unlucky. A good estimator of $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ would detect the luck. Cross-validation
cannot, because the information that separates a lucky fitted model from an unlucky one is not in the held-out
residuals. For least squares that statement is exact, and the proof is short.

## Conditional and expected error

Three targets sit in a row, each an average of the one before <Cite id="bates2024" />:

- $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$, the error of the model fitted to this sample, with the training inputs
  $\Xmat = (\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_n)$ and outputs $\yvec$ both fixed;
- $\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat = \expect\brack{\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal \mid \Xmat}$, averaged over the outputs that
  could have come with these inputs;
- $\operatorname{Err} = \expect\brack{\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat}$, averaged over inputs as well.

For a deployed model, $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ matters. For a researcher
<NoteLink to="comparing-learning-algorithms">comparing learning algorithms</NoteLink> on a problem, $\operatorname{Err}$
matters. The question is which one a cross-validation estimate tracks.

### Least squares: cross-validation is independent of the conditional error

Assume the linear model $y_i = \xvec_i^\top\thetavec + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \iid \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$
independent of the inputs, a least-squares fit $\hat\thetavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$ with
$\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ of full column rank, and squared-error loss. Let new inputs have mean $\boldsymbol{0}$
and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$.

**Step 1: the conditional error depends on the data only through $\hat\thetavec$.** A new example has
$y - \xvec^\top\hat\thetavec = \varepsilon + \xvec^\top(\thetavec - \hat\thetavec)$. The noise is independent of
$\xvec$ and has mean 0, so the cross term vanishes, and

$$
\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = \sigma^2 + (\hat\thetavec - \thetavec)^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec).
$$

**Step 2: the cross-validation estimate depends on the data only through the residuals.** Replace $\yvec$ by
$\yvec + \Xmat\kappavec$ for any $\kappavec \in \reals^p$. Least squares is equivariant: every fold's fit moves from
$\hat\thetavec^{-k}$ to $\hat\thetavec^{-k} + \kappavec$, so each held-out residual
$y_i + \xvec_i^\top\kappavec - \xvec_i^\top(\hat\thetavec^{-k} + \kappavec) = y_i - \xvec_i^\top\hat\thetavec^{-k}$ is
unchanged, and so is $\operatorname{CV}$. Take $\kappavec = -\hat\thetavec$. Then $\yvec + \Xmat\kappavec$ is the
residual vector $\evec = (\eye - \Hmat)\yvec$ of the full fit, with hat matrix $\Hmat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}
\Xmat^\top$. So $\operatorname{CV}$ computed from $\yvec$ equals $\operatorname{CV}$ computed from $\evec$: it is a
function of $\evec$ and $\Xmat$ alone.

**Step 3: given the inputs, $\hat\thetavec$ and $\evec$ are independent.** Both are linear in $\yvec$, which is
Gaussian given $\Xmat$, so they are jointly Gaussian. Their covariance is

$$
\cov(\hat\thetavec, \evec \mid \Xmat) = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top(\sigma^2\eye)(\eye - \Hmat)^\top
= \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}(\Xmat^\top - \Xmat^\top\Hmat) = \boldsymbol{0},
$$

because $\Xmat^\top\Hmat = \Xmat^\top$. Jointly Gaussian variables with zero covariance are independent.

Steps 1 to 3 give the result: $\operatorname{CV}$ and $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ are independent given $\Xmat$
<Cite id="bates2024" />. Two data sets with the same inputs and different noise would have different conditional
errors, and the cross-validation estimate of one is as informative about the other's error as about its own.

### What the independence costs

Write $\operatorname{CV} - \operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = (\operatorname{CV} - \operatorname{Err}_\Xmat) -
(\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal - \operatorname{Err}_\Xmat)$ and square. Given $\Xmat$, the two brackets are independent and
the second has mean 0, so the cross term vanishes:

$$
\expect\brack{(\operatorname{CV} - \operatorname{Err}_\Dcal)^2} = \expect\brack{(\operatorname{CV} -
\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat)^2} + \expect\brack{\var(\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal \mid \Xmat)}.
$$

As an estimate of the conditional error, cross-validation pays the full variance of $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ on top
of its error as an estimate of $\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat$. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani also show that when $n$ and
$p$ grow together, $\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat$ varies around $\operatorname{Err}$ with variance of order $1/n^2$, while
$\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ varies with variance of order $1/n$ <Cite id="bates2024" />. So $\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat
\approx \operatorname{Err}$, and the correlation between $\operatorname{CV}$ and $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ tends to 0.

The proof used only step 2's invariance, so it holds for every estimator that is a function of the least-squares
residuals: hold-out validation, $K$-fold and <Gloss name="loo">leave-one-out cross-validation</Gloss>, the
<NoteLink to="bootstrap-estimates-of-prediction-error">bootstrap estimators</NoteLink> and Mallows' $C_p$
<Cite id="bates2024" />.

### Beyond least squares

For other learners there is no exact theorem, but simulations show the same pattern. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman
found that across training sets the cross-validation estimate and the conditional error of classifiers are only weakly
correlated, while the estimate tracks the expected error well <Cite id="hastie2009" />. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani
found small positive correlations for <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, larger than for
least squares but still weak <Cite id="bates2024" />. The figure has a nearest-neighbour learner for comparison.

## Worked example

Take $p = 20$ standard-normal inputs, $y = x_1 + \dots + x_5 + \varepsilon$ with $\sigma^2 = 1$, least squares without
an intercept, $n = 100$ and 10-fold cross-validation.

1. **The expected error.** For Gaussian inputs, $\operatorname{Err} = \sigma^2\paren{1 + p/(n - p - 1)}$ (derived
   below). With $n = 100$ and $p = 20$ that is $1 + 20/79 = 1.253$.
2. **What the folds estimate.** Each fold model is fitted to 90 rows, so $\operatorname{CV}$ estimates the expected
   error at $n = 90$: $1 + 20/69 = 1.290$. Its bias as an estimate of $\operatorname{Err}$ is $+0.037$.
3. **A simulation of 1000 training sets** (the figure's default problem with the inputs redrawn each time) gives a mean
   conditional error of 1.26 with standard deviation 0.09 across training sets, and a mean $\operatorname{CV}$ of 1.29
   with standard deviation 0.21. The correlation between $\operatorname{CV}$ and $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ is 0.01.
4. **Which target is closer.** The root mean squared difference between $\operatorname{CV}$ and
   $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ is 0.231. Between $\operatorname{CV}$ and $\operatorname{Err} = 1.253$ it is 0.214. The
   difference of their squares, $0.231^2 - 0.214^2 = 0.0076$, is close to the variance of $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$,
   $0.09^2 \approx 0.008$, as the decomposition above predicts.

With the inputs held fixed, a data set whose fitted model happens to be good, with $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = 1.15$,
gets the same distribution of cross-validation estimates as one whose model is poor, with
$\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = 1.40$.

<Derivation title="The expected error of least squares with Gaussian inputs">
By step 1, $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = \sigma^2 + (\hat\thetavec - \thetavec)^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\hat\thetavec -
\thetavec)$. Given $\Xmat$, $\hat\thetavec - \thetavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ has
mean $\boldsymbol{0}$ and covariance $\sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$. For a random vector $\uvec$ with mean
$\boldsymbol{0}$ and covariance $\Cmat$,
$\expect[\uvec^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\uvec] = \trace(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\Cmat)$. So
$\operatorname{Err}_\Xmat = \sigma^2\paren{1 + \trace\paren{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}}}$.

When the rows of $\Xmat$ are independent $\Gauss(\boldsymbol{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ has a
<NoteLink to="wishart-distribution">Wishart distribution</NoteLink> with $n$ degrees of freedom and scale
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, and the mean of its inverse is $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}/(n - p - 1)$ for $n > p + 1$. Taking the
expectation over $\Xmat$,
$\operatorname{Err} = \sigma^2\paren{1 + \trace(\eye_p)/(n - p - 1)} = \sigma^2\paren{1 + p/(n - p - 1)}$
<Cite id="bates2024" />.
</Derivation>

## Assumptions

The exact independence needs the homoskedastic Gaussian linear model, a least-squares fit and squared-error loss. The
asymptotic statements need $n$ and $p$ to grow in proportion with $n > p$. The broader claim, that cross-validation
tracks $\operatorname{Err}$ and not $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$, rests on simulations for other learners and losses.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the estimate as a property of the deployed model.** A cross-validation estimate says how the procedure
  does on training sets like this one. For the error of the model that will be deployed, score that model on a test
  set it has never seen. A test set is an unbiased estimate of $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ for the model it scores (see
  <NoteLink to="hold-out-validation">hold-out validation</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>).
- **Intervals for the wrong target.** An interval built from the spread of the fold errors is too narrow for either
  target, and narrower still for $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$; see
  <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>.
- **The size of the fold models.** $\operatorname{CV}$ estimates the expected error at $n(K - 1)/K$ rows, not at $n$.
  When the learning curve is steep, the gap is visible, as in step 2 of the worked example.
- **Selection makes it worse.** The minimum of cross-validation estimates over many candidates is biased low for the
  chosen candidate; see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>.
