<Definition>
**Repeated $K$-fold cross-validation** draws $R$ independent random partitions of the data into $K$ folds, computes the
<NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold estimate</NoteLink> $\operatorname{CV}_K^{(r)}$ for each, and reports
their average $\overline{\operatorname{CV}}_R = \frac1R\sum_{r=1}^R \operatorname{CV}_K^{(r)}$. **Monte Carlo
cross-validation** draws $B$ independent random splits, each holding out $n_v$ points and training on the rest, and
averages the $B$ hold-out estimates <Cite id="xu2001" />; it is also called repeated learning–testing, shuffle-split or
repeated random subsampling <Cite id="burman1989" />. **Leave-$p$-out** cross-validation averages over all
$\binom{n}{p}$ ways of holding out $p$ points.
</Definition>

## Problem

A $K$-fold estimate depends on which points land in which fold. Run it twice with different random partitions and the
two numbers differ, although the data and the model are the same. When the difference between two candidates is
smaller than this partition noise, one run cannot tell them apart.

The estimate has two sources of randomness. The **data** $\Dcal$ are one random sample, and the **partition** is a
second random draw made by the analyst. Repetition averages over the second. It cannot touch the first.

## Mechanism

- **Repeated $K$-fold.** Shuffle with a new seed, cut into $K$ folds, cross-validate, and repeat $R$ times. Every
  point is tested exactly $R$ times, once per repeat. Common settings are 10 repeats of 10-fold or 5 repeats of
  5-fold. Scikit-learn's `RepeatedKFold` and `RepeatedStratifiedKFold` implement it.
- **Monte Carlo.** For $b = 1, \dots, B$, draw a random subset of $n_v$ points, fit on the other $n - n_v$, and score
  on the subset. The training size and the number of splits are chosen independently, unlike in $K$-fold where
  $n_v = n/K$ fixes both. A point is tested a random number of times; with $n_v/n = 0.1$ and $B = 10$ splits, a point is
  never tested with probability $0.9^{10} = 0.35$, and with $B = 50$ with probability $0.005$. Scikit-learn's
  `ShuffleSplit` implements it.
- **Leave-$p$-out.** Every subset of $p$ points is held out once. For $p = 1$ it is
  <NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">leave-one-out</NoteLink>. The number of splits grows fast:
  $\binom{20}{2} = 190$, but $\binom{100}{10} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{13}$. Exhaustive leave-$p$-out is feasible only for
  tiny $n$ or for estimators with closed forms <Cite id="arlot2010" />, and Monte Carlo cross-validation is the
  practical approximation of it.

Each scheme is unbiased for the error at its training size: $\operatorname{Err}(n(K-1)/K)$ for repeated $K$-fold and
$\operatorname{Err}(n - n_v)$ for Monte Carlo and leave-$n_v$-out, by the argument of $K$-fold cross-validation applied
to each split. Repetition changes the variance, not the expectation.

## What repetition buys

Condition on the data $\Dcal$. The random partition is the only remaining randomness, and the $R$ repeats are
independent draws of it. Write $\operatorname{CV}_\infty = \expect[\operatorname{CV}_K \mid \Dcal]$ for the average
over all partitions of these data, the "complete" $K$-fold estimate <Cite id="kohavi1995" />, and
$\sigma^2_\text{part} = \var(\operatorname{CV}_K \mid \Dcal)$ for the variance due to the partition. An average of $R$
independent draws has

$$
\expect\brack{\overline{\operatorname{CV}}_R \mid \Dcal} = \operatorname{CV}_\infty,
\qquad
\var\paren{\overline{\operatorname{CV}}_R \mid \Dcal} = \frac{\sigma^2_\text{part}}{R}.
$$

Now let the data vary. The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total variance</NoteLink> splits the variance
of the estimate into the variance of its conditional mean and the mean of its conditional variance:

$$
\var\paren{\overline{\operatorname{CV}}_R} = \var_\Dcal\paren{\operatorname{CV}_\infty} +
\frac{\expect_\Dcal\brack{\sigma^2_\text{part}}}{R}.
$$

The first term is the variance due to the data. It does not depend on $R$ and is the floor that repetition approaches.
The second term is the partition noise, and it falls as $1/R$. The same argument shows that leave-$p$-out has the
smallest variance among the Monte Carlo schemes that hold out $p$ points: Monte Carlo cross-validation equals
leave-$p$-out plus partition noise with mean zero given the data, and adding independent noise of mean zero can only
add variance.

Three consequences follow.

1. **Diminishing returns.** If the partition noise is a small part of the total, a single run is nearly as good as
   many. If it is large, as for small $n$ and unstable learners, a few repeats remove most of it.
2. **No approach to the truth for these data.** As $R \to \infty$ the estimate converges to $\operatorname{CV}_\infty$,
   a fixed function of the data. It does not converge to the true error $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ of the model fitted
   to them. Under many practical circumstances, repeating cross-validation to estimate the accuracy of the model at hand
   is therefore of little use, and <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink> computed from the
   repeats mislead <Cite id="vanwinckelen2012" /> (see <NoteLink to="what-cross-validation-estimates">what
   cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>).
3. **The spread over repeats is not the uncertainty.** The standard deviation of the $R$ repeated estimates measures
   $\sigma_\text{part}$, the partition noise. It says nothing about the variance due to the data, which is usually
   larger. Treating the $RK$ fold scores as independent and dividing their standard deviation by $\sqrt{RK}$ gives a
   <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> that shrinks to zero as $R$ grows, a false precision (see
   <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>)
   <Cite id="bengio2004" />.

Repetition earns its cost when comparing candidates. Two candidates scored on the same repeated partitions have a
difference whose partition noise falls as $1/R$, so small but real differences become visible. A test of that
difference must still account for the overlap of the training sets (see
<NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled t-test</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="five-by-two-cross-validation-test">5×2 cross-validation test</NoteLink>).

## Leave-$p$-out and the training fraction

Monte Carlo cross-validation separates two choices that $K$-fold ties together: how many points to hold out and how many
splits to average. Holding out more points makes each fold model worse and the estimate more pessimistic, but it changes
what the estimate is good for. For choosing among <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> models,
leave-one-out, like <Gloss name="aic" /> and $C_p$, does not select the true model with probability tending to one.
Leave-$n_v$-out does, when the held-out fraction $n_v/n$ tends to one as $n$ grows <Cite id="shao1993" />. Monte Carlo
cross-validation with a large held-out fraction is the computable version. Zhang related the delete-$d$ criteria to the
final prediction error criterion and studied their $r$-fold and repeated learning–testing approximations
<Cite id="zhang1993" />. For estimating the error of the chosen model, as opposed to choosing it, a small held-out
fraction remains the right choice (see <NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">consistency and
efficiency of selection criteria</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

Take the figure's default setting, $n = 60$ points from two Gaussian classes and a 5-nearest-neighbour classifier, with
the dataset control at 2. Twenty independent analysts each run 10-fold cross-validation on these data.

1. **One run each.** Their 20 estimates of the <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink>
   average $0.288$ with standard deviation $0.025$ between analysts. Two analysts can differ by five points or more on
   identical data.
2. **Fifty repeats each.** Each analyst averages 50 partitions, 500 fits. Their 20 estimates now have standard
   deviation $0.003$, close to $0.025/\sqrt{50} = 0.0035$, and average $0.287$.
3. **The truth.** The model fitted to all 60 points has true error $0.206$ on 4,000 fresh points. All 20 analysts
   overestimate it by eight points, and repetition has made them agree on the overestimate.

On the first five datasets of the same size the gap between the converged value and the true error ranges from
$-0.09$ to $+0.08$, while the partition standard deviation stays near $0.02$. The error due to the data is several
times the partition noise, so 50 repeats reduce the total error of the estimate only a little.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting the spread over repeats as a confidence interval.** It is the partition noise only (consequence 3).
- **Testing on the pooled fold scores.** A <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> on $RK$ correlated
  scores rejects far too often; use a corrected test.
- **Monte Carlo with few splits.** With $B$ small, some points are never tested and others several times, which adds
  variance compared with $K$-fold of the same cost.
- **Repeating instead of collecting data.** When the data variance dominates, repetition improves the estimate's
  reproducibility, not its accuracy.
- **Different seeds for different candidates.** The variance reduction for
  comparisons comes from scoring every candidate on the same partitions.
