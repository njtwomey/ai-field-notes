<Definition>
Draw $B$ <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> samples $\Dcal^{*1}, \dots, \Dcal^{*B}$, each of $n$ examples
drawn with replacement from the training sample $\Dcal$, and fit a model $\hat f^{*b}$ to each. Let $C^{-i}$ be the set
of samples that do not contain example $i$. The **leave-one-out bootstrap** estimate of prediction error is

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\abs{C^{-i}}}\sum_{b \in C^{-i}} \ell\paren{y_i, \hat f^{*b}(\xvec_i)}.
$$

The **.632 estimator** is $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(.632)} = 0.368\,\overline{\operatorname{err}} + 0.632\,
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$, where $\overline{\operatorname{err}}$ is the training error of the model fitted to
all of $\Dcal$ <Cite id="efron1983" />. The **.632+ estimator** raises the weight on
$\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$ as the model overfits more <Cite id="efron1997" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="cross-validation">Cross-validation</NoteLink> estimates prediction error by holding out each point once.
Its estimate is nearly unbiased but variable: with $K$ folds it averages over one random partition, and leave-one-out
averages over models that are almost identical. The bootstrap averages over many overlapping training sets of size $n$,
which smooths the estimate. Efron introduced bootstrap estimators of prediction error as lower-variance alternatives to
cross-validation <Cite id="efron1983" />.

The direct bootstrap estimate fails. Fit $\hat f^{*b}$ to each resample and score it on the original sample:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{boot} = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell\paren{y_i, \hat f^{*b}(\xvec_i)}.
$$

The resample $\Dcal^{*b}$ plays the training set and $\Dcal$ plays the test set, but the two overlap. Most of the points
scored were in the fit, and $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{boot}$ is optimistic for the same reason the training
error is (see <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>). The estimators
below keep the bootstrap's averaging and remove the overlap. All three answer the question "how well will this learning
procedure predict new data?", the *estimate* question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>.

## Mechanism

### How much of the sample a resample contains

A bootstrap sample makes $n$ independent draws, each picking example $i$ with probability $1/n$. Example $i$ is missed
by one draw with probability $1 - 1/n$, so it is missed by all $n$ with probability

$$
\Pr\paren{i \notin \Dcal^{*b}} = \paren{1 - \frac1n}^n \;\longrightarrow\; e^{-1} \approx 0.368
\quad (n \to \infty).
$$

The limit follows from $\log(1 - 1/n)^n = n\log(1 - 1/n) = -1 - \frac{1}{2n} - \dots \to -1$. At $n = 100$ the
probability is already $0.366$. A resample therefore contains about $63.2\%$ of the distinct examples, some of them
several times, and leaves out the other $36.8\%$. Each example is left out of about $0.368B$ of the $B$ resamples.

### The leave-one-out bootstrap

$\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$ scores each example only on the models that never saw it, the members of
$C^{-i}$. It is cross-validation with random, overlapping training sets. Each model is trained on about $0.632n$
distinct examples. If the <NoteLink to="learning-curves-and-sample-size">learning curve</NoteLink> is still falling at
$n$, a model trained on fewer distinct examples predicts worse, and $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$ overestimates
the error of the model fitted to all $n$. Its bias is roughly that of 2-fold cross-validation, whose models see $n/2$
examples <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

### The .632 estimator

The training error $\overline{\operatorname{err}}$ is biased low and $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$ is biased
high. The .632 estimator takes a fixed mixture of the two,

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(.632)} = 0.368\,\overline{\operatorname{err}} + 0.632\,\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}.
$$

The weights come from Efron's analysis of the bias of the leave-one-out bootstrap <Cite id="efron1983" />. Their
effect is to pull $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$ down towards the training error, by the share of examples that a
resample leaves out <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

The fixed weights assume that the training error is a sensible anchor. For a model that fits the training data
perfectly, $\overline{\operatorname{err}} = 0$ whatever its true error, and the .632 estimator is $0.632$ times the
leave-one-out bootstrap. The worked example below shows how badly that can fail.

### The .632+ estimator

Efron and Tibshirani scale the weight by how much the model overfits <Cite id="efron1997" />. Two quantities measure
that.

**The no-information error rate** $\gamma$ is the error the model would have if inputs and outputs were independent. It
is estimated by scoring the full-data model on every pairing of an output with an input, matched or not:

$$
\hat\gamma = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n \ell\paren{y_i, \hat f(\xvec_j)}.
$$

For a two-class problem under <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink>, let $p_1$ be the fraction of examples
in class 1 and $q_1$ the fraction of inputs that the model assigns to class 1. A random pairing is wrong when it pairs a
class-1 output with a class-0 prediction or the reverse, so $\hat\gamma = p_1(1 - q_1) + (1 - p_1)q_1$.

**The relative overfitting rate** compares the excess of the leave-one-out bootstrap over the training error with
the largest excess possible:

$$
\hat R = \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)} - \overline{\operatorname{err}}}{\hat\gamma -
\overline{\operatorname{err}}}.
$$

$\hat R = 0$ when the model does as well on held-out points as on training points, and $\hat R = 1$ when its held-out
error is as bad as no information. Values outside $[0, 1]$ are clipped to it, and $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$
is capped at $\hat\gamma$ <Cite id="efron1997" />.

The .632+ estimator moves the weight from 0.632 towards 1 as $\hat R$ grows:

$$
\hat w = \frac{0.632}{1 - 0.368\hat R}, \qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(.632+)} = (1 - \hat w)\,\overline{\operatorname{err}} + \hat w\,
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}.
$$

At $\hat R = 0$, $\hat w = 0.632$ and the estimator is the .632 estimator. At $\hat R = 1$, $\hat w = 0.632/0.632 = 1$
and the estimator is the leave-one-out bootstrap, which ignores the training error entirely. Between the two, the
weight on the training error falls as the evidence of overfitting grows.

### Out-of-bag error

A <NoteLink to="bagging">bagged</NoteLink> ensemble, such as a <NoteLink to="random-forest">random forest</NoteLink>,
already holds the $B$ bootstrap fits. Averaging, for each example $i$, the predictions of the models in $C^{-i}$ gives
an out-of-bag prediction for $i$. Its average loss is the **out-of-bag error** <Cite id="breiman1996" />
<Cite id="breiman2001" />. It differs from $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$ in one step: the leave-one-out bootstrap
averages the losses of the single models, and the out-of-bag error takes the loss of their averaged prediction. It
therefore estimates the error of an ensemble of about $0.368B$ models. Once $B$ is large, that is close to the error of
the full ensemble, and the estimate costs no fits beyond the ensemble itself.

## Worked example

Two classes of equal size, labels independent of the inputs, and a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier. Every estimator
should report the true error rate, 0.5.

1. **Training error.** Each training point is its own nearest neighbour, so $\overline{\operatorname{err}} = 0$.
2. **Direct bootstrap.** A point in the resample (probability 0.632) is its own neighbour and is classified correctly.
   A point outside it (probability 0.368) is classified by an unrelated neighbour and is wrong half the time. So
   $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{boot} \approx 0.368 \times 0.5 = 0.184$.
3. **Leave-one-out bootstrap.** Only resamples without point $i$ score it, so every prediction is a coin toss, and
   $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)} \approx 0.5$.
4. **.632 estimator.** $0.368 \times 0 + 0.632 \times 0.5 = 0.316$, too low by 0.184.
5. **.632+ estimator.** Balanced classes and balanced predictions give $\hat\gamma = 0.5 \times 0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.5 =
   0.5$. Then $\hat R = (0.5 - 0)/(0.5 - 0) = 1$, $\hat w = 1$, and $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(.632+)} = 0.5$.

These are expectations. The figure below draws one such data set, $n = 50$ points in two dimensions with $B = 200$
resamples (its defaults, seed 1). Its training error is 0 and $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)} = 0.486$. The .632
estimate is $0.632 \times 0.486 = 0.307$. Since $\hat\gamma = 0.5$, the relative overfitting rate is
$\hat R = 0.486/0.5 = 0.971$, the weight is $\hat w = 0.632/(1 - 0.368 \times 0.971) = 0.984$, and the .632+ estimate
is $0.984 \times 0.486 = 0.478$. The true error of the fitted classifier, measured on 4000 fresh points, is 0.514, and
10-fold cross-validation gives 0.440.

A model that overfits less shows the correction working in between. In the same figure, set nine neighbours and a
class separation of 1.5. The training error is $\overline{\operatorname{err}} = 0.22$, the leave-one-out bootstrap is
$\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)} = 0.361$, and $\hat\gamma = 0.5$.

1. **.632 estimate.** $0.368 \times 0.22 + 0.632 \times 0.361 = 0.081 + 0.228 = 0.309$.
2. **Relative overfitting rate.** $\hat R = (0.361 - 0.22)/(0.5 - 0.22) = 0.141/0.28 = 0.504$.
3. **Weight.** $\hat w = 0.632/(1 - 0.368 \times 0.504) = 0.632/0.815 = 0.776$.
4. **.632+ estimate.** $(1 - 0.776) \times 0.22 + 0.776 \times 0.361 = 0.049 + 0.280 = 0.329$.

The true error is 0.254. On this data set every bootstrap estimate is pessimistic: the leave-one-out bootstrap by
0.107, the .632+ estimate by 0.075 and the .632 estimate by 0.055. 10-fold cross-validation gives 0.260.

## Cost

Each bootstrap estimator fits $B$ models. Every example needs enough resamples that omit it: with $B = 50$, about 18
resamples omit a given example, and the chance that none does is $0.632^{50} \approx 10^{-10}$. The no-information rate
needs the $n^2$ predictions of one model, which for 0–1 loss reduce to the class fractions above. The out-of-bag error
of a bagged ensemble is free.

## Trade-offs

- **Bias against variance.** In Efron and Tibshirani's 24 simulation experiments, mostly classification under 0–1 loss,
  the .632+ estimator had lower <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> than cross-validation
  <Cite id="efron1997" />. Kohavi's experiments on real data sets found that the .632 bootstrap has low variance but
  very large bias on some problems, and recommended stratified 10-fold cross-validation for choosing between classifiers
  <Cite id="kohavi1995" />.
- **The same estimand as cross-validation.** Like cross-validation, the bootstrap estimators average over training sets.
  They estimate the expected error of the procedure more than the error of the model fitted to the data at hand
  <Cite id="bates2024" />; see <NoteLink to="what-cross-validation-estimates">what cross-validation
  estimates</NoteLink>.
- **Duplicated rows.** A resample contains repeated examples, which some learners treat differently from distinct
  ones: a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier and a tree grown to purity both fit a duplicated example twice as firmly.

## Pitfalls

- **Scoring the resample's models on the full sample.** That is $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{boot}$, optimistic
  by construction.
- **The .632 estimator with an interpolating model.** When the training error is near 0, the .632 estimate is about
  $0.632\,\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}^{(1)}$, far too low. Use .632+ or cross-validation.
- **Resampling dependent rows.** Rows from the same patient, site or period must be resampled together, as whole
  groups or blocks. Otherwise copies of one group land on both sides and the out-of-bag points are not new (see
  <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Preprocessing outside the resampling.** Every step that learns from data is refitted on each resample, as in
  <NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>.
- **Out-of-bag error after tuning on it.** Choosing a forest's hyperparameters by the out-of-bag error and reporting
  the same error is selection on the estimate; see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>.
