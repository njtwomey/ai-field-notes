<Definition>
Split the indices $1, \dots, n$ into $K$ disjoint **folds** of nearly equal size, and let $\kappa(i)$ be the fold that
contains point $i$. Let $\hat f^{-k}$ be the model fitted to all the data except fold $k$. The **$K$-fold
cross-validation** estimate of prediction error is

$$
\operatorname{CV}_K = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell\paren{y_i, \hat f^{-\kappa(i)}(\xvec_i)}.
$$

Each point is predicted exactly once, by a model that did not see it. It is the most common form of
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> <Cite id="stone1974" />
<Cite id="geisser1975" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="hold-out-validation">Hold-out validation</NoteLink> sets part of the data aside. Every held-out point is
lost to fitting, and the estimate rests on one random split of one validation set. With small data both costs are
large: a validation set big enough for a precise estimate leaves too little to fit.

$K$-fold cross-validation rotates the validation role. Each of the $K$ folds is held out once, so every point is used
for validation, and each fit still sees most of the data. Grouping the sum by folds shows the estimate as an average of
$K$ hold-out estimates,

$$
\operatorname{CV}_K = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{n_k}{n}\,\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_k,
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i : \kappa(i) = k}\ell\paren{y_i, \hat f^{-k}(\xvec_i)},
$$

where $n_k$ is the size of fold $k$. The method answers the chapter's first question, **estimate**, for one model, and
the second, **select**, when the estimate is computed for each candidate and minimised. It does not answer the third:
the smallest of several cross-validation scores is optimistic about the model that achieved it (see
<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>).

## Mechanism

1. **Shuffle** the rows and cut them into $K$ blocks. Without the shuffle, data sorted by class, time or source give
   folds that differ systematically. Classification uses <NoteLink to="stratified-cross-validation">stratified
   folds</NoteLink>; records that share an entity use <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped folds</NoteLink>.
2. **Fit** the whole pipeline on the $K - 1$ training folds: every data-dependent step, from scaling to
   <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink>, belongs inside the loop (see
   <NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>).
3. **Predict** the held-out fold and record each point's loss.
4. **Average** the $n$ losses. The per-fold averages $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_k$ are kept as well, for a rough
   spread.
5. **Select**, if there are candidates: compute $\operatorname{CV}_K$ for each on the same folds, so that differences
   reflect the candidates and not the partition, and keep the smallest.
6. **Refit** the chosen candidate on all $n$ points. The $K$ fold models are discarded.

With $n$ not divisible by $K$, the first $n \bmod K$ folds take one extra point, as in scikit-learn's `KFold`.

## What $K$-fold cross-validation estimates

Write $\operatorname{Err}(m)$ for the expected prediction error of the learner trained on $m$ random points: the true
error $\operatorname{Err}_{\Dcal_m}$ of the fitted model, averaged over training sets $\Dcal_m$ of size $m$. Take
equal folds of size $n/K$, so every fold model is trained on $m = n(K - 1)/K$ points.

Consider one term of the sum, $\ell(y_i, \hat f^{-\kappa(i)}(\xvec_i))$. The model $\hat f^{-\kappa(i)}$ is fitted to
the $m$ points outside fold $\kappa(i)$. The point $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ is not among them, and it is independent of them
because the data are independent draws. Conditional on the training points, the term is therefore the loss of a fixed
model at a fresh point, whose expectation is $\operatorname{Err}_{\Dcal_m}$ for that training set. Averaging over
training sets gives $\operatorname{Err}(m)$. Every term has the same expectation, so

$$
\expect\brack{\operatorname{CV}_K} = \operatorname{Err}\paren{\tfrac{K-1}{K}\,n}.
$$

$K$-fold cross-validation is unbiased for the expected error at training size $n(K - 1)/K$, not at $n$. The model put
to use is trained on all $n$ points. While the learning curve $\operatorname{Err}(m)$ is still falling at $n$, the
estimate is **pessimistic** for it, by $\operatorname{Err}(n(K-1)/K) - \operatorname{Err}(n)$ <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
The estimate also targets the average over training sets, not the error of the one model fitted to the data at hand;
<NoteLink to="what-cross-validation-estimates">what cross-validation estimates</NoteLink> treats that distinction.

The size of the bias follows from the shape of the learning curve. Suppose the excess error over the irreducible error
falls as $c/m$, which holds for least squares with $p$ coefficients ($c \approx p\sigma^2$ for noise variance
$\sigma^2$) and for other regular parametric models. Then the excess error estimated by $K$-fold cross-validation is

$$
\frac{c}{n(K-1)/K} = \frac{K}{K-1}\cdot\frac{c}{n},
$$

the true excess error inflated by $K/(K-1)$:

| $K$ | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 20 | $n$ (leave-one-out) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inflation $K/(K-1)$ | 2.00 | 1.50 | 1.25 | 1.11 | 1.05 | $n/(n-1)$ |

Two-fold cross-validation doubles the excess error; ten-fold adds 11%. Flexible models with steep learning curves
suffer more. Burman added a correction term, built from the errors of the fold models and the full model on the whole
sample, that removes the leading term of this bias <Cite id="burman1989" />.

## Choosing $K$

- **Bias** falls as $K$ grows, by the inflation factor above, and is smallest for
  <NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">leave-one-out</NoteLink>, $K = n$.
- **Variance** does not fall in the same way. The fold models of a large $K$ share almost all their training data, so
  their errors are strongly correlated, and the average of correlated errors keeps much of their variance. The variance
  of $\operatorname{CV}_K$ is a sum of the variance of single errors, the covariance of errors within a fold and the
  covariance of errors in different folds, and the last term does not shrink with $n$ <Cite id="bengio2004" />. For
  unstable learners such as <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision trees</NoteLink>, both 2-fold and leave-one-out
  estimates had high variance in Kohavi's experiments <Cite id="kohavi1995" />.
  <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">Variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink> derives the
  decomposition.
- **Cost** is $K$ fits per candidate.
- **For selection, not assessment,** a pessimistic bias that is equal across candidates does not change their ranking.
  The bias is not equal: flexible candidates have steeper learning curves and lose more from training on fewer points,
  so a small $K$ tilts the choice towards simpler models. Experiments on regression subset selection found 10-fold
  better than leave-one-out for choosing among submodels <Cite id="breiman1992" />.

**Defaults.** Five or ten folds are standard. Kohavi recommended stratified 10-fold cross-validation for choosing among
classifiers, even when the computing budget allows more folds <Cite id="kohavi1995" />. Scikit-learn's `cross_val_score`
and `GridSearchCV` use 5 folds by default, stratified for classifiers. With very large data a single validation set is
enough; with very small data, <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">repeating</NoteLink> the $K$-fold
split with fresh partitions reduces the part of the variance that comes from the partition.

## Worked example

Six observations $y = (2, 4, 3, 7, 5, 9)$ are to be predicted by their training mean, with
<NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink>. The folds are $\set{1, 2}$, $\set{3, 4}$ and $\set{5, 6}$, so
$K = 3$.

1. **Fold 1** is held out. The training mean of $(3, 7, 5, 9)$ is 6. The squared errors at $y_1 = 2$ and $y_2 = 4$ are
   $16$ and $4$.
2. **Fold 2.** The mean of $(2, 4, 5, 9)$ is 5. The errors at 3 and 7 are $4$ and $4$.
3. **Fold 3.** The mean of $(2, 4, 3, 7)$ is 4. The errors at 5 and 9 are $1$ and $25$.
4. **Average.** $\operatorname{CV}_3 = (16 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 1 + 25)/6 = 54/6 = 9.0$.

For comparison, the overall mean is 5, the residuals are $(-3, -1, -2, 2, 0, 4)$, and the training error is
$34/6 = 5.67$. Leaving one point out moves the mean away from that point by $1/(n-1)$ of its residual, so each
leave-one-out residual is $n/(n-1) = 6/5$ times the training residual, and leave-one-out gives
$5.67 \times 1.44 = 8.16$.

The three numbers follow the formula of the previous section. Let the observations have variance $\sigma^2$. The error
of a mean of $m$ points at a new point has variance $\sigma^2$ from the new point and $\sigma^2/m$ from the mean, so
$\operatorname{Err}(m) = \sigma^2(1 + 1/m)$. With $n = 6$:

- the model in use, trained on 6 points, has $\operatorname{Err}(6) = 1.17\sigma^2$;
- leave-one-out targets $\operatorname{Err}(5) = 1.20\sigma^2$;
- 3-fold targets $\operatorname{Err}(4) = 1.25\sigma^2$;
- the training error has expectation $\sigma^2(1 - 1/n) = 0.83\sigma^2$.

The sample variance, $34/5 = 6.8$, estimates $\sigma^2$. It puts the target at $1.17 \times 6.8 = 7.9$, the 3-fold
estimate's expectation at $8.5$, and the training error's at $5.7$. The excess error of the mean predictor is
$\sigma^2/m$, the $c/m$ form with $c = \sigma^2$, and the 3-fold estimate inflates it by $3/2$: from $\sigma^2/6$ to
$\sigma^2/4$.

## Cost

$K$ fits per candidate, and $K\abs{\Lambda}$ fits for a search over a set $\Lambda$ of hyperparameter values. The fits
are independent and run in parallel. Each fit sees $n(K-1)/K$ points, so for learners whose cost grows faster than
linearly in $n$ the total is less than $K$ full fits. For linear smoothers, leave-one-out needs only one fit (see
<NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">leave-one-out cross-validation</NoteLink>).

## Trade-offs

- **Data efficiency against cost.** Every point validates once and trains $K - 1$ times, at $K$ times the cost of a
  hold-out <Cite id="arlot2010" />.
- **Dependence on the partition.** A different random partition gives a different estimate. Repetition averages this
  out (see <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">repeated and Monte Carlo
  cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **No honest <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>.** The $K$ fold estimates share training data, so
  the standard deviation of the fold estimates divided by $\sqrt K$ is too small, and no unbiased estimator of the
  variance exists for all distributions <Cite id="bengio2004" />.

## Pitfalls

- **No shuffle.** Scikit-learn's `KFold` does not shuffle by default. On rows sorted by class or by date, contiguous
  folds hold out whole classes or whole periods.
- **Different folds for different candidates.** Comparing candidates on different partitions adds partition noise to
  every difference. Fix the folds, or the seed, once.
- **Reporting the best score.** The minimum of $\operatorname{CV}_K$ over many candidates is biased low (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
- **Averaging a non-additive metric over folds.** $F_1$ and the <Gloss name="auroc">area under the ROC curve</Gloss>
  computed per fold and averaged differ from the same metric computed on all $n$ out-of-fold predictions. For $F_1$
  under <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">class imbalance</NoteLink>, pooling the <Gloss name="tp">true-positive</Gloss>,
  <Gloss name="fp">false-positive</Gloss> and <Gloss name="fn">false-negative</Gloss> counts over folds is the least
  biased choice; per-fold averaging is biased, and fails when a fold has no positives <Cite id="forman2010" />.
- **Dependent rows.** Repeated measurements of one entity, nearby points in space and consecutive points in time make
  random folds optimistic (see <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="spatial-and-blocked-cross-validation">spatial and blocked</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="time-series-cross-validation">time-series</NoteLink> cross-validation).
