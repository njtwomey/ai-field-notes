<Definition>
Let each example $(\xvec_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, carry a location $\svec_i$ in space or time. **Blocked
cross-validation** partitions the region into contiguous blocks, assigns
whole blocks to $K$ folds, and runs $K$-fold cross-validation on those folds. **Buffered leave-one-out** predicts each
point $i$ from a model fitted to the points farther than a radius $r$ from $\svec_i$. Both keep test points away from
the training points, so that the estimate measures prediction at a distance like the one at which the model will be used
<Cite id="roberts2017cv" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Measurements taken close together in space or time are usually alike. Soil carbon at two sites 10 m apart, the
temperature on consecutive days and the land cover of neighbouring pixels are correlated. This dependence is called
**autocorrelation**: the correlation $\rho(d)$ between two residuals is a function of the distance $d$ between their
locations, and it falls towards 0 as $d$ grows. The distance at which it becomes negligible is the **range**.

Random $K$-fold cross-validation assigns points to folds without regard to location. Every test point then has
training points at about the typical spacing of the sample. A flexible model can predict the test point from those
neighbours. It does so through the residual that the neighbours share, which a model fitted far away cannot use. The
cross-validation error is then an estimate of the **interpolation error**: the error at new points scattered among the
training points.

Many models are used elsewhere: a species-distribution model projected to a new region, a biomass map extrapolated
across a continent, a forecaster run on next month. There the nearest training point is far away, the residuals no
longer correlate, and the error is larger. A random-fold estimate of it is optimistic. Ploton et al. found that a
<NoteLink to="random-forest">random forest</NoteLink> predicting forest biomass in central Africa explained more than
half the variance under random cross-validation and almost none under spatial validation <Cite id="ploton2020" />.

The same optimism corrupts selection. Random folds reward the models that exploit neighbours best: large ensembles of
deep trees, <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink>, and inputs that encode location. Selection
by random folds can therefore pick a model that transfers worse than its rivals <Cite id="valavi2019" />.

## Mechanism

### What random folds measure

Take a target that is a smooth residual field plus independent noise, $y(\svec) = r(\svec) + \varepsilon$. The field
$r$ has mean 0, variance $\sigma_r^2$ and correlation $\rho(d)$ at distance $d$. The noise $\varepsilon$ has variance
$\sigma_\varepsilon^2$ and is independent of everything else. Predict $y$ at a new location $\svec_0$ by the value
$y_j$ at the nearest training location $\svec_j$, which is at distance $d$ from $\svec_0$. The prediction error is
$y_0 - y_j = (r_0 - r_j) + (\varepsilon_0 - \varepsilon_j)$. The two brackets are independent and each has mean 0.
Their variances are $\var(r_0 - r_j) = \sigma_r^2 + \sigma_r^2 - 2\sigma_r^2\rho(d)$ and
$\var(\varepsilon_0 - \varepsilon_j) = 2\sigma_\varepsilon^2$. So the expected squared error is

$$
\expect\brack{(y_0 - y_j)^2} = 2\sigma_r^2\paren{1 - \rho(d)} + 2\sigma_\varepsilon^2.
$$

The error depends on the data only through the distance $d$ from the test point to the training data. Close
neighbours ($\rho(d) \approx 1$) make the error nearly $2\sigma_\varepsilon^2$. Distant ones ($\rho(d) \approx 0$) make
it $2\sigma_r^2 + 2\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, worse than the constant prediction 0, whose error is
$\sigma_r^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2$. A cross-validation scheme therefore estimates the deployment error when its test
points sit at the same distances from their training data as the deployment points do. Random folds reproduce the
distances of interpolation and nothing else.

Real learners are more complicated than copying a neighbour, but the mechanism carries over. Any model that can
recover a point's residual from nearby points gains from short test distances. The gain needs no location input:
covariates that vary smoothly in space make nearby points close in feature space too, so a nearest-neighbour or tree
model finds the same neighbours through the covariates.

### Blocked cross-validation

1. Divide the region into blocks: squares or hexagons of side $b$ in space, contiguous intervals in time, or natural
   units such as catchments or administrative regions.
2. Give each point the label of its block, and assign blocks to $K$ folds, at random or balancing the fold sizes.
3. Run <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink> with the blocks as groups: every
   block is wholly in one test fold.

A test point in the interior of a held-out block is at least its distance to the block's edge from every training
point. Larger blocks move test points farther from the training data and raise the estimate. The usual rule sets $b$
larger than the range of the residual autocorrelation, estimated from an empirical variogram of the residuals of a
first model <Cite id="roberts2017cv" /> <Cite id="valavi2019" />. Blocking only the test fold leaves the training data
of the other folds untouched, so the cost is the $K$ fits of ordinary $K$-fold cross-validation.

### Buffered leave-one-out and h-block

Buffered leave-one-out predicts each point $i$ from a model fitted to the points with $\norm{\svec_j - \svec_i} > r$.
The buffer of radius $r$ removes the near neighbours from the training set, so every test distance is at least $r$
<Cite id="lerest2014" />. In a <NoteLink to="time-series">time series</NoteLink> the same construction is **h-block**
cross-validation: delete $h$ observations on each side of the test observation <Cite id="burman1994" />. **hv-block**
cross-validation tests a block of $2v + 1$ observations with $h$ removed on each side, which makes the selection of a
model consistent where h-block is not <Cite id="racine2000" />. Rolling-origin evaluation, the standard for forecasting,
is covered in <NoteLink to="time-series-cross-validation">time-series cross-validation</NoteLink>.

### Matching the deployment distances

The right buffer or block size is set by the use of the model, not by the range alone. Milà et al. match the
distribution of the nearest-neighbour distances from test points to training points during cross-validation to the
distribution of the distances from the prediction locations to the training points <Cite id="mila2022" />. Their
**nearest-neighbour distance matching** (NNDM) leave-one-out removes training points around each test point only as far
as that matching requires. When the prediction locations lie among the training points, it removes nothing and is
ordinary leave-one-out. When they lie far away, it acts like a buffer. A $K$-fold version, kNNDM, builds spatial folds
by the same matching and costs $K$ fits <Cite id="linnenbrink2024" />. The principle is the one in
<NoteLink to="matching-the-split-to-deployment">matching the split to deployment</NoteLink>: a test point should be as
new as a deployment point, along the dimension in which it will be new.

## Worked example

Let the residual field have $\sigma_r^2 = 1$ and an exponential correlation $\rho(d) = e^{-d/\ell}$ with range
parameter $\ell = 0.2$. Let the noise variance be $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 0.25$. Take $n = 100$ training sites spread
uniformly over the unit square. The model predicts each site by its nearest training site.

1. **Random leave-one-out.** For points scattered uniformly at density $n$ per unit area, the mean distance to the
   nearest neighbour, ignoring edge effects, is $1/(2\sqrt n) = 0.05$. At $d = 0.05$, $\rho = e^{-0.25} = 0.779$. The
   error is $2(1 - 0.779) + 2(0.25) = 0.94$.
2. **Deployment 0.3 away.** The model will map a region whose points are about $0.3$ from the nearest training site.
   At $d = 0.3$, $\rho = e^{-1.5} = 0.223$, and the error is $2(1 - 0.223) + 0.5 = 2.05$.
3. **The constant model.** Predicting 0 everywhere has error $\sigma_r^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1.25$ at any
   distance.
4. **Buffered leave-one-out with $r = 0.3$.** Each held-out site is predicted from sites at least 0.3 away, so its
   nearest-neighbour distance is about 0.3, and the estimate is about 2.05.

Random leave-one-out rates the nearest-neighbour model better than the constant (0.94 against 1.25). Buffered
leave-one-out, matched to the deployment distance, rates it worse (2.05 against 1.25), which is the true ordering at
the deployment sites. The random estimate is wrong about the size of the error and about which model to choose.

## Cost

Blocked $K$-fold cross-validation costs $K$ fits, like ordinary $K$-fold. Buffered and NNDM leave-one-out cost one
fit per test point, $n$ in all, with a different training set for each, so the linear-smoother shortcut of
<NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">leave-one-out cross-validation</NoteLink> does not apply. kNNDM brings
the cost back to $K$ fits.

## Trade-offs

- **Pessimism from large blocks.** Large blocks remove whole parts of the covariate space from each training fold.
  The test fold then asks the model to extrapolate in its inputs as well as in space, which the final model, trained on
  every block, may never have to do. The estimate can exceed the deployment error.
- **Fewer, unequal folds.** Large blocks give few groups. Folds then differ in size and in their mix of conditions,
  which raises the variance of the estimate.
- **The target decides the scheme.** When the model will map the same region from which a probability sample (a random
  or stratified random design) was drawn, the deployment points are interpolation points. Design-based estimates from
  the sample are then unbiased for the map's accuracy, random cross-validation comes close to them, and spatial
  cross-validation overstates the error <Cite id="wadoux2021" />. Blocking helps when the prediction locations are
  farther from the data than the sample's own spacing: a new region, a clustered sample with gaps, or the future.

## Pitfalls

- **Dropping coordinates does not remove the leak.** Covariates that are themselves spatially smooth carry location.
  Turn off "coordinates as features" in the figure: random cross-validation is still optimistic for the new region.
- **Tuning with random folds.** Hyperparameters and feature sets chosen by random cross-validation favour models that
  memorise neighbourhoods. Use the blocked folds for selection as well as for the final estimate, nested if the
  estimate must be honest about the selection (see <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
  cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Blocks smaller than the range.** A grid of blocks narrower than the autocorrelation range leaves test points
  correlated with training points across the block edges. The estimate stays optimistic.
- **Preprocessing across the split.** Smoothing, gap filling or spatial interpolation of inputs computed on the whole
  map before splitting carries test-block information into training. Fit them inside the folds, as in
  <NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>.
