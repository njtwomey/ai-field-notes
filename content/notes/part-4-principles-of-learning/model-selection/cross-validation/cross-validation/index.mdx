<Definition>
Cross-validation estimates how well a learning procedure predicts new data by fitting it to part of the data and
scoring it on the rest, with the roles rotated so that every point is scored by a model that did not see it
<Cite id="stone1974" />. With $K$ folds, $\kappa(i)$ the fold of point $i$ and $\hat f^{-k}$ the fit without fold $k$,
the estimate is $\operatorname{CV} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell\paren{y_i, \hat f^{-\kappa(i)}(\xvec_i)}$. This topic
covers the ways of splitting the data, the closed forms for linear smoothers, the bootstrap alternatives, and what the
estimate measures and how precisely <Cite id="arlot2010" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The training error of a fitted model is optimistic, by an amount that grows with the model's flexibility
(<NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>). Cross-validation answers the
chapter's first question, how well a candidate will predict, for any learner and any loss. It needs no likelihood and
no parameter count, which the <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information criteria</NoteLink> need. The price is
refitting: $K$ fits per candidate.

The estimate serves two jobs. **Selection** computes $\operatorname{CV}$ for each candidate on the same folds and keeps
the smallest. **Assessment** reports the error of the final model. The two cannot share data: the smallest of many
cross-validation scores is biased low, which <NoteLink to="selection-bias-and-nested-cross-validation">selection bias
and nested cross-validation</NoteLink> treats. Current defaults are 5- or 10-fold, stratified for classification,
grouped when records cluster, nested for assessment on small data, and a single validation set for large models
<Cite id="kohavi1995" />.

## Main ideas

**Hold out, then rotate.** <NoteLink to="hold-out-validation">Hold-out validation</NoteLink> fits on one part and scores
the rest. It is unbiased for the model fitted to the smaller part, its <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
error</NoteLink> falls as $1/\sqrt{n_\text{val}}$, and it is the default when one fit is expensive.
<NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold cross-validation</NoteLink> rotates the held-out part so that every
point validates once. Each fold model is trained on $n(K - 1)/K$ points, so the estimate is pessimistic while the
learning curve still falls, and more so for small $K$. After selection, the chosen candidate is refitted on all $n$
points.

**Leave-one-out and its closed forms.** <NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">Leave-one-out</NoteLink> is the
case $K = n$: the least biased scheme, and a high-variance one for unstable learners. For a linear smoother
$\hat\yvec = \Smat\yvec$, the held-out residual is $(y_i - \hat y_i)/(1 - S_{ii})$, so one fit gives all $n$ and the
PRESS statistic <Cite id="allen1974" />. <NoteLink to="generalised-cross-validation">Generalised
cross-validation</NoteLink> replaces each leverage $S_{ii}$ by the mean $\trace\Smat/n$. It needs only the residuals and
the trace, and no estimate of the noise variance.

**Repetition and resampling.** <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">Repeated and Monte Carlo
cross-validation</NoteLink> average over fresh random partitions. Repetition removes the variance due to the partition,
not the variance due to the data. Holding out a fraction of the data that tends to one makes the selection consistent
<Cite id="shao1993" />. The <NoteLink to="bootstrap-estimates-of-prediction-error">bootstrap estimators</NoteLink>
score each point on the resamples that did not draw it. The leave-one-out bootstrap is pessimistic, the .632 estimator
mixes it with the training error, and .632+ corrects that mix when the model overfits heavily <Cite id="efron1997" />.

**The split must match deployment.** A test fold should be as new to the model as deployment data will be.
<NoteLink to="stratified-cross-validation">Stratified folds</NoteLink> keep the class proportions of the whole data set,
which keeps rare classes in every fold. <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">Grouped folds</NoteLink> keep all
records of a patient or user together; record-wise folds let the model recognise the group and make the estimate
optimistic. <NoteLink to="spatial-and-blocked-cross-validation">Blocked folds</NoteLink> hold out contiguous regions,
because under autocorrelation random folds measure interpolation error. Every step that learns from data, from scaling
to target encoding and <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink>, is refitted inside each training
fold (<NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>).

**What the estimate measures.** Cross-validation estimates the expected error $\operatorname{Err}$ of the procedure over
training sets of its size. It tracks the conditional error $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ of the one model fitted to these
data only weakly, and for least squares not at all <Cite id="bates2024" />
(<NoteLink to="what-cross-validation-estimates">what cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>). The fold errors are
correlated, because the training sets overlap. The naive standard error $s/\sqrt K$ is therefore too small, and no
unbiased estimator of the variance exists <Cite id="bengio2004" />. Nested schemes give intervals with close to nominal
coverage (<NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>).

**Selection reuses the data.** The candidate with the smallest cross-validation score won partly by luck on these
folds, so its score understates its error (<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model
selection</NoteLink>). An honest estimate scores the whole procedure, search included, on data the search never saw:
<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink> on small data, a locked test set on large data
<Cite id="varma2006" />. The <NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink> makes the
selection itself less sensitive to fold noise.

  leave-one-out and GCV on one problem, with the one-standard-error choice.
