<Definition>
Split the indices $1, \dots, n$ at random into a **training set** $T$ of $n_\text{tr}$ points and a **validation set**
$V$ of $n_\text{val} = n - n_\text{tr}$ points. Fit the model to $T$ alone, giving $\hat f_T$. The **hold-out
estimate** of prediction error is the average loss on the validation set,

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{ho} = \frac{1}{n_\text{val}}\sum_{i \in V} \ell\paren{y_i, \hat f_T(\xvec_i)}.
$$

It estimates the error of $\hat f_T$ on new data from the same distribution <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The training error of a fitted model is optimistic: the model was chosen to fit those points, so it fits them better
than it fits new ones (see <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>). An
honest estimate needs points the fit never saw. Hold-out validation sets some aside before fitting.

The method serves two of the chapter's three questions. It **estimates** the error of one model. It **selects** among
candidates when each is fitted to $T$ and the one with the smallest validation error is kept. The third question,
**assessment** of the chosen model, needs data that the selection did not touch: a separate test set, which turns the
two-way split into the three-way split of <NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model
assessment</NoteLink>.

Holding data out has two costs. The model is fitted to $n_\text{tr} < n$ points, so it is usually worse than the model
fitted to all $n$. The estimate rests on one random split and $n_\text{val}$ points, so it is noisy. The size of the
validation set trades one cost against the other.

## Mechanism

1. **Split.** Shuffle the indices and cut them into $T$ and $V$. The split must imitate how the model will meet new
   data: by group when records share an entity, forward in time for forecasting (see
   <NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">train, validation and test splits</NoteLink>). For classification,
   split each class separately (<NoteLink to="stratified-cross-validation">stratification</NoteLink>).
2. **Fit** every data-dependent step on $T$ only: scaling, imputation, encoding,
   <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink> and the model itself.
3. **Score.** Predict each point of $V$ and average the losses.
4. **Select**, if there are several candidates: fit each to $T$, score each on $V$ and keep the best.
5. **Refit or keep.** Small models are usually refitted to $T \cup V$ with the chosen settings. Large models are often
   kept as fitted to $T$, because a refit is expensive and the number of training steps chosen on $V$ may not transfer
   to a larger training set.

A common default for a three-way split is half the data for training and a quarter each for validation and testing
<Cite id="hastie2009" />. Such proportions are conventions. The next two sections give the quantities that should set
them.

## What the estimate estimates

Condition on the training set $T$. The fitted model $\hat f_T$ is then a fixed function. The validation points are
independent draws from the data distribution and independent of $T$, so the losses $Z_i = \ell(y_i, \hat f_T(\xvec_i))$,
$i \in V$, are <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss> given $T$. Write
$\operatorname{Err}_T = \expect_{(\xvec, y)}\brack{\ell(y, \hat f_T(\xvec))}$ for the true error of $\hat f_T$ and
$\tau_T^2 = \var(Z_i \mid T)$ for the variance of one loss. The average of $n_\text{val}$ independent losses then has

$$
\expect\brack{\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{ho} \mid T} = \operatorname{Err}_T,
\qquad
\var\paren{\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{ho} \mid T} = \frac{\tau_T^2}{n_\text{val}}.
$$

The hold-out estimate is unbiased for the error of the model it scores, and its standard deviation is
$\tau_T/\sqrt{n_\text{val}}$. The sample standard deviation $s$ of the $n_\text{val}$ losses estimates $\tau_T$, so
$s/\sqrt{n_\text{val}}$ is a valid <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>. For the
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> with <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error
rate</NoteLink> $p$, $\tau_T^2 = p(1 - p)$ and the standard error is $\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n_\text{val}}$. Because
the validation errors are independent binomial trials, the exact intervals of <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set
bounds</NoteLink> apply. No other estimator in this chapter has this property: the fold errors of
<NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold cross-validation</NoteLink> share training data, and their standard
error is only approximate.

The model that is used is usually the one refitted to all $n$ points, with true error $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$. The
estimate's error about that target splits into two parts,

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{ho} - \operatorname{Err}_\Dcal =
\underbrace{\paren{\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{ho} - \operatorname{Err}_T}}_{\text{validation noise}} +
\underbrace{\paren{\operatorname{Err}_T - \operatorname{Err}_\Dcal}}_{\text{cost of fitting to fewer points}}.
$$

The first part has mean zero and variance $\tau_T^2/n_\text{val}$, which falls as the validation set grows. The second
part is positive on average while the <NoteLink to="learning-curves-and-sample-size">learning curve</NoteLink> is still
falling at $n$, and it grows as the validation set grows, because the training set shrinks. For a model whose excess
error falls as $c/m$ in the training size $m$, as least squares with $p$ coefficients does with $c \approx p\sigma^2$
for noise variance $\sigma^2$, holding out a fraction $v$ costs about

$$
\frac{c}{(1 - v)n} - \frac{c}{n} = \frac{c}{n}\cdot\frac{v}{1 - v}.
$$

Holding out a quarter adds a third of the excess error $c/n$; holding out half doubles it. The best fraction minimises
the sum of the variance and the squared mean of the two parts. It depends on $n$ and on how steep the learning curve
is. Simulations for linear classifiers found that giving two thirds of the cases to training was close to optimal for
datasets of 100 or more with a strong signal, and that smaller or noisier datasets should give more to training
<Cite id="dobbin2011" />.

## How large the validation set must be

The standard error fixes the validation size needed for a given precision. For an error rate near $p$, the half-width
of an approximate 95% interval is $1.96\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n_\text{val}}$. A target half-width $h$ needs

$$
n_\text{val} \ge \frac{1.96^2\,p(1 - p)}{h^2}.
$$

Halving the half-width needs four times the points. A target stated relative to the error, $h = \beta p$, gives
$n_\text{val} \ge 1.96^2(1 - p)/(\beta^2 p)$, which grows as $1/p$: accurate classifiers need large validation sets
to measure. At $p = 0.01$ and $\beta = 0.2$ the bound is $3.84 \times 0.99/(0.04 \times 0.01) \approx 9{,}500$. An
analysis of handwriting recognition reached the same order: at an error rate near 1%, a test set of 10,000 independent
characters guarantees with 95% confidence that the true error is at most 1.25 times the observed one
<Cite id="guyon1998" />. For a comparison of two models on the same validation set, a paired test is more sensitive than
two separate intervals (see <NoteLink to="mcnemars-test">McNemar's test</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

A classifier is to be built from $n = 1{,}000$ labelled examples.

1. **One split.** Hold out 200 and train on 800. The model makes 30 errors on the 200, so
   $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{ho} = 0.150$. The standard error is
   $\sqrt{0.15 \times 0.85/200} = \sqrt{0.000638} = 0.025$, and the approximate 95% interval is
   $0.150 \pm 0.049$, from 0.10 to 0.20.
2. **The precision needed.** Two models whose errors differ by one point cannot be told apart by such intervals. A
   half-width of $h = 0.01$ at $p = 0.15$ needs $n_\text{val} \ge 3.84 \times 0.15 \times 0.85/0.0001 = 4{,}898$
   validation points, more than the whole dataset. With 1,000 examples, a single hold-out cannot resolve
   one-point differences; <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold cross-validation</NoteLink>, which validates
   on all 1,000, is the usual answer.
3. **Selection on the validation set.** Suppose 20 candidate models all have true error 0.15 and are scored on the same
   200 points. If their 20 estimates were independent with standard deviation 0.025, the smallest would be about
   $0.150 - 1.87 \times 0.025 = 0.103$ on average, since 1.87 is the expected maximum of 20 independent standard normal
   variables. Reporting 0.103 as the chosen model's error overstates its accuracy by almost five points. Estimates on
   shared validation points are positively correlated, which shrinks the effect, but the direction is the same. The
   remedy is a test set the selection never saw (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model
   selection</NoteLink>).

## Cost

One fit per candidate and one pass over the validation set. Hold-out is the cheapest error estimate, which is why it is
the default when fitting is expensive: deep networks are tuned on a single fixed validation set, with $K$-fold
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> reserved for small data (see
<NoteLink to="model-selection-at-scale">model selection at scale</NoteLink>).

## Trade-offs

- **Pessimistic bias.** The estimate describes a model fitted to $n_\text{tr}$ points. While the learning curve is still
  falling, it overstates the error of the model refitted to all $n$.
- **Variance from one split.** A different random split gives a different estimate. Small data make both the
  validation noise and the split-to-split variation large. In small genomic studies with feature selection, the simple
  split-sample estimate was seriously biased and had a larger <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> than
  <Gloss name="loo" />, 10-fold cross-validation and the .632+ bootstrap <Cite id="molinaro2005" />. Averaging over many
  random splits is <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">Monte Carlo cross-validation</NoteLink>.
- **Data efficiency.** Each point is used either to fit or to validate. $K$-fold cross-validation uses every point for
  both, at $K$ times the cost <Cite id="arlot2010" />.
- **When it is the right choice.** Large datasets, where $n_\text{val}$ in the thousands still leaves most of the data
  for fitting; expensive models; a fixed external test set; and time-ordered data, where the last period is the natural
  validation set.

## Pitfalls

- **An unrepresentative split.** A random split shifts the class proportions between the two parts, in opposite
  directions. A classifier that predicts the majority class of its training set scores 33.3% on Fisher's iris data,
  three classes of 50, but averaged 21.7% over random two-thirds/one-third hold-out splits, because the class that is
  over-represented in training is under-represented in validation <Cite id="kohavi1995" />. Stratified splits remove
  the shift.
- **Reporting the validation error after selecting on it.** The best of several validation scores is optimistic, as in
  step 3 of the worked example. Repeated looks at the same validation set during development have the same effect (see
  <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>).
- **Too small a validation set.** A difference of one point of accuracy on 500 validation points is within the noise.
- **Leakage through preprocessing.** A scaler, encoder or feature selector fitted before the split sees the validation
  points (see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>).
- **Records of one entity on both sides.** Several rows per patient, user or session make the validation error measure
  recognition of the entity; split by entity (see <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped
  cross-validation</NoteLink>).
