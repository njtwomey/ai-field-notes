<Definition>
**Regularisation** is any change to a learning procedure that restricts the fitted model in order to reduce its
variance, accepting some bias in exchange. The most common form adds a **penalty** $\Omega(\wvec) \ge 0$ on the
parameters $\wvec$ to the empirical risk,

$$
\hat\wvec_\lambda = \argmin_{\wvec}\ \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f_{\wvec}(\xvec_i), y_i) + \lambda\,\Omega(\wvec),
$$

with a **regularisation strength** $\lambda \ge 0$. Other forms bound $\Omega(\wvec)$, stop an iterative fit early,
perturb the training data or restrict the set of variables. The strength is a hyperparameter, chosen from data
<Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">Empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> over a rich class fits the training
data closely, noise included, and the <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance
decomposition</NoteLink> shows the cost: the fitted function changes a lot from one training set to the next.
Regularisation pulls the fit towards a simple default, such as $\wvec = \zeros$, so that it moves less in response to
the noise of any one sample. At $\lambda = 0$ the fit is unregularised; as $\lambda \to \infty$ it returns the default
whatever the data say.

For <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>, regularisation replaces a discrete choice among models by
a continuous family indexed by $\lambda$. The rest of the chapter then chooses $\lambda$. The usual defaults are to
standardise the features, leave the intercept unpenalised, and choose $\lambda$ on a logarithmic grid by
cross-validation. Libraries scale the loss by $1$, $\frac1n$ or $\frac{1}{2n}$, so the same $\lambda$ means different
amounts of regularisation in different software. Regularised coefficients are biased towards zero, so
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink> computed as if the fit were unpenalised are wrong.

## Main ideas

**Penalty, constraint and prior.** A penalty $\lambda\Omega(\wvec)$, a constraint $\Omega(\wvec) \le t$ and a constraint
on the loss give the same solutions for convex problems, with $\lambda$, $t$ and the loss level linked through the data
<Cite id="oneto2016" /> (<NoteLink to="penalised-and-constrained-regularisation">penalised and constrained
regularisation</NoteLink>). The constraint form explains sparsity: the round $L^2$ ball is touched off the axes, while
the corners of the $L^1$ ball sit on them. A penalty is also a negative log prior. <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge
regression</NoteLink> is the posterior mode under a Gaussian prior, with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ for noise variance
$\sigma^2$ and prior variance $\tau^2$, and the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> is the mode under a Laplace prior
<Cite id="bishop2006" /> <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />. <NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">Structural risk
minimisation</NoteLink> states the same idea for nested hypothesis classes <Cite id="vapnik1998" />.

**Shrinkage.** The <NoteLink to="james-stein-estimator">James–Stein estimator</NoteLink> shows that shrinking can beat
the unbiased estimate everywhere. For $d \ge 3$ Gaussian means observed once each, multiplying the observations $\xvec$
by $1 - (d - 2)\sigma^2/\norm{\xvec}^2$ lowers the expected total squared error for every value of the means
<Cite id="james1961" />. <NoteLink to="tikhonov-regularisation">Tikhonov regularisation</NoteLink> solves ill-posed
inverse problems by damping the component of singular value $s$ by the filter factor $s^2/(s^2 + \lambda)$
<Cite id="tikhonov1963" />. With a design matrix and an identity penalty it is ridge regression; with a difference
operator it penalises roughness. The <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of
freedom</NoteLink>, $\trace\Smat$ for a linear smoother $\hat\yvec = \Smat\yvec$, measure how much freedom a given
$\lambda$ leaves.

**Sparsity.** With orthonormal features and least-squares coefficient $z$, ridge returns $z/(1 + \lambda)$ and the lasso
returns $\operatorname{sgn}(z)\max(\abs z - \lambda/2, 0)$, in the scaling of the definition above: ridge shrinks every
coefficient, the lasso removes the small ones. <NoteLink to="sparsity-inducing-penalties">Sparsity-inducing
penalties</NoteLink> compare these thresholding rules: $L^1$ is convex but shifts large coefficients towards zero, while
$L^0$, bridge, SCAD and MCP leave them unbiased at the price of a non-convex objective.
<NoteLink to="structured-sparsity-penalties">Structured sparsity penalties</NoteLink> remove whole groups (group lasso),
make neighbours equal (fused lasso, total variation) or make a matrix low-rank (nuclear norm).
<NoteLink to="best-subset-and-stepwise-selection">Best subset and stepwise selection</NoteLink> impose $L^0$ by search.
Best subset beats the lasso at high <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratios</Gloss> and loses at low ones
<Cite id="hastie2020best" />.

**Paths and the choice of strength.** A <NoteLink to="regularisation-paths">regularisation path</NoteLink> computes the
fit over a decreasing grid of $\lambda$, each fit warm-started from the last. The ridge path is smooth and the lasso
path piecewise linear. <NoteLink to="choosing-the-regularisation-strength">Choosing the regularisation
strength</NoteLink> compares the rules: $K$-fold cross-validation with or without the
<NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink>, leave-one-out and <Gloss name="gcv" /> for
linear smoothers, $C_p$ and <Gloss name="sure" /> when the noise variance is known, the evidence, and the L-curve and
discrepancy principle for inverse problems. The smallest validation error along the path is optimistic for the $\lambda$
that attains it, so assessing the tuned model needs data the choice never saw.

**Regularisation without a penalty.** Stopping an iterative solver early regularises it: started at zero,
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> after $t$ steps is a spectral filter like ridge with
$\lambda \approx 1/t$ (<NoteLink to="iterative-regularisation">iterative regularisation</NoteLink>). Gradient flow's
risk is within a factor 1.69 of ridge's at the matching $\lambda$, for every design <Cite id="ali2019" />. Training on
noisy inputs is exactly ridge regression for a linear model with squared error, and a penalty on the input gradient for
a nonlinear one <Cite id="bishop1995" />; dropout acts as an adaptive $L^2$ penalty
(<NoteLink to="noise-injection-as-regularisation">noise injection as regularisation</NoteLink>). Fewer features, smaller
trees and fewer basis functions are structural restrictions of the same kind.
