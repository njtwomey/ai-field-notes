<Definition>
Let $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ hold $p$ predictors and $\yvec \in \reals^n$ the targets, and for a set
$S \subseteq \set{1, \dots, p}$ let $\operatorname{RSS}(S)$ be the residual sum of squares of the least-squares fit on
the columns in $S$. **Best subset selection** of size $k$ returns

$$
\hat S_k = \argmin_{\abs{S} = k} \operatorname{RSS}(S), \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, p.
$$

**Forward stepwise selection** builds a nested sequence instead, adding at each step the predictor that lowers the
residual sum of squares most; **backward stepwise selection** starts from all $p$ and drops the predictor whose removal
raises it least. **Forward stagewise regression** moves one coefficient at a time by a small amount towards the
residual. Each method produces one candidate model per size $k$, and the size is chosen by
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or an <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information
criterion</NoteLink> <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="linear-regression">Least squares</NoteLink> on all $p$ predictors is unbiased but can have a large
variance, and it keeps every predictor however little it contributes. Dropping a predictor whose coefficient is small
adds a little bias and removes that coefficient's variance. A model on fewer predictors is also easier to read and
cheaper to measure.

Subset selection makes this trade directly: it restricts the number of nonzero coefficients, the $\ell_0$ "norm"
$\norm{\wvec}_0 = \#\set{j : w_j \neq 0}$. Best subset of size $k$ is the constrained problem

$$
\min_{\wvec}\ \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \norm{\wvec}_0 \le k,
$$

the $\ell_0$ member of the family whose $\ell_1$ member is the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> and whose
$\ell_2$ member is <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> (see
<NoteLink to="penalised-and-constrained-regularisation">penalised and constrained regularisation</NoteLink>). The
constraint set is not convex, so none of the convex machinery applies, and the problem is combinatorial.

The methods answer the select question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink> twice over. The
search chooses a subset for each size, and validation chooses the size. Both choices use the data, so the training
error of the chosen model is optimistic for two reasons: the fit and the search.

## Best subset

**Residual sums of squares are nested.** Adding a column to a least-squares fit cannot raise its residual sum of
squares, because the old fit is still available with the new coefficient at zero. The best residual sum of squares
$\operatorname{RSS}(\hat S_k)$ therefore falls as $k$ grows, reaching the full least-squares value at $k = p$. The
training error cannot choose $k$; it always prefers $k = p$.

**The best subsets need not be nested.** $\hat S_2$ need not contain $\hat S_1$. Best subset compares every subset of
each size on its own merits, which is its advantage over the stepwise methods and the source of its cost: there are
$\binom{p}{k}$ subsets of size $k$ and $2^p$ in all.

**Orthonormal predictors: hard thresholding.** When $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = \eye$, write $z_j = \xvec_j^\top\yvec$ for the
least-squares coefficient of predictor $j$, where $\xvec_j$ is column $j$. Projecting $\yvec$ onto orthonormal columns
gives

$$
\operatorname{RSS}(S) = \norm{\yvec}^2 - \sum_{j \in S} z_j^2,
$$

so the best subset of size $k$ is the $k$ predictors with the largest $\abs{z_j}$, and each keeps its least-squares
coefficient $z_j$. In penalised form, $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}_0$ separates into one problem
per coefficient: keeping $w_j = z_j$ costs $\lambda$, and setting $w_j = 0$ costs $z_j^2$ in extra residual. The
solution is **hard thresholding**,

$$
\hat w_j = z_j \cdot \indicator\brack{\abs{z_j} > \sqrt\lambda}.
$$

The lasso on the same design gives soft thresholding, $\sgn(z_j)\max(\abs{z_j} - \lambda/2, 0)$: it selects the same
kind of set but also shrinks every survivor by $\lambda/2$ (see
<NoteLink to="sparsity-inducing-penalties">sparsity-inducing penalties</NoteLink>). Best subset keeps the survivors
unshrunk, which is its strength when the kept effects are large and its weakness when they are small and noisy.

**Exact search.** The leaps-and-bounds algorithm of Furnival and Wilson prunes the tree of subsets with the nesting
property: a subset whose residual sum of squares already exceeds the best known value for a smaller size cannot contain
a better subset of that size <Cite id="furnival1974" />. It made best subset practical for about 30 to 35 predictors.
Bertsimas, King and Mazumder wrote the problem as a mixed-integer quadratic program and solved it with a commercial
solver, warm-started by a first-order heuristic, reaching certified optima for $p$ in the hundreds and good solutions
for $p$ in the thousands <Cite id="bertsimas2016" />. In general the problem is NP-hard <Cite id="natarajan1995" />.

## Stepwise methods

**Forward stepwise** starts from the intercept alone and adds one predictor per step. At each step it fits every
one-predictor extension of the current set $S$ and keeps the one with the smallest residual sum of squares. The cost
of a step is small because the drop in residual sum of squares has a closed form. Let $\rvec$ be the residual of the
fit on $S$ and let $\tilde\xvec_j$ be column $j$ with its projection onto the columns of $S$ removed. Adding $j$ adds
the direction $\tilde\xvec_j$ to the fitted space, and the residual's projection onto it is removed:

$$
\operatorname{RSS}(S) - \operatorname{RSS}(S \cup \set{j}) = \frac{(\tilde\xvec_j^\top\rvec)^2}{\norm{\tilde\xvec_j}^2}.
$$

The residual $\rvec$ is orthogonal to the columns of $S$, so $\tilde\xvec_j^\top\rvec = \xvec_j^\top\rvec$. Forward
stepwise therefore adds the predictor whose correlation with the current residual is largest **after** the predictor
has been adjusted for those already in the model. A <NoteLink to="qr-decomposition">QR decomposition</NoteLink> updated
one column at a time gives every $\tilde\xvec_j$ cheaply.

**Backward stepwise** starts from all $p$ predictors and removes, at each step, the one whose removal raises the
residual sum of squares least; for least squares that is the predictor with the smallest $t$ statistic. It needs $n > p$
for the first fit to exist.

Both stepwise methods produce nested sequences, one model per size. A predictor added early is never removed by forward
stepwise, and one removed early never returns in backward stepwise. Hybrid versions consider an addition and a deletion
at each step. The <NoteLink to="wrapper-feature-selection">wrapper methods</NoteLink> of Part III apply the same
searches to any learner, scored by a validation estimate instead of the residual sum of squares.

**Classical stopping rules.** Older software stopped forward stepwise when no candidate's $F$ statistic for addition
exceeded a threshold, an "$F$-to-enter". The $F$ statistic at a step is the maximum over the remaining candidates, so it
does not have an $F$ distribution, and the nominal <NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink> of
the threshold is wrong <Cite id="miller2002" />. Choosing $k$ by validation avoids this.

## Forward stagewise and the lasso

**Forward stagewise** makes many small moves instead of a few full refits. Start from $\wvec = \zeros$ with the residual
$\rvec = \yvec$, on standardised predictors. Repeat:

1. find the predictor $j$ most correlated with the current residual, $j = \argmax_j \abs{\xvec_j^\top\rvec}$;
2. move its coefficient a small amount $\epsilon$ in the direction of that correlation,
   $w_j \leftarrow w_j + \epsilon\,\sgn(\xvec_j^\top\rvec)$;
3. update the residual, $\rvec \leftarrow \rvec - \epsilon\,\sgn(\xvec_j^\top\rvec)\,\xvec_j$.

Unlike forward stepwise, stagewise never refits the coefficients already in the model, and the same predictor can be
chosen many times. The coefficients grow slowly. After enough steps the fit approaches least squares, and the number of
steps is the <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> parameter (see
<NoteLink to="iterative-regularisation">iterative regularisation</NoteLink>).

As $\epsilon \to 0$, the path of forward stagewise is piecewise linear and coincides with a variant of
<NoteLink to="least-angle-regression">least-angle regression</NoteLink>. When every lasso
<NoteLink to="regularisation-paths">coefficient path</NoteLink> is monotone it is the lasso path itself
<Cite id="efron2004" />. In general the infinitesimal stagewise path solves the monotone lasso, the path that reduces
the residual sum of squares fastest per unit of $\ell_1$ arc length <Cite id="hastie2007stagewise" />. Forward stagewise
is thus the bridge between greedy selection and the convex $\ell_1$ penalty, and $L_2$ boosting with linear base
learners is the same algorithm (see <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient boosting</NoteLink>).

## Choosing the size

Each method gives a sequence of models $\hat S_0, \hat S_1, \dots$; the remaining choice is $k$, a one-dimensional
selection. The standard tool is $K$-fold cross-validation over $k$ (see <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold
cross-validation</NoteLink>), often with the <NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink>,
which prefers the smallest $k$ within one <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the best. The
search must run inside each fold: the subsets are refound on the training folds, and the held-out fold scores the subset
of size $k$ found without it. Scoring the subsets found on all the data by cross-validation reuses the data that chose
them and is optimistic (see <NoteLink to="selection-inside-cross-validation">selection inside
cross-validation</NoteLink>).

Mallows' $C_p$, <Gloss name="aic" /> and <Gloss name="bic" /> are the analytic alternatives (see
<NoteLink to="mallows-cp-and-covariance-penalties">Mallows' $C_p$ and covariance penalties</NoteLink>). They charge for
$k$ parameters. The search makes that charge too small.

**The search spends degrees of freedom.** The <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of
freedom</NoteLink> of a fit are $\operatorname{df} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i \cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$. Take orthonormal
predictors and pure noise, $\yvec = \varepsilonvec$ with $\varepsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye)$. Best subset
of size 1 picks the predictor $j^\star$ with the largest $\abs{z_j}$ and fits $\hat\yvec = z_{j^\star}\xvec_{j^\star}$.
The true mean is zero, so $\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \expect[\hat y_i y_i]$, and summing over $i$,

$$
\sigma^2\operatorname{df} = \expect\brack{\hat\yvec^\top\yvec} = \expect\brack{z_{j^\star}\xvec_{j^\star}^\top\yvec}
= \expect\brack{\max_j z_j^2}.
$$

Each $z_j/\sigma$ is standard normal, and the $z_j$ are independent because the columns are orthonormal. With $p = 10$
the expected maximum of ten $\chi^2_1$ variables is 3.80, so the one-predictor model spends 3.8 degrees of freedom
rather than one. With $p = 100$ it spends 7.7. A $C_p$ or AIC that counts $k$ parameters understates the
<NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink> by the difference
<Cite id="ye1998" /> <Cite id="tibshirani2015search" />. Cross-validation with the search inside the folds pays this
cost automatically.

## Worked example

Take three standardised predictors of unit length. Let $\xvec_1$ and $\xvec_2$ be orthogonal, and let $\xvec_3$ lie
mostly between them:

$$
\xvec_3 = 0.9\cdot\frac{\xvec_1 + \xvec_2}{\sqrt2} + \sqrt{0.19}\,\evec,
$$

with $\evec$ a unit vector orthogonal to both. The target is $\yvec = \xvec_1 + \xvec_2$, with no noise, so
$\norm{\yvec}^2 = 2$. The inner products are $\xvec_1^\top\xvec_3 = \xvec_2^\top\xvec_3 = 0.9/\sqrt2 = 0.636$, and the
correlations with the target are $\xvec_1^\top\yvec = \xvec_2^\top\yvec = 1$ and
$\xvec_3^\top\yvec = 0.9\sqrt2 = 1.273$.

1. **Size 1.** A single unit-length predictor removes $(\xvec_j^\top\yvec)^2$ from $\norm{\yvec}^2$. The residual sums
   of squares are $2 - 1 = 1$ for $\xvec_1$ or $\xvec_2$ and $2 - 1.62 = 0.38$ for $\xvec_3$. Best subset and forward
   stepwise both choose $\set{3}$.
2. **Forward stepwise, size 2.** With $\xvec_3$ in the model, adding $\xvec_1$ fits the two-column least squares with
   Gram matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.636 \\ 0.636 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and correlations $(1, 1.273)$. Its residual sum
   of squares is 0.319, and adding $\xvec_2$ gives the same by symmetry. Forward stepwise reaches $\set{1, 3}$ with
   $\operatorname{RSS} = 0.319$.
3. **Best subset, size 2.** The pair $\set{1, 2}$ fits $\yvec$ exactly: $\operatorname{RSS} = 0$. Forward stepwise
   cannot reach it, because it committed to $\xvec_3$ at step 1.
4. **Backward stepwise.** All three predictors fit exactly. Removing $\xvec_3$ leaves the exact fit $\set{1, 2}$, while
   removing $\xvec_1$ or $\xvec_2$ leaves 0.319, so backward stepwise drops $\xvec_3$ and finds the best pair. At size 1
   it must drop $\xvec_1$ or $\xvec_2$ and ends at $\operatorname{RSS} = 1$, against the best single predictor's 0.38.

| Size $k$ | Best subset | Forward stepwise | Backward stepwise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $\set{3}$, 0.38 | $\set{3}$, 0.38 | $\set{1}$, 1.00 |
| 2 | $\set{1, 2}$, 0 | $\set{1, 3}$, 0.319 | $\set{1, 2}$, 0 |
| 3 | $\set{1, 2, 3}$, 0 | $\set{1, 2, 3}$, 0 | $\set{1, 2, 3}$, 0 |

Each greedy method is wrong at one size. The predictor that summarises the others best on its own is the wrong
predictor to build on, which is the failure of forward stepwise. The pair that works only together is invisible to it
one predictor at a time. Backward stepwise sees the pair, because it starts from a model that contains it.

## Cost

- **Best subset** fits $2^p$ least-squares problems by brute force, about $10^6$ at $p = 20$. Leaps and bounds cuts this
  by orders of magnitude for $p$ up to about 35. Mixed-integer solvers reach the hundreds or thousands, at minutes of
  solver time per size $k$ <Cite id="bertsimas2016" />.
- **Forward stepwise** to size $k$ evaluates about $kp$ candidates, each in $O(n)$ with an updated QR decomposition, so
  the whole sequence costs about as much as one least-squares fit. It works for $p > n$, up to $k = n$.
- **Backward stepwise** costs about the same but needs $n > p$.
- **Forward stagewise** takes many steps of size $\epsilon$, each costing $O(np)$; the smaller $\epsilon$, the more
  steps.
- **Validation** multiplies each cost by the number of folds, since the search reruns inside every fold.

## Trade-offs

Hastie, Tibshirani and Tibshirani compared best subset, forward stepwise, the lasso and the relaxed lasso on simulated
regressions over a range of <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratios</Gloss> <Cite id="hastie2020best" />. Their
findings:

- **The signal-to-noise ratio decides.** Best subset and the lasso each win in a different regime. At high
  signal-to-noise ratios best subset does better, because its kept coefficients are unshrunk. At low ratios the lasso
  does better, because its shrinkage reduces variance where the coefficients are hard to estimate.
- **Forward stepwise is close to best subset.** The exact search buys little over the greedy one on most problems.
- **The relaxed lasso** refits the lasso's selected set with a blend of the lasso and least-squares coefficients. It
  matched or beat the others across nearly all settings.

The penalty view explains the pattern. The $\ell_0$ penalty selects and leaves the survivors alone; the $\ell_1$
penalty selects and shrinks. Shrinkage helps when the noise is large relative to the effects.

## Pitfalls

- **Inference after selection.** The $p$-values and <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>
  that least-squares software reports for the final subset assume the subset was fixed in advance. After a search they
  are too small. In Freedman's simulation, 50 pure-noise predictors were screened on 100 rows and the survivors
  refitted; the refitted model had a highly significant overall $F$ test although no predictor was related to the target
  <Cite id="freedman1983" />.
- **Instability.** Small changes in the data change which subset is best, especially among correlated predictors,
  whose subsets fit almost equally well. The chosen predictors are a fragile answer to which predictors matter (see
  <NoteLink to="stability-of-feature-selection">stability of feature selection</NoteLink>).
- **Searching outside the folds.** Running the search on all the data and then cross-validating the chosen subset
  gives an optimistic estimate, often badly so when $p$ is large (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
- **Counting $k$ as the degrees of freedom.** The search spends more than $k$, as above, so $C_p$ and AIC computed
  with $k$ favour models that are too large.
- **Standardise for stagewise.** Forward stagewise and the lasso compare correlations, so predictors must be on a
  common scale. Best subset and stepwise least squares are unaffected by the scale of a predictor.
