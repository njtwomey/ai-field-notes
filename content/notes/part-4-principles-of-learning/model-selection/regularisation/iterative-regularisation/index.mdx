<Definition>
Let $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ have <Gloss name="svd">singular value decomposition</Gloss>
$\Xmat = \Umat\Dmat\Vmat^\top$ with singular values $d_1 \ge \dots \ge d_r > 0$, and let $z_j = \uvec_j^\top\yvec$. An
iterative solver for least squares started at $\wvec_0 = \zeros$ and stopped after $t$ steps is **iterative
regularisation**: the iterate $\wvec_t$ is the estimate, and the number of steps $t$ is the regularisation parameter.
For the linear iterations, such as <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>, the iterate is a
**spectral filter** of the least-squares solution,

$$
\wvec_t = \sum_{j=1}^r \phi_j(t)\,\frac{z_j}{d_j}\,\vvec_j, \qquad 0 \le \phi_j(t) \le 1,
$$

with **filter factors** $\phi_j(t)$ that rise towards 1 as $t$ grows, fastest for the largest $d_j$
<Cite id="engl1996" /> <Cite id="hansen1998" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The least-squares solution $\sum_j (z_j/d_j)\vvec_j$ divides each data coordinate by its singular value. Noise is spread
evenly over the coordinates, so in a direction with a small $d_j$ the noise is amplified by $1/d_j$. When the singular
values decay, those directions dominate the least-squares solution with noise.

An iterative solver started at zero recovers the directions in order of their singular values. Gradient descent on
$\frac12\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ moves fastest along directions of high curvature $d_j^2$, so it fits the
well-determined directions in a few steps and the noisy ones only after many. Stopping in between keeps the signal and
leaves most of the amplified noise out. The number of steps does what $\lambda$ does in
<NoteLink to="tikhonov-regularisation">Tikhonov regularisation</NoteLink>.

The error to the true coefficients therefore first falls, while the signal directions are fitted, and then rises, while
the noise directions are. This behaviour is called **semiconvergence**. The iteration converges, but to the
unregularised solution; the useful estimate lies on the way.

## Landweber iteration

Landweber's iteration is gradient descent on $\frac12\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ with a fixed step $\omega$
<Cite id="landweber1951" />:

$$
\wvec_{t+1} = \wvec_t + \omega\,\Xmat^\top(\yvec - \Xmat\wvec_t), \qquad \wvec_0 = \zeros.
$$

The filter factors follow from the iteration in singular coordinates. Let $c_j(t) = \vvec_j^\top\wvec_t$. Since
$\vvec_j^\top\Xmat^\top = d_j\uvec_j^\top$ and $\uvec_j^\top\Xmat\wvec = d_jc_j$,

$$
c_j(t + 1) = c_j(t) + \omega d_j\paren{z_j - d_jc_j(t)}.
$$

Subtracting the fixed point $z_j/d_j$ from both sides gives

$$
c_j(t + 1) - \frac{z_j}{d_j} = (1 - \omega d_j^2)\paren{c_j(t) - \frac{z_j}{d_j}}.
$$

From $c_j(0) = 0$ the gap starts at $-z_j/d_j$ and is multiplied by $1 - \omega d_j^2$ at every step, so

$$
c_j(t) = \brack{1 - (1 - \omega d_j^2)^t}\frac{z_j}{d_j},
\qquad
\phi_j^{\text{Lw}}(t) = 1 - (1 - \omega d_j^2)^t.
$$

The iteration converges when $\abs{1 - \omega d_j^2} < 1$ for every $j$, that is for $0 < \omega < 2/d_1^2$. Each
direction's gap shrinks geometrically at its own rate $1 - \omega d_j^2$: fast for large $d_j$, slowly for small.
Components in the null space of $\Xmat$ are never touched, which is why the limit is the minimum-norm least-squares
solution (see <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink>).

The iterate $\wvec_t$ is linear in $\yvec$, so the fit $\Xmat\wvec_t = \Smat_t\yvec$ is a linear smoother with
$\Smat_t = \Umat\,\diag(\phi_j(t))\,\Umat^\top$. Its <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of
freedom</NoteLink> are $\operatorname{df}(t) = \sum_j \phi_j(t)$, rising from 0 at $t = 0$ to the rank $r$.
<Gloss name="gcv" />, $C_p$ and leave-one-out can choose $t$ exactly as they choose $\lambda$.

## Spectral filters

Many regularisers are spectral filters of the least-squares solution, differing only in $\phi_j$:

| Method | Filter factor $\phi_j$ | Parameter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Truncated SVD | $1$ if $j \le k$, else $0$ | rank $k$ |
| Tikhonov, ridge | $d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda)$ | penalty $\lambda$ |
| Landweber, gradient descent | $1 - (1 - \omega d_j^2)^t$ | steps $t$ |
| Gradient flow | $1 - e^{-d_j^2\tau}$ | time $\tau$ |

**Steps against penalty.** For a small step, $(1 - \omega d_j^2)^t \approx e^{-\omega d_j^2t}$, so Landweber behaves
like gradient flow run for time $\tau = \omega t$. Compare the two ends of the spectrum with Tikhonov at
$\lambda = 1/\tau$, writing $x = d_j^2\tau$:

- **Weak directions, $x \ll 1$.** Gradient flow keeps $1 - e^{-x} \approx x$ and Tikhonov keeps
  $x/(1 + x) \approx x$. They agree, with $\lambda = 1/(\omega t)$.
- **Strong directions, $x \gg 1$.** Gradient flow leaves out $e^{-x}$ and Tikhonov leaves out $1/(1 + x)$. Gradient flow
  passes strong directions almost unchanged, so it acts more like truncated SVD, a sharp cut-off, than like ridge's
  gradual one.

The <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> note tabulates the same comparison for a neural network's
quadratic approximation.

**Conjugate gradients.** The conjugate-gradient method applied to the normal equations (CGLS) is also a semiconvergent
iteration, and it reaches a useful iterate in far fewer steps than Landweber. Its iterate is the best fit within a
Krylov subspace that depends on $\yvec$, so its filter factors depend on the data and it is not a linear smoother
<Cite id="hansen1998" /> (see <NoteLink to="iterative-linear-solvers">iterative linear solvers</NoteLink>).

**Boosting.** $L_2$ boosting fits a base learner to the residuals over and over. With a linear base learner whose
smoother $\Smat$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $s_j \in [0, 1]$, the fit after $m$ rounds is
$\paren{\eye - (\eye - \Smat)^m}\yvec$, a spectral filter with factors $1 - (1 - s_j)^m$. The number of rounds is the
regularisation parameter, and Bühlmann and Yu chose it with a corrected <Gloss name="aic" /> from the trace of that
operator <Cite id="buhlmann2003" /> (see <NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">gradient boosting</NoteLink>). Forward
stagewise regression is the same idea with one coordinate moved per step (see
<NoteLink to="best-subset-and-stepwise-selection">best subset and stepwise selection</NoteLink>).

## Gradient flow and ridge

The filter factors say that early stopping and ridge are similar. Ali, Kolter and Tibshirani made the similarity
quantitative <Cite id="ali2019" />. Take $\yvec = \Xmat\wvec^\star + \varepsilonvec$ with noise of variance $\sigma^2$
and any fixed $\Xmat$. Compare gradient flow at time $\tau$ with ridge at $\lambda = 1/\tau$ (in the scaling
$\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$ for ridge and $\frac12\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ for the flow).

In singular coordinates, a spectral filter's estimation risk splits into one bias and one variance term per direction:

$$
\expect\norm{\wvec_\phi - \wvec^\star}^2 = \sum_j \brack{(1 - \phi_j)^2\paren{\vvec_j^\top\wvec^\star}^2
+ \phi_j^2\frac{\sigma^2}{d_j^2}}.
$$

The bias term comes from the fraction $1 - \phi_j$ of the true coordinate that the filter leaves out; the variance term
from the noise $\uvec_j^\top\varepsilonvec$, of variance $\sigma^2$, divided by $d_j$ and kept in the fraction $\phi_j$.
Compare the two filters term by term, with $x = d_j^2\tau$.

- **Bias.** Gradient flow leaves out $e^{-x}$ and ridge $1/(1 + x)$. Since $e^x \ge 1 + x$, $e^{-x} \le 1/(1 + x)$, so
  gradient flow's bias term is never larger.
- **Variance.** The ratio of the variance terms is
  $\paren{(1 - e^{-x})(1 + x)/x}^2$. It is 1 as $x \to 0$ and as $x \to \infty$, and its maximum, near $x = 1.79$, is
  about 1.686.

Every term of gradient flow's risk is therefore at most 1.686 times the matching term of ridge's, and so is the sum. Ali
et al. state the bound as 1.6862 for the estimation risk and the in-sample prediction risk, for every $\wvec^\star$ and
every design. When both methods are tuned optimally and the coefficients follow a spherical prior, the ratio of their
best risks lies between 1 and 1.2147 <Cite id="ali2019" />. Early stopping is never much worse than ridge at its best,
and it costs no more than the iterations it runs.

## Choosing when to stop

Iterative regularisation needs a stopping rule, the counterpart of
<NoteLink to="choosing-the-regularisation-strength">choosing the regularisation strength</NoteLink>.

- **Validation.** Track the error on held-out data and keep the best iterate. This is the standard practice for
  neural networks (see <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink>).
- **The discrepancy principle.** Stop at the first $t$ with $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec_t} \le \tau\delta$, where $\delta$
  is the norm of the noise and $\tau$ is a safety factor slightly above 1 <Cite id="morozov1966" />
  <Cite id="engl1996" />. The residual falls monotonically along Landweber iterations, so the rule stops at a
  well-defined step, and no fit beyond it is needed.
- **Criteria on the degrees of freedom.** For a linear iteration, GCV or $C_p$ with $\operatorname{df}(t) =
  \sum_j\phi_j(t)$.
- **Rules from complexity.** For gradient descent in a <Gloss name="rkhs">reproducing kernel Hilbert space</Gloss>,
  stopping when a local empirical complexity of the kernel class matches the step count gives minimax-optimal rates
  without held-out data <Cite id="raskutti2014" />. Yao, Rosasco and Caponnetto gave stopping times as functions of the
  sample size that make kernel gradient descent consistent <Cite id="yao2007" />.

## Worked example

Take three directions with singular values $\dvec = (3, 1, 0.3)$, true coefficients $(1, 1, 1)$ in the right singular
basis, and noise $(0.1, -0.1, 0.2)$ along the left singular vectors, of norm $\delta = 0.245$. The data coordinates are
$z_j = d_j + e_j = (3.1, 0.9, 0.5)$, and the least-squares solution is $z_j/d_j = (1.033, 0.900, 1.667)$. Its error is
$\norm{(0.033, -0.100, 0.667)} = 0.675$, almost all of it from the weakest direction, where noise 0.2 is divided by 0.3.

Run Landweber with $\omega = 0.1$, inside the limit $2/d_1^2 = 0.22$. The contraction factors are
$1 - \omega d_j^2 = (0.1, 0.9, 0.991)$.

1. **After 10 steps** the filter factors are $(1 - 0.1^{10}, 1 - 0.9^{10}, 1 - 0.991^{10}) = (1.000, 0.651, 0.086)$.
   Tikhonov at $\lambda = 1/(\omega t) = 1$ gives $(0.900, 0.500, 0.083)$. The weakest direction is filtered almost
   identically; the strongest is passed fully by Landweber and shrunk 10% by Tikhonov.
2. **The error along the iteration.** With $\norm{\wvec_t - \wvec^\star}$ and the residual $\norm{\yvec -
   \Xmat\wvec_t}$:

   | $t$ | 0 | 10 | 50 | 80 | 101 | 150 | 200 | 1000 |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
   | error | 1.732 | 0.951 | 0.409 | 0.177 | 0.105 | 0.260 | 0.407 | 0.675 |
   | residual | 3.266 | 0.554 | 0.318 | 0.243 | 0.201 | 0.129 | 0.082 | 0.000 |

   The error falls to 0.105 at $t = 101$ and climbs back to the least-squares 0.675. The residual falls throughout,
   so it cannot show where to stop on its own.
3. **The discrepancy principle** with $\tau = 1$ stops at the first step whose residual is below $\delta = 0.245$:
   $t = 79$, with error 0.183. It stops a little early, which costs 0.08 against the best step.
4. **Tikhonov's best** on the same data is at $\lambda = 0.058$ with error 0.152. Landweber's best step does better
   here, because it passes the first two directions almost unchanged while Tikhonov shrinks them.

## Pitfalls

- **The step limit.** Landweber diverges for $\omega \ge 2/d_1^2$. The default $\omega = 1/d_1^2$ is safe and needs an
  estimate of the largest singular value, which a few power iterations give.
- **Slow progress in weak directions.** A direction with $\omega d_j^2 = 10^{-4}$ needs about $10^4$ steps to be fitted.
  When the right amount of regularisation keeps such directions, Landweber is slow, and conjugate gradients or a direct
  Tikhonov solve are cheaper.
- **The schedule changes the filter.** The filter factors above assume a constant step and a start at zero. A decaying
  step, momentum or an adaptive optimiser such as Adam gives different filters, and a nonzero start keeps its
  component in the null space of $\Xmat$.
- **Conjugate gradients overshoot fast.** Because CGLS converges quickly, its semiconvergence minimum is sharp, and
  running a few steps past it loses much of the benefit. A stopping rule matters more than for Landweber.
- **Selection by validation is still selection.** Choosing the best of many iterates on a validation set makes that
  validation error optimistic for the chosen iterate (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
