<Definition>
Let $L(\wvec) \ge 0$ be a training loss, such as the residual sum of squares $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$, and let
$\Omega(\wvec) \ge 0$ be a regulariser, such as $\norm{\wvec}_2^2$ or $\norm{\wvec}_1$. Regularisation can be posed in
three forms:

- **Tikhonov (penalised) form:** minimise $L(\wvec) + \lambda\,\Omega(\wvec)$ for a penalty weight $\lambda \ge 0$;
- **Ivanov (constrained) form:** minimise $L(\wvec)$ subject to $\Omega(\wvec) \le t$ for a budget $t \ge 0$;
- **Morozov (discrepancy) form:** minimise $\Omega(\wvec)$ subject to $L(\wvec) \le \delta$ for a loss level
  $\delta \ge 0$.

When $L$ and $\Omega$ are convex, every solution of one form solves the other two for matching values of the
parameters <Cite id="oneto2016" />. The matching values depend on the data.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A regularised fit balances two quantities: how well $\wvec$ fits the training data, measured by $L$, and how complex
$\wvec$ is, measured by $\Omega$. There are three ways to state the balance. One can charge a price $\lambda$ per unit
of complexity and minimise the total bill. One can fix a complexity budget $t$ and fit as well as the budget allows.
One can fix an acceptable training loss $\delta$ and find the simplest $\wvec$ that reaches it.

The three statements describe the same set of compromises. Each compromise is a point on the curve of best achievable
pairs $(\Omega, L)$. A price $\lambda$ picks the point where the curve has slope $-\lambda$. A budget picks the point at
$\Omega = t$. A loss level picks the point at $L = \delta$. Moving the parameter of any one form moves the solution
along the same curve.

The forms differ in what their parameter means, and so in how it is chosen. The three names come from the Soviet school
of <NoteLink to="tikhonov-regularisation">ill-posed problems</NoteLink>: Tikhonov's penalised form
<Cite id="tikhonov1963" />, Ivanov's quasi-solutions under a norm bound <Cite id="ivanov1962" /> and Morozov's
discrepancy principle <Cite id="morozov1966" />.

- **$\lambda$** has no meaning outside the optimisation. Its scale changes with the units of $L$. It is chosen by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or another estimate of prediction error.
- **$t$** has the units of $\Omega(\wvec)$. It is the form that <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">learning
  theory</NoteLink> analyses: generalisation bounds hold for a fixed ball $\set{\wvec : \norm{\wvec} \le t}$, and
  <NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural risk minimisation</NoteLink> chooses among nested balls.
- **$\delta$** has the units of the loss. When the noise level is known, a fit should leave residuals about as large as
  the noise. For least squares with $n$ observations of noise variance $\sigma^2$, that gives
  $\delta \approx n\sigma^2$: the discrepancy principle (see
  <NoteLink to="choosing-the-regularisation-strength">choosing the regularisation strength</NoteLink>).

## The three forms

Write $\hat\wvec_\lambda$ for a minimiser of the penalised objective $L + \lambda\Omega$. As $\lambda$ grows, the fit
trades training loss for simplicity. The trade is monotone.

**Claim.** If $0 \le \lambda_1 < \lambda_2$, then $\Omega(\hat\wvec_{\lambda_2}) \le \Omega(\hat\wvec_{\lambda_1})$
and $L(\hat\wvec_{\lambda_2}) \ge L(\hat\wvec_{\lambda_1})$.

Write $\Omega_k = \Omega(\hat\wvec_{\lambda_k})$ and $L_k = L(\hat\wvec_{\lambda_k})$. Each $\hat\wvec_{\lambda_k}$
minimises its own objective, so it does at least as well there as the other solution:

$$
L_1 + \lambda_1\Omega_1 \le L_2 + \lambda_1\Omega_2,
\qquad
L_2 + \lambda_2\Omega_2 \le L_1 + \lambda_2\Omega_1.
$$

Adding the two inequalities cancels the losses and leaves $\lambda_1\Omega_1 + \lambda_2\Omega_2 \le \lambda_1\Omega_2 +
\lambda_2\Omega_1$, that is $(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)(\Omega_2 - \Omega_1) \le 0$. Since $\lambda_2 > \lambda_1$,
$\Omega_2 \le \Omega_1$. The first inequality then gives $L_1 - L_2 \le \lambda_1(\Omega_2 - \Omega_1) \le 0$, so
$L_2 \ge L_1$. The argument uses no convexity.

The claim defines two maps from the penalty to the other parameters,

$$
t(\lambda) = \Omega(\hat\wvec_\lambda), \qquad \delta(\lambda) = L(\hat\wvec_\lambda).
$$

A larger price buys a smaller budget and accepts a larger loss. At $\lambda = 0$ the budget is the complexity of the
unregularised fit; as $\lambda \to \infty$ the budget falls to $\min\Omega$, which is 0 for a norm.

## Their equivalence

The equivalence has an easy direction, which holds for any $L$ and $\Omega$, and a hard direction, which needs
convexity.

**Penalised solutions are constrained solutions.** Let $\lambda > 0$ and $t = t(\lambda)$. Suppose some $\wvec$ had
$\Omega(\wvec) \le t$ and $L(\wvec) < L(\hat\wvec_\lambda)$. Then
$L(\wvec) + \lambda\Omega(\wvec) < L(\hat\wvec_\lambda) + \lambda\Omega(\hat\wvec_\lambda)$, which contradicts the
optimality of $\hat\wvec_\lambda$. So $\hat\wvec_\lambda$ solves the Ivanov problem with budget $t(\lambda)$. The same
argument with the roles of $L$ and $\Omega$ swapped shows that $\hat\wvec_\lambda$ solves the Morozov problem with level
$\delta(\lambda)$: any $\wvec$ with $L(\wvec) \le \delta(\lambda)$ and $\Omega(\wvec) < \Omega(\hat\wvec_\lambda)$ would
again lower the penalised objective, because $\lambda > 0$.

**Constrained solutions are penalised solutions, for convex problems.** Let $L$ and $\Omega$ be convex, and let
$\hat\wvec$ solve the Ivanov problem with a budget $t > \min\Omega$, so that some $\wvec$ satisfies
$\Omega(\wvec) < t$ strictly. This is Slater's condition, and it gives
<NoteLink to="lagrangian-duality">strong duality</NoteLink>. The <Gloss name="kkt" /> conditions then hold at
$\hat\wvec$ with a multiplier $\lambda \ge 0$:

$$
\zeros \in \partial L(\hat\wvec) + \lambda\,\partial\Omega(\hat\wvec),
\qquad
\lambda\paren{\Omega(\hat\wvec) - t} = 0,
$$

where $\partial$ denotes the set of <NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradients</NoteLink>. The first condition says that
zero is a subgradient of $L + \lambda\Omega$ at $\hat\wvec$. For a convex function that is the condition for a global
minimum, so $\hat\wvec$ minimises the penalised objective with this $\lambda$. The second condition, complementary
slackness, sets $\lambda = 0$ when the constraint is slack: a budget larger than the unregularised fit needs costs
nothing. The Morozov form reduces the same way. Its Lagrangian is $\Omega + \mu(L - \delta)$ with a multiplier
$\mu \ge 0$, and when $\mu > 0$ its minimiser is the penalised solution with $\lambda = 1/\mu$.

**The multiplier is a price.** Let $p(t)$ be the smallest loss the Ivanov problem achieves with budget $t$. The
multiplier of the constraint is the rate at which the optimal loss falls as the budget grows,

$$
\lambda = -\frac{dp}{dt},
$$

wherever $p$ is differentiable (see <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multipliers</NoteLink>, where the
multiplier is a sensitivity). A large $\lambda$ means a small budget, where one more unit of
norm would lower the training loss a lot. A $\lambda$ near 0 means the budget barely binds.

## The geometry of L1 and L2 balls

The constrained form gives the picture behind sparsity. The Ivanov solution is the point of the ball
$\set{\wvec : \Omega(\wvec) \le t}$ where the smallest level set of $L$ first touches it. At that point the KKT
condition says $-\nabla L(\hat\wvec) \in \lambda\,\partial\Omega(\hat\wvec)$ for a smooth loss: the downhill direction
of the loss must lie in the **normal cone** of the ball, the set of directions that point straight out of it.

- **The $L^2$ ball** is round. At each boundary point the normal cone is a single ray, along $\hat\wvec$ itself. A
  boundary point on a coordinate axis has its ray along that axis, and only loss gradients pointing exactly that way
  land there. Such gradients form a set of measure zero, so ridge solutions almost never have a zero coefficient.
- **The $L^1$ ball** $\set{\wvec : \norm{\wvec}_1 \le t}$ is a cross-polytope. At the vertex $t\evec_1$ the
  subdifferential of $\norm{\wvec}_1$ is $\set{(1, s_2, \dots, s_p) : s_j \in [-1, 1]}$, so the normal cone is the
  full-dimensional set of directions $\gvec$ with $g_1 \ge \abs{g_j}$ for every $j$. An open set of loss gradients
  lands on the vertex. Sparsity is therefore generic: it survives small changes to the data.
- **$L^q$ balls with $q < 1$** are not convex and are spikier still. Their vertices attract even more gradients, but
  the hard direction of the equivalence fails (see the pitfalls).
- **The $L^\infty$ ball** is a cube. Its vertices have every $\abs{w_j}$ equal to $t$, so it pulls coefficients to a
  common magnitude rather than to zero.

The <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> note draws the contact of the squared-error ellipses with the $L^1$ diamond
for two coefficients. <NoteLink to="sparsity-inducing-penalties">Sparsity-inducing penalties</NoteLink> follows the
same geometry to non-convex balls, and <NoteLink to="structured-sparsity-penalties">structured sparsity
penalties</NoteLink> to balls whose corners are whole groups or low-rank matrices.

## Worked example

Take an orthonormal design, $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = \eye$, with two coefficients. The least-squares coefficients are
$\zvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec$, and the residual sum of squares is $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 = c + \norm{\wvec - \zvec}^2$
with a constant $c = \norm{\yvec}^2 - \norm{\zvec}^2$. Drop $c$ and use $L(\wvec) = \norm{\wvec - \zvec}^2$ with
$\zvec = (3, 1)$.

**Ridge.** With $\Omega = \norm{\wvec}_2^2$ the penalised solution is $\hat\wvec_\lambda = \zvec/(1 + \lambda)$. At
$\lambda = 1$ it is $(1.5, 0.5)$, with budget $t = 1.5^2 + 0.5^2 = 2.5$ and loss $\delta = 1.5^2 + 0.5^2 = 2.5$.

1. *Ivanov with $t = 2.5$.* The closest point to $\zvec$ in the disc $\norm{\wvec}_2^2 \le 2.5$ lies on the segment from
   the origin to $\zvec$, at radius $\sqrt{2.5} = 1.581$. Since $\norm{\zvec} = \sqrt{10} = 3.162$, it is
   $\zvec \times 1.581/3.162 = 0.5\,\zvec = (1.5, 0.5)$.
2. *Morozov with $\delta = 2.5$.* The point of the disc $\norm{\wvec - \zvec}^2 \le 2.5$ nearest the origin lies on the
   same segment, at distance $\sqrt{2.5}$ from $\zvec$: $\zvec(1 - 1.581/3.162) = (1.5, 0.5)$.
3. *The price.* The best loss under budget $t$ is $p(t) = (\sqrt{10} - \sqrt t)^2$, so
   $dp/dt = -(\sqrt{10} - \sqrt t)/\sqrt t$. At $t = 2.5$ this is $-(3.162 - 1.581)/1.581 = -1.0$, which is $-\lambda$.

**Lasso.** With $\Omega = \norm{\wvec}_1$, minimising $(w_j - z_j)^2 + \lambda\abs{w_j}$ in each coordinate gives
soft thresholding at $\lambda/2$ (derived in <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>). At $\lambda = 2$
the solution is $(3 - 1, \max(1 - 1, 0)) = (2, 0)$, with budget $t = 2$ and loss $\delta = 1^2 + 1^2 = 2$.

1. *Ivanov with $t = 2$.* Projecting $\zvec$ onto the diamond $\abs{w_1} + \abs{w_2} \le 2$ subtracts a common
   amount $\theta$ from both coordinates and clips at zero, with $\theta$ set so that the result has $L^1$ norm 2.
   With $\theta = 1$ the result is $(2, 0)$, of norm 2.
2. *Morozov with $\delta = 2$.* The smallest diamond $\abs{w_1} + \abs{w_2} = s$ that touches the disc
   $\norm{\wvec - \zvec}^2 \le 2$ has its edge $w_1 + w_2 = s$ at distance $\sqrt 2$ from $\zvec$. That distance is
   $(3 + 1 - s)/\sqrt 2$, so $s = 2$, and the contact point is $\zvec - (1, 1) = (2, 0)$: a vertex of the diamond.
3. *The price.* For budgets $t \le 2$ only the first coordinate is active, the best point is $(t, 0)$ and
   $p(t) = (3 - t)^2 + 1$. Then $dp/dt = -2(3 - t) = -2$ at $t = 2$, which is $-\lambda$.

**The map depends on the data.** Change the data to $\zvec' = (4, 1)$ and keep $\lambda = 2$. The lasso now gives
$(3, 0)$, with budget $t = 3$ instead of 2. The same penalty is a different constraint on a different sample.

## Assumptions

- **Convexity for the hard direction.** Penalised solutions solve the constrained problems for any $L$ and $\Omega$.
  The converse needs $L$ and $\Omega$ convex and a strictly feasible point.
- **A strictly feasible point.** The Ivanov problem needs $t > \min\Omega$ and the Morozov problem needs
  $\delta > \min L$. At the boundary the multiplier can be infinite: $t = 0$ forces $\wvec = \zeros$, which is
  $\lambda = \infty$.
- **Unique solutions for a clean map.** When $L + \lambda\Omega$ has several minimisers, as the lasso can when
  $p > n$, $t(\lambda)$ can take several values and the correspondence is between sets of solutions.

## Pitfalls

- **A fixed $\lambda$ is not a fixed constraint across resamples.** The maps $t(\lambda)$ and $\delta(\lambda)$ are
  computed from the sample. In $K$-fold <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> each fold's fit
  with the same $\lambda$ has a different budget. Cross-validating over $\lambda$ and over $t$ therefore pick different
  models. Tibshirani's original lasso used the relative budget $s = t/\norm{\hat\wvec_{\text{OLS}}}_1$
  <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />; glmnet and scikit-learn use $\lambda$.
- **Non-convex penalties break the equivalence.** With $\Omega(\wvec) = \norm{\wvec}_0$, the number of non-zero
  coefficients, the Ivanov form with $t = k$ is best-subset selection of size $k$ (see
  <NoteLink to="best-subset-and-stepwise-selection">best subset and stepwise selection</NoteLink>). The penalised form
  minimises $\operatorname{RSS}_k + \lambda k$ over $k$, where $\operatorname{RSS}_k$ is the residual sum of squares of
  the best subset of size $k$. It can only reach sizes on the lower convex hull of the points
  $(k, \operatorname{RSS}_k)$. If the best subsets of sizes 0 to 3 have $\operatorname{RSS} = 10, 6, 5, 1$, size 2 is
  never chosen: the slope from size 1 to size 3 is $-2.5$, steeper than the slope $-1$ from size 1 to size 2, so every
  $\lambda$ that prefers size 2 to size 1 prefers size 3 to size 2.
- **Software scales the loss differently.** Writing the loss as $\frac{1}{2n}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ instead of
  $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ divides the matching $\lambda$ by $2n$ and leaves $t$ unchanged. A budget is portable
  between conventions; a penalty weight is not.
- **The penalised form is the one to compute.** It is unconstrained, so <NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">coordinate
  descent</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="proximal-gradient-methods">proximal gradient methods</NoteLink> apply directly.
  The constrained form needs a projection onto the ball at every step, and the Morozov form needs a search over
  $\lambda$ until the loss hits $\delta$.
