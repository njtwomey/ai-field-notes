<Definition>
A **sparsity-inducing penalty** is a regulariser $\sum_j p_\lambda(\abs{w_j})$ whose per-coefficient term
$p_\lambda(\theta)$, $\theta \ge 0$, has a strictly positive slope at $\theta = 0$, so that penalised estimates contain
exact zeros. The main members are the $L^0$ count, the $L^1$ norm of the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink>, the
bridge penalties $\lambda\theta^q$ with $0 < q < 1$ <Cite id="frank1993" />, the smoothly clipped absolute deviation
(SCAD) <Cite id="fan2001" /> and the minimax concave penalty (MCP) <Cite id="zhang2010" />. For orthonormal features,
each penalty acts on each least-squares coefficient $z$ through a **thresholding rule** $\hat w = \Theta_\lambda(z)$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A penalty decides how much a coefficient must earn in fit before it is allowed to be non-zero, and how much it is taxed
once it is. Ridge's penalty $\lambda w^2$ has zero slope at the origin. A coefficient pays almost nothing for being
slightly non-zero, so no coefficient is ever set exactly to zero. A penalty with a positive slope at the origin charges
a fixed price for the first unit of size, and a coefficient whose evidence is weaker than that price stays at zero. That
is the source of sparsity, seen in the geometry of
<NoteLink to="penalised-and-constrained-regularisation">penalised and constrained regularisation</NoteLink> as the
corners of the constraint ball.

The $L^1$ penalty keeps charging the same slope $\lambda$ for every unit of size. A large coefficient, whose existence
is not in doubt, is still pulled towards zero by $\lambda$. Fan and Li asked for three properties of a good penalty
<Cite id="fan2001" />:

- **sparsity:** small coefficients are set to zero;
- **near-unbiasedness:** large coefficients are left almost unchanged;
- **continuity:** the estimate changes continuously with the data, so a small change in the data cannot make a
  coefficient jump.

No convex penalty has all three. $L^1$ has sparsity and continuity. The $L^0$ count has sparsity and unbiasedness. SCAD
and MCP get all three by letting the slope of the penalty fall to zero for large coefficients, which makes the penalty
concave and the objective non-convex.

## Thresholding rules

With an orthonormal design, $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = \eye$, the problem separates. With least-squares coefficients
$\zvec = \Xmat^\top\yvec$ and the loss written as $\frac12\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$, each coefficient solves

$$
\hat w_j = \argmin_{w}\ \tfrac12(z_j - w)^2 + p_\lambda(\abs{w}).
$$

Its solution $\Theta_\lambda(z_j)$ is the thresholding rule of the penalty. With correlated features,
<NoteLink to="coordinate-descent">coordinate descent</NoteLink> applies the same rule to one coefficient at a time,
with $z_j$ replaced by the least-squares coefficient of feature $j$ on the current partial residual
<Cite id="breheny2011" />. The rule therefore describes each penalty completely.

**$L^1$: soft thresholding.** With $p_\lambda(\theta) = \lambda\theta$ the solution is
$S_\lambda(z) = \sgn(z)\max(\abs z - \lambda, 0)$, derived in the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> note.
Coefficients with $\abs z \le \lambda$ become zero, and every other coefficient moves towards zero by exactly
$\lambda$.

**$L^0$: hard thresholding.** With $p_\lambda(\theta) = \frac{\lambda^2}{2}\indicator[\theta \ne 0]$, a coefficient is
either zero, costing $\frac12 z^2$ in fit, or free to equal $z$, costing $\frac{\lambda^2}{2}$ in penalty. It is kept
when $\frac12 z^2 > \frac{\lambda^2}{2}$:

$$
H_\lambda(z) = z\,\indicator[\abs z > \lambda].
$$

Kept coefficients are not shrunk at all, but the estimate jumps from 0 to $\pm\lambda$ as $\abs z$ crosses $\lambda$.

**Bridge, $0 < q < 1$.** With $p_\lambda(\theta) = \lambda\theta^q$ the rule shrinks large coefficients by
$\lambda q\abs{w}^{q - 1}$, which vanishes as $\abs w$ grows. Like $L^0$ it jumps: for $q = \frac12$ and $\lambda = 1$
the estimate is 0 for $\abs z \le 1.5$ and jumps to $\pm 1$ beyond (derivation below).

**MCP: firm thresholding.** The minimax concave penalty is

$$
p_\lambda(\theta) = \begin{cases} \lambda\theta - \dfrac{\theta^2}{2\gamma} & \theta \le \gamma\lambda, \\[4pt]
\tfrac12\gamma\lambda^2 & \theta > \gamma\lambda, \end{cases}
\qquad \gamma > 1.
$$

Its slope $p_\lambda'(\theta) = (\lambda - \theta/\gamma)_+$ starts at $\lambda$, like $L^1$, and falls linearly to
zero at $\theta = \gamma\lambda$. For $0 < w \le \gamma\lambda$ the scalar objective has derivative
$w - z + \lambda - w/\gamma$, which vanishes at $w = \gamma(z - \lambda)/(\gamma - 1)$. That point lies in the range
exactly when $z \le \gamma\lambda$. Beyond it the penalty is flat and $\hat w = z$. So

$$
\Theta^{\text{MCP}}_\lambda(z) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}S_\lambda(z) & \abs z \le \gamma\lambda,
\\[4pt] z & \abs z > \gamma\lambda. \end{cases}
$$

The rule is continuous: at $\abs z = \gamma\lambda$ both pieces equal $z$. It is zero below $\lambda$, unbiased above
$\gamma\lambda$, and linear in between. As $\gamma \to \infty$ it becomes soft thresholding; as $\gamma \to 1$ it
becomes hard thresholding. Zhang chose this penalty as the one that is least concave among penalties with these
properties, hence "minimax concave" <Cite id="zhang2010" />.

**SCAD.** The smoothly clipped absolute deviation has slope
$p_\lambda'(\theta) = \lambda$ for $\theta \le \lambda$, $(a\lambda - \theta)/(a - 1)$ for
$\lambda < \theta \le a\lambda$, and 0 beyond, with $a > 2$. Its rule is soft thresholding for $\abs z \le 2\lambda$,
the identity for $\abs z > a\lambda$, and the linear interpolation
$\paren{(a - 1)z - \sgn(z)a\lambda}/(a - 2)$ in between. Fan and Li suggest $a = 3.7$ <Cite id="fan2001" />.

<Derivation title="The jump of the bridge rule">
For $w > 0$ the scalar objective is $h(w) = \frac12(z - w)^2 + \lambda w^q$. At the threshold value $\tau$ of $z$, the
non-zero local minimum $w^\star$ ties with $w = 0$. Two conditions hold there.

1. Stationarity: $h'(w^\star) = 0$, so $z = w^\star + \lambda q\,(w^\star)^{q - 1}$.
2. The tie: $h(w^\star) = h(0) = \frac12 z^2$. Expanding, $\frac12(w^\star)^2 - zw^\star + \lambda(w^\star)^q = 0$, and
   dividing by $w^\star$ gives $z = \frac12 w^\star + \lambda(w^\star)^{q - 1}$.

Equating the two expressions for $z$ gives $\frac12 w^\star = \lambda(1 - q)(w^\star)^{q - 1}$, so

$$
w^\star = \paren{2\lambda(1 - q)}^{1/(2 - q)}, \qquad \tau = w^\star + \lambda q\,(w^\star)^{q - 1}.
$$

For $q = \frac12$ and $\lambda = 1$: $w^\star = 1^{2/3} = 1$ and $\tau = 1 + \frac12 = 1.5$. The estimate is 0 for
$z \le 1.5$ and jumps to 1 at $z = 1.5$. The limits check the formula: as $q \to 1$, $w^\star \to 0$ and
$\tau \to \lambda$, the soft threshold with no jump; as $q \to 0$, $\tau \to \sqrt{2\lambda}$, the hard threshold of the
penalty $\lambda\indicator[\theta \neq 0]$.
</Derivation>

## L0 and L1

The $L^0$ penalty counts non-zero coefficients. Penalising or constraining it is
<NoteLink to="best-subset-and-stepwise-selection">best-subset selection</NoteLink>: for each size $k$, find the $k$
features whose least-squares fit has the smallest residual sum of squares. In general this is NP-hard
<Cite id="natarajan1995" />, and exhaustive search over $2^p$ subsets is limited to a few dozen features. With an
orthonormal design it is easy: the best subset of size $k$ keeps the $k$ largest $\abs{z_j}$, and the penalised form is
hard thresholding.

The $L^1$ norm is the tightest convex function below the $L^0$ count on the box $\norm{\wvec}_\infty \le 1$, its convex
envelope. Replacing $L^0$ by $L^1$ turns a combinatorial search into a convex problem with a global minimum, a
continuous path in $\lambda$ and fast solvers <Cite id="tibshirani1996" />. Under conditions on the design, such as the
<Gloss name="rip">restricted isometry property</Gloss> of <NoteLink to="compressed-sensing">compressed
sensing</NoteLink>, the two problems even have the same solution. The price is the shrinkage of large coefficients by
$\lambda$ and, through it, two failures of the lasso as a selector <Cite id="hastie2015sparsity" />:

- **It needs a large $\lambda$ to remove noise features**, and that $\lambda$ shrinks the real ones. The lasso therefore
  tends to choose a smaller $\lambda$ and admit some noise features.
- **It can be inconsistent.** When a noise feature is correlated with the true ones beyond a limit set by the design,
  no value of $\lambda$ recovers the true set, even with unlimited data <Cite id="zou2006" />.

## Non-convex penalties

SCAD and MCP remove the bias on large coefficients while keeping sparsity and continuity. With a suitable
$\lambda_n$ falling as the sample grows, SCAD has the **oracle property**: with probability tending to one it sets the
zero coefficients to zero, and it estimates the non-zero ones as efficiently as least squares fitted to the true
support <Cite id="fan2001" />.

The objective is non-convex, which has three consequences.

- **Local minima.** The objective $\frac{1}{2n}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \sum_j p_\lambda(\abs{w_j})$ is convex
  only when the curvature of the loss outweighs the concavity of the penalty. With standardised features this holds
  when the smallest eigenvalue of $\Xmat^\top\Xmat/n$ exceeds $1/\gamma$ for MCP, or $1/(a - 1)$ for SCAD
  <Cite id="breheny2011" />. When $p > n$ that eigenvalue is 0, and different starting points can lead to different
  solutions.
- **Coordinate descent still works**, because each one-dimensional problem is convex when $\gamma > 1$ or $a > 2$:
  the scalar objective has curvature $1 - 1/\gamma > 0$. Solving along a decreasing grid of $\lambda$ from the all-zero
  solution, each fit warm-started from the last, follows a continuous local path <Cite id="breheny2011" />.
- **A second tuning parameter.** $\gamma$ or $a$ moves the penalty between $L^1$ and $L^0$, and it is usually fixed
  ($\gamma = 3$ for MCP, $a = 3.7$ for SCAD) or chosen with $\lambda$ by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>.

## The adaptive lasso

The adaptive lasso keeps convexity and reduces the bias by giving each coefficient its own weight
<Cite id="zou2006" />:

$$
\hat\wvec = \argmin_{\wvec}\ \tfrac12\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\sum_j \frac{\abs{w_j}}{\abs{\tilde w_j}^\nu},
$$

with $\tilde\wvec$ a pilot estimate, such as least squares or ridge, and $\nu > 0$. A coefficient that the pilot finds
large gets a small penalty, and one that the pilot finds near zero gets a large one. With orthonormal features and
$\nu = 1$, $\tilde w_j = z_j$, and the rule is soft thresholding with a threshold $\lambda/\abs{z_j}$ that falls as
$\abs{z_j}$ grows:

$$
\hat w_j = \sgn(z_j)\paren{\abs{z_j} - \frac{\lambda}{\abs{z_j}}}_+.
$$

The shrinkage of a large coefficient falls like $1/\abs{z_j}$, and the adaptive lasso has the oracle property
<Cite id="zou2006" />. It is a lasso problem after rescaling column $j$ by $\abs{\tilde w_j}^\nu$, so any lasso solver
fits it. The pilot uses the same data, and its errors pass into the weights.

## Worked example

Take $\lambda = 1$, noise of standard deviation $\sigma = 1$, and four least-squares coefficients
$z = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 4$. The rules give:

| $z$ | hard ($L^0$) | soft ($L^1$) | MCP, $\gamma = 3$ | SCAD, $a = 3.7$ | adaptive lasso |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 1.5 | 1.5 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 0.5 | 0.83 |
| 2.5 | 2.5 | 1.5 | 2.25 | 1.79 | 2.1 |
| 4 | 4 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 3.75 |

Each entry follows from the rules above.

1. **$z = 0.5$** is below every threshold, so all rules return 0.
2. **$z = 1.5$**: hard thresholding keeps it unchanged. Soft subtracts $\lambda$. MCP multiplies the soft value by
   $\gamma/(\gamma - 1) = 1.5$. SCAD is still in its soft range ($\abs z \le 2\lambda$). The adaptive lasso subtracts
   $\lambda/\abs z = 0.67$.
3. **$z = 2.5$**: MCP gives $1.5 \times 1.5 = 2.25$. SCAD is in its middle range:
   $(2.7 \times 2.5 - 3.7)/1.7 = 1.79$.
4. **$z = 4$**: MCP and SCAD are past their flat points ($\gamma\lambda = 3$ and $a\lambda = 3.7$) and return $z$.
   Soft thresholding is still short by $\lambda = 1$.

The risk $\expect(\Theta_\lambda(z) - \theta)^2$ for $z \sim \Gauss(\theta, 1)$, computed by
<NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical integration</NoteLink>, shows the trade. Least squares, $\Theta(z) = z$,
has risk 1 everywhere.

| true $\theta$ | hard | soft | MCP | SCAD |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.80 | 0.15 | 0.34 | 0.19 |
| 2 | 1.24 | 1.59 | 1.66 | 1.83 |
| 5 | 1.00 | 2.00 | 1.02 | 1.13 |

At $\theta = 0$ soft thresholding is best: it shrinks noise hardest. At $\theta = 5$ its constant bias of 1 adds $1^2$
to the variance, and its risk is $1 + \lambda^2 = 2$, while MCP and SCAD have returned to 1. Near the threshold,
$\theta = 2$, every rule is worse than least squares, because the data cannot tell whether the coefficient should be
there.

## Choosing λ

$\lambda$ sets the level of evidence a coefficient needs. Two reference points help.

- **The universal threshold.** With $p$ orthonormal features that are all noise, the $z_j$ are independent
  $\Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, and their largest absolute value is close to $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln p}$ (see
  <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequalities</NoteLink>). The threshold $\lambda = \sigma\sqrt{2\ln p}$
  therefore removes all pure-noise coefficients with high probability <Cite id="donoho1994" />. For $p = 1000$ it is
  $3.72\sigma$. It is the threshold of <NoteLink to="wavelet-denoising">wavelet denoising</NoteLink>, and it is
  conservative when the aim is prediction.
- **Prediction error.** Cross-validation or <NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk
  estimate</NoteLink> along the <NoteLink to="regularisation-paths">regularisation path</NoteLink>. For the lasso the
  number of non-zero coefficients is an unbiased estimate of the degrees of freedom; for hard thresholding the degrees
  of freedom exceed the number kept, because the choice of which to keep used the data.

## Pitfalls

- **Selection and estimation on the same data.** The coefficients kept by a threshold are those whose $\abs{z_j}$
  happened to be large, so refitting them without shrinkage overstates them. A coefficient of true size $\theta = 1$ at
  noise $\sigma = 1$, kept by a hard threshold at $\lambda = 1$ when $z > 1$, has expected value
  $\expect[z \mid z > 1] = 1 + \varphi(0)/(1 - \Phi(0)) = 1.80$, where $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal
  density and distribution function. The nearly unbiased rules of SCAD and MCP remove the bias of large coefficients,
  not this selection bias near the threshold. <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">Confidence intervals</NoteLink> and
  p-values after selection need methods built for it.
- **Non-convex solutions depend on the algorithm.** Two solvers, or one solver from two starting points, can return
  different SCAD or MCP fits on the same data. Report the algorithm and the path.
- **Standardise first.** A threshold applies to $z_j$, whose scale is that of feature $j$. Without standardisation the
  penalty selects by units.
- **Unbiased is not lower risk.** Near the threshold, the non-convex rules have higher risk than soft thresholding, and
  in low signal-to-noise settings the lasso often predicts better than less biased penalties
  <Cite id="hastie2015sparsity" />.
