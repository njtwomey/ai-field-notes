<Definition>
For ridge or <NoteLink to="tikhonov-regularisation">Tikhonov regularisation</NoteLink>,
$\hat\wvec_\lambda = \argmin_\wvec \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$ with $n$ observations and $p$
coefficients, a **rule for the regularisation strength** maps the data to one value $\hat\lambda$. Six rules are in
common use:

- **<NoteLink to="cross-validation">Cross-validation</NoteLink>** minimises a held-out estimate of prediction error,
  optionally moved to a larger $\lambda$ by the one-standard-error rule.
- **Generalised cross-validation** (GCV) minimises a closed-form approximation to leave-one-out.
- **Mallows' $C_p$**, or Stein's unbiased risk estimate, minimises an unbiased estimate of
  <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">in-sample prediction error</NoteLink>.
- **The evidence** maximises the <NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal
  likelihood</NoteLink> of a Gaussian prior on $\wvec$.
- **The L-curve** picks the corner of the curve of $\log\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda}$ against
  $\log\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\wvec_\lambda}$.
- **The discrepancy principle** picks the $\lambda$ at which the residual is as large as the noise.
</Definition>

## Problem

Every regulariser has a strength: the ridge $\lambda$, the lasso's $\lambda$, a smoothing parameter, a number of
training steps. Too little leaves the noise in the fit; too much removes the signal. The best value depends on the noise
level and on how large the true coefficients are, neither of which is known.

The rules differ in three ways, and the differences decide which one fits a problem.

1. **What they target.** Cross-validation, GCV and $C_p$ estimate **prediction error**, the error on new targets. The
   L-curve and the discrepancy principle come from inverse problems, where the target is the **solution error**
   $\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda - \wvec^\star}$, the distance from the true coefficients $\wvec^\star$. The evidence is the
   probability of the data under a prior, which is a good target when the prior describes $\wvec^\star$.
2. **What they need.** Cross-validation needs data held out and refits. $C_p$ and the discrepancy principle need the
   noise variance $\sigma^2$. The evidence needs a prior. GCV and the L-curve need nothing but the fit.
3. **What they cost.** GCV, $C_p$, the evidence, the L-curve and the discrepancy principle all read off one
   <Gloss name="svd">singular value decomposition</Gloss> of $\Xmat$. $K$-fold cross-validation needs $K$ fits per
   $\lambda$.

All six are **selection** rules in the sense of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>. Each one
reuses the data that fit the model, so the value of its criterion at $\hat\lambda$ is an optimistic estimate of the
chosen model's error (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
Assessing the chosen model needs data the rule never saw.

## Methods

Throughout, write the singular value decomposition $\Xmat = \Umat\Dmat\Vmat^\top$ with singular values
$d_1 \ge \dots \ge d_p$, and $z_j = \uvec_j^\top\yvec$ for the data's coordinate along the $j$-th left singular vector.
The ridge fit keeps the fraction $\phi_j = d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda)$ of each coordinate, the **filter factor**. Its
residual sum of squares, degrees of freedom and coefficient norm are

$$
\operatorname{RSS}(\lambda) = \sum_j (1 - \phi_j)^2 z_j^2 + \norm{\rvec_\perp}^2,
\qquad
\operatorname{df}(\lambda) = \sum_j \phi_j,
\qquad
\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda}^2 = \sum_j \paren{\frac{\phi_j z_j}{d_j}}^2,
$$

where $\rvec_\perp$ is the part of $\yvec$ outside the column space of $\Xmat$. Every criterion below except $K$-fold
cross-validation is a function of these three numbers.

**Cross-validation and the one-standard-error rule.** <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold
cross-validation</NoteLink> fits on $K - 1$ folds, scores the held-out fold, and averages. It needs no model of the
noise and works for any loss and any learner, which makes it the default. The
<NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink> takes the largest $\lambda$ whose error is
within one <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the minimum <Cite id="breiman1984" />. It prefers
a simpler model that the folds cannot distinguish from the best, and it pays for that in prediction error when the truth
has many small effects. The <NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-ridge-penalty">ridge example</NoteLink> works both through
on one data set.

**Leave-one-out and GCV.** For a linear smoother, leave-one-out needs one fit per $\lambda$, through the shortcut
$(y_i - \hat y_i)/(1 - S_{ii})$. <NoteLink to="generalised-cross-validation">Generalised cross-validation</NoteLink>
replaces every leverage $S_{ii}$ by the mean $\operatorname{df}/n$,
$\operatorname{GCV}(\lambda) = n\operatorname{RSS}(\lambda)/(n - \operatorname{df}(\lambda))^2$
<Cite id="golub1979" />.

**$C_p$ and SURE.** With the noise variance $\sigma^2$ known, Mallows' $C_p$,
$\operatorname{RSS}(\lambda)/n + 2\sigma^2\operatorname{df}(\lambda)/n$, is an unbiased estimate of the in-sample
prediction error <Cite id="mallows1973" />. For ridge it coincides with
<NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk estimate</NoteLink>. GCV is $C_p$ with
$\sigma^2$ estimated by $\operatorname{RSS}/n$, to first order in $\operatorname{df}/n$.

**The evidence.** Treat the penalty as a prior, $\wvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \alpha^{-1}\eye)$, with Gaussian noise of
precision $\beta = 1/\sigma^2$. The posterior mean is the ridge fit with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$ (see
<NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>). The marginal likelihood
$p(\yvec \mid \alpha, \beta)$ integrates the coefficients out. In the singular coordinates each $z_j$ is Gaussian with
variance $\sigma^2 + d_j^2/\alpha$ and each coordinate of $\rvec_\perp$ has variance $\sigma^2$, so

$$
\ln p(\yvec \mid \alpha, \beta) = -\frac12\sum_{j=1}^p \brack{\ln\paren{2\pi(\sigma^2 + d_j^2/\alpha)}
+ \frac{z_j^2}{\sigma^2 + d_j^2/\alpha}} - \frac{n - p}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{\norm{\rvec_\perp}^2}{2\sigma^2}.
$$

Maximising it over $\alpha$, and over $\beta$ too when $\sigma^2$ is unknown, is
<NoteLink to="empirical-bayes-for-hyperparameters">empirical Bayes</NoteLink>. MacKay's fixed-point updates do it in a
few iterations: $\gamma = \sum_j \beta d_j^2/(\alpha + \beta d_j^2)$, $\alpha \leftarrow \gamma/\norm{\mvec}^2$ and
$\beta \leftarrow (n - \gamma)/\operatorname{RSS}$, with $\mvec$ the posterior mean <Cite id="mackay1992" />
<Cite id="bishop2006" />. The evidence chooses the prior that makes the data most probable. It is the best rule when the
coefficients look like draws from that prior and can be poor when they do not.

## The L-curve

The L-curve plots, for every $\lambda$, the point

$$
\paren{\log\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\wvec_\lambda},\ \log\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda}}.
$$

On a problem whose singular values decay, the curve has the shape of an L <Cite id="hansen1992" />.

- **The vertical arm (small $\lambda$).** The filter keeps the small singular values, and the noise in their
  coordinates is divided by $d_j$. The coefficient norm grows without bound while the residual barely falls, because the
  fit is already as close to the data as the noise allows.
- **The horizontal arm (large $\lambda$).** The filter removes signal. The residual grows while the coefficient norm
  shrinks slowly.
- **The corner** is where neither norm can fall without the other rising sharply. Hansen and O'Leary define it as the
  point of maximum curvature of the log-log curve <Cite id="hansen1993" />. With $\hat\rho(\lambda)$ and
  $\hat\eta(\lambda)$ the two logarithms and primes for derivatives in $\lambda$, the curvature is

  $$
  \kappa(\lambda) = \frac{\hat\rho'\hat\eta'' - \hat\rho''\hat\eta'}{\paren{(\hat\rho')^2 + (\hat\eta')^2}^{3/2}},
  $$

  the curvature of any parametrised plane curve. Hansen derived a closed form for Tikhonov regularisation that needs
  only $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\wvec_\lambda}$, $\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda}$ and the derivative of the latter, all from
  the singular value decomposition <Cite id="hansen1998" />.

The L-curve needs no noise variance and no held-out data. It was designed for discrete ill-posed problems, whose
singular values decay steadily to the level of the noise. Where the singular values are all of similar size, there is no
sharp corner, and the maximum curvature can sit anywhere, including the end of the grid. Vogel showed that the L-curve
choice need not converge as the discretisation is refined: it can stay almost constant while the optimal $\lambda$
falls, and so over-regularise <Cite id="vogel1996" />.

## The discrepancy principle

Morozov's discrepancy principle chooses the $\lambda$ at which the residual equals the size of the noise
<Cite id="morozov1966" />:

$$
\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\wvec_\lambda} = \tau\delta,
$$

with $\delta$ the norm of the noise vector, $\delta^2 \approx n\sigma^2$, and a safety factor $\tau \ge 1$. The residual
grows monotonically with $\lambda$, from the least-squares residual at $\lambda = 0$ to $\norm{\yvec}$, so the equation
has exactly one root when $\tau\delta$ lies between them. The rule reflects a simple principle: a fit should not explain
the data more closely than the noise allows.

**Why it over-regularises for prediction.** Write $\fvec = \Xmat\wvec^\star$ for the noise-free targets and
$\Smat_\lambda$ for the ridge smoother, so $\hat\yvec = \Smat_\lambda\yvec$. The expected residual sum of squares is

$$
\expect\operatorname{RSS}(\lambda) = \norm{(\eye - \Smat_\lambda)\fvec}^2 + \sigma^2\paren{n - 2\trace\Smat_\lambda
+ \trace\Smat_\lambda^2}.
$$

The first term is the squared bias. The second comes from the noise: $\expect\norm{(\eye - \Smat)\varepsilonvec}^2 =
\sigma^2\trace(\eye - \Smat)^2$, expanded. The eigenvalues of $\Smat_\lambda$ lie in $[0, 1]$, so
$\trace\Smat_\lambda^2 \le \trace\Smat_\lambda$, and the noise term is at most $\sigma^2(n - \operatorname{df})$.
Setting $\expect\operatorname{RSS} = n\sigma^2$ therefore requires

$$
\text{squared bias} = \sigma^2\paren{2\operatorname{df} - \trace\Smat_\lambda^2} \ge \sigma^2\operatorname{df}.
$$

The squared bias must be at least $\sigma^2$ per effective parameter. The prediction-optimal $\lambda$ balances bias
against variance and typically has less bias than that, so the discrepancy principle chooses a larger $\lambda$. Its
classical justification is different: as the noise level $\delta$ tends to zero, the discrepancy choice makes the
solution error tend to zero, at the optimal rate when the true solution is moderately smooth <Cite id="engl1996" />.
Replacing the target $n\sigma^2$ by $(n - \operatorname{df})\sigma^2$ removes most of the excess bias.

## Worked example

Take $n = 20$ observations and $p = 4$ coefficients, with singular values $\dvec = (4, 2, 0.5, 0.1)$ and true
coefficients $\wvec^\star = (1, 1, 1, 1)$ in the right singular basis. The noise-free coordinates are
$\theta_j = d_j w^\star_j = (4, 2, 0.5, 0.1)$. The noise has $\sigma = 0.5$; take its realisation along the four
directions to be $(0.3, -0.4, 0.6, -0.5)$, so $\zvec = (4.3, 1.6, 1.1, -0.4)$, and its squared length in the other 16
directions to be $\norm{\rvec_\perp}^2 = 3.6$. Every quantity follows from the formulas of the Methods section.

| $\lambda$ | 0.01 | 0.03 | 0.1 | 0.3 | 1 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\operatorname{df}$ | 3.46 | 3.13 | 2.77 | 2.40 | 1.95 | 1.49 |
| $\operatorname{RSS}$ | 3.642 | 3.704 | 3.833 | 4.129 | 4.698 | 5.721 |
| $\operatorname{GCV}$ | 0.2662 | 0.2604 | **0.2584** | 0.2665 | 0.2884 | 0.3341 |
| $C_p$ | 0.2686 | 0.2635 | **0.2610** | 0.2664 | 0.2837 | 0.3234 |
| $\ln p(\yvec)$ | −20.67 | −19.17 | −18.12 | **−18.03** | −19.51 | −23.69 |
| $\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda}$ | 3.20 | 2.58 | 2.09 | 1.64 | 1.28 | 1.03 |
| true risk | 0.0397 | 0.0360 | 0.0323 | **0.0309** | 0.0388 | 0.0808 |

The true risk is the expected in-sample error of the fit, $\frac1n\expect\norm{\Smat_\lambda\yvec - \fvec}^2 =
\frac1n\sum_j\brack{(1 - \phi_j)^2\theta_j^2 + \sigma^2\phi_j^2}$. GCV and $C_p$ estimate the prediction error
$\sigma^2 + $ risk, so their values sit near $0.25 + 0.03$.

Minimised over a fine grid, the rules choose:

| Rule | $\hat\lambda$ | true risk | solution error $\norm{\hat\wvec - \wvec^\star}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| best for risk | 0.25 | 0.0308 | 1.19 |
| GCV | 0.078 | 0.0330 | 1.62 |
| $C_p$ ($\sigma$ known) | 0.091 | 0.0326 | 1.54 |
| evidence ($\sigma$ known) | 0.195 | 0.0309 | 1.24 |
| L-curve corner | 1.31 | 0.0442 | 1.28 |
| discrepancy, $\tau = 1$ | 1.55 | 0.0487 | 1.31 |
| best for solution error | 0.41 | 0.0315 | 1.14 |

1. **GCV and $C_p$** follow the data's realised error. This noise draw makes the fit look slightly better at small
   $\lambda$ than it is in expectation, and both rules undersmooth by a factor of about 3. Their risk is 7% above the
   best.
2. **The evidence** lands close to the best, because the true coefficients have the size the fitted prior assigns
   them.
3. **The discrepancy principle** needs $\operatorname{RSS} = n\sigma^2 = 5$, which happens at $\lambda = 1.55$. At the
   risk-optimal $\lambda = 0.25$ the expected residual sum of squares is only 4.38, with a squared bias of 0.09. At
   $\lambda = 1.55$ the squared bias is 0.63, seven times more, as the derivation predicts. Its risk is 58%
   above the best. With the target $(n - \operatorname{df})\sigma^2$ instead it chooses $\lambda = 0.66$, with risk
   0.034.
4. **The L-curve** also lands near $\lambda = 1.3$. Its corner sits where $\norm{\hat\wvec_\lambda}$ stops falling
   quickly, once the two weakest directions, $d_3 = 0.5$ and $d_4 = 0.1$, are mostly filtered out.
5. **Solution error** is minimised at a larger $\lambda$, 0.41, than risk. Estimating $\wvec^\star$ penalises the noise
   amplified by $1/d_j$ in weak directions more than prediction does, so the inverse-problem rules' preference for more
   regularisation is less costly for that target.

## Simulation

<SelectorsCompared />

The figure's problem has $n = 50$ rows of $p = 20$ standard-normal predictors, each correlated 0.9 with its neighbour,
noise $\sigma = 1$, and either smooth coefficients $w^\star_j = \sin(\pi j/21)$ or coefficients drawn from
$\Gauss(0, 1)$. Over 100 data sets, the mean prediction error (the noise floor is 1) and mean squared solution error of
each rule's choice were:

| Rule | smooth: prediction | smooth: solution | random: prediction | random: solution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| best $\lambda$ for prediction | 1.170 | 0.51 | 1.452 | 3.66 |
| 10-fold CV | 1.196 | 0.64 | 1.520 | 4.29 |
| one-standard-error rule | 1.311 | 0.27 | 1.771 | 6.20 |
| leave-one-out | 1.196 | 0.62 | 1.520 | 4.31 |
| GCV | 1.195 | 0.61 | 1.517 | 4.25 |
| evidence ($\alpha$ and $\beta$ fitted) | 1.346 | 2.34 | 1.508 | 4.17 |
| L-curve corner | 1.272 | 1.52 | 1.666 | 6.00 |
| discrepancy ($\sigma$ known) | 1.337 | 0.40 | 1.726 | 6.00 |

- **The cross-validation family is robust.** 10-fold, leave-one-out and GCV agree with each other to within 0.003
  and lose about 15% of the excess error of the best $\lambda$ in both settings.
- **The evidence depends on its prior.** With coefficients drawn from a Gaussian it is the best rule. With smooth
  coefficients it chooses $\lambda$ about seven times too small and doubles the excess error.
- **The one-standard-error rule and the discrepancy principle** both regularise more than prediction needs. With smooth
  coefficients that buys the smallest solution errors of all the rules; with random ones it costs on both counts.
- **The L-curve** is erratic on a well-conditioned problem. With random coefficients its maximum curvature sits at the
  end of the grid in at least half the data sets.

## Trade-offs

| Rule | Needs | Targets | Cost per $\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $K$-fold CV | refits | prediction error | $K$ fits |
| one-standard-error | refits | a simpler model near the best | $K$ fits |
| <Gloss name="loo" />, GCV | a linear smoother | prediction error | one fit and the leverages or the trace |
| $C_p$, SURE | $\sigma^2$ | in-sample prediction error | one fit and the trace |
| evidence | a prior | marginal likelihood | one fit and a determinant |
| L-curve | decaying singular values | a balance of the two norms | one fit |
| discrepancy | $\sigma^2$ | solution error as $\sigma \to 0$ | one fit |

Practice follows the table. For prediction, use $K$-fold cross-validation, or GCV or leave-one-out when the model is a
linear smoother and refitting is expensive. Use the evidence for Bayesian models whose prior is believed, and when
several hyperparameters must be tuned at once, since its gradient is available. Use the L-curve and the discrepancy
principle for inverse problems, where the coefficients themselves are the object of interest and the noise level is
known from the instrument.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting the criterion's minimum.** The minimum of a cross-validation or GCV curve is optimistic about the chosen
  model. Assess it on held-out data or with <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>.
- **An underestimated noise level.** The discrepancy principle and $C_p$ take $\sigma^2$ as given. Estimating it from
  the residuals of a flexible fit makes it too small, and the discrepancy principle then undersmooths.
- **The edge of the grid.** A rule whose choice sits at the end of the grid has not found an interior optimum. Extend
  the grid; for the L-curve, check that a corner exists by plotting the curve.
- **Correlated noise.** Every rule here assumes independent errors. Positively correlated noise looks like signal, and
  cross-validation, GCV and the discrepancy principle then choose too small a $\lambda$.
- **Different scalings.** A $\lambda$ chosen for $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$ is a different
  number for $\frac{1}{2n}\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \alpha\norm{\wvec}^2$. Convert before transferring a value
  between software.
