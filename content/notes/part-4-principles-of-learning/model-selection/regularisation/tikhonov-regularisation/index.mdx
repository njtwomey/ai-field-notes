<Definition>
Let $\Amat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ be a forward operator, $\bvec \in \reals^m$ noisy data and
$\Lmat \in \reals^{k \times n}$ a penalty operator. The **Tikhonov solution** with regularisation parameter
$\lambda > 0$ is

$$
\xvec_\lambda = \argmin_{\xvec}\ \norm{\Amat\xvec - \bvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\Lmat\xvec}^2
= (\Amat^\top\Amat + \lambda\Lmat^\top\Lmat)^{-1}\Amat^\top\bvec,
$$

unique when the null spaces of $\Amat$ and $\Lmat$ meet only at $\zeros$ <Cite id="tikhonov1963" />
<Cite id="phillips1962" />. **Standard form** has $\Lmat = \eye$ and penalises the size of $\xvec$; a difference
operator $\Lmat$ penalises its roughness. With $\Amat$ a design matrix and $\Lmat = \eye$ it is
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Many measurements are blurred versions of what one wants to know. A camera out of focus averages neighbouring pixels.
A thermometer responds slowly and smooths a temperature history. A tomography scanner records line integrals of a
density. In each case the data are $\bvec = \Amat\xvec_{\text{true}} + \evec$, with $\Amat$ a smoothing operator and
$\evec$ noise, and the task is to recover $\xvec_{\text{true}}$.

Smoothing destroys fine detail: two signals that differ only by a rapid wiggle produce almost the same blurred data.
Undoing the blur must therefore magnify rapid wiggles enormously, and the noise is full of them. The least-squares
solution fits the data and returns noise magnified by factors of thousands. Tikhonov regularisation adds a cost for
solutions that are large or rough. The fit then gives up the detail the data cannot support, and keeps the detail they
can. This is the oldest form of regularisation, introduced for integral equations by Phillips and by Tikhonov in the
early 1960s, before ridge regression brought the same estimator to statistics <Cite id="hoerl1970" />.

## Ill-posed problems

Hadamard called a problem well posed when a solution exists, is unique and depends continuously on the data. A problem
that fails the third condition is **ill posed**: arbitrarily small changes in the data make large changes in the
solution. Discretised, an ill-posed problem becomes a matrix $\Amat$ whose singular values decay gradually towards zero
with no clear gap, so that its <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> is huge
<Cite id="hansen1998" />.

The <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> shows the damage. Write
$\Amat = \sum_{i} s_i\uvec_i\vvec_i^\top$ with singular values $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \dots > 0$, left singular vectors
$\uvec_i$ and right singular vectors $\vvec_i$. The least-squares solution expands $\bvec$ in the left vectors and
divides by the singular values,

$$
\xvec_{\text{LS}} = \sum_i \frac{\uvec_i^\top\bvec}{s_i}\vvec_i
= \xvec_{\text{true}} + \sum_i \frac{\uvec_i^\top\evec}{s_i}\vvec_i.
$$

For white noise of variance $\sigma^2$, each coefficient $\uvec_i^\top\evec$ has standard deviation $\sigma$, because
the $\uvec_i$ are orthonormal. The error along $\vvec_i$ therefore has standard deviation $\sigma/s_i$. With singular
values running down to $10^{-6}$, noise of size $10^{-3}$ produces errors of size $10^3$.

**The discrete Picard condition.** The true coefficients are
$\vvec_i^\top\xvec_{\text{true}} = \uvec_i^\top\bvec_{\text{true}}/s_i$ with
$\bvec_{\text{true}} = \Amat\xvec_{\text{true}}$. A solution of reasonable size needs the data coefficients
$\abs{\uvec_i^\top\bvec_{\text{true}}}$ to decay at least as fast as the $s_i$ <Cite id="hansen1998" />. The noisy
coefficients $\abs{\uvec_i^\top\bvec}$ follow that decay only until they reach the noise floor $\sigma$, and then level
off while the $s_i$ keep falling. Plotting $s_i$, $\abs{\uvec_i^\top\bvec}$ and their ratio against $i$, the **Picard
plot**, shows where the data stop carrying information. Components beyond that index cannot be recovered by any method;
the question is only how to stop them from swamping the rest.

## Filter factors

For standard form, $\Lmat = \eye$, substitute the SVD into the solution. With
$\Amat^\top\Amat = \Vmat\Sigmamat^2\Vmat^\top$ and $\Vmat\Vmat^\top = \eye$ (the same algebra as in
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>),

$$
\xvec_\lambda = \sum_i \varphi_i\,\frac{\uvec_i^\top\bvec}{s_i}\vvec_i,
\qquad
\varphi_i = \frac{s_i^2}{s_i^2 + \lambda}.
$$

Here $\Sigmamat$ is the diagonal matrix of singular values. Each component of the least-squares solution is multiplied
by a **filter factor** $\varphi_i \in (0, 1)$.

- **Large singular values**, $s_i \gg \sqrt\lambda$: $\varphi_i \approx 1$, and the component passes almost unchanged.
- **Small singular values**, $s_i \ll \sqrt\lambda$: $\varphi_i \approx s_i^2/\lambda$, and the component is suppressed.

So $\sqrt\lambda$ acts as a soft cutoff on the singular values. **Truncated SVD** uses a hard cutoff instead,
$\varphi_i = 1$ for $i \le k$ and 0 beyond; Tikhonov rolls off smoothly around $s_i = \sqrt\lambda$.

**Stability.** The factor that multiplies the noise coefficient $\uvec_i^\top\evec$ is
$\varphi_i/s_i = s_i/(s_i^2 + \lambda)$. Since $s_i^2 + \lambda \ge 2s_i\sqrt\lambda$ by the inequality of arithmetic
and geometric means,

$$
\frac{\varphi_i}{s_i} = \frac{s_i}{s_i^2 + \lambda} \le \frac{1}{2\sqrt\lambda},
$$

with equality at $s_i = \sqrt\lambda$. Without regularisation the amplification is $1/s_n$, unbounded as the smallest
singular value falls. With it, no component amplifies the noise by more than $1/(2\sqrt\lambda)$. The solution depends
continuously on the data again, which is the sense in which regularisation restores well-posedness.

**Bias and variance.** Write $c_i = \vvec_i^\top\xvec_{\text{true}}$ for the true coefficients. The error splits into a
regularisation error and a perturbation error,

$$
\xvec_\lambda - \xvec_{\text{true}} = \sum_i (\varphi_i - 1)\,c_i\,\vvec_i + \sum_i \frac{\varphi_i}{s_i}(\uvec_i^\top\evec)\,\vvec_i,
$$

and for white noise its expected squared norm is

$$
\expect\norm{\xvec_\lambda - \xvec_{\text{true}}}^2 = \sum_i (1 - \varphi_i)^2c_i^2 + \sigma^2\sum_i \frac{\varphi_i^2}{s_i^2}.
$$

The first sum, the squared bias, grows with $\lambda$. The second, the variance, shrinks with it. The best $\lambda$
balances them, as in the <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink>. The sum
$\sum_i\varphi_i$ is the number of components effectively kept, the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> of the fitted data
$\Amat\xvec_\lambda$.

## General penalty operators

Penalising $\norm{\xvec}^2$ pulls the solution towards zero. Often the prior belief is that $\xvec$ is smooth, not that
it is small. A difference operator encodes that belief:

$$
\Lmat_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 & & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \\ & & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in \reals^{(n-1) \times n},
\qquad
(\Lmat_2\xvec)_i = x_i - 2x_{i+1} + x_{i+2}.
$$

$\norm{\Lmat_1\xvec}^2$ sums squared slopes and $\norm{\Lmat_2\xvec}^2$ sums squared curvatures. Their null spaces are
unpenalised: constants for $\Lmat_1$, straight lines for $\Lmat_2$. Shrinking towards zero becomes shrinking towards
the best constant or the best line. With $\Amat = \eye$ and $\Lmat_2$ this is the Whittaker smoother, the discrete
cousin of the roughness penalty in <NoteLink to="smoothing-penalties-and-curvature">smoothing penalties and
curvature</NoteLink>.

The SVD no longer diagonalises the problem, because $\Amat$ and $\Lmat$ have different singular vectors. The
**generalised SVD** of the pair does: it gives generalised singular values $\gamma_i$, and the filter factors become
$\gamma_i^2/(\gamma_i^2 + \lambda)$, with $\varphi_i = 1$ on the null space of $\Lmat$ <Cite id="hansen1998" />. When
$\Lmat$ is square and invertible, the substitution $\yvec = \Lmat\xvec$ turns the problem into standard form with
operator $\Amat\Lmat^{-1}$.

The penalty is also a prior. If $\evec \sim \MvGauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye)$ and $\xvec$ has density proportional to
$\exp(-\lambda\norm{\Lmat\xvec}^2/(2\sigma^2))$, the negative log posterior is $\norm{\Amat\xvec - \bvec}^2 +
\lambda\norm{\Lmat\xvec}^2$ up to the factor $1/(2\sigma^2)$, and $\xvec_\lambda$ is the posterior mode and mean (see
<NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>). A difference operator makes the prior improper along its null
space, which leaves those components to the data.

## Choosing λ and the alternatives

$\lambda$ sets the cutoff, and choosing it is the main practical difficulty.
<NoteLink to="choosing-the-regularisation-strength">Choosing the regularisation strength</NoteLink> compares the
methods used for Tikhonov problems:

- the **discrepancy principle**, which picks the $\lambda$ whose residual $\norm{\Amat\xvec_\lambda - \bvec}^2$ equals
  the expected noise energy $m\sigma^2$ (the Morozov form of
  <NoteLink to="penalised-and-constrained-regularisation">penalised and constrained regularisation</NoteLink>);
- the **L-curve**, which picks the corner of the curve of $\log\norm{\Lmat\xvec_\lambda}$ against
  $\log\norm{\Amat\xvec_\lambda - \bvec}$;
- <NoteLink to="generalised-cross-validation">generalised cross-validation</NoteLink>, which needs no noise level.

Iterative solvers regularise by stopping early. Landweber iteration and <Gloss name="cg">conjugate gradients</Gloss>
have filter factors of their own, and the number of iterations plays the role of $1/\lambda$ (see
<NoteLink to="iterative-regularisation">iterative regularisation</NoteLink>). For a convolution, the
<NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> diagonalises $\Amat$, the filter factors act frequency by
frequency, and replacing $\lambda$ by a noise-to-signal power ratio at each frequency gives the
<NoteLink to="wiener-filter">Wiener filter</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Take $\Amat = \diag(1, 0.1, 0.001)$, so that the SVD is trivial: $\uvec_i = \vvec_i = \evec_i$ and $s = (1, 0.1,
0.001)$. Any $\Amat$ reduces to this case, because $\Umat$ and $\Vmat$ rotate $\bvec$ and $\xvec$ without changing
lengths. The condition number is $1000$. Let $\xvec_{\text{true}} = (1, 1, 1)$, and let the noise have standard
deviation $\sigma = 0.01$, with the draw $\evec = (0.01, -0.01, 0.01)$.

1. **The data.** $\bvec = \Amat\xvec_{\text{true}} + \evec = (1.01, 0.09, 0.011)$. The third true data coefficient,
   $0.001$, is a tenth of the noise level: the Picard condition fails there, and no method can recover $x_3$.
2. **Least squares.** Dividing by the singular values gives $\xvec_{\text{LS}} = (1.01, 0.9, 11)$. The noise $0.01$ in
   the third component is multiplied by $1/0.001 = 1000$, and the error norm is $10.0$.
3. **Tikhonov with $\lambda = 10^{-4}$**, so $\sqrt\lambda = 0.01$. The filter factors are
   $\varphi = (1/1.0001,\ 0.01/0.0101,\ 10^{-6}/(1.01 \times 10^{-4})) = (0.9999, 0.990, 0.0099)$. The solution is
   $\xvec_\lambda = (1.010, 0.891, 0.109)$, with error norm $0.90$. The first two components are kept. The third is
   suppressed, and its error, almost all bias, is bounded by $\abs{x_3} = 1$.
4. **The noise bound.** The largest noise amplification is $1/(2\sqrt\lambda) = 50$, against 1000 for least squares.
5. **Expected error over the noise.** From the bias–variance formula with $c = (1, 1, 1)$ and $\sigma = 0.01$:

| $\lambda$ | squared bias | variance | expected squared error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{-6}$ | 0.250 | 25.0 | 25.3 |
| $10^{-5}$ | 0.826 | 0.837 | 1.66 |
| $10^{-4}$ | 0.980 | 0.020 | 1.00 |
| $10^{-3}$ | 1.006 | 0.008 | 1.02 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 1.250 | 0.003 | 1.25 |

Least squares has expected squared error $\sigma^2\sum_i 1/s_i^2 = 10^{-4}(1 + 100 + 10^6) = 100$. The best $\lambda$,
near $10^{-4}$, cuts it to 1.0, and nearly all of what remains is the third component, which the data cannot inform.

## Assumptions

- **A linear forward model.** $\Amat$ is known and fixed. Nonlinear inverse problems are regularised the same way but
  solved iteratively, often by Tikhonov-regularised Gauss–Newton steps.
- **Gaussian white noise** for the bias–variance formula and the discrepancy principle. Correlated noise with
  covariance $\Cmat$ is whitened first, by multiplying the problem by $\Cmat^{-1/2}$.
- **A penalty that matches the solution.** The quadratic penalty suits solutions that are small or smooth in the sense
  of $\Lmat$.

## Pitfalls

- **Quadratic penalties smear edges.** A jump has a large squared slope, so $\norm{\Lmat_1\xvec}^2$ replaces it by a
  ramp. For piecewise-constant solutions use the absolute slope, total variation (see
  <NoteLink to="structured-sparsity-penalties">structured sparsity penalties</NoteLink>).
- **Do not form $\Amat^\top\Amat$ for an ill-conditioned $\Amat$.** It squares the condition number. Solve the stacked
  least-squares problem $\begin{pmatrix}\Amat \\ \sqrt\lambda\Lmat\end{pmatrix}\xvec \approx \begin{pmatrix}\bvec \\
  \zeros\end{pmatrix}$ by <Gloss name="qr" />, or compute one SVD and reuse it for every $\lambda$.
- **The inverse crime.** Simulating data with the same discretised $\Amat$ that the reconstruction uses hides the model
  error of the discretisation, and makes a method look better than it will on real data <Cite id="kaipio2005" />. Test
  on data generated at a finer resolution.
- **$\lambda$ depends on the scaling.** Rescaling $\Amat$, $\bvec$ or $\Lmat$ changes the $\lambda$ that gives the same
  solution. Report the residual and penalty norms, or the effective degrees of freedom, alongside $\lambda$.
