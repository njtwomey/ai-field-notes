<Definition>
**Noise injection** trains a model $f$ on randomly perturbed inputs: each time an example $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ is used, the
input is replaced by $\xvec_i + \epsilonvec$ with a fresh draw of noise $\epsilonvec$ of mean $\zeros$ and covariance
$\sigma^2\eye$. Training then minimises the expected loss

$$
\tilde L(f) = \sum_{i=1}^n \expect_{\epsilonvec}\brack{\ell\paren{f(\xvec_i + \epsilonvec), y_i}}.
$$

For squared error, $\tilde L$ equals the training loss plus $\sigma^2$ times a Tikhonov penalty on the gradient
$\nabla_\xvec f$, up to terms of order $\sigma^4$ that do not change the minimiser to order $\sigma^2$
<Cite id="bishop1995" />. **Dropout** is multiplicative noise of the same kind <Cite id="srivastava2014" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A model trained on jittered copies of each input sees, around every training point, a small cloud of inputs that share
one target. It can fit all of them only by predicting nearly the same value across the cloud. A model whose output
changes steeply with its input pays for the steepness on every noisy copy. Noise injection therefore penalises the
model's sensitivity to its input, the same quantity that a smoothness penalty on
$\nabla_\xvec f$ penalises, and the noise variance $\sigma^2$ plays the part of the penalty strength.

The noise also enlarges the training set. Each epoch sees different inputs, so the model cannot memorise the exact
training points. Noise injection is the simplest form of <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink>,
the one that encodes no invariance beyond "nearby inputs have nearby outputs".

## Linear models: exactly ridge

Let $f(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec$ and the loss be squared error. For one example, the residual on the noisy input is
$(y_i - \wvec^\top\xvec_i) - \wvec^\top\epsilonvec$. Squaring and taking the expectation over $\epsilonvec$, the cross
term vanishes because $\expect\epsilonvec = \zeros$, and $\expect(\wvec^\top\epsilonvec)^2 =
\wvec^\top\expect[\epsilonvec\epsilonvec^\top]\wvec = \sigma^2\norm{\wvec}^2$. Summing over the $n$ examples,

$$
\tilde L(\wvec) = \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + n\sigma^2\norm{\wvec}^2.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> with $\lambda = n\sigma^2$, exactly, for any noise
distribution with this mean and covariance. Three consequences follow.

- **The penalty grows with $n$.** Each example contributes its own $\sigma^2\norm{\wvec}^2$, so for a fixed noise level
  the <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> keeps pace with the data, unlike a fixed $\lambda$, whose
  effect fades as $n$ grows.
- **The noise shape is the penalty shape.** Noise with covariance $\Sigmamat$ gives the generalised ridge penalty
  $n\wvec^\top\Sigmamat\wvec$. Noise proportional to each feature's standard deviation is ridge on standardised
  features.
- **It is regression dilution.** Least squares on inputs measured with error shrinks the slope towards zero by the same
  factor. The attenuation that is a bias in <NoteLink to="measurement-error-and-proxies">measurement-error</NoteLink>
  problems is the shrinkage that noise injection buys deliberately.

## Nonlinear models: a penalty on the gradient

For a nonlinear $f$, expand around $\xvec$ to second order in the noise, with $\gvec = \nabla_\xvec f(\xvec)$ the
gradient and $\Hmat$ the <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> of $f$ in $\xvec$:

$$
f(\xvec + \epsilonvec) = f(\xvec) + \epsilonvec^\top\gvec + \tfrac12\epsilonvec^\top\Hmat\epsilonvec + O(\norm{\epsilonvec}^3).
$$

Write $r = f(\xvec) - y$ for the residual. Then

$$
\paren{f(\xvec + \epsilonvec) - y}^2 = r^2 + 2r\,\epsilonvec^\top\gvec + (\epsilonvec^\top\gvec)^2
+ r\,\epsilonvec^\top\Hmat\epsilonvec + O(\norm{\epsilonvec}^3).
$$

Take the expectation term by term. The term linear in $\epsilonvec$ vanishes. $\expect(\epsilonvec^\top\gvec)^2 =
\sigma^2\norm{\gvec}^2$ and $\expect[\epsilonvec^\top\Hmat\epsilonvec] = \sigma^2\trace\Hmat$. For noise symmetric about
zero the third-order terms vanish too, leaving

$$
\expect_{\epsilonvec}\paren{f(\xvec + \epsilonvec) - y}^2 = r^2 + \sigma^2\paren{\norm{\nabla_\xvec f}^2 +
r\,\trace\Hmat} + O(\sigma^4).
$$

The first penalty term, $\sigma^2\norm{\nabla_\xvec f}^2$, is a Tikhonov regulariser of first order: it penalises the
slope of the function. The second, $\sigma^2 r\trace\Hmat$, can be negative, so the expansion is not yet a penalty in
the usual sense. Bishop's argument removes it <Cite id="bishop1995" />. Averaged over the targets at $\xvec$, the second
term involves $f(\xvec) - \expect[y \mid \xvec]$. The minimiser of $\tilde L$ is the regression function plus a
correction of order $\sigma^2$, so at the minimum this factor is $O(\sigma^2)$ and the whole term is $O(\sigma^4)$. To
order $\sigma^2$, training with noise has the same minimiser as

$$
\sum_i \paren{f(\xvec_i) - y_i}^2 + n\sigma^2\,\overline{\norm{\nabla_\xvec f}^2},
$$

where the bar averages the squared gradient over the input distribution. The equivalence is between minimisers, not
between the values of the two objectives. It needs no assumption that the individual residuals are small, because the
average over targets does the work.

The same expansion explains what other noise does <Cite id="an1996" />.

- **Output noise.** Noise added to the targets changes the squared-error objective by a constant, $\sigma^2$ per
  example, and has no effect on the minimiser.
- **Weight noise.** Noise added to the parameters penalises $\norm{\nabla_\wvec f}^2$, the sensitivity of the output to
  the weights, which favours flat regions of the loss (see <NoteLink to="flat-minima-and-sharpness">flat minima and
  sharpness</NoteLink>). An found it improved generalisation on a classification task but not on a regression task.
- **Kernel density view.** Training on noisy inputs minimises the loss under a smoothed estimate of the input
  distribution, the training points convolved with the noise density. Holmström and Koistinen analysed
  training with additive noise this way, which turns the choice of $\sigma$ into the choice of a kernel bandwidth
  <Cite id="holmstrom1992" />.

## Dropout as adaptive $L^2$

<NoteLink to="dropout">Dropout</NoteLink> replaces feature $x_{ij}$ by $0$ with probability $\delta$ and by
$x_{ij}/(1 - \delta)$ otherwise, so the perturbed input $\tilde\xvec_i$ is unbiased, $\expect\tilde\xvec_i = \xvec_i$,
with independent coordinates of variance $\frac{\delta}{1 - \delta}x_{ij}^2$.

Take a <NoteLink to="generalised-linear-model">generalised linear model</NoteLink> with natural parameter
$\eta = \wvec^\top\xvec$, log-partition function $A$, and negative log-likelihood
$A(\wvec^\top\xvec) - y\,\wvec^\top\xvec$ up to terms free of $\wvec$. <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic
regression</NoteLink> has $A(\eta) = \ln(1 + e^\eta)$ and $A''(\eta) = p(1 - p)$, with $p$ the predicted probability.
Least squares has $A(\eta) = \eta^2/2$ and $A'' = 1$. Under dropout the term $y\,\wvec^\top\xvec$ has the same
expectation as without it, because the input is unbiased. The change in expected loss is all in $A$, and a second-order
expansion of $A$ around $\eta_i = \wvec^\top\xvec_i$ gives

$$
\expect A(\wvec^\top\tilde\xvec_i) - A(\wvec^\top\xvec_i) \approx \tfrac12 A''(\eta_i)\var(\wvec^\top\tilde\xvec_i)
= \tfrac12 A''(\eta_i)\frac{\delta}{1 - \delta}\sum_j x_{ij}^2w_j^2.
$$

Summing over examples, dropout adds the penalty

$$
R(\wvec) = \frac12\cdot\frac{\delta}{1 - \delta}\sum_j \paren{\sum_i A''(\eta_i)x_{ij}^2} w_j^2
$$

<Cite id="wager2013" />. The bracket is the $j$-th diagonal entry of the <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher
information</Gloss> $\Xmat^\top\Vmat\Xmat$, with $\Vmat = \diag(A''(\eta_i))$. Dropout is therefore, to second order, an
$L^2$ penalty applied after each feature is rescaled by the inverse square root of its Fisher information. Wager, Wang
and Liang call it adaptive for two reasons.

- **Rare features are penalised little.** A feature that is zero in most examples contributes to few terms of its
  bracket. Ridge penalises its weight as much as any other's, while dropout barely penalises it. Rare but
  discriminative features, common in text, keep large weights.
- **Confident examples count little.** In logistic regression, $A'' = p(1 - p)$ is small where the model is confident.
  A feature that appears only in confidently classified examples is penalised little.

For least squares, $A'' = 1$ and the penalty is $\frac{\delta}{2(1 - \delta)}\sum_j\paren{\sum_i x_{ij}^2}w_j^2$, ridge
scaled per feature by its energy, as in the <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> note. Additive Gaussian noise of
variance $\sigma^2$ in the same calculation gives $\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\sum_i A''(\eta_i)\norm{\wvec}^2$: one penalty
strength for all features, weighted by the examples' curvature.

## Worked example

**Input noise in a linear fit.** Fit $y = wx$ through the origin to $\xvec = (1, 2, 3)$ and $\yvec = (1, 2, 2)$. The
sums are $\sum x_i^2 = 14$ and $\sum x_iy_i = 11$.

1. Least squares gives $w = 11/14 = 0.786$.
2. Train on noisy inputs with $\sigma^2 = 0.5$. The penalty is $n\sigma^2 = 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$, so the minimiser is
   $w = 11/(14 + 1.5) = 0.710$, a shrinkage factor of $14/15.5 = 0.903$.
3. Least squares on 200 000 noisy copies of each point, $\sum\tilde x y/\sum\tilde x^2$, gives 0.7097, against the
   predicted 0.7097.

**Dropout's penalty on a rare feature.** Logistic regression on $n = 100$ examples has two binary features. Feature 1 is
1 in 50 examples, where the model predicts $p = 0.5$. Feature 2 is 1 in 5 examples, where it predicts $p = 0.95$. The
Fisher weights are

$$
\sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)x_{i1}^2 = 50 \times 0.25 = 12.5, \qquad \sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)x_{i2}^2 = 5 \times 0.0475 = 0.2375.
$$

With $\delta = 0.5$, the penalty is $\frac12(12.5\,w_1^2 + 0.2375\,w_2^2)$. Dropout penalises the rare, confidently
predicted feature 53 times less than the common one. Ridge, with $\lambda(w_1^2 + w_2^2)$, would penalise them equally.
Ridge on standardised features penalises each $w_j$ in proportion to its feature's variance, 0.25 against 0.0475, a
ratio of 5.3. Dropout's larger ratio also reflects the model's confidence on the examples where feature 2 appears.

## Assumptions

- **Small noise.** The gradient penalty is a second-order expansion. Large noise moves inputs outside the region where
  the model's <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> holds, and adds higher-order terms.
- **Squared error for the gradient form.** For other losses the second-order expansion weights the gradient penalty by
  the loss's curvature, as the generalised linear model calculation shows.
- **Fresh noise each time.** The equivalence is with the expected loss. A fixed set of noisy copies, drawn once,
  approximates it, with the gap shrinking as the number of copies grows.

## Pitfalls

- **Noise in the wrong units.** Isotropic noise on unstandardised inputs perturbs small-scale features more, relative to
  their spread, than large-scale ones, and the implied penalty treats them unequally. Standardise first, or scale the
  noise per feature.
- **Equivalent minimiser, different optimisation.** Stochastic noise makes the gradient noisy, so training with noise
  converges more slowly than minimising the explicit penalty. For a linear model, ridge is cheaper and exact.
- **Noise level is a hyperparameter.** $\sigma$ must be chosen like any regularisation strength, by validation (see
  <NoteLink to="choosing-the-regularisation-strength">choosing the regularisation strength</NoteLink>).
- **Not every noise regularises.** Target noise in squared error only adds a constant. Weight noise and input noise
  penalise different things, and An found that weight noise helped only on his classification problem
  <Cite id="an1996" />.
- **Train and test differ.** The model is trained on noisy inputs and evaluated on clean ones. For dropout the scaling
  by $1/(1 - \delta)$ keeps the expected input equal in the two modes; for additive noise no correction is needed,
  because the noise has mean zero.
