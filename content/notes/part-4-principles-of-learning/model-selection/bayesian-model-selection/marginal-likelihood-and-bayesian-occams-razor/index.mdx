<Definition>
A model $\Mcal$ is a likelihood $p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec, \Mcal)$ together with a prior $p(\thetavec \mid \Mcal)$ on its
parameters $\thetavec \in \reals^p$. Its **marginal likelihood**, or **evidence**, is the probability of the data
$\Dcal$ with the parameters integrated out,

$$
p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) = \int p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec, \Mcal)\, p(\thetavec \mid \Mcal)\, d\thetavec.
$$

It is the prior predictive probability of the observed data: the probability the model assigned to them before any
parameter was fitted. Ranking candidate models $\Mcal_1, \dots, \Mcal_J$ by their evidence is
<NoteLink to="bayesian-model-selection">Bayesian model selection</NoteLink> with equal prior probabilities on the models
<Cite id="mackay1992" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Fitting a model makes its training likelihood larger, and a larger model always fits at least as well as a smaller one
nested inside it. The maximised likelihood therefore cannot choose between models of different size. The evidence does
not fit anything. It asks how probable the data were under the model as specified, prior included, so a model earns
evidence only by having predicted the data in advance.

A probability distribution over data sets sums to one. A model with a narrow prior can produce only a few kinds of data
set, so it gives each of them high probability. A flexible model can produce many kinds, so it spreads the same unit of
probability thinly and gives each one less. When the data are of a kind both models can produce, the narrow model wins.
When the data are of a kind only the flexible model can produce, the flexible model wins, because the narrow model gave
them almost no probability. This is the **Bayesian Occam's razor**: the simplest model that can account for the data
has the largest evidence, and the penalty for flexibility comes from normalisation, not from an added term
<Cite id="mackay2003" />.

<OccamDensities />

The answer depends on the prior as much as on the likelihood. Two models with the same likelihood and different priors
are different models, and they have different evidence.

## The evidence

### What it answers

<NoteLink to="model-selection">Model selection</NoteLink> asks three questions (see
<NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>): how well a
candidate predicts, which candidate to choose, and how well the chosen one predicts. The evidence answers a version of
the second. Under equal prior probabilities $P(\Mcal_j) = 1/J$, <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>
applied to the model index gives

$$
P(\Mcal_j \mid \Dcal) = \frac{p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^J p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal_k)},
$$

so the model with the largest evidence is the most probable model. The ratio of two evidences is the
<NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factor</NoteLink>, which owns the comparison of two models and
Lindley's paradox. The posterior probability is meaningful when one of the candidates generated the data. The evidence
estimates nothing about predictive error directly, so it does not answer the first or the third question.

### The evidence is a sequential predictive score

The <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">chain rule of probability</NoteLink> writes the joint probability of
$\Dcal = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$ as a product of conditionals. Applied to the evidence,

$$
\log p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid y_1, \dots, y_{i-1}, \Mcal),
$$

where each factor $p(y_i \mid y_{<i}, \Mcal) = \int p(y_i \mid \thetavec)\, p(\thetavec \mid y_{<i})\, d\thetavec$ is
the posterior predictive density of point $i$ given the points before it, and the first factor is the prior predictive
$p(y_1 \mid \Mcal)$. The log evidence is the total log score of a forecaster that predicts each point from the ones
before it. The identity holds for every ordering of the data, because the joint probability does not depend on the
order.

Averaging over orderings gives a <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> reading. Fong and Holmes
showed that the log evidence equals the sum over $p = 1, \dots, n$ of the exhaustive leave-$p$-out cross-validation
score, the average log predictive density of $p$ held-out points given the other $n - p$, averaged over all
$\binom{n}{p}$ held-out sets <Cite id="fong2020" />. The term with $p = n$ predicts every point from the prior alone.
The evidence therefore weighs predictions made from very little data as heavily as predictions made from nearly all of
it. That explains its sensitivity to the prior, and it is the main difference from
<NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">leave-one-out cross-validation</NoteLink>, which scores only predictions
from $n - 1$ points.

## The Occam factor

For $n$ observations the likelihood $p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)$ is sharply peaked at the maximum-likelihood estimate
$\hat\thetavec$, with a width that shrinks like $n^{-1/2}$. When the prior is much broader than the peak, the integral
in the definition is the height of the peak times its width. In one dimension, write $\sigma_{\theta \mid \Dcal}$ for
the width of the posterior and $\sigma_\theta$ for the width of the prior, and approximate the prior density at the
peak by $1/\sigma_\theta$:

$$
p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) \approx \underbrace{p(\Dcal \mid \hat\theta, \Mcal)}_{\text{best fit}} \times
\underbrace{\frac{\sigma_{\theta \mid \Dcal}}{\sigma_\theta}}_{\text{Occam factor}}.
$$

The **Occam factor** is the fraction of the prior's range that remains plausible after seeing the data. It is at most
about one, and it is small when the prior was broad and the data pinned the parameter down. A model pays it once per
parameter, so a flexible model with many parameters must improve the fit by a factor that beats the product of its
Occam factors <Cite id="mackay1992" />.

In $p$ dimensions the same argument is the <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink>. Let
$\Amat = -\nabla^2 \log p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)p(\thetavec)$ be the curvature of the log posterior at its mode
$\hat\thetavec$. Then

$$
\log p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) \approx \log p(\Dcal \mid \hat\thetavec) + \underbrace{\log p(\hat\thetavec) + \frac{p}{2}\log 2\pi
- \frac12 \log\abs{\Amat}}_{\log \text{Occam factor}}.
$$

The term $(2\pi)^{p/2}\abs{\Amat}^{-1/2}$ is the volume of the posterior, and $p(\hat\thetavec)$ is one over the volume
of the prior when the prior is flat near the mode. For $n$ independent observations $\Amat$ grows like $n$, so
$\log\abs{\Amat} = p\log n + O(1)$ and the log Occam factor is $-\frac{p}{2}\log n + O(1)$. Dropping the $O(1)$ terms
and multiplying by $-2$ gives the <NoteLink to="bayesian-information-criterion">Bayesian information
criterion</NoteLink>. BIC keeps the part of the Occam factor that grows with $n$ and discards the part that depends on
the prior.

The penalty is not a count of parameters. A parameter that the data do not constrain has a posterior as wide as its
prior, so its Occam factor is one and it costs nothing. A parameter whose prior already concentrates where the data put
it also costs little. The evidence charges mainly for the directions in which the data are much more informative than
the prior. In a linear-Gaussian model their number is the effective number of parameters $\gamma$ of
<NoteLink to="empirical-bayes-for-hyperparameters">evidence maximisation</NoteLink>, which equals the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> of the fit.

## Worked example

Let $y_1, \dots, y_{16}$ be independent $\Gauss(\mu, 1)$, with sample mean $\bar y = 0.5$. Compare four models of the
mean: $\Mcal_0$ fixes $\mu = 0$, and $\Mcal_\tau$ gives $\mu$ a $\Gauss(0, \tau^2)$ prior with $\tau = 0.5$, $2$ or
$20$.

**Step 1: split off the best fit.** Write $y_i - \mu = (y_i - \bar y) + (\bar y - \mu)$. The cross term sums to zero, so
$\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 + n(\bar y - \mu)^2$ and

$$
p(\Dcal \mid \mu) = p(\Dcal \mid \hat\mu)\, \exp\paren{-\tfrac{n}{2}(\bar y - \mu)^2}, \qquad \hat\mu = \bar y.
$$

The first factor is the same for every model, so the models differ only through the second.

**Step 2: the model with no parameter.** $\Mcal_0$ sets $\mu = 0$, so its evidence is
$p(\Dcal \mid \hat\mu)\, e^{-n\bar y^2/2} = p(\Dcal \mid \hat\mu)\, e^{-2}$. Its factor is $e^{-2} = 0.135$. It pays
nothing for flexibility and everything for misfit.

**Step 3: the models with a prior.** Integrating the second factor against the $\Gauss(0, \tau^2)$ prior is a
<NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>. The exponential is $\sqrt{2\pi/n}$ times a
$\Gauss(\mu \mid \bar y, 1/n)$ density in $\mu$, and the integral of a product of two Gaussian densities is a
Gaussian density of the difference of their means with the variances added:

$$
\frac{p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal_\tau)}{p(\Dcal \mid \hat\mu)} = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}}\; \Gauss\paren{\bar y \mid 0,\, \tau^2 +
\tfrac1n}.
$$

This is the exact Occam factor. When $\tau$ is large the Gaussian density is about $1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,\tau)$, and the
factor tends to $(1/\sqrt n)/\tau$: the posterior width $0.25$ over the prior width $\tau$.

**Step 4: the numbers.**

| Model | prior sd $\tau$ | posterior sd of $\mu$ | Occam factor | log factor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\Mcal_0$ | 0 | 0 | 0.135 | −2.00 |
| $\Mcal_{0.5}$ | 0.5 | 0.224 | 0.300 | −1.20 |
| $\Mcal_2$ | 2 | 0.248 | 0.120 | −2.12 |
| $\Mcal_{20}$ | 20 | 0.250 | 0.0125 | −4.38 |

**Step 5: read the table.** $\Mcal_{0.5}$ has the largest evidence. Its prior expected small means, and $\bar y = 0.5$
is a small mean, so most of its prior mass stayed plausible. $\Mcal_{20}$ has the same best fit and almost the same
posterior as $\Mcal_{0.5}$, yet its evidence is $e^{4.38 - 1.20} = 24$ times smaller, because it spent 99% of its prior
on means the data ruled out. $\Mcal_0$ fits worse than $\Mcal_{20}$ but beats it, since a misfit of $e^{-2}$ costs less
than an Occam factor of $1/80$. The sequential reading gives the same verdict: $\Mcal_{20}$ predicted the first
observation with a $\Gauss(0, 401)$ density, which is at most $0.020$ anywhere.

## Polynomial degree

A polynomial of degree $d$ with an isotropic Gaussian prior on its $d + 1$ coefficients is
<NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink>, whose evidence has a closed form.
Raising $d$ improves the best fit and adds a coefficient whose Occam factor is below one. The evidence first rises,
while each coefficient buys more fit than it costs, and then falls.

<EvidenceAgainstDegree />

The held-out log predictive density in the figure is the truth that the evidence is trying to track. The two curves
usually peak at nearby degrees. They differ in what they reward: the held-out score judges the posterior predictive
after all $n$ points, while the evidence also charges for the predictions made from the first few.

## Pitfalls

- **Vague priors.** The evidence of a model with a $\Gauss(0, \tau^2)$ prior falls like $1/\tau$ once $\tau$ is much
  wider than the posterior, while the posterior barely changes. An arbitrarily vague prior makes any model lose to a
  simpler one. An improper prior has an arbitrary constant and no evidence at all. Choose prior scales from the problem
  (see <NoteLink to="prior-choice">prior choice</NoteLink>) and report how the comparison moves with them.
- **Evidence is not predictive accuracy.** The evidence asks whether the data were probable under the prior, and the
  predictive criteria (<NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">WAIC</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS-LOO</NoteLink>) ask whether the posterior
  predicts new points. Lotfi and colleagues give cases where the evidence prefers the model that generalises worse,
  including <NoteLink to="neural-architectures">neural architectures</NoteLink>; they propose the conditional marginal
  likelihood, which drops the first $m - 1$ terms of the sequential sum <Cite id="lotfi2022marginal" />.
- **Every candidate wrong.** When no candidate generated the data, the posterior model probabilities still concentrate
  on one model as $n$ grows, the one closest to the truth in <Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss>. A
  probability near one then says that the data distinguish the candidates, not that the chosen one is right. See
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">Bayesian model averaging</NoteLink> for the M-open case.
- **Computing it from posterior draws.** The harmonic mean of the likelihood over posterior draws is a consistent
  estimator of the evidence with infinite variance in common models <Cite id="newton1994" />. Use a closed form when the
  model is conjugate, the Laplace approximation when the posterior is near Gaussian, and otherwise bridge sampling,
  nested sampling or thermodynamic integration <Cite id="llorente2023" />.
- **Maximising it over many hyperparameters.** The evidence is an integral over parameters, not over hyperparameters.
  Maximising it over a rich family of priors fits the prior to the data and can overfit like any training objective;
  see <NoteLink to="empirical-bayes-for-hyperparameters">empirical Bayes</NoteLink>.
- **Reusing it as an assessment.** The evidence of the model that won a comparison is the largest of $J$ noisy scores,
  so it is optimistic about that model in the same way as the best cross-validation score
  (<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Assess the chosen model on
  held-out data.
