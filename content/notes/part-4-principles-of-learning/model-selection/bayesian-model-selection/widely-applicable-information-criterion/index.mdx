<Definition>
Let a model have pointwise likelihood $p(y_i \mid \thetavec)$ for observations $y_1, \dots, y_n$ that are independent
given $\thetavec$, and let $\thetavec^{(1)}, \dots, \thetavec^{(S)}$ be draws from the posterior
$p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal)$. The **log pointwise predictive density** and the
**<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective number of parameters</NoteLink>** are

$$
\operatorname{lppd} = \sum_{i=1}^n \log\paren{\frac1S\sum_{s=1}^S p(y_i \mid \thetavec^{(s)})}, \qquad
p_{\text{WAIC}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \var_{s}\brack{\log p(y_i \mid \thetavec^{(s)})},
$$

where $\var_s$ is the sample variance over the $S$ draws. The **widely applicable information criterion** estimates the
expected log predictive density of new data by
$\widehat{\operatorname{elpd}}_{\text{WAIC}} = \operatorname{lppd} - p_{\text{WAIC}}$, or on the deviance scale
$\operatorname{WAIC} = -2\,\widehat{\operatorname{elpd}}_{\text{WAIC}}$ <Cite id="watanabe2010" />
<Cite id="gelman2014predictive" />. Larger elpd, or smaller WAIC, is better.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A Bayesian model predicts with its <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">posterior predictive distribution</NoteLink>, which
averages the likelihood over the posterior. The natural score for that prediction is the log density it gives to new
observations. The data that formed the posterior are the only data at hand, and scoring the posterior predictive on them
is optimistic: each point pulled the posterior towards itself before being scored. This is the optimism of the training
error (<NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>), now for a predictive
distribution instead of a point estimate.

WAIC subtracts an estimate of that optimism, as AIC does for a maximum-likelihood fit
(<NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">Akaike information criterion</NoteLink>). AIC's correction counts
parameters. WAIC's correction measures how much each point's log-likelihood moves across the posterior. A point whose
fit is stable across posterior draws contributes nothing. A point whose fit depends on parameters that only it
constrains contributes a lot, because removing it would change its prediction most. The sum over points behaves like a
parameter count when the prior is weak and the model is regular, and it is smaller when the prior constrains the
parameters.

WAIC answers the first question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>, how well a candidate
predicts, for a Bayesian model. It needs one posterior fit per candidate and no held-out data.

## Details

### The target

Let $\tilde y_i$ be a new observation from the same distribution as $y_i$, at the same covariates when there are any.
The **expected log pointwise predictive density** of the fitted model is

$$
\operatorname{elpd} = \sum_{i=1}^n \expect_{\tilde y_i}\brack{\log p(\tilde y_i \mid \Dcal)}, \qquad
p(\tilde y_i \mid \Dcal) = \int p(\tilde y_i \mid \thetavec)\, p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal)\, d\thetavec.
$$

It is $n$ times the negative expected <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">log loss</NoteLink> of the posterior
predictive on fresh data, and the difference between two models' elpd is a difference in their expected
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink> from the truth, as for AIC. The lppd
is the same sum with each $\tilde y_i$ replaced by the observed $y_i$, and with the posterior predictive estimated by
the average over draws.

### The correction

The derivation for a model where everything is Gaussian shows where the correction comes from. Let
$y_i \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known, and give $\mu$ a flat prior. The posterior is
$\mu \mid \Dcal \sim \Gauss(\bar y, v)$ with $v = \sigma^2/n$, and the posterior predictive is
$\Gauss(\bar y, \sigma^2 + v)$. Write $d_i = y_i - \bar y$ and $c = \sigma^2 + v$.

1. **The lppd.** With exact posterior averages, $\operatorname{lppd} = \sum_i \log\Gauss(y_i \mid \bar y, c) =
   -\frac n2\log(2\pi c) - \frac{1}{2c}\sum_i d_i^2$.
2. **The elpd.** A new $\tilde y_i \sim \Gauss(\mu_0, \sigma^2)$ is independent of $\bar y$, so
   $\expect(\tilde y_i - \bar y)^2 = \sigma^2 + (\bar y - \mu_0)^2$ given the data. Then
   $\operatorname{elpd} = -\frac n2\log(2\pi c) - \frac{n}{2c}\paren{\sigma^2 + (\bar y - \mu_0)^2}$.
3. **The optimism.** Over data sets, $\expect\sum_i d_i^2 = (n - 1)\sigma^2$ and
   $\expect(\bar y - \mu_0)^2 = \sigma^2/n$. The expected difference is $\expect[\operatorname{lppd} - \operatorname{elpd}] = \frac{1}{2c}\paren{n\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 - (n - 1)\sigma^2} =
   \frac{\sigma^2}{c} = \frac{n}{n + 1}$.
4. **The posterior variance of each log-likelihood.** Under the posterior, $\mu = \bar y + e$ with
   $e \sim \Gauss(0, v)$, and $\log p(y_i \mid \mu) = -(d_i - e)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ plus a constant. Since $e$ is Gaussian
   with mean zero, $\var[(d_i - e)^2] = 4d_i^2 v + 2v^2$, so the variance of the log-likelihood is
   $d_i^2 v/\sigma^4 + v^2/(2\sigma^4)$.
5. **Its sum.** $\expect[p_{\text{WAIC}}] = (n - 1)\sigma^2 \cdot \frac{v}{\sigma^4} + \frac{nv^2}{2\sigma^4} = \frac{n -
   1}{n} + \frac{1}{2n} = 1 - \frac{1}{2n}$.

The optimism of the lppd is $n/(n + 1)$ and $p_{\text{WAIC}}$ estimates it by $1 - 1/(2n)$. Both tend to $1$, the
number of parameters, and AIC's penalty is recovered. The variance does the counting: it is large exactly for the
points with large residuals $d_i$, whose fit the parameter can trade against the others.

### Equivalence with leave-one-out

Bayesian leave-one-out cross-validation estimates the elpd by
$\operatorname{elpd}_{\text{loo}} = \sum_i \log p(y_i \mid \Dcal_{-i})$, where $\Dcal_{-i}$ is the data without point
$i$. Watanabe proved that WAIC and Bayesian leave-one-out are asymptotically equivalent as random variables, in regular
and in singular models <Cite id="watanabe2010" />. Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry show how close the two are by expanding
both in the cumulants of the pointwise log-likelihood $\log p(y_i \mid \thetavec)$ under the posterior
<Cite id="vehtari2017" />. The log of the posterior predictive density, $\log p(y_i \mid \Dcal)$, is the posterior mean
of the log-likelihood plus half its posterior variance plus terms in the higher cumulants. Subtracting the variance,
WAIC's term is the mean minus half the variance plus those same higher terms. The leave-one-out term has the same first
three terms of the expansion and different higher terms. WAIC is therefore a truncated approximation of leave-one-out.
When the higher cumulants are not negligible, which happens when one point moves the posterior a lot, WAIC is biased
towards the lppd, that is, towards optimism.

### Singular models

AIC and <Gloss name="bic" /> assume a regular model: the <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss> is
invertible and the posterior is near Gaussian for large $n$. Mixtures, hidden-unit models, neural networks and
reduced-rank regressions are **singular**: different parameter values give the same distribution, the Fisher information
is degenerate, and the parameter count does not measure complexity. Watanabe derived WAIC from singular
learning theory, and it remains an asymptotically unbiased
estimate of the predictive loss in that setting <Cite id="watanabe2010" />. In a singular model $p_{\text{WAIC}}$ is
usually smaller than the number of parameters.

### WBIC

Watanabe also gave a singular-model replacement for BIC. The **widely applicable Bayesian information criterion** is the
posterior mean of the negative log-likelihood $-\log p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)$ under a tempered posterior, proportional
to $p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)^{\beta}p(\thetavec)$ with inverse temperature $\beta = 1/\log n$. It has the same asymptotic
expansion as the negative log marginal likelihood, in regular and singular models alike <Cite id="watanabe2013" />. WBIC
estimates the evidence and WAIC estimates predictive accuracy. They target different quantities, as BIC and AIC do (see
<NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

Five observations from $\Gauss(\mu, 1)$ are $y = (-0.5, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 2.6)$, with mean $\bar y = 0.9$. The prior on
$\mu$ is flat, so the posterior is $\Gauss(0.9, 0.2)$ and the posterior predictive is $\Gauss(0.9, 1.2)$. The table uses
the exact posterior averages of steps 1 and 4 above, the limit of infinitely many draws.

| $y_i$ | $d_i$ | $\log p(y_i \mid \Dcal)$ | $p_{\text{WAIC}, i}$ | elpd$_{\text{WAIC},i}$ | elpd$_{\text{loo},i}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| −0.5 | −1.4 | −1.827 | 0.412 | −2.239 | −2.256 |
| 0.4 | −0.5 | −1.114 | 0.070 | −1.184 | −1.187 |
| 0.8 | −0.1 | −1.014 | 0.022 | −1.036 | −1.037 |
| 1.2 | 0.3 | −1.048 | 0.038 | −1.086 | −1.087 |
| 2.6 | 1.7 | −2.214 | 0.598 | −2.812 | −2.837 |
| sum | | −7.217 | 1.140 | −8.357 | −8.403 |

1. **The variance term.** For the first point, $p_{\text{WAIC},1} = d_1^2 v + v^2/2 = 1.96 \times 0.2 + 0.02 = 0.412$.
2. **The criterion.** $\widehat{\operatorname{elpd}}_{\text{WAIC}} = -7.217 - 1.140 = -8.357$, and
   $\operatorname{WAIC} = 16.71$.
3. **Leave-one-out.** Exact leave-one-out predicts $y_i$ from $\Gauss(\bar y_{-i}, 1 + 1/4)$, with $\bar y_{-i}$ the
   mean of the other four. It gives $-8.403$, within $0.05$ of WAIC. The two extreme points carry most of the
   correction, and WAIC's error is largest there.
4. **Against the truth.** If the true mean is $\mu = 1$, the elpd of this posterior predictive on new data is $-7.155$.
   Both estimates are about $1.2$ too low on this data set, whose points are more spread out than unit-variance noise
   usually makes them. The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of
   $\widehat{\operatorname{elpd}}_{\text{WAIC}}$, $\sqrt{n}$ times the standard deviation of the pointwise terms, is
   $1.8$, so the miss is within one standard error.

Two of the five points have $p_{\text{WAIC},i} > 0.4$, the level above which Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry found WAIC
unreliable in simulation <Cite id="vehtari2017" />. With five points and a flat prior, each extreme point really does
move the posterior.

## Pitfalls

- **Weak priors and influential points.** When any $p_{\text{WAIC},i}$ exceeds about $0.4$, the second-order expansion
  is poor and WAIC is biased towards optimism. Use <NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS
  leave-one-out</NoteLink>, which has a diagnostic for exactly this case, or exact refits.
- **What a point is.** WAIC needs a factorised likelihood, and the factorisation decides what "new data" means. In a
  hierarchical model, a point can be one observation (predicting a new observation in a known group) or one group
  (predicting a new group). The two give different answers to different questions. Choose the unit that matches the
  prediction task, as <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink> does.
- **Dependent data.** For <NoteLink to="time-series">time series</NoteLink>, leave-one-out and WAIC score predictions of
  past points from future ones. Use leave-future-out evaluation (see
  <NoteLink to="time-series-cross-validation">time-series cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Differences without standard errors.** WAIC is a sum of $n$ terms. To compare two models, take the pointwise
  differences of their elpd terms; their sum is the estimated difference, and $\sqrt n$ times their standard deviation
  is its standard error <Cite id="vehtari2017" />. A difference below about two standard errors is weak evidence.
- **Reading $p_{\text{WAIC}}$ as a parameter count.** It measures how much the fit varies over the posterior. Strong
  priors, hierarchical shrinkage and singular structure all make it smaller than the number of parameters. A badly
  misfitting model makes it larger, because the variance term $d_i^2 v$ grows with each residual $d_i$; a constant
  fitted to a sine can have $p_{\text{WAIC}}$ of five or more. It need not be an integer.
- **Selecting on it and then reporting it.** The WAIC of the model that won a comparison of many candidates is
  optimistic, like the best <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> score
  (<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Assess the chosen model on
  held-out data.
