<Definition>
Let $D(\thetavec) = -2\log p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)$ be the **deviance** of a model with parameters $\thetavec$, and let
$\bar\thetavec = \expect[\thetavec \mid \Dcal]$ be the posterior mean. The **effective number of parameters** and the
**deviance information criterion** are

$$
p_D = \overline{D} - D(\bar\thetavec), \qquad \operatorname{DIC} = D(\bar\thetavec) + 2p_D = \overline{D} + p_D,
$$

where $\overline{D} = \expect[D(\thetavec) \mid \Dcal]$ is the posterior mean of the deviance
<Cite id="spiegelhalter2002" />. Smaller DIC is better. From posterior draws $\thetavec^{(1)}, \dots, \thetavec^{(S)}$,
$\overline{D}$ is the average of $D(\thetavec^{(s)})$ and $\bar\thetavec$ is the average of the draws.
</Definition>

## Intuition

DIC is AIC with Bayesian ingredients. AIC takes the deviance of the maximum-likelihood fit and adds twice the number of
parameters to correct its optimism (<NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">Akaike information
criterion</NoteLink>). DIC replaces the fit by the posterior mean and the count by $p_D$, a measure of how many
parameters the data actually determine.

$p_D$ measures how far the deviance rises, on average, when the parameters wander over the posterior instead of sitting
at its centre. A parameter that the data pin down has a narrow posterior, but moving it across that posterior still
changes the deviance by about one unit, because the posterior width matches the likelihood's curvature. A parameter that
the prior pins down has a posterior much narrower than the likelihood's curvature allows, so moving it barely changes
the deviance. $p_D$ therefore counts the parameters the data determine and discounts the ones the prior determines.

Spiegelhalter and colleagues introduced DIC for <NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">hierarchical models</NoteLink> fitted
by <Gloss name="mcmc" />, where the number of parameters is ill-defined: a random effect per group is a parameter, but
shrinkage makes it worth less than one <Cite id="spiegelhalter2002" />. DIC became the default criterion of the
<NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">BUGS</NoteLink> software. It answers the first question of
<NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>, how well a candidate predicts, by an analytic correction
instead of held-out data.

## Details

### Why $p_D$ counts parameters

Expand the deviance to second order about the posterior mean. Let $\Imat$ be the observed information, the Hessian of
$-\log p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)$ at $\bar\thetavec$, and assume $\bar\thetavec$ is close enough to the maximum-likelihood
estimate that the gradient term is negligible. Then $D(\thetavec) \approx D(\bar\thetavec) + (\thetavec -
\bar\thetavec)^\top \Imat (\thetavec - \bar\thetavec)$. Take the posterior expectation. A scalar equals its trace, and
the trace is cyclic, so

$$
p_D = \expect\brack{(\thetavec - \bar\thetavec)^\top \Imat (\thetavec - \bar\thetavec) \mid \Dcal} =
\trace\paren{\Imat\, \Sigmamat}, \qquad \Sigmamat = \cov(\thetavec \mid \Dcal).
$$

For a Gaussian prior with precision $\Lambdamat$ and a near-Gaussian posterior, $\Sigmamat \approx (\Imat +
\Lambdamat)^{-1}$, so $p_D \approx \trace\paren{\Imat(\Imat + \Lambdamat)^{-1}}$. With a flat prior this is
$\trace\eye_p = p$, the parameter count of AIC. With an informative prior each eigen-direction contributes
$\lambda_j/(\lambda_j + \text{prior precision})$, a number between 0 and 1. In
<NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear regression</NoteLink> $p_D$ is exactly MacKay's $\gamma$, the
trace of the hat matrix, the <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>.

### Why $2p_D$ is the penalty

The posterior mean deviance $\overline{D}$ is itself an optimistic measure of fit, and $D(\bar\thetavec)$ more so.
Spiegelhalter and colleagues argued, by the same expansion as for AIC, that the expected deviance of the plug-in
prediction $p(\tilde y \mid \bar\thetavec)$ on new data exceeds $D(\bar\thetavec)$ by about $2p_D$
<Cite id="spiegelhalter2002" />. Plummer showed that DIC approximates a penalised loss function whose penalty comes from
a <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> argument, and that the approximation is valid only when
the effective number of parameters is much smaller than the number of independent observations. When it is not, as in
disease mapping with one random effect per area, DIC under-penalises the more complex models <Cite id="plummer2008" />.

### DIC predicts with a point, not with the posterior

DIC scores the plug-in density $p(\tilde y \mid \bar\thetavec)$. <Gloss name="waic" /> and leave-one-out score the
posterior predictive $p(\tilde y \mid \Dcal)$, the average of the likelihood over the posterior. The two coincide only
when the posterior is concentrated. Watanabe noted that DIC is not asymptotically equivalent to Bayesian
cross-validation in singular models <Cite id="watanabe2010" />. Gelman, Hwang and Vehtari recommend WAIC or
leave-one-out over DIC for this reason <Cite id="gelman2014predictive" />.

### An alternative $p_D$

Gelman and colleagues give a second estimate, $p_V = \frac12\var\brack{D(\thetavec) \mid \Dcal}$, half the posterior
variance of the deviance <Cite id="gelman2013" />. For a Gaussian posterior and flat prior the deviance is
$D(\bar\thetavec)$ plus a $\ChiSq_p$ variable, whose variance is $2p$, so $p_V = p$. $p_V$ needs no point estimate and
is always positive. It is also more variable, because a variance is estimated from the draws. With an informative prior
the posterior mean is not the best fit, the deviance gains a term linear in $\thetavec - \bar\thetavec$, and $p_V$
exceeds $p_D$; the figure below shows the gap.

## Worked example

**Shrinkage lowers $p_D$.** Take $n = 10$ observations $y_i \sim \Gauss(\mu, 1)$ and the prior $\mu \sim \Gauss(0,
\tau^2)$. The deviance is $\sum_i (y_i - \mu)^2$ plus a constant, so $\Imat = n$, and the posterior variance is
$v = 1/(n + 1/\tau^2)$. The deviance is exactly quadratic in $\mu$, so $p_D = nv = n/(n + 1/\tau^2)$:

| prior sd $\tau$ | 0.1 | 0.3 | 1 | 10 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p_D$ | 0.09 | 0.47 | 0.91 | 1.00 |

A prior with $\tau = 0.1$ has precision 100, worth a hundred observations of $\mu$ against the data's ten, so the data
determine less than a tenth of the parameter.

**The parameterisation changes $p_D$.** Observe $k$ successes in $n = 10$ Bernoulli trials with a uniform prior on the
success probability $\theta$. The posterior is $\operatorname{Beta}(k + 1, n - k + 1)$ and the deviance is
$D(\theta) = -2[k\log\theta + (n - k)\log(1 - \theta)]$. Its posterior mean follows from
$\expect[\log\theta] = \psi(k + 1) - \psi(n + 2)$, where $\psi$ is the digamma function. The plug-in point depends on
the scale on which the posterior mean is taken: the mean of $\theta$, or the mean of the log-odds
$\eta = \log(\theta/(1 - \theta))$ mapped back to $\theta$.

| $k$ | $\overline{D}$ | $\bar\theta$ | $p_D$ for $\theta$ | $\theta$ from $\bar\eta$ | $p_D$ for $\eta$ | $p_V$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 14.73 | 0.500 | 0.87 | 0.500 | 0.87 | 0.75 |
| 1 | 7.48 | 0.167 | 0.61 | 0.138 | 0.84 | 0.95 |
| 0 | 1.82 | 0.083 | 0.08 | 0.051 | 0.78 | 1.65 |

With $k = 5$ the posterior is symmetric and both parameterisations agree. With $k = 1$ the two values of $p_D$ differ by
$0.23$. With $k = 0$ the posterior is piled against $\theta = 0$, and $p_D$ is $0.08$ on one scale and $0.78$ on the
other. The model, the data and the posterior are identical in every row; only the coordinates differ. $p_V$ does not
depend on coordinates, but it gives a third answer.

## Pitfalls

- **Not invariant to reparameterisation.** $D(\bar\thetavec)$ depends on which function of $\thetavec$ is averaged, as
  the binomial example shows. Using the posterior median or mode instead of the mean changes $p_D$ again. Fix the
  parameterisation before comparing models and say which it is.
- **Negative $p_D$.** When the posterior mean is a poor summary, $D(\bar\thetavec)$ can exceed $\overline{D}$ and
  $p_D < 0$. This happens with bimodal posteriors and mixture models, where the mean of the parameters can lie between
  modes in a region of low likelihood. For mixtures and random-effect models the missing variables can be treated as
  parameters or integrated out, which gives several different DICs; Celeux and colleagues compared them and found that
  they behave differently <Cite id="celeux2006" />.
- **Under-penalising complex models.** The penalty $2p_D$ is an asymptotic approximation, and it is too small when
  $p_D$ is large relative to $n$, as in hierarchical models with many groups and few observations each
  <Cite id="plummer2008" />.
- **The focus in hierarchical models.** The deviance can condition on the group parameters (predicting new
  observations in existing groups) or integrate them out (predicting new groups). The two give different $p_D$ and
  answer different questions <Cite id="spiegelhalter2014" />.
- **Using it today.** WAIC and <NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS
  leave-one-out</NoteLink> use the same posterior draws, score the full posterior predictive and work in singular
  models. DIC remains in older software and older papers; read it as a rough AIC for models with priors.
