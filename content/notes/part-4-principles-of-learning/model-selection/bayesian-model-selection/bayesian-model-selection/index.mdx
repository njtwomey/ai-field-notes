<Definition>
Bayesian <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink> compares models that each consist of a likelihood and
a prior, using the posterior distribution instead of a single fitted parameter. One family of methods scores a model by
its evidence, the probability of the data under the prior. The other scores it by how well its posterior predictive
distribution predicts new observations. Both can also tune hyperparameters, and both lead to averaging over models
instead of choosing one.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A Bayesian model is fitted by computing a posterior, not by maximising a likelihood, so the classical criteria do not
apply directly. AIC and <Gloss name="bic" /> assume a maximum-likelihood fit with a countable number of parameters
(<NoteLink to="information-criteria">information criteria</NoteLink>). Hierarchical models, <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian
processes</Gloss>, mixtures and neural networks with priors have neither: shrinkage makes a parameter worth less than
one, and singular models make the parameter count meaningless. The methods of this topic answer the chapter's first two
questions for such models, how well a candidate will predict and which candidate or hyperparameter to choose, using the
posterior draws or closed forms that the fit already produces. Assessing the chosen model still needs data the choice
never saw (<NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>).

## Main ideas

**The evidence and Occam's razor.** The marginal likelihood $p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal)$ averages the likelihood over the
prior. It equals the best-fit likelihood times an Occam factor, the fraction of the prior that survives the data, so a
flexible model pays for its flexibility without an added penalty term. The log
evidence is also a sequential predictive score, the sum of each point's predictive density given the points before it,
which explains its sensitivity to the prior
(<NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood and Bayesian Occam's
razor</NoteLink>). The ratio of two evidences is the <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes
factor</NoteLink>, and BIC is its large-sample approximation.

**Empirical Bayes.** Maximising the evidence over hyperparameters, such as a prior scale, a ridge penalty, a noise level
or kernel length-scales, sets them without held-out data and without a grid. In the normal means problem it reproduces
the <NoteLink to="james-stein-estimator">James–Stein estimator</NoteLink>; in <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear
regression</NoteLink> it gives MacKay's fixed-point updates; in Gaussian processes it is the standard fit. With many
hyperparameters it becomes a training objective and can overfit
(<NoteLink to="empirical-bayes-for-hyperparameters">empirical Bayes for hyperparameters</NoteLink>).

**Predictive accuracy from posterior draws.** The expected log predictive density of new data is the Bayesian analogue
of test error. Scoring the posterior predictive on the training points is optimistic, and two corrections use only
posterior draws. The <NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">widely applicable information
criterion</NoteLink> subtracts the summed posterior variance of each point's log-likelihood and is valid for singular
models. <NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS leave-one-out</NoteLink> estimates exact
Bayesian leave-one-out by importance weighting, stabilised by a Pareto tail fit whose shape $\hat k$ flags the points
where it fails. The older <NoteLink to="deviance-information-criterion">deviance information criterion</NoteLink> plugs
in the posterior mean and counts effective parameters by $p_D$; it depends on the parameterisation.

**Evidence or prediction.** The two families answer different questions. The evidence asks which model is most
probable, and it is meaningful when one candidate generated the data (the M-closed setting). Predictive criteria ask
which posterior predicts new data best, and they remain meaningful when no candidate is true (M-open). The evidence
depends strongly on vague priors; predictive criteria depend on them weakly.

**Averaging instead of choosing.** <NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">Bayesian model averaging</NoteLink> weights
each model's predictions by its posterior probability. It is optimal in the M-closed setting, and its weights collapse
onto one model as data accumulate. Stacking of predictive distributions chooses weights for leave-one-out accuracy,
which suits the M-open setting.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with the <NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>, which
  defines the evidence and the Occam factor used by the next note and by model averaging.
- <NoteLink to="empirical-bayes-for-hyperparameters">Empirical Bayes</NoteLink> applies the evidence to continuous
  hyperparameters.
- <NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">WAIC</NoteLink> introduces the predictive target, the elpd, and
  the lppd; <NoteLink to="deviance-information-criterion">DIC</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS-LOO</NoteLink> estimate the same target in other
  ways and are best read after it.
- <NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">Bayesian model averaging</NoteLink> comes last; it uses the evidence for BMA
  weights and PSIS-LOO for stacking weights.
