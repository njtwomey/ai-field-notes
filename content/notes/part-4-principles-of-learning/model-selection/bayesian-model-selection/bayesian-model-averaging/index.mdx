<Definition>
Let $\Mcal_1, \dots, \Mcal_J$ be candidate models with prior probabilities $P(\Mcal_j)$ and evidences
$p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal_j)$. **Bayesian model averaging** (BMA) predicts a new observation $\tilde y$ with the mixture

$$
p(\tilde y \mid \Dcal) = \sum_{j=1}^J P(\Mcal_j \mid \Dcal)\; p(\tilde y \mid \Dcal, \Mcal_j), \qquad
P(\Mcal_j \mid \Dcal) = \frac{p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal_j)\, P(\Mcal_j)}{\sum_k p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal_k)\, P(\Mcal_k)},
$$

where $p(\tilde y \mid \Dcal, \Mcal_j)$ is the posterior predictive of model $j$. It treats the model index as one more
unknown and integrates it out, as <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian inference</NoteLink> integrates out
parameters <Cite id="hoeting1999" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Choosing one model and predicting with it treats the choice as certain. When the data leave two models nearly equally
probable, that certainty is false, and predictions from the chosen model are overconfident: their intervals ignore the
chance that the other model was right. Averaging keeps every candidate in play, weighted by how well it predicted the
data. Selection is the special case in which one weight is set to one.

Averaging and selection differ only when the data cannot separate the models. As $n$ grows, the posterior model
probabilities concentrate, and BMA turns into selection of the most probable model. BMA is therefore soft selection,
not a way of combining models that are each partly right <Cite id="minka2002" />. Which of these is wanted depends on
whether the true model is among the candidates.

## Details

### Why averaging is optimal when a candidate is true

Suppose the data were generated by first drawing a model from $P(\Mcal_j)$, then parameters from its prior, then data.
Any predictive rule $q(\tilde y \mid \Dcal)$ is judged by its expected <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">log
loss</NoteLink> $\expect[-\log q(\tilde y \mid \Dcal)]$ over this whole process. Condition on the observed $\Dcal$.
Given $\Dcal$, the new observation is distributed as the BMA predictive $p(\tilde y \mid \Dcal)$, because BMA is that
conditional distribution computed by the sum rule. The expected log loss of $q$ given $\Dcal$ is then the
<NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy</NoteLink>

$$
\expect\brack{-\log q(\tilde y \mid \Dcal) \mid \Dcal} = H\paren{p(\cdot \mid \Dcal)} + \KL\paren{p(\cdot \mid \Dcal) \,\|\,
q(\cdot \mid \Dcal)},
$$

and the divergence is zero only for $q = p$. BMA therefore has the smallest expected log loss of any rule, for every
data set, when the expectation is over the prior that BMA assumes. The same argument with any
<NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">proper scoring rule</NoteLink> gives the same conclusion. Raftery, Madigan and
Hoeting found that BMA over <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> models predicted better than
any single selected model on held-out data <Cite id="raftery1997" />.

### Three settings

Bernardo and Smith distinguish three relations between the candidates and the truth <Cite id="bernardo1994" />:

- **M-closed:** one candidate is the true model. Posterior model probabilities mean what they say, and BMA is optimal.
- **M-complete:** the true model exists and can be written down, but it is too complex to use, so the candidates are
  approximations to it.
- **M-open:** no candidate is true, and the true process cannot be written down. This is the usual case in machine
  learning.

Outside M-closed, the posterior model probabilities still concentrate on one model as $n$ grows: the candidate closest
to the truth in <Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss>. If the truth is a blend of two candidates, or is
better approximated by a mixture of their predictions than by either one, BMA still picks one of them. Minka's example
is a target that is predicted perfectly by a vote of three hypotheses, and BMA, given more data, moves its weight onto a
single hypothesis and gets worse <Cite id="minka2002" />.

### Stacking predictive distributions

In the M-open setting the weights should be chosen for the quantity of interest, predictive accuracy. **Stacking of
predictive distributions** chooses weights on the simplex to maximise the leave-one-out log score of the mixture
<Cite id="yao2018" />:

$$
\hat\wvec = \argmax_{\wvec \ge 0,\ \sum_j w_j = 1}\; \sum_{i=1}^n \log \sum_{j=1}^J w_j\, p(y_i \mid \Dcal_{-i}, \Mcal_j).
$$

The leave-one-out densities come from
<NoteLink to="pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-leave-one-out">PSIS-LOO</NoteLink> at the cost of one posterior fit
per model. Using held-out densities instead of training densities stops the weights from favouring the model that
overfits most, the same reason <NoteLink to="stacking">stacking</NoteLink> of point predictions uses out-of-fold
predictions. The weights do not collapse as $n$ grows when a mixture predicts better than any single model.

A simpler alternative is **pseudo-BMA**, which replaces the evidence in the BMA formula by the estimated predictive
accuracy, $w_j \propto \exp(\widehat{\operatorname{elpd}}_{\text{loo}, j})$. These are the Bayesian counterparts of
<NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">Akaike weights</NoteLink>. Like BMA weights they rank models one at a time
and concentrate on one model; Yao and colleagues stabilise them with the Bayesian bootstrap (pseudo-BMA+) and found
stacking better in their M-open experiments <Cite id="yao2018" />.

### Computing the average

For a handful of candidates the average is a finite sum. Variable selection with $p$ inputs has $2^p$ models, too many
to enumerate when $p$ exceeds about twenty. Two approximations are standard <Cite id="hoeting1999" />:

- **Occam's window** keeps only the models whose posterior probability is within a factor, such as 20, of the best, and
  drops any model that has a simpler, more probable submodel <Cite id="madigan1994" />.
- **MCMC over models** ($\text{MC}^3$) runs a <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink> whose states are
  models and whose <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">stationary distribution</NoteLink> is the posterior over
  models, and averages over the visited models.

## Worked example

Ten observations $y_i \sim \Gauss(\mu, 1)$ have mean $\bar y = 0.6$. Model $\Mcal_0$ fixes $\mu = 0$; model $\Mcal_1$
gives $\mu$ a $\Gauss(0, 1)$ prior. The prior model probabilities are equal.

1. **The evidences.** The sample mean is sufficient. Under $\Mcal_0$, $\bar y \sim \Gauss(0, 0.1)$, with density $0.209$
   at $0.6$. Under $\Mcal_1$, $\bar y \sim \Gauss(0, 1 + 0.1)$, with density $0.323$. The Bayes factor is
   $B_{10} = 1.55$.
2. **The posterior model probabilities.** $P(\Mcal_1 \mid \Dcal) = 1.55/2.55 = 0.61$ and $P(\Mcal_0 \mid \Dcal) = 0.39$.
3. **The two predictives.** $\Mcal_0$ predicts $\tilde y \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. $\Mcal_1$ has posterior mean
   $10 \times 0.6/11 = 0.545$ and variance $1/11$, so it predicts $\tilde y \sim \Gauss(0.545, 1.091)$.
4. **The average.** BMA predicts the mixture $0.39\,\Gauss(0, 1) + 0.61\,\Gauss(0.545, 1.091)$, with mean
   $0.61 \times 0.545 = 0.33$ and variance $1.13$, wider than either component because it includes the uncertainty about
   which mean is right.
5. **Against the truth.** The expected log score $\expect\log q(\tilde y)$ of each rule, computed by
   <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical integration</NoteLink> over $\tilde y$, depends on the true mean:

   | true $\mu$ | predict with $\Mcal_0$ | predict with $\Mcal_1$ | BMA |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- |
   | 0 | −1.419 | −1.557 | −1.471 |
   | 0.5 | −1.544 | −1.422 | −1.435 |

Selection picks $\Mcal_1$, the more probable model. If $\mu = 0$ it loses $0.138$ nats per prediction against the
right model; BMA loses $0.052$. If $\mu = 0.5$ selection is best and BMA gives up $0.013$. Averaging trades a small loss
when the selected model is right for a large saving when it is wrong.

The figure follows the weights as $n$ grows, for polynomial regressions when one candidate is the true model and when
none is.

With the default sine target and noise $\sigma = 0.2$, the rules agree at large $n$. At $n = 384$ BMA puts 0.96 of its
weight on degree 5, pseudo-BMA 1.00, pseudo-BMA+ 0.99 and stacking 1.00. Degree 5 has the best held-out log density,
0.168 per point against 0.126 for degree 3. Stacking concentrates here because no mixture of the three predictive
distributions improves the leave-one-out score of degree 5 alone; its weights stay spread only when different models
predict different observations well. At $n = 24$ the rules disagree. Degrees 3 and 5 have nearly equal leave-one-out
scores, and stacking splits 0.53 and 0.47 between them. BMA puts 0.86 on degree 5, although degree 3 predicts better on
fresh data, $-0.045$ per point against $-0.224$.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading weights as truth in M-open problems.** A posterior model probability of $0.99$ says that the data
  distinguish the candidates, not that the winner is correct. When no candidate is true, use predictive weights
  (stacking) and judge them by held-out accuracy.
- **Prior sensitivity.** BMA weights are ratios of evidences, so they inherit the evidence's
  sensitivity to vague priors on parameters (see <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes
  factors</NoteLink>). A model with an over-wide prior gets almost no weight whatever the data. Stacking uses posterior
  predictive densities and is much less sensitive.
- **Averaging parameters instead of predictions.** The average of a coefficient across models mixes estimates of
  different quantities, because a coefficient means different things in models with different other inputs. Average
  predictions, which have the same meaning in every model.
- **The prior over models.** In variable selection, a uniform prior over all $2^p$ subsets puts most of its mass on
  models with about $p/2$ inputs. State the prior on model size, for example uniform on the number of inputs.
- **Assessing the average.** The weights were fitted on the data, so the training log score of the average is
  optimistic. Assess BMA or stacking on held-out data, with the weights refitted inside each training fold.
