<Definition>
Let $\yvec = \fvec + \epsilonb$, with fixed inputs, mean vector $\fvec \in \reals^n$ and independent noise of variance
$\sigma^2$. Fit a least-squares model with $p$ coefficients, and let $\operatorname{RSS}_p$ be its residual sum of
squares and $\overline{\operatorname{err}} = \operatorname{RSS}_p/n$ its training error. With an estimate
$\hat\sigma^2$ of the noise variance, **Mallows' $C_p$** is

$$
C_p = \frac{\operatorname{RSS}_p}{\hat\sigma^2} - n + 2p
$$

in Mallows' scale, or $\overline{\operatorname{err}} + 2p\hat\sigma^2/n$ in the scale of the prediction error
<Cite id="hastie2009" />. For any
fitting rule with fitted values $\hat y_i$, the **covariance penalty** estimate of the in-sample prediction error is
$\overline{\operatorname{err}} + \frac2n\sum_{i=1}^n\widehat{\cov}(\hat y_i, y_i)$ <Cite id="mallows1973" />
<Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The training error of a fit is optimistic, because each target helped place the fitted value it is then compared with.
For squared error the expected optimism is $\frac2n\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$, which is $2p\sigma^2/n$ for least squares
with $p$ coefficients (see <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>).
$C_p$ adds that amount back. The result estimates the error the fit would make on fresh targets at the same inputs,
without holding any data out.

The covariance penalty keeps the general form and drops the parameter count. It asks directly how much each fitted
value moves when its own target moves. That makes it apply to fits that have no parameter count: smoothers, trees,
<NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink> and procedures that search over models.

Both answer the first question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>, how well a candidate will
predict, and both reuse all $n$ points for fitting and scoring. They target the **in-sample** error, at the training
inputs. They do not account for new inputs, which a held-out set or
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> does.

## What $C_p$ estimates

Mallows' scale has a direct reading. Let $\Hmat_p$ be the $n \times n$ projection onto the columns of the
$p$-coefficient design, so $\hat\yvec = \Hmat_p\yvec$. Define the **scaled total squared error** of the fit,

$$
J_p = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\expect\norm{\hat\yvec - \fvec}^2.
$$

$J_p$ measures how far the fitted values are from the true means, in units of the noise variance. It splits into
variance and bias.

1. **Variance.** $\hat\yvec - \expect\hat\yvec = \Hmat_p\epsilonb$, whose expected squared length is
   $\sigma^2\trace\Hmat_p = p\sigma^2$.
2. **Bias.** $\expect\hat\yvec - \fvec = -(\eye - \Hmat_p)\fvec$. Write $B_p = \norm{(\eye - \Hmat_p)\fvec}^2$, the part
   of $\fvec$ the model cannot represent. It is zero when the model contains $\fvec$.
3. **Together,** $J_p = p + B_p/\sigma^2$.

Now take the expectation of $C_p$ with $\sigma^2$ known. The residual vector is $(\eye - \Hmat_p)\yvec = (\eye -
\Hmat_p)\fvec + (\eye - \Hmat_p)\epsilonb$. Its expected squared length is $B_p + (n - p)\sigma^2$, because
$\eye - \Hmat_p$ is a projection of rank $n - p$. So

$$
\expect[C_p] = \frac{B_p + (n - p)\sigma^2}{\sigma^2} - n + 2p = p + \frac{B_p}{\sigma^2} = J_p.
$$

$C_p$ is an unbiased estimate of $J_p$ when $\sigma^2$ is known. A model without bias has $\expect[C_p] = p$, and a
biased model sits above that. The **$C_p$ plot** draws $C_p$ against $p$ for every candidate with the line $C_p = p$.
Candidates near or below the line have little bias, and among them the smallest $C_p$ has the smallest estimated total
error <Cite id="mallows1973" />.

The two scales are affine images of each other: $C_p^\text{Mallows} = (n/\hat\sigma^2)\,C_p^\text{error} - n$. They
choose the same model.

## Estimating $\sigma^2$

$C_p$ needs $\hat\sigma^2$ from outside the candidate being scored. The usual choice is the residual variance of the
largest model under consideration, $\hat\sigma^2 = \operatorname{RSS}_\text{full}/(n - p_\text{full})$, which is
unbiased when the largest model has no bias. With that choice the largest model always has $C_p = p_\text{full}$
exactly.

The choice matters. A $\hat\sigma^2$ from a candidate that overfits is too small, which shrinks every penalty exactly
when large models need it. A $\hat\sigma^2$ from a biased model is too large and pushes the choice towards small
models. Replicate measurements at the same inputs give an estimate that depends on no model.

## Covariance penalties

Efron wrote the optimism as a covariance and turned it into a recipe for any fitting rule
<Cite id="efron1986" /> <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />:

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_\text{in} = \overline{\operatorname{err}} + \frac2n\sum_{i=1}^n\widehat{\cov}(\hat y_i, y_i).
$$

The penalty in units of $\sigma^2$, $\operatorname{df} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$, is the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> of the rule. There are three ways to
compute the covariances.

- **Linear smoothers.** When $\hat\yvec = \Smat\yvec$ with $\Smat$ fixed, $\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \sigma^2 S_{ii}$ and
  the penalty is $2\sigma^2\trace\Smat/n$. This covers <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing splines</NoteLink> and $k$-nearest neighbours at fixed $k$.
- **Stein's lemma.** For Gaussian noise, $\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \sigma^2\expect[\partial\hat y_i/\partial y_i]$, so the
  divergence of the fit estimates the degrees of freedom from one sample. This gives
  <NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk estimate</NoteLink>, which owns the derivation.
- **The parametric bootstrap.** For any rule, simulate the covariances. This is Efron's general method.

**The parametric bootstrap, step by step.**

1. Fit a flexible model with little bias to get a mean vector $\tilde\fvec$ and a noise estimate $\hat\sigma^2$.
2. For $b = 1, \dots, B$, draw $\yvec^{(b)} = \tilde\fvec + \hat\sigma\,\zvec^{(b)}$ with $\zvec^{(b)}$ standard
   Gaussian, and refit the rule to get $\hat\yvec^{(b)}$. Every step of the rule is repeated, including any search over
   models.
3. Estimate each covariance by the sample covariance over the $B$ replicates,
   $\widehat{\cov}_i = \frac{1}{B - 1}\sum_b \hat y_i^{(b)}\paren{y_i^{(b)} - \bar y_i}$ with $\bar y_i$ the mean of the
   $y_i^{(b)}$.
4. Add $\frac2n\sum_i\widehat{\cov}_i$ to the training error of the original fit.

Repeating the search in step 2 is the point of the method. A rule that selects variables, stops early or tunes a
penalty uses $\yvec$ to decide its own shape, and its degrees of freedom exceed the number of coefficients it ends with.
Ye called the result the **generalised degrees of freedom** of the procedure <Cite id="ye1998" />. A parameter count
cannot see the search; the simulated covariance can.

Efron compared covariance penalties with cross-validation and found that cross-validation behaves like a randomised
version of a covariance penalty. When the noise model is believable, the penalty estimates the in-sample error with
less variance <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.

## $C_p$ and AIC

For Gaussian errors with known $\sigma^2$, the maximised log-likelihood of a least-squares fit is
$\log\hat L = -\operatorname{RSS}_p/(2\sigma^2) - \frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)$. Then

$$
\AIC = \frac{\operatorname{RSS}_p}{\sigma^2} + 2p + n\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) = C_p + n + n\ln(2\pi\sigma^2).
$$

The last two terms are the same for every candidate, so <NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">AIC</NoteLink> and
$C_p$ choose the same model. With $\sigma^2$ estimated inside each model,
$\AIC = n\ln(\operatorname{RSS}_p/n) + 2(p + 1)$ up to a constant. Expanding the logarithm around $\sigma^2$ for
candidates whose $\operatorname{RSS}_p/n$ is close to $\sigma^2$ gives
$n\ln(\operatorname{RSS}_p/n) \approx n\ln\sigma^2 + \operatorname{RSS}_p/\sigma^2 - n$, which again differs from $C_p$
by a constant. $C_p$, AIC, leave-one-out and <Gloss name="gcv">generalised cross-validation</Gloss> are asymptotically
equivalent, and each chooses a model whose error approaches the best candidate's when no candidate is exactly right
<Cite id="li1987" /> (see <NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">consistency and efficiency of
selection criteria</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

Let $n = 40$ inputs be equally spaced on $[-1, 1]$ and $f(x) = 1 + 2x - 1.5x^2 + 0.6x^3$, with noise $\sigma = 1$. The
candidates are polynomials with $p = 1, \dots, 6$ coefficients, of degree $p - 1$. The true model has $p = 4$, but its
cubic term is small.

1. **Bias.** Projecting $\fvec$ onto each design gives $B_p = 88.48$, $9.20$ and $0.38$ for $p = 1, 2, 3$, and zero
   from $p = 4$. So $J_p = p + B_p$ is $89.48$, $11.20$, $3.38$, $4$, $5$ and $6$. The best candidate for prediction is
   $p = 3$: dropping the cubic costs $0.38$ in bias and saves $1$ in variance.
2. **One sample.** A draw of the noise gives the residual sums of squares below. The largest model gives
   $\hat\sigma^2 = 44.26/34 = 1.302$, an overestimate of the true value 1 on this sample.
3. **$C_p$.** For $p = 3$, $C_p = 46.88/1.302 - 40 + 6 = 2.01$. The other rows follow in the same way.

| $p$ | $\operatorname{RSS}_p$ | $C_p$ | $J_p$ (truth) | $C_p^\text{error}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 169.71 | 92.37 | 89.48 | 4.308 |
| 2 | 64.73 | 13.72 | 11.20 | 1.748 |
| 3 | 46.88 | 2.01 | 3.38 | 1.367 |
| 4 | 45.97 | 3.31 | 4.00 | 1.410 |
| 5 | 45.69 | 5.10 | 5.00 | 1.468 |
| 6 | 44.26 | 6.00 | 6.00 | 1.497 |

4. **Choose.** $C_p$ is smallest at $p = 3$, which is also the minimiser of the true $J_p$. The error scale,
   $\overline{\operatorname{err}} + 2p\hat\sigma^2/n$, ranks the candidates identically. AIC with $\sigma^2$ estimated
   in each model, $40\ln(\operatorname{RSS}_p/40) + 2(p + 1)$, gives $14.35$ at $p = 3$ against $15.56$ at $p = 4$,
   and chooses $p = 3$ too.
5. **Read the plot.** $p = 1$ and $p = 2$ sit far above the line $C_p = p$: they are biased. $p = 3$ to $6$ sit close
   to it. $C_p$ does not recover the true degree; it recovers the degree that predicts best at this $n$.

## Assumptions

- **Fixed inputs and independent noise with a common variance.** The optimism formula and $\expect[C_p] = J_p$ need
  both. Heteroscedastic noise needs a weighted version.
- **Least squares for the $2p$ penalty.** Other linear smoothers need $\trace\Smat$, and adaptive rules need the
  covariance penalty.
- **Gaussian noise only for the AIC equivalence and Stein's route.** $C_p$'s unbiasedness needs only the first two
  moments of the noise.

## Pitfalls

- **Minimising $C_p$ over a large search.** The minimum of many $C_p$ values is biased low, and the chosen subset's
  $C_p$ understates its error. Mallows warned against reading the minimiser as the answer and recommended inspecting the
  plot <Cite id="mallows1973" />. Searching many subsets also adds degrees of freedom the $2p$ penalty does not count;
  the parametric bootstrap counts them (see <NoteLink to="best-subset-and-stepwise-selection">best subset and stepwise
  selection</NoteLink>).
- **A $\hat\sigma^2$ from the wrong model.** An overfitting model underestimates $\sigma^2$, and a biased one
  overestimates it. Either moves the choice.
- **Reading in-sample error as test error.** $C_p$ estimates the error at the training inputs. New inputs, especially
  outside the range of the training data, raise the error further.
- **Counting coefficients of a regularised fit.** A ridge or lasso fit with $p$ coefficients does not have $p$ degrees
  of freedom. Use $\trace\Smat$ for ridge and the divergence for the lasso.
