<Definition>
Let a model $\Mcal$ be a family of densities $f(y \mid \thetavec)$ with $p$ free parameters, fitted by maximum
likelihood to $n$ independent observations $\Dcal$, with maximised log-likelihood $\log\hat L$. The **Bayesian
information criterion** is

$$
\BIC = -2\log\hat L + p\ln n.
$$

For a smooth prior on $\thetavec$ and large $n$, $\BIC = -2\ln p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) + O(1)$, where $p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal)$
is the marginal likelihood of the model. Among candidates fitted to the same data, the one with the smallest BIC has
approximately the largest posterior probability under equal prior probabilities <Cite id="schwarz1978" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<Gloss name="bic" /> asks a different question from <NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">AIC</NoteLink>. AIC
asks which fitted model will predict new data best. BIC asks which model is most probable given the data, in a Bayesian
analysis that gives each candidate a prior over its parameters.

The <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> answers that question. It averages
the likelihood over the prior instead of maximising it. A model with many parameters spreads its prior over a large
space, and only a small region of that space fits the data well. The average over the prior then includes a factor for
the fraction of the prior volume that fits: the **Occam factor**. With $n$ observations each parameter is pinned down to
a width of order $1/\sqrt n$, so the fitting fraction of a $p$-dimensional prior shrinks like $n^{-p/2}$. Its logarithm,
times $-2$, is the penalty $p\ln n$.

The penalty grows with $n$, while AIC's $2p$ does not. BIC exceeds AIC's penalty once $\ln n > 2$, that is for
$n \ge 8$, and in large samples it chooses smaller models than AIC.

## The derivation

The marginal likelihood of $\Mcal$ with prior density $\pi(\thetavec)$ is

$$
p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) = \int L(\thetavec)\,\pi(\thetavec)\,d\thetavec,
$$

where $L(\thetavec) = \prod_i f(y_i \mid \thetavec)$ is the likelihood. The log-likelihood is written $\log L$ because
this chapter writes $\ell$ for a loss.

**Step 1: a peak at the estimate.** For large $n$ the likelihood is concentrated near the maximum-likelihood estimate
$\hat\thetavec$, and a smooth prior is nearly constant over that region. Expand $\log L$ to second order around
$\hat\thetavec$, where its gradient is zero:

$$
\log L(\thetavec) \approx \log\hat L - \tfrac12(\thetavec - \hat\thetavec)^\top\Amat(\thetavec - \hat\thetavec), \qquad
\Amat = -\nabla^2\log L(\hat\thetavec).
$$

**Step 2: a <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integral</NoteLink>.** Replace $\pi(\thetavec)$ by
$\pi(\hat\thetavec)$ and integrate the Gaussian (the <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace
approximation</NoteLink>):

$$
p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) \approx \hat L\,\pi(\hat\thetavec)\,(2\pi)^{p/2}\abs{\Amat}^{-1/2}.
$$

**Step 3: separate the growth in $n$.** The observations are independent, so $\Amat$ is a sum of $n$ terms. Write
$\Amat = n\hat\Jmat$, where $\hat\Jmat$ is the observed information per observation; it settles to a fixed matrix as $n$
grows. Then $\abs{\Amat} = n^p\abs{\hat\Jmat}$, and taking logarithms,

$$
\ln p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal) \approx \log\hat L - \frac p2\ln n + \underbrace{\ln\pi(\hat\thetavec) + \frac p2\ln 2\pi -
\frac12\ln\abs{\hat\Jmat}}_{O(1)}.
$$

**Step 4: drop what does not grow.** $\log\hat L$ grows like $n$ and $\frac p2\ln n$ grows like $\ln n$. The bracketed
terms tend to constants. Dropping them and multiplying by $-2$ gives BIC <Cite id="schwarz1978" />.

The error of BIC as an estimate of $-2\ln p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal)$ is therefore of order one: it does not vanish as $n$
grows. It becomes negligible only relative to terms that grow. The dropped constant depends on the prior, which BIC
ignores. For one prior the constant vanishes: the **unit-information prior**, a Gaussian centred near the estimate with
covariance $\hat\Jmat^{-1}$, which carries as much information as one observation. Its log-density at its centre is
$-\frac p2\ln 2\pi + \frac12\ln\abs{\hat\Jmat}$, which cancels the other two terms. Kass and Wasserman showed that for
nested models with this prior, the BIC difference approximates the log Bayes factor with an error of order
$n^{-1/2}$ <Cite id="kass1995reference" />.

## Bayes factors and posterior probabilities

For two models fitted to the same data, $\BIC_1 - \BIC_0 \approx -2\ln B_{10}$, where $B_{10}$ is the Bayes factor of
$\Mcal_1$ against $\Mcal_0$. With prior probabilities equal across candidates $\Mcal_1, \dots, \Mcal_J$, the posterior
probabilities are approximately

$$
P(\Mcal_j \mid \Dcal) \approx \frac{\exp(-\BIC_j/2)}{\sum_{k=1}^J \exp(-\BIC_k/2)}.
$$

These weights have the same form as Akaike weights, with a penalty that grows with $n$. They serve for
<NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">Bayesian model averaging</NoteLink> when the evidence itself is hard to compute.
Kass and Raftery read a difference $2\ln B_{10}$ of 0 to 2 as barely worth mentioning, 2 to 6 as positive, 6 to 10 as
strong and above 10 as very strong evidence <Cite id="kass1995" />. The same thresholds apply to BIC differences.

The two-part code of <NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">minimum description length</NoteLink> reaches the same
penalty from coding: describing $p$ parameters to precision $1/\sqrt n$ costs $\frac p2\log_2 n$ bits.

## Consistency

Suppose one candidate contains the distribution that generated the data, and call the smallest such candidate the true
model $\Mcal^\ast$. A criterion is **consistent** when the probability that it selects $\Mcal^\ast$ tends to one as
$n \to \infty$. BIC is consistent, and AIC is not <Cite id="claeskens2008" />. Compare $\Mcal^\ast$ with one other
candidate, in two cases.

**A larger model that contains $\Mcal^\ast$.** Let it have $\Delta p$ extra parameters. The likelihood-ratio statistic
$\Lambda = 2(\log\hat L_\text{large} - \log\hat L_\ast)$ is approximately $\ChiSq_{\Delta p}$ when $\Mcal^\ast$ is true
(Wilks' theorem). It stays of order one as $n$ grows, because the extra parameters only fit noise. BIC prefers the
larger model when $\Lambda > \Delta p\ln n$. The threshold grows without bound, so the probability of overfitting tends
to zero. AIC prefers the larger model when $\Lambda > 2\Delta p$, a fixed threshold. Its probability of overfitting
tends to $P(\ChiSq_{\Delta p} > 2\Delta p)$, which is not zero.

| | $\Delta p = 1$ | $\Delta p = 2$ | $\Delta p = 5$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AIC, any large $n$ | 0.157 | 0.135 | 0.075 |
| BIC, $n = 100$ | 0.032 | 0.010 | $< 10^{-3}$ |
| BIC, $n = 1000$ | 0.0086 | 0.0010 | $< 10^{-5}$ |

**A smaller model that misses part of the truth.** Its best member differs from the generator by a
<Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss> $\delta > 0$ per observation. The likelihood ratio in favour of
$\Mcal^\ast$ then grows like $2n\delta$, by the <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss>. That linear growth beats
any penalty difference of order $\ln n$, so both AIC and BIC reject the smaller model with probability tending to one.

With finitely many candidates, both cases together give $P(\BIC \text{ selects } \Mcal^\ast) \to 1$. The argument only
needs a penalty $\lambda_n$ per parameter with $\lambda_n \to \infty$ and $\lambda_n/n \to 0$
<Cite id="nishii1984" />. <NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">Consistency and efficiency of
selection criteria</NoteLink> sets this against what consistency costs in prediction.

## Worked example

A coin is tossed $n$ times and shows $k$ heads. $\Mcal_0$ fixes the probability of heads at $\theta = 0.5$, with no free
parameters. $\Mcal_1$ leaves $\theta$ free with a uniform prior, so $p = 1$. Under $\Mcal_1$ the exact marginal
likelihood is $1/(n + 1)$ times the binomial coefficient, so the exact Bayes factor is
$B_{01} = (n + 1)\binom nk 2^{-n}$ (see <NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factors and model
comparison</NoteLink>).

Take $n = 100$ and $k = 60$.

1. **Log-likelihoods.** $\log\hat L_0 = 100\ln 0.5 = -69.31$. Under $\Mcal_1$, $\hat\theta = 0.6$ and
   $\log\hat L_1 = 60\ln 0.6 + 40\ln 0.4 = -67.30$. The difference is $2.01$.
2. **BIC.** $\BIC_0 = 138.63$ and $\BIC_1 = 134.60 + \ln 100 = 139.21$. $\Mcal_0$ has the smaller BIC by $0.58$.
3. **The approximate Bayes factor.** $B_{01} \approx e^{0.58/2} = 1.34$.
4. **The exact Bayes factor.** $(n + 1)\binom{100}{60}2^{-100} = 1.10$. BIC overstates the support for $\Mcal_0$ by a
   factor of $1.22$.
5. **The dropped constant.** For a Bernoulli parameter, $\hat J = 1/(\hat\theta(1 - \hat\theta))$, and the uniform prior
   has $\pi(\hat\theta) = 1$. The bracket of Step 3 is $\frac12\ln 2\pi + \frac12\ln(0.6 \times 0.4) = 0.205$, and
   $e^{0.205} = 1.23$ is the factor BIC missed. Keeping it gives $1.34/1.23 = 1.09$.

The same steps for outcomes that sit at the edge of significance at the 5% level give:

| $n$ | $k$ | exact $B_{01}$ | BIC | BIC with the $O(1)$ term |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 60 | 1.10 | 1.34 | 1.09 |
| 1,000 | 531 | 3.70 | 4.62 | 3.69 |
| 10,000 | 5,098 | 11.7 | 14.6 | 11.7 |
| 100,000 | 50,310 | 36.9 | 46.3 | 36.9 |

BIC gets the growth of the Bayes factor right, but it misses a constant factor of about $1.25$ at every $n$. The
constant is the $O(1)$ term, set by the prior and the curvature. The full Laplace approximation, which keeps it, is
accurate to three figures from $n = 1000$.

## Assumptions

- **Independent observations and a regular model.** The Laplace step needs a unique interior peak whose curvature grows
  linearly in $n$. Mixtures with an unknown number of components, <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov models</Gloss> and
  neural networks are singular: their curvature is degenerate, and the penalty $p\ln n$ is too large. Watanabe's WBIC
  replaces $p$ by a learning coefficient for such models (see
  <NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">widely applicable information criterion</NoteLink>).
- **A smooth, proper prior that does not depend on $n$.** BIC ignores the prior's shape, which is safe only when the
  prior is roughly as spread as the unit-information prior.
- **$p$ fixed while $n$ grows.** With $p$ comparable to $n$ the dropped terms are not small.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming a true model.** Consistency is a guarantee about the case where a candidate is true. When every candidate
  is an approximation, BIC still picks one with growing confidence, and the one it picks predicts worse than AIC's
  choice in moderate samples. For prediction, use AIC or <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>
  (see <NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">consistency and efficiency of selection
  criteria</NoteLink>).
- **Ambiguous $n$.** The penalty needs the number of independent observations. For clustered data, censored survival
  times or a binomial response with many trials per row, the right $n$ is unclear, and different software counts
  differently.
- **Reading BIC as the evidence.** BIC differs from $-2\ln p(\Dcal \mid \Mcal)$ by a constant that does not vanish, as
  the worked example shows. When the evidence can be computed, compute it.
- **Forgetting that Bayes factors depend on the prior.** The exact Bayes factor depends on the
  width of the prior over the extra parameters (Lindley's paradox). BIC hides this by fixing an implicit
  unit-information prior.
- **Selecting over many models.** Like AIC, BIC corrects for fitting within one model, not for the search across many.
  The smallest of thousands of BIC values is biased low (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting
  in model selection</NoteLink>).
