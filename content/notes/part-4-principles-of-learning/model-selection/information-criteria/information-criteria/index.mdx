<Definition>
An information criterion scores a fitted model by its fit to the training data plus a penalty that grows with its
complexity. The penalty is chosen so that the score estimates a property of the model that the training fit overstates:
its expected log-likelihood or squared error on new data, or its
<NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink>. For a model with $p$
parameters fitted by maximum likelihood, with maximised likelihood $\hat L$, the criteria take the form
$-2\log\hat L + \text{penalty}$, and among candidates fitted to the same data the smallest score is preferred
<Cite id="burnham2002" /> <Cite id="claeskens2008" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The training error of a fitted model is optimistic, and the optimism has a formula. For the in-sample error it is
$\frac2n\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$, which equals $2p\sigma^2/n$ for least squares with $p$ coefficients and noise
variance $\sigma^2$ (<NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>). An
information criterion estimates that optimism analytically and adds it back. Each candidate is fitted once, on all the
data, with no refitting on folds. The criteria answer the chapter's first question, how well a candidate will predict,
or a different one, which candidate is the true model.

They suit likelihood models compared in moderate numbers: nested regressions, the order of an
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">autoregressive model</NoteLink>, <Gloss name="glm">generalised linear
models</Gloss>, and smoothing parameters through their effective degrees of freedom.
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">Cross-validation</NoteLink> is the alternative when the learner has no likelihood or no
count of its parameters.

## Main ideas

**Penalising the optimism.** The <NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">Akaike information criterion</NoteLink>,
$\AIC = -2\log\hat L + 2p$, estimates $-2$ times the expected log-likelihood that the fitted model gives a fresh sample
<Cite id="akaike1974" />. The maximised log-likelihood overstates that quantity by about $p$, and the derivation by
Taylor expansion around the best parameter gives the penalty. When the model is wrong, Takeuchi's criterion replaces
$2p$ by a trace of two information matrices. In Gaussian regression the exact penalty gives
$\operatorname{AICc} = \AIC + 2p(p + 1)/(n - p - 1)$, the version to use when $n/p < 40$ <Cite id="hurvich1989" />
<Cite id="burnham2002" />. Only differences in AIC carry meaning, and $\exp(-\Delta_j/2)$, normalised over the
candidates, gives Akaike weights. <Gloss name="loo">Leave-one-out cross-validation</Gloss> of the log-likelihood is
asymptotically equivalent to AIC <Cite id="stone1977aic" />.

**Squared error and covariance penalties.** <NoteLink to="mallows-cp-and-covariance-penalties">Mallows'
$C_p$</NoteLink>, $\overline{\operatorname{err}} + 2p\hat\sigma^2/n$, is the squared-error version: it estimates the
in-sample prediction error of a least-squares fit with $p$ coefficients <Cite id="mallows1973" />. For Gaussian errors
with known variance, AIC is $C_p$ rescaled and the two choose the same model. Efron's covariance penalty
$\frac2n\sum_i\widehat{\cov}(\hat y_i, y_i)$ extends $C_p$ to any fitting rule, with the covariance estimated by a
parametric bootstrap <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.

**Divergence as degrees of freedom.** For a Gaussian mean estimated by any weakly differentiable rule
$\hat\muvec(\yvec)$, <NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk estimate</NoteLink>
$\norm{\yvec - \hat\muvec}^2 - n\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec$ is unbiased for the risk
<Cite id="stein1981" />. The divergence $\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec$ plays the part of $p$, as the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>. For soft thresholding it counts
the observations above the threshold, and for the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> it is the number of non-zero
coefficients <Cite id="zou2007" />.

**Approximating the evidence.** The <NoteLink to="bayesian-information-criterion">Bayesian information
criterion</NoteLink>, $\BIC = -2\log\hat L + p\ln n$, approximates $-2$ times the log marginal likelihood by a
<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace expansion</NoteLink>, with an error that stays bounded as $n$ grows
<Cite id="schwarz1978" />. BIC differences therefore approximate $-2$ times log
<NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factors</NoteLink> <Cite id="kass1995" />. Its penalty per
parameter, $\ln n$, exceeds AIC's 2 once $n \ge 8$.

**Consistency against efficiency.** A criterion is consistent if it picks the smallest true candidate with probability
tending to one, and efficient if its choice predicts asymptotically as well as the best candidate. Penalties of 2 per
parameter (AIC, $C_p$, leave-one-out, <Gloss name="gcv" />) are efficient; penalties that grow with $n$ but more slowly
than $n$ (BIC, Hannan–Quinn) are consistent; and no criterion is both
(<NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">consistency and efficiency of selection
criteria</NoteLink>) <Cite id="shao1997" />. Prediction calls for an efficient criterion, and identification of a
believable true model for a consistent one.

**Assumptions and data reuse.** The criteria compare models fitted to the same observations and the same response.
Their derivations need a regular model with an interior optimum, which mixtures, change points and neural networks
violate; the <NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">widely applicable information criterion</NoteLink>
covers such singular models. A penalised fit uses its effective degrees of freedom in place of $p$. The penalty counts
the parameters of one candidate, not the size of the search. Comparing thousands of subsets by AIC, as stepwise
selection does, overfits like any other selection, and the smallest criterion value is optimistic for the model that
attains it (<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).

## How the notes fit together

- Start with the <NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">Akaike information criterion</NoteLink>, whose derivation
  is the template for the others.
- <NoteLink to="mallows-cp-and-covariance-penalties">Mallows' $C_p$ and covariance penalties</NoteLink> give the
  squared-error version, and <NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk estimate</NoteLink>
  extends it to nonlinear estimators such as thresholding and the lasso.
- The <NoteLink to="bayesian-information-criterion">Bayesian information criterion</NoteLink> has a different target,
  the evidence, and needs <NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">the Laplace approximation</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">Consistency and efficiency of selection
  criteria</NoteLink> comes last and compares all of them with cross-validation.
