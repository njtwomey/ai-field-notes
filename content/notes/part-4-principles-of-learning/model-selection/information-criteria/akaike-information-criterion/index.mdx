<Definition>
Let $y_1, \dots, y_n$ be independent draws from an unknown density $g$, and let a model be a family of densities
$f(y \mid \thetavec)$ with $p$ free parameters $\thetavec \in \reals^p$. Write $\log L(\thetavec) = \sum_{i=1}^n
\log f(y_i \mid \thetavec)$ for the log-likelihood, $\hat\thetavec$ for its maximiser and $\log\hat L =
\log L(\hat\thetavec)$. The **Akaike information criterion** is

$$
\AIC = -2\log\hat L + 2p.
$$

It estimates $-2\,\expect\brack{\sum_{i=1}^n \log f(Y_i^0 \mid \hat\thetavec)}$, where $Y_1^0, \dots, Y_n^0$ is a fresh
sample from $g$ independent of the data. Among models fitted to the same data, the one with the smallest AIC is expected
to give new data the highest likelihood <Cite id="akaike1973" /> <Cite id="akaike1974" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The maximised log-likelihood measures how well a fitted model describes the data that chose its parameters. Every
observation is used twice: once to pull $\hat\thetavec$ towards itself and once to score the result. The score is
therefore optimistic, and more so for a model with more parameters to pull. A larger model that contains a smaller one
always has the larger $\log\hat L$, so the raw likelihood cannot choose between them.

AIC subtracts the expected size of that optimism. Akaike showed that for a regular model the optimism of the
log-likelihood is about one unit per parameter. Half of it comes from the fit being better on its own data than the best
parameter of the model would be. The other half comes from the fitted parameter being worse on new data than that best
parameter. The factor $-2$ is a convention that puts AIC on the scale of the deviance. The same logic for squared error
gives Mallows' $C_p$ (see <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>), and
the two coincide for Gaussian errors.

AIC answers the first question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>: how well will a candidate
predict? It does so without holding data out, so all $n$ observations both fit and score each candidate.

## What AIC estimates

A fitted model predicts well when the density $f(\cdot \mid \hat\thetavec)$ is close to $g$. The
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink> measures the distance:

$$
\KL\paren{g \,\|\, f(\cdot \mid \hat\thetavec)} = \expect_g[\log g(Y)] - \expect_g[\log f(Y \mid \hat\thetavec)],
$$

where $Y \sim g$ is a new observation and $\hat\thetavec$ is held fixed. The first term is the same for every model. So
ranking models by divergence is the same as ranking them by the **expected log-likelihood of a fresh sample**,

$$
Q(\thetavec) = n\,\expect_g[\log f(Y \mid \thetavec)],
$$

evaluated at $\thetavec = \hat\thetavec$. Larger $Q(\hat\thetavec)$ means smaller divergence. $Q(\hat\thetavec)$ depends
on the data through $\hat\thetavec$, and the target of AIC is its average over data sets, $\expect[Q(\hat\thetavec)]$.

This chapter writes $\ell$ for a loss. The log-likelihood is written $\log L$ to avoid the clash.

## The derivation

The plug-in estimate of $Q(\hat\thetavec)$ is $\log L(\hat\thetavec)$. Its bias is
$b = \expect[\log L(\hat\thetavec) - Q(\hat\thetavec)]$. The derivation computes $b$ for large $n$.

**The best parameter and two matrices.** Let $\thetavec_0$ maximise $Q$. It is the parameter whose density is closest to
$g$ in Kullback–Leibler divergence, and it equals the true parameter when the model contains $g$. Write
$\svec(y) = \nabla_{\thetavec}\log f(y \mid \thetavec_0)$ for the score of one observation. Since $\thetavec_0$
maximises $Q$, the score has mean zero under $g$. Define two $p \times p$ matrices:

$$
\Jmat = -\expect_g\brack{\nabla^2_{\thetavec}\log f(Y \mid \thetavec_0)}, \qquad \Kmat = \expect_g\brack{\svec(Y)\svec(Y)^\top}.
$$

$\Jmat$ is the expected curvature of the log-density at $\thetavec_0$ and $\Kmat$ is the variance of the score. Assume
$\Jmat$ is positive definite.

**Step 1: the spread of the estimate.** The estimate solves $\nabla\log L(\hat\thetavec) = \zeros$. Expand the gradient
around $\thetavec_0$ to first order:

$$
\zeros = \nabla\log L(\hat\thetavec) \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \svec(y_i) + \nabla^2\log L(\thetavec_0)\,(\hat\thetavec -
\thetavec_0).
$$

By the <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">law of large numbers</NoteLink>,
$\nabla^2\log L(\thetavec_0) \approx -n\Jmat$. Solving,
$\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0 \approx \Jmat^{-1}\frac1n\sum_i \svec(y_i)$. The scores are independent with mean zero and
covariance $\Kmat$, so by the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink>
$\sqrt n(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0)$ is approximately Gaussian with mean zero and covariance
$\Jmat^{-1}\Kmat\Jmat^{-1}$.

**Step 2: split the bias into three parts.** Add and subtract $\log L(\thetavec_0)$ and $Q(\thetavec_0)$:

$$
\log L(\hat\thetavec) - Q(\hat\thetavec) = \underbrace{[\log L(\hat\thetavec) - \log L(\thetavec_0)]}_{(a)} +
\underbrace{[\log L(\thetavec_0) - Q(\thetavec_0)]}_{(b)} + \underbrace{[Q(\thetavec_0) - Q(\hat\thetavec)]}_{(c)}.
$$

Write $\uvec = \hat\thetavec - \thetavec_0$.

- **(b)** has expectation zero. At the fixed parameter $\thetavec_0$, the log-likelihood of the sample is a sum of $n$
  terms whose mean is $\expect_g\log f(Y \mid \thetavec_0)$, so it is an unbiased estimate of $Q(\thetavec_0)$.
- **(a)** is the gain of the fitted parameter on its own data. Expand $\log L(\thetavec_0)$ in a
  <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor series</NoteLink> around $\hat\thetavec$, where the gradient is zero and the
  Hessian is about $-n\Jmat$: $\log L(\thetavec_0) \approx \log L(\hat\thetavec) - \frac n2\uvec^\top\Jmat\uvec$. So
  $(a) \approx \frac n2\uvec^\top\Jmat\uvec$.
- **(c)** is the loss of the fitted parameter on new data. Expand $Q(\hat\thetavec)$ around $\thetavec_0$, where the
  gradient of $Q$ is zero and its Hessian is exactly $-n\Jmat$: $Q(\hat\thetavec) \approx Q(\thetavec_0) -
  \frac n2\uvec^\top\Jmat\uvec$. So $(c) \approx \frac n2\uvec^\top\Jmat\uvec$ as well.

**Step 3: take the expectation.** The bias is the expectation of $(a) + (c)$, which is $n\,\uvec^\top\Jmat\uvec$. A
scalar equals its own trace, and the trace is cyclic, so

$$
b \approx \expect\brack{n\,\uvec^\top\Jmat\uvec} = \trace\paren{\Jmat\,n\expect[\uvec\uvec^\top]} =
\trace\paren{\Jmat\Jmat^{-1}\Kmat\Jmat^{-1}} = \trace\paren{\Kmat\Jmat^{-1}}.
$$

**Step 4: a correct model.** When the model contains $g$, the information identity holds: the variance of the score
equals the expected negative Hessian, $\Kmat = \Jmat$, and both are the <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher
information</Gloss> of one observation. Then $b \approx \trace\eye_p = p$. So $\log\hat L - p$ is an approximately
unbiased estimate of $\expect[Q(\hat\thetavec)]$, and multiplying by $-2$ gives AIC.

The two halves of the penalty are visible in Step 2. Term (a) is the optimism of the training fit and term (c) is the
shortfall of the fitted parameter against the best one. Each is half a $\ChiSq_p$ variable when the model is correct,
with mean $p/2$.

## Misspecified models: TIC

When the model does not contain $g$, $\Kmat \ne \Jmat$ and the penalty is $2\trace(\Kmat\Jmat^{-1})$. Replacing the two
matrices by their sample versions at $\hat\thetavec$,

$$
\hat\Kmat = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla\log f(y_i \mid \hat\thetavec)\,\nabla\log f(y_i \mid \hat\thetavec)^\top,
\qquad \hat\Jmat = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla^2\log f(y_i \mid \hat\thetavec),
$$

gives the **Takeuchi information criterion** $\operatorname{TIC} = -2\log\hat L + 2\trace(\hat\Kmat\hat\Jmat^{-1})$
<Cite id="burnham2002" /> <Cite id="claeskens2008" />.

A one-parameter example shows the size of the correction. Fit the model $\Gauss(\mu, 1)$, whose variance is fixed at 1,
to data whose true variance is $\tau^2$. The score is $s(y) = y - \mu_0$ and the second derivative of the log-density
is $-1$. So $J = 1$, $K = \var(Y) = \tau^2$, and the correct penalty is $2\tau^2$ instead of $2$. With $\tau^2 = 4$ the
fit of the mean is four times as noisy as the model believes, and its optimism is four times as large.

TIC is rarely used. $\hat\Kmat$ is a noisy estimate, and its error often exceeds the error of assuming $\Kmat = \Jmat$.
For a model close enough to $g$ to be worth choosing, $\trace(\Kmat\Jmat^{-1})$ is close to $p$
<Cite id="burnham2002" />.

## AICc for small samples

The derivation drops terms that shrink as $n$ grows. For <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>
with Gaussian errors the bias can be computed exactly, and the exact answer is larger than $p$ when $p$ is a sizeable
fraction of $n$.

Let $\yvec = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonb$ with a fixed $n \times q$ design $\Xmat$ of full rank and independent noise
$\epsilonb \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye)$. The parameters are $\betavec$ and $\sigma^2$, so $p = q + 1$. The maximum
likelihood estimates are the least-squares $\hat\betavec$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = \operatorname{RSS}/n$, with
$\operatorname{RSS} = \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\betavec}^2$.

1. **The training term.** Substituting $\hat\sigma^2$ into the Gaussian log-likelihood gives
   $-2\log\hat L = n\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2) + n$.
2. **The fresh term.** Let $\yvec^0 = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonb^0$ be fresh targets at the same inputs. Then
   $-2\log L^0 = n\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2) + \norm{\yvec^0 - \Xmat\hat\betavec}^2/\hat\sigma^2$. The fresh noise is
   independent of the fit, so $\expect\norm{\yvec^0 - \Xmat\hat\betavec}^2 = n\sigma^2 + \norm{\Xmat(\hat\betavec -
   \betavec)}^2$ given the training data.
3. **The gap.** Subtracting, the gap is $(n\sigma^2 + \norm{\Xmat(\hat\betavec - \betavec)}^2)/\hat\sigma^2 - n$. Write
   $W = \norm{\Xmat(\hat\betavec - \betavec)}^2/\sigma^2$ and $V = \operatorname{RSS}/\sigma^2$. Then the gap is
   $n(n + W)/V - n$.
4. **Two chi-squared variables.** $W \sim \ChiSq_q$ and $V \sim \ChiSq_{n-q}$, and they are independent, because the
   fit and the residuals lie in orthogonal subspaces (see <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared
   distribution</NoteLink>). So $\expect W = q$ and $\expect[1/V] = 1/(n - q - 2)$.
5. **The expectation.** $\expect[\text{gap}] = n(n + q)/(n - q - 2) - n = 2n(q + 1)/(n - q - 2) = 2np/(n - p - 1)$.

The exact penalty is therefore $2pn/(n - p - 1)$, and

$$
\operatorname{AICc} = -2\log\hat L + \frac{2pn}{n - p - 1} = \AIC + \frac{2p(p + 1)}{n - p - 1}
$$

<Cite id="sugiura1978" /> <Cite id="hurvich1989" />. The extra term vanishes as $n \to \infty$, so AICc and AIC agree in
large samples. With $n = 20$ and $q = 5$ coefficients, $p = 6$ and the exact penalty is $240/13 = 18.5$, against AIC's 12.
AIC undercharges by more than three parameters, and so it favours large models in small samples. Burnham and Anderson
recommend AICc whenever $n/p < 40$ <Cite id="burnham2002" />. Hurvich and Tsai derived the same correction for
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">autoregressive models</NoteLink>, where it is the default criterion of several
order-selection tools <Cite id="hurvich1989" />.

## Differences and Akaike weights

Only differences in AIC carry meaning. The constant $\expect_g\log g(Y)$ dropped from the divergence is unknown, so an
AIC value on its own says nothing about how good a model is. Let $\Delta_j = \AIC_j - \min_k \AIC_k$ for candidates
$\Mcal_1, \dots, \Mcal_J$. The **Akaike weights**

$$
w_j = \frac{\exp(-\Delta_j/2)}{\sum_{k=1}^J \exp(-\Delta_k/2)}
$$

turn the differences into relative support that sums to one. A difference of 2 gives a weight ratio $e^{1} = 2.7$, and
a difference of 10 gives $e^5 = 148$. Burnham and Anderson read $\Delta_j \le 2$ as substantial support, 4 to 7 as
considerably less and above 10 as essentially none <Cite id="burnham2002" />. The weights also serve for averaging the
predictions of the candidates, a frequentist analogue of <NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">Bayesian model
averaging</NoteLink>.

## AIC, cross-validation and Mallows' Cp

AIC is one of several estimates of the same predictive quantity.

- **<NoteLink to="leave-one-out-cross-validation">Leave-one-out cross-validation</NoteLink>** of the log-likelihood
  estimates $\expect[Q(\hat\thetavec)]$ by refitting without each point. Stone proved that it is asymptotically
  equivalent to AIC <Cite id="stone1977aic" />. Without the assumption $\Kmat = \Jmat$ it tracks TIC.
- **<NoteLink to="mallows-cp-and-covariance-penalties">Mallows' $C_p$</NoteLink>** is the squared-error version. For a
  Gaussian model with known $\sigma^2$, $-2\log\hat L = \operatorname{RSS}/\sigma^2 + n\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$, so AIC is
  $C_p$ rescaled and shifted, and the two choose the same model.
- **<NoteLink to="widely-applicable-information-criterion">WAIC</NoteLink>** extends the idea to Bayesian posteriors and
  to singular models such as mixtures and neural networks, where the Taylor expansion of Step 2 fails.

When the true model is not among the candidates, and in particular when it has infinitely many parameters, AIC is
**efficient**: the prediction error of the model it chooses approaches that of the best candidate as $n$ grows
<Cite id="shibata1980" /> <Cite id="shibata1981" />. When one candidate is true, AIC keeps choosing a larger model with
positive probability. <NoteLink to="consistency-and-efficiency-of-selection-criteria">Consistency and efficiency of
selection criteria</NoteLink> explains why no criterion has both properties.

## Worked example

Twenty observations are drawn from $\Gauss(0.3, 1)$. The analyst does not know the generator and compares two models.
The data have mean $\bar y = 0.35$ and mean square $\frac1n\sum_i y_i^2 = 1.02$.

- $\Mcal_1$: $\Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, with the mean fixed at zero; $p = 1$.
- $\Mcal_2$: $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$; $p = 2$.

For a Gaussian with maximum-likelihood variance $\hat\sigma^2$, $-2\log\hat L = n\paren{\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2) + 1}$.

1. **Fit $\Mcal_1$.** The mean is fixed, so $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i y_i^2 = 1.02$. Then $-2\log\hat L =
   20(\log 6.409 + 1) = 57.15$ and $\AIC = 57.15 + 2 = 59.15$.
2. **Fit $\Mcal_2$.** $\hat\mu = 0.35$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = 1.02 - 0.35^2 = 0.8975$. Then $-2\log\hat L = 54.60$ and
   $\AIC = 54.60 + 4 = 58.60$.
3. **Choose.** $\Mcal_2$ has the smaller AIC by $0.56$. Its Akaike weight is $1/(1 + e^{-0.28}) = 0.57$, weak support.
   AICc adds $2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2/18 = 0.22$ to $\Mcal_1$ and $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3/17 = 0.71$ to $\Mcal_2$, giving $59.38$
   and $59.30$: the same choice by a margin of $0.08$.
4. **The truth.** The generator is known here, so the quantity AIC estimates can be computed. For a fitted
   $\Gauss(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma^2)$ and new data from $\Gauss(0.3, 1)$, $-2Q = n\paren{\log(2\pi\hat\sigma^2) + (1 +
   (\hat\mu - 0.3)^2)/\hat\sigma^2}$. This gives $58.53$ for $\Mcal_1$ and $56.94$ for $\Mcal_2$. $\Mcal_2$ does predict
   better, by $1.59$.
5. **The optimism on this sample.** The gap between $-2Q$ and $-2\log\hat L$ is $1.37$ for $\Mcal_1$ and $2.34$ for
   $\Mcal_2$. AIC charged 2 and 4. On one sample the gap is random, and AIC is right only on average.
6. **The optimism on average.** $\Mcal_2$ contains the generator, and it is a regression on a constant with $q = 1$, so
   the exact expected gap is $2np/(n - p - 1) = 80/17 = 4.71$. AIC's 4 is too small and AICc's $4 + 0.71$ is exact.
   $\Mcal_1$ is misspecified, because its mean is wrong. A simulation of 200,000 samples puts its expected gap at about
   $2.19$, close to AICc's $2.22$.

## Assumptions

- **Independent observations from a fixed distribution.** The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem in
  Step 1 need them. For regression, the observations are pairs $(\xvec_i, y_i)$ and $f$ is the conditional density.
- **A regular model.** $\thetavec_0$ is unique and interior, the log-density is twice differentiable and $\Jmat$ is
  invertible. Mixtures with an unknown number of components, change points and neural networks break this.
- **Maximum likelihood.** The penalty counts the parameters fitted by maximising the likelihood. A penalised or smoothed
  fit needs its <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink> in place of $p$.
- **$p$ fixed while $n$ grows.** When $p$ grows with $n$, the neglected terms do not vanish, and AICc or
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> is safer.

## Pitfalls

- **Different data or responses.** Models are compared only on exactly the same observations of the same response. A
  model of $\log y$ and a model of $y$ have likelihoods on different scales. Adding the Jacobian term $-\sum_i \log y_i$
  to the log model's log-likelihood puts it on the scale of $y$. Missing values that drop different rows from different
  models silently break the comparison.
- **Forgetting a parameter.** The noise variance of a regression, the dispersion of a negative binomial and every free
  mixture weight count in $p$. Software differs on whether it counts them.
- **Selecting over many models.** AIC corrects for the reuse of data within one fit. Choosing the smallest of many AIC
  values reuses the data a second time, so the winning AIC is biased low as an estimate of its own model's predictive
  performance. Stepwise searches over thousands of subsets overfit in this way (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
- **Reading AIC as a test.** A difference in AIC is not a p-value. For two nested models that differ by one parameter,
  AIC prefers the larger whenever the likelihood-ratio statistic exceeds 2, which happens with probability
  $P(\ChiSq_1 > 2) = 0.157$ when the smaller model is true.
- **Small samples.** With $n/p$ below about 40, plain AIC undercharges and favours large models. Use AICc.
