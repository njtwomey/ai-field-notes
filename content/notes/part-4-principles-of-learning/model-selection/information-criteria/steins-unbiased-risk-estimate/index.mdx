<Definition>
Let $\yvec \sim \Gauss(\muvec, \sigma^2\eye_n)$ with unknown mean $\muvec \in \reals^n$ and known $\sigma^2$, and let
$\hat\muvec(\yvec)$ be an estimator of $\muvec$ that is weakly differentiable with integrable
<NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink>. **Stein's unbiased risk estimate** is

$$
\operatorname{SURE}(\yvec) = \norm{\yvec - \hat\muvec(\yvec)}^2 - n\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{\partial\hat\mu_i}{\partial y_i}(\yvec).
$$

It is unbiased for the risk: $\expect\brack{\operatorname{SURE}(\yvec)} = \expect\norm{\hat\muvec(\yvec) - \muvec}^2$
<Cite id="stein1981" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The risk of an estimator, $\expect\norm{\hat\muvec - \muvec}^2$, depends on the unknown $\muvec$, so it cannot be
computed. The residual $\norm{\yvec - \hat\muvec}^2$ can be computed, but it is optimistic: each $y_i$ pulled
$\hat\mu_i$ towards itself. The size of the pull is the covariance $\cov(\hat\mu_i, y_i)$, the quantity behind
<NoteLink to="mallows-cp-and-covariance-penalties">covariance penalties</NoteLink>. A covariance is a property of the
distribution of $\yvec$, not of the one sample at hand.

Stein's lemma removes that obstacle for Gaussian noise. It says the covariance equals $\sigma^2$ times the expected
derivative $\partial\hat\mu_i/\partial y_i$. The derivative can be computed at the observed $\yvec$: nudge one
observation and see how far its own fitted value follows. Summing over $i$ gives the **divergence** of the estimator,
$\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec = \sum_i \partial\hat\mu_i/\partial y_i$, which acts as its degrees of freedom. SURE is the
residual sum of squares corrected by that divergence, and it needs only one sample and no refitting.

SURE answers the first question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>, how well an estimator will
do, for a whole family of estimators at once. Minimising it over a tuning parameter, such as a threshold or a penalty,
selects the parameter.

## Stein's lemma

Let $Y \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and let $g$ be a function with $\expect\abs{g'(Y)} < \infty$ that is the integral of
its derivative. Then

$$
\expect[(Y - \mu)\,g(Y)] = \sigma^2\,\expect[g'(Y)].
$$

The Gaussian density $\varphi_\sigma(y) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-(y - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ satisfies
$\varphi_\sigma'(y) = -\frac{y - \mu}{\sigma^2}\varphi_\sigma(y)$. So

$$
\expect[(Y - \mu)g(Y)] = \int g(y)(y - \mu)\varphi_\sigma(y)\,dy = -\sigma^2\int g(y)\varphi_\sigma'(y)\,dy =
\sigma^2\int g'(y)\varphi_\sigma(y)\,dy,
$$

by <NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink>. The boundary term $g\varphi_\sigma$ vanishes at
$\pm\infty$ under the integrability condition <Cite id="stein1981" />. In $n$ dimensions with independent coordinates,
the same argument applied to coordinate $i$ with the others held fixed gives
$\expect[(y_i - \mu_i)\hat\mu_i(\yvec)] = \sigma^2\expect[\partial\hat\mu_i/\partial y_i]$.

## The derivation of SURE

Write the error of the estimator as the residual plus the noise:
$\hat\muvec - \muvec = (\hat\muvec - \yvec) + (\yvec - \muvec)$. Expanding the square,

$$
\norm{\hat\muvec - \muvec}^2 = \norm{\yvec - \hat\muvec}^2 + \norm{\yvec - \muvec}^2 + 2(\hat\muvec - \yvec)^\top(\yvec
- \muvec).
$$

Take expectations term by term.

1. **The noise.** $\expect\norm{\yvec - \muvec}^2 = n\sigma^2$.
2. **The cross term, part one.** $\expect[\yvec^\top(\yvec - \muvec)] = \expect\norm{\yvec - \muvec}^2 = n\sigma^2$,
   because $\expect[\muvec^\top(\yvec - \muvec)] = 0$.
3. **The cross term, part two.** By Stein's lemma in each coordinate,
   $\expect[\hat\muvec^\top(\yvec - \muvec)] = \sigma^2\expect[\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec]$.
4. **Combine.** $\expect\norm{\hat\muvec - \muvec}^2 = \expect\norm{\yvec - \hat\muvec}^2 + n\sigma^2 +
   2\sigma^2\expect[\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec] - 2n\sigma^2$.

The right-hand side is the expectation of $\operatorname{SURE}(\yvec)$, which proves unbiasedness. Comparing with the
covariance penalty, $\sum_i\cov(\hat\mu_i, y_i) = \sigma^2\expect[\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec]$: the divergence is an
unbiased one-sample estimate of the <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>.

**Linear smoothers.** For $\hat\muvec = \Smat\yvec$ with $\Smat$ fixed, the divergence is $\trace\Smat$, a constant.
Then $\operatorname{SURE} = \operatorname{RSS} - n\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\trace\Smat$, which is $n$ times Mallows' $C_p$ in
the error scale minus $n\sigma^2$. For least squares, <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing splines</NoteLink>, SURE and $C_p$ choose the same model. SURE matters for
estimators whose divergence changes with $\yvec$.

**James–Stein.** The estimator $\hat\muvec = \paren{1 - c/\norm{\yvec}^2}\yvec$ with $c = (n - 2)\sigma^2$ has residual
$c^2/\norm{\yvec}^2$ and divergence $n - c(n - 2)/\norm{\yvec}^2$. Substituting, its SURE is
$n\sigma^2 - (n - 2)^2\sigma^4/\norm{\yvec}^2$, which is below $n\sigma^2$, the risk of $\hat\muvec = \yvec$, for every
$\yvec$ once $n \ge 3$. So the shrunken estimate has smaller risk than $\yvec$ whatever $\muvec$ is
<Cite id="stein1956" /> <Cite id="james1961" /> <Cite id="stein1981" /> (see
<NoteLink to="james-stein-estimator">James–Stein estimator</NoteLink>).

## Soft thresholding and the lasso

**Soft thresholding.** The estimator $\hat\mu_i = \operatorname{sgn}(y_i)\max(\abs{y_i} - t, 0)$, with threshold
$t \ge 0$, sets small observations to zero and shrinks the rest towards zero by $t$. It is the
<NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink> for an orthonormal design. Its derivative is 1 where $\abs{y_i} > t$ and 0 where
$\abs{y_i} < t$, so the divergence is the number of observations above the threshold. The residual of coordinate $i$
is $y_i$ when it is zeroed and $\pm t$ otherwise. So

$$
\operatorname{SURE}(t) = -n\sigma^2 + \sum_{i=1}^n\min(y_i^2, t^2) + 2\sigma^2\,\#\set{i : \abs{y_i} > t}.
$$

The first sum rewards a small threshold and the count rewards a large one. Donoho and Johnstone's SureShrink chooses the
threshold of each level of a wavelet transform by minimising $\operatorname{SURE}(t)$, which needs only a sort of the
$\abs{y_i}$ <Cite id="donoho1995adapting" /> (see <NoteLink to="wavelet-denoising">wavelet denoising</NoteLink>).

**The lasso.** Let $\hat\betavec$ minimise $\frac12\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\betavec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\betavec}_1$, with
fitted values $\hat\muvec = \Xmat\hat\betavec$. Let $\Acal$ be the set of non-zero coefficients and $\svec_\Acal$ their
signs. The optimality conditions on $\Acal$ give $\Xmat_\Acal^\top(\yvec - \Xmat_\Acal\hat\betavec_\Acal) =
\lambda\svec_\Acal$. When $\Xmat_\Acal$ has full column rank, solving gives

$$
\hat\muvec = \Xmat_\Acal(\Xmat_\Acal^\top\Xmat_\Acal)^{-1}\paren{\Xmat_\Acal^\top\yvec - \lambda\svec_\Acal}.
$$

For almost every $\yvec$, a small change in $\yvec$ leaves $\Acal$ and $\svec_\Acal$ unchanged. Locally, $\hat\muvec$ is
then a projection of $\yvec$ onto the columns of $\Xmat_\Acal$ minus a constant vector. The Jacobian is the projection
matrix, whose trace is $\abs\Acal$. So the divergence is the number of non-zero coefficients, and $\abs\Acal$ is an
unbiased estimate of the lasso's degrees of freedom <Cite id="zou2007" />. Tibshirani and Taylor extended the result to
any design: the degrees of freedom equal the expected rank of $\Xmat_\Acal$ <Cite id="tibshirani2012lasso" />.

The result is surprising. The lasso uses $\yvec$ to choose which coefficients to keep, a search that usually adds
degrees of freedom beyond the count of kept coefficients. The shrinkage of the kept coefficients by $\lambda$ removes
exactly as many degrees of freedom as the search adds, in expectation. SURE for the
<NoteLink to="regularisation-paths">lasso path</NoteLink> is therefore
$\operatorname{RSS}(\lambda) - n\sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2\abs{\Acal(\lambda)}$, computable along the path at no extra cost
(see <NoteLink to="choosing-the-regularisation-strength">choosing the regularisation strength</NoteLink>).

**Hard thresholding does not get this discount.** The estimator $\hat\mu_i = y_i\indicator[\abs{y_i} > t]$, the
orthonormal version of <NoteLink to="best-subset-and-stepwise-selection">best-subset selection</NoteLink>, jumps at
$\pm t$. Stein's lemma needs continuity, and the plain divergence, the count of kept coordinates, misses the jumps.
Tibshirani extended Stein's formula to such discontinuous estimators <Cite id="tibshirani2015search" />.

<Derivation title="Degrees of freedom of hard thresholding">
Take one coordinate, $Y \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and $g(y) = y\indicator[\abs y > t]$. The function has derivative 1
on $\abs y > t$ and 0 on $\abs y < t$, and it jumps by $+t$ at $y = t$ (from 0 to $t$) and by $+t$ at $y = -t$ (from
$-t$ to 0).

Split the integral in the proof of Stein's lemma at $\pm t$. On each piece, integration by parts gives the derivative
term plus boundary terms, and the boundary terms at $\pm t$ no longer cancel: each leaves the size of the jump times
the density there. So

$$
\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\cov(g(Y), Y) = P(\abs Y > t) + \frac{t}{\sigma}\brack{\varphi\paren{\frac{t - \mu}{\sigma}} +
\varphi\paren{\frac{t + \mu}{\sigma}}},
$$

with $\varphi$ the standard Gaussian density. The first term is the expected count; the second is the extra price of
the search. With $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$ and $t = 2$, the first term is $0.046$ and the second is $2 \times 2 \times
0.054 = 0.216$. A coordinate that is pure noise costs $0.26$ degrees of freedom, almost six times what the count
suggests.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Let $n = 10$, $\sigma = 1$ and $\muvec = (4, 0, 0, -3, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0)$. One draw of the noise gives

$$
\yvec = (4.6, -1.1, 0.4, -3.7, 1.3, -0.3, 2.5, 0.9, -1.6, 0.2).
$$

Apply soft thresholding at $t = 1$.

1. **The estimate.** Coordinates with $\abs{y_i} > 1$ are $4.6, -1.1, -3.7, 1.3, 2.5, -1.6$. They shrink by 1 to
   $3.6, -0.1, -2.7, 0.3, 1.5, -0.6$; the other four become 0.
2. **The residual.** The six kept coordinates each contribute $t^2 = 1$. The four zeroed ones contribute
   $0.4^2 + 0.3^2 + 0.9^2 + 0.2^2 = 1.10$. So $\norm{\yvec - \hat\muvec}^2 = 7.10$.
3. **The divergence.** Six coordinates exceed the threshold, so $\nabla\cdot\hat\muvec = 6$.
4. **SURE.** $7.10 - 10 + 2 \times 6 = 9.10$.
5. **The truth.** The generator is known, so the loss on this sample is
   $\norm{\hat\muvec - \muvec}^2 = 0.96$, and the risk at $t = 1$, the average loss over noise draws, is $6.59$.

Repeating for other thresholds:

| $t$ | residual | divergence | SURE | loss on this sample | risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 10 | 10.00 | 7.66 | 10.00 |
| 0.5 | 2.04 | 7 | 6.04 | 2.42 | 6.54 |
| 1 | 7.10 | 6 | 9.10 | 0.96 | 6.59 |
| 1.5 | 13.00 | 4 | 11.00 | 2.46 | 8.85 |
| 2 | 18.56 | 3 | 14.56 | 5.90 | 12.34 |

The risk column is the average of SURE over noise draws, by unbiasedness. Its minimum, $6.21$, is at $t \approx 0.75$.
On this sample SURE is smallest near $t = 0.4$, while the loss is smallest near $t = 1$. The noise in this draw is
small, $\norm{\yvec - \muvec}^2 = 7.66$ against an expected 10, and SURE cannot know that. With $n = 10$ its sampling
error is a few units, larger than the differences between thresholds. SureShrink works because a wavelet level holds
hundreds or thousands of coefficients, and the error of SURE per coefficient shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$.

## Assumptions

- **Gaussian noise with known variance.** Stein's lemma characterises the Gaussian. For other noise, the covariance
  penalty still holds but the divergence no longer estimates it. An estimated $\sigma^2$ must come from elsewhere, for
  example the median absolute value of the finest wavelet coefficients divided by $0.6745$.
- **Weak differentiability.** The estimator must be continuous and almost everywhere differentiable. Soft thresholding,
  the lasso and James–Stein qualify; hard thresholding and best-subset selection do not.
- **Independent coordinates with a common variance.** For correlated noise the divergence is replaced by
  $\trace(\Sigmamat\,\partial\hat\muvec/\partial\yvec)$ with $\Sigmamat$ the noise covariance.

## Pitfalls

- **High variance for small $n$.** SURE is unbiased, not accurate. On short vectors its minimiser can be far from the
  best threshold, as in the worked example.
- **Minimising SURE reuses the data.** The SURE value at its minimum is biased low as an estimate of the chosen
  estimator's risk, as any minimum of noisy estimates is (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting
  in model selection</NoteLink>). The divergence counts the fit at a fixed threshold, not the choice of threshold.
- **Counting kept coefficients for a discontinuous rule.** For hard thresholding, best subset and stepwise selection,
  the count of kept coefficients understates the degrees of freedom. Use the extended formula or a parametric bootstrap
  <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.
- **Using the wrong $\sigma^2$.** SURE's penalty scales with $\sigma^2$, so an underestimate favours small thresholds
  and overfitting.
