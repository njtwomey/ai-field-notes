<Definition>
A data set has $n = 200$ rows. Each row has two standard-normal inputs $(x_1, x_2)$ and a label $y = 1$ when
$\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2} + 0.3\varepsilon > 1.18$, with $\varepsilon$ standard normal, and $y = 0$ otherwise. Learner A is
a <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink> of depth 4 trained on $(x_1, x_2)$. Learner B is the same tree
trained on the inputs rotated by 45°, $\paren{(x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt2,\ (x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt2}$. Using these 200 rows, decide
whether A is more accurate than B at the 5% level with each of: <NoteLink to="mcnemars-test">McNemar's test</NoteLink>
on a hold-out third, the <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> on 10-fold
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>, the paired t-test on 10 repeats of 10-fold
cross-validation, the corrected resampled t-test on the same 10 repeats, and the 5×2 cross-validation t and F tests.
</Definition>

## Setup

- **The truth.** The class boundary is a circle, and a circle looks the same after any rotation. The rotated inputs have
  the same joint distribution with $y$ as the original ones, so B trained on a random sample faces exactly the problem A
  faces. Their expected accuracies are equal at every training size. The null hypothesis of every test holds, and any
  rejection is a <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss>.
- **On this sample.** A fixed sample can still suit one learner. Fitted to all 200 rows and scored on 2000 fresh rows, A
  is less accurate than B by 0.0005: nothing.
- **What each test sees.** Every score below is accuracy, and every difference is A's accuracy minus B's, with both
  learners trained and tested on the same rows.

## Solution

1. **McNemar's test.** Train both on a random two thirds (133 rows) and score both on the other 67. Both are right on 50
   rows and both wrong on 9; only A is right on $b = 5$ and only B on $c = 3$. Under the null, $b \sim \Binom(8,
   1/2)$, and the exact two-sided p-value is $0.73$. No difference.
2. **Paired t-test on 10-fold cross-validation.** The ten fold differences have mean $-0.010$ and standard deviation
   $0.070$. Then $t = -0.010/(0.070/\sqrt{10}) = -0.45$ on 9 degrees of freedom, $p = 0.66$. No difference. Dietterich
   found this test rejects up to twice as often as it should <Cite id="dietterich1998" />, but here it does not.
3. **Paired t-test on 10 repeats of 10-fold.** Ten repeats give 100 fold differences, with mean $\bar d = 0.029$ and
   standard deviation $\hat\sigma = 0.0916$. Treating them as independent, $t = 0.029/(0.0916/10) = 3.17$ on 99 degrees
   of freedom, $p = 0.002$. **A is declared better.**
4. **Corrected resampled t-test on the same 100 differences.** Each fold trains on $n_1 = 180$ rows and tests on $n_2 =
   20$, so the variance factor is $1/100 + 20/180 = 0.121$ instead of $0.01$ <Cite id="nadeau2003" />. Then $t = 0.029
   /(0.0916\sqrt{0.121}) = 0.91$, $p = 0.37$. No difference.
5. **5×2 cross-validation t-test.** Five replications of 2-fold cross-validation give the differences (0.03, 0.10),
   (0.00, 0.04), (−0.01, −0.08), (0.03, 0.03) and (0.07, 0.05). The pooled variance $\frac15\sum_i s_i^2 = 0.00118$, and
   $\tilde t = 0.03/\sqrt{0.00118} = 0.87$ on 5 degrees of freedom, $p = 0.42$. No difference.
6. **Combined F test.** $f = 0.0282/(2 \times 0.0059) = 2.39$ against $F(10, 5)$, $p = 0.17$ <Cite id="alpaydin1999" />.
   No difference.

Five tests find nothing and one declares A better. The one is the test with the most data behind it: 100 fits of each
learner.

## Why they disagree

**The 100 differences are not 100 measurements.** All of them come from the same 200 rows. Two folds from different
repeats share most of their training rows, and the test rows of one fold are in the training sets of the others. If the
200 rows happen to suit A, every one of the 100 differences leans towards A together. Averaging them converges on what
this one sample says, here $+0.029$, and not on the population difference, 0. The first repeat alone gave $-0.010$. The
repeats differ because of the partition, and their average removes that noise, but the sample's own lean remains.

**The naive test divides by the wrong number.** It estimates the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>
of $\bar d$ as $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{100}$, as if each difference brought new information. With the differences correlated
at about $\rho$, the variance of their mean is $\sigma^2(\rho + (1 - \rho)/J)$, which stops falling once $J$ passes
$1/\rho$. The naive standard error, 0.009, is under a third of the corrected one, 0.032. The corrected test's factor
$n_2/n_1$ stands in for the correlation it cannot estimate from the folds (see
<NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled t-test</NoteLink>).

**The other tests avoid the trap differently.**

- McNemar's test uses one training set and asks only whether these two fitted models differ on the hold-out rows.
- The 5×2 tests estimate the variance from the disagreement between the two halves of each replication, whose training
  sets do not overlap (see <NoteLink to="five-by-two-cross-validation-test">5×2 cross-validation test</NoteLink>).
- The paired t-test on one 10-fold run has the same flaw as the naive test but only ten differences, so the error is
  smaller.

Over many data sets the rejection rates measure each test's level. At the default 100 rows, the naive test on 10 repeats

## What it illustrates

- **Correlated resamples.** Folds of the same data share information, so their errors move together and the naive
  standard error is too small; see <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of
  cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>.
- **Repetition does not add data.** Repeating cross-validation steadies the estimate for this data set but cannot reduce
  the uncertainty that comes from the data set itself; see <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">
  repeated and Monte Carlo cross-validation</NoteLink>.
- **Which question each test answers.** McNemar's test compares two fitted models; the resampled tests compare learning
  algorithms at a training size; see <NoteLink to="comparing-learning-algorithms">comparing learning
  algorithms</NoteLink>.
- **Replicable verdicts.** The corrected 10×10 test changes its verdict least when the data are re-partitioned
  <Cite id="bouckaert2004" />. The <NoteLink to="bayesian-comparison-of-classifiers">Bayesian correlated
  t-test</NoteLink> on the same differences gives probabilities 0.11, 0.16 and 0.72 that B is practically better, that
  they are equivalent within one point of accuracy, or that A is practically better: no decision.

## Variations

- **A real difference.** With noise of standard deviation 0.6 added to B's inputs and 100 rows, B is about 15 points
  less accurate. The corrected test then rejects on about 60% of data sets, the combined F test on about 40% and
  McNemar's test on about a fifth. The naive test rejects on nearly all, and its false rejections under the null are
  hidden among true ones, which is why its flaw is easy to miss.
- **More rows.** At larger $n$ each fold's test set is larger and the fold differences are less noisy, but the naive
  test's level stays far above 5%, because the ratio of its standard error to the true one depends on $\rho$ and $J$,
  not on $n$: at 300 rows it rejects on about 63% of data sets. The corrected test drifts a little above its level,
  to about 9%, the liberal side that Nadeau and Bengio predict for unstable learners such as trees.
- **Fewer repeats.** The second panel of the figure in <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled
  t-test</NoteLink> shows the naive test's false-rejection rate growing with each repeat, from about 8% with one 10-fold
  run to about 60% with ten.
