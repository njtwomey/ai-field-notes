<Definition>
Train two learning algorithms A and B on the same $J$ random train–test splits of one data set of $n$ rows, each split
with $n_1$ training and $n_2$ test rows, and let $d_j$ be the difference of their scores on split $j$. The **corrected
resampled t-test** of Nadeau and Bengio uses

$$
t = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\paren{\dfrac1J + \dfrac{n_2}{n_1}}\hat\sigma^2}},
$$

with $\bar d$ and $\hat\sigma^2$ the mean and sample variance of the $d_j$, and reads it against a t distribution with
$J - 1$ degrees of freedom <Cite id="nadeau2003" />. The term $n_2/n_1$ accounts for the correlation between the $d_j$
that the overlap of the splits creates.
</Definition>

## What it answers

The question is about algorithms, not fitted models: trained on data sets of $n_1$ rows from this distribution, does A
score higher than B on average? One train–test split gives one difference, and its noise mixes the luck of the test set
with the luck of the training set. Resampling gives many differences, and the obvious test is a
<NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> on them. That test is badly wrong. The splits are drawn from one
data set, so their training sets overlap, their <NoteLink to="test-sets">test sets</NoteLink> overlap, and the
differences rise and fall together. The paired t-test assumes they are independent and divides their spread by
$\sqrt J$, which understates the uncertainty of $\bar d$ more and more as $J$ grows.

Dietterich found that this "resampled paired t-test" has a false-rejection rate far above its nominal level, rising with
the number of splits, and that the paired t-test on the ten folds of one 10-fold cross-validation rejected up to twice
as often as it should <Cite id="dietterich1998" />. Nadeau and Bengio kept the resampling and fixed the variance.

## Hypotheses

$H_0: \mu = 0$, where $\mu = \expect[d_j]$ is the expected difference in score between A and B when each is trained on
$n_1$ rows drawn from the population and tested on fresh rows. The expectation is over the training set, the test set
and any internal randomness of the learners. The alternative is $\mu \ne 0$, or a one-sided alternative fixed in
advance.

## Assumptions

- The $J$ splits are drawn independently at random from one data set: repeated hold-out (Monte Carlo cross-validation)
  or $r$ repeats of $K$-fold <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>, with both algorithms scored
  on the same splits.
- The differences are exchangeable: each has the same variance $\sigma^2$, and every pair has the same correlation
  $\rho$.
- That correlation is close to the test fraction, $\rho \approx n_2/n$. This is the heuristic part of the test; the next
  section says where it comes from and when it fails.
- $\bar d$ is approximately normal, which holds for scores averaged over tens of test rows.

## Test statistic

**The variance of the mean of correlated differences.** With $\var(d_j) = \sigma^2$ and $\cov(d_j, d_k) = \rho\sigma^2$
for $j \ne k$, the variance of the mean sums $J$ variances and $J(J - 1)$ covariances:

$$
\var(\bar d) = \frac{1}{J^2}\paren{J\sigma^2 + J(J - 1)\rho\sigma^2} = \sigma^2\paren{\rho + \frac{1 - \rho}{J}}.
$$

As $J$ grows the variance falls to $\rho\sigma^2$, not to zero. The shared part of the differences, the part due to
this particular data set, does not average away.

**What the sample variance estimates.** For exchangeable variables the expected sample variance is the variance minus
the covariance, because the shared part cancels in the deviations from the mean (the same identity drives the bias of
the naive <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> in
<NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>):

$$
\expect[\hat\sigma^2] = \sigma^2 - \rho\sigma^2 = (1 - \rho)\sigma^2.
$$

**The unbiased correction.** Factor $(1 - \rho)$ out of $\var(\bar d)$:

$$
\var(\bar d) = (1 - \rho)\sigma^2\paren{\frac1J + \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad
\expect\brack{\paren{\frac1J + \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}}\hat\sigma^2} = \var(\bar d).
$$

The naive estimate $\hat\sigma^2/J$ keeps only the first term. Its ratio to the truth is
$(1 - \rho)/(J\rho + 1 - \rho)$, which falls towards zero as $J$ grows: the more splits, the more overconfident the
naive test.

**Choosing $\rho$.** The correlation cannot be estimated from the $d_j$ of one data set: one run of resampling gives no
unbiased estimate of the variance of the mean (<Cite id="bengio2004" />, see
<NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>). Nadeau and
Bengio fix it with a deliberately crude surrogate <Cite id="nadeau2003" />. Suppose the loss on a test row did not
depend on the training set at all, so the score on split $j$ is the mean of a fixed function $f$ over its $n_2$ test
rows.

1. One split's score then has variance $\var(f)/n_2$.
2. Two independent random test sets of size $n_2$ from $n$ rows share $n_2^2/n$ rows on average, and only shared rows
   contribute to the covariance of their means, so the covariance is $(n_2^2/n)\var(f)/n_2^2 = \var(f)/n$.
3. The correlation is the ratio: $\rho_0 = (\var(f)/n)\big/(\var(f)/n_2) = n_2/n$.

Then $\rho_0/(1 - \rho_0) = n_2/(n - n_2) = n_2/n_1$, and the corrected variance is $(1/J + n_2/n_1)\hat\sigma^2$, the
statistic in the definition. For $r$ repeats of $K$-fold cross-validation, $J = rK$ and $n_2/n_1 = 1/(K - 1)$; 10
repeats of 10-fold give $1/100 + 1/9 = 0.121$ in place of $0.01$, a standard error 3.5 times the naive one.

The surrogate ignores the correlation that comes from shared training rows. Nadeau and Bengio note that it is a good
approximation for stable algorithms and a poor one for unstable algorithms such as <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision
trees</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink> with small $k$. When the true
$\rho$ exceeds $n_2/n$, the corrected test still rejects too often; when it is smaller, the test is conservative.

## Decision rule

Reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $\abs t \ge t_{1 - \alpha/2,\, J - 1}$. A $1 - \alpha$ interval for $\mu$ is $\bar d
\pm t_{1 - \alpha/2,\, J - 1}\sqrt{(1/J + n_2/n_1)\hat\sigma^2}$.

## Worked example

A data set of $n = 200$ rows, two learners A and B, and 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation, so $J = 100$,
$n_1 = 180$ and $n_2 = 20$. The data come from the figure's simulation with no noise on B's inputs and $n = 200$: the
two learners are equally good, and A minus B in accuracy on 2000 fresh rows is $-0.0005$.

1. **The differences.** Over the 100 folds, A's accuracy minus B's has mean $\bar d = 0.029$ and standard deviation
   $\hat\sigma = 0.0916$. A looks 2.9 points better.
2. **The naive test.** $\text{se} = 0.0916/\sqrt{100} = 0.00916$, $t = 0.029/0.00916 = 3.17$ on 99 degrees of freedom,
   $p = 0.002$. It declares A better.
3. **The corrected test.** The factor is $1/100 + 20/180 = 0.121$, so $\text{se} = 0.0916\sqrt{0.121} = 0.0319$ and
   $t = 0.029/0.0319 = 0.91$, $p = 0.37$. It finds no difference.
4. **The interval.** The corrected 95% interval for $\mu$ is $0.029 \pm 1.98 \times 0.0319 = [-0.034, 0.092]$. It
   contains the true value, about zero.
5. **Why the data favour A.** The first repeat alone gives $\bar d = -0.010$ over its ten folds; the mean over all
   repeats is $+0.029$. Averaging repeats converges on what this one data set says, and this data set happens to suit A
   a little. The correction keeps that data-set-level uncertainty in the standard error, which repetition cannot remove
   (see <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">repeated and Monte Carlo cross-validation</NoteLink>).

## In practice

- **10 repeats of 10-fold.** Bouckaert and Frank measured how often a test's verdict changes when the data are
  re-partitioned at random. The corrected test on 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation was the most replicable of the
  tests they compared, and the <NoteLink to="five-by-two-cross-validation-test">5×2 cross-validation test</NoteLink> the
  least <Cite id="bouckaert2004" />. Bouckaert calibrated the degrees of freedom of several tests by simulation and
  recommended the same 10-by-10 design, using all 100 differences, with 10 degrees of freedom
  <Cite id="bouckaert2003" />.
- **Number of splits.** Nadeau and Bengio found too little power at $J = 5$ random hold-out splits and little gain
  beyond $J = 15$ <Cite id="nadeau2003" />.
- **Same splits for both learners.** Pairing on the same splits removes the variance the two learners share.
  Different seeds for different learners throw that away.
- **Any score.** The test applies to any score averaged over test rows: accuracy, log-loss, <Gloss name="mse">mean
  squared error</Gloss>.

## Effect size

Report $\bar d$ in the score's units with the corrected interval, here $0.029$ with $[-0.034, 0.092]$. An interval that
spans zero and stays within a margin that no one would care about supports practical equivalence; the
<NoteLink to="bayesian-comparison-of-classifiers">Bayesian correlated t-test</NoteLink> turns the same corrected
variance into posterior probabilities of a practically meaningful difference.

## Pitfalls

- **Pooling folds into a plain t-test.** A paired t-test on the $rK$ fold scores of repeated cross-validation, with $J -
  1$ degrees of freedom and $\hat\sigma/\sqrt J$, is the resampled t-test this note corrects.
- **A heuristic, not an identity.** The correction is exact only when $\rho = n_2/n$. For very unstable learners or very
  small data sets the true correlation can be larger, and the test then rejects more often than its level.
- **One data set.** The test concerns training sets drawn from the distribution that produced this data set. It says
  nothing about other data sets; comparisons over many data sets use ranks (see
  <NoteLink to="comparing-classifiers-over-multiple-datasets">comparing classifiers over multiple data sets</NoteLink>).
- **Tuning inside the comparison.** If either algorithm's hyperparameters were tuned on the same folds, its scores are
  optimistic; tune inside each training split, as in <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
  cross-validation</NoteLink>.
- **Many comparisons.** Testing a new method against several baselines multiplies the
  <Gloss name="fp">false-positive</Gloss> risk; see <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>.
