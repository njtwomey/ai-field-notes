<Definition>
The **variance of a benchmark score** is the spread of the score an algorithm obtains when the whole experiment is
re-run with fresh random choices: the split of the data into training and <NoteLink to="test-sets">test sets</NoteLink>,
the random seed of initialisation, the order in which the data are visited, data augmentation, dropout masks and the
<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink>. A comparison between algorithms is informative
only when the difference in their scores is large compared with this variance, and the variance can only be measured by
re-running the experiment with those sources randomised <Cite id="bouthillier2021" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A paper reports that a new method scores 85.0 against a baseline's 83.6. Both numbers come from one training run on
one fixed split. Run the baseline again with another seed and it may score 85.2. The reported difference then measures
which run was luckier, not which method is better.

Dietterich named four sources of variation in a comparison of learning algorithms <Cite id="dietterich1998" />:

1. the random choice of the test data;
2. the random choice of the training data;
3. internal randomness of the learning algorithm, such as initial weights;
4. random labelling error, which bounds the error any algorithm can reach.

A test that accounts for the second and third must run each algorithm several times. Deep learning adds more internal
sources: data order, augmentation, dropout, non-deterministic <NoteLink to="floating-point-arithmetic">floating-point
arithmetic</NoteLink> on <Gloss name="gpu" plural />, and the randomness of the hyperparameter search itself.

## Sources and their sizes

Bouthillier and colleagues measured each source on five tasks, from <Gloss name="cifar-10" />
<NoteLink to="image-classification">image classification</NoteLink> to <Gloss name="bert" /> on <Gloss name="glue" />,
randomising one source at a time over 200 runs <Cite id="bouthillier2021" />.

- **Data sampling dominates.** Resampling the training and test data (by the bootstrap) gave the largest variance. Model
  initialisation gave generally less than half of it, about as much as the order in which <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic
  gradient descent</Gloss> visits the data.
- **The test set alone explains much of it.** The spread due to data sampling matched a binomial model of a finite test
  set: an accuracy measured on $n_\text{test}$ cases has standard deviation about $\sqrt{a(1 - a)/n_\text{test}}$ at
  accuracy $a$. With $a = 0.9$ and $n_\text{test} = 1000$ that is 0.0095, nearly a point of accuracy.
- **Hyperparameter search adds as much as initialisation.** Re-running <NoteLink to="random-search">random
  search</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="grid-search">grid search</NoteLink> or <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian
  optimisation</NoteLink> with a new seed changed the final score about as much as changing the initialisation.
- **The variance is as large as published gains.** The score variance on these benchmarks was of the same order as the
  improvements between successive state-of-the-art results.

Seeds alone already matter.

- Henderson and colleagues split ten runs of the same <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement-learning</Gloss> algorithm, with
  the same hyperparameters, into two groups of five seeds; a t-test between the groups' learning curves gave
  $p = 0.0016$ <Cite id="henderson2018" />.
- Reimers and Gurevych re-ran a published named-entity tagger with 86 seeds and got test
  <NoteLink to="precision-recall-and-f-score">F1 scores</NoteLink> from 89.99 to 91.00; the score reported in its paper,
  91.21, was above every one of them <Cite id="reimers2017" />.
- Picard trained a <Gloss name="resnet" /> on CIFAR-10 with $10^4$ seeds: accuracy averaged 90.02 with standard
  deviation 0.23, and the best and worst seeds differed by 1.82 points <Cite id="picard2021" />.

A seed chosen as the best of many is an optimistic score, in the same way as the best of many hyperparameter settings
(see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).

## Fixing a source is not the same as removing it

Fixing a seed makes a run reproducible. It does not make the comparison fair. With the split fixed, every run measures
the algorithm on that split, and the average over many seeds converges to a number specific to that split. The
expected performance on new data differs from it by the split's luck, which no number of seeds reveals. Bouthillier and
colleagues show that randomising only the initialisation, with everything else fixed, gives an average whose error stays
at the level of very few independent runs however many seeds are added; randomising every source they could reach
brought it much closer to an average of independent runs <Cite id="bouthillier2021" />. The same argument applies to
<NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">repeated cross-validation</NoteLink>: repetition removes
partition noise, not the noise of the data.

## What to report

- **Randomise every source you can.** Draw a new split, a new seed and a new data order for each run, rather than
  fixing all but one <Cite id="bouthillier2021" />.
- **Use several splits.** Prefer multiple random splits, or <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>,
  to a single fixed test set.
- **Run several times and report the spread.** Report the mean and standard deviation, or an interval, over runs. Deep
  reinforcement learning papers commonly use 3 to 10 runs. Agarwal and colleagues recommend interval estimates from a
  stratified bootstrap, the interquartile mean as a robust average, and the full distribution of scores
  <Cite id="agarwal2021" />.
- **Compare with a probability of improvement.** Bouthillier and
  colleagues recommend the probability that A beats B on a random re-run, $P(A > B)$, with a confidence interval,
  calling a difference meaningful when the interval's upper end passes 0.75. Detecting $P(A > B) = 0.75$ at
  $\alpha = \beta = 0.05$ takes 29 runs of each algorithm <Cite id="bouthillier2021" />.
- **Know the noise floor.** Melis, Dyer and Blunsom estimated the combined noise of seeds, non-deterministic arithmetic
  and finite test sets in language modelling at about 0.4 perplexity on Penn Treebank, and took a gap of 1.0 as
  statistically robust <Cite id="melis2018" />.
- **Test with the right test.** On one data set, the <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled
  t-test</NoteLink> accounts for the overlap between resampled runs; across data sets, see
  <NoteLink to="comparing-classifiers-over-multiple-datasets">comparing classifiers over multiple data sets</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

A new method scores 0.850 and a baseline 0.836 on one run each, on the figure's benchmark, where one honest run has
standard deviation $s = 0.029$.

1. **One run each.** The difference of two independent runs has standard deviation $s\sqrt2 = 0.041$. The gap of 0.014
   is 0.34 of it. If the two methods were equally good, a gap at least this large in the new method's favour would occur
   with probability about $P(Z > 0.34) = 0.37$. A single run supports no conclusion.
2. **Ten runs each.** Averaging ten fully randomised runs of each divides the standard deviation of the difference by
   $\sqrt{10}$, to 0.013. The same gap is now 1.1 standard deviations, still weak evidence.
3. **The caveat.** Runs that resample one benchmark data set are not independent draws from the population, so 0.013
   understates the uncertainty about new data, as in <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of
   cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>. A test that accounts for the overlap, such as the corrected resampled t-test,
   is needed before claiming a difference.

## Pitfalls

- **Best-of-$k$ seeds.** Reporting the best seed of a new method against one seed of the baseline builds a gain from
  noise.
- **Fixing everything for reproducibility.** A fully seeded experiment reproduces one draw. It says nothing about the
  spread.
- **Variance after selection.** Hyperparameters chosen on the test set, or a checkpoint chosen by test score, make
  every run optimistic, and averaging runs does not remove a shared bias (see
  <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>).
- **Unequal tuning.** A difference in tuning effort between methods is a systematic difference, not variance, and no
  number of runs averages it away (see <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in
  comparisons</NoteLink>).
- **<NoteLink to="standard-error">Standard error of the mean</NoteLink> of runs as the uncertainty.** It measures the
  noise of the runs on these data, not the uncertainty about the population.
