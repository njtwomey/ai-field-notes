<Definition>
The **5×2 cross-validation test** compares two learning algorithms A and B on one data set. Five times, the data are
split at random into two halves; each algorithm trains on one half and is tested on the other, then the roles swap. Let
$p_i^{(j)}$ be A's score minus B's on fold $j \in \{1, 2\}$ of replication $i \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$, $\bar p_i$ the
mean of replication $i$ and $s_i^2 = (p_i^{(1)} - \bar p_i)^2 + (p_i^{(2)} - \bar p_i)^2$. Dietterich's statistic

$$
\tilde t = \frac{p_1^{(1)}}{\sqrt{\frac15\sum_{i=1}^5 s_i^2}}
$$

is approximately t-distributed with 5 degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis of equal
expected scores <Cite id="dietterich1998" />. Alpaydın's **combined F test**
replaces the single numerator with all ten differences <Cite id="alpaydin1999" />.
</Definition>

## What it answers

The test addresses the same question as the <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled
t-test</NoteLink>: trained on data sets of this size from this distribution, does algorithm A score higher than B? The
difficulty is also the same. Differences computed on overlapping splits of one data set are correlated, and a
<NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> that treats them as independent rejects far too often.

Dietterich's answer is a design that limits the overlap. In 2-fold cross-validation the two training sets are disjoint,
and each test set is the other fold's training set. Within one replication, no training row is shared. The variance is
estimated from the disagreement between the two folds of each replication, and five replications give five such
estimates to pool.

## Hypotheses

$H_0: \mu = 0$, where $\mu$ is the expected difference in score between A and B when each is trained on $n/2$ rows
from the population. The alternative is $\mu \ne 0$, or a one-sided alternative fixed in advance. The training size is
half the data, so the test compares the algorithms at $n/2$ rows, not at $n$.

## Assumptions

Dietterich lists the approximations the derivation rests on, and says that two of them are false
<Cite id="dietterich1998" />:

1. Each difference $p_i^{(j)}$ is approximately normal, which for accuracy needs each half to hold at least about 30
   rows.
2. The two differences of one replication are independent. They are not: the two folds swap the same two halves.
3. The five variance estimates $s_i^2$ are independent. They are not, since all replications use the same data, but
   Dietterich judged this the least harmful approximation.
4. The numerator $p_1^{(1)}$ is independent of the denominator. It is one of the terms in $s_1^2$, so it is not
   exactly independent.

The test is a calibrated approximation, not an exact result. Its justification is the simulations that measured its
false-rejection rate.

## Test statistic

**Under the approximations.** Let each difference be normal with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, independent
within a replication. Then:

1. **One replication's variance.** $p_i^{(1)}$ and $p_i^{(2)}$ are two independent draws, and their sample variance with
   divisor $2 - 1 = 1$ is $s_i^2 = (p_i^{(1)} - p_i^{(2)})^2/2$, because each deviation from the mean of two numbers is
   half their difference. Their difference $p_i^{(1)} - p_i^{(2)}$ is $\Gauss(0, 2\sigma^2)$ whatever $\mu$ is, so
   $s_i^2/\sigma^2 = \paren{(p_i^{(1)} - p_i^{(2)})/\sqrt{2}\sigma}^2 \sim \chi^2_1$.
2. **Pooled over replications.** With the five replications independent, $\sum_i s_i^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_5$, so
   $\frac15\sum_i s_i^2$ estimates $\sigma^2$ with 5 degrees of freedom.
3. **The numerator.** Under $H_0$, $p_1^{(1)}/\sigma \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$.
4. **The ratio.** A standard normal over the square root of an independent $\chi^2_5/5$ has a t distribution with 5
   degrees of freedom, which gives $\tilde t \sim t_5$.

**Why a single numerator.** The obvious numerator is the mean of all ten differences. Dietterich found that its
variance was occasionally very poorly estimated by the pooled $s_i^2$, because the ten differences are correlated
<Cite id="dietterich1998" />. One difference has the variance $\sigma^2$ that the denominator estimates. He also
reported that fewer or more than five replications raised the risk of false rejections.

**The combined F test.** The choice of $p_1^{(1)}$ is arbitrary: relabelling the replications or the folds puts any of
the ten differences in the numerator, and the verdict can change <Cite id="alpaydin1999" />. Alpaydın uses all ten.
Under $H_0$ each $p_i^{(j)}/\sigma$ is standard normal, so under the same independence approximations $N = \sum_{i,j}
\paren{p_i^{(j)}}^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{10}$, while $M = \sum_i s_i^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_5$. The ratio of each to
its degrees of freedom is

$$
f = \frac{N/10}{M/5} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^5\sum_{j=1}^2 \paren{p_i^{(j)}}^2}{2\sum_{i=1}^5 s_i^2} \sim F(10, 5),
$$

in which $\sigma^2$ cancels. Large $f$ is evidence against $H_0$. The test is two-sided by construction, since it
squares the differences.

## Decision rule

- **5×2cv t:** reject at level $\alpha$ when $\abs{\tilde t} \ge t_{1 - \alpha/2,\, 5}$, which is 2.571 at
  $\alpha = 0.05$.
- **Combined F:** reject when $f \ge F_{1 - \alpha}(10, 5)$, which is 4.74 at $\alpha = 0.05$
  <Cite id="alpaydin1999" />.

## Worked example

Two learners on a data set of $n = 200$ rows, with five replications of 2-fold cross-validation. Each fold trains on 100
rows and tests on the other 100, so the accuracy differences are multiples of 0.01. This is the data set of
<NoteLink to="is-one-classifier-better-than-another">is one classifier better than another?</NoteLink>, on which the two
learners are equally good.

| Replication $i$ | $p_i^{(1)}$ | $p_i^{(2)}$ | $\bar p_i$ | $s_i^2 = (p_i^{(1)} - p_i^{(2)})^2/2$ |
| --------------- | ----------- | ----------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------- |
| 1               | 0.030       | 0.100       | 0.065      | 0.00245                               |
| 2               | 0.000       | 0.040       | 0.020      | 0.00080                               |
| 3               | −0.010      | −0.080      | −0.045     | 0.00245                               |
| 4               | 0.030       | 0.030       | 0.030      | 0                                     |
| 5               | 0.070       | 0.050       | 0.060      | 0.00020                               |

1. **Pooled variance.** $\sum_i s_i^2 = 0.0059$, so $\frac15\sum_i s_i^2 = 0.00118$ and its square root is 0.0344.
2. **Dietterich's t.** $\tilde t = 0.030/0.0344 = 0.87$. With 5 degrees of freedom, $p = 0.42$. No difference.
3. **Combined F.** The squared differences sum to 0.0282, so $f = 0.0282/(2 \times 0.0059) = 2.39$. Against $F(10,
   5)$, $p = 0.17$. No difference.
4. **Reading.** Seven of the ten differences are positive and their mean is 0.026, which looks like a 3-point advantage
   for A. The replications disagree among themselves by as much (replication 3 favours B), and both tests say the
   evidence is weak. The paired t-test on the 100 differences of 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation on the same data
   gives $p = 0.002$, the <Gloss name="fp">false positive</Gloss> that the
   <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled t-test</NoteLink> removes.

## How well it works

<TypeIErrorRates highlight={['fiveByTwoT', 'combinedF']} />

- **Level.** In Dietterich's simulations the 5×2cv test held its nominal level in most settings, and its quantiles were
  somewhat conservative in the tails <Cite id="dietterich1998" />. It can fail when the
  <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rates</NoteLink> vary widely between replications. Alpaydın found
  that the combined F test had a lower false-rejection rate than the 5×2cv t and more power <Cite id="alpaydin1999" />.
  In the figure both stay near or below 5% when the learners are equal.
- **Power.** Each model trains on half the data, and the variance estimate has only 5 degrees of freedom. Among the
  tests with an acceptable false-rejection rate, Dietterich found 5×2cv slightly more powerful than
  <NoteLink to="mcnemars-test">McNemar's test</NoteLink>. Nadeau and Bengio found it the least powerful of the
  procedures they compared, and at times too liberal <Cite id="nadeau2003" />.
- **Replicability.** Bouckaert and Frank re-ran each test on ten random partitions of the same data. The 5×2cv test
  changed its verdict most often, and the corrected test on 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation least often
  <Cite id="bouckaert2004" />. A test whose answer depends on the seed of the partition is hard to report.

Current practice follows from these results. Raschka's survey recommends the combined F test for small data sets
<Cite id="raschka2018" />. When the algorithms are cheap enough for 100 fits, the corrected resampled t-test on
10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation is more powerful and more replicable.

## Effect size

Report the mean of the ten differences, here 0.026, with the caution that its <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
error</NoteLink> is not $s/\sqrt{10}$: the ten differences are correlated. The test supplies a verdict, not an interval.
For an interval, use the <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled t-test</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Half-size training sets.** The comparison is at $n/2$ rows. Two algorithms whose ranking changes with the amount of
  data, such as a flexible model that overtakes a simple one as $n$ grows, can rank differently at $n$.
- **Averaging the ten differences into a t-test.** A t-test of the ten differences against $s/\sqrt{10}$ ignores their
  correlation, which is the problem the design exists to avoid.
- **Different partitions for A and B.** Both algorithms must be trained and tested on the same five pairs of halves.
- **Choosing the numerator after seeing the data.** Reporting the most favourable of the ten t statistics inflates the
  <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss>; use $p_1^{(1)}$ as defined, or the combined F test.
- **Few rows.** With fewer than about 60 rows each half is too small for the normal approximation, and the variance
  estimate is very noisy.
