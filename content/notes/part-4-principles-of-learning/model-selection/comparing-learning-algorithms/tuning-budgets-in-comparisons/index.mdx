<Definition>
The **tuning budget** of a method in a comparison is the effort spent choosing its hyperparameters: the number of
configurations tried, the compute they used and the size of the space they were drawn from. What a comparison measures
is a method together with its tuning, so a difference in budgets is a difference in the methods compared. A fair
comparison either gives every method the same budget under the same search procedure, or reports each method's
performance as a function of the budget <Cite id="dodge2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A learning algorithm with hyperparameters is not one model but a family. Its reported score is the score of the member
that a search found, and a longer search finds a better member. A new method tuned for a week against a baseline run at
the defaults from its original paper compares a week of search with none. The new method's advantage can come entirely
from the search.

The bias runs one way. Authors tune the method they propose with care and the baselines with less, because the
proposal is what they want to work. Nobody needs to act in bad faith for this to happen. The reader sees two numbers and
cannot see the budgets behind them.

## Re-evaluations with equal budgets

Several large studies re-ran published comparisons with every method tuned by the same procedure and budget.

- **<Gloss name="language-model">Language models</Gloss>.** Melis, Dyer and Blunsom tuned standard
  <Gloss name="lstm" plural /> and two newer recurrent architectures, recurrent highway networks and an architecture
  found by <Gloss name="nas">neural architecture search</Gloss>, with the same black-box tuner, using thousands of
  trials. On Penn Treebank at 10 million parameters the tuned LSTM reached a test perplexity of 59.6, against 63.5 for
  the highway network and 62.7 for the searched architecture. The published results for the newer architectures had
  beaten the LSTM baselines of the time <Cite id="melis2018" />.
- **<Gloss name="gan" plural />.** Lucic and colleagues tuned several generative adversarial network variants with 100
  hyperparameter samples each and found that most reach similar scores given enough tuning and random restarts. Given
  enough budget, a "bad" model could beat a "good" one, and with a small budget no statistically significant ranking was
  possible. They concluded that improvements can come from a higher computational budget and more tuning rather than
  from algorithmic changes <Cite id="lucic2018" />.
- **<NoteLink to="metric-learning">Metric learning</NoteLink>.** Musgrave, Belongie and Lim found papers comparing
  against baselines with different network backbones, embedding sizes, augmentation and optimisers, and tuning on the
  test set for lack of a validation set. Papers had reported relative improvements of over 100% over the contrastive
  loss. Re-run with the same backbone and 50 rounds of <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian
  optimisation</NoteLink> per loss, cross-validated on held-out classes, the progress since then was close to flat
  <Cite id="musgrave2020" />.

## Performance as a function of budget

Dodge and colleagues proposed reporting, for each method, the expected best validation score of a
<NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink> with $b$ trials, as a curve over $b$ <Cite id="dodge2019" />.

**The expected maximum.** Let $V_1, \dots, V_b$ be the validation scores of $b$ independent random trials, each with
<Gloss name="cdf">cumulative distribution function</Gloss> $F(v) = P(V \le v)$. The best of them is at most $v$ exactly
when all $b$ are, and the trials are independent, so

$$
P\paren{\max_i V_i \le v} = F(v)^b.
$$

Given $N$ trials already run, with scores $v_{(1)} \le \dots \le v_{(N)}$, replace $F$ by the empirical distribution
function $\hat F$, which jumps by $1/N$ at each score. The expected best of $b$ draws from it is

$$
\hat\expect\brack{\max_i V_i} = \sum_{j=1}^N v_{(j)}\paren{\hat F(v_{(j)})^b - \hat F(v_{(j - 1)})^b},
$$

the sum of each score times the probability that it is the best of the $b$. At $b = 1$ the curve is the mean score of a
random configuration. As $b$ grows it rises towards the best score observed. One pool of $N$ trials gives the whole
curve without running new searches.

**What the curve shows.** A method with a narrow region of good hyperparameters starts low and climbs; a method that
works over a wide region starts high and flattens. The curves can cross. On five-way sentiment classification,
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> had a higher expected validation accuracy than a
convolutional network for budgets below about 10 trials, and the network won above it <Cite id="dodge2019" />. A
comparison at one budget is a comparison at one point of two curves.

**Validation against test.** The expected maximum of validation scores is optimistic about the selected configuration,
because the best of $b$ noisy scores is biased upwards (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in
model selection</NoteLink>). The figure plots the fresh-data error of the configuration each search selects, which has
no such bias.

## Worked example

**The expected maximum by hand.** Five random trials of a method gave validation accuracies 0.70, 0.74, 0.78, 0.80 and
0.85, so $\hat F$ climbs by 0.2 at each.

1. **One trial.** $b = 1$ gives the mean, 0.774.
2. **Two trials.** The probability that each score is the best of two draws is $\hat F(v_{(j)})^2 - \hat
   F(v_{(j-1)})^2$: 0.04, 0.12, 0.20, 0.28 and 0.36. The expected best is $0.70 \times 0.04 + 0.74 \times 0.12 + 0.78
   \times 0.20 + 0.80 \times 0.28 + 0.85 \times 0.36 = 0.803$.
3. **Five trials.** The weights become $\hat F^5$ differences: 0.0003, 0.0099, 0.0675, 0.2499 and 0.6723, and the
   expected best is 0.832, close to the largest observed score.

**Two learners on one problem.** In the figure's default setting, 150 training rows, $k$-nearest neighbours and an RBF
<Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss> are each given a pool of 30 random configurations.

1. **At one trial.** A random configuration of $k$-NN has expected test
   error 0.322; a random SVM configuration 0.387. Most of the SVM's space underfits or overfits, and its
   median configuration has error 0.45.
2. **The crossing.** By three trials the two are level, at 0.28. From then on the SVM pulls ahead: 0.240 against 0.270
   at five trials, 0.183 against 0.273 at thirty.
3. **Unequal budgets.** Give $k$-NN thirty trials and the SVM two, and $k$-NN looks better, 0.273 against 0.304. With
   equal budgets of thirty the SVM's error is a third lower. The ranking reported depends on a choice the reader never
   sees.

## A fair protocol

- **Same procedure, same budget.** Tune every method, baselines included, with the same search procedure and the same
  number of trials or the same compute. Report the budget.
- **Comparable <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search spaces</NoteLink>.** A space that includes the
  baseline's known good region, on the same scales (log scales for rates and penalties) as the proposed method's.
- **Tune without the test set.** Select on a validation set or by
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> inside the training data; the test set scores the selected
  configuration once (see <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>).
- **Report the curve.** Expected performance against budget shows where each method wins. A single number hides it.
- **Count the seeds.** Random restarts are part of the budget: the best of ten seeds is a search over seeds, and the
  search's own randomness adds variance (see <NoteLink to="variance-in-machine-learning-benchmarks">variance in machine
  learning benchmarks</NoteLink>) <Cite id="bouthillier2021" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Defaults as the baseline.** A baseline's published hyperparameters were tuned for another data set, another code
  base or another budget.
- **Budget in trials when costs differ.** One trial of a large model costs more than one of a small model. Equal trials
  can be unequal compute; state which budget is equal.
- **Search spaces of different sizes.** A method with more hyperparameters needs more trials to cover its space. Giving
  it a larger space without a larger budget handicaps it; giving it a larger budget without saying so flatters it.
- **Reporting the best validation score as the result.** It carries <NoteLink to="winners-curse">the winner's
  curse</NoteLink> of the search. Report the test score of the configuration the search selected.
