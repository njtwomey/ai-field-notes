<Definition>
Comparing learning algorithms is the statistics of deciding whether a difference in score between two or more models,
or the algorithms that produced them, is larger than the noise in its measurement. The noise comes from the test set,
the training set, the learners' internal randomness and the tuning. Which test is valid depends on what is being
compared and on how the scores were resampled.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every claim that a method improves on another rests on a comparison: a new architecture against a baseline, a tuned
model against the one in production, a family of algorithms across a benchmark suite. A score difference on its own is
not evidence. Two runs of the same algorithm with different seeds or splits differ too, and the comparison is
informative only when the difference is large against that spread.

The question also comes in three sizes, and each needs its own test <Cite id="dietterich1998" />:

- **Two fitted models on one test set.** Is this model more accurate than that one on the cases it will face?
- **Two algorithms on one data set.** Trained on data sets of this size from this distribution, does one algorithm
  score higher on average? Resampling the training data is then part of the experiment.
- **Algorithms across many data sets.** Does one algorithm tend to win across problems? The data set becomes the unit of
  replication.

## Main ideas

**Pair the comparison.** Score both models on the same test cases and both algorithms on the same splits. The
differences then cancel what the two have in common, such as easy and hard cases or lucky and unlucky splits.
<NoteLink to="mcnemars-test">McNemar's test</NoteLink> takes this to its limit for two classifiers on one test set: only
the cases on which they disagree carry information, and under equal accuracy each disagreement is a fair coin.

**Resampled scores are correlated.** Cross-validation folds of one data set share their training rows, so their score
differences rise and fall together. A <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink> that treats them as
independent understates the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>, more so the more folds it is given,
and declares differences that are not there. The <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled
t-test</NoteLink> inflates the variance by a term for the overlap, $n_2/n_1$; the
<NoteLink to="five-by-two-cross-validation-test">5×2 cross-validation test</NoteLink> limits the overlap by design. The
reason is the one behind the <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation
estimates</NoteLink>: one data set cannot reveal how much its own luck moves the result. The
<NoteLink to="is-one-classifier-better-than-another">worked example</NoteLink> runs all of these tests on one pair of
equally good classifiers.

**Across data sets, use ranks.** Scores on different data sets are not on one scale. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test
compares two algorithms, and the Friedman test with the Nemenyi critical difference compares several, on their ranks
within each data set <Cite id="demsar2006" />; see <NoteLink to="comparing-classifiers-over-multiple-datasets">comparing
classifiers over multiple data sets</NoteLink>.

**Ask about practical differences.** A significance test cannot conclude that two algorithms are equivalent, and with
enough data it rejects any difference, however small. <NoteLink to="bayesian-comparison-of-classifiers">Bayesian
comparison</NoteLink> reports the posterior probabilities that one algorithm is practically better, that the other is,
or that the two lie within a region of practical equivalence fixed in advance <Cite id="benavoli2017" />.

**Measure the variance you are comparing against.** Seeds, splits, initialisation, data order and the
<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink> all move a score. A single run per method cannot
tell progress from luck; randomising every source over several runs can (see
<NoteLink to="variance-in-machine-learning-benchmarks">variance in machine learning benchmarks</NoteLink>).

**Compare methods with equal effort.** A method's score is the score of the configuration its search found, so a
longer search wins on its own. Equal tuning budgets, or curves of performance against budget, keep the comparison about
the methods (see <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink>).

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="mcnemars-test">McNemar's test</NoteLink>, the simplest valid comparison, and the question it
  leaves open: what if the training set had been different?
- The two resampling tests, the <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled t-test</NoteLink> and the
  <NoteLink to="five-by-two-cross-validation-test">5×2 cross-validation test</NoteLink>, answer it on one data set. The
  first is the usual choice when the algorithms can be run 100 times.
- <NoteLink to="comparing-classifiers-over-multiple-datasets">Comparing classifiers over multiple data sets</NoteLink>
  moves to benchmark suites, and <NoteLink to="bayesian-comparison-of-classifiers">Bayesian comparison of
  classifiers</NoteLink> revisits both levels with posterior probabilities.
- <NoteLink to="variance-in-machine-learning-benchmarks">Variance in machine learning benchmarks</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink> cover the
  <NoteLink to="experimental-design">experimental design</NoteLink> that any test depends on.
- <NoteLink to="is-one-classifier-better-than-another">Is one classifier better than another?</NoteLink> works every
  test on one data set.
