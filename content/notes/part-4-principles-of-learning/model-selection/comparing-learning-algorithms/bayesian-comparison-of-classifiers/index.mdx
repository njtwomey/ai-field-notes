<Definition>
A Bayesian comparison of two learning algorithms A and B puts a <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">posterior
distribution</NoteLink> on their mean difference in score $\mu$ and reports three probabilities: $P(\mu < -r)$, that B
is practically better; $P(-r \le \mu \le r)$, that the two are practically equivalent; and $P(\mu > r)$, that A is
practically better. The interval $[-r, r]$ is the **region of practical equivalence** (rope), the differences too small
to matter, fixed before the analysis <Cite id="benavoli2017" />. The **Bayesian correlated t-test** gives the posterior
from <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> on one data set <Cite id="corani2015" />; the
**Bayesian signed-rank test** gives it from scores on many data sets.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A significance test answers a question nobody asked: if the two algorithms were exactly equal, how surprising would
these data be? Benavoli, Corani, Demšar and Zaffalon list what that leaves out <Cite id="benavoli2017" />:

- **The probability of the hypotheses.** A <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is $P(\text{data at least this
  extreme} \mid H_0)$, not the probability that $H_0$ or $H_1$ is true.
- **The size of the effect.** Two algorithms are never exactly equal, so with enough data any point null is rejected.
  On one of their data sets the same tiny difference gives $p = 0.077$ from 15 folds and $p = 0.048$ from 100.
- **Evidence for equality.** A large p-value says only that the data did not reject. It cannot say the algorithms are
  equivalent.

The Bayesian comparison replaces the point null with a region. A difference of 0.002 in accuracy is not worth a paper or
a deployment, whatever its p-value. Fixing the rope at $r = 0.01$, one point of accuracy, as Benavoli and colleagues do
<Cite id="benavoli2017" />, the three posterior probabilities say directly how sure one can be that the difference is
negligible, or that it favours either side. A probability above 0.95 for any of the three is a decision; otherwise the
data are not enough.

## The correlated t-test

**The model.** Cross-validation on one data set gives $n$ differences $\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ in score, one per
fold, for example $n = 100$ from 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation. Model them as

$$
\xvec = \mathbf 1\mu + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(\mathbf 0, \Sigma), \qquad
\Sigma_{ii} = \sigma^2, \quad \Sigma_{ij} = \rho\sigma^2 \ (i \ne j),
$$

the exchangeable correlation of the <NoteLink to="corrected-resampled-t-test">corrected resampled t-test</NoteLink>,
with $\mu$ the mean difference and $\rho$ the correlation that overlapping training sets induce.

**The correlation cannot be learnt from the folds.** Corani and Benavoli show that the maximum-likelihood estimate of
$\rho$ is 0 whatever the data: the likelihood decreases in $\rho$ over its whole range <Cite id="corani2015" />. This
is the Bayesian face of the result that one run of cross-validation contains no unbiased estimate of its own variance
(see <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>). The
test therefore fixes $\rho$ at Nadeau and Bengio's heuristic, the test fraction $\rho = n_\text{test}/n_\text{total}$,
which is $1/K$ for $K$-fold cross-validation <Cite id="nadeau2003" />.

**The posterior.** With $\rho$ fixed, the model is a normal model whose only unknowns are $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, and the
data enter through two summaries. The mean $\bar x$ has variance $\sigma^2(\rho + (1 - \rho)/n)$, and the sample
variance $\hat\sigma^2$ has expectation $(1 - \rho)\sigma^2$, so the variance of $\bar x$ is estimated by

$$
\paren{\frac1n + \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}}\hat\sigma^2,
$$

the same corrected variance as in the frequentist test. Under the standard non-informative prior for a normal mean and
variance, the posterior of $\mu$ is a <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t distribution</NoteLink> with
$n - 1$ degrees of freedom, located at $\bar x$, with that scale <Cite id="benavoli2017" />:

$$
\mu \mid \xvec \sim t_{n - 1}\paren{\bar x,\ \paren{\frac1n + \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}}\hat\sigma^2}.
$$

The frequentist and Bayesian versions share their numbers. With no rope, $P(\mu > 0 \mid \xvec)$ equals one minus the
one-sided p-value of the corrected resampled t-test <Cite id="corani2015" />. What changes is the reading: the posterior
gives probabilities of the hypotheses and, with a rope, of practical equivalence.

<PosteriorWithRope />

The figure's default data set has 200 rows and a small true difference, about 0.007, inside a rope of 0.01. The
correlated posterior spreads over all three regions: the probabilities that B is practically better, that the two are
equivalent and that A is practically better are about 0.20, 0.26 and 0.54, and none passes 0.95. The posterior that
ignores the correlation is narrow and puts 0.65 on A. Raising the noise on B's inputs to 0.4 moves the posterior to the
right, and the probability that A is practically better passes 0.99.

## Many data sets: the signed-rank test

Across data sets the scores are not on a common scale, which is why the frequentist comparisons use ranks (see
<NoteLink to="comparing-classifiers-over-multiple-datasets">comparing classifiers over multiple data sets</NoteLink>).
The Bayesian signed-rank test keeps the ranks' robustness and adds the rope <Cite id="benavoli2017" />.

- **The prior.** The per-data-set differences $z_1, \dots, z_q$ are draws from an unknown distribution, given a
  Dirichlet process prior with a single pseudo-observation at $z_0 = 0$ and strength $s = 0.5$.
- **The posterior.** The posterior of that distribution puts weights $(w_0, w_1, \dots, w_q) \sim
  \operatorname{Dir}(s, 1, \dots, 1)$ on the points $z_0, z_1, \dots, z_q$.
- **The three probabilities.** For each posterior draw of the weights, compute the weighted share of pairs $(i, j)$
  whose mean $(z_i + z_j)/2$ falls left of, inside or right of the rope. These three shares are the quantities
  $\theta_\text{left}$, $\theta_\text{rope}$, $\theta_\text{right}$, the signed-rank analogues of "which side wins".
  Monte Carlo over the weights gives their joint posterior, drawn as a cloud of points in a triangle whose corners are
  the three outcomes.

<SignedRankSimplex />

The figure's default draws accuracy differences on $q = 30$ data sets from a normal distribution with mean $\delta =
0.005$ and standard deviation $\tau = 0.01$: A is better by half a point on average. The frequentist signed-rank test
rejects equality, $p = 0.005$. With a rope of $r = 0.01$ the Bayesian test gives probabilities 0.000, 0.999 and 0.001
that B is practically better, that the two are equivalent and that A is practically better. The true $\theta$ of the
generating distribution is $(0.017, 0.743, 0.240)$, whose largest share is the rope's, so the verdict of equivalence is
correct. Setting the rope to 0
removes the equivalence region, and the posterior probability that A is better becomes 0.999, the Bayesian counterpart
of the significant p-value.

Unlike the Wilcoxon test, it needs no symmetry assumption. Benavoli and colleagues compare
<NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink> (nbc) with the averaged one-dependence estimator (aode) on 54 UCI data
sets. The frequentist signed-rank test gives $p \approx 10^{-6}$, and the Bayesian test gives probabilities 0.000, 0.103
and 0.897 for nbc practically better, equivalent and aode practically better: strong but not decisive evidence for aode
at the 0.95 threshold. For aode against the hidden naive Bayes classifier (hnb) the probabilities are 0.001, 0.956 and
0.042, a decision for practical equivalence that no significance test can make <Cite id="benavoli2017" />.

A **hierarchical model** joins the two levels: each data set's cross-validation differences follow the correlated model
with its own mean $\mu_i$, and the $\mu_i$ are drawn from a common Student t distribution. It shrinks the noisy
per-data-set means towards each other and is the authors' preferred model, at the cost of fitting by
<Gloss name="mcmc">Markov chain Monte Carlo</Gloss> <Cite id="benavoli2017" />. The `baycomp` Python package implements
the correlated t-test, the sign and signed-rank tests and the hierarchical model.

## Worked example

The data set of <NoteLink to="is-one-classifier-better-than-another">is one classifier better than another?</NoteLink>:
$n = 200$ rows, 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation, two learners that are equally good. The 100 fold differences in
accuracy have $\bar x = 0.029$ and $\hat\sigma = 0.0916$.

1. **Correlation.** 10-fold cross-validation tests on a tenth of the data, so $\rho = 0.1$ and $\rho/(1 - \rho) = 1/9$.
2. **Posterior.** The scale is $0.0916\sqrt{1/100 + 1/9} = 0.0319$, so $\mu \sim t_{99}(0.029, 0.0319^2)$.
3. **Without a rope.** $P(\mu > 0) = 0.817$. The corrected resampled t-test's two-sided p-value is 0.365, and its
   one-sided p-value $0.183 = 1 - 0.817$.
4. **With a rope of 0.01.** $P(\mu < -0.01) = 0.11$, $P(\abs\mu \le 0.01) = 0.16$, $P(\mu > 0.01) = 0.72$. No
   probability reaches 0.95: the data do not decide.
5. **Ignoring the correlation.** With $\rho = 0$ the scale is $0.0916/10 = 0.0092$ and $P(\mu > 0.01) = 0.98$, a
   confident and wrong verdict for A. This is the overconfidence of the naive <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired
   t-test</NoteLink>, in Bayesian form.

## Pitfalls

- **The correlation is assumed.** $\rho = n_\text{test}/n_\text{total}$ is a heuristic. For unstable learners the true
  correlation can be larger, and the posterior is then too narrow, as the corrected test is too liberal.
- **The rope must be fixed in advance.** Choosing $r$ after seeing the posterior can produce any conclusion. It should
  be set by what difference matters for the application: a point of accuracy, a cost, a latency budget.
- **Probabilities about this distribution.** On one data set the posterior concerns training sets from that
  distribution, like the frequentist tests. Generalising across problems needs many data sets.
- **Undecided is an answer.** When no probability passes the threshold, the right report is that more data, or more
  data sets, are needed, not the largest of the three.
- **Bayes does not fix the design.** An under-tuned baseline or a test set reused for selection biases the differences,
  and the posterior inherits the bias (see <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in
  comparisons</NoteLink>).
