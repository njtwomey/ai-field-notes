<Definition>
A data set has $n = 50$ rows, 25 of each of two classes in random order. Each row has $2J$ features, all independent
standard normal and independent of the label. Candidate $j$, for $j = 1, \dots, J$, is a 5-nearest-neighbour classifier
that uses only features $2j - 1$ and $2j$. Every candidate is scored by stratified 5-fold
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> on the same folds, and the candidate with the highest
accuracy is selected. Find the expected cross-validation accuracy of the selected candidate for $J = 1$, 10 and 100, its
true accuracy, and the estimate that nested cross-validation (5 outer folds, 5 inner folds) gives for the whole
procedure.
</Definition>

## Setup

- **The true accuracy** of a classifier $f$ is $\prob(f(\xvec) = y)$ for a new row $(\xvec, y)$ drawn from the same
  distribution. On new data each class has probability $\frac12$.
- **The cross-validation accuracy** of candidate $j$, written $\operatorname{CV}_j$, is the fraction of the 50 rows that
  candidate $j$ classifies correctly when fitted without the row's fold.
- **The reported score** is the best of them, $\max_j\operatorname{CV}_j$, the number a tuning run prints. Accuracy is
  higher-is-better, so <NoteLink to="winners-curse">the winner's curse</NoteLink> pushes this score up rather than down.
- **The nested estimate** runs the whole search, including its inner 5-fold cross-validation, inside each of 5 outer
  training sets of 40 rows, and scores the chosen candidate on the outer test fold of 10 rows (see
  <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of one candidate's cross-validation accuracy across data sets.

## Solution

1. **Every candidate has true accuracy exactly 0.5.** A new label $y$ is independent of the features, so it is
   independent of the prediction $f(\xvec)$ of any classifier fitted to other data. Then
   $\prob(f(\xvec) = y) = \sum_c \prob(f(\xvec) = c)\,\prob(y = c) = \frac12\sum_c\prob(f(\xvec) = c) = \frac12$. The
   selected candidate is one of them, so its true accuracy is also 0.5, and so is that of the whole procedure.
2. **One candidate's score is unbiased but noisy.** With $J = 1$ nothing is selected, and $\operatorname{CV}_1$ averages
   about 0.5. A hold-out set of 50 rows would have standard deviation $\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5/50} = 0.071$. The
   cross-validation score varies more, because the folds share training rows: across simulated data sets
   $\sigma \approx 0.088$.
3. **The scores of different candidates are nearly independent.** Each candidate reads its own two features, which are
   independent of every other candidate's. The candidates share only the labels and the folds.
4. **The best of $J$ is pushed into the tail.** For $J$ independent Gaussian scores with mean 0.5 and standard deviation
   $\sigma$, the expected maximum is $0.5 + \sigma\, m_J$, where $m_J$ is the expected maximum of $J$ standard
   Gaussians: $m_{10} = 1.539$ and $m_{100} = 2.508$ (see <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal
   inequalities</NoteLink>).
   - $J = 10$: $0.5 + 0.088 \times 1.539 = 0.635$.
   - $J = 100$: $0.5 + 0.088 \times 2.508 = 0.721$.

   The sub-Gaussian maximal inequality $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln J}$ bounds the excess by $0.189$ and $0.267$, so the expected
   best is at most $0.689$ and $0.767$, whatever the dependence between the candidates.
5. **Nested cross-validation sees through the selection.** In each outer fold the search chooses a candidate using the
   40 outer training rows only. The 10 outer test rows played no part in the choice, so the chosen classifier scores
   them with expected accuracy 0.5, by step 1. The nested estimate averages these scores and is unbiased for 0.5, up to
   the small pessimism of training on fewer rows.

**Simulation on the engine.** Repeating the whole experiment on many simulated data sets gives:

| $J$ | data sets | mean best CV accuracy | best above 0.6 | mean nested estimate |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 200 | 0.502 | 13% | 0.499 |
| 10 | 100 | 0.631 | 74% | 0.505 |
| 100 | 40 | 0.706 | 100% | 0.498 |

The best score matches the prediction of step 4 at $J = 10$ and falls a little short of it at $J = 100$, because the
shared labels and folds correlate the candidates slightly and the accuracy moves in steps of $1/50$. With 100
candidates, every data set produced a classifier that cross-validates above 60%, although none can beat a coin. The
nested estimate stays at 0.5 for every $J$. Its spread across data sets, a standard deviation of 0.07 to 0.09, comes
from the outer folds of 10 rows, not from bias.

## What it illustrates

- **Selection makes an unbiased estimate biased.** Each candidate's cross-validation score is honest on its own. The
  maximum of them is not. This is <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model
  selection</NoteLink>, and its size follows the <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequality</NoteLink>:
  logarithmic in $J$ and proportional to the noise $\sigma$ of one score.
- **Nested cross-validation assesses the procedure.** Its outer folds never inform the choice, so it returns the true
  accuracy of "search and refit" on average (<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Published examples.** Varma and Simon found the same pattern for shrunken centroids and <Gloss name="svm">support
  vector machines</Gloss> tuned on null data: cross-validation errors below 30% on many data sets with a true error of
  50%, removed by nesting <Cite id="varma2006" />. Cawley and Talbot showed that the bias can match the differences
  between algorithms <Cite id="cawley2010" />.
- **The extreme case is selection without any held-out data.** Choosing features on all the rows before
  cross-validating is a search over a very large number of candidates whose scores have all seen the test folds; see
  <NoteLink to="selection-inside-cross-validation">selection inside cross-validation</NoteLink>.

## Variations

- **More rows.** $\sigma$ falls roughly like $1/\sqrt n$. With $n = 200$, simulation gives $\sigma = 0.045$ and a best
  of 100 candidates of 0.614 on average, close to $0.5 + 0.045 \times 2.508 = 0.613$ and still well above the truth.
- **Correlated candidates.** A grid over the number of neighbours $k$ for one fixed feature pair gives candidates that
  make nearly the same predictions. Their scores are strongly correlated, and the best of 100 such candidates is far
  less optimistic than the best of 100 independent ones.
- **Real signal.** If one feature pair carries information about the label, it usually wins, and its score is close to
  honest. The optimism is largest when many candidates are equally good, and pure noise is the limit of that case.
- **Repetition.** Repeating 5-fold cross-validation five times with fresh splits and averaging lowers $\sigma$ from
  0.087 to 0.076 in simulation with $J = 20$, and the mean best score from 0.658 to 0.643. Most of the noise comes from
  the 50 rows themselves, not from the split, so repetition shrinks the bias only a little. The cure is held-out data,
  not a different splitter.
