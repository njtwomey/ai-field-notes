<Definition>
Selection bias in <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink> is the optimism of a validation score that
was used to choose the model it reports. Choosing the best of several candidates by their estimated errors favours the
candidate whose estimate was luckiest, so the winning score understates the winner's true error
<Cite id="cawley2010" />. This topic covers the size of that bias, the procedures that assess a selected model honestly,
and the rules and mechanisms that limit how much of the validation noise the selection can fit.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every tuned model passes through a selection step: a grid or <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink> over
hyperparameters, a choice among model families, a choice of features, a choice of training checkpoint. The score that
wins the search is the number most often reported, in papers, competitions and model cards. On small data with many
candidates it can be far from the truth. Varma and Simon tuned classifiers on data with no signal and obtained
cross-validation errors below 30% on many data sets whose true error was 50% <Cite id="varma2006" />.

The notes here answer three practical questions. How optimistic is the reported score? How can the selected model be
assessed without that optimism? And how can the selection itself be made less sensitive to noise, so that it picks a
good model rather than a lucky one?

## Main ideas

**The winner's curse.** Each candidate's validation score is unbiased on its
own; the minimum of $J$ of them is not. If each estimate errs with a sub-Gaussian scale $\sigma$, the expected optimism
of the minimum is at most $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln J}$, whatever the dependence between candidates, by the
<NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequality</NoteLink>. The same noise can also choose a worse model than the
best candidate. <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">Overfitting in model selection</NoteLink> derives both
effects and the fold-based bias correction of Tibshirani and Tibshirani.

**Assess on data the selection never saw.** An honest estimate of a selected model needs examples that took no part in
the choice. A large data set reserves a test set and scores it once, the three-way split of
<NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>. A small one uses
<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>: the whole search runs inside each training
set of an outer cross-validation, and the outer folds score the procedure "search, then refit". The nested estimate
assesses that procedure, not a single hyperparameter value, and the final model comes from the search run on all the
data.

**Prefer what the data can resolve.** Near its minimum a cross-validation curve is flat, and the minimiser moves with
the split. The <NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink> picks the simplest candidate
within one <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the minimum, trading a difference the folds cannot
resolve for a choice less driven by noise. Fewer candidates, coarser grids, lower-variance criteria and averaging over
near-equal candidates serve the same end.

**Adaptive reuse costs more than selection.** Choosing among a fixed list costs a logarithmic factor. Designing each new
model after reading the previous one's holdout score can cost far more: a plain holdout of $n$ examples supports only
about $n$ adaptive queries. The <NoteLink to="reusable-holdout">reusable holdout</NoteLink> reveals less per query.
Thresholdout answers from the training set unless the holdout disagrees, and the Ladder updates a leaderboard only for
significant improvements, so that its error grows only logarithmically with the number of submissions.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>, which sets out the
  bias and its size. It depends on <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold cross-validation</NoteLink> and the
  maximal inequality.
- <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">Nested cross-validation</NoteLink> and the
  <NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink> build on it: one for assessing, one for
  selecting.
- The <NoteLink to="reusable-holdout">reusable holdout</NoteLink> extends the argument from a fixed list of candidates
  to adaptive analysis and leaderboards.
- <NoteLink to="how-optimistic-is-the-best-cross-validation-score" /> works the whole story on noise labels: the best
  of 100 cross-validation scores averages 0.71 against a true accuracy of 0.5, and nested cross-validation returns 0.5.
