<Definition>
Let $\Mcal_1, \dots, \Mcal_J$ be candidate models with true prediction errors $\operatorname{Err}_1, \dots,
\operatorname{Err}_J$. Let $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j$ be an estimate of $\operatorname{Err}_j$ from validation
data, such as a hold-out or a <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> score, and let
$\hat\jmath = \argmin_j \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j$ be the selected candidate. **Overfitting in model selection** is
the fitting of the validation noise by the selection step <Cite id="cawley2010" />. It has two effects. The selected
estimate is biased low, $\expect\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath} < \expect\operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath}$,
which is **selection bias** in the reported score. The selected model can be worse than the best candidate,
$\operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath} > \min_j\operatorname{Err}_j$, which is overfitting of the selection criterion.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Each validation estimate is the candidate's true error plus noise from the finite validation sample. Taking the minimum
over $J$ estimates favours the candidate whose noise happened to be most negative. The winning score therefore reflects
the candidate's quality and its luck. The luck does not carry over to new data.

The training error is optimistic for the same reason: a fit adapts to the noise of the data it was fitted on (see
<NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>). Selection is a fit with $J$
possible outcomes, and the validation data play the role of the training data. One candidate scored on held-out data
gives an unbiased estimate. The minimum of many such estimates does not.

The size of the effect depends on three things:
- **The number of candidates $J$.** More candidates give the noise more chances to produce a low score.
- **The noise scale $\sigma$** of each estimate. It falls as the validation set grows.
- **How close the candidates are.** If one candidate is better than the rest by much more than $\sigma$, it wins almost
  every time, and its score is nearly unbiased. The bias is largest when many candidates are about equally good, as in
  a fine hyperparameter grid or a search over pure noise.

The same mechanism appears in <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple testing</NoteLink>, where the smallest of many
p-values is small by chance, and in <NoteLink to="winners-curse">the winner's curse</NoteLink> of significance testing,
where the effects that pass a filter are exaggerated.

## The winner's curse in model selection

Write the error of each estimate as $\varepsilon_j = \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j - \operatorname{Err}_j$. Assume that
each estimate is unbiased, $\expect\varepsilon_j = 0$, and that each $-\varepsilon_j$ is sub-Gaussian with variance
proxy $\sigma^2$. The estimates may be dependent; scores computed on the same validation set always are.

**The selected score is optimistic.** The optimism of the selected estimate is

$$
\operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath} - \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath} = -\varepsilon_{\hat\jmath} \le \max_{1 \le j
\le J}\paren{-\varepsilon_j}.
$$

The inequality holds because $\hat\jmath$ is one of the $J$ indices, whatever rule chose it. The right side is the
largest of $J$ mean-zero sub-Gaussian variables. The <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">sub-Gaussian maximal
inequality</NoteLink> bounds its expectation without any assumption on the dependence:

$$
\expect\brack{\operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath} - \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath}} \le \sigma\sqrt{2\ln J}.
$$

With probability at least $1 - \delta$, the optimism is below $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(J/\delta)}$, by the
<NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> behind the same inequality.

**The bound is nearly attained for equal, independent candidates.** If every candidate has the same true error and the
estimation errors are independent Gaussians, then $\hat\jmath$ is
the index of the most negative $\varepsilon_j$, and the optimism equals $\max_j(-\varepsilon_j)$ exactly. Its
expectation is $\sigma$ times the expected maximum of $J$ standard Gaussians: $1.54\sigma$ for $J = 10$, $2.51\sigma$
for $J = 100$ and $3.24\sigma$ for $J = 1{,}000$. The bound gives $2.15\sigma$, $3.03\sigma$ and $3.72\sigma$. The
growth is logarithmic: a hundred times more candidates roughly doubles the bias.

**Dependence lowers the bias.** Neighbouring values on a hyperparameter grid give nearly the same model and nearly the
same estimation error. A grid of 100 strongly correlated values behaves like far fewer independent candidates. The
maximal inequality still holds but overstates the bias.

**The selected model can be worse.** Let $j^\star = \argmin_j\operatorname{Err}_j$ be the best candidate. Because
$\hat\jmath$ minimises the estimate,
$\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath} \le \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{j^\star}$. Adding and subtracting the
estimation errors gives

$$
\operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath} = \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath} - \varepsilon_{\hat\jmath} \le
\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{j^\star} - \varepsilon_{\hat\jmath} = \operatorname{Err}_{j^\star} + \varepsilon_{j^\star} -
\varepsilon_{\hat\jmath} \le \min_j\operatorname{Err}_j + 2\max_j\abs{\varepsilon_j}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequality</NoteLink> for absolute values bounds the expected excess
error of the selected model by $2\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(2J)}$. Selection is reliable when the gaps between candidates are
larger than this. When they are smaller, the noise decides, and adding candidates can make the chosen model worse even
as its reported score improves <Cite id="ng1997" />.

## How large is the bias?

The noise scale $\sigma$ sets the units.
- **A hold-out set** of $m$ examples scored with the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> gives an
  average of $m$ independent losses in $[0, 1]$. Its deviation is sub-Gaussian with $\sigma = 1/(2\sqrt m)$ by
  <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's lemma</NoteLink>, and its standard deviation is
  $\sqrt{\operatorname{Err}(1 - \operatorname{Err})/m}$.
- **A $K$-fold cross-validation estimate** averages $n$ held-out losses, but the fold models share training data, so
  its standard deviation is at least that of a hold-out set of size $n$ and often larger (see
  <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>). In the
  simulation below, with $n = 50$ and pure-noise labels, it is about $0.088$ against $\sqrt{0.25/50} = 0.071$ for a
  hold-out set of 50.

Studies on real and simulated data show biases from negligible to severe.
- Varma and Simon tuned shrunken-centroid classifiers and <Gloss name="svm">support vector machines</Gloss> on data with
  no difference between the classes. The cross-validation error of the tuned classifier was below 30% on 18.5% and 38%
  of the data sets respectively, while the true error was 50% <Cite id="varma2006" />.
- Tibshirani and Tibshirani found the bias small when $n = 400$ and $p = 100$ but large when $n = 40$ and $p = 1{,}000$.
  With no signal, the minimum 10-fold error of nearest shrunken centroids averaged 0.384 against a true 0.5
  <Cite id="tibshirani2009bias" />.
- Cawley and Talbot showed that the bias can be as large as the differences between the learning algorithms being
  compared. It grows with the number of hyperparameters tuned, and a low-variance selection criterion matters as much as
  an unbiased one <Cite id="cawley2010" />.

## Correcting the minimum

Three routes give an honest estimate for the selected model.

1. **Fresh data.** Score the selected model once on a test set that played no part in the selection. This is the
   three-way split of <NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model
   assessment</NoteLink>.
2. **Nested cross-validation.** Repeat the whole selection inside each training set of an outer cross-validation, and
   score the selected model on the outer test fold (<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
   cross-validation</NoteLink>). It costs about $K_\text{out}$ times the original search.
3. **Estimate the bias from the folds.** Tibshirani and Tibshirani proposed a correction that reuses the fold errors of
   one $K$-fold run and needs no new fits <Cite id="tibshirani2009bias" />.

The third route works as follows. Let $\lambda$ index the candidates, $\kappa(i)$ be the fold of point $i$, $C_k$ the
points of fold $k$ and $n_k$ their number. The error curve of fold $k$ is

$$
e_k(\lambda) = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i \in C_k}\ell\paren{y_i, \hat f^{-k}_\lambda(\xvec_i)},
$$

where $\hat f^{-k}_\lambda$ is candidate $\lambda$ fitted without fold $k$, and with equal folds
$\operatorname{CV}(\lambda) = \frac1K\sum_k e_k(\lambda)$. Let $\hat\lambda$ minimise $\operatorname{CV}$ and
$\hat\lambda_k$ minimise $e_k$. The bias estimate is

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Bias}} = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K\brack{e_k(\hat\lambda) - e_k(\hat\lambda_k)},
$$

and the corrected estimate is $\operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda) + \widehat{\operatorname{Bias}}$.

The motivation has two parts. Each $e_k(\hat\lambda_k)$ is the minimum of a noisy error curve, like
$\operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda)$, so it shares its optimism; each $e_k(\hat\lambda)$ is closer to an honest score of the
chosen candidate. The difference of the two mimics the gap between the minimum and an honest estimate. Every term is
non-negative, so the correction never lowers the estimate. In the authors' simulations it was accurate in most settings
and too large for <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink> and boosting with very small folds
<Cite id="tibshirani2009bias" />. The correction is approximate: $\hat\lambda$ was chosen using fold $k$ too, so
$e_k(\hat\lambda)$ is not fully independent of the selection.

## Worked example

**Bias of the best of 100 equal models.** A validation set has $m = 200$ examples. Each of $J = 100$ classifiers has
true error $0.20$, and their validation errors are independent.

1. **Noise scale.** Each validation error has standard deviation $\sqrt{0.2 \times 0.8/200} = 0.0283$.
2. **Expected minimum.** By the Gaussian approximation, the expected maximum of 100 standard Gaussians is $2.508$, so
   the best validation error is about $0.20 - 2.508 \times 0.0283 = 0.20 - 0.071 = 0.129$. A model with 20% error is
   reported at about 13%.
3. **The guarantee.** Hoeffding's lemma gives the proxy $\sigma = 1/(2\sqrt{200}) = 0.0354$. The maximal inequality
   bounds the expected optimism by $0.0354 \times \sqrt{2\ln 100} = 0.0354 \times 3.035 = 0.107$, whatever the
   dependence. With probability 95%, the optimism is below
   $0.0354 \times \sqrt{2\ln 2{,}000} = 0.0354 \times 3.899 = 0.138$.
4. **Sample size.** Keeping the bound on the expected optimism below $0.01$ needs
   $3.035/(2\sqrt m) \le 0.01$, so $m \ge 151.7^2 \approx 23{,}000$ validation examples. Under the Gaussian
   approximation with $\operatorname{Err} = 0.2$, $2.508 \times 0.4/\sqrt m \le 0.01$ needs $m \ge 10{,}064$.

**The fold-based correction by hand.** Three candidates are compared by 5-fold cross-validation with the 0–1 loss. The
fold errors $e_k(\lambda)$ are:

| candidate | fold 1 | fold 2 | fold 3 | fold 4 | fold 5 | $\operatorname{CV}$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\lambda_1$ | 0.30 | 0.22 | 0.26 | 0.28 | 0.24 | 0.26 |
| $\lambda_2$ | 0.24 | 0.28 | 0.20 | 0.26 | 0.27 | 0.25 |
| $\lambda_3$ | 0.28 | 0.25 | 0.24 | 0.22 | 0.31 | 0.26 |

1. **Select.** $\lambda_2$ has the smallest mean, so $\hat\lambda = \lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda) =
   0.25$.
2. **Per-fold minima.** The smallest entry in each column is 0.24, 0.22, 0.20, 0.22 and 0.24.
3. **Per-fold gaps.** Subtract each from the $\lambda_2$ entry of the same fold: $0.24 - 0.24 = 0$, $0.28 - 0.22 =
   0.06$, $0.20 - 0.20 = 0$, $0.26 - 0.22 = 0.04$ and $0.27 - 0.24 = 0.03$.
4. **Average.** $\widehat{\operatorname{Bias}} = (0 + 0.06 + 0 + 0.04 + 0.03)/5 = 0.026$.
5. **Correct.** The corrected estimate is $0.25 + 0.026 = 0.276$.

The three candidates have the same mean error to within 0.01, and each wins some folds. The correction reads this as
evidence that the minimum owes part of its value to noise.

## Avoiding it

- **Assess on data the selection never saw:** a test set, <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
  cross-validation</NoteLink>, or a <NoteLink to="reusable-holdout">reusable holdout</NoteLink> when the same holdout
  must answer many adaptive queries.
- **Lower the noise of the criterion.** Larger validation sets and
  <NoteLink to="repeated-and-monte-carlo-cross-validation">repeated cross-validation</NoteLink> reduce $\sigma$. Scoring
  every candidate on the same folds makes the differences between candidates less noisy than the scores themselves.
- **Search less.** Fewer hyperparameters, coarser grids and ranges set from prior knowledge lower the effective $J$.
  Cawley and Talbot recommend regularising the selection criterion itself, for example with priors on the
  hyperparameters <Cite id="cawley2010" />.
- **Prefer the simpler candidate within the noise.** The <NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error
  rule</NoteLink> picks the most regularised candidate whose score is within one <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
  error</NoteLink> of the best.
- **Average instead of selecting.** Averaging the predictions of the top candidates, or
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-model-averaging">Bayesian model averaging</NoteLink>, spreads the bet over the candidates that
  noise cannot separate.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting the tuned score as the model's performance.** The best cross-validation score of a search is a selection
  statistic, not an estimate of the chosen model's error. The same holds for a validation score used for
  <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> or checkpoint selection.
- **Comparing algorithms by their tuned scores.** An algorithm with more hyperparameters, or a larger search budget,
  gets more optimism. Tibshirani and Tibshirani warn that comparing minimum cross-validation errors across model
  families can mislead for this reason <Cite id="tibshirani2009bias" />; see
  <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink>.
- **Uncounted candidates.** Every variant tried counts towards $J$: feature sets, preprocessing choices, architectures
  and random seeds, including those tried informally and discarded. Choices made after looking at earlier scores are
  adaptive, and their cost can exceed $\sqrt{2\ln J}$ (see <NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">adaptive data
  analysis</NoteLink>).
- **Selection inside preprocessing.** Choosing features on all the data before cross-validation is selection over a huge
  number of candidates with no held-out data at all. <NoteLink to="selection-inside-cross-validation">Selection inside
  cross-validation</NoteLink> treats that case.
- **Trusting more search.** When the gaps between candidates are below the noise, a longer search lowers the reported
  error and can raise the true error of the chosen model.
