<Definition>
Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a learning procedure that includes <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>: given
a data set, it scores every candidate $\lambda \in \Lambda$ by an **inner**
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> with $K_\text{in}$ folds, picks the best, and refits it on
the whole data set. **Nested cross-validation** splits the $n$ examples into $K_\text{out}$ **outer** folds, runs
$\mathcal{A}$ on all data except outer fold $k$, and scores the resulting model on fold $k$. The nested estimate is the
average loss over all $n$ outer predictions,

$$
\operatorname{CV}_\text{nested} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell\paren{y_i, \mathcal{A}\paren{\Dcal^{-\kappa(i)}}(\xvec_i)},
$$

where $\kappa(i)$ is the outer fold of point $i$ and $\Dcal^{-k}$ is the data without outer fold $k$
<Cite id="varma2006" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A search over hyperparameters reports the best cross-validation score among its candidates. That score is optimistic,
because the candidate won partly through luck on the same folds that scored it (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). The optimism grows with the
number of candidates and shrinks with the amount of data. On small data sets it can exceed the differences between the
methods being compared <Cite id="cawley2010" />.

An honest estimate needs examples that the search never saw. A separate test set provides them, but on a small data set
it wastes examples and gives a noisy estimate from one split. Nested cross-validation treats the search as part of the
learning algorithm and cross-validates the whole thing. Every example serves once as an outer test point, and no outer
test point ever influences the choice it is used to score.

## Mechanism

1. **Split** the data into $K_\text{out}$ outer folds. Stratify for classification and group when records cluster, as
   for any <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold cross-validation</NoteLink>.
2. **For each outer fold $k$:**
   1. Take the outer training set $\Dcal^{-k}$, of about $n(K_\text{out} - 1)/K_\text{out}$ examples.
   2. Split $\Dcal^{-k}$ into $K_\text{in}$ inner folds. Score every candidate $\lambda \in \Lambda$ by inner
      cross-validation on $\Dcal^{-k}$ only, and let $\hat\lambda_k$ be the best.
   3. Refit candidate $\hat\lambda_k$ on all of $\Dcal^{-k}$.
   4. Predict outer fold $k$ with the refitted model and record its losses.
3. **Average** the losses over all $n$ outer predictions. This is the nested estimate.
4. **Fit the final model** by running the same search once on all $n$ examples and refitting its winner.

Every data-dependent step, including scaling, imputation, <NoteLink to="feature-selection">feature selection</NoteLink>
and resampling, belongs inside step 2.2, for the reasons given in
<NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>. The inner search can be a grid, a
<NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink> or <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian
optimisation</NoteLink>; nested cross-validation does not depend on how the candidates are proposed, only on the search
being repeated from scratch in every outer fold.

In scikit-learn, nested cross-validation is a `GridSearchCV` or `RandomizedSearchCV` object passed as the estimator to
`cross_val_score`.

## What it estimates

Nested cross-validation is ordinary $K$-fold cross-validation applied to the procedure $\mathcal{A}$. Two steps show
what that means.

1. **Each outer loss is an honest hold-out loss.** The model $\mathcal{A}(\Dcal^{-k})$ depends only on $\Dcal^{-k}$:
   its inner folds, its choice $\hat\lambda_k$ and its refit all use examples outside fold $k$. The examples of fold $k$
   are independent of $\Dcal^{-k}$. So, conditionally on $\Dcal^{-k}$, the expected loss at an outer test point is the
   true error of the fitted model, $\operatorname{Err}_{\Dcal^{-k}}(\mathcal{A})$.
2. **Average over the folds and the data.** Taking expectations over $\Dcal^{-k}$ turns each term into
   $\expect\operatorname{Err}_{\Dcal^{-k}}(\mathcal{A})$, the expected error of the procedure trained on
   $n(K_\text{out} - 1)/K_\text{out}$ examples. So $\expect\operatorname{CV}_\text{nested}$ equals that expected error.

The best inner score has no such identity. The minimum $\min_\lambda\operatorname{CV}(\lambda)$ is not the
cross-validation estimate of any procedure fixed in advance, because the minimisation looks at the scores of every fold
before choosing.

Three consequences follow.
- **It assesses the procedure, not one hyperparameter value.** Different outer folds can choose different
  $\hat\lambda_k$. The nested estimate averages over these choices, including the occasions on which the search picks
  badly.
- **It is slightly pessimistic.** Each outer training set is smaller than $n$. If the learning curve is still falling,
  the procedure trained on all $n$ examples does better, as for any $K$-fold estimate.
- **It estimates an expected error, not the error of the final model.** Like every cross-validation estimate, it tracks
  the error averaged over training sets of its size better than the error of the one model fitted to these data (see
  <NoteLink to="what-cross-validation-estimates">what cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>).

## The final model

The outer loop produces $K_\text{out}$ models and up to $K_\text{out}$ different choices $\hat\lambda_k$. None of them
is the model to deploy. The final model comes from step 4: the search run on all $n$ examples, with its winner refitted
on all of them. The nested estimate is the reported performance of that procedure.

The spread of the outer choices $\hat\lambda_1, \dots, \hat\lambda_{K_\text{out}}$ is a diagnostic. If every outer fold
chooses the same value, the selection is stable and the final search will likely choose it too. If the choices scatter
across the grid, the candidates are not separable at this sample size, and the
<NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink> or averaging over candidates may serve better
than the single winner.

## Worked example

The figure below at its defaults runs the problem by hand. There are $n = 60$ examples with labels that are pure noise,
exactly 30 of each class. There are $J = 10$ candidates, each a 5-nearest-neighbour classifier on its own pair of
standard normal features. Every candidate has true accuracy 0.5. The outer and inner loops both use stratified 5-fold
cross-validation, so each outer test fold has 12 examples and each outer training set 48.

| outer fold | candidate chosen | best inner accuracy | outer test accuracy |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 5 | 0.627 | 0.417 (5 of 12) |
| 2 | 2 | 0.622 | 0.583 (7 of 12) |
| 3 | 0 | 0.591 | 0.417 (5 of 12) |
| 4 | 9 | 0.662 | 0.583 (7 of 12) |
| 5 | 2 | 0.664 | 0.500 (6 of 12) |

1. **The inner scores are optimistic.** Every best inner accuracy is between 0.59 and 0.67, although no candidate can
   beat 0.5 on new data. Each is the best of ten noisy scores.
2. **The choices are unstable.** Five outer folds pick four different candidates. On noise there is no right answer, so
   the choice follows the noise of each outer training set.
3. **The nested estimate.** The outer predictions are right $5 + 7 + 5 + 7 + 6 = 30$ times out of 60, so
   $\operatorname{CV}_\text{nested} = 0.500$.
4. **The unnested estimate.** The search run once on all 60 examples picks candidate 3 with a cross-validation accuracy
   of 0.633. This is the number a tuning run would report.
5. **The truth.** The final model, candidate 3 refitted on all 60 examples, scores 0.505 on 4,000 fresh examples.

The unnested estimate overstates the accuracy by 0.13; the nested estimate is within 0.01 of the truth here. The nested
estimate is noisy in general: each outer score rests on 12 examples, with standard deviation about
$\sqrt{0.25/12} = 0.14$. Over many data sets it averages close to 0.5, as
<NoteLink to="how-optimistic-is-the-best-cross-validation-score">the example on noise labels</NoteLink> shows.

## Cost

A search over $\abs{\Lambda}$ candidates with $K_\text{in}$ folds costs $K_\text{in}\abs{\Lambda}$ fits plus one refit.
Nested cross-validation runs it $K_\text{out}$ times, once per outer training set, and the final model needs one more
run on all the data:

$$
\paren{K_\text{out} + 1}\paren{K_\text{in}\abs{\Lambda} + 1} \text{ fits}.
$$

With $K_\text{out} = K_\text{in} = 5$ and a grid of 50 values this is $6 \times 251 = 1{,}506$ fits, against 251 for the
search alone. The worked example costs $6 \times 51 = 306$ fits. The outer folds are independent and run in parallel.

Practice adjusts the cost to the data:
- **Small data:** 5 or 10 outer folds, often repeated with fresh splits, because one partition gives a noisy estimate.
  Krstajic and colleagues found the variation across splits large enough that they recommend repeating both the search
  and the nested assessment <Cite id="krstajic2014" />.
- **Large data or expensive models:** the outer loop collapses to one held-out test set, and the inner loop to one
  validation set. This is the train, validation and test split, a nested hold-out.
- **Fewer inner folds or a cheaper search:** 3 inner folds, a random search with a fixed budget, or
  early stopping of poor trials cut the cost without touching the outer loop's
  honesty.

## Trade-offs

- **Assessment against selection.** Nested cross-validation is needed to report an unbiased performance. It is less
  needed to choose. Wainer and Cawley compared nested and flat cross-validation for choosing among 12 classifiers on 115
  binary data sets: flat cross-validation chose algorithms of similar quality, when each had few hyperparameters
  <Cite id="wainer2021" />. The flat score is still optimistic and should not be reported.
- **Variance.** The nested estimate includes the variability of the selection itself, so it varies more across splits
  than a cross-validation score of one fixed model. The naive <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of
  the outer scores is too small, as for any cross-validation estimate. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani give a nested scheme
  for intervals with close to nominal coverage <Cite id="bates2024" />; see
  <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>.
- **Bias from smaller training sets.** Each outer training set has $n(K_\text{out} - 1)/K_\text{out}$ examples and each
  inner training set $n(K_\text{out} - 1)(K_\text{in} - 1)/(K_\text{out}K_\text{in})$, which is 32 of 50 for $5 \times
  5$ folds. On a steep learning curve the inner search may prefer simpler models than the full data would support.

## Pitfalls

- **Selecting on the outer scores.** Comparing several algorithms by their nested estimates and keeping the best turns
  the outer loop into a selection step, and the winning nested score is optimistic again. An honest assessment of that
  choice needs another level of nesting or a held-out test set.
- **Reporting an outer fold's model or the modal $\hat\lambda_k$.** The deployed model is the search refitted on all the
  data. The outer models exist only to score the procedure.
- **Preprocessing outside the outer loop.** A scaler, an encoder or a feature filter fitted on all the data leaks the
  outer test folds into every search (<NoteLink to="selection-inside-cross-validation">selection inside
  cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Splitting levels differently.** When records cluster by patient, user or time, both the outer and the inner splits
  must respect the clusters. Grouped outer folds with random inner folds let the inner search reward memorisation of
  groups (see <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Comparing inner and outer scores as if they measured the same thing.** The gap between them is an estimate of the
  optimism, but it also contains the effect of the smaller inner training sets.
