<Definition>
A holdout sample $S$ of $n$ examples is used **adaptively** when each new model $f_t$, or more generally each query, may
depend on the answers $R_1, \dots, R_{t - 1}$ already given for earlier ones. A **reusable holdout** is a mechanism that
answers such a sequence of queries so that, with high probability, every answer stays close to the true value on the
underlying distribution, for a number of queries far larger than a plain holdout supports. Thresholdout
<Cite id="dwork2015" /> answers general queries; the Ladder <Cite id="blum2015ladder" /> answers the narrower question
of a leaderboard: the true loss of the best model submitted so far.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A holdout score is an honest estimate of a model fixed before the holdout is looked at. Selecting the best of $J$ fixed
models costs only a $\sqrt{2\ln J}$ factor in the error of the estimate (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Development rarely works that
way. A score comes back; the analyst changes a feature, a threshold or an architecture; the next model is scored on the
same holdout. Each score leaks a little information about the particular holdout sample, and the next model is chosen
partly to fit it. After many rounds the holdout behaves like training data, and its scores overstate how well the models
do on new data. <NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">Adaptive data analysis</NoteLink> quantifies this: a plain holdout
of size $n$ answers about $n$ adaptive queries before its answers can be pushed off by a constant.

The remedy is to reveal less. An answer that does not depend on the fine detail of the holdout sample cannot be used to
fit that detail. Thresholdout reveals nothing about the holdout as long as the training data give the same answer. The
Ladder reveals a new number only when a submission is clearly better than the best so far. In both, the analyst learns
few bits per query, and the cost of adaptivity is paid per bit revealed, not per query asked.

## How a plain holdout fails

Blum and Hardt's boosting attack shows the failure in its simplest form <Cite id="blum2015ladder" />. On a binary task,
submit $k$ random label vectors for the $n$ holdout points, keep those that score above one half, and submit their
coordinate-wise majority vote. Each kept vector agrees with the true labels slightly more often than chance, by
accident of the sample, and the vote adds these accidents up. The vote's holdout accuracy is $\frac12 +
\Omega(\sqrt{k/n})$, although its accuracy on new data is exactly $\frac12$. The bias grows like $\sqrt k$, not like
$\sqrt{\ln k}$. <NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">Adaptive data analysis</NoteLink> derives the constant: about
$\Phi\paren{\sqrt{k/(\pi n)}} - \frac12$, with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function.

The attack needs no access to the data beyond the scores. An analyst who tunes against a public leaderboard by trial and
error runs a slower, unintended version of it.

## Thresholdout

Thresholdout splits the data into a training set $S_t$, which the analyst uses freely, and a holdout $S_h$, which only
the mechanism reads <Cite id="dwork2015" /> <Cite id="dwork2015holdout" />. A query is a function $\phi$ of an example,
such as the loss of a model, and $S[\phi]$ is its average over a sample $S$. The mechanism has a threshold $T$, a noise
scale $\sigma$ and a budget $B$.

1. For each query, compare the training average $S_t[\phi]$ with the holdout average $S_h[\phi]$, with Laplace noise
   added to the threshold.
2. If they agree to within the noisy threshold, answer with the training average $S_t[\phi]$. The holdout has revealed
   nothing.
3. If they disagree, answer with the holdout average plus Laplace noise of scale $\sigma$, and spend one unit of the
   budget. Stop when the budget is spent.

A query on which training and holdout agree is one where the analyst has not overfitted the training set, and the
training answer is already accurate. Only the overfitted queries cost budget, and each of them reveals a noisy, private
number. The privacy of the answers bounds how far any later query can be fitted to the holdout, and the mechanism stays
accurate for a number of queries exponential in $n$ as long as few of them overfit <Cite id="dwork2015holdout" />.
<NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">Adaptive data analysis</NoteLink> gives the transfer theorem from
<NoteLink to="differential-privacy">differential privacy</NoteLink> to generalisation on which this rests.

For <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>, Thresholdout replaces the validation set. The analyst
trains and tunes on $S_t$, and asks the mechanism for the validation score of each candidate. Candidates that generalise
get their training score back; those that overfit get a noisy holdout score, and the budget counts how often that
happened.

## Leaderboards and the Ladder

A competition leaderboard ranks submissions by their loss on a holdout. Its purpose is narrower than estimating every
submission's loss: it must report the loss of the best submission so far. Blum and Hardt define the **leaderboard
error** of the reported values $R_1, \dots, R_k$ as

$$
\operatorname{lberr}(R_1, \dots, R_k) = \max_{1 \le t \le k}\abs{\min_{1 \le i \le t} R_\Dcal(f_i) - R_t},
$$

where $R_\Dcal(f)$ is the true loss of submission $f$ on the distribution, with values in $[0, 1]$
<Cite id="blum2015ladder" />.

**The Ladder.** Fix a step $\eta > 0$ and start with $R_0 = \infty$. For each submission $f_t$, compute its holdout loss
$R_S(f_t)$.
- If $R_S(f_t) < R_{t - 1} - \eta$, report $R_t = R_S(f_t)$ rounded to a multiple of $\eta$.
- Otherwise report the previous value, $R_t = R_{t - 1}$.

A submission that does not beat the best by at least $\eta$ learns nothing it did not already know.

**Why it works: few transcripts.** The analyst is a fixed rule that maps the reported values so far to the next
submission. The derivation counts the transcripts the Ladder can produce.
1. **Few updates.** The reported value lies in $[0, 1]$ after the first update and falls by at least $\eta$ at every
   later one, so there are at most $1/\eta + 1$ updates.
2. **Few values.** Each reported value is a multiple of $\eta$ in $[0, 1]$, one of at most $1/\eta + 1$ values.
3. **Few transcripts.** A transcript of $t$ rounds is fixed by the rounds at which updates happen and the values
   reported there. Encoding the rounds and values takes at most $B = (1/\eta + 2)\log_2(4t/\eta)$ bits, so at most $2^B$
   transcripts exist, and the analyst can produce at most $2^B$ distinct submissions.
4. **Union bound.** For each fixed submission, <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>
   gives $\prob\paren{\abs{R_\Dcal(f) - R_S(f)} > \varepsilon} \le 2e^{-2\varepsilon^2 n}$. A
   <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> over the at most $2^B$ possible submissions makes every holdout
   loss accurate to $\varepsilon$ at once, except with probability at most $2^{B + 1}e^{-2\varepsilon^2 n}$.
5. **From losses to the leaderboard.** On that event, the minimum holdout loss over the submissions so far is within
   $\varepsilon$ of the minimum true loss. The Ladder reports a value within $\eta$ of the minimum holdout loss, because
   it skips only improvements smaller than $\eta$ and rounds to a multiple of $\eta$. So
   $\abs{\min_{i \le t}R_\Dcal(f_i) - R_t} \le \varepsilon + \eta$ for every $t$.

The failure probability is at most $\exp\paren{-2\varepsilon^2 n + B + 1}$. Choosing $\eta$ of order $n^{-1/3}$ balances
the two error terms and gives a leaderboard error of order $\log^{1/3}(kn)/n^{1/3}$ with high probability, for any
analyst and any number of submissions $k$ <Cite id="blum2015ladder" />. The dependence on $k$ is logarithmic, against
$\sqrt k$ for a full-precision leaderboard under the boosting attack.

**The parameter-free Ladder.** A fixed step is hard to choose before a competition starts. The parameter-free variant
keeps the loss vector of the best submission so far and updates only when a one-sided
<NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired $t$-test</NoteLink> on the per-example loss differences finds the new submission
better; the step then shrinks as the scores improve. Blum and Hardt describe the test as roughly a 0.15
<NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink> and call it a heuristic: the adaptively chosen
submissions break the test's assumptions <Cite id="blum2015ladder" />.

## Worked example

A competition holds out $n = 10^6$ examples and wants confidence $1 - \delta = 0.95$. Compare three regimes.

1. **Fixed submissions.** For $k = 1{,}000$ submissions fixed in advance, Hoeffding's inequality with a union bound
   makes every score accurate to $\sqrt{\ln(2k/\delta)/(2n)} = \sqrt{10.6/(2 \times 10^6)} = 0.0023$.
2. **A full-precision leaderboard under attack.** The boosting attack's bias is about
   $\Phi\paren{\sqrt{k/(\pi n)}} - \frac12$. For $k = 1{,}000$ this is $\Phi(0.0178) - 0.5 = 0.007$. For $k = 10^6$ it
   is $\Phi(0.564) - 0.5 = 0.214$: a random vote looks 21 points better than chance.
3. **The Ladder's guarantee.** Take $\eta = 0.015$ and require $2^{B + 1}e^{-2\varepsilon^2 n} \le \delta$, which holds
   when $2\varepsilon^2 n \ge B + 1 + \ln(1/\delta)$.
   - For $k = 1{,}000$: $B = (66.7 + 2)\log_2(4{,}000/0.015) = 68.7 \times 18.0 = 1{,}238$ bits, so
     $\varepsilon = \sqrt{(1{,}238 + 1 + 3.0)/(2 \times 10^6)} = 0.025$ and the leaderboard error is at most
     $0.025 + 0.015 = 0.040$.
   - For $k = 10^6$: $B = 68.7 \times \log_2(2.67 \times 10^8) = 68.7 \times 28.0 = 1{,}922$ bits, so
     $\varepsilon = 0.031$ and the error is at most $0.046$.

A thousandfold increase in submissions moves the Ladder's guarantee from 0.040 to 0.046, while the worst case for a
full-precision leaderboard grows thirtyfold. At $k = 1{,}000$ the Ladder's worst-case guarantee is looser than what the
attack achieves on a plain leaderboard: the guarantee covers every analyst, and its constants are conservative.

## In practice

- **Lock the final test set.** Model development uses a validation set, or
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> on the training data; the test set is scored once at the
  end. Nested schemes give the same separation on small data (see <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
  cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Coarse answers.** Competitions limit the number of submissions per day and report scores to few decimals. Both
  shrink the information revealed per query. The Ladder makes the same idea exact by reporting only significant
  improvements.
- **Private final rankings.** Competitions rank on a private holdout scored once, so that overfitting the public
  leaderboard costs the overfitter. Practical budgets, two-tier <NoteLink to="test-sets">test sets</NoteLink> and
  holdout rotation are covered in <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>.
- **Measured overfitting is often small.** A study of more than one hundred Kaggle competitions compared public and
  private leaderboards and found little evidence of substantial adaptive overfitting <Cite id="roelofs2019" />. Real
  analysts are not adversaries, and the worst-case bounds overstate the typical bias.

## Pitfalls

- **Counting submissions, not adaptivity.** A thousand models fixed in advance cost a $\sqrt{\ln k}$ factor. Ten models,
  each designed after reading the previous one's holdout score, can already overfit. What matters is how much each
  answer reveals and how the next query depends on it.
- **Reusing the training set as the holdout.** Thresholdout compares training and holdout answers; it does not make the
  training scores honest. The guarantees cover the holdout answers only.
- **Spent budgets.** Once Thresholdout's budget is spent, or once the Ladder has updated many times, the guarantee
  weakens. A fresh holdout restores it.
- **Leaderboard accuracy is not per-model accuracy.** The Ladder guarantees the best-so-far score. It does not report an
  accurate loss for each submission, and a submission that does not improve gets the old number back.
- **Noise is not free.** Thresholdout's answers are noisier than the plain holdout's. For a handful of queries the plain
  holdout is more accurate.
