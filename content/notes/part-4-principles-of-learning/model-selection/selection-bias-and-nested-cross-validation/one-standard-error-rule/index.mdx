<Definition>
Let the candidates $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_J$ be ordered from the most complex to the simplest, for example by an
increasing penalty. Let $\operatorname{CV}(\lambda)$ be the $K$-fold
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> error of candidate $\lambda$, and
$\operatorname{SE}(\lambda)$ its <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>, the standard deviation of the
$K$ fold errors divided by $\sqrt K$. Let $\hat\lambda_\text{min}$ minimise $\operatorname{CV}$. The
**one-standard-error rule** selects the simplest candidate whose error is within one standard error of the minimum
<Cite id="breiman1984" /> <Cite id="hastie2009" />:

$$
\hat\lambda_\text{1SE} = \text{the simplest } \lambda \text{ with } \operatorname{CV}(\lambda) \le
\operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda_\text{min}) + \operatorname{SE}(\hat\lambda_\text{min}).
$$
</Definition>

## Problem

A cross-validation curve is often flat near its minimum. Several candidates have errors that differ by less than the
noise in the estimates, and which of them comes out lowest depends on the particular split into folds. The minimiser
$\hat\lambda_\text{min}$ then moves from one split to the next, and its score is biased low because it won a contest
decided partly by noise (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).

Complexity is not free when the data cannot tell the candidates apart. A simpler model has fewer effective parameters,
varies less from sample to sample, is easier to interpret and, for the <NoteLink to="lasso">lasso</NoteLink>, uses fewer
features. The one-standard-error rule accepts extra complexity only when it buys an improvement larger than the
estimate's own uncertainty. It answers the select question of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>,
not the assess question: it chooses a candidate and says nothing unbiased about its error.

## Mechanism

1. **Cross-validate every candidate on the same folds.** Keep the fold errors $e_k(\lambda)$, the mean loss of the model
   fitted without fold $k$ on fold $k$, for $k = 1, \dots, K$.
2. **Compute the mean and its standard error** for each candidate:

   $$
   \operatorname{CV}(\lambda) = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K e_k(\lambda), \qquad
   \operatorname{SE}(\lambda) = \frac{1}{\sqrt K}\sqrt{\frac{1}{K - 1}\sum_{k=1}^K\paren{e_k(\lambda) - \operatorname{CV}(\lambda)}^2}.
   $$

3. **Find the minimum** $\hat\lambda_\text{min}$ and set the threshold
   $t = \operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda_\text{min}) + \operatorname{SE}(\hat\lambda_\text{min})$.
4. **Select the simplest candidate** with $\operatorname{CV}(\lambda) \le t$. Refit it on all the data.

The rule needs an order of simplicity. For a penalty, larger $\lambda$ is simpler. For a
<NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision tree</NoteLink>, fewer leaves are simpler; the rule comes from the pruning of
classification and regression trees <Cite id="breiman1984" />. For $k$-nearest neighbours, larger $k$ is simpler. For
polynomial regression, lower degree is simpler. The glmnet package reports both choices for the lasso and the
<NoteLink to="elastic-net">elastic net</NoteLink>, as `lambda.min` and `lambda.1se`.

## Why one standard error

The rule treats the standard error as the resolution of the curve. Two candidates whose estimates differ by less than
the noise of either estimate are not separated by the data. The minimum is also biased low by an amount of the order of
its standard error: with $J$ roughly independent, roughly equal candidates, the expected bias is about
$\operatorname{SE}\sqrt{2\ln J}$ (see <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequalities</NoteLink>). Moving up by
one standard error partly undoes this bias before candidates are compared against the minimum.

The rule is a heuristic, not a test. Three things make the comparison rough.
- **The standard error is too small.** The fold errors share training data and are correlated, and no unbiased
  estimator of the variance of $K$-fold cross-validation exists <Cite id="bengio2004" />. The usual standard error
  understates the uncertainty (see <NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation
  estimates</NoteLink>).
- **It ignores the pairing.** The candidates are scored on the same folds, so their errors are positively correlated.
  The difference between two candidates is less noisy than either score. A test on the paired fold differences would
  resolve smaller gaps than one standard error of a single score.
- **The factor 1 is a convention.** No argument makes one standard error optimal; a multiple $c$ of the standard error
  gives a family of rules that trade estimated error for simplicity.

## Worked example

The data are $n = 60$ rows of $d = 30$ inputs $\xvec \sim \Gauss(\boldsymbol 0, \eye_{30})$, with target
$y = \xvec^\top\betavec + \varepsilon$, $\beta_j = 0.85^{j - 1}$ and $\varepsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$.
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge regression</NoteLink> is cross-validated with 10 folds over eleven penalties,
half a decade apart from 0.01 to 1000. The numbers are those of the figure below at its defaults (seed 1). The fold
errors of the best penalty, $\lambda = 3.16$, are 3.981, 1.285, 1.322, 0.473, 1.050, 1.537, 0.372, 1.376, 1.044 and
1.214.

1. **Mean.** The ten fold errors sum to 13.65, so $\operatorname{CV}(3.16) = 1.365$.
2. **Spread.** The squared deviations from 1.365 sum to 8.886. Dividing by $K - 1 = 9$ gives a variance of 0.987 and a
   standard deviation of 0.994. One fold, with error 3.981, contributes 6.84 of the 8.886.
3. **Standard error.** $\operatorname{SE}(3.16) = 0.994/\sqrt{10} = 0.314$.
4. **Threshold.** $t = 1.365 + 0.314 = 1.680$.
5. **Scan the candidates.** The full curve, from the most complex candidate to the simplest, with the true error of each
   ridge fit to all 60 rows, measured on 20 000 fresh rows:

| penalty $\lambda$ | $\operatorname{CV}(\lambda)$ | within $t = 1.680$? | true error |
| ---: | ---: | --- | ---: |
| 0.01 | 1.639 | yes | 1.852 |
| 0.0316 | 1.633 | yes | 1.846 |
| 0.1 | 1.615 | yes | 1.827 |
| 0.316 | 1.568 | yes | 1.774 |
| 1 | 1.468 | yes | 1.660 |
| 3.16 | **1.365** | yes | 1.553 |
| 10 | 1.454 | yes | 1.695 |
| 31.6 | 1.927 | no | 2.263 |
| 100 | 2.645 | no | 3.191 |
| 316 | 3.246 | no | 4.054 |
| 1000 | 3.564 | no | 4.546 |

6. **Select.** The simplest candidate below the threshold is $\lambda = 10$, with $\operatorname{CV}(10) = 1.454$. The
   rule gives up an estimated 0.088 of error, less than the standard error of 0.314, for about three times more
   shrinkage.
7. **Against the truth.** The true error is smallest at $\lambda = 3.16$, the cross-validation minimum, where it is
   1.553. The rule's choice has true error 1.695, worse by 0.142. Here the simpler model costs more than its estimated
   0.088.

The standard error is so wide that every candidate from $\lambda = 0.01$ to $\lambda = 10$ lies within it. The folds
cannot tell those seven penalties apart, and the rule resolves the tie in favour of the simplest.

## Cost

The rule costs nothing beyond the cross-validation it reads, provided the fold errors are kept rather than only their
means.

## Trade-offs

- **Prediction against sparsity.** Chen and Yang studied the rule for the lasso. It often selected the true variables
  more reliably than the minimum, but for estimating the regression function or for prediction it often did worse. They
  also found that the standard error it uses is often a poor estimate of the standard deviation of the cross-validation
  error <Cite id="chen2021onese" />. The rule suits a goal of a simple or sparse model more than a goal of minimum
  prediction error.
- **Stability.** Because the threshold sits above a flat region, the selected candidate moves less across splits than
  the minimiser does, as long as the curve rises steeply on the simple side.
- **Bias toward underfitting.** On a curve that rises slowly on the simple side, the rule can move far from the minimum
  and choose a model that is clearly too simple once more data arrive.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the selected score as an assessment.** The 1-SE choice was selected using the same folds; its score is not
  an honest estimate of its error. <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">Nested cross-validation</NoteLink> with the
  rule inside the inner loop assesses the procedure.
- **Using the wrong standard error.** The rule uses the standard error at the minimiser, not at each candidate, and the
  standard error of the mean of the fold errors, not their standard deviation. Dividing by $\sqrt K$ matters: with 10
  folds, the standard deviation is about three times the standard error.
- **No order of simplicity.** The rule needs candidates ordered by complexity. Across unrelated model families, such as
  a forest against a <Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss>, "simplest" is undefined.
- **Too few folds.** With $K = 3$ or 5, the standard error rests on three or five numbers and is itself very noisy.
  Repeating the cross-validation with fresh splits and averaging the fold errors gives a steadier curve and threshold.
