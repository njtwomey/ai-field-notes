<Definition>
A **hyperparameter** is a setting of a learning procedure that is fixed before training, such as a
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>, a penalty strength or a tree depth. **Hyperparameter search** chooses
a configuration $\lambdavec$ from a search space $\Lambda$ to minimise a validation loss $f(\lambdavec)$, measured by
training with $\lambdavec$ and evaluating on held-out data or by
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>. Each evaluation of $f$ is expensive and noisy, and $f$ has
no usable <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>.
</Definition>

## Problem

Training a model once may take minutes or days, and there may be ten or more hyperparameters. Trying every
combination is impossible. The search has to decide which configurations to try, in what order, and how much training
to spend on each before judging it.

## Mechanism

### Grid search and random search

**Grid search** tries every combination of a few values per hyperparameter. With $K$ values for each of $D$
hyperparameters it costs $K^D$ trials. **Random search** draws each of $N$ trials independently from a distribution over
$\Lambda$, typically uniform on a logarithmic scale for scale parameters such as learning rates.

Random search is usually better at the same budget, because the validation loss typically depends strongly on only a
few hyperparameters, and which ones varies from dataset to dataset <Cite id="bergstra2012" />. Suppose only one of $D$
hyperparameters matters. A grid of $N = K^D$ trials tests only $K = N^{1/D}$ distinct values of it, since every other
trial repeats one of those values with a different setting of the irrelevant ones. Random search tests $N$ distinct
values. With $D = 2$ and $N = 9$, the grid tries 3 values of the important hyperparameter and random search tries 9.

The same argument gives a budget rule that does not depend on $D$. If a region holds a fraction $q$ of the search
space, $N$ random trials miss it with probability $(1 - q)^N$. For the best 5% of configurations, $q = 0.05$, and
$1 - 0.95^{60} = 0.954$: sixty random trials land in the top 5% with probability above 95%, whatever the dimension.

### Bayesian optimisation

Grid and random search ignore the results of earlier trials. **Bayesian optimisation** fits a probabilistic
**surrogate model** of $f$ to all trials so far, usually a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink>,
and chooses the next trial by maximising an **acquisition function** that trades off a low predicted loss against
high uncertainty <Cite id="jones1998" /> <Cite id="snoek2012" />.

At a candidate $\lambdavec$, the surrogate predicts $f(\lambdavec) \sim \Gauss(\mu, s^2)$. Let $f^*$ be the best loss
found so far. The **improvement** is $I = \max(f^* - f(\lambdavec), 0)$, and the **expected improvement** (EI) is its
mean. Substituting $f(\lambdavec) = \mu + s z$ with $z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, the improvement is positive when
$z < z^* = (f^* - \mu)/s$, so

$$
\operatorname{EI}(\lambdavec) = \int_{-\infty}^{z^*}(f^* - \mu - s z)\,\phi(z)\,dz = (f^* - \mu)\,\Phi(z^*) + s\,\phi(z^*),
$$

where $\phi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal density and distribution function. The second step uses
$\int_{-\infty}^{z^*}z\phi(z)\,dz = -\phi(z^*)$, since $\phi'(z) = -z\phi(z)$. The first term rewards a predicted mean
below the incumbent (exploitation). The second rewards uncertainty (exploration). Both are needed: EI is positive
wherever $s > 0$, so no region is ruled out forever.

### Successive halving and Hyperband

Most bad configurations are visibly bad early in training. **Successive halving** starts $n$ configurations with a
small budget $r$ each (epochs, or a subsample of the data), keeps the best $1/\eta$ of them, multiplies the budget by
$\eta$, and repeats until one remains <Cite id="jamieson2016" />. The difficulty is choosing $n$: many configurations
with a small starting budget risk discarding a slow starter, while few configurations with a large budget waste less
but explore less. **Hyperband** runs several brackets of successive halving that span this trade-off, from aggressive
(many configurations, tiny budgets) to plain random search (few configurations, full budget)
<Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.

## Worked example

**Expected improvement.** The best validation error so far is $f^* = 0.28$. Candidate A has surrogate prediction
$\mu = 0.30$, $s = 0.05$; candidate B has $\mu = 0.27$, $s = 0.005$. For A, $z^* = -0.4$ and
$\operatorname{EI} = (-0.02)(0.345) + 0.05(0.368) = 0.0115$. For B, $z^* = 2$ and
$\operatorname{EI} = 0.01(0.977) + 0.005(0.054) = 0.0100$. EI prefers A, whose mean is worse, because A has a real
chance of a large improvement while B can improve by at most a little.

**Successive halving.** Take $n = 27$ configurations, $\eta = 3$ and a starting budget of 1 epoch. The rounds run 27
configurations for 1 epoch, 9 for 3, 3 for 9 and 1 for 27. Each round costs 27 epochs, 108 in total, against
$27 \times 27 = 729$ to train every configuration fully. Hyperband with a maximum budget of 27 and $\eta = 3$ runs four
brackets, starting with 27 configurations at budget 1, 12 at 3, 6 at 9 and 4 at 27.

## Cost

Each trial costs one training run, or $K$ for $K$-fold cross-validation. Random and grid search parallelise
perfectly. Bayesian optimisation is sequential by nature and adds the cost of refitting the surrogate, which for a
Gaussian process grows as the cube of the number of trials. It pays off when training is expensive and the budget is
tens to hundreds of trials. Successive halving and Hyperband cut the cost per configuration, often by an order of
magnitude.

## Trade-offs

- **Search space.** Sampling scale parameters on a log scale matters more than the choice of search algorithm. A
  learning rate drawn uniformly from $[10^{-5}, 1]$ spends 90% of its trials above $0.1$.
- **<NoteLink to="early-stopping">Early stopping</NoteLink> assumes rankings persist.** Successive halving discards
  configurations that start slowly, such as those with small learning rates or strong
  <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>, even if they would finish best.
- **<NoteLink to="feature-selection">Selection bias</NoteLink>.** The best of many validation scores is an optimistic
  estimate of that configuration's error. The more configurations are tried, the larger the optimism. Report the error
  of the selected configuration on data not used in the search, or use nested cross-validation <Cite id="cawley2010" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Tuning on the test set.** Any score used to choose a configuration is no longer an unbiased estimate of error.
- **Grids with coarse spacing miss narrow optima.** A grid of 3 values per axis cannot find a peak narrower than its
  spacing, however many other axes it has.
- **Comparing methods with unequal tuning.** A new method tuned with 100 trials against a baseline tuned with 10
  measures tuning effort, not the methods.
