<Definition>
**Automated machine learning** (<Gloss name="automl" />) is the automatic construction of a predictive pipeline from
data: the preprocessing steps, the learning algorithm and all their hyperparameters <Cite id="hutter2019automl" />.
Its core is the <Gloss name="cash" /> problem. Given learning algorithms $A^{(1)}, \dots, A^{(J)}$ with hyperparameter
spaces $\Lambda^{(1)}, \dots, \Lambda^{(J)}$ and $K$ validation splits of the data $\Dcal$, it seeks

$$
(A^\star, \lambdavec^\star) \in \argmin_{j \in \{1, \dots, J\},\ \lambdavec \in \Lambda^{(j)}} \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
L\paren{A^{(j)}_{\lambdavec},\ \Dcal^{(k)}_\text{train},\ \Dcal^{(k)}_\text{valid}},
$$

where $L(A_{\lambdavec}, \Dcal_\text{train}, \Dcal_\text{valid})$ is the validation loss of algorithm $A$ with
hyperparameters $\lambdavec$ trained on $\Dcal_\text{train}$ <Cite id="thornton2013" />. Most systems solve it by
Bayesian optimisation, start the search from configurations that worked on earlier data sets, and return an ensemble
of the models trained during the search.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Applying machine learning to a new table of data involves a chain of choices. Missing values must be imputed, categories
encoded, features scaled or selected, and a learner chosen and tuned. Each choice interacts with the others: the best
penalty of a linear model depends on how the features were scaled, and whether feature selection helps depends on the
learner. An expert makes these choices from experience and a few experiments. AutoML makes them by search, treating the
whole pipeline as one configuration and the whole chain of choices as one search space.

The approach rests on three observations. Algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning are the same kind of problem,
so one optimiser can do both. Past data sets carry information about which pipelines tend to work. And a search
trains many good models, so combining several of them usually beats keeping only the best.

AutoML is model selection at its widest. Every risk of model selection applies with more force, because the number of
candidates compared on the validation data is larger than in any hand-run search.

## The CASH problem

The CASH problem becomes an ordinary hyperparameter search by adding one categorical **root** hyperparameter
$\lambda_r \in \{1, \dots, J\}$ that names the algorithm. The search space is

$$
\Lambda = \{\lambda_r\} \cup \Lambda^{(1)} \cup \dots \cup \Lambda^{(J)},
$$

and the hyperparameters of $\Lambda^{(j)}$ are **conditional**: they are active only when $\lambda_r = j$
<Cite id="thornton2013" />. Preprocessing steps add further root-level choices, each with conditional children. The
result is a tree-structured space (see <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">hyperparameter search
spaces</NoteLink>).

A tree-structured space is the union of its branches, not their product. Take three learners: logistic regression with
a penalty $C$; a support vector machine with $C$, a kernel width $\gamma$ and a kernel choice; and a random forest with
the number of trees, the fraction of features tried at each split and the minimum leaf size. The joint space has
$1 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 8$ hyperparameters, but a single configuration activates at most 4 of them. A search method must cope
with this structure. Gaussian-process surrogates assume a fixed-dimensional input and handle it poorly. Random-forest
surrogates, as in <NoteLink to="sequential-model-based-algorithm-configuration">SMAC</NoteLink>, and the
<NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">tree-structured Parzen estimator</NoteLink> handle it directly, which is
why AutoML systems use them.

The validation loss in the CASH objective is computed by $K$-fold cross-validation or a single hold-out split. Every
fitted step of the pipeline, from the imputer to the learner, is refitted on each training split
(see <NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>).

## Systems

<AutoMlLoop />

- **Auto-WEKA** posed the CASH problem and solved it over the WEKA library: 27 base classifiers, 10 meta-methods,
  2 ensemble methods and a choice of feature-selection methods, 786 hyperparameters in a four-level tree. It searched
  the space with SMAC and with TPE <Cite id="thornton2013" />.
- **Auto-sklearn** searched pipelines of scikit-learn: 15 classifiers, 14 feature preprocessing methods and 4 data
  preprocessing methods, 110 hyperparameters in all. It added two components to Bayesian optimisation by SMAC. A warm
  start ran first the best configurations of the 25 most similar of 140 earlier data sets. An ensemble of 50 was built
  from all the models evaluated during the search, by ensemble selection <Cite id="feurer2015autosklearn" />.
- **Auto-sklearn 2.0** replaced the similarity-based warm start with a fixed portfolio of 32 pipelines, allocated
  budget by successive halving, and chose between hold-out and cross-validation with a learned policy
  <Cite id="feurer2022autosklearn2" />. The portfolio construction is described in
  <NoteLink to="warm-starting-hyperparameter-optimisation">warm-starting hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink>.
- **TPOT** represents a pipeline as a tree of operators and searches over trees by genetic programming, so the shape of
  the pipeline is searched as well as its settings <Cite id="olson2016tpot" />.
- **AutoGluon-Tabular** does not search the CASH space. It trains a fixed list of model types with good defaults,
  bags each one, and stacks them in several layers. Its authors report that this combination uses a time budget
  better than searching for the single best model <Cite id="erickson2020autogluon" />.

The AutoML Benchmark compared 9 frameworks on 71 classification and 33 regression tasks under time limits of one and
four hours. AutoGluon had the highest average rank, and in most scenarios the frameworks beat the strongest baseline,
a tuned random forest. The most accurate frameworks also produced the slowest models at prediction time, sometimes by
orders of magnitude <Cite id="gijsbers2024amlb" />.

## Ensembles of the search

A search over $N$ configurations trains $N$ models, and the default output, the single best, discards the rest. Many
of the others are nearly as good and make different errors. **Ensemble selection** combines them using only their
predictions on the validation data <Cite id="caruana2004ensemble" />.

1. Store every model's predictions on the validation set.
2. Start from an empty ensemble. The ensemble predicts the unweighted average of its members' predictions.
3. Add the model, from the whole library, whose addition gives the lowest validation loss. A model may be added more
   than once.
4. Repeat for a fixed number of rounds, and return the ensemble with the lowest validation loss seen.

A model added $c$ times out of $R$ rounds has weight $c/R$, so the result is a weighted average with non-negative
weights that sum to 1. Selection **with replacement** keeps the validation loss from rising once the useful models are
used up, because re-adding a good model is always available. Caruana et al. built libraries of about 2000 models and
optimised the ensemble directly for the metric of interest, such as accuracy or the area under the ROC curve
<Cite id="caruana2004ensemble" />. Feurer et al. found that stacking and direct numerical optimisation of the weights
overfitted the validation set, while ensemble selection was fast and robust <Cite id="feurer2015autosklearn" />.

Ensemble selection is a form of <NoteLink to="stacking">stacking</NoteLink> with a constrained meta-model. The
constraint is what makes it safe to fit on a small validation set.

## Worked example

A search has trained three regression models. Their predictions on four validation points with targets
$\yvec = (3, 1, 4, 2)$ are:

| model | point 1 | point 2 | point 3 | point 4 | validation MSE |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A | 2.6 | 1.6 | 3.4 | 2.4 | 0.260 |
| B | 3.6 | 0.8 | 4.8 | 1.4 | 0.350 |
| C | 3.2 | 1.4 | 4.4 | 2.6 | 0.180 |

The <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> of A is
$\paren{0.4^2 + 0.6^2 + 0.6^2 + 0.4^2}/4 = 0.26$, and similarly for B and C.

1. **Round 1.** The best single model is C, with 0.180. The ensemble is $\{C\}$.
2. **Round 2.** Averaging C with A gives errors $(-0.1, 0.5, -0.1, 0.5)$ and a mean squared error of 0.130. Averaging
   C with B gives $(0.4, 0.1, 0.6, 0.0)$ and 0.1325. Adding C again leaves 0.180. Selection adds A.
3. **Round 3.** The members' errors in $\{C, A\}$ sum to $(-0.2, 1.0, -0.2, 1.0)$ over the four points. Adding B, whose
   errors are $(0.6, -0.2, 0.8, -0.6)$, gives the sum $(0.4, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4)$; divided by 3, the errors are
   $(0.13, 0.27, 0.20, 0.13)$ and the mean squared error is 0.037. Adding A gives 0.153 and adding C gives 0.127.
   Selection adds B, and the ensemble is the equal average of A, B and C.
4. **Rounds 4 and 5.** Adding A gives 0.041, then adding B gives 0.025. The ensemble $\{C, A, B, A, B\}$ has weights
   $0.2$, $0.4$ and $0.4$.

The validation error fell from 0.180 for the best single model to 0.037 with three equal members and 0.025 with
tuned weights. The last fall is suspect. Four validation points cannot support three weights tuned to the second
decimal. With replacement, each round adds a degree of freedom in the weights, and the validation loss of the ensemble
falls as the weights adapt to the noise of those four points. The same validation data chose the configurations, the
models and the weights, so none of these numbers estimates the error on new data.

## Assessing an AutoML system

An AutoML run is a learning algorithm whose output is a pipeline. Its own validation score is a selection statistic
over thousands of candidates, and is optimistic in the way described in <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">
overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>. Honest assessment needs data the run never saw: a held-out test set, or
<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink> with the whole AutoML run inside each outer
fold. Benchmarks such as the AutoML Benchmark fix the time budget and the hardware, and score every framework on the
same outer test folds <Cite id="gijsbers2024amlb" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting the search's own score.** The best validation loss of a large search, and the validation loss of the
  ensemble built on the same data, are both optimistic. Report a test score.
- **Preprocessing outside the search.** Imputing, encoding or selecting features on all the data before handing it to
  an AutoML system leaks the validation folds into every candidate. Give the system raw data and let it fit every step
  inside its folds.
- **A validation set too small for the search.** Thousands of candidates, plus ensemble weights, compared on a few
  hundred validation rows will fit the noise. Prefer <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> for
  small data, and limit the ensemble.
- **Unequal budgets.** AutoML frameworks, and AutoML against a hand-tuned baseline, are comparable only at the same
  time limit and hardware. A framework that fails or times out on a task needs an explicit rule, such as scoring it as
  the constant predictor.
- **Ignoring prediction cost.** An ensemble of 50 stacked models can be far more accurate and far slower than one
  tuned model. Deployment constraints belong in the objective, not in an afterthought.
- **Treating AutoML as a substitute for problem framing.** The search optimises the loss it is given on the data it is
  given. The choice of target, metric, split and features remains the user's, and leakage in the data defeats any
  search.
