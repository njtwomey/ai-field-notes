<Definition>
Let $\wvec^\star(\lambdavec)$ be the parameters that training produces with continuous hyperparameters $\lambdavec \in
\reals^D$, and let $L(\lambdavec) = L_\text{val}\paren{\wvec^\star(\lambdavec)}$ be the resulting validation loss.
**Gradient-based hyperparameter optimisation** computes the **hypergradient** $\nabla_{\lambdavec} L$ by differentiating
through training, and minimises $L$ by <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> on $\lambdavec$. The
hypergradient is computed either by the **implicit function theorem** at a minimiser of the training loss
<Cite id="bengio2000" />, or by **unrolled differentiation** through the steps of the training algorithm
<Cite id="maclaurin2015hyper" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">Hyperparameter search</NoteLink> treats $L$ as a black box.
<NoteLink to="grid-search">Grid search</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink> and Bayesian
optimisation need a number of trials that grows with the number of hyperparameters that matter, so they handle tens of
hyperparameters at most. Some models have far more: a separate penalty on each weight, a
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> for each step of a schedule, the weights of a network that augments
the training data. These hyperparameters are continuous, and training depends smoothly on them. For such
hyperparameters, the hypergradient points downhill in all $D$ dimensions at once, at the cost of about one extra
training-sized computation.

## Mechanism

### The bilevel problem

Hyperparameter optimisation is a **bilevel** problem: an outer minimisation over $\lambdavec$ whose objective contains
an inner minimisation over $\wvec$,

$$
\min_{\lambdavec} L_\text{val}\paren{\wvec^\star(\lambdavec)}, \qquad \wvec^\star(\lambdavec) = \argmin_{\wvec}
L_\text{tr}(\wvec, \lambdavec),
$$

where $L_\text{tr}$ is the training objective with its penalties and $L_\text{val}$ is the loss on validation data.
Write $p$ for the number of parameters. By the chain rule,

$$
\nabla_{\lambdavec} L = \paren{\frac{\partial\wvec^\star}{\partial\lambdavec}}^\top\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{val},
$$

where $\partial\wvec^\star/\partial\lambdavec$ is the $p \times D$ Jacobian of the trained parameters. When
$L_\text{val}$ depends on $\lambdavec$ directly as well, its partial derivative
$\partial L_\text{val}/\partial\lambdavec$ is added. The two methods differ in how they get
$\partial\wvec^\star/\partial\lambdavec$.

### Implicit differentiation

Assume training reaches a minimiser of $L_\text{tr}$ at which the Hessian is invertible. Then for every $\lambdavec$
near the current one,

$$
\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{tr}\paren{\wvec^\star(\lambdavec), \lambdavec} = \zeros.
$$

Differentiating this identity in $\lambdavec$ gives

$$
\Hmat\,\frac{\partial\wvec^\star}{\partial\lambdavec} + \Jmat = \zeros, \qquad \Hmat =
\nabla^2_{\wvec}L_\text{tr}, \qquad \Jmat = \frac{\partial^2 L_\text{tr}}{\partial\wvec\,\partial\lambdavec^\top},
$$

where $\Hmat$ is the $p \times p$ Hessian and $\Jmat$ is the $p \times D$ matrix of mixed derivatives, both at
$\paren{\wvec^\star(\lambdavec), \lambdavec}$. The implicit function theorem guarantees that $\wvec^\star$ is
differentiable when $\Hmat$ is invertible, and solving gives

$$
\frac{\partial\wvec^\star}{\partial\lambdavec} = -\Hmat^{-1}\Jmat.
$$

Substituting into the chain rule,

$$
\nabla_{\lambdavec} L = -\Jmat^\top\Hmat^{-1}\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{val} = -\Jmat^\top\vvec, \qquad \text{where }
\Hmat\vvec = \nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{val}.
$$

The order of operations matters for the cost. Forming $\Hmat^{-1}\Jmat$ needs $D$ solves with $\Hmat$. Solving for the
single vector $\vvec$ first needs one, whatever $D$ is. This is reverse-mode differentiation applied to the optimality
condition (see <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink>). The result needs only the trained parameters,
not the path that training took to reach them.

### Ridge regression

<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge regression</NoteLink> makes every quantity explicit. Take

$$
L_\text{tr}(\wvec, \lambda) = \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2 + \lambda\norm{\wvec}^2, \qquad L_\text{val}(\wvec) =
\frac1m\norm{\yvec_v - \Xmat_v\wvec}^2,
$$

with $n$ training rows in $\Xmat$ and $m$ validation rows in $\Xmat_v$. The gradient of the training objective is
$\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{tr} = -2\Xmat^\top(\yvec - \Xmat\wvec) + 2\lambda\wvec$. Its derivative in $\wvec$ is
$\Hmat = 2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\Imat)$, and its derivative in $\lambda$ is $\Jmat = 2\wvec$. Hence

$$
\frac{d\wvec^\star}{d\lambda} = -(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\Imat)^{-1}\wvec^\star, \qquad \frac{dL}{d\lambda} =
-\gvec^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\Imat)^{-1}\wvec^\star,
$$

where $\gvec = \nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{val} = -\frac2m\Xmat_v^\top(\yvec_v - \Xmat_v\wvec^\star)$. The Cholesky factor of
$\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \lambda\Imat$ from the fit serves for the extra solve.

Now give each weight its own penalty, $\sum_j \lambda_j w_j^2$, so that $D = p$. Then
$\Jmat = 2\operatorname{diag}(\wvec)$, and with $\vvec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat + \operatorname{diag}(\lambdavec))^{-1}\gvec$,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda_j} = -v_j w^\star_j, \qquad j = 1, \dots, p.
$$

All $p$ hypergradients come from one solve. A grid over $p$ penalties would need $r^p$ fits.

### Unrolled differentiation

Training is often stopped before it converges, and some hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, do not change the
minimiser at all, only the path to it. Unrolled differentiation treats the $T$ steps of the training algorithm,

$$
\wvec_{t+1} = \Phi_t(\wvec_t, \lambdavec), \qquad t = 0, \dots, T - 1,
$$

as one long differentiable computation from $\lambdavec$ to $L_\text{val}(\wvec_T)$. For gradient descent with learning
rate $\eta_t$, $\Phi_t(\wvec, \lambdavec) = \wvec - \eta_t\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{tr}(\wvec, \lambdavec)$, and the
$\eta_t$ themselves can be hyperparameters.
- **Reverse mode** backpropagates from $L_\text{val}(\wvec_T)$ through all $T$ steps. Its cost is a small multiple of
  training, but it must store or recompute every $\wvec_t$, $O(pT)$ memory. Maclaurin, Duvenaud and Adams recompute the
  trajectory backwards by exactly reversing <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> with momentum, and
  tuned thousands of hyperparameters, including <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">step-size schedules</NoteLink>
  and initialisation scales <Cite id="maclaurin2015hyper" />.
- **Forward mode** carries the $p \times D$ Jacobian $\Zmat_t = \partial\wvec_t/\partial\lambdavec$ along with training,
  using $\Zmat_{t+1} = \Amat_t\Zmat_t + \Bmat_t$ with $\Amat_t = \partial\Phi_t/\partial\wvec$ and
  $\Bmat_t = \partial\Phi_t/\partial\lambdavec$. Its memory is $O(pD)$ and does not grow with $T$, so it suits few
  hyperparameters and long training, and it gives a hypergradient at every step, which allows the hyperparameters to be
  updated during training <Cite id="franceschi2017" /> (see <NoteLink to="forward-mode-autodiff">forward-mode
  differentiation</NoteLink>).
- **Truncation** backpropagates through only the last $k$ steps. Shaban et al. found that a few steps often perform
  about as well as the full unroll with much less memory <Cite id="shaban2019" />.

### Approximate solves

For a neural network, $\Hmat$ is too large to form or factor. Implicit differentiation then solves $\Hmat\vvec = \gvec$
approximately, using only Hessian-vector products, which cost about two gradient evaluations each.
- **<Gloss name="cg">Conjugate gradients</Gloss>.** Pedregosa's HOAG solves the inner problem and the linear system only
  approximately, with tolerances that shrink over the outer iterations, and proves that the outer iterates still
  converge <Cite id="pedregosa2016" />.
- **The Neumann series.** If $\norm{\Imat - \alpha\Hmat} < 1$ for a step size $\alpha$, then
  $\Hmat^{-1} = \alpha\sum_{j=0}^\infty(\Imat - \alpha\Hmat)^j$, and truncating after $k$ terms gives an approximation
  that needs $k$ Hessian-vector products. Lorraine, Vicol and Duvenaud used it to tune millions of hyperparameters,
  among them the weights of a network that augments the training data <Cite id="lorraine2020" />.

The truncated Neumann series and truncated unrolling are closely related: $k$ terms of the series match $k$ steps of
gradient descent unrolled from the optimum.

## Worked example

Fit ridge regression without an intercept to three training points, $x = (1, 2, 3)$ and $y = (1, 3, 2)$, and validate
on two, $x_v = (1, 2)$ and $y_v = (0.5, 1.5)$, with the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss>.

1. **The trained weight.** With one feature, $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$ and $\Xmat^\top\yvec = 1 + 6 + 6 = 13$,
   so $w^\star(\lambda) = 13/(14 + \lambda)$.
2. **The validation loss.** $L = \frac12\brack{(0.5 - w)^2 + (1.5 - 2w)^2}$, with derivative
   $dL/dw = -(0.5 - w) - 2(1.5 - 2w) = 5w - 3.5$.
3. **The implicit derivative.** The ridge formula gives $dw^\star/d\lambda = -w^\star/(14 + \lambda)$.
4. **At $\lambda = 1$.** The weight is $w^\star = 13/15 = 0.8667$. The loss is
   $\frac12(0.3667^2 + 0.2333^2) = 0.0944$. The two derivatives are $dL/dw = 5(0.8667) - 3.5 = 0.8333$ and
   $dw^\star/d\lambda = -0.8667/15 = -0.0578$. Their product is the hypergradient,
   $dL/d\lambda = 0.8333 \times (-0.0578) = -0.0481$. It is negative, so a larger penalty lowers the validation loss.
5. **One step on $\ln\lambda$.** Penalties are searched on a log scale, so descend on $\ln\lambda$, whose derivative is
   $dL/d\ln\lambda = \lambda\,dL/d\lambda = -0.0481$. With step size $\eta = 10$,
   $\ln\lambda \leftarrow 0 + 10 \times 0.0481 = 0.481$, so $\lambda = 1.62$.
6. **Further steps.** Repeating the computation gives $\lambda = 2.86$ after two steps and $4.55$ after three, with
   validation losses $0.0688$, $0.0376$ and $0.0250$.
7. **The optimum.** The loss is smallest where $dL/dw = 0$, at $w = 0.7$, so
   $\lambda^\star = 13/0.7 - 14 = 4.57$ with $L = \frac12(0.2^2 + 0.1^2) = 0.025$. Three hypergradient steps have nearly
   reached it, each costing one fit and one extra solve.

A central <Gloss name="finite-difference">finite difference</Gloss> confirms step 4:
$\paren{L(1 + h) - L(1 - h)}/2h = -0.0481$ for $h = 10^{-5}$.

## Cost

- **Implicit differentiation:** one training run to (near) convergence, plus one linear solve with the Hessian, or $k$
  Hessian-vector products for a $k$-term approximation. The cost does not grow with $D$.
- **Reverse-mode unrolling:** about two to three times the cost of training, and memory for $T$ copies of the
  parameters unless the trajectory is reversed or recomputed (see <NoteLink to="gradient-checkpointing">gradient
  checkpointing</NoteLink>).
- **Forward-mode unrolling:** about $D$ times the cost of training, with memory $O(pD)$.

Every outer step retrains, so a run of $S$ outer steps costs $S$ trainings. Warm-starting each training from the
previous $\wvec^\star$ cuts this sharply when $\lambdavec$ changes little between steps.

## Trade-offs

- **Many hyperparameters.** Gradients scale to thousands or millions of continuous hyperparameters, where black-box
  search cannot <Cite id="lorraine2020" />. With one or two hyperparameters, a grid or
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> is simpler and finds the global minimum more
  reliably.
- **Continuous, differentiable hyperparameters only.** The number of layers, the choice of optimiser and a tree's
  depth have no gradient. Relaxing such choices to continuous weights is the route taken by differentiable
  <NoteLink to="neural-architecture-search">neural architecture search</NoteLink>.
- **Local minima.** $L(\lambdavec)$ is not convex in general, even for ridge regression. Descent finds the minimum
  downhill from its start, so starts are often chosen by a coarse search.
- **The evidence as an alternative.** For a Bayesian model, the
  <NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> can be differentiated in
  the hyperparameters with no validation data at all (see <NoteLink to="empirical-bayes-for-hyperparameters">empirical
  Bayes for hyperparameters</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">Gaussian-process
  hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>).
- **Meta-learning.** The same bilevel calculus, with an initialisation in place of $\lambdavec$, gives MAML and implicit
  MAML (see <NoteLink to="meta-learning">meta-learning</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Overfitting the validation set.** With $D$ hyperparameters tuned on $m$ validation points, the validation set has
  become a training set. Lorraine et al. tuned a separate <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss> for each of
  the more than 50 million parameters of AlexNet on 50 validation examples, reached 100% validation accuracy, and saw
  much lower test accuracy <Cite id="lorraine2020" />. Report the final error on data the hyperparameters never saw (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
- **An inexact inner solution.** The implicit formula assumes $\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{tr} = \zeros$. Stopping training
  early makes the hypergradient wrong by an amount that grows with the remaining training gradient; HOAG controls this
  by tightening the tolerance as it goes <Cite id="pedregosa2016" />.
- **A singular Hessian.** Without a penalty, or with more parameters than data, $\Hmat$ can be singular and
  $\wvec^\star$ is not a function of $\lambdavec$. A small fixed penalty or damping, $\Hmat + \epsilon\Imat$, restores a
  solve.
- **Short-horizon bias.** A hypergradient through a truncated unroll measures the effect of $\lambdavec$ over the last
  few steps only. It can favour a learning rate that helps now and hurts later, the same greediness that affects
  <NoteLink to="population-based-training">population-based training</NoteLink>.
- **Scale of the step.** The hypergradient has the units of the loss divided by those of $\lambdavec$. Descending on
  $\ln\lambda$ and scaling the step by the size of the loss keeps one step size usable across problems.
