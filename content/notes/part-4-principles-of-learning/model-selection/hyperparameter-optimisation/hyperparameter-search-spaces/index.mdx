<Definition>
A **hyperparameter search space** $\Lambda$ is the set of configurations $\lambdavec = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_D)$
that a <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink> may try. It states, for each of the $D$
hyperparameters, a **type** (real, integer, categorical or boolean), a **range**, a **scale** (linear or logarithmic)
and a **condition**: the values of other hyperparameters under which it applies. Most searches also put a distribution
on $\Lambda$, usually uniform on the chosen scales, from which random trials are drawn and which Bayesian optimisation
treats as its domain. The search minimises a validation loss $L(\lambdavec)$ over $\Lambda$ and can return nothing
outside it.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The search space is where prior knowledge enters a search. A search algorithm decides the order in which configurations
are tried. The search space decides which configurations can be tried at all, and how much of the budget each region
receives.

Three mistakes in the space cost more than any choice of algorithm.
- **A range that excludes the optimum.** The search returns the best configuration on the boundary, and a finer search
  does not help.
- **A linear scale for a quantity that acts multiplicatively.** A <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>
  drawn uniformly from $[10^{-5}, 1]$ lands above $0.1$ in 90% of trials, and below $10^{-3}$ in 0.1% of them.
- **A flat space for a conditional one.** Varying a polynomial kernel's degree while the kernel is linear produces
  duplicate trials that change nothing.

The space also sets how much the search can overfit. Each extra hyperparameter, and each extra decade of range, adds
candidates whose validation scores the search compares. The best of more noisy scores is more optimistic (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).

## Details

### Types of hyperparameter

- **Real.** The learning rate $\eta$, a penalty strength, a dropout probability $p \in [0, 1)$, a kernel width.
- **Integer.** The number of layers, a tree depth, the number $k$ of neighbours, the number of trees.
- **Categorical.** The optimiser (<Gloss name="sgd" /> or Adam), the kernel (linear, RBF or polynomial), the activation
  function. The values have no order, so a search must not treat "polynomial" as lying between "linear" and "RBF".
- **Boolean.** Whether to standardise the inputs, whether to use <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch
  normalisation</NoteLink>.
- **Conditional.** A hyperparameter that exists only for some values of another: momentum only for SGD, the width
  $\gamma$ of an <Gloss name="rbf-kernel" /> only when the kernel is RBF, the degree only for a polynomial kernel.

Conditional hyperparameters make the space a tree. Its root holds the unconditional choices, and each branch adds the
hyperparameters that its choice brings with it. Bergstra et al. called these **tree-structured** spaces and designed the
<NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">tree-structured Parzen estimator</NoteLink> to search them
<Cite id="bergstra2011" />.

### Log scales

A hyperparameter acts **multiplicatively** when doubling it has a similar effect wherever it starts. Penalty strengths,
learning rates, the SVM's $C$ and $\gamma$, and the number of hidden units behave this way over several orders of
magnitude. Such a hyperparameter is searched on a log scale: the search works with $\ln\lambda$, and a random trial
draws $\ln\lambda$ uniformly.

The **log-uniform** distribution on $[a, b]$, with $0 < a < b$, has density

$$
p(\lambda) = \frac{1}{\lambda\ln(b/a)}, \qquad a \le \lambda \le b.
$$

Each decade receives the same probability. For any $c$ with $a \le c$ and $10c \le b$,

$$
\prob(c \le \lambda \le 10c) = \int_c^{10c}\frac{d\lambda}{\lambda\ln(b/a)} = \frac{\ln 10}{\ln(b/a)},
$$

which does not depend on $c$. The uniform distribution on $[a, b]$ gives the decade $[c, 10c]$ probability
$9c/(b - a)$, which grows tenfold with each decade. Over $[10^{-5}, 1]$, the log-uniform distribution gives each of the
five decades probability $0.2$, while the uniform one gives the top decade $0.9/(1 - 10^{-5}) \approx 0.90$.

Hyperparameters close to an upper limit are searched on the log scale of their distance from it. A momentum or Adam
decay rate $\beta \in [0.9, 0.9999]$ is searched as $1 - \beta \in [10^{-4}, 0.1]$ on a log scale. The averaging
horizon of such a rate is about $1/(1 - \beta)$ steps, so this scale spreads trials evenly over horizons from 10 to
10,000 steps.

### Encoding the space in a unit cube

Search libraries map $\Lambda$ to the unit cube $[0, 1]^m$, search there, and map back.
- A real hyperparameter on $[a, b]$ maps to $u = (\lambda - a)/(b - a)$ on a linear scale and to
  $u = (\ln\lambda - \ln a)/(\ln b - \ln a)$ on a log scale.
- An integer or categorical hyperparameter with $r$ values maps to $r$ equal bins of $[0, 1]$. Surrogate models such as
  a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> often use a one-hot code instead, so that no order
  between the values is implied (see <NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">categorical encoding</NoteLink>).
- An inactive conditional hyperparameter takes a fixed default, so that it does not affect the surrogate.

Uniform draws in the cube then decode to log-uniform draws on log scales and to uniform draws over the values of an
integer or a category. <NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="quasi-random-search">quasi-random search</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> all work in these coordinates.

### Combined algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimisation

The choice of learning algorithm can itself be the root of a conditional space. Let $A^{(1)}, \dots, A^{(J)}$ be
learning algorithms with hyperparameter spaces $\Lambda^{(1)}, \dots, \Lambda^{(J)}$. The <Gloss name="cash" /> problem
is

$$
\paren{j^\star, \lambdavec^\star} = \argmin_{j \in \set{1, \dots, J},\ \lambdavec \in \Lambda^{(j)}} L_j(\lambdavec),
$$

where $L_j(\lambdavec)$ is the <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> loss of algorithm $A^{(j)}$
with hyperparameters $\lambdavec$ <Cite id="thornton2013" />. Written as one space, its root is a categorical
hyperparameter with $J$ values, and $\Lambda^{(j)}$ hangs below value $j$. Auto-WEKA solved this problem for the
classifiers of the WEKA library with <NoteLink to="sequential-model-based-algorithm-configuration">sequential
model-based algorithm configuration</NoteLink> <Cite id="thornton2013" />, and
<NoteLink to="automated-machine-learning">automated machine learning</NoteLink> systems extend the root to whole
pipelines <Cite id="hutter2019automl" />.

### Coupled hyperparameters and reparameterisation

Two hyperparameters are **coupled** when the good values of one depend on the other. A search on axes aligned with the
original hyperparameters then needs many points to trace a diagonal valley. A
change of coordinates can align the valley with an axis.

The RBF <Gloss name="svm" /> is the standard case. Keerthi and Lin showed that as $\gamma \to 0$ with $C\gamma$ held
fixed, the RBF SVM approaches a linear SVM whose penalty is set by $C\gamma$ <Cite id="keerthi2003" />. Good settings
then lie along lines of constant $C\gamma$. In the tabulated SVM error used in this chapter's figures, every setting
within $0.002$ of the lowest error lies on the line $\log_{10}C + \log_{10}\gamma = -1$. Searching over $\log_{10}C$ and
$\log_{10}(C\gamma)$ puts that valley along one axis.

Deep learning has coupled pairs too. The best learning rate grows with the batch size, and the effect of
<Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss> in SGD depends on the product of decay and learning rate.
<NoteLink to="tuning-deep-learning-hyperparameters">Tuning deep-learning hyperparameters</NoteLink> treats these.

## Worked example

**Linear against log scale.** The penalty $C$ of an SVM is searched over $[10^{-2}, 10^3]$, five decades. At
$\gamma = 10^{-2}$ the error is lowest at $C = 10$.

1. **Uniform on $[10^{-2}, 10^3]$.** The probability of a trial below $C = 10$ is
   $(10 - 0.01)/(1000 - 0.01) = 0.0100$.
2. **Log-uniform.** The same probability is the fraction of decades below 10: $(\log_{10}10 - \log_{10}0.01)/5 = 3/5 =
   0.6$.
3. **Twenty trials.** Uniform sampling puts $20 \times 0.01 = 0.2$ trials below $C = 10$ on average, and puts none there
   with probability $0.99^{20} = 0.82$. Log-uniform sampling puts 12 there on average.

Four searches in five on the linear scale never test a value of $C$ on the lower side of the optimum.

**A conditional space.** An SVM search has the penalty $C$ on a log scale, a kernel in $\set{\text{linear},
\text{RBF}, \text{polynomial}}$, a width $\gamma$ for the RBF and polynomial kernels, and a degree in $\set{2, 3, 4}$
for the polynomial kernel. A grid takes 5 values of each real hyperparameter.

1. **As a tree.** The linear kernel gives 5 configurations (one per $C$). The RBF kernel gives $5 \times 5 = 25$. The
   polynomial kernel gives $5 \times 5 \times 3 = 75$. The tree has $5 + 25 + 75 = 105$ configurations.
2. **As a flat product.** Every hyperparameter varies everywhere: $5 \times 3 \times 5 \times 3 = 225$ trials.
3. **Duplicates.** Under the linear kernel the flat grid runs each value of $C$ with 15 settings of $\gamma$ and the
   degree that the model ignores, so 75 trials give 5 distinct models. Under the RBF kernel 75 trials give 25. The flat
   grid spends $225 - 105 = 120$ trials, more than half its budget, on duplicates.

**Encoding a point.** The configuration $C = 10$, RBF kernel, $\gamma = 0.01$ in the tree-structured space encodes to
the unit cube as follows. The penalty gives $u_C = (\log_{10}10 + 2)/5 = 0.6$. The kernel's three bins are
$[0, \tfrac13)$, $[\tfrac13, \tfrac23)$ and $[\tfrac23, 1]$, and the RBF kernel takes the centre of the second, $0.5$.
The width, searched over $[10^{-3}, 10^2]$, gives $u_\gamma = (\log_{10}0.01 + 3)/5 = 0.2$.

## Pitfalls

- **An optimum on the boundary.** If the best trials sit at the edge of a range, the range is too narrow. The Deep
  Learning Tuning Playbook calls a search space suspicious when its best point lies close to the boundary, and widens
  the range in that dimension <Cite id="godbole2023" />.
- **Scales that depend on the data.** The useful range of $\gamma$ depends on the scale of the inputs, so the inputs are
  standardised before the range is fixed. A penalty on a loss summed over the $n$ examples has an optimum about $n$
  times larger than the same penalty on the mean loss, so a range copied between libraries or data sizes can miss.
- **Integers by rounding a real.** Rounding a uniform draw on $[1, 4]$ to the nearest integer gives 1 and 4 half the
  probability of 2 and 3. Draw integers uniformly over their values, or round a draw on $[0.5, 4.5]$.
- **Order imposed on categories.** Encoding a category as an integer and searching it with a model that assumes
  smoothness implies that neighbouring codes give similar losses.
- **A space chosen after looking at the test set.** A range narrowed to where earlier models scored well on the test
  set has used the test set for selection. The test score of the final model is then optimistic (see
  <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>).
- **More hyperparameters than the data can choose between.** Random search found that only a few hyperparameters
  mattered on each dataset <Cite id="bergstra2012" />. Every extra hyperparameter adds noise to the comparison without
  adding much accuracy, which raises the optimism of the best score.
