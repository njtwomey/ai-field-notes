<Definition>
A **hyperparameter** is a setting of a learning procedure that is fixed before training, such as a
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>, a penalty strength or a tree depth. **Hyperparameter optimisation**
chooses a configuration $\lambdavec$ from a search space $\Lambda$ to minimise a validation loss $L(\lambdavec)$,
measured by training with $\lambdavec$ and scoring on held-out data or by
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>. Each evaluation of $L$ is expensive and noisy, and usually
has no usable <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> <Cite id="hutter2019automl" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Training a model once may take minutes or days, and a learner may have ten or more hyperparameters. Trying every
combination is impossible. The search has to decide which configurations to try, in what order, and how much training
to spend on each before judging it. Hyperparameter optimisation answers the chapter's second question, which
candidate to choose, when the candidates form a large or continuous space.

Practice today runs in stages. <NoteLink to="random-search">Random</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="quasi-random-search">quasi-random search</NoteLink> comes first, while the ranges are still being learned.
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> takes the final study, and multi-fidelity early
stopping cuts the cost when trainings are long <Cite id="godbole2023" />. Libraries such as scikit-learn's
`GridSearchCV`, Optuna and Ray Tune implement these strategies behind one interface <Cite id="akiba2019" />.

## Main ideas

**The search space comes first.** A <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink> gives each
hyperparameter a type, a range, a scale and the conditions under which it applies. The scale often matters more than
the search algorithm: a learning rate drawn uniformly from $[10^{-5}, 1]$ spends 90% of its trials above $0.1$, so
scale parameters are searched on a logarithmic scale. Choosing the learning algorithm and its hyperparameters together
is the CASH problem.

**Designs that ignore earlier results.** <NoteLink to="grid-search">Grid search</NoteLink> scores every combination of a
few values per hyperparameter, at a cost equal to the product of the numbers of values. It tests only as many distinct
values of each hyperparameter as its axis has. <NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> draws trials
independently and tests a new value of every hyperparameter in each trial, which wins when few hyperparameters matter
<Cite id="bergstra2012" />. Sixty random trials land in the best 5% of the space with probability
$1 - 0.95^{60} \approx 0.95$, whatever its dimension. Quasi-random designs such as Sobol sequences cover the space more
evenly still. All three run in parallel and can be stopped at any time.

**Searches that learn from earlier results.** <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> fits
a surrogate of $L$, usually a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink>, to all trials so far and
chooses the next trial by an <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition function</NoteLink>
<Cite id="jones1998" /> <Cite id="snoek2012" />. With surrogate mean $\mu$, standard deviation $\sigma$ and best value
$f^\star$ so far, <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected improvement</NoteLink> is
$(f^\star - \mu)\Phi(z) + \sigma\varphi(z)$ with $z = (f^\star - \mu)/\sigma$: large where the mean is low or the
uncertainty high. It pays when trainings are expensive, the hyperparameters number fewer than about 20 and the budget is
tens to hundreds of trials.

**Spending less on poor trials.** Most bad configurations are visibly bad early in training.
<NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-hyperparameter-optimisation">Multi-fidelity methods</NoteLink> judge configurations on few
epochs or a subset of the data and train only the survivors fully. <NoteLink to="successive-halving">Successive
halving</NoteLink> keeps the best $1/\eta$ at each rung and multiplies their budget by $\eta$. With 27 configurations,
$\eta = 3$ and a starting budget of one epoch, it spends 108 epochs against 729 for full training of all 27
<Cite id="jamieson2016" />. <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> hedges over how aggressive to be
<Cite id="li2018hyperband" />. These methods assume that early rankings persist, and they discard slow starters such as
small learning rates or strong regularisation.

**Gradients and populations.** For continuous hyperparameters,
<NoteLink to="gradient-based-hyperparameter-optimisation">gradient-based hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink>
differentiates the validation loss through training, by the implicit function theorem or by unrolling the optimiser, at
about one extra linear solve per hypergradient. <NoteLink to="population-based-training">Population-based
training</NoteLink> trains many models in parallel and replaces weak members with perturbed copies of strong ones, so
the search returns a schedule of hyperparameters within the budget of one parallel search <Cite id="jaderberg2017" />.

**The search reuses the validation data.** The best of many validation scores is an optimistic estimate of the chosen
configuration's error, and the optimism grows with the number of trials
(<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Bayesian optimisation
sharpens the effect by concentrating trials where earlier scores were low, including where they were low by chance. The
error of the result needs data the search never saw: a test set, or <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
cross-validation</NoteLink> with the whole search inside each outer fold <Cite id="cawley2010" />. Comparing two methods
tuned with unequal budgets measures the tuning effort, not the methods
(<NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink>).
