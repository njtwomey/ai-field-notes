<Definition>
Let $L(\lambdavec)$ be the validation loss of a learning procedure run with hyperparameters
$\lambdavec = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$ from a <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink>
$\Lambda = \Lambda_1 \times \dots \times \Lambda_d$. **Hyperparameter importance** is a measure of how much of the
variation of $L$ is due to each hyperparameter $\lambda_j$, or to each group of hyperparameters acting together. Three
measures are in common use. **Functional <Gloss name="anova" />** (fANOVA) decomposes the variance of $L$ over $\Lambda$
into a part for every subset of the hyperparameters <Cite id="hutter2014" />. **Ablation** measures the loss gained by
switching each hyperparameter from a default value to a tuned value <Cite id="fawcett2016ablation" />. **Tunability**
measures the loss gained by tuning a hyperparameter while the others stay at their defaults
<Cite id="probst2019tunability" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A search space of ten hyperparameters is usually dominated by two or three of them. Bergstra and Bengio found that the
validation loss of neural networks depends strongly on few hyperparameters and that which few varies from data set to
data set, which is why <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink> beats grid search
<Cite id="bergstra2012" />. Knowing which hyperparameters matter pays three ways. A later search can fix the unimportant
ones and spend its budget on the rest. A default can be chosen for a hyperparameter that hardly moves the loss. And the
importance explains the learner: a support vector machine whose error depends mostly on the kernel width $\gamma$ is
limited by its kernel, not its penalty.

Importance is a property of a function, the loss surface $L(\lambdavec)$, and of the region over which the function is
examined. A hyperparameter can be decisive over a wide range and irrelevant over the narrow range where the good
settings lie. Every measure below therefore states a reference region or a reference configuration.

The surface is not observed directly. A tuning run evaluates $L$ at $N$ configurations, each evaluation noisy and
expensive. Practical importance analysis fits a surrogate model to those evaluations and measures importance on the
surrogate.

## Functional ANOVA

Functional ANOVA writes $L$ as a sum of a constant, one function of each hyperparameter, one function of each pair, and
so on up to one function of all $d$ together. The variance of $L$ then splits into one share per term, and the share of
a term is its importance.

### The decomposition

Put a product weighting $w(\lambdavec) = \prod_j w_j(\lambda_j)$ on $\Lambda$: each $w_j$ is a probability density, or
a set of grid weights, over the range of $\lambda_j$. For a subset $U \subseteq \{1, \dots, d\}$, write $\lambdavec_U$
for the hyperparameters in $U$, and define the **marginal** $m_U(\lambdavec_U) = \expect[L \mid \lambdavec_U]$: the
loss averaged under $w$ over the hyperparameters outside $U$, with those in $U$ held fixed. The marginal of the empty
set is the overall mean $\mu = \expect L$. The terms are defined from the smallest subsets upwards:

$$
f_\emptyset = \mu, \qquad f_U(\lambdavec_U) = m_U(\lambdavec_U) - \sum_{V \subsetneq U} f_V(\lambdavec_V).
$$

A main effect is a marginal minus the mean, $f_{\{j\}}(\lambda_j) = m_{\{j\}}(\lambda_j) - \mu$. A pair term is what the
marginal of the pair adds to the two main effects:

$$
f_{\{j,k\}}(\lambda_j, \lambda_k) = m_{\{j,k\}}(\lambda_j, \lambda_k) - m_{\{j\}}(\lambda_j) - m_{\{k\}}(\lambda_k) + \mu.
$$

The definition with $U = \{1, \dots, d\}$ gives $m_U = L$, so the terms add back to the loss exactly:
$L(\lambdavec) = \sum_{U} f_U(\lambdavec_U)$.

### The variance splits

The terms are orthogonal under $w$. The key property is that each term with $U$ non-empty averages to zero over any one
of its own hyperparameters: for $j \in U$, averaging $f_U$ over $\lambda_j$ with the others fixed gives 0. The proof is
an induction on the size of $U$ and is given below.

Take two different subsets $U \ne V$, and pick a hyperparameter $j$ that is in one but not the other, say $j \in U$ and
$j \notin V$. The term $f_V$ does not depend on $\lambda_j$, and under the product weighting $\lambda_j$ is independent
of the rest. Averaging over $\lambda_j$ first,

$$
\expect\brack{f_U f_V} = \expect\brack{f_V \cdot \expect_{\lambda_j}\brack{f_U}} = \expect\brack{f_V \cdot 0} = 0.
$$

Every non-empty term also has mean zero, by the same property. Subtract $\mu$ from both sides of the decomposition,
square and take the expectation. The cross terms vanish, and

$$
\var L = \sum_{U \ne \emptyset} \var f_U, \qquad \text{importance of } U = \frac{\var f_U}{\var L}.
$$

The importances of all non-empty subsets sum to 1. The importance of a single hyperparameter is the fraction of the
variance of the loss that its main effect explains; the importance of a pair is the fraction that the two explain
together beyond their main effects.

<Derivation title="Each term averages to zero over its own hyperparameters">
Claim: for every non-empty $U$ and every $j \in U$, $\expect_{\lambda_j}[f_U] = 0$, where $\expect_{\lambda_j}$
averages over $\lambda_j$ under $w_j$ with the other hyperparameters fixed.

Under the product weighting, averaging the marginal $m_U$ over $\lambda_j$ gives the marginal of the smaller set:
$\expect_{\lambda_j}[m_U] = m_{U \setminus \{j\}}$. This is the tower property of conditional expectation, and it
needs $\lambda_j$ to be independent of the other hyperparameters, which the product form guarantees.

Assume the claim for every subset smaller than $U$. Average the definition of $f_U$ over $\lambda_j$:

$$
\expect_{\lambda_j}[f_U] = m_{U \setminus \{j\}} - \sum_{V \subsetneq U,\ j \notin V} f_V - \sum_{V \subsetneq U,\ j
\in V} \expect_{\lambda_j}[f_V].
$$

The terms with $j \notin V$ do not depend on $\lambda_j$ and pass through the average. The terms with $j \in V$ are
smaller than $U$, so by the induction hypothesis they average to 0. The subsets $V \subsetneq U$ with $j \notin V$ are
exactly the subsets of $U \setminus \{j\}$, so

$$
\expect_{\lambda_j}[f_U] = m_{U \setminus \{j\}} - \sum_{V \subseteq U \setminus \{j\}} f_V = m_{U \setminus \{j\}} -
m_{U \setminus \{j\}} = 0,
$$

where the last step is the definition of $f_{U \setminus \{j\}}$ rearranged. The base case $U = \{j\}$ is
$\expect_{\lambda_j}[m_{\{j\}} - \mu] = \mu - \mu = 0$.
</Derivation>

### From trials to a surrogate

A tuning run does not give $L$ on a grid. It gives $N$ pairs $(\lambdavec_i, L(\lambdavec_i))$, scattered where the
search went. Hutter, Hoos and Leyton-Brown fit a <NoteLink to="random-forest">random forest</NoteLink> to the pairs and
apply the decomposition to the forest's prediction instead of to $L$ <Cite id="hutter2014" />. A regression tree is
piecewise constant on axis-aligned boxes, so its marginal over any subset of hyperparameters is a weighted sum over its
leaves. They give an algorithm that computes the marginals of a forest in time linear in the size of its trees. The
importances are then those of the surrogate, and they are only as accurate as the surrogate is.

Their findings set the pattern for later work. On the perplexity of online latent Dirichlet allocation, the batch size
explained 65% of the variance and its interaction with $\kappa$, which controls how quickly old information is
forgotten, another 18%. Across the several hundred hyperparameters of the Auto-WEKA space and on combinatorial solvers,
a few hyperparameters accounted for most of the variation <Cite id="hutter2014" />. Van Rijn and Hutter ran the analysis
on 100 data sets from OpenML. For RBF <Gloss name="svm">support vector machines</Gloss> the kernel width $\gamma$ was
most important, then the penalty $C$; for random forests, the minimum number of samples per leaf and the fraction of
features tried at each split; for <Gloss name="adaboost" />, the depth of the trees and, less, the
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> <Cite id="vanrijn2018importance" />.

### Importance in the good region

The variance over the whole space is dominated by the worst settings. A region where training diverges or the model
predicts a constant contributes a large share of $\var L$, and the hyperparameter that controls entry to that region
looks important even though every tuned configuration avoids it. Hutter, Hoos and Leyton-Brown measure importance among
good settings by capping the loss before the analysis: every value above a threshold $y_{\max}$, such as the 25%
quantile of the observed losses or the loss of the default, is replaced by $y_{\max}$ <Cite id="hutter2014" />. All
remaining variation then comes from regions better than the threshold. With this cap their single main effects still
explained a sizeable share of the variance, but less than over the whole space.

## Ablation

Ablation answers a narrower question: a tuned configuration $\lambdavec^\star$ beats a default $\lambdavec^0$, so
which changes produce the gain? Fawcett and Hoos build an **ablation path** from $\lambdavec^0$ to $\lambdavec^\star$
<Cite id="fawcett2016ablation" />.

1. Start at the default. Let $D$ be the set of hyperparameters on which the current configuration and
   $\lambdavec^\star$ differ.
2. For each $j \in D$, evaluate the configuration with $\lambda_j$ switched to its tuned value.
3. Keep the switch that lowers the loss most, record the gain, and remove $j$ from $D$.
4. Repeat until the configuration equals $\lambdavec^\star$.

The order of the path ranks the hyperparameters, and the recorded gains say how much each contributed. A path over
$\abs{D} = k$ differing hyperparameters needs $k + (k - 1) + \dots + 1 = k(k + 1)/2$ evaluations. Conditional
hyperparameters are switched together with the hyperparameter that activates them.

Ablation is local: it explains one pair of configurations, and the gain of a switch depends on the switches made before
it. Each evaluation is a full training run, so Biedenkapp et al. replace the evaluations with predictions from a
surrogate fitted to the tuning run's data, which made ablation 33 to 14 727 times faster across their scenarios
<Cite id="biedenkapp2017ablation" />.

**Local sensitivity** is the simplest local measure. Starting from the incumbent $\lambdavec^\star$, vary one
hyperparameter at a time over its range, with the others fixed, and record how much the loss changes. It shows how
flat the optimum is along each axis, which tells a user how precisely each hyperparameter must be set. It ignores
interactions, because no two hyperparameters move together.

## Tunability

Tunability asks how much is gained by tuning at all <Cite id="probst2019tunability" />. Let $R^{(t)}(\lambdavec)$ be
the risk of a configuration on data set $t$, $\lambdavec^0$ a reference default, and $\lambdavec^{(t)\star}$ the best
configuration on data set $t$. The tunability of the algorithm on data set $t$ is

$$
d^{(t)} = R^{(t)}(\lambdavec^0) - R^{(t)}(\lambdavec^{(t)\star}).
$$

The tunability of hyperparameter $j$ is the same difference when only $\lambda_j$ is tuned and the others stay at their
defaults. Averages over data sets summarise both.

Probst, Boulesteix and Bischl measured tunability on 38 OpenML data sets with the <Gloss name="auc">AUC</Gloss>,
relative to defaults that they optimised across the data sets. The mean tunability was 0.042 for support vector
machines, 0.024 for elastic-net <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, 0.014 for
<Gloss name="xgboost" />, 0.012 for CART trees and 0.006 for both $k$-nearest neighbours and random forests. For the
support vector machine the kernel, $\gamma$ and the polynomial degree were the most tunable hyperparameters and the cost
$C$ was not; for random forests, the number of features tried at each split <Cite id="probst2019tunability" />.

The three measures answer different questions. Functional ANOVA describes the whole surface over a region. Ablation
explains one improvement. Tunability prices the decision to tune.

## Worked example

Take nine cells of the SVM surface in the figure below: the cross-validated error, in percent, at
$\log_{10} C \in \{-2, 0.5, 3\}$ and $\log_{10}\gamma \in \{-3, -1.5, 0\}$, with equal weights on each axis.

| $\log_{10} C$ \ $\log_{10}\gamma$ | $-3$ | $-1.5$ | $0$ | marginal $m_C$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $-2$ | 12.8 | 9.9 | 7.8 | 10.17 |
| $0.5$ | 8.0 | 4.1 | 6.8 | 6.30 |
| $3$ | 5.9 | 8.0 | 7.2 | 7.03 |
| marginal $m_\gamma$ | 8.90 | 7.33 | 7.27 | $\mu = 7.83$ |

1. **Mean.** The nine values sum to 70.5, so $\mu = 70.5/9 = 7.83$.
2. **Marginals.** Each row mean is $m_C$ and each column mean is $m_\gamma$, as in the table margins.
3. **Main effects.** $f_C = m_C - \mu = (2.33, -1.53, -0.80)$ and $f_\gamma = m_\gamma - \mu = (1.07, -0.50, -0.57)$.
4. **Their variances.** With equal weights, $\var f_C = (2.33^2 + 1.53^2 + 0.80^2)/3 = 8.44/3 = 2.81$ and
   $\var f_\gamma = (1.07^2 + 0.50^2 + 0.57^2)/3 = 1.71/3 = 0.57$.
5. **Total variance.** The squared deviations of the nine values from 7.83 sum to 48.14, so $\var L = 48.14/9 = 5.35$.
6. **Interaction.** The interaction takes the rest: $\var f_{C\gamma} = 5.35 - 2.81 - 0.57 = 1.97$. At the cell
   $(\log_{10} C, \log_{10}\gamma) = (0.5, -1.5)$ it is $4.1 - 6.30 - 7.33 + 7.83 = -1.70$: the pair does 1.7 points
   better than the sum of its main effects predicts.
7. **Importances.** $C$ explains $2.81/5.35 = 53\%$, $\gamma$ explains $0.57/5.35 = 11\%$ and the interaction 37%.

On these nine cells $C$ is the most important hyperparameter. On the full $21 \times 21$ grid the same computation
gives $\gamma$ 95%, $C$ 2.5% and the interaction 2.5%, because the full grid includes the cliff at
$\log_{10}\gamma \ge 1.5$, where the error exceeds 38% whatever $C$ is. Restricting $\log_{10}\gamma$ to
$[-3, 0]$ gives $C$ 40%, $\gamma$ 7% and the interaction 53%. Capping the full grid at its 25% quantile, an error of
6.5%, gives $C$ 14%, $\gamma$ 24% and the interaction 62%. The good settings lie along a diagonal valley in which a
larger $C$ calls for a smaller $\gamma$, and a diagonal valley is an interaction.

## Uses

- **Shrinking the search space.** Fix the hyperparameters with negligible main effects and interactions at good
  values, and search the rest. Hutter, Hoos and Leyton-Brown found that configuring only the most important
  hyperparameters often gave good results, and sometimes faster than searching the full space
  <Cite id="hutter2014" />.
- **Priors for the next search.** Van Rijn and Hutter turned the distributions of good values across data sets into
  priors for random search and obtained significant speed-ups <Cite id="vanrijn2018importance" />; see
  <NoteLink to="warm-starting-hyperparameter-optimisation">warm-starting hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink>.
- **Defaults.** A hyperparameter with low tunability can be left at a good default.
- **Explaining a tuned model.** Ablation tells a reader which of the tuned changes did the work.

## Pitfalls

- **Importance is relative to the range.** Widening a hyperparameter's range into a region of failure makes it more
  important without changing the good settings. Report the region, and consider capping the loss.
- **Interactions do not belong to either hyperparameter.** A large pair term means that neither hyperparameter can be
  tuned alone. A search that fixes one of them at a value good on average can miss the valley.
- **The surrogate is a model.** Importances computed from a random forest fitted to 50 noisy trials carry the forest's
  error. Trials gathered by an adaptive search cluster near the optimum, so the surrogate is poor far from it, which is
  exactly where the variance of the whole space comes from.
- **Dependent hyperparameters break the decomposition.** The variance split needs a product weighting. Conditional
  hyperparameters, and constraints such as a minimum split size that must not exceed a leaf size, make the natural
  weighting non-product, and the shares then depend on how the dependent region is handled.
- **The data are reused.** Importances computed from the same tuning run that chose the configuration describe that
  run's validation set. A search space pruned with them and then searched again on the same validation data inherits
  the optimism of the first search (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model
  selection</NoteLink>). Estimate importance on earlier data sets, or keep the final assessment on fresh data.
- **Importance is not where the optimum is.** A hyperparameter can explain little variance and still have a sharp
  optimum that matters for the last percentage point.
