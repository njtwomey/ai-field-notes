<Definition>
**Tuning a deep network** means choosing the hyperparameters of its training run by validation performance: the
<Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> and its schedule, the optimiser's other constants, the
<NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink> and number of steps, and the strength of each
regulariser. Each trial is a full training run, so the budget is tens of trials, not thousands. Current practice tunes
in rounds. It fixes the model, the optimiser and the training budget, sweeps the learning rate on a logarithmic scale
first, then tunes the few hyperparameters that interact with it, and repeats promising settings over several random
seeds <Cite id="godbole2023" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The learning rate sets the size of every update. Set it ten times too large and training diverges; ten times too small
and training crawls and stops far from a good solution within the step budget. Every other hyperparameter's effect is
visible only once the learning rate is in the right range: a better <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>
cannot be detected in a run that diverged. Bengio called the initial learning rate "often the single most important
hyper-parameter", to be tuned to within about a factor of 2 <Cite id="bengio2012practical" />.

The other hyperparameters fall into groups by how they interact with it. The batch size, momentum and schedule change
the effective step size, so the best learning rate moves when they move. Regularisers such as weight decay and dropout
interact with it more weakly. Architecture sizes change the best learning rate unless the network is parameterised to
prevent it (see <NoteLink to="maximal-update-parameterisation">maximal update parameterisation</NoteLink>). The order
of tuning follows the strength of these interactions.

Tuning a network is also a selection among noisy candidates. Two runs with different seeds differ in their initial
weights and in the order of the minibatches, and their validation scores differ by an amount that can be as large as
the effect being tuned. The best of many runs is then partly the luckiest.

## Which hyperparameters, in which order

Godbole et al. classify hyperparameters by their role in an experiment <Cite id="godbole2023" />.
- **Scientific** hyperparameters are those whose effect the experiment measures, such as the number of layers.
- **Nuisance** hyperparameters must be re-tuned for each value of the scientific ones before the comparison is fair.
  The learning rate is almost always a nuisance hyperparameter: comparing two depths at one shared learning rate
  compares the depths and how well that rate suits each.
- **Fixed** hyperparameters are held constant, which saves trials and restricts the conclusion to that value.

For Adam they recommend tuning only the base learning rate with fewer than 10 trials; the learning rate and $\beta_1$
with 10 to 25; the learning rate, $\beta_1$ and $\epsilon$ with more than 25; and $\beta_2$ as well only with
substantially more <Cite id="godbole2023" />. Choi et al. showed how much this matters. Comparisons between optimisers
reversed when the <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search spaces</NoteLink> of their hyperparameters changed,
and with enough tuning a more general optimiser, such as <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> against momentum, never did
worse <Cite id="choi2019optimizers" />.

A working order:
1. **Fix the recipe.** Take a published architecture, optimiser and schedule for a similar problem, and choose the
   number of training steps the budget allows.
2. **Choose the batch size for throughput,** as large as the hardware supports while the time per step stays nearly
   constant. The batch size is not a hyperparameter to tune for validation performance: with the learning rate and
   <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> re-tuned, the same final performance is reachable at any
   batch size given enough steps <Cite id="godbole2023" /> <Cite id="shallue2019" />.
3. **Sweep the learning rate** on a logarithmic grid, with warmup and decay in place.
4. **Tune its partners** jointly with it: momentum or $\beta_1$, weight decay, the warmup length.
5. **Tune regularisation** (dropout, label smoothing, augmentation strength) once the model fits well.
6. **Confirm with seeds.** Rerun the best few settings with new seeds before choosing.

## The learning rate

**Sweep on a log scale.** Good learning rates span orders of magnitude across models, so a grid in
$\log_{10}\eta$ with steps of $0.5$ (a factor of about 3) or $1/3$ (a factor of about 2) covers the range evenly. A
uniform grid on $[10^{-5}, 1]$ would put 90% of its points above $0.1$. If the best value is at the edge of the grid,
the grid is extended in that direction until the best value is interior.

**The loss is asymmetric in the learning rate.** Below the optimum the validation loss rises slowly, because training
makes less progress in the fixed number of steps. Above the optimum it rises sharply, because the updates overshoot,
and beyond some rate training diverges. The best learning rate often sits close to that edge. A sweep therefore needs
finer steps on the high side than the low side, and a value slightly below the best is a safer choice than one slightly
above.

**The range test.** Smith's learning-rate range test is a one-run shortcut: train for a few epochs while the learning
rate rises linearly from very small to large, and read off the range over which the accuracy improves before it
degrades <Cite id="smith2017clr" />. It locates the scale cheaply. It does not replace a sweep, because a rate that
works for a few epochs at the start of training is not the best rate for a full schedule.

**Schedules.** The learning rate that is tuned is the peak of a schedule, usually a linear warmup followed by a decay
(see <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedules</NoteLink>). A decaying schedule is defined
relative to the total number of steps, so the best peak rate depends on the step budget. Truncated runs of a decaying
schedule are poor proxies for full runs, which limits what <NoteLink
to="multi-fidelity-hyperparameter-optimisation">multi-fidelity methods</NoteLink> can do for deep networks unless each
fidelity runs its own complete schedule.

## Batch size and the learning rate

When the batch size changes, the best learning rate moves. For <Gloss name="sgd" /> the move follows from the noise in
the gradient. Let $\gvec$ be the gradient of the training loss at the current parameters and $\Sigmab$ the covariance of
a single example's gradient. A minibatch of $B$ examples drawn at random gives a gradient estimate with mean $\gvec$
and covariance approximately $\Sigmab/B$.

**$k$ small steps.** Take $k$ steps with batch size $B$ and learning rate $\eta$. Assume that the gradient changes
little over the $k$ steps, so each step uses the same $\gvec$ with independent noise. The parameters move by
$-k\eta\gvec$ on average, and the noise of the $k$ steps adds, giving covariance $k\eta^2\Sigmab/B$.

**One large step.** Take one step with batch size $kB$ and learning rate $\eta'$. The parameters move by $-\eta'\gvec$
on average, with covariance $\eta'^2\Sigmab/(kB)$.

**Matching.** The means agree when $\eta' = k\eta$. The covariances then agree too, since
$(k\eta)^2\Sigmab/(kB) = k\eta^2\Sigmab/B$. Multiplying the batch size by $k$ and the learning rate by $k$ reproduces
$k$ small steps in one, in mean and in noise. This is the **linear scaling rule** <Cite id="goyal2017" />, and the
ratio $\eta/B$ sets the scale of the noise in SGD's trajectory <Cite id="smith2018batch" />.

The rule fails where its assumption fails.
- **Early in training** the gradient changes quickly, so $k$ small steps are not one large step. Goyal et al. needed a
  warmup of the learning rate to train with batches of 8192 images <Cite id="goyal2017" />.
- **At large batch sizes** the gradient noise is no longer what limits progress. McCandlish et al. predict the
  **critical batch size**, beyond which a larger batch stops cutting the number of steps, from the noise scale
  $B_\text{simple} = \trace\Sigmab/\norm{\gvec}^2$ <Cite id="mccandlish2018" />. Shallue et al. measured the number of
  steps to reach a target error across 35 workloads. They found three regimes as the batch size grows: steps fall in
  proportion to the batch size, then fall more slowly, then stop falling. Where the regimes begin varied greatly
  between workloads, and disagreements in the literature about large batches were largely explained by differences in
  tuning <Cite id="shallue2019" />.
- **Adaptive optimisers** normalise each step by a running gradient scale, so the linear rule does not carry over.
  Malladi et al. derive a **square-root scaling rule** for Adam and <Gloss name="rmsprop" />: multiply the batch size by
  $\kappa$, multiply the learning rate by $\sqrt\kappa$, replace $1 - \beta_1$ and $1 - \beta_2$ by $\kappa$ times their
  values, and divide $\epsilon$ by $\sqrt\kappa$ <Cite id="malladi2022sde" />.

Scaling rules give the starting point after a change of batch size. The learning rate is then re-tuned near it.

## Regularisation and the rest

**Weight decay** interacts with the learning rate through the optimiser. With Adam, an L2 penalty added to the loss is
rescaled per coordinate; decoupled weight decay (AdamW) applies a uniform shrinkage, and Loshchilov and Hutter found
that the best decay then depends much less on the learning rate, so the two can be tuned more separately
<Cite id="loshchilov2019" />; see <NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay">decoupled weight decay</NoteLink>. Libraries
differ in whether the decay is multiplied by the learning rate, so a tuned value does not transfer between them
unchanged.

**Dropout, label smoothing and augmentation** are tuned after the optimisation hyperparameters, on a model that fits the
training data well. Their best values grow with the model's capacity and shrink with the amount of data.

**Width and depth** change the best learning rate under the standard parameterisation. Under µP the optimisation
hyperparameters tuned on a narrow proxy transfer to a wide target, while regularisation hyperparameters such as dropout
and weight decay do not <Cite id="yang2021mup" />; see <NoteLink to="model-selection-at-scale">model selection at
scale</NoteLink>.

## Search algorithms and budgets

**Exploration, then exploitation.** Godbole et al. use <NoteLink to="quasi-random-search">quasi-random search</NoteLink>
while they are still learning which hyperparameters matter and over which ranges. Its trials cover the space evenly and
do not depend on earlier results, so a study can be re-analysed for a different objective without rerunning it. Once the
search space is settled, they recommend <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> for the
final study, where only the best configuration matters <Cite id="godbole2023" />.

**Boundaries.** After a study, check where the best trials lie. If they crowd against the edge of a range, the range was
too narrow and the study is repeated with it widened <Cite id="godbole2023" />.

**Seeds.** Run-to-run variation sets a floor below which tuning cannot see. Bouthillier et al. found that the variance
from data sampling, initialisation and hyperparameter choice markedly changed the results of benchmarks
<Cite id="bouthillier2021" />. Differences between settings smaller than the spread over seeds are not evidence; see
<NoteLink to="variance-in-machine-learning-benchmarks">variance in machine learning benchmarks</NoteLink>.

**The expected best of $N$ trials.** The best validation score of a search grows with the number of trials, so a score
is meaningful only with its budget. Dodge et al. report the expected best score as a function of the budget
<Cite id="dodge2019" />. If $N$ trials have independent scores $V_1, \dots, V_N$ with distribution function $F$, the
best of them is at most $v$ exactly when every one is, so

$$
\prob\paren{\max_i V_i \le v} = \prod_{i=1}^{N}\prob(V_i \le v) = F(v)^N.
$$

With $F$ replaced by the empirical distribution function $\hat F$ of the trials already run, the expected best of $n$
trials is $\sum_v v\,\brack{\hat F(v)^n - \hat F(v^-)^n}$, where the sum runs over the distinct observed values and
$\hat F(v^-)$ is the fraction of trials strictly below $v$ <Cite id="dodge2019" />. A curve of this quantity against $n$
shows how fast a model's score improves with tuning effort, which is the fair way to compare a cheaply tuned model with
an expensively tuned one (see <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

The figure trains a small network on the two-moons problem and sweeps the learning rate of Adam over
$\eta = 10^{-3.5}, 10^{-3}, \dots, 10^{0.5}$, with 3 seeds per rate, batches of 32 and 200 steps. The mean validation
cross-entropy at each rate, with the standard deviation over seeds:

| $\log_{10}\eta$ | $-3.5$ | $-3$ | $-2.5$ | $-2$ | $-1.5$ | $-1$ | $-0.5$ | $0$ | $0.5$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| mean | 0.534 | 0.344 | 0.309 | 0.233 | 0.215 | 0.246 | 0.260 | 1.210 | 2.014 |
| sd | 0.078 | 0.015 | 0.011 | 0.016 | 0.007 | 0.021 | 0.040 | 1.020 | 2.355 |

1. **The shape.** From $10^{-3.5}$ the loss falls steadily for two decades. Above the best rate it rises by 0.045 over
   the next decade, then jumps: at $\eta = 1$ the three seeds give 2.35, 0.90 and 0.38, and two of the three runs end
   worse than a constant prediction, which scores $\ln 2 = 0.69$. This is the asymmetry of the learning-rate curve.
2. **The best rate.** The lowest mean is at $\eta = 10^{-1.5} \approx 0.032$, with mean 0.215. It is interior to the
   grid, so the grid need not be extended.
3. **Is it better than its neighbours?** The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of a mean of 3 runs
   is about $\text{sd}/\sqrt 3$: about 0.004 at $10^{-1.5}$, 0.009 at $10^{-2}$ and 0.012 at $10^{-1}$. The gap to
   $10^{-2}$ is $0.233 - 0.215 = 0.018$, about twice the standard error of the difference,
   $\sqrt{0.004^2 + 0.009^2} = 0.010$. The gap to $10^{-1}$ is 0.031, about $2.4$ standard errors. The optimum is
   located to within about half a decade, which is what a half-decade grid can resolve.
4. **Refine.** A second sweep with steps of $\log_{10}\eta = 1/6$ between $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$ would locate the
   optimum to within a factor of 1.5, near Bengio's recommended factor of 2.
5. **Change the optimiser.** With SGD and momentum 0.9 the same sweep has its lowest mean, 0.202, at $\eta = 0.1$,
   half a decade higher than Adam's, and one seed ends at $\eta = 1$ with a loss of 92. The best learning rate
   belongs to the optimiser and must be re-tuned when the optimiser changes.
6. **Change the batch size.** With SGD and batches of 8, the loss at $\eta = 10^{-0.5}$ is 0.63, near chance; with
   batches of 128 it is 0.23, close to the best. Larger batches have less gradient noise, and the range of usable
   learning rates extends upwards, as the linear scaling argument predicts. With a fixed number of steps the larger
   batch also sees more examples, so the comparison is not of equal data.

The selection used the same validation set that reports 0.215. With 9 rates the optimism is small next to the gaps in
step 3, but the reported number is still the minimum of nine noisy means (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Tuning other hyperparameters at a fixed, wrong learning rate.** A regulariser or architecture that looks worse at
  one learning rate may be better at its own best rate. Re-tune the learning rate for each candidate being compared.
- **Changing the batch size without re-tuning.** The best learning rate, and often momentum and weight decay, move with
  the batch size. A batch-size comparison at one learning rate measures the mismatch.
- **Tuning on short runs of a long schedule.** A truncated cosine schedule has not decayed, and its ranking of learning
  rates can differ from that of the full run. Shorten the schedule, not the run.
- **The best seed as the result.** The best of several seeds is a selection statistic. Report the mean and spread over
  seeds at the chosen setting.
- **Grids that stop at the edge.** A best value at the boundary of the grid means the optimum may lie outside it.
- **Unequal tuning in comparisons.** A new method tuned with many trials against a baseline tuned with few measures
  the tuning effort. Choi et al. showed that optimiser rankings can be reversed by the search space alone
  <Cite id="choi2019optimizers" />.
- **Selecting on the test set.** Every choice above, including the stopping step and the checkpoint, is made on
  validation data. The test set is used once, at the end.
