<Definition>
**Quasi-random search** is <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink> with the independent draws replaced by
a **space-filling design**: the first $N$ points $\uvec^{(1)}, \dots, \uvec^{(N)}$ of a low-discrepancy sequence in the
unit cube $[0, 1]^m$, such as a Sobol or Halton sequence, or a Latin hypercube of $N$ points. Each point is decoded into
a configuration through the encoding of the <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink>, scored
by the validation loss, and the best is kept. A random shift or scrambling of the design makes repeated searches
independent while keeping it even.
</Definition>

## Problem

Independent draws clump and leave gaps. On one axis, $N$ uniform points have an expected largest gap of

$$
\frac{1}{N + 1}\sum_{k=1}^{N+1}\frac1k \approx \frac{\ln N}{N},
$$

against $1/N$ for evenly spread points. For $N = 60$ the expected largest gap is $0.077$, 4.6 times the even spacing
of $0.017$. A narrow optimum inside a gap is missed. A grid has no gaps but repeats values on every axis
(see <NoteLink to="grid-search">grid search</NoteLink>). Quasi-random designs aim for both: no large gaps, and $N$
distinct values on every axis.

## Mechanism

### Discrepancy

Evenness is measured by **discrepancy**. For a point set $P = \set{\uvec^{(1)}, \dots, \uvec^{(N)}}$ in $[0, 1]^m$, the
**star discrepancy** is the largest error made by using the fraction of points in an anchored box as its volume:

$$
D^*_N(P) = \sup_{\avec \in [0, 1]^m}\abs{\frac{\#\set{i : \uvec^{(i)} \in [\zeros, \avec)}}{N} - \prod_{j=1}^m a_j},
$$

where $[\zeros, \avec) = [0, a_1) \times \dots \times [0, a_m)$ and $\#$ counts points. A **low-discrepancy sequence**
has $D^*_N \le c_m(\ln N)^m/N$ for all $N$, with $c_m$ a constant depending on the dimension. Independent uniform
points have a discrepancy of order $N^{-1/2}$ <Cite id="caflisch1998" />.

Discrepancy controls integration error through the **Koksma–Hlawka inequality**: the average of $f$ over $P$ differs
from $\int_{[0,1]^m} f$ by at most $V(f)\,D^*_N(P)$, where $V(f)$ is the variation of $f$ in the sense of Hardy and
Krause <Cite id="owen2013" />. This is why quasi-Monte Carlo integration converges faster than
<NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo integration</NoteLink> for smooth integrands.

### What discrepancy guarantees for search

Search needs a different consequence: a large region cannot be empty.

**Every anchored box larger than the discrepancy holds a point.** Let $B = [\zeros, \avec)$ have volume
$\operatorname{vol}(B) > D^*_N$. By the definition of $D^*_N$,

$$
\frac{\#\set{i : \uvec^{(i)} \in B}}{N} \ge \operatorname{vol}(B) - D^*_N > 0,
$$

so at least one point lies in $B$.

**Every box larger than $2^m D^*_N$ holds a point.** A general box $[\avec, \bvec)$ is a signed combination of $2^m$
anchored boxes, by inclusion and exclusion over its corners. In two dimensions,
$[\avec, \bvec) = [\zeros, \bvec) - [\zeros, (a_1, b_2)) - [\zeros, (b_1, a_2)) + [\zeros, \avec)$. Both the count and
the volume obey the same combination, so the error of the count is at most $2^m D^*_N$. A box whose volume exceeds that
bound contains a point.

This is a deterministic version of the quantile argument for random search, which guarantees a hit only with probability
$1 - (1 - q)^N$. It has two limits. The guarantee covers axis-aligned boxes only: a thin diagonal valley can have a
large area and still contain no large axis-aligned box. And the bound $(\ln N)^m/N$ beats $N^{-1/2}$ only when $N$ is
large compared with the dimension: for $m = 10$ it needs $N$ far beyond any
tuning budget. In tuning, the useful property of these sequences
is that their projections onto one or two coordinates are themselves low-discrepancy.

### Halton sequences

The **radical inverse** in base $b$ reflects the base-$b$ digits of an integer about the radix point. If
$i = \sum_{k \ge 0} a_k b^k$ with digits $a_k \in \set{0, \dots, b - 1}$, then

$$
\phi_b(i) = \sum_{k \ge 0} a_k b^{-k-1}.
$$

In base 2, $1, 2, 3, 4$ are $1, 10, 11, 100$ in binary and map to $0.1, 0.01, 0.11, 0.001$ in binary: $0.5, 0.25, 0.75,
0.125$. Each new point falls in the largest remaining gap. The **Halton sequence** in $m$ dimensions uses the first $m$
primes $p_1, \dots, p_m$ as bases: $\uvec^{(i)} = (\phi_{p_1}(i), \dots, \phi_{p_m}(i))$ <Cite id="halton1960" />. Each
coordinate is a one-dimensional low-discrepancy sequence. Among the first $p^k$ points of the coordinate in base $p$,
exactly one lies in each interval $[j p^{-k}, (j + 1)p^{-k})$.

### Sobol sequences

A **Sobol sequence** uses base 2 in every coordinate and gives each coordinate its own binary matrix of direction
numbers <Cite id="sobol1967" />. Its first coordinate is the base-2 radical inverse. The construction makes the first
$2^k$ points balanced: in two dimensions, every rectangle of the dyadic form $[j_1 2^{-k_1}, (j_1 + 1)2^{-k_1}) \times
[j_2 2^{-k_2}, (j_2 + 1)2^{-k_2})$ with $k_1 + k_2 = k$ holds exactly one point. The balance holds when $N$ is a power
of 2. The first point of both sequences is the origin, a corner of the space, so implementations often skip it.

### Latin hypercubes

A **Latin hypercube** of $N$ points divides each axis into $N$ equal slices and places exactly one point in each slice
of each axis <Cite id="mckay1979" />. Coordinate $j$ of point $i$ is $(\pi_j(i) + v_{ij})/N$, where
$\pi_1, \dots, \pi_m$ are independent random permutations of $\set{0, \dots, N - 1}$ and $v_{ij}$ is uniform on
$[0, 1)$. Every one-dimensional projection is stratified, so no gap on any axis exceeds $2/N$. Pairs of coordinates are
not controlled: a Latin hypercube can put all its points on the diagonal. McKay, Beckman and Conover showed that for an
output monotone in each input, the mean over a Latin hypercube has a variance no larger than the mean over independent
draws <Cite id="mckay1979" />. A Latin hypercube is built for a fixed $N$ and cannot be extended by one point, while a
sequence can.

### Randomisation

A deterministic sequence gives the same trials every time, so two searches are not independent repeats. A **random
shift** adds one uniform vector $\Delta$ to every point modulo 1, $\uvec^{(i)} \mapsto (\uvec^{(i)} + \Delta) \bmod 1$.
Every shifted point is uniform on the cube, so averages over a shifted design are unbiased, and independent shifts give
independent repeats. **Scrambling** permutes the digits of each coordinate at random and preserves the balance of a
Sobol set <Cite id="owen2013" />.

## In practice

Bousquet et al. compared low-discrepancy sequences with random search for tuning
<NoteLink to="neural-language-model">LSTM language models</NoteLink> and image classifiers, and found suitable
hyperparameters with far fewer training runs <Cite id="bousquet2017" />. The
<NoteLink to="tuning-deep-learning-hyperparameters">Deep Learning Tuning Playbook</NoteLink> prefers quasi-random
search, a shifted and scrambled Halton sequence, over adaptive black-box optimisers during the exploration phase of
tuning <Cite id="godbole2023" />. Its reasons are that the trials spread out more than random ones, that a non-adaptive
design lets the tuning objective change after the trials have run, that the results are reproducible, and that all
trials can run in parallel. Bayesian-optimisation libraries use Sobol points or Latin hypercubes for the initial design
before the surrogate takes over (see <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

**Halton points by hand.** The first four Halton points in two dimensions, skipping the origin, have first coordinates
$\phi_2(1), \dots, \phi_2(4) = 0.5, 0.25, 0.75, 0.125$. In base 3, $1, 2, 3, 4$ are $1, 2, 10, 11$, so
$\phi_3(1), \dots, \phi_3(4) = \tfrac13, \tfrac23, \tfrac19, \tfrac49$. Decoded to the <Gloss name="svm" /> search space
$\log_{10}C = -2 + 5u_1$ and $\log_{10}\gamma = -3 + 5u_2$, the four trials and their tabulated cross-validated errors
are:

| $i$ | $\uvec^{(i)}$ | $(\log_{10}C, \log_{10}\gamma)$ | error |
| ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 | $(0.5, 0.333)$ | $(0.5, -1.33)$ | 0.046 |
| 2 | $(0.25, 0.667)$ | $(-0.75, 0.33)$ | 0.116 |
| 3 | $(0.75, 0.111)$ | $(1.75, -2.44)$ | 0.050 |
| 4 | $(0.125, 0.444)$ | $(-1.38, -0.78)$ | 0.071 |

Each coordinate fills the largest gap left by the earlier points. The first trial lands in the diagonal valley of good
settings, within $0.007$ of the table's minimum of $0.040$.

**Gaps on one axis.** Sixteen uniform draws on $[0, 1]$ have an expected largest gap of
$\frac{1}{17}\sum_{k=1}^{17}\frac1k = 3.44/17 = 0.202$. The first 16 points of the base-2 radical inverse, starting from
0, are the multiples of $1/16$, with every gap equal to $0.0625$.

**Search on the SVM surface.** Over 500 runs of 64 trials each, the average best error is $0.0449$ for independent
draws, $0.0449$ for Latin hypercubes and $0.0465$ for randomly shifted Sobol points. The worst run of the 500 reaches
$0.0536$, $0.0517$ and $0.0517$. On this surface the designs differ mainly in their bad runs, not in their average. The
good settings lie in a narrow diagonal valley, and evenness in axis-aligned boxes, which is what discrepancy measures,
does not guarantee a point inside a thin diagonal strip.

## Cost

Generating the points costs nothing next to the trials: a radical inverse takes $O(\log N)$ operations per coordinate.
The search costs the same $N$ evaluations as random search, and the trials are as easy to run in parallel. A sequence
can be extended one trial at a time; a Latin hypercube of a new size must be drawn afresh.

## Trade-offs

- **Gains are largest for small budgets and few important hyperparameters.** These are the settings where independent
  draws are most likely to leave a large gap.
- **Against random search.** A quasi-random design keeps random search's indifference to hyperparameters that do not
  matter, because each projection onto a coordinate is still a well-spread set of $N$ values. It adds evenness, not
  adaptivity.
- **Against Bayesian optimisation.** Neither design learns from earlier scores. When evaluations are expensive and
  sequential, an adaptive method spends fewer of them near poor regions.
- **Halton in high dimensions.** Coordinates with large prime bases start with long increasing runs: for $i < 29$,
  $\phi_{29}(i) = i/29$ and $\phi_{31}(i) = i/31$, so the first 28 points lie on a line in those two coordinates.
  Scrambled Halton sequences break this correlation, which is why the playbook scrambles <Cite id="godbole2023" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The origin as a trial.** Unscrambled Sobol and Halton sequences start at $\zeros$, the corner of the space where
  every hyperparameter is at its lower bound.
- **Sobol balance at powers of 2.** A Sobol design of 50 points is less balanced than one of 32 or 64.
- **Unrandomised repeats.** Rerunning a deterministic sequence reproduces the same trials. Repeats that are meant to
  measure the spread of the search need independent shifts or scrambles.
- **Evenness on the wrong scale.** A design is even in the encoded cube. If a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning
  rate</Gloss> is encoded on a linear scale, an even design is even in the wrong coordinates (see
  <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search spaces</NoteLink>).
- **Reporting the best score.** A quasi-random search selects among $N$ noisy scores like any other search, and its best
  score is optimistic (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
