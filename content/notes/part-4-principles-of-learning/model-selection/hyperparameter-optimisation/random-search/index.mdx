<Definition>
Let $p$ be a distribution over a <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink> $\Lambda$, usually
uniform on each hyperparameter's scale. **Random search** draws $N$ configurations
$\lambdavec^{(1)}, \dots, \lambdavec^{(N)}$ independently from $p$, evaluates the validation loss $L$ at each, and
returns the configuration with the lowest loss. Bergstra and Bengio showed that it finds configurations as good as
<NoteLink to="grid-search">grid search</NoteLink> with a fraction of the trials, because the loss usually depends on few
of the hyperparameters <Cite id="bergstra2012" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A grid with $r$ values on each of $D$ axes costs $r^D$ trials. With five hyperparameters and five values each, that is
3,125 trials, and each still tests only five values of every hyperparameter. A search over many hyperparameters needs a
design whose cost can be set to any budget $N$, and whose trials are not wasted on hyperparameters that turn out not to
matter. Which hyperparameters matter is not known in advance, and it changes from one dataset to the next
<Cite id="bergstra2012" />.

## Mechanism

### Low effective dimensionality

Suppose the loss depends, to a good approximation, only on a subset $S$ of $d$ of the $D$ hyperparameters:
$L(\lambdavec) \approx g(\lambdavec_S)$, where $\lambdavec_S$ collects the coordinates in $S$. Call $d$ the
**effective dimensionality** of the problem.

- **A grid** with $r$ values per axis has $N = r^D$ points. Their projection onto the coordinates in $S$ has only
  $r^d = N^{d/D}$ distinct points. Every other trial repeats one of those with a different setting of the
  hyperparameters that do not matter.
- **Random search** draws each $\lambdavec^{(i)}$ from $p$. If $p$ is a product of independent distributions, one per
  hyperparameter, the projection of $\lambdavec^{(i)}$ onto $S$ is a draw from the marginal of $p$ on $S$. The $N$
  projected trials are therefore $N$ independent draws on the $d$ important coordinates.

The projection of a random search in $D$ dimensions is a random search in $d$ dimensions with the same $N$. Its result
depends on $d$ and not on $D$, and it needs no knowledge of which coordinates are in $S$. With $D = 2$, $d = 1$ and
$N = 9$, a 3 by 3 grid tests 3 values of the important hyperparameter and random search tests 9. With $D = 4$, $d = 2$
and $N = 81$, a 3 by 3 by 3 by 3 grid tests only 9 distinct pairs of the important ones.

Bergstra and Bengio fitted <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian-process</Gloss> models to the validation error of neural networks
on several datasets. On most datasets only a few of the hyperparameters mattered, and different ones mattered on
different datasets <Cite id="bergstra2012" />.

### How many trials: the quantile argument

The second argument needs no assumption about the loss at all. Let $G \subseteq \Lambda$ be any set of good
configurations, for example the configurations whose loss lies in the best 5%, and let $q = \prob_p(\lambdavec \in G)$
be its probability under $p$. The trials are independent, so

$$
\prob\paren{\text{no trial lands in } G} = (1 - q)^N.
$$

To land in $G$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$, it suffices that $(1 - q)^N \le \delta$, that is

$$
N \ge \frac{\ln\delta}{\ln(1 - q)} \approx \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{q},
$$

where the approximation uses $-\ln(1 - q) \approx q$ for small $q$. For $q = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.05$ the bound is
$\ln 0.05/\ln 0.95 = (-2.996)/(-0.0513) = 58.4$, so 59 trials suffice, and 60 trials give
$1 - 0.95^{60} = 0.954$. The number does not depend on $D$.

The guarantee is about probability under $p$, not about the loss. A good region that is narrow in each of $d$ important
coordinates has $q$ equal to the product of its widths, which shrinks exponentially with $d$. If the best region is 10%
of each of three important ranges, then $q = 0.001$ and 95% confidence needs about 3,000 trials. The quantile argument
explains why random search is reliable at finding a configuration better than most, not why it finds the best.

### The expected rank of the best trial

The same calculation gives the typical quality of the best trial. Let $F$ be the distribution function of the loss
$L(\lambdavec)$ when $\lambdavec \sim p$, and assume $F$ is continuous. Each trial's quantile
$U_i = F(L(\lambdavec^{(i)}))$ is then uniform on $[0, 1]$, and the best trial has quantile $U_{(1)} = \min_i U_i$. For
$0 \le u \le 1$, $\prob(U_{(1)} > u) = (1 - u)^N$, so

$$
\expect U_{(1)} = \int_0^1 \prob(U_{(1)} > u)\,du = \int_0^1 (1 - u)^N\,du = \frac{1}{N + 1}.
$$

The best of 60 trials lies on average in the best $1/61 = 1.6\%$ of the space. Doubling the budget halves this
fraction. How much loss that buys depends on how steeply $F$ rises near the minimum: on a flat-bottomed surface, a
smaller quantile changes the loss little.

### Anytime, parallel and reusable

The trials are independent. They run in parallel with no coordination, failed trials can be dropped, and the search can
stop at any time or be extended later. The first $n$ trials of a run are themselves a random search of budget $n$, so
one run gives the whole curve of best loss against budget. Dodge et al. use this to report the expected validation
performance of the best model as a function of the number of trials, which shows whether a comparison between two
methods would change with more tuning <Cite id="dodge2019" />.

## Worked example

The cross-validated error of an RBF SVM on two iris species is tabulated over $\log_{10}C \in [-2, 3]$ and
$\log_{10}\gamma \in [-3, 2]$, with a lowest value of $0.040$. Random search draws both coordinates uniformly.

1. **The good set.** Evaluated on a fine grid, 5% of the box has error at or below $0.0498$. Call this the best 5%,
   so $q = 0.05$.
2. **The guarantee.** Sixty trials land in it with probability $1 - 0.95^{60} = 0.954$. Over 2,000 runs of 60 trials on
   the surface, 95.6% did.
3. **The expected rank.** The best of 60 trials lies on average in the best $1/61 = 1.6\%$. On this surface the 1%
   and 2% quantiles of the error are $0.0443$ and $0.0455$, and the average best of 60 trials over the 2,000 runs is
   $0.0450$.
4. **Against a grid in two dimensions.** Both hyperparameters matter here, so $d = D = 2$. A 9 by 9 grid (81 trials)
   finds $0.046$, and 81 random trials find $0.0444$ on average. The two designs are close.
5. **Against a grid with two irrelevant hyperparameters.** Add two hyperparameters that do not affect the error, so
   $D = 4$ and $d = 2$. A grid of 81 trials now has 3 values per axis and only 9 distinct $(C, \gamma)$ pairs. Its best
   is $0.059$. Random search with 81 trials still finds $0.0444$ on average, because its projection onto $(C, \gamma)$
   is unchanged.

## Cost

Random search costs $N$ evaluations, each $K$ fits under $K$-fold
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>, plus one refit: the same as a grid of $N$ points. It adds
no cost for choosing trials. Unlike a grid, $N$ can be any number, and one more trial can be added when budget remains.

## Trade-offs

- **No memory.** Random search ignores the scores of earlier trials. When evaluations are expensive and the important
  hyperparameters are few, <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> reaches a given loss in
  fewer trials by placing later trials near good earlier ones.
- **Uneven coverage.** Independent draws clump and leave gaps, especially for small $N$.
  <NoteLink to="quasi-random-search">Quasi-random search</NoteLink> spreads the trials evenly and keeps the projection
  property.
- **A strong baseline.** Bergstra and Bengio proposed random search as the baseline against which adaptive methods are
  judged <Cite id="bergstra2012" />. In neural architecture search, random search with
  <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> performed at least as well as a leading method on two standard
  benchmarks <Cite id="li2020randomnas" /> (see <NoteLink to="neural-architecture-search">neural architecture
  search</NoteLink>).
- **Early stopping.** Random search spends a full training run on every trial, including hopeless ones.
  <NoteLink to="successive-halving">Successive halving</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> draw
  configurations at random and stop the poor ones early <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.
- **Tools.** scikit-learn's `RandomizedSearchCV` draws a set number of configurations from given distributions and
  scores each by cross-validation <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The distribution is the method.** Random search draws from $p$, so a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning
  rate</Gloss> drawn uniformly instead of log-uniformly concentrates the trials in the top decade (see
  <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search spaces</NoteLink>).
- **Reporting the best of $N$.** The best validation score of $N$ trials is biased low, and the bias grows with $N$ (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). The quantile argument is
  about the true loss; with noisy scores, the trial that looks best is not always the trial that is best.
- **One run as evidence.** Two runs with different seeds return different configurations and scores. Report the
  spread over seeds, or the expected best as a function of budget <Cite id="dodge2019" />.
- **Unequal budgets in comparisons.** A method given 100 trials against a baseline given 10 has been tuned more, not
  shown to be better (see <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink>).
- **Many important hyperparameters.** The advantage over a grid comes from low effective dimensionality. When all $D$
  hyperparameters matter and $N$ is small, random search covers the space no better than a grid of the same size, as in
  step 4 of the example.
