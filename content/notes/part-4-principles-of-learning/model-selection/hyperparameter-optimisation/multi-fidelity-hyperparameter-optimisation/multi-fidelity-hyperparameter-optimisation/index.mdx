<Definition>
**Multi-fidelity hyperparameter optimisation** searches over hyperparameter configurations using evaluations of
different cost and accuracy. A **fidelity** is a budget at which a configuration can be evaluated: a number of training
epochs, a fraction of the training set, an image resolution, or a number of trees or cross-validation folds. Low
fidelities are cheap and only approximately rank the configurations; the highest fidelity is the full training run whose
validation loss is the objective. The methods of this topic spend most of the budget at low fidelities and reserve the
highest for configurations that look promising there.
</Definition>

## What it is for

<NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian
optimisation</NoteLink> treat each evaluation as a complete training run. For deep networks a run takes hours to days,
so a full-fidelity search affords tens of configurations. Most of those runs are visibly poor after a fraction of their
training: a <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> that diverges, or a network far behind the others after
one epoch. Stopping them early frees budget for more configurations. In a typical search with a reduction factor of 3,
the same budget that trains 4 configurations fully screens 27 at low fidelity (see
<NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink>).

Multi-fidelity methods are standard in current tuning tools for this reason. Optuna provides median, successive-halving
and Hyperband pruners <Cite id="akiba2019" />, and Ray Tune provides ASHA and Hyperband schedulers. Feurer and Hutter's
survey of hyperparameter optimisation treats multi-fidelity methods as one of its main families
<Cite id="hutter2019automl" />.

## Main ideas

**Low-fidelity rankings.** Everything rests on one assumption: configurations that do well at low fidelity tend to do
well at full fidelity. The assumption fails when learning curves cross. A small learning rate or strong regularisation
starts slowly and finishes well; a model trained on 10% of the data prefers less capacity than the same model on all of
it. Every method in this topic is a different answer to how far the cheap ranking can be trusted.

**Fixed schedules.** <NoteLink to="successive-halving">Successive halving</NoteLink> trains many configurations at a
small budget, keeps the best fraction $1/\eta$, multiplies their budget by $\eta$ and repeats, so every round costs the
same. <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> runs several such schedules, from aggressive to none, to hedge
against not knowing when rankings settle. <NoteLink to="asynchronous-successive-halving">ASHA</NoteLink> runs successive
halving on many parallel workers without waiting for rounds to finish. These methods descend from fixed-budget
<NoteLink to="best-arm-identification">best-arm identification</NoteLink> in bandits <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.

**Stopping rules.** A simpler family stops a single run when it falls behind. The **median stopping rule** of Google
Vizier stops a trial at step $t$ if its best loss so far is worse than the median of the other trials' running-average
losses up to $t$ <Cite id="golovin2017vizier" />. It needs no schedule and no model, and like successive halving it
trusts the current ranking.

**Models of the learning curve.** <NoteLink to="learning-curve-extrapolation">Learning-curve extrapolation</NoteLink>
fits a parametric curve such as $a + bt^{-c}$ to the first epochs of a run and predicts its final loss, so a run that is
behind but falling fast can be kept. Freeze-thaw optimisation pauses and resumes runs according to such predictions.

**Models across configurations and fidelities.** <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink>
keeps Hyperband's schedule and draws new configurations from a density model fitted at the largest budget with enough
results. <NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-bayesian-optimisation">Multi-fidelity Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> models the
loss jointly over configuration and fidelity, learns how the cheap evaluations relate to the expensive one, and chooses
both the configuration and the fidelity of each evaluation by information per unit cost.

**Data reuse.** Multi-fidelity methods compare many configurations on the same validation set, and the configuration
they return is the one with the lowest observed loss. That loss is optimistic, more so the more configurations were
screened. The final model still needs an assessment on data the search never saw (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>).

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink>: its schedule, its equal-cost rounds and
  the condition under which it keeps the best configuration underlie the rest.
- <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="asynchronous-successive-halving">ASHA</NoteLink> build
  directly on it: one hedges over its aggressiveness, the other removes its synchronisation.
- <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink> needs Hyperband and the
  <NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">tree-structured Parzen estimator</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="learning-curve-extrapolation">Learning-curve extrapolation</NoteLink> stands on its own; it replaces the
  ranking at a budget with a forecast.
- <NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-bayesian-optimisation">Multi-fidelity Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> comes last; it
  needs the <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> topic, in particular
  <NoteLink to="entropy-search">entropy search</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-upper-confidence-bound">GP-UCB</NoteLink>.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The topic assumes <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink>, and some familiarity with
<NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> of a single training run. For the deep-learning setting, where
<NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedules</NoteLink> interact with truncated runs, see
<NoteLink to="tuning-deep-learning-hyperparameters">tuning deep-learning hyperparameters</NoteLink>.
<NoteLink to="population-based-training">Population-based training</NoteLink> also stops weak runs early but changes the
survivors' hyperparameters during training. In <NoteLink to="neural-architecture-search">neural architecture
search</NoteLink>, low-fidelity proxies and weight sharing are the main tools for making the search affordable.
