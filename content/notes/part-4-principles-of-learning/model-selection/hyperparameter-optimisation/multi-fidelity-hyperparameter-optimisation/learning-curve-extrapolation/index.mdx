<Definition>
**Learning-curve extrapolation** predicts the validation loss a training run will reach at its full budget $R$ from the
losses it has reached so far. A model of the curve, such as $y(t) = a + bt^{-c}$ in the number of epochs $t$, is fitted
to the first $t_0$ observations and evaluated at $R$. A <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter
search</NoteLink> uses the prediction to stop runs that are unlikely to beat the best configuration found so far
<Cite id="domhan2015" />, or, in **freeze-thaw** optimisation, to decide which of many paused runs to continue
<Cite id="swersky2014freezethaw" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="successive-halving">Successive halving</NoteLink> ranks configurations by the loss they have reached at a
small budget. That ranking ignores the shape of each curve: a run that is behind but still falling steeply is treated
like a run that is behind and flat. Curves that cross defeat it.

A learning curve carries more information than its last point. Its slope and curvature say how much further it is
likely to fall. A model of the curve turns the first $t_0$ epochs into a forecast of the final loss, and the forecast,
not the current loss, can be compared with the incumbent. The question is whether the forecast is accurate enough at
small $t_0$ to beat the naive rule "the loss at $t_0$ ranks the runs".

This note is about curves over training time within one run. <NoteLink to="learning-curves-and-sample-size">Learning
curves over training-set size</NoteLink> are a different object with similar mathematics, used to decide whether more
data would help.

## Mechanism

### Parametric curve models

Validation loss against epochs usually falls fast and then flattens towards an asymptote. Families with that shape
include

$$
y(t) = a + bt^{-c} \quad \text{(power law)}, \qquad y(t) = a + be^{-ct} \quad \text{(exponential)},
$$

with $a$ the asymptote, $b > 0$ the scale and $c > 0$ the rate. Domhan, Springenberg and Hutter combined eleven such
families, including power laws, exponentials and sigmoid-shaped growth models such as the Janoschek and MMF curves, in a
weighted ensemble. They fitted it by <Gloss name="mcmc">Markov chain Monte Carlo</Gloss>, which gives a predictive
distribution for the final value rather than a point <Cite id="domhan2015" />.

### Fitting a power law by variable projection

The power law is linear in $a$ and $b$ and non-linear only in $c$. Least squares can therefore be solved in one
dimension. Let $(t_1, y_1), \dots, (t_m, y_m)$ be the observed epochs and losses, and fix $c$. Write
$\phi_i = t_i^{-c}$, and $\bar\phi$, $\bar y$ for the means. Minimising $\sum_i (y_i - a - b\phi_i)^2$ over $a$ and $b$
is simple <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink> of $y$ on $\phi$, with solution

$$
\hat b(c) = \frac{\sum_i (\phi_i - \bar\phi)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_i (\phi_i - \bar\phi)^2}, \qquad
\hat a(c) = \bar y - \hat b(c)\,\bar\phi.
$$

Substituting back, the residual sum of squares at the best $a$ and $b$ is the part of $\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$ that
the regression does not explain:

$$
\operatorname{RSS}(c) = \paren{1 - \rho(c)^2}\sum_{i}(y_i - \bar y)^2,
$$

where $\rho(c)$ is the sample correlation between the losses $y_i$ and $t_i^{-c}$. Minimising over all three parameters
is therefore the same as choosing the exponent $c$ whose transformed epochs $t^{-c}$ correlate best with the losses,
then reading $\hat a$ and $\hat b$ from the regression. This is **variable projection**: the linear parameters are
projected out and only $c$ is searched, on a grid and then by a one-dimensional method. The prediction at the full
budget is $\hat y(R) = \hat a + \hat b R^{-\hat c}$.

### Stopping on a prediction

Domhan et al. stop a run when the model is confident that it will not beat the incumbent. With $y^\star$ the best final
loss so far and $Y_R$ the run's final loss under the predictive distribution, the run stops when

$$
\prob(Y_R < y^\star \mid y_1, \dots, y_m) < \delta,
$$

with $\delta = 0.05$ in their experiments: training stops when the model is 95% certain the run cannot win. On
<NoteLink to="image-classification-datasets">image classification benchmarks</NoteLink> this predictive termination sped
up SMAC and <Gloss name="tpe" /> by a factor of about two <Cite id="domhan2015" />. A rule that compares the point
prediction $\hat y(R)$ with $y^\star$ is cheaper but ignores how uncertain the prediction is, and early in a run the
uncertainty is large.

Model-based extrapolation contrasts with model-free rules. The
**<NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-hyperparameter-optimisation">median stopping rule</NoteLink>** of Google Vizier stops a
run at step $t$ if its best loss so far is worse than the median of the other runs' running average losses up to $t$
<Cite id="golovin2017vizier" />. It needs no curve model, and it shares the weakness of successive halving with curves
that cross.

### Models across configurations

A parametric fit uses one run's curve only. Later methods share information across runs, so that a configuration
similar to one already trained can be predicted from fewer epochs.

- **Freeze-thaw Bayesian optimisation** places a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> over the
  asymptotes $f(\lambdavec)$ of all configurations, and models each curve as a second Gaussian process centred on its
  asymptote with an exponential-decay kernel <Cite id="swersky2014freezethaw" />. It keeps a basket of paused ("frozen")
  runs and new candidates, and uses <NoteLink to="entropy-search">entropy search</NoteLink> to choose which run to
  resume ("thaw") or start.
- **LC-Net** predicts curves with a Bayesian neural network that takes the hyperparameters as input and ends in a
  specialised learning-curve layer built from the parametric families <Cite id="klein2017lcnet" />.
- **LC-PFN** is a transformer trained on ten million synthetic curves drawn from a parametric prior, which outputs the
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">posterior predictive distribution</NoteLink> of the curve's continuation in one
  forward pass. Adriaensen et al. report a closer approximation to the posterior than Markov chain Monte Carlo, over
  10,000 times faster, and 2 to 6 times speed-ups on 45 of their data sets from a stopping rule built on it
  <Cite id="adriaensen2023lcpfn" />.

<Derivation title="The exponential-decay kernel of freeze-thaw">
Freeze-thaw models a curve, after subtracting its asymptote, as a mixture of exponential decays $e^{-\lambda t}$ with
random weights. Give the decay rates $\lambda \ge 0$ a gamma mixing density $\psi(\lambda) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
\lambda^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\lambda\beta}$ with $\alpha, \beta > 0$. The covariance between the curve at epochs $t$ and $t'$
is the integral of the product of the basis functions against the mixing density <Cite id="swersky2014freezethaw" />:

$$
k(t, t') = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}e^{-\lambda t'}\,\psi(\lambda)\,d\lambda
= \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_0^\infty \lambda^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\lambda(t + t' + \beta)}\,d\lambda.
$$

The integral is a <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma function</NoteLink>: substituting $u = \lambda(t + t' + \beta)$
gives $\int_0^\infty \lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda(t + t' + \beta)}\,d\lambda = \Gamma(\alpha)/(t + t' + \beta)^\alpha$.
So

$$
k(t, t') = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{(t + t' + \beta)^\alpha}.
$$

The variance $k(t, t) = \beta^\alpha/(2t + \beta)^\alpha$ falls to zero as $t$ grows, so samples from the process decay
towards the asymptote, as training curves do. Being a mixture of positive-definite kernels
$e^{-\lambda t}e^{-\lambda t'}$ with non-negative weights, $k$ is itself positive definite.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

The engine's seeded family has 27 power-law curves over $R = 81$ epochs, with crossing $\kappa = 0.5$ and observation
noise of standard deviation $0.01$ (the figure's opening settings).

1. **One curve.** Curve 0 shows losses $0.855, 0.489, 0.369, 0.316, 0.254, 0.211, 0.227, 0.196, 0.190$ in its first nine
   epochs. Its true form is $0.0822 + 0.766\,t^{-0.916}$, with final loss $0.0959$ at epoch 81.
2. **The fit.** Variable projection on the nine points gives $\hat c = 0.908$, then $\hat a = 0.0814$ and
   $\hat b = 0.773$. The residual sum of squares is $0.0010$, of the order of $6 \times 0.01^2 = 0.0006$ that noise of
   standard deviation $0.01$ leaves on nine points after fitting three parameters.
3. **The prediction.** $81^{-0.908} = e^{-0.908 \ln 81} = e^{-3.99} = 0.0185$, so
   $\hat y(81) = 0.0814 + 0.773 \times 0.0185 = 0.0957$. The true final loss is $0.0959$. The loss at epoch 9, $0.190$,
   is twice the final value.
4. **A stopping decision.** Suppose curve 3 has already been trained fully, with final loss $y^\star = 0.088$. The
   point prediction $0.0957$ is above $y^\star$, so curve 0 stops at epoch 9 and saves 72 of its 81 epochs, correctly.
   The margin $0.0957 - 0.088 = 0.008$ is smaller than the noise in one observed loss. A rule that stops only at 95%
   confidence needs the uncertainty of the prediction, which a single least-squares fit does not give.

Across all 27 curves, the extrapolation and the naive prediction "final loss equals the loss at $t_0$" compare as
follows, by the <Gloss name="mean-absolute-error">mean absolute error</Gloss> of the predicted final loss and Kendall's
$\tau$ between predicted and true final losses:

| epochs seen $t_0$ | power law: error | power law: $\tau$ | loss at $t_0$: error | loss at $t_0$: $\tau$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 0.100 | 0.43 | 0.226 | **0.53** |
| 5 | 0.023 | **0.79** | 0.142 | 0.64 |
| 9 | 0.017 | **0.85** | 0.076 | 0.78 |
| 27 | 0.003 | **0.97** | 0.025 | 0.92 |

With three epochs, three noisy points determine three parameters, and the fits rank the curves worse than the raw
losses do. From five epochs on, the extrapolation ranks better and its error is several times smaller. When the true
curves are exponential decays instead, the power law is the wrong model: at $t_0 = 9$ its mean error is $0.19$, and it
still ranks better than the raw loss ($\tau = 0.47$ against $0.26$), but its individual predictions can be far off.

## Cost

Fitting a parametric model is negligible next to an epoch of training: a power-law fit by variable projection takes
microseconds to milliseconds. Ensembles fitted by Markov chain Monte Carlo cost seconds to minutes per prediction, which
Domhan et al. ran on spare processor cores alongside training <Cite id="domhan2015" />. Freeze-thaw's Gaussian process
costs cubically in the number of configurations, with the curves handled by a block structure. A pretrained LC-PFN
predicts in one forward pass.

## Trade-offs

- **Against successive halving.** Extrapolation can keep a slow starter that successive halving would drop, because it
  sees the slope. It needs enough epochs to fit the model and a family that matches the curves.
  <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> needs no model and hedges by brackets instead.
- **Point fits against predictive distributions.** A least-squares fit is cheap and transparent but gives no measure of
  confidence, so a stopping threshold on it is either timid or reckless. Bayesian ensembles and LC-PFN give the
  probability that a run beats the incumbent directly.
- **Per-run against shared models.** A per-run fit knows nothing of other configurations. Freeze-thaw and LC-Net learn
  from all curves, at the cost of a joint model over the <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search
  space</NoteLink>.
- **Relation to early stopping.** <NoteLink to="early-stopping">Early stopping</NoteLink> ends one run when its own
  validation loss stops improving. Extrapolation ends a run that is still improving but will not improve enough.

## Pitfalls

- **<NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">Learning-rate schedules</NoteLink>.** A decaying schedule bends the curve
  when the rate drops: the loss falls sharply after each decay step. Curves fitted before the decay underestimate the
  final improvement, and stepwise schedules break the smooth families entirely.
- **Overfitting curves.** Validation loss can rise late in training as the model overfits. Monotone families cannot
  represent this, so they predict a lower final loss than the run reaches. Predict the best loss over the run, as early
  stopping would return, or use a family that allows a rise.
- **Too few points.** With $t_0$ close to the number of parameters, the fit follows the noise (the first row of the
  table). Smooth the losses or wait for more epochs before trusting a prediction.
- **Extrapolating far.** A prediction at 81 epochs from 9 multiplies the budget by 9. Small errors in $\hat c$ change
  $R^{-\hat c}$ a lot when $R/t_0$ is large. The same caution applies to the scaling-law extrapolations of
  <NoteLink to="model-selection-at-scale">model selection at scale</NoteLink>.
