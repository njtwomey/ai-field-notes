<Definition>
**Asynchronous successive halving** (<Gloss name="asha" />) runs
<NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink> on parallel workers without waiting for rungs to fill
<Cite id="li2020asha" />. Whenever a worker is free, it looks for a configuration that ranks in the top
$\lfloor m_k/\eta \rfloor$ of the $m_k$ results finished so far at some rung $k$ and has not been promoted. If it finds
one, starting from the highest rung, it trains that configuration to the next rung's budget. Otherwise it draws a new
configuration and trains it at the bottom rung. The bottom rung keeps growing until the overall budget runs out, so the
number of configurations need not be fixed in advance.
</Definition>

## Problem

Synchronous successive halving evaluates every configuration of a rung, ranks them, and only then starts the next rung.
On parallel workers that causes three kinds of waste <Cite id="li2020asha" />:

- **Idle workers at rung boundaries.** The last few jobs of a rung occupy a few workers while the rest wait. Upper rungs
  have fewer configurations than workers, so most workers idle there.
- **Stragglers and failures.** One slow or crashed run holds up the whole rung. Training times differ between
  configurations: in one of Li et al.'s benchmarks, a full training run took 30 minutes on average with a standard
  deviation of 27 minutes <Cite id="li2020asha" />.
- **Late results.** No configuration reaches the full budget $R$ until every lower rung is complete. On a large search
  that takes many multiples of the time to train one model, and there is no fully trained model to look at before then.

Running independent copies of successive halving, one per worker, removes the waiting but returns nothing until a whole
bracket finishes on one worker. Li et al. aimed instead at returning a good fully trained configuration in little more
than the time to train one <Cite id="li2020asha" />.

## Mechanism

### The promotion rule

Fix a minimum budget $r$, a maximum $R$, a reduction factor $\eta$ and an early-stopping rate $s$, so rung $k$ trains to
budget $r_k = r\eta^{s + k}$ for $k = 0, \dots, K$ with $r_K \le R$. Each rung keeps the list of configurations
evaluated at it and their losses. When a worker becomes free:

1. For $k = K - 1, K - 2, \dots, 0$: let $m_k$ be the number of results at rung $k$. If some configuration among the
   best $\lfloor m_k/\eta \rfloor$ of them has not yet been promoted, promote it: train it to $r_{k+1}$ and record its
   loss at rung $k + 1$.
2. If no rung has a promotable configuration, draw a new configuration and train it to $r_0$.

Promotions are checked from the top down, so the work goes to the configurations closest to $R$ first. A configuration
needs only $\eta$ results at its rung to be promoted, not the whole rung.

### Why the rule approaches the synchronous one

Synchronous successive halving keeps the best $\lfloor n_k/\eta \rfloor$ of a full rung of $n_k$. ASHA applies the same
fraction to the $m_k$ results it has. Let $q$ be the $1/\eta$ quantile of the losses that the rung would contain if it
were complete. The empirical $1/\eta$ quantile of the first $m_k$ results estimates $q$, and its error shrinks as
$m_k^{-1/2}$. A configuration is **mispromoted** when ASHA promotes it early but it would not be in the top
$\lfloor n_k/\eta \rfloor$ of the full rung. That happens only to configurations whose loss lies between $q$ and the
current estimate, so the fraction of mispromotions falls as the rung grows. Li et al. argue that the number of
mispromotions in a rung of $n$ configurations is roughly $\sqrt{n}$, by the convergence of the empirical distribution
function, so the fraction mispromoted is about $1/\sqrt{n}$ <Cite id="li2020asha" />.

<Derivation title="A heuristic count of mispromotions">
Treat the losses at rung $k$ as independent draws from a continuous distribution $F$, and let $F_m$ be the empirical
distribution function of the first $m$ results. The <Gloss name="dkw">Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality</Gloss>
gives $\prob\paren{\sup_x \abs{F_m(x) - F(x)} > \varepsilon} \le 2e^{-2m\varepsilon^2}$, so $\abs{F_m - F}$ is of order
$m^{-1/2}$ uniformly. ASHA promotes after the $m$-th result a configuration whose empirical quantile $F_m(L)$ is at most
$1/\eta$. Its true quantile $F(L)$ is then at most $1/\eta + c\,m^{-1/2}$ for a constant $c$ with high probability, so
it is mispromoted only if $F(L)$ falls in a band of width about $c\,m^{-1/2}$ above $1/\eta$. Each new result lands in
that band with probability about $c\,m^{-1/2}$. Summing over the results of a rung of size $n$,

$$
\sum_{m=1}^{n} c\,m^{-1/2} \le c\paren{1 + \int_1^n x^{-1/2}\,dx} < 2c\sqrt{n}.
$$

The count of mispromotions grows as $\sqrt{n}$ and their fraction as $1/\sqrt{n}$. At higher rungs the configurations
are no longer independent draws, because they were selected from the rung below, and the argument is only a guide
<Cite id="li2020asha" />.
</Derivation>

### The first fully trained configuration

A configuration reaches $R$ only after passing every rung, so the earliest it can finish, starting from scratch at each
rung, is the sum of the rung budgets. With $s = 0$ and $R = r\eta^K$,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{K} r\eta^k = R\sum_{j=0}^{K}\eta^{-j} < \frac{R}{1 - 1/\eta} = \frac{\eta}{\eta - 1}R \le 2R,
$$

where the middle step writes $r\eta^k = R\eta^{-(K - k)}$ and sums the geometric series, and the last uses $\eta \ge 2$.
ASHA reaches this bound when there are enough workers that the $\eta$ results needed for each promotion arrive in the
time one evaluation at that rung takes. With resumed training the first configuration reaches $R$ after exactly $R$
<Cite id="li2020asha" />. Synchronous successive halving with $W$ workers cannot return anything at $R$ before
$\sum_{k < K}\lceil n_k/W \rceil r_k + R$, which grows with the number of configurations.

### ASHA within Hyperband

ASHA runs one bracket, with one early-stopping rate $s$. An asynchronous version of
<NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> loops over brackets with different $s$, each run as ASHA
<Cite id="li2020asha" />. Li et al. found that the most aggressive bracket alone usually did best on their benchmarks,
which is why ASHA is often used on its own.

## Worked example

Take $n = 81$ configurations, $r = 1$, $R = 9$ and $\eta = 3$, so the rungs are 81 configurations at budget 1, 27 at
3 and 9 at 9. Eight workers run them; an evaluation takes as long as its budget. The curves are the seeded family of the
figure ($\kappa = 0.5$, no noise, seed 4).

1. **Synchronous.** Rung 0 has 81 jobs of length 1 on 8 workers: $\lceil 81/8 \rceil = 11$ rounds, 11 time units. Rung
   1 has 27 jobs of length 3: $\lceil 27/8 \rceil = 4$ rounds, 12 time units. Rung 2 has 9 jobs of length 9: 2 rounds,
   18 time units. The first configuration trained to $R$ finishes at $11 + 12 + 9 = 32$, the whole search at
   $11 + 12 + 18 = 41$. It spends $81 + 81 + 81 = 243$ budget units, and the workers are busy 74% of the time.
2. **ASHA.** The first eight bottom-rung results arrive at time 1, and the best $\lfloor 8/3 \rfloor = 2$ are promoted
   at once. The first configuration trained to $R$ finishes at time 13, the bound $1 + 3 + 9$ of the mechanism, against
   32. The search also ends at time 41, with the workers busy 86% of the time.
3. **The price.** ASHA promoted 28 configurations from rung 0 instead of 27, and 13 from rung 1 instead of 9. Of these,
   5 are mispromotions: 1 at rung 0 and 4 at rung 1, where the rungs are small. The extra evaluations cost
   $1 \times 3 + 4 \times 9 = 39$ budget units, so ASHA spends 282 against 243.
4. **The result.** Both return the configuration with the best final loss, $0.126$.

On a single worker the contrast is starker. Synchronous successive halving finishes its first configuration at $R$ after
$81 + 81 + 9 = 171$ time units. ASHA finishes one after 26, and the rest of the search fills in around it.

## Cost

ASHA spends slightly more budget than synchronous successive halving on the same configurations, because mispromoted
configurations are trained further. In exchange it keeps workers busy, tolerates slow and failed jobs, and returns a
fully trained configuration within about $2R$ of starting when there are enough workers. Li et al. report linear
speed-ups with the number of workers, runs on 500 workers, and, on one benchmark with 25 workers, a good configuration
in about the time to train a single model <Cite id="li2020asha" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Asynchronous against synchronous.** With few workers, equal training times and a fixed set of configurations, the
  synchronous schedule wastes little and makes no mispromotions. ASHA's gains grow with the number of workers and with
  the spread of training times.
- **Against <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>.** ASHA samples configurations at
  random. Model-based sampling, as in <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink>, needs results
  to fit its model and is harder to run asynchronously at scale; on one benchmark Li et al. found ASHA faster than
  BOHB's synchronous scheduler to a good configuration, and BOHB slightly better at the end <Cite id="li2020asha" />.
- **Against population-based training.** <NoteLink to="population-based-training">Population-based training</NoteLink>
  also stops weak runs asynchronously, but it changes the hyperparameters of the survivors during training instead of
  only deciding which runs continue.
- **Tools.** Ray Tune's `ASHAScheduler` and Optuna's `SuccessiveHalvingPruner` implement ASHA, the latter as a pruner
  that stops trials of any sampler at the rungs <Cite id="akiba2019" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Small rungs promote on little evidence.** The first promotions at each rung use only $\eta$ results. With noisy
  low-budget losses, those early promotions are close to random.
- **The bottom rung never stops growing.** Without a cap on configurations or total budget, ASHA keeps sampling new
  configurations. Set the budget, or the number of trials, explicitly.
- **Bias toward fast configurations.** A configuration that trains quickly finishes its rung sooner and is compared
  earlier. When cost and quality are correlated, for example small models that train fast and finish worse, the early
  promotions favour the cheap configurations.
- **All the pitfalls of successive halving.** Crossing curves, budget-dependent schedules and the optimistic validation
  loss of the winner apply unchanged.
