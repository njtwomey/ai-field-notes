<Definition>
**BOHB** (Bayesian optimisation and Hyperband) is a <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter
search</NoteLink> that keeps the budget schedule of <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> and changes how
configurations are drawn <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />. Hyperband draws them uniformly at random. BOHB draws a fixed
fraction $\rho$ at random and the rest from a <NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">tree-structured Parzen
estimator</NoteLink> (TPE) fitted to the results at the largest budget that has enough of them. The model sees every
result of every bracket, so later brackets start from configurations that earlier brackets found promising.
</Definition>

## Problem

Hyperband and <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> fail in opposite ways.

- **Hyperband** finds a good configuration quickly, because it judges many configurations at small budgets. It keeps
  drawing configurations at random, so with a large budget it improves little faster than
  <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink>.
- **Bayesian optimisation** learns where good configurations lie, so given enough evaluations it converges to the
  optimum. Each evaluation is a full training run, and until the model has enough of them it does no better than random
  search, so it starts slowly.

Falkner, Klein and Hutter showed the two patterns on a six-hyperparameter neural network: Hyperband ahead early,
Bayesian optimisation ahead late <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />. A method that combines Hyperband's
<NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> with a model's proposals should be ahead at both ends. The
difficulty is that the model must learn from results at many different budgets, most of them small and less reliable.

## Mechanism

### The schedule

BOHB runs Hyperband's brackets unchanged. With minimum and maximum budgets $b_{\min}$ and $b_{\max}$, reduction factor
$\eta$ and $s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_\eta(b_{\max}/b_{\min}) \rfloor$, bracket $s$ draws
$\lceil (s_{\max} + 1)\eta^s/(s + 1) \rceil$ configurations and runs
<NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink> on them from budget $b_{\max}\eta^{-s}$. Every
evaluation of a configuration $\lambdavec$ at a budget $b$ is stored with its loss in a set $D_b$, one per budget.

### Drawing a configuration

Each new configuration is drawn as follows <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />:

1. With probability $\rho$, draw it uniformly at random from the <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search
   space</NoteLink>.
2. Otherwise, choose the **model budget**: the largest budget $b$ with $\abs{D_b} \ge N_{\min} + 2$, where
   $N_{\min} = d + 1$ for $d$ hyperparameters. If no budget has that many results, draw at random.
3. Split $D_b$, of size $N_b$, into the best $N_{b,l} = \max(N_{\min}, \lfloor qN_b \rfloor)$ results and the worst
   $N_{b,g} = \max(N_{\min}, N_b - N_{b,l})$, with $q$ a fraction such as 15%.
4. Fit one multivariate <NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density estimate</NoteLink> $\ell(\lambdavec)$
   to the good configurations and another, $g(\lambdavec)$, to the bad ones.
5. Draw $N_s$ candidates from $\ell$ with its bandwidths multiplied by a factor $b_w > 1$, and return the candidate with
   the largest ratio $\ell(\lambdavec)/g(\lambdavec)$.

Steps 3 to 5 are TPE, with one difference: TPE multiplies one-dimensional densities, while BOHB uses one
multidimensional kernel density per group to capture interactions between hyperparameters. Maximising $\ell/g$ maximises
<NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected improvement</NoteLink> under the model, as the TPE note derives. The
reference implementation, HpBandSter, uses $\rho = 1/3$, $q = 15\%$, $N_s = 64$, $b_w = 3$ and $\eta = 3$.

### Why the largest budget with enough results

The goal is a low loss at $b_{\max}$. Results at small budgets are plentiful but rank configurations less faithfully
than results at large budgets. BOHB resolves this by always modelling the largest budget it can. Early in the search
only the smallest budgets have enough results, and the model guides the search by cheap, rough evidence. As results
accumulate at larger budgets, the model moves up, and eventually it uses only full-budget results. Conclusions drawn at
small budgets are then overridden by results at the budget that matters <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />.

The threshold $N_{\min} + 2$ with $N_{\min} = d + 1$ ties the move to the dimension: a density over more
hyperparameters needs more points before it says anything. Each group gets at least $N_{\min}$ points. With few
results the two groups therefore overlap, both drawing on the middle results, and the overlap shrinks as results
accumulate <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />.

### The worst case: a bound against random search

Misleading low budgets can steer the model to the wrong region. The random fraction $\rho$ bounds the damage. In the
last bracket of each Hyperband pass, $s = 0$, all $s_{\max} + 1$ configurations are trained to $b_{\max}$, and on
average a fraction $\rho$ of them were drawn at random. One pass of BOHB therefore trains about $\rho(s_{\max} + 1)$
random configurations to the full budget.

A pass costs about $(s_{\max} + 1)^2 b_{\max}$ (see <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink>). Random search
with that budget trains $(s_{\max} + 1)^2$ random configurations to $b_{\max}$. The ratio is

$$
\frac{(s_{\max} + 1)^2}{\rho(s_{\max} + 1)} = \frac{s_{\max} + 1}{\rho},
$$

so even if every model-based proposal were useless, BOHB samples the space at full budget at least
$\rho/(s_{\max} + 1)$ as often as random search. It is at most $(s_{\max} + 1)/\rho$ times slower, and it still
converges, because random search does <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />. The same argument with $\rho = 1$ gives Hyperband's
factor of $s_{\max} + 1$.

## Worked example

Take $b_{\max} = 81$, $b_{\min} = 1$, $\eta = 3$, so $s_{\max} = 4$ and each pass runs five rounds of successive halving
(the brackets are tabulated in <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink>). Let there be $d = 2$ hyperparameters, so
$N_{\min} = 3$ and a budget needs $N_{\min} + 2 = 5$ results before it can be modelled.

1. **Run 1, bracket 4: 81 configurations at budget 1.** No results exist, so all 81 are random. After the run,
   $D_1, D_3, D_9, D_{27}, D_{81}$ hold 81, 27, 9, 3 and 1 results.
2. **Run 2, bracket 3: 34 configurations at budget 3.** Budgets 1, 3 and 9 have at least 5 results; 27 and 81 do not.
   The model budget is 9. With $N_9 = 9$, the good group has $\max(3, \lfloor 0.15 \times 9 \rfloor) = 3$ results and
   the bad group $\max(3, 9 - 3) = 6$. With $\rho = 1/3$, about 11 of the 34 configurations are random and 23 come from
   the density ratio.
3. **Run 3, bracket 2: 15 configurations at budget 9.** Run 2 added 3 results at 27, so $\abs{D_{27}} = 6 \ge 5$. The
   model moves to budget 27.
4. **Run 4, bracket 1** models budget 27 again, with 11 results there. **Run 5, bracket 0** finds 5 results at
   budget 81 and models the full budget.
5. **The second pass.** All five runs model budget 81, which now has 10 to 15 results.

With $d = 6$ the threshold is 9 results. Runs 2 and 3 model budget 9, runs 4 and 5 model budget 27, and the model
reaches the full budget only in the second pass. More hyperparameters keep the model on cheaper, rougher budgets for
longer.

The worst-case bound for this schedule is $(s_{\max} + 1)/\rho = 5/(1/3) = 15$: if the model were useless, BOHB would
need up to 15 times the budget of random search to sample as many random configurations at full budget.

## Cost

BOHB costs what Hyperband costs in training, plus fitting two kernel density estimates per drawn configuration. A
kernel density estimate costs time linear in the number of results, negligible beside training. BOHB parallelises by
drawing configurations for the next run of successive halving whenever a worker is free, preferring waiting jobs at
small budgets. Falkner et al. report close to linear speed-ups up to four workers and about 15 times with 32 workers
<Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Against Hyperband and TPE alone.** Falkner et al. report BOHB ahead of both Hyperband and Bayesian optimisation on
  <Gloss name="svm">support vector machines</Gloss>, feed-forward and Bayesian neural networks, deep
  <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss> and <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional
  networks</NoteLink> <Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />. The gain over Hyperband appears late, once the model has
  full-budget results.
- **Against ASHA.** BOHB's rungs are synchronous within each run of successive halving. With many workers and training
  times that vary a lot, Li et al. found <NoteLink to="asynchronous-successive-halving">ASHA</NoteLink> faster to a good
  configuration, and BOHB slightly better at the end on one benchmark <Cite id="li2020asha" />.
- **Other models.** The same scheme works with other proposal mechanisms. DEHB replaces the density model with
  differential evolution, with which Awad, Mallik and Hutter report better results on high-dimensional and discrete
  spaces <Cite id="awad2021dehb" />. <NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-bayesian-optimisation">Multi-fidelity Bayesian
  optimisation</NoteLink> models the budget as an input instead of fitting one model per budget.

## Pitfalls

- **A misleading minimum budget.** The model's first proposals come from the smallest budgets. If those rank
  configurations badly, the early proposals concentrate in the wrong region until results at larger budgets take over.
  The random fraction limits the damage; it does not prevent it.
- **Blindness to cost.** The model learns which configurations are good, not which are cheap. Li et al. observed BOHB
  favouring computationally expensive configurations on one benchmark, which reduced the number trained to completion
  within a fixed time <Cite id="li2020asha" />. Under a wall-clock budget, a model that prefers expensive
  configurations finishes fewer of them.
- **High dimension.** With $d$ large, $N_{\min} = d + 1$ delays every model, and kernel density estimates in many
  dimensions are poor. BOHB then behaves much like Hyperband for most of its budget.
- **The incumbent's loss.** The best validation loss found is optimistic about that configuration's error (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
