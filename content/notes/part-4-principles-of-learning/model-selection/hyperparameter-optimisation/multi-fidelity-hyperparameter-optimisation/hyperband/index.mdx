<Definition>
**Hyperband** is a multi-fidelity <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink> that runs
<NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink> several times with different trade-offs between the
number of configurations and the budget each starts with <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />. Given a maximum budget $R$
per configuration and a reduction factor $\eta$, it runs $s_{\max} + 1$ **brackets**, $s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_\eta R
\rfloor$. Bracket $s$ starts about $\eta^s$ random configurations at budget $R\eta^{-s}$. The most aggressive bracket
judges many configurations after a tiny budget; the most conservative, $s = 0$, is random search with full training. All
brackets cost about the same, and Hyperband returns the configuration with the lowest validation loss seen.
</Definition>

## Problem

Successive halving needs the number of configurations $n$ fixed in advance. With a fixed total budget, $n$ sets the
first budget at which configurations are judged, $r = R/n$ when one configuration is to reach $R$. A large $n$ explores
many configurations but judges them after a tiny budget, where rankings may be wrong. A small $n$ judges reliably but
explores little.

The right choice depends on how quickly the ranking of the learning curves settles, which differs between problems
and is not known before the search. In the language of the
<NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive-halving analysis</NoteLink>, it depends on the envelope $\gamma(t)$ and on
the gaps between final losses, neither of which is observed. Li et al. call this the "$n$ versus $B/n$" trade-off
<Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.

## Mechanism

### The brackets

Hyperband removes the choice by trying a geometric range of values of $n$. With $s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_\eta R
\rfloor$ and minimum budget 1, bracket $s = s_{\max}, s_{\max} - 1, \dots, 0$ draws

$$
n_s = \left\lceil \frac{s_{\max} + 1}{s + 1}\,\eta^s \right\rceil
$$

configurations at random and runs successive halving on them from budget $r_s = R\eta^{-s}$. Rung $i = 0, \dots, s$
trains $\lfloor n_s\eta^{-i} \rfloor$ configurations to budget $r_s\eta^i$, so rung $s$ reaches $R$.

Hyperband returns the configuration with the smallest validation loss seen in any bracket. Bracket $s_{\max}$ is the
most exploratory: it starts with budget 1. Bracket 0 starts $s_{\max} + 1$ configurations at $R$ and stops none, which
is <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink>. Each step down in $s$ divides the number of configurations by
about $\eta$ and multiplies their first budget by $\eta$.

### Every bracket costs about the same

Rung $i$ of bracket $s$ trains about $n_s\eta^{-i}$ configurations to budget $r_s\eta^i$, so, ignoring the rounding,
it costs

$$
n_s\eta^{-i} \cdot r_s\eta^i = n_s r_s \approx \frac{s_{\max} + 1}{s + 1}\,\eta^s \cdot R\eta^{-s} =
\frac{(s_{\max} + 1)R}{s + 1}.
$$

The bracket has $s + 1$ rungs, so its cost is about

$$
B_s \approx (s + 1)\cdot\frac{(s_{\max} + 1)R}{s + 1} = (s_{\max} + 1)R.
$$

The factor $(s_{\max} + 1)/(s + 1)$ in $n_s$ is chosen for exactly this: a bracket with fewer rungs gets proportionally
more configurations, so that every bracket spends the same budget $(s_{\max} + 1)R$. One pass through all brackets costs
about

$$
B_{\text{HB}} \approx (s_{\max} + 1)^2 R.
$$

### The price of the hedge

Suppose that, in hindsight, bracket $s^\star$ would have been the right choice for the problem. Running bracket
$s^\star$ alone costs $(s_{\max} + 1)R$. Hyperband runs it as one of $s_{\max} + 1$ brackets of equal cost, so it
reaches the same result after at most $s_{\max} + 1$ times that budget. The factor is $\lfloor \log_\eta R \rfloor + 1$,
logarithmic in the ratio of the largest to the smallest budget. Li et al. prove that Hyperband needs a budget only log
factors larger than successive halving with the best $n$, without knowing the envelope or the distribution of final
losses <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.

The bound also limits what the hedge can gain. The most exploratory bracket evaluates $\eta^{s_{\max}} \approx R$
configurations for a budget of $(s_{\max} + 1)R$, where random search would spend $R$ per configuration. If training
time grows linearly with the budget, Hyperband is therefore at most about $R/(s_{\max} + 1)$ times faster than random
search. Superlinear training costs, such as a kernel method whose cost grows quadratically with the number of training
points, raise that ceiling <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.

### Repeating and growing

A pass over the brackets has a finite budget. Li et al. recommend repeating it until the overall budget runs out
<Cite id="li2018hyperband" />. When $R$ itself is not known, the **infinite-horizon** variant runs passes with growing
$R$, in the style of the doubling trick of <NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

**The brackets for $R = 81$, $\eta = 3$.** Then $s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_3 81 \rfloor = 4$ and there are five
brackets. The formula for $n_s$ and the halving rule give:

| bracket $s$ | $n_s$ | rungs: configurations at budget | budget from scratch | budget when resuming |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | 81 | 81 at 1, 27 at 3, 9 at 9, 3 at 27, 1 at 81 | 405 | 297 |
| 3 | 34 | 34 at 3, 11 at 9, 3 at 27, 1 at 81 | 363 | 276 |
| 2 | 15 | 15 at 9, 5 at 27, 1 at 81 | 351 | 279 |
| 1 | 8 | 8 at 27, 2 at 81 | 378 | 324 |
| 0 | 5 | 5 at 81 | 405 | 405 |

1. **Bracket 3 step by step.** $n_3 = \lceil 5 \cdot 27/4 \rceil = \lceil 33.75 \rceil = 34$ and $r_3 = 81/27 = 3$.
   The rungs keep $\lfloor 34/3 \rfloor = 11$, then $\lfloor 11/3 \rfloor = 3$, then 1. The cost from scratch is
   $34 \cdot 3 + 11 \cdot 9 + 3 \cdot 27 + 1 \cdot 81 = 102 + 99 + 81 + 81 = 363$.
2. **Equal costs.** The five brackets cost 405, 363, 351, 378 and 405 epochs, all close to
   $(s_{\max} + 1)R = 5 \times 81 = 405$. The rounding in $n_s$ and $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ causes the differences.
3. **The whole pass.** It starts $81 + 34 + 15 + 8 + 5 = 143$ configurations for 1,902 epochs from scratch, or 1,581
   when promoted configurations resume. The approximation $(s_{\max} + 1)^2 R = 25 \times 81 = 2{,}025$ is close.
   Random search with 1,902 epochs trains 23 configurations to 81 epochs.

The table printed in Li et al. (2018) lists 27, 9 and 6 configurations for brackets 3, 2 and 1. The formula of their
Algorithm 1, which BOHB also uses, gives 34, 15 and 8, as above <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />
<Cite id="falkner2018bohb" />.

**What the hedge buys.** The figure runs each bracket of $R = 27$, $\eta = 3$ on its own, repeated $s_{\max} + 1 = 4$
times so that it costs about as much as one pass of Hyperband, on seeded learning curves with noise of standard
deviation $0.01$. Averaged over 20 seeds, the mean final loss of the returned configuration is:

| crossing $\kappa$ | bracket 3 | bracket 2 | bracket 1 | bracket 0 (random search) | Hyperband |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | **0.079** | 0.083 | 0.097 | 0.105 | 0.084 |
| 0.4 | **0.085** | 0.088 | 0.103 | 0.109 | 0.095 |
| 0.8 | 0.110 | **0.091** | 0.105 | 0.113 | 0.103 |
| 1 | 0.218 | **0.095** | 0.108 | 0.116 | 0.105 |

When early rankings hold ($\kappa$ small), the most aggressive bracket is best. When curves cross ($\kappa$ near 1), it
is worst by far, because it keeps the configurations that start fast and end badly. Hyperband is never the best column,
because it spends three quarters of its budget on brackets that are not the best one. It is never the worst either, and
it beats random search at every $\kappa$. That is the hedge.

## Cost

One pass of Hyperband costs about $(s_{\max} + 1)^2R$ budget units, the cost of $(s_{\max} + 1)^2$ full trainings.
Within a pass, the brackets are independent and can run in parallel; within a bracket, each rung waits for its slowest
configuration. Hyperband adds no modelling cost. The returned configuration is the one with the lowest loss seen, which
may come from a lower budget than $R$ when the curves are noisy.

## Trade-offs

- **Hedge against commitment.** When the rankings settle early on a family of problems, the most aggressive bracket
  alone is cheaper. Li et al. found that bracket the most effective in many of their experiments, and ASHA runs only
  one bracket for that reason <Cite id="li2020asha" />.
- **Choosing $R$ and the minimum budget.** These set $s_{\max}$ and therefore everything else. The minimum budget must
  still carry information: one epoch on a tiny subset may rank configurations at random.
- **Random sampling.** Hyperband draws configurations independently and learns nothing from earlier brackets.
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink> keeps the brackets and draws configurations from a
  model of the results so far.
- **Synchronous rungs.** <NoteLink to="asynchronous-successive-halving">Asynchronous successive halving</NoteLink>
  promotes without waiting for a rung to fill, and loops over brackets the same way for an asynchronous Hyperband.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the table's best column as the method.** The most aggressive bracket wins on many benchmarks. Running only
  that bracket gives up the protection against crossing curves, which is the reason Hyperband exists.
- **Budget units that do not scale.** The cost argument assumes that training to budget $t$ costs $t$. A fixed overhead
  per configuration, such as loading data or compiling a model, makes the many small runs of the aggressive brackets
  more expensive than the formulas say.
- **Hyperparameters that depend on the budget.** A learning-rate schedule defined over $R$ epochs, or a regularisation
  strength tuned for the full data set, behaves differently at a small budget. The rankings at low budget then measure
  the wrong thing.
- **The incumbent's loss.** Hyperband reports the minimum of many noisy validation losses, which is biased low (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
