<Definition>
**Multi-fidelity Bayesian optimisation** extends <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>
to an objective $f(\lambdavec, z)$ that can be evaluated at several **fidelities** $z$, such as a fraction of the
training set, a number of epochs or a coarser simulation, at a cost $c(\lambdavec, z)$ that grows with $z$. The target
is the full-fidelity loss $f(\lambdavec, z_{\max})$. One probabilistic surrogate models $f$ over configurations and
fidelities jointly, so a cheap evaluation informs the prediction at full fidelity. An
<NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition function</NoteLink> then chooses both the next configuration and the
fidelity to evaluate it at, by trading what the evaluation reveals about the full-fidelity optimum against its cost.
</Definition>

## Problem

The schedules of <NoteLink to="successive-halving">successive halving</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> fix in advance how much budget each configuration gets.
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink> adds a model but fits a separate one at each budget
and uses only one of them at a time. Neither learns how the fidelities relate to each other, and neither decides, for a
particular configuration, whether a cheap evaluation is worth more than an expensive one.

Low-fidelity results are also biased. A model trained on 10% of the data prefers smaller models and stronger
regularisation than the same model trained on all of it, so the best configuration at low fidelity is not the best at
full fidelity. Optimising the cheap objective alone converges to the wrong configuration. Using it well requires a model
of how it differs from the expensive one.

## Mechanism

### A joint surrogate: the autoregressive model

Kennedy and O'Hagan's autoregressive model links two fidelities, a cheap $f_{\text{lo}}$ and an
expensive $f_{\text{hi}}$, by <Cite id="kennedy2000" />

$$
f_{\text{hi}}(\lambdavec) = \rho\, f_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec) + \delta(\lambdavec),
$$

with a scale $\rho$, and with $f_{\text{lo}} \sim \GP(0, k_{\text{lo}})$ and a discrepancy
$\delta \sim \GP(0, k_\delta)$ independent <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian processes</NoteLink>. The expensive
function is the cheap one, rescaled, plus a correction that a Gaussian process can learn from a few expensive
evaluations.

The model defines one Gaussian process over pairs $(\lambdavec, z)$ with $z \in \set{\text{lo}, \text{hi}}$. Its
covariances follow from bilinearity of covariance and the independence of $f_{\text{lo}}$ and $\delta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cov\paren{f_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec), f_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec')} &= k_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec, \lambdavec'), \\
\cov\paren{f_{\text{hi}}(\lambdavec), f_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec')} &= \cov\paren{\rho f_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec) +
\delta(\lambdavec), f_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec')} = \rho\, k_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec, \lambdavec'), \\
\cov\paren{f_{\text{hi}}(\lambdavec), f_{\text{hi}}(\lambdavec')} &= \rho^2 k_{\text{lo}}(\lambdavec, \lambdavec') +
k_\delta(\lambdavec, \lambdavec').
\end{aligned}
$$

The cross term $\rho\, k_{\text{lo}}$ is what transfers information: an observation of $f_{\text{lo}}$ at $\lambdavec'$
changes the posterior of $f_{\text{hi}}$ at every $\lambdavec$ near $\lambdavec'$. Stacking all observations at both
fidelities, the posterior mean and variance of $f_{\text{hi}}$ come from the standard Gaussian process regression
formulas with this kernel. The hyperparameters, including $\rho$, are fitted by maximising the
<NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> (see
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">Gaussian-process hyperparameter learning</NoteLink>).

### Continuous fidelities: kernels over the budget

When the fidelity is continuous, the kernel takes it as an input. FABOLAS treats the fraction $s \in (0, 1]$ of the
training set as a fidelity and uses a product kernel <Cite id="klein2017fabolas" />

$$
k\paren{(\lambdavec, s), (\lambdavec', s')} = k_\lambda(\lambdavec, \lambdavec') \cdot \phi(s)^\top \Sigmamat\, \phi(s'),
\qquad \phi(s) = \paren{1, (1 - s)^2}^\top,
$$

with $\Sigmamat$ a $2 \times 2$ <Gloss name="positive-semi-definite">positive semi-definite</Gloss> matrix. For a fixed
configuration, the loss as a function of $s$ is then a <NoteLink to="bayesian-linear-regression">Bayesian linear
regression</NoteLink> on the basis $1$ and $(1 - s)^2$: it changes most for small subsets and flattens as $s \to 1$. A
second model of the same form, with basis $(1, s)$ on the logarithm of the cost, predicts how long an evaluation will
take. Swersky, Snoek and Adams's multi-task Bayesian optimisation handles a discrete set of related tasks or data
subsets with a kernel that is a product of a configuration kernel and a task covariance matrix
<Cite id="swersky2013mtbo" />.

### Choosing the configuration and the fidelity

With a joint surrogate, an acquisition function scores pairs $(\lambdavec, z)$. Two families are common.

**Information per unit cost.** Let $\lambdavec^\star$ be the minimiser of the full-fidelity loss, unknown, and
$p(\lambdavec^\star \mid \Dcal)$ its posterior distribution under the surrogate. An evaluation at $(\lambdavec, z)$
returns a loss $y$ and changes that posterior. FABOLAS chooses the pair that maximises <Cite id="klein2017fabolas" />

$$
\alpha(\lambdavec, z) = \frac{\expect_{y}\brack{\KL\paren{p(\lambdavec^\star \mid \Dcal \cup \set{(\lambdavec, z, y)})
\,\|\, u}}}{c(\lambdavec, z) + c_{\text{overhead}}},
$$

with $u$ the uniform distribution over the <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink>. The
numerator is the <NoteLink to="entropy-search">entropy-search</NoteLink> measure of how concentrated the belief about
the optimum's location will be after the evaluation, averaged over its possible outcomes $y$ under the surrogate.
Information about $\lambdavec^\star$ is measured at full fidelity, while the cost is paid at the fidelity evaluated. A
cheap evaluation wins when it is nearly as informative, and loses when the model already knows what it would show. The
overhead term $c_{\text{overhead}}$, the time the optimiser itself takes, stops it from choosing evaluations so cheap
that they cost less than deciding on them.

**Confidence bounds with a known bias.** MF-GP-UCB assumes a bound on how far each fidelity $m$ can be from the target
fidelity $M$, $\sup_{\lambdavec}\abs{f^{(M)}(\lambdavec) - f^{(m)}(\lambdavec)} \le \zeta^{(m)}$, with $\zeta^{(M)} = 0$
<Cite id="kandasamy2016mfgpucb" />. Written for minimisation, the derivation takes two steps.

**Step 1: a lower bound from each fidelity.** With a separate Gaussian process per fidelity, posterior mean
$\mu^{(m)}_t$ and standard deviation $\sigma^{(m)}_t$ after $t$ evaluations, the usual confidence argument of
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-upper-confidence-bound">GP-UCB</NoteLink> gives, with high probability,
$f^{(m)}(\lambdavec) \ge \mu^{(m)}_t(\lambdavec) - \beta_t^{1/2}\sigma^{(m)}_t(\lambdavec)$. The bias bound gives
$f^{(M)} \ge f^{(m)} - \zeta^{(m)}$. Together,

$$
f^{(M)}(\lambdavec) \ge \mu^{(m)}_t(\lambdavec) - \beta_t^{1/2}\sigma^{(m)}_t(\lambdavec) - \zeta^{(m)}
\quad \text{for every } m.
$$

**Step 2: the tightest bound.** Every fidelity's bound holds, so the largest of them does:
$\varphi_t(\lambdavec) = \max_m \brack{\mu^{(m)}_t - \beta_t^{1/2}\sigma^{(m)}_t - \zeta^{(m)}}(\lambdavec)$. The
next configuration minimises this optimistic estimate of the target loss, $\lambdavec_{t+1} = \argmin_{\lambdavec}
\varphi_t(\lambdavec)$.

The fidelity is chosen separately: the cheapest $m$ at which the model is still uncertain at $\lambdavec_{t+1}$,
$\beta_t^{1/2}\sigma^{(m)}_t(\lambdavec_{t+1}) \ge \gamma^{(m)}$ for a threshold $\gamma^{(m)}$, or $M$ if there is none
<Cite id="kandasamy2016mfgpucb" />. A cheap fidelity is used while it can still teach something at the chosen
configuration. Once it is known there to within $\gamma^{(m)}$, it cannot say more about $f^{(M)}$ than its bias
$\zeta^{(m)}$ allows, and the next fidelity up is used. BOCA extends the idea to a continuous fidelity space
<Cite id="kandasamy2017boca" />.

### Across training time

Epochs are a fidelity too. Freeze-thaw Bayesian optimisation models every configuration's learning curve with a
Gaussian process over training time and decides which paused run to continue; it is described with the other curve

## Worked example

The Forrester function $f(x) = (6x - 2)^2\sin(12x - 4)$ on $[0, 1]$ plays the full-fidelity validation loss, and its
standard cheap version $f_{\text{lo}}(x) = 0.5f(x) + 10(x - 0.5) + 5$ plays a
<NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-hyperparameter-optimisation">low-fidelity evaluation</NoteLink>
<Cite id="forrester2008" />. Suppose a low-fidelity evaluation costs one tenth of a full one.

1. **The trap.** $f$ has its minimum $-6.021$ at $x = 0.757$. $f_{\text{lo}}$ has its minimum $0.665$ at $x = 0.092$,
   because the tilt $10(x - 0.5)$ favours small $x$. Optimising the cheap function alone returns $x = 0.092$, where the
   full-fidelity loss is $f(0.092) = -0.518$, a regret of $5.50$. The two functions have a correlation of $0.74$ over a
   fine grid, so they are related, but the relation is not a monotone one.
2. **The structure.** Rearranging the definition, $f(x) = 2f_{\text{lo}}(x) - 20(x - 0.5) - 10 = 2f_{\text{lo}}(x) -
   20x$. This is the autoregressive model with $\rho = 2$ and a linear discrepancy $\delta(x) = -20x$.
3. **Learning the relation.** Suppose $f_{\text{lo}}$ is known well, from cheap evaluations, and the discrepancy is
   modelled as linear, $\delta(x) = d_0 + d_1x$. Three expensive evaluations, at $x = 0, 0.5, 1$, give
   $f = 3.027, 0.909, 15.830$, where $f_{\text{lo}} = 1.514, 5.455, 17.915$. The three equations
   $f(x) = \rho f_{\text{lo}}(x) + d_0 + d_1 x$ are $3.027 = 1.514\rho + d_0$, $0.909 = 5.455\rho + d_0 + 0.5d_1$ and
   $15.830 = 17.915\rho + d_0 + d_1$. Their solution is $\rho = 2$, $d_0 = 0$, $d_1 = -20$: substituting,
   $2 \times 1.514 = 3.027$, $2 \times 5.455 - 10 = 0.909$ and $2 \times 17.915 - 20 = 15.830$, up to rounding.
4. **The payoff.** The model $2f_{\text{lo}}(x) - 20x$ is now exactly $f$, and its minimiser is $x = 0.757$. The full
   fidelity was evaluated three times. The cheap evaluations cost a tenth each, so twenty of them add the cost of two
   full evaluations.

The example is idealised: the discrepancy really is linear here, and a Gaussian process with a smooth kernel only
approximates such structure from data. The lesson carries over. The cheap fidelity is useful through a learned
relation, not as a stand-in for the target.

## Cost

A Gaussian-process surrogate costs time cubic in the number of evaluations, and cheap evaluations are many.
Multi-fidelity models therefore hit that cost sooner than single-fidelity Bayesian optimisation. Information-based
acquisitions such as entropy search add Monte Carlo approximations of the posterior over the optimum at each step, which
is why FABOLAS includes the optimiser's own overhead in the cost <Cite id="klein2017fabolas" />. The payoff is in the
evaluations: Klein et al. report that FABOLAS often finds high-quality solutions 10 to 100 times faster than Bayesian
optimisation at full fidelity and than Hyperband on <Gloss name="svm">support vector machines</Gloss> and neural
networks <Cite id="klein2017fabolas" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Model against schedule.** A joint model decides per configuration how much to spend and can exploit a strong
  relation between fidelities. A fixed schedule needs no model and is robust when the relation is weak or changes across
  the search space. In Li et al.'s comparisons, the most aggressive bracket of Hyperband tended to outperform FABOLAS
  with lower variance across trials <Cite id="li2020asha" />.
- **Discrete against continuous fidelities.** A few discrete fidelities suit the autoregressive and multi-task models. A
  continuous budget, such as a data fraction, suits a kernel over the budget and lets the acquisition choose any
  fidelity.
- **Information against confidence bounds.** Entropy-based acquisitions measure what a cheap evaluation teaches about
  the optimum directly but are expensive to compute. MF-GP-UCB is cheap and has regret guarantees, but needs the bias
  bounds $\zeta^{(m)}$ and thresholds $\gamma^{(m)}$, which are rarely known.

## Pitfalls

- **Assuming the cheap fidelity is a noisy copy.** Low fidelities are biased, not just noisy. A model that treats
  them as unbiased observations of the target, with extra noise, inherits the trap of the worked example.
- **Learning curves that cross.** When configurations change rank between small and large budgets, the correlation
  between fidelities is low, and the model gains little from cheap evaluations. It should then learn to spend on
  full-fidelity evaluations; a misspecified kernel that forces a high correlation will not.
- **Costs that the model gets wrong.** Choosing by information per unit cost needs a cost model. If the cost depends on
  the configuration, as training time does on the model size, a cost model that ignores this favours expensive
  configurations at small budgets.
- **The incumbent's loss.** The surrogate's recommendation is selected using noisy validation results, and its predicted
  or observed loss is optimistic (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model
  selection</NoteLink>).
