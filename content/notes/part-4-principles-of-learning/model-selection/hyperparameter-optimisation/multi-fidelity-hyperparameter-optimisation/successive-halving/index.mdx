<Definition>
**Successive halving** is a schedule for evaluating $n$ hyperparameter configurations under a budget such as training
epochs. It trains every configuration with a small budget $r$, keeps the best $\lfloor n/\eta \rfloor$ by validation
loss, trains the survivors to $\eta r$, keeps the best $1/\eta$ of those, and continues until the budget reaches a
maximum $R$ or one configuration is left. The integer $\eta \ge 2$ is the **reduction factor**; $\eta = 2$ gives the
halving of the name. Karnin, Koren and Somekh introduced it for stochastic best-arm identification
<Cite id="karnin2013" />, and Jamieson and Talwalkar applied it to <Gloss name="hpo">hyperparameter optimisation</Gloss>
<Cite id="jamieson2016" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> trains every configuration it draws to the full budget $R$. Most
of those runs are visibly poor long before they finish: a learning rate that diverges, or a network that is far behind
the others after one epoch. Training them to the end spends most of the budget on configurations that were never in
contention.

Stopping runs early saves that budget, but it raises two questions. When should a run be judged, and how many runs
should be kept at each judgement? Successive halving answers both with one rule. It judges all surviving runs at the
same budgets, which grow geometrically, and keeps a fixed fraction each time. It reuses the vocabulary of
<NoteLink to="best-arm-identification">best-arm identification</NoteLink>: each configuration is an arm, a unit of
budget is a pull, and the goal is to name the arm with the best final loss, not to do well along the way.

## Mechanism

### The schedule

Draw $n$ configurations $\lambdavec_1, \dots, \lambdavec_n$ at random from the
<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink>. Let $L_i(t)$ be the validation loss of
configuration $i$ after a budget of $t$ units, such as $t$ epochs, and let $\nu_i = L_i(R)$ be its **final loss** at the
maximum budget $R$. Successive halving runs in **rungs** $k = 0, 1, \dots, K$:

1. Rung $k$ trains each of its $n_k$ configurations to budget $r_k = r\eta^k$ and records $L_i(r_k)$. Rung 0 holds all
   $n_0 = n$ configurations.
2. The best $n_{k+1} = \lfloor n_k/\eta \rfloor$ of them, by $L_i(r_k)$, go on to rung $k + 1$. The rest stop for good.
3. Rungs continue while $r_k \le R$ and $n_k \ge 1$, and $K$ is the last of them. With $n = \eta^K$ and
   $r = R\eta^{-K}$, the last rung has one configuration trained to $R$.

The search returns the configuration with the lowest loss at the highest rung. Li et al. write the same schedule with an
**early-stopping rate** $s$, which starts the first rung at $r\eta^s$ instead of $r$ <Cite id="li2020asha" />.

### Every rung costs the same

Rung $k$ trains about $n\eta^{-k}$ configurations to budget $r\eta^k$, so it costs

$$
n_k r_k \approx n\eta^{-k} \cdot r\eta^k = nr,
$$

independent of $k$. The early rungs spread the budget thinly over many configurations; the late rungs spend it on a few.
With $K + 1$ rungs the total is about $(K + 1)nr$. When the schedule is set so that one configuration reaches $R$, that
is $n = \eta^K$ and $r = R/n$, then $nr = R$ and

$$
B_{\text{SH}} = (K + 1)R = (\log_\eta n + 1)R.
$$

Training all $n$ configurations to $R$ costs $nR$. Successive halving costs $\log_\eta n + 1$ full trainings instead of
$n$. With $n = 27$ and $\eta = 3$, that is 4 full trainings instead of 27.

When a promoted configuration resumes from its checkpoint instead of restarting, rung $k$ only adds
$r_k - r_{k-1}$ per configuration, and the total falls further, to $\sum_k n_k(r_k - r_{k-1})$ with $r_{-1} = 0$.

### When the best configuration survives

Early judgements can be wrong: a configuration that is behind at a small budget may finish ahead. The question is how
far the losses at small budgets may stray from the final losses before successive halving discards the best
configuration. The argument follows Jamieson and Talwalkar <Cite id="jamieson2016" />.

Number the configurations so that $\nu_1 < \nu_2 \le \dots \le \nu_n$; configuration 1 has the best final loss. Let
$\gamma(t)$ be a non-increasing **envelope** that bounds how far every curve can be from its final loss at budget $t$:

$$
\abs{L_i(t) - \nu_i} \le \gamma(t) \quad \text{for all } i \text{ and all } t \le R.
$$

**Step 1: a large gap fixes the order.** Suppose $\nu_i - \nu_1 > 2\gamma(t)$. Then

$$
L_i(t) \ge \nu_i - \gamma(t) > \nu_1 + \gamma(t) \ge L_1(t).
$$

The first inequality is the envelope for configuration $i$. The second rearranges the assumed gap. The third is the
envelope for configuration 1. So at budget $t$, configuration 1 is ahead of every configuration whose final loss is more
than $2\gamma(t)$ worse.

**Step 2: what rung $k$ must resolve.** At rung $k$, configuration 1 is discarded only if at least $n_{k+1}$ of the
other configurations in the rung are ahead of it. By step 1, only configurations with $\nu_i - \nu_1 \le 2\gamma(r_k)$
can be ahead. The configurations are numbered by final loss, so there are fewer than $n_{k+1}$ such configurations in
the whole set, and therefore in the rung, when

$$
\nu_{n_{k+1} + 1} - \nu_1 > 2\gamma(r_k).
$$

**Step 3: the condition for success.** Successive halving returns configuration 1 if this holds at every rung
$k = 0, \dots, K - 1$:

$$
\gamma(r_k) < \tfrac{1}{2}\paren{\nu_{n_{k+1}+1} - \nu_1} \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, \dots, K - 1.
$$

The condition explains the design. Rung 0 keeps $n/\eta$ configurations, so it only has to separate the best
configuration from the one ranked $n/\eta + 1$ overall. That gap is large, and a crude estimate at a tiny budget
suffices. The last rungs keep one or two configurations and must resolve the smallest gaps, $\nu_2 - \nu_1$ at the
end. They get the largest budgets. Training every configuration to one common budget $t$ and choosing the best needs
$\gamma(t) < \frac12(\nu_2 - \nu_1)$ for all $n$ of them, which is the hardest gap paid for at every configuration.
Jamieson and Talwalkar turn this into budgets <Cite id="jamieson2016" />. Let $\gamma^{-1}(\alpha)$ be the smallest
budget at which the envelope falls to $\alpha$ or below. With $\eta = 2$, successive halving returns the best
configuration once its total budget exceeds a quantity at most

$$
2\lceil \log_2 n \rceil \paren{n + \sum_{i=2}^{n} \gamma^{-1}\paren{\tfrac{\nu_i - \nu_1}{2}}},
$$

in which each configuration pays only for its own gap. Uniform allocation is guaranteed only with
$n\gamma^{-1}\paren{\tfrac{\nu_2 - \nu_1}{2}}$, the hardest gap paid by all $n$ configurations, and for some curves it
needs that much.

The condition is sufficient, not necessary. Curves that converge in parallel keep their order even when each is far from
its final loss, so successive halving often succeeds when the envelope is wide. What it needs in practice is that the
ranking at small budgets agrees with the ranking at $R$, not that the losses do.

## Worked example

Take $n = 9$ configurations, $\eta = 3$, $r = 1$ epoch and $R = 9$ epochs, so the rungs are 9 configurations at 1 epoch,
3 at 3 and 1 at 9. The learning curves are the engine's seeded power-law family with crossing $\kappa = 0.5$ and no
noise (seed 4, the figure's opening settings), so the final losses are known:

| configuration | $L_i(1)$ | $L_i(3)$ | $\nu_i = L_i(9)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.730 | 0.275 | **0.131** |
| 1 | 0.981 | 0.486 | 0.322 |
| 2 | 0.710 | 0.450 | 0.343 |
| 3 | 0.790 | 0.524 | 0.390 |
| 4 | 0.817 | 0.425 | 0.237 |
| 5 | 0.640 | 0.401 | 0.313 |
| 6 | 0.843 | 0.438 | 0.304 |
| 7 | 0.694 | 0.288 | 0.155 |
| 8 | 0.954 | 0.542 | 0.389 |

1. **Rung 0.** All nine train for 1 epoch. The order at 1 epoch is 5, 7, 2, 0, 3, 4, 6, 8, 1. The best
   $\lfloor 9/3 \rfloor = 3$, configurations 5, 7 and 2, go on. Configuration 0, the best by final loss, ranks fourth
   with $0.730$ against $0.710$ for the third and stops. Cost: 9 epochs.
2. **Rung 1.** Configurations 5, 7 and 2 train to 3 epochs, with losses $0.401$, $0.288$ and $0.450$. Configuration 7
   goes on. Cost: $3 \times 3 = 9$ epochs from scratch.
3. **Rung 2.** Configuration 7 trains to 9 epochs and finishes at $0.155$. Cost: 9 epochs.

Successive halving returns configuration 7, the second best of the nine, with a regret of $0.155 - 0.131 = 0.024$. It
spent 27 epochs; training all nine to 9 epochs costs 81. With resumed training, rungs 1 and 2 add only 2 and 6 epochs
per configuration, and the total is $9 + 3 \times 2 + 1 \times 6 = 21$ epochs.

The envelope condition of the mechanism explains why the guarantee does not apply here. Rung 0 keeps 3, so it must
separate configuration 0 from the fourth best by final loss, configuration 6. Their gap is $0.304 - 0.131 = 0.173$,
which needs $\gamma(1) < 0.087$. At 1 epoch the losses lie 0.33 to 0.66 above their final values. The guarantee fails by
a wide margin, and the near miss comes from rank order, not from closeness. At 3 epochs configuration 0 already has the
lowest loss of all nine. A schedule that judged first at 3 epochs would have kept it, at a cost of fewer configurations
for the same budget. That trade-off is what <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> hedges.

Over 100 seeds of curves with $n = 27$, $\eta = 3$, $R = 27$ and noise of standard deviation $0.01$, the best
configuration reaches the last rung in 66% of runs at $\kappa = 0$, 30% at $\kappa = 0.5$ and never at $\kappa = 1$.
The mean regret of the returned configuration is $0.004$, $0.022$ and $0.155$. Random search with the same budget, 108
epochs, trains 4 configurations fully and has a mean regret of about $0.05$ at every $\kappa$. Successive halving wins
clearly when early rankings are informative, breaks even near $\kappa = 0.75$, and loses badly when they are reversed.
Losing the exact best configuration is common and usually cheap: a near-best configuration survives instead.

## Cost

A run of successive halving with $n = \eta^K$ configurations and $r = R/n$ costs $(K + 1)R$ budget units from scratch,
or less with resumed training. Measured in configurations evaluated per unit of budget, it explores
$n/(K + 1)$ times as many configurations as random search. The rungs are synchronous: every configuration of a rung
must finish before any is promoted, so with parallel workers the slowest run of a rung holds up the next.
<NoteLink to="asynchronous-successive-halving">Asynchronous successive halving</NoteLink> removes that wait.

## Trade-offs

- **The number of configurations.** For a fixed total budget, a large $n$ explores widely but judges first at a tiny
  budget. A small $n$ judges later and more reliably but explores less. The right $n$ depends on how fast the rankings
  settle, which is unknown in advance. <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink> runs several values of $n$.
- **The reduction factor.** A larger $\eta$ means fewer rungs, fewer survivors and larger jumps in budget. Li et al.
  suggest $\eta = 3$ or $4$ and found results not very sensitive to the choice <Cite id="li2018hyperband" />.
- **The budget unit.** Epochs, training steps, a fraction of the training set, image resolution or the number of trees
  all work, provided the cost grows with the budget and the loss at a small budget ranks configurations as the loss at
  $R$ does. scikit-learn's `HalvingRandomSearchCV` uses the number of training samples by default.
- **Against <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>.** Successive halving chooses which
  configurations to continue, not which to try. <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink>
  replaces its random draws with a model.

## Pitfalls

- **Slow starters.** A small <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>, strong regularisation or a large model
  starts slowly and finishes well. Successive halving systematically discards such configurations when the first rung is
  too early. Curves that cross are the failure case, not an edge case.
- **Schedules tied to the budget.** A learning-rate schedule that decays over the full $R$ epochs is still near its peak
  after one epoch, so a short run is not a truncated long run. Either give each rung its own complete schedule or use a
  schedule without a fixed end (see <NoteLink to="tuning-deep-learning-hyperparameters">tuning deep-learning
  hyperparameters</NoteLink>).
- **Noise at small budgets.** The loss after one epoch depends heavily on the seed. With noisy rankings, a lucky
  configuration survives and an unlucky better one stops. Larger first budgets or repeated evaluation reduce this.
- **Reporting the winner's loss.** The returned configuration was selected for a low validation loss, so that loss is
  optimistic (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Assess it on
  data the search never saw.
