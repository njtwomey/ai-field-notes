<Definition>
**Neural architecture search** (<Gloss name="nas" />) chooses the structure of a neural network, such as its
operations, connections and depth, by search instead of by hand. With a set of candidate architectures $\Acal$,
weights $\wvec$ and training and validation losses $L_\text{train}$ and $L_\text{val}$, it solves the bilevel problem

$$
\min_{a \in \Acal} L_\text{val}\paren{\wvec^\star(a), a} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \wvec^\star(a) = \argmin_{\wvec}
L_\text{train}(\wvec, a).
$$

A method is defined by three choices: the **<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink>**
$\Acal$, the **search strategy** that proposes architectures, and the **performance estimation** that approximates
$L_\text{val}(\wvec^\star(a), a)$ without always training $a$ to convergence <Cite id="elsken2019nas" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An architecture is a hyperparameter with structure. Choosing between a $3 \times 3$ and a $5 \times 5$ convolution, or
deciding which earlier layer feeds the next one, is a categorical choice like choosing a kernel; deciding the number of
layers is an integer choice. NAS is therefore <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter
optimisation</NoteLink> over a large, discrete, conditional space, with an evaluation that costs a full training run
<Cite id="li2020randomnas" />.

Two facts shape the field. The space is enormous, so it must be designed to contain good architectures and little
else. And each evaluation is expensive, so most of the ingenuity goes into estimating an architecture's quality
cheaply. Both make the results hard to compare: a well-designed space can make any search look good, and a cheap
estimate can rank architectures wrongly.

## Search spaces

- **Chain-structured spaces** describe a network as a sequence of layers, each with a type (convolution, pooling, dense)
  and its hyperparameters (width, kernel size, stride). The first <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement-learning</Gloss> search
  predicted such a sequence layer by layer, including skip connections <Cite id="zoph2017nas" />.
- **Cell-based spaces** search for a small building block, a **cell**, and stack copies of it in a fixed outer skeleton.
  The NASNet space searches two cells: a **normal cell** that keeps the spatial resolution and a **reduction cell** that
  halves it <Cite id="zoph2018nasnet" />. Each cell is a small <Gloss name="dag">directed acyclic graph</Gloss>: every
  new node combines two earlier nodes, each through an operation from a fixed list. The number of stacked cells and the
  number of filters are set by hand, so a cell found on a small proxy network can be stacked deeper and wider for the
  final model.

The cell-based design is what made NAS affordable. It shrinks the space from whole networks to small graphs, and it
transfers: a cell found on <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> was stacked into an <Gloss name="imagenet" /> classifier
<Cite id="zoph2018nasnet" />. It also fixes much of the network by hand, which limits what the search can discover
<Cite id="yang2020nashard" />.

## Search strategies

### Reinforcement learning

Zoph and Le trained a recurrent **controller** that outputs an architecture token by token. The validation accuracy of
the trained architecture is the reward, and the controller's parameters are updated by the
<NoteLink to="score-function-estimator">score-function gradient</NoteLink> (REINFORCE) of the expected reward. The
controller sampled 12 800 architectures, with 800 networks training on 800 <Gloss name="gpu" plural /> at a time, and
the best reached a test error of 3.65% on CIFAR-10 <Cite id="zoph2017nas" />. Searching cells instead of whole networks,
NASNet used 500 GPUs for 4 days <Cite id="zoph2018nasnet" />.

### Evolution

Evolutionary search keeps a population of trained architectures, mutates good ones and discards others. Real et al.
use **aging** (or regularised) evolution <Cite id="real2019regularized" />.

1. Train $P$ random architectures as the initial population, held in a queue.
2. Draw $S$ members of the population at random, and take the most accurate as the parent.
3. Mutate the parent: change one operation, or reconnect one input of one node. Train and evaluate the child, and add
   it to the population.
4. Remove the **oldest** member of the population, not the worst. Repeat from step 2.

Removing the oldest means that an architecture survives only through descendants that are retrained and re-evaluated. An
architecture that scored well by luck of its training run cannot dominate for long, which is a defence against noisy
evaluations. With $S = 1$ the procedure reduces to a kind of <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink>. Each
experiment evaluated 20 000 models on 450 GPUs in about 7 days. In side-by-side runs in the NASNet space, aging
evolution found good architectures faster than reinforcement learning and random search, especially early in the search
<Cite id="real2019regularized" />. Evolution of architectures is discrete; the continuous
<NoteLink to="evolution-strategies-and-covariance-matrix-adaptation">evolution strategies</NoteLink> of optimisation
search real vectors.

### Gradient-based search: DARTS

DARTS makes the search space continuous so that architectures can be optimised by
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> <Cite id="liu2019darts" />. Each edge $(i, j)$ of a cell,
from node $i$ to node $j$, carries every candidate operation $o \in \Ocal$ at once, weighted by a
<NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax</NoteLink> of architecture parameters
$\alphavec^{(i,j)} \in \reals^{\abs{\Ocal}}$:

$$
\bar o^{(i,j)}(\xvec) = \sum_{o \in \Ocal} \frac{\exp\alpha^{(i,j)}_o}{\sum_{o' \in \Ocal}\exp\alpha^{(i,j)}_{o'}}\, o(\xvec).
$$

The list $\Ocal$ includes a **zero** operation, which cuts the edge. The architecture is now the vector $\alphavec$ of
all edges' parameters, and the bilevel problem of the definition has a continuous outer variable. At the end of the
search, each edge keeps its operation of largest weight, and each node keeps its two strongest incoming edges from
distinct earlier nodes, with the zero operation excluded from both choices.

**The architecture gradient.** The outer objective is $L_\text{val}(\wvec^\star(\alphavec), \alphavec)$, and
$\wvec^\star(\alphavec)$ requires training to convergence for every $\alphavec$. DARTS replaces $\wvec^\star$ with
one step of gradient descent from the current weights $\wvec$, with step size $\xi$:

$$
\wvec'(\alphavec) = \wvec - \xi\,\nabla_{\wvec} L_\text{train}(\wvec, \alphavec).
$$

Differentiate $L_\text{val}(\wvec'(\alphavec), \alphavec)$ with respect to $\alphavec$. The chain rule gives a direct
term, from the second argument, and an indirect term through $\wvec'$. The derivative of $\wvec'$ with respect to
$\alphavec$ is $-\xi\,\nabla^2_{\wvec,\alphavec} L_\text{train}(\wvec, \alphavec)$, so

$$
\nabla_{\alphavec} L_\text{val}(\wvec', \alphavec) - \xi\,\nabla^2_{\alphavec,\wvec} L_\text{train}(\wvec,
\alphavec)\,\nabla_{\wvec'} L_\text{val}(\wvec', \alphavec).
$$

The second term is a matrix of mixed second derivatives times a vector $\vvec = \nabla_{\wvec'} L_\text{val}$. It is
computed without forming the matrix, by a central <Gloss name="finite-difference">finite difference</Gloss> of the
architecture gradient of the training loss along $\vvec$: for a small $\epsilon$,

$$
\nabla^2_{\alphavec,\wvec} L_\text{train}(\wvec, \alphavec)\,\vvec \approx \frac{\nabla_{\alphavec} L_\text{train}(\wvec +
\epsilon\vvec, \alphavec) - \nabla_{\alphavec} L_\text{train}(\wvec - \epsilon\vvec, \alphavec)}{2\epsilon}.
$$

This costs two extra forward and backward passes instead of a product of size $\abs{\alphavec} \times \abs{\wvec}$
<Cite id="liu2019darts" />. With $\xi = 0$ the second term vanishes and the update treats the current weights as
optimal, the **first-order** approximation. The search alternates one step on $\alphavec$ using validation batches with
one step on $\wvec$ using training batches. The same one-step unrolling appears in
<NoteLink to="gradient-based-hyperparameter-optimisation">gradient-based hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink> and in
<NoteLink to="meta-learning">MAML</NoteLink>.

On CIFAR-10 DARTS reported a test error of $2.76 \pm 0.09\%$ after 4 GPU-days of search with the second-order
gradient, and $3.00 \pm 0.14\%$ after 1.5 GPU-days with the first-order one. In the same comparison, NASNet-A had cost
2000 GPU-days and AmoebaNet-A 3150 <Cite id="liu2019darts" />.

## Performance estimation

Training every candidate to convergence is the dominant cost, so NAS methods estimate quality cheaply.

- **Lower fidelity.** Train for fewer epochs, on a subset of the data, at lower resolution, or with fewer cells and
  filters, and rank by that score. The ranking at low fidelity must agree with the ranking at full fidelity; see
  <NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-hyperparameter-optimisation">multi-fidelity hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="learning-curve-extrapolation">learning-curve extrapolation</NoteLink>.
- **Weight sharing.** Treat every architecture in the space as a subgraph of one large network, the **supernet**, and
  train the supernet's weights once. An architecture is then scored with the shared weights, without training of its
  own. ENAS used weight sharing to find a recurrent cell on a single GPU in about 10 hours, which its authors put at
  more than 1000 times cheaper in GPU-hours than earlier searches <Cite id="pham2018enas" />. DARTS is a weight-sharing
  method too: its mixed edges are a supernet.

Weight sharing trades accuracy of the ranking for cost. Yu et al. compared NAS search policies against random
selection of architectures and found that, on average, state-of-the-art methods performed similarly to the random
policy. Weight sharing degraded the ranking of candidates to the point where it did not reflect their stand-alone
performance <Cite id="yu2020nassearch" />.

## Worked example

**How large is the DARTS space?** A DARTS convolutional cell has 7 nodes: two inputs (the outputs of the two previous
cells), four intermediate nodes, and an output that concatenates the four intermediate nodes. The operation list has 8
entries: $3 \times 3$ and $5 \times 5$ separable convolutions, $3 \times 3$ and $5 \times 5$ dilated separable
convolutions, $3 \times 3$ max pooling, $3 \times 3$ average pooling, the identity, and zero <Cite id="liu2019darts" />.

1. **Inputs of each node.** Intermediate node $m = 0, 1, 2, 3$ can read from the two cell inputs and the $m$ earlier
   intermediate nodes, $m + 2$ predecessors in all. The discrete cell keeps 2 of them, in
   $\binom{m+2}{2}$ ways: 1, 3, 6 and 10. Together, $1 \times 3 \times 6 \times 10 = 180$ wirings.
2. **Operations.** Each of the $4 \times 2 = 8$ kept edges takes one of the 7 non-zero operations: $7^8 = 5\,764\,801$
   choices.
3. **One cell.** $180 \times 5\,764\,801 \approx 1.04 \times 10^9$ cells.
4. **Two cells.** The normal and the reduction cell are chosen independently: $(1.04 \times 10^9)^2 \approx 1.1
   \times 10^{18}$ architectures.

Exhaustive search is out of the question, and so is random search over more than a vanishing fraction of the space.
Every method relies on the space being smooth enough that a sample of a few thousand architectures, or a gradient,
finds a good region.

**Discretising one edge.** After search, an edge has architecture parameters $1.2$ for the $3 \times 3$ separable
convolution, $0.4$ for the $5 \times 5$ separable convolution, $-0.3$ for max pooling, $0.9$ for the identity and $0.0$
for zero; say the other three operations have parameters so negative that their weights are negligible. The softmax
weights are $e^{1.2}/Z = 0.368$, $0.166$, $0.082$, $0.273$ and $0.111$, with
$Z = e^{1.2} + e^{0.4} + e^{-0.3} + e^{0.9} + e^{0} = 9.01$. The edge becomes a $3 \times 3$ separable convolution.
During search, though, the edge computed a mixture in which the convolution had weight 0.37 and the identity 0.27.
The discrete network is not the network whose validation loss was optimised, and the gap between the two is one
reason DARTS results vary from run to run.

## Evaluating NAS

NAS results are hard to compare, and much of the field's progress has been in learning how to compare them.

- **Random baselines.** Li and Talwalkar found that random search with <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early
  stopping</NoteLink> performed at least as well as ENAS on both standard benchmarks, Penn Treebank and CIFAR-10. Random
  search with weight sharing reached a state-of-the-art result on Penn Treebank <Cite id="li2020randomnas" />. DARTS's
  own random-search baseline reached $3.29 \pm 0.15\%$ on CIFAR-10 at the same 4 GPU-days, against $2.76\%$
  <Cite id="liu2019darts" />.
- **The training protocol dominates.** Yang, Esperança and Carlucci compared 8 methods on 5 data sets against the
  average randomly sampled architecture of each space. Many methods struggled to beat that average. In the DARTS space,
  tricks of the final training protocol, such as longer training, cutout and auxiliary towers, had more effect on the
  reported error than the search. The space's accuracy range was so narrow that the random seed changed the ranking of
  architectures, and the hand-designed skeleton mattered more than the searched operations
  <Cite id="yang2020nashard" />.
- **Search failures.** Zela et al. found search spaces in which DARTS converged to degenerate architectures with poor
  test error. The failures coincided with high curvature of the validation loss in architecture space, and
  regularising the search made it robust <Cite id="zela2020darts" />.
- **<NoteLink to="tabular-benchmark-datasets">Tabular benchmarks</NoteLink>.** NAS-Bench-101 trained 423 000 unique
  architectures of a small cell space on CIFAR-10, more than 5 million models in all, so that a search can be run by
  table lookup and compared exactly <Cite id="ying2019nasbench" />.

Lindauer and Hutter turned these lessons into a checklist: release code, compare in the same space with the same
training pipeline, include random search, run several seeds, and report the full cost <Cite id="lindauer2020nas" />.

## Practice today

Cell-based search on <NoteLink to="image-classification">image classification</NoteLink> peaked around 2018 to 2020. The
ideas persist in narrower forms. Hardware targets are added to the objective, so that the search trades accuracy against
latency or memory on a given device. EfficientNet used architecture search to design a small baseline network and then
scaled its depth, width and resolution together by a fixed rule <Cite id="tan2019" />. For large
<NoteLink to="transformer">transformers</NoteLink> the architecture is mostly chosen by hand, and the remaining choices,
such as depth against width, are made by ablations on small models and extrapolated with scaling laws (see
<NoteLink to="model-selection-at-scale">model selection at scale</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Selecting on the test set.** A search runs on a validation split, but the choice among several search runs, seeds
  or retrained candidates is easily made by test error. The final test error is then a selection statistic. Keep a
  test set that no stage of the search or the choice of runs has seen.
- **Crediting the search for the space.** A well-designed space makes random architectures good. Report the score of
  random architectures from the same space, trained with the same protocol.
- **Unequal training protocols.** An architecture trained for 1500 epochs with augmentation and
  <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> cannot be compared with a baseline trained for 600 without.
  Retrain baselines under the same protocol.
- **One seed per architecture.** When the accuracy differences between architectures are smaller than the variation
  across training seeds, a single run per architecture ranks noise.
- **Trusting a weight-sharing ranking.** Scores from shared weights may not reflect stand-alone performance. Retrain the
  top candidates from scratch before choosing.
- **The proxy-to-target gap.** A cell searched on a shallow network of 8 cells may rank differently when stacked into
  20 cells for the final model <Cite id="yang2020nashard" />.
