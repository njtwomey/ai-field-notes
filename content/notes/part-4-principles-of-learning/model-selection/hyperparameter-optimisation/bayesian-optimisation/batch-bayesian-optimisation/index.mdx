<Definition>
**Batch Bayesian optimisation** chooses $q$ configurations $\lambdavec_{t+1}, \dots, \lambdavec_{t+q}$ to evaluate at
the same time, one per parallel worker, from the surrogate fitted to the $t$ evaluations so far. The natural score of a
batch is the **multi-point expected improvement** (q-EI), the posterior mean of the gain of the batch's best member
over the incumbent $f^\star_t$:

$$
\operatorname{qEI}_t(\lambdavec_{t+1}, \dots, \lambdavec_{t+q}) =
\expect\brack{\max\paren{f^\star_t - \min_{j \le q} f(\lambdavec_{t+j}),\ 0} \mid \Dcal_t}.
$$

Heuristics such as the **constant liar** build a batch one point at a time by pretending that each chosen point has
already returned a value <Cite id="ginsbourger2010" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> is sequential: it fits the surrogate, chooses one
configuration, waits for its validation loss, and only then chooses the next. A cluster with $q$ workers then runs one
training at a time and leaves $q - 1$ idle. Running the same acquisition on every worker does not help.
<NoteLink to="expected-improvement">Expected improvement</NoteLink> is a deterministic function of the posterior, so
every worker computes the same maximiser and trains the same configuration $q$ times.

A batch method has to choose $q$ different configurations whose results are jointly informative, before any of them
returns. It trades information against wall-clock time. A batch of $q$ points chosen at once is worth less than $q$
points chosen one after another, since later points cannot use earlier results, but it finishes about $q$ times sooner.
<NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> parallelises perfectly and learns nothing; batch Bayesian
optimisation sits between the two.

## Mechanism

### The value of a batch

The improvement of a batch is the improvement of its best member. For $q$ outcomes $f_1, \dots, f_q$ with improvements
$I_j = \max(f^\star_t - f_j, 0)$,

$$
\max_{j}\, I_j = \max\paren{f^\star_t - \min_j f_j,\ 0},
$$

because the largest gain comes from the smallest value, and the gain is zero only when every value is at least
$f^\star_t$. Taking the posterior expectation gives q-EI as defined above <Cite id="ginsbourger2010" />. Two bounds
follow from $I_k \le \max_j I_j \le \sum_j I_j$ for every $k$, since all $I_j \ge 0$:

$$
\max_j \operatorname{EI}_t(\lambdavec_{t+j}) \le \operatorname{qEI}_t \le \sum_j \operatorname{EI}_t(\lambdavec_{t+j}).
$$

The lower bound is attained when the batch repeats one point: if $\lambdavec_{t+2} = \lambdavec_{t+1}$ then
$f_2 = f_1$ and the second evaluation adds nothing. The upper bound is approached when at most one member can improve
at a time. q-EI is large for batches whose members are good and whose outcomes are not strongly correlated, so it
rewards diversity without a separate diversity term.

q-EI depends on the joint posterior of $(f_1, \dots, f_q)$, a
<NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> for a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian
process</Gloss>. It has a closed form for $q = 2$ and needs $q$-dimensional normal probabilities beyond that. In
practice it is estimated by <NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo</NoteLink>: draw joint samples of the $q$
outcomes, compute the improvement of each sample's minimum, and average <Cite id="ginsbourger2010" />. Writing each
sample as $\mu + \Lmat\zvec$, with $\Lmat$ the Cholesky factor of the posterior covariance and $\zvec$ fixed standard
normals, makes the estimate differentiable in the batch's locations, and BoTorch maximises it by gradient ascent that
way <Cite id="balandat2020" />. The search is over $q\,d$ coordinates at once, which grows hard as $q$ grows.

### Pretending: the kriging believer and the constant liar

Ginsbourger, Le Riche and Carraro replace the joint search by $q$ ordinary EI searches <Cite id="ginsbourger2010" />.

1. Choose $\lambdavec_{t+1}$ by maximising EI.
2. Pretend it has returned a value $L$, the **lie**, add $(\lambdavec_{t+1}, L)$ to the data, and recompute the
   posterior with the same kernel hyperparameters.
3. Choose $\lambdavec_{t+2}$ by maximising EI under the new posterior, and repeat until $q$ points are chosen.

The lie changes the posterior where it matters. In a noise-free model the posterior standard deviation at
$\lambdavec_{t+1}$ drops to zero, so its EI becomes $\max(f^\star - L, 0)$, which is zero whenever $L \ge f^\star$. The
spread also shrinks nearby, so EI falls around the pending point, and the next maximiser lies elsewhere. The choice of
lie sets how far elsewhere.

- **Kriging believer:** $L = \mu_t(\lambdavec_{t+1})$, the posterior mean. Conditioning a Gaussian process on its own
  mean leaves the mean unchanged and only shrinks the variance. A mean below the incumbent also lowers $f^\star$ for
  later points.
- **Constant liar:** one constant for every pending point, usually the minimum, mean or maximum of the observed values.
  A larger lie raises the posterior mean around each pending point and pushes the next point further away. Ginsbourger
  et al. found $L = \min y$ the best of the variants on the Branin function, with $L = \max y$ more exploratory
  <Cite id="ginsbourger2010" />.

Both cost $q$ posterior updates and $q$ ordinary EI searches. Snoek, Larochelle and Adams average EI over many
simulated outcomes of the pending points instead of a single lie, which is the expectation the lie approximates
<Cite id="snoek2012" />.

### Local penalisation

González, Dai, Hennig and Lawrence avoid refitting the model at all <Cite id="gonzalez2016" />. Suppose $f$ is
Lipschitz with constant $K$: $\abs{f(\lambdavec) - f(\lambdavec')} \le K\norm{\lambdavec - \lambdavec'}$. Let $M$ be the
minimum of $f$. Every point within distance $r_j = (f(\lambdavec_j) - M)/K$ of a pending point $\lambdavec_j$ then has
value above $M$, so the minimiser is not in that ball. The value $f(\lambdavec_j)$ is unknown, with posterior
$\Gauss(\mu_t(\lambdavec_j), \sigma_t^2(\lambdavec_j))$. The probability that a candidate $\lambdavec$ lies outside the
ball is

$$
\varphi_j(\lambdavec) = \prob\paren{f(\lambdavec_j) < M + K\norm{\lambdavec - \lambdavec_j}}
= \Phi\paren{\frac{M + K\norm{\lambdavec - \lambdavec_j} - \mu_t(\lambdavec_j)}{\sigma_t(\lambdavec_j)}}.
$$

The next point maximises the acquisition multiplied by these penalisers, $\alpha_t(\lambdavec)\prod_j
\varphi_j(\lambdavec)$. Each penaliser is near 0 close to its pending point, equals 0.5 at the distance
$(\mu_t(\lambdavec_j) - M)/K$, and approaches 1 beyond it. In practice $M$ is estimated by the best observed value and
$K$ by the largest gradient norm of the posterior mean <Cite id="gonzalez2016" />.

### Parallel Thompson sampling

<Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> chooses a point by minimising one draw from the posterior.
Each worker draws its own sample, so $q$ workers choose $q$ different points with no coordination. Kandasamy et al. show
that synchronous and asynchronous parallel Thompson sampling keep regret bounds close to the sequential ones
<Cite id="kandasamy2018" />.

### Asynchronous workers

Trainings finish at different times. An asynchronous scheme proposes a new configuration as soon as one worker is free,
treating the configurations still running as pending points: they receive lies, penalisers or fantasised samples. No
worker waits for a whole batch. <NoteLink to="asynchronous-successive-halving">Asynchronous successive
halving</NoteLink> applies the same idea to <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink>
<Cite id="li2020asha" />.

## Worked example

A search minimises validation error, with incumbent $f^\star = 0.20$. Two workers are free. The posterior at three
candidates is

| candidate | posterior | EI alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | $\Gauss(0.19, 0.02^2)$ | $0.0140$ |
| A$'$ | $\Gauss(0.19, 0.02^2)$, next to A, correlation 0.9 with A | $0.0140$ |
| B | $\Gauss(0.20, 0.03^2)$, far from A, uncorrelated with A | $0.0120$ |

**Step 1: single-point EI.** For A, $z = 0.5$ and $\operatorname{EI} = 0.01 \times 0.6915 + 0.02 \times 0.3521 =
0.0140$. For B, $z = 0$ and $\operatorname{EI} = 0.03 \times 0.3989 = 0.0120$. Ranking by EI alone fills the batch with
A and A$'$.

**Step 2: q-EI of each pair.** Estimated from four million joint draws, $\operatorname{qEI}(A, A') = 0.0164$ and
$\operatorname{qEI}(A, B) = 0.0214$. The near-copy adds $0.0024$ to A's EI; B adds $0.0074$. Both pairs respect the
bounds: $0.0140 \le 0.0164 \le 0.0280$ and $0.0140 \le 0.0214 \le 0.0260$.

**Step 3: the effect of correlation.** For A paired with a near-copy of correlation $\rho$, q-EI is $0.0140$ at
$\rho = 1$, $0.0164$ at $\rho = 0.9$, $0.0195$ at $\rho = 0.5$ and $0.0220$ at $\rho = 0$. A second evaluation is worth
more the less its outcome is predictable from the first.

**Step 4: a constant liar.** Pretend A returned $L = \min y = 0.20$, the incumbent. A's EI becomes
$\max(0.20 - 0.20, 0) = 0$. Through the correlation, A$'$'s posterior becomes $\Gauss(0.199, 0.0087^2)$ and its EI
falls to $0.0040$. B is uncorrelated with A, keeps its EI of $0.0120$, and is chosen. The heuristic reaches the better
pair without computing q-EI.

**Local penalisation, in numbers.** On the Forrester function take $K = 50$, $M = -6.0$, and a pending point with
$\mu_t = -5.5$ and $\sigma_t = 0.3$. At distance $0.01$ the penaliser is $\Phi((-6.0 + 0.5 + 5.5)/0.3) = \Phi(0) = 0.5$.
At distance $0.03$ it is $\Phi(1.0/0.3) = 0.9996$. The excluded radius is about $(\mu_t - M)/K = 0.01$.

## Cost

A constant liar costs $q$ posterior updates and $q$ acquisition maximisations per batch, each as cheap as one sequential
step. Local penalisation needs no updates, only $q$ maximisations of a reweighted acquisition. Monte Carlo q-EI costs a
Cholesky factor of a $q \times q$ covariance per sample and an optimisation over $q d$ coordinates. All are negligible
next to $q$ trainings. The saving is wall-clock time: with $q$ workers a budget of $N$ evaluations takes about $N/q$
rounds.

## Trade-offs

- **Batch size against efficiency.** Each batch is chosen with less information than sequential choices would have, so
  batch methods need more evaluations than sequential ones to reach the same loss, and fewer rounds.
- **Lies against joint scoring.** Lies are cheap and work with any acquisition but can distort the model: a lie far
  from the posterior mean bends a smooth Gaussian process on both sides of the pending point. q-EI scores batches
  exactly but its optimisation scales poorly in $q$.
- **Synchronous against asynchronous.** Waiting for a whole batch wastes workers when training times vary.
  Asynchronous proposals keep every worker busy at the cost of always deciding with some results outstanding.

## Pitfalls

- **Identical proposals.** Running a sequential optimiser on $q$ workers without pending-point handling trains the same
  configuration $q$ times. Libraries mark running trials as pending for this reason.
- **Refitting hyperparameters on lies.** Fitting the kernel's hyperparameters to pretended values fits the model to
  invented data. The heuristics keep the hyperparameters fitted to real evaluations.
- **The incumbent's score.** A batch evaluates more configurations in the same time, which makes the best observed
  score more optimistic, as for any search (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model
  selection</NoteLink>).
