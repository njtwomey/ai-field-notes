<Definition>
**Sequential model-based algorithm configuration** (SMAC) is <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian
optimisation</NoteLink> with a <Gloss name="random-forest">random forest</Gloss> as the surrogate
<Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. After $t$ evaluations of an objective $f(\lambdavec)$, such as a validation loss or a
solver's runtime, it fits $B$ regression trees $T_1, \dots, T_B$ to the configurations and their costs. The forest's
prediction at a configuration $\lambdavec$ is the mean $\mu_t(\lambdavec)$ of the trees' predictions, and its
uncertainty is their variance $\sigma_t^2(\lambdavec)$. SMAC evaluates next the configuration with the largest
<NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected improvement</NoteLink> under $\Gauss(\mu_t, \sigma_t^2)$, alternating
model-chosen configurations with random ones.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> is the default surrogate of Bayesian optimisation, and it fits three
features of many tuning problems badly.

- **Categorical and conditional hyperparameters.** A kernel measures distance between configurations. The distance
  between the optimisers "Adam" and "<Gloss name="sgd" />" has no natural value. A conditional hyperparameter, such as
  the momentum of SGD, has no value at all when Adam is chosen. Such spaces arise whenever a search chooses among
  algorithms, which is the CASH problem of <NoteLink to="automated-machine-learning">automated machine
  learning</NoteLink> (see <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search spaces</NoteLink>).
- **Many evaluations.** Exact GP regression costs $O(t^3)$ time to fit $t$ evaluations. Algorithm configuration runs a
  solver thousands of times, and a cubic cost per step then dominates the search.
- **Irregular costs.** Runtimes are heavy-tailed and change abruptly when a parameter switches a solver's strategy. A
  stationary kernel assumes the same smoothness everywhere.

SMAC was built for the configuration of combinatorial solvers, such as the 76 parameters of the CPLEX
mixed-integer programming solver, over sets of problem instances <Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. Machine learning uses it
for the same reasons: auto-sklearn tunes whole scikit-learn pipelines with it <Cite id="feurer2015autosklearn" />.

## Mechanism

SMAC keeps the loop of <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> and changes the surrogate,
the acquisition's arithmetic, the inner search and the bookkeeping of noisy costs.

### The forest as a surrogate

Each of the $B$ trees is a regression tree grown on a <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> resample of the $t$
evaluations, as in <NoteLink to="bagging">bagging</NoteLink>. At every split a tree considers a random fraction $p$ of
the hyperparameters, as in a <NoteLink to="random-forest">random forest</NoteLink> <Cite id="breiman2001" />. The
original SMAC used $B = 10$, $p = 5/6$ and stopped splitting nodes with fewer than 10 points
<Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. The forest predicts with the empirical mean and variance over trees,

$$
\mu_t(\lambdavec) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b(\lambdavec), \qquad
\sigma_t^2(\lambdavec) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \paren{T_b(\lambdavec) - \mu_t(\lambdavec)}^2,
$$

where $T_b(\lambdavec)$ is the mean cost in the leaf of tree $b$ that contains $\lambdavec$.

The variance stands in for posterior uncertainty. Near many evaluations every bootstrap sample contains similar points,
the trees grow similar leaves, and their predictions agree. In a sparsely evaluated region the trees' splits depend on
which few points each bootstrap sample drew, so the trees disagree and $\sigma_t$ is large. The variance is not a
Bayesian posterior: nothing makes it calibrated, and it measures disagreement among trees rather than distance from
the data.

### Categorical and conditional hyperparameters

A tree splits a categorical hyperparameter by sending some categories left and the rest right, so categories need no
distance. A conditional hyperparameter needs a value even when inactive. One encoding gives every inactive
hyperparameter a fixed placeholder value outside its range. A split on the parent hyperparameter (the optimiser, say)
then separates the subspaces, and splits on the child (the momentum) fall only where the child is active. SMAC3 models
spaces with several levels of conditions this way <Cite id="lindauer2022smac3" />.

### Expected improvement on a log scale

SMAC treats the forest's output as Gaussian and scores candidates by <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected
improvement</NoteLink> over the incumbent $f^\star_t$. For a validation loss this is the usual closed form. For
runtimes SMAC fits the forest to log costs, $o_i = \ln f(\lambdavec_i)$, because log runtimes are far easier to predict
<Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. The forest then gives a Gaussian for $\ln f(\lambdavec)$, which makes $f(\lambdavec)$
<NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal</NoteLink>. The improvement that matters is in seconds, not log
seconds, so SMAC computes the expected improvement of the cost itself.

Fix a candidate and write $Y = \ln f(\lambdavec) \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The improvement is
$\max(f^\star - e^{Y}, 0)$, positive exactly when $Y < c$ with $c = \ln f^\star$. Its expectation splits into two
integrals over $y < c$, with $\varphi_{\mu,\sigma}$ the density of $Y$:

$$
\expect\brack{\max(f^\star - e^{Y}, 0)} = f^\star\int_{-\infty}^{c}\varphi_{\mu,\sigma}(y)\,dy
- \int_{-\infty}^{c} e^{y}\varphi_{\mu,\sigma}(y)\,dy.
$$

The first integral is $\Phi(v)$ with $v = (c - \mu)/\sigma$. For the second,
<NoteLink to="completing-the-square">complete the square</NoteLink> in the exponent:

$$
y - \frac{(y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} = \mu + \frac{\sigma^2}{2} - \frac{(y - \mu - \sigma^2)^2}{2\sigma^2},
$$

so $e^{y}\varphi_{\mu,\sigma}(y) = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}\,\varphi_{\mu + \sigma^2,\sigma}(y)$, a Gaussian density with
its mean shifted by $\sigma^2$, scaled by the log-normal mean $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. Integrating it up to $c$ gives
$e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}\,\Phi\paren{(c - \mu - \sigma^2)/\sigma} = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}\,\Phi(v - \sigma)$. Together,

$$
\operatorname{EI}_{\exp}(\lambdavec) = f^\star\,\Phi(v) - e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}\,\Phi(v - \sigma), \qquad
v = \frac{\ln f^\star - \mu}{\sigma},
$$

which is the criterion SMAC maximises <Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. SMAC3's default for <Gloss name="hpo">hyperparameter
optimisation</Gloss> uses this log-scale EI with a random forest and a Sobol initial design
<Cite id="lindauer2022smac3" />.

### Maximising the acquisition

A forest's mean is piecewise constant, so EI has no useful gradient. SMAC computes EI at every configuration evaluated
so far, starts a local search from the ten with the largest EI, and moves to the best neighbour until none improves. A
neighbour changes one categorical value, or one numerical value by a Gaussian step of standard deviation 0.2 on the
hyperparameter's range rescaled to $[0, 1]$. SMAC adds 10,000 random configurations and sorts all candidates by EI
<Cite id="hutter2011smac" />.

### Interleaved random configurations

SMAC alternates the configurations from that sorted list with configurations drawn uniformly at random
<Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. The random ones give the forest data the acquisition would never choose, which matters
because the forest's spread can understate uncertainty far from the data. In a finite space every configuration keeps a
positive probability of being tried, which is the basis of SMAC's convergence argument.

### Intensification

When the cost is noisy, as for runtimes on random instances or validation losses over seeds, one evaluation cannot show
that a challenger beats the incumbent. SMAC races them: the incumbent gets one more run, then the challenger is run on
the instances and seeds the incumbent has used, doubling its number of runs each round. The challenger is rejected as
soon as its mean cost over the shared runs is worse, and becomes the incumbent if it matches the incumbent on all of
them <Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. The same racing with every challenger drawn at random and no model is ROAR, SMAC's
model-free baseline.

## Worked example

A search tunes a pipeline by validation error. The incumbent is $f^\star_t = 0.10$. A forest of $B = 5$ trees predicts
at two candidates:

| candidate | tree predictions | description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | $0.11, 0.10, 0.09, 0.12, 0.10$ | a numerical neighbour of the incumbent |
| B | $0.16, 0.07, 0.13, 0.06, 0.15$ | a different preprocessing choice, tried twice so far |

**Step 1: moments.** For A the mean is $0.104$. The squared deviations are $0.000036, 0.000016, 0.000196, 0.000256,
0.000016$, whose mean is $0.000104$, so $\sigma_A = 0.0102$. For B the mean is $0.114$ and the variance $0.001704$, so
$\sigma_B = 0.0413$. The trees agree about A and disagree about B.

**Step 2: expected improvement.** For A, $z = (0.10 - 0.104)/0.0102 = -0.392$, with $\Phi(z) = 0.3474$ and
$\varphi(z) = 0.3694$, so $\operatorname{EI}_A = (-0.004)(0.3474) + 0.0102 \times 0.3694 = 0.0024$. For B,
$z = -0.339$, $\Phi(z) = 0.3672$, $\varphi(z) = 0.3766$ and $\operatorname{EI}_B = (-0.014)(0.3672) + 0.0413 \times
0.3766 = 0.0104$.

**Step 3: choice.** B has the worse mean, but its spread gives it four times A's expected improvement, so SMAC
evaluates B next. The next configuration in SMAC's list is drawn at random.

**A runtime.** A solver's incumbent takes $f^\star = 10$ s. For a candidate the forest gives the log runtime mean
$\mu = \ln 12 = 2.485$ and $\sigma = 0.6$. The median runtime $e^{\mu} = 12$ s is worse than the incumbent. Then
$v = (\ln 10 - \ln 12)/0.6 = -0.304$, $\Phi(v) = 0.381$, $\Phi(v - \sigma) = \Phi(-0.904) = 0.183$, and
$e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2} = 14.37$ s. So $\operatorname{EI}_{\exp} = 10 \times 0.381 - 14.37 \times 0.183 = 1.18$ s: a
38% chance of beating the incumbent, by about 3.1 s on average when it does.

## Cost

Fitting $B$ trees to $t$ evaluations with $d$ hyperparameters takes about $O(B\,p\,d\,t\log t)$ time, and a prediction
takes $B$ root-to-leaf descents. A Gaussian process takes $O(t^3)$ to fit and $O(t^2)$ per predictive variance. At
thousands of evaluations the forest is faster by orders of magnitude. The local search adds a few thousand cheap forest
predictions per step. The dominant cost remains the evaluations of $f$.

## Trade-offs

- **Against a Gaussian process.** On low-dimensional continuous spaces with smooth objectives and few evaluations, a
  GP's calibrated uncertainty wins. SMAC3 itself uses a GP with a Matérn 5/2 kernel and expected improvement for such
  problems, and the random forest for structured hyperparameter spaces <Cite id="lindauer2022smac3" />.
- **Against TPE.** <NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">TPE</NoteLink> also handles conditional spaces and
  scales linearly in $t$, but models each hyperparameter separately. Trees model interactions between hyperparameters
  through nested splits.
- **Multi-fidelity.** SMAC3 combines the forest with <NoteLink to="hyperband">Hyperband</NoteLink>'s budgets, as
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink> combines TPE with them, and reports better results
  than BOHB on its examples <Cite id="lindauer2022smac3" />.
- **Instances.** For algorithm configuration the forest takes features of the problem instance as extra inputs, and
  predictions are averaged over the training instances <Cite id="hutter2011smac" />. This handles costs that vary by
  instance without running every configuration on every instance.

## Pitfalls

- **Flat uncertainty far from the data.** Beyond the last evaluation in some direction, every tree predicts the value
  of its outermost leaf, so the spread stays at its value near the boundary however far the candidate lies. EI then
  under-explores unvisited corners. The interleaved random configurations are the safeguard; removing them to save
  evaluations removes it.
- **Too few trees.** With $B = 10$ the variance is estimated from ten numbers and is itself noisy. A small $\sigma_t$
  from chance agreement among trees can make EI ignore a region.
- **Piecewise-constant means.** Many candidates share one prediction, so EI has ties and plateaus. Gradient-based
  maximisers of the acquisition do not work. The local search and the random candidates do.
- **The incumbent's score.** Intensification guards against a challenger that wins one lucky run, but the incumbent's
  mean over its runs is still the minimum over many configurations and is biased low (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Its error needs instances
  or data the search never used.
