<Definition>
**Constrained Bayesian optimisation** solves

$$
\min_{\lambdavec \in \Lambda} f(\lambdavec) \quad \text{subject to} \quad c_k(\lambdavec) \le \tau_k, \quad
k = 1, \dots, K,
$$

when each constraint function $c_k$, like the objective $f$, is expensive and is observed only by evaluating a
configuration $\lambdavec$. It fits a surrogate to each constraint as well as to $f$ and maximises the **expected
constrained improvement**: <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected improvement</NoteLink> over the best
feasible value observed, multiplied by the posterior probability that $\lambdavec$ is feasible
<Cite id="gardner2014" /> <Cite id="gelbart2014" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">Hyperparameter searches</NoteLink> often have limits that cannot be checked in
advance:

- a model must fit in 16 GB of accelerator memory;
- inference must take under 10 ms per example;
- a compressed model must keep its accuracy within one point of
  the full one.

Gardner et al. give the last case as their example: a parameter setting is feasible only if it reaches the exact model's
test accuracy <Cite id="gardner2014" />. Each limit is a function $c_k(\lambdavec)$ measured by the same run that
measures the validation loss. A penalty, such as adding a large constant to $f$ when a limit is broken, makes $f$
discontinuous, and the surrogate must then model a cliff. Ignoring the limit wastes evaluations: plain
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> heads for the unconstrained minimum, which is
often infeasible because the best-fitting models are also the largest.

## Mechanism

### Separate surrogates

Fit one <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> to the objective values and one to each constraint's values. After
$t$ evaluations the objective's posterior at $\lambdavec$ is $\Gauss(\mu_t(\lambdavec), \sigma_t^2(\lambdavec))$ and
constraint $k$'s is $\Gauss(\mu_{k,t}(\lambdavec), \sigma_{k,t}^2(\lambdavec))$. The constraint values are smooth
functions of the hyperparameters even when feasibility is a hard yes or no, which is why modelling $c_k$ beats modelling
a feasible or infeasible label.

### The constrained improvement

An infeasible configuration is of no use, however low its loss. So the incumbent is the best **feasible** observation,
$f^\star_t = \min\set{y_i : c_k(\lambdavec_i) \le \tau_k \text{ for all } k}$, and the improvement of a candidate counts
only if it is feasible:

$$
I_C(\lambdavec) = \Delta(\lambdavec)\,\max\paren{f^\star_t - f(\lambdavec), 0},
$$

where $\Delta(\lambdavec)$ is 1 when every constraint holds and 0 otherwise <Cite id="gardner2014" />.

**Expectation.** Model the objective and the constraints as independent given $\lambdavec$. Then $\Delta(\lambdavec)$
and $f(\lambdavec)$ are independent under the posterior, the expectation of their product is the product of their
expectations, and

$$
\operatorname{EI}_C(\lambdavec) = \expect\brack{\Delta(\lambdavec)}\,\expect\brack{\max\paren{f^\star_t - f(\lambdavec),
0}} = \operatorname{PF}(\lambdavec)\,\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambdavec).
$$

The second factor is the ordinary closed form of expected improvement, against the feasible incumbent.

**Probability of feasibility.** For one constraint the first factor is a Gaussian tail:

$$
\operatorname{PF}(\lambdavec) = \prob\paren{c(\lambdavec) \le \tau} =
\Phi\paren{\frac{\tau - \mu_{c,t}(\lambdavec)}{\sigma_{c,t}(\lambdavec)}}.
$$

With $K$ independent constraints it is the product of $K$ such factors. Correlated constraints need a multivariate
normal probability instead <Cite id="gardner2014" />. The probability of feasibility has the form of the probability of
improvement (see <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition functions</NoteLink>) with the threshold $\tau$ in
place of the incumbent.

### Before any feasible point

With no feasible evaluation the incumbent does not exist and EI is undefined. The search then maximises the
probability of feasibility alone until it finds a feasible point <Cite id="gelbart2014" />. This phase exploits: it
evaluates where feasibility is most likely, and each evaluation either confirms the region or lowers its probability
and sends the search elsewhere.

### Infeasible evaluations still teach

An infeasible evaluation never becomes the incumbent, but its objective and constraint values are added to both
surrogates. Evaluations on the infeasible side of a boundary show where the boundary lies, so the search can approach
the constrained optimum from both sides without sampling widely inside the feasible region
<Cite id="gardner2014" />.

## Worked example

A search tunes a model's validation error under a memory limit of $\tau = 16$ GB. The best feasible error so far is
$f^\star = 0.20$. The surrogates give, at three candidates:

| candidate | error posterior | memory posterior (GB) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | $\Gauss(0.170, 0.020^2)$ | $\Gauss(17, 1^2)$ |
| B | $\Gauss(0.195, 0.010^2)$ | $\Gauss(12, 2^2)$ |
| C | $\Gauss(0.200, 0.040^2)$ | $\Gauss(15, 1^2)$ |

**Step 1: expected improvement.** For A, $z = (0.20 - 0.17)/0.02 = 1.5$, so $\operatorname{EI}_A = 0.03 \times 0.9332 +
0.02 \times 0.1295 = 0.0306$. For B, $z = 0.5$ and $\operatorname{EI}_B = 0.005 \times 0.6915 + 0.01 \times 0.3521 =
0.0070$. For C, $z = 0$ and $\operatorname{EI}_C = 0.04 \times 0.3989 = 0.0160$. Unconstrained, A wins by a factor of
two.

**Step 2: probability of feasibility.** For A, $\Phi((16 - 17)/1) = \Phi(-1) = 0.159$. For B, $\Phi(2) = 0.977$. For C,
$\Phi(1) = 0.841$.

**Step 3: the product.** $\operatorname{EI}_C(A) = 0.0306 \times 0.159 = 0.0049$,
$\operatorname{EI}_C(B) = 0.0070 \times 0.977 = 0.0068$ and $\operatorname{EI}_C(C) = 0.0160 \times 0.841 = 0.0134$.
The search evaluates C: an uncertain error, a good chance of fitting in memory. A's low predicted error is probably
unattainable, and B's feasibility is nearly certain but its possible gain is small.

## Cost

Each constraint adds one surrogate to fit per step, so the overhead grows linearly in $K$. The acquisition is a product
of closed forms, as cheap as plain EI. When the objective and the constraints come from the same run, the evaluation
cost is unchanged.

## Trade-offs

- **Coupled against decoupled evaluations.** When a constraint can be measured without the full objective, such as
  memory from one forward pass, evaluating the constraint alone is cheaper. Choosing which function to evaluate next is
  a separate decision, which Gelbart, Snoek and Adams treat <Cite id="gelbart2014" />.
- **Hard against probabilistic constraints.** A noisy constraint, such as latency, can be required to hold with high
  probability, $\prob(c(\lambdavec) \le \tau) \ge 1 - \delta$, rather than in one measurement
  <Cite id="gelbart2014" />.
- **Constraints against a second objective.** When the limit is negotiable, such as "faster is better" rather than
  "under 10 ms", it belongs in <NoteLink to="multi-objective-bayesian-optimisation">multi-objective Bayesian
  optimisation</NoteLink>, which returns the whole trade-off.

## Pitfalls

- **Crashes without a constraint value.** A run that runs out of memory may return no measurement at all. A binary
  feasible-or-failed outcome needs a classification surrogate, which is less informative than a measured value. Logging
  the quantity that failed, such as peak memory, gives a regression target.
- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> assumed.** Larger models use more memory and also fit better, so
  objective and constraint are often correlated. The product formula ignores this and is only an approximation then.
- **A misleading probability of feasibility.** Early in the search the constraint's surrogate is uncertain everywhere,
  and $\operatorname{PF}$ near $0.5$ everywhere tells the search little. A few space-filling evaluations before the
  constrained acquisition takes over help.
- **Reporting.** The incumbent's error is the minimum over feasible evaluations and is optimistic like any search's
  best score (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Its
  feasibility should be confirmed by a fresh measurement when the constraint is noisy.
