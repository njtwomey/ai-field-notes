<Definition>
**GP-UCB** is a <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> rule with a
<Gloss name="gp">Gaussian-process</Gloss> surrogate. To minimise $f$, at step $t$ it evaluates

$$
\lambdavec_t = \argmin_{\lambdavec \in \Lambda} \paren{\mu_{t-1}(\lambdavec) - \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\lambdavec)},
$$

the candidate whose <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">lower confidence bound</NoteLink> is smallest, where
$\mu_{t-1}$ and $\sigma_{t-1}$ are the posterior mean and standard deviation after $t - 1$ evaluations and $\beta_t$ is
an exploration weight. Srinivas, Krause, Kakade and Seeger gave a schedule for $\beta_t$ under which the **cumulative
regret** $R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\paren{f(\lambdavec_t) -
f(\lambdavec^\star)}$ grows more slowly than $T$, where $\lambdavec^\star$ minimises $f$ <Cite id="srinivas2010" />. The
name comes from the maximisation form, $\mu_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\sigma_{t-1}$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

GP-UCB carries the bandit rule <NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink> to a continuum of arms
whose values are correlated. UCB1 adds to each arm's sample mean a bonus that shrinks with the arm's pulls. GP-UCB adds
to each candidate's posterior mean a multiple of its posterior standard deviation, which shrinks wherever evaluations
are nearby. One evaluation narrows the posterior over a whole neighbourhood, so the method learns about candidates it
has never tried.

The rule is **optimism in the face of uncertainty**: act as if each candidate were as good as the posterior plausibly
allows. Optimism corrects itself. If a candidate is chosen for its optimistic bound and turns out worse, the posterior
around it rises and narrows, and its bound stops winning. If the true minimiser looks unpromising, its bound stays low
while it is unexplored, and it is eventually evaluated.

The weight $\beta_t$ sets how optimistic the bound is. A bound $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ standard deviations below the mean holds
for one candidate with probability about $1 - e^{-\beta_t/2}$. To hold for every candidate and every step at once, the
bound must widen slowly as the number of candidates and steps grows. That is why $\beta_t$ grows with $t$.

## Regret

The analysis measures a search by its regret rather than by its final answer. The **instantaneous regret** of step $t$
is $r_t = f(\lambdavec_t) - f(\lambdavec^\star) \ge 0$, the excess of the evaluated value over the minimum. The
cumulative regret is $R_T = \sum_{t \le T} r_t$. A rule is **no-regret** when $R_T/T \to 0$: on average, its evaluations
approach the minimum.

No-regret also bounds the quality of the best evaluation. The smallest instantaneous regret is at most the average, so

$$
\min_{t \le T} f(\lambdavec_t) - f(\lambdavec^\star) \le \frac{R_T}{T}.
$$

For <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink> this is the guarantee that matters: the best
configuration evaluated gets close to the best in $\Lambda$. The guarantee is about the true values $f(\lambdavec_t)$,
not about the noisy observed values.

## The regret bound on a finite set of candidates

Take a finite set $\Lambda$ of $\abs{\Lambda}$ candidates, such as a grid. Assume that $f$ is a sample from the
Gaussian-process prior $\GP(0, k)$ with $k(\lambdavec, \lambdavec) \le 1$, and that each observation is
$y_t = f(\lambdavec_t) + \varepsilon_t$ with independent noise $\varepsilon_t \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma_n^2)$. The surrogate
is that same Gaussian process, so its posterior is the true conditional distribution of $f$. Fix a failure probability
$\delta \in (0, 1)$ and set

$$
\beta_t = 2\log\paren{\frac{\abs{\Lambda}\,t^2\pi^2}{6\delta}}.
$$

**Theorem** <Cite id="srinivas2010" />. With probability at least $1 - \delta$, for every $T \ge 1$,

$$
R_T \le \sqrt{C_1 T \beta_T \gamma_T}, \qquad C_1 = \frac{8}{\log(1 + \sigma_n^{-2})},
$$

where $\gamma_T$ is the maximum information gain defined below. The proof has four steps.

**Step 1: the bounds hold everywhere at once.** Given the first $t - 1$ observations, $f(\lambdavec)$ is
$\Gauss(\mu_{t-1}(\lambdavec), \sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec))$. A standard normal variable $U$ satisfies
$\prob(\abs{U} > c) \le e^{-c^2/2}$ for $c > 0$. So

$$
\prob\paren{\abs{f(\lambdavec) - \mu_{t-1}(\lambdavec)} > \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\lambdavec)} \le e^{-\beta_t/2}
= \frac{6\delta}{\abs{\Lambda}\,\pi^2 t^2}.
$$

By the <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> over the $\abs{\Lambda}$ candidates and all steps $t \ge 1$,
the probability that any bound fails is at most $\sum_{t \ge 1} 6\delta/(\pi^2 t^2) = \delta$, because
$\sum_{t \ge 1} 1/t^2 = \pi^2/6$. The schedule for $\beta_t$ was chosen to make this sum equal $\delta$. From here on,
assume every bound holds.

**Step 2: the regret of a step is at most the width of the chosen interval.** The rule picks $\lambdavec_t$ with the
smallest lower bound, so in particular no larger than the lower bound at $\lambdavec^\star$. With
$s = \sqrt{\beta_t}$,

$$
f(\lambdavec^\star) \ge \mu_{t-1}(\lambdavec^\star) - s\,\sigma_{t-1}(\lambdavec^\star)
\ge \mu_{t-1}(\lambdavec_t) - s\,\sigma_{t-1}(\lambdavec_t).
$$

The upper bound at the chosen point gives $f(\lambdavec_t) \le \mu_{t-1}(\lambdavec_t) + s\,\sigma_{t-1}(\lambdavec_t)$.
Subtracting the two,

$$
r_t = f(\lambdavec_t) - f(\lambdavec^\star) \le 2\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\sigma_{t-1}(\lambdavec_t).
$$

Optimism pays here. The regret is charged to the posterior uncertainty at the point the rule chose to evaluate, and
evaluating there reduces that uncertainty.

**Step 3: the total uncertainty evaluated is bounded by the information gained.** Square and sum Step 2, using that
$\beta_t$ increases with $t$:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t^2 \le 4\beta_T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t).
$$

Write $u_t = \sigma_n^{-2}\sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t)$. Since the prior variance is at most 1, $u_t$ lies in
$[0, \sigma_n^{-2}]$. On that interval the concave function $\log(1 + u)$ lies above its chord from 0 to
$\sigma_n^{-2}$, so $u \le \sigma_n^{-2}\log(1 + u)/\log(1 + \sigma_n^{-2})$. Multiplying by $\sigma_n^2$,

$$
\sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t) \le \frac{\log(1 + u_t)}{\log(1 + \sigma_n^{-2})}.
$$

The sum $\frac12\sum_t \log(1 + u_t)$ is exactly the information the observations $y_1, \dots, y_T$ carry about $f$
(derived below), and it is at most $\gamma_T$. So
$\sum_t \sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t) \le 2\gamma_T/\log(1 + \sigma_n^{-2})$ and

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t^2 \le \frac{8\beta_T\gamma_T}{\log(1 + \sigma_n^{-2})} = C_1\beta_T\gamma_T.
$$

**Step 4: from squares to the sum.** By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
$R_T = \sum_t r_t \cdot 1 \le \sqrt{T\sum_t r_t^2} \le \sqrt{C_1 T \beta_T \gamma_T}$.

<Derivation title="The information in the observations">
The <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between the observations $\yvec_T = (y_1, \dots,
y_T)$ and $f$ is $I(\yvec_T; f) = h(\yvec_T) - h(\yvec_T \mid f)$, with $h$ the
<NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>. Given $f$, the observations are $f(\lambdavec_t)$
plus independent noise, so $h(\yvec_T \mid f) = \frac{T}{2}\log(2\pi e\sigma_n^2)$.

By the chain rule, $h(\yvec_T) = \sum_t h(y_t \mid y_1, \dots, y_{t-1})$. Given the earlier observations, $y_t$ is
Gaussian with variance $\sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t) + \sigma_n^2$: the posterior variance of $f(\lambdavec_t)$ plus
the noise. A Gaussian of variance $v$ has entropy $\frac12\log(2\pi e v)$. Subtracting,

$$
I(\yvec_T; f) = \frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{\sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t) + \sigma_n^2}{\sigma_n^2}
= \frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\paren{1 + \sigma_n^{-2}\sigma_{t-1}^2(\lambdavec_t)}.
$$

The points $\lambdavec_t$ may depend on earlier observations, but each conditional distribution is still Gaussian with
that variance, so the identity holds for any sequential rule.
</Derivation>

## The maximum information gain

The bound depends on the kernel through the **maximum information gain**, the most that any $T$ noisy evaluations can
reveal about $f$:

$$
\gamma_T = \max_{A \subseteq \Lambda,\ \abs{A} = T} I(\yvec_A; f) = \max_{A} \frac12\log\det\paren{\eye + \sigma_n^{-2}\Kmat_A},
$$

where $\Kmat_A$ is the kernel matrix of the points in $A$. A kernel whose functions are smooth makes nearby values
nearly redundant, so $\gamma_T$ grows slowly. Known rates for inputs in $d$ dimensions are:

| kernel | $\gamma_T$ |
| --- | --- |
| linear | $O(d\log T)$ |
| squared exponential | $O\paren{(\log T)^{d+1}}$ |
| Matérn with smoothness $\nu$ | $O\paren{T^{d/(2\nu + d)}(\log T)^{2\nu/(2\nu + d)}}$ |

The first two are from Srinivas et al. <Cite id="srinivas2010" />. The Matérn rate is Vakili, Khezeli and Picheny's,
tight up to logarithmic factors <Cite id="vakili2021" />. In each case $\gamma_T$ grows more slowly than $T$, so
$R_T/T \le \sqrt{C_1\beta_T\gamma_T/T} \to 0$ and GP-UCB is no-regret. For the <Gloss name="rbf-kernel">squared
exponential kernel</Gloss> the dimension enters only through the power of $\log T$. For a
<NoteLink to="covariance-functions">Matérn kernel</NoteLink> the exponent $d/(2\nu + d)$ approaches 1 as $d$ grows,
which is one form of the curse of dimensionality in
Bayesian optimisation.

The analysis extends beyond a finite set. For a compact box in $d$ dimensions and a kernel whose sample paths are smooth
enough, $\beta_t$ gains a term of order $d\log t$. For a fixed function $f$ with a bounded norm in the kernel's
<NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space">reproducing kernel Hilbert space</NoteLink> and bounded noise,
$\beta_t = 2B + 300\gamma_t\log^3(t/\delta)$, with $B$ an upper bound on the squared norm <Cite id="srinivas2010" />.
Russo and Van Roy turned the same confidence-bound argument into a Bayesian regret bound for
<Gloss name="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</Gloss> <Cite id="russo2014" />.

## Worked example

**The schedule.** On a grid of $\abs{\Lambda} = 201$ candidates with $\delta = 0.1$,

$$
\beta_1 = 2\log\frac{201 \times 1 \times 9.87}{0.6} = 2\log 3306 = 16.2.
$$

At $t = 10$ the argument gains a factor of $10^2$ and $\beta_{10} = 16.2 + 2\log 100 = 25.4$; at $t = 100$,
$\beta_{100} = 16.2 + 2\log 10^4 = 34.6$. The bound therefore sits $4.0$, $5.0$ and $5.9$ standard deviations below
the mean. A step whose chosen point has posterior standard deviation $0.1$ has, by Step 2, regret at most
$2 \times 4.0 \times 0.1 = 0.8$ at $t = 1$.

**Regret in practice.** The figures below run the confidence bound on the Forrester function, minimum $-6.02$ at
$\lambda = 0.757$, with a shallow local minimum near $\lambda = 0.14$ <Cite id="forrester2008" />. Each run starts from
3 random evaluations and makes 20 more. Over seeds 1 to 5, the cumulative regret of those 20 evaluations was:

| exploration weight | mean cumulative regret | runs stuck in the local minimum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\beta = 0$ | 35.6 | 1 of 5 |
| $\beta = 1$ | 38.8 | 1 of 5 |
| $\beta = 4$ | 26.1 | 0 of 5 |
| schedule $\beta_t$, $\delta = 0.1$ | 52.9 | 0 of 5 |

A run stuck in the local minimum adds about 5 to the regret at every step, and its cumulative regret passes 100. The
theoretical schedule never gets stuck, but it keeps evaluating the ends of the interval and pays about twice the regret
of $\beta = 4$ over this short horizon. The bound's constants are set to hold for every function the prior can produce,
which makes them conservative for any one function. Srinivas et al. found by
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> that dividing their $\beta_t$ by 5 improved results
<Cite id="srinivas2010" />.

## Assumptions

- **The prior is right.** The finite-set theorem assumes that $f$ is drawn from the same Gaussian process the surrogate
  uses, with known kernel hyperparameters and noise. Hyperparameters refitted by
  <NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> break the assumption, and
  no guarantee covers them.
- **The bound's scale is known.** The RKHS version needs an upper bound $B$ on the function's norm, which is rarely
  known.
- **The acquisition is maximised exactly.** The analysis assumes the global minimiser of the lower bound is found at
  every step.

## Pitfalls

- **Using the theoretical $\beta_t$ verbatim.** It over-explores at practical budgets. A small constant, or the schedule
  scaled down, is the usual choice; the right value depends on how well calibrated the surrogate is.
- **Reading the bound as a stopping rule.** The bound involves $\gamma_T$ and constants that are not computed in
  practice. It shows that the method converges, not when to stop.
- **Ignoring dimension.** The guarantee weakens as $d$ grows. Bayesian optimisation with a plain Gaussian process works
  best with fewer than about 20 hyperparameters <Cite id="frazier2018" />, which motivates the methods of
  <NoteLink to="high-dimensional-bayesian-optimisation">high-dimensional Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>.
