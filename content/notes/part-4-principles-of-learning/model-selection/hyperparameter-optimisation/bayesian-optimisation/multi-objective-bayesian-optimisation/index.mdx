<Definition>
Let $f_1, \dots, f_M$ be $M$ objectives to minimise over configurations $\lambdavec \in \Lambda$, each expensive to
evaluate. A configuration $\lambdavec$ **dominates** $\lambdavec'$ if it is no worse in every objective and strictly
better in at least one. The **Pareto set** is the set of configurations that no configuration dominates, and its image
in objective space is the **Pareto front**. **Multi-objective Bayesian optimisation** approximates the Pareto front in
few evaluations. It fits a surrogate to each objective and chooses each evaluation by an acquisition such as the
expected increase of the **hypervolume** dominated by the evaluations so far <Cite id="emmerich2006" />, or by
<NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected improvement</NoteLink> on a randomly weighted scalarisation of the
objectives (ParEGO) <Cite id="knowles2006parego" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Hyperparameters often trade one goal against another. A larger network has lower validation error and higher
latency. A higher quantisation rate saves memory and loses accuracy (see <NoteLink to="model-quantisation">model
quantisation</NoteLink>). <NoteLink to="neural-architecture-search">Architecture search</NoteLink> for devices scores
accuracy, latency and size together.

A single weighted sum $\sum_m w_m f_m$ needs the weights before the search, when the trade-off is still unknown. It also
cannot reach every Pareto-optimal configuration, as the worked example shows. Running a separate search for each of
several weightings multiplies the cost. The goal is one search whose evaluations spread along the whole front, so the
trade-off can be chosen afterwards from evaluated configurations.

## Mechanism

### The hypervolume indicator

The quality of a set of evaluated points needs one number before an acquisition can score improvements to it. Fix a
**reference point** $\rvec \in \reals^M$ that every point of interest beats in every objective, such as the worst
acceptable error and latency. The **hypervolume** of a set $\Pcal$ of points in objective space is the volume of the
region they dominate and $\rvec$ bounds,

$$
\operatorname{HV}(\Pcal) = \operatorname{vol}\paren{\bigcup_{\yvec \in \Pcal} [y_1, r_1] \times \cdots \times
[y_M, r_M]}
$$

<Cite id="zitzler1999" />. Adding a point that the set already dominates adds no volume, and adding a non-dominated
point inside the reference box adds some. A larger hypervolume therefore means a front that is closer to the ideal,
wider, or both.

**Two objectives.** Sort the non-dominated points by the first objective, $y_{1,1} < y_{1,2} < \dots < y_{1,n}$. Being
mutually non-dominated, they are then sorted in decreasing order of the second objective. Between $y_{1,i}$ and the next
value $y_{1,i+1}$ (with $y_{1,n+1} = r_1$), the points dominating a vertical slice are points $1$ to $i$, and the lowest
of them in the second objective is point $i$. The slice is dominated from $y_{2,i}$ up to $r_2$, so

$$
\operatorname{HV}(\Pcal) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \paren{y_{1,i+1} - y_{1,i}}\paren{r_2 - y_{2,i}},
$$

a staircase of rectangles computed in $O(n\log n)$ time for the sort.

### Expected hypervolume improvement

The **hypervolume improvement** of a new outcome $\yvec$ over the current front $\Pcal_t$ is
$\operatorname{HVI}(\yvec) = \operatorname{HV}(\Pcal_t \cup \set{\yvec}) - \operatorname{HV}(\Pcal_t)$. The outcome of a
candidate is unknown, with posterior $\fvec(\lambdavec) = (f_1(\lambdavec), \dots, f_M(\lambdavec))$ from one surrogate
per objective. The **expected hypervolume improvement** is

$$
\operatorname{EHVI}_t(\lambdavec) = \expect\brack{\operatorname{HVI}\paren{\fvec(\lambdavec)} \mid \Dcal_t}
$$

<Cite id="emmerich2006" />. It is the multi-objective form of expected improvement. With one objective,
$\Pcal_t$ is the incumbent $f^\star_t$, its hypervolume is the length $r - f^\star_t$, and adding $y$ lengthens it by
$\max(f^\star_t - y, 0)$ when $y \le r$. So for $r$ above every plausible value,
$\operatorname{HVI}(y) = \max(f^\star_t - y, 0)$ and EHVI equals EI.

With independent Gaussian posteriors per objective, EHVI has closed forms that split the non-dominated region into
boxes and integrate over each. Their cost grows quickly with the number of objectives, partly because computing the
hypervolume itself does <Cite id="daulton2020" />. Daulton, Balandat and Bakshy estimate it by
<NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo</NoteLink> over posterior samples instead, which extends it to
batches of $q$ points (qEHVI, see <NoteLink to="batch-bayesian-optimisation">batch Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>)
and gives exact gradients of the estimate for optimising the acquisition <Cite id="daulton2020" />. BoTorch implements
it <Cite id="balandat2020" />.

### ParEGO: random scalarisations

ParEGO turns the problem into a sequence of single-objective ones <Cite id="knowles2006parego" />. Each iteration:

1. Normalise every objective to $[0, 1]$.
2. Draw a weight vector $\wvec$ with $w_m \ge 0$ and $\sum_m w_m = 1$ from an evenly spread set.
3. Scalarise every evaluation by the **augmented Chebyshev function**
   $$
   s_{\wvec}(\yvec) = \max_m w_m y_m + \rho\sum_m w_m y_m, \qquad \rho = 0.05.
   $$
4. Fit one <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> to the scalarised values and evaluate the configuration with the
   largest expected improvement.

Each weight vector aims the search at one part of the front, and the random weights spread the evaluations along it.
The small linear term breaks ties between points that share the maximum term, so a weakly dominated point never scores
as well as the point that dominates it.

**Why the maximum and not the sum.** Let $\pvec$ be on the Pareto front with positive coordinates, and choose weights
$w_m \propto 1/p_m$, so that $w_m p_m = c$ for every $m$. If some point $\qvec$ had $\max_m w_m q_m < c$, then
$w_m q_m < w_m p_m$, hence $q_m < p_m$, for every $m$: $\qvec$ would dominate $\pvec$, a contradiction. So $\pvec$
minimises $\max_m w_m y_m$, and every Pareto point is reachable by some weighting. A weighted sum has no such
guarantee: a Pareto point that lies above the straight segment joining two others has a larger weighted sum than one of
those two, for every choice of positive weights.

## Worked example

A search trades validation error $f_1$ against latency $f_2$ in milliseconds. The reference point is
$\rvec = (0.30, 100)$. The evaluations so far have the non-dominated outcomes

$$
\pvec_1 = (0.10, 80), \qquad \pvec_2 = (0.15, 40), \qquad \pvec_3 = (0.25, 20).
$$

**Step 1: the hypervolume.** The points are sorted by error, and their latencies fall. The three rectangles are
$(0.15 - 0.10)(100 - 80) = 1.0$, $(0.25 - 0.15)(100 - 40) = 6.0$ and $(0.30 - 0.25)(100 - 20) = 4.0$, so
$\operatorname{HV} = 11.0$ (units of error times milliseconds).

**Step 2: a new outcome.** A candidate returns $\qvec = (0.12, 50)$. It beats $\pvec_1$ on latency and $\pvec_2$ on
error, so nothing dominates it. With it the staircase has four steps: $0.02 \times 20 = 0.4$, $0.03 \times 50 = 1.5$,
$0.10 \times 60 = 6.0$ and $0.05 \times 80 = 4.0$, a total of $11.9$. The improvement is $0.9$, the area of the box
$[0.12, 0.15] \times [50, 80]$ that only $\qvec$ dominates. An outcome such as $(0.16, 45)$, dominated by $\pvec_2$,
adds nothing.

**Step 3: a point no weighted sum finds.** Replace $\pvec_2$ by $\pvec_2' = (0.20, 55)$, still dominated by neither
neighbour. The segment from $\pvec_1$ to $\pvec_3$ passes through latency 40 at error 0.20, below 55. So $\pvec_2'$ lies
above the segment, and for any positive weights its weighted sum exceeds that of $\pvec_1$ or $\pvec_3$. With
Chebyshev weights proportional to $(1/0.20, 1/55)$, the maximum term is $1$ for $\pvec_2'$, $\max(0.5, 1.45) = 1.45$
for $\pvec_1$ and $\max(1.25, 0.36) = 1.25$ for $\pvec_3$, so $\pvec_2'$ wins.

## Cost

Each objective needs its own surrogate, so model fitting grows linearly in $M$. ParEGO's acquisition costs one EI per
candidate. Exact EHVI costs grow with the number of points on the front and steeply with $M$. Monte Carlo EHVI costs
one hypervolume-improvement computation per posterior sample per candidate <Cite id="daulton2020" />. Both remain small
next to training runs for two or three objectives.

## Trade-offs

- **EHVI against ParEGO.** EHVI scores the whole front at once and is the stronger acquisition when it is affordable.
  ParEGO needs only single-objective machinery and one surrogate, and scales to many objectives, but each iteration
  serves one weighting.
- **Pareto front against constraints.** When a limit is fixed in advance, such as latency under 10 ms, a
  <NoteLink to="constrained-bayesian-optimisation">constrained search</NoteLink> spends no evaluations on the parts of
  the front that break it.
- **Few objectives.** With four or more objectives almost every point is non-dominated, the front is a large surface,
  and a fixed budget covers it sparsely.

## Pitfalls

- **The reference point steers the search.** Hypervolume counts only the region below $\rvec$. A reference point that is
  too generous rewards extreme points nobody wants. One that is too strict ignores points just outside it.
- **Scales.** Hypervolume and scalarisations depend on the units of each objective. Normalise them, or state the
  reference point in meaningful units.
- **An optimistic front.** Every point on the reported front is the best of many evaluations on validation data in its
  part of the trade-off, so the front is biased towards the origin, as a single best score is (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Reassess the chosen
  configuration on held-out data and measure latency on the deployment hardware.
- **Noisy objectives.** Latency and accuracy measurements vary between runs, and a point that looks non-dominated by
  chance displaces a genuinely better one. Repeated measurements or noise-aware acquisitions are needed when the noise
  is comparable to the trade-off.
