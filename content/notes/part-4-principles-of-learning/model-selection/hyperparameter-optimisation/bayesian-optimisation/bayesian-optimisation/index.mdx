<Definition>
**Bayesian optimisation** minimises an objective $f(\lambdavec)$ over a
<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink> $\Lambda$ when each evaluation is expensive, noisy
and gives no gradient. It keeps a probabilistic **surrogate** of $f$, fitted to every evaluation so far, and chooses the
next evaluation by maximising an **acquisition function** $\alpha_t(\lambdavec)$ computed from the surrogate's
posterior. In machine learning the objective is usually the validation loss of a model trained with hyperparameters
$\lambdavec$ <Cite id="snoek2012" /> <Cite id="garnett2023" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Training a model with one setting of its hyperparameters can take hours. A budget of a few dozen trainings rules out a
fine grid and makes every trial count. <NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> ignores what earlier trials
showed. Bayesian optimisation uses it: a trial that scored badly makes its neighbourhood less attractive, and a region
with no trials stays attractive because it is uncertain.

The method pays when an evaluation costs far more than the bookkeeping around it. That means expensive trainings, few
hyperparameters (it works best below about 20 dimensions <Cite id="frazier2018" />) and budgets of tens to hundreds of
trials. The same loop tunes engineering designs evaluated by slow simulations, where it became standard as "efficient
global optimization" <Cite id="jones1998" />.

## Main ideas

**The loop.** Every method in this topic runs the same cycle <Cite id="shahriari2016" />:

1. Evaluate an initial design of a few points, chosen at random or by a space-filling design such as a Latin hypercube
   (see <NoteLink to="quasi-random-search">quasi-random search</NoteLink>).
2. Fit the surrogate to the evaluations $\Dcal_t = \set{(\lambdavec_i, y_i)}_{i=1}^{t}$, where $y_i = f(\lambdavec_i) +
   \varepsilon_i$ includes noise. With a Gaussian process this gives a posterior mean $\mu_t(\lambdavec)$ and standard
   deviation $\sigma_t(\lambdavec)$ at every candidate.
3. Choose $\lambdavec_{t+1} = \argmax_{\lambdavec} \alpha_t(\lambdavec)$, an inner optimisation over a function that is
   cheap to evaluate.
4. Evaluate $y_{t+1}$, add it to the data, and return to step 2 until the budget is spent.
5. Recommend a configuration: the best observed, $f^\star_t = \min_i y_i$, or the minimiser of the posterior mean.

The walk-through in <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition functions</NoteLink> runs this loop one evaluation
at a time.

**The surrogate.** A <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> is the standard surrogate. It gives
calibrated error bars from few points, and its kernel hyperparameters are refitted by
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> at every step, with a Matérn 5/2
kernel and <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">one length-scale per hyperparameter</NoteLink> as the usual
defaults <Cite id="snoek2012" />. <NoteLink to="random-forest">Random forests</NoteLink>
(<NoteLink to="sequential-model-based-algorithm-configuration">SMAC</NoteLink>) and density estimates
(<NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">TPE</NoteLink>) replace it for mixed, conditional or large search
spaces.

**The acquisition function.** The acquisition trades exploitation, evaluating where the posterior mean is low, against
exploration, evaluating where it is uncertain. <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">Expected improvement</NoteLink>
averages the gain over the incumbent and is the common default.
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-upper-confidence-bound">GP-UCB</NoteLink> takes an optimistic bound and carries a regret
guarantee. Thompson sampling minimises a posterior draw. <NoteLink to="entropy-search">Entropy search</NoteLink> chooses
the evaluation that teaches most about the optimum.

**Extensions of the loop.** Parallel workers need several proposals at once
(<NoteLink to="batch-bayesian-optimisation">batch Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>). Many hyperparameters need structure
in the surrogate (<NoteLink to="high-dimensional-bayesian-optimisation">high-dimensional Bayesian
optimisation</NoteLink>). Unknown feasibility, such as a model that runs out of memory, needs a model of the constraint
(<NoteLink to="constrained-bayesian-optimisation">constrained Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>). Several objectives,
such as accuracy and latency, need a Pareto front (<NoteLink to="multi-objective-bayesian-optimisation">multi-objective
Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>).

**Data reuse.** The search evaluates many configurations on the same validation data and keeps the best. The best
validation loss is therefore biased low, as in every search
(<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). Bayesian optimisation
sharpens the effect, because it concentrates evaluations where earlier values were low, including values that were low
by noise. The error of the recommended configuration needs data the search never saw: a test set or
<NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition functions</NoteLink>, which compares the rules on one
posterior and on a full run. <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">Expected improvement</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process-upper-confidence-bound">GP-UCB</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="entropy-search">entropy
search</NoteLink> each derive one family. The surrogates come next:
<NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">TPE</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="sequential-model-based-algorithm-configuration">SMAC</NoteLink> trade the Gaussian process for models that
scale to mixed spaces and many trials. The batch, high-dimensional, constrained and multi-objective notes extend the
loop. <NoteLink to="tuning-a-support-vector-machine-by-bayesian-optimisation">Tuning a support vector machine by
Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> runs it against random search at an equal budget.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The surrogate is a Gaussian process, so <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> regression and its
<NoteLink to="covariance-functions">covariance functions</NoteLink> come first, with the problem setting of
<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink>. The bandit notes of Part VII, especially
<NoteLink to="upper-confidence-bound-algorithm">UCB1</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson
sampling</NoteLink>, are the discrete-arm versions of the same trade-off.
<NoteLink to="multi-fidelity-hyperparameter-optimisation">Multi-fidelity hyperparameter optimisation</NoteLink> cuts the
cost of each evaluation by stopping poor trials early, and
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink> combines the two approaches. Derivative-free
optimisers such as <NoteLink to="evolution-strategies-and-covariance-matrix-adaptation">CMA-ES</NoteLink> are the
alternative when evaluations are cheap enough to spend by the thousand.
