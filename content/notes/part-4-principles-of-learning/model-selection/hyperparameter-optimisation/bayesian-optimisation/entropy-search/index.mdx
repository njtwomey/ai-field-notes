<Definition>
**Entropy search** is a family of <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition functions</NoteLink> for
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> that score a candidate $\lambdavec$ by the
<NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between its observation $y$ and an unknown property of
the optimum, given the evaluations $\Dcal_t$ so far. For minimising $f$ the property is either the minimiser
$\lambdavec^\star = \argmin f$ or the minimum value $f^\star = \min f$:

$$
\alpha^{\text{ES}}_t(\lambdavec) = I\paren{y; \lambdavec^\star \mid \Dcal_t, \lambdavec}, \qquad
\alpha^{\text{MES}}_t(\lambdavec) = I\paren{y; f^\star \mid \Dcal_t, \lambdavec}.
$$

Entropy search (ES) and predictive entropy search (PES) target the location <Cite id="hennig2012" />
<Cite id="hernandezlobato2014" />. Max-value entropy search (MES) targets the value <Cite id="wang2017mes" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<Gloss name="ei">Expected improvement</Gloss> and the confidence bound look for good values now. Entropy search looks
for evaluations that teach where the optimum is. The two aims differ. A candidate whose value is almost surely mediocre
can still be informative, if its value would separate two hypotheses about where the minimum lies. A candidate whose
value is almost surely good may teach nothing new, if the posterior already knows it.

The posterior over $f$ implies a distribution over the minimiser, $p(\lambdavec^\star \mid \Dcal_t)$. Early in a search
that distribution is spread over $\Lambda$; at the end it should be concentrated on one small region. Its
<NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> measures the remaining uncertainty. Entropy search chooses the evaluation that
is expected to shrink that entropy the most. The same idea chooses queries in
<NoteLink to="bayesian-active-learning">Bayesian active learning</NoteLink>, where the unknown is the model rather than
the optimum.

The target suits <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink>. A search is judged by the
configuration it recommends at the end, not by the sum of the losses it evaluated on the way. Information about the
optimum is what improves the final recommendation.

## Two ways to write the information

Mutual information is symmetric, $I(y; \lambdavec^\star) = I(\lambdavec^\star; y)$, so the acquisition has two
expansions. Writing $\entropy$ for entropy, with everything conditioned on $\Dcal_t$ and the candidate $\lambdavec$:

$$
I(y; \lambdavec^\star) = \entropy\brack{p(\lambdavec^\star)} - \expect_{y}\entropy\brack{p(\lambdavec^\star \mid y)}
= \entropy\brack{p(y)} - \expect_{\lambdavec^\star}\entropy\brack{p(y \mid \lambdavec^\star)}.
$$

**Entropy search** uses the first form: the entropy of the minimiser's distribution now, less its expected entropy after
the observation <Cite id="hennig2012" />. Both terms involve $p(\lambdavec^\star \mid \Dcal_t)$, a distribution over the
whole <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink> with no closed form. Hennig and Schuler
approximate it on a set of representer points with <NoteLink to="expectation-propagation">expectation
propagation</NoteLink>, and approximate the expectation over $y$. The method is accurate but expensive, and the cost
grows quickly with the dimension.

**Predictive entropy search** uses the second form <Cite id="hernandezlobato2014" />. The first term is the entropy of
the one-dimensional predictive distribution of $y$, which is Gaussian. The second needs the predictive distribution of
$y$ given that the minimiser is at $\lambdavec^\star$, averaged over samples of $\lambdavec^\star$. The samples are the
minimisers of approximate posterior draws built on <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">random Fourier
features</NoteLink>, as in <NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</NoteLink>. The conditioning is
approximated by expectation propagation. Every entropy is now of a one-dimensional distribution, which is cheaper and
more stable than ES.

## Max-value entropy search

Wang and Jegelka replaced the minimiser $\lambdavec^\star \in \Lambda$ by the minimum value $f^\star \in \reals$, a
single number <Cite id="wang2017mes" />. With noise-free observations $y = f(\lambdavec)$,

$$
\alpha^{\text{MES}}_t(\lambdavec) = \entropy\brack{p(y \mid \Dcal_t, \lambdavec)}
- \expect_{f^\star}\entropy\brack{p(y \mid \Dcal_t, \lambdavec, f^\star)}.
$$

The first distribution is the Gaussian posterior $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, with $\mu = \mu_t(\lambdavec)$ and
$\sigma = \sigma_t(\lambdavec)$. Knowing that the minimum of $f$ is $f^\star$ tells, at least, that
$f(\lambdavec) \ge f^\star$. MES keeps only that constraint, so the second distribution is the Gaussian truncated to
$[f^\star, \infty)$. Both entropies then have closed forms.

**Step 1: the truncated density.** Write $u = (f - \mu)/\sigma$ and $\gamma = (\mu - f^\star)/\sigma$, the number of
standard deviations by which the mean lies above the minimum. The constraint $f \ge f^\star$ is $u \ge -\gamma$, which
has probability $1 - \Phi(-\gamma) = \Phi(\gamma)$. The truncated density is

$$
q(f) = \frac{\varphi(u)}{\sigma\,\Phi(\gamma)}, \qquad f \ge f^\star.
$$

**Step 2: its entropy.** Since $-\log q(f) = \log\sigma + \log\Phi(\gamma) + \frac12\log 2\pi + \frac12 u^2$,

$$
\entropy[q] = \log\sigma + \log\Phi(\gamma) + \tfrac12\log 2\pi + \tfrac12\expect_q[u^2].
$$

**Step 3: the second moment.** Integrating by parts with $\varphi'(u) = -u\varphi(u)$, $\int_{-\gamma}^{\infty} u^2\varphi(u)\,du = \brack{-u\varphi(u)}_{-\gamma}^{\infty} + \int_{-\gamma}^{\infty}\varphi(u)\,du
= -\gamma\varphi(\gamma) + \Phi(\gamma)$, using $\varphi(-\gamma) = \varphi(\gamma)$. Dividing by the mass
$\Phi(\gamma)$,

$$
\expect_q[u^2] = 1 - \frac{\gamma\,\varphi(\gamma)}{\Phi(\gamma)}.
$$

**Step 4: the drop in entropy.** The untruncated Gaussian has entropy $\log\sigma + \frac12\log(2\pi e)$
(<NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>). Subtracting Step 2 with Step 3 inserted,

$$
\entropy\brack{\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)} - \entropy[q] = \frac{\gamma\,\varphi(\gamma)}{2\,\Phi(\gamma)} - \log\Phi(\gamma).
$$

Averaging over $K$ samples $f^\star_1, \dots, f^\star_K$ of the minimum gives the acquisition,

$$
\alpha^{\text{MES}}_t(\lambdavec) \approx \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\brack{\frac{\gamma_k\varphi(\gamma_k)}{2\Phi(\gamma_k)}
- \log\Phi(\gamma_k)}, \qquad \gamma_k = \frac{\mu_t(\lambdavec) - f^\star_k}{\sigma_t(\lambdavec)}.
$$

Each term is non-negative. Truncation shrinks the variance, and among densities with a given variance the Gaussian has
the largest entropy, so the truncated density has less entropy than the Gaussian it came from. A candidate scores highly
when its posterior reaches down to the sampled minima, so that the constraint cuts off a large part of its distribution.

<MaxValueInformation />

**Sampling the minimum.** The samples $f^\star_k$ come from the posterior of $\min f$. One way draws functions from the
posterior and takes their minima. Wang and Jegelka's cheaper way treats the posterior values on a dense set of
candidates as independent, so that $\prob(\min f > y) \approx \prod_i \paren{1 - \Phi((y - \mu_i)/\sigma_i)}$. They fit
a Gumbel distribution to this function at its quartiles and sample from the Gumbel by inversion
<Cite id="wang2017mes" />. Samples above the incumbent are moved down to it, since the minimum cannot exceed a value
already observed.

**A link to the probability of improvement.** The score for one sample depends on $\gamma$ alone and falls steadily as
$\gamma$ grows, as the right panel of the figure shows. Maximising it over candidates is therefore the same as
minimising $\gamma$, that is, maximising $\Phi(-\gamma) = \prob(f(\lambdavec) < f^\star)$. With a single sample, MES is
the probability of improvement with the target $f^\star$ <Cite id="wang2017mes" />. Averaging over several samples
gives a rule with no exact equivalent among the simpler acquisitions.

## Worked example

The posterior has three samples of the minimum, $f^\star \in \set{-1.2, -1.0, -0.9}$, and the incumbent is $-0.9$. Four
candidates have posterior means and standard deviations

| candidate | $\mu$ | $\sigma$ | $\gamma$ for the three samples | MES | EI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $-0.80$ | $0.10$ | $4.0,\ 2.0,\ 1.0$ | $0.132$ | $0.008$ |
| B | $0$ | $1.00$ | $1.2,\ 1.0,\ 0.9$ | $0.307$ | $0.100$ |
| C | $-0.50$ | $0.40$ | $1.75,\ 1.25,\ 1.0$ | $0.225$ | $0.033$ |
| D | $-0.95$ | $0.05$ | $5.0,\ 1.0,\ -1.0$ | $0.465$ | $0.054$ |

**One term by hand.** For $\gamma = 1$, $\varphi(1) = 0.2420$ and $\Phi(1) = 0.8413$. The first part is
$0.2420/(2 \times 0.8413) = 0.1438$ and the second is $-\log 0.8413 = 0.1728$, so the term is $0.3166$ nats. Every
candidate has one sample with $\gamma = 1$ and so shares this term.

**Candidate D.** Its sample $f^\star = -0.9$ gives $\gamma = -1$, with $\Phi(-1) = 0.1587$. The term is
$(-1)(0.2420)/(2 \times 0.1587) - \log 0.1587 = -0.7625 + 1.8409 = 1.0785$. The posterior puts 84% of D's mass below
$-0.9$, and that sample says the minimum is no lower than $-0.9$: the constraint would cut away most of the
distribution. The sample at $-1.2$ gives $\gamma = 5$ and a term of essentially zero. The average is
$(0.0000 + 0.3166 + 1.0785)/3 = 0.465$.

**The choice.** MES chooses D, whose value would settle whether the minimum lies near $-0.95$ or further down. EI with
incumbent $-0.9$ chooses B, the wide posterior whose lower tail could reach far below the incumbent. The two rules
answer different questions: where to look for a low value, and which observation tells most about how low the minimum
is.

## Assumptions

- **A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian-process</Gloss> posterior with calibrated variance.** Every entropy is computed from it.
- **The truncation approximation.** Conditioning on $f^\star$ changes the posterior everywhere, not only at the
  candidate. MES keeps only $f(\lambdavec) \ge f^\star$ at the candidate. The Gumbel sampler also treats correlated
  values as independent, which makes the sampled minima lower than the posterior implies.
- **Noise-free observations.** The derivation truncates the value $f(\lambdavec)$, not the noisy observation $y$. With
  substantial noise the score needs a correction, which later work supplies <Cite id="garnett2023" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Few samples of the optimum.** With small $K$ the acquisition is noisy and changes from step to step. With $K = 1$
  MES is the probability of improvement against a random target.
- **Cost of ES and PES.** Both need expectation propagation and many samples, and are slow beyond a few dimensions. MES
  costs about as much as expected improvement once the samples are drawn.
- **Recommending the best observation.** An information-based search evaluates points to learn, not to score well, so
  its best observed value can lag behind. The recommendation at the end is the minimiser of the posterior mean, which
  also avoids reporting a value that is low only by noise.
