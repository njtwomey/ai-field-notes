<Definition>
An RBF <Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss> separates two iris species, versicolor and virginica
<Cite id="fisher1936" />. Its kernel is $k(\xvec, \xvec') = \exp(-\gamma\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^2)$ and its penalty on
margin violations is $C$. The validation error $f(\lambdavec)$, with $\lambdavec = (\log_{10} C, \log_{10}\gamma)$ on
the box $[-2, 3] \times [-3, 2]$, is the error of ten repeats of stratified 5-fold cross-validation, tabulated on a
21 by 21 grid and interpolated between nodes. Its lowest value is $0.040$, at $\log_{10} C = 1$ and
$\log_{10}\gamma = -2$. With a budget of $N = 30$ evaluations, compare Gaussian-process expected improvement with random
search: which configuration does each return, how far is its true error from $0.040$, and how much does the answer vary
with the seed?
</Definition>

## Setup

- **Search space.** Both hyperparameters are searched on a log scale, as is standard for SVMs
  <Cite id="hsu2003" /> (see <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search spaces</NoteLink>).
- **Objective.** $f$ is the tabulated cross-validated error, so an evaluation costs nothing here and the true error of
  any configuration is known. In a real search each evaluation is a cross-validation run.
- **Bayesian optimisation.** The surrogate is a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> with a Matérn 5/2 kernel on
  the box rescaled to the unit square, with targets standardised. Its length-scale and variance are refitted by
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-process-hyperparameter-learning">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> before every choice. The first
  5 evaluations form a Latin hypercube. Each later evaluation maximises <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected
  improvement</NoteLink> over a 41 by 41 grid of candidates, which contains every node of the table. The incumbent
  $f^\star_t$ is the best value observed after $t$ evaluations.
- **Random search.** <NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> draws 30 configurations uniformly on the box
  and keeps the best <Cite id="bergstra2012" />.
- **Score.** For each method and each budget $t \le 30$, the true error of the configuration it would return. Each
  method runs with seeds 1 to 20.

## Solution

### One run, step by step

Seed 1 shows how the search behaves.

1. **Initial design.** The five evaluations are spread over the box. The best is $0.066$ at
   $(\log_{10} C, \log_{10}\gamma) = (-1.71, -0.60)$. The worst, $0.418$ at $(0.77, 1.67)$, is in the overfitting
   region of large $\gamma$.
2. **A short length-scale first.** Fitted to five points, the length-scale is $0.05$ of the box's side, so the
   posterior is uncertain almost everywhere and expected improvement peaks next to the incumbent. Evaluation 6, at
   $(-1.5, -0.75)$, gives $0.070$.
3. **Exploration.** With six points the length-scale jumps to $0.28$. Evaluations 7 and 8 go to the edges of the box,
   $(3, -0.5)$ and $(-2, -2)$, where the posterior is widest, and return $0.077$ and $0.114$.
4. **Finding the valley.** Evaluation 9, at $(2.25, -3)$, returns $0.048$ and becomes the incumbent. It lies in the
   diagonal valley of good settings, where a larger $C$ pairs with a smaller $\gamma$.
5. **Refining.** Evaluations 10 to 13 test the edge of the box at large $C$. Evaluation 14, at $(1.63, -2.5)$, gives
   $0.0455$. Evaluation 16, at $(1, -2.125)$, gives $0.043$, one grid step from the best node.
6. **Diminishing returns.** The remaining 14 evaluations refine around the valley and make occasional trips to unvisited
   edges. None improves on $0.043$. The largest expected improvement falls from $0.029$ at the first choice to
   $0.0002$ at the last: the surrogate expects little more from the budget.

Random search with seed 1 finds $0.049$ on its third trial and nothing better in the next 27.

### Twenty seeds

The mean over 20 seeds of the true error of the returned configuration, by budget:

| evaluations $t$ | expected improvement | random search | seeds where EI is better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 0.0570 | 0.0678 | 11 of 20 |
| 10 | 0.0466 | 0.0523 | 16 of 20 |
| 15 | 0.0447 | 0.0507 | 18 of 20 |
| 20 | 0.0430 | 0.0497 | 18 of 20 |
| 30 | 0.0418 | 0.0479 | 19 of 20 |

At 5 evaluations both methods have only their initial designs. The Latin hypercube spreads its five points over the box
and beats five uniform draws on average, $0.0570$ against $0.0678$, though the uniform draws do better in 9 of the 20
seeds. After that expected improvement pulls further ahead. Every one of its 20 runs reaches an error of $0.045$ or less
within 30 evaluations, after a median of 11.5. Random search reaches $0.045$ in 7 of its 20 runs. At 30 evaluations
expected improvement is within $0.0018$ of the best error on average, and random search within $0.0079$.

<SvmSearchStudy />

The surface is easy for random search. About 5% of the box has error below $0.050$, within $0.01$ of the minimum (see
<NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink>), so 30 random trials land there with probability
$1 - 0.95^{30} = 0.79$. The gain from a surrogate grows as the good region shrinks and as evaluations become more
expensive.

### Noisy evaluations

A real cross-validation score is noisy. With noise of standard deviation $0.02$ added to each evaluation, about the
binomial <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> $\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95/100} = 0.022$ of an
<NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink> near $0.05$ measured on 100 examples, and with the
Gaussian process now fitting a noise variance, the means over 20 seeds become:

| evaluations $t$ | EI, true error | EI, observed best | random, true error | random, observed best |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 0.0550 | 0.0283 | 0.0593 | 0.0340 |
| 20 | 0.0574 | 0.0223 | 0.0560 | 0.0277 |
| 30 | 0.0532 | 0.0165 | 0.0555 | 0.0230 |

Both methods return the configuration with the lowest observed score, which is now often a lucky draw. The reported
best score falls with every evaluation while the true error of the returned configuration barely improves. At 30
evaluations expected improvement reports $0.0165$ for a configuration whose true error is $0.0532$. Set the noise to
$0.02$ in the figure above to see the two curves part. The optimism is larger
for expected improvement than for random search, because it re-evaluates near low scores, including scores that were
low by chance.

Recommending instead the configuration with the lowest posterior mean, rather than the lowest observed score, gives a
true error of $0.052$ at 30 evaluations, averaged over the 20 seeds, with a posterior mean there of $0.042$. The
posterior mean averages neighbouring evaluations and shrinks lucky scores, so it is both the better choice and the less
optimistic estimate. It is still optimistic.

## What it illustrates

- **A surrogate uses earlier scores.** Expected improvement concentrates evaluations along the valley it has found;
  random search keeps sampling the whole box. The surrogate's advantage is largest at moderate budgets, as in
  <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> generally <Cite id="snoek2012" />.
- **The exploration weight comes from the posterior.** Early evaluations go to the edges of the box because the
  posterior spread is largest there. The balance is set by the closed form of
  <NoteLink to="expected-improvement">expected improvement</NoteLink> <Cite id="jones1998" />.
- **Refitting matters.** The length-scale fitted to five points is far too short. Refitting it at every step corrects it
  within one evaluation here, but an overconfident length-scale can stop expected improvement from exploring at all
  <Cite id="bull2011" />.
- **Compare over seeds.** A single run can favour either method: random search beat expected improvement at 10
  evaluations in 4 of 20 seeds. Comparisons need several seeds and equal budgets <Cite id="dodge2019" /> (see
  <NoteLink to="tuning-budgets-in-comparisons">tuning budgets in comparisons</NoteLink>).
- **Selection makes scores optimistic.** The best observed score of any search is biased low, and an adaptive search
  more so (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). The error of
  the tuned SVM needs data the search never used, such as a test set or <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
  cross-validation</NoteLink>.

## Variations

- **A finer budget question.** Stopping when the largest expected improvement falls below a threshold, such as $0.001$,
  would have ended the seed-1 run after 21 evaluations with the same result.
- **A grid.** A <NoteLink to="grid-search">grid</NoteLink> of 30 points, such as 5 by 6, tests five values of $C$ and
  six of $\gamma$, and whether it hits the valley depends on where its lines fall.
- **More hyperparameters.** Adding hyperparameters that do not affect the error leaves random search unchanged, since
  its projection onto $(C, \gamma)$ is the same. A Gaussian process with one length-scale per hyperparameter can learn
  that they do not matter, but needs evaluations to do so (see
  <NoteLink to="high-dimensional-bayesian-optimisation">high-dimensional Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink>).
- **Parallel workers.** With four workers, the batch methods of <NoteLink to="batch-bayesian-optimisation">batch
  Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> propose four configurations per round.
