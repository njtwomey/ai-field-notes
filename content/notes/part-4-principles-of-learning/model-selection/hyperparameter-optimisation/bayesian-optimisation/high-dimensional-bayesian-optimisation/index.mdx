<Definition>
**High-dimensional Bayesian optimisation** is <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> of
an objective $f(\lambdavec)$ over $D$ hyperparameters, with $D$ in the tens to thousands, under an assumption that
makes the surrogate learnable from few evaluations. The common assumptions are that $f$ varies only within a
low-dimensional subspace, searched through a random linear embedding (REMBO) <Cite id="wang2016rembo" />; that $f$ is a
sum of functions of small groups of hyperparameters <Cite id="kandasamy2015" />; or that a local model is enough, kept
inside a trust region that moves with the incumbent (TuRBO) <Cite id="eriksson2019turbo" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> predicts well only near its evaluations, within about one length-scale in
each coordinate. With length-scale 0.2 on the unit cube, a lattice with one point per length-scale needs $5^D$ points:
25 in two dimensions and about 9.8 million in ten. A budget of a few hundred evaluations therefore leaves almost every
candidate far from the data, where the posterior mean is the prior mean and the spread is the prior spread.
<NoteLink to="expected-improvement">Expected improvement</NoteLink> then rewards candidates for being far from the data,
and in many dimensions almost every candidate is. Most of the volume of a cube is also near its boundary: in 20
dimensions $1 - 0.9^{20} = 88\%$ of $[0, 1]^{20}$ lies within 0.05 of a face. A search led by uncertainty spends its
budget on corners and faces <Cite id="hvarfner2024" />.

Machine learning problems rarely use all $D$ directions equally. Bergstra and Bengio found that the validation error of
neural networks depended strongly on only a few of their hyperparameters, and which few varied by dataset
<Cite id="bergstra2012" />. That low effective dimensionality is why <NoteLink to="random-search">random
search</NoteLink> beats a grid, and the methods below use it, or a weaker structure, to make a surrogate learnable.

## Details

### Low effective dimensionality and random embeddings

A function $f : \reals^D \to \reals$ has **effective dimensionality** $d_e$ if there is a subspace $\Tcal$ of
dimension $d_e$ such that $f(\xvec_\top + \xvec_\perp) = f(\xvec_\top)$ for every $\xvec_\top \in \Tcal$ and every
$\xvec_\perp$ orthogonal to $\Tcal$, and no smaller subspace has this property <Cite id="wang2016rembo" />. The
hyperparameters that do not matter, or combinations of them, span the directions orthogonal to $\Tcal$.

Random embedding Bayesian optimisation (REMBO) draws a matrix $\Amat \in \reals^{D \times d}$ with independent standard
normal entries, $d \ge d_e$, and optimises the $d$-dimensional function $g(\yvec) = f(\Amat\yvec)$ by ordinary Bayesian
optimisation <Cite id="wang2016rembo" />. The search never needs to know $\Tcal$.

**Why the embedding contains an optimiser.** Let the columns of $\Phimat \in \reals^{D \times d_e}$ be an orthonormal
basis of $\Tcal$. Every point splits as $\xvec = \Phimat\Phimat\transpose\xvec + \xvec_\perp$, so
$f(\xvec) = f(\Phimat\Phimat\transpose\xvec)$: only the coordinates $\Phimat\transpose\xvec$ matter. Given any $\xvec$,
a point $\yvec$ with $f(\Amat\yvec) = f(\xvec)$ exists if $\Phimat\transpose\Amat\yvec = \Phimat\transpose\xvec$ has a
solution. The matrix $\Phimat\transpose\Amat$ is $d_e \times d$. Its entries are independent standard normals, because
each column of $\Amat$ is an isotropic Gaussian vector and $\Phimat$ has orthonormal columns. A $d_e \times d$ Gaussian
matrix with $d \ge d_e$ has rank $d_e$ with probability 1, so its range is all of $\reals^{d_e}$ and the equation has a
solution. Applied to a minimiser $\xvec^\star$, this gives a $\yvec^\star$ with $g(\yvec^\star) = f(\xvec^\star)$. The
argument does not involve $D$: an objective in a billion dimensions with $d_e = 2$ is a two-dimensional problem for
REMBO, and Wang et al. ran it on exactly that case <Cite id="wang2016rembo" />.

**The box.** Hyperparameters live in a box $\Xcal$, usually rescaled to $[-1, 1]^D$, and $\Amat\yvec$ may fall
outside it. REMBO evaluates the nearest point of the box instead, $g(\yvec) = f(p_\Xcal(\Amat\yvec))$, where
$p_\Xcal$ clips each coordinate. It searches $\yvec$ over $\Ycal = [-\sqrt{d}, \sqrt{d}]^d$, a choice made from
simulations rather than from the theorem <Cite id="wang2016rembo" />. The clipping bends the embedding along the faces
of the box, makes $g$ harder to model, and can exclude the optimiser. Letham et al. analyse these failures and restrict
the search to the part of the embedding that maps inside the box <Cite id="letham2020alebo" />.

### Additive structure

An additive model assumes that $f$ splits over disjoint groups of hyperparameters,

$$
f(\lambdavec) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} f_m\paren{\lambdavec^{(m)}},
$$

where $\lambdavec^{(m)}$ holds the hyperparameters of group $m$, each of size $p$ <Cite id="kandasamy2015" />. Placing
an independent Gaussian process on each term gives a Gaussian process for $f$ whose kernel is the sum of the groups'
kernels. Each term is learned from all evaluations, as a <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive
model</NoteLink> learns its smooth terms. The acquisition also splits: an <Gloss name="ucb">upper confidence
bound</Gloss> that is a sum over groups is maximised group by group, so the inner search costs $M$ problems of dimension
$p$ instead of one of dimension $D$. Kandasamy, Schneider and Póczos prove a regret bound that depends on $D$ only
linearly when $p$ is fixed <Cite id="kandasamy2015" />. The grouping is unknown in practice and is chosen by the
<NoteLink to="marginal-likelihood-and-bayesian-occams-razor">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> among random partitions.

### Trust regions

TuRBO gives up a global model <Cite id="eriksson2019turbo" />. It fits a Gaussian process with
<NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">one length-scale per hyperparameter</NoteLink> to the evaluations of
the current run and proposes points only inside a box, the **trust region**, centred on the incumbent. The box has base
side $L$ on the unit cube, stretched per coordinate in proportion to the fitted length-scales $\ell_i$ with the volume
kept at $L^D$: $L_i = \ell_i L / (\prod_j \ell_j)^{1/D}$. Candidates inside it are chosen by
<NoteLink to="thompson-sampling">Thompson sampling</NoteLink>.

The side length adapts as in classical <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods">trust-region methods</NoteLink>. A proposal
that improves on the incumbent is a success; one that does not is a failure. After $\tau_\text{succ}$ consecutive
successes the side doubles, up to $L_\text{max}$; after $\tau_\text{fail}$ consecutive failures it halves. When $L$
falls below $L_\text{min}$ the region is discarded and a new run starts with side $L_\text{init}$. The published
settings are $L_\text{init} = 0.8$, $L_\text{max} = 1.6$, $L_\text{min} = 2^{-7}$, $\tau_\text{succ} = 3$ and
$\tau_\text{fail} = \lceil D/q \rceil$ for batches of $q$ proposals <Cite id="eriksson2019turbo" />. Several trust
regions can run at once, with Thompson sampling across them deciding which region gets the next evaluation. The local
model is accurate because it is local, and the search stays global through restarts.

### Priors that make the plain loop work

Two recent results keep the ordinary global loop and change the Gaussian process's hyperparameter priors. SAASBO puts
a sparsity-inducing prior on the inverse length-scales, so that every hyperparameter is irrelevant unless the data show
otherwise, which is <NoteLink to="automatic-relevance-determination">automatic relevance determination</NoteLink> with
a strong preference for few relevant inputs <Cite id="eriksson2021saasbo" />. Hvarfner, Hellsten and Nardi trace many
failures of plain Bayesian optimisation in high dimensions to length-scales that are too short for the dimension, and
fix them by scaling the length-scale prior with $D$ <Cite id="hvarfner2024" />. Longer length-scales make the surrogate
smoother and less complex, which reduces the uncertainty-driven exploration of corners.

## Worked example

The figure's objective is the Forrester function <Cite id="forrester2008" /> of $x_1$ alone on the box $[-1, 1]^2$, so
$D = 2$ and $d_e = 1$. Its minimisers are the line $x_1 = x_1^\star = 2 \times 0.7572 - 1 = 0.514$. REMBO with $d = 1$
draws $\Amat = (a_1, a_2)\transpose$ and searches $y \in [-1, 1]$.

1. **Where the embedding goes.** The point $\Amat y$ has first coordinate $a_1 y$. Clipping to the box changes a
   coordinate only when it leaves $[-1, 1]$, and the second coordinate does not affect $f$. So $g(y) = f(a_1 y)$ while
   $\abs{a_1 y} \le 1$.
2. **When it contains the minimiser.** Some $y \in [-1, 1]$ gives $a_1 y = 0.514$ exactly when $\abs{a_1} \ge 0.514$.
   With the figure's default $\Amat = (0.9, 1.2)\transpose$ that holds, at $y = 0.571$.
3. **How often.** With $a_1 \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, $\prob(\abs{a_1} \ge 0.514) = 2(1 - \Phi(0.514)) = 0.607$. The theorem
   promises a minimiser of $f$ on the unbounded line $\Amat y$. The bounded search region $\Ycal$ loses it 39% of the
   time here.
4. **Repairs.** Independent embeddings fail independently: with three of them, the chance that all miss is
   $0.393^3 = 0.061$. A larger $\Ycal$ or $d > d_e$ also raises the success probability, at the cost of a larger
   <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">search space</NoteLink>.

**The trust region in twenty dimensions.** With $D = 20$ and one proposal at a time, TuRBO halves the region after
$\tau_\text{fail} = 20$ consecutive failures. Its initial region of side $0.8$ covers $0.8^{20} = 1.2\%$ of the unit
cube's volume. Seven halvings take $L$ from $0.8$ to $0.00625 < 2^{-7}$, so a region is abandoned after at least 140
failures.

## Assumptions

- **REMBO** needs low effective dimensionality, along any directions, and a guess of $d \ge d_e$. If every
  hyperparameter matters, the embedding discards real structure.
- **Additive models** need the interactions between groups to be weak. A <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning
  rate</Gloss> and a batch size that interact strongly must share a group.
- **Trust regions** assume that good configurations can be reached by local improvement from some restart. They give up
  the global model's ability to jump between distant basins in one step.

## Pitfalls

- **Clipping artefacts.** Points of a random embedding often fall outside the box, and many of them clip to the same
  face, so $g$ has flat regions and kinks that a stationary kernel models badly <Cite id="letham2020alebo" />.
- **Categorical hyperparameters.** Random linear embeddings, additive Gaussian processes and length-scale priors assume
  continuous coordinates. Mixed spaces need encodings or tree-based surrogates such as
  <NoteLink to="sequential-model-based-algorithm-configuration">SMAC</NoteLink>.
- **Too few evaluations for any surrogate.** Below a few multiples of $D$ evaluations, a surrogate in $D$ dimensions has
  little to learn from. <NoteLink to="random-search">Random search</NoteLink> or
  <NoteLink to="evolution-strategies-and-covariance-matrix-adaptation">CMA-ES</NoteLink> are strong baselines there, and
  a method that does not beat them on the problem at hand has not shown its structure assumption holds.
- **Finding the important hyperparameters first.** <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-importance">Hyperparameter
  importance</NoteLink> analysis of earlier runs, or ARD length-scales, often shows a few hyperparameters that matter.
  Searching those and fixing the rest is the simplest form of dimension reduction.
