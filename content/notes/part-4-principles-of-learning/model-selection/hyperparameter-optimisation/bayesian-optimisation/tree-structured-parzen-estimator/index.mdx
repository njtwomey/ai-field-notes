<Definition>
The **tree-structured Parzen estimator** (TPE) is a sequential model-based search for hyperparameters
<Cite id="bergstra2011" />. After $n$ trials it splits them at the $\gamma$-quantile $y^\star$ of their losses into a
good group, the best $\lceil \gamma n \rceil$, and a bad group. It fits one
<NoteLink to="kernel-density-estimation">kernel density estimate</NoteLink> $\ell(\lambdavec)$ to the good
configurations and another, $g(\lambdavec)$, to the bad ones. It then draws candidates from $\ell$ and evaluates the
candidate with the largest ratio $\ell(\lambdavec)/g(\lambdavec)$. Under TPE's model this ratio orders candidates by
<Gloss name="ei">expected improvement</Gloss> over $y^\star$.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian-process</Gloss> surrogate models the loss given the configuration, $p(y \mid \lambdavec)$.
It handles continuous hyperparameters on a box well. Real search spaces are messier. They mix real, integer and
categorical hyperparameters, and some hyperparameters exist only when others take certain values: a second layer's width
only when there are two layers, a kernel width only for an <Gloss name="rbf-kernel" /> (see
<NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search-spaces">hyperparameter search spaces</NoteLink>). A Gaussian process needs a kernel
for such a space, and its cost grows as the cube of the number of trials.

TPE avoids both problems by modelling the other conditional. It models $p(\lambdavec \mid y)$, the distribution of
configurations given how well they did, with one density for good results and one for bad. A density over a mixed,
conditional space can be built one hyperparameter at a time, following the space's own structure. Fitting it costs
time linear in the number of trials.

## Mechanism

### The model

Fix a fraction $\gamma \in (0, 1)$ and let $y^\star$ be the $\gamma$-quantile of the observed losses, so that
$\prob(y < y^\star) = \gamma$. TPE models the configurations as drawn from one of two densities, depending on which
side of $y^\star$ their loss falls:

$$
p(\lambdavec \mid y) = \begin{cases} \ell(\lambdavec) & y < y^\star, \\ g(\lambdavec) & y \ge y^\star. \end{cases}
$$

The density $\ell$ describes where good configurations lie, and $g$ where the others lie. Both are Parzen estimators,
which is the older name for kernel density estimates: a mixture of one kernel per observation in the group, plus a
broad prior component so that no region gets zero density. A continuous hyperparameter gets a Gaussian kernel truncated
to its range, on a log scale when the hyperparameter is searched on one. A categorical one gets a kernel that keeps most
of its mass on the observed category and spreads the rest uniformly <Cite id="watanabe2023" />.

### Why the ratio is expected improvement

Expected improvement over the threshold $y^\star$ is

$$
\operatorname{EI}_{y^\star}(\lambdavec) = \int_{-\infty}^{y^\star} (y^\star - y)\,p(y \mid \lambdavec)\,dy.
$$

**Step 1: <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>.** TPE models $p(\lambdavec \mid y)$ and $p(y)$, not
$p(y \mid \lambdavec)$, so rewrite $p(y \mid \lambdavec) = p(\lambdavec \mid y)\,p(y)/p(\lambdavec)$.

**Step 2: the marginal density of configurations.** Averaging the model over $y$, a fraction $\gamma$ of the mass comes
from $\ell$ and the rest from $g$:

$$
p(\lambdavec) = \int p(\lambdavec \mid y)\,p(y)\,dy = \gamma\,\ell(\lambdavec) + (1 - \gamma)\,g(\lambdavec).
$$

**Step 3: the numerator.** Over the range of integration, $y < y^\star$, the model says $p(\lambdavec \mid y) =
\ell(\lambdavec)$, which does not depend on $y$ and comes out of the integral:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{y^\star}(y^\star - y)\,p(\lambdavec \mid y)\,p(y)\,dy = \ell(\lambdavec)\int_{-\infty}^{y^\star}(y^\star - y)\,p(y)\,dy
= \ell(\lambdavec)\,c,
$$

where $c > 0$ depends on the distribution of losses but not on $\lambdavec$.

**Step 4: divide.** Combining the steps and dividing numerator and denominator by $\ell(\lambdavec)$,

$$
\operatorname{EI}_{y^\star}(\lambdavec) = \frac{c\,\ell(\lambdavec)}{\gamma\,\ell(\lambdavec) + (1 - \gamma)\,g(\lambdavec)}
= \frac{c}{\gamma + (1 - \gamma)\,g(\lambdavec)/\ell(\lambdavec)}.
$$

The right-hand side increases with $\ell(\lambdavec)/g(\lambdavec)$. Maximising expected improvement is therefore the
same as maximising the <NoteLink to="density-ratio-estimation">density ratio</NoteLink> <Cite id="bergstra2011" />. The
same model gives the <NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">probability of improvement</NoteLink>
$\prob(y < y^\star \mid \lambdavec) = \gamma\ell/(\gamma\ell + (1 - \gamma)g)$, which also increases with the ratio. So
under TPE's model, expected improvement and probability of improvement choose the same point <Cite id="watanabe2023" />.

The improvement is measured against the quantile $y^\star$, not against the best loss. With $\gamma = 0.15$ and 40
trials the target is the sixth-best loss. Several trials already beat it, which keeps $\ell$ broad enough to explore.

### Proposing a point

The ratio is maximised approximately. TPE draws $N_s$ candidates from $\ell$, scores each by $\ell/g$, and evaluates
the best. Drawing from $\ell$ concentrates candidates where good configurations have been seen; scoring by the ratio
discounts places where bad configurations are just as dense. The usual defaults are $\gamma = 0.15$, $N_s = 24$
candidates and 10 random trials before the densities take over <Cite id="watanabe2023" />. A larger $N_s$ maximises the
ratio more exactly and exploits more.

### The tree structure

"Tree-structured" refers to conditional search spaces <Cite id="bergstra2011" />. The space is a tree: the choice of a
model type decides which hyperparameters exist below it. The original TPE keeps a separate one-dimensional density for
each hyperparameter, fitted only to the trials in which that hyperparameter was active, and multiplies them. This
handles conditional spaces naturally but ignores interactions between hyperparameters. The multivariate variant puts a
product kernel on each trial, which captures interactions, and Watanabe recommends it <Cite id="watanabe2023" />.

## Worked example

A <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> is searched on a log scale, mapped to $\lambda \in [0, 1]$. Six
trials gave validation losses

| $\lambda$ | 0.10 | 0.30 | 0.45 | 0.55 | 0.70 | 0.90 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| loss | 0.40 | 0.28 | 0.21 | 0.24 | 0.33 | 0.45 |

Take $\gamma = 1/3$. The good group is the best $\lceil 6/3 \rceil = 2$ trials, $\lambda = 0.45$ and $0.55$, and the
threshold is $y^\star = 0.24$. The bad group is $\lambda = 0.10, 0.30, 0.70, 0.90$. For hand calculation, use plain
Gaussian kernels of bandwidth $h = 0.15$ with no truncation or prior component, so each density is the average of
$\varphi((\lambda - \lambda_i)/h)/h$ over its group.

**Candidate $\lambda = 0.50$.** Both good trials are $0.05$ away, a standard score of $0.333$, and each kernel is
$\varphi(0.333)/0.15 = 0.3774/0.15 = 2.516$. So $\ell(0.50) = 2.516$. The bad trials are $0.40$, $0.20$, $0.20$ and
$0.40$ away, with kernels $0.076$, $1.093$, $1.093$ and $0.076$, so $g(0.50) = 2.338/4 = 0.585$. The ratio is
$\ell/g = 4.30$.

**Candidate $\lambda = 0.62$.** The good kernels are $1.399$ and $2.385$, so $\ell = 1.892$. The bad kernels are
$0.007$, $0.273$, $2.307$ and $0.466$, so $g = 0.763$. The ratio is $2.48$. The candidate sits between the best trials
and the bad trial at $0.70$.

**Candidate $\lambda = 0.20$.** The good kernels are $0.663$ and $0.175$, so $\ell = 0.419$. Two bad trials are $0.10$
away, and $g = 1.067$. The ratio is $0.39$.

**Expected improvement.** With $\gamma = 1/3$, EI is proportional to $1/(1/3 + \tfrac23\,g/\ell)$: $2.05$ at $0.50$,
$1.66$ at $0.62$ and $0.49$ at $0.20$, in units of the constant $c$. TPE evaluates $\lambda = 0.50$ next, between the
two good trials. The probability that the loss beats $y^\star$, $\gamma\ell/(\gamma\ell + (1 - \gamma)g)$, is $0.68$,
$0.55$ and $0.16$, in the same order.

## Cost

Fitting the two densities to $n$ trials costs $O(n)$ per hyperparameter, and scoring $N_s$ candidates costs $O(N_s\,n)$.
A Gaussian process costs $O(n^3)$ to fit. TPE therefore stays fast for thousands of trials, and each proposal takes
milliseconds. It is the core of Hyperopt and the main sampler of Optuna <Cite id="akiba2019" />
<Cite id="watanabe2023" />, and the multi-fidelity method
<NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation-and-hyperband">BOHB</NoteLink> uses the same pair of densities. Like other
sequential methods, it proposes one trial at a time. Parallel workers that ignore each other's pending trials can
propose near-duplicates; inserting pending trials as provisional bad results spreads them out.

## Trade-offs

- **Against a Gaussian process.** TPE has no explicit model of $f$, so it cannot report a posterior mean or an
  uncertainty, and it extrapolates poorly from few trials. On low-dimensional continuous problems with tight budgets,
  Gaussian-process methods often find good configurations in fewer trials. TPE is better suited to large, mixed and
  conditional spaces and to budgets of hundreds of trials or more.
- **The fraction $\gamma$.** A small $\gamma$ fits $\ell$ to the very best trials and exploits; a large $\gamma$ blurs
  good and bad and explores. The quantile also depends on noise: with noisy losses, the good group contains lucky
  trials.
- **Independent dimensions.** The original univariate TPE misses interactions, such as a learning rate whose best value
  depends on the batch size. The multivariate variant captures them at a small extra cost.

## Pitfalls

- **Too few random trials.** The densities are meaningless with two or three good trials. Ten or more random trials
  first, depending on the dimension, keep the first densities from locking onto an early accident.
- **Bandwidths.** The kernel widths set how far $\ell$ reaches. Too narrow, and TPE resamples near past trials; too
  wide, and it reduces to <NoteLink to="random-search">random search</NoteLink>. Implementations clip the bandwidth from
  below for this reason <Cite id="watanabe2023" />.
- **The ratio is not a density.** The ratio is large where $g$ is small, which includes regions with no trials at all.
  The prior component in both densities and drawing candidates from $\ell$ keep the search from chasing empty regions.
- **The best trial's score.** TPE concentrates trials where losses were low, so the best validation loss is optimistic,
  as for every search (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).
