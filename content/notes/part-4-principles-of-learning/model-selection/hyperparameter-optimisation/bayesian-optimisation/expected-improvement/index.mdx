<Definition>
Let $f(\lambdavec)$ be an objective to minimise, such as a validation loss, and let $f^\star_t$ be the best value
observed after $t$ evaluations. The **improvement** of a value $f$ is $\max(f^\star_t - f, 0)$: how far it falls below
the incumbent, or zero if it does not. The **expected improvement** of a candidate $\lambdavec$ is the mean of the
improvement under the surrogate's posterior for $f(\lambdavec)$,

$$
\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambdavec) = \expect\brack{\max\paren{f^\star_t - f(\lambdavec), 0} \mid \Dcal_t}.
$$

When that posterior is $\Gauss(\mu_t(\lambdavec), \sigma_t^2(\lambdavec))$, as for a <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian
process</Gloss>,

$$
\operatorname{EI}_t(\lambdavec) = \paren{f^\star_t - \mu_t}\Phi(z) + \sigma_t\varphi(z), \qquad
z = \frac{f^\star_t - \mu_t}{\sigma_t},
$$

where $\mu_t$ and $\sigma_t$ are evaluated at $\lambdavec$, and $\Phi$ and $\varphi$ are the standard
<NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">normal distribution</NoteLink> function and density <Cite id="mockus1978" />
<Cite id="jones1998" />. <NoteLink to="bayesian-optimisation">Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> evaluates the candidate
with the largest EI next.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A candidate's value is unknown before it is evaluated, but the surrogate gives a distribution for it. Some of that
distribution may lie below the incumbent. Expected improvement adds up those possible gains, each weighted by its
probability, and ignores outcomes that do no better than the incumbent. A candidate whose value is sure to be worse
than $f^\star_t$ scores zero. A candidate whose value is sure to equal $f^\star_t - 0.1$ scores $0.1$.

The asymmetry is what makes EI explore. A wide posterior has a long lower tail that can reach far below the incumbent,
and its upper tail costs nothing because losses are truncated at zero. Widening the posterior therefore raises EI even
when the mean stays put. A narrow posterior with a mean below the incumbent offers a nearly certain but small gain. EI
weighs the two on one scale, the units of $f$, which is why it is the default acquisition of most Bayesian optimisation
software.

The method is old. Mockus, Tiešis and Žilinskas proposed it in 1978 <Cite id="mockus1978" />. Jones, Schonlau and
Welch made it standard for expensive engineering simulations in 1998 under the name efficient global optimization
(EGO), with a kriging surrogate and a branch-and-bound search for the maximum of EI <Cite id="jones1998" />. Snoek,
Larochelle and Adams brought it to the tuning of machine learning models <Cite id="snoek2012" />.

## Derivation

Fix a candidate and drop $\lambdavec$ and $t$ from the notation: the posterior is $f \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with
$\sigma > 0$, and the incumbent is $f^\star$.

**Step 1: standardise.** Write $f = \mu + \sigma U$, where $U \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$. Then

$$
f^\star - f = f^\star - \mu - \sigma U = \sigma(z - U), \qquad z = \frac{f^\star - \mu}{\sigma}.
$$

The improvement is $\max(\sigma(z - U), 0) = \sigma\max(z - U, 0)$, because $\sigma > 0$.

**Step 2: restrict to the improving outcomes.** The improvement is positive exactly when $U < z$. Its expectation is an
integral over that half-line,

$$
\operatorname{EI} = \sigma\int_{-\infty}^{z} (z - u)\,\varphi(u)\,du
= \sigma\paren{z\int_{-\infty}^{z}\varphi(u)\,du - \int_{-\infty}^{z}u\,\varphi(u)\,du}.
$$

**Step 3: evaluate the two integrals.** The first is $\Phi(z)$ by definition. For the second, the standard normal
density satisfies $\varphi'(u) = -u\,\varphi(u)$, so $u\,\varphi(u)$ has antiderivative $-\varphi(u)$, and
$\varphi(u) \to 0$ as $u \to -\infty$:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{z}u\,\varphi(u)\,du = \brack{-\varphi(u)}_{-\infty}^{z} = -\varphi(z).
$$

**Step 4: collect.** Substituting both integrals,

$$
\operatorname{EI} = \sigma\paren{z\Phi(z) + \varphi(z)} = (f^\star - \mu)\Phi(z) + \sigma\varphi(z),
$$

since $\sigma z = f^\star - \mu$. When $\sigma = 0$ the posterior is a point mass at $\mu$, and the improvement is
$\max(f^\star - \mu, 0)$ with certainty.

The compact form $\operatorname{EI} = \sigma\,\tau(z)$ with $\tau(z) = z\Phi(z) + \varphi(z)$ separates the scale from
the shape. The function $\tau$ is positive for every $z$, close to $z$ for large positive $z$ (a sure improvement of
size about $f^\star - \mu$) and close to zero for large negative $z$.

## Exploration and exploitation

The two terms of the closed form have separate meanings. The first, $(f^\star - \mu)\Phi(z)$, is positive when the mean
is below the incumbent and grows as the mean falls: it rewards **exploitation**. The second, $\sigma\varphi(z)$, grows
with the standard deviation: it rewards **exploration**.

The <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink> make this exact. Differentiate
$\operatorname{EI} = \sigma\tau(z)$ using $\tau'(z) = \Phi(z) + z\varphi(z) + \varphi'(z) = \Phi(z)$, and
$\partial z/\partial\mu = -1/\sigma$, $\partial z/\partial\sigma = -z/\sigma$:

$$
\frac{\partial\operatorname{EI}}{\partial\mu} = \sigma\,\Phi(z)\cdot\paren{-\frac{1}{\sigma}} = -\Phi(z) < 0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\operatorname{EI}}{\partial\sigma} = \tau(z) + \sigma\,\Phi(z)\cdot\paren{-\frac{z}{\sigma}} = \varphi(z) > 0.
$$

EI always rises when the mean falls with the spread fixed, and always rises when the spread grows with the mean fixed.
A candidate can therefore win either way. The contours of equal EI in the $(\mu, \sigma)$ plane slope upwards: along
each one, a worse mean is bought back by a wider posterior, at the exchange rate
$d\sigma/d\mu = \Phi(z)/\varphi(z)$.

## The margin ξ

Expected improvement can be made more exploratory by measuring improvement against $f^\star - \xi$ for a margin
$\xi \ge 0$ instead of against $f^\star$:

$$
\operatorname{EI}_\xi = \delta\,\Phi\paren{\frac{\delta}{\sigma}} + \sigma\,\varphi\paren{\frac{\delta}{\sigma}}, \qquad
\delta = f^\star - \mu - \xi.
$$

The derivation is the one above with $f^\star - \xi$ in place of $f^\star$. A margin shrinks the exploitation term of
candidates whose mean is just below the incumbent, which are the candidates EI would otherwise refine forever. The idea
is Kushner's, who used it with the probability of improvement <Cite id="kushner1964" />. The margin is in the units of
$f$, so a useful value depends on the scale of the objective. Libraries that standardise the observed values can use
one small margin for every problem, and many use none.

shallow local minimum sooner, and costs precision near the global minimum. No margin is best for every objective.

## Worked example

A search tunes a classifier by validation error. The incumbent is $f^\star = 0.20$. The surrogate's posterior at two
candidates is

| candidate | $\mu$ | $\sigma$ | description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $0.22$ | $0.04$ | an unexplored region, probably worse |
| B | $0.19$ | $0.005$ | next to the incumbent, almost surely a little better |

**Candidate A.** The standardised gap is $z = (0.20 - 0.22)/0.04 = -0.5$. The normal tables give $\Phi(-0.5) = 0.3085$
and $\varphi(-0.5) = 0.3521$. The exploitation term is $(-0.02)(0.3085) = -0.00617$, and the exploration term is
$0.04 \times 0.3521 = 0.01408$. So $\operatorname{EI}_A = 0.00791$: a 31% chance of improving, by about $0.026$ on
average when it does.

**Candidate B.** Here $z = (0.20 - 0.19)/0.005 = 2$, with $\Phi(2) = 0.9772$ and $\varphi(2) = 0.0540$. The
exploitation term is $0.01 \times 0.9772 = 0.00977$ and the exploration term $0.005 \times 0.0540 = 0.00027$, so
$\operatorname{EI}_B = 0.01004$.

**Choice.** EI prefers B, by about a quarter. B's gain is nearly certain and close to $0.01$; A's gain is uncertain but
can be several times larger. The probability of improvement prefers B far more strongly, $0.977$ against $0.309$,
because it ignores the size of the gain.

**With a margin $\xi = 0.01$.** For A, $\delta = 0.20 - 0.22 - 0.01 = -0.03$, $\delta/\sigma = -0.75$, and
$\operatorname{EI}_A = (-0.03)(0.2266) + 0.04 \times 0.3011 = 0.00525$. For B, $\delta = 0$, so
$\operatorname{EI}_B = 0.005\,\varphi(0) = 0.005 \times 0.3989 = 0.00199$. The margin erases most of B's sure gain, and
EI now chooses A.

## Noisy evaluations

With noisy evaluations, the best observed value $f^\star_t$ is a minimum of noisy numbers and is biased low, as in
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>. EI against a lucky incumbent
undervalues every candidate and can stall. The common fix replaces $f^\star_t$ by the smallest posterior mean at the
evaluated points, $\min_{i \le t} \mu_t(\lambdavec_i)$, which shrinks lucky observations towards their neighbours
<Cite id="frazier2018" /> <Cite id="garnett2023" />. The knowledge gradient (see
<NoteLink to="acquisition-functions">acquisition functions</NoteLink>) handles noise more directly, at a higher
computational cost.

## Convergence

With a fixed Gaussian-process prior, EI eventually finds the minimum of any function in the prior's
<Gloss name="rkhs">reproducing kernel Hilbert space</Gloss>, and Bull gave its convergence rates <Cite id="bull2011" />.
The same paper shows a weakness of practice: when the kernel's hyperparameters are re-estimated from the data at each
step, EI can fail to find the minimum at all. An overconfident length-scale makes the posterior spread small away from
the evaluations, so EI never pays for the evaluation that would reveal its mistake. Bull proposes modified estimators of
the hyperparameters that recover the rates of a fixed prior. A few random evaluations mixed into the search guard
against the same failure in practice.

## Assumptions

- **A Gaussian posterior.** The closed form needs it. With other surrogates, such as <NoteLink to="random-forest">random
  forests</NoteLink> in <NoteLink to="sequential-model-based-algorithm-configuration">SMAC</NoteLink>, EI is computed
  from a Gaussian fitted to the surrogate's mean and spread.
  <NoteLink to="tree-structured-parzen-estimator">TPE</NoteLink> reaches a version of EI through density ratios instead.
- **One evaluation at a time.** EI values the next evaluation as if it were the last. It is myopic: it does not plan
  for the evaluations that follow.
- **A calibrated spread.** EI's exploration is only as good as $\sigma_t$.

## Pitfalls

- **Vanishing values.** Far from the incumbent, $z$ is very negative and EI is of order $\sigma\varphi(z)/z^2$, which
  underflows to zero in floating point. Large regions then have zero EI and zero gradient, and a gradient-based search
  for the maximum of EI stalls there. The problem grows with the number of evaluations and the dimension. Ament et al.
  compute $\log\operatorname{EI}$ stably instead (LogEI), with the same maximisers and much better behaved optimisation
  <Cite id="ament2023" />.
- **Refining forever.** Once the posterior is narrow around the incumbent, EI can keep choosing points a hair away from
  it. A margin, a minimum distance between evaluations, or a stopping rule on the largest EI ends the stall.
- **Reporting the incumbent's score as its error.** EI chases low observed values. The incumbent's validation score is
  therefore optimistic, and its error needs data the search did not use, such as a test set or
  <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>.
- **Parallel evaluations.** Running EI on several workers proposes the same point on each.
  <NoteLink to="batch-bayesian-optimisation">Batch Bayesian optimisation</NoteLink> extends EI to several points at
  once.
