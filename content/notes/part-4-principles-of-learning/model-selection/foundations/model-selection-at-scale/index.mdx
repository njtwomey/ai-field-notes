<Definition>
**Model selection at scale** is model selection when a single training run of the final model costs a large share of
the available compute, so that the candidates cannot each be trained at full size and cross-validated. It replaces the
standard tools with cheaper ones: a single large validation set instead of $K$ folds; small proxy models whose tuned
hyperparameters transfer to the large one; scaling laws fitted to small runs and extrapolated; and selection among the
checkpoints and seeds of a few full-size runs. Assessment then depends on held-out benchmarks that the training data
must not contain.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="cross-validation">Cross-validation</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
cross-validation</NoteLink> spend compute to save data. They fit each candidate $K$ times, sometimes $K^2$ times, so
that every example can serve for both fitting and scoring. A large model reverses the scarcity. Data are plentiful,
often billions of tokens or millions of images, and a single run can take weeks on thousands of accelerators. Fitting
even one candidate $K$ times is unaffordable, and fitting each candidate once at full size is often unaffordable too.

The questions of model selection stay the same: estimate, select, assess. The answers move. Estimation becomes cheap and
precise, because a validation set of millions of examples has a tiny <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard
error</NoteLink>. Selection becomes the expensive step, and it is done mostly on small models whose behaviour predicts
the large one. Assessment becomes fragile, because the test data may have leaked into a training corpus scraped from the
web.

## One validation set

With $n_\text{val}$ independent validation examples, the validation error of a fixed model has standard error
$s/\sqrt{n_\text{val}}$, where $s$ is the standard deviation of the per-example loss. For an accuracy near $0.9$ on
$n_\text{val} = 10\,000$ examples, the standard error is $\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1/10\,000} = 0.003$. A validation set of
that size separates models that differ by a percentage point. The training set loses only a small fraction of its data
to the validation set, so the hold-out estimate is nearly unbiased for the model trained on everything (see
<NoteLink to="hold-out-validation">hold-out validation</NoteLink>). $K$-fold cross-validation would cost $K$ training
runs to reduce a variance that is already negligible.

Three cautions apply.

- **Correlated examples.** The tokens of one document, the frames of one video and the turns of one conversation are not
  independent. The <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss> is the number of documents or sessions, and the
  standard error is larger than the token count suggests. Split by document or source (see
  <NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink>).
- **Repeated use.** The same validation set guides many decisions over a project, including architecture, data mixture,
  <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> and stopping time. Each decision consumes some of its independence.
  Godbole et al. warn against fitting the validation set adaptively over time <Cite id="godbole2023" />.
- **Distribution.** The validation set should come from the distribution the model will meet. A held-out slice of the
  pretraining corpus measures pretraining loss, not downstream quality.

## Tuning on proxies

Most hyperparameters of a large model are chosen on smaller models of the same family. The difficulty is that the best
hyperparameters move as the model grows. Under the standard parameterisation of a Transformer, the best learning rate
shifts as the width grows, so a value tuned on a small model is wrong for the large one.

<NoteLink to="maximal-update-parameterisation">Maximal update parameterisation</NoteLink> (µP) scales
the initialisation and learning rate of each layer with the width so that the optimal hyperparameters stay fixed as the
width grows. The procedure, µTransfer, parameterises the target model in µP, tunes a small version and copies
the hyperparameters across <Cite id="yang2021mup" />.

- Yang et al. transferred hyperparameters from a 13M-parameter proxy to BERT-large (350M parameters), at a total tuning
  cost equal to one pretraining of BERT-large. They transferred from a 40M-parameter proxy to the 6.7B-parameter
  <Gloss name="gpt-3" /> architecture, at 7% of the pretraining cost. Both tuned models beat the published results
  <Cite id="yang2021mup" />.
- Optimisation hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, initialisation scales and parameter multipliers, transfer.
  <NoteLink to="regularisation">Regularisation</NoteLink> hyperparameters, such as dropout and
  <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>, do not <Cite id="yang2021mup" />.
- Transfer across width has theoretical support. Transfer across depth, batch size, sequence length and training time
  was verified empirically on Transformers, with caveats <Cite id="yang2021mup" />.

The proxy must be close enough to the target that the ranking of candidates is preserved. A proxy that is too small,
or trained too briefly, can rank two choices in the opposite order from the target.

## Extrapolating from small runs

Some choices cannot be made on one proxy, because they concern scale itself: how large the model should be, how many
tokens to train it on, and which of two architectures will be better at full size. These are made by fitting a
**scaling law** to a family of small runs and extrapolating.

Kaplan et al. found that the test loss of <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss> falls as a power law in
the number of parameters $N$, the number of training tokens $D$ and the training compute $C$, over more than seven
orders of magnitude, with training compute $C \approx 6ND$ <Gloss name="flop">floating-point operations</Gloss>
<Cite id="kaplan2020" />. Hoffmann et al. trained over 400 models from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters on 5 to
500 billion tokens. They fitted $L(N, D) = E + A/N^\alpha + B/D^\beta$, with constants $E$, $A$, $B$, $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ fitted to the runs, and concluded that compute-optimal training scales $N$ and $D$ equally: each doubling of the
model calls for a doubling of the tokens <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />. OpenAI predicted aspects of GPT-4's performance
from models trained with at most 1/1000 of its compute <Cite id="openai2023gpt4" />.

A scaling law turns model selection into curve fitting. Two candidate architectures are compared by their fitted curves
at the target compute, not by their losses at the small scales where they were measured. The fit is an extrapolation,
and its error grows with the distance to the target. Pretraining loss extrapolates more reliably than downstream task
metrics. Wei et al. describe abilities that are absent in smaller models and present in larger ones, and that cannot be
predicted by extrapolating the smaller models <Cite id="wei2022emergent" />. Schaeffer et al. argue that many such jumps
come from the choice of a nonlinear or discontinuous metric, such as <Gloss name="exact-match">exact-match</Gloss>
accuracy, and that continuous metrics of the same models change smoothly <Cite id="schaeffer2023mirage" />. The fitting
and the compute-optimal trade-off are worked through in the
<NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">autoregressive transformer language model</NoteLink> note.

## Checkpoint selection

A long run saves checkpoints, and the final model is chosen among them. The playbook recommends retrospective
checkpoint selection: keep the best checkpoints of a fixed-length run by validation score and pick the best at the end,
because the best checkpoint is often not the last <Cite id="godbole2023" />. This is <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early
stopping</NoteLink> applied after the fact.

Fine-tuning adds the random seed to the candidates. Dodge et al. fine-tuned <Gloss name="bert" /> hundreds of times on
each of four <Gloss name="glue" /> tasks, changing only the seed. Distinct seeds gave substantially different results,
and <NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">weight initialisation</NoteLink> and data order contributed comparably to the
variance <Cite id="dodge2020" />. Keeping the best of many seeds is selection over noisy candidates, and its validation
score is optimistic in the same way as the best of many hyperparameters (see
<NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>).

Averaging is an alternative to selecting. Wortsman et al. average the weights of models fine-tuned from one
pretrained model with different hyperparameters, a "model soup", instead of keeping only the one with the best
held-out validation accuracy <Cite id="wortsman2022soups" />.

## Contamination

A test set assesses a model honestly only if no part of it informed the model. For models pretrained on web-scale
corpora, public benchmarks may sit in the training data. Yang et al. found that 8% to 18% of the HumanEval coding
benchmark overlaps with the pretraining sets RedPajama-Data-1T and StarCoder-Data. They also showed that rephrased test
items evade n-gram decontamination, and that a 13B-parameter model trained on rephrased test data can reach the
benchmark performance of GPT-4 <Cite id="yang2023" />.

Contamination does not always help. Magar and Schwartz pretrained BERT models on corpora containing downstream test
data and found cases where the model memorised the contaminated data without exploiting it on the task
<Cite id="magar2022" />. Detection is possible without the training data. Oren et al. test whether a model assigns
higher likelihood to a benchmark in its canonical order than to shuffled orders, which an exchangeable benchmark
would not show unless it had been memorised <Cite id="oren2023" />. The practice of detection and prevention is covered
in <NoteLink to="benchmark-contamination">benchmark contamination</NoteLink>.

Reuse of a public test set is a separate risk. Recht et al. built new test sets for <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> and
<Gloss name="imagenet" /> following the original collection process and found accuracy drops that they attribute to a
distribution shift between the old and new test sets rather than to adaptive reuse <Cite id="recht2019" />. Across more
than a hundred Kaggle competitions, Roelofs et al. found little evidence of overfitting to the public leaderboard
<Cite id="roelofs2019" />. Large test sets resist reuse; contamination defeats them.

## Worked example

A team has the compute budget of Gopher, a 280-billion-parameter model trained on 300 billion tokens, and must choose
the model size.

1. **The budget.** With $C \approx 6ND$, Gopher's run costs $6 \times (2.8 \times 10^{11}) \times (3 \times 10^{11})
   \approx 5.0 \times 10^{23}$ floating-point operations.
2. **The selection.** From scaling laws fitted to runs of 70 million to 16 billion parameters, Hoffmann et al. predicted
   that this compute is better spent on a smaller model trained on more data <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />.
3. **The candidate.** Chinchilla has $N = 7 \times 10^{10}$ parameters and was trained on $D = 1.4 \times 10^{12}$
   tokens, so $6ND \approx 5.9 \times 10^{23}$, a similar budget. It has a quarter of Gopher's parameters and about
   $4.7$ times its tokens.
4. **The assessment.** Chinchilla outperformed Gopher on a large range of downstream evaluations
   <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />. The decision was made without training a second 280-billion-parameter model.
5. **The validation precision.** A held-out set of $10\,000$ multiple-choice items scored at about 70% accuracy has
   standard error $\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3/10\,000} = 0.0046$. Differences of two percentage points between the two
   models are over four standard errors. Differences of half a point are within noise.

## Pitfalls

- **Rank reversals between scales.** A choice that wins on a small proxy can lose at full size. Verify transfer on at
  least two proxy sizes, and prefer parameterisations designed for transfer.
- **Extrapolating the wrong quantity.** Pretraining loss follows smooth power laws; benchmark accuracy often does not.
  An extrapolated benchmark score has a much wider error than the fit suggests.
- **Selecting on the benchmark.** Choosing checkpoints, prompts or seeds by their score on the reported benchmark turns
  it into a validation set (see <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>). Keep a held-out set for the
  final number.
- **Reporting the best seed.** The best of many fine-tuning runs is an optimistic estimate of what the procedure
  delivers. Report the spread over seeds, or the expected best score as a function of the number of runs.
- **Trusting a contaminated test set.** A strong score on a public benchmark may measure memorisation. Prefer test sets
  created after the training data was collected, or kept private.
- **Too few tokens of validation per decision.** A validation set that is small relative to the number of decisions
  made on it overfits like a small one. Refresh it, or keep a second set for confirmation.
