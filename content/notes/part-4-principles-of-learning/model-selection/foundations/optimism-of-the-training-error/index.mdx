<Definition>
Let $y_i = f(\xvec_i) + \varepsilon_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, with the inputs $\xvec_i$ fixed and the noise
$\varepsilon_i$ independent with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$. Fit a model to the sample $\Dcal$ and let $\hat y_i$ be
its fitted value at $\xvec_i$. Let $y_i^0$ be a fresh target at the same input, independent of $\Dcal$. The **in-sample
error** is $\operatorname{Err}_\text{in} = \frac1n\sum_i \expect_{y^0}\brack{\ell(y_i^0, \hat y_i)}$, the **training
error** is $\overline{\operatorname{err}} = \frac1n\sum_i \ell(y_i, \hat y_i)$, and the **optimism** is their difference
$\operatorname{op} = \operatorname{Err}_\text{in} - \overline{\operatorname{err}}$. For squared error its expectation
over the training targets is

$$
\omega = \expect_\yvec[\operatorname{op}] = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \cov(\hat y_i, y_i)
$$

<Cite id="efron1986" /> <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Each training target $y_i$ is used twice. It helps build the fit, and then it scores the fit. When it helps build the
fit, it pulls $\hat y_i$ towards itself, noise included. When it scores the fit, part of its noise has already been
absorbed, so the residual $y_i - \hat y_i$ is smaller than the error the fit makes on a fresh target at the same input.
The optimism is the average size of that head start.

The covariance $\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$ measures the pull directly. Over repeated samples with the same inputs, a fit that
follows its own targets closely moves $\hat y_i$ up whenever $y_i$ is high, and the covariance is large. A rigid fit
barely notices any single target, and the covariance is small. The optimism adds the pulls over all points. It is the
reason the training error cannot choose the flexibility of a model (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-and-underfitting">overfitting and underfitting</NoteLink>), and it is what Mallows' $C_p$, the
Akaike information criterion and Stein's unbiased risk estimate add back.

## In-sample and extra-sample error

Two kinds of new data can score a fit.

- **In-sample error** keeps the training inputs and draws fresh targets at them. It isolates the effect of fitting the
  noise, and it is the quantity the covariance formula describes exactly. It answers a fixed-design question, such as
  predicting the outcome of a repeated experiment at the same settings.
- **Extra-sample error** $\operatorname{Err}$ draws a new input as well as a new target, from the distribution that
  produced the data. It is the test error in the usual sense, and it is what a held-out set and cross-validation
  estimate.

Extra-sample error is usually the larger of the two, because new inputs fall between and beyond the training inputs,
where the fit is less certain <Cite id="rosset2020" />. For least squares with a correct linear model, the next section
gives $\operatorname{Err}_\text{in} = \sigma^2(1 + p/n)$. For Gaussian inputs with mean zero, no intercept and
$n > p + 1$, the extra-sample error is $\sigma^2(1 + p/(n - p - 1))$. With $n = 50$ and $p = 10$ these are
$1.20\sigma^2$ and $1.26\sigma^2$. Analytic corrections such as $C_p$ target the in-sample error. They rank models well
for selection even though they understate the extra-sample error <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

<Derivation title="Extra-sample error of least squares with Gaussian inputs">
Let the rows of the $n \times p$ design $\Xmat$ and a new input $\xvec$ be independent draws from $\Gauss(\zeros,
\Sigmamat)$, and let $y = \xvec^\top\betavec + \varepsilon$ with independent noise of variance $\sigma^2$. Least squares
gives $\hat\betavec - \betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\epsilonb$. Conditional on $\Xmat$, this has mean zero
and covariance $\sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$.

The new error is $y - \xvec^\top\hat\betavec = \varepsilon - \xvec^\top(\hat\betavec - \betavec)$. The two terms are
uncorrelated, so its expected square is
$\sigma^2 + \sigma^2\expect\brack{\xvec^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec}$. The new input is independent of $\Xmat$ with
$\expect[\xvec\xvec^\top] = \Sigmamat$, so the second term is
$\sigma^2\trace\paren{\Sigmamat\,\expect[(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}]}$.

The matrix $\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ has a <NoteLink to="wishart-distribution">Wishart distribution</NoteLink> with $n$ degrees
of freedom and scale $\Sigmamat$, whose inverse has mean $\Sigmamat^{-1}/(n - p - 1)$ for $n > p + 1$. The trace is then
$\trace\eye_p/(n - p - 1) = p/(n - p - 1)$, and

$$
\operatorname{Err} = \sigma^2\paren{1 + \frac{p}{n - p - 1}}.
$$
</Derivation>

## The covariance formula

Fix $i$ and write $f_i = f(\xvec_i)$. The fresh target is $y_i^0 = f_i + \varepsilon_i^0$, and the training target is
$y_i = f_i + \varepsilon_i$. Expand both squared errors around $f_i$.

**The fresh target.** The fresh noise $\varepsilon_i^0$ has mean zero and is independent of $\hat y_i$, so the cross
term vanishes:

$$
\expect\brack{(y_i^0 - \hat y_i)^2} = \expect\brack{(\varepsilon_i^0)^2} + \expect\brack{(f_i - \hat y_i)^2} = \sigma^2 +
\expect\brack{(f_i - \hat y_i)^2}.
$$

**The training target.** The training noise $\varepsilon_i$ is not independent of $\hat y_i$, because $y_i$ was used to
compute $\hat y_i$. The cross term remains:

$$
\expect\brack{(y_i - \hat y_i)^2} = \sigma^2 + \expect\brack{(f_i - \hat y_i)^2} + 2\expect\brack{\varepsilon_i(f_i -
\hat y_i)}.
$$

**The cross term is a covariance.** $f_i$ is a constant and $\expect[\varepsilon_i] = 0$, so
$\expect[\varepsilon_i f_i] = 0$ and the cross term is $-2\expect[\varepsilon_i\hat y_i]$. Since
$\varepsilon_i = y_i - \expect[y_i]$, the product $\expect[\varepsilon_i\hat y_i]$ is $\cov(y_i, \hat y_i)$.

**Subtract.** The difference between the two expected errors at point $i$ is $2\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$. Averaging over the
$n$ points gives

$$
\omega = \expect\brack{\operatorname{Err}_\text{in}} - \expect\brack{\overline{\operatorname{err}}} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n
\cov(\hat y_i, y_i).
$$

The identity needs no assumption about $f$ or about how the fit was made. It holds for a polynomial, a tree, a neural
network or a procedure that searches over models, provided the inputs are fixed and the noise is independent with
common variance. Efron extended covariance penalties beyond squared error, to a class of error measures that includes
the deviance of exponential families <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.

## Least squares

For a linear smoother, $\hat\yvec = \Smat\yvec$ with $\Smat$ independent of $\yvec$. Then
$\hat y_i = \sum_j S_{ij}y_j$, and since the targets are independent only the $j = i$ term covaries with $y_i$:
$\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = S_{ii}\sigma^2$. Summing,

$$
\omega = \frac{2\sigma^2}{n}\trace\Smat.
$$

Least squares with a full-rank $n \times p$ design projects onto the column space of $\Xmat$, with
$\Smat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$ and $\trace\Smat = p$. So

$$
\omega = \frac{2p\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \expect\brack{\operatorname{Err}_\text{in}} = \expect\brack{\overline{\operatorname{err}}} +
\frac{2p\sigma^2}{n}.
$$

Each coefficient adds $2\sigma^2/n$ to the optimism. The trace $\trace\Smat$ generalises the parameter count to
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing
splines</NoteLink> and $k$-nearest neighbours, where it is called the
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>. In general the effective degrees of
freedom are defined as $\operatorname{df} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$, so that
$\omega = 2\sigma^2\operatorname{df}/n$ for any fit.

**Estimating the in-sample error.** Adding an estimate of $\omega$ to the training error estimates the in-sample error.

- With $\hat\sigma^2$ from a low-bias model, $\overline{\operatorname{err}} + 2\hat\sigma^2 p/n$ is Mallows' $C_p$
  <Cite id="mallows1973" />. With $\trace\Smat$ in place of $p$, it covers linear smoothers. See
  <NoteLink to="mallows-cp-and-covariance-penalties">Mallows' Cp and covariance penalties</NoteLink>.
- For Gaussian noise, the log-likelihood is the squared error up to constants, and the same correction is the
  <NoteLink to="akaike-information-criterion">Akaike information criterion</NoteLink>.
- For a fit that is weakly differentiable in $\yvec$, Stein's lemma turns each covariance into the expected derivative
  $\sigma^2\expect[\partial\hat y_i/\partial y_i]$, which can be estimated from one sample (see
  <NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk estimate</NoteLink>).
- For any fit, the covariances can be estimated by refitting to targets simulated from a model of the noise, a
  parametric bootstrap <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.

Efron showed that cross-validation behaves like a randomised version of a covariance penalty. When the noise model is
believable, the penalty estimates the in-sample error with less variance <Cite id="efron2004covariance" />.

The figure measures the optimism by simulation. It refits each procedure to 500 training sets on one fixed design and
compares the in-sample minus training error with $2\sigma^2\operatorname{df}/n$. At the defaults, $n = 50$, $\sigma = 1$
and a degree-9 polynomial with $p = 10$ coefficients, the measured optimism is $0.408 \pm 0.009$ against
$2p\sigma^2/n = 0.40$. The extra-sample error, $1.37$, exceeds the in-sample error, $1.20$, because the polynomial
extrapolates poorly near the ends of $[-1, 1]$.

## Worked example

**Two extremes.** Take $n$ points and noise variance $\sigma^2$.

1. **The mean.** The fit $\hat y_i = \bar y$ for every $i$ has $\cov(\bar y, y_i) = \sigma^2/n$. The sum over $i$ is
   $\sigma^2$, so $\operatorname{df} = 1$ and $\omega = 2\sigma^2/n$. One parameter, one unit of optimism.
2. **An interpolator.** One-nearest-neighbour regression at a training point returns that point's own target, so
   $\hat y_i = y_i$ and $\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \sigma^2$. Then $\operatorname{df} = n$ and $\omega = 2\sigma^2$. The
   training error is zero, and the in-sample error is $\expect(y_i^0 - y_i)^2 = 2\sigma^2$, the variance of the
   difference of two independent noises.

**Least squares with ten coefficients.** Let $n = 50$, $p = 10$, $\sigma^2 = 1$ and a correct linear model.

1. The expected training error is $\sigma^2(n - p)/n = 0.80$, since the residual vector lies in an $(n - p)$-dimensional
   space.
2. The optimism is $2p\sigma^2/n = 0.40$, so the expected in-sample error is $0.80 + 0.40 = 1.20$.
3. With Gaussian inputs, the expected extra-sample error is $1 + 10/39 = 1.26$. The in-sample correction recovers most,
   but not all, of the gap to new inputs.

**Selection adds optimism.** Let the target be pure noise, $\yvec = \epsilonb$ with $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $n = 100$, and
let $\xvec_1, \dots, \xvec_M$ be $M = 100$ orthonormal candidate features. The procedure picks the single feature most
correlated with $\yvec$ and fits its coefficient by least squares. Ye called the covariance-based count of such a
procedure its generalised degrees of freedom <Cite id="ye1998" />.

1. **The fit.** Write $z_j = \xvec_j^\top\yvec$. The $z_j$ are independent $\Gauss(0, 1)$, because the features are
   orthonormal. The procedure picks $\hat\jmath$ with the largest $z_j^2$ and fits $\hat\yvec = z_{\hat\jmath}\,
   \xvec_{\hat\jmath}$.
2. **Its degrees of freedom.** With $f = 0$, $\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i) = \expect[\hat\yvec^\top\epsilonb] =
   \expect[z_{\hat\jmath}\,\xvec_{\hat\jmath}^\top\epsilonb] = \expect[z_{\hat\jmath}^2] = \expect[\max_j z_j^2]$. The
   largest of 100 independent $\chi^2_1$ variables has mean $7.71$, by <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical
   integration</NoteLink> of its tail. So $\operatorname{df} = 7.71$, not 1.
3. **The errors.** The expected training error is $(n - 7.71)/n = 0.923$. The expected in-sample error is
   $1 + \expect\norm{\hat\yvec}^2/n = 1.077$. The optimism is $0.154 = 2 \times 7.71/100$.
4. **The wrong correction.** Counting one parameter gives $C_p = 0.923 + 0.02 = 0.943$. The empty model $\hat\yvec = 0$
   has $C_p = 1.00$. The naive count prefers the noise feature. The honest count, $0.923 + 0.154 = 1.077$, rejects it.

The figure above simulates this procedure when it is set to the best of $M$ noise features with $n = 100$. At
$M = 100$, 500 training sets give $\operatorname{df} = 7.77 \pm 0.11$ and an optimism of $0.157$.

## Pitfalls

- **Counting parameters after a search.** Selection, stepwise regression, <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early
  stopping</NoteLink> and tuning all use $\yvec$ to decide the model. Their optimism exceeds $2p\sigma^2/n$ for the $p$
  coefficients finally fitted, as the worked example shows. The covariance definition counts the search; a parameter
  count does not.
- **Estimating $\sigma^2$ from the same flexible fit.** A model that overfits has a small residual variance, which
  shrinks the correction exactly when it is needed. Estimate $\sigma^2$ from a low-bias model with few degrees of
  freedom relative to $n$, or from replicates.
- **Reading in-sample error as test error.** The optimism corrects for fitting noise at the training inputs. It does not
  account for new inputs, which raise the error further, especially for flexible fits that extrapolate. Use a held-out
  set or <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> when the inputs at deployment are new.
- **The optimism is an expectation.** On one sample the realised gap $\operatorname{op}$ can be larger, smaller or even
  negative. The correction is unbiased on average, not exact.
- **Optimism one level up.** The validation score of a model chosen from many candidates is optimistic for the same
  reason the training error is: the score helped make the choice (see
  <NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>).
