<Definition>
Let a family of models be ordered by a flexibility parameter $c$, such as a polynomial degree, a number of parameters
or an inverse penalty $1/\lambda$. Fit each member to the same training sample $\Dcal$ of $n$ examples
$(\xvec_i, y_i)$. Let $\overline{\operatorname{err}}(c)$ be the fitted model's average loss on $\Dcal$, its **training
error**, and let $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal(c)$ be its expected loss on a new example from the same distribution, its
**test error**. A member **overfits** when a less flexible member has lower test error while its own training error is
lower. A member **underfits** when a more flexible member has lower test error and lower training error
<Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A training sample carries two things: the signal, which new data share, and the noise, which is particular to this
sample. A model that is too rigid cannot represent the signal. It makes the same systematic error on the training
points and on new points, so both errors are high and close together. That is underfitting. A model that is flexible
enough to represent the signal can, if it is more flexible still, also bend towards the noise of individual training
points. The bends lower the training error. On new points the noise is different, so the bends add error instead of
removing it. That is overfitting.

The two failures are the two ends of the <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance trade-off</NoteLink>
<Cite id="geman1992" />. Underfitting is high bias: the average fit over many training samples misses the truth.
Overfitting is high variance: the fit changes a lot from one training sample to the next, because each sample's noise
pulls it a different way.

## Training and test error against flexibility

**Training error never rises with flexibility.** Suppose the model classes are nested, $\Hcal_1 \subseteq \Hcal_2
\subseteq \cdots$, as polynomials of increasing degree are, and each model minimises the training loss over its class.
The minimiser over $\Hcal_c$ is also a member of $\Hcal_{c+1}$, so the minimum over $\Hcal_{c+1}$ is at most as large:

$$
\overline{\operatorname{err}}(c + 1) = \min_{h \in \Hcal_{c+1}} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, h(\xvec_i)) \le
\min_{h \in \Hcal_c} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, h(\xvec_i)) = \overline{\operatorname{err}}(c).
$$

A class rich enough to interpolate the $n$ points, such as polynomials of degree $n - 1$ in one input with distinct
$x_i$, reaches a training error of zero.

**Test error is U-shaped.** For squared error, the expected test error at an input splits into the noise variance
$\sigma^2$, the squared bias and the variance of the fit (see <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance
decomposition</NoteLink>). Added flexibility lowers the bias, quickly at first while the class gains the shape of the
signal, then slowly once the signal is captured. Added flexibility raises the variance throughout: for least squares on
a fixed design each extra coefficient adds $\sigma^2/n$ to the variance averaged over the training inputs. The sum
falls, flattens and rises. The bottom of the U is the best flexibility for this family at this sample size. The left arm
is underfitting and the right arm is overfitting <Cite id="hastie2009" />.

**Training error cannot choose the flexibility.** Training error is minimised by the most flexible member of the family,
so selecting by training error always overfits. The gap between the two errors is the
<NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation gap</NoteLink>. For least squares with $p$ coefficients the expected
gap at the training inputs is $2p\sigma^2/n$, which grows with the flexibility the training error rewards (see
<NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>). Model selection replaces the
training error by an estimate of the test error: a held-out set,
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or an <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information
criterion</NoteLink>.

## Symptoms

The test error is unknown, so overfitting and underfitting are recognised from quantities that can be measured.

- **A large gap between training and validation error** signals overfitting. A training accuracy of 100% with a
  validation accuracy far lower is the extreme case.
- **Training and validation error both high and close together** signal underfitting. More flexibility would lower
  both.
- **Validation error that rises while training error keeps falling**, along a hyperparameter or along training
  iterations, locates the onset of overfitting. A <NoteLink to="validation-curves">validation curve</NoteLink> plots the
  first; <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> stops at the second.
- **Fits that change a lot under resampling.** Refitting on <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> resamples or
  on cross-validation folds gives very different predictions or coefficients when the variance is high.
- **Large or oscillating coefficients.** A high-degree polynomial through noisy points has coefficients of alternating
  sign and large magnitude, which cancel at the data and swing between them.
- **Structure left in the residuals.** Residuals that trend with an input, or a learning curve that flattens at a high
  error, signal underfitting (see <NoteLink to="learning-curves-and-sample-size">learning curves and sample
  size</NoteLink>).

## Remedies

Each remedy moves the model along the trade-off, so each fixes one failure at the risk of the other.

- **Against overfitting:**
  - collect more training data, which lowers the variance at every flexibility and moves the bottom of the U towards
    more flexible models;
  - reduce the flexibility directly: fewer features or basis functions, shallower trees, larger $k$ in
    <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours">$k$-nearest neighbours</NoteLink>;
  - add a penalty, as in <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> and the other forms of
    <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>;
  - average many fits, as <NoteLink to="bagging">bagging</NoteLink> does;
  - stop training early, inject noise or apply <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> and
    <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">data augmentation</NoteLink> in neural networks.
- **Against underfitting:**
  - add features, basis functions or capacity;
  - weaken the penalty;
  - train longer, since an optimiser that has not converged underfits whatever the class.

The amount of each remedy is itself chosen on held-out data. That choice is <NoteLink to="model-selection">model
selection</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Draw $n = 20$ training points with $x$ uniform on $[-1, 1]$ and $y = \sin(\pi x) + \varepsilon$, where the noise has
standard deviation $\sigma = 0.3$, so $\sigma^2 = 0.09$. Fit polynomials of degree $d$ by least squares and measure each
fit on 2000 fresh test points from the same distribution. The figure above at its default settings shows this sample.

| Degree $d$ | Coefficients $p$ | Training error | Test error | Test − training |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 | 0.272 | 0.287 | 0.015 |
| 3 | 4 | 0.105 | 0.113 | 0.008 |
| 9 | 10 | 0.058 | 4.03 | 3.97 |

1. **Degree 1 underfits.** A straight line cannot follow one period of a sine. Its training and test errors are both
   about three times the noise variance $0.09$, and they nearly agree: the line makes the same systematic error
   everywhere.
2. **Degree 3 fits well.** A cubic captures the shape of $\sin(\pi x)$ on $[-1, 1]$. Its test error, $0.113$, is
   $0.023$ above the noise floor $\sigma^2 = 0.09$, which no predictor can beat.
3. **Degree 9 overfits.** Ten coefficients for 20 points let the curve chase individual noisy points. The training
   error falls to $0.058$, below the noise variance, which is possible only because the fit has absorbed some of the
   training noise. The test error is $4.03$, 45 times the noise floor. The 11% of test points that fall outside the
   training range $[-0.93, 0.83]$ contribute 96% of it, because the polynomial swings far from the data there. Inside
   the range the test error is $0.203$, still almost twice that of the cubic.
4. **Training error chooses wrongly.** Training error falls at every degree in the table, so it would pick degree 9, or
   degree 15 if offered. Test error picks degree 3.

## Pitfalls

- **The U-shape is not a law.** For models with far more parameters than examples, test error can fall a second time
  after the model interpolates the training data, a pattern called <NoteLink to="double-descent">double
  descent</NoteLink> <Cite id="belkin2019" />. Interpolating noisy data can then predict well (see
  <NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink>). Zero training error is not by itself evidence of
  overfitting. Test error is.
- **Parameter count is not flexibility.** A heavily penalised model with many parameters can be more rigid than a small
  unpenalised one. Neural networks large enough to fit random labels exactly can still generalise when trained on true
  labels <Cite id="zhang2017" />. Measure flexibility by behaviour, for example by
  <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>.
- **A small gap is not a good model.** A constant predictor has almost no gap and a large error. Underfitting shows as
  high error with a small gap.
- **Selection overfits too.** Choosing among many candidates by their validation error fits the noise of the validation
  set, in the same way that fitting parameters fits the noise of the training set (see
  <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). The validation error of the
  winner is then optimistic.
- **Leakage hides overfitting.** Duplicates across the split, or preprocessing fitted on all the data, make the
  validation error look like the training error (see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>).
- **Overfitting is relative to the sample size.** A model that overfits 20 points can be the best choice for 2000.
  When the data and prior knowledge do not determine the right model, a flexible learner encodes random quirks of the
  sample instead <Cite id="domingos2012" />.
