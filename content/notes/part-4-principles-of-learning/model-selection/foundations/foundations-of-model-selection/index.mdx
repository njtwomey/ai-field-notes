<Definition>
The foundations of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink> explain why a model cannot be chosen by
how well it fits its own training data, and set out what is used instead. Training error falls as a model grows more
flexible, while error on new data first falls and then rises. Model selection therefore estimates the error on new
data, and keeps the data that choose a model apart from the data that assess it <Cite id="hastie2009" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every learner has settings that fix how flexible it is: a polynomial degree, a penalty $\lambda$, a number of
neighbours, a tree depth, the size of a network, the number of training steps. Choosing them is the most common decision
in <NoteLink to="machine-learning-in-practice">applied machine learning</NoteLink>, and it is made on data. These notes
give the reasoning behind that decision. They say what goes wrong when the training error is used, how large the error
is, which data each estimate may use, and how to read the result. The rest of the chapter, from
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> to <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter
optimisation</NoteLink>, builds on them.

## Main ideas

**Training error is biased, and the bias grows with flexibility.** A model that is too rigid misses the signal and has
high error on training and new data alike. A model that is too flexible fits the noise of its sample, so its training
error is low and its error on new data high (<NoteLink to="overfitting-and-underfitting">overfitting and
underfitting</NoteLink>). Training error is minimised by the most flexible candidate, so it cannot choose.

**The bias has a formula.** The <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training error</NoteLink>
is the gap between the error on fresh targets at the training inputs and the training error. For squared error its
expectation is $\frac2n\sum_i\cov(\hat y_i, y_i)$, which is $2p\sigma^2/n$ for least squares with $p$ coefficients.
Adding an estimate of it to the training error gives Mallows' $C_p$, the <Gloss name="aic">Akaike information
criterion</Gloss> and <NoteLink to="steins-unbiased-risk-estimate">Stein's unbiased risk estimate</NoteLink>
<Cite id="efron2004covariance" />. Choosing among models with the same data adds optimism that a parameter count misses.

**Selection and assessment need separate data.** Choosing the candidate with the lowest validation error is itself a
fit, and the winner's validation error is biased low. <NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">Model
selection and model assessment</NoteLink> keeps the two tasks apart: a training set to fit, a validation set to choose
and a test set to assess, or, when data are scarce, <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested
cross-validation</NoteLink>. This data-reuse argument runs through the whole chapter.

**A validation curve shows where a learner fails.** Plotting training and cross-validated error against one
hyperparameter separates underfitting, where both are high, from overfitting, where they diverge
(<NoteLink to="validation-curves">validation curves</NoteLink>). Its minimum is the selection, and its
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink> show how sharply the data determine it.

**Large models change which tools are affordable.** When one training run takes weeks, $K$-fold cross-validation gives
way to one large validation set, hyperparameters are tuned on small proxy models and transferred, and scaling laws
extrapolate from small runs. Assessment then has to guard against test data that leaked into the training corpus
(<NoteLink to="model-selection-at-scale">model selection at scale</NoteLink>).
