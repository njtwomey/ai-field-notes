<Definition>
Draw 100 points with $x_i \sim \Unif(-1, 1)$ and $y_i = \sin(\pi x_i) + \varepsilon_i$, where
$\varepsilon_i \sim \Gauss(0, 0.3^2)$. Keep the first 75 as the development set $\Dcal$ and lock the last 25 away as a
test set. The learner is a <NoteLink to="p-splines">P-spline</NoteLink> smoother with penalty $\lambda$, and the
candidates are 25 values of $\lambda$ spaced evenly in $\log\lambda$ from $10^{-4}$ to $10^4$. On $\Dcal$:

1. draw the validation curve, the training error and the 10-fold cross-validation error against $\lambda$;
2. estimate each candidate's error by 10-fold cross-validation, with fold standard errors;
3. choose $\lambda$ by the minimum and by the one-standard-error rule;
4. measure how optimistic the minimum cross-validation score is, against the true error of the chosen fit;
5. estimate the error of the whole procedure by nested cross-validation;
6. refit at the chosen $\lambda$ on all of $\Dcal$ and score the fit once on the test set.

The true error of any fit is measured on 5,000 fresh points from the same distribution.
</Definition>

## Setup

The P-spline fit $\hat f_\lambda$ is a sum of 18 cubic B-splines on 15 equal intervals of $[-1, 1]$, with
coefficients $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_{18}$. The coefficients minimise

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \paren{y_i - \hat f_\lambda(x_i)}^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=3}^{18}\paren{\beta_j - 2\beta_{j-1} + \beta_{j-2}}^2
$$

over the $n$ rows fitted <Cite id="eilers1996" />. The penalty charges second differences of neighbouring
coefficients, so a large $\lambda$ forces the coefficients onto a straight line and the fit towards a line in $x$. The
fit is a linear smoother, $\hat\yvec = \Smat_\lambda\yvec$, and its effective degrees of freedom
$\operatorname{df}(\lambda) = \trace\Smat_\lambda$ fall from 18 at $\lambda = 0$ to 2 as $\lambda \to \infty$ (see
<NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective degrees of freedom</NoteLink>). A larger $\lambda$ is a simpler
model.

The loss is squared error. For the fit to the 75 development rows:

- the **training error** $\overline{\operatorname{err}}(\lambda)$ is its <Gloss name="mse" /> on those 75 rows;
- the **true error** $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal(\lambda) = \expect\brack{(Y - \hat f_\lambda(X))^2}$ is its expected
  squared error on a new point, with $\Dcal$ held fixed. It is at least the noise variance $\sigma^2 = 0.09$, and the
  excess over 0.09 is the fit's squared distance from $\sin(\pi x)$;
- $\operatorname{CV}(\lambda) = \frac1{10}\sum_{k=1}^{10} e_k(\lambda)$ is the mean of the fold errors $e_k(\lambda)$ of
  10-fold cross-validation, where $e_k$ is the mean squared error on fold $k$ of the fit to the other nine folds;
- $\operatorname{SE}(\lambda) = s(\lambda)/\sqrt{10}$ is its <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>,
  with $s(\lambda)$ the standard deviation of the ten fold errors.

The minimiser is $\hat\lambda_\text{CV} = \argmin_\lambda \operatorname{CV}(\lambda)$. The
<NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error rule</NoteLink> chooses $\hat\lambda_\text{1SE}$, the largest
$\lambda$ with
$\operatorname{CV}(\lambda) \le \operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda_\text{CV}) + \operatorname{SE}(\hat\lambda_\text{CV})$
<Cite id="breiman1984" />.

Each step answers one of the three questions of <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>. **Estimate**
asks how well a candidate will predict. **Select** asks which candidate to keep. **Assess** asks how well the kept model
will predict. A step that answers a later question with data already used for an earlier one gives an optimistic
answer <Cite id="hastie2009" />. Each step below names its question and the data it uses.

## Solution

All numbers are for data set 56, the one the figure opens on. Its numbers sit close to the averages over 200 data sets,
which are given under Simulation.

### Step 1: a validation curve

The <NoteLink to="validation-curves">validation curve</NoteLink> plots the training error and the cross-validation
error against $\lambda$. It is a diagnostic for the estimate step. Part of it:

| $\lambda$ | $\operatorname{df}$ | $\overline{\operatorname{err}}$ | $\operatorname{CV}$ | $\operatorname{SE}$ | $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{-4}$ | 17.86 | 0.0616 | 0.1585 | 0.0464 | 0.1143 |
| 0.1 | 11.30 | 0.0669 | 0.0967 | 0.0192 | 0.1005 |
| 1 | 7.85 | 0.0728 | 0.0932 | 0.0170 | 0.0966 |
| 2.15 | 6.87 | 0.0744 | 0.0921 | 0.0161 | 0.0963 |
| 4.64 | 5.98 | 0.0762 | 0.0919 | 0.0154 | 0.0968 |
| 10 | 5.21 | 0.0796 | 0.0948 | 0.0148 | 0.0997 |
| 21.5 | 4.53 | 0.0886 | 0.1062 | 0.0149 | 0.1094 |
| 46.4 | 3.95 | 0.1108 | 0.1340 | 0.0166 | 0.1338 |
| $10^4$ | 2.05 | 0.2716 | 0.2961 | 0.0281 | 0.3111 |

The training error falls at every step from $\lambda = 10^4$ to $\lambda = 10^{-4}$, from 0.2716 to 0.0616. At
$\lambda = 10^4$ the fit is close to a straight line, and both errors are high and close together: the model underfits.
At $\lambda = 10^{-4}$ the training error is 0.0616, below the noise variance 0.09, while the cross-validation error is
0.1585: the model overfits. The training error scores each fit on the rows it was fitted to. It is optimistic by an
amount that grows with $\operatorname{df}$ (see <NoteLink to="optimism-of-the-training-error">optimism of the training
error</NoteLink>), so it would select the smallest $\lambda$ on the grid.

### Step 2: cross-validate

This step answers **estimate**. The 75 rows are split once into 10 folds of 7 or 8 rows, and every candidate is scored
on the same folds, so that differences between candidates are not differences in the split. Each fold model is fitted
to 67 or 68 rows and scored on the rest. Every row validates once and trains nine fold models, and no row scores a
model that was fitted to it (see <NoteLink to="k-fold-cross-validation">$K$-fold cross-validation</NoteLink>).

The curve is lowest at $\hat\lambda_\text{CV} = 4.64$, with $\operatorname{CV} = 0.0919$, $\operatorname{SE} = 0.0154$
and $\operatorname{df} = 5.98$. Between $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = 10$ the curve varies by 0.003, a fifth of one
standard error. The folds cannot place the minimum more precisely than that range. The standard error itself is too
small, because the fold errors share training rows and are positively correlated (see
<NoteLink to="variance-of-cross-validation-estimates">variance of cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>).

### Step 3: choose with one standard error

This step answers **select**. It reuses the cross-validation scores of step 2 and computes nothing new. To more digits,
the minimum is 0.09186 and its standard error 0.01538, so the threshold is

$$
\operatorname{CV}(\hat\lambda_\text{CV}) + \operatorname{SE}(\hat\lambda_\text{CV}) = 0.09186 + 0.01538 = 0.1072.
$$

The largest $\lambda$ with $\operatorname{CV}(\lambda) \le 0.1072$ is 21.5, with 0.1062; the next value, 46.4, has
0.1340. So $\hat\lambda_\text{1SE} = 21.5$, with 4.53 effective degrees of freedom against 5.98 at the minimum.

### Step 4: how optimistic is the minimum?

This step uses the truth, which only a simulation has. It asks how well the cross-validation minimum answers
**assess**, the question it was not designed for.

The fit at $\hat\lambda_\text{CV} = 4.64$ has true error $\operatorname{Err}_\Dcal = 0.0968$. The best value on the
grid is 0.0963, at $\lambda = 2.15$, so the cross-validation choice loses 0.0005. The one-standard-error choice has
true error 0.1094. Measured as excess over the noise variance, the minimum's fit is 0.0068 from $\sin(\pi x)$ and the
one-standard-error fit 0.0194, almost three times as far.

The best cross-validation score, 0.0919, is 0.0049 below the true error 0.0968 of the fit it chose. The training error
at the same $\lambda$, 0.0762, is 0.0206 below it. Two biases act on the minimum. The fold models are fitted to 67 or 68
rows rather than 75, which makes each score pessimistic. Taking the smallest of 25 noisy scores favours the candidate
whose folds happened to be easy, which makes the minimum optimistic (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). The 75 development rows have
both chosen $\lambda$ and scored the choice.

One data set cannot separate a bias from noise. Over 200 data sets, the best score averaged 0.0969 against an average
true error of 0.1012 for the chosen fit: optimistic by 0.0043, about 4%, which is three Monte Carlo standard errors.
On any one data set the gap scattered with standard deviation 0.019, four times the bias, and the best score was below
its true error on 109 of the 200. The optimism is small here because the 25 candidates form a one-parameter family
whose scores rise and fall together. With many unrelated candidates it grows like $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln J}$ for $J$
candidates, as <NoteLink to="how-optimistic-is-the-best-cross-validation-score" /> shows on pure noise.

### Step 5: nested cross-validation

This step answers **assess** honestly. <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">Nested cross-validation</NoteLink> splits
the 75 rows into 10 outer folds. Inside each outer training set of 67 or 68 rows, it runs the whole search of step 2:
an inner 10-fold cross-validation of all 25 penalties, fitted on about 61 rows each, the minimum, and a refit on the
outer training set. The refitted model is then scored on the outer test fold, which took no part in the search
<Cite id="varma2006" />. The cost is $10 \times 25 \times 10 = 2{,}500$ fits for the inner searches, against 250 for
step 2.

The search chose $\lambda = 4.64$ in seven outer folds, and 2.15, 0.1 and 0.0464 in the other three. The ten outer
scores ranged from 0.022 to 0.209. Their mean, the nested estimate, is 0.0949 with standard error 0.0177. The true
error of the final model is 0.0968.

The nested estimate assesses the procedure "search 25 penalties by 10-fold cross-validation, then refit at the
minimum", not the one value $\lambda = 4.64$ <Cite id="cawley2010" />. Over 200 data sets it averaged 0.1002 against
0.1012, a difference of 0.0010, smaller than its Monte Carlo standard error of 0.0014. It removes the bias of step 4.
It does not remove the variance: on one data set it erred with standard deviation 0.019, as the best score did, and it
was within one standard error of the truth on 126 of the 200. Cross-validation tracks the expected error of a
procedure more closely than the error of the one fit at hand (see
<NoteLink to="what-cross-validation-estimates">what cross-validation estimates</NoteLink>).

### Step 6: the test set

This step answers **assess** with data nothing has touched. The final model is the P-spline at
$\hat\lambda_\text{CV} = 4.64$ refitted on all 75 development rows, the model the search returns. Its mean squared error
on the 25 test rows is 0.0888, with standard error 0.0204 (the standard deviation of the 25 squared errors over
$\sqrt{25}$). The truth, 0.0968, lies within half a standard error.

Over 200 data sets the test error averaged 0.0990 against 0.1012, a difference within simulation error. The test set
is unbiased because it is independent of every choice. It is also the noisiest estimate here, with standard deviation
0.027 about the truth, because it averages 25 squared errors where the nested estimate averages 75. The test rows stay
honest only while they are used once. Scoring the one-standard-error fit on them as well, and keeping the better of the
two, would make them validation data (see <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink>).

The procedure was repeated on 200 data sets (seeds 1 to 200), each with its own development and test rows and the
same 5,000 fresh points for the truth. Each estimate is compared with the true error of the final model, the fit at
$\hat\lambda_\text{CV}$, which averaged 0.1012:

| Estimate of the final model's error | Mean | Mean minus truth | Standard deviation of (estimate minus truth) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| training error at $\hat\lambda_\text{CV}$ | 0.0796 | −0.0216 | 0.018 |
| best 10-fold cross-validation score | 0.0969 | −0.0043 | 0.019 |
| nested 10 × 10-fold cross-validation | 0.1002 | −0.0010 | 0.019 |
| test error on 25 rows | 0.0990 | −0.0023 | 0.027 |

The Monte Carlo standard error of each mean difference is about 0.0014, and 0.0019 for the test set. The training
error and the best score are biased low; the nested and test estimates are not detectably biased.

The true errors of the choices themselves:

| Choice | Mean true error | Mean excess over $\sigma^2 = 0.09$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| best $\lambda$ on the grid | 0.0989 | 0.0089 |
| cross-validation minimum | 0.1012 | 0.0112 |
| one-standard-error rule | 0.1075 | 0.0175 |

The one-standard-error choice had 4.85 effective degrees of freedom on average, against 6.97 for the minimum. It had the
lower true error on 38 of the 200 data sets.

## What it illustrates

| Step | Question | Data it uses | Optimistic? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Training error | estimate | the rows each fit was fitted to | yes, by about 0.02 |
| 2. 10-fold CV | estimate | each row scores fits that did not see it | no; slightly pessimistic |
| 3. One-standard-error rule | select | the scores of step 2 | not an estimate |
| 4. Best CV score | assess | the rows that chose $\lambda$ | yes, by about 0.004 |
| 5. Nested CV | assess | outer folds the search never saw | no |
| 6. Test set | assess | 25 rows used once | no |

- **Training error cannot select.** It falls with every reduction in $\lambda$, below the noise variance at the flexible
  end (<NoteLink to="overfitting-and-underfitting">overfitting and underfitting</NoteLink>).
- **The folds resolve the minimum only roughly.** On data set 56 every $\lambda$ from 0.00215 to 21.5, four decades,
  scores within one standard error of the minimum. The <NoteLink to="one-standard-error-rule">one-standard-error
  rule</NoteLink> uses that slack to buy a simpler fit. Here the simpler fit cost 0.006 in mean true error.
- **Reusing data for assessment is optimistic.** The best cross-validation score is biased low because the same folds
  chose the candidate (<NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>). For a
  one-parameter family the bias is small; for a large search it is not.
- **Honest assessment removes bias, not variance.** Nested cross-validation and the test set were unbiased over 200 data
  sets, and on any one data set each was off by about 0.02. A reported error needs its standard error
  (<NoteLink to="model-selection-and-model-assessment">model selection and model assessment</NoteLink>).
- **Nested cross-validation and the test set compete for rows.** With 100 rows, locking 25 away leaves 75 for fitting
  and gives the noisiest of the honest estimates. On data this small, nested cross-validation over all 100 rows is the
  usual alternative (<NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">train, validation and test splits</NoteLink>).

## Variations

- **A larger search.** Searching the number of intervals as well as $\lambda$, or several learners at once, gives more
  candidates whose scores do not move together. The best score's optimism grows, and the gap between it and the nested
  estimate widens (<NoteLink to="how-optimistic-is-the-best-cross-validation-score" />).
- **More data.** Standard errors shrink like $1/\sqrt n$, the minimum is located more precisely, and a single validation
  split becomes adequate (<NoteLink to="hold-out-validation">hold-out validation</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="model-selection-at-scale">model selection at scale</NoteLink>).
- **Closed forms.** The P-spline is a linear smoother, so leave-one-out and
  <NoteLink to="generalised-cross-validation">generalised cross-validation</NoteLink> need one fit per $\lambda$
  instead of ten (<NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-ridge-penalty">cross-validating a ridge penalty</NoteLink>).
- **Structured or preprocessed data.** Grouped or time-ordered rows need folds that respect the structure
  (<NoteLink to="grouped-cross-validation">grouped cross-validation</NoteLink>), and any step fitted to data belongs
  inside the folds (<NoteLink to="cross-validating-a-pipeline">cross-validating a pipeline</NoteLink>).
