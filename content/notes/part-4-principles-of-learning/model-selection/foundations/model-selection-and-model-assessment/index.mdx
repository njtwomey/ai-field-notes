<Definition>
Let $\Mcal_1, \dots, \Mcal_J$ be candidate models: different algorithms, feature sets or hyperparameter values.
**<NoteLink to="model-selection">Model selection</NoteLink>** estimates the test error $\operatorname{Err}_j$ of each
candidate and chooses the one with the smallest estimate. **Model assessment** estimates the test error of the chosen
model, the number to report and to act on <Cite id="hastie2009" />. The two need separate data, because the estimate
that chose a model is biased low as an estimate of that model's error.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Choosing the candidate with the best validation score is a fit. The fit has $J$ options instead of a continuum of
parameters, and its data are the validation set instead of the training set. Like any fit it exploits noise: a candidate
whose validation points happened to be easy scores better than its true error, and the selection favours exactly such
candidates. After the choice, the winner's validation score is a training error for the selection step. Reporting it as
the model's performance repeats, one level up, the mistake of reporting the training error of a fitted model (see
<NoteLink to="overfitting-and-underfitting">overfitting and underfitting</NoteLink>).

An honest assessment needs data that played no part in any choice. Then the chosen model is fixed before those data are
looked at, and their average loss is an unbiased estimate of its error.

## The two goals

**Selection needs a ranking.** Selection succeeds when it picks a candidate whose test error is close to the smallest
among the candidates. An estimator with a constant bias, such as one that overstates every error by $0.02$, ranks the
candidates correctly and selects well. Cross-validation trained on $(K - 1)n/K$ points has such a bias, and it still
selects well when the bias is similar across candidates.

**Assessment needs a number.** Assessment succeeds when its estimate is close to the chosen model's error, with a
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> small enough to act on. A constant bias that is harmless for
selection is a direct error in the assessment.

**Selecting on an estimate makes it optimistic.** Let $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j$ be an unbiased estimate of
$\operatorname{Err}_j$, such as the mean loss on a validation set that the candidates were not trained on, and let
$\hat\jmath = \argmin_j \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j$ be the selected candidate. Three steps give the direction of
the bias.

1. $\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath} \le \widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j$ for every $j$, on every validation
   set, because $\hat\jmath$ minimises the estimate.
2. Taking expectations, $\expect[\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath}] \le \expect[\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_j]
   = \operatorname{Err}_j$ for every $j$, so $\expect[\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath}] \le \min_j
   \operatorname{Err}_j$.
3. $\min_j \operatorname{Err}_j \le \operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath}$ on every validation set, because the chosen
   candidate is one of the $J$.

Joining the steps,

$$
\expect\brack{\widehat{\operatorname{Err}}_{\hat\jmath}} \le \min_j \operatorname{Err}_j \le
\expect\brack{\operatorname{Err}_{\hat\jmath}}.
$$

The selected score understates even the best candidate's error, and the best candidate's error understates the error
of the candidate actually chosen. The gap grows with the number of candidates and with the noise in each estimate. For
$J$ independent Gaussian estimates with a common standard deviation $\sigma$, it is at most about
$\sigma\sqrt{2\ln J}$ (see <NoteLink to="overfitting-in-model-selection">overfitting in model selection</NoteLink>).

## The three-way split

With enough data, the standard protocol splits the examples at random into three disjoint parts
<Cite id="hastie2009" />:

- a **training set**, on which every candidate is fitted;
- a **validation set**, on which each fitted candidate is scored, and the best is chosen;
- a **test set**, used once, to score the chosen model.

Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman suggest 50% for training and 25% each for validation and test as a typical split
<Cite id="hastie2009" />. The test estimate is unbiased because, given the training and validation sets, the chosen
model is fixed, and the test losses are $n_\text{test}$ independent draws whose mean is its test error. For
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> and a true <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error
rate</NoteLink> $e$, the test estimate has standard error $\sqrt{e(1 - e)/n_\text{test}}$. The split itself, and how to
make it mimic deployment (by group, by time, by place), are covered in
<NoteLink to="train-validation-and-test-splits">train, validation and test splits</NoteLink>.

After assessment, the chosen model is usually refitted on the training and validation sets together, or on all the
data, before deployment. The refitted model has seen more data, so its error is at most a little above the test
estimate when the learning curve is still falling. The test estimate is then slightly conservative for the deployed
model.

## When data are scarce

A three-way split wastes data when $n$ is small. A quarter of the data scores the candidates and another quarter sits
in the test set, and each estimate is noisy. Two protocols reuse the data instead.

- **Cross-validation for selection, a test set for assessment.** $K$-fold
  <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> on the non-test data replaces the single validation set,
  so every non-test point serves for both fitting and scoring. The test set remains untouched until the end.
- **Nested cross-validation for both.** An inner cross-validation selects within each training set of an outer one, and
  the outer test folds score the selected models <Cite id="varma2006" />. No outer fold informs the selection that it
  scores. The outer average estimates the error of the whole procedure, "select by inner cross-validation, then refit",
  rather than of one fitted model (see <NoteLink to="nested-cross-validation">nested cross-validation</NoteLink>).

Cawley and Talbot showed that, with many hyperparameters and few examples, the optimism of the best cross-validation
score can exceed the differences between the learning algorithms being compared <Cite id="cawley2010" />. The best
cross-validation score is a selection statistic, not an assessment.

## Worked example

Twenty classifiers are scored on a validation set of $n_\text{val} = 100$ examples. Suppose all twenty have the same
true error rate $e = 0.30$, and that their validation errors are independent.

1. **The noise in one estimate.** Each validation error is a proportion over 100 examples, with standard deviation
   $\sigma = \sqrt{0.30 \times 0.70/100} = 0.0458$. A normal approximation is adequate at this size.
2. **The winner's score.** The smallest of 20 independent normal draws lies on average $1.87$ standard deviations below
   their common mean. The winner's expected validation error is therefore $0.30 - 1.87 \times 0.0458 = 0.214$.
3. **The bound.** The maximal-inequality bound $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln J}$ gives $0.0458 \times \sqrt{2\ln 20} = 0.0458 \times
   2.45 = 0.112$, against the exact $0.086$.
4. **The truth.** Every candidate has error $0.30$, so the winner has error $0.30$. The selection gained nothing, and
   its reported score of $0.21$ overstates the accuracy by 8.6 percentage points.
5. **The assessment.** A test set of $n_\text{test} = 1000$ examples scores the winner with standard error
   $\sqrt{0.30 \times 0.70/1000} = 0.0145$. Its estimate falls within $0.30 \pm 0.028$ with probability about 0.95.

Correlated candidates, such as one model at neighbouring hyperparameter values, have correlated validation errors, and
the optimism is smaller than for independent ones. Genuinely different true errors also shrink it, because the
selection then picks the better candidates for real reasons.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting the selection score.** The best validation or cross-validation score among many candidates is biased low.
  Report a test score, or a nested cross-validation estimate.
- **Choosing on the test set.** Every decision made after looking at test scores, such as keeping one feature set over
  another or stopping training at the best test epoch, turns the test set into a validation set. Repeated queries
  overfit a test set in the same way <Cite id="dwork2015" />. Competition leaderboards limit this with submission
  budgets and with mechanisms such as the Ladder <Cite id="blum2015ladder" />. A study of over a hundred Kaggle
  competitions found little overfitting of the public leaderboards in practice <Cite id="roelofs2019" />. See
  <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="reusable-holdout">reusable
  holdout</NoteLink>.
- **Selection hidden in preprocessing.** <NoteLink to="feature-selection">Feature selection</NoteLink>, imputation or
  scaling fitted on all the data before the split is selection on the test data. Every data-dependent step belongs
  inside the training part (see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>).
- **A test set too small to assess.** With 100 test examples and an error near 0.3, the standard error is 0.046. Two
  models 3 points apart cannot be told apart on such a test set.
- **Assessing a different model from the one deployed.** The test set scores the model fitted on the training data.
  Nested cross-validation scores the procedure. Either is a proxy for the refitted model that is finally deployed, and
  the report should say which it is.
