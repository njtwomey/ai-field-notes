<Definition>
For a predicted region $A$ and a true region $B$, the Dice coefficient is <Cite id="dice1945" />

$$
D = \frac{2\abs{A \cap B}}{\abs{A} + \abs{B}} \in [0, 1].
$$

For a segmentation into classes, the generalised Dice score weights each class $l$ by $w_l = 1/\paren{\sum_n r_{ln}}^2$,
where $r_{ln}$ indicates that pixel $n$ belongs to class $l$ <Cite id="sudre2017" />, and mean <Gloss name="iou" />
averages the per-class IoU.
</Definition>

## Dice is the F1 score

Treat each pixel as a binary prediction. The pixels in $A \cap B$ are <Gloss name="tp">true positives</Gloss>, those in
$A$ only are <Gloss name="fp">false positives</Gloss>, and those in $B$ only are <Gloss name="fn">false
negatives</Gloss>. Then $\abs{A} + \abs{B} = 2\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}$, so

$$
D = \frac{2\,\text{TP}}{2\,\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN}} ,
$$

which is the F1 score of the pixel labels (see <NoteLink to="precision-recall-and-f-score">precision, recall and
F-score</NoteLink>). <Gloss name="tn">True negatives</Gloss>, the background pixels both agree on, do not appear. That
is why Dice suits segmentation, where the background usually dwarfs the object: pixel accuracy would be close to 1 for a
prediction that misses the object entirely.

## Dice and IoU

IoU is $J = \text{TP}/(\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN})$. Writing Dice in the same terms,

$$
D = \frac{2J}{1 + J}, \qquad J = \frac{D}{2 - D}.
$$

The map $J \mapsto 2J/(1 + J)$ is increasing, so Dice and IoU rank segmentations identically; Dice is always at least
IoU, with equality only at 0 and 1. The two differ as averages, though: the mean Dice over images is not the Dice of
the mean IoU.

**Worked example.** Two $3 \times 4$ rectangles on a grid each cover 12 pixels and overlap in 6. Then
$D = 12/24 = 0.5$ and $J = 6/18 = 0.333$, and indeed $2J/(1 + J) = 0.5$.

## Many classes

**Mean IoU** computes IoU per class over the whole test set, then averages over classes. Every class counts equally,
so a rare class matters as much as the background.

**Generalised Dice** pools the classes into one ratio,

$$
\text{GD} = \frac{2\sum_l w_l \sum_n r_{ln} p_{ln}}{\sum_l w_l \sum_n (r_{ln} + p_{ln})}, \qquad w_l = \frac{1}{\paren{\sum_n r_{ln}}^2} ,
$$

where $p_{ln}$ is the predicted label or probability. The inverse-squared-volume weights stop large classes dominating
the ratio, so small structures such as lesions still count.

## As a loss

With predicted probabilities $p_n \in [0, 1]$ in place of hard labels, $1 - D$ becomes the soft Dice loss, which is
differentiable. It optimises the overlap directly and copes with <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">class
imbalance</NoteLink> better than per-pixel cross-entropy, though its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> are
less stable when a structure is very small. The two are often summed.

## Pitfalls

- **Empty masks.** If both $A$ and $B$ are empty, Dice is $0/0$. Libraries return 1, 0, or skip the case, which changes
  averages over images where the structure is absent.
- **Boundary errors.** Dice measures volume overlap; a prediction with a thin spur far from the object loses little
  Dice. Pair it with a boundary metric such as the <NoteLink to="hausdorff-distance">Hausdorff distance</NoteLink>.
- **Size bias.** A few pixels of error are a large drop in Dice for a small structure and a negligible one for a large
  organ. Report per-structure results.
