<Definition>
For finite sets $X$ and $Y$ of points, the directed Hausdorff distance and the Hausdorff distance are
<Cite id="huttenlocher1993" />

$$
h(X, Y) = \max_{\xvec \in X} \min_{\yvec \in Y} \norm{\xvec - \yvec}, \qquad
H(X, Y) = \max\set{h(X, Y),\, h(Y, X)} .
$$
</Definition>

## Reading the definition

$h(X, Y)$ finds, for each point of $X$, the nearest point of $Y$, and reports the worst of those distances. It is not
symmetric: every point of $X$ can be close to $Y$ while some point of $Y$ is far from $X$. Taking the larger of the two
directions gives a symmetric quantity. $H$ is a metric on non-empty compact sets, and $H(X, Y) \le \varepsilon$ says
exactly that each set lies within distance $\varepsilon$ of the other.

For segmentation, $X$ and $Y$ are the boundary pixels of the predicted and true regions. $H$ is then the largest
boundary error anywhere, the complement of volume-overlap measures such as
<NoteLink to="dice-and-segmentation-overlap">Dice</NoteLink>, which barely notice a thin error far from the object.

**Worked example.** $X = \set{(0,0), (1,0), (2,0)}$ and $Y = \set{(0,1), (1,1), (2,1), (5,1)}$. Every point of $X$ is 1
from $Y$, so $h(X, Y) = 1$. The point $(5, 1)$ is $\sqrt{10} = 3.162$ from its nearest point $(2, 0)$ of $X$, so
$h(Y, X) = 3.162$ and $H = 3.162$: the single stray point decides the value.

## Robust versions

Because $H$ is a maximum, one mislabelled pixel can dominate it. Two variants use the whole distribution of boundary
distances instead:

- **HD95** replaces the maximum over both directions by the 95th percentile of all boundary-to-boundary distances.
  It is standard in medical image segmentation challenges.
- **Average symmetric surface distance** (ASSD) averages all the distances from each boundary to the other.

## Pitfalls

- **Units.** Report distances in physical units (millimetres), using the voxel spacing, not in voxels; anisotropic
  voxels distort distances otherwise.
- **Empty predictions.** $H$ is undefined when one set is empty. Challenges substitute a fixed large value, which then
  dominates averages.
- **Percentile definitions differ.** Some implementations take the 95th percentile of each direction and then the
  maximum, others pool both directions first. State which.
