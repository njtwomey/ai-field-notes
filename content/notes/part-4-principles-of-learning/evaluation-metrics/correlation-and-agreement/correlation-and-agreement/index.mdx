<Definition>
**Correlation** measures how strongly two variables are associated: whether high values of one go with high values of
the other. **Agreement** measures whether two variables are equal, or whether raters assign the same category to the
same item. Association is weaker than agreement: predictions that are always twice the target correlate perfectly
with it and agree with it nowhere.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The measures in this topic serve three purposes in machine learning. They evaluate predictions when the scale or order
matters more than the exact value, as when a model's quality scores are compared with human ratings. They screen
features for association with a target or with each other before modelling. They quantify how reliable human labels
are, by measuring agreement between annotators before those labels are used for training or evaluation.

## Main ideas

**Linear association.** <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation" /> divides the covariance of two variables by the product
of their standard deviations. Geometrically it is the cosine of the angle between the centred data vectors, so it lies
in $[-1, 1]$. It detects only linear association, and one outlier can move it far <Cite id="casella2002" />.

**Monotone association from ranks.** <NoteLink to="rank-correlation" /> replaces the values by their ranks. Spearman's
$\rho$ is the Pearson correlation of the ranks, and Kendall's $\tau$ counts concordant minus discordant pairs. Both are
unchanged by any strictly increasing transform of either variable.

**Association between categories.** For two categorical variables there is no order to rank.
<NoteLink to="nominal-association" /> rescales the chi-squared statistic of the contingency table to $[0, 1]$ as
Cramér's V or Tschuprow's T. Theil's U measures the fraction of one variable's entropy explained by the other, and it
is asymmetric.

**Agreement, with and without chance correction.** <NoteLink to="concordance-correlation-coefficient" /> measures
agreement with the line $y = x$ for continuous ratings. It equals Pearson's $r$ times a factor that penalises
differences in mean and scale. For categorical ratings, observed agreement includes agreement expected by chance from
the category frequencies. <NoteLink to="fleiss-kappa" /> removes that chance agreement for any number of raters
<Cite id="fleiss1971" />, generalising <NoteLink to="cohens-kappa" /> for two.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation" />, which the rank and concordance notes build on. The rank-correlation
note relaxes linearity to monotonicity. The concordance note tightens association to agreement. The nominal-association
and Fleiss' kappa notes handle categorical variables: the first measures association, the second agreement between
raters.
