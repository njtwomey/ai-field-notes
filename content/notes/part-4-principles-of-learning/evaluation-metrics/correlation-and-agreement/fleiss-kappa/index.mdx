<Definition>
$N$ items are each rated by $n$ raters into one of $k$ categories. Let $n_{ij}$ be the number of raters who put item
$i$ in category $j$, so $\sum_j n_{ij} = n$. Define

$$
P_i = \frac{\sum_j n_{ij}^2 - n}{n(n - 1)}, \qquad \bar P = \frac1N\sum_i P_i, \qquad
p_j = \frac{1}{Nn}\sum_i n_{ij}, \qquad \bar P_e = \sum_j p_j^2.
$$

Fleiss' kappa is $\kappa = (\bar P - \bar P_e)/(1 - \bar P_e)$ <Cite id="fleiss1971" />.
</Definition>

## Where the pieces come from

**Observed agreement.** For item $i$ there are $n(n - 1)$ ordered pairs of distinct raters. The pairs that agree on
category $j$ number $n_{ij}(n_{ij} - 1)$. Summing over categories,

$$
P_i = \frac{\sum_j n_{ij}(n_{ij} - 1)}{n(n - 1)} = \frac{\sum_j n_{ij}^2 - n}{n(n - 1)},
$$

the fraction of rater pairs that agree on item $i$. $\bar P$ averages it over items.

**Chance agreement.** $p_j$ is the share of all ratings that fall in category $j$. If two ratings were drawn
independently from these pooled frequencies, they would agree with probability $\bar P_e = \sum_j p_j^2$.

**The correction.** $\kappa$ rescales observed agreement so that chance-level agreement scores 0 and perfect agreement
scores 1:

$$
\kappa = \frac{\bar P - \bar P_e}{1 - \bar P_e}.
$$

The numerator is agreement beyond chance; the denominator is the most agreement beyond chance that was possible.
Agreement worse than chance gives a negative value.

## Relation to the two-rater kappas

Fleiss' kappa does not require the same raters for every item, only the same number. With two raters it reduces to
Scott's $\pi$, which also takes chance agreement from the pooled category frequencies <Cite id="scott1955" />.
<NoteLink to="cohens-kappa">Cohen's kappa</NoteLink> differs: it takes chance agreement from each rater's own
frequencies, so it credits two raters who disagree about how common a category is with less expected agreement.

## Worked example

Five items, four raters, three categories:

| Item | Category 1 | Category 2 | Category 3 | $P_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 4 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 2 | 2 | 2 | 0 | 1/3 |
| 3 | 0 | 3 | 1 | 1/2 |
| 4 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1/6 |
| 5 | 0 | 0 | 4 | 1 |

For item 2, $\sum_j n_{2j}^2 = 8$ and $P_2 = (8 - 4)/12 = 1/3$. So $\bar P = 0.6$. The pooled frequencies are
$p = (7, 6, 7)/20 = (0.35, 0.30, 0.35)$, so $\bar P_e = 0.335$ and

$$
\kappa = \frac{0.6 - 0.335}{1 - 0.335} = 0.398.
$$

Raters agree on 60% of pairs, but random ratings with these frequencies would agree on 33.5%, more than half of that.

## In machine learning

Fleiss' kappa is the usual report of label quality for crowdsourced and expert annotation with more than two
annotators per item. Low agreement bounds what a model can achieve: when annotators disagree, the labels themselves
are noisy, and model accuracy against any single annotator is limited.

## Pitfalls

- **The kappa paradoxes.** With one very common category, $\bar P_e$ is close to 1, and $\kappa$ can be low even when
  raw agreement is high. Report observed agreement and the category frequencies alongside $\kappa$.
- **Verbal labels are arbitrary.** Conventional bands such as "moderate agreement" for 0.4–0.6 have no statistical
  basis, and suitable thresholds depend on the task.
- **Unordered categories only.** Kappa treats every disagreement as equally bad. For ordinal scales, a weighted kappa
  or an intraclass correlation credits near misses.
