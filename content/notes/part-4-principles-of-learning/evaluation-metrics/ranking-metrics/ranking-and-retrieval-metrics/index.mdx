<Definition>
**Ranking and retrieval metrics** score an ordered list of items returned for a query against judgements of which items
are relevant. They reward relevant items placed near the top. A metric is computed per query and then averaged over a
set of queries.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Search engines, recommender systems, <Gloss name="qa">question answering</Gloss> and
<Gloss name="rag">retrieval-augmented generation</Gloss> all return a ranked list, and a user reads only its top.
<NoteLink to="classification-metrics">Classification metrics</NoteLink> ignore the order of the list. The metrics here
weight each position by how likely a user is to reach it, and they are the standard offline scores for comparing ranking
models before an online test <Cite id="manning2008" />.

## Main ideas

**Cutting the list at $k$.** <NoteLink to="precision-and-recall-at-k" /> treats the top $k$ results as a set and
computes precision and recall on it. The two scores ignore the order within the top $k$ and everything below it.
<NoteLink to="hit-rate-and-r-precision" /> covers two variants: hit rate asks whether any relevant item appears in the
top $k$, and R-precision sets $k$ to the number of relevant items $R$ for each query, where precision and recall
coincide.

**Rewarding early relevant items.** <Gloss name="ap">Average precision</Gloss> averages precision at the rank of each
relevant item, so it rewards rankings that put all relevant items early. Mean reciprocal rank uses only the first
relevant item and scores $1/\text{rank}$. <NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-and-mean-reciprocal-rank" /> compares the
two and their averages over queries.

**Graded relevance and position discounts.** When relevance has grades, such as "perfect", "good" and "fair",
<NoteLink to="normalised-discounted-cumulative-gain" /> gives each item a gain and divides it by $\log_2(\text{rank} +
1)$. Dividing the total by the score of the ideal ordering gives a value in $[0, 1]$ <Cite id="jarvelin2002" />.

**Each metric models a user.** A cut-off at $k$ models a user who reads exactly $k$ results. Reciprocal rank models a
user who stops at the first relevant result. The logarithmic discount models a user whose chance of continuing falls
slowly with depth. <NoteLink to="choosing-a-metric">Choosing a metric</NoteLink> amounts to choosing which of these
users the system serves.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="precision-and-recall-at-k" />, which defines the vocabulary of relevant items and cut-offs. Hit
rate and R-precision are short variations on it. Average precision and reciprocal rank also reward the order of the
list. NDCG adds graded relevance and closes the topic.
