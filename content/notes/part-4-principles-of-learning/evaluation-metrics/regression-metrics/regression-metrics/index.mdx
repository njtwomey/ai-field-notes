<Definition>
**Regression metrics** score predictions $\hat y_i$ of real-valued targets $y_i$ on a test set of $n$ examples. Most
are averages of a per-example error, such as $(y_i - \hat y_i)^2$ or $\abs{y_i - \hat y_i}$. Others compare that
average with a baseline, usually the prediction of the mean of $y$.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A regression model predicts prices, demands, durations, concentrations or any other quantity on a continuous scale.
A metric reduces its test errors to one number for model selection and reporting. Each metric rewards a different
summary of the predictive distribution. Mean squared error is minimised by the conditional mean, and mean absolute error
by the conditional median <Cite id="hastie2009" />. Two models can therefore rank in opposite orders under the two
metrics when the errors are skewed or contain outliers.

## Main ideas

**The metric fixes the target statistic.** <NoteLink to="squared-and-absolute-error-metrics" /> shows that squared
error estimates means and absolute error estimates medians. Root mean squared error (RMSE) returns squared error to
the units of $y$, and it is always at least the mean absolute error. The gap between the two grows with the spread of
the error sizes.

**Scale-free comparison with a baseline.** The coefficient of determination $R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}$
divides the model's sum of squared errors by that of the constant prediction $\bar y$. A value of 1 is perfect, 0
matches the mean and a negative value is worse than the mean. <NoteLink to="r-squared-and-explained-variance" /> also
covers explained variance, which ignores a constant bias, and adjusted $R^2$.

**Errors matched to the target's noise.** Heavy tails, multiplicative errors and variances that grow with the mean
call for other scores. <NoteLink to="robust-and-distributional-regression-losses" /> covers log-cosh, which behaves
like squared error for small misses and absolute error for large ones, squared log error for relative misses, and the
Tweedie deviance for targets whose variance grows as $\mu^p$, such as insurance claims and counts.

**Metric and loss are often the same function.** Each score here can also be a training objective. Training with the
metric used for evaluation aligns the two, and the <NoteLink to="regression-losses" /> topic treats the same functions
as losses.
