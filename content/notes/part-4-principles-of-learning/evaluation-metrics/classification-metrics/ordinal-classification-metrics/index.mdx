<Definition>
Let $n$ items have true classes $y_i$ and predicted classes $\hat y_i$ in $\set{1, \dots, K}$, and let $C_{jk}$ count
the items with true class $j$ predicted as $k$. The mean absolute error is
$\mathrm{MAE} = \frac1n\sum_i \abs{\hat y_i - y_i}$. The macro-averaged MAE averages the per-class MAE over the true
classes. Quadratic weighted kappa is $\kappa_w = 1 - \sum_{jk} (j - k)^2 C_{jk} \big/ \sum_{jk} (j - k)^2 E_{jk}$, where
$E_{jk}$ is the count expected if predictions were independent of the truth with the same marginals. Kendall's
$\tau_b$ and Spearman's $\rho$ measure how well the predicted order matches the true order <Cite id="gutierrez2016" />.
</Definition>

## Accuracy and the size of errors

For an ordinal label, a prediction of "moderate" for a "mild" case is a smaller error than "severe". Accuracy counts
both as one mistake. Two classifiers with equal accuracy can therefore differ greatly in how far their errors land. The
metrics below read the <NoteLink to="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</NoteLink> $\Cmat$ with the order in mind. They
weight a cell by its distance from the diagonal, or they compare rankings.

## Mean absolute error on the class index

$\mathrm{MAE}$ treats the classes as the integers $1, \dots, K$ and averages the distance between prediction and truth.
In terms of the confusion matrix,

$$
\mathrm{MAE} = \frac1n \sum_{j,k} \abs{j - k}\, C_{jk}.
$$

It lies in $[0, K - 1]$, and it is 0 only for a perfect predictor. Its unit is "classes". The integer coding assumes
that every step between neighbouring classes costs the same, which is the one interval assumption an ordinal metric
usually accepts. See <NoteLink to="squared-and-absolute-error-metrics" />.

**Macro-averaged MAE.** Under <NoteLink to="class-imbalance">class imbalance</NoteLink>, MAE is dominated by the
frequent classes. A predictor that always outputs a middle class can score a low MAE on data concentrated in the middle.
Baccianella et al. average the MAE of each true class instead <Cite id="baccianella2009" />:

$$
\mathrm{MAE}^M = \frac{1}{\abs{\Kcal}} \sum_{j \in \Kcal} \frac{\sum_k \abs{j - k}\, C_{jk}}{\sum_k C_{jk}},
$$

where $\Kcal$ is the set of classes present in the test data. The constant middle-class predictor now pays the full
distance for every outer class, however rare. This is the ordinal counterpart of
<NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">balanced accuracy</NoteLink>; see
<NoteLink to="averaging-multiclass-metrics" />.

## Quadratic weighted kappa

Weighted kappa corrects a weighted disagreement for the disagreement expected by chance <Cite id="cohen1968" />. With
row totals $r_j = \sum_k C_{jk}$, column totals $c_k = \sum_j C_{jk}$ and the chance-expected counts
$E_{jk} = r_j c_k / n$,

$$
\kappa_w = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j,k} w_{jk} C_{jk}}{\sum_{j,k} w_{jk} E_{jk}},
$$

with weights $w_{jk} = (j - k)^2$ for the quadratic version and $\abs{j - k}$ for the linear one. The numerator divided
by $n$ is the mean squared error of the predictions on the class index. The denominator divided by $n$ is the mean
squared error of a predictor that draws its predictions independently of the truth from the same marginal
distribution. So quadratic weighted kappa is one minus the ratio of the model's squared error to a shuffled model's
squared error. It is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 at chance level and negative below it. A constant predictor has
$E_{jk} = C_{jk}$ for every cell, since its predictions are trivially independent of the truth, so its kappa is 0
whatever its accuracy.

The squared weights penalise large errors more than MAE does. Quadratic weighted kappa is scale-free, which makes it
comparable across tasks with different $K$, and it is common in competitions on essay scoring and disease grading. See
<NoteLink to="cohens-kappa" /> for the unweighted statistic.

## Rank correlations

A rank correlation asks whether items with higher true classes get higher predictions, and ignores the class values
themselves. Kendall's $\tau_b$ compares every pair of items. A pair is concordant when its true and predicted orders
agree and discordant when they disagree. With $P$ concordant pairs, $Q$ discordant pairs, $n_0 = n(n - 1)/2$ pairs in
all, and $T_y$, $T_{\hat y}$ the pairs tied on the truth or on the prediction,

$$
\tau_b = \frac{P - Q}{\sqrt{(n_0 - T_y)(n_0 - T_{\hat y})}}.
$$

Ordinal data have many ties, since $K$ is small and $n$ is large, and the tie correction in the denominator is what
makes $\tau_b$ reach 1 for a perfect predictor <Cite id="kendall1945" />. Spearman's $\rho$ is the
<NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">Pearson correlation</NoteLink> of mid-ranks. Both are undefined for a constant
predictor, whose predictions have no order. A predictor that is off by exactly one class in the same direction
everywhere keeps a perfect rank correlation while its MAE is 1, so rank correlations suit tasks where only the order of
the items matters. See <NoteLink to="rank-correlation" />.

## Other measures

- **Accuracy within one class** counts a prediction as correct when $\abs{\hat y - y} \le 1$. It is common in age
  estimation and credit rating, and it ignores everything about errors larger than one.
- **Mean squared error** on the class index penalises large errors quadratically, as quadratic weighted kappa does
  without the chance correction.
- **Ordinal classification index.** Cardoso and Sousa define a measure from the cheapest path through the confusion
  matrix that is consistent with the order, which combines the size of errors with their dispersion
  <Cite id="cardoso2011" />.

### Worked example

Ten items in three classes, with two predictors of equal accuracy $0.7$. The rows are the true classes, the columns the
predictions:

$$
\Cmat_A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\Cmat_B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Predictor B differs only in sending one class-1 item to class 3 instead of class 2.

- **MAE.** A makes three errors of size 1, so $\mathrm{MAE}_A = 0.3$. B makes two of size 1 and one of size 2, so
  $\mathrm{MAE}_B = 0.4$.
- **Macro-MAE.** A's per-class errors are $(1/4, 2/4, 0)$ with mean $0.25$; B's are $(2/4, 2/4, 0)$ with mean $0.33$.
- **Quadratic weighted kappa.** The row totals are $(4, 4, 2)$ for both. A's column totals are $(4, 3, 3)$, so
  $\sum_{jk} (j - k)^2 E_{jk} = 12.6$ and $\kappa_w = 1 - 3/12.6 = 0.76$. B's column totals are $(4, 2, 4)$, which gives
  $\sum (j-k)^2 E_{jk} = 14.0$, a weighted disagreement of $1 + 4 + 1 = 6$, and $\kappa_w = 1 - 6/14 = 0.57$.
- **Kendall's $\tau_b$** falls from $0.71$ to $0.56$.

Accuracy does not tell the two apart. Every ordinal metric prefers A.

## Pitfalls

- **Integer coding is an assumption.** MAE and weighted kappa treat the steps between classes as equal. If the classes
  are unevenly spaced in meaning, a cost matrix that states the real cost of each error replaces the equal steps of the
  integer coding.
- **Kappa depends on the marginals.** The same confusion pattern gives different kappas under different class
  distributions, because the chance term changes.
- **Report more than one metric.** MAE says how far errors land, macro-MAE whether rare classes are served, kappa how
  much better than chance, and accuracy how often the answer is exact.
