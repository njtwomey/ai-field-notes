<Definition>
With spaces removed from both strings, let $\mathrm{chrP}_n$ be the fraction of the candidate's character n-grams that
also occur in the reference and $\mathrm{chrR}_n$ the fraction of the reference's character n-grams that occur in the
candidate, counts clipped as in <NoteLink to="bleu">BLEU</NoteLink>. With $\mathrm{chrP}$ and $\mathrm{chrR}$ their
arithmetic means over $n = 1, \dots, N$,

$$
\mathrm{chrF}_\beta = (1 + \beta^2)\,\frac{\mathrm{chrP} \cdot \mathrm{chrR}}{\beta^2\,\mathrm{chrP} + \mathrm{chrR}},
$$

with $N = 6$ <Cite id="popovic2015" />. It lies in $[0, 1]$, often reported times 100, and higher is better.
</Definition>

## Why characters

Word n-gram metrics treat "sat" and "sits", or "Haus" and "Hauses", as unrelated. Most of their characters agree, and
character n-grams see that. The effect is largest for languages with rich inflection or compounding, where one word
carries what English spreads over several. Popović compared word 4-grams with character n-grams of lengths up to 6 and
found character 6-grams correlated best with human rankings; treating the space as a character did not help, so spaces
are removed <Cite id="popovic2015" />.

Averaging over $n = 1$ to 6 mixes short n-grams, which reward the right letters and word fragments, with longer ones,
which reward whole words and word boundaries.

## The weight on recall

$\beta$ sets how much more recall counts than precision: $\beta = 1$ is the harmonic mean, and larger $\beta$ favours
covering the reference. The 2015 paper compared $\beta = 1$ and $\beta = 3$ and found $\beta = 3$ better at the segment
level <Cite id="popovic2015" />. Later experiments against direct human assessments settled on $\beta = 2$, and added
word unigrams and bigrams to the character n-grams to form chrF++ <Cite id="popovic2017" />. The SacreBLEU
implementation uses $\beta = 2$ by default, so "chrF" in recent papers usually means chrF2.

## Worked example

Reference "the cat is on the mat", candidate "the cat sat on the mat". Without spaces they are "thecatisonthemat" (16
characters) and "thecatsatonthemat" (17 characters). Averaging the six orders gives $\mathrm{chrP} = 0.611$ and
$\mathrm{chrR} = 0.655$, so

$$
\mathrm{chrF}_2 = 5 \cdot \frac{0.611 \times 0.655}{4 \times 0.611 + 0.655} = 0.646.
$$

The same pair has sentence-level BLEU 0 because no 4-gram matches, while chrF gives substantial credit for the
five shared words and the shared "at" in "cat" and "sat". With $\beta = 1$ and $\beta = 3$ the score is $0.632$ and
$0.650$: recall is slightly higher than precision here, so weighting it more raises the score.

## Pitfalls

- **Character overlap is not meaning.** Two words that share letters are not necessarily related, and a paraphrase
  with different words scores low.
- **Word order matters only through n-grams up to length 6.** A long-range reordering that keeps local fragments
  intact is barely penalised.
- **State β and the n-gram settings.** chrF1, chrF2, chrF3 and chrF++ are different numbers; report which.
