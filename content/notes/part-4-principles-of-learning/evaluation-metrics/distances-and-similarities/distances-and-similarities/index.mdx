<Definition>
A **distance** $d(\xvec, \yvec)$ is a non-negative number that is zero when two objects coincide and grows as they move
apart. A **similarity** is large for close objects and small for distant ones. This topic covers distances and
similarities between feature vectors, and between whole sets of matched points.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Distances appear throughout machine learning as a component of other methods.
<NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours" /> and clustering need a distance between examples. Retrieval systems rank
documents by the similarity of their embeddings to a query. Anomaly detectors flag points far from the bulk of the
data. Representation learning compares embeddings from two models, or from two versions of one model. In each case the
choice of distance decides which examples count as close.

## Main ideas

**Length or direction.** <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity" /> covers the Minkowski distances
$\norm{\xvec - \yvec}_p$, with Manhattan ($p = 1$), Euclidean ($p = 2$) and Chebyshev ($p = \infty$) as special
cases. Cosine similarity measures only the angle between two vectors and ignores their lengths. On unit vectors the two
agree: $\norm{\uvec - \vvec}^2 = 2(1 - \cos\theta)$, so normalising embeddings turns one into the other. The same note
covers computing all pairs efficiently <Cite id="pedregosa2011" />.

**Scaling by the data.** Euclidean distance treats every direction alike, so a correlated or badly scaled feature
dominates it. The <NoteLink to="mahalanobis-distance" /> uses the inverse covariance $\Sigmamat^{-1}$ of the data, which
makes it the Euclidean distance after whitening. It is also the exponent of the Gaussian density, which ties it to
outlier detection and to <NoteLink to="metric-learning" />.

**Comparing point sets up to a transform.** Two embeddings of the same items can differ by a rotation and still carry
the same information. <NoteLink to="procrustes-analysis" /> finds the rotation, and optionally translation and scale,
that best superimposes one matched set on the other, from a singular value decomposition. The remaining squared error,
the disparity, measures how the two shapes differ.
