<Definition>
For a random vector $\xvec \in \reals^d$ with mean $\muvec \in \reals^d$ and symmetric positive-definite covariance matrix
$\Sigmamat \in \reals^{d \times d}$, the **Mahalanobis distance** between a point $\xvec$ and the mean $\muvec$ is <Cite id="mahalanobis1936" />:

$$
D_M(\xvec, \muvec) = \sqrt{(\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec)}.
$$

Between two points $\xvec, \yvec \in \reals^d$, the pairwise distance is:

$$
D_M(\xvec, \yvec) = \sqrt{(\xvec - \yvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \yvec)}.
$$

When $\Sigmamat = \sigma^2 \Imat$ (spherical covariance), $D_M$ reduces to standard Euclidean distance scaled by $1/\sigma$:
$D_M(\xvec, \yvec) = \frac{1}{\sigma}\norm{\xvec - \yvec}_2$.
</Definition>

## Why Euclidean distance fails in correlated spaces

Standard <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">Euclidean distance</NoteLink> $\norm{\xvec - \yvec}_2$ treats
every coordinate axis independently and symmetrically. In real datasets, this assumption leads to two major failure modes:

1. **Scale heterogeneity:** If feature 1 is measured in millimeters ($\sigma_1 \approx 1000$) and feature 2 in meters
   ($\sigma_2 \approx 1$), Euclidean distance is dominated entirely by feature 1, ignoring meaningful variation in feature 2.
2. **Correlation and directionality:** When features covary, data points disperse along oblique axes. Points that deviate from
   the center along the direction of natural correlation are expected fluctuations, whereas deviations orthogonal to that direction
   represent severe anomalies.

Euclidean distance measures absolute spatial separation; Mahalanobis distance measures separation **in units of standard deviation**
along the principal axes of variation.

## Equivalence to statistical whitening

The metric tensor defining Mahalanobis distance is the inverse covariance $\Sigmamat^{-1}$. Because $\Sigmamat$ is symmetric
and positive definite, it has an eigendecomposition:

$$
\Sigmamat = \Vmat \Lambdamat \Vmat^\top,
$$

where $\Vmat = [\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_d]$ is an orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors and $\Lambdamat = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d)$
contains strictly positive eigenvalues ($\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2$). The inverse square root matrix, or **whitening matrix**, is:

$$
\Sigmamat^{-1/2} = \Vmat \Lambdamat^{-1/2} \Vmat^\top.
$$

Applying the whitening transformation to center and uncorrelate the data:

$$
\zvec = \Sigmamat^{-1/2} (\xvec - \muvec),
$$

the covariance of $\zvec$ becomes the identity matrix:

$$
\cov(\zvec) = \Sigmamat^{-1/2} \Sigmamat \Sigmamat^{-1/2} = \Imat_d.
$$

Substituting $\zvec$ into the Euclidean norm reveals the fundamental identity:

$$
\norm{\zvec}_2^2 = \zvec^\top \zvec = (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1/2} \Sigmamat^{-1/2} (\xvec - \muvec) = (\xvec - \muvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\xvec - \muvec) = D_M(\xvec, \muvec)^2.
$$

Mahalanobis distance in the original coordinate space is **identically equal to standard Euclidean distance in the whitened space**.
Elliptical isoprobability contours in $\xvec$ map to concentric spherical contours in $\zvec$.

## Relationship to Gaussian likelihood and outlier detection

In a multivariate Gaussian distribution $\xvec \sim \Gauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$, the probability density is:

$$
p(\xvec) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \det(\Sigmamat)^{1/2}} \exp\paren{-\frac{1}{2} D_M^2(\xvec, \muvec)}.
$$

The level sets of constant probability density $p(\xvec) = c$ are precisely the ellipsoids of constant Mahalanobis distance $D_M(\xvec, \muvec) = r$.

### The $\chi^2$ distribution of squared distances

Under the Gaussian assumption, the whitened variable $\zvec = \Sigmamat^{-1/2}(\xvec - \muvec) \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat_d)$ is a vector of
$d$ independent standard normal random variables. Consequently, the squared Mahalanobis distance is the sum of $d$ squared standard normals:

$$
D_M^2(\xvec, \muvec) = \sum_{k=1}^d z_k^2 \sim \chi^2(d).
$$

This distribution provides exact, principled statistical thresholds for anomaly detection:
- In $d = 2$, $D_M^2 \le 5.99$ encompasses $95\%$ of probability mass ($\chi^2_{0.95}(2) \approx 5.99$, or $D_M \approx 2.45$).
- Any observation with $D_M^2 > \chi^2_{1 - \alpha}(d)$ can be rejected as an outlier at significance level $\alpha$.

## Metric learning and generalized Mahalanobis distances

In supervised classification, Euclidean distance between raw feature vectors often groups different classes together.
**Distance metric learning** replaces the empirical inverse covariance $\Sigmamat^{-1}$ with a parameterised positive semi-definite matrix $\Mmat \succeq 0$:

$$
d_\Mmat(\xvec, \yvec) = \sqrt{(\xvec - \yvec)^\top \Mmat (\xvec - \yvec)}.
$$

Factoring $\Mmat = \Lmat^\top \Lmat$ for some transformation matrix $\Lmat \in \reals^{k \times d}$:

$$
d_\Mmat(\xvec, \yvec) = \sqrt{(\xvec - \yvec)^\top \Lmat^\top \Lmat (\xvec - \yvec)} = \norm{\Lmat \xvec - \Lmat \yvec}_2.
$$

Methods like **Large Margin Nearest Neighbor (LMNN)** <Cite id="weinberger2009" /> optimize $\Mmat$ via semidefinite programming so that
$k$-nearest neighbours always belong to the same class while points from differing classes are separated by a large margin.
Modern deep metric learning (e.g. triplet loss and contrastive representation learning) can be viewed as learning a nonlinear neural feature map
$\phi_\theta(\xvec)$ such that the Euclidean distance $\norm{\phi_\theta(\xvec) - \phi_\theta(\yvec)}_2$ acts as an optimal generalized metric.
