<Definition>
For a reference image $x$ and a distorted image $y$ with $N$ pixels, and $\text{MAX}$ the largest possible pixel value
(255 for 8-bit images, 1 for images scaled to $[0, 1]$),

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(x_n - y_n)^2, \qquad
\text{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\text{MAX}^2}{\text{MSE}} = 20\log_{10}\text{MAX} - 10\log_{10}\text{MSE} \ \text{dB}.
$$
</Definition>

## Reading the number

PSNR is the <Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> on a logarithmic scale, normalised by the dynamic range. Higher
is better, and identical images give $+\infty$. Because of the logarithm, halving the
<Gloss name="rmse">root-mean-squared error</Gloss> adds $20\log_{10}2 = 6.02$ dB, and PSNR differences are ratios of
MSE: a 3 dB gain is roughly a halving of the MSE. Typical lossy 8-bit image and video coding gives 30 to 50 dB.

**Worked example.** An 8-bit image with MSE 100 has $\text{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}(255^2/100) = 28.13$ dB; reducing the MSE
to 25 raises it to $34.15$ dB, a gain of $6.02$ dB for halving the RMS error. Adding independent Gaussian noise of
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma$ gives $\text{MSE} \approx \sigma^2$, so
$\sigma = 15$ grey levels corresponds to about $20\log_{10}(255/15) = 24.61$ dB.

## What PSNR misses

PSNR weighs every pixel error the same, whatever its location or structure. Errors that preserve structure, such as a
uniform brightness shift or a mild contrast change, cost as much as noise with the same energy, although people barely
notice them. The figure in <NoteLink to="structural-similarity-index">SSIM</NoteLink> tunes five distortions to the same
MSE, hence the same PSNR, and shows how differently they look. PSNR and SSIM are related analytically for some
distortions, but they can rank images differently <Cite id="hore2010" />.

PSNR remains useful for comparing versions of one method on one image, where the distortion type is fixed, and as a
fidelity target for codecs and denoisers.

## Pitfalls

- **State MAX.** Floating-point images in $[0, 1]$ need $\text{MAX} = 1$. Using 255 on such an image adds $48.1$ dB.
- **Colour.** Implementations compute PSNR on the luminance channel only, or average it over RGB channels, or pool the
  MSE over channels first. The results differ.
- **Averaging over images.** The mean PSNR over a set of images is not the PSNR of the mean MSE; low-error images
  dominate the first.
