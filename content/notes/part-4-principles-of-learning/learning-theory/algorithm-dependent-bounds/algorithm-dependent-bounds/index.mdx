<Definition>
Algorithm-dependent bounds control the generalisation gap $R(A(S)) - \hat R_S(A(S))$ of a learning algorithm $A$
through properties of $A$ itself: how much its output changes when one training example is replaced, how far its output
distribution is from a prior fixed in advance, how few examples determine its output, or how much information its
output carries about the sample. The size of the hypothesis class does not appear. These bounds can be small for
learners whose classes are rich enough to fit any labelling, where every uniform bound is vacuous.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink> bound holds for every hypothesis in the class at
once, so it cannot be smaller than the gap of the worst hypothesis the class contains. For large models that worst case
is extreme. A network that can fit random labels belongs to a class in which some hypotheses fit the training set
perfectly and predict no better than chance elsewhere, and yet the network that <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> returns on real labels has a small gap <Cite id="zhang2017" />. Explaining that gap requires a bound
that looks at the returned predictor and the procedure that produced it.

The same need arises in practice. Regularised <NoteLink to="linear-models">linear models</NoteLink> and kernel machines
generalise in feature spaces of any dimension. <Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> generalises better
when stopped early. The estimate from a holdout set consulted repeatedly during model development stops being unbiased.
Each of these is a statement about an algorithm, and each has a bound in this sub-chapter.

## Main ideas

**Insensitivity to one example.** An algorithm is uniformly $\beta$-stable when replacing one training example changes
its loss at every point by at most $\beta$. Its expected gap is then at most $\beta$, by a renaming argument that swaps
a training example with a fresh one, and McDiarmid's inequality gives a high-probability bound
(<NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic stability</NoteLink>). Strong convexity makes regularised empirical
risk minimisation stable with $\beta = 2L^2/(\lambda m)$
(<NoteLink to="stability-of-regularised-empirical-risk-minimisation">stability of regularised ERM</NoteLink>). A limited
budget of steps makes stochastic gradient descent stable with $\beta \le 2L^2\sum_t\eta_t/m$
(<NoteLink to="stability-of-stochastic-gradient-descent">stability of stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink>).

**A change of measure from a prior.** A PAC-Bayes bound charges a randomised predictor $Q$ the divergence
$\KL(Q \,\|\, P)$ from a prior $P$ fixed before the data, and it holds for every $Q$ at once
(<NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>). It generalises the
<NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink> from single hypotheses to distributions. Optimising the posterior
around trained weights gives non-vacuous certificates for neural networks, such as 0.161 for a network with 472,000
parameters on binary <Gloss name="mnist" /> (<NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">non-vacuous generalisation
bounds</NoteLink>) <Cite id="alquier2024" />.

**A few examples determine the output.** If a learner's output can be rebuilt from $k$ of its $m$ training examples,
then the output generalises like a hypothesis chosen from about $\binom mk$ candidates, whatever the class. The support
vectors of a <Gloss name="svm">support vector machine</Gloss> are the standard example
(<NoteLink to="sample-compression-bounds">sample compression bounds</NoteLink>).

**Information about the sample.** The expected gap is at most $\sqrt{2\sigma^2 I(S; W)/m}$, where $I(S; W)$ is the
mutual information between the sample and the output
(<NoteLink to="information-theoretic-generalisation-bounds">information-theoretic generalisation bounds</NoteLink>).
This language connects the other ideas. A finite class has $I(S; W) \le \ln k$, a compression scheme of size $k$ has
conditional mutual information at most $k\ln(2m)$, a differentially private algorithm has small mutual information, and
the mutual information is the average PAC-Bayes divergence with the best prior <Cite id="hellstrom2023" />.

**Reuse of a holdout.** When each query to a holdout set depends on earlier answers, the
<NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bound</NoteLink> no longer applies, and $k$ adaptive queries can bias the
estimates by order $\sqrt{k/m}$ instead of $\sqrt{\ln k/m}$. Answers that are differentially private, a distributional
form of stability, keep the estimates valid with a sample that grows like $\sqrt k$
(<NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">adaptive data analysis</NoteLink>).

## How the notes fit together

- **Start with** <NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic stability</NoteLink>. It defines the notions of
  stability and derives the bound that the next two notes feed with constants.
- **The two stability notes** compute $\beta$ for an exact minimiser
  (<NoteLink to="stability-of-regularised-empirical-risk-minimisation">regularised ERM</NoteLink>) and for an optimiser
  stopped early (<NoteLink to="stability-of-stochastic-gradient-descent">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink>).
- **PAC-Bayes** follows from the Occam bound and the Kullback–Leibler divergence. The
  <NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">non-vacuous bounds</NoteLink> example applies it end to end.
- **Sample compression** needs only the <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> and the <Gloss name="pac" />
  setting, and can be read on its own.
- **The information-theoretic note** comes after stability and PAC-Bayes, because it unifies them.
- **Adaptive data analysis** comes last. It uses stability in the form of differential privacy and applies it to the
  evaluation of models rather than to their training.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The sub-chapter assumes the <NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation gap</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> and the uniform bounds of
<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence-and-capacity">uniform convergence and capacity</NoteLink>, against which these bounds
are compared. The tools come from Part I: <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">McDiarmid's inequality</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian random variables</NoteLink>, and from
<NoteLink to="information-theory">information theory</NoteLink>: the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink>. Adaptive data analysis also uses
<NoteLink to="differential-privacy">differential privacy</NoteLink>.

<NoteLink to="margins-and-norms">Margins and norms</NoteLink> give the scale-sensitive class-based bounds that
algorithm-dependent bounds compete with for neural networks. <NoteLink to="flat-minima-and-sharpness">Flat
minima</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink> describe what the
training algorithm prefers, which is what these bounds measure. <NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">Limits of
uniform convergence</NoteLink> explains why class-based bounds cannot account for the small gap of some interpolating
learners. In practice, <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink>,
<NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink> are
the engineering counterparts of the results here.
