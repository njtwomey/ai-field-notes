<Definition>
A **sample compression scheme** of size $k$ for a learning algorithm $A$ is a pair of maps. The **compression map**
$\kappa$ selects from any training sample $S = ((x_i, y_i))_{i=1}^m$ a subsequence $S_I = (z_i)_{i \in I}$ of at most
$k$ examples, the **compression set**. The **reconstruction map** $\rho$ turns any such subsequence into a hypothesis,
and the scheme reproduces the algorithm: $A(S) = \rho(S_{\kappa(S)})$. In the realisable setting the reconstruction must
also label every example of $S$ correctly <Cite id="littlestone1986" />. The **sample-compression bound** states that,
with probability at least $1 - \delta$, an output rebuilt from $k$ examples and correct on the remaining $m - k$ has
risk at most $(\ln\binom{m}{k} + \ln(1/\delta))/(m - k)$ <Cite id="littlestone1986" /> <Cite id="floyd1995" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Many learners return a hypothesis that is fixed by a few of their training examples. The tightest axis-aligned
rectangle around the positive points is fixed by the leftmost, rightmost, highest and lowest of them. A
<NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> is fixed by its support vectors: delete any
other point and the solution does not change. The <NoteLink to="perceptron">perceptron</NoteLink> is fixed by the
examples on which it made a mistake.

If a hypothesis is built from $k$ examples, the other $m - k$ examples played no part in choosing it. They are a fresh
test set for it. A <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bound</NoteLink> on those $m - k$ points then controls its
risk. The learner chose which $k$ examples to keep, so the test-set bound must hold for every possible choice at once.
There are $\binom{m}{k}$ choices, and a <NoteLink to="union-bound" /> over them costs $\ln\binom{m}{k} \approx
k\ln(em/k)$ in the exponent.

The capacity in this bound is the size of the compression set, not a property of the hypothesis class. A kernel SVM
chooses from a class of infinite VC dimension, and its compression bound depends only on how many support vectors it
used. The bound depends on the algorithm and on the sample it ran on.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, compression makes the
move that exploits structure through the algorithm: it concentrates on the examples left out, and it makes the bound
uniform over subsets of the sample rather than over a class.

## Compression schemes

A scheme is defined by its two maps, and the maps need not be computationally simple.

- **Rectangles.** For axis-aligned rectangles in $\reals^D$, keep the positive points that are extreme in some
  coordinate: at most $2D$ of them. The reconstruction returns the smallest rectangle containing them, which contains
  every positive point and, in the realisable case, no negative point. The size is $k = 2D$, equal to the VC dimension.
- **Support vector machines.** Keep the support vectors, the points with dual variable $\alpha_i > 0$. The
  reconstruction retrains the SVM on them with the same $C$ and kernel. The dual solution restricted to the support
  vectors still satisfies the optimality conditions of the smaller problem, so the retrained SVM is the original one.
- **Online learners with a mistake bound.** Run an online algorithm such as the perceptron through the sample until it
  makes no more mistakes, and keep the examples on which it updated. Replaying those examples rebuilds the final
  hypothesis. A mistake bound of $M$ gives a compression scheme of size $M$ <Cite id="floyd1995" />.

A compression scheme may also carry a few bits of side information, such as which of the kept points are positive. The
bound then gains the logarithm of the number of possible messages.

## The bound

**Theorem (realisable).** Let $A$ have a compression scheme of fixed size $k$, and let the data be drawn
<Gloss name="iid" /> from $\Dcal$. With probability at least $1 - \delta$, if $A(S)$ is correct on every example of $S$
outside its compression set, then

$$
R(A(S)) \le \frac{\ln\binom{m}{k} + \ln(1/\delta)}{m - k} \le \frac{k\ln(em/k) + \ln(1/\delta)}{m - k}.
$$

The derivation has three steps <Cite id="littlestone1986" />.

1. **Fix the indices.** Fix a set $I \subseteq \set{1, \dots, m}$ of $k$ indices, chosen before the data are seen. The
   hypothesis $h_I = \rho(S_I)$ depends only on the $k$ examples indexed by $I$. The other $m - k$ examples are
   independent of $S_I$, so they are independent of $h_I$.
2. **Concentrate on the rest.** Suppose $R(h_I) > \epsilon$. Each of the $m - k$ other examples is classified
   correctly with probability less than $1 - \epsilon$, independently. The probability that $h_I$ is correct on all of
   them is less than $(1 - \epsilon)^{m - k} \le e^{-\epsilon(m - k)}$, using $1 - \epsilon \le e^{-\epsilon}$.
3. **Union over index sets.** The output $A(S)$ equals $h_I$ for the set $I = \kappa(S)$ that the compression map
   chooses. If $A(S)$ has risk above $\epsilon$ and is correct outside its compression set, then some $h_I$ has risk
   above $\epsilon$ and is correct outside $I$. By the <NoteLink to="union-bound" /> over the $\binom{m}{k}$ index
   sets, this has probability less than $\binom{m}{k}e^{-\epsilon(m - k)}$. Setting this equal to $\delta$ and solving
   for $\epsilon$ gives the first bound. The second uses $\binom{m}{k} \le (em/k)^k$.

The proof is the realisable finite-class bound of <NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">realisable and
agnostic learning</NoteLink>, applied to the $\binom{m}{k}$ hypotheses that the sample itself can produce, with
$m - k$ examples left to test them. Littlestone and Warmuth's original statement bounds the failure probability by
$\binom{m}{k}(1 - \epsilon)^{m - k}$ <Cite id="littlestone1986" />.

**A size chosen by the data.** The number of support vectors is not known in advance. Splitting the confidence
$\delta$ evenly over the sizes $k = 1, \dots, m$ replaces $\ln(1/\delta)$ by $\ln(m/\delta)$, and the bound then holds
for the size the algorithm actually used.

**Errors outside the compression set.** If $A(S)$ makes $r$ errors on the $m - k$ examples outside its compression set,
step 2 becomes a test-set bound with $r$ errors. With <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's
inequality</NoteLink> and the union bound over index sets and sizes,

$$
R(A(S)) \le \frac{r}{m - k} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln\binom{m}{k} + \ln(m/\delta)}{2(m - k)}}.
$$

Binomial-tail and relative-entropy versions are tighter when $r/(m - k)$ is small <Cite id="graepel2005" />.

**Compression and the VC dimension.** A class with a compression scheme of size $k$ has finite VC dimension, so it is
<Gloss name="pac" /> learnable. Littlestone and Warmuth asked whether the converse holds. Moran and Yehudayoff proved
that every class of VC dimension $d$ has a compression scheme of size $\exp(O(d))$ <Cite id="moran2016" />. Whether size
$O(d)$ always suffices is open.

## Support vectors as a compression set

The soft-margin SVM with penalty $C$ solves a convex problem whose solution
is $f(x) = \sum_i\alpha_i y_i k(x_i, x) + b$. A point with $\alpha_i = 0$ satisfies $y_i f(x_i) \ge 1$: it lies
outside the margin, on the correct side. So the SVM is correct on every point outside its support set, even when the
data are not separable and some support vectors are training errors. The realisable bound applies with $k$ equal to
the number of support vectors and no errors outside the set.

Vapnik gave a related bound in expectation. The leave-one-out error of an SVM is at most the fraction of support
vectors, because leaving out a non-support vector does not change the solution. The
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">expected risk</NoteLink> of an SVM trained on $m - 1$ points is therefore at
most $\expect[k]/m$ <Cite id="vapnik1998" />.

## Worked example

**Rectangles in the plane.** Take axis-aligned rectangles in $\reals^2$, so $k = 4$ and the VC dimension is $d = 4$.
Take $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$.

1. **The compression bound.** $\ln\binom{1000}{4} = \ln(4.14 \times 10^{10}) = 24.45$ and $\ln 20 = 3.00$. The bound
   is $(24.45 + 3.00)/996 = 0.0276$.
2. **The VC bound.** The realisable VC bound of Blumer et al., $\epsilon = \frac{2}{m}\paren{d\log_2(2em/d) +
   \log_2(2/\delta)}$ <Cite id="blumer1989" />, gives $\frac{2}{1000}(4 \times 10.41 + 5.32) = 0.0939$.
3. **Sample sizes.** For $\epsilon = 0.05$ the compression bound needs $m = 497$ and the VC bound $m = 2{,}044$. For
   $\epsilon = 0.01$ they need 3,216 and 12,287.

Both bounds have the same $d\ln m/m$ shape. The compression bound's constants are about four times smaller.

**An SVM with support vectors.** Take $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$, with the size chosen by the data, so the bound
is $(\ln\binom{1000}{k} + \ln(1000/0.05))/(1{,}000 - k)$.

| Support vectors $k$ | Bound on $R$ |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | 0.065 |
| 50 | 0.216 |
| 100 | 0.369 |
| 200 | 0.634 |

The <NoteLink to="kernel-support-vector-machine">Gaussian-kernel SVM</NoteLink> chooses from a class of infinite VC
dimension, so no VC bound applies at all. The compression bound is informative when the support vectors are a small
fraction of the sample. It becomes vacuous when they are a large fraction: at $k = 200$ the bound is 0.63 whatever the
test error is.

**Errors outside the set.** If an SVM-like learner with $k = 50$ makes $r = 10$ errors outside its compression set, the
Hoeffding form gives $10/950 + \sqrt{(\ln\binom{1000}{50} + \ln 20{,}000)/1{,}900} = 0.011 + 0.329 = 0.34$. The
square root makes the agnostic form much looser than the realisable one.

## Assumptions

- **Independent, identically distributed data.** Step 2 needs the examples outside the compression set to be
  independent of those inside it.
- **The scheme must reproduce the algorithm exactly.** The bound is about $\rho(S_I)$. A numerical solver that stops at
  a tolerance can return a slightly different solution when retrained on the support vectors, and then the bound is
  about the retrained solution.
- **Reconstruction from the compression set alone.** The map $\rho$ may not look at the other examples, or the test-set
  argument of step 2 fails.

## Pitfalls

- **The compression set counts, not the class.** A learner that keeps half its sample gets a
  <NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">vacuous bound</NoteLink> even from a simple class. Kernel SVMs with a
  short lengthscale or a large $C$ on noisy data can have most points as support vectors.
- **Compression size is random.** The data-dependent size needs the union over $k$; quoting the fixed-$k$ bound at the
  observed $k$ understates the risk slightly.
- **A different question from the VC dimension.** The bound certifies one algorithm on one sample. It does not say how
  hard the class is to learn in the worst case; the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>
  and the <NoteLink to="sample-complexity-lower-bounds">lower bounds</NoteLink> do.
- **Related to Occam and <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>.** A compression set is a
  description of the hypothesis by $k$ indices into the sample. The <NoteLink to="occam-bound" /> charges description
  length, and PAC-Bayes bounds over compression sets combine the two views <Cite id="graepel2005" />.
