<Definition>
Let $S = (Z_1, \dots, Z_m)$ be a sample of $m$ independent draws from $\Dcal$ and let a learning algorithm, possibly
randomised, output a hypothesis $W$ with conditional distribution $P_{W \mid S}$. The
**<NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">expected generalisation gap</NoteLink>** is
$\operatorname{gen} = \expect\LB R(W) - \hat R_S(W)\RB$, the expectation taken over the sample and the algorithm. If for
every fixed hypothesis $w$ the loss $\ell(w, Z)$ with $Z \sim \Dcal$ is $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian, then

$$
\abs{\operatorname{gen}} \le \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\, I(S; W)}{m}},
$$

where $I(S; W)$ is the mutual information between the sample and the output <Cite id="xu2017" />
<Cite id="russo2016bias" />. A loss with values in $[0, 1]$ is $\frac12$-sub-Gaussian, which gives
$\abs{\operatorname{gen}} \le \sqrt{I(S; W)/(2m)}$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A hypothesis overfits when it encodes accidents of its training sample: the particular noise in the labels, the
particular points that happened to be drawn. To encode them, the output must carry information about the sample. An
output that is nearly independent of the sample cannot overfit it, because its training loss is then nearly an average
over fresh data. The <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> $I(S; W)$, measured in nats,
counts how much the output reveals about the sample, and the bound converts nats into a gap.

Three cases fix the scale.

- **A constant algorithm** has $I(S; W) = 0$ and no gap.
- **A finite class.** For an algorithm choosing among $k$ hypotheses, $I(S; W) \le \entropy(W) \le \ln k$, and the bound
  becomes $\sqrt{\ln k/(2m)}$ for a $[0, 1]$ loss. This is the finite-class rate of
  <NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">agnostic learning</NoteLink>, in expectation and without a
  <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink>. An algorithm whose choice is predictable, so that $\entropy(W)$ is
  small and below $\ln k$, has a correspondingly smaller bound.
- **Noise injection.** Adding noise to the output, as stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics adds noise to each step
  (<NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-langevin-dynamics">stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics</NoteLink>), limits how
  much the output can reveal, and so limits the gap.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, the mutual information
plays the role that capacity plays in a uniform bound, and the change of measure of
<NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink> replaces the union bound. The
mutual information is the average PAC-Bayes divergence when the prior is the marginal law of the output:
$I(S; W) = \expect_S\KL(P_{W \mid S} \,\|\, P_W)$. PAC-Bayes bounds hold with high probability for any fixed prior;
the mutual-information bound holds in expectation for the best prior in this average sense.

## The mutual-information bound

The derivation compares the joint law of $(S, W)$ with the law under which the output is independent of the sample. Let
$g(s, w) = R(w) - \hat R_s(w)$ be the gap of hypothesis $w$ on sample $s$, so $\operatorname{gen} = \expect g(S, W)$.

**Step 1: an independent copy has no gap.** Let $(\bar S, \bar W)$ have the same marginal laws as $S$ and $W$ but be
independent of each other. For each fixed $w$, the empirical risk on a sample that did not influence $w$ is unbiased:
$\expect_{\bar S}\hat R_{\bar S}(w) = R(w)$. Averaging over $\bar W$ gives $\expect g(\bar S, \bar W) = 0$.

**Step 2: the decoupled gap is sub-Gaussian.** For fixed $w$, $g(\bar S, w) = \frac1m\sum_i (R(w) - \ell(w, \bar Z_i))$
is an average of $m$ independent centred $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian variables, so it is $\sigma/\sqrt m$-sub-Gaussian
(<NoteLink to="sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian random variables</NoteLink>): for every real $t$,

$$
\ln\expect_{\bar S} e^{t\,g(\bar S, w)} \le \frac{t^2\sigma^2}{2m}.
$$

Averaging the <Gloss name="mgf">moment generating function</Gloss> over the independent $\bar W$ keeps the same bound.

**Step 3: change of measure.** The Donsker–Varadhan inequality states that
$\expect_{\mu} f \le \KL(\mu \,\|\, \nu) + \ln\expect_\nu e^f$ for any two distributions $\mu$, $\nu$ and any
function $f$ <Cite id="donsker1975" />. Take $\mu$ the joint law of $(S, W)$, $\nu$ the product law of
$(\bar S, \bar W)$ and $f = t\,g$. The divergence of a joint law from the product of its marginals is the mutual
information, so

$$
t\,\operatorname{gen} \le I(S; W) + \frac{t^2\sigma^2}{2m} \qquad \text{for every real } t.
$$

**Step 4: optimise over $t$.** For $t > 0$, $\operatorname{gen} \le I(S; W)/t + t\sigma^2/(2m)$, which is smallest at
$t = \sqrt{2mI(S; W)/\sigma^2}$, where it equals $\sqrt{2\sigma^2 I(S; W)/m}$. Negative $t$ gives the same bound on
$-\operatorname{gen}$, which proves the bound on $\abs{\operatorname{gen}}$.

Russo and Zou proved the first version for finite classes, with the information that the output carries about the
vector of empirical risks <Cite id="russo2016bias" />. Xu and Raginsky stated it with $I(S; W)$, which is easier to
bound <Cite id="xu2017" />.

The mutual-information bound controls the expected gap only. A high-probability bound needs a stronger quantity, such
as a bound on the divergence $\KL(P_{W \mid S} \,\|\, P_W)$ for most samples, which is a PAC-Bayes bound
<Cite id="hellstrom2023" />.

**The Gibbs algorithm.** The algorithm that samples $W$ with probability proportional to
$Q(w)\exp(-\lambda\hat R_S(w))$, for a fixed distribution $Q$ and inverse temperature $\lambda$, is the Gibbs posterior
of <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>. For a loss in $[0, 1]$, Xu and Raginsky report that
$I(S; W) \le 2\lambda$, which gives $\abs{\operatorname{gen}} \le \sqrt{\lambda/m}$, and that a direct argument
improves the gap bound to $\lambda/(2m)$ <Cite id="xu2017" />. The inverse temperature is the dial between fitting the
sample and revealing it.

## Conditional mutual information

The mutual information can be infinite for an algorithm whose expected generalisation gap is small. A deterministic
algorithm with a continuous output, such as the sample mean of continuous data, determines $W$ exactly from $S$, so
$I(S; W) = \infty$ and the bound says nothing. For thresholds on the real line, a class of
<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> 1, Bassily, Moran, Nachum, Shafer and Yehudayoff
showed that every proper consistent learner has, for some distributions, mutual information that grows without bound as
the domain grows <Cite id="bassily2018" />. Two refinements repair this.

**Conditional mutual information.** Steinke and Zakynthinou draw a supersample $\tilde Z$ of $m$ pairs of independent
examples and a uniform vector $U \in \set{0, 1}^m$ that picks one example from each pair for training. The conditional
mutual information is $\mathrm{CMI} = I(W; U \mid \tilde Z)$: how much the output reveals about which member of each
pair was used, given both. It is the information-theoretic version of the ghost sample of
<NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink>. For a loss in $[0, 1]$,
$\abs{\operatorname{gen}} \le \sqrt{2\,\mathrm{CMI}/m}$ <Cite id="steinke2020" />. Three upper bounds on $\mathrm{CMI}$
follow.

- **Always finite.** $\mathrm{CMI} \le m\ln 2$, because $U$ has $m$ bits.
- **Compression.** An algorithm whose output is determined by $k$ of its training examples has
  $\mathrm{CMI} \le k\ln(2m)$ (<NoteLink to="sample-compression-bounds">sample compression bounds</NoteLink>).
- **VC classes.** For a class of VC dimension $d$, some empirical risk minimiser has $\mathrm{CMI} \le d\ln m + 2$.

**Individual-sample mutual information.** Bu, Zou and Veeravalli bound the gap by the information the output carries
about each example separately: $\abs{\operatorname{gen}} \le \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sqrt{2\sigma^2 I(W; Z_i)}$, which is
never larger than the bound with $I(S; W)$ <Cite id="bu2020" />. For the sample mean of $m$ Gaussian vectors in $d$
dimensions, $I(S; W)$ is infinite while $I(W; Z_i) = \frac d2\ln\frac{m}{m - 1}$ is finite.

## Worked example

**A finite class.** An algorithm chooses one of $k = 1{,}024$ hypotheses, the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0-1
loss</NoteLink> is used, and $m = 1{,}000$.

1. **The information.** $I(S; W) \le \entropy(W) \le \ln 1{,}024 = 6.93$ nats.
2. **The bound.** $\abs{\operatorname{gen}} \le \sqrt{6.93/2{,}000} = 0.059$.
3. **Against the uniform bound.** The high-probability uniform bound over the same class at $\delta = 0.05$ is
   $\sqrt{\ln(2k/\delta)/(2m)} = \sqrt{10.6/2{,}000} = 0.073$. The information bound is smaller and holds only in
   expectation.
4. **Sample size.** An expected gap of at most 0.02 needs $m \ge 6.93/(2 \times 0.02^2) \approx 8{,}700$.

**A noisy mean.** Examples $z_i \in [0, 1]$; the algorithm outputs $W = \bar z + \xi$, the sample mean plus independent
noise $\xi \sim \Gauss(0, \tau^2)$; the loss $\ell(w, z) = \min(1, (w - z)^2)$ lies in $[0, 1]$, so $\sigma = \frac12$.

1. **The information.** $W$ depends on $S$ only through $\bar z$, so $I(S; W) = I(\bar z; \bar z + \xi)$. The mean of
   $m$ variables in $[0, 1]$ has variance at most $1/(4m)$. A Gaussian channel with input variance $v$ and noise
   variance $\tau^2$ carries at most $\frac12\ln(1 + v/\tau^2)$ nats (<NoteLink to="channel-capacity">channel
   capacity</NoteLink>), so $I(S; W) \le \frac12\ln\paren{1 + 1/(4m\tau^2)}$.
2. **Numbers.** With $m = 100$ and $\tau = 0.05$, $4m\tau^2 = 1$ and $I(S; W) \le \frac12\ln 2 = 0.35$ nats. The bound
   is $\sqrt{0.35/200} = 0.042$.
3. **Against the truth.** For the <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> the expected gap of
   $\bar z + \xi$ is exactly $2\operatorname{var}(z)/m \le 1/(2m) = 0.005$, whatever $\tau$ is, and the clipping at 1
   rarely acts when $\tau$ is small. The bound is non-vacuous and about eight times too large.
4. **Less noise.** With $\tau = 0.005$, $I(S; W) \le 2.31$ nats and the bound grows to 0.107. As $\tau \to 0$ the bound
   grows without limit, while the true gap stays at most 0.005. The information bound penalises how precisely the
   output is reported, not how much the gap is.
5. **More data.** At $\tau = 0.05$ the information shrinks like $1/(8m\tau^2)$, so the bound falls like $1/m$. At
   $m = 1{,}000$ it is 0.0049, and the smallest $m$ with a bound below 0.01 is $m = 477$.

## Pitfalls

- **Expectation, not high probability.** The bound controls $\expect[R(W) - \hat R_S(W)]$. Markov's inequality turns it
  into a probability bound with $1/\delta$ dependence, much weaker than $\ln(1/\delta)$.
- **Deterministic algorithms.** On continuous data, a deterministic output often has infinite mutual information with
  the sample. Use the conditional or individual-sample versions, or add noise.
- **The sub-Gaussian condition is on the loss for each fixed hypothesis.** Unbounded losses such as the squared loss on
  unbounded data are not sub-Gaussian, and the bound does not apply to them as stated.
- **The information is hard to compute.** $I(S; W)$ involves the unknown distribution and the whole algorithm. In
  practice it is bounded through structure: the entropy of a finite output, a channel with known noise, a compression
  scheme, or <NoteLink to="differential-privacy">differential privacy</NoteLink>, which gives
  $I(S; W) \le \frac12\varepsilon^2 m$ for an $\varepsilon$-differentially private algorithm <Cite id="steinke2020" />.
