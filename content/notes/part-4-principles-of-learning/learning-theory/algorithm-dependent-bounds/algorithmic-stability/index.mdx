<Definition>
Let $A$ be a learning algorithm that maps a sample $S = (z_1, \dots, z_m)$ of examples $z_i = (x_i, y_i)$ to a
hypothesis $A(S)$, and let $\ell(h, z) \in [0, M]$ be the loss of hypothesis $h$ on example $z$. Write $S^{(i)}$ for the
sample with $z_i$ replaced by another example $z_i'$. The algorithm is **uniformly $\beta$-stable** if

$$
\abs{\ell(A(S), z) - \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z)} \le \beta
$$

for every sample $S$, every index $i$, every replacement $z_i'$ and every test example $z$. For a uniformly
$\beta$-stable algorithm and a sample of $m$ independent draws from a distribution $\Dcal$, the
<NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">expected generalisation gap</NoteLink> is at most $\beta$, and with probability at
least $1 - \delta$

$$
R(A(S)) \le \hat R_S(A(S)) + \beta + (2m\beta + M)\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2m}}
$$

<Cite id="bousquet2002" /> <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A bound by <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink> asks how far the empirical risk can be
from the true risk for the worst hypothesis in the class. The learner returns only one hypothesis, and that hypothesis
may be far from the worst. Stability asks a different question about the learner itself: how much does its output
depend on any single training example?

If the output hardly depends on any one example, then each training example plays almost the same role as a fresh test
example. A training example $z_i$ influences $A(S)$ by at most $\beta$ in loss. Remove that influence and $z_i$ is just
an independent draw from $\Dcal$, on which the expected loss is the true risk. So the training loss on $z_i$ can
understate the true risk by at most about $\beta$. Averaging over the $m$ examples gives an expected gap of at most
$\beta$.

The extreme cases fix the scale. A constant algorithm, which ignores the data, has $\beta = 0$ and no gap. A lookup
table, which memorises every training label and guesses elsewhere, can change its loss at $z_i$ by the whole range $M$
when $z_i$ is replaced, so $\beta = M$ and the bound says nothing. Useful algorithms sit in between. The sample mean has
$\beta = O(1/m)$, and so do <NoteLink to="stability-of-regularised-empirical-risk-minimisation">regularised empirical
risk minimisation</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="stability-of-stochastic-gradient-descent">stochastic gradient
descent</NoteLink> run for a limited time. The <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours">$k$-nearest-neighbour</NoteLink>
average of labels in $[0, 1]$ moves by at most $1/k$ when one label changes, so its stability improves with $k$ rather
than with $m$.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, stability skips the step
that makes a bound uniform over a class. It exploits structure in the algorithm instead. The concentration step still
applies, through <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">McDiarmid's inequality</NoteLink>, because the gap is a function
of the $m$ independent examples that no single example can move much. No class,
<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy">covering number</NoteLink> appears in the result.

## Notions of stability

The literature uses several notions. They differ in what is perturbed (an example removed or replaced), where the
change is measured (at a fixed point, at the perturbed point, or on average) and whether a supremum or an expectation
is taken over samples. The table orders them from strongest to weakest.

| Notion | Condition | What it gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniform stability | $\sup_{S, i, z_i', z}\abs{\ell(A(S), z) - \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z)} \le \beta$ | Expected gap $\le \beta$; high-probability bound via McDiarmid |
| Hypothesis stability | $\expect_{S, z}\abs{\ell(A(S), z) - \ell(A(S^{\setminus i}), z)} \le \beta$ | High-probability bound with $1/\delta$ in place of $\ln(1/\delta)$ |
| On-average replace-one stability | $\expect_{S, z_i', i}[\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i)] \le \beta$ | Expected gap $\le \beta$, and nothing more |
| Error stability | $\abs{R(A(S)) - R(A(S^{\setminus i}))} \le \beta$ | Sanity-check bounds for the leave-one-out estimate <Cite id="kearns1999" /> |

Here $S^{\setminus i}$ is the sample with $z_i$ removed. Removal and replacement are interchangeable up to a factor of
2: an algorithm that changes by at most $\beta$ when one example is removed changes by at most $2\beta$ when one is
replaced, by the triangle inequality through $S^{\setminus i}$ <Cite id="bousquet2002" />. Bousquet and Elisseeff state
their results for removal; this note and Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David use replacement <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.

Hypothesis stability gives a bound whose deviation term is $\sqrt{(M^2 + 12Mm\beta)/(2m\delta)}$, which grows like
$1/\sqrt\delta$ rather than $\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)}$ <Cite id="bousquet2002" />.

Two further variants:

- **Randomised algorithms.** When $A$ uses its own randomness, as <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>
  does, the condition is applied to $\expect_A \ell(A(S), z)$, the loss averaged over the algorithm's internal
  randomness, with the samples still fixed <Cite id="hardt2016sgd" />.
- **Distributional stability.** <NoteLink to="differential-privacy">Differential privacy</NoteLink> asks that the whole
  distribution of the output change by at most a factor $e^\varepsilon$ when one example is replaced. It implies
  uniform stability in the randomised sense, with $\beta = (e^\varepsilon - 1)M$ for every loss in $[0, M]$, and it
  survives post-processing and composition, which is what
  <NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">adaptive data analysis</NoteLink> needs.

The idea is older than its name. Rogers and Wagner (1978) and Devroye and Wagner (1979) bounded the error of local rules
such as $k$-nearest neighbours through the effect of one deleted example <Cite id="rogers1978" />
<Cite id="devroye1979" />. Kearns and Ron used stability to justify the leave-one-out estimate of the error
<Cite id="kearns1999" />. Mukherjee, Niyogi, Poggio and Rifkin showed that stability is sufficient for generalisation
and characterises the consistency of <Gloss name="erm">empirical risk minimisation</Gloss> <Cite id="mukherjee2006" />.
In Vapnik's general learning setting, some problems are learnable although uniform convergence fails, and stability
rather than uniform convergence is the condition that characterises learnability <Cite id="shalevshwartz2010" />.

## The generalisation bound

The bound comes from two facts. The expected gap equals an average stability, and the gap concentrates around its
expectation. Fix a uniformly $\beta$-stable algorithm $A$, a loss in $[0, M]$ and a sample $S$ of $m$ independent draws
from $\Dcal$. Let $S' = (z_1', \dots, z_m')$ be a second, independent sample, the <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">ghost
sample</NoteLink> that supplies the replacements.

**Step 1: the expected gap is an average stability.** The true risk of the output is an expectation over a fresh
example. For each $i$, the ghost example $z_i'$ is independent of $S$, so

$$
\expect_S R(A(S)) = \expect_{S, S'}\, \ell(A(S), z_i').
$$

Now rename. Swapping the names of $z_i$ and $z_i'$ maps the pair $(S, z_i')$ to the pair $(S^{(i)}, z_i)$, and both
pairs consist of $m + 1$ independent draws from $\Dcal$. The two pairs have the same distribution, so

$$
\expect_{S, S'}\, \ell(A(S), z_i') = \expect_{S, S'}\, \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i).
$$

The expected training loss is $\expect_S \hat R_S(A(S)) = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \expect_S\, \ell(A(S), z_i)$. Averaging
the renamed identity over $i$ and subtracting gives

$$
\expect_S\LB R(A(S)) - \hat R_S(A(S))\RB = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \expect_{S, S'}\LB\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i)\RB.
$$

Each term on the right compares the loss at $z_i$ of a hypothesis trained without $z_i$ and of one trained with it.
Uniform stability bounds each term by $\beta$, so the expected gap is at most $\beta$. The identity is exact. It says
that the expected generalisation gap is the expected increase in loss at a training point when that point is swapped
out of the training set <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.

**Step 2: the gap has bounded differences.** Let $\Phi(S) = R(A(S)) - \hat R_S(A(S))$. Replace $z_i$ by $z_i'$.

- The true risk $R(A(S)) = \expect_z \ell(A(S), z)$ changes by at most $\beta$, because the loss at every $z$ changes by
  at most $\beta$.
- The empirical risk is an average of $m$ terms. The $m - 1$ terms at the unchanged examples each change by at most
  $\beta$. The term at position $i$ changes from $\ell(A(S), z_i)$ to $\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i')$, two losses at different
  points, so it changes by at most $M$. The average changes by at most $\frac{m-1}{m}\beta + \frac{M}{m} \le \beta +
  \frac{M}{m}$.

So $\Phi$ changes by at most $c = 2\beta + M/m$ when any one example is replaced.

**Step 3: concentrate.** <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">McDiarmid's inequality</NoteLink> for a function of $m$
independent examples with bounded differences $c$ gives

$$
\prob\paren{\Phi(S) - \expect\Phi(S) \ge t} \le \exp\paren{-\frac{2t^2}{mc^2}}.
$$

Setting the right side to $\delta$ and solving gives
$t = c\sqrt{m\ln(1/\delta)/2} = (2m\beta + M)\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$.

**Step 4: combine.** With probability at least $1 - \delta$, $\Phi(S) \le \expect\Phi(S) + t \le \beta + t$, which is
the bound in the definition.

<Derivation title="Why the renaming step is legitimate">

The examples $z_1, \dots, z_m, z_1', \dots, z_m'$ are <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss>.
Any function of them has the same expectation after two of them are swapped, because the swap does not change their
joint distribution. Apply this to the function $g(z_1, \dots, z_m, z_i') = \ell(A(z_1, \dots, z_m), z_i')$ and the swap
of $z_i$ with $z_i'$. The swapped function is
$\ell(A(z_1, \dots, z_{i-1}, z_i', z_{i+1}, \dots, z_m), z_i) = \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)$. Hence
$\expect \ell(A(S), z_i') = \expect \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)$. The argument needs no property of $A$; stability enters only
when the difference is bounded.

</Derivation>

### Reading the bound

The bound has two terms with different roles.

- **The expected gap, $\beta$.** This term is tight in the sense of Step 1: it is the average effect of swapping one
  example.
- **The fluctuation, $(2m\beta + M)\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$.** For a single fixed hypothesis,
  <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> gives $M\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$. The extra
  $2m\beta$ is the price of the output depending on the sample. It stays bounded only if $\beta = O(1/m)$. With
  $\beta = \kappa/m$ the bound reads $\kappa/m + (2\kappa + M)\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$, which is of order $1/\sqrt m$.
  With $\beta$ of order $1/\sqrt m$ the fluctuation term no longer shrinks, and the bound is vacuous.

Later work removed most of this overhead. Feldman and Vondrák proved a gap of
$O\paren{\beta\ln(m)\ln(m/\delta) + M\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/m}}$ for a loss in $[0, M]$. For
$\beta = O(1/\sqrt m)$ this is of the same order as the sampling error of a single fixed hypothesis, up to logarithms
<Cite id="feldman2019stability" />. Bousquet, Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy sharpened the bound and proved lower bounds
showing it is sharp up to a logarithmic factor <Cite id="bousquet2020sharper" />.

## Worked example

Take examples $z_i \in [0, 1]$, the hypothesis space $[0, 1]$ and the <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared
loss</NoteLink> $\ell(w, z) = (w - z)^2$, so the loss lies in $[0, 1]$ and $M = 1$. The algorithm returns the sample
mean, $A(S) = \bar z = \frac1m\sum_i z_i$.

1. **The stability constant.** Replacing $z_i$ by $z_i'$ moves the mean by $\abs{z_i' - z_i}/m \le 1/m$. For any test
   point $z$, the loss changes by $\abs{(w - z)^2 - (w' - z)^2} = \abs{w - w'}\,\abs{w + w' - 2z} \le \frac1m \cdot 2$.
   So $\beta = 2/m$.
2. **The expected gap.** The bound says $\expect[R(\bar z) - \hat R_S(\bar z)] \le 2/m$. The exact value is known. If
   the examples have variance $\sigma^2$, then $\expect R(\bar z) = \sigma^2(1 + 1/m)$ and
   $\expect \hat R_S(\bar z) = \sigma^2(1 - 1/m)$, so the expected gap is $2\sigma^2/m$. A variable in $[0, 1]$ has
   $\sigma^2 \le 1/4$, so the true expected gap is at most $1/(2m)$. The stability bound is four times too large in the
   worst case.
3. **The high-probability bound.** With $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$, $\ln(1/\delta) = 3.00$ and $2m\beta + M = 5$.
   The bound is $0.002 + 5\sqrt{3.00/2{,}000} = 0.002 + 0.194 = 0.196$. A single fixed hypothesis would have Hoeffding
   width $\sqrt{3.00/2{,}000} = 0.039$. The factor of 5 is $2m\beta + M$, with $2m\beta = 4$ and $M = 1$.
4. **Vacuous or not.** The loss lies in $[0, 1]$ and the expected loss of any $w \in [0, 1]$ is at most $1$, so a gap
   bound of 0.196 is informative. It is still about 400 times the largest possible expected gap, $1/(2m) = 0.0005$.
5. **Sample size.** For the fluctuation term to be at most $0.05$, the sample must satisfy
   $5\sqrt{3.00/(2m)} \le 0.05$, that is $m \ge (5/0.05)^2 \cdot 3.00/2 = 15{,}000$.

The example shows the typical pattern. The expected-gap statement $\beta$ is within a small factor of the truth. The
high-probability statement pays a factor $2m\beta + M$ in front of the usual $\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/m}$ term.

## Assumptions

- **Independent examples.** Step 1 swaps examples, and Step 3 needs independence for McDiarmid's inequality.
- **Bounded loss.** The bound uses the range $M$ in Step 2. Unbounded losses, such as the squared loss on unbounded
  targets, need a bounded domain or a different argument.
- **A supremum over all samples.** Uniform stability is a worst case over every sample, including samples that the
  distribution never produces. Weaker, distribution-dependent notions trade this for weaker conclusions.

## Pitfalls

- **Stability is a property of the algorithm, not of the class.** Two learners with the same hypothesis class can have
  very different stability. Unregularised least squares on a degree-9 polynomial is unstable;
  <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> on the same class is stable.
- **Exact empirical risk minimisation is usually not uniformly stable for the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0-1
  loss</NoteLink>.** A single example can flip which hypothesis wins, and the 0-1 loss then jumps by 1. Stability
  results apply to convex surrogate losses, smooth algorithms and averaged or randomised predictors.
- **The constant depends on the convention.** Removal and replacement differ by a factor of 2, and the factor in the
  regulariser ($\lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$ or $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec}^2$) changes another factor of 2. Compare constants
  only after fixing both.
- **A small expected gap is not a small gap with high probability.** The bound $\beta$ on the expected gap can be tight
  while the high-probability bound is vacuous, as in the worked example of
  <NoteLink to="stability-of-regularised-empirical-risk-minimisation">regularised empirical risk
  minimisation</NoteLink>.
