<Definition>
Let $\ell(\wvec, z)$ be a loss that is convex in the parameters $\wvec \in \reals^d$ and $L$-Lipschitz in $\wvec$, that
is $\abs{\ell(\wvec, z) - \ell(\wvec', z)} \le L\norm{\wvec - \wvec'}$ for every example $z$. **Regularised
<Gloss name="erm">empirical risk minimisation</Gloss>** returns the minimiser $\wvec_S$ of

$$
F_S(\wvec) = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \ell(\wvec, z_i) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\norm{\wvec}^2, \qquad \lambda > 0.
$$

This algorithm is uniformly $\beta$-stable with

$$
\beta = \frac{2L^2}{\lambda m},
$$

and replacing one example moves the minimiser by at most $\norm{\wvec_S - \wvec_{S^{(i)}}} \le 2L/(\lambda m)$
<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" /> <Cite id="bousquet2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The penalty $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec}^2$ makes the objective a bowl with curvature at least $\lambda$ in every
direction. One training example contributes only $1/m$ of the data term. Replacing it tilts the bowl by a force of at
most $2L/m$, because the loss of the old and the new example each have slope at most $L$. A bowl of curvature $\lambda$
pushed by a force $2L/m$ moves its bottom by at most $2L/(\lambda m)$. The loss is $L$-Lipschitz, so the loss at any
test point moves by at most $L$ times that.

The penalty strength $\lambda$ has two opposing effects. A large $\lambda$ makes the bowl steep, so the minimiser barely
moves, but it also pulls $\wvec_S$ towards zero and away from the parameters that fit the data. A small $\lambda$ lets
the minimiser follow the data closely, and then single examples move it a lot. The best $\lambda$ balances the two, and
the balance gives a rate of order $1/\sqrt m$.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, this note supplies the
constant $\beta$ that <NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic stability</NoteLink> turns into a bound. The
structure exploited is strong convexity of the objective, not the size of the hypothesis class. The bound does not
depend on the dimension $d$, so it applies unchanged to kernel methods with infinite-dimensional feature spaces.

## The stability bound

The derivation uses one property of strongly convex functions and the fact that $F_S$ and $F_{S^{(i)}}$ differ in a
single term.

**Step 1: strong convexity at a minimiser.** The objective $F_S$ is $\lambda$-strongly convex: it is a convex function
plus $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec}^2$ (<NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex functions</NoteLink>). A
$\lambda$-strongly convex function $F$ with minimiser $\wvec^*$ satisfies, for every $\vvec$,

$$
F(\vvec) - F(\wvec^*) \ge \frac\lambda2\norm{\vvec - \wvec^*}^2.
$$



**Step 2: apply it twice.** Write $\wvec = \wvec_S$ and $\wvec' = \wvec_{S^{(i)}}$. Step 1 for $F_S$ at its minimiser
$\wvec$, evaluated at $\vvec = \wvec'$, and for $F_{S^{(i)}}$ at its minimiser $\wvec'$, evaluated at $\vvec = \wvec$,
gives two inequalities. Adding them gives

$$
\LB F_S(\wvec') - F_{S^{(i)}}(\wvec')\RB + \LB F_{S^{(i)}}(\wvec) - F_S(\wvec)\RB \ge \lambda\norm{\wvec' - \wvec}^2.
$$

**Step 3: the objectives differ in one term.** The two samples share every example except the $i$-th, and they share
the penalty. So $F_S(\vvec) - F_{S^{(i)}}(\vvec) = \frac1m\LB\ell(\vvec, z_i) - \ell(\vvec, z_i')\RB$ for every $\vvec$,
and the left side of Step 2 equals

$$
\frac1m\LB\ell(\wvec', z_i) - \ell(\wvec, z_i)\RB + \frac1m\LB\ell(\wvec, z_i') - \ell(\wvec', z_i')\RB
\le \frac{2L}{m}\norm{\wvec' - \wvec},
$$

where each bracket is bounded by the Lipschitz condition.

**Step 4: solve.** Combining Steps 2 and 3, $\lambda\norm{\wvec' - \wvec}^2 \le \frac{2L}{m}\norm{\wvec' - \wvec}$, so
$\norm{\wvec' - \wvec} \le 2L/(\lambda m)$.

**Step 5: from parameters to losses.** For any test example $z$, the Lipschitz condition gives
$\abs{\ell(\wvec, z) - \ell(\wvec', z)} \le L\norm{\wvec - \wvec'} \le 2L^2/(\lambda m)$. This holds for every sample,
index, replacement and test example, which is uniform stability with $\beta = 2L^2/(\lambda m)$.

<Derivation title="Strong convexity at a minimiser">

A function $F$ is $\lambda$-strongly convex if $F(\vvec) \ge F(\uvec) + \inner{\gvec}{\vvec - \uvec} +
\frac\lambda2\norm{\vvec - \uvec}^2$ for every $\uvec$, $\vvec$ and every subgradient $\gvec$ of $F$ at $\uvec$
(<NoteLink to="subgradients">subgradients</NoteLink>). At a minimiser $\wvec^*$ the zero vector is a subgradient.
Setting $\uvec = \wvec^*$ and $\gvec = \zeros$ gives $F(\vvec) \ge F(\wvec^*) + \frac\lambda2\norm{\vvec - \wvec^*}^2$.
The sum of a convex function and $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec}^2$ is $\lambda$-strongly convex because
$\frac\lambda2\norm{\vvec}^2 = \frac\lambda2\norm{\uvec}^2 + \inner{\lambda\uvec}{\vvec - \uvec} +
\frac\lambda2\norm{\vvec - \uvec}^2$ exactly, and the convex part contributes the inequality without the quadratic
term.

</Derivation>

With $\beta$ in hand, <NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic stability</NoteLink> gives the expected gap

$$
\expect_S\LB R(\wvec_S) - \hat R_S(\wvec_S)\RB \le \frac{2L^2}{\lambda m},
$$

and, for a loss bounded by $M$, a high-probability bound with the extra term $(2m\beta + M)\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$.
Since $2m\beta = 4L^2/\lambda$ does not shrink with $m$, the high-probability bound is of order
$(L^2/\lambda + M)/\sqrt m$. Feldman and Vondrák's sharper bound replaces the $L^2/\lambda$ factor by logarithmic terms
times $\beta$ <Cite id="feldman2019stability" />.

Bousquet and Elisseeff proved the original version for kernel methods: with penalty $\lambda\norm{g}_k^2$ in a
<NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space">reproducing kernel Hilbert space</NoteLink> with $k(x, x) \le \kappa^2$
and a loss that is $\sigma$-Lipschitz in the prediction, removing one example changes the loss by at most
$\sigma^2\kappa^2/(2\lambda m)$ <Cite id="bousquet2002" />. Their penalty is twice the one used here and they remove
rather than replace an example, which accounts for the factor of 4 between the two constants.

## Fitting against stability

The stability bound controls the gap, not the risk. The risk also depends on how well $\wvec_S$ fits.
For any fixed comparison vector $\uvec$, $\wvec_S$ minimises $F_S$ and the penalty is
non-negative, so

$$
\expect_S \hat R_S(\wvec_S) \le \expect_S F_S(\wvec_S) \le \expect_S F_S(\uvec) = R(\uvec) + \frac\lambda2\norm{\uvec}^2.
$$

The last equality holds because $\uvec$ is fixed, so its empirical risk is unbiased for its true risk. Adding the
expected gap gives an **<NoteLink to="oracle-inequalities">oracle inequality</NoteLink>**:

$$
\expect_S R(\wvec_S) \le R(\uvec) + \frac\lambda2\norm{\uvec}^2 + \frac{2L^2}{\lambda m} \qquad \text{for every } \uvec.
$$

The middle term is the cost of <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>, which grows with $\lambda$. The
last term is the cost of instability, which shrinks with $\lambda$. For comparison vectors with $\norm{\uvec} \le B$,
the choice $\lambda = 2L/(B\sqrt m)$ equalises the two terms at $LB/\sqrt m$ each:

$$
\expect_S R(\wvec_S) \le \min_{\norm{\uvec} \le B} R(\uvec) + \frac{2LB}{\sqrt m}.
$$

This matches the rate that <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> gives for the
norm-bounded linear class $\set{\wvec : \norm{\wvec} \le B}$ with inputs of norm at most 1, but the regulariser reaches
it without restricting the class <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. The trade-off is the
<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance trade-off</NoteLink> in the language of stability, and choosing
$\lambda$ from the data is a task for <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or
<NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural risk minimisation</NoteLink>.

## Worked example: ridge regression

Take inputs with $\norm{x} \le 1$, targets $\abs{y} \le 1$, and the loss
$\ell(\wvec, (x, y)) = \frac12(\inner{\wvec}{x} - y)^2$. Regularised ERM with this loss is
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>.

1. **Bounding the region.** The <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> is not Lipschitz on all of
   $\reals^d$, so first confine the minimisers. Because $\wvec_S$ minimises $F_S$, $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec_S}^2 \le F_S(\wvec_S) \le F_S(\zeros) =
   \frac1m\sum_i \frac12 y_i^2 \le \frac12$. So $\norm{\wvec_S} \le 1/\sqrt\lambda$ for every sample.
2. **The Lipschitz constant on that region.** The gradient of the loss is $(\inner{\wvec}{x} - y)x$. On the ball
   $\norm{\wvec} \le 1/\sqrt\lambda$ its norm is at most $\abs{\inner{\wvec}{x}} + \abs{y} \le 1/\sqrt\lambda + 1 = L$.
   The ball is convex, so the derivation above applies with this $L$. The largest loss on the ball is
   $M = \frac12 L^2$.
3. **The stability constant.** $\beta = 2L^2/(\lambda m)$. With $m = 10{,}000$ and $\lambda = 0.1$,
   $L = 1 + 3.162 = 4.162$, $L^2 = 17.3$ and $\beta = 34.6/1{,}000 = 0.035$.
4. **Reading the numbers.** The table evaluates the expected gap $\beta$, the high-probability bound at $\delta = 0.05$,
   and the sample size that makes $\beta \le 0.01$, for four penalties.

| $\lambda$ | $L$ | Expected gap $\beta$ | Largest loss $M$ | Bound at $\delta = 0.05$ | $m$ for $\beta \le 0.01$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.01 | 11.0 | 2.42 | 60.5 | vacuous | 2,420,000 |
| 0.1 | 4.16 | 0.035 | 8.66 | vacuous (8.6) | 34,650 |
| 1 | 2.00 | 0.0008 | 2.00 | 0.22 | 800 |
| 10 | 1.32 | 0.000035 | 0.87 | 0.019 | 35 |

5. **Vacuous or not.** At $\lambda = 0.1$ the expected gap is 0.035, which is small next to losses of order 0.1 to 1.
   The high-probability bound is 8.6, almost the largest possible loss, because $2m\beta = 693$ multiplies the
   fluctuation term. At $\lambda = 10$ both bounds are small, but the minimiser is confined to
   $\norm{\wvec} \le 0.32$ and cannot fit a target with a larger slope: the cost now sits in the fit, as the oracle
   inequality predicts.
6. **No dimension.** Nothing in the table depends on $d$. Unregularised least squares has expected optimism
   $2d\sigma^2/m$ for noise variance $\sigma^2$ (<NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation gap</NoteLink>), which
   grows with $d$.

The same argument covers the <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink>. The
<Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss> $\max(0, 1 - y\inner{\wvec}{x})$ is 1-Lipschitz in $\wvec$ when
$\norm{x} \le 1$, so the soft-margin objective with penalty $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec}^2$ has $\beta = 2/(\lambda m)$
for the hinge loss <Cite id="bousquet2002" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The Lipschitz constant must hold where the minimisers live.** For the squared and
  <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">logistic losses</NoteLink> the constant depends on a bound on $\norm{\wvec}$, which
  itself depends on $\lambda$. Ignoring this and quoting $L = 1$ gives a bound that is too small.
- **The penalty's scaling sets the constant.** $\frac\lambda2\norm{\wvec}^2$, $\lambda\norm{\wvec}^2$ and a penalty on
  the sum rather than the average of the losses differ by factors of 2 or $m$. A penalty $\lambda$ on the sum of the
  losses is the per-example penalty $\lambda/m$, so it gives a stability constant that does not shrink with $m$.
- **Stability needs the exact minimiser.** The derivation uses the optimality of $\wvec_S$. An approximate minimiser is
  stable only up to its <NoteLink to="approximation-estimation-optimisation-trade-off">optimisation error</NoteLink>;
  for the stability of the optimiser itself, see <NoteLink to="stability-of-stochastic-gradient-descent">stability of
  stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink>.
- **Convexity is essential.** Without it the objective can have several minima, and replacing one example can make the
  algorithm jump between them.
