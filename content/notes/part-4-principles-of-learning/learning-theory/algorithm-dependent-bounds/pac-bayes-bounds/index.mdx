<Definition>
Let $\Hcal$ be a set of hypotheses, $\ell(h, z) \in [0, 1]$ a loss, and $P$ a distribution on $\Hcal$, the **prior**,
chosen before the sample $S$ of $m$ independent examples is drawn. For any distribution $Q$ on $\Hcal$, the
**posterior**, the **Gibbs risk** $R(Q) = \expect_{h \sim Q} R(h)$ is the risk of the randomised predictor that draws a
fresh $h \sim Q$ for each prediction, and $\hat R_S(Q) = \expect_{h \sim Q}\hat R_S(h)$ is its empirical counterpart.
For $m \ge 8$ and $\delta \in (0, 1)$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over $S$, every $Q$ satisfies

$$
\operatorname{kl}\paren{\hat R_S(Q) \,\|\, R(Q)} \le \frac{\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(2\sqrt m/\delta)}{m},
$$

where $\operatorname{kl}(q \,\|\, p) = q\ln\frac qp + (1 - q)\ln\frac{1 - q}{1 - p}$ is the Kullback–Leibler divergence
between Bernoulli laws. Consequently

$$
R(Q) \le \hat R_S(Q) + \sqrt{\frac{\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(2\sqrt m/\delta)}{2m}}
$$

<Cite id="seeger2002" /> <Cite id="maurer2004" /> <Cite id="mcallester1999" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink> gives each hypothesis $h$ in a countable class a share
$\delta P(h)$ of the failure probability, so a hypothesis pays $\ln(1/P(h))$ in its bound. A learner that returns a
single hypothesis with small prior weight pays a lot, even when many nearby hypotheses fit the data equally well.

A PAC-Bayes bound lets the learner return a whole distribution $Q$ over hypotheses, and charges it
$\KL(Q \,\|\, P)$ instead. If $Q$ concentrates on a single $h$ in a countable class, then
$\KL(Q \,\|\, P) = \ln(1/P(h))$ and the Occam bound returns. If $Q$ spreads over a large region that the prior also
covers, the divergence is small even when every individual hypothesis in the region has tiny prior weight. The bound
rewards a posterior that is broad where the data allow it.

For a network with real-valued weights, a natural posterior is a Gaussian centred on the trained weights. Its width
can be large in directions where perturbing the weights does not change the training error, and must be small in
directions where it does. A solution that tolerates large perturbations admits a wide posterior with a small divergence
from a broad prior, and so a small bound. This is the link between PAC-Bayes and
<NoteLink to="flat-minima-and-sharpness">flat minima</NoteLink>.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, PAC-Bayes replaces the
<NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> of the uniform step by a change of measure, and the capacity term
becomes a divergence that depends on the output of the learner. The prior plays the role that the code plays in the
Occam bound. It must be fixed before the data, but it need not be anyone's belief, and the bound holds whatever
distribution generated the data <Cite id="langford2005" />.

## Priors, posteriors and Gibbs risk

The words come from <NoteLink to="bayesian-statistics">Bayesian statistics</NoteLink>, and the objects differ.

- **The prior** is any distribution over hypotheses fixed before seeing $S$. It may use other data, such as a separate
  sample or the random initialisation of a network, as long as it does not use $S$.
- **The posterior** is any distribution, chosen after seeing $S$. It need not be the Bayesian posterior. The bound holds
  simultaneously for every $Q$, so the learner can optimise $Q$ to make the bound small.
- **The Gibbs predictor** draws $h \sim Q$ afresh for each input. The bound controls its risk $R(Q)$. The majority vote
  of $Q$, which predicts the label that most of $Q$'s mass predicts, has risk at most $2R(Q)$: whenever the vote errs,
  at least half of $Q$ errs.

For a fixed $\lambda > 0$, the **Gibbs posterior**
$Q_\lambda(h) \propto P(h)\exp(-\lambda\hat R_S(h))$ minimises $\lambda\hat R_S(Q) + \KL(Q \,\|\, P)$ over all $Q$
<Cite id="alquier2024" />. When the loss is the negative log-likelihood and $\lambda = m$,
$\exp(-m\hat R_S(h))$ is the likelihood of the sample under $h$, and the Gibbs posterior is the Bayesian posterior
(<NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">Bayesian inference</NoteLink>).

## The bound and its derivation

A linear form of the bound, due to Catoni, has the following derivation <Cite id="catoni2007" />
<Cite id="alquier2024" />. For any fixed $\lambda > 0$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, every $Q$ satisfies

$$
R(Q) \le \hat R_S(Q) + \frac{\lambda}{8m} + \frac{\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(1/\delta)}{\lambda}.
$$



**Step 1: one hypothesis, an exponential moment.** Fix $h$. The losses $\ell(h, z_i)$ are independent and lie in
$[0, 1]$, so each term of $\lambda(R(h) - \hat R_S(h)) = \sum_i \frac\lambda m(R(h) - \ell(h, z_i))$ is a centred
variable with range $\lambda/m$. <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's lemma</NoteLink>
(<NoteLink to="sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian random variables</NoteLink>) bounds the
<Gloss name="mgf">moment generating function</Gloss> of each term by $\exp(\lambda^2/(8m^2))$, and independence
multiplies them:

$$
\expect_S \exp\paren{\lambda(R(h) - \hat R_S(h))} \le \exp\paren{\frac{\lambda^2}{8m}}.
$$

**Step 2: average over the prior.** The prior does not depend on $S$, so the order of the two expectations can be
swapped (Tonelli's theorem for non-negative functions):

$$
\expect_S\, \expect_{h \sim P}\exp\paren{\lambda(R(h) - \hat R_S(h))} \le \exp\paren{\frac{\lambda^2}{8m}}.
$$

**Step 3: Markov's inequality.** The inner expectation $X_S = \expect_{h \sim P}\exp(\lambda(R(h) - \hat R_S(h)))$ is a
non-negative <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink>. By
<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's inequality</NoteLink>, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$
\ln X_S \le \frac{\lambda^2}{8m} + \ln\frac1\delta.
$$

**Step 4: change of measure.** The Donsker–Varadhan inequality states that for any function $f$ on $\Hcal$ and any
distribution $Q$,

$$
\expect_{h \sim Q} f(h) \le \KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln\expect_{h \sim P} e^{f(h)}
$$

<Cite id="donsker1975" />. Apply it with $f(h) = \lambda(R(h) - \hat R_S(h))$. The left side is
$\lambda(R(Q) - \hat R_S(Q))$ and the logarithm on the right is $\ln X_S$. On the event of Step 3, for every $Q$,

$$
\lambda\paren{R(Q) - \hat R_S(Q)} \le \KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \frac{\lambda^2}{8m} + \ln\frac1\delta.
$$

**Step 5: divide by $\lambda$.** This gives the linear bound.

The event in Step 3 does not involve $Q$. That is why the bound holds for all posteriors at once, and why $Q$ may be
chosen after looking at the data. No union bound over hypotheses appears.

<Derivation title="The Donsker–Varadhan inequality">

Take a finite $\Hcal$ for simplicity and $Q$ with $Q(h) > 0$ only where $P(h) > 0$. Define the
<NoteLink to="expectation-propagation">tilted distribution</NoteLink> $P_f(h) = P(h)e^{f(h)}/Z$ with
$Z = \expect_{h \sim P}e^{f(h)}$. Then

$$
0 \le \KL(Q \,\|\, P_f) = \sum_h Q(h)\ln\frac{Q(h)Z}{P(h)e^{f(h)}} = \KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln Z - \expect_{h \sim Q}f(h).
$$

Rearranging gives the inequality. Equality holds when $Q = P_f$. With $f = -\lambda\hat R_S$, the minimiser of
$\lambda\hat R_S(Q) + \KL(Q \,\|\, P)$ is therefore the Gibbs posterior $Q_\lambda \propto P e^{-\lambda\hat R_S}$. The
general case replaces sums by integrals and $Q(h)/P(h)$ by the density $dQ/dP$.

</Derivation>

**Choosing $\lambda$.** The right side is smallest at $\lambda = \sqrt{8m(\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(1/\delta))}$, where it
equals $\hat R_S(Q) + \sqrt{(\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(1/\delta))/(2m)}$. But $\lambda$ must be fixed before the data, and
the best value depends on $Q$, which depends on the data. The standard fix is a union bound over a grid of $J$ values of
$\lambda$, each with confidence $\delta/J$, which adds $\ln J$ to the numerator. McAllester's original bound pays in
this way: $R(Q) \le \hat R_S(Q) + \sqrt{(\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(1/\delta) + \frac52\ln m + 8)/(2m - 1)}$
<Cite id="mcallester1999" /> <Cite id="alquier2024" />.

## The kl and Catoni forms

The bound in the definition replaces Hoeffding's lemma by a sharper exponential moment. Maurer proved that for $m \ge 8$
independent variables in $[0, 1]$ with mean $\mu$ and average $\hat\mu$,
$\expect\exp(m\operatorname{kl}(\hat\mu \,\|\, \mu)) \le 2\sqrt m$ <Cite id="maurer2004" />. Running Steps 2 to 4 with
$f(h) = m\operatorname{kl}(\hat R_S(h) \,\|\, R(h))$ gives
$m\,\expect_{h \sim Q}\operatorname{kl}(\hat R_S(h) \,\|\, R(h)) \le \KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(2\sqrt m/\delta)$. The
binary divergence is jointly convex, so by Jensen's inequality the expectation over $Q$ can move inside, which gives the
**Seeger–Maurer kl bound** of the definition <Cite id="seeger2002" />. It needs no tuning parameter.

The kl bound is an implicit statement about $R(Q)$. The bound is the largest $p$ with
$\operatorname{kl}(\hat R_S(Q) \,\|\, p) \le c$, where $c = (\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(2\sqrt m/\delta))/m$. Two explicit
relaxations show its behaviour.

- **Pinsker's inequality** $\operatorname{kl}(q \,\|\, p) \ge 2(q - p)^2$ gives $R(Q) \le \hat R_S(Q) + \sqrt{c/2}$,
  the **McAllester form** in the definition, with better constants than McAllester's original
  <Cite id="alquier2024" />.
- **Near zero empirical risk,** $\operatorname{kl}(0 \,\|\, p) = -\ln(1 - p) \ge p$, so $\hat R_S(Q) = 0$ gives
  $R(Q) \le c$. The bound decays like $1/m$ instead of $1/\sqrt m$, the PAC-Bayes analogue of the
  <NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">realisable rate</NoteLink>.

Catoni's form gives an explicit bound that keeps the $1/m$ behaviour <Cite id="catoni2007" />. For a constant $C > 0$
fixed in advance, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, every $Q$ satisfies

$$
R(Q) \le \frac{1 - \exp\paren{-C\hat R_S(Q) - (\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(1/\delta))/m}}{1 - e^{-C}}.
$$

## Worked example

A linear classifier $h_{\wvec}(x) = \sgn\inner{\wvec}{x}$ in $d = 100$ dimensions is trained on $m = 10{,}000$ examples.
The prior is $P = \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$, fixed in advance. The posterior is $Q = \Gauss(\hat\wvec, \sigma^2\eye)$,
centred on the trained weights $\hat\wvec$ with $\norm{\hat\wvec} = 10$. Suppose a Monte Carlo estimate on the training
set gives Gibbs training error $\hat R_S(Q) = 0.05$ for $\sigma = 1$. Take $\delta = 0.05$.

1. **The divergence.** For two Gaussians with identity-scaled covariances,
   $\KL(\Gauss(\hat\wvec, \sigma^2\eye) \,\|\, \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)) = \frac12\paren{d\sigma^2 + \norm{\hat\wvec}^2 - d - d\ln\sigma^2}$
   (<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>). For $\sigma = 1$ this is
   $\frac12\norm{\hat\wvec}^2 = 50$ nats, whatever $d$ is.
2. **The complexity term.** $\ln(2\sqrt m/\delta) = \ln(2 \cdot 100/0.05) = \ln 4{,}000 = 8.29$, so
   $c = (50 + 8.29)/10{,}000 = 0.0058$.
3. **Four bounds on the Gibbs risk.**
   - McAllester form: $0.05 + \sqrt{0.0058/2} = 0.05 + 0.054 = 0.104$.
   - McAllester's original constants: $0.115$.
   - Seeger–Maurer kl form: the largest $p$ with $\operatorname{kl}(0.05 \,\|\, p) \le 0.0058$ is $p = 0.077$.
   - Catoni's form with $C = 0.5$: $0.076$.

   The kl and Catoni forms are tighter because the empirical risk is small,
   where $\operatorname{kl}(\hat R_S(Q) \,\|\, p)$ rises steeply in $p$.
4. **The width of the posterior.** A narrower posterior lowers the Gibbs training error towards the error of
   $\hat\wvec$ itself, but raises the divergence: $\sigma = 0.5$ gives $\KL = 81.8$, $\sigma = 0.25$ gives 141.8 and
   $\sigma = 0.1$ gives 230.8. The best $\sigma$ balances the two, and finding it is the optimisation of
   <NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">non-vacuous generalisation bounds</NoteLink>.
5. **Sample size.** For the McAllester width $\sqrt{c/2}$ to fall to 0.02, $c$ must fall to 0.0008, which needs
   $m \approx 74{,}000$ (solving $m = (50 + \ln(2\sqrt m/0.05))/0.0008$ by iteration). For the kl bound to sit 0.02
   above an empirical risk of 0.05 needs only $m \approx 17{,}000$.
6. **Vacuous or not.** All four bounds are well below 0.5, so they are informative. A
   <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC bound</NoteLink> for half-spaces in 100 dimensions depends on $d$;
   this bound depends on $\norm{\hat\wvec}$ and $\sigma$, and the same numbers hold in $d = 10^6$.

## Pitfalls

- **The prior must not depend on $S$.** Centring the prior on weights trained on $S$ voids the bound. A prior may use a
  separate part of the data, which is then not part of $S$.
- **The bound is for the randomised predictor.** It controls $R(Q)$, not the risk of the mean weights $\hat\wvec$.
  Statements about a deterministic predictor need a separate step, such as the factor 2 for the majority vote or a
  margin argument.
- **The Gibbs training error must itself be estimated.** It is an expectation over $Q$, usually estimated by sampling.
  The sampling error needs its own bound and its own share of $\delta$.
- **A free parameter must be fixed in advance or paid for.** Optimising $\lambda$ or $C$ after seeing the data needs a
  union bound over a grid.
- **Scale invariance is not automatic.** Rescaling the weights of $h_{\wvec}$ leaves the classifier unchanged but
  changes $\KL(Q \,\|\, P)$ for a fixed prior. The prior's scale must be chosen with care, often by a union bound over
  a grid of scales.
