<Definition>
Let $h : \Xcal \to \reals$ be a real-valued score and classify by $\sgn(h(x))$, with labels $y \in \set{-1, +1}$. The
**margin** of $h$ on an example $(x, y)$ is $y\,h(x)$: positive when the example is classified correctly, and large
when it is classified with room to spare. For a scale $\gamma > 0$, the **empirical margin loss**
$\hat R_\gamma(h) = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\indicator[y_i h(x_i) < \gamma]$ is the fraction of the $m$ training examples
with margin below $\gamma$. A **margin bound** states that, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the sample,
every $h$ in a class $\Hcal$ has risk $R(h) \le \hat R_\gamma(h) + C(\Hcal, \gamma, m, \delta)$, where the capacity
term $C$ falls as $\gamma$ grows <Cite id="bartlett1998" /> <Cite id="bartlett2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC bound</NoteLink> for linear classifiers in $\reals^D$ grows with the
dimension $D$, because half-spaces in $\reals^D$ shatter $D + 1$ points. A <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support
vector machine</NoteLink> with a Gaussian kernel works in an infinite-dimensional feature space, so the VC bound for it
is infinite. Yet such classifiers generalise well when they separate the data by a wide gap.

The margin explains this. Shattering a set of points with a wide gap is much harder than shattering it with any gap. A
unit-norm linear function on inputs of norm at most $X$ can separate at most about $(X/\gamma)^2$ points in every way
with margin $\gamma$, whatever the dimension (see the
<NoteLink to="pseudo-dimension-and-fat-shattering-dimension">fat-shattering dimension</NoteLink>). A classifier that
leaves most training points far from its boundary is therefore drawn from an effectively small class, and the bound
charges it for that small class.

The price is the training points that sit inside the margin. Counting them as errors makes $\hat R_\gamma(h)$ at least
the training error. A larger $\gamma$ shrinks the capacity term and counts more points as margin errors. The bound
holds for every $\gamma$, and the best one balances the two terms.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, the margin bound is the
move that exploits structure. Concentration and symmetrisation are as in the
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher bound</NoteLink>. The new step is to replace the 0–1 loss, whose class
is as rich as the VC dimension allows, with a Lipschitz loss whose class is controlled by a norm.

## The margin loss

The 0–1 loss of $\sgn(h)$ is $\indicator[y\,h(x) \le 0]$, a step function of the margin. A step has no Lipschitz
constant, so the <NoteLink to="contraction-lemma" /> cannot pass from the scores to the losses. The **ramp loss** at
scale $\gamma$ replaces the step with a slope:

$$
\rho_\gamma(u) = \begin{cases} 1 & u \le 0, \\ 1 - u/\gamma & 0 < u < \gamma, \\ 0 & u \ge \gamma. \end{cases}
$$

Three properties make it the bridge between the losses.

1. **It lies above the 0–1 loss:** $\indicator[u \le 0] \le \rho_\gamma(u)$, so the ramp risk bounds the error rate.
2. **It lies below the margin indicator:** $\rho_\gamma(u) \le \indicator[u < \gamma]$, so the empirical ramp loss is at
   most $\hat R_\gamma(h)$.
3. **It is $1/\gamma$-Lipschitz** and takes values in $[0, 1]$, so the contraction lemma and the Rademacher bound both
   apply to it.

The <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">hinge loss</NoteLink> $\max(0, 1 - u)$ lies above $\rho_1$. Any bound stated with
$\hat R_1$ therefore also holds with the average <Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss> in its place.

## The bound for linear and kernel classes

**Theorem (fixed margin).** Let $\Hcal$ be a class of real-valued functions and fix $\gamma > 0$ before seeing the
data. With probability at least $1 - \delta$ over an <Gloss name="iid" /> sample of size $m$, every $h \in \Hcal$
satisfies

$$
R(h) \le \hat R_\gamma(h) + \frac{2}{\gamma}\Rad_m(\Hcal) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

The derivation has four steps.

1. **Sandwich the 0–1 loss.** By property 1, $R(h) = \expect\indicator[y\,h(x) \le 0] \le \expect\rho_\gamma(y\,h(x))$.
2. **Bound the ramp risk uniformly.** The ramp losses $(x, y) \mapsto \rho_\gamma(y\,h(x))$ form a class of functions
   with values in $[0, 1]$. The <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher generalisation bound</NoteLink> (proved
   with <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">McDiarmid's inequality</NoteLink> and
   <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink>) gives, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ and for every
   $h$ at once,
   $\expect\rho_\gamma(y\,h(x)) \le \frac1m\sum_i\rho_\gamma(y_i h(x_i)) + 2\Rad_m(\rho_\gamma \circ \Hcal) +
   \sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$.
3. **Pass to the margin indicator.** By property 2, $\frac1m\sum_i\rho_\gamma(y_i h(x_i)) \le \hat R_\gamma(h)$.
4. **Contract.** The map $u \mapsto \rho_\gamma(y_i u)$ is $1/\gamma$-Lipschitz for each label $y_i = \pm 1$. The
   contraction lemma gives $\Rad_m(\rho_\gamma \circ \Hcal) \le \Rad_m(\Hcal)/\gamma$.

Chaining the four inequalities proves the theorem. Only the last step depends on the class, through $\Rad_m(\Hcal)$.

**Linear classes.** Let $\Hcal_B = \set{\xvec \mapsto \wvec^\top\xvec : \norm{\wvec} \le B}$ on inputs with
$\norm{\xvec} \le X$. Cauchy–Schwarz and Jensen's inequality give $\hat\Rad_S(\Hcal_B) \le
\frac{B}{m}\sqrt{\sum_i\norm{\xvec_i}^2} \le BX/\sqrt m$ (derived in the
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> note). The bound becomes

$$
R(h) \le \hat R_\gamma(h) + \frac{2BX}{\gamma\sqrt m} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

The sign of $\wvec^\top\xvec$ does not change when $\wvec$ is rescaled. Only the ratio $\gamma/B$ matters, so one can
fix $B = 1$ and measure the **normalised margin** $y\,\wvec^\top\xvec/\norm{\wvec}$, the signed distance of $\xvec$ to
the hyperplane. The capacity term is then $2X/(\gamma\sqrt m)$: the ratio of the data radius to the margin, divided by
$\sqrt m$. The dimension $D$ of $\xvec$ does not appear.

**Kernel classes.** Let $k$ be a kernel with feature map $\phi$ into a
<NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space">reproducing kernel Hilbert space</NoteLink> (<Gloss name="rkhs" />),
and let $\Hcal_B = \set{x \mapsto \inner{\wvec, \phi(x)} : \norm{\wvec} \le B}$. The same two steps apply in the
feature space, and $\norm{\sum_i\sigma_i\phi(x_i)}^2 = \sigmavec^\top\Kmat\sigmavec$ for the kernel matrix
$K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$. Its expectation over the signs is $\trace\Kmat$, so

$$
\hat\Rad_S(\Hcal_B) \le \frac{B\sqrt{\trace\Kmat}}{m} \le \frac{B\max_i\sqrt{k(x_i, x_i)}}{\sqrt m}
$$

<Cite id="bartlett2002" />. For the Gaussian kernel $k(x, x) = 1$, so $\trace\Kmat = m$ and the term is $B/\sqrt m$.
The feature space is infinite-dimensional, and the bound is the same as for unit-norm inputs in the plane. Using the
empirical complexity costs a slightly larger confidence term,
$3\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$ in place of $\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$ <Cite id="mohri2018" />.

### Choosing the margin after training

The theorem fixes $\gamma$ in advance. In practice $\gamma$ is read off the trained classifier, for example as the
smallest normalised margin of a hard-margin SVM. A <NoteLink to="union-bound" /> over a grid of margins makes the bound
hold for every $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ at once. With the inputs scaled so that $X = 1$, every normalised margin lies in
$[-1, 1]$, and

$$
R(h) \le \hat R_\gamma(h) + \frac{4}{\gamma\sqrt m} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta) + 2\ln\log_2(4/\gamma)}{2m}}.
$$

For inputs of norm at most $X$, replace $\gamma$ by $\gamma/X$ throughout.

The factor 4 in place of 2 and the $\ln\log_2$ term are the cost of choosing $\gamma$ from the data
<Cite id="mohri2018" />. The same argument over a grid of norms $B$ handles a classifier whose norm is read off after
training.

<Derivation title="The union bound over margins">

Take the grid $\gamma_k = 2^{-k}$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$ and give scale $\gamma_k$ the confidence
$\delta_k = \delta/(k(k + 1))$. These sum to $\delta$, because $\sum_k(\frac1k - \frac1{k+1}) = 1$. By the union bound,
with probability at least $1 - \delta$ the fixed-margin theorem holds at every $\gamma_k$ simultaneously.

Now take any $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ and let $k$ be the smallest index with $\gamma_k \le \gamma$. Then
$\gamma/2 < \gamma_k \le \gamma$.

1. **The margin loss.** A margin below $\gamma_k$ is a margin below $\gamma$, so $\hat R_{\gamma_k}(h) \le
   \hat R_\gamma(h)$.
2. **The capacity term.** $1/\gamma_k < 2/\gamma$, so $\frac{2}{\gamma_k}\Rad_m(\Hcal) < \frac{4}{\gamma}\Rad_m(\Hcal)$.
3. **The confidence term.** $k < \log_2(1/\gamma) + 1 = \log_2(2/\gamma)$, so $k(k + 1) < (k + 1)^2 <
   \log_2(4/\gamma)^2$. Hence $\ln(1/\delta_k) < \ln(1/\delta) + 2\ln\log_2(4/\gamma)$.

Substituting the three inequalities into the bound at $\gamma_k$, with $\Rad_m(\Hcal) \le 1/\sqrt m$ for unit-norm
$\wvec$ and $X = 1$, gives the displayed bound. A finer grid, $\gamma_k = r^{-k}$ with $r$ close to 1, brings the factor
4 down towards 2 at the cost of a larger logarithmic term.

</Derivation>

### The support vector machine

The soft-margin SVM minimises $\frac12\norm{\wvec}^2 + C\sum_i\max(0, 1 - y_i\wvec^\top\xvec_i)$. Take $\gamma = 1$ in
the unnormalised scale and $B = \norm{\wvec}$. The hinge loss lies above $\rho_1$, so the bound reads

$$
R(h) \le \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\max(0, 1 - y_i\wvec^\top\xvec_i) + \frac{2\norm{\wvec}X}{\sqrt m} +
\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2m}},
$$

with a union bound over $\norm{\wvec}$ for a norm chosen after training. The SVM objective has the same two
ingredients: an average hinge loss and a penalty on $\norm{\wvec}$. The constant $C$ sets the exchange rate between
them. This is the <NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural risk minimisation</NoteLink> reading of the
SVM <Cite id="shawetaylor1998" /> <Cite id="vapnik1998" />.

## Worked example

Take normalised margins on inputs of norm at most $X = 1$, $m = 10{,}000$ training points and $\delta = 0.05$.

**The capacity and confidence terms.** The table gives the terms of the bound beyond $\hat R_\gamma(h)$. For a margin
fixed in advance they are $2/(\gamma\sqrt m) + \sqrt{\ln 20/(2m)}$. For a margin read off after training they are
$4/(\gamma\sqrt m) + \sqrt{(\ln 20 + 2\ln\log_2(4/\gamma))/(2m)}$.

| Margin $\gamma$ | $X/\gamma$ | Fixed in advance | Chosen after training | $m$ for 0.1 (after training) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.05 | 20 | 0.412 | 0.818 | 669,584 |
| 0.1 | 10 | 0.212 | 0.418 | 174,560 |
| 0.2 | 5 | 0.112 | 0.217 | 47,180 |
| 0.5 | 2 | 0.052 | 0.096 | 9,238 |

The last column solves for the sample size at which the terms fall to 0.1. The capacity term dominates, so the sample
size scales as $(4X/(0.1\gamma))^2$: halving the margin quadruples it.

**Trading margin errors against capacity.** Suppose a linear classifier's training margins satisfy
$\hat R_{0.1} = 0.01$, $\hat R_{0.2} = 0.04$ and $\hat R_{0.5} = 0.30$. The bound with $\gamma$ chosen after training
is $0.01 + 0.418 = 0.428$ at $\gamma = 0.1$, $0.04 + 0.217 = 0.257$ at $\gamma = 0.2$, and $0.30 + 0.096 = 0.396$ at
$\gamma = 0.5$. The best scale is $\gamma = 0.2$, with a bound of 0.26. A test error of 0.03 would make the bound loose
by a factor of about 8, but not vacuous.

**Against the VC bound.** The VC route for half-spaces in $\reals^D$ has VC dimension $d = D + 1$ and, through
<NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's lemma</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">Sauer–Shelah lemma</NoteLink>, a gap of at most
$2\sqrt{2d\ln(em/d)/m} + \sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$. At $m = 10{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$ this is 0.27 for $D = 10$,
0.69 for $D = 100$, 1.64 for $D = 1{,}000$ and 2.84 for $D = 10{,}000$. The VC bound is vacuous from $D = 1{,}000$ on.
The margin bound at $\gamma = 0.2$ stays at 0.22 plus $\hat R_\gamma$ in every dimension, including the
infinite-dimensional feature space of a Gaussian kernel.

## Assumptions

- **Independent, identically distributed data,** as in every
  <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform-convergence</NoteLink> bound.
- **A bounded data radius.** The constant $X$ is the largest input norm, or $\sqrt{\trace\Kmat/m}$ in the empirical
  version. Without a bound on the inputs, a margin has no scale to be compared with.
- **Binary labels.** For $K$ classes the margin is $h_y(x) - \max_{j \ne y}h_j(x)$, and the contraction step needs a
  vector form, which adds a factor that grows with $K$ <Cite id="mohri2018" />.

## Pitfalls

- **An unnormalised margin means nothing.** Multiplying $\wvec$ by 10 multiplies every margin by 10 and leaves the
  classifier unchanged. A margin must be divided by $\norm{\wvec}$ (or the class norm $B$) and compared with $X$ before
  it enters a bound. Raw margins of different models cannot be compared.
- **Outliers inflate $X$.** One input of large norm raises the worst-case radius for everyone. The trace form
  $\sqrt{\trace\Kmat}/m$ averages the norms instead, and is the better choice for heavy-tailed inputs.
- **The minimum margin is fragile.** A bound with $\hat R_\gamma = 0$ uses the smallest training margin, which one
  mislabelled point can drive to zero. The bound holds for every $\gamma$; the best $\gamma$ usually lets a few points
  inside the margin.
- **A bias term changes the class.** The linear bound assumes $\wvec^\top\xvec$ without an offset. An offset $b$ is
  handled by appending a constant coordinate to $\xvec$, which increases $X$ and makes the normalisation include
  $b$.
- **The bound is about the class, not the algorithm.** It holds for every classifier with the stated norm and margins.
  It says nothing about why a training method finds large margins; that is the subject of
  <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink> and of the
  <NoteLink to="margin-theory-of-boosting">margin theory of boosting</NoteLink>.
