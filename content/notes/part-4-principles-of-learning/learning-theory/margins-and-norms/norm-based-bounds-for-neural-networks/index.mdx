<Definition>
A **norm-based bound** for a neural network $f_{\Wmat}$ with weight matrices $\Wmat_1, \dots, \Wmat_L$ bounds its risk
by a margin loss plus a capacity term of the form $C(\Wmat)\,X/(\gamma\sqrt m)$, up to logarithmic factors. Here
$C(\Wmat)$ is a function of the weight norms (a product of Frobenius or spectral norms, or a path norm), $X$ bounds the
input norm, $\gamma$ is the margin and $m$ the sample size <Cite id="bartlett1998" /> <Cite id="bartlett2017" />. The
number of parameters enters at most through logarithms. The **spectrally normalised margin bound** takes $C(\Wmat)$ to
be the product of the layers' spectral norms times a correction for their shapes <Cite id="bartlett2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A network with a million weights trained on fifty thousand examples has far more parameters than data. A bound that
counts parameters cannot say anything about it. Such networks still generalise on real data, while the same architecture
can also fit random labels perfectly <Cite id="zhang2017" />. Whatever controls generalisation must depend on the
particular weights the training found, not only on the architecture.

Bartlett's answer for two-layer networks was that the size of the weights matters more than their number
<Cite id="bartlett1998" />. The argument is the one behind <NoteLink to="margin-bounds">margin bounds</NoteLink> for
linear classifiers. A function with small weights changes slowly, so it cannot separate many points with a wide margin
in every way. The class of networks with bounded weight norms can be large in parameter count and small in
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink>.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, these bounds make the
move that exploits structure: concentration and <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink> are standard,
and the capacity is measured by norms that the trained network actually has.

## Counting parameters fails

A <Gloss name="relu" /> network with $W$ weights and $L$ layers has VC dimension $O(WL\ln W)$, and some architectures
reach $\Omega(WL\ln(W/L))$ <Cite id="bartlett2019vc" />. A <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
bound</NoteLink> is therefore vacuous whenever $W$ exceeds $m$, which is the regime of most modern networks.

The random-label experiment shows that the failure is not a weakness of the VC argument alone. Zhang et al. trained
standard image networks on <Gloss name="cifar-10" /> with the labels replaced by random ones, and the networks reached
zero training error <Cite id="zhang2017" />. A network class that fits random labels has empirical Rademacher complexity
near its maximum on that sample. Any bound that depends only on the class, the sample size and the confidence then has
to be vacuous for that class. A useful bound must look at the trained weights, for instance through a norm that is small
after training on real labels and large after training on random ones (see
<NoteLink to="measuring-capacity-empirically">measuring capacity empirically</NoteLink>).

## Norm-based capacity

**A two-layer network.** Let $f(\xvec) = \sum_{j=1}^h v_j\,\sigma(\uvec_j^\top\xvec)$ with $h$ hidden units, the
<NoteLink to="activation-functions">ReLU</NoteLink> $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$, output weights with
$\norm{\vvec}_1 \le B_1$, input weights with $\norm{\uvec_j}_2 \le B_2$ for every unit, and inputs with
$\norm{\xvec} \le X$. The empirical Rademacher complexity of this class $\Fcal$ satisfies

$$
\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \frac{2B_1B_2X}{\sqrt m},
$$

whatever the width $h$ and the input dimension. The derivation has three steps.

1. **A scaled convex hull.** Write $\Gcal = \set{\xvec \mapsto \sigma(\uvec^\top\xvec) : \norm{\uvec} \le B_2}$ for the
   hidden units. Dividing $f$ by $\norm{\vvec}_1$ gives a weighted average of members of $\Gcal$ and their negatives,
   with weights $\abs{v_j}/\norm{\vvec}_1$. So $\Fcal \subseteq \set{c\,g : 0 \le c \le B_1,\
   g \in \mathrm{conv}(\Gcal \cup -\Gcal)}$. The convex hull is symmetric about 0, so the supremum over $c$ is attained
   at $c = B_1$, and $\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le B_1\hat\Rad_S(\mathrm{conv}(\Gcal \cup -\Gcal))$.
2. **The convex hull leaves the supremum unchanged.** A linear function of $g$, such as $\sum_i\sigma_i g(\xvec_i)$,
   attains its supremum over a convex hull at one of its generators (the same step as in the
   <NoteLink to="margin-theory-of-boosting">margin theory of boosting</NoteLink>). The generators are $\pm g$ with
   $g \in \Gcal$, so the supremum is $\sup_{g \in \Gcal}\abs{\sum_i\sigma_i g(\xvec_i)}$.
3. **Contract through the ReLU.** The ReLU is 1-Lipschitz and $\sigma(0) = 0$. The Ledoux–Talagrand contraction
   inequality with absolute values then gives
   $\expect\sup_{\uvec}\abs{\sum_i\sigma_i\,\sigma(\uvec^\top\xvec_i)} \le 2\,\expect\sup_{\uvec}\abs{\sum_i\sigma_i\uvec^\top\xvec_i}$
   <Cite id="ledoux1991" /> (the factor 2 comes from the absolute value; see the <NoteLink to="contraction-lemma" />).
   The right side is $2B_2\,\expect\norm{\sum_i\sigma_i\xvec_i} \le
   2B_2\sqrt{\sum_i\norm{\xvec_i}^2} \le 2B_2X\sqrt m$ by Cauchy–Schwarz and Jensen's inequality.

Dividing by $m$ and multiplying the three steps together gives the bound.

**Deeper networks.** Applying the same three steps once per layer, from the output down, multiplies the bound by 2 and
by that layer's norm at every layer. The result is $2^L\prod_j B_j\,X/\sqrt m$ for a depth-$L$ network, which grows
exponentially with depth even when every norm is at most 1 <Cite id="neyshabur2015norm" />. Golowich, Rakhlin and Shamir
removed the exponential factor for Frobenius norms. If every layer has $\norm{\Wmat_j}_F \le M_j$, then

$$
\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \frac{X\paren{\sqrt{2L\ln 2} + 1}\prod_{j=1}^L M_j}{\sqrt m}
$$

<Cite id="golowich2018" />. The **path norm** is another choice: the sum over all input-to-output paths of the product
of the absolute weights along the path. It is unchanged by any rescaling of the weights that leaves the function
unchanged <Cite id="neyshabur2015norm" />.

## Spectrally normalised margin bounds

**The Lipschitz constant.** A network $f(\xvec) = \sigma_L(\Wmat_L\sigma_{L-1}(\cdots\sigma_1(\Wmat_1\xvec)))$ is a
composition of linear maps and activations. The linear map $\Wmat_i$ stretches a difference of inputs by at most its
spectral norm $\norm{\Wmat_i}_\sigma$, the largest singular value (see <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">matrix
norms</NoteLink>). A $\rho_i$-Lipschitz activation stretches it by at most $\rho_i$. Composing the layers,

$$
\norm{f(\xvec) - f(\xvec')} \le \paren{\prod_{i=1}^L\rho_i\norm{\Wmat_i}_\sigma}\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}.
$$

The product of spectral norms bounds how fast the outputs can change. Dividing the margin by it gives a margin that does
not change when the outputs are rescaled.

**The bound.** For $k$ classes, the margin of $f$ on $(\xvec, y)$ is $f(\xvec)_y - \max_{j \ne y}f(\xvec)_j$, and
$\hat R_\gamma(f)$ is the fraction of training points with margin at most $\gamma$. Bartlett, Foster and Telgarsky
proved that, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, every network and every $\gamma > 0$ satisfy

$$
R(f) \le \hat R_\gamma(f) + \tilde O\paren{\frac{\norm{\Xmat}_F\,R_{\Wmat}}{\gamma m}\ln W + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{m}}},
\qquad
R_{\Wmat} = \paren{\prod_{i=1}^L\rho_i\norm{\Wmat_i}_\sigma}\paren{\sum_{i=1}^L\frac{\norm{\Wmat_i^\top - \Mmat_i^\top}_{2,1}^{2/3}}{\norm{\Wmat_i}_\sigma^{2/3}}}^{3/2}
$$

<Cite id="bartlett2017" />. The symbols are:

- $\norm{\Xmat}_F = \sqrt{\sum_i\norm{\xvec_i}^2}$, so $\norm{\Xmat}_F/m \le X/\sqrt m$;
- $\Mmat_i$ is a reference matrix fixed in advance, such as zero or the initialisation;
- $\norm{\Amat}_{2,1}$ is the sum of the Euclidean norms of the columns of $\Amat$;
- $W$ is the largest layer width, which enters only through the logarithm, and $\tilde O$ hides further logarithmic
  factors.

The first factor of $R_{\Wmat}$ is the Lipschitz constant. The second corrects for the shape of each layer: it is about
$L^{3/2}$ times a typical ratio $(\norm{\Wmat_i^\top - \Mmat_i^\top}_{2,1}/\norm{\Wmat_i}_\sigma)$, which is small when
the weights stay close to the reference or have few large directions. The bound has no explicit dependence on the
number of classes.

The proof covers the class of networks with bounded spectral complexity $R_{\Wmat}$ layer by layer, then uses a
Rademacher margin bound. Neyshabur, Bhojanapalli and Srebro obtained a bound of the same flavour, with the product of
spectral norms times a sum of Frobenius-to-spectral ratios, from a <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes</NoteLink>
argument <Cite id="neyshabur2018" />.

**Rescaling.** A ReLU network computes the same function when one layer's weights are multiplied by $c > 0$ and
another's are divided by $c$. The product of norms does not change, so product-form bounds respect this symmetry. A sum
of norms does not, and a bound built from sums must first rebalance the layers.

## What the bounds predict

- **Random labels.** On CIFAR-10, the margins of AlexNet divided by its spectral complexity are much smaller with random
  labels than with true labels, although the unnormalised margins look alike. The Lipschitz constant tracks the
  <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">excess risk</NoteLink> during training <Cite id="bartlett2017" />.
- **Sample size.** Nagarajan and Kolter trained 5-layer networks of width 1,024 on <Gloss name="mnist" /> with batch
  size 1. The distance from initialisation grew like $m^{0.4}$ and the product of spectral norms grew with $m$. The
  norm-based bounds of Bartlett et al. and Neyshabur et al. then grew like $m^{0.68}$, while the test error fell like
  $m^{-0.43}$ <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />. A bound that grows with the sample size cannot explain why more data helps.
- **Hyperparameters.** A study of over 10,000 <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink>
  found that the product of spectral norms correlates strongly and negatively with generalisation across changes in
  hyperparameters. The path norm correlated positively, and sharpness-based measures did best <Cite id="jiang2020" />.

The bounds therefore get the direction right for some comparisons, such as true against random labels, and wrong for
others, such as depth, batch size and sample size.

## Worked example

**Two-layer network.** Take a network with $h = 1{,}000$ hidden ReLU units on inputs in $\reals^{784}$ with
$\norm{\xvec} \le X = 1$, so $W = 784 \times 1{,}000 + 1{,}000 = 785{,}000$ weights. Train it on $m = 60{,}000$
examples. Suppose it reaches $\hat R_\gamma = 0.02$ at a margin with $B_1B_2X/\gamma = 10$, meaning the margin is a
tenth of the largest output the norm constraints allow.

1. **The norm-based term.** The fixed-margin theorem of <NoteLink to="margin-bounds" /> with the two-layer complexity
   gives a capacity term $\frac{2}{\gamma} \times \frac{2B_1B_2X}{\sqrt m} = \frac{4 \times 10}{\sqrt{60{,}000}} =
   \frac{40}{244.9} = 0.163$.
2. **The confidence term.** At $\delta = 0.05$ it is $\sqrt{\ln 20/120{,}000} = 0.005$.
3. **The bound.** $R \le 0.02 + 0.163 + 0.005 = 0.19$. It is loose but not vacuous.
4. **The sample size for a gap of 0.05.** The capacity term falls to 0.045 at $m = (40/0.045)^2 \approx 790{,}000$.
5. **The parameter count.** With $W = 785{,}000$ weights and $m = 60{,}000$, any VC bound is vacuous.

The norm-based bound depends on the ratio $B_1B_2X/\gamma$. Doubling it doubles the capacity term and quadruples the
sample size needed. The width $h$ does not appear, so the same bound holds for $h = 10^6$ units with the same norms.

**A deep network.** Take $L = 10$ layers with every Frobenius norm $M_j = 2$ and $X = 1$. The Golowich bound's
product is $2^{10} = 1{,}024$, multiplied by $\sqrt{20\ln 2} + 1 = 4.72$. At $m = 60{,}000$ the Rademacher complexity
bound is $4{,}837/244.9 = 19.7$, far above 1. Norms that look modest per layer multiply into a vacuous bound with depth,
unless the margin $\gamma$ grows in proportion.

## Assumptions

- **Lipschitz activations.** The ReLU, leaky ReLU and max-pooling are 1-Lipschitz. The contraction steps need this.
- **Norm bounds known in advance.** As with every margin bound, norms read off the trained network need a union bound
  over a grid of norm values, which costs logarithmic terms.
- **Independent, identically distributed data,** as in every uniform-convergence bound.

## Pitfalls

- **Norm-based is not the same as small.** The capacity terms are typically above 1 for networks trained without
  explicit constraints. Their use is in comparisons, and even there the large-scale evidence is mixed
  <Cite id="jiang2020" />.
- **The bound depends on the reference matrix.** Measuring $\Wmat_i - \Mmat_i$ from the initialisation rather than from
  zero changes the bound by large factors, and neither choice is correct in general <Cite id="jiang2020" />.
- **Uniform convergence may be the limit.** Some networks trained by gradient descent generalise while every uniform
  convergence bound over the networks they could output is vacuous (see
  <NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">the limits of uniform convergence</NoteLink>). Norm-based bounds are
  <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink> bounds, so they inherit this limit.
- **Products hide depth.** A product of $L$ norms each slightly above 1 grows exponentially with $L$. A bound that looks
  depth-free in its statement can still grow quickly with depth in practice.
