<Definition>
Let $\Ccal = \bigcup_n \Ccal_n$ be a family of target classes, where $\Ccal_n$ holds functions on inputs of dimension
$n$ and each target $c$ has a representation of size $\mathrm{size}(c)$. A learner **efficiently PAC learns** $\Ccal$
using hypothesis class $\Hcal$ if, for every $n$, $c \in \Ccal_n$, distribution $\Dcal$ and $\epsilon, \delta \in
(0, 1)$, it runs in time polynomial in $n$, $\mathrm{size}(c)$, $1/\epsilon$ and $1/\delta$, and returns a hypothesis
in $\Hcal$ that can be evaluated in polynomial time and has $R(h) \le \epsilon$ with probability at least
$1 - \delta$. The learning is **proper** if $\Hcal = \Ccal$ and **improper** (representation-independent) otherwise
<Cite id="valiant1984" /> <Cite id="kearns1994book" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The statistical theory of <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC learning</NoteLink> counts
examples. A class of finite <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> needs few of them, and
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> succeeds
(<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-statistical-learning">fundamental theorem of statistical learning</NoteLink>).
That theory says nothing about how long the minimisation takes. Searching an exponentially large class for a
hypothesis that fits the sample can be infeasible even when a few hundred examples carry enough information.

Valiant's original definition required a polynomial-time learner <Cite id="valiant1984" />. Efficient PAC learning keeps
that requirement and separates two questions. The sample complexity asks how much data is enough. The computational
complexity asks whether a learner can use that data in reasonable time. The answers can differ sharply.

The output representation matters. A learner that must return a hypothesis from the target class may face an
NP-hard search, while a learner allowed a larger class may find a good hypothesis quickly. The larger class costs
examples, because its capacity is larger, but it can save exponential time.

## Definition

The definition has four parts.

- **Size parameters.** The input dimension $n$ (for example the number of Boolean variables) and $\mathrm{size}(c)$
  (for example the number of terms of a formula) measure the problem. A learner must be polynomial in both, so that
  harder targets may take longer but not exponentially longer.
- **Accuracy and confidence.** The running time is polynomial in $1/\epsilon$ and $1/\delta$. A polynomial in
  $\ln(1/\delta)$ is usually achievable, by repeating a learner and picking the best run on fresh examples.
- **Hypothesis evaluation.** The returned hypothesis must be evaluable in polynomial time. Without this condition a
  learner could return the training sample itself as an encoded "hypothesis" and push all the work to prediction time.
- **The sample size.** A polynomial-time learner reads at most polynomially many examples, so efficient learnability
  implies a polynomial sample complexity.

The **consistency problem** for $\Hcal$ asks, given a labelled sample, for some $h \in \Hcal$ with zero training error,
or a report that none exists. It links the computational and statistical questions in both directions.

1. **Consistency gives learning.** If $\ln\abs{\Hcal_n}$ is polynomial in $n$ and the consistency problem is solvable in
   polynomial time, then running the solver on $m = (\ln\abs{\Hcal_n} + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$ examples is an
   efficient realisable learner, by the <NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">realisable bound</NoteLink>.
2. **Proper learning gives consistency.** Suppose a proper learner exists. Given a sample of $m$ points labelled
   consistently with some $c \in \Ccal$, run the learner on the uniform distribution over those points with
   $\epsilon < 1/m$. A hypothesis with risk below $1/m$ on that distribution misclassifies none of the $m$ points. So
   the learner solves the consistency problem with probability at least $1 - \delta$: the problem is in RP, the class
   of problems solved by randomised polynomial-time algorithms with one-sided error <Cite id="pitt1988" />.

The second direction is the route to hardness results. If the consistency problem for $\Ccal$ is NP-hard, proper
efficient learning of $\Ccal$ is impossible unless $\mathrm{RP} = \mathrm{NP}$, which is believed false.

## Proper and improper learning

**Conjunctions are efficiently and properly learnable.** A conjunction of literals over $n$ Boolean variables is
consistent with a sample if it keeps every literal that all positive examples satisfy. The elimination algorithm starts
with the conjunction of all $2n$ literals and, for each positive example, deletes the literals that the example
falsifies. It never errs on a negative example, because its hypothesis always contains the target's literals and is
therefore at least as strict. It runs in time $O(nm)$. With $\abs{\Hcal} = 3^n + 1$, so that
$\ln\abs{\Hcal} \le n\ln 3 + \ln 2$, it needs $O((n + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon)$ examples <Cite id="valiant1984" />.

**3-term DNF is hard to learn properly.** A 3-term DNF formula is a disjunction $T_1 \vee T_2 \vee T_3$ of three
conjunctions. Pitt and Valiant reduced graph 3-colouring to its consistency problem, so 3-term DNF is not efficiently
properly learnable unless $\mathrm{RP} = \mathrm{NP}$ <Cite id="pitt1988" />.

**3-term DNF is easy to learn improperly.** Distributing the disjunction over the conjunctions gives

$$
T_1 \vee T_2 \vee T_3 = \bigwedge_{u \in T_1,\, v \in T_2,\, w \in T_3}(u \vee v \vee w),
$$

a 3-CNF formula: a conjunction of clauses with three literals each. There are at most $(2n)^3$ such clauses. Treat each
clause as a new Boolean variable, computed from the input in polynomial time. A 3-CNF formula is then a conjunction over
the new variables, and the elimination algorithm learns it in time polynomial in $n^3$ and $m$. The output is a 3-CNF
formula, not a 3-term DNF formula, but it has small risk, which is all PAC learning asks <Cite id="pitt1988" />
<Cite id="kearns1994book" />.

The separation is about representation, not about information. Both classes have VC dimension polynomial in $n$; the
NP-hardness comes from insisting that the answer be written as three terms.

## Hardness results

Representation hardness does not show that a class is hard to learn: an improper learner may still succeed. Hardness for
every representation needs a different assumption.

- **Cryptographic hardness.** Kearns and Valiant showed that efficiently learning polynomial-size Boolean formulae,
  deterministic finite automata or constant-depth threshold circuits, with any polynomially evaluable hypothesis class,
  would break RSA encryption, factor Blum integers and decide quadratic residuosity <Cite id="kearns1994" />. The
  proof places the decryption functions of a public-key cryptosystem inside the class. Anyone can generate labelled
  examples of them from the public key, so a learner for the class would decrypt.
- **DNF.** Whether polynomial-size DNF formulae are efficiently PAC learnable is open. The fastest known algorithm runs
  in time $2^{\tilde O(n^{1/3})}$ <Cite id="klivans2004" />.
- **Agnostic learning.** Kearns, Schapire and Sellie defined efficient agnostic learning
  <Cite id="kearns1994agnostic" />. In that setting the consistency problem becomes approximate error minimisation,
  which is NP-hard even for simple classes. For halfspaces, when some halfspace agrees with a fraction $1 - \epsilon$ of
  the examples, finding a halfspace that agrees with a fraction $\tfrac12 + \epsilon$ is NP-hard
  <Cite id="guruswami2009" />. Approximately maximising agreements is NP-hard for monomials and several other simple
  classes as well <Cite id="bendavid2003agreement" />. Minimising a convex <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">surrogate
  loss</NoteLink> in place of the 0–1 loss is the practical answer to this hardness.

## Worked example

**Elimination on four variables.** Let the target be $x_1 \wedge \neg x_3$ over $n = 4$ variables. The algorithm starts
from $x_1 \wedge \neg x_1 \wedge x_2 \wedge \neg x_2 \wedge x_3 \wedge \neg x_3 \wedge x_4 \wedge \neg x_4$.

1. The positive example $(1, 0, 0, 1)$ falsifies $\neg x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ and $\neg x_4$. They are deleted, leaving
   $x_1 \wedge \neg x_2 \wedge \neg x_3 \wedge x_4$.
2. The positive example $(1, 1, 0, 0)$ falsifies $\neg x_2$ and $x_4$, leaving $x_1 \wedge \neg x_3$.
3. The negative example $(0, 1, 1, 1)$ is skipped. The hypothesis already labels it negative.

Two positive examples recover the target exactly here. In general the hypothesis may keep extra literals that no
positive example has falsified yet, and the PAC bound limits how much risk they cause.

**Proper against improper, in examples.** Take $n = 10$ variables, $\epsilon = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.05$, and use the
realisable bound $m \ge (\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$.

| Learner | Hypothesis class | $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ | Examples | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proper | 3-term DNF, $\abs{\Hcal} \le 3^{3n}$ | $30\ln 3 = 33.0$ | 720 | NP-hard search |
| Improper | 3-CNF, $\abs{\Hcal} \le 2^{(2n)^3}$ | $8{,}000\ln 2 = 5{,}545$ | 110,964 | polynomial |

The improper learner needs 154 times as many examples. It trades data for computation: the proper learner's 720
examples suffice statistically, but finding a consistent 3-term DNF formula is an NP-hard search.

## Pitfalls

- **Proper hardness is not learning hardness.** An NP-hardness proof for the consistency problem of $\Ccal$ rules out
  only learners that output hypotheses in $\Ccal$. 3-term DNF is the standard example.
- **Worst-case hardness is about adversarial distributions.** The hardness results hold for some distributions and
  targets. Learning under a fixed distribution, such as the uniform distribution on the Boolean cube, can be easier.
- **Polynomial is not practical.** The improper 3-term DNF learner uses $(2n)^3$ features. For $n = 100$ that is
  8,000,000 features, polynomial in $n$ but heavy in practice.
- **The hypothesis must be evaluable.** Allowing any output representation without the polynomial-evaluation
  condition makes every class trivially "learnable" by storing the data.
