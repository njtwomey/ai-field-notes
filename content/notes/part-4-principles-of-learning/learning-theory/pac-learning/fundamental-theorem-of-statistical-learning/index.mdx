<Definition>
Let $\Hcal$ be a class of functions from a domain $\Xcal$ to $\set{0, 1}$, and let the loss be the
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink>. The **fundamental theorem of statistical learning** states that the
following are equivalent:

1. $\Hcal$ has the uniform convergence property;
2. every empirical risk minimiser is an agnostic PAC learner for $\Hcal$;
3. $\Hcal$ is agnostic PAC learnable;
4. $\Hcal$ is PAC learnable in the realisable case;
5. every empirical risk minimiser is a PAC learner for $\Hcal$ in the realisable case;
6. the VC dimension $d = \VCdim(\Hcal)$ is finite.

When $d$ is finite, the agnostic sample complexity is $\Theta((d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2)$ and the realisable one
lies between constant multiples of $(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$ and $(d\ln(1/\epsilon) + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$
<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" /> <Cite id="blumer1989" />.
</Definition>

## Statement

The theorem says that, for binary classification, one number decides learnability. A class whose VC dimension is
finite is learned by the simplest algorithm, minimising training error, at a rate fixed by $d$. A class whose VC
dimension is infinite cannot be learned by any algorithm, however clever, to a fixed accuracy from a fixed number of
examples on every distribution.

The six conditions mix three kinds of statement.

- **A property of the class and of random samples:** uniform convergence. For every $\epsilon, \delta$ there is an
  $m^{\mathrm{UC}}(\epsilon, \delta)$ such that, for every distribution $\Dcal$, a sample of that size has
  $\sup_{h \in \Hcal}\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \le \epsilon$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$
  (<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink>).
- **Properties of learning:** some algorithm, or every <Gloss name="erm" />, reaches accuracy $\epsilon$ with
  confidence $1 - \delta$ from finitely many examples, in the realisable or agnostic setting
  (<NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC learning</NoteLink>).
- **A combinatorial property:** the largest set that $\Hcal$ shatters is finite
  (<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>).

The theorem collects results of Vapnik and Chervonenkis, Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler and Warmuth, and Ehrenfeucht,
Haussler, Kearns and Valiant <Cite id="vapnik1971" /> <Cite id="blumer1989" /> <Cite id="ehrenfeucht1989" />. In the
<NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, it says that the third move,
making a bound uniform, is not only sufficient for learning but necessary.

## How the pieces connect

The proof goes round a cycle of implications: $1 \Rightarrow 2 \Rightarrow 3 \Rightarrow 4 \Rightarrow 6 \Rightarrow 1$,
with $2 \Rightarrow 5 \Rightarrow 4$ alongside.

**$1 \Rightarrow 2$: uniform convergence makes ERM succeed.** Suppose every $h$ has $\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \le
\epsilon/2$. Let $\hat h$ minimise the training error and $h^\dagger$ minimise the risk over $\Hcal$. Then

$$
R(\hat h) \le \hat R_S(\hat h) + \frac\epsilon2 \le \hat R_S(h^\dagger) + \frac\epsilon2 \le R(h^\dagger) + \epsilon.
$$

The first and last inequalities use uniform convergence; the middle one uses the definition of ERM. So every ERM is an
agnostic PAC learner with $m_\Hcal(\epsilon, \delta) \le m^{\mathrm{UC}}(\epsilon/2, \delta)$
(<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink>).

**$2 \Rightarrow 3$, $3 \Rightarrow 4$, $2 \Rightarrow 5$ and $5 \Rightarrow 4$ are immediate.** An ERM is a learner.
An agnostic learner is a realisable learner, because in the realisable case the best risk in the class is 0.

**$4 \Rightarrow 6$: infinite VC dimension defeats every learner.** Suppose $\VCdim(\Hcal) = \infty$ and a learner
receives $m$ examples. The argument has four steps, the same as in the
<NoteLink to="no-free-lunch-theorem">no-free-lunch theorem</NoteLink>.

1. **A hard distribution.** Take a set $C$ of $2m$ points shattered by $\Hcal$, and let $\Dcal$ be uniform on $C$
   with labels from a target $f$ drawn uniformly from all $2^{2m}$ labellings. Every labelling is in $\Hcal$, so the
   problem is realisable.
2. **Unseen points.** The sample contains at most $m$ distinct points, so at least half of $C$, carrying at least half
   the mass, is unseen. On each unseen point the learner is wrong with probability $1/2$ over the draw of $f$. Hence
   $\expect_{f, S}[R(\hat h)] \ge 1/4$, and some fixed target $f$ has $\expect_S[R(\hat h)] \ge 1/4$.
3. **From expectation to probability.** Because $R(\hat h) \le 1$,
   $\expect_S[R(\hat h)] \le 1/8 + \prob(R(\hat h) > 1/8)$, so $\prob(R(\hat h) > 1/8) \ge 1/8$.
4. **No sample size works.** The argument holds for every $m$, so no $m(\epsilon, \delta)$ achieves $\epsilon = 1/8$ and
   $\delta < 1/8$, and $\Hcal$ is not PAC learnable even in the realisable case.

**$6 \Rightarrow 1$: finite VC dimension gives uniform convergence.** This direction has three steps.

1. **Symmetrisation.** A large deviation $R(h) - \hat R_S(h)$ is replaced by a large difference between the errors on
   $S$ and on an independent ghost sample $S'$ of the same size
   (<NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink>).
2. **A finite projection.** On the $2m$ points of $S$ and $S'$ the class produces at most $\Pi_\Hcal(2m)$ labellings,
   where $\Pi_\Hcal$ is the growth function. A <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> over them and
   <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> give a deviation of order
   $\sqrt{\ln\Pi_\Hcal(2m)/m}$.
3. **Sauer–Shelah.** When $\VCdim(\Hcal) = d$, $\Pi_\Hcal(2m) \le (2em/d)^d$, so $\ln\Pi_\Hcal(2m)$ grows only like
   $d\ln m$, and the deviation goes to zero
   (<NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">growth function and Sauer–Shelah lemma</NoteLink>).

## The quantitative version

The cycle also fixes the rates. There are absolute constants $C_1$ and $C_2$ such that, for a class of VC dimension
$d < \infty$ <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />:

| Quantity | Lower bound | Upper bound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniform convergence $m^{\mathrm{UC}}$ | $C_1(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2$ | $C_2(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2$ |
| Agnostic sample complexity | $C_1(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2$ | $C_2(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2$ |
| Realisable sample complexity | $C_1(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$ | $C_2(d\ln(1/\epsilon) + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$ |

The lower bounds come from hard distributions
(<NoteLink to="sample-complexity-lower-bounds">sample-complexity lower bounds</NoteLink>). The realisable upper bound
holds for every ERM. Hanneke removed its $\ln(1/\epsilon)$ factor with a learner that takes a majority vote of ERM
outputs on overlapping subsamples, so the realisable sample complexity is $\Theta((d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon)$
<Cite id="hanneke2016" />.

## Worked example

Halfspaces in $\reals^n$ have VC dimension $d = n + 1$. Ask for every training error within $0.05$ of its risk with
confidence $0.95$, which makes ERM's excess risk at most $0.1$. The VC bound
$\sqrt{(d(\ln(2m/d) + 1) + \ln(4/\delta))/m}$ of the VC dimension note reaches $0.05$ at these sample sizes:

| Input dimension $n$ | $d$ | Sample size $m$ | $m/d$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 3 | 13,916 | 4,639 |
| 10 | 11 | 45,881 | 4,171 |
| 100 | 101 | 405,373 | 4,014 |
| 1,000 | 1,001 | 4,000,252 | 3,996 |

The sample size per unit of VC dimension settles near 4,000: the sample complexity is linear in $d$, as the theorem
says. The ratio falls slowly because the fixed $\ln(4/\delta)$ term shrinks relative to the term proportional to $d$ as
$d$ grows. By contrast, the class of classifiers $\sgn(\sin(\omega x))$ on the line has one parameter and infinite VC
dimension. No sample size appears in its row: the $4 \Rightarrow 6$ argument shows that some distribution defeats every
learner at every $m$.

## Where it stops holding

The equivalences are special to binary classification with the 0–1 loss.

- **Multiclass with many labels.** With finitely many labels the Natarajan dimension plays the role of $d$
  (<NoteLink to="natarajan-dimension">Natarajan dimension</NoteLink>). Different ERM learners can then have different
  sample complexities, and a class can be learnable by one ERM and not by another <Cite id="daniely2015" />.
- **Real-valued losses.** For bounded real-valued functions, finiteness of the fat-shattering dimension at every scale
  characterises uniform convergence and implies agnostic learnability for regression; the pseudo-dimension gives a
  stronger sufficient condition (<NoteLink to="pseudo-dimension-and-fat-shattering-dimension">pseudo-dimension and
  fat-shattering dimension</NoteLink>) <Cite id="alon1997" />.
- **General learning problems.** In Vapnik's general setting, with arbitrary losses, some problems are learnable while
  uniform convergence fails and ERM does not work. Stability of the learner, not uniform convergence, then
  characterises learnability <Cite id="shalevshwartz2010" /> (<NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic
  stability</NoteLink>).
- **Undecidable learnability.** In a related model, choosing from a class of functions the one with nearly the largest
  expectation, whether some classes are learnable is independent of the standard axioms of set theory. No finitary
  combinatorial parameter like the VC dimension characterises learnability in that model <Cite id="bendavid2019" />.
- **Computation.** The theorem counts examples, not time. A class of finite VC dimension can be infeasible to learn in
  polynomial time (<NoteLink to="efficient-pac-learning">efficient PAC learning</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Learnability is distribution-free.** An infinite VC dimension rules out a guarantee over all distributions. On a
  fixed distribution, or with a prior over hypotheses, such a class can still be learned, as the
  <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink> shows for countable classes.
- **The constants are not part of the theorem.** The equivalence says that the rate is $(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2$
  up to constants. The numbers in the worked example come from one particular upper bound and are far above what
  learning needs on most distributions.
- **Parameter count is not VC dimension.** The theorem is about $\VCdim(\Hcal)$. One parameter can give infinite VC
  dimension, and constrained classes with many parameters can have small VC dimension.
