<Definition>
Let $\Hcal$ be a class of classifiers, $\Dcal$ a distribution over labelled examples and $R(h)$ the 0–1 risk. The
**realisable** setting assumes that some $h^* \in \Hcal$ has $R(h^*) = 0$. A learner is **consistent** if it returns a
hypothesis with zero training error. For a finite class, every consistent learner reaches $R(\hat h) \le \epsilon$ with
probability at least $1 - \delta$ once $m \ge (\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$. The **agnostic** setting
makes no assumption on $\Dcal$ and asks for $R(\hat h) \le \min_{h \in \Hcal}R(h) + \epsilon$. Empirical risk
minimisation achieves it once $m \ge 2(\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(2/\delta))/\epsilon^2$ <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />
<Cite id="haussler1992" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A hypothesis with risk $\epsilon$ makes a mistake on a fraction $\epsilon$ of examples. In the realisable setting a
learner only has to rule out every hypothesis with risk above $\epsilon$. One mistake in the sample is enough to rule a
hypothesis out, and a hypothesis with risk $\epsilon$ shows its first mistake after about $1/\epsilon$ examples. The
sample size therefore grows as $1/\epsilon$.

In the agnostic setting every hypothesis may make mistakes, including the best one. Ruling a hypothesis out means
showing that its <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink> is higher than the best one's by
$\epsilon$. Telling two error rates apart by $\epsilon$ is an estimation problem, and estimating a rate to within
$\epsilon$ takes about $1/\epsilon^2$ examples, because the standard deviation of an average of $m$ losses shrinks as
$1/\sqrt m$.

Both bounds follow the first three moves of the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a
generalisation bound</NoteLink>. The excess risk is decomposed, a single hypothesis is controlled by a concentration
inequality, and a <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> makes the control uniform over the finite class.
The two settings differ only in the concentration step.

Valiant's original definition of <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC learning</NoteLink> was
realisable <Cite id="valiant1984" />. Haussler and Kearns, Schapire and Sellie introduced the agnostic version, which
is the one practice needs <Cite id="haussler1992" /> <Cite id="kearns1994agnostic" />.

## The realisable bound

Call a hypothesis **bad** if $R(h) > \epsilon$. The learner fails exactly when it returns a bad hypothesis. A
consistent learner returns a hypothesis with zero training error, so it can fail only if some bad hypothesis has zero
training error. The derivation bounds the probability of that event in three steps.

1. **One bad hypothesis.** Fix a bad $h$. On one random example it is correct with probability
   $1 - R(h) < 1 - \epsilon$. The $m$ examples are independent, so

$$
\prob\paren{\hat R_S(h) = 0} < (1 - \epsilon)^m \le e^{-\epsilon m},
$$

   using $1 - \epsilon \le e^{-\epsilon}$.

2. **All bad hypotheses.** At most $\abs{\Hcal}$ hypotheses are bad. The union bound adds their probabilities:

$$
\prob\paren{\exists h \in \Hcal: R(h) > \epsilon \text{ and } \hat R_S(h) = 0} \le \abs{\Hcal}\,e^{-\epsilon m}.
$$

3. **Inversion.** The right side is at most $\delta$ when $m \ge (\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon$.
   Equivalently, at a fixed $m$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ every hypothesis with zero training error has

$$
R(h) \le \frac{\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(1/\delta)}{m}.
$$

The argument never uses the realisability assumption. The assumption guarantees only that a consistent hypothesis
exists, so that a consistent learner can return one. Whenever a learner observes zero training error, the bound holds
for its output, whether or not the problem is realisable.

## The agnostic bound

In the agnostic setting the best hypothesis $h^\dagger = \argmin_{h \in \Hcal}R(h)$ may have positive risk, and no
hypothesis need be consistent. Empirical risk minimisation (<Gloss name="erm" />) returns
$\hat h = \argmin_{h \in \Hcal}\hat R_S(h)$.

1. **One hypothesis.** For a fixed $h$, the losses $\indicator[h(x_i) \ne y_i]$ are independent, lie in $[0, 1]$ and
   have mean $R(h)$. <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> gives
   $\prob(\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} > t) \le 2e^{-2mt^2}$.
2. **All hypotheses.** The union bound over $\Hcal$ gives
   $\prob(\exists h: \abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} > t) \le 2\abs{\Hcal}e^{-2mt^2}$. Setting this to $\delta$, with
   probability at least $1 - \delta$ every $h \in \Hcal$ satisfies

$$
\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \le t_m = \sqrt{\frac{\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(2/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

3. **Decomposition.** On that event,

$$
R(\hat h) \le \hat R_S(\hat h) + t_m \le \hat R_S(h^\dagger) + t_m \le R(h^\dagger) + 2t_m.
$$

   The first and third inequalities are the <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform deviation</NoteLink>; the middle
   one holds because $\hat h$ minimises the empirical risk. Requiring $2t_m \le \epsilon$ gives
   $m \ge 2(\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(2/\delta))/\epsilon^2$.

The same event also bounds the risk of the returned hypothesis from its training error: $R(\hat h) \le \hat R_S(\hat h)
+ t_m$. A one-sided version, which needs only the upper deviations, replaces $\ln(2/\delta)$ by $\ln(1/\delta)$.

## Why the rates differ

The rates differ because of the variance of the losses, not because of the union bound. The
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> of a hypothesis with risk $p$ is a Bernoulli variable with variance
$p(1 - p)$. Hoeffding's inequality ignores the variance and bounds the deviation as if $p = 1/2$. The realisable
argument works only at the bad hypotheses with risk near $\epsilon$, and their losses have variance about $\epsilon$. An
average of $m$ such losses has standard deviation about $\sqrt{\epsilon/m}$, which is below $\epsilon$ once
$m \gtrsim 1/\epsilon$.

<NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's inequality</NoteLink> uses the variance and interpolates between the
two settings. Write $R^\dagger = R(h^\dagger)$ for the smallest risk in the class and
$L = \ln(2\abs{\Hcal}/\delta)$. With probability at least $1 - \delta$, ERM satisfies

$$
R(\hat h) \le R^\dagger + 2\sqrt{\frac{2R^\dagger L}{m}} + \frac{5L}{m}.
$$

When $R^\dagger = 0$ the excess risk is of order $L/m$, the realisable rate. When $R^\dagger$ is a constant it is of
order $\sqrt{L/m}$, the agnostic rate. Bounds of this form are called **optimistic rates**. They show that the
$1/\epsilon$ rate needs only a small best-in-class risk, not exact realisability. Lower noise near the decision
boundary gives intermediate rates (<NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">fast rates and noise
conditions</NoteLink>).

<Derivation title="The optimistic rate from Bernstein's inequality">
**Concentration.** For a fixed $h$ with risk $p = R(h)$, the losses lie in $[0, 1]$ with variance
$p(1 - p) \le p$. One-sided Bernstein inequalities in each direction, at confidence $\delta/(2\abs{\Hcal})$ each, and a
union bound over $\Hcal$ give, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, for every $h \in \Hcal$,

$$
\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \le \sqrt{\frac{2R(h)L}{m}} + \frac{2L}{3m}.
$$

**Chaining the inequalities.** On this event, as in the agnostic bound,

$$
R(\hat h) \le \hat R_S(\hat h) + \sqrt{\frac{2R(\hat h)L}{m}} + \frac{2L}{3m}
\le \hat R_S(h^\dagger) + \sqrt{\frac{2R(\hat h)L}{m}} + \frac{2L}{3m}
\le B + \sqrt{\frac{2R(\hat h)L}{m}},
$$

where $B = R^\dagger + \sqrt{2R^\dagger L/m} + 4L/(3m)$.

**Solving for $R(\hat h)$.** Write $x = \sqrt{R(\hat h)}$ and $a = \sqrt{2L/m}$. The inequality reads
$x^2 \le B + ax$. Its positive root gives $x \le (a + \sqrt{a^2 + 4B})/2 \le a + \sqrt B$, and so
$x^2 \le B + ax \le B + a^2 + a\sqrt B$.

**Simplifying.** Because $\sqrt{2R^\dagger L/m} \le 2\sqrt{R^\dagger}\sqrt{4L/(3m)}$, $B$ is at most
$(\sqrt{R^\dagger} + \sqrt{4L/(3m)})^2$, and $\sqrt B \le \sqrt{R^\dagger} + \sqrt{4L/(3m)}$. Substituting,

$$
R(\hat h) \le R^\dagger + \sqrt{\frac{2R^\dagger L}{m}} + \frac{4L}{3m} + \frac{2L}{m} + \sqrt{\frac{2R^\dagger L}{m}} +
\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}\,\frac{L}{m},
$$

and $4/3 + 2 + \sqrt{8/3} = 4.97 \le 5$.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Take decision stumps on 50 numerical features: each hypothesis picks a feature, one of 1,000 candidate thresholds and a
sign. The class has $\abs{\Hcal} = 50 \times 1{,}000 \times 2 = 100{,}000$ hypotheses and
$\ln\abs{\Hcal} = 11.51$. Set $\epsilon = 0.02$ and $\delta = 0.05$.

1. **Realisable.** $m \ge (11.51 + \ln 20)/0.02 = (11.51 + 3.00)/0.02 = 726$ examples.
2. **Agnostic, ERM.** $m \ge 2(11.51 + \ln 40)/0.02^2 = 2 \times 15.20/0.0004 = 76{,}009$ examples, 105 times as many.
3. **Small best-in-class risk.** With $R^\dagger = 0.05$ and $L = \ln(2 \times 100{,}000/0.05) = 15.20$, the optimistic
   rate reaches excess risk $0.02$ at $m = 22{,}163$. Its constants are worse than the realisable bound's: at
   $R^\dagger = 0$ it asks for $3{,}803$ examples instead of 726.

The bounds also certify a trained stump. With $m = 5{,}000$ examples and a training error of $0.06$, the one-sided
agnostic bound gives $R(\hat h) \le 0.06 + \sqrt{(11.51 + 3.00)/10{,}000} = 0.06 + 0.038 = 0.098$. A stump with zero
training error on the same sample has $R(\hat h) \le 14.51/5{,}000 = 0.0029$. Neither bound is vacuous, because
$\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ is small compared with $m$. A held-out set of the same size would give a tighter certificate,
because a <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bound</NoteLink> has no $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ term.

## Assumptions

- **A finite class** fixed before the data are seen. Infinite classes replace $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ by a capacity measure
  such as the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>; countable classes with a prior use
  the <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink>.
- **Independent, identically distributed examples** and the 0–1 loss.
- **Realisability** in the first setting. It is a joint property of the class and the distribution: the labels must be
  a deterministic function that belongs to $\Hcal$.

## Pitfalls

- **Label noise breaks realisability.** When the label of any input is random with a probability bounded away from 0
  and 1, every hypothesis has positive risk, and only the agnostic bound applies.
- **Zero training error is evidence only for a small class.** The realisable bound $R \le (\ln\abs{\Hcal} +
  \ln(1/\delta))/m$ is small only when $\ln\abs{\Hcal} \ll m$. A large class can fit any labels, including noise, and
  then its bound exceeds 1.
- **The comparison point differs.** The realisable bound controls $R(\hat h)$; the agnostic bound controls
  $R(\hat h) - \min_{h \in \Hcal}R(h)$. A small agnostic excess risk says nothing about the approximation error
  $\min_{h \in \Hcal}R(h)$ itself.
- **The constants are not the rates.** Bounds with better rates can have worse constants, as the optimistic rate shows
  at $R^\dagger = 0$.
