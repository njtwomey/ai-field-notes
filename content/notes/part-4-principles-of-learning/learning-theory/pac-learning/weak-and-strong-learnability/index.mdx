<Definition>
In the realisable setting, a learner is a **$\gamma$-weak learner** for a class $\Ccal$ if, for every target
$c \in \Ccal$, every distribution $\Dcal$ over inputs and every $\delta \in (0, 1)$, it returns from enough examples a
hypothesis $h$ with $R(h) \le \tfrac12 - \gamma$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$. The class is **weakly
learnable** if a $\gamma$-weak learner exists for some fixed $\gamma > 0$, and **strongly learnable** if it is PAC
learnable, with every accuracy $\epsilon > 0$ attainable. Kearns and Valiant asked whether the two notions coincide
<Cite id="kearns1994" />. Schapire proved that they do: a class is weakly learnable if and only if it is strongly
learnable, and the proof is an algorithm, **boosting** <Cite id="schapire1990" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A weak learner is only slightly better than random guessing. It may be wrong on 45% of the examples, as long as it is
right more often than not on whatever distribution it is given. A strong learner must reach any accuracy asked of it.
The two seem far apart, but a weak learner that works on every distribution can be pointed at the examples it currently
gets wrong. Reweighting the data towards past mistakes forces each new weak hypothesis to be informative where the
previous ones were not. A vote of these hypotheses corrects most of their individual mistakes.

The phrase "every distribution" carries the argument. A rule of thumb that is slightly better than chance on the
natural data distribution, but not on reweighted versions of it, cannot be boosted. The equivalence also needs the
edge $\gamma$ to be fixed: an edge that may shrink as the distribution changes gives no guarantee.

## Definitions

Let $\Ccal$ be a class of target functions $\Xcal \to \set{-1, +1}$ and let $\Bcal$ be the class of hypotheses that the
weak learner returns, the **base class**.

- **Strong learnability** is realisable <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC
  learnability</NoteLink>: for every $\epsilon, \delta \in (0, 1)$, every $c \in \Ccal$ and every $\Dcal$, the learner
  returns $h$ with $R(h) \le \epsilon$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$, from
  $m(\epsilon, \delta)$ examples.
- **Weak learnability** fixes $\epsilon = \tfrac12 - \gamma$. The weak learner's edge $\gamma$ is the amount by which
  it beats random guessing.
- **The weak learning assumption on a sample.** Boosting applies the weak learner to weighted versions of one training
  sample. The assumption it uses is: for every weighting $D$ of the training examples, the weak learner returns
  $h \in \Bcal$ with weighted error $\sum_i D(i)\indicator[h(x_i) \ne y_i] \le \tfrac12 - \gamma$.

The definitions say nothing about computation. Schapire's theorem holds in both the statistical and the
polynomial-time versions: if the weak learner runs in polynomial time, so does the strong learner it is turned into
<Cite id="schapire1990" />.

## The boosting theorem

Schapire's original construction was recursive. It trains three weak hypotheses on filtered distributions and takes
their majority, which turns error $\alpha$ into at most $g(\alpha) = 3\alpha^2 - 2\alpha^3$, and it repeats this on the
majority <Cite id="schapire1990" />. Freund's boost-by-majority and Freund and Schapire's AdaBoost replaced the
recursion with a single weighted vote <Cite id="freund1995" /> <Cite id="freund1997" />. The proof below is AdaBoost's
training-error bound.

**The algorithm.** Given $m$ examples with labels $y_i \in \set{-1, +1}$, start from uniform weights
$D_1(i) = 1/m$. In round $t = 1, \dots, T$:

1. the weak learner returns $h_t$ with weighted error $\epsilon_t = \sum_i D_t(i)\indicator[h_t(x_i) \ne y_i] =
   \tfrac12 - \gamma_t$;
2. the vote weight is $\alpha_t = \tfrac12\ln((1 - \epsilon_t)/\epsilon_t)$;
3. the weights become $D_{t+1}(i) = D_t(i)\exp(-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i))/Z_t$, where $Z_t$ makes them sum to 1.

The output is $H(x) = \sgn(f(x))$ with $f = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t h_t$ (<NoteLink to="adaboost">AdaBoost</NoteLink>).

**The bound.** The training error of $H$ satisfies

$$
\hat R_S(H) \le \prod_{t=1}^{T}2\sqrt{\epsilon_t(1 - \epsilon_t)} = \prod_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{1 - 4\gamma_t^2} \le
\exp\paren{-2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma_t^2}.
$$

The derivation has four steps.

1. **Unroll the weights.** Applying the update $T$ times gives
   $D_{T+1}(i) = \exp(-y_i f(x_i))/(m\prod_t Z_t)$, because the exponents add up to $-y_i\sum_t\alpha_t h_t(x_i)$.
2. **Bound the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> by the exponential loss.** $H$ errs on example $i$
   only if $y_i f(x_i) \le 0$, and then $\exp(-y_i f(x_i)) \ge 1$. Hence

$$
\hat R_S(H) \le \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\exp(-y_i f(x_i)) = \sum_{i=1}^{m}D_{T+1}(i)\prod_{t=1}^{T}Z_t = \prod_{t=1}^{T}Z_t,
$$

   since the weights $D_{T+1}$ sum to 1.
3. **Compute each normaliser.** The correctly classified examples carry weight $1 - \epsilon_t$ and are multiplied by
   $e^{-\alpha_t}$; the others carry $\epsilon_t$ and are multiplied by $e^{\alpha_t}$. So
   $Z_t = (1 - \epsilon_t)e^{-\alpha_t} + \epsilon_t e^{\alpha_t}$. The chosen $\alpha_t$ minimises this, and
   substituting it gives $Z_t = 2\sqrt{\epsilon_t(1 - \epsilon_t)} = \sqrt{1 - 4\gamma_t^2}$.
4. **Use $1 + u \le e^u$.** With $u = -4\gamma_t^2$, $\sqrt{1 - 4\gamma_t^2} \le \exp(-2\gamma_t^2)$, and the product
   of these is the exponential of the sum.

Under the weak learning assumption every $\gamma_t \ge \gamma$, so $\hat R_S(H) \le e^{-2\gamma^2 T}$. The training
error is a multiple of $1/m$, so it is exactly zero once $e^{-2\gamma^2T} < 1/m$, that is after

$$
T > \frac{\ln m}{2\gamma^2}
$$

rounds <Cite id="freund1997" />.

**From the training sample to the distribution.** A consistent hypothesis is not yet a strong learner: its
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">true risk</NoteLink> must also be small. The vote $H$ belongs to the class of
signs of weighted sums of $T$ base hypotheses. If the base class has VC dimension $d_\Bcal$, that class has VC dimension
of order $Td_\Bcal\ln(Td_\Bcal)$ <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. With $T$ of order $\ln m/\gamma^2$, the capacity grows
only logarithmically in $m$, and the realisable <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC bound</NoteLink> makes
$R(H)$ as small as required for large enough $m$. Weak learnability therefore implies strong learnability. The converse
is immediate, because a strong learner run with $\epsilon = \tfrac12 - \gamma$ is a weak learner.

The bound is also an instance of the <NoteLink to="hedge-and-exponential-weights">exponential weights</NoteLink>
algorithm run on the training examples. Freund and Schapire derived AdaBoost from Hedge, and the weak learning
assumption is equivalent, by the minimax theorem, to the target being a <NoteLink to="weighted-majority">weighted
majority</NoteLink> of base hypotheses with margin at least $2\gamma$ <Cite id="schapire2012" />.

## Worked example

Take a weak learner with edge $\gamma = 0.1$ (weighted error at most 0.4) and $m = 1{,}000$ training examples.

1. **Zero training error.** $T > \ln 1{,}000/(2 \times 0.1^2) = 6.91/0.02 = 345.4$, so 346 rounds suffice.
2. **The bound after 100 rounds.** If every round has $\epsilon_t = 0.4$, the product bound is
   $(2\sqrt{0.4 \times 0.6})^{100} = 0.96^{50} = 0.130$. The looser exponential form gives $e^{-2 \times 0.01 \times 100}
   = e^{-2} = 0.135$.
3. **A smaller edge.** With $\gamma = 0.05$, zero training error needs $T > 6.91/0.005 = 1{,}382$ rounds. Halving
   the edge quadruples the rounds.
4. **Schapire's recursion.** Starting from error $0.4$, the map $\alpha \mapsto 3\alpha^2 - 2\alpha^3$ gives $0.352$,
   $0.285$, $0.197$, $0.101$ and $0.029$ after five levels. Level $k$ calls the weak learner $3^k$ times, so five levels
   use 243 calls for an error of 2.9%.

## Pitfalls

- **Weak on every distribution is a strong requirement.** A learner whose edge vanishes on some reweighting breaks the
  guarantee. In practice the edges $\gamma_t$ of later rounds shrink, as the simulation shows, and the bound
  $e^{-2\sum_t\gamma_t^2}$ slows down accordingly.
- **The theorem is about the realisable setting.** With <NoteLink to="label-noise">label noise</NoteLink> no hypothesis
  is consistent, the weak learning assumption cannot hold on every reweighting, and the training-error bound stops
  describing the true risk. Long and Servedio showed that <NoteLink to="label-noise-models">random classification
  noise</NoteLink> defeats every boosting algorithm that minimises a convex potential, AdaBoost included
  <Cite id="long2010" />.
- **Zero training error is not the end of training.** The VC argument predicts that more rounds raise the capacity and
  eventually hurt. AdaBoost's test error often keeps falling after the training error reaches zero, which the
  <NoteLink to="margin-theory-of-boosting">margin theory of boosting</NoteLink> explains.
- **Multiclass needs a stronger weak learner.** Beating random guessing among $K > 2$ classes ($1 - 1/K$ error) is not
  enough to boost; the multiclass analogue needs error below $\tfrac12$ or a modified assumption
  <Cite id="schapire2012" />.
