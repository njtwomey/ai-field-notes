<Definition>
In nonparametric regression, $m$ pairs are drawn with $\xvec_i \in [0, 1]^d$ and $y_i = f(\xvec_i) + \varepsilon_i$,
where the noise $\varepsilon_i$ has mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$, and $f$ is known only to lie in a smoothness
class. For the **Hölder class** $\Sigma(s, L)$ of functions with smoothness $s > 0$ and constant $L$, the minimax
<Gloss name="mse">mean squared error</Gloss> is of order

$$
\inf_{\hat f}\ \sup_{f \in \Sigma(s, L)}\ \expect\norm{\hat f - f}_{L_2}^2 \asymp m^{-2s/(2s + d)},
$$

where the infimum runs over all estimators <Cite id="stone1982" />. Local averaging with a well-chosen bandwidth
attains the rate, and no estimator beats it on every function of the class.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A nonparametric estimate of $f(\xvec)$ averages the labels of training points near $\xvec$. The size $h$ of the
neighbourhood sets two errors that move in opposite directions.

- **Bias.** Points within distance $h$ have $f$ values that differ from $f(\xvec)$ by up to $Lh^s$ when $f$ has
  smoothness $s \le 1$. The squared bias is of order $L^2h^{2s}$.
- **Variance.** A neighbourhood of side $h$ in $d$ dimensions holds about $mh^d$ points. Their average label has noise
  variance $\sigma^2/(mh^d)$.

Shrinking $h$ cuts the bias and raises the variance. Balancing $h^{2s}$ against $1/(mh^d)$ gives
$h \asymp m^{-1/(2s + d)}$ and an error of order $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$. The dimension enters only through the variance:
a neighbourhood of fixed side holds a fraction $h^d$ of the data, which vanishes quickly as $d$ grows. This is the
<NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours">curse of dimensionality</NoteLink> as a rate.

The rate is also a floor. A <NoteLink to="minimax-lower-bounds">minimax lower bound</NoteLink> builds many functions in
the class that differ only in small bumps, each bump too small for the data to detect. No estimator can tell which
bumps are present, and the bumps it misses cost exactly $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$.

## Smoothness classes

For $0 < s \le 1$, the **Hölder class** $\Sigma(s, L)$ on $[0, 1]^d$ holds the functions with

$$
\abs{f(\xvec) - f(\xvec')} \le L\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}^s \quad\text{for all } \xvec, \xvec'.
$$

$s = 1$ is the Lipschitz class. For $s > 1$, write $s = k + \beta$ with an integer $k$ and $0 < \beta \le 1$; then
$\Sigma(s, L)$ requires every partial derivative of order $k$ to be in $\Sigma(\beta, L)$ <Cite id="tsybakov2009" />.
Larger $s$ means a smoother function, which data at a distance can predict more accurately. Sobolev classes, which
bound the $L_2$ norm of derivatives instead of their pointwise variation, give the same rates.

The rate depends on the error measure. For the squared $L_2$ norm it is $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$; for the sup norm it is
$(m/\ln m)^{-s/(2s + d)}$, slower by a logarithm <Cite id="stone1982" />.

## The upper bound

The **partitioning estimator** is the simplest local average. Divide $[0, 1]^d$ into $h^{-d}$ cubes of side $h$, and
predict at $\xvec$ the mean label of the training points in the cube $A(\xvec)$ that contains it, or 0 if the cube is
empty. Take $\xvec$ uniform on $[0, 1]^d$, $f \in \Sigma(s, L)$ with $s \le 1$, and $\abs{f} \le F$. The derivation
splits the error at $\xvec$ into three parts <Cite id="gyorfi2002" />.

1. **Bias.** Given that the cube is not empty, the estimate's mean is the average of $f$ over the training points in
   it. Each lies within the cube's diameter $\sqrt d\,h$ of $\xvec$, so the bias is at most $L(\sqrt d\,h)^s$ and the
   squared bias at most $L^2d^sh^{2s}$.
2. **Variance.** Given $N$ points in the cube, the estimate averages $N$ independent noises and has variance
   $\sigma^2/N$. $N$ is binomial with $m$ trials and success probability $h^d$, and
   $\expect[\indicator[N > 0]/N] \le 2/(mh^d)$, so the variance term is at most $2\sigma^2/(mh^d)$.
3. **Empty cubes.** A cube is empty with probability $(1 - h^d)^m \le e^{-mh^d} \le 1/(emh^d)$, and an empty cube
   costs at most $F^2$.

Adding the three parts,

$$
\expect\norm{\hat f - f}^2 \le L^2d^s h^{2s} + \frac{2\sigma^2 + F^2/e}{mh^d}.
$$

The first term increases with $h$ and the second decreases. Setting the
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> to zero gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
h^* &= \paren{\frac{d\,C}{2s\,A\,m}}^{1/(2s + d)}, \\
\expect\norm{\hat f - f}^2 &\le \paren{1 + \frac{2s}{d}}A^{d/(2s + d)}\paren{\frac{d\,C}{2s\,m}}^{2s/(2s + d)},
\end{aligned}
$$

with $A = L^2d^s$ and $C = 2\sigma^2 + F^2/e$. The rate is $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$.

**Beyond $s = 1$.** A local average fits a constant in each neighbourhood. However smooth $f$ is, its bias falls no
faster than $h^2$ in the interior of the domain, and only like $h$ near the boundary. To exploit larger $s$, fit a
polynomial of degree $\lfloor s\rfloor$ in each neighbourhood (<NoteLink to="local-regression">local polynomial
regression</NoteLink>). Its bias is of order $h^s$, and the same balance gives the rate $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$
<Cite id="tsybakov2009" />. <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">Kernel ridge regression</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="smoothing-splines">smoothing splines</NoteLink> reach the same rate through a penalty instead of a
bandwidth; the <NoteLink to="local-rademacher-complexity">local Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> of a kernel class with
eigenvalues $j^{-2s}$ gives $m^{-2s/(2s + 1)}$ in one dimension <Cite id="wainwright2019" />.

## The lower bound

The lower bound uses <NoteLink to="minimax-lower-bounds">Assouad's lemma</NoteLink> on a hypercube of bumpy functions
<Cite id="tsybakov2009" />. Take $s \le 1$ for concreteness.

1. **Bumps.** Divide $[0, 1]^d$ into $K = h^{-d}$ cubes of side $h$ with centres $\mathbf c_j$. Let $\phi$ be a bump
   supported on the unit cube centred at 0, vanishing on its boundary, with $\phi \in \Sigma(s, 1)$. For
   $\omega \in \set{0, 1}^K$ let

   $$
   f_\omega(\xvec) = \sum_{j=1}^K \omega_j\,Lh^s\,\phi\paren{\frac{\xvec - \mathbf c_j}{h}}.
   $$

   Each $f_\omega$ is in $\Sigma(s, L)$: the bumps have disjoint supports, vanish where they meet, and scaling by $h$
   in the argument and $h^s$ in height keeps the Hölder constant at $L$.
2. **Separation.** Switching bump $j$ changes $f$ only on cube $j$, by $L^2h^{2s}h^d\norm{\phi}_2^2$ in squared
   $L_2$ norm. For any estimate $\hat f$, let $\hat\omega_j$ indicate which of the two versions of cube $j$ is closer
   to $\hat f$. If $\hat\omega_j \ne \omega_j$, the triangle inequality gives
   $\int_{\text{cube } j}(\hat f - f_\omega)^2 \ge \frac14L^2h^{2s + d}\norm{\phi}_2^2$. Summing over cubes,
   $\norm{\hat f - f_\omega}^2 \ge \frac14L^2h^{2s + d}\norm{\phi}_2^2\,\rho_H(\hat\omega, \omega)$.
3. **Closeness.** Neighbouring $\omega$ and $\omega'$ differ in one bump. With Gaussian noise, the sample's
   <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink> is
   $m\,\expect[(f_\omega - f_{\omega'})^2]/(2\sigma^2) = mL^2h^{2s + d}\norm{\phi}_2^2/(2\sigma^2)$. Choosing
   $h^{2s + d} = \sigma^2/(mL^2\norm{\phi}_2^2)$ makes it $\frac12$, and Pinsker's inequality gives total variation at
   most $\frac12$.
4. **Assouad.** The expected Hamming distance is at least $\frac K2(1 - \frac12) = K/4$ for some $\omega$. Hence

   $$
   \sup_\omega\expect\norm{\hat f - f_\omega}^2 \ge \frac14L^2h^{2s + d}\norm{\phi}_2^2 \cdot \frac{h^{-d}}{4}
   = \frac{L^2\norm{\phi}_2^2}{16}\,h^{2s} \asymp m^{-2s/(2s + d)}.
   $$

Many bumps ($h$ small) mean many coordinates to get wrong, but each is
cheap. Few bumps mean each is costly but detectable. The balance point is the same $h \asymp m^{-1/(2s + d)}$ that the
upper bound chose.

## Worked example

**One dimension.** Take $d = 1$, $s = 1$, $L = 1$, $\sigma = 0.5$ and $\abs f \le 1$, so $A = 1$ and
$C = 2(0.25) + 1/e = 0.868$.

1. **The bound at $m = 1{,}000$.** $h^* = (0.868/2{,}000)^{1/3} = 0.076$, about 13 cells. The bound is
   $3 \times 0.076^2 = 0.017$.
2. **The measurement.** In the figure, $k$-NN with $k \approx m^{2/3}$ on the sine target has
   <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">excess risk</NoteLink> 0.011 at $m = 640$, below the bound at
   $m = 1{,}000$. The bound holds for every Lipschitz function, the sine is smoother than that, and $k$-NN adapts its
   neighbourhoods to the data; all three make it conservative.
3. **Sample size.** The bound falls as $m^{-2/3}$. It reaches 0.001 at $m \approx 71{,}000$.
4. **The lower bound.** With the tent bump $\phi(u) = (\frac12 - \abs u)_+$, $\norm{\phi}_2^2 = \frac1{12}$, and
   $h^3 = 12\sigma^2/m = 0.003$ at $m = 1{,}000$, so $h = 0.144$. The bound is $h^2/192 = 1.1 \times 10^{-4}$. Upper
   and lower bounds differ by a factor of 160 in constants and agree in the exponent $-2/3$.

**Ten dimensions.** The same constants with $d = 10$ give $A = 10$ and an upper bound of 3.3 at $m = 1{,}000$ and 1.0
at $m = 10^6$, both vacuous, since predicting 0 has error at most 1. The bound reaches 0.1 only at $m \approx 1.3
\times 10^{12}$. The rate exponent $2/12$ means that each tenfold improvement costs a millionfold more data.

**The exponent across dimensions.** The factor by which $m$ must grow to cut the error tenfold is $10^{(2s + d)/2s}$:

| $d$ | Exponent, $s = 1$ | Factor, $s = 1$ | Exponent, $s = 2$ | Factor, $s = 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.67 | 32 | 0.80 | 18 |
| 2 | 0.50 | 100 | 0.67 | 32 |
| 5 | 0.29 | 3,200 | 0.44 | 180 |
| 10 | 0.17 | $10^6$ | 0.29 | 3,200 |
| 100 | 0.02 | $10^{51}$ | 0.04 | $10^{26}$ |

A parametric model with a fixed number of parameters has exponent 1 and factor 10 in every dimension.

## Escaping the curse

The rate is a worst case over the whole class. Methods that work in high dimensions assume more structure than
smoothness, and the minimax rate over the smaller class is faster.

- **More smoothness.** The exponent $2s/(2s + d)$ approaches 1 as $s$ grows relative to $d$. Smoothness $s = d$ gives
  the rate $m^{-2/3}$ in every dimension.
- **Additive structure.** If $f(\xvec) = \sum_j f_j(x_j)$, each component is a one-dimensional problem, and the rate
  is the one-dimensional $m^{-2s/(2s + 1)}$ (see <NoteLink to="generalised-additive-model">generalised additive
  models</NoteLink>).
- **Low intrinsic dimension.** If the inputs lie near a manifold of dimension $d_0 < d$, $k$-NN regression adapts and
  attains rates governed by $d_0$ without knowing it <Cite id="kpotufe2011" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The rate is an exponent, not a prediction.** Two estimators with the same rate can differ by large constants,
  and the minimax bound holds for the worst function in the class, not the one at hand.
- **The bandwidth depends on the smoothness.** The optimal $h$ involves $s$, which is unknown. Choosing $h$ from the
  data, by <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> or by splitting the sample, adapts to the
  unknown smoothness <Cite id="gyorfi2002" />.
- **A local constant fit caps the rate.** Local averaging and $k$-NN reach the optimal rate for $s \le 1$. For
  smoother $f$, their boundary bias of order $h$, and interior bias of order $h^2$ at best, keep them below it.
- **The derivations assume a bounded input density and finite noise variance.** Without a lower bound on the density,
  regions with few points have their own, slower rate.
