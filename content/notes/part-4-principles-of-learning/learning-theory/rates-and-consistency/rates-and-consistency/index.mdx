<Definition>
Rates and consistency is the part of <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">learning theory</NoteLink> that asks how
the **excess risk** $R(\hat h) - R^*$ of a learned predictor, its risk above the
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes risk</NoteLink> $R^*$, behaves as the sample size $m$ grows. **Consistency** is
convergence to zero. A **rate** is a function $r_m$ with excess risk of order $r_m$ on a stated family of distributions.
Upper bounds show that a method achieves a rate; minimax lower bounds show that no method beats it
<Cite id="devroye1996" /> <Cite id="tsybakov2009" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A generalisation bound for a fixed class says how far the risk of empirical risk minimisation lies above the best risk
in the class. Does the risk reach the Bayes risk when the class grows with
the data? How fast does it fall, and under what assumptions on the distribution? Is a given method as fast as any method
can be? Does minimising a <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">convex surrogate</NoteLink> such as the logistic or
<Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss> also minimise classification error? The answers decide how much data a
problem needs, which losses are safe to train with, and when a better algorithm is worth looking for.

## What a rate is

Write $\Delta_m = R(\hat h_m) - R^*$, or the excess over the best in a class when the class is fixed. A rate is a
statement such as $\expect[\Delta_m] \le C\,m^{-\gamma}$ for every distribution in a family $\Pcal$, or the same with
probability $1 - \delta$ and a $\ln(1/\delta)$ term.

- **Slow rates.** Empirical risk minimisation over a class of <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
  dimension</NoteLink> $d$ has excess risk of order $\sqrt{d/m}$ over the best in the class, for every distribution. The
  square root comes from <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>: a mean of $m$ bounded
  losses fluctuates by about $1/\sqrt m$.
- **Fast rates.** Realisable problems, low-noise problems and strongly convex losses give order $d/m$. There the
  variance of the excess loss shrinks with its mean, and <NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's
  inequality</NoteLink> turns small variance into a smaller deviation. To halve a slow-rate bound needs four times the
  data; to halve a fast-rate bound needs twice.
- **Nonparametric rates.** Without a fixed class, rates depend on the smoothness $s$ of the target and the dimension
  $d$, as $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$.
- **No rate without assumptions.** For every rule some distribution makes the excess risk fall arbitrarily slowly
  (<NoteLink to="consistency-of-learning-algorithms">consistency of learning algorithms</NoteLink>). Every rate is a
  rate over a restricted family.

An **upper bound** holds for one method on every distribution in $\Pcal$. A **lower bound** holds for every method on
some distribution in $\Pcal$. When the two have the same order, the rate is the **minimax rate** of $\Pcal$, and a
method that attains it is rate-optimal.

## Main ideas

**Consistency without a class.** Local averaging rules let their neighbourhoods shrink as data arrive and reach the
Bayes risk on every distribution. <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-consistency">Nearest-neighbour consistency</NoteLink>
shows the two limits: one neighbour converges to $\expect[2\eta(1 - \eta)]$, at most twice the Bayes risk, and $k$
neighbours with $k \to \infty$, $k/m \to 0$ converge to $R^*$ itself.

**Calibrated surrogates.** Classifiers are trained on convex surrogates of the 0–1 loss.
<NoteLink to="classification-calibrated-losses">Classification-calibrated losses</NoteLink> are those whose minimiser
has the sign of the Bayes rule. The ψ-transform converts surrogate excess risk into 0–1 excess risk. For the smooth
losses, the 0–1 excess risk is bounded by the square root of the surrogate excess risk.

**Low noise gives fast rates.** <NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">Fast rates and noise
conditions</NoteLink> derives the Bernstein condition from Massart's and Tsybakov's conditions on how often $\eta$ is
near $\frac12$, and the excess-risk exponent $(1 + \alpha)/(2 + \alpha)$ that interpolates between slow and fast.

**Localisation.** <NoteLink to="local-rademacher-complexity">Local Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> measures only the
functions near the best one. Its fixed point replaces the global complexity in the bound and gives fast rates for
infinite classes, including kernel classes.

**Lower bounds.** <NoteLink to="minimax-lower-bounds">Minimax lower bounds</NoteLink> reduce estimation to testing
between hypotheses that the data cannot separate, by Le Cam's, Fano's and Assouad's methods.
<NoteLink to="minimax-rates-for-nonparametric-regression">Minimax rates for nonparametric regression</NoteLink> applies
them to prove that $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$ cannot be beaten, which shows that the dependence of this rate on the dimension,
the <NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours">curse of dimensionality</NoteLink>, holds for every method.
