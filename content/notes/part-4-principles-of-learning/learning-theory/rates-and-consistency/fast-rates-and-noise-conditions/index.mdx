<Definition>
In binary classification with regression function $\eta(\xvec) = \prob(y = +1 \mid \xvec)$, a **noise condition** limits
how much probability sits near the decision boundary $\eta = \frac12$. **Massart's condition** asks that
$\abs{2\eta(\xvec) - 1} \ge h$ everywhere, for some margin $h \in (0, 1]$. **Tsybakov's condition** with exponent
$\alpha \ge 0$ asks that $\prob(\abs{2\eta(\xvec) - 1} \le t) \le Ct^\alpha$ for every $t > 0$ <Cite id="mammen1999" />
<Cite id="tsybakov2004" />. Each implies a **Bernstein condition**: the variance of the excess loss of any classifier is
bounded by a power of its excess risk. Under it, empirical risk minimisation has excess risk of order
$m^{-(1 + \alpha)/(2 + \alpha)}$, between the slow rate $m^{-1/2}$ and the fast rate $m^{-1}$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The agnostic bound for <Gloss name="erm" /> over a finite class has excess risk of order $\sqrt{\ln N/m}$, and the
realisable bound has order $\ln N/m$ (<NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">realisable and agnostic
learning</NoteLink>). The two differ because of variance. In the realisable case a bad hypothesis errs with probability
at least $\epsilon$ and the best one never errs, so a hypothesis whose excess risk is $\epsilon$ is caught by about
$1/\epsilon$ examples. In the agnostic case two hypotheses may disagree on many inputs where the labels are close to
coin flips. Their difference in loss then has large variance and tiny mean, and telling them apart needs about
$1/\epsilon^2$ examples.

A noise condition says that such near-coin-flip inputs are rare. Where $\eta$ is far from $\frac12$, each disagreement
with the Bayes classifier adds a large amount to the excess risk. A classifier with small excess risk must then
disagree rarely, and its excess loss has small variance. <NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's
inequality</NoteLink>, whose deviation term scales with the standard deviation, turns small variance into a faster rate.
In the language of the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for generalisation bounds</NoteLink>, the noise
condition is structure that the concentration step exploits.

Labels need not be noise-free. Under Massart's condition with $h = 0.5$, every input has $\eta \in [0, 0.25]$ or
$[0.75, 1]$, and the Bayes risk can be as large as 0.25. The excess risk still falls like $1/m$.

## Massart and Tsybakov noise

Let $\Dcal$ be a distribution on $\Xcal \times \set{-1, +1}$ and $h^*(\xvec) = \sgn(2\eta(\xvec) - 1)$ the
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes classifier</NoteLink>, with Bayes risk $R^*$. For any classifier $h$, the
excess risk is a weighted disagreement:

$$
R(h) - R^* = \expect\brack{\abs{2\eta(\xvec) - 1}\,\indicator[h(\xvec) \ne h^*(\xvec)]}.
$$

The weight $\abs{2\eta - 1}$ is the gap between the error of the wrong and the right label at $\xvec$.

- **Massart's condition** <Cite id="massart2006" />: $\abs{2\eta(\xvec) - 1} \ge h$ for every $\xvec$. Each
  disagreement with the Bayes classifier costs at least $h$. With $h = 1$ the labels are deterministic and the problem
  is realisable by $h^*$.
- **Tsybakov's condition** with exponent $\alpha$ <Cite id="tsybakov2004" />: $\prob(\abs{2\eta(\xvec) - 1} \le t) \le
  Ct^\alpha$ for all $t > 0$. $\alpha = 0$ is no condition. Larger $\alpha$ puts less mass near the boundary, and
  $\alpha = \infty$ recovers Massart's condition. If $\xvec$ has a bounded density and $\eta$ crosses $\frac12$
  with slope bounded away from zero, the set $\abs{2\eta - 1} \le t$ is a band around the boundary of width
  proportional to $t$, and the condition holds with $\alpha = 1$.

## The Bernstein condition

For a classifier $h$, the **excess loss** $Z_h = \indicator[h(\xvec) \ne y] - \indicator[h^*(\xvec) \ne y]$ takes
values in $\set{-1, 0, 1}$, has mean $R(h) - R^*$, and is non-zero only where $h \ne h^*$. Hence

$$
\var(Z_h) \le \expect[Z_h^2] = \prob(h(\xvec) \ne h^*(\xvec)).
$$

The class satisfies the **Bernstein condition** with exponent $\beta \in [0, 1]$ and constant $B$ if
$\var(Z_h) \le B\,(R(h) - R^*)^\beta$ for every $h$ <Cite id="bartlett2006empirical" /> <Cite id="vanerven2015" />.
Both noise conditions imply it.

**Massart gives $\beta = 1$.** Each disagreement costs at least $h$, so
$R(h) - R^* \ge h\,\prob(h \ne h^*)$. Then $\var(Z_h) \le \prob(h \ne h^*) \le (R(h) - R^*)/h$, which is the
condition with $\beta = 1$ and $B = 1/h$.

**Tsybakov gives $\beta = \alpha/(1 + \alpha)$.** Write $A$ for the disagreement event $\set{h \ne h^*}$ and fix
$t > 0$. On $A$, discard the inputs with weight at most $t$:

$$
R(h) - R^* \ge t\,\prob\paren{A \cap \set{\abs{2\eta - 1} > t}} \ge t\paren{\prob(A) - Ct^\alpha}.
$$

Choose $t$ so that $Ct^\alpha = \prob(A)/2$, that is $t = (\prob(A)/2C)^{1/\alpha}$. Then
$R(h) - R^* \ge \frac12\prob(A)^{(1 + \alpha)/\alpha}(2C)^{-1/\alpha}$, and solving for $\prob(A)$ gives

$$
\var(Z_h) \le \prob(A) \le c_\alpha\,(R(h) - R^*)^{\alpha/(1 + \alpha)}, \qquad
c_\alpha = \paren{2(2C)^{1/\alpha}}^{\alpha/(1 + \alpha)}.
$$

Conversely, a bound $\prob(h \ne h^*) \le c\,(R(h) - R^*)^{\alpha/(1 + \alpha)}$ for every $h$ implies Tsybakov's
condition with exponent $\alpha$, so the two are equivalent <Cite id="bartlett2006" />.

The Bernstein condition also holds for losses other than 0–1: for the
<NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> over a convex class of functions with
$\abs{f(\xvec) - y} \le 1$, it holds with $\beta = 1$ relative to the best function in the class
<Cite id="bartlett2005" />. This is why least-squares regression over a fixed finite-dimensional class has excess risk
of order $d/m$ without any noise condition.

## The rates

Take a finite class $\Hcal$ of $N$ classifiers that contains the Bayes classifier $h^*$, a sample of size $m$, and ERM
$\hat h$. Write $\Delta_h = R(h) - R^*$ and $L = \ln(N/\delta)$. The derivation has four steps.

1. **Concentrate each excess loss.** $Z_h - \expect Z_h$ lies within $2$ of zero. By Bernstein's inequality, with
   probability at least $1 - \delta/N$,

   $$
   \Delta_h - \hat\Delta_h \le \sqrt{\frac{2\var(Z_h)L}{m}} + \frac{4L}{3m},
   $$

   where $\hat\Delta_h = \hat R_S(h) - \hat R_S(h^*)$ is the empirical mean of $Z_h$.
2. **Make it uniform.** The <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> over the $N$ classifiers makes all $N$
   inequalities hold at once with probability at least $1 - \delta$.
3. **Use ERM.** $\hat h$ minimises the empirical risk and $h^* \in \Hcal$, so $\hat\Delta_{\hat h} \le 0$. With the
   Bernstein condition,

   $$
   \Delta_{\hat h} \le \sqrt{\frac{2B\,\Delta_{\hat h}^\beta L}{m}} + \frac{4L}{3m}.
   $$

4. **Solve for $\Delta$.** The unknown appears on both sides.
   - With $\beta = 1$, the inequality has the form $x \le a\sqrt x + c$, which implies $x \le a^2 + 2c$
     (<NoteLink to="completing-the-square">complete the square</NoteLink>). Hence

     $$
     R(\hat h) - R^* \le \paren{2B + \frac83}\frac{\ln(N/\delta)}{m}.
     $$

   - With general $\beta$, either $\Delta \le 2c = 8L/3m$, or the square-root term is the larger and
     $\Delta \le 2\sqrt{2B\Delta^\beta L/m}$. Raising to the power $2/(2 - \beta)$ gives

     $$
     R(\hat h) - R^* \le \max\set{\paren{\frac{8BL}{m}}^{1/(2 - \beta)},\ \frac{8L}{3m}}.
     $$

With $\beta = 0$ this is the slow rate $\sqrt{L/m}$; with $\beta = 1$ it is the fast rate $L/m$. Under Tsybakov's
condition $\beta = \alpha/(1 + \alpha)$, and the exponent of $1/m$ is

$$
\frac{1}{2 - \beta} = \frac{1 + \alpha}{2 + \alpha}.
$$

**Infinite classes.** For a class of <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> $d$
containing the Bayes classifier, Massart and Nédélec show that ERM has expected excess risk of order
$\frac{d}{mh}\paren{1 + \ln\frac{mh^2}{d}}$ when $h > \sqrt{d/m}$, and of order $\sqrt{d/m}$ otherwise. A matching
lower bound of order $\min\set{\sqrt{d/m},\, d/(mh)}$ holds for every estimator <Cite id="massart2006" />. The rate is
$1/m$ for a fixed margin $h$ and degrades smoothly to the slow rate as $h$ shrinks to $\sqrt{d/m}$. The uniform step
over an infinite class needs the variance-sensitive capacity of
<NoteLink to="local-rademacher-complexity">local Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> in place of the union bound.

**Lower bounds.** Without a noise condition the slow rate cannot be beaten. The hard distributions in
<NoteLink to="sample-complexity-lower-bounds">sample complexity lower bounds</NoteLink> put $\eta$ at $\frac12 \pm
\epsilon$, which violates every Massart condition with $h > 2\epsilon$. The rates above are therefore rates over a
restricted family of distributions, in the sense of <NoteLink to="minimax-lower-bounds">minimax lower bounds</NoteLink>.

## Other sources of fast rates

The Bernstein condition is the common thread <Cite id="vanerven2015" />:

- **Realisable learning** is Massart's condition with $h = 1$.
- **Strongly convex losses**, such as the squared loss over a convex class, satisfy the condition with $\beta = 1$
  relative to the best function in the class, with no assumption on the noise.
- **Exp-concave losses**, such as the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">logistic loss</NoteLink> on bounded scores, fall
  under the stochastic mixability condition of van Erven et al. It generalises stochastic exp-concavity and governs fast
  rates in <NoteLink to="online-learning">online learning</NoteLink>. Its form for proper learners, the central
  condition, is equivalent to the Bernstein condition for bounded losses <Cite id="vanerven2015" />.
- **Surrogate losses** inherit faster comparison inequalities under Tsybakov noise (see
  <NoteLink to="classification-calibrated-losses">classification-calibrated losses</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

Take a finite class of $N = 1{,}000$ thresholds that contains the Bayes classifier, $m = 10{,}000$ examples and
$\delta = 0.05$, so $L = \ln(1{,}000/0.05) = \ln 20{,}000 = 9.90$.

1. **No noise condition.** $\beta = 0$ and $B = 1$. Steps 1–3 give
   $\Delta \le \sqrt{2L/m} + 4L/3m = 0.0445 + 0.0013 = 0.046$. The agnostic Hoeffding bound
   $2\sqrt{\ln(2N/\delta)/2m}$ gives the same value, 0.046.
2. **Massart with $h = 0.5$.** $B = 1/h = 2$, so $\Delta \le (2 \times 2 + 8/3) \times 9.90/10{,}000 = 0.0066$. The
   bound is seven times smaller.
3. **Sample size for excess risk 0.01.** Solving $\sqrt{2L/m} + 4L/3m = 0.01$ gives $m \approx 200{,}700$ without a
   noise condition. With $h = 0.5$, $m = 6.67\,L/0.01 \approx 6{,}600$, thirty times fewer. With $h = 0.1$ the fast
   bound needs $m \approx 22{,}400$; the benefit shrinks as the margin does.
4. **Tsybakov exponents.** With $B = 1$, the general bound at $m = 10{,}000$, and the sample size it needs for
   $\Delta \le 0.01$, are

| $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | Rate exponent | Bound at $m = 10{,}000$ | $m$ for 0.01 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | 0.50 | 0.089 | 792,000 |
| 0.5 | 0.33 | 0.60 | 0.055 | 171,000 |
| 1 | 0.50 | 0.67 | 0.040 | 79,000 |
| 3 | 0.75 | 0.80 | 0.021 | 25,000 |
| $\infty$ | 1 | 1.00 | 0.0079 | 7,900 |

None of these bounds is vacuous. The general form loses a constant factor against the sharp $\beta = 1$ and $\beta = 0$
calculations above (0.089 against 0.046 at $\alpha = 0$), because it solves the quadratic crudely. The ordering is what
matters: each step up in $\alpha$ divides the required sample size by a large factor.

## Pitfalls

- **The condition concerns the distribution, not the class.** A learner cannot check Massart's or Tsybakov's
  condition from the sample, and the bounds above assume the Bayes classifier lies in the class. Without that, the
  Bernstein condition must hold relative to the best classifier in the class, which can fail even when the noise is
  low.
- **Fast rates are about excess risk.** The risk itself converges to $R^* > 0$. A training error near $R^*$ says nothing
  about which rate applies.
- **Constants grow as the margin shrinks.** The fast bound scales with $1/h$. When $h$ is below about $\sqrt{d/m}$ the
  slow rate is the better bound, and the minimax rate switches over at that point.
- **Not all fast rates are noise conditions.** The $d/m$ rate of least squares comes from the curvature of the loss,
  and the realisable $1/m$ rate from zero noise; both are instances of the Bernstein condition, not of a margin on
  $\eta$.
