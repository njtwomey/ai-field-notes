<Definition>
The **minimax risk** of estimating a quantity $\theta(P)$ from $m$ independent draws of an unknown distribution $P$ in a
family $\Pcal$ is the worst-case risk of the best estimator,
$\inf_{\hat\theta}\sup_{P \in \Pcal}\expect_P[\Phi(\rho(\hat\theta, \theta(P)))]$, for a metric $\rho$ and an
increasing $\Phi$. A **minimax lower bound** shows that this quantity is at least some rate in $m$. The standard proofs
pick a finite set of distributions in $\Pcal$ whose parameters are far apart but whose samples are statistically close,
and show that any estimator good on all of them would be a test that separates them, which is impossible
<Cite id="yu1997" /> <Cite id="tsybakov2009" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

An upper bound says what one method achieves. It cannot say whether a better method exists. A lower bound over a family
$\Pcal$ answers that: if every estimator has worst-case risk at least $r_m$ on $\Pcal$, then a method with risk of
order $r_m$ is **rate-optimal**, and effort spent on a better algorithm is wasted. If the gap between the best upper
bound and the best lower bound is large, either the analysis or the method can be improved.

The lower bound must be over a family, not a single distribution. For any single $P$, the estimator that ignores the
data and outputs $\theta(P)$ has risk zero. Minimax asks for one estimator that works for every member of the family,
and charges it for the member on which it does worst.

Lower bounds of this kind give the <NoteLink to="sample-complexity-lower-bounds">sample-complexity lower
bounds</NoteLink> of <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">probably approximately correct (PAC)
learning</NoteLink>, the $m^{-2s/(2s + d)}$ limit of
<NoteLink to="minimax-rates-for-nonparametric-regression">nonparametric regression</NoteLink>, and the $\sqrt T$
<NoteLink to="lower-bounds-for-online-learning">regret lower bounds</NoteLink> of <NoteLink to="online-learning">online
learning</NoteLink>.

## Mechanism

### Reduction to testing

Choose parameters $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M$, each $\theta_j = \theta(P_j)$ for some $P_j \in \Pcal$, that are
$2\delta$-separated: $\rho(\theta_j, \theta_k) \ge 2\delta$ for $j \ne k$. Write $P_j^m$ for the distribution of a
sample of size $m$ from $P_j$.

1. **An estimator gives a test.** Given $\hat\theta$, let $\psi = \argmin_j \rho(\hat\theta, \theta_j)$, the index of
   the nearest candidate. If $\rho(\hat\theta, \theta_j) < \delta$, every other candidate is more than $\delta$ away by
   the triangle inequality, so $\psi = j$. Hence $\prob_j(\rho(\hat\theta, \theta_j) \ge \delta) \ge
   \prob_j(\psi \ne j)$.
2. **Markov's inequality.** $\expect_j[\Phi(\rho(\hat\theta, \theta_j))] \ge \Phi(\delta)\,\prob_j(\rho(\hat\theta,
   \theta_j) \ge \delta)$.
3. **Restrict the supremum.** The worst case over $\Pcal$ is at least the worst case over the $M$ chosen distributions.

Together:

$$
\inf_{\hat\theta}\sup_{P \in \Pcal}\expect_P\brack{\Phi(\rho(\hat\theta, \theta(P)))}
\ge \Phi(\delta)\,\inf_{\psi}\max_{j \le M}\prob_j(\psi \ne j).
$$

The right side is the minimax error of a test between $M$ hypotheses. The methods below bound it from below. Each
trades separation against closeness: a larger $\delta$ raises $\Phi(\delta)$ but makes the hypotheses easier to tell
apart.

### Le Cam's two-point method

With $M = 2$, the testing error is bounded by the overlap of the two sample distributions <Cite id="lecam1973" />.
For any test, let $A$ be the event that it answers 2. The total variation distance is
$\operatorname{TV}(P, Q) = \sup_A \abs{P(A) - Q(A)}$ (see <NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon">
f-divergences</NoteLink>). Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_j \prob_j(\psi \ne j) &\ge \frac12\paren{P_1^m(A) + P_2^m(A^c)} = \frac12\paren{1 - \paren{P_2^m(A) - P_1^m(A)}} \\
&\ge \frac12\paren{1 - \operatorname{TV}(P_1^m, P_2^m)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound is exact for the average error: the likelihood-ratio test, which answers 2 where the density of $P_2^m$
exceeds that of $P_1^m$, attains it (the <NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma">Neyman–Pearson lemma</NoteLink>).

Total variation of a product of $m$ distributions has no closed form, so it is bounded through the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink>, which adds over independent draws:
$\KL(P_1^m \,\|\, P_2^m) = m\KL(P_1 \,\|\, P_2)$. Two inequalities convert it <Cite id="tsybakov2009" />:

- **Pinsker:** $\operatorname{TV} \le \sqrt{\KL/2}$, useful when the divergence is small.
- **Bretagnolle–Huber:** $1 - \operatorname{TV} \ge \frac12 e^{-\KL}$, which stays positive for every divergence.

The resulting recipe: choose two hypotheses whose parameters differ by $2\delta$ and whose sample divergence
$m\KL(P_1 \,\|\, P_2)$ is a constant, then read off the rate from $\delta$ as a function of $m$.

### Fano's method

Two hypotheses give at most a constant factor, which is the right answer for one-dimensional problems but loses the
dependence on dimension. Fano's method uses $M$ hypotheses. Let $V$ be uniform on $\set{1, \dots, M}$ and let the
sample $X$ be drawn from $P_V^m$. **Fano's inequality** bounds the error of any test $\psi(X)$ in terms of the
<NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> $I(V; X)$ <Cite id="cover2006" />:

$$
\prob(\psi \ne V) \ge 1 - \frac{I(V; X) + \ln 2}{\ln M}.
$$

The maximum error over $j$ is at least this average error. The mutual information is at most the largest pairwise
divergence, because $I(V; X) = \frac1M\sum_j\KL(P_j^m \,\|\, \bar P^m)$ with $\bar P^m$ the mixture, and the
divergence is convex in its second argument:

$$
I(V; X) \le \frac{1}{M^2}\sum_{j, k}\KL(P_j^m \,\|\, P_k^m) \le m\max_{j, k}\KL(P_j \,\|\, P_k).
$$

The recipe: find a **packing**, $M$ parameters that are $2\delta$-separated, with $\ln M$ large, and choose $\delta$
so that $m\max\KL \le \frac14\ln M$. Then the testing error is at least about $\frac12$ and the minimax risk is at least
$\Phi(\delta)/2$ <Cite id="wainwright2019" />. Packings come from
<NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy">metric entropy</NoteLink>: the log-packing number of the parameter
set plays the role of capacity.

<Derivation title="Fano's inequality">
Let $E = \indicator[\psi \ne V]$ and $p_e = \prob(E = 1)$. Expanding the conditional entropy $\entropy(E, V \mid X)$ in
two orders gives $\entropy(V \mid X) = \entropy(E \mid X) + \entropy(V \mid E, X)$, since $E$ is a function of $V$
and $X$. Now $\entropy(E \mid X) \le \entropy(E) \le \ln 2$. Given $E = 0$, $V = \psi(X)$ is determined; given
$E = 1$, $V$ is one of the other $M - 1$ values, so $\entropy(V \mid E, X) \le p_e\ln(M - 1)$. Hence
$\entropy(V \mid X) \le \ln 2 + p_e\ln M$. With $V$ uniform, $\entropy(V \mid X) = \ln M - I(V; X)$, and rearranging
gives $p_e \ge 1 - (I(V; X) + \ln 2)/\ln M$.
</Derivation>

### Assouad's lemma

When the loss splits into a sum over coordinates, a hypercube of hypotheses gives a cleaner bound
<Cite id="yu1997" />. Index the hypotheses by $\omega \in \set{0, 1}^d$, and let $\rho_H(\omega, \omega')$ be the
Hamming distance, the number of coordinates that differ. Then for any estimate $\hat\omega$
<Cite id="tsybakov2009" />,

$$
\max_\omega \expect_\omega[\rho_H(\hat\omega, \omega)] \ge \frac d2\min_{\rho_H(\omega, \omega') = 1}
\paren{1 - \operatorname{TV}(P_\omega^m, P_{\omega'}^m)}.
$$

Each coordinate is a two-point problem between neighbours of the cube, and the $d$ problems add.

1. **Maximum at least average.** The maximum over $\omega$ is at least the average over all $2^d$ vertices,
   $2^{-d}\sum_\omega\sum_{j=1}^d\prob_\omega(\hat\omega_j \ne \omega_j)$.
2. **Pair the vertices.** For each coordinate $j$, group the vertices into $2^{d-1}$ pairs that differ only in
   coordinate $j$. For a pair $(\omega, \omega')$, the test $\hat\omega_j$ errs under one or the other, and by the
   two-point bound $\prob_\omega(\hat\omega_j \ne \omega_j) + \prob_{\omega'}(\hat\omega_j \ne \omega'_j) \ge 1 -
   \operatorname{TV}(P_\omega^m, P_{\omega'}^m)$.
3. **Add up.** Each coordinate contributes at least $2^{-d}\,2^{d-1}\min(1 - \operatorname{TV}) = \frac12\min(1 -
   \operatorname{TV})$, and there are $d$ coordinates.

## Worked example

**A coin (Le Cam).** Estimate the bias $p$ of a coin from $m$ flips. Take $p \in \set{\frac12 - \delta, \frac12 +
\delta}$ with $\delta = 0.05$. The per-flip divergence is $\KL = 2\delta\ln\frac{1 + 2\delta}{1 - 2\delta} = 0.1\ln(1.1/0.9)
= 0.0201$ nats.

1. **At $m = 100$.** The sample divergence is $2.01$. The exact total variation between the two
   <NoteLink to="binomial-distribution">binomial distributions</NoteLink> is $0.683$, so every estimator has
   $\prob(\abs{\hat p - p} \ge 0.05) \ge (1 - 0.683)/2 = 0.159$ for one of the two coins.
2. **Through the divergence.** Bretagnolle–Huber gives $\frac14e^{-2.01} = 0.034$, a weaker but valid bound. Pinsker
   gives $\operatorname{TV} \le \sqrt{2.01/2} = 1.00$, which is vacuous here: Pinsker is useful only when $m\KL$ is
   below about 1.
3. **Sample size.** An error probability of at most 0.05 needs $(1 - \operatorname{TV})/2 \le 0.05$, which first holds
   at $m = 269$. The Bretagnolle–Huber route gives $m \ge \ln 5/0.0201 = 80$. The sample mean with
   <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> guarantees it at $m = \ln(40)/(2\delta^2) =
   738$. The best estimator needs between 269 and 738 flips.

**A Gaussian mean in $d$ dimensions (Assouad).** Observe $m$ draws of $\Gauss(\thetavec, \sigma^2\eye_d)$ and estimate
$\thetavec$ in squared error.

1. **The cube.** Let $\thetavec_\omega = \delta(2\omega - \ones)$, so each coordinate is $\pm\delta$. If
   $\hat\theta_j$ has the wrong sign, $(\hat\theta_j - \theta_j)^2 \ge \delta^2$, so
   $\norm{\hat\thetavec - \thetavec_\omega}^2 \ge \delta^2\rho_H(\hat\omega, \omega)$ with $\hat\omega$ the signs of
   $\hat\thetavec$.
2. **Neighbours are close.** Neighbouring vertices differ by $2\delta$ in one coordinate, so
   $\KL = m(2\delta)^2/(2\sigma^2) = 2m\delta^2/\sigma^2$. With $\delta = \sigma/(2\sqrt m)$, $\KL = \frac12$ and
   Pinsker gives $\operatorname{TV} \le \frac12$.
3. **Assouad.**
   $\expect\norm{\hat\thetavec - \thetavec}^2 \ge \delta^2 \cdot \frac d2 \cdot \frac12 = \frac{d\sigma^2}{16m}$ for the
   worst vertex.

The sample mean has risk exactly $d\sigma^2/m$, so it is rate-optimal and the bound is off by a factor of 16. With
$d = 10$, $\sigma = 1$ and a target risk of 0.01, every estimator needs $m \ge 10/(16 \times 0.01) = 63$ draws, and
the sample mean needs 1,000.

Fano's method with a Varshamov–Gilbert packing of $2^{d/8}$ cube vertices, pairwise at Hamming distance at least
$d/8$ <Cite id="tsybakov2009" />, gives the same $d\sigma^2/m$ rate with the constant $\ln 2/1024$ for $d \ge 32$.
Both methods find the dimension; Assouad's lemma loses less in the constant when the loss splits over coordinates.

## Trade-offs

- **Le Cam** is the simplest. It gives the right rate for one-dimensional parameters and for functionals such as a
  mean or a density at a point, and nothing better than a constant in the dimension.
- **Fano** handles general metrics and gives dimension and entropy factors. It needs a packing and loses large
  constants, and it bounds the error only when $\ln M$ is large.
- **Assouad** gives sharper constants when the loss is a sum over coordinates or cells, as in regression with a
  hypercube of local bumps.
- **The choice of hypotheses sets the strength of the bound.** All three are mechanical once the hypotheses are chosen.
  Finding hypotheses that are far apart in the loss and close in divergence is the creative step, and a poor choice
  gives a valid but weak bound.

## Pitfalls

- **A lower bound is over a family.** "No estimator beats $m^{-1/2}$" always means "on some member of $\Pcal$". A
  smaller family, such as one with a <NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">noise condition</NoteLink>, can have
  a faster minimax rate.
- **Constants are rarely tight.** Lower bounds settle the rate. The constants 1/16 and $\ln 2/1024$ above are
  artefacts of the method, and comparing them with an upper bound's constant says little.
- **Total variation, not KL, controls testing.** Bounds through the divergence lose information. When the exact
  total variation is computable, as for the coin, it gives the sharpest two-point bound.
- **Worst case is not typical case.** The hard hypotheses are chosen adversarially. A method can beat the minimax rate
  on the distributions met in practice.
