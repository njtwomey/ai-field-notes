<Definition>
Let $\Gcal$ be a class of functions with values in $[-1, 1]$ and $T(g) \ge 0$ a measure of size, usually the second
moment $\expect[g^2]$. The **local Rademacher complexity** of $\Gcal$ at radius $r$ is the
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> of the functions of size at most $r$,
$\Rad_m\set{g \in \Gcal : T(g) \le r}$. If a function $\psi$ bounds it, with $\psi(r)/\sqrt r$ non-increasing, then
$\psi$ has a unique positive **fixed point** $r^* = \psi(r^*)$. When every $g$ has variance at most $B\,\expect[g]$,
$r^*$ controls the gap between empirical and true means across the whole class, and bounds the excess risk of empirical
risk minimisation <Cite id="bartlett2005" /> <Cite id="koltchinskii2006" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The global bound of <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> depends on the largest
variance in the class, which empirical risk minimisation does not need. Under a
<NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">Bernstein condition</NoteLink>, the excess loss $g = \ell_h - \ell_{h^*}$
of a hypothesis with small excess risk has small variance, and it is these functions whose empirical means must be
accurate. Functions far from the best one have large excess risk, and an error in their empirical mean of the size of
their own variance does not make them look good.

Localisation makes this precise. Restrict the class to a ball of radius $r$ around the best hypothesis. The functions in
the ball fluctuate by about $\sqrt{r/m}$, and their supremum by the local complexity $\psi(r)$. Accuracy at scale $r$
needs $\psi(r) \le r$: the fluctuations must be no larger than the radius. The smallest such $r$ is the fixed point.
For a class with $p$ effective parameters $\psi(r) \approx \sqrt{rp/m}$, so $r^* \approx p/m$. The global complexity is
$\psi(1) \approx \sqrt{p/m}$, the square root of that.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for generalisation bounds</NoteLink>, localisation replaces the
"make it uniform" step for infinite classes when the structure step supplies a variance condition. It does for
Rademacher complexity what <NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's inequality</NoteLink> does for a single
hypothesis.

## Localisation and the fixed point

Let $z_1, \dots, z_m$ be independent draws from $P$. For a function $g$ write $Pg = \expect[g(z)]$ and
$P_m g = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m g(z_i)$. For a class $\Fcal$, let

$$
\Rad_m(\Fcal) = \expect\brack{\sup_{g \in \Fcal}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \sigma_i\,g(z_i)},
$$

with independent signs $\sigma_i = \pm 1$, as in the global definition.

**Sub-root functions.** A function $\psi : [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty)$ is **sub-root** if it is non-decreasing and
$\psi(r)/\sqrt r$ is non-increasing <Cite id="bartlett2005" />. Examples are $c\sqrt r$, constants, and sums of these.
A non-zero sub-root function has exactly one positive fixed point $r^* = \psi(r^*)$, and $r \ge \psi(r)$ holds exactly
when $r \ge r^*$. The non-increasing ratio also gives, for $r \ge r^*$,

$$
\psi(r) = \sqrt r\,\frac{\psi(r)}{\sqrt r} \le \sqrt r\,\frac{\psi(r^*)}{\sqrt{r^*}} = \sqrt{r\,r^*}.
$$

This inequality is the whole reason for the definition: above the fixed point, the local complexity grows only like
$\sqrt r$.

**Local complexities are sub-root.** If $\Gcal$ is star-shaped around 0 (with $g$ it contains $\alpha g$ for every
$\alpha \in [0, 1]$) and $T(\alpha g) \le \alpha^2 T(g)$, then $r \mapsto \Rad_m\set{g \in \Gcal : T(g) \le r}$ is
sub-root <Cite id="bartlett2005" />. Convex classes containing 0 are star-shaped, and $T(g) = Pg^2$ scales
quadratically.

## The fixed-point bound

**Theorem** (Bartlett, Bousquet and Mendelson). Let every $g \in \Gcal$ take values in $[a, b]$ and satisfy
$\var(g) \le T(g) \le B\,Pg$. Let $\psi$ be sub-root with fixed point $r^*$, and suppose
$\psi(r) \ge B\,\Rad_m\set{g \in \Gcal : T(g) \le r}$ for all $r \ge r^*$. Then for every $K > 1$ and $x > 0$, with
probability at least $1 - e^{-x}$, every $g \in \Gcal$ satisfies

$$
Pg \le \frac{K}{K - 1}P_m g + \frac{704K}{B}r^* + \frac{x\paren{11(b - a) + 26BK}}{m}.
$$

If $\Gcal$ is star-shaped around 0 and $T$ scales quadratically, the constants 704 and 26 improve to 6 and 5
<Cite id="bartlett2005" />.

The derivation keeps only the structure; $c$ denotes an absolute constant that may change from line to line.

1. **Rescale.** Fix $r \ge r^*$ and replace each $g$ by $g_r = g \cdot r/\max(r, T(g))$. A function of size at most $r$
   is unchanged; a larger one is shrunk until its size is $r$. Every $g_r$ has variance at most $r$, and for a
   star-shaped class it lies in $\set{g : T(g) \le r}$, so the rescaled class $\Gcal_r$ has
   $\Rad_m(\Gcal_r) \le \psi(r)/B$. Without star shape, the class is split into shells of size between $r\lambda^k$
   and $r\lambda^{k+1}$ and each shell is bounded separately, which gives the constants 704 and 26 in place of 6 and 5.
2. **Concentrate the supremum.** Talagrand's inequality for suprema of empirical processes, in Bousquet's form, is a
   <NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein inequality</NoteLink> for
   $V_r = \sup_{\Gcal_r}(Pg_r - P_m g_r)$ <Cite id="boucheron2013" />. After
   <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink> of its mean, with probability at least $1 - e^{-x}$,

   $$
   V_r \le c\paren{\Rad_m(\Gcal_r) + \sqrt{\frac{rx}{m}} + \frac{(b - a)x}{m}}
   \le c\paren{\frac{\sqrt{r\,r^*}}{B} + \sqrt{\frac{rx}{m}} + \frac{(b - a)x}{m}},
   $$

   using step 1 and the sub-root inequality $\psi(r) \le \sqrt{r\,r^*}$. The variance bound $r$ in the square root is
   the point of rescaling: a global bound would have the worst variance in the class there.
3. **Undo the rescaling.** If $T(g) \le r$, then $g_r = g$ and $Pg - P_m g \le V_r$. If $T(g) > r$, then
   $Pg - P_m g = (T(g)/r)(Pg_r - P_m g_r) \le (B\,Pg/r)\,V_r$. Choose $r$ so that $V_r \le r/(BK)$. In the first case
   $Pg - P_m g \le r/(BK)$; in the second $Pg - P_m g \le Pg/K$. Either way,
   $Pg - P_m g \le Pg/K + r/(BK)$, which rearranges to

   $$
   Pg \le \frac{K}{K - 1}P_m g + \frac{r}{B(K - 1)}.
   $$

4. **Solve for $r$.** The condition $V_r \le r/(BK)$ holds once
   $c\sqrt{r\,r^*}/B \le r/(3BK)$ and the other two terms are each at most $r/(3BK)$, that is, for
   $r \ge c\,(K^2 r^* + B^2K^2 x/m + BK(b - a)x/m)$. Substituting this $r$ in step 3 gives the theorem's form, a
   multiple of $r^*/B$ plus a multiple of $x/m$.

## Excess risk of empirical risk minimisation

Apply the theorem to the excess-loss class $\Gcal = \set{\ell_h - \ell_{h^*} : h \in \Hcal}$, where $h^*$ minimises
the risk over $\Hcal$. Then $Pg = R(h) - R(h^*)$ is the excess risk. For <Gloss name="erm" />,
$P_m(\ell_{\hat h} - \ell_{h^*}) \le 0$, so the first term on the right is non-positive and $K$ can be taken close to
1:

$$
R(\hat h) - R(h^*) \le c\paren{\frac{r^*}{B} + \frac{(b - a + B)\ln(1/\delta)}{m}}
\quad\text{with probability at least } 1 - \delta.
$$

The hypothesis $\var(g) \le B\,Pg$ is the Bernstein condition with exponent 1. It holds under Massart's noise condition
in classification, and for the <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> over a convex class with values
in a bounded range, with no noise condition <Cite id="bartlett2005" />. Bartlett and Mendelson extend the argument to
general exponents <Cite id="bartlett2006empirical" />. A version with the empirical local complexity, computed on the
sample and localised around $\hat h$, gives a bound of the same order that can be evaluated from data
<Cite id="bartlett2005" />.

## Computing the fixed point

**Finite classes.** Let $\Gcal$ have $N$ functions with values in $[-1, 1]$. For one $g$ with $Pg^2 \le r$, the sum
$\frac1m\sum_i\sigma_i g(z_i)$ averages $m$ independent terms bounded by 1 with variance at most $r$. The Bernstein-type
<NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequality</NoteLink> for $N$ such sums gives
<Cite id="boucheron2013" />

$$
\Rad_m\set{g : Pg^2 \le r} \le \sqrt{\frac{2r\ln N}{m}} + \frac{\ln N}{3m}.
$$

Multiplying by $B$ gives a sub-root $\psi$. Setting $\psi(r) = r$ and solving the quadratic in $\sqrt r$ gives
$r^* \approx 2B^2\ln N/m$. The global complexity, $\psi(1)/B \approx \sqrt{2\ln N/m}$, is the square root of the local
one up to constants, which is the slow rate of the finite-class bound in
<NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's finite-class lemma</NoteLink>.

**VC classes.** For a class of $\set{0, 1}$-valued functions of <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
dimension</NoteLink> $d$, the same route through entropy bounds gives $r^*$ of order $d\ln(m/d)/m$
<Cite id="bartlett2005" />.

**Kernel classes.** Let $\Fcal$ be the unit ball of a <NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space">reproducing kernel
Hilbert space</NoteLink> with $k(\xvec, \xvec) \le 1$, and $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots$ the eigenvalues of the
kernel's integral operator under $P$. Then <Cite id="bartlett2005" />

$$
\Rad_m\set{f \in \Fcal : Pf^2 \le r} \le \paren{\frac2m\sum_{j=1}^\infty \min\set{r, \lambda_j}}^{1/2},
$$

and a matching lower bound holds up to a constant for $r \ge 1/m$. Eigenvalues above $r$ each contribute $r$, as a free
parameter would, and eigenvalues below $r$ contribute only their size. With polynomial decay $\lambda_j = j^{-2s}$, the
sum is about $r^{1 - 1/(2s)}$, so the fixed point solves $r^2 \approx r^{1 - 1/(2s)}/m$, giving
$r^* \approx m^{-2s/(2s + 1)}$. This is the <NoteLink to="minimax-lower-bounds">minimax rate</NoteLink> for regression
functions of smoothness $s$ in one dimension (<NoteLink to="minimax-rates-for-nonparametric-regression">minimax rates
for nonparametric regression</NoteLink>), and the rate that the critical-radius analysis gives for
<NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel ridge regression</NoteLink> <Cite id="wainwright2019" />. The empirical
version replaces $\lambda_j$ by the eigenvalues of the normalised Gram matrix $\frac1m K$, which the data determine.

## Worked example

**A finite class under Massart noise.** Take $N = 1{,}000$ classifiers containing the
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes classifier</NoteLink>, Massart noise with margin $h = 0.5$, $m = 10{,}000$ and
$\delta = 0.05$. For the 0–1 excess loss, $T(g) = Pg^2$ is the disagreement probability, $B = 1/h = 2$ and $b - a = 2$.

1. **The local complexity bound.** With $\ln N = 6.91$,
   $\psi(r) = 2\paren{\sqrt{2r \times 6.91/10{,}000} + 6.91/30{,}000} = 0.0743\sqrt r + 0.00046$.
2. **The fixed point.** Solving $r = 0.0743\sqrt r + 0.00046$ gives $\sqrt r = 0.0801$ and $r^* = 0.0064$.
3. **Compare with the global complexity.** At $r = 1$ the same bound gives $\psi(1)/B = 0.037$. The localised scale
   $r^*/B = 0.0032$ is twelve times smaller.
4. **The theorem's bound.** With $x = \ln(1/\delta) = 3.0$ and $K$ close to 1,
   $704\,r^*/B + x(11 \times 2 + 26 \times 2)/m = 2.26 + 0.022 = 2.28$. An excess risk is at most 1, so this bound is
   vacuous.
5. **Sample size for excess risk 0.01.** Every term scales as $1/m$, so the bound reaches 0.01 at about
   $m = 2.28$ million.

The same problem has a direct bound of 0.0066 at $m = 10{,}000$, and needs $m \approx 6{,}600$ for 0.01, from
<NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">Bernstein's inequality and a union bound</NoteLink>. Both bounds fall as
$1/m$. The factor of 350 between them comes from the constant 704, which arises from splitting the class into shells and
from Talagrand's inequality. Localisation is worth its constants for infinite classes, where no
<NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> applies, and for classes whose fixed point is far below the global
complexity, such as kernel classes.

**A kernel class.** With eigenvalues $\lambda_j = j^{-2}$ ($s = 1$), the fixed point of
$\psi(r) = \paren{\frac2m\sum_j\min\set{r, \lambda_j}}^{1/2}$ is $r^* = 0.0054$ at $m = 10{,}000$ and $0.00025$ at
$m = 10^6$. A hundredfold increase in $m$ divides $r^*$ by 21, close to $100^{2/3} = 21.5$. The global complexity
bound $1/\sqrt m$ falls only tenfold, from 0.01 to 0.001.

## Pitfalls

- **Localisation needs a variance condition.** Without $\var(g) \le B\,Pg$, the fixed point is of the order of the
  global complexity and nothing is gained. The condition is where noise assumptions or the curvature of the loss enter.
- **The ball is around the best function, not around zero.** Localising the loss class itself around 0 controls
  $P\ell_h$, not the excess risk, and gives the slow rate whenever the best risk in the class is positive
  <Cite id="bartlett2005" />.
- **The constants are large.** The general theorem's constants make it vacuous at sample sizes where simpler
  arguments are not, as the worked example shows. Its value lies in the rate and in the data-dependent version.
- **The fixed point is of a bound, not of the complexity.** The theorem needs a sub-root upper bound $\psi$, and $r^*$
  depends on which bound is used.
