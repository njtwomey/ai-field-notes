<Definition>
Complexity regularisation is the part of <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">learning theory</NoteLink> that
chooses the complexity of a model from the data and bounds the risk of the choice. It ranks candidate classes,
hypotheses or procedures by a measure of complexity, penalises the <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical
risk</NoteLink> accordingly, and proves that the selected model is nearly as good as the best candidate an oracle would
pick <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" /> <Cite id="massart2007" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every generalisation bound in the chapter is stated for a class fixed in advance. In practice the class is not fixed:
the analyst chooses a polynomial degree, a tree size, a number of hidden units or a regularisation strength after
looking at the data. A small class underfits and a large one overfits, and the right size depends on the unknown
distribution. This is the
<NoteLink to="approximation-estimation-optimisation-trade-off">approximation–estimation trade-off</NoteLink>.

Complexity regularisation turns bounds into selection rules and gives the selection its own guarantee. The same ideas
justify the penalty term of <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>, the choice of a hyperparameter by
a <NoteLink to="cross-validation">validation set</NoteLink>, and model-selection criteria such as the
<NoteLink to="information-criteria">Bayesian information criterion</NoteLink>. They also explain when a class of
infinite capacity, such as all polynomials, can still be learned.

## Main ideas

**Penalise by the bound.** <NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">Structural risk minimisation</NoteLink> orders
the classes $\Hcal_1 \subset \Hcal_2 \subset \cdots$, gives class $k$ a share $\delta\pi_k$ of the failure
probability, and picks the hypothesis that minimises training error plus its class's generalisation bound. The bound
that drives the choice also certifies it. Not knowing the right class in advance adds to the bound a term
$\ln(1/\pi_k)$, which grows slowly. A countable union of classes of finite VC dimension is learnable this way, with a
sample size that depends on the hypothesis competed with.

**Compare with an oracle.** An <NoteLink to="oracle-inequalities">oracle inequality</NoteLink> bounds the risk of a
data-driven choice by the best risk among the candidates plus a remainder. Hold-out selection among $M$ candidates has
a remainder of order $\sqrt{\ln M/n}$ for a validation set of $n$ examples, with no capacity term. An inequality with
leading constant 1 bounds the excess risk over the best candidate, and one with a larger leading constant does not.

**Mix rather than select.** For the <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink>, any rule that returns one
of the candidates has a worst-case remainder of order $\sqrt{\ln M/n}$. Exponentially weighted mixtures of the
candidates achieve $(\ln M)/n$, because the average of two predictors loses less than the worse of them. Aggregation is
the oracle-inequality view of <NoteLink to="hedge-and-exponential-weights">exponential weights</NoteLink>.

**Count bits.** <NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">Minimum description length</NoteLink> scores a model by the
length of the shortest code for the data that uses it. A two-part code charges $L(h)$ bits for the hypothesis and
$-\log_2 P(D \mid h)$ for the data, which is the trade-off of the <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink> in
code lengths. For $k$ real parameters the first part costs about $\frac k2\log_2 n$ bits, the penalty of the Bayesian
information criterion. Refined minimum description length replaces the two parts with the normalised maximum likelihood
code, whose penalty is the parametric complexity of the model class <Cite id="grunwald2007" />.

## How the notes fit together

- **Start with** <NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural risk minimisation</NoteLink>. It needs the
  <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink>, whose weighted union bound it applies to classes, and the
  <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC bound</NoteLink> for each class.
- <NoteLink to="oracle-inequalities">Oracle inequalities</NoteLink> generalise the guarantee of structural risk
  minimisation to any selection or aggregation rule. Hold-out selection is the simplest case and rests only on the
  <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bound</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink>.
- <NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">Minimum description length</NoteLink> is the information-theoretic form of
  the same trade-off. It can be read independently after the Occam bound and the
  <NoteLink to="source-coding-theorem">source coding theorem</NoteLink>.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The sub-chapter assumes the finite-class and Occam bounds of <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC
learning</NoteLink> and the capacity measures of <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence-and-capacity">uniform convergence and
capacity</NoteLink>. Oracle inequalities with fast remainders, of order $1/m$ rather than $1/\sqrt m$, need the
variance-sensitive tools of <NoteLink to="rates-and-consistency">rates and consistency</NoteLink>, in particular
<NoteLink to="local-rademacher-complexity">local Rademacher complexity</NoteLink>. Data-dependent penalties, such as the
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">empirical Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> or a margin, lead to
<NoteLink to="margins-and-norms">margins and norms</NoteLink>. On the practical side,
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information
criteria</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> in the model-selection notes are the
procedures this sub-chapter analyses. Overparameterised models that fit their training data exactly fall outside the
penalised picture; <NoteLink to="bias-variance-and-interpolation">bias–variance and interpolation</NoteLink> covers
them.
