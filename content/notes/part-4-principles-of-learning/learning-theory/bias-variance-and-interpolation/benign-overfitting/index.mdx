<Definition>
A predictor **interpolates** when it fits every training example exactly, noise included. Its overfitting is **benign**
when its <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">excess risk</NoteLink> over the best predictor nevertheless tends to
zero as the sample size $n$ grows. For the minimum-norm interpolant in <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear
regression</NoteLink> with covariance $\Sigmamat$, eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots$, Bartlett, Long,
Lugosi and Tsigler characterised benign overfitting through two **effective ranks**

$$
r_k(\Sigmamat) = \frac{\sum_{i > k}\lambda_i}{\lambda_{k+1}}, \qquad
R_k(\Sigmamat) = \frac{\paren{\sum_{i > k}\lambda_i}^2}{\sum_{i > k}\lambda_i^2}.
$$

With $k^* = \min\set{k \ge 0 : r_k(\Sigmamat) \ge bn}$ for a constant $b > 1$, overfitting is benign when $r_0/n$,
$k^*/n$ and $n/R_{k^*}$ all tend to zero <Cite id="bartlett2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The classical argument against interpolation is that a function passing through every noisy target must bend to fit
the noise, and the bending shows up as error on new inputs. The <NoteLink to="double-descent">double descent</NoteLink>
curve already shows that this need not happen when the model has many more parameters than examples. Benign
overfitting asks when the error of the interpolant goes all the way down to the noise level.

The answer for linear regression is a division of labour among the directions of the input space. A few directions with
large variance carry the signal, and the interpolant estimates them roughly as least squares would. Very many
directions with tiny variance carry almost no signal. The interpolant uses them to fit the noise: each noisy residual
is absorbed by a small coefficient spread over thousands of these directions. Because each of those directions has
tiny variance, the part of the fit that absorbs the noise contributes little to the prediction at a new input.

Two things can go wrong. With too few small directions, the noise cannot be spread thinly enough and the variance stays
large. With too much total variance in the small directions, they also absorb the signal, and the estimate of the large
directions is shrunk towards zero. The effective ranks measure exactly these two failures.

## The minimum-norm interpolant

Let $\xvec \in \reals^p$ (or a Hilbert space) have mean zero and covariance $\Sigmamat$, and let
$y = \xvec^\top\thetavec^* + \varepsilon$ with noise of variance $\sigma^2$, independent of $\xvec$. Stack $n$ training
inputs as the rows of $\Xmat$, with $p > n$ and $\Xmat$ of full row rank. The minimum-norm interpolant is

$$
\hat\thetavec = \Xmat^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-1}\yvec,
$$

the solution that <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">gradient descent from zero</NoteLink> finds. Its excess risk is
$R(\hat\thetavec) = \expect[(y - \xvec^\top\hat\thetavec)^2] - \sigma^2 =
(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec^*)^\top\Sigmamat(\hat\thetavec - \thetavec^*)$.

Write $\Pmat = \Xmat^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-1}\Xmat$ for the projection onto the row space and substitute
$\yvec = \Xmat\thetavec^* + \varepsilonvec$, where $\varepsilonvec$ holds the $n$ noise terms:

$$
\hat\thetavec - \thetavec^* = -(\eye - \Pmat)\thetavec^* + \Xmat^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-1}\varepsilonvec.
$$

The first term is the part of $\thetavec^*$ the data cannot see; the second is the fitted noise. Averaging over the
noise, the cross term vanishes and the excess risk splits into a bias and a variance, as in the
<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink>:

$$
B = \thetavec^{*\top}(\eye - \Pmat)\Sigmamat(\eye - \Pmat)\thetavec^*, \qquad
V = \sigma^2\trace\paren{(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-2}\Xmat\Sigmamat\Xmat^\top}.
$$

## How the tail acts as a ridge

The mechanism becomes visible when the coordinates are split at an index $k$, in the eigenbasis of $\Sigmamat$. Write
$\Xmat = [\Xmat_{\le k}, \Xmat_{> k}]$ for the first $k$ coordinates and the rest. Then

$$
\Xmat\Xmat^\top = \Xmat_{\le k}\Xmat_{\le k}^\top + \Xmat_{> k}\Xmat_{> k}^\top.
$$

1. **The tail Gram matrix is nearly a multiple of the identity.** Entry $(i, j)$ of $\Xmat_{> k}\Xmat_{> k}^\top$ is a
   sum over the tail coordinates of $x_{il}x_{jl}$. On the diagonal its mean is $\sum_{l > k}\lambda_l$. Off the
   diagonal its mean is zero. When the tail has many coordinates of comparable size, both concentrate, and
   $\Xmat_{> k}\Xmat_{> k}^\top \approx \lambda_{\text{eff}}\eye_n$ with $\lambda_{\text{eff}} = \sum_{l > k}\lambda_l$.
   The condition $r_k \ge bn$ says that the tail has enough effective dimensions, compared with $n$, for this to hold.
2. **The head is fitted by ridge regression.** The first $k$ coordinates of $\hat\thetavec$ are $\Xmat_{\le k}^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-1}\yvec \approx \Xmat_{\le k}^\top(\Xmat_{\le k}\Xmat_{\le k}^\top +
   \lambda_{\text{eff}}\eye)^{-1}\yvec$, which is <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> on the
   head with penalty $\lambda_{\text{eff}}$. Its variance is about $\sigma^2 k/n$, as for least squares with $k$
   parameters. Its bias is small when $\lambda_{\text{eff}}$ is small compared with $n\lambda_i$ for the directions $i$
   that carry the signal; $r_0 \ll n$ guarantees this for the largest one.
3. **The tail absorbs the noise.** The tail coordinates of $\hat\thetavec$ are about
   $\Xmat_{> k}^\top\yvec/\lambda_{\text{eff}}$. Their contribution to the variance is about
   $\sigma^2\trace(\Xmat_{> k}\Sigmamat_{> k}\Xmat_{> k}^\top)/\lambda_{\text{eff}}^2 =
   \sigma^2 n\sum_{l > k}\lambda_l^2/(\sum_{l > k}\lambda_l)^2 = \sigma^2 n/R_k$. It is small when $R_k \gg n$: many
   tail directions of similar size.

The tail does two jobs at once. It interpolates the noise at a variance cost of $n/R_k$, and it regularises the head
like a ridge penalty of size $\lambda_{\text{eff}}$. This is the heuristic behind the theorem; the proof makes each
approximation precise.

## When overfitting is benign

**Theorem** <Cite id="bartlett2020" />. Under sub-Gaussian assumptions on $\xvec$ and the noise, there are constants
$b, c > 1$ such that, for $\delta < 1$ with $\ln(1/\delta) < n/c$: if $k^* \ge n/c$ then
$\expect R(\hat\thetavec) \ge \sigma^2/c$; otherwise, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$
R(\hat\thetavec) \le c\norm{\thetavec^*}^2\norm{\Sigmamat}\max\set{\sqrt{\frac{r_0}{n}}, \frac{r_0}{n},
\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}} + c\ln(1/\delta)\,\sigma_y^2\paren{\frac{k^*}{n} + \frac{n}{R_{k^*}}},
$$

and $\expect R(\hat\thetavec) \ge \frac{\sigma^2}{c}\paren{\frac{k^*}{n} + \frac{n}{R_{k^*}}}$, where $\sigma_y^2$ is
the sub-Gaussian parameter of the noise. The variance term is sharp up to constants.

The three ratios read directly from the mechanism. $r_0/n$ small means the total variance is small compared with $n$
times the largest eigenvalue, so the ridge implied by the tail does not swamp the head. $k^*/n$ small means only a few
directions need to be estimated as in least squares. $n/R_{k^*}$ small means the tail has many more effective
dimensions than there are examples.

Two families of spectra show how narrow the condition is <Cite id="bartlett2020" />:

- **Infinite dimension, fixed covariance.** For $\lambda_k = k^{-\alpha}\ln^{-\beta}(k + 1)$, overfitting is benign if
  and only if $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta > 1$: the eigenvalues must decay just fast enough to have a finite sum.
- **Finite dimension with an isotropic floor.** For $\lambda_k = \gamma_k + \varepsilon_n$ on $p_n$ coordinates, with
  $\gamma_k$ decaying exponentially, overfitting is benign if and only if $p_n$ grows faster than $n$ and the total
  floor $\varepsilon_n p_n$ is $o(n)$ but not exponentially small. High dimension with a small, flat tail is the generic
  benign case.

## Worked example

Take $n = 100$ examples, noise $\sigma = 1$, and a signal $\thetavec^*$ of norm 1 spread over five directions of
variance 1. Predicting zero has excess risk $\thetavec^{*\top}\Sigmamat\thetavec^* = 1$. Least squares on the five true
directions alone, an oracle that knows where the signal is, has excess risk about $\sigma^2 \cdot 5/(100 - 5 - 1) =
0.053$. The tail varies across four cases, and the excess risks are averages over 40 simulated training sets.

| Tail | $r_0$ | $r_5$ | $R_5$ | Excess risk | Bias | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10,000 directions of variance 0.001 | 15 | 10,000 | 10,000 | **0.071** | 0.009 | 0.065 |
| 200 directions of variance 0.05 | 15 | 200 | 200 | 0.98 | 0.005 | 0.98 |
| 10,000 directions of variance 0.1 | 1,005 | 10,000 | 10,000 | 0.84 | 0.83 | 0.010 |
| isotropic: all 2,000 directions variance 1 | 2,000 | 1,995 | 1,995 | 1.00 | 0.95 | 0.053 |

1. **The benign case.** The first tail has total variance $\lambda_{\text{eff}} = 10$. The three ratios are
   $r_0/n = 0.15$, $k^*/n = 0.05$ (with $k^* = 5$ for any $b$ between 1 and 100) and $n/R_5 = 0.01$. The interpolant
   fits all 100 noisy targets exactly, and its excess risk, 0.071, is close to the oracle's 0.053. The bias matches step
   2 of the mechanism: a ridge of 10 on the head shrinks it by a factor $100/110$, and $(1 - 100/110)^2 = 0.008$.
2. **Too few tail directions.** The second tail has the same total variance but only $R_5 = 200 = 2n$ effective
   dimensions. The noise cannot be spread thinly, and the variance, 0.98, is as large as the signal. The approximation
   of step 1 also fails here, so the measured variance exceeds the heuristic $n/R_5 = 0.5$.
3. **Too heavy a tail.** The third tail has total variance 1,000, so $r_0/n \approx 10$. Its implied ridge of 1,000
   shrinks the head by $100/1{,}100$, and the bias is $(1 - 0.091)^2 = 0.83$, as measured. The noise is absorbed well,
   variance 0.010, but the signal is absorbed too.
4. **Isotropic features.** With every direction alike, the interpolant cannot tell signal from tail. It recovers a
   fraction $n/p = 0.05$ of $\thetavec^*$ and its bias is about $1 - n/p = 0.95$, as the
   <NoteLink to="double-descent">double descent</NoteLink> formula predicts.

The theorem's constants $b$ and $c$ are not given explicitly, so the bound cannot be evaluated to a number. It decides
which cases are benign as $n$ grows, and the table shows the same ordering at $n = 100$.

## Beyond linear regression

- **Ridge and kernels.** Tsigler and Bartlett removed the independence assumption, gave sharp bounds on the bias, and
  covered ridge regression and kernel regression. They found conditions under which the optimal ridge penalty is
  negative: the tail already over-regularises the head, and a negative penalty undoes some of it
  <Cite id="tsigler2023" />.
- **Harmless interpolation.** Muthukumar, Vodrahalli, Subramanian and Sahai showed that the error of fitting pure noise
  decays to zero with the number of features, and named two failure modes: signal "bleeding" into many alias features,
  and noise overfitted by sparse feature selectors <Cite id="muthukumar2020" />.
- **Benign, tempered, catastrophic.** Many interpolating methods are neither benign nor catastrophic. Kernels with
  power-law spectra, including the Laplace kernel and the <Gloss name="relu" /> <Gloss name="ntk">neural tangent
  kernel</Gloss>, overfit in a **tempered** way: noise in the training set causes a bounded but nonzero excess risk.
  Deep networks trained to interpolation behave the same way empirically, while those stopped early are closer to benign
  <Cite id="mallinar2022" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Benign is not optimal.** In the worked example the interpolant's 0.071 is worse than the oracle's 0.053, and a
  tuned ridge would be at least as good as the interpolant. Benign overfitting says that interpolation costs little,
  not that it helps.
- **The spectrum is the whole story.** The same estimator is benign, high-variance or high-bias depending only on the
  covariance. Isotropic features in high dimension are not benign.
- **Asymptotic and in high dimension.** The definition is a limit along a sequence of problems with $n \to \infty$ and
  the covariance changing with $n$. A fixed covariance in infinite dimension is benign only for a narrow band of
  eigenvalue decay.
- **<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">Uniform convergence</NoteLink> bounds can be vacuous here.** The interpolant's
  training error is zero and its risk is small, but bounds that hold uniformly over a class containing it can be vacuous
  in this regime (see <NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">limits of uniform convergence</NoteLink>).
