<Definition>
Bias–variance and interpolation is the part of <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">learning theory</NoteLink>
that explains test error through the spread of learned predictors over training sets, and that studies predictors with
zero training error on noisy data. Its classical result is the decomposition of squared error into noise, squared bias
and variance <Cite id="geman1992" />. Its modern results describe when and why interpolating models, from minimum-norm
least squares to wide neural networks, still generalise <Cite id="belkin2019" /> <Cite id="bartlett2021" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

The classical account of overfitting says that test error follows a U against model size: bias falls, variance rises,
and the best model sits in between. Penalised selection, as in <NoteLink to="complexity-regularisation">complexity
regularisation</NoteLink>, aims for the bottom of the U. Modern practice contradicts the picture. Neural networks with
far more parameters than examples fit their training data exactly, can fit random labels too, and still predict well
on real labels. Capacity-based bounds are vacuous for them.

These notes reconcile the two. They keep the decomposition, which always holds, and replace the assumption that
variance must grow with the number of parameters. The answer runs through the learning algorithm: which of the many
interpolating solutions it returns, and how the structure of the data lets that solution fit noise without harm.

## Main ideas

**Error splits into bias, variance and noise.** For <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink>, the
expected test error at an input is the noise variance plus the squared distance of the average predictor from the truth
plus the spread of the predictors around their average. Rigid procedures have high bias, flexible ones high variance
(<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink>).

**Test error can fall again past the interpolation threshold.** As the number of parameters $p$ passes the number of
examples $n$, the minimum-norm least-squares fit's test error peaks and then decreases, because more parameters allow
smaller-norm interpolants (<NoteLink to="double-descent">double descent</NoteLink>). A tuned ridge penalty removes the
peak.

**The optimiser chooses the interpolant.** Gradient descent from zero on underdetermined least squares returns the
minimum-norm solution, and on separable <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> it converges
in direction to the maximum-margin separator. The bias of the algorithm, not the size of the class, sets the effective
capacity (<NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink>).

**Interpolation can be harmless.** The minimum-norm interpolant in <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear
regression</NoteLink> has near-optimal risk when the input covariance has a few large directions for the signal and very
many small ones that absorb the noise. Two effective ranks of the covariance decide the question exactly
(<NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink>).

**Wide networks behave like kernels.** In the infinite-width limit with the NTK scaling, a network's parameter
gradients act as fixed features, and training on squared loss is kernel regression with the
<NoteLink to="neural-tangent-kernel">neural tangent kernel</NoteLink>. The limit learns no features, which practical
networks do, so it explains part of deep learning, not all of it.

**The geometry of the minimum and the limits of bounds.** <NoteLink to="flat-minima-and-sharpness">Flat
minima</NoteLink> admit broad <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes</NoteLink> posteriors and correlate with
generalisation in many studies, but rescaling a <Gloss name="relu" /> network changes sharpness without changing the
function. For some learners every <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink> bound over the
hypotheses they might output is vacuous although they generalise well
(<NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">limits of uniform convergence</NoteLink>), so algorithm-dependent
arguments are needed.

## How the notes fit together

- **Start with** the <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink>. It needs only
  expectations and variances.
- <NoteLink to="double-descent">Double descent</NoteLink> derives the minimum-norm interpolant and its test error in a
  Gaussian model. It is the entry point for the rest.
- <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">Implicit regularisation</NoteLink> explains why gradient descent returns the
  minimum-norm or maximum-margin solution; <NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink> explains when
  that solution generalises. They can be read in either order.
- The <NoteLink to="neural-tangent-kernel">neural tangent kernel</NoteLink> carries the linear results to wide networks.
  <NoteLink to="flat-minima-and-sharpness">Flat minima and sharpness</NoteLink> and the
  <NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">limits of uniform convergence</NoteLink> examine two candidate
  explanations of generalisation in deep networks and where each fails.
