<Definition>
**Implicit regularisation** is the preference of an optimisation algorithm for particular solutions of an objective with
many minimisers, or with none, when no penalty term asks for it. Two results are exact.
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on underdetermined least squares, started at $\wvec_0$,
converges to $\Xmat^{+}\yvec + (\eye - \Xmat^{+}\Xmat)\wvec_0$, which is the minimum-norm solution when
$\wvec_0 = \zeros$. Gradient descent on the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">logistic loss</NoteLink> of linearly
separable data diverges in norm, and its direction converges to that of the hard-margin <Gloss name="svm">support vector
machine</Gloss> <Cite id="soudry2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

An overparameterised model has many settings of its parameters that fit the training data exactly. Neural networks can
even fit random labels <Cite id="zhang2017" />. The training loss cannot tell these solutions apart, yet they predict
differently on new inputs. Which one is returned is decided by the algorithm: its starting point, its update rule and
its step size. The algorithm acts as a regulariser that was never written down.

Neyshabur, Tomioka and Srebro made this argument for neural networks. Networks with more hidden units than needed to
fit the data generalised better, not worse, so the capacity that controlled generalisation was not the number of
parameters. They proposed that it was a norm, kept small by the optimiser <Cite id="neyshabur2015b" />. The linear
results below make that proposal exact. They identify the effective class the algorithm searches, a small norm ball or
a large margin, so that the norm-based and margin-based bounds of the
<NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink> can be applied to the
algorithm's output rather than to the whole class.

## Least squares

Let $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ hold $n$ examples of $p > n$ features, with full row rank $n$, and let
$\yvec \in \reals^n$ hold the targets. The loss $L(\wvec) = \frac12\norm{\Xmat\wvec - \yvec}^2$ has a whole affine
subspace of minimisers with $L = 0$. Gradient descent with step size $\eta$ updates

$$
\wvec_{t+1} = \wvec_t - \eta\,\Xmat^\top(\Xmat\wvec_t - \yvec).
$$

The limit follows in three steps.

1. **The updates stay in the row space.** Every update is $\Xmat^\top$ times a vector of length $n$, so it lies in the
   row space of $\Xmat$. Let $\Pmat = \Xmat^{+}\Xmat$ be the <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal
   projection</NoteLink> onto the row space, where $\Xmat^{+}$ is the
   <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">pseudoinverse</NoteLink>. Then $(\eye - \Pmat)\wvec_t =
   (\eye - \Pmat)\wvec_0$ for every $t$: the component of the iterate in the null space of $\Xmat$ never changes.
2. **The residual vanishes.** The residual $\rvec_t = \Xmat\wvec_t - \yvec$ obeys
   $\rvec_{t+1} = (\eye - \eta\Xmat\Xmat^\top)\rvec_t$. The eigenvalues of $\Xmat\Xmat^\top$ are the squared singular
   values $s_1^2 \ge \dots \ge s_n^2 > 0$. For $0 < \eta < 2/s_1^2$ every eigenvalue of $\eye - \eta\Xmat\Xmat^\top$
   lies in $(-1, 1)$, so $\rvec_t \to \zeros$ and the iterates converge to an interpolating solution.
3. **Identify the limit.** Every interpolating solution is $\Xmat^{+}\yvec$ plus a vector of the null space, because
   $\Xmat^{+}\yvec$ is the only interpolant in the row space (see the
   <NoteLink to="double-descent">double descent</NoteLink> note). By step 1 the limit's null-space component is
   $(\eye - \Pmat)\wvec_0$. Therefore

   $$
   \wvec_\infty = \Xmat^{+}\yvec + (\eye - \Xmat^{+}\Xmat)\wvec_0.
   $$

Started at $\wvec_0 = \zeros$, gradient descent returns the minimum-norm interpolant. Started elsewhere, it returns the
minimum-norm interpolant plus the component of $\wvec_0$ in the null space of $\Xmat$. The same argument holds for
<Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>, whose updates are also multiples of rows of $\Xmat$. It fails for
adaptive methods such as Adam, which rescale each coordinate and leave the row space; on some underdetermined problems
they converge to solutions that generalise worse <Cite id="wilson2017" />.

**Early stopping is a soft version of the minimum-norm limit.** Write $\Xmat = \Umat\Smat\Vmat^\top$ in its
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink>. From $\wvec_0 = \zeros$, the
component of $\wvec_t$ along the right singular vector $\vvec_i$ is

$$
\paren{1 - (1 - \eta s_i^2)^t}\frac{\uvec_i^\top\yvec}{s_i}, \qquad \text{while ridge gives} \qquad
\frac{s_i^2}{s_i^2 + \lambda}\,\frac{\uvec_i^\top\yvec}{s_i}.
$$

Both multiply the minimum-norm solution's components by filter factors between 0 and 1 that are small for small
singular values. Stopping after $t$ steps acts roughly like a ridge penalty $\lambda \approx 1/(\eta t)$
(<NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink>).

## Worked example: one equation, two unknowns

Take one example $\xvec = (1, 2)$ with target $y = 5$, so the solutions of $\xvec^\top\wvec = 5$ form a line in the
plane. Take $\eta = 0.1$.

1. **The minimum-norm solution.** $\wvec^* = \xvec\,y/\norm{\xvec}^2 = (1, 2)$, with $\norm{\wvec^*} = 2.236$. It is the
   point of the line nearest the origin.
2. **The contraction.** The residual is multiplied by $1 - \eta\norm{\xvec}^2 = 0.5$ at every step. From the origin,
   $\wvec_t = (1 - 0.5^t)\wvec^*$: the iterates walk straight along $\xvec$ and close half the remaining gap each step.
3. **A different start.** From $\wvec_0 = (2, 0)$, the component along the line, in the direction
   $(-2, 1)/\sqrt5$, is $(1.6, -0.8)$. It is kept, so the limit is $(1, 2) + (1.6, -0.8) = (2.6, 1.2)$, with norm
   2.864. It fits the example exactly and is 1.789 away from $\wvec^*$.
4. **Early stopping against ridge.** Ridge gives $\wvec_\lambda = \frac{5}{5 + \lambda}\wvec^*$. Gradient descent from
   the origin matches it exactly when $1 - 0.5^t = 5/(5 + \lambda)$: $\lambda = 5$ after one step, 1.67 after two, 0.71
   after three and 0.16 after five.

## Logistic loss on separable data

Let the data be linearly separable through the origin, and absorb the labels $y_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ into the inputs,
$\zvec_i = y_i\xvec_i$, so that a separator has $\wvec^\top\zvec_i > 0$ for all $i$. The logistic loss
$L(\wvec) = \sum_i\ln(1 + e^{-\wvec^\top\zvec_i})$ is positive everywhere and tends to 0 along every separating
direction as $\norm{\wvec} \to \infty$. It has no minimiser, and gradient descent sends $\norm{\wvec_t}$ to infinity.

Let $\hat\wvec = \argmin\set{\norm{\wvec}^2 : \wvec^\top\zvec_i \ge 1 \text{ for all } i}$ be the hard-margin
<NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> without bias. Soudry, Hoffer, Nacson, Gunasekar
and Srebro proved that, for a small enough constant step size,

$$
\wvec_t = \hat\wvec\,\ln t + \rhovec_t,
$$

where $\norm{\rhovec_t}$ grows at most like $\ln\ln t$ <Cite id="soudry2018" />. The direction
$\wvec_t/\norm{\wvec_t}$ converges to $\hat\wvec/\norm{\hat\wvec}$, but only at the rate $1/\ln t$.

**Why the maximum margin.** Once $\norm{\wvec_t}$ is large, every term of the gradient,
$-\sum_i\zvec_i/(1 + e^{\wvec_t^\top\zvec_i}) \approx -\sum_i e^{-\wvec_t^\top\zvec_i}\zvec_i$, is weighted by a factor
exponentially small in the point's margin. The points with the smallest margin dominate, and the gradient becomes a
non-negative combination of them. The <NoteLink to="karush-kuhn-tucker-conditions">Karush–Kuhn–Tucker
conditions</NoteLink> of the hard-margin problem say the same of $\hat\wvec$: it is a non-negative combination of the
support vectors, the points with the smallest margin. A direction that is a fixed point of gradient descent must
satisfy those conditions.

**Numbers.** Take $\zvec_1 = (1, 2)$, $\zvec_2 = (2, 0.5)$ and $\zvec_3 = (5, -0.5)$. The first two are the support
vectors, and $\hat\wvec = (3/7, 2/7)$ gives the largest normalised margin, $1/\norm{\hat\wvec} = 1.941$. Gradient
descent on the mean loss with $\eta = 0.1$, started at the origin, first moves along the mean of the $\zvec_i$, which
points $19.7°$ away from $\hat\wvec$. Its angle to $\hat\wvec$ is $11.4°$ after 10 steps, $2.2°$ after 100 and $1.0°$
after 1,000. It then overshoots slightly and is still $1.9°$ away after a million steps. Each tenfold increase in $t$
adds 1.18 to $\norm{\wvec_t}$, close to $\norm{\hat\wvec}\ln 10 = 1.19$, as the $\hat\wvec\ln t$ term predicts.

Training past zero training error keeps moving the classifier towards the maximum margin, even while the validation loss
rises, so the training loss is a poor stopping signal for classification <Cite id="soudry2018" />.

On non-separable data the iterates converge in direction on the separable part of the data and to a finite solution on
the rest <Cite id="ji2019" />.

## Beyond linear models

- **Other optimisers, other geometries.** <NoteLink to="mirror-descent">Mirror descent</NoteLink> with a potential
  $\psi$ converges, on underdetermined <NoteLink to="linear-regression">linear regression</NoteLink>, to the interpolant
  with the smallest <NoteLink to="bregman-divergences-and-scoring-rules">Bregman divergence</NoteLink> from the start,
  whatever the step size. On separable classification, steepest descent with respect to a norm tends to the
  maximum-margin direction in that norm <Cite id="gunasekar2018" />.
- **Matrix factorisation.** Gradient descent on a factorised matrix $\Umat\Vmat^\top$ from a small start fits observed
  entries with low-rank solutions. Gunasekar et al. conjectured that it minimises the nuclear norm
  <Cite id="gunasekar2017" />. For deeper factorisations the bias towards low rank is stronger than any norm
  <Cite id="arora2019" />.
- **Initial scale.** For diagonal linear networks $w_j = u_j^2 - v_j^2$, a large initialisation gives the minimum
  $\ell_2$-norm solution (the kernel regime) and a small one approaches the minimum $\ell_1$-norm solution (the rich
  regime) <Cite id="woodworth2020" />. The kernel regime is the regime of the
  <NoteLink to="neural-tangent-kernel">neural tangent kernel</NoteLink>.
- **Homogeneous networks.** For <Gloss name="relu" /> networks without biases trained on the logistic loss, the
  normalised margin increases once the training loss is small, and it converges to a Karush–Kuhn–Tucker point of a
  maximum-margin problem over the parameters <Cite id="lyu2020" />.
- **Stochasticity and step size.** With a small finite step size, the mean iterate of stochastic gradient descent with
  random shuffling follows gradient flow on a modified loss: the original loss plus a penalty on the norms of the
  minibatch gradients. For small batches the penalty scales with the ratio of step size to batch size
  <Cite id="smith2021" />. It favours regions where the loss is flat (see
  <NoteLink to="flat-minima-and-sharpness">flat minima and sharpness</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **The limit depends on the start.** The least-squares limit keeps the null-space part of $\wvec_0$. A large random
  initialisation is not a small-norm start, and the solution's norm includes it.
- **The optimiser matters.** Results for gradient descent do not transfer to adaptive methods, momentum variants or
  <NoteLink to="second-order-methods">second-order methods</NoteLink> without a new argument.
- **Which solution, not why it generalises.** Implicit regularisation names the solution. A bound still has to show
  that small-norm or large-margin solutions generalise, for example a <NoteLink to="margin-bounds">margin
  bound</NoteLink> or a <NoteLink to="norm-based-bounds-for-neural-networks">norm-based bound</NoteLink>. For some
  overparameterised problems, the minimum-norm interpolant itself generalises poorly
  (<NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink> describes when it does not).
- **Limits are slow.** The maximum-margin direction is reached at rate $1/\ln t$. A network trained for a finite number
  of steps sits far from the limit the theory describes.
