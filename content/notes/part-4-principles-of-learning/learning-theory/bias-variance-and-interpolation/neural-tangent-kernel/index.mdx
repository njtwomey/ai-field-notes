<Definition>
Let $f(\xvec; \thetavec)$ be a network with scalar output and parameters $\thetavec \in \reals^P$. Its **neural
tangent kernel** (<Gloss name="ntk" />) at $\thetavec$ is

$$
\Theta_{\thetavec}(\xvec, \xvec') = \nabla_{\thetavec} f(\xvec; \thetavec)^\top\nabla_{\thetavec} f(\xvec'; \thetavec).
$$

Under gradient flow on a loss of the training outputs, the outputs move according to this kernel. Jacot, Gabriel and
Hongler showed that, as the widths of the hidden layers tend to infinity under the NTK parameterisation, the kernel at
initialisation converges to a deterministic limit $\Theta^\infty$ and stays equal to it throughout training. Training
the network on the <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> is then kernel regression with
$\Theta^\infty$ <Cite id="jacot2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A gradient step on one training example changes every parameter a little, and through them it changes the network's
output at every input. The neural tangent kernel measures that spill-over: $\Theta(\xvec, \xvec_i)$ is how much the
output at $\xvec$ moves when the parameters take a small step that raises the output at $\xvec_i$. Inputs whose
gradients point in similar directions move together.

In a very wide network each parameter has a tiny effect on the output. Fitting the data needs only a tiny change in
each parameter, so the gradients, and with them the kernel, barely change during training. The network then behaves
like its first-order Taylor expansion around the initial parameters,

$$
f(\xvec; \thetavec) \approx f(\xvec; \thetavec_0) + \nabla_{\thetavec} f(\xvec; \thetavec_0)^\top(\thetavec - \thetavec_0),
$$

a model that is linear in $\thetavec$ with fixed features $\phivec(\xvec) = \nabla_{\thetavec} f(\xvec; \thetavec_0)$
<Cite id="lee2019" />. A linear model with fixed features is a kernel method, and the kernel of these features is the
NTK. Training is "lazy": the network fits the data without learning new features.

The NTK connects deep learning to kernel methods. In the kernel regime, the
<NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink> of <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> is the minimum norm in the kernel's <NoteLink to="reproducing-kernel-hilbert-space">reproducing
kernel Hilbert space</NoteLink>, and generalisation can be analysed with kernel tools.

## The dynamics in function space

Let the training set be $(\xvec_i, y_i)_{i=1}^n$ and the loss $L(\thetavec) = \frac12\sum_i(f(\xvec_i; \thetavec) -
y_i)^2$. Gradient flow is $d\thetavec/dt = -\nabla_{\thetavec} L$. Write $f_t(\xvec) = f(\xvec; \thetavec_t)$ and
$\Theta_t = \Theta_{\thetavec_t}$.

1. **Parameter velocity.** By the chain rule, $\dfrac{d\thetavec}{dt} = -\sum_i \nabla_{\thetavec} f(\xvec_i;
   \thetavec_t)\,(f_t(\xvec_i) - y_i)$.
2. **Output velocity.** The output at any input $\xvec$ changes at the rate
   $\nabla_{\thetavec} f(\xvec; \thetavec_t)^\top d\thetavec/dt$. Substituting step 1 gives

   $$
   \frac{d f_t(\xvec)}{dt} = -\sum_{i=1}^n \Theta_t(\xvec, \xvec_i)\,(f_t(\xvec_i) - y_i).
   $$

   This holds exactly for any network. The kernel is all that links the parameters to the outputs.
3. **A constant kernel gives a linear equation.** Suppose $\Theta_t = \Theta$ for all $t$. Stack the training residuals
   into $\uvec_t = f_t(\Xmat) - \yvec$ and write $\Kmat = \Theta(\Xmat, \Xmat)$ for the $n \times n$ kernel matrix.
   Step 2 at the training inputs reads $d\uvec_t/dt = -\Kmat\uvec_t$, so

   $$
   \uvec_t = e^{-\Kmat t}\uvec_0.
   $$

   Along each eigenvector of $\Kmat$ the residual decays at the rate of its eigenvalue. If the smallest eigenvalue is
   positive, the training loss goes to zero exponentially fast.
4. **The prediction.** Integrating step 2 at a new input with $\uvec_t$ from step 3 gives

   $$
   f_t(\xvec) = f_0(\xvec) - \Theta(\xvec, \Xmat)\Kmat^{-1}(\eye - e^{-\Kmat t})\uvec_0.
   $$

   As $t \to \infty$, $f_\infty(\xvec) = f_0(\xvec) + \Theta(\xvec, \Xmat)\Kmat^{-1}(\yvec - f_0(\Xmat))$. This is
   <NoteLink to="kernel-ridge-regression">kernel regression</NoteLink> with kernel $\Theta$ and no ridge, fitted to the
   residual of the network at initialisation. Stopping at a finite $t$ shrinks the directions with small eigenvalues,
   the analogue of <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> as a ridge penalty.

## The infinite-width limit

Take a two-layer network $f(\xvec) = \frac{1}{\sqrt M}\sum_{r=1}^M a_r\sigma(\wvec_r^\top\xvec)$ with $M$ hidden units,
activation $\sigma$, and all parameters drawn independently from $\Gauss(0, 1)$ at initialisation. The factor
$1/\sqrt M$ in front, rather than in the variance of $a_r$, is the **NTK parameterisation**. Its kernel sums the
contributions of the output weights $a_r$ and the input weights $\wvec_r$:

$$
\Theta(\xvec, \xvec') = \frac1M\sum_{r=1}^M\brack{\sigma(\wvec_r^\top\xvec)\sigma(\wvec_r^\top\xvec') +
a_r^2\,\sigma'(\wvec_r^\top\xvec)\sigma'(\wvec_r^\top\xvec')\,\xvec^\top\xvec'}.
$$

The kernel is an average of $M$ independent terms. As $M \to \infty$, the <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss>
gives the deterministic limit

$$
\Theta^\infty(\xvec, \xvec') = \expect_{\wvec}\brack{\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec)\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec')} +
\xvec^\top\xvec'\,\expect_{\wvec}\brack{\sigma'(\wvec^\top\xvec)\sigma'(\wvec^\top\xvec')}.
$$

It also stays fixed during training. The gradient with respect to each $\wvec_r$ carries the factor $1/\sqrt M$, so
fitting the data moves each $\wvec_r$ by $O(1/\sqrt M)$, and the kernel changes by $O(1/\sqrt M)$. Jacot et al. proved
the convergence for deep <Gloss name="mlp">fully connected networks</Gloss>, and positive-definiteness of
$\Theta^\infty$ for inputs on the sphere and non-polynomial activations <Cite id="jacot2018" />. Du, Zhai, Póczos and
Singh showed that finite but polynomially wide two-layer networks reach zero training loss at a linear rate set by the
smallest eigenvalue of the limiting kernel matrix <Cite id="du2019b" />. Arora et al. gave the first non-asymptotic
proof that a sufficiently wide trained network matches kernel regression with the NTK <Cite id="arora2019b" />.

At initialisation $f_0$ is itself random. In the infinite-width limit it is a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian
process</NoteLink> whose covariance is the first term of $\Theta^\infty$, the
<NoteLink to="gaussian-processes-and-bayesian-linear-regression">neural network Gaussian process</NoteLink> kernel
<Cite id="lee2018" />. The trained network is $f_0$ plus kernel regression on its residual, so its predictions are also
Gaussian <Cite id="lee2019" />.

<Derivation title="The ReLU kernel in closed form">
For <Gloss name="relu" />, $\sigma(z) = \max(0, z)$ and $\sigma'(z) = \indicator[z > 0]$. Let $\xvec$ and $\xvec'$ be
unit vectors at angle $\varphi$, and $\wvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$. Only the projection of $\wvec$ onto the plane of
the two inputs matters, and its direction is uniform on the circle.

- **The derivative term.** $\sigma'(\wvec^\top\xvec)\sigma'(\wvec^\top\xvec') = 1$ when $\wvec$ makes an acute angle
  with both inputs. Those directions form an arc of angle $\pi - \varphi$ out of $2\pi$, so the expectation is
  $(\pi - \varphi)/(2\pi)$.
- **The activation term.** $\expect[\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec)\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec')] =
  (\sin\varphi + (\pi - \varphi)\cos\varphi)/(2\pi)$, the first-order arc-cosine kernel <Cite id="cho2009" />.

With $\xvec^\top\xvec' = \cos\varphi$, the two terms add to

$$
\Theta^\infty(\varphi) = \frac{\sin\varphi + 2(\pi - \varphi)\cos\varphi}{2\pi}.
$$

It equals 1 at $\varphi = 0$, $1/(2\pi) = 0.159$ at $\varphi = \pi/2$ and 0 at $\varphi = \pi$.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Train an infinitely wide two-layer ReLU network, both layers, on two unit inputs at a right angle, $\xvec_1 = (1, 0)$
and $\xvec_2 = (0, 1)$, with targets $\yvec = (1, -1)$. Take a symmetric initialisation in which every hidden unit has
a twin with the opposite output weight, so that $f_0 \equiv 0$ and the kernel is unchanged.

1. **The kernel matrix.** From the closed form, $\Theta^\infty(0) = 1$ and $\Theta^\infty(\pi/2) = 0.159$, so
   $\Kmat = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.159\\ 0.159 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
2. **Its eigenvectors.** $\Kmat$ has eigenvalue 1.159 along $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and 0.841 along $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$. The
   targets lie along the second.
3. **Training.** The residual starts at $\uvec_0 = -\yvec$ and shrinks as $e^{-0.841t}$. It falls to 1% of its size at
   $t = \ln 100/0.841 = 5.5$. Gradient descent with step size 1 multiplies it by $1 - 0.841 = 0.159$ per step and needs
   three steps.
4. **Predictions.** At a unit input at angle $\alpha$ from $\xvec_1$, $f_\infty = \Theta^\infty(\alpha) -
   \Theta^\infty(\pi/2 - \alpha)$ divided by 0.841, because $\yvec$ is an eigenvector. The prediction is 1 at
   $\alpha = 0°$, 0.574 at $22.5°$, 0 at $45°$, $-0.574$ at $67.5°$ and $-1$ at $90°$. At $\alpha = -45°$, outside the
   two training inputs, it is 0.841.

No feature of the inputs was learned: the kernel was
known before the data were seen.

## Lazy training and feature learning

The kernel regime is a consequence of scaling, not of width alone. Chizat, Oyallon and Bach showed that any model whose
output is multiplied by a large factor $\alpha$, with the loss rescaled by $1/\alpha^2$, trains lazily and behaves like
its linearisation. In their experiments, deep <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink>
trained in the lazy regime performed worse than the same networks trained normally <Cite id="chizat2019" />. For
diagonal linear networks, the scale of the initialisation moves the implicit bias from the minimum $\ell_2$ norm of the
kernel regime to a sparsity-favouring bias of the "rich" regime <Cite id="woodworth2020" />.

The NTK parameterisation is not the only infinite-width limit. Yang and Hu classified the parameterisations whose
infinite-width limits are stable, and found that the NTK limit cannot learn features. Their maximal update
parameterisation has an infinite-width limit that does learn features
(<NoteLink to="maximal-update-parameterisation">maximal update parameterisation</NoteLink>) <Cite id="yang2021tp4" />.

The kernel can still be strong. Arora et al. computed the NTK of convolutional architectures exactly; on
<Gloss name="cifar-10" /> the resulting pure kernel method was 10% more accurate than an earlier kernel benchmark, and
6% less accurate than the corresponding finite network trained without <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch
normalisation</NoteLink> <Cite id="arora2019b" />. For two-layer ReLU networks with a trained first layer, a
generalisation bound of order $\sqrt{\yvec^\top(\Hmat^\infty)^{-1}\yvec/n}$, where $\Hmat^\infty$ is the limiting kernel
matrix of that layer, separates true from random labels on <Gloss name="mnist" /> and CIFAR: random labels need a large
kernel norm to fit <Cite id="arora2019c" />.

## Pitfalls

- **The limit needs the right scaling.** The kernel stays fixed under the NTK parameterisation with step sizes of order
  one. Standard parameterisations with tuned learning rates leave the regime, and practical networks learn features.
- **Finite width is not infinite width.** The kernel of a finite network changes during training by an amount that
  shrinks like $1/\sqrt{\text{width}}$. For the widths used in practice the change can be large, especially in deep
  layers.
- **The initial function adds variance.** $f_\infty$ includes the random $f_0$ and its residual fit. Without a
  symmetric initialisation, predictions vary from seed to seed even in the limit.
- **A positive-definite kernel matrix is required.** Duplicate inputs with different labels make $\Kmat$ singular, and
  the residual along its null space never decays.
- **Kernel regression interpolates.** With no ridge, the trained network fits <NoteLink to="label-noise">label
  noise</NoteLink> exactly. Whether that harms prediction depends on the kernel's spectrum
  (<NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink>).
