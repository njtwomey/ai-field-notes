<Definition>
Let $\hat L(\thetavec)$ be the training loss and $\thetavec^*$ a minimum with <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink>
$\Hmat = \nabla^2\hat L(\thetavec^*)$. **Sharpness** measures how fast $\hat L$ rises around $\thetavec^*$. Common
measures are the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}(\Hmat)$, the trace $\trace\Hmat$, and the worst-case rise in a ball
of radius $\rho$,

$$
S_\rho(\thetavec^*) = \max_{\norm{\epsilonvec} \le \rho}\hat L(\thetavec^* + \epsilonvec) - \hat L(\thetavec^*).
$$

A minimum is **flat** when these are small. The flat-minima hypothesis holds that flat minima generalise better than
sharp ones <Cite id="hochreiter1997b" /> <Cite id="keskar2017" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A flat minimum is a wide valley: the parameters can move some distance without the training loss rising much. Three
arguments link width to generalisation.

- **Robustness to shift.** The test loss surface differs from the training loss surface. If the difference roughly
  shifts the surface, a wide valley loses less at the training minimum than a narrow one.
- **Description length.** A point in a wide valley can be specified with few bits, because low precision suffices.
  Hochreiter and Schmidhuber used this <NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">minimum description length</NoteLink>
  argument to propose flat minima <Cite id="hochreiter1997b" />.
- **PAC-Bayes.** A wide valley admits a broad distribution of parameters with low training loss. A
  <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bound</NoteLink> charges such a distribution little. This is the one
  argument that gives a bound.

Keskar et al. found that training with large minibatches converged to sharper minima than small minibatches, and
generalised worse <Cite id="keskar2017" />. In networks with <Gloss name="relu" /> units, however, sharpness depends on
how the same function is parameterised.

## Measures of sharpness

At a minimum the gradient vanishes, so a second-order Taylor expansion gives

$$
\hat L(\thetavec^* + \epsilonvec) \approx \hat L(\thetavec^*) + \tfrac12\epsilonvec^\top\Hmat\epsilonvec.
$$

The measures are two summaries of the same quadratic form.

1. **Worst case.** Over the ball $\norm{\epsilonvec} \le \rho$, the quadratic form is largest along the top eigenvector
   of $\Hmat$, by the Rayleigh quotient. So $S_\rho \approx \frac12\rho^2\lambda_{\max}(\Hmat)$.
2. **Average case.** For a random perturbation $\uvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye)$,
   $\expect[\uvec^\top\Hmat\uvec] = \sigma^2\trace\Hmat$, so the expected rise is $\frac12\sigma^2\trace\Hmat$.

The top eigenvalue is computed without forming the Hessian, by power iteration on Hessian–vector products, which
<NoteLink to="automatic-differentiation">automatic differentiation</NoteLink> provides at the cost of about two
gradients each. Keskar et al. used a relative version of $S_\rho$ over a box scaled to the size of each parameter
<Cite id="keskar2017" />.

**Step size selects flatness.** <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> with step size $\eta$ on the
quadratic model multiplies the component along an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ by $1 - \eta\lambda$ at every
step. The iteration is stable only if $\abs{1 - \eta\lambda} < 1$, that is $\lambda < 2/\eta$. Gradient descent can
therefore settle only at minima with $\lambda_{\max} \le 2/\eta$: a larger step size admits only flatter minima. Cohen
et al. observed that full-batch gradient descent on neural networks typically drives $\lambda_{\max}$ up to about
$2/\eta$ and keeps it there, a regime they named the **edge of stability** <Cite id="cohen2021" />. Measured sharpness
then reflects the step size as much as the data.

## The link to PAC-Bayes

A PAC-Bayes bound controls the risk of a randomised predictor that draws its parameters from a posterior $Q$. Take a
prior $P = \Gauss(\thetavec_0, \sigma^2\eye)$ fixed before the data, such as one centred on the initialisation, and the
posterior $Q = \Gauss(\thetavec, \sigma^2\eye)$ around the trained parameters. For a loss with values in $[0, 1]$, with
probability at least $1 - \delta$ over a sample of size $m$, every $Q$ satisfies

$$
\expect_{\thetavec' \sim Q}R(\thetavec') \le \expect_{\thetavec' \sim Q}\hat R(\thetavec') +
\sqrt{\frac{\KL(Q \,\|\, P) + \ln(2\sqrt m/\delta)}{2m}}
$$

<Cite id="mcallester1999" /> <Cite id="maurer2004" />. The derivation of the sharpness form has three steps.

1. **The <Gloss name="kl" /> term.** For two Gaussians with the same covariance,
   $\KL(Q \,\|\, P) = \norm{\thetavec - \thetavec_0}^2/(2\sigma^2)$. It falls as the posterior widens.
2. **The perturbed training loss.** By the average-case measure, $\expect_Q\hat R(\thetavec') \approx \hat R(\thetavec)
   + \frac12\sigma^2\trace\Hmat$. It rises as the posterior widens, at a rate set by the sharpness.
3. **The trade-off.** Substituting,

   $$
   \expect_Q R \lesssim \hat R(\thetavec) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\trace\Hmat +
   \sqrt{\frac{\norm{\thetavec - \thetavec_0}^2/(2\sigma^2) + \ln(2\sqrt m/\delta)}{2m}}.
   $$

   The best $\sigma$ balances the second and third terms. A flat minimum, with small $\trace\Hmat$, allows a large
   $\sigma$, a small KL and a tight bound. A sharp minimum forces a small $\sigma$ and a large KL.

The prior's $\sigma$ must not depend on the data. Choosing it from a grid of $K$ values fixed in advance, with a
<NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> over the grid, adds $\ln K$ to the numerator. Neyshabur et al.
proposed combining expected sharpness and the norm through this bound <Cite id="neyshabur2017" />. Dziugaite and Roy
optimised the posterior's mean and variances directly and obtained the first non-vacuous bounds for networks with more
parameters than training examples (<NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">non-vacuous generalisation
bounds</NoteLink>) <Cite id="dziugaite2017" />.

## Worked example

Take $m = 50{,}000$ training examples, $\delta = 0.05$, a training loss of 0.02 and a distance
$\norm{\thetavec - \thetavec_0} = 2$ from the prior's centre. Then $\ln(2\sqrt m/\delta) = \ln 8{,}944 = 9.10$. The
table minimises the bound of step 3 over $\sigma$ for three sharpness levels.

| $\trace\Hmat$ | Best $\sigma$ | Sharpness term | KL (nats) | Width term | Bound | $m$ for a bound of 0.1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 0.076 | 0.029 | 344 | 0.060 | **0.109** | 67,531 |
| 100 | 0.036 | 0.063 | 1,589 | 0.126 | 0.209 | 661,005 |
| 1,000 | 0.017 | 0.136 | 7,363 | 0.272 | 0.427 | 6,593,826 |

1. **The flat minimum.** With $\trace\Hmat = 10$, a posterior of width $\sigma = 0.076$ raises the training loss by
   only $\frac12(0.076)^2 \times 10 = 0.029$. Its KL is $4/(2 \times 0.076^2) = 344$ nats, and the width term is
   $\sqrt{(344 + 9.1)/100{,}000} = 0.060$. The bound is $0.02 + 0.029 + 0.060 = 0.109$.
2. **Sharper minima.** Balancing the two terms gives a best $\sigma$ proportional to $(\trace\Hmat)^{-1/3}$. Each
   tenfold increase in $\trace\Hmat$ therefore shrinks the best $\sigma$ by about $10^{1/3} = 2.2$, raises the KL by
   about $10^{2/3} = 4.6$, and multiplies the excess of the bound over the training loss by about 2.2.
3. **Sample size.** The sample needed for a bound of 0.1 grows nearly in proportion to $\trace\Hmat$: from 68 thousand
   to 6.6 million examples.
4. **Vacuity.** None of the three bounds is vacuous, but the sharpest is twenty times its training loss. The quadratic
   approximation also needs the loss to stay quadratic over perturbations of size $\sigma\sqrt{\dim\thetavec}$, which
   is large for a network with millions of parameters; real bounds evaluate $\expect_Q\hat R$ by sampling.

## The reparameterisation problem

A ReLU unit is positively homogeneous: $\operatorname{relu}(\alpha z) = \alpha\operatorname{relu}(z)$ for $\alpha > 0$.
A two-layer network $\Wmat_2\operatorname{relu}(\Wmat_1\xvec)$ therefore computes the same function as
$(\Wmat_2/\alpha)\operatorname{relu}(\alpha\Wmat_1\xvec)$. The rescaling changes the curvature of the loss in each
layer, in opposite directions. Dinh, Pascanu, Bengio and Bengio used such symmetries to turn any minimum into an
arbitrarily sharp one that computes the same function and therefore generalises identically
<Cite id="dinh2017b" />.

**A two-parameter example.** Let $f(x) = w_2\operatorname{relu}(w_1x)$ with one training example $x = 1$, $y = 1$, and
loss $\hat L = \frac12(w_1w_2 - 1)^2$ for $w_1 > 0$.

1. **The minima.** Every point with $w_1w_2 = 1$ is a minimum, and every one computes $f(x) = x$ for $x > 0$.
2. **The Hessian.** At a minimum, the gradient $(w_1w_2 - 1)(w_2, w_1)$ vanishes and the Hessian is
   $\begin{pmatrix}w_2^2 & w_1w_2\\ w_1w_2 & w_1^2\end{pmatrix}$, the outer product of $(w_2, w_1)$ with itself.
3. **Its eigenvalues.** They are 0, along the valley of minima, and $w_1^2 + w_2^2$ across it. At $(w_1, w_2) =
   (\alpha, 1/\alpha)$, $\lambda_{\max} = \alpha^2 + 1/\alpha^2$.
4. **Same function, any sharpness.** $\alpha = 1$ gives $\lambda_{\max} = 2$, $\alpha = 2$ gives 4.25 and $\alpha = 10$
   gives 100.01. The function is the same at every $\alpha$.

Sharpness alone cannot predict generalisation unless the parameterisation is pinned down. Two responses exist.
Scale-invariant measures, such as sharpness relative to the size of each weight, remove the effect of rescaling. The
PAC-Bayes view accepts the dependence: the isotropic perturbation $\Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\eye)$ produces a different
random function at each $\alpha$, so the bound legitimately differs, and the norm term
$\norm{\thetavec - \thetavec_0}^2$ grows with the rescaling as well.

## Sharpness-aware minimisation

Sharpness-aware minimisation (<NoteLink to="remote-sensing-image-metrics">SAM</NoteLink>) minimises the worst-case loss
in a ball, $\min_{\thetavec}\max_{\norm{\epsilonvec} \le
\rho}\hat L(\thetavec + \epsilonvec)$. A first-order expansion gives the inner maximiser
$\hat\epsilonvec = \rho\nabla\hat L(\thetavec)/\norm{\nabla\hat L(\thetavec)}$. Each step evaluates the gradient at
$\thetavec + \hat\epsilonvec$ and applies it at $\thetavec$, at the cost of two gradients per step. Foret et al.
reported better generalisation on <Gloss name="cifar-10" />, CIFAR-100, <Gloss name="imagenet" /> and fine-tuning tasks,
and robustness to <NoteLink to="label-noise">label noise</NoteLink> <Cite id="foret2021" />.

## Evidence

- **For.** In a study of more than 40 complexity measures over more than 10,000 trained
  <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink>, sharpness-based measures, the PAC-Bayes bound
  and Keskar's sharpness, predicted generalisation best <Cite id="jiang2020" />.
- **Against.** In modern settings, including transformers trained on ImageNet and the fine-tuning of
  <Gloss name="clip" /> and <Gloss name="bert" />, even reparameterisation-invariant adaptive sharpness correlated
  poorly with generalisation. It tracked training settings such as the learning rate, and in several cases sharper
  minima had lower out-of-distribution error <Cite id="andriushchenko2023" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Compare within one parameterisation.** Sharpness values from networks with different scalings, normalisation
  layers or architectures are not comparable. With <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink>,
  scaling the weights before a normalised layer leaves the function unchanged and rescales the Hessian.
- **Sharpness can be a symptom.** At the edge of stability, $\lambda_{\max}$ at the end of training is set by the step
  size. A correlation between flatness and generalisation may run through the learning rate or the batch size.
- **Under cross-entropy on separable data, sharpness shrinks as the weights grow.** Both the loss and its Hessian
  shrink, so a minimum looks flatter the longer it is trained. Compare sharpness at a fixed training loss.
- **Quadratic approximations are local.** $\frac12\rho^2\lambda_{\max}$ describes small balls. In high dimension the
  random perturbations of a PAC-Bayes posterior are long, and the loss along them is not quadratic.
