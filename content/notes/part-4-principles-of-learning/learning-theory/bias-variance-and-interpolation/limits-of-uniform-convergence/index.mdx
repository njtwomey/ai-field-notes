<Definition>
Let an algorithm $A$ map a sample $S$ of $m$ examples from $\Dcal$ to a hypothesis $h_S$, and let $L$ be a loss with
true and <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risks</NoteLink> $L_\Dcal(h)$ and $\hat L_S(h)$. The
**tightest algorithm-dependent uniform convergence bound** $\epsilon_{\mathrm{unif\text{-}alg}}(m, \delta)$ is the
smallest $\epsilon$ for which some set $\Scal_\delta$ of samples has probability at least $1 - \delta$ and

$$
\sup_{S \in \Scal_\delta}\ \sup_{h \in \Hcal_\delta}\abs{L_\Dcal(h) - \hat L_S(h)} \le \epsilon,
\qquad \Hcal_\delta = \set{h_{S'} : S' \in \Scal_\delta},
$$

so the class is pruned to the hypotheses the algorithm outputs on typical samples. Nagarajan and Kolter constructed
learners whose <NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation gap</NoteLink> is at most $\epsilon$ with probability
$1 - \delta$, while $\epsilon_{\mathrm{unif\text{-}alg}}(m, \delta) \ge 1 - \epsilon$ <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink> bound controls the gap of the learned hypothesis by
the largest gap over a whole class. The obvious complaint is that the class is too big: VC classes of networks contain
functions that fit random labels, which the learner never outputs on real data <Cite id="zhang2017" />. The obvious
repair is to shrink the class, for instance to networks of small norm, until it holds only what the algorithm can
produce. The definition above takes that repair to its limit.

The limit still fails because of how uniform convergence handles the bad samples. It is allowed to ignore one set of
samples with probability $\delta$, the same set for every hypothesis. It is not allowed to ignore a different bad sample
for each hypothesis. Suppose every learned $h_S$ has its own bad sample $S'$, built from $S$, on which it is wrong
everywhere, and suppose $S'$ is distributed exactly like a typical training sample. Then for most $S$, both $S$ and
$S'$ are typical. The sample $S'$ is in $\Scal_\delta$, the hypothesis $h_S$ is in $\Hcal_\delta$, and the pair shows a
gap near 1.

Such learners fit the training data with a decision boundary that is simple at large scale and full of small
fluctuations placed exactly at the training points. The fluctuations leave the test error near 0, because fresh data
almost never lands on them. They wreck any bound that has to hold for every sample at once.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, this result limits the
move that makes the bound uniform. It says when the algorithm, not a class, has to carry the argument.

## The construction

**The data.** Each input has two blocks, $\xvec = (\xvec_1, \xvec_2)$ with $\xvec_1 \in \reals^K$ for a small $K$ and
$\xvec_2 \in \reals^D$ with $D$ much larger than $m$. The label $y = \pm 1$ is a fair coin. The signal block is
$\xvec_1 = 2y\,\uvec$ for a fixed $\uvec$ with $\norm{\uvec}^2 = 1/m$. The noise block is
$\xvec_2 \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \frac{32}{D}\Imat_D)$, independent of $y$, so $\norm{\xvec_2}^2 \approx 32$
<Cite id="nagarajan2019" />.

**The learner.** A linear classifier $h(\xvec) = \wvec_1^\top\xvec_1 + \wvec_2^\top\xvec_2$ starts at zero and takes one
gradient step of size 1 on $\sum_i y_i h(\xvec_i)$. The result is

$$
\wvec_1 = \sum_i y_i\xvec_{1,i} = 2m\uvec, \qquad \wvec_2 = \sum_i y_i\xvec_{2,i}.
$$

The first block points along the true signal. The second is a sum of $m$ independent Gaussian vectors, each of squared
norm about 32, so $\norm{\wvec_2}^2 \approx 32m$.

**Fresh points are classified correctly.** For a new example, the margin is

$$
y\,h(\xvec) = y\,\wvec_1^\top(2y\uvec) + y\,\wvec_2^\top\xvec_2 = 4m\norm{\uvec}^2 + y\,\wvec_2^\top\xvec_2 =
4 + y\,\wvec_2^\top\xvec_2.
$$

The new $\xvec_2$ is independent of $\wvec_2$, so $\wvec_2^\top\xvec_2$ is Gaussian with variance
$\norm{\wvec_2}^2 \times 32/D \approx 1024\,m/D$. When $D$ is much larger than $m$, the noise term is small against 4,
and the test error is tiny.

**Training points are classified with a large margin.** For a training example $i$,
$y_i\wvec_2^\top\xvec_{2,i} = \norm{\xvec_{2,i}}^2 + \sum_{j \ne i}y_iy_j\,\xvec_{2,j}^\top\xvec_{2,i}$. The first term
is about 32 and the sum is small, so the training margin is about $4 + 32 = 36$.

**The bad sample.** Build $S'$ from $S$ by negating every noise block: $(\xvec_{1,i}, -\xvec_{2,i})$ with the same
label. The margin of $h_S$ on the $i$-th point of $S'$ is about $4 - 32 = -28$. Every point of $S'$ is misclassified.
The Gaussian is symmetric, so $S'$ has exactly the distribution of a training sample.

**Uniform convergence fails.** Fix any set $\Scal_\delta$ of probability at least $1 - \delta$, with $\delta \le 1/4$.
The map $S \mapsto S'$ preserves the distribution, so $S$ and $S'$ both lie in $\Scal_\delta$ with probability at least
$1 - 2\delta \ge \frac12$. With high probability $h_S$ also has test error below $\epsilon$ and misclassifies all of
$S'$. So some sample $S_\star$ has all four properties, and

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{unif\text{-}alg}} \ge \abs{L_\Dcal(h_{S_\star}) - \hat L_{S'_\star}(h_{S_\star})} \ge 1 - \epsilon.
$$

Nagarajan and Kolter prove this for the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> and for the ramp loss at any
margin $\gamma \in [0, 1]$, with $D$ of order $m\ln(m/\delta)$ and $m\ln(1/\epsilon)$ or more
<Cite id="nagarajan2019" />.

**A neural network.** The same failure appears in an experiment without noise coordinates. Two classes lie uniformly on
concentric spheres of radius 1 and 1.1 in $\reals^{1000}$. A two-layer <Gloss name="relu" /> network with 100,000 hidden
units is trained by <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> until 99% of the training points have margin
at least 10. As $m$ grows from 4,096 to 65,536 the test error falls like $m^{-1/2}$. The bad sample projects each
training point onto the other sphere and flips its label; it has the training distribution, and the network
misclassifies all of it <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />. The learned boundary bends around each training point, much as in
the <NoteLink to="adversarial-examples">adversarial examples</NoteLink> literature.

## What remains

- **Bounds that are not uniform over samples.** <NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">Algorithmic stability</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="sample-compression-bounds">sample compression</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="information-theoretic-generalisation-bounds">information-theoretic bounds</NoteLink> do not take a
  supremum over a class, so the argument above does not apply to them directly.
- **One-sided bounds.** The argument needs a two-sided bound: it uses a sample on which the empirical error is far
  above the true error. Most existing tools are two-sided. Nagarajan and Kolter show that standard
  <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>, usually stated one-sided, are also no smaller than the
  two-sided quantity in their examples <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />.
- **Surrogates.** Uniform convergence can still bound a surrogate predictor that is coupled to the learned one. Setting
  $\wvec_2$ to zero in the linear example gives a classifier with few effective parameters, small test error and a
  <NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">non-vacuous bound</NoteLink> <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />. Negrea,
  Dziugaite and Roy make this systematic: they bound the risk of the learned predictor through surrogates obtained by
  conditioning or denoising, which belong to classes with uniformly small gaps <Cite id="negrea2020" />.
- **Measurement.** A <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bound</NoteLink> is not uniform over hypotheses and is
  unaffected. It certifies the learned predictor without explaining it.

## Worked example

Take the linear construction with $m = 1{,}000$ and $D = 10^6$ noise coordinates, so $D/m = 1{,}000$.

1. **Test error.** The noise term on a fresh point has variance about $1024m/D = 1.02$, so standard deviation about 1.
   A fresh point is misclassified when the noise term is below $-4$: probability about $\Phi(-4) = 3 \times 10^{-5}$.
2. **Training margins.** About $4 + 32 = 36$ for every training point, so the training error and the margin loss at
   any $\gamma$ below about 30 are 0.
3. **Error on the bad sample.** Each negated point has margin about $4 - 32 = -28$: an
   <NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rate</NoteLink> of 1. A check by simulation with $m = 100$ and
   $D = 10^5$ gives training margins between 33 and 39 and negated margins between $-31$ and $-25$.
4. **A norm-based margin bound.** The weight norm is $\norm{\wvec} = \sqrt{\norm{\wvec_1}^2 + \norm{\wvec_2}^2} \approx
   \sqrt{4m + 32m} = 6\sqrt m = 190$. The inputs have norm $X \approx \sqrt{32} = 5.66$. The capacity term of the
   <NoteLink to="margin-bounds">margin bound</NoteLink> is
   $2\norm{\wvec}X/(\gamma\sqrt m) = 2 \times 6 \times 5.66/\gamma = 67.9/\gamma$. At $\gamma = 30$, where the margin
   loss is still 0, the term is 2.26. It does not depend on $m$, so more data never makes it informative.
5. **Risk against the bounds.** The true risk is about $3 \times 10^{-5}$. The norm-based bound is above 2 at every
   sample size. The theorem says that no uniform convergence bound over the hypotheses this learner outputs can do
   better than $1 - \epsilon$.

The norm of $\wvec_2$ grows like $\sqrt m$, and this matches what Nagarajan and Kolter measured in real networks: on
<Gloss name="mnist" />, the distance of the weights from their initialisation grew like $m^{0.4}$, and norm-based bounds
grew with $m$ <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />.

## Assumptions

- **Two-sided uniform convergence.** The result is about bounds on $\abs{L_\Dcal(h) - \hat L_S(h)}$, which is what the
  standard proofs by <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink> give.
- **A symmetry of the distribution.** The construction needs a map from typical samples to typical samples, here the
  negation of the noise block or the swap of spheres, on which the learned hypothesis fails.
- **Overparameterisation.** The noise dimension $D$ must exceed $m$ by a logarithmic factor, so that the learner can
  memorise the noise of each training point.

## Pitfalls

- **Not a statement about learnability.** Uniform convergence still characterises which classes are learnable in the
  worst case (see the <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-statistical-learning">fundamental theorem of statistical
  learning</NoteLink>). The examples show that it can fail to explain a particular algorithm on a particular
  distribution. Shalev-Shwartz et al. gave learnable problems, outside binary classification, where uniform convergence
  fails <Cite id="shalevshwartz2010" />; that result is about learnability and is separate from this one.
- **Not a statement that the bounds are loose by a constant.** Norm-based bounds such as the
  <NoteLink to="norm-based-bounds-for-neural-networks">spectrally normalised margin bound</NoteLink> are numerically
  large <Cite id="bartlett2017" /> <Cite id="neyshabur2018" />. The examples here show something stronger: the best
  uniform convergence bound is near 1 in a setting where the test error is near 0.
- **Not proved for every deep network.** The theorem covers the constructed examples; for standard networks there is an
  experiment and a conjecture. Training with explicit <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink>, such as
  <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>, is not covered <Cite id="nagarajan2019" />.
