<Definition>
Let $\Hcal$ be a class of hypotheses, $\ell$ a loss, $R(h)$ the true risk of $h$ under a distribution $\Dcal$, and
$\hat R_S(h)$ its empirical risk on a sample $S$ of $m$ independent examples from $\Dcal$. The **uniform deviation** of
$\Hcal$ on $S$ is

$$
\sup_{h \in \Hcal}\abs{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)}.
$$

$\Hcal$ has the **uniform convergence property** if there is a function $m_\Hcal(\epsilon, \delta)$ such that, for
every $\epsilon, \delta \in (0, 1)$, every distribution $\Dcal$ and every $m \ge m_\Hcal(\epsilon, \delta)$, the uniform
deviation is at most $\epsilon$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over $S$ <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. A
sample on which it is at most $\epsilon$ is called **$\epsilon$-representative**.
</Definition>

## Intuition

For one fixed hypothesis $h$, the empirical risk $\hat R_S(h)$ is an average of $m$ independent losses. The
<NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers" /> makes it converge to $R(h)$, and <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's
inequality</NoteLink> bounds the deviation at $\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$ for losses in $[0, 1]$. That is the guarantee
of a held-out test set.

A learning algorithm does not evaluate a fixed hypothesis. It looks at the sample and picks the hypothesis that does
best on it. The chosen hypothesis depends on $S$, and the empirical risk of a hypothesis chosen for having small
empirical risk is biased downwards. Hoeffding's inequality for each fixed $h$ says nothing about $h$ chosen this way.

The fix is to ask for more: a sample on which every hypothesis in the class has empirical risk close to its true risk
at the same time. Then it no longer matters which one the algorithm picks. That demand is uniform convergence. It is
harder to satisfy than pointwise convergence, and whether it holds depends on the size, or **capacity**, of the class.

## Definition

Two strengths of the property appear in the literature.

- **Glivenko–Cantelli class** for a fixed distribution: the uniform deviation tends to zero almost surely as
  $m \to \infty$. The name comes from the <NoteLink to="glivenko-cantelli-theorem-and-dkw-inequality" />, which proves
  it for the class of half-lines.
- **Uniform Glivenko–Cantelli class**: the convergence holds at a rate that is the same for every distribution. This
  is the uniform convergence property of the definition, the one learning theory uses, because the distribution is
  unknown.

The function $m_\Hcal(\epsilon, \delta)$ is the **sample complexity of uniform convergence**. For a finite class with a
loss in $[0, 1]$, Hoeffding's inequality and the <NoteLink to="union-bound" /> give

$$
m_\Hcal(\epsilon, \delta) \le \left\lceil\frac{\ln(2\abs{\Hcal}/\delta)}{2\epsilon^2}\right\rceil.
$$

Uniformity over a finite class adds $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ to $\ln(2/\delta)$.

## Why ERM needs it

**Uniform convergence is sufficient.** Let $\hat h$ minimise the empirical risk over $\Hcal$
(<Gloss name="erm" />), and let $h^*$ be a hypothesis with $R(h^*) = \inf_{h \in \Hcal}R(h)$. Write
$\Delta = \sup_{h}\abs{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)}$. Then

$$
R(\hat h) \le \hat R_S(\hat h) + \Delta \le \hat R_S(h^*) + \Delta \le R(h^*) + 2\Delta.
$$

The first and last steps use the definition of $\Delta$, each for one hypothesis. The middle step uses that $\hat h$
minimises the empirical risk. On an $\epsilon/2$-representative sample, ERM is within $\epsilon$ of the best in the
class. The uniform convergence property therefore makes ERM an agnostic PAC learner with sample complexity at most
$m_\Hcal(\epsilon/2, \delta)$ <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. The same three-term argument is the decomposition step of
the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>; see
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation" />.

**Only one side of the argument needs uniformity.** The last step concerns the fixed hypothesis $h^*$, for which
Hoeffding's inequality suffices. The first step concerns $\hat h$, which depends on the data, and needs the one-sided
uniform bound $\sup_h(R(h) - \hat R_S(h))$.

**Pointwise convergence is not enough.** Let $x$ be uniform on $[0, 1]$, let every label be $y = 1$, and let $\Hcal$
contain, for every finite set $A \subset [0, 1]$, the classifier $h_A(x) = \indicator[x \in A]$ together with the
constant classifier $h_1(x) = 1$. Each $h_A$ predicts 0 almost everywhere, so $R(h_A) = 1$, and for each fixed $A$ the
empirical risk converges to 1. Uniform convergence fails: for $A = \set{x_1, \dots, x_m}$, the classifier $h_A$ has
empirical risk 0, so $\Delta = 1$ for every $m$. An ERM that breaks ties towards $h_A$ memorises the sample and has
true risk 1, while $h_1$ has risk 0. The class can fit any labelling of any finite sample: its
<NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> is infinite.

**For binary classification, it is also necessary.** The
<NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-statistical-learning" /> states that a binary class with the 0–1 loss has the
uniform convergence property if and only if it is PAC learnable, if and only if its VC dimension is finite
<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. In more general learning problems, learnability can hold without uniform convergence
<Cite id="shalevshwartz2010" />.

## The tools that prove it

Every bound on the uniform deviation makes the third move of the recipe: it turns a deviation bound for one hypothesis
into one for the whole class. The tools differ in how they count the class.

| Tool | Class it handles | Capacity it pays | Deviation at confidence $1 - \delta$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <NoteLink to="union-bound" /> | finite | $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ | $\sqrt{\ln(2\abs{\Hcal}/\delta)/(2m)}$ |
| <NoteLink to="glivenko-cantelli-theorem-and-dkw-inequality">DKW inequality</NoteLink> | half-lines | none | $\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$ |
| <NoteLink to="symmetrisation" /> with the <NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">growth function</NoteLink> | binary, VC dimension $d$ | $\ln\Pi_\Hcal(2m) \le d\ln(2em/d)$ | $\sqrt{8(\ln\Pi_\Hcal(2m) + \ln(4/\delta))/m}$ |
| <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity" /> | real-valued, bounded | $\Rad_m(\ell \circ \Hcal)$ | $2\Rad_m + \sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$ |
| <NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy">Covering numbers</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="chaining-and-dudley-entropy-integral">chaining</NoteLink> | real-valued, metric structure | metric entropy at every scale | of order $\sqrt{d/m}$ for VC classes |

The Rademacher route is completed by <NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma" />, which bounds the Rademacher
complexity of a finite projection, and the <NoteLink to="contraction-lemma" />, which carries a bound from scores to
Lipschitz losses. Each row is uniform over all distributions, so each is pessimistic for any particular one.

## Worked example

**A finite class.** Let $\Hcal$ be 1,000 threshold classifiers on a grid, with the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1
loss</NoteLink>, $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$.

1. **One hypothesis.** Hoeffding's inequality gives a deviation of $\sqrt{\ln 40/2{,}000} = 0.043$.
2. **All 1,000 at once.** The union bound gives $\sqrt{\ln(2{,}000/0.05)/2{,}000} = \sqrt{10.60/2{,}000} = 0.073$.
   Uniformity over 1,000 hypotheses costs a factor 1.7.
3. **ERM.** The excess risk of ERM over the best grid threshold is at most $2 \times 0.073 = 0.146$.
4. **Sample size.** An excess risk of at most $0.05$ needs $\Delta \le 0.025$, so
   $m \ge \ln(40{,}000)/(2 \times 0.025^2) = 8{,}478$. A single held-out evaluation to the same accuracy needs
   $\ln 40/(2 \times 0.025^2) = 2{,}952$.

**All thresholds.** The class of all thresholds on the line is infinite. The DKW route of
<NoteLink to="glivenko-cantelli-theorem-and-dkw-inequality" /> bounds its uniform deviation at $m = 1{,}000$ by
$0.094$. The general route through symmetrisation and the growth function $\Pi(2m) = 2{,}001$ gives $0.310$. Neither
is vacuous, and they differ by a factor 3.3 on the same class. The general tools trade sharpness for generality.

**The exact answer.** For the class of half-line indicators $\indicator[x \le t]$ the law of the uniform deviation is
known exactly: it is the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic. At $m = 1{,}000$ its 95% quantile is $0.043$, equal to the DKW
value to two digits and seven times below the symmetrisation bound for the same class (see
<NoteLink to="symmetrisation" />).

## Assumptions

- **Independent, identically distributed examples.** Every tool above uses independence. Dependent data need mixing
  conditions or other arguments.
- **Bounded losses.** The deviation bounds use losses in a bounded range. Unbounded losses need truncation or
  moment conditions.
- **A class fixed before the data.** The supremum is over a class chosen in advance. A class chosen after looking at
  the sample, such as a ball around the trained weights, needs a union bound over the possible choices.

## Pitfalls

- **Uniform convergence is a worst case.** It bounds the deviation of every hypothesis, including those the algorithm
  would never choose. <NoteLink to="algorithm-dependent-bounds">Algorithm-dependent bounds</NoteLink>, such as
  <NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability" />, can be much tighter. For overparameterised models, bounds through uniform
  convergence can be provably vacuous even when the algorithm generalises; see
  <NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence" />.

- **Uniform over distributions is not uniform over the class only.** A class can be Glivenko–Cantelli for one
  distribution and fail to have the uniform convergence property; learning theory needs the version that holds for
  every distribution.
