<Definition>
Let $(T, d)$ be a pseudometric space and $A \subseteq T$. A set $C \subseteq T$ is an **$\epsilon$-cover** (or
$\epsilon$-net) of $A$ if every $a \in A$ has some $c \in C$ with $d(a, c) \le \epsilon$. The **covering number**
$N(\epsilon, A, d)$ is the size of the smallest $\epsilon$-cover, and $\ln N(\epsilon, A, d)$ is the **metric entropy**
of $A$ at scale $\epsilon$. For a class $\Fcal$ of functions, $d$ is a distance between functions, such as the sup norm
or an empirical $L_2$ distance on a sample.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> makes a bound uniform over a finite class, at the price of
$\ln\abs{\Hcal}$. Most classes are infinite: a linear model has a continuum of weight vectors. A covering replaces the
infinite class with a finite one that is everywhere close to it. Every function in the class has a representative in the
cover within distance $\epsilon$. If the distance controls the risks, the representative's risk and empirical risk are
within $\epsilon$ of the function's, and the union bound over the cover does the rest.

The covering number is the size of the smallest such cover. It grows as $\epsilon$ shrinks, and the rate of growth is
the capacity of the class. A class described by $p$ real parameters has $\ln N(\epsilon) \approx p\ln(1/\epsilon)$: the
entropy grows like the logarithm of the resolution, with the dimension as its coefficient. A class of smooth functions
has $\ln N(\epsilon)$ growing like a power of $1/\epsilon$, which is much faster.

Discretising a parameter space for a union bound is the oldest way to make a bound uniform. Covering numbers make it
precise, and they measure capacity in any metric, for real-valued classes as well as binary ones. In the
<NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink> they serve the third move,
making a single-hypothesis bound uniform over the class.

## Definitions

**Packing numbers.** A set $P \subseteq A$ is **$\epsilon$-separated** if $d(p, q) > \epsilon$ for all distinct
$p, q \in P$. The **packing number** $M(\epsilon, A, d)$ is the size of the largest $\epsilon$-separated subset.
Covering and packing numbers are equivalent up to a factor of 2 in the scale:

$$
M(2\epsilon, A, d) \le N(\epsilon, A, d) \le M(\epsilon, A, d).
$$

- **Right inequality.** A maximal $\epsilon$-separated set $P$ is an $\epsilon$-cover. A point of $A$ farther than
  $\epsilon$ from every member of $P$ could be added to $P$, contradicting its maximality.
- **Left inequality.** Two points of a $2\epsilon$-separated set cannot share a ball of radius $\epsilon$, because by
  the triangle inequality they would be within $2\epsilon$ of each other. Each point of the packing needs its own
  member of the cover.

Packings are easier to bound from below, covers from above. The equivalence lets a proof use whichever is convenient.

**Internal and external covers.** A cover with $C \subseteq A$ is internal. Allowing $C$ outside $A$ can only make the
cover smaller, and the internal covering number at scale $2\epsilon$ is at most the external one at scale $\epsilon$.

**Metrics on function classes.** Let $S = (z_1, \dots, z_m)$ be a sample. The distances used most are

$$
\norm{f - g}_\infty = \sup_z\abs{f(z) - g(z)}, \qquad
d_{p, S}(f, g) = \paren{\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\abs{f(z_i) - g(z_i)}^p}^{1/p}.
$$

The empirical distance $d_{p, S}$ depends on $f$ only through its **projection**
$f_{|S} = (f(z_1), \dots, f(z_m)) \in \reals^m$. Covering $\Fcal$ in $d_{p, S}$ is therefore covering the finite-
dimensional set $\Fcal_{|S}$ in a scaled $\ell_p$ norm. Since $d_{1, S} \le d_{2, S} \le d_{\infty, S} \le
\norm{\cdot}_\infty$, the covering numbers are ordered the same way:
$N(\epsilon, \Fcal, d_{1, S}) \le N(\epsilon, \Fcal, d_{2, S}) \le N(\epsilon, \Fcal, \norm{\cdot}_\infty)$.

## Covering numbers of common classes

### The unit ball in $p$ dimensions

Let $B$ be the unit ball of any norm $\norm{\cdot}$ on $\reals^p$. Then

$$
\paren{\frac1\epsilon}^p \le N(\epsilon, B, \norm{\cdot}) \le \paren{1 + \frac2\epsilon}^p.
$$

The proof compares volumes. Write $\mathrm{vol}$ for volume in $\reals^p$, which scales as $\mathrm{vol}(rB) =
r^p\,\mathrm{vol}(B)$.

1. **Upper bound.** Take a maximal $\epsilon$-separated set $P \subseteq B$; it is an $\epsilon$-cover. The balls of
   radius $\epsilon/2$ around its points are disjoint, because their centres are more than $\epsilon$ apart. They all
   lie inside the ball of radius $1 + \epsilon/2$. Adding their volumes gives
   $\abs{P}(\epsilon/2)^p\mathrm{vol}(B) \le (1 + \epsilon/2)^p\mathrm{vol}(B)$, so
   $\abs{P} \le (1 + 2/\epsilon)^p$.
2. **Lower bound.** $N$ balls of radius $\epsilon$ that cover $B$ have total volume at least $\mathrm{vol}(B)$, so
   $N\epsilon^p\mathrm{vol}(B) \ge \mathrm{vol}(B)$ and $N \ge \epsilon^{-p}$.

So $\ln N(\epsilon, B) \approx p\ln(1/\epsilon)$ for small $\epsilon$. At $p = 10$ and $\epsilon = 0.1$, the covering
number lies between $10^{10}$ and $21^{10} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{13}$. A ball of radius $R$ is a scaled unit ball, so
its covering number at scale $\epsilon$ is that of $B$ at scale $\epsilon/R$.

### Classes with Lipschitz parameters

Let $\Fcal = \set{f_{\thetavec} : \norm{\thetavec} \le B}$ with $\thetavec \in \reals^p$, and suppose that
$\abs{f_{\thetavec}(z) - f_{\thetavec'}(z)} \le L\norm{\thetavec - \thetavec'}$ for every $z$. An $(\epsilon/L)$-cover
of the parameter ball maps to an $\epsilon$-cover of $\Fcal$ in the sup norm, so

$$
N(\epsilon, \Fcal, \norm{\cdot}_\infty) \le \paren{1 + \frac{2LB}{\epsilon}}^p.
$$

This covers <NoteLink to="linear-models">linear models</NoteLink> with bounded inputs ($L$ is the input norm bound) and
any loss that is Lipschitz in their output.

### Lipschitz functions on an interval

Let $\Fcal$ be the 1-Lipschitz functions from $[0, 1]$ to $[0, 1]$. This class has no finite parametrisation, and its
entropy grows like $1/\epsilon$ rather than $\ln(1/\epsilon)$:

$$
\ln N(\epsilon, \Fcal, \norm{\cdot}_\infty) \le \frac{2\ln 3}{\epsilon} + \ln\paren{1 + \frac2\epsilon}.
$$

<Derivation title="A cover of the Lipschitz functions">

Let $\eta = \epsilon/2$ and place grid points $x_k = k\eta$ for $k = 0, \dots, K - 1$, where $K - 1 = \lfloor 1/\eta
\rfloor$. For a 1-Lipschitz $f$, round each grid value down to the grid of multiples of $\eta$:
$q_k = \eta\lfloor f(x_k)/\eta\rfloor$, so $0 \le f(x_k) - q_k < \eta$. Let $g$ be the step function equal to $q_k$
on $[x_k, x_{k+1})$.

1. **$g$ is within $\epsilon$ of $f$.** For $x \in [x_k, x_{k+1})$, $\abs{f(x) - q_k} \le \abs{f(x) - f(x_k)} +
   \abs{f(x_k) - q_k} < \eta + \eta = \epsilon$, using the Lipschitz property for the first term.
2. **Consecutive steps move by at most one level.** $q_{k+1} - q_k = (f(x_{k+1}) - f(x_k)) + (r_k - r_{k+1})$ with
   $r_k = f(x_k) - q_k \in [0, \eta)$. The first bracket lies in $[-\eta, \eta]$ and the second in $(-\eta, \eta)$, so
   the difference lies in $(-2\eta, 2\eta)$. Being a multiple of $\eta$, it is $-\eta$, $0$ or $\eta$.
3. **Count the step functions.** The first value $q_0$ has at most $1/\eta + 1$ choices and each of the $K - 1 \le
   1/\eta$ later steps has 3. So $N(\epsilon) \le (1/\eta + 1)\,3^{1/\eta}$. Substituting $\eta = \epsilon/2$ gives the
   bound.

The order $1/\epsilon$ cannot be improved. Tent functions of height $\epsilon$ and half-width $\epsilon$, placed on
$\lfloor 1/(2\epsilon)\rfloor$ disjoint intervals and switched on or off independently, give
$2^{\lfloor 1/(2\epsilon)\rfloor}$ 1-Lipschitz functions whose pairwise sup distances are all $\epsilon$.

</Derivation>

The same phenomenon holds in higher dimension: functions on $[0, 1]^q$ with $s$ bounded derivatives have
$\ln N(\epsilon) \asymp \epsilon^{-q/s}$ <Cite id="kolmogorov1959" />. The exponent $q/s$ is the source of the
<NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours">curse of dimensionality</NoteLink> in
<NoteLink to="minimax-rates-for-nonparametric-regression">nonparametric rates</NoteLink> <Cite id="wainwright2019" />.

### Binary classes of finite VC dimension

Let $\Hcal$ be a class of $\set{0, 1}$-valued functions with <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
dimension</NoteLink> $d$, and let $P$ be any distribution, for instance the empirical distribution of a sample.
Haussler's packing bound states <Cite id="haussler1995" />

$$
M(\epsilon, \Hcal, L_1(P)) \le e(d + 1)\paren{\frac{2e}{\epsilon}}^d.
$$

For $\set{0, 1}$-valued functions $\abs{f - g}^2 = \abs{f - g}$, so the $L_2$ distance is the square root of the $L_1$
distance and $N(\epsilon, \Hcal, L_2(P)) \le e(d + 1)(2e/\epsilon^2)^d$. The bound does not depend on the sample size.
For $d = 10$ and $\epsilon = 0.1$ it gives $\ln N \le \ln(11e) + 10\ln(200e) = 66.4$. The
<NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">growth function</NoteLink> bound on the number of distinct
labellings of $m = 10{,}000$ points is $d\ln(em/d) = 79.1$ in logarithm, and it keeps growing with $m$. A cover only
needs to resolve labellings to within $\epsilon$, not to tell them all apart.

Real-valued classes are covered through the
<NoteLink to="pseudo-dimension-and-fat-shattering-dimension">pseudo-dimension and the fat-shattering
dimension</NoteLink>, which play the role of $d$.

## A bound by discretisation

The simplest use of a cover gives a uniform bound in three moves: concentrate on each cover element, take a union
bound over the cover, and pay $\epsilon$ twice to pass from the cover to the class.

**Setting.** Let $\Fcal$ be a class of <Gloss name="loss-function">loss functions</Gloss> $f(z) = \ell(h(x), y)$ with
values in $[0, 1]$, write $\expect f$ for the risk $R(h)$ and $\hat\expect_S f = \frac1m\sum_i f(z_i)$ for the
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk</NoteLink> $\hat R_S(h)$ on an <Gloss name="iid" /> sample $S$
of size $m$. Let $C$ be an $\epsilon$-cover of $\Fcal$ in the sup norm with
$\abs{C} = N(\epsilon, \Fcal, \norm{\cdot}_\infty)$, chosen before seeing the data. Its members can be taken with values
in $[0, 1]$, since clipping a function to $[0, 1]$ does not increase its distance to a $[0, 1]$-valued $f$.

**Claim.** With probability at least $1 - \delta$, every $f \in \Fcal$ satisfies

$$
\abs{\expect f - \hat\expect_S f} \le 2\epsilon + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2N(\epsilon, \Fcal, \norm{\cdot}_\infty)/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

**Derivation.**

1. **Concentrate (Hoeffding).** For each fixed $g \in C$, <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's
   inequality</NoteLink> gives $\prob(\abs{\expect g - \hat\expect_S g} > t) \le 2e^{-2mt^2}$.
2. **Make it uniform over the cover (union bound).** The probability that some $g \in C$ deviates by more than $t$ is
   at most $2\abs{C}e^{-2mt^2}$. Setting this to $\delta$ gives $t = \sqrt{\ln(2\abs{C}/\delta)/(2m)}$. With probability
   at least $1 - \delta$, every member of the cover satisfies $\abs{\expect g - \hat\expect_S g} \le t$.
3. **Approximate.** For $f \in \Fcal$ pick $g \in C$ with $\norm{f - g}_\infty \le \epsilon$. Averages of functions
   that differ by at most $\epsilon$ everywhere differ by at most $\epsilon$, so $\abs{\expect f - \expect g} \le
   \epsilon$ and $\abs{\hat\expect_S f - \hat\expect_S g} \le \epsilon$.
4. **Triangle inequality.** $\abs{\expect f - \hat\expect_S f} \le \abs{\expect f - \expect g} + \abs{\expect g -
   \hat\expect_S g} + \abs{\hat\expect_S g - \hat\expect_S f} \le 2\epsilon + t$.

**Choosing the scale.** The bound holds for every $\epsilon$, so the best $\epsilon$ can be taken (a union bound over a
grid of scales costs a negligible amount). A fine scale shrinks $2\epsilon$ but raises $\ln N(\epsilon)$. For a
$p$-parameter class with $\ln N(\epsilon) = p\ln(1 + 2LB/\epsilon)$, taking $\epsilon = 1/\sqrt m$ gives a bound of
order $\sqrt{p\ln(LBm)/m}$. The factor $\ln m$ comes from resolving the class at the scale $1/\sqrt m$ in one step;
<NoteLink to="chaining-and-dudley-entropy-integral">chaining</NoteLink> removes it.

## Worked example: a bounded linear classifier

A linear classifier on $\reals^{10}$ scores $\thetavec^\top\xvec$, with inputs $\norm{\xvec} \le 1$, weights
$\norm{\thetavec} \le B = 5$, and labels $y = \pm 1$. Its loss is the sigmoid loss
$\ell = 1/(1 + e^{y\thetavec^\top\xvec})$, which lies in $[0, 1]$ and upper-bounds half the
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink>.

1. **Lipschitz constant.** The sigmoid has slope at most $\frac14$, so changing $\thetavec$ to $\thetavec'$ changes the
   loss by at most $\frac14\abs{(\thetavec - \thetavec')^\top\xvec} \le \frac14\norm{\thetavec - \thetavec'}$. So
   $L = \frac14$ and $LB = 1.25$.
2. **Covering number.** $N(\epsilon) \le (1 + 2.5/\epsilon)^{10}$.
3. **Evaluate at $m = 10{,}000$, $\delta = 0.05$.** The bound $2\epsilon + \sqrt{(10\ln(1 + 2.5/\epsilon) +
   \ln 40)/20{,}000}$ is smallest near $\epsilon = 0.002$. There $\ln N = 10\ln 1251 = 71.3$, the square root is
   $\sqrt{(71.3 + 3.7)/20{,}000} = 0.061$, and the bound is $0.004 + 0.061 = 0.065$. Every hypothesis has risk within
   $0.065$ of its empirical risk.
4. **Other sample sizes.** The bound is $0.19$ at $m = 1{,}000$ and $0.022$ at $m = 100{,}000$. A target gap of
   $0.05$ needs about $m = 17{,}800$.
5. **Comparison with the Rademacher bound.** The bound is not vacuous: the loss lies in $[0, 1]$ and the bound is
   $0.065$. It is not the best available. The <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> of
   the linear class, with inputs of norm at most $X = 1$, is at most $BX/\sqrt m = 0.05$, and the
   <NoteLink to="contraction-lemma">contraction lemma</NoteLink> multiplies it by $L = \frac14$. The Rademacher bound on
   the one-sided gap is then $2 \times 0.0125 + \sqrt{\ln 20/20{,}000} = 0.037$. It does not depend on the dimension
   $p = 10$; the covering bound grows with $p$ through the term $p\ln(1/\epsilon)$.

## From covers to Rademacher complexity

Covers also bound the <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink>, through
<NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's lemma</NoteLink>, and then the
<NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink> argument turns that into a generalisation bound. The advantage
over the sup-norm argument is that the cover can be taken on the sample, in $d_{2, S}$, after the data are seen.

Let $\Fcal$ take values in $[-1, 1]$ and let $C$ be an $\epsilon$-cover of $\Fcal_{|S}$ in $d_{2, S}$ with values in
$[-1, 1]$. For each $f$ pick $g \in C$ within $\epsilon$. For any signs $\sigmavec \in \set{-1, +1}^m$, the
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

$$
\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sigma_i f(z_i) \le \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sigma_i g(z_i) +
\frac1m\norm{\sigmavec}_2\norm{f_{|S} - g_{|S}}_2 \le \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sigma_i g(z_i) + \epsilon,
$$

because $\norm{\sigmavec}_2 = \sqrt m$ and $\norm{f_{|S} - g_{|S}}_2 = \sqrt m\,d_{2, S}(f, g) \le \sqrt m\,\epsilon$.
Taking the supremum over $f$ and the expectation over $\sigmavec$, and applying Massart's lemma to $C$ (whose vectors
have Euclidean norm at most $\sqrt m$),

$$
\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \inf_{\epsilon > 0}\paren{\epsilon + \sqrt{\frac{2\ln N(\epsilon, \Fcal, d_{2, S})}{m}}}.
$$

This is the **one-step discretisation bound**. Chaining applies the same argument at a sequence of scales and replaces
the single term by Dudley's entropy integral.

## Pitfalls

- **The metric matters.** A class can have an infinite sup-norm covering number and small empirical covering numbers.
  Thresholds $x \mapsto \indicator[x \ge \theta]$ on $\reals$ are at sup distance 1 from each other for any two
  different $\theta$, so no finite sup-norm cover at $\epsilon < 1$ exists. On a sample of $m$ points they realise
  only $m + 1$ labellings. The bound by discretisation needs a data-independent cover; symmetrisation is what allows
  the empirical one.
- **Cover the loss class, not only the predictors.** The bound concerns $\ell \circ h$. A Lipschitz loss transfers a
  cover of the predictors with the Lipschitz constant; the 0–1 loss does not transfer it, because a small change in a
  real-valued score can flip a label.
- **Internal against external covers.** Statements differ by a factor of 2 in $\epsilon$. Check which is meant before
  plugging in numbers.
- **The scale is a free parameter.** A bound quoted at a fixed $\epsilon$ is usually far from its best value. Optimise
  over $\epsilon$, or use the entropy integral.
