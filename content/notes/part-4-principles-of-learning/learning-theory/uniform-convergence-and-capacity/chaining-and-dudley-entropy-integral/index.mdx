<Definition>
Let $\Fcal$ be a class of real functions, $S = (z_1, \dots, z_m)$ a sample, $d_{2, S}(f, g) =
\sqrt{\frac1m\sum_i(f(z_i) - g(z_i))^2}$ the empirical $L_2$ distance, and $D = \sup_{f \in \Fcal}d_{2, S}(f, 0)$.
**Chaining** writes each $f$ as a sum of links between successive approximations from covers at scales $D, D/2, D/4,
\dots$ and bounds the supremum of a random process scale by scale. For the empirical
<NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> it gives **Dudley's entropy integral bound**

$$
\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \inf_{\alpha \ge 0}\paren{4\alpha + \frac{12}{\sqrt m}\int_\alpha^{D/2}
\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon, \Fcal, d_{2, S})}\,d\epsilon}
$$

<Cite id="dudley1967" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy">cover</NoteLink> of a class at one scale $\epsilon$ bounds its
Rademacher complexity in one step. Each $f$ is within $\epsilon$ of a cover element, and
<NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's lemma</NoteLink> prices the cover:

$$
\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \inf_{\epsilon > 0}\paren{\epsilon + D\sqrt{\frac{2\ln N(\epsilon, \Fcal, d_{2, S})}{m}}}.
$$

The single scale forces a trade-off between the approximation term and the size of the cover. A small $\epsilon$ makes
the approximation term small, but then the cover is large, and Massart's lemma charges each of its elements at the full
size $D$ of the class. Most of those elements are close to one another, and the lemma's bound does not use the distances
between them.

The cost shows in the rates.

- **Parametric classes.** If $\ln N(\epsilon) \approx p\ln(1/\epsilon)$, the best $\epsilon$ is about $\sqrt{p/m}$
  and the bound is of order $\sqrt{p\ln(m/p)/m}$. The true complexity is of order $\sqrt{p/m}$, so the one-step bound
  loses a factor $\sqrt{\ln m}$.
- **Nonparametric classes.** If $\ln N(\epsilon) \approx K\epsilon^{-q}$, the two terms balance at $\epsilon \approx
  (K/m)^{1/(q + 2)}$ and the bound falls as $m^{-1/(q + 2)}$. For 1-Lipschitz functions on an interval, $q = 1$ and
  the one-step rate is $m^{-1/3}$, while chaining gives $m^{-1/2}$.

## Mechanism

Chaining approximates each function in many steps. The first step goes from 0 to a coarse cover, the next from the
coarse cover to a finer one, and so on. Coarse covers are small and fine covers are large, but a link between
successive covers is short, as short as the finer scale. Massart's lemma at each scale then charges a large count only
against a short link.

**Setup.** Treat each function through its projection $f_{|S} \in \reals^m$, write $\inner{\sigmavec}{f} =
\sum_i\sigma_i f(z_i)$, and note that $d_{2, S}(f, g) = \norm{f_{|S} - g_{|S}}_2/\sqrt m$. Let $\epsilon_j = D2^{-j}$
for $j = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, let $T_j$ be a smallest $\epsilon_j$-cover of $\Fcal$ in $d_{2, S}$, and let $\pi_j(f) \in
T_j$ be a cover element within $\epsilon_j$ of $f$. Take $T_0 = \set{0}$, which is a $D$-cover by the definition of
$D$. Fix the depth $J \ge 1$ and a sign vector $\sigmavec \in \set{-1, +1}^m$.

1. **Telescope.** Since $\pi_0(f) = 0$, every $f$ splits into a residual and $J$ links:

   $$
   f = \paren{f - \pi_J(f)} + \sum_{j=1}^J\paren{\pi_j(f) - \pi_{j-1}(f)}.
   $$

2. **Bound the residual (Cauchy–Schwarz).** $\frac1m\inner{\sigmavec}{f - \pi_J(f)} \le \frac1m\norm{\sigmavec}_2
   \norm{f_{|S} - \pi_J(f)_{|S}}_2 \le \frac1m\sqrt m\cdot\sqrt m\,\epsilon_J = \epsilon_J$.
3. **The links are few and short.** As $f$ ranges over $\Fcal$, the link $\pi_j(f) - \pi_{j-1}(f)$ takes at most
   $\abs{T_j}\abs{T_{j-1}} \le N(\epsilon_j)^2$ values, because $\epsilon_{j-1} > \epsilon_j$ makes $T_{j-1}$ the
   smaller cover. By the triangle inequality each link has $d_{2, S}$-length at most $\epsilon_j + \epsilon_{j-1} =
   3\epsilon_j$, so Euclidean length at most $3\epsilon_j\sqrt m$.
4. **Price each scale (Massart).** <NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma">Massart's lemma</NoteLink>, the
   <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequality</NoteLink> for Rademacher sums, bounds the expected largest
   link at scale $j$:

   $$
   \expect_{\sigmavec}\brack{\sup_{f}\frac1m\inner{\sigmavec}{\pi_j(f) - \pi_{j-1}(f)}}
   \le \frac{3\epsilon_j\sqrt m\sqrt{2\ln N(\epsilon_j)^2}}{m} = \frac{6\epsilon_j}{\sqrt m}\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon_j)}.
   $$

5. **Add the scales.** The supremum of a sum is at most the sum of the suprema. Taking expectations of steps 1 to 4
   gives **Dudley's sum**:

   $$
   \hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \epsilon_J + \frac{6}{\sqrt m}\sum_{j=1}^J\epsilon_j\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon_j)}.
   $$

6. **Turn the sum into an integral.** $\epsilon_j = 2(\epsilon_j - \epsilon_{j+1})$, and $N(\epsilon)$ grows as
   $\epsilon$ shrinks, so $\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon)} \ge \sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon_j)}$ on $[\epsilon_{j+1}, \epsilon_j]$.
   Each term is therefore at most an integral over its interval:

   $$
   \epsilon_j\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon_j)} \le 2\int_{\epsilon_{j+1}}^{\epsilon_j}\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon)}\,d\epsilon.
   $$

   Summing over $j = 1, \dots, J$ gives $\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le \epsilon_J + \frac{12}{\sqrt m}
   \int_{\epsilon_{J+1}}^{D/2}\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon)}\,d\epsilon$.
7. **Stop at the scale $\alpha$.** For $0 < \alpha \le D/4$, let $J$ be the largest integer with $\epsilon_{J+1} \ge
   \alpha$. Then $\epsilon_{J+2} < \alpha$, so $\epsilon_J = 4\epsilon_{J+2} < 4\alpha$, and the integral from
   $\epsilon_{J+1}$ is at most the integral from $\alpha$. This gives Dudley's bound. For $\alpha > D/4$ it holds
   trivially, because $\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) \le D$ by Cauchy–Schwarz.

When the integral converges at 0, $\alpha$ can be taken to 0 and the bound is $\frac{12}{\sqrt m}\int_0^{D/2}
\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon)}\,d\epsilon$. The integral is a number that depends on the class and not on $m$, so the
complexity falls as $1/\sqrt m$ with no logarithm.

**Rates.** For $\ln N(\epsilon) \approx K\epsilon^{-q}$, the integrand behaves like $\epsilon^{-q/2}$.

| entropy | one-step rate | chaining rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $p\ln(1/\epsilon)$ (parametric) | $\sqrt{p\ln m/m}$ | $\sqrt{p/m}$ |
| $K\epsilon^{-q}$, $q < 2$ | $m^{-1/(q+2)}$ | $m^{-1/2}$ |
| $K\epsilon^{-q}$, $q > 2$ | $m^{-1/(q+2)}$ | $m^{-1/q}$ |

For $q > 2$ the integral diverges at 0 and $\alpha$ is needed: balancing $4\alpha$ against $\alpha^{1 - q/2}/\sqrt m$
gives $\alpha \approx m^{-1/q}$.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, chaining sharpens the
step that makes a bound uniform. The result enters a generalisation bound through the
Rademacher bound: with probability $1 - \delta$, every $f$ in a class of losses in $[0, 1]$ has
$\expect f \le \hat\expect_S f + 2\hat\Rad_S(\Fcal) + 3\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)}$ <Cite id="bartlett2002" />.

## Worked example

**Lipschitz regression.** Predict $y \in [0, 1]$ from $x \in [0, 1]$ with a 1-Lipschitz function $h : [0, 1] \to [0, 1]$
and the <NoteLink to="regression-losses">absolute loss</NoteLink> $\abs{h(x) - y}$. The absolute value is 1-Lipschitz,
so the loss class is covered as well as the predictors are. The covering-numbers note gives
$\ln N(\epsilon) \le 2.2/\epsilon + \ln(1 + 2/\epsilon)$ in the sup norm, which also bounds the $d_{2, S}$ covering
number. The losses lie in $[0, 1]$, so $D \le 1$ and the integral runs to $\frac12$.

1. **Evaluate the integral.** $\int_0^{1/2}\sqrt{2.2/\epsilon + \ln(1 + 2/\epsilon)}\,d\epsilon = 2.26$. The leading
   part alone is $\sqrt{2.2}\int_0^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/2}\,d\epsilon = 1.48 \times 1.41 = 2.10$. The integral converges,
   so $\alpha = 0$ is nearly optimal.
2. **Compare the two bounds on $\hat\Rad_S$.**

   | $m$ | one-step | Dudley |
   | ---: | ---: | ---: |
   | $10^4$ | 0.148 | 0.263 |
   | $10^6$ | 0.031 | 0.027 |
   | $10^8$ | 0.0067 | 0.0027 |

   The one-step bound falls as $m^{-1/3}$ and Dudley's as $m^{-1/2}$. Dudley's bound wins beyond about $m = 4 \times
   10^5$. Below that its constant 12 costs more than the logarithm saves.
3. **The generalisation bound.** At $m = 10^6$ and $\delta = 0.05$, Dudley's bound gives
   $2 \times 0.027 + 3\sqrt{\ln 40/(2 \times 10^6)} = 0.054 + 0.004 = 0.058$ on the gap between the test and training
   absolute error. The one-step bound gives $0.066$.
4. **Sample size.** A gap of $0.05$ needs $m \approx 1.36$ million with chaining and $2.3$ million with one step.

**A VC class.** For a binary class of <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> $d = 10$,
Haussler's bound $N(\epsilon) \le e(d + 1)(2e/\epsilon^2)^d$ <Cite id="haussler1995" /> gives
$\int_0^{1/2}\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon)}\,d\epsilon = 3.63$ and a Dudley bound of $12 \times 3.63/\sqrt m$. At $m = 10^4$
that is $0.44$, against $0.16$ for the one-step bound with the same covering numbers and $0.13$ for Massart's lemma with
the Sauer–Shelah count, $\sqrt{2d\ln(em/d)/m}$. The chaining bound has the better rate but a larger constant, and it
overtakes the logarithmic bound only at sample sizes far beyond any data set. Its value here is the rate: it is the step
that proves the agnostic <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">sample complexity</NoteLink>
$O((d + \ln(1/\delta))/\epsilon^2)$ of the <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-statistical-learning">fundamental
theorem of statistical learning</NoteLink> without a $\ln(1/\epsilon)$ factor.

## Cost

- **Covering numbers at every scale.** The integral needs $N(\epsilon)$ from the size of the class down to $\alpha$. For
  classes known only through a dimension, a bound such as Haussler's or Mendelson and Vershynin's supplies it.
- **Large constants.** The factors 4 and 12 come from the dyadic scales and the factor-of-3 link lengths. Different
  proofs give different constants, and none is small. Numerical bounds from chaining are rarely tight at practical
  sample sizes.
- **A Rademacher bound, not a deviation bound.** The integral bounds an expectation. Concentration of the supremum
  (McDiarmid or Talagrand's inequality) still has to be added for a high-probability statement.

## Trade-offs

- **Generic chaining.** Dudley's bound uses one cover per scale for all functions. Talagrand's generic chaining lets the
  sequence of approximations adapt to each function, and its $\gamma_2$ functional matches the expected supremum of a
  <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian process</Gloss> up to a universal constant <Cite id="talagrand2014" />. Dudley's integral
  can be larger than $\gamma_2$ by a factor that grows with the size of the index set.
- **Lower bounds.** Sudakov's minoration bounds the expected supremum of a Gaussian process from below by
  $c\,\epsilon\sqrt{\ln N(\epsilon)}$ at every scale <Cite id="vershynin2018" />. Dudley's integral sums the same
  quantity over scales, so up to constants the two differ by at most the number of scales that matter.
- **Localisation.** Chaining over the whole class still bounds the worst function. Restricting the class to functions
  with small variance before chaining gives the <NoteLink to="local-rademacher-complexity">local Rademacher
  complexity</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">fast rates</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale of the metric.** The integral is in the empirical $L_2$ distance, normalised by $1/m$ inside the root. Using
  the unnormalised <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">Euclidean distance</NoteLink> on $\reals^m$
  changes the formula by factors of $\sqrt m$.
- **The upper limit.** Covers above the diameter of the class are trivial ($N = 1$), so the integral stops at $D/2$ or
  $D$ depending on the proof. Integrating to infinity is harmless because the integrand is then zero.
- **A divergent integral is not a failure.** For rich classes ($q \ge 2$) the integral diverges at 0. The bound with
  $\alpha > 0$ is still valid and gives the rate in the table.
