<Definition>
Let $x_1, \dots, x_m$ be independent draws from a distribution on $\reals$ with <Gloss name="cdf">cumulative
distribution function</Gloss> $F(t) = \pr(X \le t)$. The **empirical distribution function** is
$F_m(t) = \frac1m\abs{\set{i : x_i \le t}}$, the fraction of the sample at or below $t$. The **Glivenko–Cantelli
theorem** states that $\sup_{t \in \reals}\abs{F_m(t) - F(t)} \to 0$ almost surely as $m \to \infty$, for every $F$. The
**Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz (DKW) inequality**, with Massart's constant, states that for every $m$, every $F$ and every
$\epsilon > 0$,

$$
\pr\paren{\sup_{t \in \reals}\abs{F_m(t) - F(t)} > \epsilon} \le 2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}
$$

<Cite id="dvoretzky1956" /> <Cite id="massart1990" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

For one fixed $t$, $F_m(t)$ is the mean of $m$ independent indicators $\indicator[x_i \le t]$, each with mean $F(t)$.
The <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers" /> makes $F_m(t)$ converge to $F(t)$, and
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink> bounds the deviation by
$2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}$. There are infinitely many $t$, and pointwise convergence at each one does not by itself control
the largest deviation.

Two facts make the whole line behave like a finite set of points. Both $F_m$ and $F$ are non-decreasing, and both run
from 0 to 1. Knowing them at $k$ well-chosen points pins them down everywhere else to within $1/k$, because neither
can rise by more than its total between neighbouring points. A finite set of points is handled by a
<NoteLink to="union-bound" />.

In the language of <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence" />, the class of indicator functions of half-lines,
$\set{x \mapsto \indicator[x \le t] : t \in \reals}$, has true means $F(t)$ and empirical means $F_m(t)$. The
Glivenko–Cantelli theorem says this infinite class has the uniform convergence property. It was the first such
result, and the general theory is named after it: a class is a **Glivenko–Cantelli class** when its uniform deviation
tends to zero almost surely.

## The theorem

**Glivenko–Cantelli theorem.** For independent draws from any distribution $F$ on $\reals$,

$$
D_m = \sup_{t \in \reals}\abs{F_m(t) - F(t)} \to 0 \quad \text{almost surely}.
$$

The proof below assumes that $F$ is continuous; the general case replaces $F(t_j)$ by left limits at the jumps
<Cite id="vandervaart1998" />.

1. **Choose a grid.** Fix an integer $k \ge 1$. Because $F$ is continuous, there are points
   $t_0 = -\infty < t_1 < \dots < t_{k-1} < t_k = +\infty$ with $F(t_j) = j/k$: the $j/k$ quantiles. Set
   $F(\pm\infty)$ and $F_m(\pm\infty)$ to $1$ and $0$ as the limits.
2. **Sandwich by monotonicity.** Take any $t$ in a cell $[t_{j-1}, t_j]$. Both functions are non-decreasing, so
   $F_m(t) \le F_m(t_j)$ and $F(t) \ge F(t_{j-1}) = F(t_j) - 1/k$. Subtracting,

   $$
   F_m(t) - F(t) \le F_m(t_j) - F(t_j) + \frac1k.
   $$

   In the same way, $F_m(t) \ge F_m(t_{j-1})$ and $F(t) \le F(t_{j-1}) + 1/k$, so
   $F_m(t) - F(t) \ge F_m(t_{j-1}) - F(t_{j-1}) - 1/k$.
3. **Reduce to the grid.** The two inequalities hold in every cell, so

   $$
   D_m \le \max_{0 \le j \le k}\abs{F_m(t_j) - F(t_j)} + \frac1k.
   $$

   The deviation at the endpoints $t_0$ and $t_k$ is zero.
4. **Converge on the grid.** For each of the $k - 1$ interior points, the strong law of large numbers gives
   $F_m(t_j) \to F(t_j)$ almost surely. A maximum of finitely many sequences that each tend to zero tends to zero, so
   $\limsup_m D_m \le 1/k$ almost surely.
5. **Let the grid get finer.** Step 4 holds for each $k = 1, 2, \dots$, and a countable intersection of almost sure
   events is almost sure. So $\limsup_m D_m \le 1/k$ for every $k$ at once, which means $D_m \to 0$ almost surely.

**A finite-sample bound from the same argument.** Replace step 4 by Hoeffding's inequality at each interior grid
point and a union bound over the $k - 1$ points. With probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$
D_m \le \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2(k-1)/\delta)}{2m}} + \frac1k.
$$

The grid size $k$ trades the two terms. A finer grid shrinks $1/k$ but grows the logarithm. At $m = 1{,}000$ and
$\delta = 0.05$ the best choice is around $k = 200$, with $D_m \le 0.072$. This discretise-and-union argument is the
simplest form of a cover; see <NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy" />. It always pays an extra factor
of about $\sqrt{\ln m}$.

## The DKW inequality

The DKW inequality removes the extra factor. For every $m$, every distribution and every $\epsilon > 0$,

$$
\pr\paren{D_m > \epsilon} \le 2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}.
$$

Dvoretzky, Kiefer and Wolfowitz proved it with an unspecified constant $C$ in place of 2 <Cite id="dvoretzky1956" />.
Massart proved that $C = 2$ works for all $\epsilon$ and that no smaller constant does <Cite id="massart1990" />.

The bound is **the same as Hoeffding's bound for one fixed $t$**. Making the statement hold for every $t$ at once
costs nothing in the exponent or the constant. The class of half-lines is so well structured that its worst point is
no harder to control than a single point.

**Inverting.** Setting $2e^{-2m\epsilon^2} = \delta$ and solving gives the deviation at confidence $1 - \delta$, and
solving for $m$ gives the sample size for a target $\epsilon$:

$$
\epsilon(m, \delta) = \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2m}}, \qquad m(\epsilon, \delta) = \frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2\epsilon^2}.
$$

With probability at least $1 - \delta$, the band $F_m(t) \pm \epsilon(m, \delta)$ contains $F(t)$ for every $t$
simultaneously. This is a distribution-free **confidence band** for a cdf.

**Tightness.** Kolmogorov's limit law for the same quantity is
$\pr(\sqrt m D_m > \lambda) \to 2\sum_{k \ge 1}(-1)^{k-1}e^{-2k^2\lambda^2}$ for continuous $F$; see
<NoteLink to="kolmogorov-smirnov-test" />. Its first term is exactly the DKW bound at $\epsilon = \lambda/\sqrt m$.
The second term is tiny in the tail: at $\lambda = 1.358$ it is $2e^{-8\lambda^2} \approx 8 \times 10^{-7}$. The DKW
bound is therefore almost exact for large $m$ at the confidence levels used in practice.

## Threshold classifiers

The theorem bounds the uniform deviation of a learning problem directly. Let $x \in \reals$, $y \in \set{-1, +1}$,
and let $\Hcal$ be the thresholds $h_t(x) = +1$ for $x > t$ and $-1$ otherwise. With the 0–1 loss, $h_t$ errs on
positives at or below $t$ and on negatives above $t$:

$$
R(h_t) = \underbrace{\pr(X \le t, Y = +1)}_{F_+(t)} + \underbrace{\pr(X > t, Y = -1)}_{G_-(t)}.
$$

The empirical risk $\hat R_S(h_t)$ is the same expression with sample frequencies $\hat F_+$ and $\hat G_-$. The
triangle inequality gives

$$
\sup_{t}\abs{R(h_t) - \hat R_S(h_t)} \le \sup_t\abs{\hat F_+(t) - F_+(t)} + \sup_t\abs{\hat G_-(t) - G_-(t)}.
$$

$F_+$ is a cdf: it is the cdf of the variable equal to $X$ when $Y = +1$ and to $+\infty$ otherwise. $G_-$ is a
survival function: $G_-(t) = 1 - H(t)$, where $H$ is the cdf of the variable equal to $X$ when $Y = -1$ and to
$-\infty$ otherwise. Its deviations are those of $H$. The DKW inequality holds for any distribution, including one
with an atom at an endpoint, so each supremum exceeds $\epsilon$ with probability at most $2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}$.
Allocating $\delta/2$ to each term, with probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$
\sup_{h \in \Hcal}\abs{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)} \le 2\sqrt{\frac{\ln(4/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

<Gloss name="erm" /> then has excess risk at most twice this over the best threshold. There is no VC dimension and
no symmetrisation in this argument; the order structure of the line does all the work.

## Worked example

**A confidence band.** Take $m = 100$ draws and $\delta = 0.05$. Then
$\epsilon = \sqrt{\ln 40/200} = \sqrt{3.689/200} = 0.136$. The band $F_{100}(t) \pm 0.136$ contains the true cdf
everywhere with probability at least 0.95. The exact 5% critical value of $D_{100}$, from the Kolmogorov–Smirnov null
law, is $0.134$, so the DKW band is 1.3% wider than the exact one. At $m = 10$ the DKW half-width is $0.429$ against
an exact $0.409$.

**Sample size.** A band of half-width $\epsilon = 0.05$ at $\delta = 0.05$ needs
$m \ge \ln 40/(2 \times 0.05^2) = 737.8$, so 738 draws. A half-width of $0.01$ needs 18,445 draws. The cost grows as
$1/\epsilon^2$ and only as $\ln(1/\delta)$ in the confidence.

**Grid against DKW.** At $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$, the DKW deviation is $\sqrt{\ln 40/2{,}000} = 0.043$. The
grid-and-union bound of the previous section gives

| Grid points $k$ | 20 | 50 | 200 | 1,000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bound on $D_{1000}$ | 0.108 | 0.082 | 0.072 | 0.074 |

The best grid bound is 1.7 times the DKW bound.

**Threshold classifiers.** At $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$, the uniform deviation over all thresholds is at most
$2\sqrt{\ln 80/2{,}000} = 0.094$, and ERM's excess risk is at most $0.187$. An excess risk of at most $0.05$ needs
$\sqrt{\ln 80/(2m)} \le 0.0125$, that is $m \ge 14{,}023$. The general route through
<NoteLink to="symmetrisation" /> and the growth function $\Pi(2m) = 2m + 1$ gives $0.310$ for the uniform
deviation at $m = 1{,}000$, more than three times larger.

## Assumptions

- **Independent, identically distributed draws.** Dependent data, such as a time series, need mixing conditions.
- **A fixed $F$.** The band is for the distribution the sample came from. If the parameters of $F$ are estimated from
  the same sample, as in testing normality with a fitted mean and variance, the deviation is smaller than the band
  assumes and the test is miscalibrated.
- **One dimension.** The proof uses the order of the real line. In $\reals^d$ the class of lower-left orthants
  $\set{x : x \le t}$ is still a Glivenko–Cantelli class, with VC dimension $d$, but the proof needs the
  <NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">growth function</NoteLink> and the constants change
  <Cite id="devroye1996" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Pointwise is not uniform.** A 95% interval for $F(t)$ at each $t$ does not give a 95% band for the whole cdf. At
  $m = 100$ the pointwise interval for a median is about $\pm 0.098$, against $\pm 0.134$ for the band.
- **Not every class is Glivenko–Cantelli.** The class of indicators of all finite sets on $[0, 1]$ has uniform
  deviation 1 for every $m$ under a continuous distribution. Uniform convergence needs a capacity condition such as a
  finite <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink> <Cite id="wainwright2019" />.
- **<NoteLink to="discrete-distributions">Discrete distributions</NoteLink>.** The DKW inequality still holds when $F$
  has jumps, but the exact Kolmogorov law does not: it is conservative there.
