<Definition>
Let $\Hcal$ be a class of functions from $\Xcal$ to $\set{-1, +1}$ and $C = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$ a sequence of $m$
points of $\Xcal$. The **projection** $\Hcal_C = \set{(h(x_1), \dots, h(x_m)) : h \in \Hcal}$ is the set of labellings
of $C$ that the class produces. The **growth function** is $\Pi_\Hcal(m) = \max_{C} \abs{\Hcal_C}$, the maximum over
all sequences of $m$ points. The **Sauer–Shelah lemma** states that if $\VCdim(\Hcal) = d < \infty$, then
$\Pi_\Hcal(m) \le \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i}$ for every $m$ <Cite id="sauer1972" /> <Cite id="shelah1972" />
<Cite id="vapnik1971" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A generalisation bound for a finite class contains a term $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ for making a deviation bound hold for every
hypothesis at once. An infinite class has $\abs{\Hcal} = \infty$, and that bound says nothing. On a fixed sample,
however, two hypotheses that label every sample point the same way are indistinguishable: they have the same
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk</NoteLink>. Only the projection $\Hcal_C$ matters, and it is
finite, with at most $2^m$ members. The growth function is the size of the largest projection.

<NoteLink to="symmetrisation" /> is the step that lets a bound depend on the class only through its projection on a
finite sample. After symmetrisation, the bound pays $\ln\Pi_\Hcal(2m)$ in place of $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$. If
$\Pi_\Hcal(m) = 2^m$, that cost is $2m\ln 2$, which grows as fast as the sample, so the bound does not shrink as $m$
grows. The Sauer–Shelah lemma says this happens only up to the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
dimension</NoteLink>. Beyond $m = d$, the growth function is a polynomial of degree $d$, its logarithm is about
$d\ln m$, and the bound shrinks like $\sqrt{d\ln m/m}$.

The lemma makes the third move of the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation
bound</NoteLink>, making a bound uniform over an infinite class, possible for binary classifiers.

## The growth function

Three properties follow from the definition.

- $\Pi_\Hcal(m) \le 2^m$, because there are $2^m$ labellings of $m$ points.
- $\Pi_\Hcal(m) \le \abs{\Hcal}$, because each hypothesis gives one labelling.
- $\Pi_\Hcal(m) = 2^m$ exactly when some set of $m$ points is shattered, so $\VCdim(\Hcal)$ is the largest $m$ with
  $\Pi_\Hcal(m) = 2^m$.

The growth function of several classes is known exactly.

- **Thresholds on the line**, $h_a(x) = \sgn(x - a)$, have $\Pi(m) = m + 1$. Sorting the $m$ points, a threshold
  labels a prefix $-1$ and the rest $+1$, and there are $m + 1$ places to cut.
- **Intervals on the line**, $+1$ inside $[a, b]$, have $\Pi(m) = 1 + m + \binom{m}{2}$. A labelling is a run of
  consecutive positive points, fixed by its first and last point ($\binom{m}{2}$ runs of length at least two), a single
  point ($m$ runs) or no point (one labelling).
- **Half-planes in $\reals^2$** realise $m^2 - m + 2$ labellings of $m$ points in general position (no three on a
  line). Cover's function-counting theorem gives the count $2\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{m-1}{k}$ for homogeneous
  half-spaces in $\reals^n$, and a half-plane in $\reals^2$ is a homogeneous half-space in $\reals^3$ after appending
  a constant coordinate <Cite id="cover1965" />.

The intervals reach the Sauer–Shelah bound with $d = 2$ exactly: the lemma cannot be improved in general.

## The lemma and its proof

**Sauer–Shelah lemma.** If $\VCdim(\Hcal) = d < \infty$, then for every $m$

$$
\Pi_\Hcal(m) \le \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i},
$$

and for $m \ge d \ge 1$ the sum is at most $(em/d)^d$.

The proof establishes a stronger statement: **every projection has at most as many labellings as the sample has
shattered subsets.** For a set $C$ of $m$ points,

$$
\abs{\Hcal_C} \le \abs{\set{B \subseteq C : \Hcal \text{ shatters } B}}.
$$

Every shattered subset has at most $d$ points, by the definition of the VC dimension. There are $\binom{m}{i}$ subsets
of size $i$, so the right-hand side is at most $\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i}$, which is the lemma. The empty set counts
as shattered whenever $\Hcal$ is non-empty, because the one labelling of no points is produced.

The stronger statement is proved by induction on $m$, for every class at once <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.

**Base case.** For $m = 1$ and $C = \set{c}$: if $\Hcal$ gives $c$ both labels, $\abs{\Hcal_C} = 2$, and the shattered
subsets are $\emptyset$ and $\set{c}$. If it gives one label, $\abs{\Hcal_C} = 1$, and only $\emptyset$ is shattered.
Both sides agree.

**Inductive step.** Let $C = \set{c_1, \dots, c_m}$ and $C' = \set{c_2, \dots, c_m}$. Split the labellings of $C$ by
what they do on $C'$. Define two sets of labellings of $C'$:

- $Y_0$: the labellings $(y_2, \dots, y_m)$ that appear in $\Hcal_C$ with at least one value of $y_1$;
- $Y_1$: the labellings $(y_2, \dots, y_m)$ that appear in $\Hcal_C$ with both values of $y_1$.

Each labelling of $C'$ in $Y_1$ accounts for two members of $\Hcal_C$, and each in $Y_0$ but not $Y_1$ for one, so

$$
\abs{\Hcal_C} = \abs{Y_0} + \abs{Y_1}.
$$

**Bounding $Y_0$.** $Y_0$ is the projection $\Hcal_{C'}$. By the induction hypothesis on the $m - 1$ points of $C'$,
$\abs{Y_0}$ is at most the number of subsets of $C'$ shattered by $\Hcal$. These are the shattered subsets of $C$ that
do not contain $c_1$.

**Bounding $Y_1$.** Let $\Hcal'$ be the hypotheses $h$ for which some $h' \in \Hcal$ agrees with $h$ on $C'$ and
disagrees on $c_1$. Then $Y_1 = \Hcal'_{C'}$, and the induction hypothesis applied to $\Hcal'$ bounds $\abs{Y_1}$ by
the number of subsets $B \subseteq C'$ that $\Hcal'$ shatters. If $\Hcal'$ shatters $B$, then $\Hcal$ shatters
$B \cup \set{c_1}$: each labelling of $B$ is produced by some $h \in \Hcal'$, and its partner $h'$ produces the same
labelling with $c_1$ flipped, so both values at $c_1$ occur. So $\abs{Y_1}$ is at most the number of shattered subsets
of $C$ that contain $c_1$.

**Adding.** Every shattered subset of $C$ either omits $c_1$ or contains it, so the two counts add to the number of
shattered subsets of $C$. This completes the induction.

<Derivation title="The polynomial bound: the sum is at most (em/d)^d">

Let $m \ge d \ge 1$. Because $d/m \le 1$, each term with $i \le d$ satisfies $(d/m)^d \le (d/m)^i$. Multiplying the
sum by $(d/m)^d$ therefore gives

$$
\paren{\frac{d}{m}}^d\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i} \le \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i}\paren{\frac{d}{m}}^i \le
\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\paren{\frac{d}{m}}^i = \paren{1 + \frac{d}{m}}^m \le e^d.
$$

The second inequality adds non-negative terms. The equality is the binomial theorem. The last step uses
$1 + u \le e^u$ with $u = d/m$. Dividing by $(d/m)^d$ gives $\sum_{i \le d}\binom{m}{i} \le (em/d)^d$.

</Derivation>

## Where the lemma enters a bound

Symmetrisation and a <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> over the projection on a double sample give, for
the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> and every $\epsilon$ with $m\epsilon^2 \ge 2$ (see
<NoteLink to="symmetrisation" />),

$$
\pr\paren{\sup_{h \in \Hcal}\abs{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)} > \epsilon} \le 4\,\Pi_\Hcal(2m)\,e^{-m\epsilon^2/8}.
$$

Here $R(h)$ is the true risk, $\hat R_S(h)$ the empirical risk on the sample $S$ of size $m$, and the probability is
over $S$. Setting the right-hand side to $\delta$ and solving for $\epsilon$ gives the uniform deviation at confidence
$1 - \delta$:

$$
\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{8\paren{\ln\Pi_\Hcal(2m) + \ln(4/\delta)}}{m}} \le
\sqrt{\frac{8\paren{d\ln(2em/d) + \ln(4/\delta)}}{m}}.
$$

The inequality is the Sauer–Shelah lemma at $2m$ points. The same substitution into
<NoteLink to="massarts-finite-class-lemma" /> bounds the Rademacher complexity of the class by
$\sqrt{2d\ln(em/d)/m}$.

## Worked example

**Counting.** Half-planes in the plane have $d = 3$. The table sets the Sauer–Shelah bound beside the exact count for
points in general position and beside $2^m$.

| Points $m$ | $2^m$ | Sauer–Shelah, $\sum_{i \le 3}\binom{m}{i}$ | General position, $m^2 - m + 2$ | $(em/3)^3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | 8 | 8 | 8 | 20 |
| 4 | 16 | 15 | 14 | 48 |
| 5 | 32 | 26 | 22 | 93 |
| 8 | 256 | 93 | 58 | 381 |
| 20 | 1,048,576 | 1,351 | 382 | 5,951 |

At $m = 4$ the lemma already rules out one labelling, and half-planes miss two: the two ways of labelling the
diagonals of a convex quadrilateral. At $m = 20$ the class produces at most 0.13% of all labellings. The bound
$(em/d)^d$ is looser than the sum by a factor of about 4 here, but it is still polynomial.

**A deviation bound.** Take $m = 1{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$. The sum at $2m = 2{,}000$ points is
$1 + 2{,}000 + 1{,}999{,}000 + 1{,}331{,}334{,}000 \approx 1.333 \times 10^9$, so $\ln\Pi(2m) \le 21.01$. With
$\ln(4/\delta) = \ln 80 = 4.38$,

$$
\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{8 \times (21.01 + 4.38)}{1{,}000}} = 0.451.
$$

The bound guarantees only that every half-plane's true error is within 45 percentage points of its training error. At
$m = 10{,}000$ the bound falls to $0.161$. Solving $\epsilon(m) \le 0.1$ numerically gives $m \approx 28{,}000$, and
$\epsilon(m) \le 0.05$ gives $m \approx 128{,}000$. For comparison, a held-out set of 1,000 points pins down the
error of one fixed classifier to within $\sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2m)} = 0.043$ by
<NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>, ten times tighter. Both the constant of
symmetrisation and the growth function widen the bound: dropping the factor $8$ alone would give $0.159$, and dropping
$\ln\Pi(2m)$ alone would give $0.187$.

## Assumptions

- **Binary labels.** The growth function counts labellings in $\set{-1, +1}^m$. Multiclass classifiers use the
  <NoteLink to="natarajan-dimension" />, and real-valued classes use
  <NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy">covering numbers</NoteLink> or the
  <NoteLink to="pseudo-dimension-and-fat-shattering-dimension">pseudo-dimension</NoteLink>.
- **A worst case over point sets.** $\Pi_\Hcal(m)$ maximises over all samples, so it does not depend on the data
  distribution. Points in special position (collinear, clustered) produce fewer labellings than the growth function
  allows.

## Pitfalls

- **The lemma bounds the growth function, not the projection on the data.** A bound that uses the realised projection
  $\abs{\Hcal_S}$ of the observed sample is data-dependent and needs its own argument, such as the empirical
  <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity" />.
- **$(em/d)^d$ is not the sharp form.** For small $m$ it exceeds $2^m$: at $m = d$ it equals $e^d > 2^d$. Use the
  binomial sum when the numbers matter.
- **Finite VC dimension, not a finite parameter count, gives the polynomial.** A one-parameter class such as
  $\sgn(\sin(\omega x))$ has $\Pi(m) = 2^m$ for every $m$.
