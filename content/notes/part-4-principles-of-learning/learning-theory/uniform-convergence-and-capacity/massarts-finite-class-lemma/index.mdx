<Definition>
Let $A \subset \reals^m$ be a finite set of vectors, $r = \max_{\avec \in A}\norm{\avec}_2$ the largest Euclidean norm
in it, and $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m$ independent Rademacher signs, each $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac12$.
**Massart's finite class lemma** states that

$$
\expect_{\sigmavec}\brack{\max_{\avec \in A}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\sigma_i a_i} \le \frac{r\sqrt{2\ln\abs{A}}}{m}
$$

<Cite id="massart2000" />. The left-hand side is the empirical Rademacher complexity of $A$.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity" /> of a class on a sample $S$ depends on the class only through its
**projection**, the set of vectors $(f(z_1), \dots, f(z_m))$ that its functions take on the $m$ sample points. For
binary classifiers the projection is finite, with at most $\Pi_\Hcal(m)$ members, where $\Pi_\Hcal$ is the
<NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">growth function</NoteLink>. Massart's lemma turns a count of
the projection into a number for the complexity.

Each vector $\avec$ gives a Rademacher sum $\sum_i\sigma_i a_i$. That sum has mean zero and spread about $\norm{\avec}$,
and its tails are Gaussian-like: it is <NoteLink to="sub-gaussian-random-variables">sub-Gaussian</NoteLink> with
variance proxy $\norm{\avec}^2$. The largest of $N$ such sums grows like $\sqrt{2\ln N}$ times their common scale, by
the <NoteLink to="maximal-inequalities">maximal inequality</NoteLink> for sub-Gaussian variables. Massart's lemma is
that inequality for Rademacher sums. A projection with $N$ members therefore costs a factor $\sqrt{2\ln N}$, the same
logarithm that the <NoteLink to="union-bound" /> charges a finite class.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, the lemma is the last
step of making a bound uniform: after <NoteLink to="symmetrisation" /> has reduced the class to its projection, the
lemma bounds the complexity of the projection by its size and largest norm.

## Statement and proof

**Lemma.** For finite $A \subset \reals^m$ with $r = \max_{\avec \in A}\norm{\avec}_2$,

$$
\expect_{\sigmavec}\brack{\max_{\avec \in A}\sum_{i=1}^m\sigma_i a_i} \le r\sqrt{2\ln\abs{A}}.
$$

Dividing by $m$ gives the form in the definition. The proof bounds an exponential moment and then optimises a free
parameter, as in the proof of any <NoteLink to="chernoff-bounds">Chernoff bound</NoteLink>
<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.

Write $Z = \max_{\avec \in A}\sum_i\sigma_i a_i$ and fix $\lambda > 0$.

1. **Exponentiate (Jensen).** The function $u \mapsto e^{\lambda u}$ is convex, so
   <Gloss name="jensens-inequality" /> gives $e^{\lambda\expect Z} \le \expect e^{\lambda Z}$.
2. **Replace the maximum by a sum.** The exponential of a maximum is the maximum of the exponentials, which is at most
   their sum, because every term is positive:

   $$
   \expect e^{\lambda Z} \le \sum_{\avec \in A}\expect e^{\lambda\sum_i\sigma_i a_i}.
   $$

3. **Factorise (independence).** The signs are independent, so the expectation of the product is the product of the
   expectations. Each factor is $\expect e^{\lambda\sigma_i a_i} = \frac12(e^{\lambda a_i} + e^{-\lambda a_i})
   = \cosh(\lambda a_i)$:

   $$
   \expect e^{\lambda\sum_i\sigma_i a_i} = \prod_{i=1}^m\cosh(\lambda a_i).
   $$

4. **Bound each factor.** $\cosh u \le e^{u^2/2}$ for every real $u$ (derived below). So the product is at most
   $\exp(\lambda^2\sum_i a_i^2/2) = \exp(\lambda^2\norm{\avec}^2/2) \le \exp(\lambda^2 r^2/2)$.
5. **Collect.** Steps 1 to 4 give $e^{\lambda\expect Z} \le \abs{A}\,e^{\lambda^2 r^2/2}$. Taking logarithms and
   dividing by $\lambda$,

   $$
   \expect Z \le \frac{\ln\abs{A}}{\lambda} + \frac{\lambda r^2}{2}.
   $$

6. **Optimise $\lambda$.** The first term falls and the second rises with $\lambda$. Setting the derivative
   $-\ln\abs{A}/\lambda^2 + r^2/2$ to zero gives $\lambda = \sqrt{2\ln\abs{A}}/r$. Both terms then equal
   $r\sqrt{\ln\abs{A}/2}$, and their sum is $r\sqrt{2\ln\abs{A}}$.

<Derivation title="Why cosh u ≤ exp(u²/2)">

Both sides have power series with only even powers:

$$
\cosh u = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{u^{2k}}{(2k)!}, \qquad e^{u^2/2} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{u^{2k}}{2^k\,k!}.
$$

Compare the coefficients term by term. $(2k)! = \prod_{j=1}^{k}(2j - 1)(2j)$, and each factor pair satisfies
$(2j - 1)(2j) \ge 2j$, so $(2k)! \ge \prod_{j=1}^k 2j = 2^k k!$. Every coefficient of $\cosh u$ is therefore at most
the matching coefficient of $e^{u^2/2}$, and every power $u^{2k}$ is non-negative. The inequality is the special case
of Hoeffding's lemma for a variable that takes the values $\pm a$ with equal probability.

</Derivation>



- **Only the largest norm enters.** Vectors with smaller norm are dominated in step 4. A set with one long vector and
  many short ones is bounded as if every vector were long.
- **With absolute values.** Some texts define the complexity with $\abs{\sum_i\sigma_i a_i}$. Applying the lemma to
  $A \cup (-A)$ gives $r\sqrt{2\ln(2\abs{A})}$ for that version.
- **One vector gives zero.** For $\abs{A} = 1$ the bound is $0$, which is exact: a single Rademacher sum has mean zero.

## From Massart to the VC bound

Let $\Hcal$ be a class of classifiers $h : \Xcal \to \set{-1, +1}$ with <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
dimension</NoteLink> $d$, and use the <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink>
$\ell(h, (x, y)) = \indicator[h(x) \ne y]$. Five steps give the VC bound.

1. **Write the loss through the labels.** For labels in $\set{-1, +1}$, $\indicator[h(x) \ne y] = (1 - y\,h(x))/2$.
2. **Remove the constant and the labels.** For the loss class $\ell \circ \Hcal$ on a sample $S$,

   $$
   \hat\Rad_S(\ell \circ \Hcal) = \expect_{\sigmavec}\brack{\frac1m\sum_i\frac{\sigma_i}{2}}
   + \frac12\expect_{\sigmavec}\brack{\sup_{h}\frac1m\sum_i(-\sigma_i y_i)\,h(x_i)} = \frac12\hat\Rad_S(\Hcal).
   $$

   The first expectation is zero. In the second, the labels $y_i$ are fixed, so $-\sigma_i y_i$ are again independent
   Rademacher signs.
3. **Apply Massart.** The projection of $\Hcal$ on $x_1, \dots, x_m$ has at most $\Pi_\Hcal(m)$ vectors, each in
   $\set{-1, +1}^m$ with norm $r = \sqrt m$. So $\hat\Rad_S(\Hcal) \le \sqrt{2\ln\Pi_\Hcal(m)/m}$.
4. **Apply Sauer–Shelah.** For $m \ge d$, $\ln\Pi_\Hcal(m) \le d\ln(em/d)$, so

   $$
   \Rad_m(\Hcal) \le \sqrt{\frac{2d\ln(em/d)}{m}}.
   $$

5. **Insert into the Rademacher bound.** With probability at least $1 - \delta$, every $h \in \Hcal$ satisfies
   $R(h) \le \hat R_S(h) + 2\Rad_m(\ell \circ \Hcal) + \sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$ (see
   <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity" />). Steps 2 and 4 turn this into

   $$
   R(h) \le \hat R_S(h) + \sqrt{\frac{2d\ln(em/d)}{m}} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
   $$

This is the VC bound with the constants of the Rademacher route <Cite id="mohri2018" />. The $\ln m$ factor comes from
the union over the projection inside Massart's lemma.
<NoteLink to="chaining-and-dudley-entropy-integral">Chaining</NoteLink> removes it, at the cost of larger constants.

## Worked example

**The worst case.** Take $A = \set{-1, +1}^m$, every sign vector. Then $\abs{A} = 2^m$ and $r = \sqrt m$, so the lemma
gives $\sqrt m\sqrt{2m\ln 2}/m = \sqrt{2\ln 2} = 1.177$. The true value is exactly 1: for each draw of the signs, the
vector $\avec = \sigmavec$ is in $A$ and gives $\frac1m\sum_i\sigma_i^2 = 1$. The lemma is 18% above the truth here.
This is the situation of any class on a sample it shatters, which the figure shows for the first few points.

**A measured projection.** Intervals on the line realise 79 of the $2^{12} = 4{,}096$ labellings of the figure's 12
points (seed 1). The lemma gives $\sqrt{2\ln 79/12} = 0.853$. The Monte Carlo estimate over 500 sign vectors is
$0.589 \pm 0.007$. The bound is 45% above the measurement and below the trivial value 1. At 7 points or fewer the
bound exceeds 1 and is vacuous, because $\ln\abs{A}$ is not yet small next to the number of points.

**A VC bound.** Linear classifiers on 10 features are half-spaces in $\reals^{10}$, with $d = 11$. Take
$m = 10{,}000$ and $\delta = 0.05$. The complexity term is

$$
\sqrt{\frac{2 \times 11 \times \ln(e \times 10{,}000/11)}{10{,}000}} = \sqrt{\frac{22 \times 7.812}{10{,}000}} = 0.131,
$$

and the confidence term is $\sqrt{\ln 20/20{,}000} = 0.012$. The bound on $R(h) - \hat R_S(h)$ is $0.143$ for every
linear classifier at once.

| $m$ | Complexity term | Bound on the gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 | 0.348 | 0.387 |
| 10,000 | 0.131 | 0.143 |
| 100,000 | 0.047 | 0.051 |

Solving for the sample size, the gap bound reaches $0.1$ at $m = 22{,}492$ and $0.05$ at $m = 104{,}650$. The bound is
not vacuous at these sizes. At $m = 10{,}000$ it is twelve times the one-sided Hoeffding margin of $0.012$ for a single
classifier tested on 10,000 held-out points. For a network with $d$ in the millions and $m$ in the tens of thousands,
the complexity term exceeds 1 and the bound is vacuous.

## Assumptions

- **A finite set.** The lemma needs $\abs{A} < \infty$. An infinite projection, as for real-valued functions, is first
  approximated by a finite cover; see <NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy" />.
- **Rademacher signs.** The proof uses only that each $\sigma_i a_i$ is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy $a_i^2$.
  Standard Gaussian variables in place of the signs give the same bound for the Gaussian complexity.

## Pitfalls

- **The lemma is loose when norms differ.** Only $r = \max\norm{\avec}$ appears. If most of $A$ has small norm, a
  bound by covering numbers at several scales is tighter.
- **$\ln\abs{A}$ must be small next to $m$.** With $\abs{A} = \Pi_\Hcal(m) = 2^m$, the bound is above 1 for every
  $m$. The lemma gives a decaying bound only for a class of finite VC dimension, or a projection otherwise known to be
  small.
- **The extra $\sqrt{\ln m}$ is an artefact.** The VC bound obtained this way has rate $\sqrt{d\ln m/m}$, while the
  worst-case rate is $\sqrt{d/m}$; chaining recovers it <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.
