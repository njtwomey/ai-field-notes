<Definition>
**Double descent** is the behaviour of test error as a function of model capacity $p$ (for example the number of
parameters) at a fixed training-set size $n$. In the under-parameterised regime $p < n$ the error follows the
classical U-shaped curve. It peaks at the **interpolation threshold** $p \approx n$, where the model first fits the
training data exactly. In the over-parameterised regime $p > n$, where the learner returns the interpolating solution of
minimum norm, the error decreases again <Cite id="belkin2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Below the threshold, adding parameters trades bias for variance as the
<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink> describes. At $p = n$ there is
exactly one set of parameters that fits the training data, and it must pass through every noisy target. With no
freedom left, the fit bends violently to do so, and its variance is unbounded.

Above the threshold there are infinitely many interpolating solutions. The learner has to pick one.
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> started at zero, and <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge
regression</NoteLink> in the limit of a vanishing penalty, both pick the one of **minimum norm**. The more parameters
there are, the more ways there are to fit the data, and the smaller the norm of the smallest interpolant becomes. A
small norm means a smooth, low-variance function. Extra parameters then act as an implicit regulariser rather than as
extra capacity to overfit. This is why very large neural networks can fit random labels perfectly and still generalise
on real labels <Cite id="zhang2017" />.

## The minimum-norm interpolant

Let $\Xmat \in \reals^{n \times p}$ with $p > n$ have full row rank $n$. The system $\Xmat\wvec = \yvec$ has infinitely
many solutions. The one with smallest $\norm{\wvec}$ solves

$$
\min_{\wvec}\ \tfrac12\norm{\wvec}^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Xmat\wvec = \yvec.
$$

The Lagrangian is $\tfrac12\norm{\wvec}^2 - \lambdavec^\top(\Xmat\wvec - \yvec)$. Setting its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> in $\wvec$ to zero gives $\wvec = \Xmat^\top\lambdavec$. Substituting into
the constraint gives $\Xmat\Xmat^\top\lambdavec = \yvec$, so

$$
\hat\wvec = \Xmat^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top)^{-1}\yvec = \Xmat^{+}\yvec,
$$

the <NoteLink to="pseudoinverse">Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse</NoteLink> solution. Two common procedures return it:

- **Ridgeless limit.** The ridge solution $\Xmat^\top(\Xmat\Xmat^\top + \lambda\eye)^{-1}\yvec$ tends to $\hat\wvec$ as
  $\lambda \to 0^+$.
- **Gradient descent from zero.** Every gradient of $\norm{\yvec - \Xmat\wvec}^2$ has the form
  $-2\Xmat^\top(\yvec - \Xmat\wvec)$, which lies in the <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">row space</NoteLink> of
  $\Xmat$. Starting from $\wvec_0 = \zeros$, every iterate stays in the row space. The only interpolating solution in
  the row space is $\Xmat^\top\lambdavec$ for some $\lambdavec$, which is $\hat\wvec$.

## A model where the whole curve can be computed

Let each input have $D$ independent standard Gaussian features, $\xvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye_D)$, and
$y = \xvec^\top\betavec + \varepsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$. The learner uses only the first $p$ features and
fits them by least squares, or by the minimum-norm interpolant when $p > n$. Split $\betavec$ into the part
$\betavec_p$ on the used features and the rest. The unused features are independent of the used ones, so they add
noise of variance $\norm{\betavec_{\text{rest}}}^2$. Write $s^2 = \norm{\betavec_{\text{rest}}}^2 + \sigma^2$ for the
total noise. The expected test error is then <Cite id="belkin2020" />

$$
\expect[\text{test error}] =
\begin{cases}
s^2\paren{1 + \dfrac{p}{n - p - 1}}, & p \le n - 2, \\[2ex]
\norm{\betavec_p}^2\paren{1 - \dfrac{n}{p}} + s^2\paren{1 + \dfrac{n}{p - n - 1}}, & p \ge n + 2.
\end{cases}
$$

Both branches blow up as $p \to n$. For $p \le n - 2$ the only error beyond $s^2$ is variance. For $p \ge n + 2$ the
first term is bias, which grows with the part of $\betavec_p$ the interpolant cannot see, and the second is variance,
which falls as $p$ grows.

<Derivation title="Where the two branches come from">
The test error of a coefficient vector $\wvec$ on the used features is
$\expect[(y - \xvec_p^\top\wvec)^2] = \norm{\betavec_p - \wvec}^2 + s^2$, because the used features are independent
with unit variance.

**Below the threshold.** Write the targets as $\yvec = \Xmat_p\betavec_p + \etavec$, where $\etavec$ collects the
unused features and the noise and has independent entries of variance $s^2$, independent of $\Xmat_p$. Least squares
gives $\hat\wvec - \betavec_p = (\Xmat_p^\top\Xmat_p)^{-1}\Xmat_p^\top\etavec$, so

$$
\expect\norm{\hat\wvec - \betavec_p}^2 = s^2\,\expect\trace\paren{(\Xmat_p^\top\Xmat_p)^{-1}}.
$$

$\Xmat_p^\top\Xmat_p$ is Wishart with $n$ degrees of freedom and identity scale, so its inverse has mean
$\eye/(n - p - 1)$ and the trace has mean $p/(n - p - 1)$.

**Above the threshold.** The minimum-norm interpolant is
$\hat\wvec = \Xmat_p^{+}\yvec = \Pmat\betavec_p + \Xmat_p^{+}\etavec$, where $\Pmat = \Xmat_p^{+}\Xmat_p$ projects onto
the $n$-dimensional row space of $\Xmat_p$. Then
$\betavec_p - \hat\wvec = (\eye - \Pmat)\betavec_p - \Xmat_p^{+}\etavec$. The first part is orthogonal to the row space
and the second lies in it, so their squared norms add. The row space is a uniformly random $n$-dimensional subspace of
$\reals^p$, so $\expect\norm{(\eye - \Pmat)\betavec_p}^2 = (1 - n/p)\norm{\betavec_p}^2$. The second part has
$\expect\norm{\Xmat_p^{+}\etavec}^2 = s^2\,\expect\trace\paren{(\Xmat_p\Xmat_p^\top)^{-1}} = s^2\,n/(p - n - 1)$, by the
same Wishart identity with the roles of $n$ and $p$ swapped.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Take $D = 200$ features, $n = 40$ training points, noise $\sigma = 0.3$ and $\norm{\betavec} = 1$, so predicting zero
has error $1 + 0.09 = 1.09$.

With the signal spread evenly over all 200 features, $\norm{\betavec_p}^2 = p/200$. The formula gives a test error of
1.40 at $p = 10$, 4.07 at $p = 30$ and 4.62 at $p = 50$: every small model is worse than predicting zero, because each
added feature adds more variance than signal. Past the peak the error falls to 1.14 at $p = 120$ and 0.91 at $p = 200$.
The largest interpolating model is the best of all.

With a decaying signal, $\beta_j \propto 1/j$, the first few features carry most of the signal. The classical regime now
has a proper minimum, 0.195 at $p = 11$. The error at $p = 200$ is again 0.91, far worse than the classical optimum.
Whether the second descent beats the first depends on how the signal is spread across features.

## Where double descent appears

Belkin et al. observed the curve for random Fourier feature models, two-layer networks and ensembles of trees
<Cite id="belkin2019" />. Nakkiran et al. found it in deep networks, both against model width and against the number
of training epochs, and showed that adding training data can move the peak and make a model worse
<Cite id="nakkiran2020" />. Hastie et al. derived the asymptotic risk of ridgeless least squares as $p/n$ tends to a
constant, for isotropic and correlated features <Cite id="hastie2022" />.

## Assumptions

- **An implicit bias towards small norm.** The second descent needs a learner that picks a simple interpolant among
  many. Minimum-norm least squares and gradient descent from a small initialisation do. An arbitrary interpolant does
  not generalise.
- **Noise near the threshold.** The peak comes from fitting noise with no spare capacity. With no noise and no
  missing features, $s^2 = 0$ and the peak disappears.

## Pitfalls

- **The peak is avoidable.** Ridge regression with a tuned penalty removes the spike at $p = n$
  <Cite id="hastie2022" />. The spike is a property of unregularised interpolation, not of model size.
- **Parameter count is the wrong axis for many models.** For deep networks, the location of the peak depends on the
  number of samples the model can fit, which depends on architecture and training time as well as width
  <Cite id="nakkiran2020" />.
- **Double descent does not overturn the bias–variance decomposition.** The decomposition still holds at every $p$.
  What fails is the assumption that variance must rise with the number of parameters.
