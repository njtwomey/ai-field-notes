<Definition>
The <NoteLink to="principles-of-learning">foundations of learning</NoteLink> theory set up the problem that every later
bound answers. Examples are drawn independently from an unknown distribution $\Dcal$; a learner sees $m$ of them and
returns a predictor $h$; the predictor is judged by its risk $R(h)$, the expected loss on a new example. The foundations
define the risks and their gaps, split the error of a learned predictor into parts, and state what no learner can do
without assumptions <Cite id="vapnik1998" /> <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.
</Definition>

## What it is for

A training error is a number computed on data the learner has already used. Whether that number predicts performance on
new data is the central question of <NoteLink to="supervised-learning">supervised learning</NoteLink>. It decides how
much to trust a model, how much data to collect, how large a model class to use and whether a held-out set is large
enough. The notes here give the vocabulary for that question and the first answers: which quantities to compare, why the
training error is biased, which errors come from the class, the sample and the optimiser, and why some assumption about
the problem is unavoidable.

## Main ideas

**Risk against empirical risk.** The true risk $R(h)$ cannot be computed; the empirical risk $\hat R_S(h)$ on the
sample $S$ can. Their difference is the <NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation gap</NoteLink>. For a predictor
fixed in advance the gap averages to zero. For a predictor chosen because it fits $S$, the gap is positive on average,
and a held-out sample is the direct way to measure it.

**Empirical risk minimisation and its errors.** <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">Empirical risk
minimisation</NoteLink> returns the member of a class $\Hcal$ with the smallest training loss. Its excess risk over the
Bayes risk $R^*$ splits into an approximation error, from the choice of $\Hcal$, and an estimation error, from the
finite sample. The estimation error is at most twice the largest gap over the class, which is what the rest of the
chapter bounds. An optimiser that stops early adds a third term, and under a computing budget the three trade off
(<NoteLink to="approximation-estimation-optimisation-trade-off">the approximation, estimation and optimisation
trade-off</NoteLink>).

**Assumptions are necessary.** Averaged over all possible targets, every learner has error one half off the training
set (the <NoteLink to="no-free-lunch-theorem">no free lunch theorem</NoteLink>). A learner predicts well only through
its <NoteLink to="inductive-bias">inductive bias</NoteLink>: a restricted class, a preference such as a small norm, or a
prior. Learning theory makes the bias explicit as the class $\Hcal$ and measures its size.

**Consistency without rates.** Some rules, such as $k$-nearest neighbours with $k \to \infty$ and $k/m \to 0$, reach
the Bayes risk for every distribution as $m$ grows. No rule does so at a rate that holds for every distribution
(<NoteLink to="consistency-of-learning-algorithms">consistency of learning algorithms</NoteLink>)
<Cite id="devroye1996" />. Rates need assumptions on the class or the distribution.

**The recipe.** Every bound in the chapter decomposes the error, concentrates the loss of one predictor, makes the
statement uniform over the class, exploits structure, and inverts the result into an accuracy or a sample size (see
<NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">statistical learning theory</NoteLink>). The foundations supply the first
move.

## How the notes fit together

- Start with <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory" /> for the setting and the recipe, then the
  <NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation gap</NoteLink> for the quantities a bound compares.
- <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">Empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> defines the learner the theory
  analyses first, and the <NoteLink to="approximation-estimation-optimisation-trade-off">three-way trade-off</NoteLink>
  extends its decomposition to approximate optimisers.
- <NoteLink to="inductive-bias">Inductive bias</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="no-free-lunch-theorem">no free lunch
  theorem</NoteLink> explain why a class must be fixed before the data are seen.
- <NoteLink to="consistency-of-learning-algorithms">Consistency</NoteLink> states the asymptotic goal and why it gives
  no finite-sample guarantee by itself.
- <NoteLink to="deriving-a-generalisation-bound">Deriving a generalisation bound</NoteLink> works the recipe end to end
  on threshold classifiers with <NoteLink to="label-noise">label noise</NoteLink>, and is best read after the next two
  sub-chapters.

## Prerequisites and next steps

The notes need <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, the <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">law of large
numbers</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>, and the idea of a
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes decision rule</NoteLink>.

From here the chapter goes to <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">probably approximately correct
(PAC) learning</NoteLink>, which bounds the estimation error for finite classes, and to
<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence-and-capacity">uniform convergence and capacity</NoteLink> for infinite ones. The
<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink> is the squared-error analogue of the
approximation–estimation split, and <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> is the practical way to
estimate the risk that the theory bounds.
