<Definition>
Let $\Dcal$ be a distribution over input–label pairs, $\ell$ a loss, $R(h) = \expect_{(x, y) \sim \Dcal}[\ell(h(x), y)]$
the true risk of a predictor $h$, and $\hat R_S(h)$ its average loss on a training sample $S$ of $m$ independent draws
from $\Dcal$. The **generalisation gap** of $h$ on $S$ is

$$
\mathrm{gap}_S(h) = R(h) - \hat R_S(h).
$$

For a learning algorithm $A$ that maps a sample to a predictor, the gap of interest is $R(A(S)) - \hat R_S(A(S))$, and
its average over samples is the **expected generalisation gap**. A learned predictor with a small training error and a
large gap **overfits** <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A predictor's training error is measured on the same points that were used to choose it. The learner picked the
predictor because it does well on those points, partly because it fits their noise and their accidents. On fresh
points the accidents differ, and the error rises. The generalisation gap measures how much the training error flatters
the predictor.

The gap depends on how much freedom the learner had. A learner that chooses among a few rigid predictors cannot fit
much noise, and its gap is small. A learner that can fit any labelling, such as a lookup table or a very large network,
can drive its training error to zero on any data, and its gap is then its whole test error. Statistical learning theory
is largely a theory of the gap: it bounds $R(h) - \hat R_S(h)$ in terms of the sample size $m$ and a measure of the
learner's freedom (see <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">statistical learning theory</NoteLink>).

## Training, test and true risk

Three numbers describe a predictor $\hat h = A(S)$ learned from $S$.

- **The true risk** $R(\hat h)$ is its expected loss on a new draw from $\Dcal$. It is the quantity that matters, and it
  cannot be computed, because $\Dcal$ is unknown.
- **The training risk** $\hat R_S(\hat h) = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\ell(\hat h(x_i), y_i)$ is its average loss on the
  training sample. It is known exactly, and it is biased downwards.
- **The test risk** $\hat R_T(\hat h)$ is its average loss on a held-out sample $T$ of $m_{\text{test}}$ points drawn
  from $\Dcal$ independently of $S$ and not used in any choice. Conditional on $S$, the predictor $\hat h$ is fixed and
  the test losses are independent draws with mean $R(\hat h)$, so the test risk is an unbiased estimate of the true
  risk.

The generalisation gap is the first number minus the second. The test risk is an estimate of the first number, so
$\hat R_T(\hat h) - \hat R_S(\hat h)$ estimates the gap. Some texts, Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David's among them, call the
true risk $R(\hat h)$ itself the generalisation error.

## Why the gap of a learned predictor is biased

For a predictor $h$ fixed before the sample is drawn, each training loss is an independent draw with mean $R(h)$, so
$\expect_S[\hat R_S(h)] = R(h)$ and the expected gap is zero. A learned predictor depends on the sample, and this
breaks the argument.

**Empirical risk minimisation is optimistic.** Let $\hat h$ minimise $\hat R_S$ over a class $\Hcal$, and let $h^*$ be
the member of $\Hcal$ with the smallest true risk. Three steps give the direction of the bias.

1. $\hat R_S(\hat h) \le \hat R_S(h^*)$ on every sample, because $\hat h$ minimises the empirical risk and $h^*$ is one
   of the candidates.
2. $\expect_S[\hat R_S(h^*)] = R(h^*)$, because $h^*$ does not depend on $S$.
3. $R(h^*) \le R(\hat h)$ on every sample, because $h^*$ has the smallest true risk in the class.

Taking expectations of the first and third inequalities and joining them through the second,

$$
\expect_S\brack{\hat R_S(\hat h)} \le R(h^*) \le \expect_S\brack{R(\hat h)}.
$$

On average the training error of <Gloss name="erm" /> is below even the best risk achievable in the class, and the
expected gap is non-negative.

**The gap equals the effect of seeing each point.** A second identity says where the gap comes from for any algorithm.
Let $S^{(i)}$ be the sample with its $i$-th point $z_i = (x_i, y_i)$ replaced by an independent fresh point. Then

$$
\expect_S\brack{R(A(S)) - \hat R_S(A(S))} = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \expect\brack{\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i)}
$$

<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. The right side is the average increase in the loss on a point when that point is
removed from training. An algorithm whose output barely changes when one point changes has a small expected gap. This is
the starting point of <NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic stability</NoteLink>.

<Derivation title="The replace-one identity">
Write $\ell(h, z)$ for the loss of $h$ on the pair $z$. The training risk averages $\ell(A(S), z_i)$ over $i$, so
$\expect[\hat R_S(A(S))] = \frac1m\sum_i\expect[\ell(A(S), z_i)]$.

For the true risk, draw a fresh point $z_i'$ independent of $S$. Then $\expect[R(A(S))] = \expect[\ell(A(S), z_i')]$ for
every $i$, because $R$ is the expected loss on an independent draw. Now exchange the names of $z_i$ and $z_i'$. The pair
$(S, z_i')$ has the same distribution as the pair $(S^{(i)}, z_i)$, because both consist of $m + 1$ independent draws
from $\Dcal$ with one of them held out. Hence $\expect[\ell(A(S), z_i')] = \expect[\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)]$. Averaging
over $i$ and subtracting the training risk gives the identity.
</Derivation>

**An exact gap for least squares.** Fit a linear model with $p$ coefficients by least squares to $m$ points with fixed
inputs and targets $y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$, where the noise has variance $\sigma^2$. Measure the error at the
same inputs with fresh noise. Whatever the regression function $f$, the expected training error and the expected error
on fresh noise differ by

$$
\frac{2p\sigma^2}{m}
$$

<Cite id="hastie2009" />. The gap grows in proportion to the number of fitted parameters and shrinks in proportion to
the sample size. With $m = 50$ points, $p = 10$ coefficients, $\sigma^2 = 1$ and a correct linear model, the expected
training error is $\sigma^2(1 - p/m) = 0.8$, the expected error on fresh noise is $\sigma^2(1 + p/m) = 1.2$, and the gap
is $0.4$.

<Derivation title="The least-squares gap">
Stack the inputs into the design $\Xmat$ ($m \times p$, full column rank) and the targets into
$\yvec = \fvec + \epsilonb$, with $\fvec$ the vector of $f(x_i)$ and $\epsilonb$ independent noise with mean 0 and
variance $\sigma^2$. Least squares predicts $\hat\yvec = \Hmat\yvec$ with the hat matrix
$\Hmat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$, an orthogonal projection of rank $p$, so $\trace\Hmat = p$.

The training residual is $(\Imat - \Hmat)\yvec = (\Imat - \Hmat)\fvec + (\Imat - \Hmat)\epsilonb$. The cross term has
mean 0, and $\expect\norm{(\Imat - \Hmat)\epsilonb}^2 = \sigma^2\trace(\Imat - \Hmat) = \sigma^2(m - p)$. So the
expected training error is $\frac1m\norm{(\Imat - \Hmat)\fvec}^2 + \sigma^2(m - p)/m$.

On fresh noise $\epsilonb'$ at the same inputs, the error vector is
$\Hmat\yvec - \fvec - \epsilonb' = -(\Imat - \Hmat)\fvec + \Hmat\epsilonb - \epsilonb'$. The three terms are
uncorrelated, and $\expect\norm{\Hmat\epsilonb}^2 =
\sigma^2\trace\Hmat = \sigma^2 p$. So the expected error is $\frac1m\norm{(\Imat - \Hmat)\fvec}^2 + \sigma^2p/m +
\sigma^2$.

Subtracting, the bias terms cancel and the gap is $\sigma^2 + \sigma^2p/m - \sigma^2(m - p)/m = 2p\sigma^2/m$.
</Derivation>

**The extreme case.** A model that can fit any labelling has a training error near zero whatever the labels are. Zhang
et al. trained standard <NoteLink to="convolutional-networks">convolutional networks</NoteLink> on
<Gloss name="cifar-10" /> images with labels replaced by random ones. The networks fit the random labels exactly, so
their training error was zero, while their test accuracy could only be chance, 10% <Cite id="zhang2017" />. The gap was
the whole test error, about $0.9$. The same architectures trained on the true labels had small gaps, so the capacity of
the class alone does not decide the gap of the trained network. This observation is the subject of
<NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">the limits of uniform convergence</NoteLink>.

## Bounding the gap

A learned predictor's gap cannot be bounded by looking at that predictor alone, because the predictor was chosen using
$S$. Bounds instead hold for every predictor the learner could have returned. If the learner chooses from a class
$\Hcal$, then

$$
R(\hat h) - \hat R_S(\hat h) \le \sup_{h \in \Hcal}\paren{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)},
$$

whatever rule chose $\hat h$. The right side, the uniform deviation, involves only the class and the sample. For a
finite class of $N$ predictors and losses in $[0, 1]$, <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's
inequality</NoteLink> and the <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> give, with probability at least
$1 - \delta$,

$$
R(\hat h) - \hat R_S(\hat h) \le \sqrt{\frac{\ln N + \ln(1/\delta)}{2m}}.
$$

Infinite classes replace $\ln N$ by a capacity such as the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC
dimension</NoteLink> or the <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink>; the general theory is
<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink>. Bounds that use the algorithm rather than the class
follow from the replace-one identity above (stability) or from a prior over predictors
(<NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>).

## Measuring the gap

A held-out sample measures the gap with a known accuracy. Conditional on $S$, the test losses of $\hat h$ are
independent and bounded, so for <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> Hoeffding's inequality gives, with
probability at least $1 - \delta$,

$$
\abs{\hat R_T(\hat h) - R(\hat h)} \le \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2m_{\text{test}}}}.
$$

The training error $\hat R_S(\hat h)$ is known exactly, so the same interval, shifted, covers the gap.

**Worked example.** A classifier has training error $0.02$ on its training set and makes 110 mistakes on a held-out set
of $m_{\text{test}} = 1\,000$ points, a test error of $0.11$.

1. **The estimate.** The estimated gap is $0.11 - 0.02 = 0.09$.
2. **Its accuracy.** At $\delta = 0.05$ the Hoeffding radius is $\sqrt{\ln(40)/2\,000} = \sqrt{3.689/2\,000} = 0.043$.
3. **The interval.** With probability at least $0.95$, the true risk lies in $[0.067, 0.153]$ and the gap in
   $[0.047, 0.133]$. The gap is positive: the classifier overfits.
4. **Sample size.** Measuring the gap to within $\pm 0.01$ at the same confidence needs
   $m_{\text{test}} \ge \ln(40)/(2 \cdot 0.01^2) = 18\,445$ test points.

The exact binomial tail gives a shorter interval than Hoeffding's, especially for small
<NoteLink to="accuracy-and-balanced-accuracy">error rates</NoteLink> (see <NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set
bounds</NoteLink>).

<NoteLink to="cross-validation">Cross-validation</NoteLink> reuses the data to estimate the expected risk of the
algorithm at sample size close to $m$, which is a different quantity from the risk of the one predictor $\hat h$.

## Pitfalls

- **A small gap is not a good model.** A constant predictor has a tiny gap and a large error. The gap measures
  overfitting; underfitting shows as a large training error with a small gap.
- **Reusing the test set shrinks the apparent gap.** Each model choice made by looking at the test error makes the test
  set part of the training data. After many rounds, the test error is optimistic for the same reason the training error
  is <Cite id="dwork2015" />. The fix is a fresh test set or a protocol from
  <NoteLink to="adaptive-data-analysis">adaptive data analysis</NoteLink>.
- **Distribution shift is not overfitting.** A test set drawn from a slightly different distribution adds a second gap.
  Recht et al. built new <NoteLink to="test-sets">test sets</NoteLink> for CIFAR-10 and <Gloss name="imagenet" />
  following the original collection process and found accuracy drops of 3–15% and 11–14%; they attribute the drops to
  harder images rather than to <NoteLink to="test-set-reuse">test-set reuse</NoteLink> <Cite id="recht2019" />.
- **Leakage hides the gap.** Duplicates between training and test sets, or features computed using test labels, make the
  test error optimistic (see <NoteLink to="data-leakage">data leakage</NoteLink>).
- **The gap can be negative on one sample.** $R(\hat h) - \hat R_S(\hat h)$ is a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random
  variable</NoteLink>. Only its average, for ERM, is guaranteed to be non-negative.
