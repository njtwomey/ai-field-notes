<Definition>
Let $R^*$ be the <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes risk</NoteLink>, $\Hcal$ a hypothesis class with best member
$h^*_\Hcal$, $\hat h$ the <Gloss name="erm">empirical risk minimiser</Gloss> on a sample $S$ of $m$ points, and
$\tilde h$ the hypothesis an optimiser actually returns. The expected **excess risk** of $\tilde h$ splits into three
terms <Cite id="bottou2007" />:

$$
\expect\brack{R(\tilde h) - R^*} = \underbrace{R(h^*_\Hcal) - R^*}_{\text{approximation}} + \underbrace{\expect\brack{R(\hat h) - R(h^*_\Hcal)}}_{\text{estimation}} + \underbrace{\expect\brack{R(\tilde h) - R(\hat h)}}_{\text{optimisation}}.
$$

A richer class lowers the first term, more data lower the second, and more computation lowers the third. When the
number of examples or the computing time is limited, lowering one term raises another.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A learned predictor can fall short of the best possible predictor for three separate reasons.

- **The class is too small.** No member of $\Hcal$ matches the target. Straight lines cannot fit a sine, however much
  data there is.
- **The sample is too small.** The empirical risk is a noisy estimate of the true risk, so its minimiser is not the best
  member of the class.
- **The optimiser stops early.** Training stops at a hypothesis whose empirical risk is above the minimum, because
  time ran out or because exact minimisation is too expensive.

The <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> note splits the excess risk into
the first two terms. Bottou and Bousquet added the third and showed that it changes the advice when computing time,
not data, is the scarce resource <Cite id="bottou2007" />. With unlimited time, the best plan is to optimise exactly and
choose the class to balance approximation against estimation. With limited time and abundant data, a crude optimiser
that processes more examples can beat an exact optimiser that processes fewer.

## The three-way decomposition

Write $\Ecal_{\text{app}}$, $\Ecal_{\text{est}}$ and $\Ecal_{\text{opt}}$ for the three terms in the definition. The
decomposition is a telescoping sum: adding and subtracting $R(h^*_\Hcal)$ and $R(\hat h)$ in
$R(\tilde h) - R^*$ gives the three differences, and taking expectations over the sample gives the definition. The
expectation is over $S$ and over any randomness of the optimiser.

**Signs.** The approximation error is non-negative, because no predictor beats the Bayes risk. The estimation error is
non-negative, because $h^*_\Hcal$ has the smallest true risk in $\Hcal$ and $\hat h$ is in $\Hcal$. The optimisation
error can be negative: the returned $\tilde h$ has a larger empirical risk than $\hat h$ but can have a smaller true
risk. This is how <NoteLink to="early-stopping">early stopping</NoteLink> regularises.

**What controls each term.**

| Term | Depends on | Falls when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\Ecal_{\text{app}}$ | $\Hcal$ and $\Dcal$ only | the class grows |
| $\Ecal_{\text{est}}$ | $\Hcal$, $m$ and $\Dcal$ | the sample grows; rises as the class grows |
| $\Ecal_{\text{opt}}$ | the optimiser, its tolerance $\rho$ and its running time | the tolerance shrinks |

**Bounding estimation and optimisation together.** Suppose the optimiser returns $\tilde h \in \Hcal$ with
$\hat R_S(\tilde h) \le \hat R_S(\hat h) + \rho$ for a tolerance $\rho \ge 0$. Write
$U = \sup_{h \in \Hcal}\abs{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)}$ for the <NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform deviation</NoteLink>
of the class on the sample. Then

$$
R(\tilde h) - R(h^*_\Hcal) = \underbrace{R(\tilde h) - \hat R_S(\tilde h)}_{\le U} + \underbrace{\hat R_S(\tilde h) - \hat R_S(\hat h)}_{\le \rho} + \underbrace{\hat R_S(\hat h) - \hat R_S(h^*_\Hcal)}_{\le 0} + \underbrace{\hat R_S(h^*_\Hcal) - R(h^*_\Hcal)}_{\le U}.
$$

Each bracket is bounded as marked: the first and last by the definition of $U$, the second by the tolerance, and the
third because $\hat h$ minimises the empirical risk. Taking expectations,

$$
\Ecal_{\text{est}} + \Ecal_{\text{opt}} \le 2\,\expect[U] + \rho.
$$

The statistical part is the uniform deviation; for a class of linear predictors in
$d$ dimensions with bounded loss, $\expect[U] \le c\sqrt{d/m}$ for a constant $c$ <Cite id="bottou2007" />. The
computational part is the tolerance. There is no point making $\rho$ much smaller than $\expect[U]$: the extra
optimisation is lost in the statistical noise. This is the "decompose" move of the
<NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, extended by one term.

Under conditions that give <NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">fast rates</NoteLink> (strongly convex losses,
low noise), the same argument gives the sharper form
$\Ecal \le c\paren{\Ecal_{\text{app}} + ((d/m)\ln(m/d))^\alpha + \rho}$ with an exponent $\alpha \in [\frac12, 1]$ set
by the noise <Cite id="bottou2007" />. The rates themselves are the subject of
<NoteLink to="rates-and-consistency">excess risk and rates</NoteLink>.

## Small-scale and large-scale learning

The learner chooses three things: the class $\Hcal$, the number of examples $m$ it uses and the tolerance $\rho$. It
minimises $\Ecal = \Ecal_{\text{app}} + \Ecal_{\text{est}} + \Ecal_{\text{opt}}$ subject to two budgets, the examples
available $m \le m_{\max}$ and the computing time $T(\Hcal, m, \rho) \le T_{\max}$ <Cite id="bottou2007" />.

- **Small-scale learning.** The number of examples is the binding constraint. Time is plentiful, so $\rho$ can be made
  negligible and $m = m_{\max}$. Only $\Ecal_{\text{app}}$ and $\Ecal_{\text{est}}$ remain, and the problem is the
  classical choice of class: <NoteLink to="model-selection">model selection</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="regularisation">regularisation</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural
  risk minimisation</NoteLink>.
- **Large-scale learning.** Time is the binding constraint. Using all the examples with a small tolerance is too
  slow, so the learner must trade: fewer examples optimised accurately, or more examples optimised crudely. The answer
  depends on how the optimiser's running time grows with $m$ and with $1/\rho$.

For <NoteLink to="linear-models">linear models</NoteLink> in $d$ dimensions with a convex, smooth loss, Bottou and
Bousquet compared four optimisers. Here $\kappa$ is the condition number of the Hessian at the optimum and $\nu$ a
constant tied to the gradient noise <Cite id="bottou2007" />:

| Optimiser | Cost of one step | Time to reach tolerance $\rho$ | Time to reach excess error $\epsilon$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> | $O(md)$ | $O(md\kappa\ln(1/\rho))$ | $O\paren{\frac{d^2\kappa}{\epsilon^{1/\alpha}}\ln^2\frac1\epsilon}$ |
| Second-order gradient descent | $O(d^2 + md)$ | $O((d^2 + md)\ln\ln(1/\rho))$ | $O\paren{\frac{d^2}{\epsilon^{1/\alpha}}\ln\frac1\epsilon\ln\ln\frac1\epsilon}$ |
| <Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> | $O(d)$ | $O(d\nu\kappa^2/\rho)$ | $O(d\nu\kappa^2/\epsilon)$ |
| Second-order stochastic gradient descent | $O(d^2)$ | $O(d^2\nu/\rho)$ | $O(d^2\nu/\epsilon)$ |

As optimisers, the stochastic methods are the worst: their time to reach a
tolerance grows like $1/\rho$, against $\ln(1/\rho)$ for gradient descent. As learners, they are the best: their time to
reach an excess error $\epsilon$ grows like $1/\epsilon$ and does not depend on the statistical exponent $\alpha$, while
gradient descent's grows like $\epsilon^{-1/\alpha}$, which is $1/\epsilon^2$ in the slow-rate case $\alpha = \frac12$.
A step of stochastic gradient descent costs $O(d)$ whatever $m$ is, so within a fixed time it can visit far more
examples, and the estimation error it saves outweighs the optimisation error it incurs. This is the theoretical case
for <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-theory">stochastic gradient descent</NoteLink> on large datasets
<Cite id="bottou2018" />.

## Worked example

**A small-scale problem.** The <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> note
fits polynomials to $m = 20$ noisy points of $\sin(2\pi x)$ by exact least squares, so $\Ecal_{\text{opt}} = 0$. Lines
have approximation error $0.196$ and mean estimation error $0.030$; cubics have $0.0044$ and $0.017$. The cubic class
wins, with excess risk $0.021$ against $0.226$. Here the trade-off is the classical two-way one, settled by the choice
of class.

**A large-scale budget.** Take a linear model with $d = 100$ parameters, $m_{\max} = 10^7$ examples and a
budget of $T_{\max} = 10^9$ operations. The calculation sets every constant ($c$, $\kappa$, $\nu$) to 1 and uses the
fast-rate form $\Ecal_{\text{est}} \approx d/m$ (dropping the logarithm) and $\Ecal_{\text{opt}} \approx \rho$. The
approximation error is the same for both optimisers and is left out.

1. **Gradient descent on all the data.** One pass costs $md = 10^9$ operations, the whole budget. It manages one step,
   and $\rho$ stays large.
2. **Gradient descent on a subsample.** With $m$ examples, reaching tolerance $\rho$ costs $md\ln(1/\rho)$ operations,
   so $m = 10^7/\ln(1/\rho)$. The excess error is $d/m + \rho = 10^{-5}\ln(1/\rho) + \rho$. Setting its derivative in
   $\rho$ to zero gives $\rho = 10^{-5}$, so $\ln(1/\rho) = 11.5$ and $m = 8.7 \times 10^5$. The excess error is
   $100/(8.7 \times 10^5) + 10^{-5} = 1.25 \times 10^{-4}$.
3. **Stochastic gradient descent.** Each step costs $d = 100$ operations, so the budget buys $10^7$ steps, one pass over
   all $10^7$ examples. The estimation error is $d/m = 10^{-5}$, and the tolerance after $t = 10^7$ steps is
   $\rho = 1/t = 10^{-7}$. The excess error is $1.01 \times 10^{-5}$.

Under the same budget, stochastic gradient descent reaches an excess error twelve times smaller. Gradient descent is
held back by its cost per step: to optimise accurately it must discard 91% of the data, and the discarded data are worth
more than the accuracy. The numbers depend on the constants that were set to 1, but the conclusion follows from the
orders in the table.

## Pitfalls

- **The terms are not observable separately.** Each involves a true risk, and $h^*_\Hcal$ and $R^*$ are unknown. On
  synthetic problems they can be computed; on real data only their sum, the test error, is measured.
- **Optimisation error is not always harmful.** Stopping before the empirical minimum can lower the true risk. The
  decomposition then shows a negative optimisation error offsetting part of the estimation error.
- **The decomposition depends on the class.** Enlarging $\Hcal$ moves error from the approximation term to the
  estimation term without changing the total. Comparing the terms of two models only makes sense for a common class.
- **Over-parameterised models break the classical picture.** Large networks have approximation and optimisation errors
  near zero and an estimation error far smaller than capacity-based bounds predict. See
  <NoteLink to="double-descent">double descent</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign
  overfitting</NoteLink>.
- **Not the bias–variance decomposition.** The <NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance
  decomposition</NoteLink> splits the squared error of a fixed procedure around its average prediction. The
  approximation–estimation split compares against the best predictor in the class. The two coincide only in special
  cases.
