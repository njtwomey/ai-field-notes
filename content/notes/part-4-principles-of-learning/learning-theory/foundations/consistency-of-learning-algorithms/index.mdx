<Definition>
A learning rule maps each sample $S$ of $m$ independent draws from a distribution $\Dcal$ to a predictor $h_m$. Its risk
$R(h_m)$ is a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> through $S$. The rule is **consistent** for
$\Dcal$ if $\expect[R(h_m)] \to R^*$ as $m \to \infty$, where $R^*$ is the <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes
risk</NoteLink> of $\Dcal$, and **strongly consistent** if $R(h_m) \to R^*$ with probability one. It is **universally
consistent** if it is consistent for every distribution $\Dcal$ <Cite id="devroye1996" />. Universally consistent rules
exist, but none has a rate of convergence that holds for every distribution.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Consistency asks the weakest question one can ask of a learner: given unlimited data, does it eventually do as well as
anything could? A rule that fails this test has an error floor that no amount of data removes. A rule that passes it
may still be slow.

Two things can stop a learner from reaching the Bayes risk. Its class may be too small, which leaves an approximation
error at every sample size. Or its estimates may stay noisy, which leaves an estimation error that never averages away.
A universally consistent rule must avoid both on every distribution at once. It must let its class grow with the
sample, so that the approximation error vanishes, and grow it slowly enough that the estimation error vanishes too.
<NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours">$k$-nearest neighbours</NoteLink> with $k \to \infty$ and $k/m \to 0$ is the
standard example: neighbourhoods shrink, so the rule becomes local enough to follow any target, and the number of
neighbours grows, so their average label settles down.

## Definitions

Let $\Dcal$ be a distribution on $\Xcal \times \Ycal$, $\ell$ a loss, $R(h)$ the risk of a predictor, and
$R^* = \inf_h R(h)$ the Bayes risk over all measurable predictors. A learning rule is a sequence of maps
$(h_m)_{m \ge 1}$ from samples of size $m$ to predictors.

- **Consistent for $\Dcal$:** $\expect[R(h_m)] \to R^*$. Since $R(h_m) \ge R^*$, this is the same as $R(h_m) \to R^*$ in
  probability.
- **Strongly consistent for $\Dcal$:** $R(h_m) \to R^*$ almost surely.
- **Universally (strongly) consistent:** (strongly) consistent for every $\Dcal$ on $\Xcal \times \Ycal$.

A different notion compares with a class rather than with the Bayes risk. <Gloss name="erm" /> over a class $\Hcal$ is
**consistent for $\Hcal$** if $R(\hat h_m) \to \inf_{h \in \Hcal} R(h)$ for every $\Dcal$. For binary classification
this holds exactly when $\Hcal$ has finite <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>, so that
<NoteLink to="uniform-convergence">uniform convergence</NoteLink> holds over the class <Cite id="vapnik1998" />. The
statement is part of the <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-statistical-learning">fundamental theorem of statistical
learning</NoteLink>. On an infinite domain, ERM over a fixed class with finite VC dimension is not universally
consistent: for some distribution the class contains no Bayes predictor, and the approximation error stays at every
sample size.

Universal consistency is therefore a property of rules whose class grows with $m$: local averaging rules,
<NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural risk minimisation</NoteLink> over a sequence of nested classes,
and sieve estimators <Cite id="devroye1996" />.

## Stone's theorem

Stone's theorem gives conditions under which a local averaging rule is universally consistent
<Cite id="stone1977" />. Such a rule estimates the regression function $\eta(x) = \expect[y \mid x]$ by a weighted
average of the training labels,

$$
\hat\eta_m(x) = \sum_{i=1}^m W_{m,i}(x)\,y_i,
$$

with non-negative weights $W_{m,i}(x)$ that depend on $x$ and the training inputs and sum to 1. For classification with
labels in $\set{0, 1}$, the rule predicts 1 when $\hat\eta_m(x) > \frac12$. Write $X$ for a new input drawn from the
marginal of $\Dcal$, independent of the sample. The theorem's three conditions are, for every distribution of $X$
<Cite id="devroye1996" />:

1. **No point dominates in aggregate.** There is a constant $c$ such that
   $\expect\brack{\sum_i W_{m,i}(X) f(X_i)} \le c\,\expect[f(X)]$ for every non-negative $f$ with
   $\expect[f(X)] < \infty$.
2. **The weights become local.** For every $a > 0$,
   $\expect\brack{\sum_i W_{m,i}(X)\,\indicator[\norm{X_i - X} > a]} \to 0$.
3. **No single weight stays large.** $\expect\brack{\max_i W_{m,i}(X)} \to 0$.

Under these conditions the classification rule is universally consistent, and the regression estimate satisfies
$\expect[(\hat\eta_m(X) - \eta(X))^2] \to 0$ whenever $\expect[y^2] < \infty$.

The second condition controls the bias: the estimate at $x$ uses labels from points that are ever closer to $x$, so it
averages values of $\eta$ near $\eta(x)$. The third condition controls the variance: no single noisy label carries a
fixed share of the estimate, so the noise averages out. The first is a technical regularity that rules out weights that
concentrate on a few points for some input distributions.

**$k$-nearest neighbours.** The $k$-NN rule gives weight $1/k$ to each of the $k$ nearest training inputs and 0 to the
rest. The largest weight is $1/k$, so the third condition holds when $k \to \infty$. The $k$
<NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink> lie within a radius that tends to zero when $k/m \to 0$,
which gives the second condition. The first holds with a constant that depends only on the dimension. Hence
<Cite id="devroye1996" />:

> If $k \to \infty$ and $k/m \to 0$, the $k$-nearest-neighbour rule is universally consistent.

The choice $k = \lceil\sqrt m\,\rceil$ satisfies both conditions. Histogram rules with cells of side $h_m \to 0$ and
$mh_m^d \to \infty$, and kernel rules with the same bandwidth conditions, are universally consistent by the same
theorem. The details for nearest neighbours are in <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-consistency">nearest-neighbour
consistency</NoteLink>.

**When $k$ stays fixed.** Each of the two conditions on $k$ is necessary for some distribution. With $k = 1$ in binary
classification, the risk of the nearest-neighbour rule converges to $\expect[2\eta(X)(1 - \eta(X))]$, which lies between
$R^*$ and $2R^*(1 - R^*)$ <Cite id="cover1967" />. It equals $R^*$ only when $\eta(X)$ takes no values other than $0$,
$\frac12$ and $1$. The nearest neighbour comes ever closer to $x$, but its label is still a single noisy draw.

## No uniform rate

Universal consistency says the risk converges for every distribution. It says nothing about how fast, and no
statement about speed can hold for every distribution.

**Theorem (arbitrarily slow rates).** Let $a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \dots$ be positive numbers converging to zero, with
$a_1 \le \frac1{16}$. For every sequence of classification rules there is a distribution with $R^* = 0$ such that
$\expect[R(h_m)] \ge a_m$ for every $m$ <Cite id="devroye1996" />.

The sequence $a_m$ can be chosen to decrease as slowly as desired. Every rule, including every universally consistent
one, is that slow on some problem. A finite-sample version is starker: for every rule, every sample size $m$ and every
$\epsilon > 0$, some distribution with $R^* = 0$ gives $\expect[R(h_m)] \ge \frac12 - \epsilon$
<Cite id="devroye1996" />. The construction puts the input mass on many more points than $m$, with labels set by a
target the rule cannot guess from the few points it has seen. It is the argument of the
<NoteLink to="no-free-lunch-theorem">no free lunch theorem</NoteLink> in a form that applies to one rule at a time.

**Rates need assumptions.** Every rate in <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">learning theory</NoteLink> comes
with a restriction on the distribution:

- **A restricted class.** Relative to a class of VC dimension $d$, ERM's excess risk over the best in class falls like
  $\sqrt{d/m}$, for every distribution. The price is the approximation error, which this rate ignores.
- **Smoothness.** If $\eta$ is Lipschitz on $[0, 1]^p$, the best achievable rate for the squared error of a regression
  estimate is $m^{-2/(2 + p)}$ <Cite id="tsybakov2009" />. See
  <NoteLink to="minimax-rates-for-nonparametric-regression">minimax rates for nonparametric regression</NoteLink>.
- **Low noise.** Margin and noise conditions on $\eta$ near the decision boundary give rates faster than
  $1/\sqrt m$ (see <NoteLink to="rates-and-consistency">excess risk and rates</NoteLink>).

## Worked example

**Fixed $k$ against growing $k$.** In the figure, the labels are $y = \sin(\pi x) + \sigma\varepsilon$ with
$\sigma = 0.5$, so the Bayes risk for <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> is $\sigma^2 = 0.25$.

1. **$k = 1$.** The prediction at $x$ is one noisy label from a point near $x$. Its error on a fresh noisy target is the
   sum of two independent noise variances, so the risk tends to $2\sigma^2 = 0.5$. At $m = 320$ it is about $0.49$. The
   excess risk stays near $0.25$, the whole Bayes risk again.
2. **$k = 10$.** The average of 10 noisy labels has noise variance $\sigma^2/10$, so the risk tends to
   $\sigma^2(1 + 1/10) = 0.275$, which is about what the figure shows at $m = 320$. The excess risk stays at $0.025$.
3. **$k = \sqrt m$.** At $m = 320$, $k = 18$ and the risk is about $0.266$. The noise term $\sigma^2/k$ keeps falling as
   $k$ grows, and the neighbourhoods keep shrinking because $k/m = 1/\sqrt m \to 0$. Only this rule's excess risk tends
   to zero.

**The cost of dimension.** Under the Lipschitz assumption, halving the excess risk multiplies the required sample size
by $2^{(2 + p)/2}$. In one dimension that factor is $2.8$. In ten dimensions it is $2^6 = 64$. Consistency holds in
every dimension; the sample sizes at which it becomes visible do not.

**Classification.** Suppose $\eta(x)$ is $0.1$ or $0.9$ everywhere, so $R^* = 0.1$. The 1-NN risk tends to
$2 \times 0.1 \times 0.9 = 0.18$, nearly twice the Bayes risk, whatever the sample size. A $k$-NN rule with
$k = \lceil\sqrt m\,\rceil$ tends to $0.1$.

## Pitfalls

- **Consistency is asymptotic.** A consistent rule can have a large error at every sample size anyone will collect, and
  an inconsistent rule, such as 1-NN, is often competitive in practice when the noise is small.
- **Consistency for one class is not consistency.** ERM over a fixed class converges to the best in the class, not to
  the Bayes risk. The two meet only when the class contains a Bayes predictor.
- **A rate holds only under an assumption on the distribution.** "Converges at rate $1/\sqrt m$" always means "for
  distributions satisfying a stated condition". The slow-rates theorem shows that some condition is unavoidable.
- **Consistency of the surrogate is not consistency of the classifier.** Minimising a
  <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">convex surrogate</NoteLink> loss consistently gives a consistent classifier only for
  <NoteLink to="classification-calibrated-losses">classification-calibrated losses</NoteLink>.
