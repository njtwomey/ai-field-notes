<Definition>
The **inductive bias** of a learning algorithm is any basis it uses for choosing one hypothesis over another other than
agreement with the training data <Cite id="mitchell1980" />. It takes two forms. A **restriction bias** limits the
hypotheses the learner can return to a class $\Hcal$. A **preference bias** ranks the hypotheses, through a penalty, a
prior or the order in which a search visits them. Every learner that predicts better than chance on unseen inputs has
an inductive bias, and it predicts well when the bias matches the target.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A finite sample fixes the labels of the points it contains and nothing else. Many hypotheses agree with the sample and
disagree everywhere else. Choosing among them needs a rule that the data do not supply: prefer straight lines, prefer
smooth functions, prefer short descriptions, copy the nearest example. That rule is the inductive bias.

too rigid (the line) or free enough to fit the noise (the high-degree interpolant).

## Why learning needs a bias

Mitchell's argument makes the need exact for classification over a finite set of inputs <Cite id="mitchell1980" />.
Suppose the learner has no restriction: every subset of the inputs is a possible concept. After seeing labelled
examples, the concepts consistent with them are those that agree on the seen inputs, with any labels on the rest. For
each unseen input, exactly half of these consistent concepts label it positive. An unbiased learner therefore has no
basis to classify any unseen input. It can only memorise the training labels and look them up.

The <NoteLink to="no-free-lunch-theorem">no free lunch theorem</NoteLink> makes the same point on average: over all
targets, every learner has off-training-set error $\frac12$. Its consequence is that the advantage of any learner on any
problem comes entirely from its bias.

**Counting the concepts.** Describe inputs by 5 binary features, so there are $2^5 = 32$ distinct inputs and
$2^{32} \approx 4.3 \times 10^9$ concepts. After the labels of 10 distinct inputs are seen, $2^{22} \approx 4.2 \times
10^6$ concepts remain consistent, and for each of the 22 unseen inputs exactly half of them, $2^{21}$, label it
positive. The data have ruled out 99.9% of the concepts and still say nothing about any unseen input.

## Restriction and preference

**Restriction bias** chooses the class. Conjunctions of conditions on the features are a classical example: each feature
is required to be 1, required to be 0 or ignored, which gives $3^5 = 243$ concepts out of $2^{32}$, about one in
$1.8 \times 10^7$ <Cite id="mitchell1980" />. Linear classifiers, polynomials of bounded degree and trees of bounded
depth are restrictions in the same sense. A restriction enters generalisation bounds through the size or capacity of
$\Hcal$: $\ln\abs{\Hcal}$ for a finite class, the <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">VC dimension</NoteLink>
or the <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> for an infinite one.

**Preference bias** keeps a large class and ranks its members. <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">Ridge
regression</NoteLink> prefers small weights; decision-tree learners prefer short trees, through greedy growth and
pruning; the <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">support vector machine</NoteLink> prefers a large margin; Bayesian
methods prefer hypotheses with high prior probability. A preference enters bounds through a weight $\pi(h)$ chosen
before the data. The <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink> pays $\ln(1/\pi(h))$ for the hypothesis returned,
so a hypothesis described in $b$ bits under a prefix code, with $\pi(h) = 2^{-b}$, pays $b\ln 2$ nats. PAC-Bayes bounds
pay the divergence between a posterior and the prior (see <NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>).
<NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">Structural risk minimisation</NoteLink> combines the two: a nested sequence
of restricted classes with a preference for the smaller ones.

Both forms trade the same two errors. A strong bias leaves few hypotheses to choose from, so the choice is reliable and
the <NoteLink to="approximation-estimation-optimisation-trade-off">estimation error</NoteLink> is small, but the best
available hypothesis may be far from the target, so the <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">approximation
error</NoteLink> is large. A weak bias reverses the balance. In the language of the
<NoteLink to="bias-variance-decomposition">bias–variance decomposition</NoteLink>, a stronger inductive bias usually
means more statistical bias and less variance.

In the <NoteLink to="statistical-learning-theory">recipe for a generalisation bound</NoteLink>, the inductive bias is
what the "make it uniform" step pays for. A restriction is paid for with a capacity term, and a preference with a prior
term. An unrestricted learner on an infinite domain has infinite capacity, and no bound of this kind exists for it.

## Worked example: sample size with and without a restriction

A target concept on the 32 inputs above is a conjunction. A learner sees $m$ examples drawn from a distribution
$\Dcal$ on the inputs and returns a concept consistent with them. The realisable finite-class bound of
<NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">realisable learning</NoteLink> says that any consistent learner over a
class $\Hcal$ that contains the target has risk at most $\epsilon$ with probability at least $1 - \delta$ once

$$
m \ge \frac{\ln\abs{\Hcal} + \ln(1/\delta)}{\epsilon}
$$

<Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. Take $\epsilon = 0.1$ and $\delta = 0.05$, so $\ln(1/\delta) = 3.00$.

1. **Restricted to conjunctions.** $\ln 243 = 5.49$, so $m \ge (5.49 + 3.00)/0.1 = 85$ examples suffice, whatever
   $\Dcal$ is.
2. **Unrestricted.** $\ln 2^{32} = 32\ln 2 = 22.18$, so the bound needs $m \ge (22.18 + 3.00)/0.1 = 252$ examples.
3. **Reading the second number.** Under the uniform distribution, 252 draws leave each of the 32 inputs unseen with
   probability $(31/32)^{252} \approx 3 \times 10^{-4}$. The unrestricted learner reaches risk 0.1 only by seeing almost
   every input and memorising its label. Its guarantee comes from coverage, not from generalisation, and on a domain
   with more inputs than examples it gives nothing.

The restricted learner needs a third of the examples and generalises to inputs it has never seen. It does so only
because the target is a conjunction. If the target were an arbitrary concept, the conjunction learner would have a
large approximation error that no sample size removes.

## Inductive bias in common learners

| Learner | Restriction | Preference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <NoteLink to="linear-regression">Linear regression</NoteLink> | Linear functions of the features | Least squares |
| Ridge and lasso | Linear functions | Small $\ell_2$ or $\ell_1$ norm |
| <NoteLink to="k-nearest-neighbours">$k$-nearest neighbours</NoteLink> | None beyond the metric | Nearby inputs have similar labels |
| <NoteLink to="decision-tree">Decision trees</NoteLink> | Axis-aligned splits | Short trees, informative early splits |
| <NoteLink to="support-vector-machine">Support vector machines</NoteLink> | Linear in a kernel's feature space | Large margin |
| <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian processes</NoteLink> | Set by the kernel | Prior given by the kernel: smoothness and length scale |
| <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">Convolutional networks</NoteLink> | Local filters shared across positions | Set by the optimiser and the initialisation |
| Over-parameterised networks | Very large classes | Implicit: <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">small norm or large margin</NoteLink> reached by <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> |

Weight sharing in a convolutional layer restricts the class to functions that commute with translation, an assumption
about images built into the architecture. <NoteLink to="data-augmentation">Data augmentation</NoteLink> expresses the
same kind of assumption through the data instead: it asks the learner to give the same label to transformed copies of an
input. The optimiser is part of the bias as well: among the many networks that fit the data, gradient descent from a
small initialisation returns particular ones, which is the subject of <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit
regularisation</NoteLink>.

A bias can itself be learned. When many related tasks are available, the class or prior can be fitted to them, so that
a new task from the same family is learned from fewer examples <Cite id="baxter2000" />. This is the theoretical setting
of <NoteLink to="meta-learning">meta-learning</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Bias here is not the statistical <Gloss name="bias">bias of an estimator</Gloss>.** An inductive bias is an
  assumption; statistical bias is the difference between an estimator's mean and its target. A stronger inductive bias
  often increases the statistical bias, but the two words name different things.
- **"Assumption-free" learners are not.** $k$-nearest neighbours assumes that the metric reflects similarity of labels,
  and a neural network assumes whatever its architecture, initialisation and optimiser favour.
- **A bias that fits the benchmark may not fit the task.** A learner chosen because it wins on one family of problems
  carries a bias suited to that family <Cite id="wolpert1996" />. Its advantage transfers only to tasks with the same
  structure.
- **More data weakens a preference but not a restriction.** A preference is overruled when the data are strong enough;
  a restriction is not, and its approximation error stays at every sample size.
