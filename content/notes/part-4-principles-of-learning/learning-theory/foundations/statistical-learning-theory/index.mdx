<Definition>
**Statistical learning theory** studies learning from examples drawn independently from an unknown distribution. A
learner sees a sample $S$ of $m$ labelled examples from a distribution $\Dcal$, chooses a predictor $h$, and is judged
by its risk $R(h)$, the expected loss on a new example from $\Dcal$. The theory asks which learners have small risk, how
fast the risk falls as $m$ grows, and how many examples a target accuracy needs. Its answers are bounds that hold with
probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the sample, for every distribution in a stated family <Cite id="vapnik1998" />.
</Definition>

## The learning problem

The setting has five ingredients.

- **Data.** Pairs $(x, y)$ in $\Xcal \times \Ycal$ drawn from a distribution $\Dcal$ that the learner does not know. The
  sample $S = ((x_i, y_i))_{i=1}^m$ consists of $m$ independent draws from $\Dcal$
  (<Gloss name="iid" />).
- **A loss.** $\ell(h(x), y) \ge 0$ measures the cost of predicting $h(x)$ when the label is $y$: the
  <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">0–1 loss</NoteLink> $\indicator[h(x) \ne y]$ for classification, the
  <NoteLink to="regression-losses">squared loss</NoteLink> $(h(x) - y)^2$ for regression.
- **Risks.** The true risk $R(h) = \expect_{(x, y) \sim \Dcal}[\ell(h(x), y)]$ is the quantity the learner is judged by
  and cannot be computed.
  The empirical risk $\hat R_S(h) = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\ell(h(x_i), y_i)$ can be computed. The
  <NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes risk</NoteLink> $R^* = \inf_h R(h)$, over all measurable predictors, is the
  best any predictor can do.
- **A class.** A set $\Hcal$ of predictors the learner chooses from. The class encodes the learner's
  <NoteLink to="inductive-bias">inductive bias</NoteLink>.
- **A learning algorithm.** A map $A$ from samples to predictors. The central example is
  <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> (<Gloss name="erm" />), which
  returns $\hat h = \argmin_{h \in \Hcal}\hat R_S(h)$.

Two quantities describe how well a learned predictor $\hat h$ does. The <NoteLink to="generalisation-gap">generalisation
gap</NoteLink> $R(\hat h) - \hat R_S(\hat h)$ measures how far the risk of $\hat h$ exceeds its empirical risk. The
excess risk $R(\hat h) - R^*$ says how far it is from the best possible predictor. The theory bounds both, in terms of
$m$, the confidence $\delta$ and a measure of the size of $\Hcal$.

## The questions

- **Consistency.** Does the risk converge to the Bayes risk as $m \to \infty$? For every distribution? Some rules are
  universally consistent, such as $k$-nearest neighbours with $k \to \infty$ and $k/m \to 0$ (see
  <NoteLink to="consistency-of-learning-algorithms">consistency of learning algorithms</NoteLink>).
- **Learnability.** For which classes does some learner reach the best risk in the class, for every distribution, from
  finitely many examples? For binary classification the answer is the classes of finite
  <NoteLink to="vapnik-chervonenkis-dimension">Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) dimension</NoteLink>
  (see <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-statistical-learning">the fundamental theorem of statistical
  learning</NoteLink>).
- **Sample complexity.** How many examples are needed for accuracy $\epsilon$ with confidence $1 - \delta$? This is the
  question of <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">probably approximately correct (PAC)
  learning</NoteLink> <Cite id="valiant1984" />.
- **Rates.** How fast does the excess risk fall with $m$: like $1/\sqrt m$, like $1/m$, or more slowly? What conditions
  on $\Dcal$ decide it? (see <NoteLink to="rates-and-consistency">excess risk and rates</NoteLink>)
- **Lower bounds.** Can any learner do better than a given bound? Matching lower bounds show which upper bounds are
  tight (see <NoteLink to="sample-complexity-lower-bounds">sample complexity lower bounds</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="minimax-lower-bounds">minimax lower bounds</NoteLink>).
- **Computation.** Which learnable classes can be learned in polynomial time? (see
  <NoteLink to="efficient-pac-learning">efficient PAC learning</NoteLink>)

## The recipe for a bound

Nearly every generalisation bound is built by the same five moves.

<RecipeDiagram />

**1. Decompose.** Split the excess risk of ERM into an approximation error, which depends only on the class, and an
estimation error, which depends on the sample. The estimation error is at most twice the uniform deviation of the class:

$$
R(\hat h) - R^* = \underbrace{R(h^*_\Hcal) - R^*}_{\text{approximation}} + \underbrace{R(\hat h) - R(h^*_\Hcal)}_{\le\, 2\sup_{h \in \Hcal}\abs{R(h) - \hat R_S(h)}},
$$

where $h^*_\Hcal$ is the best member of the class. The derivation is in the
<NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> note; an optimiser that stops early
adds a third term (<NoteLink to="approximation-estimation-optimisation-trade-off">the approximation, estimation and
optimisation trade-off</NoteLink>). From here on, the task is to bound the uniform deviation.

**2. Concentrate.** For one predictor $h$ fixed before the data are seen, $\hat R_S(h)$ is an average of $m$ independent
losses with mean $R(h)$. For losses in $[0, 1]$, <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's inequality</NoteLink>
gives $\prob(\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} \ge \epsilon) \le 2e^{-2m\epsilon^2}$.
<NoteLink to="bernsteins-inequality">Bernstein's inequality</NoteLink> and the exact binomial tail are sharper when the
risk is small. Applied to a held-out set, this step alone is a complete bound: the
<NoteLink to="test-set-bounds">test-set bound</NoteLink>.

**3. Make it uniform.** The learner's predictor depends on the sample, so the bound must hold for every $h \in \Hcal$ at
once.

- For a finite class, the <NoteLink to="union-bound">union bound</NoteLink> multiplies the failure probability by
  $\abs\Hcal$. The deviation becomes $\sqrt{(\ln\abs\Hcal + \ln(2/\delta))/(2m)}$ (see
  <NoteLink to="realisable-and-agnostic-learning">realisable and agnostic learning</NoteLink> and the
  <NoteLink to="occam-bound">Occam bound</NoteLink>).
- For an infinite class, <NoteLink to="symmetrisation">symmetrisation</NoteLink> replaces the true risk by the risk on
  a second, ghost sample, so only the finitely many labellings that $\Hcal$ produces on $2m$ points matter. Their number
  is the <NoteLink to="growth-function-and-sauer-shelah-lemma">growth function</NoteLink> $\Pi_\Hcal(2m)$, which is
  polynomial in $m$ when the VC dimension is finite <Cite id="vapnik1971" />.
- The <NoteLink to="rademacher-complexity">Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> $\Rad_m(\Hcal)$ measures how well the class
  correlates with random signs, and bounds the expected uniform deviation directly <Cite id="bartlett2002" />.
  <NoteLink to="covering-numbers-and-metric-entropy">Covering numbers</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="chaining-and-dudley-entropy-integral">chaining</NoteLink> refine it further.

**4. Exploit structure.** Counting labellings ignores how the class is used. Finer bounds use more information:

- the margin and norm of the returned predictor (<NoteLink to="margin-bounds">margin bounds</NoteLink>);
- the algorithm's insensitivity to one example (<NoteLink to="algorithmic-stability">algorithmic stability</NoteLink>);
- a prior over predictors (<NoteLink to="pac-bayes-bounds">PAC-Bayes bounds</NoteLink>);
- a small subset of the sample that determines the output (<NoteLink to="sample-compression-bounds">sample
  compression</NoteLink>);
- low noise near the decision boundary, which gives fast rates (<NoteLink to="fast-rates-and-noise-conditions">fast
  rates and noise conditions</NoteLink>).

**5. Invert and read.** A bound of the form $\prob(\text{deviation} \ge \epsilon) \le f(m, \epsilon)$ is solved for
$\epsilon$ at a chosen $\delta$, or for the sample size $m$ that a target $\epsilon$ needs. The resulting number is
compared with a measured test error. A bound above the trivial value (1 for the 0–1 loss) is **vacuous**: true, but
uninformative.

## The recipe in numbers

Thresholds on the real line, $h_t(x) = \indicator[x \ge t]$, classify with 0–1 loss. Take $m = 500$ and $\delta = 0.05$.

1. **A finite grid of 100 thresholds.** Concentration and the union bound give a uniform deviation of
   $\epsilon = \sqrt{\ln(2 \cdot 100/0.05)/(2 \cdot 500)} = \sqrt{8.29/1\,000} = 0.091$. ERM's estimation error is at
   most $2\epsilon = 0.18$. To bring the estimation error down to $0.05$ (so $\epsilon = 0.025$) the bound needs
   $m = \ln(4\,000)/(2 \cdot 0.025^2) = 6\,635$ examples.
2. **All thresholds.** The class is infinite, so the union bound fails, but it produces only $m + 1$ labellings of $m$
   points: $\Pi_\Hcal(m) = m + 1$ and the VC dimension is 1. The Vapnik–Chervonenkis inequality in the form
   $\prob(\sup_h\abs{\hat R_S(h) - R(h)} > \epsilon) \le 8\,\Pi_\Hcal(m)\,e^{-m\epsilon^2/32}$ <Cite id="devroye1996" />
   gives $\epsilon = \sqrt{32\ln(8 \cdot 501/0.05)/500} = 0.85$ at $m = 500$. The estimation-error bound $2\epsilon$ is
   above 1 and vacuous. Reaching $\epsilon = 0.025$ needs about $965\,000$ examples.
3. **Reading the numbers.** The two classes are almost the same: a grid of 100 thresholds approximates every threshold
   to within $0.01$ in risk when the inputs are spread evenly. The 145-fold difference in sample size comes from the
   constants of the symmetrisation argument, not from the classes. Sharper uniform laws for this class, such as the
   <NoteLink to="glivenko-cantelli-theorem-and-dkw-inequality">Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality</NoteLink>, close
   most of the gap.

The note <NoteLink to="deriving-a-generalisation-bound">deriving a generalisation bound</NoteLink> works through every
move on one problem, with each bound evaluated against the measured gap.

## The main themes

- **Capacity controls generalisation.** The gap of a learner choosing from $\Hcal$ is bounded by a capacity of $\Hcal$
  divided by $\sqrt m$: $\ln\abs\Hcal$, the VC dimension or the Rademacher complexity. The theory's long-running
  question is which capacity is the right one.
- **Assumptions are unavoidable.** Without restricting the class or the distribution, no learner generalises (the
  <NoteLink to="no-free-lunch-theorem">no free lunch theorem</NoteLink>), and no learner has a uniform rate of
  convergence (see <NoteLink to="consistency-of-learning-algorithms">consistency of learning algorithms</NoteLink>).
- **Uniform convergence is sufficient and, for ERM over a class, necessary.** For binary classification, finite VC
  dimension, uniform convergence, learnability and the success of ERM are equivalent
  <Cite id="shalevshwartz2014" />. Beyond ERM, bounds that depend on the algorithm (stability, PAC-Bayes, compression,
  <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink>) can be much tighter.
- **Two rates.** Agnostic learning gives excess risk of order $1/\sqrt m$; the realisable case and low-noise conditions
  give order $1/m$. Variance-sensitive concentration (Bernstein) and <NoteLink to="local-rademacher-complexity">local
  Rademacher complexity</NoteLink> explain the difference.
- **<NoteLink to="model-selection">Model selection</NoteLink> is part of the theory.** Penalising the empirical risk by
  a capacity term turns a bound into a learning rule (<NoteLink to="structural-risk-minimisation">structural risk
  minimisation</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="oracle-inequalities">oracle inequalities</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="minimum-description-length">minimum description length</NoteLink>).
- **Modern models strain the classical picture.** Large networks fit random labels and still generalise on real ones.
  Classical capacity bounds are vacuous for them, test error can fall again past the interpolation threshold, and
  uniform convergence can provably fail to explain their generalisation (see
  <NoteLink to="limits-of-uniform-convergence">the limits of uniform convergence</NoteLink>). This motivates
  <NoteLink to="non-vacuous-generalisation-bounds">non-vacuous bounds</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="benign-overfitting">benign overfitting</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="implicit-regularisation">implicit regularisation</NoteLink>.
