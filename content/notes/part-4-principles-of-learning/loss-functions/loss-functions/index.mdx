<Definition>
A **loss function** $\ell(\hat y, y)$ assigns a non-negative cost to a prediction $\hat y$ when the truth is $y$.
Training minimises its average over the training set, often plus a regulariser. The loss fixes what the model learns to
predict: a mean, a median, a quantile, a class probability or an ordering.
</Definition>

## What it is for

Every supervised model, and most self-supervised ones, is trained by minimising a loss with a gradient-based or convex
optimiser. The loss is often not the metric the model is judged by. Accuracy, recall at $k$ and <Gloss name="ndcg" />
are piecewise constant in the model's parameters and give no useful gradient, so training uses a smooth or convex
substitute whose minimiser has the right properties. Choosing that substitute well is most of what this topic is about.

## Main ideas

**The loss chooses the statistic.** For regression, squared loss is minimised by the conditional mean, absolute loss by
the conditional median and the <NoteLink to="robust-and-distributional-regression-losses">pinball loss</NoteLink> by a
conditional quantile <Cite id="hastie2009" />. <NoteLink to="regression-losses" /> also covers Huber and log-cosh
losses, which are quadratic near zero and linear in the tails, so that outliers pull the fit less.

**Surrogates stand in for the 0–1 loss.** Counting classification errors gives a non-convex, piecewise constant loss.
<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses" /> replaces it by a convex function of the margin $y f(\xvec)$, such as the logistic,
hinge or exponential loss. A convex surrogate is classification-calibrated, so minimising it still yields the
<NoteLink to="bayes-decision-rule">Bayes classifier</NoteLink>, exactly when it is differentiable at zero with negative
slope <Cite id="bartlett2006" />. Log loss is also a proper <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">scoring rule</NoteLink>,
so it trains calibrated probabilities as well.

**Normalisers can be too expensive to compute.** A softmax over a vocabulary or a catalogue of $V$ items costs $O(Vd)$
per prediction, almost all of it in the normaliser. <NoteLink to="approximate-softmax" /> covers the three ways around
it: sample the denominator, factorise it into a tree, or train the model so the normaliser is already one.

**Orderings need their own losses.** Ranking metrics depend only on the order of the scores.
<NoteLink to="ranking-losses" /> covers pointwise losses that fit each relevance label, pairwise losses such as
<Gloss name="ranknet" /> that penalise misordered pairs, and listwise losses that compare whole lists.
<Gloss name="lambdarank" /> weights each pair by the change in NDCG that swapping it would cause.

**Embeddings are trained by comparison.** When the goal is a distance rather than a label, the loss compares examples
with each other. <NoteLink to="contrastive-and-metric-learning-losses" /> covers pair and
<NoteLink to="triplet-and-margin-losses">triplet losses</NoteLink> with a margin,
<NoteLink to="proxy-and-classification-based-metric-losses">proxy losses</NoteLink> with one learned vector per class,
and the InfoNCE loss of contrastive learning.

## How the notes fit together

Start with <NoteLink to="regression-losses" /> and <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses" />, which state the idea that a loss
targets a statistic and that a substitute loss can keep the right minimiser. The approximate-softmax subtopic follows
the classification losses into large output spaces. Ranking losses and the contrastive and metric-learning losses
extend the same reasoning to orderings and to embeddings, and can be read in either order.
