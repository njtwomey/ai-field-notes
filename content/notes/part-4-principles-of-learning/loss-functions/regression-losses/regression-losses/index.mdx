<Definition>
A **regression loss** $\ell(y, \hat y) = \rho(y - \hat y)$ scores a real-valued prediction $\hat y$ of a target $y$
through the residual $r = y - \hat y$. The common choices are the squared loss $\frac12 r^2$, the absolute loss
$\abs{r}$, the **pinball** (quantile) loss $\rho_\tau(r) = r\,(\tau - \indicator[r < 0])$ for $\tau \in (0, 1)$, the
**Huber** loss

$$
\rho_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \frac12 r^2, & \abs{r} \le \delta, \\ \delta\paren{\abs{r} - \frac12\delta}, & \abs{r} > \delta, \end{cases}
$$

and the **log-cosh** loss $\log\cosh r$. The minimiser of the expected loss over constant predictions is a statistic
of the distribution of $y$: the mean, the median, the $\tau$-quantile, or a robust compromise.
</Definition>

## What each loss estimates

Minimising the expected loss $\expect[\rho(y - c)]$ over a constant $c$ picks a summary of the distribution of $y$.
Applied conditionally on $\xvec$, the same calculation says what the best predictor $\hat f(\xvec)$ estimates. Let $y$
have distribution function $F$ and set the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of the expected loss with
respect to $c$ to zero.

**Squared loss.** $\frac{d}{dc}\expect[\frac12(y - c)^2] = -\expect[y - c] = 0$ gives $c = \expect[y]$. The optimal
predictor is the conditional mean $\expect[y \mid \xvec]$.

**Absolute loss.** The derivative of $\abs{y - c}$ in $c$ is $-1$ when $y > c$ and $+1$ when $y < c$. So
$\frac{d}{dc}\expect\abs{y - c} = -\prob(y > c) + \prob(y < c) = 2F(c) - 1$, which is zero at $F(c) = \frac12$: the
**median**.

**Pinball loss.** $\rho_\tau(y - c)$ has slope $-\tau$ in $c$ when $y > c$ and $1 - \tau$ when $y < c$. Then

$$
\frac{d}{dc}\expect[\rho_\tau(y - c)] = -\tau\,\prob(y > c) + (1 - \tau)\,\prob(y < c) = F(c) - \tau,
$$

which is zero at $c = F^{-1}(\tau)$, the **$\tau$-quantile**. Under-predictions cost $\tau$ per unit and
over-predictions cost $1 - \tau$, so the optimum sits where a fraction $\tau$ of the mass lies below it. The absolute
loss is the case $\tau = \frac12$, up to a factor of 2. Minimising the pinball loss over linear functions is
**quantile regression** <Cite id="koenker1978" />.

**Huber loss.** The derivative is the clipped residual $\psi_\delta(r) = \max(-\delta, \min(\delta, r))$, so the optimum
solves $\expect[\psi_\delta(y - c)] = 0$. Residuals within $\delta$ count in full, as for the mean; larger residuals
count as $\pm\delta$, as for the median. As $\delta \to \infty$ the optimum is the mean, and as $\delta \to 0$ it is the
median. Huber derived this loss as the minimax choice for estimating a location when a fraction of the data comes from
an arbitrary contaminating distribution <Cite id="huber1964" />.

**Log-cosh loss.** Its derivative is $\tanh r$. For small $r$, $\log\cosh r \approx \frac12 r^2$; for large $\abs{r}$,
$\log\cosh r \approx \abs{r} - \ln 2$. It behaves like a Huber loss with $\delta \approx 1$ but is smooth everywhere,
which suits Newton-type optimisers.

### Losses as likelihoods

Minimising a loss is maximum likelihood under the noise density proportional to $e^{-\rho(r)}$. The squared loss
$\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}r^2$ is the negative log density of a Gaussian, the absolute loss $\abs{r}/b$ that of a
<NoteLink to="laplace-distribution">Laplace distribution</NoteLink>, and the pinball loss that of an asymmetric Laplace.
Heavy-tailed noise calls for a loss that grows slowly in the tails.

## Influence and robustness

An **M-estimator** $\hat\theta$ of location solves $\sum_i\psi(y_i - \hat\theta) = 0$, where $\psi = \rho'$. Add a
single observation at $y$ with weight $\epsilon$. Differentiating the estimating equation
$\epsilon\,\psi(y - \theta_\epsilon) + (1 - \epsilon)\expect[\psi(Y - \theta_\epsilon)] = 0$ at $\epsilon = 0$ gives

$$
\left.\frac{d\theta_\epsilon}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon = 0} = \frac{\psi(y - \theta)}{\expect[\psi'(Y - \theta)]}.
$$

This is the **influence function**: the effect of a small amount of contamination at $y$ on the estimate
<Cite id="hampel1974" />. It is proportional to $\psi$. For the squared loss $\psi(r) = r$ is unbounded, so one wild
observation can move the mean arbitrarily far. For the absolute, pinball, Huber and log-cosh losses $\psi$ is bounded,
and no single observation can move the estimate by more than a fixed amount.

## Worked example

Take the five values $1, 2, 3, 6, 18$, where 18 is an outlier, and find the constant that minimises each loss.

- **Squared**: the mean, $30/5 = 6$. The outlier alone contributes $18/5 = 3.6$.
- **Absolute**: the median, 3.
- **Huber, $\delta = 2$**: 3.5. At $c = 3.5$ the residuals are $-2.5, -1.5, -0.5, 2.5, 14.5$, which clip to
  $-2, -1.5, -0.5, 2, 2$ and sum to zero.
- **Huber, $\delta = 5$**: 4.25. The residuals $-3.25, -2.25, -1.25, 1.75, 13.75$ clip to
  $-3.25, -2.25, -1.25, 1.75, 5$, which sum to zero.
- **Log-cosh**: 3.19, close to the median because $\tanh$ saturates within about 2 units.
- **Pinball, $\tau = 0.7$**: 6. With $n\tau = 3.5$, the minimiser is the 4th smallest value.

Replacing 18 by 1,800 moves the mean to 362.4 and leaves the median, both Huber estimates, the log-cosh estimate and the
0.7-quantile unchanged, because the outlier's clipped pull is the same.

## Choosing a loss

- **The prediction should estimate a mean** (for totals, expected costs, anything summed over many cases): squared
  loss. The sum of conditional means is the mean of the sum; medians and quantiles do not add.
- **Typical values matter and outliers are errors**: absolute or Huber loss. Choose $\delta$ on the scale of the good
  residuals, for example a small multiple of a robust estimate of their <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
  deviation</NoteLink>.
- **A quantile or an interval is needed**: pinball losses at the required levels, as in
  <NoteLink to="probabilistic-forecasting">probabilistic forecasting</NoteLink>.
- **Targets that are positive and skewed**, such as counts or claim sizes: deviance losses matched to the variance
  function; see <NoteLink to="robust-and-distributional-regression-losses">log-cosh, squared log error and Tweedie
  deviance</NoteLink>.

The loss used for training need not be the metric used for evaluation, but the predictor it produces estimates the
statistic of its own loss. Training with squared loss and evaluating by mean absolute error rewards the mean when the
median is wanted. See <NoteLink to="squared-and-absolute-error-metrics">MSE, RMSE and MAE</NoteLink> for the metrics.

## Assumptions

- **A scale for $\delta$.** The Huber and log-cosh losses are not scale invariant. Their thresholds are in the units of
  $y$, so the residual scale must be known or estimated, or the targets standardised.

## Pitfalls

- **Non-smooth losses and <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> methods.** The absolute and pinball losses have a
  kink at zero and constant gradients elsewhere, so plain <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink>
  does not settle at the minimum without a decaying step size. <Gloss name="lp">Linear programming</Gloss>,
  <Gloss name="irls">iteratively reweighted least squares</Gloss> or the smooth Huber and log-cosh approximations avoid
  this.
- **Crossing quantiles.** Fitting separate models for several $\tau$ can produce a 0.9-quantile below the 0.5-quantile
  at some inputs. Fit them jointly or sort the predictions.
- **Robust losses do not fix leverage.** Bounded $\psi$ limits the influence of outlying targets, not of outlying
  inputs. A point with an extreme $\xvec$ can still dominate a linear fit.
