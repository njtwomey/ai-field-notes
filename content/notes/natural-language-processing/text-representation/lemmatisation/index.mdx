<Definition>
A **lemma** is the dictionary form of a word: the form listed as a headword. Lemmatisation maps a word token, together
with its part of speech, to its lemma: $(\text{"ran"}, \text{verb}) \mapsto \text{"run"}$. It undoes **inflection**,
the changes that mark number, tense, person or degree, and leaves **derivation**, which forms new words such as
"happiness" from "happy", in place <Cite id="manning2008" /> <Cite id="jurafsky2025" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="stemming">stemmer</NoteLink> removes suffixes by rule and knows no words. It cannot join irregular forms,
because "mice", "ran" and "was" share no suffix with "mouse", "run" and "be". It also produces keys that are not words,
such as "happi", and it joins words that only look related, such as "news" and "new". A lemmatiser uses a lexicon, so
its outputs are words and its joins are inflections of one word.

## Mechanism

Lemmatisation is **morphological analysis**: decomposing a word into a stem and affixes, such as
"cats" = "cat" + plural "-s", and returning the stem's dictionary form. A full analyser uses a lexicon of stems and
a finite-state model of each language's inflection rules. English inflection is simple enough that a lexicon and a
handful of rules cover most words.

**WordNet's morphy.** WordNet is a lexical database of English that groups nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs into
sets of synonyms <Cite id="miller1995" />. Version 3.0 holds 147,306 distinct lemma names in 117,659 synonym sets.
Its lemmatiser, morphy, works in three stages for a word $w$ with part of speech $p$.

1. **Exception list.** Look up $w$ in a list of irregular forms for $p$: "mice" → "mouse" (noun), "ate" → "eat" (verb),
   "better" → "good" (adjective).
2. **Detachment rules.** Otherwise, try each suffix rule for $p$ and keep the results that are lemmas in WordNet.
   Nouns: -s → ∅, -ses → -s, -xes → -x, -zes → -z, -ches → -ch, -shes → -sh, -men → -man, -ies → -y. Verbs: -s → ∅,
   -ies → -y, -es → -e, -es → ∅, -ed → -e, -ed → ∅, -ing → -e, -ing → ∅. Adjectives: -er → ∅, -est → ∅, -er → -e,
   -est → -e.
3. **Otherwise** return $w$ unchanged, including when $w$ itself is a lemma.

The check against the lexicon is what separates this from stemming. The rule -ing → ∅ turns "meeting" into "meet"
only for a verb. The rule -ed → ∅ turns "chased" into "chas", which is not in WordNet, so the rule -ed → -e is used
and gives "chase".

**The part of speech is an input.** The same string has different lemmas under different parts of speech. A
lemmatiser run over text first tags each token with a part-of-speech tagger, a <NoteLink to="sequence-labelling">sequence
labelling</NoteLink> model, then lemmatises each token with its tag.

## Worked example

Outputs of <Gloss name="nltk" />'s WordNet lemmatiser <Cite id="bird2009" />, with the part of speech given:

| Word | As noun | As verb | As adjective |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| meeting | meeting | meet | meeting |
| leaves | leaf | leave | leaves |
| does | doe | do | does |
| was | wa | be | was |
| running | running | run | running |
| better | better | better | good |
| mice | mouse | mice | mice |

Without a tag, NLTK treats every word as a noun. "does" then becomes "doe", a female deer, and "was" becomes "wa",
WordNet's entry for the abbreviation of Washington State. The part of speech is not optional in practice.

Some forms stay ambiguous even with a tag. The verb "saw" is a form of "see" and also the verb "to saw"; "axes" is the
plural of both "ax" and "axis". Morphy returns every candidate that is in WordNet, and a lemmatiser must pick one.

## Stemming against lemmatisation

| | Stemming | Lemmatisation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output | a stem, often not a word ("happi") | a dictionary word ("happy") |
| Resources | suffix rules only | lexicon, exception lists, part-of-speech tagger |
| Irregular forms ("mice", "ran", "was") | not joined | joined |
| Derivation ("relation", "relational") | joined ("relat") | kept apart |
| Unknown words | stemmed by rule | returned unchanged |
| Errors | over- and under-stemming | tagging errors, missing lexicon entries |
| Vocabulary of _Emma_ (7,096 types) | 4,518 terms with Porter | 5,460 terms with WordNet and tags |
| Speed | very fast | slower: tagging dominates |

The last rows use the 161,975 lower-cased alphabetic tokens of _Emma_ from the NLTK sample. Lemmatisation reduces the
vocabulary by 23%, Porter stemming by 36%. The difference is derivation: stemming joins "general", "generous" and
"generate"; lemmatisation keeps them apart, correctly for the first two.

## Cost

Lemmatising running text costs one tagging pass, which is linear in the length of the text, plus one lexicon lookup per
token. The lexicon is the main resource. For a language without one, lemmatisation is unavailable and stemming or
<NoteLink to="tokenisation">subword tokenisation</NoteLink> is the fallback.

## Trade-offs

- **Precision over recall.** Lemmatisation merges fewer forms than stemming, so it makes fewer false matches and finds
  fewer true ones. In English retrieval the difference in effectiveness between the two is small
  <Cite id="manning2008" />.
- **Readable features.** Lemmas are words, so features built on them can be shown to people, for example as the top
  weights of a linear classifier.
- **Useful for rich morphology.** Languages such as Finnish, Czech or Arabic have dozens of inflected forms per lemma.
  Lemmatisation there shrinks the vocabulary much more than in English.
- **Tag errors propagate.** A mistagged token gets a wrong lemma, as "does" did above.
