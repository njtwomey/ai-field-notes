<Definition>
A text representation is a function from strings to vectors that a model can compare or learn from. Classical
representations are built by a fixed pipeline: normalisation, tokenisation, filtering, morphological reduction,
feature extraction and weighting, each step a hand-chosen rule. Distributed representations replace the last stages
with vectors learned from co-occurrence or prediction <Cite id="manning2008" /> <Cite id="jurafsky2025" />.
</Definition>

## Components

In pipeline order:

1. **String to tokens.** <NoteLink to="text-normalisation">Text normalisation</NoteLink> (Unicode forms, case folding,
   accents, numbers, contractions) and <NoteLink to="tokenisation">tokenisation</NoteLink> into words or, with
   <NoteLink to="byte-pair-encoding">byte-pair encoding</NoteLink> or
   <NoteLink to="wordpiece-and-unigram-tokenisation">WordPiece and unigram tokenisation</NoteLink>, subwords.
2. **Filtering and reduction.** <NoteLink to="stop-words">Stop words</NoteLink> are removed or down-weighted.
   <NoteLink to="stemming">Stemming</NoteLink> strips suffixes by rule; <NoteLink to="lemmatisation">lemmatisation</NoteLink>
   maps inflected forms to dictionary words.
3. **Features.** Each word is an index, a <NoteLink to="one-hot-encoding-of-words">one-hot vector</NoteLink>. A
   document is a <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink>, optionally extended with
   <NoteLink to="word-n-grams">word n-grams</NoteLink> for local order, or built from
   <NoteLink to="character-n-grams-and-shingles">character n-grams and shingles</NoteLink> for robustness and
   near-duplicate detection. <NoteLink to="feature-hashing-for-text">Feature hashing</NoteLink> fixes the dimension
   without a vocabulary.
4. **Weighting.** <NoteLink to="term-frequency-inverse-document-frequency">TF-IDF</NoteLink> and its
   <NoteLink to="term-weighting-variants">variants</NoteLink> reweight counts by rarity and length.
5. **Dense vectors.** <NoteLink to="co-occurrence-matrices-and-pointwise-mutual-information">Co-occurrence matrices and PMI</NoteLink> describe
   words by their neighbours, the basis of <NoteLink to="distributional-semantics">distributional semantics</NoteLink>.
   Learned <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink> compress the same information; averaged or trained
   <NoteLink to="document-embeddings">document embeddings</NoteLink> and transformer
   <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence embeddings</NoteLink> give one vector per text.

## Data flow

Take "The cats aren't chasing mice." through one common configuration.

| Stage | Output |
| --- | --- |
| Normalise (NFKC, case fold, expand contractions) | "the cats are not chasing mice." |
| Tokenise | the, cats, are, not, chasing, mice |
| Remove stop words, keep negations | cats, not, chasing, mice |
| Porter stems | cat, not, chase, mice |
| Unigrams and bigrams | cat, not, chase, mice, "cat not", "not chase", "chase mice" |
| Weight | a sparse vector with 7 non-zero coordinates over the collection's vocabulary |

Each stage merges or drops information. Case folding merges "Apple" and "apple". The stop list drops "are". The stemmer
turns "chasing" into "chase", but it cannot join "mice" to "mouse"; a lemmatiser would. The bigrams restore that "not"
applies to "chase". The explorer below runs these stages on six sentences.

## Variants

### Comparison of representations

$V$ is the vocabulary size, $L$ the document length and $d$ a chosen dense dimension.

| Representation | Dimension | Sparsity | Captures | Loses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One-hot word | $V$ | one non-zero | word identity | all similarity between words |
| Bag of words (counts) | $V$ | at most $L$ non-zeros | which words, how often | order; synonymy |
| Binary bag | $V$ | at most $L$ | which words | frequency; order |
| TF-IDF | $V$ | at most $L$ | which words, how often, weighted by rarity | order; synonymy |
| Word n-grams | up to $V^n$, pruned | very sparse | local order, phrases, negation | order beyond $n$ |
| Character n-grams | up to $\abs{\Sigma}^n$ | sparse | spelling, morphology, typos | word boundaries (partly) |
| $w$-shingle set | unbounded set | a set | exact wording | frequency; meaning of paraphrases |
| MinHash signature | $K$ integers | dense | an estimate of shingle resemblance | the text itself |
| Hashed features | $m$ | at most $L$ | same as the unhashed features, with collisions | reversibility |
| Word–context PPMI row | $V$ contexts | sparse | usage of a word type | word order; polysemy |
| <Gloss name="svd" /> of PPMI or latent semantic analysis (LSA) | $d$ | dense | latent dimensions of usage or topic | interpretability; rare detail |
| Topic proportions (latent Dirichlet allocation, LDA) | number of topics | dense, on the simplex | thematic mixture | everything below topic level |
| Static word embedding | $d$ | dense | similarity between word types | polysemy |
| Averaged or SIF document vector | $d$ | dense | overall content | order; detail in long texts |
| Paragraph vector (doc2vec) | $d$ | dense | content, some local order | needs optimisation per new text |
| Contextual token and sentence embeddings | $d$ per token or per text | dense | meaning in context | interpretability; cost |

The rows run roughly from symbolic to learned. Sparse representations are interpretable, cheap, exact about wording
and blind to synonymy. Dense representations capture similarity of meaning, cost more to compute, and are hard to
inspect. Retrieval and classification systems often combine one of each.

### Where the representations are used

- **Classification.** Linear models on TF-IDF n-grams remain strong baselines for
  <NoteLink to="text-classification">text classification</NoteLink>; <NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink>
  uses raw counts.
- **Topic modelling.** <NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis">LSA</NoteLink>,
  <NoteLink to="probabilistic-latent-semantic-analysis">pLSA</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">LDA</NoteLink> factorise the document–term matrix.
- **Near-duplicate detection.** Shingles and MinHash, indexed by
  <NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hashing</NoteLink>.
- **Language modelling.** <NoteLink to="n-gram-language-model">N-gram language models</NoteLink> use the same
  n-gram counts as features; neural models start from subword tokens and learn everything after.
- **Evaluation.** <NoteLink to="bleu">BLEU</NoteLink>, <NoteLink to="rouge">ROUGE</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="chrf">chrF</NoteLink> compare word and character n-grams; <NoteLink to="bertscore">BERTScore</NoteLink>
  compares contextual embeddings.

## Pitfalls

- **The pipeline is part of the model.** Train and apply it identically, and fit every statistic (vocabulary, idf,
  stop list from document frequency) on training data only.
- **No step is neutral.** Each merges or drops distinctions; check what the task needs before applying it. Negation,
  capitalised names and numbers are the usual casualties.
- **Neural models expect raw text.** Transformer tokenisers include their own normalisation. Stemming, stop-word removal
  and lower-casing before a pretrained model usually hurt it.
