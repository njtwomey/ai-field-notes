<Definition>
A sentence embedding is a map $f_{\thetavec}$ from a text $s$ to a vector $\uvec = f_{\thetavec}(s) \in \reals^d$, trained so
that the cosine similarity $\cos(\uvec, \vvec)$ of two embeddings is high when the texts mean similar things. Most
sentence embeddings are built from a transformer encoder: the encoder outputs one vector per token, and a pooling step
reduces them to one vector <Cite id="reimers2019" />.
</Definition>

## Why one vector per text

Many tasks compare texts: semantic search, clustering, duplicate detection, retrieval for question answering. A
**cross-encoder** reads both texts together, such as BERT with the input "`[CLS]` text A `[SEP]` text B", and outputs a
similarity score. Cross-encoders are accurate, because every token of one text attends to every token of the other.
They cost one full forward pass per pair. Finding the most similar pair among $n$ sentences needs $n(n - 1)/2$ passes:
49,995,000 for $n = 10{,}000$, which Reimers and Gurevych estimated at about 65 hours on a GPU <Cite id="reimers2019" />.

A **bi-encoder** embeds each text once, independently. The $n$ embeddings cost $n$ passes. Comparing them is a matrix
product, and a <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour index</NoteLink> finds the closest vectors
without comparing all pairs. The same search took about 5 seconds with Sentence-BERT <Cite id="reimers2019" />. The
price is that the two texts never interact inside the model, so a bi-encoder is usually less accurate than a
cross-encoder on the same pair. A common design retrieves candidates with a bi-encoder and re-ranks the top few with a
cross-encoder.

## Pooling

A transformer encoder turns tokens $1, \dots, T$ into vectors $\hvec_1, \dots, \hvec_T \in \reals^d$. Three poolings are
common.

- **`[CLS]` pooling** takes the output at the first, special token. BERT's pretraining uses this vector to predict
  whether two sentences are consecutive <Cite id="devlin2019" />.
- **Mean pooling** averages the token vectors. Batches pad short texts to a common length, so the average must skip the
  padding. With a mask $m_i = 1$ for real tokens and $m_i = 0$ for padding,

  $$
  \uvec = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{T} m_i \hvec_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{T} m_i}.
  $$

- **Max pooling** takes the maximum of each coordinate over the real tokens.

Without fine-tuning, none of these works well. On semantic textual similarity benchmarks, the `[CLS]` vector and the
mean of BERT's output vectors both scored below the average of GloVe word vectors <Cite id="reimers2019" />. The
encoder was trained to predict masked tokens, not to place similar sentences near each other.

## Sentence-BERT

Sentence-BERT (SBERT) fine-tunes a pretrained encoder so that pooled embeddings are directly comparable
<Cite id="reimers2019" />. It uses a **siamese** network: the same encoder, with shared weights, embeds both texts of a
training pair, $\uvec = f_{\thetavec}(a)$ and $\vvec = f_{\thetavec}(b)$. The loss depends on the training data.

- **Classification on natural language inference (NLI).** Each pair is labelled entailment, contradiction or neutral.
  A linear layer reads the concatenation $(\uvec, \vvec, \abs{\uvec - \vvec})$ and a softmax predicts the label:
  $\operatorname{softmax}(\Wmat [\uvec; \vvec; \abs{\uvec - \vvec}])$ with $\Wmat \in \reals^{3 \times 3d}$. The layer is
  discarded after training. Its only purpose is to shape the geometry of $\uvec$ and $\vvec$.
- **Regression on similarity scores.** The loss is the squared error between $\cos(\uvec, \vvec)$ and a gold score.
- **Triplets.** Given an anchor $a$, a positive $p$ and a negative $n$, the loss is $\max(\norm{\uvec_a - \uvec_p} -
  \norm{\uvec_a - \uvec_n} + \epsilon, 0)$ with margin $\epsilon$, which pushes the positive closer than the negative.

SBERT with mean pooling, trained on NLI data, outperformed averaged GloVe vectors and raw BERT embeddings on the
semantic textual similarity benchmarks, and mean pooling performed better than `[CLS]` pooling in its ablation
<Cite id="reimers2019" />.

## In-batch negatives

Later sentence-embedding models mostly train with a contrastive loss on pairs of related texts, such as a question and
its answer. Within a batch of $B$ pairs $(a_j, b_j)$, each $a_j$ must pick its own $b_j$ out of all $B$ candidates, with
the loss $-\log \big( e^{\cos(\uvec_j, \vvec_j)/\tau} / \sum_{k} e^{\cos(\uvec_j, \vvec_k)/\tau} \big)$ at temperature
$\tau$. The other $B - 1$ texts in the batch serve as negatives at no extra cost. This is the InfoNCE loss of
<NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive learning</NoteLink>, and larger batches give more negatives.

## Worked example

Let an encoder output 3-dimensional token vectors. Sentence A has two real tokens, $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 2, 1)$, and is
padded to length 3; the encoder's output at the padding position is $(3, -1, 0)$. Sentence B has three real tokens,
$(1, 1, 0)$, $(0, 1, 2)$ and $(1, 0, 1)$.

- **Masked mean pooling.** $\uvec_A = (0.5, 1, 1)$ and $\uvec_B = (\tfrac23, \tfrac23, 1)$. Their cosine similarity is
  $\uvec_A^\top \uvec_B / (\norm{\uvec_A}\norm{\uvec_B}) = 0.970$.
- **Unmasked mean.** Averaging all three positions of A gives $(\tfrac43, \tfrac13, \tfrac23)$, and the cosine similarity
  drops to 0.847.

The padding position changed the similarity by 0.12. Sentence A's embedding would also change with the batch it
happened to be padded in.

## Pitfalls

- **Cosine similarity is not calibrated across models.** A similarity of 0.8 means different things for different
  models and even different domains. Choose thresholds on labelled data.
- **Length and domain shift.** Models trained on sentences degrade on long documents, which exceed the context window
  or average away their content. Split long texts into passages.
- **Asymmetric tasks.** A query and a document are different kinds of text. Models for retrieval are often trained with
  separate prefixes or encoders for the two sides.
