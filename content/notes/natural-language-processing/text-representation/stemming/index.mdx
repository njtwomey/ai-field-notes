<Definition>
A **stemmer** is a function from words to strings, called stems, built from rules that remove affixes. It
**conflates** words that share a stem into one term, so "connect", "connected", "connecting" and "connection" all
count as "connect". A stem need not be a word: Porter's algorithm maps "happy" and "happiness" to "happi"
<Cite id="porter1980" />. Stemming uses no dictionary and no part of speech.
</Definition>

## Problem

A <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> treats each surface form as a separate feature. English inflection
("cat", "cats"; "chase", "chased", "chasing") and derivation ("relate", "relation", "relational") split the evidence
for one idea across many features. A query for "connection" misses a document that says "connected". A stemmer maps
those forms to one term, which shrinks the vocabulary and raises recall.

## Mechanism

### Lovins: longest match

The first published stemmer, by Lovins, holds a list of 294 endings with conditions. It removes the longest ending that
matches and meets its condition, in one pass, then repairs the stem with transformation rules such as turning a final
"rpt" into "rb" (so "absorption" and "absorb" meet) <Cite id="lovins1968" />. One pass is fast, but the list must
contain every compound ending.

### Porter: five steps and a measure

Porter's algorithm removes compound suffixes one layer at a time in five steps, so that "generalizations" loses "s",
then "ization", then "al" <Cite id="porter1980" />. Each rule has the form

$$
(\text{condition})\ S_1 \to S_2,
$$

which replaces the suffix $S_1$ by $S_2$ when the word ends in $S_1$ and the stem left after removing $S_1$ meets the
condition.

**The measure.** Call a letter a vowel if it is a, e, i, o or u, or a "y" that follows a consonant; every other letter
is a consonant. Write a run of one or more consonants as $C$ and a run of one or more vowels as $V$. Every word then
has the form

$$
[C](VC)^m[V],
$$

where brackets mark an optional part and $m \ge 0$ counts the vowel–consonant pairs. The number $m$ is the word's
**measure**. It approximates the number of syllables that end in a consonant, and so the length of the stem.

| $m$ | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | tr, ee, tree, y, by |
| 1 | trouble, oats, trees, ivy |
| 2 | troubles, private, oaten, orrery |

"trouble" has letters in the pattern CCVVCCV, which collapses to $CVCV$, one $VC$ pair, so $m = 1$.

**Why a measure.** A suffix rule must not fire on a word that only looks as if it has the suffix. Removing "-ate" from
"activate" leaves "activ" ($m = 2$), a real stem. Removing it from "rate" would leave "r" ($m = 0$), which is no stem at
all. Porter's step 4 removes "-ate" only when $m > 1$, which keeps "rate" whole. The conditions encode a single idea:
the longer the remaining stem, the more likely the ending is a true suffix.

Other conditions test the end of the stem: $^*S$ (it ends in "s"), $^*v^*$ (it contains a vowel), $^*d$ (it ends in a
double consonant) and $^*o$ (it ends consonant–vowel–consonant, where the last consonant is not w, x or y, as in "hop"
or "fil").

**The steps.** Within a step only the rule with the longest matching suffix is considered. If its condition fails,
the step does nothing.

1. **Step 1a** removes plurals: SSES → SS, IES → I, SS → SS, S → ∅.
2. **Step 1b** removes "-ed" and "-ing" when the stem contains a vowel, then repairs the stem: "hopp" loses a doubled
   consonant ("hop"), and a short stem of form $^*o$ with $m = 1$ gains an "e" ("fil" → "file"). It also rewrites
   $(m > 0)$ EED → EE.
3. **Step 1c** turns a final "y" into "i" when the stem has a vowel, so "happy" and "happiness" can meet.
4. **Step 2** $(m > 0)$ maps double suffixes to single ones: ATIONAL → ATE, IZATION → IZE, BILITI → BLE and 17 more.
5. **Step 3** $(m > 0)$ maps or removes: ICATE → IC, ALIZE → AL, FUL → ∅, NESS → ∅, and three more.
6. **Step 4** $(m > 1)$ removes single suffixes: AL, ANCE, ENCE, ER, IC, ABLE, IBLE, ANT, EMENT, MENT, ENT, ION (only
   after "s" or "t"), OU, ISM, ATE, ITI, OUS, IVE, IZE.
7. **Step 5** removes a final "e" when $m > 1$, or when $m = 1$ and the stem is not $^*o$, and reduces a final "ll" to
   "l" when $m > 1$.

### Worked example

Stem four words. $m$ is always measured on the stem that would remain.

- **generalizations.** Step 1a, S → ∅: "generalization". Step 2, IZATION → IZE, with stem "general" of $m = 3$:
  "generalize". Step 3, ALIZE → AL, with stem "gener", $m = 2$: "general". Step 4, AL → ∅, with stem "gener",
  $m = 2 > 1$: **gener**.
- **oscillators.** Step 1a: "oscillator". Step 2, ATOR → ATE: "oscillate". Step 4, ATE → ∅, stem "oscill" with
  $m = 2$: "oscill". Step 5b, a final "ll" with $m > 1$: **oscil**.
- **filing.** Step 1b, ING → ∅, since "fil" has a vowel: "fil". "fil" has $m = 1$ and ends consonant–vowel–consonant,
  so an "e" is restored: **file**.
- **argument.** Step 4 finds MENT as the longest matching suffix. The stem "argu" has $m = 1$, which fails $m > 1$.
  The step does nothing, and the shorter suffix ENT is never tried. The stem is **argument**.

Porter's algorithm has about 60 rules and no dictionary, and it runs in time linear in the word length.

### Snowball and Lancaster

Porter later wrote Snowball, a small language for writing stemmers, and a revised English stemmer in it, often called
Porter2 <Cite id="porter2001" />. It fixes known defects: "generalizations" becomes "general" rather than "gener",
"dying" becomes "die", and a list of exceptional words ("news", "skies") is handled explicitly. Snowball stemmers
exist for many European and other languages.

The Lancaster (Paice/Husk) stemmer applies a table of about 115 rules iteratively until no rule applies, each rule
saying whether stemming may continue <Cite id="paice1990" />. It is more aggressive: "organization" becomes "org" and
"maximum" becomes "maxim".

## Over- and under-stemming

Two kinds of error follow from rules without a dictionary <Cite id="paice1990" />.

- **Over-stemming** conflates words with different meanings. Porter maps "universe", "university" and "universal" all
  to "univers"; "general", "generous" and "generate" all to "gener"; "news" to "new"; "organization" to "organ".
- **Under-stemming** leaves related words apart. Porter maps "absorb" to "absorb" and "absorption" to "absorpt";
  "alumnus" to "alumnu" and "alumni" to "alumni"; "Europe" to "europ" and "European" to "european". Irregular forms
  are never joined: "ran" stays "ran", "mice" stays "mice", "was" becomes "wa".

A more aggressive stemmer trades under-stemming for over-stemming. Lovins, Porter and Lancaster sit at different points
on this trade-off; none has fewer errors of both kinds than another.

## Effect on vocabulary size

Take the 161,975 lower-cased alphabetic tokens of _Emma_ in the <Gloss name="nltk" /> corpus sample <Cite id="bird2009" />, which have
7,096 distinct word types.

| Normaliser | Distinct terms | Reduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| none | 7,096 | 0% |
| WordNet lemmatiser with tagged part of speech | 5,460 | 23% |
| Porter (1980) | 4,518 | 36% |
| Snowball (Porter2) | 4,337 | 39% |
| Lancaster | 3,765 | 47% |

The order of the rows is the order of aggressiveness. The <NoteLink to="lemmatisation">lemmatiser</NoteLink> merges
only inflections of one word; the stemmers also merge derivations.

## Cost

A stemmer is a few hundred lines of code, needs no training data, and processes millions of words per second. It must
be applied to the query and the documents with the same stemmer, and it runs after
<NoteLink to="text-normalisation">case folding</NoteLink>, since its rules expect lower-case letters.

## Trade-offs

- **Recall against precision.** In retrieval, stemming raises recall and can lower precision. For English the net
  effect is modest and varies from query to query; it is larger for languages with richer morphology
  <Cite id="manning2008" />.
- **Language-specific.** Porter's rules are for English. Applying them to another language produces nonsense stems.
- **Superseded in neural models.** Subword tokenisers such as <NoteLink to="byte-pair-encoding">byte-pair
  encoding</NoteLink> learn frequent word pieces from data, and a neural model learns which pieces relate. Stemming
  remains useful for sparse representations, keyword search and small data sets.
- **Stems are not readable.** "gener" and "happi" are keys, not words. Display the original words to users.
