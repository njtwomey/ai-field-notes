<Definition>
Slide a window of $L$ tokens on each side over a corpus. Each target word $w$ and each context word $c$ inside its
window form a pair; let $\Dcal$ be the multiset of pairs, $\#(w, c)$ the count of a pair, $\#(w) = \sum_c \#(w, c)$
and $\#(c) = \sum_w \#(w, c)$. The **word–context matrix** has entries $\#(w, c)$. The **pointwise mutual information**
of a pair is

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(w, c) = \log \frac{p(w, c)}{p(w)\, p(c)} = \log \frac{\#(w, c)\, \abs{\Dcal}}{\#(w)\, \#(c)},
$$

and **positive PMI** is $\operatorname{PPMI}(w, c) = \max(\operatorname{PMI}(w, c), 0)$ <Cite id="church1990" />
<Cite id="levy2015" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> describes documents by their words. A word–context matrix turns
the idea around and describes words by their neighbours. Row $w$ lists how often each context word appears near $w$.
Two words whose rows are similar are used in similar contexts, and by the
<NoteLink to="distributional-semantics">distributional hypothesis</NoteLink> they tend to mean similar things.

Raw counts fail at this for the same reason raw term counts fail for documents. "the" is near every noun, so every
noun row is dominated by its "the" column, and all nouns look alike. PMI asks a better question: does $c$ appear near
$w$ more often than it would if the two were independent?

## Details

### The matrix and the window

The context can be a window of words, a sentence, a whole document, or a syntactic relation. With documents as
contexts, the matrix is the transpose of the document–term matrix, and similar rows mean words about the same topic;
this is the matrix of <NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis">latent semantic analysis</NoteLink>. With a small window,
similar rows mean words that fill the same slot, such as "cat" and "dog", or "Tuesday" and "Friday"
<Cite id="turney2010" />. A window of one or two words gives more syntactic, substitutable neighbours; a window of five
or more gives more topical ones. Many implementations weight a context by $1/\text{distance}$ or by $(L - d + 1)/L$ at
distance $d$.

### Deriving PMI from counts

Estimate the joint probability of a pair by its relative frequency, $\hat p(w, c) = \#(w, c) / \abs{\Dcal}$, and the
marginals by $\hat p(w) = \#(w)/\abs{\Dcal}$ and $\hat p(c) = \#(c)/\abs{\Dcal}$. If $w$ and $c$ were independent,
the pair would have probability $p(w)\,p(c)$. The ratio of observed to expected is

$$
\frac{\hat p(w, c)}{\hat p(w)\, \hat p(c)} = \frac{\#(w, c)/\abs{\Dcal}}{\#(w)\,\#(c)/\abs{\Dcal}^2} = \frac{\#(w, c)\,
\abs{\Dcal}}{\#(w)\,\#(c)},
$$

and PMI is its logarithm. PMI is 0 under independence, positive when the pair is more frequent than chance and
negative when less. Its average over all pairs, weighted by $p(w, c)$, is the
<NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between the word and context variables, hence the
name. Frequent context words have large $\#(c)$, which divides out, so "the" no longer dominates.

### Why clip at zero

Negative PMI says a pair occurs less often than chance. Estimating that needs a lot of data: a pair expected 0.1 times
and observed 0 times gives no evidence either way, yet its PMI is $\log 0 = -\infty$. Most cells of the matrix are
zero; in the worked example below, 186 of the 256 cells are. PPMI replaces every negative or undefined value by 0,
which keeps the matrix sparse and non-negative. PPMI is the standard weighting for count-based word vectors
<Cite id="jurafsky2025" /> <Cite id="levy2015" />.

### Rare contexts and smoothing

PMI is biased towards rare contexts. A context seen once, next to $w$, gets $\log(\abs{\Dcal}/\#(w))$, the largest
value available to that row. Levy, Goldberg and Dagan transferred a fix from word2vec: raise the context counts to a
power $\alpha$ before normalising,

$$
P_\alpha(c) = \frac{\#(c)^\alpha}{\sum_{c'} \#(c')^\alpha}, \qquad \operatorname{PMI}_\alpha(w, c) = \log
\frac{\hat p(w, c)}{\hat p(w)\, P_\alpha(c)},
$$

with $\alpha = 0.75$ <Cite id="levy2015" />. Because $x \mapsto x^{0.75}$ compresses large counts more than small ones,
$P_\alpha(c) > \hat p(c)$ for rare contexts and $P_\alpha(c) < \hat p(c)$ for frequent ones. The PMI of rare contexts
falls and that of frequent contexts rises.

### Shifted PPMI and SVD

Skip-gram with $k$ negative samples, at its optimum, sets the dot product of a word and a context vector to
$\operatorname{PMI}(w, c) - \log k$ <Cite id="levy2014" />; the derivation is in
<NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink>. The explicit analogue is the **shifted PPMI** matrix,
$\max(\operatorname{PMI}(w, c) - \log k, 0)$.

A PPMI row has one entry per context, tens of thousands of dimensions, mostly zero. A truncated
<NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> $\Mmat \approx \Umat_d \Sigmamat_d
\Vmat_d^{\tr}$ keeps the $d$ directions of largest variance. The rows of $\Umat_d \Sigmamat_d^{\,p}$ are dense
$d$-dimensional word vectors. Levy et al. found that $p = 0.5$ or $p = 0$ works better than the textbook $p = 1$ on
similarity tasks, and that with such choices and context smoothing, PPMI and its SVD perform comparably to skip-gram
embeddings on many word-similarity and analogy benchmarks <Cite id="levy2015" />.

## Worked example

Take the thirteen sentences "the cat is a pet", "the dog is a pet", "we fed the cat", "we fed the dog", "the vet saw
the cat", "the vet saw the dog", "the cat chased the mouse", "the dog chased the cat", "the mouse ate the cheese", "the
mouse ate the grain", "we ate the cheese", "the cat slept" and "the dog slept". They have 58 tokens and 16 word types.
Use a window of $L = 2$ on each side within a sentence. There are $\abs{\Dcal} = 154$ pairs.

"cat" has 14 pairs: "the" 7 times, "chased" 2 times, and "a", "fed", "is", "saw" and "slept" once each. "the" appears
in 47 pairs as a context and "slept" in 4. So

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(\text{cat}, \text{the}) = \log_2 \frac{7 \times 154}{14 \times 47} = 0.712, \qquad
\operatorname{PMI}(\text{cat}, \text{slept}) = \log_2 \frac{1 \times 154}{14 \times 4} = 1.459 \text{ bits}.
$$

"the" was cat's most frequent context by a factor of 3.5 and is now weighted half as much as "slept". With context
smoothing, $P_{0.75}(\text{slept}) = 0.034$ instead of 0.026 and $P_{0.75}(\text{the}) = 0.218$ instead of 0.305, and
the two PMIs become 1.054 and 1.195 bits.

Cosine similarities between rows:

| Pair | Counts | PPMI | PPMI, $\alpha = 0.75$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| cat, dog | 0.993 | 0.939 | 0.919 |
| cat, mouse | 0.887 | 0.409 | 0.555 |
| mouse, cheese | 0.904 | 0.823 | 0.862 |
| cat, cheese | 0.650 | 0.068 | 0.208 |

With counts, "cat" and "cheese" have similarity 0.650, only because both occur next to "the". PPMI drops it to 0.068
and keeps "cat" and "dog", which share "fed", "saw", "chased", "slept" and "is a", at 0.939.

## Assumptions

- **Context is a bag.** Within the window, position is ignored unless contexts are marked with their offset.
- **Counts are reliable.** PMI of pairs seen a few times is noisy. Minimum counts, smoothing and large corpora address
  this.

## Pitfalls

- **Unclipped PMI of unseen pairs is $-\infty$.** Use PPMI, or add pseudo-counts before taking logarithms.
- **The matrix is $V \times V$.** With $V = 100{,}000$ it has $10^{10}$ cells. Store it sparsely and restrict contexts to
  the most frequent words.
- **Window and weighting are hyperparameters.** Levy et al. showed that differences often attributed to algorithms came
  from such choices <Cite id="levy2015" />. Compare methods with matched settings.
