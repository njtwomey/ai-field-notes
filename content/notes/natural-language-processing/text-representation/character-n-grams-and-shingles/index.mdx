<Definition>
The **character n-grams** of a string are its substrings of $n$ consecutive characters, usually after padding the
string with boundary markers. The **$w$-shingles** of a document are its runs of $w$ consecutive tokens, and its
**shingling** $S(A)$ is the set of them. The **resemblance** of documents $A$ and $B$ is the <NoteLink to="hamming-jaccard-and-exact-match">Jaccard similarity</NoteLink> of
their shinglings, $r(A, B) = \abs{S(A) \cap S(B)} / \abs{S(A) \cup S(B)}$, and the **containment** of $A$ in $B$ is
$c(A, B) = \abs{S(A) \cap S(B)} / \abs{S(A)}$ <Cite id="broder1997" />.
</Definition>

## Character n-grams

### Robustness to spelling and morphology

Word features treat "colour" and "color" as unrelated, and a typo creates a new, unseen word. Character n-grams split
each word into overlapping pieces, so variants share most of their pieces. Write the word with a start marker `<` and
an end marker `>`, which keep prefixes and suffixes distinct from word-internal substrings. "where" becomes `<where>`,
and its character trigrams are `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere`, `re>`.

| Pair | Bigram Jaccard | Trigram Jaccard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| colour, color | 5 / 8 = 0.625 | 3 / 8 = 0.375 |
| receive, recieve | 5 / 11 = 0.455 | 3 / 11 = 0.273 |
| running, runner | 4 / 11 = 0.364 | 3 / 10 = 0.300 |

As word features, each pair has Jaccard similarity 0. Shorter n-grams tolerate more variation; longer ones are more
specific. Character n-grams also handle languages without word boundaries and words never seen in training, which
share n-grams with seen words. They act as a soft form of <NoteLink to="stemming">stemming</NoteLink> that needs no
rules.

### Uses

- **Retrieval.** McNamee and Mayfield indexed documents by overlapping character n-grams instead of words and found
  n-grams of length 4 and 5 effective across European languages, with the largest gains in morphologically rich
  languages <Cite id="mcnamee2004" />.
- **Word vectors.** fastText represents a word as the sum of vectors for its character n-grams with $3 \le n \le 6$,
  plus the word itself. An unseen word still gets a vector from its n-grams <Cite id="bojanowski2017" />. See
  <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink>.
- **Evaluation and identification.** <NoteLink to="chrf">chrF</NoteLink> scores translations by character n-gram
  overlap. Character n-gram profiles are a standard baseline for language identification.

The number of distinct character n-grams is bounded by $\abs{\Sigma}^n$ for an alphabet $\Sigma$, far smaller than a
word vocabulary for small $n$, but each word produces as many n-grams as it has characters, so documents have many
more features. <NoteLink to="feature-hashing-for-text">Feature hashing</NoteLink> keeps the dimension fixed.

## Shingling documents

Broder defined shingling to decide whether two web pages are "roughly the same" <Cite id="broder1997" />. Each
document becomes the set of its $w$-word shingles, and two documents are compared as sets.

**Worked example.** Broder's example document is "a rose is a rose is a rose". Its 4-shingles are
\{(a, rose, is, a), (rose, is, a, rose), (is, a, rose, is)\}: 5 shingle tokens, but only 3 distinct shingles. Take a
second document, "a rose is a flower which is a rose".

| $w$ | $\abs{S(A)}$ | $\abs{S(B)}$ | $\abs{S(A) \cap S(B)}$ | Resemblance $r$ | Containment $c(A, B)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 3 | 5 | 3 | 0.600 | 1.000 |
| 2 | 3 | 6 | 3 | 0.500 | 1.000 |
| 3 | 3 | 7 | 3 | 0.429 | 1.000 |
| 4 | 3 | 6 | 1 | 0.125 | 0.333 |

With $w = 1$ shingling is the set of words, a binary <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink>, and the
documents look similar because they use the same words. Longer shingles require the same words in the same order.
Larger $w$ lowers resemblance and makes a match stronger evidence of copying. Containment answers a different
question, whether $A$ appears inside $B$, and is asymmetric.

A one-word edit changes up to $w$ shingles. "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog" against the same sentence
with "jumped" has resemblance 0.778, 0.600 and 0.400 for $w = 1, 2, 3$.

## Estimating resemblance with MinHash

Comparing shingle sets directly costs time proportional to their size for each pair, and a web crawl has billions of
pairs. MinHash replaces each set by a short signature whose agreement rate estimates the resemblance
<Cite id="broder1997" />.

Let $\pi$ be a random permutation of all possible shingles, and let $h_\pi(A) = \min_{a \in S(A)} \pi(a)$ be the
position of $A$'s first shingle in that order. Consider the shingle $x^\star$ of $S(A) \cup S(B)$ that comes first
under $\pi$. Every shingle of the union is equally likely to be $x^\star$, because $\pi$ is uniform. If $x^\star$ is in
$S(A) \cap S(B)$, then it is the minimum of both sets and $h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B)$. If it is in only one set, the other
set's minimum is a different shingle, so the values differ. Therefore

$$
\pr\big(h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B)\big) = \frac{\abs{S(A) \cap S(B)}}{\abs{S(A) \cup S(B)}} = r(A, B).
$$

With $K$ independent permutations, the fraction of agreeing minima, $\hat r = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\indicator[h_{\pi_k}(A) = h_{\pi_k}(B)]$, is an average of $K$ Bernoulli($r$) variables. It is unbiased, with <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> $\sqrt{r(1 - r)/K}$. For $r = 0.5$, $K = 64$ gives a standard deviation of 0.0625 and $K = 256$ gives 0.031.
Each document is stored as $K$ integers, whatever its length. In practice a random hash function stands in for each
random permutation. The general framework of hashing near items to the same bucket, of which MinHash is one family, is
<NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hashing</NoteLink>, which also turns signatures into an
index that finds similar pairs without comparing all of them.

Broder, Glassman, Manasse and Zweig sketched 30 million web documents from a 1996 AltaVista crawl using 10-word
shingles. They found 3.6 million clusters of similar documents, holding 12.3 million documents in total; 2.1 million of
the clusters contained only identical documents <Cite id="broder1997web" />. Search engines
use the same method to drop near-duplicate pages from an index and from results <Cite id="manning2008" />.

## Assumptions

- **Sets, not multisets.** Shingling ignores how often a shingle repeats: "a rose is a rose is a rose" has 5 shingle
  tokens but 3 shingles.
- **Surface similarity.** Resemblance measures shared wording, not shared meaning. Paraphrases have low resemblance.
  <NoteLink to="document-embeddings">Document embeddings</NoteLink> measure similarity of meaning.

## Pitfalls

- **Choice of $w$ or $n$.** Too small and unrelated texts share shingles by chance; too large and a single edit
  breaks every shingle that spans it. Values of 4 to 10 words are common for web pages; 5 to 9 characters for short
  texts.
- **Normalise first.** Case, whitespace and markup differences create spurious differences between shingles. Apply
  <NoteLink to="text-normalisation">normalisation</NoteLink> before shingling.
- **Short documents.** A text shorter than $w$ tokens has no $w$-shingles. Fall back to the whole text as one shingle,
  or to a smaller $w$.
