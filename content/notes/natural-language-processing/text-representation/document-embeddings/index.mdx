<Definition>
A **document embedding** maps a text $s$ of any length to a vector $\vvec_s \in \reals^d$, with $d$ in the tens to
hundreds, so that texts with similar content have nearby vectors. Classical constructions build $\vvec_s$ from
pretrained <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word vectors</NoteLink> $\vvec_w$: the plain average, a weighted average such
as **SIF** (smooth inverse frequency) <Cite id="arora2017" />, or a vector trained per document, as in **doc2vec**
<Cite id="le2014" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> is sparse and has one dimension per word, so "the car broke down"
and "my automobile failed" share no non-zero coordinate. Word vectors place "car" near "automobile". Combining the
word vectors of a text gives a dense vector in which texts with related words are close, even with no word in common.

## Averaging word vectors

Let $\Emat \in \reals^{V \times d}$ hold the word vectors as rows, and let $\xvec_s$ be the bag-of-words count vector
of a text of length $L$. The average of the word vectors is

$$
\bar\vvec_s = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^{L} \vvec_{w_i} = \frac{1}{L} \Emat^{\tr} \xvec_s,
$$

because $\Emat^{\tr}$ applied to the count vector adds each word's row once per occurrence. The average is a linear
projection of the bag of words from $V$ dimensions to $d$. It inherits the bag's blindness to order and adds the
similarity structure of $\Emat$.

The plain average has a defect. Frequent words such as "the", "of" and "and" occur in every text, and their vectors are
added to every average. All averages then share a large common part and have high cosine similarity with each other.
Weighting each word vector by its <NoteLink to="tf-idf">TF-IDF</NoteLink> weight is a common fix. SIF
derives a weighting from a model.

## SIF: smooth inverse frequency

### The model

Arora, Liang and Ma model a sentence as generated around a **discourse vector** $\cvec_s$ <Cite id="arora2017" />. Each
word is emitted independently, either from its unigram probability $p(w)$, with probability $\alpha$, or from a
log-linear model around the discourse:

$$
p(w \mid s) = \alpha\, p(w) + (1 - \alpha) \frac{\exp\inner{\vvec_w}{\tilde\cvec_s}}{Z_{\tilde\cvec_s}}, \qquad
\tilde\cvec_s = \beta\,\cvec_0 + (1 - \beta)\,\cvec_s,
$$

where $Z_{\tilde\cvec}$ normalises over the vocabulary. The first term lets frequent words appear whatever the topic.
The fixed vector $\cvec_0$, orthogonal to $\cvec_s$, is a **common discourse** direction shared by all sentences, for
example syntax.

### Deriving the weights

Estimate $\tilde\cvec_s$ by maximum likelihood, assuming $Z_{\tilde\cvec}$ is roughly the same, $Z$, for every
$\tilde\cvec$, which holds when word vectors are spread roughly uniformly. The log-likelihood of word $w$ is

$$
f_w(\tilde\cvec) = \log\Big(\alpha\, p(w) + \frac{1 - \alpha}{Z} e^{\inner{\vvec_w}{\tilde\cvec}}\Big).
$$

Its gradient is

$$
\nabla f_w(\tilde\cvec) = \frac{\frac{1 - \alpha}{Z} e^{\inner{\vvec_w}{\tilde\cvec}}}{\alpha\, p(w) + \frac{1 -
\alpha}{Z} e^{\inner{\vvec_w}{\tilde\cvec}}}\, \vvec_w.
$$

Expand to first order around $\tilde\cvec = \zeros$, where $e^0 = 1$:

$$
f_w(\tilde\cvec) \approx f_w(\zeros) + \frac{(1 - \alpha)/Z}{\alpha\, p(w) + (1 - \alpha)/Z} \inner{\vvec_w}{\tilde\cvec}
= f_w(\zeros) + \frac{a}{p(w) + a} \inner{\vvec_w}{\tilde\cvec}, \qquad a = \frac{1 - \alpha}{\alpha Z},
$$

dividing numerator and denominator by $\alpha$. The log-likelihood of the sentence is the sum over its words, and it is
linear in $\tilde\cvec$. On the unit sphere, a linear function $\inner{\uvec}{\tilde\cvec}$ is maximised by
$\tilde\cvec \propto \uvec$, so

$$
\tilde\cvec_s \propto \sum_{w \in s} \frac{a}{p(w) + a}\, \vvec_w.
$$

Each word vector is weighted by $a/(p(w) + a)$. A rare word, $p(w) \ll a$, gets weight near 1. A frequent word,
$p(w) \gg a$, gets weight near $a/p(w)$, inversely proportional to its frequency, smoothed by $a$. Arora et al. found
$a$ between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$ to work well, and divide the sum by the sentence length.

### Common component removal

The weighted averages still contain the common direction $\cvec_0$. Estimate it as the first right singular vector
$\uvec$ of the matrix whose rows are the weighted averages of all sentences in a collection (the first principal
direction, without centring), and remove each sentence's projection on it:

$$
\vvec_s \leftarrow \vvec_s - \uvec \uvec^{\tr} \vvec_s.
$$

This is the component shared by all sentences, so removing it leaves what distinguishes them. With both steps, SIF
beat plain averages and several trained sentence encoders on semantic textual similarity benchmarks
<Cite id="arora2017" />. Removal needs many sentences; with a handful, the first singular vector describes the
sample, not the language.

## Worked example

Give six words 3-dimensional vectors and unigram probabilities:

| Word | $\vvec_w$ | $p(w)$ | SIF weight, $a = 10^{-3}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| the | $(2, 2, 0)$ | 0.05 | 0.020 |
| cat | $(1, 0, 1)$ | 0.0005 | 0.667 |
| dog | $(1, 0.2, 0.8)$ | 0.0005 | 0.667 |
| sat | $(0, 1, 1)$ | 0.0002 | 0.833 |
| stock | $(0, 1, -1)$ | 0.0003 | 0.769 |
| fell | $(-1, 1, -1)$ | 0.0002 | 0.833 |

Embed "the cat sat", "the dog sat" and "the stock fell". The plain averages are $(1, 1, 0.667)$, $(1, 1.067, 0.6)$ and
$(0.333, 1.333, -0.667)$. The first and third sentences share only "the", yet their cosine similarity is 0.512,
because the vector of "the" is the longest of the three in each sum.

With SIF weights, the averages become $(0.235, 0.291, 0.500)$, $(0.235, 0.335, 0.456)$ and $(-0.265, 0.547, -0.534)$.
The cosine of the first and third falls to $-0.337$, while the first and second stay similar at 0.995 (0.998 before).
The weight of "the" is 0.020, so it no longer sets the direction of every sentence.

## doc2vec: paragraph vectors

Le and Mikolov learn a vector per document directly, by the same kind of prediction task that trains word2vec
<Cite id="le2014" /> <Cite id="mikolov2013" />.

- **PV-DM** (distributed memory). Each document $s$ has a trainable vector $\dvec_s$. To predict a word from the words
  before it, the model combines $\dvec_s$ with the context word vectors, by averaging or concatenation, and applies a
  softmax over the vocabulary. $\dvec_s$ acts as a memory of what the rest of the document is about.
- **PV-DBOW** (distributed bag of words). $\dvec_s$ alone predicts words sampled from the document, ignoring order, as
  skip-gram predicts context words from a centre word.

Word vectors and softmax weights are shared across documents. A new document has no vector, so one is found at test
time by gradient descent on its words, with all shared parameters frozen. Inference is therefore an optimisation, not
a forward pass, and its result depends on the number of steps and the learning rate. Le and Mikolov recommend
combining the PV-DM and PV-DBOW vectors.

## Relation to other representations

- A truncated SVD of the document–term matrix, as in <NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis">latent semantic
  analysis</NoteLink>, gives dense document vectors from counts alone.
- The topic proportions of <NoteLink to="latent-dirichlet-allocation">latent Dirichlet allocation</NoteLink> are a
  document embedding on the simplex, whose coordinates can be read as topics.
- <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">Sentence embeddings</NoteLink> from fine-tuned transformer encoders read word order
  and context, and they outperform averaged word vectors on semantic similarity <Cite id="reimers2019" />. Averages and
  SIF remain strong, cheap baselines.

## Assumptions

- **Order carries little meaning.** Averages and PV-DBOW ignore order; PV-DM uses it only within a window.
- **Word vectors transfer.** SIF assumes the pretrained word vectors and word frequencies suit the target domain.

## Pitfalls

- **Unweighted averages are dominated by frequent words.** Weight or remove them.
- **Long documents average away their content.** A 5,000-word document's average is close to the corpus mean. Split
  long documents into passages.
- **Estimate $p(w)$ and $\uvec$ on a large corpus.** Estimates from the few texts being compared are noisy.
- **doc2vec inference is stochastic.** The same document can receive different vectors on two runs. Fix seeds and
  increase the number of inference steps.
