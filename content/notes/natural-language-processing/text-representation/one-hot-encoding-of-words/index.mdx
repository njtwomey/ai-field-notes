<Definition>
Fix a vocabulary $\Vcal = \set{w_1, \dots, w_V}$ and an index $i(w)$ for each word. The **one-hot encoding** of word
$w$ is the standard basis vector $\evec_{i(w)} \in \set{0, 1}^V$: a 1 in coordinate $i(w)$ and 0 elsewhere. A text of
$T$ tokens becomes a $T \times V$ matrix with one 1 per row. A word not in $\Vcal$ is mapped to a reserved unknown
token, written `<unk>`, which has its own index <Cite id="jurafsky2025" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A one-hot vector is the input format that linear algebra requires
for a symbol that has no numeric value. It is the same construction that
<NoteLink to="categorical-encoding">categorical encoding</NoteLink> applies to any discrete feature, applied to a
feature with tens of thousands of levels.

The encoding says which word occurred and nothing about what the word means.

## Details

### All distinct words are equidistant

For basis vectors, $\evec_i^{\tr} \evec_j = 1$ when $i = j$ and 0 otherwise. For two different words $i \ne j$:

$$
\norm{\evec_i - \evec_j}^2 = \evec_i^{\tr}\evec_i - 2\,\evec_i^{\tr}\evec_j + \evec_j^{\tr}\evec_j = 1 - 0 + 1 = 2,
$$

so $\norm{\evec_i - \evec_j} = \sqrt 2$ and the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> is $\evec_i^{\tr}\evec_j / (\norm{\evec_i}\norm{\evec_j})
= 0$. "cat" is exactly as far from "dog" as from "carburettor". Any method that
compares one-hot vectors by distance, inner product or angle sees every pair of distinct words as equally unrelated.

### Linear models learn one parameter per word

A linear function of a one-hot vector reads off a single coordinate: $\wvec^{\tr} \evec_i = w_i$. A classifier on
one-hot inputs therefore learns a separate weight for each word, and nothing it learns about "cat" transfers to "cats"
or "kitten". Rare words get few training examples, so their weights are poorly estimated.

### An embedding layer is a matrix times a one-hot vector

Let $\Emat \in \reals^{V \times d}$ hold one $d$-dimensional row per word. Then $\Emat^{\tr} \evec_i$ is row $i$ of
$\Emat$. An embedding layer computes exactly this product, implemented as a table lookup because the product with a
one-hot vector touches one row. Training $\Emat$ so that similar words get similar rows gives
<NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink>, which replace the fixed geometry of one-hot vectors, where
every pair is at distance $\sqrt 2$, with a learned one.

### A bag of words is a sum of one-hot vectors

Summing the rows of a text's $T \times V$ one-hot matrix gives a vector whose coordinate $i$ counts the occurrences of
word $i$. That sum is the text's <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink>. Summing discards the row order,
which is why a bag of words cannot tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog".

### Sparsity and storage

A one-hot vector has one non-zero out of $V$. Storing it densely costs $V$ numbers; storing it sparsely costs one
integer, the index. Every practical system stores the index and never builds the vector.

## Choosing the vocabulary

The vocabulary is fixed from training text. Words that occur fewer than a minimum count $c_{\min}$ times are left out,
because their parameters could not be learned. At test time, every word outside $\Vcal$ becomes `<unk>`.

**Worked example.** Take the 161,975 lower-cased alphabetic tokens of _Emma_ from the <Gloss name="nltk" /> corpus sample
<Cite id="bird2009" />. Build the vocabulary from the first half of the book and encode the second half.

| Minimum count $c_{\min}$ | Vocabulary size $V$ | Tokens of the second half mapped to `<unk>` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5,225 | 3.6% |
| 2 | 3,015 | 5.8% |
| 5 | 1,458 | 10.2% |

Even with every word of the first half kept, 3.6% of the tokens of the second half are unknown, and 1,871 distinct
words are lost. Raising $c_{\min}$ from 1 to 5 shrinks $V$ by a factor of 3.6 and nearly triples the unknown rate. The
unknown words are the rare ones, such as names and specific nouns, which often carry the most information about a
text.

Three alternatives avoid a single `<unk>` token. Subword tokenisers such as
<NoteLink to="byte-pair-encoding">byte-pair encoding</NoteLink> split an unknown word into known pieces.
<NoteLink to="character-n-grams-and-shingles">Character n-grams</NoteLink> represent any word by its substrings.
<NoteLink to="feature-hashing-for-text">Feature hashing</NoteLink> maps any word to a column without a vocabulary.

## Assumptions

- **Words are atomic symbols.** Morphology, spelling and meaning are invisible. Relations between words must come
  from elsewhere: <NoteLink to="stemming">stemming</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="lemmatisation">lemmatisation</NoteLink>
  merge forms before encoding, and <NoteLink to="distributional-semantics">distributional representations</NoteLink>
  learn similarity from context.
- **The vocabulary is closed.** The encoding has no place for a new word except `<unk>`.

## Pitfalls

- **The index order is arbitrary.** Indices are often assigned by frequency rank, but a model must not treat the index
  as a number. Feeding $i(w)$ as a single numeric feature imposes an order that means nothing.
- **Vocabularies must match.** A model trained with one index map fails silently with another. Store the vocabulary
  with the model.
- **Dense one-hot matrices do not fit.** A $T \times V$ dense matrix for a book of $T = 160{,}000$ tokens with
  $V = 50{,}000$ has $8 \times 10^9$ entries. Use indices or sparse matrices.
