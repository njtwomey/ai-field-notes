<Definition>
Byte-pair encoding (BPE) learns an ordered list of merges from a corpus. It starts with every word split into single
symbols (characters or bytes) and repeats one step until the vocabulary reaches a target size: count every adjacent
pair of symbols, weighted by word frequency, and replace every occurrence of the most frequent pair $(a, b)$ by the new
symbol $ab$. The vocabulary is the base symbols plus one new symbol per merge <Cite id="sennrich2016" />.
</Definition>

## Origin

BPE began as a data-compression scheme: replace the most frequent pair of bytes in a file by an unused byte, and repeat
<Cite id="gage1994" />. Sennrich, Haddow and Birch applied the same greedy step to the characters of words to build a
subword vocabulary for <NoteLink to="machine-translation">neural machine translation</NoteLink>. Frequent words end up
as single symbols, and rare words split into frequent pieces, so the translation model needs no out-of-vocabulary token
<Cite id="sennrich2016" />.

## The learning algorithm

1. **Pre-tokenise and count.** Split the corpus into words and count each distinct word. Represent each word as its
   sequence of characters followed by an end-of-word marker `</w>`. The marker keeps "est" at the end of "newest"
   distinct from "est" at the start of "estate".
2. **Count pairs.** For every adjacent pair of symbols $(a, b)$, sum the counts of the words in which it occurs, once
   per occurrence.
3. **Merge.** Take the pair with the highest count, append $(a, b)$ to the merge list, and replace $a$ followed by $b$
   by the single symbol $ab$ in every word.
4. **Repeat** steps 2 and 3 for a chosen number of merges $M$.

The final vocabulary size is the number of base symbols plus $M$, less any symbols that no longer occur on their own.
$M$ is the one hyperparameter; translation and <Gloss name="language-model">language models</Gloss> use tens of
thousands of merges.

**Encoding new text.** Split a word into base symbols and apply the learned merges in the order they were learned.
Because every base symbol is in the vocabulary, every word has an encoding. The merge list, not the vocabulary alone,
determines the segmentation: two vocabularies with the same symbols but different merge orders can encode a word
differently.

**Cost.** A naive implementation recounts all pairs after each merge, which costs $O(M \cdot S)$ for $S$ symbols in the
distinct-word table. Practical implementations keep a pair-count table and an index from each pair to the words that
contain it, and update only the words touched by a merge.

## Worked example

Take the corpus of Sennrich et al.: "low" 5 times, "lower" 2 times, "newest" 6 times and "widest" 3 times. Written as
symbols, the words are `l o w </w>`, `l o w e r </w>`, `n e w e s t </w>` and `w i d e s t </w>`. The corpus has
$5 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 6 + 6 \cdot 7 + 3 \cdot 7 = 95$ symbols.

Several pairs occur in both "newest" and "widest". The pairs `e s`, `s t` and `t </w>` each occur $6 + 3 = 9$ times.
With ties broken by the first pair met when reading the corpus, the first ten merges are:

| Step | Merge | Count | Step | Merge | Count |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `e` + `s` | 9 | 6 | `n` + `e` | 6 |
| 2 | `es` + `t` | 9 | 7 | `ne` + `w` | 6 |
| 3 | `est` + `</w>` | 9 | 8 | `new` + `est</w>` | 6 |
| 4 | `l` + `o` | 7 | 9 | `low` + `</w>` | 5 |
| 5 | `lo` + `w` | 7 | 10 | `w` + `i` | 3 |

After ten merges the corpus reads `low</w>`, `low e r </w>`, `newest</w>` and `wi d est</w>`, which is 28 tokens instead
of 95. The unseen word "lowest" starts as `l o w e s t </w>`. Replaying the merges gives `l o w es t </w>` after step
1, `l o w est</w>` after step 3, and `low est</w>` after step 5. The model receives two tokens it has seen in training,
`low` and `est</w>`, instead of one unknown word.

## Byte-level BPE

Character-level BPE still needs a base symbol for every character, and Unicode has more than 150,000. A byte-level BPE
runs on the UTF-8 bytes of the text, so there are exactly 256 base symbols and every string, in any script, has an
encoding. <Gloss name="gpt-2" /> uses byte-level BPE with 50,000 merges; its vocabulary of 50,257 tokens is the 256
bytes, the 50,000 merged symbols and one end-of-text token <Cite id="radford2019" />.

GPT-2 pre-tokenises with a regular expression that separates letters, digits and punctuation, so that merges cannot
join, for example, a word and the full stop after it. Without that rule the vocabulary fills with variants such as
"dog", "dog." and "dog!".

A merged byte sequence need not be a whole UTF-8 character. A token may hold half of a multi-byte character, so decoding
a single token can give invalid text even though decoding a whole sequence is exact.

## Properties

- **Greedy and deterministic.** Each merge is chosen by frequency alone, without reference to any downstream model.
  The segmentation of a word is unique.
- **Frequency order.** Early merges are frequent pieces; late merges are long, rare pieces, often whole words. Cutting
  the merge list shorter gives a smaller, finer vocabulary.
- **No probabilities.** BPE has no likelihood for a segmentation, so it cannot rank alternatives. The
  <NoteLink to="wordpiece-and-unigram-tokenisation">unigram language model</NoteLink> tokeniser has one, and can
  sample segmentations.

## Pitfalls

- **The merge list is part of the model.** Retraining the tokeniser changes every token id; the embedding table must be
  retrained with it.
- **Tie rules and pre-tokenisers change the vocabulary.** Two implementations with different tie rules or pre-tokenisers
  learn different vocabularies from the same corpus.
- **Uneven compression across domains.** Merges learned on prose split code, numbers and other languages into many
  short tokens, which lengthens their sequences.
