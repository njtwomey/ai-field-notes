<Definition>
A **word n-gram** is a sequence of $n$ consecutive tokens $(w_i, \dots, w_{i+n-1})$. A document of $L$ tokens has
$L - n + 1$ n-gram tokens. A **bag of n-grams** counts every n-gram, usually for all orders $n$ from 1 up to a maximum,
as features of the document. A **collocation** is an n-gram whose words occur together more often than their separate
frequencies predict, such as "strong tea" or "Frank Churchill" <Cite id="manning1999" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> cannot tell "dog bites man" from "man bites dog". Their bigrams
differ: \{"dog bites", "bites man"\} against \{"man bites", "bites dog"\}. Bigram features capture negation ("not good"),
compound names ("New York", "machine learning") and short phrases, all of which unigram counts break apart. N-grams
keep order within a window of $n$ tokens and discard it beyond.

The same objects appear in three roles. As features they extend the bag of words. In an
<NoteLink to="n-gram-language-model">n-gram language model</NoteLink> they are the conditioning contexts of next-word
probabilities. In evaluation, <NoteLink to="bleu">BLEU</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="rouge">ROUGE</NoteLink> count
n-grams shared with a reference.

## Vocabulary growth

Take the 161,975 lower-cased alphabetic tokens of _Emma_ in the <Gloss name="nltk" /> corpus sample <Cite id="bird2009" />.

| $n$ | Distinct n-grams | Share seen once |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 7,096 | 38.8% |
| 2 | 65,913 | 72.5% |
| 3 | 131,033 | 89.8% |
| 4 | 155,485 | 97.2% |

The number of possible n-grams is $V^n$. The number observed is bounded by the $L - n + 1$ n-gram tokens, and for
$n \ge 3$ it approaches that bound: 4-grams are nearly all distinct, and 97% of them occur once. A feature that occurs
once in training cannot be weighted reliably and rarely recurs at test time.

Growth with text length is also faster for larger $n$. In the first 10,000 tokens of _Emma_ there are 1,764 distinct
unigrams and 7,084 bigrams; in the first 160,000 there are 7,040 and 65,277. Sixteen times more text gave four times
the unigram vocabulary and nine times the bigram vocabulary. Bigram and trigram feature sets therefore need pruning by
minimum count, <NoteLink to="feature-hashing-for-text">feature hashing</NoteLink>, or both.

## Collocations

Most frequent bigrams are not collocations. The most frequent bigrams of _Emma_ are "to be" (608) and "of the" (566),
which are frequent because their words are. A collocation measure compares the observed count of a pair with the count
expected if the two words were independent.

### Pointwise mutual information

For words $a$ and $b$, with $p(a, b)$ the probability that a bigram is "$a$ $b$" and $p(a)$, $p(b)$ the probabilities
of $a$ in first position and $b$ in second,

$$
\operatorname{PMI}(a, b) = \log_2 \frac{p(a, b)}{p(a)\, p(b)} = \log_2 \frac{c(a\,b)\, N}{c(a)\, c(b)},
$$

where $N$ is the number of bigram tokens and the $c$ are counts <Cite id="church1990" />. Under <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>
$p(a, b) = p(a) p(b)$ and PMI is 0. Each extra bit means the pair occurs twice as often as independence predicts.
Church and Hanks called it the association ratio and used it to find word associations for lexicography.

**PMI favours rare pairs.** If $a$ and $b$ each occur exactly once, and together, then
$c(a\,b) = c(a) = c(b) = 1$ and $\operatorname{PMI} = \log_2 N$, the largest value possible. _Emma_ has
$N = 161{,}974$ bigrams, so such a pair scores $\log_2 N = 17.31$ bits, and 79 bigrams tie at that maximum, among them
"scalloped oysters" and "inconveniently shy". The score is an estimate from one observation. A minimum count fixes the
ranking: among bigrams seen at least 20 times, the highest PMI goes to "maple grove" (12.35 bits), "colonel campbell"
and "robert martin", which are names in the novel.

### Dunning's likelihood ratio

Dunning proposed a test that accounts for how much evidence a count carries <Cite id="dunning1993" />. Arrange the
bigrams in a $2 \times 2$ table by whether the first word is $a$ and whether the second is $b$:

| | second is $b$ | second is not $b$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| first is $a$ | $k_{11}$ | $k_{12}$ |
| first is not $a$ | $k_{21}$ | $k_{22}$ |

Under the <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink>, the probability that the second word is $b$ is the same, $\pi$, whether or not the first is
$a$. Under the alternative it is $\pi_1$ after $a$ and $\pi_2$ otherwise. Each row is a binomial sample, so the
log-likelihood of the table is

$$
\ell(\pi_1, \pi_2) = k_{11} \log \pi_1 + k_{12} \log(1 - \pi_1) + k_{21} \log \pi_2 + k_{22} \log(1 - \pi_2).
$$

The maximum-likelihood estimates are $\hat\pi_1 = k_{11}/(k_{11} + k_{12})$, $\hat\pi_2 = k_{21}/(k_{21} + k_{22})$
and, under the null, $\hat\pi = (k_{11} + k_{21})/N$. The likelihood-ratio statistic is

$$
G^2 = 2\big(\ell(\hat\pi_1, \hat\pi_2) - \ell(\hat\pi, \hat\pi)\big) = 2 \sum_{i,j} k_{ij} \log \frac{k_{ij}}{E_{ij}},
$$

where $E_{ij}$ is the count expected under independence, the row total times the column total divided by $N$. The
second form follows by substituting the estimates: $\hat\pi_1 / \hat\pi = k_{11} / E_{11}$, and likewise for the other
cells. Under the null, $G^2$ is approximately $\chi^2$ with one degree of freedom.

$G^2$ is a sum of observed counts times log ratios, so a pair seen once contributes little however surprising it is.
Pearson's $\chi^2 = \sum (k_{ij} - E_{ij})^2 / E_{ij}$ tests the same hypothesis but divides by $E_{ij}$, which is tiny
for rare words, and Dunning showed that it overstates the significance of rare events.

### Worked example

In _Emma_, "frank" is the first word of 208 bigrams, "churchill" the second word of 224, and "frank churchill" occurs
151 times, so the table is $k_{11} = 151$, $k_{12} = 57$, $k_{21} = 73$, $k_{22} = 161{,}693$. The expected count is
$E_{11} = 208 \times 224 / 161{,}974 = 0.288$.

| Bigram | $k_{11}$ | PMI (bits) | $G^2$ | Pearson $\chi^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| frank churchill | 151 | 9.04 | 1,882.2 | 79,175 |
| of the | 566 | 2.04 | 826.2 | 1,410 |
| inconveniently shy | 1 | 17.31 | 26.0 | 161,974 |

For the single-occurrence pair, $\chi^2$ equals $N$, the value of a perfect association in a large sample, and ranks
it above "frank churchill". $G^2 \approx 2(\ln N + 1) = 26.0$ is significant but small, because one observation is
little evidence. The top five bigrams of _Emma_ by $G^2$ are "i am" (2,936), "mr knightley" (2,621), "mrs weston"
(2,297), "to be" (1,884) and "frank churchill" (1,882). $G^2$ finds associations that are both strong and well
attested, which includes frequent grammatical pairs; PMI with a count threshold finds names and fixed phrases.

## Skip-grams as features

A **$k$-skip-bigram** is a pair of tokens with at most $k$ tokens between them <Cite id="guthrie2006" />. It tolerates
an inserted word: "not very good" contains the 1-skip-bigram "not good". A sequence of $L$ tokens has
$\sum_{g=1}^{k+1} (L - g) = (k + 1) L - (k + 1)(k + 2)/2$ $k$-skip-bigrams, counting the ordinary bigrams ($g = 1$).
"the cat sat on the mat" ($L = 6$) has 5 bigrams, 9 1-skip-bigrams and 12 2-skip-bigrams. Guthrie et al. measured how many
trigrams of test documents appear in the training data; skip-grams from a 50-million-word corpus covered about as many
as ordinary trigrams from a corpus four times larger. The pairs of a word
and every word in a window around it are also the training data of the skip-gram
<NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embedding</NoteLink> model and of
<NoteLink to="co-occurrence-matrices-and-pointwise-mutual-information">co-occurrence matrices</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Order matters only locally.** Dependencies longer than $n$ tokens are invisible.
- **Contiguity.** Plain n-grams require adjacency. Skip-grams relax it.

## Pitfalls

- **Feature explosion.** Unpruned trigram features outnumber training documents by orders of magnitude. Use a
  minimum count, a maximum feature count or hashing, and regularise the model.
- **Stop-word removal before n-grams invents n-grams.** Removing "of" from "king of France" creates the bigram
  "king france", which never occurred. Form n-grams first (see <NoteLink to="stop-words">stop words</NoteLink>).
- **PMI without a count threshold ranks noise.** Require a minimum count, or rank by $G^2$.
- **Significance is not importance.** $G^2$ ranks "i am" highly because it is strongly and frequently associated, not
  because it is an interesting phrase. Filter by part-of-speech patterns such as adjective–noun when looking for terms.
