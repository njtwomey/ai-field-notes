<Definition>
**Distributional semantics** represents each word by a vector computed from the contexts in which the word occurs in a
corpus, and measures similarity of meaning by similarity of those vectors, usually their cosine. It rests on the
**distributional hypothesis**: words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings
<Cite id="harris1954" /> <Cite id="turney2010" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Nobody defines "wampimuk" for you, but after reading "the wampimuk climbed the tree" and "we fed the wampimuk nuts",
you know roughly what kind of thing it is. Its contexts place it among small animals. Distributional semantics does
this with counts. Every word gets a vector of its contexts, and words with overlapping contexts get nearby vectors.

This is the step from a <NoteLink to="one-hot-encoding-of-words">one-hot representation</NoteLink>, in which every two
words are equally unrelated, to a representation in which "cat" is closer to "dog" than to "cheese". The similarity is
learned from unlabelled text alone.

## The hypothesis

Harris stated it as a claim about linguistic structure: "if we consider words or morphemes A and B to be more
different in meaning than A and C, then we will often find that the distributions of A and B are more different than
the distributions of A and C" <Cite id="harris1954" />. Difference of meaning correlates with difference of
distribution. Firth put it as a slogan: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" <Cite id="firth1957" />.

The hypothesis says nothing about which contexts to use or how to compare them. Those choices define the different
models, and they decide what kind of similarity the vectors capture.

## Kinds of context

Turney and Pantel classify <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">vector space models</NoteLink> of meaning by their matrix <Cite id="turney2010" />.

| Matrix | Rows | Columns | Similar rows mean |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| term–document | words | documents | words about the same topic ("doctor", "nurse", "hospital") |
| word–context | words | nearby words, in a window | words used the same way ("doctor", "surgeon") |
| pair–pattern | word pairs | patterns joining them ("X cuts Y") | pairs in the same relation ("mason : stone", "carpenter : wood") |

The term–document matrix, reduced by a truncated SVD, is <NoteLink to="latent-semantic-analysis">latent semantic
analysis</NoteLink> <Cite id="deerwester1990" />. The word–context matrix, reweighted by positive pointwise mutual
information, is described in <NoteLink to="co-occurrence-matrices-and-pointwise-mutual-information">co-occurrence matrices and PMI</NoteLink>.
Narrow windows favour similarity (words that can replace each other); wide windows and whole documents favour
relatedness (words that appear together).

**Second-order similarity.** Two words need not co-occur to be similar. "cat" and "kitten" may never appear in the same
sentence, but both appear near "purr", "fed" and "vet". Their rows are similar because they share contexts, which is a
second-order relation: similarity of first-order co-occurrence profiles.

## Worked example

In the thirteen-sentence corpus of <NoteLink to="co-occurrence-matrices-and-pointwise-mutual-information">co-occurrence matrices and
PMI</NoteLink> ("the cat is a pet", "we fed the dog", "the mouse ate the cheese" and ten more), with a window of two
words and PPMI weighting, the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> of the rows of "cat" and "dog" is 0.939 and of "cat" and "cheese" is
0.068. "cat" and "dog" never occur within two words of each other; in "the dog chased the cat" they are three
apart. Their similarity comes entirely from sharing the contexts "fed", "saw", "chased", "slept" and "is a". Reducing the PPMI matrix to two dimensions with a truncated SVD,
taking each word's vector as its row of $\Umat_2 \Sigmamat_2^{1/2}$, gives cosine 0.991 for "cat" and "dog", 0.734
for "cat" and "mouse" and 1.000 for "cheese" and "grain", which occur in identical contexts.

## Count and predict

Two families of models implement the hypothesis.

- **Count models** build a word–context matrix, reweight it (PPMI, <Gloss name="tf-idf" />), and optionally reduce its dimension with
  an <Gloss name="svd" />. LSA and PPMI–SVD are count models.
- **Predict models** learn a vector per word by training it to predict its contexts, or the reverse. Word2vec's
  skip-gram and CBOW are predict models <Cite id="mikolov2013" />; see <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word
  embeddings</NoteLink>.

Baroni, Dinu and Kruszewski compared the two across many lexical semantics tasks and parameter settings, and reported
"a thorough and resounding victory" for the predict models <Cite id="baroni2014" />. The picture then changed. Levy and
Goldberg showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises a shifted PMI matrix, so the two families
optimise related objectives <Cite id="levy2014" />. Levy, Goldberg and Dagan then showed that much of the predict
models' advantage came from design choices, such as subsampling frequent words, smoothing the context distribution and
weighting nearer contexts more, rather than from prediction itself. With those choices transferred to PPMI and SVD,
count models performed comparably, and no method won on every task <Cite id="levy2015" />.

## From word types to word tokens

A count or predict model gives one vector per word type. "bank" gets a single vector that mixes the riverside and the
financial sense in proportion to their frequencies. Contextual models, such as the encoders trained by
<NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">masked language modelling</NoteLink>, give each occurrence of a word its own
vector computed from the sentence around it. They apply the distributional hypothesis at the level of individual
tokens: the representation of a word in a sentence is a function of its context in that sentence.

## Assumptions

- **Meaning is reflected in usage.** The hypothesis is a correlation, not an identity. Properties that are rarely
  stated in text, such as the colour of common objects, are weakly represented.
- **One corpus, one meaning.** The vectors describe usage in the training corpus, including its biases and its
  domain.

## Pitfalls

- **Antonyms are distributionally similar.** "hot" and "cold" appear in the same contexts ("the water is ___"), so they
  get similar vectors. Distributional similarity is not synonymy.
- **Similarity against relatedness.** Evaluation sets differ in which one they measure. Choose the window and the
  evaluation to match the task.
- **Rare words get poor vectors.** A word seen a handful of times has too few contexts. Subword models such as fastText
  share information through <NoteLink to="character-n-grams-and-shingles">character n-grams</NoteLink>.
