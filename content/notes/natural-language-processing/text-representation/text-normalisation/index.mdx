<Definition>
Text normalisation is a function $\nu$ from strings to strings, applied before counting or tokenising, that maps
strings a system should treat as equal to one canonical string. Two words $u$ and $v$ become one vocabulary entry when
$\nu(u) = \nu(v)$. The set of words that map to the same string is an **equivalence class**
<Cite id="manning2008" />. Common steps are Unicode normalisation, case folding, accent stripping, and rules for
punctuation, numbers and contractions.
</Definition>

## Why normalise

A <NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink> counts strings, so "The" and "the" are different features unless
they are made equal. Every feature split this way halves the evidence for each half. In Jane Austen's _Emma_ (about 161,000
word tokens, taken from the <Gloss name="nltk" /> corpus sample <Cite id="bird2009" />), "the" occurs 4,846 times in lower case and 358
times as "The". The alphabetic word types number 7,663 with case kept and 7,204 after lower-casing, so 459 types were
case variants of other types.

Normalisation trades **precision** for **recall**. Merging more variants lets a query or a model match more texts
(recall rises). Merging variants with different meanings creates false matches (precision falls). No normalisation is
neutral: each rule decides which distinctions the representation keeps.

## Unicode normalisation

A Unicode string is a sequence of code points. Several code-point sequences can display as the same text.

- **Canonical equivalence.** "é" can be the single code point U+00E9, or "e" followed by the combining acute accent
  U+0301. The two strings have lengths 1 and 2 and compare unequal byte by byte. They are canonically equivalent: they
  represent the same abstract character.
- **Compatibility equivalence.** The ligature "ﬁ" (U+FB01), the superscript "²", the circled digit "①", the
  full-width "Ａ" and the Roman numeral "Ⅻ" are formatting variants of "fi", "2", "1", "A" and "XII". They are
  compatibility equivalent: the same text with a presentational difference.

Unicode Standard Annex 15 defines four normal forms <Cite id="whistler2026uax15" />.

| Form | Decompose | Recompose | "é" as U+0065 U+0301 | "ﬁ" | "x²" |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NFD | canonical | no | U+0065 U+0301 | ﬁ | x² |
| NFC | canonical | yes | U+00E9 | ﬁ | x² |
| NFKD | compatibility | no | U+0065 U+0301 | fi | x2 |
| NFKC | compatibility | yes | U+00E9 | fi | x2 |

NFC changes no visible text, so it is always safe to apply. NFKC also folds compatibility characters, which loses
information: "x²" becomes "x2" and "½" becomes "1⁄2" (the digits around a fraction slash U+2044). Search engines and
tokenisers commonly apply NFKC; a system that must round-trip text exactly applies NFC or nothing.

## Case folding

Lower-casing maps upper-case letters to lower case. **Case folding** is the Unicode operation designed for
case-insensitive comparison, and it differs from lower-casing for some characters <Cite id="unicode2026casefolding" />.

- German "ß" has no single upper-case letter. Lower-casing leaves "Straße" as "straße". Case folding gives
  "strasse", which equals the case folding of "STRASSE".
- Greek has two lower-case sigmas. Lower-casing "ΣΑΣ" gives "σας", with the word-final "ς". Case folding gives "σασ",
  so that a final and a medial sigma compare equal.
- Turkish dotted capital "İ" lower-cases to "i" followed by a combining dot (two code points) in the default mapping.
  Turkish text needs a language-specific mapping, because Turkish "I" lower-cases to dotless "ı".

What case folding loses is the information that capitals carry. "US" (the country) and "us" (the pronoun) merge.
"Apple" the company and "apple" the fruit merge. Sentence-initial capitals are noise; capitals inside a sentence often
mark a name. Named-entity recognisers therefore keep case, while bag-of-words retrieval usually folds it
<Cite id="manning2008" />. A compromise folds only sentence-initial words, a heuristic called truecasing when it is
learned from data.

## Accent stripping

Accent stripping removes diacritics. The standard implementation is: decompose to NFD, delete every combining mark
(Unicode category M), and recompose. "résumé" becomes "resume" and "naïve" becomes "naive".

Two limits follow from the method. It removes only diacritics that decompose. "Ø" in "Ørsted" and "Ł" in "Łódź" are
letters with no decomposition, so the same procedure gives "Ørsted" and "Łodz", with only the acute accents on "ó" and "ź"
removed. It also merges distinct words. English "résumé" (a CV) and "resume" (continue) become one term. In Spanish,
"año" (year) becomes "ano" (anus), and in French "où" (where) becomes "ou" (or). Accent stripping helps when users type
without accents; it harms when accents distinguish words.

## Punctuation, numbers and contractions

- **Punctuation.** Deleting punctuation joins "U.S.A." and "USA" and splits "e-mail" or joins it as "email", depending
  on whether the hyphen becomes a space or nothing. Punctuation that carries meaning, such as the decimal point in
  "3.14", the "+" in "C++" or the "@" in an email address, needs rules that run before deletion.
- **Numbers.** Each distinct number is a separate, usually rare, word type. Replacing every digit sequence by one
  placeholder, such as `<num>`, merges them. The representation keeps the fact that a number occurred and loses its
  value, so "3 cats" and "300 cats" become identical.
- **Contractions.** Expanding "don't" to "do not" and "isn't" to "is not" makes the negation a separate token, "not",
  which a <NoteLink to="stop-words">stop-word</NoteLink> filter or a negation feature can then see. The expansion is
  ambiguous for "'s", which can be "is" ("it's"), "has" ("she's gone") or the possessive ("the cat's"), and for "'d",
  which can be "would" or "had".

## Worked example

Normalise "The Café's résumé-writing course starts on 3 May; it isn't full." with five steps in order: NFKC, case
folding, accent stripping, contraction expansion and number replacement, then split into words.

1. NFKC changes nothing here: there are no compatibility characters. If "é" was typed as "e" plus a combining accent,
   NFKC recomposes it to U+00E9.
2. Case folding gives "the café's résumé-writing course starts on 3 may; it isn't full."
3. Accent stripping gives "the cafe's resume-writing course starts on 3 may; it isn't full."
4. Contraction expansion gives "the cafe resume-writing course starts on 3 may; it is not full." The possessive "'s"
   after the noun "cafe" is dropped.
5. Number replacement gives "... starts on `<num>` may; ...".

The final tokens are `the`, `cafe`, `resume-writing`, `course`, `starts`, `on`, `<num>`, `may`, `it`, `is`, `not`,
`full`. The month "May" has become the modal verb "may", and "résumé" can now match "resume" (continue). Both are
losses caused by rules that helped elsewhere.

## Pitfalls

- **Normalise training and query text identically.** A model trained on case-folded text sees unknown words when a
  query is not folded. The normaliser is part of the model.
- **Lossy steps break round-tripping.** A tokeniser that applies NFKC or lower-casing cannot reproduce the original
  string from its tokens (see <NoteLink to="tokenisation">tokenisation</NoteLink>).
- **Case and accent rules are language-specific.** Case and accent rules for English are wrong for Turkish, German or Vietnamese. Apply
  language-specific rules when the language is known.
- **Normalisation interacts with later steps.** A <NoteLink to="stop-words">stop-word list</NoteLink> written in lower
  case misses "The" unless case is folded first, and a <NoteLink to="stemming">stemmer</NoteLink> expects lower-case
  ASCII letters.
