<Definition>
Let a collection hold $N$ documents over a vocabulary of $V$ terms. The **bag-of-words** vector of document $d$ is
$\xvec_d \in \naturals^V$, where $\mathrm{tf}_{t,d}$, the term frequency, counts the occurrences of term $t$ in $d$.
The document frequency $\mathrm{df}_t$ is the number of documents that contain $t$. **TF-IDF** replaces each count by

$$
w_{t,d} = \mathrm{tf}_{t,d} \cdot \log \frac{N}{\mathrm{df}_t},
$$

so that a term counts for more when it is frequent in the document and rare in the collection
<Cite id="sparckjones1972" /> <Cite id="salton1988" />.
</Definition>

## Bag of words

A bag of words keeps which terms occur and how often, and discards their order. "dog bites man" and "man bites dog"
have the same vector. Stacking the vectors of all documents gives the $N \times V$ document–term matrix, which is sparse:
a document uses a tiny fraction of the vocabulary.

In the vector space model, a query is a short document, and documents are ranked by the cosine similarity between
their vectors and the query's <Cite id="salton1975" />. The cosine divides by both vector lengths, so a long document
does not win only by having more words (see <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">cosine
similarity</NoteLink>). Probabilistic models read the same counts as draws from a
<NoteLink to="multinomial-distribution">multinomial distribution</NoteLink>, as in multinomial
<NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink>.

Raw counts have one defect. The most frequent terms, such as "the" and "of", occur in nearly every document and
dominate every similarity, yet say nothing about what a document is about.

## Why the inverse document frequency

### As surprisal

Pick a document uniformly at random. The probability that it contains term $t$ is $\mathrm{df}_t / N$. Learning that a
document contains $t$ carries $-\log(\mathrm{df}_t / N) = \log(N/\mathrm{df}_t)$ nats of information. A term in every
document carries none; a term in one document carries $\log N$. Spärck Jones proposed weighting terms by this
specificity <Cite id="sparckjones1972" />.

### As a log-odds of relevance

The probabilistic retrieval model gives a sharper derivation <Cite id="robertson2009" />. Represent document $d$ by
binary indicators $x_t \in \set{0, 1}$ for the presence of each query term. Let $R$ be the event that $d$ is relevant to
the query, and rank documents by the log-odds $\log P(R \mid d) / P(\bar{R} \mid d)$. By Bayes' theorem this equals
$\log P(d \mid R) / P(d \mid \bar{R})$ plus a constant that does not depend on $d$.

Assume the indicators are independent given relevance, with $p_t = P(x_t = 1 \mid R)$ and $q_t = P(x_t = 1 \mid
\bar{R})$. Then

$$
\log \frac{P(d \mid R)}{P(d \mid \bar{R})} = \sum_t \Big( x_t \log \frac{p_t}{q_t} + (1 - x_t) \log \frac{1 - p_t}{1 -
q_t} \Big) = \sum_t x_t \log \frac{p_t (1 - q_t)}{q_t (1 - p_t)} + \text{const}.
$$

Each query term present in $d$ adds its own weight. Without relevance judgements, two estimates are natural. Almost all
documents are not relevant, so $q_t \approx \mathrm{df}_t / N$. Nothing is known about $p_t$, so set $p_t = \tfrac12$,
which makes $p_t / (1 - p_t) = 1$. The weight becomes

$$
\log \frac{1 - q_t}{q_t} = \log \frac{N - \mathrm{df}_t}{\mathrm{df}_t} \approx \log \frac{N}{\mathrm{df}_t} \quad
\text{when } \mathrm{df}_t \ll N.
$$

This is the IDF. Adding $\tfrac12$ to each count for smoothing gives the Robertson–Spärck Jones weight $\log\big((N -
\mathrm{df}_t + \tfrac12) / (\mathrm{df}_t + \tfrac12)\big)$ used by BM25 <Cite id="robertson2009" />.

### Term frequency

The IDF says which terms matter; the term frequency says how much document $d$ is about them. Salton and Buckley
compared many combinations and found that $\mathrm{tf} \times \mathrm{idf}$ weights with length-normalised document
vectors rank well across collections <Cite id="salton1988" />. Common variants replace $\mathrm{tf}$ by $1 +
\log\mathrm{tf}$ for $\mathrm{tf} > 0$, which damps repeated terms <Cite id="manning2008" />.

## BM25

BM25 scores document $d$ against query $q$ by

$$
\operatorname{BM25}(d, q) = \sum_{t \in q} \mathrm{idf}_t \cdot \frac{\mathrm{tf}_{t,d}\, (k_1 + 1)}{\mathrm{tf}_{t,d} +
k_1 \big(1 - b + b\, L_d / \bar{L}\big)},
$$

where $L_d$ is the length of $d$ in tokens, $\bar{L}$ is the average length, and $k_1 \ge 0$ and $b \in [0, 1]$ are
parameters <Cite id="robertson2009" />. Typical values are $k_1 \in [1.2, 2]$ and $b = 0.75$.

**Saturation.** At average length the term-frequency factor is $f(\mathrm{tf}) = \mathrm{tf}(k_1 + 1) / (\mathrm{tf} +
k_1)$. It is 0 at $\mathrm{tf} = 0$ and 1 at $\mathrm{tf} = 1$, it increases, and it approaches $k_1 + 1$ as
$\mathrm{tf} \to \infty$. The first occurrence of a term is the strongest evidence; the tenth adds little. With $k_1 =
0$ the factor is 1 for any $\mathrm{tf} > 0$, so only presence counts. As $k_1 \to \infty$ the factor tends to
$\mathrm{tf}$, so counts are used raw. The shape approximates a model in which a term's count in a document follows one
Poisson rate when the document is about the term and a lower rate when it is not <Cite id="robertson2009" />.

**Length normalisation.** A long document repeats terms because it is long, not because it is more about them. Divide
the term frequency by $B = 1 - b + b L_d / \bar{L}$, which is 1 at average length and grows with $L_d$. Substituting
$\mathrm{tf}/B$ into the saturating factor gives

$$
\frac{(\mathrm{tf}/B)(k_1 + 1)}{\mathrm{tf}/B + k_1} = \frac{\mathrm{tf}\,(k_1 + 1)}{\mathrm{tf} + k_1 B},
$$

which is the BM25 factor. With $b = 0$ length is ignored; with $b = 1$ term frequency is divided by the relative length.

<Bm25Saturation />

BM25 is a scoring formula over bag-of-words vectors. It remains a standard baseline for ranking documents against a
keyword query, and for retrieving candidates before a neural model re-ranks them.

## Worked example

Take five documents:

1. "the cat sat on the mat" (6 tokens)
2. "the dog sat on the log" (6 tokens)
3. "the cat chased the dog" (5 tokens)
4. "a dog and a cat played" (6 tokens)
5. "the bird sang" (3 tokens)

So $N = 5$ and $\bar{L} = 26/5 = 5.2$. "the" is in 4 documents, "cat" in 3 and "mat" in 1. With natural logarithms,
$\mathrm{idf}_{\text{the}} = \log(5/4) = 0.223$, $\mathrm{idf}_{\text{cat}} = \log(5/3) = 0.511$ and
$\mathrm{idf}_{\text{mat}} = \log 5 = 1.609$. The word that names document 1's topic weighs seven times as much as
"the".

For the query "cat mat", the TF-IDF scores (sums of $w_{t,d}$ over the query terms) are $0.511 + 1.609 = 2.120$ for
document 1, $0.511$ for documents 3 and 4, and 0 for documents 2 and 5.

BM25 with $k_1 = 1.2$, $b = 0.75$ and the non-negative IDF $\log\big(1 + (N - \mathrm{df}_t + \tfrac12)/(\mathrm{df}_t +
\tfrac12)\big)$ gives 1.811 for document 1, 0.548 for document 3 and 0.507 for document 4. TF-IDF tied documents 3 and
4. BM25 ranks document 3 higher, because it has 5 tokens against document 4's 6, so its single "cat" is stronger
evidence.

## Assumptions

- **Order carries no meaning.** Negation, word order and phrases are lost unless n-grams are added as extra terms.
- **Terms are independent.** Synonyms are unrelated dimensions: "car" and "automobile" never match. Dense
  <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence
  embeddings</NoteLink> address this.

## Pitfalls

- **The Robertson–Spärck Jones IDF can be negative.** $\log\big((N - \mathrm{df}_t + \tfrac12)/(\mathrm{df}_t +
  \tfrac12)\big)$ is negative when a term is in more than half the documents: "the" above gets $-1.10$. A document then
  loses score for containing a query term. Implementations add 1 inside the logarithm or clip the weight at zero.
- **Vocabulary and preprocessing change everything.** Stop-word lists, stemming and lower-casing change $\mathrm{tf}$
  and $\mathrm{df}$ and so every weight. Fit them on the collection, not on the query.
- **IDF is a collection statistic.** Scores from different collections, or from shards with different $N$ and
  $\mathrm{df}$, are not comparable.
