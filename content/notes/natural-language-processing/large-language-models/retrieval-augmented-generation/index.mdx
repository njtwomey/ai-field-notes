<Definition>
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) produces an output $y$ for an input $x$ in two stages. A **retriever** returns
the top $k$ passages $z$ from a document collection with relevance probabilities $p_\eta(z \mid x)$. A **generator**
$p_\theta(y \mid x, z)$ produces text conditioned on the input and the passages. The collection is a non-parametric
memory: it can be updated or inspected without retraining the generator <Cite id="lewis2020" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A language model stores knowledge in its weights. That knowledge is fixed at training time, cannot be traced to a
source, and is unreliable for facts that appeared rarely in training data. Knowledge-intensive tasks, such as
open-domain question answering and fact verification, need facts the model may not have and answers that can be traced
to evidence. Retrieval supplies relevant text at inference time, and the generator reads it.

How the retriever finds passages, whether by sparse term matching as in
<NoteLink to="tf-idf">TF-IDF</NoteLink> or by nearest neighbours of dense
<NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">embeddings</NoteLink>, is a separate subject and is not covered here. This note treats
the retriever as given and covers how the generator uses what it returns.

## Mechanism

### The passage as a latent variable

Lewis et al. used a dense retriever that scores a passage by the inner product of a query embedding $\qvec(x)$ and
a passage embedding $\dvec(z)$, and normalises over the retrieved top $k$ passages:
$p_\eta(z \mid x) \propto \exp\big(\dvec(z)^\top \qvec(x)\big)$. The generator was a pretrained
sequence-to-sequence model (BART) that reads the input and one passage concatenated <Cite id="lewis2020" />.

Which passage is the right one is not observed. Treating it as a latent variable and summing it out gives two models,
which differ in how often the passage may change.

**RAG-sequence.** One passage $z$ is drawn for the whole output, and every token is generated from it. The joint
probability is $p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, z)$, and marginalising over the top $k$ passages gives

$$
p_{\text{seq}}(y \mid x) \approx \sum_{z \in \operatorname{top-}k} p_\eta(z \mid x) \prod_{i=1}^{N}
p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{<i}),
$$

for an output of $N$ tokens. The approximation is the truncation of the sum to the retrieved passages.

**RAG-token.** A fresh passage $z_i$ is drawn independently from $p_\eta(\cdot \mid x)$ for each output token $i$, and
token $y_i$ is generated from it. The joint probability of the output and all $N$ passage choices is
$\prod_i p_\eta(z_i \mid x)\, p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z_i, y_{<i})$. Summing over $(z_1, \dots, z_N)$, each $z_i$ appears in
one factor only, so the sum of products becomes a product of sums:

$$
p_{\text{tok}}(y \mid x) \approx \prod_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{z \in \operatorname{top-}k} p_\eta(z \mid x)\,
p_\theta(y_i \mid x, z, y_{<i}).
$$

RAG-token can take one fact from one passage and the next fact from another. RAG-sequence must explain the whole output
with one passage, but it effectively reweights the passages by how well each explains the output: the posterior
$p(z \mid x, y) \propto p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, z)$ sharpens as the output grows.

### Worked example

Two passages are retrieved with $p_\eta(z \mid x) = (0.7, 0.3)$. The output has two tokens. Under passage 1 their
probabilities are $0.9$ and $0.8$; under passage 2, $0.2$ and $0.1$.

- **RAG-sequence.** $0.7 \times 0.9 \times 0.8 + 0.3 \times 0.2 \times 0.1 = 0.504 + 0.006 = 0.510$. The posterior of
  passage 1 given the output is $0.504 / 0.510 = 0.988$.
- **RAG-token.** $(0.7 \times 0.9 + 0.3 \times 0.2)(0.7 \times 0.8 + 0.3 \times 0.1) = 0.69 \times 0.59 = 0.407$.

RAG-token keeps mixing in passage 2 at the prior weight 0.3 at every token, even after the first token has shown that
passage 2 explains the output poorly, so it assigns this output lower probability.

### Training and decoding

Training minimises $-\log p(y \mid x)$ on input-output pairs, with no label saying which passage is relevant. Lewis et
al. kept the passage encoder and its index fixed and fine-tuned the query encoder and the generator
<Cite id="lewis2020" />. Re-encoding the collection after every update would be too expensive.

RAG-token's per-token marginal is a proper next-token distribution, so standard beam search applies. RAG-sequence's
likelihood does not factor over tokens. Lewis et al. ran beam search under each passage separately, pooled the
hypotheses, and scored each by the marginal $p_{\text{seq}}$. Scoring a hypothesis under passages whose beams did not
produce it needs extra forward passes; a faster approximation sets those terms to zero <Cite id="lewis2020" />.

<Derivation title="What gradient the retriever receives">
For RAG-sequence, write $p(y \mid x) = \sum_z p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, z)$. Differentiating the log with
respect to the retriever's parameters $\eta$,

$$
\nabla_\eta \log p(y \mid x) = \sum_z \frac{p_\theta(y \mid x, z)\, \nabla_\eta p_\eta(z \mid x)}{p(y \mid x)} = \sum_z
\frac{p_\eta(z \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, z)}{p(y \mid x)} \nabla_\eta \log p_\eta(z \mid x) = \sum_z p(z \mid x, y)\,
\nabla_\eta \log p_\eta(z \mid x).
$$

The gradient raises the log-probability of each passage in proportion to its posterior given the correct output. Passages
that help the generator produce $y$ are pulled up, and the retriever learns relevance from the generator's success
without relevance labels. This is the same posterior weighting as the E-step of
<NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation-maximisation</NoteLink>.
</Derivation>

### Retrieval in the prompt of a large language model

With a large decoder-only model, the usual form of RAG places all $k$ retrieved passages in the prompt, followed by the
question, and generates once: $p(y \mid x, z_1, \dots, z_k)$. There is no explicit marginalisation. Attention reads all
passages in one forward pass and can combine facts from several, as RAG-token can. The model needs no fine-tuning, and
the retriever and generator can be changed independently. Instructions typically ask the model to answer only from the
passages and to cite them. See <NoteLink to="prompting">prompting</NoteLink>.

Long contexts are not read uniformly. Liu et al. placed the passage containing an answer at different positions among
distractor passages. Accuracy was highest when the relevant passage came first or last, and dropped when it was in the
middle <Cite id="liu2024b" />. The order in which retrieved passages are placed therefore matters.

## Cost

Retrieval adds a query-encoding step and an index lookup per input. The generator's input grows by $k$ passages. For
RAG-sequence and RAG-token, the generator runs once per passage, $k$ times per input. For in-prompt RAG, the prompt
grows by $k$ passage lengths, and attention cost grows with it.

## Trade-offs

- **Updatable knowledge.** Replacing the collection updates the system's facts without retraining. Lewis et al. swapped
  a 2016 Wikipedia index for a 2018 one and the model's answers to questions about world leaders changed accordingly
  <Cite id="lewis2020" />.
- **Provenance.** Generated claims can be traced to the passages that supported them, which makes them checkable.
- **Retrieval errors propagate.** If the retriever misses the relevant passage, the generator either answers from its
  weights or says it cannot answer. The earlier retriever-reader pipeline for open-domain QA had the same dependence
  <Cite id="chen2017" />.
- **Context can conflict with the weights.** A retrieved passage can contradict what the model learned in training.
  Which source the model follows depends on the model and the prompt, and neither choice is right in every case.
