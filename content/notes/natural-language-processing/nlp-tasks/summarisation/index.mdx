<Definition>
Summarisation maps a source document $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ of $n$ tokens to a summary $y$ that is much shorter and
keeps the source's most important content. **Extractive** summarisation builds $y$ from sentences or phrases copied
from $x$. **Abstractive** summarisation generates $y$ token by token and may use words and sentences that do not appear
in $x$ <Cite id="jurafsky2025" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Extraction is safe but rigid: every sentence it outputs is true to the source, but it cannot compress, merge or
paraphrase. Generation can write a concise summary in its own words, but it can also write things the source does not
say. Most of the history of summarisation is a trade between those two properties.

## Extractive summarisation

An extractive system scores each source sentence and selects a subset that fits a length budget. Scores can come from
position, word frequency, similarity to the document centroid, or a trained classifier that labels each sentence as in
or out of the summary. Selection penalises redundancy, so that two near-identical sentences are not both chosen. On news,
where writers put the key facts first, taking the first three sentences (the **lead-3** baseline) is hard to beat.

## Abstractive summarisation

Abstractive summarisation is a <NoteLink to="sequence-to-sequence">sequence-to-sequence</NoteLink> problem. An encoder
reads $x$ and an attentional decoder generates $y$ with $p(y \mid x) = \prod_t p(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x)$. Two failure
modes of the plain attentional model motivated the pointer-generator network <Cite id="see2017" />. The plain attentional model reproduced rare
source words, such as names and numbers, inaccurately, because they are out of its vocabulary or have poorly trained
embeddings. It also repeated itself.

### The pointer-generator

At decoder step $t$, <NoteLink to="bahdanau-attention">attention</NoteLink> gives weights $a^t_i \ge 0$ over source
positions $i$, with $\sum_i a^t_i = 1$, and a vocabulary distribution $P_{\text{vocab}}(w)$. The model also computes a
**generation probability** $p_{\text{gen}} \in [0, 1]$ from the context vector, the decoder state and the decoder input,
through a sigmoid. It treats the next word as coming from one of two sources: generate from the vocabulary with
probability $p_{\text{gen}}$, or copy the word at a source position drawn from the attention distribution with
probability $1 - p_{\text{gen}}$. Marginalising over the two sources and over copy positions gives

$$
P(w) = p_{\text{gen}}\, P_{\text{vocab}}(w) + (1 - p_{\text{gen}}) \sum_{i \,:\, x_i = w} a^t_i.
$$

The distribution is over the union of the fixed vocabulary and the words of this source document. A source word
outside the vocabulary has $P_{\text{vocab}}(w) = 0$ and can still be produced by copying <Cite id="see2017" />.

**Coverage.** The coverage vector $c^t_i = \sum_{t' < t} a^{t'}_i$ records how much attention each source position has
received so far. It is fed into the attention computation, and a coverage loss $\sum_i \min(a^t_i, c^t_i)$ penalises
attending again to positions already covered. The loss is bounded by 1 at each step and discourages repetition
<Cite id="see2017" />.

### Worked example

At one decoder step $p_{\text{gen}} = 0.6$. The source mentions the surname "Okonkwo" at two positions, which receive
attention 0.3 and 0.1. "Okonkwo" is not in the vocabulary, so its probability is
$P = 0.6 \times 0 + 0.4 \times (0.3 + 0.1) = 0.16$. The word "said" does not appear in the source and has
$P_{\text{vocab}} = 0.05$, so $P = 0.6 \times 0.05 = 0.03$.

### Pretrained models and prompting

Pretrained <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> encoder-decoders fine-tuned on summarisation pairs replaced task-specific architectures. <Gloss name="t5" />
casts every task as text to text and summarises when its input is prefixed with "summarize:" <Cite id="raffel2020" />.
Large <NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">decoder-only language models</NoteLink> summarise from an instruction, with no summarisation-specific training. <Gloss name="gpt-2" />
produced summaries when the text "TL;<Gloss name="doubly-robust" />:" was appended to an article <Cite id="radford2019" />. See
<NoteLink to="prompting">prompting</NoteLink>.

## Evaluation

<NoteLink to="rouge">ROUGE</NoteLink> measures $n$-gram and longest-common-subsequence overlap between a system summary
and reference summaries, with recall as the primary quantity <Cite id="lin2004" />. ROUGE rewards content selection but
does not check whether the summary's claims are supported by the source. <NoteLink to="bertscore">BERTScore</NoteLink>
matches tokens by contextual embeddings, which tolerates paraphrase but also does not check support.

## Faithfulness

A summary is **faithful** if every claim in it is supported by the source. Maynez et al. had annotators label summaries
from several neural abstractive systems on the XSum dataset and found substantial amounts of hallucinated content in
the output of every system <Cite id="maynez2020" />. They distinguished **intrinsic** hallucinations, which misrepresent
information in the source, from **extrinsic** hallucinations, which add information that cannot be verified from the
source. Some extrinsic content was factually true, but it was still unfaithful. Pretrained models were more faithful as
well as better by ROUGE, and a textual entailment score correlated better with human faithfulness judgments than ROUGE
did <Cite id="maynez2020" />. Entailment-based checking is described in
<NoteLink to="natural-language-inference">natural language inference</NoteLink>, and the general problem in
<NoteLink to="hallucination-and-faithfulness">hallucination and faithfulness</NoteLink>.

A cause lies in the training data. XSum's reference summaries are single sentences written by journalists, and they
often contain information that is not in the article body. A model trained to reproduce such references learns to
add unsupported content <Cite id="maynez2020" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Reference summaries are one of many good summaries.** Low ROUGE does not imply a bad summary, and a high score does
  not imply a faithful one.
- **<Gloss name="position-bias">Position bias</Gloss>.** On news, models learn to summarise the lead. The same model on documents whose key content comes
  late, such as scientific papers or meeting transcripts, misses it.
- **Long inputs.** Transformer attention cost grows quadratically with length, so long documents are truncated or split,
  and truncated content cannot appear in the summary.
