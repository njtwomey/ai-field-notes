<Definition>
Text classification maps a document $d$, a sequence of tokens, to one label $y$ from a finite set $\mathcal{Y}$ of $K$
classes. A classifier outputs scores or probabilities $p(y \mid d)$ for every $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ and predicts the
highest. Sentiment analysis ($\mathcal{Y} = \{\text{positive}, \text{negative}\}$), topic labelling, spam filtering and
intent detection are text classification tasks <Cite id="jurafsky2025" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A classifier needs a fixed-length representation of a variable-length text. The three families of methods differ in
where that representation comes from. Bag-of-words models count words. Fine-tuned encoders learn a vector for the whole
text from a pretrained transformer. Large language models read the text and a description of the labels in a prompt,
and the label is whatever the model predicts next.

## Bag-of-words models

A <NoteLink to="tf-idf">bag-of-words</NoteLink> representation replaces $d$ by the vector
$\xvec \in \reals^{V}$ of counts $n_w$ of each vocabulary word $w$, for a vocabulary of $V$ words. Word order is lost.
For topic and sentiment, which words appear carries most of the signal.

**Multinomial naive Bayes.** A generative model treats the document's tokens as independent draws from a class-specific
distribution $\thetavec_y$ over the vocabulary, where $\theta_{y,w}$ is the probability that a token of class $y$ is
$w$ <Cite id="mccallum1998" />. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>,

$$
p(y \mid d) \propto p(y) \prod_{w} \theta_{y,w}^{n_w}.
$$

The maximum-likelihood estimate of $\theta_{y,w}$ is the fraction of class-$y$ tokens that are $w$. A word never seen
with class $y$ would get probability zero and veto that class, so the estimate is smoothed. With add-one (Laplace)
smoothing,

$$
\hat\theta_{y,w} = \frac{N_{y,w} + 1}{N_y + V},
$$

where $N_{y,w}$ counts occurrences of $w$ in training documents of class $y$ and $N_y = \sum_w N_{y,w}$. The
<NoteLink to="naive-bayes">naive Bayes</NoteLink> note covers the model in general.

**The decision is linear in the counts.** For two classes, take logarithms of the ratio of posteriors:

$$
\log \frac{p(y = 1 \mid d)}{p(y = 0 \mid d)} = \underbrace{\log \frac{p(y=1)}{p(y=0)}}_{b} +
\sum_{w} n_w \underbrace{\log \frac{\theta_{1,w}}{\theta_{0,w}}}_{\beta_w} = b + \betavec^\top \xvec.
$$

Naive Bayes is therefore a linear classifier in $\xvec$. <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">Logistic regression</NoteLink>
has the same form, $p(y = 1 \mid d) = \sigma(b + \betavec^\top \xvec)$ with $\sigma$ the logistic function.
The two models differ in how they fit $b$ and $\betavec$. Naive Bayes sets each $\beta_w$ from counts of $w$
alone, in one pass over the data. Logistic regression maximises the conditional likelihood of the labels, which
adjusts correlated words jointly. With $K > 2$ classes the same argument gives $K$ linear scores and a softmax, which is
<NoteLink to="multinomial-logistic-regression">multinomial logistic regression</NoteLink>. TF-IDF weights, word bigrams
and binary presence features often improve both models.

### Worked example

Two training documents are positive, "great fun film" and "great acting". Two are negative, "boring film" and "dull
boring plot". The vocabulary has $V = 7$ words, each class has $N_y = 5$ tokens, and the priors are $1/2$. Smoothed
estimates have denominator $5 + 7 = 12$:

| word | $\hat\theta_{+,w}$ | $\hat\theta_{-,w}$ | $\beta_w = \log(\hat\theta_{+,w} / \hat\theta_{-,w})$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| great | $3/12$ | $1/12$ | $\log 3$ |
| fun | $2/12$ | $1/12$ | $\log 2$ |
| boring | $1/12$ | $3/12$ | $-\log 3$ |
| film | $2/12$ | $2/12$ | $0$ |

The test document "great fun but boring film" contains "but", which is not in the vocabulary and is dropped. The log
odds are $0 + \log 3 + \log 2 - \log 3 + 0 = \log 2$, so the posterior odds are $2 : 1$ and
$p(+ \mid d) = 2/3$. "great" and "boring" cancel, "film" is neutral, and "fun" decides the label.

## Fine-tuned encoders

A pretrained encoder such as BERT maps the token sequence to one vector per token. BERT prepends a special `[CLS]`
token, and its final hidden state $\hvec \in \reals^{H}$ serves as the representation of the whole text
<Cite id="devlin2019" />. A classification layer gives

$$
p(y \mid d) = \operatorname{softmax}(\Wmat \hvec + \bvec)_y, \qquad \Wmat \in \reals^{K \times H},
$$

and all parameters, the encoder's included, are trained on labelled examples by cross-entropy. The encoder was
pretrained by <NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">masked language modelling</NoteLink> on unlabelled text, so it
already represents word order, negation and context. Fine-tuning needs far fewer labelled examples than training from
scratch; see <NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">transfer learning and fine-tuning</NoteLink>. Mean-pooled
<NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence embeddings</NoteLink> with a linear classifier on top are a cheaper
alternative that leaves the encoder frozen.

## Zero-shot and few-shot classification with language models

A generative language model can classify with no task-specific training. A prompt states the task and the text, and
each label $y$ is written as a word or phrase $v(y)$, its **verbaliser**, such as "positive" and "negative". The model
scores each label by the probability of its verbaliser as the continuation,

$$
\hat y = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \; \log p_{\text{LM}}\big(v(y) \mid \text{prompt}(d)\big),
$$

summing token log-probabilities when a verbaliser has several tokens. Adding a few labelled examples to the prompt gives
few-shot classification <Cite id="brown2020" />; see <NoteLink to="prompting">prompting</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="in-context-learning">in-context learning</NoteLink>. A model trained for
<NoteLink to="natural-language-inference">natural language inference</NoteLink> gives another zero-shot classifier: it
scores how strongly the text entails a hypothesis such as "This text is about sport" for each label
<Cite id="yin2019" />.

## Assumptions

- **One label per document.** Multi-label tasks, where a document can have several topics, use $K$ independent binary
  classifiers with sigmoid outputs instead of one softmax.
- **Training and test texts come from the same distribution.** A sentiment classifier trained on film reviews degrades
  on product reviews, because the vocabulary that signals sentiment changes.
- **Naive Bayes assumes tokens are independent given the class.** Repeated and correlated words are counted as separate
  evidence, which pushes its probabilities towards 0 and 1.

## Pitfalls

- **Imbalanced classes.** Accuracy rewards predicting the majority class. Report per-class precision and recall or
  macro-averaged F1 <Cite id="manning2008" />.
- **Probabilities are not calibrated by default.** Naive Bayes is overconfident, and fine-tuned networks often are too.
  Recalibrate before thresholding; see <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">classifier calibration</NoteLink>.
- **Verbaliser choice changes zero-shot results.** "good" and "positive" have different prior probabilities under the
  model, which biases the argmax toward one label.
- **Shortcuts.** A model can learn cues that correlate with the label in the training set, such as a product name or a
  user handle, instead of the content.
