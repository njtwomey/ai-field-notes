<Definition>
Natural language inference (NLI) takes a **premise** $p$ and a **hypothesis** $h$, both sentences, and assigns one of
three labels. **Entailment**: a typical human reading $p$ would infer that $h$ is most likely true. **Contradiction**:
they would infer that $h$ is most likely false. **Neutral**: neither follows <Cite id="dagan2006" />
<Cite id="bowman2015" />. The relation is judged by ordinary reading, not by strict logical proof.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Deciding whether one sentence follows from another requires lexical knowledge (a dog is an animal), handling of
negation and quantifiers, and world knowledge about what situations are compatible. NLI packs these abilities into one
classification task with a simple input and output, which made it a standard test of sentence understanding.

## Datasets

The PASCAL Recognising Textual Entailment (RTE) challenges posed the two-way version, entailment or not, with a few
hundred to a few thousand examples per round <Cite id="dagan2006" />. The Stanford NLI corpus (SNLI) scaled the task to
570,000 human-written pairs <Cite id="bowman2015" />. Crowd workers were shown an image caption as the premise and wrote
three hypotheses: one definitely true, one that might be true and one definitely false given the caption. MultiNLI used
the same protocol with premises from ten genres of written and spoken English, 433,000 pairs in all
<Cite id="williams2018" />. Its test data has a **matched** section, from genres seen in training, and a **mismatched**
section, from genres not seen, to measure cross-genre generalisation.

| premise | hypothesis | label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A man is playing a guitar on stage. | A man is performing music. | entailment |
| A man is playing a guitar on stage. | The man is asleep in bed. | contradiction |
| A man is playing a guitar on stage. | The man is a famous musician. | neutral |

## Models

A **cross-encoder** reads both sentences together. BERT takes `[CLS]` $p$ `[SEP]` $h$ `[SEP]` and applies a softmax
layer with three outputs to the final `[CLS]` vector <Cite id="devlin2019" />. Self-attention compares every token of
$p$ with every token of $h$ in every layer. This is the standard architecture and the most accurate.

A **sentence encoder** maps $p$ and $h$ to vectors $\uvec$ and $\vvec$ separately and classifies a combination such as
$[\uvec; \vvec; \abs{\uvec - \vvec}; \uvec \odot \vvec]$, where $[\cdot\,;\cdot]$ is concatenation and $\odot$ the
elementwise product. It is less accurate, but each sentence is encoded once. Training sentence encoders on NLI data
was an early route to general-purpose <NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">sentence embeddings</NoteLink>.

## Annotation artefacts

Crowd workers writing a contradiction often negate the premise, and workers writing an entailment often generalise it.
The hypothesis alone therefore carries information about the label. A text classifier that sees only the hypothesis
labelled about 67% of SNLI and 53% of MultiNLI test pairs correctly, against 33% for chance on three balanced classes
<Cite id="gururangan2018" />. Words such as "nobody", "sleeping" and "never" signalled contradiction, and generic words
such as "animal" and "outdoors" signalled entailment. A model can exploit these cues without comparing the two
sentences, so benchmark accuracy overstates inference ability. A **hypothesis-only baseline** is the standard check: a
model's gain over it measures what the model gets from reading the premise.

## Uses

**Zero-shot classification.** To assign text $d$ one of $K$ labels, write a hypothesis for each label, such as "This
text is about $\langle$label$\rangle$", and run an NLI model with $d$ as premise. The label whose hypothesis has the
highest entailment probability wins <Cite id="yin2019" />. No training data for the target labels is needed, only
descriptive label names.

**Faithfulness checking.** A generated sentence is supported by a source if the source entails it. Maynez et al. found
that an entailment score correlated better with human judgments of summary faithfulness than
<NoteLink to="rouge">ROUGE</NoteLink> did <Cite id="maynez2020" />. See
<NoteLink to="hallucination-and-faithfulness">hallucination and faithfulness</NoteLink>.

### Worked example

A news sentence, "The striker scored twice in the second half", is to be labelled sport, politics or technology. An NLI
model gives these logits (entailment, neutral, contradiction) for the three hypotheses "This text is about
$\langle$label$\rangle$":

| label | logits | $P(\text{entailment})$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sport | $(2.1, 0.3, -1.5)$ | 0.838 |
| politics | $(-0.4, 1.0, 0.9)$ | 0.115 |
| technology | $(0.8, 1.1, -0.2)$ | 0.368 |

Each row's entailment probability is the softmax of its logits at the first position. The prediction is sport. The
three entailment probabilities sum to 1.321, not 1, because each comes from a separate three-way softmax. Dividing by
the sum gives a distribution over labels, $(0.635, 0.087, 0.279)$.

## Pitfalls

- **Label noise at the boundary.** Neutral and entailment are often confused by annotators, because "most likely true"
  is a judgment. Inter-annotator agreement bounds achievable accuracy.
- **Artefacts transfer poorly.** A model that learned negation words as contradiction cues fails on pairs where the
  hypothesis has negation but is entailed.
- **Zero-shot NLI depends on the hypothesis template.** "This text is about sport" and "This is a sports article" can
  give different predictions.
