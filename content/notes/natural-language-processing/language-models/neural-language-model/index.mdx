<Definition>
A neural <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> predicts the next word $w_t$ from its context with a neural
network. Each word $w$ in a vocabulary of size $V$ has a learned embedding $\Cmat_w \in \reals^m$. The network maps the
embeddings of the context to a vector of scores $\yvec \in \reals^V$, and a softmax turns the scores into the
distribution $p(w_t = j \mid
\text{context}) = e^{y_j} / \sum_{k} e^{y_k}$. The embeddings and the network are trained together to minimise the
cross-entropy of the next word <Cite id="bengio2003" />.
</Definition>

## The problem with counts

An <NoteLink to="n-gram-language-model">n-gram model</NoteLink> treats each context as a separate row of a table. It
learns nothing about "the dog was running in a room" from "the cat was walking in the bedroom", because "dog" and "cat"
are unrelated symbols to it. The number of contexts grows as $V^{n-1}$, so almost every long context is unseen, and
smoothing can only back off to shorter contexts. Bengio et al. called this the
<NoteLink to="curse-of-dimensionality-for-neighbours">curse of dimensionality</NoteLink> of language modelling
<Cite id="bengio2003" />.

A neural model replaces the table with a function of word vectors. If "dog" and "cat" have nearby embeddings, the
network computes nearby predictions for contexts that contain them, and a training sentence about one moves the
predictions for the other.

## The feed-forward model

Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent and Jauvin predict $w_t$ from the previous $n - 1$ words <Cite id="bengio2003" />.

1. **Look up and concatenate.** $\xvec = (\Cmat_{w_{t-1}}, \dots, \Cmat_{w_{t-n+1}}) \in \reals^{(n-1)m}$.
2. **<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">Hidden layer</NoteLink>.** $\hvec = \tanh(\dvec + \Hmat \xvec)$ with
   $\Hmat \in \reals^{h \times (n-1)m}$.
3. **Scores.** $\yvec = \bvec + \Umat \hvec + \Wmat \xvec$, with $\Umat \in \reals^{V \times h}$ and an optional direct
   connection $\Wmat \in \reals^{V \times (n-1)m}$ from the embeddings to the output.
4. **Softmax.** $p(w_t = j \mid w_{t-n+1:t-1}) = \operatorname{softmax}(\yvec)_j$.

**Training.** The loss for one position is $-\log p(w_t \mid \cdot) = -y_{w_t} + \log \sum_k e^{y_k}$. Its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with respect to the scores is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \yvec}\big(-\log \operatorname{softmax}(\yvec)_{w_t}\big) = \pvec - \evec_{w_t},
$$

the predicted distribution minus the one-hot vector of the observed word. Backpropagation carries this error to
$\Umat$, $\Hmat$ and the embeddings of the context words. An embedding row $\Cmat_w$ receives an update every time $w$
appears in any context, so it is shaped by all of them. This is how the model shares evidence between words that occur
in similar contexts.

**Results.** On the Brown corpus the best network had a test perplexity about 24% lower than the best smoothed n-gram
model, and about 8% lower on the larger <Gloss name="ap" /> News corpus <Cite id="bengio2003" />. The learned embeddings
were an early form of the <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink> later trained on their own.

## Recurrent language models

A fixed window still forgets everything more than $n - 1$ words back. A recurrent language model replaces the window
by a hidden state $\svec_t \in \reals^H$ that summarises the whole prefix <Cite id="mikolov2010" />:

$$
\svec_t = \sigma(\Amat \xvec_t + \Rmat \svec_{t-1}), \qquad p(w_{t+1} \mid w_{1:t}) = \operatorname{softmax}(\Vmat
\svec_t),
$$

where $\xvec_t$ is the one-hot vector (equivalently, the embedding) of the current word $w_t$, $\Amat$, $\Rmat$ and
$\Vmat$ are the input, recurrent and output weights, and $\sigma$ is the logistic function. The same weights are used at
every position. Training unrolls the recurrence over the sequence and applies
<NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">backpropagation through time</NoteLink>. Mikolov et al. reported that a
mixture of several recurrent models reduced perplexity by about 50% relative to a state-of-the-art backoff n-gram model
<Cite id="mikolov2010" />. Simple recurrent networks struggle to carry information over long gaps because of vanishing
gradients, and <NoteLink to="long-short-term-memory">long short-term memory (LSTM)</NoteLink> language models became the
standard neural language model until transformers replaced them.

## Worked example

Count the parameters of a feed-forward model with $V = 10{,}000$, embedding size $m = 100$, $n = 4$ (three context
words), $h = 500$ hidden units and no direct connection.

| Part | Shape | Parameters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Embeddings $\Cmat$ | $10{,}000 \times 100$ | 1,000,000 |
| Hidden weights $\Hmat$ | $500 \times 300$ | 150,000 |
| Hidden bias $\dvec$ | $500$ | 500 |
| Output weights $\Umat$ | $10{,}000 \times 500$ | 5,000,000 |
| Output bias $\bvec$ | $10{,}000$ | 10,000 |
| **Total** | | **6,160,500** |

A 4-gram table over the same vocabulary has $10^{12}$ possible contexts, each with its own distribution over $10^4$
words. The network grows linearly with the window: one more context word adds $m h = 50{,}000$ weights to $\Hmat$. The
output layer holds 81% of the parameters, and computing it costs $V h = 5 \times 10^6$ multiply-adds per predicted word.

## Cost of the softmax

Every prediction normalises over the whole vocabulary, which costs $O(V h)$ per word in training and in evaluation.
Factorising the softmax into word classes or a tree, or estimating its gradient from a sample of words, reduces the cost
<Cite id="jurafsky2025" />. Subword <NoteLink to="tokenisation">tokenisation</NoteLink> also caps $V$ at tens of
thousands of tokens while removing unknown words.

## Assumptions

- **A fixed vocabulary.** Words not seen in training map to an unknown-word token.
- **The recurrence can summarise the prefix.** A fixed-size state must hold everything the model needs about the past,
  which limits how much long-range information survives.

## Pitfalls

- **Perplexities need the same vocabulary and tokenisation** to be compared with n-gram results.
- **Sequential computation.** A recurrent model computes $\svec_t$ after $\svec_{t-1}$, so training cannot be
  parallelised over positions. The <NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">transformer language
  model</NoteLink> removes this constraint.
