<Definition>
An autoregressive transformer language model factorises the probability of a token sequence $x_1, \dots, x_T$ by the
chain rule, $p_{\thetavec}(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_{\thetavec}(x_t \mid x_{<t})$, and computes every conditional with
one decoder-only <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink>. A causal mask lets position $t$ attend only to
positions $1, \dots, t$. The output at position $t$ passes through a linear layer and a softmax over the vocabulary to
give $p_{\thetavec}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{\le t})$ <Cite id="radford2018" />.
</Definition>

## The training objective

Given a corpus of token sequences, the parameters $\thetavec$ minimise the average negative log-likelihood of each token
given the tokens before it:

$$
\Lcal(\thetavec) = -\frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_{\thetavec}(x_t \mid x_{<t}).
$$

This is the cross-entropy between the data and the model, so minimising it is maximum likelihood, and its exponential
is the perplexity (see <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy and perplexity</NoteLink>).

**One pass gives every prediction.** The <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink> sets every attention score
from position $t$ to a later position to $-\infty$. The output at position $t$ therefore depends only on $x_{\le t}$,
and the loss terms for all $T$ positions come out of one forward pass over the sequence. A recurrent
<NoteLink to="neural-language-model">neural language model</NoteLink> needs $T$ sequential steps for the same work.

**Teacher forcing.** In training, the prediction at position $t$ is conditioned on the true prefix $x_{<t}$ from the
data, never on the model's own earlier predictions. At generation time the prefix is the model's own output, sampled one
token at a time (see <NoteLink to="decoding-strategies">decoding strategies</NoteLink>). The model is never trained on
its own mistakes, so an early error puts it in a state that it did not see in training. This mismatch is called
exposure bias.

## From GPT to GPT-3

- **GPT** pretrained a 12-layer decoder on the BooksCorpus with the objective above, then fine-tuned the whole network
  with a small task head on each labelled task <Cite id="radford2018" />.
- **GPT-2** scaled the recipe to 1.5 billion parameters and a 40 GB web corpus, WebText. It performed several tasks,
  such as reading comprehension and translation, without fine-tuning, by conditioning on a prompt that described the
  task <Cite id="radford2019" />.
- **GPT-3** had 175 billion parameters, trained on 300 billion tokens. Given a few examples of a task in its context
  window, it performed the task without any gradient update. This in-context learning improved with model size
  <Cite id="brown2020" />.

The architecture changed little between the three. The scale of parameters, data and compute changed by orders of
magnitude. The comparison <NoteLink to="gpt-2-vs-deepseek-v3">GPT-2 and DeepSeek-V3</NoteLink> shows what changed in
later models.

## Training compute

A transformer with $N$ parameters (excluding embeddings) spends about $2N$ floating-point operations per token in the
forward pass: each parameter takes part in one multiply and one add. The backward pass costs about twice the forward
pass, since it computes gradients with respect to both the activations and the weights. Training on $D$ tokens therefore
costs

$$
C \approx 6 N D \text{ FLOPs}.
$$

Attention adds a term that grows with the context length. It is small when the model width is large compared with the
context <Cite id="kaplan2020" />.

## Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. trained models over several orders of magnitude of size and data and found that the test loss follows
power laws. When the other factors do not limit it, the loss falls as $L(N) = (N_c / N)^{\alpha_N}$ with $\alpha_N
\approx 0.076$ in the number of parameters, and as $L(D) = (D_c/D)^{\alpha_D}$ with $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$ in the
number of tokens. Width and depth mattered little at a fixed $N$. For a compute-efficient allocation they found $N
\propto C^{0.73}$: most extra compute should go into a larger model, trained on comparatively little data and stopped
before convergence <Cite id="kaplan2020" />.

### The Chinchilla allocation

Hoffmann et al. trained over 400 models, from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters on 5 to 500 billion tokens, with
the learning-rate schedule matched to each run's length <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />. One of their three analyses fits

$$
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},
$$

where $E$ is the loss of an ideal model on this data, the second term is the shortfall of a finite model and the third
the shortfall of finite data. Their fitted values are $E = 1.69$, $A = 406.4$, $B = 410.7$, $\alpha = 0.34$ and $\beta
= 0.28$.

**Deriving the optimum.** Fix the compute $C$ and substitute $D = C / (6N)$. The loss as a function of $N$ alone is

$$
L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B \Big(\frac{6N}{C}\Big)^{\beta}.
$$

The first term falls with $N$ and the second rises with it, because a larger model affords fewer tokens. Set the
derivative to zero:

$$
-\alpha A N^{-\alpha - 1} + \beta B \Big(\frac{6}{C}\Big)^{\beta} N^{\beta - 1} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
N^{\alpha + \beta} = \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B} \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\beta}.
$$

So

$$
N_{\text{opt}}(C) = G \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{a}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}}(C) = G^{-1} \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{b}, \qquad
G = \Big(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Big)^{1/(\alpha + \beta)},
$$

with $a = \beta / (\alpha + \beta)$ and $b = \alpha / (\alpha + \beta)$. The exponents sum to one. The fitted values give
$a = 0.45$ and $b = 0.55$. Hoffmann et al.'s other two analyses, which read the optimum directly off families of
training runs, gave $a \approx b \approx 0.5$: parameters and tokens should grow in equal proportion, about 20 tokens
per parameter <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />. Kaplan's allocation, $N \propto C^{0.73}$, instead put most compute into
parameters. Hoffmann et al. attributed the difference mainly to Kaplan's use of one learning-rate schedule for all
training lengths, which penalised the shorter runs of small models.

**The test.** Gopher had 280 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens. Chinchilla used about the same compute
for 70 billion parameters and 1.4 trillion tokens. Chinchilla outperformed Gopher on almost every evaluation and
reached 67.5% on the MMLU benchmark, more than 7 points above Gopher <Cite id="hoffmann2022" />. A smaller model is
also cheaper to fine-tune and to serve.

### Worked example

Take Chinchilla's budget, $C = 6 \cdot 70 \times 10^9 \cdot 1.4 \times 10^{12} = 5.88 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs. The fitted
loss is

$$
L(70\text{B}, 1.4\text{T}) = 1.69 + \frac{406.4}{(7 \times 10^{10})^{0.34}} + \frac{410.7}{(1.4 \times 10^{12})^{0.28}} =
1.937,
$$

and for Gopher, with $C = 5.04 \times 10^{23}$, $L(280\text{B}, 300\text{B}) = 1.993$. The formula ranks Chinchilla
above Gopher at less than 1.2 times Gopher's compute. At $C = 5.76 \times 10^{23}$ the closed form gives $N_{\text{opt}}
= 3.2 \times 10^{10}$ and $D_{\text{opt}} = 3.0 \times 10^{12}$, about 93 tokens per parameter. The parametric fit and the
direct analyses disagree by a factor of about two in model size at this budget. A later replication refitted the
parametric model to data reconstructed from the paper and obtained estimates consistent with the direct analyses
<Cite id="besiroglu2024" />.

## Assumptions

- **Loss is the target.** Scaling laws predict pretraining cross-entropy. Downstream accuracy is a noisier and sometimes
  abrupt function of the loss.
- **Data is not repeated.** The fits assume each token is seen about once. Repeating a limited corpus for many epochs
  gives less than the fit predicts.
- **Training compute only.** The allocation ignores inference. A model that will serve many requests is often trained
  smaller and on more tokens than compute-optimal, because it is cheaper to run.

## Pitfalls

- **Extrapolation is sensitive to the constants.** Small changes in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ move $N_{\text{opt}}$ by large
  factors at budgets far beyond the fitted range.
- **Tokenisation and data define the loss.** Losses fitted on one tokeniser and corpus do not transfer to another, and
  "tokens" is not a unit of information.
- **Exposure bias is not fixed by more data.** Generated text can drift or repeat, because the model conditions on its
  own outputs; decoding and later fine-tuning address this.
