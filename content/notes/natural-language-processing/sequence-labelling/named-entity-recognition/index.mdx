<Definition>
Named-entity recognition (NER) takes a tokenised sentence and returns a set of typed spans $(i, j, c)$: tokens $i$ to $j$
form a name of type $c$. The CoNLL-2003 shared task, the standard English and German benchmark, uses four types:
person (PER), location (LOC), organisation (ORG) and miscellaneous (MISC) <Cite id="tjongkimsang2003" />. NER is solved
as <NoteLink to="sequence-labelling">sequence labelling</NoteLink> by encoding the spans as BIO or BIOES tags.
</Definition>

## What makes it hard

- **Ambiguity of type.** "Washington" is a person, a city, a state or a government depending on context.
- **Ambiguity of boundary.** "Bank of America Stadium" is one location, not an organisation followed by a word.
- **Unseen names.** Most names in a test set are rare or absent from the training data, so a model must recognise a name
  from its form and context: capitalisation, suffixes such as "-ville", or a preceding "Mr.".
- **Little labelled data.** CoNLL-2003 English has about 14,000 training sentences, far too few to learn word meanings
  from scratch.

## Feature-based CRFs

The classical approach is a linear-chain <NoteLink to="conditional-random-field">conditional random field</NoteLink>
over BIO tags, with hand-written feature templates <Cite id="jurafsky2025" />:

- the word, lower-cased, and the words in a window around it;
- the word shape, which maps upper-case letters to `X`, lower-case to `x` and digits to `d`, so "McDonald" becomes
  `XxXxxxxx`, often shortened to `XxXx`;
- prefixes and suffixes of a few characters;
- whether the word appears in a gazetteer, a list of known names;
- the part-of-speech and chunk tags of the word.

Each template produces thousands of binary features, and the CRF learns a weight for each feature and tag. The
transition features learn that, for example, `I-PER` follows only `B-PER` or `I-PER`. The accuracy of such systems rests
mostly on the features and gazetteers, which take expert effort and do not transfer across languages or domains.

## BiLSTM-CRF

Lample et al. replaced the feature templates with learned representations <Cite id="lample2016" />. Each word is
represented by a pretrained word embedding concatenated with a character-level embedding, computed by a bidirectional
LSTM over the word's characters, which captures shape and affixes. A bidirectional LSTM over the words gives a
context-dependent vector per token, and a CRF layer on top scores tag transitions. The model reached 90.94 F1 on
CoNLL-2003 English without gazetteers or language-specific features, and the BIOES scheme worked slightly better than
BIO. The architecture and its training are in <NoteLink to="bidirectional-lstm-conditional-random-field">BiLSTM-CRF</NoteLink>.

## Transformer token classification

A pretrained transformer encoder, such as BERT, is fine-tuned with a linear layer and softmax over tags at each token
<Cite id="devlin2019" />. The encoder's self-attention already conditions every token on the whole sentence, so a CRF
layer is often omitted. A word split into several subword tokens still has one tag. The usual convention, used by BERT,
predicts the tag at the first subword and ignores the other subwords in the loss and at decoding.

Fine-tuned BERT-large reached 92.8 F1 on the CoNLL-2003 English test set <Cite id="devlin2019" />.

## Evaluation: span-level F1

The CoNLL evaluation counts whole spans <Cite id="tjongkimsang2003" />. A predicted span is correct only if its start,
end and type all equal those of a gold span. With $\text{tp}$ correct predicted spans, $\hat{S}$ predicted spans and $S$
gold spans,

$$
P = \frac{\text{tp}}{\hat{S}}, \qquad R = \frac{\text{tp}}{S}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R},
$$

pooled over all spans in the test set (micro-averaged). See <NoteLink to="precision-recall-and-f-score">precision,
recall and F-score</NoteLink>.

### Worked example

Take the 12 tokens "Barack Obama spoke at the United Nations in New York City ." with gold spans [PER Barack Obama],
[ORG United Nations] and [LOC New York City]. A tagger predicts [PER Barack Obama], [ORG United Nations] and [LOC New
York], tagging "City" as `O`.

- **Span level.** Two of the three predicted spans match exactly, so $P = 2/3$, $R = 2/3$ and $F_1 = 0.667$. The
  truncated location counts as a false positive and a false negative at once.
- **Token level.** Only "City" has the wrong BIO tag (`O` instead of `I-LOC`), so token accuracy is $11/12 = 0.917$.

Token accuracy rewards the model for the many easy `O` tokens and barely registers the boundary error that makes the
extracted location wrong.

## Pitfalls

- **Boundary errors count twice.** Under exact matching, a span off by one token is both a false positive and a false
  negative. Report partial-match or per-type scores alongside exact F1 when boundaries are uncertain.
- **Annotation guidelines differ.** Whether "the" in "the United Nations" or a title such as "President" belongs to a
  span varies between datasets. Models trained on one convention lose points on another.
- **Domain shift.** Taggers trained on news underperform on social media, clinical text or code, where capitalisation
  and name forms differ.
- **Nested entities.** "Bank of America Stadium" contains the organisation "Bank of America". Flat BIO tagging can mark
  only one of them.
