<Definition>
A statistical hypothesis is a set of probability distributions that could have generated the data. The **null
hypothesis** $H_0$ is the default set, usually "no effect". The **alternative hypothesis** $H_1$ is a disjoint set
that represents the departure of interest. A **test** is a rule that maps the data to one of two decisions: reject
$H_0$, or do not reject $H_0$ <Cite id="neyman1933" />.
</Definition>

## Ingredients of a test

A test has three parts <Cite id="casella2002" />.

1. **A test statistic** $T(\Xcal)$, a function of the sample $\Xcal$. It is chosen so that values far from what $H_0$
   predicts are evidence for $H_1$.
2. **A sampling distribution under $H_0$.** This is the distribution of $T(\Xcal)$ when the data are generated by a
   distribution in $H_0$. It is the reference against which an observed value is judged unusual.
3. **A critical region** $\Ccal_\alpha$, the set of statistic values that lead to rejection. It is fixed before the data
   are seen. For a statistic whose large values count against $H_0$, it has the form
   $\Ccal_\alpha = \set{t : t \ge c_\alpha}$, with the critical value $c_\alpha$ chosen so that

$$
\sup_{P \in H_0} P\paren{T(\Xcal) \in \Ccal_\alpha} \le \alpha.
$$

The left-hand side is the **size** of the test. The bound $\alpha$ is the significance level. A rejection when $H_0$
is true is a Type I error, so the size is the largest Type I error rate over the distributions in $H_0$.

## Simple and composite hypotheses

A hypothesis is **simple** when it contains exactly one distribution, for example $H_0: p = 0.01$ for a known model
family. It is **composite** when it contains several, for example $H_1: p > 0.01$, or $H_0: \mu = 0$ when the variance
is unknown. With a composite $H_0$ the size is a supremum, so the critical region must respect every distribution in
$H_0$ <Cite id="lehmann2005" />.

## Example: a defect rate

A factory claims that at most 1% of its items are defective. An inspector samples $n = 200$ items and counts the
defective ones, $S$. Items are independent, so $S \sim \Binom(n, p)$, where $p$ is the defect rate. The hypotheses are

$$
H_0: p = 0.01, \qquad H_1: p > 0.01.
$$

The claim is "at most 1%", a composite null $p \le 0.01$, but the test can be calibrated at $p = 0.01$ alone. The
tail probability $P_p(S \ge c)$ increases with $p$: generate each item's defect as $\indicator\set{U_i \le p}$ from a
uniform $U_i$, and raising $p$ can only turn non-defects into defects, so the count can only grow. The largest
rejection probability over the null is therefore reached at its boundary, $p = 0.01$, and a region with size at most
$\alpha$ there has size at most $\alpha$ over the whole null.

Large counts are evidence for $H_1$, so the critical region is $S \ge c$. The critical value is the smallest integer
with $P_{H_0}(S \ge c) \le \alpha$. For $\alpha = 0.05$, $P_{H_0}(S \ge 5) = 0.052$ is too large and
$P_{H_0}(S \ge 6) = 0.016$ is not, so $c = 6$. The test rejects the claim when 6 or more items are defective. Because
$S$ is discrete, the size is 0.016, below the nominal 0.05.

If the inspector finds $s = 10$ defects, the p-value is $P_{H_0}(S \ge 10) \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$, and $H_0$ is
rejected. The observed rate, 5%, is also far above the claimed 1%, which matters as much as the decision.

With $n = 20$ items the same test rejects at 2 defects, because $P_{H_0}(S \ge 2) = 0.017$ and
$P_{H_0}(S \ge 1) = 0.18$. Small samples can still detect a large departure. They cannot detect a small one. The power
against a true rate of 2% is the probability of reaching the critical region under that rate:
$P_{p = 0.02}(S \ge 6) = 0.21$ for $n = 200$. With $n = 1000$ the critical value is 16, and
$P_{p = 0.02}(S \ge 16) = 0.85$.

## Rejecting and not rejecting

The two decisions are not symmetric. The critical region is calibrated under $H_0$ only, so a rejection comes with a
guarantee: it is wrong with probability at most $\alpha$ when $H_0$ holds. Not rejecting comes with no such guarantee.
It means that the data are compatible with $H_0$, not that $H_0$ is true. A test with low power fails to reject most
false nulls. The <NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors">power</NoteLink> of the test against the effects that matter
decides how much a non-rejection says.

Evidence and decision are also distinct. The <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> grades how unusual the data are
under $H_0$ on a continuous scale. The decision compares it with $\alpha$ and discards the grading.

## Choosing $H_0$ and $H_1$

- $H_0$ is the hypothesis whose false rejection is the more costly error, because the test controls only that error
  rate directly. In a drug trial, $H_0$ is "the drug has no effect".
- $H_1$ decides the shape of the critical region. $H_1: p > 0.01$ puts the region in the upper tail. $H_1: p \ne 0.01$
  splits it between both tails.
- Both are fixed before the data are examined. Choosing the alternative after seeing which way the data point
  invalidates the size calculation.

## Pitfalls

- **"Accepting" $H_0$.** A non-significant result is not evidence of no effect unless the test had high power against
  every effect of practical size.
- **Testing a straw man.** A point null such as $\mu = 0$ is almost never exactly true. With enough data it is always
  rejected. Report an estimate and a confidence interval so that the size of the departure is visible.
- **A statistic that misses the question.** A test of means has little power against a change in variance or shape.
  The test statistic must measure the departure that $H_1$ describes.
