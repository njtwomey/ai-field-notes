<Definition>
**Sample-size planning** fixes, before data are collected, the significance level $\alpha$, the target power
$1 - \beta$ and the smallest effect that matters, $\Delta$, and solves the power equation of the planned test for the
sample size $n$. For a fixed $n$, the same equation gives the **minimum detectable effect** (MDE), the smallest effect
detected with power $1 - \beta$ <Cite id="cohen1988" />.
</Definition>

## Inputs

1. **The test and its sidedness**, for example a two-sided comparison of two means.
2. **The significance level** $\alpha$, usually 0.05.
3. **The target power** $1 - \beta$, usually 0.8 or 0.9.
4. **The smallest effect of interest** $\Delta$. It comes from the application, such as the smallest price change worth
   shipping, not from a previous study's estimate.
5. **The variability** $\sigma$ of the outcome, from pilot data or earlier studies.

Only the ratio $d = \Delta/\sigma$ enters the calculation for means.

## Formulas

For a statistic that is approximately normal with standard error $\text{se}(n)$, a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test has
power $1 - \beta$ against $\Delta$ when, ignoring the far tail,

$$
\Delta = \paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}} \, \text{se}(n),
$$

where $z_q$ is the standard normal $q$-quantile. For a one-sided test, $z_{1-\alpha}$ replaces $z_{1-\alpha/2}$.

**Derivation.** Let the estimate be $\hat\theta \sim \Gauss(\theta, \text{se}(n)^2)$ and the statistic
$Z = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)/\text{se}(n)$. When the true effect is $\Delta = \theta - \theta_0$, $Z$ is normal with
mean $\Delta/\text{se}(n)$ and variance 1. The test rejects when $\abs Z \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$. Ignoring the
rejections in the wrong direction, which have negligible probability for a real effect, the power is

$$
P(Z \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}) = 1 - \Phi\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} - \frac{\Delta}{\text{se}(n)}}.
$$

Setting this to $1 - \beta$ gives $z_{1-\alpha/2} - \Delta/\text{se}(n) = -z_{1-\beta}$, which rearranges to the
equation above. The distribution under $H_1$ must be centred $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ standard errors beyond the null to
reach the critical value, and a further $z_{1-\beta}$ standard errors beyond that so that a fraction $1 - \beta$ of it
lies past the critical value. For $\alpha = 0.05$ and power 0.8 the total is $1.960 + 0.842 = 2.80$ standard errors.

Solving for $n$ needs only the standard error of each design. For one mean it is $\sigma/\sqrt n$; see
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink>. For two independent means with $n$ per group the variances
add, giving $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$. For $n$ pairs it is the standard error of the mean difference, $\sigma_D/\sqrt n$.
Squaring $\Delta = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})\,\text{se}(n)$ and solving:

| Design | Standard error | $n$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One mean | $\sigma/\sqrt n$ | $\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}^2 / d^2$ |
| Two means, $n$ per group | $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$ | $2\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}^2 / d^2$ per group |
| Paired, differences with sd $\sigma_D$ | $\sigma_D/\sqrt n$ | $\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}^2 \sigma_D^2 / \Delta^2$ pairs |

For two proportions $p_1$ and $p_2$, with $\bar p = (p_1 + p_2)/2$, the usual formula is

$$
n = \frac{\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1 - \bar p)}
  + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_1(1 - p_1) + p_2(1 - p_2)}}^2}{(p_1 - p_2)^2}
$$

per group. The first term uses the variance under $H_0$ and the second the variance under $H_1$.

<Derivation title="The two-proportion formula">
The estimate is $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2$, with $n$ per group. Under $H_0: p_1 = p_2$ the common proportion is estimated
by $\bar p$, and the test's standard error is $\text{se}_0 = \sqrt{2\bar p(1 - \bar p)/n}$. Under $H_1$ the true
standard error is $\text{se}_1 = \sqrt{\paren{p_1(1 - p_1) + p_2(1 - p_2)}/n}$.

The test rejects when $\hat p_1 - \hat p_2 \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\text{se}_0$. Under $H_1$ the estimate is
approximately $\Gauss(p_1 - p_2, \text{se}_1^2)$, so the power is
$1 - \Phi\paren{(z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\text{se}_0 - (p_1 - p_2))/\text{se}_1}$. Setting it to $1 - \beta$ gives

$$
p_1 - p_2 = z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\text{se}_0 + z_{1-\beta}\,\text{se}_1.
$$

Both standard errors are proportional to $1/\sqrt n$. Multiplying through by $\sqrt n$, squaring and solving gives the
formula above.
</Derivation>

## Worked examples

- **Two means.** For $d = 0.5$, $\alpha = 0.05$ and power 0.8, the normal formula gives
  $n = 2(1.960 + 0.842)^2/0.25 = 62.8$, so 63 per group. The exact t-test needs 64 per group, because the
  <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">t distribution</NoteLink> has heavier tails than the normal.
- **Two proportions.** Detecting a rise in conversion from 10% to 12% at the same $\alpha$ and power needs 3,841 users
  per group. Small differences between proportions near zero need very large samples.
- **Minimum detectable effect.** With 100 per group the MDE is $2.80\sqrt{2/100} = 0.40$ standard deviations. Effects
  smaller than that are likely to be missed.

## Design levers

- **Sample size.** The detectable effect scales as $1/\sqrt n$. Halving it needs four times the data.
- **Variance reduction.** Pairing, blocking, adjusting for a baseline covariate or measuring more precisely reduce
  $\sigma$, which acts like extra data. A <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired design</NoteLink> with within-pair
  correlation $\rho$ multiplies the variance of a difference by $1 - \rho$.
- **Allocation.** For a fixed total $N$, the standard error of a difference is proportional to
  $\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/(N - n_1)}$, which is smallest at $n_1 = N/2$ when the variances are equal. A $k{:}1$ allocation
  multiplies the required total by $(1 + k)^2/(4k)$, which is $9/8$ for 2:1: 141 observations in total for $d = 0.5$,
  instead of 126 for 1:1.
- **Sidedness and α.** A one-sided test or a larger $\alpha$ reduces $n$, but only if chosen for reasons fixed in
  advance.

## Pitfalls

- **Planning around a published estimate.** Published effects are inflated by selection for significance. Planning for
  them yields underpowered studies.
- **Optimistic variance.** A pilot study's standard deviation is itself noisy. The required $n$ is proportional to
  $\sigma^2$, so underestimating $\sigma$ by 20% gives $0.8^2 = 0.64$ of the needed $n$: 36% too few.
- **Attrition and clustering.** Inflate $n$ for expected dropout. When units are sampled in clusters, such as pupils in
  classes, multiply by the design effect $1 + (m - 1)\rho$ for cluster size $m$ and intra-cluster correlation $\rho$.
- **Post-hoc power.** Computing power from the observed effect after the study says nothing that the p-value did not.
