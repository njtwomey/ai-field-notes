<Definition>
Let the data $X$ have density $f_0$ under a simple null $H_0$ and $f_1$ under a simple alternative $H_1$. For
$k \ge 0$, the **likelihood ratio test** rejects $H_0$ when

$$
\Lambda(x) = \frac{f_1(x)}{f_0(x)} > k.
$$

The **Neyman–Pearson lemma** states that if this test has size $\alpha$, no other test of size at most $\alpha$ has
greater power against $H_1$ <Cite id="neyman1933" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A critical region $\Ccal$ spends probability under $H_0$, the size $\int_\Ccal f_0$, to buy probability under $H_1$, the
power $\int_\Ccal f_1$. Each point $x$ costs $f_0(x)$ and returns $f_1(x)$. The best purchase with a fixed budget
$\alpha$ takes the points with the highest return per unit cost, $f_1(x)/f_0(x)$, until the budget runs out. That is
the likelihood ratio region.

The region is decided by the likelihood ratio, not by which values of $x$ look large. For $H_0: X \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$
against $H_1: X \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma_1^2)$, the ratio is

$$
\Lambda(x) = \frac{\sigma_1^{-1} e^{-x^2/(2\sigma_1^2)}}{e^{-x^2/2}} = \frac{1}{\sigma_1}
\exp\set{\frac{x^2}{2}\paren{1 - \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}}},
$$

an increasing function of $x^2$ when $\sigma_1 > 1$. The most
powerful test rejects for large $\abs x$. With $\sigma_1 = 2$ and $\alpha = 0.05$ it rejects when $\abs x \ge 1.96$.
Under $H_1$, $X/2$ is standard normal, so its power is $P(\abs{X/2} \ge 0.98) = 2\paren{1 - \Phi(0.98)} = 0.33$. The
upper-tailed test $x \ge 1.645$ has the same size and power $1 - \Phi(0.82) = 0.21$. When $\sigma_1 < 1$ the ratio
decreases in $x^2$, and the most powerful test rejects for $x$ close to zero.

## Proof

Let $\phi(x) = \indicator\set{\Lambda(x) > k}$ be the likelihood ratio test with size $\alpha$, and $\psi(x) \in [0, 1]$
any test with size at most $\alpha$, where $\psi(x)$ is the probability of rejecting at $x$. For every $x$,

$$
\paren{\phi(x) - \psi(x)}\paren{f_1(x) - k f_0(x)} \ge 0.
$$

When $f_1(x) > k f_0(x)$, $\phi(x) = 1 \ge \psi(x)$. When $f_1(x) < k f_0(x)$, $\phi(x) = 0 \le \psi(x)$. Both
factors have the same sign. Integrating over $x$,

$$
\int (\phi - \psi) f_1 \ge k \int (\phi - \psi) f_0 = k\paren{\alpha - \text{size}(\psi)} \ge 0.
$$

The equality uses $\int \phi f_0 = \alpha$, the size of $\phi$, and $\int \psi f_0 = \text{size}(\psi)$. The final
inequality holds because $k \ge 0$ and $\psi$ has size at most $\alpha$. The left-hand side is the power of $\phi$
minus the power of $\psi$, so $\phi$ is at least as powerful. The proof is the budget argument of the intuition made
exact: wherever $\psi$ departs from $\phi$, it either buys a point with return below $k$ or skips one with return
above $k$.

For discrete data, the likelihood ratio test may not attain size $\alpha$ exactly. The lemma then holds for a
randomised test that rejects with a probability $\gamma$ on the boundary $\Lambda(x) = k$; see
<NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink>.

## From the lemma to familiar tests

Take $n$ observations from $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known, $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ and $H_1: \mu = \mu_1$ with
$\mu_1 > \mu_0$. The likelihood under mean $\mu$ is $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\set{-\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$.
In the ratio the constants cancel, and expanding $(x_i - \mu)^2 = x_i^2 - 2\mu x_i + \mu^2$ the $x_i^2$ terms cancel
too:

$$
\log \Lambda = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \brack{(x_i - \mu_0)^2 - (x_i - \mu_1)^2}
= \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \brack{2(\mu_1 - \mu_0)x_i - (\mu_1^2 - \mu_0^2)}
= \frac{n(\mu_1 - \mu_0)}{\sigma^2}\, \bar x - \frac{n(\mu_1^2 - \mu_0^2)}{2\sigma^2},
$$

which increases in the sample mean $\bar x$. The most powerful test rejects when $\bar x$ is large, which is the
upper-tailed <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink>. Its critical value depends only on $\mu_0$, $\sigma$, $n$ and
$\alpha$, not on $\mu_1$. The same test is therefore most powerful against every $\mu_1 > \mu_0$ at once: it is
**uniformly most powerful** (UMP) for $H_1: \mu > \mu_0$.

This generalises. When the likelihood ratio is monotone in a statistic $T$ for every pair of parameter values, the test
that rejects for large $T$ is UMP for one-sided alternatives. This is the Karlin–Rubin theorem. One-parameter
<NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential families</NoteLink> have this property with $T$ their
<NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistic</NoteLink> <Cite id="casella2002" />.

<Derivation title="Monotone likelihood ratios in exponential families">
For a one-parameter exponential family $f(x \mid \eta) = h(x)\exp\set{\eta\, T(x) - A(\eta)}$ and $\eta_1 > \eta_0$,

$$
\frac{f(x \mid \eta_1)}{f(x \mid \eta_0)} = \exp\set{(\eta_1 - \eta_0)\, T(x) - A(\eta_1) + A(\eta_0)},
$$

which is increasing in $T(x)$. So for every alternative $\eta_1 > \eta_0$, the region $\set{\Lambda > k}$ is a region
$\set{T > c}$. The value $c$ is fixed by the size at $\eta_0$, which does not involve $\eta_1$. The same test is
therefore the Neyman–Pearson test against every $\eta_1 > \eta_0$, which is what uniformly most powerful means. The
normal mean with known $\sigma$ is the case $\eta = \mu/\sigma^2$ and $T(x) = \sum_i x_i$.
</Derivation>

## Limits

- **Composite hypotheses.** The lemma needs both hypotheses to be single distributions. For two-sided alternatives no
  UMP test exists in general: the best test against $\mu > \mu_0$ is powerless against $\mu < \mu_0$. Theory then
  restricts the class, for example to unbiased tests, and the two-sided t-test is uniformly most powerful unbiased
  <Cite id="lehmann2005" />.
- **Nuisance parameters.** An unknown variance makes $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ composite. The generalised likelihood ratio
  test, which maximises each likelihood over the unknown parameters, is then the standard approach. For the normal
  mean it gives the t-test.
- **Correct model.** Optimality holds under the assumed densities. Under a misspecified model another test can be more
  powerful.
