<Definition>
A hypothesis test either rejects the null hypothesis $H_0$ or does not. A **Type I error** rejects $H_0$ when it is
true. A **Type II error** fails to reject $H_0$ when it is false. Their probabilities are

$$
\alpha = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true}), \qquad \beta = P(\text{do not reject } H_0 \mid H_1 \text{ true}).
$$

The **power** of the test is $1 - \beta$, the probability of detecting a real effect <Cite id="neyman1933" />.
</Definition>

## The four outcomes

| | $H_0$ true | $H_0$ false |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reject $H_0$ | Type I error (false positive), probability $\alpha$ | Correct detection, probability $1 - \beta$ |
| Do not reject $H_0$ | Correct, probability $1 - \alpha$ | Type II error (false negative), probability $\beta$ |

The significance level $\alpha$ is chosen in advance. $\beta$ is not a single number. It depends on how large the true
effect is, how much data there is and which test is used.

## Power function

The power function gives the rejection probability for every possible truth:
$\pi(\theta) = P_\theta(\text{reject } H_0)$. Under $H_0$ it is at most $\alpha$. Under $H_1$ it is the power
<Cite id="wasserman2004" />.

For a z-test of a mean with known standard deviation $\sigma$, $n$ observations and true shift $\Delta$, the power
follows from where the statistic sits. $\bar X$ is $\Gauss(\mu_0 + \Delta, \sigma^2/n)$, so

$$
Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n} = Z_0 + \delta, \qquad Z_0 \sim \Gauss(0, 1), \qquad
\delta = \frac{\Delta\sqrt n}{\sigma}.
$$

The shift $\delta$ is the effect measured in standard errors. The two-sided test with critical value
$c = z_{1-\alpha/2}$ rejects when $Z \ge c$ or $Z \le -c$. The two events are disjoint, so

$$
\pi(\Delta) = P(Z_0 \ge c - \delta) + P(Z_0 \le -c - \delta)
= 1 - \Phi\paren{c - \frac{\Delta\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}} + \Phi\paren{-c - \frac{\Delta\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}}.
$$

At $\Delta = 0$ this is $1 - \Phi(c) + \Phi(-c) = \alpha$, the size. As $\delta$ grows, the first term tends to 1 and
the second to 0.

## The trade-off

- **Lower $\alpha$** moves the critical value outwards. False positives become rarer and misses more common.
- **Larger $n$** narrows the sampling distributions relative to the effect. $\beta$ falls at a fixed $\alpha$.
- **Larger effects** are easier to detect. Power is always stated for a particular effect size.
- **One-sided tests** put all of $\alpha$ in one tail. They gain power in the chosen direction and have none in the
  other.

## Planning a sample size

Choose the smallest effect worth detecting, $\Delta > 0$, and a target power $1 - \beta$. For a real effect the second
term of the power formula is tiny: it is the chance of a significant result in the wrong direction. Dropping it and
setting the power to $1 - \beta$,

$$
1 - \Phi(c - \delta) = 1 - \beta \iff c - \delta = z_\beta = -z_{1-\beta} \iff \delta = z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}.
$$

The detectable shift is the critical value plus the power quantile, both in standard errors. Substituting
$\delta = \Delta\sqrt n/\sigma$ and solving for $n$,

$$
n \approx \paren{\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}{\Delta / \sigma}}^2.
$$

For $\alpha = 0.05$, power $0.8$ and an effect of half a standard deviation, $n \approx (1.96 + 0.84)^2 / 0.25
\approx 32$. Halving the effect quadruples the required $n$ <Cite id="cohen1988" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Underpowered studies.** A study with power 0.3 misses most real effects. Its significant results also
  overestimate the effect, because only lucky, large estimates cross the threshold.
- **Post-hoc power.** Computing power from the observed effect after the study adds no information beyond the
  p-value.
- **Absence of evidence.** Failing to reject $H_0$ is not evidence that $H_0$ is true, unless the test had high power
  against the effects that matter.
