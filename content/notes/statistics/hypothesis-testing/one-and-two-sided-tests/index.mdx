<Definition>
Let $\theta$ be a parameter with null value $\theta_0$. A **one-sided** test has the alternative $H_1: \theta >
\theta_0$ (upper-tailed) or $H_1: \theta < \theta_0$ (lower-tailed), and its <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">critical region</NoteLink> lies in one tail of the
test statistic's null distribution. A **two-sided** test has $H_1: \theta \ne \theta_0$, and its critical region is
split between both tails <Cite id="casella2002" />.
</Definition>

## Critical regions and p-values

Take a statistic $Z$ that is $\Gauss(0, 1)$ under $H_0$ and shifts upwards when $\theta > \theta_0$. At level $\alpha$:

| Alternative | Reject when | <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> for observed $z$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\theta > \theta_0$ | $z \ge z_{1-\alpha}$ | $1 - \Phi(z)$ |
| $\theta < \theta_0$ | $z \le -z_{1-\alpha}$ | $\Phi(z)$ |
| $\theta \ne \theta_0$ | $\abs{z} \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$ | $2\paren{1 - \Phi(\abs{z})}$ |

Here $\Phi$ is the standard normal cdf and $z_q$ its $q$-quantile. At $\alpha = 0.05$ the one-sided critical value is
$1.645$ and the two-sided one is $1.960$. For an observed $z = 1.8$, the upper-tailed p-value is $0.036$ and the
two-sided p-value is $0.072$: the same data are significant one-sided and not two-sided.

## Power

A one-sided test puts all of $\alpha$ in the tail where the effect is expected. Its critical value is closer to zero,
so it detects effects in that direction more often. Under an effect, $Z = Z_0 + \delta$ with $Z_0 \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$
and $\delta$ the effect in <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink>; see <NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors">Type I and Type II
errors</NoteLink>. The two powers are

$$
\pi_{\text{one}}(\delta) = 1 - \Phi(z_{1-\alpha} - \delta), \qquad
\pi_{\text{two}}(\delta) = 1 - \Phi(z_{1-\alpha/2} - \delta) + \Phi(-z_{1-\alpha/2} - \delta).
$$

For a true shift of $0.3$ <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviations</NoteLink> and $n = 25$, $\delta = 0.3\sqrt{25} = 1.5$. The upper-tailed test has
power $1 - \Phi(0.145) = 0.44$ and the two-sided test $1 - \Phi(0.46) + \Phi(-3.46) = 0.32$.
For an effect in the opposite direction, the one-sided test has power below $\alpha$: it cannot detect it.

## Choosing the sidedness

- **Use a one-sided test** only when a departure in the other direction would lead to the same action as no
  departure, and this is decided in advance. A non-inferiority trial that asks whether a new treatment is no worse
  than the standard is an example.
- **Use a two-sided test** when a departure in either direction would matter or be reported. This is the default in
  most scientific work, where a harmful effect is as important as a beneficial one.
- **Never choose after seeing the data.** Picking the tail that the data point towards doubles the Type I error rate of
  a nominal level-$\alpha$ one-sided test, to $2\alpha$. The procedure rejects when $Z \ge z_{1-\alpha}$ or
  $Z \le -z_{1-\alpha}$, whichever tail the data favour. Under $H_0$ these disjoint events each have probability
  $\alpha$, so the procedure rejects with probability $2\alpha$.

A two-sided level-$\alpha$ test is equivalent to two one-sided tests, each at level $\alpha/2$. Its confidence
interval counterpart is the two-sided $(1 - \alpha)$ interval; a one-sided test corresponds to a one-sided bound. See
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence intervals</NoteLink>.

## Asymmetric null distributions

For symmetric statistics, the two-sided p-value is twice the smaller one-sided p-value. For asymmetric <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">discrete
distributions</NoteLink>, doubling and other definitions disagree. With $S \sim \Binom(20, 0.3)$ under $H_0$ and $s = 10$
observed, the upper tail is $P(S \ge 10) = 0.048$. Doubling gives $0.096$. Summing the probabilities of all outcomes
no more likely than $s = 10$ gives $0.083$ <Cite id="lehmann2005" />. Software differs in which it reports, so state
the definition used.

## Pitfalls

- **Switching to one-sided to reach significance.** It halves the p-value by construction and is a common form of
  selective reporting.
- **Ignoring the untested direction.** A one-sided test that finds nothing says nothing about effects in the other
  direction, which may be large.
- **Mismatched intervals.** Reporting a two-sided 95% interval beside a one-sided test at 0.05 gives inconsistent
  conclusions for results near the threshold.
