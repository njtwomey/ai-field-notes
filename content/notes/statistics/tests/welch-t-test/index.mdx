<Definition>
Welch's t-test compares the means $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ of two independent groups. It estimates each group's variance
separately and refers the statistic to a <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">t distribution</NoteLink> whose degrees of freedom are approximated from the data
<Cite id="welch1947" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$. The two-sided alternative is $H_1: \mu_1 \ne \mu_2$. One-sided alternatives, such as
$\mu_1 > \mu_2$, are chosen before seeing the data.

## Assumptions

- Observations are independent within and between the groups.
- Each group mean is approximately normal. This holds for normal data, and for other distributions when the group is
  large enough for the <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss> to apply.
- The variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$ may differ. That is the difference from Student's pooled test.

## Test statistic

With sample means $\bar x_1, \bar x_2$, sample variances $s_1^2, s_2^2$ and sizes $n_1, n_2$:

$$
t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}.
$$

The denominator is the <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of the difference in means, with each group contributing its own variance.

## Decision rule

Under $H_0$, $t$ approximately follows a t distribution with the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
<Cite id="satterthwaite1946" />:

$$
\nu = \frac{\paren{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}^2}{\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}}.
$$

$\nu$ is usually not an integer. It lies between $\min(n_1, n_2) - 1$ and $n_1 + n_2 - 2$. Reject $H_0$ at level
$\alpha$ when $\abs{t} > t_{1-\alpha/2,\,\nu}$, or equivalently when the two-sided <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> is below $\alpha$.

## Where the degrees of freedom come from

The numerator behaves as in the pooled test. The group means are independent and normal, so under $H_0$,

$$
Z = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{\sqrt V} \sim \Gauss(0, 1), \qquad V = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}.
$$

$V$ is estimated by $\hat V = s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2$, and $t = Z\sqrt{V/\hat V}$. If $\nu\hat V/V$ were a
$\ChiSq_\nu$ variable independent of $Z$, then $t$ would be exactly t distributed with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, as in
the <NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample derivation</NoteLink>. It is not: $\hat V$ is a weighted sum of two
independent $\chi^2$ variables with different weights, and such a sum is not a scaled $\chi^2$.

Satterthwaite's approximation picks the $\nu$ for which $\nu\hat V/V$ has the same mean and variance as $\ChiSq_\nu$
<Cite id="satterthwaite1946" />. The mean matches for every $\nu$, because $\expect[\hat V] = V$. For the variance,
each $(n_j - 1)S_j^2/\sigma_j^2$ is $\ChiSq_{n_j-1}$, which has variance $2(n_j - 1)$, so
$\var(S_j^2) = 2\sigma_j^4/(n_j - 1)$. The two groups are independent, so

$$
\var(\hat V) = \frac{2(\sigma_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{2(\sigma_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}.
$$

A $\ChiSq_\nu$ variable has variance $2\nu$. Setting $\var(\nu\hat V/V) = \nu^2\var(\hat V)/V^2$ equal to $2\nu$ and
solving gives

$$
\nu = \frac{2V^2}{\var(\hat V)} = \frac{\paren{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}^2}
{\dfrac{(\sigma_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \dfrac{(\sigma_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}}.
$$

The unknown $\sigma_j^2$ are replaced by $s_j^2$, which gives the formula in the decision rule. When one group
dominates $\hat V$, $\nu$ approaches that group's $n_j - 1$, because the variance estimate is then essentially one
sample variance.

<Derivation title="Why ν lies between min(n₁, n₂) − 1 and n₁ + n₂ − 2">
Write $a_j = s_j^2/n_j$ and $m_j = n_j - 1$, so that $\nu = (a_1 + a_2)^2/(a_1^2/m_1 + a_2^2/m_2)$.

**Upper bound.** By the <NoteLink to="dot-product">Cauchy–Schwarz inequality</NoteLink> applied to the vectors $(a_1/\sqrt{m_1}, a_2/\sqrt{m_2})$ and
$(\sqrt{m_1}, \sqrt{m_2})$,

$$
(a_1 + a_2)^2 \le \paren{\frac{a_1^2}{m_1} + \frac{a_2^2}{m_2}}(m_1 + m_2),
$$

so $\nu \le m_1 + m_2 = n_1 + n_2 - 2$. Equality holds when $a_j/m_j$ is the same for both groups.

**Lower bound.** Let $m = \min(m_1, m_2)$. Then
$a_1^2/m_1 + a_2^2/m_2 \le (a_1^2 + a_2^2)/m \le (a_1 + a_2)^2/m$, since $a_1, a_2 \ge 0$. So $\nu \ge m$.
</Derivation>

## Why not the pooled test

Student's pooled test estimates one common variance,
$s_p^2 = \brack{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}/(n_1 + n_2 - 2)$, and uses $n_1 + n_2 - 2$ degrees of freedom
<Cite id="student1908" />. The pooled estimate is dominated by the larger group. When the smaller group has the larger
variance, the standard error is underestimated and the test rejects a true null far more often than $\alpha$. When
the larger group has the larger variance, the test becomes conservative and loses power.

<TypeIErrorMaps />

With equal variances or equal group sizes the two tests nearly agree. When the variances really are equal, Welch loses
very little power. Using Welch's test by default is therefore the safer choice <Cite id="delacre2017" />.

## Effect size

Report the difference in means with its <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink>,
$(\bar x_1 - \bar x_2) \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,\nu}\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}$. A standardised <NoteLink to="effect-size">effect size</NoteLink> divides the
difference by a <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>. With unequal variances, state which one is used, for example the control
group's.

## Pitfalls

- **Paired data.** Measurements on the same units before and after are not independent groups. Use a <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink>.
- **Testing variances first.** Choosing between the pooled and Welch tests with a preliminary test of equal variances
  distorts the overall error rate. Use Welch directly.
- **Skewed small samples.** With strongly skewed data and small groups, the t approximation is poor for either test.
  Consider a <NoteLink to="permutation-tests">permutation test</NoteLink> or a transformation.
