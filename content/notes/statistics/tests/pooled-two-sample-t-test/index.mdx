<Definition>
The pooled two-sample t-test compares the means $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$ of two independent groups, assuming both are
normal with a common variance $\sigma^2$. It estimates $\sigma^2$ from both samples together and tests
$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ with a t statistic on $n_1 + n_2 - 2$ degrees of freedom <Cite id="student1908" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ against $H_1: \mu_1 \ne \mu_2$, or a one-sided alternative fixed in advance. More generally,
$H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = \Delta_0$.

## Assumptions

- Observations are independent within and between groups.
- Each group is normal, or large enough for its mean to be approximately normal.
- **Both groups have the same variance.** This is the assumption that distinguishes the test from
  <NoteLink to="welch-t-test">Welch's t-test</NoteLink>, and the one most often violated.

## Test statistic

With sample means $\bar x_j$, sample variances $s_j^2$ and sizes $n_j$ for groups $j = 1, 2$, the pooled variance is
the degrees-of-freedom-weighted average of the two sample variances:

$$
s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}.
$$

The statistic is

$$
t = \frac{\bar x_1 - \bar x_2}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}}.
$$

**Null distribution.** The derivation follows the <NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample t-test</NoteLink>, one
piece at a time.

1. **The numerator.** The two sample means are independent, with $\bar X_j \sim \Gauss(\mu_j, \sigma^2/n_j)$. A
   difference of independent normals is normal, and the variances add:
   $\var(\bar X_1 - \bar X_2) = \sigma^2/n_1 + \sigma^2/n_2$. Under $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$,
   $$
   Z = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{\sigma\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} \sim \Gauss(0, 1).
   $$
2. **The variance estimate.** Within each group, $(n_j - 1)S_j^2/\sigma^2 \sim \ChiSq_{n_j - 1}$. The groups are
   independent, and a sum of independent $\chi^2$ variables is $\chi^2$ with the degrees of freedom added, so
   $$
   V = \frac{(n_1 + n_2 - 2)S_p^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{(n_1 - 1)S_1^2}{\sigma^2} + \frac{(n_2 - 1)S_2^2}{\sigma^2}
   \sim \ChiSq_{n_1 + n_2 - 2}.
   $$
   This is why the pooled variance weights each $s_j^2$ by its degrees of freedom $n_j - 1$. It also makes $S_p^2$
   unbiased: $\expect[V] = n_1 + n_2 - 2$, so $\expect[S_p^2] = \sigma^2$.
3. **Independence.** Each $S_j^2$ is independent of its own $\bar X_j$, and the groups are independent of each other.
   So $V$ is independent of $Z$.

Dividing, the common $\sigma$ cancels:

$$
\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n_1 + n_2 - 2)}} = \frac{\bar X_1 - \bar X_2}{S_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}} = T
\sim t_{n_1 + n_2 - 2}.
$$

The cancellation needs the same $\sigma$ in both groups. With unequal variances, the numerator's scale
$\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}$ and the pooled estimate no longer match, and the statistic is not t
distributed. That is the case <NoteLink to="welch-t-test">Welch's test</NoteLink> handles.

## Decision rule

Reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ when $\abs{t} \ge t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n_1 + n_2 - 2}$. The two-sided p-value is
$2 P(T_\nu \ge \abs t)$.

## Example

Group 1 has $n_1 = 12$, $\bar x_1 = 23.1$ and $s_1 = 3.2$. Group 2 has $n_2 = 10$, $\bar x_2 = 20.4$ and $s_2 = 2.9$.

$$
s_p = \sqrt{\frac{11 \times 3.2^2 + 9 \times 2.9^2}{20}} = 3.07, \qquad
t = \frac{2.7}{3.07\sqrt{1/12 + 1/10}} = \frac{2.7}{1.31} = 2.05, \qquad \nu = 20.
$$

The two-sided p-value is $0.053$, just above 0.05. The 95% interval for the difference is $[-0.04, 5.44]$. It barely
includes zero, and it also includes differences of 5, which would matter. The data are inconclusive rather than
evidence of no difference. Welch's test on the same numbers gives $t = 2.07$ and $p = 0.051$: with similar variances
the two tests agree.

## Effect size

Cohen's $d = (\bar x_1 - \bar x_2)/s_p$ uses the pooled standard deviation; in the example $d = 0.88$. Hedges' $g$
corrects its small-sample bias, here to $g = 0.85$; see <NoteLink to="effect-size">effect size</NoteLink>. Under
$H_1$ the statistic has noncentrality $d\big/\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$. For a fixed total $N = n_1 + n_2$, the function
$1/n_1 + 1/(N - n_1)$ is convex and symmetric about $N/2$, so it is smallest, and the noncentrality largest, when the
groups have equal size. A 2:1 split has $1/n_1 + 1/n_2 = 9/(2N)$ against $4/N$ for 1:1, so it needs $9/8$ times the
total sample for the same power.

## When to prefer Welch's test

When the variances differ, the pooled estimate is dominated by the larger group. If the smaller group has the larger
variance, the pooled test underestimates the standard error and rejects true nulls far more often than $\alpha$.
Welch's test loses almost no power when the variances are equal, so it is the safer default
<Cite id="delacre2017" />. The pooled test remains exact under its assumptions and is the building block of
one-way ANOVA and linear regression, which assume a common error variance.

## Pitfalls

- **Testing variances first.** Choosing between pooled and Welch with a preliminary variance test distorts the error
  rate of the procedure as a whole. Decide in advance.
- **Paired observations.** Measurements on the same units are not independent groups. Use the
  <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink>, which is usually far more powerful.
- **Many groups.** Running pooled t-tests for every pair of several groups is multiple testing. Use ANOVA with a
  correction for the follow-up comparisons.
