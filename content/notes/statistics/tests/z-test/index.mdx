<Definition>
The one-sample z-test compares the mean $\mu$ of a population with a null value $\mu_0$, using $n$ independent
observations and the known population standard deviation $\sigma$. The statistic

$$
Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
$$

is standard normal under $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$, exactly for normal data and approximately for large $n$
<Cite id="casella2002" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ against $H_1: \mu \ne \mu_0$, or a one-sided alternative $\mu > \mu_0$ or $\mu < \mu_0$ fixed in
advance.

## Assumptions

- The observations are independent.
- $\sigma$ is known from outside the sample, for example from a long-running, stable measurement process.
- The data are normal, or $n$ is large enough for the central limit theorem to make $\bar X$ approximately normal.

The known $\sigma$ is the demanding assumption. When $\sigma$ is estimated from the same data, use the
<NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample t-test</NoteLink>.

## Test statistic

$Z$ is the difference between the sample mean and $\mu_0$ in units of the
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> $\sigma/\sqrt n$. It measures how many standard errors the
estimate lies from the null value.

**Null distribution.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Their sum is normal, because a
sum of independent normal variables is normal: the <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">moment-generating
function</NoteLink> of the sum is the product $\prod_i \exp(\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2) = \exp(n\mu t + n\sigma^2 t^2/2)$,
which is the moment-generating function of $\Gauss(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$. Dividing by $n$ gives

$$
\bar X \sim \Gauss\paren{\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}}.
$$

The mean is $\mu$ by linearity of expectation. The variance is $\sigma^2/n$ because variances of independent terms
add and scaling by $1/n$ multiplies a variance by $1/n^2$. Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation
of a normal variable gives a standard normal one, so under $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$,

$$
Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \sim \Gauss(0, 1).
$$

**Distribution under the alternative.** If instead $\mu = \mu_0 + \Delta$, write
$Z = (\bar X - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n) + \Delta/(\sigma/\sqrt n)$. The first term is standard normal, so
$Z \sim \Gauss(\Delta\sqrt n/\sigma, 1)$: the whole distribution shifts by the effect measured in standard errors.
The power of the two-sided test at level $\alpha$ is therefore

$$
P(\abs Z \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}) = 1 - \Phi\paren{z_{1-\alpha/2} - \frac{\Delta\sqrt n}{\sigma}}
+ \Phi\paren{-z_{1-\alpha/2} - \frac{\Delta\sqrt n}{\sigma}}.
$$

For data that are not normal, the central limit theorem makes $\bar X$ approximately normal with the same mean and
variance, and the same statements hold approximately for large $n$.

## Decision rule

With $\Phi$ the standard normal cdf and $z_q$ its $q$-quantile, at level $\alpha$:

- two-sided: reject when $\abs{z} \ge z_{1-\alpha/2}$, with p-value $2\paren{1 - \Phi(\abs z)}$;
- upper-tailed: reject when $z \ge z_{1-\alpha}$, with p-value $1 - \Phi(z)$.

The z-test is the likelihood ratio test for the normal mean with known variance. The upper-tailed version is uniformly
most powerful against $\mu > \mu_0$; see the <NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma">Neyman–Pearson lemma</NoteLink>.

## Example

A test is scaled so that scores have standard deviation $\sigma = 15$ in the population. A class of $n = 36$ pupils
averages $\bar x = 105$. Is its mean different from $\mu_0 = 100$?

$$
z = \frac{105 - 100}{15/\sqrt{36}} = \frac{5}{2.5} = 2.0, \qquad p = 2\paren{1 - \Phi(2.0)} = 0.046.
$$

The test rejects at $\alpha = 0.05$. The 95% interval is $105 \pm 1.96 \times 2.5 = [100.1, 109.9]$. If the true
difference were exactly 5 points, the statistic would be centred at $5/2.5 = 2$, and the power formula above gives
$1 - \Phi(1.96 - 2) + \Phi(-3.96) = 0.52$: a class of 36 detects it only about half the time. See
<NoteLink to="type-i-and-type-ii-errors">Type I and Type II errors</NoteLink>.

## Estimating σ

Replacing $\sigma$ with the sample standard deviation $s$ while keeping the normal critical value ignores the
uncertainty in $s$. For normal data the statistic then follows a t distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom, and the
test rejects true nulls with probability $P(\abs{T_{n-1}} \ge 1.96)$ instead of 0.05: 12% of the time at nominal 5% for
$n = 5$, 8% for $n = 10$ and 6% for $n = 30$. For large $n$ the difference disappears, which is why large-sample z-tests
with estimated standard errors are acceptable.

## Large-sample z-tests

Any estimator that is approximately normal with a known or well-estimated standard error gives a z-test.

- **One proportion:** $z = (\hat p - p_0)\big/\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n}$; see the
  <NoteLink to="binomial-test">binomial test</NoteLink> for the exact version.
- **Two proportions:** with pooled proportion $\bar p$ under $H_0: p_1 = p_2$,
  $z = (\hat p_1 - \hat p_2)\big/\sqrt{\bar p(1 - \bar p)(1/n_1 + 1/n_2)}$. Conversion rates of 12% and 10% with
  1,000 users each give $z = 1.43$ and $p = 0.15$.
- **Regression coefficients and maximum-likelihood estimates:** the Wald test
  $z = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)/\widehat{\text{se}}(\hat\theta)$.

## Effect size

Report the difference $\bar x - \mu_0$ with its confidence interval. The standardised effect is
$d = (\bar x - \mu_0)/\sigma$; for the class, $d = 5/15 = 0.33$.

## Pitfalls

- **σ from the same data.** It is a t-test in disguise, with inflated false positives when $n$ is small.
- **Dependence.** Clustered or autocorrelated observations make $\sigma/\sqrt n$ too small.
- **Small samples from skewed populations.** The normal approximation to $\bar X$ may be poor, especially for one-sided
  tests.
