<Definition>
The one-sample t-test compares the mean $\mu$ of a normal population with a null value $\mu_0$ when the standard
deviation $\sigma$ is unknown. With sample mean $\bar X$ and sample standard deviation $S$ from $n$ independent
observations, the statistic

$$
T = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}
$$

follows Student's t distribution with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom under $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ <Cite id="student1908" />.
</Definition>

## Hypotheses

$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$. The alternative is two-sided, $\mu \ne \mu_0$, unless a one-sided alternative was fixed in
advance.

## Assumptions

- The observations are independent.
- They come from a normal distribution, or $n$ is large enough for $\bar X$ to be approximately normal. The test is
  robust to mild skew for $n$ of about 20 or more. Heavy tails and outliers reduce its power.
- $\sigma$ is unknown and estimated from the same data. With a known $\sigma$, the
  <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink> applies.

## Test statistic

$S = \sqrt{\sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2/(n - 1)}$ estimates $\sigma$, and $S/\sqrt n$ estimates the standard error of
$\bar X$. $T$ is the distance from $\bar X$ to $\mu_0$ in estimated standard errors. Estimating $\sigma$ adds
uncertainty, which the t distribution accounts for with heavier tails than the normal.

**Null distribution.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Three facts hold:

1. $\bar X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$, so under $H_0$, $Z = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n) \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$;
   see the <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink>.
2. $V = (n - 1)S^2/\sigma^2$ has the <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">$\ChiSq_{n-1}$ distribution</NoteLink>.
3. $\bar X$ and $S^2$ are independent. This holds only for normal samples.

The <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student t distribution</NoteLink> with $\nu$ degrees of freedom is the
distribution of $Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$ for independent $Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ and $V \sim \ChiSq_\nu$. Substituting the two
pieces above,

$$
\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n - 1)}} = \frac{(\bar X - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}{S/\sigma} = \frac{\bar X - \mu_0}{S/\sqrt n} = T.
$$

The unknown $\sigma$ cancels, which is what makes $T$ usable as a test statistic. So $T \sim t_{n-1}$ under $H_0$
<Cite id="casella2002" />. The degrees of freedom are $n - 1$ rather than $n$ because $S^2$ measures spread around
$\bar X$, which is itself fitted to the data: the $n$ deviations $X_i - \bar X$ sum to zero, so only $n - 1$ of them are
free.

<Derivation title="Why the sample mean and variance are independent, and why V is χ²">
Let $\Qmat$ be an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix whose first row is $(1, \dots, 1)/\sqrt n$. Such a matrix exists:
complete the unit vector $\ones/\sqrt n$ to an orthonormal basis, for example with <NoteLink
to="gram-schmidt">Gram–Schmidt</NoteLink>. Standardise the data, $W_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, so that
$\wvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$, and rotate: $\yvec = \Qmat\wvec$.

- **The rotated vector is standard normal.** $\yvec$ is Gaussian with covariance
  $\Qmat\eye\Qmat^\top = \eye$. Its coordinates $Y_1, \dots, Y_n$ are therefore independent
  $\Gauss(0, 1)$.
- **The first coordinate carries the mean.** $Y_1 = \sum_i W_i/\sqrt n = \sqrt n\,(\bar X - \mu)/\sigma$.
- **The rest carry the spread.** Rotation preserves length, so $\sum_i Y_i^2 = \sum_i W_i^2$. Then
  $$
  \sum_{i=2}^n Y_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n W_i^2 - Y_1^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n W_i^2 - n \bar W^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (W_i - \bar W)^2
  = \frac{(n - 1)S^2}{\sigma^2}.
  $$

$\bar X$ is a function of $Y_1$ alone and $S^2$ is a function of $Y_2, \dots, Y_n$ alone. These are independent, which
proves fact 3. $V$ is a sum of $n - 1$ squared independent standard normals, which is the definition of $\ChiSq_{n-1}$,
proving fact 2 <Cite id="casella2002" />.
</Derivation>

## Decision rule

With $t_{q, \nu}$ the $q$-quantile of the t distribution with $\nu = n - 1$ degrees of freedom, reject $H_0$ at level
$\alpha$ when $\abs{t} \ge t_{1-\alpha/2, \nu}$. The two-sided p-value is $2 P(T_\nu \ge \abs t)$.

The critical values exceed the normal $1.96$ and approach it as $n$ grows:

| $n$ | 2 | 3 | 6 | 11 | 16 | 31 | 101 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $t_{0.975, n-1}$ | 12.71 | 4.30 | 2.57 | 2.23 | 2.13 | 2.04 | 1.98 |

The two-sided t-test is the generalised likelihood ratio test for the normal mean with unknown variance. It is
uniformly most powerful among unbiased tests <Cite id="lehmann2005" />.

## Example

Sixteen measurements have $\bar x = 5.2$ and $s = 1.2$. Test $H_0: \mu = 4.5$.

$$
t = \frac{5.2 - 4.5}{1.2/\sqrt{16}} = \frac{0.7}{0.3} = 2.33, \qquad \nu = 15, \qquad p = 0.034.
$$

The test rejects at $\alpha = 0.05$. The 95% interval, $5.2 \pm 2.131 \times 0.3 = [4.56, 5.84]$, excludes 4.5. A
z-test with the same numbers would report $p = 0.020$, overstating the evidence.

## Effect size

Report $\bar x - \mu_0$ with its <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink>. The standardised
effect is $d = (\bar x - \mu_0)/s$; in the example $d = 0.58$. Under $H_1$, $T$ has a noncentral t distribution with
noncentrality $\sqrt n\,(\mu - \mu_0)/\sigma$, which gives the power. With $n = 16$ and a true $d = 0.5$, power is 0.46.

## Pitfalls

- **Outliers.** One extreme value inflates $s$ and can hide a real shift, or create one. Inspect the data; for
  heavy-tailed data a Wilcoxon signed-rank test or a permutation test is more reliable.
- **Dependence.** Repeated measurements over time are often autocorrelated, which makes $s/\sqrt n$ too small.
- **Paired data.** Before-and-after measurements are analysed as a one-sample t-test on the differences; see the
  <NoteLink to="paired-t-test">paired t-test</NoteLink>.
