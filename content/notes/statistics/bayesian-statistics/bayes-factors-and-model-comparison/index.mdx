<Definition>
For two models $M_0$ and $M_1$ with parameters $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$, the **marginal likelihood** (evidence) of
model $M_j$ is $p(y \mid M_j) = \int p(y \mid \theta_j, M_j)\, p(\theta_j \mid M_j)\, d\theta_j$. The **Bayes
factor** is their ratio,

$$
B_{10} = \frac{p(y \mid M_1)}{p(y \mid M_0)}, \qquad
\frac{P(M_1 \mid y)}{P(M_0 \mid y)} = B_{10} \times \frac{P(M_1)}{P(M_0)}.
$$

It is the factor by which the data change the odds of $M_1$ against $M_0$ <Cite id="kass1995" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The marginal likelihood is the prior predictive probability of the observed data: the average of the likelihood over
the model's prior. A model that spreads its prior over many parameter values predicts many possible data sets, so it
gives each one a small probability. A simpler model makes sharper predictions. If the data fall where the simpler model
predicted, it wins. This automatic penalty for flexibility is the **Bayesian Occam's razor**. The price is that the
penalty depends on how widely the prior is spread, even over values the data rule out.

The posterior-odds formula follows from <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> applied to the model
index: $P(M_j \mid y) \propto p(y \mid M_j)\,P(M_j)$, and the normaliser cancels in the ratio.

Kass and Raftery <Cite id="kass1995" /> give a scale for reading $B_{10}$: 1 to 3 is barely worth mentioning, 3 to 20
positive, 20 to 150 strong, and above 150 very strong evidence for $M_1$.

## Worked example: testing a coin for fairness

$M_0$ fixes $\theta = 0.5$. $M_1$ gives $\theta$ a uniform prior. With $k$ heads in $n$ tosses,

$$
p(y \mid M_0) = \binom nk 2^{-n}, \qquad
p(y \mid M_1) = \int_0^1 \binom nk \theta^k(1 - \theta)^{n - k}\, d\theta = \binom nk B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = \frac{1}{n + 1}.
$$

The last step uses $B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = k!\,(n - k)!/(n + 1)!$. Under $M_1$ every $k$ from 0 to $n$ is equally
likely. For outcomes whose two-sided <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink>, from the normal approximation to the binomial, is about 0.05:

| $n$ | $k$ | $z$ | two-sided $p$ | $B_{01}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 | 60 | 2.00 | 0.046 | 1.1 |
| 1,000 | 531 | 1.96 | 0.050 | 3.7 |
| 10,000 | 5,098 | 1.96 | 0.050 | 11.7 |
| 100,000 | 50,310 | 1.96 | 0.050 | 36.9 |

Each row rejects $M_0$ at the 5% level, yet the Bayes factor favours $M_0$, and more strongly as $n$ grows.

## Lindley's paradox

The table follows a formula. With $z = (k - n/2)/\sqrt{n/4}$, the normal approximation to the binomial gives
$\binom nk 2^{-n} \approx \phi(z)/\sqrt{n/4}$, where $\phi$ is the standard normal density. Therefore

$$
B_{01} = (n + 1)\binom nk 2^{-n} \approx \frac{2(n + 1)}{\sqrt n}\,\phi(z) \approx \sqrt{\frac{2n}{\pi}}\; e^{-z^2/2}.
$$

At fixed $z$, and so at a fixed p-value, $B_{01}$ grows like $\sqrt n$. For any <NoteLink to="significance-level">significance level</NoteLink> there is a sample
size at which a result that is just significant is strong evidence for the null. This is **Lindley's paradox**
<Cite id="lindley1957" />.

The two methods answer different questions. The p-value measures how surprising the data are under $M_0$ alone. The
Bayes factor compares $M_0$ with a specific alternative. When $n$ is large, $\hat\theta = k/n$ is within about
$1/\sqrt n$ of 0.5. The uniform prior of $M_1$ spreads its mass over $[0, 1]$ and assigns only about $1/\sqrt n$ of
it to values that close to 0.5, so $M_1$ predicted the data poorly.

## Sensitivity to the prior

For a normal mean with known $\sigma$, test $M_0: \mu = 0$ against $M_1: \mu \sim \Gauss(0, \tau^2)$. The sample mean
$\bar y$ is sufficient, with $\bar y \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2/n)$ under $M_0$ and $\bar y \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2/n +
\tau^2)$ under $M_1$. The ratio of these two densities at $\bar y$, with $z = \sqrt n\,\bar y/\sigma$, is

$$
B_{01} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{n\tau^2}{\sigma^2}}\; \exp\paren{-\frac{z^2}{2}\cdot\frac{n\tau^2}{\sigma^2 + n\tau^2}}.
$$

As $\tau \to \infty$ the exponential tends to $e^{-z^2/2}$ and the square root grows without bound, so $B_{01} \to
\infty$ for any data. With $n = 100$, $\sigma = 1$ and $z = 1.96$, the prior scales $\tau = 0.1, 1, 10, 100$ give
$B_{01} = 0.54, 1.5, 14.7, 146$. The same data favour $M_1$ or strongly favour $M_0$ depending on a prior scale that
barely changes the posterior for $\mu$ under $M_1$.

Consequences:

- An <NoteLink to="prior-choice">improper prior</NoteLink>, $p(\theta) \propto 1$, has an arbitrary constant. The constant cancels in a posterior but not in
  a marginal likelihood. Bayes factors need proper priors on every parameter that is not shared by both models.
- Bayes factors are only as meaningful as the priors within the models. Report how $B_{10}$ changes over a range of
  reasonable priors.
- $-2\log B_{10}$ is approximated by the difference in BIC, which corresponds to a unit-information prior; see
  <NoteLink to="information-criteria">information criteria</NoteLink>.

## Predictive model comparison: LOO and WAIC

An alternative is to compare models by how well they predict new data. The **expected log pointwise predictive
density** of a model is estimated by leave-one-out <NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink>:

$$
\widehat{\text{elpd}}_{\text{loo}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(y_i \mid y_{-i}), \qquad
p(y_i \mid y_{-i}) = \int p(y_i \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid y_{-i})\, d\theta,
$$

where $y_{-i}$ is the data without observation $i$. Refitting $n$ times is expensive. From $S$ draws of the full
posterior, $p(y_i \mid y_{-i})$ can be estimated by <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> with weights $1/p(y_i \mid \theta^{(s)})$.
**PSIS-LOO** stabilises these weights by fitting a <NoteLink to="extreme-value-theory-for-anomalies">generalised Pareto distribution</NoteLink> to the largest ones; the fitted shape
$\hat k$ above 0.7 flags observations where the estimate is unreliable <Cite id="vehtari2017" />.

The **widely applicable information criterion** <Cite id="watanabe2010" /> is

$$
\text{WAIC} = -2\paren{\sum_{i=1}^n \log \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^S p(y_i \mid \theta^{(s)}) - \sum_{i=1}^n \var_s \log p(y_i \mid
\theta^{(s)})},
$$

where the first sum is the log pointwise predictive density on the observed data and the second, the posterior variance
of each point's log-likelihood, is the <NoteLink to="effective-degrees-of-freedom">effective number of parameters</NoteLink>. WAIC is asymptotically equal to LOO and remains
valid for singular models such as mixtures and neural networks, where AIC and BIC do not apply.

Predictive criteria and Bayes factors differ in how they treat priors. LOO and WAIC use the posterior, so a vague prior
that the data overwhelm hardly affects them. The Bayes factor uses the <NoteLink to="bayesian-inference">prior predictive distribution</NoteLink>, so it is
sensitive to vague priors.

## Assumptions

- Both models' priors are proper and chosen with the same care as the likelihoods.
- For posterior model probabilities, the candidate set contains a model close to the truth. When every model is wrong,
  Bayes factors still pick one with increasing confidence as $n$ grows; predictive criteria are then the better target.

## Pitfalls

- **Default vague priors.** A wide prior under the alternative inflates $B_{01}$. Choose the scale from the problem.
- **Computing the marginal likelihood from posterior draws.** The harmonic-mean estimator
  $\paren{\frac1S\sum_s 1/p(y \mid \theta^{(s)})}^{-1}$ has infinite variance in common models. Use bridge sampling,
  nested sampling or a closed form.
- **Treating $B_{10}$ as a posterior probability.** It becomes one only after multiplying by prior odds.
- **Comparing WAIC or LOO values without their <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard errors</NoteLink>.** Both are sums over observations; compare the
  pointwise differences and their standard error.
