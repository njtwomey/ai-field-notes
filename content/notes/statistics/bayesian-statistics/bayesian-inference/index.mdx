<Definition>
**Bayesian inference** describes an unknown parameter $\theta$ by a probability distribution. A **prior** $p(\theta)$
states what is known before the data $y$. A **likelihood** $p(y \mid \theta)$ states how the data arise for each
$\theta$. The **posterior**

$$
p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(y)}, \qquad p(y) = \int p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta,
$$

is the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> of $\theta$ given the observed $y$.
Every conclusion (an estimate, an interval, a prediction, a decision) is a summary of the posterior
<Cite id="gelman2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A Bayesian model is a joint distribution $p(y, \theta) = p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$ over data and parameters.
Observing $y$ fixes one slice of that joint distribution. The posterior is that slice, renormalised to integrate to 1.
The normalising constant $p(y)$ does not depend on $\theta$, so the shape of the posterior is the product of prior and
likelihood:

$$
p(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta).
$$

Where the likelihood is flat, the posterior follows the prior. Where the likelihood is sharp, the posterior follows the
data. As the number of independent observations $n$ grows, the log-likelihood is a sum of $n$ terms and the log-prior
is one term, so the data dominate any prior that puts positive density near the true value.

The frequentist view treats $\theta$ as fixed and the data as random, and judges procedures by their behaviour over
repeated samples. The Bayesian view conditions on the one observed data set and makes probability statements about
$\theta$ directly. A 95% <NoteLink to="credible-intervals-and-highest-posterior-density">credible interval</NoteLink>
contains $\theta$ with posterior probability 0.95. A 95% <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence
interval</NoteLink> comes from a procedure that covers $\theta$ in 95% of repeated samples.

## Sequential updating

Data can be processed in batches. Suppose $y_1$ and $y_2$ are conditionally independent given $\theta$. Then

$$
p(\theta \mid y_1, y_2) \propto p(y_2 \mid \theta)\, p(y_1 \mid \theta)\, p(\theta) \propto p(y_2 \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid y_1).
$$

The posterior after $y_1$ is the prior for $y_2$. The order of the batches does not matter, and updating once with all
the data gives the same posterior as updating in steps.

## The posterior predictive distribution

A prediction for a new observation $\tilde y$ must account for the uncertainty in $\theta$. Assume $\tilde y$ and $y$
are conditionally independent given $\theta$, so that $p(\tilde y \mid \theta, y) = p(\tilde y \mid \theta)$.
Marginalising $\theta$ out of the joint posterior of $(\tilde y, \theta)$ gives

$$
p(\tilde y \mid y) = \int p(\tilde y, \theta \mid y)\, d\theta = \int p(\tilde y \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid y)\, d\theta.
$$

This is the **posterior predictive distribution**. It is a mixture of the sampling distributions $p(\tilde y \mid
\theta)$, weighted by the posterior. By the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total variance</NoteLink>,

$$
\var(\tilde y \mid y) = \expect\brack{\var(\tilde y \mid \theta) \mid y} + \var\brack{\expect(\tilde y \mid \theta) \mid y}.
$$

The first term is the noise of a single observation. The second is the uncertainty about $\theta$. A **plug-in**
prediction $p(\tilde y \mid \hat\theta)$ at a point estimate $\hat\theta$ keeps only the first term, so its
intervals are too narrow when $n$ is small.

Before the data are seen, the same integral with the prior in place of the posterior gives the **prior predictive
distribution** $p(y) = \int p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta$. It is the distribution of data sets the model
considers plausible, and it is also the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">marginal likelihood</NoteLink> used in
<NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factors</NoteLink>.

## Worked example: a coin

A coin lands heads with unknown probability $\theta \in [0, 1]$. The prior is $\theta \sim \Beta(2, 2)$, which has
mean 0.5 and mild preference for values near the middle. In $n = 10$ tosses the coin shows $k = 7$ heads. The
likelihood is $p(k \mid \theta) = \binom{10}{7}\theta^7(1 - \theta)^3$. Multiplying by the prior density
$\theta(1 - \theta)/B(2, 2)$ and dropping every factor that does not involve $\theta$,

$$
p(\theta \mid k) \propto \theta^{7}(1 - \theta)^{3} \cdot \theta^{1}(1 - \theta)^{1} = \theta^{8}(1 - \theta)^{4}.
$$

This is the kernel of the $\Beta(9, 5)$ density, so the posterior is $\Beta(9, 5)$; the
<NoteLink to="conjugate-priors">conjugate priors</NoteLink> note derives this update in general. Its mean is
$9/14 = 0.643$. The <Gloss name="mle">maximum-likelihood</Gloss> estimate is $7/10 = 0.7$. The prior pulls the estimate
toward 0.5 by the weight of four pseudo-observations. The central 95% posterior interval is $[0.386, 0.861]$.

The probability that the next five tosses are all heads is a posterior predictive probability:

$$
P(\text{5 heads} \mid k) = \int_0^1 \theta^5\, p(\theta \mid k)\, d\theta = \frac{B(14, 5)}{B(9, 5)}
= \frac{9 \cdot 10 \cdot 11 \cdot 12 \cdot 13}{14 \cdot 15 \cdot 16 \cdot 17 \cdot 18} = 0.150.
$$

Plugging the posterior mean into the sampling model gives $0.643^5 = 0.110$, below the posterior predictive
probability 0.150. The average of $\theta^5$ exceeds the fifth power of the average of $\theta$, because $\theta^5$ is
convex (Jensen's inequality).

For the number of heads $\tilde k$ in the next 10 tosses, the expected binomial variance is
$\expect[10\,\theta(1 - \theta) \mid k] = 2.14$. The uncertainty about $\theta$ adds
$\var(10\,\theta \mid k) = 100 \times 0.0153 = 1.53$. The posterior predictive variance is their sum, 3.67. The plug-in
binomial variance at $\theta = 0.643$ is only 2.30.

## Computing the posterior

The posterior has a closed form only for <NoteLink to="conjugate-priors">conjugate</NoteLink> pairs and a few other
models. Otherwise the integral $p(y)$ is intractable, and the posterior is approximated:

- **Grid approximation** evaluates prior times likelihood on a grid of $\theta$ values and normalises. It works for one
  or two parameters.
- **<NoteLink to="laplace-approximation">Laplace approximation</NoteLink>** fits a Gaussian at the posterior mode, with
  covariance equal to the inverse <NoteLink to="hessian">Hessian</NoteLink> of $-\log p(\theta \mid y)$.
- <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> draws dependent samples whose
  distribution converges to the posterior. It needs only the unnormalised density $p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$.
- <NoteLink to="variational-inference">Variational inference</NoteLink> fits the closest member of a tractable family
  by optimisation.

With draws $\theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(S)}$ from the posterior, every summary is a Monte Carlo average: the posterior
mean is $\frac1S\sum_s \theta^{(s)}$, and a posterior predictive draw is $\tilde y^{(s)} \sim p(\tilde y \mid
\theta^{(s)})$. <NoteLink to="probabilistic-programming">Probabilistic programming</NoteLink> languages automate this.

## The Bayesian workflow

Fitting one model is one step of an iterative process <Cite id="gelman2020" /> <Cite id="mcelreath2020" />:

1. **Build the model.** Write the likelihood and priors. Simulate data from the prior predictive distribution. If the
   simulated data are implausible (negative counts, heights of 10 metres), the priors are wrong.
2. **Fit.** Compute or sample the posterior. For MCMC, check convergence with
   <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo-diagnostics">MCMC diagnostics</NoteLink> before reading any result.
3. **Check.** A **posterior predictive check** draws replicated data sets $y^{\text{rep}}$ from $p(y^{\text{rep}} \mid
   y)$ and compares a test statistic $T(y^{\text{rep}})$ with $T(y)$. The **posterior predictive p-value**
   $P(T(y^{\text{rep}}) \ge T(y) \mid y)$ near 0 or 1 shows a feature of the data that the model does not reproduce.
4. **Compare and expand.** Change the model where the checks fail. Compare candidates by predictive accuracy
   (<NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">LOO and WAIC</NoteLink>) and report how conclusions change.

## Assumptions

- The likelihood is a correct description of how the data arise, up to the unknown $\theta$. The posterior is only as
  good as the model.
- The prior is proper, or the posterior is checked to be proper; see <NoteLink to="prior-choice">prior
  choice</NoteLink>.
- The posterior predictive formula assumes new data are exchangeable with the observed data given $\theta$.

## Pitfalls

- **Reporting only the posterior mode.** The mode (the <Gloss name="map-estimate" /> estimate) is not invariant to
  reparameterisation and ignores the spread of the posterior. Report intervals or the whole distribution.
- **Reading a credible interval as a confidence interval.** A credible interval conditions on the data; its coverage
  over repeated samples depends on the prior and is not guaranteed.
- **Plug-in prediction.** Predicting with a point estimate drops the parameter uncertainty and gives intervals that are
  too narrow.
- **Unchecked computation.** An MCMC run that has not converged returns numbers that look like a posterior but are not.
