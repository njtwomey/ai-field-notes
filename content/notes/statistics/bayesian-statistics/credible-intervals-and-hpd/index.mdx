<Definition>
A $(1 - \alpha)$ **credible set** for a parameter $\theta$ is a set $C$ with posterior probability
$P(\theta \in C \mid y) = 1 - \alpha$. The **equal-tailed** (central) interval $[q_{\alpha/2}, q_{1-\alpha/2}]$ runs
between two posterior quantiles. The **highest posterior density** (HPD) region is
$C_c = \set{\theta : p(\theta \mid y) \ge c}$, with $c$ chosen so that $C_c$ has probability $1 - \alpha$
<Cite id="gelman2013" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A credible interval is a statement about $\theta$ given the data: with the stated prior and model, $\theta$ lies in
the interval with probability $1 - \alpha$. Many intervals hold the same posterior mass. The equal-tailed interval is
the easiest to compute, from two quantiles or two order statistics of posterior draws. The HPD region is the most
compact: it is built by lowering a horizontal line through the density until the region above it holds mass
$1 - \alpha$. For a symmetric unimodal posterior the two coincide. For a skewed posterior the HPD region shifts toward
the mode and is shorter.

## The HPD region is the shortest

**Claim.** Among all sets with posterior probability $1 - \alpha$, the HPD region $R = C_c$ has the smallest length
(volume, in several dimensions).

**Proof.** Let $S$ be any other set with $P(S \mid y) = P(R \mid y) = 1 - \alpha$, and write $\abs{\cdot}$ for length.
Removing the common part $R \cap S$ from both sets leaves equal masses: $P(R \setminus S \mid y) = P(S \setminus R \mid
y)$. On $R \setminus S$ the density is at least $c$, so $P(R \setminus S \mid y) \ge c\abs{R \setminus S}$. On $S
\setminus R$ the density is below $c$, so $P(S \setminus R \mid y) \le c\abs{S \setminus R}$. Combining,
$c\abs{R \setminus S} \le c\abs{S \setminus R}$, hence $\abs{R} \le \abs{S}$.

For a unimodal density the HPD region is an interval $[l, u]$. Minimising $u - l$ subject to $F(u) - F(l) = 1 - \alpha$,
with $F$ the posterior cdf, a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ gives $1 = \lambda p(u \mid y)$ and $1 = \lambda p(l \mid
y)$. The endpoints of the shortest interval have equal density, $p(l \mid y) = p(u \mid y)$. For a multimodal
posterior the HPD region can be a union of disjoint intervals, which the equal-tailed interval never is.

## Worked example

With a uniform prior on a success probability $\theta$ and 1 success in 12 trials, the posterior is $\Beta(2, 12)$.
Its mode is $1/12 = 0.083$ and its mean is $2/14 = 0.143$.

| 95% interval | Endpoints | Width |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equal-tailed | $[0.019, 0.360]$ | 0.341 |
| HPD | $[0.005, 0.319]$ | 0.314 |

The HPD endpoints both have density 0.729. The equal-tailed interval excludes $[0, 0.019]$, where the density is higher
than at 0.36.

With 0 successes in 10 trials the posterior is $\Beta(1, 11)$, whose density decreases from $\theta = 0$. The 95% HPD
interval is $[0, 0.238]$, one-sided. The equal-tailed interval $[0.002, 0.285]$ excludes the most probable values.

## Invariance

Let $\phi = g(\theta)$ with $g$ strictly increasing. Quantiles transform with $g$: $P(\phi \le g(q) \mid y) =
P(\theta \le q \mid y)$. The equal-tailed interval for $\phi$ is therefore $[g(q_{\alpha/2}), g(q_{1-\alpha/2})]$, the
transformed interval for $\theta$. HPD regions do not transform this way. The density of $\phi$ is
$p(\theta \mid y)/g'(\theta)$, and the Jacobian changes which values have the highest density. The HPD interval for a
log-odds is not the log-odds of the HPD interval for a probability. Report equal-tailed intervals when readers will
reparameterise, and HPD intervals when the shortest summary on a fixed scale matters.

## Credible and confidence intervals

A <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> is a procedure that covers the fixed $\theta$ in a
fraction $1 - \alpha$ of repeated samples. A credible interval conditions on the observed sample and needs a prior.

**When they agree numerically.** Let $y_1, \dots, y_n \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known and a flat prior
on $\mu$. The posterior is $\Gauss(\bar y, \sigma^2/n)$, and the 95% credible interval is $\bar y \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt
n$, the same numbers as the z confidence interval. More generally, the Bernstein–von Mises theorem states that for
regular models with a prior that is positive and continuous at the true value, the posterior approaches a Gaussian
centred on the maximum-likelihood estimate with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information. The two intervals
then agree to first order as $n \to \infty$.

**Coverage averaged over the prior.** If $\theta$ is drawn from the prior and $y$ from the model, a $(1 - \alpha)$
credible set covers $\theta$ with probability exactly $1 - \alpha$:

$$
P(\theta \in C(y)) = \int P\paren{\theta \in C(y) \mid y}\, p(y)\, dy = \int (1 - \alpha)\, p(y)\, dy = 1 - \alpha.
$$

For a fixed $\theta$ the coverage can be higher or lower, depending on how well the prior matches $\theta$.

**When they differ.** For 0 successes in 10 trials, the 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence interval is $[0, 0.308]$ and the
95% HPD credible interval under a uniform prior is $[0, 0.238]$. The confidence interval guarantees coverage for every
$\theta$; the credible interval uses the prior to be shorter.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading a confidence interval as a credible interval.** "$\theta$ lies in $[a, b]$ with probability 0.95" is a
  posterior statement. It needs a prior and a model.
- **Monte Carlo error.** Interval endpoints estimated from $S$ posterior draws are noisy, and tail quantiles are
  noisier than the median. Tail quantiles need thousands of effective draws.
- **HPD from samples.** Estimating an HPD interval from draws takes the shortest window containing a fraction
  $1 - \alpha$ of the sorted draws. It is unstable for flat or multimodal posteriors.
- **Prior sensitivity.** With little data the interval reflects the prior. Report the prior, and the interval under an
  alternative prior when it matters.
