<Definition>
The **prior** $p(\theta)$ is the distribution of a parameter before the data are seen. It is part of the model, with the
likelihood. Priors are commonly classed as **informative** (encoding substantive knowledge), **weakly informative**
(excluding implausible values while leaving the data to decide among plausible ones), and **reference** or **formal**
priors built by a rule, such as the **Jeffreys prior** $p(\theta) \propto \sqrt{\det I(\theta)}$, where $I(\theta)$ is
the <Gloss name="fisher-information">Fisher information</Gloss> <Cite id="kass1996" />. A prior is **improper** if it
does not integrate to a finite value.
</Definition>

## Intuition

No prior is neutral. A uniform prior on a probability $\theta$ is not uniform on the log-odds $\log\frac{\theta}{1 -
\theta}$: the <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink> gives the log-odds a logistic density,
peaked at 0. "Flat" depends on the parameterisation. Formal rules such as Jeffreys' remove that arbitrariness by
producing the same prior whatever parameterisation is used. Weakly informative priors take a different route: they
accept that the prior matters and use it to rule out values that are impossible on scientific grounds, such as an effect
of a school coaching programme of 500 test points.

With plenty of data the choice rarely matters, because the log-likelihood grows with $n$ and the log-prior does not.
It matters when data are few, when a parameter is weakly identified, for scale parameters of
<NoteLink to="hierarchical-models">hierarchical models</NoteLink>, and always for
<NoteLink to="bayes-factors-and-model-comparison">Bayes factors</NoteLink>.

## The Jeffreys prior

**Invariance.** Let $\phi = g(\theta)$ be a smooth one-to-one reparameterisation of a scalar parameter. The Fisher
information $I(\theta) = \expect\brack{\paren{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(y \mid \theta)}^2}$ transforms by
the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>:
$\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\log p = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p\cdot\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}$, so

$$
I_\phi(\phi) = I_\theta(\theta)\paren{\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}}^2, \qquad
\sqrt{I_\phi(\phi)} = \sqrt{I_\theta(\theta)}\,\abs{\frac{d\theta}{d\phi}}.
$$

The right-hand identity is exactly the change-of-variables rule for densities. So if $\theta$ has prior density
$\propto\sqrt{I_\theta(\theta)}$, then $\phi$ has density $\propto\sqrt{I_\phi(\phi)}$. Applying Jeffreys' rule in
either parameterisation gives the same prior <Cite id="jeffreys1946" />.

**Bernoulli.** For one observation $y \in \set{0, 1}$ with $p(y \mid \theta) = \theta^y(1 - \theta)^{1 - y}$,

$$
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log p(y \mid \theta) = -\frac{y}{\theta^2} - \frac{1 - y}{(1 - \theta)^2}.
$$

The Fisher information is minus its <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>. With $\expect[y] = \theta$,

$$
I(\theta) = \frac{\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{1 - \theta}{(1 - \theta)^2} = \frac1\theta + \frac{1}{1 - \theta} =
\frac{1}{\theta(1 - \theta)}.
$$

The Jeffreys prior is $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{-1/2}(1 - \theta)^{-1/2}$, the $\Beta(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$
distribution. It puts more mass near 0 and 1, where a single observation carries the most information about $\theta$.

Other standard cases: for a normal mean with known variance, $I(\mu) = 1/\sigma^2$ is constant and the Jeffreys prior is
flat. For a normal <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>, $I(\sigma) = 2/\sigma^2$ and
the prior is $p(\sigma) \propto 1/\sigma$, flat on $\log\sigma$. For a Poisson rate,
$p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-1/2}$. In several dimensions the square root of the
<NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> can behave badly, and reference priors <Cite id="kass1996" /> handle
nuisance parameters more carefully.

## Improper priors

A prior such as $p(\mu) \propto 1$ on $\reals$ is not a probability distribution, but $p(y \mid \mu)\,p(\mu)$ can
still integrate to a finite value, and the posterior is then a proper distribution. The posterior must be checked.

- The flat prior on a normal mean gives the proper posterior $\Gauss(\bar y, \sigma^2/n)$ after one observation.
- The **Haldane prior** $\Beta(0, 0)$, $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{-1}(1 - \theta)^{-1}$, gives posterior $\Beta(k, n -
  k)$. With $k = 0$ successes this is $\Beta(0, n)$, whose density $\propto \theta^{-1}(1 - \theta)^{n-1}$ is not
  integrable at 0. The posterior does not exist.
- A flat prior on $\log\tau$ for the group-level standard deviation of a hierarchical model gives an improper
  posterior, because the likelihood stays positive as $\tau \to 0$ <Cite id="gelman2006" />.

<Gloss name="mcmc" /> software samples from an improper posterior without complaint and returns meaningless draws.
Improper priors are also unusable in Bayes factors, because their arbitrary constant does not cancel.

## Weakly informative priors

A weakly informative prior is proper and wider than the actual knowledge, but narrow enough to exclude values that are
absurd. It regularises: it stabilises estimates when the likelihood is flat in some direction, such as a logistic
regression with separable classes, where the <Gloss name="mle">maximum-likelihood</Gloss> coefficient is infinite.
Gelman et al. <Cite id="gelman2008" /> recommend, for <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>
with inputs scaled to standard deviation 0.5, independent Cauchy priors with centre 0 and scale 2.5 on the coefficients.
A coefficient of 5 on that scale changes a probability from 0.08 to 0.92 across two standard deviations of the input.

**Vague is not the same as uninformative.** A $\Gauss(0, 10^2)$ prior on the intercept of a logistic regression is
vague. Transformed to the probability scale, it puts 65% of its mass on probabilities below 0.01 or above 0.99. A
$\Gauss(0, 1.5^2)$ prior puts 90% of its mass between 0.08 and 0.92.

**Scale parameters.** For a standard deviation, a half-normal or half-Student-t prior with a scale set from the
problem keeps mass away from implausibly large values and allows values near 0 <Cite id="gelman2006" />.

**Prior predictive checks.** The effect of a prior on a complex model is easiest to judge on the data scale. Draw
$\theta$ from the prior, simulate a data set from the likelihood, and check that the simulations cover plausible data
and little else.

## Worked example: zero failures

A procedure has 0 failures in $n = 10$ attempts. The posterior for the failure probability $\theta$ depends on the
prior, because the data are few and all on one side:

| Prior | Posterior | Posterior mean | 95% upper bound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uniform, $\Beta(1, 1)$ | $\Beta(1, 11)$ | 0.083 | 0.238 |
| Jeffreys, $\Beta(\frac12, \frac12)$ | $\Beta(\frac12, 10\frac12)$ | 0.045 | 0.171 |
| Haldane, $\Beta(0, 0)$ | $\Beta(0, 10)$, improper | none | none |
| Informative, $\Beta(2, 18)$ | $\Beta(2, 28)$ | 0.067 | 0.153 |

The upper bound is the 0.95 posterior quantile. The informative prior encodes a failure rate near 10% from 20 earlier
attempts. Its posterior is narrower than the uniform prior's because it carries more information, and its mean is
lower than 0.1 because the ten new attempts all succeeded.

## Assumptions

- Formal rules assume the likelihood is correct; the Jeffreys prior depends on the sampling model, so it changes if
  the stopping rule or design changes.
- A weakly informative prior assumes the analyst can state an order of magnitude for each parameter on a known scale.
  Standardising inputs and outputs makes this possible.

## Pitfalls

- **Improper posteriors.** Check that the posterior integrates, analytically or by the behaviour of the sampler.
- **Conjugate "noninformative" limits.** $\InvGamma(\epsilon, \epsilon)$ on a variance with small $\epsilon$ is close
  to the improper $1/\sigma^2$ prior and can dominate the posterior near 0.
- **Choosing the prior after seeing the data.** A prior tuned to the data counts the data twice.
- **No sensitivity analysis.** When the data are weak, report the posterior under at least two reasonable priors.
