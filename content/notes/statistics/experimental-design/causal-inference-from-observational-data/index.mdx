<Definition>
**Causal inference from observational data** estimates the effect of a treatment $W$ on an outcome $Y$ when the
treatment was not assigned at random. A **confounder** is a variable that affects both treatment and outcome. With
potential outcomes $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$, the average treatment effect $\tau = \expect[Y(1) - Y(0)]$ is identified from
observational data if a set of measured covariates $X$ satisfies **unconfoundedness**, $(Y(0), Y(1)) \perp W \mid X$,
and **positivity**, $0 < P(W = 1 \mid X = x) < 1$ for every $x$ <Cite id="imbens2015" /> <Cite id="hernan2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

In a <NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">randomised experiment</NoteLink>, treatment is assigned
independently of the units, so the groups are comparable. In observational data, the people who chose or were given
treatment differ from those who were not: sicker patients get the aggressive therapy, keener users adopt the new
feature. The raw comparison mixes the effect of treatment with the effect of those differences. If all the differences
that matter are measured, the comparison can be made within groups of units that share the same values of those
variables. Within each such group treatment is "as if random", and the group-level effects can be averaged.

## The adjustment formula

Assume unconfoundedness given $X$, positivity, and consistency ($Y = Y(w)$ when $W = w$). For each treatment level $w$,

$$
\expect[Y(w)] = \expect_X\brack{\expect[Y(w) \mid X]} = \expect_X\brack{\expect[Y(w) \mid X, W = w]} =
\expect_X\brack{\expect[Y \mid X, W = w]}.
$$

The first step is the law of total <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>. The second uses unconfoundedness: given $X$, $Y(w)$ does not depend on
$W$. The third uses consistency. Positivity makes $\expect[Y \mid X = x, W = w]$ defined for every $x$. Therefore

$$
\tau = \sum_x P(X = x)\paren{\expect[Y \mid X = x, W = 1] - \expect[Y \mid X = x, W = 0]},
$$

with an integral for continuous $X$. Every quantity on the right is observable. The formula weights the within-stratum
effects by the stratum's share of the **whole** population, not of the treated group.

## Which variables to adjust for

Pearl's **back-door criterion** selects the variables to adjust for from a causal graph (a <NoteLink to="bayesian-network">directed acyclic
graph</NoteLink> with an arrow from each cause to its effects) <Cite id="pearl2009" />. A back-door path is a path from
$W$ to $Y$ that starts with an arrow into $W$. A set $Z$ satisfies the criterion if

1. no variable in $Z$ is a descendant of $W$, and
2. $Z$ blocks every back-door path from $W$ to $Y$.

A path is blocked by $Z$ if it contains a non-collider in $Z$ (a chain $A \to B \to C$ or fork $A \leftarrow B \to C$
with $B \in Z$), or a **collider** $A \to B \leftarrow C$ such that neither $B$ nor any of its descendants is in $Z$.
Then $P(y \mid \operatorname{do}(w)) = \sum_z P(y \mid w, z)\,P(z)$, the adjustment formula.

The criterion rules out three common mistakes:

- **Adjusting for a mediator.** A variable on the causal path $W \to M \to Y$ is a descendant of $W$. Conditioning on
  it removes part of the effect being estimated.
- **Adjusting for a collider.** If $W \to C \leftarrow Y$ or $W \to C \leftarrow U \to Y$, conditioning on $C$ opens a
  path and creates bias where there was none. Selecting a sample on $C$ does the same.
- **Adjusting for everything available.** More covariates are not always safer; the right set depends on the graph.

## Propensity scores

The **propensity score** is $e(x) = P(W = 1 \mid X = x)$ <Cite id="rosenbaum1983" />.

**Balancing property.** $W \perp X \mid e(X)$. Proof: $P(W = 1 \mid X, e(X)) = P(W = 1 \mid X) = e(X)$, because $e(X)$ is
a function of $X$. Averaging over $X$ given $e(X)$ gives $P(W = 1 \mid e(X)) = \expect[e(X) \mid e(X)] = e(X)$. The two
agree, so given $e(X)$ the treatment carries no further information about $X$.

**Sufficiency for adjustment.** If unconfoundedness holds given $X$, it holds given $e(X)$ alone:

$$
P\paren{W = 1 \mid Y(0), Y(1), e(X)} = \expect\brack{P(W = 1 \mid Y(0), Y(1), X) \mid Y(0), Y(1), e(X)} =
\expect\brack{e(X) \mid Y(0), Y(1), e(X)} = e(X).
$$

The middle step uses unconfoundedness given $X$. A high-dimensional $X$ can be replaced by one number for matching or
stratification.

**Inverse propensity weighting.** Weighting each treated unit by $1/e(X)$ recovers the mean treated outcome of the whole
population:

$$
\expect\brack{\frac{WY}{e(X)}} = \expect\brack{\frac{W\,Y(1)}{e(X)}} = \expect\brack{\frac{\expect[W \mid X, Y(1)]\,Y(1)}{e(X)}}
= \expect\brack{\frac{e(X)\,Y(1)}{e(X)}} = \expect[Y(1)],
$$

where the third step uses unconfoundedness, $\expect[W \mid X, Y(1)] = e(X)$. Similarly $\expect[(1 - W)Y/(1 - e(X))] =
\expect[Y(0)]$. The same weights appear in <NoteLink to="reweighting-biased-samples">reweighting biased samples</NoteLink>
and in <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink>. Doubly robust estimators combine the
weights with an outcome regression and stay consistent if either model is correct.

## Worked example

A hospital records 600 patients. Severity is the only confounder: severe cases are more often treated and recover less
often.

| Severity | Treated: recovered / total | Untreated: recovered / total | Effect in stratum | $e(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mild (300) | 81 / 90 = 0.90 | 168 / 210 = 0.80 | $+0.10$ | 0.30 |
| Severe (300) | 126 / 210 = 0.60 | 45 / 90 = 0.50 | $+0.10$ | 0.70 |
| All (600) | 207 / 300 = 0.69 | 213 / 300 = 0.71 | $-0.02$ | |

The crude comparison says treatment lowers recovery by 2 points. Within each severity level it raises recovery by 10
points. The adjustment formula gives $0.5 \times 0.10 + 0.5 \times 0.10 = 0.10$. Inverse propensity weighting gives the
same answer: the weighted treated mean is $\frac{1}{600}\paren{81/0.3 + 126/0.7} = 0.75$, the weighted untreated mean is
$\frac{1}{600}\paren{168/0.7 + 45/0.3} = 0.65$, and the difference is 0.10. This reversal is an instance of
<NoteLink to="simpsons-paradox">Simpson's paradox</NoteLink>. Whether the adjusted or crude figure is right depends on the
causal graph: severity is a cause of treatment, not a consequence, so it is a confounder and adjustment is correct.

## Assumptions

- **Unconfoundedness** (no unmeasured confounding). It cannot be tested from the data. It is a substantive claim that
  every common cause of treatment and outcome has been measured.
- **Positivity.** Every covariate pattern has a chance of both treatments. Propensities near 0 or 1 give huge weights,
  high variance, and results that rest on extrapolation.
- **Consistency and no interference.** Each unit's observed outcome is its potential outcome under the treatment it
  received, and does not depend on others' treatments.
- **Correct models.** Regression and propensity models must be flexible enough; a misspecified model leaves residual
  confounding.

## When unconfoundedness is not credible

Designs that use other sources of variation in treatment include **instrumental variables** (a variable that shifts treatment
but affects the outcome only through it), **difference-in-differences** (parallel trends before and after a change),
and **regression discontinuity** (treatment assigned by a threshold on a score). Each replaces unconfoundedness by a
different untestable assumption. A **sensitivity analysis** reports how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be
to explain away the estimate.

## Pitfalls

- **Controlling for post-treatment variables**, including mediators and colliders.
- **Reading every coefficient of an adjustment regression causally.** The covariates' coefficients are not causal
  effects; only the treatment's coefficient has the adjustment set built for it.
- **Checking balance only on means.** After matching or weighting, compare the covariate distributions of the groups,
  including variances and tails.
- **Treating a good predictive model as a causal one.** A model can predict $Y$ from $W$ and $X$ well while $W$ has no
  effect on $Y$.
