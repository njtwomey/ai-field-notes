<Definition>
A **factorial design** studies $k$ factors at once by running every combination of their levels. In a **$2^k$
design** each factor has two levels, coded $-1$ and $+1$, and the $N = 2^k$ runs cover all sign patterns. The **main
effect** of factor A is the average response at A $= +1$ minus the average at A $= -1$. The **interaction** of A and
B is half the difference between the effect of A at B $= +1$ and its effect at B $= -1$. A **fractional factorial**
$2^{k-p}$ design runs a chosen $1/2^p$ fraction of the combinations <Cite id="box2005" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The traditional alternative changes one factor at a time while holding the others fixed. That approach uses each run
for one comparison only and cannot see interactions: if temperature helps only at high catalyst concentration, varying
temperature at the default concentration finds nothing. A factorial design varies all factors together in a balanced
pattern. Every run contributes to the estimate of every effect, and the pattern also estimates how the effect of one
factor depends on the others. Fisher argued for factorial experiments on these grounds <Cite id="fisher1935b" />.

## Effects as contrasts

Write the design as a table with one column of signs per factor. The column for an interaction is the elementwise
product of its factors' columns. For a $2^3$ design in standard order (A changes fastest):

| Run | A | B | C | AB | AC | BC | ABC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $-$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ |
| 2 | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ |
| 3 | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| 4 | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $-$ |
| 5 | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| 6 | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ |
| 7 | $-$ | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |
| 8 | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ |

Every effect is the same kind of contrast. With $c_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ the entries of an effect's column and $y_i$ the
response of run $i$,

$$
\text{effect} = \frac{1}{N/2}\sum_{i=1}^N c_i y_i = \bar y_{+} - \bar y_{-}.
$$

For a main effect, this is the difference of averages over the runs at each level. For AB, the runs with AB $= +1$ are
those where A and B have the same sign, and the formula equals half the change in the effect of A between the two
levels of B, which matches the definition.

**Orthogonality.** Any two distinct columns agree in sign on exactly half of the runs, so their dot product is 0. In a
regression of $y$ on the columns, $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = N\Imat$. Each coefficient is $\hat\beta = \frac1N\sum_i c_iy_i$,
half the effect, and the estimates do not depend on which other terms are in the model.

**Precision.** If the runs have independent errors with variance $\sigma^2$, an effect is a sum of $N$ terms with
coefficients $\pm 2/N$, so

$$
\var(\text{effect}) = N\cdot\frac{4}{N^2}\sigma^2 = \frac{4\sigma^2}{N}.
$$

This is the variance of a two-group comparison with $N/2$ runs per group, for every one of the $2^k - 1$ effects at
once. Estimating three main effects one at a time with the same precision would need a separate two-group comparison
for each, three times as many runs, and would say nothing about interactions.

## Worked example

A $2^3$ experiment with factors A, B and C gives, in the standard order above,

$$
y = 60, 72, 54, 68, 52, 83, 45, 80.
$$

| Effect | A | B | C | AB | AC | BC | ABC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Estimate | 23.0 | $-5.0$ | 1.5 | 1.5 | 10.0 | 0.0 | 0.5 |

For A: the runs at A $= +1$ average $(72 + 68 + 83 + 80)/4 = 75.75$ and those at A $= -1$ average $52.75$, a difference
of 23. The AC interaction of 10 means the effect of A depends on C. At C $= -1$ the effect of A is $((72 - 60) + (68 -
54))/2 = 13$; at C $= +1$ it is $((83 - 52) + (80 - 45))/2 = 33$. Half their difference is 10. Reporting the main effect
of A alone (23) would hide that A matters much more at the high level of C.

The eight runs give one observation per combination and no replication, so $\sigma$ cannot be estimated from pure
error. Two common approaches: assume the highest-order interactions are negligible and use them to estimate $\sigma^2$,
or plot the ordered effects against normal quantiles, where negligible effects fall on a line through 0 and real
effects stand off it.

## Fractional factorials

With many factors, $2^k$ runs are too many: 7 factors need 128. Most high-order interactions are negligible, so a
fraction suffices. A $2^{3-1}$ half fraction keeps the four runs with ABC $= +1$ (runs 2, 3, 5 and 8). The equation
**I = ABC** that selects them is the **defining relation**. In those four runs the A column equals the BC column, B
equals AC and C equals AB. Each contrast estimates a sum of two effects, and the pairs are **aliases**:

$$
\hat\ell_A = A + BC, \qquad \hat\ell_B = B + AC, \qquad \hat\ell_C = C + AB.
$$

Multiplying the defining relation by a factor gives its alias, using $A^2 = I$: $A \cdot ABC = BC$. With the data
above, the half fraction ($y = 72, 54, 52, 80$) gives $\hat\ell_A = 23.0$, $\hat\ell_B = 5.0$ and $\hat\ell_C = 3.0$. The
first is right because $BC = 0$. The second has the wrong sign: $B = -5$ is masked by its alias $AC = 10$.

The **resolution** of a fraction is the length of the shortest word in its defining relation. In a resolution III
design (such as I = ABC) main effects are aliased with two-factor interactions. In resolution IV they are aliased only
with three-factor interactions, and two-factor interactions with each other. In resolution V, main effects and
two-factor interactions are all clear of each other. Choose the fraction so that the effects believed to matter are not
aliased with each other.

## Assumptions

- Run order is randomised, so drifts in time do not align with any effect column.
- The response is roughly linear between the two levels of each factor. A two-level design cannot detect curvature;
  centre points (all factors at a middle level) test for it.
- Errors are independent with constant variance, as for any <NoteLink to="ols-inference">least-squares analysis</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Interpreting main effects in the presence of a large interaction.** The main effect averages over the other
  factor's levels and may describe no real condition.
- **Forgetting the alias structure.** A large contrast in a fractional design estimates a sum of aliased effects. Follow
  up with a second fraction (a fold-over) when the aliases cannot be ruled out.
- **Blocking a factorial.** When the runs do not fit in one homogeneous batch, assign the blocks by the sign of a
  high-order interaction column, which is then confounded with the block difference instead of a main effect
  (<NoteLink to="blocking-and-stratification">blocking</NoteLink>).
