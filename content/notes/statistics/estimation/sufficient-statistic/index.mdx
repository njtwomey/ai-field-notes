<Definition>
Let $\Xcal = (X_1, \dots, X_n)$ have a distribution $P_\theta$ from a family indexed by $\theta$. A statistic
$T = T(\Xcal)$ is **sufficient** for $\theta$ when the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> of $\Xcal$ given $T = t$ is the same
for every $\theta$ <Cite id="fisher1922" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Once $T$ is known, the rest of the sample is a draw from a distribution that does not involve $\theta$. It could be
regenerated by a random number generator without knowing $\theta$, so it cannot carry information about $\theta$. Any
inference about $\theta$, whether an estimate, a test or a posterior, can be based on $T$ alone without loss.

For $n$ tosses of a coin with heads probability $p$, the number of heads $S = \sum_i X_i$ is sufficient. A particular
sequence $x$ with $s$ heads has probability $p^s(1 - p)^{n-s}$, and $S = s$ has probability
$\binom{n}{s}p^s(1 - p)^{n-s}$. Dividing,

$$
P(X = x \mid S = s) = \frac{p^s(1 - p)^{n-s}}{\binom{n}{s}p^s(1 - p)^{n-s}} = \frac{1}{\binom{n}{s}}.
$$

Every sequence with $s$ heads is equally likely, whatever $p$ is. The order of the
heads says nothing about $p$.

## Factorisation theorem

$T$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if and only if the joint density or mass function factorises as

$$
f(x \mid \theta) = g\paren{T(x), \theta}\, h(x)
$$

for some functions $g \ge 0$ and $h \ge 0$, where $h$ does not depend on $\theta$ <Cite id="casella2002" />. The
parameter meets the data only through $T(x)$.

**Proof for discrete data.** The two directions are proved separately.

**Sufficiency implies factorisation.** Let $t = T(x)$. Because the event $\set{X = x}$ is contained in
$\set{T = t}$,

$$
f(x \mid \theta) = P_\theta(X = x) = P_\theta(X = x \mid T = t)\, P_\theta(T = t).
$$

By sufficiency the first factor does not depend on $\theta$. Take $h(x) = P(X = x \mid T = T(x))$ and
$g(t, \theta) = P_\theta(T = t)$.

**Factorisation implies sufficiency.** Suppose $f(x \mid \theta) = g(T(x), \theta)\, h(x)$ and fix $t$ with
$P_\theta(T = t) > 0$. For $x$ with $T(x) = t$,

$$
P_\theta(X = x \mid T = t) = \frac{f(x \mid \theta)}{\sum_{y : T(y) = t} f(y \mid \theta)}
= \frac{g(t, \theta)\, h(x)}{g(t, \theta) \sum_{y : T(y) = t} h(y)}
= \frac{h(x)}{\sum_{y : T(y) = t} h(y)}.
$$

The factor $g(t, \theta)$ cancels, so the conditional distribution does not depend on $\theta$. For $x$ with
$T(x) \ne t$ the conditional probability is zero for every $\theta$. The continuous case replaces sums by integrals
and needs measure-theoretic care, but the result is the same.

## Examples

| Model | Likelihood | Sufficient statistic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X_i \iid \Bern(p)$ | $p^{\sum x_i}(1 - p)^{n - \sum x_i}$ | $\sum_i x_i$ |
| $X_i \iid \Poisson(\lambda)$ | $e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{\sum x_i} \big/ \prod_i x_i!$ | $\sum_i x_i$ |
| $X_i \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\sigma$ known | $\propto \exp\set{-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\bar x - \mu)^2} \exp\set{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}$ | $\bar x$ |
| $X_i \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | depends on $\sum_i x_i$ and $\sum_i x_i^2$ | $\paren{\bar x, s^2}$ |
| $X_i \iid \Unif(0, \theta)$ | $\theta^{-n}\, \indicator\set{\max_i x_i \le \theta}$ | $\max_i x_i$ |

In the known-variance normal model, the second exponential factor is $h(x)$: the scatter around $\bar x$ carries no
information about $\mu$. The factorisation comes from adding and subtracting $\bar x$ inside each square:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \paren{(x_i - \bar x) + (\bar x - \mu)}^2
= \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2 + n(\bar x - \mu)^2.
$$

The cross term $2(\bar x - \mu)\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)$ vanishes because the deviations from the mean sum to zero.
Substituting into the normal likelihood splits it into a factor that involves $\mu$ only through $\bar x$ and a factor
free of $\mu$. In the uniform model the likelihood is zero for $\theta$ below the sample maximum and
decreasing above it, so only the maximum matters.

Some models have no sufficient statistic smaller than the data. For a Cauchy location model, the minimal sufficient
statistic is the full set of order statistics: only the order of the observations can be discarded.

## Exponential families

A family is an <NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> when its density can be written
$f(x \mid \eta) = h(x) \exp\set{\eta^\top T(x) - A(\eta)}$. The factorisation is built in, so $T$ is sufficient, and
for $n$ independent observations $\sum_i T(x_i)$ is sufficient whatever $n$ is. The Bernoulli, Poisson, normal and
gamma families are exponential families. The uniform family $\Unif(0, \theta)$ is not, because its support depends on
$\theta$: its sufficient statistic, the maximum, is not a sum.

## Uses

- **Minimal sufficiency.** Many statistics are sufficient; the full data always are. A **minimal** sufficient
  statistic is a function of every other sufficient statistic. It is the most compressed summary that loses nothing.
- **Rao–Blackwell theorem.** For any unbiased estimator $\hat\theta$, the conditional expectation
  $\expect[\hat\theta \mid T]$ is also unbiased and has variance no larger. Good estimators are functions of a
  sufficient statistic <Cite id="lehmann2005" />. Sufficiency is what makes $\expect[\hat\theta \mid T]$ an estimator:
  the conditional distribution of the data given $T$ does not involve $\theta$, so the conditional expectation can be
  computed without knowing $\theta$. The rest is the
  <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">laws of total expectation and variance</NoteLink>:
  $\expect\brack{\expect[\hat\theta \mid T]} = \expect[\hat\theta] = \theta$, and
  $\var(\hat\theta) = \expect\brack{\var(\hat\theta \mid T)} + \var\paren{\expect[\hat\theta \mid T]} \ge
  \var\paren{\expect[\hat\theta \mid T]}$. For coin tosses, the unbiased estimator $X_1$ of $p$ improves to
  $\expect[X_1 \mid S] = S/n$.
- **Tests.** Most powerful tests in exponential families reject for large or small values of the sufficient statistic;
  see the <NoteLink to="neyman-pearson-lemma">Neyman–Pearson lemma</NoteLink>.
- **Storage and streaming.** For a model with a sum-type sufficient statistic, a data pipeline need keep only running
  totals, such as counts, sums and sums of squares, to support inference about the parameters.

## Pitfalls

- **Sufficiency is relative to the model.** $\bar x$ is sufficient for $\mu$ only if the data are normal with known
  variance. Discarding the rest of the data then removes the evidence needed to check that assumption, such as outliers
  or skew.
- **Sufficient for one parameter only.** A statistic sufficient for a mean may lose information about a variance or a
  tail probability that a later question needs.
- **Numerical drift.** Computing a variance from running sums $\sum x_i$ and $\sum x_i^2$ loses precision when the
  mean is large relative to the spread. Welford's update keeps the same information stably.
