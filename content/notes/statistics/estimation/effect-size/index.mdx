<Definition>
An **effect size** is a number that measures the magnitude of a difference or association in a population. A **raw**
effect size is stated in the outcome's units, for example a difference in means of 4 mmHg. A **standardised** effect
size divides by a <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>. The most common is Cohen's

$$
d = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_2}{\sigma},
$$

the difference in means of two groups in units of their common standard deviation $\sigma$ <Cite id="cohen1988" />.
</Definition>

## Why significance is not size

A <NoteLink to="p-value">p-value</NoteLink> depends on the effect and on the sample size. With $n$ observations per group and known $\sigma$, the
difference in means has <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$, because the two groups' variances $\sigma^2/n$ add. The <NoteLink to="z-test">z
statistic</NoteLink> is the observed difference over this standard error, which in units of $\sigma$ is
$d/\sqrt{2/n} = d\sqrt{n/2}$. The effect enters once; the sample size enters as a square root that grows without limit.
For $n = 10{,}000$ per group, an effect of $d = 0.03$ gives $z = 2.12$ and $p = 0.034$: significant, and too small to
matter in most applications. With $n = 10$, an effect of $d = 0.8$ often fails to reach significance. A result should
report the estimated effect and its
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink>, whatever the p-value.

## Standardised mean differences

With sample means $\bar x_1, \bar x_2$, sample standard deviations $s_1, s_2$ and sizes $n_1, n_2$:

- **Cohen's d** uses the pooled standard deviation,
  $s_p = \sqrt{\brack{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}/(n_1 + n_2 - 2)}$, and estimates $d$ by
  $(\bar x_1 - \bar x_2)/s_p$.
- **Hedges' g** removes the small-sample bias of Cohen's d by multiplying it by
  $J = 1 - 3/\paren{4(n_1 + n_2) - 9}$ <Cite id="hedges1981" />. The bias arises because $S_p$ underestimates $\sigma$
  on average: the square root is concave, so $\expect[S_p] < \sqrt{\expect[S_p^2]} = \sigma$ by Jensen's inequality,
  and dividing by a denominator that is too small inflates $d$. $J$ is a close approximation to the exact correction,
  which involves a ratio of <NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma functions</NoteLink>. For $n_1 = n_2 = 5$, $J = 0.90$; for 50 per group,
  $J = 0.99$.
- **Intervals for d.** The estimate $\hat d$ is a scaled <NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">t statistic</NoteLink>: $\hat d\,\big/\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}$ is the pooled
  t statistic, which follows a noncentral <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">t distribution</NoteLink>. A
  confidence interval for $d$ inverts that distribution.
- **Glass's Δ** divides by the control group's standard deviation. It is preferred when the treatment changes the
  spread.
- **Paired designs** use $d_z = \bar d / s_d$, the mean difference over the standard deviation of the differences. It
  is not comparable with a between-group $d$, because $s_d$ depends on the correlation within pairs
  <Cite id="lakens2013" />.

## Reading a d

Cohen suggested 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 as small, medium and large in behavioural research <Cite id="cohen1988" />. He
intended these only for when nothing better is known. The practical meaning depends on the field: a $d$ of 0.2 in
mortality is large.

For two normal groups with equal variance, $d$ converts to quantities that need no statistical training
<Cite id="mcgraw1992" />:

| $d$ | Overlap $2\Phi(-d/2)$ | $U_3 = \Phi(d)$ | $P(\text{treated} > \text{control}) = \Phi(d/\sqrt 2)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.2 | 92% | 58% | 0.56 |
| 0.5 | 80% | 69% | 0.64 |
| 0.8 | 69% | 79% | 0.71 |

$\Phi$ is the standard normal cdf. $U_3$ is the share of the treatment group above the control group's mean.

**Derivation.** Measure in units of $\sigma$ with the control group at 0, so that control is $\Gauss(0, 1)$ and
treatment $\Gauss(d, 1)$.

- **$U_3$.** The share of the treatment group above 0 is $P(\Gauss(d, 1) > 0) = P(\Gauss(0, 1) > -d) = \Phi(d)$.
- **Probability of superiority.** For independent draws $X_T$ and $X_C$, the difference $X_T - X_C$ is
  $\Gauss(d, 2)$, since independent variances add. So $P(X_T > X_C) = P\paren{\Gauss(0, 1) > -d/\sqrt 2} =
  \Phi(d/\sqrt 2)$.
- **Overlap.** The two densities cross at the midpoint $d/2$. Below it the control density is the larger, above it the
  treatment density. The shared area is the part of each curve on the far side of the crossing:
  $P(\Gauss(d, 1) < d/2) + P(\Gauss(0, 1) > d/2) = 2\Phi(-d/2)$.

## Other effect sizes

- **Proportions:** the risk difference $p_1 - p_2$, the risk ratio $p_1/p_2$ and the odds ratio
  $\frac{p_1/(1 - p_1)}{p_2/(1 - p_2)}$. The risk difference is the easiest to act on. The odds ratio is what <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic
  regression</NoteLink> estimates.
- **Association:** the correlation coefficient $r$, or the share of variance explained, $r^2$ or $\eta^2$.
- **Regression:** a coefficient in the outcome's units per unit of the predictor, which is a raw effect size.

## Pitfalls

- **Standardising away meaning.** A raw effect in meaningful units, such as days of hospital stay, is usually more
  useful than $d$. Standardise to compare across scales, not by default.
- **Significance filtering.** Among studies that reached $p < 0.05$, the published effect sizes overestimate the true
  effect, most strongly when power is low.
- **Different denominators.** Two studies that report $d$ with different standard deviations, pooled, control-group
  or within-pair, are not comparable.
