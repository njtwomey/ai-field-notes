<Definition>
A $(1 - \alpha)$ **confidence interval** for a parameter $\theta$ is a pair of statistics $L(\Xcal) \le U(\Xcal)$ such
that

$$
P_\theta\paren{L(\Xcal) \le \theta \le U(\Xcal)} \ge 1 - \alpha \quad \text{for every } \theta.
$$

The probability is over the sample $\Xcal$; $\theta$ is fixed. $1 - \alpha$ is the **confidence level**, and the
left-hand side is the **coverage** <Cite id="neyman1937" />.
</Definition>

## What the level means

The confidence level describes the procedure, not a particular interval. If the procedure is repeated on many
independent samples, a fraction $1 - \alpha$ of the intervals contain $\theta$. Once the data are observed, the
computed interval either contains $\theta$ or it does not. The statement "$\theta$ lies in $[4.56, 5.84]$ with
probability 0.95" is a Bayesian credible-interval statement and needs a prior.

## Interval for a mean

For $n$ independent observations from $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, with sample mean $\bar x$ and sample standard deviation
$s$, the $(1 - \alpha)$ interval is

$$
\bar x \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-1}\, \frac{s}{\sqrt n},
$$

where $t_{q, \nu}$ is the $q$-quantile of Student's t distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom. When $\sigma$ is
known, $\sigma$ replaces $s$ and the normal quantile $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ replaces the t quantile.

**Derivation.** The interval comes from a **pivot**: a function of the data and the parameter whose distribution does
not depend on any unknown. Here the pivot is $T = (\bar X - \mu)/(S/\sqrt n)$, which has the $t_{n-1}$ distribution
whatever $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are; see the <NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample t-test</NoteLink>. With
$c = t_{1-\alpha/2, n-1}$ and the symmetry of the t distribution,

$$
1 - \alpha = P\paren{-c \le \frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt n} \le c}
= P\paren{\bar X - c\frac{S}{\sqrt n} \le \mu \le \bar X + c\frac{S}{\sqrt n}}.
$$

The second event is the first rearranged: multiply through by $S/\sqrt n > 0$, subtract $\bar X$ and negate, which
reverses both inequalities. The probability statement is about the random endpoints; $\mu$ is fixed throughout.

For example, $n = 16$, $\bar x = 5.2$ and $s = 1.2$ give a standard error $1.2/4 = 0.3$ and
$t_{0.975, 15} = 2.131$. The 95% interval is $5.2 \pm 0.64 = [4.56, 5.84]$.

Using $z_{0.975} = 1.96$ with the estimated $s$ ignores the uncertainty in $s$. The coverage of that interval is
$P(\abs{T_{n-1}} \le 1.96)$: 70% for $n = 2$, 88% for $n = 5$, 92% for $n = 10$ and 94% for $n = 30$.

The half-width is proportional to $1/\sqrt n$. Halving the width needs four times as much data.

## Duality with tests

A confidence set can be built from a family of tests, and a test from a confidence set <Cite id="casella2002" />. For
each $\theta_0$, let $A(\theta_0)$ be the acceptance region of a level-$\alpha$ test of $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$. Then

$$
C(\Xcal) = \set{\theta_0 : \Xcal \in A(\theta_0)}
$$

is a $(1 - \alpha)$ confidence set. It contains exactly the parameter values that the data do not reject. Conversely,
rejecting $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ whenever $\theta_0 \notin C(\Xcal)$ is a level-$\alpha$ test.

**Proof of coverage.** Fix the true value $\theta$. By the definition of $C$, the set $C(\Xcal)$ contains $\theta$
exactly when $\Xcal \in A(\theta)$. The test of $H_0: \theta_0 = \theta$ has level $\alpha$, and for this $\theta_0$ the
null is true, so it wrongly rejects with probability at most $\alpha$: $P_\theta(\Xcal \in A(\theta)) \ge 1 - \alpha$.
Therefore $P_\theta(\theta \in C(\Xcal)) \ge 1 - \alpha$. The argument holds for every $\theta$. The converse runs the
same way: if $C$ has coverage $1 - \alpha$, the test that rejects when $\theta_0 \notin C(\Xcal)$ rejects a true
$\theta_0$ with probability at most $\alpha$.

The t interval above is this construction applied to the t-test: $\mu_0$ lies in the interval exactly when
$\abs{\bar x - \mu_0}/(s/\sqrt n) \le t_{1-\alpha/2, n-1}$, which is the acceptance region.

Consequences of the duality:

- A two-sided 95% interval for $\mu$ excludes $\mu_0$ exactly when the two-sided t-test of $\mu = \mu_0$ gives
  $p < 0.05$.
- A one-sided test corresponds to a one-sided bound, $(-\infty, U]$ or $[L, \infty)$.
- An interval reports more than a test: it shows every null value that the data reject and the precision of the
  estimate.

## Intervals for a proportion

Inverting different tests gives different intervals. For 15 successes in 20 trials:

| Interval | Construction | 95% interval |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wald | $\hat p \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n}$ | $[0.560, 0.940]$ |
| Wilson | inverts the score test | $[0.531, 0.888]$ |
| Clopper–Pearson | inverts the exact binomial test <Cite id="clopper1934" /> | $[0.509, 0.913]$ |

<Derivation title="The Wilson interval from the score test">
The score test of $H_0: p = p_0$ uses the null standard error, $z = (\hat p - p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n}$. Inverting
it collects every $p$ with $(\hat p - p)^2 \le z_{1-\alpha/2}^2\, p(1 - p)/n$. Writing $z = z_{1-\alpha/2}$ and
expanding, this is the quadratic inequality

$$
\paren{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} p^2 - \paren{2\hat p + \frac{z^2}{n}} p + \hat p^2 \le 0,
$$

whose solution set is the interval between its two roots,

$$
\frac{\hat p + z^2/(2n)}{1 + z^2/n} \pm \frac{z}{1 + z^2/n}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}.
$$

For $\hat p = 0.75$ and $n = 20$ this gives $[0.531, 0.888]$. The Wald interval instead plugs $\hat p$ into the
standard error, which gives a symmetric interval that ignores how the variance changes with $p$.
</Derivation>

The Wald interval undercovers badly near $p = 0$ or $1$ and for small $n$. Clopper–Pearson guarantees coverage of at
least 95% and is conservative. Wilson has coverage close to the nominal level.

## Pitfalls

- **Overlapping intervals.** Two 95% intervals can overlap while the difference between the estimates is significant
  at 0.05. Compare groups with an interval for the difference, not by eye.
- **Probability statements about $\theta$.** A confidence level is a property of the procedure over repeated samples.
- **Ignoring the model.** The coverage guarantee holds only when the assumptions behind the interval hold:
  independence, and approximate normality of the estimate for the t interval.
