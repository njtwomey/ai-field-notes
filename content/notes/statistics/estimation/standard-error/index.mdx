<Definition>
The **standard error** of an estimator $\hat\theta$ is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution,

$$
\text{se}(\hat\theta) = \sqrt{\var(\hat\theta)},
$$

where the variance is over repeated samples from the same population <Cite id="wasserman2004" />. For the mean
$\bar X$ of $n$ independent observations with standard deviation $\sigma$, $\text{se}(\bar X) = \sigma/\sqrt n$.
</Definition>

## Standard error of the mean

For independent $X_1, \dots, X_n$ with common variance $\sigma^2$, variances of independent terms add, so

$$
\var(\bar X) = \var\paren{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i} = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \var(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$

Taking the square root gives $\sigma/\sqrt n$. The population standard deviation $\sigma$ is usually unknown, and the
**estimated** standard error replaces it with the sample standard deviation,
$s = \sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2/(n - 1)}$:

$$
\widehat{\text{se}}(\bar X) = \frac{s}{\sqrt n}.
$$

The divisor $n - 1$ makes $S^2$ an unbiased estimate of $\sigma^2$. Expanding around the true mean,
$\sum_i (X_i - \bar X)^2 = \sum_i (X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar X - \mu)^2$. Taking expectations, the first sum has
expectation $n\sigma^2$ and the second term $n \var(\bar X) = \sigma^2$, so the total is $(n - 1)\sigma^2$. Dividing by
$n$ would underestimate $\sigma^2$, because the deviations are measured from $\bar X$, which is fitted to the same data
and is closer to them than $\mu$ is.

For example, 25 measurements with $s = 10$ have a standard error of $10/5 = 2$. The sample mean is typically within
about 2 units of the population mean, and within about 4 units in 95% of samples.

## Standard deviation and standard error

The standard deviation describes the spread of individual observations. It does not shrink as $n$ grows. The standard
error describes the spread of an estimate. It shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$: four times as much data halves it. Reporting
the standard error where the standard deviation is meant makes the data look far less variable than they are.

## Other estimators

| Estimator | Standard error |
| --- | --- |
| Proportion $\hat p$ from $n$ trials | $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$ |
| Difference of independent means | $\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2}$ |
| Mean difference of $n$ pairs | $\sigma_D/\sqrt n$, with $\sigma_D$ the standard deviation of the differences |
| Least-squares coefficient $\hat\beta_j$ | $\sigma\sqrt{\brack{(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}}_{jj}}$ |

Each row follows from the rule that variances of independent terms add:

- **Proportion.** $\hat p$ is the mean of $n$ independent <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli</NoteLink>
  variables, each with variance $p(1 - p)$, so $\var(\hat p) = p(1 - p)/n$.
- **Difference of independent means.** $\var(\bar X_1 - \bar X_2) = \var(\bar X_1) + \var(\bar X_2)$. A difference
  adds variances, it does not subtract them.
- **Paired means.** The differences $D_i$ are independent across pairs, so the mean rule applies to them directly.

<Derivation title="The least-squares standard error">
With $\yvec = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonvec$, independent errors of variance $\sigma^2$ and fixed $\Xmat$, the estimate is
$\hat\betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec = \betavec + \Amat\epsilonvec$ with
$\Amat = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$. A linear map $\Amat$ transforms a covariance $\Sigmamat$ into
$\Amat\Sigmamat\Amat^\top$, so

$$
\cov(\hat\betavec) = \Amat(\sigma^2\eye)\Amat^\top = \sigma^2 (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}
= \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}.
$$

The standard error of $\hat\beta_j$ is the square root of the $j$-th diagonal entry. With a single intercept column of
ones, $\Xmat^\top\Xmat = n$ and the formula reduces to $\sigma/\sqrt n$, the standard error of the mean.
</Derivation>

For a proportion of 0.3 estimated from 400 trials, the standard error is $\sqrt{0.21/400} = 0.023$. When no formula is
available, the bootstrap estimates a standard error as the standard deviation of the estimate across resamples of the
data.

## Use in inference

Standard errors turn an estimate into a test statistic and an interval. Most large-sample procedures have the form

$$
\text{statistic} = \frac{\hat\theta - \theta_0}{\text{se}(\hat\theta)}, \qquad
\text{interval} = \hat\theta \pm q \cdot \text{se}(\hat\theta),
$$

with $q$ a normal or t quantile. The <NoteLink to="z-test">z-test</NoteLink>, the
<NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">t-test</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence
intervals</NoteLink> for a mean all use $\text{se}(\bar X)$. By the central limit theorem the sampling distribution of
$\bar X$ is close to normal for moderate $n$ even when the data are not, which is why these procedures hold widely.

## Pitfalls

- **Dependent observations.** $\sigma/\sqrt n$ assumes independence. With positive autocorrelation, as in time series,
  or with clustering, as in repeated measures on the same patients, the true standard error is larger. For clusters
  of size $m$ with intra-cluster correlation $\rho$, the variance of a cluster's mean is
  $\frac{1}{m^2}\paren{m\sigma^2 + m(m - 1)\rho\sigma^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{m}\paren{1 + (m - 1)\rho}$, counting the $m$
  variances and the $m(m - 1)$ covariances. The variance is therefore multiplied by the design effect
  $1 + (m - 1)\rho$.
- **Heavy tails.** When the population has infinite variance, as for the Cauchy distribution, the sample mean has no
  standard error and does not settle down as $n$ grows.
- **Small samples.** With few observations $s$ is a noisy estimate of $\sigma$. For normal data
  $(n - 1)S^2/\sigma^2$ has the <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">$\ChiSq_{n-1}$ distribution</NoteLink>, whose
  relative spread is $\sqrt{2/(n - 1)}$: 47% for $n = 10$. Inference then needs the t distribution rather than the
  normal.
