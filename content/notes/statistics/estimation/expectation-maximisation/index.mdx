<Definition>
Let $\xvec$ be observed data, $\zvec$ unobserved (latent) variables and $\thetab$ parameters, with a model
$p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)$. Expectation–maximisation starts from $\thetab^{(0)}$ and repeats
<Cite id="dempster1977" />:

- **E-step.** Compute the posterior of the latent variables at the current parameters,
  $q^{(t)}(\zvec) = p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab^{(t)})$, and form
  $Q(\thetab, \thetab^{(t)}) = \expect_{q^{(t)}}\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)}$.
- **M-step.** Set $\thetab^{(t+1)} = \argmax_{\thetab} Q(\thetab, \thetab^{(t)})$.

The log-likelihood $\ell(\thetab) = \log p(\xvec \mid \thetab)$ never decreases from one iteration to the next.
</Definition>

## The problem

Maximum likelihood with latent variables asks for

$$
\ell(\thetab) = \log p(\xvec \mid \thetab) = \log \sum_{\zvec} p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab),
$$

with an integral in place of the sum for continuous $\zvec$. The logarithm of a sum does not split, so the maximiser
rarely has a closed form. If $\zvec$ were observed, the complete-data log-likelihood $\log p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)$
would usually be easy to maximise: for a mixture model it is a sum of per-component terms, each maximised by a
weighted average. EM exploits this. It replaces the unknown $\zvec$ by its expected value under the current fit, solves
the easy complete-data problem, and repeats.

## A lower bound that touches

For any distribution $q(\zvec)$ that is positive wherever the posterior is, multiply and divide by $q$ inside the
sum. Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm, $\log \expect[Y] \ge \expect[\log Y]$, then gives a lower bound:

$$
\ell(\thetab) = \log \sum_{\zvec} q(\zvec)\, \frac{p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)}{q(\zvec)}
\;\ge\; \sum_{\zvec} q(\zvec) \log \frac{p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)}{q(\zvec)}
\;=\; \Lcal(q, \thetab).
$$

The bound is $\Lcal(q, \thetab) = \expect_q\brack{\log p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab)} + \entropy[q]$, the
<NoteLink to="evidence-lower-bound">evidence lower bound</NoteLink>. The gap is exact. Write
$p(\xvec, \zvec \mid \thetab) = p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab)\, p(\xvec \mid \thetab)$ inside $\Lcal$:

$$
\Lcal(q, \thetab) = \sum_{\zvec} q(\zvec) \log p(\xvec \mid \thetab)
+ \sum_{\zvec} q(\zvec) \log \frac{p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab)}{q(\zvec)}
= \ell(\thetab) - \KL\paren{q \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab)}.
$$

So $\ell(\thetab) = \Lcal(q, \thetab) + \KL(q \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab))$ for every $q$ and every
$\thetab$. The <NoteLink to="kl-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is non-negative and is zero only when $q$ is the
posterior. Two facts follow:

- For fixed $\thetab$, the bound is tight exactly when $q = p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab)$.
- For fixed $q$, $\Lcal(q, \thetab)$ is $Q(\thetab)$ plus the constant $\entropy[q]$, so maximising the bound over
  $\thetab$ is maximising $Q$.

## The two steps and why the likelihood rises

The **E-step** sets $q^{(t)} = p(\zvec \mid \xvec, \thetab^{(t)})$. The gap vanishes at $\thetab^{(t)}$, so the bound
$\Lcal(q^{(t)}, \cdot)$ is a curve that lies below $\ell$ everywhere and touches it at $\thetab^{(t)}$. The **M-step**
moves to the maximum of that curve. Chaining the two facts above,

$$
\ell(\thetab^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \Lcal(q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t+1)}) \;\ge\; \Lcal(q^{(t)}, \thetab^{(t)}) \;=\; \ell(\thetab^{(t)}).
$$

The first inequality holds because the bound is below $\ell$ everywhere. The second holds because
$\thetab^{(t+1)}$ maximises the bound. The equality holds because the E-step made the bound tight. The likelihood
can only rise, and it rises by at least as much as the bound did. The same argument shows that any $\thetab^{(t+1)}$
that merely increases $Q$ still increases $\ell$, which is the basis of generalised EM below.

Seen as a whole, EM is coordinate ascent on $\Lcal(q, \thetab)$: the E-step maximises over $q$ and the M-step over
$\thetab$. The note on <NoteLink to="em-as-variational-inference">EM as variational inference</NoteLink> develops this
view.

## Worked example: two coins

Two coins, A and B, have unknown head probabilities $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$. Five sets of 10 tosses were each made
with one coin, chosen at random with equal probability, but the record of which coin was used is lost. The head
counts are $h = (5, 9, 8, 4, 7)$ <Cite id="do2008" />. The latent variable $z_i$ is the coin used for set $i$.

**E-step.** Start from $\theta_A = 0.6$ and $\theta_B = 0.5$. The posterior probability that set $i$ used coin A is

$$
r_i = \frac{\theta_A^{h_i}(1 - \theta_A)^{10 - h_i}}{\theta_A^{h_i}(1 - \theta_A)^{10 - h_i} + \theta_B^{h_i}(1 - \theta_B)^{10 - h_i}},
$$

giving $r = (0.45, 0.80, 0.73, 0.35, 0.65)$. The binomial coefficients cancel. Each set contributes its heads and
tails to coin A in proportion $r_i$ and to coin B in proportion $1 - r_i$: coin A is credited with $21.3$ expected
heads and $8.6$ expected tails, coin B with $11.7$ heads and $8.4$ tails.

**M-step.** With the coin assignments filled in by their expectations, each coin's estimate is its share of heads:
$\theta_A = 21.3/(21.3 + 8.6) = 0.71$ and $\theta_B = 11.7/(11.7 + 8.4) = 0.58$.

Repeating the two steps converges to $\theta_A \approx 0.80$ and $\theta_B \approx 0.52$. Had the coin labels been
recorded, the estimates would have been the plain head proportions of each coin's sets; EM reaches the same kind of
answer with each set split between the coins by its posterior probability.

## Worked example: genetic linkage

Dempster, Laird and Rubin's original illustration has 197 animals classified into four categories with counts
$y = (125, 18, 20, 34)$ and probabilities
$\paren{\tfrac12 + \tfrac\theta4,\ \tfrac{1 - \theta}{4},\ \tfrac{1 - \theta}{4},\ \tfrac\theta4}$ for an unknown
$\theta \in (0, 1)$ <Cite id="dempster1977" />.

The first probability is a sum, which makes the likelihood awkward. Treat the first category as two latent ones, with
probabilities $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac\theta4$, and let $x_2$ be the unknown number of the 125 animals that fell into the
$\tfrac\theta4$ part. Given $x_2$, the complete-data likelihood is proportional to
$\theta^{x_2 + y_4}(1 - \theta)^{y_2 + y_3}$, a binomial likelihood, maximised by

$$
\theta = \frac{x_2 + y_4}{x_2 + y_2 + y_3 + y_4}.
$$

**E-step.** Given $\theta^{(t)}$, each of the 125 animals is in the $\tfrac\theta4$ part with probability
$p^{(t)} = \tfrac{\theta^{(t)}/4}{1/2 + \theta^{(t)}/4}$, so
$\expect[x_2] = 125\, p^{(t)}$.

**M-step.** Substitute the expectation into the formula above.

From $\theta^{(0)} = 0.5$: $p^{(0)} = 0.2$, $\expect[x_2] = 25$, and
$\theta^{(1)} = (25 + 34)/(25 + 18 + 20 + 34) = 0.6082$. The next iterates are $0.6243$, $0.6265$, $0.6268$, and the
sequence converges to the maximum-likelihood estimate $\hat\theta = 0.6268$. The error shrinks by a factor of about
$0.133$ each step, explained below.

## The limit

Monotonicity guarantees that $\ell(\thetab^{(t)})$ converges when $\ell$ is bounded above. It does not guarantee that
the iterates reach the global maximum. Under mild continuity conditions, every limit point of EM is a stationary point
of $\ell$ <Cite id="wu1983" />. When $\ell$ has a single stationary point, as in the genetic-linkage example, that
point is the maximum-likelihood estimate and EM finds it from any start. Otherwise the limit depends on the start.

The symmetric mixture $\tfrac12\Gauss(\mu, 1) + \tfrac12\Gauss(-\mu, 1)$ in the figure shows all three outcomes. Its
likelihood is symmetric in $\mu$, with maxima at $\pm\hat\mu$. Started at a positive $\mu$, EM converges to $+\hat\mu$;
started at a negative one, to $-\hat\mu$. Started exactly at $\mu = 0$, every posterior probability is $\tfrac12$, the
M-step returns $0$, and EM stays at a stationary point that is not a maximum. Any perturbation moves it away, but EM
itself never does. In practice EM is run from several starts and the best likelihood is kept
<Cite id="mclachlan2008" />.

## The rate of convergence

Near the limit, EM behaves like a linear map: $\thetab^{(t+1)} - \hat\thetab \approx J\,(\thetab^{(t)} - \hat\thetab)$,
where $J$ is the Jacobian of the EM update at $\hat\thetab$. Dempster, Laird and Rubin showed that $J$ is the fraction
of missing information. With $I_{\text{com}}$ the Fisher information of the complete data and $I_{\text{obs}}$ that of
the observed data, both at $\hat\thetab$,

$$
J = I_{\text{com}}^{-1}\paren{I_{\text{com}} - I_{\text{obs}}} = \eye - I_{\text{com}}^{-1} I_{\text{obs}}.
$$

The difference $I_{\text{com}} - I_{\text{obs}}$ is the information lost by not observing $\zvec$. If little is
missing, $J$ is near zero and EM converges in a few steps. If most of the information is missing, $J$ approaches the
identity and EM crawls.

For genetic linkage at $\hat\theta = 0.6268$, the observed information is $-\ell''(\hat\theta) = 377.5$ and the
complete-data information, with $x_2$ replaced by its expectation, is
$(\expect[x_2] + y_4)/\hat\theta^2 + (y_2 + y_3)/(1 - \hat\theta)^2 = 435.3$. So $J = 1 - 377.5/435.3 = 0.133$: about
13% of the information is missing, and each iteration cuts the error by that factor. The straight line on the log-error
chart in the figure has this slope.

## Variants

The monotonicity argument needs only that each step does not decrease the bound. That freedom produces a family of
algorithms <Cite id="mclachlan2008" />.

| Variant | What changes | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generalised EM (GEM) | The M-step only increases $Q$, for example by one gradient step | No closed-form M-step |
| ECM | The M-step maximises over blocks of $\thetab$ in turn, each with the others fixed | Joint maximisation is hard, conditional ones are easy |
| ECME | Some blocks maximise $\ell$ itself instead of $Q$ | Faster convergence than ECM |
| PX-EM | Adds auxiliary parameters and maps back after each step | Slow convergence from a poorly scaled model |
| MAP-EM | Adds $\log p(\thetab)$ to the M-step objective | Regularisation; avoids degenerate solutions |
| Hard (classification) EM | The E-step assigns each point to its single most probable $\zvec$ | Speed; <NoteLink to="k-means">k-means</NoteLink> is the limit for a Gaussian mixture with equal weights and equal, shrinking spherical covariances |
| Monte Carlo EM | The E-step expectation is estimated by sampling $\zvec$ | The posterior cannot be summed or integrated exactly |
| Stochastic and online EM | Sufficient statistics are updated from small batches with a decreasing step size | Large or streaming data |
| Incremental EM | The E-step updates the posterior of one data point at a time | Large datasets, faster early progress |
| Variational EM | $q$ is restricted to a tractable family instead of the exact posterior | The posterior is intractable; the bound, not $\ell$, is maximised |
| Accelerated EM (Aitken, SQUAREM) | Extrapolates along the sequence of iterates | Much of the information is missing |

- **Conditional maximisation.** ECM replaces one hard M-step by several easy conditional ones and keeps the
  monotonicity <Cite id="meng1993" />. ECME maximises the actual likelihood in some of those steps
  <Cite id="liu1994" />, and PX-EM expands the model with parameters that the E-step can use, which speeds
  convergence <Cite id="liu1998" />.
- **Sampling and streaming.** Monte Carlo EM approximates $Q$ with posterior samples <Cite id="wei1990" />. Online EM
  replaces the full E-step by a running average of expected sufficient statistics <Cite id="cappe2009" />, and
  incremental EM updates one data point at a time <Cite id="neal1998" />.
- **Special cases with their own names.** The Baum–Welch algorithm is EM for hidden Markov models, with the
  forward–backward recursions as its E-step <Cite id="baum1970" />; the
  <NoteLink to="occasionally-dishonest-casino">occasionally dishonest casino</NoteLink> example uses those recursions.
  EM for a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink> has closed-form steps. EM for
  missing data treats the missing entries as $\zvec$ <Cite id="little2019" />.
- **Acceleration.** SQUAREM extrapolates from two EM steps at a time and keeps convergence global
  <Cite id="varadhan2008" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Local maxima and stationary points.** EM converges to a stationary point that depends on the start. Run it from
  several starts.
- **Unbounded likelihoods.** For mixtures with free variances, a component can collapse onto a single point and the
  likelihood grows without bound. MAP-EM with a prior on the variances, or a variance floor, prevents this.
- **Slow convergence.** The rate is the fraction of missing information. Well-separated clusters converge fast;
  heavily overlapping ones can take thousands of iterations.
- **Stopping rules.** A small change in $\ell$ between iterations can mean slow progress, not convergence. Monitor the
  change in the parameters as well.
