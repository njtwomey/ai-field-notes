<Definition>
The **parametric bootstrap** fits a model $F_{\hat\theta}$ to the data, draws $B$ synthetic data sets of size $n$
from $F_{\hat\theta}$, and recomputes the statistic on each. A **bootstrap hypothesis test** draws the synthetic data
sets from an estimate of the data distribution that satisfies the null hypothesis, computes the test statistic $T^{*b}$
on each, and reports $\hat p = (1 + \#\set{b : T^{*b} \ge T})/(1 + B)$, where $T$ is the observed statistic
<Cite id="efron1993" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The nonparametric <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> resamples the observed values, so it cannot produce a
value outside the sample. With small $n$ it misrepresents the tails, and it cannot handle statistics whose behaviour
depends on the tails, such as extreme quantiles. When a parametric model is credible, simulating from the fitted model
uses that information.

Testing is a separate problem. A p-value is a probability computed under the null hypothesis. Resampling the raw data
reproduces the distribution the data came from, which satisfies the null only if the null is true. The null
distribution of a statistic must be simulated from a distribution that satisfies the null by construction.

## Mechanism

**Parametric bootstrap.**

1. Fit the model: $\hat\theta$ from the data.
2. For $b = 1, \dots, B$, draw $x_1^{*b}, \dots, x_n^{*b} \iid F_{\hat\theta}$ and compute the statistic $s^{*b}$.
3. Use the spread of the $s^{*b}$ as for the nonparametric bootstrap: standard deviation, quantiles, bias.

A common use is a **parametric bootstrap test** of a composite null. To test whether data follow an exponential
distribution with Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance $D$, the tabulated null distribution of $D$ assumes a fully specified
rate; with the rate estimated, the true null distribution is more concentrated and the tabulated p-values are too
large. Fit the rate, simulate data sets from the fitted exponential, re-estimate the rate and recompute $D$ on each, and
compare the observed $D$ with the simulated values. The same approach calibrates likelihood-ratio tests whose chi-squared
approximation fails, such as a test that a variance component is zero.

**Bootstrap tests.** Hall and Wilson give two guidelines <Cite id="hall1991" />.

1. **Resample under the null.** For $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$, recentre the data, $\tilde x_i = x_i - \bar x + \mu_0$, and
   resample from $\tilde x_1, \dots, \tilde x_n$. The recentred empirical distribution has mean exactly $\mu_0$ and the
   same shape as the data. For two samples with $H_0: \mu_A = \mu_B$, recentre each group on the pooled mean.
2. **Use a pivotal statistic.** Use $T = (\bar x - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)$, recomputing $s$ in every resample, not the raw
   difference $\bar x - \mu_0$. A statistic whose null distribution depends little on unknown parameters makes the
   bootstrap p-value more accurate, with error of order $1/n$ rather than $1/\sqrt n$.

The p-value counts resamples at least as extreme as the observed statistic, $|T^{*b}| \ge |T|$ for a two-sided test.
The $+1$ in numerator and denominator counts the observed data set as one draw from the null, which keeps the test
valid for finite $B$, as for random-permutation tests.

**Duality with intervals.** A two-sided bootstrap test of $\mu = \mu_0$ at level $\alpha$ is equivalent to asking
whether $\mu_0$ lies outside the $(1 - \alpha)$ bootstrap-t interval, when both use the same pivot.

## Worked example

Ten observations: $1.2, 2.5, 0.8, 3.9, 1.7, 6.3, 2.1, 0.9, 4.4, 1.5$, with $\bar x = 2.53$ and $s = 1.79$.

**Test of $\mu = 1.5$.** The observed statistic is $T = (2.53 - 1.5)/(1.79/\sqrt{10}) = 1.82$. The
<NoteLink to="one-sample-t-test">one-sample t-test</NoteLink> gives $p = 0.103$. Recentring gives $\tilde x_i = x_i -
1.03$. With $B = 20{,}000$ resamples of the recentred data (numpy seed 2) and $T^*$ recomputed on each, the two-sided
bootstrap p-value is $0.127$. For right-skewed data the studentised mean is skewed to the left, because a resample
with a large mean also tends to have a large $s$. The bootstrap null distribution of $T$ has 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles
$-4.03$ and $1.80$, against $\pm 2.26$ for Student's t with 9 degrees of freedom. Its long left tail raises the
two-sided p-value.

**Parametric bootstrap of a median.** Suppose the data are modelled as exponential. The fitted mean is 2.53, and the
estimated median is $2.53\ln 2 = 1.75$. Simulating 20,000 exponential samples of size 10 with that mean gives a
standard error of the sample median of 0.79. The nonparametric bootstrap gives 0.68. The two differ because the
exponential model has a longer right tail than the observed values suggest; which answer is better depends on whether
the model is right.

## Cost

$B$ simulations and recomputations, as for the nonparametric bootstrap, plus a model fit per resample when the
statistic involves re-estimation. Tests at $\alpha = 0.05$ need $B \ge 1{,}000$; smaller $\alpha$ needs more.

## Trade-offs

- **Parametric against nonparametric.** The parametric bootstrap is more accurate when the model is right and can
  extrapolate beyond the observed values. It inherits every error of the model. Check the fit first.
- **Bootstrap against permutation tests.** A <NoteLink to="permutation-tests">permutation test</NoteLink> is exact
  under exchangeability but tests equality of distributions. A bootstrap test targets a specific parameter, such as a
  mean, while other features differ, and is only approximately valid.
- **Pitfall: resampling the raw data for a test.** Resampling without recentring gives the distribution of $T$ under
  the alternative that generated the data. Its p-values have no meaning as tail probabilities under $H_0$.
