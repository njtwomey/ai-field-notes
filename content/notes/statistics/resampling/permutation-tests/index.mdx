<Definition>
Let $Z$ be the data and $G$ a finite set of $M$ transformations of the data (such as all reassignments of group labels)
under which the <NoteLink to="null-and-alternative-hypotheses">null hypothesis</NoteLink> says the distribution of $Z$
does not change. For a test statistic $T$, the **permutation p-value** is the fraction of transformed data sets whose
statistic is at least as extreme as the observed one,

$$
p = \frac1M\sum_{g \in G}\indicator\set{T(gZ) \ge T(Z)}.
$$

Rejecting when $p \le \alpha$ gives a test of level at most $\alpha$ for every sample size and every choice of $T$

</Definition>

## Hypotheses

For two samples $a_1, \dots, a_m$ and $b_1, \dots, b_n$, the null hypothesis is that both come from the same
distribution, $H_0: F_A = F_B$. Under $H_0$ the pooled vector of $N = m + n$ values is **exchangeable**: its joint
distribution is unchanged by any reordering. The labels "A" and "B" then carry no information, and each of the
$\binom Nm$ ways of choosing which $m$ values are labelled A is equally likely to be the observed one.

The alternative is set by the statistic. The difference in means $T = \bar a - \bar b$ has power against a shift in
location; the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance has power against any difference in distribution.

Other designs use other transformation sets:

- **Paired data.** Under $H_0$ each within-pair difference $d_i$ is symmetric about 0, so flipping its sign leaves the
  distribution unchanged. $G$ is the set of $2^n$ sign patterns.
- **Association.** For pairs $(x_i, y_i)$ with $x$ independent of $y$ under $H_0$, permuting the $y$ values among the
  pairs leaves the distribution unchanged.
- **<NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">Randomised experiments</NoteLink>.** When treatment was assigned by
  a known random mechanism, $G$ is the set of assignments that mechanism could have produced. The test is then justified
  by the design alone.

## Assumptions

- **Exchangeability under $H_0$.** For the null $F_A = F_B$, exchangeability of the units is the only assumption. No
  normality, no equal variances, no large-sample approximation.
- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> of units.** Clustered or serially correlated observations are
  not exchangeable individually; permute whole clusters instead.

## Test statistic

Any function $T$ of the data. The two-sample difference in means is the most common; a studentised difference (the
Welch $t$ statistic) makes the test robust to unequal variances in large samples. The statistic is chosen before
looking at the data.

## Decision rule

**Exactness.** Fix an ordering of the $M$ values $T(gZ)$, $g \in G$, from largest to smallest, breaking ties against
the observed data. The observed data set is one of the $M$ equally likely members of its orbit $\set{gZ : g \in G}$,
because under $H_0$ the observed labelling is a uniformly random one of them. The event $p \le \alpha$ says that
$T(Z)$ is among the top $\lfloor\alpha M\rfloor$ values of the orbit. Conditional on the orbit, the probability of that
is at most $\lfloor\alpha M\rfloor/M \le \alpha$. Averaging over orbits,

$$
P_{H_0}(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha.
$$

Equality holds when $\alpha M$ is an integer and there are no ties. The test is exact, not asymptotic.

**Random permutations.** When $M$ is too large to enumerate ($\binom{40}{20} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{11}$), draw $B$
transformations uniformly at random and count the $b$ whose statistic is at least $T(Z)$. The p-value

$$
\hat p = \frac{1 + b}{1 + B}
$$

is still valid. Under $H_0$ the observed statistic and the $B$ random ones are $B + 1$ exchangeable values, so the rank
of the observed one among them is uniform on $\set{1, \dots, B + 1}$ (with ties broken conservatively), and $P(\hat p
\le \alpha) \le \alpha$. Omitting the $+1$ can return $\hat p = 0$ and inflates the type I error.

## Worked example

Group A: $12, 15, 11, 18, 14$ (mean 14.0). Group B: $9, 10, 13, 8, 11$ (mean 10.2). The observed difference is
$T = \bar a - \bar b = 3.8$. There are $\binom{10}{5} = 252$ ways to split the ten values into two groups of five. Six
of them give a difference of at least 3.8, and twelve give an absolute difference of at least 3.8.

- One-sided permutation p-value: $6/252 = 0.024$.
- Two-sided permutation p-value: $12/252 = 0.048$.
- For comparison, <NoteLink to="welch-t-test">Welch's t-test</NoteLink> gives $t = 2.54$ and $p = 0.038$, and the
  <NoteLink to="pooled-two-sample-t-test">pooled t-test</NoteLink> gives $p = 0.035$.

The smallest two-sided p-value attainable with five per group is $2/252 = 0.008$. The discreteness of the permutation
distribution limits how small $p$ can be with tiny samples.

## Effect size

The permutation test gives a p-value, not an estimate. Report the observed statistic, such as $\bar a - \bar b$, with
an interval. Inverting permutation tests of $H_0: F_A(x) = F_B(x - \delta)$ over a grid of shifts $\delta$ gives an
exact <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> for a location shift.

## Pitfalls

- **Testing means under unequal variances.** With the raw difference in means, the permutation test is exact for
  $F_A = F_B$, not for $\mu_A = \mu_B$. If the groups have different variances and different sizes, the type I error for
  equal means can be far from $\alpha$. Use the studentised statistic.
- **Permuting what the null does not make exchangeable.** In a regression, permuting the response tests that it is
  independent of all predictors, not that one coefficient is 0.
- **Dependence.** Permuting individual observations from clustered data understates the variability. Permute at the
  level at which randomisation or independence holds.
- **Too few permutations.** $B = 1{,}000$ gives a <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo-diagnostics">Monte Carlo
  standard error</NoteLink> of about $0.007$ at $p = 0.05$. Use more when $p$ is near the threshold or when the test
  enters a <NoteLink to="multiple-testing">multiple-testing</NoteLink> correction.
