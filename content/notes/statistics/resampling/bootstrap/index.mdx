<Definition>
Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be independent draws from an unknown distribution $F$, and let $\hat\theta = s(x_1, \dots,
x_n)$ estimate a parameter $\theta$. The **bootstrap** draws $B$ resamples $x_1^*, \dots, x_n^*$, each by sampling $n$
times with replacement from the data, and computes $\hat\theta^{*b} = s(x^{*b})$ for $b = 1, \dots, B$. The spread of
the replicates $\hat\theta^{*b}$ around $\hat\theta$ estimates the spread of $\hat\theta$ around $\theta$
<Cite id="efron1979" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A standard error or <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> needs the sampling distribution of $\hat\theta$: its variation across
repeated samples from $F$. Formulas exist for means and for a few other statistics under assumptions such as normality.
For a median, a correlation coefficient, a ratio, an <Gloss name="auroc" /> or a trimmed mean there is often no usable formula. Only one
sample is available, so the sampling distribution cannot be observed directly.

## Mechanism

**The plug-in principle.** Write the parameter as a functional of the distribution, $\theta = t(F)$. The empirical
distribution $\hat F_n$ puts mass $1/n$ on each observation. The plug-in estimate is $\hat\theta = t(\hat F_n)$. The
bootstrap applies the same substitution to the sampling process itself: the unknown relation between $F$ and a sample
drawn from it is imitated by the known relation between $\hat F_n$ and a sample drawn from $\hat F_n$. Sampling $n$
values from $\hat F_n$ is sampling $n$ values with replacement from the data. So

$$
\text{distribution of } \hat\theta - \theta \text{ under } F \;\approx\; \text{distribution of } \hat\theta^* -
\hat\theta \text{ under } \hat F_n.
$$

The right-hand side is computable by Monte Carlo. The approximation has two errors: $\hat F_n \ne F$ (statistical
error, which shrinks with $n$) and $B < \infty$ (Monte Carlo error, which shrinks with $B$).

**Bootstrap standard error and bias.** The bootstrap standard error is the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of the replicates,

$$
\widehat{\text{se}}_B = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B - 1}\sum_{b=1}^B \paren{\hat\theta^{*b} - \bar\theta^*}^2}, \qquad
\bar\theta^* = \frac1B\sum_b \hat\theta^{*b},
$$

and the bootstrap bias estimate is $\bar\theta^* - \hat\theta$.

**The mean as a check.** For $\hat\theta = \bar x$ the bootstrap distribution can be computed exactly. Each resampled
value $x_i^*$ is drawn from $\hat F_n$, which has mean $\bar x$ and variance $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i -
\bar x)^2$. The $n$ draws are independent, so

$$
\var^*(\bar x^*) = \frac{\hat\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \widehat{\text{se}}_\infty = \frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n} =
\sqrt{\frac{n - 1}{n}}\cdot\frac{s}{\sqrt n},
$$

where $s$ is the sample standard deviation with divisor $n - 1$. The ideal bootstrap ($B \to \infty$) reproduces the
textbook <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> up to the factor $\sqrt{(n - 1)/n}$, which comes from
the plug-in variance.

## Bootstrap confidence intervals

Let $\hat\theta^*_{(q)}$ denote the $q$-quantile of the replicates.

- **Percentile interval.** $[\hat\theta^*_{(\alpha/2)},\ \hat\theta^*_{(1-\alpha/2)}]$. It is correct if some
  monotone transformation $\phi = m(\theta)$ makes $\hat\phi - \phi$ symmetric about 0 with the same distribution for
  every $\phi$; the interval for $\phi$ then maps back through $m^{-1}$, and quantiles commute with $m$, so $m$ need not
  be known.
- **Basic interval.** Assume the distribution of $\hat\theta - \theta$ is approximated by that of $\hat\theta^* -
  \hat\theta$. With $a$ and $b$ the $\alpha/2$ and $1 - \alpha/2$ quantiles of $\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta$,
  $P(a \le \hat\theta - \theta \le b) \approx 1 - \alpha$. Rearranging for $\theta$ gives
  $[\hat\theta - b,\ \hat\theta - a] = [2\hat\theta - \hat\theta^*_{(1-\alpha/2)},\ 2\hat\theta -
  \hat\theta^*_{(\alpha/2)}]$. It reflects the bootstrap distribution about $\hat\theta$.
- **Bootstrap-t.** Replace $\hat\theta - \theta$ by the studentised pivot $(\hat\theta - \theta)/\widehat{\text{se}}$,
  computing $\widehat{\text{se}}$ inside each resample. It is more accurate when a stable standard-error formula exists.
- **BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated).** Use the percentile interval with adjusted tail probabilities
  <Cite id="efron1987" />. The bias correction $z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\paren{\#\set{\hat\theta^{*b} < \hat\theta}/B}$
  measures the median bias of $\hat\theta^*$. The acceleration $a$, estimated from the
  <NoteLink to="jackknife">jackknife</NoteLink> values $\hat\theta_{(i)}$ with mean $\hat\theta_{(\cdot)}$ as
  $a = \sum_i (\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3 / \brack{6\paren{\sum_i (\hat\theta_{(\cdot)} -
  \hat\theta_{(i)})^2}^{3/2}}$, measures how fast the standard error changes with $\theta$. The endpoints are the
  replicate quantiles at

$$
\alpha_{1,2} = \Phi\paren{z_0 + \frac{z_0 + z_{\alpha/2,\,1-\alpha/2}}{1 - a\paren{z_0 + z_{\alpha/2,\,1-\alpha/2}}}}.
$$

With $z_0 = a = 0$ it reduces to the percentile interval. For each endpoint separately, the coverage error of BCa
and bootstrap-t intervals is of order $1/n$; that of the percentile and basic intervals is of order $1/\sqrt n$
<Cite id="efron1993" />.

## Worked example

Ten observations: $1.2, 2.5, 0.8, 3.9, 1.7, 6.3, 2.1, 0.9, 4.4, 1.5$. The mean is 2.53 and $s = 1.79$, so the formula
standard error is $s/\sqrt{10} = 0.567$ and the ideal bootstrap standard error is $\sqrt{0.9}\times 0.567 = 0.538$.
With $B = 10{,}000$ resamples (seed 1 in numpy):

| Statistic | Estimate | Bootstrap SE | Percentile 95% | Basic 95% | BCa 95% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | 2.53 | 0.536 | $[1.56, 3.65]$ | $[1.41, 3.50]$ | $[1.69, 3.83]$ |
| Median | 1.90 | 0.683 | $[1.2, 3.9]$ | $[-0.1, 2.6]$ | $[1.2, 3.9]$ |

The t interval for the mean is $[1.25, 3.81]$. The data are right-skewed, and the BCa interval for the mean shifts
right accordingly. The basic interval for the median includes negative values, although every observation is positive:
reflecting a skewed bootstrap distribution about $\hat\theta$ moves the long tail to the wrong side.

## Cost

Each replicate recomputes the statistic, so the cost is $B$ times one evaluation. About $B = 200$ replicates give a
stable standard error. Percentile and BCa intervals use tail quantiles and need $B \ge 2{,}000$. Replicates are
independent and parallelise trivially.

## When it fails

The bootstrap needs $\hat F_n$ to imitate $F$ in the respect the statistic depends on, and the statistic to depend
smoothly on the distribution <Cite id="bickel1981" />.

- **Extremes.** For $\hat\theta = \max_i x_i$, a resample contains the observed maximum with probability
  $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \to 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$, so 63% of replicates equal $\hat\theta$ exactly. The true sampling
  distribution of the maximum of a continuous variable has no atom. The bootstrap distribution is wrong for every $n$.
- **Infinite variance.** For heavy-tailed data without a finite variance the bootstrap distribution of the mean does
  not converge to the right limit.
- **Dependent data.** Resampling individual observations destroys serial or cluster correlation, and the standard
  error comes out too small. Resample blocks of consecutive observations (block bootstrap) or whole clusters.
- **Small samples.** $\hat F_n$ has at most $n$ support points. With $n = 10$, $\hat F_n$ does not represent the
  tails of $F$.
- **Parameters on a boundary.** A variance component estimated at 0 has a nonstandard sampling distribution, and the
  nonparametric bootstrap misrepresents it.

## Trade-offs

- Compared with a formula, the bootstrap needs no derivation and no normality assumption, but costs $B$ evaluations
  and still relies on <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> and on $n$ being large enough for $\hat F_n$ to resemble $F$.
- Compared with the <NoteLink to="jackknife">jackknife</NoteLink>, it handles non-smooth statistics such as the
  median, and costs more.
- A <NoteLink to="parametric-bootstrap-and-bootstrap-hypothesis-tests">parametric bootstrap</NoteLink> resamples from
  a fitted model instead of $\hat F_n$. It is more accurate when the model is right, and wrong when it is not.
