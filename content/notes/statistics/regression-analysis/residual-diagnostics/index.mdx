<Definition>
For a least-squares fit $\hat\yvec = \Hmat\yvec$ with <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">hat matrix</NoteLink> $\Hmat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$, the
**residuals** are $\evec = \yvec - \hat\yvec = (\Imat - \Hmat)\yvec$. **Residual diagnostics** are plots and tests
computed from the residuals that detect violations of the model's assumptions: a wrong mean function, non-constant
variance (**heteroscedasticity**), correlated errors and non-normal errors.
</Definition>

## Intuition

If the model is right, the residuals are estimates of the errors $\epsilonvec$ and should look like noise: no trend
against the fitted values or any predictor, constant spread, no pattern in time order. Structure in the residuals is
structure the model missed. A curve means a missing nonlinear term; a funnel means the variance grows with the mean; a
run of same-signed residuals in time order means autocorrelation.

## Residuals are not the errors

Substituting $\yvec = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonvec$ gives $\evec = (\Imat - \Hmat)\epsilonvec$, because $(\Imat -
\Hmat)\Xmat = \zeros$. With $\cov(\epsilonvec) = \sigma^2\Imat$,

$$
\cov(\evec) = \sigma^2(\Imat - \Hmat)(\Imat - \Hmat)^\top = \sigma^2(\Imat - \Hmat), \qquad \var(e_i) = \sigma^2(1 - h_{ii}),
$$

using that $\Imat - \Hmat$ is symmetric and idempotent. $h_{ii}$ is the **leverage** of observation $i$
(<NoteLink to="leverage-and-influence">leverage and influence</NoteLink>). Residuals at high-leverage points have smaller
variance, because the fit is pulled toward those points. Residuals are also correlated with each other. Two rescalings
correct for the unequal variances:

- **Standardised residual:** $r_i = e_i/(s\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}})$, with $s^2$ the residual variance estimate.
- **Externally studentised residual:** $t_i = e_i/(s_{(i)}\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}})$, where $s_{(i)}$ is computed without
  observation $i$. Under the model $t_i \sim t_{n-p-1}$, so $\abs{t_i} > 3$ flags an outlier.

## Plots

- **Residuals against fitted values.** The main plot. Curvature indicates a wrong mean function; a funnel indicates
  variance that changes with the mean.
- **Scale–location.** $\sqrt{\abs{r_i}}$ against fitted values makes changes in spread easier to see than the raw plot.
- **Residuals against each predictor**, including predictors not in the model. A trend against an omitted variable
  means it belongs in the model.
- **Residuals in time or collection order.** Runs of positive or negative residuals indicate serial correlation. The
  Durbin–Watson statistic $\sum_i (e_i - e_{i-1})^2/\sum_i e_i^2$ is near 2 without it.
- **Normal Q–Q plot.** Plot the sorted standardised residuals against the standard normal quantiles
  $\Phi^{-1}\paren{(i - 0.5)/n}$. Normal errors give a straight line. An S-shape with the ends steeper than the middle
  indicates heavy tails; a curve bending one way indicates skew. Normality matters least of the assumptions for
  coefficient tests with large $n$ and most for <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink>.

## The Breusch–Pagan test

To test for variance that depends on variables $\zvec_i$ (often the predictors), model $\sigma_i^2 = h(\gamma_0 +
\zvec_i^\top\gammavec)$ and test $H_0: \gammavec = \zeros$ <Cite id="breusch1979" />. In Koenker's studentised form
<Cite id="koenker1981" />:

1. Fit the regression and compute squared residuals $u_i = e_i^2$.
2. Regress $u_i$ on an intercept and the $q$ variables $\zvec_i$, and record the $R^2$ of this auxiliary regression.
3. Under $H_0$, $\text{LM} = nR^2 \sim \chi^2_q$ approximately.

The logic: under homoscedasticity the expected squared residual is the same for every observation, so nothing should
predict $e_i^2$. $nR^2$ is the <NoteLink to="lagrange-multipliers">Lagrange multiplier</NoteLink> (score) statistic for $\gammavec = \zeros$. The original version
divides $u_i$ by $\hat\sigma^2$ and uses half the explained sum of squares; it assumes normal errors and is oversized
when the tails are heavy, which Koenker's version corrects.

## Sandwich standard errors

When the errors are independent with $\var(\epsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2$ not constant, least squares remains unbiased but
the usual covariance formula is wrong. Write $\Omegamat = \diag(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_n^2)$. Since $\hat\betavec -
\betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\epsilonvec$,

$$
\cov(\hat\betavec) = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\,\Xmat^\top\Omegamat\Xmat\,(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}
= (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\paren{\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2\,\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top}(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}.
$$

The "bread" $(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$ surrounds the "meat" $\Xmat^\top\Omegamat\Xmat$. With constant variance, the meat
is $\sigma^2\Xmat^\top\Xmat$ and the formula collapses to $\sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$.

White's estimator replaces each $\sigma_i^2$ by $e_i^2$ <Cite id="white1980" />:

$$
\widehat{\cov}_{\text{HC0}}(\hat\betavec) = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\paren{\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2\,\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top}(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}.
$$

A single $e_i^2$ is a poor estimate of $\sigma_i^2$, but the meat is an average over $n$ observations. By the <Gloss name="lln">law of
large numbers</Gloss>, $\frac1n\sum_i e_i^2\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top - \frac1n\sum_i \sigma_i^2\xvec_i\xvec_i^\top \to \zeros$, so the
estimate is consistent without any model for the variance. In small samples $e_i^2$ underestimates $\sigma_i^2$: with
constant variance, $\expect e_i^2 = \sigma^2(1 - h_{ii})$. **HC3** uses $e_i^2/(1 - h_{ii})^2$ instead, which also guards against high-leverage points, and is
the recommended default for small $n$.

## Worked example

Ten observations with $x = 1, \dots, 10$ and

$$
y = 2.2, 3.6, 6.9, 6.1, 11.8, 8.4, 16.9, 11.2, 21.5, 14.3.
$$

The fit is $\hat y = 0.80 + 1.726\,x$. The absolute residuals grow from 0.33 at $x = 1$ to 5.17 at $x = 9$, a funnel.

- **Breusch–Pagan (Koenker).** Regressing $e_i^2$ on $x_i$ gives $R^2 = 0.747$, so $\text{LM} = 10 \times 0.747 =
  7.47$ on 1 degree of freedom, $p = 0.006$. The test rejects constant variance.
- **<NoteLink to="standard-error">Standard errors</NoteLink> of the slope.** The classical formula gives 0.361, HC0 gives 0.331 and HC3 gives 0.459.

HC0 is smaller than the classical value here, because with $n = 10$ the squared residuals understate the variances.
HC3 corrects for this and is 27% larger than the classical standard error. The observations with the largest variance
have large $x$, far from the mean of $x$, where they have the most influence on the slope. The classical formula
ignores that.

## Pitfalls

- **Testing normality of $\yvec$ instead of the residuals.** The model assumes normal errors, not a normal response; a
  response can be bimodal because a predictor is.
- **Over-reading Q–Q plots in small samples.** With $n = 20$, normal data often produce visibly curved Q–Q plots.
- **Sandwich errors do not fix the mean.** Robust standard errors correct the variance of an estimate. They do not
  correct bias from a wrong functional form or omitted variables.
- **Heteroscedasticity or wrong scale.** A funnel often disappears after modelling $\log y$, or after using a
  <Gloss name="glm">generalised linear model</Gloss> with a variance function suited to the data
  (<NoteLink to="deviance-and-generalised-linear-model-diagnostics">GLM diagnostics</NoteLink>).
- **<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink> is not checked by these plots unless there is an order.** Clustered data need cluster-robust
  standard errors, which sum $\xvec_i e_i$ within clusters before forming the meat.
