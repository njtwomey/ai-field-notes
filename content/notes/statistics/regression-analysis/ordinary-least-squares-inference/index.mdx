<Definition>
In the **normal linear model** $\yvec = \Xmat\betavec + \epsilonvec$, the design $\Xmat$ is a fixed $n \times p$ matrix
of rank $p$ (including a column of ones for the intercept), $\betavec \in \reals^p$, and $\epsilonvec \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2\Imat_n)$. The least-squares estimate $\hat\betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec$
and the residual variance $s^2 = \norm{\yvec - \Xmat\hat\betavec}^2/(n - p)$ satisfy

$$
\hat\betavec \sim \Gauss\paren{\betavec,\ \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}}, \qquad
\frac{(n - p)\,s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-p}, \qquad \hat\betavec \text{ independent of } s^2.
$$

Every exact test and interval for the coefficients follows from these three facts <Cite id="seber2003" />.
</Definition>

## The sampling distribution of the coefficients

Substitute the model into the estimate:

$$
\hat\betavec = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top(\Xmat\betavec + \epsilonvec) = \betavec + (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\epsilonvec.
$$

$\hat\betavec$ is a fixed <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear map</NoteLink> $\Amat = (\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$ applied to the Gaussian vector
$\epsilonvec$, plus a constant, so it is Gaussian. Its mean is $\betavec$ because $\expect\epsilonvec = \zeros$. Its
covariance is

$$
\cov(\hat\betavec) = \Amat\,\cov(\epsilonvec)\,\Amat^\top = \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}
= \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}.
$$

The mean and covariance need only $\expect\epsilonvec = \zeros$ and $\cov(\epsilonvec) = \sigma^2\Imat$; normality is
needed for the exact distribution.

**Residuals.** Let $\Hmat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$, the <NoteLink to="orthogonal-projection">orthogonal
projection</NoteLink> onto the <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">column space</NoteLink> of $\Xmat$. The residual vector is $\evec = \yvec - \Xmat\hat\betavec =
(\Imat - \Hmat)\yvec = (\Imat - \Hmat)\epsilonvec$, because $(\Imat - \Hmat)\Xmat = \zeros$. $\Imat - \Hmat$ is
symmetric, idempotent and of rank $n - p$. Diagonalise it as $\Qmat\Dmat\Qmat^\top$ with $\Qmat$ orthogonal and $\Dmat$
holding $n - p$ ones and $p$ zeros. Then $\zvec = \Qmat^\top\epsilonvec/\sigma \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ and

$$
\frac{\norm{\evec}^2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\epsilonvec^\top(\Imat - \Hmat)\epsilonvec}{\sigma^2} = \zvec^\top\Dmat\zvec =
\sum_{k=1}^{n-p} z_k^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-p}.
$$

So $\expect\norm{\evec}^2 = (n - p)\sigma^2$, and $s^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$. Dividing by $n$ would underestimate
it, because fitting $p$ coefficients removes $p$ dimensions of noise.

**<NoteLink to="independence">Independence</NoteLink>.** $\hat\betavec$ and $\evec$ are jointly Gaussian, being linear in $\epsilonvec$. Their
cross-covariance is $\Amat\,\sigma^2\Imat\,(\Imat - \Hmat) = \sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top(\Imat - \Hmat) =
\zeros$. Uncorrelated jointly Gaussian vectors are independent, so $\hat\betavec$ is independent of $\evec$ and hence of
$s^2$.

## t tests and intervals for one coefficient

Write $v_{jj}$ for the $j$th diagonal entry of $(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$. The <NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of $\hat\beta_j$ is
$\text{se}(\hat\beta_j) = s\sqrt{v_{jj}}$. Then

$$
T_j = \frac{\hat\beta_j - \beta_j}{s\sqrt{v_{jj}}} = \frac{(\hat\beta_j - \beta_j)/(\sigma\sqrt{v_{jj}})}{\sqrt{s^2/\sigma^2}}
\sim t_{n-p}.
$$

The numerator is standard normal, the denominator is the square root of an independent $\chi^2_{n-p}$ variable divided
by its degrees of freedom, and that ratio is the definition of <NoteLink to="student-t-distribution">Student's t</NoteLink> distribution. Testing $H_0: \beta_j = 0$
uses $T_j = \hat\beta_j/\text{se}(\hat\beta_j)$. The $(1 - \alpha)$ <NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence interval</NoteLink> is $\hat\beta_j \pm
t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-p}\,\text{se}(\hat\beta_j)$.

The test of $\beta_j = 0$ asks whether predictor $j$ adds to the fit **given all the other predictors**. Its answer
changes when other predictors enter or leave the model.

## F tests for nested models

To test $q$ linear restrictions at once, such as $H_0: \beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0$, fit the full model (residual sum of
squares $\text{RSS}_1$, $p$ coefficients) and the restricted model ($\text{RSS}_0$). Under $H_0$,

$$
F = \frac{(\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1)/q}{\text{RSS}_1/(n - p)} \sim F_{q,\, n-p}.
$$

Large $F$ rejects $H_0$. The **overall F test** compares the model with the intercept-only model ($q = p - 1$). For a
single coefficient, $F = T_j^2$.

<Derivation title="Why the F statistic has an F distribution">
Let $\Hmat_0$ and $\Hmat$ be the projections onto the column spaces of the restricted and full designs, with ranks $p -
q$ and $p$. The restricted space lies inside the full one, so $\Hmat\Hmat_0 = \Hmat_0$ and $\Hmat - \Hmat_0$ is a
projection of rank $q$, orthogonal to $\Imat - \Hmat$. Under $H_0$ the mean $\Xmat\betavec$ lies in the restricted space,
so $\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1 = \yvec^\top(\Hmat - \Hmat_0)\yvec = \epsilonvec^\top(\Hmat - \Hmat_0)\epsilonvec$ and
$\text{RSS}_1 = \epsilonvec^\top(\Imat - \Hmat)\epsilonvec$. Diagonalising both projections in a common orthonormal
basis, as for the residuals, shows $\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1 = \sigma^2\sum_{k \in K_1} z_k^2$ and $\text{RSS}_1 =
\sigma^2\sum_{k \in K_2} z_k^2$ over disjoint index sets of sizes $q$ and $n - p$. They are independent $\sigma^2\chi^2_q$
and $\sigma^2\chi^2_{n-p}$ variables, and the ratio of each divided by its degrees of freedom is $F_{q, n-p}$.
</Derivation>

## Confidence and prediction intervals

At a new input $\xvec_0$ (with a leading 1), the estimated mean response is $\hat\mu_0 = \xvec_0^\top\hat\betavec$, with
variance $\sigma^2\xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0$.

- **Confidence interval for the mean response** $\xvec_0^\top\betavec$:
  $\hat\mu_0 \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-p}\; s\sqrt{\xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0}$.
- **Prediction interval for a new observation** $\tilde y_0 = \xvec_0^\top\betavec + \tilde\epsilon_0$:
  $\hat\mu_0 \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-p}\; s\sqrt{1 + \xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0}$.

The prediction error is $\tilde y_0 - \hat\mu_0 = \tilde\epsilon_0 - \xvec_0^\top(\hat\betavec - \betavec)$. The new
noise $\tilde\epsilon_0$ is independent of the data, so the two variances add: $\sigma^2(1 +
\xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0)$. As $n$ grows the confidence interval shrinks to zero width; the prediction
interval shrinks to $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma$, because the noise of a new observation never averages out. Both widen
away from the centre of the data, since $\xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0$ grows with the distance of $\xvec_0$
from the mean input.

## Worked example

Eight observations with $x = 1, 2, \dots, 8$ and $y = 3.1, 3.9, 6.8, 6.2, 9.5, 10.1, 13.4, 12.9$. The fit is $\hat y =
1.311 + 1.539\,x$, with $\text{RSS} = 5.364$ on $n - p = 6$ degrees of freedom, so $s^2 = 0.894$ and $s = 0.946$. Here
$(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$ has diagonal $0.607$ and $0.0238$.

| Coefficient | Estimate | SE | $t$ | $p$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intercept | 1.311 | 0.737 | 1.78 | 0.126 |
| Slope | 1.539 | 0.146 | 10.55 | 0.00004 |

With $t_{0.975, 6} = 2.447$, the 95% interval for the slope is $1.539 \pm 2.447 \times 0.146 = [1.18, 1.90]$. The
overall F statistic is $111.3 = 10.55^2$.

| $x_0$ | $\hat\mu_0$ | 95% CI for the mean | 95% prediction interval |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4.5 (the mean of $x$) | 8.24 | $[7.42, 9.06]$ | $[5.78, 10.69]$ |
| 10 (outside the data) | 16.70 | $[14.58, 18.83]$ | $[13.56, 19.85]$ |

At $x_0 = 4.5$, $\xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0 = 1/n = 0.125$ and the prediction interval is three times as
wide as the confidence interval. At $x_0 = 10$ the quantity is 0.845 and both intervals widen.

## Assumptions

In order of importance for the validity of the tests:

1. **Correct mean.** $\expect[\yvec] = \Xmat\betavec$. Omitted variables or a wrong functional form bias $\hat\betavec$,
   and no standard error fixes that.
2. **Independent errors.** Correlated errors (repeated measures, time series, clusters) make the standard errors wrong,
   usually too small.
3. **Constant variance.** With heteroscedastic errors the formula $\sigma^2(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$ is wrong; use
   sandwich standard errors (<NoteLink to="residual-diagnostics">residual diagnostics</NoteLink>).
4. **Normal errors.** Needed for exact small-sample t and <NoteLink to="f-distribution">F distributions</NoteLink>. For large $n$, the <Gloss name="clt">central limit theorem</Gloss>
   makes the tests approximately valid without it, except for <NoteLink to="prediction-intervals">prediction intervals</NoteLink>, which depend on the error
   distribution itself.

## Pitfalls

- **Many t tests instead of one F test.** Testing each of ten coefficients at 5% inflates the chance of a false
  rejection; the F test controls it for the joint hypothesis.
- **Reading a non-significant coefficient as no effect.** A large standard error, often from
  <NoteLink to="multicollinearity">correlated predictors</NoteLink>, makes the test weak.
- **Using the confidence interval for prediction.** It covers the mean response, not a new observation.
- **Inference after selection.** Choosing predictors by their <NoteLink to="p-value">p-values</NoteLink> and then reporting the same p-values
  overstates significance; the sampling distribution above assumes a design fixed in advance.
