<Definition>
In a least-squares fit with $n \times p$ design $\Xmat$ and hat matrix $\Hmat = \Xmat(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top$,
the **leverage** of observation $i$ is the diagonal entry $h_{ii} = \xvec_i^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_i$, where
$\xvec_i^\top$ is row $i$ of $\Xmat$ <Cite id="hoaglin1978" />. An observation is **influential** if deleting it
changes the fit substantially. **Cook's distance** measures the change in all fitted values when observation $i$ is
deleted <Cite id="cook1977" />:

$$
D_i = \frac{\norm{\hat\yvec - \hat\yvec_{(i)}}^2}{p\,s^2},
$$

where $\hat\yvec_{(i)} = \Xmat\hat\betavec_{(i)}$ uses the coefficients fitted without observation $i$ and $s^2$ is the
residual variance of the full fit.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Leverage is potential. A point whose inputs are far from the rest acts like a long lever on the fitted plane: the fit
must pass close to it. Whether that potential is used depends on the response. A high-leverage point that lies on the
trend of the other points confirms the fit and changes nothing when removed. The same point displaced from the trend
rotates the fit toward itself. An **outlier** (a large residual) at low leverage shifts the fit a little and is easy to
spot. The dangerous case is high leverage with a displaced response: the fit moves toward the point, so its own
residual stays small and hides it.

## Leverage

**Leverage is self-sensitivity.** The fitted values are $\hat\yvec = \Hmat\yvec$, so $\hat y_i = \sum_j h_{ij}y_j$ and
$\partial\hat y_i/\partial y_i = h_{ii}$. Leverage is the fraction by which a change in $y_i$ moves its own fitted
value.

**Range.** $\Hmat$ is symmetric and idempotent, so $h_{ii} = (\Hmat\Hmat)_{ii} = \sum_j h_{ij}^2 = h_{ii}^2 +
\sum_{j \ne i} h_{ij}^2 \ge h_{ii}^2$. Hence $0 \le h_{ii} \le 1$. $h_{ii} = 1$ means the fit passes exactly through
point $i$.

**Average.** $\sum_i h_{ii} = \trace(\Hmat) = \trace\paren{(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Xmat} = \trace(\Imat_p) = p$,
using the cyclic property of the trace. The mean leverage is $p/n$, and $h_{ii} > 2p/n$ is a common flag.

**Simple regression.** With an intercept and one predictor, $h_{ii} = \frac1n + \frac{(x_i - \bar x)^2}{\sum_j (x_j -
\bar x)^2}$. Leverage grows with squared distance from the mean input and does not depend on $y$ at all. In several
dimensions $h_{ii} - 1/n$ is a scaled Mahalanobis distance of $\xvec_i$ from the centroid of the inputs, so a point can
have high leverage while being unremarkable on each predictor separately.

## Deleting one observation

Refitting without observation $i$ does not require a new regression. Write $\Amat = \Xmat^\top\Xmat$ and $\xvec =
\xvec_i$. Deleting the observation gives $\Amat - \xvec\xvec^\top$ and $\Xmat^\top\yvec - \xvec y_i$. The
Sherman–Morrison formula gives

$$
(\Amat - \xvec\xvec^\top)^{-1} = \Amat^{-1} + \frac{\Amat^{-1}\xvec\xvec^\top\Amat^{-1}}{1 - h_{ii}},
$$

since $\xvec^\top\Amat^{-1}\xvec = h_{ii}$. Multiply it by $\Xmat^\top\yvec - \xvec y_i$ and use $\Amat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\yvec
= \hat\betavec$ and $\xvec^\top\hat\betavec = \hat y_i$:

$$
\hat\betavec_{(i)} = \hat\betavec - \Amat^{-1}\xvec\,y_i + \frac{\Amat^{-1}\xvec\,(\hat y_i - h_{ii}y_i)}{1 - h_{ii}}
= \hat\betavec - \frac{\Amat^{-1}\xvec\,\paren{(1 - h_{ii})y_i - \hat y_i + h_{ii}y_i}}{1 - h_{ii}}.
$$

The bracket is $y_i - \hat y_i = e_i$, so

$$
\hat\betavec - \hat\betavec_{(i)} = \frac{(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_i\, e_i}{1 - h_{ii}}.
$$

The change is proportional to the residual and amplified by $1/(1 - h_{ii})$. The same identity gives the
leave-one-out residual $y_i - \hat y_{(i)} = e_i/(1 - h_{ii})$, which is why leave-one-out
<NoteLink to="cross-validation">cross-validation</NoteLink> of least squares needs no refitting.

## Cook's distance

Substitute the deletion formula into the definition. $\hat\yvec - \hat\yvec_{(i)} = \Xmat(\hat\betavec -
\hat\betavec_{(i)})$, so

$$
\norm{\hat\yvec - \hat\yvec_{(i)}}^2 = \frac{e_i^2\,\xvec_i^\top\Amat^{-1}\Xmat^\top\Xmat\Amat^{-1}\xvec_i}{(1 - h_{ii})^2}
= \frac{e_i^2\,h_{ii}}{(1 - h_{ii})^2}.
$$

Dividing by $p s^2$ and writing $r_i = e_i/(s\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}})$ for the standardised residual,

$$
D_i = \frac{r_i^2}{p}\cdot\frac{h_{ii}}{1 - h_{ii}}.
$$

Influence is the product of outlyingness in $y$ ($r_i^2$) and outlyingness in $\xvec$ ($h_{ii}/(1 - h_{ii})$). Either
alone can be harmless. Cook compared $D_i$ with the quantiles of the $F_{p,\, n-p}$ distribution, because $D_i$ has the
form of the F statistic for moving $\betavec$ from $\hat\betavec$ to $\hat\betavec_{(i)}$. $D_i$ above the median of
$F_{p, n-p}$ (about 0.7 to 1 for most designs) means deleting the point moves the estimate outside a 50% confidence
region.

**Coefficient-level measures.** $\text{DFBETAS}_{ij} = (\hat\beta_j - \hat\beta_{j(i)})/(s_{(i)}\sqrt{v_{jj}})$, with
$v_{jj}$ the diagonal of $(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}$ and $s_{(i)}$ the residual standard deviation without observation
$i$, measures the change in one coefficient in standard errors; $\abs{\text{DFBETAS}} > 2/\sqrt n$ is a common flag
<Cite id="belsley1980" />. $\text{DFFITS}_i$ is the analogous change in the observation's own fitted value.

## Worked example

Eight points $x = 1, \dots, 8$, $y = 3.1, 3.9, 6.8, 6.2, 9.5, 10.1, 13.4, 12.9$ have the fit $\hat y = 1.311 + 1.539\,x$.
Add a ninth point in three places ($n = 9$, $p = 2$, median of $F_{2,7} = 0.77$):

| Ninth point | $h_{99}$ | Studentised residual | Cook's $D$ | New fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(16, 26.0)$, on the trend | 0.766 | 0.03 | 0.002 | $1.295 + 1.543\,x$ |
| $(16, 14.0)$, off the trend | 0.766 | $-6.11$ | 9.87 | $4.404 + 0.774\,x$ |
| $(4.5, 14.0)$, central outlier | 0.111 | 5.75 | 0.37 | $1.951 + 1.539\,x$ |

The two points at $x = 16$ have the same leverage, because leverage ignores $y$. The one on the trend leaves the fit
unchanged. The one off the trend halves the slope; its DFBETAS for the slope is $-10.2$. Its raw residual, $-2.79$, is
not remarkable, because the fit has moved toward it; only the studentised residual, which uses $s_{(i)}$, and Cook's
distance reveal it. The central outlier has the largest raw residual but lies at the mean of the other $x$ values, so
the slope does not change at all and only the intercept moves.

## Pitfalls

- **Deleting influential points automatically.** Influence is a reason to investigate, not to delete. The point may be
  a recording error, a different population, or the most informative observation in the data.
- **Masking.** Two high-leverage points close together each hide the other: deleting either one leaves the other to
  hold the fit in place. Single-deletion diagnostics miss them.
- **Screening residuals only.** A high-leverage outlier pulls the fit toward itself and can have a small raw residual.
  Look at leverage and Cook's distance too.
- **Extrapolation.** A new input with $\xvec_0^\top(\Xmat^\top\Xmat)^{-1}\xvec_0$ larger than every $h_{ii}$ is outside
  the region the data support, and its prediction interval is wide for that reason.
