<Definition>
A random variable $X$ has a Poisson distribution with rate $\lambda > 0$, written $X \sim \Poisson(\lambda)$, if

$$
P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

$X$ counts events in a fixed interval of time or space. $\lambda$ is the expected number of events in that interval.
</Definition>

## At a glance

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Parameter | Rate $\lambda > 0$ |
| Support | $k \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ |
| pmf | $e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k / k!$ |
| Mean | $\lambda$ |
| Variance | $\lambda$ |
| Mode | $\lfloor \lambda \rfloor$ (also $\lambda - 1$ when $\lambda$ is an integer) |

## Where it comes from

Divide the interval into $n$ equal pieces. Suppose each piece holds an event with probability $p_n$, independently of
the others. The count is then $\Binom(n, p_n)$. Let $n \to \infty$ with $n p_n \to \lambda$. The binomial pmf tends to
the Poisson pmf <Cite id="wasserman2004" />. This limit is called the law of rare events.

<BinomialLimit />

The approximation error has an explicit bound. The total-variation distance between $\Binom(n, \lambda/n)$ and
$\Poisson(\lambda)$ is at most $\lambda^2/n$ <Cite id="lecam1960" />. The Poisson model is therefore accurate when
events are individually rare, whatever the number of opportunities.

<Derivation title="The binomial limit">
Set $p_n = \lambda/n$. Then

$$
\binom{n}{k}\paren{\frac{\lambda}{n}}^k \paren{1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}}^{n-k}
= \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \cdot \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k} \cdot \paren{1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}}^{n} \cdot
\paren{1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}}^{-k}.
$$

As $n \to \infty$ with $k$ fixed, the second factor tends to 1, the third to $e^{-\lambda}$ and the fourth to 1. The
limit is $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$.
</Derivation>

## Properties

**Equidispersion.** The mean and the variance are both $\lambda$. Count data whose sample variance is much larger than
its mean is overdispersed, and a Poisson model will understate its uncertainty.

<Derivation title="Mean and variance">
Shift the summation index with $m = k - 1$:

$$
\expect[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} k\, e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\lambda^m}{m!} = \lambda.
$$

Write $k^2 = k(k-1) + k$. The factorial moment is
$\expect[X(X-1)] = e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k \ge 2} \lambda^k/(k-2)! = \lambda^2$. So
$\expect[X^2] = \lambda^2 + \lambda$ and $\var(X) = \expect[X^2] - \expect[X]^2 = \lambda$.
</Derivation>

**Closure under sums.** If $X_1 \sim \Poisson(\lambda_1)$ and $X_2 \sim \Poisson(\lambda_2)$ are independent, then
$X_1 + X_2 \sim \Poisson(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$. Merging independent streams of events adds their rates.

<Derivation title="Closure under sums">
Convolve the two pmfs and apply the binomial theorem:

$$
P(X_1 + X_2 = k) = \sum_{j=0}^{k} e^{-\lambda_1}\frac{\lambda_1^j}{j!}\, e^{-\lambda_2}\frac{\lambda_2^{k-j}}{(k-j)!}
= \frac{e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\lambda_1^j\lambda_2^{k-j}
= e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)}\frac{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^k}{k!}.
$$
</Derivation>

**Thinning.** If each event of a $\Poisson(\lambda)$ count is kept independently with probability $q$, the kept events
are $\Poisson(q\lambda)$.

## Relations to other distributions

- **Binomial.** $\Poisson(\lambda)$ is the limit of $\Binom(n, \lambda/n)$ as $n \to \infty$.
- **Exponential.** In a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, the waiting times between events are independent
  $\Exp(\lambda)$.
- **Gamma and negative binomial.** Let the rate itself vary: $\Lambda \sim \GammaD(r, \beta)$ with shape $r$ and rate
  $\beta$, and $X \mid \Lambda \sim \Poisson(\Lambda)$. Then $X$ is negative binomial with mean $r/\beta$ and variance
  $r/\beta + r/\beta^2$. The extra variance models overdispersion.
- **Gaussian.** For large $\lambda$, $\Poisson(\lambda)$ is approximately $\Gauss(\lambda, \lambda)$.

## In machine learning

Poisson regression models a count $y$ with rate $\lambda = \exp(\wvec^\top \xvec)$. It is a generalised linear model
with a log link <Cite id="nelder1972" />. Its negative log-likelihood, $\lambda - y \log \lambda$ up to a constant, is
the Poisson loss used for counts such as clicks, arrivals or photon counts.

## Pitfalls

- **Overdispersion.** Real counts often vary more than their mean. Use a negative binomial model, or check the ratio
  of variance to mean first.
- **Excess zeros.** Many zeros from a separate process (a shop that is closed, a sensor that is off) need a
  zero-inflated model.
- **Exposure.** $\lambda$ is a rate per interval. When intervals differ in length, model $\lambda = t \cdot r$ with the
  exposure $t$ as an offset.
