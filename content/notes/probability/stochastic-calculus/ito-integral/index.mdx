<Definition>
Let $W_t$ be a standard <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink> and $H_t$ a process that is
**non-anticipating**: $H_t$ depends only on the path of $W$ up to time $t$. Suppose $\expect\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt < \infty$.
For a partition $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = T$ whose largest step tends to 0, the **Itô integral** is the limit in
mean square

$$
\int_0^T H_t\, dW_t = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} H_{t_j}\, \paren{W_{t_{j+1}} - W_{t_j}}.
$$

The integrand is evaluated at the **left end** of each step <Cite id="ito1944" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: betting before the coin is tossed

Think of $W_t$ as the price of a fair asset and $H_t$ as the number of units held at time $t$. Over the step from
$t_j$ to $t_{j+1}$ the holding is $H_{t_j}$ and the price moves by $\Delta W_j = W_{t_{j+1}} - W_{t_j}$, so the gain
is $H_{t_j}\Delta W_j$. The Itô integral is the total gain. The holding must be chosen before the move is seen: that is
what "left end" and "non-anticipating" mean. A trader who could set $H$ with knowledge of the next move would win
systematically.

The ordinary <NoteLink to="riemann-stieltjes-integral">Riemann–Stieltjes integral</NoteLink> does not care where in each step the integrand is evaluated, because for a
smooth integrator the choices differ by a vanishing amount. For Brownian motion they do not, because each increment
$\Delta W_j$ is of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ and the products of increments add up to a finite amount. The left end is
the choice that keeps the integral a fair game, with mean zero.

A second picture avoids money altogether. Think of $dW_t$ as white noise, a hiss of tiny independent random kicks, and
of $H_t$ as a volume knob. The Itô integral is the total noise that comes out of the speaker when the hiss is played
through the knob. Turning the knob up makes the accumulated noise larger; turning it to zero silences it; turning it
negative flips the sign of the kicks. In the examples below, $h(t)$ is a knob setting chosen in advance, and the question
is always the same: how large is the accumulated noise?

## The integrand $H$: what it is and what it is not

The integrand $H$ is a **stochastic process**: at every time $t$, $H_t$ is a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink>. Fix one realisation of the
noise, one whole Brownian path, and $H$ becomes an ordinary function of time: the knob setting over time along that
path. Draw a different path and the knob may be turned differently. The integral $\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t$ is therefore a
random variable too, one number per path.

**The one rule: no peeking.** $H_t$ may be computed from anything known at time $t$: the path $W_s$ for every
$s \le t$, fixed parameters, and other randomness independent of the future kicks. It may not use the path after time
$t$. Formally, $H_t$ is measurable with respect to the history $\Fcal_t$ of the path up to $t$; such a process is
called **adapted** or **non-anticipating**. The rule is what makes the left-point sum fair: the knob for the step from
$t_j$ to $t_{j+1}$ is set at $t_j$, before the kick $\Delta W_j$ arrives.

**Allowed integrands**, from simplest to most general:

- **A deterministic function** $h(t)$, the same on every path: a knob set in advance, such as $h(t) = t$ or
  $h(t) = e^{-\theta(T - t)}$. These give <NoteLink to="gaussian-integral">Gaussian integrals</NoteLink>, as the next sections show.
- **A function of the current position**, $H_t = f(W_t)$: for example $W_t$, $W_t^2$ or $\sin W_t$. The knob follows
  where the path is now.
- **A function of the past path**: the running maximum $\max_{s \le t} W_s$, the running average
  $\tfrac1t \int_0^t W_s\,ds$, or the lagged value $W_{t - 0.5}$ (taken as 0 for $t < 0.5$). All of these are known at
  time $t$.
- **A decision based on the past**: $H_t = 1$ once the path has touched the level 1 and $0$ before. This is a stopping
  rule, and it is allowed because at each time the path so far says whether the level has been touched.
- **The state of a noisy system**: $H_t = g(X_t)$, where $X_t$ solves a <Gloss name="sde">stochastic differential equation</Gloss> driven by the
  same $W$. This is the case that SDEs need.

**What $H$ does not need to be.** It need not be continuous (the step functions below jump), differentiable, bounded,
Gaussian or deterministic. The Itô integral asks for far less of its integrand than of its integrator.

**Forbidden integrands** all look at the future:

- The end point $W_T$, used as the knob at every time.
- The next value $W_{t + 0.1}$, or the sign of the coming increment.
- "1 if the path will finish above 0".

**Why peeking breaks the integral.** Take $H_t = W_T$ for every $t$, a constant knob chosen by looking at the end of the
path. The left-point sum is $\sum_j W_T\,\Delta W_j = W_T \sum_j \Delta W_j = W_T \cdot W_T = W_T^2$, whose mean is
$T$, not 0. A knob that knows where the path is going always turns up when the noise is about to go its way, and the
accumulated noise stops being a fair game. The zero mean, the isometry and the martingale property below all fail.

**The size condition.** The knob must not be too loud on average: $\expect\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt < \infty$. The knob
$h(t) = t^{-1/4}$ on $[0, 1]$ blows up at $t = 0$ but is allowed, because $\int_0^1 t^{-1/2}\,dt = 2$ is finite. The
knob $h(t) = t^{-1/2}$ is not, because $\int_0^1 t^{-1}\,dt$ diverges: the noise near time 0 would be infinitely loud.
Integrands that fail this condition but are finite on every path can still be integrated as local martingales
<Cite id="karatzas1998" />.

## The simplest case: a step integrand

Let the knob be constant on each step: $H_t = h_j$ for $t_j \le t < t_{j+1}$, with $h_j$ fixed numbers. The integral is
the finite sum

$$
\int_0^T H_t\, dW_t = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} h_j\, \Delta W_j, \qquad \Delta W_j = W_{t_{j+1}} - W_{t_j}.
$$

Its distribution follows from four facts, each used once.

1. **Each increment is Gaussian with variance equal to its duration.** By the definition of Brownian motion,
   $\Delta W_j \sim \Gauss(0,\ t_{j+1} - t_j)$. Over a step of length $0.5$ the kick has variance $0.5$.
2. **Scaling a Gaussian scales its variance by the square.** If $Z \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$ and $c$ is a number, then
   $cZ$ is Gaussian with mean $c \cdot 0 = 0$ and variance $\var(cZ) = \expect[c^2 Z^2] = c^2\sigma^2$. So
   $h_j\,\Delta W_j \sim \Gauss\paren{0,\ h_j^2\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)}$. The sign of $h_j$ disappears in the square.
3. **Increments over non-overlapping steps are independent.** This is also part of the definition of Brownian motion.
   So the terms $h_j\,\Delta W_j$ are independent.
4. **A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, and the variances add.** For independent $X \sim \Gauss(0, a)$ and
   $Y \sim \Gauss(0, b)$, the <Gloss name="mgf">moment generating functions</Gloss> multiply:
   $\expect[e^{s(X+Y)}] = \expect[e^{sX}]\,\expect[e^{sY}] = e^{as^2/2} e^{bs^2/2} = e^{(a+b)s^2/2}$, which is the moment
   generating function of $\Gauss(0, a + b)$.

Putting the four facts together,

$$
\int_0^T H_t\, dW_t = \sum_j h_j\, \Delta W_j \sim \Gauss\paren{0,\ \sum_j h_j^2\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)} =
\Gauss\paren{0,\ \int_0^T H_t^2\,dt}.
$$

The last equality holds because $H_t^2$ is the constant $h_j^2$ on each step, so its ordinary integral is the sum of
height times width. The variance of the accumulated noise is the area under the squared knob setting.

A general integrand is approximated by step integrands, and the integral is defined as the limit of their integrals.
The approximation converges in mean square, not path by path; this is the measure theory the definition sweeps aside
<Cite id="oksendal2003" />.

## Worked examples with a knob set in advance

In each example the integrand $h(t)$ is a fixed function of time, not of the path, so the four facts apply and the
integral is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance $\int_0^T h(t)^2\,dt$.

**1. A constant knob.** Take $h(t) = c$ on $[0, T]$. For any partition, the sum telescopes:
$\sum_j c\,\Delta W_j = c\,(W_{t_1} - W_{t_0} + W_{t_2} - W_{t_1} + \dots + W_{t_n} - W_{t_{n-1}}) = c\,(W_T - W_0)
= c\,W_T$. So $\int_0^T c\,dW_t = c\,W_T \sim \Gauss(0, c^2 T)$, and the variance formula agrees:
$\int_0^T c^2\,dt = c^2 T$. With $c = 3$ and $T = 2$, the accumulated noise is $\Gauss(0, 18)$, with <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> $\sqrt{18} \approx 4.24$.

**2. One unit, then three.** Take $h = 1$ on $[0, 1)$ and $h = 3$ on $[1, 2]$. The integral is
$1 \cdot (W_1 - W_0) + 3 \cdot (W_2 - W_1)$. Step by step:

- $W_1 - W_0 = W_1 \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, a kick over a step of length 1 (fact 1).
- $W_2 - W_1 \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, another step of length 1 (fact 1), independent of the first (fact 3).
- $3(W_2 - W_1) \sim \Gauss(0, 3^2 \times 1) = \Gauss(0, 9)$ (fact 2).
- The sum is $\Gauss(0, 1 + 9) = \Gauss(0, 10)$ (fact 4).

Check with the formula: $\int_0^2 h(t)^2\,dt = 1^2 \times 1 + 3^2 \times 1 = 10$.

**3. Three settings, one of them negative.** Take $h = 2$ on $[0, 0.5)$, $h = -1$ on $[0.5, 1.5)$ and $h = 0.5$ on
$[1.5, 2]$.

- Variances of the three pieces: $2^2 \times 0.5 = 2$, then $(-1)^2 \times 1 = 1$, then $0.5^2 \times 0.5 = 0.125$.
- Total: $\int_0^2 h^2\,dt = 3.125$, so the integral is $\Gauss(0, 3.125)$, with standard deviation $1.768$.
- On one path, suppose the three increments are $0.4$, $-0.7$ and $0.2$. The integral on that path is
  $2 \times 0.4 + (-1) \times (-0.7) + 0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.8 + 0.7 + 0.1 = 1.6$. The negative knob turned a downward kick
  into a positive contribution.
- The chance that the integral exceeds 3 in size is $2\,\Pr(Z > 3/1.768) = 0.090$.

**4. A knob that turns up steadily: $h(t) = t$ on $[0, 1]$.** This integrand is not a step function, so approximate it
by one. With $n$ equal steps of width $1/n$, the left-point value on step $j$ is $t_j = j/n$, and the step integral is
$\sum_j t_j\,\Delta W_j \sim \Gauss\paren{0,\ \sum_j t_j^2 \cdot \tfrac1n}$ by the four facts. The variance is a <NoteLink to="fundamental-theorem-of-calculus">Riemann
sum</NoteLink> for $\int_0^1 t^2\,dt$:

| steps $n$ | variance $\sum_j t_j^2 / n$ |
| --- | --- |
| 4 | $(0 + 0.0625 + 0.25 + 0.5625)/4 = 0.21875$ |
| 10 | $0.285$ |
| 100 | $0.32835$ |
| 1000 | $0.33283$ |

The limit is $\int_0^1 t^2\,dt = 1/3$, so $\int_0^1 t\,dW_t \sim \Gauss(0, 1/3)$. Early kicks are played quietly and late
kicks loudly, so the total has less variance than $W_1$, whose variance is 1.

**5. The same integral by summation by parts.** For a smooth deterministic knob, the ordinary product rule survives.
On a partition, $\sum_j t_j\,\Delta W_j = t_n W_{t_n} - t_0 W_{t_0} - \sum_j W_{t_{j+1}}\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)$, which is
exact algebra: expand both sides and the terms match. Take the four-step path used below,
$W = (0, 0.3, -0.2, 0.4, 0.6)$ at $t = (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1)$.

- Left-point sum: $0 \times 0.3 + 0.25 \times (-0.5) + 0.5 \times 0.6 + 0.75 \times 0.2 = -0.125 + 0.3 + 0.15 = 0.325$.
- By parts: $1 \times 0.6 - 0 - (0.3 - 0.2 + 0.4 + 0.6) \times 0.25 = 0.6 - 0.275 = 0.325$.

In the limit, $\int_0^T t\,dW_t = T\,W_T - \int_0^T W_t\,dt$, with no correction term. The correction that appears in
the next section comes from products $\Delta W \cdot \Delta W$; here the products are $\Delta t \cdot \Delta W$, which add
up to nothing.

**6. A knob that fades: $h(t) = e^{-\theta(T - t)}$.** Kicks from long ago are turned down, recent ones kept almost at
full volume. The variance is

$$
\int_0^T e^{-2\theta(T - t)}\,dt = \brack{\frac{e^{-2\theta(T - t)}}{2\theta}}_0^T = \frac{1 - e^{-2\theta T}}{2\theta}.
$$

With $\theta = 1$ and $T = 1$ this is $(1 - e^{-2})/2 = 0.432$; 20,000 simulated paths give $0.429$. As $T \to \infty$
the variance settles at $1/(2\theta)$: old noise is forgotten as fast as new noise arrives. This integral is the noise
term of the <NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>, and it is why that
process has a stationary Gaussian distribution.

## Worked example: a knob that follows the path, $\int_0^T W\, dW$

Now let the knob depend on the noise itself: $H_t = W_t$, so the volume at time $t$ is wherever the path has got to.
Fact 2 no longer applies, because $h_j = W_{t_j}$ is random, and the sum of products of Gaussians is not Gaussian. What
survives is the left-point rule: $W_{t_j}$ is known before the kick $\Delta W_j$ arrives, so each term still has mean
zero.

Ordinary calculus suggests $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W_T^2$. The Itô integral gives something else.

**By hand.** Write $W_j = W_{t_j}$. For every step,

$$
W_{j+1}^2 - W_j^2 = (W_j + \Delta W_j)^2 - W_j^2 = 2W_j\,\Delta W_j + (\Delta W_j)^2.
$$

Sum over $j$. The left side telescopes to $W_T^2 - W_0^2 = W_T^2$. Rearranging,

$$
\sum_j W_j\, \Delta W_j = \tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12\sum_j (\Delta W_j)^2.
$$

The left side is the Itô sum. The last sum is the quadratic variation of the path, which converges in mean square to
$T$ (see <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink>). Therefore

$$
\int_0^T W_t\, dW_t = \tfrac12\paren{W_T^2 - T}.
$$

The extra $-\tfrac12 T$ has a clear source. At every step the left-point sum misses $\tfrac12(\Delta W_j)^2$, and
these misses add up to $\tfrac12 T$.

**The result is not Gaussian.** At $T = 1$, $W_1 \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, so $\int_0^1 W\,dW = \tfrac12(W_1^2 - 1)$ is half of
a chi-squared variable with one degree of freedom, shifted down by $\tfrac12$. It has mean
$\tfrac12(\expect[W_1^2] - 1) = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$, as every Itô integral must, but it can never be below $-\tfrac12$,
and it has a long right tail. The chance that it is below $-0.4$ is
$\Pr(W_1^2 < 0.2) = 0.345$, while a Gaussian with the same variance $\tfrac12$ puts only $0.286$ there. Mean zero and the
variance from the isometry hold for every integrand; the Gaussian shape holds only when the knob is set in advance.

**On a path with four steps.** Take $T = 1$, steps of $0.25$, and the path $W = (0, 0.3, -0.2, 0.4, 0.6)$. The
increments are $(0.3, -0.5, 0.6, 0.2)$.

- Left-point sum: $0 \times 0.3 + 0.3 \times (-0.5) + (-0.2) \times 0.6 + 0.4 \times 0.2 = -0.19$.
- Identity: $\tfrac12 W_T^2 - \tfrac12\sum(\Delta W)^2 = 0.18 - \tfrac12 \times 0.74 = -0.19$.
- Right-point sum: $0.3 \times 0.3 + (-0.2)(-0.5) + 0.4 \times 0.6 + 0.6 \times 0.2 = 0.55 = 0.18 + 0.37$.
- Average of the ends: $0.18 = \tfrac12 W_T^2$ exactly, on every path.

The left and right sums differ by $\sum(\Delta W)^2 = 0.74$, close to $T = 1$ even with four steps.

**By simulation.** Over 20,000 simulated paths with 1,000 steps on $[0, 1]$, the left-point sums average $-0.002$, the
right-point sums $0.998$ and the midpoint sums $0.498$, against the exact means $0$, $T = 1$ and $T/2 = 0.5$. The
left-point sum differs from $\tfrac12(W_T^2 - T)$ by $0.018$ on average, which is the discretisation error at 1,000
steps.

## Properties

For non-anticipating $H$ and $K$ with finite $\expect\int H^2\,dt$:

- **Linearity.** $\int (aH + bK)\,dW = a\int H\,dW + b\int K\,dW$.
- **Zero mean.** $\expect\brack{\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t} = 0$. Each term $H_{t_j}\Delta W_j$ has mean zero, because
  $\Delta W_j$ is independent of $H_{t_j}$ and has mean zero.
- **Itô isometry.** $\expect\brack{\paren{\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t}^2} = \expect\brack{\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt}$.
- **Martingale.** $M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s$ is a <NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink>: the expected
  future gain from a fair game is zero whatever has happened so far.

The isometry is proved for a step integrand; the general case follows by the limit. Expand the square of
$\sum_j H_j\Delta W_j$, where $H_j = H_{t_j}$:

$$
\expect\brack{\paren{\sum_j H_j \Delta W_j}^2} = \sum_j \expect\brack{H_j^2 (\Delta W_j)^2} + 2\sum_{i < j}
\expect\brack{H_i \Delta W_i H_j\, \Delta W_j}.
$$

In a cross term with $i < j$, everything except $\Delta W_j$ is known at time $t_j$, and $\Delta W_j$ is independent
of it with mean zero. So each cross term is zero. In a diagonal term, $H_j$ is independent of $\Delta W_j$, so
$\expect[H_j^2(\Delta W_j)^2] = \expect[H_j^2]\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)$. The sum is
$\expect\sum_j H_j^2(t_{j+1} - t_j)$, which is $\expect\int_0^T H_t^2\,dt$ for a step integrand. Both steps use the
left-point evaluation: with the right end, $H_j$ would depend on $\Delta W_j$ and neither step would hold.

## Worked examples of the isometry

**Variance of $\int_0^T W\,dW$.** The isometry gives $\expect\int_0^T W_t^2\,dt = \int_0^T t\,dt = T^2/2$. The closed
form agrees: $\var\brack{\tfrac12(W_T^2 - T)} = \tfrac14\var(W_T^2) = \tfrac14 \times 2T^2 = T^2/2$, using
$\var(W_T^2) = \expect[W_T^4] - T^2 = 3T^2 - T^2$. The simulation above gives a sample variance of $0.506$ at $T = 1$.

**A deterministic integrand.** For $H_t = t$ the integral $\int_0^T t\,dW_t$ is a limit of Gaussians with mean zero,
so it is Gaussian, with variance $\int_0^T t^2\,dt = T^3/3$. At $T = 1$ the simulated variance is $0.330$ against
$1/3$. In general, for a deterministic function $h$, $\int_0^T h(t)\,dW_t \sim \Gauss\paren{0, \int_0^T h(t)^2\,dt}$.
This fact solves the <NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink> exactly.

**Covariance of two integrals.** Applying the isometry to $H + K$ and subtracting the squares gives
$\expect\brack{\int H\,dW \int K\,dW} = \expect\int H_t K_t\,dt$. For $\int_0^1 dW = W_1$ and $\int_0^1 t\,dW$ the
covariance is $\int_0^1 t\,dt = \tfrac12$.

## Itô against Stratonovich

The **Stratonovich integral** evaluates the integrand at the average of the two ends of each step
<Cite id="stratonovich1966" />:

$$
\int_0^T H_t \circ dW_t = \lim_{n \to \infty}\sum_j \tfrac12\paren{H_{t_j} + H_{t_{j+1}}}\,\Delta W_j.
$$

For $H = W$, the four-step example shows the answer: the sum is exactly $\tfrac12 W_T^2$ on every path, so
$\int_0^T W\circ dW = \tfrac12 W_T^2$, as ordinary calculus predicts. The Stratonovich integral obeys the ordinary
chain rule. The price is that it is not a martingale: its mean is $T/2$, not 0.

For an integrand $h(W_t)$ the two integrals differ by a drift term:

$$
\int_0^T h(W_t) \circ dW_t = \int_0^T h(W_t)\, dW_t + \tfrac12\int_0^T h'(W_t)\, dt.
$$

The correction comes from $\tfrac12(h(W_{j+1}) - h(W_j))\Delta W_j \approx \tfrac12 h'(W_j)(\Delta W_j)^2$ summed over
steps. For $h(w) = w$ it is $\tfrac12 T$, the gap seen in the example.

The two conventions describe the same process when the drift is adjusted.

- **Itô** is standard in probability, finance and machine learning. Its integrals are martingales, and simulation by
  <NoteLink to="euler-maruyama-method">Euler–Maruyama</NoteLink> uses left-point evaluation directly.
- **Stratonovich** is the limit of <Gloss name="ode">ordinary differential equations</Gloss> driven by smooth noise that is made rougher and
  rougher (the Wong–Zakai theorem), and it is used for SDEs on manifolds, where the ordinary chain rule keeps the
  motion on the manifold <Cite id="sarkka2019" />.

## Assumptions

- The integrand is non-anticipating and square-integrable. Without the first, the isometry and zero mean fail. Without
  the second, the integral may exist only as a local martingale <Cite id="karatzas1998" />.
- Convergence is in mean square over paths. For a single fixed path the sums need not converge, which is why the Itô
  integral is not a pathwise
  <NoteLink to="riemann-stieltjes-integral">Riemann–Stieltjes integral</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Using the ordinary rule.** $\int_0^T W\,dW \ne \tfrac12 W_T^2$ in the Itô sense. The ordinary chain rule needs the
  correction of <NoteLink to="itos-lemma">Itô's lemma</NoteLink>.
- **Peeking ahead in simulation.** Evaluating the integrand after the noise of the same step has been drawn, for
  example with a right-point or implicit update, silently switches the convention and changes the drift.
- **Mixing conventions.** An SDE written in Stratonovich form and simulated as if it were Itô has the wrong drift.
  Convert with the rule above first.
