<Definition>
Let $X_t \in \reals$ solve $dX_t = f(X_t, t)\,dt + g(X_t, t)\,dW_t$, and let $p_t(x)$ be the density of $X_t$. The
**Fokker–Planck equation**, also called the Kolmogorov forward equation, is

$$
\partial_t p_t(x) = -\partial_x\brack{f(x, t)\,p_t(x)} + \tfrac12\,\partial_x^2\brack{g(x, t)^2\,p_t(x)}.
$$

In $\reals^d$, with $d\xvec = \fvec\,dt + \Gmat\,d\wvec$ and $\Dmat = \Gmat\Gmat^\top$,

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\nabla\cdot(\fvec\,p_t) + \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\partial_i\partial_j\paren{D_{ij}\,p_t}.
$$

It turns a random equation for particles into a deterministic <Gloss name="pde">partial differential equation</Gloss>
for their density <Cite id="risken1996" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: dye in water

Put a drop of dye in a glass of water. Each dye molecule jitters at random, and no two follow the same path. The
colour of the water, which is the density of molecules, changes smoothly and predictably: the drop blurs into a cloud
that widens over time. If the water also flows, the cloud is carried along while it widens. The Fokker–Planck equation
describes the colour, not the molecules.

Two mechanisms move probability.

- **Drift carries it.** Particles at $x$ move with velocity $f(x)$. Probability flows past $x$ at rate
  $f(x)\,p(x)$, as water carries dye.
- **Noise spreads it.** Random kicks move more particles out of crowded regions than into them, so probability flows
  down the density <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>, as in Fick's law of diffusion. The rate is
  $-\tfrac12\partial_x(g^2 p)$.

The total rate of flow past $x$ is the **probability flux** $J = fp - \tfrac12\partial_x(g^2 p)$. Probability is
conserved, so the density at $x$ rises by what flows in and falls by what flows out:
$\partial_t p = -\partial_x J$. This is the <NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">continuity
equation</NoteLink>, and substituting $J$ gives the Fokker–Planck equation.

## Derivation for pure noise: Einstein's argument

Take $dX = g\,dW$ with constant $g$ and no drift. Over a short time $h$ the particle moves by $Y = g\,\Delta W \sim
\Gauss(0, g^2h)$, independent of where it is. A particle at $x$ at time $t + h$ was at $x - Y$ at time $t$, so

$$
p_{t+h}(x) = \expect\brack{p_t(x - Y)} \approx \expect\brack{p_t(x) - Y\,\partial_x p_t(x) + \tfrac12 Y^2\,\partial_x^2
p_t(x)} = p_t(x) + \tfrac12 g^2 h\,\partial_x^2 p_t(x),
$$

using $\expect Y = 0$ and $\expect Y^2 = g^2h$; the higher terms are of order $h^2$. Subtract $p_t(x)$ and divide by
$h$:

$$
\partial_t p = \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 p.
$$

This is the <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink> with diffusivity $D = \tfrac12 g^2$. Einstein used
this argument in 1905 to connect the jitter of pollen grains to the diffusion of heat and dye
<Cite id="einstein1905" />. The factor $\tfrac12$ is the $\tfrac12$ of the second-order Taylor term, the same one that
appears in <NoteLink to="itos-lemma">Itô's lemma</NoteLink>.

## Derivation in general: Itô's lemma and a test function

Take any smooth test function $\phi(x)$ that vanishes outside a bounded interval. Itô's lemma gives

$$
d\phi(X_t) = \paren{f\,\phi' + \tfrac12 g^2\,\phi''}dt + g\,\phi'\,dW_t.
$$

The $dW$ term has mean zero. Taking expectations, and writing an expectation as an integral against $p_t$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int \phi(x)\,p_t(x)\,dx = \int \paren{f\,\phi' + \tfrac12 g^2\,\phi''}p_t\,dx.
$$

Move the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> from $\phi$ onto the other factor with
<NoteLink to="integration-by-parts">integration by parts</NoteLink>; the boundary terms vanish because $\phi$ does. Once
for the first term and twice for the second:

$$
\int f\,p_t\,\phi'\,dx = -\int \phi\,\partial_x(f p_t)\,dx, \qquad \int \tfrac12 g^2 p_t\,\phi''\,dx = \int
\phi\,\tfrac12\partial_x^2\paren{g^2 p_t}\,dx.
$$

So $\int \phi\,\partial_t p_t\,dx = \int \phi\,\brack{-\partial_x(f p_t) + \tfrac12\partial_x^2(g^2 p_t)}\,dx$ for every
test function $\phi$. Two densities that give the same integral against every test function are equal, which proves
the Fokker–Planck equation. The operator on the right is the adjoint of the operator
$\Lcal\phi = f\phi' + \tfrac12 g^2\phi''$ that acts on $\phi$, called the **generator** of the diffusion.

## Worked example 1: Brownian motion gives the heat equation

For $dX = \sigma\,dW$ from $X_0 = x_0$, the density is known: $p_t(x) = \Gauss(x; x_0, \sigma^2 t)$. Check that it
solves $\partial_t p = \tfrac12\sigma^2\,\partial_x^2 p$. Write $v = \sigma^2 t$ and $u = x - x_0$, so
$p = (2\pi v)^{-1/2}e^{-u^2/(2v)}$.

- Time derivative: $\partial_t p = \sigma^2\,\partial_v p = \sigma^2\,p\paren{-\frac{1}{2v} + \frac{u^2}{2v^2}}$.
- Space derivatives: $\partial_x p = -\frac{u}{v}p$ and $\partial_x^2 p = \paren{\frac{u^2}{v^2} - \frac1v}p$.

So $\tfrac12\sigma^2\,\partial_x^2 p = \sigma^2 p\paren{\frac{u^2}{2v^2} - \frac{1}{2v}} = \partial_t p$. The spreading
Gaussian is the heat kernel. At $\sigma = 1$ a point mass at 0 has spread to
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> 1 at $t = 1$ and 2 at $t = 4$.

## Worked example 2: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process stays Gaussian

For $dX = -\theta X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ the equation is $\partial_t p = \theta\,\partial_x(xp) + \tfrac12\sigma^2\,
\partial_x^2 p$. Rather than solve it directly, find the mean $m(t) = \int x\,p_t\,dx$ and variance. Multiply by $x$
and integrate by parts:

$$
m' = \int x\,\partial_t p\,dx = -\theta\int xp\,dx + 0 = -\theta m,
$$

because $\int x\,\partial_x(xp)\,dx = -\int xp\,dx$ and $\int x\,\partial_x^2 p\,dx = 0$. Multiplying by $x^2$ in the
same way gives $\frac{d}{dt}\expect X^2 = -2\theta\,\expect X^2 + \sigma^2$. These are the moment equations of the
<NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>. Substituting a Gaussian with this
mean and variance into the equation shows that it is an exact solution, so a Gaussian start stays Gaussian at every
time.

The stationary density has zero flux. Setting $J = -\theta xp - \tfrac12\sigma^2 p' = 0$ gives
$p'/p = -2\theta x/\sigma^2$, so $p_\infty \propto e^{-\theta x^2/\sigma^2}$, the Gaussian $\Gauss(0,
\sigma^2/(2\theta))$. With $\theta = 1$ and $\sigma = 1$ its standard deviation is $0.707$.

## Worked example 3: stationary densities in one dimension

With constant noise $g = \sigma$ and a drift that is the negative slope of a potential, $f = -U'$, zero flux means
$-U'p - \tfrac12\sigma^2 p' = 0$, and so

$$
p_\infty(x) \propto \exp\paren{-\frac{2\,U(x)}{\sigma^2}}.
$$

This is a <NoteLink to="simulated-annealing">Boltzmann distribution</NoteLink> at temperature $\sigma^2/2$. For the
double well $U(x) = \tfrac14 x^4 - \tfrac12 x^2$, with minima $U(\pm1) = -\tfrac14$ and a barrier $U(0) = 0$, the ratio
of the density at the barrier to the density at a minimum is $e^{-2 \times 0.25/\sigma^2}$: $0.61$ at $\sigma = 1$ and
$0.14$ at $\sigma = 0.5$. At small noise the particles cross the barrier rarely. Particles released in one well then
take a long time to fill the other one, which the figure shows: the density converges to $p_\infty$ on a time scale that
grows like $e^{2\Delta U/\sigma^2}$.

With $U = -\log p$ and $\sigma = \sqrt2$ the stationary density is $p$ itself. This is why
<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink>, $dX = \nabla\log p\,dt + \sqrt2\,dW$, samples from $p$;
that note gives the $d$-dimensional argument and shows that the <Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss> to
$p$ decreases along the flow.

## The backward equation and Feynman–Kac

The Fokker–Planck equation moves densities forward. Its adjoint moves expectations backward. Let
$u(x, t) = \expect\brack{\phi(X_T) \mid X_t = x}$ for a fixed payoff $\phi$. Then $u$ solves the **Kolmogorov backward
equation**

$$
\partial_t u + f\,\partial_x u + \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 u = 0, \qquad u(x, T) = \phi(x),
$$

because $u(X_t, t)$ is a <NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink> and so its Itô drift must vanish. The
**Feynman–Kac formula** extends this: with a rate $r(x)$, the discounted expectation
$u(x, t) = \expect\brack{e^{-\int_t^T r(X_s)\,ds}\phi(X_T) \mid X_t = x}$ solves the same equation with an extra
$-r\,u$ term <Cite id="kac1949" />. It turns a parabolic PDE into an average over simulated paths, which is how option
prices are computed by Monte Carlo.

## In machine learning

- **<Gloss name="diffusion-model">Diffusion models</Gloss>.** The forward noising <Gloss name="sde" /> has marginal
  densities $p_t$ that obey the Fokker–Planck equation. Rewriting that equation in two ways gives the
  <NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">reverse-time SDE</NoteLink> and the
  <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability-flow ODE</NoteLink>, the two samplers of score-based models.
- **<NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">Continuous normalising flows</NoteLink>.** With $g = 0$ the
  Fokker–Planck equation is the continuity equation of a deterministic flow. The probability-flow ODE rewrites the noise
  term as a drift, $\tfrac12\partial_x^2(g^2p) =
  \partial_x\paren{\tfrac12 g^2 p\,\partial_x\log p}$ for constant $g$, which is how a diffusion becomes a flow.
- **Sampling.** Langevin samplers, their annealed versions and <Gloss name="sgld">stochastic-gradient Langevin
  dynamics</Gloss> are justified by the stationary solution of the Fokker–Planck equation.

## Assumptions

- The density exists and is smooth enough to differentiate twice. With a point-mass start, the equation holds for
  $t > 0$.
- The Itô convention. For a Stratonovich SDE the drift in the equation is $f + \tfrac12 g\,\partial_x g$
  <Cite id="oksendal2003" />.
- Boundary conditions matter on a bounded domain: no-flux walls reflect particles, absorbing walls remove them.

## Pitfalls

- **Where the derivatives act.** The diffusion term is $\tfrac12\partial_x^2(g^2 p)$, not $\tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2
  p$. With state-dependent noise the two differ by terms in $\partial_x g$, which act as an extra drift.
- **The factor of one half.** With $dX = \sqrt2\,dW$ the diffusivity is 1, not 2. Physics texts often write the
  diffusion constant $D$ directly, with $g = \sqrt{2D}$.
- **Forward versus backward.** The forward equation transports densities with the adjoint operator; the backward
  equation transports expectations with the generator. Mixing them flips the sign of the drift term.
