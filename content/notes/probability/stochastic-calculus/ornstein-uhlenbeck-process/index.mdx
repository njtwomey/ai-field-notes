<Definition>
The **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process** with rate $\theta > 0$, mean $\mu$ and noise scale $\sigma > 0$ solves

$$
dX_t = -\theta\paren{X_t - \mu}\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t,
$$

where $W_t$ is a <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink>. Given $X_0 = x_0$, the state at time $t$
is Gaussian:

$$
X_t \sim \Gauss\paren{\mu + (x_0 - \mu)e^{-\theta t},\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\paren{1 - e^{-2\theta t}}},
$$

and as $t \to \infty$ it converges to the stationary law $\Gauss\paren{\mu, \sigma^2/(2\theta)}$
<Cite id="uhlenbeck1930" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: a ball on a spring in a gale

Tie a ball to a post with a spring and leave it in gusty wind. The spring pulls the ball back with a force
proportional to its distance from the post. The wind pushes it about at random. Without wind the ball returns to the
post along an exponential decay. Without the spring the ball wanders off like a Brownian particle, its spread growing
like $\sqrt t$. With both, the ball jitters around the post in a cloud of fixed size, where the pull on the far-flung
positions balances the spreading by the wind.

Uhlenbeck and Ornstein introduced the process for the velocity of a Brownian particle, where the linear drift is friction
<Cite id="uhlenbeck1930" />. Other readings of the same equation:

- A cup of tea cooling in a draughty room, with $\mu$ the room temperature and $1/\theta$ the cooling time.
- An interest rate that drifts back to a long-run level (the Vasicek model).
- A data point being noised in a <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss>, pulled to 0 while Gaussian noise replaces it.

## Solving it exactly

Set $\mu = 0$ to simplify; the general case follows by replacing $X$ with $X - \mu$. The drift is linear, so the
integrating factor of a <NoteLink to="first-order-linear-odes">first-order linear ODE</NoteLink> works. Apply
<NoteLink to="itos-lemma">Itô's lemma</NoteLink> to $Y_t = e^{\theta t}X_t$. The function $f(x, t) = e^{\theta t}x$
is linear in $x$, so $f'' = 0$ and there is no correction:

$$
dY_t = \theta e^{\theta t}X_t\,dt + e^{\theta t}\,dX_t = \theta e^{\theta t}X_t\,dt + e^{\theta t}\paren{-\theta X_t\,dt
+ \sigma\,dW_t} = \sigma e^{\theta t}\,dW_t.
$$

The drift cancels. Integrate from 0 to $t$ and multiply by $e^{-\theta t}$:

$$
X_t = x_0 e^{-\theta t} + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\theta(t - s)}\,dW_s.
$$

The first term is the deterministic decay. The second is an <NoteLink to="ito-integral">Itô integral</NoteLink> of a
deterministic function, so it is Gaussian with mean zero, and the Itô isometry gives its variance:

$$
\var(X_t) = \sigma^2\int_0^t e^{-2\theta(t - s)}\,ds = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\paren{1 - e^{-2\theta t}}.
$$

This proves the definition's transition law. Three facts follow.

- **Memory decays at rate $\theta$.** The mean moves from $x_0$ to 0 along $e^{-\theta t}$. The half-life is
  $\ln 2/\theta$.
- **Variance saturates.** For small $t$, $\var(X_t) \approx \sigma^2 t$, as for Brownian motion. For large $t$ it
  approaches $\sigma^2/(2\theta)$.
- **Stationary covariance.** Started from the stationary law, $\cov(X_s, X_{s+\tau}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}
  e^{-\theta\abs\tau}$. The same integral representation gives it: the two states share the noise up to time $s$,
  discounted by $e^{-\theta\tau}$.

## Worked example: the numbers at one time

Take $\theta = 2$, $\sigma = 1$, $\mu = 0$ and $x_0 = 3$. At $t = 0.5$:

- mean $3e^{-1} = 1.104$;
- variance $\tfrac14\paren{1 - e^{-2}} = 0.216$, <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $0.465$;
- stationary variance $\sigma^2/(2\theta) = 0.25$, so after half a unit of time the variance is already at 86% of its
  stationary value, while the mean is still 37% of $x_0$;
- half-life $\ln 2/2 = 0.347$.

A simulation of $2 \times 10^5$ paths with the exact transition gives mean $1.103$ and variance $0.217$.

## Worked example: the moments from their differential equations

The mean and variance can be found without solving the <Gloss name="sde" />. Taking expectations of the SDE gives
$m'(t) = -\theta m(t)$. Itô's lemma for $x^2$ gives $d(X^2) = \paren{-2\theta X^2 + \sigma^2}dt + 2\sigma X\,dW$, so
the second moment $s(t) = \expect X_t^2$ obeys $s' = -2\theta s + \sigma^2$, and the variance $v = s - m^2$ obeys

$$
v' = -2\theta v + \sigma^2.
$$

Its fixed point is $v = \sigma^2/(2\theta)$: the drift removes variance at rate $2\theta v$ and the noise adds it at rate
$\sigma^2$. Solving the linear equation from $v(0) = 0$ gives $v(t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})$
again. Moment equations of this kind are how continuous-discrete <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filters</NoteLink> propagate uncertainty between
observations <Cite id="sarkka2019" />.

## The link to AR(1)

Sample the process every $h$ time units. The exact solution over one interval gives

$$
X_{k+1} = e^{-\theta h}X_k + \epsilon_k, \qquad \epsilon_k \sim \Gauss\paren{0, \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\paren{1 -
e^{-2\theta h}}},
$$

an <NoteLink to="autoregressive-model">autoregressive model of order 1</NoteLink> with coefficient
$\phi = e^{-\theta h}$. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is the continuous-time AR(1) model, and this discretisation is
exact for every $h$, not an approximation. A daily AR(1) fit with $\phi = 0.9$ corresponds to
$\theta = -\ln 0.9 = 0.105$ per day, a half-life of $6.6$ days.

The stationary covariance $e^{-\theta\abs\tau}$ is the exponential (Matérn-½) kernel, so a
<NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink> with that kernel is a stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process;
see <NoteLink to="covariance-functions">covariance functions</NoteLink>.

## The link to Langevin dynamics

<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink> for a density $p$ is the SDE $dX = \nabla\log p(X)\,dt +
\sqrt2\,dW$. For a Gaussian $p = \Gauss(0, s^2)$ the score is $-x/s^2$, so the SDE is Ornstein–Uhlenbeck with
$\theta = 1/s^2$ and $\sigma = \sqrt2$. Its stationary variance is $\sigma^2/(2\theta) = 2/(2/s^2) = s^2$, which
confirms that Langevin dynamics targets $p$. The potential $U(x) = x^2/(2s^2)$ is quadratic, and the drift
$-U'(x)$ is linear in $x$. Every Langevin sampler behaves like an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process near a mode where
$\log p$ is close to quadratic.

## The forward process of diffusion models

The variance-preserving forward SDE of a diffusion model is <Cite id="song2021" />

$$
dX_t = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,X_t\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t,
$$

an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with time-varying rate $\theta = \beta/2$ and noise $\sigma^2 = \beta$. Its stationary
variance is $\sigma^2/(2\theta) = 1$ for every $\beta$. The same integrating factor, now $\exp\paren{\tfrac12\int_0^t
\beta(s)\,ds}$, gives

$$
X_t \mid X_0 \sim \Gauss\paren{\sqrt{\bar\alpha(t)}\,X_0,\ 1 - \bar\alpha(t)}, \qquad \bar\alpha(t) =
\exp\paren{-\int_0^t \beta(s)\,ds}.
$$

This is the one-shot noising formula $\xvec_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec$ of
<NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-probabilistic-models">denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM)</NoteLink> <Cite id="ho2020" />. If $X_0$ has unit
variance, every $X_t$ does too, hence "variance preserving". As $\bar\alpha \to 0$ the conditional mean $\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,X_0$ tends to 0 and the law of $X_t$
becomes $\Gauss(0, 1)$, the starting point for generation. With constant $\beta = 1$ the data signal decays with
half-life $2\ln 2 = 1.39$, and $\bar\alpha(5) = e^{-5} = 0.0067$.

Because the forward process is linear, a Gaussian mixture stays a Gaussian mixture, so its score is analytic at every
time. The <NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">reverse-time SDE</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability-flow ODE</NoteLink> notes use this to run a diffusion model with the
exact score.

## Assumptions

- $\theta > 0$. With $\theta = 0$ the process is Brownian motion and has no stationary law; with $\theta < 0$ it is
  pushed away from $\mu$ and its variance grows exponentially.
- Constant $\sigma$. With a state-dependent noise such as $\sigma\sqrt{X}$ (the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross model) the process
  is no longer Gaussian.

## Pitfalls

- **Rate or coefficient.** Some texts write the drift as $\theta(\mu - X)$, others as $-X/\tau$ with a time constant
  $\tau = 1/\theta$. The stationary variance $\sigma^2/(2\theta)$ changes accordingly.
- **Simulating with Euler–Maruyama.** The <NoteLink to="euler-maruyama-method">Euler–Maruyama method</NoteLink>
  inflates the stationary variance by $1/(1 - \theta h/2)$ and is unstable for $h > 2/\theta$. The exact AR(1)
  transition has neither problem.
- **Confusing the SDE with its discretisation.** DDPM's discrete $\beta_t$ per step and the continuous $\beta(t)$ per
  unit time differ by the number of steps: $\beta_t \approx \beta(t)/N$ for $N$ steps on $[0, 1]$.
