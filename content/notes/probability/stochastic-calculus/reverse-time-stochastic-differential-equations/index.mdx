<Definition>
Let $\xvec_t$ solve the **forward <Gloss name="sde" />** $d\xvec_t = \fvec(\xvec_t, t)\,dt + g(t)\,d\wvec_t$ on $[0, T]$, with marginal
densities $p_t$. The time-reversed process $\yvec_s = \xvec_{T-s}$ solves the **reverse-time SDE**

$$
d\yvec_s = \brack{-\fvec(\yvec_s, T - s) + g(T - s)^2\,\nabla\log p_{T-s}(\yvec_s)}ds + g(T - s)\,d\bar{\wvec}_s,
$$

where $\bar{\wvec}$ is a Brownian motion and $\yvec_0 \sim p_T$. Written in the original clock, with time running from
$T$ down to 0 and $dt < 0$, the same statement reads

$$
d\xvec = \brack{\fvec(\xvec, t) - g(t)^2\,\nabla\log p_t(\xvec)}dt + g(t)\,d\bar{\wvec}.
$$

The reversed process has the same law over whole paths as the forward one <Cite id="anderson1982" />
<Cite id="haussmann1986" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: the film of dye played backwards

Film a drop of dye spreading in water, then play the film backwards. The cloud contracts into a drop. Each molecule
still jitters, because in the reversed film its motion is still a random walk. But the jitter now has a bias towards
where the dye is concentrated. That bias is exactly what is needed for the cloud to shrink rather than keep spreading.

Under the forward diffusion, probability mass flows out of dense regions because the noise carries more of it
out than in. Reversing time turns that outflow into an inflow, towards higher density. The direction of increasing
density is the gradient of $\log p_t$, the **score**. The reverse drift therefore adds $g^2\nabla\log p_t$ to the
reversed forward drift $-\fvec$: noise strength times the direction uphill in density.

The reverse drift needs the density at every time, but only through its score. The normalising constant of $p_t$
cancels in $\nabla\log p_t$. This is why <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss> learn a score network and never a density.

## Derivation in one dimension via Fokker–Planck

Take $dX = f(X, t)\,dt + g(t)\,dW$ with noise that depends on time only. The marginals obey the
<NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink>

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\partial_x(f\,p_t) + \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 p_t.
$$

The key step rewrites the diffusion term. Because $\partial_x p = p\,\partial_x\log p$,

$$
\tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 p = g^2\,\partial_x^2 p - \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 p = \partial_x\paren{g^2 p\,\partial_x\log
p} - \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 p.
$$

Substituting,

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\partial_x\brack{\paren{f - g^2\,\partial_x\log p_t}p_t} - \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 p_t.
$$

Now reverse the clock. Let $q_s = p_{T-s}$, so $\partial_s q_s = -\partial_t p_t$ at $t = T - s$:

$$
\partial_s q_s = -\partial_x\brack{\paren{-f + g^2\,\partial_x\log q_s}q_s} + \tfrac12 g^2\,\partial_x^2 q_s.
$$

This is the Fokker–Planck equation of an SDE in the time $s$ with drift $-f + g^2\,\partial_x\log q_s$ and diffusion
coefficient $g$. Started from $q_0 = p_T$, that SDE has marginals $q_s = p_{T-s}$ for every $s$, so it runs the forward
marginals backwards. The sign of the diffusion term is positive, so the reversed equation is a well-posed diffusion
rather than a backward <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink>, which would be unstable. The score term turns the ill-posed backward heat equation
into an ordinary diffusion with an extra drift.

The argument matches marginals at each time. Anderson's theorem is stronger: the joint law of the whole reversed path
is that of the reverse SDE <Cite id="anderson1982" />.

## Worked example 1: reversing Brownian motion gives a Brownian bridge

Take $dX = dW$ from $X_0 = 0$ on $[0, T]$. Then $p_t = \Gauss(0, t)$ and the score is $-x/t$. The reverse drift in the
time $s$ is $-f + g^2\,\partial_x\log p_{T-s} = -Y/(T - s)$:

$$
dY_s = -\frac{Y_s}{T - s}\,ds + d\bar W_s, \qquad Y_0 \sim \Gauss(0, T).
$$

This is the SDE of a Brownian bridge pinned at 0 at time $s = T$. The pull $-Y/(T - s)$ grows without bound as
$s \to T$, which forces every reversed path back to the starting point 0.

## Worked example 2: an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with a Gaussian start

Take the variance-preserving process $dX = -\tfrac12\beta X\,dt + \sqrt\beta\,dW$ with $X_0 \sim \Gauss(0, v_0)$. Its
marginals are Gaussian, $p_t = \Gauss(0, v_t)$ with $v_t = 1 + (v_0 - 1)e^{-\beta t}$; see the
<NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>. The score is $-x/v_t$, and the
reverse SDE in the time $s$ is

$$
dY = \paren{\tfrac12\beta\,Y - \frac{\beta}{v_{T-s}}Y}ds + \sqrt\beta\,d\bar W.
$$

**Check the variance.** For a linear SDE $dY = aY\,ds + b\,dW$ the variance $u$ obeys $u' = 2au + b^2$. Here
$2a = \beta - 2\beta/v$ and $b^2 = \beta$. If $u = v_{T-s}$ then

$$
u' = \paren{\beta - \frac{2\beta}{v}}v + \beta = \beta(v - 1),
$$

which is exactly $\frac{d}{ds}v_{T-s} = -\frac{d}{dt}v_t = \beta(v_t - 1)$. So the reverse SDE retraces the forward
variances. With $\beta = 1$, $v_0 = 0.25$ and $T = 3$: $v_T = 1 - 0.75e^{-3} = 0.963$. Simulating $2 \times 10^5$
reverse paths with 3,000 Euler–Maruyama steps from $\Gauss(0, 0.963)$ gives a final variance of $0.251$, against the
target $0.25$.

**Drop the score term** and the reverse drift is only $-f = +\tfrac12\beta Y$. The variance then obeys $u' = \beta u +
\beta$, so $u(T) = (v_T + 1)e^{\beta T} - 1 = 38.4$. The simulation gives $38.5$. Reversing the drift alone does not
reverse a diffusion: the noise keeps spreading, and only the score term pulls the particles back together.

## Worked example 3: a two-mode density

Take data from the mixture $0.35\,\Gauss(-2, 0.35^2) + 0.65\,\Gauss(1.5, 0.5^2)$ and the forward process
$dX = -\tfrac12X\,dt + dW$. Given $x_0$, $X_t \sim \Gauss(a_t x_0, 1 - a_t^2)$ with $a_t = e^{-t/2}$, so $p_t$ is
again a two-component mixture with means $a_t\mu_k$ and variances $a_t^2\sigma_k^2 + 1 - a_t^2$. Its score is
analytic:

$$
\partial_x\log p_t(x) = \frac{\sum_k \pi_k\,\Gauss(x; m_k, s_k^2)\,(m_k - x)/s_k^2}{\sum_k \pi_k\,\Gauss(x; m_k,
s_k^2)}, \qquad m_k = a_t\mu_k,\ s_k^2 = a_t^2\sigma_k^2 + 1 - a_t^2.
$$

At $T = 5$, $a_T^2 = e^{-5} = 0.0067$ and $p_T$ is within a <Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss> of $3 \times 10^{-4}$ of
$\Gauss(0, 1)$. Starting $10^5$ particles from $\Gauss(0, 1)$ and running the reverse SDE with 250 Euler–Maruyama steps
puts $65.1\%$ of them in the right-hand mode, against $65\%$ in the data, and gives a mean of $0.276$ and variance
$2.99$, against the data's $0.275$ and $2.99$.

## How diffusion models use it

A diffusion model fixes a forward SDE that turns data into Gaussian noise, such as the variance-preserving
<NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>. The score $\nabla\log p_t$ of real
data is unknown, so a network $\svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, t)$ is trained to approximate it by <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">denoising score matching</NoteLink>;
<NoteLink to="tweedies-formula">Tweedie's formula</NoteLink> explains why a denoiser gives the score. Generation draws
$\xvec_T \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$ and integrates the reverse SDE with $\svec_{\thetavec}$ in place of the score,
typically with the <NoteLink to="euler-maruyama-method">Euler–Maruyama method</NoteLink> <Cite id="song2021" />.
<Gloss name="ddpm" />'s ancestral sampler is a discretisation of this reverse SDE; see
<NoteLink to="diffusion-models-as-stochastic-differential-equations">diffusion models as SDEs</NoteLink>. The
deterministic alternative with the same marginals is the <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">probability-flow
ODE</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Known terminal law.** The reverse SDE must start from $p_T$. In practice it starts from $\Gauss(\zeros, \eye)$,
  which is close to $p_T$ only when the forward process has run long enough.
- **Smooth, positive densities.** The score must exist. For data on a low-dimensional set, $p_0$ has no density and
  the score blows up as $t \to 0$; samplers stop at a small $t = \epsilon > 0$.
- **State-dependent noise.** When $g$ depends on $\xvec$ the reverse drift gains a further term,
  $\nabla\cdot(g^2)$ in one dimension <Cite id="anderson1982" />. Diffusion models use noise that depends on time only.

## Pitfalls

- **Sign and clock.** In the original clock the drift is $\fvec - g^2\nabla\log p_t$ with $dt < 0$; in the reversed
  clock it is $-\fvec + g^2\nabla\log p_{T-s}$ with $ds > 0$. Mixing the two flips the sign of the score term.
- **Using the score of the data.** The reverse drift at time $t$ needs the score of the noised density $p_t$, not of
  $p_0$. A network conditioned on $t$ provides it.
- **Reversing the drift is not enough.** Running $-\fvec$ backwards with noise spreads the particles further, as
  worked example 2 shows. All the information about the data enters through the score.
