<Definition>
**Stochastic calculus** is the calculus of processes driven by a <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian
motion</NoteLink> $W_t$. It defines integrals $\int_0^T H_t\, dW_t$ against Brownian increments, gives the chain rule
for $f(X_t)$ when $X_t$ is noisy (Itô's lemma), and uses both to make sense of stochastic differential equations
$dX_t = f(X_t, t)\,dt + g(X_t, t)\,dW_t$ <Cite id="oksendal2003" />. Its central fact is that over a short time $dt$
the squared Brownian increment $(dW_t)^2$ equals $dt$, not zero.
</Definition>

## A particle that jitters

Drop a speck of pollen into water. Water molecules hit the speck from every side, many times a second, and the speck
jitters. Over a short time $h$ its displacement is the sum of many tiny independent kicks, so by the
<NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> it is Gaussian with variance proportional to $h$.
In one dimension, with unit variance per unit time, the displacement over time $h$ is $\Delta W \sim \Gauss(0, h)$.
This is Brownian motion.

### Why the variance is $h$, and the typical kick is $\sqrt h$

The form $\Gauss(0, h)$ is easy to use and easy to take on trust. Three short arguments show where it comes from.

**The variance must grow in proportion to time.** Write $v(h)$ for the variance of the displacement over a time $h$.
Split the time into two halves. The displacement over $h$ is the displacement over the first half plus the
displacement over the second half, and the two are independent, because the molecules hitting the speck in the second
half know nothing of the first. Variances of independent sums add, so

$$
v(h) = v(h/2) + v(h/2) = 2\,v(h/2).
$$

The same argument with any split gives $v(a + b) = v(a) + v(b)$. So $v$ never decreases (a longer time adds a
non-negative variance), and a non-decreasing function with this property is a straight line through the origin:
$v(h) = \alpha h$ for some constant $\alpha \ge 0$. Doubling the time doubles the
variance. It cannot be $h^2$, because then $v(h) = h^2$ while $2\,v(h/2) = 2 \cdot h^2/4 = h^2/2$, and the two halves
would not add up to the whole.

**The constant $\alpha$ is a choice of units.** Any $\alpha$ is possible: it measures how violently the molecules kick,
and it is set by temperature and viscosity (Einstein's diffusion coefficient $D$ gives $\alpha = 2D$). *Standard*
Brownian motion fixes $\alpha = 1$: one unit of variance per unit of time. Other noise levels are written as a multiple
$\sigma W_t$, whose increments are $\sigma\,\Delta W \sim \Gauss(0, \sigma^2 h)$, so $\alpha = \sigma^2$. This is why SDEs
put the noise level in a separate coefficient $g$ and keep $dW_t$ standard.

**The typical kick is $\sqrt h$, not $h$.** Variance is a squared size, so the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>, the size of a
typical kick, is $\sqrt{\alpha h}$. Here is why no other power could work. Suppose the kick over a step of length $h$
had standard deviation $h^p$ for some power $p$. Cover the time interval $[0, 1]$ with $n$ steps of length $1/n$. The
$n$ kicks are independent, so the total variance is

$$
n \times \paren{n^{-p}}^2 = n^{1 - 2p}.
$$

| power $p$ | $n = 10$ | $n = 100$ | $n = 1000$ | as the steps shrink |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ (kick $\propto h$, like a smooth path) | $0.1$ | $0.01$ | $0.001$ | the motion vanishes |
| $\tfrac12$ (kick $\propto \sqrt h$) | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ | the same answer at every resolution |
| $\tfrac14$ | $3.16$ | $10$ | $31.6$ | the motion explodes |

With kicks proportional to $h$, which is how a smooth path moves, finer steps make the jiggling disappear: the kicks
cancel each other and shrink too fast. With any power below $\tfrac12$, finer steps make it blow up. Only $p = \tfrac12$
gives the same total variance however finely the time is chopped, so it is the only scaling that describes one motion
seen at different resolutions.

**The same result from a coin.** Let the speck step $\pm\delta$ with a fair coin every $\tau$ seconds. After time $t$ it
has taken $t/\tau$ independent steps, each of variance $\delta^2$, so its variance is $t\,\delta^2/\tau$. For this to
settle on a finite, non-zero limit as $\tau \to 0$, the ratio $\delta^2/\tau$ must stay fixed at some $\alpha$, so the
step size must be $\delta = \sqrt{\alpha\tau}$. With $\alpha = 1$ and $t = 1$, simulations give variances $0.99$ at
$\tau = 0.1$, $0.01$ and $0.001$. The central limit theorem makes the sum of many coin steps Gaussian, and the result is
$\Gauss(0, \alpha t)$: the <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink> becomes Brownian motion.

The consequence drives everything below: over a short time $h$, a Brownian kick of size $\sqrt h$ is far larger than
the step $h$ itself.

Now add a force, such as a spring pulling the speck back to the centre. The speck's motion now has two parts, and each
is an idea met earlier.

**The drift is an ordinary differential equation.** Without the water, the spring alone moves the speck by the rule
$\dot x = f(x, t)$, an <NoteLink to="ordinary-differential-equations">ordinary differential equation</NoteLink> whose
right-hand side is a <NoteLink to="vector-fields-and-flows">vector field</NoteLink> telling the speck which way to go
and how fast. For a spring of stiffness $\theta$, $f(x) = -\theta x$: the
<NoteLink to="first-order-linear-odes">exponential decay</NoteLink> $x(t) = x_0 e^{-\theta t}$. With $\theta = 2$ and
$x_0 = 1$, the speck is at $e^{-2} \approx 0.135$ after one unit of time. Over a short time $h$ the drift moves the speck
by $f(x, t)\,h$, which is one step of <NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers">Euler's method</NoteLink>.

**The noise is the Brownian kick.** Without the spring, the water alone moves the speck by $\Delta W \sim \Gauss(0, h)$,
scaled by a noise level $g(x, t)$. With $g = \sigma$ constant this is Brownian motion with variance $\sigma^2$ per unit
time, and its density spreads out as the <NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink> describes.

**Together.** Over a short time $h$ the speck moves by the drift step plus the kick:

$$
X_{t+h} = X_t + f(X_t, t)\,h + g(X_t, t)\,\Delta W, \qquad \Delta W \sim \Gauss(0, h).
$$

With $\theta = 2$, $\sigma = 0.5$, $X_t = 1$ and $h = 0.01$, the drift contributes $-2 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.01 = -0.02$. A
standard normal draw $z = 0.8$ gives the kick $0.5 \cdot \sqrt{0.01} \cdot 0.8 = 0.04$. So $X_{t+h} = 1.02$: in this
step the kick outweighed the pull. The kick scales as $\sqrt h$ and the drift as $h$, so over a single short step the
noise dominates. Over many steps the drift accumulates steadily while the kicks partly cancel, and the spring wins on
average: the speck settles into a cloud around the centre with variance $\sigma^2 / (2\theta) = 0.0625$ instead of
collapsing to 0.

Letting $h$ shrink turns the step into a **stochastic differential equation** (SDE):

$$
dX_t = \underbrace{f(X_t, t)\,dt}_{\text{drift: the ODE}} + \underbrace{g(X_t, t)\,dW_t}_{\text{noise: Brownian kicks}}.
$$

The two limits recover what came before. With $g = 0$ it is the ODE $\dot x = f$. With $f = 0$ and $g = 1$ it is
Brownian motion. The spring example, $f = -\theta x$ and $g = \sigma$, is the
<NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>, and the step above is the
<NoteLink to="euler-maruyama-method">Euler–Maruyama method</NoteLink> for simulating it.

Ordinary calculus cannot read this equation, because $dW_t$ has no $dt$ to divide by. The rest of this note explains
why, and maps the notes that fix it.

## Why ordinary calculus breaks

The kick $\Delta W$ over time $h$ has standard deviation $\sqrt h$. For small $h$, $\sqrt h$ is much larger than $h$:
at $h = 10^{-4}$ the kick is about $10^{-2}$. Two consequences follow.

**The path has no slope.** The <NoteLink to="derivative">difference quotient</NoteLink> $\Delta W / h$ has standard deviation $\sqrt h / h = 1/\sqrt h$,
which grows without bound as $h \to 0$. Its mean absolute value is $\sqrt{2/(\pi h)}$, because
$\expect\abs{Z} = \sqrt{2/\pi}$ for a standard normal $Z$. No limit exists, so $dW/dt$ is meaningless. With probability
1 a Brownian path is differentiable at no time at all. An equation like $dX = g\,dW$ cannot be divided by $dt$ and
solved as an ordinary differential equation.

**Squared increments do not vanish.** In ordinary calculus a squared increment $(\Delta x)^2$ is of order $h^2$ and
disappears in the limit. For Brownian motion $\expect[(\Delta W)^2] = h$ and $\var[(\Delta W)^2] = 2h^2$. Split
$[0, T]$ into $n$ steps of length $h = T/n$. The squared increments are independent, so their sum has

$$
\expect\brack{\sum_{j=1}^n (\Delta W_j)^2} = nh = T, \qquad \var\brack{\sum_{j=1}^n (\Delta W_j)^2} = 2nh^2 =
\frac{2T^2}{n} \to 0.
$$

The sum of squared increments over $[0, T]$ converges to the constant $T$. Each $(\Delta W)^2$ is random, but their
fluctuations average out, and in sums they behave exactly like $h$. This is the rule

$$
(dW_t)^2 = dt, \qquad dW_t\,dt = 0, \qquad (dt)^2 = 0,
$$

which is the whole of the difference between stochastic and ordinary calculus. The other two products vanish because
$\sum \abs{\Delta W_j}\, h \approx \sqrt h\, T \to 0$ and $\sum h^2 = hT \to 0$.

## What goes wrong if the rule is ignored

Take $f(w) = w^2$. The ordinary chain rule says $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW$. The right side has mean zero, because $dW$ is a
fresh increment independent of $W$. So the ordinary chain rule predicts $\expect[W_t^2] = 0$ for all $t$. The truth is
$\expect[W_t^2] = t$. The missing term is the second-order Taylor term $\tfrac12 f''(W)(dW)^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot
dt = dt$, which ordinary calculus discards and Itô calculus keeps:

$$
d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt.
$$

Taking expectations gives $d\expect[W_t^2] = dt$, so $\expect[W_t^2] = t$. Every result in this section is a
consequence of keeping that one term.

## The section

<SectionMap />

The notes build on one another in this order. Each reads on its own, given its prerequisites.

1. <NoteLink to="ito-integral">The Itô integral</NoteLink> defines $\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t$ as a limit of sums that
   evaluate $H$ at the left end of each step. It computes $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12(W_T^2 - T)$, shows that the
   evaluation point changes the answer, and proves the Itô isometry.
2. <NoteLink to="itos-lemma">Itô's lemma</NoteLink> is the chain rule with the extra $\tfrac12 g^2 f''\,dt$ term. It
   solves geometric Brownian motion and computes moments such as $\expect[e^{W_t}] = e^{t/2}$.
3. <NoteLink to="stochastic-differential-equations">Stochastic differential equations</NoteLink> are drift plus noise,
   read as integral equations. The note covers existence and uniqueness and solves Brownian motion with drift and
   geometric Brownian motion exactly.
4. <NoteLink to="euler-maruyama-method">The Euler–Maruyama method</NoteLink> simulates any SDE: one drift step plus
   one Gaussian kick of variance $g^2 h$. It converges with strong order $\tfrac12$ and weak order 1.
5. <NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>
   $dX = -\theta X\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ is the spring-and-noise model. It is Gaussian at every time, forgets its start at
   rate $\theta$, and is the forward noising process of a variance-preserving <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss>.
6. <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">The Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink> switches from particles to densities: it
   is the <Gloss name="pde">partial differential equation</Gloss> that the density $p_t(x)$ of $X_t$ obeys. For Brownian motion it is the heat
   equation.
7. <NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">The reverse-time SDE</NoteLink> runs a diffusion
   backwards. Its drift gains the term $-g^2\nabla\log p_t$, so reversing noise into data needs only the score.
8. <NoteLink to="probability-flow-ode">The probability-flow ODE</NoteLink> is a deterministic flow with the same
   densities $p_t$ as the SDE. It gives exact likelihoods and deterministic samplers.
9. <NoteLink to="tweedies-formula">Tweedie's formula</NoteLink>
   $\expect[x \mid y] = y + \sigma^2\nabla_y\log p(y)$ says that the best denoiser and the score are the same object.
10. <NoteLink to="diffusion-models-as-stochastic-differential-equations">Diffusion models as SDEs</NoteLink> assembles
    the pieces: a forward SDE, a score network trained by denoising, and a reverse-time SDE or probability-flow ODE for
    sampling <Cite id="song2021" />.
11. <NoteLink to="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</NoteLink> compares two SDEs with different drifts. It gives
    likelihood ratios and <Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergences</Gloss> between whole paths.

## Two views of one process

Every note in the section moves between two descriptions of the same random motion.

- **Particles.** Follow one path $X_t$. The tools are Itô integrals, Itô's lemma and the Euler–Maruyama simulation.
  This view is how samples are generated.
- **Densities.** Follow the distribution $p_t$ of $X_t$ over all paths. The tool is the Fokker–Planck equation, a
  deterministic partial differential equation. This view is how a diffusion model is analysed and how it is trained.

The two views meet in the probability-flow ODE, which moves individual particles deterministically so that their
density follows the same $p_t$ as the noisy SDE.

## Where it is used in machine learning

- **Diffusion models** add noise with a forward SDE and generate by solving the reverse SDE or the probability-flow
  ODE with a learned score <Cite id="song2021" />; see
  <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score-based generative models</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-probabilistic-models">DDPM</NoteLink>.
- **Langevin sampling** runs the SDE $dX = \nabla\log p(X)\,dt + \sqrt2\,dW$, whose stationary density is $p$; see
  <NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="energy-based-models">energy-based models</NoteLink>.
- **Stochastic gradient descent** with a small learning rate behaves approximately like an SDE, with noise set by the
  mini-batch <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> covariance.
- **Filtering and time series.** The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is the continuous-time AR(1) model, and
  continuous-discrete <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filters</NoteLink> propagate SDE moments between observations <Cite id="sarkka2019" />.

## Assumptions and conventions

- Noise is Brownian: Gaussian, independent increments, continuous paths. Jump noise needs other tools.
- Integrals are Itô integrals, which evaluate the integrand at the start of each step. The Stratonovich convention,
  which uses the midpoint, obeys the ordinary chain rule but changes the drift; the
  <NoteLink to="ito-integral">Itô integral</NoteLink> note converts between them.
- Limits are taken in mean square. The measure theory that makes them rigorous is in Øksendal
  <Cite id="oksendal2003" /> and Karatzas and Shreve <Cite id="karatzas1998" />. Särkkä and Solin give an applied
  treatment aimed at simulation and inference <Cite id="sarkka2019" />.
