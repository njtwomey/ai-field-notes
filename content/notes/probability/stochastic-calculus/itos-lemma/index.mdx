<Definition>
Let $X_t$ satisfy $dX_t = a_t\,dt + b_t\,dW_t$, where $W_t$ is a <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian
motion</NoteLink> and the drift $a_t$ and diffusion $b_t$ are non-anticipating processes. Let $f(x, t)$ have a
continuous second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> in $x$ and a continuous first derivative in $t$. Then $Y_t = f(X_t, t)$ satisfies

$$
dY_t = \paren{\partial_t f + a_t\, \partial_x f + \tfrac12 b_t^2\, \partial_x^2 f}dt + b_t\, \partial_x f\, dW_t,
$$

with every derivative evaluated at $(X_t, t)$. The equation is shorthand for the integral equation
$Y_t = Y_0 + \int_0^t (\dots)\,ds + \int_0^t b_s\,\partial_x f\,dW_s$, whose last term is an
<NoteLink to="ito-integral">Itô integral</NoteLink> <Cite id="ito1944" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: keep the second-order term

For a smooth path $x(t)$ the chain rule comes from a first-order <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink>,
$f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) \approx f'(x)\Delta x$. The second-order term $\tfrac12 f''(x)(\Delta x)^2$ is of size $h^2$
over a step of length $h$, and $n = T/h$ such terms sum to $Th \to 0$.

A Brownian step is of size $\sqrt h$, not $h$. Its square is of size $h$, and $n$ of them sum to $T$, not to zero. So
the second-order term survives:

$$
df = f'(X)\,dX + \tfrac12 f''(X)\,(dX)^2.
$$

With $dX = a\,dt + b\,dW$ and the rules $(dW)^2 = dt$, $dW\,dt = 0$, $(dt)^2 = 0$, the square is
$(dX)^2 = b^2\,dt$. Substituting gives Itô's lemma for $f(x)$. The extra drift $\tfrac12 b^2 f''$ is the whole
difference from ordinary calculus.

If $f$ is convex ($f'' > 0$), noise pushes $X$ up and down symmetrically,
but $f$ rises more on both sides than a straight line does, so the mean of $f(X)$ drifts up. This is
Jensen's inequality accumulated over time.

## Derivation

Take $f = f(x)$ without time dependence; the $\partial_t f$ term is added the same way as in ordinary calculus. Split
$[0, t]$ into $n$ steps of length $h$, and write $X_j$, $\Delta X_j = X_{j+1} - X_j$. Taylor's theorem gives

$$
f(X_t) - f(X_0) = \sum_j f'(X_j)\,\Delta X_j + \tfrac12\sum_j f''(X_j)\,(\Delta X_j)^2 + R_n,
$$

where $R_n$ collects third-order terms of size $\abs{\Delta X_j}^3 \approx h^{3/2}$, which sum to about $nh^{3/2} =
t\sqrt h \to 0$.

- **First sum.** With $\Delta X_j \approx a_j h + b_j\,\Delta W_j$, it converges to $\int f' a\,ds + \int f' b\,dW$.
  The left-point evaluation $f'(X_j)$ is exactly the Itô convention.
- **Second sum.** Expand $(\Delta X_j)^2 = b_j^2(\Delta W_j)^2 + 2a_jb_j h\,\Delta W_j + a_j^2 h^2$. The last two
  terms sum to zero in the limit, as in ordinary calculus. For the first, write
  $(\Delta W_j)^2 = h + \paren{(\Delta W_j)^2 - h}$:

$$
\sum_j f''(X_j)\, b_j^2\,(\Delta W_j)^2 = \sum_j f''(X_j)\, b_j^2\, h + \sum_j f''(X_j)\, b_j^2\, \paren{(\Delta
W_j)^2 - h}.
$$

The first part converges to $\int f'' b^2\,ds$. In the second part, each bracket has mean zero and variance $2h^2$,
and the brackets are uncorrelated with each other and with the factors in front (each is independent of the past).
The variance of the second part is therefore about $\sum_j \expect[(f''b^2)^2]\, 2h^2 \approx 2h\int \expect[(f''
b^2)^2]\,ds \to 0$. The fluctuations of $(\Delta W)^2$ around $h$ average away, and only the mean $h$ survives.

Putting the pieces together, $f(X_t) - f(X_0) = \int_0^t \paren{a f' + \tfrac12 b^2 f''}ds + \int_0^t b f'\,dW$.

## Worked example 1: $f(W) = W^2$

Here $X = W$, so $a = 0$ and $b = 1$. With $f' = 2w$ and $f'' = 2$:

$$
d(W_t^2) = 2W_t\,dW_t + \tfrac12 \cdot 2\,dt = 2W_t\,dW_t + dt.
$$

Integrating from 0 to $T$ gives $W_T^2 = 2\int_0^T W\,dW + T$, the same result, $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12(W_T^2 -
T)$, that the <NoteLink to="ito-integral">Itô integral</NoteLink> note obtains by telescoping.

Take expectations. The Itô integral has mean zero, so $\frac{d}{dt}\expect[W_t^2] = 1$ and $\expect[W_t^2] = t$,
which is correct because $W_t \sim \Gauss(0, t)$. A simulation with $10^6$ draws gives $0.501$, $1.001$ and $1.998$
at $t = 0.5, 1, 2$. The ordinary chain rule, $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW$, would predict $\expect[W_t^2] = 0$.

## Worked example 2: $f(W) = e^{W}$ and $\cos W$

For $f(w) = e^w$, $f' = f'' = e^w$, so

$$
d e^{W_t} = e^{W_t}\,dW_t + \tfrac12 e^{W_t}\,dt.
$$

Let $m(t) = \expect[e^{W_t}]$. Taking expectations, $m'(t) = \tfrac12 m(t)$ with $m(0) = 1$, so $m(t) = e^{t/2}$.
This matches the <Gloss name="mgf">moment-generating function</Gloss> of $\Gauss(0, t)$, $\expect[e^{W_t}] = e^{t/2}$. At $t = 1$ the value is
$1.649$, and $10^6$ simulated draws give $1.651$.

The same calculation for $f(w) = \cos w$, with $f'' = -\cos w$, gives $m'(t) = -\tfrac12 m(t)$ and
$\expect[\cos W_t] = e^{-t/2}$, which is $0.607$ at $t = 1$ (simulated: $0.606$). The mean decays because the noise
spreads $W$ over many periods of the cosine.

As a corollary, for $f(w, t) = e^{\theta w - \theta^2 t/2}$ the drift terms are
$\partial_t f + \tfrac12 f'' = (-\tfrac12\theta^2 + \tfrac12\theta^2) f = 0$. So $e^{\theta W_t - \theta^2 t/2}$ has
no drift, and it is a <NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink>, the **exponential martingale**. It reappears as
the likelihood ratio in <NoteLink to="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</NoteLink>.

## Worked example 3: geometric Brownian motion

A stock price $S_t$ is often modelled by $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$: the return over a short time has mean
$\mu\,dt$ and <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> $\sigma\sqrt{dt}$. Apply Itô's lemma to $f(s) = \log s$, with $f' = 1/s$ and
$f'' = -1/s^2$. Here $a = \mu S$ and $b = \sigma S$:

$$
d\log S = \paren{\frac{\mu S}{S} - \frac12 \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{1}{S^2}}dt + \frac{\sigma S}{S}\,dW = \paren{\mu -
\tfrac12\sigma^2}dt + \sigma\,dW.
$$

The right side has constant coefficients, so it integrates directly:

$$
S_t = S_0 \exp\paren{\paren{\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2}t + \sigma W_t}.
$$

$\log S_t$ is Gaussian, so $S_t$ is <NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal</NoteLink>. With $S_0 = 100$,
$\mu = 0.05$ and $\sigma = 0.2$ per year:

- The median after one year is $100\,e^{0.05 - 0.02} = 103.05$, because the median of $\log S_1$ is its mean.
- The mean is $100\,e^{0.05} = 105.13$. Taking expectations in $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ gives
  $\frac{d}{dt}\expect S = \mu\,\expect S$ directly.
- The probability of a loss is $\prob(\log S_1 < \log S_0) = \Phi(-0.03/0.2) = \Phi(-0.15) = 0.440$.

The ordinary chain rule would give $d\log S = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ and put the median at $100\,e^{0.05}$. The Itô
correction $-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ is the "volatility drag": a return of $+10\%$ followed by $-10\%$ leaves $0.99$ of the
start, and the noise lowers the growth rate of the median from $\mu$ to $\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2$.

## More than one dimension, and the product rule

For $\xvec_t \in \reals^d$ with $d\xvec = \avec\,dt + \Bmat\,d\wvec$, where $\wvec$ is an $m$-dimensional Brownian
motion with independent coordinates and $\Bmat$ is $d \times m$, the rules are $dW_i\,dW_j = \delta_{ij}\,dt$. The
lemma becomes

$$
df(\xvec_t) = \paren{\nabla f^\top \avec + \tfrac12 \trace\paren{\Bmat\Bmat^\top \nabla^2 f}}dt + \nabla f^\top
\Bmat\, d\wvec.
$$

When $\Bmat = g\,\eye$ the trace is $g^2\Delta f$, where $\Delta f = \sum_i \partial^2 f/\partial x_i^2$ is the
Laplacian. This is the form used to derive the <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck
equation</NoteLink>.

Applied to $f(x, y) = xy$, whose only non-zero second derivative is the mixed one, the lemma gives the **product
rule** $d(XY) = X\,dY + Y\,dX + dX\,dY$. The last term is $b_X b_Y\,dt$ when $X$ and $Y$ are driven by the same
Brownian motion and zero when they are driven by independent ones.

## In machine learning

- **Densities of diffusions.** Applying the lemma to a test function and taking expectations gives the
  <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink>, the basis of
  <NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin sampling</NoteLink> and of the reverse-time analysis of <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion models</Gloss>.
- **Closed-form noising.** The lemma applied to $e^{\theta t}X_t$ solves the
  <NoteLink to="ornstein-uhlenbeck-process">Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process</NoteLink>, which gives the one-shot noising
  formula $\xvec_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\xvec_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilonvec$ of a variance-preserving
  diffusion model.
- **Moment equations.** Taking expectations of the lemma for $f(x) = x$ and $f(x) = x^2$ gives <Gloss name="ode">ordinary differential
  equations</Gloss> for the mean and variance of a linear <Gloss name="sde" />, which is how continuous-discrete <NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filters</NoteLink> predict between
  observations <Cite id="sarkka2019" />.

## Assumptions

- $f$ must be twice differentiable in $x$. For $f(x) = \abs{x}$ the second derivative is a point mass, and the
  correction becomes a local-time term (Tanaka's formula) <Cite id="karatzas1998" />.
- The integrals are Itô integrals. In the Stratonovich convention the ordinary chain rule holds and no correction
  appears; the two forms describe the same process with different drifts <Cite id="oksendal2003" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Forgetting the correction.** The most common error in <NoteLink to="stochastic-calculus">stochastic calculus</NoteLink> is the ordinary chain rule. The mean of
  $f(X_t)$ drifts by $\tfrac12 b^2 f''$ even when $X$ has no drift.
- **Wrong coefficient.** The correction uses the squared diffusion coefficient of $X$, $b^2$, not $b$. For
  $dX = \sqrt2\,dW$, as in Langevin dynamics, the correction is $f''\,dt$.
- **Dropping cross-variation.** In several dimensions with correlated noise, $\Bmat\Bmat^\top$ has off-diagonal terms,
  and the mixed second derivatives contribute.
