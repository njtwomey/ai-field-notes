<Definition>
Let $\xvec_t$ solve $d\xvec = \fvec(\xvec, t)\,dt + g(t)\,d\wvec$, with marginal densities $p_t$. The
**probability-flow <Gloss name="ode" />** is the ordinary differential equation

$$
\frac{d\xvec}{dt} = \fvec(\xvec, t) - \tfrac12 g(t)^2\,\nabla\log p_t(\xvec).
$$

If $\xvec_0 \sim p_0$ and $\xvec_t$ follows this ODE, then $\xvec_t \sim p_t$ for every $t$: the ODE and the <Gloss name="sde" /> have
the same marginals, although the ODE has no noise <Cite id="song2021" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition: deterministic spreading

Watch a crowd from above as it disperses from a stadium. Individual people wander, cross paths and double back. Now
replace them with people who walk calmly along fixed streamlines, never crossing, each at the local average velocity of
the crowd. The density of the crowd at every moment is identical in the two pictures. Only the individual trajectories
differ.

A diffusion spreads a cloud of particles in two ways: the drift $\fvec$ carries it and the noise widens it. Noise
widens the cloud because more particles leave dense regions than enter them. The same widening can be produced without
any randomness by moving every particle outward, away from high density, at the velocity
$-\tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log p_t$. The probability-flow ODE replaces the random spreading by this deterministic outward
drift.

## Derivation from Fokker–Planck

The marginals of the SDE obey the <NoteLink to="fokker-planck-equation">Fokker–Planck equation</NoteLink>

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\nabla\cdot(\fvec\,p_t) + \tfrac12 g^2\,\Delta p_t.
$$

Write the Laplacian as the divergence of a gradient and use $\nabla p = p\,\nabla\log p$:

$$
\tfrac12 g^2\,\Delta p_t = \nabla\cdot\paren{\tfrac12 g^2\,\nabla p_t} = \nabla\cdot\paren{\tfrac12 g^2\,p_t\,\nabla\log
p_t}.
$$

Substituting,

$$
\partial_t p_t = -\nabla\cdot\brack{\paren{\fvec - \tfrac12 g^2\,\nabla\log p_t}p_t}.
$$

This has no second-order term. It is the <NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">continuity
equation</NoteLink> $\partial_t p + \nabla\cdot(\vvec\,p) = 0$ for the velocity field
$\vvec = \fvec - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log p_t$. A density carried by the deterministic flow $d\xvec/dt = \vvec(\xvec, t)$
obeys exactly this equation, so the ODE transports $p_0$ through the same $p_t$ as the SDE. The ODE is the
<NoteLink to="vector-fields-and-flows">flow of a vector field</NoteLink> that depends on the density it moves.

The same rewrite gives a whole family. For any $\lambda \ge 0$ split the diffusion term into two parts,
$\tfrac12 g^2\Delta p_t = \tfrac{\lambda^2}{2}g^2\,\Delta p_t + \tfrac{1 - \lambda^2}{2}g^2\,\nabla\cdot(p_t\nabla\log
p_t)$, and move only the second part into the drift. The result is the Fokker–Planck equation of the SDE

$$
d\xvec = \brack{\fvec - \tfrac{1 - \lambda^2}{2}\,g^2\,\nabla\log p_t}dt + \lambda g\,d\wvec,
$$

so these SDEs all have marginals $p_t$. At $\lambda = 1$ the family contains the original SDE, and at $\lambda = 0$
the probability-flow ODE. The <NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">reverse-time SDE</NoteLink>
comes from the same identity run backwards in time; its score term is $g^2\nabla\log p_t$, twice the ODE's.

## Worked example 1: Brownian motion from a Gaussian

Take $dX = dW$ with $X_0 \sim \Gauss(0, v_0)$. Then $p_t = \Gauss(0, v_0 + t)$ and the score is $-x/(v_0 + t)$. The
probability-flow ODE is

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = -\tfrac12\paren{-\frac{x}{v_0 + t}} = \frac{x}{2(v_0 + t)}.
$$

Separate variables: $d\log x = \tfrac12\,d\log(v_0 + t)$, so $x(t) = x_0\sqrt{(v_0 + t)/v_0}$. Every particle is
scaled by the same factor $\sqrt{(v_0 + t)/v_0}$, and a scaled $\Gauss(0, v_0)$ is $\Gauss(0, v_0 + t)$. The ODE
reproduces the spreading of Brownian motion with no randomness at all. A particle at $x_0 = 1$ with $v_0 = 1$ is at
$\sqrt2 = 1.414$ at $t = 1$, where the SDE would have put it anywhere in $\Gauss(1, 1)$.

## Worked example 2: exact likelihood for a variance-preserving process

Take $dX = -\tfrac12\beta X\,dt + \sqrt\beta\,dW$ with $X_0 \sim \Gauss(0, v_0)$, so $p_t = \Gauss(0, v_t)$ with
$v_t = 1 + (v_0 - 1)e^{-\beta t}$. The ODE velocity is

$$
v(x, t) = -\tfrac12\beta x + \tfrac12\beta\frac{x}{v_t} = \tfrac12\beta x\paren{\frac{1}{v_t} - 1}.
$$

Its solution is $x_t = x_0\sqrt{v_t/v_0}$; check: $\frac{d}{dt}\log x_t = \frac{v_t'}{2v_t} = \frac{-\beta(v_t - 1)}{2
v_t}$, which equals $v/x$.

**Likelihood.** Along a trajectory of a flow the log-density changes by minus the divergence of the velocity (the
instantaneous change of variables <Cite id="chen2018" />):

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\xvec_t) = -\nabla\cdot\vvec(\xvec_t, t), \qquad \text{so} \qquad \log p_0(\xvec_0) = \log
p_T(\xvec_T) + \int_0^T \nabla\cdot\vvec(\xvec_t, t)\,dt.
$$

Here $\partial_x v = \tfrac12\beta(1/v_t - 1)$, whose integral from 0 to $T$ is $\tfrac12\log(v_T/v_0)$. With
$\beta = 1$, $v_0 = 0.25$, $T = 3$ and $x_0 = 0.8$: $v_T = 0.963$, $x_T = 1.570$, and

$$
\log\Gauss(1.570; 0, 0.963) + \tfrac12\log(0.963/0.25) = -2.180 + 0.674 = -1.506 = \log\Gauss(0.8; 0, 0.25).
$$

For a trained <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss> the same computation integrates the ODE from a data point to $\xvec_T$, evaluates the
Gaussian log-density there, and adds the integrated divergence. In high dimensions the divergence is estimated with
Hutchinson's trace estimator, $\nabla\cdot\vvec = \expect_{\epsilonvec}[\epsilonvec^\top(\nabla\vvec)\epsilonvec]$, as
in FFJORD <Cite id="grathwohl2019" />.

## Worked example 3: sampling a two-mode density

Take the two-mode density $0.35\,\Gauss(-2, 0.35^2) + 0.65\,\Gauss(1.5, 0.5^2)$ noised by $dX = -\tfrac12X\,dt +
dW$ up to $T = 5$, whose score is analytic at every time. Particles started from $\Gauss(0, 1)$ at $t = 5$ and moved
backwards with the exact score end in the right-hand mode with frequency:

| sampler                                              | share in right mode (data: 0.650) |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| reverse SDE, 250 steps                               | 0.651                             |
| probability-flow ODE, 1,000 steps, from $\Gauss(0, 1)$ | 0.641                             |
| probability-flow ODE, 1,000 steps, from exact $p_5$  | 0.650                             |

The ODE is exact only if it starts from $p_T$. Starting it from $\Gauss(0, 1)$, which differs from $p_5$ by a
<Gloss name="kl">Kullback–Leibler divergence</Gloss> of $3 \times 10^{-4}$, leaves an error of about one percentage point, because a
deterministic map carries any error in its input to its output. The SDE's noise contracts such errors, and it lands on
$0.651$.

## Uses in machine learning

- **Exact likelihoods.** The ODE turns a diffusion model into a continuous
  <NoteLink to="normalising-flow">normalising flow</NoteLink>, so its log-likelihood can be computed as in worked
  example 2 <Cite id="song2021" />. The SDE sampler has no tractable likelihood.
- **Deterministic sampling.** The deterministic <NoteLink to="denoising-diffusion-implicit-models">DDIM</NoteLink>
  sampler is a discretisation of the probability-flow ODE of the variance-preserving process <Cite id="song2021b" />.
  Because the trajectories are smooth, higher-order <NoteLink to="numerical-ode-solvers">ODE solvers</NoteLink> reach
  good samples in tens of network evaluations rather than hundreds or thousands <Cite id="karras2022" />.
- **Encoding and editing.** The map from noise to data is invertible. Running the ODE forwards from an image gives its
  latent noise, which can be interpolated, edited or re-decoded (DDIM inversion).
- **Neural ODEs and flow matching.** The probability-flow ODE is a
  <NoteLink to="neural-ordinary-differential-equations">neural ODE</NoteLink> whose velocity comes from a score
  network instead of being trained by maximum likelihood.

## Assumptions

- **The score is exact.** With a learned score the ODE's marginals are only approximately $p_t$, and the likelihood
  computed along it is the likelihood of the model defined by the learned ODE, not of the SDE sampler.
- **Smoothness.** The velocity field must be Lipschitz for the ODE to have unique solutions. Near $t = 0$ the score of
  real data can be very large, so integration usually stops at a small $t = \epsilon$.

## Pitfalls

- **Same marginals, not the same paths.** The ODE and the SDE agree on the distribution at each time, not on the joint
  law of a path. A particle's ODE trajectory is not a typical SDE path.
- **Half, not all, of the score.** The ODE uses $\tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log p_t$; the reverse SDE uses $g^2\nabla\log
  p_t$. Using the full term without noise over-concentrates the samples.
- **Errors do not wash out.** Errors in the start distribution or in the score propagate through a deterministic map.
  Stochastic samplers can correct some of them; this is one reason stochastic samplers sometimes give better samples
  from the same network <Cite id="karras2022" />.
