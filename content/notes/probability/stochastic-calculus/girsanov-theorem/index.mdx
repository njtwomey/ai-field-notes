<Definition>
Let $W_t$ be a Brownian motion under a <NoteLink to="probability-space">probability measure</NoteLink> $\prob$ on
$[0, T]$, and let $\theta_t$ be a non-anticipating process with
$\expect\brack{\exp\paren{\tfrac12\int_0^T \theta_t^2\,dt}} < \infty$ (Novikov's condition). Define

$$
Z_T = \exp\paren{\int_0^T \theta_t\,dW_t - \tfrac12\int_0^T \theta_t^2\,dt}
$$

and a new measure $\mathbb{Q}$ by $d\mathbb{Q}/d\prob = Z_T$. Then under $\mathbb{Q}$ the process
$\tilde W_t = W_t - \int_0^t \theta_s\,ds$ is a Brownian motion <Cite id="girsanov1960" />. Equivalently, reweighting
the paths of $W$ by $Z_T$ turns Brownian motion into Brownian motion with drift $\theta$.
</Definition>

## Intuition: the same paths, weighted differently

Simulate many Brownian paths. A few of them happen to drift upwards. If each path is given a weight that grows with how
far it drifted up, the weighted collection looks like Brownian motion with an upward drift. Girsanov's theorem says
that the right weight exists, is explicit, and depends only on the path. No path has to be simulated with the drift.

Changing the noise level cannot be done this way. The sum of squared increments of a path reveals its noise level
exactly (its <NoteLink to="brownian-motion">quadratic variation</NoteLink> is $\sigma^2 T$), so paths with different
$\sigma$ never look alike, and no weighting turns one into the other. Two diffusions with the same noise and different
drifts are comparable path by path; two with different noise are not.

## Derivation on a grid

Consider two processes on a grid of step $h$: under $\prob$ the increments are $\Delta X_k \sim \Gauss(b_k h, \sigma^2
h)$, and under $\mathbb{Q}$ they are $\Delta X_k \sim \Gauss(c_k h, \sigma^2 h)$, where the drifts $b_k$ and $c_k$ may
depend on the path so far. The likelihood ratio of one increment is a ratio of Gaussian densities with the same
variance:

$$
\frac{\Gauss(\Delta X_k; c_k h, \sigma^2 h)}{\Gauss(\Delta X_k; b_k h, \sigma^2 h)} = \exp\paren{\frac{(c_k -
b_k)\,\Delta X_k}{\sigma^2} - \frac{(c_k^2 - b_k^2)\,h}{2\sigma^2}}.
$$

The ratio for the whole path is the product over steps, the exponential of a sum. As $h \to 0$ the sums become an
<NoteLink to="ito-integral">Itô integral</NoteLink> and an ordinary integral:

$$
\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\prob}(X) = \exp\paren{\int_0^T \frac{c_t - b_t}{\sigma^2}\,dX_t - \int_0^T \frac{c_t^2 -
b_t^2}{2\sigma^2}\,dt}.
$$

With $b = 0$, $\sigma = 1$ and $c = \theta$ this is $Z_T$. The noise variance cancels only because it is the same under
both measures, which is why the drift, and only the drift, can be changed.

The weight has mean 1 under $\prob$, as a likelihood ratio must. For constant $\theta$ this follows from
<NoteLink to="itos-lemma">Itô's lemma</NoteLink>: $Z_t = e^{\theta W_t - \theta^2 t/2}$ has no drift, so it is a
<NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink> with $\expect Z_t = Z_0 = 1$.

## Worked example 1: constant drift

Take $\theta = \mu$ constant, so $Z_T = e^{\mu W_T - \mu^2 T/2}$. Under $\mathbb{Q}$ the endpoint should have mean
$\mu T$. Check it under $\prob$, where $W_T \sim \Gauss(0, T)$:

$$
\expect_{\mathbb{Q}}[W_T] = \expect_{\prob}\brack{W_T\,e^{\mu W_T - \mu^2 T/2}} = e^{-\mu^2 T/2}\,\frac{d}{d\mu}
\expect_{\prob}\brack{e^{\mu W_T}} = e^{-\mu^2 T/2}\,\frac{d}{d\mu}e^{\mu^2 T/2} = \mu T.
$$

With $\mu = 0.5$ and $T = 2$, $10^6$ simulated values give a weighted mean of $0.999$ against $\mu T = 1$, and a mean
weight of $1.000$.

## Worked example 2: importance sampling a rare event

The probability that a Brownian motion ends above 3 at time 1 is $\prob(W_1 > 3) = 1 - \Phi(3) = 0.00135$.
<NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Plain Monte Carlo</NoteLink> with $10^4$ paths sees about 13 hits and has a
<NoteLink to="standard-error">standard error</NoteLink> of $0.00037$, 27% of the answer.

Instead simulate paths with drift $\mu = 3$, which end above 3 half the time, and correct each by the reverse likelihood
ratio $d\prob/d\mathbb{Q} = e^{-\mu X_1 + \mu^2/2}$, where $X_1 \sim \Gauss(3, 1)$ is the drifted endpoint. With $10^4$
paths the estimate is $0.00134$ with standard error $0.000025$, about 2% of the answer. The variance is 200 times
smaller for the same number of paths. This is <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> on path
space, and it is how rare events of <Gloss name="sde" plural />, such as the default of a firm or the failure of a
system, are estimated.

## Worked example 3: the KL divergence between two diffusions

Let $\prob_1$ and $\prob_2$ be the laws of the paths of $dX = b_i(X, t)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ from the same start, $i =
1, 2$. The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink> is the expected log
likelihood ratio under $\prob_1$. Under $\prob_1$, $dX = b_1\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, so substituting into the log of the
ratio above,

$$
\log\frac{d\prob_1}{d\prob_2} = \int_0^T \frac{b_1 - b_2}{\sigma}\,dW_t + \int_0^T \frac{(b_1 - b_2)^2}{2\sigma^2}\,dt.
$$

The Itô integral has mean zero, so

$$
\KL(\prob_1 \,\|\, \prob_2) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\expect_{\prob_1}\int_0^T \norm{b_1(X_t, t) - b_2(X_t, t)}^2\,dt.
$$

For $b_1 = \mu$, $b_2 = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$ on $[0, T]$ the divergence is $\mu^2 T/2$. The endpoints alone have laws
$\Gauss(\mu T, T)$ and $\Gauss(0, T)$, whose divergence is also $\mu^2 T^2/(2T) = \mu^2 T/2$. The endpoint of a Brownian
motion with constant drift carries all the information about the drift. In general the divergence between endpoint laws
is at most the divergence between path laws, by the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">data-processing
inequality</NoteLink>.

## Diffusion models: a likelihood bound

A <Gloss name="diffusion-model">diffusion model</Gloss> generates with the
<NoteLink to="reverse-time-stochastic-differential-equations">reverse-time SDE</NoteLink>
$d\xvec = [\fvec - g^2\svec_{\thetavec}]\,dt + g\,d\bar\wvec$. The true reverse process has the same form with the true
score $\nabla\log p_t$ in place of $\svec_{\thetavec}$. The two reverse SDEs have the same noise $g$, and their drifts
differ by $g^2(\nabla\log p_t - \svec_{\thetavec})$. Worked example 3 gives the divergence between their path laws, and
data processing bounds the divergence between the data distribution and the model's samples:

$$
\KL(p_0 \,\|\, p_{\thetavec}) \le \KL(p_T \,\|\, \pi) + \frac12\int_0^T g(t)^2\,\expect_{p_t}\norm{\nabla\log p_t(\xvec)
- \svec_{\thetavec}(\xvec, t)}^2\,dt,
$$

where $\pi$ is the Gaussian the sampler starts from. The integral is the score-matching loss with weight
$\lambda(t) = g(t)^2$, so training with that weight minimises an upper bound on the
<NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative log-likelihood</NoteLink> <Cite id="song2021mle" />; see
<NoteLink to="diffusion-models-as-stochastic-differential-equations">diffusion models as SDEs</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Same diffusion coefficient.** The two processes must have the same noise. With different $\sigma$ the path laws are
  mutually singular and the likelihood ratio does not exist.
- **Novikov's condition.** It guarantees that $Z_T$ has mean 1. Without it $Z$ may be a strict local martingale, and
  $\mathbb{Q}$ may lose mass <Cite id="karatzas1998" />.
- **Finite horizon.** On an infinite horizon, Brownian motions with different constant drifts are singular: the long-run
  slope $W_t/t$ identifies the drift.

## Pitfalls

- **Weight degeneracy.** The weight's second moment grows like $e^{\int\theta^2\,dt}$. For a drift change of size $\mu$
  over time $T$, the <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss> falls like $N e^{-\mu^2 T}$, so importance sampling
  on paths works only when the proposal drift is chosen near the target.
- **Sign of the correction.** $\tilde W = W - \int\theta\,ds$ is Brownian under $\mathbb{Q}$, so $W$ itself has drift
  $+\theta$ under $\mathbb{Q}$. Reweighting in the other direction uses $1/Z_T$.
