<Definition>
A **standard Brownian motion** (or **Wiener process**) is a family of <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $\set{B_t : t \ge 0}$ such that

1. $B_0 = 0$;
2. for $0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_k$ the increments $B_{t_1} - B_{t_0}, \dots, B_{t_k} - B_{t_{k-1}}$ are
   independent;
3. for $0 \le s < t$, $B_t - B_s \sim \Gauss(0, t - s)$;
4. the path $t \mapsto B_t$ is continuous, with probability 1.

Wiener proved that a process with these properties exists <Cite id="wiener1923" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Brownian motion is what a random walk looks like from far away. Take a walk with many tiny steps, speed it up and
shrink it so that its variance at time $t$ is $t$. The individual steps become invisible, and what remains is a
continuous path that jitters at every scale. The botanist Robert Brown saw this motion in pollen grains in water,
kicked by water molecules. Einstein explained it, and Wiener built the mathematics.

The variance of $B_t$ grows linearly in $t$, so its typical size grows like $\sqrt t$. This square-root scaling is
the source of every unusual property of the paths: over a short time $h$ the path moves about $\sqrt h$, which is much
larger than $h$. The paths are continuous, but no tangent exists anywhere.

## Brownian motion as a limit of random walks

Let $S_k$ be a simple <NoteLink to="random-walk">symmetric random walk</NoteLink> with steps $\pm 1$, and define the rescaled walk on $[0, 1]$

$$
W^{(n)}(t) = \frac{S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}}{\sqrt n}.
$$

At a fixed $t$, $W^{(n)}(t)$ is a sum of $\lfloor nt \rfloor$ independent steps with mean 0 and variance 1, divided by
$\sqrt n$. Its variance is $\lfloor nt \rfloor/n \to t$, and the
<NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> gives $W^{(n)}(t) \to \Gauss(0, t)$ in
distribution. Increments over disjoint time intervals use disjoint sets of steps, so they are independent, and each
converges to a Gaussian with variance equal to the length of the interval. The limits of the finite-dimensional
distributions are therefore exactly those of Brownian motion.

**Donsker's invariance principle** strengthens this to convergence of the whole path, as a random continuous function
(after linear interpolation between steps). Any step distribution with mean 0 and variance 1 gives the same limit
<Cite id="durrett2019" />. That universality is why Brownian motion appears wherever many small independent effects
accumulate.

## Brownian motion as a Gaussian process

Every finite collection $(B_{t_1}, \dots, B_{t_k})$ is a <NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">linear transformation</NoteLink> of independent Gaussian increments, so
it is <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink>. Brownian motion is therefore a <NoteLink to="gaussian-process">Gaussian process</NoteLink>
with mean 0. Its covariance follows from independent increments. For $s \le t$, write $B_t = B_s + (B_t - B_s)$:

$$
\cov(B_s, B_t) = \var(B_s) + \cov(B_s, B_t - B_s) = s + 0 = s.
$$

So $\cov(B_s, B_t) = \min(s, t)$. Conversely, a continuous mean-zero Gaussian process with this covariance is a
Brownian motion. In Gaussian-process regression, $\min(s, t)$ is the Wiener kernel. Its sample paths are rough, and
its posterior mean interpolates the data piecewise linearly.

## Properties

- **Scaling.** For $c > 0$, $\set{c^{-1/2}B_{ct}}$ is again a standard Brownian motion. Zooming in on a path by a
  factor $c$ in time and $\sqrt c$ in space gives a path with the same law. The paths are statistically self-similar.
- **Martingales.** $B_t$, $B_t^2 - t$ and $\exp(\theta B_t - \theta^2t/2)$ are
  <NoteLink to="martingale">martingales</NoteLink>. For example, $\expect[B_t^2 \mid \Fcal_s] = B_s^2 + (t - s)$
  because the increment $B_t - B_s$ is independent of the past with mean 0 and variance $t - s$.
- **Markov property.** After any fixed time $s$, or any stopping time, $B_{s + u} - B_s$ is a new Brownian motion
  independent of the path up to $s$.
- **Reflection principle.** For $a > 0$, $\prob(\max_{s \le t}B_s \ge a) = 2\prob(B_t \ge a)$. The proof reflects the
  path after its first visit to $a$, which maps paths ending above $a$ to paths ending below $a$ one-to-one. Over
  $[0, 1]$, the maximum exceeds 1 with probability $2(1 - \Phi(1)) = 0.317$, twice the probability that $B_1$ does.
- **Roughness.** With probability 1 the path is differentiable at no time <Cite id="morters2010" />. It crosses 0
  infinitely often in every interval $[0, \epsilon]$.

## Quadratic variation

Partition $[0, t]$ into $m$ intervals of length $h = t/m$, with increments $\Delta_j = B_{jh} - B_{(j-1)h}$. The
**quadratic variation** along the partition is

$$
Q_m = \sum_{j=1}^m \Delta_j^2.
$$

Each $\Delta_j \sim \Gauss(0, h)$, so $\expect[\Delta_j^2] = h$ and $\var(\Delta_j^2) = 2h^2$, using
$\expect[Z^4] = 3$ for a standard normal $Z$. The increments are independent, so

$$
\expect[Q_m] = mh = t, \qquad \var(Q_m) = 2mh^2 = \frac{2t^2}{m} \to 0.
$$

Hence $Q_m \to t$ in mean square as the partition refines. The sum of squared increments of a Brownian path over
$[0, t]$ is not random in the limit: it equals $t$. For a differentiable function $f$ the same sum is at most
$\max\abs{f'}^2h \cdot t \to 0$.

The length of the path is infinite. Since $\sum_j\abs{\Delta_j} \ge Q_m/\max_j\abs{\Delta_j}$, and continuity makes
$\max_j\abs{\Delta_j} \to 0$ while $Q_m \to t > 0$, the sum of absolute increments diverges. Its mean is
$m\sqrt{h}\sqrt{2/\pi} = \sqrt{2mt/\pi}$.

<QuadraticVariation />

Quadratic variation is why <NoteLink to="stochastic-calculus">stochastic calculus</NoteLink> differs from ordinary calculus. In a <NoteLink to="taylor-series">Taylor expansion</NoteLink> of $f(B_t)$ the
second-order term $\frac12 f''(B_t)(\Delta B)^2$ does not vanish, because $(\Delta B)^2$ sums to $\Delta t$. The
result is **Itô's formula**,

$$
df(B_t) = f'(B_t)\,dB_t + \tfrac12 f''(B_t)\,dt,
$$

which has an extra drift term compared with the ordinary <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> <Cite id="karatzas1998" />. For $f(x) = x^2$ it
gives $d(B_t^2) = 2B_t\,dB_t + dt$, consistent with $B_t^2 - t$ being a martingale.

## Worked example: hitting a barrier

A quantity follows a Brownian motion with variance $\sigma^2 = 4$ per day, so $X_t = 2B_t$. What is the probability
that it rises 3 units above its start within 1 day?

The event $\max_{s \le 1}X_s \ge 3$ is $\max_{s \le 1}B_s \ge 1.5$. The reflection principle gives

$$
\prob\paren{\max_{s \le 1}B_s \ge 1.5} = 2\prob(B_1 \ge 1.5) = 2(1 - \Phi(1.5)) = 2 \times 0.0668 = 0.134.
$$

The probability that it ends the day at least 3 units up is only $0.067$. Half the paths that touch the barrier fall
back below it by the end of the day.

## In machine learning

- **<Gloss name="diffusion-model">Diffusion models</Gloss>.** The forward process of a diffusion model adds Gaussian noise continuously. In continuous time
  it is a <Gloss name="sde">stochastic differential equation</Gloss> driven by Brownian motion, and sampling reverses it using a learned score;
  see <NoteLink to="score-based-generative-models">score-based generative models</NoteLink>.
- **<NoteLink to="langevin-dynamics">Langevin dynamics</NoteLink>.** The equation $d\xvec_t = \nabla\log p(\xvec_t)\,dt + \sqrt2\,d\wvec_t$, where $\wvec_t$
  is a Brownian motion in $\reals^d$, has stationary distribution $p$. Its discretisation is the unadjusted Langevin
  algorithm, and stochastic-gradient versions sample Bayesian posteriors.
- **<NoteLink to="gradient">Gradient</NoteLink> noise.** <Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> with a small <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> behaves approximately like <NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria">gradient flow</NoteLink>
  plus a Brownian term whose covariance is set by the mini-batch gradient noise.

## Pitfalls

- **Discrete simulations are not paths.** A simulated path on a grid is a random walk with Gaussian steps. Quantities
  such as the maximum or the first hitting time are biased on a coarse grid, because the path can cross a barrier
  between grid points.
- **Ordinary calculus fails.** $\int_0^t B_s\,dB_s = (B_t^2 - t)/2$, not $B_t^2/2$. Applying the ordinary chain rule to
  Brownian paths drops the quadratic-variation term.
- **Scale is $\sqrt t$, not $t$.** Doubling a time horizon multiplies the typical displacement by $\sqrt 2$.
