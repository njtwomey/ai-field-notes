<Definition>
Let $\xi_1, \xi_2, \dots$ be <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss> random vectors in $\reals^d$. The **random walk**
with steps $\xi_i$ started at $S_0$ is

$$
S_n = S_0 + \sum_{i=1}^n \xi_i, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

The **simple random walk** on $\integers$ has steps $\xi_i = +1$ with probability $p$ and $-1$ with probability
$q = 1 - p$; it is **symmetric** when $p = 1/2$. The simple symmetric random walk on $\integers^d$ moves at each step
to one of the $2d$ neighbouring lattice points, each with probability $1/(2d)$ <Cite id="feller1968" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A random walk is the position of a walker who takes independent random steps. It is the simplest
<NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink> on an infinite state space: the next position depends only on the
current one. It models a gambler's fortune, a diffusing particle, a stock price on a log scale and a sampler proposing
local moves.

The walk has no pull back to its start. Its typical distance from the start grows like $\sqrt n$, the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard
deviation</NoteLink> of a sum of $n$ independent steps. Whether it ever comes back depends on the dimension, because the number
of places to get lost in grows with the dimension.

## Distribution after $n$ steps

For the simple walk on $\integers$ from $S_0 = 0$, reaching $k$ after $n$ steps requires $u$ up-steps and $n - u$
down-steps with $u - (n - u) = k$, so $u = (n + k)/2$. The number of up-steps is $\Binom(n, p)$, so

$$
\prob(S_n = k) = \binom{n}{(n + k)/2}p^{(n + k)/2}q^{(n - k)/2}
$$

when $n + k$ is even and $\abs{k} \le n$, and 0 otherwise. Each step has mean $p - q$ and variance
$1 - (p - q)^2 = 4pq$, so $\expect[S_n] = n(p - q)$ and $\var(S_n) = 4npq$. The symmetric walk has mean 0 and standard
deviation $\sqrt n$.

## Absorbing barriers: gambler's ruin

Start the simple walk at $i$ with $0 \le i \le N$ and stop it on reaching 0 or $N$. Let $w_i$ be the probability of
reaching $N$ first. Conditioning on the first step gives $w_i = pw_{i+1} + qw_{i-1}$ for $0 < i < N$, with
$w_0 = 0$ and $w_N = 1$. Writing $w_i = (p + q)w_i$ and rearranging, $p(w_{i+1} - w_i) = q(w_i - w_{i-1})$: the
successive differences form a geometric sequence with ratio $r = q/p$. Summing them and normalising with $w_N = 1$,

$$
w_i = \frac{1 - r^i}{1 - r^N} \quad (p \ne q), \qquad w_i = \frac{i}{N} \quad (p = q = 1/2).
$$

With $p = 0.47$, a gambler starting with 10 units reaches 20 with probability $0.231$, against $0.5$ in a fair game.
The note on <NoteLink to="gamblers-ruin">gambler's ruin</NoteLink> works through this problem and the expected
duration. The <NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink> note derives the same formula from the optional stopping
theorem.

## Recurrence in one dimension

A state is **recurrent** if the walk returns to it with probability 1. For a Markov chain, a state $i$ is recurrent
exactly when the expected number of visits, $\sum_{n \ge 0}\prob(S_n = i \mid S_0 = i)$, is infinite. The reason is
that each return restarts the chain, so the number of visits is geometric with mean $1/(1 - f)$, where $f$ is the
return probability. The mean is finite if and only if $f < 1$.

Returns to 0 happen only at even times. With $n$ up-steps among $2n$,

$$
\prob(S_{2n} = 0) = \binom{2n}{n}(pq)^n.
$$

<NoteLink to="gamma-function">Stirling's formula</NoteLink>, $m! \sim \sqrt{2\pi m}\,(m/e)^m$, gives $\binom{2n}{n} \sim 4^n/\sqrt{\pi n}$, so

$$
\prob(S_{2n} = 0) \sim \frac{(4pq)^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.
$$

- **Symmetric walk.** Here $4pq = 1$ and $\prob(S_{2n} = 0) \sim 1/\sqrt{\pi n}$. The series $\sum_n n^{-1/2}$
  diverges, so the walk is recurrent: it returns to 0 with probability 1, and therefore infinitely often. At $n = 10$
  the exact value is $\binom{20}{10}/4^{10} = 0.1762$ and the approximation gives $0.1784$.
- **Asymmetric walk.** Here $4pq < 1$, the terms decay geometrically, and the series converges. The walk is transient:
  it drifts away in the direction of $p - q$ and, with positive probability, never returns.

The symmetric walk returns with probability 1, yet the expected return time is infinite. The walk is **null
recurrent**. A positive recurrent chain would have a <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">stationary distribution</NoteLink> with $\pi_i = 1/m_i > 0$, where $m_i$ is
the mean return time. No probability distribution on $\integers$ can put equal positive mass on every integer, and
translation invariance forces equal masses.

## Pólya's theorem

**Theorem.** The simple symmetric random walk on $\integers^d$ is recurrent for $d = 1$ and $d = 2$ and transient for
$d \ge 3$ <Cite id="polya1921" />.

The proof compares $\sum_n \prob(S_{2n} = \zeros)$ with a series of powers of $n$.

**Two dimensions.** Rotate the lattice by 45 degrees and scale by $\sqrt 2$. The four steps $(\pm1, 0)$ and
$(0, \pm1)$ become $(\pm1, \pm1)$ with all four sign combinations equally likely. The two rotated coordinates are then
independent simple symmetric walks on $\integers$. The walk is at the origin exactly when both are, so

$$
\prob(S_{2n} = \zeros) = \paren{\binom{2n}{n}4^{-n}}^2 \sim \frac{1}{\pi n}.
$$

The harmonic series diverges, so the planar walk is recurrent.

**Three or more dimensions.** A walk of $2n$ steps spends about $2n/d$ steps in each coordinate. Each coordinate is
back at 0 with probability of order $n^{-1/2}$, and the coordinates are nearly independent for large $n$, so
$\prob(S_{2n} = \zeros)$ is of order $n^{-d/2}$. For $d \ge 3$ the series $\sum_n n^{-d/2}$ converges, and the walk is
transient <Cite id="durrett2019" />. In three dimensions the return probability is about $0.34$.

## The reflection principle

For the symmetric walk from 0 and $a > 0$, the paths that reach level $a$ by time $n$ and end below $a$ are in
one-to-one correspondence with paths that end above $a$. The correspondence reflects the part of the path after its
first visit to $a$. Hence

$$
\prob\paren{\max_{k \le n}S_k \ge a} = 2\prob(S_n > a) + \prob(S_n = a).
$$

For $n = 10$ and $a = 4$ this gives $0.2266$. The maximum of a walk is about twice as likely to exceed a level as the
endpoint is. The same argument gives the distribution of the maximum of
<NoteLink to="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</NoteLink>, the scaling limit of the walk.

## In machine learning

- **Random-walk Metropolis** proposes $\xvec' = \xvec + \epsilon$ with Gaussian $\epsilon$. Between rejections the
  sampler is a random walk, so it explores a region of diameter $L$ in about $(L/\sigma)^2$ steps, where $\sigma$ is
  the proposal scale. This diffusive slowness motivates gradient-based samplers.
- **Graph embeddings** such as DeepWalk and node2vec treat truncated random walks on a graph as sentences and embed
  nodes that co-occur on walks close together.
- **Time series.** A random walk is the canonical non-stationary series; its first difference is
  <NoteLink to="white-noise-and-random-walk">white noise</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Recurrent does not mean quick.** The symmetric walk on $\integers$ returns with probability 1, but its expected
  return time is infinite.
- **Typical excursions are long.** The fraction of time a symmetric walk spends above 0 does not concentrate near
  $1/2$. It follows the arcsine law, whose density is largest near 0 and 1.
- **Dimension matters.** Intuition from one-dimensional walks, such as certain return, fails in three dimensions.
