<Definition>
A **Poisson process** with rate $\lambda > 0$ is a family of counts $\set{N(t) : t \ge 0}$, where $N(t)$ is the number
of events in $[0, t]$, such that

1. $N(0) = 0$;
2. for $0 \le t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_k$ the increments $N(t_1) - N(t_0), \dots, N(t_k) - N(t_{k-1})$ are independent;
3. for all $s, t \ge 0$, $N(s + t) - N(s) \sim \Poisson(\lambda t)$.

Equivalently, the times between successive events are independent $\Exp(\lambda)$ <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink>
<Cite id="kingman1993" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Events arrive at a constant average rate $\lambda$ per unit time, with no memory and no interaction: radioactive
decays, requests to a server, typos on a page. What happens in one interval says nothing about another. The process
is the continuous-time limit of <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flips</NoteLink>: split $[0, t]$ into $n$ short slots, put an event in each slot
independently with probability $\lambda t/n$, and let $n \to \infty$. The number of events is
$\Binom(n, \lambda t/n)$, which converges to $\Poisson(\lambda t)$.

## Three equivalent definitions

The process has three standard descriptions. Each is the most convenient for some purpose.

- **(A) Counts.** The definition above: independent increments with $\Poisson(\lambda t)$ counts.
- **(B) Gaps.** Let $T_1, T_2, \dots$ be independent $\Exp(\lambda)$ interarrival times, let the arrival times be
  $S_n = T_1 + \dots + T_n$, and let $N(t) = \max\set{n : S_n \le t}$.
- **(C) Rates.** $N(0) = 0$, the increments are independent and stationary, and in a short interval of length $h$,
  $\prob(\text{one event}) = \lambda h + o(h)$ and $\prob(\text{two or more}) = o(h)$.

Description (C) is the physical one: events are rare, individually, and happen at rate $\lambda$. It implies (A),
which implies (B), which implies (A) <Cite id="grimmett2020" />.

**From rates to counts.** Let $p_n(t) = \prob(N(t) = n)$. For no events by $t + h$, there must be none by $t$ and none
in $(t, t + h]$. By independence of increments, $p_0(t + h) = p_0(t)(1 - \lambda h + o(h))$. Rearranging and letting
$h \to 0$ gives $p_0'(t) = -\lambda p_0(t)$ with $p_0(0) = 1$, so $p_0(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$. For $n \ge 1$, $n$ events
by $t + h$ arise from $n$ by $t$ and none after, or $n - 1$ by $t$ and one after, up to $o(h)$:

$$
p_n'(t) = -\lambda p_n(t) + \lambda p_{n-1}(t).
$$

Substituting $p_n(t) = e^{-\lambda t}q_n(t)$ gives $q_n' = \lambda q_{n-1}$ with $q_0 = 1$ and $q_n(0) = 0$, so
$q_n(t) = (\lambda t)^n/n!$ by induction. Hence $N(t) \sim \Poisson(\lambda t)$.

**From counts to gaps.** The first event comes after $t$ exactly when there are no events in $[0, t]$:

$$
\prob(T_1 > t) = \prob(N(t) = 0) = e^{-\lambda t},
$$

which is the survival function of $\Exp(\lambda)$. Given $T_1 = s$, the time to the next event depends only on the
increments after $s$. These are independent of the past and have the same law as the original process, so $T_2$ is
again $\Exp(\lambda)$ and independent of $T_1$. Repeating the argument makes all gaps independent $\Exp(\lambda)$.

**From gaps to counts.** The arrival time $S_n$ is a sum of $n$ independent $\Exp(\lambda)$ variables, which has the
gamma density $f_{S_n}(s) = \lambda^n s^{n-1}e^{-\lambda s}/(n - 1)!$. There are at least $n$ events by $t$ exactly
when $S_n \le t$. Therefore $\prob(N(t) = n) = \prob(S_n \le t) - \prob(S_{n+1} \le t)$. Differentiating in $t$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\prob(N(t) = n) = \frac{\lambda^n t^{n-1}e^{-\lambda t}}{(n - 1)!} - \frac{\lambda^{n+1}t^ne^{-\lambda t}}{n!}
= \frac{d}{dt}\brack{\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda t}}.
$$

Both sides vanish at $t = 0$ for $n \ge 1$, so $\prob(N(t) = n) = e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^n/n!$.

## Memorylessness

The exponential gap is memoryless: $\prob(T > s + t \mid T > s) = e^{-\lambda(s + t)}/e^{-\lambda s} = e^{-\lambda t}$.
Having waited $s$ without an event does not shorten the remaining wait. The process restarts afresh at every fixed
time and at every event time. A bus stop served by a Poisson process with a mean gap of 10 minutes has a mean wait of
10 minutes whenever the passenger arrives.

## Arrival times given the count

Given $N(t) = n$, the $n$ arrival times are distributed as $n$ independent $\Unif(0, t)$ points, sorted. For $n = 1$
and $0 \le s \le t$:

$$
\prob(S_1 \le s \mid N(t) = 1) = \frac{\prob(N(s) = 1)\,\prob(N(t) - N(s) = 0)}{\prob(N(t) = 1)} =
\frac{\lambda se^{-\lambda s}\,e^{-\lambda(t - s)}}{\lambda te^{-\lambda t}} = \frac{s}{t}.
$$

The general case follows the same way from the joint density of $(S_1, \dots, S_n)$, which is constant on
$0 < s_1 < \dots < s_n < t$. Events of a Poisson process are scattered uniformly with no clustering and no
regularity.

## Thinning and superposition

**Thinning.** Mark each event independently as type 1 with probability $p$ and type 2 otherwise. The two marked
streams are independent Poisson processes with rates $\lambda p$ and $\lambda(1 - p)$. For the counts in $[0, t]$,

$$
\prob(N_1 = a, N_2 = b) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^{a+b}}{(a + b)!}\binom{a + b}{a}p^a(1 - p)^b =
\frac{e^{-\lambda pt}(\lambda pt)^a}{a!}\cdot\frac{e^{-\lambda(1-p)t}(\lambda(1 - p)t)^b}{b!}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">joint probability</NoteLink> factorises into two Poisson probabilities, so the counts are independent. Learning that many
type-2 events occurred says nothing about the number of type-1 events.

**Superposition.** The union of independent Poisson processes with rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ is a Poisson
process with rate $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2$. The counts add, and a sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson with
the summed mean. In the gap description, the first event of the union comes at the minimum of an $\Exp(\lambda_1)$
and an $\Exp(\lambda_2)$ time, which is $\Exp(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$. That event belongs to the first stream with
probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$.

## Inhomogeneous processes

When the rate varies in time, $\lambda(t)$, the counts satisfy $N(b) - N(a) \sim \Poisson\paren{\int_a^b
\lambda(u)\,du}$ with independent increments. Thinning simulates such a process: generate a homogeneous process with
rate $\lambda_{\max} \ge \lambda(t)$, and keep an event at time $t$ with probability $\lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}$. The
kept events form a process with rate $\lambda_{\max} \cdot \lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max} = \lambda(t)$.

## Worked example: a help desk

Calls arrive at a help desk as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda = 3$ per hour.

- **No call for 20 minutes.** The count in $1/3$ hour is $\Poisson(1)$, so the probability is $e^{-1} = 0.368$. The
  same number is $\prob(T_1 > 1/3) = e^{-3/3}$ from the gap description.
- **At least two calls in an hour.** $1 - e^{-3}(1 + 3) = 0.801$.
- **Urgent calls.** Each call is urgent with probability $1/3$, independently. Urgent calls form a Poisson process
  with rate 1 per hour, independent of the non-urgent ones. The chance of no urgent call in an hour is $e^{-1} = 0.368$,
  whatever the number of routine calls.
- **Given the total.** If 5 calls arrived in the hour, each fell in the first 20 minutes with probability $1/3$
  independently. The number in the first 20 minutes is $\Binom(5, 1/3)$, with mean $5/3$.

## In machine learning

- **Event data.** Logs of clicks, purchases or transactions are modelled as point processes. The Poisson process is
  the baseline; Hawkes processes add self-excitation, where each event raises the rate for a while.
- **Survival analysis.** A constant hazard rate is a Poisson process of failures; see
  <NoteLink to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival and hazard functions</NoteLink>.
- **Poisson regression** models counts per unit of exposure, the discrete footprint of a Poisson process with a rate
  that depends on covariates.

## Assumptions

- **Independence** of disjoint intervals. Arrivals that trigger further arrivals, such as retweets, violate it.
- **No simultaneous events.** Batches, such as a bus delivering several passengers at once, need a compound Poisson
  process.
- **Constant rate** in the homogeneous case. Daily and weekly cycles need an inhomogeneous rate.

## Pitfalls

- **The inspection paradox.** The gap containing a fixed time is longer on average than a typical gap: its mean is
  $2/\lambda$, not $1/\lambda$. Long gaps are more likely to contain the inspection time.
- **Overdispersion.** Real counts often have variance well above their mean. A Poisson process forces the two to be
  equal, so a mixture, such as the <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink>, fits better.
