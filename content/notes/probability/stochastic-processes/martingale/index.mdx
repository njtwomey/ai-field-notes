<Definition>
A **filtration** $\Fcal_0 \subseteq \Fcal_1 \subseteq \dots$ is an increasing sequence of $\sigma$-algebras; $\Fcal_n$
is the information available at time $n$. A sequence $M_0, M_1, \dots$ is a **martingale** with respect to it if, for
every $n$,

1. $M_n$ is determined by $\Fcal_n$ (it is **adapted**);
2. $\expect\abs{M_n} < \infty$;
3. $\expect[M_{n+1} \mid \Fcal_n] = M_n$.

With $\le$ in place of $=$ in condition 3 the sequence is a **supermartingale**, and with $\ge$ a
**submartingale** <Cite id="williams1991" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A martingale is the fortune of a gambler in a fair game. Whatever has happened so far, the expected fortune after the
next round equals the current fortune. A supermartingale is an unfavourable game, a submartingale a favourable one.
The increments $D_n = M_n - M_{n-1}$ are a **martingale difference sequence**: each has conditional mean 0 given the
past, but they need not be independent or identically distributed. That generality is why martingales appear
wherever information arrives sequentially.

Taking expectations of condition 3 and applying the tower property gives $\expect[M_n] = \expect[M_0]$ for every $n$.
The central question of the theory is whether this still holds at a random time chosen by looking at the process.

## Examples

- **Sums of independent zero-mean terms.** If $\xi_i$ are independent with $\expect[\xi_i] = 0$, then
  $S_n = \sum_{i \le n}\xi_i$ is a martingale with respect to $\Fcal_n = \sigma(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_n)$:
  $\expect[S_{n+1} \mid \Fcal_n] = S_n + \expect[\xi_{n+1}] = S_n$.
- **Squared sums minus variance.** If in addition $\var(\xi_i) = \sigma^2$, then $S_n^2 - n\sigma^2$ is a martingale:
  $\expect[S_{n+1}^2 \mid \Fcal_n] = S_n^2 + 2S_n\expect[\xi_{n+1}] + \sigma^2$.
- **Products of independent mean-one terms.** If $Y_i \ge 0$ are independent with $\expect[Y_i] = 1$, then
  $M_n = \prod_{i \le n}Y_i$ is a martingale. Likelihood ratios are the key case. If $X_i$ are drawn from $p_0$ and
  $Y_i = p_1(X_i)/p_0(X_i)$, then $\expect_{p_0}[Y_i] = \int p_1 = 1$, and the running likelihood ratio is a
  martingale under the null.
- **An exponential martingale for the random walk.** For the simple random walk with up-probability $p$ and
  $q = 1 - p$, the sequence $(q/p)^{S_n}$ is a martingale, because $\expect[(q/p)^{\xi}] = p(q/p) + q(p/q) = 1$.
- **Doob martingale.** For any integrable $Z$, $M_n = \expect[Z \mid \Fcal_n]$ is a martingale by the tower
  property. It reveals $Z$ gradually. This construction proves
  <NoteLink to="mcdiarmids-inequality">McDiarmid's inequality</NoteLink>.

## You cannot beat a fair game by choosing bets

A betting strategy chooses a stake $H_n$ for round $n$ using only information available before the round: $H_n$ is
determined by $\Fcal_{n-1}$ (it is **predictable**). The winnings are

$$
W_n = \sum_{k=1}^n H_k(M_k - M_{k-1}).
$$

If the $H_k$ are bounded, $W_n$ is a martingale: $\expect[H_{n+1}(M_{n+1} - M_n) \mid \Fcal_n] = H_{n+1}
\expect[M_{n+1} - M_n \mid \Fcal_n] = 0$, because $H_{n+1}$ is known at time $n$ and can be taken out of the
conditional expectation. No strategy for varying the stake turns a fair game into a favourable one.

## Stopping times and the optional stopping theorem

A **stopping time** is a random time $\tau$ with values in $\set{0, 1, 2, \dots} \cup \set{\infty}$ such that the
event $\set{\tau = n}$ is determined by $\Fcal_n$. The decision to stop at $n$ may use the past and present, not the
future. "The first time the fortune reaches 100" is a stopping time. "The time of the fortune's maximum" is not.

**Optional stopping theorem.** Let $M$ be a martingale and $\tau$ a stopping time. Then
$\expect[M_\tau] = \expect[M_0]$ if any of the following holds <Cite id="williams1991" />:

1. $\tau \le N$ for a constant $N$;
2. $\abs{M_{n \wedge \tau}} \le K$ for all $n$, for a constant $K$, and $\tau < \infty$ almost surely;
3. $\expect[\tau] < \infty$ and the increments are bounded, $\abs{M_n - M_{n-1}} \le K$.

For a supermartingale the conclusion is $\expect[M_\tau] \le \expect[M_0]$.

**Proof for a bounded stopping time.** Stopping is a betting strategy: bet one unit on every round up to $\tau$ and
nothing afterwards. With $H_k = \indicator\set{\tau \ge k}$,

$$
M_\tau = M_0 + \sum_{k=1}^N \indicator\set{\tau \ge k}(M_k - M_{k-1}).
$$

The event $\set{\tau \ge k}$ is the complement of $\set{\tau \le k - 1}$, which is determined by $\Fcal_{k-1}$. So each
$H_k$ is predictable, and each term has expectation $\expect\brack{H_k\,\expect[M_k - M_{k-1} \mid \Fcal_{k-1}]} = 0$.
Hence $\expect[M_\tau] = \expect[M_0]$. Conditions 2 and 3 follow by applying this to the bounded time $\tau \wedge n$
and letting $n \to \infty$, using dominated convergence to pass the limit through the expectation.

**The conditions matter.** Let $S_n$ be a simple symmetric random walk from 0 and $\tau$ the first time it hits 1.
The walk is recurrent, so $\tau < \infty$ almost surely, and $S_\tau = 1$. Then $\expect[S_\tau] = 1 \ne 0 =
\expect[S_0]$. None of the conditions holds: $\tau$ is unbounded, the stopped walk is unbounded below, and
$\expect[\tau] = \infty$. This is the doubling strategy in disguise. It "guarantees" a profit only for a gambler with
unlimited credit and unlimited time.

## Worked example: gambler's ruin by optional stopping

A gambler starts with $i$ units and bets one unit per round, winning with probability $p$, until reaching $N$ or 0.
Let $\tau$ be the stopping time and $w = \prob(S_\tau = N)$.

**Fair game.** $S_n$ is a martingale, and the stopped walk lies in $[0, N]$, so condition 2 applies:

$$
i = \expect[S_\tau] = Nw + 0 \cdot (1 - w) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w = \frac{i}{N}.
$$

The martingale $S_n^2 - n$ gives the duration. Optional stopping (with condition 3; $\expect[\tau] < \infty$ because
from any state the walk exits within $N$ steps with probability at least $2^{-N}$) gives
$\expect[S_\tau^2] - \expect[\tau] = i^2$. Since $\expect[S_\tau^2] = N^2w = Ni$,

$$
\expect[\tau] = Ni - i^2 = i(N - i).
$$

From $i = 10$ with $N = 20$, the game lasts 100 rounds on average.

**Unfair game.** With $r = q/p \ne 1$, the martingale $r^{S_n}$ is bounded between $\min(1, r^N)$ and
$\max(1, r^N)$ before stopping:

$$
r^i = \expect\brack{r^{S_\tau}} = wr^N + (1 - w) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w = \frac{1 - r^i}{1 - r^N}.
$$

With $p = 0.47$, $i = 10$ and $N = 20$, $w = 0.231$. The martingale $S_n - n(p - q)$ then gives
$\expect[\tau] = (i - Nw)/(q - p) = 89.6$ rounds. The same answers come from first-step analysis in the note on
<NoteLink to="gamblers-ruin">gambler's ruin</NoteLink>; the martingale route needs no difference equation.

## Doob's maximal inequality

**Theorem.** If $M$ is a non-negative submartingale and $c > 0$, then

$$
\prob\paren{\max_{k \le n}M_k \ge c} \le \frac{\expect[M_n]}{c}.
$$

Markov's inequality bounds $\prob(M_n \ge c)$ by the same quantity. Doob's inequality bounds the whole running maximum
at no extra cost.

**Proof.** Let $A_k$ be the event that $M_k \ge c$ and $M_j < c$ for all $j < k$: the first crossing happens at $k$.
The events $A_0, \dots, A_n$ are disjoint, their union is $\set{\max_{k \le n}M_k \ge c}$, and $A_k$ is determined by
$\Fcal_k$. The submartingale property gives $\expect[M_n \mid \Fcal_k] \ge M_k$, so

$$
\expect[M_n\indicator_{A_k}] = \expect\brack{\expect[M_n \mid \Fcal_k]\indicator_{A_k}} \ge
\expect[M_k\indicator_{A_k}] \ge c\,\prob(A_k).
$$

Summing over $k$ and using $M_n \ge 0$: $\expect[M_n] \ge \sum_k\expect[M_n\indicator_{A_k}] \ge
c\,\prob(\max_{k \le n}M_k \ge c)$.

**Kolmogorov's inequality.** If $S_n$ is a sum of independent zero-mean terms, $S_n^2$ is a non-negative
submartingale, by Jensen's inequality for conditional expectations. Doob's inequality applied to $S_n^2$ with
threshold $c^2$ gives $\prob(\max_{k \le n}\abs{S_k} \ge c) \le \var(S_n)/c^2$, the Chebyshev bound extended to the
whole path.

## Ville's inequality and anytime-valid inference

For a non-negative supermartingale with $M_0 = 1$, Doob's argument extends to the infinite horizon:

$$
\prob\paren{\sup_{n \ge 0}M_n \ge \frac1\alpha} \le \alpha.
$$

This is Ville's inequality. Apply it to a running likelihood ratio, which is a non-negative martingale under the null
with $M_0 = 1$. A test that rejects the null as soon as the likelihood ratio reaches $1/\alpha$ has false-positive rate
at most $\alpha$, however long the data are monitored and whenever the analyst decides to stop. This is the basis of
<NoteLink to="e-values-and-confidence-sequences">e-values and confidence sequences</NoteLink> and of the
<NoteLink to="msprt-and-always-valid-p-values">mSPRT</NoteLink> <Cite id="ramdas2023" />.

## Convergence

**Martingale convergence theorem.** A martingale with $\sup_n\expect\abs{M_n} < \infty$ converges almost surely to a
finite limit. In particular, every non-negative supermartingale converges almost surely <Cite id="williams1991" />.
The proof counts upcrossings of an interval $[a, b]$: a betting strategy that buys below $a$ and sells above $b$
cannot make money in expectation, which bounds the expected number of upcrossings. A path that failed to converge
would cross some interval with rational endpoints infinitely often.

The limit need not preserve the mean. A running likelihood ratio under the null is a non-negative martingale with mean
1 at every $n$, yet it converges to 0 almost surely when the alternative is false.

## In machine learning

- **Sequential testing.** Test martingales give A/B tests that remain valid under continuous monitoring, avoiding the
  inflation of false positives caused by <NoteLink to="peeking-and-optional-stopping">peeking</NoteLink>.
- **Concentration.** The Azuma–Hoeffding inequality bounds sums of bounded martingale differences, which gives
  generalisation bounds and the analysis of online learning.
- **Stochastic approximation.** The noise in a stochastic gradient, the difference between the sampled gradient and
  the full gradient, is a martingale difference sequence. Convergence proofs for stochastic gradient descent control
  its sum with martingale inequalities.

## Pitfalls

- **Unbounded stopping breaks fairness.** Optional stopping needs one of its conditions. Strategies that "cannot lose"
  rely on unbounded time or credit.
- **Stopping times cannot peek.** A rule that stops at the best point in hindsight is not a stopping time.
- **Almost sure convergence without $L^1$ convergence.** A martingale can converge to a limit with a different mean.
  Uniform integrability is the condition that rules this out.
