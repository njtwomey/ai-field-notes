<Definition>
The expectation of a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ is

$$
\expect[X] = \sum_x x\, p_X(x) \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad \expect[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\, f_X(x)\, dx
\quad \text{(continuous)},
$$

provided the sum or integral converges absolutely, that is $\expect\abs{X} < \infty$. Otherwise the expectation does
not exist <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Meaning

The expectation is the centre of mass of the distribution: place mass $p_X(x)$ at each point $x$, and $\expect[X]$ is
the balance point. For a fair die, $\expect[X] = (1 + 2 + \dots + 6)/6 = 3.5$. The expectation need not be a value that
$X$ can take.

The <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss> connects it to data. The average of $n$ independent draws of $X$ converges to $\expect[X]$ as
$n \to \infty$, whenever $\expect[X]$ exists.

## The law of the unconscious statistician

For a function $g$,

$$
\expect[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\, p_X(x) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \expect[g(X)] = \int g(x)\, f_X(x)\, dx.
$$

The distribution of $Y = g(X)$ is not needed. The name, abbreviated LOTUS, is a joke: the rule looks so natural that it
is often used without noticing that it is a theorem rather than the definition.

For a fair die, $\expect[X^2] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36)/6 = 91/6 \approx 15.17$. The same answer follows from the
distribution of $X^2$, which puts probability $1/6$ on each of $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36$; LOTUS skips that step. For
$X \sim \Unif(0, 1)$, $\expect[X^2] = \int_0^1 x^2\, dx = 1/3$, which agrees with integrating $y$ against the density
$1/(2\sqrt y)$ of $Y = X^2$.

The same rule holds for random vectors: $\expect[g(X, Y)] = \iint g(x, y)\, f_{X,Y}(x, y)\, dx\, dy$.

The rule is a regrouping of the sum that defines $\expect[Y]$. For discrete $X$, group the values $x$ by the value
$y = g(x)$ they produce. $P(Y = y)$ is the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">total probability</NoteLink> of the group, so

$$
\expect[Y] = \sum_y y\, P(Y = y) = \sum_y y \sum_{x : g(x) = y} p_X(x) = \sum_y \sum_{x : g(x) = y} g(x)\, p_X(x) =
\sum_x g(x)\, p_X(x).
$$

The third step replaces $y$ by $g(x)$, which is equal to it inside each group. The last step holds because every $x$
belongs to exactly one group. The continuous case is the same regrouping, written as an integral against the
push-forward measure of $X$ under $g$.

## Properties

- **Linearity.** $\expect[aX + bY + c] = a\expect[X] + b\expect[Y] + c$, for any random variables $X$ and $Y$ with
  finite expectations, **whether or not they are independent**.
- **Monotonicity.** If $X \le Y$ always, then $\expect[X] \le \expect[Y]$.
- **Products.** $\expect[XY] = \expect[X]\,\expect[Y]$ when $X$ and $Y$ are
  <NoteLink to="independence">independent</NoteLink>. Without independence the difference is the covariance.
- **Indicators.** For an event $A$, $\expect[\indicator\set{A}] = P(A)$. Probabilities are expectations.
- **Jensen's inequality.** For convex $\varphi$, $\varphi(\expect[X]) \le \expect[\varphi(X)]$. For example,
  $\paren{\expect[X]}^2 \le \expect[X^2]$, and $\expect[\log X] \le \log \expect[X]$ for positive $X$.

<Derivation title="Proofs of the properties">
**Linearity.** Apply LOTUS to the pair $(X, Y)$ with $g(x, y) = ax + by + c$, in the discrete case:

$$
\expect[aX + bY + c] = \sum_{x, y} (ax + by + c)\, p(x, y) = a \sum_x x \sum_y p(x, y) + b \sum_y y \sum_x p(x, y) + c.
$$

The inner sums are the marginals $p_X(x)$ and $p_Y(y)$, and the joint probabilities sum to 1. The result is
$a\expect[X] + b\expect[Y] + c$. The joint $p(x, y)$ was never factorised, so independence is not needed.

**Monotonicity.** $Y - X \ge 0$ always, so every term of $\expect[Y - X]$ is non-negative. By linearity,
$\expect[Y] - \expect[X] = \expect[Y - X] \ge 0$.

**Products.** Independence means $p(x, y) = p_X(x)\, p_Y(y)$. Then

$$
\expect[XY] = \sum_{x, y} xy\, p_X(x)\, p_Y(y) = \paren{\sum_x x\, p_X(x)} \paren{\sum_y y\, p_Y(y)} =
\expect[X]\, \expect[Y].
$$

**Indicators.** $\indicator\set{A}$ takes the value 1 with probability $P(A)$ and 0 otherwise, so its expectation is
$1 \cdot P(A) + 0 \cdot P(A^c) = P(A)$.

**Jensen's inequality.** A <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> lies above each of its <NoteLink to="derivative">tangent lines</NoteLink>. Let $\mu = \expect[X]$. There is a
slope $c$ with $\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(\mu) + c(x - \mu)$ for every $x$. Take expectations of both sides. By
monotonicity and linearity, $\expect[\varphi(X)] \ge \varphi(\mu) + c(\expect[X] - \mu) = \varphi(\mu)$.
</Derivation>

Linearity with indicators counts expected matches without any dependence calculation. Shuffle $n$ cards; the number of
cards in their original position is $\sum_i \indicator\set{\text{card } i \text{ fixed}}$, and each indicator has
expectation $1/n$, so the expected number of fixed points is $1$ for every $n$.

## Moments

The $k$-th moment is $\expect[X^k]$ and the $k$-th central moment is $\expect[(X - \expect[X])^k]$. The second central
moment is the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">variance</NoteLink>. A distribution need not have any moments:
the <NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution">Cauchy distribution</NoteLink> has no mean, because $\expect\abs{X} = \infty$.

## In machine learning

- **Risk.** Training minimises an expected loss $\expect_{(\xvec, y)}[\ell(f(\xvec), y)]$, estimated by the average
  loss on the training set.
- **Stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink>.** A mini-batch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient: its expectation over
  the random batch equals the gradient of the average loss.
- **Jensen's inequality** gives the <Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower bound</Gloss> used in <Gloss name="vi">variational inference</Gloss>: $\log \expect[w] \ge
  \expect[\log w]$ for a positive random weight $w$.

## Pitfalls

- **$\expect[g(X)] \ne g(\expect[X])$** in general. Equality holds for affine $g$. For convex $g$, Jensen's inequality
  gives the direction of the gap.
- **Heavy tails.** A sample mean always exists, even when $\expect[X]$ does not. For Cauchy data the sample mean does
  not settle down as $n$ grows.
