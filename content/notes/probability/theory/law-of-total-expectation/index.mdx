<Definition>
For <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $X$ and $Y$ with $\expect\abs{X} < \infty$,

$$
\expect[X] = \expect\brack{\expect[X \mid Y]}.
$$

If also $\expect[X^2] < \infty$,

$$
\var(X) = \expect\brack{\var(X \mid Y)} + \var\paren{\expect[X \mid Y]}.
$$

Here $\expect[X \mid Y]$ is the conditional <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>: the random variable
$g(Y)$, where $g(y) = \expect[X \mid Y =
y]$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Conditional expectation is a random variable

$\expect[X \mid Y = y]$ is a number: the mean of the <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional
distribution</NoteLink> of $X$ given $Y = y$. Letting $y$ vary defines a function $g(y)$, and $\expect[X \mid Y] = g(Y)$
is that function applied to the random $Y$. It is the best prediction of $X$ from $Y$ in <Gloss name="mse">mean squared
error</Gloss>: among all functions $h$, $\expect\brack{(X - h(Y))^2}$ is smallest for $h = g$.

The outer expectation in $\expect\brack{\expect[X \mid Y]}$ averages $g(Y)$ over the distribution of $Y$. For discrete
$Y$ this is $\sum_y \expect[X \mid Y = y]\, P(Y = y)$, a weighted average of group means with the group probabilities
as weights. It is the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> applied to means.

The first law is also called the tower rule or the law of iterated expectations. Blitzstein and Hwang name the two laws
Adam's law and Eve's law <Cite id="blitzstein2019" />.

## Worked example

A class has two groups. Group A has 30% of the students, with mean score 60 and variance 25. Group B has 70%, with mean
score 80 and variance 16. Let $Y$ be the group and $X$ the score of a random student.

The overall mean is $\expect[X] = 0.3 \times 60 + 0.7 \times 80 = 74$.

The expected within-group variance is $\expect[\var(X \mid Y)] = 0.3 \times 25 + 0.7 \times 16 = 18.7$.

The variance of the group means is $\var(\expect[X \mid Y]) = 0.3 \times (60 - 74)^2 + 0.7 \times (80 - 74)^2 = 58.8 +
25.2 = 84$.

The total variance is $18.7 + 84 = 102.7$. Most of the spread comes from the difference between groups, not from the
spread inside them.

## Derivation

For discrete $X$ and $Y$, write the conditional probabilities as joint over marginal and swap the order of summation:

$$
\expect\brack{\expect[X \mid Y]} = \sum_y P(Y = y) \sum_x x\, P(X = x \mid Y = y) = \sum_x x \sum_y P(X = x, Y = y) =
\sum_x x\, P(X = x) = \expect[X].
$$

For the variance, write $\var(X \mid Y) = \expect[X^2 \mid Y] - \paren{\expect[X \mid Y]}^2$ and take expectations,
using the first law on $X^2$:

$$
\expect\brack{\var(X \mid Y)} = \expect[X^2] - \expect\brack{\paren{\expect[X \mid Y]}^2}.
$$

The first law also gives $\var\paren{\expect[X \mid Y]} = \expect\brack{\paren{\expect[X \mid Y]}^2} -
\paren{\expect[X]}^2$. Adding the two lines gives $\expect[X^2] - \paren{\expect[X]}^2 = \var(X)$. The same argument
holds for continuous variables, with densities and integrals in place of probabilities and sums.

<Derivation title="Why the conditional mean is the best predictor">
Write $g(Y) = \expect[X \mid Y]$ and let $h$ be any function. Then

$$
\expect\brack{(X - h(Y))^2} = \expect\brack{(X - g(Y))^2} + 2\expect\brack{(X - g(Y))(g(Y) - h(Y))}
+ \expect\brack{(g(Y) - h(Y))^2}.
$$

The middle term is zero. Condition on $Y$: the factor $g(Y) - h(Y)$ is then a constant, and
$\expect[X - g(Y) \mid Y] = 0$ by definition of $g$. The tower rule turns this conditional zero into an unconditional
one. The last term is non-negative and is zero only when $h(Y) = g(Y)$ almost surely.
</Derivation>

## Uses

- **Two-stage models.** Let $N$ be a random count with mean $\mu_N$ and variance $\sigma_N^2$, and let the total
  $S = X_1 + \dots + X_N$ sum independent values with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, independent of $N$. Then
  $\expect[S] = \mu_N \mu$ and $\var(S) = \mu_N \sigma^2 + \mu^2 \sigma_N^2$.
- **Overdispersion.** If $X \mid \lambda \sim \Poisson(\lambda)$ and the rate $\lambda$ is itself random, then $\var(X)
  = \expect[\lambda] + \var(\lambda) > \expect[X]$. Counts from a varying rate are more spread than a Poisson count with
  the same mean; the <NoteLink to="negative-binomial-distribution">negative binomial</NoteLink> arises this way, with a
  gamma-distributed rate.
<Derivation title="Derivation of the random-sum and overdispersion formulas">
**Random sums.** Given $N = n$, the sum $S$ has mean $n\mu$ and variance $n\sigma^2$, because its $n$ terms are
independent. So $\expect[S \mid N] = N\mu$ and $\var(S \mid N) = N\sigma^2$. The two laws give

$$
\expect[S] = \expect[N\mu] = \mu_N \mu, \qquad \var(S) = \expect[N\sigma^2] + \var(N\mu) = \mu_N \sigma^2 +
\mu^2 \sigma_N^2.
$$

**Poisson mixtures.** A Poisson variable has equal mean and variance, so $\expect[X \mid \lambda] = \var(X \mid
\lambda) = \lambda$. The laws give $\expect[X] = \expect[\lambda]$ and $\var(X) = \expect[\lambda] + \var(\lambda)$.
</Derivation>

- **<Gloss name="anova">Analysis of variance</Gloss>.** Splitting the total sum of squares into within-group and
  between-group parts is the sample version of the law of total variance.

## In machine learning

- **Rao–Blackwellisation.** Replacing an estimator $\hat\theta$ by $\expect[\hat\theta \mid T]$, for a statistic $T$,
  keeps the mean and cannot increase the variance, because the law of total variance drops the non-negative term
  $\expect[\var(\hat\theta \mid T)]$. <NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo methods</NoteLink> use this to
  reduce <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> variance.
- **Uncertainty decomposition.** In an ensemble or Bayesian model, the predictive variance splits into the average
  variance each model predicts (aleatoric) and the disagreement between models' means (epistemic)
  <Cite id="kendall2017" />.

## Pitfalls

- **$\expect[X \mid Y]$ is not a number.** Treating it as a constant, instead of a function of $Y$, hides the second
  term in the variance law.
- **Both terms are needed.** Averaging within-group variances alone underestimates the total variance whenever group
  means differ.
