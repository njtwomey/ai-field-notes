<Definition>
A family of distributions is an exponential family if its densities (or pmfs) can be written

$$
p(x \mid \etavec) = h(x) \exp\paren{\etavec^\top T(x) - A(\etavec)},
$$

where $T(x)$ is the **sufficient statistic**, $\etavec$ is the **natural parameter**, $h(x) \ge 0$ is the **base
measure**, and the **log-partition function**

$$
A(\etavec) = \log \int h(x) \exp\paren{\etavec^\top T(x)}\, dx
$$

normalises the density. The natural parameter space is the set of $\etavec$ for which $A(\etavec)$ is finite
<Cite id="wainwright2008" />.
</Definition>

## Common members

| Family | $T(x)$ | Natural parameter $\eta$ | $A(\eta)$ | $h(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\Bern(p)$ | $x$ | $\log \frac{p}{1 - p}$ | $\log(1 + e^\eta)$ | $1$ |
| $\Poisson(\lambda)$ | $x$ | $\log \lambda$ | $e^\eta$ | $1/x!$ |
| $\Exp(\lambda)$ | $x$ | $-\lambda$ | $-\log(-\eta)$ | $1$ on $x \ge 0$ |
| $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $(x, x^2)$ | $\paren{\mu/\sigma^2, -1/(2\sigma^2)}$ | $-\frac{\eta_1^2}{4\eta_2} - \frac12 \log(-2\eta_2)$ | $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ |
| $\GammaD(\alpha, \beta)$ | $(\log x, x)$ | $(\alpha - 1, -\beta)$ | $\log \Gamma(\eta_1 + 1) - (\eta_1 + 1)\log(-\eta_2)$ | $1$ on $x > 0$ |
| $\Beta(\alpha, \beta)$ | $(\log x, \log(1 - x))$ | $(\alpha - 1, \beta - 1)$ | $\log B(\eta_1 + 1, \eta_2 + 1)$ | $1$ on $(0, 1)$ |

The binomial with fixed $n$, the categorical and multinomial, the geometric, the negative binomial with fixed $r$, the
Dirichlet, the Wishart and the multivariate Gaussian are also exponential families. Every family in the table is
written in its natural parameters; the familiar parameters are a reparameterisation.

**Example.** The Bernoulli pmf is $p^x (1 - p)^{1 - x} = \exp\paren{x \log\frac{p}{1 - p} + \log(1 - p)}$. So $T(x) =
x$, $\eta = \log\frac{p}{1 - p}$, the log-odds, and $A(\eta) = -\log(1 - p) = \log(1 + e^\eta)$. The inverse map,
$p = 1/(1 + e^{-\eta})$, is the logistic sigmoid.

## Moments from the log-partition function

Differentiating $A$ gives the moments of the sufficient statistic.

$$
\nabla A(\etavec) = \expect_{\etavec}[T(X)], \qquad \nabla^2 A(\etavec) = \cov_{\etavec}\brack{T(X)}.
$$

To derive them, write $A(\etavec) = \log Z(\etavec)$ with $Z(\etavec) = \int h(x) e^{\etavec^\top T(x)}\, dx$, and
differentiate under the integral sign:

$$
\nabla A(\etavec) = \frac{1}{Z(\etavec)} \int T(x)\, h(x) e^{\etavec^\top T(x)}\, dx = \int T(x)\, p(x \mid \etavec)\,
dx = \expect[T(X)].
$$

Differentiating $\nabla A(\etavec) = \int T(x)\, h(x) e^{\etavec^\top T(x) - A(\etavec)}\, dx$ once more gives

$$
\nabla^2 A(\etavec) = \int T(x) \paren{T(x) - \nabla A(\etavec)}^\top p(x \mid \etavec)\, dx = \expect\brack{T T^\top} -
\expect[T]\,\expect[T]^\top = \cov[T(X)].
$$

The second step differentiates the exponent, whose gradient is $T(x) - \nabla A(\etavec)$. Differentiation under the
integral is valid in the interior of the natural parameter space.

A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite, so $A$ is **convex**. For the Bernoulli, $A'(\eta) = 1/(1 + e^{-\eta}) =
p$ and $A''(\eta) = p(1 - p)$, the mean and variance. For the Poisson, $A'(\eta) = A''(\eta) = e^\eta = \lambda$, which
is why its mean and variance are equal.

## Maximum likelihood is moment matching

For independent observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\etavec) = \etavec^\top \sum_{i=1}^n T(x_i) - n A(\etavec) + \text{const}.
$$

It depends on the data only through $\sum_i T(x_i)$, which is therefore a <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient
statistic</NoteLink> whatever the sample size. $\ell$ is concave, because $A$ is convex, so any stationary point is the
global maximum. Setting the gradient to zero gives

$$
\expect_{\hat\etavec}[T(X)] = \nabla A(\hat\etavec) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n T(x_i).
$$

The maximum-likelihood fit is the member of the family whose expected sufficient statistic equals the sample average.
For the Gaussian, this matches the sample mean and the sample mean of squares.

## Conjugate priors

Every exponential family has a conjugate prior of the form $p(\etavec) \propto \exp\paren{\etavec^\top \tau - \nu
A(\etavec)}$. The posterior after $n$ observations has the same form, with $\tau \to \tau + \sum_i T(x_i)$ and $\nu \to
\nu + n$ <Cite id="diaconis1979" />. The prior acts like $\nu$ pseudo-observations whose sufficient statistics sum to
$\tau$. The <NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta</NoteLink> prior for a Bernoulli probability and the gamma prior for a
Poisson rate are examples.

<Derivation title="Why the prior is conjugate">
Multiply the prior by the likelihood of $n$ observations. Dropping factors that do not depend on $\etavec$,

$$
p(\etavec \mid x_{1:n}) \propto \exp\paren{\etavec^\top \tau - \nu A(\etavec)} \prod_{i=1}^n \exp\paren{\etavec^\top
T(x_i) - A(\etavec)} = \exp\paren{\etavec^\top \paren{\tau + \textstyle\sum_i T(x_i)} - (\nu + n) A(\etavec)}.
$$

This has the prior's form with updated $\tau$ and $\nu$. The base measures $h(x_i)$ do not involve $\etavec$ and are
absorbed into the normalising constant.
</Derivation>

## Maximum entropy

Among all distributions with a given expectation $\expect[T(X)] = \mvec$ (relative to base measure $h$), the one
with the largest entropy is the exponential-family member with that mean. The Gaussian is the maximum-entropy
distribution for a fixed mean and variance, and the exponential for a fixed mean on $[0, \infty)$
<Cite id="wainwright2008" />.

<Derivation title="Why the exponential family maximises entropy">
Measure entropy relative to $h$: $H(q) = -\int q(x) \log\paren{q(x)/h(x)}\, dx$. Let $p$ be the family member with
$\expect_p[T] = \mvec$ and $q$ any density with $\expect_q[T] = \mvec$. Non-negativity of the
<NoteLink to="kl-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink>, $\int q \log(q/p) \ge 0$, gives

$$
H(q) \le -\int q(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{h(x)}\, dx = -\int q(x) \paren{\etavec^\top T(x) - A(\etavec)} dx = -\etavec^\top
\mvec + A(\etavec).
$$

The right side depends on $q$ only through $\expect_q[T] = \mvec$, so it equals the same expression for $q = p$, which
is $H(p)$. Hence $H(q) \le H(p)$, with equality only when $\KL(q \,\|\, p) = 0$, that is $q = p$.
</Derivation>

## In machine learning

- **Generalised linear models** set the natural parameter to a linear function of the features,
  $\eta = \wvec^\top \xvec$ <Cite id="nelder1972" />. With a Bernoulli response this is
  <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>; with a Poisson response it is Poisson regression.
  The gradient of the log-likelihood is $\sum_i (T(y_i) - \nabla A(\eta_i))\,\xvec_i$, the residual times the input.
- **Softmax outputs.** The softmax layer of a classifier is $\nabla A$ for the categorical family, and cross-entropy
  loss is its negative log-likelihood.
- **Variational inference.** Mean-field updates and natural-gradient methods have closed forms when every conditional
  is in an exponential family.
- **Bregman divergences.** The KL divergence between two members equals a Bregman divergence of $A$ between their
  natural parameters <Cite id="banerjee2005" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Support must not depend on the parameter.** $\Unif(0, \theta)$ is not an exponential family: its support $[0,
  \theta]$ changes with $\theta$, which the product form $h(x)\exp(\cdot)$ cannot express. Its sufficient statistic is
  the maximum, not a sum.
- **Fixed shape parameters.** The binomial is an exponential family in $p$ only when $n$ is fixed, and the negative
  binomial only when $r$ is fixed. The Student $t$ and mixtures of exponential families are not exponential families.
- **Natural versus mean parameters.** The natural parameter $\etavec$ and the mean $\nabla A(\etavec)$ are different
  coordinates for the same distribution. Priors, step sizes and regularisation behave differently in each.
