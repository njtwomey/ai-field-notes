<Definition>
For events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}.
$$

For <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> with joint density $f_{X,Y}$, the conditional density of $X$ given $Y = y$ is
$f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = f_{X,Y}(x, y) / f_Y(y)$ wherever $f_Y(y) > 0$ <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Meaning

Conditioning on $B$ discards every outcome outside $B$ and rescales what is left so that it sums to 1. $B$ becomes the
new sample space. The ratio $P(A \cap B) / P(B)$ is the share of $B$'s probability that also lies in $A$.

For one roll of a fair die, let $A$ be "the roll is 6" and $B$ be "the roll is even". Then $P(A) = 1/6$, but
$P(A \mid B) = (1/6)/(1/2) = 1/3$. Knowing the roll is even leaves three equally likely faces.

For each fixed $B$, the map $A \mapsto P(A \mid B)$ satisfies the axioms of a probability measure. Every rule of
probability therefore holds conditionally: for example, $P(A^c \mid B) = 1 - P(A \mid B)$.

<Derivation title="Why conditioning gives a probability measure">
Check the three axioms for $Q(A) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$. Non-negativity holds because both probabilities are
non-negative. $Q(\Omega) = P(B)/P(B) = 1$. For disjoint events $A_1, A_2, \dots$, the sets $A_i \cap B$ are also
disjoint, so countable additivity of $P$ gives $Q\paren{\bigcup_i A_i} = \sum_i P(A_i \cap B)/P(B) = \sum_i
Q(A_i)$.
</Derivation>

## The chain rule

Multiplying the definition by $P(B)$ gives the **product rule**, $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\, P(B)$. Applying it
repeatedly gives the **chain rule** for any events with $P(A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}) > 0$. Write
$B_k = A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_k$. The product rule with $A = A_n$ and $B = B_{n-1}$ gives $P(B_n) = P(A_n \mid B_{n-1})
\, P(B_{n-1})$. Applying the same step to $P(B_{n-1})$, then to $P(B_{n-2})$, and so on down to $P(B_1) = P(A_1)$,
gives

$$
P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n) = P(A_1)\, P(A_2 \mid A_1)\, P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots
P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}).
$$

For densities the same factorisation reads

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}).
$$

Each factor conditions on everything before it. The order of the variables is arbitrary; every order gives a valid
factorisation.

**Example.** Draw three cards from a shuffled deck without replacement. The probability that all three are hearts is

$$
P(H_1 \cap H_2 \cap H_3) = \frac{13}{52} \cdot \frac{12}{51} \cdot \frac{11}{50} \approx 0.0129.
$$

Each factor accounts for the cards already removed.

## Conditional distributions

For discrete random variables, $p(x \mid y) = p(x, y)/p(y)$ is a pmf in $x$ for each fixed $y$. For continuous random
variables $P(Y = y) = 0$, so the event definition cannot be applied directly. The conditional density
$f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = f_{X,Y}(x, y)/f_Y(y)$ fills the gap. It is a slice of the joint density along $Y = y$,
rescaled to integrate to 1 in $x$.

The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> recombines conditionals into a
marginal, and <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> reverses the direction of conditioning.

## In machine learning

- **<Gloss name="ar">Autoregressive models</Gloss>.** A <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> applies the chain rule to a sequence of tokens,
  $p(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_t p(w_t \mid w_{<t})$, and learns each conditional with one network.
- **Discriminative models.** Classifiers such as <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>
  model $p(y \mid \xvec)$ directly, without modelling $p(\xvec)$.
- **Graphical models.** A <NoteLink to="bayesian-network">Bayesian network</NoteLink> chooses a chain-rule order and drops conditioning variables that do not
  matter, which is what makes large joint distributions tractable.

## Pitfalls

- **Direction matters.** $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$ are different numbers. Confusing them is the prosecutor's
  fallacy; Bayes' theorem converts one into the other.
- **Conditioning on probability-zero events.** $P(A \mid Y = y)$ for continuous $Y$ is defined through densities, and
  different limiting procedures can give different answers (the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox). The answer depends on which
  random variable the event is described by.
- **Conditioning is not causation.** $P(A \mid B) > P(A)$ says $B$ is evidence for $A$, not that $B$ causes $A$.
