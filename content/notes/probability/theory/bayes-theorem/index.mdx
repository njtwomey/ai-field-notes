<Definition>
For events $A$ and $B$ with $P(B) > 0$,

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^c)\,P(A^c).
$$

For random variables with densities, $p(x \mid y) = p(y \mid x)\, p(x) / p(y)$, with
$p(y) = \int p(y \mid x)\, p(x)\, dx$ <Cite id="bayes1763" />.
</Definition>

## Terms

| Term | Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prior | $P(A)$ | Belief in $A$ before seeing $B$. |
| Likelihood | $P(B \mid A)$ | How probable the evidence is if $A$ holds. |
| Evidence | $P(B)$ | Total probability of the evidence, under every alternative. |
| Posterior | $P(A \mid B)$ | Belief in $A$ after seeing $B$. |

In words: the posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior. The evidence only normalises it.

## Derivation

The product rule, from the definition of <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink>,
writes the joint probability two ways:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A).
$$

Dividing both sides by $P(B)$ gives Bayes' theorem. The expression for $P(B)$ is the
<NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> over the partition $\set{A, A^c}$. For
densities the same two steps apply to $p(x, y) = p(x \mid y)\,p(y) = p(y \mid x)\,p(x)$.

## The base-rate effect

A disease has prevalence $P(D) = 0.01$. A test has sensitivity $P(+ \mid D) = 0.99$ and false-positive rate
$P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.05$. The probability that a person who tests positive has the disease is

$$
P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} \approx 0.17.
$$

Most positives are false, because the healthy group is 99 times larger than the sick group. Ignoring the prior in this
way is the base-rate fallacy. The worked example
<NoteLink to="medical-diagnosis-base-rate">Medical diagnosis and the base rate</NoteLink> solves this problem in full,
with a simulation and variations.

## Odds form

Divide Bayes' theorem for $A$ by Bayes' theorem for $A^c$. The evidence cancels:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{P(A \mid B)}{P(A^c \mid B)}}_{\text{posterior odds}}
= \underbrace{\frac{P(B \mid A)}{P(B \mid A^c)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}
\times \underbrace{\frac{P(A)}{P(A^c)}}_{\text{prior odds}}
$$

Each piece of independent evidence multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio. In the example, the prior odds are
$1:99$ and a positive test has likelihood ratio $0.99/0.05 = 19.8$, giving posterior odds of about $1:5$.

## In machine learning

- **Bayesian inference** treats parameters $\theta$ as uncertain: $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \propto
  p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)$. The evidence $p(\mathcal{D})$ is usually intractable, which motivates
  sampling and variational methods.
- **Generative classifiers** such as naive Bayes model $p(\xvec \mid y)$ and $p(y)$, then classify with
  $p(y \mid \xvec)$.
- **Mixture models** use Bayes' theorem to compute each point's responsibility, the posterior probability of each
  component. See <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink>.
- **Prior shift.** A classifier trained on balanced classes but deployed where one class is rare must have its
  outputs reweighted by the new class priors, exactly as in the base-rate example.

## Pitfalls

- **Transposed conditionals.** $P(B \mid A)$ and $P(A \mid B)$ are different quantities. Treating one as the other is
  the prosecutor's fallacy.
- **Zero priors.** A hypothesis with prior probability zero keeps posterior probability zero, whatever the evidence.
- **Dependent evidence.** Multiplying likelihood ratios assumes the pieces of evidence are conditionally independent
  given the hypothesis.
