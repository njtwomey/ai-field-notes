<Definition>
**Markov's inequality.** If $X \ge 0$ and $a > 0$, then

$$
P(X \ge a) \le \frac{\expect[X]}{a}.
$$

**Chebyshev's inequality.** If $X$ has mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, then for every $k > 0$

$$
P\paren{\abs{X - \mu} \ge k\sigma} \le \frac{1}{k^2}.
$$

Both hold for every distribution that meets the conditions <Cite id="wasserman2004" />.
</Definition>

## Meaning

Markov's inequality says a non-negative quantity cannot often be much larger than its mean. If the average income is
40,000, at most a quarter of people earn 160,000 or more, whatever the distribution of incomes. Otherwise those people
alone would raise the average above 40,000.

Chebyshev's inequality says a random variable is rarely many standard deviations from its mean. At least 75% of any
distribution lies within 2 standard deviations of the mean, and at least 89% within 3. For a Gaussian the true figures
are about 95.4% and 99.7%.

The bounds use only the mean, or only the mean and variance. They must hold for the worst distribution with those
moments, so for any particular distribution they are usually far from tight.

## Proofs

**Markov.** For $X \ge 0$, the indicator of the tail satisfies $a\,\indicator\set{X \ge a} \le X$ pointwise: when
$X \ge a$ the left side is $a \le X$, and otherwise it is $0 \le X$. Taking expectations gives $a\,P(X \ge a) \le
\expect[X]$.

**Chebyshev.** Apply Markov's inequality to the non-negative variable $(X - \mu)^2$ with threshold $k^2\sigma^2$:

$$
P\paren{\abs{X - \mu} \ge k\sigma} = P\paren{(X - \mu)^2 \ge k^2\sigma^2} \le \frac{\expect\brack{(X - \mu)^2}}{k^2
\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{k^2}.
$$

The first equality holds because $\abs{X - \mu} \ge k\sigma$ and $(X - \mu)^2 \ge k^2\sigma^2$ are the same event.

## The bounds are attained

Neither bound can be improved using the same information. For Markov with $a \ge \expect[X] = m$, put probability
$m/a$ on the value $a$ and the rest on 0: then $P(X \ge a) = m/a$ exactly. For Chebyshev with $k \ge 1$, put
probability $1/(2k^2)$ on each of $\mu \pm k\sigma$ and the rest on $\mu$. Both extreme distributions pile all the tail
mass exactly at the threshold.

## Stronger bounds from more moments

Applying Markov's inequality to other functions of $X$ gives other bounds. With $g(X) = \abs{X - \mu}^p$ it uses the
$p$-th central moment. With $g(X) = e^{tX}$ for $t > 0$ it gives the **Chernoff bound**

$$
P(X \ge a) \le \min_{t > 0} e^{-ta}\, \expect\brack{e^{tX}}.
$$

The bound follows because $e^{tx}$ is increasing for $t > 0$: the events $X \ge a$ and $e^{tX} \ge e^{ta}$ are the same,
and Markov's inequality gives $P(e^{tX} \ge e^{ta}) \le e^{-ta}\expect[e^{tX}]$ for each $t$. The best $t$ gives the
tightest bound. It uses the whole <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">moment-generating function</NoteLink>. For
sums of independent bounded variables it decays exponentially in the number of terms, where Chebyshev decays only as
$1/n$. Hoeffding's and Bernstein's inequalities are bounds of this type <Cite id="boucheron2013" />.

## The weak law of large numbers

Let $\bar X_n$ be the mean of $n$ independent draws with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Then $\var(\bar X_n) =
\sigma^2/n$, and Chebyshev's inequality gives, for every $\epsilon > 0$,

$$
P\paren{\abs{\bar X_n - \mu} \ge \epsilon} \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} \to 0.
$$

This proves that the sample mean converges in probability to $\mu$.

## In machine learning

- **Generalisation bounds.** Bounds on the gap between training and test error are concentration inequalities for
  averages of losses. Hoeffding-type bounds give the familiar $\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}$ rates.
- **Sample-size estimates.** Chebyshev gives a distribution-free number of samples that guarantees a Monte Carlo
  estimate is within $\epsilon$ of its mean with probability $1 - \delta$: $n \ge \sigma^2/(\delta\epsilon^2)$.

## Pitfalls

- **Markov needs $X \ge 0$.** For variables that can be negative the inequality can fail.
- **Loose for specific distributions.** When the distribution is known, compute the tail directly. The bounds are for
  when it is not.
- **Chebyshev is vacuous for $k \le 1$.** The bound $1/k^2$ is then at least 1.
