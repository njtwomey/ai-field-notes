<Definition>
For distributions $p$ and $q$ on the same set, the cross-entropy of $q$ relative to $p$ is the expected surprisal
under $q$ of outcomes drawn from $p$:

$$
\entropy(p, q) = \expect_{X \sim p}\brack{-\log q(X)} = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x).
$$

The perplexity of $q$ on $p$ is $2^{\entropy(p, q)}$ with $\entropy$ in bits, or $e^{\entropy(p, q)}$ in nats
<Cite id="cover2006" />.
</Definition>

## Cross-entropy is entropy plus divergence

Add and subtract $\log p(x)$ inside the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>:

$$
\entropy(p, q) = \expect_p\brack{-\log p(X)} + \expect_p\brack{\log \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}} = \entropy(p) + \KL(p \,\|\,
q).
$$

The <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is non-negative and zero only when $q = p$, so
$\entropy(p, q) \ge \entropy(p)$ with equality exactly at $q = p$. The entropy $\entropy(p)$ is the unavoidable
surprisal of the data; the KL term is the extra surprisal caused by using the wrong model. In coding terms,
$\entropy(p, q)$ is the average code length when a code designed for $q$ encodes symbols that actually follow $p$ (see
the <NoteLink to="source-coding-theorem">source coding theorem</NoteLink>).

Cross-entropy is not symmetric: $\entropy(p, q) \ne \entropy(q, p)$ in general.

**Worked example.** Let $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)$ and let the model $q$ be uniform over the four
outcomes. Then $\entropy(p) = 1.75$ bits, and

$$
\entropy(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 \tfrac14 = 2 \text{ bits}, \qquad \KL(p \,\|\, q) = 2 - 1.75 = 0.25 \text{ bits}.
$$

Using the uniform model costs a quarter of a bit per outcome.

## Cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood

A classifier outputs a distribution $q_\theta(y \mid \xvec)$ over labels. Given training pairs $(\xvec_n, y_n)$, the
cross-entropy loss averages the surprisal of the observed labels:

$$
L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^N \log q_\theta(y_n \mid \xvec_n).
$$

This is the negative average log-likelihood, so minimising it is maximum likelihood. It is also a cross-entropy in the
sense of the definition: for each example, the "true" distribution is the one-hot vector that puts all its mass on the
observed label $y_n$, and $-\sum_y \indicator[y = y_n] \log q_\theta(y \mid \xvec_n) = -\log q_\theta(y_n \mid
\xvec_n)$.

Without inputs, the same argument shows that minimising cross-entropy fits the model to the empirical distribution
$\hat p(y) = \tfrac1N \sum_n \indicator[y_n = y]$:

$$
L(\theta) = -\sum_y \hat p(y) \log q_\theta(y) = \entropy(\hat p, q_\theta) = \entropy(\hat p) + \KL(\hat p \,\|\,
q_\theta).
$$

$\entropy(\hat p)$ does not depend on $\theta$, so minimising the loss minimises the KL divergence from the data to the
model. Maximum likelihood, cross-entropy minimisation and forward-KL minimisation are the same procedure.

## Binary cross-entropy

For a binary label $y \in \set{0, 1}$ and a predicted probability $\hat y = q(y = 1)$, the cross-entropy is

$$
-\brack{y \log \hat y + (1 - y) \log(1 - \hat y)},
$$

the loss of <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>. With $\hat y = \sigma(z)$ for a logit
$z$, its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is $\partial L / \partial z = \hat y - y$, and for $K$ classes with a softmax the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with
respect to the logits is $\qvec - \yvec$; see <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax and
log-sum-exp</NoteLink>. The gradient is the prediction error, which is why cross-entropy trains well with sigmoid and
softmax outputs.

## Perplexity

Perplexity turns a cross-entropy into an effective number of choices. A model with perplexity $k$ is, on average, as
uncertain about the next outcome as a uniform choice among $k$ options.

- A uniform model over $V$ outcomes has cross-entropy $\log_2 V$ bits against any $p$, so its perplexity is $V$.
- In the worked example, the true distribution has perplexity $2^{1.75} \approx 3.36$ and the uniform model $2^2 = 4$.
- A <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> with cross-entropy 3 bits per token on held-out text has perplexity $2^3 = 8$: it is as uncertain
  as if it chose uniformly among 8 tokens at each step.

Perplexity is only comparable between models that share a tokenisation, because the same text split into more tokens
has a different per-token cross-entropy.

## Label smoothing

Label smoothing replaces the one-hot target by $(1 - \epsilon)$ on the observed label plus $\epsilon/K$ on every class.
The loss becomes $\entropy(\tilde p, q)$ with the smoothed target $\tilde p$, whose minimiser is $q = \tilde p$ rather
than a one-hot vector. This stops the logits growing without bound and tends to improve calibration.

## Pitfalls

- **Zero probabilities.** $\entropy(p, q) = \infty$ if $q$ assigns probability 0 to an outcome that $p$ can produce.
  Implementations clip or work with logits and log-softmax for <NoteLink to="numerical-stability-and-conditioning">numerical stability</NoteLink>.
- **Units and averaging.** Report whether a loss is in nats or bits, and whether it is summed or averaged over tokens
  or examples; perplexity depends on both.
- **Lower bound.** Training cross-entropy cannot fall below the entropy of the labels given the inputs. Label noise sets
  a floor on the achievable loss.
