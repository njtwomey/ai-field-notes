<Definition>
For a discrete <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ with <Gloss name="pmf">probability mass
function</Gloss> $p$, the surprisal of an outcome $x$ is $-\log p(x)$, and the entropy is its
<NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink>:

$$
\entropy(X) = \expect\brack{-\log p(X)} = -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x),
$$

with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$ <Cite id="shannon1948" />. With $\log_2$ it is measured in bits; with the
<NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">natural logarithm</NoteLink>, in nats. One nat is $1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443$ bits.
</Definition>

## Surprisal

An observation that was certain to happen tells us nothing. A rare one tells us a lot. The surprisal
$s(x) = -\log p(x)$ is the measure of information that follows from three requirements:

- it depends only on the probability of the outcome, and decreases as the probability rises;
- a certain outcome, $p(x) = 1$, carries no information;
- the information in two independent outcomes adds: $s(x, y) = s(x) + s(y)$ when $p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y)$.

The last requirement says $s(pq) = s(p) + s(q)$. Among continuous decreasing functions, only $s(p) = -c \log p$ with
$c > 0$ turns products into sums, and the constant $c$ only fixes the unit. The choice $c = 1$ with $\log_2$ makes a
fair <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flip</NoteLink> worth exactly one bit.

Entropy is the average surprisal: how much information one observation of $X$ reveals on average, or, equivalently,
how uncertain $X$ is before it is observed.

## Worked examples

- **Fair coin.** $\entropy = -2 \times \tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac12 = 1$ bit.
- **Biased coin**, $P(\text{heads}) = 0.9$: $\entropy = -0.9 \log_2 0.9 - 0.1 \log_2 0.1 \approx 0.469$ bits. The
  outcome is usually heads, so on average an observation is less informative. At $p = 0.99$ it is $0.081$ bits.
- **Fair die.** Six equally likely faces give $\entropy = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits.
- **English letters.** Twenty-six equally likely letters would give $\log_2 26 \approx 4.70$ bits. Real letter
  frequencies give about $4.14$ bits per letter, and using the context of the preceding letters lowers the figure much
  further <Cite id="shannon1951" />.

## Bounds

$\entropy(X) \ge 0$, because each term $-p(x)\log p(x)$ is non-negative for $0 \le p(x) \le 1$. It is zero exactly
when one outcome has probability 1.

For $X$ with $K$ possible outcomes, $\entropy(X) \le \log K$, with equality exactly when $X$ is uniform. To see this,
compare $p$ with the uniform distribution $u(x) = 1/K$ using the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence</NoteLink>:

$$
\KL(p \,\|\, u) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{1/K} = \log K \sum_x p(x) + \sum_x p(x) \log p(x) = \log K -
\entropy(X).
$$

KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when $p = u$, so $\entropy(X) \le \log K$ with equality only for the uniform
distribution. The <NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-distributions">maximum-entropy principle</NoteLink> extends this result
to constraints.

$2^{\entropy}$, in bits, is the **effective number of outcomes**: a variable with entropy $\entropy$ is as uncertain as
a uniform choice among $2^{\entropy}$ values. The biased coin above behaves like a uniform choice among
$2^{0.469} \approx 1.38$ outcomes.

## Concavity

Entropy is a concave function of the distribution: mixing two distributions never lowers the entropy below the average
of their entropies,

$$
\entropy\paren{\lambda p + (1 - \lambda) q} \ge \lambda \entropy(p) + (1 - \lambda) \entropy(q).
$$

<Derivation title="Proof of concavity">
The function $f(t) = -t \log t$ has $f''(t) = -1/t < 0$, so it is concave on $(0, 1]$. Entropy is a sum of $f$ applied
to each coordinate, $\entropy(p) = \sum_x f(p(x))$, and a sum of concave functions of the coordinates is concave. So for
each $x$, $f\paren{\lambda p(x) + (1 - \lambda) q(x)} \ge \lambda f(p(x)) + (1 - \lambda) f(q(x))$, and summing over
$x$ gives the inequality.
</Derivation>

Concavity is why the coin's entropy curve is a dome with its peak at $p = 1/2$.

## Characterisation and operational meaning

Shannon characterised entropy by three properties of a measure $\entropy(p_1, \dots, p_K)$ of uncertainty
<Cite id="shannon1948" />:

1. it is continuous in the probabilities;
2. for uniform distributions it increases with the number of outcomes $K$;
3. **grouping**: splitting a choice into stages leaves the total unchanged. Choosing among three outcomes with
   probabilities $(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16)$ equals first choosing between two groups with probabilities
   $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, then, half the time, choosing within the second group with probabilities
   $(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)$:
   $\entropy(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16) = \entropy(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) + \tfrac12 \entropy(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)$.

The only functions with these properties are $-c \sum_k p_k \log p_k$.

Entropy has an operational meaning: by the <NoteLink to="source-coding-theorem">source coding theorem</NoteLink>,
$\entropy(X)$ bits is the smallest average number of bits per symbol with which samples of $X$ can be encoded without
loss <Cite id="cover2006" />.

## In machine learning

- **Cross-entropy loss** compares a model's predicted distribution with the observed labels; see
  <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy and perplexity</NoteLink>.
- **<NoteLink to="decision-tree">Decision trees</NoteLink>** split on the feature that most reduces the entropy of the
  labels, which is the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink> between feature and label.
- **Entropy regularisation** adds $\entropy$ of a policy or a predictive distribution to an objective to keep it from
  collapsing onto one action or class too early.
- **Active learning and exploration** favour inputs whose predictions have high entropy, where the model is most
  uncertain.

## Pitfalls

- **Units.** Bits and nats differ by a factor of $\ln 2$. Library losses use nats.
- **Entropy of a distribution, not of data.** Plugging observed frequencies into the formula underestimates the true
  entropy, badly so when there are many outcomes and few samples.
- **Continuous variables.** Replacing the sum with an integral gives the
  <NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>, which behaves differently: it can be negative
  and depends on the units of $X$.
