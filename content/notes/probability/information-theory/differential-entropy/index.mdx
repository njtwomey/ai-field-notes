<Definition>
For a continuous random variable $X$ with density $p$, the differential entropy is

$$
h(X) = \expect\brack{-\log p(X)} = -\int p(x) \log p(x)\, dx,
$$

with the integral over the support of $p$ <Cite id="cover2006" />. Like entropy, it is in nats with the natural
logarithm and in bits with $\log_2$.
</Definition>

## Not the limit of discrete entropy

Differential entropy is not what discrete entropy tends to as the variable becomes continuous. Quantise $X$ into bins
of width $\Delta$, and let $X_\Delta$ record the bin. For small $\Delta$, the probability of bin $i$ is approximately
$p(x_i)\,\Delta$, so

$$
\entropy(X_\Delta) \approx -\sum_i p(x_i)\Delta \log\paren{p(x_i)\Delta} = -\sum_i p(x_i)\Delta \log p(x_i) - \log
\Delta \sum_i p(x_i)\Delta \approx h(X) - \log \Delta.
$$

As $\Delta \to 0$ the $-\log\Delta$ term grows without bound: a continuous variable has infinite entropy, because
specifying a real number exactly takes infinitely many bits. $h(X)$ is what remains after subtracting that divergent
term. In bits, it says that describing $X$ to $n$ binary places takes about $h(X) + n$ bits.

## It can be negative

For $X$ uniform on $[0, a]$, the density is $1/a$ and

$$
h(X) = -\int_0^a \tfrac1a \log \tfrac1a\, dx = \log a.
$$

For $a = \tfrac12$, $h(X) = \log_2 \tfrac12 = -1$ bit. A density can exceed 1, and where it does its logarithm is
positive. Negative differential entropy means the variable is more concentrated than a uniform variable on an interval
of length 1; it does not mean negative information.

## It depends on scale

Rescaling $X$ shifts its differential entropy. For $Y = aX$ with $a \neq 0$, the
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink> gives $p_Y(y) = p_X(y/a)/\abs{a}$, so

$$
h(Y) = -\expect\brack{\log p_X(X) - \log \abs{a}} = h(X) + \log\abs{a}.
$$

Measuring the same quantity in millimetres instead of metres adds $\log_2 1000 \approx 9.97$ bits. In $d$ dimensions,
$h(\Amat\xvec + \bvec) = h(\xvec) + \log\abs{\det\Amat}$ for invertible $\Amat$. Differential entropy is therefore not
an absolute measure of uncertainty. Only differences between variables in the same units are meaningful.

## The Gaussian

For $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$,

$$
-\log p(x) = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
$$

Its expectation uses $\expect[(X - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$:

$$
h(X) = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \tfrac12 = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \sigma^2).
$$

For $\sigma = 1$ this is $1.419$ nats, or $2.047$ bits. For a $d$-dimensional
<NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> with covariance $\Sigmamat$, the same
steps with $\expect\brack{(\xvec - \muvec)^\top\Sigmamat^{-1}(\xvec - \muvec)} = d$ give

$$
h(\xvec) = \tfrac12 \log\paren{(2\pi)^d \det\Sigmamat} + \tfrac d2 = \tfrac12 \log \det\paren{2\pi e \Sigmamat}.
$$

The entropy grows with the log-determinant of the covariance, the log of the volume of the distribution's ellipsoid.

**The Gaussian is the most uncertain distribution with a given variance.** Among all densities on $\reals$ with
variance $\sigma^2$, the Gaussian has the largest differential entropy; see
<NoteLink to="maximum-entropy-distributions">maximum-entropy distributions</NoteLink>. A uniform distribution with the
same standard deviation has width $\sigma\sqrt{12}$ and entropy $\log_2(\sigma\sqrt{12})$, about $0.25$ bits less.

## What keeps its meaning

The quantities built from ratios of densities are unaffected by the scale problem, because Jacobians cancel in the
ratio:

- **KL divergence.** Under an invertible map $y = g(x)$, both densities pick up the same factor
  $\abs{\det J_g}^{-1}$, which cancels in $p(y)/q(y)$. So $\KL(p \,\|\, q)$ is the same in every coordinate system and
  is non-negative, as in the discrete case.
- **Mutual information.** $I(X; Y) = h(X) + h(Y) - h(X, Y)$, and rescaling $X$ adds $\log\abs{a}$ to both $h(X)$ and
  $h(X, Y)$, which cancel. Mutual information between continuous variables is non-negative and scale-invariant; see
  <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing across units.** A differential entropy is meaningful only relative to another in the same units.
- **Degenerate distributions.** A distribution concentrated on a lower-dimensional set, such as a line in the plane,
  has differential entropy $-\infty$ in the higher dimension.
- **Estimation.** Estimating $h$ from samples requires density estimation, and nearest-neighbour estimators are biased
  in high dimensions.
