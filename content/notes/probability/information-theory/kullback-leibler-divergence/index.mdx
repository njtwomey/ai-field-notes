<Definition>
For distributions $p$ and $q$ on the same space, the Kullback–Leibler divergence of $q$ from $p$ is

$$
\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \expect_{X \sim p}\brack{\log \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}} = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \quad
\text{or} \quad \int p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\, dx,
$$

with the conventions $0 \log(0/q) = 0$ and $p \log(p/0) = \infty$ for $p > 0$ <Cite id="kullback1951" />. With the
<NoteLink to="exponential-and-logarithm">natural logarithm</NoteLink> it is measured in nats; with $\log_2$, in bits.
</Definition>

## Meaning

$\log p(x) - \log q(x)$ is how much more probable $x$ is under $p$ than under $q$. The KL divergence averages this over
draws from $p$. If $p$ generates the data and $q$ is a model, $\KL(p \,\|\, q)$ is the expected extra log-loss per
observation from predicting with $q$ instead of $p$. In coding terms it is the expected number of extra bits needed to
encode samples from $p$ with a code designed for $q$ <Cite id="cover2006" />; see the
<NoteLink to="source-coding-theorem">source coding theorem</NoteLink>.

It splits into <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy</NoteLink> minus
<NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink>:

$$
\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \underbrace{-\expect_p[\log q(X)]}_{\text{cross-entropy } H(p, q)} -
\underbrace{\paren{-\expect_p[\log p(X)]}}_{\text{entropy } \entropy(p)}.
$$

## Properties

- **Non-negative.** $\KL(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0$, with equality exactly when $p = q$ (almost everywhere). This is Gibbs'
  inequality.
- **Not symmetric.** $\KL(p \,\|\, q) \ne \KL(q \,\|\, p)$ in general, and the triangle inequality fails. KL is a
  divergence, not a distance.
- **Infinite under support mismatch.** If $q(x) = 0$ where $p(x) > 0$, then $\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \infty$.
- **Invariant to reparameterisation.** Applying the same invertible transformation to both distributions leaves the
  divergence unchanged, unlike <NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropy</NoteLink>.
- **Measures dependence.** The KL divergence from the product of the marginals to a joint distribution,
  $\KL\paren{p(x, y) \,\|\, p(x)\,p(y)}$, is the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink>
  between the two variables.
- **Additive for independent parts** and, more generally, satisfies a <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>:
  $\KL\paren{p(x, y) \,\|\, q(x, y)} = \KL\paren{p(x) \,\|\, q(x)} + \expect_{p(x)}\brack{\KL\paren{p(y \mid x) \,\|\,
  q(y \mid x)}}$.

### Why KL is non-negative

The logarithm is concave, so <NoteLink to="expectation">Jensen's inequality</NoteLink>, $\expect[\log Y] \le \log \expect[Y]$, applies with
$Y = q(X)/p(X)$ and $X \sim p$:

$$
-\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \expect_p\brack{\log \frac{q(X)}{p(X)}} \le \log \expect_p\brack{\frac{q(X)}{p(X)}} = \log
\sum_{x : p(x) > 0} q(x) \le \log 1 = 0.
$$

Equality in Jensen's inequality for a strictly concave function requires $q(X)/p(X)$ to be constant on the support of
$p$, and the second inequality requires $q$ to put no mass outside it. Together these force $q = p$.

<Derivation title="Proof of the chain rule">
Factor both joints as $p(x, y) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)$ and $q(x, y) = q(x)\, q(y \mid x)$. The log-ratio splits into a
sum:

$$
\KL\paren{p(x, y) \,\|\, q(x, y)} = \expect_{p(x, y)}\brack{\log \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}} + \expect_{p(x, y)}\brack{\log
\frac{p(Y \mid X)}{q(Y \mid X)}}.
$$

The first term depends only on $X$, so it is $\KL(p(x) \,\|\, q(x))$. In the second, average over $Y$ given $X$ first:
the inner average is $\KL\paren{p(y \mid x) \,\|\, q(y \mid x)}$, which is then averaged over $p(x)$. For independent
parts the conditionals do not depend on $x$, and the chain rule reduces to a sum.
</Derivation>

## Examples

**Two coins.** Let $p = \Bern(0.5)$ and $q = \Bern(0.9)$. Then

$$
\KL(p \,\|\, q) = 0.5 \log \tfrac{0.5}{0.9} + 0.5 \log \tfrac{0.5}{0.1} \approx 0.511, \qquad
\KL(q \,\|\, p) = 0.9 \log \tfrac{0.9}{0.5} + 0.1 \log \tfrac{0.1}{0.5} \approx 0.368
$$

nats. Using the biased coin $q$ to model the fair coin $p$ costs more than the reverse, because $q$ gives the tails of a
fair coin only probability $0.1$.

**Two Gaussians.** For $p = \Gauss(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $q = \Gauss(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$,

$$
\KL(p \,\|\, q) = \log \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac12.
$$

<Derivation title="Derivation of the Gaussian formula">
The log-ratio of the densities is

$$
\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \log \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} - \frac{(x - \mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(x -
\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}.
$$

Take its expectation under $p$. $\expect_p[(X - \mu_1)^2] = \sigma_1^2$, and writing $X - \mu_2 = (X - \mu_1) +
(\mu_1 - \mu_2)$ gives $\expect_p[(X - \mu_2)^2] = \sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2$, because the cross term has mean
zero. Substituting gives $\log(\sigma_2/\sigma_1) - \frac12 + \paren{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2}/(2\sigma_2^2)$.
</Derivation>

A shift of the mean costs $(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2/(2\sigma_2^2)$, so it is measured in units of the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of
$q$. A $q$ narrower than $p$ is penalised much more than a $q$ wider than $p$.

## Forward and reverse KL

Fitting an approximation $q$ to a fixed target $p$ by minimising KL gives different answers in the two directions
<Cite id="bishop2006" />.

- **Forward, $\KL(p \,\|\, q)$.** The expectation is over $p$, and $q(x) \to 0$ where $p(x) > 0$ is heavily
  penalised. The best $q$ spreads to cover all the mass of $p$: it is **mass-covering**. For a Gaussian $q$ the optimum
  matches the mean and variance of $p$. Only the cross-entropy term depends on $q$, and for $q = \Gauss(m, s^2)$ it is
  $\log s + \expect_p[(X - m)^2]/(2s^2) + \text{const}$. Setting its derivatives to zero gives $m = \expect_p[X]$ and
  $s^2 = \var_p(X)$.
- **Reverse, $\KL(q \,\|\, p)$.** The expectation is over $q$, and $q$ is penalised for putting mass where $p$ is
  small, but not for missing regions where $p$ is large. The best $q$ concentrates on one mode of $p$: it is
  **mode-seeking** <Cite id="minka2005" />.

## In machine learning

- **Maximum likelihood.** $\KL(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta) = -\expect_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(X)] +
  \text{const}$. Maximising the average log-likelihood minimises the forward KL from the data distribution to the
  model. Cross-entropy loss for classification is the same quantity.
- **Variational inference** approximates an intractable posterior $p(\zvec \mid \xvec)$ by the $q$ that minimises the
  reverse KL, which avoids expectations under $p$. The identity $\log p(\xvec) = \text{ELBO}(q) + \KL\paren{q(\zvec)
  \,\|\, p(\zvec \mid \xvec)}$ turns this into maximising the <Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower bound</Gloss>. The identity follows by writing
  $\log p(\xvec) = \expect_q\brack{\log \frac{p(\xvec, \zvec)}{q(\zvec)}} + \expect_q\brack{\log \frac{q(\zvec)}{p(\zvec
  \mid \xvec)}}$, which holds because the two log-ratios add to $\log p(\xvec)$ for every $\zvec$. Mode-seeking is why
  variational posteriors tend to be overconfident.
- **Variational autoencoders** add $\KL\paren{q(\zvec \mid \xvec) \,\|\, p(\zvec)}$ to the loss, using the closed form
  for Gaussians <Cite id="kingma2014" />. The <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation
  trick</NoteLink> gives low-variance <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> of the rest of the loss.
- **Regularising a policy or model** towards a reference, as in <Gloss name="rlhf">reinforcement learning from human feedback</Gloss> and in
  <NoteLink to="knowledge-distillation">knowledge distillation</NoteLink>, uses a KL penalty.
- **Expectation–maximisation** alternates between making a KL term zero and maximising the rest of the bound; see
  <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Direction.** "The KL between $p$ and $q$" is ambiguous. State which distribution the expectation is over.
- **Support.** A model that assigns zero probability to an observed value has infinite forward KL. Smoothing, or
  distributions with full support, avoid this.
- **Estimating KL from samples** is hard in high dimensions. Plug-in estimates with density estimates are biased, and a
  few samples where $q$ is tiny dominate the average.
- **Not a metric.** Small KL in one direction does not imply small KL in the other, and KL values cannot be combined
  with the triangle inequality. KL is one member of the family of
  <NoteLink to="f-divergences-and-jensen-shannon">f-divergences</NoteLink>, which includes symmetric and bounded
  alternatives such as the Jensen–Shannon divergence.
