<Definition>
For discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ with joint mass function $p(x, y)$, the joint entropy is the entropy of the
pair,

$$
\entropy(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log p(x, y),
$$

and the conditional entropy is the entropy of $Y$ given $X = x$, averaged over $x$:

$$
\entropy(Y \mid X) = \sum_x p(x)\, \entropy(Y \mid X = x) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log p(y \mid x).
$$

Both are measured in the same units as entropy <Cite id="cover2006" />.
</Definition>

## Chain rule

The joint probability factors as $p(x, y) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)$, so its logarithm splits into a sum. Take minus the
expectation of both sides over $p(x, y)$:

$$
-\expect\brack{\log p(X, Y)} = -\expect\brack{\log p(X)} - \expect\brack{\log p(Y \mid X)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\entropy(X, Y) = \entropy(X) + \entropy(Y \mid X).
$$

The uncertainty of the pair is the uncertainty of $X$, plus the uncertainty that remains in $Y$ once $X$ is known.
Factoring the other way gives $\entropy(X, Y) = \entropy(Y) + \entropy(X \mid Y)$.

The same argument applied to $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$ gives the general chain
rule,

$$
\entropy(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \entropy(X_i \mid X_1, \dots, X_{i-1}).
$$

For independent variables every conditional entropy equals the plain entropy, and the joint entropy is the sum
$\sum_i \entropy(X_i)$.

## Worked example

Take the joint distribution

| | $Y = 0$ | $Y = 1$ | $p(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $X = 0$ | $1/2$ | $1/4$ | $3/4$ |
| $X = 1$ | $0$ | $1/4$ | $1/4$ |
| $p(y)$ | $1/2$ | $1/2$ | |

In bits:

- $\entropy(X) = \entropy(\tfrac34, \tfrac14) \approx 0.811$ and $\entropy(Y) = \entropy(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = 1$.
- $\entropy(X, Y) = -\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - 2 \times \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 = 1.5$.
- Given $X = 0$, $Y$ is $0$ with probability $2/3$, so $\entropy(Y \mid X = 0) = \entropy(\tfrac23, \tfrac13) \approx
  0.918$. Given $X = 1$, $Y = 1$ for certain, so $\entropy(Y \mid X = 1) = 0$. Averaging with weights $\tfrac34$ and
  $\tfrac14$ gives $\entropy(Y \mid X) \approx 0.689$.

The chain rule checks: $\entropy(X) + \entropy(Y \mid X) \approx 0.811 + 0.689 = 1.5 = \entropy(X, Y)$. The other
order gives $\entropy(X \mid Y) = 1.5 - 1 = 0.5$.

## Conditioning reduces entropy

On average, learning $X$ cannot make $Y$ more uncertain:

$$
\entropy(Y \mid X) \le \entropy(Y),
$$

with equality exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent. The difference
$\entropy(Y) - \entropy(Y \mid X)$ is the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual information</NoteLink>
$I(X; Y)$, and it equals the KL divergence between $p(x, y)$ and $p(x)\,p(y)$:

$$
\entropy(Y) - \entropy(Y \mid X) = \expect\brack{\log \frac{p(Y \mid X)}{p(Y)}} = \expect\brack{\log \frac{p(X,
Y)}{p(X)\, p(Y)}} = \KL\paren{p(x, y) \,\|\, p(x)\, p(y)} \ge 0.
$$

<NoteLink to="kl-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is non-negative and zero only when the two distributions agree,
which here means $p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y)$, independence.

The inequality holds on average only. A particular observation can increase uncertainty: in the example,
$\entropy(Y) = 1$ bit, and $\entropy(Y \mid X = 0) \approx 0.918$ is lower, but a different table can make
$\entropy(Y \mid X = x)$ larger than $\entropy(Y)$ for some $x$. Averaged over $x$, it cannot.

## Consequences

- **Subadditivity.** $\entropy(X, Y) = \entropy(X) + \entropy(Y \mid X) \le \entropy(X) + \entropy(Y)$. The joint
  uncertainty is at most the sum of the parts.
- **Functions lose information.** For any function $g$, $\entropy(g(X)) \le \entropy(X)$. Apply the chain rule to the
  pair $(X, g(X))$ in both orders: $\entropy(X) + \entropy(g(X) \mid X) = \entropy(g(X)) + \entropy(X \mid g(X))$. The
  first conditional term is 0, because $g(X)$ is determined by $X$, so
  $\entropy(g(X)) = \entropy(X) - \entropy(X \mid g(X)) \le \entropy(X)$.
- **Zero conditional entropy.** $\entropy(Y \mid X) = 0$ exactly when $Y$ is a function of $X$.

## Pitfalls

- **$\entropy(Y \mid X)$ is not $\entropy(Y \mid X = x)$.** The first is an average over $x$; the second is the entropy
  of one conditional distribution.
- **Not symmetric.** $\entropy(Y \mid X)$ and $\entropy(X \mid Y)$ generally differ, as in the example (0.689 against
  0.5 bits). Their difference is $\entropy(Y) - \entropy(X)$.
