<Definition>
The mutual information between <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variables</NoteLink> $X$ and $Y$ is the reduction in the <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> of $X$ from observing $Y$:

$$
I(X; Y) = \entropy(X) - \entropy(X \mid Y) = \sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)\, p(y)}.
$$

It is measured in bits or nats, like entropy <Cite id="cover2006" />.
</Definition>

## The identities

Every form of mutual information comes from writing the log-ratio $\log \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}$ in a different
way and taking its <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>.

**As a KL divergence.** By definition of <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink>,

$$
I(X; Y) = \expect\brack{\log \frac{p(X, Y)}{p(X)\, p(Y)}} = \KL\paren{p(x, y) \,\|\, p(x)\, p(y)}.
$$

Mutual information measures how far the joint distribution is from the product of its marginals, the distribution
the pair would have if the variables were independent.

**As a reduction in uncertainty.** Write $p(x, y) = p(x \mid y)\,p(y)$, so the log-ratio is
$\log p(x \mid y) - \log p(x)$. Its expectation is $-\entropy(X \mid Y) + \entropy(X)$:

$$
I(X; Y) = \entropy(X) - \entropy(X \mid Y).
$$

**Symmetry.** Writing $p(x, y) = p(y \mid x)\,p(x)$ instead gives $I(X; Y) = \entropy(Y) - \entropy(Y \mid X)$.
Mutual information is therefore symmetric: $I(X; Y) = I(Y; X)$.

**Through the joint entropy.** Split the log-ratio as $\log p(x, y) - \log p(x) - \log p(y)$:

$$
I(X; Y) = \entropy(X) + \entropy(Y) - \entropy(X, Y).
$$

**Self-information.** $I(X; X) = \entropy(X) - \entropy(X \mid X) = \entropy(X)$, because $X$ has no uncertainty left
once $X$ is known. This is why entropy is sometimes called self-information.

**Non-negativity.** KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when its arguments agree, so $I(X; Y) \ge 0$, with
equality exactly when $p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y)$ for all $x, y$: when $X$ and $Y$ are
<NoteLink to="independence">independent</NoteLink>. Unlike correlation, mutual information detects any dependence, not
only linear dependence.

## Worked example

For the joint distribution of the <NoteLink to="joint-and-conditional-entropy">joint and conditional entropy</NoteLink>
note, with $p(0, 0) = \tfrac12$, $p(0, 1) = \tfrac14$, $p(1, 0) = 0$ and $p(1, 1) = \tfrac14$, the entropies are
$\entropy(X) \approx 0.811$, $\entropy(Y) = 1$ and $\entropy(X, Y) = 1.5$ bits, so

$$
I(X; Y) = 0.811 + 1 - 1.5 \approx 0.311 \text{ bits}.
$$

Both conditional forms agree: $\entropy(Y) - \entropy(Y \mid X) = 1 - 0.689$ and
$\entropy(X) - \entropy(X \mid Y) = 0.811 - 0.5$. The figure starts at this distribution.

## The information diagram

The identities say that $\entropy(X)$, $\entropy(Y)$, $\entropy(X, Y)$, the two conditional entropies and $I(X; Y)$ fit
together like the regions of a two-set Venn diagram: $\entropy(X, Y)$ is the union, $I(X; Y)$ the intersection, and
$\entropy(X \mid Y)$ and $\entropy(Y \mid X)$ the two parts outside the intersection <Cite id="mackay2003" />. For two
variables every region is non-negative and the picture is exact.

For three or more variables the picture is only a mnemonic. The central region, the interaction information
$I(X; Y; Z) = I(X; Y) - I(X; Y \mid Z)$, can be negative, and a region with negative area has no Venn diagram.

**Example.** Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent fair bits and $Z = X \oplus Y$ their exclusive or. Then $I(X; Y) = 0$.
Given $Z$, however, $Y$ is determined by $X$, so $I(X; Y \mid Z) = \entropy(Y \mid Z) - \entropy(Y \mid X, Z) = 1 - 0
= 1$ bit, and $I(X; Y; Z) = 0 - 1 = -1$ bit. Two variables that are independent can become dependent once a third is
known.

## Conditional mutual information and the chain rule

The conditional mutual information is the mutual information between $X$ and $Y$ within each value of $Z$, averaged
over $Z$:

$$
I(X; Y \mid Z) = \entropy(X \mid Z) - \entropy(X \mid Y, Z) = \expect\brack{\log \frac{p(X, Y \mid Z)}{p(X \mid Z)\,
p(Y \mid Z)}} \ge 0.
$$

Mutual information obeys a <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink>. Apply the
<NoteLink to="joint-and-conditional-entropy">chain rule for entropy</NoteLink> to $\entropy(Y, Z)$ and to
$\entropy(Y, Z \mid X)$:

$$
I(X; Y, Z) = \entropy(Y, Z) - \entropy(Y, Z \mid X) = \brack{\entropy(Z) - \entropy(Z \mid X)} + \brack{\entropy(Y \mid
Z) - \entropy(Y \mid X, Z)} = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z).
$$

## Data-processing inequality

If $X \to Y \to Z$ is a <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink>, meaning $Z$ depends on $X$ only through $Y$, then processing $Y$ cannot create
information about $X$:

$$
I(X; Z) \le I(X; Y).
$$

**Proof.** Expand $I(X; Y, Z)$ with the chain rule in both orders:

$$
I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z) = I(X; Y) + I(X; Z \mid Y).
$$

The Markov property says $X$ and $Z$ are independent given $Y$, so $I(X; Z \mid Y) = 0$. Therefore
$I(X; Z) = I(X; Y) - I(X; Y \mid Z) \le I(X; Y)$, because conditional mutual information is non-negative.

No function applied to data adds information about the quantity that generated it. A feature
extractor, a compressed representation or a summary statistic can at best preserve it.

## Gaussian variables

For a bivariate normal pair with correlation $\rho$, mutual information depends only on $\rho$:

$$
I(X; Y) = -\tfrac12 \log\paren{1 - \rho^2}.
$$

**Derivation.** For continuous variables the identity $I = h(X) + h(Y) - h(X, Y)$ holds with
<NoteLink to="differential-entropy">differential entropies</NoteLink>, and a Gaussian with covariance $\Sigmamat$ has
$h = \tfrac12 \log \det(2\pi e \Sigmamat)$. With variances $\sigma_x^2$, $\sigma_y^2$ and
$\det \Sigmamat = \sigma_x^2 \sigma_y^2 (1 - \rho^2)$,

$$
I = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \sigma_x^2) + \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \sigma_y^2) - \tfrac12 \log\paren{(2\pi e)^2
\sigma_x^2\sigma_y^2 (1 - \rho^2)} = -\tfrac12 \log(1 - \rho^2).
$$

At $\rho = 0.5$ this is $0.144$ nats ($0.208$ bits); at $\rho = 0.9$, $0.830$ nats. As $|\rho| \to 1$ it grows without
bound: a continuous variable can carry unlimited information about another.

## In machine learning

- **Decision trees** choose the split that maximises the information gain $\entropy(Y) - \entropy(Y \mid X)$ between a
  candidate feature $X$ and the label $Y$, which is $I(X; Y)$ <Cite id="quinlan1986" />.
- **Feature selection** ranks features by their mutual information with the target, which catches non-linear
  relationships that correlation misses.
- **Representation learning.** Contrastive objectives such as <Gloss name="infonce" /> maximise a lower bound on the mutual information
  between two views of the same input <Cite id="oord2018" />. The information bottleneck trades off $I(X; T)$ against
  $I(T; Y)$ for a representation $T$.

## Pitfalls

- **Estimation.** Estimating mutual information from samples is hard, especially for continuous variables in high
  dimensions; plug-in estimates are biased and bounds used in practice can be loose.
- **Mutual information is not causation.** It is symmetric and says nothing about which variable influences the other.
- **Scale.** $I(X; Y)$ is bounded by $\min(\entropy(X), \entropy(Y))$ for discrete variables, so comparisons across
  pairs with different entropies need a normalised version.
