<Definition>
A gambler starts with $i$ units and bets one unit at a time. Each bet is won with probability $p$ and lost with
probability $q = 1 - p$, independently. The gambler stops on reaching $N$ units or on reaching 0. What is the
probability of reaching $N$ before going broke, and how many bets does the game last on average?
<Cite id="feller1968" />
</Definition>

## Setup

The fortune after $t$ bets, $X_t$, is a random walk on $\set{0, 1, \dots, N}$ that steps up with probability $p$ and
down with probability $q$. The states 0 and $N$ are absorbing. Let

$$
w_i = P(\text{reach } N \text{ before } 0 \mid X_0 = i), \qquad w_0 = 0, \quad w_N = 1.
$$

## Solution

Condition on the first bet. By the <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink>, and
because the walk starts afresh after each bet,

$$
w_i = p\, w_{i+1} + q\, w_{i-1}, \qquad 0 < i < N.
$$

This technique is called first-step analysis. Write $w_i = p\, w_i + q\, w_i$ and rearrange. The differences
$\Delta_i = w_{i+1} - w_i$ satisfy $p\,\Delta_i = q\,\Delta_{i-1}$, so $\Delta_i = r^i \Delta_0$ with $r = q/p$.
Summing the differences from $w_0 = 0$ gives $w_i = \Delta_0 \sum_{k=0}^{i-1} r^k$. The condition $w_N = 1$ fixes
$\Delta_0$:

$$
w_i = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1 - (q/p)^i}{1 - (q/p)^N}, & p \ne \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[2ex] \dfrac{i}{N}, & p = \tfrac{1}{2}. \end{cases}
$$

The same first-step argument applied to the expected number of bets, $D_i = 1 + p\, D_{i+1} + q\, D_{i-1}$ with
$D_0 = D_N = 0$, gives

$$
D_i = \begin{cases} \dfrac{i}{q - p} - \dfrac{N}{q - p}\, w_i, & p \ne \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[2ex] i (N - i), & p = \tfrac{1}{2}. \end{cases}
$$

**A worked case.** A roulette player bets on red, which wins with probability $p = 18/38 \approx 0.474$. Starting with
10 units and aiming for 20, the probability of success is $w_{10} \approx 0.259$, and the game lasts about 92 bets on
average. In a fair game the probability would be $0.5$ and the game would last 100 bets. Starting with 50 and aiming
for 100, the probability falls to about $0.005$.

<Derivation title="The fair case as a martingale">
When $p = 1/2$, the expected fortune after the next bet equals the current fortune, so $X_t$ is a martingale. The
optional stopping theorem applies because the game ends in finite expected time. The expected final fortune therefore
equals the starting fortune: $N \cdot w_i + 0 \cdot (1 - w_i) = i$, which gives $w_i = i/N$ without solving the
recursion. A fair game cannot be turned into a favourable one by choosing when to stop.
</Derivation>

## What it illustrates

- **First-step analysis.** Conditioning on the first move turns a question about whole paths into a linear recursion
  between neighbouring states. The same idea computes absorption probabilities and hitting times in any Markov chain.
  See <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="law-of-total-expectation">law of total expectation</NoteLink>.
- **Small edges compound.** The ratio $q/p$ is raised to the power $i$. An edge of a few percent per bet becomes an
  overwhelming disadvantage over many bets, which is how a casino profits from nearly fair games.
- **Expected value against the probability of ruin.** In the fair game the expected final fortune equals the starting
  fortune, yet the gambler ends broke with probability $1 - i/N$. The expectation hides the risk. See
  <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>.

## Variations

- **Bold play.** Against unfavourable odds, fewer and larger bets are better. With 10 units and a target of 20 at
  roulette, a single 10-unit bet on red succeeds with probability $0.474$, against $0.259$ for one-unit bets.
- **An infinitely rich opponent.** Let $N \to \infty$. If $p \le 1/2$, ruin is certain. If $p > 1/2$, the gambler is
  ruined with probability $(q/p)^i$: $0.67$ for $p = 0.51$ and $i = 10$.
- **Long games from small stakes.** In a fair game starting with 1 unit and aiming for 1,000, the game ends after the
  first bet half the time, yet its expected length is $1 \cdot 999 = 999$ bets. The rare long games dominate the
  mean.
