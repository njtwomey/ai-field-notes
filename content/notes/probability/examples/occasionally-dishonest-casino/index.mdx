<Definition>
A casino uses two dice. The fair die shows each face with probability $1/6$. The loaded die shows a six with
probability $1/2$ and each other face with probability $1/10$. Before each roll, the casino switches from the fair die
to the loaded die with probability $0.05$, and from the loaded die back to the fair die with probability $0.1$. Only
the rolls are observed. Which die was used for each roll? <Cite id="durbin1998" />
</Definition>

## Setup

The die in use at roll $t$ is a hidden state $z_t \in \set{\mathrm{F}, \mathrm{L}}$. The observed roll is
$x_t \in \set{1, \dots, 6}$. The model has three parts.

- **Transitions.** The hidden states form a <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink>: $z_t$ depends only on $z_{t-1}$, through the matrix
  $$
  \Amat = \begin{pmatrix} P(\mathrm{F} \to \mathrm{F}) & P(\mathrm{F} \to \mathrm{L}) \\ P(\mathrm{L} \to \mathrm{F}) & P(\mathrm{L} \to \mathrm{L}) \end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95 & 0.05 \\ 0.1 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix}.
  $$
- **Emissions.** Each roll depends only on the current die: $e_\mathrm{F}(x) = 1/6$ for every face, and
  $e_\mathrm{L}(6) = 1/2$, $e_\mathrm{L}(x) = 1/10$ for $x \ne 6$.
- **Start.** The first die is drawn from the chain's stationary distribution. Solving
  $\pi_\mathrm{L} \cdot 0.1 = \pi_\mathrm{F} \cdot 0.05$ with $\pi_\mathrm{F} + \pi_\mathrm{L} = 1$ gives
  $\pi_\mathrm{F} = 2/3$ and $\pi_\mathrm{L} = 1/3$.

This is a <Gloss name="hmm">hidden Markov model (HMM)</Gloss>. The <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">joint probability</NoteLink> of a state sequence and the rolls factorises as

$$
P(z_{1:T}, x_{1:T}) = \pi_{z_1}\, e_{z_1}(x_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} A_{z_{t-1} z_t}\, e_{z_t}(x_t).
$$

Two numbers follow directly. A fair run lasts $1/0.05 = 20$ rolls on average and a loaded run $1/0.1 = 10$ rolls,
because run lengths are geometric. Over a long session, the share of sixes is
$\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 5/18 \approx 0.28$.

The problem asks two different questions. **Posterior decoding** asks, for each roll separately, for
$P(z_t = \mathrm{L} \mid x_{1:T})$. **Viterbi decoding** asks for the single most probable sequence
$\argmax_{z_{1:T}} P(z_{1:T} \mid x_{1:T})$. Summing or maximising over all $2^T$ sequences directly is impossible for
$T = 300$. Both answers come from dynamic programming in $O(T K^2)$ time for $K$ hidden states.

## Solution

### Forward pass

Define the forward variable $\alpha_t(s) = P(x_{1:t}, z_t = s)$: the probability of the first $t$ rolls and of being
in state $s$ at roll $t$. The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> over the
previous state gives a recursion:

$$
\alpha_1(s) = \pi_s\, e_s(x_1), \qquad \alpha_t(s) = e_s(x_t) \sum_{r} \alpha_{t-1}(r)\, A_{rs}.
$$

The sum over $r$ is valid because, given $z_{t-1}$, the new state and roll do not depend on earlier rolls. Summing the
last forward vector gives the likelihood of the rolls, $P(x_{1:T}) = \sum_s \alpha_T(s)$.

### Backward pass

Define the backward variable $\beta_t(s) = P(x_{t+1:T} \mid z_t = s)$: the probability of the remaining rolls given
the state at roll $t$. It runs from the end:

$$
\beta_T(s) = 1, \qquad \beta_t(r) = \sum_{s} A_{rs}\, e_s(x_{t+1})\, \beta_{t+1}(s).
$$

### Posterior

The forward and backward variables split the rolls at $t$, and the two halves are conditionally independent given
$z_t$. Their product is therefore the joint probability, and <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>
normalises it:

$$
P(z_t = s \mid x_{1:T}) = \frac{\alpha_t(s)\, \beta_t(s)}{\sum_{r} \alpha_t(r)\, \beta_t(r)}.
$$

Products of many probabilities underflow for long sequences. Implementations rescale $\alpha_t$ to sum to one at each
step and divide $\beta_t$ by the same constants. The logarithms of the constants sum to $\log P(x_{1:T})$
<Cite id="rabiner1989" />.

### Viterbi

Replace the sum in the forward pass by a maximum. Define $\delta_t(s)$ as the probability of the best state sequence
that ends in $s$ at roll $t$, together with the rolls:

$$
\delta_1(s) = \pi_s\, e_s(x_1), \qquad \delta_t(s) = e_s(x_t) \max_{r}\, \delta_{t-1}(r)\, A_{rs}.
$$

Record which $r$ attained each maximum. The best final state is $\argmax_s \delta_T(s)$, and following the recorded
pointers backwards recovers the whole sequence <Cite id="viterbi1967" />. In practice the recursion runs on
log-probabilities, which turns products into sums and avoids underflow.

<Derivation title="Why the posterior path and the Viterbi path differ">
Posterior decoding picks, at each roll, the state with the higher marginal probability. The resulting sequence can
even be impossible under the model, for example if a transition in it has probability zero. Viterbi maximises the
probability of the whole sequence jointly, so it respects the transitions. Posterior decoding maximises the expected
number of correctly labelled rolls; Viterbi maximises the probability of getting every roll right at once. On the
casino they usually agree, and they differ at the edges of loaded runs.
</Derivation>

## Simulation

<CasinoDecoder />

With the default parameters and 300 rolls, both decoders label about 80% of the rolls correctly, averaged over 300
simulated sessions. Errors cluster at the start and end of loaded runs and on short loaded runs, where a few rolls do
not carry enough evidence to overcome the prior preference for staying with the fair die.

## What it illustrates

- **Latent variables.** The quantity of interest, the die, is never observed. It is inferred from the data through
  the model, as the cluster assignment is in a <NoteLink to="gaussian-mixture-model">Gaussian mixture model</NoteLink>.
  A mixture model is an HMM whose states are drawn independently at each step.
- **Conditional independence makes inference tractable.** Given the current die, the next die and the current roll
  are independent of everything earlier. That structure turns an exponential sum over state sequences into a linear
  recursion. See <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.
- **Summing versus maximising.** Forward–backward and Viterbi are the same recursion on different operations: sum and
  product, or max and product. The same pattern appears in dynamic programming generally.
- **Hidden Markov models.** The casino is the smallest useful HMM. The same algorithms decode speech, align biological
  sequences and tag parts of speech <Cite id="rabiner1989" />. This site has no separate HMM note yet; this example is
  its driver.

## Variations

- **Unknown parameters.** If the switching and loading probabilities are unknown, the Baum–Welch algorithm estimates
  them. It is <Gloss name="em-algorithm">expectation–maximisation</Gloss>: forward–backward supplies the expected state occupancies and transitions, and
  the M-step re-estimates $\Amat$ and the emissions from them <Cite id="baum1970" />.
- **A heavier load.** Raising $P(\text{six} \mid \mathrm{L})$ makes each roll more informative. At $0.9$, posterior
  decoding labels about 95% of rolls correctly. At $P(\text{six} \mid \mathrm{L}) = 1/6$ the dice are identical and
  the rolls carry no information about the state.
- **Faster switching.** When the casino switches often, runs are short and each run offers little evidence. The
  posterior moves less far from the stationary probability, and accuracy falls. With switching probabilities $0.3$
  and $0.5$ per roll, posterior decoding labels about 71% of rolls correctly.
