<Definition>
Each of $n$ people has a birthday drawn independently and uniformly from $d = 365$ days. What is the probability that
at least two of them share a birthday? How large must $n$ be for that probability to exceed one half?
<Cite id="feller1968" />
</Definition>

## Setup

The sample space is the set of all $d^n$ equally likely assignments of birthdays to people. Let $M$ be the event that
at least two people share a birthday. Its complement $M^c$ is the event that all $n$ birthdays are distinct. The
complement is easier to count, so the solution finds $P(M^c)$ and subtracts it from one.

## Solution

Count the assignments with distinct birthdays. The first person has $d$ choices, the second $d - 1$, and the $n$-th
$d - n + 1$. Dividing by $d^n$:

$$
P(M^c) = \frac{d (d - 1) \cdots (d - n + 1)}{d^n} = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \paren{1 - \frac{k}{d}},
\qquad P(M) = 1 - \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \paren{1 - \frac{k}{d}}.
$$

For $d = 365$ the probability first exceeds one half at $n = 23$, where $P(M) = 0.5073$. At $n = 50$ it is $0.970$,
and at $n = 57$ it is $0.990$.

An approximation shows where the answer comes from. For small $x$, $1 - x \approx e^{-x}$, so

$$
P(M^c) \approx \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{-k/d} = \exp\paren{-\frac{n (n - 1)}{2d}} \approx \exp\paren{-\frac{n^2}{2d}}.
$$

The exponent counts pairs. There are $n(n-1)/2$ pairs of people, and each pair shares a birthday with probability
$1/d$. For $n = 23$ there are 253 pairs, so the expected number of matching pairs is $253/365 \approx 0.69$, and
$e^{-0.69} \approx 0.50$. Setting the approximation equal to one half gives
$n \approx \sqrt{2 d \ln 2} \approx 1.18 \sqrt{d}$, which is $22.5$ for $d = 365$.

## What it illustrates

- **Counting the complement.** "At least one match" has many cases; "no match" has one. Computing
  $1 - P(\text{complement})$ is the standard route. See <NoteLink to="probability-space">probability space</NoteLink>.
- **Pairs grow quadratically.** Intuition compares $n$ with $d$. The relevant quantity is the number of pairs,
  $n^2/2$, compared with $d$. Collisions become likely at $n \approx \sqrt{d}$, not $n \approx d$.
- **Expected counts through linearity.** The expected number of matching pairs is $\binom{n}{2}/d$, by linearity of
  <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over pairs, even though the pairs are not independent.
- **Independence.** The product formula assumes the birthdays are independent. The assumption is what lets the
  probabilities of successive distinct choices multiply. See <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.

## Variations

- **Hash collisions.** A hash function with $b$-bit outputs has $d = 2^b$ values. If its outputs behave like uniform
  random values, a collision becomes likely after about $1.18 \cdot 2^{b/2}$ inputs: about 77,000 for 32 bits and about
  $5 \times 10^9$ for 64 bits. A generic attack therefore finds collisions in about $2^{b/2}$ evaluations, so a
  $b$-bit hash offers only $b/2$ bits of collision resistance <Cite id="menezes1996" />.
- **Non-uniform birthdays.** Real birthdays are not uniform across the year. Any departure from uniformity makes a
  match more likely, so 23 is an upper bound on the group size needed for real populations.
- **A match with you.** The probability that someone among $n$ others shares your birthday is
  $1 - (1 - 1/d)^n$. It reaches one half only at $n = 253$. Fixing one member of each pair removes the quadratic
  growth.
