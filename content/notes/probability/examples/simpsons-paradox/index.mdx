<Definition>
A study compared two treatments for kidney stones: open surgery (A) and percutaneous nephrolithotomy (B). Each
treated 350 patients. A succeeded in 273 and B in 289, so B looks better. Split by stone size, A succeeded more often
for small stones and for large stones. Which treatment is better? <Cite id="charig1986" /> <Cite id="julious1994" />
</Definition>

## Setup

The data are successes out of patients treated, by treatment and stone size:

| Stone size | A: open surgery | B: nephrolithotomy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small | 81 / 87 (93%) | 234 / 270 (87%) |
| Large | 192 / 263 (73%) | 55 / 80 (69%) |
| All | 273 / 350 (78%) | 289 / 350 (83%) |

Let $S$ be success, $T \in \set{A, B}$ the treatment and $L$ the event that the stone is large. The comparison within
each stratum is between $P(S \mid T, L)$ and $P(S \mid T, L^c)$. The aggregate comparison is between $P(S \mid A)$ and
$P(S \mid B)$.

## Solution

The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink>, applied within each treatment group,
writes each overall rate as a weighted average of the two stratum rates:

$$
P(S \mid T) = P(S \mid T, L^c)\, P(L^c \mid T) + P(S \mid T, L)\, P(L \mid T).
$$

The weights differ between the groups. Surgery was given to large stones far more often:
$P(L \mid A) = 263/350 = 0.75$, while $P(L \mid B) = 80/350 = 0.23$. Large stones are harder to treat under either
method. A's average is therefore weighted towards its lower, large-stone rate, and B's towards its higher,
small-stone rate:

$$
P(S \mid A) = 0.93 \times 0.25 + 0.73 \times 0.75 = 0.78, \qquad P(S \mid B) = 0.87 \times 0.77 + 0.69 \times 0.23 = 0.83.
$$

Stone size affects both which treatment a patient received and whether the treatment succeeded. It is a confounder.
To compare the treatments on the same patients, fix the case mix. Using the pooled share of large stones,
$343/700 = 0.49$, for both treatments gives standardised success rates of $0.83$ for A and $0.78$ for B. A is better,
as the stratified comparison says.

## What it illustrates

- **Averages with different weights.** A weighted average of larger numbers can be smaller than a weighted average of
  smaller numbers when the weights differ. That is the whole arithmetic of the paradox, and the law of total
  probability exposes the weights.
- **Conditioning changes the question.** $P(S \mid A) > P(S \mid B)$ and $P(S \mid A, L) > P(S \mid B, L)$ are
  different statements about different populations. See
  <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink>.
- **Confounding.** When treatment is not assigned independently of a variable that affects the outcome, the raw
  comparison mixes the effect of the treatment with the effect of the case mix. Randomised assignment makes $T$
  independent of stone size, so $P(L \mid A) = P(L \mid B)$ and the reversal cannot occur. See
  <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.

## Variations

- **When not to stratify.** Stratifying is right here because stone size was fixed before treatment and influenced
  the choice of treatment. If the third variable were caused by the treatment, such as a complication that the
  treatment itself produces, stratifying on it would remove part of the treatment's effect. The data alone cannot
  decide; the causal order of the variables does <Cite id="simpson1951" />.
- **Equal case mix.** If both treatments had seen large stones in the same proportion, the overall rates would be
  weighted alike, and A would be ahead overall by between 4 and 6 percentage points, the range of its advantage within
  the strata.
- **In machine learning.** A model can have higher accuracy than another on every subgroup of a test set and lower
  accuracy overall, if the two were evaluated on test sets with different subgroup mixes. Comparisons of models across
  datasets, or of metrics across time as the input mix shifts, need the same care.
