<Definition>
A car is behind one of three doors, equally likely, and goats are behind the other two. You pick a door. The host,
who knows where the car is, always opens one of the other two doors to reveal a goat. If both hide goats, he picks
one of them at random. He then offers you the chance to switch to the remaining closed door. Should you switch?
<Cite id="selvin1975" />
</Definition>

## Setup

Number the doors so that you pick door 1. Let $C \in \set{1, 2, 3}$ be the door hiding the car, with
$P(C = c) = 1/3$ for each door. Let $H$ be the door the host opens. The host's rule gives the likelihoods

| Car at | $P(H = 2 \mid C)$ | $P(H = 3 \mid C)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| door 1 | $1/2$ | $1/2$ |
| door 2 | $0$ | $1$ |
| door 3 | $1$ | $0$ |

Suppose the host opens door 3. The question asks for $P(C = 2 \mid H = 3)$, the probability that switching wins.

## Solution

The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> gives the probability that the host
opens door 3:

$$
P(H = 3) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} + 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} + 0 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{2}.
$$

<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> then gives the probability of each door:

$$
P(C = 1 \mid H = 3) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{3}, \qquad
P(C = 2 \mid H = 3) = \frac{1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{2}{3}.
$$

Switching wins with probability $2/3$ and staying with probability $1/3$. By symmetry the same holds if the host
opens door 2.

A shorter argument gives the same answer. Staying wins exactly when the first pick was right, which has probability
$1/3$. The host never opens the car's door, so when the first pick was wrong, the remaining closed door must hide the
car. Switching therefore wins exactly when the first pick was wrong, with probability $2/3$.

## What it illustrates

- **Conditioning on the evidence and on how it arose.** The observation "door 3 hides a goat" is the same under both
  hosts in the variations below. The posterior differs because the probability of that observation differs. The
  likelihood $P(H = 3 \mid C)$ encodes the host's rule, and the rule is part of the model. See
  <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional probability</NoteLink>.
- **Bayes' theorem with unequal likelihoods.** The host is twice as likely to open door 3 when the car is behind door 2
  as when it is behind door 1. The posterior odds of door 2 against door 1 are therefore $2:1$.
- **Intuition fails when the likelihoods are hidden.** The common answer, $1/2$, treats the two closed doors as
  symmetric. They are not: the host was free to choose between doors 2 and 3 only when the car was behind door 1.

## Variations

- **More doors, host opens all but one.** With $n$ doors, the host opens $n - 2$ goat doors and leaves one other door
  closed. Staying wins with probability $1/n$ and switching with probability $(n - 1)/n$: $0.9$ for 10 doors and
  $0.99$ for 100.
- **More doors, host opens one.** If the host opens only one goat door and you switch to one of the other $n - 2$
  closed doors at random, switching wins with probability $\frac{n - 1}{n (n - 2)}$. For $n = 10$ this is
  $0.1125$, still above the $0.1$ for staying.
- **An ignorant host.** If the host opens the other doors at random and happens to reveal only goats, switching and
  staying each win with probability $1/2$. Given that no car was revealed, the car is equally likely to be behind
  your door or behind the remaining one. With $n$ doors the host avoids the car with probability $2/n$; the
  simulation discards the other games.
