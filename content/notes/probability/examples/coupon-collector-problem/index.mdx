<Definition>
A cereal box contains one of $n$ types of coupon, each type equally likely and independent across boxes. How many
boxes must be bought, on average, to collect every type? For $n = 6$, how many rolls of a fair die does it take to see
every face? <Cite id="feller1968" />
</Definition>

## Setup

Let $T$ be the number of draws needed to collect all $n$ types. Split the collection into stages. Stage $k$ starts
when $k - 1$ distinct types are held and ends when the $k$-th new type arrives. Let $T_k$ be the number of draws in
stage $k$, so that

$$
T = T_1 + T_2 + \dots + T_n.
$$

During stage $k$, a draw is new with probability $p_k = (n - k + 1)/n$, independently of other draws. $T_k$ is
therefore the number of trials up to the first success, which has a
<NoteLink to="geometric-distribution">geometric distribution</NoteLink> with mean $1/p_k$ and variance
$(1 - p_k)/p_k^2$.

## Solution

By linearity of <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>,

$$
\expect[T] = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{n}{n - k + 1} = n \paren{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{n - 1} + \dots + 1} = n H_n,
$$

where $H_n = \sum_{j=1}^n 1/j$ is the $n$-th harmonic number. Since $H_n = \ln n + \gamma + O(1/n)$ with
$\gamma \approx 0.5772$, the expectation grows as $n \ln n + \gamma n + 1/2 + O(1/n)$.

For a die, $n = 6$ and $\expect[T] = 6 H_6 = 6 \cdot 2.45 = 14.7$ rolls. For $n = 50$, $\expect[T] \approx 225$.

The stages are independent, so their variances add:

$$
\var(T) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1 - p_k}{p_k^2} = n^2 \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{1}{j^2} - n H_n < \frac{\pi^2}{6} n^2.
$$

For the die this gives a standard deviation of about $6.2$ rolls.

## What it illustrates

- **Linearity of expectation.** $T$ has a complicated distribution, but its mean is a sum of simple means. Linearity
  needs no independence.
- **Geometric waiting times.** Each stage is a wait for a success with a fixed probability. The last stage, with
  success probability $1/n$, alone takes $n$ draws on average. That is more than the first $n/2$ stages together,
  which take about $n \ln 2 \approx 0.69 n$.
- **Variance of a sum of independent parts.** Independence of the stages is what lets the variances add. See
  <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">variance and covariance</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.
- **Tail bounds.** After $m$ draws, a particular type is still missing with probability
  $(1 - 1/n)^m \le e^{-m/n}$. A union bound over the $n$ types gives $P(T > n \ln n + c n) \le e^{-c}$, so $T$
  concentrates near $n \ln n$.

## Variations

- **The birthday problem, reversed.** The <NoteLink to="birthday-problem">birthday problem</NoteLink> asks when the
  first repeat appears, after about $\sqrt{n}$ draws. The coupon collector asks when the last new value appears, after
  about $n \ln n$ draws.
- **Coverage in machine learning.** Sampling training examples with replacement, the number of draws needed to see
  every example at least once is about $n \ln n$. After $n$ draws, a fraction $(1 - 1/n)^n \approx e^{-1} \approx 0.37$
  of the examples has not been seen. Bootstrap samples leave out the same fraction.
- **Unequal probabilities.** If the types are not equally likely, the rarest types dominate the wait. The expected
  time is at least the uniform answer.
