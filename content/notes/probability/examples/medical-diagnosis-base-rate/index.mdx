<Definition>
A disease affects 1% of a population. A test detects it in 99% of people who have it (sensitivity) and correctly
clears 95% of people who do not (specificity). A person chosen at random tests positive. What is the probability that
the person has the disease?
</Definition>

## Setup

Let $D$ be the event that the person has the disease and $+$ the event that the test is positive. The problem gives

- the prevalence, or prior: $P(D) = 0.01$;
- the sensitivity: $P(+ \mid D) = 0.99$;
- the specificity: $P(- \mid D^c) = 0.95$, so the <Gloss name="fpr">false-positive rate</Gloss> is $P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.05$.

The question asks for the posterior $P(D \mid +)$. The problem gives the reverse conditional, $P(+ \mid D)$, so Bayes'
theorem is needed to turn it round.

## Solution

The <NoteLink to="law-of-total-probability">law of total probability</NoteLink> gives the probability of a positive
test, over both groups:

$$
P(+) = P(+ \mid D)\,P(D) + P(+ \mid D^c)\,P(D^c) = 0.99 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.0594.
$$

<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink> then gives

$$
P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+)} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.17.
$$

Five in six positive results are false. The healthy group is 99 times larger than the sick group. Its false-positive
rate of 5% therefore produces 0.0495 of the population as <Gloss name="fp">false positives</Gloss>, five times the 0.0099 who are <Gloss name="tp">true
positives</Gloss>.

Counting people makes the same calculation easier to follow <Cite id="gigerenzer1995" />. Of 1,000 people, 10 are
sick, and about 10 of them test positive. Of the 990 healthy people, about 50 test positive. So 10 of the 60 positives
are sick.

## What it illustrates

- **Bayes' theorem reverses a conditional.** The test's quality is stated as $P(+ \mid D)$. The patient needs
  $P(D \mid +)$. The two differ by a factor of $P(D)/P(+)$, here $0.01/0.0594$.
- **The base-rate fallacy.** Judging the result by the sensitivity alone, and answering 99%, ignores the prior. The
  prior dominates when the condition is rare.
- **Odds and likelihood ratios.** The prior odds are $1:99$. A positive test has likelihood ratio
  $P(+ \mid D)/P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.99/0.05 = 19.8$. The posterior odds are $19.8:99$, or $1:5$, which is a probability of
  $1/6$.
- **The law of total probability** supplies the denominator by summing over the two groups.

## Variations

- **A second, independent positive test.** The posterior from the first test becomes the prior for the second. The
  odds $1:5$ multiply by $19.8$ again, giving $3.96:1$, or $P(D \mid +, +) \approx 0.80$. A third positive gives about
  $0.99$. This assumes the tests' errors are conditionally independent given disease status. Repeating the same test
  on the same sample usually violates that assumption.
- **A higher prior.** If the person is tested because of symptoms, and 10% of symptomatic people have the disease,
  then $P(D \mid +) = 0.099/(0.099 + 0.045) \approx 0.69$. The same test is far more informative in a population where
  the disease is common.
- **A negative result.** $P(D \mid -) = (0.01 \times 0.01)/(0.01 \times 0.01 + 0.99 \times 0.95) \approx 1/9{,}400$.
  A negative result from this test is strong evidence of health, because the sensitivity is high and the disease is
  rare.
- **In machine learning.** A classifier for a rare class, such as fraud, has the same structure. A high recall and a
  low false-positive rate can still give a low precision, because precision is $P(D \mid +)$.
