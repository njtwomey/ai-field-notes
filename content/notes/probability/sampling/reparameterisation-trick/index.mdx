<Definition>
Suppose $\zvec \sim q_{\phivec}$ can be generated as $\zvec = \gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)$, where $\epsilonvec \sim
p(\epsilonvec)$ does not depend on $\phivec$ and $\gvec_{\phivec}$ is differentiable in $\phivec$. Then for a
differentiable $f$,

$$
\nabla_{\phivec}\, \expect_{q_{\phivec}}\brack{f(\zvec)} = \expect_{p(\epsilonvec)}\brack{\nabla_{\phivec}\, f\paren{\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)}}.
$$

Averaging the right-hand side over samples of $\epsilonvec$ gives an unbiased <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> estimate
<Cite id="kingma2014" /> <Cite id="rezende2014" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Training objectives often contain an expectation over a distribution that depends on the parameters being trained.
The <Gloss name="elbo">evidence lower bound</Gloss> of a <Gloss name="vae">variational autoencoder</Gloss> is an example: its expectation is over the encoder's
distribution $q_{\phivec}(\zvec \mid \xvec)$. The expectation has no closed form, so it is estimated by sampling. A
sample $\zvec \sim q_{\phivec}$ is a random number, not a function of $\phivec$, so automatic differentiation cannot
pass a gradient through it.

## Mechanism

Move the randomness into an input that does not depend on $\phivec$. For a Gaussian,

$$
z \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \Gauss(0, 1).
$$

The sample is now a deterministic, differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$, with $\epsilon$ held fixed. The
chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial \mu} = f'(z), \qquad \frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial \sigma} = f'(z)\, \epsilon.
$$

In a computation graph, $\epsilon$ is an input like the data, and backpropagation handles the rest.

## Derivation

The identity in the definition follows in two steps. First, $\zvec = \gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)$ has distribution
$q_{\phivec}$, so by the <NoteLink to="expectation">law of the unconscious statistician</NoteLink> the expectation can be taken over $\epsilonvec$ instead of
$\zvec$:

$$
\expect_{q_{\phivec}}\brack{f(\zvec)} = \expect_{p(\epsilonvec)}\brack{f\paren{\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)}} = \int
f\paren{\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)}\, p(\epsilonvec)\, d\epsilonvec.
$$

Second, the density $p(\epsilonvec)$ does not depend on $\phivec$, so $\phivec$ appears only inside the integrand.
Differentiating under the integral sign, which is valid when $\nabla_{\phivec} f(\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec))$ is
bounded by an integrable function, gives

$$
\nabla_{\phivec} \int f\paren{\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)}\, p(\epsilonvec)\, d\epsilonvec = \int \nabla_{\phivec}
f\paren{\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)}\, p(\epsilonvec)\, d\epsilonvec.
$$

Written over $\zvec$ directly, the parameter sits in the density $q_{\phivec}(\zvec)$, and differentiating the
integral leads instead to the score-function estimator. The chain rule expands the integrand:
$\nabla_{\phivec} f(\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)) = \Jmat_{\gvec}^\top \nabla_{\zvec} f$, where $\Jmat_{\gvec}$ is the
<NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> of $\gvec_{\phivec}(\epsilonvec)$ with respect to $\phivec$. The estimate is unbiased because each sample's
gradient has the true gradient as its expectation.

For a <NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate Gaussian</NoteLink> $\Gauss(\muvec, \Sigmamat)$ with $\Sigmamat = \Lmat\Lmat^\top$, the map is
$\zvec = \muvec + \Lmat\epsilonvec$ with $\epsilonvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$.

## Worked example

Take $f(z) = z^2$ and $z \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The exact expectation is $\expect\brack{z^2} = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$,
so the exact gradients are $2\mu$ and $2\sigma$.

The reparameterised sample is $z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon$. The per-sample gradients are

$$
\frac{\partial z^2}{\partial \mu} = 2(\mu + \sigma\epsilon), \qquad \frac{\partial z^2}{\partial \sigma} = 2(\mu + \sigma\epsilon)\,\epsilon.
$$

Their expectations are $2\mu$ and $2\mu\,\expect[\epsilon] + 2\sigma\,\expect[\epsilon^2] = 2\sigma$, which match. With
$\mu = 1$, $\sigma = 0.5$ and a single draw $\epsilon = 0.4$: $z = 1.2$, the estimate of $\partial/\partial\mu$ is
$2.4$, and the estimate of $\partial/\partial\sigma$ is $0.96$.

```python
import torch

mu = torch.tensor(1.0, requires_grad=True)
sigma = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)
eps = torch.randn(10_000)            # noise that does not depend on the parameters
z = mu + sigma * eps                  # differentiable in mu and sigma
(z**2).mean().backward()
print(mu.grad, sigma.grad)            # ≈ 2.0 = 2μ and 1.0 = 2σ
```

## Which distributions can be reparameterised

- **Location–scale families**, such as the Gaussian, logistic, Laplace and Student $t$: $z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon$ with
  $\epsilon$ from the standard member of the family.
- **Distributions with a tractable quantile function**: $z = F_{\phivec}^{-1}(u)$ with $u \sim \Unif(0, 1)$, by
  <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">inverse-transform sampling</NoteLink>. The
  <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential</NoteLink> gives $z = -\log(u)/\lambda$.
- **Compositions**: a log-normal is $\exp$ of a reparameterised Gaussian.
- **<NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">Gamma</NoteLink>, beta and Dirichlet** have no simple differentiable sampler.
  <NoteLink to="reparameterisations-of-common-distributions">Implicit reparameterisation</NoteLink> differentiates through the cdf instead of the sampler <Cite id="figurnov2018" />.
- **Discrete distributions** cannot be reparameterised: a small change in the parameters either leaves a discrete sample
  unchanged or makes it jump. Continuous relaxations such as the
  <NoteLink to="gumbel-max-trick">Gumbel-softmax</NoteLink> approximate the discrete sample with a differentiable one.

## Trade-offs

- **Variance.** The pathwise estimator uses the derivative of $f$, which carries more information than values of $f$
  alone. Its variance is usually much lower than the score-function estimator's, but this is not guaranteed for every
  $f$ <Cite id="mohamed2020" />.
- **Requirements.** $f$ must be differentiable in $\zvec$, and the sampler must be differentiable in $\phivec$.
- **Cost.** One backward pass per sample, the same as ordinary backpropagation.

## Pitfalls

- **Non-differentiable integrands.** Where $f$ has jumps, as with thresholds, argmax or rounding, the pathwise gradient
  ignores the effect of crossing the jump. The result is biased, often severely.
- **Parameterising the scale.** $\sigma$ must stay positive. Optimise $\log \sigma$ or pass an unconstrained parameter
  through a softplus.
- **Sampling with the wrong call.** In PyTorch, `Normal(mu, sigma).sample()` blocks gradients and `rsample()` uses the
  reparameterisation. Using `sample()` gives zero gradients for the distribution parameters.
