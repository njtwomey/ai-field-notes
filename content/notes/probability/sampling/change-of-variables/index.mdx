<Definition>
Let the random vector $\xvec$ have density $p_X$ on $\reals^d$, and let $\gvec : \reals^d \to \reals^d$ be invertible
and differentiable with a differentiable inverse. Then $\yvec = \gvec(\xvec)$ has density

$$
p_Y(\yvec) = p_X\paren{\gvec^{-1}(\yvec)}\, \abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec^{-1}}(\yvec)}
= \frac{p_X(\xvec)}{\abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec}(\xvec)}}, \qquad \xvec = \gvec^{-1}(\yvec),
$$

where $\Jmat_{\gvec}$ is the Jacobian matrix of $\gvec$ <Cite id="casella2002" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A density is probability per unit length, area or volume. A map $\gvec$ moves probability mass around without
creating or destroying it. A small region of volume $\delta V$ around $\xvec$ holds mass $p_X(\xvec)\,\delta V$. The map
sends it to a region of volume $\abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec}(\xvec)}\,\delta V$ around $\yvec$. The same mass spread over a
larger volume has a lower density. Where $\gvec$ stretches space, $p_Y$ is small. Where $\gvec$ compresses space, $p_Y$
is large. Dropping the stretch factor leaves $p_X\paren{\gvec^{-1}(\yvec)}$, which is not a density. Substituting
$\yvec = \gvec(\xvec)$ shows that its integral over $\yvec$ is $\expect\brack{\abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec}(\xvec)}}$ under
$p_X$, which is not 1 in general.

## One dimension

For a strictly increasing $g$, the event $\set{Y \le y}$ equals $\set{X \le g^{-1}(y)}$. So
$F_Y(y) = F_X\paren{g^{-1}(y)}$. Differentiating with the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> gives

$$
p_Y(y) = p_X\paren{g^{-1}(y)}\, \frac{d g^{-1}}{dy}(y).
$$

For a strictly decreasing $g$ the inequality flips, $F_Y(y) = 1 - F_X\paren{g^{-1}(y)}$, and the derivative of
$g^{-1}$ is negative. Both cases combine into one formula with an absolute value:

$$
p_Y(y) = p_X\paren{g^{-1}(y)} \abs{\frac{d g^{-1}}{dy}(y)} = \frac{p_X(x)}{\abs{g'(x)}}, \qquad x = g^{-1}(y).
$$

The factor $\abs{g'(x)}$ is the local stretch: an interval of width $\delta$ near $x$ maps to an interval of width
about $\abs{g'(x)}\,\delta$ near $y$.

## Worked examples

Each example starts from $X \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$, with density $\phi(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

**Affine map.** Let $Y = aX + b$ with $a \ne 0$. Then $g^{-1}(y) = (y - b)/a$ and $\abs{g'} = \abs{a}$, so
$p_Y(y) = \phi\paren{(y - b)/a}/\abs{a}$. This is the $\Gauss(b, a^2)$ density. Location and scale families arise this
way.

**Exponential map.** Let $Y = e^X$. Then $g^{-1}(y) = \log y$ and $g'(x) = e^x = y$, so

$$
p_Y(y) = \frac{\phi(\log y)}{y}, \qquad y > 0.
$$

This is the <NoteLink to="log-normal-distribution">log-normal</NoteLink> density. The factor $1/y$ comes from the
stretch: $e^x$ spreads the right tail of $X$ over a long interval, and compresses the left tail into $(0, 1)$.

**A map that is not one-to-one.** Let $Y = X^2$. Every $y > 0$ has two preimages, $x = \pm\sqrt{y}$, each with stretch
$\abs{g'(x)} = 2\sqrt{y}$. The formula applies on each branch, and the contributions add:

$$
p_Y(y) = \frac{\phi(\sqrt{y})}{2\sqrt{y}} + \frac{\phi(-\sqrt{y})}{2\sqrt{y}} = \frac{e^{-y/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}, \qquad y > 0.
$$

This is the $\ChiSq_1$ density (see <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared distribution</NoteLink>). It is
unbounded at $y = 0$, because $g$ is flat at $x = 0$ and compresses a neighbourhood of 0 into a very short interval.

## Several dimensions

In $d$ dimensions the stretch factor is the absolute Jacobian determinant, the factor by which $\gvec$ scales small
volumes near $\xvec$ (see <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink>). The formula follows from the <NoteLink to="substitution-rule">substitution rule</NoteLink>
for <NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">multiple integrals</NoteLink>. For any set $B$,

$$
P(\yvec \in B) = P\paren{\xvec \in \gvec^{-1}(B)} = \int_{\gvec^{-1}(B)} p_X(\xvec)\, d\xvec = \int_B
p_X\paren{\gvec^{-1}(\yvec)}\, \abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec^{-1}}(\yvec)}\, d\yvec.
$$

The last step substitutes $\xvec = \gvec^{-1}(\yvec)$, which replaces $d\xvec$ by $\abs{\det
\Jmat_{\gvec^{-1}}(\yvec)}\, d\yvec$. A function whose integral over every $B$ equals $P(\yvec \in B)$ is the density of
$\yvec$, which gives the first form of the definition. The second form uses the inverse function theorem:
$\Jmat_{\gvec^{-1}}(\yvec)$ is the inverse of the matrix $\Jmat_{\gvec}(\xvec)$, so its <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> is $1/\det
\Jmat_{\gvec}(\xvec)$.

**<NoteLink to="matrix-vector-product">Linear maps</NoteLink> of Gaussian vectors.** Let $\xvec \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$ and $\yvec = \Amat\xvec + \bvec$ with
$\Amat$ invertible. The Jacobian is $\Amat$ everywhere, so

$$
p_Y(\yvec) = \frac{1}{\abs{\det \Amat}}\, (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\paren{-\tfrac{1}{2} \norm{\Amat^{-1}(\yvec - \bvec)}^2}.
$$

With $\Sigmamat = \Amat\Amat^\top$, $\abs{\det \Amat} = \sqrt{\det \Sigmamat}$ and $\norm{\Amat^{-1}(\yvec - \bvec)}^2 =
(\yvec - \bvec)^\top \Sigmamat^{-1} (\yvec - \bvec)$. The result is the
<NoteLink to="multivariate-normal-distribution">multivariate normal</NoteLink> density $\Gauss(\bvec, \Sigmamat)$. The
first identity holds because $\det \Sigmamat = \det \Amat \det \Amat^\top = (\det \Amat)^2$. The second holds because
$\norm{\Amat^{-1}\vvec}^2 = \vvec^\top \Amat^{-\top} \Amat^{-1} \vvec$ and $\Amat^{-\top}\Amat^{-1} =
(\Amat\Amat^\top)^{-1}$. This is how correlated Gaussian samples are drawn in practice: factor $\Sigmamat =
\Lmat\Lmat^\top$ by <NoteLink to="cholesky-decomposition">Cholesky decomposition</NoteLink> and set $\yvec = \bvec +
\Lmat\zvec$.

**Polar coordinates.** The map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ has Jacobian determinant $r$. A point
uniform on the unit disc has density $1/\pi$ in $(x, y)$ and therefore density $r/\pi$ in $(r, \theta)$. Integrating out
$\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ gives the radius the marginal density $2\pi \cdot r/\pi = 2r$ on $[0, 1]$, not a constant. See
<NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere">sampling a disc or sphere</NoteLink> and the
<NoteLink to="box-muller-transform">Box–Muller transform</NoteLink>, which are both derived this way.

## In machine learning

<NoteLink to="normalising-flow">Normalising flows</NoteLink> build a flexible density by pushing a simple base density, usually $\Gauss(\zeros, \Imat)$, through
a chain of invertible maps $\gvec = \gvec_K \circ \dots \circ \gvec_1$ <Cite id="rezende2015" />. Taking logarithms of
the formula, and using that determinants multiply along a chain,

$$
\log p_Y(\yvec) = \log p_X(\xvec) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec_k}(\xvec_{k-1})},
$$

where $\xvec_0 = \xvec$ and $\xvec_k = \gvec_k(\xvec_{k-1})$. Each layer is designed so that its inverse and its
Jacobian determinant are cheap. Coupling layers, for example, have triangular Jacobians, whose determinant is the
product of the diagonal <Cite id="papamakarios2021" />.

## Assumptions

- $\gvec$ must be invertible on the support of $p_X$, or the formula must be summed over every preimage.
- $\gvec$ must map $\reals^d$ to $\reals^d$. A map to fewer dimensions, such as $Y = X_1 + X_2$, has no square Jacobian.
  Its density comes from adding auxiliary variables to make the map square, then integrating them out.
- The Jacobian determinant must be non-zero wherever density is to be computed.

## Pitfalls

- **Inverting the wrong way.** The factor is $1/\abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec}}$ evaluated at $\xvec$, or equivalently
  $\abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec^{-1}}}$ evaluated at $\yvec$. Multiplying by $\abs{\det \Jmat_{\gvec}}$ instead is a common
  error, and produces a function that does not integrate to 1.
- **Discrete variables.** The formula is for densities. A <Gloss name="pmf">probability mass function</Gloss> is not rescaled by a map: for an
  invertible $g$, $P(Y = y) = P(X = g^{-1}(y))$.
- **Modes move.** The mode of $Y$ is not $g$ applied to the mode of $X$ in general. The log-normal $e^X$ with
  $X \sim \Gauss(0, 1)$ has its mode at $e^{-1} \approx 0.37$, not at $e^0 = 1$.
