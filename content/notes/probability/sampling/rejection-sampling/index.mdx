<Definition>
Let $p$ be a target density, $q$ a proposal density that can be sampled, and $M$ a constant with $p(x) \le M q(x)$ for
every $x$. Repeat: draw $x \sim q$ and $u \sim \Unif(0, 1)$, and accept $x$ if $u \le p(x)/\paren{M q(x)}$. The accepted
values are independent draws from $p$. Each proposal is accepted with probability $1/M$
<Cite id="vonneumann1951" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Many densities can be evaluated but not sampled directly. The quantile function may have no closed form, or the
density may be known only up to a normalising constant, as with a posterior density $p(\thetavec \mid \Dcal) \propto
p(\Dcal \mid \thetavec)\, p(\thetavec)$.

## Mechanism

The graph of $M q$ lies above the graph of $p$. Drawing $x \sim q$ and a height $h = u \cdot M q(x)$ produces a point
$(x, h)$ that is uniform in the region under $M q$. Keeping only the points with $h \le p(x)$ leaves points uniform in
the region under $p$. The $x$-coordinate of a point uniform under the graph of a density has that density, so the kept
$x$ values follow $p$.

The region under $M q$ has area $M$, and the region under $p$ has area 1. The fraction of points kept is therefore
$1/M$, and the number of proposals needed per accepted draw is geometric with mean $M$.

## Proof

The geometric argument can be written as a calculation. For a proposal $X \sim q$, the probability of acceptance given
$X = x$ is $p(x)/\paren{M q(x)}$. The joint probability of proposing a value in a small interval $[x, x + dx]$ and
accepting it is

$$
q(x)\, dx \cdot \frac{p(x)}{M q(x)} = \frac{p(x)}{M}\, dx.
$$

Integrating over $x$ gives the overall acceptance probability $\int p(x)/M\, dx = 1/M$. Dividing, the density of an
accepted value is $\paren{p(x)/M} / (1/M) = p(x)$. Successive attempts are independent, so accepted values are
independent.

## Unnormalised targets

The method needs $p$ only up to a constant. If $\tilde p = Z p$ with $Z$ unknown and $\tilde p \le M q$, the same
algorithm with $\tilde p$ in place of $p$ still returns exact draws from $p$. The acceptance rate becomes $Z/M$. This is
why rejection sampling works for posteriors whose evidence $p(\Dcal)$ is intractable.

## Worked example

Take the target $p(x) = 6x(1 - x)$ on $[0, 1]$, the $\Beta(2, 2)$ density (see
<NoteLink to="beta-distribution">beta distribution</NoteLink>), and the uniform proposal $q(x) = 1$ on
$[0, 1]$.

1. The target peaks at $x = 1/2$ with $p(1/2) = 1.5$. The smallest valid constant is $M = 1.5$.
2. Propose $x = 0.2$ with $u = 0.5$. The ratio is $p(0.2)/(1.5 \cdot 1) = 0.96/1.5 = 0.64$. Since $0.5 \le 0.64$,
   accept $x = 0.2$.
3. Propose $x = 0.9$ with $u = 0.5$. The ratio is $p(0.9)/1.5 = 0.54/1.5 = 0.36$. Since $0.5 > 0.36$, reject.

On average two of every three proposals are accepted.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
p = lambda x: 6 * x * (1 - x)
M = 1.5
x = rng.uniform(size=100_000)
u = rng.uniform(size=100_000)
samples = x[u <= p(x) / M]
print(len(samples) / len(x), samples.mean(), samples.var())  # ≈ 0.667, 0.5, 0.05
```

## Cost and trade-offs

- **Choice of proposal.** The acceptance rate is $1/M$, and the smallest valid $M$ is $\sup_x p(x)/q(x)$. A proposal
  close in shape to the target gives $M$ near 1. The proposal must have tails at least as heavy as the target's, or the
  supremum is infinite.
- **Dimension.** The best $M$ usually grows exponentially with dimension. For the target $\Gauss(\zeros, \Imat_d)$ and
  the slightly wider proposal $\Gauss(\zeros, \sigma^2 \Imat_d)$ with $\sigma > 1$, the ratio is $p(\xvec)/q(\xvec) =
  \sigma^d \exp\paren{-\tfrac12 \norm{\xvec}^2 (1 - \sigma^{-2})}$. The exponent is never positive, so the ratio peaks
  at the origin with value $M = \sigma^d$. With $\sigma = 1.1$ and $d = 100$, the acceptance rate is $1.1^{-100} \approx
  7 \times 10^{-5}$. Markov chain Monte Carlo methods replace rejection sampling in high dimensions.
- **Exactness.** Unlike Markov chain methods, rejection sampling returns exact, independent draws with no burn-in.
- **Adaptive envelopes.** For log-concave densities, adaptive rejection sampling builds a piecewise-exponential
  envelope from tangents of $\log p$ and refines it at every rejection <Cite id="gilks1992" />.

## Pitfalls

- **An envelope that does not cover the target.** If $p(x) > M q(x)$ anywhere, the method silently returns draws from
  $\min\paren{p, M q}$, normalised. The figure shows this when $M$ is set below the peak. Nothing in the output signals
  the error.
- **Estimating $M$ from a grid.** A supremum found on a coarse grid can miss a narrow peak. Use an analytic bound where
  possible, or add a safety margin.
- **Unbounded run time.** The number of attempts per draw is random. With a large $M$ some draws take many attempts.
