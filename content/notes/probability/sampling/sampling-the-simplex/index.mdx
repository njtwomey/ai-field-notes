<Definition>
The probability simplex with $K$ components is

$$
\Delta^{K-1} = \set{\pvec \in \reals^K : p_k \ge 0,\ \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^K p_k = 1}.
$$

A uniform draw from $\Delta^{K-1}$ is $\pvec = \evec / \sum_k e_k$, where $e_1, \dots, e_K \iid \Exp(1)$. Equivalently,
$\pvec$ is the vector of gaps between consecutive points of $0, U_{(1)}, \dots, U_{(K-1)}, 1$, where
$U_{(1)} \le \dots \le U_{(K-1)}$ are $K - 1$ sorted $\Unif(0, 1)$ draws. The uniform distribution on the simplex is
the Dirichlet distribution $\Dir(1, \dots, 1)$ <Cite id="devroye1986" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Random probability vectors appear as mixture weights, as random <NoteLink to="categorical-distribution">categorical distributions</NoteLink> in tests and simulations,
and as the weights of the Bayesian <NoteLink to="bootstrap">bootstrap</NoteLink> <Cite id="rubin1981" />. The simplex has dimension $K - 1$: the constraint
$\sum_k p_k = 1$ removes one degree of freedom. A sampler must respect the constraint and spread points evenly over it.

## Mechanism

### Normalised exponentials

Let $e_1, \dots, e_K \iid \Exp(1)$ (see <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink>),
drawn by <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">inverse-transform sampling</NoteLink> as $e_k = -\log U_k$. The joint density of $\evec$ is $\exp\paren{-\sum_k e_k}$,
which depends on $\evec$ only through the total $s = \sum_k e_k$.

Change variables from $(e_1, \dots, e_K)$ to the total $s$ and the first $K - 1$ proportions $p_k = e_k / s$, with
$p_K = 1 - \sum_{k < K} p_k$. The inverse map is $e_k = s\, p_k$. Its <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian determinant</NoteLink> is $s^{K-1}$: scaling a
$(K-1)$-dimensional slice of the simplex by $s$ multiplies its volume by $s^{K-1}$. By the
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink>, the joint density of $(s, \pvec)$ is

$$
e^{-s}\, s^{K-1}, \qquad s > 0, \ \pvec \in \Delta^{K-1}.
$$

It factorises into a function of $s$ alone and a constant in $\pvec$. So $\pvec$ is uniform on the simplex, and it is
independent of the total $s$, which has the $\GammaD(K, 1)$ density $s^{K-1}e^{-s}/(K-1)!$.

More generally, if $g_k \sim \GammaD(\alpha_k, 1)$ independently, then $\gvec / \sum_k g_k \sim \Dir(\alpha_1, \dots,
\alpha_K)$ (see <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="dirichlet-distribution">Dirichlet</NoteLink> distributions). The exponential distribution is
$\GammaD(1, 1)$, which gives $\Dir(1, \dots, 1)$.

<Derivation title="Normalised gammas are Dirichlet">
The joint density of independent $g_k \sim \GammaD(\alpha_k, 1)$ is $\prod_k g_k^{\alpha_k - 1} e^{-g_k} /
\Gamma(\alpha_k)$. Apply the same change of variables, $g_k = s\, p_k$ with Jacobian $s^{K-1}$. With $\alpha_0 =
\sum_k \alpha_k$,

$$
\prod_k \frac{(s p_k)^{\alpha_k - 1}}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)} \cdot e^{-s} \cdot s^{K-1} = \underbrace{\frac{s^{\alpha_0 - 1}
e^{-s}}{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}}_{\GammaD(\alpha_0, 1)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k)}
\prod_k p_k^{\alpha_k - 1}}_{\Dir(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)}.
$$

The powers of $s$ collect to $\sum_k (\alpha_k - 1) + K - 1 = \alpha_0 - 1$. The density factorises, so $\pvec$ is
Dirichlet and independent of the total.
</Derivation>

### Sorted-uniform spacings

Cut the unit interval at $K - 1$ independent uniform points. The $K$ pieces sum to 1 and form a point of the simplex.
The unsorted points have density 1 on the cube $[0, 1]^{K-1}$. Each of the $(K - 1)!$ orderings
is equally likely and is mapped to the same sorted vector, so the sorted points have the constant joint density
$(K - 1)!$ on $\set{0 \le u_{(1)} \le \dots \le u_{(K-1)} \le 1}$. The
map from sorted points to the first $K - 1$ gaps is linear with Jacobian determinant 1, so the gaps also have constant
density. The last gap is determined by the others.

### Normalised uniforms do not work

Dividing $K$ uniform numbers by their sum does land on the simplex, but not uniformly. The cube $[0, 1]^K$ is not
symmetric about the rays through the simplex: rays through the simplex's centre pass through more of the cube than rays
through its corners. Points pile up in the middle.

## Worked example

Take $K = 3$ and uniforms $u = (0.5, 0.2, 0.9)$.

1. Exponentials: $e = (-\log 0.5, -\log 0.2, -\log 0.9) = (0.693, 1.609, 0.105)$.
2. Their sum is $2.408$.
3. The probability vector is $\pvec = (0.288, 0.668, 0.044)$.

With spacings, sort two uniforms, say $0.2$ and $0.5$. The gaps are $(0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
K, n = 3, 100_000
e = rng.exponential(size=(n, K))
p = e / e.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)         # uniform on the simplex
print(np.mean(p.max(axis=1) <= 0.5))          # ≈ 0.25, the area of the inner triangle
# NumPy also provides this directly: rng.dirichlet(np.ones(K), size=n)
```

## Cost and trade-offs

- **Exponentials.** $K$ logarithms and one division per component. The method extends to any Dirichlet by replacing
  the exponentials with gamma draws.
- **Spacings.** $K - 1$ uniforms and a sort, which costs $O(K \log K)$. It needs no logarithms, and it is exact in
  finite precision up to the resolution of the uniforms.

## Pitfalls

- **Normalised uniforms.** As shown above, they crowd the centre of the simplex. For $K = 3$ about half of their points
  land in the inner triangle, twice the share of a uniform sampler.
- **Softmax of Gaussians.** Applying a softmax to independent Gaussian draws gives a logistic-normal distribution, not
  the uniform one. It is a valid distribution on the simplex with different properties.
- **Uniform is not "typical" in high dimensions.** For large $K$, each coordinate of a uniform draw is about $1/K$ and
  roughly exponentially distributed after scaling by $K$. Sparse probability vectors need a Dirichlet with small
  $\alpha_k$.
