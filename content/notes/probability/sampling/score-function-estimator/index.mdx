<Definition>
For a family of distributions $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ and a function $f$ that does not depend on $\thetavec$,

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}\, \expect_{p_{\thetavec}}\brack{f(\xvec)} = \expect_{p_{\thetavec}}\brack{f(\xvec)\, \nabla_{\thetavec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $\nabla_{\thetavec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ is the score. Averaging $f(\xvec)\, \nabla_{\thetavec}
\log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ over samples $\xvec \sim p_{\thetavec}$ gives an unbiased gradient estimate
<Cite id="glynn1990" /> <Cite id="williams1992" />.
</Definition>

## Mechanism

The identity follows from $\nabla p = p\, \nabla \log p$, the log-derivative trick. For a density,

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec} \int f(\xvec)\, p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)\, d\xvec = \int f(\xvec)\, \nabla_{\thetavec} p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)\, d\xvec
= \int f(\xvec)\, p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)\, \nabla_{\thetavec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)\, d\xvec.
$$

The first step moves the gradient inside the integral, which is valid when $f\, \nabla_{\thetavec} p_{\thetavec}$ is
bounded by an integrable function. The second step multiplies and divides by $p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$, since
$\nabla \log p = \nabla p / p$. The last integral is an <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> under $p_{\thetavec}$, so a sample average of
$f(\xvec)\, \nabla_{\thetavec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)$ estimates it without bias.

The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> falls on the distribution, not on $f$. The estimator therefore needs only evaluations of $f$ at samples
and the score of the distribution. $f$ can be discontinuous, a black-box simulator, or a reward from an environment.
The samples can be discrete, with sums in place of integrals.

## Baselines

The score has expectation zero: $\expect\brack{\nabla \log p_{\thetavec}} = \int \nabla p_{\thetavec} = \nabla 1 = 0$.
Any constant $b$ can therefore be subtracted from $f$ without changing the expectation:

$$
\nabla_{\thetavec}\, \expect\brack{f(\xvec)} = \expect\brack{\paren{f(\xvec) - b}\, \nabla_{\thetavec} \log p_{\thetavec}(\xvec)}.
$$

A baseline near $\expect[f(\xvec)]$ removes the part of $f$ that carries no information about the gradient, and usually
reduces the variance greatly. In <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement learning</Gloss> the baseline is a learned <Gloss name="value-function">value function</Gloss>, and $f - b$ is the
advantage.

## Worked example

Take $x \sim \Bern(\theta)$ (see <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">Bernoulli distribution</NoteLink>) and a reward
$f(1) = 3$, $f(0) = 1$. The expectation is $\theta \cdot 3 + (1 - \theta) \cdot 1 = 1 + 2\theta$, so the exact gradient
is 2.

The log-probability is $x \log\theta + (1 - x)\log(1 - \theta)$. Its derivative, the score, is $1/\theta$ when $x = 1$
and $-1/(1 - \theta)$ when $x = 0$. With $\theta = 0.25$:

- A draw $x = 1$ gives the estimate $3 \cdot 4 = 12$.
- A draw $x = 0$ gives the estimate $1 \cdot (-4/3) = -1.33$.

The expectation is $0.25 \cdot 12 + 0.75 \cdot (-1.33) = 2$, as required, but single estimates range from $-1.33$ to 12.
With the baseline $b = \expect[f] = 1.5$, the two estimates become $1.5 \cdot 4 = 6$ and $-0.5 \cdot (-4/3) = 0.67$. The
mean is still 2 and the spread is much smaller.

## In machine learning

- **Policy gradients.** REINFORCE trains a stochastic policy by weighting the score of each action taken by the return
  that followed <Cite id="williams1992" />.
- **Discrete latent variables.** Models with categorical or Bernoulli latent variables cannot use the
  <NoteLink to="reparameterisation-trick">reparameterisation trick</NoteLink>.
- **<NoteLink to="black-box-variational-inference">Black-box variational inference</NoteLink>.** Any model whose log-density can be evaluated can be fitted, with no
  model-specific derivations.

## Trade-offs

- **Generality.** Works for any distribution with a differentiable log-density or log-probability, and any $f$ for
  which $f\, \nabla_{\thetavec} p_{\thetavec}$ is bounded by an integrable function.
- **Variance.** It ignores the derivative of $f$, so it usually needs many more samples, or good baselines and control
  variates, than the reparameterisation trick for the same accuracy <Cite id="mohamed2020" />. The variance grows with the
  number of parameters and with the magnitude of $f$.

## Pitfalls

- **No baseline.** Raw rewards that are all positive push up the probability of every sampled action. Only the
  differences in size carry signal. Without a baseline, learning is slow and unstable.
- **A baseline that depends on the sample.** $b$ may depend on $\thetavec$ or on other samples, but not on $\xvec$
  itself, or the estimator becomes biased.
