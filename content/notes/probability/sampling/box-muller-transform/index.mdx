<Definition>
Let $U_1, U_2 \iid \Unif(0, 1)$. Set

$$
R = \sqrt{-2 \log U_1}, \qquad \Theta = 2\pi U_2, \qquad Z_1 = R \cos\Theta, \qquad Z_2 = R \sin\Theta.
$$

Then $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent and each is $\Gauss(0, 1)$ <Cite id="box1958" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

The <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">Gaussian</NoteLink> cdf $\Phi$ has no closed-form inverse. <NoteLink to="inverse-transform-sampling">Inverse-transform
sampling</NoteLink> therefore needs a numerical approximation of $\Phi^{-1}$ for every draw. An exact method that uses only
elementary functions is simpler to implement and to verify.

## Mechanism

The method works in <NoteLink to="multiple-integrals">polar coordinates</NoteLink>. A pair $(Z_1, Z_2)$ of independent standard Gaussians has joint density

$$
p(z_1, z_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \exp\paren{-\frac{z_1^2 + z_2^2}{2}}.
$$

The density depends only on the radius $r = \sqrt{z_1^2 + z_2^2}$. So the angle $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$
and independent of $R$. The squared radius $R^2$ is the sum of two squared standard Gaussians, which is $\ChiSq_2$
(see <NoteLink to="chi-squared-distribution">chi-squared distribution</NoteLink>), the <NoteLink to="exponential-distribution">exponential distribution</NoteLink> with
mean 2. An exponential variable with mean 2 is $-2 \log U_1$ by inverse-transform
sampling. Taking the square root gives $R$.

Each step is exact, so the output is exactly Gaussian up to floating-point rounding.

## Proof

The argument above explains why the method should work. The proof applies the
<NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change-of-variables formula</NoteLink> to the whole map. Write the map from $(u_1,
u_2) \in (0, 1)^2$ to $(z_1, z_2)$ and invert it:

$$
u_1 = \exp\paren{-\frac{z_1^2 + z_2^2}{2}}, \qquad u_2 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \operatorname{atan2}(z_2, z_1) \bmod 1.
$$

The <NoteLink to="partial-derivatives">partial derivatives</NoteLink> of the inverse are

$$
\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial z_i} = -z_i\, u_1, \qquad
\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial z_1} = -\frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{z_2}{z_1^2 + z_2^2}, \qquad
\frac{\partial u_2}{\partial z_2} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{z_1}{z_1^2 + z_2^2}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="determinant">determinant</NoteLink> of this <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobian</NoteLink> is $-\frac{u_1}{2\pi}\frac{z_1^2 + z_2^2}{z_1^2 + z_2^2} = -\frac{u_1}{2\pi}$. The
density of $(U_1, U_2)$ is 1 on the unit square, so by the change-of-variables formula

$$
p(z_1, z_2) = 1 \cdot \abs{-\frac{u_1}{2\pi}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \exp\paren{-\frac{z_1^2 + z_2^2}{2}}
= \frac{e^{-z_1^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z_2^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.
$$

The density factorises into two standard Gaussian densities, so $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent standard Gaussians.

## Worked example

Take $u_1 = 0.25$ and $u_2 = 0.1$.

1. The radius is $R = \sqrt{-2 \log 0.25} = \sqrt{2.773} = 1.665$.
2. The angle is $\Theta = 2\pi \cdot 0.1 = 0.628$ radians, which is $36°$.
3. The outputs are $Z_1 = 1.665 \cos 0.628 = 1.347$ and $Z_2 = 1.665 \sin 0.628 = 0.979$.

A general Gaussian $\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ follows as $\mu + \sigma Z_1$. A correlated pair with correlation $\rho$
follows as $X_1 = Z_1$ and $X_2 = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\, Z_2$.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
u1 = 1.0 - rng.uniform(size=100_000)       # in (0, 1], so log(u1) is finite
u2 = rng.uniform(size=100_000)
r = np.sqrt(-2.0 * np.log(u1))
z1, z2 = r * np.cos(2 * np.pi * u2), r * np.sin(2 * np.pi * u2)
print(z1.mean(), z1.var(), np.mean(z1 * z2))  # ≈ 0, 1, 0
```

## The polar method

Marsaglia's polar method avoids the sine and cosine <Cite id="marsaglia1964" />. It draws $(V_1, V_2)$ uniformly on
the square $[-1, 1]^2$ and keeps the pair only if $S = V_1^2 + V_2^2$ lies in $(0, 1)$. The kept point is <NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere">uniform on
the unit disc</NoteLink>. Its angle is uniform, so $V_1 / \sqrt{S}$ and $V_2 / \sqrt{S}$ are the cosine and sine of a uniform
angle. $S$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$: the kept point lies within radius $\sqrt s$ with probability equal to that disc's
share of the area, $\pi s / \pi = s$. $S$ is independent of the angle, because the uniform distribution on the disc is
unchanged by rotation. So $S$ can replace $U_1$. The outputs are

$$
Z_i = V_i \sqrt{\frac{-2 \log S}{S}}, \qquad i = 1, 2.
$$

The rejection step keeps a fraction $\pi / 4 \approx 0.785$ of the pairs.

## Cost and trade-offs

- **Cost.** One logarithm, one square root and one sine–cosine pair per two outputs. The polar method replaces the
  trigonometric functions with a rejection step and a division.
- **Tail accuracy.** The largest radius the transform can produce is set by the smallest representable $u_1$. With
  double-precision uniforms on a grid of $2^{-53}$, the radius is at most $\sqrt{2 \cdot 53 \log 2} \approx 8.57$. Tail
  events beyond about $8.5$ standard deviations never occur.
- **Speed.** Modern libraries use faster methods such as the ziggurat algorithm. Box–Muller remains the standard
  textbook method and is easy to vectorise.

## Pitfalls

- **Zero.** $\log 0$ is undefined. Draw $u_1$ from $(0, 1]$, for example as $1 - u$ with $u \in [0, 1)$.
- **Weak generators.** Consecutive outputs of a poor linear congruential generator are correlated. Box–Muller maps
  such pairs onto spirals in the plane instead of a Gaussian cloud <Cite id="neave1973" />.
