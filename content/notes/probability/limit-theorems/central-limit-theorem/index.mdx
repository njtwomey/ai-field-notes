<Definition>
Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be <Gloss name="iid">independent and identically distributed</Gloss> with mean $\mu$ and variance $0 < \sigma^2 < \infty$,
and let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$. The **central limit theorem** states that for every $z \in \reals$

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \prob\paren{\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} \le z} = \Phi(z),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard <NoteLink to="gaussian-distribution">normal distribution</NoteLink> function. Equivalently, $\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)$ converges in
distribution to $\Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, where $\bar X_n = S_n/n$ <Cite id="durrett2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

The <NoteLink to="law-of-large-numbers">law of large numbers</NoteLink> says $\bar X_n - \mu$ shrinks to 0. Its
<NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> is $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Multiplying by $\sqrt n$ keeps the spread fixed at $\sigma$, and the central
limit theorem describes the shape that remains: always the normal, whatever the shape of one draw.

The normal is the only possible limit. A sum of many small independent
pieces depends on each piece only through its mean and variance, to leading order. Skewness, kurtosis and all higher
features of one draw enter the sum with weights that vanish as $n$ grows. The normal is the distribution fixed by a
mean and a variance alone.

## Proof with characteristic functions

The characteristic function of a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> $X$ is $\varphi_X(t) = \expect[e^{itX}]$ for $t \in \reals$. It
exists for every distribution because $\abs{e^{itX}} = 1$. Two of its properties drive the proof.

- **Sums become products.** For independent $X$ and $Y$, $\varphi_{X + Y}(t) = \expect[e^{itX}e^{itY}] =
  \varphi_X(t)\varphi_Y(t)$. Scaling gives $\varphi_{aX}(t) = \varphi_X(at)$.
- **Lévy's continuity theorem.** If $\varphi_{Z_n}(t) \to \varphi_Z(t)$ for every $t$, then $Z_n \to Z$ in
  distribution <Cite id="billingsley2012" />.

The characteristic function of $\Gauss(0, 1)$ is $e^{-t^2/2}$. The theorem follows once the characteristic function of
the standardised sum is shown to converge to it.

Let $Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, so that $\expect[Y_i] = 0$ and $\expect[Y_i^2] = 1$, and let
$Z_n = (S_n - n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n) = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i$. Write $\varphi$ for the characteristic
function of one $Y_i$. By the two properties above,

$$
\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \varphi\paren{\frac{t}{\sqrt n}}^n.
$$

A finite second moment makes $\varphi$ twice differentiable at 0, with <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> obtained by differentiating under
the expectation: $\varphi(0) = 1$, $\varphi'(0) = i\expect[Y] = 0$ and $\varphi''(0) = -\expect[Y^2] = -1$. Taylor's
theorem gives

$$
\varphi(s) = 1 - \frac{s^2}{2} + o(s^2) \quad \text{as } s \to 0.
$$

Substituting $s = t/\sqrt n$ for fixed $t$,

$$
\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \paren{1 - \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\paren{\frac1n}}^n \to e^{-t^2/2},
$$

using $(1 + a_n/n)^n \to e^a$ whenever $a_n \to a$ (the identity holds for complex $a_n$ too). The limit is the
characteristic function of $\Gauss(0, 1)$, so Lévy's theorem gives $Z_n \to \Gauss(0, 1)$ in distribution.

The proof shows why only the variance survives. The first-order term vanishes because the mean is 0. The
second-order term, $-t^2/(2n)$, is of order $1/n$ per draw but is summed $n$ times. Every higher term is
$o(1/n)$ per draw, so it vanishes even after $n$ are summed.

<Derivation title="The same proof with moment-generating functions">
When the <NoteLink to="moment-generating-function">moment-generating function</NoteLink> $M(s) = \expect[e^{sY}]$
exists on an interval around 0, the same argument runs with real arguments. Then $M(s) = 1 + s^2/2 + o(s^2)$ and

$$
M_{Z_n}(s) = M\paren{\frac{s}{\sqrt n}}^n = \paren{1 + \frac{s^2}{2n} + o\paren{\frac1n}}^n \to e^{s^2/2},
$$

the moment-generating function of $\Gauss(0, 1)$. Pointwise convergence of moment-generating functions on an interval
around 0 implies <NoteLink to="modes-of-convergence">convergence in distribution</NoteLink>. This version is more elementary but excludes every distribution whose
moment-generating function does not exist, such as the Student $t$ with finite variance.
</Derivation>

## How fast: the Berry–Esseen theorem

The central limit theorem says nothing about the error at a given $n$. The **Berry–Esseen theorem** bounds it
<Cite id="berry1941" />. If $\rho = \expect\abs{X_1 - \mu}^3 < \infty$, then for all $n$

$$
\sup_{z \in \reals} \abs{\prob\paren{\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} \le z} - \Phi(z)} \le \frac{C\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt n}.
$$

The best known constant for identically distributed summands is $C \le 0.4748$ <Cite id="shevtsova2011" />. The rate
$1/\sqrt n$ cannot be improved in general: for a fair coin, the distribution of $S_n$ has jumps of size about
$\sqrt{2/(\pi n)}$ at its atoms, and no continuous $\Phi$ can match a jump to better than half its size. The ratio
$\rho/\sigma^3$ grows with the skewness of one draw. That is why the skewed distributions in the figure converge
slowly.

## Worked example: waiting for 30 events

Each of 30 independent tasks takes an $\Exp(1)$ time, with mean 1 and variance 1. The total time $S_{30}$ has
mean 30 and standard deviation $\sqrt{30} = 5.477$. The central limit theorem approximates

$$
\prob(S_{30} \ge 36) \approx 1 - \Phi\paren{\frac{36 - 30}{5.477}} = 1 - \Phi(1.095) = 0.1367.
$$

The exact value is known because $S_{30}$ has a <NoteLink to="gamma-distribution">gamma distribution</NoteLink> with shape 30 and rate 1: $0.1379$. The
approximation is good in the body. Further into the tail it degrades in relative terms: for $\prob(S_{30} \ge 40)$
the approximation gives $0.0339$ and the exact value is $0.0432$. The normal has a symmetric tail, but the sum of
exponentials inherits a right skew of $2/\sqrt{30} = 0.37$.

For this distribution $\rho = \expect\abs{X - 1}^3 = 12/e - 2 = 2.415$. The Berry–Esseen bound at $n = 30$ is
$0.4748 \times 2.415/\sqrt{30} = 0.209$, while the actual largest error is 0.024. The bound is a guarantee, not an
estimate.

## Extensions

- **Multivariate.** If $\xvec_i \in \reals^d$ are independent and identically distributed with mean $\muvec$ and
  covariance $\Sigmamat$, then $\sqrt n(\bar\xvec_n - \muvec) \to \Gauss(\zeros, \Sigmamat)$ in distribution. The
  proof applies the univariate theorem to every projection $\avec^\top\xvec_i$ (the Cramér–Wold device).
- **Not identically distributed.** For independent $X_i$ with variances $\sigma_i^2$ and $s_n^2 = \sum_{i \le n}
  \sigma_i^2$, the Lindeberg condition, $\frac{1}{s_n^2}\sum_{i \le n}\expect\brack{(X_i - \mu_i)^2
  \indicator\set{\abs{X_i - \mu_i} > \epsilon s_n}} \to 0$ for every $\epsilon > 0$, is sufficient for
  $(S_n - \expect S_n)/s_n \to \Gauss(0, 1)$. It says that no single term contributes a non-vanishing share of the
  variance <Cite id="durrett2019" />.
- **Smooth functions of means.** If $g$ is differentiable at $\mu$, then $\sqrt n(g(\bar X_n) - g(\mu)) \to
  \Gauss(0, g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2)$. This is the delta method, used for
  <NoteLink to="ratio-metrics-and-delta-method">ratio metrics</NoteLink> in experiments
  <Cite id="vandervaart1998" />.

## In machine learning

- **<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">Confidence intervals</NoteLink>.** The interval $\bar X_n \pm 1.96\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$ for a mean, a test accuracy or an
  <Gloss name="ab-test" /> effect rests on the central limit theorem.
- **Monte Carlo error.** The error of <NoteLink to="monte-carlo-integration">Monte Carlo integration</NoteLink> is
  approximately normal with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$, in any dimension.
- **Mini-batch gradients.** A mini-batch gradient is an average of per-example gradients. For moderate <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch sizes</NoteLink> its
  noise is approximately Gaussian, with covariance equal to the per-example gradient covariance divided by the batch
  size.
- **Wide networks.** At random initialisation, a pre-activation in a wide layer sums many independent terms, so it is
  approximately Gaussian. This is the starting point of the <Gloss name="gp">Gaussian-process</Gloss> limit of infinitely wide networks.

## Assumptions

- **Finite variance.** Without it the theorem fails. Sums of heavy-tailed variables with infinite variance converge,
  after other scalings, to non-Gaussian stable distributions. The <NoteLink to="cauchy-distribution">Cauchy distribution</NoteLink> is the standard example.
- **Independence**, or weak enough dependence. Central limit theorems exist for <NoteLink to="martingale">martingale</NoteLink> differences, mixing
  sequences and <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chains</NoteLink>, with the variance replaced by the long-run variance.

## Pitfalls

- **Tails converge slowest.** The approximation is in absolute error, uniformly in $z$. Relative error in the far
  tail can be large at any practical $n$, as the $\prob(S_{30} \ge 40)$ example shows.
- **"$n \ge 30$ is enough" is not a theorem.** The required $n$ depends on the skewness of one draw. The log-normal in
  the figure is visibly non-normal at $n = 60$.
- **Convergence in distribution is weak.** It does not imply that densities or moments converge, or that $S_n$ itself
  converges to anything.
