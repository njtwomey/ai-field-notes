<Definition>
Let $X$ be a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> whose <Gloss name="mgf">moment-generating function</Gloss> $M(t) = \expect[e^{tX}]$ is finite for some $t > 0$.
For every $a$, the **Chernoff bound** is

$$
\prob(X \ge a) \le \inf_{t > 0} e^{-ta}M(t) = \exp\paren{-\sup_{t > 0}\set{ta - \psi(t)}},
$$

where $\psi(t) = \log M(t)$ is the cumulant-generating function. For a sum $S_n$ of $n$ independent copies of $X$ it
becomes $\prob(S_n \ge na) \le e^{-nI(a)}$, with rate $I(a) = \sup_{t > 0}\set{ta - \psi(t)}$ <Cite id="chernoff1952" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="markov-and-chebyshev-inequalities">Markov's inequality</NoteLink> applied to $X$ uses only the mean, and Chebyshev's inequality uses only the variance. Both give
polynomial tails. Applying Markov's inequality to $e^{tX}$ uses every moment at once, weighted by $t^k/k!$. A large
$t$ weights the rare large values heavily, which sharpens the bound on the far tail. The best $t$ depends on how far
out the threshold $a$ is.

For sums the payoff is large. The moment-generating function of a sum of independent terms is the product of theirs,
so the exponent grows linearly in $n$. Tail probabilities of averages then decay exponentially in $n$, where
Chebyshev's inequality gives only $1/n$.

## Derivation

For $t > 0$ the map $x \mapsto e^{tx}$ is increasing, so the events $\set{X \ge a}$ and $\set{e^{tX} \ge e^{ta}}$ are
the same. Markov's inequality for the non-negative variable $e^{tX}$ gives

$$
\prob(X \ge a) = \prob\paren{e^{tX} \ge e^{ta}} \le \frac{\expect[e^{tX}]}{e^{ta}} = e^{-ta}M(t).
$$

This holds for each $t > 0$, so it holds for the infimum over $t$. Taking logarithms turns the infimum into
$-\sup_t\set{ta - \psi(t)}$.

Now let $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$ with the $X_i$ independent copies of $X$. Independence factorises the expectation,
$\expect[e^{tS_n}] = \prod_i\expect[e^{tX_i}] = M(t)^n$, so

$$
\prob(S_n \ge na) \le \inf_{t > 0} e^{-tna}M(t)^n = \exp\paren{-n\sup_{t > 0}\set{ta - \psi(t)}} = e^{-nI(a)}.
$$

The function $\psi$ is convex, because its second <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is the variance of $X$ under the exponentially tilted
distribution $e^{tx - \psi(t)}\,dP(x)$. For $a > \expect[X]$ the supremum is attained where $\psi'(t) = a$: the best
$t$ tilts the distribution until its mean equals the threshold. The rate $I(a)$ is then positive, and the bound
decays exponentially in $n$. The lower tail follows by applying the bound to $-X$.

## Three standard rates

**Gaussian.** For $X \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\psi(t) = \mu t + \sigma^2t^2/2$. Setting $\psi'(t) = a$ gives
$t = (a - \mu)/\sigma^2$ and

$$
I(a) = \frac{(a - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, \qquad \prob(\bar X_n \ge \mu + \epsilon) \le e^{-n\epsilon^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
$$

**Bernoulli.** For $X \sim \Bern(p)$, $\psi(t) = \log(1 - p + pe^t)$. Setting $\psi'(t) = pe^t/(1 - p + pe^t) = a$
for $p < a < 1$ gives $e^t = a(1 - p)/(p(1 - a))$. Substituting,

$$
I(a) = a\log\frac{a}{p} + (1 - a)\log\frac{1 - a}{1 - p} = \KL(\Bern(a) \,\|\, \Bern(p)).
$$

The rate for coin flips is the <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> between the coin with the
observed frequency and the true coin. The probability that $n$ flips of a $p$-coin look like an $a$-coin is at most
$e^{-n\KL(a \,\|\, p)}$.

**Multiplicative form.** Let $S$ be a sum of independent $\Bern(p_i)$ variables with mean $\mu = \sum_i p_i$. Then
for every $\delta > 0$

$$
\prob\paren{S \ge (1 + \delta)\mu} \le \paren{\frac{e^\delta}{(1 + \delta)^{1 + \delta}}}^\mu \le e^{-\delta^2\mu/3}
\quad (0 < \delta \le 1).
$$

The bound depends on the $p_i$ only through $\mu$, which makes it convenient for analysing randomised algorithms.

<Derivation title="The multiplicative bound">
Each factor satisfies $\expect[e^{tX_i}] = 1 + p_i(e^t - 1) \le \exp(p_i(e^t - 1))$, using $1 + x \le e^x$. The
product over $i$ gives $\expect[e^{tS}] \le \exp(\mu(e^t - 1))$. The Chernoff bound with threshold $(1 + \delta)\mu$ is
then $\exp(\mu(e^t - 1) - t(1 + \delta)\mu)$. The exponent is minimised at $t = \log(1 + \delta)$, which gives the
first form. For the second, the function $f(\delta) = \delta - (1 + \delta)\log(1 + \delta) + \delta^2/3$ satisfies
$f(0) = 0$ and $f'(\delta) = -\log(1 + \delta) + 2\delta/3$. Its derivative $f''(\delta) = 2/3 - 1/(1 + \delta)$ is increasing, so
$f'$ is convex. A <NoteLink to="convex-sets-and-functions">convex function</NoteLink> lies below its chord, and $f'(0) = 0$ and $f'(1) = 0.667 - 0.693 < 0$, so
$f' \le 0$ on $[0, 1]$. Hence $f(\delta) \le 0$, which is the claimed inequality.
</Derivation>

## The exponent is exact

The Chernoff bound misses the true probability only by a factor that is sub-exponential in $n$. **Cramér's theorem**
states that for independent identically distributed $X_i$ and $a > \expect[X]$,

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac1n\log\prob(S_n \ge na) = -I(a).
$$

The Chernoff bound gives the upper half of this limit. The lower half comes from tilting: under the tilted
distribution with mean $a$, the event $S_n \ge na$ has probability near $1/2$, and the likelihood ratio back to the
original distribution is about $e^{-nI(a)}$ <Cite id="durrett2019" />. Large deviations theory generalises this
result.

## Worked example: 100 fair coin flips

Let $S \sim \Binom(100, 1/2)$, the number of heads in 100 fair flips. It has mean 50 and variance 25. Compare the
bounds on $\prob(S \ge 75)$, the chance of an average of $a = 0.75$ or more.

| Method                                    | Bound on $\prob(S \ge 75)$ |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Chebyshev, $25/25^2$                      | $4.0 \times 10^{-2}$       |
| Hoeffding, $e^{-2 \cdot 100 \cdot 0.25^2}$ | $3.7 \times 10^{-6}$       |
| Chernoff, $e^{-100\KL(0.75 \,\Vert\, 0.5)}$ | $2.1 \times 10^{-6}$       |
| Exact binomial tail                       | $2.8 \times 10^{-7}$       |

The rate is $\KL(0.75 \,\|\, 0.5) = 0.75\log 1.5 + 0.25\log 0.5 = 0.1308$. The Chernoff bound is within a factor of
about 7 of the truth, where Chebyshev is off by a factor of $10^5$. The missing factor 7 is the polynomial prefactor
that Cramér's theorem ignores; it grows like $\sqrt n$.

Closer to the mean the advantage disappears. For $\prob(S \ge 60)$ the exact value is $0.028$, Chernoff gives $0.134$,
Hoeffding $0.135$ and Chebyshev $0.25$. Exponential bounds matter in the tails, where the exponent $nI(a)$ is large.

## In machine learning

- **Generalisation bounds.** The probability that the training error of a fixed classifier underestimates its true
  error by $\epsilon$ is a binomial tail, bounded by $e^{-n\KL(\hat p \,\|\, p)}$ or by its weaker <NoteLink to="positive-definite-matrices">quadratic form</NoteLink>
  $e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$. A union bound over a finite hypothesis class then gives
  <NoteLink to="probably-approximately-correct-learning">PAC</NoteLink> sample sizes.
- **Bandits.** <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-upper-confidence-bound">KL-UCB</NoteLink> sets each arm's <Gloss name="ucb">upper confidence bound</Gloss> by inverting the
  Bernoulli Chernoff bound, which is tighter than the Hoeffding-based bound of UCB1.
- **Randomised algorithms.** Load balancing, sketching and <NoteLink to="random-projections">random projections</NoteLink> are analysed with the multiplicative
  form.

## Assumptions

- **A finite moment-generating function** on some interval $t \in (0, t_0)$. Heavy-tailed variables, such as the
  log-normal or a Pareto, have $M(t) = \infty$ for every $t > 0$, and the method gives nothing.
- **Independence** for the product formula. <NoteLink to="martingale">Martingale</NoteLink> versions (Azuma–Hoeffding) replace independence with bounded
  martingale differences.

## Pitfalls

- **The bound is on one tail.** A two-sided bound adds the lower-tail bound, which doubles the probability for a
  symmetric case.
- **Computing $I(a)$ can be hard.** When the supremum has no closed form, a convenient upper bound on $\psi(t)$, such
  as <NoteLink to="hoeffdings-inequality">Hoeffding's lemma</NoteLink> $\psi(t) \le t\expect[X] + t^2(b - a)^2/8$, gives a weaker but explicit bound.
- **The bound is loose by a polynomial factor.** It is exact on the log scale per unit $n$, not in absolute terms.
