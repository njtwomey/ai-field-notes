<Definition>
An **activation function** is a fixed function $\phi : \reals \to \reals$ applied to each entry of a layer's
pre-activation $\avec = \Wmat\hvec + \bvec$ in a neural network, giving the layer's output $\phi(\avec)$. It supplies
the network's nonlinearity. Its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $\phi'$ multiplies every <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> that
<NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink> sends back through the unit.
</Definition>

## Role in the network

Without an activation, a stack of affine layers equals one affine layer; see
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">multilayer perceptron</NoteLink>. Any continuous activation that is not a
polynomial makes wide networks able to approximate any continuous function on a compact set
<Cite id="leshno1993" />. Expressive power is therefore not what separates the common choices. They differ in how
well gradients flow through them, what they cost to compute, and the range of their outputs.

The backward pass of a layer multiplies the incoming gradient elementwise by $\phi'(\avec)$:
$\partial\ell/\partial\avec = \phi'(\avec) \odot \partial\ell/\partial\hvec$. A unit whose $\phi'$ is near zero at its
current input passes almost no gradient to the weights below it. That unit is **saturated**. Through $L$ layers the
gradient is multiplied by $L$ such factors, so an activation whose derivative is small over much of its input range
makes deep networks hard to train.

## Sigmoid and tanh

The **logistic sigmoid** is $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$, with outputs in $(0, 1)$. Differentiating the quotient,

$$
\sigma'(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}
= \sigma(x)\paren{1 - \sigma(x)}.
$$

The product $\sigma(1 - \sigma)$ of two numbers that sum to 1 is largest when both equal $1/2$, at $x = 0$. The
largest derivative is therefore $\sigma'(0) = 1/4$. Through ten sigmoid layers, even at the best-case input, the
activations alone shrink a gradient by $4^{-10} \approx 10^{-6}$. For $\abs{x} = 5$ the derivative is $0.0066$.

The **hyperbolic tangent** $\tanh(x) = (e^x - e^{-x})/(e^x + e^{-x})$ is a rescaled sigmoid. Multiplying the
numerator and denominator by $e^{-x}$ gives $\tanh(x) = (1 - e^{-2x})/(1 + e^{-2x}) = 2\sigma(2x) - 1$. Its outputs lie
in $(-1, 1)$ and its derivative is

$$
\tanh'(x) = 4\sigma'(2x) = 4\sigma(2x)\paren{1 - \sigma(2x)} = 1 - \tanh^2(x),
$$

using $\sigma(2x) = (1 + \tanh x)/2$. The largest derivative is 1, at $x = 0$. Near the origin tanh behaves like the
identity, which makes it easier to initialise than the sigmoid. It still saturates: $\tanh'(3) = 0.0099$.

The outputs of a sigmoid are all positive, with mean near $1/2$. The gradient of the loss with respect to the weights
of the next unit, $\delta\,\hvec$, then has the same sign in every entry for a given example, so those weights can
only all increase or all decrease together. Zero-centred activations such as tanh avoid this, and training converges
faster <Cite id="lecun1998" />. Sigmoid and tanh remain in use where a bounded output is the point: gates in <Gloss name="lstm" plural /> and
output probabilities.

## ReLU and its variants

The **rectified linear unit** is $\mathrm{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ <Cite id="nair2010" />. Its derivative is 1 for
$x > 0$ and 0 for $x < 0$; at $x = 0$ implementations use 0. On the active side the gradient passes through unchanged
however large the input, so ReLU does not saturate there. It costs one comparison. For inputs drawn from a symmetric
distribution about zero, half the units output exactly zero, which gives sparse representations <Cite id="glorot2011" />.
Deep networks of ReLUs trained from scratch as well as networks with unsupervised pre-training did, which made ReLU the
default activation <Cite id="glorot2011" />.

A ReLU network computes a continuous piecewise-linear function. Each unit splits its input space with the hyperplane
$a = 0$, and the network is affine on each region of the resulting partition.

A unit whose pre-activation is negative for every training input receives no gradient and
never recovers. Such **dead units** appear after a large update pushes the bias far negative. Variants keep a small
gradient for $x < 0$:

- **Leaky ReLU**: $\phi(x) = x$ for $x > 0$ and $\alpha x$ otherwise, typically $\alpha = 0.01$
  <Cite id="maas2013" />.
- **ELU**: $\phi(x) = x$ for $x > 0$ and $e^x - 1$ otherwise <Cite id="clevert2016" />. Both one-sided derivatives
  equal 1 at 0, so its derivative is continuous. It saturates to $-1$ for large negative inputs, which pushes the mean
  activation towards zero.
- **Softplus**: $\phi(x) = \log(1 + e^x)$, a smooth ReLU. Its derivative is $\sigma(x)$.

## GELU and SiLU

The **Gaussian error linear unit** weights the input by the standard normal cdf $\Phi$ <Cite id="hendrycks2016" />:

$$
\mathrm{GELU}(x) = x\,\Phi(x), \qquad \mathrm{GELU}'(x) = \Phi(x) + x\,\varphi(x),
$$

where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density. It is the expected output of a stochastic unit that passes $x$ with
probability $\Phi(x)$ and outputs 0 otherwise: $\expect[x \cdot m] = x\,\Phi(x)$ for $m \sim \Bern(\Phi(x))$. Large
positive inputs pass almost unchanged and large negative inputs are almost zeroed, as in ReLU. Near zero the function
is smooth and slightly negative, with minimum $-0.170$ at $x = -0.752$. The tanh approximation
$\tfrac12 x\paren{1 + \tanh\paren{\sqrt{2/\pi}\,(x + 0.044715\,x^3)}}$ differs from the exact function by at most
$5 \times 10^{-4}$ on $[-5, 5]$.

The **sigmoid linear unit**, also called Swish, replaces $\Phi$ with the logistic sigmoid
<Cite id="elfwing2018" /> <Cite id="ramachandran2017" />:

$$
\mathrm{SiLU}(x) = x\,\sigma(x), \qquad
\mathrm{SiLU}'(x) = \sigma(x) + x\,\sigma(x)\paren{1 - \sigma(x)} = \sigma(x)\paren{1 + x\paren{1 - \sigma(x)}}.
$$

Its minimum is $-0.278$ at $x = -1.278$. Both GELU and SiLU are non-monotonic, and both have derivatives slightly above
1 for moderate positive inputs (maxima $1.129$ and $1.100$). They are the usual activations in transformers.

## Summary of properties

| Activation | Range                | $\phi'(0)$ | Largest $\phi'$ | Saturates for        |
| ---------- | -------------------- | ---------- | --------------- | -------------------- |
| sigmoid    | $(0, 1)$             | 1/4        | 1/4             | both sides           |
| tanh       | $(-1, 1)$            | 1          | 1               | both sides           |
| ReLU       | $[0, \infty)$        | 0 or 1     | 1               | $x < 0$ (exact zero) |
| leaky ReLU | $\reals$             | $\alpha$ or 1 | 1            | never                |
| ELU        | $(-1, \infty)$       | 1          | 1               | $x \to -\infty$      |
| GELU       | $[-0.170, \infty)$   | 1/2        | 1.129           | $x \to -\infty$      |
| SiLU       | $[-0.278, \infty)$   | 1/2        | 1.100           | $x \to -\infty$      |

## Assumptions

- The analysis of saturation assumes pre-activations of order 1. The scale of the pre-activations is set by the
  weights; see <NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">weight initialisation</NoteLink>, which chooses variances to keep
  them there for a given activation.

## Pitfalls

- **Sigmoid hidden layers in deep networks.** The derivative is at most 1/4, so gradients shrink geometrically with
  depth; see <NoteLink to="vanishing-and-exploding-gradients">vanishing and exploding gradients</NoteLink>.
- **Dead ReLUs.** A high <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> or a large negative bias can silence a unit permanently. Monitor the fraction of
  units that are zero for every input in a batch.
- **Mismatched initialisation.** Variance-preserving initialisations depend on the activation: ReLU zeroes half its
  input, so it needs twice the weight variance of tanh.
- **Non-differentiable points.** ReLU has no derivative at 0. Frameworks return 0 or 1 there by convention; with
  continuous inputs the point is hit with probability zero, so the choice rarely matters.
