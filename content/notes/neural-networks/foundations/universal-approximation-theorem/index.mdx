<Definition>
Let $K \subset \reals^n$ be compact and $\sigma : \reals \to \reals$ continuous. The **universal approximation
theorem** states that for every continuous $f : K \to \reals$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $N$ and
parameters $\alpha_j, b_j \in \reals$, $\wvec_j \in \reals^n$ such that

$$
g(\xvec) = \sum_{j=1}^N \alpha_j\,\sigma(\wvec_j^\top\xvec + b_j)
\quad\text{satisfies}\quad
\sup_{\xvec \in K} \abs{f(\xvec) - g(\xvec)} < \varepsilon,
$$

if and only if $\sigma$ is not a polynomial <Cite id="leshno1993" />. The function $g$ is a
<NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">multilayer perceptron</NoteLink> with one hidden layer of $N$ units and a linear
output.
</Definition>

## History

Cybenko proved the theorem in 1989 for continuous **sigmoidal** activations, meaning $\sigma(t) \to 1$ as
$t \to \infty$ and $\sigma(t) \to 0$ as $t \to -\infty$ <Cite id="cybenko1989" />. Hornik, Stinchcombe and White proved
it the same year for squashing functions <Cite id="hornik1989" />. Hornik then showed that the activation matters only
through being bounded and non-constant, and that the multilayer architecture, not the specific activation, gives the
power <Cite id="hornik1991" />. Leshno, Lin, Pinkus and Schocken closed the question: a continuous activation gives
universal approximation exactly when it is not a polynomial, which admits the unbounded <Gloss name="relu" /> <Cite id="leshno1993" />.

If $\sigma$ is a polynomial of degree $d$, every $\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)$ is
a polynomial of degree at most $d$ in $\xvec$. So is any finite sum of them. Polynomials of bounded degree form a
finite-dimensional space, which cannot approximate, for example, a polynomial of degree $d + 1$.

## A constructive argument in one dimension

Take $K = [0, 1]$, a continuous $f$, and $N$ hidden units. A continuous function on a closed interval is uniformly
continuous: its modulus of continuity $\omega(h) = \sup_{\abs{x - y} \le h} \abs{f(x) - f(y)}$ tends to 0 as
$h \to 0$.

**Step 1: a staircase.** Place knots $t_j = j/N$ for $j = 0, \dots, N$, and midpoints $s_j = (t_{j-1} + t_j)/2$.
With $H$ the unit step, define

$$
S(x) = f(t_0) + \sum_{j=1}^N \paren{f(t_j) - f(t_{j-1})}\,H(x - s_j).
$$

For $x$ between $s_j$ and $s_{j+1}$, the sum telescopes to $S(x) = f(t_j)$, and $\abs{x - t_j} \le 1/(2N)$. Therefore

$$
\abs{f(x) - S(x)} = \abs{f(x) - f(t_j)} \le \omega\paren{\tfrac{1}{2N}} \quad\text{for every } x \in [0, 1].
$$

**Step 2: sigmoids for steps.** A sigmoidal $\sigma$ approaches the step: $\sigma(k(x - s_j)) \to H(x - s_j)$ as
$k \to \infty$, uniformly on the set where $\abs{x - s_j} \ge \delta$ for any $\delta > 0$. Replacing each $H$ by a
steep sigmoid changes $S$ only within a small distance of each midpoint, and there by at most the size of that step,
which is itself at most $\omega(1/N)$. The resulting $g$ has the form in the definition, with $N$ hidden units,
$w_j = k$, $b_j = -ks_j$, $\alpha_j = f(t_j) - f(t_{j-1})$, and the constant $f(t_0)$ as the output bias.

**Step 3: take $N$ large.** Both errors are bounded by multiples of $\omega(1/N)$, which tends to 0. For any
$\varepsilon$, some $N$ makes the error below $\varepsilon$.

With ReLU units the same knots give the piecewise-linear interpolant of $f$: one unit $\max(0, x - t_j)$ per knot,
weighted by the change of slope there. For twice-differentiable $f$ the interpolation error is at most
$\max\abs{f''}/(8N^2)$.

<StepApproximation />

**Worked numbers.** For $f(x) = \sin(2\pi x)$ the Lipschitz constant is $2\pi$, so the staircase bound is
$\omega(1/(2N)) \le \pi/N$. With $N = 10$ hard steps the bound is $0.314$ and the measured largest error is $0.304$.
Ten ReLU units give the interpolant, with bound $(2\pi)^2/(8 \cdot 100) = 0.0493$ and measured error $0.0489$. Softer
sigmoids, with slope near $5N$, blur the steps into a smoother curve and reach an error of $0.054$ with ten units.

## Several inputs

In $n$ dimensions, each hidden unit is a **ridge function**: $\sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)$ varies only along
$\wvec$ and is constant on every hyperplane orthogonal to it. The argument has two stages.

1. **Sums of ridge functions are dense.** The span of $\set{\cos(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)}$ contains the constants
   ($\wvec = \zeros$) and is closed under products, since
   $2\cos u \cos v = \cos(u + v) + \cos(u - v)$. It separates points: for $\xvec \ne \yvec$, some $\wvec$ gives
   $\cos(\wvec^\top\xvec) \ne \cos(\wvec^\top\yvec)$. The Stone–Weierstrass theorem then makes it dense in the
   continuous functions on $K$. So $f$ is within $\varepsilon/2$ of a finite sum $\sum_m c_m\cos(\wvec_m^\top\xvec +
   b_m)$.
2. **Each ridge function is a one-dimensional problem.** On compact $K$, $t = \wvec_m^\top\xvec + b_m$ ranges over a
   bounded interval. The one-dimensional construction approximates $\cos t$ there by
   $\sum_j \alpha_j\sigma(k_j t + c_j)$, and $\sigma(k_j(\wvec_m^\top\xvec + b_m) + c_j)$ is a hidden unit with weight
   vector $k_j\wvec_m$. Approximating each of the finitely many cosines within $\varepsilon/(2\sum_m\abs{c_m})$ keeps
   the total error below $\varepsilon$.

The union of all these hidden units is one hidden layer. Cybenko's original proof is non-constructive and shorter.

<Derivation title="Cybenko's argument by duality">
Let $S$ be the closure in $C(K)$ of all finite sums $\sum_j \alpha_j\sigma(\wvec_j^\top\xvec + b_j)$. $S$ is a closed
linear subspace. Suppose $S \ne C(K)$.

**A functional that vanishes on $S$.** By the Hahn–Banach theorem there is a bounded linear functional $F$ on $C(K)$,
not identically zero, with $F(h) = 0$ for all $h \in S$. By the Riesz representation theorem,
$F(h) = \int_K h\,d\mu$ for a finite signed measure $\mu \ne 0$. In particular,

$$
\int_K \sigma(\wvec^\top\xvec + b)\,d\mu(\xvec) = 0 \quad\text{for all } \wvec, b.
$$

**The measure of every half-space is zero.** Fix $\wvec, b$ and $c$, and let
$\sigma_\lambda(\xvec) = \sigma(\lambda(\wvec^\top\xvec + b) + c)$. As $\lambda \to \infty$, $\sigma_\lambda$ tends to 1
on the open half-space $P = \set{\wvec^\top\xvec + b > 0}$, to 0 on the other side, and to $\sigma(c)$ on the
hyperplane $Z = \set{\wvec^\top\xvec + b = 0}$. The functions are uniformly bounded, so the bounded convergence theorem
gives $0 = \mu(P) + \sigma(c)\,\mu(Z)$ for every $c$. Since $\sigma(c)$ takes more than one value, $\mu(P) = \mu(Z) =
0$ for every half-space and hyperplane.

**The measure is zero.** Fix $\wvec$ and define, for bounded measurable $h : \reals \to \reals$, the linear functional
$G(h) = \int_K h(\wvec^\top\xvec)\,d\mu(\xvec)$. $G$ vanishes on the indicator of every interval, because
$\set{\xvec : \wvec^\top\xvec \in [\theta_1, \theta_2)}$ is a difference of half-spaces with hyperplane boundaries.
Hence $G$ vanishes on step functions, and by uniform limits on every bounded continuous $h$, including $\cos$ and
$\sin$. Therefore

$$
\int_K e^{i\wvec^\top\xvec}\,d\mu(\xvec) = 0 \quad\text{for every } \wvec \in \reals^n.
$$

The <NoteLink to="fourier-transform">Fourier transform</NoteLink> of $\mu$ is identically zero, so $\mu = 0$. This contradicts $\mu \ne 0$, so $S = C(K)$
<Cite id="cybenko1989" />.
</Derivation>

## What the theorem does not say

- **How many units.** The constructions above use a number of units that grows like $(1/\varepsilon)^n$ for a generic
  Lipschitz function of $n$ inputs. Barron showed that functions whose Fourier transform has a finite first moment
  $C_f$ can be approximated in mean square with error $O(C_f/\sqrt{N})$, with no exponential dependence on $n$
  <Cite id="barron1993" />. For general continuous functions, no such rate holds.
- **How to find the weights.** The theorem guarantees existence. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on a finite training set may not
  find the approximating weights, and a network that fits the training data may not generalise.
- **Why depth helps.** One hidden layer suffices in principle, but some functions that a deep network represents with
  few units need exponentially many units in one layer; see <NoteLink to="depth-versus-width">depth versus
  width</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- The domain $K$ is compact and the target is continuous. Outside $K$ the approximation carries no guarantee: a
  network fitted on $[0, 1]$ can be arbitrarily wrong at $x = 2$.
- The error is measured by the supremum on $K$. Versions for $L^p$ norms with respect to a finite measure also hold
  <Cite id="hornik1991" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading it as a reason to use one hidden layer.** The theorem is about expressivity in the limit of unlimited
  width. Practical networks are deep because depth reaches the same accuracy with far fewer parameters and trains
  well with modern techniques.
- **Reading it as specific to neural networks.** Every sufficiently rich model class, including polynomials of
  unbounded degree and <NoteLink to="fourier-series">Fourier series</NoteLink>, is a universal approximator. The theorem does not distinguish neural networks
  from these.
