<Definition>
A **spin glass** is an <NoteLink to="ising-model">Ising model</NoteLink>, with spins $s_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ and energy
$E(\svec) = -\sum_{i<j} J_{ij} s_i s_j$, whose couplings $J_{ij}$ are drawn at random with both signs and then held
fixed <Cite id="edwards1975" />. The **Sherrington–Kirkpatrick (SK) model** couples every pair of the $N$ spins with
independent Gaussian couplings $J_{ij} \sim \Gauss(0, J^2/N)$ <Cite id="sherrington1975" />. Competing couplings make
it impossible to satisfy every bond, and the energy has many local minima separated by high barriers.
</Definition>

## Random couplings

The name comes from dilute magnetic alloys, such as a few percent of iron in gold, in which the interaction between
two magnetic atoms oscillates in sign with their distance. Below a freezing temperature the spins of such an alloy
lock into a random-looking pattern, with no net magnetisation, which is disordered in space but frozen in time, like
the atoms of a glass. Edwards and Anderson modelled this with nearest-neighbour couplings of random sign on a lattice
<Cite id="edwards1975" />. Sherrington and Kirkpatrick made every pair interact, which gives the solvable mean-field
version <Cite id="sherrington1975" />.

The variance $J^2/N$ keeps the model well scaled as $N$ grows. The field on spin $i$ is
$h_i = \sum_{j \neq i} J_{ij} s_j$. For any fixed configuration the terms are independent with mean zero and variance
$J^2/N$, so $h_i$ has mean zero and variance $(N - 1)J^2/N \approx J^2$. The field stays of order $J$ however large
the system, and the total energy, a sum of $N$ such contributions, is of order $N$. Without the factor $1/N$ the
field would grow like $\sqrt N$ and no finite temperature would matter.

## Frustration

A bond is **satisfied** when $J_{ij} s_i s_j > 0$: the spins agree across a ferromagnetic bond or disagree across an
antiferromagnetic one. In a ferromagnet every bond is satisfied at once by aligning all the spins. In a spin glass
this is usually impossible.

Take any closed loop of bonds $i_1 \to i_2 \to \dots \to i_k \to i_1$ and multiply the bond terms around it:

$$
\prod_{\text{bonds in loop}} J_{ij} s_i s_j = \paren{\prod_{\text{bonds}} J_{ij}} \paren{\prod_{\text{nodes}} s_i^2} =
\prod_{\text{bonds}} J_{ij},
$$

because every node of the loop appears in exactly two of its bonds and $s_i^2 = 1$. The right-hand side does not
depend on the spins. If the product of the couplings is negative, the product of the bond terms is negative for
every configuration, so an odd number of the bond terms, at least one, is negative. Such a loop is **frustrated**: no
configuration satisfies all its bonds. With random signs, about half of all loops are frustrated.

Frustration has two consequences. The ground state is a compromise that leaves some bonds broken, and there are many
compromises of nearly equal energy. The energy landscape is therefore **rugged**: it has many local minima, in which
no single flip lowers the energy, separated by barriers that single-spin dynamics cross only slowly at low
temperature.

The figure enumerates all $2^{10} = 1024$ states of ten spins. With SK couplings it has several local minima at

appears together with its mirror image $-\svec$, which has the same energy because $E$ contains only products of two
spins. Starting from different states, zero-temperature dynamics end in different minima, most of them above the
ground energy.

For the SK model the expected number of local minima grows exponentially in $N$ <Cite id="mezard1987" />, and finding
the ground state of a spin glass on a general graph is NP-hard <Cite id="barahona1982" />. This is why spin glasses
became a test bed for optimisation: <NoteLink to="simulated-annealing">simulated annealing</NoteLink> was introduced
by Kirkpatrick, one of the authors of the SK model, as a way to escape shallow minima by cooling slowly
<Cite id="kirkpatrick1983" />.

## Replica symmetry breaking

The free energy of a spin glass must be averaged over the random couplings. The average of $\log Z$ is hard, and the
**replica method** computes it from the average of $Z^n$ for integer $n$, continued to $n \to 0$. Sherrington and
Kirkpatrick's replica-symmetric solution gives a negative <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> at low temperature, which is impossible for a system
of discrete spins, so it is wrong there. Giorgio Parisi found the correct solution by breaking the symmetry between
replicas <Cite id="parisi1979" />. It describes a low-temperature phase with a hierarchy of states nested within
states, each a valley of the landscape <Cite id="mezard1987" />. Parisi shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics for
this work.

## Why spin glasses matter for neural networks

A <NoteLink to="hopfield-network">Hopfield network</NoteLink> is an Ising model whose couplings are set by Hebb's rule,
$J_{ij} = \frac1N\sum_\mu \xi_i^\mu \xi_j^\mu$, from $P$ stored patterns $\xivec^\mu$. With few patterns the couplings
are dominated by each pattern's contribution, and the patterns are the deep minima. With many patterns the sum over
unrelated patterns behaves like random couplings, and the network turns into a spin glass whose minima are unrelated
to any pattern. Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky used the replica method to locate this transition at $P \approx 0.138N$
<Cite id="amit1985" />. Spin-glass theory thus supplied the storage capacity of <Gloss name="associative-memory">associative memory</Gloss>, and the idea that
a network's computation is a descent through an energy landscape.

## Worked example: the frustrated triangle

Take three spins joined in a triangle with $J_{12} = J_{13} = J_{23} = -1$, so every bond prefers its spins to
differ. The energy is $E(\svec) = s_1 s_2 + s_1 s_3 + s_2 s_3$. The product of the couplings is $(-1)^3 = -1$, so the
triangle is frustrated.

- **All three equal** ($+++$ or $---$): every product is $+1$, so $E = 3$ and all three bonds are broken.
- **One spin differs from the other two** (the other six states): the two products that involve the odd spin are
  $-1$ and the third is $+1$, so $E = -1$ and exactly one bond is broken.

The ground energy is $-1$, shared by six of the eight states, and no state reaches $-3$, the energy with every bond
satisfied. An unfrustrated triangle with $J_{ij} = +1$ has the two ground states $+++$ and $---$ at $E = -3$. At
temperature $T$ the frustrated triangle's six-fold ground state keeps an entropy of $\ln 6$ as $T \to 0$, while the
ferromagnetic triangle's falls to $\ln 2$. Degenerate ground states of this kind, many in number, are the hallmark of
frustration.

## Assumptions

- **Quenched disorder.** The couplings are drawn once and held fixed while the spins move. Averages over the
  couplings are taken of quantities such as $\log Z$ computed for each draw, not of $Z$ itself.
- **Mean-field geometry.** The SK model connects every pair of spins. Finite-dimensional spin glasses, such as the
  Edwards–Anderson model on a three-dimensional lattice, remain only partly understood.

## Pitfalls

- **Frustration is not disorder.** A regular antiferromagnet on a triangular lattice is frustrated with no randomness
  at all, and a lattice with random couplings arranged so that every loop has a positive product is equivalent to a
  ferromagnet by flipping the signs of some spins.
- **Local minima are not all equal.** Many minima lie well above the ground energy; a greedy descent almost always
  ends in one of them, which is why annealing or restarts are needed to find low-energy states.
- **Symmetric minima.** With no field, every minimum $\svec$ has a partner $-\svec$ of equal energy, so minima come in
  pairs.
