<Definition>
A **Boltzmann machine** is a network of $n$ binary units $s_i \in \set{0, 1}$ joined by symmetric weights
$w_{ij} = w_{ji}$, with $w_{ii} = 0$ and a bias $b_i$ per unit <Cite id="ackley1985" />. Its energy is

$$
E(\svec) = -\sum_{i<j} w_{ij}\, s_i s_j - \sum_i b_i\, s_i,
$$

and at temperature $T$ it defines the <Gloss name="boltzmann-distribution">Boltzmann distribution</Gloss> $p(\svec) = e^{-E(\svec)/T}/Z$. The units split into
**visible** units $\vvec$, which the data set, and **hidden** units $\hvec$, which the model infers. The model of the
data is the marginal $p(\vvec) = \sum_{\hvec} p(\vvec, \hvec)$, and learning adjusts the weights so that it matches the
data distribution.
</Definition>

## From memory to distribution

A <NoteLink to="hopfield-network">Hopfield network</NoteLink> stores chosen patterns as minima of an energy and
recalls them by descent. Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski asked the network for more: to learn, from examples alone, a
probability distribution over its visible units <Cite id="ackley1985" />. Two changes make this possible. The units
become stochastic, so the network samples states in proportion to $e^{-E/T}$ instead of settling into one minimum. And
hidden units, which the data never set, give the network extra degrees of freedom with which to build the
distribution.

## Stochastic units

Collect the terms of $E$ that contain unit $k$: $E(\svec) = -s_k \Delta_k + (\text{terms without } s_k)$, where
$\Delta_k = b_k + \sum_j w_{kj} s_j$ is the **energy gap** of unit $k$, the energy saved by turning it on. The two
values of $s_k$ given all the other units therefore have probabilities in the ratio $e^{\Delta_k/T} : 1$, so

$$
\pr(s_k = 1 \mid \text{all other units}) = \frac{e^{\Delta_k/T}}{e^{\Delta_k/T} + 1} = \sigma\paren{\frac{\Delta_k}{T}},
$$

with $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ the logistic function. Updating units one at a time with this rule is
<NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink>, and it converges to $p(\svec)$
<Cite id="geman1984" />. As $T \to 0$ the probability tends to $1$ when $\Delta_k > 0$ and $0$ when $\Delta_k < 0$: the
unit takes the state of lower energy, which is the deterministic Hopfield update. Temperature is a free scale here,
since dividing every weight by $T$ gives the same distribution at $T = 1$; the original paper used it to anneal, running
the chain at a high temperature first and cooling it, as in
<NoteLink to="simulated-annealing">simulated annealing</NoteLink>, to reach equilibrium faster
<Cite id="kirkpatrick1983" />.

## The learning rule

Learning maximises the average log-likelihood of the training vectors, which is the same as minimising the
<NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Kullback–Leibler divergence</NoteLink> from the data distribution to the
model's $p(\vvec)$. For one visible vector,

$$
\log p(\vvec) = \log\sum_{\hvec} e^{-E(\vvec, \hvec)/T} - \log Z, \qquad Z = \sum_{\vvec', \hvec'}
e^{-E(\vvec', \hvec')/T}.
$$

The energy is linear in each weight, with $\partial E/\partial w_{ij} = -s_i s_j$. Differentiating the first term,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{ij}} \log\sum_{\hvec} e^{-E(\vvec, \hvec)/T} = \frac{\sum_{\hvec} e^{-E(\vvec,\hvec)/T}\,
s_i s_j/T}{\sum_{\hvec} e^{-E(\vvec,\hvec)/T}} = \frac1T \sum_{\hvec} p(\hvec \mid \vvec)\, s_i s_j,
$$

an average over the hidden units with the visible units **clamped** to $\vvec$. The same calculation for $\log Z$ sums
over every state, giving $\frac1T\sum_{\svec} p(\svec)\, s_i s_j$, an average with the network **free-running**.
Averaging over the training data,

$$
\frac{\partial\, \expect_{\text{data}}[\log p(\vvec)]}{\partial w_{ij}} = \frac1T\paren{\langle s_i s_j
\rangle_{\text{data}} - \langle s_i s_j \rangle_{\text{model}}},
$$

where $\langle \cdot \rangle_{\text{data}}$ averages over training vectors and, for each, over $p(\hvec \mid \vvec)$.
The bias <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is the same with $s_i s_j$ replaced by $s_i$. Gradient ascent raises a weight when its two units are
on together more often in the clamped phase than in the free phase, and lowers it otherwise. The rule is **local**:
each weight needs only the statistics of the two units it joins.

The gradient is zero when every pairwise statistic of the model equals that of the data. Without hidden units, $\log
p(\vvec)$ is concave in the weights and biases, because the model is an
<NoteLink to="exponential-family">exponential family</NoteLink> with <NoteLink to="sufficient-statistic">sufficient statistics</NoteLink> $v_i$ and $v_iv_j$, and
the maximum-likelihood model is the one that matches the data's means and pairwise correlations. It captures nothing
beyond second order.

### Why hidden units are needed

Take four visible units and the **even-parity** distribution: uniform over the eight vectors with an even number of
ones. Each unit is on with probability $\frac12$. For any two units, fixing both leaves two free bits, of which exactly
two of the four settings give even parity, so each of the four pairs of values has probability $\frac{2}{8} =
\frac14$. These are the means and pairwise statistics of the uniform distribution over all 16 vectors, the model with
every weight and bias at zero. By concavity that model is the best a Boltzmann machine without hidden units can do, and
it is off by $\KL = \log\frac{1/8}{1/16} = \log 2 = 0.693$ nats. Hidden units make $p(\vvec)$ a mixture over hidden
states, no longer an exponential family, and a machine with two hidden units can learn parity.

## Why learning is slow

Each gradient step needs two expectations under distributions over exponentially many states. Ackley, Hinton and
Sejnowski estimated both by running Gibbs chains, with the visible units clamped and then free, annealed to
equilibrium and then sampled.

- **Equilibrium is expensive.** A chain must forget its start before its samples are valid. With large weights the
  energy landscape has deep, separated minima, and single-unit updates cross between them rarely, so equilibration
  time grows quickly as learning proceeds.
- **The gradient is a difference of noisy estimates.** The two averages are close when the model is good, and their
  difference is small compared with the sampling noise in each. Small <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rates</Gloss> and many samples are needed.
- **Every step needs a clamped chain per training vector.** With hidden units connected to each other, even
  $p(\hvec \mid \vvec)$ must be sampled rather than computed.

The <NoteLink to="restricted-boltzmann-machine">restricted Boltzmann machine</NoteLink> removes the last cost by
forbidding connections within a layer, which makes $p(\hvec \mid \vvec)$ exact, and <Gloss name="contrastive-divergence">contrastive divergence</Gloss> replaces
the equilibrium free phase with a few sampling steps from the data. Stacked restricted machines trained this way
revived the model two decades later, and deep Boltzmann machines with several <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">hidden layers</NoteLink> are trained with a
variational approximation for the clamped phase and persistent chains for the free phase
<Cite id="salakhutdinov2009dbm" />.

## Worked example: two visible units

Take two visible units with no hidden units and the data distribution $p(00) = p(11) = 0.4$,
$p(01) = p(10) = 0.1$: the units usually agree. By symmetry use one bias $b$ for both units, and $T = 1$. The model is
$p(\vvec) \propto \exp(w v_1 v_2 + b(v_1 + v_2))$.

- **First step from zero.** At $w = b = 0$ the model is uniform. The data give $\langle v_1 v_2\rangle_{\text{data}} =
  p(11) = 0.4$ and the model gives $0.25$, so $\partial/\partial w = 0.15 > 0$: the weight grows. Both give
  $\langle v_1 \rangle = 0.5$, so the bias gradient is zero at this point.
- **Optimum.** The model has two parameters and the symmetric data have two free probabilities, so the fit is exact.
  The odds of one unit on against both off fix the bias: $p(10)/p(00) = e^{b} = 0.25$, so $b = -\ln 4 = -1.386$. The
  odds of both on against both off fix the weight: $p(11)/p(00) = e^{w + 2b} = 1$, so $w = 2\ln 4 = 2.773$.
- **Check.** The unnormalised probabilities are $1, 0.25, 0.25, 1$ for $00, 10, 01, 11$, which normalise to $0.4, 0.1,
  0.1, 0.4$. Every statistic now matches and the gradient is zero.

The positive weight makes the units agree, and the negative biases stop them from being on too often, which the
weight alone would cause.

## Assumptions

- **Symmetric weights.** The energy, and so the Boltzmann distribution, exists only for $w_{ij} = w_{ji}$.
- **Binary units.** Real-valued data need other units, such as Gaussian visible units in a restricted Boltzmann machine.
- **Equilibrium samples.** The learning rule is the exact gradient only if both phases are sampled at equilibrium.

## Pitfalls

- **No likelihood to monitor.** $Z$ is intractable beyond a few dozen units, so the training likelihood is unknown.
  Estimates need annealed <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink> or exact enumeration on toy models.
- **Plateaus.** Starting from small symmetric weights, hidden units receive nearly the same gradient and can take a
  long time to specialise, as the parity example shows.
- **Noisy phases diverge.** A large learning rate with short, noisy free-phase chains can drive the weights to large
  values, after which the chains stop mixing and the estimates become worse.
