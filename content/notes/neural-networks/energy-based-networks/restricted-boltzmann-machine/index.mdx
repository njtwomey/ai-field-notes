<Definition>
A **restricted Boltzmann machine** (RBM) has $V$ visible units $\vvec \in \set{0,1}^V$ and $H$ hidden units
$\hvec \in \set{0,1}^H$, a weight matrix $\Wmat \in \reals^{V \times H}$ that joins every visible unit to every hidden
unit, and biases $\bvec$ and $\cvec$. There are no connections within a layer. The energy and the joint distribution
are

$$
E(\vvec, \hvec) = -\bvec^\top\vvec - \cvec^\top\hvec - \vvec^\top\Wmat\hvec, \qquad p(\vvec, \hvec) =
\frac{e^{-E(\vvec, \hvec)}}{Z}.
$$

It is a <NoteLink to="boltzmann-machine">Boltzmann machine</NoteLink> whose connection graph is bipartite. Smolensky
introduced it in 1986 as the **harmonium** <Cite id="smolensky1986" />; Hinton's contrastive divergence made it
practical to train <Cite id="hinton2002poe" />.
</Definition>

## The layers are conditionally independent

Fix the visible units. The terms of the energy that involve $\hvec$ are $\cvec^\top\hvec + \vvec^\top\Wmat\hvec =
\sum_j h_j\, a_j(\vvec)$, where $a_j(\vvec) = c_j + \wvec_j^\top\vvec$ is the input to hidden unit $j$ and $\wvec_j$ is
column $j$ of $\Wmat$. So

$$
p(\hvec \mid \vvec) \propto \exp\paren{\sum_j h_j a_j(\vvec)} = \prod_j e^{h_j a_j(\vvec)}.
$$

The distribution is a product of one factor per hidden unit, so the hidden units are independent given $\vvec$. Each
factor normalises over $h_j \in \set{0, 1}$ on its own:

$$
p(h_j = 1 \mid \vvec) = \frac{e^{a_j(\vvec)}}{1 + e^{a_j(\vvec)}} = \sigma\paren{c_j + \wvec_j^\top\vvec},
$$

with $\sigma$ the logistic function. The same argument with the roles swapped gives $p(v_i = 1 \mid \hvec) =
\sigma(b_i + \sum_j W_{ij} h_j)$. In a general Boltzmann machine the hidden units interact, and $p(\hvec \mid \vvec)$
has to be sampled; in an RBM it is one <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink> and a sigmoid.

## The free energy

Summing the hidden units out uses the same factorisation:

$$
p(\vvec) = \frac1Z \sum_{\hvec} e^{\bvec^\top\vvec + \sum_j h_j a_j(\vvec)} = \frac1Z\, e^{\bvec^\top\vvec}
\prod_{j=1}^H \sum_{h_j \in \set{0,1}} e^{h_j a_j(\vvec)} = \frac1Z\, e^{\bvec^\top\vvec} \prod_{j=1}^H \paren{1 +
e^{a_j(\vvec)}}.
$$

Writing $p(\vvec) = e^{-F(\vvec)}/Z$ defines the **free energy**

$$
F(\vvec) = -\bvec^\top\vvec - \sum_{j=1}^H \log\paren{1 + e^{c_j + \wvec_j^\top\vvec}},
$$

which costs $O(VH)$ to compute. The marginal is a product of one factor per hidden unit, a **product of experts**: each
hidden unit raises the probability of the visible vectors that match its weight vector, and a vector is likely only if
many experts approve of it. $Z$ still sums over $2^V$ visible vectors or $2^H$ hidden vectors and is intractable when
both layers are large.

## The likelihood gradient

Differentiating $\log p(\vvec) = -F(\vvec) - \log Z$ as for any Boltzmann machine gives

$$
\frac{\partial \log p(\vvec)}{\partial W_{ij}} = v_i\, \sigma(a_j(\vvec)) - \expect_{\vvec' \sim p}\brack{v'_i\,
\sigma(a_j(\vvec'))},
$$

and similarly $\partial/\partial b_i = v_i - \expect[v'_i]$ and $\partial/\partial c_j = \sigma(a_j(\vvec)) -
\expect[\sigma(a_j(\vvec'))]$. The first term, the <NoteLink to="energy-based-models">positive phase</NoteLink>, is exact and cheap. The second, the negative phase,
needs samples from the model.

## Block Gibbs sampling

<NoteLink to="independence">Conditional independence</NoteLink> makes each Gibbs step update a whole layer at once: draw $\hvec \sim p(\hvec \mid \vvec)$,
then $\vvec \sim p(\vvec \mid \hvec)$, each as independent <NoteLink to="bernoulli-distribution">coin flips</NoteLink>. One step costs two matrix products. The chain
still needs many steps to reach equilibrium from an arbitrary start, and many more to move between well-separated
modes.

## Contrastive divergence

**Contrastive divergence** with $k$ steps (CD-$k$) replaces the equilibrium sample of the negative phase by a short
chain started at the training vector <Cite id="hinton2002poe" />:

1. Set $\vvec^{(0)}$ to a training vector and compute $p(\hvec \mid \vvec^{(0)})$.
2. Run $k$ block Gibbs steps to reach $\vvec^{(k)}$.
3. Update $\Delta W_{ij} = \eta\paren{v^{(0)}_i\, \sigma(a_j(\vvec^{(0)})) - v^{(k)}_i\, \sigma(a_j(\vvec^{(k)}))}$, and
   the biases likewise, averaged over a mini-batch.

The idea is that a model which fits the data leaves the data distribution unchanged under <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink>, so a few
steps from the data are enough to see which way the model is pulling it. Starting at the data also removes most of the
variance of the estimate, since the chain begins where the probability mass should be. The price is bias. Hinton
showed that CD-$k$ approximately follows the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of $\KL(p_0 \| p_\infty) - \KL(p_k \| p_\infty)$, with $p_0$ the
data distribution, $p_k$ the distribution after $k$ steps and $p_\infty$ the model, not the gradient of the likelihood
<Cite id="hinton2002poe" />. Its fixed points are close to, but not at, the maximum-likelihood solution
<Cite id="carreira2005" />, and on small problems the likelihood of a CD-1 model can rise and then fall as training
continues <Cite id="fischer2010" />. Larger $k$ reduces the bias.

**Persistent contrastive divergence** (PCD) keeps its negative chains between updates instead of restarting them at
the data <Cite id="tieleman2008" />. The model changes little per update, so the chains stay near equilibrium and
explore regions far from the data, which CD never visits. It needs a small <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss>: if the model changes faster
than the chains can follow, the negative phase is stale and training oscillates.

filters of a trained model pick out parts of rows and columns, and the Gibbs chains turn random pixels into bars and
stripes.

## Worked example: one CD-1 update

Take $V = 2$ visible units and $H = 1$ hidden unit, with weights $\Wmat = (1, 1)^\top$, biases $\bvec = (0, 0)$ and
$c = -1$, and the training vector $\vvec = (1, 1)$. Use a learning rate $\eta = 0.1$.

- **Positive phase.** $a(\vvec) = -1 + 1 + 1 = 1$, so $p(h = 1 \mid \vvec) = \sigma(1) = 0.731$.
- **One Gibbs step.** Suppose the hidden sample is $h = 1$. Then $p(v_i = 1 \mid h = 1) = \sigma(0 + 1) = 0.731$ for
  both units, and suppose the visible sample is $\vvec^{(1)} = (1, 0)$. Its input is $a(\vvec^{(1)}) = -1 + 1 = 0$, so
  $p(h = 1 \mid \vvec^{(1)}) = 0.5$.
- **Update.** $\Delta W_1 = 0.1(1 \times 0.731 - 1 \times 0.5) = 0.023$, $\Delta W_2 = 0.1(1 \times 0.731 - 0 \times
  0.5) = 0.073$, $\Delta \bvec = 0.1\paren{(1, 1) - (1, 0)} = (0, 0.1)$ and $\Delta c = 0.1(0.731 - 0.5) = 0.023$.
- **Effect on the free energy.** Before the update $F(1, 1) = -\log(1 + e^{1}) = -1.313$ and
  $F(1, 0) = -\log(1 + e^{0}) = -0.693$, a gap of $0.620$. After it $F(1, 1) = -1.502$ and $F(1, 0) = -0.717$, a gap
  of $0.785$. The update lowered the free energy of the training vector more than that of the chain's sample, so the
  training vector gained probability relative to it.

## Uses

RBMs were the building block of <NoteLink to="deep-belief-network">deep belief networks</NoteLink>, whose layer-wise
pretraining started the revival of deep learning in 2006. They also served as feature extractors, as <Gloss name="collaborative-filtering">collaborative
filtering</Gloss> models over ratings, and as models of motion and documents, with Gaussian, softmax or count-valued visible
units in place of binary ones <Cite id="hinton2012practical" />.

## Assumptions

- **Binary units.** The derivation above assumes $\set{0, 1}$ units on both layers. Other unit types change the
  conditionals but keep the bipartite factorisation.
- **Equilibrium is not reached.** CD-$k$ and PCD are approximations whose quality depends on $k$, the learning rate
  and how well the chains mix.

## Pitfalls

- **Reconstruction error is not likelihood.** The error of one-step reconstructions is easy to compute and often
  reported, but it can keep falling while the likelihood falls too <Cite id="hinton2012practical" />.
- **CD makes spurious modes invisible.** Regions of high model probability far from the data are never reached by
  chains started at the data, so CD does not remove them. Samples from long chains reveal them.
- **Hidden-unit symmetry.** Starting all weights at zero gives every hidden unit the same gradient forever. Initial
  weights need small random values.
