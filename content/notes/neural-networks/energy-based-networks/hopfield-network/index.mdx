<Definition>
A **Hopfield network** is a recurrent network of $N$ binary units $s_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ with symmetric weights
$w_{ij} = w_{ji}$ and no self-connections, $w_{ii} = 0$ <Cite id="hopfield1982" />. It stores $P$ patterns
$\xivec^1, \dots, \xivec^P \in \set{-1, +1}^N$ with the Hebbian rule $w_{ij} = \frac1N \sum_{\mu} \xi^\mu_i \xi^\mu_j$
for $i \neq j$. It recalls a pattern from a partial or noisy cue by **asynchronous updates**: pick a unit $i$ and set
$s_i \leftarrow \sgn(h_i)$, where $h_i = \sum_j w_{ij} s_j$ is its **local field**. Each update keeps or lowers the
energy $E(\svec) = -\frac12 \sum_{i,j} w_{ij} s_i s_j$, and recall stops at a state that no single update changes.
</Definition>

## Memory addressed by content

A computer memory is addressed by location: give it an address and it returns what is stored there. An **associative**
or **content-addressable** memory is addressed by the content itself: give it part of a stored item, or a corrupted
copy, and it returns the whole item. John Hopfield showed in 1982 that a network of simple threshold units can do this
with no central control, because its dynamics descend an energy function whose minima are the stored items
<Cite id="hopfield1982" />. Similar networks had been studied before, notably by Amari <Cite id="amari1972" />, but
the energy function made the collective behaviour analysable with the physics of the
<NoteLink to="ising-model">Ising model</NoteLink>: the units are spins, the weights are couplings, and recall is
relaxation to a low-energy state.

## Storing patterns with Hebb's rule

Donald Hebb proposed in 1949 that a synapse strengthens when the neurons on both sides are active together
<Cite id="hebb1949" />. The storage rule

$$
\Wmat = \frac1N \sum_{\mu=1}^P \xivec^\mu {\xivec^\mu}^\top, \qquad \text{with the diagonal set to zero},
$$

is that idea for $\pm1$ units: the weight $w_{ij}$ grows by $1/N$ for every pattern in which units $i$ and $j$ agree
and falls by $1/N$ for every pattern in which they disagree. Storing a pattern needs one pass and no iteration, and
adding a pattern adds one <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">outer product</NoteLink>. The factor $1/N$ keeps the local fields of order one.

## Recall and the energy

With a bias $b_i$ per unit the energy is $E(\svec) = -\frac12\svec^\top\Wmat\svec - \bvec^\top\svec$ and the local
field is $h_i = \sum_j w_{ij}s_j + b_i$; the Hebbian network has $\bvec = \zeros$. Recall starts from the cue and
repeats the update $s_i \leftarrow \sgn(h_i)$ at units chosen in turn, in a fixed or random order. A unit whose field
is exactly zero is left unchanged.

### The energy never increases

Pick one unit $k$ and collect the terms of $E$ that contain $s_k$. In $-\frac12\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}s_is_j$ they are the
terms with $i = k$ and those with $j = k$ (the term $i = j = k$ is zero because $w_{kk} = 0$). By symmetry the two
groups are equal, so

$$
E(\svec) = -s_k \sum_{j} w_{kj} s_j - b_k s_k + (\text{terms without } s_k) = -s_k h_k + (\text{terms without } s_k).
$$

The field $h_k$ does not contain $s_k$ because $w_{kk} = 0$, so changing $s_k$ to $s_k'$ changes the energy by

$$
\Delta E = -(s_k' - s_k)\, h_k.
$$

The update sets $s_k' = \sgn(h_k)$. If this changes the unit, then $s_k' - s_k = 2\sgn(h_k)$ and
$\Delta E = -2\abs{h_k} < 0$. If it does not, $\Delta E = 0$. The energy never increases.

The network has $2^N$ states, a finite number, and every change of state strictly lowers the energy, so no state can
be visited twice. Recall therefore stops after finitely many changes, at a **fixed point**: a state in which every
unit agrees with the sign of its field, $s_i h_i \ge 0$ for all $i$. A fixed point is a local minimum of $E$ with
respect to single flips, since a flip of unit $i$ would change the energy by $2 s_i h_i \ge 0$.

The proof uses both conditions on the weights. Asymmetric weights break the step that equates the two groups of
terms, and such networks can cycle forever. **Synchronous** updates, which update every unit at once from the old
state, can also fail to converge: they can end in a cycle of two states that alternate.

## Why the stored patterns are fixed points

Set the network to a stored pattern, $\svec = \xivec^\nu$, and compute the field of unit $i$:

$$
h_i = \frac1N \sum_{\mu}\xi^\mu_i \sum_{j \neq i}\xi^\mu_j \xi^\nu_j = \frac{N - 1}{N}\,\xi^\nu_i +
\frac1N\sum_{\mu \neq \nu}\xi^\mu_i\sum_{j \neq i}\xi^\mu_j\xi^\nu_j.
$$

The first term, from $\mu = \nu$, is the **signal**: it points in the direction of the stored bit. The second term is
the **crosstalk** from the other patterns. Unit $i$ is stable when $\xi^\nu_i h_i > 0$, that is when the crosstalk
multiplied by $\xi^\nu_i$,

$$
C_i = \frac1N \sum_{\mu \neq \nu}\sum_{j \neq i} \xi^\nu_i\xi^\mu_i\xi^\mu_j\xi^\nu_j,
$$

exceeds $-(N-1)/N \approx -1$. For random patterns, with every bit an independent fair coin, $C_i$ is a sum of
$(P-1)(N-1)$ independent terms, each $\pm 1/N$ with equal probability. It has mean zero and variance
$(P-1)(N-1)/N^2 \approx P/N$, and by the <NoteLink to="central-limit-theorem">central limit theorem</NoteLink> it is
close to Gaussian. The probability that a given bit of a stored pattern is unstable is therefore about

$$
\pr\paren{C_i < -1} \approx \Phi\paren{-\sqrt{N/P}},
$$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function. With $P = 0.05N$ this is $\Phi(-4.47) = 4 \times 10^{-6}$;
with $P = 0.138N$ it is $\Phi(-2.69) = 0.0036$; with $P = 0.5N$ it is $\Phi(-1.41) = 0.079$.

The estimate describes only the first update. Each flipped bit changes the fields of all the others, and errors can
cascade. Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky analysed the full dynamics with the replica method of spin-glass theory and
found that retrieval states exist up to $P \approx 0.138N$ <Cite id="amit1985" />. At that load a retrieved pattern
still has about $1.6\%$ of its bits wrong; beyond it the retrieval states vanish abruptly and the network becomes a
<NoteLink to="spin-glass">spin glass</NoteLink> whose minima are unrelated to the patterns. If every bit of every
pattern must be a fixed point with high probability, the number of patterns can grow only in proportion to
$N/\ln N$ <Cite id="mceliece1987" />.

## Memories as attractors

Each stored pattern sits at the bottom of a **basin of attraction**: the set of states from which the dynamics end
there. A cue anywhere inside the basin is completed to the pattern. The figure below enumerates every state of a

fixed point.

## Spurious states

The minima of $E$ include states that were never stored.

- **Reversed patterns.** $E(-\svec) = E(\svec)$ because $E$ is quadratic in $\svec$, and $h_i(-\svec) = -h_i(\svec)$,
  so $-\xivec^\mu$ is a fixed point whenever $\xivec^\mu$ is. A cue closer to the reversed pattern recalls the
  reversed pattern.
- **Mixture states.** The bitwise majority of three patterns, $s_i = \sgn(\xi^1_i + \xi^2_i + \xi^3_i)$, is also a
  fixed point. For random patterns, $\xi^1_i$ agrees with the majority unless both other patterns disagree with it,
  which happens with probability $1/4$. The overlap $m^\mu = \frac1N \xivec^\mu \cdot \svec$ with each of the three
  patterns is therefore about $\tfrac34 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$, and the field is
  $h_i \approx \sum_\mu \xi^\mu_i m^\mu \approx \frac12(\xi^1_i + \xi^2_i + \xi^3_i)$, whose sign is $s_i$. Every unit
  agrees with its field, so the mixture is stable. Its energy, about $-\frac N2 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac14 = -\frac{3N}{8}$,
  is above the $-\frac N2$ of a stored pattern, so its basin is shallower.
- **Spin-glass states.** Near and beyond capacity, the crosstalk creates many minima unrelated to any pattern.

## Worked example: one pattern, one correction

Take $N = 4$ units and one stored pattern $\xivec = (+1, +1, -1, -1)$. The weights are $w_{ij} = \xi_i\xi_j/4$ for
$i \neq j$. Start from the cue $\svec = (+1, -1, -1, -1)$, which differs from $\xivec$ in unit 2.

- **Field on unit 2.** $h_2 = \frac14\paren{\xi_2\xi_1 s_1 + \xi_2\xi_3 s_3 + \xi_2\xi_4 s_4} = \frac14\paren{1 + 1 +
  1} = 0.75$, so the update sets $s_2 = +1$ and the cue becomes $\xivec$.
- **Energy before and after.** Write $a_i = \xi_i s_i$. Then $E = -\frac14\sum_{i<j} a_i a_j =
  -\frac18\paren{(\textstyle\sum_i a_i)^2 - 4}$. Before the update $\avec = (1, -1, 1, 1)$, so $\sum_i a_i = 2$ and
  $E = 0$. After it $\avec = (1, 1, 1, 1)$, so $\sum_i a_i = 4$ and $E = -1.5$.
- **Check.** $\Delta E = -1.5 = -2\abs{h_2}$, as the descent argument predicts.
- **Other units.** At $\xivec$ every field is $h_i = \frac34 \xi_i$, which agrees with $s_i$, so $\xivec$ is a fixed
  point. So is $-\xivec$, with the same energy $-1.5$.

## Variants

- **Continuous units.** Hopfield's 1984 network uses graded outputs $V_i = g(u_i)$ with a sigmoid $g$ and continuous
  time dynamics, and has an energy with the same descent property <Cite id="hopfield1984" />. It was used for
  optimisation problems such as the travelling salesman.
- **$\set{0, 1}$ units.** The 1982 paper uses units that are on or off with thresholds. The substitution
  $s_i = 2V_i - 1$ maps it onto the $\pm1$ form with biases.
- **Stochastic units.** Replacing $\sgn(h_i)$ by a random choice with $\pr(s_i = +1) = 1/(1 + e^{-2h_i/T})$ gives
  <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink> of the <Gloss name="boltzmann-distribution">Boltzmann distribution</Gloss> $e^{-E/T}$; with hidden units
  and learned weights this is a <NoteLink to="boltzmann-machine">Boltzmann machine</NoteLink>.
- **Modern Hopfield networks.** Energies that grow faster than quadratically in the overlaps store far more patterns,
  and their continuous version is attention; see <NoteLink to="modern-hopfield-network">modern Hopfield
  networks</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- **Random, unbiased patterns.** The capacity $0.138N$ assumes independent bits with equal probability of $\pm1$.
- **Symmetric weights with zero diagonal and asynchronous updates.** These guarantee convergence to a fixed point.
- **The cue is near its pattern.** A cue outside the pattern's basin is completed to something else.

## Pitfalls

- **Correlated patterns interfere.** Patterns that share many bits, such as letters drawn on a grid, have large
  overlaps, so their crosstalk is far larger than for random patterns. A network of 100 units may fail with only four
  letters stored, far below $0.138 \times 100 = 14$.
- **Fixed points include spurious states.** Convergence says only that the state is a local minimum. Check the overlap with
  every stored pattern to tell a memory from a spurious state.
- **Capacity is set by $N$.** The number of storable patterns grows only linearly with the number of units, and each
  pattern costs $N^2$ weights.
- **Updates must be asynchronous.** Updating every unit at once from the same old state can oscillate between two
  states.
