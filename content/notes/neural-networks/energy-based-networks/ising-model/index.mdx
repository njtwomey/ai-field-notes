<Definition>
The **Ising model** places a **spin** $s_i \in \set{-1, +1}$ on each of the $N$ nodes of a graph. A configuration
$\svec = (s_1, \dots, s_N)$ has the **energy**

$$
E(\svec) = -\sum_{i<j} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j - \sum_i h_i\, s_i,
$$

where the **coupling** $J_{ij}$ is non-zero only for pairs joined by an edge and $h_i$ is an external **field** at node
$i$. At **temperature** $T > 0$ the probability of a configuration is the
**<Gloss name="boltzmann-distribution">Boltzmann distribution</Gloss>** $p(\svec) = e^{-E(\svec)/T}/Z$, with the
normalising constant $Z = \sum_{\svec} e^{-E(\svec)/T}$ summed over all $2^N$ configurations <Cite id="ising1925" />.
</Definition>

## Spins, couplings and energy

Wilhelm Lenz proposed the model in 1920 as a caricature of a magnet, and his student Ernst Ising solved the
one-dimensional chain in 1925 <Cite id="brush1967" />. Each spin is a tiny magnet that points up ($+1$) or down
($-1$). A positive coupling $J_{ij} > 0$ is **ferromagnetic**: the bond contributes $-J_{ij}$ to the energy when
$s_i = s_j$ and $+J_{ij}$ when they differ, so aligned neighbours have lower energy. A negative coupling is
**antiferromagnetic** and favours opposite neighbours. A positive field $h_i$ lowers the energy of $s_i = +1$ by
$2h_i$ relative to $s_i = -1$.

The standard case is the **square lattice**: $N = L \times L$ spins on a grid with periodic edges, $J_{ij} = J$ for
the four nearest neighbours of each spin and zero otherwise, and a uniform field $h$. Each spin has four bonds and
each bond joins two spins, so the lattice has $2N$ bonds. With $J = 1$ and $h = 0$ the two **ground states**, all
spins $+1$ or all $-1$, have energy $-2N$, or $-2$ per spin. A single flipped spin in a ground state breaks its four
bonds and raises the energy by $8$.

## The Boltzmann distribution

The temperature sets how strongly the distribution prefers low energy. The ratio of the probabilities of two
configurations is

$$
\frac{p(\svec)}{p(\svec')} = e^{-(E(\svec) - E(\svec'))/T},
$$

which does not involve $Z$. As $T \to 0$ the ratio for any configuration above the ground energy tends to zero, and
the distribution concentrates on the ground states. As $T \to \infty$ every ratio tends to $1$, and the distribution
tends to uniform over all $2^N$ configurations. Temperature is measured in units of energy here (Boltzmann's constant
is set to $1$).

<Derivation title="Why the Boltzmann form: maximum entropy at fixed mean energy">
Among all distributions $p$ over configurations with a given mean energy $\sum_{\svec} p(\svec) E(\svec) = \bar E$,
the one with the largest <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> $-\sum_{\svec} p(\svec)\log p(\svec)$ is the
Boltzmann distribution. Introduce multipliers $\lambda$ for the energy constraint and $\mu$ for normalisation. The
Lagrangian is

$$
\Lcal = -\sum_{\svec} p(\svec)\log p(\svec) - \lambda\paren{\sum_{\svec} p(\svec)E(\svec) - \bar E} -
\mu\paren{\sum_{\svec} p(\svec) - 1}.
$$

Setting $\partial\Lcal/\partial p(\svec) = -\log p(\svec) - 1 - \lambda E(\svec) - \mu = 0$ gives
$p(\svec) = e^{-1-\mu}\, e^{-\lambda E(\svec)}$. Normalisation fixes $e^{1+\mu} = Z$, and the multiplier $\lambda$
is the inverse temperature $1/T$, chosen so that the mean energy equals $\bar E$. The entropy is concave in $p$ and
the constraints are linear, so this stationary point is the maximum.
</Derivation>

## Magnetisation and the phase transition

The **magnetisation** per spin is $m = \frac1N\sum_i s_i \in [-1, 1]$. With $h = 0$ the energy is unchanged when
every spin is flipped, so $\svec$ and $-\svec$ are equally likely and the expected magnetisation of any finite system
is exactly zero. The informative quantity is the typical size $\abs{m}$, or the magnetisation that survives when an
infinitesimal field $h \to 0^+$ breaks the tie after the system size $N \to \infty$. This limit, the **spontaneous
magnetisation**, is non-zero at low temperature on the square lattice and zero at high temperature. The change
happens at a sharp **critical temperature** $T_c$, the model's **phase transition**.

### Mean-field theory: why a transition appears

A single spin in a fixed field $H$ has two states with energies $\mp H$, so

$$
\expect[s] = \frac{e^{H/T} - e^{-H/T}}{e^{H/T} + e^{-H/T}} = \tanh\paren{\frac{H}{T}}.
$$

In the lattice the field felt by spin $i$ is $J\sum_{j \in \text{nb}(i)} s_j + h$, which fluctuates. **Mean-field
theory** replaces each neighbour by its average $m$, so the field is $4Jm + h$ on the square lattice. Requiring the
spin's own average to equal $m$ gives the self-consistency equation

$$
m = \tanh\paren{\frac{4Jm + h}{T}}.
$$

At $h = 0$, $m = 0$ is always a solution. The right-hand side has slope $4J/T$ at $m = 0$ and flattens towards
$\pm 1$, so it crosses the line $y = m$ at two further points $\pm m^* \neq 0$ exactly when the slope exceeds $1$,
that is when $T < 4J$. Below this temperature the aligned solutions have lower free energy and the system magnetises.
Mean-field theory therefore predicts a transition at $T = 4J$. It overestimates $T_c$ because it ignores the
fluctuations of the neighbours, which disorder the system more easily than a fixed average field would.

### Onsager's exact result

Lars Onsager solved the two-dimensional model with $h = 0$ exactly in 1944 <Cite id="onsager1944" />. On the square
lattice with coupling $J = 1$ the critical temperature is

$$
T_c = \frac{2}{\ln(1 + \sqrt2)} \approx 2.269,
$$

and C. N. Yang derived the spontaneous magnetisation below it <Cite id="yang1952" />:

$$
m(T) = \paren{1 - \sinh^{-4}(2/T)}^{1/8}, \qquad T < T_c.
$$

The exponent $1/8$ makes $m$ fall steeply to zero as $T \to T_c$. At $T_c$ itself, clusters of aligned spins of every
size appear, and the correlation between distant spins decays as a power of their distance rather than
exponentially. The one-dimensional chain has no transition at any $T > 0$: a single broken bond splits the chain into
two domains at a fixed energy cost of $2J$, while it can sit in any of $N$ places, so for large $N$ the entropy of the
broken bonds always wins.

## Simulating the model: Metropolis and Glauber dynamics

$Z$ is a sum over $2^N$ terms, so expectations on a lattice of thousands of spins cannot be computed by enumeration.
They are estimated by a <NoteLink to="markov-chain">Markov chain</NoteLink> whose stationary distribution is $p(\svec)$,
a form of <NoteLink to="markov-chain-monte-carlo">Markov chain Monte Carlo</NoteLink>. Both standard chains change one
spin at a time. Flipping spin $i$ changes only the terms that contain $s_i$, so the energy change is

$$
\Delta E_i = 2 s_i \paren{\sum_{j \in \text{nb}(i)} J_{ij} s_j + h_i},
$$

computed from the four neighbours alone.

- **Metropolis dynamics** <Cite id="metropolis1953" /> picks a site and flips it with probability
  $\min(1, e^{-\Delta E_i/T})$. Flips that lower the energy are always made.
- **Glauber dynamics** <Cite id="glauber1963" /> picks a site and flips it with probability $1/(1 + e^{\Delta E_i/T})$.
  This is the same as drawing $s_i$ afresh from its <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional
  distribution</NoteLink> given its neighbours, which is <NoteLink to="gibbs-sampling">Gibbs sampling</NoteLink>;
  physicists call it the heat-bath algorithm.

Both rules satisfy **detailed balance** with respect to $p$: for two configurations $\svec$ and $\svec'$ that differ
only at site $i$, with $\Delta E = E(\svec') - E(\svec)$, the probability flow from $\svec$ to $\svec'$ equals the
flow back. For Glauber dynamics,

$$
p(\svec)\, \frac{1}{1 + e^{\Delta E/T}} = \frac{e^{-E(\svec)/T}}{Z}\, \frac{e^{-\Delta E/T}}{e^{-\Delta E/T} + 1} =
\frac{e^{-E(\svec')/T}}{Z}\, \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\Delta E/T}} = p(\svec')\, \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\Delta E/T}},
$$

where the first step multiplies the numerator and denominator by $e^{-\Delta E/T}$ and the second uses
$E(\svec) + \Delta E = E(\svec')$. The right-hand side is the flow from $\svec'$ back to $\svec$, whose flip costs
$-\Delta E$. For Metropolis with $\Delta E > 0$, the forward flow is $p(\svec)e^{-\Delta E/T} = p(\svec')$ and the
backward flip is always accepted, so the backward flow is $p(\svec') \cdot 1$. Detailed balance makes $p$ stationary,
and because any configuration can reach any other by single flips, the chain converges to $p$. One **sweep** is $N$
single-spin updates, so each spin is visited once on average.

<IsingLattice />

Near $T_c$ the dynamics slow down sharply: large domains need many sweeps to change, a phenomenon called critical
slowing down. Below $T_c$ a random start coarsens into domains of up and down spins whose walls shrink slowly, and a
finite lattice can stay in a striped state for thousands of sweeps. A field opposed to an ordered state leaves it
**metastable**: the state persists until a large enough droplet of the favoured phase forms by chance and then grows.

## Worked example: two spins and one Metropolis step

Take two spins joined by a coupling $J = 1$ with no field. The four configurations have energies
$E(+,+) = E(-,-) = -1$ and $E(+,-) = E(-,+) = +1$. The normalising constant is
$Z = 2e^{1/T} + 2e^{-1/T} = 4\cosh(1/T)$, and the correlation of the two spins is

$$
\expect[s_1 s_2] = \frac{2e^{1/T} - 2e^{-1/T}}{4\cosh(1/T)} = \tanh(1/T).
$$

At $T = 1$ the correlation is $\tanh 1 = 0.762$. At $T = 10$ it is $\tanh 0.1 = 0.0997$: the spins are almost
independent.

Now take one spin of the square lattice at $T = 2.269$ with $J = 1$ and $h = 0$. The spin is $+1$ and all four
neighbours are $+1$. Flipping it costs $\Delta E = 2 \times 1 \times 4 = 8$, so Metropolis accepts the flip with
probability $e^{-8/2.269} = 0.0294$ and Glauber with probability $1/(1 + e^{8/2.269}) = 0.0286$. If instead two
neighbours are $+1$ and two are $-1$, then $\Delta E = 0$: Metropolis always flips the spin and Glauber flips it with
probability $1/2$.

## From magnets to neural networks

Read each spin as a neuron that fires ($+1$) or is silent ($-1$), each coupling as a synaptic weight and each field
as a bias. The zero-temperature dynamics, which set each spin to the sign of its local field, are then the update
rule of a <NoteLink to="hopfield-network">Hopfield network</NoteLink>, and the couplings can be chosen to make chosen
patterns the ground states. With random couplings of both signs the model becomes a
<NoteLink to="spin-glass">spin glass</NoteLink>, with many local minima. Learning the couplings and fields so that the
Boltzmann distribution matches data is the inverse Ising problem, which the
<NoteLink to="boltzmann-machine">Boltzmann machine</NoteLink> solves with hidden units added. As a probabilistic
model, the Ising model is a pairwise <NoteLink to="markov-random-field">Markov random field</NoteLink> over binary
variables.

## Assumptions

- **Spins are binary and interactions pairwise.** Models with more spin values (the Potts model) or continuous spins
  (the XY and Heisenberg models) behave differently; the XY model in two dimensions has no spontaneous magnetisation
  at any $T > 0$.
- **Couplings are fixed.** The Ising model describes a given $J$; the couplings are not learned.
- **Equilibrium.** The Boltzmann distribution describes a system that has had time to equilibrate. Measurements taken
  before the chain mixes describe the dynamics, not the distribution.

## Pitfalls

- **Finite lattices have no sharp transition.** $\abs{m}$ falls smoothly through $T_c$ on a finite lattice, and the
  apparent transition shifts with $L$. The exact $T_c$ is a property of the infinite lattice.
- **$\expect[m] = 0$ at $h = 0$.** Averaging $m$ over a long enough run gives zero at every temperature, because the
  chain eventually flips between the two ordered states. Average $\abs{m}$ or $m^2$ instead.
- **Units of temperature.** Some texts write $e^{-\beta E}$ with $\beta = 1/(k_B T)$, or fold $J$ into $T$; the value
  $T_c \approx 2.269$ holds for $J = 1$ and $k_B = 1$ and scales with $J$.
- **Correlated samples.** Successive configurations of a single-spin chain are strongly correlated, especially near
  $T_c$. Estimates need many sweeps between the samples used, or a cluster algorithm that flips whole domains.
