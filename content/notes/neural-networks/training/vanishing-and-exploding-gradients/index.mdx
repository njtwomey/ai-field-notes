<Definition>
In a network of $L$ layers, the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the loss $\Lcal$ with respect to the activations $\hvec_\ell$ of layer $\ell$ is
the gradient at the output multiplied by the <NoteLink to="jacobian">Jacobians</NoteLink> of every layer above $\ell$. The gradient **vanishes** when this
product shrinks geometrically with the number of layers, so that the lower layers receive updates too small to learn
from. It **explodes** when the product grows geometrically, so that updates are large enough to destroy what has been
learned or to overflow <Cite id="bengio1994" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="backpropagation">Backpropagation</NoteLink> passes the gradient down through the layers by the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain
rule</NoteLink>. Each layer multiplies it by a matrix. A product of $L$ numbers that are each around $0.8$ is $0.8^{L}$, which is
$0.0012$ for $L = 30$. A product of $L$ numbers around $1.2$ is $237$ for $L = 30$. Matrices behave like numbers here in
an average sense: each acts as the factor by which it stretches or shrinks a typical vector. Only a per-layer factor very
close to 1 gives a gradient whose size does not depend on depth.

The same product governs the forward pass. Activations at depth $L$ are the input transformed by $L$ layers, and they
too shrink or grow geometrically. The gradient problem is the backward face of a general fact: in a deep composition,
per-layer scale factors compound.

## The Jacobian product

Take a <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">multilayer perceptron</NoteLink> with pre-activations
$\avec_\ell = \Wmat_\ell\hvec_{\ell-1}$ and activations $\hvec_\ell = \phi(\avec_\ell)$ for $\ell = 1, \dots, L$. The
Jacobian of one layer is

$$
\Jmat_\ell = \frac{\partial\hvec_\ell}{\partial\hvec_{\ell-1}} = \diag\paren{\phi'(\avec_\ell)}\,\Wmat_\ell,
$$

because $\partial\avec_\ell/\partial\hvec_{\ell-1} = \Wmat_\ell$ and the elementwise $\phi$ contributes a diagonal
matrix of derivatives. By the chain rule the gradient with respect to the activations of layer $\ell$ is

$$
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_\ell} = \Jmat_{\ell+1}^\top \Jmat_{\ell+2}^\top \cdots \Jmat_L^\top\,
\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_L}.
$$

The gradient of the weights of layer $\ell$ is $\deltavec_\ell\hvec_{\ell-1}^\top$, with
$\deltavec_\ell = \phi'(\avec_\ell) \odot \partial\Lcal/\partial\hvec_\ell$. It inherits the scale of this product.

**A bound.** The <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">spectral norm</NoteLink> $\norm{\Amat}_2$ is the largest factor by which $\Amat$ stretches any vector, and it is
submultiplicative: $\norm{\Amat\Bmat}_2 \le \norm{\Amat}_2\norm{\Bmat}_2$. Therefore

$$
\norm{\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_\ell}}_2 \le \prod_{m=\ell+1}^{L} \norm{\diag\paren{\phi'(\avec_m)}}_2\,
\norm{\Wmat_m}_2\;\norm{\frac{\partial\Lcal}{\partial\hvec_L}}_2 .
$$

If every factor $\norm{\diag(\phi'(\avec_m))}_2\norm{\Wmat_m}_2$ is at most some $c < 1$, the gradient at layer $\ell$
is at most $c^{L-\ell}$ times the gradient at the top. It must vanish geometrically. For the logistic sigmoid
$\phi' \le 1/4$, so any sigmoid network whose weight matrices have spectral norm below 4 has vanishing gradients
<Cite id="pascanu2013" />. The converse does not hold: factors above 1 are necessary for explosion but not sufficient,
because the stretched direction of one layer need not align with that of the next.

## The average factor

The bound describes the worst direction. The typical size of the gradient follows from a variance calculation. Assume
the weights of layer $m$ are independent draws with mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$, and let the layer have width $n$.
Let $\gvec = \partial\Lcal/\partial\hvec_m$ be the gradient that arrives from above, independent of the weights. The $j$-th entry of $\Jmat_m^\top\gvec$ is
$\sum_{i=1}^{n} W_{m,ij}\,\phi'(a_{m,i})\,g_i$, a sum of $n$ uncorrelated terms with mean zero. Its second moment is

$$
\expect\brack{\paren{\Jmat_m^\top\gvec}_j^2} = n\,\sigma^2\,\expect\brack{\phi'(a)^2}\,\expect\brack{g^2}.
$$

Each layer multiplies the mean square of the gradient by

$$
\chi = n\,\sigma^2\,\expect\brack{\phi'(a)^2},
$$

and after $L - \ell$ layers the root mean square of the gradient has been scaled by $\chi^{(L-\ell)/2}$. The gradient
vanishes when $\chi < 1$ and explodes when $\chi > 1$. The factor $\chi$ has two parts: the weight scale $n\sigma^2$,
which initialisation controls, and the mean squared slope $\expect[\phi'(a)^2]$, which the activation and the scale of
its input control.

- **Linear units.** $\phi' = 1$, so $\chi = n\sigma^2$. Xavier initialisation, $\sigma^2 = 1/n$, gives $\chi = 1$.
- **<Gloss name="relu" />.** $\phi'$ is 1 on half of the inputs and 0 on the other half, so $\expect[\phi'^2] = 1/2$ and
  $\chi = n\sigma^2/2$. He initialisation, $\sigma^2 = 2/n$, gives $\chi = 1$.
- **Sigmoid.** $\phi' \le 1/4$, so $\expect[\phi'^2] \le 1/16$ and a weight scale near 1 gives $\chi$ far below 1.
- **Tanh.** Large weights push inputs into the flat tails of tanh, where $\phi' \approx 0$; small weights shrink the
  forward signal. Only a narrow range of scales keeps $\chi$ near 1.

<NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">Weight initialisation</NoteLink> derives the variance conditions in full.

## Worked example: a sigmoid network

Take width $n = 128$, Xavier weights with $n\sigma^2 = 1$, sigmoid units, and 30 layers. The sigmoid's outputs are
positive, with mean near $1/2$, so the second moment of the activations settles at a fixed point $q$ satisfying
$q = \expect[\sigma(a)^2]$ with $a \sim \Gauss(0, q)$. Iterating this equation numerically gives $q = 0.265$. At that
input scale the mean squared slope is $\expect[\sigma'(a)^2] = 0.0557$. So $\chi = 0.0557$, and each layer scales the
root mean square of the gradient by $\sqrt{0.0557} = 0.236$, a factor of about $1/4.2$.

After 30 layers the predicted factor is $0.236^{30} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$. The simulation with width 128 and batch 32
gives $1.5 \times 10^{-19}$. The activations do not vanish: their root mean square stays between $0.50$ and $0.52$ at every hidden layer.
A network can have well-scaled activations and vanished gradients at the same time.

## Remedies

- **Initialisation.** Choose $\sigma^2$ so that $\chi = 1$ at the start of training; see
  <NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">weight initialisation</NoteLink> <Cite id="glorot2010" /> <Cite id="he2015" />.
- **Activations.** ReLU and its variants have $\phi' = 1$ on the active side, so they do not saturate there; see
  <NoteLink to="activation-functions">activation functions</NoteLink>.
- **Normalisation.** <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">Batch normalisation</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink> rescale every layer's pre-activations to unit scale,
  so the scale of the weights stops compounding in the forward pass.
- **Residual connections.** A block $\hvec_\ell = \hvec_{\ell-1} + F(\hvec_{\ell-1})$ has Jacobian $\I + \partial F/\partial\hvec$.
  The identity term carries the gradient down unchanged; see
  <NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink>.
- **Gating in recurrent networks.** An unrolled recurrent network is a deep network with the same weight matrix at every
  layer, so the <NoteLink to="multivariable-chain-rule">Jacobian product</NoteLink> is a matrix power. <Gloss name="lstm" /> and <Gloss name="gru" /> cells add a gated additive path for the same reason
  residual connections do; see <NoteLink to="backpropagation-through-time">backpropagation through time</NoteLink>.
- **Gradient clipping.** Explosion can be capped directly. If the gradient norm $\norm{\gvec}$ exceeds a threshold
  $c$, rescale the gradient to $\gvec \cdot c/\norm{\gvec}$ <Cite id="pascanu2013" />. The rescaling keeps the gradient's
  direction and bounds the length of every step at $\eta c$ for step size $\eta$.

## Assumptions

- The variance calculation assumes weights independent of the activations and of the incoming gradient. This holds at
  initialisation. During training the weights adapt to the data and the calculation no longer applies directly.
- The factor $\chi$ is an average. At finite width each layer's factor is random, so two networks with the same $\chi$
  can differ in gradient scale by a factor of a few after tens of layers.

## Pitfalls

- **Confusing vanishing with converging.** A small gradient at the lower layers can mean those layers have learned, or
  that the signal never reaches them. Compare per-layer gradient norms at the start of training, not only at the end.
- **Clipping hides a bad scale.** Clipping prevents one large step, but a network whose gradients explode at every step
  is still badly scaled.
- **Only looking at the loss.** In a deep network with vanished gradients, the top layers train and the lower layers receive negligible updates. The
  loss still falls, slowly, and the problem is visible only in the per-layer gradient norms.
