<Definition>
For a mini-batch of $m$ examples, **batch normalisation** takes each feature $x$ of a layer's pre-activations, with
values $x_1, \dots, x_m$ across the batch, and computes

$$
\mu_B = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m x_i, \qquad \sigma_B^2 = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m (x_i - \mu_B)^2, \qquad
\hat x_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y_i = \gamma\hat x_i + \beta,
$$

where $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learned per feature and $\epsilon$ is a small constant, typically $10^{-5}$, that keeps
the division finite <Cite id="ioffe2015" />. At inference the batch statistics are replaced by running averages
collected during training, so the layer becomes a fixed affine map.
</Definition>

## Problem

The scale of a deep network's activations depends on the product of its weight scales; see
<NoteLink to="vanishing-and-exploding-gradients">vanishing and exploding gradients</NoteLink>. A careful
<NoteLink to="weight-initialisation">initialisation</NoteLink> sets that scale right at the start, but every update
changes the weights and with them the distribution of every later layer's inputs. Ioffe and Szegedy called this
change **internal covariate shift** and proposed to remove it by normalising each layer's inputs during training
<Cite id="ioffe2015" />. With batch normalisation, deep image classifiers trained with <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rates</Gloss> several times
larger than before, and reached the same accuracy in 14 times fewer steps.

## Mechanism

### Forward pass

Batch normalisation is inserted between the affine map and the nonlinearity:
$\hvec = \phi(\mathrm{BN}(\Wmat\hvec_{\text{prev}}))$. The layer's bias is dropped, because $\beta$ replaces it and
subtracting $\mu_B$ would cancel it anyway. Each feature is normalised separately. In a <NoteLink to="convolutional-layer">convolutional layer</NoteLink>, one
feature is one channel, and the statistics are taken over the batch and over every spatial position.

After normalisation each feature has mean 0 and variance close to 1 over the batch. The learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$
restore the layer's freedom to choose a scale and offset. Setting $\gamma = \sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}$ and
$\beta = \mu_B$ recovers the unnormalised input, so the layer can represent the identity.

### Inference

At inference there may be a single example, and the prediction for one example must not depend on the others. During
training each normalisation layer keeps exponential moving averages

$$
\mu \leftarrow (1 - \rho)\,\mu + \rho\,\mu_B, \qquad \sigma^2 \leftarrow (1 - \rho)\,\sigma^2 + \rho\,\sigma_B^2,
$$

with $\rho$ typically $0.1$. At inference the layer computes $y = \gamma(x - \mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon} + \beta$,
an affine map that can be folded into the preceding layer's weights.

### Backward pass

The gradient flows through $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ as well as through each $x_i$, because both statistics depend on
every example in the batch. Write $g_i = \partial\ell/\partial y_i$ and $s = \sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \epsilon}$. Then
$\partial\ell/\partial\gamma = \sum_i g_i\hat x_i$ and $\partial\ell/\partial\beta = \sum_i g_i$. For the inputs, the
<NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> through $\hat x_i = (x_i - \mu_B)/s$ gives three terms. The direct term is $\gamma g_i/s$. The mean
$\mu_B$ receives <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> $-\sum_j \gamma g_j/s$ and passes $1/m$ of it to each $x_i$. The standard deviation $s$
receives $-\sum_j \gamma g_j\hat x_j/s$, and since $\partial s/\partial x_i = (x_i - \mu_B)/(ms) = \hat x_i/m$, it passes
$\hat x_i/m$ of that to $x_i$. Summing,

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\gamma}{s}\paren{g_i - \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m g_j - \hat x_i\,\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m g_j\hat x_j}.
$$

The bracket is $\gvec$ with its projections onto $\ones$ and onto $\hat\xvec$ removed (the vectors $\ones$ and
$\hat\xvec$ are orthogonal, and $\norm{\hat\xvec}^2 = m$ when $\epsilon = 0$). The input gradient is therefore
orthogonal to both: shifting every $x_i$ by the same amount, or scaling them all by the same factor, cannot change
the loss, because the normalisation undoes it.

## Worked example

One feature takes the values $\xvec = (1, 2, 3, 6)$ over a batch of $m = 4$, with $\gamma = 2$, $\beta = 0.5$ and
$\epsilon = 0$. The batch mean is $\mu_B = 3$ and the batch variance is $\sigma_B^2 = (4 + 1 + 0 + 9)/4 = 3.5$, so
$s = 1.871$.

| $i$ | $x_i$ | $\hat x_i = (x_i - 3)/1.871$ | $y_i = 2\hat x_i + 0.5$ |
| --- | ----- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 1   | 1     | $-1.069$                     | $-1.638$                |
| 2   | 2     | $-0.535$                     | $-0.569$                |
| 3   | 3     | $0$                          | $0.500$                 |
| 4   | 6     | $1.604$                      | $3.707$                 |

Let the loss be $\ell = y_1$, so $\gvec = (1, 0, 0, 0)$. Then $\frac1m\sum g_j = 0.25$ and
$\frac1m\sum g_j\hat x_j = -0.267$. The formula gives

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\xvec} = \frac{2}{1.871}\paren{\gvec - 0.25\,\ones + 0.267\,\hat\xvec}
= (0.496,\ -0.420,\ -0.267,\ 0.191).
$$

A finite-difference check gives the same four numbers. The entries sum to zero, and their dot product with $\hat\xvec$
is zero. Raising $x_1$ raises $y_1$, as expected, but raising any other $x_i$ also changes $y_1$, through the batch mean
and variance. In batch normalisation the output for one example depends on the rest of its batch.

## Scale invariance

Let the layer be $\mathrm{BN}(\Wmat\hvec)$. Multiplying the weights by $a > 0$ multiplies every $x_i$, $\mu_B$ and
$s$ by $a$ (ignoring $\epsilon$), so $\mathrm{BN}(a\Wmat\hvec) = \mathrm{BN}(\Wmat\hvec)$. The loss $\ell(\Wmat)$ is
constant along every ray from the origin. Differentiating $\ell(a\Wmat) = \ell(\Wmat)$ in $a$ at $a = 1$ gives
$\inner{\nabla\ell(\Wmat)}{\Wmat} = 0$: the gradient is orthogonal to the weights. Differentiating in $\Wmat$ gives
$a\nabla\ell(a\Wmat) = \nabla\ell(\Wmat)$, so the gradient scales as $1/a$.

Two consequences follow. A gradient step orthogonal to $\Wmat$ can only lengthen $\Wmat$, since
$\norm{\Wmat - \eta\gvec}^2 = \norm{\Wmat}^2 + \eta^2\norm{\gvec}^2$. And the change in direction per step is about
$\eta\norm{\gvec}/\norm{\Wmat}$, which falls as $1/\norm{\Wmat}^2$ because $\norm{\gvec} \propto 1/\norm{\Wmat}$. The
effective learning rate of a normalised layer is set by $\eta/\norm{\Wmat}^2$, not by $\eta$ alone. <Gloss name="weight-decay">Weight decay</Gloss> shrinks
$\norm{\Wmat}$ and so raises the effective learning rate; see
<NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay">decoupled weight decay</NoteLink>.

## Why it helps

- **Scale control.** The forward scale of every layer is reset to 1 regardless of the weights. Depth no longer
  compounds a weight scale in the <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">forward pass</NoteLink>, and a poor initialisation matters much less.
- **Larger learning rates.** Because the loss is invariant to the scale of normalised weights, a large step cannot blow
  up the activations. Ioffe and Szegedy trained with 5 to 30 times the usual learning rate <Cite id="ioffe2015" />.
- **Smoother loss.** Santurkar et al. added noise that shifts each layer's input distribution after batch
  normalisation, and training was still fast. They found that batch normalisation makes the loss and its gradient
  change more slowly along the training path, which allows larger steps <Cite id="santurkar2018" />. Reducing internal
  covariate shift is not by itself the explanation.
- **Regularisation.** Each example's output depends on the random other members of its batch. This noise acts as a
  mild regulariser, and some networks with batch normalisation need less <NoteLink to="dropout">dropout</NoteLink> <Cite id="ioffe2015" />.

## Cost

The layer adds $2$ parameters and two stored statistics per feature. In training it adds a reduction over the batch per
feature, which on multiple devices may need communication. At inference it costs one affine map, which can be folded into
the weights of the preceding layer for free.

## Trade-offs

- **Small batches.** With $m$ examples, $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are noisy estimates, and with only a few examples per
  device the noise hurts accuracy. <NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">Layer normalisation</NoteLink> normalises over features
  within one example instead and has no batch dependence.
- **Train and test behave differently.** Training uses batch statistics; inference uses running averages. A model left
  in training mode at test time, or evaluated on data whose statistics differ from the running averages, gives wrong
  outputs.
- **Examples are not independent.** The output for one example depends on the others in its batch. This matters for
  sequence models, where padding and variable lengths corrupt the statistics, and for losses that compare examples
  within a batch.
- **<Gloss name="resnet">Residual networks</Gloss>.** Initialising $\gamma = 0$ in the last normalisation layer of each residual branch makes every
  block start as the identity, which helps <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">large-batch training</NoteLink> <Cite id="goyal2017" />.
