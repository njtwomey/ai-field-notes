<Definition>
A **learning-rate schedule** is a function $t \mapsto \eta_t$ giving the step size used at training step $t$, in
place of a constant $\eta$. It is usually written as a peak value $\eta_{\max}$ times a shape that depends on $t$ and on
the planned number of steps $T$. The shape typically rises during a short **warmup** and then **decays** towards zero.
</Definition>

## Problem

A stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is the true gradient plus noise from sampling the mini-batch. With a constant step size the noise
never averages out, and the iterates settle into a cloud around the minimum whose size grows with $\eta$. A large
$\eta$ reaches the cloud fast and leaves the loss high. A small $\eta$ gets a lower loss and takes far longer to get
there.

### The noise floor

Take a one-dimensional quadratic $L(\theta) = \tfrac12\lambda\theta^2$ with curvature $\lambda > 0$, and a stochastic
gradient $\lambda\theta_t + \xi_t$ whose noise $\xi_t$ has mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$, independent across steps.
One step of <Gloss name="sgd">stochastic gradient descent</Gloss> gives

$$
\theta_{t+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\theta_t - \eta\,\xi_t.
$$

The mean obeys $\expect[\theta_{t+1}] = (1 - \eta\lambda)\expect[\theta_t]$ and decays geometrically, faster for larger
$\eta$ (up to $\eta\lambda = 1$). The noise term is independent of $\theta_t$, so the second moment obeys
$\expect[\theta_{t+1}^2] = (1 - \eta\lambda)^2\expect[\theta_t^2] + \eta^2\sigma^2$. For $0 < \eta\lambda < 2$ this
converges to the fixed point $V$ with $V = (1 - \eta\lambda)^2 V + \eta^2\sigma^2$:

$$
V = \frac{\eta^2\sigma^2}{1 - (1 - \eta\lambda)^2} = \frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{\lambda\,(2 - \eta\lambda)}, \qquad
\expect[L] = \tfrac12\lambda V = \frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{2\,(2 - \eta\lambda)} \approx \frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{4}.
$$

The expected loss at stationarity is proportional to $\eta$. Speed wants a large $\eta$ and final accuracy wants a small
one. A schedule gets both by starting large and ending small.

For the iterates to converge to the minimiser, the steps must satisfy $\sum_t \eta_t = \infty$ and
$\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty$ <Cite id="robbins1951" />. The first condition lets the iterates travel any distance; the
second makes the accumulated noise variance, $\sum_t\eta_t^2\sigma^2$, finite. The schedule $\eta_t \propto 1/t$
satisfies both. Deep-learning schedules are designed for a fixed budget of $T$ steps instead, and do not aim for these
asymptotic conditions.

## Mechanism

<ScheduleExplorer />

- **Step decay.** Multiply $\eta$ by a factor, usually $0.1$, at fixed points in training. <Gloss name="resnet" plural /> on <Gloss name="imagenet" /> were
  trained for 90 epochs with drops at epochs 30, 60 and 80 <Cite id="goyal2017" />. Each drop produces a visible fall in
  the loss, as the noise cloud shrinks to its new, smaller size.
- **Cosine decay.** $\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\paren{1 + \cos(\pi t/T)}$
  <Cite id="loshchilov2017" />. It stays near the peak for the first part of training, falls fastest in the middle and
  flattens near $\eta_{\min}$ at the end. It has one hyperparameter besides the peak: the length $T$.
- **Warm restarts.** SGDR repeats the cosine decay in cycles, resetting $\eta$ to the peak at the start of each, with
  each cycle optionally longer than the last. The model at the end of each cycle is a usable snapshot
  <Cite id="loshchilov2017" />.
- **Inverse square root.** The original transformer used
  $\eta_t = d^{-1/2}\min\paren{t^{-1/2},\ t\,W^{-3/2}}$ for model width $d$ and $W$ warmup steps: linear growth to
  step $W$, then decay as $1/\sqrt t$ <Cite id="vaswani2017" />.
- **Linear warmup.** Any of these can start with $\eta_t = \eta_{\max}\,t/W$ for the first $W$ steps.

## Why warmup helps

**Adam's first steps.** <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> divides each coordinate of the averaged gradient by the root
of an average of squared gradients. At step 1, after bias correction, the first average is $\gvec_1$ and the second is
$\gvec_1 \odot \gvec_1$, so the step is $-\alpha\,\sgn(\gvec_1)$: every parameter moves by exactly $\alpha$, whatever its
gradient's size. For the next few steps the second-moment estimate is an average of a handful of squared gradients. Its
reciprocal square root has high variance, and parameters whose early gradients happened to be small receive steps many
times larger than they will later. Liu et al. showed that this variance is unbounded in the first steps and shrinks as
the estimate accumulates samples, and that warmup works by keeping $\alpha$ small while it is large
<Cite id="liu2020" />. With a linear warmup over $W$ steps, the first step has size $\alpha/W$ instead of $\alpha$.

**Large batches.** Goyal et al. trained ResNet-50 with batches of 8192 images by scaling the learning rate in proportion
to the <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink>, the linear scaling rule. Starting at the scaled rate hurt optimisation early in training, when the
network changes fastest. Increasing the rate linearly from the small-batch value over the first 5 epochs fixed this, and matched
the small-batch accuracy <Cite id="goyal2017" />.

**Post-LN transformers.** In a transformer that normalises after each residual addition, the gradients of the parameters
near the output are large at initialisation. Without warmup the first updates are too large. Moving the normalisation
inside the residual branch removes the need for warmup <Cite id="xiong2020" />; see
<NoteLink to="layer-normalisation">layer normalisation</NoteLink>.

## Worked example: the noise floor

Take $\lambda = 1$, $\sigma = 1$ and a start at $\theta_0 = 1$.

| $\eta$ | Steps to shrink the mean 100-fold, $\ln 100/(-\ln(1 - \eta))$ | Stationary $V = \eta/(2 - \eta)$ | Stationary $\expect[L]$ |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| 0.1    | 44                                                            | 0.0526                           | 0.0263                  |
| 0.01   | 458                                                           | 0.00503                          | 0.00251                 |

A simulation of 20 000 independent chains for 3000 steps gives stationary variances $0.0519$ and $0.00504$. The larger
step reaches its floor ten times faster, and its floor is ten times higher. Running 44 steps at $\eta = 0.1$ and then
switching to $\eta = 0.01$ reaches the lower floor within a few hundred more steps, and the loss falls by a factor of 10
shortly after the switch. This is the drop seen in the loss curve of step-decay training.

## Cost

A schedule costs nothing to compute. It adds hyperparameters: the peak, the warmup length, the decay shape and, for
cosine and step decay, the planned length $T$. A schedule tuned for one $T$ must be re-tuned if training is extended,
because cosine and step decay are defined relative to it.

## Trade-offs

- **Fixed horizon.** Cosine decay reaches its minimum only at step $T$. Stopping early leaves a large step size, and
  continuing past $T$ requires a new decay. Schedules that hold a constant rate and decay only at the end avoid this.
- **Warmup length.** Too short a warmup does not prevent early instability; too long wastes steps at a small step size.
  Warmup of a few percent of training is common.
- **Interaction with momentum and <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss>.** The effective step of momentum is $\eta/(1 - \beta)$, so changing
  $\beta$ changes the schedule's effect. With decoupled weight decay, decay per step is proportional to $\eta_t$, so the
  schedule also schedules the regularisation; see
  <NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay">decoupled weight decay</NoteLink>.
- **Schedules and linear models.** For convex problems such as
  <NoteLink to="stochastic-gradient-descent-for-linear-models">SGD for linear models</NoteLink>, the classical $1/t$ schedules with guarantees
  are the norm; the budgeted schedules above come from deep-learning practice.
