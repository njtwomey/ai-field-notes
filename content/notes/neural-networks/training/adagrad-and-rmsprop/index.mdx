<Definition>
Let $\gvec_t = \nabla L(\thetavec_t)$ be the gradient at step $t$, and let all operations on vectors act on each
coordinate $k$. **AdaGrad** accumulates squared gradients and scales each coordinate's step by their root
<Cite id="duchi2011" />:

$$
\svec_t = \svec_{t-1} + \gvec_t \odot \gvec_t, \qquad
\thetavec_{t+1} = \thetavec_t - \eta\,\frac{\gvec_t}{\sqrt{\svec_t} + \epsilon}.
$$

**RMSProp** replaces the sum with an exponential moving average with decay $\rho \in (0, 1)$, typically $0.9$
<Cite id="tieleman2012" />:

$$
\svec_t = \rho\,\svec_{t-1} + (1 - \rho)\,\gvec_t \odot \gvec_t, \qquad
\thetavec_{t+1} = \thetavec_t - \eta\,\frac{\gvec_t}{\sqrt{\svec_t} + \epsilon}.
$$

Both start from $\svec_0 = \zeros$. The constant $\epsilon$, typically $10^{-8}$, prevents division by zero.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> uses one step size $\eta$ for every parameter. Parameters
whose gradients are large need a small step for stability, and parameters whose gradients are small then barely move.
In a network this happens between layers, whose gradient scales differ, and within a layer, between weights attached to
frequent and rare input features. A word that appears once in a thousand examples receives a gradient once in a thousand
steps. With one global step size its embedding hardly learns.

## Mechanism

### Per-coordinate step sizes

Coordinate $k$ moves by $\eta\,g_{t,k}/\sqrt{s_{t,k}}$. The denominator has the units of the gradient, so the ratio is
dimensionless and the step has the size of $\eta$. Two consequences follow.

- **Scale invariance per coordinate.** If every gradient of coordinate $k$ is multiplied by $c > 0$, then $s_{t,k}$ is
  multiplied by $c^2$ and the step $\eta\,c\,g_{t,k}/(c\sqrt{s_{t,k}})$ is unchanged (for $\epsilon \to 0$). A
  coordinate with gradients of size $100$ and one with gradients of size $0.01$ move by comparable amounts.
- **Rare features catch up.** A coordinate that has seen few nonzero gradients has a small $s_{t,k}$, so its next
  nonzero gradient produces a large step.

On the first step both methods move every coordinate by exactly $\eta$ in the direction of $-\sgn(g_{1,k})$ when
$\epsilon$ is negligible: AdaGrad has $s_{1,k} = g_{1,k}^2$, so $g_{1,k}/\sqrt{s_{1,k}} = \sgn(g_{1,k})$. RMSProp has
$s_{1,k} = (1 - \rho)g_{1,k}^2$, so its first step is $\eta/\sqrt{1 - \rho} = 3.16\,\eta$ for $\rho = 0.9$.

### AdaGrad's decreasing steps

The sum $s_{t,k}$ never decreases. If the gradients of coordinate $k$ have a roughly constant mean square $G^2$, then
$s_{t,k} \approx tG^2$ and the step size is $\eta/\sqrt{t}$ in units of the gradient. A step size that decays as
$1/\sqrt t$ gives AdaGrad a regret bound of order $\sqrt T$ over $T$ steps for convex online problems, and for sparse
gradients the bound improves with the sparsity <Cite id="duchi2011" />. In training a neural network the early gradients
are large and the loss is not convex. The accumulated sum from early training keeps the steps small long after they need
to be, and AdaGrad often stops making progress.

### RMSProp's moving average

RMSProp forgets old gradients. The moving average weights the gradient $j$ steps ago by $(1 - \rho)\rho^j$, so it
reflects roughly the last $1/(1 - \rho)$ steps: 10 steps for $\rho = 0.9$. The denominator tracks the current
gradient scale. When the gradient of a coordinate shrinks, $s_{t,k}$ shrinks with it, and the step stays of order $\eta$.
Tieleman and Hinton presented it as a mini-batch version of Rprop, which uses only the sign of each gradient coordinate
<Cite id="tieleman2012" />.

Because the step stays of order $\eta$, RMSProp with a fixed $\eta$ does not settle into a minimum. Near the minimum the
gradient is small, but so is its running root mean square, and the ratio remains near 1 in size. The iterates hover at
a distance set by $\eta$. A decreasing step size removes this; see
<NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedules</NoteLink>.

## Coordinates, not directions

Dividing by $\sqrt{\svec}$ multiplies the gradient by the diagonal matrix $\Dmat = \diag(\svec)^{-1/2}$. This is a
diagonal preconditioner. On a quadratic $L(\thetavec) = \tfrac12\thetavec^\top\Amat\thetavec$ the gradient is
$\Amat\thetavec$, and preconditioned gradient descent converges at a rate set by the condition number of
$\Dmat^{1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{1/2}$ instead of $\Amat$.

**Axis-aligned curvature.** Let $\Amat = \diag(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)$. The gradient of coordinate $k$ is
$\lambda_k\theta_k$, so the running root mean square in coordinate $k$ is proportional to $\lambda_k$ times the size of
$\theta_k$. The preconditioner divides each coordinate by roughly its own curvature, and the condition number of the
preconditioned problem is close to 1.

**Rotated curvature.** Rotate the same quadratic by $45°$. With $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 25$ the Hessian becomes

$$
\Amat = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 + 25 & 1 - 25 \\ 1 - 25 & 1 + 25 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 13 & -12 \\ -12 & 13 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenvalues are still 1 and 25, but its diagonal entries are equal. Both coordinates see gradients of the same
typical size, so the preconditioner is close to a multiple of the identity. A multiple of the identity does not change
the condition number, which stays 25. A diagonal preconditioner can only correct curvature that is aligned with the
coordinate axes.

In the figure, with the default step sizes of $0.03$ for gradient descent ($\eta$ in the figure) and $0.1$ for the
adaptive methods ($\alpha$ in the figure), and 150 steps, the step counts to reach a loss below $10^{-3}$ are:

| Method           | Valley, axis-aligned | Valley, rotated 45° |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| gradient descent | 133                  | 133                 |
| RMSProp          | 34                   | 57                  |
| AdaGrad          | not within 150       | not within 150      |

AdaGrad's steps at $\eta = 0.1$ decay too quickly: after 150 steps its loss is still $0.24$ on the axis-aligned valley.
With each method's step size tuned separately over a grid for the fewest steps, AdaGrad needs 6 steps on the
axis-aligned valley ($\eta \approx 1.4$) and 75 on the rotated one. RMSProp needs 6 ($\eta \approx 0.40$) and 38.
Nesterov momentum, tuned the same way, needs 10 on both. On the axis-aligned valley the adaptive methods beat every
method that ignores coordinates; on the rotated valley their advantage is gone.

## Cost

One extra vector the size of the parameters, and an elementwise square, square root and division per step.

## Trade-offs

- **AdaGrad suits sparse, convex problems.** Linear models over sparse features, such as text classifiers, benefit from
  its per-feature steps and its decay. For deep networks its decay is usually too aggressive.
- **RMSProp needs a schedule to converge.** With a constant $\eta$ it hovers near a minimum. A decaying step size or
  averaging the iterates removes the hovering.
- **Only axis-aligned benefit.** Adaptive methods help when the scales that matter are those of individual parameters.
  For curvature along combinations of parameters, they behave like gradient descent with a rescaled step size.
- **Momentum.** RMSProp is often combined with momentum on the scaled gradient. Adding a moving average of the gradient
  and a bias correction gives <NoteLink to="adam">Adam</NoteLink> <Cite id="kingma2015" />.
