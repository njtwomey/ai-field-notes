<Definition>
**Weight initialisation** sets the starting weights of a neural network by independent random draws with mean zero and
a variance chosen from the layer's fan-in $n_{\text{in}}$ (inputs per unit) and fan-out $n_{\text{out}}$ (units fed by
each input). **Xavier** (or Glorot) initialisation uses $\var(W_{ij}) = 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})$
<Cite id="glorot2010" />. **He** (or Kaiming) initialisation uses $\var(W_{ij}) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$ for ReLU layers
<Cite id="he2015" />. Biases start at zero. Both variances are chosen so that the second moment of the activations and
of the gradients stays constant from layer to layer.
</Definition>

## Problem

A layer of a <NoteLink to="multilayer-perceptron">multilayer perceptron</NoteLink> computes pre-activations
$\avec_\ell = \Wmat_\ell\hvec_{\ell-1}$ and activations $\hvec_\ell = \phi(\avec_\ell)$. The forward pass multiplies the
input by $L$ random matrices. The backward pass of <NoteLink to="backpropagation">backpropagation</NoteLink> multiplies
the output gradient by their transposes. If each layer scales the signal by a factor $c$ on average, the signal at
depth $L$ has scale $c^L$. With $c = 0.9$ and $L = 50$ the factor is $0.005$. With $c = 1.1$ it is $117$.

The weights must be random, and not all equal. Units in a layer with identical incoming weights compute the same
function and receive the same gradient, so they stay identical for the whole of training. Random draws break this
symmetry. The remaining choice is the variance of the draws, and the scale factor $c$ depends on it.

## Mechanism

### Forward pass

Take one layer with $n_{\text{in}}$ inputs. Assume the weights $W_{ij}$ are independent, with mean 0 and variance
$\sigma^2$, and independent of the layer's input $\hvec$. The pre-activation of unit $i$ is
$a_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} W_{ij} h_j$. Each term has mean $\expect[W_{ij}]\expect[h_j] = 0$, so $a_i$ has mean
zero. The terms are uncorrelated, since $\expect[W_{ij}h_j W_{ik}h_k] = \expect[W_{ij}]\expect[W_{ik}]\expect[h_jh_k] = 0$
for $j \ne k$. The variance of the sum is the sum of the variances:

$$
\var(a_i) = \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} \expect[W_{ij}^2]\,\expect[h_j^2] = n_{\text{in}}\,\sigma^2\,\expect[h^2].
$$

The factor on the right is the **second moment** $\expect[h^2]$ of the input, not its variance. The two are equal only
when the inputs have mean zero.

**Linear and tanh units.** Near the origin $\tanh(a) \approx a$, so $\expect[h^2] \approx \var(a)$ for the previous
layer. The recursion becomes $\var(a_\ell) = n_{\text{in}}\sigma^2 \var(a_{\ell-1})$. The variance is constant across
layers when

$$
n_{\text{in}}\,\sigma^2 = 1, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}.
$$

This is **LeCun initialisation** <Cite id="lecun1998" />.

**ReLU units.** The pre-activation $a$ of the previous layer is a sum of terms $W_{ij}h_j$ that are symmetric about zero,
because $W_{ij}$ is. So $a$ is symmetric about zero, and $\mathrm{ReLU}(a)^2 = a^2$ on the half of the distribution with
$a > 0$ and $0$ on the other half. Hence

$$
\expect[h^2] = \expect[\mathrm{ReLU}(a)^2] = \tfrac12\,\expect[a^2] = \tfrac12\var(a).
$$

The recursion becomes $\var(a_\ell) = \tfrac12 n_{\text{in}}\sigma^2 \var(a_{\ell-1})$, and the variance is constant
when

$$
\tfrac12\,n_{\text{in}}\,\sigma^2 = 1, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}.
$$

This is **He initialisation** <Cite id="he2015" />. ReLU discards half of the signal's second moment, and the factor 2
restores it. ReLU outputs have a positive mean, so He initialisation keeps the second moment of $\hvec_\ell$ constant but
not its variance.

### Backward pass

Write $g_j = \partial\ell/\partial h_j$ for the gradient of the loss $\ell$ with respect to an input of the layer, and
$\delta_i = \partial\ell/\partial a_i = \phi'(a_i)\,\partial\ell/\partial h^{\text{out}}_i$ for the gradient at a
pre-activation. The chain rule gives $g_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{out}}} W_{ij}\delta_i$, a sum over the
$n_{\text{out}}$ units that input $j$ feeds. The same argument as the forward pass, assuming the weights are
independent of the $\delta_i$, gives

$$
\var(g_j) = n_{\text{out}}\,\sigma^2\,\expect[\delta^2].
$$

For linear units $\phi' = 1$ and $\expect[\delta^2] = \var(g^{\text{out}})$, so the gradient keeps its variance when
$n_{\text{out}}\sigma^2 = 1$. For ReLU units $\phi'(a)$ is 1 on half of the inputs and 0 on the other half, so
$\expect[\delta^2] = \tfrac12\var(g^{\text{out}})$ and the condition is $\tfrac12 n_{\text{out}}\sigma^2 = 1$.

### Xavier: the compromise

The forward pass wants $\sigma^2 = 1/n_{\text{in}}$ and the backward pass wants $\sigma^2 = 1/n_{\text{out}}$. Both
hold only when $n_{\text{in}} = n_{\text{out}}$. Glorot and Bengio take the harmonic mean of the two
<Cite id="glorot2010" />:

$$
\sigma^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}.
$$

A uniform distribution on $[-r, r]$ has variance $r^2/3$. Setting $r^2/3 = 2/(n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})$ gives the
common uniform form

$$
W_{ij} \sim \Unif\paren{-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}},\ \sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}}.
$$

He et al. show that for ReLU networks either the forward condition or the backward condition suffices
<Cite id="he2015" />. With fan-in scaling, the backward pass through layer $\ell$ scales the gradient's variance by
$n_{\text{out},\ell}/n_{\text{in},\ell}$. Since $n_{\text{out},\ell} = n_{\text{in},\ell+1}$, the product over all
layers telescopes to $n_{\text{in},L+1}/n_{\text{in},1}$, a ratio of two widths that does not grow with depth.

## Worked example: scale after 30 layers of width 128

All layers have $n_{\text{in}} = n_{\text{out}} = n = 128$, so Xavier's variance is $1/n$, with standard deviation
$1/\sqrt{128} = 0.0884$. He's standard deviation is $\sqrt{2/128} = 0.125$. A common naive choice is a fixed standard
deviation of 0.01.

For linear units the forward recursion multiplies the RMS by $\sqrt{n}\,\sigma$ per layer. The last column is the RMS
of $\hvec_{30}$ in the figure above, for the linear activation and the first draw of the weights; the input has RMS
1.01.

| Initialisation      | $\sigma$ | Factor per layer, linear      | After 30 layers        | Figure, linear |
| ------------------- | -------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------- | --------- |
| fixed $0.01$        | 0.01     | $0.01\sqrt{128} = 0.113$      | $0.113^{30} = 4.1\times10^{-29}$ | $4.7\times10^{-29}$ |
| Xavier              | 0.0884   | 1                             | 1                      | 1.15      |
| He                  | 0.125    | $\sqrt 2$                     | $2^{15} = 32\,768$     | $37\,600$ |

For ReLU units each factor gains a further $1/\sqrt2$. He's factor becomes 1. Xavier's becomes $1/\sqrt2$, which gives
$2^{-15} = 3.1 \times 10^{-5}$ after 30 layers, and the figure gives
$3.1 \times 10^{-5}$. The fixed $\sigma = 0.01$ gives $0.080^{30} = 1.2 \times 10^{-33}$, and the figure gives
$1.2 \times 10^{-33}$. A gradient of size $10^{-33}$ is
five orders of magnitude above the smallest normal 32-bit floating-point number, about $10^{-38}$, so it is still
represented. Every weight update it produces is negligible.

## Cost

Initialisation costs one random draw per weight, once. The choice of variance is free. The price of a wrong choice is a
network that does not train, or that trains only after many wasted steps while the optimiser rescales the weights.

## Trade-offs

- **Assumptions of the derivation.** The derivation treats the weights as independent of the activations and the
  activations within a layer as uncorrelated. At initialisation both hold. After training starts they do not, and the
  variance conditions say nothing about later layers' scale.
- **Finite width.** The conditions preserve the expected second moment. At finite width each layer multiplies the
  second moment by a random factor with mean 1, so the log of the scale performs a random walk. Across 40 draws of the
  30-layer ReLU network above, the mean square of the last layer's activations was 0.97 and the median RMS was 0.81.
  Pressing "New weights" shows the spread.
- **Activation-specific gains.** Frameworks express the rule as $\sigma = \text{gain}/\sqrt{n}$, with gain 1 for linear
  units, $\sqrt2$ for ReLU and $5/3$ for tanh. Tanh with gain 1 still shrinks slowly with depth, because
  $\abs{\tanh(a)} < \abs{a}$ for every $a \ne 0$.
- **Sigmoid.** Sigmoid outputs have mean $1/2$ and a derivative of at most $1/4$. No variance makes both passes stable;
  in the figure the sigmoid activations settle near 0.5 while the gradients shrink by about a factor of 4 per layer.
- **Other schemes.** Orthogonal matrices preserve the norm of every vector exactly in a linear network, not only on
  average. Architectures with normalisation layers or residual connections depend much less on the initial scale; see
  <NoteLink to="batch-normalisation">batch normalisation</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="residual-connections">residual connections</NoteLink>.
- **Initialisation interacts with the optimiser.** A good scale and momentum together were needed to train deep and
  recurrent networks without pre-training <Cite id="sutskever2013" />.
