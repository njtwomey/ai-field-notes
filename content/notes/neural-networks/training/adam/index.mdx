<Definition>
**Adam** updates parameters $\thetavec$ from <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> $\gvec_t = \nabla L(\thetavec_{t-1})$ at steps
$t = 1, 2, \dots$, starting from $\mvec_0 = \vvec_0 = \zeros$ <Cite id="kingma2015" />:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mvec_t &= \beta_1\mvec_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\,\gvec_t, &
\hat\mvec_t &= \frac{\mvec_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \\
\vvec_t &= \beta_2\vvec_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\,\gvec_t \odot \gvec_t, &
\hat\vvec_t &= \frac{\vvec_t}{1 - \beta_2^t}, \\
\thetavec_t &= \thetavec_{t-1} - \alpha\,\frac{\hat\mvec_t}{\sqrt{\hat\vvec_t} + \epsilon}. &&
\end{aligned}
$$

Square root and division act on each coordinate. The defaults are step size $\alpha = 10^{-3}$, decay rates
$\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.999$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$.
</Definition>

## Problem

Momentum averages gradients over time, which cancels oscillation and accumulates consistent progress; see
<NoteLink to="momentum-and-nesterov">momentum</NoteLink>. RMSProp divides each coordinate by a running root mean square
of its gradient, which equalises step sizes across parameters with very different gradient scales; see
<NoteLink to="adagrad-and-rmsprop">AdaGrad and RMSProp</NoteLink>. Adam combines the two. It also fixes a bias that
both moving averages have at the start of training because they are initialised at zero.

## Mechanism

$\mvec_t$ estimates the first moment $\expect[\gvec]$ of the gradient, and $\vvec_t$ estimates the second moment
$\expect[\gvec \odot \gvec]$, both averaged over roughly the last $1/(1 - \beta)$ steps: 10 steps for $\beta_1 = 0.9$
and 1000 for $\beta_2 = 0.999$. The step in coordinate $k$ is $\alpha$ times the ratio $\hat m_{t,k}/\sqrt{\hat v_{t,k}}$,
a <Gloss name="snr">signal-to-noise ratio</Gloss> of the gradient in that coordinate. When the gradient is consistent, $\hat m^2 \approx \hat v$ and
the step is about $\alpha$. When the gradient is mostly noise with mean near zero, $\hat m^2 \ll \hat v$ and the step is
small.

### Bias correction

Unrolling the recursion for $\mvec_t$ from $\mvec_0 = \zeros$ gives

$$
\mvec_t = (1 - \beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^t \beta_1^{t-i}\,\gvec_i.
$$

Suppose the gradients have a constant expectation, $\expect[\gvec_i] = \expect[\gvec]$ for all $i$. Taking expectations
and summing the geometric series,

$$
\expect[\mvec_t] = (1 - \beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^t \beta_1^{t-i}\;\expect[\gvec] =
(1 - \beta_1)\,\frac{1 - \beta_1^t}{1 - \beta_1}\;\expect[\gvec] = (1 - \beta_1^t)\,\expect[\gvec].
$$

The weights $(1 - \beta_1)\beta_1^{t-i}$ sum to $1 - \beta_1^t$, not to 1: the missing weight $\beta_1^t$ is the weight
that the initial value $\mvec_0 = \zeros$ still carries. Dividing by $1 - \beta_1^t$ gives an estimate with expectation
$\expect[\gvec]$. The same argument with $\gvec \odot \gvec$ in place of $\gvec$ gives
$\expect[\vvec_t] = (1 - \beta_2^t)\,\expect[\gvec \odot \gvec]$ and the correction for $\vvec_t$
<Cite id="kingma2015" />. If the expectations drift slowly, the equalities hold approximately.

The correction matters most for $\vvec$, because $\beta_2$ is close to 1. At $t = 1$ the uncorrected
$\vvec_1 = 0.001\,\gvec_1 \odot \gvec_1$ has root $0.0316\,\abs{\gvec_1}$, while $\mvec_1 = 0.1\,\gvec_1$. The uncorrected
ratio would be $0.1/0.0316 = 3.16$ times the sign of the gradient. With the correction, $\hat\mvec_1 = \gvec_1$ and
$\sqrt{\hat\vvec_1} = \abs{\gvec_1}$, and the first step is exactly $-\alpha\,\sgn(\gvec_1)$ for $\epsilon \to 0$.

### Bounded steps

Kingma and Ba bound the step in each coordinate. If $(1 - \beta_1) > \sqrt{1 - \beta_2}$ then
$\abs{\Delta\theta_{t,k}} \le \alpha(1 - \beta_1)/\sqrt{1 - \beta_2}$, which is $3.16\alpha$ for the defaults; the bound is
reached only when a single large gradient follows a long run of zeros. In the common case the step is at most about
$\alpha$ <Cite id="kingma2015" />. The step size $\alpha$ therefore sets a <NoteLink to="trust-region-methods">trust region</NoteLink> in parameter space, and a
parameter that should move by about 1 needs on the order of $1/\alpha$ steps.

The update is invariant to rescaling the gradient: multiplying every $\gvec_t$ by $c > 0$ multiplies $\hat\mvec_t$ by
$c$ and $\sqrt{\hat\vvec_t}$ by $c$, and leaves the step unchanged (for $\epsilon \to 0$). The same holds for each
coordinate separately.

## Rotation

The per-coordinate division is a diagonal preconditioner. It removes differences in curvature that lie along the
coordinate axes and does nothing for curvature along combinations of coordinates. The figure below runs Adam against
gradient descent, Nesterov momentum and RMSProp on a valley $L = \tfrac12(x^2 + 25y^2)$ with <NoteLink to="condition-number">condition number</NoteLink> 25, and
on the same valley rotated by $45°$.

With the default step sizes, $0.03$ for the gradient-descent family and $\alpha = 0.1$ for the adaptive methods, the loss
falls below $10^{-3}$ after:

| Method           | Valley, axis-aligned | Valley, rotated 45° |
| ---------------- | -------------------- | ------------------- |
| gradient descent | 133                  | 133                 |
| Nesterov         | 30                   | 30                  |
| Adam             | 31                   | 132                 |

Gradient descent and Nesterov momentum are unchanged by the rotation, because rotating the parameters rotates their
gradients and their updates with them. Adam, on the rotated valley, is no faster than plain gradient descent. With
$\alpha$ tuned separately for each valley over a grid, Adam needs 31 steps on the axis-aligned valley and 45 on the
rotated one; tuned Nesterov momentum needs 10 on both. The Rosenbrock surface in the figure has a curved valley, whose
direction changes along its length, so no fixed coordinate system aligns with it.

Neural-network losses have a partly axis-aligned structure: different layers, and different embeddings, have gradients
of very different scales. That is where Adam's per-coordinate scaling pays off.

## Worked example: bias correction under a constant gradient

Let one coordinate's gradient be the constant $g = 2$ at every step, with the defaults $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and
$\beta_2 = 0.999$. Then $m_t = (1 - 0.9^t)\cdot 2$ and $v_t = (1 - 0.999^t)\cdot 4$, and the uncorrected ratio is
$m_t/\sqrt{v_t} = (1 - 0.9^t)/\sqrt{1 - 0.999^t}$.

| Step $t$ | $1 - 0.9^t$ | $1 - 0.999^t$ | Uncorrected $m_t/\sqrt{v_t}$ | Corrected $\hat m_t/\sqrt{\hat v_t}$ |
| -------- | ----------- | ------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| 1        | 0.100       | 0.00100       | 3.16                         | 1                                    |
| 10       | 0.651       | 0.00996       | 6.53                         | 1                                    |
| 100      | 1.000       | 0.0952        | 3.24                         | 1                                    |
| 1000     | 1.000       | 0.632         | 1.26                         | 1                                    |

Without the correction the first thousand steps are several times larger than intended, peaking near step 10. The
correction makes the step exactly $\alpha$ from the first step on.

## Cost

Two extra vectors of the size of the parameters, so the optimiser state is twice the size of the model. For a model with
$10^9$ parameters in 32-bit floating point, the state takes 8 GB in addition to the 4 GB of weights and 4 GB of
gradients.

## Trade-offs

- **<Gloss name="weight-decay">Weight decay</Gloss>.** Adding an L2 penalty to the loss sends the penalty's gradient through the division by
  $\sqrt{\hat\vvec}$, which weakens the decay of parameters with large gradients. Decoupling the decay from the gradient
  step fixes this; see <NoteLink to="decoupled-weight-decay">decoupled weight decay</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="loshchilov2019" />.
- **Early instability.** In the first steps $\hat\vvec$ is estimated from few gradients and is noisy, so the step size
  varies widely between coordinates. A warmup of the step size limits the damage; see
  <NoteLink to="learning-rate-schedules">learning-rate schedules</NoteLink> <Cite id="liu2020" />.
- **Non-convergence.** Because $\hat\vvec$ can decrease, Adam's effective step can grow. Reddi et al. constructed a
  convex problem on which Adam converges to a highly suboptimal point. AMSGrad keeps the running maximum of $\vvec_t$ in the
  denominator and restores a convergence guarantee <Cite id="reddi2018" />.
- **Generalisation.** On some image-classification benchmarks, well-tuned SGD with momentum reaches better test accuracy
  than Adam <Cite id="wilson2017" />. For transformers Adam, in its decoupled form AdamW, is the default.
- **The $\epsilon$ hyperparameter.** $\epsilon$ is added to $\sqrt{\hat\vvec}$, which has the units of the gradient. When
  gradients are tiny, $\epsilon$ dominates the denominator and Adam becomes gradient descent with step size
  $\alpha/\epsilon$.
