<Definition>
**Data augmentation** replaces each training example $(\xvec, y)$ at each use by $(T(\xvec), y)$, where $T$ is drawn at
random from a family $\mathcal{T}$ of transformations chosen so that they do not change the correct label. Training
minimises the expected loss over both the data and the transformations,
$\expect_{(\xvec, y)}\expect_{T}\brack{\ell(f(T(\xvec)), y)}$ <Cite id="shorten2019" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

A model with many parameters overfits a small training set. Collecting more labelled data is the direct fix and is
often expensive. Many tasks come with invariances known in advance: a cat photographed slightly to the left, or
mirrored, is still a cat. A model has to learn such invariances from data unless they are built into the architecture or
the data.

## Mechanism

Each epoch shows the model a different transformed version of every example, so the effective training set is much
larger than the stored one, and the model is penalised for any output that changes under a transformation in
$\mathcal{T}$.

Common families:

- **Images.** Random crops and horizontal flips, colour and brightness changes, small rotations and elastic distortions.
  Elastic distortions set the state of the art for handwritten digits in 2003 <Cite id="simard2003" />. The 2012
  <Gloss name="imagenet" /> network of Krizhevsky et al. trained on random $224 \times 224$ crops of $256 \times 256$ images and their
  mirror images, enlarging the training set by a factor of 2048, and on perturbations of the colour intensities
  <Cite id="krizhevsky2012" />.
- **Audio.** Time shifts, added noise, speed changes, and masking of frequency bands and time intervals in the
  spectrogram; see <NoteLink to="spectrogram-augmentation">spectrogram augmentation</NoteLink>.
- **Text.** Replacing words with synonyms, or translating to another language and back.
- **Any input.** Adding small random noise.

### Noise as a penalty

Adding noise to the inputs is the simplest augmentation, and its effect can be computed. Let the model be linear,
$f(\xvec) = \wvec^\top\xvec$, and train on $\tilde\xvec = \xvec + \epsilonvec$ with $\expect[\epsilonvec] = \zeros$ and
$\cov(\epsilonvec) = \sigma^2\I$, drawn independently of the data. The squared error is
$(y - \wvec^\top\xvec - \wvec^\top\epsilonvec)^2$. Expanding and taking the <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> over $\epsilonvec$, the cross term
vanishes because $\expect[\epsilonvec] = \zeros$, and $\expect[(\wvec^\top\epsilonvec)^2] = \wvec^\top\cov(\epsilonvec)\wvec
= \sigma^2\norm{\wvec}^2$. So

$$
\expect_{\epsilonvec}\brack{(y - \wvec^\top\tilde\xvec)^2} = (y - \wvec^\top\xvec)^2 + \sigma^2\norm{\wvec}^2.
$$

Training with input noise minimises the squared error plus an L2 penalty on the weights: it is
<NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> with penalty $\sigma^2$ per example. For a nonlinear model a
<NoteLink to="hessian">second-order Taylor expansion</NoteLink> gives, to order $\sigma^2$, a penalty $\sigma^2\norm{\nabla_{\xvec} f}^2$ on the model's
sensitivity to its input, plus a term that is negligible near the minimum of the training loss <Cite id="bishop1995" />.
Augmentation with other transformations penalises sensitivity along the directions those transformations move the
input.

## Worked example

Take $\wvec = (1, -2)$, $\xvec = (1, 1)$, $y = 0$ and noise with $\sigma = 0.5$. Without noise, $\wvec^\top\xvec = -1$
and the squared error is 1. The penalty is $\sigma^2\norm{\wvec}^2 = 0.25 \times 5 = 1.25$, so the expected squared error
under noise is $2.25$. Averaging the squared error over 2 million noise draws gives $2.2499$.

## Cost

Transformations are computed on the fly, usually on the <Gloss name="cpu" /> while the accelerator trains on the previous batch. They
add no parameters and no inference cost. Training may need more epochs, because each epoch sees a harder, more varied
dataset.

## Trade-offs

- **Label-preserving is task-specific.** A horizontal flip preserves "cat" but turns the letter "b" into "d" and reverses
  the reading direction of text. A rotation by $180°$ turns a 6 into a 9. Every transformation must be checked against the
  task.
- **Strength.** Weak transformations add little; strong ones move examples off the data distribution and can lower
  accuracy on clean inputs.
- **Test-time augmentation.** Averaging the predictions for several transformed copies of a test input often improves
  accuracy, at a proportional inference cost.
- **Not a substitute for coverage.** Augmentation can only express invariances that someone specified. It does not
  supply variation that the transformations cannot generate, such as new object types or new recording conditions.
