<Definition>
Contrastive learning trains an encoder $f$ from pairs of related inputs $(x, y^+)$, such as two augmented crops of one
image. For each anchor $x$ the model sees the positive $y^+$ and $N - 1$ negatives $y^-_2, \dots, y^-_N$, and scores
each candidate with a critic $s(x, y)$, usually the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> of the encodings. The InfoNCE loss is the
cross-entropy of picking the positive out of the $N$ candidates:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}} = -\expect\brack{\log \frac{\exp(s(x, y^+) / \tau)}{\exp(s(x, y^+) / \tau) + \sum_{j=2}^{N} \exp(s(x, y^-_j) / \tau)}},
$$

with temperature $\tau > 0$ <Cite id="oord2018" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Without labels there is no class to predict. The data still says which inputs belong together: two crops of one
photograph, a sentence and the next one, an image and its caption. Contrastive learning turns this into a
classification problem. The "classes" are the candidates in the batch, and the correct class is the one that came from
the same source as the anchor.

To win, the encoder must keep whatever distinguishes one input from the others and discard whatever changes between
two views of one input. The choice of views therefore decides what the representation learns. If views differ in
colour, colour is discarded. If they differ in crop, position is discarded.

The negatives stop the trivial solution. An encoder that maps every input to the same vector makes the positive score
equal every negative score. The loss is then $\log N$, its value for a uniform guess.

## The loss is a bound on mutual information

Let $(X, Y)$ have joint density $p(x, y)$ with marginals $p(x)$ and $p(y)$. Draw $x$ and the positive $y_1$ from
$p(x, y)$, and draw $y_2, \dots, y_N$ independently from $p(y)$. Write $y_{1:N}$ for all $N$ candidates and let
$f(x, y) = s(x, y)/\tau$ be any critic. Then

$$
I(X; Y) \ge \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}.
$$

The proof follows Poole et al. <Cite id="poole2019" />.

**Step 1: the extra candidates carry no information.** The negatives are independent of $(X, Y_1)$, so
$I(X; Y_{1:N}) = I(X; Y_1) = I(X; Y)$.

**Step 2: any normalised guess gives a lower bound.** For any <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional density</NoteLink> $q(y_{1:N} \mid x)$,

$$
I(X; Y_{1:N}) - \expect\brack{\log \frac{q(y_{1:N} \mid x)}{p(y_{1:N})}}
= \expect\brack{\log \frac{p(y_{1:N} \mid x)}{q(y_{1:N} \mid x)}}
= \expect_x\brack{\KL\paren{p(y_{1:N} \mid x) \,\|\, q(y_{1:N} \mid x)}} \ge 0,
$$

because <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is non-negative. So
$I(X; Y) \ge \expect[\log q(y_{1:N} \mid x) / p(y_{1:N})]$ for every valid $q$.

**Step 3: build $q$ from the critic.** Let $m(x, y_{1:N}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{f(x, y_j)}$ and set

$$
q(y_{1:N} \mid x) = p(y_{1:N}) \, \frac{e^{f(x, y_1)}}{m(x, y_{1:N})}, \qquad p(y_{1:N}) = \prod_{j=1}^{N} p(y_j).
$$

This $q$ integrates to 1. Under $p(y_{1:N})$ the candidates are exchangeable, so each ratio
$e^{f(x, y_i)} / m$ has the same <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>. The $N$ ratios sum to $N$ by the definition of $m$. Each therefore has
expectation 1, and $\int q \, dy_{1:N} = \expect_{p(y_{1:N})}[e^{f(x, y_1)} / m] = 1$.

**Step 4: substitute.** The ratio $q / p(y_{1:N})$ is $e^{f(x, y_1)} / m$, so

$$
I(X; Y) \ge \expect\brack{\log \frac{e^{f(x, y_1)}}{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{j} e^{f(x, y_j)}}}
= \log N + \expect\brack{\log \frac{e^{f(x, y_1)}}{\sum_{j} e^{f(x, y_j)}}} = \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}}.
$$

Minimising the InfoNCE loss maximises a lower bound on the <NoteLink to="mutual-information">mutual
information</NoteLink> between the two views. The loss is non-negative, so the bound can never exceed $\log N$. With
$N = 4096$ candidates, no critic can certify more than $\log 4096 \approx 8.32$ nats. This is one reason contrastive
methods use large batches or large banks of negatives.

**The optimal critic.** The loss is the cross-entropy of an $N$-way classifier that must name the positive's index $i$.
Given $x$, the probability of the candidates when index $i$ is the positive is
$p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{l \ne i} p(y_l) = \frac{p(y_i \mid x)}{p(y_i)} \prod_{l} p(y_l)$. With a uniform prior over $i$,
<NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' rule</NoteLink> gives the posterior

$$
\pr(i \mid x, y_{1:N}) = \frac{p(y_i \mid x) / p(y_i)}{\sum_{j} p(y_j \mid x) / p(y_j)}.
$$

The best classifier matches this posterior, so the optimal critic is $f^*(x, y) = \log \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)} + c(x)$
for any $c(x)$ <Cite id="oord2018" />. The critic learns the log density ratio, the <Gloss name="pmi">pointwise mutual information</Gloss>, and
never has to model $p(y)$ itself. This is the same idea as noise-contrastive estimation, which fits an unnormalised
density by classifying data against noise <Cite id="gutmann2010" />.

## Temperature and hard negatives

Write $p_j$ for the softmax weight of candidate $j$ at temperature $\tau$, with $j = 1$ the positive. Differentiating
the loss for one anchor, $\mathcal{L} = -s_1/\tau + \log \sum_j e^{s_j/\tau}$, gives

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s_1} = -\frac{1 - p_1}{\tau}, \qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s_j} = \frac{p_j}{\tau} \quad (j \ge 2).
$$

Each negative is pushed away in proportion to $p_j \propto e^{s_j / \tau}$. Two negatives with similarities $s_j$ and
$s_k$ receive <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> in the ratio $e^{(s_j - s_k)/\tau}$. A small temperature makes this ratio large, so the
gradient concentrates on the hardest negatives, the ones most similar to the anchor <Cite id="wang2021" />. A large
temperature spreads it over all negatives.

### Worked example

Take one anchor with a positive at cosine similarity 0.8 and three negatives at 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5.

- At $\tau = 1$ the logits are $(0.8, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5)$. The softmax gives the positive $p_1 = 0.352$, and the loss is
  $-\log 0.352 = 1.05$ nats. The three negatives get weights 0.175, 0.213 and 0.260, nearly equal.
- At $\tau = 0.1$ the logits are $(8, 1, 3, 5)$. Now $p_1 = 0.946$ and the loss is 0.056 nats. The negatives get
  0.0009, 0.0064 and 0.0471. The hardest negative, at 0.5, receives 55 times the gradient of the easiest.

Lower temperature separates the positive more sharply and aims the gradient at the confusable negatives. Too low a
temperature also pushes apart negatives that are genuinely similar, such as two images of the same class.

## Alignment and uniformity

Normalise the encodings to the unit sphere, so $s(x, y) = f(x)^{\tr} f(y)$. Subtract the constant $\log N$ from the
loss for one anchor:

$$
\mathcal{L} - \log N = -\frac{s(x, y^+)}{\tau} + \log\paren{\frac{e^{s(x, y^+)/\tau}}{N} + \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=2}^{N} e^{s(x, y^-_j)/\tau}}.
$$

As $N \to \infty$, the first term inside the logarithm vanishes and the average over negatives tends to its expectation.
Wang and Isola show that the loss then approaches <Cite id="wang2020" />

$$
\underbrace{-\frac{1}{\tau} \expect\brack{f(x)^{\tr} f(y^+)}}_{\text{alignment}}
+ \underbrace{\expect_x \brack{\log \expect_{y^-} \brack{e^{f(x)^{\tr} f(y^-) / \tau}}}}_{\text{uniformity}}.
$$

The first term rewards positive pairs that map close together. The second penalises encodings that bunch up, and is
smallest when the encodings are spread uniformly over the sphere. See
<NoteLink to="embedding-evaluation">embedding evaluation</NoteLink> for these two quantities used as metrics.

## Methods

**Contrastive predictive coding** (CPC) introduced InfoNCE. An <Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss> summarises a sequence up to time
$t$, and the critic must pick the true encoding of step $t + k$ from negatives taken from other times and sequences
<Cite id="oord2018" />.

**Instance discrimination** treats every image in the dataset as its own class and stores one feature vector per image
in a memory bank to supply negatives <Cite id="wu2018" />.

**SimCLR** takes a batch of $N$ images and makes two augmented views of each, giving $2N$ views <Cite id="chen2020" />.
An encoder produces $\hvec = f(x)$, and a small <Gloss name="mlp" /> projection head produces $\zvec = g(\hvec)$. The critic is the cosine
similarity of $\zvec$ vectors divided by $\tau$. Each view's positive is its partner, and its negatives are the other
$2(N - 1)$ views in the batch. Chen et al. found three things. The composition of augmentations matters most, and random
cropping with colour distortion works best. The projection head improves the representation $\hvec$ taken before it,
which is what is used downstream. Larger batches help: they used batches of up to 8192 images. A linear classifier on
the ResNet-50 representation reached 76.5% top-1 accuracy on <Gloss name="imagenet" /> <Cite id="chen2020" />.

**MoCo** decouples the number of negatives from the <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch size</NoteLink> <Cite id="he2020" />. It keeps a queue of encoded keys
from recent batches, 65,536 in the paper, and uses them all as negatives. Keys in the queue were encoded at different
training steps. To keep them consistent, the key encoder is not trained by <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> but follows the query
encoder as an exponential moving average, $\thetavec_k \leftarrow m \thetavec_k + (1 - m) \thetavec_q$ with
$m = 0.999$. A key encoder copied directly from the query encoder at each step changed too fast, and training failed
<Cite id="he2020" />.

## Assumptions

- Two views of one input share the information that matters for downstream tasks, and what differs between views is
  nuisance.
- Negatives drawn from the data are mostly unrelated to the anchor. The bound in the derivation assumes they are
  independent draws from $p(y)$.

## Pitfalls

- **<Gloss name="fn">False negatives</Gloss>.** Two different images of dogs are negatives for each other. The loss pushes them apart, which
  works against class structure. The effect grows as the batch grows relative to the number of classes.
- **The bound saturates.** The estimate $\log N - \mathcal{L}$ cannot exceed $\log N$. When the true mutual information
  is larger, InfoNCE underestimates it badly, so it is a poor estimator of mutual information even when it trains good
  representations <Cite id="poole2019" />.
- **Augmentations define the invariances.** Colour jitter makes the representation ignore colour. That helps object
  classification and hurts a task, such as flower species, that depends on colour.
- **Batch size and temperature interact.** Changing either changes which negatives dominate the gradient. They must be
  tuned together.
- **Evaluate the right layer.** In SimCLR the representation before the projection head transfers better than the
  output of the head <Cite id="chen2020" />.
