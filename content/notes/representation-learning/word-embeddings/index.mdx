<Definition>
A word embedding assigns each word $w$ in a vocabulary $\Vcal$ a vector $\vvec_w \in \reals^d$, with $d$ in the tens to
hundreds and far smaller than $\abs{\Vcal}$. The vectors are trained on unlabelled text so that words appearing in
similar contexts have similar vectors, measured by dot product or cosine similarity. Word2vec's skip-gram model and GloVe
are the standard methods <Cite id="mikolov2013" /> <Cite id="pennington2014" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A one-hot vector for each word treats "cat" and "dog" as exactly as different as "cat" and "carburettor". Every pair
of one-hot vectors is orthogonal. A model built on them must learn about each word separately.

The **distributional hypothesis** says that words with similar meanings occur in similar contexts
<Cite id="harris1954" />. "Cat" and "dog" both appear near "pet", "fed" and "vet". A representation built from contexts
therefore places them close together, and anything a downstream model learns about one partly transfers to the other.

Embeddings are learned as a by-product of a prediction task on raw text, with no labels. This makes word2vec an early
instance of <NoteLink to="self-supervised-learning">self-supervised learning</NoteLink>.

## Skip-gram with negative sampling

Slide a window over a corpus. Each word $w$ at the centre and each word $c$ within $L$ positions of it form a
(word, context) pair. Let $\Dcal$ be the multiset of all such pairs, and write $\#(w, c)$, $\#(w)$ and $\#(c)$ for the
number of pairs, pairs with word $w$, and pairs with context $c$.

The **skip-gram** model keeps two vectors per word: $\vvec_w$ when the word is at the centre and $\uvec_c$ when it is a
context <Cite id="mikolov2013" />. It predicts contexts with a softmax over the vocabulary,
$p(c \mid w) = e^{\uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w} / \sum_{c'} e^{\uvec_{c'}^{\tr} \vvec_w}$. The normaliser sums over the whole
vocabulary, which costs $O(\abs{\Vcal} d)$ per pair.

**Negative sampling** replaces the softmax with binary classification <Cite id="mikolov2013b" />. Observed pairs are
labelled 1. For each observed pair, $k$ contexts $c_N$ are drawn from a noise distribution $P_n$ and labelled 0. With
$\sigma$ the logistic function, the objective for one pair is

$$
\ell(w, c) = \log \sigma(\uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w) + k\, \expect_{c_N \sim P_n}\brack{\log \sigma(-\uvec_{c_N}^{\tr} \vvec_w)}.
$$

This is <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on the score $\uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w$, and it
costs $O(k d)$ per pair instead of $O(\abs{\Vcal} d)$. Mikolov et al. used $k$ between 2 and 20 and the noise
distribution $P_n(c) \propto \#(c)^{3/4}$, which samples rare words more often than their frequency. They also discard
each occurrence of a frequent word with probability $1 - \sqrt{t / f(w)}$, where $f(w)$ is its relative frequency and
$t \approx 10^{-5}$ <Cite id="mikolov2013b" />.

## Negative sampling factorises a PMI matrix

Levy and Goldberg showed what skip-gram with negative sampling computes <Cite id="levy2014" />. Take the noise
distribution to be the empirical context distribution $P_{\Dcal}(c) = \#(c) / \abs{\Dcal}$, and sum the objective over
all pairs:

$$
\ell = \sum_{w} \sum_{c} \#(w, c) \log \sigma(\uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w)
+ \sum_{w} \#(w)\, k \sum_{c} \frac{\#(c)}{\abs{\Dcal}} \log \sigma(-\uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w).
$$

Collect the terms involving one pair $(w, c)$ and write $x = \uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w$:

$$
\ell(w, c) = \#(w, c) \log \sigma(x) + k \frac{\#(w)\, \#(c)}{\abs{\Dcal}} \log \sigma(-x).
$$

Suppose $d$ is large enough that each dot product $x$ can be set independently. The derivatives of the two logarithms
are $\frac{d}{dx} \log \sigma(x) = \sigma(-x)$ and $\frac{d}{dx} \log \sigma(-x) = -\sigma(x)$. Setting the derivative
of $\ell(w, c)$ to zero gives

$$
\#(w, c)\, \sigma(-x) = k \frac{\#(w)\, \#(c)}{\abs{\Dcal}} \sigma(x).
$$

The ratio $\sigma(-x) / \sigma(x)$ equals $e^{-x}$, because $\sigma(x) = e^{x} \sigma(-x)$. Solving,

$$
\uvec_c^{\tr} \vvec_w = x^* = \log \frac{\#(w, c)\, \abs{\Dcal}}{\#(w)\, \#(c)} - \log k = \operatorname{PMI}(w, c) - \log k.
$$

The quantity $\operatorname{PMI}(w, c) = \log \frac{p(w, c)}{p(w)\, p(c)}$ is the **pointwise mutual information**
between word and context, estimated from counts. At its optimum, skip-gram with negative sampling makes the matrix of
dot products $\Vmat \Umat^{\tr}$ equal the $\abs{\Vcal} \times \abs{\Vcal}$ matrix of PMI values shifted by $\log k$. With
$d \ll \abs{\Vcal}$ it cannot match every entry. It finds a low-rank approximation of the shifted PMI matrix, weighted
by how often each pair is observed or sampled.

This connects neural embeddings to older count-based methods. Levy and Goldberg obtained comparable word-similarity
results by building the shifted positive PMI matrix $\max(\operatorname{PMI} - \log k, 0)$ directly and taking its
truncated <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular value decomposition</NoteLink> <Cite id="levy2014" />.

### Worked example

In a corpus of $\abs{\Dcal} = 10{,}000$ pairs, suppose "coffee" appears in $\#(w) = 100$ pairs, "cup" in $\#(c) = 200$,
and the two together in $\#(w, c) = 20$. Then

$$
\operatorname{PMI} = \log \frac{20 \times 10{,}000}{100 \times 200} = \log 10 \approx 2.303.
$$

The pair occurs 10 times more often than if the words were independent. With $k = 5$ negatives the optimal dot product
is $\log 10 - \log 5 = \log 2 \approx 0.693$, and $\sigma(0.693) = 2/3$. A pair that occurs exactly as often as
independence predicts has PMI 0 and optimal dot product $-\log 5 \approx -1.609$.

## GloVe

GloVe fits the logarithm of the co-occurrence counts directly <Cite id="pennington2014" />. Let $X_{ij}$ be the number
of times word $j$ appears in the context of word $i$, and $X_i = \sum_j X_{ij}$.

Pennington et al. start from ratios. For probe words $k$, the ratio $P(k \mid i) / P(k \mid j)$ distinguishes $i$
from $j$: for $i = $ "ice", $j = $ "steam", it is large for $k = $ "solid" and small for $k = $ "gas". They ask for
vectors in which differences encode these ratios, $F\paren{(\wvec_i - \wvec_j)^{\tr} \tilde \wvec_k} = P(k \mid i) / P(k \mid j)$.
Requiring $F$ to turn a difference into a ratio gives $F = \exp$, and then $\wvec_i^{\tr} \tilde \wvec_k = \log P(k \mid i)
= \log X_{ik} - \log X_i$. The term $\log X_i$ does not depend on $k$, so it is absorbed into a bias $b_i$, and a bias
$\tilde b_k$ is added for symmetry. The model is $\wvec_i^{\tr} \tilde \wvec_k + b_i + \tilde b_k \approx \log X_{ik}$,
fitted by weighted least squares:

$$
J = \sum_{i, j:\, X_{ij} > 0} g(X_{ij}) \paren{\wvec_i^{\tr} \tilde \wvec_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}}^2,
\qquad g(x) = \min\paren{1, (x / x_{\max})^{3/4}},
$$

with $x_{\max} = 100$. The weight $g$ stops frequent pairs from dominating and gives zero weight to pairs never seen,
whose logarithm is undefined. If the biases absorb $\log X_i$ and $\log X_j$, the dot product fits
$\log X_{ij} - \log X_i - \log X_j$, which is the PMI up to an additive constant. GloVe and skip-gram fit the same kind
of matrix with different weightings.

## Analogies

Word vectors show linear regularities. The vector $\vvec_{\text{king}} - \vvec_{\text{man}} + \vvec_{\text{woman}}$ is
closer to $\vvec_{\text{queen}}$ than to any other word except the three inputs <Cite id="mikolov2013" />. The PMI view
explains when this can happen. If, for every context $c$,
$\operatorname{PMI}(\text{king}, c) - \operatorname{PMI}(\text{man}, c) \approx \operatorname{PMI}(\text{queen}, c) - \operatorname{PMI}(\text{woman}, c)$,
then the rows of the PMI matrix satisfy the analogy, and so does their linear factorisation. The condition says that
"king" differs from "man" in its contexts the way "queen" differs from "woman". Analogy benchmarks exclude the input
words from the search; without that exclusion the nearest vector is often one of the inputs.

## Assumptions

- Meaning is reflected in co-occurrence within a small window. Syntax beyond the window and word order within it are
  ignored.
- Each word type has one meaning. "Bank" gets one vector, an average of its financial and river senses weighted by
  frequency.

## Pitfalls

- **Rare and unseen words.** A word seen a few times gets a noisy vector, and a word never seen gets none. FastText
  represents each word as the sum of vectors for its character $n$-grams, so unseen words still get a vector
  <Cite id="bojanowski2017" />.
- **Documents need more than an average.** Averaging word vectors loses which words matched which. The <NoteLink
  to="word-movers-distance">word mover's distance</NoteLink> compares documents by transporting their words in the
  embedding space.
- **Frequency leaks into the geometry.** Vector norms and nearest neighbours correlate with word frequency. Normalising
  to unit length before comparing reduces the effect.
- **Social bias.** Embeddings reproduce associations in their training text, including gender stereotypes such as
  $\vvec_{\text{man}} - \vvec_{\text{woman}} \approx \vvec_{\text{programmer}} - \vvec_{\text{homemaker}}$
  <Cite id="bolukbasi2016" />. Downstream systems inherit them.
- **Static embeddings are context-free.** The same vector serves every sentence. Contextual encoders trained by
  <NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">masked language modelling</NoteLink> produce a different vector for each
  occurrence.
