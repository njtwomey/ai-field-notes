<Definition>
A non-contrastive (joint-embedding) method trains an encoder so that two augmented views $x_A$ and $x_B$ of the same
input have similar embeddings $\zvec_A$ and $\zvec_B$, using positive pairs only. Pulling views together is minimised by
a constant encoder, so every method adds one mechanism against this **collapse**. BYOL and SimSiam use an asymmetric
architecture with a predictor and a stop-gradient. Barlow Twins and VICReg add terms computed over the batch that
require each embedding dimension to vary and to be decorrelated from the others.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">Contrastive learning</NoteLink> needs many negatives, which means large batches or
a queue. The negatives do one job: they stop every input from mapping to the same point. Non-contrastive methods ask
whether that job can be done another way.

Two answers work. The first changes the dynamics. One branch predicts the other branch's output, and the other branch
is held fixed during each update. The target moves only slowly, so a collapsed solution is never pursued directly. The
second changes the loss. Collapse means the embedding does not vary across inputs, so a term that measures variation
across the batch can forbid it.

## The collapse problem

Take the loss that only asks views to agree, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \expect\brack{\norm{f(x_A) - f(x_B)}^2}$.
Every term is non-negative, so $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} \ge 0$. The constant encoder $f(x) = \cvec$ gives
$f(x_A) - f(x_B) = \zeros$ for every pair, so it attains the minimum. It is useless: it carries no information about
the input. <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</NoteLink> on $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$ alone finds it.

A subtler failure is **dimensional collapse**. The embeddings vary, but only within a low-dimensional subspace of
$\reals^D$. Every coordinate is then a linear function of a few underlying ones, and most of the capacity is wasted.

## Asymmetric methods: BYOL and SimSiam

**BYOL** has an online network with parameters $\thetavec$ (encoder, projector and predictor $q_{\thetavec}$) and a
target network with parameters $\xivec$ (encoder and projector only) <Cite id="grill2020" />. The online network sees
view $A$ and predicts the target network's projection of view $B$. With both vectors normalised to unit length, the
loss is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\thetavec} = \norm{\bar q_{\thetavec}(\zvec_A) - \bar \zvec'_B}^2 = 2 - 2 \cos\paren{q_{\thetavec}(\zvec_A), \zvec'_B},
$$

symmetrised by swapping the views. The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> updates only $\thetavec$. The target follows as an exponential moving
average, $\xivec \leftarrow \beta \xivec + (1 - \beta) \thetavec$, with $\beta$ raised from 0.996 towards 1 during
training. A ResNet-50 trained this way reached 74.3% top-1 <Gloss name="imagenet" /> accuracy under linear evaluation, with no negative
pairs <Cite id="grill2020" />.

**SimSiam** removes the moving average <Cite id="chen2021" />. Both branches share one encoder $f$ and projector. A
predictor $h$ is applied on one branch, and the other branch is passed through a stop-gradient, written
$\operatorname{sg}$, which treats its argument as a constant when differentiating:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 D\paren{h(\zvec_A), \operatorname{sg}(\zvec_B)} + \tfrac12 D\paren{h(\zvec_B), \operatorname{sg}(\zvec_A)},
$$

with $D$ the negative <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink>. Without the stop-gradient, SimSiam collapsed at once: the <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink> of
the normalised outputs across the batch fell to zero and linear-probe accuracy to 0.1%, chance level for 1000 classes
<Cite id="chen2021" />. Without the predictor it also collapsed.

### What the stop-gradient optimises

Chen and He interpret SimSiam as alternating minimisation of a two-argument loss <Cite id="chen2021" />. Let $\mathcal{T}$
be a random augmentation, $F_{\thetavec}$ the network, and $\etavec_x$ a free vector for each training image $x$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\thetavec, \etavec) = \expect_{x, \mathcal{T}}\brack{\norm{F_{\thetavec}(\mathcal{T}(x)) - \etavec_x}^2}.
$$

Minimise over $\etavec$ with $\thetavec$ fixed. The loss is a sum of separate quadratics in each $\etavec_x$. Setting the
<NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> $-2\,\expect_{\mathcal{T}}[F_{\thetavec}(\mathcal{T}(x)) - \etavec_x]$ to zero gives

$$
\etavec_x = \expect_{\mathcal{T}}\brack{F_{\thetavec}(\mathcal{T}(x))},
$$

the average embedding of $x$ over augmentations. Then minimise over $\thetavec$ with $\etavec$ fixed. Because $\etavec$
is held constant, no gradient flows into it: this is the stop-gradient. Approximating the expectation by a single
augmentation, $\etavec_x \approx F_{\thetavec}(\mathcal{T}'(x))$, and taking one gradient step on $\thetavec$ recovers
SimSiam without the predictor. The predictor $h$ is hypothesised to learn the missing expectation over augmentations,
since the best predictor of one view's embedding from another's is the conditional mean.

This view explains why the stop-gradient is needed: without it the method is plain gradient descent on
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$, which collapses. It does not prove that the alternating scheme avoids collapse, since the
constant encoder is still a fixed point of it. No general proof exists. For linear networks, Tian et al. show that the
predictor, the stop-gradient and <Gloss name="weight-decay">weight decay</Gloss> together create dynamics in which non-collapsed solutions are stable and
the predictor aligns with the eigenvectors of the embedding correlation matrix <Cite id="tian2021" />.

## Regularised methods: Barlow Twins and VICReg

These methods use a symmetric architecture and ordinary gradients, and add a term that a collapsed solution cannot
satisfy.

**Barlow Twins** standardises each embedding dimension over a batch of $B$ inputs to mean 0 and variance 1, then forms
the $D \times D$ cross-correlation matrix between the two views <Cite id="zbontar2021" />:

$$
C_{ij} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} z^A_{b,i}\, z^B_{b,j}, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BT}} = \sum_{i=1}^{D} (1 - C_{ii})^2 + \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{D} \sum_{j \ne i} C_{ij}^2.
$$

Each $C_{ij}$ is the <NoteLink to="pearson-correlation">Pearson correlation</NoteLink> between dimension $i$ of view $A$ and dimension $j$ of view $B$, so
$-1 \le C_{ij} \le 1$. The loss is zero exactly when $\Cmat = \eye$.

- **Invariance.** $C_{ii} = 1$ means dimension $i$ of the two views is perfectly correlated across the batch: the two
  views get the same value up to the standardisation.
- **No complete collapse.** A correlation of 1 requires dimension $i$ to vary across the batch. A constant embedding
  has zero variance, so standardisation cannot make it correlate: $C_{ii} = 0$ (implementations add a small $\epsilon$
  to the variance) and the invariance term equals $D$, far above its minimum of 0.
- **No dimensional collapse.** If dimensions $i$ and $j$ carry the same information, they are correlated, and $C_{ij}^2$
  is large. If the embedding lies on a single line in $\reals^D$, every pair of standardised dimensions has correlation
  $\pm 1$, and the off-diagonal term equals $\lambda D(D - 1)$.

The off-diagonal term is a redundancy-reduction penalty: each dimension must carry information that the others do not.
Zbontar et al. used $\lambda = 0.005$ and found that the method benefits from very wide embeddings, with $D$ in the
thousands <Cite id="zbontar2021" />.

**VICReg** separates the three roles into three terms over embedding batches $\Zmat, \Zmat' \in \reals^{B \times D}$
<Cite id="bardes2022" />:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \lambda\, s(\Zmat, \Zmat') + \mu \brack{v(\Zmat) + v(\Zmat')} + \nu \brack{c(\Zmat) + c(\Zmat')}.
$$

- **Invariance** $s$ is the mean squared distance between paired embeddings.
- **Variance** $v(\Zmat) = \frac{1}{D} \sum_{j=1}^{D} \max\paren{0, \gamma - \sqrt{\var(\zvec_{:,j}) + \epsilon}}$ is a
  hinge on the standard deviation of each dimension over the batch, with $\gamma = 1$. A constant encoder has every
  standard deviation near 0 and pays $\mu \gamma$ per branch. Any encoder whose dimensions each have standard deviation
  at least $\gamma$ pays nothing. The hinge rules out complete collapse without pushing variances higher than needed.
- **Covariance** $c(\Zmat) = \frac{1}{D} \sum_{i \ne j} \operatorname{Cov}(\Zmat)_{ij}^2$ penalises off-diagonal
  covariance and rules out dimensional collapse, as in Barlow Twins.

Bardes et al. used $\lambda = \mu = 25$ and $\nu = 1$. Adding the variance term to other methods, including SimSiam
without a predictor, prevented their collapse <Cite id="bardes2022" />.

## Comparison

| Method       | Against collapse                           | Needs                                   |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| BYOL         | predictor, stop-gradient, moving-average target | moving-average schedule            |
| SimSiam      | predictor, stop-gradient                   | predictor tuning                        |
| Barlow Twins | cross-correlation driven to the identity   | wide projector, batch statistics        |
| VICReg       | variance hinge and covariance penalty      | three loss weights, batch statistics    |

Self-distillation methods such as DINO sit between these families. A student network matches the output distribution
of a moving-average teacher, and the teacher's outputs are centred and sharpened to prevent collapse
<Cite id="caron2021" />. The moving-average target is the same device MoCo uses to keep its keys consistent
<Cite id="he2020" />.

## Assumptions

- As in contrastive learning, the augmentations define what the representation ignores. The two views must share the
  information that later tasks need.
- Barlow Twins and VICReg estimate correlations and variances from a batch. The batch must be large enough, relative to
  $D$, for these estimates to be meaningful.

## Pitfalls

- **Silent collapse.** Training loss can look healthy while the embedding has collapsed. Monitor the per-dimension
  standard deviation of normalised outputs across the batch, which is about $1/\sqrt{D}$ for well-spread embeddings and
  0 for collapsed ones <Cite id="chen2021" />, and the rank of the embedding covariance.
- **Fragile hyperparameters.** BYOL and SimSiam depend on the predictor's <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> and weight decay, and BYOL also on the
  target's moving-average rate. Settings that work for one architecture can collapse on another.
- **No guarantee from the loss alone.** For BYOL and SimSiam the collapsed solution remains a fixed point. Their
  success is a property of the training dynamics, not of the minimum of an objective.
