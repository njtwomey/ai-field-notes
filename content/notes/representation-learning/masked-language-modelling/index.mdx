<Definition>
Masked language modelling (MLM) trains a model on unlabelled text. For each sequence $\xvec = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ a
random set $\Mcal$ of positions is selected, and the tokens there are corrupted to give $\tilde \xvec$. A bidirectional
<NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> encoder reads $\tilde \xvec$ and is trained to predict each original
token:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\expect_{\xvec, \Mcal}\brack{\sum_{i \in \Mcal} \log p_{\thetavec}(x_i \mid \tilde \xvec)}.
$$

BERT selects 15% of positions and replaces the selected tokens with a `[MASK]` token 80% of the time, a random token 10%
of the time, and leaves them unchanged 10% of the time <Cite id="devlin2019" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A left-to-right <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> predicts each word from the words before it. Its representation of a word can use only
the left context. Many tasks, such as deciding what "it" refers to or answering a question about a passage, need both
sides.

A bidirectional encoder cannot be trained as a next-word predictor. Every position attends to every other position,
including the one it must predict, so after one layer the answer is already in the representation and the task is
trivial. Masking removes the answer from the input. The model must infer each hidden word from its context on both
sides, like a reader filling in a cloze test.

## What the model learns

For one masked position $i$, write $\xvec_{\setminus \Mcal}$ for the unmasked tokens. The expected loss at that position
is the cross-entropy between the true conditional $p(x_i \mid \xvec_{\setminus \Mcal})$ and the model's
$q(x_i) = p_{\thetavec}(x_i \mid \tilde \xvec)$. Cross-entropy splits as

$$
\expect_{x_i \sim p}\brack{-\log q(x_i)} = \entropy\paren{p} + \KL\paren{p \,\|\, q}.
$$

The <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> does not depend on the model, and the <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">KL divergence</NoteLink> is zero only
when $q = p$. So the best possible model outputs the true <NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> of each masked token given the
unmasked ones.

This differs from a <NoteLink to="autoregressive-transformer-language-model">causal language model</NoteLink> in two ways. A causal model learns
$p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$ for every $t$, and the product of these conditionals is the joint probability of the
sequence. The MLM conditionals for different masked positions are predicted independently given the same unmasked
tokens, and their product is not the joint distribution of the masked tokens. In "`[MASK]` `[MASK]` is a large city",
both "New York" and "Los Angeles" fit. Predicted independently, the first position may favour "New" and the second
"Angeles", and the product of the two conditionals scores "New Angeles" highly. MLM is a representation-learning
objective, not a generative model.

## The 80/10/10 corruption scheme

Fine-tuning and inference never show the model a `[MASK]` token. If every selected token became `[MASK]`, the model
would learn that unmasked tokens are always correct and could simply copy them. The representations of ordinary
tokens would then receive no training signal about their own identity from context, and pretraining would see a
different input distribution from fine-tuning <Cite id="devlin2019" />.

The random and unchanged cases fix this. The model now cannot tell, from a visible ordinary token, whether it is the
original.

### Worked example

With a vocabulary of $V = 30{,}522$ <Gloss name="wordpiece" /> tokens, as in the released BERT models, consider how often a visible
token is wrong.

- A position shows an ordinary token (not `[MASK]`) with probability $0.85 + 0.15 \times 0.2 = 0.88$: it was not
  selected, or it was selected and given the random or unchanged treatment.
- A position shows a random replacement with probability $0.15 \times 0.1 = 0.015$.
- Given that a position shows an ordinary token, the chance that it is a random replacement is
  $0.015 / 0.88 = 1.7\%$. The chance that it was selected for prediction at all is $0.03 / 0.88 = 3.4\%$.

The model must therefore check every visible token against its context, because about one in 60 is wrong. The random
replacement rate is low enough that the model still mostly sees real text.

For a selected position, the input equals the original token with probability $0.1 + 0.1 / V \approx 0.100$: the
unchanged case, plus the rare random draw of the original token.

## BERT

BERT pretrained a transformer encoder on MLM and a second task, next sentence prediction: given two segments, predict
whether the second followed the first in the corpus <Cite id="devlin2019" />. A special `[CLS]` token at the start of
each input has a final representation used for classification, and `[SEP]` separates the segments. BERT-Base has 12
layers, width 768 and 12 heads (110 million parameters). BERT-Large has 24 layers, width 1024 and 16 heads (340 million
parameters). Inputs are at most 512 tokens.

To use BERT on a task, a small output layer is added and the whole network is fine-tuned on labelled data (see
<NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">transfer learning and fine-tuning</NoteLink>). BERT set new results on
eleven language-understanding benchmarks, including <Gloss name="glue" /> and <Gloss name="squad" />, this way <Cite id="devlin2019" />.

RoBERTa kept the MLM objective and changed the recipe <Cite id="liu2019" />. It dropped next sentence prediction, which
did not help. It re-sampled the masked positions each time a sequence was seen (dynamic masking) instead of fixing them
once during preprocessing. It trained longer, on about ten times as much text, with larger batches. The same
architecture then matched or exceeded every model published after BERT on the benchmarks reported.

## Variants

- **Span corruption.** <Gloss name="t5" /> replaces contiguous spans of tokens, 15% of tokens in total with a mean span length of 3, by
  single sentinel tokens, and trains an encoder–decoder to output the missing spans <Cite id="raffel2020" />. The
  targets are shorter than the input, which saves computation.
- **Masking rate.** A higher rate gives more predictions per sequence but less context for each. BERT's 15% is a
  convention, not an optimum derived from theory.

## Cost

Only the selected 15% of positions contribute to the loss. A 512-token sequence yields about $0.15 \times 512 \approx 77$
predictions, while a causal language model trained on the same sequence makes 512. The encoder still processes all 512
tokens. MLM therefore extracts less training signal per unit of computation than next-token prediction.

## Assumptions

- The context around a token, on both sides, determines it well enough to make prediction a useful task.
- Masked positions are chosen uniformly at random. Whole-word masking, which masks all pieces of a word together, and span masking change which predictions are easy.

## Pitfalls

- **Not a text generator.** MLM gives conditionals for masked positions, not a joint distribution over sequences.
  Sampling text from an MLM model needs special procedures and works poorly compared with a causal model.
- **Subword leakage.** When a word is split into several WordPiece tokens and only one is masked, the others give it
  away. Whole-word masking masks all pieces of a word together.
- **Pretrain–fine-tune mismatch remains.** The `[MASK]` token still appears only in pretraining. The 80/10/10 scheme
  reduces the mismatch but does not remove it.
- **<NoteLink to="sentence-embeddings">Sentence embeddings</NoteLink> from `[CLS]`.** Without fine-tuning, the `[CLS]` vector of a pretrained MLM encoder is a weak
  sentence embedding, worse than averaged <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">GloVe</NoteLink> vectors on semantic similarity. Fine-tuning the encoder with a
  sentence-pair objective fixes this <Cite id="reimers2019" />.
