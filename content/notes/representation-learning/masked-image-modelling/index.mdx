<Definition>
Masked image modelling divides an image into $N$ non-overlapping patches, selects a random subset $\Mcal$ of them with
mask ratio $r = \abs{\Mcal} / N$, and trains a network to predict the content of the masked patches from the visible
ones. The prediction target can be the raw pixels (MAE, SimMIM), discrete tokens from a pretrained image tokeniser
(BEiT), or features. The trained encoder is kept as a representation for other tasks
<Cite id="he2022" /> <Cite id="bao2022" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

<NoteLink to="masked-language-modelling">Masked language modelling</NoteLink> works because predicting a missing word
requires understanding its sentence. The same recipe applied to images must account for a difference in density.
Words carry a lot of meaning each, so hiding 15% of them already makes the task hard. Pixels are highly redundant. A
hidden patch surrounded by visible neighbours can be filled in by interpolation, which needs no understanding of the
scene.

Masking most of the image removes that shortcut. With 75% of patches hidden, there are large regions with no nearby
visible pixels. Filling them in requires knowing what objects are and how they continue: that a visible half-wheel
implies a car, or that a leg implies a body above it <Cite id="he2022" />.

## The masked autoencoder

The MAE has four parts <Cite id="he2022" />.

1. **Patchify and mask.** A $224 \times 224$ image is cut into $16 \times 16$ patches, as in the <Gloss name="vit">vision transformer</Gloss>
   <Cite id="dosovitskiy2021" />. A random 75% of patches are removed, sampled uniformly without replacement.
2. **Encoder on visible patches only.** A vision transformer embeds each visible patch, adds its positional embedding,
   and processes only these patches. No mask tokens enter the encoder.
3. **Lightweight decoder.** The encoded visible patches are put back in their positions, and every masked position gets
   a shared learned mask token. All tokens get positional embeddings, and a small <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> decoder processes the full
   set.
4. **Pixel loss on masked patches.** A linear layer predicts the pixel values of each patch. The loss is the <Gloss name="mse">mean
   squared error</Gloss>, computed on masked patches only. The target pixels of each patch are normalised by that patch's mean
   and <NoteLink to="variance-and-covariance">standard deviation</NoteLink>, which improved the representation.

After pretraining, the decoder is discarded. The encoder is applied to whole, unmasked images for downstream tasks.

The loss is restricted to masked patches because predicting a visible patch is an identity task. Including visible
patches in the loss lowered downstream accuracy <Cite id="he2022" />.

## Why the asymmetric design saves compute

Let the encoder have $L$ transformer blocks of width $d$. A block on $n$ tokens costs about $a n d^2$ for the linear
layers and $b n^2 d$ for the attention scores and weighted sums, for constants $a$ and $b$. With mask ratio $r$ the
encoder sees $n = (1 - r) N$ tokens instead of $N$. The ratio of costs is

$$
\frac{a (1 - r) N d^2 + b (1 - r)^2 N^2 d}{a N d^2 + b N^2 d}.
$$

This lies between $(1 - r)^2$ and $1 - r$. It equals $1 - r$ when the linear layers dominate ($N \ll d$) and
approaches $(1 - r)^2$ when attention dominates ($N \gg d$). With $r = 0.75$ the encoder is between 4 and 16 times
cheaper per image. The decoder processes all $N$ tokens but is narrow and shallow: the default has 8 blocks of width
512, under a tenth of the encoder's computation per token <Cite id="he2022" />. He et al. report training 3 times or
more faster than an encoder that processes mask tokens.

### Worked example

A $224 \times 224$ image with $16 \times 16$ patches has $N = (224 / 16)^2 = 14^2 = 196$ patches. At $r = 0.75$ the
encoder sees $49$. Each attention score matrix shrinks from $196^2 = 38{,}416$ to $49^2 = 2{,}401$ entries, a factor of
16. The linear layers process 49 rows instead of 196, a factor of 4. For ViT-Large, with width $d = 1024$ much larger
than $N = 196$, the linear layers dominate and the saving is close to 4.

## Variants

- **BEiT** first trains a discrete image tokeniser, then masks about 40% of patches, replaces them with a mask
  embedding inside the encoder, and predicts the tokeniser's code for each masked patch with a softmax, exactly as
  BERT predicts words <Cite id="bao2022" />.
- **SimMIM** feeds mask tokens through the encoder, predicts raw pixels with a single linear layer, and uses an $\ell_1$
  loss on masked patches <Cite id="xie2022" />. It works with hierarchical vision transformers, whose windowed
  attention cannot drop patches as easily as a plain ViT.

## Evaluation

MAE representations are strong after fine-tuning and weaker under a frozen linear probe. A ViT-Huge encoder pretrained
with MAE and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1K reached 87.8% top-1 accuracy, the best among methods using only ImageNet-1K data
at the time <Cite id="he2022" />. Under linear probing, MAE features trail those of
<NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive</NoteLink> methods. He et al. found that fine-tuning only the last one or
two blocks closes most of the gap, which suggests MAE features are rich but not linearly separable. See
<NoteLink to="embedding-evaluation">embedding evaluation</NoteLink> for the two protocols.

## Assumptions

- Images contain structure at the scale of several patches, so that masked regions are predictable from visible ones
  given an understanding of the scene.
- The encoder must handle a variable set of tokens. This is natural for a plain transformer and awkward for a
  convolutional network, whose sliding kernels assume a full grid.

## Pitfalls

- **Train–test mismatch in token count.** The encoder sees 25% of patches during pretraining and 100% afterwards. The
  change in sequence length changes attention statistics; fine-tuning adapts to it.
- **Mask ratio is task-specific.** The 75% optimum is for natural images with $16 \times 16$ patches. Denser signals or
  smaller patches favour different ratios.
- **Reconstructions look blurry.** The mean squared error rewards the average of plausible completions. Blurry outputs
  are expected and do not mean the representation is poor.
- **Linear-probe comparisons mislead.** Ranking MAE against contrastive methods by linear probing alone understates
  MAE, and ranking by fine-tuning alone overstates it for frozen-feature use.
