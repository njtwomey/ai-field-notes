<Definition>
CLIP (contrastive language–image pretraining) has an image encoder $f$ and a text encoder $g$, each followed by a
linear projection into a shared $D$-dimensional space and normalisation to unit length. For a batch of $N$ image–text
pairs with embeddings $\uvec_i = f(\text{image}_i)$ and $\vvec_j = g(\text{text}_j)$, the logits are
$\ell_{ij} = \uvec_i^{\tr} \vvec_j / \tau$ with a learned temperature $\tau$. The loss is the average of a cross-entropy
that matches each image to its text over the rows of $\ell$ and one that matches each text to its image over the
columns <Cite id="radford2021" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A standard image classifier is trained on a fixed list of classes. Adding a class means collecting labelled images and
retraining. Captions found alongside images on the web describe far more concepts than any label set, and need no
annotation effort.

CLIP learns from 400 million such pairs <Cite id="radford2021" />. It does not try to generate the caption. It learns
which caption, among those in the batch, belongs to which image. After training, an image and a sentence that describes
it lie close together in the shared space. Classifying an image then means writing a sentence for each class and asking
which sentence is closest.

## Training objective

Within a batch, pair $i$ is the only positive for image $i$, and the other $N - 1$ texts are negatives. The
image-to-text loss is the cross-entropy of row $i$ of the logit matrix with target $i$. The text-to-image loss does the
same for column $j$:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \brack{-\log \frac{e^{\ell_{ii}}}{\sum_{j} e^{\ell_{ij}}}}
+ \frac{1}{2N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \brack{-\log \frac{e^{\ell_{jj}}}{\sum_{i} e^{\ell_{ij}}}}.
$$

<ClipDiagram />

Each half is the InfoNCE loss of <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive learning</NoteLink> with $N$
candidates. By the InfoNCE bound, each half gives $I(\text{image}; \text{text}) \ge \log N - \mathcal{L}_{\text{half}}$
<Cite id="oord2018" />. The loss trains both encoders to keep the information that images and their captions share.

CLIP used batches of 32,768 pairs, so each image is contrasted with 32,767 texts. The temperature is learned as a
log-parameterised scale, initialised to $\tau = 0.07$ and clipped so that $1/\tau$ never exceeds 100
<Cite id="radford2021" />.

Radford et al. compared this objective with generating the caption word by word and with predicting its
<NoteLink to="bag-of-words">bag of words</NoteLink>. Predicting a bag of words learned zero-shot
<Gloss name="imagenet" /> accuracy 3 times faster than generating captions, and the contrastive objective another 4
times faster <Cite id="radford2021" />. Matching is easier than generating: the model need not predict the exact wording
of a caption, only recognise which caption fits.

## Zero-shot classification

Given $K$ class names, write one sentence per class from a template, such as "a photo of a `{class}`.", and embed each
with the text encoder to get $\vvec_1, \dots, \vvec_K$. For an image with embedding $\uvec$,

$$
p(y = k \mid \text{image}) = \frac{\exp(\uvec^{\tr} \vvec_k / \tau)}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K} \exp(\uvec^{\tr} \vvec_{k'} / \tau)}.
$$

This is multinomial <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> on the image embedding with
weight vector $\vvec_k / \tau$ for class $k$. The text encoder generates the classifier's weights from the class names,
so no labelled images of the new classes are needed. It is also the training objective restricted to one image and
$K$ candidate texts.

**Prompt engineering.** A bare class name is ambiguous ("crane" the bird or the machine) and
unlike the captions seen in training. Averaging the text embeddings of many templates for each class (prompt ensembling,
80 templates for ImageNet) improved ImageNet accuracy over a single default template by about 5 percentage points
together with prompt engineering <Cite id="radford2021" />. Zero-shot CLIP reached 76.2% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet,
matching the original ResNet-50, without using any of the 1.28 million labelled training images
<Cite id="radford2021" />.

### Worked example

An image has cosine similarities 0.31, 0.26 and 0.22 with the prompts for "dog", "cat" and "horse". The similarities
look close, but CLIP's learned scale $1/\tau$ reaches 100. The logits are then 31, 26 and 22, and the softmax gives

$$
p(\text{dog}) = \frac{e^{31}}{e^{31} + e^{26} + e^{22}} = 0.993, \qquad p(\text{cat}) = 0.0067, \qquad
p(\text{horse}) = 0.0001.
$$

At $\tau = 0.07$, the value at initialisation, the logits are 4.43, 3.71 and 3.14, and the probabilities are 0.566,
0.277 and 0.157. The ranking is the same; the learned temperature decides how confident the model is.

## Related models

- **ALIGN** trains the same dual-encoder objective on 1.8 billion noisy image–alt-text pairs with minimal filtering,
  and shows that scale compensates for noise <Cite id="jia2021" />.
- **SigLIP** replaces the softmax with an independent binary classification of every image–text pair in the batch,
  $-\log \sigma\paren{z_{ij} (\uvec_i^{\tr} \vvec_j / \tau + b)}$ with $z_{ij} = 1$ for matching pairs and $-1$
  otherwise <Cite id="zhai2023" />. The loss has no normaliser over the batch, so it needs no all-to-all communication
  of embeddings across devices and works well at smaller <NoteLink to="minibatching-and-batch-size">batch
  sizes</NoteLink>.

## Uses

CLIP embeddings serve image search by text and text search by image, zero-shot classification, and as a frozen image
encoder for other models. The <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> between an image and a caption
also serves as an evaluation metric for generated images, <NoteLink to="clip-score">CLIPScore</NoteLink>.

## Assumptions

- Web captions describe the content of their images well enough, on average, to serve as supervision.
- The concepts needed downstream appear, with their names, in the pretraining captions. Zero-shot accuracy is poor on
  concepts and tasks that captions rarely cover, such as counting objects or estimating distances
  <Cite id="radford2021" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Bag-of-concepts behaviour.** Contrastive matching can be solved by detecting which objects and attributes are
  present without their relations. CLIP-style models often fail to prefer the correct caption over one with the same
  words in a different order, such as "the horse is eating the grass" against "the grass is eating the horse"
  <Cite id="yuksekgonul2023" />.
- **Prompt sensitivity.** Small changes in wording change zero-shot accuracy by several points. Report the templates
  used.
- **Distribution of the pretraining data.** The web data is not public, and its biases, including social stereotypes,
  pass into the model's similarities.
- **Temperature at inference.** Zero-shot probabilities use the learned $1/\tau$, which makes them very confident. They
  are not calibrated.
