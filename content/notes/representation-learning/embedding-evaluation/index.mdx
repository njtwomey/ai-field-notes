<Definition>
Embedding evaluation measures the quality of a representation $f: \Xcal \to \reals^D$ that is kept frozen. **Linear
probing** trains a linear classifier on $f(x)$ for a labelled task and reports its test accuracy. **$k$-nearest-neighbour
probing** classifies each test point by a similarity-weighted vote among its $k$ closest training embeddings.
**Alignment and uniformity** measure, without labels, how close the embeddings of related inputs are and how evenly
all embeddings spread over the unit sphere <Cite id="alain2016" /> <Cite id="wu2018" /> <Cite id="wang2020" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A self-supervised model is trained on a task nobody cares about, such as matching two crops of an image. Its value lies
in how useful its features are for other tasks. Measuring that directly would mean building every downstream system.
Probes are a cheap stand-in: attach the simplest possible model to the frozen features and see how well it does.

A simple probe has little capacity of its own. If it succeeds, the information was already in the features in an easily
usable form. A deep probe could learn the task from poor features, and would measure the probe rather than the
representation.

## Linear probing

Compute the features $\hvec_i = f(x_i)$ of every labelled training example once. Fit multinomial
<NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>, $p(y = c \mid \hvec) \propto \exp(\wvec_c^{\tr} \hvec + b_c)$,
by minimising cross-entropy, usually with an $\ell_2$ penalty chosen on a validation split. Report accuracy on a held-out
test set.

The fit is a convex problem in $(\wvec_c, b_c)$, because the cross-entropy of a softmax is convex in the logits and the
logits are linear in the parameters. Any reasonable optimiser reaches the same minimum. The result therefore depends on
the features and the regularisation, not on training luck, which makes it a reproducible benchmark.

Linear probing measures **linear separability**: whether the classes lie on different sides of hyperplanes in feature
space. On ImageNet it is the standard protocol for self-supervised image models. SimCLR's ResNet-50 features reached
76.5% top-1 accuracy under it <Cite id="chen2020" />. Alain and Bengio trained linear probes on every layer of a network
to see where class information becomes linearly available; it grows monotonically with depth <Cite id="alain2016" />.

## Nearest-neighbour probing

Normalise every embedding to unit length. For a test embedding $\hvec$, find the $k$ training embeddings
$\hvec_1, \dots, \hvec_k$ with the highest cosine similarity $s_i = \hvec^{\tr} \hvec_i$. Each neighbour votes for its
label with weight $e^{s_i / \tau}$, and the predicted class is

$$
\hat y = \argmax_c \sum_{i=1}^{k} \indicator[y_i = c]\, e^{s_i / \tau}.
$$

Wu et al. introduced this weighted vote with $\tau = 0.07$ <Cite id="wu2018" />. Caron et al. found $k = 20$ worked best
for their models <Cite id="caron2021" />. The temperature decides how much a closer neighbour counts: two neighbours
whose similarities differ by $\Delta$ have weights in the ratio $e^{\Delta / \tau}$.

A $k$-NN probe needs no training and no hyperparameter search beyond $k$ and $\tau$. It measures **local** structure:
whether each point's nearest neighbours share its label. A representation can have good local structure and poor
linear separability, for example when one class occupies several separate clusters. The two probes can therefore rank
models differently.

### Worked example

A test image has three nearest neighbours with cosine similarities 0.90 (class A), 0.85 (class B) and 0.80 (class B).
With $\tau = 0.07$, relative to the first neighbour's weight,

$$
e^{(0.85 - 0.90)/0.07} = e^{-0.714} = 0.490, \qquad e^{(0.80 - 0.90)/0.07} = e^{-1.429} = 0.240.
$$

Class A scores 1 and class B scores $0.490 + 0.240 = 0.730$. The weighted vote picks A, although a majority vote would
pick B. With a large $\tau$ the weights approach 1 each, and the vote becomes a plain majority.

## Alignment and uniformity

Wang and Isola define two label-free properties of normalised embeddings <Cite id="wang2020" />. For positive pairs
$(x, y)$, such as two augmentations of one input, and independent inputs $x, x'$,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \expect_{(x, y)}\brack{\norm{f(x) - f(y)}^2}, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{uniform}} = \log \expect_{x, x'}\brack{e^{-t \norm{f(x) - f(x')}^2}}, \quad t = 2.
$$

Both are better when lower. Alignment is small when related inputs map close together. Uniformity is small when
embeddings are spread out: the exponential decays with distance, so its average is large only if many pairs are close.
For unit vectors $\norm{\uvec - \vvec}^2 = 2 - 2 \uvec^{\tr} \vvec$, so both quantities are functions of cosine
similarity.

The two quantities pull against each other. A constant encoder has perfect alignment, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = 0$,
and the worst uniformity, $\log e^0 = 0$. An encoder that ignores which inputs are related can spread embeddings evenly
but has poor alignment. Useful representations score well on both. Wang and Isola showed that the
<NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive</NoteLink> InfoNCE loss approaches a sum of an alignment term and a
uniformity term as the number of negatives grows, that the two metrics agreed well with downstream accuracy, and that
optimising them directly worked as well as contrastive training <Cite id="wang2020" />.

For embeddings spread uniformly around the unit circle in two dimensions, the uniformity is
$\log \paren{e^{-4} I_0(4)} \approx -1.575$, where $I_0$ is a modified Bessel function. Clustering the points raises
it towards 0.

## Other protocols

- **Fine-tuning.** Train the whole network on the labelled task, starting from the pretrained weights (see
  <NoteLink to="transfer-learning-and-fine-tuning">transfer learning and fine-tuning</NoteLink>). This measures the
  representation as an initialisation. Masked autoencoders score much better under fine-tuning than under linear
  probing <Cite id="he2022" />.
- **Low-shot probing.** Train the probe on 1% or 10% of the labels. This measures how quickly the representation
  supports learning from few examples.
- **Word embeddings.** Static word vectors are evaluated by the rank correlation between their cosine similarities and
  human similarity ratings of word pairs, and by analogy accuracy (see <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word
  embeddings</NoteLink>).
- **Cross-lingual embeddings without parallel text.** <NoteLink to="backretrieval">Backretrieval</NoteLink> retrieves a
  target-language caption for each source-language caption and checks whether the retrieved caption's image finds the
  query's own image among the source images.

## Assumptions

- The probe tasks represent the tasks the representation will be used for. A representation tuned for ImageNet classes
  can rank poorly for fine-grained or dense prediction tasks.
- The labelled evaluation data is disjoint from the pretraining data.

## Pitfalls

- **Protocol details change rankings.** Probe regularisation, feature normalisation, data augmentation during probing,
  the layer probed and the number of training epochs all change the reported accuracy. Compare only numbers obtained
  with the same protocol.
- **Which layer.** In contrastive models the representation before the projection head transfers better than its
  output <Cite id="chen2020" />. Probing the wrong layer understates the model.
- **One protocol is not enough.** Linear probing, $k$-NN and fine-tuning can order the same models differently. A method
  that wins under one can lose under another <Cite id="he2022" />. Fine-tuning can also improve in-distribution accuracy
  while hurting robustness to distribution shift <Cite id="kumar2022" />.
- **Uniformity depends on $t$ and dimension.** Its absolute value is only comparable between embeddings of the same
  dimension, measured with the same $t$.
