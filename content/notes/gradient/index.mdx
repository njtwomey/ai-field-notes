<Definition>
For a differentiable $f : \reals^n \to \reals$, the gradient at $\xvec$ is the vector of partial derivatives

$$
\nabla f(\xvec) = \paren{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\xvec), \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\xvec)}^\top.
$$

Each partial derivative is the rate of change of $f$ when one coordinate moves and the others stay fixed
<Cite id="deisenroth2020" />.
</Definition>

## Directional derivatives

The rate of change of $f$ along a unit vector $\uvec$ is the directional derivative

$$
D_{\uvec} f(\xvec) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(\xvec + h\uvec) - f(\xvec)}{h} = \nabla f(\xvec)^\top \uvec.
$$

Every directional derivative is a dot product with the same vector. The gradient therefore summarises the behaviour
of $f$ in every direction at once.

## Steepest ascent

Write $\nabla f^\top \uvec = \norm{\nabla f} \cos\phi$, where $\phi$ is the angle between $\uvec$ and $\nabla f$.

- The rate is largest when $\phi = 0$: the gradient points in the direction of steepest increase.
- That largest rate is $\norm{\nabla f}$.
- The rate is zero when $\phi = 90°$: moving perpendicular to the gradient keeps $f$ constant to first order. So the
  gradient is perpendicular to the contour through $\xvec$.
- $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest decrease, which is the step that
  <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> takes.

## Stationary points

Where $\nabla f(\xvec) = \zeros$, no direction increases or decreases $f$ to first order. Such a point is a minimum, a
maximum or a saddle. The saddle $x^2 - y^2$ has zero gradient at the origin, yet $f$ rises along $x$ and falls along
$y$. Second derivatives, collected in the Hessian, tell the cases apart.

## Examples

| $f(\xvec)$ | $\nabla f(\xvec)$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\avec^\top \xvec$ | $\avec$ |
| $\xvec^\top \xvec = \norm{\xvec}^2$ | $2\xvec$ |
| $\xvec^\top \Amat \xvec$ | $(\Amat + \Amat^\top)\xvec$, which is $2\Amat\xvec$ for symmetric $\Amat$ |
| $\norm{\Xmat\wvec - \yvec}^2$ (as a function of $\wvec$) | $2\Xmat^\top(\Xmat\wvec - \yvec)$ |

The last row is the gradient of the least-squares loss. Setting it to zero gives the normal equations of linear
regression.

## Pitfalls

- **Scale.** The gradient depends on the units of each coordinate. Rescaling one input by 10 changes the gradient's
  direction, which is why feature scaling changes the path of gradient descent.
- **Non-differentiable points.** $\abs{x}$ has no gradient at 0. Optimisers use a subgradient there.
