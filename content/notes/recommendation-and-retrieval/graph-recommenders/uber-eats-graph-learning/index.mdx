## Context

Uber Eats is "a portal to more than 320,000 restaurant-partners in over 500 cities globally across 36 countries". Its
recommender works in two phases. **Candidate generation** pre-filters dishes and restaurants at scale, for example
removing restaurants outside the user's delivery range. A **personalised ranker** then orders the candidates using
context such as day, time and location, learning patterns such as "ordering certain types of food on specific days of
the week" <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />. The graph embeddings described here feed the ranker as features. See
<NoteLink to="multi-stage-recommendation">multi-stage recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Graph construction

The team built two bipartite graphs: one with users and dishes as nodes, where an edge carries "the number of times a
user ordered a specific dish", and one with users and restaurants, where an edge carries how many times a user ordered
from that restaurant. Ratings also inform the weights. Separate models are trained for each city, "as their graphs are
only loosely connected" <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />.

## The model

The encoder is GraphSAGE, "a specific flavor of GNN in which the aggregation function is a max or mean pooling after a
projection", chosen "because of its strong scalability"; it samples a fixed number of neighbours at one and two hops
<Cite id="liu2019ubereats" /> <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />. See <NoteLink to="graphsage">GraphSAGE</NoteLink>.
Uber made three changes.

**1. A projection per node type.** Users, dishes and restaurants have features of different sizes: dishes can be
described by word embeddings of their descriptions or image features, restaurants by menu and cuisine features. An extra
layer projects each type to a common size. In our notation, a node $v$ of type $\tau(v)$ with raw features $\xvec_v$
starts from

$$
\hvec_v^{(0)} = \Pmat_{\tau(v)}\xvec_v.
$$

**2. Weighted aggregation.** Each layer $k$ pools the neighbours $\Ncal(v)$ (sampled, with the edge weights informing
the sampling) using the edge weights $w_{vu}$, projects the pooled vector with a matrix $\Wmat_k$, projects the node's
own vector with a matrix $\Bmat_k$, and concatenates the two. With a weighted mean as the pooling function and a
non-linearity $\sigma$,

$$
\hvec_{\Ncal(v)}^{(k)} = \frac{\sum_{u \in \Ncal(v)} w_{vu}\,\hvec_u^{(k-1)}}{\sum_{u \in \Ncal(v)} w_{vu}}, \qquad
\hvec_v^{(k)} = \sigma\paren{\LB\Wmat_k\hvec_{\Ncal(v)}^{(k)} \,\Vert\, \Bmat_k\hvec_v^{(k-1)}\RB},
$$

where $\Vert$ is concatenation. Two layers give the embedding $\zvec_v = \hvec_v^{(2)}$, which depends on $v$'s own
features and on everything within two hops. The article describes exactly this recursion, with $\Wmat_1, \Bmat_1$ for
layer 1 and $\Wmat_2, \Bmat_2$ for layer 2 <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />. The same parameters serve every node, so the
model scales to large graphs and can embed a new user, dish or restaurant from its features and connections.

**3. A loss for weighted edges.** "The most impactful change was adopting a hinge loss, a type of loss that fits the
ranking of items with respect to the user better than using binary edges" <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />.

## The loss

Score a user–dish pair by $s(u, v) = \zvec_u\transpose\zvec_v$. The base max-margin loss asks that, for every positive
edge $(u, v)$, the positive dish scores above a negative dish $n$ drawn from dishes the user never ordered, by a margin
$\gamma_1$:

$$
\ell_{\text{neg}}(u, v) = \expect_{n}\LB\max\paren{0,\ s(u, n) - s(u, v) + \gamma_1}\RB.
$$

This treats every positive edge alike, although "a dish a user ordered once and a dish a user ordered ten times" are
very different signals. Uber added **low-rank positives**: for a positive edge $(u, v)$, a low-rank positive is another
edge $(u, l)$ of the same user with a lower weight, $w_{ul} < w_{uv}$. A second hinge term, with a margin $\gamma_2$ set
lower than $\gamma_1$, asks that the heavier edge score higher <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />. The article gives the loss
as a figure; written in our notation, with $\lambda$ the multiplier that sets the relative importance of the two parts,

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{(u, v) \in \Ecal}\LB\expect_n\max\paren{0,\ s(u, n) - s(u, v) + \gamma_1} + \lambda\,\expect_{l \,:\, w_{ul} < w_{uv}}\max\paren{0,\ s(u, l) - s(u, v) + \gamma_2}\RB.
$$

**Why it orders items by weight.** The loss is zero exactly when every term is zero. For a user's heaviest dish $v$, a
lighter dish $l$ and a never-ordered dish $n$, that requires

$$
s(u, v) \ge s(u, l) + \gamma_2 \quad\text{and}\quad s(u, l) \ge s(u, n) + \gamma_1,
$$

the second because $(u, l)$ is itself a positive edge with its own negative term. So the zero-loss configurations are
exactly those with $s(u, v) > s(u, l) > s(u, n)$ and gaps of at least $\gamma_2$ and $\gamma_1$. Frequently ordered
dishes rank above occasionally ordered ones, which rank above dishes never ordered. Setting $\gamma_2 < \gamma_1$ keeps
the distinction between two dishes the user likes smaller than the distinction between liked and unknown dishes, and
$\lambda$ trades off the two kinds of error.

## Downstream use

Once trained, the embeddings give similarities between users and items. Uber added "the dot product and cosine
similarity of user and items" as features to both the dish and the restaurant rankers <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />.

## Pipeline

The article describes a four-step data pipeline and a training pipeline <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />:

1. Jobs pull data from Apache Hive into HDFS as Parquet files of nodes and edges, with every property versioned by
   timestamp so that back-dated graphs can be built.
2. For a chosen date, the most recent properties of each node and edge are kept and stored on HDFS in Cypher format.
3. Cypher queries run on Apache Spark produce one graph per city.
4. The city graphs are converted to NetworkX format, and TensorFlow processes on GPUs train the model and generate the
   embeddings.

The embeddings are stored in a lookup table, from which the ranking model retrieves them when a user opens the app.

## Results

The article reports, quoted exactly <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />:

- **Offline.** Trained on four months of data and tested on the following ten days, ranking all dishes and restaurants
  in the city by cosine similarity to the user: "a performance boost of over ~20 percent compared to the existing
  production model on metrics like Mean Reciprocal Rank, Precision@K, and NDCG."
- **In the ranking model.** Adding the similarity feature gave "a 12 percent boost in AUC compared to the existing
  productionized baseline model", and "the graph learning similarity feature was by far the most influential feature in
  the recommendation model."
- **Online.** "We conducted an A/B test in San Francisco and observed a substantial improvement in engagement and
  click-through rate when leveraging the graph learning feature compared to the previous production model."

See <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">metrics for recommendation</NoteLink> for MRR, precision at $K$ and NDCG.

## Visualising the embeddings

To show what the embeddings capture, the article follows a hypothetical user who first ordered two Indian dishes,
chicken tandoori and vegetable biryani, and later a pizza, a Cobb salad, doughnuts, ma po tofu, chicken tikka masala and
garlic naan. It plots popular dishes of several cuisines with "explicit axes": the $x$ coordinate is a dish's similarity
to the user's embedding before the later orders, and the $y$ coordinate its similarity after them, a technique from
Parallax <Cite id="liu2019ubereats" /> <Cite id="molino2019parallax" />. Three patterns appear:

- Before the later orders, the closest dishes are almost all Indian, with some Chinese dishes also ranked high,
  suggesting a second-order link between the cuisines ("users who ordered many Indian dishes also ordered Chinese
  ones").
- American, Italian, Thai and Korean dishes move much closer after the later orders, through the direct orders and
  through second-order effects of the Chinese dish.
- Cuisines outside the top three increase their similarity, which the authors read as the model learning that "this
  specific user might like for new cuisine suggestions to be surfaced."

Explicit axes make the change in an embedding interpretable by projecting onto directions that have a meaning, here
"similar before" and "similar after".

## What is distinctive

- **Weights as ranks, not labels.** Instead of treating edge weight as a regression target, the loss uses it to order
  a user's positives, which fits a ranking use.
- **Heterogeneous nodes by projection.** A per-type projection lets one GNN embed users, dishes and restaurants.
- **Graphs as features.** The embeddings did not replace the ranker; similarity features from them became its strongest
  input.

## Comparison

- **PinSage** also adapts GraphSAGE to recommendation at scale, with random-walk neighbourhoods and a max-margin loss
  with hard negatives <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />; Uber's article cites it as the motivating prior work. See
  <NoteLink to="pinsage">PinSage</NoteLink>.
- **LightGCN** drops the projections and non-linearities because its nodes are IDs without features; Uber's nodes carry
  rich features, which is where GraphSAGE-style layers pay. See <NoteLink to="lightgcn">LightGCN</NoteLink>.
- **Propagation alone** already reveals community structure; see
  <NoteLink to="untrained-graph-propagation">untrained graph propagation</NoteLink>.

The article lists two directions for future work: merging the dish and restaurant graphs into one graph of all entities
so that the tasks reinforce each other, and handling new cities with little data through meta graph learning
<Cite id="liu2019ubereats" />.
