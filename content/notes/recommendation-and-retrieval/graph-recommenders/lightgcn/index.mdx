<Definition>
**LightGCN** gives every user and item a trainable embedding $\evec^{(0)} \in \reals^d$ and propagates embeddings over
the user–item interaction graph for $K$ layers:

$$
\evec_u^{(k+1)} = \sum_{i \in \Ncal_u}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\abs{\Ncal_u}}\sqrt{\abs{\Ncal_i}}}\evec_i^{(k)}, \qquad
\evec_i^{(k+1)} = \sum_{u \in \Ncal_i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\abs{\Ncal_i}}\sqrt{\abs{\Ncal_u}}}\evec_u^{(k)}.
$$

The final embeddings are weighted sums over layers, $\evec_u = \sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_k\evec_u^{(k)}$ and likewise for
items, and the score is $\hat{y}_{ui} = \evec_u\transpose\evec_i$ <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />.
</Definition>

## What was removed

Earlier graph recommenders such as <NoteLink to="graph-collaborative-filtering">NGCF</NoteLink> copied <Gloss name="gcn">graph convolutional networks</Gloss>: each layer applied a weight matrix and a
non-linear activation. He et al.'s ablations showed that neither helped <Gloss name="collaborative-filtering">collaborative filtering</Gloss>, and that removing both
improved accuracy. The extra parameters made training harder and encouraged overfitting
<Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />. LightGCN keeps only the part that carries the collaborative
signal: normalised neighbourhood aggregation. Its only parameters are the layer-0 embeddings, the same number as
<NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">matrix factorisation</NoteLink>.

The normalisation $1/\sqrt{\abs{\Ncal_u}\abs{\Ncal_i}}$ is the symmetric normalisation of graph convolutional networks.
It keeps embeddings from growing with repeated aggregation, and it discounts messages through high-degree nodes: an
item everyone interacted with says little about any one user's taste.

## Matrix form

Stack users and items into one embedding matrix $\Emat^{(k)} \in \reals^{(U + M) \times d}$. With the <NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">interaction matrix</NoteLink>
$\Rmat \in \set{0, 1}^{U \times M}$, the adjacency matrix of the bipartite graph and its degree matrix are

$$
\Amat = \begin{pmatrix} \zeros & \Rmat \\ \Rmat\transpose & \zeros \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Dmat = \diag\paren{\Amat\ones}.
$$

Row $u$ of $\Amat\Emat^{(k)}$ sums the embeddings of $u$'s items, and scaling by $\Dmat^{-1/2}$ on both sides applies
the normalisation. So the two update rules together are

$$
\Emat^{(k+1)} = \hat{\Amat}\Emat^{(k)}, \qquad \hat{\Amat} = \Dmat^{-1/2}\Amat\Dmat^{-1/2},
$$

and by induction $\Emat^{(k)} = \hat{\Amat}^k\Emat^{(0)}$. The final embeddings are

$$
\Emat = \sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_k\hat{\Amat}^k\Emat^{(0)} = \Pmat\Emat^{(0)}, \qquad \Pmat = \sum_{k=0}^{K}\alpha_k\hat{\Amat}^k.
$$

LightGCN is therefore a linear model: a fixed graph filter $\Pmat$ applied to trainable embeddings. Entry
$(\hat{\Amat}^k)_{ab}$ is a degree-weighted count of the paths of length $k$ from node $a$ to node $b$. User $u$'s final
embedding is a mixture of the initial embeddings of all nodes within $K$ hops, weighted by how strongly paths connect
them. Paths of odd length link users to items; paths of even length link users to users <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />.

<Propagation />

Powers of $\hat{\Amat}$ act as a low-pass filter on the graph: they keep components that vary smoothly along edges,
which is why even random embeddings propagated this way reveal communities. See
<NoteLink to="untrained-graph-propagation">untrained graph propagation on the karate club</NoteLink>.

## Layer combination and self-connections

The final embedding includes $\evec^{(0)}$, the node's own trained vector, and every intermediate layer. He et al. give
three reasons <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />.

1. **Over-smoothing.** Repeated multiplication by $\hat{\Amat}$ makes deep-layer embeddings of connected nodes alike.
   Keeping shallow layers preserves what distinguishes each node.
2. **Different hops mean different things.** One hop gives the items a user touched; three hops give items liked by
   similar users. Combining layers uses all of them.
3. **Self-connections for free.** Graph convolutional networks add self-loops to $\Amat$ so a node keeps its own
   information. Including $\Emat^{(0)}$ in the sum has the same effect, so LightGCN's $\Amat$ has no self-loops.

Uniform weights $\alpha_k = 1/(K + 1)$ work well and are the default.

## Training

LightGCN is trained with the <NoteLink to="bayesian-personalised-ranking">BPR</NoteLink> loss on sampled triples, with
$\ell_2$ regularisation of $\Emat^{(0)}$ only <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" /> <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />. Each <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>
step backpropagates through the $K$ propagations. On the benchmarks used for NGCF, LightGCN improved accuracy by about
16% relative on average under the same settings, and it became the standard graph baseline <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Too many layers.** Performance usually peaks at $K = 2$ to $4$. The figure shows why: with large $K$ every user's
  mixture spreads over the whole graph.
- **Cost of full propagation.** Every training step multiplies by a sparse matrix with $2\abs{\Ecal}$ non-zeros $K$
  times. On very large graphs, sampling methods such as <NoteLink to="pinsage">PinSage</NoteLink> are needed.
- **IDs only.** LightGCN has no feature input, so a node added after training has no embedding.
