<Definition>
**Graph collaborative filtering** represents interactions as a bipartite graph $\Gcal = (\Vcal, \Ecal)$ whose nodes are
users and items, $\Vcal = \Ucal \cup \Ical$, with an edge $(u, i) \in \Ecal$ when user $u$ interacted with item $i$. A
graph neural network computes node embeddings by repeated **message passing**: at layer $k + 1$, each node's embedding
is a function of its own and its neighbours' embeddings at layer $k$. Scores are <NoteLink to="dot-product">inner products</NoteLink> of the final user and
item embeddings <Cite id="wang2019ngcf" />.
</Definition>

## Collaborative filtering as paths

The graph view makes collaborative filtering explicit. Take the path $u_1 \to i_1 \to u_2 \to i_2$. User $u_1$ is
similar to $u_2$ because both interacted with $i_1$, and $u_2$ interacted with $i_2$, so $u_1$ might like $i_2$. This is
the reasoning of <NoteLink to="neighbourhood-collaborative-filtering">neighbourhood methods</NoteLink>, read off a path
of length 3.

<NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">Matrix factorisation</NoteLink> captures such paths only
implicitly, through a loss that ties $u_1$ and $u_2$ to the vector of $i_1$. A graph network puts them into the
embedding directly.

## Message passing

A <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message-passing</NoteLink> layer aggregates neighbours'
embeddings <Cite id="gilmer2017" />. Stacking layers widens what each node sees:

- After one layer, a user's embedding contains the items they interacted with.
- After two, the other users who interacted with those items.
- After three, the other items those users interacted with.

A user with few interactions receives information from the dense neighbourhoods of the few items they touched, which
helps sparse users most. Propagation alone already exposes community structure: an untrained network, or plain powers of
the normalised adjacency applied to <NoteLink to="random-fourier-features">random features</NoteLink>, separates the two factions of Zachary's karate club (see
<NoteLink to="untrained-graph-propagation">untrained graph propagation</NoteLink>).

**NGCF.** Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering transferred the design of <Gloss name="gcn">graph convolutional networks</Gloss> to the
bipartite graph. Its layer transforms messages with weight matrices $\Wmat_1$ and $\Wmat_2$, includes an element-wise
product that makes the message depend on the pair, and applies a non-linearity:

$$
\evec_u^{(k+1)} = \operatorname{LeakyReLU}\paren{\Wmat_1\evec_u^{(k)} + \sum_{i \in \Ncal_u}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\abs{\Ncal_u}\abs{\Ncal_i}}}\paren{\Wmat_1\evec_i^{(k)} + \Wmat_2\paren{\evec_i^{(k)} \odot \evec_u^{(k)}}}},
$$

where $\Ncal_u$ is the set of items adjacent to $u$. The final representation concatenates all layers' outputs
<Cite id="wang2019ngcf" />.

## Less is more

He et al. ablated NGCF and found that the weight matrices and the non-linearity did not help collaborative filtering and
could hurt it. Removing them gave <NoteLink to="lightgcn">LightGCN</NoteLink>, which propagates embeddings linearly and
was more accurate, about 16% on average over NGCF under the same settings <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />.

The reason lies in the inputs. Graph convolutional networks were designed for nodes with rich input features, where
transforming features is the point. In collaborative filtering a node is an ID with a learnable embedding and nothing
else. The useful operation is smoothing preference signals along edges, and extra transformations only add parameters
to overfit. Matching the architecture's inductive bias to the problem, not importing a general-purpose design, made the
model simpler and better.

## Variants

Further directions include the following.

- **Graph attention.** Weight neighbours by learned attention instead of fixed degree normalisation, so that some
  interactions count more.
- **Self-supervision.** SGL perturbs the graph (dropping nodes or edges) to create two views, and adds a <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive
  loss</NoteLink> that pulls the same node's embeddings in the two views together. This improves accuracy on sparse data and
  long-tail items <Cite id="wu2021sgl" />.
- **Social graphs.** DiffNet propagates influence through a user-user social graph alongside the interaction graph.
- **Attribute graphs for cold start.** A graph over attributes (genres, brands, actors) gives embeddings to attributes;
  a new item's embedding is aggregated from its attributes. See the <NoteLink to="cold-start-problem">cold-start
  problem</NoteLink>.
- **Web scale.** <NoteLink to="pinsage">PinSage</NoteLink> samples neighbourhoods by <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walks</NoteLink> to scale graph
  convolutions to billions of nodes. Its successors at Pinterest are covered in
  <NoteLink to="pinsage-family">the PinSage family</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Over-smoothing.** Many layers mix every node with the whole graph, and embeddings become alike. Two to four layers
  are typical.
- **Full-graph propagation is expensive.** Each training step on the full graph touches every edge. Large graphs need
  sampled neighbourhoods.
- **Transductive by default.** Models built on ID embeddings cannot embed a node added after training without
  retraining or an inductive design.
