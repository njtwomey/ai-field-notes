<Definition>
**Matrix factorisation** models a $U \times M$ <NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">interaction matrix</NoteLink> $\Rmat$ as the product of a user-factor matrix
$\Wmat \in \reals^{U \times K}$ and an item-factor matrix $\Vmat \in \reals^{M \times K}$, with $K \ll U, M$. Row
$\wvec_u$ of $\Wmat$ is user $u$'s latent vector and row $\vvec_i$ of $\Vmat$ is item $i$'s. The predicted rating is
$\hat{r}_{ui} = \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i$. The factors are chosen to fit the observed entries $\Ocal$ of $\Rmat$
<Cite id="koren2009mf" />.
</Definition>

## The model

<Gloss name="collaborative-filtering">Collaborative filtering</Gloss> fills in the missing entries of $\Rmat$ from the observed ones. Matrix factorisation assumes
that the full matrix is close to low rank. A rank-$K$ matrix is exactly one that can be written as
$\Wmat\Vmat\transpose$ with $K$ columns in each factor, so the model is

$$
\hat{r}_{ui} = \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{uk}v_{ik}.
$$

The low-rank assumption says that a few hidden dimensions explain most of how users rate items. In a film catalogue
the dimensions often line up with comedy versus drama, mainstream versus art house, or a period of cinema. A user's
coordinate $w_{uk}$ says how much they like dimension $k$; an item's coordinate $v_{ik}$ says how much the item has it.
The prediction is high when the two vectors point the same way. The dimensions are learned, not specified, and need not
have names <Cite id="koren2009mf" />.

The model has $K(U + M)$ parameters instead of the $UM$ entries of the matrix. For $U = 10^6$, $M = 10^5$ and $K = 64$
that is $7 \times 10^7$ parameters against $10^{11}$ cells.

## The objective

Only the observed entries carry information. Fitting every observed rating exactly is possible once $K$ is large, and
then the missing entries are arbitrary, so the fit is regularised:

$$
\min_{\Wmat, \Vmat} \sum_{(u, i) \in \Ocal} \paren{r_{ui} - \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i}^2 + \lambda\paren{\norm{\Wmat}_F^2 + \norm{\Vmat}_F^2}.
$$

The penalty weight $\lambda \ge 0$ shrinks the vectors of users and items with few ratings towards zero, where
predictions fall back to the global behaviour. The same objective is the negative log posterior of a probabilistic
model, $r_{ui} \sim \Gauss(\wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i, \sigma^2)$ with Gaussian priors $\wvec_u, \vvec_i \sim
\Gauss(\zeros, \sigma_0^2\eye)$ and $\lambda = \sigma^2/\sigma_0^2$. This is probabilistic matrix factorisation
<Cite id="mnih2007pmf" />.

## Fitting

The objective is not convex in $(\Wmat, \Vmat)$ jointly, because the product $\wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i$ couples them. It
is a quadratic in either factor with the other fixed. Two algorithms use this.

**<Gloss name="sgd">Stochastic gradient descent</Gloss>.** For one observed rating, write the error $e_{ui} = r_{ui} - \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i$.
The <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of $e_{ui}^2 + \lambda(\norm{\wvec_u}^2 + \norm{\vvec_i}^2)$ with respect to $\wvec_u$ is
$-2e_{ui}\vvec_i + 2\lambda\wvec_u$, and symmetrically for $\vvec_i$. With step size $\eta$ absorbing the factor 2, the
updates for one rating are

$$
\wvec_u \leftarrow \wvec_u + \eta\paren{e_{ui}\vvec_i - \lambda\wvec_u}, \qquad
\vvec_i \leftarrow \vvec_i + \eta\paren{e_{ui}\wvec_u - \lambda\vvec_i}.
$$

Each update costs $O(K)$, so one pass over the data costs $O(K\abs{\Ocal})$.

**Alternating least squares.** Fix $\Vmat$ and solve a <NoteLink to="ridge-regression">ridge regression</NoteLink> for each user, then fix $\Wmat$ and solve one
for each item. Each solve is exact and independent of the others, so the sweeps parallelise. See
<NoteLink to="alternating-least-squares">alternating least squares</NoteLink> <Cite id="zhou2008als" />.

## Relation to the singular value decomposition

For a fully observed matrix $\Mmat \in \reals^{m \times n}$ the <NoteLink to="singular-value-decomposition">singular
value decomposition</NoteLink> $\Mmat = \Umat\Sigmamat\Vmat\transpose$ gives the answer directly. Keeping the $K$
largest singular values and their singular vectors gives the rank-$K$ matrix closest to $\Mmat$ in <NoteLink to="matrix-norms">Frobenius norm</NoteLink> (the
Eckart–Young theorem). Setting $\Wmat = \Umat_K\Sigmamat_K^{1/2}$ and $\Vmat = \Vmat_K\Sigmamat_K^{1/2}$ puts that
approximation in the form above.

A ratings matrix is not fully observed. Filling the missing entries with zeros or means and taking the SVD treats
guesses as data, and the result is dominated by them. The objective above sums over $\Ocal$ only. It has no closed form,
which is why iterative methods "inspired by" the SVD, not the SVD itself, became the workhorses of the era. The <NoteLink to="netflix-prize">Netflix
Prize</NoteLink> community called the SGD version "SVD" even though it computes no singular value decomposition <Cite
id="koren2009mf" />.

## Biases

Much of the variation in ratings is not interaction. Some users rate everything high and some films are rated high by
everyone. Koren et al. add a global mean $\mu$ and bias terms,

$$
\hat{r}_{ui} = \mu + b_u + b_i + \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i,
$$

with $b_u$ and $b_i$ penalised like the factors. The factors then explain only what the biases cannot
<Cite id="koren2009mf" />. The figure above centres ratings on their mean, the simplest version of this.

## Limitations

Three limits of the paradigm each motivated a later family of models.

1. **Cold start.** A user or item with no observed ratings has no data to fit its vector. Its vector stays at its
   initial value, or at zero under the penalty, and the model cannot personalise for it. See the
   <NoteLink to="cold-start-problem">cold-start problem</NoteLink>.
2. **Scale.** Each ALS sweep solves $U + M$ systems of size $K$. At web scale this is still costly, and the model must
   be refitted as data arrive.
3. **Linearity.** The score is a dot product, bilinear in the two vectors. It cannot express, for example, that a
   user likes horror only in combination with comedy. Replacing the dot product with a learned function motivated
   <NoteLink to="neural-collaborative-filtering">neural collaborative filtering</NoteLink>, although a tuned dot
   product remains hard to beat.

## Assumptions

- **Low rank.** The full matrix is well approximated by rank $K$.
- **<NoteLink to="missing-data-and-imputation">Missing at random</NoteLink>.** Fitting only observed entries assumes that which entries are observed does not depend on
  their values. Users rate what they chose to watch, so this fails; see
  <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">biases in recommender feedback</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Fitting implicit feedback on observed entries only.** With clicks, every observed entry is 1, and the model
  learns to predict 1 everywhere. Use <NoteLink to="weighted-matrix-factorisation">weighted matrix
  factorisation</NoteLink> or a ranking loss.
- **Unregularised rank.** Increasing $K$ without increasing $\lambda$ fits the training ratings better and the held-out
  ratings worse, as the figure shows. Choose both on held-out data.
