<Definition>
**Bayesian personalised ranking** (BPR) trains any scoring model $\hat{x}_{ui}(\Theta)$ on <NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">implicit feedback</NoteLink> from
triples $(u, i, j)$ in which user $u$ interacted with item $i$ and not with item $j$. It maximises

$$
\text{BPR-Opt} = \sum_{(u, i, j) \in \Dcal}\ln\sigma\paren{\hat{x}_{uij}} - \lambda_\Theta\norm{\Theta}^2,
\qquad \hat{x}_{uij} = \hat{x}_{ui} - \hat{x}_{uj},
$$

where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $\Dcal$ the set of such triples <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

With implicit feedback the observed entries are all positive, and the unobserved ones mix dislikes with items never
seen. Pointwise methods regress on 1 and 0 and must decide how much to trust each 0. The deployed task is different: to
present a ranked list. BPR changes the target from predicting an entry to ordering a pair. It assumes only that a user
prefers an item they chose to one they did not, which is weaker than "the unobserved item is disliked"
<Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.

## Derivation of the criterion

Write $i >_u j$ for "user $u$ prefers $i$ to $j$". BPR seeks the parameters that are most probable given all such
observed preferences. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' theorem</NoteLink>,

$$
p(\Theta \mid {>_u}) \propto p({>_u} \mid \Theta)\,p(\Theta).
$$

Assume the pairs are independent given $\Theta$, and model each pairwise preference with the logistic function of the
score difference:

$$
p(i >_u j \mid \Theta) = \sigma\paren{\hat{x}_{uij}(\Theta)} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\hat{x}_{ui} - \hat{x}_{uj})}}.
$$

Put a Gaussian prior $\Theta \sim \Gauss(\zeros, \lambda_\Theta^{-1}\eye/2)$ on the parameters, so that
$\ln p(\Theta) = -\lambda_\Theta\norm{\Theta}^2 + \text{const}$. The log posterior is then

$$
\ln p(\Theta \mid {>_u}) = \sum_{(u, i, j) \in \Dcal}\ln\sigma\paren{\hat{x}_{uij}} - \lambda_\Theta\norm{\Theta}^2 + \text{const},
$$

which is BPR-Opt. Maximising it is <Gloss name="map-estimate">maximum a posteriori</Gloss> estimation <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.

**Link to AUC.** The per-user <Gloss name="auroc">area under the ROC curve</Gloss> is the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs ranked correctly,
$\operatorname{AUC}(u) = \frac{1}{\abs{\Ical_u^+}\abs{\Ical_u^-}}\sum_{i, j}\indicator[\hat{x}_{uij} > 0]$. BPR replaces
the step function $\indicator[z > 0]$ with the smooth $\ln\sigma(z)$, which is differentiable and increases with $z$.
Maximising BPR-Opt pushes the scores towards a high AUC.

## Mechanism

The number of triples is $\sum_u \abs{\Ical_u^+}(M - \abs{\Ical_u^+})$, too many for full-batch training. **LearnBPR**
samples triples uniformly with replacement and takes a stochastic <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> step on each. The gradient of one term is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\Theta}\ln\sigma\paren{\hat{x}_{uij}} = \paren{1 - \sigma\paren{\hat{x}_{uij}}}\frac{\partial\hat{x}_{uij}}{\partial\Theta},
$$

using $\frac{d}{dz}\ln\sigma(z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$. A pair already ranked well by a wide margin has $\sigma \approx 1$ and
contributes almost nothing; a misordered pair contributes nearly its full gradient.

**BPR-MF.** With the score $\hat{x}_{ui} = \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivatives</NoteLink> of $\hat{x}_{uij}$ are
$\vvec_i - \vvec_j$ for $\wvec_u$, $\wvec_u$ for $\vvec_i$ and $-\wvec_u$ for $\vvec_j$. With step size $\eta$ and
$g = 1 - \sigma(\hat{x}_{uij})$, one ascent step is

$$
\wvec_u \mathrel{+}= \eta\paren{g(\vvec_i - \vvec_j) - \lambda\wvec_u}, \quad
\vvec_i \mathrel{+}= \eta\paren{g\,\wvec_u - \lambda\vvec_i}, \quad
\vvec_j \mathrel{+}= \eta\paren{-g\,\wvec_u - \lambda\vvec_j}.
$$

The same criterion applies to any differentiable score: neighbourhood models (BPR-kNN), <Gloss name="fm">factorisation machines</Gloss> or
neural networks <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.

## Worked example

Take $K = 2$, $\wvec_u = (1, 0)$, a clicked item $\vvec_i = (0.5, 0.5)$ and an unclicked item $\vvec_j = (0.8, -0.2)$.
The scores are $\hat{x}_{ui} = 0.5$ and $\hat{x}_{uj} = 0.8$, so $\hat{x}_{uij} = -0.3$: the pair is misordered. Then
$\sigma(-0.3) = 0.4256$, the loss is $-\ln 0.4256 = 0.854$, and $g = 0.5744$.

With $\eta = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 0$:

- $\wvec_u \leftarrow (1, 0) + 0.05744 \cdot (-0.3, 0.7) = (0.9828, 0.0402)$,
- $\vvec_i \leftarrow (0.5, 0.5) + 0.05744 \cdot (1, 0) = (0.5574, 0.5)$,
- $\vvec_j \leftarrow (0.8, -0.2) - 0.05744 \cdot (1, 0) = (0.7426, -0.2)$.

The new scores are $\hat{x}_{ui} = 0.5679$ and $\hat{x}_{uj} = 0.7217$, so $\hat{x}_{uij} = -0.154$ and the loss falls
to $0.773$. One step moved the user vector towards the clicked item and the two item vectors apart along the user's
direction.

## Cost

Each sampled triple costs $O(K)$ for BPR-MF. Rendle et al. found that sampling triples uniformly, instead of looping
over users or items in order, converged much faster, because consecutive updates on the same user-item pair are
highly correlated <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Ranking, not calibration.** BPR scores order items but are not probabilities. A system that needs click
  probabilities, for example to combine with bids, needs a pointwise model or calibration.
- **Uniform negatives are easy.** Most random unobserved items are obviously irrelevant, so most sampled triples give
  near-zero gradient late in training. Popularity-weighted or hard-negative sampling gives more signal, at the risk of
  sampling items the user would like.
- **An unobserved item is not a negative.** BPR still treats "not interacted" as "less preferred". Items never shown
  to the user are counted against, which is the <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">exposure bias</NoteLink>
  of implicit feedback.
- **Legacy.** The pairwise view made ranking the standard target for implicit feedback. Sampled-softmax and
  contrastive objectives used to train <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower models</NoteLink> generalise it from
  one negative to many. For listwise and metric-weighted losses, see
  <NoteLink to="ranking-losses">ranking losses</NoteLink>.
