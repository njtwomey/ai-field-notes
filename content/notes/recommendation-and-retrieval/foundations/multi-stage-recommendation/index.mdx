<Definition>
A **multi-stage recommender** orders a catalogue of $M$ items for a request in a cascade of models. **Retrieval**
(candidate generation) selects $K \ll M$ candidates, typically hundreds to thousands, with a model cheap enough to
search the whole catalogue. **Ranking** scores the $K$ candidates with a more expressive model. **Re-ranking** adjusts
the top of the list for properties of the list as a whole <Cite id="covington2016youtube" />.
</Definition>

## Why one model cannot do everything

A request must be answered in tens of milliseconds. Scoring every item with a deep network that combines user and
item features costs $M$ forward passes per request. For $M = 10^9$ items that is impossible, whatever the hardware. The
cascade spends the expensive computation on the few items that could reach the top of the list.

The stages have different jobs, so they are judged differently.

1. **Retrieval** must not lose good items. It is judged by recall at $K$: the fraction of items the user would engage
   with that survive into the candidate set. It must be fast over the whole catalogue, which in practice means the
   item side of the model is computed offline and searched with an
   <NoteLink to="approximate-nearest-neighbour-search">approximate nearest-neighbour</NoteLink> index. The
   <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink> is the standard architecture. Several retrievers usually
   run in parallel (collaborative, content-based, popular, recent) and their outputs are merged.
2. **Ranking** must order the candidates precisely. It is judged by precision-oriented metrics such as
   <NoteLink to="normalised-discounted-cumulative-gain">normalised discounted cumulative gain (NDCG)</NoteLink> or by
   calibrated click and conversion probabilities. It can afford cross features between user, item and context, as in
   <NoteLink to="deepfm">DeepFM</NoteLink>, and several prediction heads for different objectives.
3. **Re-ranking** applies what depends on the whole list: <NoteLink
   to="diversified-re-ranking">diversification</NoteLink>, fairness constraints on exposure, deduplication, freshness
   and business rules.

## YouTube's design

Covington et al. described the design that became the industry template. A candidate-generation network turned a user's
watch history, searches and demographics into a user vector and treated recommendation as a softmax over millions of
videos; at serving time a <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbour-search">nearest-neighbour search</NoteLink> over video vectors
returned hundreds of candidates. A separate ranking network scored those candidates with many more features, including
the video's impressions and the time since the user last watched from the same channel, and was trained to predict
expected watch time <Cite id="covington2016youtube" />.

## Consequences of the cascade

- **Errors compound.** An item dropped at retrieval can never be ranked. Improving the ranker cannot recover recall
  lost upstream.
- **Stages see different data.** The ranker is trained on items that retrieval selected and the user was shown. Its
  training data is filtered by the stages before it, a form of
  <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">selection bias</NoteLink>.
- **Objectives must be consistent.** A retriever tuned for clicks feeds a ranker tuned for purchases a candidate set
  that may contain few purchasable items.
- **Generative models challenge the design.** A model that generates item identifiers directly can in principle
  replace the funnel with one stage. See <NoteLink to="generative-retrieval-for-recommendation">generative
  retrieval</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Evaluating stages in isolation.** A retriever with higher recall but more near-duplicates can make the final list
  worse. Evaluate the pipeline end to end.
- **Training the ranker only on shown items.** Items the ranker has never seen at the top are out of its training
  distribution, and its scores for them are unreliable.
