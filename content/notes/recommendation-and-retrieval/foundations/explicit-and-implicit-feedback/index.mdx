<Definition>
The **user–item interaction matrix** $\Rmat \in \reals^{U \times M}$ has one row per user and one column per item. Entry
$r_{ui}$ records how user $u$ interacted with item $i$ and is observed only for a small set $\Ocal$ of pairs. **Explicit
feedback** fills $r_{ui}$ with a preference the user states, such as a rating. **Implicit feedback** fills it with a
behaviour, such as a click, a purchase or minutes watched, from which a preference is inferred <Cite id="hu2008implicit"
/>.
</Definition>

## The two kinds of signal

**Explicit feedback** is direct. A rating of 5 stars says the user liked the film, and a rating of 1 says they did not,
so the matrix contains both positive and negative examples. It is scarce: most users rate few items, and those who
rate are not typical users.

**Implicit feedback** is a by-product of use. Every page view, click, purchase, skip and second of dwell time is
logged, so it is several orders of magnitude more abundant than ratings. It has three weaknesses
<Cite id="hu2008implicit" />.

1. **No negative feedback.** A click is evidence of interest. The absence of a click is not evidence of disinterest.
   An unobserved entry is ambiguous: the user saw the item and did not want it, or never saw it.
2. **Noise.** A click on a misleading thumbnail, or a video left playing, does not reflect preference.
3. **Magnitude is confidence, not preference.** Watching a series ten times indicates more confidence that the user
   likes it, not a rating ten times higher.

## Sparsity

A platform with $U = 10^7$ users and $M = 10^6$ items has $10^{13}$ cells. A user who interacts with 100 items fills a
fraction $10^{-4}$ of a row. Practical interaction matrices are often well under 1% filled. The task of collaborative
filtering is to fill in the missing entries, or at least to rank them, from the observed ones.

## How the kind of feedback shapes the model

- With explicit ratings, a model can be fitted to the observed entries only, since the observed set contains both
  high and low ratings. This is the setting of <NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">matrix
  factorisation</NoteLink> in the Netflix Prize.
- With implicit feedback, fitting only the observed entries fits a matrix of ones. The model must also use the
  unobserved entries. <NoteLink to="weighted-matrix-factorisation">Weighted matrix factorisation</NoteLink> treats
  them as weak negatives with a low weight; <NoteLink to="bayesian-personalised-ranking">BPR</NoteLink> asks only that
  an observed item score above an unobserved one <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />; sampled negatives play the same role in
  <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower models</NoteLink>.
- Users show many implicit behaviours of different strength: view, click, add to cart, purchase. Modelling them
  jointly is the subject of <NoteLink to="multi-behaviour-recommendation">multi-behaviour recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Treating every unobserved entry as a negative.** Items the system never showed look disliked. The model then
  learns to repeat past exposure. This is the <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">exposure bias</NoteLink>
  that propensity methods correct.
- **Treating implicit feedback as missing at random.** Users rate or click items they chose to look at, and the system
  chose what they could look at. The observed entries are a biased sample of the matrix.
