<Definition>
**Progress** in recommendation research is a claim that a new method beats existing ones. The claim holds only if the
comparison is fair: baselines implemented well and tuned with the same effort as the new method, on the same data
splits, preprocessing and metrics. Ferrari Dacrema, Cremonesi and Jannach found that this condition often failed for
neural recommenders published at leading conferences, so that much apparent progress was not real
<Cite id="dacrema2019" />.
</Definition>

## The 2019 study

Ferrari Dacrema et al. considered 18 neural algorithms for top-$n$ recommendation presented at top-level research
conferences (KDD, SIGIR, WWW, RecSys) and tried to reproduce each from its paper and published artefacts. From their
abstract <Cite id="dacrema2019" />:

- "Only 7 of them could be reproduced with reasonable effort."
- "6 of them can often be outperformed with comparably simple heuristic methods, e.g., based on nearest-neighbor or
  graph-based techniques."
- "The remaining one clearly outperformed the baselines but did not consistently outperform a well-tuned non-neural
  linear ranking method."

The simple methods were item-based and user-based <NoteLink to="neighbourhood-collaborative-filtering">nearest-neighbour
collaborative filtering</NoteLink>, graph-based random walks, and well-tuned
<NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">matrix factorisation</NoteLink> and linear models. 

**The extended study.** In ACM Transactions on Information Systems (2021) the same group, with Boglio, widened the
analysis to neural collaborative filtering papers published between 2015 and 2018. They report that "11 out of the 12
reproducible neural approaches can be outperformed by conceptually simple methods" and that none "was actually
consistently better than already existing learning-based techniques, e.g., using matrix factorization or linear models"
<Cite id="dacrema2021troubling" />.

## How illusory progress arises

The studies identify the following mechanisms <Cite id="dacrema2019" />.

1. **Weak or untuned baselines.** The new method is tuned extensively; baselines run with default or copied
   hyperparameters, or in a weak implementation. The gap then measures tuning effort, not ideas.
2. **Missing hyperparameter search.** Papers often do not report how hyperparameters were chosen or searched, so
   neither the result nor the baseline can be reproduced.
3. **Inconsistent preprocessing and splits.** The same dataset is filtered with different minimum-interaction
   thresholds and split differently (random, leave-one-out, by time) across papers. Numbers cannot be compared across
   papers, and each paper can choose a favourable protocol.
4. **Selective reporting.** Results appear only for the datasets and metrics on which the method wins.
5. **Unavailable code.** Without code and configurations, a method cannot be checked or built upon.
6. **Sampled metrics.** Ranking the held-out item among a small random sample of items, instead of the whole catalogue,
   can reverse which method looks better (below).

## Sampled metrics

To save computation, many papers ranked each held-out item against $m = 100$ sampled items and reported hit rate or
NDCG on that list of $m + 1$. Krichene and Rendle showed that "sampled metrics are inconsistent with their exact
version" and "do not persist relative statements, e.g., 'algorithm A is better than B', not even in expectation"
<Cite id="krichene2020sampled" />.

**Why.** Suppose the held-out item has rank $R$ among all $M$ items, so $R - 1$ items score above it. The sampled
metric sees only $m$ random items out of the other $M - 1$. The number of them that outrank the held-out item follows a
hypergeometric distribution. The item is at the top of the sampled list when none of the $R - 1$ higher items is drawn:

$$
P(\text{sampled rank} = 1) = \binom{M - R}{m}\Big/\binom{M - 1}{m}.
$$

With $m = 1$ this is $(M - R)/(M - 1)$, the fraction of other items ranked below the held-out one. Averaged over test
cases, sampled hit rate at 1 with one negative is the <NoteLink to="roc-curve-and-auc">AUC</NoteLink>. As $m$ shrinks,
every top-heavy metric collapses towards AUC, which weighs an error at rank 500 almost as much as one at rank 5
<Cite id="krichene2020sampled" />.

**Worked example.** Take $M = 10{,}000$ items and $m = 100$ sampled negatives, and compute hit rate at 10 (see
<NoteLink to="hit-rate-and-r-precision">hit rate</NoteLink>). A held-out item counts as a hit on the sampled list when
fewer than 10 of the 100 sampled items outrank it. The probability of that is

| true rank $R$ | full hit rate at 10 | sampled hit rate at 10 |
| ------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------- |
| 10            | 1                   | 1.000                  |
| 100           | 0                   | 1.000                  |
| 500           | 0                   | 0.973                  |
| 1,000         | 0                   | 0.452                  |

A model that puts held-out items around rank 500 scores almost perfectly on the sampled metric and zero on the full
one. A model that places some items at rank 1 and others at rank 2,000 can look worse on the sampled metric while being
far more useful.

## Follow-ups on specific claims

- **Dot product versus MLP.** Rendle et al. revisited the experiments of
  <NoteLink to="neural-collaborative-filtering">neural collaborative filtering</NoteLink>. With proper hyperparameter
  selection, "a simple dot product substantially outperforms the proposed learned similarities"; learning a dot product
  with an MLP is "non-trivial"; and MLP similarities are too costly for retrieval, where dot products allow fast
  maximum-inner-product search <Cite id="rendle2020ncf" />. This is why the <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower
  model</NoteLink> keeps the dot product.
- **Loss versus architecture.** The advantage reported for <NoteLink to="bert4rec">BERT4Rec</NoteLink> over
  <NoteLink to="sasrec">SASRec</NoteLink> disappears when both use the same loss <Cite id="klenitskiy2023" />.

## Evaluating a new recommender

1. **Include simple baselines.** Popularity, item-kNN, a tuned matrix factorisation and a linear model
   are cheap and often strong.
2. **Tune every method equally.** Give each the same hyperparameter search budget on the same validation data, and
   report the search spaces. See <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink>.
3. **Fix and document the protocol.** State filtering thresholds, the split (prefer a split by time, which matches
   deployment), and the candidate set for ranking metrics.
4. **Use full-catalogue metrics.** If sampling is unavoidable, use corrected estimators and say so.
5. **Report everything.** All datasets and metrics considered, including losses, with variability across seeds.
6. **Release code and configurations.**
7. **Remember that offline is not online.** Even a fair offline comparison uses data logged by another policy. See
   <NoteLink to="unbiased-offline-evaluation">unbiased offline evaluation</NoteLink> and confirm with an
   <NoteLink to="ab-test">online experiment</NoteLink>.

These findings do not invalidate neural approaches. They show that genuine progress needs rigorous evaluation.
