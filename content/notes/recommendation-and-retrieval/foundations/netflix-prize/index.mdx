<Definition>
The **Netflix Prize** was an open competition run by Netflix from October 2006 to 2009. Netflix released about 100
million anonymised movie ratings from more than 480,000 subscribers on nearly 18,000 films and offered one million US
dollars to the first team to reduce the <Gloss name="rmse">root mean squared error (RMSE)</Gloss> of its own predictor, Cinematch, by 10% on a
withheld set of ratings <Cite id="bennett2007netflix" />. The team BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos won in 2009 with a blend of
hundreds of models <Cite id="koren2009bellkor" />.
</Definition>

## The task

The competition scored one number. For a withheld set $\Tcal$ of (user, film) pairs with true ratings $r_{ui}$ and
predictions $\hat{r}_{ui}$,

$$
\operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\abs{\Tcal}}\sum_{(u,i) \in \Tcal}(r_{ui} - \hat{r}_{ui})^2}.
$$

Cinematch scored 0.9514 on the quiz half of the withheld set, so the target was about 0.856 <Cite
id="bennett2007netflix" />. The task was rating prediction on a static matrix: fill in missing entries of $\Rmat$ given
the observed ones.

## What it changed

- **A shared benchmark.** A large public dataset let thousands of teams compare methods on the same data. It made open
  competitions and public datasets a standard way to drive research.
- **Model-based <Gloss name="collaborative-filtering">collaborative filtering</Gloss> won.** The task suited latent-factor models.
  <NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">Matrix factorisation</NoteLink>, with user and item biases,
  implicit-feedback terms and time effects, was the strongest single family <Cite id="koren2009mf" />.
- **Ensembles win competitions.** The winning solution blended the predictions of many models with gradient-boosted
  <NoteLink to="decision-tree">decision trees</NoteLink>. The components included matrix factorisations of many sizes, neighbourhood models with time-dependent
  effects and restricted Boltzmann machines, each with its own training and tuning. The winners' write-up describes the
  components and the blending <Cite id="koren2009bellkor" />.

## Why the winner was not deployed

Netflix did not put the full winning blend into production. The engineers reported that the gain was not worth the
engineering effort needed to run hundreds of models, and that the business had moved from DVD ratings to streaming,
where the useful signals were plays, not stars <Cite id="amatriain2012netflix" />. Each component needed its own
training pipeline, tuning and serving path. This is an early, clear example of the gap between accuracy on a static
test set and value in a live system.

## The RMSE fixation

A single metric focused the community's effort, and it also narrowed it. Squared error on ratings says nothing about
the order of the top ten items, the diversity of a list, novelty, fairness to item providers, or whether users come
back. The following decade can be read partly as the field moving away from this single target: towards <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">ranking
metrics</NoteLink> on implicit feedback, towards <NoteLink to="beyond-accuracy-metrics">beyond-accuracy metrics</NoteLink>, and
towards online experiments on business outcomes.

## Pitfalls

- **Small RMSE gains are small.** On a 1 to 5 scale, a 1% RMSE improvement changes predictions by hundredths of a
  star. The ranking a user sees may not change at all.
- **The offline winner is a candidate, not a result.** Only an <NoteLink to="a-b-test">online experiment</NoteLink> on
  the metric the business cares about can show that a model is better for users.
