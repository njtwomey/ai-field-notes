<Definition>
A **recommender system** takes a user $u$, a catalogue of $M$ items and the context of a request, and returns a short
ranked list of items the user is predicted to value. It learns its predictions from logged user–item interactions
(ratings, clicks, purchases, views) and from features of users, items and context. The two classical families are
**content-based filtering**, which recommends items similar to those the user liked, and **collaborative filtering**,
which recommends what similar users liked <Cite id="resnick1994grouplens" />.
</Definition>

## Data flow

The input is a sparse **interaction matrix** $\Rmat$ with one row per user and one column per item. Entry $r_{ui}$ is
the feedback of user $u$ on item $i$ and is missing for almost every pair. Side information describes users (age,
location, history), items (text, images, category) and the request (time, device). A model maps these inputs to a
score $\hat{y}_{ui}$ for each candidate item, and the system shows the top-scoring items.

At scale the scoring runs in stages. A cheap **retrieval** stage cuts millions of items to hundreds, an expensive
**ranking** stage orders those, and a **re-ranking** stage applies diversity, fairness and business rules
<Cite id="covington2016youtube" />. The user's reaction to the list is logged and becomes training data for the next
model. That loop is the source of the <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">biases</NoteLink> that later
methods correct.

## Eras

The field's history divides into paradigms, each a response to a limitation of the one before.

1. **Matrix factorisation (about 2006 to 2015).** The Netflix Prize framed recommendation as filling in a ratings
   matrix under squared error. Latent-factor models won <Cite id="koren2009mf" />. Their limits were cold start,
   scale and the linearity of the dot product.
2. **Ranking and implicit feedback.** Learning-to-rank losses such as RankNet and LambdaMART (see
   <NoteLink to="ranking-losses">ranking losses</NoteLink>) and pairwise objectives such as BPR moved the target from
   predicting a rating to ordering items from clicks <Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.
3. **Feature interactions and deep learning (from about 2016).** Factorisation machines, Wide & Deep, DeepFM, neural
   collaborative filtering and two-tower retrieval replaced the dot product with learned, non-linear functions of
   many features.
4. **Sequences.** Recurrent and self-attentive models such as GRU4Rec and SASRec model what the user is doing now,
   not only who the user is.
5. **Graphs.** Treating interactions as a bipartite graph and propagating embeddings along it, as in LightGCN,
   encodes multi-hop collaborative signal <Cite id="he2020lightgcn" />.
6. **Generation.** Large language models generate item identifiers or explanations directly, collapsing the
   retrieve-then-rank pipeline into one model.

Cross-cutting concerns grew alongside: multiple objectives, fairness and diversity, and causal methods that separate
what users prefer from what the system happened to show them.

## Variants

- **Rating prediction and top-$k$ ranking.** Early work predicted explicit ratings and scored RMSE. Most deployed
  systems rank items from implicit feedback and are scored with ranking metrics.
- **Personalised and non-personalised.** A popularity list is a strong baseline and the fallback for new users.
- **Search and recommendation.** Search ranks items for an explicit query; recommendation ranks them for a user with
  no query. Both share learning-to-rank, click models and retrieval infrastructure.
