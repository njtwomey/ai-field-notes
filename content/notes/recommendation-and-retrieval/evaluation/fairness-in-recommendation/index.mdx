<Definition>
**Fairness in recommendation** concerns how the benefits of a recommender are distributed across its stakeholders.
**User (consumer) fairness** asks that recommendation quality, such as accuracy, be comparable across groups of users,
often defined by sensitive attributes such as age or gender. **Item (provider) fairness** asks that items or providers
from different groups, such as popular and niche, large and small, receive exposure in proportion to their relevance
<Cite id="chen2023biassurvey" />.
</Definition>

## Why recommenders raise fairness questions

Recommenders decide what information and which opportunities people see: jobs, news, products, artists. Models trained
on historical interactions learn and amplify the biases in them. Popular items receive most exposure, which starves
niche and new items; users with non-mainstream tastes receive worse recommendations because there is less data about
people like them. See <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="feedback-loops-in-recommendation">feedback loops</NoteLink>.

Bias, fairness and diversity form a chain. Biased observational data produce biased models; biased models allocate
exposure unfairly, under-recommending minority providers or serving niche user groups poorly; unfair allocation
produces homogeneous lists. A responsible system acts at each link: causal methods to de-bias the data, fairness-aware
training for the model, and diversification for the output.

## Where to intervene

The same three stages as in <NoteLink to="bias-mitigation">bias mitigation</NoteLink> for classification apply.

- **Pre-processing.** Resample or reweight the training data, for example to balance exposure across provider groups.
- **In-processing.** Add fairness constraints or regularisers to the loss, or train with an adversary that tries to
  predict a sensitive attribute from the representation (see the <NoteLink to="debiasing-toolkit">debiasing
  toolkit</NoteLink>).
- **Post-processing.** Re-rank the model's output to satisfy a fairness criterion. LinkedIn's talent-search ranking
  re-ranks candidates so that the distribution of a protected attribute in the top results matches a desired
  distribution. The re-ranking works on top of any ranking model, which made it practical to deploy
  <Cite id="geyik2019linkedin" />.

## Measuring it

- **Group metrics for users:** differences in accuracy or satisfaction between groups; demographic parity and equal
  opportunity where recommendation is a decision. See <NoteLink to="group-fairness-metrics">group fairness
  metrics</NoteLink>.
- **Exposure for items:** each group's share of exposure against its share of relevance; concentration of exposure by
  Gini or Herfindahl indices. See <NoteLink to="beyond-accuracy-metrics">beyond-accuracy metrics</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Metric choices conflict.** Equal exposure and exposure proportional to relevance are different goals. As in
  classification, several <NoteLink to="fairness-criteria">fairness criteria</NoteLink> cannot hold at once in general.
- **Fairness is multi-sided.** Constraints for providers can lower quality for some users, and the platform has its own
  objectives. Decide explicitly which stakeholders a constraint protects.
- **Choice of experiment metric is a fairness decision.** An <Gloss name="ab-test" /> that optimises clicks can reward a model for
  concentrating exposure. See <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in
  recommendation</NoteLink>.
