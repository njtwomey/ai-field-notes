<Definition>
**Beyond-accuracy metrics** evaluate properties of recommendations other than relevance: how different the items in a
list are (**diversity**), how much of the catalogue is ever recommended (**coverage**), how unevenly exposure is spread
(**concentration**), how unfamiliar the items are (**novelty**) and how often they are both unexpected and liked
(**serendipity**) <Cite id="ge2010serendipity" />.
</Definition>

## Why accuracy is not enough

A list of the ten most popular items can score well on relevance and still be useless: the user knew them all, and every
user sees the same ten. Biased data lead to unfair models and then to non-diverse lists; these metrics measure the last
link. They are also how the effect of <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">debiasing</NoteLink> is judged: a successful method
should raise them, often at a small cost in raw accuracy.

## Definitions

**Intra-list diversity.** For a list of $k$ items and a dissimilarity $\operatorname{dist}(\cdot, \cdot)$, such as genre
distance or one minus the <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> of embeddings,

$$
\operatorname{ILD} = \frac{2}{k(k - 1)}\sum_{i < j}\operatorname{dist}(d_i, d_j),
$$

the mean dissimilarity over the $k(k - 1)/2$ pairs <Cite id="ziegler2005diversification" />. A playlist spanning genres
scores higher than one of a single genre. Too much diversity makes a list incoherent.

**Catalogue coverage.** The fraction of the catalogue that appears in at least one list,
$\abs{\bigcup_{q \in Q} R_q}/\abs{\text{catalogue}}$. The challenge is to keep coverage high without losing relevance.

**Concentration of exposure.** Let $s_i$ be item $i$'s share of all impressions. The **Herfindahl index** is $\sum_i
s_i^2$: $1/M$ when $M$ items share exposure equally and 1 when one item has it all. The **Gini coefficient** of the
exposure counts, sorted in increasing order $x_{(1)} \le \cdots \le x_{(M)}$, is

$$
G = \frac{2\sum_{i=1}^{M} i\,x_{(i)}}{M\sum_i x_{(i)}} - \frac{M + 1}{M},
$$

0 for perfect equality and approaching 1 when one item takes all exposure. Both indicate whether niche and long-tail
items are drowned out.

**Novelty.** Usually the mean self-information of the recommended items, $\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} -\log_2 p(d_i)$,
where $p(d)$ is the fraction of users who have interacted with $d$. Rare items carry more bits <Cite
id="vargas2011novelty" />.

**Serendipity.** The share of recommendations that are both unexpected, for example absent from a naive popularity
baseline's list, and relevant. An obscure film the user loves is serendipitous; an obscure film the user hates is only
novel <Cite id="ge2010serendipity" />.

## Worked example

**Diversity.** A list has three films with genre sets $\set{\text{action}}$, $\set{\text{action}, \text{comedy}}$ and
$\set{\text{drama}}$. With dissimilarity one minus the Jaccard index, the pairwise distances are $1 - 1/2 = 0.5$, $1$
and $1$, so $\operatorname{ILD} = \frac{2}{3 \cdot 2}(0.5 + 1 + 1) = 0.833$.

**Concentration.** Two systems spread 100 impressions over five items.

| system | exposure | Herfindahl | Gini |
| ------ | -------- | ---------- | ---- |
| A      | 50, 30, 10, 5, 5 | $0.25 + 0.09 + 0.01 + 0.0025 + 0.0025 = 0.355$ | $\frac{2 \cdot 415}{5 \cdot 100} - \frac{6}{5} = 0.46$ |
| B      | 20, 20, 20, 20, 20 | $5 \times 0.04 = 0.2$ | 0 |

For A the weighted sum is $1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 10 + 4 \cdot 30 + 5 \cdot 50 = 415$.

**Novelty.** Items consumed by 50%, 10% and 1% of users carry $1$, $3.32$ and $6.64$ bits; the list's novelty is
$3.66$ bits.

## Pitfalls

- **High diversity can be reached without relevance.** A random list is diverse and useless. Report beyond-accuracy metrics beside
  relevance metrics, never alone.
- **The ranking of lists by diversity depends on the dissimilarity.** Genre distance and embedding distance can rank lists differently. State
  which is used.
- **Novelty can undermine trust.** Too many unfamiliar items make recommendations feel irrelevant.
