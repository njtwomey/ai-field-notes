<Definition>
**Unbiased offline evaluation** estimates what a metric would be over all user–item pairs, or under a new policy, from
interactions logged by an existing system. Each observed outcome is weighted by the inverse of the probability that it
was observed, its propensity $P_{ui}$, so that under-observed pairs count for as much as they would in a complete
dataset <Cite id="schnabel2016treatments" />.
</Definition>

## Why naive metrics mislead

A metric computed on logged interactions averages only over what the logging system showed and users chose to rate or
click. It is confounded by the same biases a debiased model tries to remove. Suppose a debiased model learns that a
popular item, clicked at rank 1 in the logs, is not relevant to a particular user, and does not recommend it. Naive
evaluation counts the logged click as a miss. The metric rewards imitating the old policy and penalises correcting it.

## The estimator

Let $\delta_{ui}$ be the loss or gain a metric assigns to the pair $(u, i)$ under the model being evaluated (a squared
error, or a rank-discounted gain), and let $O_{ui} = 1$ if the outcome for the pair was observed. The target is the
average over all $U \cdot M$ pairs,

$$
R = \frac{1}{UM}\sum_{u, i}\delta_{ui}.
$$

The naive estimate averages over observed pairs only, $\hat{R}_{\text{naive}} = \sum_{u,i}
O_{ui}\delta_{ui}/\sum_{u,i}O_{ui}$. Its <NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink> weights each pair by its propensity $P_{ui} = P(O_{ui} = 1)$, which
is not the target unless all propensities are equal. The inverse-propensity-scored estimate is

$$
\hat{R}_{\text{IPS}} = \frac{1}{UM}\sum_{u, i}\frac{O_{ui}}{P_{ui}}\delta_{ui}.
$$

Since $\expect[O_{ui}] = P_{ui}$, each term has expectation $\delta_{ui}$, and $\expect[\hat{R}_{\text{IPS}}] = R$
whenever every $P_{ui} > 0$ <Cite id="schnabel2016treatments" />. The same weighting applies to <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">ranking metrics</NoteLink> such as
precision at $k$ and <Gloss name="dcg" />, and to evaluating a new policy from a log of an old one, where the weight becomes the ratio of
the two policies' probabilities of the logged action; see <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy
evaluation</NoteLink>. Li et al.'s replay method, which keeps only logged rounds where a uniformly random <NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities">logging policy</NoteLink>
chose what the new policy would have chosen, is the special case with equal propensities <Cite id="li2011" />. Evaluating a whole new recommender from production logs, with clipped,
self-normalised and doubly robust estimators, is covered in <NoteLink to="counterfactual-evaluation-of-recommenders">counterfactual
evaluation of recommenders</NoteLink>.

## Worked example

Four items; the model's loss on each is $\delta = (0, 0, 1, 1)$: it is right on items 1 and 2 and wrong on items 3 and
4. The true mean loss is $0.5$. The logging system shows the items with propensities $P = (0.9, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1)$, so the
model is wrong exactly on the items users rarely see.

- The naive estimate has expectation approximately $\frac{\sum_i P_i\delta_i}{\sum_i P_i} = \frac{0.2 + 0.1}{1.7} =
  0.176$. The model looks three times better than it is.
- The IPS estimate has expectation $\frac{1}{4}\sum_i P_i\frac{\delta_i}{P_i} = \frac{1}{4}(0 + 0 + 1 + 1) = 0.5$.

The price is variance: a single observation of item 4 carries weight $1/0.1 = 10$. Clipping the weights or
self-normalising them (dividing by $\sum O_{ui}/P_{ui}$ instead of $UM$) reduces the variance at the cost of some bias
<Cite id="swaminathan2015b" />; doubly robust estimators add a model of the outcome <Cite id="dudik2011" />.

## Holistic evaluation

Debiasing aims at more than accuracy, so its evaluation must too.

- **Diversity, novelty and serendipity** measure whether <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink> was reduced. A successful debiased model
  should improve them, often at a small cost in raw accuracy. See <NoteLink to="beyond-accuracy-metrics">beyond-accuracy
  metrics</NoteLink>.
- **Fairness metrics** measure disparities: demographic parity (equal rates of positive outcomes across groups), equal
  opportunity (equal <Gloss name="tpr">true-positive rates</Gloss>), and provider-side exposure (whether small sellers or niche artists get
  exposure in line with relevance). See <NoteLink to="fairness-in-recommendation">fairness in recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Wrong propensities.** Estimated propensities that are off by a factor bias the metric by that factor. Validate them
  on randomised data where possible.
- **No support.** Pairs never shown have propensity zero; no weighting can evaluate a model on them. The estimate covers
  only what the logging policy could have shown.
- **Offline is still not online.** Unbiased estimates of logged-outcome metrics do not capture novelty effects, feedback
  loops or long-term retention. Confirm launch decisions with an <NoteLink to="a-b-test">online experiment</NoteLink>.
