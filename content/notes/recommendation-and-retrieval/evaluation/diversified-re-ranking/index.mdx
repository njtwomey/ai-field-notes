<Definition>
**Diversified re-ranking** takes candidates scored for relevance and builds a list that trades relevance against
redundancy, usually as a post-processing stage after ranking. It optimises the **slate**, the list as a whole, instead
of each item's relevance separately.
</Definition>

## Problem

A ranker scores items one at a time, so the top of its list is often a cluster of near-duplicates: five films from the
same franchise, ten versions of the same shoe. Each is relevant; together they waste the slate and cover one of the
user's interests. Popularity bias makes this worse by promoting the same items for everyone. Slate optimisation and
diversification are the output-side response. Diversity is measured by <NoteLink to="beyond-accuracy-metrics">intra-list
diversity</NoteLink> and coverage.

## Mechanism

**Maximal marginal relevance.** Start from the candidates $C$ and an empty list $S$. Repeatedly add

$$
d^* = \argmax_{d \in C \setminus S}\LB\lambda\,\text{rel}(d) - (1 - \lambda)\max_{d' \in S}\operatorname{sim}(d, d')\RB,
$$

where $\text{rel}(d)$ is the base model's relevance score, $\operatorname{sim}$ an item similarity and $\lambda \in [0,
1]$ the trade-off. The first item is the most relevant one; each later item is penalised by its similarity to the
closest item already chosen <Cite id="carbonell1998mmr" />. With $\lambda = 1$ MMR returns the relevance ranking; with
$\lambda = 0$ it maximises spread and ignores relevance.

**Determinantal point processes.** A DPP assigns each subset $S$ of the candidates a probability proportional to a
determinant, $P(S) \propto \det(\Lmat_S)$, where $\Lmat$ is a positive semi-definite kernel and $\Lmat_S$ its rows and
columns for $S$ <Cite id="kulesza2012dpp" />. A common kernel combines quality scores $q_i \ge 0$ and a similarity
matrix $\Smat$ (with $S_{ii} = 1$): $L_{ij} = q_iS_{ij}q_j$. Then $\Lmat_S = \diag(\qvec_S)\Smat_S\diag(\qvec_S)$ and

$$
\det(\Lmat_S) = \paren{\prod_{i \in S} q_i^2}\det(\Smat_S).
$$

The first factor rewards quality. The second is the squared volume spanned by the items' feature vectors when $\Smat$ is
a Gram matrix: it is 1 for orthogonal items and 0 when two are identical. One number thus measures relevance and
diversity together. Finding the most probable subset is hard, but a greedy algorithm that adds the item with the largest
gain in $\log\det$, like MMR, is fast and accurate in practice <Cite id="chen2018dpp" />. Both methods build the list
iteratively and condition each choice on what was already chosen. YouTube deployed a DPP in its recommendation system
and reported gains in user satisfaction and engagement <Cite id="wilhelm2018dpp" />.

## Cost

- MMR needs $O(k\abs{C})$ similarity evaluations for a list of $k$ from $\abs{C}$ candidates, cheap for the hundreds of
  items that reach re-ranking.
- Greedy DPP inference with incremental Cholesky updates costs $O(k^2\abs{C})$ <Cite id="chen2018dpp" />.

## Trade-offs

- **Relevance against diversity.** Each notch of diversity costs some expected relevance at the top of the list. Tune
  $\lambda$, or the kernel's balance of quality and similarity, online.
- **Which similarity.** Genre, embedding or co-consumption similarity lead to different lists.
- **Post-processing versus modelling.** Re-ranking fixes the output of a biased model; it does not fix the bias. Combine
  it with <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">debiased training</NoteLink> where possible.
