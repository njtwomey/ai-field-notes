<Definition>
**GraphSAGE** ("sample and aggregate") computes an embedding $\zvec_v$ for a node $v$ of a graph from input features
$\xvec_v$. It sets $\hvec_v^{(0)} = \xvec_v$ and, for $k = 1, \dots, K$,

$$
\hvec_{\Ncal(v)}^{(k)} = \mathrm{AGGREGATE}_k\paren{\set{\hvec_u^{(k-1)} : u \in \Ncal_k(v)}}, \qquad
\hvec_v^{(k)} = \frac{\sigma\paren{\Wmat_k\,[\hvec_v^{(k-1)} \Vert \hvec_{\Ncal(v)}^{(k)}]}}
{\norm{\sigma\paren{\Wmat_k\,[\hvec_v^{(k-1)} \Vert \hvec_{\Ncal(v)}^{(k)}]}}_2},
$$

with $\zvec_v = \hvec_v^{(K)}$. $\Ncal_k(v)$ is a fixed-size uniform sample of $v$'s neighbours drawn afresh at each
layer, $[\cdot \Vert \cdot]$ is concatenation, $\sigma$ is a non-linearity, and the weights $\Wmat_k$ and the aggregator
parameters are shared by all nodes <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Earlier node-embedding methods such as DeepWalk learn one free vector per node. They are **transductive**: a node that
was absent during training has no vector, and adding nodes requires more training. GraphSAGE is **inductive**. It learns
the functions that turn a neighbourhood into a vector, not the vectors themselves. A new node with features and edges is
embedded by running those functions <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

GraphSAGE is a <NoteLink to="message-passing-neural-network">message passing neural network</NoteLink> with two
specific choices. The update concatenates the node's own vector with the neighbourhood aggregate before a dense layer,
which acts as a skip connection between layers. The neighbourhood is a sample of fixed size, which bounds the cost of a
minibatch.

## Why sampling bounds the cost

To compute $\zvec_v$ with $K$ layers, the model needs layer-$(K-1)$ vectors of $v$'s sampled neighbours, which need
layer-$(K-2)$ vectors of their sampled neighbours, and so on. With $S_k$ samples at layer $k$, the tree of required
nodes has at most $1 + S_K + S_K S_{K-1} + \dots + \prod_{k=1}^{K} S_k$ nodes. The last term dominates, so the cost per
target node is $O(\prod_{k=1}^{K} S_k)$, whatever the graph's size or degree distribution. Without sampling, the
neighbourhood of a single node can include a large fraction of the graph, and the paper notes that the worst-case cost
of a batch is then $O(\abs{\Vcal})$ <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

The experiments use $K = 2$ with $S_1 = 25$ and $S_2 = 10$, so each target node needs at most $1 + 25 + 250 = 276$
vectors. The authors found high performance with $K = 2$ and $S_1 S_2 \le 500$ <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

In the minibatch algorithm, the sampled sets are built from the targets outwards, and the layers are then computed from
the outermost set inwards, computing only the vectors that the recursion needs <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

## Aggregators

A node's neighbours have no order, so the aggregator should be symmetric: invariant to permutations of its inputs. The
paper compares four <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

- **Mean.** The element-wise mean of $\set{\hvec_u^{(k-1)}}$.
- **GCN.** Replace the concatenation and aggregation by
  $\hvec_v^{(k)} = \sigma\paren{\Wmat \cdot \mathrm{MEAN}(\set{\hvec_v^{(k-1)}} \cup \set{\hvec_u^{(k-1)} : u \in \Ncal(v)})}$.
  This is an inductive variant of the <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolutional network</NoteLink>
  propagation rule, which differs from Kipf and Welling's by a normalisation constant <Cite id="kipf2017" />.
- **<Gloss name="lstm" />.** An LSTM run over a random permutation of the neighbours. It is more expressive but not symmetric.
- **Pooling.** Each neighbour passes through a dense layer, then an element-wise maximum is taken:
  $\max\set{\sigma(\Wmat_{\mathrm{pool}}\hvec_{u}^{(k-1)} + \bvec) : u \in \Ncal(v)}$. The authors found no significant
  difference between max and mean at this step.

The GCN aggregator lacks the concatenation. The paper reports that the concatenation, a skip connection between search
depths, gives significant gains <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

## Worked example

Take a node $v$ with $\hvec_v^{(0)} = (1, 0)$ and a sample of two neighbours with vectors $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$. The
mean aggregator gives $\hvec_{\Ncal(v)}^{(1)} = (0.5, 1)$. The concatenation is $(1, 0, 0.5, 1)$. Let
$\Wmat_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which adds the node's vector to the aggregate,
and let $\sigma$ be <Gloss name="relu" />. Then $\sigma(\Wmat_1 [\cdot]) = (1.5, 1)$, with norm $\sqrt{1.5^2 + 1^2} \approx 1.803$. The
layer-1 vector is $\hvec_v^{(1)} \approx (0.832, 0.555)$. A different uniform sample of neighbours would give a
different aggregate, so the embedding is itself a <NoteLink to="random-variable">random variable</NoteLink> during training.

## Training objectives

Without labels, GraphSAGE applies a graph-based loss to the outputs $\zvec_u$ <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />:

$$
J(\zvec_u) = -\log \sigma(\zvec_u\transpose\zvec_v) - Q \cdot \expect_{v_n \sim P_n}\log\sigma(-\zvec_u\transpose\zvec_{v_n}),
$$

where $v$ co-occurs with $u$ on a fixed-length <NoteLink to="random-walk">random walk</NoteLink>, $\sigma$ is the logistic function, $P_n$ is a negative
sampling distribution and $Q$ is the number of negatives. The loss has the form of skip-gram with negative sampling used
for <NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink>. The difference is that $\zvec_u$ is computed from $u$'s
features and neighbourhood, not looked up in a table. With labels, the loss is replaced or augmented by a task loss
such as cross-entropy.

## Relation to the Weisfeiler–Lehman test

With $K = \abs{\Vcal}$, identity weight matrices and a hash function as aggregator without non-linearity, the GraphSAGE
forward pass is the Weisfeiler–Lehman vertex-refinement test for graph isomorphism. GraphSAGE replaces the hash by
trainable aggregators, a continuous approximation of the test <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />. Later work made the
expressive power precise for sum aggregation <Cite id="xu2019" />.

## Reported results

The paper classifies unseen nodes in a citation graph and a Reddit post graph, and nodes of unseen protein–protein
interaction graphs (PPI). Micro-averaged F1 scores with supervised training <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />:

| method           | citation | Reddit | PPI   |
| ---------------- | -------- | ------ | ----- |
| raw features     | 0.575    | 0.585  | 0.422 |
| DeepWalk + features | 0.701 | 0.691  | —     |
| GraphSAGE-GCN    | 0.772    | 0.930  | 0.500 |
| GraphSAGE-mean   | 0.820    | 0.950  | 0.598 |
| GraphSAGE-LSTM   | 0.832    | 0.954  | 0.612 |
| GraphSAGE-pool   | 0.839    | 0.948  | 0.600 |

Across the three domains the supervised models improved F1 by 51% on average over node features alone, and the
proposed aggregators gained 7.4% on average over the GCN aggregator <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />.

## Role in the PinSage family

<NoteLink to="pinsage">PinSage</NoteLink> is GraphSAGE adapted to Pinterest's pin–board graph. It keeps the
per-node computation graph, shared weights and concatenation. It replaces uniform neighbour sampling by the top $T$
nodes of random-walk visit counts and the mean or max aggregator by a mean weighted by those counts
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />. The name "Sage" recurs in later Pinterest models that do not all use graphs.

## Pitfalls

- **Sampling adds variance.** Each forward pass sees a different neighbourhood, so training is noisier than with full
  neighbourhoods.
- **Uniform sampling ignores importance.** Every neighbour is equally likely to be sampled, whether it is strongly or
  weakly connected. The paper lists non-uniform samplers as future work, which PinSage then supplied.
- **The LSTM aggregator is order-dependent.** Random permutation hides the dependence in expectation but not in a
  single pass.
