<Definition>
**PinnerFormer** maps a user's $M$ most recent pin engagements to one embedding $\uvec \in \reals^{256}$ of unit length,
compatible under <NoteLink to="dot-product">cosine similarity</NoteLink> with a pin embedding $\pvec$ computed from the pin's <Gloss name="pinsage" /> vector. A causally
masked <NoteLink to="transformer">transformer</NoteLink> produces an embedding at every position of the sequence. Training uses the **dense all-action
loss**: sampled positions each predict one randomly chosen positive engagement from the following $K$ days, through a
sampled softmax with in-batch and random negatives and a log-frequency correction. The embedding is recomputed once a
day in batch and has been in production at Pinterest since autumn 2021 <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

<NoteLink to="pinnersage">PinnerSage</NoteLink> gives each user a variable number of embeddings, often 20 or more. That
suits retrieval but not ranking models: storing many 256-dimensional float16 vectors in every training row does not
scale to datasets of billions of rows, and processing more than 5,000 numbers per example adds latency. Ranking models
therefore used a weighted aggregate of PinnerSage's top embeddings. PinnerFormer replaces this with one embedding
designed as a feature for many downstream models <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

A sequence model can be run in real time, after every action, or in batch, once a day. Real-time inference is expensive
because a user may take tens or hundreds of actions a day, and stateful streaming models need infrastructure to recover
from corrupted state. Batch inference is cheap but stale: an embedding trained to predict the next action is out of
date after the user's next few actions. PinnerFormer changes the training objective so that a daily embedding loses
less <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

## Model

**Inputs.** Each action in the past year is a pin engagement: a save, click, reaction or comment. It is described by the
pin's 256-dimensional PinSage embedding, learned embeddings of the action type and the surface, the log of the action's
duration, and time features <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" /> <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />. Time enters through
the raw timestamp, the time since the user's latest action and the gap between consecutive actions. Each is encoded
with $P$ fixed periods as $\cos(2\pi t/p_i + \phi_{2i-1})$ and $\sin(2\pi t/p_i + \phi_{2i})$ plus $\log t$, a variant
of Time2vec with learned phases $\phivec$. The absolute timestamp uses periods from 15 minutes to 365 days; the relative
features use 32 periods spaced evenly on a log scale from one second to four weeks.

**Sequence encoder.** The $M$ action vectors are stacked into $\Amat \in \reals^{M \times D_{\mathrm{in}}}$, projected
to the hidden size $H$ and summed with a learned <NoteLink to="positional-encoding">positional encoding</NoteLink>: $\Vmat^{(0)} = \Amat\Wmat + \mathrm{PE}$. Each of
$L$ pre-norm layers applies

$$
\Umat^{(l)} = \Vmat^{(l-1)} + \mathrm{MHSA}\paren{\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\Vmat^{(l-1)})}, \qquad
\Vmat^{(l)} = \Umat^{(l)} + \mathrm{FFN}\paren{\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\Umat^{(l)})},
$$

with a feed-forward width of $4H$, 8 attention heads and a <NoteLink to="causal-masking">causal mask</NoteLink>, so position $t$ attends only to actions at or
before $t$. Every output row passes through a two-layer <Gloss name="mlp" /> and is divided by its norm, giving embeddings
$\evec_1, \dots, \evec_M$ <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

**Pin tower.** A pin's embedding is an MLP applied to its PinSage vector, then normalised. User and pin sides are
trained together, as in a <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink>.

## Four training objectives

Positive engagements are homefeed saves, close-ups longer than 10 seconds and click-throughs longer than 10 seconds.
Let $\Fcal_t$ be the positive engagements in the $K$ days after position $t$. The paper compares four ways of pairing
user embeddings with target pins <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />:

- **Next action.** The last embedding $\evec_T$ predicts the next action $A_{T+1}$ if it is positive.
- **SASRec.** Every position $t$ predicts the next positive action, the objective of
  <NoteLink to="sasrec">SASRec</NoteLink> with sampled softmax in place of <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">binary cross-entropy</NoteLink>
  <Cite id="kang2018sasrec" />.
- **All action.** The last embedding $\evec_T$ predicts every positive in $\Fcal_T$, up to 32 sampled.
- **Dense all action.** A random set of positions $\set{s_i}$ is chosen, and each $\evec_{s_i}$ predicts one positive
  drawn at random from $\Fcal_{s_i}$.

The dense all-action loss is the average over sampled pairs,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DAA}} = \frac{1}{\abs{S}}\sum_{s \in S} \ell\paren{\evec_s, \pvec_{j_s}}, \qquad j_s \sim \Unif(\Fcal_s),
$$

where $\ell$ is the sampled softmax below. Each user sequence yields several training pairs, one per sampled position,
and the causal mask stops a position from seeing the future it must predict. The paper observed that the mask
substantially improves performance on this task <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

**Why a long window helps batch inference.** An embedding computed at time $t$ is used for up to a day. If it is
trained to predict only the next action, the target moves with every action the user takes, and the embedding is stale
within minutes. If it is trained to predict any positive in the next $K$ days, the target is the user's interests over
that horizon, which change slowly. The embedding is then close to what it would be if recomputed after each action.

The paper also explains why dense all action beats all action. In the all-action loss, every positive's gradient flows
through the same final embedding $\evec_T$ and is averaged there. In the dense version the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradients</NoteLink> enter at different
transformer outputs and are averaged only after passing through the transformer <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

## Sampled softmax with logQ correction

For a user embedding $\uvec$, positive $\pvec$ and negatives $\nvec_1, \dots, \nvec_N$, let
$s(\uvec, \pvec) = \uvec\transpose\pvec/\tau$ with a learned temperature $\tau \ge 0.01$. The loss is
<Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />

$$
\ell(\uvec, \pvec) = -\log \frac{e^{s(\uvec, \pvec) - \log Q(\pvec)}}
{e^{s(\uvec, \pvec) - \log Q(\pvec)} + \sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{s(\uvec, \nvec_j) - \log Q(\nvec_j)}},
$$

where $Q(v)$ is the probability that pin $v$ appears in the batch given the user. $Q$ is estimated with a count-min
sketch of pin frequencies <Cite id="cormode2005" />.

**Why subtract $\log Q$.** The full softmax over the corpus $\Pcal$ has denominator $Z = \sum_{v \in \Pcal} e^{s(v)}$.
Suppose each pin $v$ enters the sampled set $S$ independently with probability $Q(v)$. Then

$$
\expect\brack{\sum_{v \in S} e^{s(v) - \log Q(v)}} = \sum_{v \in \Pcal} Q(v)\,\frac{e^{s(v)}}{Q(v)} = Z.
$$

Dividing each sampled term by its inclusion probability, which is what subtracting $\log Q$ from the logit does, makes
the sampled sum an unbiased estimate of the full denominator. Without it, pins that are often positives for other users
appear often as in-batch negatives and are pushed down for everyone <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />.

**Negatives.** In-batch negatives are the other users' positives in the batch, with the user's own positives masked.
Random negatives are drawn uniformly from the pins eligible for homefeed. Negatives are gathered across all <Gloss name="gpu" plural />, as
many as fit in memory. Random negatives alone were too easy: with them, 1,000 pins accounted for 90% of the top 10
results retrieved for 100,000 users out of an index of 1 million. The final model uses a mixed pool with the
correction <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

The three positive action types are combined in one objective with equal weight, a multi-task setup without
task-specific heads.

## Serving

Each day a workflow infers embeddings for users who engaged in the past day and merges them with the previous day's
embeddings, falling back to the old ones where new ones are missing. The results go to a key-value feature store. Pin
embeddings are cheap and are recomputed daily, then compiled into an <NoteLink to="hnsw">HNSW</NoteLink> graph for
retrieval. Because inference is offline, the model can be larger than a real-time model could be. After a logging
error, fixing the input and rerunning the affected users restores correct embeddings the next day
<Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

## Reported results

Offline evaluation embeds each held-out user at time $t$ and measures Recall@10: the share of the user's positive pins in
$(t, t + 14\,\mathrm{d}]$ that rank in the top 10 among 1 million random pins. P90 Coverage@10 is the fraction of the
index that accounts for 90% of all users' top-10 results, a measure of global diversity <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

| comparison                                          | Recall@10 | P90 Coverage@10 |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------- | --------------- |
| PinnerSage, oracle over top 5 clusters              | 0.026     | 0.130           |
| PinnerSage, oracle over top 20 clusters             | 0.046     | 0.133           |
| next action objective                               | 0.186     | 0.050           |
| SASRec objective                                    | 0.198     | 0.048           |
| all action, 28 days                                 | 0.224     | 0.028           |
| dense all action, 14 days                           | 0.223     | 0.043           |
| dense all action, 28 days (PinnerFormer)            | 0.229     | 0.042           |

A 28-day training window beats a 14-day one although evaluation uses 14 days, which the authors attribute to more labels
per sequence. PinnerSage retrieves more diverse results per user when many clusters are used
<Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

**Batch versus real time.** For SASRec, Recall@10 was 0.198 with one embedding for the whole 14 days, 0.216 with daily
updates and 0.251 with updates after every action. For PinnerFormer the three values were 0.229, 0.243 and 0.264. Moving
from real-time to daily inference cost SASRec 13.9% and PinnerFormer 8.3%; the paper summarises this as nearly halving
the gap <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

**Ablations.** Removing the PinSage input dropped Recall@10 from 0.229 to 0.142 and P90 Coverage@10 to 0.0005, so the
model retrieved nearly the same pins for everyone. Removing the timestamp dropped Recall@10 to 0.210. Longer sequences
helped with diminishing returns; the final model uses $M = 256$ <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

**Online.** Replacing the aggregated PinnerSage feature in the homefeed ranking model with PinnerFormer changed homefeed
repins by $+7.5\%$, close-ups by $+6\%$ and click-throughs by $+1\%$, and sitewide time spent by $+1\%$, daily active users
by $+0.4\%$ and weekly active users by $+0.12\%$. Adding PinnerFormer to the ads ranking models raised <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rate</Gloss>
by 7.1% on related pins, 7.3% on search and 10.0% on homefeed <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Still not real time.** A realtime SASRec-style model had higher Recall@10 than the daily PinnerFormer. The
  remaining gap is what <NoteLink to="transact">TransAct</NoteLink> later filled inside the ranker.
- **One vector for many interests.** A single embedding retrieves less diverse results than PinnerSage with many
  clusters.
- **Correction trades diversity for recall.** The logQ correction raised Recall@10 with in-batch or mixed negatives but
  lowered global coverage <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.
