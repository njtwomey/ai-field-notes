<Definition>
**PinSage** is a graph convolutional network that computes an embedding $\zvec_u \in \reals^{d}$ for every node $u$ of
a bipartite graph of pins (image items) and boards (user-curated collections of pins). Each layer aggregates a node's
**importance-based neighbourhood**: the $T$ nodes most often visited by short random walks from $u$. The aggregation is
a mean weighted by those visit counts, called **importance pooling**. Training minimises a max-margin ranking loss on
pairs of pins engaged with in sequence, with hard negatives added by a curriculum. Pinterest trained and ran it on a
graph with 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Pinterest wants an embedding for each of about 2 billion pins, for related-pin retrieval by nearest-neighbour search and
as a feature in ranking models. Each pin has an image and text annotations. Pins are also connected through boards:
the graph has 2 billion pins, 1 billion boards and over 18 billion pin–board edges <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

A spectral <NoteLink to="graph-convolutional-network">graph convolutional network</NoteLink> multiplies features by
powers of the full normalised adjacency matrix. That requires the whole graph during training, which is infeasible at
this size. <NoteLink to="graphsage">GraphSAGE</NoteLink> removes the requirement by computing each node's embedding from
a sampled, fixed-size neighbourhood and shared aggregation weights <Cite id="hamilton2017graphsage" />. PinSage keeps
that design and changes four things: how neighbourhoods are sampled, how they are pooled, how negatives are chosen, and
how training and inference are run on commodity hardware <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## The convolution

A PinSage layer, called `convolve`, maps node $u$'s current vector $\zvec_u$, its neighbours' vectors
$\set{\zvec_v : v \in \Ncal(u)}$ and neighbour weights $\set{\alpha_v}$ to a new vector
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />:

$$
\nvec_u = \gamma\paren{\set{\operatorname{ReLU}(\Qmat\zvec_v + \qvec) : v \in \Ncal(u)}, \alphavec}, \qquad
\zvec_u^{\mathrm{new}} = \frac{\operatorname{ReLU}(\Wmat\,[\zvec_u \Vert \nvec_u] + \wvec)}
{\norm{\operatorname{ReLU}(\Wmat\,[\zvec_u \Vert \nvec_u] + \wvec)}_2}.
$$

Here $[\cdot \Vert \cdot]$ is concatenation, $\Qmat, \qvec, \Wmat, \wvec$ are the layer's weights and $\gamma$ is a
symmetric pooling function. The weights are shared by every node and differ between layers. Layer 0 is the input
feature vector $\xvec_u$. After $K$ layers a two-layer network gives the output,
$\zvec_u = \Gmat_2 \operatorname{ReLU}(\Gmat_1 \hvec_u^{(K)} + \gvec)$. The paper reports that concatenating
$\zvec_u$ with $\nvec_u$ works better than averaging them, and that the final normalisation stabilises training and
makes approximate nearest-neighbour search more efficient <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

<ConvolveDiagram />

For each pin, $\xvec_u$ concatenates a 4,096-dimensional visual embedding (the sixth fully connected layer of a VGG-16
classifier), a 256-dimensional Word2Vec-style embedding of its annotations and the log of its degree. Only pins have
features, so the model uses an even number of layers: pins feed boards, boards feed pins. The reported models use
$K = 2$, pooled dimension $m = 2048$ and embedding dimension $d = 1024$ <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Importance-based neighbourhoods

GraphSAGE samples neighbours uniformly from the one-hop neighbourhood. PinSage instead simulates short random walks from
$u$, counts how often each node is visited, normalises the counts to sum to 1 and keeps the $T$ nodes with the highest
normalised counts <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />. The walks come from Pinterest's
<NoteLink to="pixie-random-walk">Pixie</NoteLink> system <Cite id="eksombatchai2018pixie" />. This has two effects.
First, the neighbourhood has a fixed size $T$, so the memory of each minibatch is bounded. Second, each neighbour carries
a score of how strongly the graph connects it to $u$, which the pooling step uses.

**Why visit counts measure importance.** Let $\Pmat$ be the transition matrix of the walk on the graph, so
$(\Pmat^k)_{uv}$ is the probability that a walk from $u$ is at $v$ after $k$ steps. Suppose each walk continues after
every step with probability $1 - c$ and stops otherwise, so walk lengths are geometric. The expected number of visits
to $v \ne u$ during one walk is the sum over steps of the probability that the walk is still running and is at $v$:

$$
\expect[\text{visits to } v] = \sum_{k \ge 1} (1-c)^{k-1} (\Pmat^k)_{uv}
= \frac{1}{c(1-c)} \cdot c \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-c)^k (\Pmat^k)_{uv}
= \frac{\pi_u(v)}{c(1-c)}.
$$

The $k = 0$ term is zero because $v \ne u$. The vector
$\pivec_u = c \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-c)^k \evec_u\transpose \Pmat^k$ is the **personalised PageRank** of $u$, where $\evec_u$ is the indicator vector of node $u$ and $\pi_u(v)$ is entry $v$. It is the stationary
distribution of a walk that restarts at $u$ with probability $c$ at each step. It satisfies
$\pivec_u = c\,\evec_u\transpose + (1-c)\,\pivec_u \Pmat$, which is the fixed-point form of the same series. By the law of
large numbers, the total count at $v$ over many walks, divided by the number of walks, converges to the expectation. The
counts are therefore proportional to $\pi_u(v)$, and so are the normalised counts. This is the paper's statement that
in the limit of infinitely many simulations the normalised counts approximate personalised PageRank scores with respect
to $u$ <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Importance pooling

PinSage sets $\gamma$ to a weighted mean, with weight $\alpha_v$ proportional to $v$'s normalised visit count
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />:

$$
\nvec_u = \sum_{v \in \Ncal(u)} \alpha_v \operatorname{ReLU}(\Qmat\zvec_v + \qvec), \qquad
\alpha_v = \frac{\pi_u(v)}{\sum_{w \in \Ncal(u)} \pi_u(w)}.
$$

A neighbour that the walks reach often contributes more than one reached once. In the paper's ablation, replacing mean
pooling by importance pooling (with hard negatives in both) raises hit rate from 46% to 67%
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Worked example.** Take $T = 3$ neighbours with visit counts 6, 3 and 1. The weights are
$\alphavec = (0.6, 0.3, 0.1)$. If the transformed neighbour vectors are $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$, the pooled
vector is $0.6\,(1, 0) + 0.3\,(0, 1) + 0.1\,(1, 1) = (0.7, 0.4)$. A plain mean would give $(0.67, 0.67)$ to two
decimals, which lets the rarely visited third neighbour pull the result as much as the most visited one.

## Training

**Positive pairs.** A labelled pair $(q, i)$ is a pin $i$ that a user interacted with immediately after pin $q$. The
training set has 1.2 billion such pairs <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Loss.** For a positive pair, PinSage uses a max-margin (hinge) ranking loss over negatives $n_k$ drawn from a
negative distribution $P_n(q)$, with margin $\Delta$ <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />:

$$
J(\zvec_q, \zvec_i) = \expect_{n_k \sim P_n(q)} \max\set{0,\; \zvec_q\transpose\zvec_{n_k} - \zvec_q\transpose\zvec_i + \Delta}.
$$

Each term is zero once the positive's score beats the negative's by at least $\Delta$. Its gradient with respect to
$\zvec_q$ is $\zvec_{n_k} - \zvec_i$ while the margin is violated and $\zeros$ afterwards. A negative contributes to
learning only while it scores within $\Delta$ of the positive. The loss is a
<NoteLink to="triplet-and-margin-losses">triplet margin loss</NoteLink> on inner products.

**Shared negatives.** Each minibatch samples 500 negatives shared by all its examples, instead of separate negatives per
example. The paper observed no difference in quality and fewer embeddings to compute <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Why random negatives are too easy.** The system must rank about 1,000 relevant pins at the top of 2 billion. Take the
1,000 pins most related to $q$. The probability that a single uniform negative falls among them is
$1{,}000 / (2 \times 10^9) = 5 \times 10^{-7}$. The probability that at least one of 500 independent uniform negatives
does is $1 - (1 - 5 \times 10^{-7})^{500} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-4}$. Almost every minibatch therefore contains no
negative that is close to the query, and the hinge terms are almost all zero. The paper states the same point as a
resolution of 1 in 500 against a required resolution of 1 in 2 million <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Hard negatives.** For each positive pair, PinSage adds hard negatives: pins ranked 2,000 to 5,000 by personalised
PageRank from $q$, sampled at random from that range. They are related to $q$ but less related than $i$. Training on
them from the start doubled the number of epochs needed to converge <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Curriculum.** The first epoch uses no hard negatives. Epoch $n$ adds $n - 1$ hard negatives per item, so the task
gets harder as training proceeds, a form of curriculum learning <Cite id="bengio2009curriculum" />. The reported run used
6 hard negatives per pin, for 7.5 billion training examples in total. The curriculum improved performance by 12% in the
paper's summary <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Systems

**Producer–consumer minibatches.** The adjacency lists and features of billions of nodes stay in CPU memory (500 GB in
total). For each minibatch a CPU producer samples neighbourhoods, re-indexes the nodes involved into a small subgraph,
extracts the matching rows of the feature matrix and samples negatives. The GPU consumer receives this subgraph and runs
the convolutions without further CPU–GPU communication. CPU work for the next minibatch overlaps GPU work on the current
one, which almost halved training time. Training ran on one machine with 32 cores and 16 Tesla K80 GPUs, with batch
sizes from 512 to 4,096, a linear learning-rate warm-up in the first epoch and exponential decay afterwards
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**MapReduce inference.** Computing each node's embedding separately recomputes shared neighbours many times. PinSage
instead computes layer by layer. One MapReduce job projects every pin with the first layer's dense transform. A second
job joins pins to their boards and pools them into board vectors. Two more jobs compute the next layer's pin vectors
from the boards. Every node's vector is computed once per layer. Embeddings for 3 billion items were generated in less
than 24 hours on a Hadoop cluster of 378 AWS d2.8xlarge nodes <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Inductive training.** The model's parameters do not depend on the number of nodes, so it trains on a subgraph and
embeds the rest. Training used 20% of the boards with the pins they touch and 70% of the labelled pairs. A subgraph of
300 million items reached the best hit rate and cut runtime by a factor of 6 compared with the full graph
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

**Retrieval.** Related pins are the nearest neighbours of a query pin's embedding, found with
<NoteLink to="locality-sensitive-hashing">locality-sensitive hashing</NoteLink> and a two-level retrieval based on the
Weak AND operator <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Reported results

Offline, each test pair $(q, i)$ is scored by whether $i$ appears among the 500 nearest neighbours of $q$ in a sample of
5 million test pins (hit rate), and by a scaled mean reciprocal rank that divides each rank by 100
<Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

| method (Table 1)           | hit rate | MRR  |
| -------------------------- | -------- | ---- |
| visual embedding           | 17%      | 0.23 |
| annotation embedding       | 14%      | 0.19 |
| combined, 2-layer MLP      | 27%      | 0.37 |
| max pooling                | 39%      | 0.37 |
| mean pooling               | 41%      | 0.51 |
| mean pooling, cross-entropy| 29%      | 0.35 |
| mean pooling, hard negs    | 46%      | 0.56 |
| PinSage                    | 67%      | 0.59 |

The neighbourhood size trades accuracy for training time: $T = 10$ gave 60% hit rate and 0.51 MRR in 20 hours,
$T = 20$ gave 63% and 0.54 in 33 hours, and $T = 50$ gave 67% and 0.59 in 78 hours. A batch size of 2,048 gave the
shortest total training time, 48.8 hours <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

In head-to-head human judgements of which of two recommendations is more related to a query pin, PinSage won 56.5% of
decided comparisons against visual embeddings, 72.5% against annotation embeddings, 60.0% against combined embeddings
and 62.4% against Pixie. In production A/B tests on homefeed, PinSage raised repin rate by 10% to 30% over the
annotation and visual embeddings, depending on the setting <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

The distribution of cosine similarities between random pairs of PinSage embeddings has kurtosis 0.43, against 2.49 for
annotation embeddings and 1.20 for visual embeddings. A wider spread separates items of different relevance and lowers
the collision rate of locality-sensitive hashing <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.

## Compared with LightGCN

| property       | LightGCN                   | PinSage                                        |
| -------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| node inputs    | trainable ID embeddings    | content features                               |
| neighbourhood  | all neighbours, full graph | top $T$ by random-walk visits                  |
| layer          | linear, normalised sum     | dense layers, importance pooling, concatenation |
| new nodes      | need retraining            | embedded from features                         |
| intended scale | benchmark graphs           | billions of nodes                              |

<NoteLink to="lightgcn">LightGCN</NoteLink> shows that when nodes are IDs without features, transformation layers and
non-linearities hurt collaborative filtering. PinSage shows that with rich content features at web scale they help. The
two results agree: whether to transform depends on whether nodes have features worth transforming.

## Pitfalls

- **Hard negatives from the start.** Without the curriculum, hard negatives double the epochs to convergence.
- **Walk settings change the model.** Walk length, number of walks and $T$ decide which signals reach a node, and
  larger $T$ costs training time.
- **Labels from the incumbent system.** The positive pairs came from engagement with Pixie's recommendations, so the
  paper compares with Pixie only in human evaluation, not offline <Cite id="ying2018pinsage" />.
- **Features only on pins.** Boards have no input features, which forces an even number of layers.
