<Definition>
A **ranking metric** such as <Gloss name="ndcg" />, <Gloss name="mrr" />, recall at $k$ or <Gloss name="auroc" />
depends on a model's scores $\svec = (s_1, \dots, s_n)$ for one list only through the order they induce. A
**<NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">surrogate loss</NoteLink>** is a continuous, usually differentiable, function of the
scores that is minimised in place of the metric. A surrogate is related to a metric in one of three ways: it bounds the
metric's loss, it approximates the metric, or it is **consistent** for the metric, meaning that minimising its
<NoteLink to="expectation">expected value</NoteLink> over the label distribution produces scores whose order maximises
the expected metric.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A ranker is judged on the order of its list, and the order does not change when a score moves a little. The metric
therefore gives no gradient. A surrogate turns "put the relevant items first" into a smooth objective that
<NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient descent</NoteLink> can reduce. The surrogate and the metric can still disagree.
The surrogate may keep falling while the metric stays flat, and a surrogate that is minimised perfectly on the
population may rank by the wrong quantity. The theory of ranking surrogates asks when reducing the surrogate must also
improve the metric.

## Ranking metrics are piecewise constant

Let $\rho_i$ be the rank of item $i$ (1 for the top), $y_i$ its relevance label and $g_i = 2^{y_i} - 1$ its gain.
Ignoring ties,

$$
\rho_i = 1 + \sum_{j \ne i}\indicator[s_j > s_i].
$$

Every common metric is a function of the ranks and the labels alone.

| Metric | Value on one list |
| --- | --- |
| <Gloss name="dcg" /> | $\sum_i g_i / \log_2(1 + \rho_i)$; NDCG divides by the DCG of the ideal order |
| <NoteLink to="mean-average-precision-and-mean-reciprocal-rank">Reciprocal rank</NoteLink> | $\max_{i:\, y_i > 0} 1/\rho_i$ |
| Recall at $k$ | fraction of relevant items with $\rho_i \le k$ |
| AUC (binary labels) | fraction of (relevant, irrelevant) pairs with $s_i > s_j$ |

Each indicator $\indicator[s_j > s_i]$ is constant on either side of the hyperplane $s_i = s_j$. The $n(n - 1)/2$
hyperplanes cut $\reals^n$ into $n!$ open cones, one per strict order of the scores. Inside a cone every rank, and so
every metric, is constant. The <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> of the metric is zero inside every cone and
undefined on the boundaries. Gradient descent on the metric itself never moves.

## Three ways a surrogate relates to a metric

- **Bound.** The surrogate is at least the metric's loss at every score vector, for example
  $L(\svec) \ge -\ln\operatorname{NDCG}(\svec)$. Driving the surrogate down raises a floor under the metric; the metric
  itself can stay flat or fall. The bound holds list by list, with no assumption about the data distribution.
- **Approximation.** The surrogate is a smoothed copy of the metric with a temperature, such as
  <Gloss name="approxndcg" /> <Cite id="qin2010approx" />, SoftRank or <Gloss name="neuralndcg" /> (see
  <NoteLink to="listwise-ranking-losses">listwise ranking losses</NoteLink>). As the temperature goes to zero the copy
  tends to the metric, and its gradient tends to zero.
- **Consistency.** Fix the features of a list and treat the labels as random. A surrogate is consistent for a metric
  when any sequence of scores whose expected surrogate loss approaches its minimum has expected metric approaching its
  maximum <Cite id="ravikumar2011ndcg" />. Consistency is a population statement. It says what an unconstrained model
  learns from infinite data, not what a given model learns from a given sample.

The first and third are properties a loss can be proved to have. The lambda gradients of LambdaRank fit the first
kind: the LambdaLoss framework shows that they are the gradients of a coarse upper bound on an NDCG-based loss
<Cite id="wang2018lambdaloss" /> (see <NoteLink to="lambda-gradients">lambda gradients</NoteLink>).

## Softmax cross-entropy bounds reciprocal rank and NDCG

Take binary labels with a set $\Pcal$ of $R \ge 1$ relevant items. The softmax of the scores is
$p_i = e^{s_i} / \sum_k e^{s_k}$. The softmax <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy loss</NoteLink>
with the target spread evenly over the relevant items is

$$
L(\svec) = -\frac{1}{R}\sum_{i \in \Pcal}\ln p_i.
$$

Bruch et al. prove that this loss bounds $-\ln$ of both reciprocal rank and NDCG
<Cite id="bruch2019softmax" />.

**Step 1: the rank of an item is at most the inverse of its softmax probability.** For every real $z$,
$\indicator[z \ge 0] \le e^{z}$: when $z \ge 0$ the right side is at least 1, and otherwise the left side is 0. Apply
this with $z = s_j - s_i$:

$$
\rho_i \le 1 + \sum_{j \ne i}\indicator[s_j \ge s_i] \le 1 + \sum_{j \ne i} e^{s_j - s_i}
= \frac{\sum_k e^{s_k}}{e^{s_i}} = \frac{1}{p_i}.
$$

The middle expression counts ties against item $i$, so the bound holds for any way of breaking ties.

**Step 2: reciprocal rank.** Reciprocal rank is the largest $1/\rho_i$ over the relevant items. By step 1,
$1/\rho_i \ge p_i$. The largest of $R$ numbers is at least their geometric mean, so

$$
\operatorname{RR} \ge \max_{i \in \Pcal} p_i \ge \paren{\prod_{i \in \Pcal} p_i}^{1/R} = e^{-L(\svec)},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad -\ln\operatorname{RR} \le L(\svec).
$$

**Step 3: NDCG.** With binary labels every relevant item has gain 1. For $\rho \ge 1$, $\log_2(1 + \rho) \le \rho$, with
equality at $\rho = 1$. So the DCG satisfies

$$
\operatorname{DCG} = \sum_{i \in \Pcal}\frac{1}{\log_2(1 + \rho_i)} \ge \sum_{i \in \Pcal}\frac{1}{\rho_i}
\ge \sum_{i \in \Pcal} p_i.
$$

The ideal DCG is $\sum_{k=1}^{R} 1/\log_2(1 + k) \le R$, since each term is at most 1. Dividing,

$$
\operatorname{NDCG} \ge \frac{1}{R}\sum_{i \in \Pcal} p_i \ge \paren{\prod_{i \in \Pcal} p_i}^{1/R} = e^{-L(\svec)},
$$

where the second inequality is the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality, the form that
<Gloss name="jensens-inequality" /> takes for the concave logarithm. Taking logs,
$-\ln\operatorname{NDCG} \le L(\svec)$.

**Across lists.** For a set of lists $q = 1, \dots, Q$, the logarithm is concave, so
$\ln\paren{\frac{1}{Q}\sum_q \operatorname{NDCG}_q} \ge \frac{1}{Q}\sum_q \ln\operatorname{NDCG}_q \ge -\frac{1}{Q}\sum_q L_q$.
The mean NDCG over lists is therefore at least $e^{-\bar L}$, where $\bar L$ is the mean training loss. The same holds
for <Gloss name="mrr" />.

The bound is loose. Uniform scores over $n$ items give $L = \ln n$ and $e^{-L} = 1/n$, while the NDCG of a random order
is far above $1/n$. With one relevant item the bound is tight only when that item takes almost all the softmax mass.
With $R \ge 2$ relevant items the floor is at most $1/R$, so it stays loose even at a perfect ranking. Lowering the loss
raises the floor $e^{-L}$.

With graded labels the gains differ, and Bruch replaces the target by $(2^{y_i} - \gamma_i)/\sum_k (2^{y_k} - \gamma_k)$
with random $\gamma_k \in [0, 1]$, which makes the loss a convex bound on NDCG with graded
labels <Cite id="bruch2021xendcg" />.

## AUC and pairwise losses

With binary labels, the AUC of one list is the fraction of (relevant $i$, irrelevant $j$) pairs that the scores order
correctly. The <NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">pairwise</NoteLink> logistic loss
$\ln\paren{1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)}}$ is at least $\ln 2$ whenever $s_i \le s_j$, because the function is decreasing in
$s_i - s_j$ and equals $\ln 2$ at zero. Averaging over the $\abs{\Pcal}\abs{\Ncal}$ pairs, with $\Ncal$ the irrelevant
items,

$$
1 - \operatorname{AUC} \le \frac{1}{\ln 2}\cdot\frac{1}{\abs{\Pcal}\abs{\Ncal}}
\sum_{i \in \Pcal}\sum_{j \in \Ncal}\ln\paren{1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)}}.
$$

This is why <NoteLink to="bayesian-personalised-ranking">Bayesian personalised ranking</NoteLink>, which maximises the
mean of $\ln\sigma(s_i - s_j)$, is a smoothed AUC.

Consistency for AUC depends on the pair loss. Gao and Zhou prove that the pairwise exponential and logistic losses are
consistent for AUC and that the pairwise hinge loss is not <Cite id="gao2015auc" />.
<NoteLink to="pointwise-recommendation-losses">Pointwise losses</NoteLink> can also be consistent for AUC. Agarwal
bounds the AUC regret by the regret of any strongly proper pointwise loss, a class that includes the logistic and
squared losses <Cite id="agarwal2014bipartite" />.

AUC weighs every misordered pair equally, wherever it sits in the list. Swapping ranks 1 and 2 costs the same as
swapping ranks 50 and 51. A loss that bounds AUC well can therefore leave NDCG and MRR, which weight the top, poorly
optimised.

## Consistency for DCG and NDCG

Fix the features of a list, and let the label vector $\yvec$ be random with the
<NoteLink to="conditional-probability">conditional distribution</NoteLink> given those features. Let
$D(\rho) = 1/\log_2(1 + \rho)$ be the discount.

**DCG.** The expected DCG of an order with ranks $\rho_i$ is $\sum_i \expect[g_i]\, D(\rho_i)$, because the ranks
are fixed and expectation is linear. The discount decreases with rank. By the rearrangement inequality, the sum is
largest when the item with the largest $\expect[g_i]$ gets rank 1, the next largest rank 2, and so on. Any loss whose
population minimiser orders items by $\expect[g_i]$ is consistent for DCG. Squared error regression on the gains is
one <Cite id="cossock2008" />.

**NDCG.** NDCG divides by the ideal DCG, $\operatorname{IDCG}(\yvec)$, which is random. The same argument gives

$$
\expect[\operatorname{NDCG}] = \sum_i \eta_i\, D(\rho_i), \qquad \eta_i = \expect\brack{\frac{g_i}{\operatorname{IDCG}(\yvec)}},
$$

so the Bayes-optimal order sorts by $\eta_i$. Ravikumar et al. show that a surrogate is NDCG-consistent exactly when
its population minimiser is an order-preserving function of $\etavec$ <Cite id="ravikumar2011ndcg" />.

**Softmax cross-entropy.** With target $t_i(\yvec) = g_i / \sum_k g_k$, the loss $-\sum_i t_i(\yvec)\ln p_i$ is linear
in the target. Its expectation is $-\sum_i \tau_i \ln p_i$ with $\tau_i = \expect[t_i(\yvec)]$. That is a cross-entropy
from $\tauvec$ to $\pvec$, minimised by <NoteLink to="kullback-leibler-divergence">Gibbs' inequality</NoteLink> at
$\pvec = \tauvec$. The minimiser sorts by

$$
\tau_i = \expect\brack{\frac{g_i}{\sum_k g_k}},
$$

which normalises by the total gain, not by the ideal DCG. The two orders agree whenever
$\sum_k g_k / \operatorname{IDCG}$ is the same for every label vector that can occur. That holds when each list has
exactly one relevant item (both normalisers equal its gain) and when the labels are a deterministic function of the
features. It fails in general. Ravikumar et al. show that the cosine, squared-error and <Gloss name="listnet" />
surrogates are not NDCG-consistent, and that replacing the target by $g_i / \operatorname{IDCG}(\yvec)$ restores
consistency <Cite id="ravikumar2011ndcg" />. They also show that the strongly NDCG-consistent surrogates of this form
are exactly the Bregman divergences between the normalised gains and an order-preserving transform of the scores. Bruch
argues that softmax cross-entropy is NDCG-consistent in the two common special cases above: a single click per query,
and graded labels where each query appears once <Cite id="bruch2021xendcg" />.

<Derivation title="A list where softmax cross-entropy ranks by the wrong quantity">
Three items have binary labels. With probability 0.35 only item 1 is relevant, $\yvec = (1, 0, 0)$. With probability
0.65 items 2 and 3 are relevant, $\yvec = (0, 1, 1)$.

The softmax target is $(1, 0, 0)$ in the first case and $(0, 0.5, 0.5)$ in the second, so
$\tauvec = (0.35, 0.325, 0.325)$. Softmax cross-entropy learns to put item 1 first.

The ideal DCG is 1 in the first case and $1 + 1/\log_2 3 = 1.631$ in the second, so
$\etavec = (0.35,\ 0.65/1.631,\ 0.65/1.631) = (0.35, 0.399, 0.399)$. NDCG prefers items 2 and 3 first.

The expected NDCG of the order 1, 2, 3 is $0.35 \cdot 1 + 0.65 \cdot (1/\log_2 3 + 1/2)/1.631 = 0.801$. The order
2, 3, 1 has expected NDCG $0.35 \cdot 0.5 + 0.65 \cdot 1 = 0.825$. Softmax cross-entropy converges to the worse order.
Regression on the gains orders by $\expect[g_i] = (0.35, 0.65, 0.65)$ and happens to be right here. Ravikumar et al.
give a two-item example with graded labels where regression on the gains is wrong and the IDCG-normalised order is
right <Cite id="ravikumar2011ndcg" />.
</Derivation>

**Metrics with no convex consistent surrogate.** For some metrics consistency is out of reach for any convex loss. Duchi
et al. show that many common pairwise surrogates are inconsistent for the pairwise disagreement loss, even under low
noise <Cite id="duchi2010consistency" />. Calauzènes et al. prove that no surrogate that is convex in the scores is
consistent for <Gloss name="ap" />, for <Gloss name="err-metric" /> or for pairwise disagreement
<Cite id="calauzenes2012" />. 

**Metrics with convex consistent surrogates.** For metrics built from sums of gains times discounts, such as DCG, convex
consistent
surrogates exist whenever the supervision can be put in a <NoteLink to="linear-programming">standard form</NoteLink>
<Cite id="buffoni2011" />. Truncated metrics such as NDCG at $k$ raise the same question for the top $k$ positions. Xia
et al. give conditions for consistency with the top-$k$ ranking and modify listwise losses to meet them
<Cite id="xia2009topk" />.

## Calibration versus ranking

A model is <NoteLink to="classifier-calibration">calibrated</NoteLink> when, among items it gives probability $q$, a
fraction $q$ are relevant. Ranking and calibration are independent properties.

- **Good ranking, poor calibration.** Every pairwise and listwise loss named here is unchanged when the same constant
  is added to all scores of a list. The loss fixes the order and the differences between scores, and leaves the level
  of each list free. The sigmoid of such a score is not a click probability, and scores of different lists are not on
  one scale.
- **Good calibration, poor ranking.** A model that predicts the overall click rate for every item is calibrated and
  has AUC 0.5.
- **Order-preserving maps.** Any strictly increasing transform of the scores keeps every ranking metric and changes the
  calibration. <NoteLink to="platt-scaling">Platt scaling</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="isotonic-calibration">isotonic regression</NoteLink> fix calibration after training without
  changing the order.

Pointwise <Gloss name="bce">binary cross-entropy</Gloss> is a <NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">proper scoring
rule</NoteLink>. Its population minimiser is the true click probability, so it gives calibration and, through Agarwal's
bound, AUC consistency. It ignores the position weighting of NDCG and MRR. Systems that need both a good order and
calibrated scores, such as ad auctions that multiply a click probability by a bid, combine the two. Yan et al. study
<NoteLink to="ranking-losses">ranking losses</NoteLink> that keep the score scale <Cite id="yan2022scale" />. Bai et al.
pair sigmoid cross-entropy with a listwise cross-entropy computed on $\sigma(s_i) / \sum_k \sigma(s_k)$, so that both
terms are minimised by the same scores, $\sigma(s_i)$ equal to the click probability <Cite id="bai2023rcr" />.

## Choosing a loss

- **One positive per list** (a click on a slate, the next item in a session): softmax cross-entropy bounds $-\ln$ MRR
  and $-\ln$ NDCG, and is NDCG-consistent.
- **Graded labels, each query seen once:** softmax cross-entropy with gain targets, XE-NDCG or lambda gradients. All
  weight the top of the list.
- **Several positives drawn from a noisy distribution:** gain targets normalised by the ideal DCG give NDCG-consistent
  losses; targets normalised by the total gain are not NDCG-consistent in general.
- **The metric is AUC or the task is retrieval of all relevant items:** pairwise logistic loss or pointwise binary
  cross-entropy. Avoid the pairwise hinge, which is not AUC-consistent.
- **Scores feed a later calculation** (an auction, a blend of objectives, a threshold): add a pointwise calibrated term
  or calibrate after training.
- **In every case:** choose the model and its hyperparameters on the metric itself, measured on held-out lists. A
  surrogate that keeps falling says nothing once the metric has stopped rising.

## Worked example

One list has four items with binary labels $\yvec = (1, 0, 1, 0)$ and scores $\svec = (0.5, 1.0, 0.0, -0.5)$. The
order is 2, 1, 3, 4, so the relevant items have ranks $\rho_1 = 2$ and $\rho_3 = 3$.

**Metrics.** Reciprocal rank is $1/2$. The DCG is $1/\log_2 3 + 1/\log_2 4 = 0.631 + 0.5 = 1.131$. The ideal DCG is
$1 + 0.631 = 1.631$, so NDCG is $0.693$. Of the four (relevant, irrelevant) pairs, (1, 4) and (3, 4)
are ordered correctly and (1, 2) and (3, 2) are not, so AUC is $0.5$.

**Step 1 of the bound.** The softmax is $\pvec = (0.276, 0.455, 0.167, 0.102)$. Then $1/p_1 = 3.62 \ge \rho_1 = 2$
and $1/p_3 = 5.97 \ge \rho_3 = 3$.

**The bound.** The loss is $L = -\tfrac{1}{2}(\ln 0.276 + \ln 0.167) = 1.537$, and $e^{-L} = 0.215$. The bound holds:
$0.215 \le 0.5$ for reciprocal rank and $0.215 \le 0.693$ for NDCG. It is loose because the relevant items hold only
44% of the softmax mass.

**Surrogate moves, metric stays.** Raising $s_1$ from 0.5 to 0.9 lowers $L$ to 1.465. Item 1 is still below item 2,
so reciprocal rank and NDCG do not change. Raising $s_1$ to 1.1 lowers $L$ to 1.442 and moves item 1 to the top.
Reciprocal rank jumps to 1 and NDCG to $(1 + 0.5)/1.631 = 0.920$.

**Pairwise bound.** The mean pairwise logistic loss over the four pairs is 0.769, so the bound gives
$1 - \operatorname{AUC} \le 0.769/\ln 2 = 1.109$. The true value is 0.5. The bound exceeds 1 and says nothing here.
Correctly ordered pairs with small margins still add to the loss: the pair (3, 4), with margin 0.5, contributes 0.474.

**Calibration.** Suppose instead two items have true click probabilities 0.10 and 0.05. Scores $(3, 2)$ and
$(-2, -3)$ give the same order and the same value of any shift-invariant ranking loss. Their sigmoids are
$(0.953, 0.881)$ and $(0.119, 0.047)$. Only the second is close to calibrated.

## Assumptions

- **Binary labels for the softmax bound.** The derivation above uses unit gains. Graded gains need a different target,
  such as the one of XE-NDCG.
- **An unconstrained model for consistency.** Consistency results assume the model can reach the population minimiser
  for every feature vector. With a restricted model class, such as a linear scorer, the population minimiser may be out
  of reach, and consistency then gives no guarantee.
- **A fixed candidate list.** The metrics and bounds are per list. When lists are sampled, as in
  <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">sampled softmax</NoteLink>, the bound applies to the sampled list, not to the full
  catalogue.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading the surrogate as the metric.** A falling training loss can leave the metric unchanged, as in the worked
  example, or lower it, for example when the loss improves the tail of the list at the expense of the top.
- **Using ranking scores as probabilities.** Scores from shift-invariant losses need a calibration step before they
  are compared across lists or multiplied by a bid.
- **Trusting a loose bound.** The softmax bound guarantees a floor, $\operatorname{NDCG} \ge e^{-L}$, not that
  NDCG moves with the loss. Two models with the same
  loss can differ widely in NDCG.
- **Assuming "convex and consistent" is always available.** For average precision and expected reciprocal rank no
  convex surrogate is consistent. A non-convex smoothed metric or a lambda-weighted loss is then the practical choice.
