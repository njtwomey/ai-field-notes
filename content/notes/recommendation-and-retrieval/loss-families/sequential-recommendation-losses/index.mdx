<Definition>
A **sequential recommendation loss** trains a model that maps a user's item history $(i_1, \ldots, i_t)$ to a score
$s_{t,j}$ for every catalogue item $j \in \Ical$, so that the item the user takes next, $i_{t+1}$, scores highly. The
main families are **next-item cross-entropy** (a softmax over the whole catalogue),
**<NoteLink to="two-tower-model">sampled softmax</NoteLink>** (the same over the positive and a sample of negatives),
**binary cross-entropy with sampled negatives** (one logistic loss per item), the **Cloze** loss of masked-item models,
and **pairwise session losses** such as TOP1 and BPR-max. The choice of loss changes what the scores estimate, and it
affects accuracy as much as the architecture does <Cite id="klenitskiy2023" /> <Cite id="petrov2023gsasrec" />.
</Definition>

## Setup

A sequence model such as <Gloss name="sasrec" /> produces an output vector $\fvec_t \in \reals^d$ at every position $t$
of a user's sequence. The score of candidate item $j$ for position $t + 1$ is the dot product
$s_{t,j} = \fvec_t\transpose\evec_j$ with the item's embedding $\evec_j \in \reals^d$ <Cite id="kang2018sasrec" />. The
catalogue has $\abs{\Ical}$ items. For one position, write $s_j$ for $s_{t,j}$ and $y = i_{t+1}$ for the label.

A causal model is trained at every position at once. One sequence of length $n$ gives $n$ labelled examples, each
position predicting its successor, and the causal mask stops position $t$ from seeing its own label. The losses below
are written for one position; the training loss sums them over positions and users.

For a fixed history, let $p_j$ be the true probability that the next item is $j$, with $\sum_j p_j = 1$. Each loss has a
population optimum: the scores that minimise its expectation over $y \sim p$. That optimum says what the trained scores
estimate.

## Next-item cross-entropy

The full-catalogue loss is the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">cross-entropy</NoteLink> of a
<NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax</NoteLink> over all items:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} = -\log \pi_y, \qquad \pi_j = \frac{e^{s_j}}{\sum_{l \in \Ical} e^{s_l}}.
$$

Its gradient with respect to a score is

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}}{\partial s_j} = \pi_j - \indicator[j = y].
$$

The label's score rises by $1 - \pi_y$ and every other score falls in proportion to its current probability. Averaged
over labels $y \sim p$, the expected gradient is $\pi_j - p_j$. It vanishes only when $\pi_j = p_j$ for every $j$, so
the population optimum of full cross-entropy is the true next-item distribution. The scores are calibrated in the sense
that the softmax probabilities sum to 1 and match $p$.

The cost is the normaliser. Each position needs all $\abs{\Ical}$ scores, which is $O(\abs{\Ical} d)$ per position and
$O(n\abs{\Ical})$ memory for the logits of one sequence. <Gloss name="bert4rec" /> trained with this loss; Klenitskiy
and Vasilev trained SASRec with it and found that SASRec then matched or beat BERT4Rec and trained faster
<Cite id="klenitskiy2023" />.

## Binary cross-entropy with sampled negatives

The original SASRec avoided the normaliser. At each position it drew one negative item $j^-$ uniformly from the items
the user had not interacted with and applied a <NoteLink to="pointwise-recommendation-losses">pointwise</NoteLink>
logistic loss to each item separately <Cite id="kang2018sasrec" />. With $k$ negatives $j^-_1, \ldots, j^-_k$ the binary
cross-entropy (BCE) loss is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}} = -\log\sigma(s_y) - \sum_{m=1}^{k}\log\paren{1 - \sigma(s_{j^-_m})},
$$

where $\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$. Each item's score is read as an independent probability $q_j = \sigma(s_j)$ that
$j$ is the next item. Nothing forces $\sum_j q_j = 1$.

### What binary cross-entropy learns

The population optimum of this loss is not $p$. Fix one item $i$ and collect every term of the loss in which $s_i$
appears. The item is the positive with probability $p_i$. It is a negative only when it is not the positive, probability
$1 - p_i$. Let $\Ical^-$ be the $\abs{\Ical} - 1$ items other than the positive. Each of the $k$ negatives is drawn
uniformly from $\Ical^-$, so item $i$ is the $m$-th negative with probability $1/\abs{\Ical^-}$. Define the **sampling
rate**

$$
\alpha = \frac{k}{\abs{\Ical^-}} = \frac{k}{\abs{\Ical} - 1}.
$$

The expected contribution of item $i$ to the loss, as a function of $q_i = \sigma(s_i)$, is

$$
\expect\brack{\mathcal{L}_i} = -p_i\log q_i - \alpha\paren{1 - p_i}\log\paren{1 - q_i}.
$$

The items do not interact in this sum, so each $q_i$ is optimised separately. Setting the derivative to zero,

$$
-\frac{p_i}{q_i} + \frac{\alpha(1 - p_i)}{1 - q_i} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
q_i = \frac{p_i}{p_i + \alpha(1 - p_i)}.
$$

The second derivative $p_i/q_i^2 + \alpha(1 - p_i)/(1 - q_i)^2$ is positive, so this is the minimum. Petrov and
Macdonald proved this result for SASRec's loss <Cite id="petrov2023gsasrec" />.

### Overconfidence

The optimum is far from calibrated when $\alpha$ is small. The negative term is weighted by $\alpha$, while the positive
term is not. Any item with $p_i \gg \alpha$ has $q_i \approx 1$. SASRec uses $k = 1$, so $\alpha = 1/(\abs{\Ical} - 1)$
and every item many times more likely than a uniform guess ($p_i \gg \alpha$) is predicted with probability close to 1.
Petrov and Macdonald call a model **overconfident** when $q_i \gg p_i$ for its highly scored items
<Cite id="petrov2023gsasrec" />.

In logits the effect is a constant shift. Taking $\log\frac{q_i}{1 - q_i}$ of the optimum gives

$$
\operatorname{logit} q_i = \operatorname{logit} p_i - \log\alpha.
$$

Every score is raised by the same $-\log\alpha$, so the population optimum still ranks items in the order of $p$.

The damage is to training, not to the optimum. The sigmoid saturates near $q = 1$, so the probabilities of the top items
are nearly equal and the positive's gradient $-(1 - q_y)$ is close to zero. The loss then spends its effort separating
the top items from the bottom ones, not ordering the top items among themselves. A top item that is sampled as a
negative contributes $-\log(1 - q_i)$, which grows without bound as $q_i \to 1$, and Petrov and Macdonald report
numerical overflow and unstable training from these rare large terms <Cite id="petrov2023gsasrec" />.

### Generalised binary cross-entropy

**gBCE** (generalised BCE) raises the positive's sigmoid to a power $\beta \in (0, 1]$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gBCE}}^{\beta} = -\log\sigma^{\beta}(s_y) - \sum_{m=1}^{k}\log\paren{1 - \sigma(s_{j^-_m})}.
$$

Since $\log\sigma^\beta = \beta\log\sigma$, the positive term is multiplied by $\beta$. The expected contribution of
item $i$ becomes $-\beta p_i\log q_i - \alpha(1 - p_i)\log(1 - q_i)$, and the same calculation gives

$$
q_i = \frac{\beta p_i}{\beta p_i + \alpha(1 - p_i)}, \qquad \operatorname{logit} q_i = \operatorname{logit} p_i + \log\frac{\beta}{\alpha}.
$$

Setting $\beta = \alpha$ gives $q_i = p_i$: the model is calibrated although it sees only $k$ negatives. Setting
$\beta = 1$ recovers plain BCE <Cite id="petrov2023gsasrec" />.

Because $\beta$ must lie between $\alpha$ and 1, and $\alpha$ depends on the catalogue and on $k$, the paper
reparameterises it with a **calibration parameter** $t \in [0, 1]$:

$$
\beta = \alpha\paren{t\paren{1 - \frac{1}{\alpha}} + \frac{1}{\alpha}} = 1 - t(1 - \alpha).
$$

$t = 0$ is BCE and $t = 1$ is full calibration. **gSASRec** is SASRec trained with gBCE and more negatives. In practice
the loss is computed as ordinary BCE on a transformed positive score
$\gamma(s_y) = \log\paren{1/(\sigma^{-\beta}(s_y) - 1)}$, which satisfies $\sigma(\gamma(s)) = \sigma^\beta(s)$ and
reuses numerically stable BCE implementations. The authors recommend $t$ between 0.75 and 0.9 with 128 to 256 negatives,
and report that $t$ very close to 1 slows training <Cite id="petrov2023gsasrec" />.

## Sampled softmax

<NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">Sampled softmax</NoteLink> keeps the softmax form and replaces the
normaliser with an estimate from $k$ sampled items. It is the standard remedy for catalogues too large for the full loss
<Cite id="jean2015sampledsoftmax" />. Negatives are drawn from a proposal distribution $Q$ over items, and each sampled
logit is corrected by subtracting $\log(kQ(j))$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SSM}} = -\log\frac{e^{s_y}}{e^{s_y} + \sum_{m=1}^{k} e^{s_{j^-_m} - \log(kQ(j^-_m))}}.
$$

The correction makes the sampled sum an unbiased estimate of $\sum_{j \ne y} e^{s_j}$, the sum over the items other
than the positive, so the whole denominator estimates the partition function $Z$.
With uniform sampling the correction is the same constant $\log(k/\abs{\Ical^-})$ for every negative. With
**in-batch negatives**, the positives of other sequences in the batch serve as negatives; they are drawn in proportion
to item popularity, and without the correction the loss penalises popular items too much. Estimating $Q(j)$ from
streaming item frequencies and subtracting $\log Q(j)$ is the **logQ correction** <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />.

Other variants choose negatives by score. **Scalable cross-entropy** finds, for each position, the items the model
currently scores highest, by an approximate <Gloss name="mips">maximum inner product search</Gloss>, and computes the
softmax over those, since the high-scoring wrong items dominate the full <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>
<Cite id="mezentsev2024sce" />.

<Derivation title="Why the logQ correction estimates the normaliser">
The full normaliser is $Z = e^{s_y} + \sum_{j \ne y} e^{s_j}$. Draw $j^-_1, \ldots, j^-_k$ independently from $Q$ over
the items other than $y$. For any function $g$,
$\expect_{j \sim Q}\brack{g(j)/Q(j)} = \sum_{j \ne y} g(j)$, the identity behind
<NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance sampling</NoteLink>. With $g(j) = e^{s_j}$,

$$
\expect\brack{\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{e^{s_{j^-_m}}}{kQ(j^-_m)}} = \sum_{j \ne y} e^{s_j},
\qquad
\frac{e^{s_j}}{kQ(j)} = e^{s_j - \log(kQ(j))}.
$$

So the denominator of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SSM}}$ is an unbiased estimate of $Z$. The loss takes the logarithm of this
estimate, and $\expect[\log\hat{Z}] \le \log Z$ by <NoteLink to="expectation">Jensen's inequality</NoteLink>, so the
sampled loss is biased low for finite $k$. The bias shrinks as $k$ grows. Without the correction, an item that $Q$
samples often has its $e^{s_j}$ counted $kQ(j)$ times on average instead of once, and its score is pushed down too far.
</Derivation>

## The Cloze objective

A bidirectional encoder cannot be trained on next-item prediction, because position $t$ would attend to $i_{t+1}$ and
copy it. BERT4Rec instead replaces a random proportion $\rho$ of the items with a mask token and predicts each masked
item from both sides with a full softmax <Cite id="sun2019bert4rec" />, the masked-language-model objective of
<Gloss name="bert" /> <Cite id="devlin2019" />. With $M$ the set of masked positions,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Cloze}} = -\sum_{t \in M}\log\pi_{t, i_t}.
$$

Training and inference differ. In training a mask sits among real items on both sides. At inference the mask is appended
after the last item and nothing lies to its right, so the model predicts from one side only, the case it saw least. Sun
et al. narrowed this gap by adding training sequences in which only the last item is masked
<Cite id="sun2019bert4rec" />.

The Cloze loss supervises fewer positions: a sequence of length $n$ gives about $\rho n$ labels per pass, against $n$
for a causal model. With $\rho = 0.2$ and $n = 200$ that is 40 labels against 200.

## Which future item is the label

Cross-entropy is minimised when the model's distribution equals the distribution of the label. The expected loss
$-\sum_j p^{\mathrm{label}}_j\log\pi_j$ is minimised over distributions $\pi$ at $\pi = p^{\mathrm{label}}$, since it
equals the <NoteLink to="entropy">entropy</NoteLink> of $p^{\mathrm{label}}$ plus
$\KL(p^{\mathrm{label}} \,\|\, \pi) \ge 0$. The choice of label therefore chooses what the model estimates.

- **Next action.** The label is $i_{t+1}$. The model estimates the next-item distribution, which changes after every
  action.
- **Next item, not a held-out item.** Covington et al. found that predicting a user's next watch worked better than
  predicting a randomly held-out watch, because a held-out label lets the model use future context it will not have at
  serving time <Cite id="covington2016youtube" />.
- **Dense all action.** <NoteLink to="pinnerformer">PinnerFormer</NoteLink> samples positions and pairs each with one
  positive engagement drawn uniformly from the $K$ days that follow it. If $W_t$ is the set of future engagements in the
  window, the label distribution is the average $\frac{1}{\abs{W_t}}\sum_{\tau \in W_t} \delta_{i_\tau}$ over the
  window, so the embedding estimates the user's interests over $K$ days. An embedding computed once a day then goes
  stale more slowly than one trained on the next action <Cite id="pancha2022pinnerformer" />.

## Pairwise session losses

<Gloss name="gru4rec" /> trains a recurrent network on sessions and uses the targets of the other sessions in a
mini-batch as negatives <Cite id="hidasi2016gru4rec" />. Its losses compare the target score $r_i$ with the scores $r_j$
of $N_S$ sampled negatives. Sampled <Gloss name="bpr" /> averages pairwise logistic losses, and TOP1 averages a sigmoid
of the score difference plus a penalty that pulls negative scores towards zero:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BPR}} = -\frac{1}{N_S}\sum_{j=1}^{N_S}\log\sigma(r_i - r_j),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TOP1}} = \frac{1}{N_S}\sum_{j=1}^{N_S}\sigma(r_j - r_i) + \sigma(r_j^2).
$$

Averaging makes the gradient vanish as $N_S$ grows <Cite id="hidasi2018topk" />. The gradient of the BPR loss with
respect to the target score is

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BPR}}}{\partial r_i} = -\frac{1}{N_S}\sum_{j=1}^{N_S}\paren{1 - \sigma(r_i - r_j)}.
$$

A negative with $r_j \ll r_i$ contributes almost 0 to the sum but still counts in $N_S$. Most sampled items are such
easy negatives, so if $N_R$ of the $N_S$ negatives score near the target, the gradient is roughly $N_R/N_S$ of a single
pair's, and it shrinks as more negatives are added.

Hidasi and Karatzoglou compare the target with the maximum negative score instead, smoothed by a softmax over the
negatives, $\omega_j = e^{r_j}/\sum_{l} e^{r_l}$ <Cite id="hidasi2018topk" />:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BPR\text{-}max}} = -\log\sum_{j=1}^{N_S} \omega_j\,\sigma(r_i - r_j) + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{N_S} \omega_j r_j^2,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TOP1\text{-}max}} = \sum_{j=1}^{N_S} \omega_j\paren{\sigma(r_j - r_i) + \sigma(r_j^2)}.
$$

The first term of BPR-max is $-\log P(r_i > r_{\max})$, with $P(r_i > r_j)$ approximated by $\sigma(r_i - r_j)$ and
$P(r_j = r_{\max})$ by $\omega_j$. Its gradient with respect to $r_i$ is a weighted average of pairwise gradients with
weights $\omega_j\sigma(r_i - r_j)$. Easy negatives get weights near zero and do not dilute it, so adding negatives no
longer shrinks the update. The last term is a softmax-weighted $\ell_2$ penalty on the negative scores with strength
$\lambda$ <Cite id="hidasi2018topk" />. These losses are the <NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">pairwise ranking
losses</NoteLink> adapted to sampled negatives.

## Worked example

Take a catalogue of $\abs{\Ical} = 10{,}001$ items, so $\abs{\Ical^-} = 10{,}000$. For one user and position, two items
have true next-item probabilities $p_A = 0.05$ and $p_B = 0.01$.

**SASRec's BCE, $k = 1$.** The sampling rate is $\alpha = 10^{-4}$. The optimum is

$$
q_A = \frac{0.05}{0.05 + 10^{-4} \cdot 0.95} = 0.9981, \qquad q_B = \frac{0.01}{0.01 + 10^{-4} \cdot 0.99} = 0.9902.
$$

Both items are predicted with probability above 0.99, although only one item can be next. In logits,
$\operatorname{logit} q_A = 6.266$ and $\operatorname{logit} q_B = 4.615$. The gap of 1.651 equals
$\operatorname{logit} 0.05 - \operatorname{logit} 0.01 = -2.944 + 4.595$: the order is kept and every logit is shifted
by $-\log 10^{-4} = 9.210$.

**gBCE, $k = 256$, $t = 0.75$.** Now $\alpha = 0.0256$ and $\beta = 1 - 0.75 \cdot 0.9744 = 0.2692$. Then

$$
q_A = \frac{0.2692 \cdot 0.05}{0.2692 \cdot 0.05 + 0.0256 \cdot 0.95} = 0.356, \qquad
q_B = \frac{0.2692 \cdot 0.01}{0.2692 \cdot 0.01 + 0.0256 \cdot 0.99} = 0.096.
$$

The logit shift is $\log(\beta/\alpha) = 2.353$ instead of 9.210, and the probabilities are off the flat top of the
sigmoid. With $t = 1$, $\beta = \alpha = 0.0256$ and the optimum is $q_A = 0.05$, $q_B = 0.01$.

**Full cross-entropy.** The softmax optimum is $\pi_A = 0.05$ and $\pi_B = 0.01$ directly, at the cost of 10,001 logits
per position.

## Evaluation pitfalls

Losses are compared through offline metrics, and the metric protocol can reverse the comparison.

- **<NoteLink to="progress-and-baselines-in-recommendation">Sampled metrics</NoteLink>.** Many papers rank the held-out
  item against 100 randomly sampled items instead of the whole catalogue. Krichene and Rendle showed that sampled
  versions of metrics such as <Gloss name="ndcg" /> and <NoteLink to="hit-rate-and-r-precision">hit rate</NoteLink> are
  inconsistent with the full metrics and can reverse the order of two models <Cite id="krichene2020sampled" />. In the
  catalogue above, suppose the held-out item has <NoteLink to="rank-and-null-space">full rank</NoteLink> 101: 100 items
  score higher. Its full hit rate at 10 is 0. Ranked against 100 negatives sampled without replacement from the 10,000
  other items, the number of higher-scoring negatives is hypergeometric with mean $100 \cdot 100/10{,}000 = 1$. The
  sampled rank is at most 10 with probability 0.99999995 and is 1 with probability 0.364, so the sampled hit rate at 10
  is essentially 1.
- **Loss and metric alignment.** A loss that samples negatives uniformly spends most of its gradient on easy items,
  while top-heavy metrics reward the order of the first few (see <NoteLink to="ranking-surrogates-and-metrics">ranking
  surrogates and metrics</NoteLink>). This is the same mismatch as between BCE's saturated top items and
  <Gloss name="ndcg" /> at 10.
- **Unequal comparisons.** An architecture comparison is confounded when the two models use different losses, as with
  the original SASRec (BCE, one negative) against BERT4Rec (full softmax) <Cite id="klenitskiy2023" />.

## Assumptions

- The optimum results assume enough data and capacity for the model to reach the population minimum for every history.
  A finite model shares parameters across histories and does not.
- The BCE analysis assumes negatives drawn uniformly, with replacement, from the items other than the positive.
  Popularity or in-batch sampling gives each item its own rate $\alpha_i$, and the same argument gives
  $q_i = p_i/(p_i + \alpha_i(1 - p_i))$.
- <NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">Implicit feedback</NoteLink> treats every item not taken next as a
  negative. An item the user would have liked but did not reach is counted against, whichever loss is used.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading BCE scores as probabilities.** A model trained with BCE and sampled negatives is overconfident by
  construction. Its outputs can be recalibrated by $\operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} q + \log\alpha$ when
  the sampling was uniform.
- **Comparing sampled softmax runs without the correction.** In-batch sampled softmax without
  <Gloss name="logq-correction">logQ correction</Gloss> learns scores shifted by $-\log Q(j)$, which demotes popular
  items <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />.
- **Adding negatives to an averaged pairwise loss.** With BPR or TOP1 averaged over negatives, more negatives dilute the
  gradient; use a max-type or softmax loss instead <Cite id="hidasi2018topk" />.
- **Reporting sampled metrics.** Rank against the full catalogue, or correct the sampled metric, before comparing models
  <Cite id="krichene2020sampled" />.
