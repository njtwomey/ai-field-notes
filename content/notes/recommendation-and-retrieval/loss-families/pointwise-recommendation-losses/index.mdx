<Definition>
A **pointwise recommendation loss** is a sum of per-example losses $\sum_{(u, i)} \ell(s_{ui}, y_{ui})$, where
$s_{ui} = f(u, i)$ is the model's score for user $u$ and item $i$ and $y_{ui}$ is the observed label: a rating, a click,
a purchase. Each term depends on one score and one label, and on nothing else in the list the item is shown in. The
common choices are squared error on ratings, <Gloss name="bce">binary cross-entropy</Gloss> on binary events, and
confidence-weighted squared error on implicit feedback <Cite id="liu2009ltr" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A pointwise loss turns recommendation into ordinary regression or classification. Every logged impression, rating or
interaction is one training row. The model is asked for the right number on each row, not for the right order among
rows. A model that gets every number right also gets every order right. A model with finite capacity must trade errors
between rows, and a pointwise loss makes that trade without regard to which rows end up at the top of a list.

The number a pointwise loss asks for has a meaning of its own. Binary cross-entropy is minimised when the predicted
probability equals the true click probability. That property, calibration, is what auctions, bid pricing and the
blending of several predicted events need. It is the main reason <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rate
(CTR)</Gloss> models in production are trained pointwise even when the product shows ranked lists.

## Squared error on ratings

For explicit ratings $r_{ui}$, such as 1 to 5 stars, the loss is $\ell = \tfrac12 (s_{ui} - r_{ui})^2$. Its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with respect to the score is the residual:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial s_{ui}} = s_{ui} - r_{ui}.
$$

For <NoteLink to="matrix-factorisation-for-recommendation">matrix factorisation</NoteLink> with
$s_{ui} = \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i$, the <NoteLink to="derivative">chain rule</NoteLink> gives
$\partial\ell/\partial\wvec_u = (s_{ui} - r_{ui})\,\vvec_i$ and
$\partial\ell/\partial\vvec_i = (s_{ui} - r_{ui})\,\wvec_u$. This is the objective of the
<NoteLink to="netflix-prize">Netflix Prize</NoteLink> era, summed over the observed ratings only
<Cite id="koren2009mf" />.

The expected loss $\expect[\tfrac12(s - r)^2 \mid u, i]$ is minimised at $s = \expect[r \mid u, i]$,
so the model estimates the mean rating.

The loss only sees observed cells. Users rate what they chose to consume, so the observed ratings are a biased sample,
and a model fit to them says nothing about items the user never considered (see
<NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">explicit and implicit feedback</NoteLink>).

## Binary cross-entropy on clicks and purchases

For a binary label $y \in \set{0, 1}$ (click or no click, purchase or no purchase) and predicted probability
$q = \sigma(s)$, with $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$, the loss is the <NoteLink to="cross-entropy-and-perplexity">negative
log-likelihood</NoteLink> of a Bernoulli label:

$$
\ell(s, y) = -y\ln\sigma(s) - (1 - y)\ln\paren{1 - \sigma(s)} = \ln\paren{1 + e^{s}} - y\,s.
$$

The second form uses $\ln\sigma(s) = s - \ln(1 + e^s)$ and $\ln(1 - \sigma(s)) = -\ln(1 + e^s)$. Since
$\tfrac{d}{ds}\ln(1 + e^s) = \sigma(s)$, the gradient is

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial s} = \sigma(s) - y.
$$

The gradient is bounded in $(-1, 1)$, pushes a clicked item up by $1 - q$ and an unclicked item down by $q$, and never
refers to another item's score.

**The minimiser is the click probability.** Let $p = P(y = 1 \mid u, i)$ be the true probability for one context. The
expected loss is $\expect[\ell] = \ln(1 + e^s) - p\,s$, whose derivative $\sigma(s) - p$ vanishes only at
$\sigma(s) = p$. The expected loss is convex in $s$, so this is the global minimum. Binary cross-entropy is a strictly
<NoteLink to="proper-scoring-rule">proper scoring rule</NoteLink>: a model that minimises it over a rich enough class
outputs calibrated probabilities. Squared error on $\sigma(s)$ is also proper, with the same minimiser, but its gradient
$(\sigma(s) - y)\,\sigma(s)(1 - \sigma(s))$ vanishes when a confident prediction is wrong, so
<NoteLink to="click-models">click models</NoteLink> use cross-entropy.

## Implicit feedback: confidence weights

Implicit data record events, not dislikes. An absent interaction may mean the user never saw the item. Two pointwise
responses exist. Treating every missing cell as a zero with full weight teaches the model that unseen means disliked.
Dropping the missing cells leaves only positives, and a model fit to them predicts 1 everywhere
<Cite id="pan2008occf" />.

**Weighted matrix factorisation** sits between the two <Cite id="hu2008implicit" />. Each cell has a binary preference
$p_{ui} = \indicator[r_{ui} > 0]$, where $r_{ui}$ counts interactions, and a confidence $c_{ui} = 1 + \alpha r_{ui}$.
The loss over all $U \times M$ cells is

$$
L = \sum_{u, i} c_{ui}\paren{p_{ui} - \wvec_u\transpose\vvec_i}^2 + \lambda\paren{\sum_u\norm{\wvec_u}^2 + \sum_i\norm{\vvec_i}^2},
$$

with gradient $\partial L/\partial s_{ui} = 2c_{ui}(s_{ui} - p_{ui})$ per cell. Hu, Koren and Volinsky fit it by
weighted <Gloss name="als" />, which handles the $UM$ terms in time linear in the number of observed interactions (see
<NoteLink to="weighted-matrix-factorisation">weighted matrix factorisation</NoteLink>). Pan et al. proposed the same
weighting and, as an alternative, sampling a subset of missing cells as negatives <Cite id="pan2008occf" />.

**What the weighted score estimates.** Suppose a user–item context is positive with probability $p$, positives carry
confidence $c$ and negatives confidence 1. The expected loss $p\,c\,(1 - s)^2 + (1 - p)\,s^2$ has derivative
$-2pc(1 - s) + 2(1 - p)s$, which vanishes at

$$
s^\star = \frac{c\,p}{c\,p + 1 - p}, \qquad \frac{s^\star}{1 - s^\star} = c\,\frac{p}{1 - p}.
$$

The optimal score is a probability whose odds are inflated by the confidence $c$. Weighting positives by $c$ is
equivalent, at the optimum, to keeping only a fraction $1/c$ of the negatives. The scores rank correctly within a
<NoteLink to="confidence-intervals">confidence level</NoteLink> but are not click probabilities.

## Negative sampling for pointwise training

A catalogue of $M$ items and $U$ users has $UM$ cells, almost all negative. Pointwise training on implicit data
therefore samples negatives. For each positive $(u, i)$, draw $m$ items $j$ from a proposal distribution $Q$ (uniform,
or proportional to popularity) and train binary cross-entropy with label 1 on $(u, i)$ and 0 on each $(u, j)$. This is
the negative-sampling objective of word2vec applied to users and items <Cite id="mikolov2013b" />.

**What the sampled model learns.** Let $P(i \mid u)$ be the probability that $u$'s next interaction is $i$. The pair
$(u, i)$ appears as a positive at rate $P(i \mid u)$ and as a sampled negative at rate $m\,Q(i)$. The binary
cross-entropy optimum derived above sets $\sigma(s_{ui})$ to the fraction of appearances that are positive:

$$
\sigma(s^\star_{ui}) = \frac{P(i \mid u)}{P(i \mid u) + m\,Q(i)}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
s^\star_{ui} = \ln P(i \mid u) - \ln Q(i) - \ln m.
$$

The score is a log ratio of the data to the proposal, not a click probability. For word2vec with $Q$ the unigram
distribution it is the shifted <Gloss name="pmi" /> of Levy and Goldberg <Cite id="levy2014" />. With a popularity
proposal, the term $-\ln Q(i)$ lowers the scores of popular items relative to $\ln P(i \mid u)$; ranking by
$s^\star_{ui} + \ln Q(i)$ recovers the order of $P(i \mid u)$. The same correction appears for the
<NoteLink to="two-tower-model">sampled softmax</NoteLink> as the <Gloss name="logq-correction">logQ correction</Gloss>
(see <NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">sampled softmax and negative sampling</NoteLink>).

## Negative subsampling and recalibration

Click logs have the opposite problem: every impression is labelled, but clicks are rare. Advertising systems keep all
clicks and a random fraction $w$ of non-clicks to cut training cost <Cite id="he2014facebook" />. Training on the
subsample changes the class prior, and the model learns the wrong probability.

**The distortion.** Let $p = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec)$ on the full data. After subsampling, a click is always kept and a
non-click is kept with probability $w$. By <NoteLink to="bayes-theorem">Bayes' rule</NoteLink>, the probability of a
click among the kept examples with features $\xvec$ is

$$
q = P(y = 1 \mid \xvec, \text{kept}) = \frac{p}{p + w(1 - p)}, \qquad \frac{q}{1 - q} = \frac{1}{w}\cdot\frac{p}{1 - p}.
$$

A model trained with binary cross-entropy on the subsample converges to $q$. Its odds are too high by the factor
$1/w$, and its logit by $-\ln w$.

**The correction.** Solving the odds equation for $p$ gives

$$
p = \frac{q}{q + (1 - q)/w}, \qquad \operatorname{logit} p = \operatorname{logit} q + \ln w.
$$

This is the recalibration used for Facebook's ad click model <Cite id="he2014facebook" />, and the prior correction of
King and Zeng for rare-event <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> <Cite id="king2001" />.
For a model whose last layer is a logit it is a constant added to the bias. The correction needs no held-out data, but
it assumes the subsampled model is otherwise calibrated (see <NoteLink to="calibration-under-imbalance">calibration
under imbalance</NoteLink>).

**Importance weighting instead.** Giving each kept non-click weight $1/w$ makes the expected weighted loss equal the
full-data loss, so the minimiser is $p$ directly. Google's ad click system subsampled queries without clicks and
reweighted them this way <Cite id="mcmahan2013adclick" />. The weights raise the variance of the gradient; the analytic
correction keeps unit weights and shifts the bias afterwards.

## Class imbalance and focal loss

With click rates near 1%, most gradient comes from easy negatives with $q \approx 0$, each contributing $q$. **Focal
loss** multiplies cross-entropy by $(1 - q_t)^\gamma$, where $q_t$ is the predicted probability of the observed label
and $\gamma \ge 0$ <Cite id="lin2017" />:

$$
\ell_{\text{focal}} = -(1 - q_t)^\gamma \ln q_t.
$$

At $q_t = 0.9$ and $\gamma = 2$ the loss is scaled by $0.01$, so confident examples fall out of the gradient. The cost
is calibration: the minimiser of the expected focal loss is not $q = p$, and focal-loss models must be recalibrated
before their outputs are read as probabilities <Cite id="charoenphakdee2021" />. For CTR prediction, where calibrated
output is the point of the pointwise model, negative subsampling followed by the analytic correction is the usual
remedy for imbalance instead (see <NoteLink to="focal-loss">focal loss</NoteLink>).

<Derivation title="Gradient of the focal loss">
Take $y = 1$ with $q = \sigma(s)$, so $q_t = q$ and $\ell = -(1 - q)^\gamma\ln q$. With $dq/ds = q(1 - q)$,

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial s} = q(1 - q)\brack{\gamma(1 - q)^{\gamma - 1}\ln q - \frac{(1 - q)^\gamma}{q}} = (1 - q)^\gamma\brack{\gamma\,q\ln q - (1 - q)}.
$$

At $\gamma = 0$ this is $q - 1$, the <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">cross-entropy gradient</NoteLink>. For
$\gamma > 0$ the factor $(1 - q)^\gamma$ shrinks the gradient on well-classified examples ($q$ near 1) faster than
cross-entropy does.
</Derivation>

## Multi-task pointwise heads

Commercial feeds predict several events per impression: click, add to cart, purchase, dwell. Each has its own pointwise
head, and the final ranking score combines them, for example expected revenue $b \cdot P(\text{click}) \cdot
P(\text{purchase} \mid \text{click})$ for a bid or price $b$. A product of predicted probabilities is only meaningful
if each factor is calibrated, which is why each head keeps a proper loss (see
<NoteLink to="multi-behaviour-recommendation">multi-behaviour recommendation</NoteLink>).

Post-click <Gloss name="cvr">conversion rate (CVR)</Gloss> is observed only on clicked impressions. A CVR model trained
on clicks alone is fit to a biased sample and applied to every impression at serving time. The
**<Gloss name="esmm">entire space multi-task model</Gloss>** (ESMM) <Cite id="ma2018esmm" /> avoids this. It models
$P(\text{click}) = \sigma(s_{\text{ctr}})$ and $P(\text{conv} \mid \text{click}) = \sigma(s_{\text{cvr}})$, and trains
two cross-entropy losses over all impressions: one on the click label with $P(\text{click})$, and one on the label
"clicked and converted" with the product $P(\text{click})\,P(\text{conv} \mid \text{click})$. The CVR head never sees a
loss of its own. It is learnt through the product, on the same impressions as the CTR head.

## Relation to ranking metrics

A pointwise loss can be consistent for ranking. The probability ranking principle states that ordering items by their
probability of relevance is optimal for retrieval effectiveness <Cite id="robertson1977prp" />. For expected
<Gloss name="dcg" /> with binary relevance, the expected DCG of an order is
$\sum_i p_i\,d_{\rho_i}$, where $p_i$ is item $i$'s relevance probability and $d_\rho = 1/\log_2(1 + \rho)$ the discount
at rank $\rho$. The discounts decrease with rank, so the rearrangement inequality puts the largest $p_i$ at the largest
discount: sorting by $p_i$ is optimal. A model that outputs the true click probabilities, sorted by score, produces that
order.

Two things break this in practice. First, the gradient $\sigma(s_{ui}) - y_{ui}$ is the same at rank 1 and rank 500, so
a model with limited capacity spends as much effort on the bottom of a list as on the top, where <Gloss name="ndcg" />
is decided. Second, the loss rewards fitting offsets that ranking ignores. A user who clicks 10% of impressions and a
user who clicks 1% need different intercepts; a ranking within each user's list is unaffected by either intercept, but
the pointwise loss penalises an error in either intercept. Pairwise and listwise losses remove the offset by depending
only on score differences within a list (see <NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">pairwise ranking losses</NoteLink>
and <NoteLink to="listwise-ranking-losses">listwise ranking losses</NoteLink>). The two are often combined: a ranker
trained with a listwise loss plus a pointwise cross-entropy term keeps calibration and gains order.

## Worked example

**Ratings.** A user rated items A, B and C with $4$, $2$ and $5$ stars. The model predicts $3.5$, $3.0$ and $4.0$. The
predicted order C, A, B is the true order, so every ranking metric is perfect. The squared-error loss is
$\tfrac12(0.25 + 1 + 1) = 1.125$, and the gradients $s - r = (-0.5, 1.0, -1.0)$ still move all three scores. The loss
spends updates on a list that is already ranked correctly.

**Clicks.** Three items with scores $s = (1.0, -0.5, 0.2)$ are shown; only the second is clicked. Then
$\sigma(s) = (0.731, 0.378, 0.550)$, the loss is $1.313 + 0.974 + 0.798 = 3.085$, and the gradients are
$\sigma(s) - y = (0.731, -0.622, 0.550)$. A pairwise logistic loss over the pairs (2, 1) and (2, 3) instead gives
gradients $(0.818, -1.486, 0.668)$: the clicked item is pushed up by the sum of its two pair terms, and each unclicked
item is pushed down by how far it outscores the clicked one.

**Recalibration.** The true CTR is 1% and non-clicks are kept with $w = 0.1$. Among 1,000 impressions, 10 clicks and
$99$ of the $990$ non-clicks are kept, so the training click rate is $10/109 = 9.17\%$. A calibrated model on the
subsample predicts $q = 0.0917$. The correction gives $p = 0.0917 / (0.0917 + 0.9083/0.1) = 0.0100$. In logits,
$\operatorname{logit} q = -2.293$ and $\ln w = -2.303$, so $\operatorname{logit} p = -4.595 = \operatorname{logit}
0.01$.

**Confidence weights.** A user–item context is positive with probability $p = 0.02$, and a
single play gives confidence $c = 1 + 40 \times 1 = 41$. The optimal weighted matrix factorisation (WMF) score is
$41 \times 0.02 / (41 \times 0.02 + 0.98) = 0.456$, about 23 times the probability.

## Assumptions

- **Labels are conditionally independent given the features.** Each row's loss ignores what else was shown. Position
  and presentation effects, which make a click depend on the rest of the list, must enter as features or be removed
  with <NoteLink to="unbiased-learning-to-rank">unbiased learning to rank</NoteLink>.
- **The recalibration assumes random subsampling.** The keep decision must be independent of the features given the
  label. Subsampling non-clicks more heavily for some users or items needs a per-stratum correction.
- **Calibration holds on the training distribution.** A model calibrated on logged impressions is calibrated on the
  items the old system chose to show, not on the whole catalogue.

## Pitfalls

- **Reading sampled scores as probabilities.** A model trained with $m$ sampled negatives per positive outputs
  $\sigma(s) = P/(P + mQ)$. Changing $m$ or $Q$ shifts every prediction. The scores rank but must not be fed to an
  auction or blended with calibrated heads.
- **Forgetting the correction after subsampling.** An uncorrected model overestimates CTR by up to $1/w$. The ranking is
  unchanged, so offline <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">ranking metrics</NoteLink> do not reveal the error;
  calibration metrics such as <NoteLink to="log-loss-and-brier-score">log loss</NoteLink> or the ratio of predicted to
  observed clicks do.
- **Weighted squared error on binary labels.** The optimal WMF score $cp/(cp + 1 - p)$ has its odds inflated by the
  confidence $c$, and the low-rank dot product that approximates it is unbounded, so fitted scores can fall outside
  $[0, 1]$. They are ranking scores, not probabilities.
- **Squared error on ratings for top-$k$ recommendation.** Low rating error does not imply good top-$k$ lists. The
  Netflix Prize's <Gloss name="rmse" /> rewarded accuracy on rated items, while users see only the items ranked first.
