<Definition>
**Lambda gradients** are per-item training signals for a ranker that stand in for the gradient of a ranking metric. For
a pair of items $(i, j)$ in one query with item $i$ more relevant and score difference $d_{ij} = s_i - s_j$, the lambda
is the <Gloss name="ranknet" /> pair gradient multiplied by the absolute change $\abs{\Delta Z_{ij}}$ in the metric $Z$
caused by swapping the ranks of $i$ and $j$:

$$
\lambda_{ij} = -\sigma(-d_{ij})\,\abs{\Delta Z_{ij}}, \qquad \lambda_i = \sum_{j:\, i \succ j}\lambda_{ij} - \sum_{j:\, j \succ i}\lambda_{ji},
$$

with $\sigma$ the logistic function and $i \succ j$ meaning $i$ is labelled more relevant than $j$. **LambdaRank**
trains a neural network by using $\lambda_i$ as $\partial C/\partial s_i$ <Cite id="burges2006" />; **LambdaMART**
fits gradient-boosted regression trees to them <Cite id="wu2010lambdamart" />.
</Definition>

## Problem

Ranking systems are judged by metrics such as <Gloss name="ndcg" />, <Gloss name="mean-average-precision" />,
<Gloss name="mrr" /> and <Gloss name="err-metric" />. Each depends on the scores only through their sort order. For a
query with items $i = 1, \dots, n$, graded labels $r_i$, gains $g_i = 2^{r_i} - 1$ and ranks $\rho_i$ (1 for the top),

$$
\operatorname{NDCG} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{IDCG}}\sum_{i = 1}^n g_i\,D(\rho_i), \qquad D(\rho) = \frac{1}{\log_2(1 + \rho)},
$$

where IDCG is the <Gloss name="dcg" /> of the ideal order <Cite id="jarvelin2002" />. A change in the scores small
enough not to swap two items leaves every $\rho_i$, and so NDCG, unchanged. NDCG is piecewise constant in the scores.
Its gradient is zero almost everywhere and undefined where two scores tie, so <NoteLink to="gradient-descent">gradient
descent</NoteLink> on NDCG does not move.

A smooth pairwise surrogate such as RankNet's logistic loss has useful gradients but is blind to position: a misordered
pair at ranks 1 and 2 costs the same as one at ranks 50 and 51 with the same score difference (see
<NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">pairwise ranking losses</NoteLink>). Swapping the first pair changes the metric
far more. Lambda gradients keep RankNet's smooth dependence on the scores and borrow the metric's dependence on the
ranks.

## Mechanism

### The change in NDCG from a swap

DCG is a sum of one term per item, $g_k D(\rho_k)$. Swapping the ranks of items $i$ and $j$ gives item $i$ the
discount $D(\rho_j)$ and item $j$ the discount $D(\rho_i)$, and changes no other term. The change is

$$
\Delta\operatorname{DCG}_{ij} = g_i D(\rho_j) + g_j D(\rho_i) - g_i D(\rho_i) - g_j D(\rho_j) = (g_i - g_j)\paren{D(\rho_j) - D(\rho_i)}.
$$

Dividing by IDCG, which does not depend on the order,

$$
\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}} = \frac{\abs{g_i - g_j}\,\abs{D(\rho_i) - D(\rho_j)}}{\operatorname{IDCG}}.
$$

The weight is a product of a label factor and a position factor. Pairs with equal labels get weight zero. Pairs whose
labels differ a lot, and pairs that straddle the top of the list where $D$ falls steeply, get large weight. For binary
labels, a swap of ranks 1 and 2 has position factor $1 - 1/\log_2 3 = 0.369$; a swap of ranks 50 and 51 has
$0.00087$.

### The lambda for a pair

RankNet's loss for a pair with $i \succ j$ is $C_{ij} = \ln(1 + e^{-d_{ij}})$, the cross-entropy of the event "$i$ above
$j$" under $P_{ij} = \sigma(d_{ij})$ <Cite id="burges2005" />. Its <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> is

$$
\frac{\partial C_{ij}}{\partial s_i} = \frac{-e^{-d_{ij}}}{1 + e^{-d_{ij}}} = -\sigma(-d_{ij}), \qquad \frac{\partial C_{ij}}{\partial s_j} = \sigma(-d_{ij}).
$$

The pair's gradient is a force of size $\sigma(-d_{ij}) \in (0, 1)$ that pushes $s_i$ up and $s_j$ down: close to 1 when
the pair is badly misordered, close to 0 when it is ordered by a wide margin.

LambdaRank multiplies that force by the swap's effect on the metric <Cite id="burges2006" />:

$$
\lambda_{ij} = \frac{\partial C_{ij}}{\partial s_i}\,\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}} = -\sigma(-d_{ij})\,\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}.
$$

The per-item lambda $\lambda_i$ sums the pairs in which item $i$ takes part, with sign $+$ when $i$ is the more
relevant item and $-$ when it is the less relevant one. The model then takes the step it would take if $\lambda_i$ were
$\partial C/\partial s_i$ for a cost $C$: for a network with weights $\wvec$,

$$
\wvec \leftarrow \wvec - \eta\sum_i \lambda_i\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial\wvec},
$$

with step size $\eta$. The ranks $\rho_i$ in $\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$ are recomputed by sorting the
current scores at every step. A scale parameter on $d_{ij}$, often written $\sigma$ in the LambdaRank papers, is
absorbed into the scores here.

### Empirical test of local optimality

LambdaRank was defined by its gradients, not by a loss. Burges et al. tested it empirically. After training, they moved
the weights a small distance in <NoteLink to="sampling-a-disk-or-sphere">random directions</NoteLink> and measured mean
NDCG on the training set. If NDCG decreases in every one of $n$ random directions, then with confidence $1 - \delta$ the
fraction of ascent directions is below $p_0$ once $n \ge \log\delta/\log(1 - p_0)$; with $\delta = p_0 = 0.01$ this
needs 459 directions <Cite id="burges2010" />. Donmez et al. ran this test for NDCG, MAP and MRR and found no ascent
direction for any of them, so with 99% confidence fewer than 1% of directions increase each metric at the trained
weights <Cite id="donmez2009lambdarank" />. The test bounds the share of ascent directions; it does not prove a local
optimum.

### The implied loss

At fixed ranks, the lambdas are the gradient of

$$
C = \sum_{i \succ j}\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}\ln(1 + e^{-d_{ij}}),
$$

a RankNet loss with pair weights. The weights depend on the ranks, which depend on the scores, so this $C$ changes
whenever two scores cross. Whether a single function has the lambdas as its gradient everywhere was an open question
<Cite id="burges2010" />.

The **LambdaLoss** framework answers it <Cite id="wang2018lambdaloss" />. It treats the ranked list $\pi$ as a hidden
variable and defines the loss of a query with labels $\yvec$ as the negative log-likelihood of a mixture,

$$
\ell(\yvec, \svec) = -\log_2\sum_\pi P(\yvec \mid \svec, \pi)\,P(\pi \mid \svec).
$$

Take $P(\pi \mid \svec)$ to put all its mass on the list sorted by score, and let each pair's likelihood depend on
the ranks in $\pi$ through an exponent,

$$
P(i \succ j \mid s_i, s_j, \pi) = \sigma(d_{ij})^{\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}}.
$$

Minimising this loss by the <Gloss name="em-algorithm" /> algorithm alternates two steps. The E-step sorts the items by
the current scores. The M-step minimises
$-\sum_{i \succ j}\log_2\sigma(d_{ij})^{\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}} = \sum_{i \succ j}\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}\log_2(1 + e^{-d_{ij}})$
with the ranks held fixed, which is LambdaRank's weighted RankNet loss. LambdaRank is therefore EM on a well-defined
loss, and LambdaMART's practice of adding one tree per round to the previous model is a warm-started M-step
<Cite id="wang2018lambdaloss" />.

The framework also shows how tightly the loss tracks NDCG. Wang et al. proved that the LambdaRank loss upper-bounds a
metric that is itself at least the NDCG cost $1 - \text{NDCG}$, so LambdaRank minimises a coarse upper bound of
$1 - \text{NDCG}$. They derived a tighter bound, NDCG-Loss2, which replaces the position factor
$\abs{D(\rho_i) - D(\rho_j)}$ by
$\delta_{ij} = \abs{D(\abs{\rho_i - \rho_j}) - D(\abs{\rho_i - \rho_j} + 1)}$, a factor that depends on how far apart
the two items sit rather than on where they sit <Cite id="wang2018lambdaloss" />.

<Derivation title="Why NDCG-Loss2 bounds the NDCG cost">
Write $G_k = g_k/\operatorname{IDCG}$ for the normalised gain and label the items by their current ranks, so item $k$
has rank $k$ and $D_k = D(k)$. Define the NDCG cost here as the shortfall from the constant $\sum_k G_k$. It differs
from $1 - \text{NDCG}$ by the constant $\sum_k G_k - 1$, so it has the same gradient and the same minimisers:

$$
\text{NDCG}_\text{cost} = \sum_k G_k - \text{NDCG} = \sum_k G_k\paren{1 - D_k}.
$$

The factor $1 - D_k$ telescopes over the gaps to the items above $k$. With
$\delta_{kl} = \abs{D(\abs{k - l}) - D(\abs{k - l} + 1)}$ and $D(1) = 1$,

$$
\sum_{l = 1}^{k - 1}\delta_{kl} = \sum_{m = 1}^{k - 1}\paren{D(m) - D(m + 1)} = D(1) - D(k) = 1 - D_k.
$$

Item $l$ sits above item $k$ exactly when $s_l > s_k$, so

$$
\text{NDCG}_\text{cost} = \sum_k G_k\sum_l \delta_{kl}\,\indicator[s_k < s_l].
$$

Group the terms by unordered pair. For a pair with $y_k > y_l$ and no tie in scores, exactly one of the two indicators
is 1, so the pair contributes
$\delta_{kl}\paren{G_k\indicator[s_k < s_l] + G_l\indicator[s_l < s_k]} = \delta_{kl}G_l + \delta_{kl}(G_k - G_l)\indicator[s_k < s_l]$.
Pairs with equal labels contribute a term that does not depend on which of the two is on top. At fixed ranks the
$\delta_{kl}$ are constants, so

$$
\text{NDCG}_\text{cost} = \sum_{y_k > y_l}\delta_{kl}\abs{G_k - G_l}\,\indicator[s_k < s_l] + \text{const}
\le \sum_{y_k > y_l}\delta_{kl}\abs{G_k - G_l}\log_2(1 + e^{-(s_k - s_l)}) + \text{const},
$$

using $\indicator[d < 0] \le \log_2(1 + e^{-d})$. The right side is NDCG-Loss2. Replacing $\delta_{kl}$ by
$\rho_{kl} = \abs{D_k - D_l}$ gives LambdaRank. Since
$\sum_{l < k}\rho_{kl} \ge \rho_{k1} = 1 - D_k = \sum_{l < k}\delta_{kl}$, the LambdaRank weights sum to more than the
NDCG cost needs, which is why its bound is the coarser one <Cite id="wang2018lambdaloss" />.
</Derivation>

### LambdaMART

<NoteLink to="gradient-boosting">Gradient boosting</NoteLink> with regression trees (MART) builds a model
$F(\xvec) = \sum_m f_m(\xvec)$ one tree at a time, fitting each tree to the negative gradient of the loss at the
current scores. MART never needs the loss itself, only its first and second derivatives with respect to each score.
Lambda gradients supply exactly that, so LambdaMART is MART with $-\lambda_i$ as the regression targets
<Cite id="wu2010lambdamart" />.

Each tree's leaf values come from a <NoteLink to="newtons-method">Newton step</NoteLink>. With
$\pi_{ij} = \sigma(-d_{ij})$, the second derivative of the implied cost at fixed ranks is

$$
w_i = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial s_i^2} = \sum_{j \ne i,\, r_j \ne r_i}\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}\,\pi_{ij}(1 - \pi_{ij}),
$$

using $\tfrac{d}{dd}\sigma(-d) = -\sigma(-d)\paren{1 - \sigma(-d)}$. The value of leaf $R$ is
$\gamma_R = -\sum_{i \in R}\lambda_i \big/ \sum_{i \in R}w_i$, shrunk by a learning rate <Cite id="burges2010" />.
Per pair, the gradient is proportional to $\pi_{ij}$ and the curvature to $\pi_{ij}(1 - \pi_{ij})$, so their ratio is
$1/(1 - \pi_{ij})$. It is near 1 for a confidently ordered pair ($\pi_{ij}$ near 0) and large for a confidently
misordered pair ($\pi_{ij}$ near 1), whose gradient stays large while its curvature vanishes. Leaves dominated by
misordered pairs therefore take the largest steps.

An ensemble of LambdaMART rankers won Track 1 of the 2010 Yahoo! <Gloss name="ltr">Learning to Rank</Gloss> Challenge
<Cite id="burges2010" />.

### Other metrics

The construction needs only the metric change from a swap, so it transfers to other metrics by replacing
$\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$ <Cite id="donmez2009lambdarank" />.

- **NDCG@$k$.** Positions below $k$ have discount 0. A pair with both items below $k$ has weight 0 and gives no
  gradient; a pair straddling position $k$ gets the full discount of the upper item.
- **Reciprocal rank (MRR).** With binary labels, reciprocal rank is $1/\rho^\ast$, where $\rho^\ast$ is the rank of the
  first relevant item. A swap changes it only if it changes $\rho^\ast$, so every pair entirely below the first relevant
  item has weight 0.
- **<Gloss name="ap">Average precision</Gloss> (MAP).** A swap between ranks $\rho_i < \rho_j$ changes the precision at
  every relevant item between them, so $\Delta\text{AP}_{ij}$ depends on the items in between.
- **ERR.** Expected reciprocal rank models a user who reads down the list and is satisfied by item $k$, once reached,
  with probability $R_k = (2^{r_k} - 1)/2^{r_{\max}}$ <Cite id="chapelle2009err" />. The change from a swap also depends
  on every item between the two. Burges showed that all the $\Delta\text{ERR}_{ij}$ of a query can still be computed in
  $O(n^2)$ with cumulative products and partial sums, and that $\Delta\text{ERR}_{ij} \ge 0$ whenever the more relevant
  item moves up <Cite id="burges2010" />.

## Worked example

One query has four items, sorted by score:

| Item | Score $s$ | Label $r$ | Gain $g$ | Rank $\rho$ | Discount $D(\rho)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 1.5 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1.000 |
| B | 1.0 | 2 | 3 | 2 | 0.631 |
| C | 0.2 | 1 | 1 | 3 | 0.500 |
| D | $-0.5$ | 0 | 0 | 4 | 0.431 |

$\operatorname{DCG} = 3(0.631) + 1(0.5) = 2.393$. The ideal order B, C, A, D gives
$\operatorname{IDCG} = 3 + 1/\log_2 3 = 3.631$, so $\operatorname{NDCG} = 0.659$. The pairs with different labels are:

| Pair ($i \succ j$) | $d_{ij}$ | RankNet push $\sigma(-d_{ij})$ | $\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$ | Lambda push $\sigma(-d_{ij})\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B, A | $-0.5$ | 0.622 | 0.305 | 0.190 |
| B, C | $0.8$ | 0.310 | 0.072 | 0.022 |
| B, D | $1.5$ | 0.182 | 0.166 | 0.030 |
| C, A | $-1.3$ | 0.786 | 0.138 | 0.108 |
| C, D | $0.7$ | 0.332 | 0.019 | 0.006 |

Check one weight: swapping B and A puts B at rank 1 and A at rank 2, so DCG becomes $3 + 0.5 = 3.5$ and NDCG
$0.964$, a change of $0.305$. By the formula,
$\abs{3 - 0}\cdot\abs{1 - 0.631}/3.631 = 0.305$.

Summing each item's pushes gives $-\lambda_i$, the direction a gradient step moves its score:

| Item | RankNet $-\partial C/\partial s$ | LambdaRank $-\lambda$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | $-1.408$ | $-0.298$ |
| B | $+1.115$ | $+0.242$ |
| C | $+0.808$ | $+0.092$ |
| D | $-0.514$ | $-0.037$ |

RankNet pushes C up with 72% of the force it applies to B. LambdaRank pushes C with 38% of B's force. The pair
(C, A) is RankNet's most misordered pair ($d = -1.3$), but lifting C above A gains less NDCG than lifting B above A,
because B has three times C's gain. LambdaRank also nearly ignores the pair (C, D) at ranks 3 and 4, whose swap changes
NDCG by only 0.019.

**Newton weights.** The LambdaMART curvatures are $w = (0.095, 0.112, 0.043, 0.029)$ for A to D. A tree leaf holding
only item B would take the value $0.242/0.112 = 2.17$ before shrinkage.

**Reciprocal rank.** With binary labels and relevant items at ranks 3 and 5 of five, reciprocal rank is $1/3$. Swapping
the item at rank 3 with the one at rank 1 raises it to 1 ($\Delta = 0.667$); with the one at rank 2, to $1/2$
($\Delta = 0.167$). Swapping the relevant item at rank 5 with the irrelevant one at rank 4 leaves the first relevant
item at rank 3, so $\Delta = 0$ and that pair gets no lambda under ΔMRR, although RankNet still pushes it.

## Cost

A query with $n$ items needs one sort, $O(n \log n)$, and one $\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$ per pair with
different labels, $O(n^2)$ in the worst case. Each $\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$ is $O(1)$ once the ranks and
IDCG are known. $\Delta\text{AP}$ and $\Delta\text{ERR}$ need prefix sums to stay $O(n^2)$. The model still takes one
backward pass per item, because the pair terms are summed into $\lambda_i$ before back-propagation
<Cite id="burges2010" />. LambdaMART adds no cost to MART beyond computing the lambdas and curvatures at each round.

## Trade-offs

- **Metric alignment against smoothness.** The weights change discontinuously when two scores cross, so the effective
  objective shifts during training. LambdaLoss casts the procedure as EM on a fixed loss, and a full M-step never
  increases that loss. LambdaRank and LambdaMART take one gradient step or add one tree per round instead of a full
  M-step, and the loss is not convex, so neither is guaranteed to converge to an optimum.
- **Dependence on the current ranking.** Early in training the ranking is poor, so the $\abs{\Delta Z}$ weights target
  the wrong positions until the ranking improves.
- **Truncated metrics starve the tail.** Under NDCG@$k$ the items that sit well below $k$ receive no gradient from
  pairs among themselves, so a relevant item that starts deep in the list may never rise.
- **Graded labels help most.** With one relevant item per list, $\abs{g_i - g_j}$ is the same for every pair and the
  weight reduces to the position factor, so lambdas only reweight by rank.
- **The metric is chosen at training time.** Lambdas for NDCG optimise NDCG; a system evaluated by MRR or ERR needs
  the matching $\Delta Z$.
