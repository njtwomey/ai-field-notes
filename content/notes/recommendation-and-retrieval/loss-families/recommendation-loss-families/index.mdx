<Definition>
A recommender or ranker learns a **scoring function** $s = f(u, i)$ for a user or query $u$ and an item $i$, and shows
items in decreasing order of score. Its **training loss** is a differentiable function of the scores that stands in for
what the product needs: good items near the top of each user's list. Loss families differ in the unit they score. A
**pointwise** loss scores one item, a **pairwise** loss a pair of items, a **listwise** loss a whole list, and
**retrieval**, **sequential** and **generative** losses a softmax over the catalogue or over item tokens
<Cite id="liu2009ltr" />.
</Definition>

## The problem: an order, learnt from feedback

The quality of a recommendation list is measured by ranking metrics such as <Gloss name="ndcg" />, recall at $k$ and
<Gloss name="mrr" />. Each depends on the scores only through the sort order. With binary relevance $y_i$ and rank
$\rho_i$ (1 for the top), $\operatorname{DCG} = \sum_i y_i / \log_2(1 + \rho_i)$. A change in the scores small enough
not to swap two items leaves every $\rho_i$ unchanged, so the metric is piecewise constant in the scores. Its
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is zero almost everywhere and undefined at ties. Gradient training therefore
needs a **surrogate**: a smooth loss whose minimisation tends to improve the metric.

The surrogate is constrained by what the training data record. Each kind of signal suits some losses and not others.

| signal | example | what it says | natural loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| explicit ratings | 1 to 5 stars | a graded label on items the user chose to rate | squared error, or pairwise on rating differences |
| impressions with clicks | a logged feed with click flags | binary labels on shown items; position-biased | <Gloss name="bce">binary cross-entropy</Gloss>, or listwise per impression list |
| purchases, conversions | orders after a click | rare binary labels, observed only after a click | multi-task cross-entropy |
| interactions only | plays, views, saves | positives; everything else is unlabelled | pairwise with sampled negatives, <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">sampled softmax</NoteLink> |
| sequences | a session of item views | the next item given the past | next-item softmax over the catalogue |

Implicit data have no labelled negatives. An item the user did not interact with may be one they never saw (see
<NoteLink to="explicit-and-implicit-feedback">explicit and implicit feedback</NoteLink>). Every loss for implicit data
must decide which non-interacted items to treat as negatives and how much to trust them.

## Components

- <NoteLink to="pointwise-recommendation-losses">Pointwise recommendation losses</NoteLink>: squared error on ratings,
  binary cross-entropy on clicks, confidence weights, negative subsampling and its recalibration.
- <NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">Pairwise ranking losses</NoteLink>: hinge (<Gloss name="ranksvm" />), logistic
  (<Gloss name="ranknet" />, <Gloss name="bpr" />) and rank-weighted pairwise losses.
- <NoteLink to="lambda-gradients">Lambda gradients</NoteLink>: LambdaRank and <Gloss name="lambdamart" />, which specify
  metric-weighted gradients instead of a loss.
- <NoteLink to="listwise-ranking-losses">Listwise ranking losses</NoteLink>: softmax cross-entropy,
  <Gloss name="listnet" />, <Gloss name="listmle" /> and their relatives.
- <NoteLink to="ranking-surrogates-and-metrics">Ranking surrogates and metrics</NoteLink>: which surrogate bounds or is
  consistent for which metric.
- <NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">Sampled softmax and negative sampling</NoteLink>: training a
  catalogue-sized softmax from a few sampled items, with the <Gloss name="logq-correction">logQ correction</Gloss>. It
  is filed under losses, because language models train their vocabulary softmax the same way.
- <NoteLink to="contrastive-losses-for-retrieval">Contrastive losses for retrieval</NoteLink>: <Gloss name="infonce" />
  with in-batch negatives for two-tower models.
- <NoteLink to="sequential-recommendation-losses">Sequential recommendation losses</NoteLink>: next-item objectives,
  per-position losses and their negatives.
- <NoteLink to="generative-recommendation-losses">Generative recommendation losses</NoteLink>: token-level
  cross-entropy over semantic item identifiers.

## Where each family puts the gradient

Consider one list of $n$ items with scores $s_1, \dots, s_n$ and labels $y_1, \dots, y_n$. The families differ in how
the gradient $\partial L/\partial s_i$ on item $i$ depends on the rest of the list.

**Pointwise.** Binary cross-entropy $L = \sum_i \brack{\ln(1 + e^{s_i}) - y_i s_i}$ has gradient
$\partial L/\partial s_i = \sigma(s_i) - y_i$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function. It depends on item $i$ alone. An
irrelevant item already at the bottom of the list is pushed down as hard as one at the top.

**Pairwise.** The logistic pair loss $L = \sum_{(i, j): y_i > y_j} \ln\paren{1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)}}$ has gradient

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i} = -\sum_{j:\, y_j < y_i}\sigma(s_j - s_i) + \sum_{j:\, y_j > y_i}\sigma(s_i - s_j).
$$

Item $i$ is pushed up by each item it should beat, most strongly by those that outscore it, and down by each item that
should beat it. The gradient depends only on score differences, so adding a constant to every score changes nothing.

**Listwise softmax.** With $p_i = e^{s_i} / \sum_k e^{s_k}$ and targets $t_i = y_i / \sum_k y_k$, the loss
$L = -\sum_i t_i \ln p_i = -\sum_i t_i s_i + \ln\sum_k e^{s_k}$ has gradient

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i} = p_i - t_i,
$$

because the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of log-sum-exp is the softmax. Each irrelevant item is
pushed down in proportion to its share $p_i$ of the probability mass. Items with high scores, the ones at the top,
receive almost all of the push. Low-scored items receive almost none.

**Lambda gradients.** LambdaRank multiplies each pairwise term by $\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}_{ij}}$, the change in
NDCG from swapping $i$ and $j$ <Cite id="burges2006" />. Swaps near the top of the list change NDCG most, so the push
concentrates there even when the scores are close together.

### What each family bounds



**Pairwise losses bound the misordered-pair count.** For any margin $x$,
$\ln(1 + e^{-x}) \ge \ln 2 \cdot \indicator[x \le 0]$: the left side is at least $\ln 2$ when $x \le 0$ and positive
otherwise. Summing over the $P$ pairs with $y_i > y_j$,

$$
L_{\text{pair}} \ge \ln 2 \sum_{(i, j)}\indicator[s_i \le s_j] = \ln 2 \cdot P\,(1 - \operatorname{AUC}),
$$

where AUC is the fraction of correctly ordered pairs, the per-list <Gloss name="auroc" />. Driving the pairwise loss to
zero drives the list's AUC to 1. AUC weights a misorder at the bottom of the list as much as one at the top.

**Softmax cross-entropy bounds NDCG.** Take one relevant item $i$, so $t_i = 1$. Its rank is
$\rho_i = 1 + \#\set{j \ne i : s_j \ge s_i}$. Every term $e^{s_j - s_i}$ with $s_j \ge s_i$ is at least 1, so

$$
L_{\text{softmax}} = -\ln p_i = \ln\Big(1 + \sum_{j \ne i} e^{s_j - s_i}\Big) \ge \ln \rho_i.
$$

With one relevant item, $\operatorname{NDCG} = 1/\log_2(1 + \rho_i)$. For $\rho \ge 1$, $\log_2(1 + \rho) \le \rho$,
with equality at $\rho = 1$. Hence
$-\ln\operatorname{NDCG} = \ln\log_2(1 + \rho_i) \le \ln\rho_i \le L_{\text{softmax}}$, or

$$
\operatorname{NDCG} \ge e^{-L_{\text{softmax}}}.
$$

Minimising softmax cross-entropy raises a lower bound on NDCG, and through $\rho_i$ on the reciprocal rank too.
Bruch et al. proved the corresponding bound for several relevant items <Cite id="bruch2019softmax" />.

**Pointwise losses bound nothing about the order.** Binary cross-entropy is a proper scoring rule: its minimiser is
$\sigma(s_i) = P(y_i = 1)$. When each item's relevance is independent of the other items shown, sorting by true
relevance probabilities maximises the expected value of the usual ranking metrics <Cite id="robertson1977prp" />, so a
perfect pointwise model gives the best ranking in expectation. An imperfect one trades errors across items and users
with no regard for position. The pointwise family's distinct output is calibration, not order.

**Lambda gradients** were defined without a loss. Wang et al. showed that LambdaRank's gradients minimise a loss that
is a coarse upper bound on an NDCG-based cost, which explains why training with them improves NDCG
<Cite id="wang2018lambdaloss" />.

## The families side by side

| family | loss on one list | gradient on item $i$ | metric it tracks | cost per list of $n$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pointwise | $\sum_i \ell(s_i, y_i)$ | $\sigma(s_i) - y_i$; independent of the list | log loss; calibration | $O(n)$ |
| pairwise | $\sum_{y_i > y_j}\phi(s_i - s_j)$ | sum over pairs; bounded by the number of pairs | AUC, misordered pairs | $O(n^2)$ pairs, or sampled |
| lambda | none; gradients $\lambda_{ij}\abs{\Delta\text{NDCG}_{ij}}$ | pairwise, weighted towards the top | NDCG | $O(n \log n + n^2)$ |
| listwise softmax | $-\sum_i t_i \ln p_i$ | $p_i - t_i$; mass at the top | NDCG, MRR, recall | $O(n)$ |
| ListMLE | $-\ln P_{\text{PL}}(\pi^\star)$ | softmax at every position | full order | $O(n^2)$, or $O(n \log n)$ |
| sampled softmax | softmax over the positive and $m$ samples | $p_i - t_i$ on the sampled set | recall at $k$ over the catalogue | $O(m)$ instead of $O(M)$ |
| contrastive (in-batch) | InfoNCE over the batch | $p_i - t_i$ over batch items | recall at $k$ | $O(B^2)$ per batch of $B$ |
| next-item | softmax over items at each position | $p_i - t_i$ per position | recall, NDCG at $k$ | $O(TM)$ for $T$ positions |
| generative | cross-entropy over identifier tokens | $p_c - t_c$ per token | recall at $k$ after <Gloss name="beam-search">beam search</Gloss> | $O(TLV)$ for $L$ tokens of vocabulary $V$ |

Here $\phi$ is a decreasing surrogate such as the hinge or logistic function, $P_{\text{PL}}(\pi^\star)$ is the
<NoteLink to="plackett-luce-model">Plackett–Luce</NoteLink> probability of the ideal permutation, $M$ is the catalogue
size and $m \ll M$ the number of sampled negatives.

## History of recommendation losses

**<Gloss name="ltr">Learning to rank</Gloss> for search.** RankSVM cast ranking as classification of preference pairs,
with a <Gloss name="hinge-loss">hinge loss</Gloss> on score differences learnt from clickthrough data
<Cite id="joachims2002clickthrough" />. RankNet replaced the hinge with a <NoteLink to="surrogate-losses">logistic
loss</NoteLink> on the pair probability $\sigma(s_i - s_j)$ and trained a neural network <Cite id="burges2005" />. Both
treat every misordered pair equally, though NDCG does not. LambdaRank abandoned the loss and wrote down the gradient, a
RankNet pair gradient scaled by the NDCG change of a swap <Cite id="burges2006" />. LambdaMART fitted those lambdas with
gradient-boosted trees and became the standard ranker for tabular features <Cite id="burges2010" />. Listwise losses
such as ListNet and ListMLE scored the whole list through a probability model of permutations <Cite id="cao2007" />
<Cite id="xia2008" />.

**Recommendation from feedback.** The <NoteLink to="netflix-prize">Netflix Prize</NoteLink> made rating prediction with
squared error the reference task <Cite id="koren2009mf" />. Most products have clicks and plays, not ratings.
<Gloss name="wmf">Weighted matrix factorisation</Gloss> fitted all user–item cells with confidence weights, treating
absences as weak zeros <Cite id="hu2008implicit" />. BPR recast implicit feedback as pairwise ranking: an interacted
item should outscore a non-interacted one, with the RankNet logistic loss on sampled triples
<Cite id="rendle2009bpr" />.

**Retrieval over the catalogue.** Deep candidate generators treat the next item as a class among millions and train a
softmax over the catalogue, approximated by sampling <Cite id="covington2016youtube" />. Two-tower models train it
with in-batch negatives, which are sampled in proportion to popularity; the logQ correction subtracts the log sampling
probability from each logit to remove that bias <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />. The same loss is InfoNCE in
contrastive learning <Cite id="oord2018" />.

**Sequences and generation.** Session models predict the next item from the items before it, at every position of the
sequence <Cite id="hidasi2016gru4rec" /> <Cite id="kang2018sasrec" />. <Gloss name="sasrec" /> was first trained with
binary cross-entropy on one sampled negative per position, <Gloss name="bert4rec" /> with a full softmax on masked
positions <Cite id="sun2019bert4rec" />. Retrained with the full softmax, SASRec matches or beats BERT4Rec, so much of
the gap had been the loss, not the architecture <Cite id="klenitskiy2023" />. Generative recommenders go one step
further and replace the item softmax by a token softmax. <Gloss name="tiger" /> encodes each item as a short tuple of
discrete codes, a <Gloss name="semantic-identifier">semantic identifier</Gloss>, and trains a
<Gloss name="seq2seq">sequence-to-sequence model</Gloss> with token-level cross-entropy to generate the next item's
codes <Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />.

The arc runs from fitting labels, to ordering pairs, to normalising over the list, and then to normalising over the
whole catalogue. Each step moves the loss closer to the question the product asks: which few items belong at the top.

## Retrieval, sequential and generative losses in one form

The last three families are listwise softmax losses whose "list" is the catalogue. For a context $u$ with true next
item $i$,

$$
L = -\ln\frac{e^{s_{ui}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M} e^{s_{uj}}}.
$$

They differ in how they make the denominator affordable.

- **Sampled softmax** keeps the positive and $m$ items drawn from a proposal $Q$, and subtracts $\ln Q(j)$ from each
  sampled logit so that the sampled loss approximates the full softmax without the proposal's bias (see
  <NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">sampled softmax and negative sampling</NoteLink>).
- **In-batch contrastive losses** use the other positives in the batch as negatives, which costs no extra item-tower
  passes (see <NoteLink to="contrastive-losses-for-retrieval">contrastive losses for retrieval</NoteLink>).
- **Next-item losses** apply the softmax at every position of a sequence, so one sequence of length $T$ gives $T$
  training examples (see <NoteLink to="sequential-recommendation-losses">sequential recommendation losses</NoteLink>).
- **Generative losses** factorise the item into $L$ tokens $c_1, \dots, c_L$ and use the chain rule,
  $-\ln P(i \mid u) = -\sum_{l=1}^{L}\ln P(c_l \mid c_{<l}, u)$. Each factor is a softmax over a codebook of $V$
  codes, so the cost is $LV$ per example instead of $M$ (see
  <NoteLink to="generative-recommendation-losses">generative recommendation losses</NoteLink>).

## Choosing a loss

- **The output feeds an auction, a bid or a blend of several predicted events.** Use a pointwise cross-entropy per
  event. Correct for any negative subsampling. Order is recovered by sorting calibrated probabilities.
- **A ranker reorders a few hundred candidates with graded or click labels per list.** Use a listwise softmax loss or
  lambda gradients (LambdaMART for tabular features). Add a pointwise term if a calibrated score is also needed.
- **Implicit feedback with a moderate catalogue and a factorisation model.** BPR or confidence-weighted matrix
  factorisation. BPR optimises per-user AUC, which weights all positions equally; rank-weighted pairwise losses such as
  <Gloss name="warp" /> move the weight to the top (see <NoteLink to="pairwise-ranking-losses">pairwise ranking
  losses</NoteLink>).
- **Retrieval from millions of items with a two-tower model.** Sampled softmax or in-batch InfoNCE, with the logQ
  correction whenever negatives are not uniform.
- **<NoteLink to="sequential-recommendation-models">Next-item prediction</NoteLink> from sequences.** Full softmax
  cross-entropy over the catalogue if it fits in memory; otherwise sampled softmax with many negatives. Binary
  cross-entropy with one negative per position is cheaper and weaker.
- **Catalogues with many new items, or one model for <NoteLink to="multi-stage-recommendation">retrieval and
  ranking</NoteLink>.** Generative losses over semantic identifiers, which share codes between similar items.

The loss also sets what the offline metric can detect. A calibration change moves log loss but not NDCG. A ranking
change near the top moves NDCG but may barely move log loss.

## Worked example

A list of six items has scores $s = (0.4, 1.3, -0.3, 0.8, -1.0, 0.1)$. Only item 3 was clicked. Sorted by score the
order is 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 5, so the clicked item is at rank $\rho_3 = 5$ and
$\operatorname{NDCG} = 1/\log_2 6 = 0.387$. This is the "one click" list of the figure.

| item | score | rank | pointwise BCE | pairwise logistic | softmax | LambdaRank |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.4 | 3 | 0.599 | 0.668 | 0.155 | 0.076 |
| 2 | 1.3 | 1 | 0.786 | 0.832 | 0.382 | 0.510 |
| 3 (clicked) | −0.3 | 5 | −0.574 | −3.181 | −0.923 | −0.805 |
| 4 | 0.8 | 2 | 0.690 | 0.750 | 0.232 | 0.183 |
| 5 | −1.0 | 6 | 0.269 | 0.332 | 0.038 | 0.010 |
| 6 | 0.1 | 4 | 0.525 | 0.599 | 0.115 | 0.026 |

Each entry is $\partial L/\partial s_i$; a positive value means a gradient step lowers the score. The LambdaRank column
uses the unnormalised NDCG swap change, which equals $\abs{\Delta\operatorname{NDCG}}$ here because the ideal
<Gloss name="dcg" /> is 1.

Item 5 sits below the clicked item and is correctly ordered. Binary cross-entropy still pushes it down by 0.269, half as
hard as it pushes the clicked item up. The softmax pushes it by 0.038 and LambdaRank by 0.010. Item 2, at the top,
gets the largest downward push under every loss, but by different margins: 2.9 times the push on item 5 under binary
cross-entropy, 2.5 times under the pairwise loss, 10 times under the softmax and 51 times under LambdaRank.

The bounds hold. The softmax loss is $-\ln p_3 = -\ln 0.077 = 2.562$. It exceeds $\ln\rho_3 = \ln 5 = 1.609$, which
exceeds $-\ln\operatorname{NDCG} = 0.950$, so $\operatorname{NDCG} = 0.387 \ge e^{-2.562} = 0.077$. The pairwise
logistic loss is 5.591. Four of the five pairs are misordered, and $4\ln 2 = 2.773 \le 5.591$.

## Assumptions

- **Labels within a list are comparable.** Pairwise and listwise losses compare items shown in the same context. They
  need consistent labels within a list, not across lists.
- **Unobserved means negative, to some degree.** Every loss for implicit feedback treats some non-interacted items as
  negatives. Their weight or sampling distribution is a modelling choice, and a biased choice biases the model.
- **Logged feedback reflects the old system.** Clicks depend on position and on what was shown. Without correction, any
  of these losses learns to reproduce the logging policy (see
  <NoteLink to="unbiased-learning-to-rank">unbiased learning to rank</NoteLink>).

## Pitfalls

- **Comparing architectures under different losses.** A change of loss can move a model more than a change of
  architecture, as the SASRec and BERT4Rec comparison showed <Cite id="klenitskiy2023" />. Hold the loss and the
  negatives fixed when comparing models.
- **Sampled scores read as probabilities.** Losses with sampled negatives learn scores shifted by the log sampling
  rate. They rank, but they are not calibrated probabilities until corrected.
- **Pair counts.** A list of $n$ items has up to $n(n - 1)/2$ pairs, so long lists dominate an unnormalised pairwise
  loss. Normalise per list or sample pairs.
- **Evaluating on sampled negatives.** Metrics computed against a few sampled negatives can reverse the order of models
  compared with metrics over the full catalogue (see
  <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">recommendation metrics</NoteLink>).
