<Definition>
**Reinforcement learning for recommendation** models the interaction between a recommender and a user as a <NoteLink
to="markov-decision-process">Markov decision process</NoteLink> $(\Scal, \Acal, P, R, \gamma)$. The **state** $s_t$
describes the user and context (recent history, profile, session); an **action** $a_t$ is an item or a slate; the
**transition** $P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$ captures how the recommendation changes the user; the **reward** $R$ scores
the response; and the **discount** $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ weights the future. The goal is a policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ that
maximises the expected return $\expect_\pi\LB\sum_{t \ge 0}\gamma^tR_{t+1}\RB$ <Cite id="afsar2022rlsurvey" />.
</Definition>

## From prediction to decisions

A supervised recommender predicts the next click and shows the items with the highest predictions. It treats each
recommendation as independent and ignores that what it shows today shapes what the user wants, knows and does tomorrow.
RL treats the recommender as an agent acting on an environment, the user, over time. It can reason about consequences:
recommending a niche item today, a short-term risk, may broaden a user's tastes and raise engagement over the coming
months.

The discount sets the horizon. The return from step $t$ is $G_t = \sum_{j \ge 0}\gamma^jR_{t+j+1}$; a reward $n$ steps
ahead is worth $\gamma^n$ as much as an immediate one. With $\gamma = 0$ the problem reduces to maximising the next
reward, the <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandit</NoteLink>. With $\gamma$ near 1 a reward 100 steps away
still counts: $0.99^{100} = 0.37$.

## The reward is the objective

The reward is the only signal the agent learns from, so it defines what success means. A reward of clicks produces
clickbait. Richer formulations include:

- **Deeper engagement:** dwell time, completion, conversion, session length.
- **Diversity and novelty:** explicit rewards for recommending varied or unfamiliar items, against <NoteLink to="feedback-loops-in-recommendation">filter bubbles</NoteLink>.
- **Long-term satisfaction:** retention, churn, lifetime value, session satisfaction as the reward.

Designing the reward is a business decision as much as a technical one, with the same trade-offs as choosing an
<NoteLink to="overall-evaluation-criterion">overall evaluation criterion</NoteLink>.

## Obstacles in practice

- **State and action spaces.** Billions of user states and millions of items rule out tabular methods. Deep function
  approximation is required, with its loss of guarantees.
- **Sparse and delayed rewards.** A user may browse hundreds of items before buying one, and a recommended series may
  pay off in weeks of viewing. Assigning credit to the action that caused a delayed reward is hard.
- **Exploration is risky.** An untrained agent exploring freely on live users can wreck their experience. Agents are
  therefore trained on logs from the existing system, which is <NoteLink to="offline-reinforcement-learning">offline RL</NoteLink>, and must
  be evaluated from those logs by <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation-in-reinforcement-learning">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink> before
  deployment. Both are hard, and a badly evaluated policy can do real damage.
- **Instability.** Deep RL is sensitive to hyperparameters and can diverge.

These hurdles make full RL a long-term goal for most organisations rather than a routine tool.

## Examples

**Off-policy <Gloss name="reinforce" /> at YouTube.** Chen et al. trained a softmax policy over millions of videos with a <NoteLink
to="policy-gradient-theorem">policy gradient</NoteLink> on logs from earlier recommenders. Each logged trajectory is
re-weighted by the importance ratio $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)/\beta(a \mid s)$ between the new policy and a learned model
$\beta$ of the logging policy. Because the system shows $K$ items at once, they replace $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ in the
objective by the probability that $a$ appears in a top-$K$ sample, $\alpha_\theta(a \mid s) = 1 - (1 - \pi_\theta(a \mid
s))^K$, which multiplies the <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> by $K(1 - \pi_\theta(a \mid s))^{K-1}$. The factor shrinks for items the policy
already favours and pushes probability to items not yet in its top $K$ <Cite id="chen2019topk" />.

**SlateQ.** A slate is an action from a combinatorial space. Under assumptions that the user consumes at most one item
from the slate and that choice follows a known choice model, Ie et al. decompose the value of a slate into the values of
its items weighted by their probabilities of being chosen, which makes Q-learning over slates tractable
<Cite id="ie2019slateq" />.

**Simulators.** <NoteLink to="debiasing-toolkit">RecoGym</NoteLink> and RecSim NG simulate users so that policies and their long-run effects can be tested before
live traffic <Cite id="rohde2018recogym" /> <Cite id="mladenov2021recsimng" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Myopic rewards in RL clothing.** An RL system with a click reward and a small $\gamma$ is a click predictor with
  extra complexity.
- **Simulator gaps.** A policy that games a simulator's user model may fail on real users.
- **Popularity through the back door.** Without explicit rewards for diversity, an RL policy trained on logs inherits
  the logging policy's <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink>. With them, it is one of the few frameworks that can actively counter it. See
  <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in recommendation</NoteLink>.
