<Definition>
An **experimentation bias** in recommendation is a systematic error in an online experiment's estimate of how a change
would perform if launched, caused by the recommender's own dynamics: its feedback loops, its shared training data and
its influence on the whole catalogue. Randomising users removes selection bias between arms; it does not remove these
<Cite id="holtz2025symbiosis" /> <Cite id="kohavi2020" />.
</Definition>

## Popularity bias meets each experiment type

No experimental method operates outside the system it tests. The most pervasive systemic bias is popularity bias, and it
interacts differently with each method.

- **A/B tests.** A new model can win on click-through rate because it is better at surfacing already popular items,
  while it reduces discovery and diversity. The test answers the question asked, "more clicks?", and the question was
  wrong. Guardrail metrics for diversity, coverage and long-term retention belong in the
  <NoteLink to="overall-evaluation-criterion">overall evaluation criterion</NoteLink>.
- **Bandits.** A naive <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">bandit</NoteLink> over items quickly treats popular items as
  winning arms and starves the long tail of traffic, so it never learns whether niche items were better. This is the
  feedback loop in its purest form <Cite id="jiang2019degenerate" />.
- **Reinforcement learning.** A reward that explicitly values diverse and novel recommendations lets an agent learn a
  policy that looks beyond popularity. See <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-for-recommendation">reinforcement
  learning for recommendation</NoteLink>.

Because popularity bias allocates exposure unequally among providers and serves non-mainstream users worse, the choice
of experiment and of its metric is also a fairness decision. See <NoteLink to="fairness-in-recommendation">fairness in
recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Interference

The stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) says that one user's outcome does not depend on which arm other users
are in. Recommenders break it. If the treatment makes some items trend, the trending items appear in the control group's
feeds too, and the measured difference shrinks. In two-sided marketplaces, treatment users can buy up stock that control
users would have bought. See <NoteLink to="interference-and-cluster-randomisation">interference and cluster
randomisation</NoteLink>.

## Symbiosis bias

Arms of a recommender experiment usually retrain on the platform's pooled logs. The data generated by arm B then train
arm A's next model, and the reverse. Each algorithm's performance is influenced by data its competitor produced, so the
experiment measures neither algorithm as it would behave alone. Holtz et al. call this **symbiosis bias**, a violation
of SUTVA's "no hidden treatments" assumption, and confirmed it in a large-scale A/B test on a global recommender system.
They compare three designs that reduce it <Cite id="holtz2025symbiosis" />:

- **Cluster-randomised:** randomise groups of users and items so that feedback stays within a cluster.
- **Data-diverted:** train each arm only on data from its own users, at the cost of less training data per arm.
- **User–corpus co-diverted:** split both users and items between arms, so each arm is a closed system.

Each reduces the bias and brings its own cost.

## Feedback over time

A short A/B test of a model that is not retrained on its own arm's data misses the loop entirely: in production the
model would retrain on the data it created, and its effect could grow or decay. Simulations show that recommenders
trained on their own confounded data become more homogeneous over time <Cite id="chaney2018feedback" />. Longer tests,
holdouts that run across retraining cycles, and data-diverted designs capture more of it. See
<NoteLink to="feedback-loops-in-recommendation">feedback loops in recommendation</NoteLink> and
<NoteLink to="novelty-and-primacy-effects">novelty and primacy effects</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Declaring victory on clicks.** Check what the winning arm recommended: its popularity profile, coverage and
  diversity.
- **Pooled training data.** If both arms retrain on shared logs, the measured effect is a blend of the two. Divert data
  or report the design's limitation.
