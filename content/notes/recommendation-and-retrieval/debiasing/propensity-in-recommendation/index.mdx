<Definition>
A **propensity** is the probability that a user–item interaction becomes observable, before any preference enters.
Writing $O_{ui} = 1$ when the interaction of user $u$ with item $i$ could be observed (the item was exposed, the rank
examined), the propensity is $\pi_{ui} = P(O_{ui} = 1)$, possibly conditional on rank, context or the logging policy. An
observed outcome is the product of observability and preference, so propensities are what must be divided out to
recover preference <Cite id="schnabel2016treatments" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Propensity affects how data are collected, how models are trained and evaluated, and how systems are deployed. Without
it, what users prefer is confused with what they happened to see.

- **Shelf visibility.** Cereal at eye level is noticed far more often than cereal on the bottom shelf. The propensity is
  the shelf's visibility, separate from the cereal's taste. A bottom-shelf cereal that sells as well as an eye-level one
  is probably preferred.

- **Posting time.** A post at peak hours reaches ten times the audience of one at 3 a.m. Viral content posted off-peak
  indicates more intrinsic appeal than moderately successful peak-hour content.

In each case an outcome count mixes propensity and preference, and comparing preference requires dividing by propensity.

## Parallels in machine learning

The same structure appears in several models.

- **<Gloss name="irt">Item response theory</Gloss>.** A test answer combines a student's ability with a question's difficulty through a logistic
  link. Positions or contexts play the role of questions, with propensity as difficulty. IRT is a rank-1 matrix
  factorisation, so propensity models are low-rank factorisations too.
- **Product of experts.** One expert models the probability of exposure, another the preference given exposure, and
  they multiply.
- **Zero-inflated models.** One process decides whether an event can happen, another its value if it can. Propensity is
  the first process.
- **Survival analysis.** In a cascade of attention, reaching rank $k$ means surviving the ranks above. See
  <NoteLink to="click-models">click models</NoteLink>.
- **<Gloss name="multi-task-learning">Multi-task learning</Gloss>, embeddings and attention.** Shared layers learn preference common to several behaviours while
  task heads learn channel-specific observation; position, device and context can enter as learned embeddings; and
  propensity acts like a soft attention mask that lets more or less preference signal through.

## Types of propensity

Five kinds are worth distinguishing, each estimated differently.

| propensity             | meaning                                                         | how it is estimated                                                                                       |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| position $\pi(k)$      | rank $k$ is examined                                            | randomised swaps; click models (position-based model, cascade); monotone curve fits such as <NoteLink to="isotonic-regression">isotonic regression</NoteLink> or a <Gloss name="gam" />   |
| item exposure $\pi_{ui}$ | user $u$ is exposed to item $i$                               | impression logs if kept; otherwise Poisson factorisation or Gamma–Poisson expectation–maximisation on clicks; activity × popularity |
| contextual $\pi(c)$    | examination given device, viewport, region, time or layout      | <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink> or GAMs over context; per-cohort estimates; hierarchical pooling                      |
| channel $\pi(\text{ch})$ | a telemetry channel (click, rating, purchase) fires given exposure | direct logging; multi-task models with a propensity head per channel                                  |
| logging policy $\pi(a \mid x)$ | the policy chose action $a$ in context $x$              | logged at decision time; else reconstructed from the policy's logic or imitated by a model                |

The last is the propensity of <NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities">logging policies</NoteLink> in bandits
and <Gloss name="ope">off-policy evaluation</Gloss>; how a production recommender obtains it, from sampled scores, store frequencies or impression
counts, is covered in <NoteLink to="counterfactual-evaluation-of-recommenders">counterfactual evaluation of
recommenders</NoteLink>. Estimating item exposure from clicks alone is worked through in
<NoteLink to="estimating-item-propensities">estimating item propensities</NoteLink>.

## Estimating position propensities

- **Randomisation.** Randomly permuting or swapping results for a small share of traffic breaks the link between rank
  and relevance. The <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through rate</Gloss> at each rank, or the ratio of click-through rates of the same document at two ranks, then
  measures examination directly. This is the gold standard. It costs user experience and engineering effort
  <Cite id="joachims2017ultr" />.
- **Expectation–maximisation on observational logs.** Fit a position-based click model by expectation–maximisation: estimate relevance given
  the current examination model, then examination given relevance. Wang et al. used a regression-based expectation–maximisation so that
  relevance is a function of features and queries need not repeat <Cite id="wang2018position" />. See
  <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">expectation–maximisation</NoteLink>.
- **Dual learning.** Learn the ranker and the propensity model together: the ranker's relevance estimates supervise the
  propensity model and the propensities de-bias the ranker's training <Cite id="ai2018dla" />.
- **Intervention harvesting.** Different versions of a ranker, deployed over time, placed the same document at
  different ranks. Those natural swaps act as interventions, and Agarwal et al. estimate <Gloss name="position-bias">position bias</Gloss> from them without
  new randomisation <Cite id="agarwal2019intervention" />.

**Relevance confounds propensity estimation.** A good logging policy puts relevant documents near the top, so high rank
and high relevance are correlated in logs. A naive estimate that divides clicks by rank attributes some of the relevance
to position and overestimates the examination of top ranks, which weakens the correction. Treating this as a causal
problem gives better propensities. Luo et al. apply the back-door adjustment with a propensity model that conditions on
the logging policy's ranking scores <Cite id="luo2024unconfounded" />.

## Several propensities at once

Position, activity, popularity, device and channel act together. There are four ways to combine them.

- **Factorised product.** Assume <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink> and multiply, $\pi(u, i, k, x) \approx
  \pi_{\text{pos}}(k)\,\pi_{\text{dev}}(x_{\text{device}})\,\pi_{\text{pop}}(i)\,\alpha_u$. Simple and interpretable;
  ignores interactions.
- **Joint model.** Fit $\pi(u, i, k, x) = \sigma(f_\theta(u, i, k, x))$ with a logistic regression, boosted trees or a
  network. Captures interactions, and can blur exposure into relevance unless constrained.
- **Hierarchical composition.** Global baselines plus deviations, $\log\pi(u, i, k, x) = \gamma_k + \delta_{k,
  x_{\text{device}}} + \phi_i + \alpha_u$, which shares strength across cohorts and allows context-specific corrections.
- **Embedded.** Rank, device and popularity enter a neural recommender as embeddings or side tokens and are learned end
  to end, with monotonicity or regularisation constraints on position features.

## Propensities change

Propensities are not fixed properties of ranks or items. Position curves flatten when infinite scroll spreads attention,
and shift when a banner or carousel is added. Item exposure drifts as the catalogue changes, and purchase propensity
peaks at holidays. Most work estimates static curves. Time-varying estimation is comparatively unexplored and remains
an open area. Its methods include smoothing weekly estimates, <Gloss name="ssm">state-space models</Gloss> such as the
<NoteLink to="kalman-filter">Kalman filter</NoteLink>, hierarchical Bayesian models with time-varying parameters, and
sequence models.

## Pitfalls

- **Stale propensities.** A curve estimated before a redesign is wrong after it. Re-estimate after interface or policy
  changes.
- **Propensities near zero.** Their inverses explode. Clip or smooth, and report how much weight was affected.
- **Positivity.** An item never exposed has propensity zero, and no re-weighting recovers its preference. Only
  exploration does.
