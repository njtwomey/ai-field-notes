<Definition>
A production recommender, the **logging policy** $\pi_0$, shows item $a$ in context $x$ (a user, or the page being
viewed) with probability $\pi_0(a \mid x)$, and the log records each impression as $(x_i, a_i, r_i, p_i)$ with reward
$r_i$ (a click or a purchase) and **propensity** $p_i = \pi_0(a_i \mid x_i)$. **Counterfactual evaluation** estimates
the expected reward per impression that a new recommender $\pi$ would earn,

$$
V(\pi) = \expect_{x}\brack{\sum_a \pi(a \mid x)\,\mu(x, a)}, \qquad \mu(x, a) = \expect[r \mid x, a],
$$

from that log, without deploying $\pi$ <Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />.
</Definition>

## Observational and interventional evaluation

The standard offline evaluation of a recommender splits logged interactions into training and validation sets and
scores the validation set with recall, precision or <Gloss name="ndcg" /> (see <NoteLink to="recommendation-metrics">recommendation
metrics</NoteLink>). That protocol treats recommendation as an **observational** problem, like predicting a product's
category from its title. A model evaluated this way learns, for example,
$P(\text{next view} = \text{iPhone} \mid \text{viewed Pixel, Galaxy})$: how well it predicts what users did while the
production recommender was choosing what they saw.

Recommendation is an **interventional** problem. Showing an item changes what users do, and users can only click items
that were shown. The quantity of interest is
$P(\text{click} \mid \text{recommend iPhone}, \text{viewed Pixel, Galaxy})$: what happens under a new intervention
<Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />. A holdout metric measures how well a new model fits the production policy's log. A
model that differs from production is penalised for every logged click it would not have caused, and receives no credit
for clicks it would have caused on items production never showed. The causal structure behind this, confounding by
the presentation policy, is set out in <NoteLink to="biases-in-recommender-feedback">biases in recommender
feedback</NoteLink>. Reweighting holdout metrics pair by pair is covered in <NoteLink
to="unbiased-offline-evaluation">unbiased offline evaluation</NoteLink>; this note evaluates whole policies.

The direct way to answer an interventional question is an <NoteLink to="a-b-test">A/B test</NoteLink>. An A/B test
shows the new recommendations to real users and measures the change in click-through or conversion. It needs
engineering work to deploy, enough traffic and time to reach a verdict, and tolerance for exposing users to a bad
variant; research teams often have no access to one. Counterfactual evaluation asks the existing log what would have
happened had users been shown the new recommendations instead. It estimates the outcome of an A/B test without running
it, and is used to decide which candidates deserve one.

## Inverse propensity scoring

The **inverse propensity scoring** (IPS) estimator reweights each logged reward by how much more, or less, often the new
recommender would make the logged recommendation:

$$
\hat V_{\text{IPS}}(\pi) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i r_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{\pi(a_i \mid x_i)}{\pi_0(a_i \mid x_i)}.
$$

The ratio $w_i$ is the **importance weight**. Suppose the production recommender shows an iPhone on the Pixel's detail
page with probability 0.4 and the new recommender would show it with probability 0.6. The new recommender makes that
recommendation $0.6/0.4 = 1.5$ times as often, so each logged click on the iPhone from the Pixel page counts 1.5 times
<Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />. The estimator is the Horvitz–Thompson estimator of survey sampling
<Cite id="horvitz1952" />, and the same inverse-probability weighting underlies propensity-score methods for
observational studies <Cite id="rosenbaum1983" />. It is <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance
sampling</NoteLink> with the logging policy as proposal.

**Unbiasedness.** Given the context $x$, the logged item is drawn from $\pi_0(\cdot \mid x)$, so

$$
\expect[w r \mid x] = \sum_a \pi_0(a \mid x)\,\frac{\pi(a \mid x)}{\pi_0(a \mid x)}\,\mu(x, a) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid x)\,\mu(x, a),
$$

provided $\pi_0(a \mid x) > 0$ for every item the new recommender might show. The production probabilities cancel, and
averaging over $x$ gives $\expect[\hat V_{\text{IPS}}] = V(\pi)$. The derivation in full, with the law of total
<NoteLink to="expectation">expectation</NoteLink>, is in <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink>.

## Where the propensities come from

IPS needs $\pi_0(a \mid x)$ for every logged impression. A production recommender offers three sources
<Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />.

- **Normalised scores.** If the recommender samples items from its scores $s_a$, the probability of item $a$ in the
  first slot is the Plackett–Luce probability $\exp(s_a)/\sum_b \exp(s_b)$, and later slots follow by <NoteLink to="hypergeometric-distribution">sampling without
  replacement</NoteLink> (see <NoteLink to="paired-comparison-models">paired comparison models</NoteLink>). A recommender that
  sorts by score and shows the top items deterministically has propensities 0 and 1, whatever the softmax of its scores
  says. Normalised scores are propensities only if the system actually samples from them.
- **Frequencies in a recommendation store.** Recommendations precomputed in batch can be counted: the share of store
  versions, or of candidate lists, in which item $a$ was recommended for context $x$.
- **Impression counts.** The share of impressions in context $x$ that showed item $a$. Impressions record what users
  were actually shown, after business rules, deduplication and layout, so they are the most direct measure of the
  probability of a recommendation. Yan prefers them for that reason.

Counted frequencies are estimates, and they are noisy for rarely shown items, which are exactly the items with the
largest weights. The general theory of exposure and position propensities is in <NoteLink
to="propensity-in-recommendation">propensity in recommendation</NoteLink>, and estimating exposure from clicks alone is
worked through in <NoteLink to="estimating-item-propensities">estimating item propensities</NoteLink>.

Most public recommendation datasets record no propensities, which is one reason counterfactual evaluation is rare in
academic papers. The Open Bandit Dataset, from a fashion e-commerce site, is an exception: every row carries the
probability with which its item was chosen <Cite id="saito2021obp" />.

## Failure one: missing support

IPS is unbiased only if the production recommender could have shown every item the new recommender shows. Let $U(x)$ be
the set of items with $\pi_0(a \mid x) = 0$. Those items never appear in the log, so the sum in the unbiasedness
argument runs over the other items only:

$$
\expect[\hat V_{\text{IPS}}] = V(\pi) - \expect_x\brack{\sum_{a \in U(x)} \pi(a \mid x)\,\mu(x, a)}.
$$

The estimator silently drops the value of every recommendation the log never made. For nonnegative rewards the bias is
downward: a new recommender that surfaces items production never showed looks worse than it is
<Cite id="sachdeva2020deficient" />. No reweighting repairs this, because there is nothing to reweight.

Support is a property of the logging policy, so it is fixed by changing that policy.

- **Exploration traffic.** Show random, otherwise unrecommended items on a small share of traffic, so that every item
  has been logged in every context. Product owners tend to resist it, because it shows users worse recommendations.
- **Stochastic recommendations.** Give every eligible item a positive probability and sample the recommendation from
  it, for example by Plackett–Luce sampling from the scores, or by mixing the ranking with a small uniform share. Every
  item has a chance to be shown, and the propensity is known exactly.

<NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities">Logging policies and propensities</NoteLink> compares these designs
and quantifies what each costs.

## Failure two: variance

The terms of IPS are independent, so $\var(\hat V_{\text{IPS}}) = \var(w r)/n$. The contribution of one item $a$ in
context $x$ to the second moment is its logging probability times the squared weighted reward:

$$
\pi_0(a \mid x)\paren{\frac{\pi(a \mid x)}{\pi_0(a \mid x)}}^2\expect[r^2 \mid x, a] = \frac{\pi(a \mid x)^2}{\pi_0(a \mid x)}\,\expect[r^2 \mid x, a].
$$

The variance grows as $1/\pi_0$ on the items the new recommender favours. Suppose production shows an item with
probability 0.001 and one click on it was logged. If the new recommender shows the item with probability 0.1, that
single click is multiplied by 100 <Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />. Whether the log happened to contain that click
moves the estimate by more than the difference between candidate recommenders. The simplest defence is to evaluate
only candidates close to production: if $\pi(a \mid x) \le M\,\pi_0(a \mid x)$ everywhere, every weight is at most
$M$. Penalties on divergence from $\pi_0$ used when learning policies are the same idea; see
<NoteLink to="off-policy-learning">off-policy learning</NoteLink>.

## Clipped IPS

**Clipped IPS** (CIPS) caps each weight at a threshold $M$ <Cite id="bottou2013" /> <Cite id="swaminathan2015" />:

$$
\hat V_{\text{CIPS}}(\pi) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(w_i, M)\,r_i.
$$

With $M = 10$, a weight of 100 counts as 10. The threshold trades bias for variance.

**Bias.** Given $x$, $\expect[\min(w, M)\,r \mid x] = \sum_a \pi_0(a \mid x)\min\paren{\pi(a \mid x)/\pi_0(a \mid x), M}\mu(x, a) = \sum_a \min\paren{\pi(a \mid x), M\pi_0(a \mid x)}\mu(x, a)$.
Subtracting $\sum_a \pi(a \mid x)\mu(x, a)$ gives

$$
\expect[\hat V_{\text{CIPS}}] - V(\pi) = -\expect_x\brack{\sum_a \paren{\pi(a \mid x) - M\pi_0(a \mid x)}_+\,\mu(x, a)},
$$

where $(z)_+ = \max(z, 0)$. For nonnegative rewards the bias is downward, and it is the value the new recommender earns
from the part of its probability that exceeds $M$ times production's. It vanishes when $M$ exceeds the largest weight.

**Variance.** For $w \ge 0$, $\min(w, M)^2 \le M w$, since $\min(w, M) \le M$ and $\min(w, M) \le w$. With rewards in
$[0, r_{\max}]$ and $\expect[w] = \expect_x\brack{\sum_a \pi(a \mid x)} = 1$,

$$
\var(\hat V_{\text{CIPS}}) \le \frac{\expect[\min(w, M)^2 r^2]}{n} \le \frac{M\,r_{\max}^2\,\expect[w]}{n} = \frac{M\,r_{\max}^2}{n}.
$$

The variance is bounded by the threshold, whatever the smallest propensity. A small $M$ gives low variance and a large
downward bias; a large $M$ recovers IPS. The best threshold depends on the log and the candidate, and tuning it is the
estimator's practical weakness <Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />.

## Self-normalised IPS

The weights have expectation 1, as shown above, but their sample average $\bar w = \frac1n\sum_i w_i$ fluctuates, and
IPS inherits the fluctuation. **Self-normalised IPS** (SNIPS) divides by the sum of the weights instead of by $n$
<Cite id="swaminathan2015b" />:

$$
\hat V_{\text{SNIPS}}(\pi) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i r_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}.
$$

- **No parameter.** Unlike CIPS, SNIPS has no threshold to tune.
- **Bounded.** SNIPS is a weighted average of the logged rewards, so it lies between the smallest and largest of them.
  IPS can report a click rate above 1.
- **Consistent, slightly biased.** The numerator divided by $n$ converges to $V(\pi)$ and the denominator divided by $n$
  converges to $\expect[w] = 1$, so the ratio converges to $V(\pi)$. A ratio of two random sums is biased in finite
  samples, by an amount of order $1/n$.
- **Invariant to reward translation.** Replacing every reward $r_i$ by $r_i + c$ changes SNIPS by exactly $c$, because
  $\sum_i w_i(r_i + c)/\sum_i w_i = \hat V_{\text{SNIPS}} + c$. IPS changes by $c\,\bar w$, which is not $c$. The IPS
  comparison of two recommenders can therefore depend on whether a non-click is coded 0 or $-1$; the SNIPS comparison
  cannot.

SNIPS has a storage cost that IPS does not. The IPS numerator needs weights only for impressions with nonzero reward,
and its denominator $n$ is a count. The SNIPS denominator needs the weight of every impression. With click-through or
conversion rates below 10%, that is ten or more times as many propensities to store and weights to compute
<Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />. Swaminathan and Joachims report that SNIPS converges faster, which repays the extra
computation in learning <Cite id="swaminathan2015b" />.

## The direct method and doubly robust

The **direct method** (DM) fits a reward model $\hat\mu(x, a)$, a <NoteLink to="click-models">click model</NoteLink> or a simulator of users, and averages its
predictions under the new recommender, $\hat V_{\text{DM}} = \frac1n\sum_i\sum_a \pi(a \mid x_i)\,\hat\mu(x_i, a)$.
It is the recommender analogue of training <Gloss name="rl">reinforcement-learning</Gloss> agents in a simulated environment such as <NoteLink to="debiasing-toolkit">RecoGym</NoteLink>
<Cite id="rohde2018recogym" />. It needs no propensities and has low variance. The model is fitted on production's
impressions, so it extrapolates on exactly the items the new recommender adds, and its error there is a bias that more
data does not remove.

The **doubly robust** (DR) estimator adds the weighted residuals of the model to DM <Cite id="dudik2011" />:

$$
\hat V_{\text{DR}}(\pi) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\brack{\sum_a \pi(a \mid x_i)\,\hat\mu(x_i, a) + w_i\paren{r_i - \hat\mu(x_i, a_i)}}.
$$

The correction is IPS applied to the residual $r - \hat\mu(x, a)$. With correct propensities its expectation is
$\expect_x[\sum_a \pi(a \mid x)(\mu(x, a) - \hat\mu(x, a))]$, which cancels the model's bias exactly. With a correct
model the residuals have mean zero under any weights. DR is unbiased if either part is right; both cases are derived in
<NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink>. Its variance is that of IPS on the residuals.
With rare binary clicks most of the residual is the randomness of the click itself, which no model predicts, so DR
removes less variance in recommendation than in problems with rewards far from zero.

## Choosing an estimator

<EstimatorLab />

In the lab, IPS is centred on the truth, and its spread grows as production's propensities for the
new recommender's items shrink. Clipping shrinks the spread and pulls the estimate down; at a small threshold the error
is almost all bias. SNIPS is nearly centred with a smaller spread and no parameter. The direct method with a page-blind
model has a small spread and a biased estimate. Doubly robust stays centred whatever the model. The production click
rate, which is what the log reports without reweighting, is far from the new recommender's value: that gap is the
observational bias.

In a comparison on synthetic data with ten actions, Saito found that IPS overtook the direct method as the log grew,
that clipping gained little over IPS, and that SNIPS had the smallest error without any tuning
<Cite id="saito2021tutorial" />. Yan's practical advice follows from this <Cite id="yan2022counterfactual" />. Keep
conventional offline evaluation, because it is standardised, comparable across papers and available before any
recommender is deployed. Add counterfactual evaluation with SNIPS when offline metrics disagree with A/B test outcomes.
The SIGIR 2016 and RecSys 2021 tutorials develop the estimators and their learning counterparts further
<Cite id="joachims2016tutorial" /> <Cite id="saito2021tutorial" />.

A recommender usually shows a list, not one item. Treating each slot as a separate impression with its own propensity
assumes that items on a page do not affect each other's rewards; <NoteLink to="slate-off-policy-evaluation">off-policy
evaluation for slates</NoteLink> covers what happens when they do. Li et al.'s replay method, which keeps only the
impressions where a uniformly random logger showed what the new policy would show, is the special case of SNIPS with
equal propensities, and was used to evaluate news recommenders offline <Cite id="li2011" />.

## Worked example

A shop recommends one accessory on phone detail pages: an iPhone or a phone case. The context $x$ is the page, Pixel or
Galaxy. The production and new recommenders show the items with these probabilities:

| page   | item   | production $\pi_0$ | new $\pi$ | weight $w = \pi/\pi_0$ |
| ------ | ------ | ------------------ | --------- | ---------------------- |
| Pixel  | iPhone | 0.4                | 0.6       | 1.5                    |
| Pixel  | case   | 0.6                | 0.4       | 2/3                    |
| Galaxy | iPhone | 0.1                | 0.5       | 5                      |
| Galaxy | case   | 0.9                | 0.5       | 5/9                    |

Six impressions were logged, with reward 1 for a click:

| impression | page   | item   | propensity $p$ | click $r$ | weight $w$ |
| ---------- | ------ | ------ | -------------- | --------- | ---------- |
| 1          | Pixel  | iPhone | 0.4            | 1         | 1.5        |
| 2          | Pixel  | case   | 0.6            | 0         | 0.667      |
| 3          | Pixel  | case   | 0.6            | 1         | 0.667      |
| 4          | Galaxy | case   | 0.9            | 0         | 0.556      |
| 5          | Galaxy | iPhone | 0.1            | 1         | 5          |
| 6          | Galaxy | case   | 0.9            | 0         | 0.556      |

- **Log average.** Three clicks in six impressions give $3/6 = 0.5$. This estimates production's click rate, not the
  new recommender's.
- **IPS.** The clicked impressions carry weights 1.5, 0.667 and 5, so
  $\hat V_{\text{IPS}} = (1.5 + 0.667 + 5)/6 = 7.167/6 = 1.194$. The estimated click rate exceeds 1, because
  impression 5, a rare recommendation that happened to be clicked, counts five times.
- **Clipped IPS, $M = 3$.** Impression 5's weight becomes 3, so $\hat V_{\text{CIPS}} = (1.5 + 0.667 + 3)/6 = 0.861$.
- **SNIPS.** The six weights sum to $8.944$, so $\hat V_{\text{SNIPS}} = 7.167/8.944 = 0.801$. The average weight
  $\bar w = 8.944/6 = 1.49$ is far from its expectation of 1, a warning that six impressions are too few. SNIPS needed
  the weights of impressions 2, 4 and 6, which IPS did not.
- **Direct method.** Suppose a click model predicts $\hat\mu = 0.5$ for the iPhone and $0.3$ for the case on the Pixel
  page, and $0.4$ and $0.2$ on the Galaxy page. The new recommender's predicted click rate is
  $0.6 \cdot 0.5 + 0.4 \cdot 0.3 = 0.42$ on a Pixel page and $0.5 \cdot 0.4 + 0.5 \cdot 0.2 = 0.30$ on a Galaxy page.
  Three impressions of each give $\hat V_{\text{DM}} = (3 \cdot 0.42 + 3 \cdot 0.30)/6 = 0.36$.
- **Doubly robust.** The corrections $w_i(r_i - \hat\mu(x_i, a_i))$ are $1.5(1 - 0.5) = 0.75$,
  $0.667(0 - 0.3) = -0.2$, $0.667(1 - 0.3) = 0.467$, $0.556(0 - 0.2) = -0.111$, $5(1 - 0.4) = 3$ and $-0.111$. They sum
  to $3.794$, with average $0.632$, so $\hat V_{\text{DR}} = 0.36 + 0.632 = 0.992$.
- **Translation.** Adding 1 to every reward raises SNIPS by exactly 1 and IPS by $\bar w = 1.49$.

The estimates range from 0.36 to 1.19. The spread shows how
each estimator treats impression 5. IPS takes it at face value, CIPS caps it, SNIPS dilutes it by the total weight, DM
ignores it, and DR adds its residual relative to the click model, weighted by 5. The <Gloss name="ess">effective sample size</Gloss>
$(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2 = 2.8$ says the six impressions carry the information of fewer than three.

## Assumptions

- **Recorded propensities.** The probability of every logged impression under production is known, or estimated well.
- **Support.** Production gave positive probability to every item the new recommender shows, in every context.
- **No unrecorded confounders.** Production chose items using only the recorded context and independent randomness.
- **No interference.** One impression's reward does not depend on the other items on the page, or on earlier
  recommendations to the same user.
- **Stationarity.** Users respond to the new recommender as they responded to the same items under production. Novelty
  effects and feedback loops break this; see <NoteLink to="feedback-loops-in-recommendation">feedback loops in
  recommendation</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Scores as propensities.** A softmax of the scores of a deterministic ranker is not a propensity. Only probabilities
  the system actually sampled from, or observed frequencies, are.
- **Clipping hides missing value.** CIPS never exceeds IPS for nonnegative rewards, so a clipped estimate can rank a
  bold candidate below a timid one. Report how much weight was clipped.
- **Reporting an estimate without diagnostics.** Report the average weight $\bar w$, the effective sample size and the
  largest weights with each estimate. An average weight far from 1 means too little support or wrong propensities.
- **Treating the estimate as the A/B result.** Counterfactual evaluation ranks candidates for an online test. It does
  not replace one, because its assumptions cannot be checked from the log alone.
