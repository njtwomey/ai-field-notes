<Definition>
A **feedback loop** in a <NoteLink to="recommender-systems">recommender system</NoteLink> is the cycle in which a model's recommendations shape user behaviour, the
behaviour is logged, and the log trains the next model. Because the model decides what users can react to, the loop
amplifies whatever biases the model has <Cite id="jiang2019degenerate" />.
</Definition>

## The cycle

Four steps repeat.

1. A model is trained on click data skewed by <Gloss name="position-bias">position bias</Gloss>. It learns a spurious link between high rank and relevance.
2. At serving time it puts the items it believes relevant, often those that already benefited from past bias, at the
   top.
3. Position bias gives those items a disproportionate share of clicks, whatever their relevance to each user.
4. The new clicks train the next model.

The result is a **Matthew effect**: the rich get richer. Popular, highly ranked items become more entrenched, while
items that might be more relevant, but were exposed less, never gather the evidence needed to prove it. Beyond
accuracy, the loop reduces diversity, creates filter bubbles and treats item providers unfairly.

## Why it degenerates

The loop is a failure of exploration. The model learns only about items it shows. An item with a low early estimate is
shown rarely, so its estimate never improves; the error persists because the model's own choices prevent the data that
would correct it. Jiang et al. analysed this dynamic as a degenerate feedback loop: without exploration or other
intervention, a user's interest estimates can drift to extremes set by what the system happened to show
<Cite id="jiang2019degenerate" />. Chaney et al. simulated recommenders trained on data they had themselves confounded
and found that the loop makes users' consumption more homogeneous and lowers the utility they get, compared with a
system that knows true preferences <Cite id="chaney2018feedback" />.

The <NoteLink to="multi-armed-bandit">multi-armed bandit</NoteLink> is the minimal model of the problem: a greedy
policy that never explores can lock onto a worse arm for ever.

## Breaking the loop

The lines of attack range from heuristic to principled.

- **Exploration.** Show some items for the information they yield, not only for their estimated reward, as bandits do.
  Randomised exposure also produces data with known propensities for later correction. See
  <NoteLink to="contextual-bandit">contextual bandits</NoteLink>.
- **Propensity correction.** Weight logged interactions by the inverse probability that they were observable, so the
  model learns preferences, not exposure. See <NoteLink to="unbiased-learning-to-rank">unbiased learning to
  rank</NoteLink> and <NoteLink to="propensity-in-recommendation">propensity</NoteLink>.
- **Causal models of popularity.** Separate the effect of popularity on exposure from preference. See
  <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink>.
- **Objectives beyond clicks.** Reward diversity, novelty and long-term satisfaction, and measure them. See
  <NoteLink to="beyond-accuracy-metrics">beyond-accuracy metrics</NoteLink> and
  <NoteLink to="reinforcement-learning-for-recommendation">reinforcement learning for recommendation</NoteLink>.
- **Simulation.** Simulators of the whole loop let a policy's long-run effect be studied before deployment. See the
  <NoteLink to="debiasing-toolkit">debiasing toolkit</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Short experiments miss it.** A loop plays out over weeks of retraining. An <Gloss name="ab-test" /> of a model that is not retrained
  on its own arm's data measures none of it. See <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation
  biases in recommendation</NoteLink>.
- **Static offline metrics reward it.** A model that imitates the logging policy scores well on logs the policy
  produced.
