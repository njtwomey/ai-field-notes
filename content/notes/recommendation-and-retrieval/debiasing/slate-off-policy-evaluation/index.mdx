<Definition>
A **slate** is an ordered list $\svec = (s_1, \ldots, s_l)$ of $l$ items shown together, such as a carousel, a row of
products or a playlist. **Off-policy evaluation for slates** estimates the expected reward a target policy $\pi$ would
earn from logs of slates chosen by a logging policy $\pi_0$. Because the space of slates is combinatorial and items
interact, it requires structural assumptions about how the slate's reward arises
<Cite id="swaminathan2017slate" />.
</Definition>

## Why slate-level inverse propensity scoring fails

The single-item case, one recommendation per impression, is developed in
<NoteLink to="counterfactual-evaluation-of-recommenders">counterfactual evaluation of recommenders</NoteLink>.
The direct extension of <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">inverse propensity scoring</NoteLink> weights each logged
slate's reward by the ratio of its probabilities,

$$
\hat{V}_{\text{IPS}}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\svec_t \mid x_t)}{\pi_0(\svec_t \mid x_t)}R_t.
$$

It is unbiased when $\pi_0$ gives positive probability to every slate $\pi$ might show. Two problems make it unusable.

1. **Cardinality and support.** With $m$ items and slates of length $l$ there are $m!/(m - l)!$ ordered slates. For $m =
   1{,}000$ and $l = 10$ that is about $9.6 \times 10^{29}$. A logging policy that spread probability evenly would give
   each slate about $10^{-30}$. The slates a new policy proposes have almost surely never been logged, the weight is
   undefined or astronomically large, and the estimate has no support <Cite id="bottou2013" />.
2. **Interactions between items.** Causal estimators usually assume that one unit's outcome does not depend on other
   units' treatments (the <Gloss name="sutva">stable unit treatment value assumption</Gloss>). On a slate this fails: two similar items compete for
   attention, and the order of songs changes how a playlist is enjoyed. The value of a slate is often more than the sum
   of its parts, which is why slates are built at all.

Research on slates is therefore a search for the right simplifying assumption about user behaviour. The more realistic
the behavioural model, the higher the variance of the estimator it permits.

## Structural assumptions

**Independent item-position rewards (IIPS).** Assume the slate's reward is a sum of per-position rewards,
$R = \sum_{k=1}^{l} r_k$, and that $r_k$ depends only on the item at position $k$, not on the rest of the slate. Then

$$
\expect_\pi[R] = \sum_{k=1}^{l}\sum_{a}\pi(s_k = a \mid x)\,\expect[r_k \mid s_k = a, x],
$$

which depends on the policy only through the marginal probabilities of each item at each position. Each position can be
importance-weighted on its own:

$$
\hat{V}_{\text{IIPS}}(\pi) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\pi(s_{t,k} \mid x_t)}{\pi_0(s_{t,k} \mid x_t)}\,r_{t,k},
$$

where $\pi(s_{t,k} \mid x_t)$ is the probability that $\pi$ puts the logged item at position $k$. The support problem
shrinks from slates to item–position pairs, and the variance falls sharply. If items do interact, the estimator is
biased, and the bias does not shrink with more data <Cite id="li2018clickope" />.

**Additive rewards.** Swaminathan et al. assume only that the expected slate reward is a sum of unknown item–position
contributions, and build a pseudoinverse estimator that is unbiased under that assumption with far less data than
slate-level IPS <Cite id="swaminathan2017slate" />.

**Sequential interactions (RIPS).** For slates consumed in order, such as playlists and video queues, assume a Markov
structure: the reward at position $k$ depends on the item at $k$ and on the user's interaction at position $k - 1$.
Reward interaction IPS uses nested <NoteLink to="expectation">expectations</NoteLink> over the prefix of the slate to estimate each position's reward. It is
asymptotically unbiased under the Markov assumption and has a better bias–variance trade-off than both slate-level IPS,
which has too much variance, and IIPS, which has too much bias <Cite id="mcinerney2020rips" />.

<NoteLink to="click-models">Click models</NoteLink> supply other structures: under a cascade model, for example, the
reward at a position depends on the items above it only through whether the user reached it <Cite id="li2018clickope"
/>.

## Worked example

A slate of $l = 2$ positions. In one logged round the logging policy put item $A$ first with probability 0.5 and item
$B$ second with probability 0.2, and showed the slate $(A, B)$ with probability 0.1. The user clicked $A$ ($r_1 = 1$)
and not $B$ ($r_2 = 0$). A target policy puts $A$ first with probability 0.9 and $B$ second with probability 0.1, and
shows $(A, B)$ with probability 0.09.

- Slate IPS: $\frac{0.09}{0.1}(1 + 0) = 0.9$.
- IIPS: $\frac{0.9}{0.5} \cdot 1 + \frac{0.1}{0.2} \cdot 0 = 1.8$.

A single round cannot show which estimator is more accurate. With $m = 1{,}000$ items and $l = 10$
positions, uniform slate probabilities multiply to numbers like $10^{-30}$, while each IIPS ratio stays a ratio of two ordinary
probabilities.

## Whole-page bias

Bias on a page is not only vertical position. The type of content (image, video), the height of an element, the text of
its snippet and the layout all affect attention. Whole-page <NoteLink to="unbiased-learning-to-rank">unbiased learning to rank</NoteLink> aims to correct for all
presentation features at once. One approach learns the structure of a causal graph linking presentation features,
relevance and clicks from data, then adjusts for every bias it finds. Large datasets with rich display features, such
as Baidu-ULTR, make this line of research possible <Cite id="zou2022baiduultr" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Unverified <NoteLink to="independence">independence</NoteLink>.** IIPS looks precise because its variance is low. Its bias is invisible in the estimate
  itself. Check the interaction assumption with a small <NoteLink to="randomised-controlled-experiments">randomised experiment</NoteLink>.
- **Logging marginals.** IIPS needs the logging policy's marginal probability of each item at each position. Log them,
  or be able to recompute them, when slates are generated.
