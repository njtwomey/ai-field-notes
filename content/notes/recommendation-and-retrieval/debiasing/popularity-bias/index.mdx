<Definition>
**Popularity bias** is the systematic over-recommendation of popular items relative to their relevance to individual
users. Popular items receive more interactions, dominate the training data, and become a low-loss default for the model,
while niche and new items are under-recommended even to users they would suit
<Cite id="chen2023biassurvey" />.
</Definition>

## How it arises and grows

A model that minimises loss on logged interactions learns that popular items are a safe prediction: they are relevant
to many users, so ranking them high is rarely wrong. Popularity itself is partly produced by the recommender, since
items it showed collected the interactions. Through the <NoteLink to="feedback-loops-in-recommendation">feedback
loop</NoteLink> the popular become more popular, a Matthew effect, and the long tail becomes invisible. Users are pushed
into mainstream filter bubbles; providers of niche items face an unequal field.

Popularity bias sits at the start of a causal chain: **data bias → unfair models → non-diverse recommendations**. Biased
data produce biased predictions; biased predictions allocate exposure unfairly across providers and user groups; unfair
allocation shows the same popular items again and again. A responsible system acts on all three links: causal methods on
the data, fairness-aware training on the model, and diversification on the output. See <NoteLink
to="fairness-in-recommendation">fairness in recommendation</NoteLink> and <NoteLink
to="diversified-re-ranking">diversified re-ranking</NoteLink>.

## Popularity as a confounder

Not all popularity is bias. Some items are popular because many people like them, and a recommender should use that. The
bias is the part of popularity that reaches clicks through exposure rather than through preference. In a causal graph,
item popularity $Z$ affects both which items are exposed and whether a user clicks, alongside the user–item match. A
model fitted to the click probability $P(C \mid U, I)$, for click $C$, user $U$ and item $I$, absorbs both paths.
The interventional quantity $P(C \mid do(U, I))$ blocks the path
through popularity. By the back-door adjustment (see <NoteLink to="causal-inference-from-observational-data">causal
inference from observational data</NoteLink>),

$$
P(C \mid do(U, I)) = \sum_z P(C \mid U, I, z)\,P(z).
$$

## PDA: deconfound, then adjust

Popularity-bias Deconfounding and Adjusting (PDA) makes this concrete <Cite id="zhang2021pda" />.

**Training.** Model the click probability as a product of a user–item match $f_\theta(u, i) > 0$ and a popularity term,

$$
P(C = 1 \mid u, i, m_i) \propto f_\theta(u, i)\cdot m_i^{\gamma},
$$

where $m_i$ is item $i$'s recent popularity and $\gamma \ge 0$ is learned. The popularity term explains the clicks that
popularity causes, so $f_\theta$ need not.

**Deconfounded ranking.** Substituting into the back-door formula,

$$
P(C = 1 \mid do(u, i)) \propto \sum_z f_\theta(u, i)\,z^{\gamma}P(z) = f_\theta(u, i)\sum_z z^{\gamma}P(z) \propto f_\theta(u, i),
$$

because the sum over $z$ does not depend on $(u, i)$. Ranking by $f_\theta$ alone removes the popularity effect.

**Adjusting.** Removing all popularity discards useful information, for example that an item is trending. PDA can
re-introduce a controlled amount at inference, ranking by $f_\theta(u, i)\,\tilde{m}_i^{\tilde\gamma}$ with
$\tilde{m}_i$ a forecast of the item's future popularity and $\tilde\gamma$ chosen by the operator. This balances
relevance against discovery.

## Counterfactual inference

Model-Agnostic Counterfactual Reasoning (MACR) estimates what a prediction would be if only item and user conformity
acted, with no user–item match. It trains a matching branch together with an item branch and a user branch whose outputs
multiply the match score. At inference it subtracts the counterfactual score, in which the match is fixed to a reference
value, from the full score, leaving the part due to the match. The method is model-agnostic: any recommender can supply
the matching branch <Cite id="wei2021macr" />.

## Measuring it

- **Popularity of recommendations.** The mean popularity of recommended items against that of items users actually
  consume.
- **Exposure concentration.** Gini or Herfindahl indices of exposure across items, and catalogue coverage. See
  <NoteLink to="beyond-accuracy-metrics">beyond-accuracy metrics</NoteLink>.
- **Accuracy by popularity bucket.** Accuracy on tail items separately from head items.

## Pitfalls

- **Debiasing to zero popularity.** Recommending obscure items at random hurts accuracy and trust. The aim is to remove
  the popularity caused by exposure, not to penalise popularity itself.
- **Evaluating on biased logs.** A debiased model looks worse on logged clicks, which favour popular items. Use
  <NoteLink to="unbiased-offline-evaluation">unbiased offline evaluation</NoteLink> or an online test.
- **Experiments can reward the bias.** An <Gloss name="ab-test" /> on <Gloss name="click-through-rate">click-through
  rate</Gloss> can favour a model that finds popular items better. See
  <NoteLink to="experimentation-biases-in-recommendation">experimentation biases in recommendation</NoteLink>.
