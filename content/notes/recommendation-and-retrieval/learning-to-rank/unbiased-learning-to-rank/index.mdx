<Definition>
**Unbiased learning to rank** (ULTR) learns a ranking function $S$ from logged clicks by minimising an
**inverse-propensity-scored** empirical risk,

$$
\hat{R}_{\text{IPS}}(S) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{d \,:\, c_{n,d} = 1}\frac{\ell\paren{S(x_n), d}}{\pi\paren{k_{n,d}}},
$$

where query $x_n$'s document $d$ was shown at rank $k_{n,d}$, $c_{n,d} = 1$ marks a click, $\ell(S(x_n), d)$ is the loss
the new ranker $S$ incurs on $d$, and $\pi(k)$ is the probability that a user examines rank $k$. Under the examination
hypothesis, $\hat{R}_{\text{IPS}}$ is an unbiased estimate of the risk that $S$ would have on true relevance labels
<Cite id="joachims2017ultr" /> <Cite id="wang2016selection" />.
</Definition>

## A counterfactual question

<NoteLink to="click-models">Click models</NoteLink> ask "what is the probability that this click means relevance?" and
estimate relevance per query–document pair, which needs repeated impressions. ULTR asks a different question: what
would the user have done under a different ranking? The ranking shown is a **treatment**, the click an **outcome**, and
the log is observational data from a policy that assigns treatments in a biased way, putting what it already believes
good at the top <Cite id="joachims2017ultr" />. The goal is to learn and evaluate a new
policy from that log.

The design is modular. A separately estimated examination model $\pi(k)$ re-weights the data, and any ranking model,
such as a ranking <Gloss name="svm" />, <Gloss name="lambdamart" /> or a neural network, is trained on the re-weighted data from query and document
features. Relevance is never estimated per pair, so ULTR works when queries do not repeat, where click models fail.

## Why the weighting is unbiased

Standard <NoteLink to="empirical-risk-minimisation">empirical risk minimisation</NoteLink> would minimise the true risk
$R(S) = \int\Delta(S(x) \mid x)\,dP(x)$, with $\Delta$ a loss computed from true relevance. Take losses that add up over
relevant documents, $\Delta(S(x) \mid x) = \sum_{d} \text{rel}(x, d)\,\ell(S(x), d)$, as for the rank of relevant
documents or <Gloss name="dcg" />. Relevance is unobserved; clicks are.

Assume the **examination hypothesis**: a document at rank $k$ is clicked if and only if it is examined and relevant, and
examination depends only on the rank, $P(E = 1 \mid k) = \pi(k)$ <Cite id="richardson2007clicks" />. Then for a document
of relevance $\text{rel}(x, d) \in \set{0, 1}$ shown at rank $k$,

$$
\expect\LB c_d\RB = \pi(k)\,\text{rel}(x, d).
$$

A click-based loss that counts each click once therefore estimates $\sum_d \pi(k_d)\,\text{rel}(x, d)\,\ell(S(x), d)$,
which down-weights relevant documents that happened to be shown low. Dividing each click by its propensity undoes this:

$$
\expect\LB\sum_{d \,:\, c_d = 1}\frac{\ell(S(x), d)}{\pi(k_d)}\RB
= \sum_d\frac{\expect[c_d]}{\pi(k_d)}\ell(S(x), d)
= \sum_d \text{rel}(x, d)\,\ell(S(x), d) = \Delta(S(x) \mid x),
$$

provided $\pi(k_d) > 0$ for every relevant document. Averaging over queries, $\expect[\hat{R}_{\text{IPS}}(S)] = R(S)$.
The loss $\ell(S(x), d)$ is evaluated at the new ranker's positions, but the weight uses the logged position, which is
where the click was generated <Cite id="joachims2017ultr" />.

This is the Horvitz–Thompson estimator of survey sampling: each sampled unit is weighted by the inverse of its
inclusion probability <Cite id="horvitz1952" />. It is also <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance
sampling</NoteLink>: data come from the logging distribution $\pi_0$ and the target is an expectation under another,
$\expect_{\pi_t}[\ell] = \expect_{\pi_0}[(\pi_t/\pi_0)\ell]$, here with a target in which every document is examined.

## The variance problem

Unbiasedness has a price. For one relevant document at rank $k$ the weighted click $c/\pi(k)$ is $1/\pi(k)$ with
probability $\pi(k)$ and 0 otherwise, so

$$
\var\LB\frac{c}{\pi(k)}\RB = \frac{\pi(k)}{\pi(k)^2} - 1 = \frac{1}{\pi(k)} - 1.
$$

The variance grows like $1/\pi$. A click at a rank examined with probability $0.001$ gets weight 1,000, and a single
lucky click can dominate the risk estimate and its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink>. The further the new ranker
moves documents from where the <NoteLink to="logging-policies-and-propensities">logging policy</NoteLink> put them, the more the estimate relies on such rare events. The figure
shows both effects: the naive click rate is biased by position, and the IPS estimate is correct on average but noisy for
items usually shown low.

Estimators trade some bias for variance:

- **Clipped IPS** caps each weight at $\tau$: $\min\set{1/\pi(k), \tau}$. Variance falls; the estimate is biased because
  the rarest, most informative clicks are down-weighted, and $\tau$ must be tuned <Cite id="bottou2013" />.
- **Self-normalised IPS** divides by the sum of the weights instead of $N$. It is biased but consistent, and usually
  more stable than IPS <Cite id="swaminathan2015b" />.
- **Doubly robust** estimators add a model of the outcome and use the weights to correct its errors. They are unbiased
  if either the propensities or the outcome model are correct <Cite id="dudik2011" />.

These are the estimators of <NoteLink to="off-policy-evaluation">off-policy evaluation</NoteLink> applied to ranks.

## Where the propensities come from

Everything rests on knowing $\pi(k)$. The options are covered in <NoteLink to="propensity-in-recommendation">propensity
in recommendation</NoteLink>: randomised swaps of results between ranks, the gold standard but costly; an <NoteLink to="expectation-maximisation">EM algorithm</NoteLink>
that fits a position-based click model to logs <Cite id="wang2018position" />; or the dual learning algorithm, which
learns the ranker and the propensity model jointly, each supplying the other's correction <Cite id="ai2018dla" />.

## Assumptions

- **Examination hypothesis.** Clicks factorise into examination by rank and relevance. Cascading behaviour, where
  examination depends on the results above, violates it.
- **Positivity.** Every relevant document has a positive probability of being examined at its logged rank. Documents
  never shown get no weight at all, which is a selection problem IPS cannot fix.
- **Correct propensities.** Errors in $\pi$ bias the estimate in proportion.

## Pitfalls

- **Weights that explode.** Deep ranks with tiny propensities make training unstable. Clip, normalise, or limit
  training to ranks with enough examination.
- **Clicks as negatives.** IPS corrects clicked (positive) documents. Unclicked documents are not evidence of
  irrelevance, and treating them as negatives reintroduces bias.
- **Evaluating on the biased log.** A debiased ranker scored with naive click metrics on the same log looks worse than
  it is. See <NoteLink to="unbiased-offline-evaluation">unbiased offline evaluation</NoteLink>.
