<Definition>
A **click model** is a generative model of user behaviour on a ranked list that separates two events for the document
$d$ at rank $k$: whether the user **examines** it, $E_k$, and whether the user finds it **attractive** or relevant once
examined, $A_d$. A click happens when both occur, $C_k = E_k \cdot A_d$. Click models differ in how they model
examination <Cite id="chuklin2015clickmodels" /> <Cite id="craswell2008cascade" />.
</Definition>

## Why clicks need a model

Clicks were hoped to be free relevance labels. Joachims et al.'s eye-tracking studies showed two biases
<Cite id="joachims2007clicks" />.

1. **Trust bias (position bias).** Users click higher-ranked results more, trusting that the engine put the best first,
   whatever those results' quality.
2. **Quality bias.** How good the whole list is changes how users click on each result.

Clicks are therefore relative judgements, not absolute ones. The first corrections were rules. **Click > skip above**
says that a clicked result is preferred to every unclicked result ranked above it, because the user saw those and
passed them over. Click models replace such rules with explicit probabilistic accounts of the user.

## The position-based model

The **examination hypothesis** says that examination depends on the position and attractiveness on the document and
query, independently <Cite id="richardson2007clicks" />:

$$
P(C = 1 \mid q, d, k) = \underbrace{P(E = 1 \mid k)}_{\pi(k)}\;\underbrace{P(A = 1 \mid q, d)}_{r(q, d)}.
$$

The **position-based model** (PBM) takes this as its whole model. The examination probability $\pi(k)$ is the
**propensity** of rank $k$.

**Estimating $\pi$ by swapping.** Show document $A$ at rank 1 to some users and, by random swap, at rank 5 to others.
Its expected click-through rates are $\pi(1)r_A$ and $\pi(5)r_A$, so their ratio is $\pi(1)/\pi(5)$: relevance cancels.
For example, 100 clicks at rank 1 and 20 at rank 5, from equal numbers of impressions, give
$\pi(1)/\pi(5) = 5$. Swaps between many pairs of ranks trace out the whole curve up to scale.

The PBM is flexible, since $\pi$ can take any shape. Its assumption is strong: examining rank 3 is equally likely
whether or not the user clicked ranks 1 and 2, and every document at a rank is equally likely to be examined.

## The cascade model

The **cascade model** derives examination from a behaviour. The user reads from the top, examines each result in turn,
clicks the first attractive one and stops <Cite id="craswell2008cascade" />. Rank $k$ is examined exactly when none of
the results above was clicked, so

$$
P(E_k = 1) = \prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\paren{1 - r_j}, \qquad P(C_k = 1) = r_k\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\paren{1 - r_j},
$$

with $r_j$ the attractiveness of the result at rank $j$. The examination probability of rank $k$ is a survival
probability: the chance of passing $k - 1$ results without stopping. If every result has the same attractiveness $r$,
then $P(E_k = 1) = (1 - r)^{k-1}$ and the rank of the click is <NoteLink
to="geometric-distribution">geometric</NoteLink>, the waiting time to the first success. With unequal $r_j$ it is the
waiting time in a sequence of independent trials with different success probabilities. See <NoteLink
to="survival-and-hazard-functions">survival and hazard functions</NoteLink>; in this view each $r_j$ is the hazard at
rank $j$.

The cascade model captures what the PBM cannot: examination of lower results depends on the results above them. It
implies at most one click per session, which is too strict for many settings.

## Richer models

- **Dependent click model.** After a click, the user continues with a rank-dependent probability instead of stopping,
  which allows several clicks. The number of clicks then parallels a negative binomial count.
- **Dynamic Bayesian network model.** Chapelle and Zhang separate attractiveness (does the snippet invite a click?) from
  satisfaction (did the page answer the query?). After a click the user is satisfied with probability $s_d$ and stops;
  otherwise they continue to the next result with a persistence probability $\gamma$ <Cite id="chapelle2009dbn" />.
- **User browsing model.** Examination depends on the rank and on the distance to the last click.

The progression runs from rules, through a single behavioural story, to stateful models of cognition. Each step fits
behaviour better and needs more data.

## Limitations

Click models estimate a relevance parameter per query–document pair from repeated impressions of that pair, ideally
under different rankings. Long-tail queries and personal search, where a query is seen once, give no repetitions. The
richer the model, the more data it needs. These limits motivated <NoteLink to="unbiased-learning-to-rank">unbiased
learning to rank</NoteLink>, which estimates only the examination model and leaves relevance to a feature-based ranker.

## Pitfalls

- **Confounding by the ranker.** In logs, relevant documents sit at high ranks. Fitting $\pi$ naively from logged CTR
  by rank attributes relevance to position and overestimates the drop-off. Use randomisation or a model that separates
  the two; see <NoteLink to="propensity-in-recommendation">propensity in recommendation</NoteLink>.
- **One model for all layouts.** A grid, a carousel and a list are examined differently. Estimate separately per
  layout and device.
