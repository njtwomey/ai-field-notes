<Definition>
A **Wide & Deep** model predicts the probability of a positive outcome, such as an app install, as

$$
P(y = 1 \mid \xvec) = \sigma\paren{\wvec_{\text{wide}}\transpose\LB\xvec, \phi(\xvec)\RB + \wvec_{\text{deep}}\transpose\avec^{(L)} + b},
$$

where $\phi(\xvec)$ are cross-product transformations of sparse features, $\avec^{(L)}$ is the last hidden layer of a
feed-forward network fed with embeddings of the sparse features and the dense features, and $\sigma$ is the logistic
function. The two parts are trained jointly <Cite id="cheng2016wide" />.
</Definition>

## Memorisation and generalisation

Cheng et al. framed recommendation as needing two abilities <Cite id="cheng2016wide" />.

- **Memorisation** learns that specific feature combinations co-occur with the label in the training data. A
  cross-product feature such as AND(installed = Netflix, impression = Hulu) is 1 only when both hold. A linear model
  over many such crosses memorises precise rules, for example that users who installed a game often install its
  sequel. It cannot score a pair of values that never appeared together, because the cross
  feature for that pair has no trained weight.
- **Generalisation** transfers to combinations not seen in training. An embedding per feature value and a network on
  top of the embeddings can score any combination, because similar values get similar embeddings. With sparse, high-rank
  interactions, such as niche users with specific tastes, this over-generalises and recommends loosely related items.

The wide part supplies the first and the deep part the second. The resulting pattern recurs: hybrid
architectures in which each component addresses one aspect of the problem.

## Mechanism

A cross-product transformation is a product of binary features,

$$
\phi_k(\xvec) = \prod_{i=1}^{d} x_i^{c_{ki}}, \qquad c_{ki} \in \set{0, 1},
$$

which is 1 exactly when every feature selected by $c_{k\cdot}$ is 1. The deep part embeds each categorical feature,
concatenates the embeddings with the dense features, and applies several ReLU layers.

**Joint training, not an ensemble.** The two logits are summed before a single sigmoid, and the gradient of the loss
flows into both parts at once. The wide part therefore only has to cover what the deep part misses, and needs only a
small number of crosses. An ensemble would train each part alone to full accuracy and average them. Cheng et al. used
FTRL with $\ell_1$ regularisation for the wide weights and AdaGrad for the deep part <Cite id="cheng2016wide" />.

## Google Play

The model ranked apps in the Google Play store, after a retrieval stage cut the catalogue to a candidate list. Each
categorical feature had a 32-dimensional embedding; the concatenated input of about 1,200 dimensions fed three ReLU
layers. The wide part crossed the user's installed apps with the impressed app. In a three-week online experiment with
1% of users per arm, Wide & Deep raised app acquisitions by 3.9% relative to the production wide-only model and by
about 1% relative to a deep-only model. Offline AUC barely differed: 0.726, 0.722 and 0.728
<Cite id="cheng2016wide" />.

## Legacy

Wide & Deep made the hybrid of a memorising component and a generalising network a template. Its weakness is that the
crosses are chosen by hand. <NoteLink to="deepfm">DeepFM</NoteLink> replaces the wide part with a
<NoteLink to="factorisation-machines">factorisation machine</NoteLink> that learns all pairwise interactions from
shared embeddings; the Deep & Cross Network replaces it with learned polynomial crosses.

## Pitfalls

- **Feature engineering in the wide part.** Choosing crosses takes domain knowledge and maintenance. Missing a cross
  leaves the memorisation to a deep part that is poor at it.
- **Offline AUC understates the online effect.** The Google Play result is a small AUC change with a clear online
  gain. Decide with an <NoteLink to="ab-test">online experiment</NoteLink>.
