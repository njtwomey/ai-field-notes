<Definition>
A **field-weighted factorisation machine** (FwFM) is a <NoteLink to="factorisation-machines">factorisation
machine</NoteLink> in which the interaction of features $i$ and $j$ is scaled by a learned weight for the pair of
fields they belong to:

$$
\hat{y}(\xvec) = w_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{m} x_iw_i + \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=i+1}^{m} x_ix_j\inner{\vvec_i}{\vvec_j}\,r_{F(i), F(j)},
$$

where $m$ is the number of features, $F(i)$ is the field of feature $i$, $\vvec_i \in \reals^K$ its embedding and
$r_{F(i), F(j)} \in \reals$ the interaction strength of the two fields <Cite id="pan2018fwfm" />.
</Definition>

## Motivation

In multi-field categorical data, such as ad impressions with user, advertiser and publisher fields, features from one
field interact differently with features from different fields. Pan et al. measured this directly: the mutual
information between field-pair conjunctions and the click label varies widely across field pairs <Cite id="pan2018fwfm"
/>. A plain FM gives every pair the same form, $\inner{\vvec_i}{\vvec_j}$. <NoteLink
to="field-aware-factorisation-machines">Field-aware FMs</NoteLink> capture the differences by giving each feature one
embedding per other field, which multiplies the parameter count by the number of fields. FwFMs are the memory-efficient
alternative. FwFMs keep one embedding per feature and move the field dependence into one scalar per field pair.

## Parameter count

Let $m$ be the number of features, $n$ the number of fields and $K$ the embedding size. Ignoring $w_0$
<Cite id="pan2018fwfm" />:

| model | linear | embeddings | field weights | total |
| ----- | ------ | ---------- | ------------- | ----- |
| FM    | $m$    | $mK$       | 0             | $m + mK$ |
| FFM   | $m$    | $m(n - 1)K$ | 0            | $m + m(n - 1)K$ |
| FwFM  | $m$    | $mK$       | $n(n - 1)/2$  | $m + mK + n(n - 1)/2$ |

Each FFM feature keeps $n - 1$ embeddings, one for each other field. An FwFM keeps one embedding and adds one scalar per
unordered field pair. Since $n \ll m$, the FwFM is about the size of an FM, and the ratio to an FFM with the same $K$ is

$$
\frac{m + mK + n(n - 1)/2}{m + m(n - 1)K} \approx \frac{1 + K}{1 + (n - 1)K}.
$$

For the Criteo data in the paper, $m = 399{,}784$ training features and $n = 26$ fields with $K = 10$ give
$4{,}397{,}949$ FwFM parameters against $100{,}345{,}784$ for the FFM, 4.4%. This is the source of the paper's headline:
FwFMs "can achieve competitive prediction performance with only as few as 4% parameters of FFMs" <Cite id="pan2018fwfm"
/>. On the Oath data, with $n = 15$, the same ratio is 7.8%.

## Results

On test sets, FwFMs beat logistic regression, Poly2 and FMs, and came close to FFMs, which scored best on training and
validation data but overfit more: test AUC 0.8614 against 0.8624 for FFMs on the Oath data, and 0.7764 against 0.7768 on
Criteo. When FFMs were shrunk, by a small $K$ and feature hashing, to the same number of parameters, FwFMs won by 0.70%
(Oath) and 0.45% (Criteo) AUC with the original linear terms, and by 0.92% and 0.47% with field-wise linear vectors
<Cite id="pan2018fwfm" />.

## Linear terms

Pan et al. also replaced the scalar linear term $x_iw_i$ with an inner product against the feature's embedding, so that
the linear part uses what the embedding has learned <Cite id="pan2018fwfm" />.

- **FwFM_LW:** the original scalar weights, $\sum_i x_iw_i$, with $m$ parameters.
- **FwFM_FeLV:** a vector per feature, $\sum_i x_i\inner{\vvec_i}{\wvec_i}$, with $mK$ parameters.
- **FwFM_FiLV:** a vector per field, $\sum_i x_i\inner{\vvec_i}{\wvec_{F(i)}}$, with $nK$ parameters.

The field-wise version fitted training data least well and generalised best, with the highest test AUC on both
datasets.

## Worked example

One impression with three active features in three fields: gender (male), advertiser (Nike) and publisher (ESPN). Take
$K = 2$, $\vvec_{\text{male}} = (1, 0.5)$, $\vvec_{\text{Nike}} = (0.8, -0.2)$, $\vvec_{\text{ESPN}} = (0.3, 1)$, linear
weights $w_0 = -1$, $w_{\text{male}} = 0.1$, $w_{\text{Nike}} = 0.3$, $w_{\text{ESPN}} = 0.2$, and field weights
$r_{G,A} = 1.2$, $r_{G,P} = 0.1$, $r_{A,P} = 0.6$.

- Linear part: $-1 + 0.1 + 0.3 + 0.2 = -0.4$.
- Inner products: $\inner{\vvec_{\text{male}}}{\vvec_{\text{Nike}}} = 0.7$,
  $\inner{\vvec_{\text{male}}}{\vvec_{\text{ESPN}}} = 0.8$, $\inner{\vvec_{\text{Nike}}}{\vvec_{\text{ESPN}}} = 0.04$.
- FwFM: $-0.4 + 1.2 \cdot 0.7 + 0.1 \cdot 0.8 + 0.6 \cdot 0.04 = 0.544$, a click probability of $\sigma(0.544) = 0.633$.
- FM, which is the FwFM with every $r = 1$: $-0.4 + 0.7 + 0.8 + 0.04 = 1.14$, probability $0.758$.

The small weight $r_{G,P} = 0.1$ says that gender–publisher pairs carry little signal here, so their large inner product
barely moves the score. An FFM would express the same by learning separate gender vectors for the publisher and the
advertiser fields.

## Low-rank FwFMs for fast serving

An FM scores many items for one user cheaply: by the linear-time identity, the sums over user and context features are
computed once per request, and each item adds work proportional only to its own fields. An FwFM breaks this, because
every field pair has its own weight: scoring costs $O(n^2K)$ per item, including all context–context pairs. Production
systems prune small field weights to cut the cost <Cite id="shtoff2024dplr" />.

Shtoff et al. instead constrain the field-interaction matrix $\Rmat \in \reals^{n \times n}$ (symmetric, zero diagonal)
to a **diagonal plus low-rank** form,

$$
\Rmat = \Umat\transpose\diag(\evec)\,\Umat + \diag(\dvec), \qquad \Umat \in \reals^{\rho \times n},\ \evec \in \reals^\rho,
$$

with $\dvec$ set so that the diagonal of $\Rmat$ is zero. A low-rank form alone cannot work: a plain FM has
$\Rmat_{\text{FM}} = \ones\ones\transpose - \eye$, whose eigenvalues are $n - 1$ and $-1$ repeated, so it has full rank,
but it is rank 1 plus a diagonal <Cite id="shtoff2024dplr" />.

Stack the active field vectors as the rows of $\Vmat \in \reals^{n \times K}$. The pairwise term is
$\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i, j}\inner{\vvec_i}{\vvec_j}R_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\trace(\Vmat\transpose\Rmat\Vmat)$. Substituting the
decomposition and writing $\Pmat = \Umat\Vmat \in \reals^{\rho \times K}$,

$$
\trace(\Vmat\transpose\Rmat\Vmat) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i\norm{\vvec_i}^2 + \sum_{r=1}^{\rho} e_r\norm{\Pmat_{r,:}}^2.
$$

Split the fields into context fields $\Ccal$ and item fields $\Ical$. Then $\Pmat = \Umat_{\Ccal}\Vmat_{\Ccal} +
\Umat_{\Ical}\Vmat_{\Ical}$, and both $\Umat_{\Ccal}\Vmat_{\Ccal}$ and $\sum_{i \in \Ccal} d_i\norm{\vvec_i}^2$ are
computed once per request. Each item costs $O(\rho\abs{\Ical}K)$: the number of context fields no longer matters
<Cite id="shtoff2024dplr" />.

On Criteo, Avazu and MovieLens, low-rank models matched or beat pruned models with the same number of parameters when
fewer than about 20% of interactions were kept. Deployed in an advertising system with 63 fields, 38 of them item
fields, a rank-3 model improved average inference latency per ad by 34.27% and the 95th-percentile ranking latency by
5.45% <Cite id="shtoff2024dplr" />.

## In neural ranking

Takiguchi, Twomey and colleagues used FwFMs as neural ranking models over document fields (query, title, description,
ingredients, country, image) for recipe search, and found that restricting interactions to query–field pairs beat
modelling all field pairs. See <NoteLink to="field-interactions-in-neural-ranking">field interactions in neural
ranking</NoteLink>.

## Pitfalls

- **Post-hoc factorisation.** Training a full FwFM and factorising $\Rmat$ afterwards can lose accuracy; Shtoff et al.
  train the low-rank form directly <Cite id="shtoff2024dplr" />.
- **Field weights are not importances.** A small $r$ can be offset by large embeddings. Inspect contributions, not
  weights alone.
