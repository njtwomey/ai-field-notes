<Definition>
**Generative retrieval** represents each item by a short sequence of discrete tokens, its identifier, and trains an
<Gloss name="ar">autoregressive model</Gloss> to generate the identifier of the item a user will want next, conditioned on the user's history:
$p(\text{id}_{t+1} \mid \text{history}) = \prod_{m} p(z_m \mid z_{<m}, \text{history})$ for identifier tokens
$z_1, z_2, \ldots$. Recommendation becomes decoding, not scoring <Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />.
</Definition>

## From discriminating to generating

A <NoteLink to="multi-stage-recommendation">multi-stage recommender</NoteLink> is discriminative: given candidates, it
assigns each a score and sorts. Generation collapses the pipeline. The model writes the identifier of the recommended
item as a <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> writes a word. At each step the next-token distribution covers every item whose identifier
begins with the tokens so far, so the whole catalogue is implicitly considered without a separate, heuristic candidate
stage. This could close the gap between complex models studied in
research and the efficiency-constrained funnels used in industry.

## Identifiers as language

The design question is what the tokens are.

- **Atomic IDs.** One token per item. The output layer is a softmax over the catalogue, which is what
  <NoteLink to="sasrec">SASRec</NoteLink> already does; nothing is shared between items.
- **Text.** The item's title. Readable and open to language-model knowledge, but titles are long, ambiguous and not
  unique.
- **Semantic IDs.** TIGER encodes each item's content embedding with a residual-quantised autoencoder into a tuple of
  codewords, such as $(12, 5, 201)$. The first codeword captures the coarsest distinction and each later one refines the
  residual. Similar items share prefixes, so the model generalises across them, and a new item gets an identifier from
  its content. A transformer encoder-decoder reads the semantic IDs of the user's history and decodes the next item's ID
  with <Gloss name="beam-search">beam search</Gloss> <Cite id="rajput2023tiger" />. See <NoteLink to="additive-and-residual-quantisation">residual
  quantisation</NoteLink>.

P5 casts many recommendation tasks, including rating, <NoteLink to="sequential-recommendation-models">sequential recommendation</NoteLink> and explanation, as text-to-text
problems for one language model, with items referred to by ID tokens in natural-language prompts
<Cite id="geng2022p5" />.

## Validity

A generator can produce a token sequence that is no item in the catalogue, the recommendation version of
hallucination. Constrained decoding with a prefix tree of valid identifiers rules this out. Without constraints, invalid
outputs must be filtered and replaced. Availability (in stock, licensed in the region) adds constraints that the
generator does not know.

## Diffusion recommenders

A related generative line treats a user's interaction vector as a noisy signal. DiffRec trains a denoising diffusion
model to reverse a noise process over interaction vectors or their latent codes and generates personalised distributions
over items. Early results show gains in diversity and coverage with competitive relevance, at a high sampling cost <Cite
id="wang2023diffrec" />.

## Pitfalls

- **Serving cost.** Beam search over identifier tokens with a large model costs far more than an approximate
  nearest-neighbour lookup. Latency limits model size.
- **Identifier drift.** Re-training the quantiser changes items' IDs, which invalidates the generator. Identifiers must
  be stable or both retrained together.
- **Popular prefixes.** Beam search favours high-probability prefixes, which can concentrate recommendations on popular
  regions of the catalogue.
