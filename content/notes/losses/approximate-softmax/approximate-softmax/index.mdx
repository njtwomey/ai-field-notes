<Definition>
A model that predicts one of $V$ classes from a state $\hvec \in \reals^d$ scores each class with $s_w =
\uvec_w\tr\hvec$ and normalises: $\pr(w \mid \hvec) = e^{s_w} / Z(\hvec)$ with $Z(\hvec) = \sum_{v \in \Vcal} e^{s_v}$.
The scores are independent of each other; only $Z$ couples them, and only $Z$ costs $O(Vd)$. An **approximate softmax**
is any of three ways to avoid that sum: estimate it from samples, replace it by a product of small sums, or train the
model so that it equals one.
</Definition>

## What the normaliser costs

Every logit is one dot product, so all $V$ of them are one matrix product: $Vd$ multiply-accumulates per prediction,
then $V$ exponentials and $V$ additions. Two regimes follow, and they explain why the same technique can be essential
or irrelevant.

In a <Gloss name="language-model">language model</Gloss> of the word2vec kind there is no depth to compare against
<Cite id="bengio2003" />. The model is an embedding lookup and one output head, so the normaliser is almost the whole
computation: with $V = 10^5$ the softmax is four orders of magnitude more work than the lookup it normalises.

In a transformer the head is one term among many. A layer of width $d$ costs about $12d^2$ multiply-accumulates per
token: $4d^2$ for the attention projections and $8d^2$ for a four-times-expanded <Gloss name="mlp" />. With $d = 4096$
and $V = 32{,}000$ the head is $131$M against $201$M for a single layer, so in a 32-layer stack it is about 2% of the
forward pass. The cost that still bites is memory rather than arithmetic: the logits for a batch of $B$ tokens are a
$B \times V$ tensor, and the backward pass needs it. At $B = 8192$ and $V = 128{,}000$ that is $10^9$ numbers, 4.2 GB in
single precision, for one tensor.

Nothing here is needed to pick a winner. Greedy decoding, beam search within a step and any ranking of the classes
depend on the logits only through their order, and $Z(\hvec)$ is one number for all of them. The normaliser is needed
when a probability's *value* is used: a training loss, a perplexity, a rescoring feature, a sampling temperature.

## The three families

Each family gives up something different, and the difference matters more than the speed.

- <NoteLink to="sampled-softmax-and-negative-sampling">Sampling the denominator</NoteLink> keeps the model exactly as
  it is and estimates $Z$ from $m \ll V$ classes drawn from a proposal $q$. With each sampled logit corrected to
  $s_j - \log(m q_j)$ the sampled sum is an unbiased estimate of $Z$; without the correction the objective is a
  different one, which is what negative sampling trains.
- <NoteLink to="hierarchical-softmax">Adding structure</NoteLink> approximates nothing. It replaces the flat
  distribution by a product of choices down a tree over the classes, which is normalised by construction, so training
  and scoring a known class cost $O(d \log V)$ and no estimate is involved <Cite id="morin2005" />. The model class
  changes, and with it the achievable fit.
- <NoteLink to="self-normalisation">Self-normalisation</NoteLink> keeps the model and the objective and gives up the
  guarantee that $Z = 1$, training it towards one instead. Scoring a known class then costs one dot product
  <Cite id="devlin2014" />.

| Family | What is approximated | Model class | Cost of scoring one known class |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sampling the denominator | the normaliser, from $m$ samples | unchanged | $O(Vd)$, or $O(md)$ while training |
| Adding structure | nothing | tree-factorised | $O(d\log V)$ |
| Self-normalisation | the constraint $Z = 1$ | unchanged | $O(d)$ |

The families compose. Adaptive softmax is a tree whose shape is chosen from the class frequencies and the hardware's
cost model <Cite id="grave2017" />; <Gloss name="nce" /> is a sampling method whose optimum is self-normalised
<Cite id="gutmann2010" />.

## Choosing

- **Training a model over a huge output space, and the full distribution is never needed.** Sample the denominator. It
  leaves the model alone, so the same weights can be scored exactly later.
- **Scoring one known class, many times, at test time.** Self-normalise, or use a tree. Both turn the $V$-way sum into
  work that does not grow with $V$.
- **The whole distribution is needed at every step**, for sampling or for an exact perplexity. Compute it. Every
  approximation here is either an estimate of $Z$ or a way of avoiding it, and neither gives all $V$ probabilities more
  cheaply than the matrix product does.
- **Rare classes must be modelled well.** Prefer sampling with a corrected proposal. A frequency-shaped tree spends its
  capacity on frequent classes, and a sampling proposal that ignores rare classes never scores them
  <Cite id="chen2016largevocab" />.

## When none is needed

Two changes have narrowed the problem since these methods were designed. Vocabularies stopped growing: subword
tokenisation holds $V$ between $32{,}000$ and $256{,}000$ whatever the language, so the head is a fixed share of a model
that has grown much deeper. And the memory cost, which was the real obstacle, is now removed by computing the loss in
chunks or in one fused kernel that never writes the $B \times V$ logits to memory <Cite id="wijmans2025" />.

What survives is the shape of the problem rather than the arithmetic. Retrieval over a catalogue of millions has no
subword trick available, so sampled softmax is standard there; and any model scored inside a search — a translation
decoder, a re-ranker — still wants a probability without a sum over the vocabulary.
