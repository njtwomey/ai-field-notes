<Definition>
**Sampled softmax** approximates the softmax cross-entropy over $M$ classes by comparing the target class with
$m \ll M$ negatives drawn from a proposal distribution $q$ over the classes. Each sampled negative $j$ enters the
softmax with the corrected logit $s_j - \log(m q_j)$, the **<Gloss name="logq-correction">logQ correction</Gloss>**,
which turns the sampled sum into an <NoteLink to="importance-sampling">importance-sampling</NoteLink> estimate of the
full normaliser <Cite id="bengio2008importance" />. **Negative sampling** replaces the softmax with independent binary
classifications: the target against each sampled negative, with no correction <Cite id="mikolov2013b" />. Both apply
wherever the output space is large: the vocabulary of a language model, the catalogue of a recommender.
</Definition>

## Problem

Many models end in a classification over a very large set of classes. A language model predicts the next word from a
vocabulary; a recommender or retrieval model predicts the item a user picks from a catalogue. In both, a context
$\xvec$ (the preceding words; a user, query or session) has scores $s_j = s(\xvec, j)$ for every class
$j = 1, \dots, M$, and the model defines

$$
\pr(j \mid \xvec) = \frac{e^{s_j}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sum_{k=1}^{M} e^{s_k},
$$

and training on a logged positive $y$ minimises the cross-entropy

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{full}} = -s_y + \log Z.
$$

The <NoteLink to="softmax-and-log-sum-exp">softmax</NoteLink> is the right model for "which one class comes next": the
next word, or the item the user picked. Its <NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> with respect to the scores is

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{full}}}{\partial s_j} = \pr(j \mid \xvec) - \indicator[j = y].
$$

Every class receives a push down equal to its current probability. The positive receives a push up
equal to $1 - \pr(y \mid \xvec)$.

The cost is the obstacle. When the score is a dot product $s_j = \uvec\transpose\vvec_j$ of $d$-dimensional vectors, as
in a language model's output layer or a <NoteLink to="two-tower-model">two-tower model</NoteLink>, computing $Z$ costs
$M d$ multiply-adds per example, forward and backward. Vocabularies hold $10^4$ to $10^6$ words; recommendation
catalogues hold $10^6$ to $10^9$ items. With $M = 10^8$ and $d = 128$, one example costs about $1.3 \times 10^{10}$
multiply-adds before any item-tower computation. The problem arose first in <NoteLink to="neural-language-model">neural
language models</NoteLink>, whose vocabularies of $10^5$ words made the output softmax the most expensive layer
<Cite id="bengio2008importance" />, and in neural machine translation, where Jean et al. used sampled softmax to train
with a very large target vocabulary <Cite id="jean2015sampledsoftmax" />. YouTube's candidate generator framed
recommendation this way, as extreme multiclass classification over millions of videos, and trained it with sampled
negatives <Cite id="covington2016youtube" />.

## Mechanism

### The logQ correction

The normaliser is a sum over all $M$ classes. Multiply and divide each term by $q_k$, where $q$ is any distribution with
$q_k > 0$ for every item:

$$
Z = \sum_{k=1}^{M} q_k \frac{e^{s_k}}{q_k} = \expect_{k \sim q}\brack{\frac{e^{s_k}}{q_k}}.
$$

Draw $j_1, \dots, j_m$ independently from $q$. The sample mean of $e^{s_{j}}/q_{j}$ is an unbiased estimate of $Z$:

$$
\hat Z = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \frac{e^{s_{j_i}}}{q_{j_i}} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \exp\paren{s_{j_i} - \log(m q_{j_i})}, \qquad
\expect[\hat Z] = Z.
$$

The estimate $\hat Z$ is an ordinary sum of exponentials over the $m$ samples, each
with its logit shifted by $-\log(m q_j)$. Substituting it for $Z$ gives the sampled softmax loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sampled}} = -s_y + \log\paren{e^{s_y} + \sum_{i=1}^{m} e^{s_{j_i} - \log(m q_{j_i})}}.
$$

The positive's own term is kept in the denominator so that the loss is a proper cross-entropy over $m + 1$ candidates
and stays non-negative <Cite id="blanc2018sampledsoftmax" />.

Bengio and Senécal introduced the sampled softmax estimator for neural language models, with an adaptive n-gram model
as $q$ <Cite id="bengio2008importance" />. Jean et al. used it to train translation models with vocabularies of 500,000
words <Cite id="jean2015sampledsoftmax" />.

A sampled item that coincides with the positive is an **accidental hit**: it is the positive scored as a negative.
Implementations usually mask such samples.

### What the uncorrected loss learns

A common shortcut drops the correction and uses the raw logits $s_{j_i}$ of the sampled items. The result is a
different estimator with a different optimum.

Fix the query and let $p_j = \pr(j \mid \xvec)$ be the true probability that the user picks item $j$. For large $m$,
the uncorrected denominator is dominated by the negatives, and by the <Gloss name="lln">law of large numbers</Gloss>

$$
\log\paren{e^{s_y} + \sum_{i=1}^{m} e^{s_{j_i}}} \approx \log m + \log \sum_{k=1}^{M} q_k e^{s_k}.
$$

Average the loss over positives $y \sim p$:

$$
\expect_{y \sim p}[\mathcal{L}] \approx -\sum_{k} p_k s_k + \log \sum_{k} q_k e^{s_k} + \log m.
$$

Set the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> with respect to $s_j$ to zero:

$$
-p_j + \frac{q_j e^{s_j}}{\sum_k q_k e^{s_k}} = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
s_j = \log p_j - \log q_j + c,
$$

with $c$ a constant shared by all items. The uncorrected loss learns the log ratio of how often the user picks the
item to how often the item is sampled. It subtracts $\log q_j$ from every score, so an item sampled 1000 times more
often than another loses $\log 1000 \approx 6.9$ nats of score. With the correction, the sampled term becomes
$q_k e^{s_k - \log q_k} = e^{s_k}$, the derivative is $-p_j + e^{s_j}/Z$, and the optimum is $s_j = \log p_j + c$,
the full softmax's answer.

When $q$ follows item popularity, the uncorrected model therefore demotes popular items. The learned score is a
<Gloss name="pmi">pointwise mutual information</Gloss> between query and item, not a log probability. A deployed
retriever that ranks by it under-recommends items that many users like. This is sometimes wanted as a crude
counterweight to <NoteLink to="popularity-bias">popularity bias</NoteLink>. It is then a choice to make explicitly,
for example by subtracting $\beta \log q_j$ with $0 \le \beta \le 1$, which leaves $(1 - \beta) \log q_j$ of the
popularity penalty in the learned score.

### In-batch negatives

A two-tower model trained on a batch of $B$ positive pairs $(\xvec_b, y_b)$ can use the other $B - 1$ positives' items
as negatives for each query. Their item vectors are already computed, so the negatives cost only the $B^2$ dot
products. The batch items are drawn from the training stream, so each is a sample from the item's frequency among
positives: the proposal $q_j$ is item $j$'s share of logged interactions. Popular items appear in almost every batch,
and tail items almost never.

Yi et al. apply the logQ correction to every logit in the batch softmax, $s^c_{bb'} = s_{bb'} - \log q_{y_{b'}}$,
including the positive's <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />. The constant $\log m$ cancels when every logit is shifted.

Frequencies in a stream change and a catalogue is too large to count exactly, so Yi et al. estimate $q_j$ online.
A hash function $h$ maps items to two arrays. $A[h(j)]$ holds the last training step at which item $j$ was seen, and
$B[h(j)]$ an estimate of the number of steps between its occurrences. When item $j$ appears at step $t$,

$$
B[h(j)] \leftarrow (1 - \alpha) B[h(j)] + \alpha \paren{t - A[h(j)]}, \qquad A[h(j)] \leftarrow t,
$$

with <Gloss name="learning-rate">learning rate</Gloss> $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, and the estimate is $\hat q_j = 1 / B[h(j)]$
per step <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />.

### Mixed negatives

In-batch negatives only contain items that appear as positives. Items that are rarely or never positives, often new
or tail items, are almost never pushed down, so the retriever can score them high for every query. **Mixed negative
sampling** adds $B'$ negatives drawn uniformly from the whole catalogue to the $B$ in-batch items
<Cite id="yang2020mns" />. The proposal is then a mixture of the frequency distribution and the uniform one, and a
logQ correction must use the mixture's probability for each item.

### Negative sampling and noise-contrastive estimation

Two binary relatives of sampled softmax come from language modelling.

**Noise-contrastive estimation** (NCE) trains an unnormalised model by classifying observed items against $K$ noise
items drawn from $q$ <Cite id="gutmann2010" />. The classifier's logit for item $j$ is $s_j - \log(K q_j)$, and the loss
is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NCE}} = -\log \sigma\paren{s_y - \log(K q_y)} - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log \sigma\paren{-\paren{s_{j_i} - \log(K q_{j_i})}},
$$

with $\sigma$ the logistic sigmoid. Mnih and Teh used it to train neural language models without computing the
normaliser <Cite id="mnih2012nce" />.

**Negative sampling**, the word2vec objective, is the same loss with the correction removed
<Cite id="mikolov2013b" />:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NEG}} = -\log \sigma(s_y) - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-s_{j_i}),
$$

with word2vec's noise distribution proportional to the unigram frequency raised to the power $3/4$.

The optimum of each follows from one fact about <NoteLink to="logistic-regression">logistic regression</NoteLink>. For
a fixed query, item $j$ appears as a positive with rate $p_j$ and as a noise sample with expected count $K q_j$. The
expected loss on item $j$'s logit $z_j$ is $-p_j \log \sigma(z_j) - K q_j \log(1 - \sigma(z_j))$. Its derivative is
$-p_j (1 - \sigma(z_j)) + K q_j \sigma(z_j)$, which is zero when

$$
\sigma(z_j) = \frac{p_j}{p_j + K q_j}, \qquad z_j = \log \frac{p_j}{K q_j}.
$$

For NCE, $z_j = s_j - \log(K q_j)$, so $s_j = \log p_j$: NCE learns normalised log probabilities, and it is a consistent
estimator <Cite id="gutmann2010" />. For negative sampling, $z_j = s_j$, so $s_j = \log p_j - \log q_j - \log K$. With
unigram noise this is the pointwise mutual information of word and context shifted by $\log K$, the result of Levy and
Goldberg <Cite id="levy2014" />. Negative sampling is therefore a classifier that learns association, useful for
<NoteLink to="word-embeddings">word embeddings</NoteLink> and not an estimator of $p$ <Cite id="dyer2014nce" />.

The four losses pair up. Sampled softmax and NCE carry the correction and recover $\log p_j$. The uncorrected sampled
softmax and negative sampling drop it and recover $\log p_j - \log q_j$ up to a constant.

## Worked example

A query has a positive with score $s_y = 1$ and three negatives $A$, $B$, $C$ with scores $0$. Item $A$ is popular,
with $q_A = 0.8$, and $q_B = q_C = 0.1$.

**Full softmax.** $Z$ over the negatives is $3$. The positive's probability is $e/(e + 3) = 0.475$ and the loss is
$0.744$ nats. Each negative is pushed down by $1/(e + 3) = 0.175$. The three negatives have equal scores, so they
receive equal pushes.

**Sampled softmax with $m = 1$, uncorrected.** The one sample is $A$ with probability $0.8$. Whichever item is drawn
gets the push $1/(e + 1) = 0.269$. The expected push is $0.8 \times 0.269 = 0.215$ on $A$ and
$0.1 \times 0.269 = 0.027$ on $B$ and on $C$. The ratio of pushes on $A$ and $B$ is $8$, the ratio of their sampling
rates, for every $m$.

**Sampled softmax with $m = 1$, corrected.** Drawing $A$ gives weight $e^{0 - \log 0.8} = 1.25$. Drawing $B$ gives
$e^{0 - \log 0.1} = 10$. The estimate of $Z$ is unbiased: $0.8 \times 1.25 + 0.2 \times 10 = 3$. The expected push is
$0.8 \times 1.25/(e + 1.25) = 0.252$ on $A$ and $0.1 \times 10/(e + 10) = 0.079$ on $B$. The ratio falls from $8$ to
$3.2$.

**Larger $m$.** Enumerating every multinomial draw gives the expected corrected pushes. At $m = 10$ they are $0.187$ on
$A$ and $0.160$ on $B$, a ratio of $1.17$. At $m = 100$ they are $0.176$ and $0.173$, a ratio of $1.02$, close to the
full softmax's $0.175$ each. The expected corrected loss is $0.611$ at $m = 1$, $0.725$ at $m = 10$ and $0.742$ at
$m = 100$, approaching $0.744$ from below.

## Cost

- **Full softmax:** $M d$ multiply-adds per example for the scores and again for the gradient, plus a forward pass of
  the item tower for all $M$ items when items have features.
- **Sampled softmax:** $m d$ per example plus the cost of drawing $m$ samples. One shared sample of $m$ negatives per
  batch is common: the $m$ item vectors are computed once and scored against all $B$ queries in a $B \times m$
  <NoteLink to="matrix-multiplication">matrix product</NoteLink>.
- **In-batch negatives:** no extra item-tower passes and $B^2 d$ multiply-adds per batch. The frequency estimate costs
  two array lookups per item.
- **Negative sampling and NCE:** $K d$ per example, with $K$ typically 5 to 20 in word2vec <Cite id="mikolov2013b" />.

## Trade-offs

**The number of negatives trades cost for bias.** The corrected estimator is unbiased for $Z$ but not for the loss,
because the loss takes the logarithm of $\hat Z$. By Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm,
$\expect[\log(e^{s_y} + \hat Z)] \le \log(e^{s_y} + Z)$, so the expected sampled loss lies below the full loss, as in
the worked example. A second-order expansion of the logarithm around $Z$ gives a gap of about
$\var[\hat Z] / (2 (e^{s_y} + Z)^2)$. Since $\var[\hat Z] = \var_{k \sim q}[e^{s_k}/q_k] / m$, the bias shrinks as
$1/m$. The gradient is biased in the same way, so for any proposal other than the model's own softmax the optimum of the
sampled loss matches the full softmax's only as $m \to \infty$ <Cite id="bengio2008importance" />.

**The proposal sets the variance.** The variance of $e^{s_k}/q_k$ under $q$ is zero when $q_k \propto e^{s_k}$, the
model's own softmax. Blanc and Rendle prove that sampled softmax gives an unbiased gradient for every $m$ exactly for
this proposal, and that the bias grows as $q$ departs from it <Cite id="blanc2018sampledsoftmax" />. A uniform proposal
rarely draws the high-scoring items that dominate $Z$, so its estimate has high variance. A popularity proposal draws
items that are often high-scoring, and in-batch negatives provide it without extra item-tower passes. Adaptive proposals
track the model: an n-gram model in Bengio and Senécal <Cite id="bengio2008importance" />, a kernel that approximates
$e^{s}$ and can be sampled in time sublinear in $M$, in Blanc and Rendle <Cite id="blanc2018sampledsoftmax" />.

**Popularity proposals demand the correction.** Without it, a model trained with in-batch negatives learns a score
that penalises each item by $\log q_j$, as derived above. Yi et al. report better retrieval with the correction than
without it on YouTube data <Cite id="yi2019sampling" />.

**In-batch negatives miss the tail.** Items that never appear as positives are never negatives. Mixed negative sampling
adds uniform samples at the cost of extra item-tower passes <Cite id="yang2020mns" />.

**Sampled softmax versus binary losses.** A softmax over the sampled candidates couples the negatives through the
normaliser: a negative is pushed down in proportion to its share of the probability mass, so the gradient concentrates
on high-scoring negatives. Negative sampling pushes each negative through its own sigmoid, independent of the others. Wu
et al. find that sampled softmax reduces popularity bias in item recommendation, and that cosine scores stop it from
learning <NoteLink to="vector-norms">vector norms</NoteLink>, which carry an item's popularity
<Cite id="wu2024sampledsoftmaxrec" />. See <NoteLink to="contrastive-losses-for-retrieval">contrastive losses for
retrieval</NoteLink> for the same loss with a temperature and normalised vectors.
