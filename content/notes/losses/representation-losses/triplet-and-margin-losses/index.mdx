<Definition>
An **embedding** $f: \Xcal \to \reals^d$ maps each input to a vector. Two inputs **match** if they share a label, for
example two photographs of the same person. A **margin loss** penalises $f$ whenever a non-matching input is not
sufficiently farther away than a matching one. The **pair loss** of Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun scores one pair at a time
and pushes non-matching pairs apart until their distance exceeds a margin $m > 0$ <Cite id="hadsell2006" />. The
**triplet loss** scores an anchor $a$, a positive $p$ that matches it and a negative $n$ that does not, and requires the
negative to be farther than the positive by a margin $\alpha > 0$ <Cite id="schroff2015" />.
</Definition>

## The two losses

Write $D(u, v) = \norm{f(u) - f(v)}$ for the <NoteLink to="pairwise-distances-and-cosine-similarity">Euclidean
distance</NoteLink> between two embeddings, and $[z]_+ = \max(0, z)$.

**Pair loss.** A pair $(u, v)$ has label $y = 1$ if it matches and $y = 0$ otherwise. The loss is

$$
L_{\text{pair}}(u, v, y) = y \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} D(u, v)^2 + (1 - y) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \brack{m - D(u, v)}_+^2 .
$$

A matching pair is pulled together with a force proportional to its distance. A non-matching pair is pushed apart only
while it is closer than $m$; beyond $m$ it contributes nothing <Cite id="hadsell2006" />. Hadsell et al. write the label
the other way round ($Y = 0$ for similar pairs); the loss is the same. Chopra, Hadsell and LeCun had used an earlier
exponential form of the same idea for face verification with a shared ("siamese") network <Cite id="chopra2005" />.

**Triplet loss.** For an anchor $a$, a positive $p$ and a negative $n$, the triplet loss of FaceNet uses squared
distances <Cite id="schroff2015" />:

$$
L_{\text{trip}}(a, p, n) = \brack{D(a, p)^2 - D(a, n)^2 + \alpha}_+ .
$$

It is zero exactly when $D(a, n)^2 \ge D(a, p)^2 + \alpha$. The loss is **relative**: it constrains the order of two
distances and not either distance on its own. Each identity's embeddings can spread along a manifold, so long as every
other identity stays at least $\alpha$ farther away. FaceNet preferred it to the pair loss for this reason: the pair
loss pulls every image of one person toward a single point <Cite id="schroff2015" />. FaceNet also normalises the
embedding, $\norm{f(x)} = 1$, and sets $\alpha = 0.2$.

## The margin and collapse

A **collapsed** embedding maps every input to the same vector, $f(x) = c$. It carries no information. A
<Gloss name="loss-function">loss function</Gloss> must not be minimised by it.

**Without the push term or the margin, collapse is optimal.** If the pair loss had only its first term, $f \equiv c$
would give $D = 0$ for every pair and a loss of $0$, the global minimum. If the triplet loss had $\alpha = 0$,
$f \equiv c$ would give $\brack{0 - 0}_+ = 0$ for every triplet: again the global minimum. A loss that only asks for
distances to be ordered, or only asks matches to be close, is satisfied by making every distance zero.

**With a margin, collapse costs a positive constant.** Under $f \equiv c$ every non-matching pair has $D = 0$, so each
contributes $\frac{1}{2}m^2$ to the pair loss, and every triplet contributes $\brack{0 - 0 + \alpha}_+ = \alpha$ to the
triplet loss. An embedding that separates the classes by the margin scores $0$. Collapse is therefore never a minimiser
when $m > 0$ or $\alpha > 0$.

**Collapse is still a stationary point of the triplet loss.** For an active triplet, where the bracket is positive,

$$
\frac{\partial L_{\text{trip}}}{\partial f(p)} = 2\paren{f(p) - f(a)}, \qquad
\frac{\partial L_{\text{trip}}}{\partial f(n)} = 2\paren{f(a) - f(n)}, \qquad
\frac{\partial L_{\text{trip}}}{\partial f(a)} = 2\paren{f(n) - f(p)} .
$$

These follow from $\partial \norm{\uvec - \vvec}^2 / \partial \uvec = 2(\uvec - \vvec)$. Every
<NoteLink to="gradient">gradient</NoteLink> is a difference of embeddings. At $f \equiv c$ all of them vanish, although
every triplet is active and the loss is $\alpha$. Near collapse the gradients are small in proportion to the distances,
so an optimiser that drifts toward $c$ receives little signal to leave. FaceNet reports that training on only the
hardest negatives can end in exactly this collapsed model <Cite id="schroff2015" />. The pair loss behaves differently:
the <NoteLink to="derivative">derivative</NoteLink> of its push term with respect to the distance is $-(m - D)$, which
tends to $-m$ as $D \to 0$. Two non-matching embeddings that are close but not identical are pushed apart with a force
near $m$, however small their distance.

**The margin needs a fixed scale.** Scaling an unnormalised embedding by $s > 1$ multiplies every squared distance by
$s^2$. Any triplet with $D(a, n) > D(a, p)$ is satisfied for large enough $s$, so the margin could be met by inflating
the embedding rather than by separating the classes. Constraining $f(x)$ to the unit sphere fixes the scale: squared
distances lie in $[0, 4]$, and $\alpha$ is a fixed fraction of that range <Cite id="schroff2015" />.

## Hard, semi-hard and easy negatives

For a given anchor and positive, a negative falls in one of three regions:

- **Hard**: $D(a, n)^2 < D(a, p)^2$. The negative is closer than the positive. The triplet is violated.
- **Semi-hard**: $D(a, p)^2 \le D(a, n)^2 < D(a, p)^2 + \alpha$. The order is right but the margin is not met. The loss
  is positive.
- **Easy**: $D(a, n)^2 \ge D(a, p)^2 + \alpha$. The loss and its gradient are zero.

The number of triplets grows as the cube of the number of training examples, and most of them are easy once training
has progressed. Sampling triplets uniformly therefore wastes most of each batch on zero gradients. **Online mining**
selects triplets inside each mini-batch. FaceNet put about 40 images per identity into each mini-batch of a few thousand
examples. It used every anchor-positive pair and, for each, a semi-hard negative
<Cite id="schroff2015" />. Hermans et al. proposed the **batch-hard** loss: for each anchor, the farthest positive and
the closest negative in a batch built from $P$ identities with $K$ images each <Cite id="hermans2017" />. Wu et al.
showed that, in high dimension, distances between random points on a sphere concentrate near $\sqrt{2}$. They sample
negatives with weights inversely proportional to that distance density, which spreads the chosen negatives over all
distances <Cite id="wu2017" />.

## Worked example

Embeddings lie on the unit circle, $\alpha = 0.2$, and the pair-loss margin is $m = 1$. The anchor is at angle $0°$ and
the positive at $40°$. For two unit vectors at angle $\theta$, $D^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta$. So
$D(a, p)^2 = 2 - 2\cos 40° = 0.468$ and $D(a, p) = 0.684$. The semi-hard band ends where
$D(a, n)^2 = 0.668$, which is an angle of $48.2°$.

| Negative at | $D(a, n)^2$ | Region    | $L_{\text{trip}}$ | Pair-loss term $\frac{1}{2}[1 - D(a, n)]_+^2$ |
| ----------- | ----------- | --------- | ----------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| $30°$       | 0.268       | hard      | 0.400             | 0.116                                         |
| $45°$       | 0.586       | semi-hard | 0.082             | 0.028                                         |
| $90°$       | 2.000       | easy      | 0                 | 0                                             |

The positive pair adds $\frac{1}{2}D(a, p)^2 = 0.234$ to the pair loss, whatever the negative. For the hard negative the
triplet gradient with respect to $f(n)$ is $2(f(a) - f(n)) = (0.268, -1.000)$; a descent step moves $f(n)$ in the
opposite direction, away from the anchor.

## Assumptions

- **Labels define similarity.** Matches come from shared class labels or from known pairs, such as two views of one
  object. The loss learns whatever notion of similarity the labels encode.
- **Unseen classes at test time.** Margin losses suit open-set problems, where new identities appear after training and
  are compared by distance, not classified into a fixed set of classes.
- **A fixed embedding scale.** The margin is meaningful only when the embedding norm is fixed, usually by
  $L_2$-normalisation.

## Pitfalls

- **Mining the hardest negatives.** The hardest negatives in a large dataset are often mislabelled or badly imaged
  examples. Training on them alone can collapse the model early in training <Cite id="schroff2015" />.
- **Margin units.** FaceNet's $\alpha$ is a margin on squared distances; Hadsell's $m$ is a margin on distances. The
  same number means different things in the two losses.
- **Benchmarks overstate progress.** Under a common protocol with the same network, embedding size and
  <NoteLink to="hyperparameter-search">hyperparameter search</NoteLink>, the 2006 pair loss scored MAP@R 26.53 on
  CUB-200 against 26.45 for <NoteLink to="proxy-and-classification-based-metric-losses">ArcFace</NoteLink> (2019) and
  23.69 for the triplet loss <Cite id="musgrave2020" />. Reported gains between margin-loss variants often reflect
  differences in training setup.
- **Contrastive learning uses another loss.** Self-supervised <NoteLink to="contrastive-learning">contrastive
  learning</NoteLink> usually trains with InfoNCE, a softmax over many negatives, rather than a pair or triplet margin
  loss.
