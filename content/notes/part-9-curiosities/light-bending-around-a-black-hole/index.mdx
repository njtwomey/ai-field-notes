<Definition>
Light passing a non-spinning (Schwarzschild) black hole of mass $M$ moves in a plane through the hole and follows the
**photon orbit equation** $\mathrm{d}^2u/\mathrm{d}\varphi^2 = 3u^2 - u$, where $u = 1/r$ is the inverse distance from
the hole in units of $GM/c^2$ and $\varphi$ is the azimuth in the ray's plane. A ray whose **impact parameter** $b$
(its offset from the hole far away) is below $b_c = 3\sqrt3 \approx 5.196$ crosses the event horizon at $r = 2$. A ray
with $b$ just above $b_c$ circles the **photon sphere** at $r = 3$ before it escapes. Every other ray is deflected by an
angle that tends to $4/b$ for large $b$. Seen from far away, these rules make the black hole's **shadow**, a sequence of
**photon rings** round it, **Einstein rings** of sources behind it, and the arched image of an accretion disc
<Cite id="luminet1979" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Gravity bends light. Far from a mass the bend is small: starlight grazing the Sun turns by 1.75 arcseconds. Near a
black hole the bend can be any size. A ray aimed a little off-centre turns through a right angle, a ray aimed closer
turns through half a circle and heads back, and a ray aimed closer still loops round the hole once or several times
before it leaves. A ray aimed closer than a critical distance never leaves: it spirals in through the horizon.

The picture of a black hole that many people know comes from the film *Interstellar* (2014): a black disc, a thin
bright ring hugging it, and a flat accretion disc whose far side appears to arch over the top and under the bottom of
the hole. Every part of that picture follows from the bending of rays. The black disc is the set of directions in
which an observer's line of sight, traced backwards, ends on the horizon. Its radius is $b_c \approx 5.2$, more than
two and a half times the horizon's radius of 2. The thin ring is light that circled the photon sphere. The arch is the
back of the disc, seen along rays that bend over the top of the hole and down onto it.

The film's black hole spins: its images were computed for a spinning (Kerr) hole with spin $a/M = 0.6$
<Cite id="james2015" />. A non-spinning (Schwarzschild) hole is simpler, because each of its rays obeys one
<Gloss name="ode">ordinary differential equation</Gloss> in one variable. Jean-Pierre Luminet published the non-spinning
picture, disc and shadow included, in 1979 <Cite id="luminet1979" />. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the
observed counterpart: a bright ring of diameter 42 microarcseconds round a dark centre in the galaxy M87, whose black
hole has a mass of $6.5 \times 10^9$ solar masses <Cite id="eht2019" />.

## Light orbits

### Units and conserved quantities

Lengths are measured in units of $GM/c^2$, where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $c$ the speed of light and $M$ the
mass of the hole: this is the choice $G = c = M = 1$. For a hole of one solar mass the unit is 1.48 km. The
Schwarzschild radial coordinate $r$ is defined so that a circle at radius $r$ has circumference $2\pi r$. The horizon
is at $r = 2$.

The geometry outside the hole is the same at every time and in every direction. These two symmetries give a light ray
two conserved quantities, its energy $E$ and its angular momentum $L$ about the hole, as they do for a planet in
<NoteLink to="kepler-orbits" />. The angular momentum fixes a plane through the hole, and the ray stays in it. In that
plane the ray's position is $(r, \varphi)$. The only combination that shapes the path is the ratio $b = L/E$, the
**impact parameter**: the distance by which the ray would miss the hole if it went straight.

### The effective potential for light

In general relativity a light ray in the Schwarzschild geometry obeys an energy equation of the same form as a planet's
in Newtonian gravity <Cite id="misner1973" /> <Cite id="hartle2003" />:

$$
\paren{\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}}^2 + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\paren{1 - \frac{2}{r}} = E^2,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \frac{L}{r^2}.
$$

Here $\lambda$ is a parameter that increases along the ray (the affine parameter, which plays the role of time). The
second term is an effective potential: the centrifugal barrier $L^2/r^2$ of Newtonian mechanics, multiplied by
$1 - 2/r$, which pulls it to zero at the horizon. Both equations follow from the Schwarzschild line element and its
two symmetries.

<Derivation title="The energy equation from the Schwarzschild line element">
The Schwarzschild geometry has the line element, in units $G = c = M = 1$,

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\paren{1 - \frac{2}{r}} \mathrm{d}t^2 + \paren{1 - \frac{2}{r}}^{-1} \mathrm{d}r^2
+ r^2 \paren{\mathrm{d}\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, \mathrm{d}\varphi^2}.
$$

Light moves along null paths, $\mathrm{d}s^2 = 0$. Put the ray in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$ and write dots for
derivatives with respect to the affine parameter $\lambda$. The coefficients do not depend on $t$ or $\varphi$, so the
geodesic equations conserve $E = (1 - 2/r)\,\dot t$ and $L = r^2 \dot\varphi$ <Cite id="hartle2003" />. Substituting
$\dot t = E / (1 - 2/r)$ into $\mathrm{d}s^2 = 0$ gives

$$
0 = -\frac{E^2}{1 - 2/r} + \frac{\dot r^2}{1 - 2/r} + \frac{L^2}{r^2}.
$$

Multiplying by $1 - 2/r$ gives $\dot r^2 + (L^2/r^2)(1 - 2/r) = E^2$.
</Derivation>

Divide the energy equation by $(\mathrm{d}\varphi/\mathrm{d}\lambda)^2 = L^2/r^4$ to remove the parameter:

$$
\frac{1}{r^4} \paren{\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\varphi}}^2 + W(r) = \frac{1}{b^2},
\qquad
W(r) = \frac{1}{r^2}\paren{1 - \frac{2}{r}}.
$$

The function $W(r)$ is the **effective potential for light**. A ray at radius $r$ moves only where $W(r) \le 1/b^2$, and
it turns round (its distance stops falling) where $W(r) = 1/b^2$. Substitute $u = 1/r$. Then
$\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}\varphi = -r^{-2}\, \mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}\varphi$ and $W = u^2 - 2u^3$, so the energy equation
becomes the **first integral**

$$
\paren{\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}\varphi}}^2 = \frac{1}{b^2} - u^2 + 2u^3.
$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $\varphi$ and divide by $2\, \mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}\varphi$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2u}{\mathrm{d}\varphi^2} = 3u^2 - u.
$$

This is the photon orbit equation. The impact parameter has dropped out: it returns only through the initial
condition. A ray arriving from infinity starts at $u = 0$ with $\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}\varphi = 1/b$.

### Comparison with Newtonian orbits

Written as $u'' + u = 3u^2$, the equation has the form of Binet's orbit equation $u'' + u = 1/h^2$ for a planet of
specific angular momentum $h$ in <NoteLink to="kepler-orbits" />. Light carries no rest mass, and the $1/h^2$ term,
which comes from the Newtonian attraction on a massive body, is absent. Without the right-hand side the equation is
$u'' + u = 0$, whose solution $u = \sin\varphi / b$ is a straight line at distance $b$ from the hole. The whole bending
of light comes from the term $3u^2$, which is the relativistic correction. For a planet the same correction adds
$3u^2$ to Binet's equation, and it makes the perihelion of Mercury precess <Cite id="hartle2003" />.

### The photon sphere and the critical impact parameter

The potential $W(r) = (1 - 2/r)/r^2$ is zero at the horizon, rises to a single peak and falls to zero far out. Its
derivative is $W'(r) = -2/r^3 + 6/r^4$, which vanishes at $r = 3$. The peak value is $W(3) = (1/9)(1/3) = 1/27$. The
radius $r = 3$ is the **photon sphere**: a ray with $1/b^2 = 1/27$ can circle the hole there, on a circular orbit of
light. The orbit equation agrees, since $u'' = 0$ at $u = 1/3$, where $3u^2 = u$. The orbit is unstable, because it sits
on a peak of the potential: a ray nudged inwards spirals into the hole, and a ray nudged outwards escapes.

The peak sets the **critical impact parameter** $b_c = \sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt3 \approx 5.196$. Two cases follow:

- A ray with $b > b_c$ has $1/b^2 < 1/27$. It meets the barrier at the outer root $r_0$ of $W(r_0) = 1/b^2$, its
  closest approach, and leaves. In terms of $r_0$, the impact parameter is $b^2 = r_0^3/(r_0 - 2)$.
- A ray with $b < b_c$ passes over the peak, never turns round, and crosses the horizon at $r = 2$.

A ray with $b$ slightly above $b_c$ turns round just outside $r = 3$, where the potential is almost flat, so it spends
many radians of azimuth near the photon sphere before it leaves.

### The horizon and the innermost stable circular orbit

The horizon at $r = 2$ is the surface from inside which nothing escapes. Massive particles obey a different effective
potential, and circular orbits of matter are stable only for $r \ge 6$ <Cite id="hartle2003" />. This radius is the
<Gloss name="isco" />. Gas in a thin accretion disc spirals slowly inwards through circular orbits until it reaches the
<Gloss name="isco" /> and then falls in, so the <Gloss name="isco" /> marks the inner edge of the disc
<Cite id="luminet1979" />.

### The deflection angle

A ray from infinity with $b > b_c$ sweeps the azimuth from the incoming direction down to its closest approach and the
same again on the way out, since the path is symmetric about the closest approach. A straight ray sweeps exactly $\pi$.
The **deflection** $\alpha$ is the excess:

$$
\alpha(b) = 2\int_0^{u_0} \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{1/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3}} - \pi,
$$

where $u_0 = 1/r_0$ is the smallest positive root of $1/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3$. Without the $2u^3$ term the integral is
$\arcsin(1) = \pi/2$ per half, and the deflection is zero.

Two limits give $\alpha(b)$ in closed form:

- **Weak field**, $b \gg b_c$: $\alpha \approx 4/b$ <Cite id="misner1973" />. In ordinary units this is
  $4GM/(c^2 b)$, which gives the 1.75 arcseconds for starlight grazing the Sun.
- **Strong field**, $b$ just above $b_c$: $b - b_c \approx 3.4823\, e^{-\alpha}$ <Cite id="luminet1979" />, so
  $\alpha \approx \ln\paren{3.4823 / (b - b_c)}$. Each extra turn of $2\pi$ round the hole needs $b - b_c$ smaller by
  the factor $e^{2\pi} \approx 535$.

<Derivation title="The weak-field deflection 4/b">
For large $b$ the term $3u^2$ is small, since $u$ stays near $1/b$ or below. Write $u = \sin\varphi / b + v(\varphi)$,
the straight line plus a correction $v$ of order $1/b^2$. To first order in $1/b$, $v'' + v = 3\sin^2\varphi / b^2 =
(3/(2b^2))(1 - \cos 2\varphi)$. A particular solution is $v = 3/(2b^2) + \cos(2\varphi)/(2b^2) =
(1 + \cos^2\varphi)/b^2$, so

$$
u(\varphi) = \frac{\sin\varphi}{b} + \frac{1 + \cos^2\varphi}{b^2}.
$$

The ray reaches infinity where $u = 0$. Near $\varphi = 0$ write $\varphi = -\delta$ with $\delta$ small: then
$-\delta/b + 2/b^2 \approx 0$, so $\delta = 2/b$. By symmetry the outgoing asymptote is at $\varphi = \pi + 2/b$. The
azimuth swept is $\pi + 4/b$, so $\alpha = 4/b$.
</Derivation>

## The shadow and the photon rings

An observer far from the hole sees light that arrives along rays ending at the observer. Traced backwards from the
observer, a ray with impact parameter $b < b_c$ ends on the horizon, so it carries no light from the sky behind. The
observer therefore sees a dark disc of radius $b_c \approx 5.196$ in impact-parameter units, the **shadow**. The shadow
is $b_c / 2 \approx 2.6$ times as wide as the horizon, because light bends towards the hole: rays that would have missed
the horizon by up to $3.2$ units still fall in.

A ray traced back with $b$ just above $b_c$ circles the photon sphere before it leaves, so it ends in a direction far
from the one it started in. Rays in a thin annulus outside the shadow reach every direction of the sky. A distant source
that a ray reaches by a deflection $\alpha$ is also reached by rays deflected by $\alpha + 2\pi$, by $\alpha + 4\pi$,
and so on, each looping once more round the hole. By the strong-field law the $n$-th of these images sits at

$$
b_n = b_c + 3.4823\, e^{-(\alpha + 2\pi n)}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$

<Cite id="luminet1979" />. Each image is $e^{-2\pi} \approx 1/535$ as wide as the one before it, so the **photon rings**
crowd onto the shadow's edge. For light sent back towards its source, $\alpha = \pi$: the asymptotic law puts the
outermost ring at $b_0 = 5.347$, against Luminet's exact $5.341$, and the next at $b_1 - b_c = 0.00028$
<Cite id="luminet1979" />.

## Einstein rings

A point source exactly behind the hole, on the line from the observer through the hole, is seen along every ray whose
deflection carries it from the observer's line of sight to the source. By the symmetry about that line the rays form a
cone, and the image is a ring, the **Einstein ring**. A source slightly off the line has two bright arcs instead, one
outside the ring and one inside it on the opposite side of the hole.

The ring's size depends on the distances. For a source at infinity and an observer at distance $r_{\text{obs}}$ from the
hole, a ray that leaves the observer at angle $\theta$ to the hole has $b \approx r_{\text{obs}}\theta$ for small
$\theta$. It reaches the source if its deflection turns it back onto the line, $\alpha \approx \theta$. With the
weak-field $\alpha = 4/b$ this gives $4/b = b/r_{\text{obs}}$, so

$$
b_E \approx 2\sqrt{r_{\text{obs}}}.
$$

For $r_{\text{obs}} = 60$ the estimate is $b_E \approx 15.5$. The exact sweep of the orbit equation from the observer
puts the ring at $b_E = 17.0$, mainly because the deflection near $b = 16$ exceeds $4/b$ by about a quarter.

### Ray tracing the sky

A picture of the lensed sky needs one ray per pixel. Spherical symmetry makes this cheap. Each backward ray stays in the
plane through the hole and its own direction, and its path depends only on $b$. So a table of one function suffices:
the azimuth $\Phi(b)$ that a ray sweeps from the observer to infinity. In flat space $\Phi = \pi - \theta$, and the
ray leaves in its starting direction. The figure builds the table once for 240 values of $b$, integrating from the
observer at $u = 1/r_{\text{obs}}$ inwards with an adaptive Dormand–Prince solver to the escape. The table is splined in
$\ln(b - b_c)$, the variable in which $\Phi$ is nearly linear near the shadow. Each pixel then takes four steps:

1. Its angle $\theta$ to the hole gives $b = r_{\text{obs}} \sin\theta / \sqrt{1 - 2/r_{\text{obs}}}$, the impact
   parameter of a ray leaving an observer at rest at radius $r_{\text{obs}}$.
2. A pixel with $b < b_c$ is in the shadow and gets no light.
3. Otherwise the spline gives $\Phi(b)$. The ray's direction at infinity is $\cos\Phi\, \evec_1 + \sin\Phi\, \evec_2$,
   where $\evec_1$ points from the hole to the observer and $\evec_2$ is the unit vector in the ray's plane
   perpendicular to it, on the pixel's side.
4. The pixel takes the brightness of the background sky in that direction.

## A thin accretion disc

Gas that falls towards a black hole with angular momentum settles into a thin disc
(<NoteLink to="why-rotating-clouds-flatten-into-discs" />), whose rings orbit at nearly Keplerian speeds
(<NoteLink to="keplerian-shear" />). Luminet's question was what a black hole surrounded by such a disc looks like
<Cite id="luminet1979" />.
The disc lies in the hole's equatorial plane, from the <Gloss name="isco" /> at $r = 6$ outwards, and the gas on it
moves on circular orbits. A distant observer views the disc at inclination $i$, the angle between the line of sight and
the disc's axis: $i = 0$ is face-on and $i = 90°$ is edge-on.

### Where a ray meets the disc

A pixel sits at impact parameter $b$ and position angle $\psi$ on the observer's image, measured from the horizontal
image axis, which lies in the disc's plane. Traced backwards, its ray moves in the plane spanned by the line of sight
$\ovec$ (the unit vector from the hole to the observer) and the image direction
$\evec = \cos\psi\, \evec_x + \sin\psi\, \evec_y$. Here $\evec_x$ and $\evec_y$ are the image's horizontal and vertical
unit vectors. At azimuth $\varphi$ along the ray, measured from $\ovec$, the ray's position points along
$\cos\varphi\, \ovec + \sin\varphi\, \evec$. Let $\hat{\zvec}$ be the disc's axis, so that
$\ovec \cdot \hat{\zvec} = \cos i$ and $\evec \cdot \hat{\zvec} = \sin\psi \sin i$. The ray is in the disc's plane where
the position has no $\hat{\zvec}$-component:

$$
\cos\varphi \cos i + \sin\varphi \sin\psi \sin i = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\cos\varphi_0 = \frac{-\sin\psi \sin i}{\sqrt{\sin^2\psi \sin^2 i + \cos^2 i}}, \qquad 0 < \varphi_0 < \pi.
$$

This is Luminet's relation <Cite id="luminet1979" />. The ray crosses the plane again at $\varphi_0 + \pi$,
$\varphi_0 + 2\pi$, and so on, until it escapes or falls in. At each crossing its radius is $r = 1/u(\varphi_n)$ from
the orbit equation with that $b$. The first crossing with $6 \le r \le 20$ lands on the disc, which is opaque, and gives
the pixel its light. A crossing outside that range lets the ray pass through the plane and go on.

- The **primary image** is the first crossing, $\varphi_0$. For a pixel above the hole ($\sin\psi > 0$),
  $\varphi_0 > \pi/2$: the ray passes over the hole and lands on the far side of the disc. Near edge-on, $\varphi_0$
  approaches $\pi$, and the far side of the disc appears as an arch over the shadow.
- The **secondary image** is the second crossing, $\varphi_0 + \pi$. For a pixel below the hole the ray first passes
  the disc's plane in front of the hole, misses the disc, goes under the hole and meets the underside of the far side.
  This is the arch under the shadow.

A figure that traced every pixel's ray would integrate tens of thousands of rays per frame. The figure instead
integrates 360 rays from infinity once, with impact parameters packed densely near $b_c$, and stores $u(\varphi)$ on a
grid of step $0.02$ in $\varphi$. A pixel's crossing radius is then a table lookup in $b$ and $\varphi$.

### Brightness and the redshift factor

Each element of the disc emits the flux $F(r)$ of a steady thin disc in the Schwarzschild geometry, the Page–Thorne
flux, which Luminet writes in closed form <Cite id="luminet1979" />. It is zero at the <Gloss name="isco" />, peaks at
$r = 9.55$ and falls as $r^{-3}$ far out.

The gas moves at a large fraction of the speed of light, so the observed light is shifted in frequency. Luminet's
redshift factor for gas on circular Keplerian orbits, seen at the image point $(b, \psi)$, is
<Cite id="luminet1979" />

$$
1 + z = \frac{1 + \Omega\, b \cos\psi \sin i}{\sqrt{1 - 3/r}},
\qquad
\Omega = r^{-3/2},
$$

where $\Omega$ is the orbit's angular velocity, the same as Kepler's in these coordinates, and $b\cos\psi$ is the
pixel's horizontal image coordinate. The factor $1/\sqrt{1 - 3/r}$ combines the gravitational redshift with the slowing
of the moving gas's clocks. The factor $1 + \Omega\, b \cos\psi \sin i$ is the Doppler shift: it is below 1 on the side
moving towards the observer and above 1 on the receding side. Write $g = 1/(1 + z)$ for the ratio of observed to
emitted frequency. The observed brightness is $g^4 F(r)$: one factor of $g$ for the energy of each photon, one for the
rate at which photons arrive and two for the change of solid angle <Cite id="luminet1979" />.

<Derivation title="The redshift factor of a circular orbit">
The ray's conserved energy $E$ and the $\hat{\zvec}$-component $L_z$ of its angular momentum fix the frequency that any
observer measures. A distant observer at rest measures $E$. An emitter on a circular orbit with angular velocity
$\Omega = \mathrm{d}\varphi/\mathrm{d}t$ measures $(E - \Omega L_z)\, \mathrm{d}t/\mathrm{d}\tau$, where $\tau$ is the
emitter's own time. For a circular orbit $\mathrm{d}\tau^2 = (1 - 2/r)\,\mathrm{d}t^2 - r^2 \mathrm{d}\varphi^2$ from
the line element, and $\Omega^2 = 1/r^3$, so $\mathrm{d}\tau/\mathrm{d}t = \sqrt{1 - 3/r}$. The ratio of emitted to
observed frequency is

$$
1 + z = \frac{1 - \Omega L_z/E}{\sqrt{1 - 3/r}}.
$$

At the distant observer the ray arrives along $\ovec$ at offset $b\evec$, so its angular momentum per unit energy is
$b\, \evec \times \ovec$. With $\evec_x$ in the disc's plane and the disc turning counter-clockwise about $\hat{\zvec}$,
the component along $\hat{\zvec}$ is $L_z/E = -b\cos\psi\sin i$, which gives Luminet's form. Spherical symmetry
conserves the whole angular-momentum vector, so the same expression holds at the emitter for both images.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

Units are $G = c = M = 1$: $b$ and $r$ in units of $GM/c^2$, angles in radians. The task is the deflection of the rays
with $b = 10$ and $b = 6$, from the integral, against the weak-field $4/b$.

1. **Closest approach for $b = 10$.** The turning point solves $1/b^2 = u_0^2 - 2u_0^3$, that is
   $0.01 = u_0^2 (1 - 2u_0)$. The smallest positive root is $u_0 = 0.11378$. Check: $0.11378^2 = 0.012946$ and
   $1 - 2 \times 0.11378 = 0.77244$, whose product is $0.010000$. The closest approach is $r_0 = 1/u_0 = 8.789$, 12%
   inside the straight-line miss distance of 10.
2. **The integral for $b = 10$.** The integrand $1/\sqrt{0.01 - u^2 + 2u^3}$ blows up at $u_0$. The substitution
   $u = u_0(1 - t^2)$, with $\mathrm{d}u = -2u_0 t\, \mathrm{d}t$, removes the singularity, and the midpoint rule on
   $t \in [0, 1]$ then converges. The azimuth swept is $2\int_0^{u_0} \ldots = 3.7320$, so
   $\alpha = 3.7320 - \pi = 0.5904$ rad $= 33.8°$. Integrating the orbit equation with Dormand–Prince to a relative
   tolerance of $10^{-10}$ gives the same digits.
3. **Weak field for $b = 10$.** $4/b = 0.4$ rad $= 22.9°$. The exact deflection is 1.48 times larger. At $b = 10$ the
   ray comes within 8.8 units, where the $3u^2$ term is not small next to $u$: $3u_0 = 0.34$.
4. **Closest approach for $b = 6$.** Now $1/36 = 0.027778 = u_0^2(1 - 2u_0)$, with root $u_0 = 0.22455$, so
   $r_0 = 4.453$. The ray clears the photon sphere at $r = 3$ by 1.45 units.
5. **The integral for $b = 6$.** The azimuth swept is $4.8610$, so $\alpha = 1.7194$ rad $= 98.5°$: the ray leaves at
   more than a right angle to its arrival. The weak-field value is $4/6 = 0.667$ rad $= 38.2°$, 2.6 times too small.
6. **Strong field for $b = 6$.** $b - b_c = 6 - 5.1962 = 0.8038$, and $\ln(3.4823/0.8038) = \ln 4.332 = 1.466$ rad, 15%
   below the exact value. The logarithm is good only close to $b_c$: at $b = 5.2$ it gives $6.808$ against the exact
   $6.810$, a deflection of more than one full turn.

## Assumptions

- **No spin and no charge.** The hole is Schwarzschild. A spinning hole drags light round with it: its shadow is
  flattened on one side and its photon orbits depend on the direction of travel <Cite id="james2015" />.
- **Geometric optics.** Light travels along rays. Wave effects matter only for wavelengths comparable to the hole's
  size.
- **A thin, opaque disc on circular orbits.** The disc has no thickness, emits from its surface, and absorbs any ray
  that hits it. Its gas follows circular geodesics, which needs $r \ge 6$.
- **Static, distant observers.** The lensed sky assumes an observer at rest at $r_{\text{obs}} = 60$. The disc image
  assumes an observer far enough away that the image coordinates are the impact parameter and position angle. A moving
  observer would see the sky aberrated and Doppler-shifted as well.

## Pitfalls

- **The shadow is not the horizon.** The dark disc has radius $b_c \approx 5.196$, not 2. It is the set of lines of
  sight that end on the horizon, magnified by the bending.
- **The photon sphere is unstable.** Light does not collect at $r = 3$. Rays near $b_c$ spend a few turns there and
  leave or fall in, and the time they spend grows only as the logarithm of $1/(b - b_c)$.
- **$4/b$ is a weak-field law.** It is 32% low at $b = 10$ and fails completely near $b_c$, where the deflection
  diverges. The Sun's 1.75 arcseconds is $4/b$ at $b \approx 4.7 \times 10^5$, the Sun's radius in units of
  $GM_\odot/c^2$.
- **Tables near $b_c$ need care.** The deflection diverges as $-\ln(b - b_c)$, so a table evenly spaced in $b$ misses
  the photon rings. The figures space the table in $\ln(b - b_c)$ or in $\sinh^{-1}((b - b_c)/0.02)$, which puts most
  entries near $b_c$.
- **Sequential colour hides the dynamic range.** With the frequency shift on, the disc's brightness spans a factor of
  more than 10 between its two sides, and the receding side almost disappears on a linear scale. The disc figure offers
  a square-root scale for this reason.
