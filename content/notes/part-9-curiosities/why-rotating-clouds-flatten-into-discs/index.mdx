<Definition>
A rotating cloud of gas or colliding particles around a central mass flattens into a disc when collisions remove energy
faster than they move angular momentum from one part of the cloud to another. Collisions conserve the cloud's total
angular momentum $\Lvec$. Among all orbits with a given angular momentum about the axis of $\Lvec$, the circular orbit
in the plane perpendicular to $\Lvec$ has the lowest energy. A cloud that has radiated all the energy it can while each
element keeps its angular momentum about that axis is therefore a thin disc of circular orbits, each at the radius
its angular momentum sets.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Discs are among the commonest shapes in astronomy. Saturn's rings, the gas and dust around young stars, the gas falling
onto white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes, and the stars of spiral galaxies all lie in thin rotating discs. Each
began as a lumpier, rounder distribution of matter with some net rotation.

The velocity of a particle orbiting a central mass splits into three parts, taken about the spin axis $z$ (the
direction of $\Lvec$): radial (towards or away from the axis), azimuthal (around the axis) and vertical (along the
axis). Only the azimuthal part contributes to the angular momentum about $z$. The radial and vertical parts carry
kinetic energy and no angular momentum about $z$.

In a round cloud the orbits lie in many planes and cross each other. Where streams of matter cross, they collide. Gas
shocks and radiates the heat; solid particles bounce inelastically. A collision removes the relative velocity of the
two colliding pieces. Matter above the midplane falling down meets matter below rising up, so vertical motion is
removed at every crossing of the midplane. Removing vertical or radial motion costs no angular momentum about $z$,
so nothing stops it.

Removing azimuthal motion is different. A piece of the cloud can lose azimuthal velocity only by handing its angular
momentum to a neighbour, because the total is conserved. Neighbours on the same circular orbit move together and no
longer collide. The only remaining contact is between neighbouring rings that slide past each other, and that friction
acts slowly. The cloud therefore loses its thickness quickly and its size slowly: it becomes a pancake.

## Details

### Collisions conserve angular momentum

Two particles $i$ and $j$ of masses $m_i$ and $m_j$ at positions $\rvec_i$ and $\rvec_j$ (length units) collide and
exchange equal and opposite impulses $\Jvec$ and $-\Jvec$ (momentum units). For smooth spheres the impulse acts along
the line joining their centres, so $\Jvec$ is parallel to $\rvec_i - \rvec_j$. The total angular momentum about the
origin changes by

$$
\Delta \Lvec = \rvec_i \times \Jvec + \rvec_j \times (-\Jvec) = (\rvec_i - \rvec_j) \times \Jvec = \zeros,
$$

because the cross product of parallel vectors is zero. The total momentum changes by $\Jvec - \Jvec = \zeros$. The
kinetic energy of the pair's relative motion along the line of centres falls, by the fraction set by how inelastic the
collision is. The lost energy becomes heat, and a gas radiates it away.

The central mass pulls each particle along $-\rvec_i$, so its force exerts no torque about the origin either. A cloud of
colliding particles in a fixed central potential therefore keeps $\Lvec$ fixed while its energy falls.

### The lowest energy at fixed angular momentum

A particle of unit mass moves in a central potential $\Phi(r)$, with $r = \norm{\rvec}$ the distance from the centre
(energy per unit mass). Its specific angular momentum is $\hvec = \rvec \times \vvec$, with $\vvec$ the velocity and
$h = \norm{\hvec}$. The velocity splits into the radial speed $v_r = \dot r$ and the tangential speed
$v_t = h / r$. The specific energy is

$$
E = \tfrac12 v_r^2 + \frac{h^2}{2 r^2} + \Phi(r).
$$

The axis $\hat{\zvec}$ points along $\Lvec$. The angular momentum splits into $h_z = \hvec \cdot \hat{\zvec}$, about the
spin axis, and the remainder $h_\perp$ perpendicular to it, with $h^2 = h_z^2 + h_\perp^2$. The orbit's inclination $i$
to the plane perpendicular to $\hat{\zvec}$ satisfies $\cos i = h_z / h$. Substituting,

$$
E = \tfrac12 v_r^2 + \frac{h_\perp^2}{2 r^2} + \Phi_{\text{eff}}(r),
\qquad
\Phi_{\text{eff}}(r) = \frac{h_z^2}{2 r^2} + \Phi(r).
$$

The term $h_\perp^2 / (2r^2)$ is the kinetic energy of the motion that tilts the orbit. For a particle crossing the
midplane, $h_\perp = r \abs{v_z}$ and the term equals $\tfrac12 v_z^2$: it is the vertical motion.

Hold $h_z$ fixed. The first two terms are non-negative, and they vanish only when $v_r = 0$ (no radial motion) and
$h_\perp = 0$ (the orbit lies in the plane perpendicular to $\Lvec$). The third term is smallest at the radius $r_c$
where $\Phi_{\text{eff}}'(r_c) = 0$:

$$
\frac{h_z^2}{r_c^3} = \Phi'(r_c)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
h_z = r_c\, v_c(r_c),
\qquad v_c(r) = \sqrt{r\, \Phi'(r)}.
$$

The speed $v_c(r)$ is the circular speed, at which gravity supplies exactly the centripetal acceleration. So the orbit
of least energy with a given $h_z$ is the circular orbit in the plane perpendicular to $\Lvec$, at the radius where a
circular orbit has that angular momentum <Cite id="pringle1981" />. Its energy is
$E_{\min}(h_z) = \tfrac12 v_c(r_c)^2 + \Phi(r_c)$.

### Why each element keeps its angular momentum about the axis

The minimum above holds $h_z$ fixed for each element, while collisions only conserve the total. The impulses that
remove vertical and radial motion leave $h_z$ alone. A vertical impulse $J \hat{\zvec}$ changes $\hvec$ by
$\rvec \times J \hat{\zvec}$, which is perpendicular to $\hat{\zvec}$. A radial impulse along $\rvec$ changes $\hvec$
by $\rvec \times \rvec = \zeros$. Only an azimuthal impulse changes $h_z$, and azimuthal impulses come from the shear
between neighbouring rings.

The vertical impulses do change $h_\perp$, the tilt of each orbit. The tilts of different elements point in different
directions and sum to zero, because $\Lvec$ lies along $\hat{\zvec}$. Collisions can therefore remove every element's
$h_\perp$ without changing $\Lvec$.

So the fast collisions, which damp the crossing streams, take each element to the minimum at its own $h_z$. Each gas
element "rids itself of as much energy as it can by colliding with other gas elements, shock heating and radiatively
cooling, while retaining its angular momentum" <Cite id="pringle1981" />.

### The Kepler case in closed form

For a point mass in units with $G = M = 1$, $\Phi(r) = -1/r$. An orbit with semi-major axis $a$ (length units),
eccentricity $e$ (dimensionless, $0 \le e < 1$) and inclination $i$ has

$$
E = -\frac{1}{2a},
\qquad
h = \sqrt{a(1 - e^2)},
\qquad
h_z = h \cos i,
$$

as derived in <NoteLink to="kepler-orbits">Kepler orbits</NoteLink>. Eliminating $a$ with $a = h_z^2 / ((1 - e^2)
\cos^2 i)$ gives the energy as a function of the orbit's shape at fixed $h_z$:

$$
E = -\frac{(1 - e^2) \cos^2 i}{2 h_z^2}.
$$

Both factors in the numerator are at most 1 and equal 1 only at $e = 0$ and $i = 0$. The minimum is
$E_{\min} = -1/(2 h_z^2)$, the circular orbit at $r_c = h_z^2$ in the plane perpendicular to $\Lvec$. Settling from
$(e, i)$ to that orbit releases

$$
\Delta E = \frac{1 - (1 - e^2) \cos^2 i}{2 h_z^2}
$$

per unit mass.

### Why the radius holds, for a while

Once vertical and radial motion are gone, a disc of circular orbits still has energy to release. In a Kepler disc a
circular orbit at radius $R$ has $h = \sqrt{R}$, which rises outwards. Moving most of the mass inwards and a little
outwards, with the outer mass carrying the angular momentum, lowers the energy at fixed $\Lvec$. Carried to the end,
"all of the matter initially in the ring ends up at the origin and all of the angular momentum is carried to infinite
radius by none of the mass" <Cite id="pringle1981" />.

That redistribution needs azimuthal forces between neighbouring rings, which the shear provides through viscosity. It
acts on the viscous time $t_\nu \sim R^2 / \nu$, with $\nu$ the kinematic viscosity (length² per time units), which for
a thin disc is far longer than the orbital period <Cite id="frank2002" />. The disc therefore keeps its radius over
many orbits and spreads only slowly, as <NoteLink to="viscous-spreading-of-an-accretion-ring" /> describes. The
shearing flow itself is the subject of <NoteLink to="keplerian-shear" />.

## Worked example

A particle orbits a central mass in units with $G = M = 1$ (lengths in units of a reference radius $r_0$, times in
units of $\sqrt{r_0^3 / GM}$, energies per unit mass in units of $GM / r_0$). Its orbit has semi-major axis $a = 1$,
eccentricity $e = 0.6$ and inclination $i = 30°$ to the plane perpendicular to the cloud's $\Lvec$.

1. **Angular momentum.** $h = \sqrt{a(1 - e^2)} = \sqrt{0.64} = 0.8$. About the spin axis,
   $h_z = 0.8 \cos 30° = 0.8 \times 0.8660 = 0.6928$, so $h_z^2 = 0.48$.
2. **Starting energy.** $E = -1/(2a) = -0.5$. The closed form agrees:
   $-(0.64)(0.75)/(2 \times 0.48) = -0.48/0.96 = -0.5$.
3. **Remove the radial motion.** Collisions that damp the eccentricity keep $h = 0.8$. The circular orbit with $h = 0.8$
   has radius $h^2 = 0.64$ and energy $-1/(2 \times 0.64) = -0.7813$. This step releases $0.2813$.
4. **Remove the tilt.** Collisions that damp the vertical motion keep $h_z = 0.6928$ and remove $h_\perp$. The circular
   orbit in the plane with $h = h_z$ has radius $r_c = h_z^2 = 0.48$ and energy $-1/(2 \times 0.48) = -1.0417$. This
   step releases $0.2604$.
5. **Check.** The formula for $\Delta E$ gives $(1 - 0.64 \times 0.75)/(2 \times 0.48) = 0.52/0.96 = 0.5417$, the sum of
   steps 3 and 4.
6. **The speed.** On the final orbit $v_c = 1/\sqrt{0.48} = 1.443$, and $r_c v_c = 0.48 \times 1.443 = 0.6928 = h_z$, as
   required.

The settling releases $0.5417$ per unit mass, more than the orbit's starting binding energy of $0.5$. The particle ends
on a circular orbit of radius $0.48$, inside its starting semi-major axis. It goes no further in, because a smaller
circular orbit in the plane has less angular momentum than $h_z = 0.6928$, and collisions that remove vertical and
radial motion cannot take any away.

## A toy cloud

The figure follows a cloud of $n$ test particles of unit mass around a central mass with $G = M = 1$. The particles do
not attract each other. The central potential is softened, $\Phi(r) = -1/\sqrt{r^2 + \varepsilon^2}$ with
$\varepsilon = 0.2$, so that particles that fall to the centre stay in a finite core instead of forcing tiny time steps.
The particles start uniformly in a spherical shell between radii $0.5$ and $1.5$. Each rotates about $z$ with velocity
$s\, v_c(r)\, (\hat{\zvec} \times \rvec)/r$, where $s$ is the spin (0 to 1). Each also has a random velocity whose
components are Gaussian with standard deviation $0.3\, v_c(r)$. A total speed above 0.9 of the escape speed
$\sqrt{-2\Phi(r)}$ is scaled down to it, so that every particle is bound.

Inelastic collisions are modelled as a drag between neighbours. For every unordered pair $(i, j)$ among each particle's
8 <NoteLink to="nearest-neighbours">nearest neighbours</NoteLink>, the pair exchanges momentum at rate $\gamma$ (per
time unit) along the line joining them:

$$
\dot{\vvec}_i \mathrel{+}= -\gamma\, \frac{(\vvec_i - \vvec_j) \cdot (\rvec_i - \rvec_j)}{\norm{\rvec_i - \rvec_j}^2}\,
(\rvec_i - \rvec_j),
\qquad
\dot{\vvec}_j \mathrel{-}= \text{the same}.
$$

The drag removes the part of the pair's relative velocity along the line of centres, as a collision between smooth
spheres does. Each pair's two forces are equal, opposite and parallel to $\rvec_i - \rvec_j$, so by the argument above
they conserve $\Lvec$ exactly. A drag towards the neighbours' full mean velocity, $\gamma(\vvec_j - \vvec_i)$, would
not: on each pair it exerts the torque $(\rvec_i - \rvec_j) \times \gamma (\vvec_j - \vvec_i)$, which is not zero when
neighbours shear past each other.

The run integrates the equations of motion with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method. The neighbour pairs are
found with a $k$-d tree every 4 steps and held fixed within each step, so every stage of the method sees torque-free
drag. Three diagnostics follow the run:

- the energy per particle, $E = \frac1n \sum_i \big(\tfrac12 \norm{\vvec_i}^2 + \Phi(r_i)\big)$;
- the angular momentum per particle, $\Lvec / n = \frac1n \sum_i \rvec_i \times \vvec_i$;
- the axis ratio $c/a = \sqrt{\lambda_3 / \lambda_1}$, where $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3$ are the eigenvalues
  of the second-moment tensor $\Smat = \frac1n \sum_i \rvec_i \rvec_i^\top$. A round cloud has $c/a$ near 1 and a thin
  disc has $c/a$ near 0.

At the defaults ($n = 400$, $s = 0.8$, $\gamma = 0.3$, $h = 0.025$, 800 steps to $t = 20$), the orbital period at radius
1 is $2\pi \approx 6.3$. The axis ratio falls from $0.96$ to $0.20$ by $t = 4$ and to $0.10$ by $t = 10$. The energy per
particle falls from $-0.61$ to $-1.56$ by $t = 4$. The rms radius in the plane first shrinks from $0.96$ to $0.58$,
because each particle settles at the radius $h_z^2$ its angular momentum sets. A particle that starts at radius $r$ and
polar angle $\theta$ has $h_z \approx s \sqrt{r} \sin^2\theta$, so its circular radius $s^2 r \sin^4\theta$ lies inside
its starting radius, and particles near the poles fall almost to the centre. After $t \approx 5$ the disc spreads
slowly outwards (rms radius $1.20$ at $t = 20$) while its energy keeps falling: the drag between sliding rings acts as a
viscosity. The relative change in $\abs{\Lvec}$ by $t = 20$ is about $5 \times 10^{-5}$, all of it the integrator's
truncation error; the net torque of the drag is zero to rounding (about $10^{-18}$).

The two controls isolate the ingredients. With $s = 0$ the cloud has almost no angular momentum, every particle's
$h_z$ is near zero, and the cloud shrinks into the softened core (rms radius $0.04$ by $t = 4$). With $\gamma = 0$ the
energy stays at $-0.61$, the orbits only phase-mix, and the axis ratio stays near $0.6$.

## Assumptions

- **Dissipation outpaces redistribution.** The argument needs collisions to remove vertical and radial motion faster
  than viscosity moves angular momentum between rings. A thin disc satisfies this, since its viscous time is many
  orbital periods.
- **The energy can leave.** A gas must radiate the heat of its collisions. A gas that cannot cool keeps its thermal
  pressure, which holds the disc thick.
- **Collisions happen at all.** Stars in a galaxy almost never collide, and gravitational encounters between them
  relax their orbits only over times far longer than the age of a large galaxy <Cite id="binney2008" />. A stellar
  system keeps its random motions, which is why elliptical galaxies stay three-dimensional while the gas in a galaxy
  settles into a disc.

## Pitfalls

- **The disc is not the state of lowest energy at fixed $\Lvec$.** It is the lowest energy at fixed angular momentum per
  element. Viscous spreading lowers the energy further by moving mass in and angular momentum out.
- **A disc's thickness is not set by the argument.** In a gas disc the thickness $H$ is set by pressure, with
  $H/R \approx c_s / v_\phi$ for sound speed $c_s$ and orbital speed $v_\phi$ <Cite id="frank2002" />. In the toy there
  is no pressure, and the thickness that remains (rms height about $0.06$) comes from the finite spacing between
  neighbours, which leaves pairs side by side in the plane unable to damp each other's vertical motion.
- **A drag towards the local mean velocity breaks angular momentum conservation.** Only a central pairwise force
  conserves $\Lvec$ exactly. A non-central drag adds a spurious torque of the order of the neighbour spacing times the
  shear, which shows up as a drift in $\abs{\Lvec}$.
- **A conserved quantity can still drift numerically.** An explicit Runge–Kutta method conserves linear invariants
  exactly but not quadratic ones such as $\Lvec = \sum_i \rvec_i \times \vvec_i$. With the classical fourth-order
  method the drift falls as $h^4$. <NoteLink to="symplectic-integrators-and-energy-drift" /> treats the same problem for
  the energy.
