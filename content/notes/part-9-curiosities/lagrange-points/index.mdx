<Definition>
The **circular restricted three-body problem** follows a massless test particle under the gravity of two masses $m_1$
and $m_2$ that move on circular orbits about their centre of mass. In the frame that rotates with the two masses, the
particle feels gravity, a centrifugal force and a Coriolis force. The **Lagrange points** L1 to L5 are the five places
where a particle at rest in that frame stays at rest: three on the line through the masses and two at the apexes of
equilateral triangles built on that line. L1, L2 and L3 are always unstable. L4 and L5 are linearly stable when the mass
ratio $\mu = m_2 / (m_1 + m_2)$ satisfies $27\mu(1 - \mu) < 1$, that is $\mu < 0.0385$ <Cite id="routh1874" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A spacecraft between the Earth and the Sun feels the Sun pull one way and the Earth pull the other. It also orbits the
Sun, and a body closer to the Sun than the Earth normally orbits faster than the Earth. At one distance the Earth's pull
weakens the Sun's pull by just enough that the spacecraft's orbital period becomes one year. The spacecraft then keeps
pace with the Earth and stays on the Sun–Earth line. That place is L1. The same balance on the far side of the Earth,
where the Earth's pull adds to the Sun's, gives L2, and a place on the far side of the Sun gives L3.

In a frame that turns once a year with the Sun and the Earth, these points are equilibria: a body placed there at rest
stays there. Two more equilibria sit $60°$ ahead of and behind the Earth on its orbit, at the corners of equilateral
triangles with the Sun and the Earth. Those are L4 and L5. Jupiter's L4 and L5 hold thousands of Trojan asteroids,
because these two points are stable when the lighter mass is small enough <Cite id="murray1999" />. The collinear points
are unstable: a spacecraft parked near the Sun–Earth L1 or L2 must fire its thrusters now and then to stay there.

## The rotating frame

**Units.** Measure masses in units of $m_1 + m_2$, lengths in units of the separation of the two masses, and times so
that $G = 1$. The masses are then $1 - \mu$ and $\mu$ with $\mu \in (0, \tfrac{1}{2}]$, their separation is 1, and by
Kepler's third law their angular velocity is $n = \sqrt{G (m_1 + m_2) / a^3} = 1$. One orbit of the masses takes $2\pi$.

**Coordinates.** Use axes that rotate with angular velocity 1 about the centre of mass, so that the masses stay fixed
on the $x$ axis: $m_1 = 1 - \mu$ at $(-\mu, 0)$ and $m_2 = \mu$ at $(1 - \mu, 0)$. Write $\rvec = (x, y)$ for the
particle's position in this frame and $r_1$, $r_2$ for its distances to $m_1$ and $m_2$:

$$
r_1 = \sqrt{(x + \mu)^2 + y^2}, \qquad r_2 = \sqrt{(x - 1 + \mu)^2 + y^2}.
$$

**Equations of motion.** In a frame rotating with angular velocity $\omegavec$, Newton's law gains two
apparent accelerations: the Coriolis term $-2\omegavec \times \dot{\rvec}$ and the centrifugal term
$-\omegavec \times (\omegavec \times \rvec)$. With $\omegavec$ of size 1 along the $z$
axis, $\omegavec \times \dot{\rvec} = (-\dot{y}, \dot{x})$ and
$-\omegavec \times (\omegavec \times \rvec) = (x, y)$. Adding the gravity of the two masses gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ddot{x} - 2\dot{y} &= x - \frac{(1 - \mu)(x + \mu)}{r_1^3} - \frac{\mu (x - 1 + \mu)}{r_2^3}, \\
\ddot{y} + 2\dot{x} &= y - \frac{(1 - \mu)\, y}{r_1^3} - \frac{\mu\, y}{r_2^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The terms $-2\dot{y}$ and $+2\dot{x}$ are the Coriolis force: it acts at right angles to the velocity and does no
work. The right-hand sides are the gradient of one function, the **effective potential**

$$
\Omega(x, y) = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{2} + \frac{1 - \mu}{r_1} + \frac{\mu}{r_2},
$$

so the equations read $\ddot{x} - 2\dot{y} = \partial \Omega / \partial x$ and
$\ddot{y} + 2\dot{x} = \partial \Omega / \partial y$ <Cite id="murray1999" />. The first term of $\Omega$ is the
centrifugal potential and the other two are the gravity of the masses, with the sign chosen so that $\Omega$ is
positive.

**The Jacobi constant.** Multiply the first equation by $\dot{x}$, the second by $\dot{y}$, and add. The Coriolis terms
cancel, $-2\dot{x}\dot{y} + 2\dot{y}\dot{x} = 0$, and what is left is

$$
\dot{x}\ddot{x} + \dot{y}\ddot{y} = \frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial x}\dot{x} + \frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial y}\dot{y}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\paren{\frac{\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2}{2}} = \frac{d\Omega}{dt}.
$$

Integrating once gives the conserved **Jacobi constant**

$$
C = 2\Omega(x, y) - \paren{\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2}.
$$

$C$ is the restricted problem's version of energy, and it is the only conserved quantity the problem has
<Cite id="musielak2014" />.

**Zero-velocity curves.** The squared speed $\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 = 2\Omega - C$ cannot be negative, so a particle with
Jacobi constant $C$ can only be where $2\Omega(x, y) \ge C$. The boundary $2\Omega(x, y) = C$ is its **zero-velocity
curve**: the particle reaches it only by coming momentarily to rest, and never crosses it. A large $C$ (a slow
particle) confines it to small regions round each mass or far outside both; a smaller $C$ opens necks between those
regions.

## The five equilibria

A particle at rest stays at rest where both components of $\nabla\Omega$ vanish, because then $\ddot{x} = \ddot{y} = 0$
and the Coriolis terms are zero too.

**L1, L2 and L3** lie on the $x$ axis. At $y = 0$, $\partial \Omega / \partial y = 0$ automatically, and the condition
is

$$
f(x) = \frac{\partial \Omega}{\partial x}(x, 0) = x - \frac{(1 - \mu)(x + \mu)}{|x + \mu|^3} - \frac{\mu (x - 1 + \mu)}{|x - 1 + \mu|^3} = 0.
$$

The two masses cut the axis into three intervals. On each one, $f$ runs from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ (or back), because
the terms in $1/|x + \mu|^2$ and $1/|x - 1 + \mu|^2$ blow up at the masses with opposite signs on either side. So $f$
has one zero in each interval: L1 between the masses, L2 beyond $m_2$ and L3 beyond $m_1$. Each zero is found by a
bracketing root-finder; the figures use Brent's method.

**L4 and L5** lie off the axis. With $y \ne 0$, dividing
$\partial \Omega / \partial y = y\paren{1 - (1-\mu)/r_1^3 - \mu/r_2^3} = 0$ by $y$ and combining it with
$\partial \Omega / \partial x = 0$ forces $r_1 = r_2 = 1$. The points at distance 1 from both masses are the apexes of
the two equilateral triangles on the segment between them:

$$
\text{L4, L5} = \paren{\tfrac{1}{2} - \mu,\ \pm\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}, \qquad
C_\text{L4} = 2\Omega(\text{L4}) = 3 - \mu(1 - \mu).
$$

<Derivation title="Why r₁ = r₂ = 1 off the axis">
Write $A = (1 - \mu)/r_1^3$ and $B = \mu / r_2^3$. Off the axis, $\partial \Omega / \partial y = 0$ gives $A + B = 1$.
Then $\partial \Omega / \partial x = x - A(x + \mu) - B(x - 1 + \mu) = x(1 - A - B) - \mu(A + B) + B = -\mu + B = 0$, so
$B = \mu$ and $r_2 = 1$. Then $A = 1 - \mu$ and $r_1 = 1$.
</Derivation>

## Stability

Linearise about an equilibrium $(x_0, y_0)$. Write $\xi = x - x_0$ and $\eta = y - y_0$ for the displacement, and
$\Omega_{xx}$, $\Omega_{xy}$, $\Omega_{yy}$ for the second derivatives of $\Omega$ at the equilibrium. To first order,

$$
\ddot{\xi} - 2\dot{\eta} = \Omega_{xx}\xi + \Omega_{xy}\eta, \qquad \ddot{\eta} + 2\dot{\xi} = \Omega_{xy}\xi + \Omega_{yy}\eta.
$$

A trial solution $\xi, \eta \propto e^{\lambda t}$ has a nonzero amplitude only when the determinant of the coefficients
vanishes:

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} \lambda^2 - \Omega_{xx} & -2\lambda - \Omega_{xy} \\ 2\lambda - \Omega_{xy} & \lambda^2 - \Omega_{yy} \end{pmatrix}
= \lambda^4 + \paren{4 - \Omega_{xx} - \Omega_{yy}}\lambda^2 + \Omega_{xx}\Omega_{yy} - \Omega_{xy}^2 = 0.
$$

The equilibrium is linearly stable when all four roots $\lambda$ are imaginary, that is when both roots of this
quadratic in $\lambda^2$ are real and negative. Then every displacement oscillates instead of growing
(<NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria" />).

**At L4 and L5**, the second derivatives are $\Omega_{xx} = \tfrac{3}{4}$, $\Omega_{yy} = \tfrac{9}{4}$ and
$\Omega_{xy} = \pm\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}(1 - 2\mu)$. Substituting, $4 - \Omega_{xx} - \Omega_{yy} = 1$ and
$\Omega_{xx}\Omega_{yy} - \Omega_{xy}^2 = \tfrac{27}{16}\paren{1 - (1 - 2\mu)^2} = \tfrac{27}{4}\mu(1 - \mu)$, so

$$
\lambda^4 + \lambda^2 + \tfrac{27}{4}\mu(1 - \mu) = 0, \qquad
\lambda^2 = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 27\mu(1 - \mu)}}{2}.
$$

Both values of $\lambda^2$ are real and negative exactly when $1 - 27\mu(1 - \mu) > 0$. Solving
$27\mu(1 - \mu) = 1$ for the smaller root gives Routh's critical mass ratio

$$
\mu_\text{c} = \frac{1}{2}\paren{1 - \sqrt{\frac{23}{27}}} \approx 0.03852,
$$

and L4 and L5 are linearly stable for $\mu < \mu_\text{c}$ <Cite id="routh1874" />. The same inequality is the
restricted limit of Routh's criterion for Lagrange's equilateral solution of three finite masses
(<NoteLink to="periodic-three-body-orbits" />). For small $\mu$, the two oscillation frequencies are close to
$\sqrt{27\mu/4}$, a slow drift along the orbit, and close to 1, the epicycle of an ordinary orbit.

<Derivation title="Second derivatives of Ω at L4">
With $\Omega = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 + y^2) + (1 - \mu)/r_1 + \mu/r_2$,

$$
\Omega_{xx} = 1 - \frac{1 - \mu}{r_1^3}\paren{1 - \frac{3(x + \mu)^2}{r_1^2}} - \frac{\mu}{r_2^3}\paren{1 - \frac{3(x - 1 + \mu)^2}{r_2^2}},
$$

$$
\Omega_{yy} = 1 - \frac{1 - \mu}{r_1^3}\paren{1 - \frac{3y^2}{r_1^2}} - \frac{\mu}{r_2^3}\paren{1 - \frac{3y^2}{r_2^2}},
\qquad
\Omega_{xy} = \frac{3(1 - \mu)(x + \mu)y}{r_1^5} + \frac{3\mu(x - 1 + \mu)y}{r_2^5}.
$$

At L4, $r_1 = r_2 = 1$, $x + \mu = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $x - 1 + \mu = -\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $y = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. Then
$\Omega_{xx} = 1 - (1 - \tfrac{3}{4}) = \tfrac{3}{4}$, $\Omega_{yy} = 1 - (1 - \tfrac{9}{4}) = \tfrac{9}{4}$ and
$\Omega_{xy} = 3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\paren{\tfrac{1 - \mu}{2} - \tfrac{\mu}{2}} = \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}(1 - 2\mu)$.
L5 has $y = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, which flips the sign of $\Omega_{xy}$ and leaves the characteristic equation
unchanged.
</Derivation>

**At L1, L2 and L3**, $\Omega_{xx} > 0$ and $\Omega_{yy} < 0$, so $\Omega_{xx}\Omega_{yy} - \Omega_{xy}^2 < 0$ and the
quadratic in $\lambda^2$ has one positive root. A positive $\lambda^2$ gives a real $\lambda > 0$, a displacement that
grows exponentially. The collinear points are unstable for every $\mu$ <Cite id="musielak2014" />.

## Worked example: μ = 0.01

Take $\mu = 0.01$, near the Earth–Moon value of 0.0122. The masses sit at $(-0.01, 0)$ and $(0.99, 0)$.

1. **A first guess for L1 and L2.** Close to $m_2$, the pull of $m_2$ balances the difference between the centrifugal
   force and the pull of $m_1$, which grows linearly with distance. To first order this gives
   $r_\text{H} = (\mu / 3)^{1/3} = (0.00333)^{1/3} = 0.1494$, the Hill radius. The guesses are
   $x_\text{L1} \approx 0.99 - 0.1494 = 0.8406$ and $x_\text{L2} \approx 0.99 + 0.1494 = 1.1394$.
2. **The roots.** At the guess $x = 0.8406$, $f = -0.0795$, and $f \to +\infty$ just left of $m_2$, so the root lies
   between 0.8406 and 0.99. Brent's method finds $x_\text{L1} = 0.84808$. The same search beyond $m_2$ gives
   $x_\text{L2} = 1.14677$, and beyond $m_1$ gives $x_\text{L3} = -1.00417$. The Hill guesses are within 0.008 of
   the roots; the error is of order $r_\text{H}^2$.
3. **L3 by expansion.** Write $x = -1 - \varepsilon$ with small $\varepsilon$. On this side both masses lie to the
   right, so $f(x) = x + (1 - \mu)/(x + \mu)^2 + \mu/(x - 1 + \mu)^2$. To first order in $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$,
   $f \approx -1 - \varepsilon + (1 - \mu)(1 - 2\varepsilon + 2\mu) + \mu/4 = -3\varepsilon + \tfrac{5}{4}\mu$, so
   $\varepsilon = 5\mu/12 = 0.004167$ and $x_\text{L3} \approx -1.004167$, the root to six digits.
4. **Jacobi constants.** $2\Omega$ at the five points is $C_\text{L1} = 3.1676$, $C_\text{L2} = 3.1543$,
   $C_\text{L3} = 3.0100$ and $C_\text{L4} = C_\text{L5} = 3 - 0.01 \cdot 0.99 = 2.9901$. A particle with
   $C > 3.1676$ cannot pass between the masses; one with $C$ between 3.1543 and 3.1676 can pass through the L1 neck but
   not leave; one with $C < 3.1543$ can leave through the L2 neck.
5. **Stability of L4.** $27\mu(1 - \mu) = 27 \cdot 0.01 \cdot 0.99 = 0.2673 < 1$, so L4 is stable. The roots are
   $\lambda^2 = (-1 \pm \sqrt{0.7327})/2 = -0.0720$ and $-0.9280$: oscillations with angular frequencies 0.268 and
   0.963, periods of 23.4 and 6.52 time units, or 3.7 and 1.04 orbits of the masses.
6. **Instability of L1.** At $x_\text{L1}$, $\Omega_{xx} = 11.13$ and $\Omega_{yy} = -4.07$, so the quadratic is
   $\lambda^4 - 3.07\lambda^2 - 45.2 = 0$ with roots $\lambda^2 = 8.43$ and $-5.37$. The positive root gives
   $\lambda = 2.90$: a displacement from L1 grows by a factor $e^{2.90 \cdot 2\pi} \approx 8 \times 10^{7}$ in one orbit
   of the masses.

**Real systems.** For the Earth and the Moon, $\mu = 0.01215$ puts L1 at $x = 0.8369$, a distance
$1 - \mu - 0.8369 = 0.151$ from the Moon, or about 58,000 km of the 384,400 km between them. For the Sun and
Jupiter, $\mu \approx 0.00095$, far below 0.0385, so Jupiter's Trojan points are stable <Cite id="murray1999" />.

## Tadpoles and horseshoes

A particle near a stable L4 does not sit still. It drifts slowly along the orbit, at the slow frequency
$\sqrt{27\mu/4}$, while making a fast epicycle once per orbit. Its zero-velocity curve explains the path. For $C$ just
above $C_\text{L4}$, the forbidden region $2\Omega < C$ is a small island round L4 and another round L5. A particle that
starts at rest touches its zero-velocity curve and then circulates round the island, so its path in the rotating frame
looks like a tadpole elongated along the orbit. A larger displacement gives a $C$ above $C_\text{L3}$, which joins the
two islands through L3 into one crescent. The particle then circulates round the whole crescent, passing L4, L3 and L5,
and turns back each time it approaches $m_2$. Its path is a horseshoe. Saturn's moons Janus and Epimetheus share an
orbit in this way <Cite id="murray1999" />.

With $\mu = 0.001$ and a start at rest at $(0, 1)$, $30°$ beyond L4, the particle librates on a tadpole between about
$39°$ and $90°$ from the $x$ axis, as seen from the centre of mass. One libration takes about 79 time units, close to
the small-amplitude value $2\pi / \sqrt{27 \cdot 0.001 / 4} \approx 76$. A start at rest at $(-1, 0)$, next to L3,
gives a horseshoe whose ends reach to about $23°$ on either side of $m_2$. The Jacobi
constant drifts by about $10^{-12}$ over 600 time units at the default tolerance.

## Pitfalls

- **Stability without a well.** $\Omega$ has a local minimum at L4, so the potential energy $-\Omega$ has a local
  maximum there. A particle at L4 sits on a hilltop of the effective potential. It is held near L4 by the Coriolis
  force, which turns a sliding particle sideways into a loop. Without the Coriolis term, L4 would be unstable.
- **Rotating-frame velocities.** A particle at rest in the rotating frame moves in the inertial frame, on a circle with
  the masses. A start velocity typed in the rotating frame is not the inertial velocity.
- **Linear is not forever.** The criterion $\mu < \mu_\text{c}$ is linear stability of the idealised problem. In the
  Solar System the other planets perturb the Trojans, and some regions near L4 and L5 empty out over billions of years.
- **Collinear points need station-keeping.** Spacecraft at the Sun–Earth L1 and L2 fly periodic orbits round the
  points, which are themselves unstable, and correct their course with small burns.
- **The restriction matters.** The model assumes circular orbits for the two masses and a particle too light to move
  them. An eccentric orbit of the masses breaks the Jacobi constant; the general problem has no such constant at all
  (<NoteLink to="three-body-problem" />).
