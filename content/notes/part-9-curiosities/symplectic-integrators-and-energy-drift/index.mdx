<Definition>
A one-step method $\Phi_h$ for a Hamiltonian system with state $(\qvec, \pvec) \in \reals^{2d}$ is **symplectic** if
every step is a symplectic map: its Jacobian $\Phi_h'$ satisfies $(\Phi_h')^\top \Jmat\,\Phi_h' = \Jmat$, with
$\Jmat = \begin{pmatrix} \zeros & \eye \\ -\eye & \zeros \end{pmatrix}$. For one degree of freedom this says that each
step preserves area in the $(q, p)$ plane; in general it preserves phase-space volume and more. The exact flow of
every Hamiltonian system is symplectic. A symplectic method of order $r$ exactly conserves a **shadow Hamiltonian**
$\tilde H = H + O(h^r)$ over exponentially long times, so its energy error stays bounded instead of drifting
<Cite id="hairer2006" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A simulation of a planet, a molecule or a pendulum often runs for millions of steps. A classical solver such as
<Gloss name="rk4" /> makes a tiny error in each step, and in a conservative system
those errors add up in one direction: the energy creeps down (or up) a little each orbit. After many orbits the planet
has spiralled inwards, although each single step was excellent. Making the steps smaller slows the drift but never
stops it.

A symplectic integrator makes errors of the same size per step but of a different kind. It never solves the original
problem exactly, but it solves a slightly different conservative problem almost exactly. The energy it conserves is
the shadow Hamiltonian, which differs from the true energy by an amount that depends on the step size $h$ and not on
how long the simulation has run. The true energy therefore wobbles inside a fixed band for ever. What does accumulate
is an error in phase: the simulated planet runs slightly ahead of or behind the real one, and its ellipse slowly turns.
For questions about long-term behaviour, such as whether an orbit stays bounded or a system is stable, a phase error
does no harm, while an energy drift gives the wrong answer.

## Hamiltonian flows preserve area

A Hamiltonian system with **Hamiltonian** $H(\qvec, \pvec)$, positions $\qvec \in \reals^d$ and momenta
$\pvec \in \reals^d$ moves by Hamilton's equations

$$
\dot{\qvec} = \nabla_{\pvec} H, \qquad \dot{\pvec} = -\nabla_{\qvec} H.
$$

The energy $H$ is constant along every solution, because $\dot H = \nabla_{\qvec}H \cdot \dot{\qvec} +
\nabla_{\pvec}H \cdot \dot{\pvec} = 0$. A second, less visible invariant is geometric. Take one degree of freedom and
a small patch of starting states in the $(q, p)$ plane. The patch moves with the flow, and its area changes at a rate
equal to the area times the divergence of the velocity field $(\partial H/\partial p, -\partial H/\partial q)$. That
divergence is $\partial^2 H/\partial q\,\partial p - \partial^2 H/\partial p\,\partial q = 0$, so the area never
changes. This is **Liouville's theorem**. In $d$ dimensions the flow preserves a stronger structure: the sum of the
oriented areas of a surface's projections onto the $d$ planes $(q_i, p_i)$. That property is **symplecticity**,
expressed by the Jacobian condition of the definition, and it implies volume preservation <Cite id="hairer2006" />.

A numerical method is a map $\Phi_h$ from one state to the next. It is symplectic if that map has the same property as
the exact flow. In one dimension the test is that the Jacobian of one step has determinant 1.

## Leapfrog

Many Hamiltonians are **separable**: $H(\qvec, \pvec) = T(\pvec) + V(\qvec)$, a kinetic energy plus a potential
energy. For a body of unit mass, $T = \tfrac12\norm{\pvec}^2$. The **Störmer–Verlet** method splits each step of size
$h$ into three substeps. In its **velocity Verlet** form (kick, drift, kick),

$$
\pvec_{n+1/2} = \pvec_n - \tfrac{h}{2}\nabla V(\qvec_n), \qquad \qvec_{n+1} = \qvec_n + h\,\pvec_{n+1/2}, \qquad
\pvec_{n+1} = \pvec_{n+1/2} - \tfrac{h}{2}\nabla V(\qvec_{n+1}).
$$

The **leapfrog** form (drift, kick, drift) exchanges the roles of $\qvec$ and $\pvec$. Both forms alternate full kicks
and full drifts and differ only in whether a step begins and ends with a half kick or a half drift. Both are second
order and time-reversible, and both need one new force evaluation per step, because the force at the end of one step is
the force at the start of the next <Cite id="hairer2006" />. The same integrator moves the sampler in
<NoteLink to="hamiltonian-monte-carlo">Hamiltonian Monte Carlo</NoteLink>.

Each substep is symplectic, and a composition of symplectic maps is symplectic. A kick changes $\pvec$ by an amount
that depends on $\qvec$ only. For one degree of freedom its Jacobian is
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\tfrac h2 V''(q) & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, a shear with determinant 1. A drift changes $\qvec$ by
an amount that depends on $\pvec$ only, with Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, also a shear.
The product of the three has determinant 1, so leapfrog preserves area exactly, whatever the step size.

Explicit methods that evaluate the whole right-hand side at once lack this structure. One step of explicit Euler,
$q_{n+1} = q_n + h p_n$ and $p_{n+1} = p_n - h V'(q_n)$, has Jacobian
$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ -hV''(q) & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $1 + h^2V''(q)$. No explicit Runge–Kutta method
is symplectic <Cite id="hairer2006" />. RK4 changes areas too, by much less: on the harmonic oscillator the factor per
step is $1 - h^6/72 + h^8/576$.

## The shadow Hamiltonian

Area preservation explains bounded energy through **backward error analysis**. The question it asks is not how far
the numerical solution is from the true one, but which equation the numerical solution solves exactly. For a
symplectic method of order $r$ applied with step $h$, the answer is the Hamiltonian system of a **modified** (shadow)
Hamiltonian

$$
\tilde H = H + h^r H_{r+1} + h^{r+1} H_{r+2} + \cdots,
$$

whose correction terms $H_k$ are built from derivatives of $H$. The series does not converge in general, but truncated
at the right order it describes the numerical solution up to an error that is exponentially small in $1/h$. Because the
numerical solution follows a Hamiltonian flow, it conserves $\tilde H$ to that accuracy, and so

$$
H(\qvec_n, \pvec_n) - H(\qvec_0, \pvec_0) = O(h^r) \quad\text{for } nh \le e^{c/h}
$$

for a constant $c > 0$, provided the solution stays in a compact region <Cite id="hairer2006" />. A non-symplectic
method also has a modified equation, but that equation is not Hamiltonian. It contains a small dissipative or
anti-dissipative part, and the energy error then grows in proportion to time, as $O(t\,h^r)$, for a method of order $r$
<Cite id="hairer2006" />.

The shadow Hamiltonian also explains the precession. Its orbits are those of a slightly different force law, and only
the exact inverse-square law closes its bound orbits (see <NoteLink to="kepler-orbits">Kepler orbits</NoteLink>). The
leapfrog orbit therefore keeps its size and shape but turns slowly, and the error that would have gone into the
energy goes into the angle of the ellipse instead.

Störmer–Verlet also conserves the angular momentum $\qvec \times \pvec$ of a central-force problem exactly, up to
rounding, because each kick is along $\qvec$ and each drift is along $\pvec$ <Cite id="hairer2006" />. Runge–Kutta
methods conserve linear invariants, but not quadratic ones like angular momentum, unless they are symplectic.

## Worked example

Take the harmonic oscillator $H = \tfrac12(q^2 + p^2)$, the step $h = 0.1$ and the start $(q_0, p_0) = (1, 0)$, so
$H_0 = 0.5$. The exact solution circles the origin with constant $H$.

1. **Explicit Euler** takes $q_1 = q_0 + h p_0 = 1$ and $p_1 = p_0 - h q_0 = -0.1$, so $H_1 = \tfrac12(1 + 0.01) =
   0.505$. The step is the linear map $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which multiplies $q^2 + p^2$ by
   exactly $1 + h^2 = 1.01$. After 100 steps ($t = 10$), $H = 0.5 \times 1.01^{100} = 1.35$, and areas have grown by
   the same factor 2.70. The orbit spirals out.
2. **Symplectic Euler** kicks first and then drifts with the new momentum: $p_1 = p_0 - h q_0 = -0.1$ and $q_1 = q_0 +
   h p_1 = 0.99$. The energy is $H_1 = \tfrac12(0.9801 + 0.01) = 0.49505$. The step is the map
   $\begin{pmatrix} 1 - h^2 & h \\ -h & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, whose determinant is $(1 - h^2) + h^2 = 1$.
3. **Its shadow Hamiltonian** is $\tilde H = \tfrac12(q^2 + p^2 - h\,qp)$. At the start $\tilde H = 0.5$. After one step
   $\tilde H = \tfrac12(0.9801 + 0.01 - 0.1 \times 0.99 \times (-0.1)) = \tfrac12(0.9901 + 0.0099) = 0.5$, and the
   same algebra shows that it is unchanged by every step. The numerical states lie on the ellipse $\tilde H = 0.5$
   for ever.
4. **The energy stays in a band.** On that ellipse, $q^2 + p^2$ ranges between $1/(1 + h/2)$ and $1/(1 - h/2)$, the
   reciprocals of the eigenvalues of the quadratic form. So $H$ oscillates between $0.5/1.05 = 0.476$ and
   $0.5/0.95 = 0.526$, an error of about 5%, which is $O(h)$ for this first-order method. It never grows.
5. **Velocity Verlet** on the same problem conserves $\tilde H = \tfrac12\paren{p^2 + (1 - h^2/4)\,q^2}$, and $H$
   stays between $0.49875$ and $0.5$, an error of $h^2/8 = 0.25\%$, which is $O(h^2)$ for a second-order method.

## Long runs on a Kepler orbit

The figure integrates a Kepler orbit with semi-major axis 1 and eccentricity $e$, in units with $GM = 1$ (period
$2\pi$), started at pericentre. It runs RK4 and leapfrog with the same step for hundreds of periods. At the defaults
($e = 0.6$ and $h = 0.05$, about 126 steps per period), the energy $H_0 = -0.5$ evolves as follows.

- **RK4** loses energy at a constant rate: $H - H_0$ is $-0.0034$ after 100 periods, $-0.010$ after 300 and $-0.035$
  after 1000. The semi-major axis shrinks with it, since $a = -1/(2H)$. Its angular momentum drifts too, by $-0.0018$
  after 300 periods.
- **Leapfrog** keeps $H - H_0$ between $-0.0005$ and $+0.0016$ for all 1000 periods. The band is set by the passage
  through pericentre, where the orbit is most curved and the step is coarsest. Halving $h$ shrinks the band by about a
  factor of four. Its angular momentum is conserved to $10^{-14}$.
- **The leapfrog ellipse precesses** by about $-0.9°$ per period, so after 300 periods its pericentre has turned
  through $-276°$. The RK4 ellipse barely turns, by $1.8°$ in the same time, but it shrinks.
- **Velocity Verlet**, the other choice in the figure, keeps $H - H_0$ between 0 and $+0.009$ at the defaults. The
  band is wider than leapfrog's at this step size and just as fixed in time.

RK4 is more accurate per step than leapfrog: it is fourth order against second order. At low eccentricity and over a
few hundred periods its drift can still be smaller than leapfrog's band. The difference is the trend. RK4's error has
no bound as $t$ grows; leapfrog's does. RK4 also costs four force evaluations per step against one, so at equal cost
leapfrog could take a step four times smaller.

## Pitfalls

- **Bounded energy is not accuracy.** The energy error stays in its band, but the phase error grows linearly in time,
  so after many periods the simulated body is in the wrong place on a nearly right orbit. Predicting where a planet is
  needs a small step or a high-order method; asking whether an orbit stays bounded suits a symplectic one.
- **Adaptive steps break the shadow.** Changing $h$ from step to step according to the state destroys the single
  modified Hamiltonian, and the energy drifts again. Variable steps need a reparametrisation of time that keeps the
  system Hamiltonian <Cite id="hairer2006" />.
- **The step must resolve the fastest part of the orbit.** On an eccentric orbit the energy band is set at pericentre.
  If $h$ is comparable with the pericentre passage time, the error band grows large and the backward error series no
  longer describes the method.
- **Separability.** Leapfrog's explicit form needs $H = T(\pvec) + V(\qvec)$. A non-separable Hamiltonian needs an
  implicit symplectic method such as the implicit midpoint rule.
- **General-purpose adaptive solvers.** The adaptive Runge–Kutta solvers of <NoteLink
  to="numerical-ode-solvers">numerical ODE solvers</NoteLink> control the local error, not the structure, and on long
  conservative runs they drift like RK4 <Cite id="hairer2006" />.
