<Definition>
A **point vortex** is a two-dimensional flow whose vorticity is concentrated at a single point $\xvec_0$ with
circulation $\Gamma$; it induces a velocity of magnitude $\Gamma/(2\pi r)$ around $\xvec_0$ at distance $r$. In an
inviscid, incompressible fluid, $N$ point vortices at $\xvec_k = (x_k, y_k)$ with circulations $\Gamma_k$ move with the
velocity that the other vortices induce at their positions. The resulting system of $2N$ <Gloss name="ode">ordinary
differential equations</Gloss>, **Kirchhoff's equations**, is Hamiltonian, with the interaction energy
$H = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_j \Gamma_k \ln r_{jk}$ as Hamiltonian <Cite id="saffman1992" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Many flows hold their spin in small, intense patches: the tip vortices behind an aircraft, the eddies shed by a
bridge pier, hurricanes on a weather map. Outside the patches the fluid barely rotates. A point vortex is the limit of
such a patch as its size shrinks to zero at fixed circulation.

A point vortex cannot move itself. Its own flow circles it symmetrically, so the net push on the vortex from its own
flow is zero. Each vortex is instead carried along by the flows of all the others, like a cork in a stream. The motion
of the whole set therefore depends only on where the vortices are and how strong they are, which turns a fluid problem
into a problem in ordinary differential equations.

The simplest cases already show the range of behaviour. Two vortices of opposite sign push each other sideways and
travel together in a straight line. Two vortices of the same sign push each other round and orbit a common centre.
Two opposite pairs, one behind the other, take turns: the rear pair narrows, speeds up and passes through the front
pair, which widens and slows, and then the roles swap. This **leapfrogging** is the two-dimensional cross-section of
two smoke rings passing through each other. Three vortices always move regularly, but four generally move chaotically
<Cite id="aref1983" />.

## Vorticity and the velocity of a point vortex

Let $\uvec = (u, v)$ be the velocity of a fluid in the plane, in metres per second. The **vorticity**

$$
\omega = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
$$

measures the local rate of spin, in radians per second (twice the angular velocity of a small fluid element). In a
two-dimensional ideal fluid (inviscid, incompressible, of constant density) vorticity is carried unchanged by the
fluid: $\mathrm{D}\omega/\mathrm{D}t = \partial\omega/\partial t + \uvec \cdot \nabla\omega = 0$
<Cite id="saffman1992" />. The fluid can stretch and rearrange a patch of vorticity, but it cannot create or destroy
vorticity.

Incompressibility, $\partial u/\partial x + \partial v/\partial y = 0$, lets the velocity be written through a
**stream function** $\psi(x, y)$, in square metres per second:

$$
u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \qquad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}.
$$

The fluid moves along the level curves of $\psi$. Substituting into the definition of $\omega$ gives Poisson's
equation $\nabla^2\psi = -\omega$. The fundamental solution of the Laplacian in the plane is
$\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln\norm{\xvec}$, since $\nabla^2 \ln\norm{\xvec} = 2\pi\,\delta(\xvec)$, so

$$
\psi(\xvec) = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\int \omega(\xvec') \ln\norm{\xvec - \xvec'}\, \mathrm{d}\xvec'.
$$

For a point vortex, $\omega = \Gamma\,\delta(\xvec - \xvec_0)$, the integral collapses to
$\psi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r$ with $r = \norm{\xvec - \xvec_0}$. Differentiating gives the two-dimensional
**Biot–Savart law** for a point vortex:

$$
u = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\,\frac{y - y_0}{r^2}, \qquad v = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\,\frac{x - x_0}{r^2}.
$$

The velocity is perpendicular to $\xvec - \xvec_0$ and has magnitude $\Gamma/(2\pi r)$: counter-clockwise for
$\Gamma > 0$, clockwise for $\Gamma < 0$. The circulation around any circle centred on the vortex is the speed times
the circumference, $\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r} \cdot 2\pi r = \Gamma$, in square metres per second.

## Kirchhoff's equations

Vorticity moves with the fluid, so each point vortex moves with the fluid velocity at its position. The velocity
there is the sum of the Biot–Savart velocities of all the vortices. The vortex's own term is infinite but points
equally in every direction around it, and it is left out <Cite id="saffman1992" />. For $N$ vortices this gives
**Kirchhoff's equations**:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_k}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_j\, \frac{y_k - y_j}{r_{kj}^2}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}y_k}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_j\, \frac{x_k - x_j}{r_{kj}^2},
$$

where $r_{kj} = \norm{\xvec_k - \xvec_j}$ and $k = 1, \dots, N$. With complex positions $z_k = x_k + i y_k$ the pair
reads as one equation, $\mathrm{d}\bar{z}_k/\mathrm{d}t = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_j/(z_k - z_j)$, where
the bar is the complex conjugate. The equations are first order in time: a vortex has a position but no inertia, so
its velocity is set by the configuration at each instant.

**Hamiltonian form.** Let

$$
H = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_j \Gamma_k \ln r_{jk}.
$$

Differentiating with respect to $y_k$ picks out the terms that contain vortex $k$, each of which appears twice in the
double sum: $\partial H/\partial y_k = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j \ne k}\Gamma_j\Gamma_k (y_k - y_j)/r_{kj}^2$. Comparing
with Kirchhoff's equations,

$$
\Gamma_k\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x_k}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial y_k}, \qquad
\Gamma_k\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y_k}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_k}.
$$

These are Hamilton's equations with $x_k$ as a coordinate and $\Gamma_k y_k$ as its conjugate momentum. The phase space
of $N$ vortices is the plane itself, $N$ times over: there are no separate velocities. $H$ is the kinetic energy of the
flow per unit fluid density, less the infinite self-energies of the vortices <Cite id="aref1983" />.

## Conserved quantities

Four quantities stay constant along every solution <Cite id="saffman1992" />:

- the **Hamiltonian** $H$, because it does not depend on time;
- the **linear impulse** $Q = \sum_k \Gamma_k x_k$ and $P = \sum_k \Gamma_k y_k$, because $H$ is unchanged when every
  vortex is translated;
- the **angular impulse** $I = \sum_k \Gamma_k (x_k^2 + y_k^2)$, because $H$ is unchanged when every vortex is rotated
  about the origin.

When the total circulation $\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_k \Gamma_k$ is not zero, $(Q, P)/\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is
the **centre of vorticity**, and it stays fixed. When $\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}} = 0$, as for an opposite pair, there is
no centre and the impulse sets the direction of travel instead.

<Derivation title="Why Q, P and I are constant">
Multiply the $x$-equation of vortex $k$ by $\Gamma_k$ and sum over $k$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_k \sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_k \Gamma_j\, \frac{y_k - y_j}{r_{kj}^2}.
$$

Swapping the names $j$ and $k$ changes the sign of each term and leaves the sum the same, so the sum equals its own
negative and is zero. The same argument gives $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}t = 0$. For the angular impulse,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2\sum_k \Gamma_k \paren{x_k \dot{x}_k + y_k \dot{y}_k}
= \frac{1}{\pi}\sum_k \sum_{j \ne k} \Gamma_k \Gamma_j\, \frac{x_k y_j - y_k x_j}{r_{kj}^2},
$$

using $-x_k(y_k - y_j) + y_k(x_k - x_j) = x_k y_j - y_k x_j$. The numerator changes sign when $j$ and $k$ are swapped,
so this sum is zero as well. $H$ is constant because
$\mathrm{d}H/\mathrm{d}t = \sum_k (\partial_{x_k} H\, \dot{x}_k + \partial_{y_k} H\, \dot{y}_k)
= \sum_k \Gamma_k(-\dot{y}_k \dot{x}_k + \dot{x}_k \dot{y}_k) = 0$.
</Derivation>

## Two vortices

Two vortices keep their separation $d$, because $H = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\Gamma_1\Gamma_2 \ln d$ is constant. The relative
position $z = z_1 - z_2$ obeys

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{z}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\paren{\frac{\Gamma_2}{z_1 - z_2} - \frac{\Gamma_1}{z_2 - z_1}}
= \frac{\Gamma_1 + \Gamma_2}{2\pi i\, z}.
$$

Writing $z = d\,e^{i\theta}$ gives $\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}t = (\Gamma_1 + \Gamma_2)/(2\pi d^2)$. A **like pair**,
or any pair with $\Gamma_1 + \Gamma_2 \ne 0$, therefore rotates rigidly about its centre of vorticity at the angular
speed $\Omega = (\Gamma_1 + \Gamma_2)/(2\pi d^2)$. An **opposite pair**, $\Gamma_2 = -\Gamma_1$, does not rotate: each
vortex moves with the speed $\abs{\Gamma_1}/(2\pi d)$ that the other induces, perpendicular to the line joining them,
and the pair travels in a straight line. The fluid between the two vortices travels with them, so the pair carries a
closed oval of fluid along.

## Leapfrogging, three vortices and chaos

Two opposite pairs on a common axis, placed so that at some instant they are side by side with half-widths $a$ (inner)
and $b$ (outer), leapfrog for ever when $\alpha = a/b$ exceeds $3 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx 0.172$ <Cite id="love1894" />.
Below that ratio the inner pair escapes ahead and the outer pair falls behind. The periodic leapfrogging motion is
itself unstable to small perturbations that break the mirror symmetry unless $\alpha > 0.382$
<Cite id="acheson2000" /> <Cite id="tophoj2013" />.

The number of conserved quantities decides how complicated the motion can be. $H$, $I$ and $Q^2 + P^2$ are three
independent quantities that are compatible in the Hamiltonian sense (their Poisson brackets vanish). A system with
$N$ degrees of freedom and $N$ such quantities is integrable: its motion is regular, periodic or quasi-periodic. Three
vortices have three degrees of freedom, so the three-vortex problem is integrable for any circulations
<Cite id="aref1983" />. Four vortices have one degree of freedom too many, and their motion is generally chaotic: two
starts that differ by $10^{-6}$ separate exponentially fast <Cite id="aref1980" />.

A **passive tracer**, a fluid particle that marks the flow, moves like a vortex of zero circulation. Tracers in the
flow of three vortices form a restricted four-vortex problem, and their paths are generally chaotic even though the
three vortices move regularly. The fluid between the vortices is stretched and folded and mixes rapidly; this
**chaotic advection** stirs fluid efficiently with very simple, regular forcing <Cite id="aref1984" />.

## Worked example

Measure circulation by the **strength** $\kappa = \Gamma/(2\pi)$, so that a vortex of strength $\kappa$ induces the
speed $\kappa/r$, and let lengths and times be in arbitrary consistent units. The figure uses the same units, and its
readouts report $H/2\pi$, $(Q, P)/2\pi$ and $I/2\pi$, which are the sums above with $\kappa_k$ in place of $\Gamma_k$.

**An opposite pair.** Strengths $+1$ at $(0, 0.5)$ and $-1$ at $(0, -0.5)$, so $d = 1$. The lower vortex induces at the
upper one $u = -\kappa_2 (y_1 - y_2)/d^2 = -(-1)(1)/1 = 1$ and $v = 0$. The upper vortex induces the same velocity at
the lower one. The pair travels in the $+x$ direction at speed $\kappa/d = 1$.

**A like pair.** Strengths $1$ and $1$ at $(\pm 0.5, 0)$. The angular speed is
$(\kappa_1 + \kappa_2)/d^2 = 2/1 = 2$, so the pair turns once every $2\pi/2 = \pi \approx 3.14$ time units.

**Three vortices.** Strengths $1, 1, 1$ at $\xvec_1 = (-1, -0.4)$, $\xvec_2 = (1, -0.6)$ and $\xvec_3 = (0.2, 1)$, the
figure's three-vortex start.

1. Vortex 2 seen from vortex 1: $\xvec_1 - \xvec_2 = (-2, 0.2)$, so $r_{12}^2 = 4.04$. Its contribution to vortex 1's
   velocity is $(-0.2/4.04,\, -2/4.04) = (-0.0495, -0.4950)$.
2. Vortex 3 seen from vortex 1: $\xvec_1 - \xvec_3 = (-1.2, -1.4)$, so $r_{13}^2 = 3.40$. Its contribution is
   $(1.4/3.40,\, -1.2/3.40) = (0.4118, -0.3529)$.
3. Vortex 1 moves at $(0.3623, -0.8480)$. The same sums give $(0.5495, 0.7450)$ for vortex 2 and
   $(-0.9118, 0.1029)$ for vortex 3.
4. The strengths are equal, so $\mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}t$ is the sum of the three $x$-velocities,
   $0.3623 + 0.5495 - 0.9118 = 0$. The $y$-velocities sum to zero as well, to rounding.
5. The distances are $r_{12} = 2.0100$, $r_{13} = 1.8439$ and $r_{23} = 1.7889$, so
   $H/2\pi = -(\ln 2.0100 + \ln 1.8439 + \ln 1.7889) = -(0.6981 + 0.6119 + 0.5816) = -1.8916$.
6. The angular impulse is $I/2\pi = (1 + 0.16) + (1 + 0.36) + (0.04 + 1) = 3.56$, and the centre of vorticity is
   $(Q, P)/\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}} = (0.2, 0)/3 = (0.067, 0)$.

The figure starts from these numbers and reports the same values of $H/2\pi$ and $I/2\pi$ at every later time.

## Pitfalls

- **Point vortices are idealised.** Real vortices have cores of finite size. When two like-signed vortices come within
  a few core radii they merge; point vortices never merge, and two of them can pass arbitrarily close.
- **The step size matters near close approaches.** The velocity grows as $1/r$, so a fixed step that is fine at large
  separations becomes too coarse when two vortices approach. A drift of $H$ or $I$ in the readouts signals a step that
  is too large. Runge–Kutta methods are not symplectic, so even a small step lets $H$ wander slowly over very long runs
  (<NoteLink to="symplectic-integrators-and-energy-drift">symplectic integrators and energy drift</NoteLink>).
- **The tracers are smoothed.** Within $0.05$ length units of a vortex, the figure's tracers feel a softened velocity
  $\kappa\,r/(r^2 + 0.05^2)$ so that the fixed step can follow them. The vortices themselves feel the exact law.
- **Two dimensions only.** A vortex pair is the cross-section of a vortex ring, but a ring's speed also depends on the
  logarithm of its core size, which has no counterpart in the plane.
- **Integrable does not mean simple.** Three vortices can still collapse to a point in finite time or scatter, for
  special choices of circulations and positions <Cite id="aref1983" />.
