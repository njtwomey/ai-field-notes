<Definition>
**Keplerian shear** is the differential rotation of a disc whose matter moves on circular
<NoteLink to="kepler-orbits">Kepler orbits</NoteLink> around a central mass $M$. The angular velocity at radius $r$ is
$\Omega(r) = \sqrt{GM / r^3}$, so it falls as $r^{-3/2}$, and the shear rate $r\, d\Omega / dr = -\tfrac32 \Omega$
measures how fast neighbouring rings slide past each other. A particle displaced from its circular orbit oscillates
about it at the epicyclic frequency $\kappa$, which for a point mass equals $\Omega$, so its orbit closes into an
ellipse.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A disc of gas or particles around a star or black hole does not rotate like a solid plate. Each ring orbits at its own
Kepler speed, and inner rings go round faster than outer ones. In units with $G = M = 1$, a ring at radius 1 completes
an orbit in $2\pi \approx 6.28$ time units and a ring at radius 4 takes $2\pi \times 8 \approx 50$. Any pattern drawn
across the disc is therefore pulled apart: a straight radial line becomes a spiral whose outer end lags behind, and
two neighbouring particles drift apart along the orbit.

The same gravity that sets the shear also holds each orbit in place. A particle nudged outwards from its circle does
not wander off. It oscillates in and out about a guiding circle while the shear carries it along. For a point mass the
in-and-out oscillation takes exactly one orbit, which is why Kepler orbits are closed ellipses. The shear does not
destabilise the flow either: the angular momentum per unit mass rises outwards, so a ring pushed outwards falls back.

## Details

### Angular velocity and shear rate

A ring of matter at radius $r$ (length units) on a circular orbit around a mass $M$ balances gravity against the
centripetal acceleration, $GM / r^2 = \Omega^2 r$, which gives

$$
\Omega(r) = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}},
\qquad
v_\phi(r) = r\, \Omega(r) = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}},
\qquad
h(r) = r^2\, \Omega(r) = \sqrt{GM r}.
$$

Here $\Omega$ is the angular velocity (radians per time unit), $v_\phi$ the orbital speed and $h$ the specific angular
momentum (angular momentum per unit mass). The angular velocity and the speed fall outwards; the angular momentum rises.

Two rings at $r$ and $r + \Delta r$ move apart along the orbit. In the frame turning with the inner ring, the outer ring
moves at the speed

$$
\Delta v = r\, \Delta\Omega \approx r\, \frac{d\Omega}{dr}\, \Delta r = -q\, \Omega\, \Delta r,
\qquad
q = -\frac{d \ln \Omega}{d \ln r}.
$$

The rate $r\, d\Omega/dr$ is the shear rate <Cite id="pringle1981" />, and the dimensionless $q$ is the shear exponent.
Kepler rotation has $q = \tfrac32$. A flat rotation curve, $v_\phi$ constant as in the outer parts of many galaxies, has
$q = 1$. Rigid rotation has $q = 0$ and no shear. The minus sign means the outer ring falls behind.

### A line of tracers winds into a spiral

Tracers start on the line at azimuth $\phi = 0$, one at each radius $r$, and each stays on its circular orbit. At time
$t$ the tracer at $r$ sits at

$$
\phi(r, t) = \Omega(r)\, t.
$$

The tracers on the inner edge run ahead and the line becomes a spiral. Its outer end lags behind the rotation, which
makes it a trailing spiral. The pitch angle $p$ between the spiral and the local circle satisfies
$\tan p = 1 / (r\, \abs{\partial \phi / \partial r})$. Since $\partial \phi / \partial r = t\, d\Omega/dr = -q\Omega t /
r$,

$$
\tan p = \frac{1}{q\, \Omega\, t}.
$$

The spiral tightens without limit: the pitch angle falls as $1/t$. Between radii $r_1 < r_2$ the line wraps
$(\Omega(r_1) - \Omega(r_2))\, t / (2\pi)$ times around the centre.

### The epicyclic frequency

A particle of unit mass on an orbit near the circle of radius $r_g$ keeps its specific angular momentum $h$, because the
central force exerts no torque. Its radial motion obeys

$$
\ddot r = -\Phi_{\text{eff}}'(r),
\qquad
\Phi_{\text{eff}}(r) = \frac{h^2}{2 r^2} + \Phi(r),
$$

with $\Phi(r)$ the gravitational potential (energy per unit mass). The circular orbit with angular momentum $h$ sits at
the minimum $r_g$ of $\Phi_{\text{eff}}$, the guiding radius, where $h^2 = r_g^3 \Phi'(r_g)$. Near the minimum,
$\Phi_{\text{eff}}(r) \approx \Phi_{\text{eff}}(r_g) + \tfrac12 \kappa^2 (r - r_g)^2$, so the radial offset oscillates
at the frequency $\kappa$ with

$$
\kappa^2 = \Phi_{\text{eff}}''(r_g) = \frac{3 h^2}{r_g^4} + \Phi''(r_g) = \frac{3 \Phi'(r_g)}{r_g} + \Phi''(r_g).
$$

With $\Omega^2 = \Phi'/r$ for circular orbits, this is $\kappa^2 = r^{-3}\, d(r^4 \Omega^2)/dr$
<Cite id="binney2008" />. For a point mass, $\Phi = -GM/r$, $\Phi' = GM/r^2$ and $\Phi'' = -2GM/r^3$, so

$$
\kappa^2 = \frac{3GM}{r_g^3} - \frac{2GM}{r_g^3} = \frac{GM}{r_g^3} = \Omega^2.
$$

The radial oscillation of a Kepler orbit therefore takes exactly one orbital period. The particle returns to the same
distance from the centre at the same azimuth, and the orbit closes. For a power law $\Omega \propto r^{-q}$ the same
formula gives $\kappa^2 = (4 - 2q)\, \Omega^2$: $\kappa = \sqrt2\, \Omega$ for a flat rotation curve and $\kappa =
2\Omega$ for rigid rotation. The azimuth swept between successive pericentres is $360° \times \Omega/\kappa$, which is
$360°$ for Kepler and $254.6°$ for a flat rotation curve, whose orbits are rosettes that do not close.

### The epicycle in the rotating frame

In the frame that turns at $\Omega$ with the circle of radius $r_g$, a nudged particle traces a small ellipse, the
epicycle, about a guiding centre. Its radial offset is $\xi = r - r_g$ and its azimuthal offset is $\eta$, measured as
arc length along the circle. A kick of $u_0$ outwards and $v_0$ along the orbit, given on the circle, produces to first
order in the kick

$$
\xi(t) = \frac{2\Omega v_0}{\kappa^2} (1 - \cos \kappa t) + \frac{u_0}{\kappa} \sin \kappa t,
\qquad
\eta(t) = -q\, \Omega\, \xi_g\, t + \frac{4\Omega^2 v_0}{\kappa^3} \sin \kappa t - \frac{2\Omega u_0}{\kappa^2}
(1 - \cos \kappa t),
$$

with the guiding centre at $\xi_g = 2\Omega v_0 / \kappa^2$. The guiding centre drifts along the orbit at
$-q\Omega\xi_g$, the shear speed at its radius. The ellipse is $2\Omega/\kappa$ times as long in $\eta$ as in $\xi$,
twice as long for Kepler, and the particle goes round it against the direction of rotation.

<Derivation title="Hill's equations and their solution">
In the rotating frame, with $\xi$ pointing outwards and $\eta$ along the rotation, the linearised equations of motion
are Hill's equations

$$
\ddot\xi - 2\Omega \dot\eta = 2 q \Omega^2 \xi,
\qquad
\ddot\eta + 2\Omega \dot\xi = 0.
$$

The terms $-2\Omega\dot\eta$ and $2\Omega\dot\xi$ are the Coriolis acceleration. The term $2q\Omega^2\xi$ is what
remains of gravity minus the centrifugal acceleration at offset $\xi$ (the tidal term).

The second equation integrates once to $\dot\eta + 2\Omega\xi = v_0$, using $\xi(0) = 0$ and $\dot\eta(0) = v_0$.
Substituting $\dot\eta = v_0 - 2\Omega\xi$ in the first equation gives

$$
\ddot\xi + (4 - 2q)\Omega^2\, \xi = 2\Omega v_0,
$$

a harmonic oscillator of frequency $\kappa = \sqrt{4 - 2q}\, \Omega$ about $\xi_g = 2\Omega v_0 / \kappa^2$. With
$\xi(0) = 0$ and $\dot\xi(0) = u_0$, its solution is the $\xi(t)$ above. Integrating $\dot\eta = v_0 - 2\Omega\xi$ from
$\eta(0) = 0$ gives $\eta(t)$, whose secular term is $(1 - 4\Omega^2/\kappa^2)\, v_0\, t = -q\Omega\xi_g t$.
</Derivation>

### Rayleigh's criterion: shear without instability

A rotating fluid without viscosity or magnetic fields is stable to axisymmetric disturbances when its specific angular
momentum $h = r^2\Omega$ rises outwards, which is the condition $\kappa^2 > 0$ <Cite id="rayleigh1917" />. The reason is
an exchange of rings. A ring moved outwards keeps its angular momentum, which is less than a circular orbit needs at its
new radius, so gravity exceeds the centrifugal force there and pulls it back in. A Kepler disc has $h = \sqrt{GMr}$,
which rises outwards, so its shear alone drives no linear instability even though $\Omega$ falls outwards. The
angular-momentum transport that lets real discs accrete needs another source. A weak magnetic field supplies one, since
it makes any flow with $d\Omega/dr < 0$ unstable <Cite id="balbus1998" />;
<NoteLink to="magnetorotational-instability" /> develops it.

## Worked example

Units are $G = M = 1$: lengths in units of a reference radius, times in units of $\sqrt{r_0^3 / GM}$, so a circular
orbit at radius 1 has $\Omega = 1$ and period $2\pi$.

1. **Two neighbouring rings.** At $r = 1$, $\Omega = 1$. At $r = 1.1$, $\Omega = 1.1^{-3/2} = 1/1.1537 = 0.8668$. The
   inner ring gains $1 - 0.8668 = 0.1332$ radians per time unit on the outer one, so it laps it every
   $2\pi / 0.1332 = 47.2$ time units, $7.5$ of its own orbits. The linear estimate $q\Omega\Delta r = 1.5 \times 0.1 =
   0.15$ is 13% high, because $\Omega$ is curved over a step of 10% in radius.
2. **The spiral after four orbits.** The line runs from $r = 1$ to $r = 3$, and $t = 4 \times 2\pi = 25.13$. At
   $r = 3$, $\Omega = 3^{-3/2} = 0.1925$. The line wraps $(1 - 0.1925) \times 25.13 / (2\pi) = 3.23$ times around the
   centre. At $r = 2$, $\Omega = 2^{-3/2} = 0.3536$ and $q\Omega t = 1.5 \times 0.3536 \times 25.13 = 13.33$, so the
   pitch angle is $\arctan(1/13.33) = 4.3°$.
3. **An azimuthal kick.** A particle on the circle $r = 1$ gets $v_0 = 0.05$ along the orbit, so its speed is 1.05.
   Hill's solution puts the guiding centre at $\xi_g = 2v_0/\Omega = 0.1$ and the radial offset between 0 and
   $2\xi_g = 0.2$. The guiding centre drifts backwards at $q\Omega\xi_g = 0.15$ arc-length units per time unit, $0.94$
   per orbit.
4. **The same kick, exactly.** The specific angular momentum is $h = 1.05$, so the exact guiding radius is
   $h^2 = 1.1025$. The energy is $E = \tfrac12 (1.05)^2 - 1 = -0.4488$, the semi-major axis $a = -1/(2E) = 1.1142$
   and the eccentricity $e = 1 - 1/a = 0.1025$, since the kick point is the pericentre. The apocentre is
   $a(1 + e) = 1.2284$, so the exact radial offset reaches $0.228$ against Hill's $0.2$. The difference is second order
   in the kick.
5. **The closure.** For this orbit the radial and orbital periods agree to all orders, since every bound Kepler orbit
   is a closed ellipse. With $u_0 = 0$ and $v_0 = 0.05$ in the figure, the pericentres fall at azimuths $360°$ and
   $720°$.

## Pitfalls

- **Several conventions for the shear rate.** The quantity $r\, d\Omega/dr$ equals $-\tfrac32\Omega$ for Kepler
  rotation. Galactic dynamics uses Oort's constant $A = -\tfrac12 r\, d\Omega/dr$, which is $\tfrac34\Omega$ for Kepler
  rotation <Cite id="binney2008" />. The exponent $q = -d\ln\Omega/d\ln r$ is $\tfrac32$. Each differs from the others
  by a factor.
- **Spiral arms in galaxies are not wound material lines.** A material arm in a galaxy with a flat rotation curve would
  wind up within a few rotations, far tighter than the arms observed, which is the winding problem
  <Cite id="binney2008" />. The spiral in the figure is kinematic winding, not a model of galactic arms.
- **Hill's solution is first order in the kick.** A radial kick also raises the energy at second order, which lengthens
  the period and adds a slow backward drift that Hill's equations miss. The figure shows it at $u_0 = 0.1$.
- **Stability to Rayleigh's criterion is not stability in general.** The criterion concerns axisymmetric disturbances of
  an ideal fluid. A weak magnetic field, which the criterion leaves out, destabilises the Kepler flow.
