<Definition>
The **Kepler problem** is the motion of a body under the acceleration $\ddot{\rvec} = -\mu\,\rvec/r^3$ towards a fixed
centre, where $\rvec$ is the body's position relative to the centre, $r = \norm{\rvec}$ and $\mu = GM$ is the
gravitational parameter of the attracting mass $M$. Every solution moves on a **conic section** with the centre at one
focus: an ellipse when the specific energy $\Ecal = \tfrac12 v^2 - \mu/r$ is negative, a parabola when it is zero and a
hyperbola when it is positive <Cite id="murray1999" />. The radius vector sweeps area at the constant rate $h/2$, where
$h$ is the specific angular momentum.
</Definition>

## Intuition

A body thrown sideways near a planet falls towards it and also moves across. If the sideways speed is small, the fall
wins and the body drops close to the planet, swings round it fast and climbs back out to where it started. If the
sideways speed is just right, the fall bends the path exactly as fast as the planet's surface curves away, and the
orbit is a circle. If the speed is large enough, the body climbs out and never comes back.

Three facts make this problem special. First, the path closes: a bound body returns to the same point with the same
velocity after one period, orbit after orbit, so the orbit is a fixed ellipse. Of all central forces only the
inverse-square law and the linear spring have this property for every bound start (Bertrand's theorem)
<Cite id="goldstein2002" />. Second, the shape of the path follows from two conserved numbers, the energy and the
angular momentum, without solving for the motion in time. Third, the timing along the ellipse is fixed by area: the
line from the centre to the body sweeps equal areas in equal times. Johannes Kepler found the ellipse and the area law
in Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars (1609) and the relation between period and size ten years later. Isaac Newton
derived all three from the inverse-square law in 1687.

## Two bodies reduce to one

Two bodies of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ at $\rvec_1$ and $\rvec_2$ attract each other with the force $G m_1 m_2/r^2$
along the line between them. Their centre of mass $(m_1\rvec_1 + m_2\rvec_2)/(m_1 + m_2)$ feels no net force, so it
moves in a straight line at constant speed. The separation $\rvec = \rvec_2 - \rvec_1$ obeys
$\ddot{\rvec} = -G(m_1 + m_2)\,\rvec/r^3$, which is the Kepler problem with $\mu = G(m_1 + m_2)$. Equivalently, the
relative motion is that of one particle of **reduced mass** $m_1 m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$ in the potential energy
$-G m_1 m_2/r$ <Cite id="goldstein2002" />. Each body then moves on a copy of the relative orbit about the centre of
mass, scaled by $m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$ for body 1 and $m_1/(m_1 + m_2)$ for body 2. The rest of this note treats one body
about a fixed centre with parameter $\mu$.

## Two conserved quantities

The **specific energy** (energy per unit mass of the body) is

$$
\Ecal = \tfrac12 v^2 - \frac{\mu}{r},
$$

where $v = \norm{\vvec}$ is the speed and $\vvec = \dot{\rvec}$ the velocity. Its time derivative is
$\vvec \cdot \dot{\vvec} + \mu\,\dot r/r^2 = -\mu\,(\vvec \cdot \rvec)/r^3 + \mu\,\dot r/r^2$, which is zero because
$\vvec \cdot \rvec = r\dot r$. So $\Ecal$ is constant.

The **specific angular momentum** is the vector $\hvec = \rvec \times \vvec$. Its derivative is
$\vvec \times \vvec + \rvec \times \ddot{\rvec} = \zeros$, because the acceleration is parallel to $\rvec$. So $\hvec$
is constant. Two consequences follow at once. The body stays in the plane through the centre perpendicular to
$\hvec$, so the problem is two-dimensional. And the area swept by the radius vector in a short time $dt$ is the
triangle $\tfrac12\norm{\rvec \times \vvec\,dt} = \tfrac12 h\,dt$, with $h = \norm{\hvec}$. The area rate $h/2$ is
constant, which is **Kepler's second law**. In the plane of the orbit, with coordinates $(x, y)$, the angular
momentum is the single number $h = x v_y - y v_x$.

## The orbit is a conic

A third conserved vector gives the shape. Take the cross product of the equation of motion with $\hvec$:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\paren{\vvec \times \hvec} = \ddot{\rvec} \times \hvec = -\frac{\mu}{r^3}\,\rvec \times (\rvec \times
\vvec) = -\frac{\mu}{r^3}\paren{\rvec\,(\rvec \cdot \vvec) - \vvec\,r^2}.
$$

The last step is the vector triple product identity $\avec \times (\bvec \times \cvec) = \bvec(\avec \cdot \cvec) -
\cvec(\avec \cdot \bvec)$. With $\rvec \cdot \vvec = r\dot r$, the right-hand side is
$\mu\paren{\vvec/r - \rvec\,\dot r/r^2}$, which is exactly $\mu\,\frac{d}{dt}(\rvec/r)$. Both sides are time
derivatives, so their difference is constant:

$$
\evec = \frac{\vvec \times \hvec}{\mu} - \frac{\rvec}{r}.
$$

The dimensionless vector $\evec$ is the **eccentricity vector**. Multiplied by $\mu$, it is the
**Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector** <Cite id="goldstein2002" />. It lies in the orbital plane, because both terms do.

Dot $\evec$ with $\rvec$. The scalar triple product gives $\rvec \cdot (\vvec \times \hvec) = \hvec \cdot (\rvec \times
\vvec) = h^2$, so

$$
\evec \cdot \rvec = \frac{h^2}{\mu} - r.
$$

Write $e = \norm{\evec}$ and let $\nu$ be the angle from $\evec$ to $\rvec$, the **true anomaly**. Then
$\evec \cdot \rvec = e r \cos\nu$, and solving for $r$ gives

$$
r = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\nu}, \qquad p = \frac{h^2}{\mu}.
$$

This is the polar equation of a conic with one focus at the centre, **eccentricity** $e$ and **semi-latus rectum**
$p$ (the distance from the focus to the orbit at $\nu = \pm 90°$). The orbit is a circle for $e = 0$, an ellipse for
$0 < e < 1$, a parabola for $e = 1$ and a hyperbola for $e > 1$. The distance is smallest at $\nu = 0$, so $\evec$
points from the centre to the **pericentre**, the closest point. This is **Kepler's first law** for bound orbits.

<Derivation title="The same conic from the Binet equation">
In <NoteLink to="polar-coordinates">polar coordinates</NoteLink> $(r, \theta)$ in the orbital plane, the radial equation
of motion is $\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 =
-\mu/r^2$ and the angular momentum is $h = r^2\dot\theta$. Substitute $u = 1/r$ and use $\theta$ as the independent
variable. The centripetal term is $r\dot\theta^2 = h^2u^3$. Then $\dot r = -\dot u/u^2 = -h\,du/d\theta$ and $\ddot r = -h\,\dot\theta\,d^2u/d\theta^2 = -h^2u^2\,d^2u/
d\theta^2$. The radial equation becomes

$$
-h^2u^2\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} - h^2u^3 = -\mu u^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u =
\frac{\mu}{h^2}.
$$

This is a harmonic oscillator in $\theta$ with a constant forcing. Its general solution is
$u = \mu/h^2\,\paren{1 + e\cos(\theta - \omega)}$ for constants $e \ge 0$ and $\omega$, which is the conic
$r = p/(1 + e\cos\nu)$ with $\nu = \theta - \omega$ <Cite id="goldstein2002" />. The constant $\omega$, the **argument
of pericentre**, is the direction of $\evec$.
</Derivation>

## Energy fixes the size

The length of $\evec$ ties the shape to the energy. Since $\vvec$ is perpendicular to $\hvec$, the length of
$\vvec \times \hvec$ is $vh$, and its dot product with $\rvec$ is $h^2$. Expanding $\mu^2 e^2 = \norm{\vvec \times
\hvec - \mu\rvec/r}^2$ gives $v^2h^2 - 2\mu h^2/r + \mu^2$, so

$$
e^2 = 1 + \frac{2\Ecal h^2}{\mu^2}.
$$

Negative energy gives $e < 1$ (a closed ellipse), zero energy gives $e = 1$ and positive energy gives $e > 1$. For an
ellipse, the pericentre and apocentre distances are $p/(1 + e)$ and $p/(1 - e)$, and the **semi-major axis** $a$ is
their mean, $a = p/(1 - e^2)$. Substituting $p = h^2/\mu$ and $1 - e^2 = -2\Ecal h^2/\mu^2$ gives

$$
\Ecal = -\frac{\mu}{2a}.
$$

The energy depends on the size of the ellipse only, not on its shape. Putting this into the definition of $\Ecal$
gives the **vis-viva equation**, the speed at any distance on a given orbit:

$$
v^2 = \mu\paren{\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}}.
$$

Two speeds at distance $r$ stand out. The **circular speed** $\sqrt{\mu/r}$ is the vis-viva speed with $a = r$. The
**escape speed** $\sqrt{2\mu/r}$ makes $\Ecal = 0$. A body launched at distance $r$ is bound if and only if its speed is
below the escape speed, whatever the direction of launch. The direction sets $h$, and therefore the eccentricity.

## Kepler's third law

An ellipse with semi-major axis $a$ has semi-minor axis $b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}$ and area $\pi ab$. The radius vector
sweeps it at the constant rate $h/2$, so the period is $T = 2\pi ab/h$. With $h = \sqrt{\mu p} = \sqrt{\mu a(1 -
e^2)}$,

$$
T = \frac{2\pi a^2\sqrt{1 - e^2}}{\sqrt{\mu a (1 - e^2)}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}.
$$

The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, which is **Kepler's third law**
<Cite id="murray1999" />. The eccentricity cancels: every orbit with the same $a$ has the same period, from the circle
to a needle-thin ellipse. For two bodies, $\mu = G(m_1 + m_2)$, so the measured period and size of a binary orbit give
the total mass.

## Worked example

Take units with $G M = 1$: distance in units of the starting radius $r_0$, and time in units of
$\sqrt{r_0^3/GM}$, so that a circular orbit at $r = 1$ has speed 1 and period $2\pi$. Start the body at
$\rvec_0 = (1, 0)$ with velocity $\vvec_0 = (0, 0.8)$.

1. **Energy.** $\Ecal = \tfrac12(0.8)^2 - 1/1 = 0.32 - 1 = -0.68$. It is negative, so the orbit is an ellipse. The
   speed $0.8$ is below the escape speed $\sqrt 2 = 1.414$.
2. **Angular momentum.** $h = x v_y - y v_x = 1 \times 0.8 - 0 = 0.8$, so $p = h^2/\mu = 0.64$.
3. **Eccentricity.** $e^2 = 1 + 2(-0.68)(0.64) = 1 - 0.8704 = 0.1296$, so $e = 0.36$.
4. **Direction of pericentre.** In the plane, $\vvec \times \hvec = (v_y h, -v_x h) = (0.64, 0)$, so $\evec = (0.64,
   0) - (1, 0) = (-0.36, 0)$. Pericentre lies on the negative $x$-axis, and the start is the apocentre.
5. **Distances.** The pericentre is at $0.64/1.36 = 0.471$ and the apocentre at $0.64/0.64 = 1$, as expected for the
   start. The semi-major axis is their mean, $0.735$, and $-\mu/(2\Ecal) = 1/1.36 = 0.735$ agrees.
6. **Period.** $T = 2\pi(0.735)^{3/2} = 2\pi \times 0.631 = 3.96$ time units, shorter than the $2\pi$ of the circular
   orbit at $r = 1$ because the ellipse is smaller.
7. **Speed at pericentre.** From the area law, $v_p = h/r_p = 0.8/0.471 = 1.70$. Vis-viva gives $v_p^2 = 2/0.471 -
   1/0.735 = 4.25 - 1.36 = 2.89$, so $v_p = 1.70$ again. The body moves $1.70/0.8 = 2.1$ times faster at pericentre
   than at apocentre, the inverse ratio of the two distances.

## Pitfalls

- **The conic is not the clock.** The orbit equation gives the path but not where the body is at a given time. That
  needs <NoteLink to="keplers-equation">Kepler's equation</NoteLink>, which has no closed-form solution.
- **Near-parabolic orbits.** As $\Ecal \to 0$ the semi-major axis $a = -\mu/(2\Ecal)$ diverges and the period
  becomes infinite. Formulas in $p$ and $e$ stay finite through $e = 1$ and are the ones to use there.
- **Two bodies, not one.** When the companion's mass is not negligible, the third law uses $\mu = G(m_1 + m_2)$.
  Using $GM$ of the heavier body alone overestimates the period by the factor $\sqrt{(m_1 + m_2)/m_1}$.
- **Real orbits precess.** The ellipse stays fixed only for an exact inverse-square force between two point masses.
  Other planets, the oblateness of the central body and general relativity add small extra forces that turn the
  eccentricity vector slowly, so the pericentre advances <Cite id="murray1999" />. A <NoteLink
  to="three-body-problem">third body</NoteLink> can change the orbit completely.
- **Close passes break fixed-step integrators.** The time scale near pericentre is about $\sqrt{r_p^3/\mu}$, which falls
  to zero for a start aimed at the mass. A fixed step that is fine elsewhere then jumps across the pericentre, and the
  energy error grows to the size of the energy. Adaptive steps or a regularising
  <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of variables</NoteLink> handle near-collisions.
