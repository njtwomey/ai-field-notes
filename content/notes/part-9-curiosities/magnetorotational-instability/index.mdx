<Definition>
The **magnetorotational instability** is the linear instability of a differentially rotating, electrically conducting
disc threaded by a weak magnetic field, whenever the angular velocity $\Omega(R)$ falls with radius $R$
<Cite id="balbus1991" />. Magnetic tension couples fluid elements on neighbouring orbits like a spring of constant
$k = (k_z v_A)^2$, with $k_z$ the vertical wavenumber of the disturbance and $v_A$ the Alfvén speed. In a Keplerian
disc, two masses joined by such a spring separate exponentially for $0 < k < 3\Omega^2$, at a rate of at most
$\tfrac34 \Omega$ <Cite id="balbus1998" />.
</Definition>

## The problem

An accretion disc needs a stress that moves angular momentum outward, or its gas never falls in
(<NoteLink to="viscous-spreading-of-an-accretion-ring" />). Molecular viscosity is many orders of magnitude too weak,
so the stress has to come from turbulence <Cite id="pringle1981" />.

A Keplerian disc without a magnetic field resists turbulence. Rayleigh's criterion says a rotating flow is stable to
axisymmetric disturbances when its specific angular momentum $R^2 \Omega$ increases outward
<Cite id="rayleigh1917" />. On Kepler orbits $R^2 \Omega = \sqrt{GMR}$, which increases with $R$, so the disc passes
the test by a wide margin. A fluid element pushed outward has less angular momentum than its new neighbours and is
pulled back. That restoring motion is the epicyclic oscillation of
<NoteLink to="keplerian-shear">Keplerian shear</NoteLink>.

Balbus and Hawley (1991) showed that a weak magnetic field changes the answer <Cite id="balbus1991" />. A field that
is too weak to matter for the disc's structure still links fluid elements on neighbouring orbits. The link turns the
stable epicycle into a runaway, and the instability grows by a factor of about 100 per orbit. A mechanical model with
the same equations shows the mechanism: two masses on neighbouring orbits joined by a spring
<Cite id="balbus1998" />.

## Hill's equations

Hill's equations describe motion near a circular orbit of radius $R_0$ in a frame that rotates with that orbit's
angular velocity $\Omega = \Omega(R_0)$ (s⁻¹). Take local Cartesian coordinates with $x = R - R_0$ pointing radially
outward and $y$ pointing along the orbital motion, both in metres, and small compared with $R_0$.

In the rotating frame, a mass feels gravity, the centrifugal acceleration $\Omega^2 R$ and the Coriolis acceleration.
Gravity and the centrifugal term cancel at $R_0$. To first order in $x$, the radial remainder is

$$
-\frac{GM}{(R_0 + x)^2} + \Omega^2 (R_0 + x) \approx \paren{\frac{2GM}{R_0^3} + \Omega^2} x = 3\Omega^2 x,
$$

using $GM / R_0^3 = \Omega^2$. This is the tidal acceleration, $-(\mathrm{d}\Omega^2 / \mathrm{d}\ln R) \, x$ for a
general rotation law. The Coriolis acceleration $-2\Omega \hat{\zvec} \times \dot{\xvec}$ for velocity
$\dot{\xvec} = (\dot x, \dot y)$ has components $(2\Omega \dot y, -2\Omega \dot x)$. With an extra force per unit mass
$(F_x, F_y)$, the equations of motion are

$$
\ddot x - 2\Omega \dot y = 3\Omega^2 x + F_x, \qquad \ddot y + 2\Omega \dot x = F_y.
$$

With no force, $x = x_0$ and $\dot y = -\tfrac32 \Omega x_0$ solve the equations: a circular orbit at $R_0 + x_0$,
sliding past the reference orbit with the Keplerian shear. The quantity $\dot y + 2\Omega x$ changes only through
$F_y$. It is the specific angular momentum relative to the reference orbit, divided by $R_0$:
$\ell \approx R_0^2 \Omega + R_0 (\dot y + 2\Omega x)$.

## Two masses on a spring

Join two equal masses by a spring. Let $\xivec = (\xi_x, \xi_y)$ be the position of the second mass relative to the
first, and let the spring give the pair a relative acceleration $-k \xivec$. The constant $k$ (s⁻²) is the square of
the frequency at which the pair would oscillate in a non-rotating frame. Hill's equations are linear, so the
separation obeys them with $F = -k \xivec$ <Cite id="balbus1998" />:

$$
\ddot \xi_x - 2\Omega \dot \xi_y = (3\Omega^2 - k) \xi_x, \qquad \ddot \xi_y + 2\Omega \dot \xi_x = -k \xi_y.
$$

**The dispersion relation.** Try $\xivec = (X, Y) e^{st}$ with constant amplitudes $X$, $Y$ and rate $s$ (s⁻¹).
Substituting gives two linear equations,

$$
(s^2 - 3\Omega^2 + k) X - 2\Omega s Y = 0, \qquad 2\Omega s X + (s^2 + k) Y = 0.
$$

A nonzero $(X, Y)$ needs a vanishing determinant,
$(s^2 - 3\Omega^2 + k)(s^2 + k) + 4\Omega^2 s^2 = 0$. Expanding,

$$
s^4 + (\Omega^2 + 2k) \, s^2 + k (k - 3\Omega^2) = 0.
$$

This is Balbus and Hawley's equation (111) for the Keplerian case, with $\omega^2 = -s^2$, epicyclic frequency
$\kappa = \Omega$ and $\mathrm{d}\Omega^2 / \mathrm{d}\ln R = -3\Omega^2$ <Cite id="balbus1998" />.

**The unstable band.** The equation is a quadratic in $S = s^2$. Its two roots sum to $-(\Omega^2 + 2k) < 0$ and
multiply to $k(k - 3\Omega^2)$. A positive root, which gives a growing mode $s = +\sqrt{S}$, exists exactly when the
product is negative:

$$
0 < k < 3\Omega^2.
$$

For $k > 3\Omega^2$ both roots are negative and both modes oscillate. At $k = 0$ the masses drift apart only
linearly, through the shear. The upper edge $3\Omega^2 = -\mathrm{d}\Omega^2 / \mathrm{d}\ln R$ is the condition
$(k_z v_A)^2 > -\mathrm{d}\Omega^2 / \mathrm{d}\ln R$ for stability in Balbus and Hawley's equation (108)
<Cite id="balbus1998" />.

**The fastest growth.** At the largest growing root, $\mathrm{d}S / \mathrm{d}k = 0$. Differentiating the quadratic
$F(S, k) = S^2 + (\Omega^2 + 2k) S + k^2 - 3\Omega^2 k = 0$ implicitly, $\mathrm{d}S / \mathrm{d}k = 0$ requires
$\partial F / \partial k = 2S + 2k - 3\Omega^2 = 0$, so $k = \tfrac32 \Omega^2 - S$. Substituting back,

$$
S^2 + (4\Omega^2 - 2S) S + \paren{\tfrac32 \Omega^2 - S}^2 - 3\Omega^2 \paren{\tfrac32 \Omega^2 - S} = 0,
$$

where the $S^2$ terms cancel and the rest reduces to $4\Omega^2 S - \tfrac94 \Omega^4 = 0$. So
$S = \tfrac{9}{16} \Omega^2$, and

$$
s_\text{max} = \tfrac34 \Omega \quad \text{at} \quad k = \tfrac{15}{16} \Omega^2.
$$

Both values match Balbus and Hawley's equation (114) <Cite id="balbus1998" />. For a general rotation law the maximum
is $s_\text{max} = \tfrac12 \abs{\mathrm{d}\Omega / \mathrm{d}\ln R}$, which is $\tfrac34 \Omega$ for $\Omega \propto
R^{-3/2}$.

## Why a weak spring destabilises

Start the two masses on a radial line, the inner one at $R_0 - \delta/2$ and the outer one at $R_0 + \delta/2$, each on
its circular orbit. The inner mass orbits faster and pulls ahead, so the spring stretches along the orbit. The tension
pulls the inner mass backwards and the outer mass forwards.

A backward pull removes angular momentum from the inner mass, so it can no longer hold its orbit and sinks inward. A
smaller orbit is faster, so the inner mass pulls further ahead. The outer mass gains angular momentum, rises to a
slower orbit and falls further behind. The separation grows, the tension grows, and the exchange runs away
<Cite id="balbus1998" />.

The spring has to be weak. A stiff spring ($k > 3\Omega^2$) snaps the masses back within a fraction of an orbit,
before the shear can stretch it. The pair then oscillates. Gravity and rotation alone make the disc stable;
the instability lives in the band where the spring is strong enough to transfer angular momentum and too weak to
resist the shear.

## Worked example

Take $G = M = 1$ and $R_0 = 1$, so $\Omega = 1$ and one orbit lasts $2\pi \approx 6.28$. Take $k = 0.5$.

1. **The quadratic.** $\Omega^2 + 2k = 2$ and $k(k - 3\Omega^2) = 0.5 \times (-2.5) = -1.25$, so
   $S^2 + 2S - 1.25 = 0$.
2. **The roots.** $S = -1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 1.25} = -1 \pm 1.5$, so $S = 0.5$ or $S = -2.5$.
3. **The growing mode.** $s = \sqrt{0.5} = 0.707$. Over one orbit the separation grows by
   $e^{0.707 \times 6.28} = e^{4.44} \approx 85$.
4. **The other mode.** $s^2 = -2.5$ is an oscillation at angular frequency $\sqrt{2.5} = 1.58$, which neither grows
   nor decays.
5. **The direction of growth.** The second equation of the pair gives $Y / X = -2\Omega s / (s^2 + k) =
   -1.414 / 1.0 = -1.41$. The separation grows along a line that trails the radial direction by
   $\arctan(1.41) = 54.7^\circ$: the outer mass falls behind as it moves out.
6. **The check.** The first equation gives $(0.5 - 3 + 0.5) X - 2 \times 0.707 \times (-1.414 X) = -2X + 2X = 0$.

At the fastest spring, $k = 15/16 = 0.9375$, the rate is $s = 0.75$. The separation grows by $e^{0.75 \times 6.28} =
e^{4.71} \approx 111$ per orbit, and the energy of the disturbance by $111^2 \approx 1.2 \times 10^4$, the "more than
$10^4$ amplification in energy per orbit" of Balbus and Hawley <Cite id="balbus1998" />.

## The magnetic field in a real disc

In a conducting disc threaded by a vertical field $B_z$, a disturbance with vertical wavenumber $k_z$ that displaces the
gas by $\xivec$ bends the field lines. Magnetic tension then pulls the gas back with acceleration $-(k_z v_A)^2 \xivec$,
with $v_A = B / \sqrt{\mu_0 \rho}$ the Alfvén speed (m s⁻¹), $\mu_0$ the vacuum permeability and $\rho$ the gas density.
The equations of motion are exactly the spring equations with $k = (k_z v_A)^2$ <Cite id="balbus1998" />. However weak
the field, long enough wavelengths put $k$ in the unstable band, so the only requirement is that an unstable wavelength
fits inside the disc's thickness: the field must be subthermal, with magnetic pressure below the gas pressure. The
instability does not saturate as a single growing mode. Simulations show it breaking into magnetised turbulence whose
correlated magnetic and velocity fluctuations carry angular momentum outward, with the magnetic (Maxwell) stress several
times the Reynolds stress <Cite id="balbus1998" />. Averaged over the turbulence, the stress acts as the viscosity of
Shakura and Sunyaev, $\nu = \alpha c_s H$ with $c_s$ the sound speed and $H$ the disc's scale height
<Cite id="shakura1973" />. The stratified simulations reviewed by Balbus and Hawley (1998) give $\alpha$ between about
0.001 and 0.01, values they regard as lower limits <Cite id="balbus1998" />.

## Assumptions

- The model is local: separations are far smaller than $R_0$, so the tidal and Coriolis terms are linear in the
  displacement.
- The spring is linear and massless, and acts along the line between the masses. The magnetic analogue holds for
  axisymmetric disturbances with vertical wavenumber in ideal magnetohydrodynamics.
- Pressure plays no part. Balbus and Hawley's equations (106) and (107) take the incompressible, Boussinesq limit,
  which removes sound waves.

## Pitfalls

- **Weak, not zero.** With no field there is no spring, and the Keplerian disc is stable. The growth rate does not
  depend on the field's strength, only on $k_z v_A$; a weaker field moves the fastest wavelength to shorter scales.
- **Stiff springs stabilise.** A strong field puts every wavelength that fits in the disc above $k = 3\Omega^2$, and
  the instability disappears.
- **The disc must conduct.** The field couples to the gas only where the gas is sufficiently ionised. Of the main
  classes of disc, Balbus and Hawley single out discs around forming stars as the ones where finite resistivity can
  matter <Cite id="balbus1998" />.
- **Linear growth is not transport.** The dispersion relation gives the onset. The value of $\alpha$ comes from the
  nonlinear turbulence and depends on the field geometry and the simulation, so no single value is canonical
  <Cite id="balbus1998" />.
