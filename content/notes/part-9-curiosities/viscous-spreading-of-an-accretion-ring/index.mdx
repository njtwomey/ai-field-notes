<Definition>
A thin **accretion disc** is gas on near-circular <NoteLink to="kepler-orbits">Kepler orbits</NoteLink> around a central
mass $M$, described by its surface density $\Sigma(R, t)$ (mass per unit area, kg m⁻²) at radius $R$ and time $t$.
Viscous torques between neighbouring annuli move angular momentum outward, and
<NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">conservation of mass</NoteLink> and angular momentum then gives the
<NoteLink to="heat-equation">diffusion equation</NoteLink> of Lynden-Bell and Pringle <Cite id="lyndenbell1974" />,

$$
\frac{\partial \Sigma}{\partial t} = \frac{3}{R} \frac{\partial}{\partial R} \brack{R^{1/2} \frac{\partial}{\partial R}
\paren{\nu \Sigma R^{1/2}}},
$$

where $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity (m² s⁻¹). For constant $\nu$, a ring of mass $m$ released at radius $R_0$ has
an exact solution in terms of the modified Bessel function $I_{1/4}$ <Cite id="pringle1981" />.
</Definition>

## The problem

Gas on a circular Kepler orbit at radius $R$ has specific angular momentum $\ell = R^2 \Omega = \sqrt{GMR}$, where
$\Omega = \sqrt{GM/R^3}$ is the angular velocity and $G$ is the gravitational constant. To move inward, the gas has to
give angular momentum away. Without a torque, gas on a circular orbit stays there forever.

In a <NoteLink to="keplerian-shear">Keplerian disc</NoteLink> the inner annuli orbit faster than the outer ones, so
neighbouring annuli slide past each other. Any friction between them speeds the outer annulus up and slows the inner
one down. The inner gas loses angular momentum and falls inward. The outer gas gains angular momentum and moves
outward.

A single ring of gas shows the consequence most clearly. Viscosity spreads the ring in both directions. In the end
nearly all the mass reaches the centre and all the angular momentum reaches arbitrarily large radii, carried by a
fraction of the mass that tends to zero <Cite id="pringle1981" />. The specific angular momentum $\sqrt{GMR}$ grows
without bound with radius, which is what lets a vanishing mass hold a fixed angular momentum.

## Mass and angular momentum of an annulus

Take an annulus between $R$ and $R + \Delta R$ in a disc with surface density $\Sigma(R, t)$ and radial velocity
$v_R(R, t)$ (m s⁻¹, positive outward). Its mass is $2\pi R \, \Delta R \, \Sigma$ and its angular momentum is
$2\pi R \, \Delta R \, \Sigma \ell$, with $\ell(R) = \sqrt{GMR}$ fixed by the orbit.

**Mass.** The annulus gains mass only through gas crossing its two edges. The mass flow outward through radius $R$ is
$2\pi R \Sigma v_R$ (kg s⁻¹). In the limit $\Delta R \to 0$, conservation of mass reads

$$
R \frac{\partial \Sigma}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial R} \paren{R \Sigma v_R} = 0.
$$

**Angular momentum.** Gas crossing the edges carries angular momentum $\ell$ per unit mass. The viscous stress adds a
torque. Let $\mathcal{T}(R, t)$ (N m) be the torque that the gas outside radius $R$ exerts on the gas inside it. The
viscous force per unit length of the circle $R$ is $\nu \Sigma R \, \mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R$, the shear rate
$R \, \mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R$ times the dynamic viscosity integrated through the disc's thickness, $\nu\Sigma$.
Multiplying by the circumference $2\pi R$ and the lever arm $R$ gives

$$
\mathcal{T} = 2\pi \nu \Sigma R^3 \frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}R}.
$$

For a Keplerian disc, $\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}R = -\tfrac{3}{2} \Omega / R$, so
$\mathcal{T} = -3\pi \nu \Sigma R^2 \Omega = -3\pi \sqrt{GM} \, \nu \Sigma R^{1/2}$. The torque is negative: the slower
outer gas holds the faster inner gas back. The annulus feels $\mathcal{T}(R + \Delta R)$ from outside and
$-\mathcal{T}(R)$ from inside, a net torque $(\partial \mathcal{T}/\partial R) \Delta R$. Conservation of angular
momentum reads

$$
R \frac{\partial (\Sigma \ell)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial R} \paren{R \Sigma v_R \ell} =
\frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{\partial \mathcal{T}}{\partial R}.
$$

**Eliminating the radial velocity.** The specific angular momentum $\ell$ does not depend on $t$. Expanding the left
side as $\ell \brack{R \, \partial_t \Sigma + \partial_R (R \Sigma v_R)} + R \Sigma v_R \, \mathrm{d}\ell/\mathrm{d}R$,
the bracket vanishes by conservation of mass. What remains fixes the mass flux:

$$
R \Sigma v_R = \frac{1}{2\pi \, \mathrm{d}\ell/\mathrm{d}R} \frac{\partial \mathcal{T}}{\partial R}.
$$

With $\mathrm{d}\ell/\mathrm{d}R = \tfrac{1}{2} \sqrt{GM} R^{-1/2}$ and $\partial_R \mathcal{T} = -3\pi \sqrt{GM} \,
\partial_R (\nu \Sigma R^{1/2})$, the factors of $\sqrt{GM}$ cancel:

$$
R \Sigma v_R = -3 R^{1/2} \frac{\partial}{\partial R} \paren{\nu \Sigma R^{1/2}}.
$$

Gas moves inward wherever $\nu \Sigma R^{1/2}$ increases outward. Substituting $R \Sigma v_R$ into conservation of
mass gives the equation in the definition, Pringle's equation (2.10) <Cite id="pringle1981" />:

$$
\frac{\partial \Sigma}{\partial t} = \frac{3}{R} \frac{\partial}{\partial R} \brack{R^{1/2} \frac{\partial}{\partial R}
\paren{\nu \Sigma R^{1/2}}}.
$$

The rate at which mass flows inward through radius $R$ is
$\dot M(R) = -2\pi R \Sigma v_R = 6\pi R^{1/2} \, \partial_R (\nu \Sigma R^{1/2})$ (kg s⁻¹). The equation is a
diffusion equation for $\Sigma$. It is linear when $\nu$ depends on $R$ alone, and nonlinear when $\nu$ depends on
$\Sigma$ through the disc's temperature.

## The spreading ring for constant viscosity

**The solution.** Place a ring of mass $m$ at radius $R_0$ at $t = 0$, so $\Sigma(R, 0) = m \, \delta(R - R_0) /
(2\pi R_0)$. Measure radius by $x = R / R_0$ and time by $\tau = 12 \nu t / R_0^2$, both dimensionless. With $\nu$
constant and zero torque at the centre, the surface density is <Cite id="pringle1981" />

$$
\Sigma(x, \tau) = \frac{m}{\pi R_0^2} \, \tau^{-1} x^{-1/4} \exp\paren{-\frac{1 + x^2}{\tau}} I_{1/4}\paren{\frac{2x}{\tau}}.
$$

Here $I_{1/4}$ is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order $1/4$
<Cite id="olver2010" />. Its large-argument form $I_{1/4}(z) \approx e^z / \sqrt{2\pi z}$ shows the early shape. For
$\tau \ll 1$, the ring is a Gaussian in $x$ about $x = 1$,
$\Sigma \approx (m / \pi R_0^2) \, x^{-3/4} (4\pi\tau)^{-1/2} \exp(-(1 - x)^2 / \tau)$, with standard deviation
$\sqrt{\tau/2}$ in $x$, or $\sqrt{6 \nu t}$ in $R$. Near the ring the gas diffuses with diffusivity $3\nu$.

<Derivation title="Separation of variables and the Green's function">
Write $\phi = R^{1/4} \Sigma$, so that $\nu \Sigma R^{1/2} = \nu R^{1/4} \phi$. Substituting into the diffusion
equation with constant $\nu$ and collecting terms gives

$$
\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = 3\nu \paren{\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial R^2} + \frac{1}{R} \frac{\partial
\phi}{\partial R} - \frac{1}{16 R^2} \phi}.
$$

The bracket is Bessel's operator of order $1/4$. The separated solutions are
$\phi = J_{\pm 1/4}(kR) \, e^{-3\nu k^2 t}$ for wavenumber $k > 0$ (m⁻¹), with $J$ the ordinary Bessel function.

Near the centre, $J_{1/4}(kR) \propto R^{1/4}$ gives a finite $\Sigma$ and a torque
$\mathcal{T} \propto \nu \Sigma R^{1/2} \to 0$: zero torque at the centre, so gas falls in freely. The other order,
$J_{-1/4}$, gives $\Sigma \propto R^{-1/2}$, a finite torque and zero mass flow at the centre, which conserves the disc
mass instead.

With the zero-torque order, superpose the modes,
$\Sigma = R^{-1/4} \int_0^\infty A(k) J_{1/4}(kR) e^{-3\nu k^2 t} k \, \mathrm{d}k$. At $t = 0$ this is a Hankel
transform of $R^{1/4} \Sigma(R, 0)$, whose inverse gives
$A(k) = \int_0^\infty R^{1/4} \Sigma(R, 0) J_{1/4}(kR) R \, \mathrm{d}R = (m R_0^{1/4} / 2\pi) J_{1/4}(kR_0)$. Weber's
second exponential integral <Cite id="olver2010" /> (DLMF 10.22.67),

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-p^2 k^2} J_\mu(ak) J_\mu(bk) \, k \, \mathrm{d}k = \frac{1}{2p^2}
\exp\paren{-\frac{a^2 + b^2}{4p^2}} I_\mu\paren{\frac{ab}{2p^2}},
$$

with $p^2 = 3\nu t$, $a = R_0$ and $b = R$, gives the solution above, since $4p^2 = \tau R_0^2$.
</Derivation>

**Where the mass goes.** At the centre, $I_{1/4}(z) \approx (z/2)^{1/4} / \Gamma(5/4)$ for small $z$, so
$\Sigma(0, \tau) = (m / \pi R_0^2) \, \tau^{-5/4} e^{-1/\tau} / \Gamma(5/4)$, where $\Gamma$ is the
<NoteLink to="gamma-function">gamma function</NoteLink>. The inflow rate at the centre is the limit of $\dot M(R)$ as
$R \to 0$, $\dot M_0 = 3\pi \nu \Sigma(0, t)$. Integrating it over time, with
$\mathrm{d}t = R_0^2 \, \mathrm{d}\tau / (12\nu)$, the mass that has reached the centre by time $\tau$ is

$$
M_\text{centre}(\tau) = \frac{m}{4 \Gamma(5/4)} \int_0^\tau s^{-5/4} e^{-1/s} \, \mathrm{d}s = m \, Q\paren{\tfrac14,
\tfrac{1}{\tau}},
$$

where $Q(a, y) = \Gamma(a, y) / \Gamma(a)$ is the regularised upper incomplete gamma function. The disc keeps
$m \, P(\tfrac14, 1/\tau) = m (1 - Q)$. For $\tau \gg 1$ the disc mass falls as
$m \, \tau^{-1/4} / \Gamma(5/4)$: slowly, but to zero.

**Where the angular momentum goes.** The disc's angular momentum is
$J = \int_0^\infty 2\pi R \Sigma \sqrt{GMR} \, \mathrm{d}R = 2m\sqrt{GMR_0} \int_0^\infty x^{3/2} (\pi R_0^2 / m)
\Sigma \, \mathrm{d}x$. The integrand is $x^{5/4}$ times the Gaussian and $I_{1/4}$ factors, which is the form of
Weber's first exponential integral, and the result is $J = m \sqrt{GMR_0}$ at every $\tau$. The gas reaching the
centre carries no angular momentum, because $\ell \to 0$ there. All of the ring's angular momentum stays in the disc.
For $\tau \gg 1$, half of it lies beyond about $R_0 \sqrt{\tau}$, a radius that grows without bound.

## Worked example

Work in units with $G = M = 1$, $R_0 = 1$ and $m = 1$, so lengths are in $R_0$, times in $1/\Omega(R_0)$, and the
orbital period at $R_0$ is $2\pi \approx 6.28$. Take $\nu = 10^{-3}$.

1. **Timescales.** The viscous time at $R_0$ is $R_0^2 / \nu = 1000$, which is $1000 / 6.28 \approx 159$ orbits.
   Choose $t = 83.3$, so $\tau = 12 \times 10^{-3} \times 83.3 = 1.00$.
2. **Surface density at the centre.** With $\tau = 1$, $\Sigma(0) = (1/\pi) \, e^{-1} / \Gamma(5/4) =
   (1/\pi)(0.3679 / 0.9064) = 0.129$ (units $m / R_0^2$).
3. **Inflow at the centre.** $\dot M_0 = 3\pi \nu \Sigma(0) = 3\pi \times 10^{-3} \times 0.129 = 1.22 \times 10^{-3}$
   ring masses per unit time.
4. **Mass at the centre so far.** $Q(\tfrac14, 1) = 0.0679$, so 6.8% of the ring has reached the centre and 93.2% is
   still in the disc.
5. **Mass either side of the initial radius.** Integrating $2\pi R \Sigma$ numerically over $R < R_0$ gives 0.355 and
   over $R > R_0$ gives 0.577. The sum, 0.932, matches step 4.
6. **Angular momentum outside the initial radius.** Integrating $2\pi R \Sigma \sqrt{R}$ over $R > R_0$ gives 0.721
   of the total $J = 1$. The 57.7% of the mass beyond $R_0$ carries 72.1% of the angular momentum.

The split sharpens with time. At $\tau = 100$, 65.2% of the ring has reached the centre, and the 34.5% beyond $R_0$
carries 99.7% of the angular momentum. Early on, slightly more than half of the mass moves outward: at $\tau = 0.01$,
50.7% of it lies beyond $R_0$. Spreading the mass evenly in $x$ would lower the mean of $\sqrt{x}$, and keeping $J$
fixed requires the extra outward shift.

## The viscous timescale

A disc changes on the viscous timescale $t_\nu = R^2 / \nu$: the time for diffusion with diffusivity of order $\nu$ to
cross a distance $R$. In the spreading ring, $\tau = 1$ is reached at $t = t_\nu / 12$ for $R = R_0$.

Molecular viscosity gives a viscous time far longer than the age of the Universe <Cite id="pringle1981" />. For
hydrogen gas at $T = 300$ K and density $\rho = 10^{-6}$ kg m⁻³, roughly the midplane of a disc around a young star at
$R = 1$ au $= 1.5 \times 10^{11}$ m:

- the number density is $n = \rho / (2.34 \, m_\text{H}) = 2.6 \times 10^{20}$ m⁻³, with $m_\text{H}$ the hydrogen
  atom's mass and 2.34 the mean molecular weight;
- with a collision cross-section $\sigma = 2 \times 10^{-19}$ m², the mean free path is
  $\lambda = 1/(n \sigma) \approx 0.02$ m;
- the mean thermal speed is $\bar v \approx 1.6$ km s⁻¹, so $\nu_\text{mol} \approx \lambda \bar v / 3 \approx 10$
  m² s⁻¹;
- the viscous time is $R^2 / \nu_\text{mol} \approx 2 \times 10^{21}$ s $\approx 7 \times 10^{13}$ yr, about 5000
  times the age of the Universe.

The Shakura–Sunyaev prescription writes the viscosity as $\nu = \alpha c_s H$, with $c_s$ the sound speed, $H$ the
disc's vertical scale height and $\alpha$ a dimensionless efficiency below 1 <Cite id="shakura1973" />. At 1 au,
$c_s \approx 1$ km s⁻¹ and $H = c_s / \Omega \approx 5 \times 10^9$ m. With $\alpha = 10^{-3}$, $\nu \approx 5 \times
10^9$ m² s⁻¹ and the viscous time drops to about $1.3 \times 10^5$ yr. Explaining an effective viscosity some $10^8$ to
$10^9$ times the molecular one requires turbulence. The
<NoteLink to="magnetorotational-instability">magnetorotational instability</NoteLink> is the accepted source of that
turbulence in ionised discs.

## Assumptions

- The disc is thin ($H \ll R$), so it is described by $\Sigma(R, t)$ alone.
- Every annulus stays on a circular Kepler orbit: the radial velocity, of order $\nu / R$, is far below the orbital
  speed, and the central mass dominates the gravity.
- The viscosity is a constant. Pringle (1981) notes that power-law $\nu(R)$ also gives closed forms, and that $\nu$
  depending on $\Sigma$ makes the equation nonlinear <Cite id="pringle1981" />.
- The inner boundary at $R = 0$ has zero torque: gas falls onto the central mass freely, as onto a black hole or a
  slowly rotating star.

## Pitfalls

- **The disc's mass is not conserved; the total is.** The zero-torque solution moves mass onto the centre, so the disc
  mass falls as $P(\tfrac14, 1/\tau)$. A check of mass conservation has to add the accreted mass.
- **Mass does not move inward first.** At early times slightly more mass lies outside $R_0$ than inside, and the mass
  beyond $R_0$ peaks near 61% at $\tau \approx 3$. The inward flow of mass is a late-time and integrated statement.
- **The order of the Bessel function is a boundary condition.** $I_{1/4}$ belongs to zero torque at the centre;
  $I_{-1/4}$ belongs to zero mass flow there. The two solutions agree near the ring at early times and differ near the
  centre.
- **Viscosity here is a stand-in.** The diffusion equation needs only a stress proportional to the shear. In real discs
  the stress comes from magnetised turbulence, and $\nu$ is an average over it.
