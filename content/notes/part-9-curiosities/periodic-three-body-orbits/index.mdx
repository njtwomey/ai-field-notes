<Definition>
Three bodies of mass 1 attract one another by Newton's law with $G = 1$. Find starting positions and velocities for
which the motion repeats exactly after some period $T$, and decide whether a small change to the start keeps the motion
near the periodic orbit. Three answers are worked out here: Euler's collinear solution (bodies at $x = -1, 0, 1$),
Lagrange's equilateral solution (bodies at the corners of a triangle with side 1), and the figure-eight with the
initial conditions of Chenciner and Montgomery <Cite id="chenciner2000" />.
</Definition>

## Setup

Body $i \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ has position $\qvec_i \in \reals^2$ and velocity $\vvec_i = \dot{\qvec}_i$, in units where the
gravitational constant and every mass equal 1. Lengths and times are then plain numbers. The equations of motion are

$$
\ddot{\qvec}_i = -\sum_{j \ne i} \frac{\qvec_i - \qvec_j}{\norm{\qvec_i - \qvec_j}^3}.
$$

A solution is **periodic** with period $T > 0$ if $\qvec_i(t + T) = \qvec_i(t)$ and $\vvec_i(t + T) = \vvec_i(t)$ for
every body and every time $t$. The general three-body problem has no formula for its solutions
(<NoteLink to="three-body-problem" />), so periodic orbits are found from symmetry or by search. Each one is a fixed
starting point to which <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical integration</NoteLink> can return after a period.

A periodic orbit is **linearly stable** when every small perturbation stays small, at most growing in proportion to
time, under the equations linearised about the orbit. It is unstable when some perturbation grows exponentially. Only a
stable periodic orbit can be seen in nature or in a simulation for long, because rounding errors perturb every computed
orbit.

The three orbits were found in very different ways. Euler described the collinear solutions in 1767 and Lagrange the
equilateral ones in 1772 <Cite id="musielak2014" />. Both are rigid rotations, solvable by hand. The figure-eight is not
a rigid motion. Moore found it in 1993 by a numerical search for orbits of a given topology <Cite id="moore1993" />, and
Chenciner and Montgomery proved its existence in 2000, unaware of Moore's work until their paper was in proof
<Cite id="chenciner2000" />.

## Solution

### Rigid rotations: Euler and Lagrange

Suppose the three bodies keep their shape and rotate about their centre of mass, at the origin, with constant angular
velocity $\omega$. Each body then moves on a circle, and its acceleration points to the origin with size $\omega^2$
times its distance from it:

$$
\ddot{\qvec}_i = -\omega^2 \qvec_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, 3.
$$

A configuration where the gravitational accelerations take this form is a **central configuration**. The rotation
solves the equations of motion exactly when the bodies start in a central configuration, each with speed
$\omega \norm{\qvec_i}$ at right angles to $\qvec_i$, all turning the same way.

**Euler's line.** Put the bodies at $\qvec_1 = (-1, 0)$, $\qvec_2 = (0, 0)$ and $\qvec_3 = (1, 0)$. Body 2 is pulled
equally both ways, so its acceleration is zero, consistent with $-\omega^2 \qvec_2 = \zeros$. Body 3 is pulled toward
body 2 at distance 1 and toward body 1 at distance 2:

$$
\norm{\ddot{\qvec}_3} = \frac{1}{1^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{5}{4} = \omega^2 \cdot 1
\quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.118.
$$

Body 1 is the mirror image of body 3. The outer bodies move at speed $\sqrt{5}/2$, body 3 upward and body 1 downward,
and the period is $T = 2\pi / \omega = 4\pi/\sqrt{5} \approx 5.620$. The energy is
$E = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{5}{4} - \paren{1 + 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}} = -1.25$ and the angular momentum is
$L = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{5}}{2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$.

**Lagrange's triangle.** Put the bodies at the corners of an equilateral triangle with side 1, centred on the origin.
Each body sits at distance $1/\sqrt{3}$ from the centre. The other two pull it with force 1 each, along directions
$30°$ either side of the line to the centre, so the sideways parts cancel and the pull toward the centre is
$2 \cos 30° = \sqrt{3}$:

$$
\sqrt{3} = \omega^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732.
$$

Each body moves at speed $\omega / \sqrt{3} = 1$, and the period is $T = 2\pi/\sqrt{3} \approx 3.628$. The energy is
$E = 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} - 3 = -1.5$ and the angular momentum is $L = 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot 1 = \sqrt{3}$.
For unequal masses the equilateral triangle is still a central configuration, with $\omega^2 = (m_1 + m_2 + m_3)/a^3$
for side $a$.

### The figure-eight

The figure-eight is a **choreography**: all three bodies chase one another around one closed curve shaped like an
eight, each a third of a period behind the next. Its total angular momentum is zero. Chenciner and Montgomery print the
initial conditions computed by Simó <Cite id="chenciner2000" />:

$$
\qvec_1 = -\qvec_2 = (0.97000436,\ -0.24308753), \quad \qvec_3 = (0, 0),
$$

$$
\vvec_3 = -2\vvec_1 = -2\vvec_2 = (-0.93240737,\ -0.86473146), \qquad T = 6.32591398.
$$

The start can be checked by hand.

1. **Shape.** Body 3 sits at the midpoint of bodies 1 and 2, so the start is collinear, an Euler configuration. Body 1
   is at distance $\sqrt{0.97000436^2 + 0.24308753^2} = \sqrt{0.940908 + 0.059092} = 1.000000$ from the origin, so the
   mutual distances are 1, 1 and 2.
2. **Momentum.** $\vvec_1 + \vvec_2 + \vvec_3 = 2\vvec_1 - 2\vvec_1 = \zeros$, so the centre of mass stays at the
   origin.
3. **Angular momentum.** Body 3 is at the origin and contributes nothing. Bodies 1 and 2 have opposite positions and
   equal velocities, so their contributions $\qvec_1 \times \vvec_1$ and $(-\qvec_1) \times \vvec_1$ cancel: $L = 0$.
4. **Energy.** $\norm{\vvec_3}^2 = 0.869383 + 0.747760 = 1.617143$ and $\norm{\vvec_1}^2 = 1.617143 / 4 = 0.404286$.
   The kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}(2 \cdot 0.404286 + 1.617143) = 1.212857$ and the potential energy is
   $-(1 + 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}) = -2.5$, so $E = -1.287143$.

The positions match Euler's line, but the velocities do not: they are oblique and give zero angular momentum, where
Euler's rotation has $L = \sqrt{5}$. Chenciner and Montgomery found the orbit by minimising the action, the time
integral of kinetic minus potential energy, over loops of the right symmetry, and proved that the minimiser has no
collisions <Cite id="chenciner2000" />.

### Stability

The three orbits differ in stability.

- **Lagrange's triangle.** A rigid equilateral rotation is linearly stable only if
  $27(m_1 m_2 + m_2 m_3 + m_3 m_1) < (m_1 + m_2 + m_3)^2$ <Cite id="routh1874" />. For equal masses the left side is
  $27 \cdot 3 = 81$ and the right side is $9$, so the triangle is unstable. The criterion holds only when one mass
  carries nearly all the weight. With $m_3 \to 0$ and $m_2 / (m_1 + m_2) = \mu$, it becomes $27\mu(1 - \mu) < 1$,
  the condition for stable Trojan points (<NoteLink to="lagrange-points" />).
- **Euler's line** is unstable for every choice of masses <Cite id="musielak2014" />.
- **The figure-eight.** Simó's computations, reported by Chenciner and Montgomery, show it to be linearly stable
  <Cite id="chenciner2000" />. Few stable periodic orbits of three equal masses are known.

## Simulation

The figure kicks body 1's $x$-velocity by $\delta$, returns to the centre-of-mass frame, and integrates by leapfrog
(<NoteLink to="symplectic-integrators-and-energy-drift" />). It measures the kicked orbit's **shape distance**: the
distance from the triangle's side lengths $(r_{12}, r_{13}, r_{23})$ to the nearest side lengths the unperturbed orbit
passes through in one period. Side lengths ignore where the triangle is and how it is turned, so a slow rotation of the
whole orbit does not count. A kick gives the system a little angular momentum, which makes a kicked figure-eight turn
slowly as a whole.

<KickedOrbits />

At the defaults ($\delta = 10^{-3}$, 1000 steps per period), the figure-eight's shape distance stays near $10^{-2}$:
over 200 periods it never exceeds $1.1 \times 10^{-2}$, and a kick of $10^{-2}$ gives at most $3.3 \times 10^{-2}$.
The bounded response is linear stability at work. Lagrange's triangle passes a shape distance of 0.1 after about 1.3
periods for $\delta = 10^{-3}$ and after about 2.9 periods for $\delta = 10^{-6}$. A thousand-fold smaller kick buys
about 1.6 periods, so the perturbation grows by a factor of roughly $10^{3/1.6} \approx 80$ per period. Euler's line is
worse: a shape distance of 0.1 comes after 0.6 periods for $\delta = 10^{-3}$ and 1.2 periods for $\delta = 10^{-6}$,
a growth of order $10^{5}$ per period. With $\delta = 0$, Euler's line survives, because the leapfrog steps keep its
mirror symmetry exactly. Lagrange's triangle passes a shape distance of 0.1 after about 7 periods from rounding errors
alone.

## What it illustrates

- **Special solutions in a chaotic problem.** The three-body problem has no general formula, yet symmetry and search
  produce exact periodic solutions (<NoteLink to="three-body-problem" />). A periodic orbit is a fixed point of the
  map that advances the state by one period.
- **Stability decides what is seen.** Linearisation about an orbit sorts perturbations into growing and bounded ones,
  as for equilibria (<NoteLink to="stability-and-equilibria" />). Unstable periodic orbits exist mathematically but last
  only a few periods in a simulation or in the sky. A stable one persists under kicks and rounding errors.
- **The restricted limit.** Routh's criterion for Lagrange's triangle becomes the stability condition of the L4 and L5
  points when one body is light, which is why Jupiter's Trojan asteroids can sit at its corners for billions of years
  (<NoteLink to="lagrange-points" />).
- **Geometric integration.** Leapfrog keeps the energy error bounded over many periods on a stable orbit, so the
  figure-eight's bounded shape distance reflects the dynamics, not the integrator
  (<NoteLink to="symplectic-integrators-and-energy-drift" />).

## Variations

- **Unequal masses.** The equilateral triangle stays a central configuration for any masses, and its rotation is stable
  when Routh's inequality holds, for example masses 1, 0.001 and $10^{-6}$. Euler's collinear configurations exist for
  any masses, with spacings set by a quintic equation, and are always unstable.
- **Elliptic homographic motions.** Starting a central configuration with too little speed for rigid rotation gives
  a triangle (or line) that keeps its shape while it shrinks and grows, each body on a Kepler ellipse.
- **More choreographies and families.** Šuvakov and Dmitrašinović searched numerically for planar periodic orbits of
  three equal masses with zero angular momentum and reported 13 distinct orbits, sorted into families by their topology
  <Cite id="suvakov2013" />.
- **A larger kick.** The figure-eight's stability is local. Kicks of a few hundredths take the orbit out of its stable
  neighbourhood: a body leaves after about 30 periods at $\delta = 0.05$ and after about 20 periods at
  $\delta = 0.1$.
