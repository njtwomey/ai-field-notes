<Definition>
The **three-body problem** asks for the motion of three point masses $m_1, m_2, m_3$ that attract one another by
Newton's inverse-square law, given their positions and velocities at one time. The equations of motion conserve the
total energy, linear momentum and angular momentum. No further integrals exist that would reduce them to formulas, as
the conservation laws do for two bodies. Most solutions are chaotic, and a bound triple of comparable masses usually
ends with one body ejected and the other two locked in a binary.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Two bodies under gravity move on conic sections, fixed once and for all by their energy and angular momentum
(<NoteLink to="kepler-orbits" />). A third body perturbs each pair's orbit all the time. When the third body is light
or far away, the perturbation is small and the motion stays close to Keplerian for a long time; the planets of the
Solar System are this case. When three comparable masses come close, they trade energy in close encounters. A close
pass between two bodies can hand a large amount of kinetic energy to the third. The motion then looks like a sequence of
encounters with no pattern, and it ends when one body leaves for good.

Burrau posed a clean example in 1913, now called the Pythagorean problem. Masses 3, 4 and 5 start at rest at the corners
of a right triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5, each mass opposite the side of its own length. Burrau's hand computation
stopped at $t = 3.35$. Szebehely and Peters followed the motion to the end in 1967 <Cite id="szebehely1967" />. The
bodies fall inward and pass through a long series of close encounters, the closest at a distance of about
$4 \times 10^{-4}$. Near $t = 31.7$ the three bodies almost return to their starting triangle, which suggests a periodic
orbit, but they do not quite arrive and the motion that follows is entirely different. Around $t = 60$ the lightest
body, mass 3, shoots through the pair formed by masses 4 and 5 and leaves on a hyperbolic path, while the pair recedes
the opposite way as a tight binary.

## Equations of motion and what they conserve

Write $\qvec_i \in \reals^3$ for the position of body $i$, $\vvec_i = \dot{\qvec}_i$ for its velocity and
$\pvec_i = m_i \vvec_i$ for its momentum. Units are chosen so that the gravitational constant is $G = 1$; masses,
lengths and times are then plain numbers. The force on body $i$ is the sum of the pull of the other two:

$$
m_i \ddot{\qvec}_i = \Fvec_i = -\sum_{j \ne i} \frac{m_i m_j \paren{\qvec_i - \qvec_j}}{\norm{\qvec_i - \qvec_j}^3},
\qquad i = 1, 2, 3.
$$

The same motion follows from the Hamiltonian, the total energy as a function of positions and momenta:

$$
H(\qvec, \pvec) = \underbrace{\sum_{i} \frac{\norm{\pvec_i}^2}{2 m_i}}_{T(\pvec)}
\underbrace{- \sum_{i < j} \frac{m_i m_j}{\norm{\qvec_i - \qvec_j}}}_{V(\qvec)},
\qquad \dot{\qvec}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial \pvec_i}, \quad \dot{\pvec}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial \qvec_i}.
$$

$T$ is the kinetic energy and $V$ the potential energy. $H$ splits into a part that depends only on momenta and a part
that depends only on positions, which is what a leapfrog integrator needs
(<NoteLink to="symplectic-integrators-and-energy-drift" />).

Three conservation laws follow from the form of the force.

1. **Linear momentum.** The force of $j$ on $i$,
   $\Fvec_{ij} = -m_i m_j (\qvec_i - \qvec_j) / \norm{\qvec_i - \qvec_j}^3$, is minus the force of $i$ on $j$. The
   forces cancel in pairs, so $\frac{d}{dt} \sum_i \pvec_i = \sum_i \Fvec_i = \zeros$. The centre of mass therefore
   moves in a straight line at constant speed.
2. **Angular momentum.** $\Lvec = \sum_i \qvec_i \times \pvec_i$ has derivative
   $\sum_i \vvec_i \times m_i \vvec_i + \sum_i \qvec_i \times \Fvec_i$. The first sum vanishes because each term crosses
   a vector with itself. In the second, the pair $(i, j)$ contributes $(\qvec_i - \qvec_j) \times \Fvec_{ij}$, which
   vanishes because $\Fvec_{ij}$ points along $\qvec_i - \qvec_j$. So $\dot{\Lvec} = \zeros$.
3. **Energy.** The chain rule gives
   $\dot{H} = \sum_i \paren{\partial H / \partial \pvec_i} \cdot \dot{\pvec}_i + \paren{\partial H / \partial \qvec_i} \cdot \dot{\qvec}_i$.
   Hamilton's equations replace $\partial H / \partial \pvec_i$ by $\dot{\qvec}_i$ and $\partial H / \partial \qvec_i$
   by $-\dot{\pvec}_i$, so the two terms cancel and $\dot{H} = 0$.

These give ten integrals in all: the energy (one), the momentum (three), the initial position of the centre of mass
(three) and the angular momentum (three). The state of three bodies in space has 18 numbers, nine positions and nine
velocities, so the ten integrals confine the motion to 8 dimensions. Eliminating time and the line of nodes brings
the count to 6 <Cite id="musielak2014" />. For two bodies the same integrals, together with the Laplace–Runge–Lenz
vector, pin each orbit to a single conic. For three bodies nothing further is available.

## Why there is no general solution

In 1889 Poincaré won King Oscar II's prize with a memoir on the three-body problem, published in revised form the next
year <Cite id="poincare1890" />. He showed that the problem has no integrals beyond the classical ones that depend
analytically on the positions and velocities, extending Bruns's 1887 result for algebraic integrals
<Cite id="musielak2014" />. In correcting an error in the prize version, Poincaré found the reason. The curves of states
that approach an unstable periodic orbit and the curves of states that leave it can cross, and once they cross once they
cross infinitely often, in a tangle that folds over itself at every scale. An orbit near the tangle wanders in a way
that no smooth function of the state can track, and this tangle was the first description of chaos. A general solution
in the sense of the two-body problem, a formula that maps any starting state to the state at time $t$, would need the
missing integrals, so none exists. Sundman later found a convergent power series for every solution with nonzero angular
momentum <Cite id="sundman1913" />. The series converges so slowly that it has no practical use, and the three-body
problem is studied with <NoteLink to="numerical-integration">numerical integration</NoteLink> and with special solutions
instead (<NoteLink to="periodic-three-body-orbits" />, <NoteLink to="lagrange-points" />).

## Sensitivity to the start

Chaos makes nearby solutions separate exponentially on average (<NoteLink to="bifurcations-and-chaos" />). Let
$\delta(t) = \norm{\qvec^A(t) - \qvec^B(t)}$ be the distance between two solutions, measured over all positions. For a
chaotic orbit, $\delta(t) \approx \delta(0) e^{\lambda t}$ for small $\delta$, with $\lambda > 0$ the largest Lyapunov
exponent. In the three-body problem the growth is not smooth. It comes in jumps, one at each close encounter, because
a small change in the distance of closest approach changes the deflection a lot. Two copies of the Pythagorean problem
started $10^{-6}$ apart (mass 3 moved along $x$) separate to $10^{-3}$ by about $t = 40$ and to the size of the system,
about 1, near $t = 57$, in the figure below. That is an average rate of $\ln(10^6) / 57 \approx 0.24$ per unit time,
an e-folding time of about 4.

The same sensitivity applies to the integrator's own errors. Each step of a numerical method commits a small error,
and chaos magnifies it at the same rate as a perturbation of the start. A computed orbit of a chaotic system is
therefore accurate only up to a horizon that grows like the logarithm of the accuracy. Beyond that horizon, the
computed orbit is a correct-looking solution that started somewhere slightly different. The statistical outcome (which
body escapes, roughly when, roughly how fast) is far more robust than the trajectory itself.

## Why one body escapes

The total energy $E$ of a bound triple is negative and fixed. When two bodies $a$ and $b$ form a close pair with
semi-major axis $a_{ab}$, the pair's internal energy is that of a Kepler orbit, $E_{ab} = -m_a m_b / (2 a_{ab})$. Once
the third body is far from the pair, the total energy splits into the pair's energy and the energy $E_\text{out}$ of the
third body's motion relative to the pair's centre of mass:

$$
E = E_{ab} + E_\text{out}, \qquad E_\text{out} = E - E_{ab} = E + \frac{m_a m_b}{2 a_{ab}}.
$$

A tight pair has a large negative $E_{ab}$. If $m_a m_b / (2 a_{ab}) > -E$, then $E_\text{out} > 0$: the outer motion
is hyperbolic, and the third body escapes to infinity. Close encounters are where the pair tightens and the third body
is kicked, so a long enough series of encounters eventually produces a pair tight enough to release the third body.
A given outer energy gives the lightest body the highest speed, and in the Pythagorean problem the lightest body is
the one ejected. For $E > 0$ the system
must split, because a bound configuration needs $E < 0$ <Cite id="musielak2014" />.

## Worked example: the Pythagorean start

The figure integrates this start, so its numbers can be checked by hand.

**Positions.** Place mass 3 at $(1, 3)$, mass 4 at $(-2, -1)$ and mass 5 at $(1, -1)$, all at rest, in units with
$G = 1$. Mass 3 faces the side from mass 4 to mass 5, of length $\norm{(1, -1) - (-2, -1)} = 3$. Mass 4 faces the side
from mass 3 to mass 5, of length $\norm{(1, -1) - (1, 3)} = 4$. Mass 5 faces the side from mass 3 to mass 4, of length
$\norm{(-2, -1) - (1, 3)} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

**Centre of mass.**
$\frac{1}{12}\paren{3 \cdot (1, 3) + 4 \cdot (-2, -1) + 5 \cdot (1, -1)} = \frac{1}{12}(3 - 8 + 5,\ 9 - 4 - 5) = (0, 0)$.
The bodies start at rest, so the total momentum and the angular momentum are both zero, and they stay zero.

**Energy.** All the energy is potential at the start:

$$
E = V = -\paren{\frac{3 \cdot 4}{5} + \frac{3 \cdot 5}{4} + \frac{4 \cdot 5}{3}} = -\paren{2.4 + 3.75 + 6.667} = -\frac{769}{60} \approx -12.817.
$$

This is the value Szebehely and Peters report <Cite id="szebehely1967" />, and the figure's energy error is measured
against it.

**Initial accelerations.** Divide each force by the body's mass. For mass 3, the pull of mass 4 is
$4 \cdot (-3, -4) / 5^3 = (-0.096, -0.128)$ and the pull of mass 5 is $5 \cdot (0, -4) / 4^3 = (0, -0.3125)$, so
$\ddot{\qvec}_3 = (-0.096, -0.441)$. The same sums give $\ddot{\qvec}_4 = (0.628, 0.096)$ and
$\ddot{\qvec}_5 = (-0.444, 0.188)$. The mass-weighted sum is
$3(-0.096, -0.441) + 4(0.628, 0.096) + 5(-0.444, 0.188) = (0.000, 0.000)$ to rounding, as momentum conservation
requires. All three accelerations point roughly toward the centre of mass, so the triangle starts to collapse.

**The end state.** In the figure's default run, masses 4 and 5 end as a binary with semi-major axis close to 0.47. Its
energy is $-4 \cdot 5 / (2 \cdot 0.47) \approx -21.3$. Mass 3 therefore leaves with
$E_\text{out} \approx -12.8 + 21.3 = +8.5$, a positive outer energy, so its escape is permanent.

## Pitfalls

- **Fixed steps fail at close encounters.** At a separation of $4 \times 10^{-4}$ the relative speed of masses 4 and 5
  reaches about 171 <Cite id="szebehely1967" />, so the pass takes a few millionths of a time unit. A fixed step small
  enough for it would take hundreds of millions of steps over the whole run. Use an adaptive method, or regularise the
  equations near each close pair, as Szebehely and Peters did, with a <NoteLink to="change-of-variables">change of
  variables</NoteLink> that removes the $1/r^2$ singularity.
- **Softening changes the problem.** Replacing $1/r$ by $1/\sqrt{r^2 + \varepsilon^2}$, with softening length
  $\varepsilon$, removes the singularity and makes fixed steps workable. It also caps the energy a close pass can
  release, so close encounters and ejections change. Softening suits large N-body simulations that model a smooth mass
  distribution, not a few-body problem.
- **Energy conservation is necessary, not sufficient.** A run can conserve energy to $10^{-7}$ and still end in a
  different state from a run at a tighter tolerance, because chaos amplifies errors that leave the energy unchanged.
  Compare runs at two tolerances before trusting a trajectory past the first few encounters.
- **No general solution is not no solution.** Every start that avoids collisions has a unique solution, and numerical
  integration computes it to any accuracy up to the chaotic horizon. What is missing is a formula.
- **The restricted problem is a different problem.** When one mass is negligible and the other two move on circles,
  the problem has a conserved quantity of its own, the Jacobi constant (<NoteLink to="lagrange-points" />). The general
  problem has no such constant.
