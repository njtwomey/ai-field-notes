<Definition>
The **Kelvin–Helmholtz instability** is the growth of waves on the interface between two layers of fluid that move
at different velocities. For a sharp interface between fluids of equal density, with no gravity or surface tension,
the interface is a **vortex sheet**, and a sinusoidal displacement of wavenumber $k$ grows exponentially at the rate
$\sigma = k\,\Delta U/2$, where $\Delta U$ is the jump in velocity across the sheet <Cite id="saffman1992" />. The
waves steepen and roll up into spirals of concentrated vorticity. Because $\sigma$ grows without bound with $k$, the
sharp-sheet problem needs a regularisation, such as Krasny's vortex blobs, before it can be computed
<Cite id="krasny1986" />.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Wind blowing over a lake raises waves; a fast jet of smoke entering still air breaks into curls; rows of curling
"billow clouds" form where a fast layer of air slides over a slow one. All three are the same instability of a shear
layer, a thin region where the velocity changes from one value to another.

The mechanism is easiest to see in the vortex-sheet picture. A shear layer carries vorticity: the velocity jumps across
it, so the circulation around a short stretch of the layer is not zero. Model the layer as a row of point vortices, each
carried by the flow of the others (<NoteLink to="point-vortex-dynamics">point vortex dynamics</NoteLink>). On a flat row
every vortex is pushed equally from both sides and nothing moves. On a wavy row the balance breaks: the vortices drift
along the sheet towards every other node, where the sheet crosses its mean line with one particular slope. Vorticity
piles up at those nodes, the extra vorticity turns the sheet there more steeply, and the turning moves still more
vortices towards the nodes. This positive feedback is the instability, and the piles of vorticity become the cores of
the spirals.

Short waves grow fastest. A wave of half the length has its nodes half as far apart, and the vortices reach them in
half the time. In a real shear layer the waves shorter than the layer's thickness see a smooth velocity profile rather
than a jump and stop growing. The sharp vortex sheet has no thickness, so nothing stops its shortest waves.

## The linear growth rate

Let the fluid above the interface move at velocity $U_1$ in the $x$ direction and the fluid below at $U_2$, both in
metres per second, with $\Delta U = U_1 - U_2$. Both fluids have the same constant density, and gravity and surface
tension are ignored. Displace the interface to $y = \eta(x, t) = \hat\eta\, e^{ikx + st}$ with a small amplitude
$\hat\eta$, a wavenumber $k > 0$ (in inverse metres) and a complex rate $s$ (in inverse seconds).

Away from the interface the flow stays irrotational, so each perturbation velocity is the gradient of a potential that
satisfies Laplace's equation and decays away from the interface:
$\phi_1 = \hat\phi_1\, e^{-ky}\, e^{ikx + st}$ above and $\phi_2 = \hat\phi_2\, e^{ky}\, e^{ikx + st}$ below. Two
conditions at $y = 0$ couple them.

1. **The interface moves with each fluid.** A fluid particle on the interface stays on it, so
   $\partial_t\eta + U_j\, \partial_x\eta = \partial_y\phi_j$ on each side. This gives
   $(s + ikU_1)\hat\eta = -k\hat\phi_1$ and $(s + ikU_2)\hat\eta = k\hat\phi_2$.
2. **The pressure is continuous.** The linearised Bernoulli equation gives the pressure perturbation on each side as
   $-\rho(\partial_t\phi_j + U_j\,\partial_x\phi_j)$, and equal pressures require
   $(s + ikU_1)\hat\phi_1 = (s + ikU_2)\hat\phi_2$.

Substituting $\hat\phi_1$ and $\hat\phi_2$ from the first condition into the second gives

$$
-\frac{(s + ikU_1)^2}{k}\,\hat\eta = \frac{(s + ikU_2)^2}{k}\,\hat\eta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (s + ikU_1)^2 + (s + ikU_2)^2 = 0.
$$

Write $s = -ik\bar{U} + \sigma$ with the mean velocity $\bar{U} = (U_1 + U_2)/2$. Then
$s + ikU_1 = \sigma + ik\Delta U/2$ and $s + ikU_2 = \sigma - ik\Delta U/2$, and the condition becomes
$2\sigma^2 - 2(k\Delta U/2)^2 = 0$. Hence

$$
\sigma = \pm\frac{k\,\Delta U}{2}.
$$

The wave drifts with the mean velocity $\bar{U}$ and its amplitude grows as $e^{k\abs{\Delta U} t/2}$. Any velocity
jump makes the interface unstable, and the rate is proportional to the wavenumber.

## Why the sharp sheet needs regularising

A rate proportional to $k$ makes the initial-value problem ill-posed: a disturbance of size $10^{-16}$ at wavenumber
$k$ reaches size 1 after time $2\ln(10^{16})/(k\,\Delta U)$, which tends to zero as $k$ grows. Rounding errors in a
computation contain every wavenumber the grid can represent, so the finer the discretisation, the sooner the computed
sheet turns ragged. Even smooth initial data does not survive: Moore showed by asymptotic analysis that the curvature of
a sinusoidally perturbed sheet becomes infinite at a finite time, before any roll-up <Cite id="moore1979" />.

The vortex-blob method replaces each point vortex by a blob with a core of size set by a smoothing parameter $\delta$.
Far from a blob the flow is that of a point vortex; near it the velocity stays bounded. Waves much longer than $\delta$
grow almost at the sharp-sheet rate, and waves much shorter than $\delta$ barely grow, which is what a shear layer of
thickness about $\delta$ does <Cite id="krasny1986" />.

## Krasny's δ-equations

Take a sheet that is periodic in $x$ with period 1 and carries circulation 1 per period, so that the velocity jumps by
$\Delta U = 1$ across it: far above the sheet the fluid moves at $-\tfrac12$, far below at $+\tfrac12$. Label the
points of the sheet by the Lagrangian parameter $\Gamma \in [0, 1)$, the circulation between the left end of the
period and the point. Discretise by $N$ blobs at $\Gamma_j = j/N$, each of circulation $1/N$.

A blob and all its periodic copies $x + n$, $n \in \integers$, form a row of vortices. Summing the point-vortex
velocity $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\frac{\Gamma}{z - z_0}$ over the row with $\sum_n \frac{1}{z - n} = \pi\cot(\pi z)$ gives
$u - iv = \frac{\Gamma}{2i}\cot\pi(z - z_0)$, whose real and imaginary parts contain
$\cosh 2\pi\Delta y - \cos 2\pi\Delta x$ in the denominator. Krasny adds $\delta^2$ to that denominator
<Cite id="krasny1986" />:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_j}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{\sinh 2\pi(y_j - y_k)}{\cosh 2\pi(y_j - y_k) - \cos 2\pi(x_j - x_k) + \delta^2},
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}y_j}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{\sin 2\pi(x_j - x_k)}{\cosh 2\pi(y_j - y_k) - \cos 2\pi(x_j - x_k) + \delta^2}.
$$

The term $k = j$ is zero, because its numerators vanish. Setting $\delta = 0$ gives Rosenhead's point-vortex sheet. As
$N \to \infty$ at $\delta = 0$ the sum becomes the **Birkhoff–Rott equation** for the continuous sheet, $\partial_t \bar{z}(\Gamma, t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\int_0^1 \pi\cot\pi\paren{z(\Gamma, t) - z(\Gamma', t)}\,
\mathrm{d}\Gamma'$, where PV is the principal value of the integral. For two nearby blobs the denominator is about
$2\pi^2 r^2 + \delta^2$ at separation $r$, so smoothing takes over below
$r \approx \delta/(\pi\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.23\,\delta$. The initial sheet is $x = \Gamma + \varepsilon\sin 2\pi\Gamma$,
$y = -\varepsilon\sin 2\pi\Gamma$ with $\varepsilon = 0.01$ <Cite id="krasny1986" />. That displacement is the growing
mode of the linearised flat sheet, so the amplitude starts growing at once at the rate $\sigma = k\Delta U/2 = \pi$,
with $k = 2\pi$.

In Krasny's computations the spiral gains more turns as $\delta$ decreases, while its overall size stays the same
<Cite id="krasny1986" />.

<Derivation title="The growth rate of the discretised δ-sheet">
Linearise about the flat sheet $x_j = \Gamma_j$, $y_j = 0$ with perturbations $\xi_j$ in $x$ and $\eta_j$ in $y$, both
proportional to $e^{2\pi i m \Gamma_j}$ for a mode number $m$ (wavelength $1/m$). Write $\theta_k = 2\pi k/N$ and
$D_k = 1 - \cos\theta_k + \delta^2$. To first order, $\sinh 2\pi\Delta y \approx 2\pi\Delta y$, and the change of the
denominator multiplies an already small numerator in $\mathrm{d}x_j/\mathrm{d}t$. In $\mathrm{d}y_j/\mathrm{d}t$ both
$\sin 2\pi\Delta x$ and the denominator change with $\Delta\xi$. Summing over the other blobs gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{\pi}{N}A\,\eta, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\eta}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\pi}{N}B\,\xi,
$$

$$
A = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{1 - \cos m\theta_k}{D_k}, \qquad
B = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{(1 - \cos m\theta_k)(\cos\theta_k\, D_k - \sin^2\theta_k)}{D_k^2}.
$$

Eliminating $\xi$ gives $\ddot\eta = -(\pi/N)^2 AB\,\eta$, so the growth rate is $\sigma = (\pi/N)\sqrt{-AB}$ when
$AB < 0$. At $\delta = 0$ the second numerator simplifies to $-(1 - \cos\theta_k)$, so $B = -A$ and
$\sigma = (\pi/N)A$; for $m = 1$ the sum is $A = N - 1$ and $\sigma = \pi(N - 1)/N$, which tends to the continuous
sheet's $\pi$. The figure's readouts evaluate this formula.
</Derivation>

## Worked example

**Physical units.** A layer of air moving at $10$ m/s over still air has $\Delta U = 10$ m/s. A wave of wavelength
$100$ m has $k = 2\pi/100 = 0.0628$ m⁻¹ and grows at $\sigma = 0.0628 \times 10/2 = 0.314$ s⁻¹, so its amplitude
multiplies by $e$ every $1/0.314 = 3.2$ s. A wave of wavelength $1$ m grows a hundred times faster, multiplying by $e$
every $0.032$ s.

**Krasny's units.** Period 1, $\Delta U = 1$ and $\varepsilon = 0.01$, the figure's defaults.

1. The fundamental mode has $k = 2\pi$, so the sharp sheet grows at $\sigma = \pi \approx 3.14$. The amplitude reaches
   $0.1$, ten times its start, at $t = \ln 10/\pi = 0.73$.
2. With $N = 150$ blobs, neighbours sit $1/150$ apart, and their denominator without smoothing is
   $1 - \cos(2\pi/150) = 0.00088$. With $\delta = 0.2$ it becomes $0.00088 + 0.04 = 0.04088$, about 47 times larger,
   so the velocity one neighbour induces on the next drops by the same factor.
3. The discrete formula of the derivation gives, for $N = 150$ and $\delta = 0.2$, $\sigma = 2.53$ for $m = 1$ against
   $3.12$ for $\delta = 0$. The amplitude now reaches $0.1$ at $t = \ln 10/2.53 = 0.91$.
4. The shortest wave the blobs can carry, $m = 75$, grows at $\sigma = 118$ with $\delta = 0$ and at $0.002$ with
   $\delta = 0.2$. Among all modes the fastest with $\delta = 0.2$ is near $m = 5$, at $\sigma \approx 5.6$.
5. A rounding error of $10^{-16}$ in mode 75 of the point-vortex sheet reaches $0.01$, the size of the deliberate
   perturbation, after $\ln(10^{14})/118 = 0.27$ time units. That is when the figure's $\delta = 0$ sheet turns ragged.

## Pitfalls

- **The rate is a linear result.** $\sigma = k\Delta U/2$ holds while the amplitude is small compared with the
  wavelength. The amplitude in the figure leaves the exponential near $0.1$, a tenth of the wavelength.
- **Equal densities and no gravity.** Gravity stabilises a heavy fluid below a light one, and then waves longer than a
  threshold set by the density difference and $\Delta U$ are stable. Surface tension stabilises short waves. Neither is
  in the vortex-sheet model.
- **δ is not viscosity.** The blob method smooths the induced velocity but conserves circulation exactly and has no
  diffusion. It mimics a shear layer of finite thickness, not a viscous one.
- **Fixed blobs run out.** The spiral's turns stretch the sheet exponentially, so the blobs in the core spread apart
  and the drawn sheet becomes a polygon there. More blobs or a larger $\delta$ keep the core resolved.
