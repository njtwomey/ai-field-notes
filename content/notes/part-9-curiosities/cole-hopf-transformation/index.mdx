<Definition>
The **Cole–Hopf transformation** is the substitution

$$
u = -2\nu\,\frac{\varphi_x}{\varphi},
$$

which maps every positive solution $\varphi(x, t)$ of the heat equation $\varphi_t = \nu\,\varphi_{xx}$ to a solution
$u(x, t)$ of the viscous Burgers equation $u_t + u\,u_x = \nu\,u_{xx}$, where $\nu > 0$ is the viscosity
<Cite id="hopf1950" /> <Cite id="cole1951" />. The initial value $u(x, 0) = u_0(x)$ corresponds to
$\varphi(x, 0) = \exp\paren{-\frac{1}{2\nu}\int_0^x u_0(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}$. Every solution of the nonlinear Burgers
equation with these initial data is therefore obtained from a solution of a linear equation, in closed form.
</Definition>

## Intuition

Burgers' equation is the simplest equation in which two effects compete. The term $u\,u_x$ moves each value of $u$
at the speed $u$, so fast parts of a wave catch up with slow parts ahead of them and the front steepens. The term
$\nu\,u_{xx}$ diffuses $u$ and smooths sharp gradients. Without viscosity the steepening wins: the front becomes
vertical at a finite time and the solution would become multivalued. With viscosity the two effects balance in a
**shock**, a thin, steep but smooth front of width proportional to $\nu$. The equation is a one-dimensional caricature
of gas dynamics and of traffic flow (<NoteLink to="physics-informed-network-for-burgers-equation">a
physics-informed network for Burgers' equation</NoteLink> treats it as a test problem).

Nonlinear equations rarely have a general closed-form solution. Burgers' equation does, because a change of variable
removes the nonlinearity entirely. Eberhard Hopf (1950) and Julian Cole (1951) found the substitution independently.

## From Burgers to the heat equation

Let $u(x, t)$ be a velocity in metres per second, $x$ in metres, $t$ in seconds and $\nu$ in square metres per second.
The derivation takes two substitutions.

**Step 1: a potential.** Write $u = \psi_x$ for a potential $\psi(x, t)$, in square metres per second. Burgers'
equation becomes $\psi_{xt} + \psi_x\,\psi_{xx} = \nu\,\psi_{xxx}$, and every term is an $x$-derivative:
$\partial_x\paren{\psi_t + \tfrac12\psi_x^2 - \nu\,\psi_{xx}} = 0$. The bracket is therefore a function of $t$ alone,
which can be absorbed into $\psi$ without changing $u = \psi_x$, so

$$
\psi_t + \tfrac12\,\psi_x^2 = \nu\,\psi_{xx}.
$$

**Step 2: a logarithm.** Write $\psi = -2\nu\ln\varphi$ with $\varphi > 0$. Then

$$
\psi_t = -2\nu\,\frac{\varphi_t}{\varphi}, \qquad \psi_x = -2\nu\,\frac{\varphi_x}{\varphi}, \qquad
\psi_{xx} = -2\nu\paren{\frac{\varphi_{xx}}{\varphi} - \frac{\varphi_x^2}{\varphi^2}}.
$$

Substituting into the equation for $\psi$:

$$
-2\nu\,\frac{\varphi_t}{\varphi} + 2\nu^2\,\frac{\varphi_x^2}{\varphi^2}
= -2\nu^2\,\frac{\varphi_{xx}}{\varphi} + 2\nu^2\,\frac{\varphi_x^2}{\varphi^2}.
$$

The two $\varphi_x^2/\varphi^2$ terms are identical and cancel. That cancellation is why the factor in front of the
logarithm is exactly $-2\nu$: any other factor leaves a quadratic term behind. Multiplying the rest by
$-\varphi/(2\nu)$ leaves the heat equation

$$
\varphi_t = \nu\,\varphi_{xx}.
$$

Combining the two steps, $u = \psi_x = -2\nu\,\varphi_x/\varphi$. The velocity depends on $\varphi$ only through
$\varphi_x/\varphi$, so a positive factor that does not depend on $x$ leaves $u$ unchanged. The initial
condition follows from $\psi(x, 0) = \int_0^x u_0$: $\varphi(x, 0) = \exp\paren{-F(x)/2\nu}$ with
$F(x) = \int_0^x u_0(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ <Cite id="whitham1974" />.

## The solution on the line

The heat equation is solved by convolution with the heat kernel
(<NoteLink to="heat-equation">heat equation</NoteLink>):
$\varphi(x, t) = (4\pi\nu t)^{-1/2}\int \varphi(\xi, 0)\, e^{-(x - \xi)^2/(4\nu t)}\,\mathrm{d}\xi$. Applying
$u = -2\nu\,\varphi_x/\varphi$ gives

$$
u(x, t) = \frac{\displaystyle\int \frac{x - \xi}{t}\, e^{-G(\xi;\, x, t)/2\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\xi}
{\displaystyle\int e^{-G(\xi;\, x, t)/2\nu}\,\mathrm{d}\xi},
\qquad G(\xi; x, t) = F(\xi) + \frac{(x - \xi)^2}{2t}.
$$

The velocity at $(x, t)$ is a weighted average of $(x - \xi)/t$, the velocity a particle needs to travel from $\xi$ to
$x$ in time $t$, with weights $e^{-G/2\nu}$ <Cite id="whitham1974" />.

**The inviscid limit.** As $\nu \to 0$ the weights concentrate at the minimiser $\xi^*$ of $G$. There
$\partial_\xi G = u_0(\xi^*) - (x - \xi^*)/t = 0$, so $x = \xi^* + t\,u_0(\xi^*)$ and $u(x, t) = u_0(\xi^*)$: the
value $u_0(\xi^*)$ has been carried from $\xi^*$ to $x$ at its own speed. This is the solution by characteristics of
the inviscid equation $u_t + u\,u_x = 0$ (<NoteLink to="transport-and-continuity-equations">method of
characteristics</NoteLink>). Characteristics first cross at the **breaking time** $t_b = -1/\min_x u_0'(x)$. After
that, some points $x$ have two minimisers of equal depth; $u$ jumps between their two values there, and that jump is
the shock. The rule "the deepest minimum wins" places the shock where the inviscid theory's equal-area rule does,
without any further assumption <Cite id="whitham1974" />.

## The shock profile and its width

A steady shock joins a state $u_L$ on the left to $u_R < u_L$ on the right. Look for a travelling wave
$u = U(x - ct)$. Burgers' equation becomes $-c\,U' + U\,U' = \nu\,U''$, which integrates once to
$-c\,U + \tfrac12 U^2 = \nu\,U' + A$ for a constant $A$. Far from the shock $U' = 0$, so
$-c\,u_L + \tfrac12 u_L^2 = -c\,u_R + \tfrac12 u_R^2$, which gives the shock speed $c = (u_L + u_R)/2$. With
$\Delta u = u_L - u_R$ and $W = U - c$ the equation becomes $\nu\,W' = \tfrac12\paren{W^2 - (\Delta u/2)^2}$, whose
solution is **Taylor's shock profile**

$$
u = c - \frac{\Delta u}{2}\tanh\paren{\frac{\Delta u\,(x - ct)}{4\nu}}.
$$

Its steepest slope, at the centre, is $\Delta u^2/(8\nu)$. Dividing the jump by that slope defines the **shock width**

$$
w = \frac{\Delta u}{\max\abs{u_x}} = \frac{8\nu}{\Delta u}.
$$

The width is proportional to the viscosity and inversely proportional to the jump: halving $\nu$ halves the width.

In the Cole–Hopf picture the shock is a crossover between two exponentials. For $u_L = 1$, $u_R = -1$ the shock is at
rest ($c = 0$), and $\varphi = e^{x/2\nu} + e^{-x/2\nu} = 2\cosh(x/2\nu)$ solves the heat equation up to a factor
$e^{t/4\nu}$, which leaves $u$ unchanged. Then $u = -2\nu\,\varphi_x/\varphi = -\tanh(x/2\nu)$. For $x < 0$ the term
$e^{-x/2\nu}$ dominates $\varphi$ and $u \approx 1$; for $x > 0$ the term $e^{x/2\nu}$ dominates and $u \approx -1$.
The shock is the region, a few multiples of $\nu$ wide, where neither term dominates.

## Worked example

**A stationary shock.** Take $u_L = 1$, $u_R = -1$ and $\nu = 0.05$, in units where lengths and times are
dimensionless.

1. The shock speed is $c = (1 + (-1))/2 = 0$ and the jump is $\Delta u = 2$.
2. The profile is $u = -\tanh\paren{2x/(4 \times 0.05)} = -\tanh(10x)$.
3. At $x = 0.1$, $u = -\tanh 1 = -0.762$; at $x = 0.2$, $u = -\tanh 2 = -0.964$. The velocity covers 76% of its
   change from $0$ to $-1$ within $0.1$ of the centre.
4. The slope at the centre is $-10$, so the width is $w = 2/10 = 0.2$, equal to $8\nu/\Delta u = 0.4/2$.

**A breaking sine.** Take $u_0 = \sin x$ on the periodic interval $[0, 2\pi)$ with $\nu = 0.05$, the figure's default.

1. The steepest downward slope of $u_0$ is $-1$, at $x = \pi$, so the inviscid wave breaks at $t_b = 1$.
2. $F(x) = \int_0^x \sin s\,\mathrm{d}s = 1 - \cos x$ ranges from $0$ to $2$, so $\varphi(x, 0) = e^{-(1 - \cos x)/0.1}$
   ranges from $e^{-20} = 2.1 \times 10^{-9}$ at $x = \pi$ to $1$ at $x = 0$.
3. The figure solves the heat equation on $378$ grid points, $\Delta x = 2\pi/377 = 0.0167$, by explicit
   <Gloss name="finite-difference">finite differences</Gloss> with $r = \nu\Delta t/\Delta x^2 = 0.4$. Each step
   replaces $\varphi_i$ by $0.2\,\varphi_i + 0.4\,(\varphi_{i-1} + \varphi_{i+1})$, a weighted average with positive
   weights, so $\varphi$ stays positive. The time step is $\Delta t = 0.4 \times 0.0167^2/0.05 = 0.0022$, and $t = 3$
   takes $1351$ steps.
4. At $t = 2$ the shock at $x = \pi$ has jump $\Delta u = 1.69$. Its measured width is $0.214$ against
   $8\nu/\Delta u = 0.237$.
5. Repeating with $\nu = 0.02$ and $\nu = 0.01$ gives widths $0.084$ and $0.043$ at $t = 2$: the width falls in
   proportion to $\nu$, and the ratio to $8\nu/\Delta u$ approaches 1 (0.96 and 0.99).

The ratio is below 1 at $\nu = 0.05$ because the sine's shock sits on a sloping background, not between two constant
states: the width $0.2$ is not small compared with the distance over which $u$ varies away from the shock.

## Pitfalls

- **The transformation is specific.** It works for Burgers' equation with constant $\nu$ and the nonlinearity $u\,u_x$
  exactly. A variable viscosity, a second space dimension with a velocity field that is not a gradient, or a different
  nonlinearity breaks the cancellation in Step 2.
- **φ spans many orders of magnitude.** $\varphi(x, 0)$ falls to $e^{-\max F/2\nu}$, which is $e^{-100}$ for the sine
  at $\nu = 0.01$. Computing $\varphi_x/\varphi$ from differences of $\varphi$ itself, or with a scheme that can make
  $\varphi$ negative (Crank–Nicolson at large time steps can oscillate), fails where $\varphi$ is tiny. Differencing
  $\ln\varphi$ and a positivity-preserving scheme avoid both problems.
- **Periodic data need zero mean.** On a periodic interval $\varphi(x, 0)$ is periodic only if $\int u_0 = 0$ over a
  period. A nonzero mean $\bar{u}$ is removed first by moving to a frame travelling at $\bar{u}$.
- **The width formula is for a shock between constant states.** On a sloping background, as for the sine, the measured
  width differs from $8\nu/\Delta u$ until the shock is narrow compared with the wave.
- **The grid must resolve the shock.** With fewer than about five grid points across $8\nu/\Delta u$, the computed
  front is too wide; the figure's readout reports the number of points.
